Cosmological long-wavelength solutions in non-adiabatic multi-fluid systems Preprint: RUP-25-28
Hayami Iizuka, Tomohiro Harada

Abstract
We develop a formulation of nonlinear cosmological perturbations on superhorizon scales in multi-fluid systems. It is based on the Arnowitt–Deser–Misner formalism combined with a spatial gradient expansion, characterized by a small parameter ϵ ≡ k / ( a ( b ) ​ H ) \epsilon\equiv k/(a^{(b)}H) , where a ⁡ ( t ) a(t) is the scale factor of the flat Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker spacetime, H ( b ) ​ ( t ) {}^{(b)}H(t) is the corresponding Hubble parameter, and k k is the comoving wavenumber. Within this framework, we explicitly construct nonlinear long-wavelength solutions for cosmological perturbations. Since multi-fluid systems are inherently non-adiabatic, these solutions admit both adiabatic and entropy modes even at nonlinear leading order in the expansion. We define adiabatic and entropy perturbations, while face non uniqueness in defining pure entropy perturbations. Using the possible variations of pure entropy initial conditions, we analyze the time evolution of physical quantities, such as the curvature perturbation and density perturbation, in the geodesic slice for two-fluid systems.



I
Introduction

Primordial black holes (PBHs) are hypothetical black holes that could have formed in the early universe
29
;
10
;
3
, particularly during the high-temperature, high-density epoch following the Big Bang. Unlike stellar black holes, which result from the gravitational collapse of massive stars, PBHs may have originated from the direct collapse of a large amplitude of density fluctuation generated in the early universe. As a result, their masses are not constrained by stellar evolution, and they can span a wide range. The PBH mass spectrum thus provides insight into small-scale cosmological perturbations that are far below the scales probed by the cosmic microwave background (CMB).

Various theoretical models have been proposed to explain the generation of curvature perturbations, which in turn generate density perturbations, particularly in the context of inflation and other early-universe scenarios. Given that the mass of PBHs is in the asteroid mass window, they are viable candidates for constituting the entirety of dark matter (see Ref.
4
for a comprehensive review).
Recent advancements in gravitational wave (GW) observations, led by the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA collaboration, have reported over a hundred events involving mergers of compact objects. PBHs are among the proposed sources of these events, offering a possible connection between PBHs and the observed GWs (see Ref.
21
for details on PBHs in the context of GWs).

Over the past three decades, cosmological nonlinear perturbation theory has made significant progress. Deriving solutions obey the gradient expansion method
26
;
19
;
6
;
22
, which follows an assumption
that a physical scale
L
is much longer than a fiducial Hubble scale. In this context, we choose the physical scale as
L^{-1}=k/a
, where
k~\text{and}~a
are some wave number and the scale factor of fiducial Friedmann‐Lemaître‐Robertson-Walker (FLRW) spacetime, respectively. Then, we can define a fictitious parameter
\epsilon\equiv k/(a{}^{(b)}H)
, where
{}^{(b)}H\equiv\dot{a}/a
. The Einstein and matter field equations are then expanded in powers of
\epsilon
, yielding solutions known as cosmological long-wavelength solutions.

Since gravitational collapse leading to the formation of a black hole is a strong gravitational phenomenon, simply solving the Einstein equations under approximations such as linear perturbation is insufficient to discuss gravitational collapse; the full Einstein equations must be solved. However, since the Einstein equations are nonlinear second-order partial differential equations, we cannot solve them analytically in general. Therefore, we need to use numerical simulation to solve these equations. Using the long-wavelength solutions as an initial condition for a numerical simulation of PBH formation is one of the interesting applications
22
;
9
;
28
;
5
.

On cosmological scales, the universe is remarkably homogeneous and isotropic, as evidenced by the near-uniformity of CMB temperature and density fluctuations
23
;
1
. At these scales, we observe that the matter content of the universe behaves as an adiabatic fluid. However, our knowledge of the universe at smaller scales remains limited. In particular, the early universe probably underwent several transitions, such as reheating and the QCD phase transition, during which the dominant energy component changed. These transitional epochs naturally gave rise to systems composed of multiple fluids with distinct thermodynamic properties. In such settings, non-adiabaticity can play a role in the transitional phase.

Non-adiabatic perturbations naturally arise in multi-fluid systems, in contrast to the purely adiabatic fluctuations dominant in effectively single fluid systems. Non-adiabatic perturbations include the so-called entropy (isocurvature) perturbation, which describes fluctuations in the relative energy densities of different fluid components without affecting the total curvature. Recent studies suggest that such entropy fluctuations can be significantly amplified on small scales. These enhanced perturbations are of considerable interest, as they may act as sources of stochastic gravitational wave backgrounds with potentially observable spectra
7
or trigger gravitational collapse leading to the formation of PBHs
18
;
28
;
5
.

Significant attention has been directed toward long-wavelength solutions in cosmological perturbation theory, particularly in the context of single-fluid systems
26
;
15
;
25
;
24
;
9
, as well as multi-component systems where one component overwhelmingly dominates the total energy density
28
. However, in a general multi-fluid system, each element can contribute comparably to the energy budget of the universe, and the amplitude of its respective density fluctuations can be of similar magnitude. In such cases, both adiabatic and non-adiabatic perturbations can influence the leading-order behavior of the long-wavelength solutions. The formation of PBHs may occur during these multi-component epochs in the early universe. To simulate PBH formation in such systems, it is essential to construct consistent long-wavelength solutions that account for the non-negligible contributions from the multiple fluids. These solutions serve as physical initial conditions necessary for numerical simulations that follow the nonlinear gravitational collapse.

On superhorizon scales, the long-wavelength approximation provides a powerful framework in which the spacetime is approximated by a collection of locally Friedmann - Lemaître - Robertson - Walker (FLRW) “separate universes”
27
. The
\delta\mathscr{N}
formalism, originating from the linear argument of Sasaki and Stewart
20
and its nonlinear extension by Lyth, Malik, and Sasaki
15
, relies on this picture: the curvature perturbation
\zeta
on a uniform-total-density slice is identified with the perturbation of the local e-folding number
\delta\mathscr{N}
.
This identification has become one of the central tools in early-universe cosmology, particularly in studies of primordial non Gaussianity
16
and curvaton scenarios
11
. Once the local e-folding number
\delta\mathscr{N}
is given in the adiabatic system, the evolution of perturbations is completely determined. This property is automatically satisfied in single-field or effectively single-field systems, where the equation of state is barotropic, and the system is adiabatic at leading order in terms of the gradient expansion.
In contrast, in non-adiabatic systems, Naruko, Takamizu, and Sasaki
17
investigated long-wavelength solutions in multi-scalar-field systems.
They developed the so-called beyond-
\delta\mathscr{N}
formalism, where the spatial dependence of the total energy density
gives time dependence of the curvature perturbation already at
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{0})
.

In multi-fluid systems with
N
components, even if each component satisfies an independent conservation equation, we have
N
independent spatial integration functions.
These functions cannot be removed by gauge transformations and represent genuine physical degrees of freedom arising from intrinsic non-adiabaticity.
As a result, the spatial freedom that appears at
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{0})
is physical rather than gauge-related, and its presence directly signals the breakdown of the standard view of the
\delta\mathscr{N}
formalism. This observation also clarifies why previous multi-component applications of the
\delta\mathscr{N}
formalism—such as the curvaton scenario
11
—remain valid only when non-adiabatic contributions are suppressed at leading order.

In this paper, we construct explicit nonlinear long-wavelength solutions for general multi-fluid systems using the Arnowitt–Deser–Misner (ADM) formalism
2
.
We provide a fully nonlinear definition of adiabatic and entropy perturbations applicable to multi-fluid cosmologies and present how to isolate an adiabatic perturbation and to define a pure entropy perturbation. We then apply this framework to two-fluid systems and explicitly demonstrate how curvature and density perturbations evolve when intrinsic non-adiabaticity contributes at leading order.
Thus, beyond the conventional
\delta\mathscr{N}
formalism, we
establish a consistent theoretical foundation for the study of nonlinear perturbations in non-adiabatic multi-component universes.

This paper is organized as follows. We write down basic equations in the ADM formalism of the Einstein equation and perform the gradient expansion to obtain long-wavelength solutions in Sec.
II
. The definition of an adiabatic perturbation and an entropy perturbation in a multi-fluid system and the derivation of the long wavelength solution at zeroth-order are discussed in Sec.
III
.
In Sec.
IV
, we restrict our discussion to two-fluid systems and present the physical properties of the long-wavelength solutions.
As an example for the leading-order long-wavelength solutions expressed by elemental functions, we consider a matter-radiation system in Sec.
V
. Sec.
VI
is devoted to concluding this paper. In this paper, we use units in which both the speed of light and Newton’s gravitational constant are set to unity,
c=1~\text{and}~G=1
.

II.2
Expansion scheme

The spacetime is assumed to be smooth on a scale larger than
k^{-1}
.
This assumption leads to the physical smoothing scale
L\equiv a/k
, where
a
is the scale factor on flat FLRW spacetime.
In the context of cosmology, it is natural to choose a certain scale as a reference,
which is typically taken as
H\equiv\dot{a}/a
,
and use it to introduce the parameter
\epsilon
defined as
\epsilon\equiv H^{-1}/L=k/(aH)

Gradient expansion is the process of expanding the basic equations in powers of
\epsilon

assuming that spatial derivatives are small compared to the comoving Hubble scale:
\epsilon\ll 1

allowing one to obtain solutions to any order.
This corresponds to focusing on waves on superhorizon scales.

Based on the above considerations, we assume two conditions. First, the conformal factor
\psi
is an identical unity somewhere in the universe.
This assumption makes
a(t)
the scale factor for the part of the Hubble Patch.
Second, there exists the coordinate
x^{k}=x^{k}_{0}
such that, in the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, the universe becomes locally homogeneous, isotropic, and spatially flat. This assumption
means that the metric of some specific local region

\differential s^{2}=-\differential t^{2}+a^{2}\eta_{ij}\differential x^{i}\differential x^{j},

(37)

where
\eta_{ij}

\psi=\order{\epsilon^0}

35
) for the background spacetime can be integrated to give

{}^{(b)}\rho_{(\alpha)}=D_{(\alpha)}a^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}},

(56)

where
D_{(\alpha)}
are an integral constant.
It leads to the Friedmann equation becoming

\quantity(\frac{\dot{a}}{a})^{2}=\frac{8\pi}{3}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}D_{(\alpha)}a^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}.

(57)

II.4
Slicing conditions

The condition of the uniform Hubble (UH) slice is expressed by

\tilde{H}=\frac{\dot{a}}{a}.

(58)

The condition of the constant mean curvature (CMC) slice is expressed by

K=-3\frac{\dot{a}}{a}.

(59)

These expressions arise due to the assumption that the background spacetime is a spatially flat FLRW universe, where the expansion is homogeneous and isotropic. In such a background, the Hubble parameter
\dot{a}/a
fully characterizes the extrinsic curvature of constant-time hypersurfaces, and the trace of the extrinsic curvature
K
is directly proportional to the Hubble parameter. Therefore, these slices are equivalent to each of them. Therefore, we will refer to these slices as the CMC slice.

Taking the CMC slicing condition, the momentum constraint (
24
) shows

J_{i}=\order{\epsilon^2}.

(60)

From this condition, we see that the comoving (C) slicing condition, which is defined by

J_{i}=0,

(61)

and CMC slice are equivalent to
\order{\epsilon}
.
We can also conclude that the CMC slice corresponds to the uniform density (UD) slice,
\rho_{\text{tot}}={}^{(b)}\rho_{\text{tot}}(t)
, to
\order{\epsilon}
from Eqs. (
46
) and (
50
).
Therefore, we can identify the lapse function under the CMC slice by using Eqs. (
44
) and (
45
)

\displaystyle\alpha=

\displaystyle\frac{A(t)}{{}^{(b)}\rho_{\text{tot}}(t)+p_{\text{tot}}(t,x^{k})}+\order{\epsilon^2},

(62)

where
A(t)
can be any function that depends only on
t
and makes the lapse function positively definite.

In the case of non-adiabatic fluids, as discussed in Ref.
15
, the lapse function at zeroth order is reduced to

\displaystyle{}^{(0)}\alpha=1-\frac{\delta p(t,x^{k})}{{}^{(b)}\rho_{\text{tot}}(t)+p_{\text{tot}}\quantity(t,x^k)},

(63)

by taking the function
A(t)
as the following form:

A(t)={}^{(b)}\rho_{\text{tot}}(t)+{}^{(b)}p_{\text{tot}}(t),

(64)

and
\delta p
is defined by

\delta p(t,x^{k})\equiv{}^{(0)}p_{\text{tot}}(t,x^{k})-{}^{(b)}p_{\text{tot}}(t).

(65)

This function is a non-linear, non-adiabatic pressure perturbation at the zeroth order.

A proper time
\tau
is defined by

\tau(t,x^{k})=\int_{x^{k}=\text{comst}}\alpha(t,x^{k})\differential t,

(66)

evaluated along the constant world line.
On the synchronous (Sync) slice, which is defined by

\alpha=\alpha(t),

(67)

the proper time simplifies to a function of coordinate time only:

\tau\quantity(t)\equiv\int^{t}\alpha\quantity(\tilde{t})\differential\tilde{t}.

(68)

In general slices where
\alpha
depends on both time and space, the proper time
\tau
is not a coordinate. However, under the Sync slice,
\tau
can be used as a new time coordinate, since it is purely a function of
t
. We can then redefine the time coordinate via
\tilde{\alpha}\differential t=\differential\tau
.
In the following Sec.
III.4.3
, we proceed with the discussion under the special case of this slicing condition: geodesic slice
\alpha=1
.
From this, comparing Eqs. (
63
) and (
67
), it can be seen that the CMC, UD, and comoving slices do not coincide with the geodesic slice (and Sync slice) even at
\order{\epsilon^0}
.
If we restrict our discussion to G, the local Hubble parameter and mean curvature are not spatially constant even at
\order{\epsilon^0}
(see Sec.
III.4.3
).
Since the Hamiltonian constraint (
46
) shows a relation between the local Hubble parameter and total energy density, the total energy density is also not spatially constant at
\order{\epsilon^0}
under the geodesic slice.
It should also be noted that on the geodesic slice, the time coordinate
t
coincides with the proper time
\tau
, and they share the same physical meaning.
Therefore, in what follows, we simply use
t
as the time coordinate under the geodesic slicing condition.

Taking the spatial gradient of both side of the Eq. (
43
) in geodesic slice, we find the following relation:

{\partial}_{i}\quantity(\frac{\dot{\psi}}{\psi})=-\frac{1}{3}{\partial}_{i}\quantity(\frac{\dot{\rho}_\text{tot}}{\rho_\text{tot}+p_\text{tot}})+\order{\epsilon^3}.

(69)

Integrating the above equation, since the first term of the right-hand side of Eq. (
69
) is
\order{\epsilon}
, we get the following relation:

\frac{\dot{\psi}}{\psi}=\order{\epsilon^0}.

(70)

We find that the conformal factor
\psi
acquires a time-dependent perturbation at
\order{\epsilon^0}
in the presence of non-adiabaticity on the geodesic slice. This behavior contrasts with the case of adiabatic fluids considered in Refs.
15
;
25
;
24
;
9
, where the conformal factor remains constant in time to
\order{\epsilon}
. In multi-fluid systems, however, the conformal factor exhibits time dependence already at leading order, due to the presence of non-adiabatic perturbations. This result is consistent with earlier studies such as Ref.
27
, which also emphasized the dynamical nature of curvature in non-adiabatic systems.

III
Long-wavelength solutions in multi-fluid systems

III.1
Adiabatic and entropy perturbations

Here, we introduce adiabatic and entropy perturbations without imposing that they are solutions of the basic equations.

III.1.1
Adiabatic perturbation

Let us consider an adiabatic perturbation and entropy perturbation conditions on a given time slice.

The background solution for the matter energy density is given by Eq. (
56
):

{}^{(b)}\rho_{(\alpha)}=D_{(\alpha)}a^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}.

(1)

Introducing the e-folding number as
\mathscr{N}(t)=\ln a(t)
, the energy density is reduced to

{}^{(b)}\rho_{(\alpha)}=D_{(\alpha)}e^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}\mathscr{N}(t)}.

(2)

If there exists a common spatially dependent time shift
\delta\mathscr{N}(t,x^{k})
such that, for all the components
(\alpha)
,

\rho_{(\alpha)}=D_{(\alpha)}\quantity[e^{\mathscr{N}(t)+\delta\mathscr{N}(t,x^k)}]^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}},

(3)

then the perturbation is called an adiabatic perturbation. Perturbations that do not satisfy this are called non-adiabatic perturbations.

There is another, apparently different, definition of an adiabatic perturbation: if there exists a common time shift
\Delta t(t,x^{k})
such that, for all
(\alpha)

\rho_{(\alpha)}={}^{(b)}\rho_{(\alpha)}(t+\Delta t(t,x^{k})),

(4)

then this perturbation is usually called an adiabatic perturbation. This is equivalent to the definition given in Eq. (
3
), provided that
a(t)
is a monotonic function of
t
. The reason is that replacing
t
by
t+\Delta t(t,x^{k})
in the background solution is equivalent to replacing
\mathscr{N}
by

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}(t+\Delta t(t,x^{k}))

\displaystyle=\mathscr{N}(t)+\mathscr{N}(t+\Delta t(t,x^{k}))-\mathscr{N}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{N}(t)+\delta\mathscr{N}(t,x^{k}),

(5)

where we have defined

\delta\mathscr{N}(t,x^{k})\equiv\mathscr{N}(t+\Delta t(t,x^{k}))-\mathscr{N}(t).

(6)

The relation between this nonlinear definition and the linear perturbative notion of adiabaticity is further discussed in Appendix
A
.

III.1.2
Energy density at an equality time

The total background energy density and the total energy density of the adiabatic perturbation are written as

\displaystyle{}^{(b)}\rho_{\text{tot}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}D_{(\alpha)}e^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}\mathscr{N}(t)}={}^{(b)}\rho_{\text{tot}}(\mathscr{N};D_{(1)},\dots,D_{(N)}),

(7)

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{tot}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}D_{(\alpha)}e^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}(\mathscr{N}(t)+\delta\mathscr{N}(t,x^{k}))}={}^{(b)}\rho_{\text{tot}}(\mathscr{N}(t)+\delta\mathscr{N}(t,x^{k});D_{(1)},\dots,D_{(N)}).

(8)

For an
N
-fluid background, we define the
\alpha\beta
–equal time for any two distinct components
\alpha
and
\beta
as the time at which the following condition is
satisfied:

{}^{(b)}\rho_{(\alpha)}={}^{(b)}\rho_{(\beta)}.

(9)

From Eqs. (
56
) and (
2
), we find the following relation

\displaystyle a_{\alpha\beta-\text{eq}}

\displaystyle=\quantity(\frac{D_{(\beta)}}{D_{(\alpha)}})^{1/\quantity[3(\Gamma_{(\beta)}-\Gamma_{(\alpha)})]},

(10)

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}_{\alpha\beta-\text{eq}}

\displaystyle\equiv\ln a_{\alpha\beta-\text{eq}},

(11)

\displaystyle{}^{(b)}\rho_{(\alpha)\alpha\beta-\text{eq}}

\displaystyle={}^{(b)}\rho_{(\beta)\alpha\beta-\text{eq}}=\quantity(D_{(\alpha)}^{\Gamma_{(\beta)}}D_{(\beta)}^{-\Gamma_{(\alpha)}})^{1/(\Gamma_{(\beta)}-\Gamma_{(\alpha)})},

(12)

\displaystyle{}^{(b)}\rho_{\text{tot},\alpha\beta-\text{eq}}

\displaystyle={}^{(b)}\rho_{\text{tot}}\quantity( \mathscr{N}_{\alpha\beta-\text{eq}};D_{(1)},\dots,D_{(N)}).

(13)

For the adiabatic perturbation Eq. (
3
), we find the same relation for
\mathscr{N}+\delta\mathscr{N}

\displaystyle\quantity[e^{\mathscr{N}+\delta\mathscr{N}}]_{\alpha\beta-\text{eq}}

\displaystyle=\quantity(\frac{D_{(\beta)}}{D_{(\alpha)}})^{1/\quantity[3(\Gamma_{(\beta)}-\Gamma_{(\alpha)})]},

(14)

\displaystyle\rho_{(\alpha)\alpha\beta-\text{eq}}(x^{k})

\displaystyle=\rho_{(\beta)\alpha\beta-\text{eq}}=\quantity(D_{(\alpha)}^{\Gamma_{(\beta)}}D_{(\beta)}^{-\Gamma_{(\alpha)}})^{1/(\Gamma_{(\beta)}-\Gamma_{(\alpha)})},

(15)

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{tot},\alpha\beta-\text{eq}}

\displaystyle=\rho_{\text{tot}}(\quantity(\mathscr{N}+\delta\mathscr{N})_{\alpha\beta-\text{eq}};D_{(1)},\dots,D_{(N)})=\rho_{\text{tot}}\quantity( \mathscr{N}_{\alpha\beta-\text{eq}};D_{(1)},\dots,D_{(N)}).

(16)

Therefore, the adiabatic energy density is the same as the background one at
\alpha\beta
-equality time.

III.1.3
Entropy perturbation

Solving the adiabatic condition, Eq. (
3
), for
\mathscr{N}+\delta\mathscr{N}
, we find the following solution

\mathscr{N}(t)+\delta\mathscr{N}(t,x^{k})=-\frac{1}{3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}\ln\quantity(\frac{\rho_{(\alpha)}}{D_{(\alpha)}}),\quad\mbox{or}\quad\delta\mathscr{N}(t,x^{k})=-\frac{1}{3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}\quantity(\frac{\rho_{(\alpha)}}{{}^{(b)}\rho_{(\alpha)}}).

(17)

From this solution, we conclude that
-1/(3\Gamma_{(\alpha)})\left(\rho_{(\alpha)}/({}^{(b)}\rho_{(\alpha)})\right)
does not depend on a component of
(\alpha)
for the adiabatic perturbation. This is rewritten as

-\frac{1}{3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}\quantity(\frac{\rho_{(\alpha)}}{{}^{(b)}\rho_{(\alpha)}})=-\frac{1}{3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}\ln\quantity(1+\delta_{(\alpha)}).

(18)

Thus, the necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of the function
\delta\mathscr{N}
is for an arbitrary pair of components,
(\alpha),~(\beta)
, satisfying

-\frac{1}{3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}\ln\quantity(1+\delta_{(\alpha)})=-\frac{1}{3\Gamma_{(\beta)}}\ln\quantity(1+\delta_{(\beta)}).

(19)

Then, it is reasonable to define an entropy perturbation
S_{(\alpha\beta)}
as

S_{(\alpha\beta)}\equiv\frac{1}{3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}\ln\quantity(1+\delta_{(\alpha)})-\frac{1}{3\Gamma_{(\beta)}}\ln\quantity(1+\delta_{(\beta)}).

(20)

III.2
Zeroth-order adiabatic and entropy perturbations

Here, we construct zeroth-order long wavelength solutions and decompose them into adiabatic and entropy perturbations.

III.2.1
Zeroth-order solutions

Integrating Eqs. (
47
) and (
49
), we find the energy density at the zeroth order under the general slice:

{}^{(0)}\rho_{(\alpha)}(t,x^{k})=C_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})\quantity(a(t)\psi^2)^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}},

(21)

where
C_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})
is an integral function which comes from integration of the energy equation for each fluid.
For later convenience, we decompose the integral function as follows:

\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})\equiv\frac{C_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})}{D_{(\alpha)}}.

(22)

Then, the energy density for each fluid is rewritten by

{}^{(0)}\rho_{(\alpha)}=D_{(\alpha)}\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})(a(t)\psi^{2})^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}.

(23)

Following the convention of Lyth, Malik, and Sasaki (2005)
15
, we define a local e-folding number
\mathscr{N}(t_{2},t_{1};x^{k})
as follows:

\mathscr{N}(t_{2},t_{1};x^{k})\equiv-\frac{1}{3}\int^{t_{2}}_{t_{1},x^{k}=\text{const}}\alpha K\differential t.

(24)

Using Eq. (
47
), this definition is reduced to

\mathscr{N}(t_{2},t_{1};x^{k})=\ln\frac{a(t_{2})}{a(t_{1})}+2\ln\frac{\psi(t_{2},x^{k})}{\psi(t_{1},x^{k})}.

(25)

For the background solution, since we take time slicing which coincides with the natural one of the FLRW solution with
\psi=1
, we do not need to take care of the spatial dependence of the function
\mathscr{N}
. Also, taking the lower end of the integral reference time
t_{0}
, the background e-folding number is defined by

\mathscr{N}(t)\equiv\mathscr{N}(t,t_{0};x^{k}),

(26)

where
t_{0}
on the left-hand side is omitted for brevity.
If we redefine
D_{(\alpha)}a(t_{0})^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}
simply as
D_{(\alpha)}
, then all the equations obtained so far can be used without any further modification.

By choosing the lower limit also at
t_{0}
for the zeroth–order solution, we can write

{}^{(0)}\rho_{(\alpha)}(t,x)=\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}(x)\,D_{(\alpha)}e^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}\,\mathscr{N}(t,t_{0};x)},

(27)

where we have redefined

\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}(x)\psi^{-6\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}(t_{0},x)

again as
\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}(x)
.
In what follows, we simply denote
\mathscr{N}(t,t_{0};x)
by
\mathscr{N}
and treat it as if it were
a time coordinate.
With this choice, the gauge dependence of
\psi(t,x)
can be absorbed into
\mathscr{N}
.

III.2.2
Adiabatic perturbation

We now discuss the necessary and sufficient condition for the zeroth-order solution (
27
) to be an adiabatic perturbation. Substituting the zeroth-order solution Eq. (
27
) into
Eq. (
3
), we obtain

\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})D_{(\alpha)}e^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}\mathscr{N}}=D_{(\alpha)}e^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}\quantity(\mathscr{N}+\delta\mathscr{N})}.

(28)

Here, assuming that the values of
\mathscr{N}
on both sides are identical, we find that the existence
of a function
\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}(x^{k})
, independent of
(\alpha)
, such that the expression reduces to

-\frac{\ln\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})}{3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}=\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}(x^{k}),\quad\mbox{or}\quad\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})=e^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}(x^{k})},

(29)

constitutes the necessary and sufficient condition for the perturbation to be adiabatic. From this, we also find the following relation:

\delta\mathscr{N}=\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}(x^{k}).

(30)

The background solution can be obtained by setting
\bar{C}=1
and
\psi=1
.
Therefore, an adiabatic perturbation possesses one degree of freedom, corresponding to
an arbitrary function of
x^{k}
. Assuming that the zeroth-order solution is an adiabatic perturbation, Eq. (
27
) can be rewritten for all
(\alpha)
as

{}^{(0)}\rho_{(\alpha)}=D_{(\alpha)}e^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}\mathscr{N}}e^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}\delta\mathscr{N}^{\mathrm{ad}}}=D_{(\alpha)}e^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}(\mathscr{N}+\delta\mathscr{N}^{\mathrm{ad}})}.

(31)

Up to this point, the discussion has been carried out at a fixed time
t
, but in fact,
the condition on
C_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})
using the actual zeroth-order solution, Eq. (
29
),
is independent of
t
. Thus, it can be shown that a perturbation that is adiabatic at
a given time is adiabatic at any other time. It should be noted that the temporal and spatial dependence of
\psi
for an adiabatic perturbation is not fixed unless the gauge is specified.

By setting
{}^{(0)}K=-3\tilde{H}
, Eq. (
46
) gives

\tilde{H}^{2}=\frac{8\pi}{3}{}^{(b)}\rho_{\text{tot}}.

(32)

Therefore, in the two-fluid case, for a zeroth-order adiabatic perturbation, the expansion rate at the equal-density time is independent of
x^{k}
and coincides with the background one.
This result naturally extends to the multi-fluid case: for a zeroth-order adiabatic perturbation, the expansion rate at the
\alpha\beta
-equality time is also independent of
x^{k}
and equal to the background one.

III.2.3
Entropy perturbation

We introduce a function
\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})
as

\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})=e^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})},

(33)

for later convenience. Then, Eq. (
27
) can be rewritten as

{}^{(0)}\rho_{(\alpha)}=D_{(\alpha)}e^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}\quantity(\mathscr{N}+\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\alpha)}(x^k))},

(34)

for all
(\alpha)
. In this way, each point
x^{k}
can be interpreted as being described by a separate universe on top of a common background, in which the evolution of each species
(\alpha)
—whether faster
or slower—is specified by
\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})
.

Since an adiabatic perturbation is represented by
\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}(x^{k})
, the quantity obtained by dividing Eq. (
27
) by Eq. (
31
), namely

\frac{{}^{(0)}\rho_{(\alpha)}}{{}^{(0)}\rho_{(\alpha)}|_{\text{ad}}}=e^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}\quantity(\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\alpha)}(x^k)-\delta\mathscr{N}^\text{ad}(x^k))}=e^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{nad}}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})},

(35)

constitutes an independent degree of freedom, where

\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{nad}}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})\coloneqq\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})-\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}(x^{k}).

(36)

This degree of freedom exists regardless of the presence of an adiabatic perturbation and is therefore naturally referred to as a non-adiabatic perturbation. Since
\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}(x^{k})
can be chosen arbitrarily, a non-adiabatic perturbation contains one arbitrary function of
x^{k}
corresponding to the adiabatic perturbation, and
(N-1)
arbitrary functions of
x^{k}
corresponding to entropy perturbations. With this definition, it follows naturally that if an entropy perturbation does not exist at a given time, it does not exist at any other time.

For
N
fluid systems, an entropy perturbation has
(N-1)
degrees of freedom, for example, corresponding to
\{\delta\mathscr{N}_{i}-\delta\mathscr{N}_{i+1}\}_{i=1,\dots,N-1}
. Using Eq. (
27
), we obtain

\displaystyle e^{\tilde{\mathscr{N}}_{\alpha\beta-\text{eq}}(x^{k})}

\displaystyle=\quantity(\frac{\bar{C}_{(\beta)}{D_{(\beta)}}}{\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}{D_{(\alpha)}}})^{1/\quantity[3\qty(\Gamma_{(\beta)}-\Gamma_{(\alpha)})]},

(37)

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{(\alpha),\alpha\beta-\text{eq}}(x^{k})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\rho}_{(\beta),\alpha\beta-\text{eq}}(x^{k})=\quantity[\qty(\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}{D_{(\alpha)}})^{\Gamma_{(\beta)}}\qty(\bar{C}_{(\beta)}{D_{(\beta)}})^{-\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}]^{1/\quantity(\Gamma_{(\beta)}-\Gamma_{(\alpha)})}.

(38)

It then follows that

h_{(\alpha\beta)}^{2}(x^{k})\equiv\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{(\alpha),\alpha\beta-\text{eq}}(x^{k})}{{}^{(b)}\rho_{(\alpha),\alpha\beta-\text{eq}}}=\left(\frac{\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}(x)^{1/\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}}{\bar{C}_{(\beta)}(x)^{1/\Gamma_{(\beta)}}}\right)^{\frac{\Gamma_{(\beta)}\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}{\Gamma_{(\beta)}-\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}}=e^{3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}\Gamma_{(\beta)}\quantity(\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\alpha)}-\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\beta)})/\quantity(\Gamma_{(\alpha)}-\Gamma_{(\beta)})},

(39)

so that

\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\alpha\beta)}\equiv\frac{1}{2}(\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\alpha)}-\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\beta)})=\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\delta\mathscr{N}^\text{nad}_{(\alpha)}-\delta\mathscr{N}^\text{nad}_{(\beta)})=\frac{\Gamma_{(\alpha)}-\Gamma_{(\beta)}}{3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}\Gamma_{(\beta)}}\ln h_{(\alpha\beta)}.

(40)

This procedure allows us to isolate the
\alpha\beta
-entropy perturbation. In fact, the entropy perturbation
S_{(\alpha\beta)}
defined in Sec.
III.1.3
is rewritten by

S_{(\alpha\beta)}=-2\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\alpha\beta)}.

(41)

Both
S_{(\alpha\beta)}
and
\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\alpha\beta)}
are fully non-linear
definitions of the
\alpha\beta
-entropy perturbation.
The quantity
\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{nad}}_{(\alpha)}
represents the non-adiabatic, non-linear
degree of freedom of each fluid component individually.

\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\alpha\beta)}
can be viewed as the difference
(\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{nad}}_{(\alpha)}-\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{nad}}_{(\beta)})

that isolates the
\alpha\beta
-entropy perturbation.
All three quantities describe the same physical degrees of freedom,
but in different representations.
In the linearized limit, they reduce to the conventional definition of the entropy
(isocurvature) perturbation familiar from linear perturbation theory.

Let us restrict our discussion to a two-fluid system.
We consider the expansion rate at the equal-density hypersurface.
As we have already seen, for an adiabatic perturbation, the expansion rate at equality time is uniform and equal to the background value.
For a non-adiabatic perturbation, using Eq. (
27
), we find

\displaystyle e^{\tilde{\mathscr{N}}_{\mathrm{eq}}(x^{k})}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\bar{C}_{(2)}(x^{k})D_{(2)}}{\bar{C}_{(1)}(x^{k})D_{(1)}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3(\Gamma_{(2)}-\Gamma_{(1)})}},

(42)

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{eq}}(x^{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{8\pi}{3}\rho_{\rm tot,eq}(x^{k})=\frac{8\pi}{3}\left[(\bar{C}_{(1)}D_{(1)})^{\Gamma_{(2)}}(\bar{C}_{(2)}D_{(2)})^{-\Gamma_{(1)}}\right]^{\frac{1}{\Gamma_{(2)}-\Gamma_{(1)}}}.

(43)

It then follows that

h(x^{k})\equiv\frac{\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{eq}}(x^{k})}{H_{\mathrm{eq}}}=\left(\frac{\bar{C}_{(1)}(x^{k})^{1/\Gamma_{(1)}}}{\bar{C}_{(2)}(x^{k})^{1/\Gamma_{(2)}}}\right)^{\frac{\Gamma_{(1)}\Gamma_{(2)}}{2\quantity(\Gamma_{(1)} - \Gamma_{(2)})}}=\exp\left[\frac{3\Gamma_{(1)}\Gamma_{(2)}}{2\quantity(\Gamma_{(1)} - \Gamma_{(2)})}(\delta\mathscr{N}_{(1)}-\delta\mathscr{N}_{(2)})\right].

(44)

Consequently, we can define the two-fluid entropy perturbation as

\delta\mathscr{N}_{(12)}=\frac{1}{2}(\delta\mathscr{N}_{(1)}-\delta\mathscr{N}_{(2)})=\frac{\Gamma_{(1)}-\Gamma_{(2)}}{3\Gamma_{(1)}\Gamma_{(2)}}\ln h(x^{k}),

(45)

which successfully isolates the entropy perturbation between the two fluids.

III.3
Defining pure entropy perturbations

From the above discussion, we find that adiabatic perturbations possess an intrinsic arbitrariness.
In the following subsubsection, we fix this arbitrariness and attempt to isolate adiabatic perturbations by adopting a concrete profile for the adiabatic mode.

III.3.1
Primeval isocurvature condition

A simple way to fix the choice of
\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}(x^{k})
is, for example,

\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}=\delta\mathscr{N}_{(1)},\quad\text{or equivalently}\quad\bar{C}_{(1)}=e^{-3\Gamma_{(1)}\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}}.

(46)

The remaining functions
\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}(x)
for
\alpha=2,\dots,N
can then be chosen freely.
In this choice, the total density fluctuation
{}^{(0)}\delta_{\text{tot}}
, which is defined by

{}^{(0)}\delta_{\text{tot}}\equiv\frac{{}^{(0)}\rho_{\text{tot}}-{}^{(b)}\rho_{\text{tot}}}{{}^{(b)}\rho_{\text{tot}}}=\frac{\sum_{\alpha}{}^{(b)}\rho_{(\alpha)}{}^{(0)}\delta_{(\alpha)}}{\sum_{\beta}{}^{(b)}\rho_{(\beta)}},

(47)

vanishes at the early universe, where the component
(1)
dominates, but subsequently can grow nonlinearly due to the entropy perturbation.
In the two-fluid case, this choice leads to

\displaystyle\delta\mathscr{N}_{(1)}

\displaystyle=\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}},

(48)

\displaystyle\delta\mathscr{N}_{(2)}

\displaystyle=\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}-2\delta\mathscr{N}_{(12)},

(49)

so that
\delta\mathscr{N}_{(1)}
and
\delta\mathscr{N}_{(2)}
are expressed as linear combinations of
\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}
and
\delta\mathscr{N}_{(12)}
.
With this convention, the pure entropy condition corresponds to setting
\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}=0
, which immediately implies
\delta\mathscr{N}_{(1)}=0
. As we will see later in Sec.
IV.3.2
, this choice corresponds to initially isocurvature condition in two-fluid systems in geodesic slicing.

III.3.2
Zero-average condition

An another choice for
\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}(x^{k})
is given by

\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\alpha}\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k}).

(50)

This can be interpreted as the reference adiabatic perturbation corresponding to a separate-universe picture obtained by averaging the
\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\alpha)}
over all components
(\alpha)
in the original non-adiabatic perturbation.

In the two-fluid case, this gives

e^{\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}(x^{k})}=\left[\bar{C}_{(1)}(x^{k})^{1/\Gamma_{(1)}}\bar{C}_{(2)}(x^{k})^{1/\Gamma_{(2)}}\right]^{-1/6}.

(51)

Consequently,
\delta\mathscr{N}_{(1)}+\delta\mathscr{N}_{(2)}
corresponds to the adiabatic perturbation, while
\delta\mathscr{N}_{1}-\delta\mathscr{N}_{(2)}
corresponds to the entropy perturbation.
Furthermore, combining Eqs. (
51
) and (
44
), we can express
\delta\mathscr{N}_{(1)}
and
\delta\mathscr{N}_{(2)}
as linear combinations of
\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}
and
\delta\mathscr{N}_{(12)}

\displaystyle\delta\mathscr{N}_{(1)}

\displaystyle=\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}+\delta\mathscr{N}_{(12)},

(52)

\displaystyle\delta\mathscr{N}_{(2)}

\displaystyle=\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}-\delta\mathscr{N}_{(12)}.

(53)

The pure entropy condition corresponds to setting
\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}=0
, which implies
\delta\mathscr{N}_{(1)}=-\delta\mathscr{N}_{(2)}
.

III.3.3
Initially isocurvature condition

Alternatively, one can use the freedom in choosing
\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}
to fix
\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})
such that, under a certain gauge condition,

\psi(t_{0},x^{k})=1.

(54)

Here, we consider one example of this approach.

The zeroth-order solution
{}^{(0)}\rho_{(\alpha)}(t,x^{k})
is integrated as in Eq. (
23
), where the background solution
{}^{(b)}\rho_{(\alpha)}(t)
is multiplied by
\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})\psi^{-6\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}
. The total density
\rho_{\mathrm{tot}}(t,x^{k})
can be computed from Eq. (
50
). Then, for the zeroth-order solution on the geodesic slice (taking the lapse function
\alpha=1
), Eqs. (
46
) and (
47
) imply that
a\psi^{2}
exactly satisfies the Friedmann equation with the background
D_{(\alpha)}
replaced by
\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}D_{(\alpha)}
.

If one further imposes the no-decaying-mode condition,
a(t)\psi^{2}(t,x^{k})
coincides exactly with the background flat FLRW solution with
D_{(\alpha)}\to\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}D_{(\alpha)}
. Therefore, at a given time
t=t_{0}
,
\psi(t_{0},x^{k})
is completely determined by
\{\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})\}_{\alpha=1,\dots,N}
.
In other words, if we require
\psi(t_{0},x^{k})
to satisfy

\psi(t_{0},x^{k})=f(x^{k})

(55)

for a given function
f(x^{k})
,
this gives
a single constraint for
\{\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})\}_{\alpha=1,\dots,N}
.
In particular, if we require

\psi(t_{0},x^{k})=1,

(56)

we will obtain a constraint for
\{\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})\}_{\alpha=1,\dots,N}
, which
is independent of the adiabatic condition and can be regarded as an isocurvature condition.

III.4
Leading-order solution with slicing conditions

III.4.1
Uniform e-folding slice

The condition for the uniform e-folding slice (
\mathscr{N}
slice) is given by

\alpha K=-3\frac{\dot{a}}{a}.

(57)

Taking this slice, we find the following relation from Eq. (
47
):

\psi=\psi(x^{k}).

(58)

Moreover, from Eq. (
49
), we find that the energy densities for the fluid components are expressed by

{}^{(0)}\rho_{(\alpha)}(t,x^{k})=c_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})a^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}(t),

(59)

where
c_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})
is an integral function for all fluid components.
Using this form of the energy densities, the mean curvature is formally expressed by

{}^{(0)}K(t,x^{k})=\sqrt{24\pi\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}c_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})a^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}(t)}.

(60)

Combining Eqs. (
57
) and (
60
), we can determine the lapse function under the
\mathscr{N}
slice. We have also shown that the
\mathscr{N}
slice is equivalent to neither the CMC slice nor the synchronous slice.

We define a density fluctuation for the component of the fluids as

{}^{(0)}\delta_{(\alpha)}(t,x^{k})\equiv\frac{{}^{(0)}\rho_{(\alpha)}(t,x^{k})-{}^{(b)}\rho_{(\alpha)}(t)}{{}^{(b)}\rho_{(\alpha)}(t)},

(61)

for all
\alpha
.
The minimum value of this function is
-1
.
The density fluctuation on
\mathscr{N}
slice is reduced to

{}^{(0)}\delta_{(\alpha)}=\bar{c}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})-1,

(62)

where we have assumed that the integral function is decomposed as follows:

c_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})=D_{(\alpha)}\bar{c}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k}).

(63)

Eq. (
62
) represents the density fluctuation for the components of the fluids that are constant in time and independent of the function
\psi(x^{k})
.

III.4.2
CMC slice

Taking CMC slice, Eqs. (
47
) and (
49
) gives

{}^{(0)}\rho_{(\alpha)}(t,x^{k})=c^{\prime}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})\quantity(a(t)\psi^2(t,x^k))^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}},

(64)

where
c^{\prime}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})
are integral function which comes from energy equation.
Putting Eq. (
64
) into Eq. (
46
), Eq. (
46
) is reduced to

\quantity(\frac{\dot{a}}{a})^{2}=\frac{8\pi}{3}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}c^{\prime}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})\quantity(a(t)\psi^2(t,x^k))^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}.

(65)

The left-hand side of the above equation corresponds to the left-hand side of the background Friedmann equation which is given by Eq. (
57
). Thus, we find the following relation:

\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}D_{(\alpha)}a^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}(t)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}c^{\prime}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})\quantity(a(t)\psi^2(t,x^k))^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}.

(66)

This equation is an algebraic equation for the function
\psi
.
Solving Eq. (
66
) for
\psi
, we formally find the cosmological long wavelength solution:

\psi(t,x^{k})=\psi(a(t);D_{(\alpha)},\bar{c}^{\prime}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})),

(67)

where we have decomposed the integral function:

c^{\prime}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})=D_{(\alpha)}\bar{c}^{\prime}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k}).

(68)

By adopting this solution to the energy density for the fluid components and incorporating the form of the energy density into the definition of the density fluctuation (
61
), we observe that the density fluctuations for the fluid components exhibit both time and spatial dependence.

III.4.3
Geodesic slice

As discussed in Sec.
II.4
, once we choose the geodesic slice, the proper time acts as the coordinate. Using the proper time as a time coordinate, the leading-order basic equations are reduced to

\displaystyle{}^{(0)}K^{2}=

\displaystyle 24\pi{}^{(0)}E,

(69)

\displaystyle{}^{(0)}K=

\displaystyle-3\frac{{\partial}_{t}\quantity(a\psi^2)}{a\psi^{2}},

(70)

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t}{{}^{(0)}K}=

\displaystyle\quantity[\frac{1}{3}{}^{(0)}K^2+4\pi{}^{(0)}\qty(E+S^i_i)],

(71)

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t}\quantity(a^3\psi^6{}^{(0)}\rho_{(\alpha)})=

\displaystyle a^{3}\psi^{6}\quantity(\Gamma_{(\alpha)}-1){}^{(0)}\rho_{(\alpha)}{}^{(0)}K.

(72)

We fix the shift vector in the following discussion as
\beta^{i}=0
.

Eqs. (
70
) and (
72
) gives

{}^{(0)}\rho_{(\alpha)}=C_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})\quantity(a\psi^2)^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}},

(73)

where
C_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})
is integral functions.
By using Eqs. (
69
) and (
73
), the Hamiltonian constraint reduces to

\quantity[\frac{\PA_t\qty(a\psi^2)}{a\psi^2}]^{2}=\frac{8\pi}{3}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}C_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})\quantity(a\psi^2)^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}.

(74)

In App.
C
, we have expanded the basic equations to the next-to-leading order, obeying the gradient expansion method. Expressing the integral form of the Euler equation at
\order{\epsilon}
, we have shown that this individual spatial component of a four-velocity
u^{i}
under the geodesic slice and normal coordinates, i.e., Gaussian normal coordinates, has a decaying mode and the term which contains non-trivial time dependence at
\order{\epsilon}
. Since the pressure gradient contributes to the last term, it may play a crucial role in the dynamics of PBH formation.

IV
Long-wavelength solutions in two-fluid systems

A two-fluid system is not only the simplest among the multi-fluid systems, but also provides physical insights into the behavior of the long-wavelength perturbations unique to
multi-component systems.
Moreover, this practically gives a reasonable approximation to many physically realistic and cosmologically motivated
multi-component systems.
Here, we consider nonlinear long-wavelength solutions in two-fluid systems.

IV.1
Background solutions

The Friedmann equation for the background solution becomes

\quantity(\frac{\dot{a}}{a})^{2}=\frac{8\pi}{3}\quantity[D_{(1)}a^{-3\Gamma_{(1)}}+D_{(2)}a^{-3\Gamma_{(2)}}].

(1)

The two-fluid system can be conveniently formulated by introducing the equality time
t_{\rm eq}
.
Introducing the energy density and the scale factor at the equality time as

\displaystyle\frac{{}^{(b)}\rho_{\text{eq}}}{2}

\displaystyle\equiv D_{(1)}^{\Gamma_{(2)}/\quantity(\Gamma_{(2)}-\Gamma_{(1)})}D_{(2)}^{\Gamma_{(1)}/\quantity(\Gamma_{(1)}-\Gamma_{(2)})},

(2)

\displaystyle a_{\text{eq}}

\displaystyle=\quantity(\frac{D_{(1)}}{D_{(2)}})^{1/[3(\Gamma_{(1)}-\Gamma_{(2)})]},

(3)

the Friedmann equation (
1
) is reduced to

\quantity(\frac{\dot{a}}{a})^{2}=\frac{8\pi}{3}\frac{{}^{(b)}\rho_{\text{eq}}}{2}\quantity[\qty(\frac{a}{a_\text{eq}})^{-3\Gamma_{(1)}}+\qty(\frac{a}{a_\text{eq}})^{-3\Gamma_{(2)}}],

(4)

in the background spacetime.
Let us introduce a new variable
y
, which is defined by

y\equiv\frac{a}{a_{\text{eq}}}.

(5)

Then, Eq. (
4
) reduces to

\frac{\dot{y}}{y}\frac{1}{\sqrt{y^{-3\Gamma_{(1)}}+y^{-3\Gamma_{(2)}}}}=\frac{H_{\text{eq}}}{\sqrt{2}},

(6)

where
H_{\text{eq}}
is the Hubble parameter at the equality time, which is defined by

H_{\text{eq}}^{2}\equiv\frac{8\pi}{3}\rho_{\text{eq}}.

(7)

The integral form of Eq. (
6
) is given by

\int_{0}^{y}\frac{\differential\tilde{y}}{\tilde{y}}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\tilde{y}^{-3\Gamma_{(1)}}+\tilde{y}^{-3\Gamma_{(2)}}}}=H_{\text{eq}}\quantity(t-t_\text{s}).

(8)

For convenience, we introduce

q(y)\equiv\int_{0}^{y}\frac{\differential\tilde{y}}{\tilde{y}}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\tilde{y}^{-3\Gamma_{(1)}}+\tilde{y}^{-3\Gamma_{(2)}}}}.

(9)

Then, we can express the solution (
8
) as follows:

y=q^{-1}(H_{\text{eq}}\quantity(t-t_\text{s})),

(10)

and adopt this function as the background solution with a normalization condition

a_{\text{eq}}=1.

(11)

The normalization condition makes the integral constants equal

D_{(1)}=D_{(2)}.

(12)

IV.2
Long-wavelength solutions

IV.2.1
Long-wavelength solutions with the geodesic slice

For the perturbed system, if we choose the geodesic slice, the Hamiltonian constraint (
74
) reduces to

\quantity[\frac{\PA_t\qty(a\Psi^2)}{a\Psi^2}]^{2}=\frac{8\pi}{3}\quantity[C_{(1)}(x^k)\qty(a\Psi^2)^{-3\Gamma_{(1)}}+C_{(2)}(x^k)\qty(a\Psi^2)^{-3\Gamma_{(2)}}].

(13)

Introducing the following quantities,

\displaystyle{}^{(0)}\Xi(t,x^{k})

\displaystyle\equiv a(t)\Psi^{2}(t,x^{k}),

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{{}^{(0)}\rho_{\text{eq}}(x^{k})}{2}

\displaystyle\equiv C_{(1)}(x^{k})^{\Gamma_{(2)}/\quantity(\Gamma_{(2)}-\Gamma_{(1)})}C_{(2)}(x^{k})^{\Gamma_{(1)}/\quantity(\Gamma_{(1)}-\Gamma_{(2)})},

(15)

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{\text{eq}}^{2}(x^{k})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{16\pi}{3}C_{(1)}(x^{k})^{\Gamma_{(2)}/\quantity(\Gamma_{(2)}-\Gamma_{(1)})}C_{(2)}(x^{k})^{\Gamma_{(1)}/\quantity(\Gamma_{(1)}-\Gamma_{(2)})},

(16)

\displaystyle{}^{(0)}\Xi_{\text{eq}}(x^{k})

\displaystyle\equiv\quantity(\frac{C_{(1)}(x^k)}{C_{(2)}(x^k)})^{1/[3(\Gamma_{(1)}-\Gamma_{(2)})]},

(17)

the leading-order Hamiltonian constraint takes the following form:

\quantity[\frac{\PA_t{}^{(0)}\Xi}{{}^{(0)}\Xi}]^{2}=\frac{8\pi}{3}\frac{{}^{(0)}\rho_{\text{eq}}(x^{k})}{2}\quantity[\qty(\frac{{}^{(0)}\Xi}{{}^{(0)}\Xi_{\text{eq}}(x^k)})^{-3\Gamma_{(1)}}+\qty(\frac{{}^{(0)}\Xi}{{}^{(0)}\Xi_{\text{eq}}(x^k)})^{-3\Gamma_{(2)}}].

(18)

Eq. (
18
) shows
{}^{(0)}\Xi(t,x^{k})
is monotonically increasing.
This is integrated to give

\int^{Y}_{0}\frac{\differential\tilde{Y}}{\tilde{Y}}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\tilde{Y}^{-3\Gamma_{(1)}}+\tilde{Y}^{-3\Gamma_{(2)}}}}=\tilde{H}_{\text{eq}}(x^{k})(t-{}^{(0)}\tilde{t}_{\text{s}}(x^{k})),

(19)

where we have introduced

\displaystyle Y\equiv\frac{{}^{(0)}\Xi(t,x^{k})}{{}^{(0)}\Xi_{\text{eq}}(x^{k})}

(20)

and
\tilde{t}_{\text{s}}=\tilde{t}_{\text{s}}(x^{k})
is the initial time which satisfies
Y(\tilde{t}_{\text{s}}(x^{k}),x^{k})=0
.
Eq. (
19
) can be rewritten in terms of
q
as

Y\quantity(t,x^k)=q^{-1}\quantity(\tilde{H}_\text{eq}(x^k)\qty(t-{}^{(0)}\tilde{t}_\text{eq}(x^k))).

(21)

By using this solution and the background solution, we find the leading-order conformal factor as follows:

\Psi(t,x^{k})=\sqrt{\frac{{q^{-1}\quantity(\tilde{H}_\text{eq}(x^k)(t-{}^{(0)}\tilde{t}_\text{s}(x^k))){}^{(0)}\Xi_{\text{eq}}(x^{k})}}{q^{-1}(H_{\text{eq}}(t-t_{\text{s}}))}}.

(22)

Determining the long-wavelength solution for the conformal factor
\Psi(t,x^{k})
needs to fix the functions
{}^{(0)}\Xi_{\text{eq}}(x^{k}),~\tilde{H}_{\text{eq}}(x^{k}),~\text{and}~\tilde{t}_{\text{s}}(x^{k})
. The parameter
{}^{(0)}\Xi_{\text{eq}}(x^{k})
is fixed by the ratio
C_{(1)}(x^{k})/C_{(2)}(x^{k})
, while
\tilde{H}_{\text{eq}}(x^{k})
is determined by their product. Accordingly, both
C_{(1)}(x^{k})
and
C_{(2)}(x^{k})
need to be uniquely specified.

IV.2.2
Asymptotic behaviours of the curvature and density perturbations

Let us consider the fluid (1) dominated limit of the function
q(Y)
.
The limit corresponds to the condition
Y\ll 1
.

Denoting the integrand in the left-hand side of Eq. (
19
) by
F
, we have

q(Y)=\int_{0}^{Y}F(\tilde{Y})\differential\tilde{Y}.

(23)

In the limit of
Y\ll 1
, the function
F(Y)
is expanded as

F(Y)=\sqrt{2}Y^{\quantity(3\Gamma_{(1)}/2)-1}+\dots.

(24)

Therefore, we find the asymptotic behaviour of the function
f(Y)
as

q(Y)\simeq\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3\Gamma_{(1)}}Y^{3\Gamma_{(1)}/2}.

(25)

Thus, we find that, in the limit of
Y\rightarrow 0
, the Hamiltonian constraint is expressed by

\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3\Gamma_{(1)}}Y^{\quantity(3\Gamma_{(1)}/2)}\simeq\tilde{H}_{\text{eq}}(x^{k})\quantity(t-\tilde{t}_\text{s}(x^k)).

(26)

The density contrast of the fluid (1),
{}^{(0)}\delta_{(1)}
, is expanded as

\displaystyle{}^{(0)}\delta_{(1)}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{\quantity(t-\tilde{t}_\text{s}(x^k))^{2}}\quantity[\qty(t-t_\text{s})^2-\qty(t-\tilde{t}_\text{s}(x^k))^2].

(27)

Assuming that we can neglect a decaying mode, the following relation is postulated

\tilde{t}_{\text{s}}(x^{k})=t_{\text{s}}.

(28)

We choose the origin of the time coordinate so that
{}^{(b)}H_{\text{eq}}t_{\text{s}}=0
.
Following the assumption, we find that, in the limit of
t\rightarrow t_{\text{s}}
, the zeroth order of the conformal factor as

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle\rightarrow{}^{(0)}\Xi_{\text{eq}}^{1/2}(x^{k})\quantity(\frac{\tilde{H}_\text{eq}(x^k)}{H_\text{eq}})^{1/(3\Gamma_{(1)})}={}^{(0)}\Xi_{\text{eq}}^{1/2}(x^{k})h^{1/\quantity(3\Gamma_{(1)})}(x^{k})=\exp\quantity[-\frac{1}{2}\delta\mathscr{N}_{(1)}].

(29)

From Eq. (
61
), the density fluctuation is reduced to

{}^{(0)}\delta_{(\alpha)}\to\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}\Psi^{-6\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}(x^{k})-1=\exp\quantity[3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}\qty(\delta\mathscr{N}_{(1)}-\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\alpha)})]-1.

(30)

Therefore,
\Psi
becomes constant in time in this limit.
In this limit, the contribution of fluid (1) dominates that of fluid (2), so that the curvature perturbation is governed by the perturbation of fluid (1). Hence, the system approaches the adiabatic regime.

Using these forms of the solutions with no decaying mode, the background and cosmological long wavelength solution for a two-fluid system are reduced to

\displaystyle a(t)

\displaystyle=q^{-1}(H_{\text{eq}}t),

(31)

\displaystyle\Psi(t,x^{k})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{q^{-1}(hH_{\text{eq}}t){}^{(0)}\Xi_{\text{eq}}(x^{k})}{q^{-1}(H_{\text{eq}}t)}}.

(32)

Next, we consider the late-time limit. In this limit, the function
q(Y)
is approximated by

q(Y)\simeq\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3\Gamma_{(2)}}Y^{(3\Gamma_{(2)})/2}.

(33)

Therefore, in the late time limit, the conformal factor
\Psi
is reduced to

\displaystyle\Psi\rightarrow{}^{(0)}\Xi_{\text{eq}}^{1/2}(x^{k})h^{1/(3\Gamma_{(2)})}(x^{k})=\exp\quantity[-\frac{1}{2}\delta\mathscr{N}_{(2)}],

(34)

which means
\Psi
becomes constant in time. This arises from the fact that, in the late-time limit, fluid (2) becomes dominant over fluid (1), making this limit equivalent to the adiabatic one. The density fluctuation for fluid (
\alpha
) is then reduces to

{}^{(0)}\delta_{(\alpha)}\to\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}\Psi^{-6\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}(x^{k})-1=\exp\quantity[3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}\qty(\delta\mathscr{N}_{(2)}-\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\alpha)})]-1,

(35)

at a late time. Therefore, the density fluctuation also becomes constant in time.

Therefore, both

\Psi
and
{}^{(0)}\delta_{(\alpha)}
become time-independent in the early- and late-time limits, with their asymptotic behavior governed by the time evolution during the intermediate transition from early to late times.

IV.2.3
Curvature perturbations and density perturbations

In two-fluid systems, we find the leading-order solution is given by Eq. (
32
). Rewriting the solution by using the functions
h(x^{k})
,
\delta\mathscr{N}_{(1)}
and
\delta\mathscr{N}_{(2)}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{q^{-1}(hH_{\text{eq}}t)}{q^{-1}(H_{\text{eq}}t)}}\exp\quantity[-\frac{1}{2}\qty(\frac{\Gamma_{(1)}}{\Gamma_{(1)}-\Gamma_{(2)}}\delta\mathscr{N}_{(1)}-\frac{\Gamma_{(2)}}{\Gamma_{(1)}-\Gamma_{(2)}}\delta\mathscr{N}_{(2)})],

(36)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{q^{-1}(hH_{\text{eq}}t)}{q^{-1}(H_{\text{eq}}t)}}\exp\quantity[-\frac{1}{2}\qty(\frac{\Gamma_{(1)}}{\Gamma_{(1)}-\Gamma_{(2)}}\delta\mathscr{N}^\text{nad}_{(1)}-\frac{\Gamma_{(2)}}{\Gamma_{(1)}-\Gamma_{(2)}}\delta\mathscr{N}^\text{nad}_{(2)}+\delta\mathscr{N}^\text{ad})]

(37)

where we choose the origin of the time coordinate as
H_{\text{eq}}t_{\text{s}}=0
. This relation shows that the curvature perturbation depends on both an adiabatic perturbation and an entropy perturbation, since the function
\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\alpha)}
is expressed by

\delta\mathscr{N}_{(\alpha)}=\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{nad}}_{(\alpha)}+\delta\mathscr{N}^{\text{ad}}.

(38)

Whereas, we find the shape of the density perturbations for an individual fluid as follows in two-fluid systems on the geodesic slice:

\displaystyle{}^{(0)}\delta_{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=\quantity(\frac{q^{-1}(hH_\text{eq}t)}{q^{-1}(H_\text{eq}t)})^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}\exp\quantity[\frac{3\Gamma_{(1)}\Gamma_{(2)}}{\Gamma_{(1)}-\Gamma_{(2)}}\qty(\delta\mathscr{N}_{(1)}-\delta\mathscr{N}_{(2)})]

\displaystyle=\quantity(\frac{q^{-1}(hH_\text{eq}t)}{q^{-1}(H_\text{eq}t)})^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}h^{2}(x^{k}).

(39)

Eq. (
39
) therefore implies that, in two-fluid systems, the density perturbations of individual components depend only on
h
or
\delta\mathscr{N}_{(12)}
.
This independence also holds for the total density perturbation

\delta_{\rm tot}
by its definition.
In contrast, the curvature perturbation
\Psi
for purely entropic perturbation depends on the definition of an adiabatic perturbation.
As a result, the local e-folding number
\delta\mathscr{N}=\zeta
is not enough to specify the physically relevant density perturbations in multi-fluid systems.
This originates from the intrinsic non-adiabaticity,
which introduces independent physical degrees of freedom not captured by a single local expansion history.

IV.3
Adiabatic and isocurvature conditions

IV.3.1
Adiabatic condition

Assuming that the adiabatic condition Eq. (
3
) is satisfied at an equality time
t_{0}=\tilde{t}_{\text{eq}}(x^{k})
in two fluid systems, the condition is expressed as

{}^{(0)}\rho_{(\alpha)}(\tilde{t}_{\text{eq}}(x^{k}),x^{k})={}^{(b)}\rho_{(\alpha)}(\tilde{t}_{\text{eq}}(x^{k})+\Delta t(x^{k})),

(40)

for
\alpha=1,~2
.
The energy densities of the two components are equal to each other at the equality time. Therefore, one can find that, from Eq. (
40
), the energy densities of the two components of the corresponding background solution are also equal to each other. Thus, the following relations are satisfied:

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{\text{eq}}(x^{k})+\Delta t(x^{k})=t_{\text{eq}}

(41)

\displaystyle{}^{(0)}{\rho}_{\text{eq}}(\tilde{t}_{\text{eq}}(x^{k}),x^{k})={}^{(b)}\rho_{\text{eq}},

(42)

where

{}^{(b)}\rho_{\text{eq}}=2{}^{(b)}\rho_{(1)}(t_{\text{eq}})=2{}^{(b)}\rho_{(2)}(t_{\text{eq}}),

(43)

and

{}^{(0)}\rho_{\text{eq}}(\tilde{t}_{\text{eq}}(x^{k}),x^{k})\equiv 2{}^{(0)}\rho_{(1)}(\tilde{t}_{\text{eq}}(x^{k}),x^{k})=2{}^{(0)}\rho_{(2)}(\tilde{t}_{\text{eq}}(x^{k}),x^{k}).

(44)

The Hamiltonian constraint Eq. (
18
) at the equality time shows

{}^{(0)}\tilde{H}_{\text{eq}}^{2}(x^{k})=\frac{8\pi}{3}{}^{(0)}{\rho}_{\text{eq}}(\tilde{t}_{\text{eq}}(x^{k}),x^{k})=\frac{8\pi}{3}{}^{(b)}{\rho}_{\text{eq}}={}^{(b)}H_{\text{eq}}^{2}.

(45)

Namely,
{}^{(0)}\tilde{H}_{\text{eq}}(x^{k})
is constant throughout space and takes the same value as the background one. In this situation, Eq. (
32
) is reduced to

\Psi=\quantity(\frac{C_{(1)}(x^k)}{C_{(2)}(x^k)})^{1/\quantity[6\qty(\Gamma_{(1)}-\Gamma_{(2)})]},

(46)

which means that the leading-order conformal factor (curvature perturbation) remains constant in time.
In Sec.
III.2.3
, we decompose the integral functions
C_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})
which arise in Eq. (
22
) as

C_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})=D_{(\alpha)}\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k}).

(47)

Then, the local Hubble parameter is expressed by

{}^{(0)}\tilde{H}_{\text{eq}}(x^{k})={}^{(b)}H_{\text{eq}}\sqrt{\bar{C}_{(1)}(x^{k})^{\Gamma_{(2)}/\quantity(\Gamma_{(2)}-\Gamma_{(1)})}\bar{C}_{(2)}(x^{k})^{\Gamma_{(1)}/\quantity(\Gamma_{(1)}-\Gamma_{(2)})}}={}^{(b)}H_{\text{eq}}h(x^{k}).

(48)

Since the pure adiabatic condition makes
{}^{(0)}\tilde{H}_{\text{eq}}(x^{k})
equal to the background one, the function
h(x^{k})
is unity. Therefore, we find the relation between
\bar{C}_{(1)}(x^{k})
and
\bar{C}_{(2)}(x^{k})
as follows:

\bar{C}_{(1)}^{-\Gamma_{(2)}}(x^{k})\bar{C}_{(2)}^{\Gamma_{(1)}}(x^{k})=1.

(49)

Considering that the leading-order energy densities, which are expressed by

{}^{(0)}\rho_{(\alpha)}(t,x^{k})=D_{(\alpha)}\bar{C}_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})\quantity(a\Psi^2)^{-3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}},

(50)

the energy densities are reduced to

{}^{(0)}\rho_{(1)}(t,x^{k})=\quantity(\frac{D_{(2)}}{D_{(1)}})^{1/\quantity[3\qty(\Gamma_{(1)}-\Gamma_{(2)})]}{}^{(b)}\rho_{(1)}(t),

(51)

{}^{(0)}\rho_{(2)}(t,x^{k})=\quantity(\frac{D_{(1)}}{D_{(2)}})^{1/\quantity[3\qty(\Gamma_{(2)}-\Gamma_{(1)})]}{}^{(b)}\rho_{(2)}(t).

(52)

Using the normalization condition for the scale factor at the equality time
or Eq. (
12
),
we find

{}^{(0)}\rho_{(\alpha)}(t,x^{k})={}^{(b)}\rho_{(\alpha)}(t),

(53)

for
\alpha=1,~2
. This condition means that the components of the leading-order density fluctuation vanish for an adiabatic perturbation. Note that the density fluctuations are generated at the second order of the long wavelength approximation.

IV.3.2
Primeval isocurvature condition

This condition corresponds to
\delta\mathscr{N}_{(1)}=0
in two-fluids systems discussed in Sec.
III.3.1
. Then, Eq. (
29
) with vanishing
\delta\mathscr{N}_{(1)}
implies

\Psi\rightarrow 1,

(54)

in the limit of
t\to 0
.
Therefore, in the early universe, the pure entropy condition constructed from the long-wavelength dynamics of inflation coincides with the standard isocurvature condition.

VI
Conclusion

We have constructed cosmological nonlinear perturbation solutions for multi-fluid systems on super-horizon scales using the ADM formalism and the spatial gradient expansion method on the general coordinates.
Using these solutions, we have investigated the relations between different slicing conditions.
As a result, we find that the CMC, C, and UD slices are equivalent to
\order{\epsilon}
.
However, these slices are equivalent to neither the geodesic slice nor the
\mathscr{N}
slice even at
\order{\epsilon^0}
.
By adopting the geodesic slice as the slicing condition, we fix the gauge completely and represent the cosmological long-wavelength solutions to
\order{\epsilon^0}
.
These growing-mode solutions are characterized by spatially dependent coefficients of the fluid energy densities, denoted by
\{C_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})\}_{\alpha=1,\dots,N}
,
where
N
is the number of the species.
The density perturbations can be nonlinearly large relative to the background flat FLRW spacetime, and thus can attain arbitrarily large amplitudes.
One of the most significant applications of these solutions is to construct initial data for the formation of PBHs, with or without imposing spherical symmetry.

Unlike the case of a single adiabatic fluid
22
;
15
;
24
;
9
, the long-wavelength solutions in this context are fixed by these
N
functions
C_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})
rather than the curvature perturbation
\Psi
. By setting these functions
C_{(\alpha)}(x^{k})
, we have shown that the zeroth-order solution can be expressed using the same function,
q
, as the background solution on the geodesic slice. As shown in Eq. (
73
), in the present case, the energy density depends on time and spatial coordinates at the zeroth order. Consequently, from Eq. (
74
), the curvature perturbation acquires time dependence from the zeroth order.

We have discussed the pure adiabatic and entropy conditions at a given time slice. Through the discussion, we define the adiabatic perturbation by Eq. (
3
) and the entropy perturbation by Eq. (
40
) in the multi-fluid system at any time slice. We have also presented three ways to separate an adiabatic perturbation and an entropy perturbation, which are given by Eqs. (
46
), (
50
), and (
56
) in the multi-fluid system. In Sec.
IV
, restricting our discussion to a two-fluid system, we derive long-wavelength solutions for two-fluid systems and have shown that in the geodesic slicing, the curvature perturbation depends on the way to isolate an adiabatic perturbation, but the density perturbation for each component
(\alpha)
does not.
Furthermore, in a matter–radiation system, we have demonstrated the time evolution of
the curvature perturbation, which satisfies the pure entropy condition during the radiation-dominated era in Sec.
V
. The curvature perturbation becomes constant in time in the limit of the early and late times, whereas depend on time around the equality time.
This time dependence originates from the non-adiabatic nature of the system.
We have also analyzed the time evolution of the density perturbations with the pure entropy conditions, showing that they become nonlinearly large and eventually approach the effectively adiabatic regime at late times.

The long-wavelength solutions discussed in this paper can be used as initial conditions for

numerical simulations of PBH formation in multi-fluid systems. It will give us a deeper insight into the threshold of PBH
formation by adopting these solutions to the conformal compactness function
8
. We leave these issues for future work.

Appendix A
Linear limit of the nonlinear adiabatic condition

First, we review the entropy perturbation in the linear order.
The adiabatic condition in the cosmological context is given by

p_{\text{tot}}=g(\rho_{\text{tot}}),

(1)

which is a barotropic condition in the context of fluid dynamics.
Irrespective of the gauge condition, the variation takes the following form

\derivative{p_\text{tot}}{\rho_\text{tot}}\delta\rho_{\text{tot}}=\derivative{p_{(1)}}{\rho_{(1)}}\delta\rho_{(1)}+\derivative{p_{(2)}}{\rho_{(2)}}\delta\rho_{(2)}

(2)

in the two-fluid system.
Introducing the speed of sound as

\displaystyle{}^{(b)}c_{(\alpha)}^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\derivative{p_{(\alpha)}}{\rho_{(\alpha)}},

(3)

\displaystyle{}^{(b)}c_{\text{tot}}^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\derivative{p_\text{tot}}{\rho_\text{tot}}=\frac{{}^{(b)}c_{(1)}^{2}\dot{\rho}_{(1)}+{}^{(b)}c_{(2)}^{2}\dot{\rho}_{(2)}}{{}^{(b)}\dot{\rho}_{(1)}+{}^{(b)}\dot{\rho}_{(2)}},

(4)

Eq. (
2
) becomes to

\frac{{}^{(b)}c_{(1)}^{2}\dot{\rho}_{(1)}+{}^{(b)}c_{(2)}^{2}\dot{\rho}_{(2)}}{{}^{(b)}\dot{\rho}_{(1)}+{}^{(b)}\dot{\rho}_{(2)}}\quantity(\delta\rho_{(1)}+\delta\rho_{(2)})={}^{(b)}c_{(1)}^{2}\delta\rho_{(1)}+{}^{(b)}c_{(2)}^{2}\delta\rho_{(2)}.

(5)

This equation can be rearranged as follows

\quantity({}^{(b)}c_{(1)}^2-{}^{(b)}c_{(2)}^2)\quantity(\frac{\delta\rho_{(1)}}{{}^{(b)}\dot{\rho}_{(1)}}-\frac{\delta\rho_{(2)}}{{}^{(b)}\dot{\rho}_{(2)}})=0.

(6)

Assuming
c_{1}^{2}\neq c_{2}^{2}
, we find

\frac{\delta\rho_{(1)}}{\dot{\rho}_{(1)}}-\frac{\delta\rho_{(2)}}{\dot{\rho}_{(2)}}=0.

(7)

In the context of the linear perturbation theory, the entropy perturbation
S^{\text{L}}_{(\alpha\beta)}
is defined by

S^{\text{L}}_{(\alpha\beta)}\equiv-{}^{(b)}H\quantity(\frac{\delta\rho_{(\alpha)}}{\dot{\rho}_{(\alpha)}}-\frac{\delta\rho_{(\beta)}}{\dot{\rho}_{(\beta)}}).

(8)

This perturbation at time

is defined as adiabatic if

S^{\text{L}}_{(\alpha\beta)}(t_{0},x^{k})=0

(9)

holds for all pairs of components
\alpha
and
\beta

This condition is equivalent to the following statement: A perturbation at time
t=t_{0}
is called a

if there exists a common spatially dependent time shift
\Delta t(t_{0},x^{k})
such that,
for all components
\alpha
,

\delta\rho_{(\alpha)}(t_{0},x^{k})={}^{(b)}\dot{\rho}_{(\alpha)}(t_{0})\,\Delta t(t_{0},x^{k}).

(10)

Linearizing the pure adiabatic condition Eq. (
3

10

S_{(\alpha\beta)}

20

S_{(\alpha\beta)}\simeq\frac{\delta_{(\alpha)}}{3\Gamma_{(\alpha)}}-\frac{\delta_{(\beta)}}{3\Gamma_{(\beta)}}.

(11)

This is the same as the conventional definition of the entropy perturbation, Eq. (
8
), in the context of the linear perturbation theory

Appendix C
