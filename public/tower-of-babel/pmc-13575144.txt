Pre-analytical considerations in the interpretation of plasma SCFA profiles
Authors: Boushra Dalile, Kristin Verbeke
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the study by Cai et al. [1], reporting longitudinal changes in plasma short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) concentrations in patients with social anxiety disorder (SAD) following internet-delivered cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT). This is, to our knowledge, a unique longitudinal investigation of circulating SCFA profiles across an extended treatment course in SAD. The two-baseline design that incorporates healthy controls, the use of generalized additive mixed models, and retention exceeding 80% at 36 months are notable methodological strengths. The finding that isobutyric acid was significantly lower in SAD patients than in matched healthy controls at both pre-treatment assessments is particularly compelling, as convergence across two independent time points lends robustness to this group difference.

It was, however, remarkable to see Cai et al. reporting substantial and consistent increases in fasting isobutyric, butyric, propionic and valeric acids after CBT at 36-month follow-up for every patient. As scientists investigating the role of SCFA metabolism within the microbiota-gut-brain axis in humans, the reported data prompt us to interpret these findings with caution. In our hands, typical fasting concentrations of propionate and butyrate range between 1–2 µM and 0.40–1 µM, respectively. At 36 months, butyrate concentrations increased dramatically and consistently across participants to concentrations that are more commonly observed acutely following direct SCFA administration. In addition, butyrate concentrations at 36 months appear to exceed those of propionate, a pattern that is unexpected in fasting blood samples. Under physiological conditions, colonic production of propionate typically equals that of butyrate (colonic SCFA concentrations are estimated to occur in a 60:20:20 ratio of acetate:propionate:butyrate). Yet, the fact that propionate undergoes considerably less splanchnic/hepatic first-pass extraction than butyrate results in higher plasma propionate relative to butyrate concentrations [2]. A consistent butyrate-dominant profile across all patients in fasting samples appears therefore, in our view, biologically implausible.

Cai et al. provide detailed information on their analytical procedures [1]. Samples were collected in a fasted state, plasma was isolated immediately after sampling and stored at −80 °C, plasma underwent a single freeze-thaw cycle, and samples from a single participant were processed within the same analytical batch. These methodological details convincingly demonstrate analytical rigor and suggest that the unexpectedly high propionate and butyrate concentrations at 36 months could not be explained by flaws in the analytical procedures.

We therefore hypothesize that a pre-analytical factor may underly these findings. In a systematic comparison of seven types of blood collection tubes, we previously demonstrated that tube type has a profound impact on measured SCFA concentrations (Table 1) [3]. Cai et al. report the use of lithium-heparin tubes. In our study, plasma from 10 healthy donors collected in such tubes yielded mean propionate and butyrate concentrations of 1.64 and 0.36 µM, respectively (Table 1) [3], which is very much in line with Cai et al.’s data at baseline, 3-month, and 6-month follow-up [1]. In contrast, gel-separator tubes, including plasma separator tubes (PST) and serum separator tubes (SST), induced substantial contamination with propionate and butyrate, but not acetate, resulting in significantly elevated propionate (3.89 and 4.38 µM, respectively) and butyrate concentrations (11.64 and 16.99 µM, respectively) (Table 1). Notably, these elevated levels closely resemble the observed values for propionate and butyrate in Cai et al.’s data at the 36-month time point. We therefore propose that an inadvertent substitution of the lithium heparin tubes with gel-separator tubes at the 36-month time point may have occurred, leading to spuriously elevated propionate and butyrate concentrations while leaving acetate largely unaffected. Such a pre-analytical discrepancy could have been sufficient to substantially determine the study’s conclusions [1].

Long-term studies inevitably carry a greater risk of procedural drift. Blood sampling may occur at different sites, by different staff, under logistical conditions that differ from the tightly controlled earlier time points. In addition, such deviations may be very difficult to verify post factum. After centrifugation and plasma transfer to dedicated (cryo) vials, the original blood tubes are typically discarded and labeling rarely includes the type of blood tube, thereby complicating post-hoc assessment of pre-analytical consistency.

A second point concerns nomenclature. Throughout the paper, isobutyric and isovaleric acid are grouped with acetic, propionic, butyric, and valeric acid under the umbrella term “SCFAs”. Strictly speaking, isobutyric acid (2-methylpropanoic acid) and isovaleric acid (3-methylbutyric acid) are branched-chain fatty acids (BCFAs), derived from microbial fermentation of branched-chain amino acids whereas the straight fatty acids acetate, propionate, and butyrate originate from carbohydrate fermentation [4]. BCFAs and SCFAs differ in microbial origins, colonic distribution, and signaling roles and conflating them risks obscuring biologically meaningful differences and complicates cross-study comparisons. This is especially pertinent here, as isobutyric acid represents the study’s most robust finding. Hence, framing it explicitly as a BCFA and contextualizing it within protein fermentation would substantially sharpen the biological interpretation.

Beyond this specific study, we wish to emphasize a broader issue. As SCFA research expands into disciplines such as psychiatry and neuroscience, where investigators may be less familiar with pre-analytical sensitivities, the risk of unrecognized methodological confounds increases. Blood tube type, processing delays, freeze-thaw cycles, and analytical platforms all exert measurable effects on SCFA concentrations, yet pre-analytical standardization remains underemphasized relative to hypothesis-driven findings. This is illustrated in the challenges that our own methodological work [3] faced in finding a publication venue (the open access journal is not indexed in PubMed, which renders the paper difficult to find), potentially limiting the accessibility of critical practical guidance to researchers entering the field.

We raise these considerations in the spirit of constructive scientific dialogue and encourage the field to treat pre-analytical standardization as a first-order methodological concern, alongside consistent and precise nomenclature. Such practices will enhance reproducibility, facilitate biological interpretation, and enable meaningful comparisons across the growing psychiatric microbiome literature.
