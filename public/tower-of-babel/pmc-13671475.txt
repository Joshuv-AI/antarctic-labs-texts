Sternoclavicular Joint Reconstruction for Chronic Anterior Instability: An Updated Sternal Docking Technique With a Modified Girth Hitch
Authors: Nate J Dickinson, Ryan J Froom, Mikalyn T DeFoor, Tomas Aguirre, Christopher R Bine, Matthew T Provencher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic anterior sternoclavicular joint instability can be functionally limiting in active patient populations, requiring reconstruction in patients who fail to achieve acceptable outcomes with nonoperative treatment. A variety of reconstruction techniques have been described including allograft or autograft figure‐of‐8 reconstruction, intramedullary graft reconstruction, synthetic suture‐based reconstruction, and tenodesis of the sternocleidomastoid or subclavius. Overall, considerable variability in techniques and outcomes exists in the current literature. Although figure‐of‐8 graft reconstruction techniques have been considered the historical gold standard for chronic sternoclavicular joint instability, it is technically demanding and carries an increased risk for mediastinal structures because of bicortical drilling. Therefore, sternal docking reconstructive techniques have gained popularity with robust, unicortical sockets and more controlled tunnel depth/placement. This technical note describes a sternal docking reconstruction technique for chronic anterior sternoclavicular joint instability using a semitendinosus allograft with a modified girth hitch for the allograft in the medial clavicle tunnel and 2 unicortical sternal sockets, allowing anatomic graft placement while minimizing posterior dissection and risk of tunnel convergence, posterior cortical fracture, and/or injury to mediastinal structures.

The sternoclavicular (SC) joint has free passive range of motion in all planes because of the lack of osseous constraint and articular congruity. 1 The SC joint anchors the upper extremity to the axial skeleton and contributes to humeral elevation, glenohumeral mechanics, and scapulothoracic motion. Because of the strength of the SC joint ligamentous complex, SC joint dislocations are rare and typically traumatic, accounting for 0.1% of all upper trunk dislocation. 2 Risk factors include young men engaged in football, wrestling, and biking sports. 2 Although nonoperative management and closed reduction remain first‐line treatment, reconstruction is generally reserved for chronic instability, high‐demand patients, or recurrent symptomatic subluxation limiting physical activity.

Surgical reconstruction of the SC joint requires appreciation for the anatomic relation of vital mediastinal structures—brachiocephalic vessels, subclavian vessels, trachea, and esophagus—as most techniques require dissection posterior to the manubrium to allow for safe drilling and graft passage. A variety of reconstruction techniques have been described including allograft or autograft figure‐of‐8 reconstruction, intramedullary graft reconstruction, synthetic suture‐based reconstruction, and tenodesis of the sternocleidomastoid or subclavius. Overall, considerable variability in techniques and outcomes exists in the current literature. Although figure‐of‐8 graft reconstruction has been considered the historical gold standard for chronic SC joint instability, it is technically demanding and carries an increased risk for mediastinal structures because of bicortical drilling. In modern practice, sternal docking reconstruction has gained popularity using short, unicortical sockets with advantages including independent tensioning of graft limbs, more controlled tunnel depth, less risk of tunnel convergence, and lower risk of iatrogenic fracture and injury to mediastinal structures. The aim of this technical note is to illustrate the treatment of chronic anterior SC joint instability (Figure 1) with the sternal docking technique using a semitendinosus allograft through a single, robust intramedullary medial clavicle tunnel and 2 unicortical lateral sternal sockets. This technique also highlights a modified girth hitch of the medial clavicle and a single intramedullary tunnel for robust medial clavicle fixation (Video 1).

This technical note presents sternoclavicular (SC) joint reconstruction for chronic anterior instability using an updated sternal docking technique with a modified girth hitch. This case was performed at The Steadman Clinic in Vail, CO. This is the case of a 62‐year‐old right‐hand‐dominant woman with persistent anterior and superior subluxation of the medial clavicle after failed conservative therapy. After full‐thickness skin flaps, the anterior capsule of the SC joint is identified and excised. In the setting of arthrosis, the anterior and inferior aspect of the medial clavicle is contoured with a sagittal saw, rongeur, and rasp. With attention on the medial clavicle, a 2.4‐mm guide pin is placed unicortically in the anterior‐to‐posterior direction centered within the clavicle, 25 mm lateral to the SC joint. This is subsequently over‐reamed 6 mm. A second guide pin is then drilled and over‐reamed 6 mm through the center of the intramedullary canal of the medial clavicle. A nitinol wire is then used to pass a shuttle suture through the intramedullary tunnel of the medial clavicle. A second shuttle suture is placed around the posterior aspect of the clavicle lateral to the unicortical drill hole to facilitate later graft passage. Attention is then turned to the lateral sternum, where two 2.4‐mm lateral‐to‐medial guide pins are placed through the cortical bone of the sternum, spaced approximately 15 mm apart in the superior‐to‐inferior position. Care is taken to make sure that the drilling trajectory is flat and in line with the sternum. Both guide pins are then over‐reamed on oscillation with a 4.5‐mm reamer to a depth of approximately 25 mm. Next, two 2.4‐mm unicortical guide pins are placed in the sternum approximately 15 mm medial to the SC joint, in line with the previously drilled sternal sockets. The guide pins are turned so that the blunt end of the pin is in the unicortical hole to prevent accidental bicortical penetration of the sternum during over‐reaming. Two shuttle sutures are passed using a nitinol wire. The semitendinosus allograft is brought onto the field. The allograft is passed around the posterior clavicle, just lateral to the unicortical drill hole in the medial clavicle. Both allograft limbs are brought to equal length before each end is individually shuttled through the medial clavicle intramedullary tunnel toward the SC joint. An equal graft length is again confirmed. Attention is then turned toward sternal docking. The first graft limb is passed through the more superior sternal socket, followed by the second graft limb being passed. The graft limbs are tensioned and fixed independently with a biotenodesis screw in both the superior and inferior sternal sockets. The remaining graft limbs are crossed anteriorly and laterally over the SC joint and incorporated into the capsular repair using interrupted figure‐of‐8 suture fixation with buried knots. The final construct is assessed through a full shoulder arc of motion to confirm maintenance of reduction of the medial clavicle. Immediate postoperative plain films show a well‐maintained reduction of the right SC joint. The postoperative rehabilitation protocol consists of a padded abduction sling for 4 weeks with immediate and gentle active‐assisted range of motion with no passive range of motion until 4 weeks postoperatively. The patient is typically able to return to full, unrestricted activity by 4 to 5 months postoperatively. Video content can be viewed at https://doi.org/10.1002/atn2.70296.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Anatomic Exposure

In the beach‐chair position, a longitudinal incision is made centered over the medial clavicle and lateral manubrium. Full‐thickness skin flaps are developed, and subperiosteal elevation of the medial clavicle is performed to expose the anterior, superior, and inferior aspects. The anterior capsule of the SC joint is identified, which often shows signs of degenerative wear and hypertrophy, and is subsequently excised (Figure 2A). The medial clavicle is then examined—subluxated anteriorly and superiorly. Care is taken to preserve the posterior capsule of the SC joint to protect the deep mediastinal neurovascular structures. Posterior dissection is minimized and kept directly on the bone when required.

(MC, medial clavicle; SC, sternoclavicular.)

In the setting of arthrosis, the anterior aspect of the medial clavicle is contoured with a sagittal saw and rongeur to resect osteophytes (Figure 2B). A limited resection of up to 3 to 5 mm of the medial clavicle can be performed without threatening stability (Figure 2C). Dissection is extended laterally on the clavicle to allow placement of the bone tunnel approximately 25 mm from the lateral border of the SC joint. Subperiosteal dissection is further continued to expose the lateral border of the sternum approximately 15 mm from the medial border of the SC joint. Care should be taken to reflect the pectoralis and deltoid fascia to allow for complete exposure. Additionally, it is imperative to create a flat bony surface for tunnel preparation on the sternum void of the overlying soft tissue.

On the back table, a semitendinosus allograft is prepared. Both ends are whipstitched and tubularized with the final diameter of the graft bulletized to 4.5 mm and a preferred length of ≥275 mm.

Medial Clavicle Intramedullary Tunnel Creation

With attention on the medial clavicle, a 2.4‐mm unicortical guide pin is placed in the anterior‐to‐posterior direction centered on the clavicle approximately 25 mm lateral to the SC joint, which is unicortically over‐reamed with a 6‐mm reamer (Figure 3A). A second 2.4‐mm guide pin is then drilled through the intramedullary canal from the center of the SC joint in a lateral direction until it connects with the previously created drill hole (Figure 3B). This guide pin is then also over‐reamed by 6 mm, creating a continuous intramedullary tunnel. A nitinol wire is used to pass a shuttle suture through the intramedullary tunnel, leaving the passing loop exiting the anterior drill hole. A separate shuttle suture is placed around the posterior aspect of the clavicle lateral to the unicortical drill hole (Figure 3C).

MC drilling. Intraoperative images of a right SC joint in the beach‐chair position showing (A) a 6‐mm unicortical socket in the MC, 25 mm lateral to the SC joint, and (B) an intramedullary 2.4‐mm guide pin, before over‐reaming to 6 mm, to create an intramedullary canal within the MC, followed by (C) passage of shuttle sutures—(1) through the MC intramedullary canal (loop stays lateral) and (2) posterior to the clavicle lateral to the unicortical drill hole (loop stays inferior). *Modified girth hitch. (MC, medial clavicle; SC, sternoclavicular.)

Lateral Sternal Unicortical Socket Creation

Two 2.4‐mm lateral‐to‐medial guide pins are placed in the sternum from the SC joint through the cortical bone of the sternum, spaced 15 mm apart in the superior‐to‐inferior position (Figure 4A). Both guide pins are over‐reamed on oscillation with a 4.5‐mm reamer to a depth of approximately 25 mm, creating a superior and inferior bone socket (Figure 4B). Next, two 2.4‐mm unicortical guide pins are placed in the sternum approximately 15 mm medial to the SC joint, in line with the previously drilled sternal sockets (Figure 4C). These guide pins are oriented slightly medial on drilling in an anterior‐to‐posterior fashion. The guide pins are turned so that the blunt end of the pin is in the unicortical hole to prevent accidental bicortical penetration of the sternum during over‐reaming. Both guide pins are then over‐reamed with a 4.5‐mm acorn reamer, followed by the passing of 2 shuttle sutures using a nitinol wire with the loops exiting laterally through the sternal sockets into the SC joint (Figure 4D).

(C) A unicortical 2.4‐mm guide pin is drilled anterior to posterior in line with the prior sternal sockets, 15 mm from the medial SC joint line, 1 superior and 1 inferior, (D) prior to over‐reaming with a 4.5‐mm reamer and passage of shuttle sutures. (LS, lateral sternum; SC, sternoclavicular.)

Allograft Passage and Sternal Docking Fixation

As all shuttle sutures have been placed for ease of graft passage (Figure 5A), the semitendinosus allograft is introduced to the surgical field (Figure 5B) and passed around the posterior clavicle (Figure 6A). Both allograft limbs are brought to equal length before each end is shuttled through the medial clavicle intramedullary tunnel toward the SC joint. The allograft limb with the less‐bulletized graft end is pulled through the tunnel first (Figure 6B), followed by the second limb to ease graft passage. An equal graft length is again confirmed (Figure 6C). A modified girth hitch configuration uses a simple knot that forms a loop around a cylindrical object (i.e., medial clavicle) which tightens under tension when loaded (Figure 7). The modified girth hitch allows the use of a single intramedullary tunnel for robust medial clavicle fixation.

Attention is then turned to sternal docking. The first graft limb is passed through the more superior sternal socket from within the SC joint (Figure 6D), through the cortical socket within the lateral sternum, and brought out anteriorly within the superior sternal socket. The second graft limb is passed in the same fashion through the more inferior sternal socket (Figure 6E). The graft limbs are then tensioned and fixed independently (Figure 6F). Although posterior and inferior manual reduction is maintained on the medial clavicle, each limb is fixed with a 5.5 mm × 8 mm PEEK biotenodesis screw (Arthrex, Naples, FL) in both the superior and inferior sternal sockets (Figure 8A).

Final allograft configuration. Intraoperative images of a right SC joint in the beach‐chair position showing final configuration of the semitendinosus allograft fixation using the sternal docking technique (A) after individual allograft limb tensioning and fixation with a biotenodesis screw in the superior and inferior sternal socket, (B) followed by incorporation of the remaining allograft limbs to each other and into the capsular repair with figure‐of‐8 sutures. (MC, medial clavicle; LS, lateral sternum; SC, sternoclavicular.)

The remaining graft limbs are crossed anteriorly and laterally over the SC joint and incorporated into the anterior capsular repair (Figure 8B). The excess graft limbs are fixed to one another and to any residual anterior SC joint capsule with high‐strength suture in an interrupted figure‐of‐8 fashion with buried knots. To reinforce the capsular repair, knotless 1.8‐mm FiberTak all‐suture anchors double loaded with SutureTape on needles (Arthrex, Naples, FL) can be anchored to the medial clavicle and used to oversee the excess graft, providing robust anterior reinforcement. The final construct is assessed through full shoulder arc of motion to confirm maintenance of reduction of the medial clavicle (Figure 9).

DISCUSSION

Traditional SC joint reconstruction with figure‐of‐8 graft passage or transosseous tunnels has been described with excellent stability and good clinical outcomes but carries known higher risks of technical complexity, mediastinal injury, and difficulty in revision settings because of the potential for tunnel convergence and/or posterior cortical fracture. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Although outcomes are mostly limited to small patient series, Millet et al. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 reported significant improvement in shoulder function and a 100% return‐to‐sport rate after SC joint reconstruction using the figure‐of‐8 technique without any major mediastinal injury but cautioned that posterior tunnel placement requires extreme caution, advocating for unicortical drilling when possible.

Modern‐day reconstructions are gravitating toward sternal docking techniques for the treatment of persistent anterior instability because of a safer risk profile, ability to independently and incrementally tension graft limbs, and the capacity to incorporate hybrid fixation. There are, however, limitations of this technique to consider in select patient cases such as the potential for less rotational stability with fixation dependent on quality bone stock and lack of robust long‐term outcome data. Despite the inherent risks, clinical outcomes after SC joint reconstruction are generally favorable. Regardless of the technique used, contemporary understanding of graft biomechanics reinforces the importance of anatomic tunnel placement, adequate graft tensioning, and augmentation of the anterior capsule to further resist anterior translation, particularly in chronic or multidirectional instability patterns. 1 This technical note outlines key pearls and pitfalls to enhance safety and reproducibility in the surgical management of chronic anterior SC joint instability through the sternal docking technique, as reviewed in Table 2.
