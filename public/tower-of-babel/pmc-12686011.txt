Context-Aware Deep Learning based Indian Footpath Damage Segmentation Dataset for Risk Assessment
Authors: Priti Chakurkar, Deepali Vora
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDamaged footpaths, including cracks and surface irregularities, pose significant risks to pedestrians in urban environments. To facilitate research in automated infrastructure monitoring and pedestrian fall-risk mitigation, we introduce the Indian Footpath Damage Segmentation Dataset, a high-resolution image collection captured using a professional SLR camera under diverse lighting and weather conditions in Pune, India. Each image has been manually annotated with pixel-level segmentation masks using the LabelMe tool, targeting a single class: footpath damage. Annotations include severity levels (low, medium, high) based on crack width, defined in consultation with civil engineering experts. To demonstrate the dataset’s utility, we trained a U-Net model with an EfficientNet-B3 encoder and attention-based decoder, and further experimented with ResNeXt50_32x4d encoders and ensemble methods incorporating a convolutional fusion head. Our best-performing ensemble model achieved a Dice score of 0.6899, Intersection over Union (IoU) of 0.6741, accuracy of 0.9317, precision of 0.7154, recall of 0.6932, and F1-score of 0.6899. The dataset is openly accessible for academic and research use on Zenodo.Subject terms: Environmental sciences, Risk factors

Background & Summary

Pedestrian safety is an essential component of urban infrastructure planning, but damaged footpaths, such as cracks, uneven surfaces, and surface wear, are one of the leading causes of trip and fall accidents, particularly among older adults and people with disabilities. The prior studies have shown that environmental conditions such as rain, low surface friction, and insufficient lighting also contribute significantly to pedestrian injuries1. However, most analyses have relied on subjective surveys or limited field inspections, which provide only partial insights into the risks associated with degraded pedestrian infrastructure. The Recent advances in computer vision and deep learning have enabled the automated detection of surface damage on roads and pavements. There are several datasets have supported this progress, including RDD20182 in Japan, CRACK500 in China3, and multi-country datasets such as RDD20204, GAPs in Germany5, and RDD20226. These collections advanced the field by moving from bounding box annotations to fine-grained segmentation with severity labels. However, these datasets primarily focus on roadways rather than pedestrian footpaths. Their geographic coverage is also biased toward vehicular roads, limiting their applicability to pedestrian safety studies. Also, there is an important challenge i.e., the measurement of crack severity. In road engineering, severity levels such as mild, moderate, or severe are defined by width and length, which help maintenance teams decide repair priorities7,8. In pedestrian contexts, the meaning of severity is broader because the impact is directly linked to safety. A narrow but deep crack may cause an elderly pedestrian to trip, whereas wide cracks may create barriers for wheelchair users. The currently existing public datasets rarely provide consistent severity labels that can be used to assess pedestrian fall risks. This absence of footpath-specific severity data is a major gap that prevents the development of models able to segment damage and classify its severity for safety analysis. Moreover, the dataset should also support integrating geographical and environmental factors such as weather or illumination conditions that affect pedestrian fall risk9,10.

The analysis shows that damage to footpaths accounts for the largest proportion of incidents with 35% alone which highlights that there is a direct impact of surface irregularities on pedestrian safety. The other factors include weather-related effects (20%) such as rainfall, which reduce surface friction, are the second most important factor. The poor lighting (15%), surface friction (10%), and obstructions or debris (15%) also contribute significantly, emphasizing that fall risk is the result of both structural and environmental conditions11–13. These findings show that sidewalk condition is one of the most critical determinants of pedestrian safety. Despite this, there is no publicly available dataset dedicated exclusively to footpath damage segmentation with severity classification. The existing studies either address isolated issues such as surface friction or weather, or they lack standardized annotations required for reproducible model development14,15.

Another limitation in prior work is the inconsistencies in the data collection procedure as many images are captured with varying proximity or framing, making it difficult to apply models consistently. Additionally, most datasets were developed for specific regions, and their findings cannot be easily generalized to other cities with different infrastructure characteristics. The manual or survey-based labeling introduces subjectivity, which further reduces the reliability of the datasets. However, there few existing resources that are designed to support predictive modeling for pedestrian fall risk, and left a scope or opportunities in applying modern machine vision for safety-focused infrastructure monitoring.

This study introduces the Indian Footpath Damage Segmentation Dataset, the first dataset designed to support pedestrian safety risk assessment through machine vision. The dataset consists of a high-resolution image of footpaths, which were collected from eight high-traffic pedestrian corridors in Pune, India, using a professional SLR camera under diverse lighting and weather conditions. The images were manually annotated with pixel-level segmentation masks using the LabelMe tool, with severity labels (low, medium, high) defined in consultation with civil engineers. The core contributions of the work are four-fold, Viz. i) it provides a high-quality, open-access dataset with precise pixel-level annotations of footpath damage; ii) it standardizes severity levels based on civil engineering field standards, and ensuring relevance for both research and practical maintenance; iii) it supports reproducibility through a structured dataset split (training, validation, testing) and detailed metadata describing collection conditions; and iv) it reports baseline results obtained using custom deep learning models along with state-of-the-art architectures (modified U-Net with EfficientNet-B3 encoder, a ResNeXt50-based encoder, and ensemble architectures), thereby demonstrating the dataset’s utility for segmentation and severity assessment tasks. By addressing the limitations of existing datasets, this resource serves as both a reference benchmark and a foundation for future work. The researchers are encouraged to extend the experiments, explore improved models, and enhance performance, thereby advancing automated pedestrian safety monitoring, predictive analytics for fall prevention, and data-driven urban infrastructure planning.

Methods

The prime aim of this work is the development of the Indian Footpath Damage Segmentation Dataset for advancing research in pedestrian safety and infrastructure risk assessment. The study followed an analytical research methodology that emphasizes reproducibility, reliability, and practical relevance. This section describes the complete process of dataset creation, beginning with systematic image collection and pre-processing, followed by annotation and expert validation, and concluding with baseline deep learning experiments. Each stage was carefully designed to produce a dataset that can serve as a benchmark resource for machine vision studies on footpath damage detection, severity classification, and predictive safety modeling.

Figure 3 presents a schematic overview of the system. The first module of the system is subjected to defining dataset requirements, followed by the systematic collection of high-resolution footpath images using a professional SLR camera. The raw images are pre-processed to standardize quality before performing data annotation and pixel-level segmentation masks and severity label assignment. The annotation is carried out in semi-automated manner under supervision of civil engineering experts, afterwards the dataset is organized into structured training, validation, and testing splits. Finally, the dataset is used to train custom deep learning models, and performance is evaluated to provide baseline benchmarks for segmentation and severity classification.

Data collection

In this work, dataset development process adopts careful consideration of image collection process16. Therefore, the Indian Footpath Damage Segmentation Dataset was developed using a systematic and standardized acquisition protocol to ensure reproducibility, geographic clarity, and consistency in framing. Unlike most existing datasets, which primarily target road pavements, this dataset is dedicated exclusively to pedestrian footpaths towards supporting pedestrian safety analysis. The images were collected from eight high-traffic pedestrian corridors in Pune, India, chosen in consultation with municipal officials to ensure coverage of locations with high pedestrian density and visible deterioration. The sites span commercial, residential, and mixed-use zones, representing diverse footpath materials and maintenance conditions. To capture structural damage in detail, we used a high-resolution Sony Alpha ILCE-7M3 camera (mirrorless, full-frame). Images were acquired at a native 6000 × 4000 pixel resolution (JPEG, 24-bit, sRGB), which were subsequently resized to 1500 × 1000 pixels for dataset release on Zenodo. This approach allows future models to take into account not only structural cracks, but also environmental influences such as slippage and reduced visibility. A total of 1,032 raw images were collected. After screening each image, 449 images were filtered based on quality factors such as resolution sharpness, framing uniformity, and minimal occlusion, leaving 583 images for further preprocessing and annotation. Data Pre-processing

Data pre-processing was conducted in multiple stages to prepare the dataset for annotation, segmentation training, and severity analysis. Raw images, captured at a native resolution of 6000 × 4000 pixels, were first resized to 1500 × 1000 pixels to balance file size with preservation of structural detail. This intermediate resolution was used for manual annotation in the LabelMe tool, and both annotated masks and corresponding resized images were preserved in the dataset directory for release on Zenodo. For segmentation model training and baseline performance analysis, the annotated images were further resized to 128 × 128 pixels, normalized to the range [0,1] using min–max scaling, and enhanced through noise reduction and contrast adjustments. Gaussian blurring was applied to suppress sensor noise, median filtering was used to preserve edge integrity, and bilateral filtering was selectively applied where textural details were critical. In addition, global histogram equalization and contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) were used to improve the visibility of faint cracks under low-light or shadowed conditions17,18. For severity classification experiments, images were resized to 512 × 512 pixels to retain fine-grained detail necessary for distinguishing severity levels (low, medium, high), with normalization and contrast enhancement applied consistently. This multi-level pre-processing pipeline ensured both consistency across the dataset and suitability of inputs for different modeling objectives19. Annotation process

In this work, the data annotation process was carried out in two complementary sub-tasks to ensure the dataset can support both automated segmentation and severity classification as shown in Fig. 4. The first sub-task focused on producing precise binary masks of damaged regions, while the second introduced severity labels to capture the relative seriousness of each annotated defect. Together, these two annotation layers provide a balanced foundation for computer vision research and safety-oriented analysis.

Schematic representation of the data annotation being adopted.

Binary segmentation annotation

For segmentation purposes, each image (1500 × 1000 pixels) was manually annotated using the LabelMe tool20, which enables polygon-based labeling and generates corresponding binary mask as ground truth. Annotators examined each image carefully and enclosed visible damaged regions with polygons. These annotations were stored in JSON format containing polygon coordinates and associated labels. The JSON files were subsequently converted into binary segmentation masks (PNG format), where damaged pixels were assigned a value of “1” and background pixels a value of “0.” This ensured that every image in the dataset had a paired binary mask, making the dataset directly usable for semantic segmentation tasks. The quality control of obtained binary mask was performed through a two-step verification process: first by the annotators themselves, and then by civil engineering experts who validated the geometric correctness of polygons and the completeness of annotations.

Severity annotation

To complement binary segmentation masks, the dataset includes severity labels (Low, Medium, and High) for each annotated crack region. The severity classification was performed using a systematic process designed to capture the maximum crack width (Wmax) with high accuracy, since maximum width is a critical indicator of pedestrian safety risk. The binary masks generated during annotation were first refined to ensure continuity and remove artifacts. Closing operations filled small gaps and breaks, while opening operations eliminated isolated noise points. This step produced a clean binary crack representation. The refined binary images were subjected to connected component analysis, where each distinct crack region was identified and labeled as an independent component. This allowed region-specific measurements rather than treating the entire image as a single structure. However, the complex crack regions often contain intersections or branching points, which can lead to biased width estimates. To address this, each primary crack region was further skeletonized using thinning operations, and intersection points along the skeleton were detected. The crack was then split into smaller, continuous segments between intersections, ensuring width analysis was carried out on structurally consistent sub-sections. For each crack segment, a distance transform was applied. At each skeleton pixel, the distance to the nearest crack boundary was calculated, and the local width was estimated as twice this value. By scanning all skeleton pixels across all segments, the maximum width (Wmax) was extracted as the representative severity feature. Based on thresholds derived in consultation with civil engineering experts and validated against field inspection practices, the following severity categories were defined:

Low severity: Wmax < 5 pixels (hairline cracks, low immediate hazard)

Low severity: Wmax < 5 pixels (hairline cracks, low immediate hazard)

Medium severity: 5 ≤ Wmax < 10 pixels (moderate cracks requiring monitoring/maintenance).

The severity labels were stored in a CSV metadata file, linked to each image and its segmentation mask. This ensured interoperability between the binary damage annotations and severity classification task. All severity assignments were reviewed by civil engineering experts to confirm the validity of thresholds and minimize subjectivity in classification.

Unlike the conventional U-Net, this framework employs dual encoders and an attention-guided fusion mechanism to improve feature representation for footpath damage detection21. The design specifically addresses two key challenges: i) Capturing multi-scale representations and ii) Preserving contextual cues. Severity prediction model

The severity prediction task in this study is built upon the EfficientNet-B3 architecture, which was selected for its strong balance between computational efficiency and predictive accuracy. EfficientNet-B0 is well-suited to capture fine-grained structural patterns in relatively small-scale datasets, making it particularly effective for crack and footpath damage analysis. To further enhance performance and training stability, the model adopts a transfer learning approach, where pretrained ImageNet weights are used to initialize the backbone. This strategy accelerates convergence while reducing the risk of overfitting, given the limited size of the dataset. The feature extraction backbone consists of the pretrained EfficientNet-B3 convolutional layers, which generate multi-scale hierarchical feature maps from the input images. To transform these high-dimensional feature maps into a compact representation, a Global Average Pooling (GAP) layer is applied, reducing the spatial dimensions and producing a condensed feature vector that preserves the global contextual information. In order to improve the model’s generalization ability, a Dropout layer with a probability between 0.3 and 0.5 is introduced after GAP. This layer randomly deactivates neurons during training, which mitigates overfitting and ensures that the network does not rely excessively on specific feature activations. Following this, a fully connected dense layer is employed to map the pooled feature vector into three distinct outputs, each corresponding to one of the severity categories (low, medium, and high). Finally, the network produces its predictions through a Softmax output layer, which converts the dense layer outputs into a probability distribution across the three severity classes. This allows the model to assign each input instance to the most likely severity level while retaining interpretability through class probabilities.

Technical Validation

The U-Net–based dual encoder ensemble was trained for 50 epochs to establish robust baseline results on the dataset. Training was conducted using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with Nesterov momentum, which provided stable updates and helped accelerate convergence compared to standard SGD. To dynamically adjust the learning rate during training, a OneCycleLR scheduler was employed, enabling rapid exploration of the parameter space in the initial phases and fine-tuning in later epochs. In order to improve training efficiency and reduce GPU memory consumption, mixed-precision training was adopted, allowing the model to compute certain operations in half precision without compromising accuracy. A combination of Dice Loss and Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) was used as the hybrid objective function. This design ensured that the model remained robust to class imbalance common in crack segmentation tasks, while maintaining strong pixel-level discrimination. The model performance was evaluated using six widely adopted metrics as shown in Fig. 6: Dice Score, Intersection over Union (IoU), Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-Score, thereby providing a holistic assessment of segmentation quality. To prevent overfitting, early stopping was applied based on validation Dice score stagnation, and the best-performing model checkpoint was saved for subsequent testing. This strategy allowed the segmentation model to capture both fine-grained damage boundaries and broader contextual cues while ensuring reproducibility. The dual-encoder attention-guided U-Net model was evaluated on both the Indian Footpath Damage Segmentation Dataset (our dataset) and the widely used CRACK500 benchmark dataset to assess its generalizability and robustness (See Table 6). On the Indian dataset, the model achieved a Dice Score of 0.6819 and an IoU of 0.6712, demonstrating strong pixel-level segmentation performance under diverse environmental and structural conditions. The relatively high accuracy (92.77%) reflects the model’s ability to discriminate between damaged and intact footpath regions, while the balanced precision (71.16%) and recall (69.78%) indicate stable performance across varying crack severities and sizes. On CRACK500, the model achieved superior results, with a Dice Score of 0.7588, IoU of 74.93, and accuracy of 97.49%, highlighting improved robustness when trained and evaluated on a large-scale standardized crack dataset. The higher recall (78.11%) compared to the Indian dataset suggests that the model is more sensitive in detecting fine cracks in benchmark data, whereas the slightly lower precision trade-off reflects an increased tendency to label ambiguous regions as cracks. The cross-dataset evaluation confirms that the model not only generalizes well to benchmark datasets but also adapts effectively to real-world pedestrian infrastructure conditions, which are often more heterogeneous and challenging.

Attention mechanisms were then evaluated independently. A baseline U-Net with attention showed modest gains (Dice 0.6281), indicating that attention alone is insufficient without strong feature extraction. However, coupling attention with advanced encoders led to more substantial improvements. The EfficientNet + Attention model achieved a Dice score of 0.6572, while the ResNeXt-50 + Attention variant achieved 0.6494, demonstrating consistent gains in both cases. Finally, the full model, which integrates dual encoders with attention-guided fusion, achieved the best results across all metrics (Dice 0.6819, IoU 0.6712, Accuracy 92.77%). This configuration not only improved pixel-level segmentation but also enhanced robustness by emphasizing damage-relevant features while suppressing background noise.

The severity prediction model, based on EfficientNet-B3 with transfer learning, was trained for 30 epochs with a batch size of 32 and an initial learning rate of 0.001, optimized using the Adam optimizer. Pretrained ImageNet weights were used to initialize the backbone, significantly improving convergence speed and reducing the risk of overfitting, especially given the limited dataset size. During training, the convolutional backbone extracted deep hierarchical features from each image, which were pooled via a Global Average Pooling (GAP) layer. To enhance generalization, Dropout regularization (rate = 0.3–0.5) was applied after pooling. The dense output layer then mapped the feature vector into three nodes corresponding to severity classes (low, medium, high). The final Softmax layer produced normalized probability distributions for class assignment. The model was trained using categorical cross-entropy loss, which is well-suited for multi-class classification tasks, and evaluated using Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-score. To further monitor learning dynamics, training and validation loss/accuracy curves were analyzed across epochs. This not only provided insights into overfitting trends but also demonstrated the effectiveness of transfer learning for small but well-curated datasets. Conversely, the medium severity class shows perfect precision (1.00) but a slightly reduced recall (0.90), indicating some medium cracks were misclassified. The macro-average F1-score of 0.96 and weighted-average F1-score of 0.95 confirm the model’s balanced performance across classes despite differences in class distribution. Usage and scope

The Indian Footpath Damage Segmentation Dataset is intended to serve as a benchmark resource for advancing research in automated infrastructure monitoring and pedestrian safety. By providing high-resolution images with pixel-level annotations and severity labels, the dataset enables the training, validation, and evaluation of deep learning models for both segmentation and severity classification tasks. From a research perspective, the dataset facilitates benchmarking of existing and novel semantic segmentation architectures. Researchers can employ it to explore improvements in encoder–decoder networks, attention mechanisms, ensemble approaches, and transformer-based architectures. The inclusion of severity labels extends its applicability beyond binary segmentation, enabling multi-class classification and severity-aware modeling. The baseline results presented in this study establish a reference point for future algorithmic improvements, providing an initial performance benchmark against which subsequent work can be compared. From a practical standpoint, the dataset offers significant potential in supporting data-driven decision-making for urban infrastructure management. Severity-aware segmentation outputs provide actionable insights for maintenance teams, allowing them to prioritize repairs based on crack intensity and potential pedestrian safety risks. This makes the dataset directly relevant to smart-city monitoring platforms, decision-support systems, and predictive maintenance frameworks. By integrating severity prediction with geospatial metadata, the dataset can further support urban planners and policymakers in developing strategies for safe, accessible, and sustainable pedestrian environments. Finally, as an open-access resource hosted on Zenodo, the dataset encourages reproducibility, collaboration, and extensibility. Researchers are invited to expand the dataset to diverse geographic contexts, incorporate multi-sensor modalities, and explore advanced learning paradigms such as self-supervised or domain-adaptive approaches. The resource is thus designed not only as a dataset but also as a foundation for future innovation in pedestrian safety and infrastructure monitoring.

Limitations

Although the Indian Footpath Damage Segmentation Dataset provides high-quality annotations and serves as a valuable resource for advancing pedestrian safety research, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, the dataset is geographically restricted to Pune, India, and therefore may not fully capture the variability of pedestrian infrastructure across different cities, climates, or construction practices. Extending the dataset to include diverse geographic and cultural contexts would improve its generalizability. Second, the images were captured using 2D imaging techniques with handheld cameras. As a result, the dataset does not incorporate depth information or three-dimensional (3D) data, which could provide richer structural insights into crack depth and surface unevenness. The absence of 3D data may limit the accuracy of severity estimation in certain scenarios, particularly where surface depth plays a critical role in pedestrian fall risk. Finally, the dataset focuses exclusively on visible footpath damage (cracks, spalling, and irregularities) and does not account for other safety-related factors such as pedestrian density, material friction coefficients, or contextual hazards (e.g., obstructions or lighting conditions). Incorporating these broader environmental factors would enable a more comprehensive understanding of pedestrian fall risks. These limitations highlight opportunities for future work, including the expansion of data collection across multiple cities, the integration of multi-sensor modalities (e.g., LiDAR or stereo vision), and the inclusion of contextual metadata to support more robust safety assessment and predictive analytics.

Recommendations for future work

There are several directions for future research. First, expanding data collection to multiple cities and diverse geographic contexts would enhance generalizability and allow comparative studies across different infrastructure types. Second, integrating multi-modal data, such as LiDAR, IMU, or weather sensors, could provide richer context for severity estimation and fall-risk analysis. Third, exploring advanced deep learning architectures, including transformer-based segmentation models and self-supervised pretraining, may yield improved accuracy and robustness over the baseline methods reported in this study. Fourth, coupling visual severity predictions with geospatial data would support the development of decision-support systems for city planners and municipal authorities. Finally, the dataset may serve as a foundation for predictive modeling frameworks that anticipate infrastructure deterioration and prioritize preventive maintenance strategies, thereby contributing to safer and more sustainable pedestrian environments.
