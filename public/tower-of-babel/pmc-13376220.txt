A robust stacked ensemble strategy with multi-optimizer CNN models for skin cancer classification
Authors: Arvind Panwar, Jyoti Agarwal, Shruti Vashist, Yogita Arora, Achin Jain, Ondrej Krejcar, Robert Frischer, Hamidreza Namazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSkin cancer is one of the most prevalent and potentially life-threatening cancers globally, making it a critical area of focus in medical research. Early detection, followed by timely and appropriate treatment, can significantly enhance patient survival outcomes. In recent years, computer-aided diagnostic systems have gained substantial attention for their ability to support accurate and efficient skin cancer diagnosis. This study presents an optimized stacked ensemble strategy to improve skin lesion classification performance. Experiments were conducted on the Melanoma Skin Cancer Dataset using four different convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures, each trained with different optimizers, including Adam, Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), Root Mean Square Propagation (RMSprop), and AdaMax (Adamax). All models were trained using categorical cross-entropy loss and evaluated based on validation accuracy, with hyperparameters such as learning rate, batch size, and number of epochs kept constant. More than 90 model combinations were tested using four ensemble strategies: soft and hard voting, weighted voting (using grid search), and stacked ensemble using various meta-learners (Gradient Boosting (GB), k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Logistic Regression (LR), and Naïve Bayes (NB)). The models and ensembles were assessed using standard performance metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC-AUC). Among the evaluated configurations, the stacked ensemble model with Gradient Boosting (GB) as the meta-classifier and M1+M2+M3+M4 as the deep learning base models achieved a validation accuracy of 97.4% and a robust independent test accuracy of 91.80% with a ROC-AUC of 96.87%. The findings demonstrate that ensemble learning, particularly stacked ensembling, significantly enhances classification accuracy and strengthens the reliability of melanoma detection on unseen datasets.Keywords: Skin cancer, CNN, Ensemble learning, OptimizerSubject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Skin cancer is among the most common and rapidly increasing forms of cancer worldwide, with millions of new cases diagnosed annually. The two main types are skin cancers of melanoma and non-melanoma. Among these, melanoma poses a significant public health concern, requiring accurate diagnostic methods for effective treatment1. Traditional diagnostic approaches, which are based on clinical expertise and dermoscopic examination, are often subjective and prone to human error. The American Cancer Society has projected a substantial increase in melanoma cases, emphasizing the urgent need for improved diagnostic tools2. With the growing availability of dermoscopic image datasets and advances in computational resources, artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning (DL), has emerged as a powerful solution for automated and accurate detection of skin cancer. Deep learning has gained great popularity in medical image analysis and has demonstrated remarkable accuracy in the classification of skin cancer3,4. In particular, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have achieved state-of-the-art results in medical imaging tasks and are now widely recognized as the most effective DL technique for skin lesion analysis. CNNs are capable of learning complex data patterns, enabling fast and accurate predictions at relatively low cost. However, single CNN architectures may suffer from overfitting and limited generalization capability. Furthermore, CNN performance is strongly influenced by optimization algorithms, which play a critical role in improving convergence and stability.

Optimization techniques are designed to identify the best possible parameters that minimize the loss function and maximize performance5. These algorithms significantly affect training speed, stability, and model accuracy. Therefore, the choice of optimizer is crucial, and exploring multiple optimizers can further enhance classification performance and reliability3. In addition, ensemble learning has recently gained traction for its ability to combine the strengths of multiple DL models while mitigating individual weaknesses6. In such approaches, weak or base learners are combined using strategies such as averaging, weighted voting, or stacking. Stacked ensembles, in particular, leverage the diversity of multiple models and use a meta-learner to aggregate predictions, thereby improving robustness and overall performance. In this study, we propose an optimized stacked ensemble deep learning framework to improve the accuracy and reliability of melanoma detection from dermoscopic images. For experimentation, we used a publicly available dataset containing 10,605 dermoscopic images labeled melanoma and non-melanoma7. More than 90 model combinations were tested using four ensemble strategies: soft and hard voting, weighted voting (using grid search), and stacked ensemble using various meta-learners (Gradient Boosting (GB), k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Logistic Regression (LR), Naïve Bayes (NB)). Models and ensembles were assessed using standard performance metrics that included precision, precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC.

Literature review

Several researchers have extensively investigated the detection and diagnosis of skin cancer. The increasing incidence of skin cancer, largely attributed to environmental factors such as air pollution and ozone depletion, has accelerated efforts to develop computer-aided systems for early diagnosis. These systems leverage machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) techniques to improve accuracy, efficiency, and reliability in the detection of skin cancer from dermoscopic images. This section reviews recent studies and approaches in this domain. A feed-forward neural network was applied to a data set of 326 dermoscopic images, achieving an accuracy of 86%8. In 2022, a CNN-based computational model was developed, obtaining 81% accuracy on a clinical dataset9. Transfer learning approaches using models such as MobileNet, Xception, and ResNet50 have also been used for skin cancer classification10. Similarly, the VGG, CapsNet and ResNet architectures were tested and demonstrated promising results11. Tembhurne12 reported a model with accuracy of 93% and recall scores of 99.7% and 86% for benign and malignant cases, respectively. Ensemble learning has been widely adopted to improve performance by integrating multiple learners. For example, an ensemble of VGG, CapsNet, and ResNet achieved superior accuracy compared to individual models13. In 2023, an IoT-enabled DL model was introduced, ODL-SCDC, which collected abnormal skin images via IoT devices and applied hyperparameter optimization, achieving a sensitivity of 97.74%, specificity of 99.7%, and precision of 99.55%14. Another ensemble of VGG16, CapsNet and ResUNet achieved 86% accuracy on the ISIC dataset, outperforming individual learners (80%, 74%, and 76%, respectively)15. Similarly, a Max Voting ensemble produced a accuracy of 93.18%16. Blending ensemble approaches were also explored for the detection of melanoma mutations, yielding 93% precision17.

An ensemble of seven deep learning models achieved 93.36% accuracy on one ISIC dataset and 85.54% on another, outperforming weighted ensembles18. A hybrid model combining Xception and MobileNetV2 reached 97.56% accuracy19. In 2024, stacking ensembles of traditional ML algorithms achieved remarkable results, with SVM (98.61%), Random Forest (97.91%), Decision Tree (95. 13%) and Gradient Boosting (95.83%), while the stacking method outperformed all with 99.3% accuracy20. Natha et al.6 proposed a Max Voting ensemble trained on two datasets, achieving 95.80% accuracy. Other researchers highlighted that existing DL approaches often focused on narrow subsets of skin diseases and were limited by small data sets. To address this, an ensemble model trained on the DermNet dataset achieved 91.73% accuracy21. Combining ResNet101 and ResNeXt101 yielded 96.12% accuracy for early-stage detection22. A hybrid CNN and Grey Wolf Optimization (GWO) model improved segmentation and edge detection, achieving 92.66% accuracy23. Two-stage ensemble approaches have also been explored. For example, a computer-aided diagnosis system fine-tuned three models in the first stage and ensembled them in the second, achieving 91.5% accuracy24. A Max Voting ensemble combining three ML models on HAM10000 and ISIC2018 datasets achieved 94.70% accuracy25. In addition, a fuzzy logic-based ensemble that integrates Xception, InceptionResNetV2 and MobileNetV2 on HAM10000 produced 95.14% accuracy26. In summary, the literature reveals that ensemble learning strategies, whether based on CNNs, hybrid DL models, or ML classifiers, consistently outperform individual learners in the detection of skin cancer. These approaches demonstrate a strong potential for robust, accurate, and reliable melanoma classification.

Methodology

This section presents the proposed methodology for developing an optimized stacked ensemble of deep learning models, where different optimizers are used to improve the performance of skin cancer detection from dermoscopic images. The approach consists of five main stages: data preprocessing and augmentation, base learner selection, optimizer tuning, stacked ensemble construction, and performance evaluation. Dataset description and preprocessing

The experiments were carried out using the HAM10000 dermoscopic image collection27 accessed through the Kaggle mirror cited in7. Although HAM10000 is originally a seven-class dataset, this study focused on a binary classification task by grouping the diagnostic categories into malignant and benign classes. Melanoma (MEL) and basal cell carcinoma (BCC) were treated as malignant, while dermatofibroma (DF), benign keratosis-like lesions (BKL), melanocytic nevi (NV), vascular lesions (VASC), and actinic keratoses and intraepithelial carcinoma / Bowen’s disease (AKIEC) were treated as benign28.The total data consists of 10,605 dermoscopic RGB images. This data is organized into two distinct folders: a training set consisting of 9,605 images (5,000 benign and 4,605 malignant) and an independent test set containing 1,000 images (500 images for each class) reserved for final evaluation. The dataset was preprocessed by resizing all images to a uniform size of pixels and normalizing pixel intensity values to the range [0, 1] using rescale=1./255. To improve model generalization and reduce overfitting, data augmentation techniques were applied during training, including random rotations of up to , width and height shifts of ±20%, shear distortion with a range of 0.2, isotropic zooming of ±20%, and horizontal flipping. All images used in both the training and independent test folders are standardized to dimensions of pixels and are in RGB color format, ensuring consistency in the input data for the models. Convolutional neural network

A Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) is an extension of the Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), designed specifically to process two-dimensional data such as images. CNNs are characterized by their deep architectures and are widely applied to image datasets. In CNNs, neurons are associated with weights, biases, and activation functions. Figure 3 illustrates the basic CNN architecture, which consists of a convolutional layer with ReLU activation, a pooling layer for feature extraction, and a fully connected layer with softmax activation for classification.

Convolutional neural network architecture.

Convolutional layer

As shown in Fig. 3, the first layer in the CNN architecture is the convolutional layer, which processes the input image. The input is convolved with filters (kernels) to extract local features, producing a feature map representation.

Activation: ReLU

The activation function used in CNNs is typically the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU), which introduces non-linearity into the network and accelerates the training process. ReLU sets all negative input values to zero while keeping positive values unchanged29.

Pooling layer

The pooling layer progressively reduces the spatial dimensions of the feature maps, thereby decreasing the number of parameters and controlling overfitting. Two common pooling methods are max pooling, which selects the maximum value, and average pooling, which computes the mean of the values within a region.

Fully connected layer

At the final stage of the CNN, the fully connected (FC) layer integrates all extracted features by connecting every neuron from the previous layers. The multidimensional feature maps are flattened into one-dimensional vectors30, which are then passed through dense layers. A softmax activation function is applied to perform multi-class classification.

Hyperparameters

All models were trained using categorical cross-entropy loss and evaluated based on validation accuracy. Hyperparameters such as learning rate, batch size, and the number of epochs were kept consistent across all models to ensure a fair comparison.

RMSProp optimizer

Root Mean Square Propagation (RMSProp), proposed by Geoffrey Hinton, is an adaptive learning rate optimization algorithm designed to train deep learning models efficiently, especially on noisy data. The key idea of RMSProp is to maintain a moving average of the squared gradients for each parameter, which acts as an estimate of gradient variance33. Unlike SGD, which uses a fixed learning rate for all parameters, RMSProp dynamically adjusts the learning rate for each parameter based on the magnitude of recent gradients.

Ensemble learning

Ensemble learning has great potential to integrate multiple deep learning models to improve prediction accuracy and dependability. Bagging, boosting, stacking and voting are four main ensemble techniques which fall under supervised ensemble learning where multiple base classifiers are selected and their outputs are combined to improve the accuracy of the prediction34. In this work, the voting and stacking ensemble techniques are chosen to integrate the output of the two main base learners.

Voting ensemble

Voting ensemble is a widely used aggregation technique in ensemble learning, where multiple base classifiers are trained independently and their predictions are combined to generate the final decision6. Other model combinations, such as M1+M2+M4 and M1+M3+M4, also showed competitive performance with Gradient Boosting as the meta classifier, achieving accuracies of 0.967 and 0.964 respectively. The overall average performance across all combinations highlights that careful selection of both base models and meta classifiers is crucial to maximize predictive performance. To ensure robust and unbiased performance evaluation of the proposed stacked ensemble, a rigorous out-of-fold (OOF) training strategy was implemented. The ensemble framework utilizes four base convolutional neural networks (CNNs) whose probability outputs serve as meta-features for a Gradient Boosting (GB) meta-classifier. During the training phase, the training dataset was partitioned into five stratified folds to maintain class distribution. For each fold, base models generated predictions on the held-out 20% validation set. This iterative process ensured that every training sample received a prediction from a model instance that had not seen it during training, producing a complete set of OOF meta-features. These features were subsequently used to train the GB meta-classifier. This methodology is critical for preventing data leakage and ensuring that the meta-learner captures the true generalization errors of the base models. The stacked ensemble achieved a mean cross-validation accuracy of 93.39% ± 0.46% with a 95% confidence interval of [93.15%, 93.62%], validated through 5-fold cross-validation repeated 3 times. Additional metrics included precision of 94.19%, recall of 91.89%, F1-score of 93.02%, and AUC of 98.25%, demonstrating consistent and stable performance across folds. The final performance was validated on an independent test set of 1,000 images. Base model predictions on this unseen test data were stacked and processed by the trained GB meta-classifier to arrive at the final diagnostic decision. The proposed stacked approach achieved a final classification accuracy of 91.80%, precision of 93.91%, recall of 89.40%, F1-score of 91.60%, and AUC of 96.87% on the independent test set, confirming its effectiveness in combining model-specific strengths to improve melanoma detection accuracy and generalization. To address the diversity of the hypothesis space, we evaluated an architecturally diverse ensemble comprising a custom CNN, DenseNet, InceptionV3, and ResNet50. This configuration achieved an accuracy of 0.963, demonstrating that combining models with varied inductive biases enhances performance compared to optimization-trajectory diversity alone.

Discussion

The results in Table 7 and Figs. 12, 13 illustrate the impact of different combinations of models and ensemble strategies on the performance of melanoma prediction. The single base model (M3) trained with the RMSProp optimizer achieved a moderate accuracy of 90.90%, indicating its individual predictive capacity. Both soft and hard voting ensembles that combined M1, M2, and M3 slightly improved performance, with soft voting that yielded 92.1% accuracy and hard voting achieving 91.7%, demonstrating the benefit of aggregating multiple models. The stacking ensemble using Gradient Boosting as meta-classifier significantly outperformed all other methods, achieving 97.4% accuracy and high precision, recall, and F1-score. This highlights the advantage of combining diverse base models with a strong meta-classifier to capture complex patterns in the dataset. Additionally, the Grid Search weighted ensemble improved the initial performance to 92.5%, showcasing how hyperparameter optimization can further fine-tune model predictions for better generalization. Figure 14 shows the AUC Score comparison of different best models of the ensemble where the proposed stacking with GB achieves the best AUC Score of 0.997. To further evaluate the robustness and generalizability of the proposed framework, the best-performing stacked ensemble model was tested on an independent external dataset, the PH dataset35, without any further training or cross-validation. The model achieved a high external validation accuracy of 91.50%, confirming its ability to generalize across different clinical cohorts and imaging sources. In general, these findings emphasize that ensemble techniques, particularly stacking with optimized weights, provide robust and highly accurate predictions in medical data sets such as the detection of skin lesions.

The substantial improvement observed with the stacked ensemble, particularly using Gradient Boosting as the meta-learner, can be attributed to its ability to model complex, non-linear relationships between the base CNN models’ predictions. Although the individual CNNs exhibit similar overall performance metrics, they tend to make different errors on specific samples due to variations in feature learning influenced by different optimizers. The meta-classifier effectively learns to weigh these diverse predictions, capturing higher-order patterns and correcting individual model weaknesses, which leads to the observed performance gain. Regarding the consistently higher number of false negatives (FN) compared to false positives (FP) across all models, including the meta-classifier, this reflects the inherent challenge in distinguishing melanoma from benign lesions that share similar visual characteristics, such as atypical nevi. The models adopt a conservative classification approach, prioritizing specificity to reduce over-diagnosis, which unfortunately results in more missed malignant cases. This trade-off highlights the difficulty of detecting clinically subtle melanomas and underscores the need for further research to improve sensitivity without compromising specificity.

To rigorously validate the performance gains of the proposed approach, statistical significance testing was conducted by comparing the stacked ensemble against the nearest-performing voting-based methods. As summarized in Table 8, paired t-tests on cross-validation folds indicate that Stacked significantly outperforms Soft Voting, Hard Voting, and Weighted Average, with p-values of 0.000684, 0.000527, and 0.003936, respectively. The corresponding Cohen’s d values are very large, reflecting strong practical effect sizes due to consistent performance differences and low variance across folds. Although the Wilcoxon signed-rank test yielded p = 0.0625, which does not meet the conventional significance threshold, all fold-wise comparisons consistently favored Stacked. This suggests a stable and robust performance improvement despite the limited sample size of cross-validation folds.

Performance analysis with extended training and early stopping

Additional experiments were conducted to evaluate model stability and convergence beyond the initial training phase. To this end, all base CNN models were retrained for a maximum of 50 epochs utilizing an early stopping mechanism based on validation loss, ensuring that each model reached an optimal state without overfitting. The SGD-based model (M2) reached convergence at epoch 17 after running for 22 total epochs, achieving a final training accuracy of 0.9264 and a test accuracy of 0.8960. The Adam (M1), RMSprop (M3), and Adamax (M4) models converged at epochs 10, 7, and 14, requiring 15, 12, and 19 total iterations respectively. This extended training protocol established more robust base learners for the subsequent ensemble phase. The evaluation of the retrained models on the held-out test set resulted in a collective performance characterized by a test loss of 0.2145, an accuracy of 0.9120, and an AUC of 0.9703. These baseline improvements directly translated to superior ensemble outcomes, as summarized in Table 12. Specifically, the stacked ensemble with Gradient Boosting as the meta-learner achieved a peak accuracy of 0.971, significantly outperforming voting-based strategies. In contrast, Soft Voting, Hard Voting, and Weighted Average obtained accuracies of 0.916, 0.914, and 0.918, respectively. Conclusion and future scope

This paper presented an optimized strategy for melanoma skin cancer detection using deep learning and ensemble learning techniques. Four CNN models were trained using different optimizers (Adam, SGD, RMSProp, and AdaMax) to introduce diversity in model behavior, which was later exploited using ensemble learning. Four ensemble strategies: Soft Voting, Hard Voting, Weighted Voting (Grid Search), and Stacked Ensemble with multiple meta-learners (GB, KNN, SVM, LR, NB) were implemented to enhance predictive performance. The experimental results revealed that ensemble strategies consistently outperformed individual CNN models. The proposed stacked ensemble achieved the highest validation accuracy of 97.4%, but when evaluated on a genuinely held-out independent test set the model yielded an accuracy of 91.80%, precision of 93.91%, recall of 89.40%, F1-score of 91.60%, and a ROC-AUC of 96.87%. While the validation result reflects the model’s learning capacity during development, the independent-test metrics represent the primary headline performance on unseen data and are used for fair comparison with prior work. These results confirm that optimizer-driven diversity combined with meta-level learning significantly enhances classification performance and generalization. Although the stacked ensemble shows strong discriminative ability on unseen data, we emphasize the independent-test performance (91.80%, AUC 96.87%) as the honest indicator of the model’s expected real-world behavior. Despite achieving high accuracy and reliability, the proposed model has scope for further improvement. Future research may focus on incorporating larger and more diverse dermoscopic datasets to enhance model scalability and robustness, and on conducting external cross-dataset validation to better quantify generalization. Additionally, integrating transfer learning refinements, domain adaptation, or self-supervised learning techniques could reduce dependency on labeled data. Real-time clinical validation and deployment in mobile or edge-based diagnostic systems could further improve practical applicability. Including explainable AI (XAI) methodologies may also improve interpretability and trustworthiness, making the system more suitable for clinical decision support.
