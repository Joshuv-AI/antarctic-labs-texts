Influence of siderite aggregate substitution on chloride resistance, durability, and gamma-ray shielding of heavy concrete
Authors: Mustafa Eken, Ayşenur Gürgen, Yusuf Kavun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the effect of replacing natural aggregates with siderite (10%, 20%, 30%, and 100%) in heavyweight concretes designed for radiation shielding. Both fine (0–4 mm) and coarse (4–16 mm) siderite aggregates were used, while the binder composition (cement + GGBFS) and water-to-binder ratio (w/b ≈ 0.40) were kept constant. Mechanical, durability, and radiation shielding properties were evaluated through compressive strength, abrasion resistance, ultrasonic pulse velocity, freeze–thaw resistance, high-temperature exposure, chloride migration, and gamma-ray attenuation tests at 1173 and 1333 keV. The results indicate that siderite incorporation generally improves compressive strength, density, and durability-related performance. These improvements may be associated with a more compact internal structure, as reflected by reduced water absorption potential (indirectly inferred from transport-related behavior), lower chloride migration, and higher ultrasonic pulse velocity values. A strong inverse relationship between compressive strength and chloride migration (R² = 0.97–0.87) confirms improved transport resistance, where GGBFS likely contributes to chemical chloride binding, while siderite mainly enhances physical aggregate packing. Gamma-ray shielding performance also improves with increasing siderite content, with coarse aggregate systems showing superior attenuation compared to fine ones. Overall, siderite-modified concretes demonstrate strong potential for durable and radiation-shielding structural applications.Keywords: Siderite, Chloride Resistance, Mechanical, Durability, Radiation shieldingSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Concrete is one of the most widely used construction materials in infrastructure and superstructure applications such as bridges, dams, tunnels, and residential buildings. Therefore, it is expected to exhibit high durability and resistance against various environmental effects. Durability is defined as the ability of a structure to withstand physical, chemical, and environmental actions throughout its service life without significant loss of performance while maintaining its functional properties1. Although concrete is generally considered a durable material, its long-term performance is strongly dependent on environmental exposure conditions and mixture design parameters2,3.

In hydraulic structures, abrasion is one of the most critical durability concerns. Abrasion refers to the progressive material loss from concrete surfaces caused by hydrodynamic actions in structures exposed to continuous or intermittent water flow4,5. Previous studies have demonstrated that fiber reinforcement, rubber modification, and optimized mix designs can improve abrasion resistance6,7. However, the contribution of high-density mineral aggregates to abrasion resistance has not been sufficiently investigated. From a mechanistic perspective, aggregate hardness, density, and interfacial bonding characteristics are considered important factors influencing surface wear resistance.

Another major durability issue is freeze–thaw damage, particularly in cold regions. Repeated freeze–thaw cycles generate internal stresses due to ice formation and hydraulic pressure, leading to microstructural deterioration8–12. Pozzolanic materials have been reported to enhance freeze–thaw resistance by refining pore structure and improving matrix performance13–17. Nevertheless, most studies focus on binder modification, whereas the role of aggregate characteristics in freeze–thaw resistance—especially in heavyweight concrete systems—remains insufficiently explored.

Concrete structures may also be exposed to elevated temperatures due to fire, industrial processes, or accidental events. High temperatures can significantly deteriorate the internal structure of concrete, resulting in reductions in both strength and durability18–21. In nuclear and industrial facilities, exposure temperatures may reach 120–350 °C or even higher22,23. Although numerous studies have examined the high-temperature behavior of concrete24–27, these investigations are largely limited to conventional aggregates. In particular, the thermal stability and decomposition-related behavior of iron-rich heavy mineral aggregates such as siderite have not been sufficiently clarified.

To reduce experimental effort and improve predictive capability, various statistical and modeling approaches have been applied in concrete research28–32. For instance, El-Mir et al.33. and Qing et al.34 demonstrated the effectiveness of regression- and entropy-based methods in evaluating durability-related performance. However, such approaches are still rarely applied to multifunctional heavyweight concretes that simultaneously address both durability and radiation shielding performance.

Recently, supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) such as fly ash and ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBFS) have been widely used to improve sustainability. However, their influence on time-dependent deformation and thermal cracking behavior remains complex. Fly ash generally exhibits autogenous shrinkage behavior similar to reference concrete, whereas GGBFS may lead to increased later-age shrinkage due to continued hydration and pore-related changes35.

In addition, SCMs are effective in reducing peak hydration temperature and thermal gradients in mass concrete systems; however, they may also influence early-age strength development and thermal cracking susceptibility. Studies have shown that cracking behavior in SCM-based concretes is strongly dependent on mix design parameters and coupled thermal–shrinkage interactions36. Despite these findings, most existing research focuses on paste or conventional concrete systems, while the interaction between SCM-modified matrices and heavyweight aggregate concretes remains insufficiently investigated.

Siderite has recently attracted attention as a promising aggregate for multifunctional concrete systems due to its high density and iron-rich mineralogical composition. Unlike conventional aggregates, siderite has the potential to contribute to mechanical performance, durability-related behavior, and radiation attenuation capacity. Nevertheless, existing studies on siderite-based concretes remain limited and are mostly restricted to isolated investigations focusing either on mechanical behavior or radiation shielding performance.

In addition to its functional advantages, the mineralogical stability of siderite is an important factor governing its performance in cementitious systems. From a mineralogical perspective, siderite (FeCO₃) is a ferrous carbonate mineral that is generally considered stable in alkaline cementitious environments. Within high-pH conditions of hydrated cement systems, siderite is expected to remain largely inert; however, limited surface-level carbonation or oxidation to iron oxide phases may occur under prolonged exposure to moisture and carbon dioxide. These transformations are typically slow and mostly confined to the surface region and are not expected to significantly affect aggregate integrity within conventional durability testing periods. In addition, the dense crystalline structure and low solubility of siderite contribute to its suitability as a heavyweight aggregate.

For example, Mokhles K. Azer et al.37 reported that naturally dense rocks can improve both mechanical properties and gamma-ray attenuation performance; however, durability aspects such as abrasion resistance, freeze–thaw behavior, and high-temperature resistance were not considered. Similarly, recent studies on supplementary cementitious materials such as rice husk ash (RHA) and sugarcane bagasse ash (SCBA) have demonstrated improvements in mechanical performance and matrix quality38. Thcccccc1c1c1cc ese effects may become more relevant when combined with heavy aggregates, where both matrix characteristics and aggregate mineralogy influence overall composite behavior.

Moreover, heavyweight concretes incorporating aggregates such as magnetite, barite, and steel slag have been widely investigated for radiation shielding applications39,40. However, most studies evaluate either mechanical–durability performance or radiation shielding performance separately, rather than integrating both aspects within a unified experimental framework. For instance, Kalauni et al.40 emphasized the importance of multi-criteria optimization in heavyweight self-compacting concrete systems, while Binici41 and Ouda42,43 eported that heavy aggregates improve both mechanical strength and radiation attenuation. Palou et al.44 further demonstrated that aggregate composition significantly influences density, mechanical behavior, and overall performance.

The performance enhancement observed in siderite-based concretes can be attributed primarily to its high density and iron-rich composition (FeCO₃), which may improve particle packing and influence overall composite behavior. In addition, changes in density and internal structure are expected to affect radiation shielding performance, since higher density and iron content contribute to gamma-ray attenuation efficiency. Furthermore, under elevated temperatures, partial decomposition of siderite may lead to phase transformations that can influence thermal stability and residual mechanical performance.

Despite these advances, a comprehensive and systematic evaluation of siderite incorporation—considering both fine (sand) and coarse aggregate replacement—on abrasion resistance, freeze–thaw durability, high-temperature performance, and radiation shielding behavior within a unified statistical framework is still lacking. Addressing this gap, the present study provides an integrated experimental and statistical assessment of siderite-modified concretes, aiming to establish correlations between durability performance and radiation shielding effectiveness.

Method

In this study, the reference mix design was prepared in accordance with TS 802 (Turkish Standards Institution, 2016) for a nominal 1 m³ concrete mixture containing siderite aggregate 48. The siderite mineral was obtained in block form and processed in the laboratory using a crusher, following TS 706 EN 12,62049. The gradation curve of the siderite aggregate (A16–C16) from the Elbistan region was carefully adjusted to achieve low porosity and high compactness50. Siderite is classified as a heavyweight aggregate with a specific gravity of 3.96 g/cm³. To prevent segregation in heavy concrete, a superplasticizer was added at 2% of cement weight.

The water-to-cement (w/c) ratio was maintained constant at 0.40 for all mixtures. Slump values and fresh concrete temperatures are presented in Table 4. The replacement of natural aggregates with siderite was performed using a mass-based batching approach (kg/m³) for practical implementation during mixing. However, to ensure strict volumetric consistency, all mixture proportions were subsequently verified and adjusted using the absolute volume method. This procedure ensured that the total concrete volume for each mix remained approximately constant at 1 m³.

Although siderite has a higher specific gravity (3.96 g/cm³) compared to natural aggregates (~ 2.7 g/cm³), the conversion from mass to volume was carefully accounted for during mix calculations. As a result, any potential volumetric imbalance arising from density differences was corrected in the final mix design stage. The resulting volumetric deviations were quantified and are presented in Table 4, with all values remaining within ± 0.3%, which is considered acceptable for concrete mix design practice.

Therefore, all mixtures maintained consistent cement paste volume and constant w/c ratio, ensuring that observed differences in mechanical, durability, and shielding properties are attributed solely to the intrinsic characteristics of siderite aggregates (density, packing density, and microstructural refinement) rather than variations in paste content or total concrete volume.

Sample preparation and curing

Concrete samples (10 × 10 × 10 cm³) were produced to evaluate compressive strength, abrasion resistance, ultrasonic sound velocity, freeze–thaw durability, high-temperature resistance (200, 400, 600, and 800 °C), and radiation shielding properties. Compressive strength tests were performed according to TS EN 12390-3 (Turkish Standards Institution, 2019)51 on three samples per mixture over 7, 28, and 90 days, totaling 81 samples. All specimens were cured in a standard curing environment at 20 ± 2 °C and ≥ 95% relative humidity until the testing ages.

Abrasion resistance

Abrasion resistance was determined using the Böhme test according to TS 2824 Annex H52. Hardened cubes (10 × 10 × 10 cm³, ± 1.5 mm) were subjected to 22 revolutions of a 750 mm horizontal disk at 30 ± 1 rpm. The total wear volume loss was recorded by measuring the samples before and after testing.

Ultrasonic testing

Ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) tests were conducted in accordance with ASTM C597 (2022)53. This non-destructive method provides insight into compressive strength by measuring the time of wave propagation through the concrete, which varies depending on the presence and volume of voids.

Freeze-thaw resistance

Freeze-thaw durability was evaluated following ASTM C666 (2015) Procedure A. Concrete samples (10 × 10 × 10 cm³) underwent 50 cycles, each consisting of 3 h at -18 °C (freezing) and 1 h at + 4 °C (thawing), for a total cycle time of 4 h54.

High-temperature resistance

Siderite-doped samples were exposed to high-temperature regimes at 200, 400, 600, and 800 °C after 90 days of curing, followed by air cooling. Post-heating, compressive strength, weight loss, and ultrasonic pulse velocity were measured on 135 samples.

Chloride migration testing and service life estimation

The non-steady-state chloride migration coefficient (Dnssm) was determined according to NT Build 492 standard55. After 28 days of curing, two cylindrical specimens (100 mm × 50 mm) were prepared from each mixture. The lateral surfaces were sealed with epoxy resin in accordance with ASTM C1202 to ensure one-dimensional chloride transport conditions. Rapid chloride migration tests were performed using a Germann Instruments Proove It system56, Prior to testing, specimens were vacuum-saturated in a calcium hydroxide solution as required by NT Build 492 to eliminate air voids and ensure full pore saturation. A constant voltage of 30 V was applied for 24 h.

Calibration samples (OPC 45(Cal) and OPC 55(Cal)) were used only for system verification and not included in performance evaluation.

The chloride migration coefficient obtained from NT Build 492 represents an effective transport parameter that inherently combines ionic diffusion, migration under electrical field, and microstructural pore connectivity effects. Therefore, it is widely accepted in durability studies as a robust indicator of chloride ingress resistance, particularly for comparative evaluation of different concrete systems with varying pore structure and matrix densification characteristics.

Concrete mixtures incorporated siderite as both fine (sand) and coarse aggregates at different replacement levels. Compressive strength was measured at 28 and 90 days according to ASTM C39/C39M. The obtained Dₙₛₛₘ values (×10⁻¹² m²/s) were used as input parameters for service life estimation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 22 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). To assess the differences between sample groups, a One-Way ANOVA was conducted. Duncan’s test was then applied at α = 0.05 to pinpoint specific differences between the groups. All experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3), and the results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Results and discussion

Water absorption and apparent porosity in siderite-modified concretes

The 90-day water absorption and apparent porosity results of siderite-modified concrete samples are presented in Fig. 5. The results indicate that the incorporation of siderite aggregate leads to a systematic reduction in both water absorption and apparent porosity compared to the reference mixture across all replacement levels and mixture series (K and A systems).

Effect of siderite aggregate incorporation on water absorption and apparent porosity of concrete mixtures.

In particular, water absorption decreases from 47.19% in the control mixture to 17.99%–35.44% for K-series mixtures and 17.51%–35.12% for A-series mixtures. Similarly, apparent porosity decreases from 17.17% in the reference mixture to 5.73%–13.15% in siderite-modified concretes.

These reductions may be associated with changes in particle packing characteristics due to the relatively high specific gravity of siderite (3.96 g/cm³). In this context, the presence of higher-density aggregates may influence the overall void structure of the composite, which can affect water transport properties.

Water absorption and porosity in cementitious composites are generally governed by capillary porosity, pore connectivity, and the characteristics of the interfacial transition zone (ITZ)13,50,60–62. Since aggregates occupy approximately 75% of the concrete volume, their physical properties can significantly influence the development of the internal void system63–66. In this regard, the observed reductions in water absorption and apparent porosity suggest a potential decrease in pore connectivity and transport pathways. However, these interpretations are based on macroscopic transport measurements and supporting literature, as direct microstructural characterization techniques such as SEM, MIP, or low-field NMR were not conducted in this study. Nevertheless, the reduced transport-related properties observed in siderite-modified mixtures indicate a potentially beneficial effect on durability-related performance under environmental exposure conditions13,67. The influence of aggregate volume fraction, curing conditions, and ITZ development should also be considered, as these parameters govern long-term transport behavior of concrete68–71. It should be noted that water absorption and apparent porosity were determined in accordance with ASTM C642. Specimens with dimensions of 100 × 100 × 100 mm were oven-dried at 105 ± 5 °C until constant mass was achieved and then cooled to room temperature. Samples were subsequently immersed in water for 48 h to ensure full saturation. The saturated surface-dry condition was obtained prior to weighing in air and in water. Water absorption and apparent porosity were calculated using the standard equations defined in ASTM C642.

Among the sand-replaced mixtures, the highest compressive strength values at all curing ages were obtained for the S100K mixture, while among the aggregate-replaced mixtures, the S100A mixture exhibited the highest performance. Overall, compressive strength increases with increasing siderite replacement ratio in both sand and aggregate systems.

This improvement may be associated with changes in packing characteristics introduced by the incorporation of higher-density siderite aggregates. In this context, the presence of heavier aggregates may influence the overall composite structure and indirectly affect mechanical performance. The observed strength development is consistent with the reduced porosity behavior discussed in Sect. 3.1, suggesting a possible relationship between transport-related properties and mechanical behavior.

Statistical analysis presented in Table 5 confirms that there is a significant difference between the reference and siderite-modified groups at all curing ages (p < 0.05). In particular, significant variations are observed between the S100A and reference groups, as well as between the S100K and reference groups across 7-, 28-, and 90-day measurements. In general, compressive strength increases with curing age, reaching maximum values at 90 days for all mixtures (Fig. 6).

The improvement in compressive strength is consistent with previous studies. Esen and Doğan (2017) reported that siderite-containing concretes exhibited compressive strength values ranging between 47.03 and 66.07 MPa, attributing this improvement to the presence of dense mineral aggregates50. These findings are in agreement with the trends observed in the present study.

In heavyweight concrete systems, barite, magnetite, and other iron-rich aggregates are widely used due to their high density and contribution to mechanical performance72–77. Siderite, with a specific gravity of 3.8–4.7 g/cm³, belongs to this category of heavyweight aggregates. Previous studies have reported that aggregates with higher density and iron content may positively influence mechanical properties50,78 Elyiğit et al. (2024) reported that the use of heavy aggregates from different origins contributes to improved physical and mechanical properties of heavyweight concrete, with iron-rich aggregates showing more pronounced effects78. In the present study, the increase in compressive strength is considered to be mainly related to the physical characteristics of siderite rather than a chemical contribution from its iron oxide content (Fe₂O₃ ≈ 52.06%).

Instead, the observed behavior may be attributed to the influence of aggregate density and grading on the overall composite structure, which can affect load transfer mechanisms within the concrete. However, these interpretations are based on mechanical performance trends and literature evidence, as direct microstructural characterization (e.g., SEM or MIP) was not conducted in this study. Nevertheless, the results are consistent with general findings reported for heavyweight concrete systems incorporating dense mineral aggregates.

From a general durability perspective, abrasion resistance in concrete is influenced by several factors such as aggregate properties, matrix strength, and interfacial bonding characteristics. In this context, the incorporation of siderite may influence the overall composite behavior through its physical properties, particularly density and hardness. However, since no direct microstructural characterization (e.g., SEM or MIP) was performed in this study, any discussion related to internal structural changes should be considered as indirect interpretation based on macroscopic test results.

These findings suggest that abrasion resistance is governed by a combination of mechanical strength and material composition rather than a single dominant parameter. Therefore, abrasion resistance should be considered as a critical durability indicator for concrete exposed to hydraulic or mechanical wear environments.

The mechanical performance of concrete is widely studied; however, abrasion resistance is equally important, particularly for hydraulic structures, pavements, and industrial floors80. Abrasion-related deterioration, although initially occurring at the surface scale, can progressively lead to significant service-life reduction.

Therefore, improving abrasion resistance through optimized material selection is essential. Previous studies have identified aggregate properties, mix design parameters, mineral additives, fibre reinforcement, and curing conditions as key factors affecting abrasion resistance81–84. Among these, aggregate characteristics are generally considered one of the most influential parameters.

Omoding et al. reported that abrasion resistance is strongly dependent on aggregate hardness and showed a correlation between compressive strength and abrasion performance. Similarly, Kumar et al.85 investigated abrasion behavior using different concrete compositions according to ASTM C113880, confirming that mineral additions generally improve resistance. Yen et al.86 also reported that higher-strength concretes tend to exhibit improved abrasion resistance, highlighting the relationship between mechanical strength and surface wear behavior.

In agreement with these studies, the improved abrasion performance of siderite-modified concretes in this study can be attributed primarily to the hardness and mineralogical nature of siderite aggregates. This effect is consistently observed across all curing ages compared to the reference mixture.

Furthermore, the observed trends are consistent with compressive strength development and transport-related properties discussed in previous sections, suggesting a coupled relationship between mechanical performance, durability indicators, and abrasion resistance. However, this relationship is based on experimental correlations rather than direct microstructural evidence.

Ultrasonic pulse velocity response to siderite incorporation

The ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) test is a non-destructive technique used to evaluate the internal quality and homogeneity of concrete by measuring the propagation time of stress waves through the material. The UPV value is calculated by dividing the wave travel distance by the transit time, providing an indirect indication of concrete density, continuity, and internal defect content53. The UPV results of reference and siderite-modified concrete samples are presented in Fig. 8; Table 7. The results indicate that UPV values increase with curing age for all mixtures, regardless of composition. For the 7-day specimens, UPV values range between 3554 and 3957 m/s, while 28-day values increase to 3710–4685 m/s. At 90 days, UPV values further increase to 3987–4702 m/s.

This behavior is consistent with the reduced water absorption and apparent porosity results discussed in Sect. 3.1, indicating a parallel trend between transport-related properties and ultrasonic response. The classification of concrete quality based on UPV values was conducted according to established literature. Concrete is generally classified as “good” when UPV exceeds 3500 m/s and “excellent” when it exceeds 4500 m/s87. In the present study, all siderite-modified mixtures exhibited higher UPV values than the reference mixture at all curing ages (7, 28, and 90 days), as shown in Fig. 8; Table 7.

The improvement in UPV values may be influenced by several factors, including aggregate density, material continuity, and aggregate–cement interaction characteristics. The role of the interfacial transition zone (ITZ) is discussed here only in a general context based on previous literature, since no direct microstructural characterization was performed in this study. Aggregate surface texture has been reported to affect bonding characteristics with the cement matrix, which may influence ultrasonic wave transmission behavior88–90. Güçlüer (2020) similarly reported that aggregate textural properties can influence both compressive strength and UPV values, highlighting the dependence of ultrasonic response on overall material quality91.

Furthermore, UPV values are commonly reported to show an inverse relationship with porosity, where lower porosity is generally associated with higher wave velocity due to reduced internal discontinuities92. The results obtained in the present study are consistent with this general trend, as siderite-modified concretes exhibited lower apparent porosity (Sect. 3.1) and higher compressive strength (Sect. 3.2), accompanied by increased UPV values compared to the reference mixture. However, these relationships should be interpreted as correlative rather than directly mechanistic in the absence of microstructural validation.

The partial evaporation of free water at this stage has been reported in the literature to temporarily improve mechanical performance under certain heating conditions67,103,104.

However, when the temperature exceeded 300 °C, a progressive reduction in compressive strength became dominant. This deterioration is generally associated with the decomposition of hydration products, dehydration effects, and thermally induced internal damage within cement-based materials105. At elevated temperatures, crack formation and progressive damage accumulation are commonly reported mechanisms contributing to strength loss.

The experimental results presented in Tables 10 and 11 show that siderite-modified concretes consistently retained higher strength than the reference mixture at all temperature levels. For example, at 400 °C, the S-30 K and S-100 K mixtures exhibited lower strength loss compared to the control mixture (R), while similar trends were observed for the A-series specimens. At 600 °C and 800 °C, although all mixtures experienced substantial strength reduction, siderite-containing concretes still maintained comparatively higher residual strength values, indicating relatively improved thermal resistance.

From a general interpretation perspective, this behavior may be related to the lower transport-related porosity and altered packing characteristics observed in siderite-modified mixtures, as discussed in Sect. 3.1. In addition, lower permeability may reduce rapid vapor transport and associated internal pressure development during heating, which has been identified in previous studies as an important factor affecting thermal damage evolution in cementitious materials. However, these interpretations are based on macroscopic performance trends and literature findings rather than direct microstructural verification.

The role of aggregate type is particularly important in high-temperature concrete behavior. The thermal expansion mismatch between aggregate particles and cement paste is widely recognized as a factor influencing crack formation and propagation. Lower mismatch levels may reduce internal stress development, whereas higher mismatch generally accelerates cracking and stiffness degradation106–109.

In addition, thermal stability is influenced by aggregate mineralogy and aggregate–paste interaction characteristics. Nevertheless, these effects are discussed here within a general literature-supported framework, since no direct microstructural characterization under elevated temperature conditions was performed in this study.

The thermal behavior of siderite aggregates is also relevant. Siderite (FeCO₃) is a ferrous carbonate mineral that may undergo thermal decomposition at elevated temperatures (typically above 400–500 °C), potentially leading to the formation of iron oxide phases and CO₂ release. Such transformations may influence the internal structure of the composite at high temperatures; however, their direct contribution to mechanical behavior cannot be experimentally confirmed within the scope of the present study. Up to approximately 300 °C, the dominant deterioration mechanisms are generally associated with dehydration and decomposition of cement hydration products such as C–S–H gel and Ca(OH)₂.

Overall, the results suggest that the chloride migration resistance of the investigated concretes is governed by a combination of binder chemistry (GGBFS-related effects) and physical transport characteristics associated with aggregate modification. Regression analysis indicated a strong statistical relationship between compressive strength and chloride migration coefficient (R² ≈ 0.97–0.88). Nevertheless, these effects should be interpreted within the broader context of long-term material performance evolution rather than as a single governing mechanism.

Overall, the results suggest that siderite incorporation, together with constant GGBFS content, may contribute to improved chloride transport resistance and durability-related performance under the investigated conditions.

At 1173 keV, MAC increased from 0.5814 ± 0.0246 g/cm² (10% aggregate) to 0.7387 ± 0.0541 g/cm² (100% aggregate). This behavior may be associated with the relatively high density and iron-rich composition of siderite-containing mixtures, which can increase photon interaction probability.

The HVL results shown in Fig. 20 demonstrated a decreasing trend with increasing siderite content, indicating improved shielding efficiency. For example, at 1173 keV, HVL decreased from 0.5117 cm to 0.3799 cm for aggregate-siderite concretes as replacement increased from 10% to 100%. Similar reductions were observed in sand-based systems.

The TVL values presented in Fig. 21 further support this trend, where higher siderite content resulted in lower required shielding thickness. At 1173 keV, TVL decreased from 1.6999 cm (10%) to 1.2620 cm (100%) in aggregate-modified concretes.

Likewise, the MFP values shown in Fig. 22 decreased with increasing siderite content, indicating reduced photon travel distance within the material and improved attenuation behavior. The lowest MFP values were consistently obtained for the 100% siderite mixtures.

All radiation shielding parameters are summarized in Table 17, which shows that aggregate-siderite concretes generally exhibited slightly better shielding performance than sand-siderite concretes under the investigated conditions. This difference may be associated with variations in bulk density, aggregate distribution, and overall material continuity between the two systems. However, direct microstructural characterization techniques such as SEM or MIP were not employed in this study; therefore, interpretations regarding internal packing characteristics and void structure should be considered qualitative and literature-supported.

From a general radiation interaction perspective, materials with higher density and higher effective atomic number generally exhibit improved photon attenuation efficiency due to increased interaction probability between photons and matter. Accordingly, siderite incorporation may contribute to improved gamma-ray attenuation behavior because of its iron-rich composition and relatively high density.

The observed superior shielding performance of aggregate-siderite concretes compared to sand-siderite systems may also be associated with differences in particle arrangement and overall composite structure. Coarse aggregates can influence the bulk density distribution and internal continuity of the concrete system, potentially affecting photon transmission behavior. However, these interpretations are based on macroscopic radiation-shielding measurements and general literature findings rather than directly verified microstructural mechanisms.

Table 17 presents a detailed comparison of the LAC, MAC, HVL, TVL, and MFP parameters for both sand-based and aggregate-based siderite concretes at energy levels of 1173 and 1333 keV. The results demonstrate that shielding performance systematically improved with increasing siderite content in all mixtures. LAC and MAC values increased, whereas HVL, TVL, and MFP values decreased correspondingly. These trends are generally consistent with the expected influence of density and iron content on gamma-ray attenuation behavior.

Compared to the reference concrete, all siderite-containing mixtures exhibited improved gamma-ray attenuation performance. The highest performance was observed in the 100%A mixture, where the linear attenuation coefficient at 1173 keV increased from 1.08 cm⁻¹ to 1.8245 cm⁻¹, corresponding to an improvement of approximately 69%. A similar increase of approximately 68% was also observed at 1333 keV. Parallel improvements were observed in MAC values, indicating enhanced attenuation capability relative to the reference mixture.

In contrast, noticeable reductions were observed in HVL, TVL, and MFP values. These reductions indicate that photons were attenuated over comparatively shorter distances within the material, resulting in improved shielding effectiveness. The observed trends suggest that siderite incorporation positively influences photon attenuation behavior under the investigated experimental conditions.

Furthermore, although all siderite-modified mixtures exhibited superior shielding performance compared to the reference concrete, the A-series samples generally demonstrated slightly higher attenuation capability than the K-series mixtures at both energy levels. This difference may be associated with variations in aggregate distribution, density characteristics, and overall composite behavior within the concrete system.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that siderite incorporation, in both sand and aggregate forms, improves the mechanical, durability-related, and radiation shielding performance of heavyweight concretes compared to the reference mixture under the investigated experimental conditions. The observed improvements are generally associated with changes in material composition, density-related characteristics, and transport-related behavior rather than a single directly verified mechanism.

The results show that increasing siderite content leads to higher compressive strength, abrasion resistance, and ultrasonic pulse velocity values, while reducing water absorption and chloride migration coefficients. Among all mixtures, the coarse aggregate siderite concrete (S100A) exhibited the most balanced overall performance, suggesting that aggregate size and mixture composition may influence the overall behavior of heavyweight concrete systems.

The improved performance observed in siderite-modified concretes may be associated with physical mixture characteristics such as aggregate density, particle distribution, and transport-related properties. Similarly, the enhanced radiation shielding behavior is considered to be related primarily to the relatively high density and iron-rich composition of siderite, which generally increase photon attenuation capability. However, these interpretations should be considered qualitative and literature-supported, since direct microstructural characterization techniques such as SEM, MIP, EDS, or TG/DTG were not employed in this study.

The main contribution of this study is the systematic evaluation of siderite as both fine and coarse aggregate within a unified experimental framework covering mechanical performance, durability-related behavior, high-temperature response, chloride transport resistance, and gamma radiation shielding characteristics. The results indicate that both binder-related effects (associated with GGBFS) and aggregate-related physical characteristics may contribute to the overall performance of the investigated concretes.

Future studies should focus on long-term field performance, economic feasibility, and behavior under coupled environmental conditions. In addition, advanced microstructural characterization techniques such as SEM, MIP, low-field NMR, and TG/DTG analyses are recommended to directly evaluate the internal structural changes and verify the interpretations proposed in the present study.
