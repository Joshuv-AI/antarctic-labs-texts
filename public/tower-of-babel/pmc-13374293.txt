Developing and evaluating the effectiveness of a web-based application to improve the mental health of infertile women: a multi-phase study protocol
Authors: Fatemeh Aliasghari, Kobra Hamdi, Zeinab Mohammadzadeh, Somayeh Abdolalipour, Mojgan Mirghafourvand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInfertility is a chronic bio-psycho-social condition associated with substantial psychological distress, particularly among women. High rates of anxiety, depression, reduced quality of life, low self-efficacy, and impaired resilience have been widely reported, while access to psychological support remains limited due to financial, social, and stigma-related barriers. Web-based mental health interventions offer a promising, accessible, and cost-effective approach to address these unmet needs. This study aims to develop and evaluate the effectiveness of a user-centered, web-based psychoeducational and supportive application designed to improve the mental health of infertile women.MethodsThis developmental-applied study will be conducted in three phases. Phase 1 involves a comprehensive needs assessment through systematic literature reviews and qualitative interviews with infertile women and multidisciplinary experts to identify educational and supportive mental health needs. Phase 2 focuses on the design and development of a web-based application using user-centered design principles, incorporating evidence-based psychoeducational content, interactive exercises, and supportive resources targeting depression, anxiety, stress, quality of life, infertility self-efficacy, and resilience. Phase 3 will evaluate the application through a randomized controlled clinical trial conducted in infertility clinics affiliated with Tabriz University of Medical Sciences. Eligible infertile women will be randomly assigned to either the intervention group, receiving access to the application for six weeks, or a control group receiving routine care. Outcomes will be assessed using validated instruments, including Depression, (Anxiety and Stress Scale – 21) DASS-21, (Fertility Quality of life) FertiQoL, Infertility Self-Efficacy Scale, Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale, and the Questionnaire for User Interaction Satisfaction. Linear mixed models will be applied to compare post-intervention outcomes between groups.DiscussionThis study contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting the role of digital mental health interventions in reproductive medicine and highlights the potential of technology-driven, patient-centered approaches to improve psychological outcomes in infertile women.

Background

Infertility is a chronic clinical condition defined as the inability to achieve a natural pregnancy after 12 months or more of regular, unprotected sexual intercourse [1]. It represents a complex bio-psycho-social health issue that affects approximately 18% of women of reproductive age and is associated with substantial psychological distress and reduced quality of life [2, 3]. The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies infertility as a disabling disease and one of the most serious global health disabilities [4]. Infertile individuals and couples commonly experience sexual dysfunction, financial strain, depression, anxiety, social stigma, marital instability, and diminished self-esteem, all of which adversely affect physical and mental well-being [2, 3, 5, 6].

Psychological distress—particularly anxiety and depression—is highly prevalent among infertile women, affecting 25–53% before treatment and up to 75% during fertility treatments [1, 5, 7]. Uncertainty regarding diagnosis and treatment outcomes, repeated medical interventions, and treatment failure further intensify emotional distress, leading to feelings of shame, hopelessness, and loss of control [2, 8, 9]. Psychological interventions have been shown to reduce distress, improve marital satisfaction, and enhance pregnancy outcomes, highlighting the importance of integrating mental health care into infertility treatment [10].

Despite this high burden, only a small proportion of infertile women seek psychological support, largely due to financial, social, and stigma-related barriers, underscoring the need for accessible and alternative care approaches [11–13]. Women undergoing assisted reproductive technologies are particularly vulnerable to psychological disorders, which may contribute to treatment discontinuation and reduced adherence [14–17]. Addressing psychosocial needs and enhancing coping resources are therefore essential for improving treatment outcomes and overall well-being [18]. Effective coping strategies play a critical role in reducing infertility-related stress, with infertility self-efficacy emerging as a key protective factor [19–21]. Higher self-efficacy is associated with better emotional regulation, greater persistence with treatment, healthier behaviors, and improved outcomes, whereas treatment failure can further diminish self-efficacy and coping capacity [22–26].

Mindful living enhances psychological insight [27]. Information and mobile health technologies improve healthcare efficiency, enable effective patient–provider communication, and support eHealth initiatives for clinical services, education, and research [28–33]. In infertility care, web- and mobile-based interventions provide education, reduce psychological distress, promote healthy behaviors, and overcome barriers such as cost, travel, scheduling, and stigma, improving treatment outcomes and offering privacy when face-to-face contact is not possible [34–38].

Studies show that infertility places a substantial psychological burden on couples, with women being more affected than men. Longer infertility duration is associated with higher levels of distress, depression, and anxiety in women, while better marital adjustment and greater social support are linked to fewer emotional problems [39]. Similarly, infertile women experience greater psychosocial stress than men, including psychological exhaustion, marital conflict, social pressures, and lower self-efficacy [40, 41]. Evidence from Iran also indicates that infertile women report higher levels of anxiety, depression, fear, and lower perceived social support compared with infertile men, likely influenced by cultural and gender roles [35, 42].

Evidence from randomized controlled trials demonstrates that online psychoeducational, cognitive-behavioral, and mind-body programs significantly reduce psychological distress—including infertility-related anxiety, depression, and stress—while enhancing self-efficacy, marital cohesion, and decision-making clarity, and in some cases, even increasing pregnancy rates [14, 43–45]. Moreover, web-based educational tools, informational applications, and instructional videos have been shown, compared with standard or traditional education, to increase patient satisfaction, improve knowledge of in vitro fertilization/assisted reproductive technology (IVF/ART) procedures, enhance treatment confidence, and reduce medication errors, although direct effects on mental health were not consistently reported [16, 18, 46].

Digital interventions targeting lifestyle modification and preconception behaviors—such as mobile health coaching programs and nutrition and physical activity applications—have proven effective in improving dietary habits, reducing tobacco and alcohol use, increasing micronutrient intake, managing body mass index, and raising user awareness. These programs generally achieve high acceptability and engagement; however, gradual declines in participation over time underscore the importance of continuous support [47–50].

Overall, this evidence highlights the strong potential of web- and mobile-based applications as cost-effective, accessible, and complementary interventions to conventional infertility care, and emphasizes the importance of developing and evaluating integrated, user-centered programs that specifically address the mental health needs of women experiencing infertility. It can be inferred that in an increasingly technology-driven world, internet-based approaches for psychological care and treatment provide promising alternatives to support women and men experiencing the emotional consequences of infertility and its treatments. Therefore, the development and evaluation of patient-centered electronic mental health tools grounded in evidence-based interventions should be a priority. Accordingly, this study will be conducted with the aim of developing and evaluating the effectiveness of a web-based application to improve the mental health of infertile women.

Methods

Study design: Developmental-Applied

Study population: The study population will include all women of reproductive age (10–54 years) diagnosed with infertility (primary, secondary, male-factor, female-factor, combined, or unexplained) with a minimum duration of one year, who are referred to specialized and subspecialized infertility and gynecology clinics in Tabriz-Iran. Eligibility will be confirmed by a physician, and participants will include those who have not responded to at least six months of prior treatment.

Study phases: This developmental-applied study will be conducted in three phases with the aim of developing and evaluating the effectiveness of a web-based application to improve the mental health of infertile women (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of study

Phase 1

Objective

To identify the structural components and educational-supportive needs related to mental health in women with infertility, for the purpose of designing the content of the web-based application.

Implementation steps

Phase 1 will be implemented in two sequential steps.

First, a comprehensive needs assessment will be conducted through systematic reviews of (1) the scientific literature on mental health in infertile women—focusing on interventions designed to reduce depression, anxiety, and stress, improve quality of life, and enhance empowerment and resilience in order to identify key determinants and effective strategies—and (2) existing national and international web-based applications, programs, and educational guidelines to identify content gaps, limitations in educational-supportive and user-centered design approaches, and shortcomings in addressing mental health outcomes. Literature searches will be conducted without time limitations in English and Persian databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, SID, Magiran, IranDoc, and Google Scholar. In this systematic review, the population (P) will include women diagnosed with primary or secondary infertility, regardless of age, cultural background, or stage of treatment. The intervention (I) will consist of national and international web-based or mobile-based applications, digital programs, and online educational or supportive platforms specifically designed to address mental health concerns in infertile women, including components such as psychoeducation, cognitive-behavioral strategies, stress management, emotional support, and empowerment or resilience training, whether self-guided or therapist-assisted. The comparison (C), where applicable, will include usual infertility care without structured digital psychological support, alternative digital interventions, face-to-face psychological programs, or no intervention/waitlist controls. The outcomes (O) of interest will include depression, anxiety, and stress, quality of life, empowerment, resilience, user engagement and adherence, usability and satisfaction, as well as identification of content gaps, design limitations, and overall effectiveness in addressing mental health outcomes.

Second, a qualitative study will be carried out using in-depth, semi-structured interviews and surveys with infertile women and expert specialists in obstetrics, gynecology, and psychology. This step aims to explore perceived needs, challenges, priorities, cultural considerations, and preferences regarding digital mental health education. Sampling will continue until data saturation is achieved.

Qualitative data will be analyzed using conventional content analysis following Graneheim and Lundman’s approach [51]. Data analysis will involve verbatim transcription of interviews, repeated reading to gain an overall understanding, coding of meaning units, classification of codes into subcategories and categories, and extraction of overarching themes. The results of this phase will inform the development and finalization of the application’s educational-supportive structure, key components, and content priorities.

Phase 2

Objective

The objective of Phase 2 is to design and develop the web-based psychoeducational and supportive application, based on the findings and educational-supportive needs identified in Phase 1.

Design and development process

In this phase, the results of the needs assessment and qualitative findings from Phase 1 will serve as the foundation for application development. The application will be designed using principles of User-Centered Design (UCD) to ensure usability, relevance, and responsiveness to users’ real needs. The overall structure, content organization, graphical design, and interactive pathways will be systematically planned and implemented.

The application will include multimedia educational modules, interactive exercises, and scenario-based activities designed to reduce depression, anxiety, and stress; improve quality of life and infertility self-efficacy; enhance resilience; and provide access to credible, evidence-based mental health resources. All content will be delivered through a simple, user-friendly, and engaging interface.

Application design stages

Conceptual design

At this stage, the overall structure of the application will be defined. Wireframes will be developed to illustrate page layouts and content organization without graphical details. A preliminary prototype will also be created to test core concepts and gather initial feedback from users.

Graphic and user interaction design

The visual and interactive aspects of the application will be developed during this stage. User Interaction (UI) design will focus on visual elements such as color schemes, typography, icons, and navigation components. User Experience (UX) design will focus on user interaction, accessibility, ease of navigation, and overall satisfaction with the application.

Development and coding

This stage involves technical implementation of the application. Backend development will include the creation of databases, application programming interfaces (APIs), and business logic. Frontend development will involve client-side coding using standard web technologies (e.g., HTML, CSS, JavaScript) and modern frameworks. During this phase, performance, security, and functionality testing will be conducted to ensure application quality.

Testing and evaluation

Prior to full implementation, the educational content will be reviewed and evaluated by expert panels consisting of obstetricians, and psychologists to ensure scientific accuracy and appropriateness. Key stakeholders—including infertile women, their spouses, midwives, faculty members from the midwifery department, and expert specialists—will participate in pilot testing of the initial version of the application. Feedback related to usability, engagement, technical performance, and responsiveness to user needs will be collected. Based on this feedback, necessary revisions and improvements will be applied to finalize the application.

Phase 3

Objective

The objective of Phase 3 is to evaluate the effectiveness, usability, and user satisfaction of the developed web-based mental health application for infertile women through a randomized controlled clinical trial (RCT).

Study design

Phase 3 will be conducted as a randomized controlled clinical trial following registration in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT).

Target population and setting

The study population will consist of women of reproductive age (10–54 years) diagnosed with infertility (primary, secondary, male factor, female factor, combined, or unexplained infertility) for at least one year. Infertility must be confirmed by a physician, and participants must have shown no response to at least six months of treatment. The study will be conducted in specialized and subspecialty women’s and infertility clinics affiliated with Tabriz University of Medical Sciences in Tabriz-Iran during the year 2026.

Sample size

The sample size in this study was calculated using G-Power software. Based on the results of the study by Hosseini et al. (2018) on the depression variable, and considering M1 = 9.60 (mean score of the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS) depression component), M2 = 6.24 (with a 35% reduction assumed due to the intervention), SD1 = 4.55, SD2 = 0.05, two-sided α = 0.05, and Power = 80%, it was calculated to be 30 people. Regarding the anxiety variable, considering M1 = 8.47 (mean DASS anxiety component score), M2 = 5.5 (with the assumption of a 35% reduction due to the intervention), SD1 = 2.29, SD2 = 0.05, two-sided α = 0.05, and Power = 95% the sample size was calculated to be 11 people. For the stress variable, considering M1 = 12.33 (mean score of the DASS stress component), M2 = 8.01 (with the assumption of a 35% reduction due to the intervention), SD1 = 3.44, SD2 = 0.05, two-sided α = 0.05, and Power = 95%, the sample size was calculated to be 18 people [52]. Given that the sample size calculated for the depression variable is high, the final sample size, taking into account a 10% dropout rate, will be 33 people in each group.

Data collection tools

Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS-21)

The DASS-21 is a self-report instrument designed to measure the severity of negative emotional symptoms across three domains: depression, anxiety, and stress. This tool is widely accepted as a standard measure not only in clinical settings but also in psychological research. The questionnaire was developed by Lovibond in 1995 as a shortened version of the original DASS-42, and due to its efficiency and psychometric validity, it is extensively used in both clinical and research environments [53].

The DASS-21 consists of 21 items, equally divided into three subscales:

Depression Subscale: Measures symptoms such as hopelessness, low mood, lack of self-confidence, feelings of worthlessness, emptiness, lack of interest in activities, anhedonia, and low energy (Items: 3, 5, 10, 13, 16, 17, 21).

Depression Subscale: Measures symptoms such as hopelessness, low mood, lack of self-confidence, feelings of worthlessness, emptiness, lack of interest in activities, anhedonia, and low energy (Items: 3, 5, 10, 13, 16, 17, 21).

Anxiety Subscale: Focuses on physiological hyperarousal, situational anxiety, tremors, shortness of breath, and irrational fear (Items: 2, 4, 7, 9, 15, 19, 20).

Anxiety Subscale: Focuses on physiological hyperarousal, situational anxiety, tremors, shortness of breath, and irrational fear (Items: 2, 4, 7, 9, 15, 19, 20).

Stress Subscale: Includes difficulty relaxing, nervous tension, irritability, and restlessness (Items: 1, 6, 8, 11, 12, 14, 18).

Stress Subscale: Includes difficulty relaxing, nervous tension, irritability, and restlessness (Items: 1, 6, 8, 11, 12, 14, 18).

Each item is scored on a four-point Likert scale (0: Did not apply to me at all to 3: Applied to me very much, or most of the time). After summing the scores for each subscale, the final score is multiplied by 2 to align with the original DASS-42 scoring. Several studies have confirmed the reliability and validity of the DASS-21. For instance, in Iran, Samani and Jokar (2006) reported Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the depression, anxiety, and stress subscales as 0.81, 0.74, and 0.78, respectively [54].

Fertility Quality of Life Questionnaire (FertiQoL)

The FertiQoL is a self-report instrument designed to assess quality of life in individuals experiencing infertility. It was developed by the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) and the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) [55]. This questionnaire contains 36 items including 24 items assess fertility-specific quality of life across four domains: emotional, social, mind/body, and relational; 10 items evaluate infertility treatment-related quality of life across two subscales: environment and tolerability and; 2 items measure overall quality of life and general health. Responses are scored on a Likert scale from 0 to 4, and domain and total scores are calculated. Following the scoring instructions, raw scores are converted to a 0–100 scale, where higher scores indicate better and more desirable quality of life.

The FertiQoL is a reliable and valid measure for assessing the impact of infertility and its treatment on quality of life. It demonstrates face and content validity, as well as convergent validity with similar instruments. Cronbach’s alpha for the fertility-specific and treatment-related subscales ranges from 0.72 to 0.90, indicating satisfactory internal consistency. In Iran, the face and content validity have been confirmed by several experts, and in the Persian version by Maroufinezhad et al. (2016), the Cronbach’s alpha for the total scale was reported as 0.90 [56].

Infertility Self-Efficacy Scale (ISE)

The Infertility Self-Efficacy Scale was developed by Cousineau et al. (2006) to assess individuals perceived confidence in their ability to manage infertility-related challenges, cope with medical treatments, and their attitudes toward diagnosis and treatment [57]. This questionnaire evaluates a participant’s perceived ability to apply cognitive, emotional, and behavioral skills related to infertility treatment, including behavioral–psychological techniques, stress management, relaxation methods, general skills, or self-help strategies.

The ISE consists of 16 items, each rated on a 9-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (not at all confident) to 9 (completely confident). Total scores range from 16 to 144, with higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy. The internal consistency reliability of the scale, measured by Cronbach’s alpha, was reported as 0.94 by Cousineau et al. (2006). In Iran, psychometric properties were examined by Ebrahimi-Far et al. (2018) on 203 women diagnosed with infertility, showing a construct validity of 0.851 and Cronbach’s alpha of 0.90, confirming the scale’s reliability and validity in the Iranian population [58].

Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC)

Resilience refers to the capacity to cope with adversity or the degree to which individuals respond to challenges. It is an ability that enables people to overcome difficulties and hardships. In this context, Connor and Davidson (2003) developed the CD-RISC after reviewing research literature from 1979 to 1991 on resilience. The psychometric properties of this scale were assessed in six populations: the general population, primary care patients, psychiatric outpatients, patients with generalized anxiety disorder, and two groups of post-trauma patients. The developers of the scale assert that it effectively differentiates resilient from non-resilient individuals in both clinical and non-clinical groups and can be used in research and clinical settings [59].

The CD-RISC contains 25 items and measures five factors: Personal competence (items 10, 11, 12, 16, 17, 23, 24, 25); Trust in instincts / tolerance of negative affect (items 6, 7, 14, 15, 18, 19, 20); Acceptance of change and secure relationships (items 1, 2, 4, 5, 8); Control (items 13, 21, 22) and; Spiritual influences (items 3, 9). Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (not true at all) to 5 (true nearly all the time). The validity and reliability of the scale were confirmed in an Iranian study by Mohammadi et al. (2005), with Cronbach’s alpha of 0.93, indicating excellent internal consistency [60].

Questionnaire for User Interaction Satisfaction (QUIS)

The QUIS is one of the most validated and widely used instruments for measuring user satisfaction with interaction with information systems. It was developed by the Human-Computer Interaction Laboratory at the University of Maryland [61]. The questionnaire contains 27 items evaluating usability and user satisfaction, grouped into five domains including Software functionality (6 items); Screen design/display (4 items); Information content and software corrections (6 items); Software learnability (6 items) and; Overall software capability (5 items). Each item is rated on a 0–9 scale, where 1 indicates the lowest satisfaction / usability and 9 indicates the highest. The Persian version has been validated, demonstrating Cronbach’s alpha = 0.94 and a test-retest reliability index of 0.993 [62].

Randomization and allocation

Participants will be assigned to the intervention or control group by a non-involved individual using block randomization method with stratification based on parity, via RAS software with blocks of 4 and 6 and a 1:1 allocation ratio. The allocation sequence will be written on paper and placed in opaque, sequentially numbered envelopes (allocation concealment). Envelopes will be opened in the order of participant entry, and the participant’s name and group assignment will be recorded and communicated to her.

Intervention implementation

Participants in the intervention group will receive access to the web-based mental health application for a 6-week intervention period. Upon initial login, users will enter baseline information, enabling tailored content delivery. Participants will receive training on application use and ongoing online support throughout the intervention. The researcher will maintain regular contact via phone calls and text messages to encourage engagement, provide reminders, and address technical issues. Brief content-related questions will be administered before and after each application module to confirm engagement and comprehension. The control group will receive routine care.

To reduce the risk of contamination, participants will be selected from individuals who do not have familial, neighborhood, or friendship relationships with one another. In addition to emphasizing and recommending that the contents of the application not be shared with others until the completion of the study, necessary measures will be implemented in the form of software-based restrictions, including individual user passwords and disabling screenshots and content transfer, to prevent participants in the intervention group from sharing the application’s materials with others. Furthermore, in order to monitor and prevent information exchange between the two study groups, the researcher will maintain ongoing interaction with all participants in both groups throughout the intervention period to remain informed of any potential contacts or communications between the groups.

Ethical considerations and data security

All participant data will be recorded using unique identification codes. No identifying information will be stored within the application database. Data will be securely stored and accessible only to authorized members of the research team. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants, and ethical guidelines for clinical trials will be strictly followed.

Statistical analysis

For application evaluation, descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation, and confidence intervals) will be used to summarize user interaction satisfaction, socio-demographic and obstetric characteristics, depression, anxiety, stress, quality of life, resilience and infertility self-efficacy scores.

To evaluate the effectiveness of the intervention, Linear Mixed Models (LMM) will be used to compare post-intervention outcomes—including depression, anxiety, stress, quality of life, resilience, and infertility self-efficacy—between the intervention and control groups.

Discussion

The present study was designed to develop and evaluate a user-centered, web-based psychoeducational and supportive application aimed at improving the mental health of infertile women. Given the substantial psychological burden associated with infertility—including elevated levels of depression, anxiety, stress, reduced quality of life, and diminished self-efficacy—this study responds to a critical need for accessible, scalable, and culturally appropriate mental health interventions within infertility care.

If effective, the web-based application could be integrated into routine infertility care as a complementary mental health support tool. It may assist healthcare providers in addressing psychological distress without increasing clinical workload, while offering patients continuous emotional support throughout the treatment process. Given the high prevalence of infertility and the limited availability of mental health services, especially in low-resource settings, scalable digital interventions may help reduce health inequities by expanding access to psychological care for infertile women.

The findings may inform health policymakers about the value of incorporating e-mental health solutions into infertility care pathways. Investment in digital psychosocial interventions could represent a cost-effective strategy to improve patient outcomes and reduce treatment discontinuation. This study provides a structured framework for the development and evaluation of digital mental health interventions in reproductive health. Future research may build on this model by examining long-term outcomes, cost-effectiveness, partner-inclusive interventions, and the impact of digital mental health support on clinical pregnancy outcomes.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has several strengths. First, the multi-phase developmental-applied design ensures that the intervention is grounded in empirical evidence and user needs. Second, the use of validated and widely accepted psychological instruments enhances the reliability and comparability of outcome measurements. Third, the randomized controlled trial design strengthens causal inference regarding intervention effectiveness. Fourth, the application incorporates user-centered design principles and continuous engagement monitoring, which may improve usability, adherence, and user satisfaction.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study is conducted in a single geographic region, which may limit generalizability to other cultural or healthcare contexts. Reliance on self-reported measures may introduce reporting bias. The relatively short intervention and follow-up period may not capture long-term psychological outcomes or sustained behavioral changes. Additionally, participants require access to the internet and sufficient digital literacy, which may exclude some women and introduce selection bias. Finally, although efforts are made to minimize contamination, complete prevention of information exchange between groups cannot be guaranteed.
