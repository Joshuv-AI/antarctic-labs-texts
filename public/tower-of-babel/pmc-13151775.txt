PTSD and CPTSD amongst parents subject to child protection court proceedings: observed rates and associated demographic and clinical covariates
Authors: Katherine Graham, Roxanna Short, Emma Facer-Irwin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Substance misuse is a commonly cited reason for the initiation of child protection proceedings. Additionally, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) shows a higher prevalence in both mothers and fathers involved in child protection services compared to the general population. Complex PTSD (CPTSD), recently included in the International Classification of Diseases 11th revision (ICD-11), has yet to be formally investigated in child protection systems using a validated tool.Objective: We aimed to identify the observed rates of PTSD and CPTSD in parents subject to child protection proceedings using the International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ) and to examine associated demographic (age, gender, ethnicity) and clinical (depression, anxiety, substance/alcohol misuse, and domestic abuse) factors.Methods: As a cross-sectional, observational study, we used routinely collected ITQ data pertaining to 93 parents accessing a Family Drug and Alcohol Court service in England between August 2022 and June 2024. We used multivariable logistic regression models to explore associations between demographic and clinical variables and presence of PTSD or CPTSD.Results: We found that over half of the parents met criteria for PTSD or CPTSD (N = 52, 55.9%), with CPTSD being more prevalent (N = 34, 36.6%) than PTSD (N = 18, 19.4%). Anxiety and depression were significantly associated with presence of PTSD or CPTSD, and 90.1% of parents assessed had histories of substance misuse.Conclusion: These findings show high rates of CPTSD and high levels of trauma and substance misuse difficulties in CP contexts, highlighting the need for integrating trauma-informed treatments and whole person care services for parents subject to CP proceedings to produce better outcomes for families.

Introduction

Substance misuse is one of the leading reasons children enter child protection (CP) proceedings in the UK, yet the complex trauma and mental health difficulties that frequently co-occur with addiction often remain inadequately addressed (Roy, 2023). This gap has profound implications: parents with serious mental illnesses (SMI) face elevated risks of losing parental rights (Kaplan et al., 2019; Salzer et al., 2021), and without trauma-informed intervention, cycles of family separation and system involvement persist across generations. In 2025 alone, 402,400 children (or 1 in every 30 children) were classified as ‘in need’ and just under 50,000 children (approximately 1 in 250 children) were placed on child protection plans in England (Department for Education, 2025), with substance misuse (alongside domestic violence, neglect, and parental mental health concerns) being commonly cited reasons for proceedings (Roy, 2021; Skinner et al., 2021).

Research consistently shows that parents entering CP proceedings report higher rates of trauma, victimization, substance misuse, and other mental health difficulties compared to the general population (Forrester, 2000; Mason et al., 2020; Suomi et al., 2023). Over 90% of mothers involved in child protection services have a history of exposure to complex trauma, with many having been in the care system themselves (Blakey & Glaude, 2021; Chemtob et al., 2011). Despite these intertwined challenges, substance misuse is often treated as the primary concern in CP cases, while underlying trauma and mental health needs remain under-identified and under-treated (Roy, 2023). This highlights the need for broad systemic change and greater awareness of the barriers people face when coming into CP proceedings, coupled with trauma informed practices and dual diagnosis (whole person care) interventions aimed at reunification (Cross et al., 2018; McKenna & Holtfreter, 2021).

Despite evidence that parents subject to child protection proceedings have elevated rates of trauma exposure and additional complex mental health needs, engagement remains challenging. Research indicates that mothers with recurrent CP involvement often present with extensive histories of childhood adversities and complex trauma, which can foster deep mistrust of professionals and non-engagement as a protective response to feelings of shame, blame and fear of re-victimization (Baginsky, 2023; Bekaert et al., 2021; Mason et al., 2020). However, relatively little research has estimated the prevalence of Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) and Complex PTSD (CPTSD) in this population. A handful of studies have previously found that, compared to the general population, parents involved with CP services report higher rates of PTSD (Christie et al., 2019; Suomi et al., 2023). Significant comorbidity with PTSD in this population has also been well-documented, including higher rates of depression and other mood disorders, maternal health needs, substance misuse, as well as higher rates of further victimization compared to parents without PTSD (Bellis et al., 2001; Chemtob et al., 2011; Suomi et al., 2023). Studies have also found that higher rates of PTSD in children whose mothers have been diagnosed with the disorder (Roberts et al., 2012). Although this has not been directly examined in CP samples, Fredman and colleagues (Fredman et al., 2019) found that parental PTSD is linked to greater parenting risks (i.e harsher parenting, difficulties regulating emotions, and abuse potentials), which in turn can be linked to child PTSD symptom development, highlighting the intergenerational transmission of trauma (Powers et al., 2022; Schechter et al., 2017). Suomi et al.’s (2023) systematic review found that although a higher prevalence of PTSD was seen in both mothers and fathers compared to the general population, mothers showed consistently higher rates of the disorder than fathers.

Perhaps most importantly, we identified no previous study which examined the prevalence of CPTSD, specifically. Emerging evidence suggests that, particularly amongst complex, treatment-seeking, and/or trauma exposed clinical populations in the UK, the prevalence of CPTSD may be higher than that of PTSD. For example, a study of sentenced male prisoners in the UK reported 16.7% prevalence of CTPSD compared with 7.7% for PTSD (Facer-Irwin et al., 2022). Similarly, Murphy et al. (2021) found that in treatment-seeking veterans in the UK, CPTSD was more frequently endorsed than PTSD (56.7% and 14.0% respectively), indicating that CPTSD was more strongly associated with childhood trauma. CPTSD has also been found to be associated with higher rates of comorbidity (including substance misuse, depression, and PD) than PTSD (Facer-Irwin et al., 2022; Jowett et al., 2024; Murphy et al., 2021) and high-risk behaviours such as self-harm, suicide, and aggression, including DV (Dyer et al., 2009; Expósito-Álvarez et al., 2025). CPTSD also typically requires longer and more specialized treatment compared to PTSD, due in part to the elevated risk and comorbidity (Gilbar, 2020; Karatzias et al., 2019).

Given the significant trauma histories, complexity, and comorbidity documented within parents subject to CP proceedings, (Suomi et al., 2023), establishing the prevalence of CPTSD using a validated tool is essential for informing our understanding of the mental health and treatment needs of this population. Clarifying the extent to which parents may be experiencing difficulties inherent to CPTSD (such as mistrust, emotional dysregulation and difficulties building and maintaining relationships) may further help to inform and shape professional engagement with this often hard-to-reach group (Mason et al., 2020). Our study therefore aimed to be the first to examine the rates of ICD-11 PTSD and CPTSD in a sample of parents subject to CP proceedings using a validated tool (the International Trauma Questionnaire; Cloitre et al., 2018). We further aimed to investigate demographic and clinical correlates of this disorder, including comorbidity with other mental health conditions (depression, anxiety) and relevant clinical characteristics (substance misuse, domestic violence history).

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional, observational study involved secondary retrieval, collection and analysis of data stemming from the routine clinical assessments and service data. As such, NHS ethical approval was not required – local approval was provided by the service clinical leads and local Trust R&D department.

Service context – family drug and alcohol courts

In the UK, trauma-informed problem-solving courts, such as Family Drug and Alcohol Courts (FDACs), have been developed to help parents in CP proceedings reunify with their children by providing holistic, wraparound support, including input from substance misuse specialists, social workers and psychologists (substance misuse specialists refer to professionals who have extensive experience working for substance misuse services and have been drug workers with service users in the past). Families can be referred into FDACs by the local authorities in cases where substance misuse or domestic violence concerns are a central factor in their current proceedings. The FDAC model, which uses a therapeutic, multidisciplinary approach that combines judicial oversight with extensive support services, has demonstrated positive outcomes for parents with co-occurring substance misuse and domestic violence concerns, including improved reunification of parents with their children (Harwin & Barlow, 2022; Martin, 2023).

Setting and participants

All parents who completed an initial assessment with the service between August 2022 and June 2024 were initially eligible, with analysis restricted to parents with completed and available scores from the ITQ. A total of 122 parents (44 fathers, 78 mothers) were assessed during this period. 93 had sufficient complete data to be included in the analysis.

Cited reasons for non-administration of the ITQ (n = 29) included: administration of an alternative trauma measure (e.g. the Trauma Screening Questionnaire, n = 6); insufficient time to complete measures (n = 10), parent declining to complete due to discomfort (n = 3); or not otherwise specified (n = 10).

Variables

PTSD and CPTSD

PTSD and CPTSD were assessed using the International Trauma Questionnaire (ITQ; (Cloitre et al., 2018)), which assesses ICD-11 PTSD and CPTSD across 18 items, each rated on a five-point Likert scale. PTSD is defined by symptom clusters of re-experiencing, avoidance, threat and functional impairment, while CPTSD requires the additional endorsement of affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, and relational disturbances. A score of ≥2 indicates symptom endorsement, and individuals can meet criteria for either probable PTSD or probable CPTSD, but not both (Cloitre et al., 2018). The ITQ demonstrates a strong internal consistency across diverse samples, with a meta-analytic Cronbach’s alpha ranging from approximately 0.85–0.90 (Nvo-Fernández et al., 2025). Throughout the remainder of the paper, CPTSD and PTSD are used, referring to a probable diagnosis.

Anxiety and depression

These were assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) (Spitzer et al., 2006), and the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) (Kroenke et al., 2001). Both tools examine symptoms spanning the past two weeks, and their clinical cut offs were used to establish their probable diagnoses (GAD-7: ≥ 10, PHQ-9: ≥ 15). The PHQ-9 and GAD-7 both demonstrate high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = .76–.92 and .92 respectively) (Chae et al., 2025; Spitzer et al., 2006)

Substance misuse history

Data pertaining to participants’ current and/or historical substance use was retrieved from service assessment reports. Consistent with existing service processes, substance misuse difficulties were assessed and reported by FDAC substance misuse specialists. To meet the criteria for substance misuse, parents had to disclose a pattern of using alcohol or class A (heroin, cocaine, ecstasy), B (cannabis and amphetamines), or C (anabolic steroids and some tranquilizers) drugs in a way that was disruptive to their daily lives, or surpassed the recommended limit by doctors. Additional information was taken from police and social service reports that highlighted parents’ problematic relationships with substances. If a parent denied substance misuse difficulties during their assessment, however, there was a notable presence of problematic use in their police or social service reports, it was coded as present. If participants had both current and previous historical use, they were categorized as ‘current’. If participants had a documented or disclosed history of substance misuse, but did not disclose any current use, nor were there disclosures noted in adjacent reports surrounding current substance misuse, they were categorized as historical.

Domestic violence victimization

Domestic violence data was also retrieved from existing FDAC records and initial assessment reports. Presence of domestic violence was identified if a parent had disclosed instances of physical, sexual, emotional or verbal abuse from their families or partners, as well as if there were documented instances of abuse from police and/or social service records in the report. Domestic violence perpetration was not included in this variable.

Demographics

Demographic variables – age (in years), gender (female vs male), ethnicity (Black, Asian, Minority / Ethnic (BAME)/other vs white British) – were retrieved from routinely collected service record data.

Procedures

Questionnaires (e.g. ITQ, PHQ-9, GAD-7) were administered by FDAC staff as part of the routine initial assessment process. All staff had received specific training in the administration and scoring of these questionnaires. All data was then extracted from existing assessment reports and clinical service data by researchers. Data was retrospectively extracted weekly, starting in May 2023 and completing on June 15th, 2024. Any discrepancies or queries relating to data coding were raised in consultation by the senior author (EFI). All data was anonymized using unique ID numbers and extracted and inputted using a data processing software (Excel, SPSS).

Statistical analysis

We describe the characteristics of each group (PTSD, CPTSD, and no disorder) with frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and means and standard deviation for age. We report the proportions of individuals with PTSD and CPTSD alongside their respective 95% Wilson confidence intervals (CI) (Wilson, 1942).

We compared the demographic (age and sex) and clinical characteristics (presence of: depression, anxiety, substance misuse, and domestic violence) between groups (first, PTSD or CPTSD vs none; and second, CPTSD vs PTSD) using a series of crude and adjusted logistic regression models. All models were adjusted for age and gender. We report crude and adjusted odds ratios (OR) with their respective 95% CIs. All analyses were conducted in Stata 19 (StataCorp, 2025).

Results

Our final sample included N = 93 parents, aged from 21–61 years, with a mean age of 37 (sd = 8.0). The majority were mothers (N = 62, 66.7%), reporting having experienced domestic violence (N = 42, 82.4%), and substance misuse (N = 82, 90.1%). 43% (n = 40) and 61% (n = 57) of the parents met criteria for anxiety and depression, respectively.

19.4% (95% Wilson CI 12.6–28.5%; n = 18), and 36.6% (95% Wilson CI 27.5–46.7%; n = 34) of the parents met the clinical threshold for PTSD and CPTSD, respectively, according to the ITQ. PTSD and CPTSD were more prevalent among mothers, the younger age groups, and those reporting clinically significant levels of depression and substance misuse (see Table 1). Anxiety was particularly prevalent among those with CPTSD (N = 23, 67.7%), compared to those with PTSD (N = 8, 44.4%) or no disorder (N = 9, 22.0%) (Table 1). Discussion

We aimed to examine the observed rates of ICD-11 PTSD and CPTSD in a CP family court context. We found that the proportions of cases with probable PTSD and CPTSD were high (19.4% and 36.6% respectively), with approximately a third of the sample meeting ICD-11 criteria for probable CPTSD. These disorders were not found to differ significantly by age or gender of parents. We also found evidence of significant comorbidity between CPTSD and other common mental health difficulties (anxiety, depression), but not PTSD. Of note, we did not find a relationship between ICD-11 PTSD/CPTSD and substance misuse or domestic violence difficulties. This investigation represents the first study, to our knowledge, to estimate the observed rates of ICD-11 PTSD and CPTSD in a CP setting.

Our findings reiterate past research highlighting that parents subject to child protection proceedings are often a highly complex and traumatized group (Suomi et al., 2023). The prevalence of PTSD identified in our study (16% in men and 21% in women) was comparable to previous estimates from a study of parents in child protection proceedings (Suomi et al., 2023) which found a PTSD prevalence of 26% among mothers and 13% in fathers. We also found higher rates of CPTSD compared to PTSD, which aligns with existing literature suggesting that CPTSD is more prevalent than PTSD in treatment seeking populations in the U.K. (Karatzias et al., 2019; Karatzias et al., 2022; Letica-Crepulja et al., 2020; Murphy et al., 2021). The higher rates of CPTSD documented could be partly attributed to the complex trauma histories of the parents being assessed, as past research highlights that many parents involved in CP proceedings were also placed in CP services during their youth and report higher rates of victimization and interpersonal trauma (Blakey & Glaude, 2021; Chemtob et al., 2011; Kaplan et al., 2019; Lewin et al., 2010; Maercker et al., 2022; Mason et al., 2020; Ostrow et al., 2021; Suomi et al., 2023). CPTSD has been categorized as a more debilitating condition than PTSD; as such, findings may provide insight into the obstacles parents face in adequately caring for their children and engaging with proceedings (Lewis et al., 2022; Murphy et al., 2021). For example, the added barrier of disturbances of self-organization in individuals with CPTSD, including negative self-concept and relationship disturbances, may interfere with the development of a therapeutic alliance and increase the likelihood of non-attendance and disengagement within a CP service context (Bachem et al., 2021).

In our study, rates of both PTSD and CPTSD were higher among mothers compared to fathers, consistent with past research from child protection proceeding contexts (Suomi et al., 2023), and the wider literature on PTSD and CPTSD prevalence (Huynh et al., 2025). More specifically, our study’s findings were consistent with the most recent meta-analysis conducted on the prevalence of CPTSD in treatment seeking populations globally, which indicated that although women have significantly higher odds of probable CPTSD than men, gender was not a significant predictor (Huynh et al., 2025). Few studies have explored the mental health of fathers within child protection systems. One study that did include fathers identified a strong association between PTSD symptom severity and child abuse perpetration potential in mothers and fathers, with a higher association in fathers (Fredman et al., 2019). This, coupled with our findings, highlights the importance of screening fathers routinely for trauma and PTSD/CPTSD in these samples, as previous research has evidenced that trauma, particularly abuse histories, is not as routinely investigated in males compared to females in treatment settings (Huntley et al., 2020).

Consistent with previous research, parents with CPTSD and / or PTSD in our sample were significantly more likely to meet criteria for anxiety and depression compared to those with no PTSD or CPTSD diagnosis (Gilbar, 2020; Hyland et al., 2017; Hyland et al., 2018; Karatzias et al., 2021; Lewis et al., 2022). There are no associations between anxiety and depression for CPTSD compared to PTSD, which differs from past research indicating that CPTSD is associated with higher rates of comorbidity amongst complex clinical and forensic samples (Facer-Irwin et al., 2022; Karatzias et al., 2019; Lewis et al., 2022; Murphy et al., 2021).

Somewhat surprisingly, neither PTSD nor CPTSD was significantly associated with substance misuse difficulties in our study, in contrast with past research suggesting high levels of comorbidity between them (Facer-Irwin et al., 2022; Harvey et al., 2025; Karatzias et al., 2019). However, this null finding likely reflects insufficient variability in substance misuse rather than a true absence of association. The presence of substance use was uniformly high across all groups: 94% of parents with PTSD and in 97% of parents with CPTSD, and 82% of parents without either disorder reported substance misuse difficulties. This ceiling effect would preclude detection of any meaningful statistical association between the variables. The high overlap between substance misuse and PTSD/CPTSD does reiterate past research which has highlighted that individuals who have experienced trauma often use substances to cope with their dysregulated emotions and trauma sequelae (Karatzias et al., 2021; Renaud et al., 2021). Comorbidity is a significant issue which can impede assessment and treatment of mental health disorders (Bahji, 2024). This comorbidity among parents in CP proceedings complicates assessment and can reduce engagement with services, highlighting the need for integrated, trauma-informed interventions. Addressing these issues simultaneously may improve parental engagement, mental health outcomes, and overall family safety. Future research is needed to examine the co-occurrence of trauma-related disorders and substance misuse within CP populations and the effects this has on proceeding outcomes.

It is also surprising that despite the often chronic and prolonged nature of domestic violence experiences, there was no observed association with CPTSD in this study. However, similar to the issues with substance misuse above, the prevalence of domestic violence was high (82% of parents reported domestic violence), and the lack of association may reflect this ceiling effect rather than a true absence of association. Past research highlights an exposure rate of domestic violence for mothers in CP proceedings to be approximately 62.6% (Lewin et al., 2010), which is lower than the prevalence we found in our sample. These elevated rates highlight the need for more thorough screening and risk assessment of domestic violence for both men and women in CP settings, and the importance of interventions that address both cumulative trauma and current safety-related needs.

The high rates of PTSD/CPTSD (19.4% and 36.6% respectively) identified in this study validate previous research that has indicated that parents involved in child protection proceedings are a highly traumatized group of individuals, with complex, intersecting needs (Suomi et al., 2023). Our results emphasize the need for the continued development of holistic, trauma-informed support for parents involved in these proceedings, as well as the ongoing development and implementation of trauma-informed courts. This is especially important given that CP proceedings have been identified as being distressing and traumatic for parents involved (Dumbrill, 2006; Philip et al., 2024), and the initiation of proceedings may activate parents’ existing trauma histories.

Routine screening and assessment of PTSD and CPTSD across CP settings should be made a clinical priority. Not only could this help to facilitate a higher level of engagement and recovery across parents subject to CP proceedings; it could also provide clinicians with an opportunity to target symptoms that may be contributing to child maltreatment, which in turn could improve mental health outcomes for vulnerable children (Blakey & Glaude, 2021; Mason et al., 2020; Schechter et al., 2017). Furthermore, improved identification of these disorders is especially important given the challenges of treating comorbid substance misuse within this population. Research has shown that treatment outcomes for people diagnosed with both PTSD and substance use disorder (SUD) are poorer than those diagnosed with only one disorder, and there are currently no universally accepted guidelines to treating this comorbidity (Roberts et al., 2022). In complex cases, it has been suggested that a case management and formulation approach is necessary to plan and coordinate responses to primary needs (Roberts et al., 2022). This is likely the best approach for the on-going development of trauma-informed family courts, due to the complex presentations and needs of parents involved.

Limitations

As a preliminary, descriptive and observational study, our investigation had a number of methodological limitations. This evaluation relied on previously collected, routinely administered data retrieved from existing service records, and as such we were limited both in terms of the quality of the data and the variables we could include in analysis. For example, the substance misuse variable lacked specificity regarding substance type, limiting interpretation of the findings. Future research should incorporate more detailed substance use categorization to examine prevalence patterns and their relationship to court outcomes and PTSD/CPTSD diagnoses. Furthermore, out of 122 parents assessed during the study timeframe, only 93 had available ITQ data. As our wide confidence intervals indicated, this reduced the sample size that was available and restricted binary and multivariate logistic regressions, impacting the conclusions that can be drawn from our investigation. Missing data for some variables (e.g. ethnicity) also precluded a thorough examination of relevant demographic associations.

Another limitation to this study relates to the scope of available data regarding both treatment engagement and court-related outcomes. Despite parents taking place in evidence-based interventions through FDAC, the dataset did not include information that specified whether individuals who screened positive for conditions such as generalized anxiety disorder, depression, PTSD, CPTSD or substance misuse subsequently received, engaged with, or completed targeted evidence-based treatments. Consequently, it was not possible to determine the extent to which identified clinical needs were translated into intervention provisions. Additionally, the dataset did not capture information regarding court outcomes or child-related characteristics (e.g. number or age of children), limiting the ability to examine associations between parental mental health needs or case outcomes, such as child placement decisions and court decisions. This was partly due to when the data was collected, as some cases remained ongoing and final court outcomes were not yet available.

The assessment and screening of PTSD/CPTSD in the present study was completed using self-report tools administered by service practitioners as part of routine assessment procedures, which also risked introducing measurement bias, particularly relating to over-inflation of symptoms and inter-rater reliability. All questionnaires were delivered in interview format, potentially reducing barriers linked to literacy, and initial training in the tool’s administration and scoring were implemented prior to the tool being introduced. Nonetheless, it is unclear if there is consistency in the ways practitioners score and administer the tools. Past research has identified several barriers that mental health workers face in asking about traumatic experiences, many of which relate to discomfort or lack of training, which may have resulted in variations to the measurement of key variables (Haugen et al., 2017). The context and setting of this study also risk introduction of reporting bias. As parents completed the questionnaires as part of a parenting assessment at the beginning of their involvement with CP proceedings, this may have influenced their responses, leading potentially to either underreporting or overreporting of symptoms to present themselves more favourably. Specifically, as CP proceedings have been identified as being traumatic for parents involved, rates may have been inflated due to activation or re-traumatization caused by proceedings. Equally, there is a risk that parents had motivation to downplay their mental health concerns and under-report due to the nature of the setting. Future studies would benefit from assessing PTSD and CPTSD using alternative interview-based measures, such as the International Trauma Interview (Seiler et al., 2023).

Conclusion

As research continues to validate the presence of co-occurring parental trauma and substance misuse difficulties in CP systems, there is an increased need for more trauma-informed family courts, and robust whole person care services supporting parents within these systems. Findings from this study reiterate the need for further evaluation and development of similar trauma-informed courts, given their potential, to reduce or prevent re-traumatization and improve outcomes for families.
