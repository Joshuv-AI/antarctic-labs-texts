Cost–Effectiveness Analysis of Bimekizumab against Interleukin-23 Inhibitors in Patients with Plaque Psoriasis in Japan
Authors: Atsuyuki Igarashi, Koichi Shiraishi, Shota Saito, Tomoya Kudo, Yukihiko Iizuka, Eiko Yamamura, Masakazu Hase
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPsoriasis is a chronic, inflammatory skin disease that significantly impairs patients’ quality of life (QOL). Several biologics with varying mechanisms of action are approved for treating moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis (PSO) in Japan; bimekizumab is the only one that selectively binds to and inhibits interleukin (IL)-17F in addition to IL-17A. However, there is currently no recommended biologics treatment sequence, and their high cost limits treatment accessibility. This study evaluated bimekizumab cost–effectiveness versus IL-23 inhibitors in patients with PSO in Japan from a public healthcare payer’s perspective, and explored variables that affect cost–effectiveness.MethodsA cohort simulation, lifetime Markov model with 2-week cycles simulated the treatment pathway in adults with moderate-to-severe PSO who had an inadequate response to previous treatments. Bimekizumab was compared with the IL-23p19 inhibitors guselkumab, risankizumab, and tildrakizumab in the first line; brodalumab was the second-line treatment for all arms. Transition to second-line treatment was triggered by not achieving a ≥ 75% improvement in Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI 75), or discontinuation due to adverse events. A network meta-analysis provided PASI response rates. QOL scores were derived from EuroQol 5-Dimension 3-Level health questionnaire responses from global bimekizumab phase 3 studies. Drug and management costs were estimated on the basis of Diagnosis Procedure Combination-based data in Japan; 2%/year discounting was applied to costs and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). Incremental cost–effectiveness ratios (ICERs) were calculated, and sensitivity and scenario analyses performed.ResultsBimekizumab generated the highest number of QALYs and was cost–effective against all IL-23 inhibitor comparators at a willingness-to-pay threshold of ¥5,000,000/QALY (bimekizumab versus guselkumab: ¥3,202,863/QALY; risankizumab: ¥4,732,268/QALY; tildrakizumab: ¥4,950,972/QALY). ICERs were sensitive to QOL scores, with PASI 100 QOL score being a key cost–effectiveness driver.ConclusionsFindings of the cost–effectiveness of bimekizumab against IL-23 inhibitors support the potential consideration of bimekizumab as a first-line treatment for moderate-to-severe PSO in Japan.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Bimekizumab, Cost–effectiveness analysis, Interleukin-17 inhibitor, Interleukin-23 inhibitor, Japan, Psoriasis

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Biologics are highly effective and tolerable options for the treatment of moderate-to-severe plaque psoriasis (PSO) in Japan, however the high costs of biologics contribute to increasing strain on Japan’s National Health Insurance system.This study evaluated the cost–effectiveness of the interleukin (IL)-17A and IL-17F inhibitor bimekizumab versus IL-23 inhibitors (guselkumab, risankizumab, and tildrakizumab) in patients with moderate-to-severe PSO in Japan, from a public healthcare payer’s perspective. What was learned from the study? Bimekizumab generated the highest number of quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) and was cost–effective against all IL-23 inhibitor comparators at a willingness-to-pay threshold of ¥5,000,000/QALY.The quality-of-life (QOL) score attributed to complete skin clearance (i.e., 100% improvement in Psoriasis Area and Severity Index) was a key driver of cost–effectiveness.Study findings indicate that it may be appropriate to consider bimekizumab as a cost–effective first-line treatment option for PSO in Japan, and highlight the importance of appropriately measuring the QOL benefit of high-efficacy PSO treatments in cost–effectiveness analyses.

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic, systemic, immune-mediated inflammatory disease that affects both the skin and joints [1–3]. The prevalence of psoriasis ranges from 0.13% in Southeast Asia to 1.88% in Western Europe [4], and has been reported at approximately 0.34% in Japan [5]. Plaque psoriasis (PSO) is the most common form, presenting as well-demarcated, erythematous areas of inflamed skin covered with silvery-white scales [2, 3]. Psoriasis is linked with social stigmatization, pain, physical disability, psychological distress, and work impairment, and has a significant detrimental effect on quality of life (QOL) [2, 6, 7]. In Japan, 21% of patients with psoriasis reported their disease as having a “very large” to “extremely large” impact on their QOL, with the impact of skin problems on QOL being greater in patients with moderate and severe psoriasis than mild psoriasis [7]. Consequently, the improvement of skin lesions has been associated with improvements in psychological and social aspects of QOL. One of the most frequently employed tools to evaluate disease severity and assess treatment success is the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI), which combines the assessment of lesion severity and the body surface area affected into a score ranging from 0 (no disease) to 72 (maximal disease severity) [3, 8]. Recent advances in treatment have made a 100% improvement from baseline in PASI (PASI 100; i.e., complete skin clearance), which is associated with a 93% achievement of a Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) score of 0 or 1, a realistic treatment goal [8–12].

Current treatments for psoriasis include topical treatments, phototherapy, and systemic agents [2, 3], and approximately 20–30% of patients have moderate-to-severe disease that requires systemic therapy beyond topical treatments [3]. This is of particular concern in Japan, where approximately one-third of patients with psoriasis have > 10% body surface area (BSA) affected and 26.6% of patients are treated with nonbiologic oral medication and 18.6% with biologics [13]. Among systemic therapies, biologics have revolutionized PSO management by offering highly effective and tolerable treatment options for patients who fail to respond to traditional therapies [14]. However, the high costs of biologics, especially in patients who switch or discontinue treatment, contribute to increasing medical expenditure in Japan and may place strain on Japan’s National Health Insurance system [15–18]. Studies analyzing Japan Medical Data Center claims data have reported direct medical costs of up to ¥11.3 million per patient-year in patients with psoriatic disease treated with biologics in Japan, demonstrating the high economic burden of more severe forms of psoriasis [15, 19]. It is therefore important to assess the cost–effectiveness of available treatment options for psoriasis, to aid healthcare providers in making informed decisions about treatment selection to optimize healthcare expenditure in Japan.

A total of 11 biologics have been approved for the treatment of psoriasis in Japan, including tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors (adalimumab, certolizumab pegol, infliximab), interleukin (IL)-12/23p40 inhibitors (ustekinumab), IL-17A inhibitors (ixekizumab, secukinumab), IL-17A/F inhibitor (bimekizumab), IL-17 receptor A inhibitor (brodalumab), and IL-23p19 inhibitors (guselkumab, risankizumab, tildrakizumab; hereafter collectively referred to as “IL-23 inhibitors”) [20]. IL inhibitors, in particular IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors, have shown superior efficacy in PSO compared with TNF inhibitors in moderate-to-severe PSO [14, 21]. Bimekizumab is a monoclonal IgG1 antibody that selectively binds to and inhibits IL-17F in addition to IL-17A; on the basis of in vitro data, this dual inhibition provides a greater reduction of inflammation compared with single inhibition of IL-17A [22]. In global phase 3 clinical trials, bimekizumab demonstrated significant and rapid efficacy improvements over both placebo and comparators with different mechanisms of action (IL-12/23p40, IL-17A, and TNF inhibitors) [23–26]. A systematic literature review and network meta-analysis on global data also demonstrated statistical superiority of bimekizumab over all other biologics in achieving PASI 90 and PASI 100 [14]. Bimekizumab was approved in Japan in 2022 for the treatment of PSO, pustular psoriasis, and psoriatic erythroderma in patients with insufficient response to conventional therapies [27]. While the cost–effectiveness of bimekizumab over the IL-17A inhibitor ixekizumab has been demonstrated [28], the economic value of bimekizumab has not been assessed against IL-23 inhibitors, which are common competing treatment options in clinical practice in Japan given their similar high efficacy to IL-17 inhibitors [14]. This analysis therefore aimed to evaluate the cost–effectiveness of bimekizumab (IL-17A/F inhibitor) against IL-23 inhibitors in patients with moderate-to-severe PSO in Japan, and explore the variables that affect cost–effectiveness.

Methods

Population

The target population for this analysis included adult patients ≥ 18 years of age with moderate-to-severe PSO, who had an inadequate response to previous treatments and were eligible for bimekizumab. Moderate-to-severe PSO was defined by PASI score ≥ 12, ≥ 10% BSA affected by psoriasis, and Investigator’s Global Assessment (IGA) score ≥ 3 (on a 5-point scale, with “0” representing complete clearance and “4” representing the most severe psoriasis). The baseline characteristics of the modeled population were based on 107 Japanese adult participants in the BE VIVID phase 3 trial of bimekizumab in PSO [29] (Table 1) who met the specified criteria for the analysis target population.

Intervention and Comparators

Bimekizumab (320 mg every 4 weeks [Q4W] for 16 weeks, then every 8 weeks [Q8W]) was compared with the three IL-23 inhibitors available in Japan: guselkumab (100 mg at weeks 0 and 4, then Q8W), risankizumab (150 mg at weeks 0 and 4, then every 12 weeks [Q12W]), and tildrakizumab (100 mg at weeks 0 and 4, then Q12W) [30]. In the model, the treatment pathway consisted of bimekizumab or IL-23 inhibitors in the first line; in the case of first-line treatment discontinuation, the IL-17 inhibitor brodalumab (210 mg every 2 weeks) was applied in the second line for all arms. The same second-line drug (brodalumab) was selected in all analyses to minimize the influence of second-line treatment on model outcomes, given that the Japanese guidance for the use of biologics in psoriasis does not include a drug sequence for treatment [20]. To allow the comparison of bimekizumab with IL-23 inhibitors, and to reflect the increasing use of higher-efficacy IL inhibitors over TNF inhibitors in clinical practice, a biologic which also inhibits the IL-17 pathway was chosen as the second-line treatment. Brodalumab was selected as it binds to the IL-17A receptor to block the activity of IL-17A, C, E, and F, whereas secukinumab and ixekizumab both inhibit only IL-17A [31, 32]. Furthermore, a network meta-analysis of biologics approved for moderate-to-severe PSO demonstrated that the efficacy of brodalumab (in terms of PASI 90 and PASI 100 achievement rates) is the highest after bimekizumab [30]. Only two lines of treatment were modeled in this analysis, given that the majority of patients with PSO in Japan receive two or fewer lines of biologics (unpublished database analysis).

The following health states were considered for each treatment line in the Markov model: induction treatment, maintenance treatment (stratified by PASI improvement into five health states: PASI < 50, PASI 50– < 75, PASI 75– < 90, PASI 90– < 100, PASI 100; a health state of PASI 50– < 75 represents a health state where the PASI score has improved by 50– < 75% from baseline), and death (Fig. 1). The model started with a 16-week induction phase on first-line treatment; treatment response (measured using PASI improvement from baseline) was assessed at 16 weeks, at which point transition to the maintenance phase or second-line induction treatment occurred. Excess mortality due to PSO was not considered.

Model Inputs

Responder Criteria

At the end of the induction phase in the first-line treatment, patients achieving PASI 75 were considered responders, and transitioned to one of the three first-line maintenance health states (PASI 100, PASI 90– < 100, or PASI 75– < 90); they remained on first-line maintenance treatment and stayed in this health state unless treatment was discontinued (Fig. 1). Patients not achieving PASI 75 were considered nonresponders and switched to second-line treatment.

The PASI improvement achievement rates of each biologic treatment were obtained from a systemic literature review and network meta-analysis comparing the short-term efficacy of bimekizumab versus other biologic systemic therapies for moderate-to-severe PSO (Table 3) [14]. For this cost–effectiveness analysis, the systematic literature review search period and network meta-analysis were updated to include publications up to 4 August 2022 [30]. The updated network meta-analysis included 105 randomized controlled trials evaluating PASI achievement rates from 10 to 16 weeks [30].

Discontinuations

During the induction phase of first-line treatment, patients who discontinued treatment owing to adverse events (AEs) switched to second-line treatment (Fig. 1); discontinuation rates owing to AEs in the induction phase were derived from clinical trials of the respective treatments (Table 3) [33–36]. In the first-line maintenance phase, all-cause treatment discontinuation rates were estimated from a multicenter retrospective study of bimekizumab [37], and a meta-analysis of drug survival of IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors for the other treatments (Table 3) [38]. Treatment discontinuation rates were assumed to be the same regardless of PASI improvement health state. Discontinuation was not considered in the maintenance phase of second-line treatment. Since excess mortality due to PSO was not considered, the mortality rate in the analysis model was set on the basis of the 2021 abridged life tables for Japan [39].

Quality of Life Scores (Utilities)

The QOL scores for each PASI improvement health state were estimated using pooled data from bimekizumab clinical trials (BE SURE, BE VIVID, BE READY, and BE RADIANT) through repeated measures analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) (Table 3) [23–26]. The EuroQol 5-Dimension 3-Level health questionnaire (EQ-5D-3L) was used to derive QOL scores, with Japanese tariffs applied. Reduction in QOL values owing to AEs was not considered in this analysis, on the basis of the expert opinion of local specialists that AEs do not result in significant QOL reduction. QOL scores were used to calculate quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) as a measure of drug effectiveness.

Costs and Healthcare Resource Use

Direct costs related to treatment with biologics, including drug and management costs, were calculated on the basis of standard treatment processes. These included costs associated with the treatment of key AEs of interest (injection site reactions, oral candidiasis, and serious infections), as recommended by clinical experts. Resource consumption items and quantities were estimated through interviews with three specialists familiar with PSO treatment, and AE incidence rates were based on both Japanese and global clinical trial data or Pharmaceutical Interview Forms [23–26, 34, 40–45] (Table 3). Resource consumption costs and AE treatment costs were estimated from Diagnosis Procedure Combination-based data in Japan, and based on the medical service fee points and drug prices as of February 2024 [46]. Costs and effectiveness were discounted at an annual rate of 2%, as stipulated by the Japanese cost–effectiveness evaluation guidelines [47].

Data Analysis and Model Validation

Costs and QALYs were reported for each biologic treatment pathway, and the incremental cost–effectiveness ratios (ICERs) of bimekizumab compared with each of the three IL-23 inhibitors were estimated. ICER represents the cost of extending survival for one QALY, and serves as an indicator of the cost–effectiveness of a drug of interest versus a comparator; treatments associated with greater QALYs at lower costs are considered “dominant.” In Japan, a drug is considered cost–effective if the ICER is less than the willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold of ¥5,000,000/QALY [48].

Sensitivity Analyses

Deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the uncertainty and robustness of the analysis model. In the univariate deterministic sensitivity analysis, model input parameters were varied to the upper and lower bounds of 95% confidence intervals or by ± 20% of the base-case value, in order to identify parameters with the greatest effect on the model results. Incremental net monetary benefit (INMB), which measures the difference in NMB between alternative interventions, was used as an indicator of cost–effectiveness; a positive INMB indicates that the ICER is below the ¥5,000,000/QALY WTP threshold (i.e., bimekizumab is cost–effective compared with the IL-23 inhibitor comparator). INMB is calculated as (incremental benefit [QALY] × ICER threshold [¥5,000,000/QALY]) − incremental cost.

In the probabilistic sensitivity analysis, probability distributions were assigned to the model input parameters and 1000 Monte Carlo simulations were run in each pairwise comparison. Statistical distributions assigned to the model input parameters are indicated in Tables 1 and 3.

Scenario Analysis

Given that the clinical guidance for the use of biologics in PSO in Japan lacks a standardized treatment algorithm [20], there may be uncertainty associated with using brodalumab in the second line across all base-case comparisons. A scenario analysis was therefore conducted to evaluate the cost–effectiveness of one line of treatment only (i.e., bimekizumab versus IL-23 inhibitors), to eliminate the influence of second-line treatment on model outcomes and assess the robustness of the base-case results.

Ethics Statement

This study did not require ethical approval, as the analyses performed were based on mathematical modeling with inputs extracted from existing literature or unpublished, aggregated data. All data referenced were anonymized and contained no personal information.

Results

Base-Case Analysis

The results of the base-case analysis are shown in Fig. 2. Compared with the IL-23 inhibitor comparators, bimekizumab generated the highest number of QALYs (20.98) over a lifetime, at a total cost of ¥47,200,088. Bimekizumab yielded an additional 0.07 QALYs than guselkumab at an incremental cost of ¥225,241, with an ICER of ¥3,202,863/QALY. Compared with risankizumab, bimekizumab yielded an additional 0.04 QALYs at an incremental cost of ¥189,727, with an ICER of ¥4,732,268/QALY. Compared with tildrakizumab, the incremental QALYs and incremental costs for bimekizumab were 0.10 QALYs and ¥492,530, respectively, with an ICER of ¥4,950,972/QALY. In all three comparisons, the ICER for bimekizumab was less than the ¥5,000,000/QALY WTP threshold, indicating that bimekizumab is a cost–effective treatment compared with all IL-23 inhibitors, thereby justifying the additional cost of bimekizumab.

Sensitivity Analyses

Deterministic Univariate Sensitivity Analysis

The univariate sensitivity analysis results are presented in Fig. 3. A positive INMB indicates that bimekizumab is cost–effective versus the IL-23 inhibitor comparator (i.e., ICER is below the ¥5,000,000/QALY WTP threshold), while a negative INMB indicates that bimekizumab is not cost–effective. Although the ranking of the parameters with the greatest impact on cost–effectiveness varied depending on the IL-23 inhibitor used as comparator, QOL scores were a key driver of cost–effectiveness across all comparisons. In particular, the QOL score of PASI 100 was among the top three most influential parameters for all bimekizumab versus IL-23 inhibitor comparisons. It was found that small changes in PASI 100 QOL score translated to large variations in ICER: in the bimekizumab versus risankizumab comparison, a 0.01 decrease or increase from base case (ICER: ¥4,732,268/QALY) in the PASI 100 QOL score led to ICERs of ¥6,516,437/QALY (INMB: –¥44,151) or ¥3,715,093/QALY (INMB: ¥65,619), respectively (Table 4).

Discussion

While existing studies have reported on the high costs of biologic treatment for psoriasis in Japan [15, 17, 19], few have considered the impact of patient QOL on the cost–effectiveness of biologic treatments. To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study assessing the cost–effectiveness of bimekizumab, the only selective inhibitor of both IL-17A and IL-17F, compared with IL-23 inhibitors for the treatment of moderate-to-severe PSO, both in Japan and globally. The results of the base case and scenario analyses suggest that bimekizumab is cost–effective compared with IL-23 inhibitors, despite modest differences in QALY, from the Japanese public healthcare payer’s perspective at a WTP threshold of ¥5,000,000/QALY. Under base-case assumptions, the ICERs of bimekizumab versus guselkumab, risankizumab, and tildrakizumab were ¥3,202,863/QALY, ¥4,732,268/QALY, and ¥4,950,972/QALY, respectively.

There are currently over ten biologics available for PSO treatment in Japan, with bimekizumab being the newest biologic approved for this indication as of 2022 [20]. However, there are no established national guidelines for treatment sequencing. Previous studies have reported the cost–effectiveness of risankizumab and secukinumab versus other biologics in PSO in Japan [49, 50], and cost–effectiveness could also be an important factor in the evaluation of bimekizumab as a relevant treatment strategy for patients with PSO. The superior clinical efficacy of bimekizumab versus adalimumab (TNF inhibitor), secukinumab (IL-17A inhibitor), and ustekinumab (IL-12/23p40 inhibitor) has been demonstrated in phase 3 trials [24–26]. A subsequent network meta-analysis reported that IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors were the most effective treatments for moderate-to-severe PSO across all PASI levels, with bimekizumab in particular demonstrating statistical superiority over all other biologics in achieving high rates of skin clearance at 10–16 weeks [14]. This was maintained in the long term—a separate network meta-analysis reported that, versus all other biologics except risankizumab 150 mg, bimekizumab achieved a significantly greater cumulative average number of days of PASI 100 response over 1 year of treatment [51]. The economic value of bimekizumab in the Japanese PSO treatment landscape is underscored by a prior analysis demonstrating the cost–effectiveness of bimekizumab over the IL-17A inhibitor ixekizumab [28], and the current analysis supporting bimekizumab as a cost–effective treatment option over IL-23 inhibitors. In addition, bimekizumab was assessed to be cost–effective relative to most other reimbursed treatments (including IL-17A, IL-23, Janus kinase, and TNF inhibitors) in both biologic-naive and TNF inhibitor-experienced patients with psoriatic arthritis (PsA) in Sweden [52]. This is notable, given that up to 30% of patients with psoriasis subsequently develop PsA, with the IL-23/17 axis playing a critical pathogenic role in both diseases [53]. A systematic literature review of the cost–effectiveness of PsA treatments also concluded that bimekizumab, alongside ixekizumab, appears to provide the most economic benefit among available biologic therapies [54].

Notably, the results of the sensitivity analyses suggest that ICERs were particularly sensitive to QOL scores. In particular, PASI 100 QOL score may be an influential parameter in the cost–effectiveness evaluation of PSO treatments, especially for drugs with high PASI 100 achievement rates such as bimekizumab; our study showed that small changes in PASI 100 QOL scores can result in substantial variations in bimekizumab versus IL-23 inhibitor ICERs. Compared with high levels of efficacy nearing but short of complete skin clearance, complete skin clearance (i.e., PASI 100) is associated with greater improvements in patient-reported symptoms, increased proportions of symptom-free days, and significant improvements in health-related QOL [12]. Given that PASI 100 is now often regarded as the ultimate or ideal goal of PSO treatment [8–11], it is important to use an appropriate preference-based measure (PBM) to accurately estimate how PASI 100 attainment translates to QOL improvements in cost–effectiveness analyses of PSO treatments, with implications on real-world practice. One such PBM is the EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level health questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L), which is recommended by the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare for deriving QOL scores [47]. In the current analysis, the EQ-5D-3L with Japanese tariffs was used to derive QOL scores. However, a literature review has noted that the EQ-5D was unable to discriminate between PASI 90– < 100 and PASI 100 QOL scores in PSO [55], despite a recent study reporting that incremental improvements in skin clearance from PASI 90 to PASI 100 translate into additional QOL benefits (as measured by the DLQI) [12]. Consequently, PSO QOL scores derived from EQ-5D responses may not adequately capture the clinical benefit of a treatment capable of achieving high levels of skin clearance. There is therefore a need to identify appropriate QOL scoring methods for PASI 100 in order to accurately illustrate to healthcare payers the “true drug value” of PSO treatments, particularly those with high PASI 100 achievement rates such as bimekizumab. A study has shown that the EQ-PSO, which adds psoriasis-specific dimensions to the EQ-5D-5L, is more responsive to improvements in skin clarity at high PASI levels, and therefore could potentially generate QOL scores that better reflect the treatment benefit of high-efficacy therapies [55].

Strengths and Limitations

One of this study’s strengths is its evaluation of bimekizumab (inhibitor of IL-17F in addition to IL-17A) versus all IL-23 inhibitors currently approved in Japan, thereby providing evidence for the economic value of bimekizumab’s unique mechanism of action [22]. In addition, the use of local Japanese data to derive healthcare resource use and costs, and the public healthcare payer perspective adopted (in line with the Japanese guidelines for cost–effectiveness analysis), support the relevance of study results to the Japanese healthcare system. The cost–effectiveness model used in this analysis also benefits from being based on the York modeling framework [56], which is a widely adopted and validated method for assessing the economic value of therapies in PSO worldwide. The results of the sensitivity analyses, which align with the base-case conclusions of the cost–effectiveness of bimekizumab over IL-23 inhibitors, further confirm the robustness of the model.

Nevertheless, the results of this study should be interpreted in the context of a few limitations. Economic modeling necessarily simplifies real-world experiences and outcomes, and cannot realistically capture all the potential experiences of patients with PSO in Japan. Firstly, in the absence of relevant, up-to-date Japanese real-world data reflecting the current treatment landscape, most model inputs (e.g., baseline characteristics, QOL scores, and PASI response rates) were based on data from clinical trials or network meta-analyses that may not be fully representative of the real-world clinical setting. The use of global data may further limit the generalizability of model findings to the Japanese population. Nonetheless, this approach was deemed to be suitable as studies have shown similar short-term efficacy/effectiveness of biologic therapy (including IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors) between Asian and white patients, suggesting that there are unlikely to be substantial differences between Japan and global PASI responses [32, 57]. While the use of a network meta-analysis to obtain PASI response rates may have introduced uncertainties into this economic evaluation, this is unavoidable given the absence of head-to-head trials in PSO between the comparators and the lack of efficacy data associated with treatment sequencing in PSO in the current literature. Moreover, deriving unadjusted discontinuation rates due to AEs from individual clinical trials (in the absence of a network meta-analysis of all treatments) does not allow for comparison of the relative cost–effectiveness of IL-23 inhibitors, which falls outside the scope of this analysis. Data on 52-week treatment discontinuation rates were also limited; in particular, deriving maintenance discontinuation rates from a real-world retrospective study for bimekizumab and a meta-analysis of drug survival for the other treatments meant that there was some risk of double counting with the discontinuation rates applied during the induction phase.

Given the absence of treatment sequencing guidelines for PSO in Japan, the choice of brodalumab as second-line treatment in this analysis may not be fully representative of real-world clinical practice, where the choice of second-line treatment is influenced by prior treatment response and tolerability. For example, within-class switching to brodalumab may not be preferred in the case of nonresponse or safety-driven discontinuation in the bimekizumab arm. Modeling brodalumab in the second line therefore potentially limits the relevance of the base case findings. Furthermore, discontinuation was not considered in second-line maintenance treatment given the lack of Japanese data on the duration of second-line treatment, treatment de-escalation and discontinuation following achievement of sustained skin clearance, the rate of transition to third-line treatments, and the overall duration of biologic use. The model therefore assumed lifelong continuation of second-line maintenance treatment, which may not be representative of real-world clinical practice. Nevertheless, the results of the scenario analysis, where only one line of treatment was evaluated to eliminate the above uncertainty surrounding second-line treatment, support the base-case findings of bimekizumab cost–effectiveness versus IL-23 inhibitors.

Finally, while the ICER is the most common summary measure used in economic evaluations, it poses some limitations. For example, since ICER is a pairwise comparison, multiple ICERs need to be calculated for evaluations of three or more strategies; ICERs also cannot be used to rank strategies as a higher/lower ICER does not necessarily imply that a strategy is more/less cost–effective than another [58]. Nevertheless, the use of ICER in this analysis remains relevant in Japan, where the national health technology assessment body uses ICER to evaluate the cost–effectiveness of drugs [47].

Conclusions

Our analysis demonstrated that bimekizumab is cost–effective against IL-23 inhibitors for the treatment of moderate-to-severe PSO, supporting the potential consideration of bimekizumab as a cost–effective first-line treatment option in Japan. Furthermore, PASI 100 QOL score was identified as a key driver of cost–effectiveness, highlighting the importance of using an appropriate PBM that can reflect the clinical benefit of high-efficacy PSO treatments in cost–effectiveness analyses.
