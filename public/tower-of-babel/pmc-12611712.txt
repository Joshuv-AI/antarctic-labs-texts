The Silent Risk of Sudan Virus: A Review Addressing Global Health Vulnerabilities
Authors: Syed Masudur Rahman Dewan, Sakif Ahamed Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimSudan Virus Disease (SVD) is a deadly hemorrhagic fever caused by the Sudan virus, first discovered in Sudan in 1976. This disease has been linked to several outbreaks in sub‐Saharan Africa, particularly in Uganda. The high fatality rate of SVD, ranging from 41% to 70%, emphasizes the critical need for effective preventive measures and treatments. This perspective piece focuses on the challenges faced by low‐income countries in handling SVD, focusing on Uganda's 2025 epidemic and the ongoing global efforts to develop vaccines and therapies.DiscussionSVD spreads primarily through contact with the bodily fluids of infected individuals or animals. The disease's prolonged incubation period complicates early detection and containment. In resource‐limited settings like Uganda, the healthcare system often struggles with inadequate infrastructure and personnel, hindering effective outbreak control. Cultural practices, such as burial rituals involving physical contact with the deceased, further exacerbate transmission. Despite advancements in monoclonal antibody treatments and vaccine development, these solutions are not yet widely available. The slow pace of clinical trials and logistical challenges prevent rapid deployment of these interventions. International collaboration is crucial to address these obstacles. By improving healthcare infrastructure, enhancing early detection systems, and ensuring equitable access to treatments, the global community can better respond to SVD outbreaks. Additionally, public education and cultural adaptation are vital in reducing transmission risks.ConclusionA comprehensive global strategy is needed to tackle SVD, with an emphasis on strengthening healthcare systems, accelerating vaccine development, and improving access to treatments in vulnerable regions. A coordinated response, with international support, can help mitigate future outbreaks and protect at‐risk populations.

1. Background

The Sudan virus is the cause of Sudan Virus Disease (SVD), a severe hemorrhagic fever that is closely linked to Ebola virus (EboV) disease. Since its discovery in Sudan in 1976, SVD has been linked to several epidemics, mostly in sub‐Saharan Africa. There have been six outbreaks in Uganda, most recently in 2025 [1]. The illness is enzootic, meaning that it spreads across the area through animal reservoirs. The high case fatality rate, which frequently ranges from 41% to 70%, emphasizes how urgently better treatment and preventative interventions are needed [2].

The objective of this perspective article is to focus on the major obstacles low‐income nations encounter while trying to control SVD, with an emphasis on the 2025 epidemic in Uganda. It looks at the challenges posed by cultural considerations, healthcare infrastructure, and the necessity of early identification and prompt action. Along with discussing current worldwide initiatives to create effective vaccinations and treatments, the article highlights the value of international cooperation and assistance in enhancing disease prevention and treatment in environments with limited resources.

2. Recent Outbreaks and the 2025 Uganda Epidemic

Particularly worrisome has been the 2025 epidemic in Uganda, where the first case was documented in January. After a nurse in Kampala passed away from the illness, several family members were found to have been affected [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), following the declaration of the Sudan virus disease (SVD) epidemic in Uganda in January 2025, a total of 14 cases have been reported by April 2025, comprising 12 confirmed instances and two plausible cases, resulting in four fatalities (two confirmed and two probable) [1]. 3. Transmission and Early Detection Challenges

Direct contact with an infected person's or animal's body fluids can spread SVD. Additionally, infected surfaces and items can spread the infection. Although it can last up to 21 days, the incubation phase often lasts 7–11 days [1]. Early detection and control efforts are made more difficult by this delayed onset of symptoms. Funeral ceremony attendees, family members, and healthcare professionals are high‐risk populations. This is mainly because of the possibility of direct exposure to the bodily fluids of infected individuals, both living and deceased, which may happen during caregiving, burial rites, and in healthcare environments [3].

The 2022 outbreak underscored the role of urban centers like Kampala as epicenters for disease dissemination, promoting transmission to rural districts such as Jinja and Masaka. The migration of individuals between these regions, frequently prompted by travel or healthcare‐seeking behavior, highlights the necessity of incorporating both urban and rural ecological factors in outbreak control [4]. The 2025 outbreak commenced with a nurse at Mulago National Referral Hospital in Kampala as the initial verified case, underscoring the possibility of transmission risk inside medical settings. The delivery of care to infected patients, frequently in environments with inadequate infection control protocols, may result in hospital‐associated outbreaks of the disease [5].

The epidemiological analysis of the SVD outbreaks in Uganda reveals notable differences in transmission patterns, case fatality rates, and regional distribution during the past 20 years. The origin of the Sudan virus exposure remains the focus of research; however, bats are suggested [6]. Transmission to humans occurs intermittently, likely via direct interaction with primary or intermediary animal hosts. Persistent human‐to‐human transmission may occur by direct or indirect contact, especially through exposure to infectious bodily fluid [7]. This incident highlights the necessity for enhanced case identification and contact tracing, especially among frontline workers who have a heightened risk of exposure. The delays in case detection during the initial stages of this outbreak underscore the necessity to enhance real‐time surveillance and diagnostic capabilities. Upon comparing the epidemiological characteristics of these outbreaks, it is evident that while SVD has predominantly been restricted to particular geographic areas, the potential for broader dissemination remains significant. Urbanization, enhanced interdistrict connectivity, and health care‐seeking patterns complicate the containment of outbreaks. Additionally, the participation of traditional healers, who have inadequate protection and infection prevention measures, as shown in the 2025 outbreak, poses a further difficulty to securing prompt medical intervention and curbing subsequent transmission [1, 6]. The integration of genetic surveillance with epidemiological surveys is crucial for effective outbreak control. Epidemiological data offer insights into transmission dynamics, while genomic investigations are essential for comprehending viral evolution and potential alterations in virulence or transmissibility. The integration of these methodologies will enhance the capacity to forecast and alleviate future outbreaks [6].

4. Developments and Advancements in Treatment: Monoclonal Antibodies

Potential treatment options for SVD are provided by developments in antibody‐based therapeutics. Monoclonal antibody (mAb) combinations that target the Sudan virus glycoprotein (GP) have been created by researchers. These monoclonal antibodies have shown protective effects in animal models and neutralizing activity in vitro [8]. The virus's quick spread emphasizes how urgently prompt actions are needed. In response, Ugandan health officials have started clinical studies for vaccinations and therapeutic monoclonal antibodies in cooperation with foreign partners [9].

Another study discovered FVM04, a mAb that targets a specially exposed epitope in the virus's receptor‐binding region. In animal studies, this antibody has demonstrated protective properties and cross‐neutralizes the SVD and EboV [10]. Because they offer targeted therapies that have the potential to drastically lower SVD‐related death rates, these advancements are vital. A study has shown that FVM04 therapy alone, administered after EboV or SVD infection, can give 100% protection against deadly illness in mice, contingent upon the timing of delivery and the antibody dosage [11, 12]. In guinea pigs, FVM04 therapy showed diminished efficacy against EboV but provided complete protection against SVD [11, 12]. The efficacy of CA45 against representatives of the Filoviridae family is remarkable, particularly given that first‐generation antibodies targeting this area (KZ52, 16F6, 4G7, and 2G4) exhibit a limited specificity range [11]. CA45 demonstrates significant neutralizing efficacy against SVD in vitro, exhibiting variable potency across different SVD strains. It demonstrates an over tenfold disparity in neutralization between SVD‐Gulu and SVD‐Boniface [13]. Researchers have documented comparable data for treatment with CA45 alone, demonstrating a reduction in fatal outcomes in mice infected with both EboV and SVD [14]. CA45 monotherapy showed partial protection in guinea pigs infected with guinea pig‐adapted (GPA)‐ EboV, but it offered complete protection against GPA‐SVD. The combination of FVM04 and CA45 treatments demonstrated superior efficacy in guinea pigs and a mouse model of SVD infection, achieving a 100% survival rate in postinfection treatment experiments [12, 14]. Results offer robust evidence for the advancement of FVM04 and CA45 as a comprehensive therapy strategy for SVD [13].

5. Ongoing Research and Global Collaboration

Research into possible cures and vaccinations is still continuing, with the creation of a vaccine for Sudan Virus Disease remaining a top goal. Even though there are a number of promising vaccine ideas, clinical studies are still in their early phases. To limit future epidemics, it is imperative to improve early warning systems, boost disease surveillance, and support healthcare infrastructure. To guarantee that vulnerable areas have access to the tools and knowledge required to fight SVD, international collaboration is crucial.

A Phase 1 clinical study for a potential vaccine against the Sudan virus was started in the United States in June 2023 by the International AIDS Vaccine Initiative (IAVI). The purpose of this experiment is to assess the vaccine's immunogenicity and safety in healthy participants [15]. A bivalent adenovirus‐vectored vaccination has also been created, and preclinical research has shown that it elicits a strong humoral response. It did not, however, shield Cynomolgus macaques from a deadly Sudan virus challenge, suggesting that more optimization is required [16]. These initiatives are a component of a larger plan to create vaccines that can offer quick and efficient defense against SVD, particularly in epidemic situations.

To expedite outbreak control measures, recently, the WHO and its collaborators initiated a clinical study for a vaccine candidate targeting Sudan virus disease, merely 4 days following the declaration of the outbreak. The vaccine candidate is a recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus developed by IAVI. The trial is to evaluate the proposed vaccine's safety and efficacy in safeguarding confirmed patients' contacts from the disease. The WHO donated US$1 million from its Contingency Fund for Emergencies and enabled access to IAVI's candidate vaccination and experimental therapies. In preparation for an outbreak, the WHO had strategically positioned these tools in Uganda. The initial 2160 doses of the vaccine have arrived in Kampala, along with investigational therapies—including a mAb and an antiviral— and are prepared for distribution [17].

6. Challenges in Responding to SVD Epidemics in Low‐Income Countries

Responding to SVD epidemics is extremely difficult for low‐income nations like Uganda. Although Uganda has learned from past epidemics, the response is sometimes hampered by a lack of funding. Inadequate buildings, medical supplies, and staffing are problems for the healthcare system. Inadequate buildings denote insufficient space, poor ventilation, lack of hygiene, and obsolete equipment, which might facilitate the transmission of infections within healthcare settings [18, 19]. Due to a lack of healthcare facilities and access to life‐saving care, rural communities are particularly susceptible. Attempts to stop the virus from spreading are further complicated by cultural customs like burial rites that require intimate touch with the deceased. A study revealed that bereaved families, after considerable effort, were informed that deceased individuals from COVID‐19 infections pose a low risk of airborne transmission, allowing families to conduct safe and dignified burials with minimal standard operating procedures. This significant prior experience fostered the belief that deceased individuals from Ebola do not transmit new infections, resulting in reluctance toward the SDB (safe and dignified burial) teams. This point is notable in their findings, since certain grieving relatives declined to surrender remains for SDB and confronted SDB personnel throughout the SVD 2023 response, mirroring occurrences in the later phases of the COVID‐19 outbreak due to distrust in the health system and its response [20]. Another study indicated that the swift interment of the deceased, without permitting family to observe the bodies, aroused suspicion that medical practitioners were retaining the corpses for the purpose of selling the body parts of their relatives [3]. Thus, conducting community engagement research to determine suitable and accepted methods for facilitating safe and dignified burials may mitigate the transmission of sickness during a future SVD outbreak.

The deficit of physicians in sub‐Saharan Africa has reached alarming levels, with merely two physicians per 10,000 individuals, in contrast to 28 in high‐income areas. The deficiency of physicians has resulted in certain physician responsibilities, such as the commencement of antiretroviral therapy, being transferred to mid‐level practitioners (MLPs) with less extensive medical training, both formally and informally. However, a Ugandan survey indicated that numerous MLPs have not received training to perform these activities [21]. Building capacity is therefore necessary to strengthen the ability of individuals, organizations, and communities to proactively address health challenges, implement preventative measures, and respond to outbreaks [22, 23]. Community health workers (CHWs) in primary care (CHW‐PCs) are trained individuals with minimal to no formal medical education who deliver patient‐facing assistance and amenities in primary care settings. CHW‐PCs perform person‐centered duties, facilitate collaborative treatment, address social aspects of health, and enhance the availability of healthcare, patient involvement, and outcomes [24]. CHWs are widely acknowledged for their contribution to enhancing primary health care service delivery. CHWs have played a crucial role in facilitating preventative, promotive, and curative health service delivery within communities for numerous years. CHWs have participated in the management of prior epidemics, including the Ebola outbreak from 2014 to 2016 in Liberia, Guinea, and Sierra Leone; the HIV/AIDS epidemic in sub‐Saharan Africa; the Cholera outbreak in Haiti and Yemen; and the Zika virus outbreak in the Americas [25, 26, 27]. CHWs have been pivotal in addressing the COVID‐19 pandemic worldwide, especially in resource‐limited regions. CHWs globally have participated in the COVID‐19 response by performing tasks including screening, disseminating information, raising awareness, assisting with vaccine registration, mobilizing community immunization efforts, and facilitating treatment support [28, 29]. With adequate training in SVD management, CHWs can significantly contribute to addressing the current SVD outbreak.

7. Addressing the Challenges in Low‐Income Countries

During the 2025 SVD outbreak, Uganda depended on overseas partners for the provision of vaccines for experimental usage [17]. A study observed that the absence of real‐time data reporting constituted a significant deficiency during the Sudan virus outbreak, hindering contact tracking and response coordination [30]. In the 2022 epidemic of Ebola in Uganda, healthcare institutions were rapidly inundated due to insufficient isolation units and intensive care unit beds [31, 32].

An equitable and inclusive global health system is essential to guarantee that all individuals, irrespective of their socioeconomic situation, have access to the resources required for maintaining health. Emerging models for more fair and internationally scalable regional governance include the African Vaccine Delivery Alliance (AVDA). The African Vaccine Delivery Alliance (AVDA) was founded in response to frustration over the sluggish pace of global initiatives to ensure equitable vaccine distribution. A worldwide crisis necessitates a collective response, and the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention, along with AVDA, persist in their efforts to provide global accessibility and connectivity to the resources essential for ending the COVID‐19 pandemic [33, 34]. AVDA is an innovative framework that has demonstrated efficacy as a model for optimal regional collaboration and coordination. Limited financing for distribution in low‐income countries resulted in delays in the delivery of vaccines from ports to the population. AVDA leverages technical expertise and a network of local, national, and global stakeholders to facilitate connections across various entities, including private sector firms and African nations, as shown by the collaboration between Johnson & Johnson and Aspen Pharmacare. AVDA has played a crucial role in orchestrating the delivery of COVID‐19 interventions to communities throughout Africa and served as the precursor to the COVID Vaccine Delivery Partnership [35]. The COVID‐19 pandemic highlighted the necessity for enhanced investment in public health systems, especially in low‐ and middle‐income nations, to ensure improved preparedness for future pandemics [35, 36]. It is imperative to implement the lessons learned from pandemics like COVID‐19 to reform global health infrastructure in anticipation of future outbreaks and health risks, thereby enhancing the preparedness of low‐income and middle‐income countries (LMICs) [36, 37].

8. Key Considerations for Improvement of SVD Prevention and Treatment in LMICs

In 2025, 3 years later, a second SVD outbreak occurred in Uganda, prompting inquiries on transmission patterns, virus evolution, and outbreak response efficacy [6]. A study by Kabami et al. indicated that the Sudan virus possesses the potential to initiate a catastrophic pandemic; however, it is less likely to do so compared to other ebolaviruses, thereby demanding urgent efforts to identify and eradicate future outbreaks swiftly [38]. A pandemic of SVD is still quite likely, especially in low‐income nations with inadequate medical facilities. The latest outbreaks in Uganda highlight how urgently better response and readiness plans are needed. Addressing these key factors is essential for ensuring that low‐ and middle‐income countries can effectively manage, treat, and prevent SVD outbreaks while reducing the significant burden the disease places on vulnerable populations.

The absence of cutting‐edge diagnostic tools poses a significant problem, as the detection of arboviruses generally necessitates molecular techniques and equipment, such as basic polymerase chain reaction (PCR) machines, which are found in very few healthcare settings [51].

Addressing the flaws in public health systems that permit the spread of illnesses like SVD is essential to safeguarding global health.
