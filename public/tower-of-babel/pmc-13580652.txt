Kikuchi–Fujimoto Disease Associated with Hashimoto’s Thyroiditis: A Case Report
Authors: Hala Alibrahim, Mohammad Yaman Haidar, Modar Doyya, Diana Yousef Saleem, Qutayba Abdullah, Zain Al Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKikuchi–Fujimoto disease (KFD) is a rare, benign, and self-limiting disorder characterized primarily by cervical lymphadenopathy and fever. Although the disease has been reported worldwide, it is more frequently observed in young women of Asian descent. The etiology of this disease remains unclear. Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease has been reported in association with several autoimmune disorders. Its coexistence with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis is rarely described in the literature.ObjectiveTo present a case of KFD associated with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis and highlight the importance of considering this diagnosis in patients with autoimmune disorders.Case PresentationA 37-year-old woman with a known history of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis who presented with rapidly enlarging cervical lymphadenopathy. Laboratory investigations, Ultrasonography, and Excisional lymph node biopsy were performed.ResultsLaboratory tests revealed mild anemia, elevated ESR, and positive anti-TPO antibodies. Ultrasound showed multiple hypoechoic cervical lymph nodes. Biopsy confirmed KFD with characteristic histopathological features. The patient improved spontaneously without specific treatment.Conclusions and SignificanceThis case underscores the importance of including KFD in the differential diagnosis of cervical lymphadenopathy, particularly in young women with autoimmune diseases. Clinicians should be aware of this rare association to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary interventions.

Introduction

Kikuchi-Fujimoto disease (KFD) is a rare, benign, self-limiting lymphadenopathy of unknown etiology, first described in Japan in 1972.1,2 It predominantly affects young women of Asian descent and may be associated with autoimmune conditions (eg, SLE) or viral infections (eg, EBV, CMV).3 However, its coexistence with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis is rarely described in the literature, making this case particularly unique. This case highlights the importance of considering KFD in the differential diagnosis of cervical lymphadenopathy in patients with autoimmune thyroiditis, especially in settings where the disease is underrecognized, such as Syria, where misdiagnosis can lead to unnecessary treatments.

The disease typically presents with fever and cervical lymphadenopathy, and resolves spontaneously within 1–6 months. Herein, we report a case of KFD in a 37-year-old woman with pre-existing Hashimoto’s thyroiditis to describe this rare association.

Case Presentation

A 37-year-old woman presented to the Department of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery with rapidly enlarging left- sided cervical lymphadenopathy for one and a half months, accompanied by fever, chills, myalgia, arthralgia, and generalized malaise. She had a 5-year history of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis. She had been receiving levothyroxine replacement therapy at a dose of 100 μg once daily.

Examination revealed several enlarged lymph nodes (0.5–3 cm) in the left posterior cervical triangle. The nodes were mobile and non-adherent to skin. Some nodes were closely packed with ill-defined borders and mild tenderness. No lymphadenopathy was detected on the contralateral side of the neck or in other regions of the body. The remainder of the head and neck examination was unremarkable. Flexible fiberoptic endoscopic examination of the nasal cavity, nasopharynx, and larynx was normal.

Histopathology showed partial effacement of nodal architecture with areas of pale necrosis surrounded by abundant karyorrhectic debris, commonly described as “nuclear dust” (Figure 2A). These areas were infiltrated by histiocytes and plasmacytoid dendritic cells. The histiocytic population included phagocytic cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm and crescent-shaped nuclei (crescentic histiocytes), as well as non-phagocytic histiocytes (Figure 2B and C). Notably, neutrophils were absent. Tuberculous lymphadenitis was excluded because no Langhans giant cells or caseating granulomas were identified, and Ziehl–Neelsen staining was negative. Immunohistochemical analysis showed positivity for CD3 and CD68. CD20 staining highlighted scattered cells but was negative in the karyorrhectic areas, while CD1a staining was negative. Based on these histopathological and immunohistochemical findings, a final diagnosis of Kikuchi disease (histiocytic necrotizing lymphadenitis) was established.

The patient was counselled about the self-limiting nature of KFD, and a conservative approach with observation only was adopted. Her levothyroxine dose (100 μg daily) was maintained unchanged throughout the follow-up period. No postoperative complications, such as bleeding, infection, or hematoma, were observed.

At 6 weeks follow-up, she reported improvement and regression of lymphadenopathy. Two months later, she developed new contralateral cervical lymphadenopathy (self-reported as large), which resolved spontaneously after another 2 months; no further investigations were done as she had traveled abroad.

Discussion

Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease (KFD) is a rare self-limiting lymphadenopathy of unknown etiology, predominantly affecting young Asian women.4–6 In the Middle East, it accounts for ~0.6% of lymph node biopsies, with only three reported Syrian cases.7–9Viral infections (eg, EBV, CMV, and HIV) and autoimmune mechanisms are leading hypotheses.10 KFD is frequently associated with SLE, Sjögren’s syndrome, and Graves’ disease.6 Therefore, ANA and anti-dsDNA screening is recommended in KFD patients, especially those with autoimmune background. The coexistence of KFD with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis is rarely reported.8,11,12

A 16-year-old Vietnamese girl presented with prolonged fever, tender cervical lymphadenopathy, and hypothyroidism. Following an extensive evaluation that excluded the common causes of fever of unknown origin, a cervical lymph node biopsy was performed and demonstrated findings consistent with Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease (KFD). The patient was also found to have an elevated anti-thyroid peroxidase antibody titre, supporting a concurrent diagnosis of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis.13 In another reported case, a 19-year-old Greek woman with a known history of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis developed fever accompanied by tender right anterior cervical lymphadenopathy. Subsequent evaluation and lymph node biopsy established the diagnosis of Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease.14 Similarly, a 29-year-old Black African woman was diagnosed with Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease and subsequently developed Hashimoto’s thyroiditis, which was identified approximately three months after the initial diagnosis of KFD.15

Hashimoto’s involves T-cell-mediated thyroid destruction11,16 and complement pathway impairment in both conditions suggests shared immunological pathways.17 Our patient had confirmed Hashimoto’s with elevated anti-TPO antibodies. Clinically, KFD presents with fever and tender cervical lymphadenopathy, often with elevated ESR and mild anemia.6 Differential diagnoses include lymphoma, SLE, tuberculosis, and viral infections.18 In our patient, tuberculosis was initially suspected due to the presence of night sweats and fever; however, it was excluded by negative Ziehl–Neelsen staining and the absence of caseating granulomas or Langhans giant cells. Lymphoma was considered; however, the absence of atypical cells, characteristic histopathological features (paracortical necrosis and karyorrhectic debris), absence of features suggestive of lymphoma (such as diffuse effacement of architecture or a monomorphic atypical lymphoid population), and a normal LDH level (200 U/L) made this diagnosis unlikely. SLE was excluded as ANA and anti-dsDNA were both negative (<1:40 and 2.0 IU/mL, respectively), and the patient had no clinical features suggestive of SLE. EBV and CMV infections were also excluded, as serological testing for EBV IgG, EBV IgM, CMV IgG, and CMV IgM were all negative.

Excisional biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosis,19 it typically shows paracortical necrosis, karyorrhectic debris, and histiocytic infiltration, with T-cell predominance (CD8+ > CD4+) and markers like CD68.20 Ultrasound findings in our patient were consistent with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis and showed hypoechoic lymph nodes with minimal vascularity. Based on the exclusion of other possible causes through detailed differential diagnosis, along with the characteristic histopathological and immunohistochemical findings, the diagnosis of Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease was confirmed.

In most cases, KFD is self-limiting and requires only supportive care, though corticosteroids may be used in severe cases.6,20 The patient was informed about the self-limiting nature of KFD and expressed satisfaction with the conservative approach. Her symptoms and lymphadenopathy resolved spontaneously, consistent with the known clinical course of the disease.

To the best of our knowledge, only one case of KFD coexisting with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis has been previously reported in Syria, in a pregnant woman from Aleppo.9 Our case represents the second documented case of this rare association in the country, and the first in a non-pregnant patient, highlighting its rarity and the importance of considering this diagnosis in patients with autoimmune thyroiditis, especially in underrecognized settings. The main limitation of this report was the lack of long-term follow-up as the patient traveled abroad. Another limitation to acknowledge is that our study was conducted on a single patient. While this case provided valuable insights, the findings may not be fully generalizable to a broader population and larger studies are required.

Conclusion

Kikuchi–Fujimoto disease should be considered in the differential diagnosis of cervical lymphadenopathy, especially in young patients with autoimmune thyroiditis. Its coexistence with Hashimoto’s thyroiditis is rare but clinically significant. Early recognition and histopathological confirmation are essential to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary treatment.
