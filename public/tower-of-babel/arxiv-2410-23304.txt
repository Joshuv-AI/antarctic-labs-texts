Approximation of length metrics by conformally flat Riemannian metrics
Andres A. Contreras Hip and Ewain Gwynne University of Chicago

Abstract
We present a proof of the folklore result that any length metric on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} can be approximated by conformally flat Riemannian distance functions in the uniform distance. This result is used to study Liouville quantum gravity in another paper by the same authors.



1
Introduction

In this note we show that any length metric space homeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be approximated by conformally flat Riemannian metrics on
\mathbb{R}^{d}.
More precisely, let
D_{0}
denote the Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{R}^{d},
and define
e^{f}\cdot D_{0}
to be the distance function associated with the Riemannian metric
e^{f}(dx_{1}^{2}+\ldots+dx_{d}^{2}).
Before we state the theorem, we will need the following definition.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
(X,\mathfrak{d})
be a metric space.
For a path
P:[a,b]\to X
, we define the
\mathfrak{d}
-length
of
P
by

\ell_{\mathfrak{d}}(P):=\sup_{T}\sum_{i=1}^{\#T}\mathfrak{d}(P(t_{i}),P(t_{i-1}))

where the supremum is over all partitions
T:a=t_{0}<\dots<t_{\#T}=b
of
[a,b]
.
We say that
\mathfrak{d}
is a
length metric
if for each
x,y\in X
, the distance
\mathfrak{d}(x,y)
is equal to the infimum of the
\mathfrak{d}
-lengths of the
\mathfrak{d}
-continuous paths from
x
to
y
. A path from
x
to
y
of minimal
\mathfrak{d}
-length is called a
geodesic
.

We say that
(X,\mathfrak{d})
is
boundedly compact
is each closed bounded subset of
X
is
\mathfrak{d}
-compact.
It is shown in
[
BBI01
, Corollary 2.5.20]
that if
\mathfrak{d}
is a length metric and
(X,\mathfrak{d})
is boundedly compact, then there is a
\mathfrak{d}
-geodesic between any two points of
X
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\bar{D}
be a boundedly compact length metric on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which induces the same topology as the Euclidean metric
D_{0}.
Let
\varepsilon>0
and
R>0.
Then there exists a bounded continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\sup_{x,y\in[-R,R]^{d}}|\bar{D}(x,y)-e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(x,y)|\leq\varepsilon.

This result is stated in dimension
2
without proof in
[
BBI01
]
right after Exercise 7.1.3.

Recall that a Riemannian metric
g
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
conformally flat
if
g=e^{f}(dx_{1}^{2}+\dots+dx_{d}^{2})
for some function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
.
In dimension
2,
every Riemannian metric is locally conformally flat. In higher dimensions this is no longer true: conformal flatness is equivalent to the vanishing of the Weyl tensor in dimension
3
or the Cotton tensor for
d\geq 4.
Nevertheless, we show that conformally flat metrics suffice to approximate any length metric, including ones which arise from a Riemannian metric which is not conformally flat and ones which do not arise from a Riemannian metric.

Our result is used in
[
CG23
]
to show that any boundedly compact length metric on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be approximated with positive probability by the random metrics arising from Liouville quantum gravity and its higher-dimensional analogs.

To prove Theorem
1.2
, we proceed as follows. We approximate our length metric by a weighted graph embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by considering geodesics between points in a fine grid (see (
2.2
)). These weights are chosen in such a way that the graph distance metric
d_{G}
coincides with
\bar{D}
for pairs of vertices. After this, we define an appropriate function
f
which is very large far away from the graph’s edges, and then we define
f
according to the weights near each edge (see (
2.3
)). These weights are chosen in such a way that the metrics
e^{f}\cdot D_{0}
and
d_{G}
are close for vertices. Additionally,
f
is chosen to be large enough away from the edges of
G
in such a manner that
e^{f}\cdot D_{0}
geodesics are forced to stay near
G
’s edges, but at the same time not too large, so that points away from the vertices of
G
are still at a small distance from the edges (see Lemma
2.8
). After this, we show that the metrics
d_{G}
and
e^{f}\cdot D_{0}
are close for adjacent vertices (Lemmas
2.5
and
2.6
) and later show that
e^{f}\cdot D_{0}
distance minimizing paths between points in a neighborhood of the graph
G
remain in a slightly thicker neighborhood of
G
(see Lemma
2.4
). Combining these lemmas together with with the fact that
d_{G}
approximates
\bar{D}
we conclude.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{S}
is a finite set of
\bar{D}

Then there exists another set

Proof.

We first consider a single pair of geodesics, and note that we can replace these two geodesics so that they intersect at either a single point or in an interval. Indeed, suppose
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
are geodesics between
x_{1},x_{2}
and
y_{1},y_{2}
respectively and that they intersect at at least two points. Let

t_{1}:=\inf\{\sigma\in[0,1]:\gamma_{1}(\sigma)\in\gamma_{2}([0,1])\},

t_{2}:=\sup\{\sigma\in[0,1]:\gamma_{1}(\sigma)\in\gamma_{2}([0,1])\},

and let
s_{1},s_{2}
such that
p_{1}:=\gamma_{1}(t_{1})=\gamma_{2}(s_{1}),
and
p_{2}:=\gamma_{1}(t_{2})=\gamma_{2}(s_{2}).
Recall that since
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
intersect at least twice,
p_{1}\neq p_{2}.
Since
\gamma_{2}
is a
\bar{D}
-length minimizing geodesic, it is also length minimizing when restricting to an interval. Therefore
\gamma_{2}|_{[s_{1},s_{2}]}
is a length minimizing path between
p_{1},p_{2}.
Thus we can replace
\gamma_{1}
by
\tilde{\gamma}_{1}=\gamma_{1}|_{[0,t_{1}]}\ast\gamma_{2}|_{[s_{1},s_{2}]}\ast\gamma_{1}|_{[t_{2},1]},
where
\ast
denotes concatenation. This procedure lets us replace two geodesics with multiple intersection points with geodesics whose intersection is a single interval (see Figures
2
and
2
). Suppose we number the geodesics in
\mathcal{S}
arbitrarily, so that the geodesics are the set
\{P_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
for some large
n.
We will inductively apply this procedure to to get rid of all intersections that are not at endpoints or single segments. First we apply the procedure to
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
to replace them with geodesics intersecting at their endpoints. Now suppose that we have replaced
\{P_{k}\}_{k=1}^{m}
with geodesics intersecting only at endpoints, where
m<n.
Let

t_{1}:=\inf\{t:P_{m+1}(t)\in\cup_{j=1}^{m+1}P_{j}([0,1])\}.

Now let
j_{1}
be such that
P(t_{1})\in P_{j_{1}}.
Then we define

t_{1}^{\prime}:=\sup\{t:P_{m+1}(t)\in P_{j_{1}}([0,1])\}.

Suppose we have defined
t_{1},\ldots t_{i},

t_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,t_{i}^{\prime},
and
j_{1},\ldots j_{i}.
Then define

t_{i+1}:=\inf\{t\geq t_{i}^{\prime}:P_{m+1}(t)\in\cup_{j\notin\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{i}\}}P_{j}([0,1])\},

let
j_{i+1}
such that
P(t_{i+1})\in P_{j_{i+1}}([0,1]),
and finally let

t_{i+1}^{\prime}:=\sup\{t:P_{m+1}(t)\in P_{j_{i+1}}([0,1])\}.

Note that the intervals

are disjoint. Now applying the above procedure we can replace
P_{m+1}
in the intervals
[t_{i},t_{i}^{\prime}]
by the corresponding segments on

thus removing multiple intersections, leaving us with only intersection at the endpoints. This completes the proof.

\mathrm{Diam}_{d}(T_{e_{1}}^{\eta+\bar{\eta}}\cap T_{e_{2}}^{\eta+\bar{\eta}})\to 0
as

it suffices to prove that

where

(x_{i},x_{i+1})
be the segment between
x_{i}
and

Then

\ell_{e^{f}\cdot D_{0}}(\tilde{P}|_{[s_{i},s_{i+1}]})=\int_{s_{i}}^{s_{i+1}}e^{f(\tilde{P}(t))}|\tilde{P}^{\prime}(t)|dt\geq\int_{s_{i}}^{s_{i+1}}e^{f(\bar{P}(t))}|\bar{P}^{\prime}(t)|,

Then for small enough

Noting that

∎

Now we will prove Lemma
2.6
.

Proof of Lemma
2.6
.

By the triangle inequality, we have that

\bar{D}(x,y)\leq\bar{D}(x,x_{1})+\bar{D}(x_{n},y)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\bar{D}(x_{i},x_{i+1}).

(2.16)

Note that
\mathrm{Diam}_{d}(T_{e}^{\theta})
tends to
\mathrm{Diam}_{d}(e)
as

Letting

if

and
\bar{\eta}
are small enough we know that

and similarly

\bar{D}(y,x_{n-1})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{20}.

Combining this with (
2.16
) we obtain that

\bar{D}(x,y)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\bar{D}(x_{i},x_{i+1})+\frac{\varepsilon}{10}.

Similarly we obtain by the triangle inequality that

\displaystyle e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(x_{1},x_{n-1})-e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(x,x_{1})-e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(y,x_{n-1})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(x_{1},x_{n-1})-\frac{\varepsilon}{10}.

Combining this with Lemma
2.7

2.6
.
∎

2.3
Proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section we prove Theorem

. We will need the following lemma.

Lemma 2.8
.

Suppose that

Then there exists a vertex

\bar{D}(x,\bar{x}),e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(x,\bar{x})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{16}

We assume Lemma

for now, and prove Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let

Let
\bar{x},\bar{y}
be as in Lemma
2.8
. Then

\displaystyle\bar{D}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bar{D}(x,\bar{x})+\bar{D}(y,\bar{y})+\bar{D}(\bar{x},\bar{y})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bar{D}(\bar{x},\bar{y})+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}.

By Lemma
2.6
we have

\bar{D}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\lesssim e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(\bar{x},\bar{y})+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

Hence

\displaystyle\bar{D}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(\bar{x},\bar{y})+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(x,y)+e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(x,\bar{x})+e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(y,\bar{y})+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}+\frac{\varepsilon}{8}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(x,y)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(\bar{x},\bar{y})+e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(x,\bar{x})+e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(y,\bar{y})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(\bar{x},\bar{y})+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d_{G}(\bar{x},\bar{y})+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}

where in the last line we used Lemma
2.5
. Now by (
2.2
) we obtain

e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(x,y)\leq d_{G}(\bar{x},\bar{y})+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\leq\bar{D}(\bar{x},\bar{y})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Now we will prove Lemma
2.8
.

Proof of Lemma
2.8
.

We will divide the proof in three steps.

Step 1:

e

This completes step 1.

Step 2:
Suppose there is an edge
e
such that
D_{0}(x,e)\leq\eta+\bar{\eta}.
Then there exists a vertex
\bar{x}
that is one of
e
’s endpoints such that
\bar{D}(x,\bar{x}),e^{f}\cdot D_{0}(x,\bar{x})\leq\frac{3\varepsilon}{64}.
Note that by step 1, it suffices to show that there exists a point

\bar{D}
with respect to
D_{0}.
This completes this case.

Step 3:
Conclusion of the proof. By steps 1 and 2, the result holds if

thus we suppose that

Let
i_{1},\ldots i_{d}

On the other hand, to show that

To show this, we take

This completes the proof.
∎
