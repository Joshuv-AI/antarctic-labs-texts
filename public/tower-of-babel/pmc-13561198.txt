Advanced esophageal adenocarcinoma in a pediatric patient with Down syndrome: a rare case report and literature review
Authors: Soumar Alziadan, Hilal Alfirzly, Yara Alturkmany Alabead, Amjad Jamil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Down syndrome (DS) is a genetic disorder characterized by the presence of a full or partial additional copy of chromosome 21, affecting approximately 1 in 700 live births[1]. Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is highly prevalent in the DS patient cohort, often leading to complications such as benign esophageal strictures or Barrett’s esophagus[2]. Beyond GI manifestations, DS is associated with a distinct oncological profile[3–5]. While individuals with DS display an elevated risk of hematological malignancies, particularly acute myeloid and lymphoid leukemias, they exhibit a 20% reduction in the overall incidence of solid tumors compared to controls[4,5]. A notable exception is testicular germ cell carcinoma, which is more prevalent in DS patients compared to the general population[5]. Esophageal cancer is the eighth most prevalent malignancy worldwide, most commonly presenting as squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and adenocarcinoma (AC), which account for 85% and 15% of diagnosed cases, respectively[3,6]. Despite this, the incidence rate of esophageal cancer is almost negligible before the age of 30[7]. Chronic GERD, Barrett’s esophagus, male sex, obesity, and tobacco smoking are well-known risk factors for developing esophageal AC[6]. Our case represents an extremely rare and paradoxical clinical presentation of esophageal AC in a 15-year-old male child with DS. Despite the reported lower incidence of solid tumors in DS and the extreme rarity of esophageal AC in childhood – particularly in the absence of traditional risk factors – our patient’s presentation defied both clinical and epidemiological expectations[4,5]. To our knowledge, only three prior cases of esophageal cancer in DS patients have been reported in the literature[8,9]. In all four cases (including ours), patients presented with inoperable, advanced-stage disease. Whether this reflects true diagnostic delay or the inherently aggressive biology of esophageal malignancy in young patients remains unclear[8,9]. The extreme rarity of these cases may have contributed to late recognition[8,9] These findings highlight the importance of maintaining clinical suspicion for rare cancers in DS patients presenting with persistent gastrointestinal symptoms, even when epidemiological evidence suggests low risk and presumed protective mechanisms remain unconfirmed. This case has been reported in line with the CARE guidelines.

Case presentation

A 15-year-old male with DS presented to the emergency department with a 3-month history of progressive dysphagia and retrosternal pain. His medical history was otherwise unremarkable, with no prior diagnosis of GERD, smoking, alcohol use, foreign body ingestion, caustic substance exposure, or esophageal trauma, and no family history of significant morbidities, including tumors. The dysphagia initially involved solids but later progressed to liquids, accompanied by retrosternal pain and occasional regurgitation of undigested food. Over the 6 months prior to presentation, an intermittent dry cough and one episode of community-acquired pneumonia were reported, and the patient had experienced a 5-kg unintentional weight loss. On clinical examination, the patient appeared dehydrated, malnourished, and pale, with no palpable abdominal masses or lymphadenopathy. Following admission, the patient received intravenous fluid resuscitation. Non-contrast computed tomography (CT) of the thorax and abdomen was performed (contrast was avoided due to impaired renal function at presentation). While intravenous contrast is the standard of care for oncologic staging, its omission was clinically necessary to avoid contrast-induced nephropathy. We acknowledge that non-contrast imaging limits detailed assessment of vascular invasion, lesion enhancement characteristics, and definitive nodal staging. Despite this limitation, the non-contrast study was sufficient to delineate the extent of mural thickening and lymphadenopathy for initial staging purposes. The CT revealed severe proximal esophageal dilation, a 12-cm longitudinal mural thickening extending from the mid-esophagus to the esophagogastric junction, and pathological lymphadenopathy involving the mediastinal and abdominal regions, as well as multiple hypodense splenic lesions concerning for metastatic involvement (Fig. 1). Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) identified an ulcerated and infiltrating lesion extending from 27 to 31 cm distal to the maxillary incisors, where severe luminal narrowing precluded further passage of the endoscope (Fig. 2). Multiple biopsies confirmed poorly differentiated AC (Fig. 3), with immunohistochemistry revealing positivity for CDX2, CK7, and MUC2/5AC. Following multidisciplinary discussion, the patient was staged as stage IV esophageal AC (presumptive T4aN3M1). The M1 designation was supported by multiple hypodense splenic lesions consistent with metastatic disease, while the T and N designations are based on non-contrast imaging and should be considered presumptive in the absence of contrast enhancement. Although palliative chemotherapy or radiotherapy was recommended following multidisciplinary discussion, the patient’s legal guardians were involved in all shared decision-making discussions. Given the patient’s age (15 years) and underlying DS, formal capacity assessment was not performed as the guardians retained legal authority for medical decisions. However, the patient consistently expressed verbal and non-verbal refusal of further interventions beyond symptomatic relief, which the guardians supported after extensive counseling about prognosis and treatment options. The family collectively opted for symptomatic relief only, including refusal of chemotherapy and radiotherapy. The decision was documented in the medical record, and palliative care was consulted for ongoing symptom management. To alleviate severe dysphagia, endoscopic stenting or percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) was attempted but was not feasible due to a tight, angulated, and nearly circumferential esophageal narrowing with a pinpoint lumen. Consequently, interventional radiology successfully performed a radiologically inserted gastrostomy (RIG) under local anesthesia – a valuable alternative when endoscopic access is not possible – to establish enteral nutrition. The patient was referred to palliative care for ongoing symptom management. Despite initial nutritional support, his condition deteriorated due to recurrent pneumonia and metabolic derangements, and he died 4 months after the initial onset of dysphagia and retrosternal pain. Discussion

Our patient, a 15-year-old male with DS, presented to the emergency department with dysphagia and odynophagia. The dysphagia initially involved solids but later progressed to liquids. Intermittent regurgitation of undigested food associated with a dry cough, one episode of community-acquired pneumonia, and a 5-kg unintentional weight loss were noted. His past medical history was otherwise unremarkable. Clinical examination revealed a malnourished, dehydrated, and pale patient. A non-contrast CT scan and EGD with biopsies revealed stage IV, esophageal AC obstructing the esophagus. A RIG was inserted under local anesthesia following the failure of first-line management of the esophageal obstruction with a stent or PEG.To date, only four cases of esophageal cancer in DS patients have been reported, including the present case (Table 1). To contextualize our case, we performed a literature search in PubMed and Google Scholar up to April 2026. We used the following Boolean strategy: (“esophageal adenocarcinoma” OR “esophageal cancer” OR “oesophageal cancer” OR “esophageal neoplasm”) AND (“Down syndrome” OR “trisomy 21”) AND (“child” OR “pediatric” OR “adolescent” OR “young” OR “teenager”). No language or date restrictions were applied. The PubMed search returned three relevant articles, all of which were included. The same search in Google Scholar returned over 100 results. We screened the first 60 records by title and abstract, as relevance diminished substantially thereafter. This process did not identify any additional unique cases beyond the three already found in PubMed. All patients were male, ranging from 14 to 44 years, and none had traditional risk factors such as smoking, obesity, or significant GERD history. Three cases were AC, and one was SCC. In our case, the interval from first dysphagia to diagnosis was approximately 3 months, with death occurring 4 months after symptom onset All presented with advanced disease (stage III/IV or widespread metastases). Whether this reflects true diagnostic delay or the inherently aggressive biology of esophageal malignancy in young patients remains unclear. Management varied: stenting was attempted in three cases (successful in two, failed in one requiring RIG), surgery was attempted but abandoned in one, and chemoradiotherapy was offered in two but declined by our patient. Outcomes were uniformly poor: three patients died within 4–6 months of presentation, and one was lost to follow-up. The consistent pattern across all cases – young age, male sex, advanced stage at diagnosis, absence of risk factors, and poor outcomes – highlights a critical clinical blind spot. Several factors may contribute to late presentation in DS patients: (1) cognitive or communication barriers may lead to underreporting of early symptoms, (2) the hypothetical perceived low risk of solid tumors in DS may lower clinical suspicion, and (3) gastrointestinal dysmotility disorders common in DS may initially mimic malignancy. These cases collectively emphasize that persistent dysphagia or unexplained weight loss in DS patients warrants prompt endoscopic investigation, regardless of age[8,9]. The diagnostic challenges in this case merit discussion. The initial clinical impression favored an esophageal dysmotility disorder (EDD), such as achalasia or an esophageal stricture secondary to asymptomatic GERD. This suspicion was supported by the fact that DS is associated with an increased incidence of EDD due to enteric nervous system (ENS) dysfunction[10]. Beyond diagnostic considerations, the unique biology of cancer in DS patients warrants attention. DS predisposes patients to certain types of malignancies, including acute myeloid/lymphoid leukemia and testicular cancer; however, the incidence of solid tumors is significantly lower in the DS population than in age-adjusted non-DS controls[4,5]. The mechanisms underlying this reduced incidence remain incompletely understood. One proposed hypothesis involves overexpression of the Down syndrome critical region 1 (DSCR1) gene on chromosome 21. Preclinical studies suggest that DSCR1 overexpression may downregulate angiogenesis-related pathways, potentially suppressing tumor growth. However, this hypothesis derives primarily from in vitro and animal models, and clinical evidence in humans is lacking. Alternative mechanisms have not been excluded, and confirmation in human studies is required[11]. Several limitations of this case report warrant acknowledgment. First, a staging CT was performed without intravenous contrast due to impaired renal function at presentation. While clinically necessary, this limits the radiological certainty of the T4a, N3, and M1 designations. The presence of multiple splenic lesions confidently supports M1 (stage IV) disease; however, the T4a and N3 designations are presumptive due to the lack of contrast-enhanced imaging. Second, as a single case report, our findings are not generalizable. Nevertheless, the extreme rarity of this presentation provides valuable clinical lessons.

Conclusion

This report presents an exceptionally rare case of esophageal AC in a 15-year-old male with DS, manifesting as dysphagia, odynophagia, and weight loss. Whether the advanced stage at presentation reflects diagnostic delay or the inherently aggressive biology of this malignancy remains unclear, though the extreme rarity of such cases in pediatric DS patients likely contributed to late recognition. This case offers three key clinical lessons: (1) DS does not confer guaranteed protection against solid tumors; (2) persistent dysphagia or unexplained weight loss in DS patients should prompt early endoscopic evaluation, regardless of age; and (3) when stenting fails due to impassable strictures, RIG provides a safe alternative for enteral nutrition and palliative support. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for rare malignancies even in populations traditionally considered low-risk.
