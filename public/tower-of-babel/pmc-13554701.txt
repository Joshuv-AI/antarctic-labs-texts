IMPACT study: large real-world evaluation of guselkumab in psoriatic arthritis integrating clinical response and treatment persistence
Authors: Valentino Paci, Roberta Foti, Ilenia Pantano, Antonio Carletto, Eleonora Celletti, Giovanni Francesco Miceli, Ignazio Cangemi, Giuseppe Lopalco, Simona Montalbano, Maria Sole Chimenti, Andrea Picchianti Diamanti, Umberto Massafra, Emilio Filippucci, Anna Parisi, Pietro Leccese, Anna Panaccione, Ilaria Puca, Giorgio Amato, Sabrina Marrone, Myriam Di Penta, Salvatore Corrao, Eneida Cela, Alessandra Rai, Enrico Tirri, Florenzo Iannone, Fabiola Atzeni, Maurizio Rossini, Francesco Ciccia, Rosario Foti, Gianluca Moroncini, Michele Maria Luchetti Gentiloni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:In psoriatic arthritis (PsA), real-world evidence complements randomised controlled trials by evaluating treatment effectiveness in routine care. Guselkumab (GUS), a selective interleukin-23p19 inhibitor, has demonstrated efficacy in phase-III trials, but real-world data remain limited.Objectives:To assess the real-world effectiveness, treatment persistence and safety of GUS in a large, multicentre PsA cohort.Design:IMPACT (Italian Multicentric PAtient-Centred assessment of Guselkumab Treatment) is a multicentre, observational, longitudinal study including consecutive adult patients with PsA treated with GUS.Methods:Clinical assessments were conducted at baseline and at 3, 6, 9 and 12 months. Effectiveness outcomes included the change from baseline in Disease Activity in Psoriatic Arthritis (DAPSA), Axial Spondyloarthritis Disease Activity Score (ASDAS) and Psoriasis Area Severity Index (PASI) scores, as well as the resolution of enthesitis and dactylitis, and treatment persistence. Predictors of DAPSA remission and treatment discontinuation were analysed using multivariable Cox regression models.Results:A total of 389 patients were included (245 (63.0%) female; median age 56.0 years), with moderate-to-high baseline disease burden and prevalent prior biologic (b-)/targeted synthetic disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARD) exposure (86.4%). Median DAPSA decreased from 24.2 at baseline to 10.5 at 12 months, with significant improvements from 3 months onward. The proportion of patients achieving DAPSA-low disease activity, or -remission increased progressively, reaching up to 58.6% (187/319) and 14.1% (45/319), respectively, at 12 months. Improvements were observed across multiple disease domains, including skin involvement (PASI-100 in 123/182 (67.6%) at 12 months), enthesitis and dactylitis (12-month resolution in 118/186 (63.4%) and 57/60 (95.0%), respectively) and axial disease (ΔASDAS −1.7 at 12 months). Treatment persistence was 78.7% at 12 months, with discontinuations mainly due to ineffectiveness; age, sex, fibromyalgia, baseline disease activity, prior b/tsDMARD exposure and corticosteroid use were associated with remission and/or discontinuation. Few adverse events were recorded, with no unexpected safety signals.Conclusion:In this large real-world cohort, GUS demonstrated sustained effectiveness in several disease domains, favourable treatment persistence and no unexpected safety signals, supporting its use in routine care of complex and b/tsDMARD-experienced PsA patients.

Introduction

Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory disease characterised by a complex intersection of musculoskeletal and cutaneous manifestations. In routine care, PsA rarely occurs in isolation: articular and skin inflammation coexist with cardiovascular, metabolic and psychosocial comorbidities, identified under the broader spectrum of ‘Psoriatic Disease’.1–3 The resulting clinical complexity translates into a substantial disease burden and warrants a personalised treatment strategy, which takes into consideration not only the objective measures of disease activity but also patients’ priorities, comorbidity constraints and the overall impact of PsA on daily life.4,5

Over the past years, the therapeutic armamentarium for PsA has expanded beyond tumour necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi) to include agents targeting and inhibiting the interleukin-23 (IL-23i) and -17 (IL-17i) pathways. 6 Guselkumab (GUS), a monoclonal antibody targeting the p19 subunit of IL-23, demonstrated efficacy and an excellent safety profile in several phase III studies.7–12

Nevertheless, translating trial efficacy into real-world effectiveness remains challenging. Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are designed to maximise internal validity, but their controlled setting and selected populations only partially capture the complexity of real-life PsA.13,14

In real-world settings, treatment effectiveness is influenced not only by disease activity across different domains but also by comorbidity burden, patient and physician perceptions, treatment accessibility and organisational factors. Therefore, pragmatic real-world studies are necessary to better understand the range of GUS effectiveness under routine-care conditions.

The IMPACT (Italian Multicentric PAtient-Centred assessment of Guselkumab Treatment) study aimed to evaluate the real-world effectiveness of GUS in a large, multicentre, routine-care cohort of patients with PsA, combining validated clinical outcomes with treatment persistence to provide a more comprehensive picture of treatment performance in everyday clinical practice.

Secondary aims included characterising the study cohort, identifying clinical predictors of remission and treatment discontinuation, and describing the safety profile of GUS in the real-life setting.

Methods

Study design and population

The IMPACT study had a multicentre, observational, longitudinal design and was conducted across 17 tertiary rheumatology centres in Italy. From January 2022 to December 2024, consecutive adult patients with PsA fulfilling the inclusion criteria were enrolled after providing informed consent and were followed for up to 12 months.

The main inclusion criteria were: (a) diagnosis of PsA, based on the rheumatologist assessment of the clinical picture and in accordance with the Classification Criteria for Psoriatic Arthritis; (b) age greater than 18 years and (c) initiation of treatment with GUS according to clinical indication and in compliance with authorised Italian reimbursement criteria. No exclusion criteria were applied. Clinical data were collected until completion of follow-up or treatment discontinuation for any reason.

The study was designed, conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

The study protocol was approved by the Lazio Regional Ethics Committee (Protocol Number 0022902/2024 – Comitato Etico Territoriale Lazio Area 2) and was accepted by all participating centres. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

Global clinical assessment

Data on demographic and baseline clinical features were collected for all patients, including sex, age, comorbidities, disease domains and the type and number of prior biologic (b-) and/or targeted synthetic (ts-) disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs).

Clinical assessment of disease activity was performed at baseline and at 12, 24, 36 and 52 weeks. These included tender joint count (68 joints – TJC) and swollen joint count (66 joints – SJC), visual analogue scale for pain (VAS pain), patient and physician global assessment (PtGA and PhGA). Laboratory data, including complete blood count, liver and renal function tests and C-reactive protein (CRP), were collected at the same timepoints.

Enthesitis was evaluated both categorically (presence vs absence at any peripheral entheseal site) and using the Leeds Enthesitis Index, whereas dactylitis was assessed categorically only.

Skin involvement was assessed at each timepoint in patients with psoriasis, using the proportion of body surface area affected by psoriasis (BSA-PSO) and the Psoriasis Area Severity Index (PASI).

The Disease Activity in Psoriatic Arthritis (DAPSA) score and the Axial Spondyloarthritis Disease Activity Score (ASDAS) based on CRP were calculated at each visit in patients with peripheral and axial involvement, respectively. Axial involvement was defined based on rheumatologist evaluation of the clinical picture and confirmed by imaging of the spine and sacroiliac joints. Specifically, the diagnosis of axial PsA was supported by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings consistent with active inflammatory and/or structural post-inflammatory lesions according to the rheumatologist and radiologist assessment and in line with the Assessment of Spondyloarthritis International Society and Outcome Measures in Rheumatology definitions for a positive MRI.15–17

MRI imaging was interpreted locally at each participating centre, without central reading or formal inter-reader reliability assessment. No patient was classified as having axial involvement based on clinical assessment alone.

Fibromyalgia was recorded based on clinical assessment by the treating rheumatologist during routine care. Systematic application of American College of Rheumatology (ACR) classification criteria for fibromyalgia was not part of the study protocol.

All patients received GUS according to one of the two approved dosing regimens for PsA: 100 mg administered subcutaneously (s.c.) at baseline, week 4 and every 8 weeks thereafter (q8w), or at baseline and every 4 weeks thereafter (q4w). At each visit, the dosing regimen was assessed, and any modification was recorded. Concomitant therapies, including conventional synthetic (cs) DMARD, corticosteroids and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), were also recorded at each timepoint.

All adverse events (AEs) occurring during the study period were recorded and classified according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of the study were the change from baseline in the DAPSA score and the proportion of patients achieving low disease activity (LDA) and remission at each time point, defined according to validated DAPSA thresholds, namely DAPSA remission (i.e. a DAPSA score ⩽4) and DAPSA LDA (i.e. a DAPSA score >4 and ⩽14), respectively.

The secondary outcomes included: (i) the change from baseline in the ASDAS score among patients with axial involvement; (ii) the treatment persistence and its difference across clinically relevant subgroups; (iii) the clinical predictors of DAPSA remission and treatment discontinuation and (iv) the proportion, type and severity of AEs recorded in the study cohort.

Data analysis

Baseline characteristics were described for the overall cohort. Continuous variables were summarised as median and interquartile range (1st–3rd quartile), whereas categorical variables were reported as absolute frequencies and percentages.

Missing data were limited to skin-related assessments and post-discontinuation follow-up assessments, with no additional missing data. Skin-related analyses were conducted using a complete-case approach, excluding patients with unavailable dermatological assessments. Post-discontinuation missing data were addressed through sensitivity analyses for the main outcomes: for continuous outcomes, a last observation carried forward approach was applied; for categorical outcomes, non-responder imputation (NRI) was performed, considering patients with missing data as non-responders. In addition, LUNDEX-adjusted response rates were calculated for key binary outcomes.

Longitudinal changes in disease activity outcomes were analysed using mixed-effects models. Specifically, linear mixed-effects models were fitted for continuous outcomes, while generalised linear mixed-effects models were used for categorical outcomes. In all mixed-effects models, timepoint was included as a fixed effect and a patient-level random intercept was added to account for within-subject correlation. No additional covariates were included in these models. Pairwise comparisons versus baseline were estimated using marginal means with Holm adjustment for multiple comparisons. Subjects who discontinued treatment were considered up to the last observation point, including all patients in the analyses. Model estimates were reported with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Treatment persistence was defined as time from the study drug initiation to treatment discontinuation for any reason. Drug retention was analysed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves, and differences between clinically relevant subgroups were assessed using the log-rank test. The retention rates and corresponding 95% CIs were reported. Drug retention used for LUNDEX calculations was defined consistently with the Kaplan–Meier analysis, in which discontinuations occurring at a given timepoint were counted as events at that timepoint.

Predictors of DAPSA remission and treatment discontinuation were explored using multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models. For the remission analysis, the event was defined as the first follow-up visit meeting the DAPSA remission threshold; patients already in DAPSA remission at baseline were excluded, and those not achieving remission were censored at the last available assessment. Covariates were selected based on expert opinion and clinical relevance, avoiding collinearity. The model stability was assessed by calculating the events-per-variable (EPV) ratio and the proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. A sensitivity analysis was performed with reduced Cox models including a smaller set of clinically selected covariates. Results were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs.

All statistical analyses and figures were produced using the R software, version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with a two-sided p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Cohort characteristics

Cohort characteristics at baseline are reported in Table 1. A total of 389 patients, mostly female (245/389, 63.0%), were included. The median age was 56.0 years (47.0–63.0) and the median BMI was 26.3 kg/m2 (23.4–30.4). Comorbidities were frequent, particularly overweight (166/389, 42.7%), cardiovascular disease (162/389, 41.7%) and metabolic disease (121/389, 31.1%); fibromyalgia was reported in 92/389 patients (23.7%). Regarding disease domains, most patients presented peripheral arthritis (373/389, 95.9%); enthesitis and dactylitis were reported in 227/389 (58.4%) and 79/389 (20.3%), respectively, while axial involvement was present in 81/389 (20.8%) patients.

Data on baseline skin involvement were available for 354 out of 389 patients (91.0%), while 35 (9.0%) had missing data about skin severity. Given the limited proportion of missing values, skin-related analyses were conducted using a complete-case approach. Among these patients, 219/354 (61.9%) presented with active psoriasis at baseline. The median BSA-PSO and PASI scores were 4.0% (1.0%–7.5%) and 4.4 (1.2–10.0), respectively.

Baseline laboratory data showed a median CRP level of 0.35 (0.20–0.80) mg/dl, and 150 out of 389 (38.6%) patients had CRP levels above the upper normal limit (u.l.n.; 0.5 mg/dl).

Most patients were b/tsDMARD-experienced (336/389, 86.4%), with a median of 2.0 (1.0–3.0) prior b/tsDMARD lines. The majority of patients (370/389, 95.1%) initiated GUS with the q8w dosing regimen. A considerable proportion of patients were receiving csDMARDs, corticosteroids and/or NSAIDs as concomitant therapy at baseline, specifically 165/389 (42.4%), 79/389 (20.3%) and 117/389 (30.1%), respectively.

Longitudinal analysis

The results of the mixed-effects models showing longitudinal changes in disease activity scores are reported in Table 2 and Figure 1. Disease activity improved early and was maintained over time. The median DAPSA significantly decreased to 15.8 (10.5-22.1) (Δ −8.4) at 12 weeks, further improving to 13.2 (8.2–18.1) (Δ −11.0) at 24 weeks, 11.1 (6.3–15.2) (Δ −13.1) at 36 weeks and 10.5 (6.2–14.2) (Δ −13.7) at 52 weeks, mirrored by consistent reductions in TJC and SJC (p < 0.0001 for all comparisons vs baseline).

Specifically, among evaluable patients at each timepoint, the proportion of patients in DAPSA-remission and -LDA significantly increased from 3/389 (0.8%) and 43/389 (11.1%) at baseline to 20/389 (5.1%) and 140/389 (36.0%) at 12 weeks, 27/367 (7.4%) and 166/367 (45.2%) at 24 weeks, 43/329 (13.1%) and 174/329 (52.9%) at 36 weeks and 45/319 (14.1%) and 187/319 (58.6%) at 52 weeks, respectively (Table 2). Improvements were also observed in patients with axial involvement of PsA, with ASDAS scores significantly decreasing from 3.3 (2.5–3.7) at baseline to 1.6 (1.3–2.1) at 52 weeks (Δ −1.7; Table 2).

Data regarding effectiveness on enthesitis and dactylitis are reported in Table 3, Figure 3 and Supplemental Figure 1. Among evaluable patients with baseline enthesitis, resolution rates progressively increased from 99/227 (43.6%) at 12 weeks to 116/211 (55.0%) at 24 weeks, 120/191 (62.8%) at 36 weeks and 118/186 (63.4%) at 52 weeks. Resolution rates for baseline dactylitis were higher, starting from 66/79 (83.5%) at 12 weeks, increasing to 65/68 (95.6%) at 24 weeks and remaining stable thereafter. During follow-up, among patients without enthesitis or dactylitis at baseline, 32 new cases of enthesitis and 2 new cases of dactylitis were observed. Half the cases (16/32; 50%) of new enthesitis resolved without discontinuation of the study drug, whereas both cases of incident dactylitis discontinued GUS due to ineffectiveness.

Regarding inflammatory markers, median CRP levels significantly decreased from 0.35 (0.20–0.80) to 0.20 (0.14–0.40) mg/dl by 52 weeks, and the proportion of patients with CRP above u.l.n. decreased from 38.6% to 15.6% over the same period (Table 2).

PtGA and PhGA showed a parallel improvement over time, and the VAS pain decreased rapidly from 7.1 (6.0–8.0) at baseline to 5.0 (4.0–7.0) at 12 weeks, with further reductions to 4.2 (3.0–6.0), 4.0 (2.0–5.0) and 3.5 (2.0–5.0) at 24, 36 and 52 weeks, respectively (Table 2).

Sensitivity analyses showed consistent results across outcomes. NRI and LUNDEX-adjusted analyses demonstrated lower resolution rates for enthesitis and dactylitis compared with the main analysis. Furthermore, a sensitivity analysis on DAPSA outcomes excluding patients with fibromyalgia is shown in Supplemental Table 1.

Finally, additional analyses stratified by prior b/tsDMARD exposure (bio-naïve, one previous b/tsDMARD and ⩾2 previous b/tsDMARDs) were performed. Longitudinal mixed-effects modelling showed significant DAPSA improvement over time across all subgroups (all within-group p < 0.0001). Although patients with ⩾2 prior b/tsDMARDs showed slightly attenuated improvement, overall DAPSA trajectories remained broadly comparable. In addition, a descriptive week-52 subgroup analysis showed numerically lower DAPSA remission/LDA and PASI-90/100 rates among patients with ⩾2 prior b/tsDMARDs, whereas enthesitis and dactylitis resolution rates were broadly comparable across subgroups (Table 4).

Drug analysis and persistence

Drug persistence was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and is shown in Supplemental Figure 2. The estimated retention rate was 84.6% (95% CI 81.1%–88.2%) at 6 months and 78.7% (95% CI 74.7%–82.8%) at 12 months. Subgroup analysis demonstrated that treatment retention differed significantly according to prior b/tsDMARD exposure, with higher persistence in b/tsDMARD-naïve patients and lower persistence among patients with ⩾2 prior b/tsDMARDs (log-rank p = 0.0001).

During follow-up, 22 (5.7%), 38 (9.8%), 10 (2.6%) and 13 (3.3%) patients discontinued GUS after the visit at 12, 24, 36 and 52 weeks, respectively, resulting in an overall discontinuation rate of 83/389 (21.3%) over 1 year. Among these, 77/83 patients discontinued treatment due to lack of effectiveness, specifically 68 due to musculoskeletal ineffectiveness, 4 due to cutaneous ineffectiveness and 5 due to both. Across the entire follow-up period, only nine AEs were recorded, six of which led to treatment discontinuation (Supplemental Table 3). Over a total exposure of 351 patient-years, this corresponded to an exposure-adjusted incidence rate of 2.56 events per 100 patient-years. Two AEs were classified as severe (grade 3, according to CTCAE v5.0), namely one case of cholangiocarcinoma, not considered drug-related by the treating physician, and one case of prostatic adenocarcinoma, corresponding to a malignancy incidence rate of 0.57 events per 100 patient-years. One death (grade 5, CTCAE v.5.0) occurred during follow-up in a 72-year-old patient with severe comorbidities, including stage 5 chronic kidney disease requiring haemodialysis, overweight and dyslipidaemia; death occurred as a complication of pre-existing conditions and, therefore, was not considered related to the study drug, and corresponded to an incidence rate of 0.29 events per 100 patient-years. The remaining mild-to-moderate AEs (grade 1–2, CTCAE v.5.0) included three cases of urticaria, one prostate infection, one recurrent bladder infection and one case of creatine phosphokinase elevation. No life-threatening AEs (grade 4, CTCAE v.5.0) were observed.

Most patients were treated with the q8w GUS dosing regimen throughout follow-up, although the q4w regimen prescription increased from 4.9% at baseline to 14.1% at 52 weeks (Table 5).

The presence of fibromyalgia was associated with a markedly lower likelihood of achieving DAPSA remission over time (HR 0.08; p = 0.017), as well as a lower risk of treatment discontinuation (HR 0.47; p = 0.024), likely reflecting the persistently high disease activity scores in the absence of objective inflammatory findings. Each additional point in the baseline DAPSA score was associated with a 6% reduction in the probability of DAPSA remission and a 3% increase in the risk of discontinuation. Regarding treatment-related factors, prior exposure to b/tsDMARDs and concomitant corticosteroid use at baseline were associated with approximately threefold and twofold increases in the risk of discontinuation, respectively (HR 3.06; p = 0.029 and HR 2.14; p = 0.0039), whereas neither factor significantly influenced the probability of achieving remission.

In the full models, the EPV ratio was 3.33 and 4.61 for predictors of DAPSA remission and treatment discontinuation, respectively. Therefore, reduced clinically driven Cox models were fitted as sensitivity analyses and are shown in Supplemental Table 2, yielding results consistent with the full models. The global Schoenfeld test did not indicate violation of the proportional hazards assumption in the reduced remission model, whereas a borderline deviation was observed in the reduced discontinuation model.

Discussion

The management of PsA in routine practice is challenged by its clinical complexity, where musculoskeletal and cutaneous manifestations coexist with cardiometabolic and psychosocial comorbidities. In everyday care, treatment decisions and outcomes are further influenced by factors only partially captured in RCTs, including patient and physician perception of disease control, treatment tolerability, access constraints and, in some settings, cost-related considerations. Consequently, in real-world cohorts, treatment response may not be fully captured by a single disease activity index, and ‘effectiveness’ should integrate domain-specific measures with broader outcomes such as treatment persistence, reduction of concomitant therapies and symptoms resolution.

In this context, robust real-world evidence (RWE) is essential to complement RCTs and to clarify how newer mechanisms of action, in our case IL-23p19 blockade, perform under routine-care conditions in heterogeneous and comorbidity-rich populations.

In the IMPACT study, four main messages emerge.

First, GUS was associated with sustained improvement in musculoskeletal disease activity in a large, multicentre, real-life cohort of patients with PsA, with early DAPSA reduction maintained through 12 months and paralleled by improvements in pain perception.

Second, GUS demonstrated effectiveness in several disease domains, with marked skin improvement, exploratory evidence of ASDAS improvement among patients diagnosed with axial PsA and progressive reduction in concomitant therapy use, particularly corticosteroids, providing a clinically intuitive, real-world picture of effectiveness extending beyond improvements in disease activity scores.

Third, patient-related factors, including sex, age, fibromyalgia, baseline disease activity, prior b/ts DMARDs exposure and corticosteroid use, were associated with the probability of achieving remission and/or treatment discontinuation, highlighting the relevance of clinical context in achieving stringent targets.

Fourth, despite high baseline disease burden and prevalent prior b/tsDMARD exposure, GUS showed favourable persistence and a reassuring safety profile, while acknowledging the potential for incomplete AE capture in real-world studies.

The primary aim of this study was to assess the real-world effectiveness on peripheral disease activity. We observed an early improvement in the DAPSA score, sustained through 12 months. In addition, the distribution of DAPSA categories shifted from predominately moderate-to-high disease activity toward LDA and remission, with DAPSA-LDA becoming the dominant category by 12 months.

Consistent improvements in pain and PtGA support the patient’s perceived clinical benefit, and the reduction of CRP levels suggests an overall anti-inflammatory effect.

Enthesitis and dactylitis, difficult domains in the management of PsA therapy, also significantly improved over follow-up. Enthesitis resolution increased progressively, whereas dactylitis showed high resolution rates from early timepoints, with few incident cases observed during follow-up.

Peripheral musculoskeletal efficacy of GUS has been established in phase III RCTs, including both bDMARD-naïve and bDMARD-experienced patients.7–12 In these trials, ACR response criteria, less commonly used in RWE, were the primary endpoints, limiting comparisons with our outcomes. Nevertheless, a pooled post hoc analysis from DISCOVER-1 and DISCOVER-2 trial reported 1-year DAPSA LDA and remission rates closely comparable to our findings, specifically of ~53% and ~18% for LDA and remission, respectively. 18

The 1-year enthesitis and dactylitis resolution rates in other pooled RCT analyses (~58% and ~75%, respectively) were also consistent with our results.19,20 Notably, lower resolution rates have been reported in RCTs focusing on bDMARD-experienced populations (~56% and ~67% for enthesitis and dactylitis, respectively), 21 which is notable given the high proportion of such patients in our cohort.

RWE from Italian and European cohorts confirms similar trends. Data from the Italian GISEA registry, including 170 patients treated with GUS, reported a numerically superior 1-year DAPSA improvement compared to our findings, with approximately 70% of patients achieving DAPSA LDA, and a 12-month drug retention of ~76%, with bDMARD-naïve status associated with a higher likelihood of achieving minimal disease activity. 22 A separate analysis of 111 patients by Ruscitti et al. 23 reported a 6-month DAPSA LDA rate of ~40%, with an 18-month drug retention of ~67%, without significant differences between clinical subgroups. Additional Italian and European real-world studies with smaller sample sizes have reported broadly comparable effectiveness and persistence outcomes.24–27

Although peripheral domains dominated the clinical phenotype, axial involvement was identified in a relevant subset of patients, in whom a reduction in the ASDAS score was observed over follow-up. However, interpretation of this outcome warrants caution for several reasons. First, MRI assessment was performed and interpreted locally, potentially introducing inter-centre variability. Second, imaging follow-up was not systematically available. Third, ASDAS score has not been specifically validated for axial PsA and includes patient-reported components that may be influenced by peripheral disease activity, pain perception and comorbid conditions. Growing data support the clinical effectiveness of GUS, and IL-23 axis inhibition, in the axial domain of PsA, both in post hoc analyses 28 and in dedicated RWE studies,29–31 showing outcomes comparable to those reported in our cohort. However, our findings regarding axial involvement are exploratory and should not be interpreted as clear evidence of axial effectiveness, but rather as a clinically relevant real-world signal, consistent with emerging literature and requiring confirmation in dedicated studies with appropriate classification of axial PsA. In this regard, a dedicated RCT is currently ongoing. 32

Cutaneous efficacy represents a well-established strength of IL-23 inhibition, and our real-world data confirm the high rates of PASI-90 and PASI-100 responses available in the literature,33,34 supporting the effective musculoskeletal and cutaneous control in routine clinical practice.

Treatment persistence is a real-world outcome reflecting not only clinical response but also safety, patient perception and healthcare-system factors. In this cohort, 12-month persistence was 78.7%, with clinically plausible lower retention among patients with multiple prior b/tsDMARDs.

In the absence of an internal comparator, treatment persistence may provide a useful contextual benchmark across real-world cohorts, although indirect comparisons across mechanisms should be interpreted very cautiously. Large RWE comparing multiple b/tsDMARDs persistence in PsA were mostly conducted before the availability of IL-23 inhibitors. In the PsABio study, the 1-year persistence was 80% for TNFi and 83.6% for ustekinumab, 35 whereas a French nationwide cohort reported substantially lower retention, with 1-year persistence of 36.8%, 46.3% and 56.0% for TNFi, IL-17i and ustekinumab, respectively. 36

More recent Italian and Spanish data are available for IL-17i, reporting 12-month retention rates of 77.5% and 81.0%, respectively.37,38 Large persistence data on JAK inhibitors are more limited; in the BIOBADASER registry, 1-year persistence in PsA was 60.6% for JAKi, compared with 69.3% for TNFi. 39

Regarding GUS persistence, comparable findings have been reported in the CorEvitas PsA/SpA Registry and in an Italian real-world analysis, which respectively reported 6-month and 1-year persistence of 79% and 80%.40,41 In addition, comparative observational studies reported long-term retention rate for GUS higher than other bDMARD classes, such as TNFi or IL-17i.42,43 Taken together, these data place the persistence observed in IMPACT within the range of available RWE benchmarks, without implying comparative superiority.

In our study, most patients remained on the standard q8w regimen; however, the proportion receiving q4w dosing increased from 4.9% at baseline to 14.1% at 12 months. Although the study was not designed to formally analyse the dosing regimen variation, this finding reflects the pragmatic use of dose escalation as a real-world strategy to manage partial or incomplete response. However, patients escalated to q4w likely represent a subgroup with more difficult-to-treat disease, and therefore no comparative interpretation between dosing regimens should be inferred.

The use of concomitant therapies, including csDMARDs, corticosteroids and NSAIDs, decreased over time. Although tapering decisions in observational cohorts are not standardised and may be influenced by multiple factors, including physician preference and local clinical practice, this overall reduction may still represent an indirect indicator of improved disease control in routine care.

Exploratory multivariable analyses provided useful insights into clinical factors associated with treatment outcomes. Female sex and older age were associated with a lower likelihood of achieving DAPSA remission, consistent with previous reports. 41 Higher baseline DAPSA was also inversely associated with remission, suggesting that a greater initial disease burden makes stringent remission targets more difficult to achieve. Fibromyalgia emerged as a strong negative predictor of remission, while also being associated with a lower risk of discontinuation. This apparently paradoxical finding is clinically coherent: fibromyalgia may inflate subjective components of DAPSA. In sensitivity analyses excluding patients with fibromyalgia, the absolute number of patients achieving DAPSA remission remained essentially unchanged despite the smaller cohort size, suggesting that very few patients with concomitant fibromyalgia achieved remission. This finding emphasises the potential limitations of treat-to-target strategies based on composite scores in patients with central sensitisation and the importance of careful interpretation of clinical scores in this context.

Regarding treatment discontinuation, prior b/tsDMARD exposure and baseline corticosteroid use were associated with a higher discontinuation risk, probably reflecting more severe or treatment-refractory disease.

Collectively, these predictors may help identify patients requiring closer monitoring and support a more realistic expectation of achievable targets within the first year of therapy. However, Cox regression analyses were exploratory, and they could be affected by model instability due to the limited number of events relative to covariates; sensitivity analysis with reduced models yielded consistent results, although a borderline deviation from the proportional hazards assumption was observed for the predictors of discontinuation, further supporting a cautious interpretation of these analyses.

Compared with previous real-world studies on GUS in PsA, IMPACT adds evidence from a large multicentre cohort enriched with patients previously exposed to b/tsDMARDs. This population likely reflects the Italian reimbursement criteria for GUS, which mainly restrict its use to patients with prior b/tsDMARD exposure, and therefore extrapolation of these findings to different healthcare systems should be performed cautiously. This study integrates longitudinal disease activity outcomes, treatment retention, predictors of remission and discontinuation, conservative sensitivity analyses accounting for attrition and stratified analyses according to prior b/tsDMARD exposure. This provides a pragmatic assessment of GUS performance in a heavily pretreated routine-care population, complementing available registry and multicentre evidence.

Limitations

Limitations are inherent to the observational design, including potential missing data, limited capture of granular variables, such as NSAID dosing or the assessment of nail psoriasis, and probable underreporting of minor AEs. In addition, post-discontinuation missing data over follow-up represent a common challenge in real-world studies and may have introduced attrition bias, potentially leading to some overestimation of observed response rates. To mitigate this issue, additional sensitivity analyses were performed, showing overall consistent results across outcomes, although a residual overestimation of effectiveness cannot be excluded. Furthermore, missing data regarding skin-related outcomes were handled with a complete-case approach, and this may have introduced selection bias. In this cohort, the real-world observational design limited the systematic collection of functional capacity data, precluding a reliable assessment of a comprehensive multidomain outcome such as minimal disease activity. This represents a relevant limitation, as reliance on individual disease activity measures, such as DAPSA, may not fully capture the multidomain nature of PsA.

Additional limitations concern both the assessment of axial involvement, for which axial outcomes should be considered exploratory as stated above, and the assessment of fibromyalgia, which was based on routine clinical evaluation rather than systematic application of formal classification criteria, potentially introducing inter-centre variability. Finally, the study was conducted in tertiary referral rheumatology centres, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other clinical settings.

Conclusion

In this large multicentre real-world cohort, GUS was associated with early and sustained improvement in several disease domains, favourable treatment persistence and no unexpected safety signals in a real-life PsA population, supporting its role as an effective option for psoriatic disease management in routine care.
