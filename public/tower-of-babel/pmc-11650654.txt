Carmaphycin B-Based Proteasome Inhibitors to Treat Human African Trypanosomiasis: Structure–Activity Relationship and In Vivo Efficacy
Authors: Lawrence
J. Liu, Karol R. Francisco, Yujie Uli Sun, Mateus Sá Magalhães Serafim, Dilini K. Amarasinghe, Thaiz R. Teixeira, Bobby Lucero, Thales Kronenberger, Waad Elsayed, Hala Elwakeel, Momen Al-Hindy, Jehad Almaliti, William H. Gerwick, Anthony J. O’Donoghue, Conor R. Caffrey
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The proteasome is essential for eukaryotic cell proteostasis, and inhibitors of the 20S proteasome are progressing preclinically and clinically as antiparasitics. We screenedTrypanosoma brucei, the causative agent of human and animal African trypanosomiasis, in vitro with a set of 27 carmaphycin B analogs, irreversible epoxyketone inhibitors that were originally developed to inhibit thePlasmodium falciparum20S (Pf20S). The structure–activity relationship was distinct from that of the human c20S antitarget by the acceptance of d-amino acids at the P3 position of the peptidyl backbone to yield compounds with greatly decreased toxicity to human cells. For the three most selective compounds, binding to the Tb20S β5 catalytic subunit was confirmed by competition with a fluorescent activity-based probe. For one compound, J-80, with its P3 d-configuration, the differential binding to the parasite’s β5 subunit was supported by both covalent and noncovalent docking analysis. Further, J-80 was equipotent against both Trypanosoma brucei gambiense and Trypanosoma brucei rhodesiensein vitro. In a mouse model of Stage 1 T. brucei infection, a single intraperitoneal (i.p.) dose of 40 mg/kg J-80 halted the growth of the parasite, and when given at 50 mg/kg i.p. twice daily for 5 days, parasitemia was decreased to below the detectable limit, with parasite recrudescence 48 h after the last dose. The in vivo proof of principle demonstrated by a potent, selective, and irreversible inhibitor of Tb20S reveals an alternative path to the development of kinetoplastid proteasome inhibitors that differs from the current focus on allosteric reversible inhibitors.

The Trypanosoma protozoan parasite causes the vector-borne disease, African Trypanosomiasis (AT) that affects both humans (HAT; Sleeping Sickness)1,2 and animals (AAT; Nagana).3 Approximately 70 million people throughout sub-Saharan Africa are at risk of infection,4 and two subspecies,Trypanosoma brucei rhodesienseandTrypanosoma brucei gambiense, are responsible for East and West African HAT, respectively.5 AAT is caused by a broader range of Trypanosoma spp. affecting hundreds of millions of sheep, cattle and goats, with annual cattle production losses being estimated at USD $1 billion.3 Transmission to humans occurs via the painful bite of an infected tsetse fly, and the disease progresses through two stages with different clinical manifestations: Stage 1 (hemolymphatic) may be asymptomatic or present flu-like symptoms, and the much more serious Stage 2 (meningo-encephalitic), which is associated with mood and behavioral changes, e.g., hallucinations and disruption of sleep cycles, that ultimately deteriorate to coma and death if the infection is not adequately treated.4,6

There is no vaccine7 and drug treatment depends on the infecting subspecies and stage of disease.8 Most of the traditional treatment options involve high frequency and/or long-duration parenteral administration of drugs that are associated with side effects,9 some potentially life-threatening,9,10 and which require significant health care resources that are challenging for what is a rural disease of poverty.11 The recent registration of the oral drug, fexinidazole,12 and the continued clinical progression of the oral drug candidate, acoziborole,13 will greatly simplify treatment. Yet, in spite of this encouraging progress, the ability of the Trypanosoma parasite to generate resistance,14,15 including cross resistance to some of the current drugs,16,17 underscores the need for pharmaco-readiness.

An exciting molecular target to have emerged over the last 20 years for the trypanosomatid parasites, i.e., African and South American trypanosomes, and Leishmania spp., is the proteasome.18−21 In humans, the 26S proteasome is a cytosolic, multisubunit, protein degradative complex that is required for proteostasis in the cell.22 The 26S complex comprises a barrel-shaped 20S proteasome core that is capped at either end by a 19S regulatory particle.23,24 The 20S core is composed of two rings of seven β subunits that are sandwiched between two rings of seven α subunits.25 The β1, β2 and β5 subunits are proteolytically active and have caspase-, trypsin- and chymotrypsin-like activities, respectively.26,27 Reversible allosteric inhibitors that bind to a unique interface between the β4 and β5 subunits of the trypanosomatid 20S proteasome to sterically block the latter’s hydrolytic activity, are in clinical development for treatment of visceral leishmaniasis.28

For the 20S proteasome of a number of protozoan29,30 and helminth31 parasites, our approach has been to directly inhibit the activities of the catalytic β subunits using synthetic analogs of carmaphycin B (CPB), a peptidyl, marine natural product derived from the cyanobacterium Symploca spp.32 CPB contains a C-terminal epoxyketone reactive group that covalently and irreversibly reacts with the active site threonine of the 20S catalytic β subunits.32 For the malaria parasite,Plasmodium falciparum, CPB analogs demonstrating nanomolar cidal activity in vitro and proof-of-principle efficacy in animal models of infection have advanced preclinically.29,33

Here, CPB and 27 analogs from the above-stated P. falciparum program were tested in vitro againstT. brucei. We describe the structure–activity relationship (SAR) that involves engagement of the Tb20S catalytic β5 subunit and the finding that a d-configuration at the P3 position of the peptidyl backbone generates large selectivity indices vs. mammalian cells. For one such compound, we demonstrate a therapeutic benefit in a mouse model of T. brucei infection, suggesting that mechanism-based, irreversible inhibitors of Tb20S might provide a new therapeutic strategy for treatment of AT and, possibly, other kinetoplastid diseases.

Results

SAR for Antitrypanosomal Activity In Vitro by CPB and Its Analogs

CPB was tested for activity against T. brucei Lister 427 in vitro using an established cell viability assay.34,35 After a 72 h incubation, an EC50 value of 31 nM was achieved, indicating that this compound has potent antitrypanosomal activity. However, CPB is also highly cytotoxic to HepG229,33 cells with a CC50 value of 13 nM. Thus, with its selectivity index (SI; HepG2 CC50/T. brucei EC50) of <1, CPB was too cytotoxic to test in a mouse infection model. Therefore, we evaluated a set of 27 analogs of CPB, many of which had reduced HepG2 cytotoxicity.

Carmaphycin B can be divided into four structural subunits: the leucine epoxyketone (EK) (P1), methionine sulfone (P2), valine (P3) and hexanoic acid (P4). The analogs tested here, for which a nomenclature has already been established,29,33 consist of a C-terminal EK warhead, various amino acid side chains at P1 through P3, and different N-terminal capping groups (Table 1). Many of the compounds in this series have a norleucine (Nle) in the P2 position and J-1 differs from CPB only by the replacement of the P2 methionine sulfone with Nle. This replacement resulted in a compound with at least a 130-fold lower antitrypanosomal activity, but also a 10-fold lower HepG2 cytotoxicity.

Examining first the P3 position, we compared l-Val in J-1 to l-Phe (J-2), 4-NH2-l-Phe (J-3), 4-Boc-NH-l-Phe (J-5) and l-Trp (J-6) and found that none improved the SI. However, J-6 was the most potent compound from this group with an EC50 value of 195 nM. We next tested four related analogs, J-7, J-9, J-10 and J-11, that had the same P2 l-Nle as J-2 through J-6 but where the P1 l-Leu had been replaced with l-Phe, and varied substituents at P3. The most selective analog (SI = 6) contained 4-NH2-l-Phe at P3 (J-9), with EC50 and CC50 values of 567 and 3,410 nM, respectively.

Next, P2 was examined, and three compounds with 4-NH2-l-Phe, 4-Boc-NH-l-Phe or l-Trp in the P2 position were coupled to derivatives with 4-NH2-l-Phe (J-12), 4-Boc-NH-l-Phe (J-14) or l-Trp (J-16) at the P3, respectively. Although these compounds were more potent (EC50 values <500 nM) than J-9, they were also more cytotoxic. J-5 (EC50 > 4000 nM) and J-14 (465 nM) both contain a P1 l-Leu and a P3 4-Boc-NH-l-Phe but differ at the P2 position (l-Nle vs. l-4-Boc-NH-l-Phe, respectively). The 10-fold increase in potency with J-14 indicates, therefore, a preference for bulky, aromatic groups at P2.

The next analog tested, J-19, had d-Trp at P3, l-Nle at P2 and l-Leu at P1. This inhibitor differed from J-6 only by the configuration of the P3 residue and had a similar antitrypanosomal activity (EC50 = 298 vs. 195 nM). However, J-19 had 286-fold lower HepG2 toxicity and, therefore, better selectivity. Substitution of the P1 l-Leu in J-19 for L-Phe in J-51 increased potency by 5-fold and toxicity by 3-fold, but with a slightly improved SI. Fixing l-Phe at P1 and l-Nle at P2, we evaluated four additional compounds with D-amino acids at P3, specifically, d-Val (J-50), D-(OtBu)-Glu (J-71), d-Glu (J-74) and d-Gln (J-76). Of these, J-71 was the most potent and selective compound with an SI of 122. These data reveal that a D-amino acid in the P3 position is the single biggest driver of selectivity for this group of compounds.

Finally, examining the P4 N-terminal cap, and using J-50 (EC50 = 319 nM) as a starting point, cyclic P4 substituents such as morpholine (J-53), cyclohexyl (J-55) and phenyl (J-56) exhibited robust antitrypanosomal activity with EC50 values ranging from 88 to 568 nM. By contrast, linear substituents such as octanoyl (J-54) and 2-(meth-oxymethoxy)acetyl (J-75) were inactive (>4000 nM). The striking difference in activity between the presence of hexanoyl (J-50; 319 nM) and octanoyl (J-54; >4000 nM) suggests a degree of steric sensitivity at the P4 position. Notably, the cyclization of P4 to cyclohexyl in J-55 (88 nM) led to improved potency against T. brucei. Overall, these data suggest some preference for hydrophobic cyclic over linear hydrocarbon groups at P4 that may also be limited by length.

The last set of four compounds (Table 2) was originally designed based on ML9, a reversible proteasome inhibitor of Mycobacterium tuberculosis 20S36 that was subsequently modified with an epoxyketone group.29 This set explored the P4 substitution of a 3-phenyl acetyl group for a hexanoyl as well as the P3 stereochemistry of an N,N-diethyl-l-Asn moiety. Whereas the P4 substitution had no major effect on antitrypanosomal activity, the L-configuration at P3 was slightly preferred, with 8.6- and 2.9-fold greater antitrypanosomal activity for J-77 vs. J-78, and J-79 vs. J-80, respectively. Although all four compounds were 36–155-fold less toxic to HepG2 cells, the P3 D-analogs were at least 1 order of magnitude less so than the L-analogs, with J-80 outstanding given its SI of 155. Interestingly, this ability to accommodate the D-configuration at P3 has been noted before for P. falciparum.29,33 In addition, it has been reported that P3 D-analogs preferentially engage the immunoproteasome (i20S) over the constitutive c20S proteasome.37 Overall, the ability to accept the D-configuration by T. brucei offers opportunities to identify inhibitors with increased selectivity.

CPB Analogs Engage the Tb20S β5 Subunit

We selected three compounds, J-71, J-77 and J-80 for follow-up studies as they shared similar potencies to that of pentamidine (Figure 1A), an approved drug for treatment of trypanosomiasis, and were the only compounds with selectivity values >80 (Figure 1B). To confirm their engagement of the Tb20S target, we used a commercially available activity-based probe, Me4BodipyFL-Ahx3Leu3VS,38 that binds covalently to the catalytic threonine residues of proteasomes and can be used to visualize the target protein via SDS-PAGE. This probe labeled two of the three catalytic subunits in T. brucei (Figure 1C), which a previous report identified as β2 (upper band) and β5 (lower band).39 When parasite lysates were preincubated with 0.1 to 10 μM of each compound for 1 h and then incubated with the probe we observed a concentration-dependent reduction in β5 labeling. Complete inhibition of β5 occurred in the presence of 1 μM of each compound. At 10 μM, J-71 also inhibited Tb20S β2 by approximately 84% based on densitometric analysis. The data suggest, therefore, that engagement of the β5 subunit is associated with the inhibition of T. brucei growth in vitro. Whether there is added value in inhibiting β2 in addition to β5 is not yet clear. It has been reported for P. falciparum that dual inhibition of β2 and β5 facilitated killing of all blood stage parasites while inhibition of β5 alone was only associated with activity against the schizont stage.40 Here, even though J-71 engaged Tb20S β2 at 10 μM (Figure 1C), the minimal concentration necessary to kill all of the parasites in vitro by this and the other inhibitors was <1 μM (Figure 1A), suggesting that β5 is the critical target.

Docking Studies with P3 d- and L-Configured Inhibitors in the 20S β5 Pocket Are Consistent with the In Vitro Bioactivity Data

J-80 was initially chosen for molecular docking studies with Tb20S β5, as it had the highest SI value and was confirmed to inhibit only the β5 subunit. An homology model of the Tb20S β5 subunit sequence (from Met103 to Ser309 using FASTA) was generated based on the crystal structure of c20S β5 (PDB ID: 8UD9), and both covalent41 and noncovalent42,43 docking simulations were performed. For comparison, similar docking simulations were also performed with the β5 subunit of human c20S.

Covalent docking simulations using Glide XP predicted J-80 to have eight potential interactions with the Tb20S β5 subunit (Figure 2A). The P1 residue (2,4-difluoro-l-Phe) interacted specifically with Lys33 and Gly47 (two interactions each), the P2 4-fluoro- l-Phe interacted with both Ser21 (two interactions) and Ser96, and the P3 N,N-diethyl-d-Asn interacted with Ala49. By contrast, J-80 formed only four potential interactions with c20S β5, involving Thr21 and Gly47 at P1, Thr21 at P2, and Ala49 at P3. Therefore, the greater selectivity of J-80 for T. brucei may be, at least in part, attributed to the change in the configuration at the P3 position and the stabilization of interactions with the other positions on the inhibitor.

To evaluate the selectivity provided by J-80 and its P3 d-amino acid substituent, we performed covalent simulations with the inhibitor’s P3 L-amino acid enantiomer, J-79 (Figure 2B), and with another pair of P3 d- and L-enantiomers, J-78 (Figure 2C) and J-77, respectively (Figure 2D). Similar to the simulations for J-80, J-78 was predicted to have fewer interactions (four) with c20S β5, compared to six with Tb20S β5. Also, the P3 d-amino acids of J-80 and J-78 occupy the S3 region in Tb20S β5 which is otherwise occupied by the P4 substituents of J-79 and J-77. Last, and unlike J-80 and J-78, the epoxyketone warhead of J-79 and J-77 is predicted to have additional interactions with both β5 targets. This would favor the stabilization of these inhibitors and is consistent with their lower EC50 and CC50 values in vitro compared to their P3 d-amino acid counterparts (Table 2).

Our noncovalent docking simulations, calculating both the GOLD scores (CHEMPLP fitness42) and binding affinities (kcal/mol)43 for Tb20S and c20S, were mostly consistent with the in vitro data generated for the inhibitors with the P3 d- or L- amino acid substituents. Large differences in the GOLD scores were predicted for the P3 d- inhibitors when bound to Tb20S β5 compared to c20S β5, i.e., 78.4 vs. 64.2, respectively, for J-80 (Figure 2A), and 74.3 vs. 52.2, respectively, for J-78 (Figure 2C). Similar trends were predicted for the respective binding affinities, i.e., −8.95 vs. −7.72 for J-80, and −8.68 vs. −8.56 for J-78. In contrast, for the P3 L-inhibitor counterparts, J-79 and J-77, the differences in the GOLD scores and binding affinities tended to be less. Thus, the GOLD scores were 84.1 and 72.5 for J-79 (Figure 2B), and 89.5 and 79.8 for J-77 (Figure 2D), when bound to Tb20S and c20S β5, respectively, and the binding affinity values for the respective targets were −9.16 and −8.81 for J-79, and −9.29 and −9.30, for J-77. Last, the GOLD scores and binding affinities indicated stronger interactions by the P3 L- configured inhibitors for both β5 targets compared to their D-configured enantiomers, consistent with their lower EC50 and CC50 values.

Finally, and focusing briefly on the N-terminal P4 substituent and its possible contribution to target binding by the inhibitors, covalent docking simulations predicted that neither the hexanoyl (J-80 and J-79) nor the cyclic (J-78 and J-77) substituent would interfere with binding to Tb20S β5 (Figure 2). Based on our simulations with J-54, which has a P4 octanoyl substituent and is inactive against the parasite, we were unable to predict whether or not the octanoyl contributes to that inactivity (Figure S1).

J-77 and J-80 Decrease Parasitemia in a Mouse Model of Stage 1 T. brucei Infection

The ability of the top three CPB analogs, J-71, J-77, and J-80, to interfere with the growth of T. brucei in a mouse model of Stage 1 infection was assessed. Briefly, six-week-old, female BALB/C female mice were infected (day 0) intraperitoneally (i.p.) with 100,000 T. b. brucei Lister 427 parasites. Parasitemia was established 2 days post infection (2 d.p.i.) as evidenced by the presence of 106–107 parasites/mL blood (Figure 3). Groups of three infected mice (n = 3) were then dosed once i.p. with 40 mg/kg of compound. Drug (pentamidine at 4 mg/kg once i.p.) and vehicle controls were also set up (each n = 3). After dosing, parasitemia was again measured 3 d.p.i.

In the vehicle control mice, parasitemia increased by almost two log orders to 108 parasites/mL from 2 to 3 d.p.i., whereas pentamidine decreased parasitemia to below the detectable limit of 2.5 × 105 parasites/mL (log10 value 5.4; Figure 3). Vehicle-treated mice were euthanized 3 d.p.i. to prevent mice dying from uncontrolled parasitemia. J-71 was seemingly ineffective as the increase in parasitemia after dosing was not statistically different from that of the vehicle control, whereas J-77 significantly limited the increase to 107.5 parasites/mL. By contrast, J-80 halted the proliferation of parasites whereby the level of parasitemia remained at ∼106.5 parasites/mL between days 2 and 3 d.p.i. Based on these data, J-80 was chosen for survival studies in mice infected with T. brucei.

J-80 Extends the Survival of Mice Infected with T. brucei

For survival studies, groups of five and three mice were set up for drug/compound and vehicle treatments, respectively. Pentamidine was administered as described above and apparently cured the mice as parasitemia never returned (not shown); also, the mice survived to the end of the experiment at 9 d.p.i. when they were euthanized (Figure 4B). By contrast, vehicle-treated mice developed parasitemia >108 parasites/mL on 3 d.p.i., and were euthanized. J-80 was administered twice daily (every 12 h) i.p. at 50 mg/kg for 5 days starting 2 d.p.i based on an intravenous dosing regimen that was determined not to be toxic to mice.29 With this dosing regimen, blood parasitemia levels remained below the limit of detection (2.5 × 105 parasites/mL), and up to 48 h after the cessation of dosing 6 d.p.i. (Figure 4A). Also, all of the treated mice appeared healthy and behaved normally during the dosing campaign. At 8 d.p.i., parasitemia became detectable in four of the five mice, and by day 9, parasitemia in all five mice ranged between 106.5 and 109 parasites/mL, at which point, the mice were euthanized, and the experiment concluded. Clearly, therefore, J-80 exerted a powerful antitrypanosomal effect.

Discussion

Two structurally similar triazolopyrimidine inhibitors of the trypanosomatid 20S proteasome are currently in clinical development for treatment of visceral leishmaniasis.28,44 Both drug candidates allosterically bind to an interface between the β4 and β5 subunits that is unique to kinetoplastids, blocking the catalytic activity of β5.45,46 Our focus with a number of parasites29−31,33 has been on the mechanism-based, covalent and irreversible inhibition of the 20S catalytic subunits using analogs of CPB, a peptidyl, epoxyketone natural product that was originally discovered in the marine cyanobacterium, Symploca sp.32

We screenedT. brucei in culture with a selection of compounds from a library of CPB analogs that had been synthesized in a campaign to identify inhibitors of the P. falciparum 20S proteasome (Pf20S).29,33 Many of these Pf20S inhibitors possessed D-stereochemistry at the P3 position which was accommodated well by the Pf20S β5 subunit and associated with impressive SI values relative to HepG2 cells29,33 Aside from factors like size limitations and preferred binding sites influencing T. brucei activity, a key finding arising from the in vitro SAR is that Tb20S also accommodates inhibitors with the d-configuration at P3, as exemplified by the two matched inhibitor pairs, J-77 vs. J-78, and J-79 vs. J-80, and as supported by the data from both the covalent and noncovalent docking simulations. Whereas all four of these inhibitors generated low- to mid-nanomolar EC50 values against the parasite, only those with the P3 d-configuration i.e., J-78 and J-80, generated CC50 values >20 μM against HepG2 cells.

Engagement of the intended Tb20S target by the top-performing CPB analogs, J-71, J-77, and J-80, was confirmed by binding-competition experiments with the activity-based probe, Me4BodipyFL-Ahx3Leu3VS, and visualization after SDS-PAGE. These experiments also indicated that inhibition of β5 is associated with parasite killing. Further, both the number of predicted interactions between J-80 (eight) and J-77 (seven) with the Tb20S β5 subunit identified by covalent docking, and the scores generated by the noncovalent docking simulations, were consistent with the in vitro cellular data. Tb20S β2 was also engaged by one of the three top inhibitors (J-71) but only at 10 μM, a concentration greater than that necessary to kill the parasite in culture. We were unable to judge whether β1 was also targeted by any of the top three CPB analogs as it could not be visualized by the activity-based probe, however, this seems unlikely given that the set of CPB analogs tested primarily react with Pf20S β5.29,33

That the inhibition of β5 is associated with trypanocidal activity is consistent with the known importance of this subunit for the survival of other parasites, including P. falciparum,29L. donovani44,45 and Trypanosoma cruzi(47) For P. falciparum, inhibition of Pf20S β5 is sufficient to decrease the survival of synchronous trophozoites48 and schizogony.40 Added value is brought by coinhibition of two or more subunits, whereby all the intraerythrocytic life stages are killed in vitro.40,49 This value extends to the demonstration that inhibition of β2 can overcome β5 resistance40,50 and also synergize with dihydroartemisinin to kill early ring stage parasites.51 These studies highlight the importance of understanding the relative contribution of individual Tb20S β subunits to survival, especially if drug resistance were to emerge to one or more subunit.

The three top-performing CPB analogs, J-71, J-77, and J-80, were tested for their ability to decrease blood parasitemia in a mouse model of Stage 1 infection by T. brucei. After a single i.p. dose, J-80 stood out as being the only compound to halt growth of the parasite over a 24 h period. Further, using a twice daily i.p. dosing regimen over 5 days, J-80 decreased parasitemia to below the detectable limit and prolonged the survival of infected mice, which appeared healthy, with parasite recrudescence only occurring 48 h after the last dose. This in vivo proof-of-principle by a mechanism-based proteasome inhibitor of Tb20S is encouraging. Nonetheless, the short plasma half-life of 2.4 h for J-80(29) necessitated its twice-daily delivery to demonstrate efficacy and, likely, contributed to the lack of cure. Further research with next-generation CPB analogs will aim to improve metabolic stability and demonstrate oral bioavailability while maintaining or improving potency and selectivity. In addition, the issue of blood-brain barrier penetrance to clear stage 2 T. brucei infection must be dealt with to eventually deliver a single-dose oral cure of HAT, as is currently the case for the drug candidate, acoziborole.13,52

Conclusions

By whole-organism screening of T. bruceiin vitro with a selection of CPB analogs designed to inhibit the Pf20S proteasome, we discovered several compounds with nanomolar antitrypanosomal potency and large selective indices with respect to HepG2 cells. We identified the Tb20S β5 subunit as the key target. Underlying the large selectivity values was the ability of Tb20S β5 to accommodate a d-amino acid at the P3 position of the peptidyl inhibitor backbone, in contrast to the c20S β5 antitarget. One compound, J-80, which was equipotent against all threeT. brucei subspecies, halted parasite growth and prolonged host survival in a mouse model of Stage 1T. brucei infection. Thus, far, the SAR for Tb20S seems similar to that demonstrated in an ongoing program targeting Pf20S, suggesting that further “piggybacking” opportunities may exist to accelerate the identification of potent and selective Tb20S inhibitors.

Methods

CPB Analogs

CPB and analogs were synthesized as described previously.29,33 Compounds were stored at 10 mM in 100% DMSO in 96-well, u-bottomed polypropylene plates that were stored at −80 °C.

T. brucei Viability Assay

SYBR green was used to measureT. brucei cell viability as previously described.34,35 Briefly, compounds were serially diluted in DMSO (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 85190) across eight concentrations ranging from 4 μM to 0.87 nM. Parasites in log-phase growth were suspended at 2 × 105 parasites/mL in HMI-9 medium under continuous agitation. Cells were dispensed as 100 μL aliquots into 96-well black or white plates with flat and clear bottoms (Corning, Cat. No. 3903) containing 1 μL of compound in 0.5% DMSO. Additional medium (99 μL) was added, and the plates were incubated for 72 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Parasites were lysed with 50 μL/well of lysis solution (30 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 7.5 mM EDTA, 0.012% saponin) (Alfa Aesar, Cat. No. A18820) and 0.12% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. T8787) containing 0.3 μL/mL SYBR Green I (10,000× in DMSO; Lonza, Cat. No. 50513). Plates were incubated in the dark for at least 1 h at r.t. Fluorescence was measured in a 2104 EnVision multilabel plate reader (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA) at 485/535 excitation/emission wavelengths. The activity of test compounds was normalized to those of the DMSO controls in the same plate. Concentration response curves and EC50 values, i.e., the concentration of compound required to generate a 50% inhibition of the parasite growth rate, were calculated using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, www.graphpad.com). At least three biological replicates each in triplicate were performed for each CPB analog.

Docking Studies

Modeling the Tb20S β5 Subunit

The Tb20S β5 sequence, truncated from Met103 to Ser309 (FASTA) was modeled after the human c20S antitarget β5 subunit (PDB ID: 8UD9). Modeling employed Maestro (Schrödinger Release 2024.1 Maestro, Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2024) and OPLS4 force-field54 as templates utilizing the advanced homology modeling tool available in the Schrödinger suite (v2023.2). Missing amino acid’s side chains were placed using Prime, followed by loop refinement using the same software. Proteins were prepared using Protein Preparation Wizard (Schrödinger LLC, New York, NY, 2024). Missing hydrogen atoms were added, bond orders were assigned using the CCD database, and protonation states of amino acids were optimized with PROPKA55 (Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2023) at pH 7.4. Given the truncated nature of the models, both the N- and C-termini were capped. Final structures were optimized for hydrogen positions using the H-bond assignment algorithm, PROPKA, in the Protein Preparation Wizard tool of Maestro, to select the most likely protonation states and tautomer for the histidine residues. Acceptance of the software suggestions was followed by optimization of the generated H-bonding species. Finally, all structures underwent global energy minimization using the steep descent method.

Covalent Molecular Docking and Pose Selection

Prior to docking simulations, ligands were prepared using LigPrep (Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2024) to assign the protonation state (at pH 7.0 ± 1.0) and the partial charges. Simulations were performed using Glide v7.7 with extra precision (XP). Initially, noncovalent docking was calculated within a pocket of 13 Å around cocrystallized ligands of the original templates. These generated up to 10 poses per ligand that were used as a starting point for covalent docking. Next, using CovDocking, covalent docking poses were generated with similar parameters by selecting an epoxy opening reaction with Thr107, i.e., Thr1, as the reactive residue. Representative poses were selected based on the docking score and by visual inspection of the warhead position covalently bound to the threonine.

Noncovalent Molecular Docking Consensus Scoring

Additional noncovalent docking was performed using GOLD 5.142 and DockThor43 (https://dockthor.lncc.br/v2/) in a consensus approach with two different scoring systems (i.e., algorithms), CHEMPLP fitness and affinity (kcal/mol), respectively. Simulations with GOLD were performed by building a default grid box of 10 Å around the cocrystallized inhibitor TDI-830456 bound to the c20S β5 subunit, which was inserted57 into the modeled Tb20S β5 structure. The parameters used in GOLD were: (i) all protein rotatable bonds were fixed; (ii) the binding site was defined with the cocrystallized ligand or the inserted position with all atoms within 10 Å; (iii) chemscore_kinase was used as the template; (iv) 200 genetic algorithm runs performed; (v) CHEMPLP used as the scoring function with no early termination allowed; and (vi) the genetic algorithm search option was set as slow. The representative pose with the highest fitness score was selected by visual inspection among the top three predicted poses. Simulations with DockThor considered the inserted grid box with a default size of 20 × 20 × 20 Å and were conducted with standard run analysis and no soft docking. A maximum of 24 generated poses was simulated considering RMSD values ≤2.0 Å for the cluster conformers. The pose with the highest affinity score (kcal/mol) was selected from among the top three predicted poses.

CPB Competition Assay with the Activity-Based Probe

CulturedT. brucei was aspirated from flasks and centrifuged for 10 min at 24,000g at 4 °C three times. After each centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded and the pellet was resuspended in PBS before centrifugation again. After the final centrifugation, the supernatant was removed, and the cell pellet were stored at −80 °C.

PBS containing 10 μM E64 was used as a lysis buffer and approximately twice the volume was added relative to the volume of the pellet so that a final concentration of 5–10 mg/mL was achieved. Cell lysis was performed on ice using a Qsonica CL-18 sonicator probe set to pulses of 2 s with 5 s rest periods over a total of 44 s. Sonicated samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 11,000g and 4 °C. The supernatant was recovered, aliquoted, flash frozen in a dry ice/acetone bath and stored at −80 °C. The protein concentration was measured using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay kit (Fisher #23227).

T. brucei lysate was diluted to a final concentration of 1 μg/μL and 14 μL was incubated with 1 μL of the specified CPB analog or DMSO concentration, diluted in 100% DMSO, for 1 h at 37 °C. Afterward, 1 μL Me4BodipyFL-Ahx3Leu3VS (Ubiqbio #UbiQ-018), diluted in 100% DMSO, was added so that a final concentration of 2 μM was achieved and coincubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Next, 8 μL 4× Bolt LDS sample buffer (Fisher #B0007) containing 250 mM DTT was added to each sample and boiled for 5 min at 95 °C. Samples were then loaded on a 4–12% Bis-Tris gel (Fisher #NW04127BOX) with 1× MOPS running buffer (Fisher #NP000102). Gels were first electrophoresed at 60 V until the dye front was approximately 15% below the comb, at which point, the voltage was increased to 120 V for the duration of electrophoresis. Gels were washed with water after electrophoresis and fixed for 5 min in 6:3:1 water/ethanol/acetic acid. Fluorescent imaging was performed using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP Imaging System with the settings optimized to visualize Qdots 525 (Bodipy). Densitometric analysis was accomplished using Bio-Rad Image Lab 6.1.

Mouse Model Efficacy Study

Vertebrate animal use was supported under a protocol approved by UC San Diego′s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Approval was granted under the Animal Welfare Act and Regulations (AWAR) Act, and the United States Public Health Service Policy on Humane Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Infections and treatments were set up and executed essentially as described.34 Briefly, six-week-old, wild-type, BALB/c, female mice were infected with 1 × 105T. b. brucei Lister 427 cells by i.p. injection. Mice were divided into groups of three or five, and 48 h after infection, compound or vehicle alone was administered i.p. in 9% DMSO and 91% corn oil at the doses indicated in the Results section. Pentamidine (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No. P0547) was used as reference drug.

Blood parasitemia was measured by microscopic examination of tail blood every 24 h starting at 48 h postinfection. Specifically, 5 μL blood was withdrawn and diluted immediately with 5 μL heparin solution (0.5 mg/mL in sterile water) before administering drug. Samples were diluted a further 100-fold in PBS at 37 °C and the parasites counted using a Neubauer chamber. The lower limit for accurate counting of cells in a Neubauer chamber is 2.5 × 105 cells/mL. Mice were monitored every day for general appearance and behavior.
