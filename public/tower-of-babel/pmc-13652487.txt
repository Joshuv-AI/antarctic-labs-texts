Introduction

Liver mucinous cystic neoplasms account for less than 5% of all liver cysts[1]. Pre-operative diagnosis remains challenging, often leading to misdiagnosis and delays in diagnosis. Therefore, recognizing their radiological features may be helpful.

Herein, we present a case of liver mucinous cystic neoplasm with a challenging diagnosis. This report was prepared in accordance with the SCARE criteria[2].

Presentation of case

A 43-year-old woman with no-comorbidities, presented with complaints of dull, aching pain in the left hypochondrial region lasting 6 months. Physical examination was normal. Biological exams were within normal ranges. Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated a single hypoechogenic cyst within the left hepatic lobe, 18.4 cm in diameter. Abdominal computed tomography was then performed, showing a hypodense cystic lesion of the left hepatic lobe with septa (Fig. 1). It compressed the hepatic pedicle, the superior biliary confluence, the main portal vein, the left hepatic vein, and the retro-hepatic inferior vena cava. It was adherent to the pancreatic head, the pylorus, the first portion of the duodenum, and the left transverse colon, without invasion. Hepatic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) concluded there was a multilobulated cyst, hyperintense on T2-weighted imaging and hypointense on T1-weighted imaging, with multiple enhancing septa and no communication with the biliary tree (Fig. 2). During a multidisciplinary consultation meeting, the diagnosis of a liver mucinous cystic neoplasm was made after ruling out the differential diagnoses, especially a liver hydatid cyst, a simple biliary cyst, and intraductal papillary neoplasm of the bile duct. In fact, even though our country is endemic for liver hydatid cysts, on the one hand the radiological features were not in favor given the presence of septa, and on the other hand the hydatid serology was negative. Similarly, simple biliary cysts do not show septa on radiological imaging. As for intraductal papillary neoplasm of the bile duct, the presence of enhancing septa on MRI was in favor, while the absence of communication with the bile ducts ruled it out. Hence, the diagnosis of liver mucinous cystic neoplasm was made. Because of its malignant potential, a left hepatectomy was indicated in a multidisciplinary consultation meeting. A left hepatectomy was performed via a Makuuchi incision a week later (Fig. 3). The anatomopathological examination of the surgical specimen revealed a cyst lined by a single layer of bland, cuboidal to low-columnar mucinous epithelium. The underlying stroma was composed of fibrocollagenous tissue with collections of histiocytes. No signs of malignancy were observed, leading to the diagnosis of non-invasive mucinous cystic neoplasm. Since the diagnosis was clear, no immunohistochemical study was performed. The postoperative course was uneventful except for a grade A bile leak, with good progress under medical treatment. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 12. She was closely followed in the outpatient department without recurrence after 3 years of follow-up, based on clinical assessments every 3 months and alternating computed tomography and MRI every 6 months. Discussion

Our case illustrated a rare entity with a challenging diagnosis. In fact, a liver mucinous cystic neoplasm could easily be misdiagnosed, especially in endemic areas such as our country. Hence, appropriate evaluation, such as magnetic resonance imaging, had to be performed.

Liver mucinous cystic neoplasm was defined by the World Health Organization in 2010 as a cyst-forming epithelial neoplasm, usually with no communication with the bile ducts. It is composed of cuboidal to columnar, variably mucin-producing epithelium associated with ovarian-type subepithelial stroma[3].

It occurred, as in our case, in women in their fifth and sixth decades[3]. It is mostly asymptomatic. Otherwise, non-specific symptoms are encountered, as in our patient: abdominal pain and distention secondary to the space-occupying effect[4]. Only 20% of cases present with elevated serum bilirubin and liver function tests due to biliary compression[5].

Since the differential diagnosis was broad, radiological features were crucial (Table 1). These diagnoses varied from benign to malignant, including hydatid cysts in endemic areas – as in our case – and intraductal papillary neoplasm of the bile duct[6,7]. Abdominal ultrasound can demonstrate a single, multilocular cystic mass with thick, uneven walls and echogenic septa [8]. Unilocular cystic masses, as in our patient, were reported in only 6–10% of cases [8]. The average size was 11 cm, ranging from 5 to 23 cm[8]. Location within the liver was the left lobe in 75% of cases [9]. Computed tomography demonstrated a uni- or multi-locular low-attenuation mass, with or without septations, associated with a thickened and/or irregular wall[10]. MRI features depended on cyst fluid protein content[10]. On T2-weighted images, septations appeared as linear low-signal-intensity structures within a high-intensity cyst[11]. Necrotic portions were hypointense on diffusion-weighted MRI, contrasting with the hyperintensity seen in cases of hepatic abscess[12]. Surgical excision is still the treatment of choice, as in our patient, because of the risk of malignant transformation[10]. It must be complete, without effraction; otherwise, recurrence and a worsened prognosis have been reported[10]. Median recurrence-free survival was 12.1 years (95% confidence interval [CI] 6.5–18.1) after complete excision[13]. The recurrence rate was 10% after complete excision versus 15.7% after incomplete excision[13].

Anatomopathological aspects allowed the ultimate diagnosis, as in our patient[14]. Immunohistochemistry should be carried out in unclear cases with difficulty visualizing the stromal layer and/or limited mucin production[14]. Ovarian-type stroma is an entity-defining feature corresponding to densely packed spindle cells. It is associated with elongated nuclei and scant cytoplasm, with variable amounts of collagen between the inner epithelium and the outer capsule[9].

After surgical resection for non-malignant mucinous cystic lesions, the median recurrence-free survival was 12.1 years (95% CI 6.5–18.1) compared with 8.4 years for cystadenocarcinomas[14]. Hence, surveillance modalities were not standardized.

Conclusion

Liver mucinous cystic neoplasms are rare tumors that are often diagnosed late due to insufficient recognition. Hence, radiological workup plays a crucial role. Complete surgical excision remains the treatment of choice.
