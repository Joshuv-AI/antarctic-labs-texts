“A Change in Culture From It Being an Optional Extra to an Integral Part of Education”—Teacher's Experiences of Teaching Relationships and Sexuality Education (RSE) to Learners With Severe/Profound Intellectual Disabilities in the Republic of Ireland
Authors: Leanne Connolly, Kathryn O'Mahony, Conor Scully
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAccording to UNESCO (2018), there is an increased need for research on teacher effectiveness regarding sexuality education. This study aims to respond to this need through capturing the views, experiences, and preparedness of teachers to support the teaching of Relationships and Sexuality Education (RSE) to learners with Severe/Profound Intellectual Disabilities in Special Schools in the Republic of Ireland (ROI).Method & ResultsResponding teachers (n = 34) to the predominantly quantitative online survey describe a lack of pre‐requisite knowledge and skills required to teach RSE to learners with Severe/Profound Intellectual Disabilities, with a lack of access to professional learning opportunities or appropriate resources.ConclusionMany teachers are not teaching RSE and therefore students who can be described as most vulnerable, are not accessing their human‐right to sexuality education. Teachers identified the supports required as Continuing Professional Development (CPD), training & support; accessible & appropriate resources; and a dedicated programme and toolkit.Keywords: human rights, Ireland, severe profound intellectual disability, sexuality education, teacher

Background and Context

History of RSE Provision

The teaching of Relationship and Sexuality Education (RSE) to children and young people internationally has been influenced by developments in human rights legislation such as the United Nations Convention of the Rights of the Child (UNCRC) (UN 1989) and more specifically for people with disabilities through the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD) (UN 2006). Article 25 (a) outlines that people with disabilities have the right to the same quality of sexual health and reproductive health programmes as provided to others (UN 2006), with literature indicating that high quality sex education provision is fundamental to the realisation of this right (Bourke et al. 2022).

Despite these rights, prejudice and discrimination exist in relation to people with intellectual disabilities' right to sexual autonomy and expression (Healy et al. 2009; Stewart et al. 2015). Ableist views of desexuality and representations of innocence (Azzopardi‐Lane 2022) may render their sexuality invisible, which contributes to barriers to appropriate education (Nelson et al. 2020).

On a national level, children's rights to Social, Personal and Health Education (SPHE) through which relationships and sexuality education (RSE) is taught, were enshrined into the Education Act (Government of Ireland 1998) in Ireland. Children's rights to protection are also provided for in the Child Care Act (Government of Ireland 1991), Children First Act (2015a) and accompanying guidelines and procedures (Government of Ireland 2001, 2004, 2017, 2023, 2025; Department of Children and Youth Affairs 2011). Therefore while children's rights to RSE and protection from abuse were enshrined in domestic law two decades prior to Ireland's ratification of the UNCRPD (2006) in 2018, Ireland has now committed to upholding the rights of young people with disabilities, including the right to sexuality education (Michielsen and Brockschmidt 2021). However, Ireland has a negative history of upholding the protection and welfare of children within education settings from sexual abuse (Morrissey 2021). From the early 1990s, both policy and curricular changes were instigated on foot of a number of national commission investigations (Morrissey 2021) and an extraordinary rise in the number of abuse cases reported (Johnston 2022). Lack of access to or inadequate sexuality education (McDaniels and Fleming 2016) in addition to added vulnerabilities has resulted in an increased likelihood for people with intellectual disabilities to be victims of abuse (UNESCO 2009), with higher rates of multiple incidents of sexual violence reported throughout and beyond childhood into adulthood (Rape Crisis Network Ireland, RCNI 2011).

International guidance states that to be impactful, “comprehensive sexuality education must become part of the formal school curriculum, delivered by well‐trained and supported teachers” (UNESCO 2018, iii). The consideration for RSE within the context of curricula in Ireland began only 30 years ago, with its provision becoming mandatory in 1999 in primary schools and 2003 in post‐primary schools. In the past decade, since the commencement of the Children's First Act (2015) in late 2017, RSE has gained significant attention both from policymakers and from the media.

Following a high profile sexual abuse case in 2018, alongside wider conversations regarding consent and sexual assault with the growing attention of the #MeToo Movement (Jaffe et al. 2021), pressure was placed on the Government to address issues of consent, particularly in the context of post‐primary curricula (McKay 2018). A review of RSE was initiated (National Council for Curriculum and Assessment [NCCA] 2019) and the Provision of Objective Sex Education Bill 2018 (an alteration to the Education Act 1998) was passed. In the years that followed, curricular developments continued at pace, but predominantly for the mainstream RSE curricula.

RSE in Irish Education System

In the context of child protection and the prevention of abuse and neglect, RSE is mandatory in all primary, special and post‐primary schools. Since the commencement of the Children First Act (2015) in December 2017, schools must report to their Board of Management on an annual basis declaring their full implementation of the SPHE curriculum, including RSE.

However, despite the mandatory nature of RSE, Irish research has demonstrated that “for most schools the teaching of RSE presents a challenge” (Keating et al. 2018, 19) even within the context of mainstream provision. Curricula for post‐primary SPHE/RSE have been continually updated in recent years at both Junior Cycle for the first 3 years of post‐primary education and Senior Cycle, the final 2–3 years of post‐primary education. However, developments for curricula at primary level or for students with intellectual disabilities have not progressed to the same extent, with special schools being “the forgotten sector” (Banks in OCO 2022, 38).

Instead, schools supporting more complex learners must differentiate and adapt the currently available resources that were designed for a mainstream cohort of learners (Morrissey et al. 2025) and do not address the cognitive differences of learners with special educational needs and disabilities (SEND). This reflects international experiences also, with a lack of suitable and appropriate resources available to learners with severe/profound intellectual disabilities (Strnadová et al. 2022), with teachers reporting cognitive abilities and level of student comprehension as a challenge (Borawska‐Charko et al. 2023). For students with Severe/Profound Intellectual Disabilities of post‐primary age, whether in special or mainstream schools, teachers must rely on curriculum guidelines published in 2007 or employ the more recent “Personal Care” learning outcomes from the revised Junior Cycle curriculum (Department of Education and Skills 2015) for students with general learning disabilities. These learning outcomes are presented to teachers without any Department of Education or Department of Health teaching resources, Continuing Professional Development (CPD) or supports.

Following a review of the provision of SPHE/RSE at primary and post‐primary schools in Ireland by the NCCA in 2019, a recommendation was made to consider how support could be provided to in‐service teachers, with co‐operation from the various Department of Education (DE) funded education support services with a specific consideration of “how best to meet the needs of students with intellectual disabilities” through the provision of CPD (NCCA 2019, 76). The review also identified a skills gap in post‐primary teachers specifically, owing to “the fact that SPHE is the only subject at post‐primary level that can be taught without any qualification or accreditation from the Teaching Council”, which the NCCA described as impacting “negatively on the status of the subject and on teacher confidence” (NCCA 2019, 75). As a result, the Department of Education awarded a tender to Dublin City University (DCU) to create a “first‐of‐its kind course” in the Republic of Ireland to “help post‐primary teachers and schools in the provision of the new SPHE/RSE curriculum for junior cycle and senior cycle students” (DCU 2022; DE 2022).

Despite the creation of a dedicated qualification pathway for post‐primary teachers to respond to the evident skills gap, the same level of action did not occur for the gap identified for supporting students with intellectual disabilities. This represents a challenge in the Irish education system and the value being placed on special educational needs more broadly.

Currently in Ireland, student teachers at primary level feel unprepared to teach RSE to students with SEND upon leaving Initial Teacher Education (Maunsell et al. 2021). Post‐Primary teachers do not have access to RSE within their subject specific qualification unless they obtain a place on the DE Post‐Graduate course (NCCA 2019), which, while it encompasses all aspects of SPHE and RSE, does not have a specific SEND focus. Once teachers obtain a teaching role supporting learners with SEND, they then have no access to in‐service professional development or adequate teaching resources to support them in teaching RSE to learners with SEND.

The overall aim of this current project is to develop Communities of Practice (COP's) between Special Schools for students with Severe/Profound Intellectual Disabilities in the Republic of Ireland to support the sharing of best practice in teaching approaches for RSE and to develop Irish based, teacher led resources and supports for RSE. This paper describes the results of one phase of this study which aimed to capture the views of teachers in Severe/Profound Special Schools in the Republic of Ireland through an online survey. Specifically, it sought to capture current RSE teaching practices; teachers' views and attitudes towards RSE provision; facilitators and barriers to teaching RSE; professional development and resource needs in order to support the teaching of RSE.

Methods

Ethical Approval and Recruitment

Participants were directed to the plain language statement and informed consent form in the Qualtrics Platform. Progression to the survey questions was prohibited unless consent was provided. Participants were made aware of the voluntary nature of their engagement as well as their rights to withdraw prior to submission of the survey response. As confidentiality and anonymity were paramount in the study, the survey questions did not gather any personal or school information or gather corresponding email addresses. Awareness was drawn to the use of the findings, communication of results and the inclusion of direct quotes from participants in research publications.

Ethical approval was obtained from DCU in June 2022 [DCUREC/2022/105]. The anonymous survey was open to any teacher supporting a learner with Severe/Profound Intellectual Disability in a Special School in the Republic of Ireland (ROI). The DE publicly available database of schools was accessed in February 2023 and a complete list of special schools obtained. Examining the DE list of special schools (n = 136), thirteen were identified as supporting students with Severe/Profound Intellectual Disabilities. As direct mailing to teachers was not possible, the schools' publicly available email address was used as the main recruitment tool. Alongside this, invitations to participate were circulated across the social media platform X and through graduate teacher networks associated with DCU.

Research Design

The online predominantly quantitative survey was designed by the lead researcher in the licensed DCU Qualtrics account. The survey gathered basic demographic data in order to better understand teachers' working context. The main survey items were predominantly Likert scale questions, which were appropriate due to the desire to capture views and attitudes towards RSE teaching practices. A bipolar five‐point scale from strongly agree to strongly disagree was used and included a neutral midpoint. This was deemed appropriate for this research as it avoids a forced‐choice and can increase reliability and validity (Blair et al. 2014). A small number of open‐text questions facilitated participants to expand on their answers and provide supporting qualitative data to further explain the quantitative data.

Demographics

The survey was closed in March 2025 with all data downloaded and imported to Microsoft Excel for analysis. There were 84 total responses to the survey, however after excluding responses that were not complete to at least 78%, 34 responses were included for analysis. According to the NCSE publicly available staffing allocations for 2022–2023, there were 112 teachers allocated to those thirteen schools (NCSE 2022). With 34 teachers completing the survey to at least 78%, the response rate was 30.3%. Due to the small sample size some caution is needed when considering the generalisability of the findings however the data provides important information from highly specialised cohort of teacher.

Of the 34 respondents, 50% held a post‐primary teaching qualification (n = 17) including Music (n = 1) and Physical Education (n = 1), with 32.4% (n = 11) holding a primary school teaching qualification. Two teachers held Montessori qualifications (5.95%), three with special educational needs qualifications, and one with a post‐graduate diploma in adult and further education.

Of the 33 teachers that declared their number of years teaching experience, 42.4% were teaching learners with severe/profound intellectual disabilities for 1 year or less (n = 14). Nine teachers had taught this cohort for 2–3 years (27.3%), six teachers had more than 10 years' experience with this cohort (18.2%) and four teachers had between 4 and 10 years' experience (12.1%). The 33 teachers supported students with age ranges of eight and under (n = 5), nine to twelve (n = 7), thirteen to fifteen (n = 7), fourteen to eighteen (n = 3). Eleven teachers supported students of varying age ranges.

Data Analysis

The quantitative data was analysed using descriptive statistics by the third author using Microsoft Excel. As the majority of the questions were yes/no or Likert scale questions, frequency charts were generated within Excel and then exported to Google Docs for sharing with the first and second authors. This allowed for clear identification of the percentage agreement to Likert and responses to yes/no questions and other multiple‐choice questions. In each occurrence, the valid percent was reported, and these percentages were then represented in a table and the number of responses to each question identified. This ensured an accurate representation of the response to each question and mitigated against missing responses skewing the data.

Qualitative responses to the open‐ended questions within the survey were exported to Microsoft Excel for qualitative analysis separately by the first author. Firstly, all comments were organised into each corresponding question. Each comment was read in full within the context of the question posed and headings were created in Excel which captured the common themes of the responses. Using frequency tallying, the comments were coded to themes. Finally, the coded themes were tallied for frequency and percentage.

It was important to be aware of the impact of quantifying qualitative data in this phase of the study. While the comments were coded to main themes that were consistent across the data, any outlier comments or comments that contrasted with the themes were noted and reported on also. This was done through the inclusion of direct quotes from participants which supported, challenged, or further qualified quantitative answers given.

Of the 34 respondents, almost half (n = 14) indicated that at the time of the survey they do not currently teach or have not taught RSE to their students, with 58.8% (n = 20) indicating that they do teach RSE. Curriculum

When asked to indicate the curriculum through which they were teaching RSE, the majority (n = 25) selected the NCCA Primary Curriculum for Severe/Profound General Learning Disabilities. A smaller percentage (45.4%) were teaching RSE through the Junior Cycle Level One Learning Programme (n = 15), with one teacher using the Junior Cycle Level Two Learning Programme and one teaching the Junior Infant Primary Curriculum. While 33 teachers replied to this question indicating the curriculum they are offering for RSE, 14 of them had already indicated that they are not teaching RSE. This suggests that the teachers answered this question based on the class level curriculum that they are offering for the teaching of all subjects rather than specifically RSE.

Teaching Resources and Reasons for Their Selection

Teachers were asked to select from a range of multiple‐choice options to describe where they sourced the resources which they find particularly supportive when teaching RSE. Of the 31 responding teachers, 58% of them sourced them from colleagues (n = 18), 48.4% online (n = 15), 38.7% from CPD or training (n = 12), 32.3% from other professionals (n = 10), and 12.9% from other sources including designing themselves and using existing classroom resources from previous teachers (n = 4).

While 72.7% (n = 24) indicated that neither they nor their colleagues had designed bespoke resources for the teaching of RSE in their school, their responses to the follow up qualitative question demonstrated a reliance on “teacher made resources, work sheets with simple instructions and objects of reference” (Teacher #2), social stories, visuals and objects of reference. While eight teachers referred to using the DE Stay Safe & Walk Tall Programmes, as well as the current NCCA Curriculum Guidelines, four teachers stated that they were not using any resources for teaching RSE.

Teachers explained the challenge with the currently available resources from the DE/DOH:

Stay safe and Walk tall we use but to be honest I don't find them great for children with SPGLD's. I usually have to differentiate it right down to the absolute very basic method of teaching. (Teacher #5)

Similarly, Teacher #33 described how “we find the existing materials outdated and of little support. We use social stories, objects of reference to implement most of the topics we cover”. This dearth of appropriate materials has led to some schools creating their own materials, as described by Teacher #15:

Our school has designed their own RSE packs which I feel are pitched better at our students in comparison to the Stay safe bodies. We also use the Healthy Bodies resources…We have visuals or objects that support the learning. We have designed social stories. (Teacher #15)

This was supported by Teacher #22, stating that they use a “school developed program adapted from different mostly UK based programmes” with Teacher #13 adding that in the absence of appropriate guidance, “another school's programme is informing our decisions around policy”. Additionally, teachers describe creating “social stories for different aspects of RSE such as masturbation, hugging, periods etc.” (Teacher #27), while one teacher described the challenge with this stating that the “SNAs created social stories for learners, and principal said that the correct biological terminology wasn't appropriate to use with GLD students” (Teacher #23).

When outlining the reasons for their decision to use alternative resources, over one third of the teachers described the reasons as being “best suited” (Teacher #11), “relevant to the needs of the child and learning style” (Teacher #27), “pitched better” to their learners (Teacher #15), and “easy to use and in a simple format” (Teacher #17). A small number of teachers stated that these resources had been recommended to them by colleagues or were “the only ones available” to them (Teacher #13).

The majority of teachers disagreed or strongly disagreed that class teachers have the necessary skills or knowledge to effectively teach RSE (75%). 96.9% of teachers agreed or strongly agreed that teaching RSE should be a collaborative approach with parents and teachers, and that collaboration with other professionals for RSE teaching is important. However, 87.6% disagreed or strongly disagreed that they are given time to collaborate and plan for RSE teaching.

Teachers were asked questions related to supports specifically available for Special Schools. The majority of teachers (75%) disagreed or strongly disagreed that teachers in special schools can access a range of supports for RSE for learners with Severe/Profound Intellectual Disabilities, with 75.1% disagreeing or strongly disagreeing that special school teachers have access to ongoing CPD and training in RSE for learners with Severe/Profound Intellectual Disabilities, with 15.6% remaining neutral and only 9.3% agreeing. Qualitative Data Responses

Teachers were asked to describe the types of supports that are important to them when teaching RSE and the challenges they experience when teaching RSE to learners with Severe/Profound Intellectual Disabilities.

Important Supports

Teachers outlined a need for supports across four main areas: access to resources that were specifically designed to cater for learners with Severe/Profound Intellectual Disabilities (68.9%); access to specific curricular guidelines and programmes for teaching RSE (38.2%); support from parents, colleagues and other professionals (37.8%); and specific training and access to CPD (13.7%).

The majority of teachers expressed a desire for specific resources to be created which align with the profile of a learner with Severe/Profound Intellectual Disabilities. Teacher #11 indicated that “it would be beneficial if there was more practical materials that were easily accessible solely focused on learners with SPGLD” with Teacher #23 adding “concrete, accessible, appropriately differentiated materials, curriculum and supports”. Teachers' description of desired resources included “prompts and easy cartoons” (Teacher #16), “visuals and suitable interactive videos” (Teacher #6) and “props…visuals like Widgets for social stories” (Teacher #30).

Additionally, teachers sought support in the form of specific RSE programmes, guidelines and curricular supports as described by Teacher #7:

A more interactive programme with differentiated activities to support the learners when they are learning about the topics, e.g., sensory stories that have been developed for difficult topics, guidebooks for teachers to inform our own practice. (Teacher #7)

This was echoed by Teacher #27 who stated the need for “guidelines on how to teach RSE that is relevant and appropriate for learners with SPGLD” (Teacher #27). Support from the school “principal, other teachers, parents and other staff including SNAs, O.T.'s SLT and Nursing” (Teacher #34) was also described as important. Another teacher described the need for “buy in of other staff that are in the room with me” (Teacher #32) as important. Additionally, Teacher #13 indicated that “advice from clinicians and conversations with parents” were required to support the teaching of RSE.

Challenges Experienced

Teachers' description of the challenges experienced was diverse and wide ranging and are presented under three main themes—internal factors, external factors and student‐related factors. Internal factors included a lack of confidence as a result of a lack of knowledge about RSE teaching as described by Teacher #29 “awkwardness around the topic, lack of confidence as no structured materials are available”, Teacher #7 “not having sufficient knowledge” and Teacher #27 “knowing if I am doing enough for each child”. Teacher #5 expanded on this when they said:

Difficulty in knowing how to teach the subject, i.e., Strangers and touches I covered this by bathing dolls (speaking through the stages of undressing in a private space) but this concept is incredibly difficult to teach learners with a SPGLD. (Teacher #5)

External factors described illustrated challenges with access to resources, CPD, time and the diversity of student age ranges and needs within the class. Teacher #3 described the “lack of resources and teacher/SNA training”, with Teacher #18 describing the lack of “resources, co‐operation with parents”. Teacher #32 and Teacher #22 described the challenge when you “may not have SNA staff on board” (#32) and the diversity of “range of ages in my class from 8 to 18” (#22).

Student‐related factors were described by teachers as challenges with the student's level of comprehension and understanding of the concepts given the abstract nature of RSE. Teachers consistently cited challenges of “assessing the child's understanding of the topic covered” (Teacher #30), “the child just not comprehending what is being said, even when broken down simply” (Teacher #17) and “them not understanding, and their cognitive ability being vastly different to their physical development” (Teacher #23). Teachers frequently cited challenges with the “pitch” of the currently available resources as a challenge as “the level of content provided in resources is pitched at a level too high for my students to access and understand” (Teacher #20).

What Teachers Need to Support Them in Teaching RSE?

Twenty‐seven teachers responded to the final qualitative question in the survey which asked what teachers need to support them in teaching RSE to learners with Severe/Profound Intellectual Disabilities. Their responses represent three main areas: CPD, training & support; accessible & appropriate resources; a dedicated programme or toolkit to follow.

CPD, Training and Support

Over one third of the responding teachers (n = 27) described the need for specific CPD, training and support. Teacher #14 described the current challenge that “appropriate training is not provided to teachers so specific training should be provided” and it was supported by Teacher #26 who stated that teachers need “training courses on every aspect of the curriculum on how to teach RSE with SPGLD resources and lesson plans”. Teacher #12 added that training should include “modelling how to teach and approach it” (Teacher #12) and Teacher #7 sought “more awareness of the ways we can teach RSE”.

Accessible and Appropriate Resources

Over one third of teachers identified the need for bespoke resources that were designed specifically for learners with Severe/Profound Intellectual Disabilities as opposed to current practice of differentiating resources that have been designed for other profiles of learners or adapting resources from other jurisdictions. Teacher #8 described the need for “appropriate and easily accessible resources that are aimed at SPGLD and simple”.

They also noted the need for CPD on the implementation of the resources as described by Teacher #4 stating “resources specific to learners with SPGLD, training in and supports to teach the content” as well as effective methodologies as stated by Teacher #30 “resources and props, for the children in our classes, it needs to be taught by role‐playing.”

Dedicated Programme and Toolkit

One third of teachers cited the need for a specific programme to follow, as described by Teacher #13, “a clear programme of work that has been approved by DES supported by parents”. This was also noted by Teacher #15 who echoed previous challenges in relation to the pitch of existing programmes when they stated a need for “a separate programme that is pitched for our student's level so they receive the information they need but in an appropriate manner” (Teacher #15). Teacher #29 reiterated the need for clear direction for the teacher in implementing the programme by stating a “proper programme designed for pupils with SPGLD with clear lessons and steps for teachers to follow to support them to deliver effect RSE for children with SPGLD” (Teacher #29).

Other Areas of Support

Additional support described by the teachers illustrates some of the existing challenges with the teaching of RSE in Special Schools. Teachers noted the need for a whole school approach to RSE, echoing responses to the previous question where Teacher #32 described the challenge of getting “buy in” from the classroom staff. Teacher #28 described the need for collaboration within and across the school, as well as with parents and that training should be a collaborative endeavour:

More CPD in the area of teaching RSE at a whole school level, which includes all personnel who support the learner. A more detailed curriculum for learners in special school settings. Opportunities for teacher and parent training to allow for a more collaborative approach. (Teacher #28)

These comments were supported by Teacher #33 who described the need for support from school management and an adaptation to current teaching styles, stating “proper and up to date CPD, support from in school management team. Team teaching might also offer support.” (Teacher #33).

Finally, a comment from Teacher #23 captures much of what has been described by the responding teachers, but with the important reflection on challenging the current culture of RSE teaching in Special Schools. They described the need for “training, time to plan, good resources, a change in culture from it being an optional extra to an integral part of education, and parental support” (Teacher #23).

Discussion

Previous Irish research illustrates the teaching of RSE is a challenge (Keating et al. 2018) and the results of this study illustrate that almost half of the respondents are not teaching RSE to their students with Severe/Profound Intellectual Disabilities. While it was hypothesised by the researchers that the implementation of RSE was in need of support, the reasons teachers provided for not teaching RSE were stark and demonstrate a significant issue with the provision of support to teachers in this area.

Perceptions of students' limited comprehension and understanding of the concepts, limited access to resources and training, and the lack of a dedicated programme are findings that are consistent with other Irish and international studies (Borawska‐Charko et al. 2023; Greene et al. 2024; Johnston 2022). Teachers of students with Moderate Intellectual Disabilities in Ireland in Johnston (2022) doctoral study “proposed a structured RSE programme as one of the elements that would lead to successful RSE for children with ModGLD” (p. 165), with Greene et al. (2024) noting that the “absence of an evidence‐based sexual health curriculum for students with IDD represents a systemic issue” (p. 19) in their US‐based study.

Two thirds of the teachers in this study had been teaching learners with Severe/Profound Intellectual Disabilities for 3 years or less. Demographic data such as age of teachers or overall length of teaching experience (including mainstream teaching) was not gathered as part of this research. However, given the persistent teacher supply issues particularly in special education roles in Ireland, it is possible that the demographic of these teachers in special schools is newly qualified teachers rather than experienced teachers.

The Irish National Teachers' Organisation (INTO), the largest teachers' trade union in Ireland, has highlighted the impact of teacher shortages in the Irish education system over the past number of years. Noting the “consistent disproportionate impact on special schools” (INTO 2025), their 2024 survey demonstrated that 56% of special schools experienced long term teacher vacancies (INTO 2025). However, while it may be suggested that the respondents were recently qualified, the data doesn't provide any additional insights into factors such as age or experience teaching mainstream students, which is a limitation in this survey.

Teachers in this study reported a lack of school based support from management and colleagues, consistent with findings from Jeyachandran and Kumar (2024), limited RSE practices established in their school, including the absence of RSE policy in one case. This represents a perfect storm in relation to RSE teaching through the combination of a lack of preparation at initial teacher education (Maunsell et al. 2021), failure on the part of the DE to provide a suitable curriculum or bespoke programme and accompanying resources (Morrissey et al. 2025; Johnston 2022), combined with the lack of available bespoke and relevant CPD and training opportunities, which has also been observed in the international context (Greene et al. 2024). Therefore, neither pre‐service teachers nor in‐service teachers are being adequately supported through curricular provision, resourcing or development of knowledge, skills and attitudes towards RSE. There is a lack of critical mass of expertise within special schools for RSE for learners with Severe/Profound Intellectual Disabilities to be able to support newly qualified teachers.

In an effort to discourage schools from the current common practice of the utilisation of external facilitators to teach RSE, also observed in other jurisdictions (Smith et al. 2013), the Irish DES Circular 0042/2018 (DES 2018a, 2018b, 2018c) states that the “qualified classroom teacher is the best placed professional” to teach RSE, adding that this can “be achieved through CPD” (p. 3). While teachers in this study indicated that they feel RSE is essential for their learners and are willing to avail of CPD opportunities to support it, there is no state funded CPD being provided for teachers of learners with Severe/Profound Intellectual Disabilities, despite recommendations from the NCCA (2019) for its provision.

Lack of provision of bespoke curricula for child safety and protection for students with intellectual disabilities has also been observed with the mandatory Stay Safe Programme (MacIntyre and Lawlor 2016) in Ireland. It is the only mandatory programme within the Irish education system, and while an adaptation was provided to the original 1991 version in 1996, the 2016 revision did not provide an adaptation for students with intellectual disabilities “without any apparent justification or explanation from the Department of Education” (Morrissey et al. 2025, 3). Special schools must negotiate their requirement for compliance with full implementation of Stay Safe as part of Child Safeguarding through a programme that was not designed for them and, as a result, schools have become curriculum adapters (Morrissey et al. 2025), which was also observed in this study in relation to RSE. Morrissey et al. (2025) observed one Severe/Profound special school principal who stated, “it wasn't written for our children and I'm not adapting it, as it's not adaptable” (Morrissey et al. 2025, 12).

This illustrates a significant issue with the value placed on RSE and Safety and Protection within the context of special educational needs. Curriculum developers (NCCA) and the Department of Education have continually failed to provide for students with intellectual disabilities in the area of SPHE and RSE. The existing Guidelines for Teachers of Students with General Learning Disabilities (Mild; Moderate; Severe/Profound) for Primary & Post‐Primary have not been updated since their publication in 2007. The publication of the revised Primary Curriculum Framework for Primary and Special Schools (2023a) includes RSE within the Wellbeing domain; however, it fails to provide bespoke learning outcomes, approaches or guidance to teachers supporting learners with intellectual disabilities. While provision for RSE in the form of learning outcomes has been included in recent curricular reform at post‐primary level through the Level 1 (NCCA 2018) and Level 2 Learning Programmes (NCCA and NABMSE 2019), the lack of accompanying programmes, resources or CPD is problematic.

Schmidt et al. (2020) aimed to identify through a scoping review, the extent and nature of sexual health education interventions for young people with intellectual disabilities. Conclusion

This paper has illustrated that students' rights to education in relationships and sexuality education, as enshrined in the Education Act (1998), UNCRC (UN 1989) or UNCRPD (UN 2006) are not being equally accessed by all, particularly students with Severe/Profound Intellectual Disabilities. The Irish education system has failed to provide adequate support for teachers and expected them to become curriculum adapters. In particular, teachers with post‐primary qualifications are particularly disadvantaged due to the lack of initial teacher training in this core area. In order to ensure children's rights are accessed, “a change in culture from it being an optional extra to an integral part of education” is required (Teacher #23). There is an opportunity in the Irish education system at present as part of broader curricular redevelopments across primary and post‐primary to ensure that teachers are supported to enact RSE curricula that fully acknowledges and upholds all students' rights to SPHE and RSE education, including students with Severe/Profound intellectual disabilities.
