Myocardial performance index in fetuses with small-for-gestational age in pregnancy: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Bixue Deng, Tao Zhang, Jianyu Huang, Runtong Chen, Xiaohong Bi, Xia Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe clinical utility and consistency of fetal Myocardial Performance Index (MPI) in assessing small for gestational age (SGA) remains contentious. Therefore, this study aimed to systematically evaluate the association between fetal MPI and SGA, and to assess the predictive value of MPI for adverse perinatal outcomes.MethodsPubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and The Cochrane Library were searched up to January 13, 2025. Studies comparing fetal MPI in singleton SGA pregnancies with healthy controls were included. Reviews, case reports, and studies without a control group were excluded. Data extraction followed PRISMA guidelines, and the protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023482293). Pooled estimates were generated using random-effects models for MPI and fixed-effects models for adverse perinatal outcomes. Subgroup, sensitivity, and meta-regression analyses were conducted.Results35 studies were included in the systematic review, with 27 eligible for meta-analysis. Fetuses with SGA had a significantly higher mean left fetal MPI compared to controls (WMD, 0.09; 95% CI, 0.06–0.11; I²=97%, P < 0.001). Subgroup analysis according to the classification of SGA revealed significant subgroup differences (P < 0.01), which remained consistent across SGA subtypes. No marked differences were found in gestational age at ultrasound and echocardiography technique. Elevated MPI was associated with an increased risk of adverse perinatal outcomes (OR, 3.71; 95% CI, 2.49–5.52; I²=0%, P < 0.001).ConclusionsThis meta-analysis demonstrates that fetal MPI is significantly elevated in SGA pregnancies, indicating impaired global ventricular function. An increased MPI is associated with a higher risk of adverse perinatal outcomes. These findings support the potential utility of MPI as a tool for monitoring and risk stratification in SGA pregnancies.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08357-7.

Background

Suboptimal fetal growth is a significant antecedent to perinatal morbidity and mortality and is associated with adverse long-term health outcomes [1]. Small-for-gestational-age (SGA) is a biometric classification for a fetus with an estimated weight or abdominal circumference below the 10th percentile for a given gestational age. This category includes constitutionally small fetuses as well as those experiencing fetal growth restriction (FGR), a pathological condition where the fetus fails to reach its genetic growth potential, often due to placental insufficiency [2, 3].

The haemodynamic adaptations required to cope with placental dysfunction can induce significant cardiac strain in the FGR fetus. This may manifest as altered ventricular filling dynamics, progressive cardiac dysfunction, and ultimately, myocardial compromise [4, 5].

The Myocardial Performance Index (MPI), also known as the Tei Index, is an established Doppler-derived measure of global ventricular function [6]. It integrates both systolic and diastolic time intervals, providing a comprehensive assessment of cardiac performance. An elevated MPI, resulting from prolonged isovolumetric periods and a shortened ejection time, reflects worsening cardiac function. Although several studies suggest that MPI is elevated in SGA/FGR fetuses and may deteriorate with disease progression [5, 7], its clinical utility and consistency across studies remain contentious. For example, Öcal et al. and Dal et al. observed no significant differences in modified MPI between FGR and normal fetuses [8, 9]. This lack of consensus hinders its routine implementation in the clinical surveillance of at-risk fetuses.

Therefore, the primary objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to quantify the difference in fetal MPI between SGA fetuses and controls. The secondary objective was to evaluate the predictive value of MPI for adverse perinatal outcomes.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [10]. The protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number: CRD42023482293), and no deviations from the registered plan occurred.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included based on the following criteria: (Population) singleton pregnancies diagnosed with SGA and a comparative group of healthy fetuses; (Exposure) prenatal evaluation of the fetal Myocardial Performance Index (MPI); (Comparison) healthy, appropriate-for-gestational-age control fetuses; (Outcomes) reported MPI values and/or association with adverse perinatal outcomes; and (Study design) observational studies (cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional).

Studies published in languages other than English were excluded, potentially introducing language bias. We excluded reviews, case reports, case series, conference abstracts, editorials, letters, and non-human (animal or cellular) studies. Studies that did not include a control group were also excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and The Cochrane Library from their inception to January 13, 2025. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (i.e., MeSH in PubMed and Emtree in EMBASE) with free-text terms. Keywords included “small for gestational age”, “fetal growth retardation”, “myocardial performance index”, “Tei index”, and their variations.

For transparency, the complete PubMed strategy was: ((“Infant, Small for Gestational Age“[Mesh] OR “small for gestational age“[tiab] OR “SGA“[tiab] OR “fetal growth restriction“[tiab] OR “intrauterine growth restriction“[tiab] OR “IUGR“[tiab]) AND (“Myocardial Performance Index“[tiab] OR “Tei index“[tiab] OR “myocardial performance“[tiab] OR “Doppler myocardial performance index“[tiab])). The reference lists of included articles and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify additional studies. The full search strategy is detailed in Table S1.

Study selection and data extraction

Study inclusion followed a structured multi-step process. Two independent reviewers (B.X.D. and T.Z.) screened titles and abstracts for eligibility. Full texts of potentially relevant articles were then evaluated by the same reviewers in a blinded manner. Inter-rater agreement was quantified using Cohen’s kappa statistic [11], with thresholds interpreted as slight (0.01–0.20), fair (0.21–0.40), moderate (0.41–0.60), substantial (0.61–0.80), and almost perfect (0.81–0.99). Disagreements were resolved through consensus, and a third reviewer (J.Y.H.) adjudicated unresolved cases.

Two reviewers (B.X.D. and R.T.C) independently extracted data using a standardised electronic form. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer. The following data were extracted from each study: first author, year of publication, country, study design, definition of SGA, sample size (cases and controls), gestational age at examination, maternal age and body mass index (BMI), echocardiographic technique, and reported outcomes. Authors were contacted for clarification or to provide missing data when necessary.

For quantitative synthesis, the MPI data presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) were extracted directly. When data were reported as median with interquartile range (IQR) or range, we converted them to mean and SD using validated statistical methods to ensure consistency for the analysis [12, 13]. In studies reporting both right and left ventricular MPI, data from the left ventricle were prioritised for analysis to maintain uniformity in assessing left heart function.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers. The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used for cohort and case-control studies, evaluating patient selection (0–4 stars), comparability of groups (0–2 stars), and outcome assessment (0–3 stars). Studies were categorised as high (7–9 stars), moderate (4–6 stars), or low (0–3 stars) quality. Cross-sectional studies were evaluated using the 11-item Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) checklist, with scores of 8–11, 4–7, and 0–3 indicating high, moderate, and low quality, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan, version 5.4) and Stata (version 17.0). The primary outcome, the difference in MPI between SGA and control fetuses, was synthesised by calculating the weighted mean difference (WMD) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). The association between an elevated MPI and adverse perinatal outcomes was analysed using odds ratios (OR) with 95% CIs. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical heterogeneity among studies was quantified using the I² statistic and Cochran’s Q test. Significant heterogeneity was defined as I² >50% or a Q-test P-value < 0.10. In the presence of significant heterogeneity, a random-effects model was employed for analysis.

To investigate sources of heterogeneity, we planned a priori subgroup and meta-regression analyses based on: publication year (< 2020 vs. ≥2020), study design, study quality (high vs. moderate), gestational age at examination (≤ 32 vs. >32 weeks), and ultrasound modality (conventional Doppler vs. Tissue Doppler Imaging).

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the influence of each individual study on the overall pooled estimate. To further characterize robustness, 95% prediction intervals were calculated under random-effects models, and influence diagnostics (DFBETAS, Cook’s distance, and hat values) were used to identify studies with disproportionate contributions to heterogeneity.

Publication bias was evaluated by visual inspection of funnel plots and formally tested using Egger’s regression asymmetry test, with P < 0.10 indicating significant bias. If publication bias was detected, the trim-and-fill method was planned to adjust the effect estimate.

Results

Study selection

The initial electronic database search yielded 636 records. After the removal of duplicates, 405 unique references were screened, of which 100 were selected for full-text review. Agreement between reviewers during initial screening was almost perfect (κ = 0.95; 95% CI, 0.91–0.98), and agreement in selecting the final study cohort was substantial (κ = 0.73; 95% CI, 0.56–0.90). Two studies reporting on the same patient cohort were consolidated and treated as a single entry. From the remaining studies, studies were excluded from the quantitative analysis because MPI was reported as a Z-score (n = 3), data were presented only in graphical format (n = 1), or data were provided as mean and range or percentages (n = 3). Ultimately, 35 studies met the eligibility criteria for the systematic review [7–9, 14–45], with 27 [7–9, 14, 22–30, 32–45] of these being included in the final meta-analysis. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

The 35 included studies were published between 1999 and 2024. The majority of studies were conducted in Asia (n = 19) and Europe (n = 11), with the remainder from Africa (n = 3), South America (n = 1), and Oceania (n = 1). All were prospective observational studies, including case-control, cohort, and cross-sectional designs.

Collectively, the studies provided data on 4026 fetuses, comprising 1586 fetuses with SGA and 2440 healthy controls. Within the SGA cohort, the diagnosis was specified as constitutional SGA in 483 cases, Fetal Growth Restriction (FGR) in 642 cases, and was unspecified in 461 cases (Table 1). Risk of bias and quality of included studies

The methodological quality of the included studies was generally high. Among the cohort and case-control studies assessed with the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, 28 were rated as high quality and one as moderate quality (Tables S3 and S4). The risk-of-bias domains across studies are summarized in Fig. 2. All cross-sectional studies were deemed of moderate quality based on the AHRQ checklist (Table S5).

Fig. 2NOS Heatmap: Domain-Level Scores and Total per Study

NOS Heatmap: Domain-Level Scores and Total per Study

Visual inspection of the funnel plot for the primary outcome did not suggest major asymmetry (Figure S1). However, Egger’s regression test indicated a potential for publication bias in the analysis of MPI (P = 0.042). A subsequent trim-and-fill analysis did not impute any studies, and the adjusted pooled estimate was identical to the original random-effects model (WMD 0.087, 95% CI: 0.065–0.110), suggesting that publication bias was unlikely to have a significant impact on the conclusion (Figure S2).

Synthesis of results

Primary outcome: myocardial performance index

Fetuses with SGA demonstrated a significantly higher mean left MPI compared to controls (WMD 0.09, 95% CI 0.06–0.11) (Figure S3). This finding was accompanied by substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 97%, P < 0.001), which likely reflects differences in SGA/FGR definitions, gestational age at assessment, population characteristics, echocardiographic equipment and techniques, and operator-dependent variability in measuring cardiac intervals.

MPI components

Analysis of the individual components of the MPI revealed that, compared to controls, SGA fetuses had a significantly prolonged isovolumetric contraction time (ICT) (WMD 3.07, 95% CI 1.31–4.84; I2 = 95%) and isovolumetric relaxation time (IRT) (WMD 8.38, 95% CI 5.99–10.78; I2 = 99%). Conversely, the ejection time (ET) was significantly shorter (WMD − 8.86, 95% CI −12.79 to −4.93; I2 = 94%) (Figure S4).

Association with adverse perinatal outcomes

Five studies [7, 18, 25, 29, 33] evaluated the association between MPI and adverse perinatal outcomes. An elevated MPI was significantly associated with an increased likelihood of adverse perinatal outcomes in SGA/FGR pregnancies (OR 3.71, 95% CI 2.49–5.52; P < 0.001), with no evidence of heterogeneity for this analysis (I² = 0%) (Figure S5). Subgroup analyses (Fig. 3) confirmed that this association was consistent across publication year, study region, gestational age at ultrasound, and study design, with no significant interactions (all P for interaction >0.05). A stronger, though not statistically significant, association was observed in studies explicitly defining FGR compared with those using unspecified SGA definitions (OR 5.71 vs. 3.09; P for interaction = 0.17).

Subgroup and meta-regression analyses

Stratified analyses revealed significant subgroup differences in MPI values by SGA type (FGR, constitutional SGA, and unspecified SGA; P = 0.001) and by study design (cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional; P = 0.001) (Fig. 4). In contrast, no significant differences emerged when stratified by gestational age at examination (≤ 32 vs. >32 weeks) or ultrasound modality (conventional Doppler vs. tissue Doppler imaging). To illustrate MPI distribution across gestational ages, a bubble plot was generated (Figure S6). Similarly, subgroup analyses by publication year, study region, or study quality did not yield significant differences.

Fig. 4Subgroup analysis of MPI between SGA/FGR and controls

Subgroup analysis of MPI between SGA/FGR and controls

For the MPI components (ICT, IRT, ET), subgroup analyses did not show significant differences based on SGA type, gestational age, or ultrasound technique. However, study design was identified as a significant source of heterogeneity for all three components (P < 0.001 for all) (Figure S7). This was further supported by the meta-regression analysis, which found that study design significantly contributed to the heterogeneity in IRT estimates (P = 0.037). No other variables were found to be significant predictors of heterogeneity for MPI, ICT, or ET (Tables S6-9).

Sensitivity analysis

Leave-one-out analysis showed that sequential exclusion of individual studies did not materially change the pooled WMD for MPI, IRT, ICT, or ET (Figure S8). This indicates that the overall estimates were robust and not driven by any single study.

Other heterogeneous analysis

Although the pooled WMD for MPI was significant, the wide 95% prediction interval (–0.077 to 0.252) reflected substantial between-study variation. Influence diagnostics (DFBETAS, Cook’s distance, and hat values) identified five potentially influential studies [7, 9, 34, 36, 37] (Supplementary Table S10). However, sequential exclusion of these studies did not affect the pooled estimates or heterogeneity, further supporting the stability of the findings.

Discussion

Main findings and interpretation

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrate that global ventricular function is impaired in SGA pregnancies, as reflected by elevated MPI compared with healthy controls. Although this pattern was consistent across SGA subtypes, substantial heterogeneity across studies reduces the certainty and generalizability of the pooled WMD estimates.

The underlying pathophysiology likely stems from placental insufficiency, a common etiology for FGR that results in chronic fetal hypoxia and undernutrition [46]. These adverse intrauterine conditions are known to precipitate cardiac dysfunction, which is a critical determinant of prognosis in FGR fetuses [14, 47, 48]. Placental-mediated compromise limits the supply of oxygen and nutrients essential for myocardial cell development and maturation. Concurrently, increased placental resistance imposes a chronic afterload on the fetal heart, leading to structural and functional adaptations aimed at maintaining cardiac output, which manifest as altered ventricular compliance and function.

Our analysis confirms that the MPI, a validated and reproducible measure of global cardiac function [49], is a sensitive marker of these adaptations. Research indicates the potential of MPI in managing pregnancies affected by conditions like diabetes mellitus, twin-twin transfusion syndrome, and intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) [7, 50, 51]. The observed elevation in MPI was driven by a prolongation of both the isovolumetric contraction time (ICT) and isovolumetric relaxation time (IRT). Notably, the magnitude of the increase was greater for IRT than for ICT (WMD: 8.30 vs. 3.07), suggesting that diastolic dysfunction may be a more prominent and earlier feature of cardiac compromise in SGA fetuses than systolic dysfunction. This aligns with a growing body of evidence indicating that FGR imposes a significant hemodynamic burden that leads to subclinical myocardial impairment, detectable even before overt signs of cardiac failure emerge [19, 22, 32, 34, 36]. Nevertheless, most available studies were cross-sectional, offering only single time-point assessments. Such designs preclude causal inference and limit understanding of temporal changes in MPI, emphasizing the need for longitudinal studies. Moreover, the considerable between-study heterogeneity observed in this meta-analysis warrants caution in interpreting pooled estimates.

A key finding from our subgroup analysis was the more pronounced elevation of MPI in FGR fetuses compared to their constitutional SGA (c-SGA) counterparts (WMD: 0.08 vs. 0.04). This suggests that MPI values may correlate with the severity of the underlying pathology, potentially offering a means to help differentiate between pathological FGR and the more benign c-SGA. While this distinction is clinically vital, we acknowledge conflicting reports, such as those by Turgut and Zhang, which found no significant MPI difference between these groups [29, 43]. Such discrepancies highlight the need for large-scale, methodologically standardized studies to clarify the role of MPI in distinguishing between these two SGA subtypes.

Our analysis did not find a significant difference in MPI values between examinations performed before and after 32 weeks of gestation, a finding consistent with some existing literature [27, 29]. This may reflect the complex and distinct pathophysiologies of early-onset (EO) versus late-onset (LO) FGR. EO-FGR is typically associated with severe placental pathology and early cardiovascular adaptation, whereas LO-FGR may represent a milder spectrum of disease with less pronounced cardiac dysfunction at the time of diagnosis.

The modality used for assessment also warrants consideration. Tissue Doppler Imaging (TDI) offers a less load-dependent evaluation of myocardial function compared to conventional pulsed-wave Doppler [52, 53]. Our quantitative analysis of TDI-derived MPI (tMPI) revealed a significant elevation in SGA fetuses (WMD 0.10, 95% CI 0.05–0.15), with this trend holding across subgroups. This finding supports the potential of tMPI for the early detection of subclinical cardiac involvement, particularly in cases where conventional echocardiography may be inconclusive.

Clinical implications and future directions

From a clinical standpoint, improving risk stratification in SGA/FGR pregnancies is paramount, as this condition is a leading cause of stillbirth, intrapartum distress, and long-term neurodevelopmental impairment [54]. While conventional fetal Doppler indices are standard for surveillance, their predictive capacity for adverse perinatal outcomes remains debated [40]. Our finding that an elevated MPI is strongly associated with adverse outcomes (OR 3.71) reinforces its potential as a valuable adjunct tool. Several studies have proposed MPI cut-off values for predicting adverse outcomes [7, 29, 33, 35, 40], but the variability in these thresholds and the small sample sizes in individual studies preclude the recommendation of a single, universal cut-off at this time. Although MPI measurement is feasible with standard ultrasound equipment and is relatively quick, its accuracy depends heavily on operator skill in Doppler acquisition and caliper placement. Broader implementation would require structured training and resource allocation to ensure reproducibility across centers. Future research must focus on validating its predictive accuracy in large, multicenter prospective trials to establish definitive clinical guidelines.

Strengths and limitations

The primary strength of this meta-analysis is its comprehensive scope, including a rigorous search strategy, formal quality appraisal of included studies, and a large pooled sample size that enhances the statistical power of our findings. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, marked heterogeneity across studies—stemming from differences in SGA/FGR definitions, gestational age at evaluation, population characteristics, equipment, and operator technique—limits the generalizability of pooled estimates. Second, although Egger’s test suggested possible small-study effects (P = 0.042), trim-and-fill analysis did not add missing studies, and the adjusted effect size was unchanged. Third, most included studies were cross-sectional, providing only a single snapshot of fetal cardiac function rather than longitudinal data. Fourth, the analysis of adverse outcomes was often limited by the use of composite endpoints, which prevents a detailed evaluation of MPI’s ability to predict specific complications. Finally, the relatively small sample sizes in many of the primary studies underscore the need for larger, well-designed prospective cohort studies to provide more definitive evidence.

Conclusion

This study underscores the significant role of MPI in evaluating fetal cardiac function in SGA/FGR cases. This cardiac dysfunction appears to be a core component of the pathophysiology of fetal growth restriction. MPI shows promise as a non-invasive indicator of subclinical compromise, detectable before abnormalities in standard Doppler indices appear. However, the current evidence base is insufficient to support routine clinical adoption. Further large-scale prospective studies are required to establish standardized cut-offs and validate its role in diagnostic and management algorithms. Large-scale, prospective studies are essential to validate its utility in predicting adverse perinatal outcomes and to understand the long-term cardiovascular consequences for children born with SGA/FGR.
