Navigation-assisted unilateral biportal endoscopic decompression using a 0-degree endoscope for upper lumbar spinal stenosis: a technical note on facet preservation
Authors: Shinya Arataki, Akiyoshi Miyamoto, Tadashi Komatsubara, Masato Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUpper lumbar spinal stenosis at L1/2 and L2/3 is technically demanding because of the narrow interlaminar spaces, steep facet orientation, and deep working corridors. Excessive bone work on the approach side may increase the risk of ipsilateral facet violation. This study aimed to describe a navigation-assisted unilateral biportal endoscopic (UBE) technique using a 0-degree endoscope for upper lumbar spinal stenosis and to discuss a structural rationale by which the straight-view configuration may function as a procedural control strategy for preserving the approach-side facet joint. This retrospective technical note included six consecutive patients with degenerative upper lumbar spinal stenosis (L1/2 or L2/3) treated with navigation-assisted UBE using a 0-degree endoscope. Patients with ossification of the ligamentum flavum, marked deformity, or concomitant fusion procedures were excluded. Perioperative outcomes and postoperative computed tomography (CT) findings were reviewed to assess the adequacy of decompression and facet preservation. All procedures were completed successfully without intraoperative complications or conversion to open surgery. Adequate bilateral decompression was achieved in all patients. Postoperative CT confirmed sufficient decompression with preservation of the approach-side facet joint. Clinical symptoms improved, with Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores for back pain decreasing from 75.0 to 35.8 and leg pain from 73.3 to 32.5. Navigation-assisted UBE using a 0-degree endoscope is a feasible and intuitive option for upper lumbar decompression. Under specific anatomical and navigational conditions, the straight-view configuration may help control excessive approach-side exposure and contribute to facet joint preservation.Keywords: Upper lumbar spine, unilateral biportal endoscopy, bilateral decompression, facet joint preservation, intraoperative navigation

Introduction

Unilateral biportal endoscopic (UBE) decompression has become an established minimally invasive option for lumbar spinal stenosis and disc herniation. By separating visualization and instrumentation through two portals under continuous irrigation, UBE provides a wide working corridor and flexible instrument handling, with reported clinical outcomes comparable to those of other minimally invasive decompression techniques when posterior stabilizing structures are appropriately preserved (1-3). Nevertheless, UBE can be technically demanding during the introduction phase, and careful case selection and workflow standardization remain important for safe and reproducible results.

Decompression at the upper lumbar levels (L1/2 and L2/3) poses distinct technical challenges compared with lower lumbar segments. These levels often present narrow interlaminar spaces and deep working corridors, and the spinous process may “overhang”, restricting endoscope angulation and instrument maneuverability. In addition, steep facet orientation and limited bony working space may make the approach-side facet less forgiving for aggressive bone work, raising concern for iatrogenic facet violation and potential postoperative instability (4-6). These anatomical characteristics can complicate the establishment of an initial endoscopic orientation and a safe bony working trajectory, particularly during early experience with UBE.

In this context, intraoperative navigation may offer advantages beyond level confirmation. Navigation can assist in determining an optimal skin incision and working trajectory to reach the intended interlaminar entry point with minimal trial-and-error, which may be particularly relevant when spinous process morphology and laminar constraints limit freedom of scope tilting. Prior technical reports have demonstrated the feasibility of contralateral decompression through a single interlaminar window and have highlighted how viewing geometry influences decompression strategy (4).

A common practical question in upper lumbar UBE is whether an angled endoscope (e.g., 30°) is necessary to achieve adequate bilateral decompression. We hypothesized that, at upper lumbar levels, contralateral tilting is mechanically restricted by spinous process impingement, and the straight viewing axis of a 0° endoscope may therefore be an advantage rather than a limitation. Specifically, the straight-view configuration may function as a mechanism that helps control ipsilateral exposure, thereby helping to avoid excessive facet resection under anatomically constrained conditions and reducing the risk of unintended ipsilateral facet over-resection.

At the same time, effective contralateral decompression remains achievable because the contralateral target is anatomically close at these levels. When combined with navigation-guided portal optimization and trajectory planning, a 0° endoscope may provide a simple and reproducible strategy for upper lumbar decompression without relying on excessive angulation.

The purpose of this technical note is to describe a navigation-assisted UBE decompression technique using a 0° endoscope for upper lumbar spinal stenosis (L1/2–L2/3) and to present a structural rationale for ipsilateral facet preservation supported by postoperative computed tomography (CT) findings. We report six consecutive cases and detail (I) preoperative anatomical considerations; (II) navigation-based incision and trajectory design; and (III) a conceptual model explaining how a straight viewing axis can reduce the risk of ipsilateral facet violation while maintaining effective contralateral decompression. Preoperative preparations and requirements

This retrospective technical note included six consecutive patients who underwent navigation-assisted UBE decompression for degenerative upper lumbar spinal stenosis at L1/2 or L2/3. All cases were classified as Schizas grade C spinal stenosis on preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). All patients were refractory to sufficient conservative treatment prior to surgery. All procedures were performed at a single institution by an experienced spine surgeon.

Patients with ossification of the ligamentum flavum, marked spinal deformity, segmental instability requiring fusion, or concomitant fusion procedures were excluded. All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Okayama Rosai Hospital (IRB No. 588). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients for publication of this article and accompanying images and video. A copy of the written consent is available for review by the editorial office of this journal.

Step-by-step description

Step 1: patient positioning and navigation setup

All patients were placed in the prone position under general anesthesia. An intraoperative navigation system (O-arm, Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA; StealthStation S8, Medtronic) was used. A reference frame was attached, and intraoperative 3D imaging was obtained for registration.

Step 2: navigation-guided planning

The navigation system was used to determine the optimal skin incision and portal trajectory, aiming to achieve a straight, reproducible path to the target interlaminar space, considering both transverse and sagittal alignment, while minimizing lateral deviation of the intended trajectory caused by spinous process morphology. Step 3: portal creation

Two small portals were created based on navigation guidance. The approach side was selected based on the side of dominant symptoms and anatomical considerations; in most cases, a left-sided approach was preferred to facilitate instrument handling, although the final decision was based on the side of dominant symptoms and anatomical considerations.

Step 4: initial decompression

A 0° endoscope was introduced through the viewing portal, and standard endoscopic instruments, including a high-speed drill and Kerrison punches, were inserted through the working portal. Initial bone work focused on careful exposure of the interlaminar space, with deliberate avoidance of excessive resection of the approach-side (ipsilateral) facet joint.

Step 5: bilateral decompression

After secure entry into the interlaminar space, bilateral decompression was performed. Because the straight viewing axis of the 0° endoscope may limit ipsilateral exposure of the facet joint, excessive exposure of the ipsilateral facet was structurally constrained. At the upper lumbar levels, the short contralateral working distance allowed effective contralateral decompression without the need for angled visualization or excessive scope manipulation.

Postoperative considerations and tasks

Operative time and perioperative complications were recorded. In cases involving multiple levels, operative time was calculated per level. Neurological status was assessed clinically during the early postoperative period. Preoperative and postoperative CT images were reviewed to evaluate the adequacy of bilateral decompression and the preservation of the approach-side facet joint.

All six procedures were completed successfully using the described navigation-assisted 0° UBE technique. No intraoperative complications, including dural tears or neurological injury, were observed, and no cases required conversion to open surgery.

The mean operative time was approximately 92 minutes per level. Estimated blood loss was minimal in all cases (approximately 5–10 mL). The mean length of stay was approximately 15.5 days. The minimum follow-up period was 3 months.

Clinical symptoms improved in all patients. The mean Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score for back pain improved from 75.0 preoperatively to 35.8 at 3 months postoperatively, while the mean VAS score for leg pain improved from 73.3 to 32.5.

Postoperative CT imaging confirmed adequate bilateral decompression at all treated levels. Importantly, the approach-side facet joints were preserved without evidence of excessive bone removal. All patients experienced improvement in preoperative neurological symptoms during the early postoperative period.

Discussion

Upper lumbar spinal stenosis presents unique technical challenges because of its distinct anatomical characteristics, including narrow interlaminar spaces, deep working corridors, and steep facet orientation. These features restrict endoscopic maneuverability and increase the risk of unintended bone resection, particularly on the approach side, where facet joint preservation is critical for maintaining postoperative stability.

Angled endoscopes are commonly used in UBE surgery to expand the field of view. However, in anatomically constrained upper lumbar segments, increased angulation may paradoxically increase exposure of the approach-side facet joint and thereby raise the risk of facet violation. The present technical note introduces a concept-driven strategy that combines navigation-assisted portal optimization with a 0° endoscope, deliberately accepting constrained exposure as a structural safety mechanism rather than attempting to overcome it.

The central concept of this technique is that the spinous processes at the upper lumbar levels mechanically restrict contralateral tilting of the endoscope. When a 0° endoscope is used, this restriction results in a straight viewing axis that helps control ipsilateral facet exposure. As a result, excessive approach-side decompression is mechanically constrained. At the same time, effective contralateral decompression remains achievable because the contralateral target is anatomically close at these levels, allowing sufficient working reach despite limited angulation. In this strategy, navigation plays a critical supporting role. By enabling precise determination of the skin incision and portal trajectory, navigation facilitates reliable access to the intended interlaminar entry point even in the presence of spinous process overhang and laminar constraints. Compared with standard fluoroscopic guidance, navigation enables real-time three-dimensional trajectory confirmation and may reduce reliance on repeated fluoroscopic checks during portal placement and interlaminar targeting. Recent studies have further highlighted the emerging role of navigation-assisted UBE and facet-preserving decompression strategies in upper lumbar pathology, supporting the importance of accurate trajectory control and preservation of posterior stabilizing structures in anatomically constrained segments (7-9). Once the interlaminar space is established, bilateral decompression can be performed efficiently and reproducibly without angled visualization. Navigation should not be interpreted as a replacement for the free-hand nature of UBE, but rather as a complementary tool that facilitates precise trajectory planning and reproducible interlaminar targeting in anatomically constrained upper lumbar segments. It is important to emphasize that this report does not suggest the superiority of a 0° endoscope over angled endoscopes in all clinical settings. Rather, it highlights that under specific anatomical and navigational conditions, the controlled limitation of ipsilateral exposure provided by a straight-view configuration may facilitate procedural control. The accompanying surgical video demonstrates this dynamic relationship between anatomy, viewing axis, and facet preservation, complementing the static information provided by figures and postoperative imaging (Video 1).

This study should be interpreted as a hypothesis-generating technical report rather than a comparative analysis, given the small number of cases and absence of a control group.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the number of cases was small, and all procedures were performed by a single experienced surgeon at a single institution, which may limit generalizability. Second, this report was designed as a technical note rather than a comparative study, and no direct comparison with angled endoscopes was performed. Third, long-term clinical outcomes and radiographic assessments of segmental stability were not evaluated. Further studies with larger cohorts and comparative designs are warranted to clarify the broader applicability of this concept.

In addition, the use of navigation may be associated with increased setup time, workflow complexity, and additional cost.

Conclusions

Navigation-assisted UBE decompression using a 0° endoscope is a feasible technical option for upper lumbar spinal stenosis. By integrating navigation-guided portal optimization with a straight endoscopic viewing axis, this approach may reduce the risk of approach-side facet violation while enabling effective bilateral decompression in anatomically constrained upper lumbar segments.
