“Locked-in Technique” Following Balloon Pullback to Reposition a Migrated Thrombus in a Coronary Aneurysm
Authors: Yuki Hayashi, Hiroyuki Yamamoto, Koki Matsuo, Tomofumi Takaya
Published: 2026

A 60-year-old woman presented with acute inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. Emergency coronary angiography revealed total occlusion of the proximal right coronary artery. Because a guidewire could not be advanced to the culprit lesion, intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) using a knuckled wire was performed and revealed a large thrombus within a coronary artery aneurysm (Supplementary Figure). An intermediate wire was subsequently advanced; however, thrombus aspiration and prolonged balloon angioplasty with a perfusion balloon failed to restore coronary flow, and the thrombus migrated distally (Figure A). We planned to reposition the thrombus into the aneurysm and seal it with a stent, a bailout strategy termed the “locked-in technique” (Figure B). A 3.0/10-mm semicompliant balloon was inflated distal to the aneurysm and pulled back proximally, returning the thrombus to the aneurysmal segment (Figure C). A 3.5/24-mm drug-eluting stent was implanted across the aneurysm and optimized by postdilatation (distally 4.0 mm and proximally 5.0 mm, respectively). Final IVUS confirmed complete sealing of the organized thrombus, and final angiography demonstrated restored coronary flow without distal embolization (Figure D,E).

Massive thrombus within a coronary aneurysm presents technical challenges during primary percutaneous coronary intervention. The locked-in technique may be a useful bailout strategy for restoring coronary flow while minimizing distal embolization. Potential complications include thrombus fragmentation, distal embolization, and late stent thrombosis. Careful balloon manipulation, IVUS-guided stent sizing, and aggressive post-dilatation are essential to mitigate these risks. Distal protection devices were considered but not used because of the aneurysmal anatomy and vessel tortuosity.

Dual-antiplatelet therapy (aspirin and prasugrel) with edoxaban was administered during hospitalization. After discharge, antithrombotic therapy was de-escalated to aspirin plus edoxaban. Based on follow-up imaging findings and the clinical course, further de-escalation to anticoagulant monotherapy is planned after 12 months.
