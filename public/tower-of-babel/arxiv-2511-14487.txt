An improved existence theorem for rigid nonlinearly elastic plates
Trung Hieu Giang, Cristinel Mardare

Abstract
A plate is rigid if its admissible displacement fields inducing vanishing two-dimensional strain tensors must vanish. We prove that the nonlinear model of Kirchhoff-Love for such a plate has a solution for any applied forces and boundary conditions. Then we give sufficient conditions on the data ensuring the rigidity of the plate. Together, these results substantially generalize an existence theorem by Rabier whereby the plate is assumed to be clamped on its entire boundary.

74B20, 74G22, 74K20.

1
Introduction

Consider a plate with thickness
2\varepsilon>0
made of a homogeneous and isotropic elastic material with Lamé constants
\lambda\geq 0
and
\mu>0
. Assume that the plate occupies in its natural state (that is, an unstressed state) the three-dimensional set

{\overline{\omega}}\times[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]

where
\omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is a bounded and connected open set whose boundary is Lipschitz-continuous in the sense of Adams & Fournier
[
1
]
, and that it is subjected to applied body and surface forces with densities
(f^{\varepsilon}_{i})\in L^{2}(\Omega^{\varepsilon};{\mathbb{R}}^{3})
per unit volume and
(g^{\varepsilon}_{i})\in L^{2}(\Gamma^{\varepsilon}_{\pm};{\mathbb{R}}^{3})
per unit area, where

\displaystyle{}\Omega^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle:=\omega\times(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon),

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\varepsilon}_{\pm}

\displaystyle:=\omega\times\{-\varepsilon,\varepsilon\},

Then Kirchhoff-Love’s two-dimensional theory of plates asserts that the displacement field

{\boldsymbol{u}}:=(u_{i}):\omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}

of the middle section of the plate arising in response to these applied forces minimizes the functional
J:V(\omega)\to{\mathbb{R}}
, where

V(\omega)\subset H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{2}(\omega)

is the set of all admissible displacement fields of the middle section of the plate,

\displaystyle{}J({\boldsymbol{u}}):=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}})\|_{\lambda,\mu}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{6}\|{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{u}})\|_{\lambda,\mu}^{2}-L({\boldsymbol{u}})

(1)

is the two-dimensional total energy of the plate, and

\displaystyle{}L({\boldsymbol{u}}):=\int_{\omega}(p^{\varepsilon}_{i}u_{i}-q^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}u_{3})dy

(2)

is the work of the two-dimensional forces acting on the middle section of the plate.

The tensor fields
{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}})=(E_{\alpha\beta}({\boldsymbol{u}}))\in L^{2}(\omega;{\mathbb{S}}^{2})
and
{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{u}})=(F_{\alpha\beta}({\boldsymbol{u}}))\in L^{2}(\omega;{\mathbb{S}}^{2})
appearing in the definition (
1
) of the functional
J
are defined by

\displaystyle{}E_{\alpha\beta}({\boldsymbol{u}})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\alpha}u_{\beta}+\partial_{\beta}u_{\alpha}+\partial_{\alpha}u_{3}\partial_{\beta}u_{3}),

(3)

\displaystyle F_{\alpha\beta}({\boldsymbol{u}})

\displaystyle:=\partial_{\alpha\beta}u_{3},

for all
{\boldsymbol{u}}=(u_{i})\in H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{2}(\omega)
, while the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\lambda,\mu}:L^{2}(\omega;{\mathbb{S}}^{2})\to{\mathbb{R}}
appearing in the same definition is defined by

\displaystyle{}\|(s_{\alpha\beta})\|_{\lambda,\mu}^{2}:=\int_{\omega}a^{\alpha\beta\sigma\tau}s_{\sigma\tau}(y)s_{\alpha\beta}(y)dy

(4)

for all
(s_{\alpha\beta})\in L^{2}(\omega;{\mathbb{S}}^{2})
, where

\displaystyle{}a^{\alpha\beta\sigma\tau}:=\frac{4\lambda\mu}{\lambda+2\mu}\delta_{\sigma\tau}\delta_{\alpha\beta}+2\mu(\delta_{\alpha\sigma}\delta_{\beta\tau}+\delta_{\alpha\tau}\delta_{\beta\sigma}).

(5)

Note that the four-order tensor
a^{\alpha\beta\sigma\tau}
is the two-dimensional elasticity tensor associated with the elastic material constituting the plate and that the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\lambda,\mu}
is equivalent with the usual norm of the Lebesgue space
L^{2}(\omega;{\mathbb{S}}^{2})
, thanks to the positivity of the Lamé constants of the elastic material constituting the plate. In fact, as is well known, the positivity of the Lamé constants can be replaced by the weaker assumption that
\mu>0
and
\lambda>-\frac{2}{3}\mu
.

Finally, the functions
p_{i}^{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(\omega)
and
q_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}(\omega)
appearing in the definition (
2
) of the linear form
L
are defined in terms of the densities of the applied forces on the plate by the relations:

\displaystyle{}p^{\varepsilon}_{i}

\displaystyle:=\int_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon}f^{\varepsilon}_{i}(\cdot,x_{3})dx_{3}+g^{\varepsilon}_{i}(\cdot,+\varepsilon)+g^{\varepsilon}_{i}(\cdot,-\varepsilon),

(6)

\displaystyle q^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\int_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon}x_{3}g^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}(\cdot,x_{3})dx_{3}+\varepsilon\Big(g^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}(\cdot,+\varepsilon)-g^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}(\cdot,-\varepsilon)\Big).

Over the past few decades, several papers have been devoted to studying existence results for this nonlinearly elastic plate model under various sets of assumptions. Nečas & Naumann
[
9
]
proved that the functional
J
has at least one minimizer when the plate is subjected to a perpendicular load. Ciarlet & Destuynder
[
5
]
proved the existence of a minimizer to
J
provided that the norms
\|p_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}
are small enough. Rabier
[
8
]
proved that if the plate is totally clamped, then the smallness assumption in
[
5
]
is not necessary for proving the existence of a minimizer to
J
.

Among the above results, most relevant to this paper is Rabier’s use of the boundary conditions to remove the smallness assumption on the tangential forces needed in
[
5
]
to prove the coerciveness of the functional
J
over the space
V(\omega)
, hence the existence of minimizers for the above Kirchhoff-Love plate model. But Rabier’s method applies only to totally clamped plates, that is, for plates whose admissible displacement fields satisfy the full Dirichlet boundary conditions associated with the Euler-Lagrange equations satisfied by the smooth minimizers of
J
.

Essential in Rabier’s approach is that a totally clamped plate is rigid, in the sense that its admissible displacement fields satisfy property (
7
) in Section
3
below, which is a necessary condition for the infimum of
J
over
V(\omega)
to be finite.

One objective of this paper, achieved in Theorem
2
, is to prove that this rigidity property is also sufficient for the existence of minimizers of
J
over
V(\omega)
.

The other objective of this paper, achieved in Theorems
3
,
4
,
5
, and
6
, is to give sufficient conditions for the rigidity property to hold. These sufficient conditions are boundary conditions on the transverse component
u_{3}
of the displacement fields
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{i})\in V(\omega)
in Theorem
3
, while they are boundary conditions on the tangential components
u_{\alpha}
of
\boldsymbol{u}
in Theorems
4
,
5
and
6
. The existence theorems obtained by combining these rigidity results with Theorem
2
improve Rabier’s existence theorem in
[
8
]
, since they hold under weaker boundary conditions than the full Dirichlet boundary conditions used in
[
8
]
; see Corollaries
1
,
2
,
3
and
4
. Note, however, that Corollary
1
only applies to convex domains
\omega
, while Corollaries
2
,
3
, and
4
apply to general domains
\omega
, as does Rabier’s existence theorem in
[
8
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we introduce the notation used in this paper. Section
3
establishes an existence theorem for the Kirchhoff-Love nonlinear plate model under the sole assumption that the plate is rigid (so irrespectively of the conditions ensuring the rigidity of the plate).

Sections
4
and
5
give several sets of sufficient conditions ensuring the rigidity of the plate: Section
4
under the assumption that
\omega
is convex and the transversal displacements vanish on its boundary, while Section
5
for general domains
\omega
under the assumption that the tangential displacements vanish on its boundary, or on a part of it that is sufficiently large in a specific sense.

3
Existence theorem for rigid plates

Let
V(\omega)
denote the set of all admissible displacements of a nonlinearly elastic plate whose total energy is the functional
J
defined by (
1
)-(
6
).

We prove in this section that if
V(\omega)
satisfies a certain rigidity assumption (see (
7
)), then the energy functional
J
has a minimizer in the set
V(\omega)
. The point of this result is that it dissociates the proof of existence of minimizers from the boundary conditions that the admissible displacements might satisfy. Thus, establishing an existence theorem reduces to verifying whether the rigidity assumption (
7
) is satisfied or not by a given plate.

To begin with, we recall the following property of the functional
J
, as well as its proof for the reader’s convenience.

Theorem 1

Let
\omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a bounded and connected open set whose boundary is Lipschitz-continuous. Then the functional
J:H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{2}(\omega)\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by (
1
)-(
6
) is sequentially weakly lower semicontinuous.

Proof.
Since
\omega
is a bounded Lipschitz in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,
H^{2}(\omega)\subset W^{1,4}(\omega)
and the embedding is compact; cf. Adams & Fournier
[
1
]
. Then, if a sequence
{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}=(u^{n}_{i})
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and an element
{\boldsymbol{u}}=(u_{i})
in the space
H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{2}(\omega)
satisfy

u_{\alpha}^{n}\rightharpoonup u_{\alpha}\text{ \ in }H^{1}(\omega)\text{ when }n\to\infty

and

u_{3}^{n}\rightharpoonup u_{3}\text{ \ in }H^{2}(\omega)\text{ when }n\to\infty,

where
\rightharpoonup
denotes the convergence with respect to the weak topology of the space in which the convergence takes place, then

u_{3}^{n}\to u_{3}\text{ \ in }W^{1,4}(\omega)\text{ when }n\to\infty,

where
\to
denotes the convergence with respect to the strong topology (induced by the norm of the space).

Consequently

\displaystyle{}E_{\alpha\beta}({\boldsymbol{u}}^{n})

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup E_{\alpha\beta}({\boldsymbol{u}})\text{ \ in }L^{2}(\omega)\text{ when }n\to\infty,

\displaystyle F_{\alpha\beta}({\boldsymbol{u}}^{n})

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup F_{\alpha\beta}({\boldsymbol{u}})\text{ \ in }L^{2}(\omega)\text{ when }n\to\infty.

Then

\displaystyle{}\|{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}})\|_{\lambda,\mu}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}}^{n})\|_{\lambda,\mu},

\displaystyle\|{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{u}})\|_{\lambda,\mu}

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{u}}^{n})\|_{\lambda,\mu},

\displaystyle L({\boldsymbol{u}})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}L({\boldsymbol{u}}_{n}),

so that, in view of the definition (
1
) of the functional
J
, we have

J({\boldsymbol{u}})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}J({\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}).

\square

The functional
J:V(\omega)\to{\mathbb{R}}
being sequentially weakly lower semicontinuous on any subset
V(\omega)
of the Hilbert space
H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{2}(\omega)
equipped with the induced topology, in order to prove that
J
has a minimizer in the set
V(\omega)
it suffices to prove that
V(\omega)
is sequentially weakly closed in
H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{2}(\omega)
and that
J
possesses a sequence of minimizers that is bounded in the space
H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{2}(\omega)
.

In the absence of any smallness assumptions of the tangential forces, like in Ciarlet & Destuynder
[
5
]
, the existence or nonexistence of a bounded sequence of minimizers for
J
depends on whether the plate is rigid or not. By definition, a plate is called rigid if the set
V(\omega)
of all its admissible displacements has the following property:

\displaystyle{}{\boldsymbol{u}}\in V(\omega)\text{ and }{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}})={\boldsymbol{0}}\text{ in }\omega\text{ implies }{\boldsymbol{u}}={\boldsymbol{0}}\text{ in }\omega.

(7)

If a plate is not rigid, then there exists
{\boldsymbol{w}}=(w_{i})\neq{\boldsymbol{0}}
in
V(\omega)
such that
{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{w}})={\boldsymbol{0}}
. Assuming that
V(\omega)=V_{H}(\omega)\times V_{3}(\omega)
where
V_{H}(\omega)
is a proper subspace of
H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)
and
V_{3}(\omega)
is a subspace of
H^{2}(\omega)
, then for each real number
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the vector field
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{t}:=(t^{2}w_{1},t^{2}w_{2},tw_{3})
belongs to
V(\omega)
and satisfies

{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{w}}_{t})=t^{2}{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{w}})={\boldsymbol{0}}.

Therefore,

J({\boldsymbol{w}}_{t}):=t^{2}\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{6}\|{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{w}})\|_{\lambda,\mu}^{2}-t^{2}\int_{\omega}p^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}w_{\alpha}dy-t\int_{\omega}(p_{3}w_{3}-q^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}w_{3})dy

for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Consequently, there exist functions
p^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}\in L^{2}(\omega)
such that

\inf_{{\boldsymbol{u}}\in V(\omega)}J({\boldsymbol{u}})=-\infty.

This proves that the existence of minimizers for a nonrigid plate cannot be proved for general applied forces (this means without any restriction on the densities
p^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}\in L^{2}(\omega)
of the applied forces). In this sense, the existence theorem of Ciarlet & Destuynder
[
5
]
, whereby the existence of minimizers of
J
is proved under the assumption that
\sum_{\alpha}\|p^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}
is small enough, is essentially optimal for nonrigid plates.

If the plate is rigid, the existence of minimizers has been established by Rabier
[
8
]
for general applied forces, but under the additional assumption that the admissible displacements
{\boldsymbol{u}}=(u_{i})\in V(\omega)
satisfy the boundary conditions
u_{i}=\partial_{\nu}u_{3}=0
on
\partial\omega
.

The next theorem improves this result by removing this additional assumption. Thus, if a plate is rigid, the existence of a minimizer for the functional
J
can be established in the general case, that is, for general applied forces. Sufficient conditions ensuring the rigidity of the plate, hence the existence of a minimizer, are given in Sections
4
and
5
.

Note that assumption (
8
) in the next theorem means that the space
V_{3}(\omega)
does not contain any non-zero affine function.

Theorem 2

Let
\omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a bounded and connected open set whose boundary is Lipschitz-continuous. Let
V(\omega)=V_{H}(\omega)\times V_{3}(\omega)
, where
V_{H}(\omega)
is closed subspace of
H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)
and

V_{3}(\omega)
is closed subspace of
H^{2}(\omega)
such that

\displaystyle{}u_{3}\in V_{3}(\omega)\textup{ and }\partial_{\alpha\beta}u_{3}=0\textup{ in }\omega\textup{ imply }u_{3}=0\textup{ in }\omega.

(8)

Assume that
V(\omega)
satisfies the following rigidity property:

\displaystyle{}{\boldsymbol{u}}\in V(\omega)\textup{ and }{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}})={\boldsymbol{0}}\textup{ in }\omega\textup{ imply }{\boldsymbol{u}}={\boldsymbol{0}}\textup{ in }\omega.

(9)

Then the functional
J
defined by (
1
)-(
6
) has a minimizer in the set
V(\omega)
.

Proof.
The proof consists in adapting Rabier’s argument to general displacement fields, which may not satisfy the boundary conditions
u_{i}=\partial_{\nu}u_{3}=0
on
\partial\omega
used in Rabier
[
8
]
. We divide the proof into six parts, numbered (i) to (vi), for clarity.

(i) Three basic observations.

The first observation is that the rigidity assumption (
9
) implies that the subspace
V_{H}(\omega)
of
H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)
satisfies the following property:

\displaystyle{}(u_{\alpha})\in V_{H}(\omega)\textup{ and }\partial_{\alpha}u_{\beta}+\partial_{\beta}u_{\alpha}=0\textup{ in }\omega\textup{ imply }u_{\alpha}=0\textup{ in }\omega.

(10)

For, if
(u_{\alpha})\in V_{H}(\omega)
satisfies
\partial_{\alpha}u_{\beta}+\partial_{\beta}u_{\alpha}=0
in
\omega
, then the displacement field
{\boldsymbol{u}}:=(u_{1},u_{2},0)
belongs to
V(\omega)
and satisfies
{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}})={\boldsymbol{0}}
in
\omega
. Hence the rigidity assumption (
9
) implies that
{\boldsymbol{u}}={\boldsymbol{0}}
, or equivalently, that
u_{\alpha}=0
in
\omega
.

Note that property (
10
) of the space
V_{H}(\omega)
implies that the semi-norm

(u_{\alpha})\in H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)\mapsto\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\|\partial_{\alpha}u_{\beta}+\partial_{\beta}u_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}\in{\mathbb{R}}

becomes a norm equivalent to the norm of the space
H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)
when it is restricted to the subspace
V_{H}(\omega)
of
H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)
, thanks to the open mapping theorem (see, e.g., Ciarlet
[
3
]
).

So there exists a constant
C_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\displaystyle{}\sum_{\alpha}\|u_{\alpha}\|_{H^{1}(\omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\|\partial_{\alpha}u_{\beta}+\partial_{\beta}u_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\text{for all }(u_{\alpha})\in V_{H}(\omega).

(11)

The second observation is that assumption (
8
) of the theorem is equivalent to the assumption that the semi-norm

u_{3}\in H^{2}(\omega)\mapsto\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\|\partial_{\alpha\beta}u_{3}\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}\in{\mathbb{R}}

becomes a norm equivalent to the norm of the space
H^{2}(\omega)
when restricted to the subspace
V_{3}(\omega)
of
H^{2}(\omega)
. This equivalence can be proved by using the open mapping theorem (see, e.g., Ciarlet
[
3
]
). So there exists a constant
C_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\displaystyle{}\|u_{3}\|_{H^{2}(\omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\|\partial_{\alpha\beta}u_{3}\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\text{for all }u_{3}\in V_{3}(\omega).

(12)

The third observation is that for each minimizing sequence
{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}
of the functional
J:V(\omega)\to{\mathbb{R}}
, there exists an integer
n_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that, for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

J({\boldsymbol{u}}^{n})\leq J({\boldsymbol{0}})=0.

Therefore,

\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}}^{n})\|_{\lambda,\mu}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{6}\|{\boldsymbol{F}}({\boldsymbol{u}}^{n})\|_{\lambda,\mu}^{2}\leq L({\boldsymbol{u}}^{n})

for all
n\geq n_{0}
. Combined with the definition of the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\lambda,\mu}
, this implies that

\displaystyle{}\frac{\mu\varepsilon}{8}\|(\partial_{\alpha}u^{n}_{\beta}+\partial_{\beta}u^{n}_{\alpha}+\partial_{\alpha}u^{n}_{3}\partial_{\beta}u^{n}_{3})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}+\frac{2\mu\varepsilon^{3}}{3}\|(\partial_{\alpha\beta}u^{n}_{3})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}\leq L({\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}),

(13)

on the one hand.

The definition of the linear form
L
implies that

\displaystyle{}L({\boldsymbol{u}}^{n})

\displaystyle=\int_{\omega}(p^{\varepsilon}_{i}u^{n}_{i}-q^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}u^{n}_{3})dy\leq\sum_{\alpha}\|p^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}\|u^{n}_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}

\displaystyle+\Big(\|p^{\varepsilon}_{3}\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}+\sum_{\alpha}\|p^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\|u^{n}_{3}\|_{H^{1}(\omega)},

which combined with inequalities (
11
) and (
12
) next implies that

\displaystyle{}L({\boldsymbol{u}}^{n})

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{C_{1}}\|(p^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}\|(\partial_{\alpha}u^{n}_{\beta}+\partial_{\beta}u^{n}_{\alpha})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{C_{2}}\Big(\|p^{\varepsilon}_{3}\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}+\|(p^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\|(\partial_{\alpha\beta}u^{n}_{3})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)},

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{C_{1}}\|(p^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}\|(\partial_{\alpha}u^{n}_{\beta}+\partial_{\beta}u^{n}_{\alpha}+\partial_{\alpha}u^{n}_{3}\partial_{\beta}u^{n}_{3})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{C_{1}}\|(p^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}\|(\partial_{\alpha}u^{n}_{3}\partial_{\beta}u^{n}_{3})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{C_{2}}\Big(\|p^{\varepsilon}_{3}\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}+\|(p^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\|(\partial_{\alpha\beta}u^{n}_{3})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}.

Then

\displaystyle{}L({\boldsymbol{u}}^{n})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu\varepsilon}{16}\|(\partial_{\alpha}u^{n}_{\beta}+\partial_{\beta}u^{n}_{\alpha}+\partial_{\alpha}u^{n}_{3}\partial_{\beta}u^{n}_{3})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}+\frac{4C_{1}}{\mu\varepsilon}\|(p^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}

(14)

\displaystyle+\sqrt{2C_{1}}\|(p^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}\|(\partial_{\alpha}u^{n}_{3})\|_{L^{4}(\omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\mu\varepsilon^{3}}{3}\|(\partial_{\alpha\beta}u^{n}_{3})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}+\frac{3C_{2}}{4\mu\varepsilon^{3}}\Big(\|p^{\varepsilon}_{3}\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}+\|(p^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}\Big),

on the other hand.

Combining inequality (
14
) with inequality (
13
) yields

\displaystyle{}\frac{\mu\varepsilon}{16}\|(\partial_{\alpha}u^{n}_{\beta}+\partial_{\beta}u^{n}_{\alpha}+\partial_{\alpha}u^{n}_{3}\partial_{\beta}u^{n}_{3})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}+\frac{\mu\varepsilon^{3}}{3}\|(\partial_{\alpha\beta}u^{n}_{3})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}

(15)

\displaystyle\leq A+B\|(\partial_{\alpha}u^{n}_{3})\|_{L^{4}(\omega)}^{2},

where

\displaystyle{}A

\displaystyle:=\frac{4C_{1}}{\mu\varepsilon}\|(p^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}+\frac{3C_{2}}{4\mu\varepsilon^{3}}\Big(\|p^{\varepsilon}_{3}\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}+\|(p^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}^{2}\Big),

(16)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{2C_{1}}\|(p^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha})\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}.

(ii) We prove here that if all minimizing sequences of
J:V(\omega)\to{\mathbb{R}}
are unbounded in the space
H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{2}(\omega)
, then all minimizing sequences
{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}=(u^{n}_{i})\in V(\omega)
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, of
J
must satisfy

\|(u^{n}_{\alpha})\|_{H^{1}(\omega)}\to\infty\text{ \ when }n\to\infty,

and

\|u^{n}_{3}\|_{H^{2}(\omega)}\to\infty\text{ \ when }n\to\infty.

To prove this, assume first that there exists a minimizing sequence
{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}
of

Theorem
2
proves that the two-dimensional model of a nonlinearly elastic plate has solutions if the surface is rigid (in the sense of the uniqueness property (
7
)). Thus, to prove an existence theorem for a plate under a specific set of data, it suffices to study its rigidity.

In the next two sections, we provide two different types of sufficient conditions guaranteeing the rigidity of a plate: first we consider the case of boundary conditions imposed on the normal component of the displacement field (Section
4
), then we consider the case of boundary conditions imposed on the tangential components of the displacement field (Section
5
). Note that these sufficient conditions might not be necessary, but they are general enough to be useful in most practical situations.

4
A first type of rigidity theorem

In this section, we study the rigidity of plates subjected to boundary conditions of place on its transverse displacement fields
u_{3}:\omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
. We prove that such a plate is rigid by first using the implication

\partial_{\alpha}u_{\beta}+\partial_{\beta}u_{\alpha}+\partial_{\alpha}u_{3}\partial_{\beta}u_{3}=0\text{ in }\omega\Rightarrow\det(\partial_{\alpha\beta}u_{3})=0\text{ in }\omega

satisfied by all vector fields
(u_{i})\in H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{2}(\omega)
, then by using boundary conditions on
u_{3}
to deduce that
u_{3}
must vanish in
\omega
.

It is already known since Rabier
[
8
]
that the above implications hold for totally clamped plates, that is, when the admissible displacement fields
(u_{i})\in H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{2}(\omega)
satisfy the boundary conditions

u_{i}=\partial_{\nu}u_{3}=0\text{ \ on }\partial\omega.

In this section, we complete Rabier’s result by showing that if the set
\omega
is convex, then it suffices to assume that the admissible displacements only satisfy the boundary conditions

u_{3}=0\text{ \ on }\partial\omega

and the uniqueness property

\partial_{\alpha}u_{\beta}+\partial_{\beta}u_{\alpha}=0\textup{ in }\omega\textup{ imply }u_{\alpha}=0\textup{ in }\omega.

The main result of this section is Theorem
3
. The proof of this theorem relies on two preliminary results, established below in Propositions
1
and
3
. The proof of Proposition
3
, which is a generalization of a well-known result stated in Proposition
2
, is itself based on Lemmas
1
,
2
and
3
.

We begin by proving the following proposition, which will be needed in the proof of Theorem
3
.

Proposition 1

Let
\omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be an open set. Then a vector field
(u_{i})\in H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{2}(\omega)
satisfies

\partial_{\alpha}u_{\beta}+\partial_{\beta}u_{\alpha}+\partial_{\alpha}u_{3}\partial_{\beta}u_{3}=0\textup{ \ in }\omega

only if

\det(\partial_{\alpha\beta}u_{3})=0\textup{ \ in }\omega.

Proof.
This lemma is a particular case of the nonlinear Saint-Venant compatibility conditions established in Ciarlet & S. Mardare
[
6
, Theorem 4.1]
, but can be established here in a simpler way by using the symmetry of higher order derivatives of distributions. More specifically, if
(u_{i})\in H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{2}(\omega)
satisfies

\partial_{\alpha}u_{\beta}+\partial_{\beta}u_{\alpha}+\partial_{\alpha}u_{3}\partial_{\beta}u_{3}=0\textup{ \ in }\omega,

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle{}2\partial_{1}u_{1}

\displaystyle=-(\partial_{1}u_{3})^{2}\textup{ \ in }\omega,

\displaystyle 2\partial_{2}u_{2}

\displaystyle=-(\partial_{2}u_{3})^{2}\textup{ \ in }\omega,

\displaystyle\partial_{1}u_{2}+\partial_{2}u_{1}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{1}u_{3}\partial_{2}u_{3}\textup{ \ in }\omega,

then

\displaystyle{}0

\displaystyle=\partial_{11}(\partial_{2}u_{2})+\partial_{22}(\partial_{1}u_{1})-\partial_{12}(\partial_{1}u_{2}+\partial_{2}u_{1})

\displaystyle=\partial_{12}(\partial_{1}u_{3}\partial_{2}u_{3})-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{11}((\partial_{2}u_{3})^{2})-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{22}((\partial_{1}u_{3})^{2})

\displaystyle=\partial_{121}u_{3}\partial_{2}u_{3}+\partial_{21}u_{3}\partial_{12}u_{3}+\partial_{11}u_{3}\partial_{22}u_{3}+\partial_{1}u_{3}\partial_{122}u_{3}

\displaystyle-\partial_{112}u_{3}\partial_{2}u_{3}-\partial_{12}u_{3}\partial_{12}u_{3}-\partial_{221}u_{3}\partial_{1}u_{3}-\partial_{21}u_{3}\partial_{21}u_{3}

\displaystyle=\partial_{11}u_{3}\partial_{22}u_{3}-(\partial_{12}u_{3})^{2}=\det(\partial_{\alpha\beta}u_{3})\text{ \ in }\omega.

\square

The next result is well known (see, e.g., Rabier
[
8
]
), but we record it here with its proof as it provides the idea to proving Proposition

.

Proposition 2

Let
\omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be an open set such that
\omega\neq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Then a function
u_{3}\in H^{2}_{0}(\omega)
satisfies

\det(\partial_{\alpha\beta}u_{3})=0\textup{ \ in }\omega

only if

u_{3}=0\textup{ \ in }\omega.

Proof.
Given any function
f\in{\mathcal{D}}(\omega)
, a repeated use of the integration by parts formula
shows that

\displaystyle{}\int_{\omega}|\nabla f|^{2}dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\omega}\Big[\partial_{22}((y_{2})^{2})(\partial_{1}f)^{2}+\partial_{11}((y_{1})^{2})(\partial_{2}f)^{2}\Big]dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\omega}\Big[(y_{2})^{2}\partial_{22}((\partial_{1}f)^{2})+(y_{1})^{2}\partial_{11}((\partial_{2}f)^{2})\Big]dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\omega}(y_{2})^{2}\Big((\partial_{12}f)^{2}+\partial_{1}f\partial_{122}f\Big)dy

\displaystyle+\int_{\omega}(y_{1})^{2}\Big((\partial_{12}f)^{2}+\partial_{2}f\partial_{112}f)\Big)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\omega}(y_{2})^{2}\Big(\partial_{1}(\partial_{1}f\partial_{22}f)-\det(\partial_{\alpha\beta}f)\Big)dy

\displaystyle+\int_{\omega}(y_{1})^{2}\Big(\partial_{2}(\partial_{2}f\partial_{11}f)-\det(\partial_{\alpha\beta}f)\Big)dy.

Since

(y_{1})^{2}\partial_{2}(\partial_{2}f\partial_{11}f)=\partial_{2}((y_{1})^{2}\partial_{2}f\partial_{11}f)

and

(y_{2})^{2}\partial_{1}(\partial_{2}f\partial_{22}f)=\partial_{1}((y_{2})^{2}\partial_{1}f\partial_{22}f),

we deduce that

\displaystyle{}\int_{\omega}|\nabla f|^{2}dy=-\int_{\omega}|y|^{2}\det(\partial_{\alpha\beta}f)dy\text{ for all }f\in{\mathcal{D}}(\omega).

Since
H^{2}_{0}(\omega)
is the closure in
H^{2}(\omega)
of
{\mathcal{D}}(\omega)
, given any function
u_{3}\in H^{2}_{0}(\omega)
, there exists a sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that
\|f_{n}-u_{3}\|_{H^{2}(\omega)}\to 0
when
n\to\infty
. Then passing to the limit when
n\to\infty
in the above equality with
f
replaced by
f_{n}
yields the equality

\displaystyle{}\int_{\omega}|\nabla u_{3}|^{2}dy=-\int_{\omega}|y|^{2}\det(\partial_{\alpha\beta}u_{3})dy\text{ for all }u_{3}\in H^{2}_{0}(\omega).

Therefore, if a function
u_{3}\in H^{2}_{0}(\omega)
satisfies
\det(\partial_{\alpha\beta}u_{3})=0
in
\omega
, then
\nabla u_{3}=0
in
\omega
, so
u_{3}=0
in
\omega
thanks to the assumption that
\omega\neq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

\square

Proposition
2
shows that if a function
u_{3}\in H^{2}(\omega)
satisfies the boundary conditions

u_{3}=\partial_{\nu}u_{3}=0\textup{ \ on }\partial\omega,

in addition to the partial differential equation

\det(\partial_{\alpha\beta}u_{3})=0\textup{ \ in }\omega,

where
\omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is a Lipschitz domain, then necessarily
u_{3}=0
in
\omega
. We will prove in Proposition

below that if

is convex, then the boundary condition

instead of the above one, suffices to conclude that

in

Lemma 1

Let
\omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a connected open set whose boundary is Lipschitz-continuous. Then

\displaystyle{}\int_{\omega}[u,v]wdy=(u,v,w)_{\partial\omega}-(u,v,w)_{\omega}

(36)

for all functions
u,v,w\in{\mathcal{C}}^{2}({\overline{\omega}})
, where

\displaystyle{}[u,v]

\displaystyle:=\partial_{11}u\partial_{22}v+\partial_{22}u\partial_{11}v-2\partial_{12}u\partial_{12}v

and

\displaystyle{}(u,v,w)_{\omega}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\omega}\Big[\partial_{11}u\partial_{2}v\partial_{2}w+\partial_{22}u\partial_{1}v\partial_{1}w-\partial_{12}u(\partial_{1}v\partial_{2}w+\partial_{2}v\partial_{1}w)\Big]dy,

\displaystyle(u,v,w)_{\partial\omega}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\partial\omega}\Big[\big(\partial_{11}u\partial_{2}v-\partial_{12}u\partial_{1}v\big)w\nu_{2}+\big(\partial_{22}u\partial_{1}v-\partial_{12}u\partial_{2}v\big)w\nu_{1}\Big]dy.

Proof.
Since the space
{\mathcal{C}}^{3}({\overline{\omega}})
is dense in
{\mathcal{C}}^{2}({\overline{\omega}})
, it suffices to prove formula (
36
) for functions
u
and
v
in
{\mathcal{C}}^{3}({\overline{\omega}})
. In this case, this formula is a consequence of the integration by parts formula, applied several times to prove the following equalities:

\displaystyle{}\int_{\omega}[u,v]wdy

\displaystyle=\int_{\omega}\Big[\big(\partial_{11}u\partial_{22}v-\partial_{12}u\partial_{12}v\big)w+\big(\partial_{22}u\partial_{11}v-\partial_{12}u\partial_{12}v\big)w\Big]dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\omega}\Big\{\partial_{2}\Big[w\big(\partial_{11}u\partial_{2}v-\partial_{12}u\partial_{1}v\big)\Big]-\partial_{2}\big(w\partial_{11}u\big)\partial_{2}v+\partial_{2}\big(w\partial_{12}u\big)\partial_{1}v\Big\}dy

\displaystyle+\int_{\omega}\Big\{\partial_{1}\Big[w\big(\partial_{22}u\partial_{1}v-\partial_{12}u\partial_{2}v\big)\Big]-\partial_{1}\big(w\partial_{22}u\big)\partial_{1}v+\partial_{1}\big(w\partial_{12}u)\partial_{2}v\Big\}dy

\displaystyle=(u,v,w)_{\partial\omega}-(u,v,w)_{\omega}.

\square

Lemma 2

Let
\omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a bounded and connected open set whose boundary is Lipschitz-continuous. For any functions
u,v,w\in H^{2}(\omega)
, define

\displaystyle{}[u,v]

\displaystyle:=\partial_{11}u\partial_{22}v+\partial_{22}u\partial_{11}v-2\partial_{12}u\partial_{12}v,

\displaystyle(u,v,w)_{\omega}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\omega}\Big[\partial_{11}u\partial_{2}v\partial_{2}w+\partial_{22}u\partial_{1}v\partial_{1}w-\partial_{12}u(\partial_{1}v\partial_{2}w+\partial_{2}v\partial_{1}w)\Big]dy.

Then

\int_{\omega}[f,f]g\,dy=-(g,f,f)_{\omega}

for all
f,g\in H^{2}(\omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\omega)
.

Proof.
For any functions
u,v,w\in{\mathcal{C}}^{2}({\overline{\omega}})
, define

\displaystyle{}(u,v,w)_{\partial\omega}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\partial\omega}\Big[\big(\partial_{11}u\partial_{2}v-\partial_{12}u\partial_{1}v\big)w\nu_{2}+\big(\partial_{22}u\partial_{1}v-\partial_{12}u\partial_{2}v\big)w\nu_{1}\Big]dy.

Given any functions
u,w\in{\mathcal{C}}^{2}({\overline{\omega}})
, Lemma
1
shows that

\displaystyle{}\int_{\omega}[u,u]wdy

\displaystyle=(u,u,w)_{\partial\omega}-(u,u,w)_{\omega},

\displaystyle\int_{\omega}[u,w]udy

\displaystyle=(u,w,u)_{\partial\omega}-(u,w,u)_{\omega},

\displaystyle\int_{\omega}[w,u]udy

\displaystyle=(w,u,u)_{\partial\omega}-(w,u,u)_{\omega}.

Moreover, an inspection of the definition of
[u,v]
and
(u,v,w)_{\omega}
shows that

[u,w]=[w,u]\text{ \ and \ }(u,u,w)_{\omega}=(u,w,u)_{\omega}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle{}\int_{\omega}[u,u]wdy

\displaystyle=(u,u,w)_{\partial\omega}-(u,w,u)_{\omega}

(37)

\displaystyle=(u,u,w)_{\partial\omega}-(u,w,u)_{\partial\omega}+\int_{\omega}[w,u]udy

\displaystyle=(u,u,w)_{\partial\omega}-(u,w,u)_{\partial\omega}+(w,u,u)_{\partial\omega}-(w,u,u)_{\omega},

for all functions
u,w\in{\mathcal{C}}^{2}({\overline{\omega}})
.

Now, let
f,g\in H^{2}(\omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\omega)
. Since the space
{\mathcal{C}}^{2}({\overline{\omega}})\cap H^{1}_{0}(\omega)
is dense in
H^{2}(\omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\omega)
, there exist sequences
(f_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
(f_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in

such that

\|f_{n}-f\|_{H^{2}(\omega)}\to 0\text{ \ and \ }\|g_{n}-g\|_{H^{2}(\omega)}\to 0\text{ \ when }n\to\infty.

Since
f_{n}=0
and

on

, the definition of
(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)_{\omega}
implies that
(f_{n},g_{n},f_{n})_{\partial\omega}=(g_{n},f_{n},f_{n})_{\partial\omega}=0
and
(f_{n},f_{n},g_{n})_{\partial\omega}=0
for all

. Then we infer from identity (

\int_{\omega}[f_{n},f_{n}]g_{n}dy=(g_{n},f_{n},f_{n})_{\omega}\text{ \ for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Since the embedding

is continuous under the assumptions of the lemma on the domain
\omega
, it is possible to pass to the limit when

the above relation to deduce that

\int_{\omega}[f,f]g\,dy=(g,f,f)_{\omega}.

\square

Lemma 3

Let
\omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a bounded and convex set. Then there exists a function
w\in H^{2}(\omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\omega)
such that the matrix field
(\partial_{\alpha\beta}w)\in L^{2}(\omega)
be positive-definite at almost all points of
\omega
.

Proof.
Let
f\in H^{1}_{0}(\omega)
be the unique solution to the equation

-\Delta f=1\text{ \ in }{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\omega).

Under the assumptions of the lemma on the domain
\omega
,
f\in H^{2}(\omega)\subset{\mathcal{C}}^{0}({\overline{\omega}})
and
f(x)>0
for all
x\in\omega
.

Define the function
w:\omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

w(y)=-\log(1+f(y))\text{ \ for all }y\in\omega.

Then
w\in{\mathcal{C}}^{0}(\omega)
and
w(y)=0
for all
y\in\partial\omega
. Besides,
w
has weak partial derivatives up to order two that satisfy

\displaystyle{}\partial_{\alpha}w

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial_{\alpha}f}{1+f}\in L^{2}(\omega),

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha\beta}w

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial_{\alpha\beta}f}{1+f}+\frac{\partial_{\alpha}f\partial_{\beta}f}{(1+f)^{2}}\in L^{2}(\omega).

Hence
u\in H^{2}(\omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\omega)
.

It remains to prove that the matrix field
(\partial_{\alpha\beta}w)
is positive-definite at almost all points of
\omega
. Since

(\partial_{\alpha\beta}w)=\frac{1}{1+f}A+\frac{1}{(1+f)^{2}}B\text{ \ a.e. in }\omega,

where
A=(A_{\alpha\beta})
and
B=(B_{\alpha\beta})
are symmetric matrix fields with entries defined by

\displaystyle{}A_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle:=-\partial_{\alpha\beta}f

\displaystyle\text{ a.e. in }\omega,

\displaystyle B_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle:=\partial_{\alpha}f\partial_{\beta}f

\displaystyle\text{ a.e. in }\omega,

it suffices to prove that
A
is positive-definite at almost all points of
\omega
and
B
is semi-positive definite at almost all points of
\omega
.

The symmetric matrix field
B
is semi-positive definite at almost all points of
\omega
since

B_{\alpha\beta}v_{\alpha}v_{\beta}=(v_{\alpha}\partial_{\alpha}f)^{2}\geq 0\text{ \ for all vector }(v_{\alpha})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}.

The symmetric matrix field
B
is positive-definite at almost all points of
\omega
since both its trace and determinant are positive at almost all points of
\omega
. For,

\displaystyle{}\operatorname{Tr}(A)

\displaystyle=-\Delta f=1>0

\displaystyle\text{ a.e. in }\omega,

\displaystyle\det(A)

\displaystyle=\det(\partial_{\alpha\beta}f)>0

\displaystyle\text{ a.e. in }\omega,

thanks to the definition of
f
as the unique solution in
H^{1}_{0}(\omega)
of the equation
-\Delta f=1
in
{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\omega)
. That the convexity of the domain
\omega
implies that
\det(\partial_{\alpha\beta}f)>0
a.e. in
\omega
has been proved by Makar-Limanov
[
7
]
.

\square

Proposition 3

Let
\omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a convex open set such that
\omega\neq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Then a function
u_{3}\in H^{1}_{0}(\omega)\cap H^{2}(\omega)
satisfies

\det(\partial_{\alpha\beta}u_{3})=0\textup{ \ in }\omega

only if

u_{3}=0\textup{ \ in }\omega.

Proof.
Let
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\omega)\cap H^{2}(\omega)
satisfy

\det(\partial_{\alpha\beta}u)=0\textup{ \ in }\omega.

Let
w\in H^{1}_{0}(\omega)\cap H^{2}(\omega)
be any function whose Hessian matrix field
(\partial_{\alpha\beta}w)\in L^{2}(\omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times 2})
is positive-definite almost everywhere in
\omega
(the existence of such a function is guaranteed by Lemma
3
).

Then Lemma
2
shows that

\int_{\omega}[u,u]w\,dy=-(w,u,u)_{\omega},

or equivalently, in view of the definition of
[\cdot,\cdot]
and
(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)_{\omega}
in the statement of Lemma
2
,

2\int_{\omega}\det(\partial_{\alpha\beta}u)w\,dy=-\int_{\omega}\Big[\partial_{11}w(\partial_{2}u)^{2}+\partial_{22}w(\partial_{1}u)^{2}-2\partial_{12}w\partial_{1}u\partial_{2}u\Big]dy.

Since
\det(\partial_{\alpha\beta}u_{3})=0
in
\omega
, we get

\int_{\omega}\Big[\partial_{11}w(\partial_{2}u)^{2}+\partial_{22}w(\partial_{1}u)^{2}-2\partial_{12}w\partial_{1}u\partial_{2}u\Big]dy=0.

But the matrix field
(\partial_{\alpha\beta}w)\in L^{2}(\omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times 2})
is positive-definite almost everywhere in
\omega
. Therefore, the vector field
(\partial_{2}u,-\partial_{1}u)
must be equal to the zero vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
almost everywhere in
\omega
. Hence the function
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\omega)
is constant, so
u=0
in
\omega
.

\square

A consequence of Proposition

, combined with Proposition

{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}})=(E_{\alpha\beta}({\boldsymbol{u}})):\omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times 2}

Note that the conclusion of the next theorem means that the set
V(\omega)
satisfies all the assumptions of Theorem

, thus ensuring the existence of minimizers for the two-dimensional model of nonlinearly elastic plates.

Theorem 3

Let
\omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a convex open set such that
\omega\neq{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Assume that the set
V(\omega)=V_{H}(\omega)\times V_{3}(\omega)
, where
V_{H}(\omega)\subset H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)
and

satisfies the following rigidity property:

\displaystyle{}u_{3}\in V_{3}(\omega)\textup{ and }\partial_{\alpha\beta}u_{3}=0\textup{ in }\omega\textup{ imply }u_{3}=0\textup{ in }\omega,

(38)

and

\displaystyle{}{\boldsymbol{u}}\in V(\omega)\textup{ and }{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}})={\boldsymbol{0}}\textup{ in }\omega\textup{ imply }{\boldsymbol{u}}={\boldsymbol{0}}\textup{ in }\omega.

(39)

Proof.
First, let
u_{3}\in V_{3}(\omega)
satisfy
\partial_{\alpha\beta}u_{3}=0
in
\omega
. Since
\omega
is connected by assumption, this implies that
u_{3}
is an affine function that vanishes on the boundary of
\omega
. Since
\omega
is also bounded by assumption, this implies that
u_{3}=0
in
\omega
.

Second, let
{\boldsymbol{u}}=(u_{i})\in V(\omega)
satisfy
{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}})={\boldsymbol{0}}

. Then Proposition

implies that

\det(\partial_{\alpha\beta}u_{3})=0\textup{ \ in }\omega.

Besides,
{\boldsymbol{u}}=(u_{i})\in V(\omega)
implies that

by the assumptions of the theorem. This means that

Then we infer from the relations

A second type of rigidity theorems

In this section, we study the rigidity of plates subjected to boundary conditions of place on its tangential displacement fields
(u_{\alpha}):\omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. We first prove that a plate is rigid if
(u_{\alpha})
vanish on the entire boundary of
\omega
as a simple consequence of the integration by parts formula. We then substantially improve this rigidity result by using the implication

\partial_{\alpha}u_{\beta}+\partial_{\beta}u_{\alpha}+\partial_{\alpha}u_{3}\partial_{\beta}u_{3}=0\text{ in }\omega\Rightarrow\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{1}u_{1}\leq 0\text{ in }\omega,\\
\partial_{2}u_{2}\leq 0\text{ in }\omega,\end{aligned}\right.

satisfied by all vector fields
(u_{i})\in H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{2}(\omega)
, then by using boundary conditions on
u_{\alpha}
, not necessarily on the entire boundary of
\omega
, to deduce that
u_{\alpha}
must vanish in
\omega
.

We begin with the simpler case where the admissible displacements fields
{\boldsymbol{u}}=(u_{i}):\omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
satisfy the boundary conditions
u_{\alpha}=0
on
\partial\omega
.

Theorem 4

Let
\omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a bounded and connected open set with Lipschitz-continuous boundary. Assume that the set
V(\omega)=V_{H}(\omega)\times V_{3}(\omega)
, where
V_{H}(\omega)\subset H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)
and
V_{3}(\omega)\subset H^{2}(\omega)
, satisfy the following two conditions:

\displaystyle{}u_{3}\in V_{3}(\omega)\textup{ and }\partial_{\alpha}u_{3}=0\textup{ in }\omega\textup{ imply }u_{3}=0\textup{ in }\omega,

(40)

and

\displaystyle{}(u_{\alpha})\in V_{H}(\omega)\textup{ implies }u_{\alpha}=0\textup{ on }\partial\omega.

(41)

Then the set
V(\omega)
satisfies the following rigidity property:

\displaystyle{}{\boldsymbol{u}}\in V(\omega)\text{ and }{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}})={\boldsymbol{0}}\text{ in }\omega\text{ implies }{\boldsymbol{u}}={\boldsymbol{0}}\text{ in }\omega.

(42)

Proof.
Let
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in V(\omega)
satisfy
{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}})={\boldsymbol{0}}
in
\omega
. Thanks to the boundary conditions (
41
) satisfied by
u_{\alpha}
, integrating this relation over
\omega
and using the integration by parts formula yields

\int_{\omega}\partial_{\alpha}u_{3}\partial_{\beta}u_{3}dy=0.

In particular then,

\int_{\omega}(\partial_{\alpha}u_{3})^{2}dy=0,

which combined with assumption (
40
) satisfied by
u_{3}
implies that

u_{3}=0\textrm{ in }\omega.

Consequently, the relation
{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}})={\boldsymbol{0}}
in
\omega
implies that

\partial_{\beta}u_{\alpha}+\partial_{\alpha}u_{\beta}=0\textrm{ in }\omega,

which in turn implies that

\partial_{\alpha\beta}u_{\sigma}=0\textrm{ in }\omega.

The set
\omega
being connected, the functions
u_{\sigma}
must be affine. Since in addition they vanish on the entire boundary of a bounded set, so necessarily at three non-collinear points, they must vanish in
\omega
:

u_{\alpha}=0\textrm{ in }\omega.

This completes the proof of the theorem.

\square

, where
V_{H}(\omega)
is any closed subspace of the space

\{(u_{\alpha})\in H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega);u_{\alpha}=0\textup{ on }\partial\omega\}

and
V_{3}(\omega)
is any closed subspace of the space
H^{2}(\omega)
whose intersection with the space of affine functions over
\omega
reduces to
\{0\}
.

In the remaining part of this section we further weaken the assumptions of Rabier’s existence theorem by showing that
u_{\alpha}
need not vanish on the entire boundary of
\omega
, but only on a part of it, for the conclusion of Corollary
2
to hold.

However, the portion of the boundary of
\omega
where
u_{\alpha}
vanish cannot be too small, as shown by the following example.
Let
\omega:=(0,1)\times(0,1)
and
\gamma_{0}:=(0,1)\times\{0\}
. Let
f\in H^{2}((0,1))
be any non-zero function that satisfies
f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=0
. Then the vector field
{\boldsymbol{u}}=(u_{i}):\omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
defined by

\displaystyle{}u_{2}(y)

\displaystyle:=0

\displaystyle\text{ for all }y\in\omega,

\displaystyle u_{3}(y)

\displaystyle:=f(y_{1})

\displaystyle\text{ for all }y=(y_{\alpha})\in\omega,

\displaystyle u_{1}(y)

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{y_{1}}(f^{\prime}(t))^{2}dt

\displaystyle\text{ for all }y=(y_{\alpha})\in\omega,

belongs to the space
V(\omega)=V_{H}(\omega)\times V_{3}(\omega)
, where

\displaystyle{}V_{H}(\omega)

\displaystyle:=\{(u_{\alpha})\in H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega);\ u_{\alpha}=0\text{ on }\gamma_{0}\},

\displaystyle V_{3}(\omega)

\displaystyle:=\{u_{3}\in H^{2}(\omega);\ u_{3}=\partial_{\nu}u_{3}=0\text{ on }\gamma_{0}\},

and satisfies

{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}})={\boldsymbol{0}}\textrm{ in }\omega,

but
{\boldsymbol{u}}
does not vanish in
\omega
.

To begin with, we study the case of a particular class of domains
\omega
, defined as follows:

Definition 1

A set
\omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is rectangle-like with respect to the vertical axis if

\displaystyle{}\omega=\{(y_{1},y_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2};\ a<y_{1}<b,\ f(y_{1})<y_{2}<g(y_{1})\}

(43)

for some real numbers
a<b
and some Lipschitz-continuous functions

f,g:[a,b]\to{\mathbb{R}}

satisfying
f(t)<g(t)
for all
t\in[a,b]
.

Remark 1

A similar definition holds for sets that are rectangle-like with respect to the horiozontal axis, in which case a theorem similar to Theorem
5
can be established by using the same argument.

The interest of Definition
1
for the purpose of this paper is that the rigidity result Theorem
4
can be improved for domains
\omega
that are rectangle-like with respect to one cartesian axis. Note that the assumption (
44
) in the next theorem means that the space
V_{3}(\omega)
does not contain non-zero constant functions.

More specifically, we have the following rigidity result:

Theorem 5

Let
\omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a rectangle-like set with respect to the vertical axis. Let
\gamma_{f}\subset\partial\omega
and
\gamma_{g}\subset\partial\omega
denote the graphs of the functions
f
and
g
appearing in the definition (
43
) of the set
\omega
. Assume that the set
V(\omega)=V_{H}(\omega)\times V_{3}(\omega)
, where
V_{H}(\omega)\subset H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)
and
V_{3}(\omega)\subset H^{2}(\omega)
, satisfy the following two conditions:

\displaystyle{}u_{3}\in V_{3}(\omega)\textup{ and }\partial_{\alpha}u_{3}=0\textup{ in }\omega\textup{ imply }u_{3}=0\textup{ in }\omega,

(44)

and

\displaystyle{}(u_{\alpha})\in V_{H}(\omega)\textup{ implies }u_{\alpha}=0\textup{ on }\gamma_{f}\cup\gamma_{g}.

(45)

Then the set
V(\omega)
satisfies the following rigidity property:

\displaystyle{}{\boldsymbol{u}}\in V(\omega)\text{ and }{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}})={\boldsymbol{0}}\text{ in }\omega\text{ implies }{\boldsymbol{u}}={\boldsymbol{0}}\text{ in }\omega.

(46)

Proof.
If a vector field
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in V(\omega)
satisfies
{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}})={\boldsymbol{0}}
in
\omega
, it satisfies in particular

\displaystyle{}\partial_{2}u_{2}=-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{1}u_{3})^{2}\leq 0\text{ \ in }\omega,

(47)

on the one hand.

On the other hand, the definition (
43
) of the set
\omega
implies that, for each
c\in(a,b)
, the intersection of the straight line
y_{1}=c
with
{\overline{\omega}}
is a line segment

\omega_{c}:=\{(c,y_{2});f(c)\leq y_{2}\leq g(c)\},

whose endpoints belong to the set
\gamma_{f}\cup\gamma_{g}
. Since
u_{2}\in H^{1}(\omega)
, a classical theorem about Sobolev spaces (see, e.g., Maz’ya
[
4
, Theorem 1, §1.1.3]
) shows that, for almost all
c\in(a,b)
, the trace of
u_{2}
on
{\omega_{c}}
is absolutely continuous and its derivative is equal to
\partial_{2}u_{2}
almost everywhere on
\omega_{c}
.

Then we infer from (
47
) and from assumptions (
45
) that

u_{2}|_{\omega_{c}}=0

for almost all
c\in(a,b)
, which in turn implies that

\displaystyle{}u_{2}=0\text{ in }\omega,

(48)

then that

\displaystyle{}(\partial_{2}u_{3})^{2}=-2\partial_{2}u_{2}=0\textrm{ in }\omega.

(49)

Next we infer from the assumption
{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}})={\boldsymbol{0}}
in
\omega
that

\partial_{2}u_{1}+\partial_{1}u_{2}+\partial_{1}u_{3}\partial_{2}u_{3}=0\textrm{ in }\omega,

which combined with (
48
) and (
49
) shows that

\partial_{2}u_{1}=0\textrm{ a.e in }\omega.

This means that
u_{1}
is constant with respect to the variable
y_{2}
, so it must vanish since its trace on
\gamma_{f}
vanishes. So

u_{1}=0\text{ in }\omega,

which combined with the relation (remember that
{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}})={\boldsymbol{0}}
in
\omega
)

E_{11}({\boldsymbol{u}}):=\partial_{1}u_{1}+\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{1}u_{3})^{2}=0\text{ in }\omega,

next implies that

\partial_{1}u_{3}=0\text{ in }\omega.

Since we also have
\partial_{2}u_{3}=0
in
\omega
(see relation (
49
) above), assumption (
44
) implies that

u_{3}=0\text{ in }\omega.

Since we already proved that
u_{1}=0
and
u_{2}=0
in
\omega
, this completes the proof of the theorem.

\square

An immediate consequence of Theorem
5
, combined with Theorem
2
, is the following existence result for partially clamped plates, the first in the literature to hold for this kind of plates without any smallness assumption on the applied forces.

Corollary 3

Let
\omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a rectangle-like set with respect to the vertical axis. Let
\gamma_{f}\subset\partial\omega
and
\gamma_{g}\subset\partial\omega
denote the graphs of the functions
f
and
g
appearing in the definition (
43
) of the set
\omega
.

Then the functional
J
defined by (
1
)-(
6
) has a minimizer in the set
V(\omega)=V_{H}(\omega)\times V_{3}(\omega)
, where

V_{H}(\omega):=\big\{(u_{\alpha})\in H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega);\ u_{\alpha}=0\textup{ on }\gamma_{f}\cup\gamma_{g}\big\}

and
V_{3}(\omega)
is any closed subspace of
H^{2}(\omega)
whose intersection with the space of affine functions over
\omega
reduces to
\{0\}
.

We conclude this section by generalizing the rigidity result of Theorem
5
. Note that the assumption (
50
) in the next theorem means that the space
V_{3}(\omega)
does not contain non-zero constant functions, while the assumption (
51
) means that the elements of the space
V_{H}(\omega)
vanish at all points of the boundary where the tangent exists and is not parallel to the given vector
(e_{\beta})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. The typical example is the rectangle-like set of Theorem
5
, for which
(e_{\beta})=(0,1)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

Theorem 6

Let
\omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a bounded and connected open subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
whose boundary is Lipschitz-continuous. Assume that the set
V(\omega)=V_{H}(\omega)\times V_{3}(\omega)
, where
V_{H}(\omega)\subset H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega)
and
V_{3}(\omega)\subset H^{2}(\omega)
, satisfy the following two conditions:

\displaystyle{}u_{3}\in V_{3}(\omega)\textup{ and }\partial_{\alpha}u_{3}=0\textup{ in }\omega\textup{ imply }u_{3}=0\textup{ in }\omega,

(50)

and the exists a non-zero vector
(e_{\beta})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
such that

\displaystyle{}(u_{\alpha})\in V_{H}(\omega)\textup{ implies }u_{\alpha}(\nu_{\beta}e_{\beta})=0\textup{ on }\partial\omega.

(51)

Then the set
V(\omega)
satisfies the following rigidity property:

\displaystyle{}{\boldsymbol{u}}\in V(\omega)\text{ and }{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}})={\boldsymbol{0}}\text{ in }\omega\text{ implies }{\boldsymbol{u}}={\boldsymbol{0}}\text{ in }\omega.

(52)

Proof.
Without losing in generality we may assume that
(e_{\beta})=(1,0)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, in which case assumption (
51
) becomes

\displaystyle{}(u_{\alpha})\in V_{H}(\omega)\textup{ implies }u_{\alpha}\nu_{1}=0\textup{ on }\partial\omega.

(53)

Let
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in V(\omega)
be a vector field that satisfies the matrix equation
{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{u}})={\boldsymbol{0}}
in
\omega
. Then

\displaystyle{}\partial_{1}u_{1}+\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{1}u_{3})^{2}

\displaystyle=0\text{ \ in }\omega,

\displaystyle\partial_{1}u_{2}+\partial_{2}u_{1}+\partial_{1}u_{3}\partial_{2}u_{3}

\displaystyle=0\text{ \ in }\omega,

\displaystyle\partial_{2}u_{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{2}u_{3})^{2}

\displaystyle=0\text{ \ in }\omega.

Integrating the first equation over
\omega
and using the integration by parts formula in conjunction with assumption (
53
) satisfied by the trace of
u_{1}
on the boundary
\gamma:=\partial\omega
of
\omega
implies that

\displaystyle{}\int_{\omega}(\partial_{1}u_{3})^{2}dy=-\int_{\partial\omega}u_{1}\nu_{1}d\gamma=0,

then that

\displaystyle{}\partial_{1}u_{3}

\displaystyle=0\text{ \ in }\omega.

(54)

Therefore, the previous system becomes

\displaystyle{}\partial_{1}u_{1}

\displaystyle=0\text{ \ in }\omega,

(55)

\displaystyle\partial_{1}u_{2}+\partial_{2}u_{1}

\displaystyle=0\text{ \ in }\omega,

\displaystyle\partial_{2}u_{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{2}u_{3})^{2}

\displaystyle=0\text{ \ in }\omega.

From these equations, we next deduce that

\displaystyle\partial_{1}(\partial_{1}u_{2})=-\partial_{1}(\partial_{2}u_{1})=-\partial_{2}(\partial_{1}u_{1})=0\text{ in }{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\omega),

\displaystyle\partial_{2}(\partial_{1}u_{2})=\partial_{1}(\partial_{2}u_{2})=-\partial_{2}u_{3}\partial_{1}(\partial_{2}u_{3})=-\partial_{2}u_{3}\partial_{2}(\partial_{1}u_{3})=0\text{ in }{\mathcal{D}}^{\prime}(\omega),

so that, since
\omega
is connected, there exists a constant
A\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\displaystyle{}\partial_{1}u_{2}=A\text{ in }\omega.

(56)

Then we infer from system (
55
) that

\displaystyle\partial_{1}u_{1}=0

\displaystyle\text{ in }\omega,

\displaystyle\partial_{2}u_{1}=-A

\displaystyle\text{ in }\omega,

which combined with the assumption that
\omega
is connected implies that the function
u_{1}
is affine; so there exists a constant
B\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\displaystyle{}u_{1}(y)=-Ay_{2}+B\text{ for all }y=(y_{\alpha})\in\omega.

(57)

Combined with assumption (
53
), the above relation implies that

\displaystyle{}-Ay_{2}+B=0\text{ for all }y_{2}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{2},

(58)

where
{\mathcal{E}}_{2}
is the projection on the vertical axis of the portion
of the boundary of
\omega
where the unit outer normal vector
\nu=(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})
is well defined and satisfies
\nu_{1}\neq 0
, that is

{\mathcal{E}}_{2}:=\{y_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}};\ \exists y_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}},(y_{1},y_{2})\in\partial^{\sharp}\omega\text{ and }\nu_{1}(y_{1},y_{2})\neq 0\},

where

\displaystyle{}\partial^{\sharp}\omega:=\{y\in\partial\omega;\ \nu(y)\text{ is well defined}\}.

Note that the boundary
\gamma:=\partial\omega
of
\omega
is Lipschitz-continuous by the assumptions of the theorem, so it has a unit outer normal vector at almost all points of
\partial\omega
. Thus the set
(\partial\omega\setminus\partial^{\sharp}\omega)
is
d\gamma
-negligible. Since the set
\omega
is also bounded by the assumptions of the theorem, this implies that the set
{\mathcal{E}}_{2}
cannot be negligible (as a subset of
{\mathbb{R}}
), otherwise the boundary of
\omega
would be contained in a straight line, which is impossible since
\omega
is bounded. Hence relation (
58
) implies that
A=B=0
, so that

u_{1}=0\text{ in }\omega

and

\partial_{1}u_{2}=0\text{ in }\omega.

Next, using this last relation and the integration by parts formula in conjunction with assumption (
53
) satisfied by the trace of
u_{2}
on the boundary of
\omega
, we deduce that

\displaystyle{}\int_{\omega}(u_{2}(y))^{2}dy=\int_{\omega}u_{2}(y)\partial_{1}\big(y_{1}u_{2}(y)\big)dy=\int_{\partial\omega}(u_{2}\nu_{1})(y)y_{1}u_{2}(y)\gamma=0.

Hence

\displaystyle{}u_{2}

\displaystyle=0\text{ \ in }\omega.

(59)

Then the last equation of system (
55
) implies that

\displaystyle{}\partial_{2}u_{3}

\displaystyle=0\text{ \ in }\omega,

(60)

which combined with relation (
54
) and with assumption (
50
) of the theorem implies that

u_{3}=0\text{ \ in }\omega.

Since we already proved that
u_{1}=0
and
u_{2}=0
in
\omega
, this completes the proof of the theorem.

\square

We conclude this section and paper by stating the existence result for the Kirchhoff-Love nonlinear plate model obtained by using the rigidity result of Theorem
6
to replace the assumptions of the existence result of Theorem
2
:

Corollary 4

Let
\omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a bounded and connected open subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
whose boundary is Lipschitz-continuous, let
(\nu_{\alpha})\in L^{\infty}(\partial\omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
denote unit outer normal vector field to the boundary of
\omega
, let
(e_{\alpha})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be any vector, and let

\gamma_{0}:=\{y\in\partial\omega;\ \nu_{\alpha}(y)e_{\alpha}\neq 0\}.

Then the functional
J
defined by (
1
)-(
6
) has a minimizer in the set
V(\omega)=V_{H}(\omega)\times V_{3}(\omega)
, where

V_{H}(\omega):=\big\{(u_{\alpha})\in H^{1}(\omega)\times H^{1}(\omega);\ u_{\alpha}=0\textup{ on }\gamma_{0}\big\}

and
V_{3}(\omega)
is any closed subspace of
H^{2}(\omega)
whose intersection with the space of affine functions over
\omega
reduces to
\{0\}
.
