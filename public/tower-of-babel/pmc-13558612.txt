Piezo channels contribute to the terminal cytodifferentiation of odontoblasts via modulation of canonical Wnt signaling
Authors: Xuguang Nie, Kelley Huang, Emily Chu, Man-Kyo Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Piezo1 and Piezo2 are mechanosensitive ion channels that transduce mechanical stimuli into intracellular biochemical signals in diverse tissues and organ systems. Their functions in odontoblast development are still not well understood. In this study, we showed that Piezo1 and Piezo2 displayed dynamic expression in odontoblasts during terminal cytodifferentiation, suggesting a functional role in this process. However, Piezo1 knockouts in odontoblasts using Wnt1-Cre (Piezo1 cKO) did not cause pronounced structural change in tooth development in a majority of the Piezo1 cKO mice. Piezo2 cKO did not show obvious tooth defect either. Therefore, we pursued a double knockout approach. A phenotype analysis of Wnt1-Cre mediated Piezo1 and Piezo2 double knockout (Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO) mice revealed impaired cytodifferentiation and morphological change in the odontoblasts. Specifically, the heights of odontoblast and ameloblast layers were lower in Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice than controls, and expression of markers for odontoblast terminal differentiation, including collagen type 1a and alkaline phosphatase, was delayed. Furthermore, levels of nuclear β-catenin and Wnt10a were remarkably decreased, indicative of compromised Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Notably, the application of the Wnt10a protein rescued the odontoblast phenotype of Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO teeth in an ex vivo organ culture system. Moreover, the intraperitoneal injection of Yoda1, a Piezo1 channel agonist, facilitated odontoblast cytodifferentiation and elevated Wnt/β-catenin signaling in dental pulp tissues at the newborn stage. Altogether, these data suggest that Piezo channels play a role in regulating the terminal differentiation of odontoblasts via modulation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling.

Introduction

Piezo channels are mechanosensory molecules that sense mechanical signals from the environment or within cellular niches, enabling various tissue types to adapt to diverse biological and environmental conditions.1–3 In vertebrates, the Piezo family currently has two members, Piezo1 and Piezo2, which share over 40% sequence homology. Piezo1 has a broader expression in a wide range of tissues in a variety of organ systems and exerts critical functions in diverse biological processes.3–7 In contrast, the prominent role of Piezo2 channels is found in the neural tissues conveying mechanosensory and somatosensory signals to the central nervous system.3–5,7–9

Crucial functions of Piezo channels have been revealed in a variety of tissues and organs using genetic models. Global knockouts (KOs) of Piezo1 or Piezo2 lead to lethal conditions due to cardiovascular defects and respiratory failure, respectively, demonstrating their crucial functions and also preventing further analysis at more advanced stages.9,10 The conditional gene knockout approach (cKO) has greatly extended our understanding of the functions of Piezo channels in developmental and pathophysiological conditions in a variety of organ systems, including mineralized tissues.6,11–15 For skeletal system development, Piezo1 displays more critical functions than Piezo2, and the deletion of Piezo1 leads to the malformation of long bones and spontaneous fractures in postnatal period.4,11,13

Teeth are masticatory organs with specific mineralized tissues. However, the functional role of Piezo channels in tooth development remains poorly understood. Tooth development is hallmarked by reciprocal epithelial- mesenchymal interactions and a complex temporal-spatial patterning.16,17 Primordial tooth germs, initially two-layer structures, undergo a series of morphological transformations known as the initiation, bud, cap and bell stages, and finally develop into a mature tooth covered with mineralized tissues. During these processes, the epithelial compartment forms the enamel organ of the tooth germ; while the mesenchyme compartment, derived from neural crest cells (NCCs), forms dental pulp tissues. The outermost-layer cells of the dental pulp, directly interfacing and guided by the epithelial compartment, elongate from cuboidal to columnar polarized cells and differentiate into odontoblasts. Once terminally differentiated, odontoblasts begin to lay down matrix for dentin formation. Meanwhile, the cells of the inner enamel epithelia, interfacing with the odontoblast layer, elongate to become ameloblasts and deposit matrix for enamel formation. These delicate developmental processes are driven by a complex genetic network that has been continuously investigated.

Apart from intrinsic genetic control, tooth development and maintenance are vigorously influenced by biomechanical stimuli.17,18 Piezo1 and Piezo2 are mechanical sensors that have both been described in mature odontoblasts and implicated in the regulatory machinery for postnatal tooth development.19,20 However, little is known about their roles in the cytodifferentiation of odontoblasts, a process whereby pre-odontoblasts became specialized dentin-forming cells. In this study, we explored the roles of Piezo channels using cKO models and pharmacological approaches and demonstrated that Piezo1 and Piezo2 collectively contribute to terminal differentiation of odontoblasts by modulating the canonical Wnt signal pathway.

Results

Robust expression of Piezo1 and Piezo2 during odontoblast terminal differentiation and dentin formation

We first examined Piezo1 and Piezo2 expression pattern in various stages of tooth development with RNAscope and antibody assays. In our previous study, we observed that Piezo1 and Piezo2 were barely or not at all detectable at the bud and cap stages by an antibody staining.6 Here, we performed RNAscope staining at perinatal stages. At the late bell stage (E18.5), we noticed weak Piezo1 expression in pre-odontoblasts and dental pulp cells (Fig. 1a–c). Expression was also seen in the pre-ameloblast layer, suggesting a role in ameloblast development (Fig. 1a–c). Striking expression of Piezo1 transcripts was observed in the odontoblast layer at the newborn stage, the time point for odontoblast terminal cytodifferentiation (Fig. 1d–f). Similarly, Piezo2 transcripts were also observed in pre-odontoblasts and odontoblasts with RNAscope staining during this period but at a relatively low level compared to Piezo1 (Fig. 1a–f). By contrast, Piezo1 and Piezo2 transcripts in Wnt1-Cre2; Piezo1loxp/loxp; Piezo2loxp/loxp double cKO (hereafter Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO) mice were substantially reduced in the odontoblast layer, suggesting a successful deletion of Piezo1/Piezo2 (Fig. 1g–i).

Altogether, dynamic expressions of Piezo channels suggest their potential roles for odontoblast differentiation and dentin formation.

Tooth development in Piezo1 cKO and Piezo2 cKO mice

To determine whether there was functional role of Piezo channels, we first examined Piezo1 cKO mice and Piezo2 cKO mice mediated by Wnt1-Cre2.6,21 As described in our recent study, the vast majority of Piezo1 cKO mice are fertile and indistinguishable from their littermates.6 Tooth development is virtually normal in Piezo1 cKO mice (Fig. S2).6 Piezo2 cKO mice died at birth due to respiratory distress, as had been previous reported,9 we did not detect any obvious change in tooth development in them (Fig. S3). Altogether, a single cKO of Piezo1 or Piezo2 in NCCs did not induce obvious defects in tooth development up to the newborn stage.

Piezo1 and Piezo2 double knockout impaired odontoblast cytodifferentiation

As functional synergy and redundancy between Piezo1 and Piezo2 exist in a variety of organ systems, we examined Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice. At E18.5 or earlier, we did not detect any apparent structural changes in tooth development (Fig. S4). Phospho-Histone H3 (PHH3) staining and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) showed no significant difference between control and Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice, suggesting that cell proliferation and mortality were not affected (Fig. S4). Overall, tooth development at the embryonic stages was virtually comparable between Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO and controls until the late bell stage.

Remarkably, at the newborn stage (P0), reduced heights of odontoblast and ameloblast layers in Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice became obvious (Fig. 2a–f, i and Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Expressions of markers of odontoblast differentiation, including dentin sialophosphoprotein (Dspp) (Fig. 2g–i), alkaline phosphatase (Alpl) (Fig. 2j–m), and collagen type 1 (Col1a1) (Fig. 2n–q), were reduced in Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice. Impaired Wnt signaling during cytodifferentiation in Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice

Wnt signaling regulates multiple processes throughout tooth development, and a solid link between Piezo1 activity and Wnt signaling has been established in multiple contexts.6,22,23 Wnt10a and β-catenin are the principal ligand and intracellular mediator, respectively, of the canonical Wnt signaling pathway during odontoblast differentiation.22–24 Therefore, we analyzed the expression of Wnt10a with RNAscope and an active form of β-catenin with a PY489 antibody.6,25 We examined E19.5 and P0 stages, the critical time points during the process of odontoblast terminal differentiation (Fig. 4a–i). We consistently detected decreases of β-catenin-pY489 and Wnt10a levels in Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice at E19.5 (Fig. 4a–f). Furthermore, expression levels of Axin2, a downstream target of β-catenin and negative feedback regulator of Wnt signaling, were also lower in Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice than controls at a stage in which Axin2 displays significant upregulation in odontoblasts26,27 (Fig. 4g–i). Restoration of Wnt signaling rescued tooth development in Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice

To further test the idea that Wnt/β-catenin signaling acted as a downstream target of Piezo signaling, we conducted an ex vivo experiment to determine whether exogenous supplementation of the Wnt10a protein could restore tooth development in Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice in an organ culture system. We started the culture from E14.5, when tooth germs are at their early cap-stage (Fig. 5a, b). After eight days in culture, tooth development proceeded to the early bell stage, and Wnt10a or vehicle was applied to the culture media (Fig. 5b–d). Consistent with in vivo data, the heights of the odontoblast and ameloblast layers of tooth explants were shorter in Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice than controls (Fig. 5e, f, h, i, k). Notably, the heights of odontoblast and ameloblast layers in Wnt10a-treated Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO tooth germs became comparable to controls (Fig. 5e–k). Supplementation of the Wnt10a peptides elevated β-catenin-pY489 to a normal level in Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO samples (Fig. 6a–c, g). Col1a1 expression was also restored (Fig. 6d–g). Yoda1 upregulated Wnt signaling in developing teeth

To further confirm the linkage of Piezo channels and Wnt signaling in developing teeth, we pharmacologically activated Piezo1 channels via the intraperitoneal application of the Piezo1 agonist Yoda1 in pregnant C57bl/6 mice (Fig. 7a). At the P0 stage, odontoblasts exhibited advanced differentiation in the cusp tip regions, characterized by conspicuous polarization and elongation (Fig. 7b, d). In comparison, Yoda1 induced synchronous odontoblast differentiation throughout the odontoblast layer compared to controls (Fig. 7c, e). Aside from the cusp tips, odontoblasts in other regions were also well pronounced and aligned in Yoda1-treated mice (Fig. 7c, e). Col1a1 staining demonstrated enhanced matrix deposition (Fig. 7f, g, l). We also assessed the activation of Wnt signaling after Yoda1 application. We detected remarkable upregulation of Wnt10a (Fig. 7h, i, l). Yoda1 also significantly increased β-catenin-pY489 in the odontoblast layer (Fig. 7j–l). Discussion

Functional roles of mechanosensitive ion channels Piezo1 and Piezo2 for tooth and its associated structures are being rapidly revealed in recent few years.6,28–32 However, their functions in odontoblast development remains elusive. Although multiple lines of evidence support a regulatory role of Piezo channels for odontoblasts, tooth defects in Piezo1 and Piezo2 cKO mice were not evident.6,19,30 Functional synergy and redundancy among Piezo1 and Piezo2 have been documented in diverse tissues and organ systems and may also exist in odontoblasts.6,11,13,33,34 Therefore, it is unsurprising that there are no pronounced structural changes or incomplete phenotype penetrance in the majority of Piezo1 cKO mice. Hence, the double cKO approach serves as a powerful tool to address the developmental roles of Piezo channels. Several lines of Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice have been described in the skeletal system, all exhibiting more striking phenotypes than single cKO mice.6,11,13 Unfortunately, these Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice died prematurely, and tooth analysis has not yet been available.6,11,13

In this study, we exploited our Wnt1-Cre2 driven Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice to investigate the role of Piezo channels for odontoblast development.6 We demonstrated that the deficiency of both Piezo1 and Piezo2 attenuated cytodifferentiation of odontoblasts and led to a reduced and disorganized matrix deposition. Furthermore, pharmacological approach with Yoda1 facilitated odontoblasts differentiation and dentin formation. Although off-target effects cannot be excluded, our interpretation is based on its consistency with that of genetic models.

It is intriguing that there is no striking phenotype in early tooth development in Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice, given that biomechanical forces should play a crucial role in shaping tooth morphology in the early stages, particularly during the process of vigorous cell proliferation and migration.18 It is reasonable to assume that other mechanical sensors may also be in play for tooth morphogenesis. It has been reported that transient receptor potential ion channels, such as TRPV4, Trek1 and Pkd1/Pkd2 can all serve as mechanosensitive channels, therefore, partial functional redundancy might exist among these channels.35 Considering that structural changes of tooth development in Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice were frequently detected at the newborn stage, we suspected that subtle changes might manifest during an early time point. Our data supports that the earliest time point for the critical requirement of Piezo function may be at the late bell stage, when terminal cytodifferentiation of odontoblasts is initiated.

The mechanism underlying how biomechanical signaling regulates the cytodifferentiation of odontoblasts remains to be determined, and the role of Piezo channels for odontoblast differentiation and activity has not been established with certainty. Recently, downstream targets of Piezo channels have been revealed in multiple contexts through molecular and genomic approaches. Piezo activity regulates the expression of numerous molecules and affects multiple signal pathways.11,12,36 Among these are fundamental signal pathways, including the mTOR, Yap, and Wnt pathways.13,36 Of particular importance, a link between Piezo activity and Wnt signaling has been established in a variety of processes.6,13,36,37 It is reported that Ca2+ influx caused by Piezo channel activation, together in conjunction with calmodulin, activates CaMKII, which in turn promotes the nuclear translocation of β-catenin to regulate its transcriptional activity for target genes.38 Furthermore, Piezo channels can regulate Wnt ligand expression.39 Our data point to a link of Piezo channel functions with Wnt10a/β-catenin cascade in odontoblast differentiation.

Wnt signaling critically regulates tooth development throughout all stages, controlling cell fate determination, proliferation, polarization, differentiation, and function.17,22,40 Wnt10a is the principal ligand expressed in pre-odontoblasts during their terminal differentiation and throughout the whole process of dentin formation, and it can regulate the expression of Dspp, a critical molecule for dentinogenesis.22 Thus, it is reasonable to postulate that Wnt10a regulates terminal differentiation and function of the odontoblasts mainly via an autocrine mechanism.22,23,41 Wnt10a mutations or Wnt signaling malfunctions cause tooth defects, including cusp and root malformation, highlighting its crucial role in patterning tooth development.23,42,43

Significantly, we demonstrated that Wnt signaling is compromised during the terminal differentiation of odontoblasts in Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice, and exogeneous Wnt10a restored odontoblast differentiation in the teeth of Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice in ex vivo culture. This result supports our idea that Piezo channels regulate odontoblast differentiation principally by modulating Wnt signaling. Even so, our data does not establish that Piezo channels act as direct upstream regulators of Wnt signaling. Alternatively, Piezo channels may function in a parallel pathway that impacts Wnt signaling. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanism of the link of Piezo channels with Wnt signaling remains to be determined.

It should be noted that Piezo1/Piezo2 deletion neither arrested odontoblast differentiation nor did it abolish dentin formation, as indicated by the presence of matrix in the late newborn stage teeth of Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice. Rather, deficiency of Piezo channels failed to boost odontoblast cytodifferentiation at a critical time point, which may lead to odontoblast hypoplasia and render tooth development more susceptible to defective processes. It would be interesting to test if postnatal regaining Piezo channel function can restore normal tooth development. The lethality of the dKO mice prevents us from further experimental determination of sufficiency of Piezo channels in postnatal stages. New model systems that can survive throughout the tooth development are needed for future studies about the roles of Piezo channels in postnatal period.

Of note, the upregulation of Wnt signaling inhibitor Dkk1, Sclerostin, and persistent expression of negative feedback regulator Axin2 in well-differentiated odontoblasts suggest that fine-tuning of Wnt signaling is required after terminal differentiation.27,41 In support of this idea, we observed reduced β-catenin-pY489 levels in secreting odontoblasts compared to still differentiating odontoblasts. This data indicate that Wnt signaling is strongly required for terminal cytodifferentiation of pre-odontoblasts, whereas it is modestly required for matured odontoblasts. Nevertheless, maintenance of a proper level of Wnt signaling appears critical for terminal differentiation and proper function.

Dentin malformations or inherited dentin defects are, in the vast majority of cases, genetic disorders that affect the structure of tooth dentin, exemplified by dentin dysplasia or dentinogenesis imperfecta caused by mutations of COL1A1 and DSPP, respectively. It should be emphasized that odontoblast development and function are governed by a complex and delicate regulatory network that involves a myriad of genes, and the malfunction of any critical gene might affect dentin formation. This explains uncategorized cases of dentin malformations. Currently, Piezo gene mutations have been detected in an increasing number of genetic disorders, and their implications in other diseases are being rigorously investigated.36,44,45 Our data highlights the potentially crucial roles of Piezo channels in the genetic network controlling odontoblast development and function.

Finally, it is worth mentioning that use of Wnt1-Cre2 transgene line is not without limitations. However, this new Cre line significantly overcomes aberrant activation of Wnt signaling, while faithful and solid genetic recombination in neural crest cells remained consistent in recent studies.46 Furthermore, absence of any phenotype in Piezo1 and Piezo2 heterozygous cKO supports that Wnt1-Cre2 does not contribute significantly to the phenotype we observed in double cKO mice. Nevertheless, although the effect of potential ectopically expressed Cre transgene cannot be excluded, its contribution is likely minimal and does not affect data interpretation. Additionally, Piezo1 and Piezo2 transcripts are both seen in pre-ameloblasts and may play a role for enamel organ development. Since Wnt1-Cre2 does not target oral epithelial layers, Piezo1 and Piezo2 channels should function normally in dental epithelial compartment of Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO, and therefore defects seen in ameloblasts of dKO are likely secondary to impaired odontoblast development. An approach using epithelial Cre lines will be insightful to address potential cell-autonomous role of Piezo channels for enamel organ development in future studies.

In conclusion, we demonstrated that signaling mediated by Piezo channels participates in odontoblast development. Disruption of Piezo1/Piezo2 genes impairs the terminal cytodifferentiation of odontoblasts and dentin formation, whereas pharmaceutical activation of Piezo1 channels facilitates cytodifferentiation and dentin formation. Our data further indicate that Piezo channels contribute to odontoblast differentiation by modulation of Wnt signaling. Altogether, these lines of evidence firmly establish the functional roles of Piezo channels for odontoblast development.

Materials and methods

Animals

Animal procedures were conducted according to the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Maryland Baltimore. Wnt1-Cre2 (B6.Cg-E2f1Tg(Wnt1-cre)2Sor/J, Stock No. 022501), Piezo1loxp/loxp (B6.Cg-Piezo1tm2.1Apat/J, Stock No. 029213), Piezo2loxp/loxp (B6(SJL)-Piezo2tm2.2Apat/J, Stock No. 027720), and C57BL/6 were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory.46–48 Mouse colonies were maintained in the School of Dentistry animal facility at University of Maryland Baltimore. Genotyping was performed using regular PCR. Cre-negative mice were used as controls. Piezo1 cKO colony was maintained by breeding Wnt1-Cre2; Piezo1 loxp/loxp mice with Piezo1 loxp/loxp mice. To generate double cKO mice, Wnt1-Cre2; Piezo1 loxp/loxp; Piezo2 loxp/+ compound mice were generated to breed with Piezo1 loxp/loxp; Piezo2 loxp/loxp mice. Wnt1-Cre2; Piezo1 loxp/+, Wnt1-Cre2; Piezo2 loxp/+, and Wnt1-Cre2; Piezo1 loxp/+; Piezo2 loxp/+ heterozygous cKO mice were generated for breeding purposes and are healthy and fertile. Embryonic stages were determined by days after detection of virginal plug and confirmed with morphology. The numbers of controls, Piezo2 cKO, Piezo1/Piezo2 dKO mice, and C57BL/6 in each stage are shown in Supplementary Tables 3–6.

Tissue processing and hematoxylin & eosin staining

All the experiments were performed on tissues of the mandibular 1st molar unless otherwise described. Jawbone tissues were collected and fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C, followed by decalcification in 0.5 mol/L EDTA at room temperature. Decalcified tissues were further processed for paraffin embedding. Frontal sections of 7 µm thickness were used unless otherwise described. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed following a standard protocol. Digital images were obtained using Leica and Olympus systems, and viewed and analyzed using Aperio Imagescope 12.4.6 (Leica Biosystems, Deer Park, IL, USA) and Olyvia 4.1.1 (Olympus, League City, TX, USA) software, respectively.

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin sections of 5 µm thickness were used. Heat-induced antigen retrieval was performed (Vector, H-3301). Antibodies for Alpl (Invitrogen, 702454), β-catenin-pY489 (Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank [DSHB], PY489), Col1a1 (Thermo Fisher, PA29569), Dspp (Thermo Fisher, PA5-76382), Neurofilament (DSHB, 2H3), PHH3 (Millipore, 06-570), Axin2 (Sigma, 61631302), Piezo1 (Invitrogen, MA5-32876) and Piezo2 (MilliporeSigma, 587474) were used with dilutions recommended by the manufacturers. Primary antibodies were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Secondary antibodies were applied at room temperature for 1.5 h. Alexa Fluor 488 and Alexa Fluor 555 secondary antibodies were used at 1/500 for signal detection (Thermo Fisher), and DAPI was used for nuclear staining (Thermo Fisher). Mounted sections were imaged and processed with Zeiss Axiovert 200 M (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany) and Nikon W1 spinning disc confocal microscopes (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

RNAscope assay

Paraffin sections were prepared with RNAnase free procedures. RNAnase free reagents were used whenever applicable. The RNAscope assay was performed using the RNAscope Fluorescent Multiplex Assays system following the manufacturer’s protocol (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Newark, CA, USA). Mouse Piezo1 (C1 probe, 400181), Piezo2 (C2 probe, 1179561), and Wnt10a (C1 probe, 401061) probes were used (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Newark, CA, USA). RNAscope labelling of Wnt10a was followed by immunohistochemical labeling using the antibody against Col1a1. The Opal system was used for signal detection for RNAscope procedures; Alexa Fluor was used for immunofluorescence.12

TUNEL for apoptosis

TUNEL was performed on paraffin sections using the DeadEnd Fluorometric TUNEL System, following the manufacturer’s protocol (Promega, Madison, WI, USA, G3250).

Tooth germ ex vivo cultures

Embryos at the E14.5 stage were collected from timely pregnant mice. Mandibular 1st molar germs were dissected out, transferred into a Corning Transwell system (MilliporeSigma, TMO140644), and cultured in 1X DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin, with and without Wnt10a (Proteintech, Ag23963). Recombinant Wnt10a was reconstituted in molecular grade water and glycerol (1:1) to a concentration of 100 µg/mL and used at concentration of 100 ng/mL which in previous reports is the most commonly used concentration of Wnt ligands in tissue culture.28 Cultured tooth germs were then harvested at a desirable time point and processed for histological examination.

Yoda1 injection

Yoda1 dosage was determined based on a previous study.49 Yoda1 (MilliporeSigma, SML1558) was dissolved in DMSO to make a 10 mg/mL stock solution. It was further diluted 1:50 with saline solutions before use. Intraperitoneal injections were performed twice a day in the early morning and later afternoon at a dose of 1 mg/kg on E18.5 and E19.5 pregnant C57BL/6 mice (3 mice for vehicle, 3 mice for Yoda1). Newborn mice were collected and analyzed. Stage-matched newborn C57bl/6 mice were used as controls.

Image analysis

Representative and comparable sections of each genotype were carefully selected for comparison. Measurement of dentin thickness was performed on the point of cementoenamel junction, and the maximal value was used to represent dentin thickness for each sample. Ameloblast and odontoblast layer heights were measured at the cusp tip.

Fluorescence signals were measured by “Analyze Particles” in the Region of Interest (ROI) using Image J software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). ROI was placed on the odontoblast layer of tooth cusps unless otherwise described. For β-catenin, the detected areas relative to the nuclei areas were calculated. Area fractions were used for quantification. Mitosis and TUNEL were quantified by positive cell percentage in the ROI.

Micro-CT examination

Mouse jawbones were dissected out and fixed with 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C, followed by dehydration with a series of graded ethanol solutions, and then subjected to µCT scanning using a µCT 45 (Scanco Medical, Bassersdorf, Switzerland). DICOM files were viewed and analyzed using Analyze 14 software (AnalyzeDirect, Overland Park, KS, USA). Dentin thickness was measured using 2-D sagittal slices. The dentin thickness of the mesial root of mandibular 1st molar at cementoenamel junction was used to represent dentin thickness for each independent sample.

Statistical analysis

All data were generated from a minimum of three independent biological replicates with a minimum of three technical replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (Dotmatics, Boston, MA, USA). All data were presented as mean ± SD. The data were analyzed using two-sided unpaired Student’s t-tests between two groups and using one-way ANOVA with Turkey’s post–hoc analysis for three groups. All data followed a normal distribution with equal variances. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The methods of statistical analysis and N numbers of the groups are indicated in the legend of each figure.
