Passivity-exploiting stabilization of semilinear single-track vehicle models with distributed tire friction dynamics
Luigi Romano, Ole Morten Aamo, Miroslav Krstić, Jan Aslund, Erik Frisk

Abstract




1
Introduction

The safe and stable operation of road vehicles has long been a central focus in automotive engineering. Loss of stability during high-speed maneuvers, such as sharp cornering, lane changes, or evasive braking, can severely compromise vehicle safety and performance. In particular, lateral instabilities such as oversteer or oscillatory yaw motions are critical risk factors in accident scenarios. For this reason, modern automotive control systems – ranging from electronic stability control (ESC) to advanced driver-assistance systems (ADAS) – rely on robust stabilization strategies that ensure predictable and reliable handling characteristics
(
26
;
48
)
.

A key determinant of vehicle stability lies in the interaction between the tire and the road surface. Rolling contact phenomena occurring inside the tire’s contact patch govern the transmission of lateral and longitudinal forces, directly influencing the vehicle’s ability to accelerate, decelerate, and follow desired paths
(
39
;
30
)
. Traditional vehicle models, such as the classic
single-track
representation
(
30
;
22
)
, approximate these forces using lumped descriptions, in which the tire forces are modeled as static nonlinearities. Whilst such simplifications have been instrumental for controller design and industry adoption
(
47
;
20
;
54
;
29
;
53
)
, they fail to capture the inherently distributed nature of the tire-road interaction. In fact, as extensively discussed in
45
;
43
;
44
;
46
;
9
;
8
;
35
, both theoretical studies and experimental evidence have demonstrated that the distributed dynamics of the tire is responsible for introducing delays, memory effects, and nonlinear behaviors that cannot be adequately captured by lumped descriptions.

Distributed tire friction models, such as the Dahl and LuGre formulations developed in
13
;
12
;
49
;
19
;
17
;
18
, address this limitation by representing tire forces as the outcome of spatially distributed dynamics of bristle-like elements. In these models, hyperbolic partial differential equations (PDEs) describe the evolution of internal deflections or state variables across the contact patch, which are then integrated to yield the net contact forces acting on the vehicle. As a result, when such models are coupled with the rigid-body equations for the lateral vehicle motion, the overall dynamics are described by an ODE-PDE interconnection. This structure offers a far more accurate and predictive description of the tire-vehicle behavior, particularly under transient conditions such as sudden steering inputs or road disturbances. However, it also complicates the design of stabilizing controllers, since the resulting system is infinite-dimensional and nonlinear. In previous works, these drawbacks have been partly overcome by replacing the PDE dynamics with lumped approximations, enabling the design of control strategies for traction and braking
(
52
;
4
;
11
;
51
)
. However, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, with the unique exception of
37
, the development of rigorous stabilization algorithms for nonlinear vehicle models with distributed tire friction, similar to those introduced in
38
, remains unexplored.

In this context, the synthesis of control and estimation algorithms considering infinite-dimensional vehicle models becomes an extremely appealing topic for automotive research, especially in light of the recent advancements in PDE control. Indeed, over the last two decades, systematic tools have been developed for the stabilization of hyperbolic PDEs and ODE-PDE interconnections. For systems with bounded control operators, early contributions employed linear-quadratic (LQ) optimal control strategies
(
3
;
28
)
, also based on the classic results contained in
50
;
16
. For problems with unbounded input and/or measurements, the backstepping method has proven to be a versatile and powerful approach, leading to a proliferation of contributions concerning observer and controller design
(
41
;
42
;
25
;
24
;
14
)
, and becoming the dominant methodology for boundary-actuated PDE and ODE-PDE systems. Over the years, PDE backstepping has been successfully extended to observer design, parameter estimation, and the stabilization of systems with time-varying delays or cascaded PDE structures, such as those considered in
5
;
2
;
7
;
10
;
6
. Lyapunov-based analyses, as performed in
1
;
23
;
55
, have often complemented these designs by providing constructive stability guarantees and conditions for exponential convergence. Robust control methods, allowing for accurate output tracking, have been presented in
15
concerning linear and linearized hyperbolic and parabolic PDEs.

Limited to linear single-track models, similar techniques to those illustrated in
15
were indeed employed in
37
to achieve motion tracking with the desired level of performance. For semilinear systems as those considered in this manuscript, however, these methods are not immediately applicable, due to the complex structure of the nonlinearities that incorporate the input term. Moreover, the strategy presented in
37
;
15
requires analyzing the closed loop behavior of the PDE subsystem
a posteriori
, without providing direct indications about stability in the desired functional space.
In semilinear single-track models, these difficulties are exacerbated by the friction nonlinearities, which are often non-smooth, non-Lipschitz, and both state and input-dependent. In fact, the intricate nature of the ODE-PDE interconnection governing the dynamics of semilinear single-track vehicle models seems to require a tailored strategy that leverages the peculiar features of the tire-road rolling contact mechanics.
In this context, the present work develops an
ad-hoc
passivity-exploiting stabilization method for all-wheel-steering vehicles. The central insight of the proposed approach is that the PDE subsystem often possesses strict dissipativity properties, as discussed in
36
;
33
. Indeed, being essentially governed by frictional mechanisms, the tire-road rolling contact process inherently dissipates energy in the form of slip losses. By strategically leveraging this property, a backstepping controller is synthesized that ensures local exponential stabilization of the coupled ODE-PDE system around prescribed equilibria. For linear single-track models, global results are derived. From an implementation perspective, compared to the approach proposed in
37
, which relies on the injection of boundary terms, the controller synthesized in this work uses only bounded functionals of the distributed states, achieving stabilization without altering the semilinear structure of the ODE-PDE interconnection.
The theoretical development is also corroborated by numerical simulations accounting for realistic operating conditions that incorporate parametric uncertainties and external disturbances.

From an application standpoint, the proposed control framework is directly relevant for enhancing vehicle safety and performance. By explicitly accounting for the distributed nature of tire-road interaction, the developed method provides a principled way to stabilize vehicles subject to complex contact dynamics, particularly under oversteer conditions at high speeds, and in the presence of micro-shimmy oscillations
(
45
;
43
;
44
;
46
;
9
;
8
;
35
)
. This represents a step beyond conventional control strategies that rely on lumped tire models, offering new opportunities for advanced stability augmentation and predictive control in automotive systems.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2
formulates the problem and introduces the main structural assumptions. The state and output-feedback stabilization strategies are then presented in Section
3
. Section
4
exemplifies the proposed approach considering realistic scenarios accounting for parametric uncertainties and external disturbances. Finally, Section
5
concludes the paper and outlines future research directions.

2.2
Assumptions and preliminaries

To facilitate the observer synthesis, two mild structural assumptions are formulated in this paper. Both relate to the dissipative properties of the friction phenomena that govern the tire-road rolling contact process. In this context, it is essential to introduce the matrix
\mathbf{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\ni\mathscr{Q}(\xi)\triangleq K_{1}(\xi)H^{-1}
. For every
\xi\in[0,1]
,
\mathscr{Q}(\xi)
is a symmetric (actually, diagonal) and positive definite matrix, that is,
\mathscr{Q}\in C^{0}([0,1];\mathbf{Sym}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
, and
\mathscr{Q}(\xi)\succ 0
. This implies that
\mathscr{Q}
defines a positive operator
\mathscr{Q}\in\mathscr{L}(L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2}))
on
L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Accordingly, Assumption
2.1
is enounced below.

Assumption 2.1

(Strict dissipativity)
.

The unbounded operator
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{A}))
, defined by

\displaystyle(\mathscr{A}\zeta)(\xi)

\displaystyle\triangleq-\Lambda\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}\zeta(\xi)}{\partial{\xi}}}+(\mathscr{K}_{3}\zeta)(\xi),

(12a)

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{A})

\displaystyle\triangleq\Bigl\{\zeta\in H^{1}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})\mathrel{\Big|}\zeta(0)=0\Bigr\},

(12b)

satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\RE\langle\mathscr{A}\zeta,\mathscr{Q}\zeta\rangle_{L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq-\omega\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\zeta(\cdot)\right\rVert}_{L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2},\end{split}

(13)

for all
\zeta\in\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{A})
and some
\omega\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
.

Assumption
2.1
is satisfied for sufficiently small
\psi_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
, in (
2.1.2
) and (
3
), and obviously for
\psi_{i}=0
, as commonly found in the literature
(
13
;
12
;
49
;
19
;
17
;
18
)
.
It also implies that
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{A}))
generates an exponentially stable
C_{0}
-semigroup on
L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})
, ensuring the existence of an inverse operator
\mathscr{A}^{-1}
on
L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Whilst, in general, other choices of positive operators are possible with the given structure of
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{A}))
, the control strategy developed in the present paper exploits the strict dissipativity properties of the PDE subsystem (
7b
), and thus requires using explicitly the matrix
\mathscr{Q}(\xi)
.
Before moving to the next assumption, an important remark is formalized below.

Remark 1
.

Typically, for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\ran(\Sigma(\cdot)y+H\cdot)\not=\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which renders it impossible to compensate for all the functionals appearing in (
7b
). In this context, the operator
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{A}))
in (
12
) often describes the frictional part of the rolling contact process, which is inherently stable. Therefore, the term
(\mathscr{K}_{3}z)
in (
7b
) will not be compensated for by the control action designed in Section
3
. It is also worth remarking that the operator
(\mathscr{K}_{3},H^{1}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2}))
is generally unbounded on
L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Therefore, its direct suppression would make the resulting closed loop ODE-PDE system quasilinear rather than semilinear, due to the presence of the control input
U(t)
in the matrix
\Sigma(v)
.

Assumption 2.2

(Dissipativity and Lipschitz continuity)
.

For every
(y,\zeta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})
, the matrix
\Sigma(y)\in\mathbf{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\zeta^{\mathrm{T}}(\xi)\mathscr{Q}(\xi)\Sigma(y)\bigl[\zeta(\xi)+(\mathscr{K}_{2}\zeta)\bigr]\dif\xi\leq 0.

(14)

Additionally, it is globally Lipschitz continuous, that is, there exists
L_{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\Sigma(y_{1})-\Sigma(y_{2})\right\rVert}

\displaystyle\leq L_{\Sigma}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y_{1}-y_{2}\right\rVert}_{2},

(15)

for all
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Again, Assumption
2.2
is fulfilled for sufficiently small
\psi_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
, in (
2.1.2
) and (
3
), and always for
\psi_{i}=0
.

Remark 2
.

Not coincidentally, the dissipativity inequality (
14
) ensures global existence and uniqueness for the mild solutions of the open-loop ODE-PDE system (
7
) (see
38
). The global Lipschitz condition (
15
), which is always verified in practical applications, is instead enforced to facilitate the implementation of an output-feedback controller.

Together, Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
permit recovering some preliminary results, as formalized in Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
.

Proposition 2.1

(Strict dissipativity)
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
hold. Then, for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the unbounded operator
(\mathscr{A}_{\Sigma}(y),\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{A}_{\Sigma}(y)))
, defined by

\displaystyle(\mathscr{A}_{\Sigma}(y)\zeta)(\xi)

\displaystyle\triangleq\theta\Sigma(y)\bigl[\zeta(\xi)+(\mathscr{K}_{2}\zeta)\bigr]+(\mathscr{A}\zeta)(\xi),

(16a)

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{A}_{\Sigma}(y))

\displaystyle=\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{A})\triangleq\Bigl\{\zeta\in H^{1}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})\mathrel{\Big|}\zeta(0)=0\Bigr\},

(16b)

also generates an exponentially stable
C_{0}
-semigroup on
L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})
. In particular,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\RE\langle\mathscr{A}_{\Sigma}(y)\zeta,\mathscr{Q}\zeta\rangle_{L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq-\omega\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\zeta(\cdot)\right\rVert}_{L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2},\end{split}

(17)

for all
(y,\zeta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{A}_{\Sigma}(y))
, with the same
\omega\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
as in (
13
).

Proof.

Since
\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, the assertion is an immediate consequence of the inequalities (
13
) and (
14
).
∎

Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
combined imply strict passivity for the PDE subsystem (
7b
)-(
7c
) with output
(\mathscr{K}_{1}z)(t)
. It is exactly this intrinsic property that will be exploited in Section
3
to design a stabilizing control law.

Proposition 2.2

(Passivity)
.

Consider the semilinear PDE

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}z(\xi,t)}{\partial{t}}}+\Lambda\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}z(\xi,t)}{\partial{\xi}}}=\theta\Sigma\bigl(v(t)\bigr)\bigl[z(\xi,t)+(\mathscr{K}_{2}z)(t)\bigr]\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+(\mathscr{K}_{3}z)(t)+Hv(t),\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad(\xi,t)\in(0,1)\times(0,T),\end{split}

(18a)

\displaystyle z(0,t)=0,\quad t\in(0,T),

(18b)

with input
v(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and output

\displaystyle F(t)\triangleq(\mathscr{K}_{1}z)(t).

(19)

Then, if Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
hold, the system (
18
)-(
19
) is
strictly passive
, and satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{t}F^{\mathrm{T}}(t^{\prime})v(t^{\prime})\dif t^{\prime}\geq V\bigl(z(\cdot,t)\bigr)-V\bigl(z_{0}(\cdot)\bigr)\\
&\qquad+\omega\int_{0}^{t}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert z(\cdot,t^{\prime})\right\rVert}_{L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}\dif t^{\prime},\quad t\in[0,T],\end{split}

(20)

with
storage function

\displaystyle V\bigl(z(\cdot,t)\bigr)\triangleq\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}z^{\mathrm{T}}(\xi,t)\mathscr{Q}(\xi)z(\xi,t)\dif\xi,

(21)

for all ICs
z_{0}\triangleq z(\cdot,0)\in L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})
and inputs
v\in C^{0}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Proof.

For simplicity, the result is proved concerning classical solutions
z\in C^{1}([0,T];L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2}))\cap C^{0}([0,T];\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{A}))
, which requires
z_{0}\in\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{A})
,
v\in C^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and possibly
\Sigma\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbf{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
.
By definition,

\displaystyle\begin{split}F^{\mathrm{T}}(t)v(t)&=v^{\mathrm{T}}(t)(\mathscr{K}_{1}z)(t)=\int_{0}^{1}v^{\mathrm{T}}(t)H\mathscr{Q}(\xi)z(\xi,t)\dif\xi\\
&=-\RE\langle\mathscr{A}_{\Sigma}(v(t))z(\cdot,t),\mathscr{Q}z(\cdot,t)\rangle_{L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})}\\
&\quad+\dfrac{1}{2}\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}}{\dif{t}}}\int_{0}^{1}z^{\mathrm{T}}(\xi,t)\mathscr{Q}(\xi)z(\xi,t)\dif\xi.\end{split}

(22)

Consequently, invoking Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
and using (
21
) gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}F^{\mathrm{T}}(t)v(t)&\geq\dot{V}\bigl(z(\cdot,t)\bigr)+\omega\mathinner{\!\left\lVert z(\cdot,t)\right\rVert}_{L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}.\end{split}

(23)

Integrating the above (
23
) immediately yields (
20
). The extension to mild solutions may be worked out using standard convergence arguments.
∎

The interpretation of Proposition
2.2
above is the following: the output (
19
) of the PDE subsystem represents the forces generated by the rolling contact process, which is essentially governed by friction, and therefore passive (for a discussion about passivity and dissipativity of distributed friction models, the reader may consult, e.g.,
36
;
33
). In the interconnection (
7
), these forces act, in turn, as the input to the ODE subsystem (
7a
). These peculiar features of the ODE-PDE coupling (
7
) will be exploited in Section
3
to synthesize a stabilizing controller.
Clearly, the strict dissipativity inequality (
17
) also ensures the existence of an inverse operator
\mathscr{A}_{\Sigma}^{-1}(y)
on
L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Accordingly, for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the matrix
\mathbf{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\ni\Psi(y)\triangleq-(\mathscr{K}_{1}\mathscr{A}_{\Sigma}^{-1}(y)H)
is introduced. Lengthy but straightforward manipulations show that
\Psi(y)
is invertible for any combination of model parameters, that is,
\Psi(y)\in\mathbf{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In this context, it is perhaps worth observing that, for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the existence of a unique matrix
\Psi(y)
, as defined bove, is ensured by the invertibility of the operator
(\mathscr{A}_{\Sigma}(y),\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{A}_{\Sigma}(y)))
. On the other hand, the invertibility of
\Psi(y)
implies the following result, which is propaedeutic to synthesizing the proposed output-feedback stabilizing controller.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
hold and consider the matrix
\mathbf{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\ni\Psi(y)\triangleq-(\mathscr{K}_{1}\mathscr{A}_{\Sigma}^{-1}(y)H)
. Then, for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, there exists a unique solution
M(\cdot,y)\in C^{1}([0,1];\mathbf{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
to the nonlocal matrix ODE

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Lambda\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}M(\xi,y)}{\partial{\xi}}}&=\theta\Sigma(y)\bigl[M(\xi,y)+(\mathscr{K}_{2}M)(y)\bigr]+(\mathscr{K}_{3}M)(y)\\
&\quad+H\Psi^{-1}(y),\quad\xi\in(0,1),\end{split}

(24a)

\displaystyle M(0,y)

\displaystyle=0,

(24b)

satisfying the normalization condition
(\mathscr{K}_{1}M)(y)=I_{2}
.

Proof.

Since
(\mathscr{A}_{\Sigma}(y),\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{A}_{\Sigma}(y)))
generates an exponentially stable
C_{0}
-semigroup on
L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})
, its spectrum cannot contain
0
, that is,
0\notin\sigma(\mathscr{A}_{\Sigma}(y))
, implying that
(\mathscr{A}_{\Sigma}(y),\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{A}_{\Sigma}(y)))
must be invertible on
L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and therefore also on
L^{2}((0,1);\mathbf{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
.
Consequently, the unique solution
M(\xi,y)
to the PDE (
24
) with BC (
24b
) is formally given by

\displaystyle M(\xi,y)=-(\mathscr{A}_{\Sigma}^{-1}(y)H)(\xi)\Psi^{-1}(y),\quad\xi\in[0,1].

(25)

It is easily verified that
M(\cdot,y)\in C^{1}([0,1];\mathbf{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
. Moreover, computing
(\mathscr{K}_{1}M)(y)
and recalling the definition of
\Psi(y)
yields the indentity
(\mathscr{K}_{1}M)(y)=I_{2}
.
∎

In the linear case (
\theta=0
), the assertion of Proposition
2.1
is equivalent to
\ran(G_{1}\Psi(\cdot)\cdot)=\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Conversely, in the semilinear one, Proposition
2.1
merely states that the equilibria
(X^{\star},z^{\star})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{A})
associated with a constant input
U^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
solve the following nonlinear system:

\displaystyle\Psi^{-1}(v^{\star})G_{1}^{+}(X^{\star}+b)+v^{\star}

\displaystyle=0,

(26a)

\displaystyle z^{\star}(\xi)+(\mathscr{A}_{\Sigma}^{-1}(v^{\star})H)v^{\star}

\displaystyle=0,

(26b)

\displaystyle v^{\star}-v(X^{\star},U^{\star})

\displaystyle=0,

(26c)

with
v^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

3
Controller design

The present section is dedicated to synthesizing state and output-feedback backstepping controllers that exponentially stabilize (
7
) around a desired equilibrium
(X^{\star},z^{\star})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathscr{D}(\mathscr{A})
as in (
26
), corresponding to a stationary input
U(t)=U^{\star}
.

3.1
State-feedback controller design

To streamline the mathematical treatment, the variables
\mathbb{R}^{2}\ni X_{\delta}(t)\triangleq X(t)-X^{\star}
,
\mathbb{R}^{2}\ni z_{\delta}(\xi,t)\triangleq z(\xi,t)-z^{\star}(\xi)
, and
\mathbb{R}^{2}\ni U_{\delta}(t)\triangleq U(t)-U^{\star}
are first introduced, so that the following system may be considered in place of the original one:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\dot{X}_{\delta}(t)=A_{1}X_{\delta}(t)+G_{1}(\mathscr{K}_{1}z_{\delta})(t),\quad t\in(0,T),\end{split}

(27a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}z_{\delta}(\xi,t)}{\partial{t}}}+\Lambda\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}z_{\delta}(\xi,t)}{\partial{\xi}}}=\theta\Sigma\bigl(v(X(t),U(t))\bigr)\\
&\qquad\times\bigl[z_{\delta}(\xi,t)+(\mathscr{K}_{2}z_{\delta})(t)\bigr]+(\mathscr{K}_{3}z_{\delta})(t)\\
&\qquad+\theta\Bigl(\Sigma\bigl(v(X(t),U(t))\bigr)-\Sigma(v^{\star})\Bigr)\bigl[z^{\star}(\xi)+(\mathscr{K}_{2}z^{\star})\bigr]\\
&\qquad+Hv\bigl(X_{\delta}(t),U_{\delta}(t)\bigr),\quad(\xi,t)\in(0,1)\times(0,T),\end{split}

(27b)

\displaystyle z_{\delta}(0,t)=0,\quad t\in(0,T).

(27c)

Furthermore, to proceed with the design of an appropriate control law, the following auxiliary variable is defined:

\displaystyle Z(t)

\displaystyle\triangleq(\mathscr{K}_{1}z_{\delta})(t)-\varpi\bigl(X_{\delta}(t)\bigr),

(28)

where
\varpi(X_{\delta}(t))\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
represents a linear
virtual control law
, whose particular expression needs yet to be specified. Finally, the following transformation is considered:

\displaystyle\zeta(\xi,t)

\displaystyle\triangleq z_{\delta}(\xi,t)-M(\xi,v^{\star})\varpi\bigl(X_{\delta}(t)\bigr),

(29)

where
M(\cdot,v^{\star})\in C^{1}([0,1];\mathbf{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
is the matrix-valued function of Lemma
2.1
. Accordingly, the entire strategy is then articulated into three main steps.

3.1.1
Step 1

Substituting (
28
) into (
27a
) provides

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{X}_{\delta}(t)&=A_{1}X_{\delta}(t)+G_{1}\Bigl[Z(t)+\varpi\bigl(X_{\delta}(t)\bigr)\Bigr],\quad t\in(0,T).\end{split}

(30)

Now, the following Lyapunov function candidate is considered:

\displaystyle V_{1}\bigl(X_{\delta}(t)\bigr)\triangleq\dfrac{1}{2}X_{\delta}^{\mathrm{T}}(t)X_{\delta}(t).

(31)

Differentiating (
31
) along the dynamics (
30
) yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{V}_{1}(t)&=X_{\delta}^{\mathrm{T}}(t)\Bigl[A_{1}X_{\delta}(t)+G_{1}\varpi\bigl(X_{\delta}(t)\bigr)\Bigr]\\
&\quad+X_{\delta}^{\mathrm{T}}(t)G_{1}Z(t),\quad t\in(0,T).\end{split}

(32)

Therefore, specifying the virtual control law as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varpi\bigl(X_{\delta}(t)\bigr)&=-G_{1}^{+}A_{1}^{*}X_{\delta}(t),\end{split}

(33)

where
\mathbf{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\ni A_{1}^{*}\triangleq A_{1}+qI_{2}
for some appropriately chosen
q\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{X}_{\delta}(t)&=-qX_{\delta}(t)+G_{1}(\mathscr{K}_{1}\zeta)(t),\end{split}

(34)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{V}_{1}(t)&\leq-q\mathinner{\!\left\lVert X_{\delta}(t)\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}+X_{\delta}^{\mathrm{T}}(t)G_{1}Z(t),\quad t\in(0,T).\end{split}

(35)

In preparation for the next step, the time derivative of the virtual control law is also computed explicitly:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{\varpi}\bigl(X_{\delta}(t),\zeta(\cdot,t)\bigr)&=\mathinner{\dfrac{\dif{}\varpi(X_{\delta})}{\dif{X_{\delta}}}}\dot{X}_{\delta}(t)=-G_{1}^{+}A_{1}^{*}\bigl[G_{1}Z(t)-qX_{\delta}(t)\bigr].\end{split}

(36)

This concludes
Step 1
.

3.1.2
Step 2

By invoking Proposition
2.1
, it is possible to transform the PDE subsystem dynamics into a target system whose exponential convergence also implies that of
Z(t)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
, the transformation (
29
), with the matrix-valued function
M(\cdot,v^{\star})\in C^{1}([0,1];\mathbf{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
as in Proposition
2.1
, converts the PDE subsystem (
27b
)-(
27c
) into

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}\zeta(\xi,t)}{\partial{t}}}+\Lambda\mathinner{\dfrac{\partial{}\zeta(\xi,t)}{\partial{\xi}}}=\theta\Sigma\bigl(v(X(t),U(t))\bigr)\\
&\qquad\times\bigl[\zeta(\xi,t)+(\mathscr{K}_{2}\zeta)(t)\bigr]+(\mathscr{K}_{3}\zeta)(t)\\
&\qquad+\theta\Bigl(\Sigma\bigl(v(X(t),U(t))\bigr)-\Sigma(v^{\star})\Bigr)\bigl[z^{\star}(\xi)+(\mathscr{K}_{2}z^{\star})\bigr]\\
&\qquad+H\Bigl[v\bigl(X_{\delta}(t),U_{\delta}(t)\bigr)-\Psi^{-1}(v^{\star})\varpi\bigl(X_{\delta}(t)\bigr)\Bigr]\\
&\qquad+\theta\Bigl(\Sigma\bigl(v(X(t),U(t))\bigr)-\Sigma(v^{\star})\Bigr)\\
&\qquad\times\bigl[M(\xi,v^{\star})+(\mathscr{K}_{2}M)(v^{\star})\bigr]\varpi\bigl(X_{\delta}(t)\bigr)\\
&\qquad-M(\xi,v^{\star})\dot{\varpi}\bigl(X_{\delta}(t),\zeta(\cdot,t)\bigr),\quad(\xi,t)\in(0,1)\times(0,T),\end{split}

(37a)

\displaystyle\zeta(0,t)=0,\quad t\in(0,T),

(37b)

with
(\mathscr{K}_{1}\zeta)(t)=Z(t)
.

Proof.

The result follows from straightforward calculations and is therefore omitted for brevity.
∎

More conveniently, the new PDE subsystem (
37
) permits to prove exponential stability in the spatial
L^{2}
-norm for the state
\zeta(\xi,t)
, which automatically implies that of
Z=(\mathscr{K}_{1}\zeta)(t)
. In order to derive the expression for the control law
U_{\delta}(t)
, the following Lyapunov function candidate is considered:

\displaystyle V_{2}\bigl(\zeta(\cdot,t)\bigr)\triangleq\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\zeta^{\mathrm{T}}(\xi,t)\mathscr{Q}(\xi)\zeta(\xi,t)\dif\xi,

(38)

which coincides with the storage function of Proposition
2.2
.
Taking the derivative of (
38
) along the dynamics (
37
) yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{V}_{2}(t)&=\int_{0}^{1}\zeta^{\mathrm{T}}(\xi,t)\mathscr{Q}(\xi)(\mathscr{A}\zeta)(\xi)\dif\xi\\
&\quad+\theta\int_{0}^{1}\zeta^{\mathrm{T}}(\xi,t)\mathscr{Q}(\xi)\Sigma\bigl(v(X(t),U(t))\bigr)\\
&\quad\times\bigl[\zeta(\xi,t)+(\mathscr{K}_{2}\zeta)(t)\bigr]\dif\xi\\
&\quad+\theta\int_{0}^{1}\zeta^{\mathrm{T}}(\xi,t)\mathscr{Q}(\xi)\Bigl(\Sigma\bigl(v(X(t),U(t))\bigr)-\Sigma(v^{\star})\Bigr)\\
&\quad\times\bigl[z^{\star}(\xi)+(\mathscr{K}_{2}z^{\star})\bigr]\dif\xi\\
&\quad+Z^{\mathrm{T}}(t)\Bigl[v\bigl(X_{\delta}(t),U_{\delta}(t)\bigr)-\Psi^{-1}(v^{\star})\varpi\bigl(X_{\delta}(t)\bigr)\Bigr]\\
&\quad+\theta\int_{0}^{1}\zeta^{\mathrm{T}}(\xi,t)\mathscr{Q}(\xi)\Bigl(\Sigma\bigl(v(X(t),U(t))\bigr)-\Sigma(v^{\star})\Bigr)\\
&\quad\times\bigl[M(\xi,v^{\star})+(\mathscr{K}_{2}M)(v^{\star})\bigr]\varpi\bigl(X_{\delta}(t)\bigr)\dif\xi\\
&\quad-Z_{M}^{\mathrm{T}}(t,v^{\star})\dot{\varpi}\bigl(X_{\delta}(t),\zeta(\cdot,t)\bigr),\quad t\in(0,T),\end{split}

(39)

where, for convenience of notation,

\displaystyle Z_{M}(t,v^{\star})

\displaystyle=(\mathscr{K}_{M}\zeta)(t,v^{\star})\triangleq\int_{0}^{1}M^{\mathrm{T}}(\xi,v^{\star})\mathscr{Q}(\xi)\zeta(\xi,t)\dif\xi,

(40)

has been introduced.

By Assumption
2.1
, the sum of the first two terms appearing on the right-hand side of (
39
) is negative definite. Moreover, recalling the definition of
\dot{\varpi}(X_{\delta}(t),\zeta(\cdot,t))
according to (
36
) provides

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{V}_{2}(t)&\leq-\omega\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\zeta(\cdot,t)\right\rVert}_{L^{2}((0,1);\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}\\
&\quad+Z^{\mathrm{T}}(t)\Bigl[v\bigl(X_{\delta}(t),U_{\delta}(t)\bigr)-\Psi^{-1}(v^{\star})\varpi\bigl(X_{\delta}(t)\bigr)\Bigr]\\
&\quad+Z_{M}^{\mathrm{T}}(t,v^{\star})G_{1}^{+}A_{1}^{*}\bigl[G_{1}Z(t)-qX_{\delta}(t)\bigr],\\

5
Conclusions

This paper presented a passivity-exploiting backstepping stabilization method for semilinear single-track vehicle models with distributed tire friction dynamics. By leveraging the strict dissipativity of the PDE subsystem, a Lyapunov functional was constructed, ensuring local exponential stability under mild structural assumptions. The proposed methodology accommodates both state-feedback and output-feedback designs, the latter being enabled by a cascaded observer whose convergence was proven using a separable Lyapunov functional.

The approach was validated numerically considering non-ideal scenarios accounting for external disturbances and uncertainties. Simulation results demonstrated that the controller effectively achieves stabilization to the desired equilibrium for an oversteer vehicle driving above the critical speed, highlighting its relevance for automotive safety and performance. In this context, it is worth mentioning that, whilst the obtained stability conditions are locally valid and somewhat conservative, they provide a rigorous foundation for future developments. Extensions of this work may focus on reducing conservativeness and establishing global stability guarantees. Moreover, whereas this paper was restricted to single-track models, more refined representations with additional degrees of freedom, such as roll, pitch, and heave, might be considered. In this case, alternative control implementations exploiting the time-scale separation between the vehicle’s rigid body motion and tire dynamics could be developed. Combined braking and steering actuation strategies may also be investigated to overcome the all-wheel-steering assumption formulated in this work and address more realistic maneuvers. Finally, further research could also explore the integration of the proposed controllers with advanced estimation schemes and its application to other rolling contact systems.
