Capacities Characterizing Removable Sets for Various Function Spaces in Carnot Groups
Zack Boone

Abstract
. We study removable sets for the Campanato, Hölder continuous, L loc p L^{p}_{\text{loc}} , and Lipschitz functions in Carnot groups. In the former three cases, we characterize removability through the use of capacities with respect to any left-invariant linear differential operator ℒ \mathcal{L} for which ℒ \mathcal{L} and ℒ t \mathcal{L}^{t} are hypoelliptic and satisfy a homogeneity condition, while in the latter case we characterize Lipschitz functions with respect to the sub-Laplacian.



1.
Introduction

For a given space of distributions
\mathcal{F}
and a differential operator
\mathcal{L}
, a set
K
is removable for
\mathcal{L}
-solutions in
\mathcal{F}
if for any domain
\Omega
and any element
f\in\mathcal{F}(\Omega)
satisfying
\mathcal{L}f=0
in
\Omega\backslash K
also satisfies
\mathcal{L}f=0
in
\Omega
. The notion of removable sets first arose in 1888 in the complex plane when Painlevé considered when a bounded analytic function on
\Omega\backslash K
has an analytic extension to
\Omega
.

One can think of a removable set for
\mathcal{L}
-solutions in
\mathcal{F}
as a set which contains no "information" with respect to
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{L}
. In the context of bounded analytic functions, this was made more precise by Ahlfors in 1947
[
1
]
when he defined a set function called analytic capacity, denoted by
\gamma
, and showed that a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{C}
is removable for bounded analytic functions if and only if
\gamma(K)=0
. Hence, the "null sets" of this set function are precisely the removable sets for bounded analytic functions in
\mathbb{C}
. Ahlfors was motivated by the Painlevé problem, which asks to give a geometric condition that characterizes removable sets for bounded analytic functions. Analytic capacity is not a geometric condition; however, the study of analytic capacity helped to give a full solution to Painlevé’s problem which was achieved by Tolsa
[
21
]
. Tolsa’s work
[
21
]
was built upon the work of many authors including Ahlfors, Denjoy, Garnett, Calderón, David, Mattila, Mel’nikov, Verdera, Nazarov, Treil, and Volberg just to name a few. See Tolsa’s article
[
22
]
written for the proceedings of the 2006 ICM for more history and results.

The study of removable sets for general differential operators and function spaces has been studied by a wide range of authors such as Carleson, Král, and Harvey-Polking. In 1963, Carleson
[
4
]
gave a removability characterization of Hölder continuous harmonic functions functions in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Harvey-Polking’s results in
[
13
]
give sufficient conditions for when a set is removable for a large class of linear partial differential equations. They achieved this level of generality by proving the existence of a partition of unity, now called the Harvey-Polking partition of unity
[
13
, Lemma 3.1]
, which incorporates derivative information. This partition of unity is fundamental to the study of removable sets and has also been applied to problems of uniform approximation
[
19
]
. The results obtained by Král’s
[
15
]
were quite general, characterizing removable sets for semielliptic partial differential operators for function spaces such as BMO, VMO, Campanato, and Hölder continuous functions.

The Lipschitz harmonic capacity was introduced by Paramonov
[
19
, (2.2)]
, motivated by problems in uniform approximation. Mattila-Paramonov
[
17
]
also showed that the Lipschitz harmonic capacity characterizes bounded removable sets for Lipschitz harmonic functions
[
17
, Proposition 2.2]
. In a follow up paper to
[
13
]
, Harvey-Polking in
[
14
]
developed capacities in a very general form. Using these capacities they were able to characterize removable sets for large classes of differential operators and function spaces.

Removable sets have also been studied in the sub-Riemannian setting, for example
[
8
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
. Capacities on the other hand have not been studied in the sub-Riemannian setting, which we do in this paper. We will adapt the capacities developed by Harvey-Polking and Paramonov in
[
14
]
and
[
19
]
respectively to the setting of Carnot groups, which we denote by
\mathbb{G}
and the background of which will be given in Section
2
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
denote the sub-Laplacian and
\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}
the horizontal gradient. To avoid technicalities, we will work with the sub-Laplacian and horizontal gradient which is generated from the Jacobian basis. See Section
2
for more details. We define the Carnot Lipschitz harmonic capacity, denoted by
\kappa
, of a bounded set
K
as

\displaystyle\kappa(K):=\sup\{|\langle\mathcal{L}f,1\rangle|:f\in\text{Lip}_{\text{loc}},\supp\mathcal{L}f\subset K,\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f\|\leq 1\}.

Above,
\supp\mathcal{L}f
refers to the distributional support of
\mathcal{L}f
. The proof that
\kappa
characterizes removable sets for Lipschitz harmonic functions in Euclidean space from Mattila-Paramonov relies on
[
19
, Lemma 4.2]
. There is an issue when one tries to adapt this proof to the Carnot setting, which is that the differential operators that we usually deal with in
\mathbb{G}
do not commute, which is indeed used in the proof of
[
19
, Lemma 4.2]
. The reader will see later that for
f
in some distribution class
\mathcal{F}
, when one is trying to characterize removability it becomes natural to try to show
\psi\mathcal{L}f\ast\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}
where
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}
and
\Gamma
is the fundamental solution to
\mathcal{L}
. We are able to show this when
\mathcal{F}=\text{Lip}_{\text{loc}}
by getting a distributional formula for
\psi\mathcal{L}f\ast\Gamma
that involves sums of functions which have horizontal derivatives on
f
, then proving that each of the functions in this sum satisfy the conditions of
\kappa
. Using this idea, we are still able to prove, like in the Euclidean setting from Mattila-Paramonov, the the characterization of removable (Carnot) Lipschitz harmonic functions through the null sets of the (Carnot) Lipschitz harmonic capacity
\kappa
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{G}
be bounded and
\mathcal{L}
the sub-Laplacian. Then
K
is removable for Lipschitz
\mathcal{L}
-solutions if and only if
\kappa(K)=0
.

We heavily rely on the fact that the sub-Laplacian is generated from horizontal vector fields and that the sub-Laplacian is
2
-homogeneous. We mention that Theorem
1.1
also holds when
\mathcal{L}
is a
2
-homogeneous linear differential operator generated by the horizontal vector fields from the Jacobian basis and for which
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{t}
(the transpose of
\mathcal{L}
) are both hypoelliptic. The condition
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{t}
being hypoelliptic guarantees the existence of a fundamental solution with certain nice homogeneity properties. See
[
20
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Characterizing removable sets for Campanato functions and Hölder continuous functions through any left-invariant homogeneous linear differential operator
\mathcal{L}
for which both
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{t}
are hypoelliptic, will actually come as consequences of our other main results. The task of characterizing removability for Campanato and Hölder continuous functions turns out to be more delicate than showing Theorem
1.1
, which we now explain why. As
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{t}
are hypoelliptic, we have the existence of a fundamental solution
\Gamma
to
\mathcal{L}
. In contrast to the local Lipschitz case, we are not able to directly show that
\psi\mathcal{L}f\ast\Gamma\in\mathcal{F}
where
\mathcal{F}
is either the Campanato or Hölder space and
f\in\mathcal{F}
. In the Campanato case, the reason for this is not having access to a commutative group law and commutative differential operators. For the Hölder case, it is because the functions
f
need not be differentiable almost everywhere. So for these more difficult cases, we will take a different strategy and the usefulness of the (Carnot) Harvey-Polking partition of unity in
\mathbb{G}
, proven by Chousionis and Tyson in
[
9
, Lemma 3.8]
will take effect. Sections
5
and
6
highlight this alternative approach where we will show that certain capacities are comparable to Hausdorff content and get a removability characterization as a consequence. Let
\kappa^{\varrho}
and
\kappa^{\delta}
denote the Campanato and Hölder capacities respectively. Our two other main results are the following:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a left-invariant differential operator homogeneous of degree
\lambda\in[1,Q)
such that both
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{t}
are hypoelliptic. For a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{G}
and
\varrho\in[\lambda,Q]
we have,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{\varrho-\lambda}(K)\lesssim\kappa^{\varrho}(K)\lesssim\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{\varrho-\lambda}(K).

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a left-invariant differential operator homogeneous of degree
\lambda\in[1,Q)
such that both
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{t}
are hypoelliptic. For a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{G}
and
\delta\in(0,1)
we have,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{Q-\lambda+\delta}(K)\lesssim\kappa^{\delta}(K)\lesssim\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{Q-\lambda+\delta}(K).

The idea for proving Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
was inspired by a result of Verdera
[
23
]
.

We also consider removable sets for
L_{\text{loc}}^{p}
,
p\in[1,\infty)
, which we can characterize in a similar way to showing Theorem
1.1
.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
will give the necessary background about Carnot groups and differential operators. Section
4
describes a generalization of the Harvey-Polking lemma and the version of Hausdorff measure that is more conducive to our calculations. The rest of the sections are devoted to characterizing removable sets through capacities for various function spaces.

2.
Definitions and Preliminaries

2.1.
Carnot groups

A
Carnot group
, denoted
\mathbb{G}
, is a connected, simply connected, and nilpotent Lie group whose Lie algebra admits a stratification of the form

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{v}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathfrak{v}_{s},\quad[\mathfrak{v}_{1},\mathfrak{v}_{i}]=\mathfrak{v}_{i+1}\text{ for }i=1,\cdots,s-1,\quad[\mathfrak{v}_{1},\mathfrak{v}_{s}]=\{0\}

and
\mathfrak{v}_{1},\cdots,\mathfrak{v}_{s}
are non-zero subspaces of
\mathfrak{g}
. We call the integer
s\geq 1
the
step
of
\mathbb{G}
. Denote the group law of
\mathbb{G}
by
\cdot
the identity element
\mathbb{G}
by
0
. We write
\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{R}^{N},\cdot)
. Notice that the condition
[\mathfrak{v}_{1},\mathfrak{v}_{i}]=\mathfrak{v}_{i+1}
for
i=1,\cdots,s-1
means that
\mathfrak{v}_{1}
generates
\mathfrak{g}
through the Lie algebra. We call
\mathfrak{v}_{1}
the
horizontal layer
of the Lie algebra and the elements of
\mathfrak{v}_{1}
are called
horizontal vector fields
. Let,

(2.1)

m_{1}:=\dim(\mathfrak{v}_{1}).

The elements of
\mathfrak{g}
are left-invariant vector fields and from the conditions of
\mathbb{G}
, the exponential map
\exp:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
is a global diffeomorphism. Elements in
\mathfrak{g}
then act on smooth functions
f
by,

(2.2)

(Xf)(x)=Xf(x)=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\left(f(x\cdot\exp(tX))\right).

Given any left-invariant vector field
X\in\mathfrak{g}
we can also define a right-invariant vector field
\widetilde{X}
by,

(2.3)

(\widetilde{X}f)(x)=\widetilde{X}f(x)=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\left(f(\exp(tX)\cdot x)\right).

Notice that equations (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) are only defined once the functions
Xf
and
\widetilde{X}f
have been found. Once they have been found, their definition becomes a derivative in the
t
-variable, and the
x
-variable is then treated as a constant.

Let
X
be a vector field in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
of the form
Xh(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}(x)\partial_{j}h(x)
where
h
is a smooth enough function.. Let

\displaystyle XI(x)=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}(x)\\
\vdots\\
a_{N}(x)\end{pmatrix}

and notice

(2.4)

Xh(x)=\langle\nabla h(x),XI(x)\rangle

where
\nabla
is the Euclidean gradient.

Fix an inner product on
\mathfrak{v}_{1}
. From here on out, we will take a basis to
\mathfrak{v}_{1}
to consist of a certain kind of orthonormal basis of elements, called the
Jacobian basis
of
\mathfrak{v}_{1}
. We describe its properties now.

Proposition 2.1
.

[
2
, Proposition 1.2.16]

Let
j\in\{1,\cdots,N\}
be given. There exists one and only one vector field, denoted by
X_{j}
, characterized by any one of the following equivalent conditions:

(a)

X_{j}|_{0}=\partial_{j}|_{0}
, i.e.

\displaystyle X_{j}\varphi(0)=\partial_{j}\varphi(0)\quad\text{ for all }\varphi\in C^{\infty}.

(ii)

If
e_{j}
denotes the
j
-th element of the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, then

\displaystyle X_{j}(0)=e_{j}.

An
X_{j}
satisfying one of the above conditions will be called a Jacobian vector field, and we take the set of elements
\mathcal{X}=\{X_{1},\cdots,X_{m_{1}}\}
to be the Jacobian basis of
\mathfrak{v}_{1}
. We now say the
sub-Laplacian
is the differential operator
\mathcal{L}
defined as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}X_{i}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}X_{i}(X_{i}).

where each
X_{i}
is a part of the Jacobian basis of
\mathfrak{v}_{1}
.

We will now define the
Carnot-Carathéodory distance
on
\mathbb{G}
. This definition is taken from
[
2
, Definition 5.2.2]
. Let
\mathcal{X}=\{X_{1},\cdots,X_{m_{1}}\}
be the Jacobian basis for
\mathfrak{v}_{1}
. Then a piecewise smooth path
\gamma:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
is called
\mathcal{X}
-subunit
if

(2.5)

\langle\dot{\gamma}(t),\xi\rangle^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\langle X_{j}I(\gamma(t)),\xi\rangle^{2}\quad\text{ for all }\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

and almost everywhere (in the sense of Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
)
t\in[0,T]
. Set
S(\mathcal{X})
to be the set of all
\mathcal{X}
-subunit paths and put

\displaystyle\ell(\gamma)=T

if
[0,T]
is the domain of
\gamma\in S(\mathcal{X})
. The Carnot-Carathéodory (CC) distance is then,

(2.6)

d(x,y):=\inf\left\{\ell(\gamma):\gamma\in S(\mathcal{X}),\gamma(0)=x,\gamma(T)=y\right\}

and the corresponding metric is,

(2.7)

\|y^{-1}\cdot x\|:=d(x,y).

Open balls are then taken as
B(x,r):=\{y:d(y,x)<r\}
. The CC-distance is symmetric, i.e.
d(x,y)=d(y,x)
, which also implies
\|p^{-1}\|=\|p\|
for all
p\in\mathbb{G}
. For
t>0
define
\delta_{t}:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}
by
\delta_{t}(X)=t^{i}X
if
X\in\mathfrak{v}_{i}
and extend to all of
\mathfrak{g}
by linearity. By conjugation with the exponential map,
\delta_{t}
induces an automorphism on
\mathbb{G}
which we also denote by
\delta_{t}
. Then
(\delta_{t})_{t>0}
is the one-parameter family of
dilations
on
\mathbb{G}
which satisfy
d(\delta_{t}(x),\delta_{t}(y))=td(x,y)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{G}
. Note that this implies
\|\delta_{t}(p)\|=t\|p\|
for all
p\in\mathbb{G}
. The Jacobian determinant of
\delta_{t}
is equal to
t^{Q}
where

(2.8)

\displaystyle Q=\sum_{i=1}^{s}i\dim\mathfrak{v}_{i}

is the
homogeneous dimension
of
\mathbb{G}
. We then have
\mathbb{G}
is diffeomorphic with
\mathfrak{g}=\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N=\sum_{i=1}^{s},\dim\mathfrak{v}_{i}
by the exponential map. The Haar measure on
\mathbb{G}
is induced by the exponential map from Lebesgue measure on
\mathfrak{g}
and the Haar measure agree, up to a constant, with the
Q
-dimensional Hausdorff measure in the metric space
(\mathbb{G},d)
. In this paper, we will work almost exclusively with the CC-distance which we do simply because it satisfies the triangle inequality.

In the case of Lemma
3.11
, the proof uses a different metric. The sub-Laplacian has a unique fundamental solution
\Gamma
. The proofs of the below facts can be found in
[
2
, Section 5]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For all
t>0
and
p\in\mathbb{G}\backslash\{0\}
,

(i)

\Gamma(p^{-1})=\Gamma(p)
,

(ii)

\Gamma(\delta_{t}(p))=t^{2-Q}\Gamma(p)
,

(iii)

\Gamma(p)>0
.

These facts allow us to define a norm in terms of
\Gamma
. The function,

(2.9)

\|p\|_{\Gamma}=\begin{cases}\Gamma(p)^{1/(2-Q)}&\text{if }p\in\mathbb{G}\backslash\{0\}\\
0&\text{if }p=0\end{cases}

defines a symmetric homogeneous norm which is
C^{\infty}
away from the origin. Symmetric means
\|p^{-1}\|_{\Gamma}=\|p\|_{\Gamma}
for all
p\in\mathbb{G}
. Now let,

(2.10)

\displaystyle d_{\Gamma}(x,y)=\|y^{-1}\cdot x\|_{\Gamma}

be the left-invariant quasi-distance induced by
\|\cdot\|_{\Gamma}
.

Taking
\mathcal{X}=\{X_{1},\cdots,X_{m_{1}}\}
to be the Jacobian basis of
\mathfrak{v}_{1}
, we define the
horizontal gradient
by

(2.11)

\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f=(X_{1}f,\cdots,X_{m_{1}}f)

where
f
is some real-valued function defined on an open set of
\mathbb{G}
. Moreover, we can define the
horizontal divergence
by

\displaystyle\text{div}_{\mathbb{G}}F=X_{1}F_{1}+\cdots+X_{m_{1}}F_{m_{1}}

where
F=(F_{1},\cdots,F_{m_{1}})
is a vector field defined on some open set of
\mathbb{G}
. Lastly, the convolution operation
\ast
will be taken to mean

(2.12)

f\ast g(x):=\int f(y)g(y^{-1}\cdot x)\,dy=\int f(x\cdot p^{-1})g(p)\,dp.

Convolutions are not commutative since
\mathbb{G}
is nonabelian. For more background on Carnot groups, see
[
2
]
and
[
11
]
.

2.2.
Left-invariant homogeneous operators and fundamental solutions.

For any
A\subset\mathbb{G}
we will let
C_{0}^{\infty}(A)
be the set of all
C^{\infty}
functions with compact support contained in
A
. For
\Omega
open denote by
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
the space of distributions on
\Omega
whose test space is
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and equip
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
with the locally convex topology, and set
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{G})
. When talking about distributions, the symbol
\langle T,\varphi\rangle
will denote the pairing of a distribution
T\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
and a test function
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

We say that a distribution
T\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
is
homogeneous of degree
\lambda
provided
\langle T,\varphi\circ\delta_{t}\rangle=t^{-Q-\lambda}\langle T,\varphi\rangle
for all
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
. A distribution which is
C^{\infty}
away from
0
and homogeneous of degree
\lambda-Q
will be called a
kernel of type
\lambda
. A differential operator
\mathcal{L}
will be called
homogeneous of degree
\lambda
, or
\lambda
-homogeneous, if

(2.13)

\mathcal{L}(T\circ\delta_{t})=t^{\lambda}(\mathcal{L}T)\circ\delta_{t}\,\,\,\,\text{ for all }T\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}.

Furthermore, a differential operator
\mathcal{L}
is said to be
hypoelliptic
if for any open
\Omega\subset\mathbb{G}
and any two distriubions
T
and
F
on
\Omega
satisfying
\mathcal{L}T=F
, then
F\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
implies
T\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
. We call a function
f
satisfying
\mathcal{L}f=0
an
\mathcal{L}
-solution.

A fundamental result in sub-Riemannian geometry, due to Folland, deals with the existence of fundamental solutions for such differential operators. Let
\mathcal{L}^{t}
denote the transpose of a differential operator
\mathcal{L}
.

Theorem 2.3
.

[
20
, Theorem 2.1]
Let
\mathcal{L}
be a differential operator on a Carnot group. Suppose
\mathcal{L}
is homogeneous of degree
\lambda
,
0<\lambda<Q
, such that both
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{t}
are hypoelliptic. Then there exists a unique kernel
K
of type
\lambda
which is a fundamental solution of
\mathcal{L}
at
0
, i.e.
\mathcal{L}K=\delta
.

For
\alpha,x\in\mathbb{G}
define
\tau_{\alpha}:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
by
\tau_{\alpha}(x):=\alpha\cdot x
. Note that by definition of a Lie group,
\tau_{\alpha}
is smooth. Then a differential operator
\mathcal{L}
is
left-invariant
if

(2.14)

\mathcal{L}(T\circ\tau_{\alpha})=(\mathcal{L}T)\circ\tau_{\alpha}\,\quad\text{ for all }\alpha\in\mathbb{G}\text{ and }T\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}

Example 2.4
.

Let
\{X_{1},\cdots,X_{m_{1}}\}
be a basis for
\mathfrak{v}_{1}
. Then
\mathcal{L}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}X_{i}^{2}
is called a
sub-Laplacian
on
\mathbb{G}
. It is easily checked that
\mathcal{L}
is left-invariant, homogeneous of degree
2
, and self-adjoint. Furthermore, by Hörmander’s theorem
[
2
, Theorem
1
in the preface]
,
\mathcal{L}
is hypoelliptic.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a linear partial differential equation and
\mathcal{F}
a space of distributions. A set
K\subset\mathbb{G}
is
removable for
\mathcal{L}
-solutions in
\mathcal{F}
if for any domain
\Omega
and any element
f\in\mathcal{F}(\Omega)
satisfying
\mathcal{L}f=0
in
\Omega\backslash K
also satisfies
\mathcal{L}f=0
in
\Omega
.

Remark 2.6
.

Notice that this definition of removability does not assume
K
is a subset of
\Omega
. This adds some interesting complexities in understanding removable sets. For example, a natural question one may ask is if removability is monotonic? In other words, if
E
is removable (with respect to a class of distributions and a PDE) and
F\subset E
, does this imply
F
is also removable? When
K
is not assumed to be a subset of
\Omega
as in Definition
2.5
, it is not clear from the definition if monotonicity holds. However, for the differential operators and function spaces we consider, the results in this paper will in fact show that removability is monotonic.

We record a simple, but very useful, proposition and the proof of which can be found in
[
2
, Page
32
]
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
f
be a smooth homogeneous function of degree
m\in\mathbb{R}
and
X
be a linear differential operator of degree
n\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
Xf
is a homogeneous function of degree
m-n
.

Remark 2.8
.

It is simple to check that the same proof which gives Proposition
2.7
also works when we relax the smoothness condition to
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{G}\backslash\{0\})
. In particular, if
k
is a kernel of type
\lambda
and
X
a linear differential operator of degree
m
, then since
k
is a smooth function on
\mathbb{G}\backslash\{0\}
, we have
Xk
is both smooth and homogeneous of degree
\lambda-Q-m
on
\mathbb{G}\backslash\{0\}
. This follows since
k
is smooth and homogeneous of degree
\lambda-Q
on
\mathbb{G}\backslash\{0\}
.

The following is well known for those who study sub-Riemannian geometry, but we record the proof anyway.

Proposition 2.9
.

Suppose
f
is both homogeneous of degree
\lambda
and continuous on
\mathbb{G}\backslash\{0\}
. Then,

|f(p)|\leq C\|p\|^{\lambda}

for a constant
C>0
which only depends on
f
and
\mathbb{G}
.

Proof.

Set
\Sigma:=\{p:\|p\|=1\}
. Since the topology on
\mathbb{G}
coincides with the topology on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we get
\Sigma
is compact as
\Sigma
is closed and bounded. Furthermore,
\Sigma
avoids the origin. Hence, by continuity, there exists
p_{0}\in\Sigma
such that
|f(p)|\leq|f(p_{0})|=:C
for all
p\in\Sigma
. Now fix any
p\in\mathbb{G}\backslash\{0\}
. Then by homogeneity of
f
,

\displaystyle|f(p)|

\displaystyle=|f(\delta_{\|p\|}\circ\delta_{\|p\|^{-1}}(p))|

\displaystyle=\|p\|^{\lambda}|f(\delta_{\|p\|^{-1}}(p))|

\displaystyle\leq C\|p\|^{\lambda}

which completes the proof.
∎

Lastly, some parts of this paper will use a specific kind of multi-index notation and product rule. Let
m_{1}
be as in (

). For a multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{\ell})\in\{1,\cdots,m_{1}\}^{\ell}
write

X_{\alpha}=X_{\alpha_{1}}\cdots X_{\alpha_{\ell}}\quad\text{ and }\quad|\alpha|=\ell.

We describe a product rule, the main purpose of which is track the homogeneity degree of the operators involved. Fix
\alpha\in\{1,\cdots,m_{1}\}^{\ell}
. For
\beta=(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{\ell})
with
\beta_{i}\in\{0,1\}
for all

, set

X_{\alpha}^{\beta}=X_{\alpha_{1}}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots X_{\alpha_{\ell}}^{\beta_{\ell}}.

We make the convention of

to be the identity operator

, i.e. no derivatives are applied. Then

when

and

when

. Therefore we get the following:

(2.17)

X_{\alpha}^{\beta}\text{ is of length }\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\beta_{i}.

Now for

, it can be checked that the product rule reads,

(2.18)

X_{\alpha}(\psi\varphi)=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}}X_{\alpha}^{\beta}(\psi)X_{\alpha_{1}}^{1-\beta_{1}}\cdots X_{\alpha_{\ell}}^{1-\beta_{\ell}}(\varphi).

As a short hand, for a fixed
\beta
set
\mskip 1.0mu\prescript{\smash{\mathrm{*}\mkern-3.0mu}}{}{\mathstrut X}_{\alpha}^{\beta}=X_{\alpha_{1}}^{1-\beta_{1}}\cdots X_{\alpha_{\ell}}^{1-\beta_{\ell}}
and

. We lastly note that for

with

, there are

possible

3.
Capacity and Removability for Lipschitz Harmonic Functions

The only differential operator we will consider in this section is the sub-Laplacian. Let
\mathcal{X}=\{X_{1},\cdots,X_{m_{1}}\}
be the Jacobian basis for
\mathfrak{v}_{1}
(see Section
2
for details). Recall that the
sub-Laplacian
is then,

(3.1)

\mathcal{L}:=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}X_{i}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}X_{i}(X_{i}).

Then
\mathcal{L}
is left-invariant, homogeneous of degree
2
, and self-adjoint. By Hörmander’s theorem
[
2
, Theorem 1 in the preface]
,
\mathcal{L}
is hypoelliptic. Hence, there exists a kernel of type
2
, denoted
\Gamma
, which is a fundamental solution to
\mathcal{L}
. For a function
f
, define the
horizontal gradient
by,

(3.2)

\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f:=(X_{1}f,\cdots,X_{m_{1}}f)

whenever the right hand side makes sense.

Definition 3.1

(Lipschitz Functions)
.

For an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{G}
let
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function. We say that
f\in\text{Lip}(\Omega)
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that,

\displaystyle|f(x)-f(y)|\leq Cd(x,y)\text{ for all }x,y\in\Omega.

Denote by
\text{Lip}(f)
the smallest such
C
, and set
\text{Lip}=\text{Lip}(\mathbb{G})
.

Definition 3.2

(Local Lipschitz Functions)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function. We say that
f\in\text{Lip}_{\text{loc}}
if for every compact set
E
, there exists a constant
C=C(E)>0
such that

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq Cd(x,y)\quad\text{ for all }x,y\in E.

Definition 3.3

(Lipschitz Capacity)
.

The capacity of a bounded
K\subset\mathbb{G}
with respect to
Lip
and the sub-Laplacian
\mathcal{L}
(as given in (
3.1
)) is defined as,

\displaystyle\kappa(K):=\sup\{|\langle\mathcal{L}f,1\rangle|:f\in\text{Lip}_{\text{loc}},\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1,\supp(\mathcal{L}f)\subset K\}.

Remark 3.4
.

The definition of Lipschitz capacity was originally given by Paramonov
[
19
, Definition 2.1]
in Euclidean space with the extra condition
\nabla f(\infty)=0
, where
\nabla
is the Euclidean gradient. This extra condition allows one to rewrite distributions in a convenient way. More specifically, one can get the distributional equality of,

f=\Phi\ast\Delta f+c

where
\Delta
is the Laplacian,
\Phi
is the fundamental solution to
\Delta
, and
c
is a constant. This relationship will not be needed for our purposes, so we omit the condition
\nabla f(\infty)=0
.

Corollary
3.6
says that if a function has a bounded horizontal gradient, then it is Lipschitz. The following lemma will help us in showing this and it is essentially one half of
[
10
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
f:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a locally Lipschitz function with
\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f\|\leq C
for some finite constant
C
. Then,
f
is Lipschitz and

\displaystyle Lip(f)\leq\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C.

Proof.

Fix
x
and
y
. Recall that
d(x,y)
is defined as an infimum over subunit paths,
\gamma
, joining
x
and
y
. By
[
2
, Theorem 5.15.5]
, this infimum is actually a minimum. So find
\gamma:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbb{G}
for which
T=d(x,y)
and
\gamma(0)=x
,
\gamma(T)=y
.
Set
\text{Im}(\gamma)=\{\gamma(t):t\in[0,T]\}\subset\mathbb{G}
. Since
\gamma
is continuous,
\text{Im}(\gamma)
is compact. As
f
is locally Lipschitz, we have
M:=\text{Lip}(f\begin{picture}\put(2.0,0.0){\line{1}{0}{8.0}}\put(2.0,0.0){\line{0}{1}{8.0}}\end{picture}\text{Im}(\gamma))<\infty
.
Now we show
f\circ\gamma
is Lipschitz on
[0,T]
. Take
a,b\in[0,L]
. Then,

\displaystyle|f\circ\gamma(a)-f\circ\gamma(b)|\leq Md(\gamma(a),\gamma(b))\leq M\text{Lip}(\gamma)|a-b|

which shows that
f\circ\gamma
is Lipschitz. Hence,

\displaystyle|f(x)-f(y)|=|f\circ\gamma(0)-f\circ\gamma(T)|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{T}(f\circ\gamma)^{\prime}(s)\,ds\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{T}\langle f(\gamma(s)),\ \dot{\gamma}(s)\rangle\,ds\right|

\displaystyle\overset{(\ref{subunit})}{\leq}\int_{0}^{T}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}(\langle X_{j}I(\gamma(s)),(\nabla f)(\gamma(s))\rangle)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m_{1}}[(X_{j}f)(\gamma(s))]^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f\|_{L^{\infty}}T

\displaystyle=\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f\|_{L^{\infty}}d(x,y)

which finishes the proof.
∎

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
f:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function satisfying
\|\nabla_{\mathbb{G}}f\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C
for some finite constant
C
. Then
f
is Lipschitz.

Proof.

By [Theorem 1.4,
[
12
]
],
f
is locally Lipschitz. Then using Lemma
3.5
, we get
f
is Lipschitz.
∎

Given Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
, one might be tempted to prove

for a compact

then show removability as a consequence. This explicitly fails though. Indeed, by

for which

Theorem 3.7

(Removability Characterization for Lip)
.

Let

be bounded. Then
K
is removable for
\mathcal{L}
-solutions in Lip if and only if
\kappa(K)=0
.

Proof.

Assume
K
is removable and take
f\in\text{Lip}_{\text{loc}}
which satisfies
\supp(\mathcal{L}f)\subset K
distributionally. Applying the definition of removability to the domain
\Omega=\mathbb{G}
, we automatically get
\mathcal{L}f\equiv 0
on
\mathbb{G}
. This gives
\kappa(K)=0
.

For the other direction, we will use the contrapositive. Assume
K
is not removable for
\mathcal{L}
-solutions in
Lip
. Then there exists a domain
D
and a locally Lipschitz function
f:D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\mathcal{L}f=0
in

but
\mathcal{L}f\neq 0
in

is not an
\mathcal{L}
-solution in the distributional sense which means there exists
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(D)
with

. Notice
\supp(\varphi\mathcal{L}f)\subset K
. Set

. Left-invariance of
\mathcal{L}
gives,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(f_{\varphi})=\varphi\mathcal{L}f\ast\mathcal{L}\Gamma=\varphi\mathcal{L}f

so that

. By Lemmas
3.8
,
3.13

we get

and

which completes the proof.
∎

Now we show the necessary lemmas which give
f_{\varphi}=\varphi\mathcal{L}\ast\Gamma

Since the horizontal gradient of a real-valued function generates a vector in

These two equalities now give,

\displaystyle\langle\varphi\mathcal{L}f,\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{L}(f\varphi),\psi\rangle-\langle\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}2X_{i}(fX_{i}\varphi),\psi\rangle+\langle f\mathcal{L}\varphi,\psi\rangle

\displaystyle\psi\mathcal{L}f=\mathcal{L}(f\psi)-2\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}X_{i}(fX_{i}\psi)+f\mathcal{L}\psi.

Therefore, by

4.
Dyadic tilings and the Harvey-Polking Lemma in
\mathbb{G}

The first part of this section is simply restating the results from Section
3
in
[
9
]
, so we direct the reader there for all the proofs.

The purpose of this section is introducing a sort of "dyadic structure" in Carnot groups which is conducive to the removability results for Sections
5
and
6
. In particular, introducing a structure which allows for a generalization of the well-known Harvey-Polking partition of unity
[
13
, Lemma 3.1]
. We use a modified Hausdorff measure, but the introduction of this measure requires some setup. Let
|\cdot|
denote Lebesgue measure.

Theorem 4.1
.

[
9
, Theorem 3.1]

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a Carnot group of homogeneous dimension
A
. There exists
\frac{1}{2}
-homotheties
f_{1},\cdots,f_{M}\in\mathbb{G}
,
M=2^{Q}
, and a compact set
T\subset\mathbb{G}
so that
T=\bigcup_{j=1}^{M}T_{j}
where
T_{j}=f_{j}(T)
. Moreover

0<|T|<\infty

and
|T_{j}\cap T_{i}|=0
whenever
j\neq i
.

We will call
T
the
fundamental tile
. Let
W=\{1,\ldots,M\}
. For
m\geq 0
and
w=w_{1}\cdots w_{m}\in W^{m}
we
introduce the notation

f_{w}=f_{w_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{w_{m}}

and
T_{w}=f_{w}(T)
. We denote by
\mathcal{D}_{m}
the family of sets
T_{w}
as
w

ranges over
W^{m}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

[
9
, Proposition 3.4]

The set
T
is the closure of an open set.

By Proposition
4.2
, select a point
p\in T
and radii
0<R^{{\it i}}<R^{{\it o}}
so that
B(p,R^{{\it i}})\subset T\subset B(p,R^{{\it o}})
. Fixing
p
, we assume that
R^{{\it i}}
has been
chosen as large as possible and
R^{{\it o}}
has been chosen as small as
possible subject to the preceding constraint. Then
R^{{\it o}}<\operatorname{diam}T
. We call
p
the

center
of
T
and
R^{{\it i}}
and
R^{{\it o}}
the
inner
and

outer radii
of
T
, respectively.

For each
w\in W^{m}
,
m\geq 0
, we define the center of the
tile

T_{w}
to be
p_{w}=f_{w}(p)
, and the inner and outer radii of
T_{w}
to be

R^{{\it i}}_{w}=2^{-m}R^{{\it i}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad R^{{\it o}}_{w}=2^{-m}R^{{\it o}},

respectively. We have
B(p_{w},R^{{\it i}}_{w})\subset T_{w}\subset B(p_{w},R^{{\it o}}_{w})
.

The Hausdorff measures and dimensions of bounded subsets of
\mathbb{G}
can
be computed using a “dyadic Hausdorff measure” constructed using the
tiles
\{T_{w}\}
. By applying a dilation, we may restrict our attention
to subsets of the initial tile
T
. We explicitly remark that we can do this since we will only be working with bounded subsets of
\mathbb{G}
. Let
{\mathcal{D}}_{*}=\cup_{m\geq 0}{\mathcal{D}}_{m}
.
For a set
A\subset T
and
s,\varepsilon>0
, define

{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathcal{D}},\varepsilon}^{s}(A)=\inf\sum_{i}(\operatorname{diam}T_{w_{i}})^{s}

where the infimum is taken over all coverings of
A
by tiles
T_{w_{i}}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{*}
with
\operatorname{diam}T_{w_{i}}<\varepsilon
. Define
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{D}}^{s}(A)=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathcal{D}},\varepsilon}^{s}(A)
. For each
s>0
,
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{D}}^{s}
is a
Borel measure on
T
. If the intersection of two
tiles has nonempty interior, then one of the tiles is contained inside
the other. As a result we can without loss of generality restrict our
attention in the definition of
{\mathcal{H}}_{{\mathcal{D}}}^{s}
to essentially disjoint
coverings.

Proposition 4.3
.

[
9
, Proposition 3.5]

For each
s>0
there exists a constant
C=C(s)>0
so that

{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(A)\leq{\mathcal{H}}^{s}_{\mathcal{D}}(A)\leq C{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(A)
for every
A\subset T
.

The following lemma is actually used to prove Proposition
4.3
, but we will use it later.

Lemma 4.4
.

There exists a constant
N>0
so that for any ball
B(q,r)
with
r\leq R^{o}
and
m\geq 0
chosen so that
2^{-m-1}R^{o}\leq r<2^{-m}R^{o}
, it holds that the number of tiles
T_{w}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{m}
which intersect
B(q,r)
is at most
N
.

The above lemma was originally stated for
r\leq 1
in
[
9
, Lemma 3.6]
, but the same proof works when we assume
r\leq R^{o}
. The following is a generalization of the Harvey-Polking lemma in
\mathbb{G}
. The lemma uses the notation of (
2.15
).

Lemma 4.5
.

[
9
, Lemma 3.8]

Let
\{T_{w_{i}}:1\leq i\leq N\}
be a finite collection of essentially
disjoint tiles, with
T_{w_{i}}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{m(i)}
. For each
i
there is a
function
\varphi_{i}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{G})
, supported in

B(p_{w_{i}},2R^{{\it o}}_{w_{i}})
, so that

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varphi_{i}(q)=1\quad\mbox{for all $q\in\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}T_{w_{i}}$.}

Moreover, for each multi-index
\alpha
there exists a constant

C_{\alpha}>0
so that

|X_{\alpha}\varphi_{i}(x)|\leq C_{\alpha}2^{m(i)|\alpha|}\quad\mbox{for all $x\in\mathbb{G}$ and $1\leq i\leq N$.}

By Proposition
4.3
, we already know our new dyadic Hausdorff measure and the original Hausdorff measure are comparable, however we will also need to show that their Hausdorff contents are comparable.

Lemma 4.6
.

For a compact
K\subset T
we have,

\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D},\infty}^{s}(K)\sim\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{s}(K)

Proof.

In all cases,
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{s}(E)\leq\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D},\infty}^{s}(E)
for any set
E\subset T
. So it suffices to show the other inequality. Let
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}^{s}
denote the
s
-dimensional spherical Hausdorff content (with respect to the CC-distance). Then it suffices to show
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D},\infty}^{s}(K)\lesssim\mathcal{S}_{\infty}^{s}(K)
. Let
\varepsilon>0
be given and find a covering of
K
by balls
\{B(x_{i},r_{i})\}_{i=1}^{M}
with

\sum_{i}(2r_{i})^{s}\leq\mathcal{S}_{\infty}^{s}(K)+\varepsilon.

First assume
r_{i}\leq R^{o}
for all
i
. The other case is trivial and we show it at the end of the proof. By Lemma
4.4
we can cover
B(x_{i},r_{i})\cap T
by tiles
\{T_{i,j}:1\leq j\leq N\}
where
N
is independent of
i
and
\operatorname{diam}T_{i,j}\sim 2r_{i}
. The claim that
N
is independent from
i
from follows
\mathbb{G}
being a doubling metric space. See the proof of
[
9
, Lemma 3.6]
. Since
K\subset T
,
K
is covered by the tiles
\{T_{i,j}\}_{i,j}
with
\operatorname{diam}T_{i,j}\leq 2Cr_{i}
for some fixed
C
. Let
r=\max_{i}r_{i}
. Then since
\operatorname{diam}T_{i,j}\leq 2Cr
for all
i,j
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D},\infty}^{s}(K)\leq\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D},C2r}^{s}(K)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j}(\operatorname{diam}T_{i,j})^{s}

\displaystyle\leq CN\sum_{i}(2r_{i})^{s}

\displaystyle\leq CN(\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{s}(K)+\varepsilon)

and we’re done in the case
r_{i}\leq R^{o}
for all
i
.

Now assume there exists
i
such that
r_{i}\geq R^{o}
. Then obviously
T
is a cover
K
as we already assumed
K\subset T
. So since
R^{o}\leq\operatorname{diam}T\leq 2R^{o}
we get,

\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D},\infty}^{s}(K)\leq(\operatorname{diam}T)^{s}\leq\sum_{i}(2r_{i})^{s}\leq\mathcal{S}_{\infty}^{s}(K)+\varepsilon

which completes the proof.
∎

Capacity and Removability for Campanato Spaces

For Sections
5
and
6
we can without loss of generality assume that any compact

K\subset T
by dilating and translating

.

Define the Campanato space with exponent
\varrho
in
\Omega
, denoted
L_{C}^{\varrho}(\Omega)
, as functions
f\in L_{\text{loc}}^{1}(\Omega)
satisfying,

(5.1)

\sup_{B(x,r)\subset\Omega}\frac{1}{r^{\varrho}}\int_{B(x,r)}\left|f(y)-f_{B(z,r)}\right|\,dy<\infty

or

(5.2)

\sup_{B(x,r)\subset\Omega}\inf_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{r^{\varrho}}\int_{B(x,r)}\left|f(y)-c\right|\,dy<\infty

where
f_{B(z,r)}=r^{-Q}\int_{B(z,r)}f(x)\,dx
.
For

, denote the quantity

as
\|f\|_{*}
. Furthermore, when
\Omega=\mathbb{G}
we set
L_{C}^{\varrho}(\mathbb{G})=L_{C}^{\varrho}.

Remark 5.1
.

Notice that when
\varrho=Q
and
\Omega=\mathbb{G}
, we get the space of functions of bounded mean oscillation (BMO). Hence, the definition of the Campanato space we have above is a slight generalization of BMO.

Now fix a differential operator
\mathcal{L}
. The following definition is basically given by Harvey and Polking in
[

.

Definition 5.2

(Campanato Capacity)
.

The capacity of a compact

with respect to

and

is defined as,

\displaystyle\kappa^{\varrho}(K):=\sup\left\{|\langle\mathcal{L}f,1\rangle|:f\in L_{C}^{\varrho},\|f\|_{*}\leq 1,\supp(\mathcal{L}f)\subset K\right\}.

Above,
\supp(\mathcal{L}f)
refers to the distributional support of
\mathcal{L}f
.

The main result for this section we are after is the following:

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a left-invariant differential operator homogeneous of degree
\lambda\in[1,Q)
such that both
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{t}
are hypoelliptic. For a compact set
K\subset T
and
\varrho\in[\lambda,Q]
we have,

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{\varrho-\lambda}(K)\lesssim\kappa^{\varrho}(K)\lesssim\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{\varrho-\lambda}(K).

Before giving the proof, we will show that a certain function is in
L_{C}^{\varrho}
. For the rest of this section, we will take
\mathcal{L}
to be a left-invariant differential operator homogeneous of degree
\lambda\in[1,Q)
. Since
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{t}
are hypoelliptic, by Theorem

, there exists a kernel of type

, denoted

\Gamma
is homogeneous of degree
\lambda-Q
on
\mathbb{G}\backslash\{0\}
. Moreover,

. The proof below is essentially the same as one of the directions in

6.
Capacity and Removability for Hölder Continuous Functions

Most of the proofs in this section will be almost exactly the same as in the previous section, so we will be light on details here.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{G}
open and
\delta\in(0,1)
. A function
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
belongs to
\text{Lip}_{\delta}(\Omega)
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

|f(x)-f(y)|\leq C\,d(x,y)^{\delta}\quad\text{ for all }x,y\in\Omega.

Let
\|\cdot\|_{\delta}
denote the norm with respect to
\text{Lip}_{\delta}
. Fix a differential operator
\mathcal{L}
.

Definition 6.2

(Hölder Capacity)
.

The capacity of a compact
K\subset\mathbb{G}
with respect to
\text{Lip}_{\delta}
and
\mathcal{L}
is defined as,

\displaystyle\kappa^{\delta}(K):=\sup\{|\langle\mathcal{L}f,1\rangle|:f\in\text{Lip}_{\delta},\|f\|_{\delta}\leq 1,\supp(\mathcal{L}f)\subset K\}.

The main result of this section is the following:

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a left-invariant differential operator homogeneous of degree
\lambda\in[1,Q)
such that both
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{t}
are hypoelliptic. For a compact
K\subset T
and
\delta\in(0,1)
we have,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{Q-\lambda+\delta}(K)\lesssim\kappa^{\delta}(K)\lesssim\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{Q-\lambda+\delta}(K).

Remark 6.4
.

Combining Theorems
5.3
and
6.3
gives another characterization of Hausdorff dimension for compact sets
K
. Take
\mathcal{L}
satisfying the above conditions and for which
\mathcal{L}
homogeneous of degree
1
. By translating and dilating
K
to be a subset of
T
, we can then consider when
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{\beta}(K)=0
for
\beta\in[0,Q)
and find either the Campanato or Hölder capacity which fits this
\beta
. The resulting capacity will then also be null. So if one suspects that the dimension of a set is strictly less than
Q
, then looking at the null sets for this capacity can give another way of finding the dimension of that set.

As before, we will first show
\Gamma\ast\mu\in\text{Lip}_{\delta}
for a Frostman measure
\mu
and
\Gamma
as in Theorem
2.3
. We provide the details of the proof of the following lemma. This proof is taken from
[
9
, Theorem 4.12]
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Fix a compact
K
and
\delta\in(0,1)
. Let
\mu
be a finite measure supported on
K
such that
\mu(B(x,r))\lesssim r^{\varrho-\lambda}
for any ball. Then,

\displaystyle f(x):=\mu(x)\ast\Gamma=\int\Gamma(y^{-1}\cdot x)\,d\mu(y)\in\text{Lip}_{\delta}.

Proof.

Let
x,z\in\mathbb{G}
. Then,

\displaystyle|f(x)-f(z)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\{\|y^{-1}\cdot z\|>2\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|\}}|\Gamma(y^{-1}\cdot x)-\Gamma(y^{-1}\cdot z)|\,d\mu(y)

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,+\int_{\{\|y^{-1}\cdot z\|\leq 2\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|\}}|\Gamma(y^{-1}\cdot x)-\Gamma(y^{-1}\cdot z)|\,d\mu(y)

\displaystyle=:I_{1}+I_{2}.

Since
\Gamma
is homogeneous of degree
\lambda-Q
on
\mathbb{G}\backslash\{0\}
, by
[
11
, Proposition 3.1.40]

(6.1)

|\Gamma(X\cdot Y)-\Gamma(X)|\lesssim\|Y\|\|X\|^{\lambda-Q-1}\quad\text{ for all }\|Y\|\leq\|X\|/2.

Then with
X=y^{-1}\cdot z
and
Y=z^{-1}\cdot x
, equation (
6.1
) gives

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|\int_{\{\|y^{-1}\cdot z\|>2\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|\}}\|y^{-1}\cdot z\|^{\lambda-Q-1}\,d\mu(y)

\displaystyle\leq\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\int_{2^{j}\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|<\|y^{-1}\cdot z\|\leq 2^{j+1}\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|}\|y^{-1}\cdot z\|^{\lambda-Q-1}\,d\mu(y)

\displaystyle\leq\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(2^{j}\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|)^{\lambda-Q-1}\mu(B(z,2^{j+1}\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|))

\displaystyle\sim\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|^{\delta}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{j(\delta-1)}

\displaystyle\sim\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|^{\delta}.

Note that,

\{y\in\mathbb{G}:\|y^{-1}\cdot z\|\leq 2\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|\}\subset\{y\in\mathbb{G}:\|y^{-1}\cdot x\|\leq 3\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|\}.

Hence,

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\|y^{-1}\cdot x\|\leq 3\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|}|\Gamma(y^{-1}\cdot x)|\,d\mu(y)+\int_{\|y^{-1}\cdot z\|\leq 2\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|}|\Gamma(y^{-1}\cdot z)|\,d\mu(y)

\displaystyle=:I_{2_{a}}+I_{2_{b}}.

Then by Proposition
2.9
,

\displaystyle I_{2_{a}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\int_{2^{-j-1}3\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|<\|y^{-1}\cdot x\|\leq 2^{-j}3\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|}\|y^{-1}\cdot x\|^{\lambda-Q}\,d\mu(y)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(2^{-j}3\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|)^{\lambda-Q}\mu(B(z,2^{-j}3\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|))

\displaystyle\lesssim\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|^{\delta}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}2^{-j\delta}

\displaystyle\sim\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|^{\delta}

and similarly,

\displaystyle I_{2_{b}}=\int_{\|y^{-1}\cdot z\|\leq 2\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|}|\Gamma(y^{-1}\cdot z)|\,d\mu(y)\lesssim\|z^{-1}\cdot x\|^{\delta}.

Therefore,
|f(x)-f(z)|\lesssim d(x,z)^{\delta}
which completes the proof.
∎

\Gamma

Fix a left-invariant differential operator
\mathcal{L}
homogeneous of degree
\lambda\in[1,Q)
. We will now find a distributional formula for
\psi\mathcal{L}f\ast\Gamma
similar to what was done in Section
3
. The Poincaré-Birhkoff-Witt Theorem says
\mathcal{L}
is a linear combination of multi-indices
X_{\alpha}
with
|\alpha|=\lambda
, where again, we are using the multi-index notation of (
2.15
). Hence, we can write
\mathcal{L}=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}X_{\alpha}
for some finite index of
\alpha
’s. So for
\psi,\varphi\in C^{\infty}
we have,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\psi\varphi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}X_{\alpha}(\psi\varphi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}(X_{\alpha}\psi)\varphi+\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{B},\beta\neq\overline{\beta}}c_{\alpha}X_{\alpha}^{\beta}(\psi)\mskip 1.0mu\prescript{\smash{\mathrm{*}\mkern-3.0mu}}{}{\mathstrut X}_{\alpha}^{\beta}(\varphi)

(7.2)

\displaystyle=\varphi(\mathcal{L}\psi)+\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{B},\beta\neq\overline{\beta}}\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}X_{\alpha}^{\beta}(\psi)\mskip 1.0mu\prescript{\smash{\mathrm{*}\mkern-3.0mu}}{}{\mathstrut X}_{\alpha}^{\beta}(\varphi).

Notice that for
\beta\neq\overline{\beta}

X_{\alpha}^{\beta}
is a homogeneous operator of degree at most
\lambda-1
. To ease notation, rewrite the second sum of (
7.2

\displaystyle\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{B},\beta\neq\overline{\beta}}c_{\alpha}X_{\alpha}^{\beta}(\psi)\mskip 1.0mu\prescript{\smash{\mathrm{*}\mkern-3.0mu}}{}{\mathstrut X}_{\alpha}^{\beta}(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\eta}P_{\eta}(\psi)Q_{\eta}(\varphi)

where
P_{\eta}

Q_{\eta}
are differential operators and
\eta
runs through some finite indexing set. Now we will come up a distributional formula in the style of Harvey-Polking for objects of the form
\psi\mathcal{L}f

\Omega\subset\mathbb{G}

\mathcal{L}f\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)

\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)

\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\langle\psi\mathcal{L}f,\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle f,\psi\mathcal{L}^{t}\phi\rangle+\sum_{\eta}\langle f,P_{\eta}^{t}(\phi)Q_{\eta}^{t}(\psi)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{L}(\psi f),\phi\rangle+\sum_{\eta}\langle P_{\eta}(fQ_{\eta}^{t}(\psi)),\phi\rangle

which means we have the distributional formula of,

(7.3)

\psi\mathcal{L}f=\mathcal{L}(\psi f)+\sum_{\eta}P_{\eta}(fQ_{\eta}^{t}(\psi)).

By

\mathcal{L}(\psi f)\ast\Gamma=\psi f

\psi\mathcal{L}f\ast\Gamma=\psi f+\sum_{\eta}P_{\eta}(fQ_{\eta}^{t}(\psi))\ast\Gamma

Now notice that by (

P_{\eta}
is a composition of left-invariant vector fields. So by iterating the formula on
[

we can rewrite (

(7.5)

\psi\mathcal{L}f\ast\Gamma=\psi f+\sum_{\eta}fQ_{\eta}^{t}\psi\ast\widetilde{P_{\eta}}\Gamma

where
\widetilde{P_{\eta}}
is a composition of right-invariant vector fields. Furthermore, it is straightforward to check that
\widetilde{P_{\eta}}
has the same homogeneity degree as
P_{\eta}

\widetilde{P_{\eta}}
is homogeneous of degree at most
\lambda-1
.

Theorem 7.2

(Removability Characterization for
L^{p}_{\text{loc}}
)
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
. Fix
\lambda\in[1,Q)
and let
\mathcal{L}
be
\lambda
-homogeneous, left-invariant linear differential operator on
\mathbb{G}
such that both
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{t}
are hypoelliptic. A compact
K\subset\mathbb{G}
is removable for
L^{p}_{\text{loc}}

\mathcal{L}
-solutions if and only if
\kappa^{p}(K)=0
.

Proof.

As before, the direction of removable implying
\kappa^{p}(K)=0
is trivial. So assume
\kappa^{p}(K)=0
. Take any
f\in L^{p}_{\text{loc}}
with
\supp(\mathcal{L}f)\subset K
. Take any
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}
. Since
\Gamma
is the fundamental solution to
\mathcal{L}
, left-invariance of
\mathcal{L}
implies

\displaystyle|\langle\mathcal{L}f,\psi\rangle|

\displaystyle=|\langle\psi\mathcal{L}f,1\rangle|

\displaystyle=\left|\langle\mathcal{L}(\psi\mathcal{L}f\ast\Gamma),1\rangle\right|.

It suffices to show
\psi\mathcal{L}f\ast\Gamma\in L^{p}_{\text{loc}}
. Indeed, first notice
\supp(\mathcal{L}(\psi\mathcal{L}f\ast\Gamma))=\supp(\psi\mathcal{L}f)\subset K
. Hence, for
A:=\|\psi\mathcal{L}f\ast\Gamma\|_{L^{p}(K)}
, if we can show
\psi\mathcal{L}f\ast\Gamma\in L_{\text{loc}}^{p}
then the assumption
\kappa^{p}(K)=0
implies

\displaystyle|\langle\mathcal{L}(\psi\mathcal{L}f\ast\Gamma),1\rangle|/A=|\langle\mathcal{L}f,\psi\rangle|/A=0

and we would be done. Recall that (
7.5
) says we have the distributional relation of,

(7.6)

\psi\mathcal{L}f\ast\Gamma=\psi f+\sum_{\eta}fQ_{\eta}^{t}\psi\ast\widetilde{P_{\eta}}\Gamma

where
Q_{\eta}^{t}
and
\widetilde{P_{\eta}}
have the properties previously discussed. We will show each function on the right hand side of (
7.6
) is in
L^{p}_{\text{loc}}
. Clearly
\psi f\in L^{p}_{\text{loc}}
. Now fix
\eta
and consider
fQ_{\eta}^{t}\psi\ast\widetilde{P_{\eta}}\Gamma
. By (
7.1
),
\widetilde{P_{\eta}}\Gamma\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}
, and clearly
fQ_{\eta}^{t}\psi\in L^{p}_{\text{loc}}
. So Young’s inequality says
fQ_{\eta}^{t}\psi\ast\widetilde{P_{\eta}}\Gamma\in L^{p}_{\text{loc}}
, which completes the proof.
