Impact of antithrombotic therapy on outcomes of incidental unruptured intracranial aneurysms in patients with ischemic stroke
Authors: Chao Chen, Yiya Xu, Yingchao He, Ting Chen, Xingyong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe coexistence of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) and unruptured intracranial aneurysms (UIAs) is relatively common, but the safety of antithrombotic therapy in this population remains uncertain. This study evaluated whether different secondary prevention regimens influence UIA progression or rupture risk.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study included patients with AIS and concomitant saccular UIAs between January 2016 and December 2021. Patients were categorized according to long-term antithrombotic exposure: single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT), anticoagulation, or no regular therapy. Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) was analyzed separately. The primary outcome was aneurysm rupture; secondary outcomes included symptomatic events and radiographic progression (≥1 mm growth).ResultsAmong 197 patients (mean age 68.4 ± 10.2 years; 53.8% female), 140 received SAPT, 36 received anticoagulation, and 21 received no regular therapy. During a mean follow-up of 28.6 ± 18.4 months, aneurysm rupture occurred in two patients (1.0%). No ruptures occurred in the anticoagulation group. Firth’s penalized logistic regression showed no significant association between antithrombotic regimen and aneurysm rupture (anticoagulation vs SAPT: OR 0.41, 95% CI 0.01-5.21; no therapy vs SAPT: OR 3.21, 95% CI 0.21-45.6; both p > 0.05).ConclusionsIn patients with small incidental saccular UIAs and AIS, standard secondary prevention regimens were not associated with an increased risk of aneurysm rupture. However, the limited number of rupture events and potential residual confounding warrant cautious interpretation. Secondary stroke prevention should remain the priority, with individualized assessment of aneurysm-related risks.

Introduction

Ischemic stroke and unruptured intracranial aneurysms (UIAs) often coexist due to shared risk factors including hypertension, atherosclerosis and smoking [1,2]. Although secondary stroke prevention typically requires antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy, concerns persist that these treatments may increase aneurysm rupture risk, particularly with dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) [3,4]. Current guidelines list UIAs as relative contraindications for thrombolysis [3,5], though emerging evidence suggests this risk may be lower than previously thought [6–8]. The management of stroke patients with incidental UIAs presents a clinical dilemma of balancing the need to prevent recurrent stroke against the potential risk of inducing aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH). Real-world data evaluating different prevention regimens, including single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT), DAPT and anticoagulation, on UIA natural history remain limited [9,10]. This retrospective cohort study investigates whether antithrombotic therapy influences UIA rupture risk, aiming to provide evidence for secondary prevention in this population.

Methods

Study design and participants

This single-center retrospective cohort study reviewed records of patients diagnosed with both acute ischemic stroke (AIS) and UIAs from January 2016 to December 2021 at Fujian Provincial Hospital. AIS was diagnosed according to the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute Ischemic Stroke [11]. UIAs were identified through initial screening of radiology reports from digital subtraction angiography (DSA), computed tomography angiography (CTA), or magnetic resonance angiography (MRA). All positive or equivocal findings were then independently confirmed by two senior neuro-interventionists (with >10 years of experience) who reviewed the source images. This two-step process balanced feasibility with diagnostic accuracy.

The study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Fujian Provincial Hospital. All procedures performed in this study were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. The data analysis for this retrospective study began in 12th November 2025, following approval by the Ethics Committee.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients were included if they met the following criteria: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) confirmed diagnosis of AIS; (3) presence of one or more saccular UIAs confirmed by cerebral angiography; (4) availability of at least 6 months of follow-up data post-stroke.

Patients were excluded for: (1) non-saccular aneurysms; (2) coexistence of other cerebrovascular malformations such as arteriovenous malformation, moyamoya disease, or cerebral amyloid angiopathy; (3) aneurysmal SAH at admission; (4) incomplete data; (5) loss to follow-up.

Data collection and definitions

Baseline data included demographics, vascular risk factors, TOAST-classified stroke etiology [12], admission NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score [13] and infarction location. Aneurysm characteristics included location, size and morphology. For patients with multiple aneurysms, the largest aneurysm was used for size and location analysis.

For outcome analysis, patients were categorized based on their predominant antithrombotic regimen during follow-up. According to medical records at discharge and follow-up information, most patients remained on a consistent long-term regimen; however, 23 patients switched from antiplatelet therapy to anticoagulation (8 with short-term DAPT bridging, 15 on SAPT; antiplatelet duration 3–26 days) and 2 patients switched from anticoagulation to antiplatelet therapy due to bleeding events (anticoagulation duration 33 and 16 days). For patients who switched regimens, we assigned them to the treatment group based on the regimen with the longer cumulative duration of exposure. Since the subsequent therapy was maintained for a substantially longer period than the initial regimen, this classification reflects the patient’s predominant exposure and is therefore methodologically justified. Based on this approach, patients were classified into three groups: (1) long-term SAPT (n = 140, including aspirin n = 92, clopidogrel n = 36, cilostazol n = 2); (2) long-term anticoagulation (n = 36, including DOACs: n = 33 and warfarin: n = 3); (3) no regular therapy (never treated or <2 consecutive weeks, n = 21). Short-term DAPT was administered to 66 patients (33.5%) as a bridging strategy (mean duration 25.2 ± 8.4 days) and was analyzed separately as a time-varying exposure rather than as a primary group assignment.

Primary outcome was defined as aneurysm rupture (confirmed aSAH). Secondary outcomes included new attributable symptoms (defined in Sec. 2.3), radiographic progression (≥1 mm growth in diameter), all-cause mortality, recurrent ischemic stroke and aneurysm intervention. New attributable symptoms were defined as the new onset or acute worsening of any of the following neurological signs occurring at least 48 h after the index stroke, after excluding new cerebral infarction or hemorrhagic transformation by brain imaging (CT or MRI). A symptom was considered attributable only if concurrent vascular imaging (CTA or MRA) showed either aneurysm enlargement (≥1 mm), new morphological changes (e.g. daughter sac and wall irregularity), or perianeurysmal edema. All potential cases were adjudicated by two independent investigators blinded to treatment assignment.

Follow-up

Patients discharged between January 2016 and December 2021 were screened via the electronic medical record system starting in May 2022. Time zero (start of follow-up) was defined as the date of hospital discharge. Follow-up was conducted through medical record review (inpatient and outpatient), structured telephone interviews and outpatient clinic visits by well-trained neurologists. All follow-up data collection was completed by August 2022. The minimum required follow-up duration was 6 months, and the censoring date was set at 31 December 2022 (administrative censoring). Patients who died were censored at the date of death, and those who underwent aneurysm intervention (endovascular coiling or surgical clipping) were censored at the date of intervention. Data analysis was performed in 2025.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. Exact 95% confidence intervals (Clopper–Pearson method) were calculated for all event rates. Absolute risk differences (ARD) with 95% CIs (Newcombe method) were calculated for pairwise treatment comparisons. For the primary outcome, we applied Firth’s penalized likelihood logistic regression to reduce small-sample bias. The model included three prespecified covariates including treatment group, aneurysm size (>7 mm versus ≤7 mm) and location (posterior versus anterior circulation). Results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Given the small number of events, this analysis is exploratory. For secondary outcomes (attributable symptoms, radiographic progression), only descriptive statistics are reported. Kaplan–Meier analysis was not performed due to non-systematic follow-up imaging for growth assessment. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 and SAS 9.4 (PROC LOGISTIC with FIRTH option).

Results

Study population

A total of 248 patients with incidental saccular UIAs were identified by screening from 2930 patients with AIS admitted to the Department of Neurology during the study period. After applying exclusion criteria, 197 patients were included in the final analysis. Reasons for exclusion were loss to follow-up (n = 36), incomplete clinical or imaging data (n = 11) and presence of other cerebrovascular malformations (n = 5). Long-term antithrombotic regimens

The majority of patients received guideline-recommended secondary prevention for ischemic stroke. During follow-up, 140 patients (71.1%) were maintained on long-term single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT), with either aspirin (n = 92, 65.7%), clopidogrel (n = 36, 25.7%), or cilostazol (n = 2, 1.4%). Anticoagulation was used in 36 patients (18.3%), with either warfarin (n = 3, 8.3%) or rivaroxaban (n = 33, 91.7%), for cardioembolic stroke caused by atrial fibrillation. 21 patients (10.7%) did not receive any regular antithrombotic therapy during follow-up (never treated or <2 weeks, excluding those discontinuing due to endpoint events). Reasons for absence or discontinuation of antithrombotic therapy were poor compliance (n = 6) and bleeding contraindications (n = 15), including hemorrhagic transformation (n = 4), gastrointestinal bleeding (n = 5), oral or nasal bleeding (n = 3), hemorrhoidal bleeding (n = 3). 66 patients (33.5%) received DAPT as a short-term bridging strategy, with mean duration of 25.2 ± 8.4 days. Subsequently, 55 (83.3%) transitioned to SAPT, 8 (12.1%) to anticoagulation and 3 (4.5%) discontinued therapy. No patients remained on long-term DAPT.

Anticoagulation patients were older (74.1 versus 68.2 years, p < 0.001), had higher rates of atrial fibrillation (55.6% versus 3.6%, p < 0.001) and cardioembolic stroke (55.6% versus 6.4%, p < 0.001). Anticoagulation patients also had larger aneurysms (4.9 versus 3.8 mm, p = 0.02) and more posterior circulation aneurysms (22.2% versus 8.6%, p = 0.03). All included aneurysms were saccular (100%). Primary outcome

Over a mean follow-up of 28.6 ± 18.4 months, the primary outcome of aneurysm rupture occurred in two patients (1.0%; 95% exact CI, 0.1%–3.6%). By treatment group, the rupture rate was 0.7% (95% CI, 0.02%–4.1%) in the SAPT group (one patient with a 4.5 mm posterior communicating artery aneurysm at 8 months post-stroke), 0% (95% CI, 0%–9.7%) in the anticoagulation group, and 4.8% (95% CI, 0.1%–23.8%) in the no-therapy group (one patient with a 4.2 mm anterior communicating artery aneurysm at 1 month post-stroke, in whom antithrombotic therapy was withheld due to massive stroke with high risk of hemorrhagic transformation).

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes of new attributable symptoms occurred in six patients (3.0%; 95% exact CI, 1.1%–6.5%). Among these, five presented with new-onset headache localized to the aneurysm site with imaging evidence of aneurysm enlargement (three in SAPT group, one in anticoagulant and one in the no-therapy), and one in SAPT group presented with oculomotor nerve palsy attributed to a growing posterior communicating artery aneurysm. 103 of 197 patients (52.3%) underwent at least one follow-up CTA or MRA. The median time to first follow-up imaging was 14 months (IQR 8–22 months). Imaging was performed at clinician discretion rather than a standardized protocol, which revealed one asymptomatic aneurysm growth (≥1 mm enlargement in diameter) in the SAPT group.

Regarding follow-up imaging, 103 of 197 patients (52.3%) underwent at least one follow-up CTA or MRA during the study period. The median time to first follow-up imaging was 14 months (IQR 8–22 months). Imaging was performed at clinician discretion rather than under a standardized protocol. Asymptomatic aneurysm growth (≥1 mm diameter increase) was detected in one patient in the SAPT group.

Aneurysm interventions

All two patients who experienced rupture and five of the six patients with new attributable symptoms underwent successful endovascular coiling. The remaining one patient with new attributable headache in the SAPT group declined intervention based on personal willingness. The single patient with asymptomatic radiographic progression also underwent successful coiling.

In addition, three patients who did not meet any endpoint events underwent elective aneurysm intervention (endovascular coiling or stent-assisted embolization) based on clinical judgment. Overall, the intervention rates were 5.0% (7/140) in the SAPT group, 5.6% (2/36) in the anticoagulation group and 9.5% (2/21) in the no-therapy group.

Stroke recurrence and mortality

Recurrent ischemic stroke occurred in 30 patients (15.2%), similarly distributed across treatment groups. All-cause mortality was 9.6% (19 patients), with causes including heart failure (n = 7), infection (n = 6), malignancy (n = 4) and other causes (n = 2).

Impact of dual antiplatelet therapy as a bridging strategy

To evaluate whether exposure to DAPT as a bridging strategy influenced aneurysm outcomes, patients who received DAPT during the acute phase and those who did not were compared. As shown in Table 3, aneurysm rupture occurred in 0 of 66 DAPT-exposed patients (0%; 95% CI, 0%–5.4%) and 2 of 131 non-exposed patients (1.5%; 95% CI, 0.2%–5.4%) (p > 0.99). New attributable symptoms occurred in two DAPT-exposed patients (3.0%) and three non-exposed patients (2.3%), while radiographic progression was detected in one DAPT-exposed patient (1.5%) and zero non-exposed patients (0%). Of note, all secondary events in the DAPT-exposed group occurred after DAPT had been discontinued (during subsequent SAPT or no therapy), not during active DAPT treatment. No inferential statistics were performed for secondary outcomes due to small numbers and non-systematic follow-up imaging.

Factors Associated with aneurysm-related endpoint events

Given the small number of rupture events (n = 2), Firth’s penalized likelihood logistic regression was performed as an exploratory analysis. The model included three prespecified covariates: treatment group, aneurysm size (>7 mm versus ≤7 mm) and location (posterior versus anterior circulation).

As shown in Table 5, no significant association was found between antithrombotic regimen and aneurysm rupture. The odds ratios for anticoagulation (OR 0.41, 95% CI 0.01–5.21) and no therapy (OR 3.21, 95% CI 0.21–45.6) compared to SAPT were highly imprecise, with wide confidence intervals crossing 1.0. Larger aneurysm size (>7 mm) showed a non-significant trend toward increased risk (OR 2.85, 95% CI 0.21–38.2). Discussion

This study demonstrates that in patients with AIS and incidental saccular UIAs, the overall risk of aneurysm rupture over long-term follow-up is low (1.0%), and no statistically significant differences were observed across different antithrombotic regimens. This study adds to the growing body of observational evidence evaluating antithrombotic therapy in this population [9,10].

Several findings warrant discussion

First, UIA prevalence in our cohort (6.7%) accorded with prior reports (6.0%–7.0%) [1,10,14], exceeding the 3% in general population [15]. This elevated prevalence likely reflects shared underlying mechanisms such as hypertension, atherosclerosis and smoking. Hypertension promotes both atherosclerosis and aneurysm formation through hemodynamic stress on vessel walls, while smoking contributes to endothelial dysfunction and vascular inflammation, ultimately leading to vessel wall injury and aneurysm development [16]. The high prevalence of hypertension (89.8%) and smoking history (31.2%) in our cohort supports this mechanistic link.

Second, the low incidence of UIA rupture and progression across treatment groups suggests that antithrombotic therapy played no determining role in these events. This finding aligns with the fundamental mechanism of aneurysm rupture, which is primarily driven by vessel wall integrity and hemodynamic stress rather than systemic hemostatic function [17,18]. This is further supported by the comparable outcomes observed in SAPT and anticoagulant groups, suggesting that even profound coagulation inhibition does not affect aneurysmal wall stability. It is noted that, beyond the absence of harm, certain antiplatelet agents, particularly aspirin, may confer protective effects on UIAs through anti-inflammatory mechanisms. The International Study of Unruptured Intracranial Aneurysms (ISUIA) cohort showed that frequent aspirin use was associated with significantly reduced risk of aneurysm rupture [19]. The lack of a detectable protective effect in our cohort may reflect population differences. The ISUIA cohort included UIAs patients without stroke, whereas all patients in our cohort experienced an AIS, placing them in a state of intense systemic inflammation that may have masked the relatively subtle anti-inflammatory effects of aspirin [19–21]. Additionally, some SAPT patients received clopidogrel, which has weaker anti-inflammatory properties than aspirin [22], potentially diluting any protective benefit.

Third, upon careful review of all 21 patients not receiving regular antithrombotic therapy, we found no evidence that the presence of a UIA itself led to withholding of therapy. The reasons for not using antithrombotics were exclusively bleeding contraindications (n = 15) or poor compliance (n = 6), not physician concern about aneurysm rupture risk.

Finally, regarding the safety of IV thrombolysis, twenty-four patients (12.2%) in our cohort received IV thrombolysis with no aneurysm-related events. This result is consistent with previous larger scale studies of a similar patient population, providing substantial evidence of safety of intravenous thrombolysis in AIS concomitant with saccular UIA [6,8,23]. More importantly, all of these thrombolyzed patients subsequently received long-term antithrombotic therapy (SAPT or anticoagulation) as secondary prevention, and none experienced aneurysm rupture during follow-up. This observation extends existing evidence by suggesting that a history of IV thrombolysis does not identify patients at increased risk of UIA rupture from subsequent long-term antithrombotic therapy. Nevertheless, current guidelines list UIAs as a relative contraindication for thrombolysis [5,24], reflecting the precautionary principle in acute stroke care. We concur that thrombolysis decisions should be individualized, weighing expected benefit against theoretical risk [24,25].

Our findings have several important clinical implications. First, they provide evidence that the presence of a small incidental UIA should not preclude guideline-recommended long-term antithrombotic therapy for secondary stroke prevention, as these medications do not significantly increase the risk of aneurysm rupture in AIS population. Second, for patients with minor stroke or high-risk TIA who require short-term DAPT [3], this short-term intensive antiplatelet strategy does not confer additional aneurysm-related risk. This is particularly important because DAPT is increasingly used in this population, yet prior safety data regarding UIAs in this context were lacking. Third, for patients with cardioembolic stroke requiring long-term anticoagulation, our study provides the first direct evidence that continuing anticoagulation is not associated with excess aneurysm-related risk compared to SAPT. This supports the continuation of indicated therapy rather than withholding it due to UIA presence, aligning with recent guidelines. Fourth, our subgroup analyses demonstrating consistent findings across aneurysm sizes (<5 and ≥5 mm) and locations (anterior and posterior circulation) suggest that these baseline characteristics do not modify the safety profile of antithrombotic therapy. While larger aneurysms (>7 mm) showed a non-significant trend toward increased risk, this likely reflects natural history rather than treatment effect, and such patients may warrant closer imaging surveillance rather than therapy modification. Taken together, these findings imply that for stroke patients with incidental small UIAs, priority should be given to optimizing secondary stroke prevention based on individual stroke recurrence risk, rather than withholding therapy based on theoretical but unproven aneurysm-related risks.

Several limitations warrant consideration

The retrospective single-center design of the study introduces inherent selection and information biases, and the modest sample size, particularly for the no-therapy and anticoagulation groups, limits statistical power to detect small differences in rare events. Additionally, non-protocolized follow-up imaging may have missed asymptomatic aneurysm growth, and treatment decisions at physician discretion introduce potential confounding by indication that multivariable analysis cannot fully exclude. The small number of events precluded any meaningful subgroup analyses, and our findings should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Finally, our findings apply only to saccular aneurysms. Fusiform, blister and dissecting aneurysms have different pathophysiologies and risk profiles, and our results should not be extrapolated to these entities.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort of patients with mostly small saccular UIAs, aneurysm rupture was uncommon, and no statistically significant differences were observed across different antithrombotic regimens (SAPT, anticoagulation, or no therapy) or with short-term DAPT exposure. However, estimates were imprecise due to the small number of events, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. The decision to use antithrombotic therapy for secondary stroke prevention in patients with incidental small saccular UIAs should be individualized, weighing the benefits of stroke recurrence reduction against the theoretical and unproven risks to the aneurysm.
