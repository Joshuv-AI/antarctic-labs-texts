Self-cascade oxygen-generating hydrogel/nanofibrous membrane composites restore periodontal microenvironment homeostasis
Authors: Ye Liang, Fan Yang, Jun Kang, Zijun Chen, Ting Zou, Shaohua Ge, Chengfei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe hypoxic environment of deep periodontal pockets favors the thriving of anaerobic microbes, leading to dysbiosis, tissue destruction, and treatment resistance. Elevating intra-pocket oxygen tension is a fundamental yet unmet need for controlling anaerobic pathogens in deep periodontal pockets. Our previous study demonstrated the therapeutic efficacy of oxygen-releasing hydrogel based on calcium peroxide (CPO) in reducing anaerobic pathogens in vitro. However, the rapid hydrolysis of unprotected CPO in an aqueous system and the potential oxidative damage induced by the intermediate hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) limit its clinical applicability. To address these issues, we developed a dual-layer, self-cascade oxygen-generating composite (PCA-GC) to sustain topical oxygen enrichment in deep periodontal pockets. The inner layer consists of a hydrophobic polycaprolactone (PCL) nanofibrous membrane encapsulating CPO (designated PCA) to achieve long-term stability and controlled hydrolysis. Surrounding this core, a flexible gelatin methacryloyl-cerium oxide hydrogel (GC) served as a protective outer layer to decompose self-generated H2O2 via its catalase-like activity, thereby preventing H2O2 accumulation and shielding adjacent tissues. Our results showed that PCA-GC sustained oxygen release for up to 5 days, demonstrating satisfactory biocompatibility with human gingival fibroblasts and periodontal ligament cells. Furthermore, PCA-GC significantly alleviated hypoxia in multispecies biofilms and effectively eradicated periodontal pathogens, including Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) and Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum). In multispecies biofilms, the proportion of P. gingivalis decreased significantly from 84.42% to 0.05%, while the commensal Streptococcus gordonii increased from 11.18% to 99.01%, reflecting a shift from a pathogen-dominated to a commensal-dominated microbial community. Additionally, PCA-GC enhanced pathogen-clearance function in macrophages under hypoxia by increasing reactive oxygen species and nitric oxide production. In rat experimental periodontitis models, PCA-GC decreased hypoxia marker expression, reduced pathogen burden and alveolar bone loss, suppressed osteoclast activity, and attenuated inflammatory response. Collectively, sustained oxygen therapy via PCA-GC effectively remodels the periodontal microenvironment, suppresses pathogenic dysbiosis, and enhances host antimicrobial responses, underscoring its potential as an adjunctive strategy for treating periodontitis.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04835-y.Keywords: Biofilm, Calcium peroxide, Dysbiosis, Infection control, Inflammation, Periodontitis

Introduction

Deep periodontal pockets and extensive bone loss are the key clinical features of severe periodontitis [1, 2]. Current evidence suggests that deep pockets and periodontal dysbiosis coexist, stemming from the hypoxic microenvironment with an average oxygen tension of 1.8% at the base of untreated pockets [3, 4]. Conventional treatments, such as scaling, root planing (SRP), and antimicrobial agents, can hardly eliminate deep periodontal pockets, with a limited reduction of 1.29 mm to 2.2 mm [5, 6]. Consequently, the persisting pocket and its hypoxia would facilitate the proliferation, rapid recolonization, and biofilm formation of anaerobic pathogens, contributing to the recurrence of periodontitis and progressive periodontal tissue destruction [7]. While periodontal surgery can reduce pocket depth and improve clinical parameters, its predictability varies across lesion types [8]. Furthermore, it does not prevent the re-establishment of anaerobic conditions during healing, particularly for patients with diabetes or smoking habits whose vascular change restricts topical oxygen supply [9]. Accordingly, localized oxygen-modulating biomaterials may offer complementary benefits by stabilizing the subgingival microenvironment beyond what mechanical or surgical debridement can accomplish.

The beneficial effects of oxygen therapy have been demonstrated by hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT). HBOT has been shown to improve bleeding indices and induce favorable microbial shifts when combined with SRP [10, 11]. More recently, an oxygen-releasing oral gel containing sodium perborate has been used to treat periodontal and peri-implant diseases, with reported clinical benefits [12]. Nonetheless, sodium perborate exhibits suboptimal activation at physiological temperature, resulting in insufficient oxygen release within the oral cavity [13]. In contrast, our previous study demonstrated that calcium peroxide (CPO), by readily hydrolyzing to generate oxygen under physiological conditions, effectively enriched local oxygen levels and suppressed Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) [14]. However, this earlier oxygen-delivery system, based on direct encapsulation of unmodified CPO within a hydrogel matrix, faces two major challenges that restrict its clinical translation. On the one hand, unmodified CPO particles undergo rapid hydrolysis upon mixing with the hydrogel, necessitating fresh preparation prior to use and leading to poor storage stability. Moreover, this premature and uncontrolled hydrolysis leads to early depletion of the oxygen source, limiting sustained oxygen release to less than 48 h even at high loading concentrations (15%) [14]. On the other hand, the intermediate byproduct of CPO decomposition is hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). While H2O2 possesses antimicrobial properties, its unbuffered accumulation can pose a severe risk of oxidative damage over prolonged exposure. Given that reactive oxygen species (ROS) overproduction is a primary driver of periodontitis progression [15], the application of CPO could further exacerbate oxidative stress, aggravating tissue destruction. Therefore, advanced engineering is required to decouple sustained oxygen release from H2O2-mediated toxicity.

To date, no periodontal oxygen-delivery platform has achieved prolonged, controlled oxygen release while actively mitigating the associated H2O2-mediated oxidative stress, a critical gap the present study seeks to address. First, to prevent premature hydrolysis and achieve controlled oxygen release, hydrophobic polymers have emerged as promising carriers for CPO particles, typically formulated as nanofibrous membranes or microspheres. In addition, thin films such as the marketed Periochip®, can be easily inserted into periodontal pockets with minimal patient discomfort [16], demonstrating the feasibility of membranes as a topical delivery platform for periodontitis. Accordingly, polycaprolactone (PCL), an FDA-approved, hydrophobic, biocompatible, and biodegradable polymer, was employed to homogeneously incorporate CPO particles via electrospinning, yielding the PCA membrane. Upon insertion into the periodontal pocket, CPO located on the nanofiber surface undergoes hydrolysis to generate H2O2, which gradually decomposes into oxygen, producing an immediate oxygenation effect. During this initial stage, additional CPO remains sequestered within the hydrophobic PCL matrix, where restricted water ingress allows gradual hydrolysis, resulting in sustained oxygen release over an extended period [17–19]. To address the second challenge, the risk of oxidative damage was mitigated by introducing nanozymes with catalase-mimicking activity to convert H2O2 into oxygen, thereby amplifying oxygen delivery while minimizing cytotoxicity. Among various nanozymes, cerium oxide (CeO2) nanoparticles could alleviate oxidative stress through a self-regenerating redox cycle between Ce3+ and Ce4+ oxidation states and have been widely employed owing to their exceptional stability and cost-effectiveness [20]. Additionally, hydrogels, with their excellent water retention and flexibility, conform to irregular periodontal pocket geometries and provide a protective interface for adjacent periodontal tissues. Accordingly, gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogel was used to embed CeO2 nanoparticles (hereafter designated as GC), serving as the outer layer adjacent to gingival tissues. Mechanistically, the dual-layer design functions as a coordinated antibacterial-cytoprotective system. The CPO-containing inner layer acts as an antimicrobial functional layer, generating H2O2 and oxygen to combat pathogens, while the CeO2-enriched hydrogel outer layer serves as a protective shield, converting excessive H2O2 into oxygen before it reaches periodontal tissues, thus preserving host compatibility (Fig. 1A).

In the present study, we systematically investigated the oxygen-releasing profiles, antibacterial and antibiofilm efficacy against multispecies biofilms, and in vivo therapeutic outcomes of this dual-layer oxygen-generating composite, aiming to remodel the periodontal microenvironment and provide a translationally viable adjunctive therapy for severe periodontitis.

Materials and methods

Materials and experimental details are provided in the Supplementary material.

Evaluation of therapeutic efficacy of PCA-GC in a rat experimental periodontitis model

Twenty-four of six- to eight-week-old male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were purchased from Zhuhai Bestest Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Zhuhai, China) and maintained at Shenzhen Glorybay Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China). All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of Shenzhen Glorybay Biotech (Protocol No. RW-IACUC-25-0003) and conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals (NIH Publication No. 8023, revised 1978). All animals were maintained under clean-grade conditions. The animal room was maintained at a temperature of 22 ± 2 ℃, relative humidity of 50–60%, with a 12-h light/dark cycle.

While a formal a priori statistical power calculation was not performed prior to the in vivo study, the initial sample size (n = 6 animals per group) was determined based on historically validated effect sizes from comparable experimental periodontitis models [21, 22]. Crucially, this group size was strategically selected to account for a potential 10–20% attrition rate due to surgical or anesthetic complications, ensuring a sufficient final viable sample size for robust evaluation. To further validate this rationale, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted based on our primary quantitative outcome (i.e., Micro-CT evaluation of alveolar bone loss). The calculation confirmed that the utilized sample size yielded a statistical power exceeding 99.9% (α = 0.05), demonstrating adequate sensitivity to detect significant therapeutic differences.

Eighteen rats were randomly allocated to establish periodontitis, and the Healthy group (n = 6) did not receive any treatment. Experimental periodontitis was induced by placing a 3 − 0 silk ligature (Johnson & Johnson, New Jersey, USA) around the cervical area of the maxillary second molar under isoflurane anesthesia (RWD, Sugar Land, USA; 5% for induction, 2% for maintenance). Mixed bacterial suspensions of Streptococcus gordonii (S. gordonii, OD660 = 0.166–0.170), P. gingivalis (OD660 = 0.271–0.279), and Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum, OD660 = 0.271–0.279) were inoculated (50 µL) into the gingival sulcus around the ligated molar every 2 days for 2 weeks. Ligatures were removed after confirmation of successful periodontitis induction, indicated by gingival bleeding, redness/swelling, and increased probing depth. Body weight and probing pocket depth (PPD) were recorded in all periodontitis-induced rats before treatment.

After a 2-week induction period, periodontitis was successfully established in all eighteen rats. The rats in different groups were housed in separate cages to minimize potential cross-contamination of oral pathogens between groups and randomly assigned to one of three treatment groups (n = 6 each): phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), PCL-GC, and PCA-GC. To demonstrate the feasibility of membrane insertion into periodontal pockets, the maxilla was stained with eosin, while the nanofibrous membrane was stained with hematoxylin to enable clear visualization of membrane placement (Fig. S1). During the treatment period, PCL or PCA membranes (0.25 mm²) were inserted into the mesial and distal periodontal pockets of the maxillary second molar gently without inducing bleeding, followed by injection of 20 µL gelatin composite along the outer surface of nanofibrous membranes (GC, containing 20 mg/mL CeO₂) using a microsyringe. Then the hydrogel/nanofibrous membrane composite was cross-linked in situ with a dental curing light. For the PBS group, 20 µL PBS was applied instead of the composite. The hydrogel/nanofibrous membrane composites were administered twice a week for 2 weeks. Before inserting the new PCL/PCA membrane, the remaining older one in the periodontal pocket was displaced. And the following administration procedure was the same as mentioned above. After 2 weeks of treatment, blood was collected for hematological analysis (red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets). Then, all rats were euthanized by overdose anesthesia, and body weight, PPD, and gingival index (GI) were evaluated. PPD and gingival conditions of six sites around the maxillary second molar were examined using a probe after removing the remaining membrane. GI scoring criteria were as follows: 0 = normal gingiva; 1 = mild inflammation, mild swelling without bleeding; 2 = moderate inflammation with bleeding on probing; 3 = severe inflammation with spontaneous bleeding.

The maxillae (containing the three molars), heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Biosharp, China) for 24 h, then stored in 70% ethanol at 4 ℃. Maxillae were scanned with a µCT 45 desktop scanner (Scanco Medical, Switzerland; 70 kV, 114 µA) to evaluate alveolar bone loss. Three-dimensional images were reconstructed with the occlusal plane not visible from the buccal side, and cervical width standardized across samples. The distance between the cementoenamel junction and alveolar bone crest (CEJ-ABC) was measured using ImageJ to quantify bone loss. Samples were then demineralized in 10% disodium EDTA for about 2 months, dehydrated in graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Section (5 μm) were prepared for histology. Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining was used to evaluate gingival and organ morphology. Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining (Sigma-Aldrich) was performed to assess osteoclasts. Immunohistochemistry was conducted with primary antibodies against hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-1α (GB151339, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α (ab307164, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and IL-1β (ab315084, Abcam). To eliminate observer bias, all histological and immunohistochemical evaluations were conducted in a blinded manner. The Micro-CT images and tissue sections were digitally scanned and coded by an independent investigator who was not involved in the surgical procedures or treatment administration. Subsequent histological analysis, morphological evaluations, and quantitative analyses were performed by two experienced researchers who were completely unaware of the group assignments.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative experiments were performed at least three times. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Inc., San Diego, USA). Prior to parametric testing, the normality of the data distribution was confirmed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. The differences between two independent groups were analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. To analyze differences among multiple groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed after verifying the homogeneity of variance. To correct for multiple comparisons, Dunnett’s post hoc test was specifically employed to compare the mean of each individual experimental group against the single control group, thereby controlling the family-wise error rate. Statistically significant values include *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Results

Fabrication and characterization of hydrogel/nanofibrous membrane composites

First, the morphology and physicochemical properties of the synthesized CPO nanoparticles were comprehensively evaluated. As illustrated in the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Fig. S2A, B), the CPO nanoparticles exhibited uniform quasi-spherical morphology. However, owing to their high surface energy and nanoscale dimensions, these primary nanoparticles naturally tended to aggregate into larger clusters. As shown in Fig. S2C, the hydrodynamic diameter (Z-average) of the CPO nanoparticles in anhydrous ethanol was found to be 309.7 nm with a Polydispersity Index of 0.24, indicating that the small primary nanoparticles assemble into stable nanoscale aggregates when suspended in an organic solution. Furthermore, the zeta potential of the CPO nanoparticles was approximately − 1.22 mV in ethanol, indicating a nearly neutral surface charge (Fig. S2D).

To achieve controlled hydrolysis of CPO, the hydrophobic PCL was utilized to encapsulate CPO via electrospinning, constructing a non-aqueous system. Under SEM observation, both PCL and PCA membranes were composed of dense, isotropic nanofiber networks. However, fibers in PCA membrane exhibited a rougher surface morphology, attributable to the encapsulation of CPO (Fig. 1B). The average diameters of PCL fibers and PCA fibers were 542.5 ± 122.8 nm and 629.1 ± 145.9 nm, respectively (Fig. S3A, B). Energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping confirmed the homogeneous distribution of calcium throughout the PCA membrane (Fig. 1C). The PCA nanofibrous membrane demonstrated a CPO loading efficiency of 13.82 ± 1.39% and a high encapsulation efficiency of approximately 82.92 ± 8.33%, confirming the successful and reliable incorporation of the oxygen generators within the polymeric matrix. The storage stability assay demonstrated that the PCA membrane effectively maintained CPO’s reactivity. After 7 days of storage, the PCA membrane exhibited a significantly lower percentage loss of H2O2 release capacity compared to the CPO dispersed in an aqueous system (5% CPO-GelMA pre-gel solution) (Fig. S3C). This result confirms that the solid, hydrophobic PCL matrix effectively isolates the encapsulated CPO, preventing premature hydrolysis. Consequently, this structural advantage eliminates the need for fresh preparation, facilitating convenient clinical storage and application.

We then injected a CeO2-containing GelMA pre-gel solution onto the nanofibrous membrane and fabricated the dual-layer composite via UV irradiation. Cross-sectional imaging showed that GelMA-CeO2 hydrogel (GC) adhered closely to the underlying PCA layer (Fig. 1D). EDS analysis demonstrated the spatial segregation of functional components, with CPO predominantly incorporated within the fibrous membrane and CeO2 mainly distributed in the GelMA hydrogel layer (Fig. 1E). This interfacial mechanical interlock was achieved by the low viscosity of the GelMA pre-gel solution, which infiltrates the pores between nanofibers. Upon UV exposure, GelMA underwent photopolymerization to form a crosslinked hydrogel network that penetrated and became entrapped within the nanofibrous membrane, thereby establishing mechanical anchoring between the two layers. Additionally, the structural integrity and functional applicability of the hydrogel were further verified. Under compressive testing, the incorporation of CeO2 significantly increased compressive modulus from 32.29 kPa to 54.71 kPa compared with the pure GelMA hydrogel (P = 0.0019; Fig. S4A, B). In lap shear tests, the GC hydrogel demonstrated an adhesive strength of 20.86 kPa to porcine tissue, showing no significant difference from the GelMA hydrogel (Fig. S4C). To evaluate the in vitro biodegradation behavior of the hydrogels, collagenase was employed to accelerate the degradation process, and the weight loss was monitored at predetermined time points. The results indicated that the GC hydrogel exhibited a significantly slower degradation rate compared to the GelMA hydrogel (Fig. S4D). After 24 h of enzymatic degradation, the weight loss of GelMA reached approximately 99.9%, while that of GC hydrogel was about 73.5%. Regarding potential biosafety, the release of cerium ions from GC hydrogels into PBS at 37 ℃ was assessed. As shown in Fig. S4E, GC hydrogels exhibited an initial burst release of Ce ions on the first day, followed by a sustained, controlled release that maintained metallic trace elements at a safe, low level. Subsequently, the oxygen-releasing profiles indicated that PCA-GC elevated oxygen tension to above 10% within 24 h and maintained sustained oxygen release for up to 5 days (Fig. 1F and G). Meanwhile, the pH of PBS gradually increased to approximately 8.5 at 24 h and to 8.7 at 48 h, consistent with the hydroxide generation during CPO hydrolysis (Fig. 1H). To verify the efficacy of the GC layer in promoting H2O2 decomposition, cumulative H2O2 concentrations after 24 h of immersion with PCA or PCA-GC were quantified first. As a result, the inclusion of GC significantly reduced the concentration of H2O2, from 671.8 ± 66.9 µM to 33.9 ± 15.8 µM in PBS (P < 0.0001; Fig. 1I) and from 147.2 ± 3.4 µM to 5.1 ± 0.8 µM in α-Minimum Essential Medium (α-MEM) (P < 0.0001; Fig. S5A). To further evaluate the time-course kinetics of the catalytic cascade, we monitored H2O2 levels at multiple consecutive time points over 24 h using a periodic PBS replenishment model. Notably, the H2O2 concentration in the PCA-GC group remained substantially and consistently lower than that in the PCA group at all tested points (Fig. S5B), demonstrating that the encapsulated CeO2 nanozymes were actively scavenging the generated H2O2 with robust catalytic efficiency. To better capture the initial kinetics and evaluate the biosafety of PCA-GC, the cumulative early-phase H2O2 release in α-MEM was continuously monitored. As shown in Fig. S5C, the H2O2 concentration reached its peak at the earliest observed time point (1 h) and subsequently exhibited a gradual, time-dependent decline, decreasing from an initial level of 11.9 ± 0.3 µM down to 7.8 ± 0.2 µM at 12 h. This declining trend indicates that the initial burst release of H2O2 from PCA-GC is transient, and the peroxide is effectively degraded over time. To determine the effect of the GC layer on alleviating PCA-induced oxidative stress, human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) and periodontal ligament cells (PDLCs) were co-cultured with PCA or PCA-GC, and intracellular ROS levels were subsequently assessed. PCA treatment significantly increased intracellular ROS levels compared to the control group (P = 0.0004; Fig. S6A, B), whereas this increase was significantly attenuated in the PCA-GC group (P = 0.0005; Fig. S6A, B). A similar trend was observed in PDLCs (Fig. S6C, D). Furthermore, the viability of both HGFs and PDLCs decreased to below 20% following 3 h of PCA treatment, whereas viability remained above 80% in the PCA-GC group (Fig. S6E, F).

Although H2O2 concentrations less than 40 µM were reported as non-hazardous to cell growth [23], its cytotoxicity varies greatly among different cells. Therefore, to further assess the biosafety of PCA-GC, cytotoxicity assays were conducted using RAW 264.7 macrophages, HGFs, and PDLCs. All cell types exhibited viability exceeding 80% relative to controls, indicating favorable cytocompatibility (Fig. S7A-C). Furthermore, in vivo study showed that rats exhibited stable weight gain after 14-day administration, with no significant changes in red blood cells (RBCs), white blood cells (WBCs), or platelets (PLTs), and no observable histopathological abnormalities in major organs (Fig. S7D-H). Taken together, these results demonstrated that PCA-GC possessed sustained oxygen-releasing capability and satisfactory biocompatibility, supporting its potential as a therapeutic strategy to alleviate hypoxia in deep periodontal pockets.

Hypoxia relief and antibiofilm effects of PCA-GC on multispecies biofilms

Biofilm formation is a stepwise process, beginning with early colonizers including S. gordonii, followed by secondary colonizers such as F. nucleatum, and culminating with late colonizers such as P. gingivalis. These communities assemble into a complex structure that shields underlying pathogens from traditional antibacterial agents. Topical administration of PCA-GC was designed to alleviate hypoxia within biofilms, potentially suppressing pathogen accumulation. To verify our hypothesis, complex biofilms were established using the aforementioned three bacterial species, and hypoxic conditions in mature biofilms under different treatments were assessed using fluorescent hypoxia probes. After 3 h of treatment, biofilms in the PCA-GC group exhibited significantly lower fluorescence intensity, indicating elevated oxygen tension compared with the control group (P = 0.001; Fig. 2A and B). Meanwhile, the PCL-GC group demonstrated a slight, non-significant reduction in fluorescence intensity compared to the control group (P = 0.0802; Fig. 2A and B), potentially attributed to the oxygen storage capacity of CeO2 [24]. At 6 h, biofilms treated with PCA-GC remained more oxygenated, whereas those in the PCL-GC group showed increased hypoxia probe fluorescence, reaching the level of the control group, indicating the limited oxygen-releasing capacity of CeO2 alone (Fig. 2C and D).

To further investigate the inhibitory effects of oxygen enrichment mediated by PCA-GC on mixed biofilms, the biofilm formation under PCA-GC treatment was evaluated. As shown in Fig. 2E, PCA-GC markedly suppressed multispecies biofilm growth, forming dispersed and discontinuous patches, in contrast to the denser structures observed in the control and PCL-GC groups. Consistently, biofilms in the PCA-GC group exhibited reduced thickness of less than 10 μm, compared with approximately 17 μm in the control groups (P = 0.0039; Fig. 2F). Additionally, the antibiofilm effect of PCA-GC on mature biofilms was evaluated because residual biofilm after mechanical debridement contributes critically to persisting inflammation. PCA-GC treatment resulted in smaller, less compact biofilm patches with decreased thickness relative to control groups (P = 0.0051; Fig. 2G, H). Furthermore, the crystal violet staining assay was employed to quantify the total biofilm biomass. As shown in Fig. S8A-D, PCA-GC exhibited a robust inhibitory effect on both biofilm development and pre-formed mature biofilms. In the biofilm formation assay, the addition of PCA-GC led to a substantial reduction in biofilm biomass compared to the control group, with the OD570 values dropping significantly from 0.99 to 0.43 (P < 0.0001; Fig. S8B). Furthermore, in the biofilm eradication assay, PCA-GC effectively disrupted the established mature biofilms, resulting in a prominent decrease in OD570 (P < 0.0001; Fig. S8D). Taken together, these findings demonstrated that PCA-GC effectively alleviated hypoxia in multispecies biofilms, suppressed their growth and maturation, and substantially disrupted pre-developed biofilms.

Selective antibacterial effects of PCA-GC and pro-inflammatory potential of multispecies biofilms with different treatments

To assess the selective antibacterial effects of PCA-GC on planktonic pathogens, P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum were chosen as representative species. PCA-GC significantly suppressed the proliferation of P. gingivalis, eliminating 100% of viable bacteria after 24 h of treatment, whereas only slight suppression occurred in the PCL-GC treatment group (P = 0.0327; Fig. 3A-C). Similarly, the growth of planktonic F. nucleatum was significantly inhibited following PCA-GC treatment, with a reduction in bacterial load by five orders of magnitude (Fig. 3D-F). In contrast, neither PCA-GC nor PCL-GC reduced the viability of S. gordonii, a commensal species enriched in healthy microbiota (Fig. 3G-I).

Given that the hydrolysis of CPO inherently contributes to an alkaline shift via the generation of calcium hydroxide, the growth of these three species was further evaluated under distinct pH gradients (7.35, 8.0, 8.5, and 9.0) to dissect the relative contributions of the alkaline environment and oxygenation to the antibacterial efficacy of PCA-GC. As shown in Fig. S9, exposure to mild alkaline conditions (pH 8.0 and 8.5), representative of the bulk pH shift induced by PCA-GC (~ 8.7), did not suppress the proliferation of S. gordonii, F. nucleatum, and P. gingivalis compared to the physiological culture condition (pH 7.35) (Fig. S9A-F). These results indicate that, within the range of the bulk alkaline shift achieved by PCA-GC, the alkaline environment alone is insufficient to eradicate these pathogens, suggesting that oxygen enrichment is the predominant antibacterial mechanism of PCA-GC against P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum. Interestingly, when the alkalinity was further increased to pH 9.0, a level that may be transiently reached in the local microenvironment immediately adjacent to the PCA-GC surface, where the pH is expected to exceed the bulk value, distinct species-specific biological responses were observed. The OD600 and bacterial density of F. nucleatum significantly increased (Fig. S9C [P = 0.0284], Fig. S9D [P = 0.0049]), indicating that a strong alkaline environment actively promotes its proliferation rather than contributing to its elimination. Conversely, for P. gingivalis, a significant suppression was detected at pH 9.0 (P < 0.0001; Fig. S9E), although it maintained a robust bacterial density of around 106 CFU/mL (Fig. S9F), indicating that alkalinity at this level exerts an inhibitory but not eradicative effect.

To investigate changes in microbial composition in mixed biofilms, the abundance of various species in biofilms subjected to different treatments was further assessed by qRT-PCR. PCA-GC significantly decreased the viable count of P. gingivalis by approximately three orders of magnitude, corresponding to a 99.9% reduction relative to untreated biofilms (Fig. 4A). Additionally, the viability of F. nucleatum, which is less susceptible than P. gingivalis within biofilm consortia, reduced by approximately 90% after PCA-GC treatment for 24 h (Fig. 4B). In comparison, the viability of S. gordonii was largely maintained, with a slight increase following PCA-GC exposure (Fig. 4C). Species composition analysis revealed a marked microbial shift: the relative abundance of P. gingivalis decreased from 84.42% in the control group to 0.05% in PCA-GC-treated biofilms, while S. gordonii increased from 11.18% to 99.01% (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, biofilms subjected to 24 h of different treatments were then co-cultured with PDLCs to evaluate whether treatment-associated microbial shift in biofilms affected their pro-inflammatory potential (Fig. 4E). Compared with the control group, PDLCs stimulated with untreated biofilms exhibited increased expression and secretion of IL-6 and IL-8 (Fig. 4F-I), suggesting that the established multispecies biofilms induced a severe inflammatory response in PDLCs. However, biofilms after PCA-GC treatment induced significantly lower levels of IL-6 (P = 0.0170 in Fig. 4F, P = 0.0275 in Fig. 4G) and IL-8 (P = 0.0013 in Fig. 4H, P = 0.0172 in Fig. 4I) in PDLCs, indicating that PCA-GC attenuated the pro-inflammatory potential of multispecies biofilms, concomitant with the observed microbial shift (Fig. 4F-I).

Bactericidal efficiency of macrophages with PCA-GC treatment under hypoxic conditions

Macrophages are key effectors of innate immunity, responsible for phagocytosis and elimination of pathogens. When encountering periodontal pathogens, the activation of the immune response significantly enhances oxygen consumption; however, oxygen availability in deep periodontal pockets is insufficient to meet the increased metabolic demand. To determine whether oxygen supplementation produced by PCA-GC could enhance the bactericidal activity of macrophages, RAW 264.7 macrophages were infected with P. gingivalis, and the intracellular and extracellular bacterial burdens were quantified under different oxygen and treatment conditions. After 6 h, the intracellular and extracellular P. gingivalis showed no significant decrease in the co-culture system under normoxia compared to those under hypoxia (Fig. 5A and B). In comparison, PCA-GC treatment significantly reduced both intracellular P. gingivalis levels (P = 0.0008) and extracellular bacteria (P = 0.0137) under hypoxic conditions, potentially attributable to the elevated local oxygen tension produced by PCA-GC during the initial phase of oxygen release (Fig. 5A and B). At 24 h, bacterial burden gradually decreased under normoxia, whereas intracellular survival of P. gingivalis in RAW 264.7 macrophages increased after incubation under hypoxia (P < 0.0001; Fig. 5C and D). PCL-GC treatment partially restored the bactericidal activity of macrophages under hypoxia after 24 h compared to the control group, indicating CeO2 nanoparticles may be potential contributors to pathogen elimination, albeit to a lesser extent than PCA-GC. Notably, PCA-GC achieved the most pronounced bactericidal effect under hypoxia, resulting in the lowest residual levels of P. gingivalis among all treatment groups.

To further evaluate the cellular response regarding enhanced pathogen clearance by PCA-GC, macrophage phagocytic activity was assessed. Hypoxia reduced bacterial engulfment in infected RAW 264.7 macrophages compared to normoxic conditions, as evidenced by decreased neutral red uptake (Fig. 5E and F). Both PCL-GC and PCA-GC treatments partially restored phagocytic capacity under hypoxia, with PCA-GC exhibiting a more pronounced effect, suggesting the recovery might be attributed to the combined effects of oxygen enrichment and the inherent property of CeO2 nanoparticles (Fig. 5E and F). Subsequently, downstream bactericidal mechanisms of activated macrophages were examined by quantifying ROS and nitric oxide (NO) production, two canonical antimicrobial effector pathways essential for macrophage-dependent pathogen clearance [25, 26]. In response to P. gingivalis infection, PCA-GC restored ROS generation under hypoxia to levels comparable to those observed under normoxia, whereas PCL-GC treatment slightly induced a statistically insignificant increase in ROS levels (P = 0.2271; Fig. 5G and H). Additionally, PCA-GC markedly increased NO concentrations under hypoxia, even exceeding those under normoxia at 6 h (Fig. 5I and J), corresponding to the initial oxygen release (Fig. 1F). Unlike the consistent increment in normoxia or PCA-GC group, NO production was significantly inhibited under hypoxia in the control or PCL-GC group for 24 h, highlighting the critical dependence of NO synthesis on sufficient oxygen availability (Fig. 5I and J). Collectively, these results indicated that PCA-GC reinforced the bactericidal activity of macrophages under hypoxic conditions by enhancing phagocytosis and augmenting ROS and NO production, thereby suggesting a potential role in reinforcing host innate immune defense within hypoxic periodontal microenvironments (Fig. 5K).

In vivo therapeutic efficacy of PCA-GC in rat experimental periodontitis model

Experimental periodontitis was established in rats by ligature placement combined with microbial challenge using P. gingivalis, F. nucleatum, and S. gordonii. PBS, PCL-GC, or PCA-GC was administered topically to evaluate their therapeutic efficacy in vivo (Fig. 6A). Pathogen invasion into the gingival tissue was evaluated by Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) using probes specific for P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum. As shown in Fig. 6B, substantially increased fluorescent signals, including P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum, were detected in the PBS group compared to the healthy group, with signal abundance remaining comparable in the PCL-GC-treated group. In contrast, a significant reduction of P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum invasion was observed in PCA-GC-treated gingival tissues (Fig. 6B). To evaluate the in vivo oxygenating efficacy of the material at the tissue level, we assessed the expression of HIF-1α, a critical biological marker for cellular hypoxia, via immunohistochemical staining. As shown in Fig. 6C, HIF-1α expression was markedly elevated in the gingival tissues of the PBS group, confirming the severe hypoxic microenvironment associated with periodontitis progression. Notably, application of the oxygen-releasing PCA-GC treatment effectively reversed this hypoxic state, resulting in significantly downregulated HIF-1α expression. In contrast, PCL-GC exhibited no significant attenuation of this hypoxic marker.

As shown in Fig. 7A and B, alveolar bone resorption was observed in the PBS group, indicating limited self-repair capability. In comparison, PCA-GC treatment significantly mitigated alveolar bone loss, as indicated by an approximately 0.2 mm reduction in the CEJ-ABC distance (P < 0.001; Fig. 7A and B). In addition, PCA-GC decreased PPD from 0.89 mm to 0.49 mm and attenuated gingival bleeding (Fig. 7C and D). Histological analysis revealed preservation of alveolar ridge height and reversal of pathological gingival epithelial changes in the PCA-GC group, characterized by reduced hyperplasia, diminished acanthosis, and shortened rete pegs (Fig. 7E). Moreover, TRAP staining showed significantly fewer osteoclasts in PCA-GC-treated rats, indicating attenuated bone destruction (P < 0.0001; Fig. 7F and G). Consistently, immunohistochemical analysis revealed markedly elevated TNF-α and IL-1β levels in the PBS group compared with healthy controls, whereas expression remained minimal in PCA-GC-treated periodontal tissues (Fig. 7H). Collectively, these results demonstrated that PCA-GC exerted in vivo therapeutic benefits by decreasing pathogen burden and hypoxia marker expression in gingival tissues, reducing inflammation, and attenuating alveolar bone resorption.

Discussion

Hypoxia in deep periodontal pockets promotes the accumulation of anaerobic pathogens such as P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum, which in turn exacerbates dysbiosis, causes local inflammation, and impairs immune cell functions [9]. Accordingly, alleviating oxygen deprivation represents a rational therapeutic strategy for targeting the pathogenic niche of periodontitis. In this study, CPO nanoparticles, widely recognized as oxygen generators, were encapsulated within a non-aqueous PCL fibrous membrane to address two critical issues: (i) premature hydrolysis caused by water present in aqueous hydrogel matrices, which necessitates on-site preparation, and (ii) rapid post-administration hydrolysis, which restricts the duration of oxygen release [19, 27, 28]. As a result, the PCA-GC composite, containing approximately 0.552 mg CPO, extended the duration of oxygen release from 24 h to 5 days, compared with a previously reported formulation in which 1 mg CPO (5% w/w) was directly encapsulated in GelMA hydrogels [14]. This finding indicates that the present oxygen-delivery system achieves more sustained oxygen release while requiring a substantially lower dose of oxygen generator. Nevertheless, given that gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) is a complex, enzyme-rich microenvironment [29], the oxygen-release profile of this system in simulated GCF requires further comprehensive evaluation in future studies. Furthermore, the incorporation of CeO2 effectively promoted the decomposition of H2O2, thereby alleviating oxidative stress in periodontal cells and enhancing cell viability. It should be noted that the H2O2 concentration recorded at each time point reflects the amount newly generated since the previous buffer exchange, rather than a cumulative residual level. The consistently lower H2O2 concentrations observed in the PCA-GC group relative to the PCA group across all time intervals therefore confirm that the CeO2 nanozyme retains sustained catalytic activity, rather than exerting only a transient, one-time scavenging effect. However, the detectable residual H2O2 in the PCA-GC group indicates that the catalytic efficiency of the CeO2 nanozyme remains limited. Future studies could explore multi-element-doped nanozymes with higher turnover frequencies to achieve more complete and rapid H2O2 elimination [30]. Although the H2O2 concentrations in α-MEM exhibited only a modest peak of 11.9 µM at 1 h, a slight decrease in cell viability (80–90%) was observed in the PCA-GC group, which may be attributable to a localized H2O2 concentration gradient near the material interface. Nonetheless, this level remains well within the cytocompatible range defined by the ISO 10993-5 standard. Furthermore, CPO hydrolysis induces an alkaline microenvironment that potentially promotes bone regeneration, supported by the elevated osteoblast proliferation, WST-1 metabolism, and ATP production at pH ~ 8.4 [31].

Notably, PCA-GC exhibited selective bactericidal activity against pathogenic species, including P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum, while sparing the commensal S. gordonii, which is commonly associated with periodontal health [32, 33]. It should be clarified that this selectivity arises from the inherent differential oxygen sensitivity among the bacterial species, rather than representing a conventionally targeted antimicrobial mechanism. Indeed, periodontitis is predominantly driven by oxygen-sensitive anaerobes, and such selective antimicrobial activity of PCA-GC would therefore facilitate a favorable microbial shift, promoting the restoration of local eubiosis. The reduction of P. gingivalis within multispecies biofilms may result from a combination of oxygen enrichment, alkaline shift, and intensified interspecies competition. Although P. gingivalis can initiate adaptive responses to oxidative stress, its inherently deficient antioxidant system, most notably the absence of catalase and homologues of soxR or soxS, renders it highly sensitive to oxygen, with complete inhibition at 10% oxygen tension [34]. The oxygen released by PCA-GC within the first 24 h maintained local oxygen tension above this threshold, indicating sufficient suppression of P. gingivalis. It is worth noting that, although P. gingivalis remained viable at pH 8.5, growth was significantly suppressed at pH 9.0. This suggests that, in regions where the local pH approaches this threshold, the alkaline microenvironment may act synergistically with oxygenation. Consequently, while the complete elimination of viable P. gingivalis observed in planktonic cultures and the 99.9% reduction within mixed biofilms following PCA-GC treatment are predominantly attributable to oxygenation, the alkaline microenvironment likely contributes as a supplementary factor. Furthermore, commensal bacteria such as S. gordonii, thrive under oxygen-sufficient conditions, contributing to interspecies antagonism within biofilms by producing endogenous H2O2, which exerts a dual inhibitory effect, suppressing P. gingivalis growth and gingipain activity while simultaneously impairing bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation [32, 35]. In contrast, the antibacterial effect of PCA-GC against F. nucleatum was comparatively less pronounced. This may reflect the relatively higher oxygen tolerance of F. nucleatum following biofilm establishment, as well as its documented capacity to shield neighboring species within polymicrobial communities, suggesting that oxygen supplementation alone may be insufficient under conditions of high pathogenic burden [36]. In addition, F. nucleatum was not only resistant to alkaline conditions but also exhibited enhanced proliferation at pH 9.0. This growth-promoting effect of alkalinity may further help explain why F. nucleatum displayed comparatively reduced sensitivity to PCA-GC treatment relative to P. gingivalis. In such cases, adjunctive strategies such as photodynamic therapy (PDT), which convert the supplied oxygen into highly reactive oxygen singlets with enhanced bactericidal potency, may be considered to further reinforce therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, the in vitro biofilm established in this study (~ 18 μm) was substantially thinner than clinical subgingival plaque (40–45 μm) [37]. Since the oxygen penetration depth and diffusion efficiency decrease progressively with increasing biofilm thickness [38], further studies employing more mature, three-dimensional microbial communities and thicker biofilms are warranted to comprehensively evaluate the antibiofilm efficacy of PCA-GC.

Beyond microbial modulation, the host immune response to pathogenic infection is central to periodontal disease progression [39, 40]. In this study, PDLCs exposed to PCA-GC-treated biofilms with S. gordonii predominance exhibited significantly reduced IL-6 and IL-8 production compared with untreated controls dominated by P. gingivalis, consistent with a reduction in biofilm-associated pathogenicity. This aligns with prior evidence that the culture supernatant of P. gingivalis drives inflammatory cytokine production in PDLCs, including IL-6 and IL-8, whereas S. gordonii supernatants do not [41]. Furthermore, P. gingivalis W83 has been reported to evolve sophisticated immune evasion mechanisms, including suppression of macrophage phagocytosis and inhibition of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS/RNS) production [42]. Oxygen therapy antagonizes these immune evasion mechanisms by downregulating the expression of key virulent factors of P. gingivalis, such as gingipains, while simultaneously fueling host defense [14]. At the host level, adequate oxygen is indispensable for macrophage phagocytosis and bactericidal activity, both of which rely heavily on oxidative metabolism [43, 44]. Oxygen enrichment, therefore, represents an effective strategy to restore immunological balance, particularly for immune cells operating under conditions of severe inflammation, which impose a high oxygen demand. In this study, PCA-GC successfully restored macrophage phagocytic activity that had been impaired by hypoxia. This restoration is likely attributable to both oxygen supply and intrinsic properties of CeO2 nanoparticles, which have been reported to be positive regulators of phagocytosis [45, 46]. In addition, PCA-GC significantly increased ROS production in macrophages under hypoxia, correlating with enhanced pathogen clearance capacity [44]. Beyond oxygen augmentation, CeO2 nanoparticles themselves exhibit versatile enzyme-mimetic activity via the reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ valence state conversion, enabling ROS generation under acidic conditions, characteristic of infection sites [47]. Interestingly, our results demonstrated that NO production was markedly inhibited by hypoxia and restored by PCA-GC treatment. This aligns with a previous report that hypoxia restricts NO production, not merely by oxygen-substrate deficiency but also by disruption of the interaction between inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and the cytoskeleton, which can be restored by prolonged reoxygenation [48]. Notably, recent evidence demonstrates that an alkaline microenvironment (pH 8.2) actively drives macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype. This shift is characterized by a marked upregulation of iNOS, which potentially enhances intracellular bactericidal capacity against engulfed pathogens [49]. Beyond facilitating pathogen clearance, such alkaline conditions exert a favorable osteoimmunomodulatory effect that promotes bone regeneration. For instance, conditioned medium from RAW 264.7 cells cultured at pH 8.2 has been shown to significantly upregulate osteogenic marker expression in osteoblasts [49]. While these results suggest that PCA-GC may help restore multiple dimensions of macrophage bactericidal function, the current evidence is derived from an immortalized cell line, which differs substantially from primary macrophages in phenotype and functional behavior. Therefore, further mechanistic investigations using primary human cells, both in vitro and in vivo, are required to fully elucidate the immunomodulatory effects of PCA-GC and the complex host-pathogen interactions following its application. Additionally, building upon our findings regarding downstream functional effectors (ROS and NO), future research will prioritize mechanistic investigations to map the specific transcriptional regulations, metabolic reprogramming, and signaling cascades driving the macrophage responses.

Despite its promising in vivo efficacy, the clinical translation of the PCA-GC composite faces several practical challenges. First, long-term retention within the dynamic microenvironment of the periodontal pocket remains challenging, and the adjunctive use of a periodontal dressing represents a potential strategy to extend retention time [50]. Second, achieving precise oxygen dosing control tailored to varying pocket depths and severities of inflammation requires further pharmacokinetic optimization. Third, the fabrication process for this dual-layer composite is relatively complex, which may hinder manufacturing scalability. Identifying suitable non-destructive cold sterilization methods further constrains its potential for industrial-scale production. Although the hydrophobic PCL matrix effectively delays water penetration and thereby prevents premature hydrolysis of CPO, the slow in vivo degradation rate of PCL means that the residual membrane may require mechanical removal during follow-up visits, potentially including an additional step to the clinical workflow. Future designs will explore the incorporation of faster-degrading polymers, such as poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), to better align the device’s degradation rate with the periodontal healing process [51]. Finally, reliance on professional in-clinic processing may impact patient compliance, underscoring the need for future technological refinement to facilitate clinical application.

In addition, more comprehensive investigations of in vivo therapeutic efficacy should be conducted in future studies. For instance, the PCA-only control group was excluded from the rat model because our in vitro assays showed that continuous H2O2 release from unprotected PCA resulted in severe acute cytotoxicity. Applying this unbuffered formulation in vivo would predictably trigger severe tissue necrosis rather than therapeutic healing, rendering it scientifically unsuitable as a baseline treatment. However, since the periodontal pocket microenvironment contains intrinsic antioxidant systems and various enzymes capable of promoting H2O2 decomposition, in vivo validation of the potential oxidative damage resulting from prolonged CPO application should be addressed in future work. Additionally, the present study primarily focused on evaluating short-term cytocompatibility via in vitro assessment. Further comprehensive investigations, including apoptosis assays, broader panels of oxidative stress markers, and long-term in vitro and in vivo biocompatibility evaluations, are warranted in future studies. Although the PCA-GC showed promising anti-biofilm activity in vitro, it should be noted that the simplified three-species biofilm model has inherent limitations in fully mimicking the complex multi-species ecology and dynamic environments found in vivo [52]. It is imperative to conduct further studies employing subgingival plaque samples collected from patients across various stages of periodontitis to evaluate the clinical antibiofilm efficacy of PCA-GC. Furthermore, the significant downregulation of HIF-1α expression provides robust biological evidence of effective tissue oxygenation [53], yet the precise oxygen tensions within periodontal pockets across different treatment groups remain unknown. Due to the severe anatomical constraints, a pocket-specific micro-oxygen sensor suitable for the rat model was not available in the present study. In vivo quantification of pocket oxygen tension using advanced, miniaturized microelectrode technologies should be further investigated in larger animal models to determine the optimal duration and magnitude of oxygen augmentation. Large-animal models such as mini-pigs or beagles, which exhibit periodontal pathogenesis and oral microbiota more similar to humans, would offer higher translational fidelity [54, 55]. Therefore, further validation in such large-animal models is warranted, particularly to assess microbial shifts and pocket oxygen dynamics in vivo following PCA-GC treatment, providing a stronger theoretical foundation for its clinical application.

Conclusion

The hydrogel/nanofibrous membrane composite (PCA-GC) effectively alleviates local hypoxia in the surrounding aqueous environment and within multispecies biofilms, while exhibiting favorable cytocompatibility within the tested range. The resulting oxygen enrichment preferentially suppresses oxygen-sensitive periodontal pathogens such as P. gingivalis, while sparing commensal bacteria such as S. gordonii, thereby contributing to a favorable shift in microbial composition and a reduction in biofilm pathogenicity. Furthermore, PCA-GC treatment also enhances immune cell antimicrobial function, which could contribute to more efficient pathogen clearance. In vivo, topical PCA-GC application reduced microbial invasion, decreased hypoxia marker expression, attenuated inflammation, and mitigated alveolar bone loss in a rat periodontitis model over a 14-day period. Taken together, these findings support PCA-GC as a potential adjunctive therapeutic strategy for local oxygen enrichment and microenvironmental remodeling in the management of periodontitis, warranting future studies with extended observation periods to evaluate its long-term efficacy.
