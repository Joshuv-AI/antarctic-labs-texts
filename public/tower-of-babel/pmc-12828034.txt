Unraveling the spatiotemporal clustering of malaria incidence and modeling a decade of epidemiological data in Jimma Zone, Oromia, Ethiopia
Authors: Abdissa Biruksew, Ashenafi Demeke, Zewdie Birhanu, Lemu Golassa, Assefa Legesse Sisay, Jihad Kemal, Delenasaw Yewhalaw
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Malaria remains a significant public health concern in Ethiopia. Understanding the historical backgrounds and future trends of malaria incidence is crucial for customizing local specific intervention strategies. The spatiotemporal patterns and putative modeling of future trends of malaria in the Jimma Zone have not been thoroughly investigated. This study aimed to model the spatiotemporal patterns and prediction of future malaria trends in Jimma Zone, Oromia, Ethiopia, Ethiopia from 2022 to 2026. This study analyzed a decade of historical malaria data (2012–2021) from 15 districts of Jimma Zone. A District-level annual parasite incidence (API) and Standardized incidence ratio (SIR) were calculated, and malaria risk maps were developed. Moran’s I and Monte Carlo statistics were used for assessing spatial clustering of malaria incidence, while time series analysis with the Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model was used for analyzing the temporal patterns and predicted for the future trends. A total of 1,718,471 individuals were screened for malaria between 2012 and 2021, with 359,655 (20.93%) confirmed cases. Plasmodium falciparum, P. vivax and mixed infections accounted for 52.71%, 43.62% and 3.67%, respectively. Malaria incidence decreased dramatically by 96.13%, with the API falling from 87.97 per 1,000 population at risk in 2012 to 3.40 in 2021. It was revealed that there was random distribution of malaria transmission across districts. API and SIR maps identified potential risk areas and cold spots. The disease exhibited seasonal patterns, peaking from August to November (maximum in September) and from March to June. Despite a decline trend, malaria remains a potential threat in areas like Chora Botor and Shebe Sonbo. Forecasting with the ARIMA (0, 1, 3) x (1, 1, 0) model for 2022–2026 suggests stability in malaria incidence, with seasonal fluctuations but no significant trends. Malaria incidence in Jimma Zone was significantly decreased from 2012 to 2021, reaching notably low risk levels by 2021. However, the disease persists in areas such as Chora Botor and Shebe Sonbo, indicating ongoing risk of transmission. The seasonal pattern of malaria underscores the need for targeted interventions during peak periods. Spatial autocorrelation analysis showed no distinct patterns, suggesting the necessity for continued surveillance in historically high-risk areas. While forecasts suggest a stable malaria cases, maintaining vigilance and adapting interventions based on emerging trends is essential to sustain control progress towards elimination. Finally, future studies should incorporate key factors like climate variables (temperature, rainfall, humidity), topographic features, intervention coverage, and other environmental determinants, which were not included in our analysis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32808-y.

Introduction

Malaria, a mosquito-borne infectious disease1, remains a major global health issue, causing millions of cases and hundreds of thousands of deaths each year2 The disease is caused by five Plasmodium species: Plasmodium falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale, P. malariae, and P. knowlesi, with P. falciparum and P. vivax being the most threatening3. Despite notable progress in malaria control, the challenge persists. The recent World Malaria Report indicates a concerning increase in malaria cases, with 5 million more cases reported in 2022, totaling an estimated 249 million cases across 85 endemic countries. Pakistan, Ethiopia, Nigeria, Uganda, and Papua New Guinea were identified as significant contributors to the increase. Disparities remain stark, as sub-Saharan Africa bears a disproportionate burden, accounting for more than 94% of global malaria cases and 95% of deaths in 2022. Tragically, children under five represent approximately 80% of these deaths. Although malaria mortality decreased steadily from 2000 to 2019, the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted services and led to a rise in deaths in 20202.

Ethiopia has made significant progress in reducing malaria cases over the last decade. However, recent data reveal a troubling surge in cases, with malaria incidence increasing by 32% from 2021 to 2022, rising from 46.3 to 60.9 cases per 1,000 population at risk2. This increase led to over 1.3 million reported cases in 2022. While the number of malaria-related deaths remains relatively low at 180, the predominance of P. falciparum, which accounts for 71.3% of cases, is concerning. P. vivax follows with 20.9%, while mixed infections account for 1.1%, and 6.7% of cases remain unidentified4. The country’s ambitious goal to reduce malaria morbidity and mortality by 50% from 2020 levels by 2025 involves eliminating indigenous cases in districts with an annual parasite incidence (API) < 10 and preventing disease re-establishment in areas previously reported as malaria-free5. With over 27% of the population at high risk of malaria2,6, the disease exhibits a clear seasonal pattern with distinct temporal and spatial dynamics.

Hence, malaria transmission in Ethiopia is highly seasonal, varying significantly with altitude and climatic conditions4,7. Areas below 2,000 m are particularly vulnerable. The main transmission period coincides with the end of the primary rainy season (June to August), peaking between September and December. Stagnant water bodies formed during this period provide ideal breeding grounds for mosquitoes, leading to a surge in malaria cases. A secondary, less intense peak often occurs during the shorter rainy season from March to June4,8. This seasonal pattern influences both short outbreaks and larger cyclic epidemics9.

The complex interplay between female Anopheles mosquitoes, human hosts and Plasmodium parasites defines the ecological framework of malaria transmission, which is also influenced by climate change10. Understanding the spatial and temporal dynamics of malaria is therefore essential for developing effective control strategies. Mapping disease incidence and forecasting future patterns using historical epidemiological data are crucial for this purpose11,12.

Advanced statistical methodology, such as ARIMA, play a pivotal role in understanding disease progression and forecasting future trends. These models, by analyzing historical malaria epidemiological data, help decipher patterns, enhance prediction accuracy, and guide targeted interventions13–15. Besides, studies have explored both temporal and spatial malaria patterns using various spatial analysis tools21,22, and ultimately guided targeted interventions. Global and local Moran’s I model have been employed to assess spatial autocorrelation and understand clustering, dispersion, or random distribution of malaria incidence across geographic spaces16–19. Given the seasonality of malaria, ARIMA models offer high predictive power20 and have been utilized to predict risks numerous infectious diseases transmission21,22. Additionally, ARIMA’s relative simplicity and stability make it effective for forecasting malaria trends23.

While some studies in Ethiopia examined malaria’s spatiotemporal distribution, comprehensive national-level analyses is not only limited, but also focused on specific localities24–30, although few studies have employed GIS and spatial statistics to understand the temporal spread of malaria across the country. A major challenge in understanding malaria dynamics in Ethiopia, especially in Jimma zone, is the limited availability of detailed published or unpublished data on the magnitude, temporal, and spatial patterns of malaria incidence. This information deficit hampers our ability to track how malaria patterns evolve over time, complicating efforts to control the disease. Additionally, research on the distinct patterns of Plasmodium species in the Zone is scarce. Compounding this issue, studies employing time series analysis to assess past trends and predict future outbreaks are hardly available. We believe that addressing these critical gaps is imperative for generating the evidence needed to inform effective malaria control and safeguard public health at large.

This study, therefore, applied both ARIMA time series analysis and Moran’s I spatial autocorrelation31 to better understand malaria series cases in Jimma Zone to unravel the temporal and spatial dependencies of the observations and make prediction of its future patterns32.

Results

Epidemiological characteristics

A decade-long analysis of malaria epidemiological data from Jimma Zone showed both concern and optimism. This comprehensive study covered 75% of the zone’s districts (15 out of 21), with all districts reported the presence of malaria. Over 1.7 million individuals (n = 1,718,471) were screened for malaria using either microscopy or RDTs, resulting in an overall positivity rate of 20.93% over the years. As illustrated in Table 1, there was a dramatic decline in malaria case series over the past decade with a peak positivity rate of 32.87% in 2012 and the rate steadily fell to just 9.40% by 2021.

Total malaria cases series district level

A ten-year journey through the malaria landscape of the Jimma Zone reveals a story of progress and persistence challenges. While the overall number of malaria cases has clearly declined, a closer look reveals notable disparities among the districts (Table 1) although the decline wasn’t uniform across all the districts. Some districts the once-heavily burdened significantly reduced malaria burden in recent years. As can be seen in Table 1, for instance, in 2012, districts like Gomma (while higher cases in 2013 than 2012), Sokoru, Limu Seka, Chora Botor, Tiro Afata, and Limu Kosa, which had over 12,000 malaria cases achieved a dramatic reduction with fewer than 1,000 or 500 cases reported in 2021. On the other hand, six districts (Agaro town, Dedo, Gera, Gummay, Omo Nada and Shebe Sonbo) showed fluctuating cases over time and generally showed increased cases between 2019 and 2021.

Sociodemographic characteristics of malaria patients

The distribution of malaria in under-five children varied significantly over the period, averaging 12.56% over the years. The most substantial change occurred between 2012 and 2016, during which the proportion of positivity increased from 5.37% to 16.11%. This indicates that infection significantly increased by about 200% in this age group during these periods. However, between 2016 and 2019, there was a period of prolonged malaria transmission in this age group before it reached a maximum of over 16.60%. Subsequently, it generally increased by over 3.51% in 2020 and 2021. Nevertheless, individuals five years of age and older carried the heavier burden of the disease during the same period, with an average prevalence of more than 87%, and typically showed a steadier trend (Fig. 1). Between 2012 and 2021, however, there was a minor decline in this older age group, from 94.63% to 83.49%, representing a decrease of approximately 11% (Fig. 1).

For females, an opposing trend was observed, with proportions decreasing between 2012 and 2015, followed by an increase from 2015 to 2021.

Distribution of Plasmodium species

Of the overall malaria case positivity rate of 20.93%, 52.71% (189,568) of the infections were caused by P. falciparum, followed by P. vivax, which accounted for 43.62% (156,877) of cases, while mixed infections accounted for 3.67% (13,210), with P. falciparum generally being the predominant parasite species. Plasmodium vivax was dominant over P. falciparum only between 2012 and 2014, and then declined (Fig. 3). Over the years, the proportion of P. falciparum showed fluctuations, starting at 53.99% in 2012 and peaking at 68% in 2021, an increase of 25.96% over the years. In particular, the highest values were recorded in 2020 (68.39%) and 2021 (68%), indicating a worrying upward trend in recent years.

In contrast, P. vivax, although significant, showed a decreasing trend starting at 42.05% in 2012 to 30.77% in 2021, decreased by 26.82%. The higher values in 2015 (49.77%) and 2016 (50.60%) indicate periods of increased prevalence before the overall decline. The proportion of mixed infections generally showed a steadily decreasing trend, starting from 3.96% in 2012 and decreasing to 1.23% in 2021, a decrease of 68.94% with a higher proportion seen in 2014 was 4.07%.

Spatial analysis of annual parasite incidence (API) and malaria transmission risk map

The average decline in API over the period was 96.13%, from 87.97 in 2012 to 3.40 in 2021. It went down from 47.6% in 2012 to 46.09% in 2013. The API decline year after year until it bottomed out in 2017 and 2019 and stabilized in a range of 2.55 to 4.06. In subsequent years, despite the general downward trend, there were minor fluctuations, with a slight increase to 4.19 in 2020 and a decrease to 3.40 in 2021. Further support for these declining trends was also found in the spatial distribution. An important example is the decrease in API observed for Chora Botor, where proportion reduced by 98.87%, from 636.86 per 1,000 populations at-risk in 2012 to 7.19 in 2021. On the other hand, Agaro recorded a 91.63% drop in API from 83.85 in 2012 to 7.02 in 2021. There was a notable decrease of approximately 97.2% in Sokoru, with API dropping from 94.63 to 2.68 in 2021. Interestingly, a malaria transmission bottleneck was noted, with the incidence reaching 1.17 in 2019. Tiro Afeta also showed notable progress, reducing its API by 97.8% from 81.6 to 1.81. Limu Kosa recorded a 97.25% decline from 69.97 in 2012 to 1.92 in 2020, but an 84.4% increase to 3.54 in 2021.

The API of Limu Seka decreased by 91.77% between 2012 and 2019, from 65.19 to 1.70, but then increased somewhat after 2019. Shebe Sonbo likewise reduced API by 95%, from 64.62 in 2012 to 3.14 in 2019, although this accomplishment was eclipsed by a massive 248.09% spike to 10.93 between 2019 and 2021.

In light of this, we created a malaria risk map of the study districts (Fig. 4), which illustrates a progressive shrinkage of malaria transmission areas over time. As a result, there were seven high API districts in 2012, including Chora Botor, Agaro Town, Gomma, Sokoru, Limu Kosa, Shebe Sonbo and Limu Seka. However, districts such as Toro Afeta and Limu Seka experienced a shift from high to moderate incidence levels, whereas Kersa had a low API. However, six districts such as Chora Botor, Agaro Town, Gomma, Sokoru, Limu Kosa and Shebe Sonbo continued to have higher incidence rates in 2013. In this context, Toro Afeta and Limu Seka shifted from higher to moderate incidence values, and Kersa had a low API.

It is interesting to note that there was a significant decline in malaria incidence from 2015 to 2021 and no district reported a high API value. Therefore, five of the 15 reported malaria cases (Gera, Gomma, Gumay, Limu Kosa and Sokoru) had low API levels. Importantly, in 2016 alone, about 50% of the district areas (Dedo, Kersa, Limu Seka, Manna, Omo Nada, Seka Chokorsa and Tiro Afeta) significantly reduced malaria incidence to a very low risk of malaria. A key example is 2019, when 93.33% of the study districts became very low malaria risk areas. Despite these laudable achievements, challenges remain in Agaro Town, Chora Botor and Shebe Sonbo, which continue to face moderate malaria burdens. In general, as the 2017 to 2021 map shows, very low malaria transmission has been achieved in over 86% of once heavily burdened areas (Table 2).

The spatial autocorrelation analysis of malaria incidence using Moran I models to detect spatial clusters revealed the presence of any statistically significant clustering of malaria incidence in the Zone (Moran I statistic = −0.0240, P value = 0.360). This result was further supported by a P- value of 0.39 and a comparable statistic of −0.024 from a Monte Carlo simulation. It is therefore evident that malaria incidence was randomly distributed (dispersed) across the study districts during the observed periods. Accordingly, the spatial incidence map (SIM) (Fig. 5) was created to provide a visual representation of the SIR across districts even in the absence of obvious spatial autocorrelation. However, this map shows variations in malaria incidence, with districts such as Chora Botor and Shebe Sonbo having higher SIR values.

Temporal analysis: seasonal patterns of malaria transmission

The monthly proportions of malaria cases followed a bimodal transmission pattern (Fig. 6a), with pronounced peaks between August and November (with a maximum peak in September), and a second minor peak from March to June, representing the major and minor malaria transmission seasons, respectively. The inter - peak periods: the period between December and February generally shows lower malaria cases, suggesting low or no malaria transmission season. The time series analysis (Fig. 6b) revealed a reciprocal pattern between P. falciparum and P. vivax monthly distributions. P. falciparum prevalence in Jimma zone peaked during the initial to mid-Wet Season (March - September), with a notable increase in the late Dry Season (December - February) despite an earlier decline.

In contrast, P. vivax demonstrates a consistent seasonal peak during the Dry Season (November-February), with its proportions often rising towards the end of the Wet Season and generally diminishing in the early Wet Season (Fig. 6b).

The proportions of the annual distribution of malaria (Fig. 7) also showed a clear decline in trend despite the fluctuations observed at different points in time. Overall, the proportion of malaria cases decreased significantly from 32.87% in 2012 to 9.40% in 2021, a reduction of 71.40%. This was particularly evident between 2014 and 2016, when it fell from 26.46% to 10.05%, a decrease of 62%. Between 2012 and 2014 it then fell by 19.54%. Between 2014 and 2016, there was a significant continuation of these declines, with the proportions dropping to 10.05%, a 62% decrease. Between 2016 and 2019, when the proportion of cases dropped from 42.5% to 5.8%, further achievement was made. But after 2019, a concerning pattern became apparent: the percentage of instances increased. It increased by 76.55% between 2019 and 2020, from 5.8% to 10.24%. However, there was a slight decrease between 2020 and 2021, with the proportion of cases falling to 9.4%, or an 8.2% decrease.

Time series and prediction of malaria trends using ARIMA model

The monthly malaria case data converted into a time series (ts_data) showed that there were strong seasonal fluctuations in addition to that observed in Fig. 6, and the case series was found to be non-stationary, which was confirmed by the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test (ADF, p-value = 0.09214), where the null hypothesis of stationarity was not rejected. Since ARIMA models assume stationarity in the data, this non-stationarity made ts_data unsuitable for ARIMA modeling to predict the next five years (2022–2026). To overcome these challenges, we decomposed the ts_data into its components: the trend, which represents the long-term pattern; seasonality, which captures periodic fluctuations; and residuals, which include the unexplained variation (Fig. 8A). A After applying a 1 st order differencing, the series transformed into a stationary process (ADF test p-value = 0.01), where we failed to reject the alternative hypothesis of stationarity. This transformation allowed us to examine the decomposed components of the difference series (Fig. 8B), which provided a solid basis for selecting appropriate time series models to achieve accurate forecasts.

Fig. 8Time series decomposition: (A) original ts_data exhibited non-stationary with trend, seasonal, and residual components. (B) Differenced time series demonstrated stationarity.

Time series decomposition: (A) original ts_data exhibited non-stationary with trend, seasonal, and residual components. (B) Differenced time series demonstrated stationarity.

Model selection and validation

We compared three ARIMA models to determine the best fit based on specific criteria (see Table 4). The first model, ARIMA (1,1,1) x (1,0,0) [12], showed no seasonal differencing and had an AIC of 2096.08 and a BIC of 2107.2. The residual analysis for this model revealed marginally no significant autocorrelation (Ljung-Box test p-value = 0.07085) (Table 5).

The second model, ARIMA (0,1,3) x (1,1,0) [12], included one degree of seasonal differencing and demonstrated an AIC of 1886.47 and a BIC of 1899.84. The residuals of this model also showed no evidence of autocorrelation (Ljung-Box test p-value = 0.3009).

In contrast, the third model, ARIMA (2,1,4), selected based on visual observation of patterns in autocorrelation function (ACF) and partial autocorrelation function (PACF) plots (see Fig. 9). This model was specified with P = 2 (included 2 lag observations in the autoregressive component), d = 1 (applied first-order differencing to achieve stationarity), and q = 4 (incorporated four lagged forecast errors in the moving average component). Despite its selection based on these criteria, ARIMA (2,1,4) had higher AIC and BIC of 2110.8 and 2130.25, respectively. Additionally, the residuals of this model exhibited a statistically significant autocorrelation (Ljung-Box test p-value < 0.001).

A lower AIC or BIC indicates a model that not only fits the data well but also avoids excessive complexity while keeping the residuals free of significant autocorrelation. In our study, the AIC values for Model 1 and Model 3 were 10% and 10.63% higher compared to Model 2. Similarly, the BIC of these models were 9.84 and 10.80% higher than that of model 2. This indicates that Model 2 achieves the optimal balance between fit and complexity, making it the most efficient choice among the evaluated models. Therefore, the ARIMA (0,1,3) x (1,1,0) model was selected as the most parsimonious and effective model for predicting malaria trends from 2022 to 2026 in the study districts (Table 4).

Discussion

This study confirmed a significant decline in malaria positivity rate within the Jimma zone over the last decade. With over 1.7 million people screened for malaria over the period in the study districts revealed an overall proportion of malaria was 20.93%. There was a significant decrease from the peak proportion of malaria positivity of 32.97% in 2012 to 9.40% in 2021. However, the distribution of malaria and the observed decline in its incidence across the districts showed variability in space and time.

The results of the current study revealed that children under the age of five constituted small and fluctuating proportions of the total malaria burden over time compared to individuals above five years old. This finding is in agreement with the previous reports from Ethiopia26,33,34 and South Africa35. This could be due to factors such as effective prevention measures, improved healthcare and deployment of health extension workers in each village6. Riley et al. (2001) hypothesized that children under five possess both innate immunity and physiological mechanisms that inhibit parasite growth. Moreover, the absence of primed T-cell responses required to initiate fever in this age group usually result in asymptomatic infections with lower parasite densities36. These factors, in addition to other potential factors such as effective prevention measures, contribute to the lower malaria prevalence observed in this age group It follows that, distribution of the distribution sex showed that males appeared to be infected than females, which is consistent with a recent study from Uganda37, a report from Oromia region of Ethiopia26, and southern part of the country34. This suggests that potential sex-based difference in susceptibility to malaria which could be attributed to differences in occupational such as agricultural activities38, behavioral such as night outdoor sleeping39 and activities40, It has been suggested that behavioral factors such as alcohol consumption increase the likelihood of mosquito bites in males41 and that physiological factors such as post-pubertal hormonal changes make males more susceptible to mosquito bites42.

The present study found that P. falciparum was a predominant species followed by a significant proportion of P. vivax and a decreasing trend in mixed infections. This is in agreement with both the global and national trends. Globally, a higher proportion of malaria- related morbidity are caused by P. falciparum, with over 95% of the cases in Africa due to this parasite2. Our findings also consistent with national figures, where P. falciparum remains the predominant Plasmodium species which accounts for 71.3% of cases in Ethiopia. This dominance is followed by P. vivax at 20.9%, while mixed infections are less common, representing 1.1% of total cases4. However, our finding of P. falciparum dominance is slightly higher than in a ten-year trend analysis of malaria cases around the Arjo-Didessa sugar developments sites in southwest Oromia, where P. falciparum accounted for 48% of cases and had a comparable positivity rate to P. vivax (42 0.4%), while mixed infections in our study were lower than 9.6% in a reported from Arjo-Didessa26. Likewise, the distribution of Plasmodium species in our study is consistent with studies reported from the western43 and southern part of Ethiopia34 and also a study that focused the national malaria survey44, reported that P. falciparum accounted for 56% of the total infections, indicating its high dominance in Ethiopia44.

The results of this study also showed a clear spatial pattern in malaria distribution. Notably, districts that previously had disproportionately high malaria burdens experienced a spatial contraction in transmission between 2012 and 2021 in Jimma zone. Over the period, the API decreased dramatically from 87.97 to 3.40 per 1000 at-risk populations, categorized the zone as a very low malaria risk area by 2021, which is consistent with recent national malaria stratification4. Despite these successes, our malaria risk map shows that Shebe Sonbo and Chora Botor remain low and medium risk areas, respectively. However, this overall decline in malaria incidence in the Jimma zone is consistent with the nationwide figure recently reported by Teka et al. (2023). Their analysis of national malaria surveillance data shows a 62% decline in malaria incidence between 2014 and 2018 in Ethiopia44. Nonetheless, the observed decrease in incidence could be attributed to the massive implementation of malaria control measures across the country45, such as vector control, indoor residual spraying, the widespread deployments of health extension workers, and the expansion of healthcare facilities and community involvements. These measures might have greatly improved public access to diagnosis and treatment46,47. In contrast to these successes, between 2019 and 2020, the API increased from 2.55 to 4.19 per 1000 per population at risk, an increase of 64.31% and indicating a slight upward trend. This is likely due to disruptions in service delivery and malaria intervention activities during the global COVID-19 pandemic48.

Additionally, SIR-based incidence map demonstrated malaria cold spot areas, and a spatial autocorrelation analysis using Moran’s I also revealed a random pattern of malaria transmission in the Jimma zone that is local and spatially independent, with transmissions limited to localized areas. It can be implied as the occurrence of malaria in one district had no significant impact on the likelihood of incidence in neighboring districts over time. Thus, factors that might have contributed to this pattern could be localized environmental factors, such as water bodies and microclimates49,50 or vegetation coverages51–53 that might have created breeding ground for mosquitoes, or effective local interventions such as targeted malaria control efforts like distribution of bed nets, indoor residual spaying, early diagnosis and treatment that might have been successful in containing the spread of the disease within localized areas4–6.

Taken together, the lack of significant spatial clustering as indicated by Moran’s I test suggests that malaria incidence is not spatially dependent across the studied districts. However, the SIM identifies areas with relatively high risk, such as Chora Botor and Shebe, which may still necessitate vigilance and targeted interventions. The data indicates that the period between 2018 and 2019 was the malaria bottleneck Jimma zone, implying that there could be a missed opportunity to eliminate malaria.

In the current study, the results of temporal analysis demonstrated distinct seasonal patterns of malaria transmission over the period. This study identified two malaria transmission seasons in the Jimma zone over the years: the high transmission occurred from August to November, peaking in September, following the main rainy season. A less intense transmission period was observed from March to July, with a peak in May and June, coinciding with a minor rainy season from April to June. This is due to the fact that water bodies create breeding ground for mosquitoes. This finding is consistent with the seasonal malaria transmission trends in Ethiopia4,7. As indicated elsewhere above, the annual proportions of malaria cases over the periods showed a general decline, with a concerning upward trajectory particularly between 2019 and 2020. In line with the Global Technical Strategy (GTS) for Malaria 2016–2023055, the implementation of the malaria intervention across the country as well as the decentralized regional, zonal and local context of prevention and control efforts may have been significant contributing factors observed decline in malaria trends in the zone.

After accounting for the observed seasonal trend in malaria cases, three ARIMA models were tested, and ARIMA (0, 1, 3) x (1, 1, 0) [12] was determined to be the best fit. The forecast for the coming period anticipates a continuation of the recent stability, with no significant upward or downward trends expected. The slight fluctuations in the forecast values reflect the seasonality captured by the model. As a result, it predicts minor seasonal variations over the forecast period. However, as we project further into the future, the prediction intervals widen, indicating increased uncertainty—a common feature of time series forecasting that emphasizes the unpredictability of long-term predictions55. The outcomes of the model’s forecasts from 2022 to 2026 suggest that the series will remain relatively stable, with minor seasonal fluctuations and increasing uncertainty over time. That means, while the forecast is cautious and assumes that data values will remain roughly constant, the prediction intervals reflect the increasing uncertainty as time moves in to the future, which is the inherent limitation of the time series models56.

Limitations of the study

This study had limitations. First, the available malaria data from the Jimma Zone health office were collected only at the district level and categorized cases into just two age groups: < 5 and ≥ 5, restricting detailed analysis of specific cohorts such as school-aged children (7–15 years), reproductive-age adults (15–49 years), and the elderly (64 + years). Second, the exclusion of malaria cases from private clinics may have resulted in underestimating the true burden of the disease. Lastly, important factors such as climate variables (temperature, rainfall, humidity), topographic features, intervention coverage, and other environmental determinants were not included in the analysis of this study.

Conclusion

Our analyses of this study reveal a significant decline in malaria incidence in Jimma Zone between 2012 and 2021, resulting in a very low-risk status by 2021. Despite this progress, the disease remains a potential public health threat, particularly in historically high-transmission areas like Cholar Botor and Shebe Sonbo. Of the two the Plasmodium species, P. falciparum predominated, though P. vivax accounted for a notable proportion.

Malaria transmission exhibited high seasonality, with case increase from August to November (post-main rainy season) and March to June (post-short rainy periods), emphasizing the need for targeted interventions during these times. Our spatial autocorrelation analysis indicated random and spatially independent transmission over time, suggesting no clear spatial patterns.

Given these findings, we recommend intensifying surveillance in historically high-risk districts like Cholar Botor and Shebe Sonbo. It is also crucial to maintain current control measures while concurrently adapting strategies based on spatial data to ensure prompt responses to any emerging transmission patterns. While current malaria control appears effective, continuous vigilance is vital to monitor dynamic changes. Our forecast suggests the current malaria trend will persist in the near future with expected high seasonality. Readers should interpret these findings with caution, recognizing the inherent uncertainties in the model’s predictions.

To gain a more comprehensive understanding of malaria dynamics, future studies should incorporate key factors like climate variables (temperature, rainfall, humidity), topographic features, intervention coverage, and other environmental determinants, which were not included in our analysis.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted in the Jimma Zone, the largest zone situated in the southwestern part of the Oromia Regional State (Fig. 12) in Ethiopia. Located at 7°13’ to 8°56’ north latitude and 35°49’ to 38°38’ east longitude, the zone borders the East Wollega Zone (north), East and Southwest Shawa Zones (northeast), Southern Nations and People’s Region (south and southeast), and Illubabor Zone (west). Administratively, it comprises 20 rural districts and 2 urban centers: Jimma town (capital of Jimma zone) and Agaro town57. As of July 2023, the recent estimate showed that Jimma Zone has a population of 3,638,103 with nearly equal number of males and females58. The zone had a comprehensive healthcare system, with the public health facilities comprising 8 government hospitals (5 primary, 3 general), 122 health centers, and 513 health posts supported by about 1461 health extension workers. Additionally, the private sector contributed with 3 hospitals and 156 small and medium-sized clinics59. Beyond its administrative characteristics, Jimma Zone is known for its rich natural resources, distinct climate and biodiversity sphere. Renowned for its coffee cultivation and production within the Oromia region, the zone also receives abundant rainfall, making it one of the wettest areas in the region. Annual precipitation reaches up to 1,500 mm (60 inches), with over 100 mm (4 inches) falling monthly between April and September. Jimma also experiences year-round warmth, with temperatures ranging from 9 °C to 28 °C. It rarely dips below 6 °C or exceeds 31 °C. The warmest period, lasting from December to April, sees average daily highs surpassing 27°C61. Communicable diseases, including malaria, account for approximately 60–80% of health concerns in the Jimma Zone. Malaria is endemic, exhibiting seasonal variations linked to Jimma’s climatic factors. These factors are believed to contribute to the disease’s persistence in the region. Notably, local health facilities are currently reporting characteristics of malaria outbreaks within specific districts [Jimma Zone Health Office, unpublished]61.

Study design and period

This study employed a retrospective design, from September 2021 to January 2023.

Data sources

Monthly malaria case data from January 2012 to December 2021 were obtained from Jimma Zone Health Office, Disease prevention and control department. This dataset included information from all 20 districts within the zone. For each district, details such as name of the districts, year, month, total number of individuals screened for malaria (using both microscopy and rapid diagnostic testcard), the monthly confirmed positive cases, the Plasmodium species detected (P. falciparum, P. vivax, or mixed infections), and demographic details included (sex and age group: <5 and ≥ 5). These data followed the standardized malaria registry format of the zone, or Oromia region. Note that data for five districts were incomplete and excluded from the analysis, resulting in a total of 15 districts being included in the study.

Furthermore, to account for population at risk over the period, estimates of the annual total population for each district were obtained from the online repository of the Ethiopian Statistical Service population website62. Spatial data like the polygon shapefiles of the district-level administrative boundaries of the Oromia Regional State in Ethiopia were retrieved from the DIVA-GIS website63. In accordance with WHO guidelines64, a malaria case in study was defined as the presence of malaria parasites in the blood of an individual, confirmed either by microscopy or a RDT.

Statistical analysis

After entering and checking the data for completeness in Microsoft Excel, it was imported into a free accessible RStudio version 2024.04.0–735.066 integrated with R software (the R Project for Statistical Computing) version 4.4.167 for analysis.

Total malaria cases, trends, and seasonality

To take into account the epidemiological aspects of malaria, the total malaria cases in the study districts were analyzed and the proportions of positive cases by age group and sex were also calculated. We also computed and graphed the monthly and annual proportions of malaria cases for each to examine the variation in malaria transmission over the specified time period. The number of reported malaria cases relative to the population at risk was then used to compute the annual malaria incidence.

Based on the established epidemiological practices67, this study employed two key metrics to examine the burden of the disease: annual parasite incidence (API) and test Positivity rate (TPR). Therefore, the API per 1,000 people at risk was estimated by dividing the number of confirmed malaria cases in a year by the population at risk and multiplying by 1,00069. The resulting value, expressed as the number of cases per 1,000 at-risk population, provides a crucial overview of the intensity of malaria transmission in the area studied. Spatial autocorrelation analysis, and identification of malaria hotspots and cold spots areas

Spatial autocorrelation of malaria incidence was analyzed using Moran’s I statistic in R version 4.4.1. This analysis examined how malaria incidence in one district relates to that in neighboring districts. The critical distance for the analysis was determined by a Global Moran’s I analysis. The Global Moran’s I statistic ranges from − 1 to 1 and tests whether values ​​at one location are independent of those at other locations. Positive values ​​indicate positive spatial autocorrelation, negative values ​​indicate negative autocorrelation, and zero indicates a random spatial pattern. Rejection of the null hypothesis suggests a non-random spatial distribution of malaria incidence and helps identify potential high or low incidence clusters12,72,73. Time series analysis and prediction of malaria trends from 2022 to 2026

A times series (ts) is a sequence of data points collected/recorded at successive points in time, often at uniform intervals. In this context, a series of data points where each data point represents the total number of malaria cases (observed data) recorded in a specific month over a period of ten years. Thus, ts analysis involves examining this data to uncover patterns such as trends, seasonal effects and cycles in observed malaria cases over the decade. Ultimately, forecasting using methods like ARIMA to predict future values based on such historical data.

A time series is a sequence of data points collected at consecutive points in time, usually at equal interval of periods. In this context, it is a series of data points, where each data point represented the total number of malaria cases (observed data) collected in a given month over a ten-year period. Therefore, time series analysis in our context involved analysis of these data to reveal patterns such as trends, seasonal effects and cycles in observed malaria cases over the decade. Eventually, forecasts are made using time series model, ARIMA to predict future values ​​based on such historical data76. Prior to applying the ARIMA method, it was crucial to confirm whether time series was stationary, that means its mean and variance remained constant over time. To check this, we conducted a statistical test called the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF), setting the statistical significance level at p-value < 0.05 to test the null hypothesis (H₀) that states that the time series has a unit root, implying non-stationarity. If the p-value falls below 0.05, we reject the null hypothesis, indicating that the series is stationary. If the p-value exceeds 0.05, the null hypothesis is not rejected, suggesting that the time series may need to be transformed to achieve stationarity. If the time series was non-stationary, we would apply differencing to stabilize the mean, typically starting with first differencing (d = 1). After differencing, we recheck for stationarity. If stationarity is achieved, we reject H₀, and no further differencing is necessary, and proceed with the subsequent steps of the ARIMA modeling process55.

The next step was model identification, which was primarily based on the identification of the autoregressive (AR) and moving average (MA) components and was carried out by examining the partial autocorrelation function (PACF) and autocorrelation function (ACF) plots of the differenced time series (Fig 0.5.10). For the AR component (p), the PACF plot was analyzed to determine the lag at which the plot showed a clear increase outside the significant limits, followed by a rapid decay around zero, indicating the corresponding order. Similarly, the MA component (q) was determined from the ACF plot of the differenced series, where a significant spike at a particular lag, followed by a sharp drop, indications of the order of the MA term. After the series was differenced, these steps were required to guarantee stationarity, which in turn aided in choosing the AR and MA parameters for the ARIMA model77,78.

The diagnostic tests: After fitting the model, the residuals were examined to ensure that they resembled white noise. This included performing the Ljung-Box test to check autocorrelation in residuals, analyzing the ACF and PACF plots for the residuals, and assessing the histogram plot to determine whether the residuals assumed normality. In the end, three ARIMA models were compared using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Finally, the model with the lowest AIC and BIC values ​​was selected to balance fit and complexity76. And in the end, the final ARIMA model was used to generate forecasts from 2022 to 2026, including prediction intervals (at 95% CI) to account for uncertainties.
