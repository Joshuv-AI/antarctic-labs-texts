Humidity may amplify the temperature-related health risks in the context of climate change
Authors: Yiling He, Wei Dong, Liang Zhao, Huibin Wang, Wenjia Cai, Zhao Liu, Hualiang Lin, Wenjun Ma, Cunrui Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Humidity and temperature may pose joint effects on human health. However, current temperature-related risk assessments generally focus on univariate temperature statistics, leading to inaccurate risk estimation. By combining temperature and humidity intensities as compound temperature-humidity events (hereinafter as compound events), we examined the effects of humidity in temperature-related health risks in the context of climate change in China using ambulance dispatch data, and further determined the high-risk types and thresholds of compound events. After assessing the health risks, compound events are defined as: i) warm and wet event: temperature ≥ 90th, humidity ≥ 55th and 70%; ii) warm and dry event: temperature ≥ 80th, humidity ≤ 10th; iii) cold and wet event: temperature ≤ 15th, humidity ≥ 70th and 70%; iv) cold and dry event: temperature ≤ 5th and humidity ≤ 25th. Results reveal that humidity can amplify the temperature-related health risks. Higher risks were found during cold and dry events and warm and wet events, posing risks as 1.102 (1.045–1.161) and 1.093 (1.068–1.118) respectively. Compound events mainly occurred in Southeast China during 1979–2019, while the frequency and gripped regions are projected to increase by 2100, and climate change will amplify these trends. Since humidity would exacerbate temperature-related health risks, therefore multiple meteorological parameters are needed in defining adverse weather conditions and conducting risk assessment accurately.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48668-z.

Introduction

Extreme temperature exposures are known to contribute to an increase in mortality and morbidity, posing significant public health problems around the world1–8. For example, during the 1995 Chicago heatwave, there were over 700 excess deaths in a single day9. The well-known 2003 heatwaves led to 15,000 excess deaths in France alone10, and over 70,000 deaths across Europe11. The 2008 cold spell in fifteen provinces of southern China increased mortality by 43.8% (95% CI: 34.8% ~ 53.4%), resulting 148,279 excess deaths12. Previous studies have shown that both high and low humidity can pose adverse effects on human health13,14. Humidity will make temperatures feel hotter or colder than it actually is, as it affects evaporation from human skin. In the hot and humid environment, sweating capability of human body will be reduced, resulting in rising body temperature and physical discomfort. While during the cold weather, the chilling effect of atmospheric humid can lower the body temperature. Therefore, a deep investment of the role of humidity in the relationship between temperature and health risks would contribute to a more appropriate assessment of temperature-related health risks.

Various temperature indices have been developed to allow for the combination of temperature and humidity, such as humidex, apparent temperature (AT) or wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT). These indices have been argued to do better than dry-bulb temperature in the health impacts assessment15–20, as they regard humidity as an integral component of environmental exposures rather than a confounding factor to be controlled in the model, thus incorporating the synergistic effects of temperature and humidity on human thermal perception. Bernard and Iheanacho showed that WBGT can more accurately predict health events related to occupational heat stress20. A study by Min et al. in South Korea also confirmed that temperature indices combined with humidity have a significantly higher correlation with heat-related mortality than single temperature indices17.

Although composite indices simplify analysis by integrating temperature and humidity into a single value, they mask differences in health risks under different temperature–humidity combinations. For example, the same WBGT value may arise from “high temperature and low humidity” and “low temperature and high humidity”, which are the two distinct environments with different mechanisms of impact and risk levels for human health. Moreover, with the process of climate change, future frequency and intensity of compound heat-humidity events are projected to escalate globally, resulting rising population exposure to the compound events21–26. Oversimplification of these complex exposures may result in underestimation of heat-related health risks. Thus, under the global warming with potentially increased compound events in the future, improved understanding of the variability of compound events and their health impacts has been highlighted.

To address the challenge of distinguishing health risk of different intensity of temperature and humidity exposures, we introduce a new definition of compound temperature-humidity event, which is selected from various combinations of temperature and humidity, thus to identify the role of humidity in temperature-related health risks. Specifically, we first assess the health risks under different compound events exposures, so as to identify the type and the thresholds of the events with high risks. We then evaluate that whether exposure to the compound events posing a higher risk than the temperature alone. We further investigate health impacts of compound temperature-humidity events over China based on current climate, and then estimate impacts during the historical and future periods under global warming scenarios. Results from this study show humidity would amplify the temperature-related health impacts, and therefore raising importance of using compound temperature indicators in the temperature-related health assessment.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study was focused on China, a climate change sensitive area with high population density, and rapid urbanization. Study was conducted in the urban districts of 13 cities, which includes Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Hangzhou, Ningbo, Taizhou, Zhoushan, Lishui, Zhengzhou, Luoyang, Xuchang, Jinan, and Huairou district in Beijing. All of the selected areas are the densely populated in China. These cities are distributed across eastern, central and northern China, covering approximately 70% of the national population27. Furthermore, the study cities span three major climate zones (subtropical monsoon, temperate monsoon, temperate continental) and encompass various urbanization levels, including megacities, large cities, medium-sized cities.

Morbidity data

Emergency ambulance dispatches, the front-line of medical services, usually relate to acute health outcomes. The number of dispatches is considered indicative of the extent of people affected by hazardous weather, and is therefore selected as the health outcome in this study. Daily records of emergency ambulance dispatches in each city were obtained from the institutions such as the Centers for Disease Control (CDC) and local Ambulance Service Centers where collect ambulance dispatch information for the whole city, covering the longest time span of 2013–2019.

Climate data

Daily observations of mean temperatures and relative humidity were obtained from the China Meteorological Data Sharing Service System (http://data.cma.cn/en). To allow for a sensitivity analysis, air pollution data was obtained from the National Urban Air Quality Real-time Publishing Platform (http://106.37.208.233:20035). The air pollutants considered were particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter less than 2.5 μm (PM2.5), ozone (O3), and sulfur dioxide (SO2).

Projections of daily mean temperature and relative humidity were obtained from 12 global climate models (GCMs), which are provided by the latest Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) (https://esgf-node.llnl.gov/search/cmip6/) (See Supplementary Table S1). To reduce uncertainties in future projections, we used a multi-model ensemble mean (MME) approach. As shown in previous studies, a sufficiently large CMIP6 ensemble (e.g., ≥ 10 models) effectively suppresses individual biases and internal variability, leading to more stable and robust climate outcomes28. These climate projections are driven by a new set of emissions and land use scenarios, which are rooted in socioeconomic pathways (shared socioeconomic pathways, SSPs) work in harmony with representative concentration pathways (RCPs) through shared policy assumptions to make the future scenarios more reasonable29,30. In this study, we considered three integrated scenarios, including SSP126 (combination of SSP1 and RCP2.6), SSP245 (combination of SSP2 and RCP4.5) and SSP585 (combination of SSP5 and RCP8.5), to represent the low, medium and high-end of future emissions and warming, respectively.

Observed climate data in China during 1979–2019 were obtained from ECMWF Reanalysis v5 (ERA5) project, which included temperature and dew-point temperature data (at 2 m above the land surface) at 0.25° × 0.25° spatial resolution. ERA5 is the fifth generation of ECMWF atmospheric reanalysis of the global climate, covering the period from 1950 to present31. With a high spatial resolution and a long, consistent record, ERA5 is a widely used and reliable reanalysis dataset. Evaluations show it accurately reproduces the spatiotemporal patterns of temperature and precipitation over China, including climatology, trends, and interannual variability32,33. Data was downloaded from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF, https://www.ecmwf.int/en/forecasts/datasets/reanalysis-datasets/era5 ). Relative humidity (at 2 m above the land surface) were then calculated by 2-m temperature and dew point temperature. In line with previous researches, we downscaled the spatial resolution of the climate data from 0.25° × 0.25° to 0.5° × 0.5° to ensure spatial consistency34–36. Meanwhile, we used the bilinear interpolation for regridding to minimize the loss of high-resolution information.

Population data

The population of 13 cities during the baseline period (2013–2019) was obtained from the Municipal Bureau of Statistics in each city. Historical population data in China during 1979 and 2000 was obtained from the inter-sectoral impact model intercomparison project (ISIMIP), which provide annual global population data for the years 1861–2005 on a 0.5° × 0.5°grid (https://www.isimip.org/gettingstarted/details/31/). And annual population data during 2001–2010 was generated from the gridded population of the world, version 4 (GPWv4), which is in line with the previous study6. Projections of future population changes in China based on SSP scenarios were obtained from Chen, et al.37. The main assumption for population growth in SSP scenarios is dependent on fertility, mortality, migration, and education rates.

Association between daily ambulance dispatches and daily mean temperature

To assess if unfavorable temperature would pose adverse health impacts on ambulance dispatches, a time-series quasi-Poisson regression analysis was used to estimate the city-specific ambulance dispatch risk (relative risk, RR) associated with daily mean temperature. A distributed lag non-linear model (DLNM) was used to examine delayed effects38. Consistent with previous studies, delayed effects were assessed for up to 7 days. The selection of degrees of freedom (df) was based on minimizing Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC)39. Specifically, we used a natural cubic spline with 3 df for temperature and a natural cubic spline with 3 df for lag to capture nonlinear temperature effects and lagged effects respectively. A natural cubic spline with 3 df per year was used to control for the potential confounding effect of long-term trends. Another time-varying confounders and known predictors of ambulance dispatches were controlled in the model; these were relative humidity, air pollutants, day of week and seasonality.

Define the compound events with high risks

There is no standard compound events definition worldwide, although they are commonly defined as concurrence of extreme temperature event with extreme humidity event identified by relative thresholds. Compound temperature-humidity events were further specified by the intensity of temperature and humidity, as: i) compound high temperature with high humidity event (warm and wet event); ii) compound high temperature with low humidity event (warm and dry event); iii) compound low temperature with high humidity event (cold and wet event); iv) compound low temperature with low humidity event (cold and dry event). To derive a universal definition and to easily conduct spatial comparison for the entire China, here we utilized the percentile threshold definition for the event which is consistent with previous studies40,41. In the present study, a series of compound events were defined to figure out the thresholds of temperature and humidity with high risks and introduced as below.

On the first step, we classified compound events with refined groups. In line with previous studies, we set multi-gradient thresholds at 5th percentile intervals for temperature and relative humidity distributions to define high-risk events of varying severity42–44. By comparing the associated health risks across different thresholds, we identified the optimal definition criteria for such events and evaluated their health risks. Specifically, daily mean temperature and relative humidity of each city were divided from 5th percentile to 95th percentile of distributions, and 20 groups of temperature and humidity were obtained respectively. Then, 400 types of compound events were obtained by pairwise combination of humidity and temperature groups, presenting as a square grid with 20*20 grids. Estimating the distribution of humidity conditional on temperature provides flexibility for quantifying compound exposure by humidity and temperature simultaneously. This approach allows us to look at humidity at a given temperature or temperature at given humidity quantile.

On the second step, to estimate risks during compound events with different thresholds, a binary event variable was used for each day in the DLNM model. Days with compound event or individual event were categorized as 1 while a normal day was categorized as 0. Separate models were used to estimate risks of heat-related ambulance dispatches for each type of events. To obtain national-level pooled estimates separately for each type of compound events, the estimated city-specific associations (β) were pooled using a univariate meta-analysis. Heterogeneity was assessed through a multivariate extension of the I2 index and Cochran Q test, which quantifies the percentage of variability due to true differences across communities.

On the third step, we defined the compound temperature-humidity events in terms of high risk and extreme criteria. For the high-risk identification, we first drew a contour map with 20*20 grids to present the risks of compound events. The inflection point where health risks rise sharply and significantly is defined as the threshold for high-risk events. Then we drew a contour map with 20*20 grids to present the risks of compound events. The temperature and humidity were divided into high and low exposure levels at 50th percentile interval. This grid graph was then divided into four quadrants, representing four categories of compound events mentioned previously. For the extreme intensity classification, we connected the temperature and humidity corresponding to their 50th percentile in each quadrant, dividing four quadrants into outer and inner triangle that lie farther and closer to the grid center, respectively. The outer triangle is the aggregation of compound events that meet the extreme criterion. And finally, in the cluster of compound events that meeting the high-risk and extreme criteria, the temperature and humidity that closest to the 50th percentile of their distribution were defined as the thresholds of compound events with high risks.

To clarify whether the impact of compound events on ambulance dispatch is greater than that defined by a single temperature variable, we evaluate the risks of ambulance dispatches during compound events and temperature events with the same temperature thresholds.

Results

Patterns of ambulance dispatches and thermal characteristics

A total number of 2.46 million ambulance dispatches were recorded during the study periods (Table S2). Warmer and wetter weather was found in southern cities compared with cities in northern part of China. The relationship between mean temperature and ambulance dispatches followed a U-shaped curve (Figure S1). The optimum temperature (OT), corresponding to the lowest point in the exposure–response curve, was higher in the southern cities and lower in the northern cities. Increased risks were found during both hot and cold days, with higher risks during extreme temperatures.

Thresholds of high-risk compound temperature-humidity events

Pooled risks of ambulance dispatches during compound temperature-humidity events in China were presented in Fig. 1. Detailed risk estimates, the three-dimensional plot illustrating the temporal pattern of risk variations, and city-specific risk results are provided in the Supplementary Material (Figure S2-S4). As shown in Fig. 1 and Figure S3, the corresponding coverage of temperature and humidity distributions exhibits a substantial variation as the risks increases from 1 to 1.08. In contrast, minor fluctuation in temperature and humidity distributions result in a significant rise in health risks when RR ≥ 1.08. Accordingly, this study sets RR = 1.08 (corresponding to the 80th percentile of the risk distribution) as the threshold for high-risk events. As suggest by previous studies, the recommended wet condition was defined as relative humidity higher than 70%45,46. Sensitivity analysis

In the sensitivity analysis, we used generalized additive model (GAM) to examine association between meteorological exposure and health effects. Results showed a similar pattern in the exposure–response curve (Figure S5). In addition, we performed robustness checks by varying the maximum lag days (6—8 days) of compound events, and the degrees of freedom for the long-term trend (4—6 per year), and the results obtained were consistent with those from the main model (Table S3).

Discussion

In present study, the modification effects of humidity in temperature-related health impacts have been discerned in China. This is different from previous studies that merge several meteorological factors into a single indicator. Conversely, we assessed the health risks of temperature and humidity exposures in all combinations. The present study also identified the types and thresholds of compound temperature-humidity exposure with high risks. We further assessed the changes in the intensity and spatial distribution of risks due to compound events exposure during historical period (1981–2010) and future period (2020–2100) under different warming scenarios. Results provide a more robust and realistic projections of future heat-related health risks in a warming world. The analysis introduced in this study extends beyond the previous studies by highlighting the multiple meteorological parameters in defining adverse weather conditions and risk projections, which are distinct from those that using temperature alone to predict health impacts.

Our results suggest that the compound exposure of high-humidity and temperature poses higher risks than that of compound low-humidity and temperature events. One reason that high humidity makes hot or cold weather more unbearable is that increased relative humidity intensifies the perceived heat or cold, thereby reducing human temperature tolerance. Additionally, during heat and humid events, higher amount of water vapor will reduce the evaporation rate of skin moisture. As a result, it reduces the effectiveness of sweating in cooling the body, thus increasing the incidence of heat-related diseases47–50. Using global urban and rural weather station data, Zhang et al. showed that humid climates raise the wet-bulb temperature (Tw), a key indicator of heat stress, leading to an additional 2—6 dangerous heat-stress days each summer in humid regions51. Liang et al. also found higher morbidity risks occurred in humid-heat weather in Shanghai, China52. Since ambulance dispatch data does not include specific etiological information, future studies should incorporate etiology-specific data for further stratification.

Temperature-related impacts are often related to multiple drivers. However, the complexities are currently neglected with common heat-related health assessment, assuming that a single temperature value can adequately predicts health impacts53–56. Although an array of heat indices has been developed to account for the effects of multiple meteorological factors, such as temperature, humidity and wind57–59. However, these indices eventually merged into a single and new index, such as WBGT, AT. In that case, the risk of different combination of temperature and humidity cannot be distinguished. To be specific, if the value of WBGT during high temperature and low humidity event is the same as that during low temperature and high humidity, then the risks reported by WBGT would be the same, which would mask the health impacts of different weather exposures. Moreover, the threshold values of WBGT with high risks cannot be directly translated into specific temperature and humidity criteria. This limitation hinders the development of meteorological early warning systems and also prevents the public from taking preventive measures based on weather forecasts. Oversimplification of these complex responses can result in underestimate the risks of temperature-related health impacts as illustrated by examples in this research.

To identify the adverse meteorological conditions posed high risks on human health, this study comprehensively evaluated the health risks under different meteorological conditions, so as to determine the high-risk temperature and humidity thresholds. Conventional investigation of temperature-related health impacts generally set the threshold of adverse temperature empirically. For example, the intensity of extreme heat or heat wave events was 90th, 95th or 98th percentile of the temperature range. However, the present results reveal that the threshold of compound events is lower than that of extreme heat or heat wave events. The reason behind this might be that the combined effects of temperature and humidity may amplify the effect of temperature on health. Therefore, a combination of non-extreme temperature and humidity exposure will produce high risks.

The identified compound thresholds in the present study (heat ≥ 80th/90th percentile, cold ≤ 5th/15th percentile) are generally consistent with findings from single-factor meteorological studies. Traditional studies commonly define extreme temperature thresholds empirically; for instance, heat or heatwave events are often defined using 95th, 98th, or 99th percentiles of temperature60,61. Notably, our results indicate that compound event thresholds are lower, indicating that at the same temperature intense, compound temperature-humidity exposure yields higher risks than temperature alone, so the high-risk threshold for compound events can be identified at a less extreme temperature percentile, which is more sensitive to capturing the adverse health effects of compound meteorological conditions.

With the process of climate change, annual total number of ambulance dispatches attributable to compound events showed an inverted U-pattern that increase first and then decrease. This is because ANs caused by cold events will continuous to decline, while ANs caused by heat events will increase in the early and middle of the twenty-first century, and tend to be stable at the end of the twenty-first century. Due to the impacts of climate change, different emission scenarios will change the composition of ANs related to compound events62–64. Nearly a third of ANs are caused by cold events under the low emission scenario, while heat and humidity events take the majority under the higher emission scenarios. In addition, higher emission scenarios would exacerbate the spatial heterogeneity of effects of compound events on ambulance dispatches in China. Although concerns about changes in climate extremes over China have been widely raised over the past decades, no research to date have considered health risks of compound temperature-humidity events over China, which may have more severe impacts on human society than single climate extremes and lie a lot of uncertainty to reveal.

This study has several limitations. First, risk estimates were derived from 13 Chinese cities; while nationally representative to some extent, broader regional data (such as Northwest and Northeast China, high-altitude areas such as the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau) are needed to validate findings. Second, the thresholds of compound events with high-risks were based on morbidity, thus generalizing to mortality and other health outcomes requires caution. Third, heat stress is also affected by solar radiation, wind, human activity, and clothing, which were not included. Fourth, future projections relied on fixed urban boundaries without accounting for urban expansion, potentially introducing uncertainties. Moreover, long-term population adaptation was not explicitly considered. Higher-resolution data and integration of urban planning and adaptive capacity models are recommended for future research. Despite these limitations, this study provides a basis for understanding high-risk compound temperature-humidity events and supports further refined projections.

Considering adverse meteorological conditions as a compound event and decomposing them to discern the thresholds that trigger health impacts can improve risk modelling and assessment, thus providing a pathway to better understand the health impacts of climate change. Compound exposure of multiple meteorological factors exacerbates the adverse health impacts, with non-extreme temperature and humidity can pose high risks. With the ongoing climate change and population changes, the frequency and spatial extent of compound events are expected to increase, result in amplifying their impacts on health. Our findings support arguments for embedding more sophisticated human heat stress models into health impacts assessment and climate projections, thereby providing more robust and realistic projections of future heat-related health risks in a warming world.
