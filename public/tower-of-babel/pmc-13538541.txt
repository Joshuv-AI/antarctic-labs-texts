Considerations for the dynamic role of bile acids during oral drug discovery and development
Authors: Zahraa Al-Tamimi, Michael J Hageman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKnowledge of gastrointestinal media is crucial for developing successful oral drug products. Bile acids are an essential component in the intestinal lumen, influencing the behavior of orally administered molecules. This review addresses the various roles of bile acids in drug discovery and development, examines the differences between humans and various preclinical models, and discusses how the physicochemical properties of drug molecules may influence their interactions with bile-containing media.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Chemistry, Drug discovery, Gastroenterology

Introduction

Bile acids are considered a vital component of the intestinal media. These biosurfactants typically form mixed micellar systems with phospholipids and other lipolysis products and are known for their major function in the absorption of lipid-soluble vitamins, nutrients, and hydrophobic molecules. Due to their unique structure and function, the bile acids are known to affect the solubilization, dissolution, and permeation of various drug molecules. This review is divided into two sections. The first section will discuss the structure and function of bile acids in humans and various preclinical models. The second section will examine the impact of bile acids on drug molecules from the perspective of the Biopharmaceutics Classification System (BCS) and the Developability Classification System (DCS). The DCS uses key factors (i.e., drug solubility, dissolution rate, and permeability) to assess the developability of the drug across different formulations and to provide a framework for predicting the in vivo performance of the active pharmaceutical ingredient (API). Throughout this review, we will focus on discussing how bile acids affect drug behavior and ultimately oral drug absorption and highlight how the physicochemical properties of the API can be used to anticipate the effect of bile acids on the behavior of orally administered drugs.

Structure and function of bile acids in human and preclinical models

The differences in composition of the intestinal fluid among different preclinical models and humans have been reported comprehensively in the literature1–8. In this section, we aim to highlight the importance of the intestinal composition during drug discovery and development and its relevance to both in vitro and in vivo testing. We will mainly provide an overview of the general species difference (in terms of bile salts and phospholipids) superimposed on potential pH variations. For more in-depth information, we recommend the reader refer back to these excellent reviews1–3. Additionally, interested readers are referred to the articles that introduce and review updates and outcomes of the Oral Biopharmaceutical Tools (OrBiTo) project within the European Union Innovative Medicines Initiative9,10. This project aimed to develop and validate tools to improve the understanding of gastrointestinal (GI) absorption and ultimately enhance predictions of oral drug product performance.

Bile acids in humans

Bile acids (BA) feature a unique structure when compared to ionic surfactants. They are formed from a rigid steroid nucleus that contains a four-ring system and a flexible acidic tail. Namely, rings A, B, and C are six-membered rings, and ring D is a five-membered ring. The ring has the following configuration: cis A/B, trans B/C, and trans C/D ring juncture. The structure of bile acids is depicted in Table 1. This is distinct from the steroid structure, which has trans A/B configuration, and from a typical ionic surfactant, which has a spatially separated, flexible, polar head and a non-polar tail11,12. This unique structure of BA creates curvature and a facial amphiphilicity for the bile acids, with a hydrophilic face, the α-face, that points down the plane and is formed by one to three OH groups. Conversely, a β-face, pointed above the plane, is formed from two methyl groups11,12. In humans, the major bile acids can be classified into two groups:

Primary bile acids are synthesized in the liver from cholesterol. These include cholic acid (CA, 3α7α12α-trihydroxy) and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA, 3α7α-dihydroxy). These primary bile acids are typically found conjugated with taurine or glycine groups at C-24, forming the corresponding bile salts. In humans, the primary bile acids comprise more than 70%, with almost an equal amount of CA and CDCA derivatives. Glycine conjugation predominates in humans compared with taurine (ratio about 2:1)4,12. The conjugated bile acids would be fully ionized at the physiological pH of the small intestine, and thus, impermeable through the cell membranes. As a result, sufficient concentrations of bile acids are reached in the intestinal lumen that permit the formation of a micellar system. This micellar system is essential for the physiological function of bile acid in lipid absorption (see below)11,13.

In healthy humans, bile acids are typically associated with phospholipids (such as lecithin), forming bile acid-phospholipid mixed micelles (BAPMMs)11. BAPMMs play a major role in lipid metabolism, the digestion and absorption of dietary fats and fat-soluble vitamins, and cholesterol solubilization and homeostasis. The presence of bile in a micellar form is necessary for these processes to occur. Additionally, the incorporation of phospholipids into the BAPMM is important for reducing the membranolytic activity of the bile acid monomers13. Bile acids can also activate nuclear receptors and, thus, can influence various metabolic pathways17. Due to the crucial role of bile acids in the solubilization of lipophilic molecules, it is also known that the composition of the intestinal media has a large effect on the solubility, dissolution, and ultimately, the absorption of poorly soluble hydrophobic drugs3. The human intestinal fluid (HIF) has large inter- and intra-subject variability in terms of pH, bile salt and phospholipid content, buffer capacity, osmolarity, and other lipid components, and can vary during certain disease states. These differences in HIF are also significant when comparing fasted versus fed states (Table 2), and have been reviewed previously3,4. Generally, there is a higher concentration of solubilizing bile salts and phospholipids postprandially, with a slightly lower pH. It is also important to mention that the intestinal mixed micellar system is complex in nature, and can vary in shape, size, and composition (i.e., mixed lipid aggregates exist in vivo as opposed to a homogenous micellar system)18,19. These differences may be crucial for the in vivo performance of the drug product and must be taken into consideration during in vitro experimental design and interpretation of results20. Furthermore, individual bile salts exhibit different critical micellar concentration (CMC) values, depending on the hydroxylation of the steroid nucleus. In general, dihydroxy bile salts have a lower CMC value (around 2 mM), while trihydroxy bile salts have a higher CMC value (around 10 mM)21. These reported CMC values might differ depending on the instrumental technique, the ionic strength, and the temperature2,22,23. The addition of phospholipids (i.e, BAPMM) would further lower the CMC23. The implications of these differences on the solubility of the drug are discussed in “Effect on solubility and dissolution”.

Although using aspirates of HIF would better mimic the actual in vivo scenario, they are not readily available, exhibit large variability depending on the sampling and analytical technique, and therefore, might be impractical during routine in vitro assessment of oral formulations20. Therefore, a standardized simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) is routinely used during drug discovery and development.

Bile acids in simulated intestinal fluid (SIF)

During drug discovery, it is a common practice to assess the solubility and dissolution of drug candidates in SIF and select the best compounds that can be successfully developed as oral dosage forms. This is particularly important for drugs with low aqueous solubility (i.e., class Ⅱ and Ⅳ molecules of the Biopharmaceutics Classification System, BCS), where poor water solubility is a main factor limiting their absorption24. The selection of the media during solubility and dissolution screening is critically important during the early stages of discovery, since a very limited amount of the API is often available. Ideally, this media should be able to mirror both the composition and solubilization capacity of the HIF, allowing a good prediction of the drug in vivo behavior during later stages of drug development. However, since the HIF is not attainable in most cases, SIF has been proposed by Galia et al. as a powerful tool during oral drug development5. The most commonly used media in the pharmaceutical industry for solubility and dissolution testing are fasted- and fed-state simulated intestinal fluids (FaSSIF and FeSSIF, respectively), which contain sodium taurocholate (NaTC) as a model bile salt along with phospholipid6,25. NaTC was chosen due to its lower pKa value and, therefore, good solubility over a wide pH range. This medium has been optimized in later years to better reflect the intestinal composition and conditions, and the addition of a mixture of two bile salts (TC and GC), lysolecithin (the hydrolytic product of lecithin), along with biorelevant concentrations of cholesterol, has been proposed in FaSSIF-V36,7 (see Table 2). The use of SIF has been shown to provide good predictions of the in vivo behavior of many small-molecule hydrophobic APIs, and many studies have demonstrated that all generations of FaSSIF correlate well with solubility in FaHIF. However, some in vitro-in vivo discrepancies still exist, and occasional compound-specific deviations cannot be fully eliminated7,26. Ideally, the best predictive in vitro model would contain a mixture of different types of bile salts in representative amounts in the GI tract, along with phospholipids. However, such media would be extremely complex (especially for fed-state media) and might introduce alternative forms of variability during the initial phases of drug discovery, which may impede progress in later stages. As a default, the previously described FaSSIF and FeSSIF are generally the first choices in early studies.

Notably, despite the large variability in HIF, Pyper et al. have shown that there are compositional boundaries to this variability. Using multidimensional analysis of HIF composition, the authors identified 8 bioequivalent media compositions that cover more than 90% of the potential variation in intestinal composition across fasted and fed states and can be used as refined SIF for in vitro solubility measurements27. Later studies were conducted, and the equilibrium solubility of different drugs was measured using the suggested bioequivalent media compositions28,29. The equilibrium solubility ranges obtained using these systems are likely more representative of in vivo conditions than those from large-scale design of experiment (DoE) studies, which might involve media compositions that are not biorelevant. The suggested bioequivalent media could serve as a valuable reference for designing and optimizing SIFs during the preformulation of oral drug candidates. Additionally, these studies revealed the importance of molecular structure and/or shape in influencing the solubility of some compounds in biorelevant media, beyond physicochemical descriptors such as pKa and partition coefficient (logP) (more details are provided in “The influence of bile acids on oral drug behavior”)28.

Bile acids presence within in vivo preclinical models

It is important to consider and understand the BA composition in pharmaceutically relevant preclinical models, as it is critical for anticipating how compositional differences (in terms of BA and BAPMM) affect the translation of in vitro data to preclinical in vivo results and, ultimately, to the clinical in vivo performance of the API, especially in fed and fasted states. A general overview of the species differences in terms of bile acid and phospholipid concentrations, and pH is provided in Tables 1 and 2. (Refer to1,30,31 for more details on animal models, and see2 for details on compositional differences).

There are several differences in the bile acid and phospholipid composition between humans and preclinical models. In rodents, one such difference is the presence of muricholic acids (MCA), which are hydroxylated at the 6-β carbon32. Additionally, there are higher levels of bile salts in rats, which are mainly taurine conjugated (compared to predominantly glycine conjugated in humans)1,3,4,8. The higher levels of the solubilizing biorelevant colloids might lead to higher solubilization of lipophilic drugs, and hence, misleadingly overestimate the absorption when transitioning to clinical models3. Another factor to consider is the absence of a gallbladder in rats, which causes a constant drip of bile salts into the intestine, regardless of the prandial state30,33. Nevertheless, there is an increase in the total plasma BA levels postprandially in rats (although this increase is considered modest compared with humans)34. As discussed by Meola et al., this constant secretion of bile might lead to an underestimation of the solubilizing potential of the formulation, which may be particularly problematic in the case of lipid-based formulations designed to solubilize drugs35. On the other hand, the biliary and gastrointestinal system of the pig is somewhat more similar to humans, and therefore might give a better prediction of the drug absorption in the postprandial state, especially in comparison to the fasted situation30,33,35. Nevertheless, dogs are often used, as a larger preclinical species, to predict food effects, particularly considering the ease of administering and testing actual various oral dosage forms33.

The pH, relative to the pKa of the drug, is also an important factor to consider, as it can impact the inherent drug solubility, stability, and absorption, as well as how it might interact with the BAPMM. For the small intestine, pH values appear similar between the preclinical animal models and humans (Table 2). However, gastric pH in preclinical models seems to show a higher variation compared to humans, as discussed by Bergstrom et al.3. The pH differences become more crucial for weakly acidic or basic drugs (i.e., those with pKa’s in the physiological range of pH 2–8). Using DoE-based investigations, other studies have found that, for acidic drugs, pH is the dominant factor influencing solubility, whereas for basic and neutral compounds, a combination of factors (including pH, bile salts, phospholipids, and free fatty acids) also has a large impact on solubility36–41.

Understanding the differences in bile salt hydrophobicity across preclinical species is also important. Generally, the hydrophobicity of bile salts will change depending on the hydroxylation state of the steroid nucleus and the conjugation state of the acidic tail group42. Conjugation with taurine and glycine decreases the pKa from 5 to 6.5 (for the unconjugated BA) to about 1–4 (for the conjugated BA)16. This will increase bile acidity, ionization, and solubility at the physiological pH of the human intestinal lumen. Additionally, the number and stereochemistry of the OH groups will also affect BA hydrophobicity. The BA with more OH groups will be less hydrophobic; tri-OH < di-OH < mono-OH. The presence of OH at positions 7β (such as UDCA), 6α (such as HC), and 6β (such as MCA) is associated with more hydrophilic BA, due to intrinsic resistance to the loss of the associated water molecules, when in that position42. For example, humans have a higher proportion of dihydroxy bile salts, which are more hydrophobic in nature. In contrast, rats have a higher proportion of the more hydrophilic taurocholate and muricholic acids43. The major bile salt in pigs is a more hydrophilic HC compared with human bile14. It is expected that these species differences would be more important to consider for poorly soluble drugs, especially those whose solvation potential is lower (i.e., higher partition coefficient, logP) and often delivered with lipid-based formulations, where solubilization by bile salt micelles and mixed micelles avoids the precipitation of the drugs. Despite these differences in bile acid hydrophobicity, anticipating the physiological implications on the drug solubilization and overall oral formulation behavior is complex. This is mainly due to the diversified, inhomogeneous nature of the bile acid pool, both in humans and preclinical models. The inhomogeneity is manifest not only between subjects, but also subject to natural oscillations that may occur as a function of circadian rhythms30.

One last factor that needs to be mentioned is the variation in the microbially modified bile acids across different preclinical species. These secondary bile acids can differ between animal species and in certain disease states, which might influence the pharmacokinetics of drugs affected by changes in the bile acid pool. In humans, microbial biotransformation is mediated by bile salt hydrolase (BSH) (responsible for taurine or glycine deconjugation), and 7α-dehydroxylase (responsible for dehydroxylation and generating the secondary bile acids)14,15. The differences in anatomy and physiology between humans and various animal models, their microbial populations, and consequently their bile acid pools, can cause the BA pool to vary in very complex ways. This aspect is infrequently evaluated and often overlooked, thus presenting an opportunity for future research, coupled with variations in the microbiome14. It is generally recognized that the microbiome probably plays a role in these endogenous pools, but it is not clear how that might be related to drug absorption. It is also important to note that many drug products are administered to patients with concurrent disease states, and alterations in bile acid levels may play a role in various conditions as primary factors, modulators, or secondary consequences of the primary disease13,44. For example, liver conditions are relevant since bile acid synthesis and regulation mainly occur in the liver, which is also responsible for drug metabolism. Consequently, many liver disorders can impact bile acid biosynthesis, transport, and homeostasis, which in turn could affect the pharmacokinetics of drugs sensitive to bile acid levels or transporters. Bile acid accumulation in the liver and circulation due to cholestatic conditions can alter drug efficacy and safety profiles by influencing drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters45,46. For example, a previous study found that the organic anion-transporting polypeptide 4C1 (OATP4C1), a renal drug transporter, is one such transporter that might be influenced by interactions with bile acid, potentially impacting the pharmacokinetics of renally excreted drugs in patients with liver failure46. These factors could be crucial for optimizing drug performance in patients with associated disease states, and research in this area remains relatively limited and represents a potential aspect for continued investigation.

It is important to understand and assess these various factors when selecting an animal model for preclinical evaluation. The previously discussed structural and compositional differences between species should be evaluated on a case-by-case basis, since not all drugs are equally affected by changes in the physicochemical properties of individual bile acids. Recognizing these factors is important when selecting an animal model for preclinical evaluation and designing experiments that better mimic the in vivo situation. The model selection could also be impacted by the formulation strategies being optimized. For example, a lipid-based formulation versus an amorphous solid dispersion might be impacted differently, magnifying or normalizing the effect of formulation variables. For example, the apparent amorphous solubility (aAS) and the supersaturation behavior of a model drug (danazol, BCS class Ⅱ) were found to differ when tested using 11 different SIF media (designed using a DoE approach)47. The differences in aAS were primarily influenced by phospholipid concentration, as well as by bile salt concentration and interactions between pH and bile salt. Another study has demonstrated the importance of biorelevant media constituents on the crystallization of supersaturated drug systems48. This was demonstrated using atazanavir and various SIF compositions. The authors have shown that the type and concentration of phospholipids can affect drug crystallization. Specifically, both phosphatidylcholine and its hydrolysis product, lyso-phosphatidylcholine, promote crystallization, with lyso-phosphatidylcholine being a more potent inducer. Additionally, it was shown that the type of bile salt in the media is also important, and using a mixture of di- and tri-hydroxy bile salts resulted in a prolonged supersaturation duration compared to media with only tri-hydroxy bile salt. Overall, these studies support the importance of considering the biorelevant media composition to better predict the in vivo behavior of supersaturating formulations. A clear understanding of the implications could also guide the development of a better formulation strategy.

The influence of bile acids on oral drug behavior

The intestinal colloidal media, composed of bile acids and phospholipids, can play a critical role in the behavior of orally administered drugs, as they can affect various processes such as drug solubility, dissolution, and permeation. The enhanced solubilization of the drugs in the presence of biorelevant media has, in part, led to the proposal of a revised version of the Biopharmaceutics Classification System (BCS)24, which is termed the Developability Classification System (DCS)49. The DCS includes solubility measurements in biorelevant media containing bile salts and lipids (e.g., in FaSSIF). This is expected to provide a better prediction of the in vivo performance of APIs, especially poorly soluble molecules that could have the solubility enhanced by bile salt-containing media, whereas, the aqueous buffer systems alone (used in the BCS) might underestimate their effective in vivo solubility. Thus, highlighting the importance of the bile acid colloidal structures on the solubilization of poorly soluble drugs; that is, BCS/DCS class Ⅱ, which exhibit low solubility but high permeability, and BCS/DCS class Ⅳ, which exhibit low solubility and low permeability49. Additionally, the DCS further divides class Ⅱ molecules into 2 subclasses: Ⅱa (absorption is dissolution rate-limited) and Ⅱb (absorption is solubility rate-limited), and focuses on the rate-limiting steps in absorption, which, in turn, guide formulation strategies. Furthermore, these subclasses take into account the compensatory relationship between solubility and permeability. For example, for DCS class Ⅱa, the poor solubility limitation can be compensated by the high permeability, as long as a fast dissolution rate is achieved, while for DCS class Ⅱb, the drug must be in a completely solubilized form and readily accessible for absorption to achieve adequate absorption49. A refined DCS (rDCS) was also later proposed50,51. The observed effects of bile acid micelles and BAPMM on oral drugs depend not only on the API’s physicochemical properties but are also influenced by the type of oral formulation used (e.g., lipid-based formulations or micellar formulations).

In the following sections, we will discuss the effect of bile salts on BCS/DCS class Ⅱ, Ⅲ, and Ⅳ compounds. We will not address BCS/DCS class Ⅰ compounds, since for these molecules, bile salts would have minimal effect, if any, due to the drug’s high solubility and permeability. We will focus on reviewing how bile acids affect the oral formulation, in terms of drug solubility, dissolution rate, lipophilicity, and permeability through biological membranes. These properties are of interest, since they may be highly influenced by the presence of biosurfactants (i.e., bile salts) and are important for identifying lead candidates and ranking formulations during drug discovery and preformulation.

Effect of bile acids on BCS/DCS Class Ⅱ and Ⅳ

Effect on solubility and dissolution

An important criterion for adequate absorption of oral drugs is sufficient solubilization and availability of the drug in the aqueous GI media. Generally, poorly-soluble compounds are favorably solubilized by the bile salt micellar system, which can act as a shuttle to transport the incorporated hydrophobic compound through the aqueous diffusion layer and close to the lipid membrane for absorption. The solubilization of various hydrophobic drugs by bile salts has been discussed in numerous studies22,53–57. Additionally, bile salts can enhance the dissolution rate of drugs through a combination of effects, including enhanced solubility, wetting effect, and diffusivity58. In particular, the dissolution rate is an important consideration for DCS class Ⅱa drugs (dissolution rate-limited). The dissolution rate can be described by Eq. 2 (Noyes–Whitney equation).

The presence of bile salts in the dissolution media can affect the dissolution rate of the drug by increasing the concentration of the drug at the interface (Cs) (due to micellar solubilization), and by enhancing the wettability (by reducing the interfacial tension between the dissolution media and the drug)58,59. For DCS class Ⅱa, formulation efforts focus on enhancing dissolution rate, typically through particle size reduction (would increase A). Molecules categorized as class Ⅱb have inherently low solubility (i.e., solubility-limited); therefore, even if the drugs can dissolve quickly in the GI fluid, the concentration of free drug is so low that the overall absorption is low. Enabling formulations or chemical modification of the compound are usually used to address the low solubility limitation. Thus, it can be expected that DCS class Ⅱ molecules would be generally sensitive to bile acids and phospholipids present in the GI media, with a tendency to have enhanced dissolution and be favorably solubilized under biorelevant conditions. If the molecules properties are too lipophilic and the interaction with the BAPMM is too extensive, though, the free drug for absorption is deleteriously impacted (Fig. 1). For DCS class Ⅳ molecules, which exhibit low solubility (poor solvation) and low permeability, bile acids are also expected to show a positive effect on their total solubilization. However, the impact can be somewhat less predictable, due to the poor permeability of these molecules. However, because the permeability is low, any increase in the free drug is further magnified. This often requires additional formulation efforts to improve the overall absorption of these challenging molecules. One interesting example is furosemide (weak acid), which is a successful drug on the market and is available through a simpler formulation strategy. Furosemide is classified as a BCS class Ⅳ compound due to its low solubility and high potency. However, it has a good solubility in FaSSIF (with a moderate dose) and can be categorized as a DCS class Ⅲ compound49. In this case, adequate bioavailability can be achieved despite the permeability limitation due to the regional-dependent permeability, which tends to be higher in the proximal segment of the small intestine for furosemide, and allows for reasonable absorption49,60. Another study demonstrated enhanced solubility for several protease inhibitors (BCS class Ⅱ and Ⅳ) in fed-state media, which contains higher concentrations of bile salts and phospholipid. This was attributed, in part, to the susceptibility of the compound to micellar solubilization61. It should be noted that other factors, such as the pH and ionization state of the API, should also be considered to gain a full understanding of the complex factors that affect oral absorption.

Many studies reported in the literature use a single bile salt when assessing the in vitro solubilization. However, as discussed earlier, the intestinal media contains a mixture of bile salts and lipids, and the micellar system could have different solubilization potential depending on the physicochemical properties of the BA and the API, and also on the different CMC values for the individual bile salts (see discussion on CMC in “Bile acids in humans”)62. Therefore, it is important to consider if a simple bile salt micellar system is over- or under-estimating the apparent solubility values that are observed for different molecules. The addition of lipids to the bile salt (i.e., BAPMM) would generally enhance the solubilization of the hydrophobic molecules, since the BAPMM has a lower CMC value, and the enhanced solubilization could be attributed to the more lipophilic nature of the micelle, along with the lower CMC11,23,62. It is important to highlight that, in some cases, the overall amount of bile salts and the presence of phospholipids are the key factors affecting the drug solubilization in the SIF, and not necessarily the specific bile salt species involved (e.g., solubility in a more complex mixture of bile salts). This effect was observed in a previous study examining solubility using a single bile salt versus a bile salt mixture, both with and without a phospholipid63. This study involved two steroidal drugs, hydrocortisone and progesterone, which are non-ionizable molecules with logP values of 1.6 and 3.87, respectively. Nevertheless, it is difficult to generalize this to drugs with varying physicochemical properties, especially ionizable molecules that may exhibit a wider range of lipophilicity.

Interplay between drug lipophilicity and ionization on the interaction with bile acid micelles

It is important to mention that the solubilization by bile salt micelles is, in most cases, driven by the lipophilicity of the molecule20. In this regard, the partition coefficient (logP) and the distribution coefficient (logD) are commonly used somewhat interchangeably to predict the likelihood of micellar solubilization22. The logP accounts for the distribution of the unionized species between an organic and an aqueous phase, as influenced by differences in solvation properties in the two media, while the logD accounts for both the unionized and ionized species, which is much more highly solvated in the aqueous media64. There is a positive correlation between the partition coefficient in the micelle/aqueous system (simple bile salt micelles or bile salt-lipid mixed micelles) and the partition coefficient in the conventionally used octanol/water system22. As discussed by Bergstrom et al., several studies indicate that compounds with a logP > 3–4, or logD (pH 6.5) > 2 are mostly sensitive to potential micellar solubilization by bile salt micelles and mixed micelles, and therefore, solubility measurements would be more representative of in vivo conditions when performed in biorelevant media rather than simple buffer systems3. Mudie et al. have provided recommendations for the selection of biorelevant dissolution media (BDM) based on drug substance solubility, pKa, and logD for immediate-release formulations in the fasted state59. They recommend adding BA and phospholipid (PL) to the dissolution media when logD > 3 (for weak acids, weak bases, and neutrals). Additionally, adding BA and PL could be considered for compounds with logD < 3, if they exhibit poor wetting properties. Markopoulos et al. have also proposed a decision tree for the selection of biorelevant media based on the DCS classification (summarized in Table 3)65.

It is important to note that a strong correlation between drug lipophilicity, affinity and subsequent solubilization by the BAPMM would be more evident for neutral, nonionizable compounds. In this case, lipophilicity and bile salts/phospholipid levels are the main factors contributing to an improved micellar solubilization and capacity to do so. Interestingly, pH could also play a role in the solubilization of neutral compounds, which could be attributed to the ionization of other ionizable components in the medium36–41. For ionizable compounds, a closer examination of the physicochemical properties of the API should be considered (e.g., drug pKa, acid/base character, solvation properties of the “media”)20. As demonstrated by Fagerberg et al., highly lipophilic weak bases or acids can still achieve improved solubility in the presence of BAPMM and with increasing the bile acid/phospholipid levels. Nevertheless, the affinity of these compounds for the micelles, and consequently the degree of solubility enhancement, is strongly influenced by the ionization state of the molecule. For example, highly ionized weak bases can still be adequately solubilized by the BAPMM, whereas highly ionized weak acids are less likely to be solubilized by the BAPMM20. Another study by Boyanov et al. has discussed the relationship between drug solubilization in the SIF and lipophilicity (i.e, log P/D) for neutral and charged molecules66. For ionizable molecules, the authors have found that, in general, there is a strong correlation between logD and solubilization in FeSSIF when the molecule is in a neutral or partially ionized form. However, poor correlation was observed when the molecule is in its fully ionized form (see Fig. 3 in the reference). Electrostatic interactions also play a role: Negatively charged drugs may exhibit similar or reduced solubility in the presence of micelles, due to electrostatic repulsion with the negatively charged bile salt micelles. A positively charged drug may exhibit increased solubility in the presence of micelles due to favorable electrostatic interactions; in some cases, this can also lead to phase separation, reducing the drug concentration in the aqueous phase. Nevertheless, in both cases (weak acids or bases), the solubility enhancement (in FeSSIF) relative to the aqueous solubility (at a specific pH) was observed to be higher for the neutral form of the drug66.

Additionally, the molecular size and the location of the charged moiety could be important parameters to consider when evaluating their affinity to bile salt micelles and mixed micelles. This becomes important, considering the complex and stepwise self-assembly process of bile salt micelles67. Positive deviations could occur with compounds containing large, rigid, planar, polyaromatic rings, which can be favorably associated within the bile salt aggregates22. Furthermore, for charged compounds with planar rings, the position of that charge could also be important. For example, cinnarizine (a weak base) has the basic nitrogen in a central and shielded position, which may reduce the likelihood of electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged micelles20. On the other hand, phenylbutazone and indomethacin, both carry a negative charge at neutral pH, exhibiting different solubilization in the presence of bile salt micelles. This could be partly attributed to the charge location in the molecule (near the center for phenylbutazone or near the periphery for indomethacin)22.

It is important to note that the enhanced solubilization imparted by the bile salts does not always correlate with enhanced permeation. If the drug is very efficiently entrapped within the micelle, there would be insufficient free drug available for absorption, implications which are discussed in the next section (see “Drug lipophilicity and the solubility-permeability interplay”).

Drug lipophilicity and the solubility-permeability interplay

The diffusion through the transcellular route is dependent on the drug lipophilicity as indicated by molecular descriptors such as logP and logD. The log P/D values are commonly used as effective correlators for the permeability across biological lipid membranes (by passive diffusion), most commonly considering the octanol-water system for measurement. Although there is an approximate correlation between the logP and permeability, it is important to note that the intestinal permeation process is complex, and multiple factors can also be involved and influence the observed outcome (e.g., efflux carriers, active transport mechanisms, and interaction with bile salts), as well as potential paracellular aspects, where more hydrophilic (i.e. low logP compounds) may take advantage of passage through the tight junctions in cell membrane52,68. An example of the complexity, NaTC has been shown to exhibit a concentration-dependent inhibition of the P-glycoprotein (P-gp) efflux transporter using a Caco-2 cellular model69. Additionally, while bile salts can enhance the solubilization of lipophilic molecules, this does not always translate to enhanced permeation. This is because the sequestration of the drug by the BAPMM might reduce the free fraction of the drug and, consequently, reduce permeation (Fig. 1). Therefore, it is challenging to anticipate the effect of NaTC (which is used in SIF) on drug permeation, since it is highly dependent on the affinity of the molecule for the micelle, its affinity for efflux and active transporters, and the dose used69. The role of the kinetic release of the drug from the BAPMM can also become important. In some cases, it is possible that the drug-containing BAPMM can serve as a reservoir for the drug70. Therefore, it is really the balance between the rate of dissolution, the capacity of solubilization, and the rate of release of the drug from the micelles, all within the GI transit time, that will eventually dictate the overall absorption.

As discussed in the previous sections, generally, more lipophilic compounds are better solubilized by the bile salt micelles, since for these compounds, micellar solubilization is driven by the hydrophobicity of the molecule22,71. However, drugs with extremely high logP values could have unfavorable absorption. As a result of high lipophilicity, low aqueous solubility, sequestration in the BAPMM, or sequestration in the lipid bilayer, there is an overall reduced permeation across the membrane and into the circulation52. Therefore, optimal lipophilicity of the API is always desired (i.e., approximately 1–572,73).

A common approach to increase the passive diffusion of molecules is to increase their lipophilicity, as is done with many new chemical entities (NCEs) in the pipeline74. Nevertheless, this is usually associated with more solubility challenges that need to be addressed during drug development75. For drugs with poor permeability (such as those with BCS/DCS class Ⅲ and Ⅳ), structural modifications are a common strategy that is used to improve the oral absorption, and can be preferred over other formulation approaches52. It is important to note that for pH-sensitive drugs, logP measurements alone cannot fully capture the in vivo permeability. For instance, furosemide has a logP of 2.3, suggesting reasonable permeability. However, because of its acidic nature, pKa of 3.8, it would have a logD of less than 0 at the pH of the intestine. As a result, it exhibits a low overall permeability, particularly in the distal segment of the GI tract60.

As discussed in the previous section, solubilization of the API in the biorelevant media is known to enhance the apparent solubility of the molecule and, theoretically, the drug absorption. However, this is not always the case, since the drug needs to be in a molecularly dissolved and free state for absorption to occur, and not in a micelle-bound state. For example, Holmstock et al. have found that for indinavir (BCS class Ⅱ), the permeability was 22-fold lower in the fed state human intestinal fluid (FeHIF) compared to the fasted state HIF76. This decreased permeability occurred despite a 6-fold higher solubility in the FeHIF, which was attributed to micellar entrapment of the drug, thereby decreasing the free drug concentration. It is important to emphasize that it is not just the free-drug concentration that influences overall drug absorption; the rate-limiting step in absorption is also important. Previous research has demonstrated that increasing micellar solubilization or colloidal drug concentration could result in an increased flux and absorption, especially when the rate-limiting step is the aqueous boundary layer (ABL)77–79. In these cases, higher flux could be achieved despite lower free drug concentration. If the flux is membrane-limited, increasing the micellar- or colloidal-bound drug concentration does not increase flux77. In both cases, the altered dissolution rate of the drug could still play a role. Thus, this emphasizes the significance of evaluating the solubility-permeability interplay when using biorelevant media. While it might be challenging to anticipate the effects on actual absorption, it is critical to understand these potential effects to even anticipate structural modification impacts on drug solubility, dissolution, and absorption. It is also worth mentioning that computational tools, such as molecular dynamic simulation, could be used to understand the molecular-level interaction between APIs and bile-containing biorelevant media, and predict the effect on drug solubilization and permeation80–83.

Permeability assays using biorelevant media

In the context of bile acids, it is relevant to discuss the various in vitro permeability assays that are typically used during the drug discovery phase. It is important to mention that permeability profiling is a complex topic, and several considerations regarding permeability assays should be taken into account during in vitro permeability testing. Several research groups have done extensive work on this research area, and the interested reader is referred to the following review for more details84. Here, we will highlight the importance of bile salts and shed light on one aspect of a much broader, more complex field that is beyond the scope of the current review.

In general, many cell-free permeation assays are compatible with biorelevant media, but only consider the passive absorption process (no paracellular or active transport)74. These non-cellular models could be useful for predicting the permeability of passively absorbed API’s, or if it is of interest to distinguish the effect of bile salt micellar solubilization on the passive diffusion74,85. Cell-based permeation assays are considered a better biological mimic, since they can include both passive and active transport mechanisms. However, the use of biorelevant media with these cellular models is limited since high concentrations of bile salts (i.e., concentrations representative of the fed-state media; FeSSIF and FeHIF), along with FaHIF, are expected to compromise the integrity of the cell monolayer and may interfere with the functioning of various transporters in cell systems. Lower concentrations of bile salts used in FaSSIF media are usually tolerated and compatible with Caco-2 models69,86–88. To overcome this limitation, several studies have proposed using a mucin-protected cellular models (e.g., Caco-2 cells), as discussed previously87,88. Additionally, it was demonstrated that using 100 mg/mL of mucin maintained sufficient cell integrity, and the monolayers were compatible even with complex biorelevant media88. Modified versions of FeSSIF have also been proposed to avoid damaging the Caco-2 monolayers89. Using biorelevant media that are more complex in nature, with higher concentrations of solubilizing agents such as bile salts, becomes valuable during in vitro assessment of drug candidates with poor solubility. Since these media can help in overcoming the solubility limitation of the compound, and are able to better predict the in vivo performance88.

As mentioned earlier, the observed effect of bile salts on orally administered drugs can also be influenced by the type of formulation and/or excipients in that formulation (e.g., lipid-based or cyclodextrin-based formulation)2,35. For example, a previous study explored how different excipients influence the equilibrium solubility of fenofibrate (a non-ionic, BCS class II drug) in simulated intestinal media with diverse compositions using a DoE approach90. The authors found that some excipients, such as Mannitol, did not affect drug solubility. Others, however, either increased or decreased it; polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) was found to enhance solubility, whereas hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) and chitosan tended to reduce it. These effects varied depending on the SIF media composition, as well as the grade and concentration of the excipient. Thus, highlighting the complex factors that could influence interactions among the drug, media and excipient and ultimately impact the solubility of poorly soluble molecules. Therefore, whether a more complex intestinal mimic is necessary during in vitro testing should be assessed on a case-by-case basis, depending on the physicochemical properties of the API and the possible formulation strategy to be employed (see Table 3 for suggested recommendations adapted from Markopoulos et al.65). This can ensure more accurate prediction of in vivo outcomes and a more effective drug development process.

Effect of bile acids on BCS/DCS class Ⅲ molecules

BCS/DCS class 3 molecules exhibit high solubility but low permeability; thus, it would be expected that bile salts may not have a huge impact on their solubilization, which is already sufficiently high that they are not dissolution rate-limited absorption. However, bile may interact with these molecules, reduce free drug concentration, and potentially may lead to decreased absorption. Many BCS class Ⅲ molecules exhibit a negative food effect, which is caused by various changes in the GI physiology, along with the increased secretion of bile acids, which is triggered upon ingesting a meal91,92. Peptide drugs stand out in this drug category, considering that many fall under BCS/DCS class Ⅲ. Peptide drugs are increasingly becoming one of the most interesting drug modalities being pursued. This is particularly evident with the recent approvals of orally available peptides, which are considered “beyond the rule of 5 (bRo5)” molecules93–96. Notable examples include the glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) analogue, semaglutide, and the macrocyclic peptide, octreotide96,97.

The major limitation of oral delivery of peptide molecules is that they are also compromised by their low enzymatic stability in the GI tract and their inherently poor permeability. This often necessitates structural modifications to optimize enzymatic stability, along with the incorporation of permeation enhancers into the formulation to overcome the permeability limitation95,98. Nevertheless, the oral bioavailability of these molecules is typically very low (with the exception of cyclosporin A, a BCS class Ⅱ compound, which has a bioavailability of up to 80% if dosed with an appropriate formulation95). Another important factor that could limit the absorption of peptide drugs is the interaction between the administered peptides, and bile salts and BAPMM present in the intestinal tract. Indeed, it has been demonstrated that peptide drugs could interact with bile salts, leading to a lower in vitro flux rate, which can be correlated with a lower free drug concentration for absorption21,99. This interaction is highly dependent on the physicochemical properties of the peptide, as well as the hydroxylation state and the concentration of the bile salt. For example, several octreotide analogs have been shown to interact with bile salts independently and with FaSSIF and FeSSIF media containing BAPMM. These interactions tend to be minimal for more hydrophilic peptide analogs (which have a lower logD values)99,100. Analogs that contain more hydrophobic moieties (e.g., Trp) and have a positively charged moiety (e.g., Lys) demonstrated stronger affinity for the bile salts, particularly the dihydroxy type21,99. The stronger affinity for the bile salt is attributed to the enhanced hydrophobic and/or electrostatic forces that seem to drive the peptide-bile interaction. Another earlier study by Fricker et al. has demonstrated that the bioavailability of octreotide was 6-fold higher in bile-duct cannulated rats, suggesting a potentially negative effect due to the presence of biliary components101.

That being said, it is important to note that there seems to be a balance between the effect of bile salts on free drug concentration, along with drug permeation. For instance, a previous study showed that bile acid sequestrants (BAS) improved the in vitro flux rate of octreotide in FeSSIF media102. However, the in vivo effect of administering the BAS, colestipol, did not appear to differ significantly from that of the control group. This effect was attributed, in part, to the decreased potency of the BAS in vivo, along with a potentially positive effect that bile could have on the peptide permeation in vivo102. It should also be pointed out that these effects are variable for different peptides and seem to be associated with key residues within the peptide sequence21,99. This suggests that structural factors could also influence how cyclic peptide interacts with the micelles. Additionally, although using a single bile salt in SIF simplifies the in vivo bile salt composition, it still provides good prediction for many small-molecule, hydrophobic APIs, as demonstrated in numerous studies. However, for peptide drugs, a different scenario may apply, and the use of dihydroxy bile salts or more complex biorelevant media during in vitro experiments might better predict in vivo outcomes in certain cases. These effects need to be recognized during oral drug discovery and development to achieve optimal outcomes.

Conclusion

Understanding the physicochemical properties of bile acids and the different roles they play in the GI tract is important for capturing the complexity following oral drug administration. As highlighted in the review, these interactions are highly dependent on the physicochemical properties of the drug molecules and the bile acid, as well as the formulation strategy. One important limitation is the scarcity of systematic, structured studies that examine a related series of molecules within a single model or across multiple model systems. Many proposed relationships between physicochemical properties and bile salt-mediated solubilization effects are based on individual case studies. As discussed in this review, parameters such as drug lipophilicity, ionization, molecular structure, and size are important in determining the interaction of drugs with bile salt micelles. Nonetheless, drug-specific interactions are also expected, and more structured investigations would be required to establish broader guidelines across a wider chemical space from a drug discovery viewpoint. Additionally, it is important to mention that although bile salts play a major role in food effects, they are only one part of the picture. Other factors, including pH alterations, gastric emptying, changes in viscosity and fluid volume, gut microbiota, and lipid content, may also play a significant role.

During in vitro testing, the complexity of the biorelevant media could be optimized on a level-based approach within a physiologically relevant framework. These modifications may be guided by the study objective and the information level required at different drug development stages, recognizing that drug- or formulation-specific considerations may be justified in some cases. For example, simpler systems might be sufficient for initial screening, while more complex biorelevant systems might be reserved for a selected set of compounds with anticipated biopharmaceutical challenges. While a complete simulation of the intestinal contents would be ideal, it may not be a necessity in all cases. A thorough understanding of the intertwined factors within the GI tract that may influence drug performance, along with the properties of the molecule under study, would help medicinal chemists and formulation scientists to make more informed decisions during the selection and development of drug compounds.
