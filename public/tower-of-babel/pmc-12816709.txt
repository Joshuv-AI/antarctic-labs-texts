DFT-guided photostable chitosan-derived carbon quantum dots as colloidal antibacterial and bioimaging agents
Authors: Rutuja Gumathannavar, Rohan More, Amruta Lohar, Manoj Kadam, Mandar M. Shirolkar, Yogesh Jadhav, Santosh Koratkar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Environmentally friendly and biocompatible nanomaterials are essential for biomedical applications demanding long-term safety, stability, and functional performance. In this study, we present a simple hydrothermal synthesis of chitosan-derived fluorescent carbon quantum dots (CQDs), utilizing chitosan as a biodegradable carbon precursor. The synthesized CQDs displayed uniform spherical morphology (~ 3 ± 1 nm), a positive surface charge (+ 12.6 mV), and strong cyan-blue photoluminescence (λem ≈ 473 nm, PLQY ≈ 17.5%) with excitation-dependent emission. Their structure and optical properties were characterized using TEM, XRD, FTIR, UV–Vis, PL, DLS, and cyclic voltammetry. Density functional theory (DFT) simulations supported experimental data, showing stable nanocluster formation through chitosan–acetic acid interactions and predicting a band gap of ≈ 3.41 eV, consistent with the experimental 3.45 eV. Nitrogen atoms were identified as major contributors to optical transitions. Biological studies revealed dose-dependent cytocompatibility in Vero cells, strong antioxidant activity (DPPH assay), and antibacterial efficacy against E. coli and S. aureus via reactive oxygen species generation and electrostatic interactions. Fluorescence microscopy confirmed efficient cellular uptake and cytoplasmic distribution. Overall, this integrated experimental–theoretical approach highlights the CQDs’ potential for sustainable applications in bioimaging, drug delivery, and theranostics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31920-3.

Introduction

Nano-bioimaging and sensing have emerged as rapidly growing areas of research owing to the unique properties of nanocrystalline materials, which offer tunable optoelectronic and spectroelectrochemical functionalities1. Among these, fluorescent quantum dots (QDs) have attracted particular interest as versatile nanomaterials for imaging, sensing, and diagnostic applications. Conventional metal chalcogenide-based QDs, such as CdSe, CdTe, ZnS, ZnSe, CuInS/Se, and PbS/Se, exhibit high fluorescence quantum yields; however, their broader use is limited by the intrinsic toxicity of heavy metals, potential environmental hazards, and stringent disposal requirements2–6. In addition, the scarcity and high cost of some constituent elements hinder large-scale production and restrict their translation into biological and clinical settings7,8. Consequently, biocompatibility, bioavailability, and biodegradability have become critical criteria in the design and development of next-generation nanomaterials for biomedical use9.

To address these challenges, green and nature-inspired synthesis routes have gained momentum. Such approaches emphasize efficient energy use, reduced waste, safer reaction conditions, and renewable precursors, making them particularly attractive for pharmaceutical and biomedical applications. In this context, chitosan-derived nanomaterials have garnered increasing attention over the past two decades10–15. Chitosan, a naturally abundant polysaccharide obtained from chitin, is biodegradable, biocompatible, and structurally versatile, thereby offering ecological sustainability and broad applicability15. Its derivatives have been widely explored as carriers in pharmaceutical, nutritional, medical, environmental, and agricultural sectors16–18 due to their physicochemical stability, ability to enable controlled drug release, enhanced cell–material interactions, and improved bioavailability19,20.

Nanotechnology further enhances the utility of QDs by exploiting their unique optoimaging and sensing capabilities for diverse biomedical applications20. QDs exhibit pronounced quantum confinement effects, whereby their optical and electronic properties depend strongly on particle size and surface states. Their optoelectronic behavior can be finely tuned through control of surface charge, chemical composition, and morphology, which in turn influences performance, stability, and biocompatibility21,22. Despite these advantages, concerns regarding cytotoxicity and long-term biosafety continue to impede the use of many QDs in vivo23. Embedding or deriving QDs from biopolymers such as chitosan offers a promising strategy to mitigate toxicity while preserving, or even enhancing, their functional properties, thus expanding their potential in bioimaging, sensing, drug delivery, and environmental monitoring24.

Recent advances in carbon-based nanomaterials highlight their expanding relevance in antimicrobial, diagnostic and therapeutic technologies. Doped carbon dots (CDs) have been extensively reviewed for their tunable optical properties and heteroatom-regulated surface chemistry, enabling applications ranging from sensing to catalysis25. Complementary work on chitosan-derived fluorescent probes highlights how this biopolymer can serve as both a precursor and a functional scaffold for developing biocompatible fluorescent materials suitable for imaging and drug-delivery platforms26. Building on this, folic acid-derived CDs conjugated to chitosan nanospheres demonstrate pH-responsive drug release and targeted anticancer theragnostic capability. Injectable oxoammonium-functional nanogels further extend biomaterials innovation by improving enzyme stability and reusability for biological delivery systems. Recent insights into polysaccharide-based CD synthesis reveal sustainable pathways for theranostic applications, emphasizing green chemistry and biological compatibility27,28.

Mechanistic studies further deepen the field, mild hydrothermal synthesis enables chitosan-based CQDs to retain precursor functionalities, yielding ROS-active and antimicrobial nanodots with enhanced efficacy. A broad review on CQDs for antibacterial applications explores how precursor choice, ROS generation and surface charge contribute to bacterial inhibition29,30. Experimental work on chitosan-CQDs with dual ROS-scavenging and anti-inflammatory behaviour demonstrates accelerated wound healing and regulation of Nrf2/NF-κB pathways. Sustainable approaches also appear in waste-derived N, S-doped CDs functionalised onto breathable fabrics, offering antioxidant and antimicrobial performance for medical textiles. Finally, a comprehensive review synthesises progress in microbial inhibition and biosensing using multifunctional CDs, highlighting their photodynamic, photothermal, and sensing mechanisms31,32.

In this work, we focus on the development of chitosan-derived colloidal fluorescent carbon quantum dots (CQDs) that are earth-abundant, biocompatible, biodegradable, and colloidally stable, with potential utility across a range of biomedical applications. We report a low-temperature hydrothermal synthesis route starting from chitosan and systematically characterize the resulting CQDs using complementary state-of-the-art spectroscopic, structural, and microscopic techniques. Their biocompatibility is evaluated through in vitro assays, including cell viability and antibacterial studies, to assess cytotoxic responses and antimicrobial efficacy. Beyond the experimental characterization, a key novelty of this study lies in the integration of hydrothermal synthesis with ab initio density functional theory (DFT) simulations to mechanistically elucidate the formation, electronic structure, and optoelectronic properties of chitosan-based CQDs. To the best of our knowledge, such a combined experimental–theoretical framework, applied specifically to chitosan-derived CQDs, has been rarely reported. This dual approach enables us to correlate surface chemistry and structural motifs with the observed photoluminescence behavior and biological performance, thereby providing deeper insight into structure–property–function relationships. Overall, the present work not only introduces a green, scalable route to multifunctional CQDs from a widely available biopolymer but also establishes a mechanistic basis for their rational design as fluorescent probes, antibacterial agents, and potential drug delivery platforms, clearly distinguishing this study from previous reports.

Experimental section

Materials and methods

Low-molecular-weight chitosan (C6H11NO4)n) with 90% acetylation (CS) (Grade Extrapure), facial acetic acid (CH3COOH) (Purity ≥ 99.9%) (AA), ascorbic acid (C6H8O6) (Purity ≥ 99.7%), Amikacin, 2,2-diphenyl, picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), nutrient broth (NB), E. coli (ATCC 8739), S. aureus (ATCC 2079) AGS cell lines were purchased from the National Centre for Cell Science (Pune, Maharashtra), and MTT reagents (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) (Purity ≥ 98%), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (≥ 99.5% purity, AR grade) (C2H6OS, RPMI 1640, fetal bovine serum (FBS), and trypsin phosphate Versene glucose were used.

Synthesis of chitosan-derived fluorescent CQDs

The synthesis of the fluorescent colloidal CQDs was performed via a hydrothermal method at low temperature with modifications to a previously reported protocol33. For the synthesis of CQDs, we utilized low-molecular-weight chitosan ((C6H11NO4)n) as a biocompatible carbon precursor. In a typical synthesis procedure, 1 g of CS powder was added to a 25 mL single-neck round-bottom flask in 20 mL of 0.5% AA. This CS precursor solution was continuously stirred under nitrogen gas at 700 rpm and 333 K until a clear solution appeared. Later, the solution was poured into a Teflon liner and sealed in a hydrothermal autoclave, followed by heat treatment at 423 K for 8 h. The heating rate was selected as 5 °C/min via a programmable muffle furnace, and the hydrothermal autoclave was allowed to cool naturally after the completion of the reaction. On the basis of the steam tables, the calculated approximate pressure at 423 K is 0.0033 GPa. The overall reaction mixture (CQDs/unreacted precursor solution) was filtered through Whatman paper, followed by syringe filtration (0.22 μm) to separate the high- and low-fluency CQDs and bulk nanoparticles from the reaction mixture. The yellow‒brown color of the CQD solution was used for further characterization and stored at ≈ 4 °C until further use.

Material characterization

The synthesized CQDs were well characterized for their structural, morphological, and optoelectronic properties. The crystallographic information was acquired via X-ray diffraction (XRD) on a Rigaku Miniflex instrument operating at 40 kV and 40 mA and equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.5406 Å). The diffraction pattern was recorded in the range of 2θ = 5°–80°. The optical absorbance spectra of the colloidal CQD dispersion sample were recorded via a Jasco V-750 UV‒visible spectrophotometer in the range of 190–900 nm with a resolution of 1 nm. The photoluminescence (PL) emission spectra were recorded on a Horiba Fluorolog (FL-1057). Chemical functional group fingerprinting was carried out via FTIR spectroscopy at room temperature on a Shimadzu IR Affinity spectrometer in the range of 400–4000 cm−1 with a diamond ATR accessory. The surface zeta potential was measured via an Anton Parr Litesizer 500. The zeta potential measurements were performed via the Univett setup. The electrochemical investigation of the band structure parameters was carried out via a Metrohm Autolab 302 N (Potentiostat/Galvanostat).

DFT studies

Density functional theory (DFT) simulations were performed to determine the possible formation of CQDs and their optical properties. The DFT simulation was performed via the Materials Studio software suite 2020 (Accelrys)34–36. All the simulations were performed with a fine grid quality. First, single CS molecules (PubChem CID: 71853) and AA molecules (PubChem CID: 176) were geometrically optimized via the DMOL3 module with generalized gradient approximation (GGA) and the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) function, i.e., the GGA–PBE function. During the geometry optimization, the energy, force, and displacement parameters for both molecules were maintained with convergence tolerances of 1 × 10–5 Ha, 0.002 Ha/Å and 0.005 Å, respectively. The core treatment All Electron Relativistic, with the DNP + basis set, Hexadecapole multipolar expansion and global orbital cutoff scheme, was used for the optimizations. These geometry-optimized molecules interact together via the Blends module, which follows the modified Flory-Huggins model37. For the interactions, the CS molecule was kept as the base, the AA molecule was kept as the screen, and the water molecule was kept as the base + screen. The calculations were performed at a reference temperature of 298 K, an energy bin width of 0.1 kcal/mol and a universal force field.

The molecular configuration obtained from the blend calculations was further utilized to obtain the CQDs. To simulate experimental conditions, a thermodynamic ensemble modeling approach was considered. The ensemble modeling was performed via the Cambridge Serial Total Energy Package (CASTEP) with GGA-PBE exchange and correlation functions38. The energy cutoff was maintained at 700 eV, norm-conserving pseudopotentials, kelling–Harmon Relativistic treatment, and self–consistent dipole correction. The parameters for ensemble modeling were as follows: Ensemble: isothermal–isoric ensemble (NPT), temperature: 423 K, pressure: 0.0033 GPa (based on the experimental conditions of the preparation of the CQD), time step: 1 fs, total simulation time: 100 ps, simulation steps: 500 and fixed basis quality to simulate the CQD, thermostat: Langevin (with a constant of 0.1 ps), Barostat: Paminello and wavefunction extrapolation scheme: first-order extrapolation with variable coefficients. In vitro biological studies

Cell viability by MTT assay

The cytotoxic effects of CQDs were assessed using Vero cells. Cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 per well in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. The CQD stock was sterilized by passing through a 0.2 μm syringe filter (Axiva), and working concentrations ranging from 500 to 15 µg/mL were obtained by diluting the stock in DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS and 1% antibiotics.

The MTT assay was carried out in 96-well flat-bottom plates as described in. Briefly, 1 × 104 cells per well were grown in 100 µL of culture medium and incubated under standard conditions until a confluent monolayer was formed. The medium was then replaced with 100 µL of CQD test samples and incubated for 24 h in separate wells, with untreated cells serving as controls. Subsequently, 10 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well, followed by 4 h of incubation in the dark. Thereafter, 100 µL of DMSO was used to solubilize the formazan crystals. Absorbance was recorded at 570 nm using a Thermo Scientific multi-scan plate reader. Antibacterial activity of CQDs

The antibacterial efficacy of the CQDs was evaluated via the well diffusion method against Escherichia coli (ATCC 8739) and Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 2079). Overnight cultures of the test bacteria were prepared in nutrient broth and adjusted to an optical density (OD600) of 0.1, corresponding to approximately 108 CFU/mL. A volume of 100 µL of each standardized bacterial suspension was uniformly spread over the surface of Mueller‒Hinton agar (MHA) plates via sterile cotton swabs. Sterile cork borers were used to create wells (6 mm diameter) in the agar medium. Varying concentrations of CQD solutions (e.g., 500 µg/ml for further double dilutions to 7.8500 µg/mL) were carefully pipetted into the wells. For the negative control, wells containing only sterile distilled water or solvent were used. The inoculated plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h under aerobic conditions. After incubation, the diameter of the zone of inhibition (ZOI) around each well was measured in millimeters via a digital Vernier caliper. The antibacterial activity of the CQDs was determined by comparing the ZOI values with those of the control groups (Fig. 5(c))40. The standard antibiotic Amikacin was kept as a control group and all the same studies was carried out for the same.

DPPH scavenging activity

The antioxidant potential of CQDs was assessed using the 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay, following established protocols, where ascorbic acid (STD) was used as the standard antioxidant agent. A freshly prepared molar solution of DPPH in methanol was kept under refrigerated conditions until use. For the assay, 150 µL of DPPH solution was mixed with 100 µL of CQD samples at concentrations ranging from 500 to 15 µg/mL. A control mixture lacking CQDs was also prepared and incubated under dark conditions for 30 min. Following incubation, the absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a Bio-Rad iMark microplate reader. Morphological, crystallographic and surface property analyses

TEM analysis was used to directly examine the morphology, size distribution, dispersion state, and lattice structure of the hydrothermally synthesized CQDs, thereby confirming their nanoscale crystalline nature. The size and morphology of the synthesized CQDs were observed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM) at different magnifications, as shown in Fig. 1a, b. The low-resolution TEM micrograph shows a uniform size distribution with a particle size of ≈ 3 ± 1 nm and a spherical morphology. The high-resolution TEM micrograph shows clear atomic/lattice arrangements, indicating the nanocrystalline nature of the CQDs. The crystallographic and structural properties of the raw CS and CQDs derived from CS were investigated via powder XRD, as shown in Fig. 1c. The XRD profiles of both CS and CQDs exhibited two broad peaks at 2θ values of 21.25° and 23.60°, indicating the formation of a highly disordered, amorphous carbon phase after hydrothermal processing. A notable feature of the diffraction pattern was the shift of the crystalline peak by 2.35° along with broadening of the full width at half maximum (FWHM), which can be ascribed to the transformation of CS into CQDs and the introduction of a high density of surface functional groups during hydrothermal treatment. The inset of Fig. 1c shows the visual appearance of the samples: (a) off-white CS powder and (b) black CQD powder.

FTIR analysis was carried out to identify the surface functional groups and chemical bonding environment originating from the chitosan precursor. The functional groups of CS and CQDs were examined using FTIR spectroscopy, as presented in Fig. 1d. A broad and intense absorption band was observed in the 3000–3700 cm⁻1 region, corresponding to hydrogen-bonded hydroxyl (–OH) groups along with symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of –N–H. Characteristic peaks initially detected at 2870 and 2915 cm⁻1 shifted to 2860 and 2933 cm⁻1, respectively51. While the weak band near 1427 cm⁻1 was associated with –C–H stretching and –CH2 deformation. A distinct absorption at 1041 cm⁻1 indicated –C–O stretching, and a band at 1566 cm⁻1 was attributed to –N–H vibrations from amine groups in chitosan. The carbonyl (–C=O) stretch appeared at 1720 cm⁻1. Additionally, signals at 1373 and 1435 cm⁻1 corresponded to –C–O and –C–N bending, confirming the presence of amine functionalities52,53. The region between 532 and 879 cm⁻1 was linked to β-1,4-glycosidic linkages or saccharide structures. A –C–N stretching vibration was evident at 1248 cm⁻1, and variations in aliphatic –C–H symmetric and asymmetric stretching were observed within 2862–3480 cm⁻1. These spectral changes suggest modifications in the dipole moment of chitosan and CQDs, confirming the transformation of native chitosan into CQDs through dehydration and chain degradation processes54,55.

Optoelectronic and electrochemical properties analysis

UV–Vis spectroscopy was employed to investigate the electronic transitions and characterize the optical absorption features associated with the conjugated domains and surface states of the CQDs. The optical characteristics of the prepared CQDs were investigated using UV–visible absorption spectroscopy, as shown in Fig. 2a. The spectra of both CS and chitosan-derived CQDs displayed an absorption edge around 400 nm, along with a minor shoulder at 350 nm, a strong absorption peak near 280 nm, and a faint shoulder around 225 nm. These features are associated with different electronic transitions of electrons upon excitation with photon energy. The optical band gap is estimated to be 3.45 eV from the tauc plot and is shown in the inset of Fig. 2a. Zeta potential measurements were performed to evaluate the surface charge and colloidal stability of the CQDs in aqueous medium, providing insight into their dispersion behaviour and potential interactions with biological membranes.The surface charge, i.e., the zeta potential, of the CQDs was estimated from DLS measurements and was found to be + 12.6 mV, as displayed in Fig. 2b. This is particularly good for the stability of the dispersion18,56. This positive charge indicates the presence of amine functional groups on the surface of the chitosan-derived CQDs. The inset of Fig. 2b shows the raw CS solution and synthesized CQD dispersion under visible light and after irradiation with 365 nm UV light. The intense bright blue (cyan) color indicates potential candidates for bioimaging, biolabeling and fluorometric sensors.

Photoluminescence (PL) measurements were performed to evaluate the emission characteristics and quantum confinement–related photophysical behavior of the CQDs. PL behavior of the CQDs was first examined under 365 nm UV light using a cabinet, followed by detailed analysis with photoluminescence spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 2c, the excitation spectrum displayed two distinct peaks: a weaker one at 275 nm and a more intense peak at 378 nm, consistent with the absorption spectra. Upon excitation at 378 nm, an emission band was observed at 473 nm, corresponding to a Stokes shift of 95 nm. Such a considerable Stokes shift is a characteristic feature of carbon QDs, where the reduced overlap between absorption and emission spectra minimizes self-absorption and enhances PL sensitivity. The large Stokes shift in the carbon dots was also related to the presence of surface states57. In addition, an excitation wavelength-dependent investigation was also performed in the range of 350–450 nm. Interestingly, the CQDs exhibited excitation-dependent behavior, and as the excitation wavelength increased, the emission wavelength redshifted, with an initial increase in intensity up to 390 nm and a subsequent decrease in intensity Fig. 2d. This excitation wavelength-dependent behavior of the CQDs is in excellent agreement with data reported previously58–60, and the PL mechanism of the CQDs is due to both the quantum confinement effect and surface defects; however, this mechanism is highly suited for various optoelectronic biolabeling and biosensing applications. The relative PLQYs of the synthesized CQDs were calculated with the known QY of the dye 9,10-diphenylanthracene (DAP). The PLQY of this molecule is 1 (100%). The PL emission spectra of 9,10-DAP and the CQDs were obtained in cyclohexane and ethanol.

Optical absorption has a broad nature, which implies that a large particle size distribution as well as shallow or deep surface defect states may be present near the band gap61. The photoluminescence spectra of the CQDs show broad emission in the range of 350–600 nm with a FWHM of 67 nm. The emission peak energy and intensity vary with the excitation energy. The redshift in the emission energy with a change in the excitation energy is due to the size distribution, and different emissive sites may arise from oxygen and nitrogen62.

To further enhance their emission performance, several key strategies are especially effective. Surface passivation with amine- or polymer-based ligands suppresses non-radiative surface traps, while heteroatom doping (N, S, P, B) creates new emissive states and boosts radiative transitions. Improving core size uniformity and graphitic crystallinity stabilizes excitons and minimizes defect-induced quenching. Rigorous purification and fractionation remove non-emissive carbon residues, leading to cleaner and stronger fluorescence. Finally, controlling interparticle interactions to prevent aggregation preserves isolated emissive centers and avoids aggregation-induced quenching. Together, these approaches significantly improve the PL intensity and quantum yield of CQDs.

Electrochemical cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were performed to understand the electronic band structure parameters according to our previous reports63–65 since these parameters play a crucial role in the development of high-throughput optoelectronic devices. In a typical CV measurement, we perform CV in a potential window of 0 V to − 1 V. CQDs possess a very wide bandgap and constraints to achieve a large potential window due to the water splitting reaction. CV was carried out in 10 mL of the nonaqueous solvents dichloromethane (DCM) and 100 mM tetra butyl ammonium perchlorate (TBAP) in a positive nitrogen environment. Figure 3a shows the controlled CV curve of the bare glassy carbon electrode and the 25 µL CQD dispersion-loaded sample. In the controlled CV curve, no peaks are present, as expected for the blank electrode, which is attributed to the absence of redox reactions. On the other hand, the CQD-loaded CV measurements demonstrate that the prominent redox peak at − 0.66 V corresponds to the reduction of the CQDs by the acceptance of an electron. This position is marked as C1 for the cathodic peaks and further as the conduction band of the CQDs. The potentials were calibrated to NHE using ferrocene as an internal standard followed by conversion to a local vacuum. Using the standard optical band gap (3.45 eV) estimated via a tauc plot, we extracted the conduction and valance band positions as − 3.84 eV and − 7.29 eV, respectively. The inset of Fig. 3b shows the electronic band structure (HOMO–LUMO positions and energy gap). Furthermore, we performed scan rate-dependent CV measurements (10–200 mV) to understand the type of reaction diffusion controlled or charge transfer controlled, as shown in Fig. 3b. The inset of Fig. 3b shows the linear fit of the square root of the scan rate vs. the cathodic current. The linear increase in the cathodic current with increasing scan rate suggests a diffusion-controlled process.

The possible formation mechanism and optical properties of the CQDs were corroborated through DFT studies. The CS and AA molecules and the resulting molecules after blending are shown in Fig. 4a. Figure 4b shows the binding energy of the interaction of the primary amine (–NH2) with AA and the secondary amine (–NH) with AA for the formation of the chitosan-acetic acid (CS-AA) complex. The interaction between the primary amine and AA occurred at a binding energy of − 1.9 kcal/mol and appeared to be sharper than the interaction between the secondary amine of chitosan and AA, which exhibited a binding energy of − 2.6 kcal/mol. Further isothermal–isobaric ensemble (NPT) modeling performed on the simulated CS-AA resulted in the formation of 3-nm-diameter CQDs composed of 231 carbon atoms, 61 hydrogen atoms, 12 nitrogen atoms and 08 oxygen atoms (Fig. 4c).

The band structure, TDOS, and PDOS of the synthesized CQD nanoclusters reveal that the valence band corresponding to the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the conduction band associated with the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) are primarily governed by energy levels originating from the C (1s) orbital. Minor contributions from C (2p), H (1s), and O (1s) orbitals are also present. Notably, the dominant states near the Fermi level arise from C (1s)–O (1s) and C (1s)–H (1s) interactions, which exhibit metallic behavior in both spin-up and spin-down configurations. In addition, the electronic structure incorporates semiconducting features attributed to N (1s)–C (2p) orbital interactions, as illustrated in Fig. 4e. These contributions are observed within the valence band energy ranges of − 6.98 to − 6.12 eV and − 5.57 to − 1.20 eV, with the HOMO located at − 6.98 eV. The conduction band displays N (1s)–C (2p) orbital contributions spanning + 3.57 to + 11.92 eV, with the LUMO positioned at + 3.57 eV. According to Koopman’s theory [Ref.17], the negative of the HOMO energy approximates the ionization potential, while the negative of the LUMO energy approximates the electron affinity. Thus, the HOMO-LUMO gap provides a first-order estimate of the excitation energy, which is directly relevant to the optical absorption behavior of the CQDs.

The resulting mixed electronic character of the CQDs is likely due to the overlap of carbon and nitrogen atomic orbitals, coupled with the electron-donating (“push”) and electron-withdrawing (“pull”) effects of nitrogen heteroatoms embedded within the CQD framework. Furthermore, the spatial arrangement and hybridization of atoms, particularly heteroatoms such as nitrogen, oxygen, and sulphur, play a critical role in modulating the electron density near the Fermi level. This directly influences the HOMO-LUMO gap and the onset of optical absorption. Electron-donating functional groups (e.g., –OH, –NH2) tend to elevate the HOMO level, whereas electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., –NO2, –CN) lower the LUMO level, collectively leading to a reduced band gap and a red-shift in absorption. These trends are reflected in the computed band gaps and corroborated by the observed spectral features.

The simulated band gap of the CQDs is approximately 3.41 eV, which closely aligns with values obtained from cyclic voltammetry measurements. Additionally, the highly symmetric band structure and DOS confirm the absence of a magnetic moment in the CQDs.

Figure 4g shows the simulated optical absorption spectrum of the CQDs in the wavelength range of 1000–190 nm, with two prominent peaks at 298 nm (major) and 230 nm (minor/hump). These peaks correspond to electronic transitions from the HOMO to LUMO and higher unoccupied states, and are consistent with the calculated band gap. The major peak at 298 nm aligns with the energy of ~ 4.16 eV, which is slightly higher than the DFT band gap due to the known underestimation of excitation energies in ground-state DFT. Importantly, the optical transitions are strongly influenced by the presence and configuration of nitrogen atoms within the CQD matrix. The N(1s)–C(2p) orbital interactions introduce localized states that facilitate π–π* and n–π* transitions, enhancing absorption in the UV region. These transitions are further modulated by the electron-donating or withdrawing nature of nitrogen-containing functional groups, which alter the electronic density distribution and transition dipole moments. Thus, the optical properties of the CQDs are not only consistent with the DFT-predicted electronic structure but are also tunable through targeted incorporation of nitrogen functionalities. This correlation underscores the role of atomic-level design in tailoring the optoelectronic behavior of CQDs.

In vitro biological studies

The cytotoxicity of CQDs was evaluated on Vero cells to establish a safe therapeutic dose. MTT assays were conducted over a concentration range of 15–500 µg/ml, with 62.5 µg/ml reducing cell viability by 55% after 24 h. According to ISO 10993-5 standards, a viability loss of less than 30% is considered non-toxic, indicating that CQDs at concentrations above 62 µg/ml could be safely used for therapeutic applications66. Detailed cytotoxicity data are presented in Fig. 5a.

The DPPH scavenging activity of the CQDs was assessed in comparison to ascorbic acid using Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus cultures. Treatment with CQDs induced a color change of DPPH from violet to orange/yellow, with the intensity of the orange coloration increasing with CQD concentration, reflecting enhanced ROS production. The lowest CQD concentration exhibited 6.02% scavenging activity, while the highest reached 59.17%. The increasing ROS generation may be attributed to the synergistic effect of CQDs and surface functional groups such as C=O and C–O67–69. These results, shown in Fig. 5b, indicate that the synthesized CQDs possess significant scavenging activity, comparable to ascorbic acid, though slightly less potent.

The antibacterial activity of the synthesized CQDs was tested against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus. A CQD concentration of 31.37 µg/ml effectively inhibited bacterial growth for up to 12 h in both strains. These results indicate that 62.5 µg/ml represents the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC), confirming the CQDs’ potent antibacterial and bactericidal effects. This was further validated by bacterial growth observations on MHA plates, and the percentage inhibition is shown in Fig. 5c.The FTIR analysis revealed hydroxyl (–OH), amino (–NH₂), carboxyl (–COOH), and carbonyl (C=O) groups. These functional groups play dual roles: (a) Antioxidant activity arises from the hydrogen-donating capacity of –OH and –NH₂ groups, neutralizing DPPH radicals. (b) Antibacterial activity is mediated by electrostatic interaction between the positively charged amino groups and negatively charged bacterial membranes, leading to membrane disruption and ROS generation, as supported by previous studies70. Amikacin, a clinically used broad-spectrum aminoglycoside antibiotic, was employed as a commercial reference antibacterial agent. Comparative antibacterial assays showed that the inhibition efficiency of the CQDs was closely comparable to that of amikacin, with approximately 80–85% similarity in bacterial growth suppression. These findings indicate that the synthesized CQDs exhibit antibacterial activity approaching that of a standard therapeutic antibiotic, underscoring their potential as alternative or adjunct antibacterial agents.

The primary antibacterial mechanism of CQDs involves ROS generation, triggered by oxidative stress induced upon exposure to the nanomaterial. This ROS production disrupts ion channel function through oxidative phosphorylation, leading to ATP depletion and impaired bacterial metabolism via energy uncoupling. Additionally, the nanoscale size and strong positive surface charge of the CQDs enable them to penetrate and damage bacterial cell walls, resulting in cell death71–73.

Bioimaging

When applied to Vero cells, the CQDs exhibited efficient cellular internalization without inducing morphological damage or cytotoxic effects. Fluorescence microscopy revealed a uniform cytoplasmic distribution of the CQDs, indicating their effective uptake and retention within the cells. The emission intensity under UV excitation confirmed that the inherent photoluminescent properties of the CQDs were preserved post cellular uptake, making them suitable for live-cell imaging, as shown in Fig. 6a, b with the magnification of 20X and 100X. The mild synthesis conditions, particularly the use of 0.5% acetic acid and a controlled temperature profile, played a crucial role in maintaining the surface functional groups of the chitosan-derived CQDs, which are essential for the bio-interface, biocompatibility and cellular targeting74. The absence of high-temperature carbonization steps or toxic dopants further enhances their safety profile for in vitro applications75,76.

While CQDs show good cytocompatibility up to 62.5 µg/mL, long-term studies remain limited. Previous studies indicate that CQDs may accumulate intracellularly, potentially altering lysosomal pH or mitochondrial function. Therefore, future in vivo studies on renal clearance and biodegradation kinetics are necessary for clinical translation. CQDs with a + 12.6 mV zeta potential and ~ 3 nm in size facilitate endocytosis via both clathrin-mediated and macropinocytosis pathways. Studies suggest that surface charge > + 10 mV enhances cellular membrane adhesion and internalization while promoting lysosomal escape, crucial for drug delivery applications.

It is important to consider their potential long-term effects, particularly regarding intracellular accumulation under physiological conditions. Given their small size and surface functionality, CQDs can interact favorably with biological systems; however, their long-term persistence in cells, especially within organelles like lysosomes or mitochondria, warrants careful evaluation to ensure they do not interfere with cellular homeostasis over extended periods. Encouragingly, some studies suggest that surface modification and appropriate functionalization may enhance their biodegradability and reduce the risk of accumulation. Nonetheless, a thorough investigation into their degradation pathways, clearance mechanisms, and interactions with physiological environments, such as pH and protein-rich media, is essential. Addressing these aspects proactively will strengthen the case for CQDs as safe and effective tools in biomedical applications, particularly for long-term therapeutic or imaging purposes.

In summary, the hydrothermally synthesized CQDs demonstrated excellent fluorescence properties and biocompatibility, enabling efficient visualization of Vero cells. Owing to their ease of preparation, tunable photoluminescence, and nontoxic nature, these materials are promising candidates for real-time cellular imaging and future theranostic applications.

From an in vivo perspective, the combination of ultrasmall size and moderate positive charge is expected to yield a characteristic biodistribution profile. CQDs with core sizes below ~ 5–6 nm typically lie near the renal filtration threshold and can undergo rapid glomerular clearance, reducing long-term accumulation in the liver and spleen and thereby mitigating chronic toxicity risks. At the same time, their cationic surface may favor the adsorption of serum proteins and enhanced uptake in highly perfused organs, such as the liver, spleen, and kidney, particularly within the mononuclear phagocyte system. This dual influence suggests that fine-tuning surface charge through additional functionalization or partial shielding of amino groups could be a powerful strategy to modulate circulation half-life, organ-specific accumulation, and clearance kinetics depending on the intended application (short-term diagnostic imaging versus sustained drug delivery). Taken together, these considerations indicate that the + 12.6 mV surface charge and ~ 3 nm size of the chitosan-derived CQDs not only support efficient cellular internalization in vitro but also point to a potentially favorable in vivo biodistribution and clearance profile, which should be systematically validated in future animal studies using quantitative pharmacokinetic and organ biodistribution analysis.

Conclusion

We successfully synthesized chitosan-derived bright cyan emissive carbon quantum dots (CQDs) via a simple, eco-friendly hydrothermal method. The CQDs demonstrated a uniform spherical morphology (≈ 3 nm), a nanocrystalline carbon phase, surface functionalization with hydroxyl/amine groups, and a positive zeta potential (+ 12.6 mV), confirming good colloidal stability. Optical investigations revealed strong cyan-blue emission at 473 nm with excitation-dependent behavior and a Stokes shift of 95 nm, alongside a moderate PLQY of ≈ 17.5%. Cyclic voltammetry was used to establish band edge positions relevant for optoelectronic applications. Importantly, DFT simulations provided atomistic insight into the formation mechanism of CQDs from chitosan–acetic acid complexes and reproduced their optoelectronic properties, predicting a band gap of ≈ 3.41 eV, which is in excellent agreement with the experimental value (3.45 eV). Theoretical analysis also highlighted nitrogen incorporation as a dominant factor influencing the electronic density of states and optical transitions, offering a mechanistic basis for tuning luminescence through precursor chemistry. Biological evaluations confirmed dose-dependent cytocompatibility (Vero cells), significant antioxidant activity (DPPH assay), and antibacterial efficacy against E. coli and S. aureus, which was mediated through ROS generation and electrostatic disruption of bacterial membranes. Fluorescence microscopy demonstrated efficient cellular uptake and uniform intracellular distribution of the CQDs, supporting their applicability for real-time bioimaging. Overall, the integration of experimental and theoretical approaches not only validates the green synthesis route but also provides a mechanistic understanding of CQD formation and optical behavior. This combined insight underscores the potential of chitosan-derived CQDs as sustainable nanomaterials for bioimaging, biosensing, targeted drug delivery, and future theranostic applications.
