Recent advances in spike-based neural coding for tactile perception
Authors: Zimeng Zhu, Kaiyun Chen, Waner Lin, Hanming Yan, Lvzhou Liang, Guodong Wang, Xian Song, Chi Zhang, Ziya Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTactile perception in artificial systems remains constrained by the von Neumann architecture, where the separation of memory and computation leads to significant latency and energy inefficiency. Neuromorphic engineering provides a biologically inspired alternative by adopting event-driven, spike-based coding, akin to neural signaling in human somatosensory systems. This review systematically examines spike-based neural coding techniques for tactile perception, focusing on three key aspects: encoding strategies, neuromorphic hardware implementations, and decoding methodologies. It compares rate coding and temporal coding in terms of biological plausibility and computational efficiency, particularly in dynamic and high-speed tactile tasks. A range of hardware platforms is evaluated, including oscillator-based encoding circuits, CMOS and memristor-based spiking neurons, and self-powered tactile sensors using triboelectric nanogenerators. On the decoding side, mechanisms such as spike-timing-dependent plasticity and spiking neural networks are analyzed for their potential to support adaptive, online learning in tactile systems. The review emphasizes co-design approaches that integrate sensing, encoding, and processing within a unified framework to achieve system-level efficiency. By bridging advances in functional materials, low-power hardware, and brain-inspired computation, this work outlines a roadmap toward artificial tactile systems with millisecond-level latency, sub-milliwatt power consumption, and high perceptual fidelity. These capabilities are essential for future applications in robotics, prosthetics, and wearable electronics.Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Electronic devices

Introduction

Tactile perception, a cornerstone of human interaction with the physical world, enables the assessment of critical object properties such as size, temperature, vibration, and texture, as well as the detection of slippage and the regulation of grasping force during complex manipulation tasks1. Similarly, in artificial systems aiming to emulate human-level perceptual competence, it is essential to associate the tactile sensing with bioinspired neural processing in a way that receptors work in the peripheral nervous system.

Traditional artificial tactile systems, rooted in the von Neumann computing paradigm, inherently suffer from a structural dichotomy between sensing and processing: tactile data captured by distributed sensors must be transmitted to centralized computational units that are physically decoupled from the sensing interface2. This centralized architecture necessitates bandwidth-heavy data transfer of redundant raw data, resulting in high latency and power consumption that hinder real-time closed-loop applications like robotic manipulation3.

To address these limitations, neuromorphic engineering has emerged as a promising alternative, offering event-driven, distributed, and memory-computation integrated processing inspired by the operation of biological nervous systems4. At the core of this paradigm, spikes serve as the fundamental units of a brain-like computational framework, encoding and transmitting information via discrete temporal events rather than continuous signals5. This inherently sparse and asynchronous communication enables highly efficient, low-latency computation, making it well-suited for real-time, embedded tactile systems. However, realizing the full energy-efficiency potential of neuromorphic computing requires dedicated hardware implementations6. Software-based approaches, though flexible, remain constrained by the von Neumann architecture, which separates memory and processing and relies on energy-intensive, clock-driven execution7. In contrast, hardware neuromorphic systems can integrate sensing, memory, and computation in a unified, event-driven architecture, paving the way for sub-milliwatt operation under stringent power and latency constraints-crucial for applications in wearable electronics and autonomous robotics8.

One of the central challenges in building neuromorphic tactile systems lies in the seamless integration of sensory output and neural computation9. While substantial progress has been achieved in the development of artificial mechanoreceptors such as piezoresistive, capacitive, and piezoelectric sensors, these devices typically produce analog signals in response to tactile stimuli. To enable compatibility with neuromorphic systems, these analog signals must be converted into spike-based representations. As a result, a critical area of ongoing research is the design of encoding schemes capable of transforming raw tactile data into event-driven spike patterns suitable for neuromorphic processing10.

Spike encoding at the level of individual neurons can be grouped into rate coding and temporal coding, which are also consistent with neural information processing strategies observed in biological systems. In rate coding, information is embedded in the average instantaneous firing rate of a neuron or a population of neurons over a given time window. Due to its simplicity and resilience to noise, rate coding has been widely adopted, especially for tasks involving static or slowly varying tactile stimuli, such as pressure magnitude estimation or object hardness classification11. In contrast, temporal coding exploits the precise timing of spikes to convey information. This includes absolute spike times with respect to a global clock12, relative delays between spikes of different neurons13, or even the temporal order in which a neuronal ensemble fires14. Such methods provide higher temporal resolution and richer representational capacity, which are essential for capturing the dynamic nature of tactile interactions15, such as vibration frequency, texture roughness, or the onset of slippage. In addition to these single-neuron coding schemes, population coding operates at the ensemble level by integrating signals across multiple afferent neuron classes, thereby enhancing the robustness and efficiency of tactile information representation16.

The hardware implementation of spike encoding introduces significant complexity to neuromorphic tactile systems17. Most existing spike encoding approaches rely on oscillators such as voltage-controlled or ring oscillators, converting sensor signals into frequency-modulated spike trains. However, these systems typically produce rigid, fixed-amplitude outputs that fail to replicate the adaptive firing behavior observed in biological neurons18. For instance, commercial ring oscillators generate uniform square waveforms lacking the dynamic variability of biological spikes, often necessitating the use of additional edge detection circuits to refine the output19. To overcome these limitations, extensive research has focused on CMOS-based and memristor-based devices20. These circuits offer enhanced biomimetic capabilities through adaptive firing behaviors and exhibit the potential for energy-efficient or even autonomous operation, positioning them as promising candidates for next-generation tactile encoding hardware.

Decoding spike trains into meaningful tactile information presents a significant challenge and can be broadly categorized into fundamental and advanced processing levels. At the fundamental level, biologically inspired synaptic plasticity mechanisms such as spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP), short-term plasticity (STP), and long-term plasticity (LTP) are employed to enable low-level feature extraction and local signal integration. These mechanisms emulate biological learning rules while enabling postsynaptic current generation and supporting the construction of dynamic, adaptive networks. In contrast, advanced processing relies on algorithmic approaches, including conventional machine learning methods and spiking neural networks (SNNs) to perform complex perceptual tasks such as texture classification, object recognition, and material discrimination. These models exploit spatial-temporal correlations in spike trains to achieve higher-level abstraction and robust inference.

While numerous reviews have explored tactile perception systems, most focus either on the development of novel tactile sensors or on downstream neuromorphic algorithms in isolation. A significant portion of this literature emphasizes emerging materials and transduction mechanisms, often without addressing how sensory signals are converted into spike-based representations and subsequently interpreted by neural-inspired systems. In contrast, this review provides a comprehensive overview of the neuron-inspired tactile coding process, encompassing spike-based encoding schemes, neuromorphic hardware implementations, and decoding strategies, as shown in Fig. 1. By systematically linking these components, we present an integrated framework that emphasizes their functional interdependence and co-design requirements. We first compare spike encoding schemes—rate, temporal, and population coding—in terms of biological plausibility, computational efficiency, and suitability for dynamic tactile inputs. Next, we examine hardware platforms including oscillator-based circuits, memristive neurons, and energy-autonomous sensors, evaluating their trade-offs in fidelity, scalability, and integration. Finally, we assess decoding strategies, ranging from synaptic plasticity mechanisms to machine learning algorithms and SNN-based inference, highlighting their roles in enabling adaptive and context-aware tactile intelligence. This unified perspective fills a critical gap in the existing literature and serves as a roadmap for advancing brain-inspired tactile systems with perceptual richness, high energy efficiency, and low latency.

Architecture of spike-based neural coding techniques.

The biological neural pathway can be divided into three main components: sensory input, spike encoding, and spike decoding. Spike encoding encompasses both rate coding and temporal coding, and can be implemented using oscillator-based or neuron-inspired circuits. Spike encoding

In tactile neural systems, spike trains are employed as transmission signals owing to their low energy consumption and capacity to convey rich temporal information. Nevertheless, most tactile sensors produce analog outputs, necessitating a conversion process. In this context, spike encoding refers to the transformation of continuous analog signals into discrete spike-based representations, as shown in Fig. 2a. At the level of individual neurons, spike encoding strategies are typically classified into two principal categories, rate coding and temporal coding, according to whether information is conveyed through the number of spikes over time or through their precise temporal patterns. Additionally, population coding is regarded as a complementary framework that captures representational patterns across neural ensembles.

Rate coding

In rate coding, neural information is transmitted via the firing rate, which results in spike trains with varying frequencies. Two primary rate coding schemes are commonly used: count rate coding and Poisson rate coding. In count rate coding, information is represented by the average number of spikes emitted by a neuron within a defined time window, typically quantified by the mean firing rate, as depicted in Fig. 2b. In contrast, Poisson rate coding models the spike generation process as a stochastic event, where the number of spikes occurring in a given time interval follows a Poisson distribution, introducing biologically plausible variability into the spike trains21, as depicted in Fig. 2c.

Rate coding is the most widely adopted spike encoding strategy, as it directly reflects stimulus intensity through spike frequency. Tan et al. converted pressure information to light pulses by coupling light-emitting diodes (LEDs) to analog-to-digital circuits, showing the frequency increase of the spike trains in response to an increasing pressure22. As shown in Fig. 3a, Zhang et al. demonstrated a quasi-linear relationship between input intensity and spiking frequencies under appropriate stimuli, and observed a reduction in firing frequency when noxious stimuli were applied, which approximates the response characteristics of biological neurons23. However, due to the discrete spike distribution and limited time steps, the representation resolution of rate coding is inherently low. Moreover, when the input is converted to a spike rate, the number of spikes required to express the same value increases as the time step increases. Consequently, achieving high model performance with rate coding necessitates a large number of time steps, leading to significant latency24.

Additionally, rate coding works well only if the intensity of the stimulus is a constant value. In this case, the information of this signal can be encoded into the spike number within the encoding window25. When the stimulus fluctuates, its temporal variations may appear as noise in the frequency spectrum. Then, frequency-domain analysis and temporal coding that contains extra information are required.

Temporal coding

Compared to rate coding, which relies solely on statistical representations, temporal coding has the potential to increase the amount of processed information and improve energy efficiency when dealing with large volumes of sequential data. By answering the question whether the exact timing and order of spikes is crucial for the information, temporal coding can be divided into several different subcategories26. Here, we only concentrate on three of them that are widely used in tactile perception.

Time-to-first-spike coding

Time-to-first-spike (TTFS) coding is the most basic temporal coding scheme, encoding in the delay between stimulus onset and the first spike, as shown in Fig. 2d. In this scheme, the arrival time of the first spike is inversely proportional to the input intensity, enabling highly energy-efficient signal representation by requiring only a single spike for representation27. In addition, TTFS exhibits low latency by requiring only a minimal number of time steps, enabling it to respond instantaneously to variations in the input signal. As illustrated in Fig. 3b, Chen et al. employed TTFS coding to encode highly dynamic tactile information, demonstrating the significant correlation between spike patterns and the curvature and stiffness of various surfaces28. Notably, TTFS coding showed high efficiency in encoding dynamic information about surface deformation during touch, along with a rapid classification speed when identifying 22 different daily-life objects during grasping. Furthermore, You et al. enhanced SNN performance using TTFS coding, achieving signal processing within 21–62 ms and encoder frequencies between 2.5 and 9.5 kHz29. This study highlighted the lower latency and broader modulating frequency range of TTFS coding compared to rate coding, thereby making SNNs more suitable for real-time applications and complex temporal signal processing tasks. Nevertheless, TTFS coding also suffers from certain limitations, including its limited capacity to represent complex or long-duration signals, its sensitivity to noise at the stimulus onset, and its strong dependence on precise temporal synchronization, which may restrict its robustness in practical scenarios.

Inter-spike interval coding

The second temporal coding scheme is inter-spike interval (ISI) coding, which encodes all relevant information into several inter-spike intervals26, as illustrated in Fig. 2e. Generally, two primary approaches to ISI coding have been identified.

The first approach encodes information based on the relative timing between grouped spike blocks. In this scheme, ISIs are categorized into short, medium, and long durations according to inter-spike interval histogram (ISIH) analysis30. Among these, short ISIs, commonly referred to as “bursts”, represent a distinct form of ISI coding. From this perspective, burst coding can be considered a subcategory of ISI coding, which has been demonstrated to be more reliable and carry more information than other spike trains31. This kind of ISI coding can be exemplified by Morse code, where information is encoded using spiking and quiescent periods. Specifically, short and long spiking times, which correspond to brief and prolonged tactile stimuli, represent dots and dashes, respectively. Similarly, variations in quiescent durations encode intra-letter and inter-letter spaces within Morse code, as shown in Fig. 3c. This approach enables the encoding and recognition of all 26 letters of the English alphabet. Wen et al. demonstrated an artificial tactile perception system that converted tactile inputs into ISI-coded electrical spikes, successfully recognizing four letters in Morse code as a proof of concept32.

The second ISI coding approach encodes information into relative timing between isolated spikes33. In particular, the n−1 ISIs between n spikes within a spike train can theoretically encode n−1 independent values. For example, Ishii et al. encoded the amplitude and phase of the oscillatory sequence using the two ISIs between three spikes34. Moreover, the ISIs can be designed using different encoding rules to carry high-dimensional information. Zhao et al. designed two different ISI encoders, including a parallel encoder and an iteration encoder25. In their design, ISIs in the parallel encoder are isolated with each other, while the latter ISIs in the iteration encoder are affected by the former ISIs.

From a broader perspective, ISI coding exhibits both advantages and limitations. Its major advantage lies in its ability to represent richer and more continuous information compared to TTFS coding, since multiple inter-spike intervals can collectively capture temporal patterns and dynamic features of the stimulus. In addition, ISI coding does not rely on a strict global time reference, making it more robust to temporal shifts and noise at stimulus onset. Nevertheless, ISI coding often requires multiple spikes to encode information, which introduces higher latency and energy consumption compared to single-spike schemes. These trade-offs suggest that ISI coding is particularly suitable for representing temporally extended or rhythm-like signals, but may be less optimal in scenarios requiring ultra-fast and energy-efficient responses.

Phase coding

The third temporal coding scheme is phase coding, which utilizes a periodic reference signal and encodes information in the relative timing between neural spikes and this reference35, as shown in Fig. 2f. The phase pattern repeats periodically as long as no changes occur between cycles26. Moreover, phase coding has been shown to significantly enhance the information encoding capacity, although its effectiveness is limited to slow (<30 Hz) fluctuations36. In contrast, fast oscillations contribute little to spike-phase coding, limiting its applicability in rapidly changing sensory environments.

A notable implementation of phase coding is found in oscillatory neural networks (ONNs). ONNs consist of coupled oscillators functioning as neurons and can effectively capture dynamical behaviors of high-order nonlinear systems37. Their ability to process information in the continuous-time domain enables them to assimilate intricate temporal patterns with greater efficiency. As illustrated in Fig. 3d, Yang et al. constructed an ONN comprising two oscillatory neurons: one functioning as a reference neuron with a fixed natural frequency, and the other serving as a sensory neuron coupled to a pressure sensor38. When stress is applied to the pressure sensor, altering its capacitance, the two neurons synchronize to a common frequency through phase interaction and adjustment enabled by phase coding.

Population coding

From the perspective of spike signal formatting, two primary encoding schemes exist. One scheme attempts to encode all relevant information within a single neuron by focusing on the density of afferents from the same class, such as rate or temporal coding. And the other one, known as population coding, distributes information across a population of neurons, as illustrated in Fig. 2g. By integrating signals from multiple afferent classes, population coding enhances information transmission, often proving to be more efficient than single-neuron encoding39.

In human tactile afferents, two classes are mainly involved in discriminative touch: slowly-adapting (SA) afferents and fast-adapting (FA) afferents. Each class contains two subtypes that encode distinct tactile features, contributing differently to population coding due to their unique skin innervation densities and spatial distributions40. SA-I afferents, within the SA class, specialize in spatial processing and are sensitive to fine details such as edges and texture41. Rapidly adapting (RA) afferents, part of the FA class, are tuned to dynamic features like motion and orientation, with information primarily conveyed through population spike counts42. Pacinian corpuscle (PC) afferents, another FA subtype, are highly responsive to high-frequency vibrations and contribute to the perception of texture and remote mechanical events43. In addition to these three major subtypes, SA-II afferents respond mainly to skin stretch and are thought to support hand conformation and shape perception, although their sparse distribution and limited temporal precision often result in their exclusion from population-level tactile models.

The integration of signals from SA-I, RA, and PC afferents offers complementary information, enhancing the robustness and resolution of tactile coding44. Saal et al. showed the improvement in spatial resolution and other features through the combination of two or three kinds of afferents45. Corniani et al. used a large-scale neural simulator to simulate the spiking activity of individual afferents belonging to these three afferent subtypes, computing how much information about each stimulus feature was encoded in the activity of different populations of afferents16.

However, the aforementioned population coding schemes typically consider the tuning properties of individual neurons independently, evaluating joint representations without accounting for inter-neuronal functional interactions. This approach is referred to as independent population coding, which is depicted in Fig. 2h. In contrast, as shown in Fig. 2i, correlated population coding arises from interactions between neurons, where macroscopic coding properties emerge from microscopic activity correlations. This approach reflects the functional dependencies within neural populations that cannot be captured by examining individual neurons alone.

Hardware implementations of spike encoding

Considerable research has been devoted to software-based implementations of spike encoding for advancing tactile neural systems. In these approaches, the encoding processes are typically executed on centralized processing units, such as computers or cloud servers. However, this architecture inherently separates the sensing components from the signal processing units, necessitating substantial data transfer between them. This data-intensive architecture leads to significant energy consumption, thereby limiting the efficiency and scalability of such systems.

In general, two hardware implementations of spike encoding have been explored18. One approach integrates the sensor with oscillating circuits, sometimes in combination with edge detection circuits, while the other interfaces the sensor directly with neuron-inspired circuits. The difference between these two implementations is the shape of the generated spike signal. The first strategy can only generate fixed-shaped spikes where no biologically featured information can be carried. In contrast, the second strategy—capable of mimicking biological activities in the brain using various neural models—can generate more biologically plausible spikes. Additionally, nanogenerators are also employed to achieve self-powered systems. A schematic overview of these representative hardware implementations is illustrated in Fig. 4.

Oscillating circuit interfaced sensing

Typically, oscillating circuits contain a large amount of circuit categories, but here we only concentrate on two kinds of oscillating circuits that are widely used in tactile neural systems.

Voltage-controlled oscillator

One typical approach is to use voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO) circuits to generate digital signals whose frequency is directly proportional to the input voltage, as depicted in Fig. 4a. In this configuration, VCOs act as analog-to-digital converters (ADCs) by converting analog voltage inputs into frequency-modulated digital signals46. These signals are subsequently transformed into spike signals via edge detectors (EDs), which count the transitions of the VCO output during a defined sampling period. Additionally, the resistance changes in the flexible tactile sensor, induced by applied force, are converted into voltage signals of varying amplitudes. Consequently, the frequency of the spike signals is positively correlated with the voltage amplitude, effectively reflecting the variations in pressure intensity.

From the perspective of spike encoding, VCOs are employed to implement rate coding. Kim et al. used a voltage-controlled oscillator (LTC6990) and its associated circuit to capture the resistance changes induced by pressure stimuli47. In their system, the tactile sensor’s voltage signals were converted into digital signals and modulated such that the spike frequency varied proportionally with the intensity of the applied pressure. As shown in Fig. 5a, Sun et al. utilized a voltage-frequency conversion module (AD654) along with its peripheral circuit as an oscillator to convert the analog voltage signals from the flexible tactile sensors into frequency-modulated spike trains48. This approach allows the output spike trains to encode both the magnitude and duration of the applied pressure.

This VCO-based rate coding strategy is advantageous due to its circuit simplicity, ease of integration, and robustness against noise. In particular, it can be readily implemented using existing commercial oscillator units (e.g., LTC6990, AD654), which greatly facilitates hardware prototyping and system-level integration without the need for complex custom circuit design. Nevertheless, the method mainly conveys stimulus intensity while offering limited temporal precision, and its effective resolution is further constrained by the linearity of the VCO as well as its power consumption, which may restrict its applicability in energy-sensitive or high-precision scenarios.

Ring oscillator

Ring oscillators (ROs) are printed organic circuits composed of an odd number of inverter stages based on complementary field-effect transistors, as shown in Fig. 4b. Generally, ROs can be regarded as a kind of VCOs, generating a series of voltage spikes according to the input stimuli. However, different from other electrical oscillators, ring oscillators are constructed with printed organic circuits and have the advantages of simplicity and low static power dissipation49.

The output signal of the ring oscillator is a square wave with oscillating frequency fo depending on the propagation delay td per stage and the number of stages N used in the ring structure, which can be described as50:

The delay time td depends on factors such as supply voltage, transistor size, and threshold voltage. In a fixed-structure RO system with a constant number of delay stages, precise control of td is essential, making its accurate modeling a critical aspect of RO circuit design51. For example, Tee et al. modulate the oscillation frequency by reducing the supply voltage via reduction of the dielectric thickness, matching the typical biological mechanoreceptor frequency range of ~0–200 Hz49.

Although the integration of the pressure sensor and synaptic transistor alone can already mimic the sensory neuron, the use of the ring oscillator was intended to replicate the generation of action potentials more accurately52. When the pressure is applied, the supply voltage to the ring oscillator increases, resulting in a constant frequency output that has a one-to-one correspondence with the input pressure19.

Using ROs in isolation would generate square-shaped pulses that have a wide pulse duration and are different from the real spikes transmitted in natural organisms53. To better emulate biological spike encoding, EDs are employed to detect the rising edges of oscillatory signals and generate sharp, temporally precise pulses. As described in Fig. 5b, the pulse width can be modulated through the structure of the ED, including the stage number of the inverter chain and the transistor size54.

ROs are always used to realize rate coding for its close relationships between the input pressure and the output frequency. For example, Chen et al. implemented rate coding by designing a tactile nervous system, where ring oscillator was used alone to generate frequency-related spikes55. Moreover, with their pulse-width modulation capability, EDs can be integrated with ROs to realize temporal coding in addition to rate coding. Tan et al. coupled light-emitting diodes (LEDs) to ROs and EDs and encoded detected information to optical spikes, implementing both rate coding and temporal coding to recognize Morse code and object movement22.

Effective integration of multiple sensory modalities is crucial for enabling robots and neuromorphic systems to process complex external information56. While strategies such as data fusion and the use of functional materials can facilitate multimodal integration and improve signal quality, challenges remain in minimizing system complexity and maintaining efficient, compact designs. Multiple ring oscillators can also be used for the realization of cross-modal integration and recognition. Tan et al. presented a multisensory system that integrated senses of vision, touch, hearing, smell and taste, encoding signals from five sensors into optical spikes and decoding the multisensory information via artificial neural networks (ANNs)57. In this study, each sensor was connected to a spike encoder which consisted of a commercial ring oscillator, edge detector, amplifier and LED, inducing system complexity and inefficiency. Furthermore, multimodal systems always require control of input timing between spike trains to avoid superposition of the input spike trains, which is a fatal drawback when constituting a compact and neat system. To address these issues, Kim et al. encoded two distinct sensory signals—amplitude and frequency—into a single voltage pulse using only one ring oscillator58.

Spiking neuron interfaced sensing

Another implementation of spike encoding is spiking neuron interfaced sensing, including several neuron models such as the leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) model, the Hodgkin–Huxley model, and the Izhikevich model. These neuron models can be implemented using one or two capacitors with leaky resistors to emulate the important features observed in biological neurons, such as spatiotemporal integration and the all-or-none firing rule.

Electrically, these three models are capable of reproducing the characteristic “firing” behavior of biological neurons. Among them, the Hodgkin–Huxley model provides a detailed and biophysically accurate representation of neuronal activity, but its complexity limits its suitability for practical hardware implementations. In contrast, the Izhikevich model combines computational efficiency with the ability to generate a wide range of biologically realistic firing patterns, making it well-suited for neuroprosthetic applications. Nevertheless, the LIF model, originally proposed by Thorpe and Gautrais, remains the most favorable for hardware implementation due to its simplicity and ease of emulating spike-like dynamics.

CMOS-based devices

CMOS-based techniques are commonly used to fabricate computing units based on conventional von Neumann architecture, such as microcontrollers, microprocessors, and system-on-chip (SoC), as illustrated in Fig. 4c. Among them, the most commonly used one in tactile neural systems is the microcontroller unit (MCU), which integrates a central processing unit (CPU), memory, and input/ output devices. By reducing the size and cost compared to designs requiring external peripherals, MCUs enable the realization of portable hardware-based tactile neural systems.

Different kinds of encoding algorithms and neuron models can be realized using an MCU. Jiang et al. used an ATmega328P MCU to implement a spike-encoding circuit, as illustrated in Fig. 5c, which converted sensor signals into frequency-coded spikes, with the spike rate proportional to applied pressure, generating the SA and FA spike trains59. Furthermore, Jiang et al. also built a bioinspired motion-cognition nerve based on a flexible multisensory neuromorphic device60. Two sensory modalities (acceleration and rotation) were converted into two spatiotemporal spike trains by a microcontroller-based flexible spike-encoding circuit using the rate coding method.

Compared to MCUs, SoCs typically integrate more advanced peripherals like a graphics unit, a Wi-Fi module, or one or more coprocessors. Chun et al. developed an ISI encoding model using a Raspberry Pi, which can convert a given tactile sensor signal to voltage spikes with different rates and patterns depending on the strength of the sustained stimulus, such as static pressure61. However, neuromorphic computing systems, which address the von Neumann bottleneck, can also be realized by COMS technology. Zhao et al. established two kinds of temporal encoders, including a parallel encoder and an iteration encoder, which are based on standard 180 nm CMOS technologies25.

Memristor-based devices

Approaches using conventional CMOS and organic integrated circuits often involve complicated architectures and suffer from energy inefficiency. Memristors, which are two-terminal electrical switching devices and the internal resistance states rely on the history of applied voltage or current, are regarded as excellent candidates to construct artificial spiking neurons62, as shown in Fig. 4d. Typical memristive devices include resistive switching memory, phase-change memory, magnetic random access memory, and ferroelectric random access memory. Due to the simple two-terminal structure, low operating energy, high switching speed, and direct in-memory computing ability, they have been widely developed for the next-generation memory and computing components.

Memristive devices are often used to implement the LIF model due to their inherent switching characteristics. The memristive device can switch from the high-resistance state to the low-resistance state at a threshold voltage, and then spontaneously turn off when the applied stimulus drops below the hold voltage63. The membrane potential increases when signals are inputted into the neuron. Once it exceeds the threshold voltage, the device switches to a low-resistance state, then the comparator receives a high input, and generates a spike as the output of the LIF neuron. You et al. designed self-rectifying dynamic memristor circuits based on Ta/IGZO/TaOx/Pt to realize the LIF function, which can achieve high spike sparsity and increase the robustness of SNNs29. The design of the LIF neuron can encode analog signals into digital ones without the use of ADCs, reducing energy consumption and space in chips.

It is worth noting that the spiking frequency of biological neurons exhibits a distinct characteristic. Under normal stimulus intensity, the spiking frequency increases with stimulus strength. However, when the generated voltage intensity becomes excessively high, the spiking frequency begins to decrease. Zhang et al. established an artificial spike afferent nerve based on a highly reliable NbOx Mott memristor23. This afferent nerve shows a quasi-linear relationship between input intensity and spiking frequencies under proper stimuli, and tends to reduce firing frequency when noxious stimuli are provided, which faithfully emulates the function of biological neurons.

Memristive devices can also be used to realize the cross-modal system. As depicted in Fig. 5d, Li et al. built cross-modal sensory neurons using a VO2 memristor within an in-sensor computing system, which can encode pressure and temperature bimodal information into spikes64. To encode temperature and pressure information, a temperature-sensing spiking neuron circuit was designed using the intrinsic temperature-dependent phase transition of the VO₂ memristor within a LIF circuit, while an artificial spiking pressure-sensing neuron circuit was developed by integrating a flexible piezoresistive sensor with the LIF circuit. This implementation demonstrates the potential of memristive devices for developing bioinspired sensory systems capable of multimodal information processing and efficient in-sensor computing.

Self-powered sensing

There exists a kind of nanogenerator-based afferent nerve devices, such as ferroelectret nanogenerators (FENGs) and triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs), serving as the sensory mechanoreceptors to replace the resistive switching pressure sensors, as illustrated in Fig. 4e. They can not only serve as the power source to realize self-power systems, but also directly convert mechanical information into action potentials to mimic the biological system65.

In a study, a flexible neurological electronic skin and its peripheral nerve was fabricated by integrating a flexible FENG with artificial synaptic thin-film transistors66, as illustrated in Fig. 5e. The flexible FENG serves as the sensory mechanoreceptor and can convert the tactile input including the magnitude and frequency of the force into presynaptic action potential pulses, which are then passed to the gate of the synaptic transistor to induce change in its postsynaptic current, mimicking the modulation of synaptic weight in biological synapse. Additionally, Choi’s group fabricated a self-powered neuromorphic tactile system composed of an artificial mechanoreceptor, resulting in reduced power consumption compared to the conventional tactile system based on von Neumann computing67. The mechanoreceptor module, which is demonstrated with a TENG as a pressure sensor with sustainable energy harvesting and a biristor as a neuron, does not demand an external energy source and can encode spike signals to act as an input neuron of an SNN.

However, the spike signals generated by these mechanoreceptors cannot be used directly but need to be processed by some regulating circuits. For instance, the output pulse from the FENG66 needs to be regulated using the rectifying and amplifying circuits. This signal processing circuit avoids the impedance mismatch between the FENG and synaptic transistor and allows the tuning of the action potential pulse through altering the period of loading force, time constant of the RC circuit and gain of the amplifier. In another study, a bistable resistor (biristor) was integrated with a TENG to construct a self-powered mechanoreceptor capable of spike encoding68. The biristor, functioning as an artificial spiking neuron, modulated the input current generated by the TENG into discrete spike trains via leaky integrate-and-fire operations, enabling low-power, event-driven tactile signal processing.

Spike decoding

After converting the analog signals from tactile sensors into spike trains, it is essential to extract meaningful tactile information from these neural representations. In this context, spike decoding refers to the process of interpreting spike trains to recover relevant tactile features, such as texture and roughness69. Typically, spike decoding can be achieved through two distinct approaches. The first involves simulating synaptic plasticity to perform fundamental tasks, while the second employs learning algorithms to accomplish more complex tasks.

Spike decoding based on synaptic plasticity

In biological neural networks, neurons are interconnected by trillions of synapses, which play an important role in complex information processing. The strength of these connections is dynamically regulated through a mechanism known as synaptic plasticity, which governs the enhancement or weakening of synaptic connections based on neural activity. As illustrated in Fig. 6a, typical synaptic plasticity includes excitatory postsynaptic current (EPSC), short-term plasticity (STP), long-term plasticity (LTP), paired pulse facilitation (PPF), spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP), and so on.

Classified by the retention time, synaptic plasticity can be simply grouped into STP and LTP70. STP operates on a timescale ranging from milliseconds to minutes, playing a crucial role in decoding temporal information and enabling synapses to adapt based on the timing and frequency of stimuli. In contrast, LTP is a persistent change in synaptic weight, spans from minutes over hours to year, leading to lasting modifications in neural networks and enabling the brain to store extensive amounts of information. These changes in synaptic strength are typically reflected in the amplitude of EPSCs generated when stimuli arrive at the presynaptic membrane.

The STDP mechanism, an advanced form of Hebbian learning, states that synaptic strength can be modulated based on the precise temporal order of pre- and postsynaptic spikes71. Similarly, SRDP is another basic learning principle in human neural networks, modifying the synaptic weight by the control of the firing frequency of presynaptic spikes72. Specifically, higher firing rate facilitates synaptic potentiation and contributes to memory formation.

Fundamental spike decoding refers to the process of utilizing synaptic plasticity to extract the spatiotemporal correlated features from the input pressure stimuli or provide sensory feedback for the artificial tactile systems73. Two emerging devices for simulating synaptic behaviors are the memristive devices and the synaptic transistors, which offer promising avenues for neuromorphic tactile sensing and processing.

Memristive devices

As a promising candidate for neuromorphic computing, memristive devices have two main application scenarios74. One is the usage in mimicking neuronal models, especially the LIF model, which is discussed in Chapter 3. The other is the capability to emulate synaptic plasticity, which enables efficient neuromorphic signal processing75.

As depicted in Fig. 6b, two-terminal memristive devices emulate biological synaptic functions by establishing a connection between a pre-neuron and a post-neuron. The resistance state of these devices represents synaptic weights, which can be dynamically modulated over time in response to synaptic activity. This capacity for modifying synaptic connections, known as synaptic plasticity, is regarded as a fundamental mechanism underlying human learning and memory. Memristive devices exhibit a switching behavior from a high-resistance state to a low-resistance state upon reaching a threshold voltage, which has been demonstrated as a promising candidate for artificial synapse. Depending on their ability to revert to the high-resistance state when the applied voltage falls below a certain hold voltage, these devices are generally categorized as either volatile or nonvolatile76. Volatile memristive devices, which spontaneously return to the high-resistance state, are well-suited for emulating STP due to their inherent nonlinearity and transient memory characteristics77. In contrast, nonvolatile devices, which maintain the low-resistance state without continuous stimulation, are more appropriate for replicating LTP78. These devices are essential for adaptive. This classification underscores their potential to replicate both transient and persistent synaptic behavior, mimicking key functional aspects of biological synapses. For instance, Wang et al. demonstrated a flexible artificial synapse exhibiting robust LTP characteristics with reliability after 1000-folded destructive tests, enabling its integration into neural networks that achieved 95.2% accuracy in handwritten digit recognition through synaptic weight modulation79. Yan et al. engineered flexible plastic-substrate memristive synapses capable of emulating both PPF and STDP. By applying distinct pulse protocols, the devices exhibited characteristic behaviors analogous to biological synapses80. These innovations collectively establish a hardware foundation for implementing bio-realistic learning rules in edge-intelligent tactile systems, where in-sensor synaptic plasticity could enable real-time feature extraction without von Neumann bottlenecks.

In addition to emulating distinct forms of synaptic plasticity, such as STP and LTP, certain artificial synaptic devices are capable of transitioning between these two states under specific conditions81, as shown in Fig. 7a. Yan et al. demonstrated that such transitions can be realized through modulations of a memristor’s resistance over different timescales82. By applying voltage pulse stimuli and monitoring the resulting EPSC in the time domain, they observed that the relaxation behavior of the memristor current reflects the nature of synaptic plasticity. Specifically, an increase in pulse amplitude led to a more pronounced and sustained change in EPSC, suggesting a transition from STP to LTP. Building upon this concept, Feng et al. employed MoS2-based memristors to emulate both volatile and nonvolatile resistive switching behaviors, effectively mimicking STP and LTP, respectively. By adjusting the current compliance during electrical stimulation, the device exhibited abrupt switching to a low resistance state upon exceeding a threshold voltage, followed by spontaneous recovery of resistance after the removal of the bias, irrespective of the polarity of the voltage sweep.

Synaptic transistors

A synaptic transistor is an emerging three-terminal device that enables continuous modulation of channel conductance through gate control, making it capable of emulating synaptic functionality with high energy efficiency, as shown in Fig. 6c. Unlike conventional transistors, which operate with discrete switching states, synaptic transistors can replicate biological synapses by exhibiting multiple resistive states in response to applied voltage83. This capability allows them to mimic the variable strength of synaptic connections, thereby facilitating more complex information processing. Unlike two-terminal memristive devices, synaptic transistors typically adopt a three-terminal structure, comprising source, drain, and gate terminals, closely resembling the structure-function relationship of biological synapses, where the gate terminal functions analogously to the presynaptic input, modulating the channel conductance that reflects synaptic weight.

Synaptic transistors offer enhanced flexibility in regulating synaptic dynamics due to their intrinsic capability for decoupled read and write operations. This structural advantage enables the realization of various forms of synaptic plasticity, including STP, LTP, and STDP, through precise control over gate voltage waveforms, duration, and frequency. For instance, Cho et al. demonstrated that in semi-floating gate synaptic transistors, transient charge accumulation enables STP, persistent charge trapping in the nitride layer leads to LTP, and modulation of synaptic weights based on spike timing realizes STDP, collectively emulating biologically inspired learning rules84. Besides, Gong et al. developed a synaptic memory device consisting of an asymmetric MoS2/CIPS ferroelectric heterostructured field-effect transistor (Afe-FET) and an integrated TENG unit. Under tribopotential modulation, the device emulated EPSC, PPF, STP, and LTP, while optical illumination triggered postsynaptic currents and synaptic weight updates, thereby realizing multimodal synaptic plasticity for neuromorphic applications85.

Building upon the emulation of EPSC, synaptic transistors serve as fundamental building blocks for constructing artificial tactile systems. Their integration into soft electronic skin has enabled neuromorphic sensorimotor loops capable of multimodal perception, pulse-train signal generation, and closed-loop actuation, effectively mimicking biological feedback pathways54. Chen et al. built a flexible artificial organic afferent nerve based on touch-triggered organic electrochemical synaptic transistors86. With the emulation of PPF and post-tetanic potentiation (PTP), the artificial afferent nerve is demonstrated to rapidly implement slip recognition and prevention actions upon the occurrence of object slippage.

These advances highlight the critical role of synaptic transistors in establishing sensory feedback mechanisms that drive adaptive, closed-loop responses. Sun et al. developed an artificial somatosensory system capable of both tactile perception and feedback by integrating flexible tactile sensors, synaptic transistors, artificial muscles, and coupling circuits48. In this system, EPSC responses of the synaptic transistor to varying pulse widths were used to define a stimulation threshold for activating artificial muscles, enabling reflex-like feedback control. Additionally, Yu et al. presented a triboelectrification-activated artificial afferent neuron based on the ion-gel-gated MoS2 postsynaptic transistor, capable of mimicking EPSC87. As described in Fig. 7b, EPSC responses were used to directly reflect the spatiotemporal characteristics of external mechanical stimuli, enabling low-energy, self-powered neuromorphic sensing and dynamic logic recognition.

Spike decoding based on machine learning

Based on the encoded information, machine learning algorithms can be broadly categorized into conventional machine learning (CML) algorithms, which rely on static input coding, and SNNs, which utilize relative spike timing coding. To date, several groups have adapted the array simulation based on flexible artificial synapses for recognition tasks, which can accelerate some intensive computation tasks.

Conventional machine learning algorithms

CML encompasses a broad spectrum of methodologies and has found widespread applications in neuromorphic computing88. It includes both non-neural statistical models, such as support vector machines (SVM), random forest regression (RF), and reservoir computing (RC), as well as biologically inspired ANNs. Within this spectrum, ANNs represent a pivotal subfield, including foundational architectures such as multilayer perceptron (MLP), convolutional neural network (CNN), recurrent neural network (RNN), and long short-term memory (LSTM), along with their innovative integrations and enhanced variants.

CML algorithms provide a viable framework for decoding spike signals generated by tactile sensors, though with a fundamental distinction in input representation. Unlike most applications of machine learning in tactile perception, a fundamental distinction in our approach lies in the preprocessing strategy, whereby CML algorithms operate on feature vectors extracted from raw spike signals using quantitative descriptors such as temporal frequency characteristics, amplitude distributions, and spatiotemporal correlation metrics. These engineered features are subsequently standardized through normalization techniques prior to network input. This preprocessing paradigm ensures dimensional consistency across heterogeneous tactile stimuli while enhancing network convergence efficiency by mitigating feature magnitude disparities.

This feature-based decoding approach has been successfully applied in previous studies. Sankar et al. implemented the spike-based decoding for texture discrimination by utilizing the spike-based features as inputs for SVM to classify the textures89, as shown in Fig. 7c. In their study, average ISI and mean spike rate of the encoded spikes were calculated in every trial window to compress the information and serve as the features for the classification algorithm.

CML can also be used in multimodal systems to process the multisensory data. Rostamian et al. proposed a sensor-integration framework for texture recognition based on multisensory data fusion, in which the spike rates generated by individual Izhikevich neurons were extracted as input features. These features were subsequently fed into two conventional classifiers—Random Forests and K-nearest neighbors—to perform accurate texture classification90. Duan et al. constructed a multilayer perceptron (MLP) to process the encoded pressure and temperature signals based on the voltage dividing effect, recognizing multimodal haptic/temperature patterns91. In their study, the maximum and mean of the output voltage signal, together with the oscillation frequency, were extracted as spike features and included as input variables of the MLP, realizing a multisensory classification system. In another example, Li et al. demonstrated a cross-modal in-sensor spiking reservoir computing (RC) system, achieving dynamic objects identification with a high accuracy of 98.1% and real-time haptic feedback64. Encoded pressure and temperature bimodal information is delivered and classified in the spiking reservoir network.

CML offers the advantage of building upon a robust foundation of well-established methodologies that have been extensively studied and optimized by the research community. These algorithms are particularly effective in multimodal systems, where they facilitate the integration and interpretation of cross-modal sensory information. However, despite the remarkable progress and widespread application of CML, their deployment on traditional computing platforms based on von Neumann architecture presents notable limitations. The inherent separation of memory and processing units in such architectures leads to frequent data movement, resulting in increased latency and energy consumption. These inefficiencies, coupled with constraints in computational throughput and hardware scalability, pose significant challenges for real-time, low-power processing in edge computing environments. Consequently, to address these inefficiencies, there is a growing need for alternative computing paradigms that can overcome these bottlenecks and support the efficient implementation of intelligent systems at the edge.

Spiking neuron networks

While CML algorithms offer a mature and well-understood framework for spike signal decoding, they typically rely on extensive preprocessing and feature engineering, and are inherently limited by the von Neumann architecture’s inefficiencies76. In contrast, SNNs, widely regarded as the third generation of neural networks, represent a biologically plausible and computationally efficient paradigm that directly operates on temporally coded spike signals, thereby enabling real-time, low-power decoding with minimal processing. There are two primary directions in the development of SNN-based decoding systems: software implementation and hardware design.

On the software side, a variety of spike-decoding algorithms have been developed using time-domain encoding schemes and spiking neuron models, enabling SNNs to perform high-accuracy decoding in both unimodal and multimodal tactile tasks. For instance, Huang et al. proposed a tactile regression spiking neural network for liquid volume estimation, utilizing a binary decoding method to convert output spike trains into volume values92. The model achieved state-of-the-art performance with a coefficient of determination up to 0.986. Similarly, Park et al. presented a noise-robust deep SNN with temporal information, using weight scaling and time-to-average-spike coding and achieving both efficiency and robustness93. These studies highlight the growing maturity of SNN software frameworks like SpikingJelly94, which facilitate flexible spike processing and training.

Software-based SNNs are frequently employed in hybrid neuromorphic architectures, where low-level spike encoding is delegated to dedicated hardware while high-level inference is executed in software. Yuan et al. developed a calibratable artificial sensory neuron that combines an epitaxial VO₂ memristor, grown by pulsed-laser deposition, with a suite of coupled sensors95. The device converted diverse analog stimuli into electrical spike trains that are subsequently processed by software SNN algorithms for downstream perception tasks. In a similar vein, Lee et al. realized a self-powered artificial mechanoreceptor array, as illustrated in Fig. 7d, in which each cell integrates a triboelectric touch sensor with a bistable resistor (biristor) to generate event-driven spikes68. And then ten touch gestures could be accurately classified based on the data generated by the proposed mechanoreceptor array with the aid of SNN simulations.

Beyond software simulations, substantial efforts have been directed toward developing neuromorphic hardware platforms that natively support spiking computation—a direction that has garnered significant attention in neuromorphic engineering. Unlike conventional ANNs, which typically rely on CPUs and GPUs for inference, SNNs are inherently compatible with hardware architectures optimized for asynchronous and event-driven computation. Representative platforms such as IBM’s TrueNorth96, Intel’s Loihi97, and the EU-funded SpiNNaker system98 have demonstrated the ability to support large-scale spiking neuron models and have been successfully applied to a range of sensory decoding tasks99–101. In parallel, emerging device technologies—including memristors and synaptic transistors—have been explored as hardware substrates for mimicking the biophysical behavior of neurons and synapses. Despite promising demonstrations of individual functionalities, current progress in these material systems remains largely confined to the emulation of isolated neuronal or synaptic operations102.

Although current neuromorphic chips have made progress in implementing spiking neuron models, most hardware realizations remain limited to local components such as neuron circuits and synaptic arrays2. These implementations often still rely on conventional computing platforms (e.g., CPUs or GPUs) for global control, parameter updates, and training. As a result, such SNN systems are typically categorized as “semi-hardware” or hardware-accelerated solutions, rather than fully autonomous, end-to-end hardware realizations103. Most existing systems integrate software-based SNN simulation frameworks with partial hardware modules17, which, despite offering improvements in energy efficiency and real-time processing, face significant challenges in scalability and complete detachment from the von Neumann architecture.

The overarching research trajectory aims to realize fully hardware-implemented SNNs capable of end-to-end spike-domain processing—spanning sensory signal transduction, neuromorphic encoding, and event-driven decision-making—within integrated neuromorphic architectures. Achieving such systems necessitates co-optimization of ultra-low-power analog/mixed-signal circuits, bioinspired spike encoding algorithms, and scalable neurosynaptic arrays, which would not only transcend von Neumann bottlenecks but also advance foundational understanding of energy-efficient temporal computation in biological and artificial neural systems.

Summary and perspectives

This review has provided a comprehensive examination of spike-based neural coding techniques for tactile perception, with emphasis on encoding strategies and their hardware implementations, as well as decoding methodologies. Spike encoding serves as the key interface between analog tactile signals and event-driven neuromorphic processing. Existing strategies, such as rate coding, temporal coding, and population coding, reflect different levels of biological plausibility and computational richness. Rate coding, the most widely implemented, offers simplicity and robustness, while temporal and population schemes capture fine temporal dynamics and ensemble-level integration crucial for representing complex tactile events. At the hardware level, implementations of spike encoding have primarily followed two approaches. Oscillator-based circuits translate analog inputs into frequency-modulated spiking outputs with relatively simple architectures, offering compactness and ease of integration. In contrast, CMOS- or memristor-based spiking neurons emulate neuronal dynamics more directly, providing richer functionality and closer alignment with biological processes. These hardware realizations demonstrate the feasibility of converting continuous tactile signals into discrete spike trains, each with distinct advantages in efficiency and scalability.

After tactile signals are transformed into spike trains, decoding becomes essential to extract meaningful features such as texture, roughness, or object identity. Two main approaches are evident. At the fundamental level, decoding leverages synaptic plasticity mechanisms emulated by memristive devices and synaptic transistors. These hardware substrates reproduce biologically inspired learning rules, enabling local feature extraction, adaptive responses, and energy-efficient in-sensor processing. At the advanced level, decoding relies on machine learning algorithms. Conventional algorithms make use of engineered spike features, while SNNs directly exploit temporal spike patterns. Together, these approaches demonstrate the potential for hierarchical processing that mirrors biological tactile pathways.

Substantial progress has been made in emulating biological tactile processing through neuromorphic encoding schemes and synaptic learning mechanisms. Yet, current systems still face major challenges in scalability, integration, and computational efficiency, reflecting the gap between biomimetic spike representations and practical hardware platforms. Hardware Implementations for Hybrid Encoding Schemes

Although spike encoding strategies such as rate coding, TTFS coding, ISI coding, and phase coding have been extensively explored at the algorithmic level104, their hardware implementation remains limited and fragmented. Most existing circuits-such as oscillators and spiking neuron modules-primarily support rate coding schemes105, with only a few studies addressing the hardware realization of TTFS coding12. This narrow focus restricts the versatility of tactile neural systems when dealing with diverse and dynamic sensory inputs. Future research should prioritize the development of configurable or hybrid hardware architectures capable of supporting multiple encoding schemes within a single platform. In addition, implementing adaptive encoding mechanisms that dynamically switch between coding strategies based on the characteristics of the input stimulus may improve information fidelity, responsiveness, and energy efficiency in real-world applications.

Device stability for emerging materials in large-scale networks

Emerging devices such as memristors and synaptic transistors are promising candidates for neuromorphic tactile systems due to their ability to emulate neuronal and synaptic behaviors with low power consumption and high integration density106. However, their deployment toward large-scale networks is critically limited by reliability concerns, including device-to-device variability, limited endurance, unstable switching behavior, and environmental sensitivity2. These issues not only hinder current applications but also raise fundamental challenges for constructing robust and scalable neuromorphic architectures. To overcome these limitations, future research should prioritize the development of new material systems with enhanced reliability and robust fabrication processes to ensure uniformity across large device arrays107. Improvements in retention characteristics, switching reproducibility, and thermal stability will be indispensable for translating device-level advances into dependable large-scale neuromorphic networks.

Toward integrated neuromorphic hardware systems

Current neuromorphic tactile systems generally implement spike encoding and decoding as separate modules, often realized through independent circuits or device arrays that are later interconnected. While such modular approaches allow proof-of-concept demonstrations, they lack the architectural cohesion necessary for constructing fully integrated neuromorphic systems74. In these fragmented designs, sensing, encoding, and computation remain physically and functionally detached108, which limits system efficiency and prevents end-to-end spike-domain processing. This separation further introduces interconnect overhead, synchronization difficulties, and additional power costs, ultimately constraining the scalability and practicality of spike-based tactile intelligence109. To advance beyond these limitations, future research should focus on achieving higher levels of integration, where sensory interfaces, spike encoders, and decoding processors are co-designed and co-fabricated within unified hardware platforms. Such monolithic integration, supported by stable and scalable device technologies, will be essential for realizing real-time, energy-efficient, and high-density neuromorphic tactile systems suitable for autonomous robotics and wearable applications.
