Black-Box Differentially Private Nonparametric Confidence Intervals Under Minimal Assumptions
Tomer Shoham, Moshe Shenfeld, Noa Velner-Harris, Katrina Ligett

Abstract
We introduce a simple, general framework that takes any differentially private estimator of any arbitrary quantity as a black box, and from it constructs a differentially private nonparametric confidence interval of that quantity. Our approach repeatedly subsamples the data, applies the private estimator to each subsample, and then post-processes the resulting empirical CDF to a confidence interval. Our analysis uses the randomness from the subsampling to achieve privacy amplification. Under mild assumptions, the empirical CDF we obtain approaches the CDF of the private statistic as the sample size grows. We use this to show that the confidence intervals we estimate are asymptotically valid, tight, and equivalent to their non-private counterparts. We provide empirical evidence that our method performs well compared with the (less-general) state-of-the-art algorithms.

Introduction and related work

Modern statistics starts with a simple ambition: learn something general about the world from finite data drawn from some underlying data-generating process or distribution. The first goal is to compress the sample into one or more statistics: functions of the data designed to illuminate population features such as a mean effect, a regression coefficient, a quantile, or a risk ratio.
We expect the variability of the statistic to decrease as the sample size increases, and to converge to some true value. But point estimates alone are not enough. Scientific claims require an assessment of uncertainty: how much would our statistic vary if we were to repeat the data collection under the same conditions?

Confidence intervals
Statisticians address this uncertainty by producing a
confidence set
: a data-dependent subset of the output space of the statistic that aims to contain the true value with a prespecified probability (the
confidence level
, typically 90–99%), over repeated sampling from the same data-generating process. The complement is the significance level
\alpha
: the tolerated probability that the returned set misses the truth. When the target is one-dimensional, the confidence set is usually an interval (a
confidence interval (CI)
). Note that since the dataset is random, the output set is random as well. We refer to the probability that it contains the true value as its
coverage
, and consider its accuracy to be the gap between the coverage and the desired confidence level.

There are two general routes to building such intervals. The first is parametric, relying on an analytically characterized (or asymptotically approximated) sampling distribution for the statistic. For example, a mean statistic, multiplied by the square root of the sample size, is approximately normally distributed, with the variance being the variance of the data distribution. Thus, constructing an interval centered at the mean of the sample, with two standard deviations below and above it, gives a
\approx 95\%
confidence interval for the mean. Unfortunately, this approach is limited to special cases where the variance can be computed directly from the data.

The second route is nonparametric and resampling-based: the idea is to approximate the unknown sampling distribution of a statistic by recombining the observed data elements themselves. The most common variant of this approach is called
bootstrapping
, initially introduced by
Efron, 1992
. Given a dataset, the bootstrap method draws multiple samples from it, each of the original sample size, by sampling with replacement, and recomputes the statistic on each.
Intuitively, these procedures attempt to mimic the variability one would see if one could resample the initial dataset. Although resamples are not independent new datasets, surprisingly, under mild conditions, their empirical distribution consistently tracks the true sampling one. These methods offer accuracy guarantees comparable (and in some cases, superior) to normal approximation and similar techniques.

The list of scalar estimations produced by this method is effectively an empirical distribution of this statistic. If this empirical distribution approximates the underlying distribution of the statistic (i.e., the distribution over fresh samples), then taking its
\alpha/2
and
1-\alpha/2
empirical quantiles produces an (approximate)
\alpha
-confidence interval.
If one further assumes that the underlying distribution of the statistic is (approximately) normal, one can additionally estimate its standard deviation from the empirical one, and construct CIs as discussed in the parametric setting. This empirical distribution enables more than constructing CIs; It can also be used to test hypotheses, validate assumptions about the statistic, and more.

Resampling and taking the quantiles is attractive because it provides an extremely general technique for constructing CIs for any function of interest on the data—regression parameters, moments, quantiles, covariances, etc.—and the resulting approximation is good under extremely minimal assumptions. Empirically, this approach often performs well even at small sample sizes (as we show in Appendix
C
).

In recent years, a technique known as Bag-of-Little-Bootstrap (

Our approach
Our work examines the potential of another, less-popular method of resampling, known as subsampling
[
26
]

Given a dataset of size

Then, it samples

m

Centering around the full dataset statistic and rescaling by a normalizing factor produces an estimation of the CDF of the statistic on
n

The CI can be constructed by taking the
\alpha/2

Preliminaries and notation

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
be a probability space and
P^{(n)}
be the product distribution. We use

\boldsymbol{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n})\sim P^{(n)}

We aim to infer a distributional functional such as a mean, quantile, or correlation. To estimate this quantity, we define a function,

\theta^{*}=\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}\underset{\boldsymbol{\omega}\sim P^{(n)}}{\mathbb{E}}\theta(\boldsymbol{\omega})

Given some constant

U_{n,\tau}(x)=\underset{\boldsymbol{\omega}\sim P^{(n)}}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau\cdot\bigl(\theta(\boldsymbol{\omega})-\theta^{*}\bigr)\leq x\,\right).

(1)

It is common practice to assume that there exists a non-decreasing sequence

and a distribution

, such that

For random variables

2.2
Differential privacy

We give a very brief introduction to differential privacy here. A more detailed description can be found in Appendix
A
.

Given some domain
\Omega
and a sample size
n\in N
, we call two datasets
\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}\in\Omega^{n}
neighbors, denoted by
\boldsymbol{\omega}\sim\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}
, if they are identical except for one of their elements.

Definition 2.4

(Differential privacy)
.

Given
\varepsilon\geq 0
,
\delta\in[0,1]
, a data domain
\Omega
and some domain of responses
\mathcal{R}
, we say that a mechanism
{\cal M}:\Omega^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}
satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy, denoted by
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP, if

\mathbb{P}({\cal M}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\in E)\leq e^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}({\cal M}(\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime})\in E)+\delta

for all
\boldsymbol{\omega}\sim\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\prime}\in\Omega^{n}
and all
E\subseteq\mathcal{R}
.
When
\delta=0
we say
\cal M
is
pure
DP, and if
\delta>0

\cal M
is said to be
approximate
DP.

Differential privacy enjoys several useful properties. First it holds under post-processing; that is, if an algorithm is differentially private, then any followup analysis of the algorithm’s output without additional access to the dataset cannot degrade the privacy guarantee (Proposition 2.1 in
[
15
]
). DP also composes well; that is, if we consecutively apply multiple differentially private mechanisms to the same dataset, the overall privacy loss can be bounded with linear and sub linear increase in privacy parameters for pure and approximate DP respectively (Lemmas
A.2
,
A.3
). A third useful fact is that privacy is amplified by subsampling; that is, if a differentially private mechanism is only applied to a random subset of the dataset, the privacy guarantees of the mechanism are amplified by approximately the chance of each element to appear in the subset (Lemma
A.4
)

Privately estimating the mean and other moments can be done using noise addition mechanisms such as Laplace and Gaussian (Definitions
A.6
,
A.8
), if the sensitivity of the statistic with respect to a change of a single element can be bounded. The median and other quantiles can be privately estimated using the inverse sensitivity mechanism (
A.12
), an instantiation of the general exponential mechanism (
A.10
) which can be used to estimate any quantity if its utility’s sensitivity is bounded.

5
Discussion

In this work we give a general method for constructing differentially-private non-parametric confidence intervals for arbitrary quantities and prove its asymptotic validity and tightness under minimal assumptions (e.g., without normal approximation). Furthermore, beyond CIs, our algorithm produces a consistent CDF of the private statistic, which can be used to simultaneously estimate CIs for several confidence levels, test hypotheses, and validate assumptions. Our approach leverages the inherent randomness in the resampling procedure to mitigate the increase in privacy loss resulting from the composition over multiple computations. We back our theoretical guarantees with empirical experiments, showing our method is competitive with the state-of-the-art. A detailed discussion of these issues and additional ones can be found in Appendix
C
.

Limitations
. Two possible limitations of our method, as we have already mentioned, are that it requires knowledge of the convergence rate
\tau_{n}
, and the existence of a privacy-preserving mechanism for the quantity of interest that induces additional error that is asymptotically negligible relative to the sampling noise. The
BLB
framework in general does not require this, because the splits are bootstrapped to the original size,
n
, and privacy is induced only in the aggregation phase.

Normal approximation
. Assuming the limiting distribution is normal, as is done by
Chadha et al., 2024
and
[
9
]
, CIs can also be constructed by estimating the variance of the statistic and using a normal-based CI. This method is asymptotically valid under an additional assumption of uniform integrability, which–intuitively speaking–prevents the distribution from having too much mass in the tails; in particular, bounded
(1+\gamma)
-moments for some
\gamma>0
are sufficient.

Data-splitting
. Another overlooked alternative method for non-parametric CIs is data splitting. Rather than subsampling or bootstrapping, we can split the data into equal-sized disjoint subsets and compute the estimator privately on each with the total privacy budget, since each individual appears in exactly one subset. Like subsampling, this method requires knowledge of the convergence rate to rescale the interval. In general, sample splitting is asymptotically suboptimal, but proves quite competitive for reasonable sample sizes. The advantage of this method is that the privacy budget is not affected by the number of splits, and the subsets are uncorrelated (unlike with subsampling), thus the statistical analysis is straightforward. See Appendix
C
for detailed discussion.

Rates and hyper-parameters
. Our accuracy guarantees are asymptotic, and do not provide formal rates (e.g., the coverage is
1-\alpha+f(n)
for some function
f
), since giving rates is impossible under such minimal assumptions, even in the non-private case. As a result, we cannot compute optimal values of
m
and
T
as a function of the sample size and the privacy parameters. The choice of
m
and
T
must depend on the optimized quantity (e.g., coverage accuracy, expected width, etc.), the additional distributional assumptions, and the properties of the estimated quantity; an extensive numerical study of this sort is an interesting direction for future work. We note that setting
T\cdot m=\omega(n)
is not a reasonable choice, since in this case some of the elements are completely discarded, so with these parameters, one might as well split the dataset into
T
disjoint subsets of size
n/T
rather than subsample, as discussed in Appendix
C
.

Complementary claims and missing proofs

B.1
Consistency of private mechanisms

The requirements of
\tau_{n}
consistency require that, with respect to the underlying distribution, the difference between the private and non-private estimators, rescaled by
\tau_{n}
, goes to zero in probability. In this section, we prove something stronger since the bounds on the privacy-preserving mechanisms hold uniformly over datasets; the same inequality holds for any underlying distribution.

Claim B.1

(Noise addition vanishes at rate
\tau_{n}
)
.

Define
{\cal M}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\theta(\boldsymbol{\omega})+N_{n}
, where
N_{n}
is independent of the data. If for any
\eta>0
,

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{n}\cdot|N_{n}|>\eta)\rightarrow 0
,
then
\tau_{n}({\cal M}(\boldsymbol{\omega})-\theta(\boldsymbol{\omega}))\xrightarrow{p}0
.

Proof.

For any fixed
\eta>0
,

\mathbb{P}\big(\tau_{n}\cdot|{\cal M}(\boldsymbol{\omega})-\theta(\boldsymbol{\omega})|>\eta\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(|\tau_{n}\cdot N_{n}|>\eta\big)\xrightarrow{p}0,

so
{\cal M}
is
\tau_{n}
-consistent.
∎

The condition of Claim
B.1
holds for the standard DP noise additions:

Laplace.
If
N_{n}\sim\mathrm{Lap}(0,b_{n})
, then for any
\eta>0
,

\mathbb{P}\big(|\tau_{n}N_{n}|>\eta\big)=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\eta}{\tau_{n}b_{n}}\right),

so the claim applies whenever
\tau_{n}\cdot b_{n}\to 0
.

Gaussian.
If
N_{n}\sim N(0,\sigma_{n}^{2})
, then for any
\eta>0
,

\mathbb{P}\big(|\tau_{n}N_{n}|>\eta\big)\leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{\eta^{2}}{2\tau_{n}^{2}\sigma_{n}^{2}}\right),

hence the claim applies whenever
\tau_{n}\cdot\sigma_{n}\to 0
.

For a concrete example, consider mean queries, where the data is bounded, w.l.o.g in [0,1]. The mean is normally distributed at rate
\tau_{n}=\sqrt{n}
from the central limit theorem. For the Laplace noise addition, if we set
b_{n}=\frac{1}{n\varepsilon}
, the mechanism is
\varepsilon
-DP, and as long as
\sqrt{n}\cdot\varepsilon\rightarrow\infty
, it is
\tau_{n}
-consistent. A similar example can be derived for Gaussian noise addition.

In this Appendix, we present additional analyses and provide further details of the experiments described in Section

Code and reproducibility.

Our implementations of the alternative methods are based on the original works: the exponential mechanism (
ExpMech
) from
Ira Globus-Harris’s
Git repository and
BLBquant
from
knchadha

C.1
Full description of all methods

We described in length our method throughout the paper. We now describe the other algorithms we compare to, and the choices of hyperparameters.

Algorithm
ExpMech

The algorithm is described in
[
11
]
. The key idea is to use the statistical property of order statistics in combination with the exponential mechanism. At the core of the approach lies the observation that the median splits the distribution into two equal halves: the probability of any data point being greater than or less than the median is exactly
0.5
. This property allows the construction of nonparametric confidence intervals by looking at the position (rank) of observations in the sorted data, rather than making assumptions about the underlying distribution. To privatize this process, one can use the exponential mechanism, where the "utility" corresponds to how well a chosen rank represents the desired quantile.

Drechsler and coauthors use a widened version of the exponential mechanism, which balances the tradeoff between errors in the rank domain and errors in the value domain. This is controlled by a parameter called
granularity
, which determines how ranks are translated into intervals. Smaller values emphasize rank accuracy, while larger values favor stability in the value domain. Since overly large granularity may dominate the resulting interval width, in our implementation, we scale it with the sample size, setting it to
0.1/\sqrt{n}
, so that the intervals continue to shrink as n grows.

Algorithm
BLBquant

The algorithm is described in
[
7
]
. The algorithm, similar to
PrivSub
, first estimates the quantity of interest on the full sample in a differentially private way. For example, for median estimation, it uses the inverse sensitivity mechanism. The privacy budget is divided equally between estimating the distribution (
\varepsilon_{\text{CDF}}
) and the variability (
\varepsilon_{\text{VAR}}
).

The dataset is then partitioned into
s
subsets. For each set, bootstrapping is used to generate
T(n)=\max\{\min\{n^{1.5}/\log n,1000\},100\}
samples of size
n
, and generate a non-private estimate for each bootstrap sample. Each estimate is centered by the full-sample estimator, and rescaled by
\sqrt{n}
(they assume normality, so this is the convergence rate). This results in
s
vectors of
T
non-private centered and scaled estimates.
For the aggregation step, a variation of the aboveThreshold is used (See Algorithm 1 in
[
7
]
). A sequence of intervals decreasing at rate
\sqrt{n}
centered at zero is fixed in advance. For each vector of non-private estimates, the number of values contained within each interval is recorded. Consequently, for each interval, a list of coverages across the splits is obtained. Using their variant of the
AboveThreshold
algorithm, the procedure stops once the median coverage falls below the confidence level, and the previous interval is returned. Intuitively, this yields a private and consistent estimate of the estimator’s variability; confidence intervals then follow by rescaling and centering at the full-sample estimator. Privacy is guaranteed by composing the budget allocated to the full-sample estimator with that of the aggregation step.

Algorithm
SampleSplitting

We also consider sample splitting, an often overlooked competitor to nonparametric confidence intervals, which we refer to as
SampleSplitting
. f the dataset is sufficiently large, instead of subsampling or bootstrapping, it can be divided into nearly equal-sized parts, and the estimator can be computed privately on each split. The full privacy budget is available for each split, since each individual appears in exactly one split. As with subsampling, knowledge of the convergence rate is needed to extrapolate from the smaller sample (the split) to the full data. In general, sample splitting is known to be asymptotically suboptimal, but it can sometimes perform well in finite samples.
The main advantage of this method is that the privacy budget is unaffected by the number of splits, and the splits remain uncorrelated, in contrast to subsampling.

As in
PrivSub
, the privacy budget is divided into two components: A portion
\varepsilon_{\text{center}}
is used to compute a private center estimate from the full dataset, and the remainder
\varepsilon_{\text{var}}
is allocated to the local estimates computed on each of the
T
splits.

Non-private bootstrap

There are many variations of the bootstrap, such as the smoothed bootstrap. In this work, the following version is employed: given a sample of size
n
,
n
observations are drawn with replacement from the original dataset, and this procedure is repeated
T(n)=\max\{\min\{5\sqrt{n},500\},200\}
times. For each bootstrap sample, the center is estimated, and the statistic of interest is computed. A confidence interval is then obtained from the empirical distribution of these estimates.

C.2
A discussion about the limitations and advantages of different methods

Selecting a private inference procedure is delicate because performance depends jointly on many factors, such as the regularity of the target functional and data distribution, the availability of accurate privacy mechanisms, how privacy composition interacts with subsampling or resampling, and more. We outline guiding questions and some discussion.

First, we need to ask: is there a privacy mechanism that accurately privatizes the statistic of interest on the full sample?
If the answer is no, i.e., there is no mechanism that satisfy
\tau_{n}
-consistency (e.g., ratios and other sensitive statistics), then BLB should be considered for its use of the subsample-and-aggregate technique. The algorithm
BLBquant
also assumes a variation of
\tau_{n}
-consistency, but the BLB framework can be modified to be more general.

Is the underlying distribution "well-behaved" at the scale of each split?
The accuracy of BLB and
SampleSplitting
relies on each split statistically representing the population so that the split-level statistic has (approximately) the same law as the full-sample statistic. This tends to hold for unimodal, light-tailed, continuous distributions, but
can fail
with heavy tails, mixture structure, or discontinuities, as shown in Figures
1
and
6
. Some splits may land in different mixture components or be dominated by tail observations, biasing the aggregate. While the choice
s=O\left(\log(n)/\varepsilon\right)
in
BLBquant
implies that these effects are asymptotically negligible, their effect is significant at a reasonable sample size.
Private subsampling CDF tolerates more heterogeneity: it only requires the classical conditions (
m\to\infty
,
m/n\to 0
) and a weak limit for the root; individual splits need not be "representative" in the BLB sense as long as the
empirical
subsampling CDF converges.

Must the method "know" the convergence rate
\tau_{n}
?
Our approach (and
SampleSplitting
with extrapolation) rescales quantiles from the
m
-scale to the
n
-scale, which
uses
the ratio
\tau_{m}/\tau_{n}
(e.g.,
\sqrt{m/n}
). BLB methods do not assume rate knowledge; they use resampling to size
n
, but at a cost: its statistical validity depends on the bootstrap approximation at the privatized-split level and on the aggregation rule.

Does one need
the whole distribution
(not just a single CI)?
Our method (
PrivSub
) and
SampleSplitting
returns an estimate of the
entire
CDF of the estimator (at the
n
-scale) as a post-processing artifact. This enables symmetry checks, tail-shape assessment, and simultaneous inference across many
\alpha
without additional privacy cost. One-shot mechanisms such as
ExpMech
and
BLBquant
typically target a single pivot.

C.3
Rates, hyper-parameters and privacy budget

Our accuracy guarantees are asymptotic, and do not provide formal rates (e.g., the coverage is
1-\alpha+f(n)
for some function
f
), since giving rates is impossible under such minimal assumptions, even in the non-private case. As a result, we cannot compute optimal values of
m
and
T
as a function of the sample size and the privacy parameters. The choice of
m
and
T
must depend on the optimized quantity (e.g., coverage accuracy, expected width, etc.), the additional distributional assumptions, and the properties of the estimated quantity.

Here, we set some underlying rules on how to choose the hyperparameters. First, subsampling requires that
m=o(n)
under minimal assumptions. In fact, in some cases, such as linear statistics, we can choose
m=O(n)

[
26
]
. A rule of thumb is to consider
m=\omega({n})
. In classic subsampling,
m
is smaller than
n
in order to decrease correlation between subsamples. In our case, a smaller
m
also balances the privacy budget (amplification by subsampling), so it has a second role. As
n\rightarrow\infty
, and the perturbation becomes negligible empirically, we can take larger
m
(approaching the non-private optimal).
Throughout our experiments, we fixed
m=n^{2/3}
, a number that was derived for non-private subsampling under further assumptions.

Another parameter is the number of subsamples,
T
. Usually, in the non-private literature,
T
is taken to be “large enough” such that the error induced from sampling
T
instead of
{n\choose m}
subsamples is negligible, and in fact, it is treated as zero when analyzing resampling methods. In our case,
T
plays a crucial role, since we pay a factor of
\sqrt{T}
or
T
(advanced or basic composition) in the privacy budget. Since we use the quantiles method,
T
has to be large enough such that we can take the upper and lower
\alpha/2
quantile of the empirical distribution. On the other hand, looking at the width of the confidence intervals, once
T
is not too small, increasing it further does not decrease the expected width, but its variability. On the contrary, it only increases the perturbation. Throughout our experiments, we fixed
T=50
, such that
T\cdot m>n
for simplicity, and did not increase it as a function of
n
.

Generally, we have that setting
T\cdot m=\omega(n)
is not a reasonable choice, since in this case some of the elements are completely discarded, so with these parameters, one might as well split the dataset into
T
disjoint subsets of size
n/T
. On the other hand, setting
T\cdot m=O(n^{2})
is also not a reasonable choice, since the perturbation would be too large for most reasonable mechanisms (i.e., noise addition).

Another hyperparameter that was not mentioned in the paper is how to split the privacy budget in
PrivSub
between the full-sample estimator and the subsets. In general, this could offer a remedy for the subsets overestimating the variability of the full-sample estimate by simply providing more budget to the splits. Since we did not derive a formal theory about how to split the budget, we only considered the default half-split. We found in different experiments that it could give much improved results, but again, we couldn’t justify our choice over different mechanisms, so we did not include it in this manuscript.

Note that throughout our empirical evaluation, we only considered pure-DP. This has two reasons: first, both
BLBquant
and
ExpMech
are pure-DP, and we wanted the comparison to be as accurate as possible. Second, advanced composition’s guarantees are asymptotic. When we chose
\delta=n^{-8}
, in the sample-size regime we chose, advanced composition gave worse
\varepsilon
than basic composition. For larger sample sizes and
T
, we expect advanced composition to outperform, but for small
n
, we only considered a relatively small
T
, and the asymptotics of basic composition did not kick in yet.

Many competing privacy-preserving methods are known to underperform for finite sample sizes, where the asymptotics kicks in only at very large sample sizes, which are irrelevant for any practical use. To our surprise, sample splitting did very well in the sample size regime we considered.

SampleSplitting
has two clear disadvantages relative to subsampling. The first, similarly to
BLBquant
, if the distribution is not "well-behaved", then splitting might create subsamples that do not reflect the underlying distribution. We show such an example in Figure
6
, where
PrivSub
has fine coverage, Non-private subsampling has some under-coverage, but
SampleSplitting
, both private and non-private, has serious under-coverage.

Another disadvantage we show in Figure
7
is that for any finite
n
, we can always find a set of hyperparameters
m,T
such that
PrivSub
is better than
SampleSplitting
. Intuitively, this happens because we can choose
m\cdot T=O(n)
, such that
m\cdot T>n
by a little, such that, after amplification by subsampling and composition, the perturbation of each subset is a bit smaller, and the subset size is a bit larger than sample splitting.

C.8
Comparison of CDF estimation

Our method of proof of the validity goes through proving point-wise (or uniform) convergence of the distribution (Theorem
3.3
). In this subsection, we show the convergence of the CDF as a function of the sample size. Figure
10
provides empirical verification of this convergence for three representative distributions under
\varepsilon=2
. Different from Figure
2
, here we chose a smaller privacy budget, so we get good convergence of the CDF for larger sample sizes.

The figure displays the estimated CDF from
PrivSub
(solid blur line) alongside the non-private empirical CDF (dashed orange line) and the theoretical CDF (dashed black line) for sample sizes n =
5000,25000,50000
.

Several patterns emerge: First, as sample size increases, both the private and non-private CDFs converge toward the theoretical CDF, with the gap narrowing systematically. But, for
n=5,000
, we can clearly see the distribution estimated by
PrivSub
is less concentrated. This is due to two effects- the first is that the private estimator has a larger variance than the non-private estimator. But mainly this is due to the effect we described earlier of over-estimation of the variability from the subsamples.
