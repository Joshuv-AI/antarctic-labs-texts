Robotic Partial Nephrectomy for Isthmic RCC in a Horseshoe Kidney With Aberrant Aortic Arterial Supply
Authors: Ameer Zurob, George Kallingal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report robotic‐assisted partial nephrectomy for isthmic clear cell renal cell carcinoma in a horseshoe kidney with aberrant aortic arterial supply. Intraoperative management utilized indocyanine green–guided near‐infrared fluorescence imaging, intraoperative ultrasonography, and ligation of aberrant arterial branches supplying the mass. Pathology demonstrated a 4.6‐cm WHO/ISUP grade 3 clear cell RCC, pathologic stage pT1b with negative surgical margins. Postoperative recovery was uncomplicated with preserved renal function and no recurrence at 6 months. This case highlights the importance of detailed anatomic delineation during nephron‐sparing surgery in complex horseshoe kidney anatomy.

1. Introduction

Horseshoe kidney (HSK) is the most common congenital renal fusion anomaly, with an estimated incidence of approximately 0.2% in the general population and a 2:1 male predominance [1]. Although renal function is often preserved, HSK is associated with increased rates of ureteropelvic junction obstruction, nephrolithiasis, urinary stasis, and recurrent infection [1, 2]. Chronic inflammation related to these abnormalities has been proposed to contribute to an increased incidence of renal malignancy in patients with HSK [3].

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is the most frequently reported malignancy associated with HSK and accounts for approximately 45% of tumors identified in these patients [3]. Tumors involving the renal isthmus are particularly challenging due to their proximity to midline structures, aberrant arterial supply, and extension across renal moieties. Historically, many of these lesions were managed through open surgical approaches to facilitate vascular control and anatomic visualization. However, advances in robotic surgery, fluorescence imaging, and intraoperative ultrasonography have increasingly expanded the feasibility of minimally invasive nephron‐sparing surgery in carefully selected patients [4–6].

We present a case of robotic‐assisted partial nephrectomy for isthmic clear cell RCC in a HSK with bilateral lower pole extension, aberrant arterial supply arising directly from the abdominal aorta, and fluorescence‐guided ureteral identification.

2. Case Presentation

A 74‐year‐old male was referred to the urology department after abdominal and pelvic computed tomography (CT) redemonstrated a 4.4 cm enhancing isthmic renal mass in a HSK with extension into both lower poles (Figure 1). The lesion was initially identified incidentally on prior imaging obtained during evaluation of postoperative abdominal symptoms following umbilical and epigastric hernia repairs. The patient denied flank pain, gross hematuria, dysuria, or constitutional symptoms. He reported chronic lower urinary tract symptoms including urinary urgency and frequency attributed to benign prostatic hyperplasia.

Coronal contrast‐enhanced CT demonstrating an isthmic renal mass with extension into the bilateral lower poles of the horseshoe kidney.

Preoperative imaging demonstrated extension of the lesion toward the lower poles of both renal moieties. Formal nephrometry scoring, including RENAL and PADUA classification, was not utilized because the anomalous HSK anatomy, aberrant renal pelvic anatomy, isthmic tumor location, and aberrant vascular supply limited the applicability of these scoring systems in this case. Given the size, location, and radiographic appearance of the lesion, there was high suspicion for RCC. After discussion of management options, the decision was made to proceed with robotic‐assisted partial nephrectomy.

3. Surgical Technique

Following induction of general anesthesia and administration of perioperative antibiotics, the patient was positioned in supine dorsal lithotomy. Initial cystoscopy demonstrated no bladder tumors, stones, or gross hematuria. Bilateral ureteral catheterization was performed using 5‐Fr open‐ended catheters with Sensor guidewire assistance. Indocyanine green (ICG) mixed with surgical lubricant was instilled into both ureters to facilitate intraoperative identification using near‐infrared fluorescence (NIRF) imaging. A Foley catheter was then placed to maintain bladder decompression throughout the remainder of the procedure.

The patient was subsequently placed in steep Trendelenburg position for the robotic portion of the procedure. A 12‐mm AirSeal access port was placed in the pelvis approximately 6 cm to the right of midline. An 8‐mm robotic camera port was placed in the midline pelvis through the urachus. Two additional 8‐mm robotic ports were placed in the left pelvis, and one additional 8‐mm robotic port was placed in the right lateral pelvis. The da Vinci Xi robotic system was docked over the head and upper chest in a configuration similar to robotic retroperitoneal lymph node dissection.

Dissection began with mobilization of the posterior peritoneum and right white line of Toldt to expose the retroperitoneum and renal isthmus. Intermittent NIRF imaging using the Firefly system facilitated continuous identification of the ureters throughout the procedure. The right ureter coursed immediately lateral to the right lower pole and remained closely associated with the isthmic renal mass. Intraoperatively, NIRF imaging identified an anomalous accessory branch of the right ureter extending toward the renal isthmus (Figure 2C). In contrast, the left ureter was visualized significantly lateral to the left lower pole and remained largely outside the operative field. This anatomy made fluorescence‐guided ureteral identification particularly useful during dissection near the isthmus and lower poles. Circumferential dissection of the isthmus identified two aberrant arterial branches arising directly from the abdominal aorta that supplied the tumor within the renal isthmus (Figure 2A). Given the aberrant segmental arterial supply to the isthmic mass and the goal of avoiding unnecessary global renal ischemia, hilar clamping was not performed. Instead, the two aberrant arterial branches supplying the tumor‐bearing isthmus were individually secured with Hem‐o‐lok clips and divided, as they supplied the tumor‐bearing isthmus rather than the remaining renal parenchyma.

Intraoperative ultrasonography was then utilized to delineate tumor margins (Figure 2B). The lesion measured approximately 5 cm intraoperatively, consistent with the preoperative CT (4.4 cm) and final pathologic measurement (4.6 cm), and extended into the lower poles of both renal moieties. Electrocautery was used to score the planned resection margins before sharp excision of the mass with grossly negative margins.

The specimen was placed in an Endo Catch retrieval bag. The collecting system was not entered during tumor excision. Frozen section biopsies from the deep base of both the right and left renal moieties were negative for malignancy. Renorrhaphy was performed using running 3‐0 V‐Loc sutures for the deep layer followed by sliding clip renorrhaphy with 2‐0 V‐Loc sutures for capsular closure bilaterally. Hemostatic powder was applied to the resection bed, and Gerota′s fascia was reapproximated. A Jackson–Pratt drain was placed before undocking the robot and closing the port sites in standard fashion.

4. Results

The procedure was completed without intraoperative complications. Total operative time was 190 min with an estimated blood loss of 150 mL.

Final pathology demonstrated a 4.6‐cm WHO/ISUP grade 3 clear cell RCC confined to the kidney, pathologic stage pT1b (Figure 3). Surgical margins were negative, and no lymphovascular invasion was identified. Frozen section biopsies obtained from both renal moieties were negative for malignancy.

Histologic features of clear cell renal cell carcinoma from the horseshoe kidney mass. (A) Low‐power view demonstrates nests of tumor cells with abundant clear cytoplasm and centrally located round nuclei surrounded by arborizing, thin‐walled “chicken‐wire” vasculature. (B) High‐power view (100x) demonstrates prominent nucleoli without pleomorphic, rhabdoid, or sarcomatoid features.

The postoperative course was uncomplicated. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 3 with preserved renal function. No postoperative urinary leak or other urinary complications occurred.

At postoperative day 15 follow‐up, the patient remained clinically stable without hematuria or infectious complications. At 3 months, serum creatinine remained stable at 0.83 mg/dL, and surveillance CT demonstrated expected postoperative changes without definitive evidence of recurrence. At 6‐month follow‐up, MRI demonstrated no residual or recurrent tumor, and the patient maintained stable renal function.

5. Discussion

Management of renal malignancy in HSK presents substantial technical challenges due to altered embryologic development and highly variable anatomy [1, 2]. During normal embryogenesis, the kidneys ascend from the pelvis while progressively receiving new arterial supply from the abdominal aorta and involuting inferior vessels. Fusion of the lower poles in HSK may arrest renal ascent, resulting in persistence of accessory and aberrant arteries arising from the aorta, iliac vessels, or inferior mesenteric artery [1]. These anatomic variations increase the technical complexity of hilar dissection, vessel identification, and nephron‐sparing surgery.

In the present case, two aberrant arterial branches arising directly from the abdominal aorta supplied the isthmic mass and required careful dissection and ligation prior to resection. The centrally located tumor also extended into the lower poles of both renal moieties, further increasing operative complexity. Historically, these anatomic challenges often favored open surgical management to facilitate vascular control and exposure. However, increasing experience with robotic surgery has expanded minimally invasive options for selected patients with HSK‐associated RCC [4–6].

Several prior reports have demonstrated the feasibility of robotic‐assisted partial nephrectomy in HSK anatomy [4–6]. However, many published cases involve lateralized tumors or less extensive vascular variation. The present case is distinguished by the combination of an isthmic tumor with bilateral lower pole extension, direct aberrant arterial supply arising from the abdominal aorta, and the adjunctive use of ICG/NIRF–guided ureteral identification and intraoperative ultrasonography to facilitate safe robotic nephron‐sparing resection. Adjunctive intraoperative imaging techniques played a significant role during this procedure. Intraoperative ultrasound facilitated accurate delineation of tumor margins, whereas NIRF imaging with ICG enabled continuous ureteral identification throughout dissection and resection. Fluorescence‐guided ureteral identification may be particularly useful in HSK anatomy given the potential for anomalous ureteral course and increased risk of iatrogenic injury during dissection near the isthmus [7]. In the present case, fluorescence guidance was especially valuable given the close relationship between the ureters, isthmus, and aberrant vasculature encountered during dissection.

Despite the complexity of the anatomy encountered, robotic nephron‐sparing surgery was completed successfully with preservation of renal function, negative surgical margins, minimal blood loss, and no postoperative complications. Although the short‐term outcomes are encouraging, longer oncologic follow‐up is necessary given the WHO/ISUP grade 3 pathology. Nevertheless, this case demonstrates that robotic nephron‐sparing surgery may be feasible in carefully selected patients with complex HSK‐associated RCC when detailed preoperative planning and adjunctive intraoperative imaging techniques are utilized.

6. Conclusion

Robotic‐assisted partial nephrectomy for RCC arising within a HSK is technically challenging due to aberrant vasculature, altered ureteral anatomy, and complex tumor location. This case demonstrates that thorough intraoperative anatomic delineation, including the use of NIRF‐guided ureteral identification and intraoperative ultrasonography, may facilitate safe nephron‐sparing management even in the setting of isthmic RCC with aberrant aortic vascular supply.
