Co-designing a physical activity intervention for low-income mothers of preschool-aged children with community leaders: a qualitative study
Authors: Deirdre Dlugonski, Abby Cecil, Elisabeth Ohrnberger, Randi Osborne, Brandi White, Rebecca Mabson, Johanna M. Hoch
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundYoung children from low-income families experience disparities in school readiness that impact short and long-term health. Family physical activity co-participation is an interactive activity that may promote positive health and academic outcomes. The purpose of this study is to describe the process and outcomes of engaging with community leaders to co-design the Families Moving Together intervention for low-income mothers and their preschool-aged children.MethodsFive action planning sessions were conducted with community leaders, experts in physical activity, early learning, or engaging low-income families (i.e., families receiving housing assistance). The PRACTical planning for Implementation and Scale-up (PRACTIS) guide provided a framework for each session to address implementation and sustainability barriers and facilitators. Qualitative data were analyzed using the Framework Analysis method with inductive and deductive coding.ResultsAction planning participants (N = 19) were aged 50 ± 13.4 years, mostly female (89%), and worked across several community organizations serving low-income mothers and children in an urban city in central Kentucky. Three core themes were identified from analyzing the transcripts. First, participants held shared values, such as respect and equity, that informed the intervention design process. Second, these core values provided a foundation for creating collaborative and sustainable solutions to achieve outcomes. Finally, participants described program design elements to remove barriers and increase facilitators. Through the action planning process, participants co-designed the core components of Families Moving Together, which included leading free family movement programs in the community and engaging trusted community members to deliver the intervention. The goal of this 6-month community-level intervention is to increase school readiness and health by engaging families in physical activity together.ConclusionsCommunity experts and the research team used the PRACTIS guide to co-design strategies to increase physical activity and school readiness that they perceived would enhance the likelihood of implementation and sustainability in the community. Participants underscored the importance of the co-design process that centers community representation and collaborative approaches to create, implement, and sustain programs in the local community. Researchers and community leaders serving low-income mothers and children in other settings may be able to replicate the action planning process to facilitate equitable health outcomes.

Background

Approximately 4 million children in the United States are living in households that receive federal rental assistance [1]. Eligible families must have an income that is less than 80% of the local median income [2]. Over 70% of these children are Black or Hispanic, nearly 75% are living in a single-parent, female-headed household, and more than three-fourths live in households with income below the federal poverty line [1]. Children in these families have lower school readiness compared to higher income children [3–5]. Low levels of school readiness are associated with an increased risk for negative academic and health outcomes in Kindergarten that continue through high school [6].

The five developmental areas of school readiness include: health and well-being, approaches to learning, language and communication development, social and emotional development, and cognitive and general knowledge [7]. In Kentucky, school readiness means that, “each child enters school ready to engage in and benefit from early learning experiences that best promote the child’s success and ability to be Ready to Grow, Ready to Learn & Ready to Succeed.” [8]. To support healthy development in each component of school readiness, parents and caregivers in Kentucky are encouraged to read, talk, play, count, move, and connect together [9]. Therefore, physical activity is an essential component of school readiness that has known relationships with motor skill, cognitive, and social emotional development [10–12]. However, parents may be less likely to recognize motor skill development through physical activity as part of readiness compared to other forms of knowledge such as, identifying letters, numbers, colors, language development, literacy, and social competence [13]. Thus, it is critical to promote physical activity for positive health and academic outcomes.

Caregivers are key role models for promoting physical activity among young children. Family physical activity co-participation, families engaging in physical activity together, may be one strategy for promoting health and school readiness. However, adult caregivers in families receiving federal rental assistance report higher rates of physical inactivity compared to the general adult population (54% versus 32%), with lower rates for women compared to men [14, 15]. Mothers and their young children spend little time engaged in physical activity together [16–18]. This lack of physical activity among female caregivers and their children reduces opportunities for active role modeling and may result in generational patterns of physical inactivity that impact immediate and long-term cognitive and health outcomes among children [19]. This presents an opportunity to use physical activity co-participation as a strategy for promoting healthy child development and parent/caregiver health.

Parent-child physical activity co-participation provides opportunities for direct physical activity role modeling, communication, and connection that may facilitate a child’s development of an active lifestyle, overall development, and school readiness. Children who engage in more physical activity with their parent(s) are more likely to be physically active [17] compared to children who engage in less physical activity with their parent(s). Parents of preschoolers have reported that being active together provides quality time to connect with other family members in addition to the physical development and health benefits [20]. Thus, physical activity co-participation may result in increased family engagement with preschool-aged children that may contribute to school readiness.

The purpose of this study was to use a community-engaged approach to co-design Families Moving Together, a community-based family physical activity co-participation intervention for low-income female caregivers and their 3- to 5-year-old children. This paper describes the community-engaged processes and outcomes of working with community leaders in the intervention co-design process.

Methods

Design and recruitment

The specific aims of this qualitative study were to: (1) Describe the action planning process that was guided by the Practical Planning for Implementation and Scale up (PRACTIS) guide [21], (2) Identify stakeholder perspectives and values that informed the co-design process, and (3) Define the outcomes of the action planning process. Community stakeholder participants were purposively recruited by word of mouth to participate in the co-design process. Co-design is an “active collaboration between stakeholders in designing solutions to a prespecified problem” [22] that is grounded in principles of equal partnership, openness, respect, and empathy [23]. The research team invited participants via email based on their expertise and involvement in organizations that were working with families in the local community. During the first action planning session, participants were asked to share the names of other community leaders who should be invited to join the action planning team. Using this snowball sampling strategy, four additional participants were added in subsequent sessions. The principal investigator or community researcher for this project contacted potential participants to determine their interest in participating. All participants reviewed and signed the informed consent document prior to participation.

Instrumentation

PRACTIS guide

The PRACTIS guide [21] is a comprehensive approach to improve the planning, implementation, dissemination, and scale-up of physical activity interventions in public health settings. This tool was selected because it emphasizes early engagement with stakeholders and the anticipation of barriers to enhance evidence translation to practice. The guide outlines four key steps: (1) characterizing the implementation setting, (2) identifying and engaging stakeholders, (3) identifying contextual barriers and facilitators, and (4) addressing these barriers through strategic planning. These steps were implemented iteratively across the five action planning sessions to improve the likelihood of implementing the intervention into real-world settings. Previous studies have cited the PRACTIS guide as an implementation framework that could structure the engagement process [24] and as a tool to offer practical direction in the planning stage for physical activity in real-world settings [25].

Demographics survey

Participants completed a brief demographic survey to report their age, race, gender, and organization. Data were collected using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) electronic data capture tools hosted at the University of Kentucky. REDCap is a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies [26].

Procedures

All study related activities were approved by a university institutional review board.

Community participants and the research team completed five, 2-hour action planning sessions, structured by the PRACTIS guide [21]. The first session was hosted in-person with a virtual option. All future sessions were hosted on Zoom based on participant preferences. The five members of the research team who facilitated the action planning sessions were all female. Three facilitators were professors with PhDs, one was a student seeking a Master of Public Health degree, and one was a community member who was a local real estate agent with a Bachelor’s degree who was serving as the Community Researcher for this project. Some of the facilitators had existing relationships with participants, but not all members of the group knew each other before this project. Two facilitators had previous experience conducting qualitative and community-engaged research. Only researchers and participants were involved in these sessions. Initially, the research team defined objectives for four sessions. In practice, five sessions were required to meet the objectives outlined at the beginning of the study. For each session, the research team presented the group with an agenda and objectives (Table 1). Many of the sessions included small group work that allowed the members to be in breakout rooms to explore the topics, discuss, and report back to the larger group. Each session was audio-recorded and transcribed for data analysis. Each member of the research team took notes during the session that were used to debrief at the next research team meeting. At the end of the action planning process, all participants who were not members of the research team received $50 per action planning session that they attended for a possible total of $250.

Data analysis

Four members of the research team were involved in conducting a content analysis to analyze and interpret the qualitative data from action planning sessions using the Framework Analysis Method [27]. The Framework Method is a nine step process that includes: transcription, familiarization, coding, developing a working analytical framework, applying the framework, charting data into a framework matrix, and then interpreting the data [27]. An initial deductive coding framework was developed with the concepts included in each of the four steps within the PRACTIS guide. Using this method, all data analysis team members read the transcripts or listened to each of the action planning sessions and then conducted deductive and inductive coding in Quirkos software for the first two sessions. The team met as a group to compare and discuss coding results, refine the coding framework, and to develop a working analytical framework. To apply the final framework, two members of the team independently coded each of the remaining action planning session transcripts. Discrepancies in coding were resolved through discussion with the whole team who then identified major themes. Finally, one member of the data analysis team charted the data into the framework matrix by theme and all team members contributed to the interpretation of data using this matrix.

Results

Participants

A total of 19 community stakeholders participated in the action planning sessions from April 2023 to June 2023. Participants ranged in age from 24 to 77 years, were primarily female (89%), and identified as Black or African American (68%) or White (32%). One participant enrolled, but did not attend any sessions. Most participants (79%) attended two or more action planning sessions and 42% attended ≥ 4 sessions. The community leaders represented a wide range of organizations with expertise engaging low-income families including non-profit leaders, early childhood education and recreation professionals, local government officials, and public housing staff and administrators. A description of the objectives, summary, and action items for each session is included in Table 1.

Themes and subthemes

The qualitative analysis of the action planning session transcripts resulted in three overarching themes: (1) approach the problem with core values of respect, access, and equity; (2) coordinate efforts to solve problems as a community; and (3) design to remove barriers and build trust. These themes explain how participant values influenced the process and outcomes of co-creating this intervention with community partners. These themes and associated subthemes are described in detail below with example quotes in Table 2 and relationships depicted in Fig. 1.

Approach the problem with core values of respect, access, and equity

Participants came to the sessions with core values that guided their interactions with the research team, other participants, the action planning process, and ultimately, the co-design of the Families Moving Together intervention. The core values of respect, access, and equity were present across the following three subthemes: (1) defining the population, (2) community representation, and (3) inclusion and equity.

Defining the Population The research team defined the target population of female caregivers and their 3- to 5-year-old children who are receiving housing assistance during the first session. Participants provided additional context about this population and asked critical questions to further identify the individuals that the team was trying to reach. For example, participants asked about the number of children who attend preschool and acknowledged that various types of caregivers (e.g., grandparents) may be involved in children’s early development.

Community Representation Participants approached the action planning process with a strong desire to include the voice of community members from the target population. Participants’ collective commitment to community representation also impacted the action planning process. As one specific example, during session 3, participants asked the research team to pause the action planning process to take time to gather input from female caregivers from the target population before moving forward with the intervention co-design process.

Inclusion and Equity Participants described strategies that advanced health equity for the target population by focusing on inclusion and equity. For example, participants thought that all caregivers (e.g., dads, grandmothers, grandfathers, mothers, aunts, uncles) should be included in the community programming related to this project. Another participant described her dissatisfaction with racial inequalities that existed within a local school district and noted that these disparities begin in Kindergarten. These core values were central to the group’s intervention plans and decisions.

Coordinate efforts to solve problem as a community

The action planning team viewed coordinated efforts as an overarching solution to increase school readiness and physical activity. These coordinated efforts were defined in the sub-subthemes of (1) principles, (2) collaboration, (3) learn from the past, (4) define stakeholders, (5) place, and (6) provisions.

Principles Participants defined underlying principles that would guide intervention strategies. These principles provided a shared vision from which participants could work to create strategies together. Examples of principles included, operating with a shared definition of physical activity, prioritizing the use of existing resources, defining school readiness holistically, and the responsibility of the whole community to prepare children for kindergarten.

Collaboration Participants frequently mentioned opportunities to bring together organizations, people, and resources to achieve the goals of the group and to support local families. In discussions about collaboration, participants often offered their own help in making connections or supporting programs which is highlighted in the example quotes (see Table 2).

Learn from the Past Several individuals in the action planning team were part of previous early learning initiatives in the community that were no longer active. These previous efforts were frequently referenced by group members to provide context about what has already been done to improve school readiness, to give examples of successful programs, to highlight possible challenges the team might experience, and to help the group learn lessons that could improve the likelihood of successfully implementing this program. For example, participants highlighted challenges they experienced with reaching families and suggested using coordinated efforts as one possible solution to challenges.

Define Stakeholders A core component of describing coordinated efforts was identifying the individuals and organizations who had the skills and resources to be able to support the community-based intervention. Some of these individuals were already participating in the sessions. Additional individuals were invited to join the action planning group after being identified as stakeholders with expertise related to the goals of this project. As one specific example of identifying existing resources, the local school district had just begun rolling out free access to an early childhood learning app that was free for all families. An action planning participant made the group aware of the app and connected the research team with the company representative to learn about the onboarding process for community organizations and families.

Place Similar to defining stakeholders, the action planning participants helped the research team to identify community places and spaces where programs could be held in the future. Some of these places were under the control of action planning participants, others were places where previous programs were hosted, and yet others were locations where participants hoped that programs could be held. Places ranged from local parks, hiking destinations, community centers, schools, churches, and fitness facilities.

Provisions There were a wide range of provisions or resources that could be used to support the intervention that already existed in the community. Some of these provisions included: guides for families to support early learning, websites about kindergarten readiness for families, a regional collaborative for school readiness, a free app for children to support school readiness, trainings for parents, a van equipped to promote movement in the community, a mobile preschool bus, and more. Participants presented these provisions as a way to coordinate efforts by using existing resources to facilitate sustainability.

Design to remove barriers and build trust

Participants described multilevel barriers to family engagement in physical activity and school readiness that could be addressed through aspects of the intervention design and implementation. At the core of these strategies was designing the intervention to enhance trust, relationship building, and sustainability of the intervention. The subthemes included: (1) multilevel barriers, (2) process (strategies for success and program design logistics), (3) relationship building and trust, and (4) sustainability.

Multilevel Barriers Participants identified barriers across individual, provider, organization, and community levels. At the individual level, community leaders described many barriers that parents experience related to physical activity and school readiness, including: lack of time, knowledge, skills, and resources.

Providers were considered individuals who had direct contact with preschool-aged children and their families such as childcare providers and staff members engaged in community school readiness and physical activity programs. At the provider level, participants primarily spoke about challenges to engaging families to promote school readiness. These included determining the best strategies for communicating with families and working to build relationships with parents to make them feel welcome and a sense of belonging.

The primary barrier at the organizational level was a lack of consistent information related to physical activity and school readiness as well as a lack of coordinated efforts to support families with young children.

Finally, participants shared community- and systems-level barriers that impacted family physical activity participation and child school readiness. These included safety concerns, inadequate public transportation systems, lack of quality childcare, and poor social determinants of health. Collectively, these multilevel barriers were identified by the group with a focus on how to design an intervention that would overcome these challenges.

Process: Strategies for Success When participants were asked to identify strategies for increasing school readiness through physical activity, they generated many ideas for programs that included family cooking classes, physical activity sessions, parent education and training, wellness parades, and field trips to expose children and their caregivers to new activities. Despite having many different program ideas, the core strategies that participants wanted to focus on developing were parent and child connection and communication to enhance holistic school readiness which includes physical activity and health. The goal of this core strategy was to empower the parent to teach their child new things or to explore new activities together to support the parent-child relationship and make changes that could be sustained at home.

Process: Program Design Logistics Beyond creating the intervention strategies, participants frequently discussed how programs should be implemented to maximize parent and family engagement. Many of the suggestions from participants were their “lessons learned” from implementing their own community programs with the target population. Some of these practical recommendations were: to keep events “short and sweet, no long workshops”, “to recruit, it’s going to need to be personal. It’s probably going to need to come from relatable people.”, allow older siblings to participate, and provide incentives and giveaways. Participants provided examples of giveaways that could be used to encourage families to participate in the intervention programs that included large items such as televisions and smaller giveaways that would support the goals of the programs such as yoga mats and other activity equipment. The overwhelming majority of these program design features were focused on how to recruit and retain families and were often grounded in participants’ own experiences trying to get families to attend previous events.

Relationship Building and Trust One subtheme underlying program development and implementation was building relationships with community members to foster trust. A trusting relationship with the community was required to successfully engage families and to achieve the intended outcomes of increasing physical activity and school readiness. Participants suggested identifying community champions as individuals who could support the research team by building relationships with participants and community members. Relationship building and developing trust were the gateway to communicating with parents which then allowed the possibility of improved outcomes.

Sustainability The research team entered the action planning process with the intent of planning for sustainability with participants. Participants shared the research team’s desire for sustainability and provided many examples of programs that were not sustained in the past. Challenges to sustainability included: lack of consistent funding, turnover among families, community organizations, and schools, and a lack of evidence for the effectiveness of previous programs. While there were no solutions to these sustainability challenges, there was a clear need for data driven decisions and centralized, collaborative efforts that could support the sustainability of future interventions.

Outcomes of Action Planning Process

The action planning process resulted in the development of the core components of Families Moving Together, a 6-month community-based intervention to increase physical activity and school readiness. To achieve these outcomes, the Families Moving Together intervention will include the following core components: (1) Delivering 6–10 h of free community-based programs that increase parent-child connection and communication opportunities through physical activity co-participation, (2) Engaging Community Champions, individuals from the community who could build trusting relationships with participants, (3) Prioritizing use of existing resources and materials, and (4) Fostering partnerships and collaborations with community organizations.

Discussion

Many preschool-aged children, especially children from low-income households, have low levels of school readiness [3–5]. Parent, family, and community engagement is essential for developing each of the core components of school readiness, including physical health and well-being. Therefore, this study aimed to co-design a community-level intervention to promote family engagement in physical activity. Results from this study highlight the need to: (1) focus on a holistic definition of school readiness that includes physical health and well-being, (2) center equity and representation to engage community partners in collaborative efforts to design and deliver community-based programs, and (3) conduct evaluations to inform future programs.

Participants in this study viewed school readiness as a holistic concept that included child health and well-being as a core component. However, participants acknowledged that parents are not always aware of what it means for their child to be prepared to enter Kindergarten with the skills necessary for success. While there are widely accepted core components of school readiness that were developed by the National Education Goals Panel [7], only 35% of states have adopted definitions of school readiness that vary by state [28], and there are no standardized set of strategies for how to develop each component of school readiness. School readiness is a complex concept and the challenges with operationalizing school readiness may be contributing to a lack of parent awareness about what it means to be school or kindergarten “ready”. Further, previous research suggests that parents are less likely to recognize motor skills as part of readiness compared to nominal knowledge (identifying letters, numbers, colors, etc.), language development, literacy, and social competence [13]. While leaders in early education may recognize the importance of physical health and well-being for school readiness, parents may not automatically make this connection. Therefore, interventions, such as the one co-designed with community leaders in this study, may need to educate parents about the important role of physical activity, health, and well-being for school readiness.

Data from this study suggests that research teams working with community partners to increase family physical activity and child school readiness must center equity and seek representation from the community of people who are being served. The co-design process and principles, such as, equal partnership, openness, respect, empathy, and design together [23] allowed the research team to effectively work with community members and leaders. One specific example of these principles in action is that the research team included a Community Researcher who lived in the community of focus and had a preschool-aged child. Another example of these co-design principles occurred between the third and fourth action planning sessions. After participants discussed strategies for increasing physical activity and school readiness, they wanted to hear from mothers of preschool-aged children before moving forward. The research team worked with community partners to schedule a listening session with low-income mothers of preschool-aged children and brought the lessons learned back to the action planning team. The research team’s alignment with community partners on the core values of equity, representation, and inclusion was an essential step towards building trusting relationships. Because the research team members shared these core values, the community partners continued to engage in the action planning sessions, and committed time, space, and other resources to make the community intervention successful. Thus, developing shared values is a critical step towards building partnerships between researchers and community partners that should be prioritized in future health promotion efforts.

The development of shared values seemed to “unlock” the gate to facilitate collaboration and shared problem solving among the action planning team members. Participants viewed collaboration as a necessary strategy for having a sustained impact that did not always happen in practice. There was a strong desire to collaborate as evidenced by participants’ willingness to help, partner, offer resources, provide space, etc. One critical aspect of collaboration was learning from previous programs that were implemented in the community. Participants shared anecdotal evidence for strategies that might be effective for promoting positive health and academic outcomes from their extensive and varied experiences working in the community. However, it was notable that there were no systematic evaluations or data available to learn about the effectiveness of previous programs. This lack of data presents an opportunity for researchers to partner with community organizations to systematically evaluate the effectiveness of new and existing programs to promote physical activity, health, and school readiness at the community level.

The Families Moving Together intervention co-designed in this study will engage Community Champions as trusted members of the community and provide free and inclusive family physical activity programs that integrate early learning strategies at the community-level. Engaging community leaders in the co-design process resulted in the commitment of resources, spaces, and partnerships that will facilitate implementation of the community intervention. This intervention is novel because it facilitates parent-child engagement in physical activity together while incorporating existing community-wide resources as strategies for improving health and academic outcomes. This intervention also engages community members in the delivery of the intervention, a strategy that previous health promotion interventions have successfully used to reach underserved populations [29].

This study had many strengths that include: inviting community leaders from many different sectors to co-design the Families Moving Together intervention with the research team and using a structured qualitative analysis to understand participant values that underpinned the process. Participants from local government, parks and recreation, early learning, public housing, local schools, and other related areas joined in this study to collaborate on the intervention design. Bringing together a group of individuals who have expertise in different areas was critical for designing creative solutions to increase physical activity and school readiness among low-income populations in the community. Understanding the values of individuals who have different perspectives and expertise can inform the work of future community-university partners who are working together to solve problems in their own community. Despite these strengths, this study was limited by a relatively small number of participants. Due to this small sample size and small number of individuals who work at some of the organizations that we included, we were unable to provide organizational affiliations along with the quotes in this study that may have provided additional context for the results. Further, there are many individuals at the community level who are working towards the goal of improving physical activity and school readiness who may have been able to contribute unique ideas. Specifically, if members of the business community were included, they may have been able to share insights on how to support and sustain community-wide efforts to promote physical activity and school readiness. Another challenge that the research team experienced in this setting was striking a balance between encouraging community members to generate strategies to solve problems in their own community while also providing information about evidence-based strategies from the existing literature. The co-design process is a significant time commitment from community members, so there were times (i.e., session 4) when the research team provided a synthesis of the information to keep the process moving forward in a timely manner. However, all participants had the opportunity to provide written and verbal feedback on information presented by the research team. More studies using the PRACTIS are needed to identify best practices for co-designing with community leaders using this tool.

In conclusion, this study used the PRACTIS guide as a framework for co-designing a community-level intervention in partnership with community leaders. The goal of the resulting Families Moving Together intervention is to increase health and school readiness by engaging families in physical activity together. The core components of the co-designed intervention include: free community movement programs for parents and children; engaging trusted individuals from the community in the intervention delivery; prioritizing the use of existing resources and materials; and building partnerships. Other research teams could consider using this method to work with communities to promote equitable health outcomes.
