Pediococcus acidilactici attenuates chronic stress-induced depression via generating metabolite indole-3-lactic acid and downregulating neuroinflammation
Authors: Yuhao Wang, Jun Gong, Kexin Geng, Xia Chen, Mengzhen Jia, Cong Yang, Silu Zhang, Jie Pan, Rongrong Wu, Zhigang Liu, Tian Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDepression, a prevalent mental disorder worldwide, is associated with pathological mechanisms including neuroinflammation. Pediococcus acidilactici has shown the potential neuroprotective effects, yet its role in ameliorating depression and the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.MethodsEight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were subjected to chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) for 6 weeks to establish a depression model. Mice were orally administered Pediococcus acidilactici CGMCC 35671 (strain GOLDGUT-EZMind) (1 × 109 CFU/day). Behavioral experiments and biochemical assays were conducted to evaluate its effects on CUMS-induced anxiety and depressive-like behaviors. Serum indole-3-lactic acid (ILA) concentrations and aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) pathway activation in the brain were quantified. To elucidate the potential mechanism by which P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 ameliorates depression, mice received intraperitoneal injections of the AHR inhibitor CH223191 (100 µL/day, 4 weeks) to block the interaction between ILA and cerebral AHR, thereby assessing its contribution to attenuating neuroinflammation and depression in CUMS mice.ResultsPediococcus acidilactici CGMCC 35671 supplementation significantly ameliorated anxiety-like, depressive-like, and social interaction deficits in CUMS mice. Excessive microglial activation in the hippocampal dentate gyrus and cortex was suppressed, with reduced levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and elevated levels of the anti-inflammatory (IL-22) in the brain. Cortical neurotransmitter levels of serotonin and dopamine were increased, and serum oxidative stress markers were attenuated. Furthermore, P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 supplementation elevated serum ILA concentrations and upregulated mRNA expression of AHR downstream markers (CYP1A1, CYP1A2, CYP1B1) in the brain. Conversely, AHR inhibitor administration abrogated the ameliorative effects of P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 on neuroinflammation and depressive symptoms in CUMS mice.ConclusionPediococcus acidilactici CGMCC 35671 metabolizes tryptophan to produce ILA, which crosses the blood-brain barrier via systemic circulation to activate the AHR pathway, thereby attenuating neuroinflammation and ameliorating depressive symptoms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-025-03580-7.

Introduction

Depression is a psychiatric disorder influenced by a combination of biological, psychological, and social factors [1]. According to the World Health Organization, approximately 4.7% of the global adult population suffers from depression annually [2]. Its hallmark symptoms, including persistent low mood and anhedonia, profoundly impair patients’ quality of life. The heterogeneous nature of depression complicates the understanding of its precise pathophysiological mechanisms, which remain incompletely elucidated. Among the prevailing theories, the monoamine hypothesis has garnered significant acceptance [3, 4], attributing depressive symptoms to deficiencies in monoaminergic neurotransmitters such as serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT) and dopamine (DA) within the brain. In parallel, neuroinflammation has emerged as a critical contributor to the etiology of depression [5]. This condition is typified by excessive activation of microglia and the overproduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [6–8]. These inflammatory processes disrupt neuronal function, leading to neurotransmitter imbalances and neural network abnormalities, thereby exacerbating depressive symptoms [9]. Moreover, excessively activated microglia release substantial amounts of reactive oxygen species [10], elevating oxidative stress levels, which further intensifies the inflammatory response. This creates a deleterious bidirectional feedback loop that aggravates the progression of depression. Current pharmacological interventions for depression primarily rely on antidepressants aimed at augmenting the availability of monoaminergic neurotransmitters in the brain, such as paroxetine, sertraline, and fluoxetine. However, these treatments are often accompanied by significant side effects [11], including headaches, insomnia, and weight changes, underscoring the urgent need for alternative therapeutic strategies. In recent years, probiotics have garnered attention as promising modulators of the gut-brain axis with potential benefits for mental health. By reshaping gut microbiota composition, strengthening intestinal barrier function, and producing bioactive metabolites, probiotics exert multifaceted effects on host physiology and psychology.

The advent of the gut-brain axis concept has spurred extensive research into the role of gut microbiota in emotion regulation. Compelling evidence suggests that gut microbiota can influence host behavior. Clinical studies have revealed significant differences in gut microbiota composition between individuals with depression and healthy controls [12]. The gut microbial structure in depressed individuals is markedly dysregulated, characterized by reduced microbial diversity [13], substantially decreased abundances of beneficial bacteria such as short-chain fatty acid-producing Roseburia and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, and anti-inflammatory Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, alongside elevated abundances of potentially pro-inflammatory bacteria, including Desulfovibrio and Bacteroides fragilis [14]. Probiotic supplementation sustained gut microbial diversity and increased the abundance of Lactobacillus in volunteers, while attenuating depressive symptoms [15]. Furthermore, microbial metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids [16], indole derivatives [17], and γ-aminobutyric acid [18], have been shown to treat emotional disorders through neural, endocrine, and immune pathways in mice. These findings position probiotics and their metabolites as novel candidates for therapeutic intervention in depression and anxiety disorders.

Notably, amino acid metabolism dysregulation, particularly of tryptophan, has been observed in patients with depression [19, 20]. Gut microbiota metabolizes tryptophan to generate indole derivatives [21], such as indole, indole-3-acetic acid, indole-3-propionic acid, and indole-3-lactic acid (ILA). ILA, a key metabolite produced by lactic acid bacteria and bifidobacteria, exhibits diverse biological activities within both the gut and the central nervous system. A recent study has demonstrated that antidepressant efficacy of microbial derived ILA, potentially mediated by activation of aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) pathway to alleviate neuroinflammation [22]. However, significant disparities persist in the ILA production capacity across different species and even at the strain level [23]. Our previous research revealed that Pediococcus acidilactici CGMCC 35671, isolated from fermented sauerkraut in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, exhibits a robust capacity to produce indole-3-lactic acid (ILA). Concurrently, studies have indicated that P. acidilactici holds potential to ameliorate mental health [24], yet the underlying mechanisms by which it modulates emotional well-being remain elusive.

In the present study, the capacities of selected lactic acid bacteria and bifidobacteria to metabolize tryptophan and produce indole-3-lactic acid (ILA) were compared. Consequently, a high-ILA-producing strain, P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671, was isolated from traditional fermented sauerkraut from the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau in China. Its effects were evaluated on a CUMS-induced depression mice model. The mechanisms through which P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 alleviates anxiety- and depression-like behaviors were explored, with a focus on neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and neurotransmitter regulation. Additionally, the role of the AHR signaling pathway in mediating these effects was explored and confirmed through the use of an AHR inhibitor CH223191 (2-methyl-2 H-pyrazole-3-carboxylic acid). These results underscore the potential of P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 as a promising probiotic candidate for the treatment of mood disorders, while highlighting the pivotal role of microbial metabolites in gut-brain axis modulation.

Materials and methods

Strain cultivation

The Pediococcus acidilactici (strain GOLDGUT-EZMind), isolated from traditionally fermented sauerkraut originating from the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau in China, was provided by WonderLab. This strain was deposited at the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC), under the accession number CGMCC 35671. The bacteria were cultured in MRS broth under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 24 h. For subsequent oral gavage in mice, the cells were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to a final concentration of 1 × 10⁹ CFU/100 µL.

Animals

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and housed in the animal facility at Northwest A&F University under standard conditions. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Northwest A&F University (Ethics No. IACUC2024-1110) and conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (Eighth Edition, ISBN-10: 0-309-15396-4).

Experiment 1 (Pediococcus acidilactici intervention)

24 mice were randomly divided into 3 groups, after one week of adaptive feeding, the mice were subjected to a six-week chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) protocol. In brief, the CUMS regimen included a variety of mild stressors [25], such as forced swimming at 4 °C for 5 min, restraint for 3 h, water deprivation for 24 h, food deprivation for 24 h, wet bedding for 24 h, tilted cages for 24 h, and tail pinching for 5 min. These stressors were randomly scheduled throughout the week to reduce predictability. Control group animals were not exposed to any stress. Pediococcus acidilactici CGMCC 35671 or PBS was administered via oral gavage (100 µL/day) [26].

Experiment 2 (AHR intervention)

To explore the mechanisms of AHR signaling pathway, 16 mice were randomly divided into 2 groups, the mice received the AHR antagonist CH-223191 (Glpbio, Shanghai, China) or corn oil at a dose of 10 mg/kg/day through intraperitoneal injection for the last 4 weeks (100 µL/day) [27].

Behavioral experiments

Open field test (OFT)

The open field test is employed to evaluate spontaneous exploratory activity and anxiety-like behavior in mice [28]. The experiment utilized an opaque, uncovered monitoring box (50 cm × 50 cm × 50 cm), with the bottom divided into 25 equal square regions. A camera positioned above the arena recorded the mice’s movement trajectories over a 5-minute period. Total distance traveled and time spent in central area were analyzed to assess spontaneous activity levels and anxiety. Prior to testing, the arena was cleaned to remove any feces from the previous mouse and wiped with 75% alcohol to eliminate odor interference from other mice.

Elevated plus maze test (EPMT)

The elevated plus maze (EPM) is commonly used to evaluate anxiety-like behavior in mice. The apparatus was elevated 70 cm above the ground and consisted of two open arms (30 cm × 8 cm), two closed arms (30 cm × 8 cm × 15 cm), and a central area (8 cm × 8 cm). Mice were placed in the central area, and their movement was recorded for 5 min. The time spent in the open arms was analyzed to assess anxiety levels. Prior to testing, the arena was cleaned to remove any feces from the previous mouse and wiped with 75% alcohol to eliminate odor interference from other mice.

Tail suspension test (TST)

Mice were suspended by the tail for 6 min by attaching them to a horizontal bar positioned 50 cm above the floor using adhesive tape applied 2 cm from the tip of the tail in a visually isolated area. A mouse was considered immobile when it floated in an upright position, making only minimal movements. The duration of immobility of tail-suspended mice during the final 4 min was recorded using timers. This procedure was conducted by a single observer who was blind to the specific experimental groups and strictly adhered to standard testing protocols [29].

Sucrose preference test (SPT)

The sucrose preference test serves as a key indicator for assessing anhedonia, which is characterized by a diminished interest in rewarding stimuli, a manifestation of emotional disturbances often used to evaluate depressive-like behaviors in mice. This experiment comprises two phases: acclimation training and testing [30]. During the acclimation training phase, two bottles containing a 1% (w/v) sucrose solution are provided in each cage for 24 h. Subsequently, one bottle is replaced with pure water for an additional 24 h, followed by a 12-hour fasting and water deprivation period. At this point, one bottle of 1% (w/v) sucrose solution and one bottle of pure water are prepared, and their initial volumes are recorded. After 12 h, the remaining volumes of both the sucrose solution and pure water are measured. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis

Indole-3-lactic acid (ILA) in strain culture medium

To prepare the samples, 200 µL of MRS culture medium (fermented for 24 h) was diluted with pre-chilled methanol (−20 °C) to a final volume of 1 mL. The diluted medium was incubated at −20 °C for 20 min before being centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C). The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane to remove particulate matter.

The filtered supernatant was immediately analyzed using HPLC (LC-16; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) [32]. For ILA quantification, 10 µL of the sample was injected into the chromatograph, equipped with a C18 column (Agilent, 4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) and a fluorescence detector set at an excitation wavelength of 282 nm and an emission wavelength of 352 nm. Chromatographic separation was performed at a column temperature of 30 °C using a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min and gradient elution with chromatographic-grade methanol (mobile phase A) and 15 mmol/L sodium dihydrogen phosphate (NaH₂PO₄, PH 2.8,mobile phase B). The gradient program was as follows: 0–12 min, 42% A; 12–28 min, 50% A; and 28–35 min, 85% A.

ILA in mouse serum

To prepare the samples, 60 µL of serum was diluted with pre-chilled methanol (−20 °C) to a final volume of 300 µL. The diluted serum was incubated at −20 °C for 20 min before being centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C). The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane to remove particulate matter.

Subsequent analyses of HPLC were the same as ILA in strain culture medium.

5-Hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) and Dopamine (DA) in mouse cortex

Cortical tissues were homogenized in saline to create a 10% (w/v) homogenate, which was centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C) to obtain the supernatant. A precipitating reagent (0.60 mol/L HClO4, 1.20 mol/L K₂HPO₄, and 2.00 mmol/L Na2EDTA) was then added to the supernatant in a 1:1 ratio. The mixture was incubated on ice for 10 min before undergoing a second centrifugation under the same conditions. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane to remove particulate matter and prepare the sample for analysis.

Immediately after filtration, the supernatant was analyzed via HPLC (LC-16; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) [33]. For quantification of 5-HT and DA, 20 µL of each sample was injected into the chromatograph, which was equipped with a C18 column (Agilent, 4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) and a fluorescence detector set at an excitation wavelength of 290 nm and an emission wavelength of 330 nm. The chromatographic separation was performed at 30 °C with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The mobile phases used were chromatographic-grade methanol (mobile phase A) and a 0.1 mol/L sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.1, containing 0.1 mmol/L disodium EDTA, mobile phase B), with an elution ratio of 1:9.

Immunofluorescence staining

After the euthanasia of the mice, brain tissues were extracted, with one-half of each brain dissected and placed into vials containing 4% (v/v) paraformaldehyde (pH 7.4) for 24 h. The specimens were subsequently embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 5 μm slices. Deparaffinization was carried out with xylene, followed by sequential washes in 100%, 90%, 80%, and 70% ethanol, each for 5 min, along with three washes in PBS (pH 7.4) for 5 min each. The slices were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (IBA-1). After this incubation, the slides were washed with PBS and incubated with a secondary antibody for 2 h. Information regarding the antibodies is provided in Additional file 1. DAPI was utilized for nuclear staining to visualize the sections. Immunofluorescence images were obtained using an inverted fluorescent microscope (DM5000 B, Germany), and positive regions were quantified using Image J software [34].

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Tissue samples were accurately weighed and placed into 2.0 mL grinding tubes. To assess mRNA levels in mouse tissues, BIOZOL (Bioer Technology, Hangzhou, China) was added. Quantification was performed using a NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China). The ueIris II qRT-PCR System First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (US Everbright, Suzhou, China) enabled the reverse transcription of total RNA into cDNA. For quantitative PCR (qPCR), the 2× SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix Kit (US Everbright, Suzhou, China) was used to quantify mRNA levels. The primer sequences are provided in Additional file 2. Ct values were normalized to GAPDH, and relative gene expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum and cortical tissues were processed to detect inflammatory cytokines and corticosterone, including tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-22 (IL-22), and corticosterone. All procedures followed the instructions provided with the ELISA kits (xl-Em0359, xl-Em0187, xl-Em0196, xl-Em0217, xl-Em1949, Xinle Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). A standard curve was generated to calculate the concentration, which was expressed as ng/g of protein.

Measurement of malondialdehyde (MDA) and superoxide dismutase (SOD)

Serum and cortical tissues were harvested from mice. Cortical tissues were homogenized in PBS to generate a 10% (w/v) homogenate, which was centrifuged to isolate the supernatant. Protein concentrations in the cortical homogenate were determined using the BCA protein assay kit. Subsequently, MDA levels and SOD activity in both serum and the cortical supernatant were quantified utilizing specific assay kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China), following the protocols provided by the manufacturer.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). One-way ANOVA was utilized to identify significant differences among the groups. Subsequently, Tukey’s multiple comparison test was applied as a post hoc analysis. Unpaired two-tailed t-test was used to compare two groups. Results are expressed as mean ± SEM, with p < 0.05 regarded as statistically significant.

Results

P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 ameliorated anxiety- and depression-like behaviors and neurotransmitter levels imbalance in CUMS-induced mice

ILA is synthesized from tryptophan through gut microbiota-mediated metabolism (Fig. 1A), initially converted to indole-3-pyruvic acid (IPyA) by aromatic amino acid aminotransferase (ArAT), followed by reduction to ILA via indolelactate dehydrogenase (fldH) or lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). This study evaluated the ILA production capacities of several common lactic acid bacteria and bifidobacteria (Fig. 1B), revealing that lactic acid bacteria generally exhibited higher ILA yields than bifidobacteria, with P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 demonstrating the highest output. Subsequently, the antidepressant potential and underlying mechanisms of P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 were investigated.

To investigate the ameliorative effects of P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 on depression in mice, a CUMS mice model was employed to induce depressive behaviors (Fig. 1C). Over a six-week experimental period, there were no significant differences in body weight among the various groups of mice (Fig. 1D). Several behavioral experiments, including the OFT, EPMT, TST, and SPT, were conducted to evaluate the effect of P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 on CUMS-induced anxiety- and depression-like behaviors. Compared to the CUMS group, P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 treatment significantly increased the time spent in the center of the OFT and the exploration time in the open arms of the EPMT (Fig. 1E & F) (p < 0.01), indicating reduced anxiety-like behaviors. Additionally, P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 treatment significantly reduced immobility time in the TST (Fig. 1G; p < 0.01) and markedly elevated the sucrose preference index in the SPT (Fig. 1H; p < 0.01), suggesting amelioration of depressive-like behaviors. Social behavior was further evaluated using the TCST (Fig. 1I), where results showed that P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 intervention significantly ameliorated the CUMS-induced impairments in sociability (p < 0.01) and preference for social novelty (p < 0.05). Furthermore, stress-induced elevations in corticosterone levels, which are associated with depression onset, were significantly reduced in the P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 treated group (Fig. 1J) (p < 0.05). This reduction demonstrates that P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 mitigates corticosterone dysregulation and alleviates depressive behaviors. Collectively, these findings suggest that P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 supplementation can effectively ameliorate CUMS-induced anxiety- and depression-like behaviors.

Depression also contributes to neurotransmitter level imbalances in the brain. To evaluate whether P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 treatment attenuates these imbalances in the brains of CUMS mice, cortical 5-HT and DA levels were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography. Compared to the CON group, the CUMS group exhibited a significant decrease in both 5-HT and DA levels (Fig. 1K & L). However, intervention with P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 significantly increased both 5-HT and DA levels, restoring them to normal values (p < 0.05, p < 0.01). These results demonstrate that P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 attenuated neurotransmitter level imbalances in CUMS-induced mice.

P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 reduced neuroinflammation and oxidative stress in CUMS-induced depressive mice

The onset of depression is frequently associated with neuroinflammation, with activated microglial cells widely recognized as key indicators of neuroinflammatory processes. Ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1 (IBA-1), a microglia- and macrophage-specific calcium-binding protein, serves as a standard marker of microglial activation. To investigate this, immunofluorescence staining for IBA-1 was performed on mouse brain tissue (Fig. 2A). The results revealed that CUMS treatment significantly increased the Iba1-positive area and integrated density in the hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG) and cortex, indicating excessive microglial activation. However, intervention with P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 significantly attenuated this overactivation.

To further elucidate its effects on cerebral inflammation, the expression of inflammation-related genes in the hippocampus and the levels of associated inflammatory cytokines in the cortex were quantified. Compared to the CON group, the CUMS group exhibited significantly elevated expression of pro-inflammatory genes encoding TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in the hippocampus, coupled with a marked reduction in the anti-inflammatory gene encoding IL-22 (Fig. 2B-E). Intervention with P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 significantly ameliorated these aberrant gene expression patterns (p < 0.05). A similar trend was observed for inflammatory cytokine levels in the cortex (p < 0.01), with the exception of IL-6 (Fig. 2F-I). These findings suggest that P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 mitigates CUMS-induced neuroinflammation by suppressing excessive microglial activation and normalizing inflammatory gene and cytokine profiles.

Oxidative stress is recognized as a trigger of neuroinflammation and a contributor to the pathogenesis of depression. To assess oxidative stress, levels of MDA, a marker of lipid peroxidation, and the antioxidant enzyme SOD were quantified in both serum and cortex. Following CUMS treatment, MDA levels were significantly elevated in both serum and cortex, whereas SOD levels were significantly reduced. Intervention with P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 significantly reversed these alterations in serum (Fig. 2J & K; p < 0.05), but no significant effect was observed in the cortex (Fig. 2L & M).

P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 increased serum ILA levels and promoted AHR activation in the cortex and hippocampus

In vitro studies demonstrated that P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 exhibits a robust capacity to metabolize tryptophan and produce ILA (Fig. 1B). ILA is known to activate the AHR signaling pathway, thereby eliciting immune responses. To investigate whether P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 modulates the AHR signaling pathway, ILA concentrations were quantified in mouse serum, and the expression of AHR pathway downstream markers, including CYP1A1, CYP1A2, and CYP1B1, was evaluated in the cortex and hippocampus. Results revealed that CUMS treatment significantly reduced serum ILA concentrations (Fig. 3A) and downregulated the expression of AHR downstream markers in the cortex and hippocampus (Fig. 3B-G). However, intervention with P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 significantly reversed these alterations, corroborating the hypothesis. These findings suggest that P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 metabolizes tryptophan to produce ILA, which may enter the brain via systemic circulation and activate the AHR signaling pathway in the hippocampus and cortex.

Inhibition of the AHR pathway abolished the antidepressant and anxiolytic effects of P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 in mice

To determine whether P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 ameliorates depression through activation of the AHR signaling pathway, the inhibitor CH223191 was administered via intraperitoneal injection to mice, while P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 was concurrently administered by oral gavage (Fig. 4A). After four weeks of CH223191 treatment, no significant changes in body weight were observed across experimental groups (Fig. 4B). CH223191 did not alter ILA concentrations in mouse serum as well (Fig. 4C). However, behavioral assessments revealed that AHR inhibition abrogated the reductions in anxiety- and depression-like behaviors induced by P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 (Fig. 4D-H). Additionally, AHR inhibition with CH223191 abrogated the ameliorative effects of P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 on aberrant neurotransmitter levels, significantly reducing 5-HT and DA levels in the cortex (Fig. 4I & J). These findings suggest that P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 may exert antidepressant and anxiolytic effects while modulating neurotransmitter levels in CUMS mice through activation of the AHR signaling pathway.

To elucidate the role of the AHR signaling pathway in ameliorating neuroinflammation and depression, the effects of P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 on neuroinflammation were evaluated in mice treated with the inhibitor CH223191. Notably, AHR inhibition with CH223191 significantly increased the population of activated microglial cells in the hippocampal DG and cortex of mice receiving P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 intervention (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, in the hippocampus, gene expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 was significantly upregulated, whereas expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-22 was markedly downregulated (Fig. 6B-E). In the cortex, levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β were significantly elevated, while the level of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-22 was significantly reduced (Fig. 6F & G & I). These findings indicate that CH223191 abrogated the beneficial effects of P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 on neuroinflammation in mice, underscoring the role of the AHR signaling pathway in mediating these effects.

Discussion

In this study, oral administration of Pediococcus acidilactici CGMCC 35671 (1 × 109 CFU/day) to CUMS-induced depressive mice significantly elevated cortical neurotransmitter levels of 5-HT and DA, suppressed excessive microglial activation in the hippocampal DG and cortex, and attenuated aberrant expression of inflammatory cytokines in the brain. Additionally, P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 mitigated serum oxidative stress, though its effect on brain oxidative stress was minimal. These changes collectively ameliorated CUMS-induced anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors. Furthermore, CGMCC 35671 supplementation increased serum ILA concentrations and upregulated the expression of downstream genes in the AHR signaling pathway in the brain. Notably, administration of the AHR inhibitor CH223191 abrogated the ameliorative effects of P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 on neuroinflammation and depressive symptoms in CUMS mice. These findings collectively indicate that the ameliorative effects of P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 on CUMS-induced neuroinflammation and anxiety-depressive behaviors may be mediated by ILA-dependent activation of the AHR pathway.

Pediococcus acidilactici, a common probiotic in fermented foods, has been primarily studied for its roles in industrial fermentation and metabolic disorders such as obesity [35, 36]. In recent years, with advancing research on the gut-brain axis [37], its potential to ameliorate emotional disorders like anxiety has been recognized [24]. Its metabolite, lactic acid, exhibits antibacterial activity [38], contributing to pathogen suppression and probiotic growth. The anxiolytic effects of P. acidilactici CCFM6432 have been attributed to lactic acid-mediated modulation of the gut microbiome [24]. In this study, we isolated P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 from fermented foods of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and identified its high ILA production capacity, which we hypothesized plays a critical role in its amelioration of neuroinflammation and depression.

Tryptophan, an essential amino acid, yields metabolites that regulate host immunity, metabolism, gut ecology, and neuroprotection [39]. As the only amino acid with an indole structure, tryptophan is metabolized by gut microbiota via ArAT to IPyA, which is subsequently reduced to ILA by fldH or LDH. This process is primarily driven by lactic acid bacteria and bifidobacteria [40, 41], with some involvement of Clostridium species [42]. Recent studies indicate that certain Bifidobacterium species can directly metabolize tryptophan to ILA via aromatic lactate dehydrogenase (Aldh) [22]. ILA, absorbed into the bloodstream and crossing the blood-brain barrier [22, 43], activates the AHR signaling pathway in the brain. AHR, a ligand-dependent transcription factor, forms a heterodimer with AHR nuclear translocator upon activation by endogenous or exogenous ligands (e.g., indole derivatives such as ILA) [44, 45], binding to dioxin/xenobiotic response elements (DRE/XRE) on DNA to induce target gene transcription (e.g., cytochrome P450 family) [46], exerting anti-inflammatory effects. Microbial indole has been shown to activate the AHR pathway, suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome formation, thereby attenuating neuroinflammation and mitigating cognitive deficits in Alzheimer’s disease [47]. Additionally, AHR pathway activation plays a pivotal role in attenuating neuroinflammation in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis mice [48]. Similar effects were observed in depressed mice treated with Bifidobacterium breve in recent studies [22], consistent with our findings. Furthermore, the AHR downstream target gene CYP1A1 metabolizes exogenous or endogenous compounds (e.g., arachidonic acid) into anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective metabolites, such as epoxyeicosatrienoic acids [49]. These metabolites inhibit the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) pathway, reducing the expression and release of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α), attenuating excessive microglial activation, and mitigating neuroinflammation [50, 51].

Depression, characterized by high heterogeneity [52], has complex and incompletely elucidated pathogenesis. Neuroinflammation has emerged as a key biological mechanism, involving aberrant immune responses in the central nervous system (CNS), primarily driven by activated microglia and astrocytes. Elevated neuroinflammatory markers, such as pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and activated microglia, are frequently observed in the brains of depressed individuals. Microglia, the CNS’s resident immune cells, maintain neural homeostasis under physiological conditions by dynamically monitoring the microenvironment [53]. However, stressors like chronic stress, infection, or metabolic dysregulation can polarize microglia from a “resting” (M0) to a “pro-inflammatory” (M1) state [54], releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species, leading to neuronal damage and impaired synaptic plasticity. In CUMS or lipopolysaccharide-induced depression models, microglial inhibitors (e.g., minocycline) have reversed depressive-like behaviors and synaptic damage [55]. In this study, CUMS upregulated inflammatory cytokine gene expression in the hippocampus and increased cortical cytokine levels, with quantitative analysis of the microglial activation marker ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (IBA-1) revealing excessive microglial activation in the hippocampal DG and cortex of depressive mice. P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 supplementation significantly ameliorated these effects, likely via ILA-mediated AHR pathway activation in the brain.

Abnormal synthesis, release, or signaling of neurotransmitters in the brain is a direct cause of mood disorders [56]. Numerous studies have reported reduced neurotransmitter levels in depressed individuals, with current antidepressants acting by enhancing available neurotransmitters [57]. Neuroinflammation disrupts neurotransmitter balance by activating indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase, diverting tryptophan metabolism toward the kynurenine pathway, reducing 5-HT synthesis, and increasing neurotoxic metabolites, exacerbating depressive symptoms [58, 59]. Furthermore, microglia-mediated neuroinflammation in the brain induces dysfunction in DA signaling pathways, resulting in reduced dopamine levels [60].

In this study, CUMS significantly reduced cortical 5-HT and DA levels in mice. Supplementation with P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 significantly attenuated these imbalances, likely through ILA-mediated mechanisms.

Despite these findings, this study has several limitations. The effects of P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 on gut microbiota composition and other metabolites were not investigated, although these may contribute to its amelioration of anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors. As ILA is only one of several AHR ligands, whether the microbiota activates the AHR signaling pathway via other metabolites remains unexplored. Future studies could employ targeted gene knockouts in microbes to investigate the roles of other metabolites in depression amelioration and further examine the gut microbiota alterations in mice following P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 intervention. Additionally, the effects of P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 on gut were not examined. Its potential to enhance intestinal barrier integrity and modulate microbial ecology may significantly contribute to depression interventions. Furthermore, this study evaluated P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 solely in the CUMS mouse model of depression. Whether similar effects occur in other depression models, such as social defeat stress or corticosterone-induced models, remains unclear. Future research may explore the efficacy of P. acidilactici CGMCC 35671 in these models to broaden the understanding of our findings across diverse depression paradigms.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that Pediococcus acidilactici CGMCC 35671 metabolizes tryptophan to produce ILA, which crosses the blood-brain barrier via systemic circulation to activate the AHR signaling pathway, thereby attenuating neuroinflammation and ameliorating depressive symptoms (Fig. 7). These findings elucidate the ameliorative mechanism of probiotic metabolites in the gut-brain axis, providing a theoretical foundation for research on neuroinflammation-related disorders.
