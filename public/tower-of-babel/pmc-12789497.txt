Pregnancy outcomes of obstetrician gynecologist mothers: a retrospective matched cohort study
Authors: Gufeng Xu, Liyuan Zhang, Yun Liao, Yue Wang, Qiong Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObstetrician-Gynecologist (OB-GYNs) mothers, serving dual roles as healthcare providers and patients, present an interesting demographic for studying this dynamic, particularly in China where empirical data on this subject is limited. The study aimed to determine whether OB-GYNs experience better pregnancy outcomes compared to non-physician women, hypothesizing that their medical background could lead to different health behaviors and outcomes. This was a retrospective matched cohort study conducted at the Women’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. It included 100 OB-GYNs who gave birth between January 2012 and April 2022 and a matched control group of 200 non-medical background women. Outcomes measured were Cesarean section rates, emergency cesarean section, operative vaginal delivery, birth weight, Apgar score, and various pregnancy and childbirth complications. Statistical analysis was performed using descriptive statistics, generalized estimating equation model and Fisher’s exact tests. Cesarean section rates were similar between OB-GYNs (32%) and non-physicians (36.5%). OB-GYNs had similar incidences of pregnancy complications compared with non-physicians except postpartum hemorrhage (0% in OB-GYNs vs. 5.5% in non-physicians, P = 0.018). The findings indicate that OB-GYNs do not differ significantly from non-physician women in terms of Cesarean section rates and incidences of pregnancy complications except postpartum hemorrhage.Keywords: Pregnancy outcomes, Obstetrician-Gynecologists, Cesarean section, Postpartum hemorrhage, Medical educationSubject terms: Outcomes research, Health occupations

Introduction

Maternal health and pregnancy outcomes are areas of significant interest in medical research, offering vital insights into the well-being of mothers and their children. These outcomes are influenced by a myriad of factors, including the mother’s health status, access to healthcare, and socio-economic conditions1,2. A particularly intriguing aspect of this research is the study of pregnancy outcomes among specific populations, notably among healthcare professionals like Obstetrician-Gynecologists (OB-GYNs).

The unique position of physicians, who are both providers and receivers of healthcare services, presents an intriguing paradox. As medical professionals, they possess extensive knowledge about pregnancy and childbirth, which theoretically could influence their pregnancy outcomes3,4. One study on US female urologists showed higher pregnancy complications and similar cesarean delivery rates compared to the general population, influenced by their older age at childbirth and higher induction rates5. However, empirical data of OB-GYNs on this subject, especially within the Chinese context, is sparse. This study aims to fill this gap by examining the pregnancy outcomes of OB-GYNs in China, comparing them with those of women from non-medical backgrounds.

The objective of this study is to investigate whether OB-GYNs experience better pregnancy outcomes than the general population.

This study adopts a comprehensive approach, employing quantitative methods to analyze data on pregnancy outcomes among OB-GYNs. The methodology involves a retrospective cohort comparing OB-GYN doctors and normal non-doctor patients matched for age, gestational week and delivery date.

While it is hypothesized that OB-GYNs might have better pregnancy outcomes, the study remains open to all possibilities, acknowledging the complex interplay of factors that influence maternal health.

The significance of this study lies in its potential to enhance our understanding of how professional medical knowledge and access to medical facilities impact personal health outcomes. The findings could have profound implications for maternal healthcare practices, influencing policies and guiding future research, especially in countries like China, where the healthcare system and cultural practices are unique.

Methods and materials

This is a retrospective cohort conducted in Women’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. Data was retrieved from hospital database. Approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the Women’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine with the number IRB-20220184-R. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the Institutional Review Board of the Women’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine waived the need of obtaining informed consent. To protect patient confidentiality, all records were fully anonymized before analysis; no identifiable information was retained in the research dataset. This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Two groups were included in the study. We included 100 Obstetrician-Gynecologists delivered at Women’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine between January 2012 and April 2022. The OB-GYNs were identified using hospital faculty registry. The control group were women with no medical background, matched 1:2 with physicians for delivery date (no more than 7 days), age (no more than 1 year), and gestational age at delivery (no more than 1 week). Multiple pregnancies were excluded from the study. Data was retrieved from hospital medical record database.

The primary outcomes were Cesarean section rates. Secondary outcomes were emergency cesarean section, operative vaginal delivery, birth weight, Apgar score at 1 min and 5 min, pregnancy complications (include gestational diabetes mellitus, fetal growth restriction, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, placenta previa, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, oligohydramnios, preterm premature rupture of membranes, placental abruption, uterine rupture, postpartum hemorrhage, Chorioamnionitis). The definition of complications as below: Gestational diabetes mellitus was defined as glucose intolerance first recognized during pregnancy, diagnosed using a one-step 75-g 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test, with abnormal values at fasting, 1 h, or 2 h meeting diagnostic thresholds. Fetal growth restriction was defined as an estimated fetal weight or abdominal circumference below the 10th percentile for gestational age confirmed by ultrasound. Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy was characterized by pruritus in pregnancy with elevated serum bile acids greater than 10 µmol/L, usually in the second or third trimester. Placenta previa referred to placental tissue covering or reaching the internal cervical os on ultrasound. Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy included gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, eclampsia, and chronic hypertension, with diagnostic thresholds based on blood pressure, proteinuria, and presence of severe features. Oligohydramnios was defined as an amniotic fluid index less than 5 cm or a single deepest pocket less than 2 cm. Preterm premature rupture of membranes was rupture of membranes before 37 weeks of gestation and prior to labor onset. Placental abruption referred to premature separation of the placenta from the uterine wall before delivery, typically diagnosed clinically. Uterine rupture was defined as complete disruption of all uterine layers, often at a scar site, and usually associated with fetal heart rate abnormalities, maternal pain, or hemodynamic instability. Postpartum hemorrhage was defined as cumulative blood loss of at least 1000 mL or blood loss with signs of hypovolemia within 24 h of birth. Chorioamnionitis, or intraamniotic infection, was a clinical diagnosis based on maternal fever with supportive findings such as maternal or fetal tachycardia, uterine tenderness, or purulent amniotic fluid.

Sample size was calculated using an online tool at ClinCalc. The parameters set for the calculation were based on an anticipated primary outcome rate of 20% in OB-GYNs and 40% in the control group, with an enrollment ratio of 1:2. The significance level (alpha) was set at 0.05, and the power (1 - beta) at 0.9. Based on these parameters, the calculated sample size required for OB-GYNs was 82, and for the control group, it was 164.

Statistical analysis was conducted using descriptive statistics, with data presented as mean ± standard deviation or as number with proportions, as appropriate. No missing data was identified. For comparison between matched groups, generalized estimating equation were used. For discrete variables with value of zero, Fisher’s exact tests were used. All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS Version 22.0(Chicago, IL).

Results

A total of 300 women were included, comprising 200 non-physicians and 100 OB-GYNs as shown in Table 1. The mean age was 31.27 ± 3.79 years in the non-physician group and 31.29 ± 3.59 years in the OB-GYNs group. Mean body mass index was 27.23 ± 12.89 kg/m² and 26.46 ± 2.99 kg/m², respectively. The mean gestational age at delivery was 38.99 ± 1.23 weeks versus 38.84 ± 1.72 weeks. Preterm labor occurred in 5 women (2.5%) in the non-physician group and 4 women (4.0%) in the OB-GYNs group. The frequency of ultrasounds in the third trimester was similar between groups (3.90 ± 1.79 vs. 3.91 ± 1.91). Regarding parity, 126 non-physicians (63%) and 65 OB-GYNs (65%) were nulliparous, while 74 (37%) and 35 (35%) had one prior birth. Education levels differed markedly: among non-physicians, 20 (10%) had completed high school, 140 (70%) college, and 40 (20%) postgraduate training, whereas in the OB-GYNs group, 0 (0%) had high school only, 10 (10%) had college, and 90 (90%) had postgraduate training. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) and P values are provided to assess group balance. The only significant difference was in education, where 90% of OB-GYNs had postgraduate education compared to 20% of non-physicians (P < 0.001).

Table 2 compares pregnancy outcomes between control mothers and OB-GYNs mothers. No significant differences were found in the rates of cesarean section (non-physicians: 36.5%, OB-GYNs: 32%, P = 0.441) or emergency cesarean section (non-physicians: 17%, OB-GYNs: 13%, P = 0.369). Operative vaginal delivery rates were also similar (non-physicians: 5.5%, OB-GYNs: 7%, P = 0.606). Birth weight averaged 3330 g for non-physicians and 3275 g for OB-GYNs (P = 0.303). Apgar scores at 1 min and 5 min after birth were nearly identical for both groups (P = 0.781 and P = 1.000, respectively).

Discussion

Our study is the first to report on the pregnancy outcomes of OB-GYNs in China, revealing that they have similar Cesarean section rates to non-physician women (32% vs. 36.5%) but similar incidence of pregnancy complications except postpartum hemorrhage (5.5% vs. 0%).

The results of our study indicate that the cesarean section (CS) rates among OB-GYNs (OB-GYNs) and women without a medical background in China are remarkably close, with OB-GYNs having a CS rate of 32% compared to 36.5% in non-physicians. This finding challenges the prevalent assumption that medical professionals consistently choose different delivery methods due to their extensive knowledge and experience. Such a pattern of similarity in CS rates is not unique to our study but is also reflected in international research. For instance, in the United States, female urologists have a CS rate of 24.3%, closely mirroring the national average of 22.9%5. Similarly, a separate study in Texas reported that doctor mothers had a CS rate of 31.8%, which was almost identical to the 32.7% rate observed in non-doctors6. These parallels across different countries and medical professions underscore that the decision to opt for a cesarean section is multifaceted, influenced by a variety of situational factors and personal preferences, rather than being solely determined by one’s medical background. Factors such as the individual’s health condition, the specifics of the pregnancy, the institutional policies of the healthcare facility, and even cultural and societal norms play a significant role in shaping these decisions. Furthermore, it’s crucial to consider the psychological aspect: despite their medical expertise, OB-GYNs, like any other expecting mothers, may have personal preferences and anxieties that influence their choice of delivery method.

A significant difference highlighted in our study is the incidence of postpartum hemorrhage (PPH). Remarkably, none of the OB-GYNs in our study experienced PPH, compared to a 5.5% occurrence rate in the non-physician group. This stark contrast can be primarily attributed to the medical expertise of OB-GYNs, which likely equips them with the skills to effectively monitor for any signs of major bleeding, while non-physician women are poorly informed of blood loss at delivery and PPH7. Their ability to promptly identify and report early stages of bleeding facilitates timely intervention, potentially preventing the progression to a severity that would meet the diagnostic criteria for PPH8. This proactive approach in managing their health, coupled with their access to immediate and appropriate medical care, may significantly reduce the risk of severe postpartum complications9,10.

Comparing these results with global studies, it is evident that while the cesarean section rates among healthcare professionals are consistent worldwide, the reduced complications among OB-GYN mothers in China are a novel finding. This disparity emphasizes the critical role of medical knowledge and access to healthcare in determining pregnancy outcomes11. These findings suggest that improving medical awareness and access to healthcare could significantly enhance pregnancy outcomes in the general population. It also indicates a need for policy reforms in maternal healthcare, focusing on educating patients and improving resource allocation.

This study suggests that the medical background and access to health care of OB-GYNs may contribute to better management of pregnancy risks, potentially due to heightened awareness and early intervention strategies. While these findings are promising, they should be interpreted with caution in clinical settings until further research substantiates these observations in broader populations.

This study’s strength lies in its focus on a unique population within a specific context, providing novel insights. However, our study has several limitations that merit consideration. Firstly, its retrospective nature may introduce biases such as selection and recall bias, limiting the ability to establish causality. The focus on a single medical specialty, OB-GYNs, and the data collection from just one hospital (Women’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine) may not represent the experiences of other medical professionals or healthcare settings, potentially affecting the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, being restricted to China, the results might not be applicable to OB-GYNs in other countries with varying healthcare systems and cultural practices. The exclusion of multiple pregnancies and the potential presence of unmeasured confounders, such as lifestyle factors and socio-economic variables, also limit the comprehensiveness and applicability of our conclusions. And the number of cases in this study might be underpowered to detect the difference in cesarean section rates and incidence of complications. These limitations underscore the need for prospective studies, broader participant selection across specialties and regions, and inclusion of multiple countries to enhance the generalizability and robustness of future research.

Future research should focus on exploring these outcomes in a more diverse set of medical specialties and geographic locations. Longitudinal studies could provide deeper insights into how continuous medical exposure influences pregnancy outcomes over time.

Conclusions

OB-GYNs have similar Cesarean section rates to non-physician women and similar pregnancy complications incidences except postpartum hemorrhage. This study highlights the potential benefits that medical knowledge and access to healthcare can have on pregnancy outcomes. It brings to light the importance of extending these benefits to a wider population to ensure better maternal and fetal health outcomes.
