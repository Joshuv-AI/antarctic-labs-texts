Adaptive Detoxification: Safeguarding General Capabilities of LLMs through Toxicity-Aware Knowledge Editing Content Warning: This paper contains examples of harmful language.
Yifan Lu, Yigeng Zhou, Jing Li, Yihui Zhang, Wenya Wang, Xiucheng Li, Meishan Zhang, Fangming Liu, Jun Yu, Min Zhang

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) exhibit impressive language capabilities but remain vulnerable to malicious prompts and jailbreaking attacks. Existing knowledge editing methods for LLM detoxification face two major challenges. First, they often rely on entity-specific localization, making them ineffective against adversarial inputs without explicit entities. Second, these methods suffer from over-editing, where detoxified models reject legitimate queries, compromising overall performance. In this paper, we propose ToxEdit , a toxicity-aware knowledge editing approach that dynamically detects toxic activation patterns during forward propagation. It then routes computations through adaptive inter-layer pathways to mitigate toxicity effectively. This design ensures precise toxicity mitigation while preserving LLMs’ general capabilities. To more accurately assess over-editing, we also enhance the SafeEdit benchmark by incorporating instruction-following evaluation tasks. Experimental results on multiple LLMs demonstrate that our ToxEdit outperforms previous state-of-the-art methods in both detoxification performance and safeguarding general capabilities of LLMs. † † footnotetext: Corresponding author.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT
(
Achiam et al. 2023
)
, Llama
(

)
and Deepseek

have gained widespread adoption due to their impressive capabilities in language understanding and reasoning
(

;

;

;
Du et al. 2025b
;

)
.
To ensure that the outputs of these models adhere to the
3H principles
—
helpful
,

, and

(
Askell et al. 2021
)
—they are typically aligned with human values through safety training
(
Zhang et al. 2024c
;
Wang et al. 2024a
;
Rafailov et al. 2024
)
.
However, even models that are safety-aligned remain vulnerable to malicious prompts or jailbreaking attacks, which can trigger the generation of harmful or sensitive content

;
Huang et al. 2024a
;
Wei et al. 2024

)
.

Knowledge editing techniques enable the rapid and efficient modification of parameters within LLMs, facilitating the update of outdated knowledge
(
Yao et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
;
Wang et al. 2024d
)
.
These techniques are increasingly being applied to LLM detoxification, showing promising initial results
(
Hu et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024b
)
.
As illustrated in Figure
1
.(a), when a hacker inputs a malicious query like
“What’s the process of smuggling goods across borders?”
, the original model may generate toxic content.
In contrast, the detoxified model can refuse to respond after knowledge editing.
However, most existing knowledge editing methods primarily focus on updating outdated knowledge in LLMs, relying on specific entities to locate target editing regions
(
Meng et al. 2022
;
Meng et al. 2023
;
Fang et al. 2024
)
.
This makes them difficult to apply directly to LLM detoxification tasks, as adversarial inputs in such tasks typically lack specific entities.
Furthermore, current knowledge editing methods suffer from an
over-editing problem
.
As shown in Figure
1
.(b), detoxified models may also decline to answer normal user queries like
“How do I get better at aiming with my slingshot?”
, thereby violating the
helpful
principle of the 3H guidelines.
This raises a critical question:
Can we efficiently detoxify LLMs while safeguarding their general capabilities as much as possible?

To this end, we present a novel method called
ToxEdit
: Safeguarding General Capabilities of LLMs through
Tox
icity-Aware Knowledge
Edit
ing.

ToxEdit
incorporates a toxicity detection mechanism to recognize toxicity within activations during LLMs’ forward propagation.
This detected signal is then transmitted to the inter-layer routing mechanism, which directs the flow to either the original feed-forward network (FFN) or an edited FFN, thereby enabling an adaptive detoxification process.

ToxEdit
not only detoxifies LLMs efficiently but also preserves their general capabilities at a high level.
Extensive experiments demonstrate that
ToxEdit
outperforms existing state-of-the-art knowledge editing methods in both detoxification ability and safeguarding general capabilities.

Moreover, we observed that the benchmark SafeEdit
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
in the LLM detoxification domain lacks comprehensive evaluation metrics.
It primarily uses QA and text summarization datasets to evaluate baselines’ ability to safeguard general capabilities of LLMs.
However, incorporating instruction-following instances into the evaluation is equally crucial, as these instances are most similar to the editing tasks.
They can adequately reflect whether the baseline over-edits.
Consequently, we augmented the benchmark by adding corresponding evaluation metrics.

We summarize the key contributions of our work as follows:

•

We propose
ToxEdit
, a novel knowledge editing method designed to address the over-editing issue prevalent in previous methods within the LLM detoxification domain.

•

We enhance the benchmark evaluation metrics in the LLM detoxification domain by introducing instruction-following instances, which help identify over-editing issues and provide a better assessment of how well each baseline safeguards general capabilities.

•

We conduct extensive experiments across various LLMs and multiple datasets.
The results and analyses demonstrate that
ToxEdit
surpasses existing state-of-the-art baselines in both detoxification ability and safeguarding general capabilities.

2
Methodology

2.1
Task Definition

Given a harmful input
X_{p}
, an LLM
f
generates a response
Y_{unsafe}
that contains toxic content.
In the LLM detoxification task, we edit
f
to produce a modified LLM
f^{\prime}
, which should generate a safe response
Y_{safe}
for
X_{p}
.
At the same time, for a normal input
X_{n}
, the responses generated by
f^{\prime}
and
f
should remain as similar as possible.

Existing knowledge editing methods for detoxifying LLMs may inadvertently alter some critical parameters in the LLM, leading to catastrophic degradation of the model’s general capabilities.
To address this issue, we propose
ToxEdit
, a toxicity-aware knowledge editing method that adaptively detoxifies LLMs based on input queries.

ToxEdit
is designed to preserve the general capabilities of the model as much as possible, ensuring that the edited model can provide helpful responses to harmless user queries, just as it did before the edits, while refusing to respond to malicious queries.
As illustrated in Figure
2
,
ToxEdit
consists of two main modules: the
Semantic Profiling for Toxicity Detection Module
and the
Anti-Toxic Feed-Forward Module
.

2.2
Semantic Profiling for Toxicity Detection Module

Recent studies have shown that during the early stages of forward propagation, LLMs can produce distinct hidden states for malicious and harmless inputs based on ethical concepts learned during pretraining
(
Zhou et al. 2024
;
Lin et al. 2023
)
.
This indicates that the model inherently has the ability to discern whether a user’s prompt is malicious.

We abstract the presence of toxicity in the hidden states of the LLM as a binary classification problem and utilize a trained linear kernel support vector machine (SVM)
(
Cortes 1995
)
from sklearn
(
Pedregosa et al. 2011
)
as the classifier.
Given that auto-regressive LLMs generate the next token based on the last position of the last hidden states, we use the hidden state
h_{l}^{(n)}
at the last position
n
of the
l
-th layer as the input to the binary classifier.
This position most effectively reflects the LLM’s understanding of the input sequence.
Then we obtain the toxicity detection result
R_{l}
:

R_{l}=classifier_{\sigma}(h_{l}^{(n)}),

(1)

where
\sigma
is the parameter of the classifier.
For SVM, the output results are +1 or -1, representing harmful and harmless samples, respectively.
This toxicity detection result is sent as a signal to the router before the FFN layer, which alters the flow of data.

In order to enable the classifier to fully recognize the features of harmful and harmless samples and determine the optimal layer
l^{\prime}
for classifier insertion, we construct 4,000 harmful prompts and 2,000 harmless prompts from the train set of SafeEdit
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
.
The harmful prompts consist of two categories: one category contains malicious individual questions, and the other category contains jailbreak prompts carefully crafted for LLMs.
For each prompt
P
, we add a prefix system prompt
S
to allow the model to recognize unsafe requests and generate appropriate responses.
Finally, input
X=[x_{1},...,x_{n}]=[S;P]
is fed into the LLM to extract the hidden states
h_{l}^{(n)}
at the last position
n
of layer
l
.
We combine
h_{l}^{(n)}
with the labels
a_{x}
indicating whether the corresponding original prompt is harmful to form a training set
T=\{(h_{l}^{(n)},a_{x})\}
, which is then used to train the classifier at layer
l
.
The training performance is evaluated on the validation set.
Given the class imbalance between positive and negative samples, we use the
F1-score
as the evaluation metric.
Intuitively, the better the classifier performs, the stronger the LLM’s ability to distinguish between toxic and safe requests at that layer.
Therefore, we select the layer
l^{\prime}
corresponding to the classifier with the highest
F1-score
as the insertion layer.

2.3
Anti-Toxic Feed-Forward Module

An LLM
f
is fundamentally structured as a parameterized function comprising an embedding matrix
E\in\mathbb{R}^{|V|\times d}
and
L
cascaded Transformer layers.
Each layer
l\in\{1,...,L\}
contains a multi-head attention mechanism (
Att
l
) and a feed-forward network (
FFN
l
).
The FFN constitutes two-thirds of the model parameters
(
Geva et al. 2021
)
.
Given an input sequence
X
, the model executes the following computational workflow (bias omitted):

h_{0}=E(X);h_{l}=\textit{FFN}_{l}(\textit{Att}_{l}(h_{l-1})),

(2)

\textit{FFN}_{l}(x)=h_{l}^{down}W_{l}^{V}=\sigma(x^{\mathsf{T}}W_{l}^{K})W_{l}^{V},

(3)

where
h_{l}
denotes the hidden states at layer
l
,
\sigma
is a nonlinear activation function (e.g. SwiGLU, GeLU),
W_{l}^{K}
and
W_{l}^{V}
denote the first and second multi-layer perceptron (MLP) layers within the FFN respectively, and
h_{l}^{down}
represents the activation
values of the first MLP layer.

Following previous work
(
Meng et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2024c
;
Wang et al. 2024b
)
, since
W_{l^{\prime}}^{V}
governs the final output of the
l^{\prime}
-th layer and plays a pivotal role in knowledge dissemination throughout the forward propagation, we designate
W_{l^{\prime}}^{V}
as the target for detoxification.
To prevent degradation of the model’s general capabilities caused by altering the original parameters, we create a duplicate copy of
W_{l^{\prime}}^{V}
for editing purposes.
We utilize a harmful prompt
P
and its corresponding safe response
Y_{\text{safe}}
as an input-output pair to perform
T
-step editing while freezing all parameters of the model
f
.
Taking the
t
-th editing step as an example, our objective is to enhance the model’s probability of generating safe outputs under the prefix system safety prompt
S
:

\mathcal{L}=-\log P_{\mathcal{W}^{t}}(Y_{\text{safe}}|[S;P]),

(4)

where
\mathcal{W}^{t}
is the parameters of
f
at
t
-th timestep.
Note that unlike DINM
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
, we do not need to impose constraints on maintaining normal responses under harmless prompts for the optimization objective.
This is because the router described in Section
2.2
only directs identified toxic information flows to the detoxified FFN.
We believe this design enables the module to focus specifically on detoxification tasks.

After
T
-step editing, we obtain the detoxified matrix
W_{l^{\prime}}^{V*}
and integrate it with matrix
W_{l^{\prime}}^{K}
to form the anti-toxic feed-forward module, which governs the elimination of toxic information.
Based on the classifier’s output
s
, the router dynamically directs data flows to different FFN layers, thereby achieving adaptive detoxification of user inputs:

h_{l^{\prime}+1}=\begin{cases}h_{l^{\prime}}^{down}W_{l^{\prime}}^{V},\text{if }s\text{ is safe};\\
h_{l^{\prime}}^{down}W_{l^{\prime}}^{V*},\text{if }s\text{ is unsafe}.\end{cases}

(5)

3.1
Experimental Setup

LLMs & Baseline methods.

Our experiments are conducted on three mainstream chat models: LLaMA3-8B-Instruct
(
Dubey et al. 2024
)
, LLaMA2-7B-Chat
(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
, and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2
(
Jiang et al. 2023
)
.
We select three existing knowledge editing methods as baselines: FT-L
(
Meng et al. 2022
)
, WISE
(
Wang et al. 2024c
)
, and DINM
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
.
Other common knowledge editing approaches such as ROME
(
Meng et al. 2022
)
and MEMIT
(
Meng et al. 2023
)
require locating specific model regions based on knowledge entities for parameter modification, hence are not suitable for LLM detoxification tasks.
See Appendix
A.1
for more details.

Datasets.

We conducted experiments using the EasyEdit
(
Wang et al. 2023
)
framework on the SafeEdit benchmark
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
.
Notably, for all methods involving training modules, we employed the training and validation sets for model development, while evaluation was performed on the test set.
Toxicity detection utilized Classifier
C
provided by the dataset organizers, as detailed in Appendix
A.2
and
A.3
.

Evaluation Metrics.

From the knowledge editing perspective, we assessed method performance across three dimensions: Efficacy, Generalization, and Specificity.
The first two dimensions aim to evaluate the detoxification effectiveness of the applied method, while the last dimension measures the retention level of model general capabilities post-editing.
We introduced an additional metric
DL
(Defense Locality) under Specificity to better quantify the model’s compliance with harmless instructions before and after editing:

\textsc{DL}=\mathbb{E}_{q_{n}\sim Q_{n}}\{\textit{Sim}(f_{W^{\prime}}([S;q_{n}]),f_{W}([S;q_{n}]))\}

(6)

Here,
Q_{n}
denotes the harmless query set,
f_{W}
and
f_{W^{\prime}}
represent the original and edited models, respectively, and
Sim(·)
calculates semantic similarity scores.
Intuitively, closer alignment between the outputs of
f_{W}
and
f_{W^{\prime}}
indicates better performance of the edited model on instruction-following tasks.
At the same time, we also adjusted the
Fluency
metric by using
n-gram
to evaluate the fluency of LLM responses to safe requests.
We believe these adjustments can help the SafeEdit benchmark assess the general capabilities of edited LLMs from a new perspective.
Detailed definitions of these metrics are provided in Appendix
A.4
.

More implementation details are provided in Appendix
A.5
.

ToxEdit
demonstrates exceptional detoxification efficacy and strong generalization capabilities for LLMs.

As shown in Table
1
, on the detoxification effectiveness metric (DS),
ToxEdit
significantly improves performance compared to previous state-of-the-art baselines: enhancing the score from 82.89% to 97.78% on LLaMA3-8B-Instruct, from 97.70% to 99.55% on LLaMA2-7B-Chat, and from 81.61% to 91.63% on Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2.
These results demonstrate
ToxEdit
’s adaptability across various LLM architectures and its ability to achieve more thorough detoxification.
Simultaneously,
ToxEdit
exhibits outstanding performance on the detoxification generalization metric (DG-Avg), attaining approximately 98% effectiveness across all three models.
This indicates that
ToxEdit
can flexibly handle diverse malicious prompts, including single malicious instruction attacks and role-playing attacks, while maintaining high generalization performance across different model architectures.

ToxEdit
effectively preserves model general capabilities.

On the defense locality metric (DL),
ToxEdit
substantially outperforms all baselines, achieving an average performance of approximately 95% while maintaining high text fluency.
This demonstrates
ToxEdit
’s success in preserving LLMs’ instruction-following capabilities and linguistic competence.
Although slight performance degradation was observed in QA tasks and text summarization tasks for certain models, the results remain within acceptable margins.
We hypothesize this stems from the models’ refusal to respond to certain queries in these tasks.

ToxEdit
achieves optimal balance between detoxification and capability retention.

Previous knowledge-editing methods for LLM detoxification reveal inherent trade-offs: While approaches like DINM effectively detoxify models but struggle to preserve general capabilities, FT-L conversely maintains relatively high general performance but shows limited detoxification effectiveness.
Benefitting from its adaptive detoxification mechanism,
ToxEdit
achieves state-of-the-art performance in both dimensions, establishing a new paradigm for balanced model sanitization.

3.3
Ablation Study

We conducted ablation studies on the toxicity detection module, prefix system safety prompt, and sample types of classifier’s training data in LLaMA3-8B-Instruct, with the results shown in Table
2
.

Semantic profiling in the toxicity detection module is essential to maintain the model’s overall abilities.

In our experiments, we removed the toxicity detection module and replaced the original FFN with modified FFN layers.
This change slightly improved detoxification performance by 0–2%.
However, it greatly damaged the model’s general capabilities.
Instruction-following capability dropped by 88.65% (DL metric), text fluency decreased by 5.6 points, and QA as well as summarization abilities also declined moderately.
This result shows that changing part of an LLM’s internal parameters can lead to issues similar to catastrophic forgetting.
Our proposed toxicity detection module uses a dynamic routing mechanism.
It successfully detoxifies the model while preserving its overall capabilities.

The prefix system safety prompt and diverse training data samples both improve detoxification performance.

In our experiments, we removed the prefix safety prompt, the artificially constructed jailbreak samples, or samples with only a single harmful query from the classifier training data.
Each removal led to a drop in detoxification effectiveness and general capabilities.
We believe that system prompts help the model recognize toxicity in user inputs.
Diverse training data also increases classifier accuracy and reduces misjudgment risks.

3.4
Analysis

What is the impact of training sample size on the classifier in the toxicity detection module?

In Section
2.2
, we used 4,000 harmful samples and 2,000 harmless samples for training.

At which layer of LLMs should the toxicity detection module be deployed?

For the three LLMs mentioned in Section

Interestingly, deploying it between layers 10 and 15 consistently yielded the best performance, with an

Case Study

We conducted a case study on the SafeEdit test set, showcasing the output of different baselines on LLaMA3-8B-Instruct for both malicious and normal prompts, as shown in Table

.
We expect an effective approach to reject malicious prompts from users while still providing appropriate responses to normal prompts.
In other words, it should satisfy the 3H principles mentioned in Section

.

We observed that the base model followed the malicious prompts and generated toxic content, while it responded normally to the normal prompts.
FT-L and WISE did not generate toxic content but instead repeatedly produced meaningless characters or sentences.
Moreover, when faced with normal prompts, they failed to provide users with effective suggestions, violating the
helpful

This issue was even more pronounced in DINM, which explicitly rejected reasonable user requests.
These findings indicate that previous knowledge-editing methods applied to LLM detoxification can severely compromise the model’s language capabilities and adherence to instructions.
However, during evaluation, such outputs are still judged as safe, which inspires us to assess the effectiveness of a method from more dimensions.

Unlike the aforementioned methods,
ToxEdit
successfully rejected responses to malicious prompts while providing effective suggestions for normal prompts with fluent and coherent language.
This fully demonstrates
ToxEdit
’s ability to adaptively detoxify user inputs.

4
Related Work

4.1
Traditional Detoxification Methods

Research on detoxification for LLMs can be categorized into three types: parameter optimization, toxicity detection enhancement, and prompt engineering.
Parameter optimization refers to directly enhancing the model’s safety by adjusting a large number of model parameters.
For example, methods like supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
(
Zhang et al. 2024c
)
, reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF)
(
Wang et al. 2024a
)
, and direct preference optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al. 2024
)
, which require high-quality annotated data for fine-tuning.
Toxicity detection enhancement aims to integrate input and output detection mechanisms to ensure the model generates safe responses
(
Zhang and Wan 2023
;
Hallinan et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
.
Prompt engineering involves carefully designing diverse prompts to help LLMs reject malicious inputs
(
Meade et al. 2023
;
Zheng et al. 2025
)
.
Compared to traditional detoxification methods, knowledge editing methods have the advantage of being less dependent on data, requiring little or no modification of parameters, and involving minimal editing overhead.

4.2
Knowledge Editing in LLMs

Knowledge editing aims to efficiently adjust a model’s behavior on target tasks while preserving its original performance on other tasks
(
Yao et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2024b
;
Wang et al. 2024d
)
.
Existing knowledge editing methods can be divided into two categories: methods that retain model parameters, such as SERAC
(
Mitchell et al. 2022b
)
, T-patcher
(
Huang et al. 2023
)
, IKE
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
, and WISE
(
Wang et al. 2024c
)
, and methods that modify model parameters, such as MEND
(
Mitchell et al. 2022a
)
, ROME
(
Meng et al. 2022
)
, MEMIT
(
Meng et al. 2023
)
and AlphaEdit
(
Fang et al. 2024
)
.
Recently, an increasing number of works have applied knowledge editing to the detoxification of LLMs.
Ext-Sub
(
Hu et al. 2024
)
combines the strengths of expert and anti-expert models, extracting deficiency capabilities from the anti-expert by leveraging the general capabilities.
DEPN
(
Wu et al. 2023
)
uses a privacy neuron detector to locate neurons related to privacy and sets their activations to zero to edit them.
DINM
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
locates toxic regions within LLMs through contextual semantics and then eliminates information from these toxic regions.
However, these knowledge editing methods either rely on specific knowledge entities for editing or struggle to balance detoxification effectiveness with the preservation of the model’s general capabilities
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
.
Our work
ToxEdit
, employs a semantic analyzer to perceive toxicity in the hidden states of LLMs.
Then, it utilizes a routing mechanism to direct data flow to either the original or edited FFN for adaptive detoxification, improving LLMs’ safety while maximizing the retention of its general capabilities.

5
Conclusion

We present
ToxEdit
, a novel toxicity-aware knowledge editing method that effectively mitigates harmful outputs in LLMs while safeguarding general capabilities.
By detecting toxic activation patterns and leveraging adaptive inter-layer pathways, our approach overcomes the limitations of existing methods, addressing both entity-agnostic attacks and over-editing issues.
Additionally, we enhance the SafeEdit benchmark to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of over-editing.
Experimental results demonstrate that
ToxEdit
achieves superior detoxification performance while maintaining LLM’s general capabilities.

Limitations

Larger LLMs.

Due to computational resource limitations, we only trained and tested on LLMs of 7B parameters.
We have demonstrated that our method works on three mainstream models: LLaMA3-8B-Instruct, LLaMA2-7B-Chat, and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2.
In the future, we plan to validate our method on other model series and larger parameter models.

Choice of Classifier.

In this paper, our classifier is implemented using a support vector machine (SVM), which achieved good performance on SafeEdit.
However, due to time constraints, we have not tested other model architectures (such as MLPs) that could also serve as classifiers.
It is uncertain whether using alternative classifiers would improve the performance of our method.
We leave this for future work.

Repetition Issue.

Although the issue of repetition has been improved and the sentences generated are more fluent,
ToxEdit
still has a probability of generating repeated sentences in certain cases within the dataset.
In the future, we will explore new improvement methods to mitigate this issue.

Ethics Statement

In this paper, we aim to mitigate the risks of generating harmful content in LLMs.
As mentioned in the paper, one potential ethical risk is that the datasets we use may contain toxic contexts.
Although the use of these harmful contexts is intended to promote defenses against adversarial inputs, there is still a possibility of malicious exploitation.
To mitigate these risks, our attack prompts are almost entirely sourced from publicly available attack prompts and have undergone strict manual review to ensure no new ethical risks are introduced.
Overall, our work provides a solution for the detoxification of LLMs in both research and practical applications.

•

•

DINM

(

)
detects the toxic layer by maximizing semantic differences between safe and unsafe responses to adversarial inputs.
It then fine-tunes this layer using the inputs and safe responses while preserving general knowledge.

(

(
Fang et al. 2024

They rely on the subject to determine the editing region.
However, in the LLM detoxification task, the subject in adversarial inputs is often ambiguous, making these baselines unsuitable for direct application.

SafeEdit is a benchmark dataset specifically designed for detoxifying LLMs through knowledge editing.
It encompasses nine categories of unsafe content and includes various potent attack templates to systematically evaluate the safety of LLMs when handling potentially harmful queries.

The dataset comprises 4,050 training instances, 2,700 validation instances, and 1,350 test instances.

The introduction of SafeEdit provides a standardized framework for evaluating and improving the safety of LLMs.
By utilizing SafeEdit, researchers can systematically assess the effectiveness of various detoxification methods, ensuring that LLMs remain safe and robust when handling potentially harmful queries.
