Comparative Analysis of Hall Effect Implementations in Hall-Magnetohydrodynamics
Kazunari Iwasaki, Kengo Tomida

Abstract
There is no standard numerical implementation of the Hall effect, which is one of the non-ideal magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) effects. Numerical instability arises when a simple implementation is used, in which the Hall electric field is added to the electric field to update magnetic fields without further modifications to the numerical scheme. In this paper, several implementations proposed in the literature are compared to identify an approach that provides stable and accurate results. We consider two types of implementations of the Hall effect. One is a modified version of the Harten-Lax-van Leer method (Hall-HLL) in which the phase speeds of whistler waves are adopted as the signal speeds; the other involves adding a fourth-order hyper-resistivity to a Hall-MHD code. Based on an extensive series of test calculations, we found that hyper-resistivity yields more accurate results than the Hall-HLL, particularly in problems where the whisler-wave timescale is shorter than the the timescale of physical processes of interest. Through both von Neumann stability analysis and numerical experiments, an appropriate coefficient for the hyper-resistivity is determined.

I
Introduction

The Hall effect is one of the non-ideal magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) effects.
It occurs in situations where
electrons are coupled to magnetic fields, while
ions are decoupled.
The characteristics of the Hall effect differ between
fully ionized and weakly ionized gases
[
33
]
.

An intriguing property of the Hall effect is that
it modifies the dispersion relation of linear MHD waves, leading
to generation of whistler and ion-cyclotron waves.
In the long-wavelength limit, these waves transition into
Alfvén waves.
The phase speed of whistler waves increases proportionally to the wavenumber,
whereas that of ion-cyclotron waves remains constant at higher
wavenumbers.

Numerically implementing the Hall effect presents significant
challenges, unlike
other non-ideal MHD effects
(Ohmic resistivity and ambipolar diffusion),
which are relatively simple to implement.
One reason is that the Hall effect causes waves to be dispersive.
In particular, as whistler waves with shorter wavelengths propagate faster,
grid-scale disturbances in magnetic fields can become significant under certain conditions.

A simple implementation of the Hall effect
involves adding the Hall electric field to the
electric field to update the magnetic field without further modifications to the numerical scheme.

Falle [11]
conducted a von Neumann stability analysis of such a naive
implementation and found it to be unconditionally unstable when
using a first-order explicit time integrator (forward Euler integrator).

The stability of this simple implementation
depends on the accuracy of the time integrator
[
20
]
.
While third-order time integrators are conditionally stable,
second-order time integrators may lead to numerical instability.

Lesur et al. [22]
confirmed that a third-order Runge-Kutta (RK3) time integrator
suppresses numerical instabilities in the nonlinear development of Hall-dominated magnetorotational instability
using
SNOOPY
, an incompressible pseudo-spectral code.
However, no studies have shown that the RK3 integrator, combined with the simple implementation, provides stable results when using Godunov-type schemes with the constrained transport (CT) methods
[
10
]
,
which is a popular combination used in many MHD simulation codes, such as

Stone et al. [35, Athena++]
,

Fromang et al. [12, RAMSES]
,

Mignone et al. [27, Pluto]
,

Collins et al. [7, Enzo]
.

Another issue with the simple implementation is that
it does not provide numerical dissipation because
the Hall electric field is oriented
perpendicular to the electric current.
This lack of numerical dissipation causes serious problems
in magnetic reconnection driven by the Hall effect
[
24
, e.g.,]
.

Several numerical methods have been proposed to suppress numerical
instabilities caused by the Hall effect.

Tóth et al. [37]

and
Lesur et al. [22]
suggested modifying
the signal speeds in the Harten-Lax-van Leer
[
18
, HLL,]
numerical fluxes
by considering the phase speed of whistler waves to estimate
the signal speeds of the two characteristics.
This method is called Hall-HLL and
is widely used in numerical simulations
of star and planet formation
[
3
,
2
,
25
]
.

Marchand et al. [26]
proposed a
modified Hall-HLL method to
improve the conservation of angular momentum in collapsing dense molecular cloud cores.

Alternative approaches for modifying time integration methods
have also been proposed.

Falle [11]
demonstrated that an implicit method stabilizes Hall-MHD.
Furthermore,

Tóth et al. [37]
showed that an implicit Hall-MHD scheme is stable even without
modifications to the numerical fluxes.

O’Sullivan & Downes [30]
,
O’Sullivan & Downes [31]

found that the Hall-MHD stability is achieved when
the magnetic field is updated using a dimensionally split method
[
1
, also see]
.

Another approach involves introducing artificial resistivity
into the induction equation.
For instance,
hyper-resistivity is added to damp whistler waves
with wavelengths comparable to the grid scale in
numerical simulations of magnetic reconnection involving the Hall effect

[
4
,
23
,
5
,
40
, e.g.,]
.
However, an appropriate choice of the hyper-resistivity
coefficient has not been thoroughly examined.
Recently,
Zier et al. [42]
proposed
a method in which
the diffusion coefficient of Ohmic resistivity is
artificially increased to stabilize the schemes.

As mentioned earlier,
various implementations of the Hall effect have been proposed in the literature.
However, comprehensive comparisons of their stability and accuracy have not yet been conducted.
In this paper, we compare the results of Hall-HLL
[
22
]
,
a modified version of Hall-HLL
[
26
]
, and
hyper-resistivity
[
4
]
.

This paper is organized as follows:
Section
II
reviews the basic properties of Hall-MHD and
describe the implementations considered in this paper.
Section
III
presents numerical experiments.

II
Basic Properties of Hall-MHD and Its Numerical Implementations

II.1
Basic Equations

The basic equations of non-ideal MHD are given by

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\rho v_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}=0,

(1)

\frac{\partial\rho{v}_{i}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}(\rho v_{i}v_{j}+{\cal T}_{ij})=0,

(2)

\frac{\partial E}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left\{Ev_{i}+{\cal T}_{ij}v_{j}+(\bm{E}_{\mathrm{ni}}\times\bm{B})_{i}\right\}=0,

(3)

and

\frac{\partial\bm{B}}{\partial t}+\bm{\nabla}\times(-\bm{v}\times\bm{B}+\bm{E}_{\mathrm{ni}})=0,

(4)

where
\rho
is the density,
\bm{v}
is the velocity,
\bm{B}
is the magnetic field,

P
is the pressure,
and
E=\rho\bm{v}^{2}/2+P/(\gamma-1)+\bm{B}^{2}/8\pi
represents the
total energy per unit volume.

{\cal T}
denotes the stress tensor, given by

{\cal T}_{ij}=\left(P+\frac{B^{2}}{8\pi}\right)\delta_{ij}-\frac{B_{i}B_{j}}{4\pi}.

(5)

\bm{E}_{\mathrm{ni}}
represents the electric field resulting
from non-ideal MHD effects, expressed as

\bm{E}_{\mathrm{ni}}=\frac{4\pi}{c}\left(\eta_{\mathrm{O}}\bm{J}+\eta_{\mathrm{H}}\frac{\bm{J}\times\bm{B}}{|\bm{B}|}+\eta_{\mathrm{A}}\bm{J}_{\perp}\right),

(6)

\bm{J}=(c/4\pi)\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{B}
is the electric current
density, and

\bm{J}_{\perp}=\bm{J}-(\bm{J}\cdot\bm{B})/|\bm{B}|
is the
\bm{J}
components
perpendicular to the local magnetic field direction.

\eta_{\mathrm{O}}
,
\eta_{\mathrm{H}}
, and
\eta_{\mathrm{A}}
correspond to
the diffusion coefficients for
Ohmic resistivity, the Hall effect, and ambipolar diffusion, respectively.
In this study, Ohmic resistivity and ambipolar diffusion are not considered
because the focus is on implementations that ensure stability and accuracy even with
\eta_{\mathrm{O}}=\eta_{\mathrm{A}}=0
.

II.2
Review of Properties of Linear Waves in Hall MHD

Although linear Hall-MHD wave propagation tests
are widely used to evaluate various methods,
most studies focus primarily on incompressible waves
propagating along the unperturbed magnetic field.
Thus, it remains unclear whether these methods can
accurately capture other types of linear waves.
In this study, we assess performance of
various methods on all linear waves in Section
III.4
.
This section provides a brief review of the physical properties of the linear waves in Hall-MHD

[
17
, e.g.,]
.

We consider a uniform, static gas with a density of
\rho_{0}
and a uniform magnetic field of
\bm{B}_{0}

as the unperturbed state.
The sound speed and Alfvén speed in the unperturbed state
are denoted by
c_{\mathrm{s}}
and
c_{\mathrm{A}}
, respectively.
We analyze perturbations proportional
to
e^{i(\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}-\omega t)}
, where
\bm{k}
is the wavenumber vector
and
\omega
is the angular frequency.
Linearizing Equations (
1
)-(
4
) with
\eta_{\mathrm{O}}=\eta_{\mathrm{A}}=0
yields
the following dispersion relation:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\omega}{c_{\mathrm{A}}k}\right)^{6}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left\{\tilde{c}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}+1+\cos^{2}\theta+(kL_{\mathrm{H}})^{2}\cos^{2}\theta\right\}\left(\frac{\omega}{c_{\mathrm{A}}k}\right)^{4}

(7)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left\{\tilde{c}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(kL_{\mathrm{H}})^{2}\cos^{2}\theta+(2\tilde{c}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}+1)\cos^{2}\theta\right\}\left(\frac{\omega}{c_{\mathrm{A}}k}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\tilde{c}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\cos^{4}\theta=0,

where
\tilde{c}_{\mathrm{s}}=c_{\mathrm{s}}/c_{\mathrm{A}}
,
k=|\bm{k}|
,
and
\theta
is the angle between

\bm{k}
and
\bm{B}_{0}
.
The Hall scale,
L_{\mathrm{H}}
, is defined as

L_{\mathrm{H}}=\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{H}}}{c_{\mathrm{A}}}

(8)

[
33
]
.
For a fully ionized plasma,
L_{\mathrm{H}}
corresponds to the ion skin depth.
When
kL_{\mathrm{H}}>1
, the dispersion relation deviates from that of ideal MHD
due to the Hall effect.

Figure 1:

Phase speeds of the fast-wave branch,
Alfvén-wave branch, and slow-wave branch
as a function of the wavenumber for
\tilde{c}_{\mathrm{s}}=1/2
and
\theta=\pi/4
.
The horizontal line represents the sound speed.
The black circles correspond to the six models
used in the convergence test conducted in Section
III.4
.

Figure
1
shows the dispersion relation for
\tilde{c}_{\mathrm{s}}=1/2
and
\theta=\pi/4
.
As in ideal MHD, three branches appear.
In ascending order of phase speeds,
they are referred to as the
“slow-wave branch”, “Alfvén-wave branch”, and “fast-wave branch”.
In the low wavenumber limit,
kL_{\mathrm{H}}\ll 1
,
the slow-wave branch, Alfvén-wave branch, and fast-wave
branch correspond to the slow, Alfvén, and fast waves, respectively.

For
kL_{\mathrm{H}}\gg 1
, the phase speeds of the fast-wave and slow-wave branches
are no longer constant with respect to the wavenumber
due to the Hall effect.
The fast-wave branch corresponds to whistler waves,
for which the phase speed is

\frac{\omega}{c_{\mathrm{A}}k}\sim kL_{\mathrm{H}}\cos\theta.

(9)

Whistler waves are right-hand circularly polarized.
The magnetic field perturbations oscillate rapidly, while
the gas remains nearly static.
The velocity perturbations are negligible compared to
the magnetic field perturbations divided by
\sqrt{4\pi\rho_{0}}
,
where
\rho_{0}
is the unperturbed density.

For
kL_{\mathrm{H}}\gg 1
, the slow-wave branch asymptotically
approaches the ion-cyclotron wave, for which the
phase speed is given by

\frac{\omega}{c_{\mathrm{A}}k}\sim\left(kL_{\mathrm{H}}\right)^{-1}\cos\theta.

(10)

Ion-cyclotron waves, which are left-hand circularly
polarized, cannot oscillate at frequencies higher than the
ion-cyclotron frequency, which is given by
c_{\mathrm{A}}/L_{\mathrm{H}}
for
\theta=0
.
Unlike whistler waves,
velocity perturbations dominate relative to
magnetic field perturbations divided by
\sqrt{4\pi\rho_{0}}
.

As
kL_{\mathrm{H}}
increases,
the Alfvén-wave branch transitions from
an Alfvén wave to a sound wave around
kL_{\mathrm{H}}\sim 1
.
Thus, in the Hall-dominated regime (
kL_{\mathrm{H}}\gg 1
),
the compressible mode (sound wave) decouples from
the incompressible modes (whistler and ion-cyclotron waves).

For
\tilde{c}_{\mathrm{s}}=1/2
,
a mode exchange occurs around
kL_{\mathrm{H}}\sim 1
between
the Alfvén-wave and slow-wave branches.
The Alfvén-wave (slow-wave)
branch transitions from an incompressible (compressible) state
to a compressible (incompressble) state.
For gases with
\tilde{c}_{\mathrm{s}}
larger than unity,
a similar mode exchange occurs
between the fast-wave and Alfvén-wave branches.

II.3
Implementations of Hall Effect Considered in this Study

A simple implementation of the Hall effect
involves adding
the Hall electric field to the
electric field used to update the magnetic field
without further modifications to the numerical scheme
(see Section
II.4
).
As mentioned in Section
I
,
a third-order time integrator is conditionally stable against
infinitesimally small perturbations, whereas
first- and second-order time integrators
are unstable
[
11
,
20
]
.
As demonstrated in Section
III.1
,
Even with a third-order time integrator,
nonlinear numerical instabilities arise near
the Nyquist wavelength when perturbation amplitudes are large.

To suppress numerical instabilities,
various implementations have been proposed in the literature.
In this section, in addition to the simple implementation,
we introduce three methods for comparison in this study.
The implementation details of these methods
in
Athena++

are described in Section
II.4
.

II.3.1
Hall-HLL

Since the Hall effect modifies the phase speeds of linear waves (Section
III.4
),
it is natural to modify the Riemann solver used to compute
the numerical fluxes.
The Hall effect makes the phase speeds of waves to
be wavenumber-dependent
(Figure
1
).
It is challenging to construct a Riemann solver incorporating
the Hall effect
because self-similarity no longer holds in the Riemann problem.
Among various approximate Riemann solvers, the HLL method
[
18
]

can be applied to any problem by setting signal speeds appropriately.

Lesur et al. [22]
modified the HLL numerical flux
by incorporating the phase speed of whistler waves.
This method is referred to as
Hall-HLL
.

In the HLL scheme
[
18
]
, by
considering two waves propagating at
the signal speeds
S_{\mathrm{L}}
and
S_{\mathrm{R}}

from the initial discontinuity at the cell boundary,
the numerical flux at the cell boundary
is constructed as follows:

\bm{F}_{\mathrm{HLL}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\bm{F}_{\mathrm{L}}&S_{\mathrm{L}}>0\\
\bm{F}_{\mathrm{R}}&S_{\mathrm{R}}<0\\
\displaystyle\frac{S_{\mathrm{R}}S_{\mathrm{L}}(\bm{U}_{\mathrm{R}}-\bm{U}_{\mathrm{L}})+S_{\mathrm{R}}\bm{F}_{\mathrm{L}}-S_{\mathrm{L}}\bm{F}_{\mathrm{R}}}{S_{\mathrm{R}}-S_{\mathrm{L}}}&\mathrm{otherwise}\\
\end{array}\right.,

(11)

where
\bm{U}_{\mathrm{L}}
and
\bm{U}_{\mathrm{R}}
are the conserved variables
of the left and right states, and

\bm{F}_{\mathrm{L}}
and
\bm{F}_{\mathrm{R}}
are the corresponding fluxes.
Expressions of the signal speeds
S_{\mathrm{L}}
and
S_{\mathrm{R}}
are replaced with

S_{\mathrm{L}}=\min(v_{\mathrm{L}}-c_{\mathrm{max,L}},v_{\mathrm{R}}-c_{\mathrm{max,R}})

(12)

and

S_{\mathrm{R}}=\max(v_{\mathrm{L}}+c_{\mathrm{max,L}},v_{\mathrm{R}}+c_{\mathrm{max,R}}),

(13)

respectively
[
8
]
, where
v_{\mathrm{L}}
and
v_{\mathrm{R}}

are the normal velocities of left and right states, respectively.

c_{\mathrm{max,L}}
(
c_{\mathrm{max,R}}
) is the maximum phase speed

c_{\mathrm{max}}
of the left (right) state.
In ideal MHD, the phase speed of fast waves
c_{\mathrm{f}}
is assigned to
c_{\mathrm{max}}
.
Note that there are other options for the expressions of

S_{\mathrm{L,R}}

[
9
, e.g.,]
.

In
Hall-HLL
,
c_{\mathrm{max}}
is given by

c_{\mathrm{max}}=\max(c_{\mathrm{f}},c_{\mathrm{w}}(k_{\mathrm{max}})),

(14)

where
c_{\mathrm{w}}
is the phase speed of whistler waves propagating along the magnetic field,

c_{\mathrm{w}}(k)=\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{H}}k}{2}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{H}}k}{2}\right)^{2}+c_{\mathrm{A}}^{2}},

(15)

and
k_{\mathrm{max}}
is the maximum wavenumber that can be resolved in numerical simulations.

Various values of
k_{\mathrm{max}}
were used in
previous studies.

Lesur et al. [22]
and
Bai & Stone [2]
adopted

k_{\mathrm{max}}=\Delta x^{-1}
, while

Marchand et al. [25]
employed
a larger value of
k_{\mathrm{max}}=\pi\Delta x^{-1}
that corresponds to
the Nyquist wavenumber.
In this study,
k_{\mathrm{max}}=\Delta x^{-1}
is adopted because
the results of
Lesur et al. [22]
and
Bai & Stone [2]
demonstrate
that
k_{\mathrm{max}}=\Delta x^{-1}
is sufficiently large to suppress numerical instabilities caused by the Hall effect.

The numerical dissipation introduced by
Hall-HLL
is estimated from the induction equation.
For simplicity, we consider a one-dimensional problem where
the Hall effect dominates (
c_{\mathrm{w}}(\Delta x^{-1})\gg c_{\mathrm{f}}
) and
the flow speed is significantly lower than
c_{\mathrm{w}}(\Delta x^{-1})
.
From Equations (
12
), (
13
), and (
14
),
the signal speeds are given by
S_{\mathrm{L}}=-c_{\mathrm{w}}(\Delta x^{-1})
and

S_{\mathrm{R}}=c_{\mathrm{w}}(\Delta x^{-1})
.

Tóth et al. [37]
derived the following equation:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial\bm{B}_{\perp}}{\partial t}\right)_{\text{\sc Hall-HLL}}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle-\frac{c_{\mathrm{w}}(\Delta x^{-1})\Delta x^{3}}{8}\frac{\partial^{4}\bm{B}_{\perp}}{\partial x^{4}}

(16)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle-\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{H}}\Delta x^{2}}{8}\frac{\partial^{4}\bm{B}_{\perp}}{\partial x^{4}},

where Equation (
15
) is applied in the limit
\eta_{\mathrm{H}}\Delta x^{-1}\gg c_{\mathrm{A}}
.

II.3.2
Modified Hall-HLL

Marchand et al. [25]
found that
the use of
Hall-HLL
significantly violates
angular momentum conservation in
simulations of gravitational collapse of dense cores in Cartesian coordinates.

A modified version of
Hall-HLL
was
proposed by
Marchand et al. [26]
.
This method is referred to as
Hall-HLLmod
in this paper.
In
Hall-HLLmod
,
Equation (
14
) is exclusively used to
compute the numerical fluxes for the magnetic field.
For other components of the numerical fluxes
used to update the hydrodynamic variables
(
\rho,\rho\bm{v},E
),
the original HLL numerical flux is applied.
It has been found that
Hall-HLLmod
significantly improves
angular momentum conservation in collapsing dense cores.

II.3.3
Hyper Resistivity

Hyper resistivity is introduced into the electric field as follows:

\bm{E}_{\mathrm{hyp}}=-\frac{4\pi}{c}\eta_{\mathrm{hyp}}\bm{\nabla}^{2}\bm{J},

(17)

where
\eta_{\mathrm{hyp}}
is a coefficient

[
4
,
23
]
.
The coefficient
\eta_{\mathrm{hyp}}
must be sufficiently
large to suppress
numerical instabilities but small enough to ensure accurate results.

To ensure that the time step limitation imposed by hyper-resistivity is less restrictive than that imposed by the Hall effect for any
\Delta x
and
\eta_{\mathrm{H}}
,
the coefficient is defined as follows:

\eta_{\mathrm{hyp}}=C_{\mathrm{hyp}}\eta_{\mathrm{H}}\Delta x^{2},

(18)

where
C_{\mathrm{hyp}}
is a free parameter.

Chacón & Knoll [5]
employed
a similar coefficient to develop
a two-dimensional implicit Hall-MHD solver.
A possible range of
C_{\mathrm{hyp}}
is estimated in Section
III.2.2
.

In a one-dimensional problem,
the dissipation term due to the hyper resistivity is expressed as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial\bm{B}_{\perp}}{\partial t}\right)_{\text{\sc hyp-resis}}=-C_{\mathrm{hyp}}\eta_{\mathrm{H}}\Delta x^{2}\frac{\partial^{4}\bm{B}_{\perp}}{\partial x^{4}}

(19)

in the long wavelength limit.
Comparison between Equations (
16
) and (
19
) shows that
their dissipation terms are identical when

C_{\mathrm{hyp}}=0.125
.

II.4.1
Inclusion of the Hall Term in the Induction Equation

Similar to Ohmic diffusion and ambipolar diffusion,
the Hall effect is incorporated into the cell-edge
electric fields.
Discretized expressions of
the electric currents, defined at the cell edges, are given by

\displaystyle(J_{x})_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k+{\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(B_{z})_{i,j+1,k+{\frac{1}{2}}}-(B_{z})_{i,j,k+{\frac{1}{2}}}}{\Delta y}

(21)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{(B_{y})_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k+1}-(B_{y})_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k}}{\Delta z}.

The remaining components,

(J_{y})_{i+1/2,j,k+1/2}
and

(J_{z})_{i+1/2,j+1/2,k}
, are computed in the same manner.

The
x
-component of the electric field induced by the Hall effect is given by

\displaystyle(E_{x})_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k+{\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(\eta_{\mathrm{H}})_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k+{\frac{1}{2}}}}{|\bm{B}_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k+{\frac{1}{2}}}|}

(22)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[(J_{y})_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k+{\frac{1}{2}}}(B_{z})_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k+{\frac{1}{2}}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-(J_{z})_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k+{\frac{1}{2}}}(B_{y})_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k+{\frac{1}{2}}}\right],

The quantities in Equation (
22
)
defined at the cell edge
(x_{i},y_{j+1/2},z_{k+1/2})
are
calculated as follows:

(\eta_{\mathrm{H}})_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k+{\frac{1}{2}}}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{kl=0}^{1}\sum_{jl=0}^{1}(\eta_{\mathrm{H}})_{i,j+jl,k+kl},

(B_{x})_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k+{\frac{1}{2}}}=\frac{1}{8}\sum_{il=0}^{1}\sum_{jl=0}^{1}\sum_{kl=0}^{1}(B_{x})_{i-{\frac{1}{2}}+il,j+il,k+kl},

(B_{y})_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k+{\frac{1}{2}}}=\frac{1}{2}\left\{(B_{y})_{i,j-{\frac{1}{2}},k}+(B_{y})_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k}\right\},

(B_{z})_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k+{\frac{1}{2}}}=\frac{1}{2}\left\{(B_{z})_{i,j,k-{\frac{1}{2}}}+(B_{z})_{i,j,k+{\frac{1}{2}}}\right\},

(J_{y})_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k+{\frac{1}{2}}}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{il=0}^{1}\sum_{jl=0}^{1}(J_{y})_{i-{\frac{1}{2}}+il,j+jl,k+{\frac{1}{2}}},

and

(J_{z})_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k+{\frac{1}{2}}}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{il=0}^{1}\sum_{kl=0}^{1}(J_{z})_{i-{\frac{1}{2}}+il,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k+kl}.

II.4.2
Implementations of Hyper-resistivity

The hyper-resistivity coefficient
\eta_{\mathrm{hyp}}
shown
in Equation (
18
) can be applied only when
\Delta x=\Delta y=\Delta z
.
To satisfy the stability condition,
\Delta x^{2}
in Equation (
18
) is replaced with
\min(\Delta x^{2},\Delta y^{2},\Delta z^{2})
.
The discretized form of hyper resistivity is given as follows:

\displaystyle(E_{x,\mathrm{hyp}})_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k+{\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{hyp}}(\eta_{\mathrm{H}})_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k+{\frac{1}{2}}}

(23)

\displaystyle\min(\Delta x^{2},\Delta y^{2},\Delta z^{2})

\displaystyle\times\Delta_{\mathrm{L}}(J_{x})_{i,j+{\frac{1}{2}},k+{\frac{1}{2}}},

where
\Delta_{\mathrm{L}}
represents the
discrete Laplacian operator, defined as:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathrm{L}}Q_{i,j,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta x^{2}}\left(Q_{i-1,j,k}-2Q_{i,j,k}+Q_{i+1,j,k}\right)

(24)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta y^{2}}\left(Q_{i,j-1,k}-2Q_{i,j,k}+Q_{i,j+1,k}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Delta z^{2}}\left(Q_{i,j,k-1}-2Q_{i,j,k}+Q_{i,j,k+1}\right)

assuming a uniform grid spacing.

II.4.3
Time Step Constraint due to the Hall Effect

Based on the von Neumann stability analysis
(Appendix
A
),
the time step constraint imposed by the Hall effect is expressed as

\Delta t_{\mathrm{H}}=\frac{C_{\mathrm{H}}}{4}\sqrt{\frac{3}{d}}\min_{i,j,k}\left\{\frac{\min(\Delta x^{2},\Delta y^{2},\Delta z^{2})}{(\eta_{\mathrm{H}})_{i,j,k}}\right\},

(25)

where
d
represents the spatial dimension.
In this study,
C_{\mathrm{H}}
is set to 0.8.

III.1
Stability of Hall-MHD with HLLD

Kunz & Lesur [20]

In this section, we analyze the stability of the RK3 integrator

In the whistler-wave propagation test,

To explore a more general situation,

III.2.2
Appropriate Range of the Hyper Resistivity Coefficient

The minimum value of
C_{\mathrm{hyp}}
is determined by the condition
that the damping rate caused by the hyper resistivity exceeds
the growth rate
\sigma
of the numerical instability.

By linearizing the discretized induction equation solved in
Athena++
,
we obtain a damping rate of
C_{\mathrm{hyp}}\eta_{\mathrm{H}}16d_{\mathrm{Ny}}^{2}\Delta x^{-2}
, considering
only the hyper-resistivity in the linear analysis,
assuming cubic cells

(\Delta x=\Delta y=\Delta z)
, where

d_{\mathrm{Ny}}
denotes the number of directions containing
Nyquist wavelength fluctuations.
Therefore,
C_{\mathrm{hyp}}
must meet the condition

C_{\mathrm{hyp}}>C_{\mathrm{hyp,min}}\sim 0.03d_{\mathrm{Ny}}^{-2}.

(31)

Note that since
d_{\mathrm{Ny}}=1
is adopted to obtain a stricter condition for
C_{\mathrm{hyp}}
,

C_{\mathrm{hyp,min}}\sim 0.03
should be regarded as an upper limit of
C_{\mathrm{hyp,min}}
.

The maximum value of
C_{\mathrm{hyp}}
is determined by
the condition that
the time step constraint caused by hyper resistivity,

\Delta t_{\mathrm{hyp}}
, is
greater than
\Delta t_{\mathrm{H}}
.
From the von Neumann stability analysis,

\Delta t_{\mathrm{hyp}}\propto d^{2}\Delta x^{2}/\eta_{\mathrm{H}}
can be
obtained.
By conducting numerical experiments on the turbulent magnetic field with
different
C_{\mathrm{H}}
,

, and
C_{\mathrm{hyp}}
, we found
that
C_{\mathrm{hyp}}

must satisfy the following condition:

C_{\mathrm{hyp}}<C_{\mathrm{hyp,max}}=0.09\left(\frac{d}{3}\right)^{-3/2}\left(\frac{C_{\mathrm{H}}}{0.8}\right)^{-1}

(32)

in order for
\Delta t_{\mathrm{hyp}}
not to affect the CFL condition (Section
II.4.3
).

III.3
Density-shear Instability

In this section,
we investigate the density-shear instability as a numerical experiment involving variable
\eta_{\mathrm{H}}
.
This instability occurs in situations where both the unperturbed magnetic field

\bm{B}_{0}
and the electron number density have
steep gradients perpendicular to
\bm{B}_{0}

[
41
]
.
The Hall effect coefficient
\eta_{\mathrm{H}}
is given by

\eta_{\mathrm{H}}=cB/(4\pi en_{e})
, where
c
is the speed of light,

B
is the magnetic field strength,

e
is the elementary electric charge, and
n_{e}

is the electron number density.

Gourgouliatos et al. [16]
conducted
numerical simulations of the density-shear instability.
We solve the full set of the Hall-MHD equations
(Equations (
1
)-(
4
)), whereas
they considered the induction equation taking into account only the Hall electric field
using a pseudo-spectral code.
However, the results are expected to be consistent with those of
Gourgouliatos et al. [16]

because the gas remains nearly static as
the magnetic field evolves,
similar to the turbulent magnetic field test (Section
III.2
).

Following
Gourgouliatos et al. [16]
,
we assume an unperturbed plane-parallel magnetic field, localized around
y=0
with a characteristic length scale of
a

as follows:

B_{x}=B_{0}\left\{\exp\left(-\frac{y^{2}}{a^{2}}\right)+\epsilon_{B}\right\},B_{y}=B_{z}=0,

(33)

where
\epsilon_{B}=10^{-2}
represents the magnetic field floor.
The electron number density has a similar
functional form to
B_{x}
:

n_{e}=n_{0}\left\{\exp\left(-\frac{y^{2}}{a^{2}}\right)+\epsilon_{n}\right\},

(34)

where
\epsilon_{n}=10^{-2}
is a floor bound for
n_{e}
.
We consider a fully ionized gas in which
the density is proportional to
n_{e}
and
is
\rho_{0}=1
at
y=0
.
The initial gas pressure is set so that the total pressure

P+B_{x}^{2}/8\pi
is spatially constant.
The results are insensitive to the total pressure
because the gas remains nearly static throughout the evolution.
In this study, we set
8\pi P/B_{x}^{2}=0.1
for
y=0
and
B_{0}=1
.

From Equation (
34
), the Hall effect coefficient is expressed as

\eta_{\mathrm{H}}=\eta_{\mathrm{H0}}\frac{|\bm{B}|/B_{0}}{\rho/\rho_{0}},

(35)

where
\eta_{\mathrm{H0}}=cB_{0}/(4\pi en_{0})
is the reference
Hall effect coefficient and is assigned a value of 100.

Wood et al. [41]
demonstrated through linear analysis that
the growth rate reaches its maximum when the perturbation wavenumber vector is aligned
to the
x
-axis.
The dispersion relation is given by

\sigma(k)=\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{H0}}}{a^{2}}\sqrt{(ka)^{2}\{2-(ka)^{2}\}},

(36)

where
\sigma
represents the growth rate and
k
denotes
the wavenumber along the
x
-axis

[
41
]
.
The maximum growth rate
\sigma_{\mathrm{max}}=\eta_{H0}/a^{2}
is obtained at

The fastest growing mode is applied to

as follows:

\displaystyle B_{y}=\delta B\cos\left(x/a\right),

(37)

where
\delta B=10^{-4}B_{0}
is the initial amplitude.
No perturbations are introduced for the other variables.

The simulations are performed in two dimensions.
The box size in the (
x
,
y
) plane spans

-3\pi a\leq x,y\leq 3\pi a
and is discretized
by
128^{2}
cells, where the characteristic scale is

,
Hall-HLLmod

III.5
Kelvin-Helmholtz Instability

The Kelvin-Helmholtz (KH) instability in the presence of the Hall effect was investigated by

Talwar & Kalra [36]
and
Sen & Chou [34]
.
Their findings indicate that for super-Alfvénic shear flows, the Hall effect
enhances the growth rate.

Pandey [32]
demonstrated that
the Hall effect destabilizes
sub-Alfvénic shear flows, which
are stable under ideal MHD conditions

[
6
]
.

III.5.1
Predictions from Linear Analyses

Before presenting the numerical setup,
we provide a brief overview of the growth rate derived by

Pandey [32]
.
The unperturbed state consists of
a uniform gas with
v_{x}(y)=\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}c_{\mathrm{A}}

for
y\geq 0
and
v_{x}(y)=-\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}c_{\mathrm{A}}
for
y<0
,
where
c_{\mathrm{A}}
denotes the Alfvén speed of the
unperturbed state, and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}
is the Alfvén Mach number of the shear flow.
The magnetic field is uniform and aligned with the shear flow
along the
x
-axis.
By considering perturbations in the form
e^{\sigma t+ikx}
,
the following dispersion relation is obtained by applying
the appropriate boundary conditions
[
36
,
34
,
32
]
,

\displaystyle(2+\tilde{\sigma}_{2}^{2}+\tilde{\sigma}_{1}^{2})\left\{Q_{1}\tilde{\sigma}_{1}(\tilde{\sigma}_{2}^{2}+1)+Q_{2}\tilde{\sigma}_{2}(\tilde{\sigma}_{1}^{2}+1)\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+kL_{\mathrm{H}}(\tilde{\sigma}_{2}^{2}-\tilde{\sigma}_{1}^{2})^{2}=0,

(40)

where
\tilde{\sigma}_{1}=\sigma/(kc_{\mathrm{A}})+i\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}
,

\tilde{\sigma}_{2}=\sigma/(kc_{\mathrm{A}})-i\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{A}}
,

Q_{j}=\sqrt{(\tilde{\sigma}_{j}kL_{\mathrm{H}})^{2}+(\tilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2}+1)^{2}}.

(41)

IV
Conclusions

In this paper, we evaluate the performance of
several numerical methods for
the Hall effect found in the literature,
which are listed in Table
1
,
based on an extensive series of test calculations.
The Hall effect is implemented in
Athena++

[
35
]
(see Section
II.4
).
Two types of implementations of the Hall effect are considered.
One is
Hall-HLL
, where the phase speed of whistler waves
is taken into account to compute the signal speeds in the HLL
numerical fluxes
[
22
]
.
The modified version of
Hall-HLL
(
Hall-HLLmod
) proposed by
Marchand et al. [26]
is also tested.
In
Hall-HLLmod
, the hydrodynamical variables
(the density, momentum, and total energy) are updated by
using the original HLL numerical fluxes, whereas
the
Hall-HLL
numerical fluxes are used to update the magnetic field.
The other implementation (
Hyp-Resis
)
introduces a fourth order
hyper-resistivity into the induction equation.

An appropriate hyper-resistivity coefficient
(
C_{\mathrm{hyp}}\sim 0.05
) is
determined to ensure both numerical
stability and high accuracy based on numerical experimants
and the von Neumann stability analysis
(Sections
III.2.2
and
III.3
,
Appendix
A
).

The difference in the performance of the methods is
clearly observed in the convergence test of linear waves (see
Section
III.4
).
In the Hall-dominated regime,

Hyp-Resis
exhibits second-order convergence for all
types of Hall-MHD linear waves, and numerical dissipation
caused by the hyper-resistivity term does not significantly affect the
accuracy of the solutions.
By contrast,
Hall-HLL
shows
significantly slower convergence than second-order
due to numerical dissipation in the ion-cyclotron wave and
sound wave, whereas it exhibits second-order convergence for
the whistler wave.
This occurs because the numerical dissipation in
Hall-HLL

is determined by the fastest phase speed among the
linear waves, which is the whistler-wave phase speed.
This behavior is also seen in the Kelvin-Helmholtz
instability test (Section
III.5
).

Hall-HLLmod
significantly reduces numerical dissipation
compared to
Hall-HLL
, but produces more diffusive results than
Hyp-Resis
.

Section
III.4.1
demonstrated that

Hyp-Resis
with
C_{\mathrm{hyp}}\sim 0.05

is a suitable choice also in terms of computational performance.
The computational cost of calculating the hyper-resistivity term is negligible
compared to the total cost.

Hyp-Resis
is slightly slower than
Hall-HLLmod
for the same resolution, but it can achieve the same accuracy with considerably fewer grid points. In other words, it can achieve a better accuracy with the same computational cost.

In summary, hyper resistivity with an appropriate coefficient ensures both numerical stability and high accuracy,
making it the optimal choice for simulating
phenomena involving the Hall effect.

Appendix A
von Neumann Analysis for Hall-MHD

We present the results of the von Neumann analysis for Hall MHD.
For whistler waves whose wavenumbers satisfying
\eta_{\mathrm{H}}k\gg c_{\mathrm{A}}
,
the ideal term
\bm{\nabla}\times(\bm{v}\times\bm{B})
is much smaller than
the Hall term in the induction equation.
In addition, the gas is nearly static because
the velocity perturbations
\delta v
are
much smaller than
\delta B/\sqrt{4\pi\rho_{0}}
.
Thus, we consider the following induction equation,

\frac{\partial\bm{B}}{\partial t}=-\bm{\nabla}\times\left(\eta_{\mathrm{H}}\frac{(\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{B})\times\bm{B}}{|\bm{B}|}\right).

(A1)

The computational volume is divided into cells with a size of

\Delta x\Delta y\Delta z
.
We assume uniform grids for simplicity.
The cell centers are defined at
(i\Delta x,j\Delta y,k\Delta z)
, and
the variable
Q
is denoted as
Q_{i,j,k}
.
The magnetic fields are defined at the cell surfaces;

(B_{x})_{i-1/2,j,k}
,

(B_{y})_{i,j-1/2,k}
, and
(B_{z})_{i,j,k-1/2}
.

As the unperturbed state, we consider a static gas
with constant density and pressure and
a uniform magnetic field of

\bm{B}_{0}=(B_{x0},B_{y0},B_{z0})
.
A plane wave perturbation with a wavenumber vector
of
\bm{\kappa}=(\kappa_{x},\kappa_{y},\kappa_{z})
is considered.
The magnetic field components are given by

\displaystyle(B_{x})_{i-{\frac{1}{2}},j,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{x0}+\delta B_{x}e^{I(\alpha_{x}(i-{\frac{1}{2}})+\alpha_{y}j+\alpha_{z}k)},

\displaystyle(B_{y})_{i,j-{\frac{1}{2}},k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{y0}+\delta B_{y}e^{I(\alpha_{x}i+\alpha_{y}(j-{\frac{1}{2}})+\alpha_{z}k)},

(A2)

\displaystyle(B_{z})_{i,j-{\frac{1}{2}},k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{z0}+\delta B_{z}e^{I(\alpha_{x}i+\alpha_{y}(j-{\frac{1}{2}})+\alpha_{z}k)},

where
\alpha_{x}=\kappa_{x}\Delta x
,
\alpha_{y}=\kappa_{y}\Delta y
,
\alpha_{z}=\kappa_{z}\Delta z
, and

I
is the imaginary unit.
Substituting Equation (
A2
) into the discretized form of Equation
(
A1
),
one obtains

\frac{\partial\delta\bm{B}}{\partial t}={\cal R}\delta\bm{B}

(A3)

where
\delta\bm{B}=(\delta B_{x},\delta B_{y},\delta B_{z})
,

{\cal R}=2\eta_{\mathrm{H}}C\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&-\sin(\alpha_{z}/2)/\Delta z&{\sin(\alpha_{y}/2)}/{\Delta y}\\
{\sin(\alpha_{z}/2)}/{\Delta z}&0&-{\sin(\alpha_{x}/2)}/{\Delta x}\\
-{\sin(\alpha_{y}/2)}/{\Delta y}&{\sin(\alpha_{x}/2)}/{\Delta x}&0\\
\end{array}\right)

(A4)

and

C=2\prod_{l=x,y,z}\cos\left(\frac{\alpha_{l}}{2}\right)\sum_{l=x,y,z}\left[\frac{B_{l0}\tan(\alpha_{l}/2)}{B_{0}\Delta l}\right].

(A5)

C
is reduced to
\bm{k}\cdot\bm{B}/B_{0}
in the small wavenumber limit (
\alpha_{l}\ll 1
).

With third-order time integrators, one obtains

\delta\bm{B}^{n+1}={\cal Q}\delta\bm{B}^{n}
, where

{\cal Q}={\cal I}+{\cal R}\Delta t+{\cal R}^{2}\Delta t^{2}/2+{\cal R}^{3}\Delta t^{3}/6
.
The discrete form is stable if the absolute values of
the eigenvalues
\lambda
of
{\cal Q}
is less than unity, or

|\lambda|=\sqrt{\left(1-\frac{\xi_{\mathrm{H}}^{2}}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(\xi_{\mathrm{H}}-\frac{\xi_{\mathrm{H}}^{3}}{6}\right)^{2}}<1,

(A6)

where

\xi_{\mathrm{H}}=\eta_{\mathrm{H}}\Delta tC\sqrt{\sum_{l=x,y,z}\left(\frac{2\sin(\alpha_{l}/2)}{\Delta l}\right)^{2}}.

(A7)

For small wavenumber limits, Equation (
A6
) reduces to that derived by

Kunz & Lesur [20, see their Appendix B]
.

Equation (
A6
) is reduced to

\xi_{\mathrm{H}}<\sqrt{3}
.
An conservative criterion for ensuring the stability of any linear wave is

\frac{4\sqrt{3}\eta_{\mathrm{H}}\Delta t}{\min(\Delta x^{2},\Delta y^{2},\Delta z^{2})}<\sqrt{3},

(A8)

where we use the fact that
C<2/\min(\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta z)
and

\sin(\alpha_{l}/2)<1
.

For second-order time integrators
where

{\cal Q}={\cal I}+{\cal R}\Delta t+{\cal R}^{2}\Delta t^{2}/2
,

|\lambda|
becomes
\sqrt{1+\xi_{\mathrm{H}}^{2}/4}
,
indicating that the discretization form is unconditionally unstable
[
11
,
20
]
.
