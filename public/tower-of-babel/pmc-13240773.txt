Lyophilized bacteria-infected tumor cells for targeted immunotherapy of lung metastases and associated fibrosis
Authors: Mo Chen, Yueyang Deng, Tianxu Fang, Tianwen Luo, Guojun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lung metastasis remains a major clinical challenge, often associated with poor prognosis due to its highly immunosuppressive microenvironment and fibrosis-induced complications. Current treatment strategies, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy, have shown limited efficacy in addressing lung metastases, and less attention has been given to their associated fibrosis. Here, we develop a ‘cell-in-cell’ delivery platform (i.e., lyophilized bacteria-infected tumor cells (LyoBT)) to simultaneously target lung metastasis and their associated fibrosis. This approach leverages the intrinsic lung tropism of tumor cells and the immunostimulatory properties of both tumor cells and bacteria, while mitigating tumorigenic and pathogenic risks through lyophilization. Notably, bacterial infection led to phenotypic changes in tumor cells. Specifically, characterization of LyoBT revealed upregulated expression of CD47, CD44, and E-cadherin, further enhancing lung targeting. Furthermore, increased calreticulin (CRT) exposure in LyoBT coupled with bacterial immune-stimulatory properties, promoted anti-tumor immunity. In a melanoma lung metastasis model, LyoBT demonstrated efficient accumulation in the lungs, leading to robust anti-tumor immune activation and significant inhibition of tumor progression. Notably, LyoBT also reduced fibrosis-associated immune cell infiltration and cytokine release, alleviating lung metastasis-induced fibrosis. Furthermore, LyoBT served as a drug delivery platform for immune checkpoint inhibitors (aPD-L1), with LyoBT@aPD-L1 demonstrating enhanced therapeutic efficacy. Our findings highlight the potential of LyoBT as a dual-functional strategy to combat both lung metastases and their associated fibrosis, offering a promising new avenue for bacterial-based cancer immunotherapy.

Introduction

Lung metastasis is a common and life-threatening complication of various primary malignant tumors, often associated with poor prognosis and high mortality rates [1,2]. Despite advancements in clinical treatments, such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy, therapeutic outcomes remain suboptimal due to a highly heterogeneous and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [[3], [4], [5]]. Moreover, lung metastases are frequently accompanied by extensive fibrosis, driven by chronic and persistent inflammation established with immunosuppressive populations and sustained profibrotic signaling [6]. This fibrosis not only promotes the recruitment of immunosuppressive cells, further exacerbating immune suppression and tumor progression [[7], [8], [9]], but also increases drug resistance and hampers drug delivery [10,11]. While fibrosis initially arises within lung metastatic niches, it often extends into surrounding lung tissues or even beyond, leading to severe respiratory complications. Despite considerable efforts to develop targeted therapies for lung metastases, effectively addressing both lung metastasis and the accompanying fibrosis remains a critical challenge. Therefore, we hypothesize that an effective therapeutic strategy should not only target metastatic lesions but also modulate the broader lung tissue environment to improve treatment efficacy against both lung metastases and the associated fibrosis.

Bacteria-mediated immunotherapy has recently emerged as a promising approach for cancer treatment, leveraging the unique therapeutic potential of live bacteria [12,13]. Unlike conventional therapies, bacteria exhibit distinct properties that allow them to target solid tumors, infiltrate tumor tissues, and modulate the tumor immune microenvironment [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. These advantages make bacteria an attractive platform for therapeutic intervention in complex tumor settings. However, despite progress in engineering bacteria for cancer therapy and drug delivery, their application in lung metastasis and fibrosis remains largely unexplored. This is primarily due to challenges of bacteria's inefficient lung targeting and concerns regarding severe side effects, particularly those associated with the uncontrollable proliferation of live bacteria [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. In addition, current bacteria-based therapies are constrained by limited carrier cell options and complex, poorly stable delivery systems [22,23]. Overcoming these limitations could open new avenues for bacterial-based strategies that not only combat lung metastases but also address the fibrotic and immunosuppressive microenvironment, ultimately improving therapeutic outcomes.

Here, we report a ‘cell-in-cell’ delivery platform—lyophilized bacteria-infected tumor cell (LyoBT)—for dual inhibition of lung metastases and their associated fibrosis (Fig. 1). This strategy leverages the natural lung tropism of tumor cells following systemic administration, while lyophilization effectively eliminates tumorigenic potential and bacterial pathogenicity, enhancing biocompatibility for in vivo applications [5,[24], [25], [26]]. Moreover, lyophilized tumor cells serve as a source of tumor antigens, complemented by the immune-modulatory properties of lyophilized bacteria. Our findings revealed that the bacteria-in-cell platform altered phenotypic characteristics of tumor cells, notably upregulating CD47, CD44, and E-cadherin, three markers that further facilitate lung targeting. Additionally, LyoBT enhanced calreticulin (CRT) exposure, a well-known damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) signal that triggers anti-tumor immune responses, which was further boosted by the bacterial immune-stimulatory properties. In a melanoma lung metastasis model, LyoBT demonstrated effective capabilities in lung targeting and activation of anti-tumor immune responses, leading to potent inhibition of lung metastatic progression. More importantly, LyoBT markedly reduced fibrosis-related cellular infiltration and suppressed fibrosis-associated cytokine release, effectively alleviating lung metastasis-associated fibrosis. Furthermore, LyoBT can serve as a versatile drug delivery vehicle for therapeutics such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PD-L1 antibodies, aPD-L1). Treatment with LyoBT@aPD-L1 further enhanced therapeutic outcomes, underscoring its potential as a promising strategy for combating lung metastases and fibrosis. In general, we present a modular platform that integrates cell-based targeting, immune activation, and microenvironment remodeling. Results and discussions

Preparation and characterization of LyoBT

Lyophilized bacteria-infected tumor cells (LyoBT) were obtained by coculturing E. coli with B16F10 tumor cells, followed by standard lyophilization. We first optimized the lyophilization conditions to ensure E. coli inactivation rather than dormancy. A dose-dependent loss of bacterial viability was observed as lyoprotectant concentrations (trehalose) reduced (Fig. S1). No E. coli colony formed in the absence of lyoprotectant, even after 72 h of incubation (Fig. S2), indicating irreversible bacterial damage and negligible pathogenic potential of the bacteria in this platform [27]. To enhance bacterial colonization, we leveraged the preferential invasion of E. coli into tumor tissue and cells under hypoxic conditions. E. coli and B16F10 cells were cocultured in a hypoxic condition (1% O2) for varying durations, achieving an increased bacteria-to-cell ratio that reached a plateau at approximately 9.7 after 16 h of incubation (Fig. S3). In this condition, LyoBT showed a clear bacterial load with maintained cell morphology (Fig. 2a and b, Fig. S4), which was stable for at least 12 h of incubation under 37 °C (Fig. S5). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images showed that LyoBT maintained the cellular structure of tumor cells (Fig. 2c). Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images further confirmed that both cell nuclei and cytoskeletons were well-preserved with a slightly decreased cell size (Fig. 2d). Importantly, lyophilized bacteria (LyoB) and LyoBT lost the ability to proliferate in vitro, as verified by colony formation (Fig. 2e), MTT assays, and live/dead staining, respectively (Fig. 2f and g), an important biosafety factor for in vivo applications. In addition, LyoBT remained structurally stable within 12 h at 37 °C and was gradually lysed afterward (Fig. S6). Furthermore, in vivo safety evaluation showed that intravenous injection of LyoBT into mice did not induce tumor formation, and all mice remained tumor-free, whereas fresh tumor cell injection led to rapid tumor growth and 100% mortality before Day 26 (Fig. 2h, Fig. S7). These findings confirmed LyoBT preserved cell integrity while eliminating tumorigenic potential and bacterial pathogenicity, ensuring biosafety for therapeutic applications.

LyoBT enhanced initiation of anti-tumor immune responses in vitro

Bacterial infections are commonly found in tumors and are associated with tumor development and progression. Hence, it has also been speculated that bacterial infection could potentially contribute to phenotypical changes in tumor cells and immune cells [28]. To investigate whether E. coli infection modulates tumor cell immunogenicity, we first assessed calreticulin (CRT) exposure, a classic damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) signal that promotes tumor cell phagocytosis by antigen-presenting cells (APCs), such as dendritic cells and macrophages [29]. Lyophilized tumor cells (LyoT) and a physical mixture of LyoB and LyoT (LyoB/LyoT) exhibited CRT expression levels comparable to fresh tumor cells (Fresh T). However, LyoBT exhibited a significant increase in CRT exposure (Fig. 2i–Table S1), which might be attributed to bacterial infection-induced stress responses [30,31]. Increased CRT exposure facilitates tumor antigen uptake and presentation by APCs, further strengthened by the presence of bacteria-derived substances in LyoBT, such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and flagellin [32,33]. To evaluate the impact on APCs, we co-culture LyoBT with bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) and macrophages (BMDMs). LyoBT significantly increased the proportion of mature DCs (Fig. 2j, Fig. S8), and promoted macrophage polarization to the M1 phenotype, yielding the highest M1/M2 ratio among all groups (Fig. 2k, Fig. S9). In addition, LyoBT-induced DC maturation and macrophage polarization were positively associated with bacterial load, which plateaued at 9.7 (Fig. S10). The immune-activating properties of LyoBT were consistently demonstrated when B16F10 melanoma cells were replaced with 4T1 breast cancer cells (Fig. S11). These data collectively supported that LyoBT enhanced antigen presentation and stimulated anti-tumor immune responses.

LyoBT exhibited lung targeting in vivo

Tumor cells have been shown lung accumulation due to their micron-sized dimension and the presence of surface proteins, such as CD47, CD44, and E-cadherin [24,34]. CD44 and E-cadherin can adhere to the lung extracellular matrix and endothelial cells, while CD47 avoids clearance by phagocytic cells and prolongs circulation [[35], [36], [37], [38]]. Notably, E. coli infection further upregulated these markers, which may be partially attributed to a host protective response or a mechanism exploited by pathogens during infection [[39], [40], [41]]. These host-pathogen interaction-mediated upregulations enhanced the lung-targeting capability of LyoBT. Compared to Fresh T and LyoT, LyoBT exhibited a 4.6-fold and 5.5-fold increase in CD47 expression, respectively (Fig. 3a). We then evaluated the biodistribution of LyoBT. Fresh T and LyoT (Cy5.5 dye-loaded) displayed a clear signal in the lungs, which was significantly higher than free dye, a cell-free form. Notably, LyoBT demonstrated superior lung accumulation. This property is likely due to the enhanced CD47, CD44, and E-cadherin expression (Fig. 3d and e), as blockade of these molecules significantly reduced its lung accumulation (Fig. S12). Additionally, E. coli signals in the lungs were significantly stronger in the LyoBT group than in the LyoB group (Fig. 3f), highlighting the limited lung accumulation of LyoB alone and the advantages of the cell-in-cell formulation. Although there were minor intensity differences between FITC and Cy5.5 signals in the lung tissue, likely due to their distinct optical properties (Fig. 3f), the overlapping fluorescence signals of E. coli (green) and tumor cells (red) (Fig. 3g) confirmed the stability of LyoBT during circulation. A detailed pharmacokinetic profile, including mass-balance studies via ICP-MS or quantitative genetic tracking via qPCR, would be essential for defining the clinical dosing windows and metabolic pathways of the LyoBT platform.

Moreover, a comprehensive evaluation of hematological and biochemical parameters in LyoBT-treated mice showed values within the normal physiological range compared with untreated healthy mice, indicating no detectable systemic inflammation, infection, or organ toxicity (Fig. S13). To further examine potential delayed or chronic effects, histological analysis of major organs was performed at 2 months post-administration. No signs of tissue damage, inflammatory infiltration, or structural abnormalities were detected (Fig. S14). These findings established LyoBT as an effective lung-targeting vehicle for bacterial delivery with minimized systemic toxicity.

LyoBT induced effective anti-tumor immune activation and therapeutic effect in a melanoma lung metastasis model

It has been well-documented that bacteria can awaken both innate and adaptive immune responses through multiple mechanisms, and dead tumor cells or tumor lysis can also elicit tumor-specific anti-tumor immunity by providing tumor-specific antigens [42,43]. Thus, we rationalize that LyoBT could induce a robust anti-tumor immune response by combining both advantages with enhanced lung accumulation. Thus, we examined the immune-stimulating effects of LyoBT in vivo. LyoBT treatment significantly increased the mature DCs and M1 tumor-associated macrophages (Fig. 4a–d) compared with controls including LyoB, LyoT, and LyoB/LyoT, leading to enhanced T cell activation [44]. Consequently, LyoBT treatment resulted in a substantial increase in lung-infiltrating (CD3+ T cells, Fig. S15) and cytotoxic CD8+ T cell (Fig. 4e and f). Due to bacteria-induced innate immune responses, there was also a significant increase of natural killer (NK) cells found in the LyoBT group, which contribute to direct tumor cell killing [45] (Fig. 4g and h). Therapeutic efficacy was assessed using a melanoma lung metastasis model. Mice were injected intravenously with luciferase-expressing B16F10 cells and subsequently treated with saline, LyoT, LyoB, LyoB/LyoT (physical mixture of LyoB and LyoT), or LyoBT (Fig. 5a). LyoBT inhibited lung metastasis-associated fibrosis in vivo

Upon metastatic colonization, tumor cells induce persistent tissue injury and chronic inflammatory signaling, which in turn activate fibroblasts and promote fibrotic remodeling [46,47]. Consequently, fibrosis is often associated with lung metastasis and facilitates metastasis progression [6]. Moreover, lung metastasis-associated fibrosis can impair the anti-tumor efficiency of immunotherapy by modulating the activity of immune cells and cytokine production [48]. For example, high-deposited collagen and activated fibroblast cells (e.g., cancer-associated fibroblasts, CAFs), the major components of lung metastasis-associated fibrosis, have been shown to induce CD8+ T cell exhaustion [[49], [50], [51]]. As LyoBT demonstrated promising anti-tumor efficacy that eliminated a primary driver of pathological fibrosis, we next investigated whether it could inhibit lung metastasis–associated fibrosis. A clear fibrosis formation associated with tumor niches was confirmed in untreated metastatic lungs. Excitingly, LyoBT treatment significantly reduced collagen deposition and activated fibroblasts, as revealed by Masson trichrome staining and αSMA staining, respectively (Fig. 6a–d) [[52], [53], [54], [55]]. Flow cytometric analysis confirmed a decrease in CAF numbers in the LyoBT group (Fig. 6e and f). Neutrophils, an important cell that can promote fibroblast activation and fibrosis, also significantly decreased (Fig. 6g and h). LyoBT treatment also decreased myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and M2 macrophages (Fig. 6i–l, Fig. S18), two cell types that can accelerate fibrosis by producing cytokines such as TGF-β. Consequently, a decreased TGF-β secretion was detected (Fig. S19). LyoBT as a delivery carrier for other therapeutics to augment therapeutic efficacy

We further explored whether LyoBT could serve as a delivery vehicle for combination therapy. With a microporous structure resembling a micro-hydrogel, LyoBT offers opportunities to load and deliver a range of therapeutics, such as macromolecules, small molecules, and nanoscale formulations.

Current bacteria-based cancer immunotherapies in clinical trials are often combined with immune checkpoint blockade owing to their potential synergistic therapeutic actions [56,57]. Since LyoBT elicited strong T cell-mediated responses, we first explored its potential for targeted delivery of anti-PD-L1 (aPD-L1) antibodies to further enhance therapeutic efficacy. aPD-L1 can block the PD-1/PD-L1 interactions between the tumor cells and T cells, thus strengthening T cell-mediated tumor killing [[58], [59], [60]]. aPD-L1 was loaded into LyoBT by simply mixing aPD-L1 solutions with LyoBT powders (LyoBT@aPD-L1, Fig. 7a) with a loading level ∼4 pg per cell. LyoBT@aPD-L1 showed sustained antibody release (Fig. S20). Remarkably, mice treated with LyoBT@aPD-L1 exhibited significantly delayed tumor growth compared with aPD-L1 or LyoBT alone (Fig. 7b, Fig. S21), with 2/7 mice achieving complete tumor remission (Fig. 7c). To demonstrate the broad applicability of the LyoBT platform, we incorporated doxorubicin (DOX), a water-soluble small-molecule chemotherapeutic, into LyoBT to generate LyoBT@DOX (Fig. S22). LyoBT@DOX exhibited robust therapeutic efficacy, demonstrating the feasibility of drug loading and functional delivery using this platform. Looking ahead, optimizing the platform for delivering diverse therapeutics, including nucleic acids and nanoparticles, and exploring additional functions such as metabolic modulation will further expand its potential.

Conclusion

In summary, we described a ‘cell-in-cell’ delivery system for the treatment of lung metastasis and their associated fibrosis. Constructed through a simple two-step procedure, LyoBT effectively eliminated tumorigenicity and pathogenicity, while altering tumor cell phenotypic characteristics to enhance lung targeting and activate anti-tumor and anti-fibrotic responses. In the melanoma lung metastatic model, LyoBT demonstrated strong potential for inhibiting both lung metastases and metastasis-associated fibrosis. Furthermore, LyoBT loaded with immune checkpoint inhibitors further augmented therapeutic efficacy. This platform offers flexibility in both bacterial species and cell carriers, allowing organ-specific targeting via different types of lyophilized cells, while bacterial components can be selected to tailor immune activation profiles. Moreover, the physical loading mechanism permits the incorporation of diverse therapeutic agents through simple rehydration, significantly enhancing its modularity. Collectively, these features establish a generalizable and adaptable strategy that may enable more precise targeting and combinatorial therapy, thereby expanding the application of bacteria-based therapeutics across a wide range of diseases.

Despite these promising findings, the current study has several limitations. While we observed the upregulation of targeting-related markers such as CD47, CD44, and E-cadherin, the underlying mechanisms driving these phenotypic changes remain to be fully elucidated and warrant further investigation. Although the feasibility of the LyoBT platform for changing cell types has been demonstrated in vitro, its generalizability would be further strengthened by comprehensive in vivo evaluation across multiple tumor models, following systematic optimization of dosing regimens, administration routes, and treatment schedules tailored to each tumor microenvironment. In addition, although the lyophilization-based inactivation strategy improves the biosafety of both tumor cells and bacteria, a comprehensive evaluation of long-term safety and performance in clinically relevant settings will be important in future studies. Furthermore, future studies should focus on elucidating bacteria–tumor interactions, improving drug loading and release profiles through engineering strategies, evaluating the synergistic or additional effects of LyoBT and its payload, and assessing translational potential in clinically relevant models.

Materials and methods

Materials, cells, and animals

PD-L1 antibody (aPD-L1) was purchased from MedChemEepress (Cat no. HY-P99145). Doxorubicin hydrochloride was purchased from Sigma (Cat no. D1515). E. coli was obtained from Geoffrey Andrew McKay's Lab at McGill University. The murine luciferase-expressed B16F10 cells (B16-luc cells) were constructed with firefly luciferase lentivirus (BPS Bioscience, INC.). B16-luc cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), 0.1% Amphotericin B (Gibco), and 0.1% Gentamicin (Gibco). Murine 4T1 cells were provided by Peter Siegel's Lab at McGill University. 4T1 cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco), 2% sodium bicarbonate (Gibco), 1% 1 M N-2-hydroxyethylpiperazine-N-2-ethane sulfonic acid (HEPES; Gibco), and 1% sodium pyruvate (Gibco). C57BL/6 mice (6-7 weeks, female) were purchased from Charles River Laboratories.

Preparation of Fresh BT, LyoT, LyoBT and LyoBT@aPD-L1

To prepare Fresh BT, 20 μl of E. coli (1 × 108 CFU/ml) was added to an antibiotic-free medium for co-culturing when the B16F10 cell density reached approximately 50%. The cells were then incubated for 16 h in a hypoxia incubator (1% O2). After incubation, Fresh BT were collected and washed with PBS to remove free E. coli. To prepare lyophilized cells (LyoB, LyoT, and LyoBT), E. coli, Fresh T, and Fresh BT were suspended in PBS and frozen at −80 °C overnight, then lyophilized. The lyophilized cells were stored at −20 °C and reconstituted in deionized water for further use. aPD-L1-loaded LyoBT was prepared by reconstituting LyoBT with aPD-L1 stock solution and then centrifuging (500 ×g, 5 min) to obtain LyoBT@aPD-L1. DOX-loaded LyoBT was prepared by reconstituting LyoBT with DOX stock solution and then centrifuging (500 ×g, 5 min) to obtain LyoBT@DOX.

Structural characterization of LyoBT

LyoT and LyoBT were stained with Phalloidin-iFluor™ 488 Conjugate (Cayman, Cat no. 20549). Fresh T was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% Trition-X100 before staining. After washing with PBS, Hoechst (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 62249) was added to the cell suspension, followed by PBS washes. These samples were examined using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) (Observer. Z1, Zeiss). LyoT and LyoBT were observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (SU-3500, Hitachi, Japan) after gold sputtering (10 nm). LyoBT suspension after 1, 6, 12, and 24 h of storage at 37 °C was imaged using a microscope.

E. coli-loading ability of LyoBT

To quantify the number of E. coli loaded in tumor cells, Fresh BT was counted, spread on LB agar plates, and incubated at 37 °C. Colony formation was calculated after overnight incubation. The E. coli-loading ability was calculated as:E.coliloadingability=colonyformationcellnumbers

The proportion and morphology of tumor cells infected with E. coli were assessed by staining LyoT and LyoBT with FITC-labeled anti-E. coli (Abcam, ab30522) for flow cytometry and CLSM analysis.

Antibody-loading ability of LyoBT@aPD-L1

To study the antibody-loading efficiency of LyoBT@aPD-L1, the rat IgG (to mimic aPD-L1) was used to form LyoBT@IgG using the same method described above. LyoBT@IgG suspension (Lyo cell: 5 × 105) was treated with an ultrasonic processor, followed by centrifugation at 20000 ×g for 10 min, and the IgG in the supernatant was detected by ELISA (Thermo Fisher). The antibody-loading ability was calculated as:Antibodyloadingability=weightofIgGcellnumbers

Antibody release from LyoBT@aPD-L1

To study the antibody release profile, 200 μl of LyoBT@IgG in pH 7.4 PBS was placed in the upper chamber of a Transwell, and 1 ml of PBS was added to the lower chamber of a 24-well plate. The plate was placed in a shaker at 100 r/min, 37 °C. Samples (n = 3) were taken from the lower chamber at 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h, and the released IgG was detected by ELISA (Thermo Fisher).

Proliferation potential of LyoBT

Fresh B and LyoB suspensions were evenly spread on LB agar plates, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C. Colony formation was calculated after overnight incubation or 72 h of incubation. Fresh T, LyoBT were stained with LIVE/DEAD™ Viability/Cytotoxicity Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat no. L3224) and analyzed by flow cytometry. For the in vitro MTT assay, Fresh T and LyoBT were seeded in a 96-well plate and cultured for 24 h and 48 h, respectively, and then cell viability was assessed using 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (Invitrogen). For the in vivo proliferation analysis, Fresh T and LyoBT (5 × 105 cells per mouse) were intravenously injected into C57BL/6 mice. Bioluminescence (from B16-luc cells) was monitored on days 12, 17, and 22 post-injection using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) spectrum (PerkinElmer). Survival was monitored.

Cell marker detection on LyoBT

Calreticulin (CRT) expression was detected on Fresh T, Fresh BT, LyoT, LyoB/LyoT (physical mixture of LyoB and LyoT), and LyoBT using CRT primary antibody (Invitrogen, Cat no. PA3-900) and secondary antibody (Invitrogen, Cat no. A21428). To analyze the expression of CD44, CD47 and E-cadherin, cells were stained with APC anti-CD44 (Biolegend, Cat no. 103012), FITC anti-mouse CD47 (Biolegend, Cat no. 127503), and APC anti-E-cadherin (Biolegend, Cat no. 147311), respectively. All antibodies were used following the manufacturer's instructions. Protein expression was detected by flow cytometry.

Immune activation by LyoBT in vitro

BMDCs and BMDMs were isolated from mice as previously described [61,62]. For BMDC differentiation, the collected cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 20 ng/ml granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) and 10 ng/ml interleukin 4 (IL-4). For BMDM differentiation, the collected cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 40 ng/ml macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) and 20 ng/ml IL-4. Seven days after differentiation, BMDCs and BMDMs were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Fresh T, LyoT, LyoB, LyoB/LyoT, and LyoBT were then added for an additional 24 h co-culture.

For DC maturation, BMDCs were stained with APC anti-CD11c (Biolegend, Cat no. 117310), Pacific Blue anti-CD80 (Biolegend, Cat no. 104724) and PE anti-CD86 (Biolegend, Cat no. 105008). For the analysis of macrophage polarization, BMDMs were stained with PE anti-CD11b (Biolegend, Cat no. 101208), Pacific Blue anti-CD80 (Biolegend, Cat no. 104724) and APC anti-F4/80 (Biolegend, Cat no.123116), or PE anti-CD11b (Biolegend, Cat no. 101208), APC anti-F4/80 (Biolegend, Cat no.123116) and BV421 anti-CD206 (Biolegend, Cat no.141717). All antibodies were used following the manufacturer's instructions. All cells were detected by flow cytometry.

Biodistribution of LyoBT

Fresh T, LyoT, and LyoBT were labeled with Cy5.5-NHS. Then, free Cy5.5, Cy5.5-Fresh T, Cy5.5-LyoT, or Cy5.5-LyoBT (5 × 105 cells per mouse) were intravenously injected into C57BL/6 mice. After 2 h, mice were sacrificed, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were collected and weighed. The fluorescent intensity of each organ was assessed by IVIS, and the fluorescence intensity per gram of tissue was calculated. To further confirm the functionality of various cell makers, Cy5.5-LyoBT was neutralized with E-cadherin (Bio X Cell, Cat no. BE0352), CD44 (R&D Systems, Cat no. AF6127), or CD47 (R&D Systems, Cat no. MAB18661) antibodies according to the manufacturer's instructions before injection.

To evaluate the biodistribution of E. coli, LyoB and Cy5.5-LyoBT were stained with FITC-labeled anti-E. coli and intravenously injected into C57BL/6 mice. After 2 h, lungs were collected for IVIS imaging. The lung sections were also prepared for CLSM imaging after Hoechst staining.

Biosafety evaluation of LyoBT

Healthy mice treated with a single injection of LyoBT were evaluated 2 weeks post-treatment and compared with untreated healthy mice. Blood samples were collected for hematological analysis, including erythrocytes, hemoglobin, hematocrit, mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), platelets (PLT), and white blood cells (WBC). Serum biochemistry analysis included creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), alanine transaminase (ALT), and aspartate transaminase (AST). Serum cytokines, including IFN-γ (BioLegend, Cat no. 430801), TNF-α (BioLegend, Cat no. 430901), IL-6 (BioLegend, Cat no. 431301), C-reactive protein (Proteintech, Cat no. KE10128), and calcitonin (AFSBio, Cat no. CEA472Mu), were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions. Major organs were also harvested for H&E staining 2 weeks and 2 months post-treatment.

Anti-tumor efficacy of LyoBT in a melanoma lung metastatic model

1 × 106 B16-luc cells were injected intravenously into C57BL/6 mice to establish the melanoma lung metastatic model. After 4 days, mice were randomly divided into 5 groups and intravenously injected with saline, LyoT, LyoB, LyoB/LyoT, or LyoBT (E.coli: 4.85 × 106 CFU; Lyo cell: 5 × 105 cells per mouse), every three days for 5 doses. Bioluminescence was monitored on days 7, 12, 17, and 22 using IVIS (PerkinElmer). Body weight and survival were recorded. Animals were euthanized when signs of compromised health were observed.

Anti-tumor efficacy of LyoBT@aPD-L1 in a melanoma lung metastatic model

The melanoma lung metastatic model was established as described above. After 4 days, the mice were randomly divided into 4 groups and treated with saline, aPD-L1, LyoBT, or LyoBT@aPD-L1 (E. coli: 4.85 × 106 CFU; Lyo cell: 5 × 105; aPD-L1: 0.1 mg/kg), every three days for 5 doses. Bioluminescence was monitored on days 7, 12, 17, 22, and 27. Survival was recorded. Animals were euthanized when signs of compromised health were observed.

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as means ± s.e.m. Student's t-test was used for two-group comparisons. One-way ANOVA was used for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.0. The threshold for statistical significance was P < 0.05.
