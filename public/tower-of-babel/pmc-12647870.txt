Beyond perfusion: a review of peptide radiopharmaceuticals for cardiovascular imaging
Authors: Viktoria E. Krol, Vani Sharma, Joanna E. Kusmirek, Maliha Zahid, Derek R. Johnson, Mukesh K. Pandey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cardiology is continually evolving towards increased personalization with targeted diagnostics and therapeutics. Peptide-based radiopharmaceuticals have emerged as a valuable tool for noninvasive, receptor-specific imaging, addressing limitations of traditional perfusion-based radiotracers like [15O]H2O, [13N]NH3, [82Rb]RbCl and [99mTc]Tc-Sestamibi, which lack molecular specificity. While these conventional tracers provide crucial insights into myocardial perfusion and ventricular function, receptor-targeted imaging can illuminate the molecular mechanisms underlying cardiovascular diseases. This, in turn, offers novel insights into disease progression, enhanced diagnostic accuracy, and a tool for companion diagnostics of molecularly targeted therapeutics. Beyond receptor-mediated targeting, recent advances in cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs), such as the development of the cardiac targeting peptide (CTP), offer new opportunities for the enhanced delivery of a therapeutic payload to the injured heart. Their biodistribution can be effectively monitored using radiolabeled analogs. This review explores the role of peptide-based radiopharmaceuticals in nuclear cardiology, highlighting their applications in receptor-mediated imaging and briefly discussing non-receptor-specific CPPs. Select examples illustrate how these innovations are advancing molecular characterization of cardiovascular diseases such as fibrosis, cardiac amyloidosis, atherosclerosis, and more, reshaping the nuclear cardiology landscape.

Introduction

Cardiovascular medicine is evolving from broad treatment approaches towards more personalized, precision healthcare1. An important advancement in this evolution is the use of molecular imaging and biology-driven precision pharmaceuticals, which often leverage overexpressed receptors as molecular targets for diagnosis, therapy, and monitoring. Nuclear medicine plays a pivotal role in cardiology, utilizing techniques like positron emission tomography (PET) and single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) imaging to assess cardiac function and diagnose cardiovascular diseases (CVD)2. Historically, nuclear cardiology has relied on perfusion-based radiotracers with simple formulations like [15O]H2O, [13N]NH3, and [82Rb]RbCl and small molecules such as [99mTc]Tc-Sestamibi, [99mTc]Tc-Tetrofosmin and the newly Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved [18F]Flurpiridaz2. These cardiac radiotracers provide clear images of flow distribution and quantitative evaluations of myocardial perfusion and left ventricular function, used to assess cardiac performance and correlate with disease prognosis. While these insights are invaluable, there is an increasing interest in the molecular characterization of cardiovascular diseases. Advances in our understanding of the cellular and molecular biology of the cardiovascular system have ushered in demand for more precise targeting. In this context, peptide-based radiopharmaceuticals with receptor-mediated binding offer a unique, noninvasive, and highly sensitive imaging modality. Typically, peptides are low molecular weight molecules ranging from 2 to 50 amino acids. This provides advantages over larger biomolecules like antibodies due to favorable biodistribution profiles, including high target uptake and rapid blood clearance. Techniques such as combinatorial peptide chemistry and phage display technology have greatly expanded the available pool of synthetic peptides that can be quickly screened and assessed. Flexible peptide synthesis allows for manipulation and optimization of specificity and pharmacokinetic properties and enables site-specific conjugation of chelators and complexes to form peptide-based radiopharmaceuticals. Moreover, well-established solid-phase peptide synthesis techniques ensure reproducible chemical structures at clinically relevant scales with a high level of purity. For these reasons, peptide-based radiopharmaceuticals have extensive clinical applications3. This review will explore the applications and adoption of novel peptide-based radiopharmaceuticals in nuclear cardiology. Given the broad scope of the review, the provided examples are non-exhaustive but, with selected examples, highlight the importance of receptor-mediated targeting, and cell-penetrating peptides (CPP) in diagnostic nuclear cardiology.

General considerations for peptide-based radiopharmaceutical design

The methods used for designing targeting peptides largely depend on what is known about the target receptor. Rational design-based approaches often focus on the evaluation of naturally occurring ligands, including key amino acid sequences for binding and known information about peptide-protein interactions. Naturally occurring peptides typically have short biological half-lives in vivo, due to rapid enzymatic degradation. The goal of peptide structure engineering is to develop analogs, which can remain intact during the circulation of the blood while maintaining their affinity and specificity for the target receptor. Common methods include the introduction of non-natural amino acids and D-amino acids, acetylation, amination, and cyclization of the peptide4. Multimerization is also commonly used to improve binding affinity or enable binding to more than one receptor5,6. Computational approaches are increasingly being used to aid peptide design, providing high-throughput simulations of molecular dynamics and interactions to predict promising structures7. Rapid screening of large libraries of target-specific peptides is becoming more accessible with methods such as phage display, significantly accelerating preclinical efforts. The biological half-life of targeting vectors is often proportional to their molecular weight, with larger compounds circulating in the blood-pool for a longer time post intravenous administration. To enable high-quality PET and SPECT images, the biological half-life is generally matched to the physical half-life (t1/2) of the radionuclide, which should be long enough for an adequate imaging signal to remain after background blood-pool clearance and target region tracer accumulation. Radionuclides such as 15O (t1/2 = 2.04 min) and 13N (t1/2 = 9.96 min) are not suitable for peptide labeling, encouraging the use of (relatively) longer-lived radionuclides such as 68Ga (t1/2 = 67.71 min), 18F(t1/2 = 109.77 min), 43Sc (t1/2 = 3.89 h), 44Sc (t1/2 = 4.04 h), 99mTc (t1/2 = 6.01 h), and 64Cu (t1/2 = 12.70 h). Translation of a peptide into a radiopharmaceutical requires radiolabeling. Organic radionuclides like 18F can be conjugated directly onto the peptide, also known as “direct labeling”, while radiometals such as 64Cu and 68Ga are complexed by chelators such as 1,4,7,10-Tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7,10-tetraacetic acid (DOTA) and 1,4,7-Triazacyclononane-1,4,7-triacetic acid (NOTA), which are conjugated to the peptide8. Both the stability of the radionuclide tag and the peptide itself are essential for ensuring effective delivery to the target site5,6. The formation of radio-metabolites and off-target accumulation of the free radionuclides can degrade the diagnostic value of PET and SPECT images. Due to the small size of peptides, the addition of a chelator may also influence its biodistribution and receptor binding affinity. In many cases, a spacer can be incorporated as a pharmacokinetic modifier9. Overall, successful peptide-based radiopharmaceuticals are characterized by high in vivo stability, rapid clearance from the blood pool and high accumulation in the targeted region, together providing a high target-to-background ratio (TBR) for quality diagnostic images.

Applications of peptide-based radiopharmaceuticals in cardiac fibrosis

Fibrosis is a dysregulated buildup of scar tissue following injury or chronic inflammation and can broadly be defined as the excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM). For minor and non-chronic injuries, the deposition of adequate amounts of ECM is key for efficient wound healing. Conversely, dysregulated accumulation of ECM components from chronic or severe injury can lead to adverse organ remodeling, dysfunction, and even failure10. Chronic triggers such as inflammation result in the activation of fibroblasts, which lead to the deposition of ECM, predominantly made up of collagen and fibronectin. In the context of cardiac fibrosis, this buildup of ECM leads to adverse cardiac remodeling and subsequent adverse effects such as arrhythmia, often progressing to more lethal cardiomyopathies. For this reason, there is a clinical need for the diagnosis and molecular targeting of active fibrosis for earlier detection, and to pave the way for more targeted antifibrotic therapies with better patient selection. For fibrosis, the predominant targeted biomarkers are the fibroblast activation protein (FAP), integrin receptors and collagen. FAP inhibitors (FAPIs) are being heavily explored in cardiology. However, these primarily consist of radiolabeled small molecule inhibitors, to which there are several dedicated reviews11,12. Peptide-based FAPIs have been utilized in oncology13,14, but to the best of the authors’ knowledge, have not yet been applied to routine clinical cardiology.

Targeting integrins in cardiac fibrosis

Integrins are critical to cell-environment communications15. In fibrosis, integrin signaling mediates the activation of fibroblast cells and fibrogenic activity of other cells such as immune cells, vascular cells and cardiomyocytes16. Integrins are a family of transmembrane receptors consisting of 19 α- and eight β-subunits expressed in 25 α/β heterodimeric combinations on the cell surface and can be targeted for imaging and therapeutic applications (Fig. 1). Integrin αvβ3 is expressed by activated fibroblasts and is known for mediating cell adhesion and influencing cell growth, proliferation, survival, and migration17,18. The binding of integrin αvβ3 to ECM proteins is facilitated through amino acid complexes containing an arginine–glycine–aspartic acid (RGD) sequence in their structure (Fig. 1). Peptides targeting αvβ3 can contain one or more RGD tripeptide sequences, either linear or cyclic. There have been several structure-activity studies performed for the optimization of RGD-based peptides, to which there are dedicated reviews19,20. One key highlight is the cyclization of RGD peptides via linkers such as thioether or disulfide bonds or through rigid aromatic rings and other heterocycles, commonly done to increase the receptor binding affinity and selectivity19,20. Borne et al. used 99mTc radiolabeled Cy5.5-RGD peptide (CRIP) to target myofibroblasts and visualized myocardial remodeling through interstitial fibrosis and collagen deposition after myocardial infarction in a mouse model21. CRIP is a 10-amino acid peptide with a bicyclic structure formed by a disulfide bridge and a thioether bridge. It is conjugated to the 99mTc chelator cPN216 through the lysine side chain at the C-terminus and contains a fluorescent cyanine dye (Cy5.5), conjugated through the lysine side chain at the N-terminus (Table 1). The uptake of CRIP in the infarct area peaked at 2 weeks post myocardial infarction (MI), with lower uptake at 4- and 12-week imaging time points, with almost no uptake in the myocardium 1 year later. Meanwhile, uptake in the remote regions increased in 12 weeks, indicative of reactive fibrosis, which may result in adverse cardiac remodeling. Fibrosis was confirmed histologically in areas corresponding to high radiotracer uptake21. The trend in CRIP uptake over time was consistent with expected changes in the expression profile of integrin αvβ3 during cardiac repair. The expression of β3 integrins is attributed to angiogenesis in the peri-infarct zone, peaking around 1 week after MI as part of the remodeling process. As inflammation resolves, collagen fibrils are cross-linked. Since CRIP is RGD receptor-specific, it does not bind typical collagen receptors such as α1β1 (Fig. 1) nor collagen type I or III fibers, resulting in lowered uptake as scar formation begins21. Similar results were reported with [99mTc]Tc-RIP (analog of CRIP without the Cy5.5 tag) in patients in a multimodality imaging study by ref. 22, where myocardial uptake of [99mTc]Tc-RIP peaked 3 weeks post-MI, corresponded to areas of perfusion defects identified through baseline myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI) and the location of scarring shown by late-gadolinium enhancement on MRI 1 year later (Fig. 2)22. Taken together, these results highlight the role of multimodality imaging in providing complementary information, bridging molecular, structural, and functional data for a more comprehensive diagnosis. These results also highlight the use of molecular imaging for early detection of active fibrosis, which may in the future be used as a marker for predicting cardiac remodeling and identifying patients for antifibrotic therapies22. Challenges associated with integrin imaging

One notable challenge with targeting the αvβ3 integrin is that it is also strongly expressed by macrophages and vascular endothelial cells25, which can complicate the interpretation of the imaging signal since uptake can also represent inflammation and angiogenesis, in addition to fibrotic activity. An opportunity for future development is the identification and targeting of cell-specific integrin subunits to increase pharmacologic specificity15.

Targeting collagen in cardiac fibrosis

Activated fibroblasts in the heart can differentiate into myofibroblasts which secrete more extracellular matrix proteins including multiple types of collagens, leading to the formation of a collagen scar10,26. Fibrosis is associated with an upregulation of several collagen types and collagen-modifying enzymes, including the structural type I, type III, and type IV collagen27,28. Caravan et al. identified a 16-amino-acid disulfide-bridged cyclic peptide through phage display to recognize type I human collagen. It was initially conjugated with Gd complex for use as an MR probe (EP-3533) and evaluated in cardiac fibrosis. Since MR probes typically have lower sensitivity than PET, the peptide structure features three chelator sites. This peptide has since been adapted for PET imaging using 68Ga bound through the tris(tert-butyl) ester-protected 1,4,7-triazacyclononane,1-glutaric acid-4,7-acetic acid (NODAGA) chelator, forming [68Ga]Ga-NODAGA-CBP8 (Table 1)29,30. Moon et al., described the use of dual-molecular probes, simultaneously targeting collagen I expression via [68Ga]Ga-CBP8 PET and allysine (biomarker for fibrogenesis) via an allysine-targeted fibrogenesis magnetic resonance enhancement probe (Gd-1,4) in a cardiopulmonary fibrosis model31. Elevated MR signal and radiotracer uptake three weeks post-transverse aortic constriction (TAC) surgery coincided with histologically confirmed interstitial fibrosis and increased right ventricular systolic pressure, adverse LV remodeling and reduced function. In a pulmonary fibrosis animal model, [68Ga]Ga-NODAGA-CBP8 has also been used for therapeutic monitoring of integrin αvβ6-targeted antibody antifibrotic treatment, showing a reduced PET signal that coincided with decreased hydroxyproline content and a lower histologic fibrosis score32, an application which could be translated to cardiac fibrosis in future studies for therapeutic monitoring of antifibrotic compounds.

An important recent development was reported by Chaher et al., on the first imaging probes that bind to collagen type III with high affinity and specificity, identified by screening several peptide-based candidates33. Both [68Ga]Ga-DOTAMA-CBP1 and [68Ga]Ga-DOTAMA-CBP2 (Table 1) showed no significant nonspecific uptake in any tissues and were cleared from the blood pool within 15–30 min. The MR analog labeled with Gd was evaluated in a post-MI model and demonstrated selective profiling of the natural turnover of collagen III, demonstrating a strong CMR signal in the infarct 10 days post-MI (when collagen III is elevated), that decreased at day 21 when collagen III is replaced by collagen I. There is an opportunity for future development to test the 68Ga-labeled version in the MI model. Given their distinct properties, changes in the ratio of collagen I to collagen III can influence the biomechanics of the heart and impair cardiac function. Mapping and quantifying the spatial and temporal changes in different types of collagens opens new possibilities for molecular studies of fibrosis mechanisms and development.

For targeting proteolyzed collagen IV, Niego et al. reported the use of a T-peptide PET tracer in a diffuse cardiac fibrosis model27. T-peptide was conjugated to the chelator 5-(8-methyl-3,6,10,13,16,19-hexaaza-bicyclo[6.6.6]icosan-1-ylamino)-5-oxopentanoic acid (MeCOSar) and radiolabeled with 64Cu (Table 1). PET/CT scans revealed significantly elevated [64Cu]Cu-T-peptide binding in the fibrotic hearts of aged transgenic β2-adrenergic receptor mice with diffuse cardiac fibrosis, and younger transgenic mice with intermediate cardiac fibrosis, compared with healthy respective controls27. The use of 64Cu (t1/2 = 12.70 h) as opposed to 68Ga (t1/2 = 67.71 min) allows for acquisition of later imaging time points, particularly useful for larger radiolabeled peptides, which may have slower background clearance.

Myocardial infarction and ischemia

Targeting integrins in myocardial infarction

Myocardial infarction (MI) is characterized by decreased or complete disruption of blood flow to the myocardium, more commonly known as a “heart attack”34. Perfusion radiotracers have thus been extensively used to study MI. Fibrogenesis is a prominent mechanism of cardiac repair after MI, and MI is often induced in animals to stimulate fibrotic activity, resulting in a notable overlap in molecular targets and, therefore, radiotracers evaluating fibrosis and MI, though they are distinct cardiomyopathies. Similarly, MI is also induced in animal models to study angiogenesis, cardiac inflammation, apoptosis and more, and thus is explored indirectly in other sections of this review. In this section, we highlight that in order to promote the clinical use of peptide-based radiotracers, there is a need for comparative studies exploring how perfusion imaging data, demonstrating disruption of blood flow, translating molecular imaging data and thereby shedding light on the molecular mechanisms of MI. As an example, Makowski et al. compared tracer uptake of [18F]F-Galacto-RGD (Table 2) targeting integrin αvβ3 and blood flow as measured by [13N]NH335. The authors noted that, in addition to the primary infarcted region, high [18F]F-Galacto-RGD uptake was also seen in areas with relatively normal or minimally compromised blood flow such as the border zone (Fig. 3). Targeting glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) in myocardial infarction

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) is a hormone which stimulates insulin secretion, mainly produced by intestinal L-cells36. Activation of GLP-1R signaling has been shown to have several cardioprotective effects, including protection against adverse remodeling and cardiac dysfunction after MI37. Stahle et al. showed that [68Ga]Ga-NODAGA-exendin-4, can be used as a probe for the upregulation of GLP-1R expression in the rat heart after MI. The authors reported 8.6-fold higher radiotracer uptake (autoradiography measurement) in the infarcted region, and an additional slightly elevated uptake in the remote, non-infarcted myocardium at 1- and 12-weeks post-MI (compared to sham). Moreover, the uptake of [68Ga]Ga-NODAGA-exendin-4 was shown to correlate with histological confirmation of CD68-positive macrophages in the infarcted area and alpha-smooth muscle actin staining in the remote myocardium38. Two radio-metabolites of the parent [68Ga]Ga-NODAGA-exendin-4 were identified via radio-HPLC analysis of the rat plasma and urine, which may confound interpretation of the imaging signal. Nonetheless, this is a significant step towards non-invasively probing GLP-1R expression, which may further illuminate its cardioprotective mechanisms and provide guidance for future therapeutic intervention of myocardial infarction/ischemia36,39.

Targeting EPO-BcR in myocardial ischemia

Myocardial ischemia is characterized as a decrease in the blood supply to the heart, often acting as a precursor to myocardial infarction. Therefore, early detection of cardiac ischemia is crucial for timely intervention and prevention of adverse cardiac events. Mohtavinejad et al. reported the synthesis and evaluation of [99mTc]Tc-DOTA-(Lys-Dabcyl6, Phe7)-ARA-290 (Table 2) as a potential radiotracer for targeting the cardiac ischemic region40. ARA-290 is a small peptide derived from the Helix B subunit of erythropoietin (EPO). Helix B is associated with protective effects of ischemic tissue, and ARA-290 exhibits an anti-hypoxia function by binding to the EPO-BcR receptor, through which it has been shown to decrease inflammation and apoptosis of ischemic myotubes in vitro41. To prolong the biological half-life of ARA-290 (~2 min in mice and rats) for imaging purposes, Mohtavinejad et al. substituted lipophilic Lys-(Dabcyl)-OH derivatives and Phe-amino acid in place of Arg6 and Ala7 of the parent peptide, respectively. The radiolabeled SPECT analog [99mTc]Tc-DOTA-(Lys-Dabcyl6 and Phe7)-ARA-290 showed significantly higher uptake in hypoxic than normoxic cells in vitro (~3-fold higher at 1 h)40.

Moreover, in vivo studies in a myocardial ischemia rat model showed significantly higher accumulation of the radiotracer in the cardiac region within 30 min post-injection, as compared to normal control (Fig. 4). Application of peptide-based radiotracers in angiogenesis

Angiogenesis (the formation of new blood vessels) is integral to wound healing and cardiac repair and is triggered as an adaptive response to ischemia42–44. There have been significant efforts to utilize angiogenic growth factors as a therapeutic strategy to improve nutritive blood flow to the ischemic myocardium2,45,46. On the other hand, dysregulated angiogenesis can result in the growth of abnormal blood vessels and vascular damage (Fig. 5)2,47,48. Indeed, angiogenesis and neovascularization have been implicated as key drivers of the growth of atherosclerotic regions in CVD and intraplaque hemorrhaging. Intraplaque angiogenesis inhibitors can therefore be deployed as a therapeutic strategy for CVD46,49. For these reasons, noninvasive molecular imaging tools for monitoring angiogenesis and pro/anti-angiogenic therapies are of high clinical value50.Fig. 5Normal versus abnormal angiogenesis.Normal and well-regulated growth of new blood vessels (angiogenesis) can restore nutritive blood flow to ischemic and injured regions of the heart, contributing to functional recovery. Targeting integrin αvβ3 in cardiac angiogenesis

As described earlier, RGD-based peptides are a key targeting vector for integrin αvβ3. Lang et al. 51 used [68Ga]Ga-NODAGA-RGD (Table 3) to monitor αvβ3 integrin expression in the healing process after MI in a mouse model. The post-MI animals showed significantly higher tracer uptake in the infarct region as compared to the sham surgery group (1.51 ± 0.36% ID/g vs. 0.72 ± 0.24% ID/g) when imaged at 7 days post induction. The specificity of the tracer was also tested by evaluating the uptake after preblocking with a competitive inhibitor, Cilengitide, which reduced uptake in MI animals to the level of sham animals. Lang et al., further assessed if [68Ga]Ga-NODAGA-RGD imaging of angiogenesis could serve as a predictor of left ventricular remodeling after cardiac stem cell therapy using murine-derived cardiac induced cells (CiC)51. Three weeks following induced MI, CiC transplantation significantly reduced cardiac remodeling, resulting in improved left ventricle ejection fraction (LVEF) and stroke volume (SV) as measured by cardiac MR. Surprisingly, the CiC-treated animals had significantly reduced focal [68Ga]Ga-NODAGA-RGD uptake in the infarct area measured at 7 days post-MI51. The authors reasoned that lower αvβ3-integrin expression in the infarct area could be due to reduced myocardial damage with the cell therapy, however, there was no difference in the scar size and wall thickness of the infarct between the control and therapy groups at the imaged time point (7 days post-MI). In a rat model, Sherif et al., showed that lower levels of αvβ3-integrin expression (i.e., less angiogenesis) measured by [18F]F-Galacto-RGD PET 7 days post-MI were associated with LV remodeling 12 weeks after acute MI52. Huang et al., further showed that in rats, cell transplantation therapy using human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and cord-blood mesenchymal stem cells (cbMSCs) resulted in increased uptake of [68Ga]Ga-RGD five days post-MI and was associated with less cardiac remodeling53. The increase in angiogenesis on day five was ascribed to therapeutically induced angiogenesis, which led to recovery in blood perfusion. These results highlight different patterns of myocardial healing and RGD-radiotracer uptake between species (e.g., mouse vs. rat). This suggests potential limitations in using angiogenesis alone as a prognostic marker for cardiac repair and functional outcome after MI51. In a human study (NCT01813045) of αVβ3 integrin expression following acute myocardial infarction, the uptake of targeting radiotracer [18F]Fluciclatide (Table 3) was compared with measures of MI severity and subsequent repair54. Uptake was specific to patients with acute infarction, particularly in regions of subendocardial infarction and hypokinesia, which were associated with subsequent functional recovery and did not bind to old established infarct. These findings suggested that [18F]Fluciclatide uptake reflects novel αvβ3 integrin expression due to re-endothelialization and angiogenesis in the peri-infarct zone, localizing segments that demonstrate later functional recovery and therefore is not a surrogate of infarction but better describes the tissue healing response. Targeting angiomotin in cardiac angiogenesis

Angiomotin (AMOT) is an angiostatin-binding protein expressed on human endothelium, which contributes to angiogenesis by controlling directional migration55. Elevated tissue expression of AMOT is associated with ischemic vascular diseases, making it a promising target for molecular imaging. Soluble cluster of differentiation 146 (sCD146) is an endogenous AMOT ligand presenting angiogenic properties56,57. Furthermore, the serum levels of sCD146 have been suggested as a novel biomarker of heart failure patients58,59. Moyon et al., evaluated [68Ga]Ga-NODAGA-sCD146 in a myocardial infarction model of angiogenesis and assessed its predictive value for myocardial tissue recovery60. The heart-to-muscle ratio of [68Ga]Ga-NODAGA-sCD146 uptake was significantly higher in the infarct region of MI animals than in SHAM. Moreover, early uptake of [68Ga]Ga-NODAGA-sCD146 was correlated with parameters such as late myocardial perfusion, lower tissue fibrosis assessed histologically, and improved functional recovery, indicating its potential as a valuable tool for assessing cardiac fibrosis and angiogenesis post-infarction. In the same study, the diagnostic value of AMOT expression and αvβ3-integrin expression was compared using [68Ga]Ga-NODAGA-sCD146 and [68Ga]Ga-RGD2, respectively60. While [68Ga]Ga-RGD2 (a dimer RGD peptide) was also shown to correlate positively with functional recovery (assessed by ultrasound measurement of LVEF), no significant correlation was found between [68Ga]Ga-RGD2 and delayed residual perfusion or fibrosis60. These results highlighted the sensitivity of [68Ga]Ga-NOGADA-sCD146 as a potential tool to predict early recovery of recent myocardial injury.

Application of peptide-based radiotracers in cardiac amyloidosis

Cardiac amyloidosis (CA) occurs when misfolded proteins deposit in the tissue and cause progressive organ damage. Most cases of CA are caused by either light chain amyloidosis (AL) or transthyretin amyloidosis (ATTR)61. The accumulation of amyloid fibrils in the heart can lead to progressive ventricular wall thickening and stiffness, which can develop into restrictive cardiomyopathy and eventually lead to heart failure62. CA is often observed in conjunction with systemic amyloidosis, encouraging diagnostic methods which allow for simultaneous multi-organ detection. Diagnosis of CA is also often delayed due to nonspecific symptoms at onset. Recently, novel therapies against CA have emerged63–65, but with often decreased effectiveness in advanced cases66. As such, there is an unmet clinical need for sensitive, quantitative and noninvasive cardiac amyloid quantification to aid in early diagnosis of amyloid cardiomyopathy (CMP) and treatment monitoring67,68. Current diagnostic tools include indirect inference through CMR and echocardiography. Bone-avid SPECT radiotracers such as 99mTc-labeled pyrophosphate (PYP) and 3-diphosphono-1,2-propanodicarboxylic acid (DPD) have also emerged as crucial clinical tools for evaluating CA69, but face several challenges, including poorly understood mechanism of action70 and suitability for ATTR CA only71.

Targeting amyloid deposits in cardiac amyloidosis

Amyloid reactive peptide p5 + 14 (Table 4) is a novel 4.7 kDa pan-amyloid targeting vector developed by ref. 72, binding all forms of amyloid through negatively charged glycosaminoglycans present within amyloid fibrils73. This synthetic polypeptide has been radiolabeled with 124I and 99mTc for PET and SPECT imaging of cardiac amyloidosis, respectively. Pan-amyloid tracers overcome the challenge of etiological heterogeneity of systemic disease74. In a preclinical model of mice with induced amyloid protein A (AA) associated amyloidosis, [99mTc]Tc-p5 + 14 detected hepatosplenic AA amyloid and heart deposits with higher sensitivity than [99mTc]Tc-PYP. Application of peptide-based radiotracers in cardiac inflammation/atherosclerosis

Cardiac inflammation is a complex cellular and tissue response to pathogenic aggression and environmental stimuli79. When triggered by toxins, microbial agents, cellular debris or other, signaling cascades are activated, recruiting inflammatory cells and secreting inflammatory mediators in resident immunocytes and cardiac cells (Fig. 8)80. When properly regulated, the inflammatory response constitutes a key defense mechanism against these stimuli79. On the other hand, chronic and elevated cardiac inflammation is considered to be a major etiopathogenetic mechanism of a broad variety of cardiovascular diseases, including atherosclerosis, myocarditis, cardiac sarcoidosis and coronary artery disease81–85. [18F]FDG has been used for PET imaging of cardiac inflammation86–88, but as a metabolic radiotracer lacks molecular and cellular specificity and is often subject to “cardiac spillover” due to myocardial muscle uptake89. Targeting SSTR2 in cardiac inflammation

Somatostatin receptor subtype 2 (SSTR2) is a G-protein-coupled receptor expressed on the surface of activated macrophages, which can be targeted using the DOTATATE peptide and radiolabeled with 68Ga or 64Cu for the study of inflammatory cardiac diseases. Targeting CXCR4 in cardiac inflammation

C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4) is a leukocyte G-protein-coupled receptor which contributes to the mediation and resolution of inflammation in patients after acute MI. The balance between triggering and resolving cardiac inflammation is delicate and critical. This balance determines whether a healing scar forms or if prolonged restrictive remodeling occurs, which leads to contractile dysfunction and adverse cardiac events79,97. CXCR4 expression is upregulated by inflammatory modulators such as transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)98. As such, while CXCR4 is expressed at low levels on circulating lymphocytes, neutrophils and macrophages, it becomes highly upregulated as these cells infiltrate inflamed tissues, making it an attractive target for noninvasive identification of activated inflammatory cells99. C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12) is a ligand to CXCR4, and the CXCR4-CXCL12 axis is of significant interest for understanding the progression and modulation of inflammation in cardiac diseases98,100–102. [68Ga]Ga-Pentixafor (Table 5) is a radiolabeled analog of CXCL12 and has been used for PET-based evaluation of CXCR4 status in inflammatory atherosclerosis and post-MI inflammation103–105. Thackeray et al. compared [68Ga]Ga-Pentixafor uptake in mice with ex vivo autoradiography, immunohistochemistry and LV flow cytometry106. [68Ga]Ga-Pentixafor identified CXCR4 upregulation in the infarct region, which peaked at 3 days post-MI and corresponded to a flow cytometry-based peak of CD45+ leukocytes and detection of CD68+ macrophages and Ly6G+ granulocytes via immunohistochemistry. Moreover, uptake could be abolished by blocking with the CXCR4 antagonist AMD3100, demonstrating the specificity of the radiotracer. In the same study, [68Ga]Ga-Pentixafor was also assessed in 12 patients 2–8 days after acute MI. Overall, uptake patterns were more heterogeneous than in the controlled animal model106. No single clinical factor predicted reliably the variation, though infarct size and early imaging time moderately correlated with signal strength. Further analysis showed that segments with elevated [68Ga]Ga-Pentixafor uptake had higher perfusion defect score, wall motion impairment, late-gadolinium enhancement (LGE) and edema scores than segments with lower uptake106. The heterogeneity in CXCR4 upregulation may have prognostic and therapeutic implications for novel drugs targeting CXCR4 or others aimed at modulating post-infarct inflammation. To this end, noninvasive imaging of CXCR4 expression using [68Ga]Ga-Pentixafor may help select ideal candidates for novel CXCR4 targeting therapies106. Towards this goal, Hess et al. evaluated [68Ga]Ga-Pentixafor PET image-guided CXCR4 targeted therapy107. In mouse studies (n = 180 total), CXCR4 expression was elevated in the infarct region over 3 days after MI compared with sham. Early and persistent uptake was a predictor of left ventricle (LV) rupture and worse functional outcome at 6 weeks (assessed via cardiac MR). Mice treated with CXCR4 blocker AMD3100 at peak expression 3 days post-MI (identified via PET) lowered LV rupture incidence compared to untreated MI (8% vs. 25%), and significantly improved contractile function at 6 weeks (+24%), supporting that CXCR4 inhibition accelerates inflammatory resolution and improves outcome in mice107. Importantly, the off-peak CXCR4 blockade for 7 days did not improve the outcome. In the same study, patients (n = 50) early (3–5 days) after MI underwent [68Ga]Ga-Pentixafor PET imaging and functional assessment with CMR. Similar to the report by Thackeray et al., patients demonstrated elevated but heterogenous signal in the infarct region. CXCR4 expression in the infarct region correlated with contractile function at the time of the original PET scan and on follow-up, emphasizing the relevance of noninvasive and personalized assessment for optimal therapeutic translation107.

Targeting CCR2 in cardiac inflammation

C-C chemokine receptor type 2 (CCR2) has a high level of expression on inflammatory populations of macrophages and monocytes108. Unlike CXCR4, which is expressed on several immune cell populations, including monocytes and macrophages, but also neutrophils, T cells and endothelial cells, CCR2 is more selectively expressed on monocytes and macrophages that also overexpress inflammatory mediators109. CCR2− macrophages have been shown anti-inflammatory effects and regulation of coronary angiogenesis and cardiac repair, while CCR2+ macrophages accumulate in the injured heart and drive inflammatory responses by generating oxidative species, cytokines (IL-1β and IL-6) and chemokines (such as CCL2), which trigger neutrophil and monocyte infiltration108–110. In hearts with ischemic injury, the recruitment of CCR2+ monocytes/macrophages has been correlated with adverse remodeling, thereby marking CCR2 as a therapeutic target with several antagonists under investigation83,111,112. Indeed, the removal of CCR2+ macrophages has been shown to attenuate heart failure by reducing inflammation, fibrosis and collateral myocardial injury108,110,112. Therefore, molecular imaging of CCR2 may be of significant clinical value for noninvasive assessment of cardiac inflammation and identification of patients most likely to respond to immunomodulatory therapies.

Extracellular loop 1 inverso (ECL1i) is a linear peptide containing seven amino acids binding to the allosteric site of CCR2113,114. Heo et al. showed that [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-ECL1i (Table 5) had a favorable biodistribution in naïve mice with fast clearance and minimal retention in major organs. In two models of cardiac injury (reperfused myocardial infarction and diphtheria toxin-induced cardiomyocyte ablation), [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-ECL1i had high myocardial uptake localizing to sites of tissue injury. This uptake was diminished in CCR2−/− mice with ischemia-reperfusion cardiac injury, demonstrating the specificity of the tracer and its utility in visualizing CCR2+ monocyte recruitment and inflammatory macrophage accumulation115,116. Lavine et al. showed that [64Cu]Cu-DOTA-ECL1i specifically bound to CD14+ monocytes and macrophages but not to neutrophils, T cells or B cells in vitro108. In the same study, seven patients who suffered a ST-segment elevation MI (STEMI) involving a major coronary artery and underwent subsequent percutaneous coronary intervention were imaged at various time points (1–164 days) post-MI with [64Cu]Cu-DOTA-ECL1i to identify CCR2 uptake and [99mTc]Tc-Tetrofosmin perfusion imaging against six healthy controls. As expected, homogeneous myocardial uptake of [99mTc]Tc-Tetrofosmin (implying normal myocardial perfusion) and minimal uptake of [64Cu]Cu-DOTA-ECL1i (consistent with a noninflammatory state) was observed in the healthy patient controls. In contrast, co-registration of SPECT/CT perfusion and [64Cu]Cu-DOTA-ECL1i PET/CT images of patients with STEMI suggested that CCR2 tracer uptake was localized to the infarct region and correlated with the perfusion deficit and regional wall motion abnormalities108. Moreover, [64Cu]Cu-DOTA-ECL1i uptake remained elevated within the infarct region across all time points assessed (up to 164 days), suggesting that CCR2+ monocytes and macrophages may persist in the heart after injury, though studies with a larger patient population are needed to verify this108.

Targeting CCR5 for cardiac inflammation imaging

C-C Chemokine Receptor 5 (CCR5) is upregulated in monocytes/macrophages and T-lymphocytes and plays a significant role in cardiac inflammation, primarily by recruiting inflammatory cells to the myocardium and activating inflammatory pathways. This leads to increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the release of other inflammatory factors117,118 (Fig. 8). The CCL5-CCR5 axis in particular has been studied extensively in the context of inflammatory atherosclerosis118–121. CCR5 status has been shown to serve as a prognostic marker for atherosclerosis progression, and CCR5 antagonists are under evaluation for their potential as inflammation modulators121–123. CCR5 imaging probes, therefore, have clinical value for illuminating atherogenesis and serve as a diagnostic tool for treatment with CCR5 antagonists124.

CCR5-targeted PET imaging has been reported using the threonine rich octapeptide D-Ala-peptide T-amide (DAPTA), radiolabeled with 64Cu125–127. Luehmann et al. demonstrated CCR5-specific binding and imaging using [64Cu]Cu-DOTA-DAPTA (Table 5) in a vascular injury mouse model and further evaluated [64Cu]Cu-DOTA-DAPTA-comb nanoparticles, which had significantly higher accumulation at the injury lesions. The DAPTA conjugated nanoparticles were then assessed in an atherosclerosis animal model by the same group, showing sensitive and specific detection of CCR5 along the progression of atherosclerotic lesions128. Wei et al. also evaluated [111In]In-DOTA-DAPTA for SPECT imaging of inflammation in an atherosclerosis mouse model129. In vitro cell binding assays confirmed significantly higher uptake in CCR5-expressing cells compared to negative controls, which was diminished when non-radiolabeled DAPTA was added for blocking, confirming the specific uptake of the radiotracer. Biodistribution in wild-type C57BL/6 control mice showed favorable pharmacokinetics and major clearance through the renal system, though the authors noted a high splenic uptake, consistent with studies done with [64Cu]Cu-DOTA-DAPTA126, attributed to a high concentration of chemokine receptors in the mononuclear phagocyte system containing CCR5-expressing splenic monocytes129. Still, ex vivo autoradiography showed that ApoE−/− mice had higher uptake of [111In]In-DOTA-DAPTA in atherosclerotic aortic lesions compared to the C57BL/6 mice. Blocking studies with DAPTA further confirmed in vivo specificity for CCR5 of the radiotracer129.

Targeting integrin αvβ3 in cardiac inflammation

Integrin αvβ3 mediates intraplaque angiogenesis is a key contributor to early atherogenic inflammation130. The expression of integrin αvβ3 by macrophages and endothelial cells in atherosclerotic lesions makes RGD-based peptides of interest for studying atherosclerosis. Laitinen et al. evaluated [18F]F-Galacto-RGD for noninvasive imaging of inflammation in atherosclerosis using hypercholesterolemic LDLR−/−ApoB100/100 mice on a western diet and normally fed adult C57BL/6 control mice131. In comparison to the adjacent normal vessel wall or adventitia, [18F]F-Galacto-RGD showed high and specific uptake in the atherosclerotic lesions of mouse aorta. Histological analysis showed that the uptake was associated with macrophage density in the lesions131.

Application of peptide-based radiotracers in cardiac cell apoptosis

Apoptosis, a regulated from of programmed cell death, plays a central role in several cardiovascular diseases including atherosclerosis, reperfusion injury, myocardial infarction, non-ischemic cardiomyopathies, as well as in viral and auto-immune myocarditis132–134. There has been a significant research effort applied to understanding the molecular mechanisms governing apoptosis and even the inhibition of cardiac apoptosis as an effective therapeutic strategy for cardiovascular diseases135,136. Noninvasive imaging could be useful for monitoring cell death dynamics and, by extension, the effectiveness of therapies aimed at decreasing cell death137.

Targeting phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) and phosphatidylserine (PS)

A hallmark of early apoptosis is the loss of cell membrane asymmetry, resulting in the translocation of certain phospholipids from the inner to the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane. Phosphatidylserine (PS) and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), which are normally predominantly located on the inner leaflet, become exposed on the cell surface. This exposure occurs relatively early in the apoptotic process and provides a high density of binding sites for targeted imaging agents that can access the extracellular space. The consistent appearance of PS and PE on the surface of dying cells makes them primary targets for radiotracers to image apoptosis in cardiac tissues (Fig. 9). Apoptosis imaging has been studied extensively using radiolabeled annexin V, a 35–36 kDa human protein exhibiting high binding affinity (0.1–7 nM) to PS. In cardiology, [99mTc]Tc-Annexin V has been used in clinical studies of MI, heart failure, atherosclerosis, cardiac allograft rejection, and arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC), where it has shown value in identifying cardiac cell death138. However, its molecular weight results in relatively slow clearance and high blood and liver background signals and non-target organ radiation burden139. Radiolabeled peptide alternatives also targeting PS, such as [64Cu]Cu-NOTA-PSBP-6, have been evaluated in oncological models, but to the best of the authors’ knowledge, have not yet been successfully utilized in cardiology studies140. Duramycin, a cyclic peptide composed of 19 amino acids, has emerged as a promising alternative to Annexin V for imaging apoptosis in cardiology. Unlike Annexin V, which targets PS, Duramycin exhibits high affinity and exclusive specificity for phosphatidylethanolamine (PE). Tan et al. used [99mTc]Tc-Duramycin (Table 6) to dynamically monitor myocardial cell death in acute MI and the anti-apoptotic effect of atorvastatin for cardioprotection141. Uptake peaked 3 days post-MI, coinciding with peak expression of TUNEL+ cells (indicating DNA fragmentation, a hallmark of apoptosis). Western blot analysis also confirmed that the pro-apoptotic protein BAX was upregulated compared to SHAM control animals at days 1, 3, and 7 (all time points studied) while anti-apoptotic BCL-2 was downregulated. For the atorvastatin-treated group, 20 mg/(kg day) was administered orally 24 h before the acute MI surgery. [99mTc]Tc-Duramycin uptake also peaked at day 3 post-MI in the treated group, but was lower than in the untreated MI group. Lastly, several studies have explored the use of [99mTc]Tc-Duramycin for imaging atherosclerotic plaques, where uptake has been shown to correlate with the progression of atherosclerosis and could potentially serve as a marker for identifying high-risk plaques143. Studies directly comparing with [99mTc]Tc-Annexin-V have shown that [99mTc]Tc-Duramycin imaging is just as effective at detecting myocardial apoptosis but is associated with less radiation burden due to the improved pharmacokinetics, which increases its translational potential144.

Targeting caspase-3 in cardiac cell apoptosis

Caspases are an enzyme family of cysteine proteases involved in the initiation and execution of cell apoptosis. Among these, caspase-3 is often referred to as the main executioner caspase, responsible for cleaving numerous cellular proteins, leading to the characteristic morphological and biochemical changes associated with apoptosis145. Direct targeting of caspase-3 could therefore more specifically indicate cells in the final stages of apoptosis. [18F]F-CP18 (Table 6), originally identified and validated by Su et al., was the first substrate-based apoptosis targeting peptide used for PET imaging in clinical trials146. Caspase-3 demonstrates a preference for the tetrapeptide sequence Asp-Glu-X-Asp (DEXD) as a substrate, cleaving the peptide bond at the C-terminal of the aspartic acid residue. CP18 incorporates the tetra-peptidic amino acid sequence Asp-Glu-Val-Asp (D-E-V-D), designed to act as a substrate for activated caspase-3.

Mechanistically, [18F]F-CP18 crosses the cell membrane at which point the substrate is cleaved in the presence of activated caspase-3, allowing a polar 18F-tagged DEVD metabolite to accumulate in the cytoplasm of apoptotic cells. The authors also report that incorporation of a short PEG chain resulted in higher tracer uptake in apoptosis-induced cells, due to enhanced cell membrane permeability, and better pharmacokinetics in vivo. CP18 is radiolabeled with 18F using the Cu(I)-catalyzed Huisgen reaction (“click chemistry”)146. The same group later investigated cardiac [18F]F-CP18 uptake in a mouse model of anthracycline cardiotoxicity. Anthracycline-based chemotherapies such as doxorubicin are effective against a range of cancers, but are associated with cardiotoxic side effects, potentially leading to cardiomyopathy and resulting heart failure in a dose-dependent manner147. Su et al. showed that doxorubicin treatment, which is associated with increased myocardial caspase-3 expression, resulted in increased [18F]F-CP18 uptake in mice, suggesting its potential utility for the detection of anthracycline-induced myocardial apoptosis148. In an atherosclerosis model, Tamarappoo et al. demonstrated that uptake of [18F]F-CP18 in aortic plaque strongly correlated with high levels of apoptotic activity by TUNEL staining and macrophage entry detected by CD68 staining, highlighting the potential use of this radiopeptide for detecting plaque apoptosis and identifying high-risk lesions149.

Non-receptor-specific radiolabeled cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs)

Upregulation and overexpression of certain receptors during/after cardiac injury allows for a high concentration of the radiotracer to accumulate in the target region, relative to healthy controls, and enables spatial and temporal evaluation of the molecular progression of disease. However, none of the receptors discussed thus far are strictly cardiac or cardiac-disease specific. For the development of cardiac-targeted therapies, a lack of tissue-specific surface receptors limits the effectiveness of traditional cardiotropic drug delivery. Therefore, non-receptor-based cardiac therapies utilizing cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) as a carrier-vector have emerged as a promising approach. CPPs are characterized by their ability to breach cell membrane barriers while delivering intact, functional therapeutic “cargos”, many times their own size. However, their general nonspecific tissue distribution can limit clinical applications. To aid in the development and evaluation of CCPs and their therapeutic drug-conjugates, PET and SPECT imaging with radiolabeled analogs serves as an invaluable tool, providing pharmacokinetic information for quantitative analysis of tissue uptake.

TAT-heart8P

Settelmeier et al. used PET to track the biodistribution and cardiac availability of an octapeptide drug (heart8P) conjugated to HIV-1 trans-activator of transcription (TAT) domain amino acid residues 48–59 (PTD, GRKKRRQRRRPQ), which served as the intracellular delivery system. The TAT-heart8P peptide-conjugate was radiolabeled with 68Ga via the NOGADA chelator, to form the overall nonspecific, cell-targeting and hydrophilic [68Ga]Ga-NODAGA-TAT-heart8P (Table 7)150. Ex vivo heart-to-blood ratio was 4:1 within 10 min of administration, suggesting rapid pharmacokinetics. Cardiac targeting peptide (CTP)

Targeted delivery of therapeutics to the heart is a core challenge in achieving high treatment efficacy with minimal off-target toxicities and, particularly in cardiology, is impeded by a lack of cardiac-specific vectors. Nonspecific uptake of CPPs in multiple tissue-cell types similarly limits their clinical utility. In 2010, Zahid et al. used phage display methodology to identify a 12-amino acid peptide sequence (NH2-APWHLSSQYSRT-COOH), capable of specifically targeting cardiomyocytes, termed as cardiac targeting peptide (CTP). As a platform technology, this has opened several opportunities for the development of cardiac-targeted therapeutics and diagnostic imaging151. The same group evaluated the biodistribution of CTP radiolabeled analog [99mTc]Tc-HYNIC-CTP (Table 7) in comparison with [99mTc]Tc-Sestamibi (clinical gold standard for perfusion imaging) in wild-type mice using SPECT152. In addition to the cardiac uptake of [99mTc]Tc-Sestamibi, significant uptake is seen in the liver, gut, and kidneys (Fig. 11a). In contrast, [99mTc]Tc-HYNIC-CTP showed almost no uptake in the liver or gut, while cardiac uptake peaked as early as 5 min post-injection. A gradual increase in the kidney and bladder suggested a renal excretion route (Fig. 11b)152. Improved organ-targeting with rapid pharmacokinetics translates to reduced radiation burden on non-target organs, ultimately improving the safety and widening the utility of nuclear cardiology scans. For applications in PET imaging, Pandey et al. evaluated [68Ga]Ga-NOTA-CTP (Table 7) for perfusion imaging in CD-1 mice153–155. Similarly to [99mTc]Tc-HYNIC-CTP, the cardiac uptake peaked within 5 min. Within 2 h post-administration, the radiotracer had mostly flushed out from all major organs, with radioactivity present predominantly in the urinary bladder and extremely small quantities in the cecum and small intestine. Conclusion

Peptide-based radiotracers are contributing significantly to the evolution of cardiovascular medicine towards more personalized and precise diagnostics. Receptor-specific radiotracers move beyond traditional perfusion-based assessments, providing an opportunity to enhance diagnostic accuracy and offer a more comprehensive understanding of disease mechanisms at the molecular level. The development of these advanced imaging tools, spurred by progress in the cellular and molecular biology of cardiovascular diseases, leverages the inherent advantages of peptides, such as their favorable biodistribution profiles and the ability to be finely tuned for bioefficacy and specificity. This review aimed to demonstrate, through select examples, how peptide-based PET and SPECT radiotracers are being developed and utilized to study a range of cardiovascular diseases by targeting specific receptors and signaling mechanisms. For the continued advancement and widespread clinical adoption of these innovative radiotracers, their integration with established diagnostic methods is paramount, emphasizing the value of multimodality imaging in providing a holistic view of cardiovascular disease. The pursuit extends to creating highly selective biomolecules and even cardiac-specific peptides, which could further refine diagnostic capabilities and pave the way for targeted therapeutic strategies. Peptide-based PET and SPECT imaging represents a significant step forward in nuclear cardiology. By enabling the noninvasive visualization and quantification of molecular processes, these techniques hold immense promise for improving early disease detection, guiding personalized treatment decisions, and ultimately enhancing patient outcomes in a new era of precision cardiovascular medicine.
