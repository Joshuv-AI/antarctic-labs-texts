HNF1B-related disease: developmental origins, molecular mechanisms, and multisystem clinical challenges
Authors: Petr Heneberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVariants or deletions involving the HNF1B gene cause HNF1B-MODY. HNF1B-MODY represents a multisystem disorder extending far beyond its original classification as a monogenic form of diabetes. HNF1B’s role in human development includes critical involvement in the organogenesis of the kidney, pancreas, liver, genitourinary tract, and nervous system. Approximately half of affected individuals carry large 17q12 deletions encompassing HNF1B and neighboring genes, correlating with broader syndromic features such as intellectual disability, autism spectrum traits, and psychiatric illness. The increasing availability of next-generation sequencing, coupled with copy number variant analysis, has improved diagnostic yield. Underdiagnosis remains common due to variable expressivity and phenotypic overlap with more prevalent disorders. Notable progress has been made in dissecting tissue-specific roles of HNF1B. Patient-derived iPSC models and single-cell transcriptomics offer new insights into early developmental pathways and transcriptional networks. These tools enable organoid-based studies and the potential discovery of therapeutics. Clinically, awareness of symptoms, such as renal cysts, persistent hypomagnesemia, pancreatic hypoplasia, and non-autoimmune diabetes, has led to the development of diagnostic scoring systems and multidisciplinary care algorithms. Although current treatment remains supportive, research into gene regulation, epigenetic modifiers, and pharmacologic rescue strategies is ongoing. HNF1B-related disease illustrates how pleiotropic gene dysfunction can present with diverse organ involvement. Integrating genomics, developmental biology, and targeted surveillance may help improve early diagnosis and patient outcomes.Keywords: 17q12 deletion, copy number variation, monogenic diabetes, multisystem disease, renal cysts, transcription factor

INTRODUCTION

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a group of monogenic forms of diabetes caused by primary β-cell dysfunction, typically characterized by early onset, autosomal dominant inheritance, preserved endogenous insulin secretion, and the absence of pancreatic autoimmunity. Although MODY accounts for a minority of diabetes cases, correct recognition has major implications for treatment, prognosis, and family screening [1].

HNF1B-MODY [also referred to as MODY5 and clinically overlapping with the renal cysts and diabetes (RCAD) spectrum] is distinctive within MODY because it frequently presents with renal developmental anomalies and multisystem involvement that may precede, accompany, or even overshadow hyperglycemia. In addition to non-autoimmune diabetes, common manifestations include congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT), cystic or dysplastic kidneys, hypomagnesemia due to renal wasting, pancreatic hypoplasia with variable exocrine dysfunction, and variable hepatic and genitourinary abnormalities. Approximately half of affected individuals carry a recurrent 17q12 deletion encompassing HNF1B and neighboring genes, which increases the likelihood of syndromic features, including neurodevelopmental and psychiatric involvement [2, 3]. This broader clinical spectrum is clinically important because it contributes to under-recognition and frequent misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes when diabetes is the presenting feature, or to isolated nephrology evaluation when renal findings dominate [1]. At the same time, recent work has expanded understanding of how HNF1B controls tissue-specific gene regulation during development, including chromatin-level mechanisms and insights from iPSC-derived organoid models [4]. A recent study by Isnard et al. [5] also connected HNF1B activity to the progression of common chronic kidney disease (CKD). In mice, postnatal loss of Hnf1b in renal tubular epithelium led to rapid tubulointerstitial injury, cell-cycle reentry, replication stress, and fibrosis. In addition, CKD-associated stresses can reduce HNF1B activity via decreased accessibility at HNF1B binding sites, consistent with epigenetic repression. In human CKD transcriptomic datasets, including an external cohort of 897 kidney samples with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) data (and 821 with histology), lower expression of HNF1B target genes correlated with reduced kidney function and greater tubular atrophy and fibrosis [5]. In this review, we therefore integrate (i) clinical phenotypes and diagnostic strategies with (ii) developmental and molecular mechanisms to provide a unified setting for understanding and managing HNF1B-related disease.

Practical diagnostic approach (with pediatric considerations)

HNF1B-related disease should be suspected in any patient with CAKUT plus at least one extrarenal feature, including persistent or unexplained hypomagnesemia, pancreatic hypoplasia or low fecal elastase-1, non-autoimmune diabetes, mild unexplained liver enzyme elevation, or genitourinary malformations [3, 6]. In pediatric practice, renal findings often precede diabetes; therefore, limiting consideration to “diabetes with renal cysts” can miss children who present first with prenatal or childhood renal hyperechogenicity, cysts, dysplasia, hypoplasia, or multicystic dysplastic kidney [7, 8]. De novo events are common, and recurrent 17q12 deletions account for a substantial proportion of cases and increase the likelihood of syndromic features, including neurodevelopmental involvement [2, 9].

Early-life presentation is commonly renal-first and may begin in utero. Prenatal ultrasound findings that should raise suspicion include bilateral hyperechogenic kidneys, cysts, dysplasia, or reduced corticomedullary differentiation; in large fetal series, HNF1B abnormalities were a leading genetic cause of bilateral hyperechogenic kidneys [7]. Postnatally, infants and children may present with CAKUT phenotypes ranging from renal cysts and dysplasia to hypoplasia or multicystic dysplastic kidney, often with slowly progressive kidney dysfunction and limited proteinuria [3]. In childhood cohorts, kidney function frequently declines slowly, but very early-onset presentations can be more severe and require closer follow-up [8].

The clinical picture may broaden over time from isolated CAKUT to the RCAD spectrum, with diabetes sometimes appearing in adolescence or adulthood rather than childhood. In parallel, biochemical clues can emerge later, including hypomagnesemia due to renal wasting and evidence of pancreatic exocrine dysfunction, which may remain subclinical unless actively tested [10, 11]. This age-dependent evolution supports a longitudinal approach in pediatric patients with CAKUT, in which renal imaging and kidney function monitoring are complemented by periodic reassessment for electrolyte abnormalities and emerging pancreatic/endocrine involvement, even when diabetes is not present at the time of initial nephrology evaluation [3, 8].

A practical first-line evaluation starts with a renal ultrasound to document the CAKUT pattern and basic laboratory assessment, including serum magnesium (and potassium and sodium), because hypomagnesemia may be overlooked unless measured and can be a key diagnostic clue when renal wasting is confirmed [11, 12]. If hyperglycemia is present, diabetes classification should include pancreatic autoantibodies and C-peptide, because HNF1B-associated diabetes is typically non-autoimmune and is frequently misclassified [1, 13]. When gastrointestinal symptoms, poor weight gain, or nutritional deficiencies suggest malabsorption, fecal elastase-1 testing can identify biochemical exocrine pancreatic dysfunction and support decisions regarding pancreatic enzyme replacement [10, 14]. Genetic testing should be selected to detect both intragenic HNF1B variants and 17q12 copy number variants (CNVs), because sequencing alone may miss recurrent deletions. For renal–pancreatic presentations, HNF1B sequencing combined with validated CNV analysis, for example, deletion/duplication analysis by MLPA or an NGS panel with validated CNV calling, is appropriate. When developmental delay or other syndromic features suggest a broader 17q12 deletion syndrome, chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) should be prioritized because it can define the genomic extent of the CNV and assess for additional pathogenic CNVs [15, 16]. In children, clinical features evolve with age, so diagnostic decisions should not depend solely on adult-derived scoring tools; pediatric-focused prediction tools may help prioritize HNF1B testing in CAKUT cohorts [17, 18].

HNF1B-related disease should be suspected in any child or adult with CAKUT plus at least one supportive feature from another organ system, or with a characteristic combination of renal and biochemical findings. In pediatric patients, suspicion should not depend on the presence of diabetes, because renal abnormalities often present first and endocrine features may appear later [7]. Genetic testing should be performed in the following settings: (i) CAKUT with bilateral hyperechogenic kidneys, renal cysts/dysplasia, or multicystic dysplastic kidney, particularly when the presentation is prenatal or early postnatal; (ii) CAKUT with persistent hypomagnesemia or documented renal magnesium wasting; (iii) non-autoimmune diabetes (autoantibody-negative) with any renal structural abnormality, pancreatic hypoplasia, or otherwise unexplained tubular electrolyte disturbance; and (iv) suspected syndromic disease (developmental delay, autism spectrum traits, seizures, or psychiatric features) with renal and/or glycemic involvement, where a 17q12 deletion is a consideration [9]. Practical clinical symptoms that should prompt testing, especially in pediatric cohorts, include prenatal or early-life bilateral hyperechogenic kidneys, small non-enlarging renal cysts, renal dysplasia/hypoplasia, multicystic dysplastic kidney, persistent hypomagnesemia, and a renal-first presentation with later emergence of pancreatic or glycemic abnormalities. Because recurrent 17q12 deletions account for a substantial proportion of cases, testing strategies should include CNV detection in addition to sequencing when clinical suspicion is present [2].

HNF1B VARIANTS LANDSCAPE AND MOLECULAR MECHANISMS

The HNF1B gene, located on chromosome 17q12, encodes hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta, a homeodomain transcription factor indispensable during embryonic development of the pancreas, kidneys, liver, and genitourinary tract. Heterozygous variants, including missense, nonsense, frameshift, and splice-site variants, account for approximately half of HNF1B–MODY cases. The remaining cases result from 17q12 microdeletions. These deletions encompass the entire HNF1B locus and adjacent genes [19, 20]. Missense and small in-frame variants are enriched in exons encoding the Pit-Oct-Unc (POU)-specific and homeodomain DNA-binding regions [3, 21, 22]. Their mechanisms of pathogenicity include haploinsufficiency, dominant-negative interference, and disruption of nuclear localization [23, 24]. In comparison, recurrent 17q12 deletions (1.3–1.8 Mb) are typically associated with syndromic phenotypes, including neurodevelopmental disorders, reflecting loss of HNF1B and other genes in the region [19]. Importantly, 30%–50% of new cases are caused by de novo variants [7, 25, 26], with important implications for clinical suspicion and contrasting the traditional perception of MODY as a familial monogenic disorder.

At the molecular level, HNF1B functions as a transcriptional regulator that acts as a homodimer or heterodimer (with HNF1A) and binds to promoter regions of key developmental genes. Loss of function impairs transcription of targets that are critical for organ development, such as FXYD2 and PKHD1 in the kidney and PDX1, SOX9, and HNF6 in the pancreas [6]. Mouse genetics show that loss of Hnf1b during early development disrupts pancreatic formation and conditional loss in renal lineages impairs ureteric bud branching and subsequent nephrogenesis, indicating tissue-specific developmental requirements [27–29]. Conditional inactivation of Hnf1b in mouse pancreatic progenitor cells causes severe hypoplasia due to reduced progenitor expansion, abnormal ductal morphology, and absence of endocrine precursors [30].

The landscape of HNF1B variants, ranging from single-nucleotide variants to large genomic deletions, directly shapes the diverse clinical phenotype of HNF1B–MODY. The gene’s key role in a transcriptional network that coordinates morphogenesis across multiple organs defines it as a master regulator whose dosage and structural integrity influence both molecular and clinical variability. Mechanistic studies converge on a clinically relevant point: reduced HNF1B activity disrupts epithelial differentiation programs in the kidney and pancreas, and this disruption maps onto the CAKUT/HNF1B–MODY/RCAD spectrum [6]. In the developing kidney, altered HNF1B dosage perturbs tubular lineage specification and segment identity, which is consistent with congenital structural anomalies and later susceptibility to tubulointerstitial injury and CKD progression [28]. In the pancreas, impaired HNF1B-dependent ductal and progenitor programs can lead to pancreatic hypoplasia, variable exocrine dysfunction, and non-autoimmune diabetes, helping explain why endocrine and exocrine findings often co-occur [30]. Kidney injury states can reduce HNF1B-associated chromatin accessibility and target-gene expression, linking epithelial stress responses to progressive CKD in models and human biopsy cohorts [5]. These observations help explain the broad expressivity of HNF1B variants, in which early developmental anomalies (CAKUT) may be the initial presentation and later metabolic features define the HNF1B–MODY/RCAD phenotype [6]. At a molecular level, these effects are consistent with HNF1B acting at promoters and distal regulatory elements to maintain epithelial gene expression programs required for organ development and tubular homeostasis.

Evidence for alternative splicing adds another layer of complexity. Alternative HNF1B transcript isoforms may contribute to tissue-specific gene regulation and phenotypic variability among patients with HNF1B-related disease [31]. Post-transcriptional regulation by noncoding RNAs has emerged more recently. Several noncoding RNAs and epigenetic regulators have been implicated in modulating HNF1B-associated transcriptional programs and may contribute to phenotypic variability and the progression of kidney disease. Although these mechanisms remain primarily experimental, they may help explain interindividual variability and could inform future therapeutic strategies [32–36].

Although HNF1A and HNF1B share similar DNA-binding domains and regulate overlapping gene sets in some epithelial tissues, their roles during pancreatic development appear largely non-redundant. Experimental studies support a distinct role of HNF1B in pancreatic development and endocrine lineage specification [30, 37–39]. From a clinical perspective, these mechanistic findings show that HNF1B-related disease should be viewed as a multisystem developmental disorder with highly variable expressivity, supporting early CNV-aware genetic testing and longitudinal multispecialty follow-up.

PANCREATIC CONSEQUENCES: DEVELOPMENTAL, ENDOCRINE, AND EXOCRINE ASPECTS

Pancreatic abnormalities are a core feature of HNF1B-related disease, manifesting as both endocrine and exocrine dysfunction (Table 2). Mouse models that mimic human HNF1B intron-2 splicing variants exhibit pancreatic hypoplasia, with ∼30% reduction in β-cell volume and significant decreases in pancreatic insulin content. These findings align with the developmental disruption of endocrine lineage specification in utero. These animals develop glucose intolerance resembling adolescent-onset diabetes in human carriers, despite preserved islet responsiveness to glucose stimulation [4].

Exocrine pancreatic impairment is also frequently observed but often remains underdiagnosed. Population studies of HNF1B variant carriers report fecal elastase 1 deficiency in ∼50%–62% of individuals with renal phenotypes, indicating subclinical or overt exocrine insufficiency [10]. Clinical assessment has identified overt exocrine failure, characterized by fecal elastase below 200 µg/g, in a substantial minority, with symptom relief following enzyme replacement therapy [10, 14]. Experimental models further document significant loss of acinar tissue and ductal disorganization that mimics this insufficiency pattern [14].

Consistent with the developmental role of HNF1B outlined above, experimental models support disruption of ductal organization and endocrine progenitor formation as key drivers of the combined endocrine–exocrine phenotype [4, 14]. Disruption of this program compromises ductal morphogenesis and acinar cell maintenance, leading to hypoplasia and increased susceptibility to pancreatitis, as shown by acinar-to-ductal metaplasia in mutant models [14]. These findings confirm the developmental link between pancreatic endocrine and exocrine pathways. Clinicians should maintain high vigilance for this combined endocrine–exocrine phenotype. Endocrine insufficiency is often misclassified as type 2 diabetes, whereas exocrine dysfunction may remain unrecognized unless specifically evaluated. The coexistence of pancreatic abnormalities with CAKUT or persistent hypomagnesemia should prompt consideration of HNF1B-related disease and CNV-aware genetic testing. RENAL AND ELECTROLYTE PHENOTYPES

Renal anomalies are the most consistent feature of HNF1B-related disease (Table 3), often preceding diabetes diagnosis and serving as the sentinel manifestation in children and adults [3, 13]. In pediatric cohorts, kidney function often declines slowly, but very early-onset cases can progress more rapidly and require closer follow-up. In addition to structural imaging, pediatric monitoring should include growth parameters, blood pressure, and serial eGFR with attention to tubular electrolyte losses that may affect symptoms, nutrition, and school performance. CAKUT includes renal cysts, renal hypoplasia, renal dysplasia, a dysplastic multicystic kidney, and renal agenesis. Because diabetes and pancreatic features may appear later, early CAKUT presentations should be viewed as potential entry points into the broader HNF1B-MODY/RCAD spectrum. Renal ultrasound commonly shows hyperechogenic kidneys with defects in corticomedullary differentiation and variable numbers of cysts; kidney size is age dependent, with hypoplasia more typical in adults and a broader size spectrum in children [8, 40]. This pattern contrasts with the symmetrical, progressive cystic enlargement typically seen in autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) [3]. Mouse models with renal epithelial loss of Hnf1b develop tubular differentiation defects and tubulointerstitial pathology, supporting a direct link between HNF1B dysfunction and the CAKUT/CKD spectrum [27, 28]. Recent Xenopus–based studies confirm that HNF1B regulates nephrogenic programs conserved across vertebrates [41].

Functionally, many affected individuals develop progressive CKD without significant proteinuria or hypertension [3]. Serial measurements of estimated glomerular filtration rate often show a gradual decline. Earlier onset and more severe impairment are seen in patients with whole-gene deletions compared to those with intragenic variants [13].

Biochemical hallmarks of HNF1B-related disease include electrolyte disturbances, particularly hypomagnesemia. Hypomagnesemia due to renal magnesium wasting is common in HNF1B-related disease (often 40%–50% in tested cohorts) and has been linked to reduced activation of distal tubular targets, including FXYD2 [11, 12]. A landmark study by Clissold et al. found that 44% of patients with HNF1B variants had hypomagnesemia compared to only 2% of controls, with hypermagnesuria and hypocalciuria, confirming tubular magnesium loss [13]. Hypomagnesemia often resists oral supplementation and may be overlooked unless serum magnesium is measured specifically [11]. Additional distal tubular abnormalities may occur, including hypokalemic, hypomagnesemic phenotypes that can resemble a Gitelman-like tubulopathy; this is generally interpreted as distal convoluted tubule dysfunction in HNF1B-related disease [11, 42].

The combination of multicystic kidneys and biochemical abnormalities, especially persistent hypomagnesemia without an apparent alternative cause, suggests HNF1B-related disease. Early recognition of this pattern is needed to prompt genetic testing and to support timely nephrology and endocrine management.

HEPATIC, GENITOURINARY, AND GASTROINTESTINAL INVOLVEMENT

Liver abnormalities in HNF1B-related disease typically manifest as mild, fluctuating elevations in serum transaminases (Table 4), which may suggest a cholestatic rather than hepatocellular pattern of injury [13, 20, 43, 44]. In some patients, imaging such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) can reveal bile duct irregularities or hypoplasia, a finding directly linked to HNF1B’s essential role in biliary development [13, 20, 45, 46]. Conditional loss-of-function studies in mice targeting Hnf1b within hepatobiliary progenitors have demonstrated significant intrahepatic bile duct paucity, ductal plate malformation, and jaundice. Hepatobiliary involvement is uncommon but documented, including neonatal cholestasis with bile duct paucity and later cholestatic phenotypes; mechanistic work supports a role for HNF1B in bile duct morphogenesis and cholangiocyte ciliary biology [37, 44, 47, 48].

Genitourinary anomalies are a frequent and diagnostically relevant feature, particularly in individuals who have both uterine and renal malformations. ∼18% of women with combined malformations carry pathogenic HNF1B variants [13, 40, 43, 49]. Abnormalities include a bicornuate or rudimentary uterus and vaginal agenesis. Isolated uterine malformations appear uncommon in the absence of associated renal anomalies [13, 40, 49]. Male reproductive tract abnormalities, including cryptorchidism and vas deferens/epididymal anomalies, have been reported in HNF1B-related disease and in 17q12 recurrent deletion syndrome; these are supported by clinical series and curated summaries rather than organ-specific mouse kidney models [3, 9, 40].

Gastrointestinal structural anomalies, although less common, include duodenal atresia and gallbladder hypoplasia. In 17q12 recurrent deletion syndrome (discussed in more detail in the next section), congenital anomalies beyond the kidney and pancreas, occasionally including gastrointestinal malformations requiring neonatal surgical management, have been reported in case series [9, 50]. These gastrointestinal manifestations reflect HNF1B’s involvement in foregut development. Hepatic, genitourinary, and gastrointestinal findings help support a syndromic diagnosis when combined with renal and metabolic features.

NEUROLOGICAL AND COGNITIVE SPECTRUM IN 17q12 DELETION

Neurological and cognitive involvement in HNF1B-related disease is predominantly associated with 17q12 deletions encompassing HNF1B and neighboring genes such as LHX1 [50, 51] (Table 5). These recurrent deletions, ∼1.4–1.8 Mb in size, are linked to neurodevelopmental abnormalities, including intellectual disability and autism spectrum disorder, and confer a significantly elevated risk of psychotic illness, with schizophrenia prevalence up to 1 in ∼1600 in general populations but markedly higher in deletion carriers [9, 50, 51]. Cognitive impairment is heterogeneous, spanning mild learning difficulties to moderate intellectual disability, with early speech delay, executive dysfunction, motor coordination issues, and autism traits frequently observed [50, 51].

Psychiatric disorders, including autism, mood disorders, and schizophrenia, are enriched in 17q12 deletion carriers. These carriers are associated with risk estimates 30-fold greater than baseline [51]. Although brain imaging studies remain limited, some cases show corpus callosum thinning or ventricular enlargement, though many individuals display normal neuroimaging [9]. Seizures are reported, but not common. Individuals with intragenic HNF1B variants rarely exhibit neurodevelopmental phenotypes, underscoring the syndromic impact of multigene deletions [50].

Cognitive and psychiatric features are frequently present before renal or metabolic symptoms, leading to neurodevelopmental referrals that may not trigger a search for systemic involvement. This risk of compartmentalized care can delay comprehensive diagnosis and organ screening [9]. Accurate identification relies on CMA or MLPA, as sequencing alone may fail to detect CNVs. Early neurocognitive assessment, educational intervention, and mental health support are critical for improving outcomes in this population.

GENOTYPE–PHENOTYPE CORRELATION

Although individual organ manifestations have been described above, genotype–phenotype correlations provide settings for predicting multisystem involvement and guiding clinical surveillance. In HNF1B-related disease, several recurring patterns can be identified, but their predictive value remains incomplete (Table 6). Intragenic HNF1B variants (missense, nonsense, splice-site, and frameshift) most commonly underlie the classical renal–pancreatic phenotype, including CAKUT, early-onset non-autoimmune diabetes, and electrolyte disturbances, such as hypomagnesemia [6, 52]. In contrast, heterozygous 17q12 microdeletions are associated with a broader, syndromic phenotype that include neurodevelopmental and psychiatric features in addition to renal and metabolic involvement, reflecting the contribution of neighboring genes [52, 53].

Variant location further modulates disease manifestation. Alterations affecting DNA-binding or dimerization domains (exons 2–4) have been associated with more severe renal impairment disease and earlier onset of diabetes, with truncating variants in these regions linked to lower eGFR at diagnosis [22]. However, consistent correlations between variant class and pancreatic endocrine or exocrine dysfunction remain limited across cohorts [2, 40, 54]. Despite these trends, genotype alone does not reliably predict clinical severity. Marked intra- and interfamilial variability suggests the influence of genetic modifiers, environmental factors, and epigenetic regulation. Mosaicism may further result in attenuated or organ-restricted phenotypes, complicating diagnosis and inheritance patterns [55]. Clinically, intragenic variants typically indicate a renal–pancreatic disease spectrum with limited neurodevelopmental involvement, whereas 17q12 deletions warrant broader multidisciplinary evaluation.

DIAGNOSTIC PITFALLS AND DELAY PATTERNS

Diagnostic delays in HNF1B-related disease arise from its pleiotropic manifestations and overlap with more common disorders, leading to frequent misdiagnosis (Table 7). Diabetes in HNF1B variant carriers often presents as non-autoimmune, non-obese, and slowly progressive, contributing to frequent misclassification as type 2 diabetes; others exhibiting severe insulin deficiency may be mislabeled as type 1 diabetes, despite lacking autoantibodies [1]. In a Lisbon cohort, the average interval between diabetes onset and correct HNF1B-MODY diagnosis was 16.5 years, highlighting significant delays even in specialist settings [1]. Similarly, there are case reports of delays of over 10 years between diabetes diagnosis and confirmation of the underlying HNF1B variant [56].

Renal presentations, particularly CAKUT, are often managed in isolation within nephrology without consideration of metabolic or syndromic features. In adult-onset presentations, HNF1B-related nephropathy is often not suspected [13], even though de novo variants account for 30%–50% of cases, leading to missed or delayed diagnoses [7, 25, 26].

The HNF1B score, developed using 17 clinical and imaging variables, has demonstrated utility in stratifying patients for genetic screening, achieving sensitivity above 98% with a cutoff of ≥8 [17]. However, it remains underutilized and may underperform in pediatric patients, whose biochemical and organ features may not yet be fully expressed [18]. Accordingly, pediatric practice should rely more heavily on renal-first triggers (CAKUT patterns, hyperechogenic kidneys, cysts/dysplasia), targeted biochemical screening (magnesium), and CNV-aware genetic testing, rather than waiting for diabetes onset. Standard diagnostic sequencing panels also often omit CNV detection, leading to 17q12 deletions being overlooked unless CMA or MLPA is specifically performed [13]. As a result of these factors, the average diagnostic delay spans nearly a decade to two decades, during which patients may undergo inappropriate treatments without adequate genetic or familial counseling [1, 56].

Improving early recognition depends on awareness of the disease’s constellation, young-onset, antibody-negative diabetes with renal anomalies, hypomagnesemia, and extrapancreatic features, and systematic application of diagnostic tools, such as the HNF1B score and integrated gene/CNV testing in relevant cases.

CLINICAL MANAGEMENT AND SURVEILLANCE RECOMMENDATIONS

Management of HNF1B-related disease requires a coordinated, multidisciplinary approach tailored to the patient’s specific variant type and organ involvement (Table 8). Regarding diabetes, patients with HNF1B-MODY often present with non-autoimmune, non-obese, insulin-requiring diabetes, frequently with stable glycemic control and a low risk of ketoacidosis. Progressive beta-cell failure means many individuals need insulin therapy relatively early, whereas sulfonylureas are typically ineffective [40]. Continuous glucose monitoring and individualized dosing are recommended to minimize hypoglycemia and maintain optimal glucose control [1]. Renal management focuses on serial monitoring of renal function, blood pressure, and proteinuria. Early nephrology referral is advised for individuals with declining eGFR or rising proteinuria. Although specific trials are lacking, the use of angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) for renoprotection is commonly extrapolated from CKD guidelines [57]. Electrolyte monitoring, particularly serum magnesium and potassium, is critical because of the high prevalence of hypomagnesemia and other tubular transport defects; symptomatic patients may require oral or occasional IV supplementation. Liver involvement is generally mild, and elevated transaminases warrant imaging and exclusion of other causes but rarely require targeted therapy. Reproductive care is essential. Women should be evaluated for uterine anomalies, particularly in the setting of fertility issues, and men may require urologic assessment for cryptorchidism or infertility. Neurodevelopmental support for those with 17q12 deletions should begin in early childhood, with follow-up into adolescence to monitor cognitive, behavioral, and psychiatric health. Post-diagnosis management is stratified according to variant type: patients with intragenic variants typically require endocrine and nephrology follow-up, regular monitoring of HbA1c, C-peptide, eGFR, structural imaging, and electrolyte panels [1]. Those with whole-gene deletions require, in addition, neurodevelopmental and psychiatric assessment, reproductive tract imaging, and genetic counseling [51].

Follow-up intervals vary: annual reviews suffice for stable renal and endocrine function, whereas multi-domain abnormalities may necessitate 6-month evaluations [1]. Surveillance should integrate multidisciplinary input from endocrinology, nephrology, genetics, neurology, psychiatry, and reproductive specialists to ensure holistic care [13].

Electronic health records can serve as platforms for integrating diagnostic red flags and prompting timely genetic referrals [17]. Such real-time clinical decision support has demonstrated efficacy in other rare disease contexts and is readily applicable to HNF1B-related syndromes. By unifying detection, diagnosis, and surveillance in a concise care pathway, this algorithm aims to close existing diagnostic gaps and optimize long-term outcomes for affected patients.

RESEARCH GAPS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

A significant advance in modeling HNF1B-related kidney disease has come from CRISPR-edited human pluripotent stem cell-derived kidney organoids. These systems reproduce key developmental abnormalities associated with HNF1B haploinsufficiency and provide insight into mechanisms underlying CAKUT phenotypes [58]. Experimental animal models further support the role of HNF1B as a central regulator of renal developmental programs [41]. Experimental studies have identified regulatory elements upstream of HNF1B that may influence renal developmental programs [46]. These findings may help explain some of the phenotypic variability observed among patients with HNF1B-related disease.

In addition to kidney findings, in vitro HNF1B knockout in human iPSCs has demonstrated impaired formation of pancreatic and foregut progenitors, supporting early developmental roles and establishing a relevant platform for therapeutic testing [59]. Experimental lineage-tracing studies further support a developmental link between ductal abnormalities and diabetes susceptibility [60]. Despite these technological advances, key gaps remain. Genetic modifiers that influence the expressivity and penetrance of HNF1B variants have not been systematically identified, and the molecular basis of phenotypic variability remains largely unresolved. Additionally, although current organoid models are valuable for studying early morphogenetic events, they seldom recapitulate late-stage or multiorgan dysfunctions, such as the concurrent kidney–pancreas–liver syndromes observed in patients. The issues with recapitulating late-stage disease and multiorgan dysfunction limit the direct clinical translatability of current organoid models. It calls for model refinements that incorporate aspects such as vascularization, tissue-tissue interactions, and immune crosstalk.

Antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) strategies represent a future direction for selected splice-altering HNF1B variants, as defined aberrant transcripts can be targeted. Preclinical work already demonstrated successful ASO screening in pancreatic cancer organoids [61]. Specific HNF1B variants may affect splice-site recognition or lead to cryptic splicing events. However, to date, no published ASO trials for HNF1B-MODY have been reported. In parallel, organoid-based drug screening emerges as a promising translational medicine strategy for identifying phenotype-rescuing compounds. Patient-derived kidney and pancreatic organoid systems may eventually support variant-specific therapeutic testing and precision medicine approaches in HNF1B-related disease. Looking ahead, gene-editing approaches such as base editing or prime editing hold long-term potential. These tools may correct pathogenic HNF1B variants directly in renal progenitors. However, issues surrounding delivery, specificity, and ethical regulation currently confine these to proof-of-concept stages. To advance toward clinical application, establishing a prospective, international HNF1B registry will be essential. Such a platform would facilitate longitudinal tracking of renal and metabolic phenotypes, allow genotype-phenotype stratification, and support integration of clinical data with patient-derived cell models. This infrastructure could also underpin early-phase interventional studies, including n-of-1 or basket trials focused on genotype-specific therapies, such as magnesium transport modulators or pathway-specific inhibitors.

The translation of mechanistic insights into therapy for HNF1B-related disease may depend on a precision medicine approach that integrates organoid-based modeling, therapeutic screening, and clinical-genomic platforms (Table 9). Organoid platforms and improved genetic testing may support future variant-specific interventions, though practical barriers remain.
