Neo-Aortic Root Dilation in Pregnancy After Arterial Switch for Complete Transposition of the Great Arteries
Authors: Ariane Hébert Métellus, François-Pierre Mongeon, Doan Hoa Do, Évelyne Raîche, Mandy Malick, Nadine Sauvé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe arterial switch operation (ASO) is standard treatment for D-transposition of the great arteries, and neo-aortic dilation is common afterward. Its behavior during pregnancy remains uncertain, especially the risk of dissection.CaseWe describe a 30-year-old woman (G6P0A5) with neo-aortic dilation (44 mm) during her first pregnancy, which remained stable on beta blockers until an uncomplicated caesarean delivery. In her subsequent pregnancy, an initial measurement of 47 mm raised concern. Expert review highlighted the challenges of accurately measuring asymmetric neo-aortas post-ASO and confirmed that the neo-aorta was stable (43 mm). Background

Dextro-transposition of the great arteries (D-TGA) is the second most prevalent cyanotic congenital heart defect.1,2 The Arterial Switch Operation (ASO), the preferred repair for 40 years, 3 has markedly improved survival (90% at 20 years), and many operated women have reached reproductive age.1,4,5

Long-term ASO sequelae include neo-aortic root dilatation (in 2/3 of women), aortic regurgitation, supravalvular pulmonary stenosis, and coronary stenosis.5–8

Late reintervention for neo-aortic regurgitation or root dilatation occurs in 4–25%6,8,9 with surgical thresholds generally >55 mmm or 45–50 mm depending on regurgitation severity.5,6

Although no aortic dissections have been reported post-ASO, some women show thin, fragile neo-aortic walls.6,7 Whether the 40% rise in cardiac output during pregnancy increases dilation or dissection risk is unknown. 10

The following case describes two pregnancies in a woman with significant neo-aortic dilation after ASO.

Case

A 30-year-old asymptomatic woman, was referred at 7 weeks of gestation to a high-risk obstetric clinic. Born with D-TGA, she underwent a balloon atrial septostomy one day after birth, followed by an ASO with the Lecompte maneuver and closure of the atrial septal defect and ductus arteriosus at 2 weeks.

Her pre-pregnancy transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) showed mild to moderate aortic regurgitation, mild pulmonary stenosis, and normal left ventricular systolic function. Her neo-aortic root and ascending aorta were never well visualized on previous TTEs. A stress echocardiogram showed no inducible ischemia with good functional capacity.

At 11 weeks of gestation, TTE revealed mild neo-aortic root dilation (42 mm). Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging at 16 and 30 weeks confirmed stable asymmetric enlargement (42 × 33 mm coronal x sagittal; 36 × 42 × 44 mm short-axis plane). She received metoprolol 12.5 mg twice daily from the early second trimester. She had a caesarean delivery at 37 + 4 weeks due to breech presentation and intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), although vaginal delivery with an assisted second stage under epidural anesthesia had been judged safe. No peripartum complication occurred.

Two years later, she presented at 7 weeks of her second ongoing pregnancy, remaining asymptomatic with a normal exercise tolerance (12.1 METs). Metoprolol was restarted.

CMR at 13 weeks showed progression of the neo-aortic root dilation (44 × 37 mm coronal × sagittal), confirmed by computed tomography angiogram (CTA) at 46 × 34 mm (coronal × sagittal) or 39 × 40 × 47 mm in the short-axis plane. A repeat CMR at 19 weeks showed further enlargement (49 × 39 mm coronal × sagittal). These findings raised concerns about measurement accuracy, ongoing dilation, risk of aortic dissection, and safety of continuing pregnancy.

We sought opinions from experts in aortic imaging, cardio-obstetrics, and adult congenital heart disease. After image review and repeat measurements, the neo-aorta was moderately dilated but relatively stable (Figure 1). Pregnancy proceeded on a beta-blocker (metoprolol 25 mg twice daily, maximal tolerated dose) with CMR monitoring using a standardized protocol. A CMR at 34 weeks showed a neo-aortic root of 41 × 41 × 43 mm (short-axis, sinus to commissure) or 41 × 46 mm (long-axis, reproducing the echocardiographic measurement), consistent with prior measurements. Overall, dilation was neither progressive nor severe enough to warrant termination of pregnancy.

The woman delivered a healthy baby by uncomplicated planned caesarean at 36 weeks under spinal anesthesia due to lower uterine segment thinning, threatened preterm labor, and potential aortic risk. The mother and baby were discharged the following day with follow-up CMR scheduled at 1-year postpartum.

Discussion

The main challenge with this woman was the limited data on dissection risk in women with ASO who have a neo-aorta > 45 mm or showing rapid progression during or between pregnancies. The case underscores the need for standardized aortic measurements, especially with asymmetric dilation.

Ideally, baseline cardiac imaging should be obtained preconception and performed by physicians experienced in complex congenital heart disease, surgical interventions, and imaging techniques. 11 Key parameters include: neo-aortic root size, degree of regurgitation, supravalvular pulmonary stenosis, and presence of myocardial ischemia from the reimplanted coronary arteries.12–14 TTE is most accessible but often requires CMR or CTA for complete assessment.

Pregnant people with a dilated aorta need trimester-based serial imaging, preferably TTE or non-contrast CMR11–13,15 with guidelines-based short-axis sinus to sinus or sinus to commissure, and long-axis reproducing TTE measurements. Coronal and sagittal planes, more subject to obliquity, should be avoided. CTA offers thinner slices (typically 0.5–0.6 mm) than CMR (6–10 mm)11,14 but cannot be repeated in pregnancy due to radiation exposure. 16

Although evidence is limited, no neo-aortic dissections have been reported during or outside pregnancy, and only two pregnancies showed > 2 mm progression (Table 1).12,17 Data from other aortopathies associated with connective tissue diseases, like Marfan, Loeys–Dietz, or vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, do not necessarily apply to TGA since the neo-aortic tissue is normal. Extrapolating data from these aortopathies may lead to an overestimation of the risk and to unnecessary interventions, including caesarean delivery, pregnancy termination, or iatrogenic preterm delivery.

There is no evidence supporting impulse reduction therapy such as a beta-blocker to limit neo-aortic root dilation in women with D-TGA after ASO, although their use is common in pregnant women with a dilated aorta. Significant doses are believed to be necessary to have a protective effect, and it is unlikely that the small dose used in this case had a significant impact. Fetal growth should be monitored, and risks and benefits should be reassessed if IUGR develops.

Although rates of caesarean birth are higher than in the general population, both vaginal delivery and CD remain acceptable options for post-ASO.12,20,21 In 2 published series, all caesarean deliveries except two cases were due to obstetrical rather than cardiac indications, and vaginal delivery was generally preferred.17,19

The Valsalva maneuver is often a concern in women with a dilated aorta; however, it can be minimized with a passive second stage, epidural anesthesia, and assisted delivery. This approach has proven safe in Marfan women with aortic roots up to 45 mm. 19 It is our practice to suggest mitigated pushing with an open glottis in women with surgically repaired aortas, including non-dilated neo-aortas post-ASO. Avoiding the Valsalva maneuver is reasonable in neo-aortic dilation, and caesarean delivery offers optimal hemodynamic control and is usually favored when the aorta exceeds 45 mm. 19

Early postpartum surveillance requires awareness that hemodynamic deterioration may occur from the rapid intravascular reabsorption of extravascular fluid and that increased blood pressure may precipitate dissection or worsen aortic regurgitation12,13,18,21

Conclusion

Preconception cardiac imaging and standardized, reproducible measurements are essential for accurate risk assessment and shared decision-making in pregnancy management.
