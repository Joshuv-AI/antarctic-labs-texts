Burden of dog bites and associated healthcare seeking behavior for rabies post-exposure prophylaxis in North and South Shahdara municipal zones of Delhi, India
Authors: Nischal Yathagiri, Amir Maroof Khan, Rahul Sharma, Anurag Agarwal, Risa Vernette Nengminza Sangma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRabies is a zoonotic disease of global public health importance which accounts for 59,000 human deaths annually. In India, about 60 million stray or free ranging dogs are found in the country posing a significant risk of increase in incidences of dog bite and transmission of rabies. The aim of this study was to assess the burden of dog bites and associated healthcare seeking behavior for rabies post-exposure prophylaxis in North and South Shahdara municipal zones of Delhi, India.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted between the period of May 2023 to November 2024 in North and South Shahdara municipal zones of Delhi. Data was collected using a semi-structured, pretested and prevalidated interviewer administered schedule from family members of selected five types of residential settlements.ResultsTotal of 800 families were interviewed. The overall annual incidence of dog bites was 25.5 per 1000 population and it ranged from 15.9 in Planned Colony to 32.1 in Jhuggi Jhopri Cluster. Around three fourth (75.5%) of the total dog bite cases reported in the last one year were by stray dogs. Category III dog bites were more common across all settlements, comprising 67/102 (65.7%) of the total cases. Category II dog bites had a higher odds of receiving rabies Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP) as per national guidelines compared to those with Category III dog bites (OR: 5.54, 95% CI: 2.25, 13.66).ConclusionsDog bite incidence in Delhi was high and varied across type of settlements. More emphasis should be placed on mass dog vaccination and animal birth control measures in areas with high incidence of dog bites. Background

Rabies is a zoonotic disease of significant global public health importance, responsible for an estimated 59,000 human deaths annually worldwide [1]. Dog bites are associated with about 96% of the rabies related mortality and morbidity. Out of the 59,000 annual human deaths estimated to occur globally due to rabies, 35% occur in India. To address the issue of rabies in the country, National Rabies Control Programme (NRCP) was approved during 12th Five Year Plan [2]. The National Action Plan for the Elimination of dog-mediated Rabies (NAPRE) launched by Government of India in 2021 provides a broad framework for combating rabies. This focus on dog mediated rabies is particularly important given the presence of an estimated 60 million stray or free-ranging dogs in the country, which contribute significantly to rabies exposure [3, 4].

Understanding the true burden of dog bites at the community level is paramount for identifying high-risk areas and evaluating the impact of control measures. While national estimates of a WHO-sponsored survey eighteen years ago reported an annual animal bite incidence of 17 per 1000 population [5], and a recent (2022-23) community based survey in 15 states reported incidence of 6.6 animal bites and 5.6 dog bites per 1000 population annually [6], there remains a notable lack of granular, community based data.

We found few community based studies on the burden of dog bite estimation from Delhi. One study in 2005 reported the annual incidence of dog bite cases as 4/1000 population, whereas another study in 2016 reported the annual incidence as 25.2/1000 population [7, 8].

We observed that studies from Delhi were around a decade old and many other available studies related to dog bites were facility based, capturing only those who seek care, and were often limited to bite exposure management rather than comprehensive dog bite burden estimation. Community based studies on burden of dog bites are essential to understand current trends, identify vulnerable populations and locations, and provide evidence to the program to develop targeted prevention and control strategies. We aimed to find the burden of dog bites and associated healthcare seeking behavior for rabies Post-Exposure Prophylaxis in North and South Shahdara municipal zones of Delhi.

Methods

A cross-sectional study was conducted from May 2023 to November 2024 to find out the incidence of dog bites in North and South Shahdara municipal zones of Delhi. Previous studies have shown that dog bite incidence varies according to the type of settlements [9, 10]. Based on the report by the Centre for Policy Research, we divided the study area into five categories of settlements with distinctive features in terms of level of civic amenities, access to basic services and the status of houses and land [11]. The five types of residential settlements were Jhuggi Jhopri Clusters (JJCs), Urbanized Villages, Resettlement Colonies, Unauthorised Colonies and Planned Colonies. The classification provided by Centre for Policy Research India categorises residential settlements in Delhi into eight types [11]. However, for the purpose of our study, we merged few categories i.e. Slum Designated Areas into Jhuggi Jhopri Clusters and Regularised-Unauthorised Colonies type into Unauthorised colonies depending upon the similarities in the type of residential settlements and feasibility.

The five types of residential settlements were defined as follows:

Jhuggi Jhopri Clusters (JJCs): Comprised of squatter settlements located on public land that has been occupied and built on without permission, often referred to as encroachments.

Jhuggi Jhopri Clusters (JJCs): Comprised of squatter settlements located on public land that has been occupied and built on without permission, often referred to as encroachments.

Resettlement Colonies (JJ Resettlement Colonies): Comprised of settlements where eligible residents from JJCs are allotted plots after eviction, designed with an intention to provide basic services and planning in contrast to the JJCs they replace.

Resettlement Colonies (JJ Resettlement Colonies): Comprised of settlements where eligible residents from JJCs are allotted plots after eviction, designed with an intention to provide basic services and planning in contrast to the JJCs they replace.

Urbanized Villages (Urban Villages): Comprised of rural villages that have been notified to shift into the urban ambit by an act, making Master Plan and building bye-laws applicable.

Urbanized Villages (Urban Villages): Comprised of rural villages that have been notified to shift into the urban ambit by an act, making Master Plan and building bye-laws applicable.

Unauthorised Colonies: Comprised of settlements built in contravention of zoning regulations or on illegally subdivided agricultural land, where plot buyers possess semi-legal tenure documents.

Unauthorised Colonies: Comprised of settlements built in contravention of zoning regulations or on illegally subdivided agricultural land, where plot buyers possess semi-legal tenure documents.

Planned Colonies: Comprised of approved settlements on land demarcated as development area where housing units comply with planning norms and are fully serviced with infrastructure.

Planned Colonies: Comprised of approved settlements on land demarcated as development area where housing units comply with planning norms and are fully serviced with infrastructure.

Considering an estimated annual dog bite incidence of 3% from previous study [8], for 95% confidence level and design effect of 2, the sample size came out to be 4000. Considering an average family size of five, we surveyed 800 families to cover the required sample.

From each of the five types of residential settlements, one area was purposively selected based on feasibility. Based on the population proportion to size for residential settlements available for Delhi [11] the number of families surveyed was divided among the five types of residential settlements i.e. Seemapuri- 288 families (36%) from JJC, Ghazipur- 56 families (7%) from urbanized village, Nand Nagri- 104 families (13%) from resettlement colony, Laxmi Nagar- 152 families (19%) from unauthorised colony, Dilshad Garden- 200 families (25%) from planned colony [supplementary file].

From each selected area, one block was randomly selected and the families were selected by systematic random sampling. Those families who have been residing in the household in the selected areas for at least one year were considered as residents of the area for the purpose of the study.

Pet dogs were defined as those dogs which are owned, socialized, and usually fed properly. They are often kept indoors or in specific outdoor areas. Stray dogs were defined as dogs which are not owned and are found in public areas, such as streets, parks etc. They get their food from either humans or solid food waste dumping. Community dogs as dogs which roam freely in public areas but are informally accepted, fed, or protected by groups, neighbourhoods, or establishments without formal ownership.

Annual incidence is defined as the rate of new dog bite events over a period of last one year for the population at risk. Lifetime incidence is defined as the rate of dog bite events ever in a lifetime for the population at risk. Denominator for lifetime incidence is person years of exposure of population under study i.e. sum of the ages of all individuals under study population considering a person is at risk of dog bites throughout his lifetime.

A semi-structured, pretested and prevalidated interviewer administered schedule consisting of sociodemographic characteristics, history of dog bite of all the family members, history of dog bite wound management and healthcare seeking behavior for rabies PEP was developed for this study and used for data collection [supplementary file].

Approval of the Institutional Ethics Committee- Human Research (IEC-HR), University College of Medical Sciences, University of Delhi was obtained vide letter no. IECHR-2023-59−31 dated 30th April 2023.

The data was entered in Microsoft Excel Spreadsheet and cleaned. Data analysis was done in SPSS 20.0 version software. We report proportions for categorical variables such as status of pet dog ownership, category of dog bites, type of biting dog, type of health facility visited, and status of rabies PEP received and mean and standard deviation for numerical variables such as age, income, number and type of health facilities contacted etc. Chi square test was used to test significance of association between sex of dog bite victims, type of biting dog and category of dog bite with rabies PEP as per national guidelines. Odds ratios with 95% CI were computed to find out the strength of the association.

Discussion

We surveyed 800 families covering 4005 individuals across five selected types of urban residential settlements in North and South Shahdara municipal zones of Delhi. We found a high annual incidence of dog bites of 25.5 per 1000 when compared with rates reported in a recent national survey from 2023 [6]. The presence of unvaccinated free roaming dogs or street dogs, amidst human settlements is a major contributor to the high incidence in India [4]. This is especially concerning in certain settlements, such as Jhuggi Jhopri (JJ) Clusters, urbanized villages and resettlement colonies where the incidence was much higher due to low socioeconomic status, high population densities and are ignored in many aspects such as drainage, solid waste management and animal birth control activities.

Our findings are similar to other studies done in near similar settings. A 2015 study in rural low-income areas of Bengaluru reported an incidence of 25 per 1,000 [12], comparable to our overall rate. Another study from Goa in 2018, focusing on children, found an incidence of 33.6 per 1,000 [13], which is even higher. Field workers in Goa in 2020 also reported a rate of 33 per 1,000 [14] due to more outdoor exposure. These studies support our observation that people in certain work or living conditions are at higher risk of dog bites.

Some studies from rural areas reported substantially higher dog bite incidence. For example, two studies- one in Tamil Nadu (2013–14) and another in rural Punjab (2020) found very high incidence rates of 68 per 1,000 [10, 15]. These rural areas may have more frequent contact with animals.

A WHO-sponsored national survey in 2006 found an incidence of 17 per 1,000 [5], while a recent national survey in 2023 reported a sharp decline to 5.6 per 1,000 [6]. The much higher dog bite incidence in our study as compared to this may be due to our study being done in localised areas with high population densities. Another possible reason may be that the study published in 2023 had its data collection done around Covid-19 pandemic and so, might have shown a lower dog bite incidence.

A review study in 2021 showed that incidence can range widely, from 2.6 to 25 per 1,000, depending on the location and method of data collection [16]. Data shows that Delhi’s dog bite incidence has increased from 4 per 1,000 in 2005 [7] to 25.2 per 1,000 in 2016 [8], our findings are also similar to the Delhi study in 2016 [8].

Our findings also indicate that around three-fourth of dog bites are inflicted by stray dogs, often occurring unprovoked. Most dog bites occur when people are engaged in daily activities, between 3 pm and 9 pm or 9 am and 3 pm, which suggests greater exposure to stray or unrestrained animals during these day time hours. Most studies show that males and those in younger age group are more vulnerable to dog bites [6, 8, 17]. Our study also showed similar findings.

We found that over one-third of victims resorted to applying indigenous treatments such as mustard oil, red chili powder or haldi to their wounds as first aid which are not recommended for preventing rabies. The use of indigenous first aid treatments observed in our study aligns with studies across India which reported low rates of proper wound washing and use of chilli, turmeric or other remedies [5, 6, 8, 10].

Data from across India indicates wide variability in rabies PEP rates ranging from 32.5% to 96.1% [7, 18], underscoring inconsistencies in access and uptake. Studies done in Delhi in 2005 found that rabies PEP rate was 32.5% and in 2016 it rose to 79.2% [7, 8]. Despite some improvements, a recent nationwide survey (2022–2023) revealed 20.5% of dog bite cases received no ARV, and nearly half (49.1%) who started did not complete [6]. Several factors impede complete rabies PEP adherence, including limited access to anti-rabies clinics, insufficient public awareness, issues with vaccine supply, forgotten follow-up dates, waiting time and long distances to health facilities.

Even though ARV coverage was around three-fourth but RIG coverage among Category III bites was low despite larger proportion of dog bite cases belonging to Category III. The low RIG administration rate has also been reported in other surveys; a WHO-sponsored survey in 2006 reported RIG use as low as 2.1% [5], and a 2017 study found only 4% of Category III victims received immunoglobulins [19]. Even in 2019, multi-centric data showed that only 46.2% of Category III exposures received RIG [20]. A recent nationwide survey (2022–2023) reported that only 11·2% of eligible bite victims received rabies immunoglobulin, mostly from tertiary care facilities [6]. This is critically important because Category III bites unequivocally demand RIG in addition to ARV for effective rabies PEP. The reasons for this persistent deficit are likely multifactorial, mainly incorrect bite categorisation, general lack of awareness and unavailability of RIG. This trend is concerning as RIG is essential in providing immediate passive immunity, for individuals who experience high-risk Category III bites. There is an urgent need to bridge these gaps, to ensure that dog bite victims with Category III bites, receive the full spectrum of rabies PEP.

The study had certain limitations, study was conducted in only two municipal zones of Delhi, thus cannot be generalised to whole of Delhi. The unequal distribution of sample size across the settlements due to use of population proportion to size sampling, comparison between the various settlements should be done with caution. There are chances of recall bias despite our efforts to minimize it by using triangulation methods, by checking records and by using community based festivals and sociocultural events as reference points for dates.

Conclusions

Dog bite incidence in North and South Shahdara municipal zones of Delhi was high and it varied across settlements with lower socioeconomic status and higher population densities being with higher incidence, while settlements with higher socioeconomic status and lower population densities with lower incidence. Despite good ARV uptake, the shortfall in RIG administration for high risk Category III bites highlights the need for periodic training of health workers.

We recommend that more emphasis should be placed in mass dog vaccination, animal birth control measures and solid waste management in areas with high incidence of dog bites. More community based studies on a regular basis are needed to find out the burden and trend of dog bites across the country.
