LigGen—a GEN-AI based ligand generation approach for de-novo drug design
Authors: Anshul Yadav, Natarajan Arul Murugan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNavigating the vast chemical space to identify potent therapeutic agents with optimal pharmacokinetic properties remains a formidable bottleneck in pharmaceutical development. While virtual screening is limited to the exploration of existing chemical libraries, de novo design offers a complementary approach by constructing novel chemical entities “from scratch,” potentially accessing unexplored regions of the chemical universe. De novo design may lead to novel scaffolds or compounds when compared to searching the compounds from chemical libraries. This study introduces LigGen, a novel tool for de novo drug design (DNDD) that combines fragment-based drug design (FBDD) with recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and Monte Carlo Simulated Annealing (MCSA) to generate ligands for target proteins. LigGen is effective in generating novel and synthesizeable high-affinity ligands for various biological drug-targets. The efficacy of the tool is demonstrated by testing it on CrossDocked dataset, as well as by generating ligands for proteins Beta-secretase 1 (BACE1), Monoamine Oxidase B (MAO-B), and Acetylcholinesterase (AChE). LigGen presents a promising approach for advancing drug discovery, offering a robust platform for novel drug design.Keywords: Fragment-based drug design, Recurrent neural networks, Monte Carlo simulated annealingSubject terms: Chemistry, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Drug discovery

Introduction

The pharmaceutical industry faces significant challenges in the discovery and development of novel therapeutic agents. One of the major obstacles in the drug design and development process was the time and financial investment required for the creation of novel therapeutic agents1. It is estimated that, of 10000 drug candidates, only 5 will advance from preclinical testing to human testing, and of those, only 1 will reach the market2. It is well-known that in the hunt for new novel drug candidates, a very small portion of the chemical space has been explored. Because the chemical space is so enormous, with certain chemical libraries3 having compounds in the range , it is not feasible to screen all the compounds in these libraries. Instead, designing novel drug candidates using atom-by-atom construction or from fragments can be a pragmatic approach. Consequently, proposing molecules with novel scaffolds and synthesizing them for inclusion into drug development is a challenging problem for medicinal and organic chemists2. Researchers all across the world turned to computational methods like virtual screening (VS), molecular docking (MD), de-novo drug design (DNDD), and artificial intelligence (AI) to reduce these difficulties and barriers.

One may broadly categorise the current approaches used in structure-based drug design into two groups. Virtual screening (VS), also known as “finding” ligands for a certain receptor, is the focus of the first category. In VS, a vast number of molecules are screened to identify those that bind effectively to the receptor’s binding pocket. Virtual screening’s primary benefit is that it reduces the amount of synthetic work required to find novel lead compounds4. Virtual screening offers many benefits, but it still takes a lot of time since it needs to filter through a lot of compounds to find the right compound for a particular receptor. De Novo Drug Design is another category of structure-based drug design as it focuses on “building” the ligands. The technique of developing novel drug compounds with computational growth methods that meet a predetermined set of requirements is known as de novo drug design (DNDD)5. “De novo” means “from the beginning,” highlighting the fact that this process may create new molecular entities without requiring a starting template2.

A dominant strategy within DNDD is the gradual assembly of tiny components into larger ligand molecules through the use of fragment-based drug design (FBDD). These components can be fragments or atoms. Genetic algorithms are often utilized to build ligands. The main benefit of this approach is that new structures that aren’t in any database may be proposed4. For the purpose of creating ligands for various proteins, fragment-based drug design has been widely utilised by both industry and academia in recent years6. Fragments are essentially low-weight, tiny organic molecules that satisfy the “rule of three”: molecular weight (MW) < 300, ClogP 3, and a number of hydrogen bond acceptors 37. The rationale behind FBDD lies in its ability to probe a vast chemical space using a relatively small library; since the number of possible small fragments is manageable compared to the astronomical number of drug-sized molecules, combining these fragments allows for a more efficient exploration of chemical diversity. Furthermore, FBDD emphasizes Ligand Efficiency (LE), a metric that normalizes binding affinity by the number of heavy atoms. High LE indicates that each atom in the fragment contributes significantly to binding, ensuring that the final molecule maintains high quality interactions as it grows in size. Consequently, FBDD offers benefits such as higher hit rates, enhanced physicochemical properties, and a higher potential for chemical uniqueness8.

A number of tools exist for de novo design. Among them, the program LUDI9 operates by using collections of fragments that can be freely positioned within designated subpockets termed “interaction sites.” These fragments are subsequently linked together through spacers or linkers to form viable, larger molecular structures. ilib diverse10 is a tool used for cataloging ‘drug-like’ compound libraries starting from a well-balanced molecular fragment set with high druglikeness11. CombiSmog12 utilizes a knowledge-based potential that is derived from a collection of 1,000 protein-ligand complexes. Additionally, it employs a Monte Carlo ligand growth algorithm in order to build new and unique ligands. LigBuilder V313 uses genetic algorithm (GA) for the creation of ligands and offers functionalities like Multi-Target Drug Design and Fragment-Based Drug Design. The successful use of de novo design methodologies has been documented in numerous published examples, which include the discovery of ligands of FK506 binding protein-1214, DNA gyrase15, thrombin employing LUDI16, and dihydroorotate dehydrogenase using Sprout17.

Another popular technique for de-novo design includes ligand generation using deep neural networks such as RNNs. Natural language processing and translation are among the machine learning tasks that Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) are very good at solving. While early Neural network-based RNNs have been utilised to predict properties like aqueous solubility18, recent focus has shifted to generation. Preferred areas of chemical space were sampled using RNNs acting as autoencoders to create a latent representation of molecular structure19. Crucially, a number of research teams have shown that RNNs can be used to create canonical SMILES strings and that transfer learning can be used to improve them20. AstraZeneca researchers have expanded SMILES-generating RNNs to reinforcement learning by using this idea. Using this method, they were able to produce sets of structures with high target activity and other desired characteristics21. Pocket2Mol22 is a popular deep learning-based tool for ligand generation. It is a generative model that designs molecules by explicitly considering the three-dimensional shape and chemical properties of a protein’s binding pocket.

By combining RNNs with traditional computer techniques, researchers can increase the accuracy and efficiency of the drug discovery process. By combining the most beneficial features of both strategies, this combination provides a more robust platform for the development of novel drugs. While emerging deep learning approaches such as diffusion and flow-matching models offer geometric generation capabilities, they often operate as “black boxes” regarding the chemical logic of assembly. In contrast, the tool proposed in this study, LigGen, utilizes a transparent, iterative growth process that mimics the physical addition of substructures, offering greater interpretability in the design pathway. This paper introduces LigGen, a novel program for structure-based de novo drug design that addresses the critical need for generating synthesizable lead compounds with high binding affinity. LigGen leverages the strengths of both recurrent neural networks (RNNs) for intelligent fragment selection and fragment-based drug design (FBDD) principles to iteratively construct ligands within the target protein’s binding pocket. Crucially, LigGen employs a Monte Carlo Simulated Annealing algorithm for ligand growth, allowing for efficient exploration of conformational space and optimization of binding interactions. LigGen integrates a synthesizability score into its optimization process, ensuring that the generated ligands are not only predicted to bind effectively but are also chemically tractable/synthesizeable. This multi-objective optimization, combining binding affinity (assessed using Autodock-Vina) and synthesizability, represents a significant improvement over existing methods. On the fly generation of the fragment to grow ligands through RNN-GRU architecture is another significant difference when compared to existing de novo design tools.

Methods

Monte Carlo simulated annealing

Monte Carlo simulated annealing (MCSA) is a stochastic optimization technique inspired by the annealing process in metallurgy and material science. MCSA is a standard, well-established technique and is widely used to identify the global minimum23. Similar to how slowly cooling a metal or material allows its atoms to arrange themselves in a low/least-energy, stable configuration, MCSA probabilistically explores solution space to find the optimal solution for a given problem24. This approach is particularly valuable for tackling complex problems with many local minima, where traditional optimization methods might fail to find optimal solution corresponding to global minimum.

The MCSA algorithm initiates with an empty ligand state (a ligand with no atoms). In each subsequent iteration, a new neighbouring ligand state is generated by proposing a modification to the current ligand. This modification involves adding a chemically valid fragment to the existing ligand structure. The “energy” of a given ligand state is defined as a weighted sum of two critical scores: its binding affinity, as estimated by the Autodock Vina, and its synthesizability score, derived from RDKit’s SAscore.

This objective function is designed to operate as a dynamic trade-off rather than a rigidly normalized equation. By allowing the raw Vina score (typically negative kcal/mol) and the SA score (positive penalty) to interact directly, the algorithm naturally favors larger, high-affinity binders only when the gain in binding energy sufficiently outweighs the penalty incurred by increased synthetic complexity.

Note that unlike the standard Boltzmann distribution in statistical mechanics, physical constants such as the Boltzmann constant () or the ideal gas constant (R) are omitted from this equation, as they are mathematically absorbed into the definition of the algorithmic temperature itself.

The “temperature” is a control parameter that starts at a high value, promoting broad exploration of the chemical space and acceptance of even unfavorable moves. As the simulation progresses, the temperature is gradually lowered by a factor of alpha after each accepted modification. This annealing schedule progressively reduces the probability of accepting energetically unfavorable states, allowing the system to converge towards optimal or near-optimal ligand structures. This controlled decrease in temperature ensures that the algorithm shifts from an exploratory phase to a refinement phase, guiding the ligand generation towards high-quality solutions.

Recurrent neural network

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) are a special type of neural network that’s good at handling data that comes in a specific order, like words, sentences, paragraphs or music. They do this by having loops in their connections, allowing them to remember what they have seen before. At each step, it combines the new information it receives with what it learned from the previous step using the same underlying parameters (Fig. 1).

Training a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) for sequence modelling generally involves using maximum likelihood estimation to predict the next token in the target sequence based on the tokens from previous steps. At each step, the model generates a probability distribution for the next likely character, with the goal of maximizing the probability assigned to the correct token21:

The recurrent neural network (RNN) model used in LigGen is based on a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) architecture, implemented using PyTorch. The model operates over SELFIES (Self-Referencing Embedded Strings)25 tokens, which are robust, simple, string-based molecular representation. Unlike SMILES-based models which require complex grammar masking to ensure validity, the SELFIES representation guarantees that every generated token sequence corresponds to a valid molecular graph. This is critical in generative models to avoid chemically invalid outputs, which are more frequent when using SMILES25. During inference, the model first generates SELFIES, which are then automatically converted to SMILES before being passed to LigGen for ligand generation.The architecture comprises of Embedding layer (256 dimensions) converting token indices to dense vectors, Stacked GRU layers (3 layers, 512 hidden units), and finally a linear output layer mapping to token probabilities. Our generative model employs a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) architecture. We selected GRU over Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) units because they offer a favourable balance between performance and computational efficiency, often achieving comparable results in sequence modelling tasks while using fewer parameters26. Given that fragments are used as modular building blocks rather than full-molecule templates, the GRU architecture efficiently captures the local substructural syntax required for valid fragment generation without the computational overhead of Transformer-based models.

Once a recurrent neural network (RNN) is trained on target fragments represented as SELFIES strings, it can generate new fragments based on the conditional probability distributions it learned during training. The process begins with a start token as the initial input. At each subsequent timestep, an output token is sampled from the predicted probability distribution P() over the vocabulary X. This sampled token is then used as the input for the next timestep. The sequence generation continues until an end token is sampled, signalling the completion of the sequence21. Using an RNN for fragment generation offers distinct advantages over static fragment libraries. Unlike a fixed database where the chemical space is discrete and limited to enumerated entries, the generative model learns a probability distribution over the chemical space. This allows for interpolation, enabling the generation of chemically valid fragments that may not be explicitly present in the training set, thereby expanding the potential diversity of building blocks beyond a fixed list. However, it is essential to note that the generative capabilities remain influenced by the underlying distribution of the training data. Additionally, the generative approach offers computational efficiency by eliminating the need to store or query massive fragment libraries in memory during ligand growth.

3d Structure formation and scoring

LigGen generates a single 3D conformer using ETKDG embedding algorithm27, during this growth phase for computational efficiency. Since the MCSA protocol requires thousands of iterations, generating and docking multiple conformers at every step would be computationally prohibitive. However, the subsequent local optimization step (BFGS) allows the ligand to adjust its torsion angles within the pocket, effectively exploring the local conformational space without the need for an exhaustive ensemble search. RDKit stands out as a reliable open-source toolkit widely used in cheminformatics for a broad spectrum of tasks, including molecular modelling, computational chemistry, and machine learning.

Additionally, RDKit includes a tool for calculating the Synthetic Accessibility (SA) score, which estimates the ease of synthesizing a given molecule in the laboratory. RDKit sascorer package is used for calculating Synthesizability score of ligands. The SA score considers factors such as molecular complexity, the presence of specific functional groups, and overall structural features. The fragmentScore captures the “historical synthetic knowledge” by analyzing common structural features in a large number of already synthesized molecules. It is the mean of contributions of all fragments in the molecule28. The complexityScore is a numerical value that indicates whether or not the molecules have complex structural properties. It is determined by combining the sizePenalty, macrocyclePenalty, stereoComplexityScore, and ringComplexityScore28.

AutoDock Vina is a widely used software for molecular docking that predicts the preferred orientation of a small molecule (ligand) when bound to a protein (receptor), aiding in the assessment of the molecule’s binding affinity29. The scoring function in AutoDock Vina evaluates different poses of the ligand by estimating their binding affinities. This is achieved through a combination of empirical and knowledge-based approaches that consider factors such as hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic interactions and spatial complementarity between the ligand and the receptor29. 3d conformer is used for the calculation of binding affinity by Autodock vina and synthesizability score by Rdkit. The weighted sum of these two is taken in the Metropolis acceptance criterion, which governs the acceptance or rejection of the newly formed ligand. The aim of this acceptance criteria is such that it tries to decrease the binding affinity score and increase the synthesizability score of the ligand.

BFGS

The Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS) algorithm is a widely used iterative technique for addressing unconstrained nonlinear optimization problems30. It is a member of the quasi-Newton technique family, which aims to approximate the Newton-Raphson method without requiring the second derivatives to be calculated31. By using an estimate of the objective function’s Hessian matrix, BFGS incorporates curvature information to identify the direction of descent.32. This approximation is iteratively improved using gradient evaluations through a generalized secant method. This approach is advantageous compared to Newton’s method, as it avoids the need to compute the exact Hessian at each step, striking a balance between convergence speed and computational efficiency32. This algorithm is widely utilized in many domains, including machine learning, computational chemistry, and structural optimization, due to its fast handling of large-scale optimization problems. Numerous real-world applications have proven to its reliability and efficacy, and many optimization software packages use it. One such popular software using the BFGS algorithm for minimisation is AutoDock-Vina, it utilizes the BFGS algorithm to refine the ligand conformation and achieve a more accurate prediction of binding modes and affinities.

Fragments to ligands

LigGen starts by taking SMILES of fragments and grid box details like size and centre (six variables). Grid box is essential for calculating the Autodock-vina score and locally optimizing the pose of the generated ligand. The program starts the building of the ligand from user-given coordinates which are inside the grid box. Input SMILES to LigGen can be generated using the RNN network or can be from an already available fragment library.

During each iteration program selects a random fragment and attaches it to the ligand that is computed thus far. This stochastic selection process is a deliberate design choice intended to maximize the diversity of the generated chemical space. By utilizing different random seeds for each generation run, LigGen prevents mode collapse—a common issue in deterministic optimizers—ensuring that the algorithm explores a broad range of topological solutions rather than repeatedly converging on a single, identical local minimum. To attach the chosen fragment to the ligand, the program stochastically selects an attachment point within the ligand’s SELFIES string. Rather than restricting growth to pre-defined reaction templates (e.g., BRICS or RECAP rules) which limits the explore-able chemical space to known reaction pathways, this stochastic approach allows for the exploration of novel topological connections. To ensure chemical validity despite the absence of rigid templates, the algorithm employs a strict sanitization protocol. Immediately after a stochastic attachment is proposed, the resulting molecule is validated using RDKit’s sanitization function. Any attachment that results in a chemically invalid graph (e.g., valence violations or aromaticity issues) is automatically rejected, triggering a new attachment attempt.The SMILES of the new ligand undergo 3D structure formation. Since the coordinates of this new ligand are entirely different from the previous one, the structure of the new ligand is aligned with the previous one. This alignment utilizes the Maximum Common Substructure (MCS) to constrain the conformation of the parent structure, ensuring that the new fragment grows into the pocket without disrupting the established binding mode of the previous iteration. Even after the alignment, the position may not be optimal (having the least Autodock-vina score), so local energy minimization of the ligand is performed using Autodock-vina, which utilizes the BFGS algorithm to find the conformer providing the least binding affinity.

Acceptance and rejection of a state are carried out using Metropolis acceptance criteria, which depend on the total score and current temperature. The total score comprises the weighted sum of the Autodock-vina score and synthesizability score given by RDKit on this newly optimized ligand. Ligands that have a higher synthesizability score have a higher probability of being generated in the lab, while those with a lower synthesizability score carry a higher Autodock-vina score weight, thus offering better binding affinity. The temperature of the system decreases by a factor of alpha after every accepted state, where alpha ranges between 0 and 1, making it harder for the program to accept the worst state in the later stage of ligand generation. Results and discussion

In this section, we evaluate the performance of LigGen in comparison with various de novo drug design tools. Section “Crossdocked dataset” presents LigGen’s performance on the CrossDock dataset, while section “Comparative analysis of LigGen for alzheimer’s-related targets: BACE1, MAO-B, and AChE” discusses LigGen’s performance on three Alzheimer ’s-related proteins: BACE1, MAO-B, and AChE. The ligands generated using LigGen were constructed from fragments produced by LigGen’s recurrent neural network (RNN), which was initially trained on the ChEMBL dataset comprising approximately 0.5 million compounds to learn the grammar of SMILES strings. To make the model generate drug-relevant fragments, this RNN was fine-tuned using LigBuilder V3’s curated fragment library. This library includes fragments which are “Bio-Like” and “Drug-Like”, derived via retrosynthetic fragmentation (e.g., cleaving rotatable bonds while preserving functional groups) from sources such as ChEMBL, DrugBank33, WDI34, and SuperDrug35. LigBuilder V3’s fragment dataset, which contained approximately 2,000 fragments, enables it to generate novel, diverse fragments while maintaining compatibility with known chemical scaffolds.

Ligand efficiency and molecular obesity analysis

To address the potential bias of molecular obesity where binding affinity improves simply due to increased molecular size rather than specific interactions, we analyzed the Ligand Efficiency (LE) of the generated library. LE normalizes the binding affinity by the heavy atom count (), providing a robust metric for interaction quality.

Ligand–protein interaction analysis

The ligand-protein interaction profiles were analyzed using LigPlot+46. To analyze the interaction quality in the most favorable binding environments identified by LigGen, we selected the three protein targets (PDB IDs: 3O96, 1H36, 1E8H) that exhibited the highest average binding affinities across the CrossDocked test set. For each of these targets, a representative generated ligand was selected for 2D interaction profiling. The interaction diagrams (Figure 5) reveal detailed hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions between the ligands and key amino acid residues within the binding sites. In the protein pocket 3O96, the ligand forms strong hydrogen bonds with residues Thr82(A), Asn54(A), Glu17(A), and Arg273(A), with bond distances ranging from 2.89 Å to 3.19 Å, suggesting stable ligand accommodation in the pocket. Similarly, in the protein pocket 1H36, the ligand exhibits significant interactions with residues Tyr420(A), Tyr609(A), and adjacent polar groups, establishing hydrogen bonds between 2.82 Å and 3.24 Å, which indicates strong stabilization within the active site. For the protein pocket 1E8H, multiple hydrogen bonds were observed with residues Ser101(A), Ile102(A) Gly103(A), Gly110(A), Ser257(A), Phe254(A) and Lys545(A), showing distances between 2.89 Å and 3.28 Å, further supported by several surrounding hydrophobic contacts that enhance ligand stability. Across all three complexes, the combination of hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions demonstrates efficient ligand accommodation within the binding pockets, implying a high binding affinity and potential inhibitory activity of the screened ligands.

Comparative analysis of LigGen for alzheimer’s-related targets: BACE1, MAO-B, and AChE

While section “Crossdocked dataset” established the statistical performance of LigGen on the standardized CrossDocked benchmark, this section focuses on three specific, clinically relevant targets: Beta-secretase 1 (BACE1), Monoamine Oxidase B (MAO-B), and Acetylcholinesterase (AChE). For these high-value targets, we shift our analysis from broad statistical metrics to specific biophysical assessments. Consequently, rather than relying on static 2D interaction plots (as in section “Ligand–protein interaction analysis”), we employ Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations and MM/PBSA calculations (detailed in section “Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation and MM/PBSA binding energy analysis”) to provide a rigorous, dynamic analysis of the ligand-protein interactions. LigBuilder V3, Pocket2Mol, and REINVENT 447 are representative tools for diverse de novo design strategies: Genetic Algorithms, Deep Learning (Graph-based), and Reinforcement Learning (RL), respectively. Specifically, REINVENT was included to compare our fragment-based growth strategy against an established RL approach that optimizes whole-molecule generation using the same AutoDock Vina scoring function. For the REINVENT baseline, we utilized the REINVENT 4 framework configured with a ’staged learning’ strategy. The optimization was conducted over 20 steps per stage with a batch size of 128 sequences. The agent was rewarded using a geometric mean of three components: the AutoDock Vina score (weight = 0.7), QED (weight = 0.1), and a synthesizability filter based on custom SMARTS alerts (weight=0.2), ensuring a comparable multi-objective optimization to LigGen. We have generated results for three different targets with PDB IDs 2G9448, 2V5Z49, and 7D9O50. The designed ligands were evaluated based on their Binding Affinities (using AutoDock Vina), Synthesizability score (estimates how easy it would be to synthesize a molecule) and Similarity score (how similar a molecule is with already available ligand databases).

A total of 100 ligands were generated for each protein using all three programs, LigGen, Pocket2Mol and LigBuilder. Subsequently, these compounds underwent docking using AutoDock-Vina.

Structural comparison with native inhibitors

To validate that LigGen captures biologically relevant binding modes, we compared the predicted poses of our top-scoring generated ligands with the crystallographic conformations of the known native inhibitors (ZPQ for BACE1, SAG for MAO-B, and H0L for AChE).

As shown in Fig. 8, the generated ligands exhibit a high degree of spatial overlap with the native inhibitors. Quantitatively, the Tanimoto similarity (based on ECFP4 fingerprint) between the top generated ligands and the native inhibitors was found to be 0.42 (2G94), 0.19 (2V5Z), and 0.45 (7D9O). These moderate scores indicate that LigGen is not simply reproducing the native scaffold (which would yield a score near 1.0) but is instead designing novel chemotypes that satisfy the same pharmacophoric requirements.

Synthesizability analysis

Synthesizability score of ligands was calculated using RDKit sascorer package. Sascorer package can characterize molecule synthetic accessibility as a score between 1 (easy to make) and 10 (very difficult to make). The count of ligands having synthesizability score smaller than the value of 5 is listed in Table 6 and Fig. 9 depicts the distribution of synthesizability score for ligands generated for different targets. It is observed that for certain targets, LigGen produces a distribution with slightly higher average SA scores compared to baseline methods. This reflects the algorithm’s prioritization of high-affinity binding modes over trivial synthesizability. While some baseline methods optimize for simple, drug-like heuristics at the cost of binding potency, LigGen effectively explores the “complex-but-synthesizable” chemical space to identify deeper energy minima within the binding pocket.

Ligand similarity comparison

In this section, we assess the similarity of the generated ligands to those available in ChEMBL library using SwissSimilarity. By comparing our ligands with the extensive collection in the ChEMBL database, we aim to evaluate their novelty. This comparison helps in understanding how closely our ligands resemble existing compounds and provides insights into their uniqueness and potential for further research or development. To assess the novelty of the generated compounds, we performed a similarity screening against the ChEMBL database (version 29) using the SwissSimilarity web server. We utilized Extended Connectivity Fingerprints (ECFP4) for this analysis, as they robustly capture topological substructures and are widely accepted for structural similarity comparisons. The similarity score ranges from 0 (completely different) to 1 (identical). As illustrated in Fig. 10, the distribution of similarity scores for LigGen-derived ligands centers around 0.3–0.4 across all three targets. This low average similarity indicates that LigGen is not merely retrieving known inhibitors from the chemical space but is successfully generating novel chemical entities with distinct scaffolds. This ability to access unexplored regions of chemical space while maintaining high binding affinity is a key advantage of the de novo design approach. Conclusion

In this study, we developed a novel de-novo ligand generation tool which used fragments as building blocks. The fragment generation itself is based on generative AI based on RNN which was pretrained on ChEMBL dataset and fine-tuned on LigBuilder’s fragment library. We evaluated LigGen’s performance on CrossDock dataset and three Alzheimer’s-related proteins to provide a comparison with several state-of-the-art de novo drug design tools, including Pocket2Mol, LigBuilder V3, and others. On the CrossDock dataset, LigGen demonstrated competitive binding affinities, with a Vina score of -7.508 ± 1.83, outperforming all baseline methods like 3D-SBDD, FLAG, and DrugGPS. LigGen also showcased strong diversity in the generated molecules while maintaining reasonable synthesizability. Although some tools like Pocket2Mol showed higher QED values, LigGen consistently produced ligands with good Lipinski compliance, suggesting its balance between drug-likeness and innovation.

In the Alzheimer’s-related protein analysis, ligands produced by LigGen consistently showed better binding affinities compared to those from LigBuilder and Pocket2Mol. LigGen demonstrated a balanced approach between ligand synthesizability and structural complexity, as seen in its ability to produce ligands with moderate synthesizability scores.

To facilitate broader adoption and usability, we have also developed a free-to-use web server for ligand generation, accessible at58. This allows researchers to generate ligands conveniently using LigGen without requiring local installations.

Overall, the results indicate that LigGen is a robust tool for fragment-based ligand generation, capable of producing high-quality ligands with competitive docking scores and favourable synthesizability. LigGen provides a novel method for generating ligands using a fragment-based de novo drug design approach. While the optimization process relies on AutoDock Vina, introducing a potential bias towards this scoring function, the robustness of the generated ligands was orthogonally validated using 100ns Molecular Dynamics simulations. Furthermore, this explicit optimization distinguishes LigGen from data-driven deep learning baselines (e.g., Pocket2Mol) which exhibit “implicit circularity.” Since these models are trained on curated datasets of protein-ligand complexes already selected for high affinity, their generative capabilities are inherently dependent on the specific distributions of known binders. In contrast, LigGen’s physics-driven approach minimizes energy independently of training set biases, allowing for the discovery of novel binding modes that may not be represented in existing structural databases.
