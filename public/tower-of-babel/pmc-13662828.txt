Occupancy‐Guided Continuous Antipsychotic Dosing Provides a Translational Model for Evaluating Extrapyramidal Motor Liability in Mice
Authors: Yosefu Arime, Kaoru Irahara, Noriaki Ohkawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe therapeutic effects of antipsychotic drugs are primarily achieved through dopamine D2 receptor antagonism. However, excessive striatal D2 receptor blockade is associated with extrapyramidal side effects (EPS) in schizophrenic patients. Clinical studies indicate a therapeutic window of approximately 60%–80% striatal D2 receptor occupancy, with higher occupancy increasing the risk of EPS. However, conventional rodent dosing paradigms rarely maintain clinically comparable receptor occupancy, limiting their translational relevance. Here, we investigated whether continuous antipsychotic administration designed to achieve clinically relevant striatal D2 receptor occupancy can be used to evaluate extrapyramidal motor liability in mice. Using an occupancy‐guided dosing paradigm, we continuously administered haloperidol or olanzapine and assessed catalepsy as an index of EPS. To validate catalepsy as an EPS‐like phenotype, we examined whether it could be attenuated by an anticholinergic agent, consistent with the clinical management of EPS. Catalepsy was observed only under high‐occupancy haloperidol condition. A significant increase in catalepsy time was observed only in mice receiving haloperidol at a dose producing approximately 90% D2 receptor occupancy on day 3, whereas no catalepsy was detected under lower occupancy conditions with either haloperidol or olanzapine. Importantly, haloperidol‐induced catalepsy was significantly attenuated by scopolamine. Together, these findings demonstrate that an occupancy‐guided continuous antipsychotic dosing paradigm enables evaluation of EPS‐like behavior under clinically relevant D2 receptor occupancy conditions in mice, providing a translational framework linking clinical PET studies with preclinical research.Keywords: antipsychotics, catalepsy, dopamine D2 receptor occupancy, extrapyramidal side effects, schizophrenia

Introduction

Antipsychotic drugs are the cornerstone of schizophrenia treatment. Their clinical efficacy against positive symptoms such as hallucinations and delusions is strongly associated with dopamine D2 receptor antagonism in the striatum [1, 2]. Positron emission tomography (PET) studies in patients with schizophrenia have identified a therapeutic window of approximately 60%–80% striatal D2 receptor occupancy. When occupancy exceeds 75%–85%, the risk of extrapyramidal side effects (EPS) markedly increases, except for partial D2 agonists [3, 4, 5]. When EPS occurs in patients, dose reduction or concomitant anticholinergic medications are necessary. However, anticholinergic agents can impair cognition and limit the clinical utility of the antipsychotics [6, 7]. Therefore, defining the relationship between sustained D2 receptor occupancy and EPS is critical, not only to improve the translational validity of animal studies, but also to the development of mechanism‐based approaches to EPS mitigation. Despite the established clinical framework, most rodent studies of antipsychotics rely on acute dosing or once or twice‐daily injections. In humans, the half‐lives of antipsychotics are approximately 12–54 h, but in rodents, they are typically only 2–5 h [8, 9, 10, 11]. Thus, conventional paradigms produce rapid fluctuations in brain drug levels in rodent models, with near‐complete elimination at trough.

The rationale for antipsychotic dosing in animal studies has usually been based on behavioral endpoints (e.g., the suppression of psychostimulant‐induced hyperlocomotion) or allometric scaling by body surface area [12, 13, 14]. However, these approaches do not directly account for clinically defined D2 receptor occupancy. We have previously established a continuous antipsychotic dosing regimen that maintains 60%–80% striatal D2 receptor occupancy in mice for several weeks. This regimen suppressed acute phencyclidine (PCP)‐induced hyperlocomotion in chronic PCP‐treated mice, a well‐established pharmacological model of schizophrenia [15]. However, it is unclear whether occupancy‐guided continuous antipsychotic administration can model dose‐dependent adverse effects such as EPS in mice.

Here, we investigate whether continuous antipsychotic treatment designed to maintain clinically relevant striatal D2 receptor occupancy can predict EPS in mice. We also examine whether the anticholinergic drug scopolamine attenuates antipsychotic‐induced catalepsy.

Materials and Methods

Animals

All procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Dokkyo Medical University (Permit Number: 1112) and conducted in accordance with Japanese animal welfare rules and guidelines. Male C57BL/6J mice (8 weeks old at treatment initiation) were housed, four per cage, at 23 (±1) °C, with a 12‐h light/dark cycle (lights on 07:00–19:00) and ad libitum food and water.

Drugs

Haloperidol (HAL) (H1512, Sigma‐Aldrich, MO, USA) and olanzapine (OLZ) (O1141, Sigma‐Aldrich) were dissolved in 2% glacial acetic acid in sterile water adjusted to a pH of ~5 with 1 N NaOH. Scopolamine hydrobromide n‐hydrate (198‐07971, Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan) was dissolved in saline.

Rationale for Dosage of Continuous Antipsychotic Treatment

The aim of the present study was to evaluate whether effective or higher dosage causes movement‐related adverse effects in mice. Our previous study revealed the occupancy levels of striatal dopamine D2 receptors are as follows: HAL 0.5 mg/kg/day (day 3; 80.6% ± 2.97%, day 13; 63.5% ± 3.55%), HAL 0.75 mg/kg/day (day 3; 90.7% ± 1.13%, day 13; 78.3% ± 1.19%), OLZ 7.5 mg/kg/day (day 3; 80.9% ± 3.70%, day 13; 70.8% ± 2.80%), and OLZ 10 mg/kg/day (day 3; 79.9% ± 1.44%, day 13; 73.4% ± 2.78%) [15]. Thus, we used HAL 0.75 mg/kg/day on day 3 as the high dose and the other doses as the therapeutic dosage.

Surgery

An osmotic minipump (ALZET model 1002 for vehicle and HAL, and model 2002 for OLZ, CA, USA) containing either the vehicle (VEH), HAL (0.5 or 0.75 mg/kg/day), or OLZ (7.5 or 10 mg/kg/day) was implanted subcutaneously in each mouse under sevoflurane anesthesia.

Catalepsy Test

Using a modified bar test developed in previous studies [16, 17], we assessed catalepsy on days 3 and 13 to align with the time points at which striatal D2 receptor occupancy was previously quantified under identical dosing conditions [15] (Figure 1A). After 30‐min acclimation, each mouse was gently placed with its forelimbs on a bar (3 mm diameter, 3.5 cm height) and its hindlimbs on the laboratory bench (Figure 1B). The latency to terminate catalepsy (forelimbs on floor or climbing) was recorded, with a 120‐s cutoff. Each session comprised three trials, and the mean latency was used in our analysis. Body weight was expressed as a percent change relative to day 1. To assess pharmacological reversal, mice intraperitoneally received scopolamine (0.2 or 1 mg/kg) or saline 30 min before testing (Figure 2A).

Statistics

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS Statistics (IBM, NY, USA), and EZR graphical interface for R. Data were analyzed using correlation analysis, two‐way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post hoc tests, and Kruskal–Wallis tests followed by post hoc Steel–Dwass tests. p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Catalepsy Test During Continuous Antipsychotic Treatment

To evaluate extrapyramidal motor behavior under an occupancy‐guided continuous antipsychotic dosing paradigm, catalepsy time was measured on days 3 and 13 after minipump implantation in C57BL/6J mice. Continuous antipsychotic administration revealed a significant main effect of treatment on catalepsy time (two‐way repeated measures ANOVA: F(4, 34) = 4.506, p < 0.01), with no significant day × treatment interaction (F(4, 34) = 1.191, p = 0.333) or main effect of day (F(1, 34) = 0.034, p = 0.854). Post hoc analysis revealed a significant increase in catalepsy time only in the HAL 0.75 mg/kg/day group on day 3 (p < 0.01), whereas no significant differences were observed in other groups or at day 13 (Figure 1C,D). We observed no significant differences in body weight change across treatment groups during continuous antipsychotic administration and no correlation between body weight change and catalepsy time (Figure S1A–E), suggesting comparable systemic drug exposure.

To examine the relationship between D2 receptor occupancy and EPS‐like behavior, we analyzed catalepsy time in relation to estimated striatal D2 receptor occupancy based on our previous work conducted under identical dosing conditions [15]. Catalepsy was observed only under the high‐occupancy condition with haloperidol (approximately 90% D2 receptor occupancy) (Figure 1E).

Effects of an Anti‐Cholinergic Agent on Continuous Antipsychotic Treatment‐Induced Cataleptic Behavior

Subsequently, we evaluated the effects of the anti‐cholinergic agent, scopolamine, on continuous haloperidol‐induced cataleptic behavior in mice on day 3. A Kruskal–Wallis test revealed a significant difference between groups (p = 0.014). Post hoc analysis with the Steel–Dwass test found that scopolamine 1 mg/kg significantly decreased the prolongation of catalepsy time induced by HAL 0.75 mg/kg/day (p < 0.05) compared to saline‐treated controls (Figure 2B).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated extrapyramidal motor behavior using a continuous antipsychotic dosing paradigm to reproduce clinically relevant striatal D2 receptor occupancy in mice. Under this occupancy‐guided paradigm, catalepsy was observed only under high‐occupancy haloperidol condition (> 90%), whereas no catalepsy was observed under lower‐occupancy haloperidol or olanzapine conditions. These findings indicate that the occupancy‐guided dosing paradigm enables evaluation of EPS‐like behavior under clinically relevant pharmacodynamic conditions. The temporal specificity of catalepsy is consistent with the pharmacodynamic profile of this dosing regimen, including sustained striatal D2 receptor occupancy.

In patients with schizophrenia, antipsychotic‐induced EPS are commonly managed with anticholinergic agents [18]. Consistent with this, we found that scopolamine (1 mg/kg) reversed haloperidol‐induced catalepsy, supporting its interpretation as an EPS‐like phenotype. However, scopolamine at this dose is known to impair working memory in mice [19]. Furthermore, lower doses of scopolamine (0.3 mg/kg) impair social recognition memory [20]. These findings indicate that antipsychotic‐induced catalepsy is a pharmacologically modifiable extrapyramidal motor phenotype. They also highlight the clinical trade‐off between motor symptom relief and cognitive side effects of anticholinergic medication.

One limitation of the present study is that only haloperidol was examined under a high D2 receptor occupancy condition. Although D2 receptor occupancy is an important determinant of EPS liability, drug‐specific effects, including differences in receptor binding profiles between haloperidol and olanzapine, may also contribute to the distinct behavioral outcomes observed [21]. In addition, substantial inter‐individual variability in catalepsy time was observed among mice receiving chronic antipsychotic treatment. Catalepsy time did not correlate with changes in body weight, suggesting that dopamine D2 receptor levels or their functional state may have contributed to these individual differences [22]. Moreover, EPS in patients with schizophrenia encompass a range of motor symptoms, including parkinsonian features and tardive dyskinesia. Although catalepsy is a well‐established measure of extrapyramidal motor effects, it does not capture dyskinesia following long‐term antipsychotic exposure. Tardive dyskinesia‐like phenotypes can be assessed in rodents using paradigms such as vacuous chewing [23]. Incorporating additional behavioral assays may improve the predictive accuracy of preclinical models of antipsychotic‐induced EPS. In addition, muscarinic receptor blockade by scopolamine may influence dopaminergic transmission, including striatal dopamine release [24, 25]. Therefore, the attenuation of catalepsy by scopolamine may reflect both cholinergic and dopaminergic mechanisms, which should be addressed in future studies.

Overall, a striatal D2 receptor occupancy‐guided approach appears to bridge a critical gap between clinical observations and animal models. It thereby improves the translational validity of preclinical antipsychotic research.
