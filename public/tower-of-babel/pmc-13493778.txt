Detrimental influence of high neuroticism on visuospatial selective attention: behavioral and ERP evidence
Authors: Yan Zhang, Xianghong Zhan, Shan Cao, Jinchai Deng, Yong Liu, Dandan Jia, Aishe Gao, Shan Li, Wenju Zhang, Zhongpeng Qin, Lei Huo, Jingwen Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuroticism constitutes a risk factor for mood disorders and cognitive impairment, but its impact on visuospatial selective attention remains unclear, especially in young people. Our study aimed to explore the detrimental influence of neuroticism and its underlying electrophysiological mechanisms. Participants (31 with low neuroticism and 31 with high neuroticism) underwent electrocardiogram (EEG) signal acquisition and event-related potentials (ERP) component identification while performing attention search tasks. High-neuroticism participants showed a significant decline in the accuracy relative to the low-neuroticism group. The amplitudes of the N2-posterior contralateral component (N2pc) and sustained posterior contralateral negativity (SPCN) were significantly larger in the high-neuroticism group, indicating dysfunctions in attentional resource allocation and information retention in visual short-term memory (VSTM). Regardless of the electrode site examined, high-neuroticism participants exhibited a larger P3a than those in the low-neuroticism group, which was closely related to their difficulty in disengaging attention from novel stimuli. The amplitude and latency of P3a differed significantly across electrode sites in the frontal, parietal, and temporal lobes. P3a latencies gradually increased from the frontal zero (FZ) to the central zero (CZ) site and then to the parietal zero (PZ) site. Meanwhile, P3a amplitudes displayed a statistically significant linear decrease, implying that the frontal lobe activity primarily contributed to the P3a response. Importantly, the interaction between neuroticism and electrode site significantly affected P3b amplitudes. P3b amplitudes exhibited an increasing tendency at PZ in the high-neuroticism group, where it was the largest, but the differences were not statistically significant at FZ and CZ between the two groups. It has concluded that high-neuroticism individuals have difficulty tracking targets. These data suggest that high levels of neuroticism affect the visuospatial selective attention of college students in a multi-dimensional way, presumably due to dysfunctions in attentional resource allocation, information maintenance in VSTM, and attention control.Keywords: Neuroticism, Event-related potentials, Attention, N2pc, SPCN, P3a, P3bSubject terms: Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Neuroticism is a dimension of temperament characterized by two key features: a tendency to experience negative emotions and to interpret environmental demands as threatening. Individuals with this personality trait have a tendency to retain and experience negative thoughts and feelings1. Neuroticism is associated with cognitive functional decline2 and may even lead to the onset of mild cognitive impairment (MCI)3 or Alzheimer’s disease (AD)4. Longitudinal studies have clarified this relationship. For instance, high-neuroticism individuals show a 2.24-fold higher risk of developing MCI than healthy controls after a 10-year follow-up5. Additionally, a higher degree of neuroticism in midlife (aged 38–52) was associated with an increased risk of AD dementia during a 38-year follow-up6. An increasing number of studies are demonstrating that an increase in neuroticism or negative affect scores may be a sign of MCI7,8 or AD4 in the elderly, as neuroticism is a personality trait associated with impaired attention, memory, and error detection9. Detecting these differences in younger populations may help delineate early markers of vulnerability, although whether this directly links to later-life pathology remains unclear.

As an important dimension of cognition, visuospatial attention biases associated with neuroticism have long been a focus of research. However, the findings in this field have been very inconsistent. Most studies suggest that high-neuroticism individuals are burdened with negative emotions such as anxiety and depression, which greatly affect their attentional search performance10. This is illustrated by the observation that increasing neuroticism, as identified via the Eysenck personality questionnaire (EPQ) scale, is correlated with a decline in change detection accuracy and attentional control over the visual field11. Research on attentional blinks (ABs) revealed that high neuroticism is accompanied by larger ABs and lower overall target accuracy, purportedly by influencing the amount of attentional investment and flexibility12. There are several theoretical perspectives on the negative influence of high neuroticism on the selective attention. Frederickson et al.13 postulated that positive emotions can broaden the “thought-action repertoires” and build “enduring resources”, while negative emotions prevent high-neuroticism individuals from attending to items broadly and building lasting psychological reserves, leading to the author proposing a broaden-and-build theoretical model. The limited-resource hypothesis posits that cognitive processing resources are finite. Accordingly, the inferior search performance of highly neurotic individuals may be attributed to their negative emotions, which consume additional attentional resources14. Other theories argue that high-neuroticism individuals may be incompetent at professions that require intense attention (such as combat aviation)15, but perform well in most creative or effort-demanding tasks. Additionally, the benefits of the brooding rumination induced by worrying in response to stress or challenges may account for such individuals’ creativity and good cognitive performance to some extent16. Attentional control theory offers a framework to reconcile these findings17, which suggests that high neuroticism impairs processing efficiency (the relationship between task performance and processing resource use) more than performance effectiveness (quality of performance)18. Additionally, Saylik et al.19 elucidates that, when completing difficult or stressful tasks, high neuroticism causes high levels of worry-related arousal and task-irrelevant mental activities such as rumination, which demands more attentional resources to deal with ongoing tasks and task-irrelevant mental activities. If compensatory effort or an increased use of attentional resources in response to negative emotions can meet processing needs, task performance (accuracy) remains unaffected or even superior, and only a longer reaction time and greater brain activity may be observed. Conversely, if the enhanced effort or increased allocation of attentional resource cannot meet processing needs, task performance will be affected, and the accuracy will significantly decrease. Eysenck et al.20 suggested that high-neuroticism individuals performed worse than low-neuroticism only in tasks requiring central executive functions of switching or inhibition. For example, in a dual-stream rapid serial visual presentation task, in which undergraduate students were asked to focus on one stream at a time, distracters produced longer ABs in high-neuroticism individuals, as measured on the International Personality Item Pool 10-item scale21. Bredemeier et al.21 interpreted this as indicating that attention control deficits in high-neuroticism individuals were due to negative emotions capturing more of their attention, from which they had difficulty disengaging their attention.

To date, previous findings have suggested that differences in attention resource allocation and attentional control are the theoretical foundation for the impact of trait neuroticism on selective attention, but the temporal dynamics, specifically, at which processing stages (e.g., early selection, working memory maintenance, or late cognitive control) these effects arise, remain unclear. To dissect this time course and its underlying mechanisms, event-related potentials (ERPs) were employed. This technique afforded the temporal resolution necessary to track distinct neurocognitive components of visuospatial selective attention.

Among these components, several have been established as markers of specific attentional operations that are critical for understanding the spatiotemporal dynamics of visual selection. The N2pc, a brainwave elicited over the posterior scalp electrodes contralateral to attended items. It is typically observed when the participants are asked to identify a fixed-feature target that pops out from a uniform array of distractors. This component is a widely used index for the moment-to-moment allocation of visuospatial attention resources22. The N2pc component appears to reflect a spatial filtering process that suppresses irrelevant distractors and facilitates the further processing of selected visual information23. Visual stimuli selected for further processing then pass into the visual short-term and working memory and elicit a later ERP component called sustained posterior contralateral negativity (SPCN). This brainwave begins approximately 300–450 ms after stimulus onset and persists for the duration of the retention interval24. SPCN is sharply sensitive to memory load25 and reflects the amount of information maintained in the VSTM26. Another important aspect of selective attention is the deployment or orientation of attention toward the source of visual stimuli. This orientation involves disengagement, movement, and reengagement. The P3a component, originating from the prefrontal lobe, frontal lobe, cingulate gyrus, and hippocampus, shows the strongest activation in the central and frontal regions27. It is elicited when attention shifts away from the current cognitive task to a novel stimulus and is therefore considered an index of stimulus-driven, involuntary attention allocation or the capturing of attention28. The P3b component, originating from temporal parietal lobe, is thought to reflect the process of tracking target stimuli and goal-driven voluntary attention allocation29. The P3b amplitudes reflect the amount of attentional resources allocated to tracking target stimuli, while the latency represents the duration of sustained attention.

The evidence suggests high-neuroticism individuals have the tendency to experience negative emotions and are generally more emotionally unstable than low-neuroticism individuals. The overload of excessive negative emotions means they fail to build lasting psychological reserves, and extra cognitive resources are allocated to processing their existing negative emotions. Hence, the selection and processing of visual stimuli become so demanding that they fail to function, and their attentional control function may suffer. On these grounds, we hypothesized that trait neuroticism affects the visuospatial selective attention of young people. That is, in this non-emotional task, high neuroticism is expected to impair attention search performance (reduced accuracy) and enhance the attention-related ERP components (increased N2pc, P3a, P3b, and SPCN amplitudes). As conceptualized by attentional control theory, a predisposition to experience negative emotions may lead to attentional capture and difficulties in disengagement, requiring the allocation of additional cognitive resources to task performance. This resource competition may partially account for the observed selective attention dysfunction. To validate above hypothesis and explore the underlying electrophysiological mechanisms, a modified novel task combining an oddball paradigm with an attention search paradigm was adopted to track multiple visual-attentional processing components, such as N2pc (deployment of selective attention)30, SPCN (visual working memory consolidation/maintenance)31, P3a (disengagement and re-orientation of attention to novel stimuli)32, and P3b (tracking target stimuli)33. Investigating these electrophysiological components in college students may help characterize the attentional signatures of high neuroticism.

Materials and methods

Participants

College students (n = 456, aged 18–24) recruited from Henan University of Traditional Chinese Medicine participated in the questionnaire survey as the participants. The 88-item Chinese version of the EPQ scale was used to assess the levels of neuroticism. EPQ includes four subscales: psychoticism, neuroticism, extraversion, and lying. The neuroticism subscale has shown to be effective in evaluating individuals’ personality traits34 or stable emotional state, and the Cronbach’s alpha is 0.896. Participants were divided into the high-neuroticism group if the N-scale conversion score of the EPQ scale was > 61.5 points and the low-neuroticism group if it was < 38.5 points. Then, the 72 participants who met the high and low-neuroticism criteria were voluntarily enrolled for ERP detection and were matched 1:1 according to gender and age (± 2 years). Ten participants were excluded from the final analyses because of pairing problems or loss from trial due to eye blinks and movements. The remaining 62 participants matched 1:1 according to gender and age respectively served as the high-neuroticism [9 men and 22 women; mean age 21.20 years, with standard deviation (SD) of 0.85] and low-neuroticism groups (9 men and 22 women; mean age 21.20 years, with SD of 1.19). All participants were with Chinese Han nationality, spoke Chinese as their mother language, were right-handed; had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and denied history of neurological or psychological disorders, cognitive impairment, or brain injury. They all signed informed consent forms before participation and were paid RMB 200 as financial compensation. The study was ethically approved and supervised by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Henan University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (2019HL-136-01) and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Procedure

Neuropsychological testing

Visual selective attention was detected with the map search subtest (number of target symbols found among distractors within and 2 min) of the Test of Everyday Attention (TEA). The test-retest reliability of map search (min) was 0.83 and that of map search (2 min) was 0.86. The ability for dynamic transformation of spatial representations was assessed using the mental rotation test, comprising 20 questions worth 2 points eac35. This paper-and-pencil test of mental rotation was used to establish substantial internal consistency (Kuder-Richardson 20 = 0.88) and test-retest reliability (0.83). These neuropsychological scales were employed to evaluate participants’ selective attention and ability to judge spatial relationships, both of which were essential for fulfilling the map search task in our study.

ERP paradigm

The experimental paradigm was initially designed to study one or two specific cognitive processing components or attention-related components36. To precisely and efficiently assess a broader range of visual and attention processing components in a single-test task, Bolduc-Teasdale modified the previously published paradigms and developed a new approach for clinical use by combining the oddball paradigm and visual search paradigm37. This approach was adopted in the present study.

Stimuli and presentation

Each participant firstly performed one practice block of 40 trials, followed by 5 blocks of 80 experimental trials (400 trials total). A computer screen located 1.14 m in front of the participants presented each visual search display in sequence. Participants were instructed to fixate on the fixation point at the center of the screen and remain focus. Each trial was initiated by pressing the spacebar key. After initiation, a variable interval with a mean of 600 ms (600 ± 200 ms, random jitter) was presented, immediately followed by an empty screen interval of 500 ms. Then, visual search stimuli were presented for 150 ms. Participants had 2000 ms to respond to regular stimuli and 1500 ms to respond to no-go stimuli. An example of the trial sequence is presented in Fig. 1.

Experimental paradigm. (A) Timeline of experiment. (B) Different types of stimuli and exact frequency of each stimulus type: I: Frequent color standard stimulus (52.5%): frequent opening position (bottom, right, or left) of frequent color; II: Frequent color target stimulus(17.5%): infrequent opening position (top) of frequent color; III: Rare color standard stimulus (15%): frequent opening position (bottom, right, or left) of rare color; IV: Rare color target stimulus (5%): infrequent opening position (top) of rare color; V: same color stimulus (10%).

The visual search display incorporated four colored squares with a gap of 0.33° on one side, each square subtending a visual angle of 1°×1°. The placement of the four colored squares is shown in Fig. 1. The centers of the upper two squares were located closer to the fixation point, at 1.5° below and 3.5° to the left or right of the fixation point. The centers of the lower two squares were located further from the fixation point, at 3° below and 5° to the left or right of the fixation point. The squares could be red, green, or blue. When all the four squares were green (40 trials, accounting for 10% of the total), they were deemed target-absent stimuli (no-go trials), and the participants were instructed to withhold a response. When squares of two colors appeared, the square with a color different from the other three was the target stimulus. For example, if the three same-colored squares were red (distractors), the remaining square (green or blue) would be the target. The green target square combined with the red distractors was the regular (frequent) color visual stimulus, accounting for 70% of the trials (280 trials in total); and the blue target square combined with the red distractors was the rare (infrequent) color visual stimulus, accounting for 20% of the trials (80 trials in total). The regular (frequent) color and rare (infrequent) color stimuli shared the same visual properties and behavioral requirement, except for color. Target squares (green or blue) were presented with equal probability at each of the four possible positions (near-right from fixation, near-left from fixation, far-right from fixation, and far-left from fixation). Additionally, each target square (green or blue) had an opening gap on one of the four (top, bottom, left, and right) sides, and the location of the opening gap was equally likely to be up, down, left, or right (25% each). To maximize the amplitude of the N2pc, the target green or blue square with the small gap on the top side was presented either in the lower left or lower right visual field38. All parameters were counterbalanced among the participants. To prevent motor lateralization effects, the response hand related to target searching was also counterbalanced among participants. To avoid low-level sensory responses, a chromameter (Minolta CS100) was adopted to calibrate the intensity of the different colors of the squares.

The main task of a participant was to search for the target square (the one with a color different from the others), identify its opening position, and press the “F” key when the gap was at the top (infrequent opening position, p = 0.25), or “J” when the gap was at the bottom, right, or left side (frequent opening position, p = 0.75). All ERP components were elicited by the visual stimuli, which varied in terms of the target square opening, color, and frequency. By presenting the visual stimuli either to the left or to the right of the fixation point, we elicited the lateralized components of N2pc and SPCN. The appearance of a target stimulus (green or blue square with a gap at the top) with a small probability (25%) among the standard stimuli (green or blue square with a gap at bottom, right, or left) elicited the P3b component. Target-absent stimuli (no-go trials) combined with rare (infrequent) color visual stimuli served as the novelty stimuli that helped elicit the P3a component, as explained in Bolduc-Teasdale (2012)29.

EEG recording and analysis

ERP detection was conducted in a standardized soundproof and electrically shielded laboratory of Henan Provincial Engineering Technology Research Center for Chinese and Western Integrative Prevention and Treatment of Brain Cognitive Disease. Prior to EEG recording, all participants underwent a standardized preparation protocol which included a full explanation of the task and acclimatization to the laboratory setting. After washing and drying the participants’ hair, we placed a 64-channel Neuroscan adaptive Ag/AgCl electrode cap on their head. Horizontal electrooculogram (HEOG) and vertical electrooculogram (VEOG) were recorded with two pairs of electrodes, respectively, to screen horizontal eye movements and eye blinks. One pair was placed above and below the right eye, and the other pair was placed 1 cm from the lateral canthi. Quik-Gel electrode paste was used to keep electrode impedance below 5 kΩ throughout the experiment.

Offline analysis was performed. The EEG and EOG signals were acquired at 1000 Hz and subsequently down-sampled offline to 512 Hz for analysis. The data were then bandpass filtered (0.5–30 Hz) and notch filtered (48–52 Hz). The mean EEG values of bilateral mastoid M1 and M2 were used as a reference. The independent component analysis technique was used to correct for eye blinks, horizontal eye movements, heartbeats, and myokinetic artifacts. The EEG was segmented into 1000-ms epochs, including from 200-ms pre-stimulus to 800-ms post-stimulus. The baseline was 200 ms before the onset of stimuli. To avoid anticipatory responses, all response data with reaction times less than 200 ms or beyond 2000 ms were excluded. EEG epochs containing amplitudes exceeding ± 100 µV were also excluded. The remaining 1000-ms epochs were averaged for all combinations of conditions. Finally, the grand average ERPs of all participants in each group were obtained (for figures only).

The E-prime 2.0 system was adopted to write the program and present the attention search paradigm stimuli, and the Curry8 acquisition system with Neuroscan SynAmps 64-channel amplifier was applied to record and transmit ERP signals. MATLAB 2013b software and plugins such as EEGLAB and ERPLAB were used to preprocess the EEG signals and obtain the corresponding ERP components.

Using the protocols of previous studies29,37 and a visual inspection of ERP waveform and topography, we analyzed the four main ERP components N2pc, SPCN, P3a, and P3b. The final percentages of valid trials (correct response trials) included for analysis were all above 85% for all conditions in both the high- and low-neuroticism groups. For these valid trials, the amplitudes of the P3a, P3b, N2pc, and SPCN components were all greater than 35 µV. All components were analyzed in terms of both average amplitude and peak latency.

Waveform subtraction methods were used to separate specific ERP components39. According to previous studies40 and thorough observation of the waveform and topographic maps, PO7 and PO8 electrode sites were selected to determine differential waves. Lateralized N2pc and SPCN components were obtained by subtracting the ipsilateral neural activity. Contralateral activity was defined as the signal at PO7 when the target was in the right visual field, and at PO8 when the target was in the left visual field. Ipsilateral activity was defined as the signal at PO7 for left visual field targets, and at PO8 for right visual field targets29. The time windows of 220–265 ms and 320–660 ms were used to calculate the average N2pc and SPCN amplitudes at the PO7/PO8 electrode sites, where these components exhibited their maximal amplitudes.

According to previous studies41–43 and the scalp topographies of P3a and P3b over frontal, parietal, and temporal regions, three electrode sites at the frontal zero (FZ), central zero (CZ), and parietal zero (PZ) were selected for group comparisons. The P3a wave was acquired by averaging the waveforms of no-go same-color trials and frequent color standard trials respectively, and then subtracting the frequent color standard stimulus signals from the no-go same-color stimulus signals. To obtain the P3b, the average signals of the frequent color standard trials were subtracted from those of the frequent color target trials. The average amplitudes and latencies of the P3a and P3b components within the time windows of 370–580 ms and 550–760 ms, respectively, were compared between groups.

Statistical analysis

SPSS23.0 software was applied for descriptive statistics and data analysis. All data in this study were quantitative and described as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD). The scores for the mental rotation test and Map Searching subtest of the TEA were compared via independent sample t-test to study high- and low-neuroticism group differences. The independent-samples t-test was applied to analyze behavioral results (reaction time and accuracy) between the two groups to investigate the influence of trait neuroticism on attention search performance. Behavioral data from different conditions were only used to elicit relevant waves; therefore, no further statistical analyses were conducted on them. To investigate group differences in visual selective attention, four waves from the ERP data were computed and analyzed: N2pc, SPCN, P3a, and P3b. Average amplitudes and peak latencies at PO7/PO8 electrode sites of the lateralized N2pc and SPCN components of the high- and low-neuroticism groups were compared using an independent-samples t-test. Given the frontal-parietal scalp distribution of the P3a and P3b components, three electrode sites at FZ, CZ, and PZ were chosen for comparison between groups. Average amplitudes and peak latencies of P3a and P3b components were subjected to two-way repeated measures ANOVA with high neuroticism and low neuroticism as between-subject factors and sites (FZ, CZ, PZ) as within-subject factors. The Bonferroni method was used to correct p values in multiple comparisons. If the spherical test results did not satisfy the spherical distribution hypothesis (p < 0.05), the Geisser–Greenhouse method was used to correct p values. All tests were two-tailed, with the significant level set at ɑ = 0.05. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Electrophysiological results

N2pc and SPCN components

Using an independent sample t-test, we compared the components of N2pc and SPCN (derived by the difference between the contralateral and ipsilateral waveforms at the PO7/PO8 electrode sites) between the high- and low-neuroticism groups. Neither N2pc latency [t (60) = 0.346, p = 0.730, Cohen’s d = 0.088] nor SPCN latency [t (60) = -0.280, p = 0.781, Cohen’s d = -0.075] was significant different between the two groups. N2pc amplitude was significantly larger for the high-neuroticism group (M = -2.63 µV, SD = 1.69) than for the low-neuroticism group (M = -1.83 µV, SD = 1.33) group, t (60) = -2.073, p = 0.043, Cohen’s d = -0.526 (see Fig. 3). The difference in SPCN amplitude was statistically significant between the two groups, t (60) = -2.059, p = 0.044, Cohen’s d = 0.523. Compared with low-neuroticism participants (M = -1.98 µV, SD = 1.46), high-neuroticism participants (M = -2.86 µV, SD = 1.87) exhibited increased SPCN amplitudes (see Fig. 3).

P3a and P3b components

A 2 × 3 repeated measures ANOVA was conducted to compare the average amplitudes and peak latencies of P3a and P3b components, with neuroticism (high neuroticism, low neuroticism) used as a between-subject factor and electrode sites (FZ, CZ, PZ) as within-subject factors.

As shown in Fig. 4, the P3a waveforms were obtained by subtracting the frequent color standard stimuli signal from the same-color stimulus signal. The P3a amplitude results showed that the main effect of neuroticism was significant (see Fig. 5A), [F (1, 60) = 5.546, p = 0.022, ηp² = 0.085]. The P3a amplitude in the high-neuroticism group (M = 6.767 µV, SD = 0.682) was greater than that in the low-neuroticism group (M = 4.497 µV, SD = 0.682) regardless of electrode (FZ, CZ, or PZ). The main effect for electrode site was also significant (see Fig. 5A and B), [F (1, 60) = 39.375, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.396], with the largest P3a at FZ (M = 7.165 µV, SD = 0.586), followed by CZ (M = 6.030 µV, SD = 0.502), and PZ (M = 3.701 µV, SD = 0.510), and there was a statistically significant linear decrease in amplitude across the three electrode sites (from FZ, CZ, to PZ) [F (1, 60) = 45.036, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.429] (see Fig. 5C). The interactions between neuroticism and electrode site for P3a amplitude were not significant (p = 0.462). The P3a latency results showed that the main effect was significant for electrode site [F (1, 60) = 14.432, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.194]. Moving from FZ (M = 487.097 ms, SD = 7.333) to CZ (M = 504.581 ms, SD = 5.629) and PZ (M = 520.645 ms, SD = 4.987), the latencies gradually extended, and the difference between any two electrode sites was statistically significant. The interactions of neuroticism × site [F (1, 60) = 0.199, p = 0.751, ηp² = 0.003] and the main effect of neuroticism [F (1, 60) = 0.235, p = 0.629, ηp² = 0.004] for P3a latencies were not significant.

On post-hoc analysis, the P3b amplitude exhibited a tendency to be increased in the high-neuroticism group relative to the low-neuroticism group (p = 0.084) at PZ (see Fig. 7C); whereas at FZ (p = 0.453) and CZ (p = 0.404), the differences were not statistically significant between the two groups. In the P3b latency results, neither the neuroticism main effect [F (1, 60) = 1.067, p = 0.306, ηp² = 0.017], electrode site main effect [F (1, 60) = 2.300, p = 0.116, ηp² = 0.037], nor neuroticism × site interaction effect [F (1, 60) = 1.478, p = 0.234, ηp² = 0.024] was significant.

Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate whether high neuroticism affects visuospatial selective attention in young adults and to identify the underlying electrophysiological mechanisms using a combined oddball and attention search paradigm. By comparing high- and low-neuroticism groups on behavioral performance and multiple ERP components (N2pc, SPCN, P3a, and P3b), we sought to determine which stages of attentional processing are most vulnerable to the influence of this personality trait.

Neuroticism is associated with increased risk of age-related cognitive decline, including MCI and AD, in older populations. The present findings demonstrate that attentional differences associated with this trait are already detectable in young adults, highlighting the importance of early identification and potential intervention, even though the direct connection to later-life pathology remains to be established through longitudinal studies.

Studies have shown that, among the elderly, an increase in neuroticism scores is closely associated with late-life cognitive decline7,44,45, and that neuroticism alters the transcriptome of the frontal cortex related to Tau protein, thus contributing to the onset of AD46. However, few studies have investigated cognitive dysfunction among young people with high neuroticism, which prompted the design of the present study. In our study, the neuropsychological results of the map search subtest within 1 and 2 min and mental rotation test did not differ between the high- and low-neuroticism groups (p > 0.05), indicating no significant differences in the groups’ spatial relationships judgment and selective attention. These results seemed inconsistent with those of Aschwanden et al. (2020) and Kondracki et al. (2023)47, who found that the high neuroticism among college students was closely linked to an increased likelihood of cognitive failures, such as inattentiveness, forgetfulness, or action accidents, resulting from minor slips or lapses in cognitive control, rather than stable cognitive decline. Barros-Loscertales et al. (2006)48 reasoned that the minor slips or lapses of the high-level neuroticism college students were correlated with a decreased volume and function of the hippocampus, a structure located in the medial temporal lobe that is crucial for attention, memory, and orientation. There is no directly related literature on neuroticism and mental rotation or Map Search of the TEA, and we were the first to investigate them. We argue that the fact that the college students are a relatively young and high-IQ population may partially explain the above controversy. Furthermore, high-neuroticism participants likely experience a slow transition toward a global decline in selective attention or other cognitive functions; thus, neuropsychological tests are not sensitive enough to evaluate and discriminate such individuals.

Of note, the ERP visuospatial attention search task administered in this study served as a highly sensitive method for assessing the impact of trait neuroticism on selective attention. According to the behavioral results, high-neuroticism participants had poorer accuracy than low-neuroticism participants, while reaction times did not differ between the two groups. These results are consistent with previous reports that higher levels of neuroticism predicted decreased change detection accuracy during change detection tasks11 and that high neuroticism scores are associated with reduced overall accuracy in affective Go/No-Go tasks49. This suggests that, compared to low-neuroticismindividuals, those with high neuroticism tend to be less accurate in various visual search tasks50. These findings are consistent with the limited-resource hypothesis, which posits that cognitive processing resources are finite. High-neuroticism individuals have a greater propensity for experiencing task-unrelated thoughts or worries, even in neutral contexts, which may divert attentional resources from the ongoing task and impair performance. This interpretation is supported by the present finding that high-neuroticism participants showed lower accuracy despite similar reaction times, suggesting reduced processing efficiency rather than a simple speed-accuracy trade-off51,52.

The larger amplitudes of N2pc, SPCN, P3a, and P3b observed in high-neuroticism participants in our study may reflect reduced processing efficiency rather than direct emotional processing. According to attentional control theory, high-neuroticism individuals are more susceptible to task-unrelated worries or intrusive thoughts, even in neutral contexts, which can consume attentional resources and necessitate compensatory effort to maintain task performance53. This compensatory effort is reflected in enhanced neural activity across multiple attentional stages: increased N2pc suggests greater investment in target selection, enhanced SPCN indicates heightened visual short-term memory maintenance, larger P3a reflects heightened involuntary attention to novel stimuli, and increased P3b suggests greater difficulty tracking targets. These findings indicate that high neuroticism is associated with broader alterations in attentional control processes, rather than reflecting online processing of negative emotional content per se.

N2pc, a difference wave between the contralateral and ipsilateral electrodes to the attended side of the visual field54, originates when the subject focuses his or her attention during a visual search and identifies target objects in visuospatial cognition tests55. By manipulating different expectancy strategies, which caused participants to redefine the target’s features, Liu et al. (2016)56 found that the N2pc amplitude increased with the enhanced discriminative difficulty of selective attention, indicating that more attentional resources were needed, and N2pc reflected the regulation of top-down (goal-driven) attentional deployment. Hickey et al. (2006)57 reported that, in the circular search arrays comprising a highly salient distractor singleton of a different color and a less salient target singleton with a different form, the distractor-evoked N2pc preceded the target-evoked N2pc, indicating that participants’ attention was first captured by the more salient, but task-irrelevant, distractor before shifting to the target, advocating for the stimulus-driven attentional control theory in the visual search field. Although it remains debatable whether N2pc reflects the goal-driven or stimulus-driven attention control process, it is widely accepted that N2pc is a useful tool for directly assessing the locus or deployment of spatial attention during a visual search58. Of note, no studies have yet reported the relationship between N2pc and the level of neuroticism. We were the first to observe that the N2pc amplitude was significantly increased in high-neuroticism participants (p = 0.043), and that their N2pc latency was normal (p = 0.730), which indicates that, relative to low-neuroticism participants, high-neuroticism participants may have more difficulty suppressing irrelevant distractors or identifying lateralized target stimuli during target selection. Neuroticism involves a propensity for anxiety and negative affect. In support of this view, research on anxiety using paradigms like the additional singleton task has revealed a larger target N2pc amplitude under certain conditions—a finding that aligns with our own N2pc results in high-neuroticism individuals, thereby partially reinforcing our conclusions59. Moreover, since the N2pc amplitude is known to reflect the attention allocated to search tasks60, the greater N2pc amplitude in high-neuroticism participants in the present study demonstrates that there was an increased investment of attentional resources, while processing demands may not have been fully met, which could contribute to the observed decrease in detection accuracy.

The amplitude of the SPCN component, an electrophysiological index of the encoding and maintenance of information in the VSTM61, was also found to be increased in high-neuroticism participants (p = 0.044). Consistent with the present SPCN amplitude data, recent studies reported that participants with psychological stress exhibited a greater SPCN amplitude than the control in a dot-probe task62, and threatening stimuli elicited larger SPCN than neutral stimuli63, suggesting psychological stress or negative emotions enhance information retention in the VSTM. Nevertheless, the present task used neutral colored stimuli, not emotional content. Thus, the larger SPCN in high-neuroticism individuals more likely reflects a trait‑related tendency toward over‑maintenance of any visual information, rather than emotion‑specific processing. This interpretation, while plausible, remains inferential and should be tested in future studies using emotional versus neutral stimuli. The greater amplitude of N2pc and SPCN in our study has confirmed that high-neuroticism participants exhibit increased attention allocation toward visual stimuli processing, prolonged maintenance of visual representations, and subsequent alterations in the in-depth analysis of information within VSTM64.

P3a, elicited by an infrequent distractor (non-target or novel) stimulus inserted randomly into the standard/target sequence, is an index of disengagement of attention and the re-orientation toward novel stimuli65. As suggested by our study, the main effect of neuroticism was statistically significant for P3a amplitude (p = 0.022), and high-neuroticism participants exhibited greater P3a amplitudes than those with low neuroticism, regardless of electrode site. This is consistent with the study of Rossignol et al. (2008)66 in which mixed anxious-depressed subjects showed enhanced visual processing of emotional stimuli and produced a larger P3a than the control group, suggesting that negative emotions have a detrimental effect on involuntary attention. Fjell et al. (2005)67 suggested that, high-neuroticism individuals, owing to their dysfunctional attention control, show a greater tendency to be distracted by novel stimuli, which they interpreted as reflecting difficulties in attentional disengagement. This type of “too much involuntary attention” or a dysfunction of the involuntary attention orientating response may account for the higher P3a amplitude in response to the emergence of novel stimuli. However, the P3a component alone cannot distinguish between enhanced initial capture, slowed disengagement, or both. Given that our task did not directly manipulate disengagement, the interpretation of difficulty in disengagement, though plausible and theoretically grounded, remains an inference rather than a direct measurement. Future studies using spatial cueing or attentional dwell time paradigms are needed to clarify this issue. It is widely accepted that attentional control, one of the executive functions modulated by the frontal lobes, is involved in the process of shifting attention from the current cognitive task to novel stimuli and the production of the P3a component. In the present study, P3a amplitudes and latencies were both significantly different between groups. P3a latencies gradually increased from FZ to CZ, and onto PZ, whereas P3a amplitudes displayed a statistically significant linear decrease, implying that frontal lobe activity was the main contributor to P3a68. Furthermore, though the interaction effect of neuroticism and electrode site was not significant for P3a amplitude, increased frontal lobe activity in the high-neuroticism group was observed in the scalp topography relative to low-neuroticism participants, which could be interpreted as reflecting greater activation of their frontal circuits and potentially indicating a hyper-vigilant state.

P3b is generated by parietotemporal activity associated with sustained attention when the participants are keeping track of targets69. In the present study, the main effect of electrode sites was statistically significant (p < 0.001), with the largest P3b at PZ, followed by CZ and FZ, and the three electrode sites showed a statistically significant linear decrease in amplitude (p < 0.001), partially supporting the findings that the parietal and temporal lobes are the neural loci of P3b generation70. Importantly, a neuroticism × site interaction was found (p = 0.031). Post-hoc analysis revealed that P3b amplitude tended to be higher in the high- than low-neuroticism group (p = 0.084) at PZ, whereas there were no significant differences between the high- and low-neuroticism groups at FZ (p = 0.453) and CZ (p = 0.404). Conceptually similar findings have been reported in other populations with high negative affect (e.g., children with a history of physical maltreatment), who also exhibited larger P3b amplitudes than controls71,72. This further implies that high-neuroticism participants have difficulty keeping track of targets, and P3b amplitude serves as a valuable electrophysiological marker for distinguishing high and low levels of neuroticism.

In brief, the present study demonstrates that high neuroticism is associated with altered visuospatial selective attention in college adults. Specifically, compared to low-neuroticism participants, those with high neuroticism showed lower behavioral accuracy and enhanced amplitudes of N2pc, SPCN, and P3a, along with a trend toward larger P3b amplitude at parietal sites. These ERP findings suggest that trait neuroticism affects multiple stages of attentional processing, including target selection (N2pc), visual short-term memory maintenance (SPCN), involuntary orienting to novel stimuli (P3a), and sustained target tracking (P3b). Therefore, the observed ERP components may serve as candidate neural markers for future research on attentional differences associated with neuroticism.

Limitations

While this study advances our understanding of how trait neuroticism influences visuospatial attention in young adults, several limitations merit consideration. Firstly, the exclusive use of an attentional search paradigm constrains the generalizability of our findings, as other well-established approaches (e.g., Posner cueing, flanker tasks, or multiple object tracking) may yield different mechanistic insights. Secondly, the restriction of our sample to East Asian college students limits population-level inferences due to potential cultural, educational, and geographic confounds. Thirdly, our focus on young adults overlooks known age-related attentional dynamics; future investigations should incorporate lifespan perspectives, including older adults and clinical cohorts (e.g., individuals with mild cognitive impairment or alzheimer’s disease), to enhance clinical translatability. Fourthly, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about causality or temporal progression of the observed attentional differences. Fifthly, ERP components are indirect measures of neural activity; interpretations linking amplitude differences to specific cognitive processes are inferential rather than direct. Finally, conclusions based on component-level analyses (N2pc, SPCN, P3a, P3b) should be interpreted with caution, as each component may reflect multiple overlapping cognitive operations.
