Six-year performance of risk-based selection for lung cancer screening in the Manchester Lung Health Check cohort
Authors: Patrick Goodley, Haval Balata, Hilary A Robbins, Richard Booton, Matthew Sperrin, Philip A J Crosbie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveRisk prediction models are used to determine eligibility for targeted lung cancer screening. However, prospective data regarding model performance in this setting are limited. Here we report the performance of the PLCOm2012 risk model, which calculates 6 year lung cancer risk, in a cohort invited for lung cancer screening in a socioeconomically deprived area.Methods and analysisCalibration (expected/observed (E/O) lung cancer diagnoses over 6 years) and discrimination (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve) of PLCOm2012 and other models was performed in Manchester Lung Health Check (M-LHC) participants, where PLCOm2012 ≥1.51% was used prospectively to determine screening eligibility. Lung cancers diagnosed by any route were captured within 6 years of risk assessment, for both screened and non-screened participants. Performance of a range of models was evaluated.ResultsOut of 2541 attendees, 56% were high-risk (n=1430/2541) and offered screening; 44% were low-risk (n=1111/2541) and not screened. Over 6 years, 7.3% (n=105/1430) and 0.9% (n=10/1111) were diagnosed with lung cancer in the high and low-risk cohorts, respectively (p<0.0001). Risk was underestimated in both high-risk, screened (E/O 0.68 (0.57–0.82)) and low-risk, unscreened groups (E/O 0.61 (0.33–1.14)). Most other models also underestimated risk.ConclusionRisk-based eligibility using PLCOm2012 successfully classified most eventual lung cancer cases in the high-risk, screened group. Prediction models generally underestimated risk in this socioeconomically deprived cohort, irrespective of screening status. The effect of screening on increasing the probability of lung cancer diagnosis should be considered when interpreting measures of prediction model performance.Keywords: Lung cancer (non-small cell), Cancer screening, Epidemiology

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

The Manchester Lung Health Check pilot programme applied the PLCOm2012 risk model to determine eligibility for screening in a socioeconomically deprived cohort. Follow-up of both the screened and unscreened groups demonstrated that PLCOm2012 stratified high risk participants into a high-risk group for screening, as few lung cancers arose in the low-risk, unscreened group. Risk models generally underestimated risk, due to a combination of population risk and the impact of the screening intervention.

Introduction

Low dose CT (LDCT) screening reduces lung cancer mortality.1 2 Evidence suggests the use of risk prediction models to calculate a person’s individual risk, and thereby determine screening eligibility, may be a more effective approach than categorical criteria as recommended in US Preventive Services Task Force guidelines (USPSTF).3,5 In England’s Targeted Lung Health Check (TLHC) programme two models are used: PLCOm2012 at a threshold of ≥1.51% 6 year risk, and LLPv2 at a threshold of ≥2.5% 5 year risk.6,8 However, despite clinical implementation, models have been developed and mostly validated in study cohorts that were not offered LDCT screening.49,11 Most data on model performance in screened cohorts come from modelling studies and secondary analysis of clinical trial data.3 6 The LLPv2 model was used in the UK Lung Screening (UKLS) trial and the PanCan lung cancer risk prediction model, a precursor to PLCOm2012, was used in the single-arm PanCan cohort study.12 13 Early data from the International Lung Screening Trial (comparing PLCOm2012 ≥1.51% to USPSTF2013 criteria) and the German HANSE trial (comparing PLCOm2012 ≥1.58% to NELSON criteria) support the hypothesis that the PLCOm2012 risk model outperforms categorical eligibility criteria in terms of sensitivity and predictive value, although discrimination and calibration measures have not yet been reported.14 15 A number of uncertainties about model performance remain including how to select the optimal model and screening threshold for a given population. As screening implementation expands, it is important to validate the models in real-world clinical programmes to enable selection of the best-performing model and optimal thresholds.

The Manchester Lung Health Check (M-LHC) pilot was based in areas of high socio-economic deprivation and offered annual LDCT screening to individuals at high risk. In this study, both high risk, screened (PLCOm2012 6-year ≥1.51%) and low risk, unscreened (PLCOm2012<1.51%) M-LHC participants were followed systematically for lung cancer occurrence. Here, we describe lung cancer incidence over 6 years, the full prediction period for the PLCOm2012 model, following initiation of the M-LHC in the screened and unscreened groups. We assess the predictive performance of the PLCOm2012 model, which was prospectively applied to define eligibility for screening, and compare its performance to other published models.

Materials and methods

Manchester Lung Health Check (M-LHC)

The M-LHC was a pilot of community-based targeted lung cancer screening for ever-smokers aged 55–74 invited by letter from 14 primary care practices in socioeconomically deprived parts of Manchester, commencing in 2016. Free Lung Health Checks were offered on mobile units, during which lung cancer risk assessments and LDCT screening were performed.16 17 Attendees with Prostate, Lung PLCOm2012 risk≥1.51% were offered two rounds of annual LDCT screening, while attendees with PLCOm2012 risk<1.51% were not screened. All participants were allocated to the referent category of the ethnicity predictor in PLCOm2012 (‘white’) due to uncertainty about the transportability of ethnicity-based risk associations from the North American populations in which the model was developed. Lung cancer cases arising among attendees (high risk, screened and low risk, unscreened participants) during the 6 years following the baseline round were identified by lung cancer multidisciplinary team records, as documented in the Cancer Outcomes Services Data set submission data from the hospitals serving the areas invited to screening, up to October 2022. Case note review was performed to confirm diagnoses, stages, and routes of detection. The primary outcome was lung cancer diagnosis. Subsequent metachronous cancers were not included. Following the second round, participants with indeterminate nodules were referred to existing nodule surveillance pathways. Some areas invited to the M-LHC pilot were also invited to the subsequent North and East Manchester LHC (NEM-LHC) screening programme, which commenced in 2019.18 Cancers detected through surveillance or the NEM-LHC programme were classified as screening cancers, distinct from non-screening cancers that were diagnosed following symptomatic or incidental presentation.

Statistical analysis

All individuals attending a Lung Health Check (LHC) appointment were stratified according to PLCOm2012 score into either a high-risk (PLCOm2012 score ≥1.51%, those offered screening) or low-risk cohort (PLCOm2012 score<1.51%, not offered screening). Welch two sample t-test was used to compare continuous variables with visually symmetric distribution between categories of interest (eg, high-risk vs low-risk cohorts, screening vs non-screening cancer cases), Wilcoxon rank sum test was used for continuous non-symmetric variables, Pearson’s χ2 test for categorical variables from independent samples, and McNemar’s test for comparisons within the same sample. Statistical analysis was performed using R V.4.2. Predictor and outcome data were complete for the cohort, assuming full ascertainment of lung cancer cases.

The TRIPOD Checklist for Prediction Model Validation was followed.19 The PLCOm2012 model was evaluated in terms of discrimination (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC)), calibration (ratio of expected to observed cases (E/O), calibration curve) and decision curve analysis. This analysis was performed separately for the high risk (screened) and the low risk (unscreened) groups to avoid bias, as screening was expected to increase the likelihood of cancer diagnosis in the high-risk group. The PLCOm2012 model was recalibrated based on the full M-LHC cohort to explore whether this could have improved risk stratification. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV) and number-needed-to-screen to detect one lung cancer (NNS) were calculated for eligibility thresholds used in other studies and clinical programmes, assuming that the same cancers would have arisen despite changes in who was screened. The sample size was determined by the number of M-LHC participants consenting to the study.

Other risk scores were calculated retrospectively, using the lcmodels R package and published parameters.20 These were also externally validated: LCRAT, LCRAT with added probability of lung cancer diagnosis in the presence of an NLST-style screening protocol (‘LCRAT with screening’, based on 12.4% higher incidence in CT screened arm of NLST), LLPv2, LLPv3, LCDRAT, Pittsburgh, Bach, UCL-I, UCL-D.1221,24 Most models predict the risk of lung cancer diagnosis in the absence of screening, with several exceptions: ‘LCRAT with screening’ predicts lung cancer diagnosis in the presence of screening, and LCDRAT and UCL-D predict lung cancer death. Cause of death determinations were unavailable, so deaths among cancer cases who had evidence of active lung cancer prior to death on review of clinical and radiological records were classified as lung cancer deaths for the predicted outcome in LCDRAT and UCL-D. Exploratory analysis compared the predictive performance of PLCOm2012 and LLPv2 at currently used risk thresholds and categorical eligibility criteria used in the US (USPSTF2013: age 55–80, 30 pack-year smoking history, smoked within the last 15 years; USPSTF2021: age 50–80, 20 pack-years, smoking within last 15 years), assuming that the same cancers would have been detected using any of these approaches.5 8 25 Since the M-LHC age range was 55–74, comparisons between USPSTF2013 and USPSTF2021 were not affected by their different starting ages.

Patient and public involvement

People affected by lung cancer were integral to the design of the M-LHC pilot, as part of the MacMillan Cancer Improvement Partnership, and focus groups were supportive of the role of research to lead to improvements in lung cancer detection and outcomes.

Results

Study population

2541 people completed an LHC and consented to inclusion in the analysis (see table 1). The mean age was 64.1±5.5 years, 51% were female and 57% were in the most deprived socioeconomic decile in England according to index of multiple deprivation; 56% of participants (n=1430/2541) were offered screening due to ‘high-risk’ (defined as PLCOm2012 score ≥1.51%) and 44% (n=1111/2541) were ‘low-risk’ and therefore not eligible for screening; 98.6% of the high-risk cohort (n=1410/1430) underwent at least one screening LDCT scan (n=1384 at baseline (T0) and n=1194 in the second round (T1)) as previously reported.16 17 From 2019, 25% (n=348/1410) of high-risk M-LHC pilot participants had at least one additional screening scan in the separately implemented NEM-LHC programme. Lung cancers

Cumulative lung cancer incidence at 6 years was 4.5% of all M-LHC attendees (n=115/2541), of which 77% (n=88) were detected via screening (figure 1 and table 2). Most lung cancers occurred in the high-risk, screened cohort (91%, n=105/115). These included 61 diagnosed during the M-LHC pilot (42 at baseline, 19 in the second round) and 54 diagnosed after; of these approximately half (n=26/54) were detected as a consequence of screening (n=12 following nodule surveillance and n=14 through NEM-LHC screening) and the remainder through usual care (n=28/54). 76% of lung cancers were diagnosed at an early stage (I–II, n=87/115); the proportion was significantly higher for screen detected cancers versus those identified in usual care (82% (n=72/88) vs 56% (n=15/27), p=0.005). Late-stage (III–IV) cancer was diagnosed in 1.1% of participants (n=28/2541), predominantly in the high-risk, screened group (1.7%, n=25/1430).

Calibration

PLCOm2012 predicted that 5.0% of the high-risk and 0.55% of the low-risk group would be diagnosed with lung cancer in 6 years (in the absence of screening). Observed 6 year incidence was 7.3% in high-risk, screened participants (n=105/1430, 95% CI 6.0 to 8.8%) and 0.90% in low-risk, unscreened participants (n=10/1111, 95% CI 0.43 to 1.65%) as shown in figure 1. Thus, PLCOm2012 underestimated risk by approximately one-third overall (E/O 0.68 (95% CI 0.57 to 0.81)), and the underestimation was apparent in both high-risk, screened (E/O 0.68 (0.57–0.82)) and low-risk, unscreened groups (E/O 0.61 (0.33–1.14)). The ‘LCRAT with screening’ model also underestimated risk in the screened group (E/O 0.77 (0.63–0.93)). Other models (which predict risk in the absence of screening) also underestimated lung cancer risk, except LLPv2, with E/O 1.02 (0.84–1.24) in the screened group and overestimation in the unscreened group (E/O 2.48 (1.29–4.75)). The number of lung cancer deaths was similar to that predicted by LCDRAT in the screened group (E/O 1.07 (0.78–1.47)). Calibration curves are shown in online supplemental figure S2. On recalibration of PLCOm2012 based on the full cohort (screened and unscreened), the E/O ratio improved to 0.95 (0.79–1.14) in the high-risk group (threshold ≥1.51%), while overestimation (E/O 1.53, (0.83–2.84)) occurred in the in unscreened group. This reclassified 43% of low-risk participants (n=481/1111) as high-risk, including 6 of the 10 cancers actually observed in the unscreened group.

Discrimination

PLCOm2012 discrimination was good in the overall cohort, with AUC 0.78 (95% CI 0.74 to 0.82, online supplemental table S2), and when stratified by risk group (screened AUC 0.68 (0.63–0.73), unscreened AUC 0.60 (0.42–0.78)). Other lung cancer incidence models’ AUCs ranged 0.61–0.69 in the screened group. LCDRAT showed the strongest discrimination, for the different outcome of lung cancer death, among screenees (AUC 0.76 (0.68–0.84)).

Eligibility thresholds

The sensitivity of a 1.51% PLCOm2012 screening threshold was 0.91, with specificity 0.45 (see table 4). Decision curve analysis is shown in online supplemental figure S3. At the 1.51% threshold, there was a net benefit of 3.3% (95% CI 2.6 to 4.1%) over screening no one, that is, targeted screening using this eligibility threshold would lead to approximately three more cancers being detected per 100 people compared with no screening being undertaken. This was similar to screening everyone (net benefit 3.1% (2.3–4.0%)). The median PLCOm2012 score for patients diagnosed with cancer in the low-risk, unscreened group was 0.7% (IQR 0.4%–0.9%). Two cases had a PLCOm2012 score between 1.00% and 1.51% (scores 1.13% and 1.42%). Using a lower eligibility threshold of 1.3%, as suggested by US National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidance,26 would have classified 80 additional participants into the high-risk group and potentially yielded one additional cancer, assuming that the same cancers would have been diagnosed under different screening eligibility criteria. Had LLPv2 been used with a 2.5% threshold, an estimated 85% of cancers (n=98/115) would have arisen among screen-eligible attendees, compared with the 91% (n=105/115) identified using PLCOm2012≥1.51%. Had eligibility been determined using either criterion, as in England’s TLHC programme, 19% more attendees would be screen-eligible (n=1705 vs 1430 using PLCOm2012), with potentially two more cancers detected. If an LLPv2 threshold of 5% were used, as in the Yorkshire Lung Screening Trial,27 63% of cancers (n=73/115) were estimated to be detected in the resulting screen-eligible group. Using USPSTF2021 categorical criteria, a similar number of participants in the 55–74y age range could have been eligible for screening (n=1440 vs 1430 using PLCOm2012), but with fewer eventual cancers observed in this group (n=98 vs 105, McNemar test p=0.07).

Discussion

Main findings

This community-based lung cancer screening pilot, performed in a high-risk population, offered risk-based targeted LDCT screening using the PLCOm2012 risk model at threshold ≥1.51%. Over half (56%) of the cohort was high-risk and underwent LDCT screening. Overall, 44% more lung cancers were diagnosed than had been expected using PLCOm2012, which predicts 6 year lung cancer risk in the absence of screening. Risk underestimation was seen in both the high-risk, screened and low-risk, unscreened groups, although cancer cases in the unscreened group were not clustered just below the eligibility threshold. Approximately half of diagnoses (53%, n=61/115) were made during the pilot’s two screening rounds. Most lung cancers occurred in the high-risk, screened group (91%, n=105/115), and most of these were screen-detected. Three quarters of lung cancers in the high-risk cohort were early stage (76% stage I-II), in contrast to the national stage distribution prior to implementation of screening where a minority are diagnosed at early stage (31% stage I–II).28 Most risk prediction models analysed here underestimated risk (except LLPv2) with discrimination in the screened group ranging AUC 0.61–0.79. Modelling of USPSTF categorical eligibility criteria in this cohort suggested that fewer cancer cases may have been captured in the high-risk screened group.

Relation to existing literature

There is limited evidence relating to lung cancer risk model performance in real-world clinical practice where they have been applied to determine screening eligibility. Our results differ from an analysis in unscreened UK research cohorts, which found models overestimated risk.9 For instance, in the same 55–74 age range in the UK Biobank cohort, PLCOm2012 overestimated risk by a quarter (E/O 1.26 (1.19–1.33), in contrast to E/O 0.68 (0.57–0.82) in M-LHC). This may be due to higher cancer incidence in the M-LHC cohort as a consequence of screening. This phenomenon has been demonstrated in randomised trials, where CT screening arms yielded 12.8% (NLST) and 13.6% (NELSON) higher lung cancer incidence than control groups after median 6.5 and 10 years of follow-up, respectively.1 2 Additionally, our real-world cohort has much greater socioeconomic deprivation than clinical trial populations.29 Previous exploration by Robbins et al of the 2.6-fold higher M-LHC cancer detection in the initial screening rounds compared with NLST found that differences in risk score distribution and eligibility criteria partly account for the difference. However, there were 60% additional cases in M-LHC after accounting for these. Stratified analysis suggested older age, obstructive lung disease and living in areas of socioeconomic disadvantage may further explain the difference.30 Indeed, most risk models have been developed in research cohorts susceptible to healthy volunteer bias.31 32 Conversely, our findings may not generalise to more affluent screened populations with lower cancer risk profiles.

The PanCan cohort study prospectively used the PanCan model for risk-based eligibility, and retrospectively validated the subsequent PLCOm2012 model, in a high risk screened cohort (6 year lung cancer risk≥2.0%).13 PLCOm2012 calibration was similar (E/O 0.72 in PanCan vs 0.68 in this study’s screened group) but discrimination weaker (AUC 0.61 vs 0.68).

The UKLS trial, which prospectively evaluated the LLPv2 model at eligibility threshold≥4.5% 5 year risk, subsequently reported results of its single-screen design.12 Model validation was performed based on national registry lung cancer cases among all respondents who completed a risk questionnaire (n=75 958). LLPv2 overestimated risk 2.4-fold, and the recalibrated LLPv3 model overestimated by 1.3-fold. However, only 2.6% of included respondents (n=1987) ended up in the LDCT intervention group, so the reported model performance mostly reflects cancer risk in the absence of screening. The LLPv2 model’s overestimation in the UKLS context may have resulted in better calibration in the high-risk M-LHC group, in the presence of screening, alongside overestimation in the low risk, unscreened M-LHC group. Depending on the eligibility threshold used, overestimation risks drawing low-risk participants into screening which could increase the rate of false positives.

The Alberta Lung Cancer Screening Study (n=1737) offered three annual LDCT screens to participants based on a PLCOm2012≥1.5% threshold. It also reported cancer detection in the low risk, unscreened group. After an average of 6 years’ follow-up, only one cancer occurred in 857 low risk individuals (detection rate 0.12% among those with PLCOm2012<1.5%), compared with 30 cancers in 805 high-risk screenees (3.7%).33 In M-LHC, cancer cases were not clustered in those with risk scores just under 1.51% (median 0.7%, IQR 0.4%–0.9%), so lowering the threshold would have substantially increased the number of screens performed but not captured many more cancers. Although sample sizes are limited, similarly low incidence of cancer in Alberta and Manchester’s low-risk groups provides reassurance that using risk-based eligibility at this threshold does not exclude substantial numbers of eventual cancer cases.

Considerations for risk prediction

All risk models in current use predict lung cancer diagnosis in the absence of screening. It is important to consider this when evaluating their performance in screened populations, as cancer detection is expected to be higher because of the screening intervention. Indeed, for a model perfectly calibrated in an unscreened population, underestimation of lung cancer incidence (E/O<1) is an expected feature of a successful screening programme. Discrimination measures for incidence in the whole cohort will also be exaggerated as screening enriches higher risk groups with additional detected cancers, with the lower risk group retaining low incidence. Meanwhile, overestimation of lung cancer deaths (E/O>1) could be expected if lung cancer mortality were reduced, with accompanying reduction of AUC for the whole cohort.

There is currently no consensus on how to validate risk models in targeted lung cancer screening, although tackling this will be important to enable model adjustment and the optimisation of selection criteria for different settings. Given the intended effect of targeted screening to detect more cancers, performance should be evaluated separately in screened and unscreened cohorts. It should also be noted that splitting a cohort by risk necessarily diminishes the apparent discrimination of the model, so AUC values will not be comparable to unscreened cohorts. This was seen in this study, where the AUC of PLCOm2012 dropped from 0.78 across the whole cohort to 0.68 in the high risk, screened group and 0.60 in the low risk, unscreened group. Therefore, interpretation of AUCs must be noted as depending on the risk distribution of the cohort being studied, rather than being an independent measure of the risk model. In practice, model performance to classify participants to either side of the eligibility threshold is of primary importance. Recalibrating models according to the risk profiles of different target populations is technically simple and could be based on cancer registry data. An alternative approach would be to change the eligibility threshold in each target population, although we would not recommend this as a miscalibrated model can easily be misinterpreted by users.

Exploratory recalibration of the PLCOm2012 model in the full cohort could have drawn 6 of 10 cancer cases observed in the low-risk, ineligible group above a 1.51% eligibility threshold. However, this would come at a cost of screening almost half of the unscreened cohort. This may be counterproductive in terms of cost-effectiveness and added exposure to harm from screening. It should be noted that, if such a recalibrated model had been applied in practice, which increased predicted risk scores and screened many more participants, there would have likely been more cancers observed because more individuals underwent screening. Basing recalibration on cancer registry data from an unscreened population could avoid such an increase in risk resulting from the screening effect.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is its evaluation of lung cancer detection in a real-world screening programme that prospectively applied risk-based eligibility, further supporting this approach. It studies the type of high-risk population that is being prioritised by the national TLHC service. Another strength is the reporting of outcomes for the low-risk, unscreened group, which has not been included in most other screening studies. A limitation is that the M-LHC pilot comprised two screening rounds; had it been continuous over the full 6 year follow-up period, more lung cancers may have been detected in the high-risk group. The single-arm cohort design means that there is no unscreened high-risk group for comparison. The cohort comprised M-LHC attendees but did not include other invitees who did not participate (26% participated, n=2541 of 9926 invited ever-smokers), introducing the possibility of selection bias. Regional lung cancer data may have missed cases in participants who moved out of the region, so the cancers reported represent a minimum number. It should be emphasised that only PLCOm2012 was used to determine screening eligibility in this programme, so comparisons with the performance of other models may be biased by this (eg, the discrimination of PLCOm2012 may be inflated for the overall cohort). If screening thresholds were changed in practice, the provision of screening to different participants would likely alter cancer detection in those who changed from being ineligible to eligible, or vice versa. Differences in time horizons used by the models should be noted when interpreting the AUCs, which means they are not all directly comparable. Additionally, it should be noted that the performance measures used here are population-dependent. For example, model performance could change if a different age range was invited or disease prevalence differed. The 55–74 age range is also currently the invited age range in the UK, but transportability of these measures to other settings may be affected by population differences.

Conclusion

In this targeted lung cancer screening programme, risk-based eligibility using PLCOm2012 threshold ≥1.51% performed well to classify participants into high risk and low risk groups. Most participants who developed lung cancer had been identified as high risk at baseline and underwent LDCT screening, with no suggestion that a lowered eligibility threshold would have feasibly included more eventual cancer cases. However, most models, including PLCOm2012, underestimated risk. This is likely due to inherent elevated risk in the population targeted, and the screening intervention itself leading to increased probability of lung cancer diagnosis. Prior to changes in practice, any anticipated benefit must be considered alongside the risk of additional harm in the target population. Caution should be exercised to avoid disadvantaging any high-risk subpopulations by underestimating their lung cancer risk, either in terms of eligibility for screening or healthcare resource allocation. Further studies directly comparing risk model performance will be valuable to inform practice. Model recalibration in larger cohorts may be required to improve risk prediction in the context of targeted lung cancer screening and inform risk thresholds for screening eligibility.
