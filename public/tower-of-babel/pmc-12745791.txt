Toward a general understanding of thermal performance curves in biology
Authors: Samraat Pawar, Dimitrios-Georgios Kontopoulos
Published: 2025

Temperature profoundly influences biological processes at all scales, from enzyme kinetics to ecosystem-level metabolism. Despite the enormous physiological diversity of life on Earth—from unicellular microbes to plants and animals—there is a remarkable similarity in how individual-level traits (e.g., metabolic rate, locomotion, growth rate) respond to temperature (1–3). These relationships are captured by thermal performance curves (TPCs), which describe how the rate or magnitude of a biological trait varies continuously with temperature (2, 4). TPCs are typically unimodal and left-skewed (Fig. 1), rising in an Arrhenius-like (that is, exponentially) manner with temperature up to an optimum and then declining steeply beyond a critical upper threshold (5, 6). Numerous mathematical models for TPCs have been proposed over the last century [indeed, the first unimodal TPC model, to the best of our knowledge, was proposed exactly 100 y ago! (4)]. Many of these are mechanistic because they incorporate constraints from cellular biochemical kinetics and thermodynamics, including enzyme denaturation, membrane stability, and resource supply limitations. While these models all reproduce the characteristic asymmetric TPC shape, they differ in which specific constraints they invoke. As a result, there has long been debate about the principles governing the shapes of TPCs and whether there are unifying constraints across species and traits (4, 8–14). Resolving this debate is a major challenge because the mechanistic underpinnings and sources of variation in TPC shape are crucial for a general understanding of organismal thermal physiology. It is also an urgent one because we need to be able to more accurately predict how biological systems cope with natural and anthropogenic climate variation (12, 15, 16).

In their new study, Arnoldi et al. (7) tackle the challenge from a new angle—asking whether a single, general mathematical model can explain why thermal performance curves (TPCs) across organisms and traits all share a broadly similar asymmetric shape. Motivated by the concept of model sloppiness; where complex models are highly sensitive to only a few key parameter combinations but largely insensitive to the rest (17). They reason that the multitude of mechanistic TPC models may simply represent rescaled expressions of the same underlying relationship between temperature and performance. Starting from the minimal assumption that a trait’s rate increases exponentially with temperature (Arrhenius-like) up to an optimum and then declines sharply, they show analytically that any smooth unimodal TPC converges to a single universal shape—a global attractor—when appropriately scaled. In its first-order form, this “Universal Thermal Performance Curve” (UTPC) is expressed asy(x)=ex(1−x),

where x=(T−Topt)/(Tc−Topt). This formulation captures the essential geometry of TPCs with only two degrees of freedom—Topt and the thermal range E=Tc−Topt—with all other mechanistic complexities effectively absorbed into these parameters. Crucially, the UTPC predicts consistent trade-offs among TPC parameters: For example, a steeper rise on the cold side necessarily entails a higher Topt and/or a narrower thermal breadth on the warm side, reflecting the empirical pattern that warm-adapted species tend to operate within narrower temperature ranges.

Arnoldi et al. then test the UTPC model using a massive dataset of thousands of published TPCs, including 2,710 curves spanning prokaryotes, plants, insects, and vertebrates. Remarkably, when each curve is shifted to its own Topt and scaled by its thermal range and peak, all data collapse onto the UTPC. The collapse is not perfect—there is some scatter—but the universal curve fits the normalized trends remarkably well. This striking convergence supports the idea that a simple exponential rise below the thermal optimum, combined with correlations among Topt, thermal breadth, and the steepness at the rise, funnels evolution of TPCs into a common shape. This simple mathematical explanation for an empirically ubiquitous pattern is a significant advance; It explains why independent models and data yield similar left-skewed curves by a small set of universal constraints (including trade-offs).

For applied science, the UTPC offers practical benefits. This means that if we can estimate just two parameters (Topt and Tc) from an organism’s empirically measured TPC, we have a strong prior for its entire temperature response. This is useful for parameterizing population-to-ecosystem models under climate change, where data are often sparse. Also, because Arnoldi et al. demonstrate the collapse across a vast range of taxa, researchers can use the UTPC as a baseline for comparative studies. For example, community ecologists could ask whether climate change is pushing species off the UTPC in systematic ways, and evolutionary physiologists could look for outliers whose TPCs reflect unique constraints.

Despite its elegance, the UTPC is not a one-size-fits-all solution, and should be viewed as a null model with important limitations. First, it assumes that the ascending limb of the TPC follows a single Arrhenius process. In some systems, performance rises in more complex ways (for example, linear increases at low temperatures, or multiple phases of increase below Topt as in the six-parameter Sharpe-Schoolfield model) (4, 18). If the actual rise-phase deviates strongly from a simple exponential, the UTPC fit may be poor (Fig. 1C). Second, the UTPC relies on a rapid decline beyond Topt. If the performance drop is gradual or multistepped, the shape will differ. Arnoldi et al. specifically note that a purely symmetric Gaussian TPC shape never converges to the UTPC (Fig. 1D). Thus, any trait with a right-skewed tail or secondary inflections will deviate from the universal curve. Third, by construction, y(x)=ex(1−x) reaches zero at x=1 and never goes below. This means that the UTPC cannot directly model situations in which the raw performance becomes negative (e.g., net population growth rates crossing below zero or an organism’s net energetics). Arnoldi et al. note that the solution is to add a constant offset to make all values positive before fitting. While this trick works; it emphasizes that the UTPC is about the shape of the TPC, not absolute values or baselines. Fourth, because the UTPC has only two parameters, it ignores other factors that might influence TPC shape. In reality, TPCs can shift via changes in enzyme efficiency, protein stability, or membrane composition that may indirectly constrain Topt. For example, the Johnson-Lewin model shows that Topt can depend on three underlying parameters (19, 20). Populations might adapt by modifying those additional parameters in complex ways, creating systematic deviations from the UTPC. Fifth, a TPC parameter of a given trait (e.g., Topt of development rate) may correlate with a TPC parameter of a different trait (e.g., Topt of fecundity rate) (21) or with other traits (e.g., body size). Such correlations would also result in systematic departures from the UTPC, depending on the trait’s level of organization (2).

Nevertheless, the UTPC’s true value lies in revealing and interpreting such deviations from its predicted shape and their mechanistic causes. Are there species or traits that genuinely escape the UTPC? Do acclimation or evolution shift organisms along its parameters (e.g., Topt), or alter the curve shape entirely? Like other biological null models, the UTPC compels us to ask: How much of the observed variation reflects fundamental physical constraints, and how much arises from biological complexity? In doing so, it advances thermal biology toward general principles, through a baseline, akin to those established by the neutral theories of population genetics (22) and biodiversity (23), and the more mechanistic metabolic theory of ecology (15, 24). By formalizing a null expectation, the UTPC helps researchers identify meaningful and biologically informative departures.

Finally, a key issue is how the apparent collapse of TPCs onto the UTPC—and what we can infer from it—is influenced by data quality and trait resolution. For instance, can Tc and Topt (and thus their difference) be uniquely estimated when data are noisy or when the postoptimum decline is highly nonlinear? This challenge is amplified when analyzing traits at higher levels of biological organization (e.g., population growth or ecosystem flux), where the aggregation of multiple underlying processes can obscure the simplicity the UTPC aims to capture. To reliably extract UTPC parameters and diagnose meaningful deviations, we may need targeted, high-resolution measurements of TPCs across organizational levels within the same system. Developing a systematic framework for such analyses—one that accounts for scale and mechanistic layering—offers a promising direction for future work. For now, the UTPC offers an elegant and broadly applicable null model: Most temperature responses fall on this simple curve, and where they do not, new biological understanding awaits.
