Spectacles with non‑refractive opaque features for paediatric myopia: a safety pilot trial
Authors: Ravi C. Bakaraju, Jennie Diec, Daniel Tilia, Klaus Ehrmann, Ranjay Chakraborty
Published: 2026

Introduction

Optical myopia-control strategies predominantly introduce competing defocus, extend depth of focus, or modify retinal contrast [1]. A single-vision spectacle lens utilising non-refractive, light-absorbing opaque features to alter spatiotemporal retinal luminance during natural eye movement without defocus, blur, or scatter was developed [2]. Visual performance in adults was superior to a competing defocus design [3]. In-vivo myopia efficacy is unknown, and experimental evidence that partial occlusion could potentiate myopiagenesis [4], underpinning the need for paediatric safety data. A contact-lens counterpart demonstrated safety in a small pilot [5]; however, spectacle delivery is preferable for younger children. This 14-month, three-phase, within-participant crossover pilot assesses safety (primary) and axial elongation (secondary) in myopic children.

Methods

The study was conducted at five Australian sites with ethics approval and trial registration. Myopic children aged 7–15 years (<-0.50D) were enrolled; exclusion criteria included ocular pathology, binocular vision anomalies, and prior myopia-control treatment (Supplementary Figure 1). Test spectacles incorporated optical films bearing non-refractive opaque features ( ~ 950 features, 0.03 ×1.5 mm, across ~50 mm diameter) adhered to single-vision lenses matched in vertex power; control spectacles were identical without films. Phase 1 (0-6 months): control; Phase 2 (6–12 months): A.R.R.E.S.T. films; Phase 3 (12–14 months): control. Visits were 2–monthly.

The primary variable was non-cycloplegic axial length by non-contact optical biometry; cycloplegic data were not collected. Safety assessments included slit-lamp examination as required, high-contrast visual acuity (HCVA), and participant-reported comfort. Analysis was intention-to-treat (ITT). Linear mixed models with random intercepts for participant and eye nested within participant, adjusting for baseline age and axial length. Two participants (P04, P11) discontinued before Phase 2 completion; missing data were handled under a missing-at-random assumption. Phase was the fixed factor; growth slopes and matched 2-month increments were secondary outcomes.

Results

Twenty-three children were enrolled, 22 analysed (15 females; 10.7 ± 2.2 years of age; baseline axial length: 23.59 ± 0.61 mm; Table 1). No adverse events occurred; HCVA at dispensing was within 1 line of best-corrected acuity. Six-month axial elongation was significantly lower in Phase 2 than Phase 1: estimated marginal mean 0.057 mm (95% CI 0.013 to 0.102) versus 0.102 mm (0.058 to 0.146); difference −0.045 mm ( − 0.066 to −0.024), p < 0.001. Growth slopes declined from 0.017 to 0.011 mm/month (Δ of −0.006 mm/month, p < 0.001). Group-level axial-length trajectories are shown in Fig. 1. Matched 2-month increments were 0.027 mm (Phase 1), 0.015 mm (Phase 2), and 0.026 mm (Phase 3). Discussion and conclusion

Axial elongation was lower in the test phase; post-cessation growth returned to baseline without rebound. As control always preceded test, the −0.045 mm difference carries expected contributions from age-related deceleration and seasonal variation. The absence of a concurrent randomised control group limits causal inference. Refractive measurements were non-cycloplegic; cycloplegic confirmation is warranted in future trials. This approach differs from defocus- or scatter-based lenses by preserving single-vision correction and targeting temporal contrast modulation.

Together with the published contact-lens pilot study [5], these data provide a dual-modality safety foundation for single vision lenses featuring opaque non-refractive features. Wear-time adherence was heterogeneous (Table 1), precluding dose-response evaluation. The directional efficacy signals warrant deeper investigation in a properly powered randomised controlled trial, now underway.

Recent reports show that some defocus-based spectacle designs produce retinal shadows [6], raising the hypothesis that spatiotemporal contrast modulation may contribute to therapeutic effects via mechanisms analogous to the proposed temporal contrast signal; testing this requires dedicated retinal imaging and electrophysiological studies.
