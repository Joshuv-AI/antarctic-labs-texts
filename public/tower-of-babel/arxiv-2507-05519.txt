Modeling Deontic Modal Logic in the s(CASP) Goal-directed Predicate Answer Set Programming System
Gopal Gupta, Abhiramon Rajasekharan Alexis R. Tudor, Elmer Salazar, The University of Texas at Dallas, Richardson, TX, USA, Joaquín Arias, CETINIA, Universidad Rey Juan Carlos, Madrid, Spain

Abstract
We consider the problem of implementing deontic modal logic. We show how (deontic) modal operators can be expressed elegantly using default negation (negation-as-failure) and strong negation present in answer set programming (ASP). We propose using global constraints of ASP to represent obligations and impermissibilities of deontic modal logic. We show that our proposed representation results in the various paradoxes of deontic modal logic being elegantly resolved.

Introduction

Modal logics extend classical logic by introducing modal operators like “it is necessary that” and “it is possible that” to reason about possibility, necessity, and related modalities. It is used to formally model statements about what could, must, or ought to be true across possible worlds or circumstances.
Deontic modal logic is a modal logic that formalizes reasoning about normative concepts such as obligation (OB), permission (PE), and impermissibility (IM). It uses modal operators to express statements like “It ought to be the case that A” or “It is permitted that B”. Deontic logic helps model ethical, legal, and normative systems by capturing how actions are classified as obligatory, permissible, or forbidden.

Modal logics, including deontic logic, have been heavily studied for decades. In this paper, we focus on showing how modal logics can be represented in answer set programming (ASP), a formalism for knowledge representation and reasoning

. The negation operator used in modal logics can be divided into two categories: negation appearing next to a proposition and that appearing next to a modal operator. The former is mapped to

This results in an elegant encoding of deontic logic formulas in ASP. We show that the paradoxes of deontic logic are elegantly resolved by using this representation. Finally, we give a larger example to illustrate our ideas.

The main contribution of this paper is to show how modal logics, particularly deontic logic,
can be directly represented in ASP in an elegant manner, and how this representation elegantly allows the paradoxes of deontic logics to be resolved. Our work is a step towards showing that goal-directed predicate ASP indeed is a good candidate for knowledge representation and reasoning and for emulating a large part of human thinking. Much of the insight for this work comes from our attempt to use the s(CASP) goal-directed predicate ASP system for modeling modal logics

4.3
Preempting Obligations and Impermissibilities

As noted earlier, obligations may be violated and impermissible

Likewise, if an impermissible situation arises, then the denial representing the impermissibility must be dropped. Also known as the Chisholm’s Paradox

5.2
Forrester’s Paradox

Forrester’s paradox is a variation of the contrary-to-duty paradox and is stated as follows:

1. It is forbidden for a person to kill, i.e., OB
\neg kill

2. But if a person kills, he ought to kill gently, i.e.,
kill\rightarrow\textrm{OB}kill\_gently

3. If a person kills gently, then the person kills, i.e.,
kill\_gently\rightarrow kill

Suppose, that Smith kills Jones. Then he ought to kill him gently. But, by the axiom NEC, Smith ought to kill Jones, which contradicts the first obligation, that Smith ought not to kill Jones. These 3 statements can be encoded as:

⬇

1

kill
␣:-

not

-kill,

not

kill.

2

:-

kill
,

not

kill_gently
.

3

kill
␣:-

kill_gently.

With this encoding, in any possible world where
kill
is true,
kill_gently
will also be true. So it is not possible for Smith to kill, without killing gently. Additionally, the above theory admits possible worlds where
-
kill
holds.

5.4
Ross’s and Good Samaritan Paradox

Finally, we consider Ross’s Paradox and the Good Samaritan paradox. Ross’s paradox arises because of or-introduction. An example is the following:

1. It is obligatory that the letter is mailed.

2. If the letter is mailed, then the letter is posted or the letter is burned.

3. Therefore, it is obligatory that the letter is posted or the letter is burned.

This is encoded as:
\textrm{OB}mail
;
mail\rightarrow post\vee burn
. Therefore
\textrm{OB}(post\vee burn)
. The paradox arises due to the or-introduction rule: if the statement “to mail a letter, we post it”, is true, then “to mail a letter, we post it or burn it” is also true. However, it should be noted that “to mail a letter, we post it or burn it” is true by virtue of “to mail a letter, we post it”. Essentially, we cannot have a model where “to mail a letter, burn it” holds by itself.
In our formalism, this will be encoded as:

⬇

1

:-

not

mail
.

2

post
␣:-

mail.

3

burn
␣:-

mail.

The third rule seems to have been just foisted upon with no basis. Therefore Ross’s paradox is really not a paradox.

Likewise, the Good Samaritan paradox states that: (i) It ought to be the case that Jones helps Smith who has been robbed; (ii) If Smith has been robbed and Jones helps Smith, then Smith has been robbed.
(iii) Therefore, it ought to be the case that Smith has been robbed. i.e. OB
(rob\wedge help)

(rob\wedge help)\rightarrow rob

rob

⬇

%

%

␣:-

rob,

%

First of all, the rule in line 3 will always produce

as false under ASP semantics, since it’s a co-recursive rule. Assume for the moment that we have co-inductive ASP
[
12
]
where a rule such as

␣:-

p.
has two models:
\{

=

\}
and
\{

=

\}
. In such a case, the possible world only exists because we
assume

help
must also be there (along with the assumption that Jones has been robbed). So the Good Samaritan paradox is not really a paradox either.

Note that the statement “It ought to be the case that Jones helps Smith who has been robbed” can be interpreted as “If Smith is robbed, then Jones ought to help him”, which will be encoded as

⬇

Consider a more complex example taken from

John is obligated to return his friend Smith’s car, and the least he can do is return it by noon with the same level of battery charge it had when he borrowed it. It is permissible to not maintain the same battery level if the car needs a battery change.

This example makes use of “least one can do” modal operator
[
26

that corresponds to a secondary obligation. If the primary obligation is met, there may be secondary obligations or alternative action that must also be fulfilled.
The main obligation in this example is to return the car (whether or not by noon or with a properly charged battery). If the primary obligation is met, we up the ante, and demand that the secondary obligations are also fulfilled. However, either one or both of these secondary obligations may not be met, in which case the main obligation of at least returning the car must be met.
If the primary obligation (to return the car) is not met, then secondary obligations (return before noon, return with appropriate battery level) do not apply. This example also illustrates how such hierarchical obligations can be modeled in ASP.

7
Related Work

There is a huge amount of work on resolving the paradoxes of deontic logic going back decades (e.g.,
[
17
]
), however, to the best of our knowledge, none of the proposed approaches model deontic obligation and impermissibility as constraints that the accessible worlds must satisfy.
Many researchers have also attempted to model deontic logic with ASP, however, unlike us, none map deontic modal operators to ASP’s default and strong negation. In addition, most ASP-based approaches cannot handle predicates (only proposition), whereas in our case, deontic logic formulas can be mapped to arbitrary predicate answer set programs along with constraints over reals due to our use of the s(CASP) system. There are several works that use non-monotonic reasoning to attempt to resolve the paradoxes
[
19
,
22
,
21
]
. These efforts rely
indirectly
on the connection between modal operators and default and strong negations. Kowalski and Satoh have proposed using abductive logic programming, which is closely related to ASP, for implementing deontic logic
[
22
,
21
]
. In their approach, possible worlds can be ordered according to a preference relation. This preference relation is employed to select the correct world (model) in order to resolve these paradoxes. In our approach, in contrast, this preference is encoded in the logic of the program itself. The inappropriate worlds get “killed” in our method.

Dyadic deontic logic has also been proposed to handle the contrary-to-duty paradox by introducing a conditional operator that allows for obligations to be relative to a condition
[
28
]
.
This means that instead of just saying “You ought to do A,” we can say “If B is the case, you ought to do A”.
This allows for a more nuanced representation of obligations, including those that arise as a consequence of violating other obligations.
Our method can be thought of as taking the context into account as well to disable constraints that represent obligations in case these obligations are violated. So, for instance, in our method, the obligation to go and help becomes inapplicable if Jones ends up not going.

Conclusion

In this paper we showed how modal logics can be implemented with answer set programming. We showed how negation in modal logic maps to the two forms of negation—default and strong—present in ASP. We showed how there is a 1-1 correspondence between alethic modal logic and ASP. We showed how the obligation and impermissibility operators of deontic modal logic elegantly map to global constraints (denials) of ASP, resulting in a 1-1 correspondence between deontic modal logic and ASP as well. We also showed how contrary-to-duty and other paradoxes of deontic logic can be elegantly resolved using our mapping based on conditionally-enforced global constraints.
Conditional enforcement of constraints is also quite useful in knowledge representation, as our extended example about obligation to return a borrowed car shows. Our work was initially motivated by our desire to automatically convert legal and policy documents into s(CASP) programs using Large Language Models. These legal and policy documents also contain normative sentences for which deontic modal logic is the most natural representation
[
11
]
. The various examples illustrate the directness and elegance of our encoding.
