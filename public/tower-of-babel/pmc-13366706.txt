Assessment of the perceptions and practices of traditional hemorrhoid healers in the North Gondar Zone, Ethiopia
Authors: Gebre Ayanaw Alula, Yideg Abinew, Sefenew Gebeyehu, Gebeyehu Yibeltie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHemorrhoids are a common but often under-reported health problem in Ethiopia, particularly in rural areas where access to biomedical services is limited. In such contexts, traditional healers play an important role in providing care for sensitive conditions, including hemorrhoids. However, empirical evidence on their practices remains limited. This study aimed to explore the diagnostic methods, treatment approaches, perceived effectiveness, and sustainability challenges of traditional hemorrhoid healers in the North Gondar Zone.MethodsA qualitative cross-sectional study design was employed. In-depth semi-structured interviews were conducted with 15 purposively selected traditional healers. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis to identify key themes related to diagnosis, treatment, and challenges.ResultsThe findings showed that hemorrhoids were primarily diagnosed through direct physical examination and patient-reported symptoms, without the use of biomedical diagnostic tools. Treatments mainly involved locally sourced medicinal plants, often combined with substances such as butter or honey, and administered through topical applications, herbal fumigation, or sitz baths. Participants reported strong confidence in the effectiveness of these treatments for early-stage hemorrhoids, while acknowledging limited effectiveness in severe cases. Key challenges included lack of legal recognition, environmental degradation affecting medicinal plant availability, social stigma, and weak collaboration with the biomedical sector.ConclusionTraditional healers play a significant role as primary healthcare providers in underserved communities. Strengthening their role through formal recognition, promoting sustainable use of medicinal plants, and fostering collaboration with biomedical services may improve healthcare delivery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-026-05410-6.Keywords: Traditional healers, haemorrhoids, indigenous medicine, perceptions and practices, herbal therapy, north Gondar Zone, Ethiopia

Introduction

Haemorrhoids, commonly known as piles, are vascular disorders of the anorectal plexus characterized by abnormal dilation, engorgement, and inflammation of hemorrhoidal veins [1]. They commonly present with pain, rectal bleeding, itching, and discomfort during defecation. Although many cases are self-limiting, persistent or complicated haemorrhoids may require long-term management or surgical intervention [2]. Globally, haemorrhoidal disease is a significant public health concern, with prevalence influenced by lifestyle, diet, and access to healthcare [3, 4]. Studies from Africa and other regions have shown that anorectal conditions, including haemorrhoids, are common but often underreported due to stigma, limited access to care, and delayed health-seeking behavior [5, 6].

In low- and middle-income countries such as Ethiopia, access to biomedical healthcare services is limited, particularly in rural and remote areas. Structural barriers, including poverty, inadequate infrastructure, and shortage of trained health professionals, further constrain access to formal care [7, 8]. Similar challenges have been reported across sub-Saharan Africa, where many individuals rely on informal or traditional healthcare providers for conditions considered sensitive or stigmatized [9]. As a result, traditional medicine remains widely utilized, with a large proportion of the population depending on indigenous healing practices for various health problems [10].

While traditional medicine has been widely studied in general, fewer studies have specifically examined the traditional management of haemorrhoids [1, 11]. In many African and global contexts, treatment of anorectal conditions by traditional healers involves the use of medicinal plants, topical applications, herbal preparations, and culturally grounded diagnostic approaches, yet these practices remain poorly documented and under-researched [12, 13]. Traditional healers, therefore, play an important role in managing haemorrhoids, particularly in rural settings, where they are often the first point of care. In North Gondar Zone, they are highly trusted, especially for conditions considered private or stigmatizing, such as haemorrhoids, which encourages patients to seek traditional care rather than formal biomedical services [14, 15].

Despite their important role, there is limited empirical evidence focusing specifically on the perceptions, diagnostic approaches, and treatment practices of traditional hemorrhoid healers. Most existing studies in Ethiopia and elsewhere have focused broadly on traditional medicine or ethnobotanical knowledge, with little attention to disease-specific practices such as haemorrhoid management [16]. This creates a gap in understanding how healers conceptualize the condition, how they diagnose and treat it, and how effective these approaches are in real-world settings.

In addition, traditional healers face multiple challenges, including lack of formal recognition, declining availability of medicinal plants, limited knowledge transfer to younger generations, and weak collaboration with biomedical health systems [15]. These challenges are further compounded by the absence of standardized training, regulation, and integration frameworks, raising concerns about safety, consistency, and continuity of care [17, 18].

Understanding the perceptions and practices of traditional hemorrhoid healers is therefore essential to fill this knowledge gap and to provide evidence for improving healthcare delivery. Such understanding can support culturally appropriate integration of traditional and biomedical systems while preserving valuable indigenous knowledge.

Against the backdrop of increasing global interest in complementary and alternative medicine and the need for culturally sensitive healthcare, this study aimed to investigate the perceptions, diagnostic approaches, treatment practices, and challenges of traditional hemorrhoid healers in the North Gondar Zone of Ethiopia. The findings are intended to contribute to evidence-based policy and promote the integration of traditional practices into the formal healthcare system while supporting the conservation of indigenous medical knowledge.

Methods and materials

Study area

The study was carried out in the North Gondar Zone of the Amhara National Regional State, Ethiopia, specifically targeting the districts of Debark, Dabat, and Janamora. Geopolitically, this zone is bordered by the Central Gondar Zone in the south, the Tigray region in the north, Wag Hemra in the east, and South Gondar in the southeast. Debark town, the administrative capital of the zone, lies roughly 103 km north of Gondar city and about 830 km from Addis Ababa. The zone is located at an altitude of around 2,850 m above sea level (North Gondar Zone Administration Office, 2024). Agriculture is the dominant livelihood in the region, with crop farming forming the main economic base of rural households.

The study area was deliberately chosen because of its pronounced cultural diversity and the strong dependence of local communities on traditional medicinal practices (supported by previous ethnomedicine and cultural studies conducted in the region) [19]. The selection of this area is also consistent with prior research indicating that rural highland communities in Ethiopia rely heavily on indigenous knowledge systems for primary healthcare.

Study design

This study adopted a qualitative research approach, which is widely used for exploring socially and culturally constructed phenomena such as perceptions, practices, and lived experiences. Qualitative research is particularly appropriate when the aim is to obtain an in-depth understanding of participants’ perspectives in their natural context without numerical measurement or statistical generalization [20].

Within this qualitative approach, a descriptive qualitative study design was employed. This design is used to provide a comprehensive and straightforward description of participants’ experiences with minimal interpretation, making it suitable for studies that aim to document real-life practices and indigenous knowledge systems [21].

Data were collected at a single point in time using in-depth interviews with open-ended questions, making the study cross-sectional in terms of timing only. In qualitative research, in-depth interviews are widely recommended for exploring sensitive and culturally embedded topics, as they allow participants to freely express their experiences in detail [22].

The descriptive qualitative design used in this study is consistent with established qualitative research frameworks and is appropriate for capturing detailed accounts of traditional diagnostic methods, treatment practices, and perceived challenges among traditional healers in their natural setting.

Study participants

The study population comprised traditional healers recognized by their communities for their expertise in the treatment of hemorrhoids in the districts of Debark, Dabat, and Janamora. Although no formal registry exists, local health offices and community leaders confirmed their widespread presence. A total of 15 traditional healers were purposively selected based on their years of practice, established professional reputation, and active involvement in hemorrhoid treatment.

Participants were selected using an intentional (purposive) sampling technique, which is widely used in qualitative research to identify information-rich cases rather than for statistical representation. This approach is appropriate for exploring in-depth experiences and culturally embedded practices.

All participants were male, reflecting prevailing sociocultural norms in which traditional healing knowledge is predominantly transmitted through male lineage. Female traditional healers exist but were not actively involved in hemorrhoid treatment in the selected districts during the study period. Participants were selected to ensure rich indigenous knowledge and substantial clinical experience relevant to the study objectives.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Traditional healers aged 18 years and above who had at least two years of experience in treating hemorrhoids and were actively practicing in the study area were eligible for inclusion. Participants were required to provide informed verbal consent after being fully informed about the study objectives, procedures, and confidentiality measures.

Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants were clearly informed of their right to refuse participation or withdraw from the study at any stage without any consequences. Individuals who declined participation or withdrew consent were excluded from the study and were not subjected to any further contact.

Participants were excluded if they did not meet the inclusion criteria (i.e., less than two years of experience, not actively practicing, or refusal to participate). This ensures that exclusion criteria are not duplicated but logically aligned with inclusion criteria.

Sample size and sampling technique

A total of 15 traditional hemorrhoid healers were selected from the three districts of Debark, Dabat, and Janamora using a purposive sampling technique. Purposive sampling is widely recommended in qualitative research for selecting information-rich participants who have direct experience and in-depth knowledge of the phenomenon under study [23]. This approach was appropriate for identifying traditional healers with substantial practical experience in hemorrhoid treatment.

The sample size was guided by the principle of data saturation, which refers to the point at which no new themes or meaningful insights emerge from additional data collection [24]. In qualitative studies, sample size is not determined statistically but rather by the depth and completeness of the information obtained. In this study, saturation was reached after the 13th interview; however, two additional interviews were conducted to confirm consistency and ensure that no new information emerged.

The final sample size of 15 participants is consistent with established qualitative research guidance, which suggests that relatively small sample sizes are appropriate when the focus is on rich, detailed, and context-specific data rather than statistical generalization [20]. Efforts were also made to ensure variation across the three districts, allowing for a broader representation of traditional practices and beliefs within the study area.

Data collection

Data were collected using in-depth interviews (IDIs) guided by a semi-structured interview guide consisting of open-ended questions developed specifically for this study (Supplementary File 1). The development of the interview guide followed a systematic process to ensure clarity, relevance, and reliability of the data collection tool.

First, a comprehensive review of relevant literature and similar qualitative studies was conducted to identify key thematic areas related to traditional hemorrhoid healing practices. Based on this review, an initial draft of interview questions was developed covering participants’ understanding of hemorrhoids, diagnostic approaches, treatment practices, perceived effectiveness and safety, and associated challenges.

Second, the draft interview guide was reviewed and refined by subject experts in public health and ethnomedicine to ensure content validity, cultural appropriateness, and clarity of the questions.

Third, the interview guide was translated from English into Amharic and then back-translated to ensure consistency in meaning and linguistic accuracy.

Fourth, a pilot test was conducted with two traditional healers outside the study area to assess clarity, flow, cultural appropriateness, and respondent understanding. Based on feedback from the pilot test, minor modifications were made to improve question clarity and sequencing.

In-depth interviews were selected as the primary data collection method because they allow for detailed exploration of sensitive and culturally embedded practices such as traditional hemorrhoid treatment, which may not be easily discussed in group settings. This approach enabled participants to freely share their experiences in a confidential environment without fear of judgment.

Focus group discussions were not used because group settings may limit openness, introduce social desirability bias, and reduce participants’ willingness to discuss sensitive health practices.

All interviews were conducted in Amharic at private locations convenient for participants and lasted approximately 45–60 min. With informed consent, all interviews were audio-recorded and supplemented with field notes. Recordings were transcribed verbatim and translated into English to ensure accuracy and consistency during analysis.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using thematic analysis following the six-step framework of Braun and Clarke: familiarization with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the final report [25].

All interviews were transcribed verbatim in Amharic and translated into English. The translated transcripts were carefully reviewed and cross-checked against the original audio recordings to ensure accuracy and to minimize transcription and translation errors.

The analysis process began with repeated reading of all transcripts to achieve data familiarization and to gain an overall understanding of participants’ narratives. In the next step, initial inductive codes were generated line-by-line from meaningful segments of the data without using any predefined categories. These codes represented basic units of meaning related to participants’ experiences, perceptions, and practices.

To enhance analytical rigor, two independent researchers conducted the coding process separately. A preliminary codebook was developed after initial coding and was continuously refined as new codes emerged from the data. Coding differences between the two researchers were discussed in detail, and disagreements were resolved through consensus to ensure consistency and reduce interpretive bias.

In the next stage, codes with similar meanings or conceptual relationships were systematically grouped into categories. These categories were then further examined to identify broader patterns of meaning, which formed the basis for theme development. Themes were not pre-determined but emerged through an iterative process of comparing codes and categories against the full dataset.

The emerging themes were then reviewed and refined through repeated comparison with the original transcripts, ensuring that each theme accurately reflected participants’ accounts. Themes were merged, refined, or separated where necessary to improve conceptual clarity and internal consistency. Each theme was then clearly defined and named to reflect its underlying meaning.

The final thematic structure included: [1] Indigenous understanding and diagnosis of hemorrhoids [2], Traditional treatment practices [3], Perceived effectiveness and safety, and [4] Structural, environmental, and sociocultural challenges.

Although the analysis was primarily inductive, relevant literature was used during the interpretation phase to contextualize and compare findings with existing studies. To enhance trustworthiness, peer debriefing and member checking were conducted, and reflexive notes were maintained throughout the analysis process to minimize researcher bias and ensure analytical rigor.

Trustworthiness of the Study

The trustworthiness of this qualitative study was ensured using the criteria of credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability, as described by Lincoln and Guba [26], and further elaborated by Shenton [27].

Credibility was ensured through prolonged engagement with participants during fieldwork, iterative probing during interviews, member checking, and peer debriefing within the research team. These strategies are recommended to enhance the accuracy and truthfulness of qualitative findings by ensuring that participants’ perspectives are correctly represented.

Dependability was maintained through a clear and systematic documentation of all research procedures, including participant recruitment, data collection using audio-recorded in-depth interviews, verbatim transcription, and thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-step framework. This audit trail ensures that the research process is transparent and could be followed or audited if necessary [26].

Confirmability was ensured through maintaining an audit trail of raw interview data, coded transcripts, and theme development matrices, alongside reflexive journaling throughout the research process to minimize researcher bias. These procedures ensured that findings were grounded in participants’ narratives rather than researcher interpretation [27].

Transferability was addressed by providing thick descriptions of the study setting, participant characteristics, and detailed presentation of findings, enabling readers to assess the applicability of the results to similar rural and culturally comparable contexts [27].

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics and professional background of traditional hemorrhoid healers

A total of 15 traditional hemorrhoid healers participated in the study. All participants were male, with ages ranging from 41 to 62 years. The majority, 9 (60%), were in the 51–62 age group, while 6 (40%) were aged 41–50 years.

Regarding professional experience, most participants, 13 (86.7%), had more than 10 years of practice in traditional healing, while the remaining 2 (13.3%) had between 2 and 10 years of experience.

In terms of knowledge acquisition, 11 participants (73.3%) reported that they inherited their healing knowledge from family members, while 4 (26.7%) acquired it through apprenticeship under experienced healers.

Participants were drawn from three districts: Janamora 7 (46.7%), Debark 5 (33.3%), and Dabat 3 (20%), ensuring geographic representation across the study area. All participants reported active involvement in hemorrhoid treatment practices and indicated that none had formal biomedical training (Table 1).

Indigenous conceptualization and diagnosis of hemorrhoids

Traditional healers described hemorrhoids mainly in terms of observable symptoms such as pain, swelling, bleeding, itching, and general discomfort. Although their understanding does not fully align with biomedical classifications, they were able to differentiate between mild and severe cases based on symptom intensity and the extent to which the condition affected daily activities.

Diagnosis was primarily based on visual inspection, palpation, and detailed patient narratives. Participants emphasized that effective diagnosis depends strongly on trust and confidentiality, which encourage patients to openly describe their symptoms and condition. As one participant explained:

“Patients only tell the full story when they feel safe and respected” (P3, 54 years, Debark).

No laboratory tests or biomedical diagnostic tools were used. Instead, diagnosis relied on experiential knowledge and pattern recognition developed through years of practice. Healers also described hemorrhoids as a condition influenced by multiple interconnected factors, including physical strain, dietary habits, and spiritual balance.

For example, one participant stated:

“This condition comes when the body is not balanced what we eat, how we live, and even our spiritual state all matter” (P11, 49 years, Janamora).

This reflects a holistic understanding of the condition that integrates physical, behavioral, and spiritual dimensions of health.

Treatment modalities and therapeutic practices

Traditional healers reported the use of a wide range of medicinal plants, including leaves, roots, and bark, which were prepared in different forms such as decoctions, pastes, and oils. Commonly mentioned plants included Aloe vera, Ocimum lamiifolium, Ruta chalepensis, and Croton macrostachyus. These remedies were used either individually or in combination depending on the severity and type of symptoms presented by the patient.

Plant-based preparations were frequently mixed with locally available substances such as butter, honey, or oils, which were believed to enhance therapeutic effectiveness and reduce irritation during application. Treatment methods varied and included oral administration, topical application, herbal steaming, and sitz baths. As one participant explained:

“We treat both inside and outside so that the illness is fully removed” (P9, 58 years, Janamora).

Some healers also described the combined use of multiple herbal preparations to enhance treatment outcomes. In addition to herbal remedies, a few participants reported the use of prayer and symbolic practices, which they believed supported healing by addressing spiritual aspects of illness.

Treatment approaches were highly individualized and adjusted according to patient condition. Healers also emphasized the importance of lifestyle modifications, including dietary changes and reduced physical strain, as part of the healing process. As one participant noted:

“The medicine works best when the patient also changes how they live” (P1, 61 years, Debark).

This reflects an integrated approach that combines herbal treatment with behavioral and lifestyle interventions.

Perceptions of effectiveness, safety, and therapeutic boundaries

Participants expressed strong confidence in the effectiveness of their traditional treatments, particularly for early-stage hemorrhoids. Effectiveness was commonly assessed based on observed patient recovery, reduced symptom recurrence, repeat consultations, and sustained community trust. As one participant stated:

“We have seen many patients recover completely after our treatment” (P7, 62 years, Dabat).

Safety was an important consideration in treatment practices. Healers reported that they carefully selected medicinal plants and adjusted dosages based on patient characteristics such as age, physical strength, and condition severity. One participant explained:

“We adjust the dose depending on the strength and age of the patient” (P5, 47 years, Debark).

Despite their confidence in treatment outcomes, participants also acknowledged clear therapeutic boundaries. Severe cases, particularly those involving heavy bleeding, infection, or complications, were considered beyond their capacity. In such situations, patients were advised to seek biomedical care. As one healer noted:

“When the condition becomes serious, we refer the patient to the hospital” (P12, 51 years, Janamora).

However, referrals were described as informal and depended on the healer’s judgment as well as the patient’s willingness to accept biomedical services.

Structural, environmental, and sociocultural challenges

Participants reported several interrelated challenges that affect their traditional healing practice. A major concern was the lack of formal recognition and legal protection, which leaves traditional healers operating outside the formal healthcare system and limits their institutional support and professional recognition.

Environmental challenges were also frequently mentioned, particularly the declining availability of medicinal plants. Participants attributed this mainly to deforestation and environmental degradation, which have reduced access to key therapeutic resources. As one participant explained:

“Many plants we use are disappearing due to deforestation” (P15, 59 years, Janamora).

Sociocultural changes were identified as another important factor influencing healthcare-seeking behavior. Although traditional medicine remains culturally trusted, participants observed that many patients now prefer biomedical services as a first option and only seek traditional care when modern treatments are unsuccessful. This shift was described as gradually affecting the demand for traditional healing services.

Finally, participants expressed concern about the decline in intergenerational knowledge transfer. Younger generations were perceived as less interested in learning traditional healing practices, raising concerns about the sustainability of this knowledge system. As one healer stated:

“Young people are no longer interested in learning our practice” (P6, 54 years, Dabat).

Discussion

This study offers an in‑depth examination of the views, treatment approaches, and practical difficulties faced by traditional hemorrhoid healers in the North Gondar Zone, highlighting their lasting role within Ethiopia’s pluralistic healthcare landscape. Overall, the findings demonstrate that traditional healers play a central role in providing accessible, culturally acceptable healthcare, while relying on indigenous knowledge systems, experiential diagnosis, and herbal-based treatments. The results show that these healers function not only as primary providers of health care, but also as crucial custodians of indigenous medical knowledge and cultural traditions, especially within rural areas and among socioeconomically disadvantaged communities [28].

These findings are consistent with prior studies in Ethiopia and other African contexts, which emphasize the continued reliance on traditional medicine in settings where access to biomedical services is limited. Similar studies have also reported that traditional healers are often the first point of care due to their accessibility, affordability, and cultural acceptability [29, 30].

The dominance of male practitioners and the dependence on passing knowledge from one generation to the next correspond to empirical findings from Ethiopia and other African contexts, where therapeutic authority is commonly structured in patriarchal terms and succession usually follows hereditary lines [31, 32]. However, the decline in the participation of younger generations raises serious questions about the long‑term sustainability and preservation of these medical traditions. This observation is in line with previous studies that highlight the impact of modernization, formal education, and urbanization on the decline of traditional knowledge systems [33]. This trend may be linked to increasing urbanization, formal education priorities, and shifting sociocultural values. Without systematic documentation, clearly defined training pathways, and strong institutional support, these indigenous knowledge systems remain highly vulnerable to gradual erosion and potential extinction [34].

Diagnostic practices based on hands-on physical assessment and drawing out patients’ own accounts of their symptoms illustrate forms of clinical reasoning that are deeply shaped by culture, rather than by purely nosological or strictly biomedical taxonomies [35]. Comparable diagnostic approaches have been widely described in a variety of traditional African healing systems and have been shown to possess practical and therapeutic efficacy within their specific local health environments [36]. The use of patient narratives and trust-building as diagnostic tools further reflects findings from previous ethnomedical studies that emphasize the role of social interaction in traditional diagnosis [37]. The holistic understanding of hemorrhoidal disease by healers –framed in relation to diet, occupational and physical stress, broader lifestyle conditions, and aspects of spiritual balance – aligns closely with Kleinman’s explanatory model, which emphasizes the culturally constituted character of the meanings and therapeutic practices [38]. This suggests that traditional healers apply a context-sensitive and integrative approach that considers both physical and sociocultural determinants of health.

The use of medicinal plants, topical remedies, and herbal baths highlights the crucial role of biological diversity in traditional healthcare systems. The identified medicinal plants such as Aloe vera, Ocimum lamiifolium, Ruta chalepensis, and Croton macrostachyus have also been documented in previous ethnobotanical studies for their therapeutic properties, supporting their traditional use [39, 40]. However, the absence of standardized dosing protocols, thorough clinical testing, and established pharmacovigilance frameworks creates major obstacles to their integration into conventional medical services [41]. Despite these limitations, the reported use of locally available medicinal plants reflects a strong reliance on indigenous ecological knowledge and sustainable resource utilization. Although traditional healers recognize the limitations of their treatments and may direct patients with severe or complicated conditions to biomedical facilities, the lack of structured referral systems hinders continuity of care and consistent follow-up of patients.

Structural marginalization remains a major barrier to effective practice. The lack of formal legal recognition and the routine omission of traditional medicine from public health initiatives reflect broader policy shortcomings that undervalue these medical systems, even though they are widely used [42]. This marginalization limits collaboration between traditional and biomedical practitioners, which could otherwise enhance healthcare delivery in resource-limited settings. Previous studies have shown that establishing structured integration between traditional and biomedical systems can improve trust, accessibility, and overall health outcomes [43, 44].

Environmental degradation presents a further challenge to the long-term viability of traditional hemorrhoid treatments by reducing both the availability and the variety of medicinal plant species. This finding is consistent with studies reporting that deforestation and habitat loss significantly threaten medicinal plant resources used in traditional healthcare systems [45]. This highlights the direct link between environmental sustainability and the preservation of indigenous medical knowledge. Therefore, conservation strategies, including protection of natural habitats and promotion of community-based cultivation of medicinal plants, are essential to sustain both biodiversity and traditional medical practices [46].

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study provides rich insights into the perceptions and practices of traditional hemorrhoid healers in North Gondar Zone. The use of in-depth interviews allowed participants to openly share their experiences in their own words, giving a deep understanding of culturally rooted practices. Including participants from different districts also improved the depth and context of the findings.

To ensure quality, the study improved credibility through detailed participant interviews and use of direct quotations. The analysis was done carefully and systematically, which supports dependability and confirmability of the findings.

However, the study has some limitations. It included only 15 male participants, so it does not reflect the views of female healers and may limit transferability. The findings are based on self-reported data, which may include recall or social desirability bias. The study was also limited to one area, and the researcher’s perspective may have influenced the interpretation of data.

Conclusions and recommendations

Traditional hemorrhoid healers constitute a vital component of the healthcare system in the North Gondar Zone, particularly in rural communities where biomedical services are limited and health-seeking behavior is strongly influenced by cultural beliefs. This study demonstrates that these healers rely on indigenous diagnostic methods and locally sourced herbal remedies, which are widely trusted by the community and considered effective, especially for early-stage hemorrhoidal conditions.

However, their practices face significant challenges, including a lack of formal legal recognition, depletion and reduced availability of medicinal plant species, weak and sporadic collaboration with the biomedical sector, and the gradual erosion of intergenerational knowledge transfer.

This study contributes to the growing body of knowledge on traditional healthcare systems by providing empirical evidence on the practices, perceptions, and challenges of traditional hemorrhoid healers in Ethiopia. The findings underscore the complementary role of traditional and biomedical health systems and highlight the potential benefits of integrating these models within culturally appropriate frameworks.

To address these challenges, policies should formally recognize and support traditional healers, promote conservation and sustainable use of medicinal plants, and encourage structured collaboration with biomedical professionals to ensure the safe and effective integration of traditional and modern healthcare services. Future research should explore female traditional healers and other regions to enhance the generalizability and comprehensiveness of findings.

Ethical Consideration

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Research and Publication Directorate of Debark University (REC/DKU/048/2024), and the study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association, 2013). Data were collected between February 3 and April 14, 2025.

Before participation, all participants were clearly informed about the purpose and procedures of the study in a language they understood. Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants were assured that they could refuse or withdraw at any time without any consequences.

To ensure confidentiality, no personal identifiers were recorded, and each participant was assigned a code. All data were securely stored in password-protected files and were accessible only to the research team. Identifying information was removed during transcription and reporting.

For participants who were unable to read and write, the consent information was read aloud in the presence of an impartial witness. After confirming their understanding and willingness to participate, consent was documented using a thumbprint, and the witness provided a signature to verify the process.
