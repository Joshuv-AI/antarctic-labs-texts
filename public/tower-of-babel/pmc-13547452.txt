The mtDNA–cGAS–STING Axis in Acute Kidney Injury and Maladaptive Repair: Preclinical Evidence for Traditional Chinese Medicine and Natural Product-Based Interventions
Authors: Xun Mu, Xisheng Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute kidney injury (AKI) is a heterogeneous syndrome characterized by substantial variation in etiology, clinical course, and recovery. Persistent tubular stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and dysregulated inflammatory and immune responses can prolong tissue injury and lead to incomplete kidney recovery and maladaptive repair. When released into the cytosol, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) can activate cyclic GMP–AMP synthase (cGAS), which generates 2′,3′-cGAMP and activates stimulator of interferon genes (STING). Downstream TBK1–IRF3 and NF-κB signaling programs amplify inflammation, regulated cell death, and failed repair. This structured narrative review follows the sequence of mitochondrial injury, mtDNA release, cGAS–STING activation, inflammation and regulated cell death, and maladaptive repair. We examine how these mechanisms vary across AKI etiologies, cell types, and disease stages and critically appraise preclinical evidence for traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) formulas and preparations, bioactive fractions and extracts, isolated natural products, and natural product-based delivery systems. Genetic and pharmacological studies support the involvement of this axis in selected AKI models. However, the mitochondrial origin of cGAS-sensed cytosolic DNA, the requirement for cGAS–STING signaling in observed protective effects, and the relevance of these mechanisms to human AKI remain incompletely established. Most TCM and natural product studies use cisplatin models and initiate treatment before or at the time of injury; they generally report parallel improvements in kidney injury endpoints and reductions in pathway-associated markers. Thus, selected interventions show preclinical renoprotective activity in specific models, but neither clinical efficacy nor in vivo pathway dependence has been demonstrated. Future translational studies should prioritize post-injury administration, causal pathway validation, kidney-compartment exposure, safety assessment, and functional testing in human-relevant systems.Keywords: acute kidney injury, mitochondrial DNA, cGAS–STING, maladaptive repair, traditional Chinese medicine, natural products

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is characterized by an abrupt decline in kidney function and/or reduced urine output. It commonly develops in settings such as sepsis, perioperative ischemia, circulatory failure, exposure to iodinated contrast media, and drug-induced nephrotoxicity. Reported incidence and mortality vary with the underlying condition, clinical setting, and diagnostic criteria used.1–3 AKI does not always resolve completely. In some patients, kidney dysfunction persists as acute kidney disease (AKD), increasing the subsequent risks of chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular events, and death.1–6 Management remains largely supportive and includes treatment of the underlying cause, optimization of volume status and hemodynamics, avoidance of further nephrotoxic exposure, and kidney replacement therapy when indicated.1–3 No broadly applicable mechanism-targeted therapy has yet demonstrated clinical benefit in established AKI.

Renal tubular epithelial cells have high energy requirements and contain abundant mitochondria, which makes them particularly susceptible to ischemic, hypoxic, toxic, and inflammatory stress.3,7,8 Mitochondrial injury can release oxidized or fragmented mtDNA into the cytosol, where cGAS converts ATP and GTP into 2′,3′-cGAMP. This second messenger drives STING trafficking from the endoplasmic reticulum to the Golgi and activates TBK1–IRF3, NF-κB, and related signaling programs.7–14 By coupling organelle damage to innate immune activation, the mtDNA–cGAS–STING axis may contribute to sterile inflammation, tubular cell death, and maladaptive repair after AKI.7,15–20

TCM formulas, naturally derived bioactive compounds, and delivery systems have increasingly been investigated as modulators of mitochondrial homeostasis and cGAS–STING-associated inflammation. The supporting studies, however, vary widely in model relevance and evidentiary strength. Findings from acute-phase AKI, maladaptive repair after AKI, isolated renal fibrosis models, and nonrenal inflammatory models address different questions and should not be treated as equivalent. The multicomponent nature of these interventions does not lessen the need for direct mechanistic testing. Lower expression of pathway proteins, improved inflammatory markers, or molecular docking alone cannot establish direct engagement of cGAS or STING.

Unlike previous reviews that have focused primarily on the basic biology of cGAS–STING or on individual AKI models, this review is organized around two considerations: the source of cytosolic DNA and the strength of causal evidence. We distinguish findings obtained during acute injury from those related to maladaptive repair and assess TCM and natural product studies according to pathway dependence, treatment timing, in vivo exposure, and validation in human-relevant systems. Our aim is to define what the current evidence can—and cannot—support and to identify the main barriers to mechanistic and clinical translation.

Literature Search and Evidence Appraisal

We searched PubMed/MEDLINE for peer-reviewed English-language articles and supplemented the search by screening reference lists and journal websites and by verifying PubMed records and digital object identifiers. The search covered literature published through July 20, 2026. Search domains included AKI and its major etiologies, mtDNA–cGAS–STING signaling, TCM and natural product interventions, and kidney-targeted delivery and tissue exposure. For the principal AKI evidence synthesis, eligible studies were required to report a defined kidney-injury outcome together with relevant pathway measurements. Fibrosis and nonrenal studies were retained only as supportive evidence for maladaptive repair or direct target engagement and were not used to support claims of acute-phase AKI efficacy. Studies limited to network pharmacology or molecular docking were not used to support efficacy or pathway-dependence claims. The complete search strategies and study-selection criteria are provided in Supplementary Material S1.

Evidence appraisal considered the relevance of each model to AKI, confirmation of the mitochondrial origin of cytosolic DNA, causal involvement of the pathway, direct-target evidence, treatment timing, and translational information. Cytosolic mtDNA quantification, mtDNA depletion, or reconstitution can support the involvement of mtDNA. Pathway dependence was considered supported only when the treatment effect changed after knockdown, knockout, bypass rescue, or another appropriate functional intervention. Molecular docking was not accepted as evidence of direct binding. We did not perform a meta-analysis or formal risk-of-bias assessment; the appraisal criteria and extracted data are presented in Supplementary Material S1.

Role and Heterogeneity of the mtDNA–cGAS–STING Axis in AKI

Mitochondrial Injury, mtDNA Release, and Pathway Activation

Under homeostatic conditions, mtDNA is packaged by proteins such as mitochondrial transcription factor A and physically segregated from the cytosol, while aberrant DNA is cleared by nucleases and mitochondrial quality-control systems.21,22 During AKI, ischemia, hypoxia, nephrotoxins, inflammatory mediators, and oxidative stress can disrupt cristae architecture, membrane potential, and respiratory-chain function, leading to accumulation of mtDNA in the cytosol.7,8,21–24 Oxidative modification also renders mtDNA relatively resistant to nuclease degradation and may prolong DNA-sensing signals.21

Release of mtDNA can occur through several membrane pathways. In cisplatin-induced AKI, PGAM5 dephosphorylates BAX and promotes pore formation in the outer mitochondrial membrane; inhibition of the PGAM5–BAX axis reduces both mtDNA release and cGAS–STING activation.8 Pharmacological inhibition and genetic manipulation of BAX pore formation provide further support for a similar mechanism in wasp sting-induced AKI.25 Mechanistic studies of BAX/BAK macropores and VDAC oligomerization provide additional biological context for mtDNA efflux.23,24 The relative contribution of these pore-forming mechanisms is nevertheless likely to vary among AKI etiologies and requires cell-specific, time-resolved investigation.

Upon binding cytosolic DNA, cGAS generates 2′,3′-cGAMP, which promotes STING trafficking, oligomerization, and palmitoylation and initiates TBK1–IRF3- and NF-κB-dependent transcriptional programs.9–14 In cisplatin-induced AKI, evidence from mtDNA depletion and reconstitution, cGAS or STING deficiency, and pharmacological STING inhibition provides relatively strong functional support for this pathway.7,18 In sepsis and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) models, cGAS–STING signaling also intersects with NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and other forms of regulated cell death.15,26–28

Importantly, improved mitochondrial function or lower abundance of cGAS–STING-related proteins does not establish that the DNA sensed by cGAS originated from mitochondria. Without cytosolic mtDNA quantification, depletion/reconstitution, or source-directed manipulation, such findings are better described as cGAS–STING-associated changes in the setting of mitochondrial or DNA injury rather than as mtDNA-specific pathway activation.7,15,29,30 Figure 1 summarizes the major links among mitochondrial injury, mtDNA release, intracellular STING trafficking, and downstream effects in AKI.

Differences Across Etiologies, Cell Types, and Disease Stages

Cisplatin models have principally implicated mitochondrial injury, PGAM5–BAX-dependent mtDNA release, NF-κB-mediated inflammation, and ferroptosis.7,8,18,31–33 Ischemia–reperfusion models have highlighted RIP3–mitofilin signaling, lipid accumulation, and tubular–macrophage crosstalk.17,19,34,35 Sepsis and LPS models place greater emphasis on NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis, endoplasmic reticulum stress, PERK signaling, and lactylation-dependent mechanisms.15,16,27,28,36,37 Contrast-associated injury, wasp stings, and triptolide nephrotoxicity provide additional, more model-specific evidence.25,38,39 Because these models differ in initiating insult, immune context, and observation period, findings from one setting cannot be assumed to apply to another.

Most studies have focused on renal tubular epithelial cells, although infiltrating monocytes and macrophages, dendritic cells, and endothelial cells may also contribute to local DNA sensing and inflammatory amplification.15,16,35,37,40–42 Single-cell analyses show that tubular and myeloid states in AKI are continuous, dynamic, and spatially heterogeneous and are not adequately represented by a simple M1/M2 dichotomy.40–42 Omics approaches are valuable for mapping these states and generating mechanistic hypotheses, but lineage tracing and cell-specific perturbation are still needed to establish causality.

Disease stage also shapes the biological consequences of pathway activation. During acute injury, STING may amplify inflammation and cell death; persistent or dysregulated signaling may also contribute to cellular senescence, metabolic reprogramming, and fibrosis.16,20,34 Human kidney single-cell multiomic analyses have revealed stable transcriptional and chromatin-regulatory changes during the transition from normal to failed-repair proximal tubular states and have identified potential drivers such as NFAT5.43 A single time point cannot reliably distinguish a transient stress response from persistent pathogenic signaling. Longitudinal sampling within the same model, spanning injury establishment, functional recovery, and maladaptive repair, would better define stage-specific effects.

Extended Mechanisms and Human Evidence

Beyond the canonical TBK1–IRF3 and NF-κB pathways, recent studies have connected cGAS–STING signaling with ferroptosis, lactylation, and lipid metabolic reprogramming.32,34–37 Proposed mechanisms include SNORD3A-dependent regulation of STING transcription, LDHB K156 lactylation, HMGB1 lactylation and neutrophil extracellular trap formation, and HK3- or ACOT8-associated lipid abnormalities.32,34–37 Single-cell and genetic studies have also shown that ferroptotic stress impairs proximal tubular redifferentiation after injury, sustains proinflammatory cell states, and worsens inflammation and fibrosis.44 These data provide a plausible link between acute ferroptotic injury and subsequent maladaptive repair, but they do not show that the process is wholly dependent on cGAS–STING. Because most of these mechanisms are supported by single, recent studies—and several were examined only under pretreatment conditions—they should be viewed as emerging refinements of the core pathway rather than as broadly established parallel mechanisms.

Human evidence remains substantially weaker than evidence from animal and cellular models. An integrated analysis of human kidney single-nucleus transcriptomes and plasma proteomes identified markers of proximal tubular maladaptation, several of which were associated with severe AKI after cardiac surgery.45 The study did not, however, directly measure cGAS–STING activity. Urinary mtDNA has been associated with mitochondrial disruption and kidney dysfunction in AKI, but mtDNA detected in biofluids may originate from the kidney, circulating immune cells, or other organs and may also engage DNA-sensing pathways such as TLR9.29,30 Single-cell atlases of urine and kidney tissue capture injury and repair states, yet a validated link between fluid biomarkers, intrarenal pathway activity, and clinical outcomes has not been established.41,42 Human evidence and complementary evidence relevant to maladaptive repair are summarized in Supplementary Table S4.

A practical framework for human validation would combine plasma or urinary mtDNA and cGAMP with phosphorylated pathway components and interferon-stimulated genes. Such studies require strict control of platelet and leukocyte contamination, centrifugation protocols, freeze–thaw cycles, DNA extraction, and normalization. Patient-derived tubular cells, kidney organoids, and tissue explants could then be used to determine whether the observed associations are functionally relevant.

Pharmacological Modulation as a Mechanistic Benchmark

Studies of STING agonists and inhibitors show that the pathway can be modulated at several molecular sites. Interpretation is complicated, however, by species-specific structural differences, gene-deletion strategies, treatment timing, and the disease stage examined.14,46–48 Measurements of cGAMP, p-STING, p-TBK1, and p-IRF3, together with assessment of STING trafficking or degradation, provide more direct evidence of pathway activity than total cGAS or STING abundance alone.

H-151, SN-011, and Y-320 inhibit STING signaling by disrupting palmitoylation, occupying the cyclic dinucleotide-binding pocket, or engaging an allosteric site in the transmembrane domain, respectively. Each has shown kidney-protective effects in selected AKI models.18,19,28,31,46,47 In contrast, DMXAA remained protective against cisplatin nephrotoxicity in mice with systemic STING deficiency, and SR717 did not reproduce this effect.48 These divergent findings show that a protective phenotype produced by a single experimental agonist or inhibitor is not, by itself, evidence of pathway dependence. Reserpine pretreatment also attenuated cisplatin-induced AKI and ferroptosis-associated changes, but the available data are primarily associative.33 Pharmacological findings therefore need to be interpreted alongside genetic perturbation, functional rescue, target engagement, and tissue exposure data. Preclinical Evidence for Traditional Chinese Medicine and Natural Product-Based Interventions

For clarity, we classify the interventions as TCM formulas and preparations; bioactive fractions, extracts, and isolated natural products; and natural product-based delivery systems. The appraisal focuses on model relevance, treatment timing, pathway dependence, and in vivo exposure. Although most studies report improvements in kidney function, histopathology, or inflammatory markers, the strength of mechanistic evidence and the availability of translationally relevant data vary considerably.

Traditional Chinese Medicine Formulas and Preparations

The Yi-Shen-Xie-Zhuo formula improved kidney function and attenuated histopathological injury in cisplatin-induced AKI and HKC-8 cell models. It also reduced reactive oxygen species, restored mitochondrial membrane potential, and lowered cGAS–STING-associated inflammatory and apoptotic markers.49 Attenuation of the protective effect after cGAS or STING knockdown in vitro provides preliminary functional support for pathway involvement. The animal study, however, used pretreatment and did not include in vivo cell-specific validation, post-injury administration, or kidney pharmacokinetic assessment.

Shenqi Fuzheng injection attenuated kidney dysfunction, oxidative stress, DNA damage, and apoptosis and reduced mtDNA release and cGAS–STING-associated signaling in HK-2 cells and in mice bearing 4T1 breast tumors that received repeated low-dose cisplatin.50 The model more closely resembles an oncology setting than a conventional short-course AKI model, but tumor burden, repeated chemotherapy, and prolonged exposure may influence the findings. Whether the preparation alters the antitumor activity of cisplatin remains unresolved. More complete chemical fingerprinting and tissue exposure data are also needed.

Studies of maladaptive repair and renal fibrosis provide supportive mechanistic clues. Zhen Wu decoction modulated NRF2–TFAM signaling, mitochondrial bioenergetics, and mtDNA-associated inflammation in unilateral ureteral obstruction (UUO).51 Nephropathy II decoction regulated STING signaling and oxidative phosphorylation in folic acid nephropathy, UUO, and fibrosis after bilateral ischemia–reperfusion injury; microscale thermophoresis indicated that its candidate constituent neochlorogenic acid binds murine STING.52 Huangqi-Danshen decoction attenuated adenine-induced renal fibrosis through an SCD1–cGAS–STING-associated mechanism.53 Compound Danshen Dripping Pill also disrupted the STING–TBK1 interaction in nonrenal inflammatory models.54 This last finding supports the ability of a multicomponent preparation to interfere with a key step in STING signaling, but it does not demonstrate efficacy in AKI. Together, these studies may inform mechanisms of persistent inflammation and maladaptive repair after AKI, but they cannot replace direct evidence from acute-phase AKI.

Bioactive Fractions, Extracts, and Isolated Natural Products

Astragalus polysaccharide improved kidney function and attenuated histopathological injury in rhabdomyolysis-associated AKI. It also reduced cGAS–STING–IRF3-associated markers and the CD86-positive proinflammatory myeloid phenotype.55 Because treatment began 7 days before glycerol administration, these data primarily support preventive renoprotection accompanied by pathway-associated changes. Because canonical STING signaling involves trafficking from the endoplasmic reticulum to the Golgi rather than nuclear translocation, the reported localization requires independent confirmation and was not treated as mechanistic localization evidence in our appraisal.

In tumor-bearing mice receiving repeated low-dose cisplatin, 6-shogaol improved kidney function and reduced renal macrophage infiltration. In HK-2 cells, the additional protective effect of 6-shogaol was attenuated after STING knockout, providing limited in vitro evidence of pathway dependence.56 The animal model was not a conventional short-course AKI model, and molecular docking indicates only a potential binding mode; it does not establish direct engagement of STING.57

Total tanshinones and flavonoids extracted from Epimedium interfered with STING–IRF3 binding and formation of the functional STING signalosome, respectively.58,59 Carnosic acid directly bound the STING C-terminal tail and prevented recruitment of TBK1.60 Together, these studies support STING as a potential molecular target of several naturally derived bioactive constituents. Whether these interactions are pharmacologically relevant in AKI remains uncertain because kidney exposure and activity in human STING systems have not yet been demonstrated.

Natural Product-Based Delivery Systems

Silk fibroin peptide nanofibers, micelles, and polysaccharide nanoparticles have been used to improve the solubility and in vivo exposure of selected natural products in AKI models.61–67 All included delivery-system studies initiated treatment before or concurrently with injury, and none established pathway dependence. Reporting of empty-carrier controls and pharmacokinetic data was also incomplete in several studies. Fucoidan–ferulic acid nanoparticles were the only included system for which higher plasma and kidney concentrations were reported, and these measurements were limited to a single time point.66 Post-injury treatment, quantitative tissue-distribution studies, and safety assessment remain priorities. Most studies reported parallel improvements in mitochondrial or DNA injury, cGAS–STING-associated markers, and kidney outcomes. However, only the Yi-Shen-Xie-Zhuo formula and 6-shogaol studies included limited in vitro tests of pathway dependence, whereas pharmacokinetic characterization and functional validation in human systems were largely absent. The evidence therefore supports kidney-protective effects in selected preclinical models, but it does not establish cGAS–STING signaling as a necessary mediator of these effects across interventions or disease settings.

Direct-binding evidence for a candidate constituent of Nephropathy II decoction and for carnosic acid was obtained in fibrosis and nonrenal models, respectively. Both observations still require confirmation in acute-phase AKI and in human-relevant systems. As of the search date, we identified no clinical study showing that any of these interventions improves kidney outcomes in patients with AKI through modulation of cGAS–STING. Accordingly, the term “renoprotection” in this review refers only to findings from cellular or animal studies.

Evidence Needed for Translation

Mechanistic Causality and Human Validation

The validation strategy needs to match the proposed site of action. For interventions thought to limit mtDNA release or act upstream of cGAS, informative experiments include cytosolic mtDNA quantification, mtDNA depletion and reconstitution, and bypass with exogenous 2′,3′-cGAMP. Claims of direct STING targeting require re-expression of wild-type STING and relevant binding-site mutants, supported by biophysical binding or intracellular target engagement assays. Cell-type-specific cGAS or STING models in tubular, myeloid, and endothelial compartments would help identify where the pathway is functionally required and whether that requirement changes across disease stages.

Human evidence can be developed sequentially, from associations in body fluids to tissue and cellular localization and, ultimately, functional testing. Plasma or urinary mtDNA, cGAMP, and interferon-stimulated genes may support exploratory stratification, but their association with cGAS–STING activity must be distinguished from signaling through other DNA sensors, including TLR9.30 Patient-derived tubular cells, kidney organoids, and tissue explants offer systems in which to test candidate mechanisms and treatment responses directly.

Product Quality, in vivo Exposure, and Safety

Reproducibility of formulas and extracts depends on adequate reporting of botanical identity and authentication, processing methods, extraction procedures, batch-to-batch quality control, and chemical fingerprints. Frameworks such as the ConPhyMP guidelines can be combined with serum pharmacochemistry and metabolite identification to determine which parent compounds or metabolites reach plasma and kidney tissue.72 Reporting of animal experiments should also follow ARRIVE 2.0 and include sample-size justification, randomization, blinding, exclusion criteria, treatment timing, and appropriate representation of both sexes or a clear justification for using a single sex.73

Without exposure data from plasma, relevant kidney compartments, and the target cell population, it is difficult to determine whether an intervention reaches its proposed site of action at pharmacologically meaningful concentrations. A single-time-point measurement of kidney concentration cannot substitute for full pharmacokinetic profiling, exposure–response analysis, or confirmation of effective intracellular exposure.74 Injectable and nanomedicine formulations also require evaluation of complement activation, hepatic and splenic uptake, immune responses after repeated administration, and material clearance.69,75 Complex formulas, extracts, and natural compounds warrant systematic assessment for nephrotoxicity, hepatotoxicity, impurities or contaminants, and interactions with commonly used antimicrobials, chemotherapeutics, immunosuppressants, and anticoagulants.

Therapeutic Window and Clinical Study Design

Because cGAS–STING contributes to antimicrobial innate immunity and immune recognition of immunogenic tumors, it cannot be regarded simply as a unidirectional “harmful switch.”76,77 Regimens initiated after kidney injury has developed are particularly important because they more closely reflect clinical treatment. Comparisons between inflammatory and repair phases may also reveal stage-dependent effects. In sepsis- and tumor-associated models, kidney protection needs to be assessed alongside infection control, antitumor efficacy, and overall immune function to identify risks associated with prolonged systemic inhibition.

Clinical development will require stratification by AKI etiology, treatment window, and modifiable biological features. Sepsis-, ischemia-, cisplatin-, and contrast-associated AKI differ in initiating mechanisms and concomitant care and should not be pooled without appropriate stratification. Early studies may focus on predictable high-risk settings and incorporate persistent kidney dysfunction, major adverse kidney events, patient-centered outcomes, and safety endpoints.78–80 Before clinical testing, a candidate intervention should ideally be supported by clinically relevant models, efficacy after injury has developed, evidence that the proposed mechanism is functionally required, adequate in vivo exposure, a defined safety profile, and validation in human-relevant systems. These considerations describe the completeness of the translational evidence rather than fixed criteria for initiating clinical development.

Limitations of This Review

This review focused on PubMed/MEDLINE and reference tracking and did not systematically search Embase, Web of Science, or Chinese-language databases. Relevant TCM studies not indexed in PubMed may therefore have been missed. As a structured narrative review, our evidence framework compares model relevance, causal strength, and translational gaps; it does not replace dual-reviewer screening, formal risk-of-bias assessment, or quantitative synthesis. The descriptive summary of 12 animal studies is restricted to the defined eligibility criteria. Animal sex, sample size, randomization, blinding, and exclusion criteria were recorded as reported, and unreported items were not inferred. Substantial heterogeneity remains across models, cell lines, STING interventions, and pathway measurements, and both independent replication and validation in human systems remain limited.

Conclusions

The mtDNA–cGAS–STING axis links mitochondrial injury with inflammatory amplification and maladaptive repair in AKI, but its contribution varies by etiology, cell type, and disease stage. Cisplatin, sepsis, and ischemia–reperfusion models provide functional evidence of differing strength, while ferroptosis, lactylation, and lipid metabolic reprogramming have broadened the mechanistic framework. Causal evidence in human systems remains limited, as does the specificity of fluid biomarkers for intrarenal pathway activity.

Studies of TCM formulas and preparations, bioactive fractions and extracts, isolated natural products, and natural product-based delivery systems mainly support kidney protection accompanied by pathway-associated changes in selected preclinical models. They do not establish clinical efficacy or general in vivo dependence on cGAS–STING. Future studies should move beyond preventive designs and establish pathway causality, reproducible product characterization, clinically relevant kidney and target-cell exposure, safety, and activity in human-relevant systems.
