VN-EGNN: E(3)- and SE(3)-Equivariant Graph Neural Networks with Virtual Nodes Enhance Protein Binding Site Identification
Authors: Florian Sestak, Lisa Schneckenreiter, Johannes Brandstetter, Sepp Hochreiter, Andreas Mayr, Günter Klambauer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We present VN-EGNN, a novel approach to binding site identification that significantly advances predictive performance. By integrating virtual nodes into E(n)– and SE(n)-equivariant graph neural networks (EGNNs) and extending the message-passing scheme, we address limitations of traditional GNNs in modeling complex geometric entities such as binding pockets and at the same time get neural representations of binding sites. Our extensive experiments demonstrate that VN-EGNN sets a new state-of-the-art in locating binding site centers on the COACH420, HOLO4K, and PDBbind2020 datasets, showcasing a marked improvement in the DCC/DCA success rates over existing methods. These results underscore the potential of VN-EGNN in drug discovery and protein-ligand interaction studies.

Introduction

With the advent of AlphaFold [1, 2], millions of 3D structures of proteins have been unlocked for further investigation by the scientific community [3, 4]. The 3D structure of a protein can provide crucial information about its function, and drug discovery is one of the most important fields that profits from these 3D structures [5–7]. It has been envisioned that the availability of 3D structures will allow to purposefully design drugs that alter protein function in a desired way. To enable structure-based drug design, further computational methods such as docking and binding site identification need to be employed [8–10]. Docking predicts where a known small molecule, or ligand, binds within a protein’s active site (see, e.g., Shan et al. [11]). In contrast, binding site identification locates regions on the protein likely to form pockets that interact with unknown ligands [12]. These tasks are fundamentally different: most proteins lack known ligands, making binding site identification essential for uncovering protein function, guiding drug design, and identifying potential targets. Both approaches have seen major advances through deep learning, particularly geometric deep learning [13–19].

The identification of binding sites relies on the successful combination of physical, chemical and geometric information. Initially, machine learning methods for binding site prediction were based on carefully designed input features due to their tabular processing structure. For instance, FPocket [20] relies on Voronoi tessellation and alpha spheres [21] and additionally takes an electronegativity criterion into account. P2Rank, a random-forest-based method, makes use of the protein surface [22]. With the advent of end-to-end deep learning, the need for manually crafted input features has diminished. This progress is largely due to advances in convolutional neural networks [23] and graph neural networks [24–28], which are capable of learning feature representations directly from data. These advances have contributed to refinements in predictive modeling across various molecular tasks; some studies have applied explainability techniques to aid interpretation of model behavior, as in Kotobi et al. [29]. Note that graph-based machine learning techniques have been explored for various biomedical tasks, including drug repositioning, disease association prediction, and modeling molecular interactions such as lncRNA–miRNA relationships [30–33].

For instance, binding site prediction has increasingly adopted deep learning-based approaches, following the broader move toward learning directly from data. DeepSite [34] is a voxel-based 3D convolutional neural network for binding site prediction. Convolutional operations on the 3D space are, however, computationally very demanding and so quickly other approaches to tackle binding site identification were developed, e.g., DeepSurf [35] or PointSite [36]. DeepSurf operates on surface-based representations and places several voxelized grids on the protein’s surface, while PointSite is based on a form of sparse convolutions to reduce the computational overhead and keep sparse regions in the 3D space sparse. Convolutional networks, however, often struggle with binding site identification. This is likely due to the irregular shapes of protein structures and the fact that proteins can be arbitrarily rotated and shifted in 3D space [37]. To overcome these challenges, geometric deep learning methods have gained traction. Among them, group-equivariant architectures, particularly graph-based approaches like EquiPocket [37], have demonstrated strong performance. These architectures are designed to be equivariant to Euclidean transformations in 3D space (E(3)). As a result, models built on such architectures further allow to retrieve predictions that are equivariant (or invariant) to rotations and translations, making them especially well-suited for binding site prediction.

We follow earlier approaches (see top left of Fig. 1) and treat binding site identification as a segmentation task. Unlike voxel grid-based methods, our approach builds upon E(n)-equivariant graph neural networks (EGNNs), as proposed by Satorras et al. [27]. We enhance these with virtual nodes and a tailored message passing scheme to mitigate oversquashing (see Sect. A.1 [SI]). Ultimately, this extension yields useful neural representations of binding sites.

At the input layer the coordinates represent the physical positions of atoms or residues, while the node features may encode properties such as atom type or chemical characteristics.

As the network progresses through its layers, the representations become more abstract: the coordinates evolve into what one might refer to as neural coordinates, and the node features into (additional) neural features. Although these intermediate representations lack direct physical interpretation, the network’s update equations are carefully designed to preserve E(3)/SE(3)-equivariance.

In contrast to methods such as MACE [38], Nequip [39], or SEGNN [40], EGNNs operate on scalar features, e.g., distances, and use scale operations for coordinate updates. Thus, EGNNs operate efficiently [41] without resorting to compute-expensive higher order features, and, most importantly, allow for efficient coordinate update of virtual nodes. To construct a method for binding site identification (detailed information on the representation of binding sites is provided in Sect. B.3 [SI]), we first consider this task as a semantic segmentation problem, with the goal of predicting whether a specific node in the graph can be classified as a binding site node or not. Thereby nodes representing atoms/residues within a certain radius to experimentally observed protein binding ligands are marked as true binding site nodes in contrast to the remaining nodes (see bottom left part in Fig. 1 - magenta vs. turquoise nodes). Given that setting, binding site identification is considered to be a node-level binary classification task and thus a semantic segmentation problem.

Virtual Nodes to Alleviate Oversquashing

Joshi et al. [42] investigated the expressive power of EGNNs in greater detail and argue that these networks can suffer from oversquashing, which hinders their ability to capture long-range dependencies. Long-range dependencies are however a critical factor to consider in protein prediction tasks, and to mitigate oversquashing in EGNNs, we enrich \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal {P}}$$\end{document}P with virtual nodes to obtain an enriched graph \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal {G}}$$\end{document}G and adapt the associated message passing algorithm for EGNNs. Thereby, virtual nodes are characterized by having edges to and from all other non-virtual nodes in the graph, i.e., each virtual node has an edge to and from all protein atoms/residues (the physical entities). It is noteworthy to mention that in line with the EGNN idea, also the newly inserted virtual nodes have 3D coordinates. The new adapted message passing scheme for EGNNs with virtual nodes is visualized in the right part of Fig. 1 and makes up the first part of our suggested binding site prediction method "Virtual-Node Equivariant GNN" (VN-EGNN). It was however a priori unclear, whether the 3D coordinates associated to the virtual nodes would be meaningful for the task at hand. Virtual Nodes form Abstract Binding Site Representations

A first experiment (see Fig. 2), where we assigned random coordinates to the virtual nodes and trained the network to minimize semantic segmentation loss using observed experimental training data, demonstrated that the virtual node locations converged towards the actual physical binding positions of ligands on the protein. This inspired us in learning to predict the locations of virtual nodes directly by extending the semantic segmentation loss objective \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal {L}}_{\textrm{segm}}$$\end{document}Lsegm with an additional location loss objective \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal {L}}_{\textrm{bsc}}$$\end{document}Lbsc, which aims for minimizing the distances to observed ligand binding site centers, and makes up the second building block of VN-EGNN.

Altogether the composite objective of VN-EGNN is (1) to correctly classify whether a node is within a certain radius of a region to which potential ligands can bind (segmentation problem), and, (2) to predict the coordinates of binding site centers (geometric location prediction). This new objective now also suggests that virtual nodes are to be considered a direct representation of binding sites. The other (non-coordinate) features, which are associated to virtual nodes may therefore be considered to form an abstract neural representation of a protein binding site.

Section A.3 [SI] provides a comprehensive overview and positioning of VN-EGNN as a method for binding site identification.

Initialization of Virtual Nodes in VN-EGNN

In contrast to physical nodes it is not a-priori clear how to initialize the neural 3D coordinates and additional neural properties of virtual nodes. A desirable property is that VN-EGNN predictions are in general E(3)/SE(3)-invariant. This means that if the input protein is rotated or translated in 3D space, the predicted binding sites should rotate or translate accordingly. In other words, the predictions should remain consistent with the geometry of the protein, regardless of its orientation. This then also requires that virtual nodes are initialized equivariantly with respect to E(3)/SE(3) transformations of the protein. This means that when the protein is rotated or translated in 3D space, the initial positions of the virtual nodes must transform in the same way. Note, that it is possible, to equivariantly initialize virtual nodes. To do so, we can initialize the coordinates of the virtual nodes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{z}^0_k$$\end{document}zk0 at the center of mass (see Fig. 3, left), concretely the average coordinates of the physical nodes [43, 44], while the initial features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{v}^0_k$$\end{document}vk0 are learned feature vectors that are pairwise distinct. These choices lead to maintenance of the equivariance properties, but guarantee that the virtual nodes are updated differently during message passing.

Note, that the center of mass is not necessarily a very meaningful value for binding site identification, although it ensures equivariant binding site predictions. In practice, it might restrict the architecture quite a lot. In line with new developments such as Abramson et al. [1], we relaxed the initialization procedure to specifically exploit our prior knowledge, that ligands might tend to bind to surface areas of proteins. In detail, our relaxed VN-EGNN initialization procedure distributes the K virtual nodes evenly across a sphere using a Fibonacci grid [45], of which the radius is defined as the distance between the protein center and its most distant atom. The virtual node properties \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{v}^0_k$$\end{document}vk0 are initialized by averaging over the initial features \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{h}^0_i$$\end{document}hi0. This procedure is simple and efficient (see Fig. 3, right).

To prevent virtual nodes from starting at identical locations during different epochs of training, we randomly rotate the sphere with the initial locations of virtual nodes. This can be considered a data augmentation approach and should prevent overfitting the learned model to the training data. Equivalently, we could alternatively randomly rotate the protein itself.

A further alternative virtual node coordinate initialization strategy is detailed in Sect. B.4 [SI], a justification of our Fibonacci Grid based initialization approach can be found in Sect. Self-Confidence Module

Experiments

Datasets

We applied VN-EGNN to four datasets, namely scPDB [48], PDBbind [49], COACH420, and HOLO4K, which were previously utilized by other methods for binding site identification, to evaluate its efficacy in identifying binding sites. The main characteristics of these datasets can be found in Sect. D.3 [SI]. For comprehensive data preparation across these datasets, we utilized versions, from which solvent atoms were excluded and erroneous structures have been removed.

We observe a domain shift in the HOLO4K dataset compared to scPDB, COACH420, and PDBBind2020. HOLO4K, frequently used as a benchmarking dataset [22], exhibits notable differences. Specifically, the number of chains per sample in HOLO4K is greater than in the other datasets (see Fig. 4, left). Furthermore, the number of binding sites per entry is higher in HOLO4K (see Fig. 4, right). These observations can be attributed to the presence of numerous proteins or protein complexes in HOLO4K, many of which are composed of multiple individual polypeptide chains. Model Training & Inference

Our model architecture includes five VN-EGNN layers, in which each layer is implemented according to our suggested heterogeneous message passing scheme as detailed above. In all layers, the feature and message dimensions are consistently set to 100. We apply layer normalization and Dropout [50] in each message passing layer. The SiLU activation function [51] is used across all layers. Analogous to Pei et al. [52], we apply scaling (dividing by 5) and re-scaling (multiplying by 5) on the coordinates and use the Huber loss [53] for the coordinates (which empirically showed to be slightly more effective). In our pipeline, we employ the AdamW optimizer [54] for a total of 1500 epochs and select the best checkpoint based on performance on the validation dataset. The learning rate is set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-3}$$\end{document}10-3. After 100 epochs, we reduce the learning rate by a factor of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-1}$$\end{document}10-1 if the model does not improve for 10 epochs. Further details on hyperparameters can be found in Sect. D.5 [SI].

For the initial residue node features, we use pre-trained ESM-2 residue embeddings [55], following Corso et al. [13] and Pei et al. [52]. For virtual nodes, we derive their features by averaging the residue node features across the entire protein. A linear layer maps these input features to the initial VN-EGNN features (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{h}^0_n$$\end{document}hn0, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{v}_k^0$$\end{document}vk0).

We use the position of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α-carbons as residue node locations. Virtual nodes are connected to all residue nodes but not to each other. Their coordinate initializations are as described above (uniformly across the surface of a sphere by a Fibonacci grid + rotations).

To account for the possibility of different virtual nodes converging at identical locations, we use the Mean Shift Algorithm [56] during inference to cluster virtual nodes that are in close spatial proximity. Within each cluster, we determine representative self-confidence scores and positions by averaging those of the virtual nodes, treating the cluster as a single entity. Given the large complexes in HOLO4K, we process each chain individually using VN-EGNN and then merge the predicted pocket centers.

Binding Site Prediction Evaluation

The accurate evaluation of binding site predictions is crucial for advancing our understanding of molecular interactions and improving predictive models. To assess the performance of our approach, we rely on DCC and DCA success rates, which are widely recognized in the field of binding site identification (see e.g., Chen et al. [57]).

DCC measures the distances between predicted and known binding site centers, while DCA measures the shortest distance between predicted binding site centers and any atom of the ligands. Predictions are deemed successful if they meet a predefined spatial threshold for both DCC and DCA. The DCC/DCA success rate is defined as the ratio of successful predictions to the total number of true (i.e., annotated) binding sites. For our experiments, we adopt a threshold of 4 Å consistent with previous studies. Specifically, Stepniewska-Dziubinska et al. [58] applies this threshold to compute a DCC success rate, while Zhang et al. [37] uses it to compute both a DCC success rate and a DCA success rate.

In line with Chen et al. [37, 57] and Stepniewska-Dziubinska et al. [58], we only consider the top M predicted binding sites for each protein, selected based on their highest self-confidence scores \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat{c}_k}$$\end{document}c^k. Thereby, M corresponds to the number of known binding sites for the protein. Each known binding site is then subsequently aligned with the closest predicted binding site, and the DCC/DCA success rate is calculated based on this alignment.

On COACH420, VN-EGNN exhibits the best DCA score and on PDBbind it yields the same DCA score as P2Rank. Note that there is limited comparability with P2rank, as this method utilizes a different training set, which is potentially more similar to the HOLO4K dataset. As previously outlined, HOLO4K contains many proteins or protein complexes, which are composed of individual polypeptide chains. This represents a significant domain shift in the training data, posing challenges for all methods, except for P2rank.

Analysis of VN-EGNN Predictions

We show exemplary visualizations of our model’s predictions in Fig. 7.Fig. 7Upper Row. This visualization, created using PyMOL, illustrates the binding site prediction using a trained VN-EGNN model. The protein is depicted with annotated binding sites shown as violet protein regions, alongside its known (ground truth) ligand molecules highlighted in violet as well (PDB: 1MXI-A). Yellow spheres represent initial positions of the VN-EGNN virtual nodes. Following L message-passing steps according to the VN-EGNN heterogeneous message-passing scheme, the model generates preliminary predictions for the positions of these virtual nodes. These preliminary predictions are then clustered, with the cluster centers serving as the final binding center position predictions, which are visualized as violet spheres. Self-confidence scores are displayed adjacent to the virtual node predictions. Arrows in the visualization represent positional changes from the initial virtual node positions (yellow spheres) to preliminary VN-EGNN predictions and highlight the shifts achieved through the message-passing process. For improved visualization, only a subset of the initial virtual node positions is shown. The visualization illustrates that our model effectively distributes virtual nodes across space, demonstrating strong alignment with ground truth binding site positions. Lower Row. Similar to the Upper Row, predictions for two further proteins (PDB: 1ODI and 3LPK) are shown, but confidence scores are skipped and annotated binding sites are not shown as violet protein regions

Insights from Ablation Experiments

The experimental results of ablating VN-EGNN key components are shown in Tab. 2. The results from these ablations demonstrate that plain EGNN consistently performs the worst among all tested configurations. Each key component introduced in VN-EGNN appears to enhance performance, with the most substantial improvements attributed to the integration of virtual nodes and the heterogeneous message-passing scheme together with ESM-2 embeddings, replacing homogenous messag-passing with ESM-2 embeddings. Overall, these results highlight the importance of the core innovations in VN-EGNN. Discussion and Conclusions

Main findings Our results indicate that VN-EGNN, a geometric Deep Learning method with SE(3)-equivariance, is the most accurate method for protein binding site identification. Our novel method VN-EGNN extends EGNNs with virtual nodes and a heterogeneous message passing scheme. These new assets improve the learning dynamics by ameliorating the oversquashing problem and enable the learning of representations of hidden geometric entities. Concretely, our experiments demonstrate that VN-EGNN exhibits high predictive performance in terms of DCC and DCA and sets a new state-of-the-art on COACH420, HOLO4K and PDBbind2020. We attribute our improvement to the direct prediction of binding site centers, rather than inferring them from the geometric center of segmented areas, a common practice in previous methods. Relying on segmentation can lead to inaccuracies, especially if a single erroneous prediction impacts the calculated center. Overall, VN-EGNN yields highly accurate predictions of binding site centers.

Comparison with previous work In contrast to previous methods [35, 37], which primarily utilized surface information, based on Sanner et al. [64, 65], or methods that operated on atom-level information [34, 58, 59], our approach exclusively relies on residue-level information, specifically using \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α-carbons as physical nodes. This strategy significantly enhances computational efficiency during both training and inference due to the reduced size of the input graphs. Our results support the finding by Jumper et al. [2], that residue-level information inherently includes all relevant side-chain conformations.

Broader Significance VN-EGNN is currently limited to predicting binding pockets of proteins similar to those in PDB. However, those predicted binding sites can be used as proposal regions for other methods, which could lead to improved performance and efficiency for docking methods.

Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, VN-EGNN is the first approach that explicitly models binding sites in a way that allows them to be characterized by vector representations. These representations appear to be particularly beneficial for downstream tasks, such as blind docking [66].
