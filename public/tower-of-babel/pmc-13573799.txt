Multimodal Spinal Anesthesia in Somalia: Evidence, Implementation Considerations, and a Safety Framework for Resource-Limited Settings — A Narrative Review
Authors: Hodo Aideed Asowe, Samira Abdullahi Mo’alim, Mohamed Hussein Adam, Ahmed Mahad Sheikh Mohamed, Mohamed Adam Mahamud, Bashiru Garba, Jamal Hassan Mohamoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSafe anesthesia remains difficult to deliver in many resource-limited settings because of shortages of trained providers, monitoring equipment, oxygen, essential medicines, recovery facilities, and critical-care support. Spinal anesthesia is widely used for cesarean delivery and selected lower abdominal, urologic, perineal, and lower-limb procedures. Its contemporary use extends beyond the intrathecal injection to include patient selection, dose planning, hemodynamic management, multimodal analgesia, monitoring, postoperative observation, and rescue readiness.ObjectiveThis narrative review examines the available evidence, implementation considerations, and minimum safety requirements for multimodal spinal anesthesia in Somalia and comparable resource-limited settings.MethodsA structured narrative literature search was conducted using PubMed/MEDLINE, Google Scholar, ScienceDirect, Cochrane-related sources, WHO and WFSA resources, and relevant global surgery literature. Sources addressing spinal or neuraxial anesthesia, obstetric anesthesia, multimodal analgesia, anesthesia safety, low-resource surgery, and Somalia’s anesthesia and critical-care context were considered. Evidence was synthesized thematically and categorized as published clinical evidence, international safety standards, contextual interpretation, or proposed implementation recommendations.FindingsSpinal anesthesia may reduce dependence on some components of general anesthesia infrastructure for appropriately selected patients, but it is not inherently safe. Minimum requirements include a trained provider, patient assessment, blood-pressure and oxygen-saturation monitoring, intravenous access, oxygen, vasopressors, airway rescue equipment, postoperative observation, and protocols for hypotension, failed block, high spinal block, and conversion to general anesthesia. Introduction

Safe surgery depends on safe anesthesia. This is simple to say, but difficult to achieve in many low-resource settings where hospitals may have shortages of trained anesthesia providers, oxygen, monitoring equipment, blood products, essential medicines, recovery-room staff, and intensive care support. The global surgery literature has shown that lack of access to safe, affordable surgical and anesthesia care remains a major public health problem, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.1 In such settings, the choice of anesthetic technique is not only a clinical decision; it is also a health-system decision.2 Spinal anesthesia is one of the oldest and most widely used regional anesthesia techniques. Traditionally, it has been used for operations below the umbilicus, including cesarean section, lower-limb orthopedic surgery, urologic surgery, lower abdominal surgery, and some trauma procedures.3

In this review, multimodal spinal anesthesia refers to an integrated perioperative pathway in which the spinal block is combined with structured patient selection, appropriate dose planning, prevention and treatment of hypotension, selective use of intrathecal or systemic adjuncts, opioid-sparing postoperative analgesia, monitoring, patient communication, recovery observation, and readiness to manage block failure, high spinal block, or conversion to general anesthesia. It therefore represents a broader safety and perioperative-care pathway rather than an intrathecal injection alone.3

Although the technique is old, its role is not outdated. Modern practice increasingly treats spinal anesthesia as part of a broader multimodal perioperative plan rather than as a single injection of local anesthetic. This includes careful patient selection, correct dosing, use of suitable adjuvants, prevention and treatment of hypotension, multimodal postoperative analgesia, monitoring, patient education, and readiness to manage complications.3 In low-resource settings, spinal anesthesia may offer several practical advantages. For selected patients and procedures, it can reduce the need for airway instrumentation, inhalational anesthetic agents, ventilators, and complex anesthesia machines. It can also provide reliable surgical anesthesia and early postoperative analgesia. Regional and neuraxial anesthesia have therefore been described as valuable approaches in resource-limited environments when used safely and appropriately.4 However, spinal anesthesia is not automatically safe because it is simple or inexpensive. It still requires trained providers, sterile technique, pulse oximetry, blood pressure monitoring, intravenous access, oxygen, vasopressors, resuscitation drugs, and a plan for failed or high spinal block.5

Somalia is a particularly relevant setting for this review. Recent workforce evidence shows that Somalia has a severe shortage of surgical, anesthesia, and obstetric providers. Omar et al6 reported only 22 anesthesia specialists in Somalia, equal to approximately 0.2 anesthesia specialists per 100,000 population. Omar et al documented a severe shortage of surgical, anesthesia, and obstetric personnel in Somalia, including only 22 anesthesia specialists, equivalent to approximately 0.2 specialists per 100,000 population.6 Mohamed and Garba subsequently described continuing limitations in oxygen availability, monitoring, intensive-care readiness, emergency preparedness, and referral systems.7 These challenges make it important to identify anesthesia strategies that are safe, realistic, and adaptable to the Somali context. Multimodal spinal anesthesia may be useful for essential surgical care, especially cesarean delivery, trauma procedures, lower-limb operations, urologic surgery, wound debridement, and selected lower abdominal procedures. Still, it should not be promoted as a shortcut around weak systems. The goal should be to strengthen spinal anesthesia as one safe option within a broader surgical and anesthesia safety framework.

Patient understanding also matters. A 2025 study from Mogadishu found important gaps in patients’ knowledge of anesthesia and the anesthesiologist’s role, showing the need for better preoperative counseling and culturally appropriate patient education in Somalia.8 This is especially relevant for spinal anesthesia because patients are often awake during surgery and may feel anxious if the procedure is not clearly explained beforehand.8 Despite the widespread use of spinal anesthesia, Somalia lacks published multicenter data on block failure, spinal-induced hypotension, high spinal block, conversion to general anesthesia, postoperative pain, respiratory complications, neurologic symptoms, unplanned referral, and patient satisfaction. The country also lacks a nationally validated implementation pathway defining minimum staffing, monitoring, rescue, recovery, and audit requirements for spinal anesthesia. This review therefore does not assess the effectiveness of an existing Somali protocol. Instead, it synthesizes international and Somalia-specific evidence to identify minimum implementation requirements, contextual priorities, evidence limitations, and areas requiring local validation.6,7,9,10 The review is primarily intended for anesthesia clinicians and clinical educators, while its implementation, infrastructure, and audit recommendations are also relevant to hospital administrators, professional bodies, and health policymakers.

This narrative review aims to synthesize current evidence on multimodal spinal anesthesia, examine its potential application in Somalia, identify the minimum safety and implementation requirements for its use, and propose priorities for local audit and future validation.

Methods

Literature Search Strategy

This narrative review examined available evidence on multimodal spinal anesthesia and its potential application in Somalia and similar resource-limited settings. A structured search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Google Scholar, ScienceDirect, Cochrane-related sources, and resources from the World Health Organization and the World Federation of Societies of Anaesthesiologists. The final search was completed in May 2026.

Search terms included combinations of “spinal anesthesia”, “neuraxial anesthesia”, “multimodal analgesia”, “intrathecal adjuvants”, “cesarean delivery”, “spinal-induced hypotension”, “failed spinal anesthesia”, “anesthesia safety”, “low-resource settings”, “Somalia”, “anesthesia workforce”, and “critical care capacity”. Boolean operators AND and OR were used to combine terms, and reference lists of relevant reviews, guidelines, and Somalia-specific studies were also screened.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Eligible sources included original studies, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews, professional guidelines, international safety standards, health-system assessments, and authoritative reports. Publications were included when they addressed spinal or neuraxial anesthesia, obstetric anesthesia, intrathecal adjuvants, monitoring, hypotension, postoperative care, workforce capacity, patient education, or implementation in low-resource settings.

Sources were excluded when they were unrelated to the review question, duplicated another publication, lacked sufficient methodological or clinical detail, or consisted of unsupported opinion. Somalia-specific evidence was prioritized whenever available, while broader international evidence was used when local clinical data were limited.

Source Selection and Evidence Appraisal

The searches identified 80 records: 58 through database searching and 22 through additional sources, including WHO and WFSA resources, reference-list searching, and Somalia-specific publications. After removing 14 duplicates, 66 records were screened by title and abstract. Thirty-two records were excluded because they were outside the review scope. The full texts of 34 articles and reports were assessed for eligibility, of which 18 were excluded because they were not directly relevant, provided insufficient methodological or clinical detail, had limited applicability to the review setting, or met other exclusion criteria. Sixteen publications were included in the final narrative synthesis. The selection process is presented in Figure 1.

The 16 included publications comprised five original studies, two systematic reviews or meta-analyses, five narrative or methodological reviews, two international guidelines or safety standards, one global surgery report, and one Somalia-focused commentary.

SANRA and Thematic Synthesis

The SANRA framework was used to strengthen the structure, transparency, referencing, scientific reasoning, and reporting quality of the review. It was not used as a study-level risk-of-bias tool.

Relevant information was organized thematically around the clinical role of spinal anesthesia, minimum safety and monitoring requirements, cesarean delivery, hypotension management, intrathecal adjuvants, provider competence, patient education, facility readiness, and local audit priorities. Published evidence, international safety standards, contextual interpretation, and author-proposed recommendations were considered separately to avoid presenting contextual proposals as locally validated clinical guidance.

Somalia-Specific Evidence

Somalia-specific sources mainly described workforce shortages, critical-care limitations, emergency readiness, hospital infrastructure, and patient understanding of anesthesia. No detailed local studies were identified reporting spinal failure, hypotension, high spinal block, conversion to general anesthesia, postoperative pain, respiratory complications, neurologic outcomes, or patient satisfaction. The recommendations should therefore be understood as an evidence-informed implementation framework rather than a validated national guideline.11

Statistical Analysis

No statistical analysis, meta-analysis, pooled effect estimation, or statistical modelling was conducted because this was a narrative review and no primary dataset was collected.

Quantitative results from individual studies were reported descriptively where relevant. Findings were not pooled because the included publications differed substantially in study design, patient population, procedure, intervention, outcome definitions, and healthcare setting.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval and informed consent were not required because this narrative review was based entirely on previously published literature and publicly available documents. No human participants were recruited, no clinical intervention was conducted, and no identifiable patient information was collected or reported.

Key Thematic Findings

Spinal Anesthesia Remains Relevant, but Its Role Has Changed

The reviewed literature shows that spinal anesthesia remains clinically important, especially for surgery below the umbilicus. However, its modern value lies in being part of a planned perioperative pathway3 described multimodal spinal anesthesia as an evolving technique supported by advances in spinal needles, local anesthetics, intrathecal adjuvants, ultrasound assistance, and enhanced recovery principles. In practice, multimodal spinal anesthesia means that the provider does more than perform the block. The provider must assess the patient, choose an appropriate dose, anticipate hypotension, prepare vasopressors, confirm monitoring, plan postoperative analgesia, communicate with the surgical team, and prepare for rescue if the block fails. This approach makes spinal anesthesia safer and more predictable.

Low-Resource Settings May Benefit from Spinal Anesthesia, but Only with Basic Safety Systems

The evidence supports spinal anesthesia as a useful option in low-resource settings because it may reduce reliance on general anesthesia infrastructure for selected procedures4 noted that regional and neuraxial anesthesia can be effective, low-cost, and suitable for many common operations in resource-limited environments.

However, the evidence also makes clear that spinal anesthesia cannot replace a weak safety system. A spinal anesthetic may avoid intubation, but it does not remove the need for pulse oximetry, blood pressure monitoring, oxygen, intravenous access, emergency drugs, airway backup, and trained personnel. Evidence from cesarean-delivery studies indicates that selected intrathecal adjuvants, including neuraxial opioids, can prolong postoperative analgesia; however, their use should be matched to each facility’s capacity for postoperative observation and management of adverse effects.5 This is an important finding for Somalia. Spinal anesthesia may be practical, but it must be supported by minimum safety standards. Otherwise, a low-cost technique can become high risk.

Obstetric Surgery Is One of the Most Important Applications

Cesarean delivery is one of the clearest areas where spinal anesthesia is relevant. It avoids airway manipulation, allows the mother to remain awake, provides dense surgical anesthesia, and can support early bonding after birth. It is also often more feasible than general anesthesia in facilities with limited airway equipment or postoperative ventilation support. Post-cesarean analgesia is a major part of multimodal spinal anesthesia. Low-dose neuraxial morphine can provide effective post-cesarean analgesia as part of a multimodal regimen, but its use requires appropriate risk assessment, postoperative observation, and monitoring for sedation and respiratory depression.3 Enhanced recovery recommendations after cesarean delivery also emphasize scheduled non-opioid analgesia, early mobilization, prevention of nausea and vomiting, and patient-centered care.3

For Somalia, this is especially important because cesarean delivery is a lifesaving procedure. A safe spinal anesthesia pathway for cesarean section should include preoperative assessment, blood pressure monitoring, left uterine displacement, vasopressor readiness, oxygen availability, neonatal readiness, postoperative pain control, and clear observation after surgery.

Intrathecal Adjuvants Can Improve Analgesia, but They Must Be Used Carefully

Adjuvants such as fentanyl, morphine, clonidine, and dexmedetomidine may improve the quality or duration of spinal anesthesia. Sun et al reported that intrathecal dexmedetomidine prolonged sensory and motor block and the duration of analgesia compared with fentanyl, although its potential effects on bradycardia, hypotension, and delayed motor recovery should be considered.12 More recent evidence from cesarean delivery also suggests that intrathecal adjuvants can prolong analgesia and reduce postoperative opioid use, although certainty varies by drug and outcome.

This finding is useful, but it needs careful interpretation. In low-resource settings, the best drug is not always the drug that gives the longest analgesia. The best drug is the one that provides benefit without creating risks that the hospital cannot monitor or manage. For example, neuraxial morphine may improve post-cesarean pain control, but it also requires appropriate monitoring for sedation and respiratory depression. Dexmedetomidine may prolong analgesia, but it may also prolong motor block or contribute to bradycardia and hypotension. Therefore, adjuvant use in Somalia should be guided by local monitoring capacity, staff training, drug availability, and patient risk.

Hypotension Remains a Major Safety Concern

Spinal-induced hypotension is one of the most common and clinically important complications, especially in obstetric patients. It can cause maternal nausea, vomiting, dizziness, reduced consciousness, and potential compromise of uteroplacental perfusion. Prevention and early treatment are therefore central to safe spinal anesthesia. The reviewed evidence supports a proactive approach: assess risk, ensure intravenous access, monitor blood pressure frequently after block placement, position the patient appropriately, use fluids rationally, and keep vasopressors immediately available. In low-resource settings, hypotension protocols should be simple, visible, and practiced regularly. It is not enough to know that hypotension can happen; the team must be ready to treat it within minutes.

Checklists and Simple Safety Tools Improve Reliability

Checklists are useful in low-resource and fragile settings because they help teams avoid missing important safety steps before anesthesia and surgery.13 The WHO Surgical Safety Checklist and structured anesthesia-equipment checks can help teams confirm patient identity, functioning monitoring, oxygen and suction availability, emergency-drug readiness, and preparedness to manage anticipated complications.13,14 These simple tools also support international anesthesia safety standards, which emphasize trained providers, proper monitoring, oxygenation, circulation monitoring, essential medications, and safe handover of care.5 For Somalia, a short spinal anesthesia checklist could be a practical way to improve consistency, support teamwork, and reduce preventable risks.

Somalia Faces a Severe Anesthesia Workforce Challenge

Somalia’s anesthesia workforce gap is a central reason why this topic matters6 reported a severe shortage of surgical, anesthesia, and obstetric providers, including only 22 anesthesia specialists. The Global Anesthesia Workforce Survey demonstrates persistent international inequalities in anesthesia-provider density and workforce growth, particularly in low-resource regions.3 This does not mean that spinal anesthesia should be delegated casually. Rather, it means that Somalia needs structured training, supervision, protocols, and continuing professional development for all providers involved in anesthesia. The technique may be relatively simple to learn compared with advanced general anesthesia, but safe decision-making around spinal anesthesia requires clinical judgment and rescue skills.

Patient Education Is a Safety Issue

The 2025 Mogadishu study by Hussein et al found important gaps in patient knowledge about anesthesia and the anesthesiologist’s role. This matters because patients receiving spinal anesthesia are usually awake during surgery. Poor explanation can increase fear, mistrust, movement during surgery, refusal of spinal anesthesia, or dissatisfaction after surgery. Good counseling should be simple and direct. Patients should be told that spinal anesthesia numbs the lower body, that they may feel touch or pressure but should not feel sharp pain, that leg weakness is temporary, and that they should report pain, breathing difficulty, severe nausea, chest discomfort, or anxiety. In Somalia, this counseling should be available in Somali language and adapted to local literacy levels.6,7

Discussion

Spinal anesthesia should be framed in Somalia as a safety-dependent component of essential surgical care, not as a cheaper substitute for general anesthesia. Regional and neuraxial anesthesia can reduce dependence on complex general anesthesia infrastructure for selected operations, but international safety standards still require a trained anesthesia provider, oxygenation, monitoring, circulation support, essential medications, and safe postoperative handover.4,5 Therefore, the practical question is not simply whether spinal anesthesia can be performed, but whether it can be performed safely for the specific patient, procedure, provider, and facility.

Somalia-specific evidence makes this point urgent. Omar et al6 reported only 22 anesthesia specialists in Somalia, approximately 0.2 per 100,000 population, while Mohamed and Garba7 described continuing gaps in anesthesia workforce capacity, oxygen supply, ICU readiness, monitoring, emergency preparedness, and referral systems. A nationwide assessment of emergency and critical care services in 131 Somali hospitals found a median emergency and critical care readiness score of 0.31 and reported that more than 80% of assessed hospitals were not ready to provide emergency and critical care using a readiness threshold of 0.5.15 These findings support the main argument of this review: spinal anesthesia may be useful in Somalia, but unsafe expansion without monitoring, oxygen, vasopressors, airway rescue equipment, and recovery observation could increase preventable harm.

Cesarean delivery is the highest-impact area for standardization. Cesarean section is not a new indication for spinal anesthesia; rather, the modern contribution is the multimodal pathway around the block. A Somalia-adapted cesarean spinal pathway should include preoperative assessment, baseline blood pressure, left uterine displacement, frequent blood pressure measurement after the spinal injection, immediate availability of vasopressors, neonatal readiness, antiemetic and analgesic planning, and postoperative observation. This approach aligns the traditional clinical strengths of spinal anesthesia with contemporary multimodal practice and obstetric safety priorities.3,5,14

Intrathecal adjuvants should be introduced cautiously and only where monitoring capacity is adequate. Evidence suggests that adjuvants such as fentanyl, morphine, clonidine, and dexmedetomidine may improve block quality or prolong postoperative analgesia.12,14 However, the safest option in Somalia is not necessarily the longest-acting drug. Neuraxial morphine, sedating adjuvants, or drugs that increase bradycardia, hypotension, delayed motor recovery, nausea, vomiting, urinary retention, sedation, or respiratory depression require staff who can observe patients after surgery and respond quickly. Where recovery-room staffing or vital-sign documentation is weak, scheduled paracetamol, NSAIDs when not contraindicated, wound infiltration, and carefully selected rescue opioids may be safer first-line components of multimodal analgesia.

Implementation should be phased. The first priority is a minimum spinal anesthesia safety package: trained provider, sterile equipment, functioning pulse oximetry, non-invasive blood pressure monitoring, intravenous access, oxygen, suction, vasopressors, emergency drugs, airway rescue equipment, and observation until hemodynamic stability and motor recovery are adequate. The second priority is standardization of high-volume services, especially cesarean delivery, lower-limb surgery, urologic procedures, anorectal surgery, wound debridement, and selected lower abdominal operations. More advanced regional techniques, ultrasound-guided blocks, continuous neuraxial techniques, or routine long-acting neuraxial opioids should come later and should be introduced first in tertiary hospitals or training hubs.

Patient education and checklists are low-cost safety interventions. A Mogadishu study found important gaps in patient knowledge about anesthesia and the anesthesiologist’s role, making Somali-language counseling especially important for awake patients undergoing spinal anesthesia.8 Counseling should explain expected numbness, pressure sensations, temporary leg weakness, and warning symptoms such as pain, severe nausea, dizziness, chest discomfort, anxiety, or breathing difficulty. Similarly, surgical and anesthesia checklists can reduce missed safety steps in low-resource and conflict-affected settings.13,16

The main policy implication is that Somalia should build safer spinal anesthesia systems rather than simply increase the number of spinal anesthetics performed. Hospitals should collect a small core dataset: procedure type, spinal dose, block failure, hypotension, high spinal block, conversion to general anesthesia, postoperative pain, respiratory events, neurologic symptoms, unplanned referral, and patient satisfaction. Local audit would allow Somali hospitals to identify whether spinal anesthesia practice is becoming safer, where training is needed, and which facilities are ready for more advanced multimodal regional anesthesia pathways.

Proposed Implementation Framework for Somalia

The practical priority for Somalia is to build safer spinal anesthesia systems, not simply to increase the number of spinal anesthetics performed. Each hospital should develop a simple written protocol covering indications, contraindications, consent, patient positioning, local anesthetic dose ranges, adjuvant selection, expected block height, timing of incision, management of hypotension, failed spinal anesthesia, high spinal block, conversion to general anesthesia, postoperative analgesia, and discharge from recovery.5,16 These protocols should be supported by a short spinal anesthesia checklist confirming patient identity, consent, baseline vital signs, intravenous access, functioning pulse oximetry, blood pressure monitoring, oxygen, suction, vasopressors, emergency drugs, airway equipment, and postoperative observation before the block is performed.13,16 Training should focus on clinical judgment and rescue skills, not only needle placement. Providers should be trained to select appropriate patients, recognize contraindications, use aseptic technique, choose safe doses, monitor block height, treat hypotension promptly, communicate with awake patients, manage failed or high spinal block, and escalate early when the operation changes or the patient becomes unstable.5,6 Because Somalia has a severe shortage of anesthesia specialists, regional training hubs, supervised mentorship, simulation sessions, morbidity and mortality review, and continuing professional development are essential to make spinal anesthesia safer across tertiary, regional, and district hospitals.6,7

Patient education should also be treated as a safety intervention. Somali-language counseling should explain that spinal anesthesia numbs the lower body, that pressure or touch may be felt without sharp pain, that leg weakness is temporary, and that symptoms such as pain, severe nausea, breathing difficulty, chest discomfort, dizziness, or anxiety should be reported immediately.8 Finally, local audits should be introduced to measure spinal failure, hypotension, high spinal block, conversion to general anesthesia, postoperative pain, complications, and patient satisfaction. Without local outcome data, Somalia will not be able to know whether spinal anesthesia practice is becoming safer or merely more common.6,7

Spinal anesthesia should be adapted to the setting where it is used. In high-resource countries, spinal anesthesia is usually supported by specialist anesthesia teams, reliable oxygen, continuous monitoring, vasopressor protocols, recovery units, and formal audit systems. Somalia cannot simply copy this model immediately. Instead, the priority should be to build a safe and realistic minimum standard that can work consistently across Somali hospitals.4–7

For Somalia, the safest starting point is to strengthen single-shot spinal anesthesia for common and high-impact operations, especially cesarean delivery, lower-limb surgery, urologic procedures, anorectal surgery, wound debridement, and selected lower abdominal operations. More advanced techniques, such as epidural infusions, combined spinal-epidural anesthesia, continuous neuraxial techniques, and advanced regional blocks, should be introduced gradually, beginning in tertiary hospitals and training hubs where monitoring, oxygen, rescue drugs, trained staff, and recovery observation are more reliable.4,5 Cesarean delivery should be the first area for standardization because it is one of the most important lifesaving uses of spinal anesthesia in Somalia. A practical cesarean spinal pathway should include preoperative assessment, left uterine displacement, frequent blood pressure checks, vasopressor readiness, neonatal preparation, postoperative observation, and multimodal pain control. Intrathecal opioids and other adjuvants may be helpful, but they should be used only in facilities that can monitor patients safely after surgery.3,14

Overall, Somalia should use spinal anesthesia carefully and strategically. The goal is not simply to perform more spinal anesthetics, but to make each spinal anesthetic safer. This requires basic monitoring, trained providers, clear protocols, careful drug selection, patient education, and local audit before moving toward more advanced techniques. Limitations

This review has several limitations. First, as a narrative review, it provides a structured thematic synthesis rather than pooled effect estimates or a formal study-level risk-of-bias assessment. Selection and interpretation bias therefore remain possible despite the structured search process and use of the SANRA reporting framework.

Second, Somalia-specific evidence on spinal anesthesia practice and outcomes remains limited. Available Somali studies primarily describe anesthesia workforce capacity, emergency and critical-care readiness, and patients’ knowledge of anesthesia. They do not provide multicentre estimates of spinal failure, spinal-induced hypotension, high spinal block, conversion to general anesthesia, postoperative pain, respiratory complications, neurological outcomes, or patient satisfaction.

Third, the proposed implementation framework has not been prospectively evaluated in Somali hospitals. It should therefore be understood as an evidence-informed framework for local adaptation and assessment rather than as a validated national guideline.

Fourth, health facilities in Somalia differ considerably in staffing, monitoring capacity, oxygen availability, medication supply, recovery services, and referral pathways. Recommendations suitable for a tertiary hospital in Mogadishu may not be immediately applicable to regional, district, rural, humanitarian, or conflict-affected facilities.

Fifth, much of the clinical evidence concerning intrathecal adjuvants, enhanced recovery, postoperative monitoring, and advanced regional techniques comes from settings with stronger staffing, monitoring, and recovery systems. These findings may not be directly transferable to facilities where postoperative observation and rescue capacity are limited.

Finally, the review primarily included English-language and publicly accessible sources. Relevant hospital audits, training records, Ministry of Health documents, and reports from humanitarian organizations may not have been indexed or publicly available. Although the literature-selection process was summarized numerically, the search was conducted as part of a narrative review and did not involve independent duplicate screening or a prospectively registered systematic-review protocol.

Conclusion

Multimodal spinal anesthesia may have an important role in selected surgical and obstetric services in Somalia, but its safety, feasibility, and effectiveness have not yet been established through local clinical outcome studies. Its implementation should therefore be conditional on appropriate patient selection, trained providers, minimum monitoring, oxygen and vasopressor availability, airway rescue capacity, standardized management of hypotension and failed or high spinal block, postoperative observation, and clear patient communication.

Cesarean delivery represents an important initial area for protocol standardization, while intrathecal adjuvants should be selected according to each facility’s ability to provide postoperative monitoring and manage adverse effects. The immediate priority is not simply to increase the number of spinal anesthetics performed, but to establish reliable safety systems and collect local outcome data.

Prospective multicentre audits should measure spinal failure, hypotension, high spinal block, conversion to general anesthesia, postoperative pain, respiratory events, neurologic complications, unplanned referral, and patient satisfaction. Such evidence is necessary before locally validated clinical, cost, or outcome claims can be made.
