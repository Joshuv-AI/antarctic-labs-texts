Optimal control under uncertainty with joint chance state constraints: almost-everywhere bounds, variance reduction, and application to (bi-)linear elliptic PDEs Thanks: Version from August 24, 2026.
Rene Henrion, Georg Stadler, Florian Wechsung

Abstract
We study optimal control of PDEs under uncertainty with the state variable subject to joint chance constraints. The controls are deterministic, but the states are probabilistic due to random variables in the governing equation. Joint chance constraints ensure that the random state variable meets pointwise bounds with high probability. For linear governing PDEs and elliptically distributed random parameters, we prove existence and uniqueness results for almost-everywhere state bounds. Using the spherical-radial decomposition (SRD) of the uncertain variable, we prove that when the probability is very large or small, the resulting Monte Carlo estimator for the chance constraint probability exhibits substantially reduced variance compared to the standard Monte Carlo estimator. We further illustrate how the SRD can be leveraged to efficiently compute derivatives of the probability function, and discuss different expansions of the uncertain variable in the governing equation. Numerical examples for linear and bilinear PDEs compare the performance of Monte Carlo and quasi-Monte Carlo sampling methods, examining probability estimation convergence as the number of samples increases. We also study how the accuracy of the probabilities depends on the truncation of the random variable expansion, and numerically illustrate the variance reduction of the SRD.



keywords

Optimal control under uncertainty, risk-averse, joint chance state constraints, spherical-radial decomposition, elliptic PDE, variance reduction.

Introduction

While deterministic optimization and control problems governed by
partial differential equations (PDEs) are a well establish research
area
[
6
,
23
,
39
]
, only
over the last one or two decades researchers have started to systematically study
these problems in the presence of uncertainty, e.g.,
[
25
,
16
,
10
,
2
,
26
,
29

.
Optimization under uncertainty introduces new theoretical and algorithmic difficulties because uncertainty can substantially increase the dimensionality of the problem. This adds complexity to optimization problems governed by PDEs, as it necessitates random space approximation and one requires adapted objectives and constraints.
Here we focus
on probabilistic constraints on the state in optimal control
problems governed by elliptic equations. In particular, we consider joint chance state constraints which require that
realizations of the distribution of states must satisfy pointwise
bound constraints with a certain probability. A
precise formulation is given next.

1.1
Problem statement

For a bounded, sufficiently regular domain
{\mathcal{D}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, typically

d\in\{1,2,3\}
, we consider an optimal control problem under
uncertainty, i.e.:

\min_{u\in U_{\!\text{ad}},\,y\in X}\mathcal{J}(u,y),

(1)

where
U_{\!\text{ad}}

is a convex closed set of admissible controls, and
X
the function space of the random states. The uncertainty enters through the equation that relates the control
u
and the state
y
. We use a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
, i.e.,
\omega\in\Omega
are events,
\mathcal{A}
is a sigma-algebra on
\Omega
and
\mathbb{P}
is a probability measure. The governing equation is an elliptic PDE with random variable
\xi
defined in
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
and taking values
in a Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
. The state
y(\omega)
and the control
u
satisfy the equation

A(u)y(\omega)+B\xi(\omega)+Cu=f,

(2)

where for
u\in U_{\!\text{ad}}
,
A(u):Y\mapsto H^{-1}({\mathcal{D}})
with
Y
a subspace of
H^{1}({\mathcal{D}})
is a linear invertible elliptic operator that is
differentiable with respect to

,
B:\mathscr{H}\mapsto H^{-1}({\mathcal{D}})
and

are bounded linear operators,

The equation form (

linear and bilinear PDEs, and we will give concrete examples later in
this introduction.
Additionally to
eq.

1

eq.

2

\mathbb{P}(\omega\in\Omega\,|\,\underline{y}({\boldsymbol{x}})\leq y({\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)\leq\bar{y}({\boldsymbol{x}})\text{ for a.\,a.\ }{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathcal{D}})\geq p,

(3)

The constraint

and

In this paper, we develop efficient methods to approximate and solve (
1

2

Chance constraints were introduced in the 1950s
[

Recently, these probabilistic constraints have been considered in the context of infinite-dimensional optimization, in particular

Numerical approaches for chance constraints can roughly be classified in (1) methods that relax the constraints, resulting in formulations with nicer properties (e.g., convexity, differentiability) and (2) methods that approximate the original chance constraints.
One method falling into the first class is using the Conditional Value-at-Risk, but other relaxations are possible (e.g.,
[
30
,
27
]
). Facilitating solution methods through relaxation of the chance constraint
comes at the cost of potentially significant conservatism of the resulting solutions.
The second class of approaches approximate the original chance constraints. This approximation could be discrete (as in sample average approximation
[
32
]
) or smooth (e.g.,
[
33
,
19
,
8
,
40
]
). Consequently, solution algorithms from discrete or non-linear optimization are used. In this paper, we follow an approach based on the
spherical-radial decomposition
(SRD) of elliptically distributed (e.g., Gaussian) random vectors, which has been shown to be successful both in the theoretical analysis of probability functions
[
40
,
41
,
21
,
42
,
17
]
and in numerical solutions of chance-constrained optimization problems
[
34
,
22
,
13
,
3
]
.

We emphasize that (
3
) is a
joint
chance constraint, that is, it considers the probability over a whole system of random inequalities. Alternatively, one could formulate
individual
chance constraints, where the probability is taken over all inequalities individually:

\mathbb{P}\left(\omega\in\Omega\,|\,\underline{y}({\boldsymbol{x}})\leq y({\boldsymbol{x}},\omega)\leq\bar{y}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right)\geq p\qquad\text{ for a.\,a.\ }{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathcal{D}}.

The difference lies in enforcing uniform versus pointwise constraints, which must be satisfied with high probability. Individual constraints are less restrictive, allowing for lower optimal objective function values. Furthermore, in specific models (e.g., when control and random parameters are separated as in (
2
)), individual constraints can be reformulated into explicit deterministic equivalents. In contrast, joint constraints address the need for uniform constraint satisfaction, often reflecting practical requirements. In particular, random states that meet individual bounds with high probability at each point may still fail to meet these bounds uniformly over the entire domain with high probability (see, e.g.,
[
3
, Sec. 4.2]
).

Note that in the limit
p\to 1
, the chance constraint (
3
) turns into an “almost-sure” constraint. Nevertheless, in general, it is not recommended to formulate an almost-sure constraint as a chance constraint because the latter degenerates in the limit to a constraint violating standard constraint qualifications
[
17
]
. We refer to
[
18
,
17
,
15
]
for theoretical and numerical approaches to treat almost-sure constraints.

1.3
Contributions, limitations and overview

In this paper, we make the following
contributions
.
(1) We extend existence, uniqueness, and convexity results to problems with joint chance
almost everywhere
state constraints.
(2) We prove theoretically and illustrate numerically that, compared to standard Monte Carlo (MC) sampling, using the spherical-radial decomposition (SRD) substantially reduces the variance for estimation of low, high, or radially close-to-symmetric probabilities.
(3) Using smoothing properties characteristic of PDE operators, we illustrate the innate dimension reduction for the random state variable and discuss its use in approximating chance constraints.
(4) We present a systematic numerical study of control problems governed by linear and bilinear elliptic problems, comparing standard and SRD-based MC and quasi-MC sampling. We find that a SRD quasi-MC method requires 1-2 orders of magnitude fewer samples than a standard MC method for the same accuracy, which directly translates to a corresponding speedup.

Our approach also has some
limitations
.
(1) For the problems we consider here, the map from the uncertainty to the PDE solution (and thus the constraint) is linear. The methods can be generalized to problems where the constraint function depends nonlinearly on the random variables. However, some advantages of the proposed methods are lost with increasing nonlinearity.
(2) We also assume that for fixed control
u
, the governing equation is linear. Note, however, that we do not require that the control enter linearly, as we illustrate using a bilinear control example.
(3) For the problems considered here, the variance reduction achieved by using SRD MC sampling over standard MC sampling directly translates into computational speed-up. Although variance reduction also applies to problems where the constraint depends nonlinearly on the random variable, the increased computational cost of SRD MC in nonlinear settings may negate the advantages gained from reduced variance.

Finally, we give an
overview
.
Section

2
presents basic existence, uniqueness and convexity results for the case that the governing equation (
2
) is linear in the control
u
. In particular, existing results are generalized to
almost everywhere
state constraints. In
section

3
, the spherical-radial decomposition is introduced for general elliptically distributed random variables, and reduced variance results are shown for the case of very small, large or close-to-symmetric sets. Furthermore, we provide derivatives of the probability function. In
section

4
, we apply the general framework specifically to the control problem (
1
), discuss truncation of the random variable expansion, and compute explicit gradient formulas of the probability. Finally, in
sections

5
and
6
, we perform comprehensive numerical experiments for a linear and a bilinear control problem, and discuss the results in the context of our theoretical findings.

The probability function (

Likewise, the assumptions of

Theorem 4
.

Assume that
\hat{\mathcal{J}}
is coercive, convex, and lower semicontinuous, and that

Then, (

By convexity and lower semicontinuity,

3
Spherical-radial decomposition

In this section, we revisit the
spherical-radial decomposition
(SRD) as a methodology for addressing chance constraints in a slightly more general context than required for our target optimal control problem
eq.

1
,
eq.

2
and
eq.

3
. In particular, in
sections

3.1
and
3.2
, we assume an elliptically distributed random variable (generalizing the Gaussian distribution in our target problem) and that the constraint function is convex (generalizing the linear constraint function needed in
eq.

3
).

The SRD has a somewhat greater level of complexity compared to smoothing methods
[
8
,
36
,
33
]
and sample average approximation
[
32
]
, which are applicable to any distribution accessible through sampling.
Although the SRD is confined to a specific class of multivariate distributions, including Gaussian distributions, it capitalizes on their intrinsic properties to provide three significant benefits:
Firstly, it facilitates the analysis of the original problem (e.g., Lipschitz continuity or differentiability of the chance constraint) without necessitating approximations at the outset
[
40
,
41
,
21
]
. This approach has been utilized in
[
42
,
17
]
to derive optimality conditions for a continuous PDE optimal control problem. Secondly, the SRD has reduced variance in probability estimation compared to both direct MC and quasi-MC sampling, which is advantageous for numerically solving chance-constrained optimization problems. Thirdly, the SRD provides explicit gradient expressions for the probability function in both the original and numerically approximated formulations. This is crucial for addressing chance constraints through nonlinear optimization methods. While gradients for numerical approximations are also obtainable with smoothing methods, they cannot be derived from single samples, unlike when using the SRD.
The first aspect is less significant in this context as our focus is on numerical solutions rather than theoretical analysis. The second aspect, concerning variance reduction, has not been systematically explored so far, and we shall examine it more thoroughly in
section

3.2
. As for the third aspect, we will derive and employ explicit gradient formulas for the probability functions to solve the optimal control problems outlined in
examples

1
and
2
.

3.1
General setting

It is well known
[
12
, eq. (2.12)]
that
elliptically
distributed
n
-dimensional random vectors
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
admit a decomposition

{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}={\boldsymbol{m}}+\tau L{\boldsymbol{\theta}},

(16)

where
{\boldsymbol{m}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
L\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
,
k\leq n
,
\tau
is a one-dimensional non-negative random variable, and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is a random vector uniformly distributed on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{k-1}
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Moreover,
\tau
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
are independently distributed. With
\tau
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
playing the role of a radial and a spherical variable (in the sense of polar coordinates), respectively, (
16
) is referred to as the spherical-radial decomposition of
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
. A prominent example is a (nondegenerate) Gaussian random vector
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{m}},\Sigma)
, which yields the decomposition (
16
) with
k=n
,
\tau
having a chi-distribution with
n
degrees of freedom and
L
satisfying
LL^{T}=\Sigma
. Accordingly, the Gaussian probability of some measurable subset
M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be represented as

\mathbb{P}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\in M)=\int\limits_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\mu_{\chi}(\{r\geq 0:{\boldsymbol{m}}+rL{\boldsymbol{v}}\in M\})d\mu_{U}({\boldsymbol{v}}),

(17)

where
\mu_{\chi}
is the one-dimensional Chi-distribution with
n
degrees of freedom and
\mu_{U}
is the uniform distribution over

\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.

In this paper, we use the spherical-radial decomposition (SRD) in combination with the inequality
g(u,{\boldsymbol{\zeta}})\leq 0
, where
g:U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
U
is a Banach space of controls and
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{m}},\Sigma)
is an
n
-dimensional Gaussian random vector. We assume that
g(u,\cdot)
is convex for all
u\in U
.
Defining the probability function
\varphi:U\to[0,1]
as

\varphi(u):=\mathbb{P}(g(u,{\boldsymbol{\zeta}})\leq 0)\qquad(u\in U),

(18)

(
17
) yields that

\varphi(u)=\int\limits_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\mu_{\chi}(\{r\geq 0:g(u,{\boldsymbol{m}}+rL{\boldsymbol{v}}\leq 0\})d\mu_{U}({\boldsymbol{v}})\qquad(u\in U).

A considerably more convenient representation can be provided, if the mean
{\boldsymbol{m}}
is strictly feasible for
u\in U
:

g(u,{\boldsymbol{m}})<0.

(19)

In
[
40
, Prop. 3.11]
it was shown that (
19
) holds if

\varphi(u)\geq 0.5
and there exists
any
Slater point
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(not necessarily
{\boldsymbol{m}}
), i.e.,
g(u,{\boldsymbol{z}})<0
. Both conditions are mild; the latter is because the target probability level
p
in a chance constraint is usually close to one.
Under (
19
), the one-dimensional set

\{r\geq 0:g(u,{\boldsymbol{m}}+rL{\boldsymbol{v}})\leq 0\}

reduces to the interval
[0,\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{v}})]
,
where

\rho\left(u,{\boldsymbol{v}}\right):=\sup\left\{r\geq 0:g\left(u,{\boldsymbol{m}}+rL{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)\leq 0\right\}.

(20)

Note that
\rho\left(u,{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)=\infty
is possible.
Hence,

\mu_{\chi}(\{r\geq 0:g(u,{\boldsymbol{m}}+rL{\boldsymbol{v}}\leq 0\})=\mu_{\chi}([0,\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{v}})])=F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{v}}))-F_{\chi}(0)=F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{v}})),

where
F_{\chi}
denotes the distribution function of the one-dimensional Chi-distribution with
n
degrees of freedom. Here, to include the case
\rho\left(u,{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)=\infty
, we adopt the convention that
F_{\chi}(\infty)=1
. Consequently, the probability function simplifies to

\varphi(u)=\int\limits_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{v}}))d\mu_{U}({\boldsymbol{v}})\qquad(u\in U).

(21)

For numerical purposes, the spherical integral (
21
) is usually approximated by a finite sum

\varphi(u)\approx\tilde{\varphi}(u):=N^{-1}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}F_{\chi}\left(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})\right)\qquad(u\in U),

(22)

where
\{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
are samples uniformly distributed on the sphere. Such samples can be obtained, for example, by normalizing to unit length a set of MC or quasi-MC samples of the standard Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For more efficient methods of creating such samples, see, e.g.,

[
1
]
.

Although the following analysis focuses on Gaussian distributions, the methods also apply to other elliptical distributions, including multivariate t-distributions, symmetric Laplace, or hyperbolic distributions. The main difference lies in the precise form of the one-dimensional radial distribution (i.e., the Chi-distribution for the Gaussian case), whereas the spherical component remains consistent. Additionally, techniques from the Gaussian framework can be adapted to Gaussian-like distributions, including log-normal, truncated Gaussian, and Gaussian mixture distributions.

3.2
Variance reduction by spherical-radial decomposition

Here, we assume (
19
) to ensure the validity of the form (
21
). We will show that for large sets (and thus high probability), for small sets
(and thus low probability), and for radially near-symmetric sets about the mean, the variance ratio of the
probability estimators obtained with the SRD and with
standard MC approaches zero (as both tend to zero
individually). We fix a control
u
(which does not play any role in this analysis) and recall the standard MC estimator

\varphi(u)\approx N^{-1}\#\{i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}:g(u,{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i})\leq 0\}=N^{-1}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{I}_{\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:g(u,{\boldsymbol{z}})\leq 0\}}({\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}),

(23)

where
\{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
are samples of
the Gaussian random vector
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
and
\mathcal{I}_{C}
refers to the
characteristic function of the set
C
. In contrast, the
estimator based on the SRD with the MC
samples
\{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
on the sphere is (
22
). Since the effect of the sample size
N
on the variance is the same for both estimators, we may restrict ourselves to compare the variances of the elementary estimators

\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{MC}}:=\operatorname{Var}\mathcal{I}_{\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:g(u,{\boldsymbol{z}})\leq 0\}}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}}),\qquad\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{SRD}}:=\operatorname{Var}F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})),

(24)

where
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{m}},\Sigma)
, and

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
is uniformly distributed
on the sphere.
It is well known that
\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{MC}}=p(1-p)
, where

p=\varphi(u)=\mathbb{P}(\{g(u,{\boldsymbol{\zeta}})\leq 0\})=\mathbb{E}\mathcal{I}_{\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:g(u,{\boldsymbol{z}})\leq 0\}}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}})=\mathbb{E}F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})).

(25)

As shown in
[
40
, eq. (1.5)]
, the estimate
\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{SRD}}\leq\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{MC}}
holds.
The following example shows
that the variances can be equal.

Example 5
.

Consider the function
g(u,{\boldsymbol{z}}):=z_{1}
, so that the inequality
z_{1}\leq 0
defines a closed half-space with the origin on its boundary. Suppose
further that
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
. Then, clearly,

p=1/2
, from which follows
\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{MC}}=1/4
. On the other hand, the definition
(
20
) of
\rho
implies that

\rho\left(u,{\boldsymbol{v}}\right):=\sup\left\{r\geq 0:rv_{1}\leq 0\right\}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\mbox{if }v_{1}\geq 0,\\
\infty&\mbox{if }v_{1}<0.\end{array}\right.

Since
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is uniformly distributed on the sphere, one concludes that, by symmetry, the events
\theta_{1}\geq 0
and
\theta_{1}<0
occur with the same probability 1/2. Thus,

\mathbb{P}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=0)=\mathbb{P}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\infty)=1/2
,
and therefore both events
F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))=0
and
F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))=1
happen with probability 1/2. This means that
\mathbb{E}F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))=\mathbb{E}[F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))]^{2}=1/2
. Therefore,

\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{SRD}}=\mathbb{E}[F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))]^{2}-[\mathbb{E}F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))]^{2}=1/4=p(1-p)=\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{MC}}.

This example shows that
\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{SRD}}\leq\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{MC}}
cannot be improved in general. However, in some situations,
\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{SRD}}
is substantially smaller than
\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{MC}}
, as shown in the following two lemmas:

Lemma 6
.

With
p
from (
25
) and
\rho^{\inf/\sup}:=\inf/\sup\{\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{v}})\mid{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathbb{S}^{K-1}\}
, we have

\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{SRD}}\leq\min\{(1-p)(p-F_{\chi}(\rho^{\inf})),p(F_{\chi}(\rho^{\sup})-p)\}.

Proof.

From (
25
), it follows that

\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{SRD}}=\mathbb{E}[F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))]^{2}-[\mathbb{E}F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))]^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[F_{\chi}(\rho^{\sup})F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))]-p^{2}=p(F_{\chi}(\rho^{\sup})-p).

For the second estimate, we use the identity
\operatorname{Var}X=\operatorname{Var}(1-X)
, which holds for any random variable
X
, and obtain that

\displaystyle\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{SRD}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[1-F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))]^{2}-[\mathbb{E}(1-F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})))]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[(1-F_{\chi}(\rho^{\inf}))(1-F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}})))]-(1-p)^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-F_{\chi}(\rho^{\inf}))(1-p)-(1-p)^{2}=(1-p)(p-F_{\chi}(\rho^{\inf})).

This lemma implies that for
\rho^{\inf}\to\infty
(growing sets with growing probability), one has that

\frac{\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{SRD}}}{\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{MC}}}\leq\frac{(1-p)(p-F_{\chi}(\rho^{\inf}))}{p(1-p)}=1-\frac{F_{\chi}(\rho^{\inf})}{p}\to 0.

(26)

Here, we used that
\rho^{\inf}\to\infty
implies
F_{\chi}(\rho^{\inf})\to 1
and that
F_{\chi}(\rho^{\inf})\leq\mathbb{E}F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))=p
necessarily implies
p\to 1
. Thus, the ratio between the variances of the SRD and the standard MC estimators becomes arbitrarily small for high probabilities
p
.

Similarly, for
\rho^{\sup}\to 0
(shrinking sets with decreasing probability, yet always containing the mean
{\boldsymbol{m}}
of the given Gaussian distribution), one has that

\frac{\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{SRD}}}{\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{MC}}}\leq\frac{p(F_{\chi}(\rho^{\sup})-p)}{p(1-p)}=\frac{F_{\chi}(\rho^{\sup})-p}{1-p}\to 0.

Similar as above, we used that
F_{\chi}(\rho^{\sup})\to 0
for
\rho^{\sup}\to 0
, and that
F_{\chi}(\rho^{\sup})\geq p
also implies
p\to 0
. This means that the ratio between the variances of the SRD and the standard MC estimators goes to zero as
p\to 0
(under the additional assumption that the mean of the Gaussian distribution is contained in the set, which—following the remarks below (
19
)—was automatically satisfied in the first case because
p\geq 0.5
may be assumed due to
p\to 1
).

We may interpret
\rho_{\delta}:=\rho^{\sup}-\rho^{\inf}\geq 0
as a measure of radial asymmetry of the set
\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:g(u,{\boldsymbol{z}})\leq 0\}
. Clearly, for
\rho_{\delta}=0
, one has that
\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{v}})
is constant for all
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
so that the set becomes perfectly symmetric (i.e., a ball) around zero.

Lemma 7
.

Under the assumptions of
lemma

6
and also assuming that
p\in(0,1)
, one has that
\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{SRD}}\leq L^{2}\rho_{\delta}^{2}
, where
L
is the maximum of the density
\mu_{\chi}=F^{\prime}_{\chi}
of the given
\chi
-distribution (which is bounded for any dimension).

Proof.

Thanks to
p\in(0,1)
, there exists a unique
p
-quantile
\rho^{*}
of the one-dimensional Chi-distribution such that
F_{\chi}(\rho^{*})=p
. One easily derives
\rho^{*}\in[\rho^{\inf},\rho^{\sup}]
because otherwise a contradiction with
\mathbb{E}F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))=p
would arise from the fact that the distribution function
F_{\chi}
is strictly increasing. Now, exploiting this last property once more, we arrive at

\displaystyle\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{SRD}}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Var}(F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}))=\int\limits_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathbb{S}^{K-1}}[F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{v}}))-\mathbb{E}F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{v}}))]^{2}d\mu_{U}({\boldsymbol{v}})

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathbb{S}^{K-1}}[F_{\chi}(\rho(u,{\boldsymbol{v}}))-F_{\chi}(\rho^{*})]^{2}d\mu_{U}({\boldsymbol{v}})\leq(F_{\chi}(\rho^{\sup})-F_{\chi}(\rho^{\inf}))^{2}

\displaystyle=(F^{\prime}_{\chi}(\tilde{\rho})(\rho^{\sup}-\rho^{\inf}))^{2}\leq L^{2}\rho_{\delta}^{2}\qquad(\tilde{\rho}\in[\rho^{\inf},\rho^{\sup}]).

Note that
Lemma

7
implies that the SRD estimator has zero variance for balls centered at zero. Therefore, a single ray is sufficient to compute the exact probability of a ball.
This is of course well-known.
Now, consider a sequence of sets with the same probability
p
converging to a centered ball. In this sequence,
\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{MC}}=p(1-p)
is the fixed variance of the MC estimator, while
\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{SRD}}
converges to zero. Thus, the variance ratio between the estimators reduces in favor of the SRD as shown in
Lemma

6
.

Finally, we revisit
Example

5
. For the half-space
considered there,
\rho^{\inf}=0
and
\rho^{\sup}=\infty
. Hence, the estimate from
lemma

6
only yields the already known relation
\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{SRD}}\leq p(1-p)=\operatorname{V}_{\!\mathrm{MC}}
. Moreover,
\rho_{\delta}=\infty
, whence
Lemma

7
does not provide additional information either. The reason is that this half space is neither a large nor a small space (its probability is equal to
1/2
, which is far from
1
and
0
) nor a symmetric set. This explains why in this situation the variance of the SRD MC estimator is not reduced compared to the classical MC estimator.

3.3
Differentiability and computation of gradients of the probability function

When numerically addressing a chance constraint such as (
3
), compactly written as
\varphi(u)\geq p
with
\varphi
defined in (
11
), it is essential to compute not only the value of
\varphi(u)
, but also its derivatives. Analytical properties of
\varphi
, such as Lipschitz continuity or differentiability, have been studied before
[
40
,
41
,
21
,
42
]
.
Given our focus on numerical methods, we concentrate on the discretized probability function
\tilde{\varphi}
defined in (
22
), which uses the samples
\{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Specifically, we aim at identifying conditions guaranteeing its differentiability and derive an explicit form of the derivative suitable for implementation. Since the Chi-distribution function
F_{\chi}
is continuously differentiable, with its derivative being the density
\mu_{\chi}
, the problem reduces to verifying the partial differentiability of the function
\rho(u,\cdot)
with respect to
u
. We now fix some
\bar{u}\in X
and assume that the random constraint function
g
from
section

3.1
has the typical form relevant for joint chance constraints, namely

g:=\max\limits_{j=1,\ldots,p}g_{j},

(27)

where the
g_{j}:U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
are affine. In particular,
g(u,\cdot)
is convex for all
u\in U
as required in
section

3.1
. Analogously to (
20
), we define

\rho_{j}\left(u,{\boldsymbol{v}}\right):=\sup\left\{r\geq 0:g_{j}\left(u,{\boldsymbol{m}}+rL{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)\leq 0\right\}\qquad(j=1,\ldots,p).

(28)

Under the additional assumption

g_{j}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{m}})<0\qquad(j=1,\ldots,p)

(29)

it follows that (
19
) holds. Moreover,

\rho\left(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)=\min\limits_{j=1,\ldots,p}\rho_{j}\left(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}\right).

(30)

In the following we denote by
f_{\chi}
the density of the one-dimensional Chi-distribution
\mu_{\chi}
with
n
degrees of freedom and we adopt the convention that

f_{\chi}(\infty)=\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}f_{\chi}(t)=0
.

Proposition 8
.

Let
U
be a Banach space. In the setting above and under assumption (
29
), if for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
satisfying
\rho(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})<\infty
holds

\#\{j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}:\rho\left(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}\right)=\rho_{j}\left(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}\right)\}=1,

(31)

then
\tilde{\varphi}
is continuously differentiable at
\bar{u}
with derivative

\nabla\tilde{\varphi}(\bar{u})=-N^{-1}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\frac{f_{\chi}(\rho_{j_{i}}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))}{\langle\nabla_{z}g_{j_{i}}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{m}}+\rho_{j_{i}}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})L{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}),L{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}\rangle}\nabla_{u}g_{j_{i}}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{m}}+\rho_{j_{i}}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})L{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}).

(32)

Here, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
, the index
j_{i}
is one satisfying (
31
), which is unique if
\rho(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})<\infty
.

Proof.

Our setting allows us to invoke
[
42
, Cor. 3.2]
for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
to argue that the functions
F_{\chi}(\rho_{j}(u,{\boldsymbol{v}}))
are continuously differentiable at all
(u,{\boldsymbol{v}})\in\mathcal{V}_{j}(\bar{u})\times\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, where
\mathcal{V}_{j}(\bar{u})
is some neighborhood of
\bar{u}
. Moreover, for all
{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, one has that

\nabla[F_{\chi}(\rho_{j}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{v}}))](\bar{u})=\frac{-f_{\chi}(\rho_{j}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}))}{\langle\nabla_{z}g_{j}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{m}}+\rho_{j}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}})L{\boldsymbol{v}}),L{\boldsymbol{v}}\rangle}\nabla_{u}g_{j}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{m}}+\rho_{j}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}})L{\boldsymbol{v}}).

(33)

Actually, the application of
[
42
, Cor. 3.2]
requires the verification of a certain growth condition. However, since
g_{j}
is affine, this condition is easily verified along the lines of
[
17
, Lem. 6]
.
To prove (
31
), consider first an index
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
with
\rho(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})<\infty
. It follows from
[
21
, Lem. 1]
and (
31
) that
\rho(\cdot{\boldsymbol{,}}{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})=\rho_{j_{i}}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})
locally around
\bar{u}
.
Therefore, by (
33
)

\nabla[F_{\chi}(\rho(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))](\bar{u})=\frac{-f_{\chi}(\rho_{j_{i}}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))}{\langle\nabla_{z}g_{j_{i}}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{m}}+\rho_{j_{i}}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})L{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}),L{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}\rangle}\nabla_{u}g_{j_{i}}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{m}}+\rho_{j_{i}}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})L{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}),

(34)

which yields (
32
). Now, consider an index
i
where
\rho(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})=\infty
, whence
\rho_{j}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})=\infty
for all
j=1,\ldots,p
. Consequently,
F_{\chi}(\rho(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))=F_{\chi}(\rho_{j}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
.
By the remark above, all
F_{\chi}(\rho_{j}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))
are continuously differentiable at
\bar{u}
with vanishing derivative
[
42
, Lem. 3.3]
. This shows that
F_{\chi}(\rho(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))
itself is continuously differentiable with zero derivative at
\bar{u}
and, hence, (
34
) also holds in this second case thanks to
f_{\chi}(\infty)=0
. Finally, (
32
) follows from (
34
) and the definition of
\tilde{\varphi}
in (
22
).

The next proposition shows that the difficult-to-verify condition (
31
), required for differentiability of
\tilde{\varphi}
, is generically satisfied under a common constraint qualification that
is weaker than the prominent
Linear Independence Constraint Qualification
(LICQ):

Proposition 9
.

If the following so-called ’Rank-2-Constraint Qualification’

\displaystyle\forall{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},i,j\in\{1,\ldots,p\},i\neq j:

\displaystyle 0=g(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{z}})=g_{i}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{z}})=g_{j}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{z}})\Longrightarrow\textrm{ rank }\{\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{z}}}g_{i}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{z}}),\nabla_{{\boldsymbol{z}}}g_{j}(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{z}})\}=2

(35)

holds at
\bar{u}\in U
, then a random sample

\{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
satisfies (
31
) with probability one.

Proof.

Following
[
41
, Lem. 4.3]
, (
35
) implies that

\mu_{U}(\{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}:\rho(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}})<\infty,\,\#\{j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}:\rho\left(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}\right)=\rho_{j}\left(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}\right)\}=1\})=1.

This shows that (
31
) is satisfied with probability one for each
i=1,\ldots,N
separately, hence it is satisfied with probability one simultaneously for all
i=1,\ldots,N
.

4
Application of spherical-radial decomposition to the control problem

4.1
Discretization of joint state constraints

To replace the continuous state constraint with a finite number of
inequalities, we use a continuous linear map
E:Y\to\mathbb{R}^{M}
. If

Y
continuously embeds into the space of continuous function, then
the map
E
can be chosen as the evaluation of the state

y(\omega)
at the points
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{M}\}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
. If the dimension of
{\mathcal{D}}
is
d=1
,
Y\subset H^{1}({\mathcal{D}})\hookrightarrow\mathcal{C}^{0}({\mathcal{D}})
and thus point evaluation is
continuous. If
d=2,3
and the data is smooth, then additional
regularity arguments for elliptic operators can be used to
show that
Y\subset H^{2}({\mathcal{D}})
, which again continuously embeds into the
space of continuous functions, and thus choosing
E
as point
evaluation is feasible. If
d>3
or no additional regularity can be
used,
E
can be chosen as local averages of the state variable

y(\omega)
centered at the points
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{M}\}
. For ease
of notation, in the following we write
E
as point evaluation, i.e.,

E(y):=(y({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}),\ldots,y({\boldsymbol{x}}_{M}))
. The following derivations also apply to
E
being a local averaging operator. Note that here we
consider the points
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}
as given, e.g., as a uniform grid of
points covering the domain
{\mathcal{D}}
. It is also possible to choose the set of points
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{M}\}
adaptively using probabilistic information, thus keeping the number of points
M
moderate,

[
3
]
. Now, with no matter which choice of a discrete subset
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{M}\}
of the domain, we end up at the accordingly modified chance constraint (
3
):

\mathbb{P}(\omega\in\Omega\,|\,\underline{y}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{j})\leq y({\boldsymbol{x}}_{j},\omega)\leq\bar{y}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{j})\quad\forall j=1,\ldots,M)\geq p,

(36)

To write the probability in this chance constraint
for the concrete governing equation (
2
) in the form
\mathbb{P}(\max_{1\leq j\leq M}g_{j}(u,{\boldsymbol{z}}))
,
we define the functions
g_{j}
as

\begin{array}[]{rcl}g_{j}(u,{\boldsymbol{z}})&:=&\left[A(u)^{-1}\left(f-Cu-B\xi_{0}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}z_{k}Be_{k}\right)-\bar{y}\right]({\boldsymbol{x}}_{j})\\[5.59721pt]
g_{M+j}(u,{\boldsymbol{z}})&:=&\left[\underline{y}-A(u)^{-1}\left(f-Cu-B\xi_{0}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}z_{k}Be_{k}\right)\right]({\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}),\end{array}

(37)

where we assume the form (
10
) for the random process
\xi
, with the concrete distribution
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
. In particular,
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
represents the realizations
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(\omega)
of
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
. This fits our abstract setting from
section

3
with the choices
p:=2M,n:=K
. Clearly, the
g_{j}
are affine in
{\boldsymbol{z}}
for
j=1,\ldots,2M
as required in
section

3.3
.

4.2
Implicit dimension reduction in probability space

We now turn to the approximation in random space. A standard method to efficiently describe a Gaussian random variable or field is its Karhunen-Loeve (KL) expansion. For instance, for a random field
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(\xi_{0},\mathcal{C}_{0})
over the physical domain
{\mathcal{D}}
such a (possibly infinite) expansion is

\xi\left(\omega\right)=\xi_{0}+\sum_{k}\zeta_{k}^{\xi}(\omega)e_{k}^{\xi},

(38)

where
e_{k}^{\xi}\in L^{2}({\mathcal{D}})
are eigenvectors (or functions) of
\mathcal{C}_{0}
, and
\zeta_{k}^{\xi}(\omega)
are independent Gaussian random variables with variances
\lambda_{k}^{\xi}
, where
\lambda_{1}^{\xi}\geq\lambda_{2}^{\xi}\geq\ldots\geq 0
are the eigenvalues corresponding to
e_{k}^{\xi}
. The rate at which the sequence
\{\lambda_{k}^{\xi}\}_{k}
decays controls the approximation error when the KL expansion (
38
) is truncated.

Although a truncated version of (
38
) is an efficient (in terms of the number of terms and thus the dimension) representation for the random variable
\xi(\omega)
, it may not be efficient for the state random variable
y(\omega)
, which is subject to the joint chance constraints. To find a representation tailored to the state, note that (
9
) implies that

y(\omega)=y_{0}^{u}-A(u)^{-1}B\xi(\omega)=:y_{0}^{u}-\sum_{k}\zeta_{k}^{y}(\omega)e_{k}^{y},

(39)

where
y_{0}^{u}:=A(u)^{-1}(f-B\xi_{0}-Cu)
is the mean of the state variable. For fixed
u
,
e_{k}^{y}
are the eigenfunctions of

A(u)^{-1}B\mathcal{C}_{0}B^{\star}A(u)^{-\star}
, with the “
-\star
” denotes the inverse adjoint operator. Moreover, in this KL-type expansion,
\zeta_{k}^{y}(\omega)
are independent Gaussian random variables with variance
\lambda_{k}^{y}
, where
\lambda_{1}^{y}\geq\lambda_{2}^{y}\geq\ldots\geq 0
are the eigenvalues corresponding to
e_{k}^{y}
.

Due to smoothing properties of the inverse elliptic operator

A(u)^{-1}
, the variances
\lambda_{k}^{y}
of the random variables in (
39
) typically decay faster than the variances
\lambda_{k}^{\xi}
for (
38
). Thus, using (
39
) instead of (
38
) allows a more efficient finite-dimensional approximation of the random variable. To illustrate this, we show in the numerical example in
section

5
that
K=20
coefficients allow a highly accurate representation of
y(\omega)
, while an accurate representation of
\xi(\omega)
would require a substantially larger number of expansion coefficients; see
fig.

1
.

Note that in (
38
),
\lambda_{k}^{\xi}
and
e_{k}^{\xi}
depend only on the distribution of the random variable but not on the control
u
. When the PDE operator
A
is independent of
u
, only the mean
y_{0}^{u}
in (
39
) depends on the control, whereas
\lambda_{k}^{y}
and
e_{k}^{y}
are independent of
u
. For both scenarios, eigenfunctions and eigenvalues can be computed upfront and used throughout a numerical optimization algorithm to find the optimal control.
However, if
A(u)
is a function of
u
, then
\lambda_{k}^{y}
and
e_{k}^{y}
are also functions of
u
and need to be recomputed whenever the control changes (such as in a numerical optimization algorithm). Thus, while (
39
) provides an efficient way to represent the random state variable, in situations where the PDE operator depends on
u
, utilizing (
39
) may not be computationally efficient to evaluate the probability
\varphi(u)
(or its approximation (
22
)), and its derivative with respect to
u
.

We now extend the results from the previous section to

6.1
Setup and discretization

In
Example

2
, we let
\xi\sim\mathcal{N}(\xi_{0},\mathcal{C}_{0})
, where the covariance operator
\mathcal{C}_{0}:L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D)
is given by the inverse elliptic operator
(-\alpha\Delta+I)^{-2}
; see
[
7
]
.
We only consider an upper bound for the state variable, i.e., formally set
\underline{y}=-\infty
in (

We use finite elements to approximate the governing equation as well as the prior covariance operator. In particular, we use an
n

V_{h}\subset H^{1}_{0}({\mathcal{D}})
, and write the governing equation (
6
) in the form

[A+M(u)]y(\omega)=M(f+\xi(\omega))

where, for

A,M(u)
and
M
through the weak forms

\langle Ay,v\rangle:=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\nabla y\cdot\nabla v\,dx,\quad\langle M(u)y,v\rangle:=\int_{\mathcal{D}}uyv\,dx,\quad\langle Mg,v\rangle:=\int_{\mathcal{D}}gv\,dx.

In the following, we associate each operator with its corresponding matrix, i.e.,

A_{ij}=\langle A\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\rangle
,
where
\{\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}\}
is a basis for the space
V_{h}
. As usual in the finite element method, we denote by

the coefficient vector corresponding to the finite element function

(which we simply denote by

again), and similar for the all other variables.
Expressed in terms of the above operators, the discretized prior covariance is
\mathcal{C}_{0}=([\alpha A+M]^{-1}M)^{2}
and samples of
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0},\mathcal{C}_{0})

[
7

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}={\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}+[\alpha A+M]^{-1}L{\boldsymbol{z}}
,
where
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a draw from an i.i.d. standard normal distribution, and
L
satisfies
LL^{T}=M
.
For brevity and to emphasize that the following analysis does not depend on the choice of the covariance operator,
we denote
\tilde{L}:=[\alpha A+M]^{-1}L
. Next, we sketch the computation of the probability function and its gradient for this bilinear control problem.

where

draws from the uniform distribution of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

{\boldsymbol{f}}
are the coefficients of the discretization of

We denote

(51)

Note that the partial derivatives (
47

48

\nabla\tilde{\varphi}(u)
given by (

(A(u)^{-1})^{\prime}h

\tilde{\varphi}(u)

\tilde{\varphi}(u)
the variables

and
{\boldsymbol{y}}_{0}^{u}
as independent of each other, but constrain the objective by the equation for the mean state and for the state samples (

\nabla\tilde{\varphi}(u)
by taking partial variations of the corresponding Lagrangian function
[

The subsequent two lemmas are slight extensions of the results from

Lemma 11
.

Let
U,\tilde{Y}
be normed spaces,
K\subseteq\tilde{Y}
be a convex subset, and
g:U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\tilde{Y}
be a linear mapping. Suppose further that
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
is an
n
-dimensional random vector on some probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
whose distribution has a log-concave density. Then, the set

M:=\{u\in U:\mathbb{P}(g(u,{\boldsymbol{\zeta}})\in K)\geq p\}

is convex for arbitrary
p\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

Let
p\in[0,1]
be arbitrary. Define the set-valued mapping

H:U\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

H(u):=\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:g(u,{\boldsymbol{z}})\in K\}\qquad(u\in U).

Note that the images
H(u)
are convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for all
u\in U
thanks to
K
being convex and
g
being linear. Although convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
need not be Borel measurable, they are Lebesgue measurable (see
[
28
]
) and hence, with the random vector
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
having a density by assumption, the probability
\mathbb{P}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\in H(u))
is well defined for all
u\in U
. Now, let
u^{1},u^{2}\in M
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
be arbitrarily given. This means that
\mathbb{P}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\in H(u^{1}))\geq p
and
\mathbb{P}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\in H(u^{2}))\geq p
. We have to show that
\lambda u^{1}+(1-\lambda)u^{2}\in M
. We claim that

H(\lambda u^{1}+(1-\lambda)u^{2})\supseteq\lambda H(u^{1})+(1-\lambda)H(u^{2}),

(52)

where the right hand side is the set of element-wise sums.
In fact, assuming
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\lambda H(u^{1})+(1-\lambda)H(u^{2})
, there must exist
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1}\in H(u^{1})
and
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}\in H(u^{2})
such that
{\boldsymbol{z}}=\lambda{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1}+(1-\lambda){\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}
. Accordingly,
g(u^{1},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1}),g(u^{2},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2})\in K
and, hence, by linearity of
g
and convexity of
K
:

g(\lambda u^{1}+(1-\lambda)u^{2},{\boldsymbol{z}}))=g(\lambda(u^{1},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1})+(1-\lambda)(u^{2},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2}))=\lambda g(u^{1},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{1})+(1-\lambda)g(u^{2},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{2})\in K.

This proves that
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in H(\lambda u^{1}+(1-\lambda)u^{2})
. By (
52
), we continue as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\in H(\lambda u^{1}+(1-\lambda)u^{2}))

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\in\lambda H(u^{1})+(1-\lambda)H(u^{2}))

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle[\mathbb{P}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\in H(u^{1}))]^{\lambda}\cdot[\mathbb{P}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\in H(u^{2}))]^{1-\lambda}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle p^{\lambda}p^{1-\lambda}=p

Here, the second inequality follows from Prékopas Theorem stating that a log-concave density (assumed here) induces a log-concave probability measure
[
35
, Theorem 4.2.1]
. Thus, we have shown the desired relation
\lambda u^{1}+(1-\lambda)u^{2}\in M
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
U,\tilde{Y}
be Banach spaces,
K\subseteq\tilde{Y}
a weakly closed subset and
g:U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\tilde{Y}
a weakly sequentially continuous mapping. Suppose further that
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
is an
n
-dimensional random vector on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
. Then, the probability function
\tilde{\varphi}:U\to[0,1]
defined by

\tilde{\varphi}(u):=\mathbb{P}(g(u,{\boldsymbol{\zeta}})\in K)\qquad(u\in U)

is weakly sequentially upper semicontinuous.

Proof.

Observe first that

\{\omega\in\Omega\mid g(u,{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(\omega))\in K\}\in\mathcal{A}
for arbitrarily
u\in U

because the sets
\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid g(u,{\boldsymbol{z}})\in K\}
are closed by weak sequential continuity of
g
and weak closedness of
K
.
Fix an arbitrary
\bar{u}
and let
u_{n}\rightharpoonup\bar{u}
be a weakly convergent sequence.
Denote by
u_{n_{l}}
a subsequence such that

\lim\sup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{\varphi}(u_{n})=\lim_{l\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{\varphi}(u_{n_{l}}).

(53)

Define the sets
A,A_{n}\in\mathcal{A}
by

A:=\left\{\omega\in\Omega:g\left(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\left(\omega\right)\right)\in K\right\};\quad A_{n}:=\left\{\omega\in\Omega:g\left(u_{n},{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\left(\omega\right)\right)\in K\right\}\quad\left(n\in\mathbb{N}\right).

Now, consider an arbitrary
\omega\in\Omega\setminus A
. Then,
g\left(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\left(\omega\right)\right)\notin K
. Since
g
is weakly sequentially continuous, we have
g\left(u_{n},{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\left(\omega\right)\right)\rightharpoonup g\left(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\left(\omega\right)\right)
. Since
\tilde{Y}\setminus K
is weakly open, it follows that

\forall\omega\in\Omega\setminus A\quad\exists n_{0}(\omega):g(u_{n},{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\left(\omega\right))\notin K\quad\forall n\geq n_{0}(\omega).

Denoting by
\chi_{C}

the characteristic function of a set
C
, it follows that

\chi_{A_{n}}\left(\omega\right)\rightarrow 0
as
n\to\infty
for all
\omega\in\Omega\backslash A
. By the dominated convergence theorem,

\int_{\Omega\backslash A}\chi_{A_{n}}\left(\omega\right)\mathbb{P}\left(d\omega\right)\rightarrow 0\quad\forall\omega\in\Omega\backslash A\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

On the other hand,
\chi_{A_{n}}\left(\omega\right)\leq\chi_{A}\left(\omega\right)=1
for
\omega\in A
, whence

\displaystyle\lim_{l\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{\varphi}(u_{n_{l}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{l\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(g\left(u_{n_{l}},{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\right)\in K\right)=\lim_{l\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}\chi_{A_{n_{l}}}\left(\omega\right)\mathbb{P}\left(d\omega\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lim_{l\rightarrow\infty}\sup\int_{\Omega\backslash A}\chi_{A_{n_{l}}}\left(\omega\right)\mathbb{P}\left(d\omega\right)+\lim_{l\rightarrow\infty}\sup\int_{A}\chi_{A_{n_{l}}}\left(\omega\right)\mathbb{P}\left(d\omega\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{l\rightarrow\infty}\sup\int_{A}\chi_{A_{n_{l}}}\left(\omega\right)\mathbb{P}\left(d\omega\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lim_{l\rightarrow\infty}\sup\int_{A}\mathbb{P}\left(d\omega\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(A\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(g\left(\bar{u},{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}\right)\in K\right)=\tilde{\varphi}(\bar{u}).

Combining this with (
53
) results in
\varphi
being weakly sequentially upper semicontinuous in
\bar{u}
.
