Harnessing Vinyl Cation Reactivity Enables Oxime Ether Formation and an Unusual Base-Promoted Rearrangement to Esters
Authors: Wenhua Lin, Djamila Azrou, Shanshan Zhang, Régis Guillot, Marie Vayer, Aurélien Alix, Vincent Gandon, Christophe Bour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Harnessing vinyl cation reactivity under controlled conditions remains a longstanding challenge in carbocation chemistry, as the ionization of vinyl triflates and related precursors is often slow, limiting the development of general and selective reaction manifolds involving vinyl cations. Here, we report an aluminum-catalyzed strategy that generates and intercepts vinyl cation intermediates from vinyl triflates, enabling direct access to oxime ethers using readily available nitroalkanes. Oxime ether formation often exhibits a pronounced Z selectivity (up to Z/E = 93:7, depending on the substrate), affording geometrically defined products. Mechanistic studies supported by DFT calculations suggest the intermediacy of an N-oxomethaniminium species, revealing a previously unrecognized reactivity pathway. In addition to oxime ether formation, these intermediates undergo a base-promoted rearrangement to esters, enabling a base-gated tandem transformation directly from vinyl triflates. A broad substrate scope (64 examples) demonstrates the robustness of this vinyl cation platform and provides unified access to defined oxime ethers and esters.

Introduction

Vinyl cations are among the most reactive yet kinetically challenging carbocationic intermediates, as their clean generation from practical precursors is often slow and requires elevated temperatures. While stabilized sp2 carbocations can now be accessed and exploited with high chemo- and regioselectivity, extending comparable control to weakly resonance-stabilized dicoordinate (sp) vinyl cations remains considerably more difficult. Recent advances in Lewis acid catalysis and weakly coordinating anion chemistry have begun to address this limitation, enabling heterolytic activation pathways that grant access to well-defined vinyl cation reactivity. Building on these developments, we asked whether controlled ionization of vinyl triflates could unlock new nitrogen-centered transformations and divergent rearrangement pathways beyond classical vinylation chemistry.

Considerable effort has therefore been devoted to developing methods for the clean generation and selective trapping of vinyl cations. Practical access most commonly proceeds via heterolytic cleavage of C­(sp2)–X bonds (X = Cl, Br, OSO2R, N2 +···), where ion pairing and solvent effects critically govern the concentration and lifetime of the resulting vinyl cations. In this context, weakly coordinating anions (WCAs) play a pivotal role. Indeed, highly delocalized weakly basic counterions such as perfluoroalkoxyaluminates (e.g., [Al­(OC­(CF3)3)4]−, Krossing’s anion) , and carboranes (e.g., [HCB11Cl11]−), minimize ion pairing and favor vinyl cation formation/reactivity under comparatively mild conditions (Scheme A, left for carboranes). Consistent with this paradigm, we have reported lithium-catalyzed vinylations of arenes and difluoroenoxysilanes that proceed via highly reactive vinyl cation intermediates paired with [Al­(OC­(CF3)3)4]−, thereby illustrating the ability of this weakly coordinating anion to support productive vinyl cation generation and trapping under mild conditions (Scheme A, right). Importantly, this conceptual framework extends beyond classical WCAs to include counterions that combine low nucleophilicity with substantial charge delocalization. In this regard, aluminates featuring the [AlCl3OTf]− anion have been shown to be fully compatible with vinyl cation generation and reactivity, as established in seminal studies. On this basis, vinyl triflates are activated under the Lewis-acidic conditions provided by AlCl3, facilitating heterolytic C–OTf bond cleavage and the generation of vinyl cation intermediates that undergo transition-metal-free intramolecular vinylation reactions (Scheme A, right). ,

With reliable access to well-defined vinyl cations in hand, target selection becomes a strategic consideration. We therefore directed our attention toward oxime ethers, a functional class prevalent in numerous bioactive scaffolds, including fluvoxamine, siponimod, ridogrel, and oxiconazole. Beyond their pharmaceutical relevance, oxime ethers also find broad application in agrochemistry as insecticidal agents and in materials science. From a reactivity standpoint, oxime ethers serve as robust imine surrogates. , Beyond accessibility, controlling oxime ether geometry remains nontrivial, and Z-enriched oxime ethers are comparatively less common from direct oxime-ether-forming manifolds. The n→π* donation from the oxygen to the CN bond enhances both kinetic and thermodynamic stability relative to imines and hydrazones, while the −CN–O– motif provides a versatile handle for the elaboration of nitrogen-containing architectures. However, direct and mild access to (stereodefined) oxime ethers from simple precursors remains uncommon, and prior vinyl cation-enabled entries from nitroalkanes have typically required harsh conditions and provided modest yields (Scheme B).

The classical synthesis of oxime ethers involves condensation of aldehydes or ketones to hydroxylamine (NH2OH), followed by O-alkylation (Scheme B, top left). However, the instability and corrosive nature of free NH2OH raise significant safety concerns, which have limited its direct use in synthesis. To address these issues, safer multistep strategies have been widely adopted since the early 2000s, relying on the alkylation of protected hydroxylamine derivatives (e.g., BocNHOH, CbzNHOH, or PhthNOH) using alkyl halides or alcohols under Mitsunobu conditions, followed by deprotection and condensation. While operationally safer, these approaches remain step-intensive. More recently, alternative methods aiming at the direct in situ generation or activation of NH2OH equivalents have been reported. Among these, Li and co-workers described the in situ generation of NH2OH from NO x over MgO nanoparticles, while Zhang and co-workers employed electrochemical activation of nitromethane (MeNO2) to access isoxazoline aldoximes. Earlier studies by Kadentsev, and later by Mel’nikova, demonstrated that MeNO2 can function as both an oxygen and nitrogen source for oxime formation via electrophilic addition to alkynes proceeding through vinyl cation intermediates. Nevertheless, the harsh reaction conditions and modest yields reported in these studies limited broader adoption of this approach (Scheme B).

Building on prior work, we wondered whether vinyl triflates could serve as practical precursors to oxime ethers under mild conditions. We envisioned that activation of the C­(sp2)–O bond would generate a highly electrophilic intermediate that could be trapped by the nitro compound acting as a 1,3-dipole analogue, ultimately enabling O-to-C oxime transfer (Scheme C, left). Although a concerted pathway may be considered, a stepwise mechanism involving an N-oxomethaniminium intermediate remains plausible at this stage (Scheme C, right).

Guided by these design principles, we demonstrate that controlled ionization of vinyl triflates enables selective interception by nitroalkanes to afford oxime ethers, often with a pronounced Z selectivity (Scheme D). Mechanistic studies (kinetics, NMR experiments, and DFT calculations) support a well-defined pathway involving an N-oxomethaniminium intermediate. This pathway also reveals an unusual base-promoted rearrangement to esters, which can be performed directly from vinyl triflates in a tandem sequence. Collectively, these studies show that controlled vinyl cation capture generates oxime ethers that act as programmable intermediates, enabling divergent rearrangement pathways. This late-stage transformation further highlights the synthetic potential of O-functionalized oxime scaffolds accessed through vinyl cation chemistry.

Results and Discussion

Reaction Development and Optimization

Having established a conceptual framework for controlled vinyl cation capture, we first identified conditions that maximize selective interception of the cationic intermediate by nitroalkanes. Using vinyl triflate 1a as a model system, AlCl3 (20 mol %) in MeNO2 (2a) at 80 °C for 2 h in the presence of 4 Å molecular sieves afforded oxime ether 3 in 92% yield (Table , entry 1; see also Table S1 in the Supporting Information). These conditions were therefore adopted as the standard for subsequent studies. The beneficial role of 4 Å MS was confirmed by control experiments, which showed that their omission led to partial hydrolysis of 1a and a reduced yield of 3 (68%, entry 2). Other Group 13 trihalides, including BCl3, GaCl3, InCl3 and AlBr3, were also competent promoters of the reaction, delivering 3 in 65–86% yield, although none matched the performance of AlCl3 (entry 4). In contrast, the Krossing salt, Li­[Al­(OC­(CF3)3)4], previously shown to facilitate vinyl cation generation, furnished 3 in 94% yield (entry 5), consistent with the ability of WCAs to enhance the electrophilicity and stability of the vinyl cation intermediate. Given the similar performance of Li­[Al­(OC­(CF3)3)4] and AlCl3, and the greater availability and lower cost of the latter, AlCl3 was selected as the catalyst of choice for further studies. Under otherwise analogous conditions, stoichiometric Brønsted acids (HOTf or H2SO4) also promoted product formation, affording 3 in 73 and 61% yield respectively (entry 6). Although less efficient than AlCl3 under the conditions examined, these results indicate that protic activation may also be viable.

Solvent effects were next examined using 10 equiv of MeNO2 (entries 7–10), revealing a pronounced dependence on medium polarity. The reaction proceeded moderately in the strongly protic solvent hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP, 63%, entry 7), but was noticeably less efficient in 1,2-dichloroethane (DCE, 25%, entry 8) and hexane (17%, entry 9), and was completely suppressed in toluene, tetrahydrofuran (THF), and acetonitrile (MeCN) (entry 10). Notably, in the absence of a catalyst, formation of 3 was still observed in 33% yield (entry 11), suggesting that slow heterolytic cleavage of the vinyl triflate can occur under polar conditions at 80 °C. Taken together, these results show that AlCl3 in MeNO2 under anhydrous conditions is an efficient and simple system for promoting the generation of vinyl cations and their subsequent interception by nitromethane, yielding the corresponding oxime ethers in high yield under mild, transition-metal-free conditions.

Scope

To demonstrate the efficiency and generality of this aluminum-catalyzed oxime ether formation, a broad range of substrates was examined (Scheme ). As mentioned above (Table ), the model substrate 3 can be obtained in 89% isolated yield under the optimized reaction conditions. The robustness of the process was further demonstrated by a gram-scale synthesis of 3 from 1a (5 mmol, 2.30 g) performed with a 10-fold decrease in AlCl3 loading relative to the standard conditions in order to minimize catalyst loading, yet affording the desired product in a sustained 65% yield. The lower yield in this case was attributed to partial hydrolysis of the product, which occurred despite the presence of 4 Å molecular sieves as well as to the reduced amount of AlCl3 employed.

Various benzyl-substituted derivatives (4–10) were successfully prepared in good to excellent yields (78–92%). The structures of 3, 4, and 6 were unambiguously confirmed by X-ray crystallographic analysis. Notably, these oxime ethers are bench-stable and can be handled and stored without special precautions. The presence of an electron-withdrawing group on the benzene ring proved essential, as substrates lacking such substituents preferentially underwent intramolecular Friedel–Crafts cyclization rather than the desired transformation. However, the benzylic substituent itself was not required and could be replaced with a range of alkyl groups. Vinyl triflates bearing methyl (11, 82%), ethyl (12, 68%), propyl (13, 56%), n-butyl (14, 86%), and 4-phenylbutyl (15, 28%) substituents all furnished the corresponding oxime ethers. Other groups such as cyclohexylmethyl (16, 45%), allyl (17, 42%), and phenyl (18, 64%) were also well tolerated, underscoring the robustness of the transformation. Importantly, no competing cyclization or C–H activation processes were detected under these conditions. In contrast, the lower yields obtained with substrates bearing longer alkyl chains (e.g., 15 and 16) were attributed to competing intramolecular vinylation pathways.

Replacement of the ester group with a ketone was also feasible, although the corresponding product 19 was isolated in only 34% yield due to similar side reactions. Replacement of the ester group by a hydrogen atom furnished 20–22 in good to moderate yields (20, 71%; 21, 25%; 22, 36%) from the corresponding 1-phenylvinyl triflates. These diminished yields were ascribed to elimination pathways leading to ethynylbenzene byproducts. The aromatic moiety of the substrate proved crucial for stabilizing the vinyl cation intermediate and could not be replaced by an alkyl substituent (see the Supporting Information). Nevertheless, the arene framework could be functionalized with both electron-withdrawing and -donating groups, affording 23–31 in moderate to excellent yields (22–93%). The structure of 23 was confirmed by X-ray crystallography. In line with earlier observations, intramolecular vinylation again limited the yield of 30 to 22%.

Finally, more extended π-systems, such as phenanthrene (32–33) and heteroaromatic pyridyl derivatives (34), were also compatible with the reaction conditions, delivering the corresponding oxime ethers in moderate yields (40–64%). These results further highlight the structural diversity accommodated by this transformation.

To further demonstrate the generality of the method, we next expanded the scope to other nitroalkanes (Scheme ). Notably, this extension provides straightforward access to Z-enriched oxime ethers, a stereochemical outcome that can be challenging to obtain selectively by alternative approaches. Reaction of various vinyl triflates with nitroethane 2b efficiently furnished the corresponding oxime ethers 35–43 in synthetically useful yields across a broad substrate range. Across the substrate set, the reaction displays a recurrent Z preference, with Z/E ratios ranging from ∼50:50 to 93:7 depending on the vinyl triflate and nitroalkane partner (see the Supporting Information for details). The relative configurations of the major and minor isomers were unambiguously assigned by comprehensive 1D and 2D NMR analyses, and the structure of one representative diastereomer (36) was confirmed by single-crystal X-ray diffraction. Encouraged by these results, additional nitroalkanes were examined. 1-Nitropropane 2c proved to be an efficient nucleophile, providing the corresponding oxime ethers 44–49 in moderate to good yields (34–85%) with E/Z ratios comparable to those observed with nitroethane. Secondary nitroalkanes, including 2-nitropropane 2d and nitrocyclohexane 2e, also participated smoothly, affording oxime ethers 50–56 in high purity. Finally, functionalized nitro derivatives were compatible with the reaction conditions, as illustrated by ethyl 2-nitroacetate, which furnished compound 57 in 30% yield after 24 h. By contrast, aryl-substituted nitroalkanes such as (nitromethyl)­benzene and (2-nitroethyl)­benzene did not afford the desired oxime ethers under the conditions using 10 equiv of nitroalkane in HFIP; only the hydrolysis product of the vinyl triflate was observed (see the Supporting Information, part 3.4). To further demonstrate the potential for postfunctionalization and the synthetic utility of the oxime ether scaffold, several downstream transformations were explored using compound 3 (see Supporting Information, part 5).

Mechanistic Studies

The selective addition of nitromethane to this vinyl triflate 1a raises the question of whether this transformation proceeds through a concerted or a stepwise oxime transfer (vide supra). Related reactions have historically been proposed to operate via a dioxazolidine-like intermediate (Scheme A, left, see Figure S4 in the SI for an analysis of the coordination modes of AlCl3 to 1). However, alternative mechanistic pathways can also be considered, including a stepwise oxime transfer via an N-oxomethaniminium aluminum-enolate (Scheme A, right). Alternatively, nitromethane may add to hydrolysis- and/or elimination-derived species originating from the vinyl triflate. The latter indirect pathways were ruled out by control experiments, as no reaction occurred when starting from the β-ketoester S1a or from a monosubstituted alkyne (Scheme B). These results also argue against the involvement of a hidden superacid. Moreover, these control reactions underscore the broader versatility of the present reaction conditions relative to earlier reports, which relied on alkyne substrates. , To clarify the origin of the hydrolysis side product S1a, we conducted a control experiment in a mixed solvent system of MeNO2 and D2O (1:1). Under these conditions, the ketone S1a was obtained in 83% yield with clear deuterium incorporation at the α-position (Scheme C). This isotopic labeling demonstrates that hydrolysis occurs during the reaction and that exchange of deuterium at the α-carbon is possible under the acidic conditions generated in situ. Attempts to intercept this putative enol/enolate intermediate with TMSCl or benzyl bromide were unsuccessful, consistent with a transient, tightly associated aluminum enolate that rapidly evolves toward oxime ether formation. These findings are consistent with the formation of a reactive cationic vinyl intermediate that can be intercepted by water, accounting for the observed side product. To further probe the mechanism, a kinetic isotope effect (KIE) experiment was conducted by performing the reaction in a 1:1 mixture of MeNO2 and CD3NO2. Analysis of the product distribution revealed a small KIE of k H/k D = 1.19 (Scheme D). The absence of a significant primary KIE suggests that the C–H activation at nitromethane does not contribute appreciably to the turnover-limiting transition state.

To gain mechanistic insight, DFT calculations were performed at the ωB97X–D/def2–TZVPP level to examine the pathway leading to oxime formation (Scheme E and see the Supporting Information for computational details). We initially considered that AlCl3 promotes generation of a vinyl cation from the corresponding vinyl triflate, followed by a concerted addition of nitromethane analogous to the previously reported pathway (see Scheme A, left). , However, the computed activation barriers for the trans- and cis-addition of MeNO2 (33.2 and 32.7 kcal·mol–1, respectively) are incompatible with a reaction occurring at 80 °C within 2 h (Figure S4). Even higher barriers were obtained for subsequent deprotonation steps from the corresponding intermediates (D or F in Figure S4). Moreover, attempts to form intermediates with deprotonated nitromethane (CH2NO2 –) were unsuccessful (D′ or F′ in Figure S5), as the anion invariably added to the phenyl ring rather than to the central sp-carbon atom. These results argue against a concerted or base-assisted pathway involving free vinyl cations. We therefore considered an alternative scenario in which AlCl3 not only facilitates vinyl cation formation but remains coordinated throughout the subsequent steps (see metal-assisted fragmentation, Scheme A, right and Scheme E). Coordination of AlCl3 to the ester carbonyl is slightly endergonic (H, +5.8 kcal·mol–1) but dramatically lowers the barrier for nitromethane addition, with TS HI and TS HJ located at 14.3 and 11.0 kcal·mol–1, respectively. The trans-addition product I (+0.7 kcal·mol–1) represents a dead end, whereas the cis-addition product J (−3.4 kcal·mol–1) is kinetically favored and undergoes exergonic deprotonation to afford K (−11.5 kcal·mol–1). Accordingly, the observed Z bias is consistent with a stereodefining, coordination-controlled addition manifold, while the subsequent fragmentation rationalizes the formation and reactivity of the N-oxomethaniminium species. Upon addition of 1 equiv of AlCl3 to 1a, the fluorine resonance associated with the OTf group undergoes a pronounced downfield shift, consistent with substrate coordination to AlCl3. Concomitant perturbations of the ester proton signals further suggest involvement of the carbonyl oxygen in a secondary coordination mode. These spectroscopic observations are consistent with the DFT-calculated structures and support a mechanism involving persistent AlCl3 coordination during the key bond forming steps. These data indicate that AlCl3 is not merely a triflate abstractor but remains engaged through coordination, which both lowers the barrier for productive addition and controls alkene geometry.

From intermediate K, an energetically accessible pathway emerges, consistent with the hypothesis formulated in Scheme A, right: K readily converts into the aluminum enolate L (−6.6 kcal·mol–1) via TS KL (−4.2 kcal·mol–1), accompanied by elimination of an N-oxomethaniminium species (blue part in L). Although rarely discussed outside theoretical or mass spectrometric studies, this electrophilic cation subsequently reacts with the enolate part of L to afford complex M (−46.0 kcal·mol–1) in a strongly exergonic manner via TS LM (−0.4 kcal·mol–1).

The transformation of L into M should also account for the stereoselectivity at the oxime moiety. TS LM was recomputed with additional nitro derivatives toward E or Z oximes (Table ). The results are qualitatively in good agreement with the observed major stereoisomer. With nitroethane (entry 1), the transition state toward the Z isomer is 1.7 kcal/mol more stable than the transition state toward the E isomer. The difference in free energy increases to 2.6 kcal/mol with nitropropane (entry 2), which is in line with the increase of the proportion of the Z isomer compared to nitroethane. With methyl nitroformate (entry 3), the predicted major isomer becomes the E one, with a difference of free energy of activation of 1.5 kcal/mol. This is also in line with the experimental outcome when using ethyl nitroformate.

An explanation for the Z selectivity with nitroalkanes is the steric hindrance between the AlCl3 fragment and the alkyl chain (Figure ). Taken together, these computational and spectroscopic data define a mechanistic pathway that differs fundamentally from those previously reported. Rather than acting solely as an initiator for triflate abstraction, AlCl3 remains persistently coordinated, lowering the barrier for productive nitromethane addition and steering the reaction through a nonclassical yet energetically viable cascade. The DFT results, notably the reduction of the key addition barrier to 11.0 kcal·mol–1 and an overall maximum barrier of 21.6 kcal·mol–1, provides a mechanistic framework for AlCl3-mediated activation and oxime ether formation. From K, fragmentation toward N-oxomethaniminium, followed by electrophilic capture, explains the reactivity that would otherwise be difficult to rationalize.

Divergent Reactivity of Vinyl Cation-Derived Oxime Ethers

Controlled capture of vinyl cations provides rapid access to oxime ethers from vinyl triflates. The synthetic impact of this platform is further amplified if these products can serve as divergent linchpins. In particular, the unusual N–O functionality conjugated to an enol ester suggested that vinyl cation-derived oxime ethers might display reactivity patterns distinct from classical oxime ether chemistry. Guided by this hypothesis, we found that base addition switches the outcome: oxime ether 3 can be converted into ester 60, establishing a base-gated sequence in which vinyl triflates 1 afford either oxime ethers or, upon base treatment, esters.

After optimization, K2CO3 (3 equiv) in MeNO2 at 120 °C in the presence of AlCl3 (20 mol %) afforded ester 60 in 61% yield in a one-pot, two-step protocol directly from vinyl triflate 1a (Scheme A and Table S2). Further yield improvement was limited by competing pathways leading to ketone formation, which also complicated purification. The structure of 60 was unambiguously confirmed by single-crystal X-ray diffraction.

Although related α-benzoyloxy ester derivatives may in some cases be accessible through classical strategies, the present transformation should be viewed from a different perspective. Rather than serving as a general replacement for established ester syntheses, this sequence demonstrates that vinyl cation-derived oxime ethers are not merely isolable N–O-containing products, but can also serve as divergent synthetic precursors. In this context, ester formation reveals an unexpected base-gated reactivity mode, in which the same vinyl triflate/nitroalkane platform can be directed either toward oxime ethers or, upon base treatment, toward ester products. Importantly, this transformation does not simply reproduce a classical ester synthesis through a different route. Instead, it suggests that the oxime ether intermediate can act as an internal oxygen source in a peroxide-free oxygen-transfer/fragmentation process, conceptually related to recently reported cyanide-enabled oxygen-transfer reactions in β-oxoester chemistry. In line with this rationale, using MeNO2 as both reagent and solvent allows sequential oxime ether formation and base-promoted rearrangement in a single vessel.

The tandem sequence proved general across vinyl triflates bearing electron-withdrawing substituents at benzylic and/or aromatic positions, delivering esters 60–68 in 31–61% yield (Scheme A). While yields are moderate for some substrates, the transformation is operationally simple, reproducible across electronically diverse examples, and provides unified access to structurally varied esters directly from vinyl triflates. Electron-neutral and electron-rich analogues preferentially undergo competing pathways (Friedel–Crafts/vinylation).

Several experiments support a fragmentation/oxygen-transfer scenario rather than a stepwise mechanism via hydrolysis products. Based on these observations, we propose that the rearrangement proceeds through extrusion of CN-containing fragment en route to the ester (Scheme B). Although free HCN can polymerize at elevated temperature, such processes typically require prolonged reaction times. We therefore hypothesized that any transient HCN (or related CN-containing species) is rapidly quenched under the basic conditions. Consistent with the formation of gaseous byproducts under the rearrangement conditions, a CO2-trapping experiment was conducted by connecting the reaction vessel to an aqueous Ca­(OH)2 trap; formation of a white precipitate from the initially clear solution provides direct evidence for CO2 evolution. In addition, deuterium-exchange experiments show that the α-hydrogen in 60 -d is introduced under basic conditions from nitromethane, rather than transferred from oxime ether 3, and the hydrolysis-derived ketone S1a does not undergo conversion to the ester 60 under the rearrangement conditions, arguing against ketone intermediacy. Collectively, these data support a fragmentation pathway involving a CN extrusion followed by acyl-transfer/retro-Dieckmann-type steps (Scheme C); a detailed mechanistic proposal is provided in the Supporting Information.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed a strategy for controlled vinyl cation interception that converts vinyl triflates into oxime ethers using nitroalkanes and uncovers an unusual base-promoted rearrangement to esters. This aluminum-catalyzed protocol promotes triflate abstraction to access reactive vinyl cation intermediates and uniquely allows nitroalkanes to be employed as safer and readily available surrogates for hydroxylamine. The transformation is operationally simple, scalable, and displays broad substrate scope, as demonstrated across 64 examples. Mechanistic investigations, supported by DFT calculations, are consistent with a vinyl cation manifold and reveal the involvement of an unusual N-oxomethaniminium cation intermediate. Beyond oxime ether formation, we disclose an unusual base-promoted rearrangement to esters, which can be accessed directly from vinyl triflates in a tandem sequence. Overall, these results establish nitroalkanes as practical reagents for vinyl cation interception and highlight vinyl cation-derived oxime ethers as versatile intermediates for divergent reactivity leading to esters. Ongoing efforts are directed toward extending this strategy to more complex nitroalkane partners and toward the downstream functionalization of the resulting products into useful molecules, including biologically relevant compounds.
