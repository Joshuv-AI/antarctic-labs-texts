Histopathologic findings of the lens capsule and persistent hyperplastic primary vitreous in Korean pediatric cataract patients
Authors: Chan-Ho Cho, Ji Yeon Kim, Woo Gyeong Kim, Joo Yeon Kim, Hyun Woong Kim, Yikyeong Chun, Wan Soo Kim, Yeon Mee Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to measure lens capsule thickness and investigate histopathologic characteristics of persistent hyperplastic primary vitreous (PHPV) in Korean pediatric cataracts. We analyzed lens capsules from 116 eyes of 83 pediatric cataract patients treated between 2011 and 2015. The mean thickness of the anterior/posterior capsule was 7.21 ± 1.74/4.39 ± 1.41 μm. PHPV was observed in 11.2% (13/116) of eyes. Histologic examination revealed that PHPV is typically characterized by retrolenticular membranes with hypercellular membrane tissue comprising vascular structures and/or mesenchymal cells, seen in 69% of cases. Only 3 patients had hyaloid arteries and endothelium-lined blood vessels in the retrolenticular membranes, whereas six eyes showed only mesenchymal cells. Lens capsule thickness did not significantly vary with age or the presence of PHPV in Korean pediatric cataracts. Introduction

Pediatric cataracts are rare and primarily caused by congenital factors. Meta-analysis reviews have reported its global prevalence to range from 0.32 to 22.9 per 10,000 children1. Additionally, its prevalence in the Asian population is approximately 3.78 per 10,000 children2. Persistent hyperplastic primary vitreous (PHPV) is a rare congenital eye anomaly where the hyaloid vessel system fails to regress after birth3–5. The hyaloid vessel is a transient intraocular vascular system formed during ocular development. This system consists of the hyaloid artery, vasa hyaloidea propria, and tunica vasculosa lentis. The hyaloid vessel extends from the optic disc to the posterior surface of the lens, providing nutrients to the developing eye. As retinal vascularization progresses and the primary vitreous disappears by birth, the hyaloid vessel gradually regresses. PHPV is caused by the failure to complete involution of the hyaloid vascular system, resulting in various ocular complications.

The key mechanisms of hyaloid vessel regression are apoptosis and autophagy; however, their exact mechanisms are not well-defined6. Several molecular factors, such as arf, p53, frizzled-5, and ephrin-A5, have been associated with unsuccessful hyaloid regression in animal models7. Hyaloid system failure is also potentially caused by genetic changes affecting astrocytes. Mutations in the NDP, FZD4, LRP5, and ATOH7 genes are critical in PHPV development8.

Retrolental fibrovascular membranes located behind the lens are a characteristic pathological feature of PHPV. The invasion of the vascularized membrane into the lens can cause intraocular hemorrhage, a leading cause of congenital cataracts. Goldberg, who coined the term “persistent fetal vasculature,” used this term instead of PHPV to emphasize the role of fetal vessels in this condition9.

Understanding the vascular component of retrolental tissues is crucial for comprehending PHPV pathogenesis. However, the morphologic changes of retrolental vascular structures after birth and the regression of these vessels’ pathogenesis are still unclear10,11. Furthermore, there is a lack of thorough data on the lens capsule in children, especially in Korean patients. Therefore, this cross-sectional study aims to (1) measure lens capsule thickness in Korean pediatric cataract patients and (2) investigate the histopathological and immunohistochemical characteristics of retrolental tissues in patients with PHPV, correlating these findings with clinical features.

Materials and methods

Subject, inclusion criteria, and ethical approval

This cross-sectional study involved pediatric patients aged < 16 who underwent cataract surgery at Inje University Haeundae Paik Hospital from January 2011 to March 2015. The exclusion criteria for the study were being 16 years of age or older at the time of surgery or undergoing surgery for traumatic cataracts. The study involved 116 cataracts from 83 consecutive patients diagnosed with unilateral or bilateral congenital cataracts. Histopathological analysis was conducted on the anterior and posterior lens capsule, as well as the retrolental membrane collected during cataract surgery. Clinical analysis was performed using the patient’s medical records.

This study adheres to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and is approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Inje University Haeundae Paik Hospital for the collection and use of biologic specimens and clinical data for research (approval no. 2023-12-019). Preoperatively, written consent was obtained from the parents of all patients for the collection of lens capsules and retrolental membrane samples and participation in the study.

Epidemiological and clinical data analysis

For epidemiological analysis, we examined the patient’s age and gender at the time of surgery. Clinical data analysis was also performed to analyze the type of cataract. In addition, the patients’ biometry (using IOLMaster 500; Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany) and specular microscopy (using SP-8800; Konan Medical Inc., Nishinomiya, Hyogo, Japan) were measured and examined preoperatively. Specifically, we examined the axial length, keratometry, and cylindrical values for biometric measurements. Specular microscopy was used to measure corneal endothelial cell density, hexagonality, and pachymetry values. Furthermore, we investigated any ophthalmic and systemic abnormalities as potential underlying conditions.

Operation procedure and clinical diagnosis of PHPV

All surgical procedures were performed by a single surgeon (W.S.K.) under general anesthesia. The procedure began with a 2.75 mm clear corneal incision at the upper corneal limbus, followed by the creation of a continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis measuring 5–5.5 mm in diameter, and then hydrodissection was then performed. Phacoemulsification and cortex aspiration were then carried out using the Infiniti Vision System (Alcon, Fort Worth, TX, USA). Posterior capsulotomy of 4–4.5 mm was performed, and a thorough anterior vitrectomy was performed after retrieving the posterior capsules in younger patients with smaller eyes (typically younger than 18 months). In older patients with cataracts, intraocular lens implantation was performed in the bag with optic capturing.

The diagnosis of PHPV complicated with pediatric cataracts was established by a single surgeon (W.S.K.) based on the typical findings of PHPV, including retrolental opacity, vitreous stalk, and intralenticular hemorrhage. These findings were observed during slit lamp examination, fundus examination, or B-scan ultrasonography (Fig. 1)12.

Clinical diagnostic clues of PHPV: (A) Direct visualization of any component of the fetal vasculature, cataract, or intralenticular hemorrhage. (B, C) Retrolental fibrovascular membrane or vitreous stalk. PHPV, persistent hyperplastic primary vitreous.

Tissue collection and histopathologic examination

Postoperatively, the lens capsular tissues removed through anterior or posterior capsulorrhexis were placed on a sterilized chemical indicator strip (3M™ Comply™ Ethylene Oxide Chemical Indicator Strip, 3M Korea, Seoul, Korea) and immediately sent to the pathology laboratory (Fig. 2). An additional retrolental membrane was obtained from all suspected cases of PHPV, which was sent to the pathology laboratory. Among the 13 clinically proven PHPVs, three were too small to undergo histological examination. Therefore, 10 cases of PHPV were processed for histopathological and immunohistochemical studies. In the pathology laboratory, the capsular tissue was treated with 10% neutral-buffered formalin, dehydrated in alcohol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. The paraffin blocks were then divided into 4 μm thick slices and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) as part of the routine procedure. In addition to H&E, a Periodic Acid-Schiff (PAS) stain was performed to measure lens capsule thickness. PAS staining helps in clearly outlining the lens capsule, as it shows a positive reaction for its basement membrane glycoprotein components. Two experienced pathologists (Y.M.K. and Ji.Y.K.), who were blinded to the clinical information, examined all microscopic findings. The thickness of the lens capsule was measured using the image analyzer. The examiner manually clicked the two points on the outer and inner surfaces of the capsule without lens epithelium on hematoxylin and eosin (X400), and the program calculated the thickness.

Lens capsule thickness measurement

The SlideViewer program (3D HISTECH, Budapest, Hungary, Version 2.6.0.166179) was used to measure the thickness of the lens capsule. The examiner manually clicked on two points on the outer and inner surfaces of the capsule, avoiding areas with lens epithelium on H&E and/or PAS-stained slides. The program then calculated the thickness. To measure capsule thickness, 5–10 randomly selected areas along the entire lens perimeter were measured at even distances, and the mean thickness was measured. The average value was then calculated (Fig. 3). Cases identified as posterior lenticonus were excluded from the capsule thickness analysis.

Immunohistochemical study

Immunohistochemistry was conducted using an automated immunostainer (BenchMark XT staining system, Ventana). The following antibodies were used to identify endothelial cells at various stages of development: CD31 (Dako; mouse monoclonal, clone JC70A, 1:100 dilution), CD34 (Thermo Scientific; mouse monoclonal, clone QBEnd/10, 1:1000 dilution), and Von Willebrand factor (Dako; mouse monoclonal, clone F8/86, 1:300 dilution). Vimentin (Invitrogen; mouse monoclonal, clone V9, 1:800 dilution) and pancytokeratin (Dako; mouse monoclonal, clone MNF116, 1:200 dilution) were also used to identify mesenchymal and epithelial cells, respectively. The staining was graded from 1 + to 3 + based on the relative development of vascular structures and the presence of mesenchymal cells.

Data analysis

Characteristics of the patient population were compared among cataract types and age groups using χ2 tests for normal variables and analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests for numerical variables. Mean values for the thickness of the anterior and posterior lens capsules were measured and then compared between study groups, which were categorized by age, gender, and predominant type of cataract. The comparison was conducted using the ANOVA test, and p-values were calculated.

Results

Epidemiological and clinical data of patients

Among the 83 patients included in the study, 45 (54.2%) were male. The mean age at the time of cataract surgery for the 116 eyes was 77.9 ± 47.7 months. The predominant cataract type was the posterior subcapsular type, accounting for 36.2% (42/116) of cases, followed by nuclear sclerosis at 18.1% and zonular/lamellar types at 15.5% (Table 1).

In ocular biometry, the mean axial length and keratometry value were 22.28 mm and 43.53 D, respectively. Specular microscopy revealed a mean endothelial cell density of 3,192 cells/mm2, with a mean hexagonality of 65.4% and a mean corneal thickness of 515.7 μm (Table 1).

Lens capsule thickness

After excluding the 12 eyes with lenticonus, 101 anterior and 57 posterior capsules were analyzed (Table 2). The mean thickness of the anterior capsule in the entire group was 7.20 ± 1.78 μm, without significant differences based on age, sex, or cataract type (ANOVA test results, p = 0.556, p = 0.274, p = 0.151, respectively). The mean thickness of the posterior capsule was 4.36 ± 1.36 μm, also showing no significant difference based on age, sex, or cataract type (ANOVA test results, p = 0.624, p = 0.733, p = 0.107, respectively) (Table 2).

Histopathological and immunohistochemical findings of the retrolental membrane in PHPV

The clinical characteristics of patients with PHPV are summarized in Table 4. The PHPV group comprised five boys and seven girls, with an average age of 43.9 (range, 2–123) months at the time of surgery. Bilateral PHPV was observed in 8.3% (1/12) of patients. Patients with bilateral PHPV underwent cataract extraction 2 weeks apart. Ten out of 12 patients were involved in their left eye. PHPV more commonly occurs in the posterior compared to the anterior capsule. Among the patients, three, three, and two boys had mental retardation, developmental delay, and chromosomal abnormality, respectively. Torticollis was observed in one girl with bilateral PHPV (Table 4).

A histological review of the excised specimens, either posterior or anterior capsules, revealed nine cases with hypercellular membrane tissue containing vascular structures with mesenchymal cells and collagen (Table 4; Fig. 4). The membrane sizes ranged from 0.18 × 0.1 mm to 1.4 × 1.1 mm in diameter. Vessels were not easily visible in the H&E staining, except for one case that displayed numerous capillary vessels in the fibrocalcific stroma (Case 6; Table 4; Fig. 4). In the H&E staining, six cases, including one case of bilateral PHPV, only revealed nests of mesenchymal (spindle) cells intermingled with collagen bundles in the membrane tissues (Table 4).

Immunohistochemistry studies confirmed the presence of endothelium-lined blood vessels in three cases using three different endothelial cell markers (CD31, CD34, and Von Willebrand factor) (Table 4; Fig. 4). These three endothelial cell markers were not differentially expressed in the vascular structures. In five cases, including the one with bilateral PHPV, only mesenchymal cells were observed. These cells tested positive for the immunohistochemical marker vimentin but negative for endothelial cell markers. In three cases of clinically diagnosed PHPV, fibrovascular membrane tissue could not be identified with H&E or immunohistochemistry (Table 4). The immunohistochemistry results for pancytokeratin showed non-specific background staining in the retrolenticular membranes and lens capsules (data not shown).

Discussion

This study found that the mean capsule thickness of the anterior and posterior capsules was 7.21 μm and 4.39 μm in 116 eyes of Korean pediatric cataract patients, respectively. The differences in sex, cylindrical value, corneal endothelial cell density, hexagonality, anterior capsule thickness, and posterior capsule thickness were not significant between PHPV and non-PHPV cases. Mesenchymal cells were detected in most cases of PHPV, with or without vascular structures, supporting the hypothesis that endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) may be a potential mechanism in PHPV vascular regression.

Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) or EndMT is a cellular mechanism that involves cytoskeleton reorganization, resulting in epithelial or endothelial cells acquiring a mesenchymal phenotype. EMT and EndMT are associated with fibroproliferative diseases such as cancer progression, metastasis, cardiac fibrosis, kidney fibrosis, and subretinal fibrosis associated with neovascular age-related macular degeneration13,14. In our study, mesenchymal cells were found in most cases (69.2%), with or without vascular structures. This supports the hypothesis that EndMT may play a potential mechanism in the vascular regression process of PHPV. The varied proportions of vascular structures and mesenchymal cells in the retrolental tissue indicate that the transition from fetal vascular structures to mesenchymal cells is not influenced by the patient’s age or the size of retrolental membranes at the time of surgery (Table 4). Further studies are needed to determine whether fetal mesenchymal transition is the main cause of hyaloid vessel regression in PHPV.

Lens capsule thickness in pediatric patients has been rarely reported. In 1942, Klein10 reported that the anterior and posterior poles of the lens capsule were 6–9 μm and 2–3 μm, respectively, in four children aged < 15 years. In 2006, Barraquer et al.11 measured the lens capsule thickness of a 12-year-old child, showing a capsule thickness of about 6–7 μm and 1–2 μm at the anterior and posterior poles, respectively. Our study, which analyzed a relatively large number of subjects compared to other studies, indicates that the anterior and posterior lens capsule thickness does not significantly change after birth.

We excluded posterior lenticonus from the capsular thickness analysis because of its direct effect on the morphological and physiological characteristics of the posterior capsule. Additionally, obtaining intact posterior capsules from eyes with lenticonus was difficult. Although it was not included in the table, we measured the thickness of the anterior (n = 11, n = 102) and posterior capsules (n = 7, n = 58) in the 12 eyes with posterior lenticonus and the remaining 102 eyes during the study. No significant difference was observed in the thickness of both the anterior (7.47 μm vs. 7.18 μm, p = 0.596, by independent t-test) and posterior capsules (4.74 μm vs. 4.35 μm, p = 0.497, by independent t-test) between the two groups. However, this result should be cautiously interpreted due to the possibility that only the undamaged portion of the capsule obtained from lenticonus was measured.

Clinically, PHPV presents a wide range of clinical manifestations, including elongated ciliary processes, intraocular hemorrhage, retinal fold, retinal detachment, and cataracts, ultimately leading to severe visual impairment15. Most PHPV cases are unilateral, with bilateral PHPV cases commonly associated with systemic abnormality16. In this study, one of 12 patients (8.3%) had bilateral PHPV, which was accompanied by torticollis. The prevalence of bilateral PHPV was consistent with that reported in other countries, with a range of 2.4–11% according to Sanghvi et al.17 and Jain18 from India.

In pediatric cataracts caused by PHPV, the average age at surgery was 43.9 months, which was longer than those in Australia (11 months)19, China (39.3 months)20, and Michigan, USA (10.1 months)21. This difference may be due to the different timing of pediatric cataract diagnosis in each country. These findings suggest that early ophthalmological examination and identification of cataracts during infancy are crucial.

Compared to other ocular biometric measurements, PHPV eyes have a significantly shorter axial length, higher keratometry values, and thinner corneal thickness than non-PHPV eyes. This is because PHPV is generally accompanied by delayed ocular development22. However, no differences were observed in anterior and posterior lens capsule thickness based on PHPV. The lens capsule acts as a basement membrane for lens epithelial cells, indicating that delayed eye development does not significantly affect this membrane.

A few previous studies have described well-formed vascular structures in the retrolental membranes of PHPV4,23–25. Classical histological features of the PHPV include mixtures of mesenchymal cells and vascular structures in the anterior vitreous, integrated with either the posterior or anterior capsule. In addition to vascular structures, the retrolental membrane tissues also contain hemosiderin-laden macrophages23, glial cells4,24 as well as the smooth muscle, nervous, and epithelial cells26.

In our study, unlike previous studies, only one case showed endothelium-lined blood vessels with routine H&E stain, and only three cases confirmed vessels with the help of immunohistochemistry. Six PHPV cases, including one bilateral PHPV, showed only mesenchymal cells mixed with collagen bundles. Our study suggests that not all PHPV cases have vascular structures in the retrolental membrane tissues, despite its name, “persistent fetal vasculature.” Histology failed to detect fibrovascular membrane tissue in three clinically diagnosed cases of PHPV, indicating that the tissue may be too small to be detected through histology.

This study is the first to report a clinical and pathological analysis of PHPV and lens capsule thickness in Korean pediatric patients. Unlike previous studies, it thoroughly investigates lens capsule thickness in the pediatric age group, which may provide a clinical reference for pediatric cataract surgery. In addition, the results of this study are expected to help better understand the pathogenesis of pediatric cataracts accompanied by PHPV. However, this study has some limitations. First, the small volume of specimens limited histopathological examination in three cases. The fibrovascular membrane was likely eliminated during the tissue processing. Second, the number of PHPV cases included was relatively small.

In conclusion, our study found that (1) 11% of Korean pediatric cataract surgery patients had PHPV. (2) Lens capsule thickness did not differ significantly based on age and PHPV in Korean pediatric cataract patients. (3) Not all PHPV cases exhibit vascular structures in the retrolental membrane tissues after birth. (4) In most cases (69%), mesenchymal cells with or without vascular structures are present, suggesting that the EndMT could be one possible mechanism in the vascular regression process of PHPV. (5) The mesenchymal transition of remnant fetal vascular structures in PHPV does not depend on the patient’s age at surgery or the size of retrolental membranes.
