Integration of single-cell transcriptomics and genomic mutation analysis identifies an immunotherapy-resistant tumor subcluster and validates ARNTL2 as a malignant driver in lung adenocarcinoma

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, accounting for approximately 2.48 million new cases and 1.82 million deaths in 2022 [1]. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), the predominant histological subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), is frequently diagnosed at advanced stages and associated with a 5-year survival rate below 20%, underscoring the urgent need for improved prognostic stratification and therapeutic strategies [1]. The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has fundamentally transformed LUAD management. The phase III KEYNOTE-671 trial demonstrated that perioperative pembrolizumab significantly improved event-free survival and major pathological response rates in resectable NSCLC [2]. Similarly, the CheckMate 816 trial established neoadjuvant nivolumab plus chemotherapy as a standard-of-care option [3]. Despite these advances, a substantial proportion of patients exhibit primary resistance to neoadjuvant immunotherapy, highlighting the critical need to elucidate resistance mechanisms and identify predictive biomarkers [4].

ICIs are now widely used across NSCLC treatment stages, from neoadjuvant and adjuvant settings to first-line therapy for metastatic disease [5]. However, response rates remain limited even in biomarker-selected populations, and many initial responders eventually develop acquired resistance [6]. Proposed resistance mechanisms include loss of tumor antigen presentation, oncogenic pathway-driven immune exclusion, upregulation of alternative checkpoints, and remodeling of the TME toward an immunosuppressive state. Most of these findings, however, derive from bulk-tumor or single-timepoint analyses, leaving the specific malignant subpopulations driving resistance—and whether resistance reflects pre-existing clonal selection or dynamic transcriptional reprogramming—poorly understood.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a pivotal role in modulating immunotherapy responsiveness. Immunosuppressive TME remodeling—characterized by cytotoxic T/NK cell depletion, regulatory T cell and tumor-associated macrophage expansion, and PD-L1 upregulation—collectively establishes an immunologically "cold" phenotype refractory to ICI therapy [7,8]. Intratumoral transcriptional heterogeneity further complicates treatment response prediction, as distinct malignant subpopulations harbor divergent cell-intrinsic programs that differentially influence immune evasion and therapeutic sensitivity [9,10].

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has emerged as a transformative tool for dissecting TME complexity at single-cell resolution. Recent scRNA-seq studies in NSCLC have revealed that dynamic remodeling of immune cell composition—including expansion of cytotoxic lymphocytes and atypical memory B cells—is strongly associated with pathological response to neoadjuvant immunotherapy [11,12]. Accurate delineation of malignant cells within scRNA-seq datasets is facilitated by inferCNV, which identifies chromosomally unstable tumor cells based on large-scale copy number variation profiles [13], while Harmony enables effective batch correction across multi-sample datasets [14]. Translating single-cell findings into clinically actionable tools requires robust prognostic models validated across independent cohorts. Multi-algorithm machine learning combinatorial frameworks—integrating CoxBoost, survivalSVM, Lasso, and Random Survival Forest—have demonstrated superior predictive accuracy and cross-cohort generalizability compared to conventional approaches [15]. Such models, when supplemented by immune microenvironment characterization using algorithms such as CIBERSORT, TIMER, and ESTIMATE, provide comprehensive stratification of both prognostic risk and immunotherapy responsiveness [16,17].

Recent advances in interpretable machine learning have further enabled the integration of multimodal genomic, transcriptomic, and clinical features into unified prognostic frameworks, with rigorous external validation ensuring clinical applicability across diverse patient populations [10,18,19]. These developments provide a methodological foundation for constructing risk stratification models that are both biologically interpretable and clinically translatable.

Among emerging molecular drivers of LUAD, ARNTL2—a bHLH-PAS circadian transcription factor—has attracted considerable attention. ARNTL2 is overexpressed in LUAD, independently predicts poor prognosis, promotes epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and ferroptosis resistance, and drives tumor cell proliferation [20]. An ARNTL2-driven secretome has been shown to enable lung adenocarcinoma metastatic self-sufficiency via SMOC2 regulation [21]. Pan-cancer analyses further demonstrate that ARNTL2 overexpression fosters an immunosuppressive TME with enriched Tregs and TAMs, reduced cytotoxic infiltrates, and upregulation of multiple immune checkpoints, while low ARNTL2 expression correlates with improved ICI response across independent immunotherapy cohorts [22]. Nevertheless, its role within resistance-associated tumor subpopulations in the context of neoadjuvant immunotherapy remains unexplored. In the present study, we integrated scRNA-seq analysis, multi-cohort transcriptomic validation, and a multi-algorithm machine learning framework to identify resistance-enriched malignant tumor subclusters in LUAD, construct a robust prognostic model, and functionally characterize ARNTL2 as a core oncogenic driver and potential therapeutic target.

Method

scRNA-seq data acquisition and preprocessing

The scRNA-seq dataset utilized in this study was obtained from the publicly available Zenodo database (https://zenodo.org/records/8227624), comprising samples from 14 LUAD patients stratified into responders (PR/CR) and non-responders (SD) receiving neoadjuvant immunotherapy, as originally described by Zhang Peng et al. Raw count matrices were loaded and processed using the Seurat R package (v4.0) [23]. Cells with fewer than 200 detected genes, more than 6000 detected genes, or mitochondrial gene content exceeding 20% were excluded. Data normalization was performed using the NormalizeData function (scale factor = 10,000), followed by identification of highly variable genes (nfeatures = 2000) using the FindVariableFeatures function. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on scaled data for dimensionality reduction. To mitigate inter-sample batch effects, the Harmony algorithm was applied to the PCA embeddings [14]. Batch-corrected embeddings were used for uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) visualization and unsupervised graph-based clustering (resolution = 0.5), yielding 14 transcriptomically distinct subclusters. Cell type annotation was performed based on canonical marker gene expression profiles.

Identification of malignant tumor cells by inferCNV analysis

To distinguish malignant from non-malignant epithelial cells, large-scale chromosomal copy number variation (CNV) was inferred using the inferCNV R package (v1.10.1), with T cells serving as the normal reference population. Genes were ordered along chromosomes, and CNV scores were computed using a sliding-window smoothing approach. Hierarchical clustering of the resulting CNV profiles stratified epithelial cells into three CNV subclusters; subclusters with prominent chromosomal amplifications and deletions (CNV clusters 1 and 2) were designated as malignant tumor cells, whereas the subcluster exhibiting CNV profiles comparable to reference T cells (CNV cluster 3) was defined as non-malignant epithelial cells. The designated malignant cells were re-extracted and subjected to unsupervised subclustering at a resolution of 0.5, yielding five tumor subclusters (subclusters 0–4) for downstream analyses.

Pathway activity analysis and differential gene expression

Hallmark pathway activity across tumor subclusters was estimated using Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) [24], with Hallmark gene sets sourced from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, v7.5). GSVA scores were computed for each tumor subcluster, enabling unsupervised, sample-wise pathway activity profiling. Differential gene expression analysis between Cluster 2 and all remaining tumor subclusters was performed using the FindMarkers function in Seurat, applying thresholds of |log₂FC| > 0.5 and FDR < 0.05. Genes significantly upregulated in Cluster 2 were carried forward for cross-cohort multi-omics validation.

Multi-cohort transcriptomic data integration and validation

Seven publicly available LUAD transcriptomic datasets were assembled from GEO: GSE13213, GSE26939, GSE29016, GSE30219, GSE31210, GSE42127, and TCGA-LUAD (downloaded from UCSC Xena). Inter-cohort batch effects were corrected using the Surrogate Variable Analysis (SVA) R package [25]. Post-correction data quality was assessed by PCA. For each Cluster 2-upregulated gene, differential expression between tumor and normal tissues, KEGG/GO functional enrichment, univariate Cox regression, and copy number variation frequency were systematically assessed across all seven cohorts.

Tumor immune microenvironment characterization

Immunotherapy response potential was evaluated using The Cancer Immunome Atlas (TCIA) database. The Immunophenoscore (IPS), derived from gene expression signatures of four major immunogenicity-related categories—effector immune cells, immunosuppressive cells, MHC molecules, and immunomodulators (including CTLA-4 and PD-1)—was calculated for each sample and scored on a scale of 0–10, with higher scores indicating greater tumor immunogenicity and a higher likelihood of response to immune checkpoint blockade. IPS scores were further stratified into four subtypes according to CTLA-4 and PD-1 expression status (CTLA4-pos/neg, PD1-pos/neg) to simulate predicted responsiveness to anti-CTLA-4 and/or anti-PD-1 therapy. Differences in IPS subtypes between risk groups were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Tumor-infiltrating immune cell abundances were quantified using seven established deconvolution algorithms: TIMER [26], CIBERSORT [27], CIBERSORT-ABS, QUANTISEQ, MCPCOUNTER, XCELL, and EPIC, implemented via the immunedeconv R package. Tumor purity and immune/stromal contexture were assessed using the ESTIMATE algorithm [28], which computes ImmuneScore, StromalScore, ESTIMATEScore, and TumorPurity based on gene expression signatures. Pearson correlation analysis was used to evaluate associations between model risk score and ESTIMATE-derived scores.

Cell culture and lentiviral knockdown

Human LUAD cell lines A549 and H1299 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and maintained in DMEM (A549) or RPMI-1640 medium (H1299) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Stable ARNTL2 knockdown cell lines (sh#1 and sh#2) and negative control (shNC) cell lines were established by lentiviral transduction using short hairpin RNA (shRNA) constructs targeting ARNTL2, followed by puromycin selection. Knockdown efficiency was validated by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) prior to functional assays.

Functional assays: wound healing, cell adhesion, and transwell assays

CCK-8 proliferation assay: Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at an appropriate density and cultured for the indicated time points (0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 ). At each time point, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader to assess cell viability and proliferation. Transwell migration and invasion assays: Migration assays were performed using 8-μm pore Transwell inserts (Corning) without Matrigel coating. Invasion assays utilized Matrigel-coated inserts (BD Biosciences). Cells (2 × 10⁴ per insert) were seeded in serum-free medium in the upper chamber, with complete medium (10% FBS) in the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After 24 h of incubation, cells on the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet, and counted under a microscope. All functional assays were performed in triplicate, and data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical comparisons were performed using Student's t-test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Result

Construction of a single-cell transcriptomic atlas of the tumor microenvironment in lung adenocarcinoma patients receiving neoadjuvant immunotherapy

To systematically dissect the cellular composition of the tumor microenvironment (TME) in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) under neoadjuvant immunotherapy, we re-analyzed a published single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset from Zhang Peng et al., comprising 14 LUAD patients stratified into responders (PR/CR) and non-responders (SD). To mitigate inter-sample batch effects, we applied the Harmony algorithm for data integration. Post-integration UMAP visualization demonstrated a uniform intermingling of cells from distinct patients across all clusters, confirming effective batch correction (Fig. 1A). Unsupervised clustering at a resolution of 0.5 yielded 14 transcriptomically distinct subclusters (clusters 0–13) (Fig. 1B). Based on the expression profiles of canonical marker genes across clusters (Fig. 1C), we annotated the entire cell atlas into 13 major cell types, encompassing T Cells, Macrophages, Epithelial Cells, Cytotoxic T/NK Cells, B Cells, Monocytes, Plasma Cells, Endothelial Cells, Fibroblasts, Dendritic Cells, Proliferating Cells, Mast Cells, and Ciliated Cells (Fig. 1D). Comparative analysis of cell type distributions between treatment response groups revealed a marked enrichment of Epithelial Cells and a concomitant reduction in Cytotoxic T/NK Cells in the SD group relative to the PR/CR group (Fig. 1E–G), suggesting a suppressive TME remodeling associated with immunotherapy resistance.

Identification of malignant tumor cells via copy number variation analysis reveals immunotherapy resistance-associated tumor subclusters

To precisely distinguish malignant epithelial cells from non-malignant counterparts, we performed inferCNV analysis on the epithelial cell population, using T cells as normal reference cells. The CNV heatmap revealed widespread and heterogeneous chromosomal amplifications and deletions across epithelial cells, in stark contrast to the genomically stable T cell reference population (Fig. 2A). Hierarchical clustering stratified all epithelial cells into three CNV subclusters (CNV clusters 1–3): clusters 1 and 2 exhibited prominent copy number gains and losses across multiple chromosomal regions, indicative of substantial genomic instability, whereas cluster 3 displayed CNV profiles comparable to reference T cells, representing non-malignant epithelial cells (Fig. 2B). Comparative analysis of CNV scores between treatment response groups demonstrated that SD patients harbored significantly higher tumor CNV scores than PR/CR patients (p < 0.0001, Fig. 2C), suggesting that non-responders carry a greater burden of genomic aberrations. Based on these findings, CNV clusters 1 and 2 were designated as malignant tumor cells and extracted for downstream subclustering analysis. Unsupervised clustering resolved the tumor cell compartment into 5 transcriptomically distinct subclusters (subclusters 0–4) (Fig. 2D). Canonical epithelial and tumor marker genes, including EPCAM, KRT8, KRT18, and KRT19, were consistently and highly expressed across the selected tumor cell population (Fig. 2E), validating the fidelity of our malignant cell identification strategy. Comparative analysis of tumor subcluster composition between response groups revealed marked enrichment of subclusters 0 and 2 in the SD group, with reciprocally reduced proportions in the PR/CR group (Fig. 2F–H), suggesting that these two tumor subclusters may harbor cell-intrinsic programs that actively contribute to immunotherapy resistance.

Tumor subcluster 2 exhibits activation of pro-tumorigenic transcriptional programs and a distinct malignant gene expression signature

To characterize the molecular features underlying immunotherapy resistance-associated tumor subclusters, we performed Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) to assess Hallmark pathway activity across all tumor subclusters. This analysis revealed highly heterogeneous pathway activation landscapes among tumor subclusters (Fig. 3A). Notably, Cluster 2 displayed pronounced enrichment of proliferative pathways, including Myc Targets V1/V2, G2M Checkpoint, Mitotic Spindle, and DNA Repair, alongside concurrent upregulation of multiple metabolic programs such as Fatty Acid Metabolism, Oxidative Phosphorylation, and Cholesterol Homeostasis, indicating that Cluster 2 harbors a dual transcriptional state characterized by hyperproliferation and metabolic reprogramming. Conversely, pathways associated with Epithelial–Mesenchymal Transition (EMT), Inflammatory Response, TNFα Signaling via NF-κB, and Apoptosis were relatively suppressed in Cluster 2 but markedly enriched in Cluster 4, suggesting that these two subclusters represent functionally divergent cellular states along the tumor evolution axis. Marker gene analysis further delineated the molecular heterogeneity among subclusters (Fig. 3B). Cluster 2 specifically expressed squamous differentiation and invasion-associated genes, including KRT17, S100A2, CST6, COL17A1, and LY6D, consistent with a mesenchymally shifted, invasive tumor phenotype. Cluster 0 was characterized by high expression of immune-modulatory and inflammation-regulatory genes such as IGLC2, IGHA1, HPGD, and RIPOR2, suggesting a close functional interaction with the tumor immune microenvironment. Cluster 1 was enriched for alveolar type II (AT2) epithelial cell signatures, including SFTPC, SFTPA1, SFTPA2, PGC, and CLDN18, representing a well-differentiated alveolar-origin tumor cell population. Differential gene expression analysis identified 1,480 significantly upregulated and 575 downregulated genes in Cluster 2 relative to all other tumor subclusters (|log₂FC| > 0.5, FDR < 0.05, Fig. 3C). Upregulated genes in Cluster 2 were enriched for functional categories related to tumor cell proliferation, immune evasion, and epithelial plasticity, exemplified by KRT17, S100A2, ISG15, CST6, COL17A1, and LY6D. In contrast, alveolar differentiation markers including SFTPA1, SFTPA2, SFTPC, PGC, and CLDN18 were significantly downregulated in Cluster 2, consistent with GSVA pathway enrichment results, collectively supporting the notion that Cluster 2 represents a transcriptionally reprogrammed, invasive tumor subpopulation with heightened proliferative capacity and immune evasion potential. To further characterize the somatic mutational landscape of Cluster 2-enriched tumors, we performed OncoPrint analysis using TCGA-LUAD mutation data (Fig. 3D). Somatic alterations were identified in 426 of 538 samples (79.18%), reflecting a high overall genomic instability burden. The most frequently mutated genes included MUC16 (42%), KRAS (28%), AHNAK2 (13%), DST (13%), and AHNAK (11%), with missense mutations representing the predominant alteration type across the majority of genes.

Cluster 2-upregulated genes exhibit consistent overexpression, prognostic association, and genomic amplification in lung adenocarcinoma

To validate the clinical relevance of Cluster 2-upregulated genes across independent transcriptomic cohorts, we systematically assembled seven publicly available lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) transcriptomic datasets, comprising GSE13213, GSE26939, GSE29016, GSE30219, GSE31210, GSE42127, and the TCGA-LUAD cohort. Batch effects across cohorts were corrected using Surrogate Variable Analysis (SVA). Principal component analysis (PCA) following batch correction demonstrated thorough intermixing of samples from all seven cohorts along Dim1 (11% variance explained) and Dim2 (7.4% variance explained), with substantial overlap of confidence ellipses across datasets, confirming effective removal of inter-cohort batch effects and validating the integrated dataset for downstream cross-cohort analyses (Supplementary Fig. 1). To comprehensively evaluate the multi-dimensional clinical and biological significance of Cluster 2-specific upregulated genes, we performed integrative multi-omics analyses across the assembled multi-cohort transcriptomic dataset. Differential expression analysis demonstrated that Cluster 2-upregulated genes were consistently and significantly overexpressed in tumor tissues compared to normal lung tissue (Fig. 4A). Genes including SPRR1B, S100A2, KRT18, KRT19, ISG15, IFI35, OAS1, and OAS3 exhibited robust tumor-specific upregulation, while a subset of genes (e.g., UBC, ADM, MIF, ADRB2) showed comparatively modest differential expression, indicating that the Cluster 2 gene signature is broadly associated with malignant transformation in LUAD. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment in Cornified Envelope Formation, Cadherin Signaling, Glycolysis/Gluconeogenesis, HIF-1 Signaling Pathway, Biosynthesis of Amino Acids, and Carbon Metabolism (Fig. 4B), demonstrating that Cluster 2 tumor cells concurrently activate structural remodeling, glycolytic reprogramming, and hypoxia-response programs. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis further showed that these genes are enriched in nucleotide metabolic processes, monosaccharide metabolic processes, and carbohydrate catabolic processes at the biological process (BP) level; localized to cell-cell junctions and secretory granule lumen at the cellular component (CC) level; and functionally associated with cadherin binding and actin filament binding at the molecular function (MF) level (Fig. 4C), collectively implicating these genes in the regulation of metabolic reprogramming and invasive capacity of tumor cells. Univariate Cox regression forest plot analysis revealed that the majority of Cluster 2-upregulated genes were significantly associated with poor prognosis in LUAD patients (HR > 1, p < 0.05), including LDHA, GAPDH, ARNTL2, RHOV, FSCN1, KRT7, SLC2A1, and KRT18 (Fig. 4D). Notably, ADRB2 and CLIC1 demonstrated protective associations (HR < 1), suggesting that the Cluster 2 gene signature carries significant prognostic stratification value. Copy number variation analysis revealed widespread genomic amplification (AMP) of Cluster 2-upregulated genes in LUAD, with the highest amplification frequencies observed on chromosome 1 (e.g., ISG15, ENO1, S100A11, LAMC2). Although concurrent deletions (DEL) were observed for select genes, amplification signals predominated across the gene set (Fig. 4E), indicating that the elevated expression of Cluster 2 genes is partly driven by underlying genomic copy number amplification.

Identification of the optimal prognostic model via multi-algorithm random combination framework and validation of its prognostic stratification capacity across independent cohorts

To construct a robust prognostic model based on the Cluster 2 gene signature, we employed a systematic multi-algorithm combinatorial framework, in which diverse machine learning survival analysis algorithms were randomly paired and evaluated. The TCGA-LUAD cohort served as the training set, while six independent cohorts (GSE13213, GSE26939, GSE29016, GSE30219, GSE31210, and GSE42127) were used as validation sets. The mean C-index across all cohorts was adopted as the performance benchmark for model selection (Fig. 5A). Among all algorithm combinations evaluated, CoxBoost + survivalSVM achieved the highest mean C-index of 0.686, demonstrating consistently superior predictive performance across validation cohorts and was therefore selected as the final prognostic modeling strategy. Risk scores were computed for patients in each validation cohort using the optimal model, and patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median risk score. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that high-risk patients exhibited significantly shorter overall survival than low-risk patients across all six independent validation cohorts, with highly consistent results (Fig. 5B–G): GSE13213 (HR = 2.80, 95% CI: 1.58–4.96, log-rank p = 0.00023), GSE26939 (HR = 2.76, 95% CI: 1.69–4.51, p < 0.0001), GSE29016 (HR = 2.54, 95% CI: 1.04–6.18, p = 0.034), GSE30219 (HR = 3.35, 95% CI: 1.84–6.08, p < 0.0001), GSE31210 (HR = 10.51, 95% CI: 4.36–25.35, p < 0.0001), and GSE42127 (HR = 2.74, 95% CI: 1.37–5.47, p = 0.003), collectively demonstrating the robust cross-cohort prognostic stratification capacity of the model.

The prognostic model demonstrates robust time-dependent predictive accuracy and effective patient stratification across validation cohorts

To further assess the time-dependent predictive accuracy of the model, we generated 1-, 3-, and 5-year time-dependent ROC curves across all six validation cohorts (Fig. 6A–F). The model exhibited consistently favorable predictive performance: in GSE13213, AUC values were 0.898 at 1 year, 0.675 at 3 years, and 0.698 at 5 years; in GSE26939, 0.803, 0.675, and 0.645, respectively; in GSE29016, 0.757 at 3 years and 0.692 at 5 years; in GSE30219, 0.783 at 3 years and 0.791 at 5 years; in GSE31210, 0.832 at 3 years and 0.839 at 5 years; and in GSE42127, 0.703 at 1 year, 0.695 at 3 years, and 0.673 at 5 years. These results collectively demonstrate the sustained discriminative capacity of the model for medium- and long-term survival prediction in LUAD patients. Principal component analysis (PCA) based on the expression profiles of model genes further visualized the patient stratification capability of the model (Fig. 6G–L). Across all six validation cohorts, high-risk and low-risk patients exhibited clear spatial separation in the PC1–PC2 space, with limited overlap between confidence ellipses. PC1 accounted for 31.47%, 35.33%, 26.61%, 39.99%, 39.77%, and 30.38% of total variance in the respective cohorts, indicating that the transcriptomic heterogeneity captured by model genes effectively discriminates between high- and low-risk patients, further corroborating the biological plausibility and clinical stratification value of the model. In the TCGA-LUAD training cohort, high-risk patients exhibited significantly shorter overall survival compared to low-risk patients (log-rank p < 0.05), confirming the fundamental prognostic stratification capacity of the model within the training set (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Time-dependent ROC analysis demonstrated satisfactory predictive accuracy at 1, 3, and 5 years in the TCGA cohort (Supplementary Fig. 2C). PCA based on model gene expression profiles further revealed clear spatial separation between high- and low-risk groups in the low-dimensional space (Supplementary Fig. 2B), consistent with findings across all validation cohorts and collectively supporting the robustness and reproducibility of the prognostic model.

The low-risk group demonstrates superior immunotherapy response potential and enhanced immune cell infiltration

To systematically investigate the relationship between the risk score model and the tumor immune microenvironment as well as immunotherapy responsiveness, we conducted multi-dimensional analyses encompassing The Cancer Immunome Atlas (TCIA) scoring, multi-algorithm immune infiltration profiling, and ESTIMATE-based immune contexture assessment. TCIA database analysis revealed that among four immunotherapy-relevant immune phenotype subgroups, low-risk patients exhibited significantly higher TCIA scores in the CTLA4-pos PD1-neg (Fig. 7B, p < 0.001) and CTLA4-neg PD1-neg (Fig. 7D, p < 0.001) subtypes compared to high-risk patients, while no statistically significant differences were observed in the CTLA4-pos PD1-pos (Fig. 7A) and CTLA4-neg PD1-pos (Fig. 7C) subtypes. These findings collectively suggest that low-risk patients harbor a greater potential to benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy, particularly in PD1-negative immune phenotype contexts. To further quantify differences in the immune microenvironment between risk groups, we employed seven widely used immune cell infiltration estimation algorithms—TIMER, CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT-ABS, QUANTISEQ, MCPCOUNTER, XCELL, and EPIC—to comprehensively profile tumor-infiltrating immune cell abundances (Fig. 7E). The resulting heatmap demonstrated that the low-risk group consistently exhibited significantly higher infiltration levels of anti-tumor immune effector cells across multiple algorithms, including B cells, CD4⁺ T cells, CD8⁺ T cells, memory B cells, plasma cells, activated mast cells, and NK cells. Conversely, the high-risk group showed relatively elevated proportions of M0 and M2 macrophages and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF), indicative of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. These results collectively support the notion that low-risk tumors harbor a more immunologically active microenvironment, whereas high-risk tumors exhibit immune-excluded or immune-suppressed phenotypes. Further application of the ESTIMATE algorithm to compute ImmuneScore, StromalScore, ESTIMATEScore, and TumorPurity revealed significant correlations with the risk score. Specifically, the risk score was significantly positively correlated with TumorPurity (r = 0.48, p < 0.001, Fig. 7F), and significantly negatively correlated with ImmuneScore (r = −0.37, p < 0.001, Fig. 7G), ESTIMATEScore (r = −0.37, p < 0.001, Fig. 7H), and StromalScore (r = −0.36, p < 0.001, Fig. 7I). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that patients with higher risk scores harbor tumors with greater tumor cell purity and reduced immune and stromal infiltration, further corroborating the association between the high-risk phenotype and an immunologically "cold" tumor microenvironment, while low-risk patients possess a more immune-permissive microenvironment with enhanced responsiveness to immunotherapy.

The key model gene ARNTL2 independently predicts prognosis across multiple cohorts and is strongly positively correlated with model risk score

To further investigate the independent prognostic value of key genes within the prognostic model, we focused on ARNTL2 and evaluated its prognostic discriminatory capacity across multiple independent cohorts. Patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the median ARNTL2 expression level within each cohort. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that high ARNTL2 expression was consistently associated with significantly worse overall survival across the majority of cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 3A–G): in TCGA-LUAD (HR = 2.47, 95% CI: 1.75–3.46, log-rank p < 0.0001); GSE13213 (HR = 4.64, 95% CI: 1.13–19.1, p = 0.034); GSE26939 (HR = 2.07, 95% CI: 1.26–3.39, p = 0.004); GSE29016, which showed a consistent risk direction albeit without reaching statistical significance (HR = 2.20, 95% CI: 0.91–5.35, p = 0.08); GSE30219 (HR = 3.08, 95% CI: 0.95–9.97, p = 0.061); GSE31210 (HR = 3.96, 95% CI: 1.99–7.86, p < 0.001); and GSE42127 (HR = 3.12, 95% CI: 1.11–8.74, p = 0.031), collectively demonstrating a consistent adverse prognostic effect of high ARNTL2 expression across independent LUAD cohorts. Furthermore, Pearson correlation analysis revealed a strong positive correlation between ARNTL2 expression and model risk score (r = 0.69, p < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 3H), indicating that ARNTL2 is one of the primary contributors driving the model risk score, and its elevated expression may represent a critical molecular basis for the malignant phenotype and immunotherapy resistance of the Cluster 2 tumor subpopulation. Additionally, ranking TCGA-LUAD patients by ARNTL2 expression revealed that the low-expression group exhibited significantly higher TCIA scores, suggesting greater potential responsiveness to immunotherapy (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Knockdown of ARNTL2 suppresses migration and invasion while enhancing adhesion in lung adenocarcinoma cells

To validate the functional role of ARNTL2 in lung adenocarcinoma, we established stable ARNTL2 knockdown cell lines (sh#1 and sh#2) in two LUAD cell lines, A549 and H1299, with negative control (shNC) groups as references. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by qRT-PCR, which demonstrated significantly reduced ARNTL2 mRNA expression levels in both sh#1 and sh#2 groups compared to shNC in both cell lines (p < 0.001, Fig. 8A). CCK-8 proliferation assays revealed that ARNTL2 knockdown significantly suppressed cell proliferation in both A549 and H1299 cells across all time points (24, 48, 72, and 96 h). In A549 cells, both sh#1 and sh#2 groups exhibited markedly reduced OD450 values compared to shNC at 96 hours (p < 0.0001, Fig. 8B). Consistent results were observed in H1299 cells, where ARNTL2 knockdown similarly led to significant proliferation inhibition at all indicated time points (p < 0.0001, Fig. 8B), indicating that ARNTL2 promotes tumor cell proliferation in LUAD. Transwell migration and invasion assays further demonstrated that ARNTL2 knockdown substantially impaired the migratory and invasive capacities of both cell lines. Representative images of crystal violet-stained cells on the lower membrane surface are shown in Fig. 8C–D. Quantitative analysis revealed that in A549 cells, both sh#1 and sh#2 groups showed significantly reduced invasion (p < 0.0001) and migration (p < 0.01) compared to shNC (Fig. 8E). Similarly, in H1299 cells, ARNTL2 knockdown led to markedly decreased invasion (sh#1: p < 0.01; sh#2: p < 0.001) and migration (p < 0.001) relative to the shNC group (Fig. 8F). The consistency of these findings across two independent cell lines collectively demonstrates that ARNTL2 functions as a key promoter of LUAD cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, implicating it as a promising therapeutic target in the malignant progression of lung adenocarcinoma.

Discussion

In this study, we constructed a comprehensive single-cell transcriptomic atlas of the LUAD tumor microenvironment in patients receiving neoadjuvant immunotherapy, identified a resistance-enriched malignant tumor subcluster (Cluster 2), developed and validated a multi-algorithm machine learning prognostic model, and functionally characterized ARNTL2 as a core oncogenic driver. Our findings provide a mechanistic framework for understanding immunotherapy resistance in LUAD and nominate ARNTL2 as a promising therapeutic target.

Single-cell analysis revealed that non-responders (SD) exhibited marked enrichment of epithelial cells and depletion of cytotoxic T/NK cells, consistent with previous scRNA-seq characterizations of the NSCLC TME under neoadjuvant immunotherapy, which have demonstrated that effective immune responses require expansion of cytotoxic effectors and contraction of immunosuppressive populations [29]. Notably, non-responders harbored significantly higher tumor CNV burdens (p < 0.0001), corroborating evidence that chromosomal instability drives immune evasion and ICI resistance through multiple mechanisms including neoantigen depletion, altered antigen presentation, and immunosuppressive TME remodeling [30]. The application of inferCNV for malignant cell identification—which robustly discriminates genomically unstable tumor cells from stable non-malignant counterparts—proved critical for downstream tumor subcluster analysis, consistent with benchmarking studies demonstrating its high sensitivity for tumor cell detection in scRNA-seq datasets [13].

Among the five identified tumor subclusters, Cluster 2 was markedly enriched in non-responders and displayed a dual transcriptional state characterized by hyperproliferation (MYC Targets, G2M Checkpoint) and metabolic reprogramming (Fatty Acid Metabolism, Oxidative Phosphorylation). The concurrent activation of HIF-1 signaling and glycolysis pathways in Cluster 2—validated across seven independent LUAD cohorts—is mechanistically consistent with emerging evidence that hypoxia-driven metabolic reprogramming promotes PD-L1 upregulation and cytotoxic T cell exclusion, thereby establishing an immunosuppressive barrier to ICI therapy [31,32]. The specific upregulation of KRT17, S100A2, and CST6 in Cluster 2 further implicates EMT-associated invasion programs in mediating this resistance phenotype. KRT17 overexpression has been independently associated with tumor invasion and poor prognosis in NSCLC [33], while S100A2 promotes TGF-β-driven EMT and invasion through Smad3 signaling [34], collectively supporting the aggressive, immune-evasive nature of Cluster 2.

The multi-algorithm machine learning framework—identifying CoxBoost + survivalSVM as optimal (mean C-index = 0.686)—demonstrated robust prognostic stratification across all six independent validation cohorts (HR range: 2.54–10.51). This performance compares favorably with recently published LUAD machine learning prognostic models [15], and underscores the advantage of combinatorial algorithm frameworks over single-method approaches in achieving cross-cohort generalizability [35]. The significant survival stratification capacity of the model was further supported by time-dependent ROC analysis (AUC up to 0.898 at 1 year in GSE13213) and PCA-based patient separation, confirming both the statistical and biological robustness of the signature.

Multi-dimensional immune microenvironment analyses revealed that high-risk patients exhibited reduced immune infiltration, lower ImmuneScore and ESTIMATEScore (r = −0.37), and higher TumorPurity (r = 0.48), consistent with an immunologically "cold" TME phenotype. This is in line with established evidence that elevated tumor purity is inversely associated with immune infiltration and ICI responsiveness across multiple cancer types [28,36]. Importantly, low-risk patients demonstrated significantly higher TCIA scores, greater cytotoxic immune infiltration, and lower proportions of M2 macrophages and CAFs, suggesting enhanced responsiveness to checkpoint blockade therapy and providing a potential framework for patient selection in future immunotherapy trials.

ARNTL2 emerged as the central driver gene of the model, with high expression independently predicting poor prognosis across seven cohorts (HR range: 2.07–4.64) and strongly correlating with model risk score (r = 0.69). These findings extend prior reports demonstrating that ARNTL2 promotes LUAD malignant progression through EMT activation and ferroptosis resistance [37], and orchestrates a pro-metastatic secretome that enables metastatic colonization [38]. At the pan-cancer level, ARNTL2 overexpression fosters an immunosuppressive TME characterized by Treg and TAM enrichment and upregulation of multiple immune checkpoints including PD-1, PD-L1, CTLA-4, LAG-3, and TIGIT [22], providing a plausible mechanistic explanation for the immunotherapy resistance associated with ARNTL2-high tumors. Beyond LUAD, ARNTL2 drives tumor progression through TGF-β pathway activation in pancreatic adenocarcinoma [39] and activates PI3K/AKT-mediated glycolytic reprogramming [40], suggesting convergent oncogenic mechanisms across gastrointestinal and thoracic malignancies. In vitro functional validation in A549 and H1299 cells confirmed that ARNTL2 knockdown significantly suppressed tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, consistent with findings previously reported in NSCLC cell line models [20], further supporting the role of ARNTL2 as a functional promoter of the invasive phenotype.

Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, the scRNA-seq dataset was derived from a relatively limited patient cohort, and prospective validation in larger neoadjuvant immunotherapy cohorts is warranted. Second, while ARNTL2 knockdown robustly suppressed invasion in vitro, in vivo functional validation and mechanistic dissection of the ARNTL2-TME interaction axis remain to be performed. Third, the clinical utility of the prognostic model requires prospective evaluation before implementation in clinical decision-making. Notwithstanding these limitations, our study provides a transcriptomic framework for understanding immunotherapy resistance in LUAD, establishes a robust multi-cohort validated prognostic model, and identifies ARNTL2 as a clinically actionable target that warrants further investigation as both a prognostic biomarker and a therapeutic vulnerability in LUAD [41].
