An LLM-enhanced AI agent for rapid diagnosis of acute aortic dissection in multi-center settings
Authors: Hunian Li, Yuchen Pei, Tao Hu, Xiaoyu Hu, Jiazhen Luo, Manli Zhu, Jie Li, Yutao Ma, Li Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAcute aortic dissection (AAD) is a high-mortality cardiovascular emergency, yet early diagnosis before computed tomography angiography (CTA) remains challenging. We developed AAD-Agent as a pre-imaging triage aid—not as a replacement for CTA—and evaluated it in a retrospective, multi-center study. Using 869 suspected patients from 3 Chinese hospitals for internal validation and 200 from 2 additional hospitals for external validation, we compared AAD-Agent against a machine learning (ML) ensemble model. The ML-ensemble model performed well internally (accuracy 0.948, F1-score 0.964) but deteriorated externally (0.540, 0.600). In contrast, AAD-Agent maintained external stability across LLMs: DeepSeek-R1 (accuracy 0.715, F1-score 0.820), GPT-3.5 (0.715, 0.825), and GPT-4o (0.710, 0.803). Although the case-enriched design inflates these metrics, prevalence-adjusted negative predictive value (≈99.6% at 1% prevalence) supports AAD-Agent as a safe, low-cost rule-out aid to defer CTA in low-risk patients. Its low external specificity precludes confirmatory diagnosis, limiting use to rule out pending prospective validation.

Introduction

Acute aortic dissection (AAD) is a life-threatening cardiovascular emergency, posing a significant challenge to doctors worldwide due to its high fatality rate and rapid progression.1,2 This disorder is characterized by an intimal tear that permits blood extravasation into the aortic wall.3 As a time-sensitive disease, the mortality rate rises 1%–2% per h post-onset.4,5,6 Despite recent advances, timely diagnosis remains difficult, particularly in primary care and resource-limited settings where computed tomography angiography (CTA), the confirmatory standard, is not readily available.7 Moreover, unnecessary CTA scans, which account for up to 97.3% of CTAs ordered for suspected AAD, incur radiation exposure, contrast-induced nephropathy, and excess costs.8

Existing pre-imaging risk stratification tools have notable shortcomings. The aortic dissection detection risk score (ADD-RS) alone has low sensitivity in low-risk patients (5%–11%).9,10 Combining ADD-RS with D-dimer (<500 ng/mL) increases sensitivity to >98% but yields poor specificity (18%–57%), leading to unnecessary CTAs and increased costs.8,11 Therefore, a fast, accessible, and generalizable screening tool, even with modest specificity, is urgently needed to safely rule out AAD before confirmatory CTA can be performed, particularly in settings where CTA is not immediately accessible.

In China, the clinical burden of AAD is compounded by high mortality, substantial costs, and the frequent misdiagnosis or delayed detection, especially in primary care settings with limited diagnostic expertise and resources. Although initiatives such as medical consortia have been implemented to strengthen primary healthcare, emergency systems remain overstretched, underscoring the pressing need for cost-effective diagnostic aid.12,13

ML models have been studied in the field of AAD, covering mortality risk prediction,14,15,16,17,18 in-hospital adverse events prediction,19,20,21,22 therapeutic decision-making,23,24 and medical image analysis.25,26,27,28,29,30,31 However, their value in early AAD diagnosis remains limited due to out-of-distribution issues and black box characteristics, which undermine generalization and interpretability. For instance, Huo et al.'s study relied on demographic and fundamental laboratory indicators (e.g., age, gender, blood lipids, white blood cells [WBCs], and red blood cells [RBCs]) but overlooked strongly correlated clinical features, such as chest pain and hypertension, and lacked interpretable probabilistic assessments.14 Similarly, Liu et al. used 77 indicators across 4 machine learning (ML) methods for diagnostic prediction, achieving moderate performance (recall and specificity around 75%), although collecting such an extensive set of variables is impractical in emergency department (ED) settings.15 Arita et al.'s ECG-based model demonstrated poor specificity (93% false-positive rate) and a low F1-score (0.128), suggesting limited diagnostic utility.16 These approaches either miss critical clinical context, demand excessive input variables, or suffer from poor generalizability and interpretability, and they cannot process unstructured narrative data (e.g., free-text chief complaints) or actively guide diagnostic workflows, thereby limiting their integration into busy EDs.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated clinical reasoning capabilities, including triage accuracy comparable to resident physicians in ED settings.32 In January 2025, the DeepSeek-R1 release uniquely displayed reasoning chains, thereby enhancing the transparency of LLMs.33 However, while these LLM systems remain reactive tools, they cannot proactively drive diagnostic workflows, synthesize incomplete clinical data, adjust to evolving clinical contexts, or plan multi-step diagnosis strategies. These limitations restrict their ability to fully alleviate the diagnostic burden on physicians managing high-risk conditions, such as AAD, efficiently.

To address this gap, we developed AAD-Agent, an LLM-enhanced autonomous AI agent that emulates hierarchical emergency reasoning. It actively acquires data in three stages (demographics/symptoms→routine blood tests→D-dimer), dynamically updates risk estimates, and provides interpretable outputs. Designed as a rapid pre-imaging triage aid, AAD-Agent aims not to replace CTA but to safely rule out low-risk patients and defer unnecessary imaging, particularly in resource-limited settings or during initial triage to prioritize high-risk patients for confirmatory CTA.

In this retrospective multi-center study, we compared the AAD-Agent with a conventional ML-ensemble model using data from 869 clinically suspected AAD patients from 3 Chinese hospitals (internal validation) and 200 patients from 2 additional hospitals (external validation). The primary aim was to evaluate whether an LLM-based autonomous agent could achieve more stable diagnostic performance across heterogeneous datasets than a traditional ML model trained on the same structured variables. Crucially, AAD-Agent is intended solely as a pre-imaging screening support; any negative prediction must be integrated with overall clinical judgment, and positive cases warrant immediate imaging confirmation.

Results

Cohort baseline characteristics

During the study period from January 2022 to October 2023, a total of 2,116 patients were clinically suspected of AAD and admitted to EDs at 3 hospitals. After exclusion, 869 patients were included and were randomly divided into a training set (n = 695) and an internal validation set (n = 174) at an 80:20 ratio.

For external validation, an independent cohort of 200 patients was enrolled from 2 additional hospitals (details in STAR Methods). This validation is crucial for identifying domain shifts, a phenomenon that reflects the discrepancy in data distributions arising from different collection protocols or settings, shown in Figure 1. Diagnostic performance of ML ensemble and AAD-Agent

The AAD-Agent leverages the knowledge comprehension and logical reasoning capabilities of LLMs to perform hierarchical and structured data integration for AAD risk prediction. DeLong’s test revealed no statistically significant differences in AUC between the ML ensemble model and the AAD-Agent using GPT-4o (AUC = 0.674, p = 0.1327) or Claude-3.5 (AUC = 0.671, p = 0.4259). Despite the AUC comparisons, all LLM-based AAD-Agents demonstrated substantially superior external robustness over the ML-ensemble model in terms of F1-score (bootstrap 95% confidence interval [CI] excluding 0). Notably, all AAD-Agents achieved higher external sensitivity than the ML-ensemble model (e.g., GPT-3.5: 0.865 vs. 0.445), indicating a substantial reduction in the missed diagnosis rate. Caution

Due to the case-enriched study design (AAD prevalence 69.5% in the total cohort vs. <1% in real-world ED chest pain populations), the reported positive predictive values (PPVs) and accuracy are inflated, whereas the negative predictive values (NPVs) are underestimated (i.e., true NPVs in real practice would be higher). Sensitivity and specificity are largely independent of disease prevalence. Therefore, readers should focus on sensitivity and specificity when evaluating the agent’s intrinsic diagnostic performance. The true PPVs and NPVs under realistic prevalence (e.g., 1%) are provided in the sensitivity analysis later.

Sensitivity analysis for low-prevalence settings

The low NPVs observed in the case-enriched external cohort (Table 3) should be interpreted in the context of the unusually high AAD prevalence in this dataset (77.5%). Because PVs are strongly prevalence dependent, reduced NPV in such an enriched cohort is mathematically expected and does not necessarily reflect poor intrinsic diagnostic performance. In contrast, sensitivity and specificity are largely prevalence independent and, therefore, provide more stable estimates of diagnostic capability across different clinical settings. Converting PVs to a clinically relevant prevalence using Bayes’ theorem is a standard approach recommended for evaluating a diagnostic test in case-enriched studies. Under real-world ED prevalence conditions, the estimated NPV would be expected to increase substantially. Comparison of feature distribution offset and model performance

To visually illustrate the distribution shifts between the training and external validation cohorts, we provide boxplots of key continuous variables (diastolic blood pressure [DBP], D-dimer, and the WBC count) in Figure S1. Conclusion

The AAD-Agent represents a preliminary proof of concept for LLM-enhanced autonomous agents in emergency cardiovascular rule-out screening. Using only low-cost and rapidly available clinical and laboratory data, it maintained sensitivity above 0.80 across heterogeneous cohorts. Although its overall discriminative performance was modest, with AUC values of 0.532–0.674, its specificity was low. This trade-off may be acceptable in the specific context of pre-imaging triage, where the primary goal is to help rule out AAD rather than confirm the diagnosis. Prospective validation in unselected, real-world emergency populations is essential before any clinical application.

Discussion

The AAD-Agent is designed as a rule-out triage adjunct rather than a full replacement for CTA. In low-prevalence populations with rare AAD, its high NPV enables clinicians to safely defer immediate CTA for low-risk patients, mitigating unnecessary radiation, contrast exposure, and medical expenditures. Embedding hierarchical clinical reasoning with an autonomous agent architecture substantially boosts AAD identification and decision-making in ED settings. External validation confirms that the AAD-Agent delivers more stable sensitivity and F1-score across heterogeneous cohorts than traditional ML models, relying solely on cheap, instantly obtainable clinical and laboratory data.

Robustness, calibration, and generalization of LLM-based agents

Beyond conventional discrimination metrics, the AAD-Agent demonstrated superior reliability in clinical decision support. Decision curve analysis (Figure 4) showed that in the external validation, although the ML ensemble achieved the highest net benefit at thresholds ≤0.3, DeepSeek-R1 (Chinese) became superior at thresholds ≥0.4. This threshold-dependent reversal suggests that AAD-Agents are preferable when clinicians adopt a higher risk tolerance for confirmatory imaging, aligning with their intended role as a pre-imaging triage tool.

Calibration curves (Figure 5) further revealed that all AAD-Agents maintained stable probability estimates close to ideal calibration across both internal and external datasets, whereas the ML ensemble exhibited overconfidence in low-risk intervals internally and systematic underestimation of risk externally. Thus, the probability outputs by the AAD-Agent are more reliable for clinical decision-making.

The striking difference in external robustness between the AAD-Agent and the ML-ensemble model can be explained by two factors. First, the AAD-Agents operate on narrative clinical descriptions that incorporate contextual semantics, making them less sensitive to exact numerical thresholds and distributional shifts. In contrast, the ML-ensemble model relies on fixed feature distributions learned from the training set; when the external cohort showed markedly different D-dimer levels, blood pressure, and WBC counts (Table 3), its decision boundaries became ineffective. Second, the hierarchical, multi-stage reasoning of the AAD-Agent (demographics/symptoms→blood count→D-dimer) mimics a clinician’s adaptive workflow, dynamically recalibrating risk estimates as more data become available. This design reduces overfitting to any single data distribution.

To ensure a fair comparison, the ML-ensemble model underwent systematic hyperparameter tuning using GridSearchCV with 5-fold cross-validation, a two-stage coarse-to-fine search, and optimization for mean cross-validated AUC (see Table S1). Despite this rigorous procedure, the ensemble still failed to generalize to the external cohort. This finding underscores a fundamental limitation of conventional ML in multi-center settings: even optimal hyperparameters cannot compensate for distribution shift when the training and target distributions differ substantially. It also highlights that the superior generalization of AAD-Agents does not stem from a lack of tuning in the comparator but from fundamentally different inductive biases.

Although conventional ML models and zero-shot LLM-based agents are not methodologically identical, they represent two clinically relevant paradigms for early AAD screening: a supervised structured-data model optimized on the development cohort and a prompt-based reasoning agent that uses a pretrained LLM without task-specific fine-tuning. We, therefore, included this comparison not to claim direct algorithmic equivalence but to evaluate whether a zero-shot LLM-agent framework can maintain screening performance under external cohort shift when applied to the same staged clinical inputs. This deployment-oriented comparison is particularly relevant for emergency triage, where rapid adaptation across institutions, limited local training data, and interpretable stepwise reasoning are important. Accordingly, we interpret the findings as exploratory evidence of external robustness and workflow feasibility, rather than as definitive proof that LLM-based agents are universally superior to conventional ML models.

Clinical utility, workflow integration, and comparison with physicians

The most widely adopted pre-imaging risk stratification tool, ADD-RS combined with D-dimer, has demonstrated high sensitivity but low specificity (18%–57%) in prior studies. However, its real-world application depends on the consistent documentation of specific clinical features (e.g., pulse deficit, mediastinal widening on chest X-ray), which are often inconsistently recorded across centers, particularly in resource-limited or busy emergency settings. In our multi-center retrospective dataset, several ADD-RS components were not uniformly documented, precluding a direct head-to-head comparison, yet this very limitation underscores a practical gap that the AAD-Agent is uniquely positioned to fill.

Unlike static risk scores, the AAD-Agent offers three interconnected practical advantages.

Speed and feasibility

In contrast to previous models that required extensive variable sets or lacked interpretability, the present study focuses on rapidly accessible indicators that can be feasibly applied even in primary hospitals, ensuring both timeliness and cost-effectiveness. This approach aligns with China’s healthcare infrastructure and socioeconomic realities, offering a more practical and scalable solution. We emphasize that the AAD-Agent is not intended to replace CTA, the diagnostic gold standard. Rather, it is designed as a pre-imaging triage tool for clinical scenarios where CTA is not readily available, such as in primary EDs without 24/7 CT capability, during ambulance transport, or in resource-constrained settings. In such contexts, a rapid, low-cost screening tool with moderate-to-high sensitivity (0.76–0.87) and acceptable NPV can help prioritize patients for urgent transfer or immediate CTA. This trade-off, prioritizing sensitivity over specificity, is justified because missing a diagnosis is far more harmful than performing an unnecessary CTA.

Scalability across hospital tiers

The most significant difference between our study and previous studies is that we have extensively validated our approach across multiple levels with heterogeneous patient populations. While the conventional ML-ensemble model showed significant deterioration during external validation (F1-score dropping from 0.964 to 0.600), all AAD-Agents maintained robust performances (F1-score > 0.8) across cohorts with substantially different clinical profiles (Table 3). Our results suggest that LLM-based AAD-Agents may be more robust to between-site differences in distribution than conventional ML models. This finding aligns with previous observations that LLMs possess inherent generalization capabilities beyond their training distributions.34 Nevertheless, this finding should be interpreted cautiously, and further validation is needed before this observation can be generalized.

User-friendly workflow integration

Traditional LLM-based tools are passive question-answering systems that require clinicians to manually formulate queries. In contrast, the AAD-Agent proactively structures the diagnostic workflow by guiding physicians through a hierarchical, stepwise data-acquisition process (demographics→hematology→biomarkers), dynamically updating risk estimates and recommending CTA when high-risk features are detected. Unlike standard LLM interfaces,35,36,37 the AAD-Agent provides proactive, case-based guidance, reducing cognitive burden and enhancing usability in fast-paced EDs.

To further assess clinical relevance, we compared diagnostic performance between 18 primary care physicians and 5 AAD-Agents on a subset of 50 real-world cases (Figure 6). Physicians exhibited a high miss rate of 19.6% and an extremely elevated misdiagnosis rate of 53.3%. In stark contrast, Claude-3.5 and DeepSeek-R1 (English) achieved the lowest miss rate (4.0%) at the cost of higher misdiagnosis rates (20.0% and 12.0%, respectively). These findings provide hypothesis-generating evidence that AAD-Agents may serve as effective safety nets, but this interpretation requires confirmation in large, real-time clinical studies with physicians operating under actual workflow constraints.

Thus, rather than competing with ADD-RS, the AAD-Agent offers a complementary, automated alternative for scenarios in which risk-score components are unavailable, stepwise decision support is preferred, or clinicians seek an objective, data-driven second opinion to inform triage decisions. Deployed responsibly in a human-in-the-loop system, the AAD-Agent offers a transformative opportunity to enhance diagnostic safety and reduce errors globally, though prospective validation remains essential.

Implication of the case-enriched design for performance interpretation

A critical methodological consideration is the case-enriched sampling strategy employed in this study. The AAD prevalence in our internal and external cohorts was approximately 70% and 78%, respectively, which is substantially higher than the <1% prevalence typically seen in real-world ED chest pain populations. This enrichment was necessary to ensure adequate statistical power for model training with a balanced number of positive and negative cases. However, it has important implications for how we interpret our performance metrics.

Measures, such as accuracy, PPV, and F1-score, are prevalence dependent and, therefore, inflated in our dataset. Conversely, NPV is underestimated. For a screening tool intended for rule-out, NPV is the most clinically relevant metric. The low NPV values observed in the case-enriched external cohort (Table 3) should be interpreted in the context of the unusually high AAD prevalence in this dataset (77.5%). Because PVs are strongly prevalence dependent, reduced NPV in such an enriched cohort is mathematically expected and does not necessarily reflect poor intrinsic diagnostic performance. In contrast, sensitivity and specificity are largely prevalence-independent and therefore provide more stable estimates of diagnostic capability across different clinical settings.

To bridge the gap between our enriched design and clinical reality, we applied Bayes’ theorem to adjust for a hypothetical 1% AAD prevalence, a realistic estimate for undifferentiated ED chest pain. Using the observed sensitivity (∼0.85) and specificity (∼0.4) of the best-performing AAD-Agent, the estimated NPV was approximately 99.6%, meaning fewer than 4 false-negative results per 1,000 negative tests. This mathematical exercise strongly supports the AAD-Agents’ potential as a safe, rule-out tool to defer unnecessary CTA in low-risk patients. In pre-hospital triage or primary EDs without 24/7 CTA, the primary goal is to rule out AAD (high NPV) and to avoid missing a true case. The cost and risk of a false-positive CTA, though real, are acceptable in this specific pre-imaging triage context, because the consequence of a missed AAD-rapidly progressive mortality is disproportionately more severe than the finite risk of an unnecessary CTA.

Indeed, the widely used ADD-RS with D-dimer strategy has similarly low specificity (18%–57%) and is still recommended for rule-out purposes. Therefore, the AAD-Agent is clinically suitable for its intended use as a rapid pre-imaging screening aid, not as a replacement for CTA, but as a tool that can increase clinicians’ confidence in deferring immediate CTA when the result is negative and expedite triage for confirmatory imaging when the result is positive. Furthermore, we emphasize that sensitivity and NPV, rather than overall accuracy or F1-score, should be the primary metrics for evaluating the clinical utility of this tool, and that prospective validation in unselected cohorts is essential to confirm these estimates.

Limitations of the study

This study has several important limitations. First, its retrospective design and reliance on unstructured electronic medical records led to inconsistent documentation and substantial data loss (59% of eligible patients were excluded). Compared to included patients, excluded patients had fewer males and lower AAD prevalence, indicating potential selection bias (detailed in Data S1). Prospective studies with standardized data collection are needed.

Second, all external validation datasets were collected exclusively from central China, limiting generalizability to other regions, ethnic populations, and healthcare systems. Multi-center validation across more geographically and clinically diverse settings is, therefore, needed. Third, as detailed in the “implication of the case-enriched design” section previously, the enrichment of our cohorts inflates PPV and underestimates NPV. Readers should, therefore, prioritize sensitivity and NPV when evaluating the AAD-Agent’s rule-out potential. Fourth, direct comparison with the established ADD-RS plus D-dimer strategy was not feasible because several required variables were not consistently documented across centers. A head-to-head prospective comparison is a high priority for future research.

Fifth, AAD-Agent was designed for early pre-imaging screening; its applicability to post-diagnosis management, surgical decision support, or long-term prediction remains untested. Finally, the simulated case-based evaluation cannot fully replicate real clinical complexity, and long-term patient outcomes were not assessed.

Additionally, while we reported the proportions of type A and type B AAD (50.7% and 49.3%, respectively), information on false lumen patency or occlusion was not available in the retrospective electronic medical records. Because clinical presentation may vary with false lumen status, the diagnostic performance of AAD-Agent across these subgroups could not be assessed. Future prospective studies should collect these details to enable more granular validation.

We strongly caution against clinical use until such prospective studies are completed.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval No. TJ-IRB20220457), and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent from patients was waived. All patient data were de-identified prior to analysis.

This retrospective study used de-identified electronic medical record data from adult patients with clinically suspected AAD presenting to the emergency departments of five hospitals in China. Sex was included as a baseline characteristic (Table 1), the development cohort (n = 869) was 76.2% male and 23.8% female; while the external validation cohort (n = 200) was 73.0% male and 27.0% female. Compared with included patients, excluded patients (n = 1247) had significantly fewer males (66.9% vs 76.5%, p <0.001) and slightly lower AAD prevalence (60.9% vs 65.5%, p = 0.033) compared to included patients (n = 869). These findings indicate a selection bias (full details in Table S2). This may affect the generalizability of our findings, particularly to populations with a different sex ratio or lower disease prevalence.

Method details

A case-enriched sampling strategy was used to ensure a sufficient number of AAD cases for model training. This retrospective study analyzed ED patients from 3 hospitals (January 2022 to October 2023). Inclusion: Adults (18–80 years) with clinically suspected AAD by emergency physicians who underwent CTA, and with symptom onset within 14 days prior to ED admission. Exclusion: pregnancy, trauma, or incomplete medical records (defined as >40% missing data for key variables). Clinically suspected AAD was defined as any adult patient for whom an emergency physician ordered a CTA to rule out AAD based on chest/back pain, blood pressure differential, or other suggestive features.

Finally, 869 patients were included and then split into two datasets (80% for training and 20% for internal validation). From January 2024 to May 2024, 101 patients meeting the aforementioned criteria were enrolled in Cohort 1 for external validation. Additionally, 99 patients from a regional tertiary hospital in the same province (January 2020 to April 2025) were selected as Cohort 2 (details shown in Figure 1). We report our results in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline and the published recommendations for ML models.

Data processing

Extraction from electronic medical records included the following features: age, gender, time of onset (day), sudden onset vs. gradual, pain status, pain intensity, pain characteristics, accompanying symptoms, SBP, DBP, and previous medical history. Relevant laboratory features were also collected, including WBC count, RBC count, and D-dimer. The selection criteria for the above indicators were based on existing research. All feature variables were detected in the initial 24 hours of patient admission.

Continuous variables with missing values (proportion<5% for all variables) were imputed using the median of the training set. Categorical variables with missing values were excluded from analysis. No imputation was performed for variables with >5% missingness. For the purposes of this study, sudden-onset was defined as symptom onset reaching peak intensity within 1 hour.

Machine learning model construction

All continuous features (age, SBP, DBP, RBC, WBC, and D-dimer) were standardized using Z score normalization based on the training set mean and standard deviation; the same scaling parameters were applied to validation sets. A fixed random seed (42) was set for all stochastic processes (data splitting, model training, and bootstrap resampling).

We trained four distinct ML models—multivariable logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and support vector machine (SVM). 5-fold cross-validation on the training set was conducted to assess their individual performance. Hyperparameter tuning was performed using GridSearchCV with a two-stage coarse-to-fine search, optimizing for mean cross-validated AUC. The final hyperparameter combinations are summarized in Table S1. An ensemble model was then built using a probability-based approach (maximum a posteriori probability from the four classifiers). The complete hyperparameter tuning history for all models is provided in Table S4.

Comparison with primary care physician

An exploratory physician-comparison analysis was conducted between 18 primary care physicians and five LLM-based AAD-Agents on a randomly selected subset of 50 real-world clinical cases from the internal validation set. These 18 physicians were undergoing standardized training at community hospitals. They had a mean clinical experience of 4.2 years (range 2–8 years) and all worked in primary care settings.

The 50 cases were randomly sampled from the internal validation cohort at an 1:1 ratio of AAD-positive to AAD-negative cases. All cases were de-identified and presented in randomized order through an online survey platform. Each case was presented as a structured narrative including demographics, symptoms, vital signs, routine blood tests, and D-dimer results—identical to the input format used for the AAD-Agent. Physicians were asked to make a binary decision: “AAD suspect” or “non-AAD,” without time constraints or access to external references. All assessments were performed independently and blinded to the AAD-Agent outputs and to the true diagnoses. The same 50 cases were independently evaluated by the five AAD-Agents using the same input format. Diagnostic performance was compared using false-negative rate (1-sensitivity) and false-positive rate (1-specificity).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

All continuous variables were described as mean [±standard deviation] if normally distributed, or as median [interquartile range] otherwise. The t-test was used for variables that were normally distributed. Nonparametric tests, such as the Mann-Whitney U test or the Kruskal-Wallis test, were used for variables that did not follow a normal distribution. Calibration performance was evaluated using calibration plots. Clinical net benefit was assessed through decision curve analysis (DCA), with the threshold probability range set at 0–30%.

To evaluate the distribution shift between external validation cohorts and training cohorts, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test was employed to compare distribution differences between continuous variables, while the χ2 test was used to compare distribution differences between categorical variables. A KS statistic > 0.2 or p <0.05 was considered to indicate significant distribution differences.

For model performance comparison, the DeLong test was employed to compare the AUC differences between the ML-ensemble models and individual AAD-Agent models on external validation sets. Bootstrap resampling (1000 repetitions) was used to calculate 95% CIs for metrics such as F1-score, sensitivity, and specificity, and to assess the significance of inter-group differences via bootstrap estimation (differences were considered significant if the 95% CIs did not contain zero). No adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied because the comparisons were pre-specified and exploratory; p-values are reported as nominal.

To assess potential biases introduced by data exclusion, the χ2 test or Mann-Whitney U test was employed to compare the differences between the inclusion and exclusion groups in key variables such as age, gender, and prevalence of AAD.

Implementation details

We obtained publicly available and commercial versions of DeepSeek-R1 (20250120) from DeepSeek, GPT-3.5-turbo (20230422) and GPT-4o (version 20240513) GPTs from OpenAI, and Claude-3.5-sonnet (version 20240620) from Anthropic. No fine-tuning of the LLM was performed on our dataset; the agent uses prompt-based reasoning with the LLM’s pre-existing medical knowledge. Specifically, for LLMs, we used their application programming interfaces (APIs) with the LangChain library. This combination enables us to automatically feed prompts into each LLM to evaluate the predictive performance of AAD-Agents for AAD. To ensure the model generates more factual and consistent responses to the same prompt, we set the “temperature” parameter to 0.

Model performance evaluation

All different prediction models were evaluated on the same test set. Several commonly used evaluation metrics, including AUC, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, accuracy, and F1-score, were used to evaluate the performance of these models.
