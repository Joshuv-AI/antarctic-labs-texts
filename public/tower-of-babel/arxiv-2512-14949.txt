Abstract
. We revisit Bourgain’s 1981 counterexample to the sequential completeness of the ‘pointwise plus domination’ convergence on ℓ 1 \ell_{1} from the perspective of vector lattices. In this setting, we show that for sequences the associated notion of Bourgain–uo convergence coincides with ordinary order convergence. Motivated by Bourgain’s construction, we introduce a strengthened, subsequence-invariant notion of Cauchy sequence: a sequence ( x n ) (x_{n}) in a vector lattice E E is called 𝖡𝗎𝗈 \mathsf{Buo} - Cauchy if for every strictly increasing sequence ( n k ) (n_{k}) the differences x n k + 1 − x n k x_{n_{k+1}}-x_{n_{k}} converge to 0 0 in order in E E . We study the resulting sequential completeness problem in several concrete Banach lattices. Our main results are twofold. On the positive side, we prove that the classical sequence lattices c 0 c_{0} and ℓ ∞ \ell_{\infty} are sequentially 𝖡𝗎𝗈 \mathsf{Buo} -complete: every 𝖡𝗎𝗈 \mathsf{Buo} -Cauchy sequence converges in order, and hence in the 𝖡𝗎𝗈 \mathsf{Buo} sense. In addition, we establish a sharp metric characterisation for bounded Lipschitz function lattices: the vector lattice Lip b ​ ( X ) \mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X) of bounded Lipschitz functions on a metric space ( X , d ) (X,d) is sequentially 𝖡𝗎𝗈 \mathsf{Buo} -complete if and only if X X is uniformly discrete. On the negative side, we show that vector lattices of continuous or Lipschitz functions over metric spaces with non-isolated points fail sequential 𝖡𝗎𝗈 \mathsf{Buo} -completeness. In particular, C ⁡ ( X ) C(X) , C b ​ ( X ) C_{b}(X) , C 0 ​ ( X ) C_{0}(X) and Lip b ​ ( X ) \mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X) are not sequentially 𝖡𝗎𝗈 \mathsf{Buo} -complete whenever X X is not uniformly discrete.

1.
Introduction

In his 1981 paper
[
3
]
, J. Bourgain investigated a strengthened
mode of convergence on
\ell_{1}
: coordinatewise convergence combined with
domination by a single element of
\ell_{1}
. Bourgain showed, via an elegant
finite-block construction, that this convergence is not sequentially complete,
answering a question posed by Jan Mikusiński.

A natural abstract framework for Bourgain’s phenomenon is provided by vector
lattices. In this setting, coordinatewise convergence is replaced by

unbounded order
(
\mathsf{uo}
) convergence, while domination is interpreted as
order boundedness by a single positive element. Recall that a sequence

(x_{n})
in a vector lattice
E
is said to
\mathsf{uo}
-converge
to
x\in E
,
written
x_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{uo}}x
, if

|x_{n}-x|\wedge u\xrightarrow{\mathrm{o}}0\qquad\text{for every }u\in E_{+},

that is, all truncations of
|x_{n}-x|
converge to zero in order.

In many familiar settings,
\mathsf{uo}
-convergence coincides with classical pointwise
or almost-everywhere convergence. For example, in
c_{0}
and
\ell_{p}
it is
exactly coordinatewise convergence, while in standard function spaces it agrees
with almost-everywhere convergence. This leads naturally to the following
definition.

Definition 1.1

(Bourgain–uo convergence)
.

A sequence
(x_{n})\subset E

\mathsf{Buo}
-converges
to
x\in E
if

x_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{uo}}x
and there exists
y\in E_{+}
such that

|x_{n}|\leqslant y
for all
n
.

Although this definition may appear stronger than order convergence, we show
that for sequences it introduces no genuinely new convergence notion: Bourgain–uo
convergence coincides with ordinary order convergence; only the Cauchy concept we use later is non-standard. We are indebted to Vladimir Troitsky and Mitchell Taylor for pointing this out to us.

Proposition 1.2
.

For a sequence
(x_{n})
in a vector lattice
E
and
x\in E
the following are equivalent:

(i)

x_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{Buo}}x
;

(ii)

x_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{o}}x
(order convergence).

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): Suppose
x_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{uo}}x
and
|x_{n}|\leqslant y
for some
y\in E_{+}
. Set
w:=(|x|-y)^{+}\in E_{+}
. For every
n
we have
|x_{n}|\leqslant y
, hence on
\{w>0\}
we have
|x_{n}|\leqslant y<|x|
, so

|x_{n}-x|\wedge w\ \geqslant\ (\,|x|-|x_{n}|\,)\wedge w\ \geqslant\ (|x|-y)\wedge w\ =\ w.

If
w>0
, this contradicts
|x_{n}-x|\wedge w\xrightarrow{\mathrm{o}}0
(take
u=w
in the definition of
\mathsf{uo}
). Hence
w=0
and so
|x|\leqslant y
, i.e. the sequence
(x_{n})
and its limit
x
all lie in the order interval
[-y,y]
. On
[-y,y]
we have

|x_{n}-x|\ =\ |x_{n}-x|\wedge(2y)\ \leqslant\ 2\bigl(|x_{n}-x|\wedge y\bigr),

and since
\mathsf{uo}
-convergence gives
|x_{n}-x|\wedge y\xrightarrow{\mathrm{o}}0
, we obtain
|x_{n}-x|\xrightarrow{\mathrm{o}}0
, i.e.
x_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{o}}x
. (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): If
x_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{o}}x
, there exists a decreasing sequence
(z_{k})\downarrow 0
with
|x_{n}-x|\leqslant z_{k}
for all
n\geqslant N_{k}
. In particular the set
\{x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\cup\{x\}
is order bounded, so there is
y\in E_{+}
with
|x_{n}|\leqslant y
for all
n
(for instance
y:=|x|+\sup_{k}z_{k}
). For every
u\in E_{+}
and
n\geqslant N_{k}
,

|x_{n}-x|\wedge u\ \leqslant\ z_{k}\wedge u\ \downarrow 0,

so
x_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{uo}}x
, and thus
x_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{Buo}}x
by definition. ∎

The novelty lies instead
in the associated Cauchy condition.

Definition 1.3
.

A sequence
(x_{n})
in a vector lattice
E
is called
\mathsf{Buo}
-
Cauchy
if for
every strictly increasing sequence
(n_{k})
the differences

x_{n_{k+1}}-x_{n_{k}}
converge to
0
in order in
E
.

This Cauchy condition is strictly stronger than the usual one induced by order
convergence and captures the oscillatory behaviour underlying Bourgain’s
construction. The central theme of this paper is to determine which classical
Banach lattices are sequentially complete with respect to this notion.

Our first main result concerns sequence spaces. We show that both
c_{0}
and

\ell_{\infty}
are sequentially
\mathsf{Buo}
-complete: every
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy sequence admits
a pointwise limit in the same space and converges to it in order.

We then turn to function lattices. For bounded Lipschitz functions, the answer
is governed entirely by the metric geometry of the underlying space. Writing

\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
for the vector lattice of bounded Lipschitz functions on a
metric space
(X,d)
, we prove that
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
is sequentially

\mathsf{Buo}
-complete if and only if
X
is uniformly discrete. In particular,

\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
fails
\mathsf{Buo}
-completeness whenever
X
has a non-isolated point
or, more generally, whenever points of
X
can accumulate at arbitrarily small
scales.

As further applications, we show that the Banach lattices
C(X)
,
C_{b}(X)
and

C_{0}(X)
are not sequentially
\mathsf{Buo}
-complete under mild topological assumptions
on
X
. We conclude with several open problems, including the

\mathsf{Buo}
-sequential completeness of
\ell_{p}
spaces in the reflexive range

1<p<\infty
and of free Banach lattices
\mathrm{FBL}[\ell_{p}(\Gamma)]
.

2.
Vector lattices of continuous functions

In this subsection we consider spaces of continuous functions and their bounded/vanishing-at-infinity variants.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space with a non-isolated point
x_{0}\in X
. Then:

(1)

if
X
is compact, the Banach lattice
C(X)
is not sequentially
\mathsf{Buo}
-complete;

(2)

the Banach lattice
C_{b}(X)
of bounded continuous functions on
X
is not sequentially
\mathsf{Buo}
-complete;

(3)

if
X
is locally compact and there exists a decreasing sequence of relatively compact open neighbourhoods
(U_{n})
of
x_{0}
with
\bigcap_{n}U_{n}=\{x_{0}\}
, then
C_{0}(X)
is not sequentially
\mathsf{Buo}
-complete.

Proof.

For (i) and (ii), define

f_{n}(x):=\bigl(1-nd(x,x_{0})\bigr)_{+},\qquad x\in X.

Each
f_{n}
is continuous (indeed
n
-Lipschitz), satisfies
0\leqslant f_{n}\leqslant 1
, and

f_{n}\downarrow\mathds{1}_{\{x_{0}\}}
pointwise. Since
x_{0}
is not isolated, the
indicator
\mathds{1}_{\{x_{0}\}}
is discontinuous and therefore does not belong to

C(X)
or
C_{b}(X)
.

We verify directly that
(f_{n})
is
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy in
C(X)
(respectively
C_{b}(X)
).
Let
(n_{k})
be strictly increasing and set
d_{k}:=f_{n_{k+1}}-f_{n_{k}}
. For each

x\in X
the scalar sequence
(f_{n}(x))_{n}
is decreasing and convergent, hence

d_{k}(x)\to 0
. Thus
d_{k}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{uo}}0
(uo-convergence is pointwise in vector
lattices of real-valued functions). Moreover,
|d_{k}|\leqslant 1
for all
k
, and the
constant function
1
belongs to
C(X)
and to
C_{b}(X)
, so the differences are
order bounded by a single element. Hence
d_{k}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{Buo}}0
, and by
Proposition
1.2
we have
d_{k}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{o}}0
.
Since
(n_{k})
was arbitrary,
(f_{n})
is
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy.

If
(f_{n})
were
\mathsf{Buo}
-convergent in
C(X)
or
C_{b}(X)
, then by
Proposition
1.2
it would converge in order to some
f
in the
same space, and in particular it would converge pointwise to
f
. By uniqueness
of pointwise limits we would have
f=\mathds{1}_{\{x_{0}\}}
, a contradiction. Hence

C(X)
and
C_{b}(X)
are not sequentially
\mathsf{Buo}
-complete.

For (iii), assume
X
is locally compact and fix a decreasing neighbourhood base

(U_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
at
x_{0}
consisting of relatively compact open sets with

\bigcap_{n}U_{n}=\{x_{0}\}
. By regularity there exist continuous functions

g_{n}:X\to[0,1]
with

g_{n}(x_{0})=1,\qquad\supp g_{n}\subset U_{n}\qquad(n\in\mathbb{N}).

Define
f_{1}:=g_{1}
and
f_{n+1}:=f_{n}\wedge g_{n+1}
for
n\geqslant 1
. Then

0\leqslant f_{n+1}\leqslant f_{n}\leqslant 1
for all
n
,
f_{n}(x_{0})=1
, and
\supp f_{n}\subset U_{n}
.
In particular
f_{n}\in C_{0}(X)
for all
n
and
f_{n}\downarrow\mathds{1}_{\{x_{0}\}}

pointwise. The same argument as above shows that
(f_{n})
is
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy in
C_{0}(X)
.
Since
\mathds{1}_{\{x_{0}\}}\notin C_{0}(X)
, the sequence
(f_{n})
has no
\mathsf{Buo}
-limit in

C_{0}(X)
, so
C_{0}(X)
is not sequentially
\mathsf{Buo}
-complete.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

The construction above extends to any completely regular space
X
that contains a non-isolated point
x_{0}
admitting a countable neighbourhood base
(U_{n})
with
\bigcap_{n}U_{n}=\{x_{0}\}
. By complete regularity one can choose
g_{n}\in C_{b}(X)
with
g_{n}(x_{0})=1
and
\supp g_{n}\subset U_{n}
, and then proceed as in the proof of Proposition
2.1
. We do not attempt a full topological classification of those
X
for which
C(X)
,
C_{b}(X)
or
C_{0}(X)
are sequentially
\mathsf{Buo}
-complete.

2.1.
c_{0}
and
\ell_{\infty}

In this subsection we study
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy sequences in the classical sequence
lattices
c_{0}
and
\ell_{\infty}
. Recall that in these spaces

\mathsf{uo}
-convergence is just coordinatewise convergence of sequences (see, for
instance,
[
4
, §3]
).

Let
(x_{n})\subset c_{0}
(or
(x_{n})\subset\ell_{\infty}
) be
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy. Fix a
coordinate
j\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the scalar sequence
(x_{n}(j))_{n}
in
\mathbb{R}
.
The definition of
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy in the one-dimensional lattice
\mathbb{R}
implies
that
(x_{n}(j))_{n}
is a Cauchy sequence in the usual sense (indeed, for
every subsequence
(n_{k})
the differences
x_{n_{k+1}}(j)-x_{n_{k}}(j)

converge to
0
in
\mathbb{R}
). Hence
(x_{n}(j))_{n}
converges in
\mathbb{R}
for every

j
, and so
(x_{n})
admits a pointwise limit
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

The next lemma shows that this limit already belongs to the ambient Banach
lattice.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(x_{n})\subset c_{0}
(respectively
(x_{n})\subset\ell_{\infty}
) be

\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy and let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
denote its pointwise limit. Then

x\in c_{0}
(respectively
x\in\ell_{\infty}
).

Proof.

We treat the cases of
c_{0}
and
\ell_{\infty}
separately.

Case
c_{0}
.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that
x\notin c_{0}
. Then there exist

\varepsilon>0
and a strictly increasing sequence of indices

(\ell_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
|x(\ell_{i})|>3\varepsilon
for all
i
. Set

A\ :=\ \{\ell_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset\mathbb{N}.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
define

E_{n}\ :=\ \bigl\{k\in A:|x_{m}(k)|\geqslant\varepsilon\text{ for some }m\leqslant n\bigr\},

and

F_{n}\ :=\ \bigl\{k\in A:\sup_{m>n}|x_{m}(k)|\leqslant 3\varepsilon\bigr\}.

Since each
x_{m}\in c_{0}
, the set
\{k\in A:|x_{m}(k)|\geqslant\varepsilon\}
is
finite, so
E_{n}
is a finite union of finite sets and therefore finite.
Moreover, if
k\in F_{n}
, then
y(k):=\sup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}|x_{m}(k)|>3\varepsilon

(because
k\in A
), and the condition
\sup_{m>n}|x_{m}(k)|\leqslant 3\varepsilon

forces the existence of some
m\leqslant n
with
|x_{m}(k)|>3\varepsilon
. Hence

F_{n}\ \subset\ \bigcup_{m\leqslant n}\bigl\{k\in A:|x_{m}(k)|>3\varepsilon\bigr\},

so
F_{n}
is finite as well. Thus for every
n
the set

A\setminus(E_{n}\cup F_{n})

is infinite.

We now construct strictly increasing sequences
(n_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and

(k_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
with
k_{i}\in A
such that

(1)

|x_{n_{i+1}}(k_{i})-x_{n_{i}}(k_{i})|>\varepsilon\qquad(i\in\mathbb{N}).

Set
n_{1}:=1
. Since
A\setminus(E_{n_{1}}\cup F_{n_{1}})
is infinite, we can
choose
k_{1}\in A\setminus(E_{n_{1}}\cup F_{n_{1}})
. Then
k_{1}\notin E_{n_{1}}

implies
|x_{m}(k_{1})|<\varepsilon
for all
m\leqslant n_{1}
, so in particular

|x_{n_{1}}(k_{1})|<\varepsilon
. As
k_{1}\notin F_{n_{1}}
, we have

\sup_{m>n_{1}}|x_{m}(k_{1})|>3\varepsilon,

so there exists
n_{2}>n_{1}
with
|x_{n_{2}}(k_{1})|>3\varepsilon
.

Proceeding inductively, suppose
n_{1}<\cdots<n_{i}
and
k_{1}<\cdots<k_{i}
have
been chosen with
k_{j}\in A
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant i
. Since

A\setminus(E_{n_{i}}\cup F_{n_{i}})
is infinite and
\{k_{1},\dots,k_{i}\}
is
finite, we can choose

k_{i+1}\in A\setminus(E_{n_{i}}\cup F_{n_{i}})\quad\text{with}\quad k_{i+1}>k_{i}.

Then
k_{i+1}\notin E_{n_{i}}
implies

|x_{m}(k_{i+1})|<\varepsilon
for all
m\leqslant n_{i}
, while

k_{i+1}\notin F_{n_{i}}
implies

\sup_{m>n_{i}}|x_{m}(k_{i+1})|>3\varepsilon,

so we may choose
n_{i+1}>n_{i}
such that

|x_{n_{i+1}}(k_{i+1})|>3\varepsilon
. Hence

|x_{n_{i+1}}(k_{i+1})-x_{n_{i}}(k_{i+1})|\geqslant|x_{n_{i+1}}(k_{i+1})|-|x_{n_{i}}(k_{i+1})|>3\varepsilon-\varepsilon=2\varepsilon>\varepsilon,

and (
1
) holds (after relabelling indices if necessary).

Thus we have constructed strictly increasing sequences
(n_{i})
and
(k_{i})

with
k_{i}\in A
such that (
1
) holds for all
i
. Let

d_{i}:=x_{n_{i+1}}-x_{n_{i}}\in c_{0}
. Then for each
i
we have

|d_{i}(k_{i})|=|x_{n_{i+1}}(k_{i})-x_{n_{i}}(k_{i})|>\varepsilon.

We claim that
(d_{i})
is not order bounded in
c_{0}
. Indeed, suppose there
exists
0\leqslant z\in c_{0}
with
|d_{i}|\leqslant z
for all
i
. Then

z(k_{i})\geqslant|d_{i}(k_{i})|>\varepsilon
for all
i
, which is impossible because

z\in c_{0}
can have
|z(k)|>\varepsilon
only for finitely many
k
. Thus

(d_{i})
is not order bounded and hence cannot converge to
0
in order.

However, the sequence
(x_{n})
is
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy, so for every strictly
increasing sequence
(n_{i})
the differences
x_{n_{i+1}}-x_{n_{i}}

must converge to
0
in order, which contradicts the conclusion above.
Therefore our assumption
x\notin c_{0}
was false, and so
x\in c_{0}
.

Case
\ell_{\infty}
.

Suppose
(x_{n})\subset\ell_{\infty}
is
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy and let
x
be its
pointwise limit as above. We first show that
(x_{n})
is norm bounded in

\ell_{\infty}
.

Assume, towards a contradiction, that

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|x_{n}\|_{\infty}=\infty.

We may then choose inductively a strictly increasing sequence

(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of integers such that

\|x_{n_{k+1}}\|_{\infty}\ \geqslant\ \|x_{n_{k}}\|_{\infty}+(k+1)\qquad(k\in\mathbb{N}).

Set
d_{k}:=x_{n_{k+1}}-x_{n_{k}}\in\ell_{\infty}
. By the triangle inequality,

\|d_{k}\|_{\infty}=\|x_{n_{k+1}}-x_{n_{k}}\|_{\infty}\geqslant\|x_{n_{k+1}}\|_{\infty}-\|x_{n_{k}}\|_{\infty}\geqslant k+1,

so the sequence
(d_{k})
is not norm bounded.

On the other hand, since
(x_{n})
is
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy and
(n_{k})
is a strictly
increasing sequence, the difference sequence
(d_{k})
must converge to
0

in order in
\ell_{\infty}
. Every order-null sequence is order bounded: if

|d_{k}|\leqslant z_{m}
for all
k\geqslant N_{m}
with a decreasing sequence

(z_{m})\downarrow 0
, then

y:=|d_{1}|\vee\cdots\vee|d_{N_{1}-1}|\vee z_{1}\in(\ell_{\infty})_{+}

satisfies
|d_{k}|\leqslant y
for all
k
. In particular the set

\{d_{k}:k\in\mathbb{N}\}
is contained in the order interval
[-y,y]
, and every
order interval in a Banach lattice is norm bounded. Hence
(d_{k})
is norm
bounded, contradicting
\|d_{k}\|_{\infty}\geqslant k+1
.

Therefore
(x_{n})
is norm bounded, and we may set

M:=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|x_{n}\|_{\infty}<\infty.

For each fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
we then have

|x(k)|=\lim_{n\to\infty}|x_{n}(k)|\leqslant\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|x_{n}\|_{\infty}=M,

so
\|x\|_{\infty}\leqslant M
and hence
x\in\ell_{\infty}
.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

In the
\ell_{\infty}
case, the above argument also shows that any

\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy sequence
(x_{n})\subset\ell_{\infty}
is norm bounded:

\sup_{n}\|x_{n}\|_{\infty}<\infty
.

Theorem 2.5
.

The Banach lattices
c_{0}
and
\ell_{\infty}
are sequentially
\mathsf{Buo}
-complete.

Proof.

We again treat the two cases separately.

Case
c_{0}
.

Let
(x_{n})\subset c_{0}
be
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy. By the discussion preceding
Lemma
2.3
there is a pointwise limit

x\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
, and by Lemma
2.3
we have
x\in c_{0}
.

Since
c_{0}
is a vector lattice of real-valued sequences,
\mathsf{uo}
-convergence
coincides with coordinatewise convergence. Hence
x_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{uo}}x

in
c_{0}
. To conclude that
x_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{Buo}}x
it suffices, by the
definition of
\mathsf{Buo}
-convergence, to show that
(x_{n})
is order bounded by a
single element of
c_{0}
.

Define

y(k)\ :=\ \sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|x_{n}(k)|,\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

Clearly
y\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
|x_{n}|\leqslant y
pointwise for all
n
. We claim that

y\in c_{0}
. Suppose not. Then there exist
\varepsilon>0
and an infinite
subset
A\subset\mathbb{N}
such that

y(k)>3\varepsilon\qquad\text{for all }k\in A.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
define

E_{n}\ :=\ \bigl\{k\in A:|x_{m}(k)|\geqslant\varepsilon\text{ for some }m\leqslant n\bigr\},\qquad F_{n}\ :=\ \bigl\{k\in A:\sup_{m>n}|x_{m}(k)|\leqslant 3\varepsilon\bigr\}.

As in the proof of Lemma
2.3
, both
E_{n}
and

F_{n}
are finite for every
n
, so
A\setminus(E_{n}\cup F_{n})
is infinite.

We now construct strictly increasing sequences
(n_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(k_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

with
k_{i}\in A
such that

(2)

|x_{n_{i+1}}(k_{i})-x_{n_{i}}(k_{i})|>\varepsilon\qquad(i\in\mathbb{N}).

Set
n_{1}:=1
. Choose
k_{1}\in A\setminus(E_{n_{1}}\cup F_{n_{1}})
.
Then
|x_{n_{1}}(k_{1})|<\varepsilon
and, since
k_{1}\notin F_{n_{1}}
,
there exists
n_{2}>n_{1}
with
|x_{n_{2}}(k_{1})|>3\varepsilon
, so
(
2
) holds for
i=1
.

Assume
n_{1}<\cdots<n_{i}
and
k_{1}<\cdots<k_{i}
have been chosen and

k_{i}\in A\setminus(E_{n_{i}}\cup F_{n_{i}})
.
Since
k_{i}\notin F_{n_{i}}
, there exists
n_{i+1}>n_{i}
with
|x_{n_{i+1}}(k_{i})|>3\varepsilon
.
As
k_{i}\notin E_{n_{i}}
we have
|x_{n_{i}}(k_{i})|<\varepsilon
, hence

|x_{n_{i+1}}(k_{i})-x_{n_{i}}(k_{i})|\geqslant|x_{n_{i+1}}(k_{i})|-|x_{n_{i}}(k_{i})|>3\varepsilon-\varepsilon=2\varepsilon>\varepsilon,

which gives (
2
).

Finally, since
A\setminus(E_{n_{i+1}}\cup F_{n_{i+1}})
is infinite, we may pick

k_{i+1}\in A\setminus(E_{n_{i+1}}\cup F_{n_{i+1}})
with
k_{i+1}>k_{i}
.
This completes the construction.

Set
d_{i}:=x_{n_{i+1}}-x_{n_{i}}\in c_{0}
. Then

|d_{i}(k_{i})|=|x_{n_{i+1}}(k_{i})-x_{n_{i}}(k_{i})|>\varepsilon\qquad(i\in\mathbb{N}).

If there existed
0\leqslant z\in c_{0}
with
|d_{i}|\leqslant z
for all
i
, then

z(k_{i})\geqslant|d_{i}(k_{i})|>\varepsilon
for all
i
, contradicting the fact that

z\in c_{0}
has only finitely many coordinates exceeding
\varepsilon
in
absolute value. Thus the sequence
(d_{i})
is not order bounded in
c_{0}
,
and hence cannot converge to
0
in order, which contradicts the

\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy property of
(x_{n})
applied to the subsequence
(x_{n_{i}})_{i}
.

Therefore our assumption
y\notin c_{0}
was false, and we must have

y\in c_{0}
. Since
|x_{n}|\leqslant y
for all
n
and
x_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{uo}}x
in

c_{0}
, the definition of
\mathsf{Buo}
-convergence yields
x_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{Buo}}x
.
Thus
c_{0}
is sequentially
\mathsf{Buo}
-complete.

Case
\ell_{\infty}
.

Let
(x_{n})\subset\ell_{\infty}
be
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy and let
x\in\ell_{\infty}
be
its pointwise limit, as provided by Lemma
2.3
.
By the same lemma and its proof,
(x_{n})
is norm bounded:

M:=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|x_{n}\|_{\infty}<\infty.

Since
\ell_{\infty}
is a vector lattice of real-valued sequences,
coordinatewise convergence coincides with
\mathsf{uo}
-convergence, so

x_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{uo}}x
in
\ell_{\infty}
.

Define

y(k)\ :=\ \sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|x_{n}(k)|,\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

Then
|x_{n}|\leqslant y
pointwise for all
n
, and

\|y\|_{\infty}=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}y(k)=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|x_{n}(k)|=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|x_{n}\|_{\infty}\leqslant M<\infty,

so
y\in(\ell_{\infty})_{+}
. Thus the sequence
(x_{n})
is order bounded by

y
in
\ell_{\infty}
. Since we already know
x_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{uo}}x
, the
definition of
\mathsf{Buo}
-convergence implies
x_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{Buo}}x
.

Hence
\ell_{\infty}
is sequentially
\mathsf{Buo}
-complete as well.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

Combining Lemma
2.3
with
Theorem
2.5
, we see that every
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy sequence in

c_{0}
or in
\ell_{\infty}
has a pointwise limit in the same space and in
fact converges to this limit in order (equivalently, in the
\mathsf{Buo}
sense).
In particular, both
c_{0}
and
\ell_{\infty}
enjoy full sequential

\mathsf{Buo}
-completeness.

Without resolving sequential
\mathsf{Buo}
-completeness for
\ell_{p}
-spaces in the reflexive range, we record the following lemma that may prove useful.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
1\leqslant p<\infty
. If
(x_{n})\subset\ell_{p}
is
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
is its coordinatewise limit, then
x\in\ell_{p}
.

Proof.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that
x\notin\ell_{p}
. Then
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|x(j)|^{p}=\infty
, hence for every
K\in\mathbb{N}

the tail sum
\sum_{j\geqslant K}|x(j)|^{p}
is infinite.

We construct strictly increasing indices
(n_{i})
and pairwise disjoint finite intervals

I_{i}=[k_{i},\ell_{i})\cap\mathbb{N}\qquad(i\in\mathbb{N})

such that

(3)

\bigl\|(x_{n_{i+1}}-x_{n_{i}})\,\mathds{1}_{I_{i}}\bigr\|_{p}>1\qquad(i\in\mathbb{N}).

Set
n_{1}:=1
and choose
k_{1}
so large that
\|x_{n_{1}}\mathds{1}_{[k_{1},\infty)}\|_{p}<\tfrac{1}{4}
.
Since
\sum_{j\geqslant k_{1}}|x(j)|^{p}=\infty
, choose
\ell_{1}>k_{1}
so that

\|x\mathds{1}_{I_{1}}\|_{p}>2
for
I_{1}=[k_{1},\ell_{1})
.
Because
I_{1}
is finite and
x_{n}\to x
coordinatewise, choose
n_{2}>n_{1}
with

\|(x_{n_{2}}-x)\mathds{1}_{I_{1}}\|_{p}<\tfrac{1}{4}
. Then

\|(x_{n_{2}}-x_{n_{1}})\mathds{1}_{I_{1}}\|_{p}\geqslant\|x\mathds{1}_{I_{1}}\|_{p}-\|(x_{n_{2}}-x)\mathds{1}_{I_{1}}\|_{p}-\|x_{n_{1}}\mathds{1}_{I_{1}}\|_{p}>2-\tfrac{1}{4}-\tfrac{1}{4}>1,

so (
3
) holds for
i=1
.

Assume
n_{i},k_{i},\ell_{i}
have been chosen. Choose
k_{i+1}>\ell_{i}
so large that

\|x_{n_{i+1}}\mathds{1}_{[k_{i+1},\infty)}\|_{p}<\tfrac{1}{4}
.
Since
\sum_{j\geqslant k_{i+1}}|x(j)|^{p}=\infty
, choose
\ell_{i+1}>k_{i+1}
so that

\|x\mathds{1}_{I_{i+1}}\|_{p}>2
for
I_{i+1}=[k_{i+1},\ell_{i+1})
.
Finally choose
n_{i+2}>n_{i+1}
so that
\|(x_{n_{i+2}}-x)\mathds{1}_{I_{i+1}}\|_{p}<\tfrac{1}{4}
.
The same estimate as above gives (
3
) for
i+1
.

Now set
d_{i}:=x_{n_{i+1}}-x_{n_{i}}
. If
(d_{i})
were order bounded in
\ell_{p}
, there would exist
0\leqslant y\in\ell_{p}

with
|d_{i}|\leqslant y
for all
i
, hence
\|y\mathds{1}_{I_{i}}\|_{p}\geqslant\||d_{i}|\mathds{1}_{I_{i}}\|_{p}>1
for all
i
.
Since the intervals
I_{i}
are disjoint,

\|y\|_{p}^{p}\ \geqslant\ \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\|y\mathds{1}_{I_{i}}\|_{p}^{p}\ >\ \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}1\ =\ \infty,

contradicting
y\in\ell_{p}
. Thus
(d_{i})
is not order bounded, so it cannot converge to
0
in order.

But
(x_{n})
is
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy, so for the subsequence
(x_{n_{i}})
the differences
x_{n_{i+1}}-x_{n_{i}}=d_{i}

must converge to
0
in order, a contradiction. Therefore
x\in\ell_{p}
.
∎

3.
Lipschitz function spaces

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. For each
x\in X
, define the
isolation radius

d(x):=\inf\{d(x,y):y\in X,\ y\neq x\}\in[0,\infty].

The
uniform discreteness constant
of
X
is

\delta(X):=\inf_{x\in X}d(x)\in[0,\infty].

We say
X
is
uniformly discrete
if
\delta(X)>0
.

We write
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
for the vector lattice of all
bounded

Lipschitz functions
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
, ordered pointwise.

In vector lattices of real-valued functions
\mathsf{uo}
-convergence agrees with pointwise convergence (see
[
4
, §3]
).

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and let
g:X\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded and uniformly continuous.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define the
inf-convolution

g_{n}(x):=\inf_{y\in X}\bigl(g(y)+n\,d(x,y)\bigr)\qquad(x\in X).

Then:

(1)

each
g_{n}
is bounded and
n
-Lipschitz;

(2)

g_{n}\leqslant g
pointwise;

(3)

\|g_{n}-g\|_{\infty}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

(i)
Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
x,x^{\prime}\in X
. For any
y\in X
, the triangle inequality gives

g(y)+n\,d(x,y)\leqslant g(y)+n\,d(x^{\prime},y)+n\,d(x,x^{\prime}).

Taking the infimum over
y
on the right-hand side yields
g_{n}(x)\leqslant g_{n}(x^{\prime})+n\,d(x,x^{\prime})
.
By symmetry,
g_{n}(x^{\prime})\leqslant g_{n}(x)+n\,d(x,x^{\prime})
, hence
|g_{n}(x)-g_{n}(x^{\prime})|\leqslant n\,d(x,x^{\prime})
.

For boundedness, taking
y=x
shows
g_{n}(x)\leqslant g(x)
, so
\sup_{x}g_{n}(x)\leqslant\|g\|_{\infty}
.
Also, we have
g(y)\geqslant-\|g\|_{\infty}
and
d(x,y)\geqslant 0
, hence
g_{n}(x)\geqslant-\|g\|_{\infty}
.

(ii)
Taking
y=x
in the definition of
g_{n}(x)
gives

g_{n}(x)\leqslant g(x)+n\cdot 0=g(x)
.

(iii)
Define the modulus of uniform continuity

\omega(t):=\sup\{|g(x)-g(y)|:d(x,y)\leqslant t\}\qquad(t\geqslant 0).

Then
\omega(t)\to 0
as
t\downarrow 0
and
\omega(t)\leqslant 2\|g\|_{\infty}
for all
t\geqslant 0
.

For any
x,y\in X
,

g(y)\geqslant g(x)-|g(x)-g(y)|\geqslant g(x)-\omega(d(x,y)),

so

g(y)+n\,d(x,y)\geqslant g(x)-\omega(d(x,y))+n\,d(x,y)=g(x)-\bigl(\omega(d(x,y))-n\,d(x,y)\bigr).

Taking the infimum over
y\in X
yields

g_{n}(x)\geqslant g(x)-\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\bigl(\omega(t)-nt\bigr).

Set
\alpha_{n}:=\sup_{t\geqslant 0}(\omega(t)-nt)\geqslant 0
. Together with
g_{n}\leqslant g
we get

0\leqslant g(x)-g_{n}(x)\leqslant\alpha_{n}\qquad(x\in X),

hence
\|g-g_{n}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\alpha_{n}
.

It remains to show
\alpha_{n}\to 0
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and choose
t_{0}>0
so that
\omega(t_{0})<\varepsilon
.
For
t\in[0,t_{0}]
we have
\omega(t)-nt\leqslant\omega(t)\leqslant\omega(t_{0})<\varepsilon
.
For
t>t_{0}
, using
\omega(t)\leqslant 2\|g\|_{\infty}
gives

\omega(t)-nt\leqslant 2\|g\|_{\infty}-nt_{0}.

If
n>2\|g\|_{\infty}/t_{0}
, then
2\|g\|_{\infty}-nt_{0}<0<\varepsilon
.
Thus
\omega(t)-nt<\varepsilon
for all
t\geqslant 0
, so
\alpha_{n}\leqslant\varepsilon
.
Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary,
\alpha_{n}\to 0
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space with
\delta(X)=0
and
d(x)>0
for all
x\in X

(i.e.,
X
is discrete but not uniformly discrete). Then for every
\varepsilon>0

and every finite set
F\subset X
, there exist distinct points
a,b\in X\setminus F

with
d(a,b)<\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and a finite set
F\subset X
.
If
F\neq\varnothing
, set
\eta:=\min_{x\in F}d(x)>0
; if
F=\varnothing
, set
\eta:=\varepsilon
.
Let

\theta:=\frac{1}{4}\min\{\varepsilon,\eta\}>0.

Since
\delta(X)=0
, there exists
a\in X
with
d(a)<\theta
.
Then
a\notin F
, because
d(x)\geqslant\eta>\theta
for all
x\in F
.

By the definition of
d(a)
, choose
b\neq a
such that

d(a,b)<d(a)+\theta<2\theta\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\min\{\varepsilon,\eta\}.

In particular
d(a,b)<\varepsilon
. We claim that
b\notin F
.
Indeed, if
b\in F
then

d(b)\leqslant d(b,a)=d(a,b)<\eta/2,

contradicting
d(b)\geqslant\eta
by definition of
\eta
.
Thus
a,b\in X\setminus F
are distinct and satisfy
d(a,b)<\varepsilon
.
∎

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and let
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
be the vector lattice of bounded
Lipschitz functions
X\to\mathbb{R}
, ordered pointwise. Then
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
is sequentially

\mathsf{Buo}
-complete if and only if
\delta(X)>0
.

Proof.

For necessity, assume
\delta(X)=0
. We construct a
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy sequence in

\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
with no order limit.

We first produce a closed set
A\subset X
and a sequence
(b_{n})\subset X\setminus A
such that

t_{n}:=\mathrm{dist}(b_{n},A):=\inf_{a\in A}d(b_{n},a)\in(0,1)\quad\text{and}\quad t_{n}\downarrow 0.

Case A: there exists
x_{0}\in X
with
d(x_{0})=0
.

Then
x_{0}
is non-isolated, so there exist
b_{n}\neq x_{0}
with
d(b_{n},x_{0})\to 0
.
Let
A:=\{x_{0}\}
. Then
t_{n}=\mathrm{dist}(b_{n},A)=d(b_{n},x_{0})\downarrow 0
; passing to a tail, assume
t_{n}<1
for all
n
.

Case B:
d(x)>0
for all
x\in X
.

Then
X
is discrete, and since
\delta(X)=0
it is not uniformly discrete.
We build inductively pairs
(a_{n},b_{n})
of distinct points such that

d(a_{n},b_{n})<\frac{1}{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}\cap\{b_{1},\dots,b_{n}\}=\varnothing.

Choose any distinct
a_{1},b_{1}
with
d(a_{1},b_{1})<1
.
Assuming
(a_{1},b_{1}),\dots,(a_{n-1},b_{n-1})
chosen, let

F:=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n-1},b_{1},\dots,b_{n-1}\}.

By Lemma
3.3
, there exist distinct
a_{n},b_{n}\in X\setminus F
with
d(a_{n},b_{n})<1/n
.
Set
A:=\{a_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Then
b_{n}\notin A
and

t_{n}=\mathrm{dist}(b_{n},A)\leqslant d(b_{n},a_{n})<\frac{1}{n}<1,

so
t_{n}\downarrow 0
. Moreover
t_{n}>0
since
b_{n}\notin A
and
d(b_{n})>0
.

In both cases, define the bounded uniformly continuous function

g(x):=\sqrt{\mathrm{dist}(x,A)}\wedge 1\qquad(x\in X).

(Here
x\mapsto\mathrm{dist}(x,A)
is
1
-Lipschitz and
t\mapsto\sqrt{t}\wedge 1

is uniformly continuous on
[0,\infty)
.)

g
is not Lipschitz.

For each
n
, choose
a^{\prime}_{n}\in A
such that
d(b_{n},a^{\prime}_{n})<2t_{n}
(possible by the definition of
t_{n}
).
Then
g(a^{\prime}_{n})=0
and
g(b_{n})=\sqrt{t_{n}}
, hence

\frac{|g(b_{n})-g(a^{\prime}_{n})|}{d(b_{n},a^{\prime}_{n})}>\frac{\sqrt{t_{n}}}{2t_{n}}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{t_{n}}}\longrightarrow\infty,

so
g\notin\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
.

Approximation by bounded Lipschitz functions.

By Lemma
3.2
, the inf-convolutions

g_{n}(x):=\inf_{y\in X}\bigl(g(y)+n\,d(x,y)\bigr)

belong to
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
and satisfy
\|g_{n}-g\|_{\infty}\to 0
.

(g_{n})
is
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy.

Let
(n_{k})
be strictly increasing. Then

\|g_{n_{k+1}}-g_{n_{k}}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|g_{n_{k+1}}-g\|_{\infty}+\|g-g_{n_{k}}\|_{\infty}\longrightarrow 0.

Define
\varepsilon_{m}:=\sup_{k\geqslant m}\|g_{n_{k+1}}-g_{n_{k}}\|_{\infty}
. Then
\varepsilon_{m}\downarrow 0
, and for all
k\geqslant m
,

|g_{n_{k+1}}-g_{n_{k}}|\leqslant\varepsilon_{m}\,\mathds{1}_{X},

where
\mathds{1}_{X}
is the constant
1
function. Since
\varepsilon_{m}\mathds{1}_{X}\downarrow 0
in
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
,
it follows that
g_{n_{k+1}}-g_{n_{k}}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{o}}0
.
Thus
(g_{n})
is
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy.

(g_{n})
has no order limit in
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
.

If
g_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{o}}h
in
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
, then order convergence implies pointwise convergence, so
g_{n}(x)\to h(x)
for each
x
.
But
g_{n}\to g
uniformly, hence pointwise, so
h=g
, contradicting
g\notin\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
.
Therefore
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
is not sequentially
\mathsf{Buo}
-complete when
\delta(X)=0
.

For sufficiency, assume
\delta:=\delta(X)>0
.
Let
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded. For distinct
x,y\in X
we have
d(x,y)\geqslant\delta
, hence

\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{d(x,y)}\leqslant\frac{2\|f\|_{\infty}}{\delta}.

Thus every bounded function is Lipschitz, and consequently

\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)=\ell_{\infty}(X)

as vector lattices (both are all bounded functions on
X
with the pointwise order).

It therefore suffices to show that
\ell_{\infty}(X)
is sequentially
\mathsf{Buo}
-complete.
Let
(f_{n})\subset\ell_{\infty}(X)
be
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy.

Pointwise convergence.

Fix
x\in X
. For any strictly increasing
(n_{k})
, the differences

f_{n_{k+1}}-f_{n_{k}}
converge to
0
in order in
\ell_{\infty}(X)
, hence in particular

f_{n_{k+1}}(x)-f_{n_{k}}(x)\to 0\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}.

Thus
(f_{n}(x))_{n}
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathbb{R}
, hence convergent.
Define
f(x):=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}(x)
.

Step 2: uniform boundedness.

We claim
\sup_{n}\|f_{n}\|_{\infty}<\infty
.
Suppose not. Choose a strictly increasing
(n_{k})
with

\|f_{n_{k+1}}\|_{\infty}\geqslant\|f_{n_{k}}\|_{\infty}+(k+1)\qquad(k\in\mathbb{N}).

Set
d_{k}:=f_{n_{k+1}}-f_{n_{k}}
. Then

\|d_{k}\|_{\infty}\geqslant\|f_{n_{k+1}}\|_{\infty}-\|f_{n_{k}}\|_{\infty}\geqslant k+1,

so
(d_{k})
is not norm bounded.
However
(d_{k})
converges to
0
in order by
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchyness, hence it is order bounded:
there exists
0\leqslant y\in\ell_{\infty}(X)
with
|d_{k}|\leqslant y
for all
k
.
Then
\|d_{k}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|y\|_{\infty}
for all
k
, a contradiction.
Thus
M:=\sup_{n}\|f_{n}\|_{\infty}<\infty
, and in particular
|f(x)|\leqslant M
for all
x
, so
f\in\ell_{\infty}(X)
.

Step 3: order convergence.

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
define

z_{m}(x):=\sup_{n\geqslant m}|f_{n}(x)-f(x)|,\qquad x\in X.

Then
0\leqslant z_{m+1}\leqslant z_{m}
pointwise, and
z_{m}(x)\downarrow 0
for each
x
since
f_{n}(x)\to f(x)
.
Also
z_{m}(x)\leqslant 2M
for all
x
, hence
z_{m}\in\ell_{\infty}(X)
.
Finally, for every
n\geqslant m
we have
|f_{n}-f|\leqslant z_{m}
, so
f_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathrm{o}}f
in
\ell_{\infty}(X)
.
By Proposition
1.2
, this is equivalent to
f_{n}\xrightarrow{\mathsf{Buo}}f
.
Therefore
\ell_{\infty}(X)
(and hence
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
) is sequentially
\mathsf{Buo}
-complete.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Proposition
3.4
shows that sequential
\mathsf{Buo}
-completeness of
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)

depends only on the metric geometry of
X
: it holds exactly when
X
is uniformly discrete.
In particular:

(1)

if
X
has a non-isolated point, then
\delta(X)=0
and
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
is not sequentially
\mathsf{Buo}
-complete;

(2)

if
X
is discrete but not uniformly discrete (so
d(x)>0
for all
x
but
\delta(X)=0
), then
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
is not sequentially
\mathsf{Buo}
-complete;

(3)

if
\delta(X)>0
, then
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)=\ell_{\infty}(X)
and
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
is sequentially
\mathsf{Buo}
-complete.

The above reasoning was inspired by a characterisation of compact metric spaces provided by
[
6
]
.

Free Banach lattices

For a Banach space
X
, the free Banach lattice
\mathrm{FBL}(X)
is the
Banach lattice generated by
X
in a universal way: there exists a
canonical linear isometric embedding

\delta:X\longrightarrow\mathrm{FBL}(X),

such that every bounded operator
T:X\to E
into a Banach lattice
E

extends uniquely to a lattice homomorphism

\widehat{T}:\mathrm{FBL}(X)\to E\qquad\text{with}\qquad\|\widehat{T}\|=\|T\|.

This universal property determines
\mathrm{FBL}(X)
up to lattice
isomorphism; see
[
2
,
8
]
for details and further structure.

Even for classical spaces
X
, the geometry of
\mathrm{FBL}(X)
can be
subtle. In particular, the free Banach lattices

\mathrm{FBL}[\ell_{p}(\Gamma)]
(
1<p<\infty
) play an important role,
yet their order structure is far from completely understood.

Let
1<p<\infty
and
\Gamma
be an infinite index set.
If
(f_{n})\subset\mathrm{FBL}[\ell_{p}(\Gamma)]
is
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy, then:

•

(f_{n})
is norm bounded in
\mathrm{FBL}[\ell_{p}(\Gamma)]
;

•

for every
x^{\ast}\in B_{\ell_{q}(\Gamma)}
(where
q
is conjugate to
p
),
the scalar sequence
f_{n}(x^{\ast})
converges in
\mathbb{R}
;

•

hence
(f_{n})
admits a pointwise limit
f:B_{\ell_{q}(\Gamma)}\to\mathbb{R}
.

This shows that every
\mathsf{Buo}
-Cauchy sequence in
\mathrm{FBL}[\ell_{p}(\Gamma)]

converges in the
\mathsf{uo}
-sense to a pointwise limit
f
.
The remaining step for full
\mathsf{Buo}
-convergence would be to find order bounds
witnessing that convergence.

A natural candidate is the decreasing sequence

z_{m}(x^{\ast}):=\sup_{n\geqslant m}|f_{n}(x^{\ast})-f(x^{\ast})|,\qquad x^{\ast}\in B_{\ell_{q}(\Gamma)}.

We always have
z_{m}\downarrow 0
pointwise and

|f_{n}-f|\leqslant z_{m}
for all
n\geqslant m
.
The unresolved difficulty is to determine whether
z_{m}
belongs to

\mathrm{FBL}[\ell_{p}(\Gamma)]
.

Question 4.3

1<p<\infty

\Gamma
infinite.
For every
\mathsf{Buo}

(f_{n})

At present this appears to be open and requires new structural insight
