Use of public health benefits to design air pollution emission abatement strategies
Authors: Edgar Josué Arellano-Hernández, Victor Almanza, Aron Jazcilevich, David Rojas-Rueda, Luis Gerardo Ruiz-Suárez, Agustín García, Iván Y. Hernández-Paniagua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents a framework to identify feasible urban emission control strategies, using observed changes in air pollutants and economic activities during the Mexico City COVID-19 lockdown. The novelty of our framework lies in integrating an anomaly-based observational approach with economic activity indicators and a health impact assessment (HIA) to propose potential non-lockdown emission control strategies. Unlike descriptive existing studies, our approach explicitly links short-term air quality anomalies to sectoral activity changes, allowing for the inference of likely source categories contributing to observed pollutant reductions and the translation of these findings into targeted, economically feasible policy recommendations. The framework integrates a HIA to evaluate targeted restrictions on traffic, industrial activity, and other economic sectors. NO2 and PM2.5 exhibited the largest reductions (33.6% and 20.5%, respectively) during the Full lockdown, mostly due to decreased mobility and reduced industrial operations. By contrast, O3 increased showing that solely reducing precursor emissions is insufficient for effective O3 control. Sustained reductions in NO2 (18.3%) and PM2.5 (6.5%) like those observed during the New Normal phase could prevent an estimated 1,559 premature deaths annually from PM2.5 reductions alone, with additional avoided mortality of 542 from NO2 reductions as a non-additive co-benefit, representing an optimal balance between public health gains and economic feasibility. Our results highlight that interventions targeting the transportation sector (e.g., adopting low-emission diesel and electrification) and complementary policies in construction would further enhance emission reductions both in NO2 and PM2.5 without excessive economic costs. Although reductions in PM2.5 yield the greatest estimated avoided mortality, reductions in NO2 are an important co-benefit of mitigation strategies focused on the transport sector, yet these should not be prioritised over PM2.5. This framework provides a robust basis for designing integrated emission abatement strategies by emphasising the joint consideration of NO2 and PM2.5, and is adaptable for other urban areas in economically developing countries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55037-3.

Introduction

The COVID-19 lockdown measures were characterised by significant reductions in mobility, commercial activity, and industrial operations, which resulted in marked declines of air pollutant emissions. This unexpected experiment yielded marked reductions in ambient concentrations of carbon monoxide (CO; 10–51%), nitrogen dioxide (NO2; 15–66%), and particulate matter (PM10 and PM2.5, 5–51%)1–3 in cities worldwide. By contrast, ambient ozone (O3) concentrations frequently remained stable or even increased (by up to 13%), a counterintuitive response explained by shifts in the photochemical sensitivity of O3 production. Under typical high-NOX urban conditions, O3 accumulation is suppressed by titration with NO. However, during the lockdowns the greater reduction in NOX than in VOC emissions shifted atmospheric chemistry into a more NOX-limited regime, thereby enhancing O3 photochemical production and resulting in net increases in O3 concentrations despite reductions in other primary pollutants4,5 (Figure S1). Concentrations of other pollutants such as sulphur dioxide (SO2) exhibited no significant changes6,7. While overall improvements in air quality were expected as a result of lockdown implementations, the magnitude of pollutants reduction varied from city-to-city, driven by the stringency of lockdown measures, the spatial distribution of emission sources and local meteorological conditions.

With the gradual easing of lockdown measures implemented globally between 2021 and 2022, increases in air pollutant concentrations were observed owing to the resumption of human activities8. For instance, satellite observations revealed that NO2 returned to pre-pandemic levels as travel and industrial operations gradually resumed in urban areas of China, the East Coast of the U.S., Europe, India, Brazil, and Central Africa9. PM2.5 concentrations also increased in cities worldwide, reaching and even exceeding their pre-pandemic levels7,10. By analysing air quality data from lockdown phases with different stringency in combination with economic data from both the lockdown and gradual recovery phases, it is possible to assess real-world scenarios achievable through potential clean air policies. Furthermore, analyses of air quality data recorded during lockdowns conducted without computational air pollution models, minimise uncertainties associated with emission inventories, chemical mechanisms, and meteorological data.

Most existing studies have focused only on quantifying pre- and post-lockdown changes in air quality, while only a few have addressed the potential public health benefits of such variations. These have found that reductions in NO2 and PM2.5 yielded the greatest benefits, accounting for more than 70% of total estimated avoided premature mortality11–13. Similarly, Kumar et al.14 analysed the effects of lockdown measures on PM2.5 levels in five Indian cities and determined that 630 premature were prevented as a result of decreases in PM2.5 during the lockdown. In a further analysis, Blackman et al.15 reported reductions of 15% and 21% for ambient PM2.5 and NO2, respectively, in Bogotá, Colombia, which could avoid 23% annual premature mortality if maintained long-term. Nevertheless, most measures implemented during lockdowns are not transferable to business-as-usual conditions due to their economic and social impacts. It is important to note that those estimates are derived from observational studies that conducted HIA applying concentration-response functions (CRFs) obtained from long-term epidemiological cohort studies. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to move beyond descriptive analyses by explicitly linking air quality responses to sector-specific indicators, which allows for the attribution of observed changes to emission sources. This integration, including a HIA, allows for the incorporation of policy-relevant insights into sustained and economically feasible emission reduction policies within an effective framework.

Like many countries worldwide, Mexico implemented social distancing measures in mid-March 2020 to mitigate the spread of COVID-19. These included the mandatory use of face masks on closed environments and restrictions on commercial and industrial establishments. Table 1 summarises the dates for each phase and the specific restriction measures implemented during the first year of COVID-19 pandemic in Mexico City. These measures included limiting capacity to 25 people, closing non-essential businesses (such as private sector operations, activities in shopping centres or theatres), partial lockdowns for individuals with chronic illnesses, suspension of school activities, promotion of remote work, and limitations on both public and private vehicular mobility (e.g., reducing public transport capacity by 30%). Although these restrictions remained in effect until May, activities classified as essential were permitted to continue, including medic activities, national security, and fundamental sectors of the economy. A gradual re-opening of commercial and industrial activities began in June and continued until November. However, during November, when a resurgence in COVID-19 cases prompted the government to re-establish restrictions16,17. These different lockdown scenarios permit for the analysis of real-world restrictions and their impact on air pollutant concentrations, providing valuable insights for developing effective air pollution mitigation strategies.

This study identified potential emission control strategies that can yield public health benefits associated with reductions in air pollutant concentrations similar to those observed during the COVID-19 lockdown. Mexico City was selected as a case study considering its complex emissions structure, which includes the presence of mobile, area and industrial sources, as in many urban areas worldwide, and its atmospheric chemistry, typical of most cities in economically developing countries. The city also benefits from a well-established air quality monitoring network, which facilitates the acquisition of high-quality and high-precision air pollutant data. While the quantitative health estimates reported here are specific to Mexico City and not directly transferable, the methodological framework and the qualitative insights into source-specific emission reductions provide a transferable approach that can be adapted to other cities. The resultant HIA framework provides a basis for ranking future air quality policies according to their projected impact on human health. Firstly, net changes in NO2, O3, and PM2.5 were quantified across six COVID-19 phases. Mobility patterns and industrial activity were analysed to assess emission source changes. Then, we conducted a HIA to estimate the potential public health benefits that could be achieved if the pollutant concentration reductions observed during each phase were replicated under normal conditions over one year.

Our analysis focuses on PM2.5, NO2, and O3, as these pollutants exhibited measurable changes during the COVID-19 lockdown and have well-established CRFs derived epidemiological studies. While the health effects of air pollution involve complex mixtures of pollutants, PM2.5 has been consistently identified as the leading environmental risk factor for mortality worldwide, with robust evidence linking it to cardiovascular disease, respiratory illness, and premature death18,19. Recent systematic reviews indicate that PM2.5 accounts for over 4 million premature deaths annually, representing approximately 7.6% of total global mortality20. Furthermore, although NO2 and O3 also have independent health effects, studies suggest that the health burden of PM2.5 is significantly greater, contributing to roughly 80% of air pollution-related mortality21. Focusing on PM2.5, NO2, and O3, while acknowledging this limitation, allows conservative estimates of potential health benefits achievable through targeted emission control strategies.

Results

The baseline pollutant concentration

The daily mean concentrations of NO2, O3 and PM2.5 were calculated for the COVID-19 period within Mexico City using Eq. (1). Figure 1 shows the daily means, model fitting, and trends for these pollutants from 2016 to 2020. F–tests confirmed that our truncated Fourier series-based model significantly captures the short-term fluctuations in pollutant concentrations (p < 0.05) more effectively than simple linear models that lack independent variables, with residual standard errors below 9% for all three pollutants (Table 2). Although the magnitudes of the calculated trends are modest, statistically significant decreasing trends (p < 0.01) were observed for NO2 (4.8% yr− 1) and PM2.5 (2.9% yr− 1), while O3 exhibited no significant trend (p > 0.05). It should be highlighted that accounting for these modest, yet persistent trends, relative to inter-annual variations, is critical, as their cumulative effect over the 4-year baseline would bias the calculated anomalies if neglected. Our model explicitly parameterises and removes these trends, thereby isolating the transient lockdown signal from background changes and ensuring a reliable evaluation of its impact on air pollutant concentrations22–24. This methodological step addresses a common limitation in existing lockdown studies25–28, which frequently overlook the influence of pre-existing steady trends, and prevents the potential distortion of results that could arise from an overly extended baseline.

Net changes in air pollutants during the COVID-19 period

We assessed the net changes in NO2, O3 and PM2.5 concentrations by comparing hourly anomalies during each phase of the COVID-19 period to the corresponding baseline periods (2016–2019). The significance of these changes was determined using the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test (See Methods)6. Figure 2 shows the mean net changes in anomalies by COVID-19 phase for each pollutant alongside along with the levels of significance. The most substantial reductions were observed for NO2 concentrations, with decreases ranging from 2.4 ppb (18.3%) over the New Normal Period to 4.4 ppb (33.6%) during Full Lockdown, when the most stringent movement restrictions were in place. There was a subsequent increase in NO2 by 1.4 ppb (21.2%) when economic activities resumed to 30% capacity over the so-called Stability Phase. PM2.5 concentrations decreased significantly during the Full Lockdown, with a reduction of 8.1 µg m− 3 (20.5%) and decreased further by 3.4 to 5.9 µg m− 3 (6.5–11.8%) during the New Normal and Second Outbreak phases. By contrast, O3 concentrations increased during Partial Lockdown by 3.6 ppb (16.5%), likely due to an imbalanced reduction in NOx and VOCs, which in turn might have led to diminished O3 titration with NO2.

Potential avoided annual mortality

Table 3 presents the estimated variations in annual premature deaths based on sustained net changes in NO2, O3 and PM2.5 over a 1-year period. To avoid overestimation of potential impacts, the assessment for suspended particles was limited to PM2.5 (the most relevant pollutant in terms of public health) and to NO2 and O3, which have comparatively minor independent negative health impacts22,29. As shown, the greatest potential for preventing annual premature deaths resides in PM2.5 reductions, with estimates ranging from 1,559 [95% CI 1,184–1,743] to 3,640 [2,777–4,061], corresponding to the observed decreases during the Second Outbreak and New Normal phases, respectively. For NO2, the decrease during the New Normal and Full Lockdown could potentially prevent between 542 [CI 95% 273–1,068] and 978 [494–1,923] deaths annually. By contrast, the data indicate that increases in O3 and NO2 comparable to those observed during the Partial Lockdown and Stability phases could result in an additional 850 [95% CI 0–2,072] and 170 [85–336] annual premature deaths, respectively.

Discussion

Identification of feasible measures

Our analysis of pollutant concentration changes during the different phases of the COVID-19 lockdown demonstrates the feasibility of achieving PM2.5 and NO2 reductions under real-world conditions. Using the estimated avoidance of premature deaths during the COVID-19 phases as an indicator of public health benefits associated with air quality improvements, it is evident that reductions in PM2.5 concentrations yield the most substantial health benefits, followed by reductions in NO2 (Table 3). This was particularly evident for the Full Lockdown phase, which exhibited substantial reductions in both pollutants and, consequently, a decrease in associated mortality. However, the negative economic consequences of a full lockdown, such as company closures, job losses, and reduced industrial activity, highlight the significant financial burden associated with drastic mobility restrictions. Therefore, implementing similar measures is unsuitable as a long-term air quality strategy. Significant reductions in both PM2.5 (6.5%) and NO2 (18.3%) concentrations were observed for the New Normal phase, along with corresponding reductions in premature mortality. This finding suggests that less stringent, yet substantial, interventions can contribute to emission reductions that yield significant public health benefits. Therefore, the New Normal scenario represents an optimal balance between public health gains and economic feasibility.

Our analysis shows that achieving significant O3 reductions is more challenging than for PM2.5 and NO2, even under a full lockdown conditions5. This complexity arises from the non-linear relationship between O3 production and its precursor levels. Thus, the observed increases in ambient O3 during periods of reduced NOX levels suggest that during the lockdown, the urban core of Mexico City transitioned towards a less VOC-limited regime or even a transitional regime. To confirm that shift in O3 chemical regime, we examined the relationship between NO2 and O3 anomalies across phases. During the Partial Lockdown, the NO2:O3 anomaly ratio was − 0.87 (i.e., O3 increased while NO2 decreased), consistent with a VOC-limited regime where NOX reductions lead to diminished titration and net O3 production. This interpretation is supported by studies in Mexico City that have characterised the urban core as VOC-limited based on detailed measurements of VOC/NOX ratios and photochemical modelling4. During the Full Lockdown, when both NOX and VOC emissions were substantially reduced (as indicated by declines in both mobility and industrial activity), the NO2:O3 anomaly ratio shifted to -0.32, suggesting a transition towards a more NOX-limited regime.

Peralta et al.4 reported that the Mexico City atmosphere moved from a VOC-limited to a transitional regime during the lockdown based on analysis of O3 production efficiency and precursor ratios. Therefore, the observed O3 increases reflect the dominant VOC-limited chemistry in the urban core, whereas the more limited O3 response during the Full Lockdown indicates the effect of concurrent VOC reductions6. These regime-dependent responses highlight that effective O3 mitigation requires integrated strategies that address both NOX and VOC emissions, with particular attention to reducing VOCs from non-mobile sources, such as solvents and industrial processes. Nevertheless, the substantial mortality benefits associated with PM2.5 and NO2 reductions highlight the importance of prioritising these pollutants to maximise short-term public health outcomes, while recognising that integrated multi-pollutant strategies are necessary for the long-term management of O3.

Emissions from the transport sector

Changes in the TomTom Traffic Index30 revealed a strong association between decreases in both PM2.5 (R2 = 0.69, p < 0.05) and NO2 concentrations (R2 = 0.89, p < 0.01) and reduced vehicle usage during several lockdown phases (Table 1). The pronounced reduction in NO2 and its significant correlation with the drastic decline in vehicle mobility6, provide real-world validation for the Mexico City emissions inventory. It attributes NOX emissions to the following transport subsector: private transport (34.7%), low-capacity public transport (21.7%), heavy-duty transport (10.1%), high-capacity public transport (8.8%), motorcycles (7.9%), and agricultural and construction machinery (3.2%)31. This observational evidence supports the rationale for prioritising this sector when designing control strategies. However, only mobility restrictions of a scale similar to those implemented during the New Normal phase are likely to be feasible in the long-term. Therefore, an effective air quality improvement strategy could prioritise reducing emission from mobile sources, while minimising negative economic impacts. Motor vehicles are the largest sources of both of PM2.5 in Mexico City, accounting for approximately 41% of total emissions31. It is noteworthy that according to the official emissions inventory, PM2.5 sources are more distributed across sectors compared to the more concentrated sources of NOX. This is consistent with our observation that significant city-wide PM2.5 reductions occurred only during the Full Lockdown, when restrictions extended beyond transport to include industrial and area sources.

Strengthening and enforcing stricter diesel emissions standards presents a key opportunity to achieve substantial PM2.5 and NOX emissions reduction32. Within the transport sector, diesel-powered vehicles represent the major share, accounting for an estimated 78% of NO2 and 74% of PM2.5 emissions from mobile sources. Although, low-emission diesel technology is currently available in the Mexican market, its implementation lags behind that of the USA and Canada, partly due to the outdated NOM-044 diesel emissions standard33. Since reducing NO2 and PM2.5 emissions from diesel vehicles represent a feasible strategy, adopting vehicles equipped with low-emission diesel technology, specifically diesel particulate filters (DPF) and selective catalytic reduction systems (SCR), offers significant potential. DPFs can reduce PM2.5 emission by up to 90%, while SCR systems can reduce NOX emissions between 30% and 60%34. The use of DPF and SCR systems is already mandatory in the major economies of Asia, Europe, North America, and some countries of Latin America, making these technologies widely available and increasingly cost-effective. For example, in California, the costs for EPA 2010-compliant standards fell by around 25% in 2019. Retrofit costs for DPF and SCR systems range from approximately $100 to $1,000 USD per vehicle, depending on vehicle size and system type35.

Emission reduction measures

Ultra-low sulphur diesel (ULSD) is required for compliance with Euro V and EPA 10 emission standards and has been available in Mexico City since 2000. It plays a critical role in reducing PM2.5 and NOX emissions by enabling the effective operation of DPFs and SCR systems. All units of the bus rapid transit system and public owned bus services in Mexico City already operate with Euro V and Euro VI compliant engines. Large private delivery fleets also participate already in low-emission agreements with the Mexico City local government to comply with stricter air quality regulations. Despite the widespread availability of ULSD, many transport units that operate in Mexico City continue to use high-sulphur fuels, which contributes to increased emissions. Regulations should be strengthened to mandate that all transport fleets, including freight and private commercial vehicles, comply with Euro VI or EPA 10 emissions standards. For instance, long-haul heavy-load trucks entering and leaving Mexico City along major motorways have access to ULSD, yet these vehicles often fail to meet the most stringent emission standards.

Industrial activities, such as construction and manufacturing, experienced significant declines during the Partial Lockdown, Full Lockdown and New Normal phases of the COVID-19 pandemic, as indicated by the MIIA (Table 1). The observed correlation between these sectoral reductions and concurrent decreases in PM2.5 levels only during these restriction phases, suggest a substantial contribution from these sectors to primary emissions. This inference is supported by the official emissions inventory, which identifies that construction and industrial processes account for approximately 55% of total PM2.5 emissions31. Among industrial activities, construction sites represent a significant opportunity for targeted PM2.5 and NOX emission reductions, as they predominantly rely on diesel-powered generators and heavy machinery. Mandating the installation of DPFs on construction heavy machinery should be a primary control policy. DPFs have demonstrated high efficacy in reducing PM2.5 emissions, achieving reductions greater than 90% in previous field tests conducted in Mexico36. Incentivising the adoption of low-emission diesel technologies and electric construction equipment is crucial for mitigating emissions from the construction sector.

Promoting active mobility and enhanced mass transit can encourage a shift away from private vehicles, for example through additional bus rapid transit lines and metro expansion. Simultaneously, public transport electrification should be reinforced by gradually replacing diesel and gasoline vehicles with hybrid or fully electric alternatives37. This transition offers environmental and long-term economic benefits through reduced energy costs. However, substantial upfront investment in fleet renewal and charging infrastructure represents a significant barrier, particularly in developing economies, although these costs may be offset over time by operational savings and public health gains. Implementation barriers also include the informal nature of many transport operations, fragmented ownership structures complicating enforcement, and the need for reliable electricity supply. Successful adoption therefore requires integrated policy design, including financial incentives, phased implementation, strengthened vehicle standards, and alignment with broader urban planning. Supplementary measures could include vehicle-free days and promoting remote work, alongside thorough analysis of spatial and socioeconomic factors to ensure an equitable transition.

Mortality reduction

Our health impact assessment highlights the substantial public health benefits of maintaining air quality improvements similar to those observed during the COVID-19 period. The estimated prevention of 3,640 premature deaths annually due to PM2.5 reductions during the Full Lockdown period (representing a 6% reduction in mortality) is consistent with findings from other major urban areas worldwide. For contextual perspective, studies in Delhi and Beijing reported comparable relative mortality reductions associated with decreases in PM2.5 levels during the lockdown. However, direct comparison of absolute mortality estimates across cities is limited by differences in population size and age structure, baseline mortality rates, pre-existing pollution levels, and exposure patterns. For example, Delhi has both larger population and higher baseline PM2.5 concentrations than Mexico City, which contribute to higher absolute attributable mortality, whereas Beijing’s population age structure differs substantially from that in Mexico City. Despite these differences, the consistency of similar relative health benefits across diverse urban settings supports the generalisability of our finding that sustained PM2.5 reductions yield substantial public health benefits. Furthermore, our estimate that PM2.5 reductions during the Full Lockdown could prevent approximately 6% of annual mortality is consistent with the range of relative impacts reported in comparable megacities14,38.

The magnitude of our mortality estimates is supported by extensive epidemiological evidence linking PM2.5 exposure to cardiovascular and respiratory diseases. Recent cohort studies indicate that each 10 µg m− 3 reduction in annual PM2.5 exposure is associated with a 6–8% decrease in all-cause mortality 38. Moreover, our findings regarding NO2 reductions (preventing 542–978 annual deaths) align with growing evidence about independent health effects of NO2, particularly on respiratory outcomes21. These estimates underscore the significant public health burden of air pollution in Mexico City and demonstrate the potential benefits of maintaining even modest improvements in air quality through targeted emission control strategies. While our analysis presents separate estimates for PM2.5 and NO2 to report pollutant-specific health burdens, it is important to note that these estimates are not additive. PM2.5 is the primary driver of air pollution-attributable mortality, and the health benefits of emission control strategies should be evaluated primarily through the lens of PM2.5 reductions, with NO2 benefits interpreted as partially overlapping co-benefits.

Contribution and limitations

This study uses the COVID-19 lockdown as an observational period to identify feasible clean air quality policies that could yield public health benefits while minimising negative economic impacts, focusing exclusively on short-term mortality effects associated with changes in outdoor air pollution. The proposed framework can be adapted to cities with diverse economic profiles, considering that a set local data is available, including air quality monitoring data, sectoral activity reports, local emission inventories, and population health statistics. Using these data, the framework facilitates the design of targeted interventions. For instance, economically developed countries can focus on the complete electrification of public transport, expanding low-emission zones, and regulating emission control technologies for the industrial sector. Economically developing countries can implement a gradual transport fleet renewal program, strengthen fuel quality regulations, and promote combined programs such as introducing compress natural gas and hybrid buses. In low income countries, policies should focus on cleaner fuel standards, emission controls for informal transport fleets and investment in efficient and affordable mass transit systems. For the construction sector globally, regulations and financial incentives must transition towards cleaner technologies, including the use of low-emission machines, enforcing the installation of DPFs on diesel-powered equipment, and promoting the switch to electric and hybrid construction vehicles.

A limitation of this study is the inability to account for changes emissions from sources located elsewhere but transported to Mexico City, despite employing a robust methodology to minimise the influence of meteorological factors on pollution data. While our anomaly-based approach accounts for typical seasonal and regional background conditions, it cannot fully isolate the influence of anomalous episodic transboundary pollution, such as large-scale smoke plumes from biomass burning within the broader Megalopolis, which have been reported to significantly influence air quality in the city39. Future studies could use detailed atmospheric transport models combined with resolved emissions inventories to better account for transboundary pollution contributions40. Additionally, the spatial distribution of the monitoring network is not homogeneous within Mexico City. Rural areas, especially those in peri-urban regions, tend to have fewer stations than heavily urbanised areas31, which may introduce spatial bias in exposure assessments and potentially lead to underestimation of health effects in underrepresented populations.

A further limitation is the absence of a control city or formal quasi-experimental design. Our analysis therefore relies on a pre-post anomaly comparison with adjustment for long-term trends and meteorological variability, which, while robust, does not fully eliminate confounding. Also, the absence of routine indoor air quality data makes it difficult to consider the possible contribution of enhanced indoor emissions during lockdown, when most of the population was confined to their homes. This adds to the possible underestimation of health impacts in residential areas within heavily urbanised areas of Mexico City. Therefore, pollutant measurements with a finer spatial resolution are required to accurately quantify the contributions of various emission sources. Future studies could address this limitation by employing spatial interpolation techniques, such as kriging or land-use regression models, to construct high-resolution exposure surfaces and reduce exposure misclassification. These approaches would enable more refined HIA and better capture the intra-urban variability in air pollution exposure and associated health risks.

Furthermore, our health impact estimates are sensitive to the choice of CRFs. Although, WHO-recommended CRFs were used to maintain methodological consistency with international guidelines, the use of alternative functions would change the absolute magnitude of our mortality estimates. Future studies could incorporate sensitive analyses by comparing the impact of multiple CRF sources on the estimated mortality results. We highlight that an inherent methodological limitation of this and similar HIA is the difficulty of attributing health effects to individual pollutants within multi-pollutant environments. For example, PM2.5 and NO2 are emitted from common emission sources, particularly transport and combustion processes, and exhibit correlated spatiotemporal concentration patterns. The CRFs applied in HIA are typically derived from epidemiological cohort studies that adjust for co-pollutants to varying degrees, however, conducting a complete statistical separation of their effects is unattainable given exposure correlations. Consequently, pollutant-specific mortality estimates may capture overlapping health effects, and summing the estimates for all pollutants would led to overestimate the total attributable mortality. This limitation is common to HIA globally and reflects fundamental challenges in multi-pollutant epidemiology41.

Here, we presented pollutant-specific estimates separately, highlighting that PM2.5 must be prioritised as the primary indicator of health burden, which is consistent with the existing epidemiological evidence and recommend caution against additive interpretation. While our analysis focuses on premature mortality, we acknowledge that a more comprehensive assessment would also include morbidity outcomes such as hospital admissions, asthma exacerbations, and lost workdays. Future studies incorporating such outcomes would provide a more complete evaluation of the public health benefits not fully captured by mortality estimates alone. Finally, future research using source-oriented or multi-pollutant modelling approaches may help to refine mortality estimates, meanwhile single-pollutant CRFs remain as the standard for HIA.

Conclusions

This study investigated potential emission control strategies in Mexico City, using public health benefits as indicator for evaluating their effectiveness. We found that reductions in PM2.5 and NO2 concentrations during various phases of the COVID-19 lockdown resulted in significant declines in premature mortality, demonstrating the potential of targeted emission control strategies to improve air quality. In particular, the observed reduction in mortality associated with PM2.5 and NO2 decreases highlights that future control measures must prioritise reducing their emissions. Adopting low-emission diesel technologies, transport electrification, and regulating emissions from the construction sector could significantly contribute to this aim. Reductions in NO2 concentrations appear achievable within a shorter timeframe than for PM2.5 and may also yield significant public health co-benefits. However, our analysis suggests that an effective O3 control is more complex, as its concentrations proved challenging to reduce even under stringent lockdown conditions. The observed O3 increase during periods of reduced NOX suggests the urban core operated under a VOC-limited or transitional chemical regime. Therefore, integrated and multi-pollutant strategies are required for a successful long-term O3 management, which extend beyond precursors control alone and include targeted reductions in VOC emissions from key non-mobile sources, such as solvents and industrial processes.

Within a public policy context, our findings highlight the importance of sustainable interventions over drastic temporary restrictions. Emission control policies based on the New Normal phase, including strengthened diesel emissions regulations, promotion of active mobility and remote working, and public transport electrification, offer feasible pathways for maintaining air quality improvements while minimising economic impacts. Notably, the New Normal phase achieved significant pollutant reductions for NO2 and PM2.5 (18.3% and 6.5%, respectively) without the severe economic disruption of a Full lockdown, representing an optimal benchmark for balancing public health and economic priorities. Further measures in the construction sector, such as low-emission machinery and DPF installation, could also yield significant reductions. However, the long-term sustainability of these measures extends beyond our observational scope and will require integrated policymaking and investment. As our study period concludes in February 2021, future research extending the time series would be valuable for validating emission rebound patterns and assessing long-term air quality trajectories. Overall, this study provides a framework for designing effective clean air policies that balance environmental, economic and public health priorities in Mexico City and other urban areas facing similar challenges.

Data and methods

Description of the conceptual model and data analyses

Figure 3 presents the conceptual model and corresponding information flow for the proposed methodology developed in this study. Each process stage is detailed in the corresponding subsection indicated in each box. The analysis begins by compiling data on activity restrictions during the COVID-19 period, along with meteorological data and air pollutant concentrations for Mexico City from 2016 to 2021. We selected a four-year baseline period (2016–2019) to minimise the impact of inter-annual meteorological variability and to account for underlying long-term trends in air pollutants42,43. This approach accounts inherently for the effects of pre-existing emission control policies, as their impacts are captured within the modelled baseline trend and seasonal cycles. Thus, the calculated anomalies therefore isolate the specific changes attributable to the lockdown relative recent air quality conditions. The initial step involves dividing the COVID-19 period into the lockdown phases (Sect. 5.2), characterised by varying stringency measures and resultant changes in air pollutant concentrations. Then, the effects of meteorology, seasonality and trends on the monitoring data are minimised for each phase (Sect. 5.4), following the method described by Hernández-Paniagua et al.6. Anomalies for NO2, PM2.5 and O3 are calculated for both the baseline and for each COVID-19 phase (Sect. 5.5). Net changes in air pollutants are determined by comparing anomalies between the baseline and COVID-19 phases. These net changes are subsequently used to estimate the potential avoided premature deaths if these changes were sustained annually (Sect. 5.6). Finally, we identify emission sources that are subject to feasible controls, based on the observed effects of restrictions during the COVID-19 period (Sect. 3).

The COVID-19 phases and mobility and economic data

Six distinct phases of the COVID-19 period in Mexico City were identified from February 2020 to February 2021, in accordance with official government reports16,17. Table 1 summarises the dates corresponding to each phase, outlining the specific restriction measures implemented. Data regarding private and public mobility during these six phases were obtained from the Mexico City Government website44. Private mobility was quantified using the TomTom Traffic Index, which monitors changes in vehicular flow relative to uncongested conditions on typical days30. Public transport mobility data report fluctuations in passenger numbers relative to a standard day in 2019. Finally, the Monthly Index of Industrial Activity (MIIA) for Mexico City was used as an economic indicator for each of the six phases. It reports percentage variations observed during COVID-19 across various economic sectors (mining, power generation and transmission, construction, and manufacturing) relative to the 2013 baseline industrial production45.

Air pollutant and meteorological data

The Mexico City is the largest urban area in Mexico, covering approximately 1,500 km2 and the most populous city with 8.8 million inhabitants46. Within Mexico City, the six criteria air pollutants (CO, NO, NO2, SO2, PM10, and PM2.5), and seven meteorological parameters (wind speed, wind direction, temperature, rainfall, solar radiation, relative humidity, and pressure) have been monitored continuously since 1986. Table 4 summarises the measurement methods, regulations and instrument performance characteristics (noise, range and detection limits) for NO2, O3 and PM2.5. The monitoring instruments adhere to Mexican Official Standards and the technical guidelines of the United States Environmental Protection Agency47. Air pollutant monitoring data are summarised and reported as hourly averages. Figure 4 shows the locations of the 23 active air quality monitoring stations during 2016–2021. Hourly data for NO2, O3 and PM2.5, along with meteorological data from the COVID-19 period, were obtained from the Mexico City Atmospheric Monitoring System website (SIMAT)47. All selected monitoring sites have a minimum data capture of 75% 1-h averages for NO2, O3 and PM2.5 during the studied period. Therefore, no imputation or substitution methods were employed, i.e. our analyses were carried out using only observational data.

Controlling for meteorological variability in air pollutant data

To minimise the influence of rapid meteorological changes on air pollutant concentrations, days characterised by stagnant or rainy weather were excluded6,51. Stagnant conditions between 2016 and 2021 were identified using the Air Stagnation Index (ASI), a binary metric determined by the following daily mean thresholds: surface wind speed < 3.2 m s− 1, upper-level wind speed at 500 mb < 13 m s− 1, and daily total precipitation < 1 mm52,53. Days meeting all three criteria were classified as stagnant and excluded from the analysis. Upper-level wind speed data at 500 mb were acquired from soundings at Mexico City International Airport, available from the University of Wyoming (http://weather.uwyo.edu/upperair/sounding.html). Daily precipitation for Mexico City were obtained from the Level 3 product of the Global Precipitation Measurement mission at a 10 km resolution54. Additionally, to minimise associations between rainfall and air pollutant levels, days with significant precipitation (exceeding a 10 mm daily average) were excluded from the analysis, following the approach by Sun et al.55. Overall, during the entire period of study, 22% of days were removed, with removal rates ranging from 16% (Phase 3; Full Lockdown) to 24% (Phase 6; Second outbreak) depending on the phase, ensuring unbiased inter-annual comparisons between the baseline (2016–2019) and lockdown (2020–2021) periods.

Calculation of net pollutant changes during COVID-19

For O3, we applied the CRF to the total population (Pop = 8,811,341) using chronic respiratory mortality as the outcome, consistent with the extended American Cancer Society cohort analysis made by Jerrett et al.63, which assessed long-term O3 effects on respiratory mortality across all ages.

We acknowledge that CRF selection represents an important source of structural uncertainty in HIA41. To evaluate the sensitivity of our mortality estimates to this choice, we repeated the analysis using two alternative CRF sets (Supplementary Table S1). Firstly, we applied the updated CRFs from the recently published HRAPIE-2 project65, which provides the most current WHO guidance on concentration-response functions for health risk assessment. The HRAPIE-2 CRFs for PM2.5 [RR = 1.10 per 10 µg m− 3, 95% CI = 1.06–1.13; based on Orellano et al.66] and NO2 [RR = 1.05 per 10 µg m− 3, 95% CI = 1.03–1.07; based on Kasdagli et al.67] are derived from substantially larger evidence bases (53 and 34 cohort studies, respectively) than the CRFs used in our primary analysis. Secondly, we applied the earlier meta-analyses by Hoek et al.68 for PM2.5 [RR = 1.062 per 10 µg m− 3] and Faustini et al.69 for NO2 [RR = 1.04 per 10 µg m− 3]. Our primary estimates fall between these two alternative sets: the HRAPIE-2 CRFs yield PM2.5 mortality estimates approximately 23% higher and NO2 estimates approximately 143% higher than Table 3, while the earlier Hoek CRF yields PM2.5 estimates approximately 21% lower. The relative ranking of lockdown phases by health benefit remains unchanged across all CRF specifications, confirming that our policy conclusions are robust to CRF selection.

The CRFs employed here were derived predominantly from North American and European cohorts. Their application to Mexico City may introduce bias related to differences in PM2.5 chemical composition (with substantial contributions from secondary organic aerosols and mineral dust in Mexico City), population age structure, underlying cause-of-death profiles, and time-activity exposure patterns. The HRAPIE-2 guidance recommends the use of global CRFs over regional ones because they are more stable and derived from a broader body of evidence65. While recent evidence suggests that concentration-response relationships for PM2.5 are broadly transferable across populations19, local or regional epidemiological cohort studies would reduce this transferability uncertainty. In the absence of Mexico-specific CRFs, we follow WHO guidance for HIA applications and present our estimates as indicative of the direction and relative magnitude of health benefits rather than precise predictions.

It is important to note that PM2.5 and NO2 share common emission sources, particularly motor vehicles and combustion processes, and exhibit correlated ambient concentrations70. Consequently, the CRFs used in this analysis are derived from epidemiological studies in which exposures to these pollutants were inherently correlated, limiting the ability to fully isolate their independent health effects. Thus, the mortality estimates for PM2.5 and NO2 reported here should therefore not be summed, as doing so would risk double-counting health benefits. Finally, we present pollutant-specific estimates separately to illustrate the potential magnitude of benefits, but the total health burden attributable to emission control strategies is best approximated by the PM2.5 estimate, with NO₂ reductions representing a co-benefit of measures targeting shared sources.
