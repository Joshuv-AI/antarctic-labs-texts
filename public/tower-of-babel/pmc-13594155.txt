Cell-type specific analyses in blood and gut identify cis-eQTL matching 140 IBD risk loci and entrectinib as repurposing candidate

ABSTRACT
Genes whose expression is affected, in a consistent manner, by GWAS-identified risk variants and the disease process, constitute preferred drug targets. We herein combine cis-eQTL analysis in 27 sorted blood cell populations and 43 intestinal cell types identified by single cell RNA-Seq in the ileum, colon and rectum, with information on gene expression in patients, to search for putative drug targets for inflammatory bowel disease. We detect >95 K cis-eQTL that affect >13 K e-genes and cluster in >24 K regulatory modules. We uncover matching regulatory modules for 140 risk loci, implicating >300 e-genes not previously connected with inflammatory bowel disease, and find 152 matching e-genes whose expression is perturbed in the blood or gut of patients. We identify entrectinib, a small molecule inhibiting the NRLP3 inflammasome by binding NEK7, as a possible repurposing candidate.

Introduction

The growing prevalence of common complex diseases (CCD) threatens the sustainability of health care systems. There is a need for more effective prevention and treatment1–3.

Predisposition to most CCD has a considerable inherited component4. Accordingly, GWAS have systematically uncovered tens to hundreds of risk loci that explain up to ∼~50% of the heritability5,6. Identifying the genes that are perturbed by the risk variants in these loci is a key goal, as these constitute preferred drug targets7–10.

A common feature of CCD is that most associations are driven by regulatory as opposed to coding variants. For example, amongst the > 200 risk loci mapped for inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), only 14 encompass ORF-altering variants in the credible set (i.e., the variants in high linkage disequilibrium (LD) with high posterior probability to be causal), thereby convincingly pinpointing causative genes and hence putative drug targets11. The prevailing hypothesis is that for the remaining loci, regulatory variants perturb the expression of causative genes in cis. The challenges are to identify the causative regulatory variants, and—most importantly—the genes they perturb in cis. A common approach to achieve the latter is eQTL analyses in disease relevant cell types collected from healthy individuals. Indeed, it is reasonable to assume that: (i) the effect of most regulatory variants will manifest by genotype-specific differences in messenger levels (i.e., eQTL effects), and (ii) the corresponding eQTL, caused by common variants, are detectable in healthy individuals. If a cis-eQTL underpins a GWAS-identified risk locus it will exhibit an association pattern (vector of association log(1/p) values of all the SNPs in the locus with the gene’s expression levels, henceforth referred to as EAP for expression association pattern) that is very similar to the association pattern of the risk locus (henceforth referred to as DAP for disease association pattern), provided that GWAS and eQTL studies were conducted in cohorts of same ethnicity (and hence same LD structure). A number of colocalization methods have been developed to quantify the resemblance between DAP and EAP12–15.

Several datasets have been assembled towards that goal. The most prominent is GTEx that provides eQTL information for 52 tissues in up to 838 subjects16. A common observation from colocalization experiments is that “matching” eQTL are only found for ∼~25% of risk loci, raising questions about the molecular underpinnings of the remaining loci17. For example, we previously identified matching eQTL in 63 of 200 analyzed IBD risk loci using 23,650 eQTL identified in six circulating immune cell types and intestinal biopsies at three locations (CEDAR-1 dataset)15.

A plausible explanation for this high proportion of “orphan” risk loci, is that the corresponding eQTL remain to be discovered. This could be because the relevant cell populations were underrepresented in the samples studied to date, because the manifestation of the eQTL is context dependent (f.i. after initiation of the disease process), or because the eQTL discovered thus far (with small sample sizes when compared to disease GWAS) are distinct from the ones that drive CCD18.

In this work, we followed up on the first hypothesis, i.e., the relevant cell populations are underrepresented in the samples studied to date, in the context of IBD. Towards that goal we established the CEDAR-2 eQTL dataset based on (i) bulk transcriptome data from 27 circulating immune cell populations for 200 healthy individuals, and (ii) single-cell RNA (scRNA) transcriptome data for intestinal biopsies collected at three locations (ileum, colon and rectum) from 60 healthy individuals. We report the identification of matching cis-eQTL for 140 of 206 examined IBD risk loci, identify more than 300 novel candidate causal genes, and pinpoint opportunities for drug repurposing.

Results

60,113 cis-eQTL affecting 11,874 eQTL genes in 27 circulating immune cell populations cluster in 22,067 cis-acting regulatory modules

We collected blood from 251 healthy individuals of both sexes (Supplementary Data 1). We genotyped all individuals with Illumina’s OmniExpress array interrogating ∼~700 K SNPs, and augmented genotype data to ∼~6.3 million (M) variants by imputation. For each individual, in addition to collecting peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC), we sorted 26 distinct immune cell populations by fluorescent activated or magnetic cell sorting (FACS or MACS) (SFig. 1 and Supplementary Data 2). We produced next generation sequencing (NGS) libraries for each of the 27 cellular fractions of each individual (5292 mRNA SMART-Seq HT libraries), and generated an average of ∼~12.6 M, 2 × 150 bp (3180 libraries) or ∼~13 M, 2 × 50 bp (2112 libraries) paired-end reads per library on Illumina instruments (Supplementary Data 3). Transcriptome data were used to check the assignment of libraries to individuals and cell types, and errors corrected (SFig. 2A, B). The numbers of genes detected averaged 16,615 per cell type (range: 12,776–19,042) (Supplementary Data 4). Hierarchical clustering of cell types based on average gene expression levels generated a dendrogram largely consistent with hematopoietic ontogeny (SFig. 2C).

We performed cis-eQTL analyses using a custom-made pipeline including QTLtools19, regressing expression level on alternate allele dosage and including an average of 24.4 expression principal components (PC) in the model (Supplementary Data 4; M&M). We identified 60,113 eQTL (within cell type FDR ≤≤ 0.05), influencing 11,874 eQTL genes (e-genes) (Supplementary Data 5). The number of eQTL detected per cell type (average: 2226; range: 608–3448) was largely determined by the number of samples available for analysis (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${r}^{2}$$\end{document}r2 = 0.67) (SFig. 3A). The number of eQTL detected in PBMC was larger than expected given sample numbers, consistent with PBMC encompassing multiple cell types. The number of eQTL detected in plasmocytes was lower than expected. Controlling for the variable abundance of immunoglobulin transcripts did not increase the number of detected plasmocyte eQTL (SFig. 3B). On average, cis-eQTL explained 19.7% (range: 3.2−83.6%) of variance in gene expression (SFig. 3C). Secondary cis signals (range: 2−5) were detected for 6% of cis-eQTL, when repeating eQTL analysis conditional on the previous signals (SFig. 3D).

A module can be active in cell type A but not in cell type B because: (i) the gene is expressed in cell type A but not in cell type B, (ii) the gene is expressed in both cell types but the eQTL is only active in cell type A, or (iii) the gene is in distinct modules in cell type A and B (i.e., different EAP in cell types A and B). Expression of the gene was below detection levels in cell type B (i.e., first scenario) in 17.7% of cases. Analysis of the modules indicated that the third scenario (module switch) accounts for 8.5% of cases. We performed an interaction test to evaluate the importance of scenario 2 (see M&M) in the remaining 73.8% of the cases. We estimated the proportion of alternative hypothesis (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\pi }_{1}$$\end{document}π1) following Storey et al.20. at 94%, hence indicating that the eQTL was indeed not active in cell type B in 69.6% of cases despite the fact that the gene was expressed in cell type B (Fig. 1B-C). Estimates of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\pi }_{1}$$\end{document}π1 were nearly identical when considering genes that were more strongly expressed in cell type A (95%), or genes that were more strongly expressed in cell type B (93%) (SFig. 3E), hence arguing against reduced gene expression generating false positive scenarios 2 by affecting statistical power. For modules active in more than one cell type, the direction of the effects was the same across cell types in 96.1% of the cases, yet there were 250 cases for which the sign of the effect switched between cell types (Fig. 1D-E; Supplementary Data 6). The proportion of shared eQTL with opposite sign increased with ontogenic distance between cell types (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{{logit}}=\,$$\end{document}plogit=4 × 10−33; Supplementary Data 7). Modules were assigned to nodes and leaves in the ontogenic dendrogram using the patterns of module sharing across cell types (vectors of 0’s and 1’s) (see M&M), suggesting extensive remodeling of eQTL activity during hematopoiesis, including the common loss of progenitor cell eQTL and gain of new eQTL by differentiated cells (Fig. 1F). Genes with cis-eQTL assigned towards the root (presumed to be active in progenitor cells, n = 2776) were enriched in GO terms: antigen processing and presentation, amino-acid metabolism, organic acid and small molecule metabolic processes, and cell motility, while genes with cis-eQTL assigned towards the leaves (n = 9201) did not show enrichment in any GO terms (Supplementary Data 8). Sharing of modules by cell types was largely determined by their ontogenic proximity (p = 9 × 10−68; SFig. 3F). However, this may reflect excess sharing of gene expression as much as excess sharing of polymorphic gene regulation between closely related cell types. To distinguish between these two factors, we restricted the analyses to genes subject to multiple regulatory modules. For those we measured (i) the excess sharing of the same module between cell types, and (ii) the excess switching of modules between cell types. We showed that B cells, T cells (including CD4 and CD8) monocytes/dendritic cells, and granulocytes were forming four clusters, with excess module sharing within cluster, and excess module switching between clusters (SFig. 3G).

We merged gene-specific modules with matching EAP “across genes”, yielding a total of 22,067 regulatory modules. Modules were augmented with 7402 tier-2 eQTL matching at least one significant cis-eQTL in the module, recruiting 759 extra genes (M&M; Supplementary Data 9). We observed 2470 modules (11.2%) comprising more than one gene. On average such multigenic modules encompassed 2.9 genes (range: 2−32) and were active in 9.3 cell types (SFig. 3H). Gene-specific modules active in multiple (but not all) cell types had more chance to join a multigenic module than gene-specific modules active in only one cell type (SFig. 3I). eQTL effects with opposite sign (negative θθ) were observed for 48.8% of multigenic modules. For the 5,856 modules encompassing more than one EAP (whether from the same or different genes), we combined constituent association patterns in a consensus EAP representing the module (see M&M). We developed a web browser to visualize the activity of the regulatory modules across the genome and cell types (SFig. 4; https://tools.giga.uliege.be/cedar/publihpq).

Single-cell RNA Seq analysis of intestinal biopsies reveals 35,010 eQTL affecting 3007 genes and clustering in 3337 modules

We collected biopsies in the terminal ileum (IL), transverse colon (TC), and rectum (RE) (locations) of 60 healthy individuals. Epithelium and lamina propria (fractions) were separated, location- and fraction-specific cell suspensions hash-tagged, and subjected to scRNA-Seq using a 10X Chromium platform and Illumina sequencers. We obtained quality-filtered sequence data for a total of 293,801 cells from 57 individuals (5154 cells per individual on average). The number of reads per cell averaged 48,628, the number of unique molecular identifiers (UMI) per cell 7422, and the number of genes detected per cell 2,035 (Supplementary Data 10). Cells were assigned to one of nine sets corresponding to anatomical location (IL, TC, RE) and cell category (epithelial, immune, stromal) (see M&M). K-means clustering of the cells, by set, yielded a total of 276 clusters. Samples were merged using Harmony21, and a hierarchical tree (of clusters) constructed using the Euclidean distances between the clusters’ centroids in Harmony space. Leaves (corresponding to clusters in the original nine cell sets) and nodes in the tree were assigned to 13 epithelial, 14 lymphoid, 6 myeloid, and 10 stromal cell types (43 cell types in total) using cell-type specific gene signatures from the literature22–29 (Fig. 2; SFig. 5 and 6; and Supplementary Data 11). Numbers of cells were relatively evenly distributed across locations (Fig. 2B). Epithelial cells were more abundant than lymphoid, myeloid and stromal fractions combined (Fig. 2C). Myeloid, lymphoid and endothelial cells from the three locations overlapped well in UMAP space, while absorptive and secretory epithelial cells as well as fibroblast from distinct locations did not, supporting larger effects of location on the transcriptome for the latter (Fig. 2E). Epithelial, stromal, myeloid, and lymphoid cell types clustered in the tree as expected (except for glia and mast cells) (Fig. 2F). The segregation of ileal, colonic and rectal clusters occurred mostly at terminal branches of the tree, with the exception of absorptive epithelium, in agreement with the overlap in UMAP space (SFig. 6J).

We conducted eQTL analyses by leaf and node across the tree (551 analyses). Analyses were performed using the same custom-made pipeline including QTLtools, a pseudo-bulk approach, and leaf/node-specific PEER factors30 to correct for hidden confounders (including variable cell-type proportions) (SFig. 5). We detected 35,010 cis-eQTL (within leaf/node FDR ≤≤ 0.05) affecting 3007 e-genes (Supplementary Data 12). The number of eQTL detected per leaf/node was largely determined by the number of cells in the leaf/node with, however, a higher yield per cell for epithelial than for other categories. It plateaued at ∼~1100 presumably limited by sample size (57 individuals) (Fig. 3A). A second independent effect was detected for 259 cis-eQTL, and a third for two (Supplementary Data 12). As for circulating immune populations, we merged cis-eQTL in 3345 gene-specific modules when sharing similar EAP (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left|\theta \right|\ge$$\end{document}θ≥ 0.6), and augmented modules with 22,904 tier-2 eQTL that matched at least one significant eQTL in the module (see M&M) (Supplementary Data 13). We assigned modules to the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of all active nodes/leaves. Most modules mapped towards the root of the tree, as expected as this is where the number of cells per node is largest and hence detection power highest (Fig. 3B). However, the numbers of leaves/nodes in which a module was active was over-dispersed: modules tended to be either active in fewer leaves/nodes or in more leaves/nodes than expected assuming random assortment (p < 0.001; see M&M) (Fig. 3C), reminiscent of circulating immune cells (Fig. 1A). This suggests that numerous cell-type specific eQTL also exist in the gut. Accordingly, we observed 524 modules that were only active in one of the 43 cell-types, of which 429 were also location-specific. The latter were mainly eQTL that were active either in enterocytes or their precursors from the small intestine (SI = IL) or in colonocytes or their precursors from the large intestine (LI = TC + RE), respectively (Fig. 3D). We developed a 3D application to visualize the activity of eQTL on a UMAP, vividly illustrating the location- and cell-type-specific activity of some modules (Fig. 3E). For gene-specific modules active in more than one leaf/node, the sign of the eQTL effect was the same in all leaves/nodes for 99.2% of the modules. For 27 genes, however, the effect differed depending on the leaf/node. We mined the gene-specific catalogue for modules that were labeled lymphoid- or myeloid-specific in biopsies (two cell type categories that are also present in blood), but were “Not detected” in blood (despite the higher statistical power in blood versus gut samples). We reasoned that such a list might be enriched in cis-eQTL that would not be active in circulating immune cell population(s), but would reveal themselves in the same immune cell population(s) once becoming gut-resident. Hundred-thirty-two modules matched this pattern, of which 97 assigned to the lymphoid lineage and 35 to the myeloid lineage in the gut (Supplementary Data 18). Fourteen of the latter were mastocyte-specific eQTL, a cell type which was not represented in our blood collection, hence explaining why these were only found in the gut. For the remaining 118, we compared the intestinal EAP with that of the cognate circulating immune cells. The number and type of circulating immune cells considered for comparison depended on the cell type to which the module was assigned in the gut (Supplementary Data 16). For example, if a module was assigned to B cells in the gut, its EAP would be compared to naïve B cells, memory B cells and plasmocytes in the blood, while if a module was assigned to plasmocytes in the gut, it would only be compared to plasmocytes in the blood. The number of comparisons per gut EAP averaged 4.7 (range: 1–18). Scenarios 1, 2 and 3 accounted for 35.1, 56.7 and 8.2% of comparisons in the myeloid lineage, and 20.2, 56.4 and 23.4% in the lymphoid lineage, and these proportions did differ significantly (p = 0.0002). Moreover, both differed very significantly from the equivalent proportions in blood (pmyel = 3.8 × 10−5, plymph < 10−16). Differences were primarily driven by a higher proportion of scenario 1 (induction of gene expression), for gut-specific eQTL in the myeloid lineage, and a higher proportion of scenario 3 (module switch) for gut-specific eQTL in the lymphoid lineage (Fig. 4C).

Allele-specific mapping bias could have generated false positive compartment-specific eQTL. We excluded this for all but two genes (LGALS9 and COL16A1), by applying WASP31 to the intestinal data (SFig. 8).

Thus, it appears that there not only exist many cell-type-specific eQTL, but that—for a given cell type—eQTL can be context-specific, f.i. manifest in some anatomical compartments (resident) but not in others (circulating). CCL20 and CCL24 constitute two interesting such examples (Fig. 4D–G).

To gain additional insights in what may underpin compartment-specific eQTL, we generated scRNA-Seq data using the same chemistry (GEM-X Flex gene expression, 10X Genomics) for whole blood and intestinal biopsies (IL, TC and RE) from a single healthy individual. GEM-X Flex interrogates the transcriptome of formalin-fixed samples, and is assumed to better reflect native cell type proportions by freezing biological state and preserving fragile cells. We obtained QC-ed data for a total of 23,261 cells (blood: 6883, IL: 6909, TC: 4374, RE: 5095). The four datasets were integrated into one object with Harmony21, clustered and annotated using Seurat24 (SFig. 9A). Comparing the relative abundance of the different immune cell populations between compartments revealed a number of noteworthy features (SFig. 9B): (i) granulocytes amount to a much higher proportion of immune cells in the blood (∼~57%) than in the gut where they exhibit a rostro-caudal gradient (IL∼~2%, TR∼~6%, RE∼~11%); they are primarily composed of neutrophils in the blood (∼~98%) and of mastocytes in the gut (∼~93%); (ii) monocytes account for a higher proportion of immune cells in the blood (∼~16%), where they are mainly composed of CD14+ monocytes (∼~88%), than in the gut (∼~6%), where they are mainly composed of macrophages and dendritic cells (∼~83%), (iii) B lymphocytes account for a higher proportion of immune cells in the gut (∼~44%) than in the blood (∼~2.4%); plasmocytes account for ∼~86% of B lymphocytes in LI ( = TC + RE), for ∼~14% in SI ( = IL), and ∼~9% in blood; (iv) T lymphocytes are more abundant in the SI (∼~53%) than in the LI (∼~37%) and in the blood (∼~25%). We then examined the reasons underlying the compartment-specificity of the eQTL referred to in Supplementary Data 18. For the eQTL that were active in myeloid cells in the gut but not in the blood, the main reasons were (i) mastocyte-specific eQTL, a cell type that was not isolated and analyzed per se in the blood, and (ii) macrophage- or dendritic cell-specific eQTL, cell types that derive from monocytes upon migration in the gut, yet are absent in the blood. The latter differentiation process appears to be accompanied by the induction of new eQTL whether due to induction of gene expression (scenario 1), of novel gene regulation (scenario 2), or of module switch (scenario 3) (see above). For the eQTL that were active in lymphoid cells in the gut but not in blood, we did not observe similar shifts in cell identity. This suggests that upon migration into the gut, the transcriptome of T and B lymphocytes responds to a new microenvironment, which reveals new eQTL, yet without causing marked changes in cell identity (SFig. 9C). Of note, a large proportion of the lymphoid gut-specific eQTL were detected in plasmocytes.

Identifying cis-eQTL driving inherited predisposition to IBD

We then mined our database of blood and intestinal cis-eQTL for EAP matching DAP for IBD. We defined the boundaries of 206 risk loci reported by de Lange et al.32, including 173 IBD loci (considering CD and UC patients jointly in GWAS), 157 CD loci and 125 UC loci, by visual examination of the local association patterns obtained with (Europeans-only) ∼~25 K cases and ∼~35 K controls (Supplementary Data 19). Thirty-two risk loci that encompass composite peaks were further subdivided in sub-risk loci, to be confronted separately to cis-eQTL in addition to the complete risk locus. The numbers above correspond to ∼~51% of the 173 IBD risk loci, ∼~51% of the 157 CD risk loci and ∼~49% of the 125 UC risk loci with at least one DAP-EAP match with FDR ≤≤ 0.10, and to ∼~1/375 (IBD), ∼~1/341 (CD) and ∼~1/621 (UC) of realized DAP-EAP confrontations resulting in a match (FDR ≤≤ 0.10). We repeated the same analysis for 58 risk loci for dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) uncovered in large European case-control cohorts33. Risk for DCM is a priori less likely (than risk for IBD) to be influenced by perturbation of gene expression in blood and gut. Thus, the number of matches between DCM DAP and blood/gut EAP should be lower than for IBD. Indeed, applying the exact same procedure to DCM, detected matching DAP-EAP for ∼~17% of DCM risk loci, corresponding to one match every ∼~1923 realized DAP-EAP confrontations, i.e., approximately five times less compared to IBD, CD and UC (p ≤≤ 1.4 × 10−14). Moreover, the number of realized DAP-EAP confrontations per Kb of risk loci was 3.1 for DCM versus ∼~4.6 for IBD, indicating that fewer genes in DCM risk loci than in IBD risk loci are expressed in blood and/or gut. Taken together, this strongly supports the biological relevance of the DAP-EAP matches for IBD, CD and UC (Supplementary Data 23).

Matching EAP were detected in both blood and gut for 77 (55%) risk loci, only in blood for 33 (24%), and only in the gut for 30 (21%) (SFig. 10A). The number of DAP-matching e-genes averaged 4 per risk locus, ranging from 1 to 34 (i.e., for the 140 risk loci with at least one match). Local gene density explained only ∼~25% of the differences in number of matching e-genes per risk locus. For risk loci with matching DAP-EAP in both blood and gut, the e-genes involved were generally different. As a consequence, the number of e-genes matching DAP in both blood and gut was 57 (11%), only in blood 212 (41%), and only in gut 246 (48%) (SFig. 10B). Modules that were active only in the gut made the strongest contribution to matching DAP-EAP (Fig. 5B, C), despite the fact that they only account for ∼~10% of the modules (Fig. 4A). There was a strong correlation between the number of matching e-genes in blood and gut for a given risk locus, despite the fact that the genes involved mostly differed (SFig. 10C, D). Within the gut, all cell types contributed approximately evenly to DAP-EAP matches, except enterocyte precursors for CD and UC, and myeloid cells for UC, which contributed less (Fig. 5B, C). In circulating immune cells, regulatory modules active in all cell types contributed most to DAP-EAP matches, reminiscent of a previous report15 (SFig. 10H, I). We note the modest enrichment (1.3-fold) of modules active only in circulating natural killer (NK) cells. Only 12.5% of DAP matching e-genes were shared between the two diseases. This proportion only increased to 31.8% when restricting the analysis to the 25 risk loci associated with both CD and UC. This possibly underscores the distinct molecular determinism of the two pathologies (SFig. 10E–G).

To the best of our knowledge, no eQTL-based connection with IBD was previously reported for 366 of the 556 genes (Supplementary Data 22). Reactome analysis conducted with the complete gene list highlighted four pathways: interferon gamma signaling (found entities: CIITA, IRF5, IRF6, PTPN2 and MHC class I and II genes), interleukin-6 signaling (IL6ST, IL6R and JAK2), chemokines and their receptors (CXCR1, CXCR2, CCR2, CCR6, CXCL2, CXCL5, CCL20), and RUNX regulated immune response and cell migration (ITGA4, ITGAL) (Supplementary Data 24). We scanned the literature, for the 481 protein coding genes out of the list, for functional evidence (other than association or differential expression-based) regarding epithelial barrier function, innate or adaptive immunity that would be considered as support for causality if generated as follow-up of the GWAS and eQTL colocalization. We found such support for 221 of the 556 genes (Table 1 and Supplementary Data 25 and 26). This included six genes associated with monogenic forms of human inflammatory bowel disease (BACH2, CARMIL2, PMVK, SLC9A3, TMEM50B and TNIP1). We submitted the 481 DAP-matching protein-coding e-genes mixed with 481 non-DAP-matching control protein-coding e-genes blindly to two trained scientists, and asked them to scan the literature for evidence that genetic perturbation of the corresponding genes affects susceptibility to colitis in rodents. The first person detected 84 such cases in the DAP-matching e-genes versus 36 in the control e-genes (pp = 1.4 × 10−6), while the second detected 97 cases in DAP-matching versus 42 in control e-genes (pp = 2.3 × 10−7). This strongly suggests that our list of DAP-matching e-genes is indeed enriched in causative genes for IBD. After a final review of the literature, we kept 88 DAP-matching e-genes that upon genetic perturbation affect susceptibility to colitis in a rodent model (Supplementary Data 26). Of note, we observed a significant correlation (pp = 0.012) between the best θθ-value for a gene and the reporting of an effect of genetic perturbation of that gene on susceptibility to colitis in the mouse. Biological functions of IBD-matching e-genes

List of 221 e-genes with DAP-matching EAP and published evidence (documented in Supplementary Data 26 and Supplementary Material) for an effect on one or more functions in one or more cell types participating in epithelial barrier, innate and adaptive immunity, or other IBD-relevant mechanisms. Genes affecting multiple categories of functions are italicized.

The number of genes with incriminating functional evidence averaged 1.46 per risk locus (across the 140 risk loci), with a maximum of 15 for the rs3197999 locus on chromosome 3 (48.48–50.23 Mb). There were multiple risk loci with more than one strongly supported candidate gene. For example, the chr1:rs3180018 locus harbors the DAP-matching IL6R e-gene, member of the inflammatory cytokine pathway highlighted by the Reactome analysis, but also PMVK causing IBD-like manifestations in a compound heterozygote34. Along similar lines, the chr5:rs17656349 locus harbors the DAP-matching IRGM gene, regulating autophagy and response to various pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), and also TNIP1 coding for the “TNFAIP3 interacting protein 1”, knowing that de novo mutations in TNFAIP3 are associated with juvenile IBD35,36. Similarly, the chr16:rs28449958 locus encompasses CARMIL2 causing very early onset IBD37–39, and also SMPD3, known to regulate TNF-αα response in macrophages and B cells, and to influence the severity of DSS-induced colitis when modulated in mice40–42. There was no correlation between the number of genes with supporting functional evidence in a risk locus and its odds ratio on disease.

Particularly noteworthy is the observation that variants increasing the expression of the cystic fibrosis-causing CFTR gene in stromal and/or epithelial precursor cells increase the risk for UC while possibly decreasing the risk for CD (Fig. 5B). This may be related to recent reports that loss-of-function coding variants in CFTR protect against CD43. In addition, we found that variants affecting the expression of PRKAA1 (shown to phosphorylate and modulate CFTR activity44), CDK19 (shown to control the CFTR pathway in the intestinal epithelium of mice45), ADCY3 and PRKAR2A (both linked to ββ2 adrenergic-dependent CFTR expression46) also affect IBD susceptibility, although these effects were often detected in cells other than the intestinal epithelium. We further observed that variants that decrease SLC9A3 expression in enterocytes increase UC risk. Loss-of-function mutations in the SLC9A3 sodium-proton antiporter cause congenital diarrhea 3 and 847.

Recently, the whole exome of ∼~30,000 IBD patients was sequenced, and rare-variant burden tests (MAF < 0.001) conducted for 11,978 genes11. The distribution of burden test pp-values for 298 of our DAP-matching e-genes with sequence information did not depart from expectations under the null (Fig. 5C). Nevertheless, our list of 556 includes TAGAP, one of nine genes with one associated rare coding variant identified in this study: a rare missense variant (E147K) protecting against CD (OR: 0.786). TAGAP has an EAP in enterocyte progenitors that matches the DAP of a CD risk locus on chromosome 6 (rs212388) with positive θθ (0.79, FDR = 0.04), hence compatible with the protective effect of the missense variant. Of note, borderline DAP-EAP hits were observed for two other genes in Sazanovs’ list of nine: CCR7 (memory CD4 in gut) and RELA (plasmocytes in gut). However, for both the positive sign of θθ did, a priori, not match the increased risk associated with the reported missense variants. One gene, ATG4C, was further incriminated in this study based on a mutational burden attributed to three missense variants (suggestive signal). ATG4C was not part of our list, but ATG16L2, a paralogue of the ATG16L1 autophagy gene not previously incriminated in IBD, yielded a borderline signal with a convincing θθ of 0.77 despite a modest eQTL signal (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${p}_{{window\; adj}}$$\end{document}pwindowadj = 0.27).

We noticed 10 instances where distinct, cell type-specific EAP from the same gene match DAP from IBD risk loci that were considered different albeit adjacent (QPRT, EIF2B4, TNIP1, PRXL2B, SLC35E2B, IRGM, CDK11B, CD74, SLC25A15, ZNF589). For example, an EAP for IRGM in memory CD8 cells matches the UC DAP in risk locus chr5:rs17656349, while a distinct IRGM EAP in naïve CD4 cells matches the CD DAP in the adjacent chr5:rs11741861 locus. In the same risk locus, a CD74 EAP in colonocyte precursors of the large intestine matches the UC DAP in the chr5:rs17656349 risk locus, while a distinct CD74 EAP in mature enterocytes of the small intestine matches the CD DAP in the adjacent chr5:rs11741861 locus (Fig. 5D). Similar observations were made for multiple sub-risk loci as defined above (Supplementary Data 22). Thus, complex “composite” disease association patterns may reflect the effect of distinct risk variants that perturb the expression of the same gene in different cell types, with possibly distinct effects on disease. Also, the DAP for CD and UC, even when overlapping and considered as the same risk locus, may differ and match distinct EAP. For example, the CD DAP in risk locus chr6:rs1819333 matches an EAP for RNASET2 that is detected in nearly all circulating immune populations, while the distinct UC DAP in the same risk locus matches a CCR6 EAP active only in NKT cells.

We also observed at least 10 e-genes with DAP-matching EAPs in multiple cell types, for which the sign of θθ differed between cell types either for the same (CD: FADS1, IL18R1; UC: TOM1; IBD: PRXL2B, UBE2L3) or for different forms of IBD (CD74, IL1R2, IRGM, PRXL2B, CD244 ( = SLAMF4), SLC25A15) (f.i. Fig. 5F and Supplementary Data 26). This calls for caution when defining the desired effect of a drug (activator or inhibitor) based on the sign of θθ.

The CEDAR2 website [https://tools.giga.uliege.be/cedar/publihpq] allows for convenient visual inspection of the matching DAP-EAP patterns underpinning our analyses.

Entrectinib, a small molecule NEK7 inhibitor, is a repurposing candidate for IBD

It is not obvious that pharmacological targeting of causative genes (that upon perturbation by common regulatory variants affect the chance to develop the disease), will succeed in reversing the disease process once initiated, i.e., be curative. Additional prioritization of candidate genes whose expression is also perturbed by the disease process itself may be useful. To that end, we collected blood from 55 active CD patients, performed RNA-Seq on the same 27 fractionated circulating immune cell populations, and performed differential expression analysis between cases and controls (Supplementary Data 27). We additionally consulted lists of genes that were shown, from scRNA-Seq data, to be differentially expressed between intestinal biopsies of IBD cases and controls28, as well as lists of proteins that were differentially abundant in plasma of IBD cases and controls48. The expression of 109 of our 556 e-genes differed in cases in a manner that was consistent with the sign of θθ (i.e., both risk variant and disease increase expression, or both risk variant and disease decrease expression) in one of the three data sets (circulating immune populations, biopsies or plasma), of 40 e-genes in two of the three data sets, and of three e-genes in the three data sets (Fig. 5G).

One hundred and eighty-one drugs targeting 179 e-genes are or have been used/tested to treat IBD49,50 (Supplementary Data 28). Eleven of these overlapped with our list of 556 e-genes: CXCR2 (elubrixin: abandoned for lack of efficacy), IL6R (TJ301: phase 2 ongoing), IL6ST (olamkicept: phase 2 completed with positive results), IMPDH2 (mycophenolate: abandoned for undocumented reasons), ITGA4 (multiple in phase 2 and two approved including vedolizumab), ITGAL (efalizumab: abandoned for safety issues including multifocal leukoencephalopathy), JAK2 (multiple with positive results after phase 2 for peficitinib), NDUFAF1 (metformin: phase 2 ongoing), PDCD1 (rosnilimab: phase 2 ongoing), TEC (ritlecitinib: phase 2 completed with positive results for CD and UC) and TNFSF15 (several: phase 2 ongoing) (Supplementary Data 22). For seven of these (IL6R, IL6ST, ITGA4, ITGAL, JAK2, IMPDH2, PDCD1), the effect (presumed activator vs inhibitor) of at least some of the drugs was in agreement with the sign of θθ. For six (CXCR2, IL6ST, IL6R, ITGAL, JAK2, NDUFAF1), the effect of disease on expression/abundance level was compatible with the effect of the drug (Supplementary Data 22).

We further identified 45 genes in our list of candidates, targeted by known drugs that—to the best of our knowledge—have not been tested in the context of IBD (Supplementary Data 22). For 22 of these, the activity of at least one drug (likely inhibitor or activator) was consistent with the sign of θθ. The corresponding drugs were in phase 1 for three (PIM3, PRKDC, RPS6KB1), phase 2 for ten (ATP2A1, CDK11B, CDK19, ERAP2, HLA-DRB1, IL18R1, IL18RAP, KIR2DL1, OPRL1, PPP5C), phase 3 for two (INPP5D, PRKAA1), and approved for at least one disease other than IBD for seven (CD19, CFTR, CYP3A5, LAMA2, NEK7, PTGIR, TUBB4B). For nine of the 22 genes (underlined), expression was affected by the disease process in a manner consistent with θ,θ, at least in one of the three datasets. A mouse model of colitis exists for four of the 22 (bold) (Supplementary Data 22). Detailed examination of the drugs that were in phase 3 or higher (Supplemental Material 1), identifies entrectinib (ENB), in particular, as an intriguing repurposing candidate for IBD. Entrectinib (ENB) is a potent tyrosine multikinase small-molecule inhibitor that targets the NTRK, ROS1 and ALK oncogenes, approved by the FDA for the treatment of various tumors51. It was shown to bind to arginine 121 of NEK7, thereby inhibiting its interaction with NLRP352. Downregulation of NEK7 by intraperitoneal injection of lentiviruses expressing anti-NEK7 shRNAs was shown to attenuate DSS-induced colitis53. In mouse models, ENB effectively reduced symptoms of NLRP3 inflammasome-related diseases (other than colitis)52. ENB has a high safety profile and is well tolerated by almost all patients without cumulative toxicity54. Genetic variants that increase NEK7 expression in naïve B cells increase the risk for IBD (θθ = 0.94; FDR = 0.02), while NEK7 expression is increased in a majority of circulating immune cells of active CD patients, and in non-inflamed colonic epithelium of CD patients when compared to controls28. On the downside, there is some evidence, albeit non-significant, that the same genetic variants decrease NEK7 expression in some gut-resident immune cells (https://tools.giga.uliege.be/cedar/publihpq), while NEK7 expression is down-regulated in some circulating immune cells of active CD patients (f.i., eosinophils, Supplementary Data 22), and these findings require further scrutiny.

Discussion

Expanding the catalogue of cis-eQTL

We herein increase the number of regulatory modules (RM) in IBD relevant cell types (circulating immune cell populations and intestinal mucosa) from ∼~9720 in a previous study15, to ∼~24,745, i.e., a 2.5-fold increase. In addition, our data allows us to assign previously recognized RM to specific sub-cell types, which may provide novel insights into gene regulation and pathogenesis. This marked increase in the number of RM, despite analysis of the same tissue samples (blood and biopsies), reflects the high proportion of eQTL that are cell type-specific (reminiscent of those reported in circulating immune cells55,56, and across multiple tissues by GTEx16), anatomical location-specific (f.i. hundreds of enterocyte eQTL appear to be specific for either the small or large intestine), or even context-specific (f.i. manifest in specific gut-resident cell types but not in their circulating counterparts), and which remained buried in the previous bulk-type analyses.

The degree of cell type-specificity of RM obviously depends on the chosen level of cell type granularity. While for blood, we followed standard FACS/MACS-based immunophenotyping procedures57, yielding 26 distinct immune cell types in addition to PBMC, we developed a more dynamic approach for the intestinal scRNA-Seq data. We constructed a hierarchical tree of cell clusters (using transcriptome information), assumed to recapitulate the clusters’ ontogenic relationships. We then performed eQTL analysis separately for each one of 276 leaves and 275 nodes in the tree, and subsequently merged similar EAP into RM across leaves/nodes. We assumed that RM must be turned on at specific developmental time points, i.e., at specific nodes or leaves in the tree, and remain “on” for variable branch lengths towards the leaves. Accordingly, the RM signal is expected to be strongest in the node in which it was turned “on”. Going up towards the leaves, signal strength should decrease as the number of cells per node diminishes. Going down towards the root, signal strength should decrease as cells in which the RM is active are being diluted by cells in which it is not. In practice, however, we didn’t see the predicted log(1/p) clines up and downwards from a node of maximum significance (presumably because power is affected by multiple factors). We therefore assigned RM to the MRCA of all active nodes/leaves. This ontogenic analysis framework, that is independent of cell type annotation, considerably increased eQTL yield. For comparison, using this approach we identified 35,010 eQTL affecting 3007 e-genes and clustering in 3081 RM, while a more standard analysis by 43 cell type yielded 3743 eQTL affecting 1309 e-genes and clustering in 1368 RM. It could be applied to several other analyses of single cell data.

We provide strong evidence for the existence of compartment-specific eQTL, and more specifically for eQTL that manifest themselves in gut-resident immune cell populations, while not being detectable in the cognate circulating cells. These included ∼~1/4 myeloid-specific eQTL and ∼~3/4 lymphoid-specific eQTL. Most gut-specific eQTL of the myeloid lineage were either detected in mastocytes or macrophages, i.e., cell types that acquire this terminal differentiation stage upon migration in tissues, accompanied by profound changes in their transcriptome. Accordingly, the gut-specificity of a larger proportion of these myeloid eQTLs resulted from scenario 1, i.e., induction of gene expression in the gut (35% vs 20%). A contrario, gut-specific eQTLs of the lymphoid lineage were detected in cell types that appeared indistinguishable from their circulating counterparts on the basis of their transcriptome. Accordingly, the gut-specificity of a larger proportion of these lymphoid eQTLs resulted from scenario 3, i.e., a switch in regulatory module (23% vs 8%). The procedures used for eQTL detection in blood and gut were not identical, and, hence, one should be attentive to possible technical rather than biological causes of the inferred gut-specificity. The eQTL detection pipeline used for the blood samples included a preprocessing step to avoid variant-dependent mapping bias, which was not applied to the gut samples. We excluded this as a major source of false-positive gut-specific eQTLs, as the support for only one out of 132 candidates was affected by preprocessing of the data with WASP31 (SFig. 8). We also note that the RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis procedure differed between the blood and gut samples. The blood RNAs were extracted using the Maxwell 16 LEV simplyRNA Tissue Kit (Promega #AS1280) on a Maxwell 16 instrument (Promega) or manually using the AllPrep DNA/RNA Micro Kit (Qiagen #80284) with the QIAshredder (Qiagen #79656) and converted to full-length cDNA using SMART-Seq HT Kit (Takara #634436), which poly-A selects mRNAs, while the gut RNAs were released and cDNA synthesized using the Chromium “Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ Reagent Kits with Feature Barcoding technology for Cell Surface Protein, v3.0 or v3.1” technology. While we used both intronic and exonic reads for eQTL analyses in blood and gut, the gene coverage was more uniform in blood than in gut (typically, most exons equally covered in blood, while 3’ exons more prominently covered in gut). Also, the proportion of intronic versus exonic reads (and hence the proportion of captured pre-mRNAs versus captured mature mRNA) is expected to differ between blood and gut samples. One can imagine scenarios in which these idiosyncrasies generate eQTL that would only be detectable in gut or blood. For instance, imagine that alternative splicing generates transcripts that contain either exon I or exon II. Imagine also, that a “splicing variant” (say allele B) favors the exon II-containing transcripts. A cDNA synthesis method that covers both exons I and II would not detect differences in transcript levels between AA, AB, and BB individuals. However, a method that only interrogates one of the exons (say II) would conclude that the gene’s expression level is higher in BB than in AB than in AA individuals. With regard to the pre-mRNA versus mature mRNA ratio, variants that would affect the splicing rate of pre-mRNA or the degradation rate of mature mRNA may generate eQTL that are only detectable when interrogating either pre-mRNA or mature mRNA population (see SFig. 11). And, hence, the proportion of pre- versus mature mRNA that is interrogated by the utilized methods may affect the ability to detect the corresponding “eQTL”. However, it is well established that the genome space devoted to the control of transcription rate is substantially larger than the genome space devoted to the control of alternative splicing, splicing, or degradation rate. It is therefore reasonable to speculate, that such mechanisms could only account for a minority of observed gut-specific eQTL. For statistical reasons, we restricted this preliminary search of compartment-specific eQTL to those that are active in the gut but not in the blood. However, there is no reason to believe that the reverse situation, i.e., compartment-specific eQTL that are active in blood but not gut, does not occur as well. Obtaining an accurate estimate of how common compartment-specific eQTL truly are, however, will require larger sample numbers (especially for the biopsies).

Expanding the catalogue of IBD risk loci with matching cis-eQTL

By confronting DAP to this expanded eQTL catalogue, we reveal matching EAP for 140 of 206 studied IBD risk loci, i.e., a ∼~2.3-fold increase when compared to our previous study15. eQTL detected in the gut made a large contribution to DAP matches despite the fact that we only analyzed 57 individuals: while gut-specific RM only account for 10% of all RM (Fig. 4), they contribute 21% of the 140 EAP-matching IBD risk loci, and 48% of DAP-matching e-genes (SFig. 10A, B). eQTL that are specific for gut-resident lymphocytes and for mature enterocytes contributed most to DAP-EAP matches (Fig. 5B, C). In addition to uncovering RM that are specific for previously under-represented cell types, splitting risk loci in sub-DAP also proved effective: it revealed DAP-EAP matches for nine of the 32 risk loci on which it was applied.

It has been argued that it may be more effective—in order to find more DAP-matching eQTL—to perform eQTL analysis in one tissue type (f.i., blood) collected for (tens of) thousands of individuals, rather than in many cell types collected for hundreds of individuals. To test this, we repeated the DAP-EAP matching exercise using PBMC eQTL information from ∼~30 K European individuals58. Using this data (Võsa), we identified DAP-matching EAP for 55 loci involving 97 genes. Using our own PBMC information (i.e., 187 individuals), we identified DAP-matching EAP for 32 IBD risk loci involving 42 genes, with 20 overlapping loci and 17 overlapping genes. Using our full blood data set (27 immune cell populations), we identified DAP-matching EAP for 110 loci involving 310 genes, of which 52 loci and 61 genes overlapped with Võsa. Using our complete data set (blood and gut), we detected DAP-matching EAP for 140 loci involving 556 genes, of which 54 loci and 66 genes overlapped with Võsa. Thus, the Võsa data enabled the identification of matching EAP for one IBD risk locus (rs1479918), and 31 e-genes that were missed with our data. However, we identified matching EAP for 86 IBD risk loci, and 490 e-genes that were missed with Võsa’s data. These findings clearly indicate that a large number of additional matches are uncovered when performing eQTL analyses in multiple, disease-relevant cell types.

While our study quite dramatically increases the number of IBD risk loci with matching EAP, at least one third still remain orphan. Common risk variants for CCD, including regulatory once, are generally assumed to operate in healthy individuals, thereby increasing their probability to develop the disease. This justifies the use of cohorts of healthy individuals to discover eQTL that may underpin the risk to develop CCD, including the GTEx cohort16. It is becoming increasingly apparent, however, that for at least part of risk loci, the effect of the causative regulatory variants only manifest once the disease process has been initiated. Indeed, performing eQTL analyses in intestinal biopsies from patients has uncovered 190 cis-eQTL (out of 8,881) that were inflammation-dependent59. Also, performing eQTL analyses in blood stimulated with TCR and TLR agonists doubled the number of detected cis-eQTL when compared to unstimulated blood, uncovering new DAP-matching EAP, albeit not in extra risk loci60. More recently, an eQTL study conducted using colon samples from IBD patients indicated that ∼~10% of detected eQTL might be disease-specific. This included several instances, where the disease-process activates a distinct regulatory module (i.e., other regulatory variants drive gene expression in healthy versus diseased condition). Performing colocalization with these disease-specific eQTL resulted in the nomination of 16 (out of 194) additional target coding genes for IBD (ABO, APOBR, CTSS, EFCAB13, EMILIN3, FERMT1, GATD3A, HLA-DQA1, HLA-DRB1, HLA-DRB6, FNGR2, LRRC25, P4HA2, PARK7, PWP2, TNFRSF14)61. However, it is noteworthy that four of these (APOBR, CTSS, ARK7, TNFRSF14) are also nominated in this and/or a previous study conducted with samples from healthy individuals15. Also, by using single cell RNA-sequencing of intestinal biopsies, Alegbe et al.62 identified 764 “interaction eQTL” modulated by gut inflammation status (primarily active in enterocytes), leading to the nomination of new target genes in two additional IBD risk loci (out of 180). Thus, performing colocalization with eQTL detected in samples from IBD patients indeed uncovers additional target genes, albeit a small proportion. The blood samples of affected individuals used in this work to examine the effect of disease on gene expression will be used, in the future, to uncover affected-specific eQTL.

Expanding the catalogue of DAP-matching e-genes

It was initially assumed that finding a DAP-matching eQTL would be relatively uncommon, yet that it would provide strong evidence for causal involvement of the corresponding e-genes. Contrary to expectations, we herein report as many as 556 DAP-matching e-genes in 140 IBD risk loci, ranging from 1 to 34 e-genes per locus. Are all these genes causally involved in IBD, some or none? In other words, what is the proportion of “red herring” eQTL amongst our findings63?

There are two formal tests of gene causality: the reciprocal hemizygosity test, which is impractical in humans64,65, and the burden test, which has been used to demonstrate the causality of, for instance, NOD266,67. We took advantage of available whole exome sequence data for ∼~30 K IBD patients to test for an excess burden of ORF-disrupting variants in 298 of our DAP-matching e-genes11. One gene only (TAGAP) harbored a single, low-frequency variant significantly associated with CD. There was no other signal, whether considering the e-genes individually or as a group (Fig. 5C). While the burden test is in principle appealing, it has so far yielded few causative genes despite the considerable investments made, whether for IBD11 or other polygenic diseases68. This is possibly due to the fact that the corresponding CCD result from regulatory variants that perturb the expression of the causal genes in specific, disease-relevant cell types, while coding variants in these genes, which affect their function in all tissues, cause distinct pathologies. Thus, we don’t believe that the overall negative burden test conclusively excludes the genes’ causality, but rather that it leaves the field with no effective test of gene causality.

Amongst the strongest arguments in favor of the fact that several DAP-matching e-genes in our list are causally involved in IBD are (i) the observation that the DAP-EAP “hit rate” with the CEDAR-2 data set (focusing on immune and intestinal cell types) is ∼~5-fold lower for dilated cardiomyopathy (CDM) when compared to inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) (p≤p≤1.4 × 10−14), (ii) the observation that the scientific literature reports an effect on colitis-susceptibility following genetic perturbation in mice ∼~2.3 times more often for DAP-matching than for non-DAP-matching e-genes (p≤p≤ 1.4 × 10−6), and (iii) the observation of a correlation between the strength of the colocalization (θθ-value) and the reporting of a mouse colitis model (pp = 0.012).

It is often assumed that there is one causative gene to be found for each risk locus. Yet, we herein present quite compelling evidence that there may very well be risk loci with multiple causative genes controlled by the same or distinct sets of regulatory variants (see examples, in results, of loci with strong functional candidates that additionally harbor less well-known genes underpinning early onset, Mendelian forms of IBD). This is somewhat reminiscent, yet at a different scale, of the former demonstration that QTL effects may arise under an infinitesimal architecture69. An additional level of complexity may result from the fact that a single gene may affect disease outcome through multiple pathways, operating for instance in different cell types, triggered by the same or by different regulatory modules. For example, INAVA was shown to play a pivotal role in PRR-induced signaling, cytokine secretion and bacterial clearance in peripheral macrophages and intestinal myeloid cells70, yet at the same time to regulate the stability of adherens junctions of intestinal epithelial cells (where we see the best DAP-match)71. Also, ATG16L1 is increasingly understood to affect disease outcome through autophagy-dependent but also -independent mechanisms72, in agreement with our observation of a DAP-match in eosinophils but also colonocyte precursors.

A contrario, arguments suggesting that not all DAP matching e-genes are causally involved in IBD pathogenesis, are (i) the relatively modest enrichment of Reactome pathways (both in terms of number of highlighted pathways and significance of the enrichments; Supplementary Data 24), and (ii) the troubling observation that when the DAP of a risk locus matches EAP in both blood and gut, the e-genes involved are generally different. It is worth noting that the omnigenic model73,74 asserts that—at least for some risk loci—the effects of regulatory variants on disease are not mediated by the perturbation of (a) causative gene(s) in cis, a sobering thought for positional cloners. This does not mean that one will not observe DAP-matching e-genes, but that these e-genes do not directly participate in the disease process.

Insights into the pathogenesis of IBD

An important observation of this study is that eQTL that are only active in intestinal tissue make a disproportionate contribution to DAP-matching e-genes as compared to eQTL that are only active in circulating immune cells, in agreement with the predominant intestinal disease manifestations of IBD. Within the intestinal compartment, however, no specific cell type stood out, other than (i) the absence of any DAP-matching eQTL in enterocyte precursors, and (ii) the reduced participation of eQTL active in gut-resident myeloid cell types in UC risk loci when compared to CD risk loci (Fig. 5B, C). Unsupervised Reactome analysis of the 556 DAP-matching e-genes reveals four enriched pathways, highlighting the importance of inflammation, innate immunity and the cross-talk between innate and adaptive cellular immunity, as expected75 (Supplementary Data 26). Further “manual” consultation of the literature uncovered published, experimentally-supported links for nearly halve of DAP-matching e-genes covering nearly all facets of mucosal immunity including epithelial homeostasis and barrier function, multiple types of pattern-recognition receptors, signaling in innate immunity including IL6- and TNFαα-signaling, biology of macrophages and dendritic cells, leucocyte extravasation and trafficking, autophagy and ubiquitin-dependent processes, inflammatory mediators including prostaglandins, glucocorticoids, and the complement system, antigen presentation, signaling in adaptive immunity including BCR- and TCR-dependent, multiple classes of T and B lymphocytes, as well as other mechanisms including oxidative stress, metabolism of sphingosine and non-proteinogenic amino acids, gut motility, and host-microbiome interactions (Table 1 and Fig. 6). Jointly, perturbation of components of adaptive immunity seem to predominate. It is important to recognize, however, that the interrogation of biological functions is inherently biased by assay availability and “en vogue” paradigms, providing what is certainly a partial if not biased view. Genetic support for repurposing opportunities in IBD

We propose entrectinib (ENB) as a promising repurposing candidate for CD. Entrectinib is approved to treat solid tumors driven by specific oncogenic tyrosine kinases51. It is safe and well supported by patients54. It was recently shown in vitro to inhibit the NLRP3 inflammasome by binding to NEK752, one of our DAP-matching e-genes that is affected by the disease process in a consistent manner, at least in some cell types. Moreover, it was shown in in vivo models that shRNA-mediated NEK7 inhibition or ENB administration affects the outcome of inflammatory conditions including colitis52,53. Thus, it appears that clinical trials to test the efficacy of ENB for IBD could be undertaken. On a cautionary note, however, it appears that the CD/IBD risk variants that are associated with an increase in NEK7 expression in circulating naïve B lymphocytes may have the opposite effect (i.e., decrease NEK7 expression) in gut-resident T and B lymphocytes (including plasmocytes) (albeit not statistically significant). If these effects prove to be true, and if they contribute to the risk to develop IBD, pharmacological downregulation of NEK7 may in fact have adverse effect. Also, it was demonstrated recently that the effect of NEK7 degradation on IL1ββ production (in this case using a cereblon glue degrader), may be less potent in primates than in the mouse76.

We identify at least two instances where IBD risk variants, rather counterintuitively, decrease the expression levels of what are assumed to be positive mediators of inflammation, in particular IL18R1 and IL18RAP on the one hand, and PTGIR on the other (Supplemental material). Thus, “hypomorphic” IL18 and prostacyclin pathways may increase the risk to develop IBD, despite the fact that these pathways participate actively in the inflammatory process once initiated. These observations obviously call for caution when intending to use activators of the corresponding pathways to treat active IBD patients. It suggests, however, that treatment of IBD patients in active phase versus relapse should maybe be differentiated. It is conceivable that activation of the IL18 and prostacyclin pathways might help to prevent relapse, particularly after disease remission has been achieved following surgery.

It is now well established that the genetic architecture of CCD involves hundreds if not thousands of risk loci. Moreover, the architecture of individual risk loci may itself be fractionable, involving multiple genes perturbed by the same or distinct variants, and affecting disease outcome through multiple pathways. The multiple risk loci and genes therein are unlikely to affect disease outcome independently. It seems more likely that they trigger secondary effects along pathways that progressively merge into a decreasing number of “highways”, that ultimately determine disease. Such “highways” would involve the “core genes” as defined in the omnigenic model73,74. If the molecular species that underpin these secondary effects can be quantified, they should be amenable to QTL analysis, and reveal DAP-matching EAP for one risk locus if operating close to the cis-effects, or multiple risk loci if operating closer to the disease. This should, in theory, allow reconstruction of the topology of the pathways linking the multiple risk variants with the disease. It seems that the components of the upper parts of the network (i.e., the “highways”) would make for better drug targets than those at the bottom of the tree. Of note, the abundance of the components of the upper part of the network is expected to be most strongly correlated with polygenic risk scores for the corresponding disease and—if conveniently measurable—may constitute biomarkers capturing both genetic and environmental effects.

It is a widely held and supported view that causative genes underpinning risk loci identified by GWAS constitute preferred drug targets7–10. Yet, as already stated in results, it is not obvious that pharmacological targeting of a gene product that, upon perturbed expression by risk variants, increases the risk for a still healthy individual to develop a disease will necessarily reverse the disease process, i.e., be curative. Indeed, once the disease process is initiated, it’s maintenance may rely less on factors that triggered it in the first place. A more straightforward expectation is that targeting such causative gene products in still healthy individuals may have a preventive effect. Yet, measuring the efficacy of such (personalized) preventive drug treatments will be difficult, and its implementation is hard to envisage to date. As mentioned before, part of the missing DAP-matching eQTL may only manifest themselves after initiation of the disease process. It may be argued that the causative genes that underpin these context-specific eQTL may constitute even better drug targets. eQTL studies conducted in cohorts of affected individuals may therefore advantageously complement present eQTL studies which are primarily using healthy individuals.

Methods

Identifying cis-eQTL modules in bulk RNA-Seq data of 27 sorted circulating immune cell populations

Sample collection

We collected 40 ml of venous blood (EDTA) from 251 healthy European subjects at the academic hospital of the University of Liège (CHU) between October 2018 and November 2022. Written informed consent was obtained prior to donation in agreement with the recommendations of the declaration of Helsinki for experiments involving human subjects. The experimental protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the CHU Liège (reference number: 2017/214). Data collected from the electronic medical records (EMR) included birth date, age at sampling, ancestry, sex, weight, height, smoking and alcohol history, declared ethnicity, family history of disease, surgical and medication history, blood type when available, and known allergies. The following hematological parameters were measured: counts of white blood cells, neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, basophils, platelets, red blood cells, hemoglobin concentration, hematocrit, mean cell volume, mean cell hemoglobin, mean cell hemoglobin concentration, red cell distribution width and mean platelet volume (Supplementary Data 1).

SNP genotyping

Genomic DNA was isolated from frozen EDTA-blood using the NucleoMag Blood 200 μL kit (Macherey-Nagel) on a KingFisher robot (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Individuals were genotyped for 713,606 SNPs using Illumina’s Human OmniExpress BeadChips, an iScan system, and the Genome Studio software following the guidelines of the manufacturer. We confirmed the European ancestry of the participants by PCA (using the HapMap population as reference) as well as the absence of duplicated or related individuals (pi hat > 0.185). All individuals had less than 3% of missing genotypes. We excluded variants with call rate ≤ 0.95 or deviating from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (p ≤ 3 × 10−4) using plink (v1.9), leaving 689,223 quality-controlled variants. After lifting over to GRCh38 using Picard LiftoverVcf (v2.7.1), we phased and imputed to whole genome using the TOPMed Imputation Server (v1.6.6) and the TOPMed r2 reference panel. We removed variants with imputation score (R²) ≤ 0.7 or minor allele frequency (MAF) ≤ 0.05 using bcftools (v1.11), leaving genotypes at 6,299,998 QC-ed variants.

Cell sorting

Granulocytes were isolated from blood using the EasySep™ Direct Human Pan-Granulocyte Isolation Kit (StemCell Technologies #19659) within one hour after collection. Neutrophils and eosinophils were then isolated from the recovered granulocytes using the EasySep™ Human Neutrophil Isolation Kit (StellCell Technologies #17957) and EasySep™ Human Eosinophil Isolation Kit (StemCell Technologies #17956), respectively. Cells were recovered in cell homogenization buffer provided with the Maxwell 16 LEV simply RNA Tissue Kit (Promega #AS1280) or the AllPrep DNA/RNA Micro Kit (Qiagen #80284), and immediately frozen at −80 °C until use.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) were isolated from fresh blood using SepMate™−50 (IVD) collection tubes (StemCell Technologies #85460) and Lymphoprep™ density gradient medium (StemCell Technologies #07861). After two washing steps, PBMC were stained with two panels of antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C. Cells suspended in PBS were then filtered using a 100 µm CellTrics filter (Sysmex #04004-2328). Cell sorting was performed on a FACS Aria III instrument (BD Biosciences) calibrated using CS&T beads (BD Biosciences). Fluorescence compensations were performed using CompBeads (BD Biosciences #552843). After exclusion of debris and doublets, we targeted monocytes (classical, non-classical and intermediate), T lymphocytes (including γδ, mucosal-associated invariant T cells (MAIT) cells, naive and memory regulatory T cells, naive and memory CD8+ T cells, naive and memory CD4+ T cells, Th1, Th2, Th17 and Th1/17 helper T cells), B lymphocytes (naive, memory and plasmocytes), dendritic cells (plasmacytoid and myeloid), natural killer cells (NK and NKT) and innate lymphoid cells (ILC). Panels of antibodies used, definition of sorted cells, and gating strategies are described in SFig. 1 and Supplementary Data 29. Purity of the sorted cell populations ranged from 78% to 99%. Up to 20,000 cells of each cell population were sorted directly on the cell homogenization buffer and frozen immediately at −80 °C until use. No residual samples remain.

Bulk RNA sequencing

Total RNA was purified from sorted cells using the Maxwell 16 LEV simplyRNA Tissue Kit (Promega #AS1280) on a Maxwell 16 instrument (Promega) or manually using the AllPrep DNA/RNA Micro Kit (Qiagen #80284) with the QIAshredder (Qiagen #79656), according to their respective manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quantity was determined for all samples using the Quant-iT RiboGreen RNA Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific #R11490), while the RNA quality was evaluated for a subset of samples using the RNA 6000 Pico Kit on a 2100 Bioanalyzer instrument (Agilent #5067-1513). Reagents for cDNA and library preparation were dispensed with a Mantis Liquid Handler (Formulatrix) using half the recommended volumes. Full-length cDNA was generated from 1 ng of total RNA using the SMART-Seq HT Kit (Takara #634436), which poly-A selects mRNAs. Obtained cDNA quantity and quality were determined for all samples using the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific #P7589), and for some using the High Sensitivity DNA kit on a 2100 Bioanalyzer instrument (Agilent #5067-4626), respectively. Uniquely indexed libraries were constructed from 300 pg of cDNA using the Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina #FC-131-1096) and custom-made 24 forward and 16 reverse primers. The libraries’ quantity was assessed for all samples by qPCR using a KAPA Library Quantification Kit (Roche#07960140001), while the libraries’ quality was checked for all samples using a QIAxcel Advanced technology (Qiagen). The libraries were pooled and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 instrument (Illumina), to an average read depth of 11 ± 5 million paired-end reads per sample (University of Geneva core facility (Geneva): 2 × 50 bp (2112 samples); GIGA Genomics platform (Liège): 2 × 150 bp (3180 samples)). We performed RNA-seq for 5292 samples, corresponding to 27 cell types from 196 individuals. No residual samples remain.

Read mapping and quantification

Demultiplexing and FASTQ conversion were performed using bcl2fastq (v2.20). Read quality was assessed with FastQC (v0.12.1) and multiQC (v0.9). Reads were mapped to the GRCh38 (Ensembl release 105) human genome build using STAR (v2.7.1a). The STAR re-implementation of the WASP algorithm was used with a custom VCF file containing both reference and alternative alleles for all SNPs. The alignments that did not pass WASP filtering or that overlapped indels were removed from the resulting BAM files using samtools (v1.9). Alignment metrics were collected using Picard CollectRnaSeqMetrics (v2.7.1) (Supplementary Data 3). Matching of genomic and transcriptome genotypes was evaluated with QTLtools mbv (v1.3.1) (SFig. 2A). Unstranded gene counts were generated with HTSeq (v0.6.1p1). If samples were split across two sequencing lanes, we summed up the respective counts. To detect mislabeled samples, reads counts were normalized using the variance stabilizing transformation from the DESeq2 R package, and a t-SNE analysis was conducted using the 500 most variable genes (SFig. 2). Supplementary Data 3 reports the characteristics of the 5030 RNA-seq libraries that passed the quality control procedure.

Transcriptome-based hierarchical clustering of circulating immune cell types

A dendrogram was constructed based on the RNA-seq data, using 1-|Spearman’s correlation| between average (across all individuals) gene expression levels, using the “average”, “ward.d” and “ward.d2” methods. For each method, we built 32 dendrograms with from 250 to 8000 (by steps of 250), genes with best F statistic (cell type effect) from ANOVA. Within each method, we assessed the reliability of each dendrogram in a way inspired by the bootstrap procedure: for each node in the dendrogram, we computed the proportion among the 31 other trees that shared the same split. Within each method, we selected the dendrogram(s) with the highest sum of “bootstrap-like” values. Memory CD4 T cells, PBMCs, and granulocytes were ignored in this analysis as they encompass multiple cell types.

Cis-eQTL analyses

For each blood cell type, we filtered out the genes with less than 5 counts in more than 80% of samples, normalized the raw read counts using the DESeq2 R package and residualized them for age, sex, RNA extraction method, proportion of reads in each sequencing batch, top 3 genotype principal components (PCs) and top 13–36 expression PCs to maximize the number of cis-eQTLs. Supplementary Data 4 reports the number of samples, genes, and top expression PCs for each cell type. We removed variants with MAF ≤ 0.05 in the retained samples using bcftools (v1.9) for each cell type. We performed eQTL mapping using QTLtools19 in a 2 Mb cis-window centered at the transcription start site and with the integrated rank normal transformation of the phenotypes. QTLtools applies two sequential corrections for multiple testing. The first corrects for the multiple markers that are tested in each cis-window. To account for the linkage disequilibrium (LD) that may exist between these markers, correcting for multiple testing is accomplished by applying a permutation scheme in which the expression phenotypes are randomized with respect to the unaltered vectors of marker genotypes (to maintain the LD structure). For each permutation pass, the best nominal p-value (across all markers in the window) is stored, yielding a list of NN best p-values (where N is the number of permutations) under the null. The significance of the nominal p-values for the real (unpermuted) data, accounting for the testing of multiple markers per window, is then estimated as the proportion of equally low or lower p-values in the list of NN best p-values under the null. To obtain accurate estimates of these corrected p-values, even when inferior to 1/NN, while limiting the number of permutations to run, QTLtools uses an approximation method based on the beta distribution77, using data from 10,000 permutations. For each gene, QTLtools keeps the marker with the best corrected p-value in the window, yielding a list of window-corrected best p-values (one per gene) across the genome. These corrected p-values are then subjected to a second round of correction (for the testing of multiple genes) using the false discovery rate (FDR). eQTL with “within cell type FDR” ≤ 0.05 were considered significant.

Agglomerating cis-eQTL in gene-specific and across-genes cis-acting regulatory modules (RM)

To assess if the expression levels of a given gene are affected by the same regulatory variants in a given pair of tissues, we compared the corresponding eQTL association patterns (EAP) using the θθ metric devised in Momozawa et al.15 Two EAP were compared if at least one of them was significant (FDR ≤ 0.05). We only used variants that had a pp-value below 0.05 in at least one of the two EAP to compute θθ. Two EAP were assumed to be part of the same cis-acting regulatory module (RM) if |θθ| ≥ 0.6 (cfr. ref.15), and if the window-adjusted p-value of the eQTL (for non-significant eQTL) was ≤≤ 0.12, as these parameter values were shown to yield an agglomeration FDR ≤≤ 0.05 in a permutation test (see DAP-EAP matching section, hereafter). To better describe the connectivity of the modules, we retrospectively also computed θθ for pairs of non-significant EAP if they were assigned to the same module. We first constructed gene-specific modules, i.e., we only confronted EAP from the same gene yet from different cell types. In a second stage, we constructed across-gene modules by evaluating the similarity of the EAP of different genes in the same or in different tissues, using the union of the overlapping 2 Mb windows, and using the same threshold values as above. We defined a representative EAP for each module as the EAP with the lowest adjusted pp-value for ββ. For all EAP in a module, the sign of ββ was compared to that of the representative EAP. If the modules encompassed more than one EAP, we performed a meta-analysis to combine the constituent EAP in a consensus EAP representing the module. For each variant of the complete window (2–4 Mb), we converted nominal pp-values to zz-score which we squared and summed across all EAP in the module. The corresponding sum was assumed to have a chi-squared distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the number of EAP in the module. Quantifying the statistical significance of the overdispersion of module activity across cell types

We quantified the dispersion for the real data, as the variance of the sum of active cell types across modules. We then performed “permutations”, in the sense that—for a given cell type—the activity states (1’s and 0’s) were permuted between modules. This was repeated for all cell types, and the dispersion of that permutation computed as the variance of the sum of “active” cell types across modules. The statistical significance of the real dispersion was then defined as the proportion of permutations that yielded as large or larger variance than the real data.

Assigning modules to nodes and leaves in the ontogenic dendrogram

Gene-specific modules were assigned to nodes and leaves of the best supported ontogenic tree (SFig. 2C). Modules that encompassed only one cell type were assigned to the leaves of the tree, while modules that encompassed more than one cell type were assigned to the node corresponding to the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of those cell types. Descendants of such MRCA nodes that were not encompassed by the module were assumed to have lost the eQTL effect. If these were part of another regulatory module for the same gene, the “loss” was converted to a “switch”. Memory CD4 T cells, PBMC and granulocytes were ignored in this analysis (cfr. above).

Testing for an excess sharing of RM between cell types

We expect that an eQTL detected in cell type ii (f.i. B lymphocytes) will be shared more often with cell type jj (f.i. T lymphocytes) that is ontogenetically closely related to ii, than with cell type kk (f.i. neutrophils) that is less closely related. In our regulatory module-based analysis framework, this means that monogenic regulatory modules (cRM) active in cell type ii will more often be active in cell type jj (and hence shared between ii and jj) than in cell type kk. This constitutes our alternative hypothesis (H1). The corresponding null hypothesis (H0) assumes that a monogenic cRM that is active in cell type ii, has equal probability to be active in cell type jj as in cell type kk. For various reasons, the number of eQTL detected differs between cell types (SFig. 3A), and this will affect the sharing probabilities. Probing the causes of the cell type-specificity of gene-specific RM

Why is a cis-eQTL for gene “X” detected in cell type a but not b? We distinguished three possible scenarios: (i) Module switch: gene “X” is subject to a cis-eQTL effect in both cell type a and b, but the variants involved are distinct (i.e. dissimilar EAP), and the two cis-eQTL are assigned to different RM, (ii) Lack of expression cell type b: gene “X” is expressed at too low levels in cell type b to allow for the detection of a cis-eQTL effect, and (iii) Conditional eQTL effects: gene “X” is expressed at sufficient levels in cell type b, but there is no evidence for a cis-eQTL effect (significant variants x cell type interaction effect). To test the statistical significance of the third scenario, we performed an interaction test using a linear model including genotype effect, cell type effect, genotype x cell type interaction effect, and a random intercept (to account for individual, across-cell type effects on a gene’s expression level), in R (lmer function of lme4 and lmerTest packages). To mitigate scale-dependent effects, we used within-cell-type, QQ normalized expression values (the same as the ones used for eQTL analysis). This removes cell type effects but doesn’t preclude testing the interaction effect. We estimated the proportion of true alternative hypotheses amongst the realized tests (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\pi }_{1}$$\end{document}π1 as defined by Storey and Tibshirani20) separately for genes whose expression level was higher in cell type a than in cell type b on the one hand and genes whose expression level was higher in cell type b than in cell type a on the other hand, to evaluate to what extend lower (and hence noisier) expression levels in cell type b may inflate the number of significant interactions.

Identifying cis-eQTL modules in single-cell RNA-Seq data from intestinal biopsies at three anatomical locations

Sample collection

Gut biopsies were obtained from 60 healthy adults (27 females and 33 males, average age was 54 years, ranging from 23 to 75) that were visiting the University Hospital of the University of Liège as part of a screening campaign for colon cancer between June 2019 and December 2021. Written informed consent was obtained prior to donation in agreement with the recommendations of the Declaration of Helsinki for experiments involving human subjects. The experimental protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of CHU Liège (reference number: 2017/214). Two to four biopsy “bites” were collected from rectum (RE) and transverse colon (TC) for all participants, while biopsies from the terminal ileum (IL) were obtained for 52. Biopsies were collected in 40 ml of RPMI-1640 culture medium (Lonza Bioscience, 12–167 F) supplemented with 2 mM L-Glutamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 25030024) and 10% FBS (Sigma, F7524) on ice, and processed freshly within one hour from the time of colonoscopy. Data collected from the electronic medical record (EMR) were the same as for the individuals providing blood samples (Supplementary Data 1). No residual samples remain.

SNP genotyping

Was conducted using the same procedure as for the circulating immune cell population samples (see above). We kept 686,493 SNPs interrogated by Illumina’s OmniExpress array after quality control, while genotypes at 6,352,658 variant positions were kept after imputation and quality control.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Biopsies from the three locations (IL, TC, RE) were processed in parallel using so-called two-step protocols22,28 with some modifications. Fractionation of epithelial (EC) and lamina propria (LP) cell layers: The biopsies delivered in the transport media were collected by passing the media through a 100 μm cell strainer (Pluriselect Life Science, 43-50100-50), and transferred to a 50 ml EZFlip tube (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10571663) with 25 ml of pre-warmed Epithelial Strip Buffer consisting of HBSS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 14170088), 5 mM EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, AM9260G), 15 mM HEPES (Lonza Bioscience, 17-737E) and 5% FBS and a magnetic stirring bar. The sample was agitated with gentle stirring (130 rpm) for 10 min at 37 °C by placing the tube upside down on a magnetic stirrer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 50088009) in a 37 °C incubator. After adding DTT to a final concentration of 1 mM (VWR, 443852 A), the sample was incubated for another 10 min with agitation at 37 °C. The sample was taken out of the incubator and shaken by hand vigorously for 10–15 s. It was passed through a 100 μm cell strainer to fractionate EC in the flow-through in a new 50 ml canonical tube, while the LP remained on the cell strainer. The LP sample on the strainer was rinsed with Washing Buffer (HBSS supplemented with 1 mM EDTA and 1% FBS) and kept on ice in a 6-well containing Wash Buffer. Dissociation of EC: The tube with the EC fraction was filled with ice-cold Washing Buffer up to 50 ml and centrifuged at 500 rcf for 5 min at 4 °C. After carefully removing the supernatant, the sample was transferred to a 1.5 ml siliconized microtube (Sigma, T4816) using 100 μl of TrypLE Express enzyme solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 12604-013). The sample was then mixed ten times using a 200 μl tip and incubated in a water bath at 37 °C for 5 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 1 ml of ice-cold Wash Buffer. As we generally obtained larger numbers of cells from LP than EC, the LP cell suspension was divided into two or three tubes, while EC was kept in one tube (total 10 tubes). Preprocessing of scRNA-Seq data

Raw sequencing data were preprocessed with the Cellranger software version 7.1.0 with standard parameters and GRCh38 (Ensembl release 103) human genome build as reference. Processed counts were further analyzed in R (version 4.3.1) within the Seurat tools ecosystem (Seurat version 4.1.324). For each sample, mRNA read counts and hashtag barcode read counts were loaded to R and quality-checked. We excluded (i) hashtag barcodes with <300 reads per individual, (ii) cells with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge \,$$\end{document}≥50% mitochondrial reads, (iii) cells with <200 different genes expressed, (iv) cells with <5 hashtag barcode reads. Demultiplexing of cells was done using two different algorithms implemented in Seurat: HTODemux78 (positive.quantile 0.999, clusterization function - kmeans) and MULTIseqDemux79 with automated threshold finding. Cells identified as singlets of the same tissue type by the two methods were kept for further analysis. To meet the RAM usage requirements, variable features selection was done in five sample batches with the “mean.var.plot” algorithm (3000 features per batch). We retained the intersection between the five batches. We then integrated the sample batches in the space defined by the first 50 principal components using Harmony21. The UMAP plots shown throughout the manuscript (f.i. Fig. 2D, E) are in this coordinate system. Yet, to better differentiate cell types and improve the clustering, we further split the data in two stages. We first used Hashtag information to separate cells by anatomical location (IL, TC, RE). Within each anatomical location we repeated the variable feature selection and integration process. In each of these three sub-datasets, we identified cell clusters with the Louvain algorithm. Based on marker gene expression, we assigned clusters to three groups: immune (expressing PTPRC), epithelial (expressing EPCAM), and endothelial plus other cells (expressing VWF, PECAM1, CDH5, or not included in previous groups). Within each one of these nine sub-groups, we again repeated variable features selection, integration, and clustering (Louvain clustering algorithm with resolution parameter 1.5). This yielded a total of 276 clusters across the nine data sets (SFig. 5).

Constructing a hierarchical tree of cell clusters

We computed, for each of the 276 location- and cell-type specific cell clusters (obtained as described above), the mean coordinate vector in the space of the first 50 Harmony coordinates. Then we computed the Euclidean distance between these vectors followed by hierarchical clustering (function hclust, stats R package, “complete” agglomeration method). The final dendrogram was constructed with the dendextend80 R package.

Cis-eQTL analysis

We performed eQTL analysis for each leaf and node in the hierarchical tree, provided that the median cells per patient was ≥≥ 5, and that number of patients with cells in the leaf/node was ≥≥ 30. This left 401 leaves/nodes for eQTL analysis. Within each analyzed leaf or node, all cells were treated as pseudo-bulk, i.e., as if all reads were derived from one mega-cell. Resulting gene expression data were normalized using DESeq2, residualized for age, sex, and five genotype PCs, and corrected for hidden confounders utilizing the probabilistic estimation of expression residuals (PEER) algorithm30. We restricted eQTL analysis to genes expressed in more than 10% of samples and with mean proportion of reads > 5 × 10−7. eQTL analyses, window-specific multiple testing correction of nominal p-values, and computation of leaf/node-specific q-values followed the same procedures used for blood.

Agglomerating cis-eQTL in gene-specific and across-genes cis-acting regulatory modules (RM)

The construction of cis-acting regulatory modules (RM) was done in a similar way as for the circulating immune cell populations, across all 276 leaves and 275 nodes of the hierarchical tree. For non-genome-wide-significant eQTL to be added to modules, their window-corrected p-value had to be ≤≤ 0.012, as this yielded mergers with FDR ≤≤ 0.05 (see DAP-EAP part, hereafter).

Cell type annotation and cell type to hierarchical tree map

Cell type annotation was largely done by visually inspection of the expression profiles of 49 cell type-specific gene signatures obtained from the literature22–29 using the Seurat AddModuleScore function81, and the Azimuth human PBMC reference mapping program for immune cells24, on our global UMAP (i.e., all 293,801 QC-ed cells) (Fig. 2D). In essence, the distribution of a cell type-specific gene-signature on the UMAP was confronted with the distribution of the cells from each node/leaf of our hierarchical tree, looking for best matches. A given cell-type was assigned to the best matching node (and hence all descendent nodes/leaves). We further distinguished location-specific (i.e., ileum, colon and rectum) nodes and leaves within cell type-specific sections of the tree. The workflow and outcome of this analysis are shown in SFig. 8 and Supplementary Data 11.

Assigning regulatory modules to intestinal cell types

Regulatory modules encompass one or more EAP that can be active in one or more nodes/leaves of the hierarchical tree. If all nodes/leaves in which a module is active belong to the same cell type (see previous section), the module was assigned to that cell type (and possibly anatomical location within cell type). If nodes/leaves belonging to a module correspond to multiple cell types, the module was assigned to one of 29 supergroups, listed in Supplementary Data 11 and 17. As an example, if a module was active in CD4 and CD8, it was assigned to the T lymphocyte supergroup.

Exploring the distribution of the number of nodes/leaves in which regulatory modules are active

As for blood, each module is characterized by a vector of 0’s and 1’s informing us about the nodes/leaves in which the module is active. In the case of the intestinal scRNA-Seq data, the length of the vector is 155 leaves + 246 nodes = 401 elements. The length of the vector is less than the sum of the total number of leaves and nodes in the module, because some nodes/leaves didn’t have any active module in them. The sum of 1’s in a module vector, i.e., the total number of leaves/nodes in which the module is active, is what we are looking at in Fig. 3C. More specifically, we are looking at the distribution of this sum across all modules. Conversely, the activity of a leaf/node can be summarized by a vector of 3345 (gene-specific modules) or 3081 (across-gene modules) 0’s and 1’s, indicating which module is active in the corresponding leaf/node. To verify whether the observed distribution differs from the expected one, assuming that the activity of a module in a given leaf/node is independent of its activity in other leaves/nodes, we assigned the 0’s and 1’s of a given leaf/node randomly to the modules, i.e., we randomly permuted module id within leaf/node. We then summed the number of 1’s across the modules and examined the distribution of this sum across modules (gray bars in Fig. 3C). We know that the activities of a module in adjacent nodes/leaves in the tree are not independent, as these have cells in common. To properly evaluate the statistical significance of the apparent “overdispersion” of module activity (too many modules either active in few nodes/leaves, or in many nodes/leaves), we restricted to the analysis to “parent” nodes of the 49 distinct cell types, selected such that no cell could be part of more than one such cell type (i.e., none of the selected nodes is ancestor of any other one). We quantified the dispersion for the real data, as the variance of the sum of active nodes across modules. We then performed “permutations”, in the sense that—for a given node—the activity states (1’s and 0’s) were permuted between modules. This was repeated for all nodes, and the dispersion of that permutation computed as the variance of the sum of “active” nodes across modules. The statistical significance of the real dispersion was then defined as the proportion of permutations that yielded as large or larger variance than the real data.

3D plots of cis-eQTL activity

We developed an application to visualize the activity of an eQTL of interest on a 3D UMAP plot. Briefly, for each cell in the dataset, we identified the 100 nearest neighbors in the space defined by the 50 first expression principal components using the Annoy algorithm [https://github.com/spotify/annoy] implemented in the Seurat FindNeighbors function. We eliminated cell-centered neighborhoods encompassing cells from only one individual, less than five cells with non-null expression for the e-gene of interest, and MAF < 0.05 for the eQTL’s top SNP amongst the individuals with cells in the neighborhood. We then performed eQTL analysis in the remaining neighborhoods, one at a time, using a mixed model82,83 including allele dosage (for the eQTL’s top SNP) as fixed regression, and individual as random effect. For each neighborhood, we then multiplied -log(p-value) of the eQTL effect by the sign of the regression coefficient (ββ), assigned it to the cell defining the neighborhood, and plotted it as the third, z dimension of a 3D plot, at the x-y coordinate position corresponding to the position of the reference cell in 2D UMAP space.

Gut biopsies from terminal ileum, transverse colon and rectum ( ~30 mg each) were collected in 40 ml of ice-cold Advanced DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco 12634-010). Comparing the yield of DAP-EAP matches between IBD and dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM)

To evaluate the relevance of the DAP-EAP matches observed for IBD, we repeated the same analyses for dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM). DCM has a genetic architecture that is comparable to that of IBD, yet it is expected to be driven primarily by eQTL operating in the cardiovascular system rather than in immune cells or in the gut. Thus, we would expect that the yield of matching DAP-EAP in the CEDAR-2 set will be substantially lower for DCM than for IBD. Using the data from a large DCM GWAS conducted in Europeans33, we defined the boundaries of 58 DCM risk loci using the exact same procedure as the one used for IBD. We did not split risk loci in sub-loci and excluded, for the comparison, these splitting events from the IBD results. We then conducted DAP-EAP colocalization using our θθ-based approach following the exact same procedure and significance thresholds as for IBD. We compiled the number of DAP-EAP confrontations realized for DCM as well as for IBD, CD and UC. The results from IBD, CD and UC were kept separate (rather than aggregated as in Fig. 5A) for comparison. The outcome of interest was the proportion of realized DAP-EAP confrontations that yielded a match. The success rates of DCM and IBD, DCM and CD, DCAM and UC were compared using a 2 × 2 contingency table test implemented with the chisq.test(M) function in R.

Defining the activity profile of DAP-EAP matching, gene-specific regulatory modules

We selected, by risk locus, disease, and gene, all DAP-matching EAP with FDR ≤≤ 0.10. All selected EAP were considered to be part of the same gene-specific module as they all matched a common DAP. This module was then assigned to one of five categories in blood (lymphoid, myeloid, granulocytes, multiple cell types or undetected) depending on the cell type(s) in which the corresponding eQTL were detected, and to one of seven categories in the gut (lymphoid, myeloid, enterocyte precursors, mature enterocytes, stromal, multiple cell types, undetected) depending on the nodes/leaves of the hierarchical tree in which the corresponding eQTL were detected. For the gut, and as before, the eQTL was assigned to the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of all active nodes/leaves, and the corresponding MRCA node/leave was then assigned to one of the seven categories. We then compared the distribution of the DAP-EAP matches in this 5 × 7 grid with the equivalent distribution of all monogenic modules. We performed a goodness-of-fit test on a cell-by-cell basis as before.

Comparing the proportion of genes that upon genetic perturbation affects susceptibility to colitis in rodent models between DAP-matching and not-DAP-matching protein-coding e-genes

If the genetic perturbation of genes selected by virtue of a DAP-EAP match have a higher probability to affect the susceptibility of mice to induce colitis (whether increased or decreased susceptibility) than randomly selected genes, this would support the fact that the DAP-matching e-genes are enriched in causative genes for IBD. To test this as objectively as possible, we submitted our list of 481 protein-coding DAP-matching e-genes, mixed with 481 protein-coding non-DAP-matching genes, “blindly” to two scientists trained in the life sciences. We asked them to review the literature for all these genes with the objective to find reports of an effect of gene perturbation of the corresponding genes on the susceptibility to induced colitis in a rodent model. We defined genetic perturbation as: constitutive or conditional germ-line knock-out, constitutive or conditional overexpressing germ-line transgene, or knock-out in immune cells transferred to RAG−/− mice. We did not consider knock-down with siRNA or pharmacological perturbation, as the specificity of those interventions were considered to be more difficult to prove. The 481 “control” genes were matched for expression profile across the 27 blood cell types, and the 43 gut cell types. For each DAP-matching e-gene we identified as non-DPA-matching control, the e-gene (hence with a cis-eQTL) that was the closest (Euclidian distance) in multi-tissular expression space. The statistical significance of the difference in “success” rate (i.e., the proportion of genes with such report) between DAP-matching and non-DAP-matching e-genes was determined using a standard goodness-of-fit test.

Effects of IBD on gene expression in circulating immune cell populations

We collected blood from 55 active CD patients and 100 healthy controls, fractionated 26 circulating immune cell populations by FACS or MACS following the same protocol as for eQTL analysis, and performed RNA-Seq on the 26 fractionated cell populations as well as on PBMC as for eQTL analysis (vide supra), all as one batch. Differential expression analysis between controls and patients was performed for all expressed genes by cell type using the DESeq2 R package84, with the apeglm method for effect size shrinkage85. Genes with a fold change ≥ ≥ 1.5 and FDR ≤ ≤ 0.05 were considered differentially expressed (Supplementary Data 27). Supplemental Table 22 lists, for 556 genes with DAP-matching EAP, the blood cell types for which CD has a significant effect on gene expression (FDR ≤ ≤ 0.05, log2 fold change ≥ ≥ 1.5). For each such significant effect, Supplementary Data 22 provides cell type, effect (log2 fold change), and FDR. If, for a given gene, the majority sign of the effects (across cell type) corresponds to the majority sign of theta (across cell types) (f.i. CD most often increases the gene’s expression, and the theta between the gene’s EAPs and the DAP is most often positive), the effect is labelled consistent (“TRUE”), otherwise not (“FALSE”).

Effects of IBD on gene expression level in intestinal cells

Were obtained from Supplemental Table 3 in Kong et al.28. Kong et al. performed scRNA-Seq on biopsies from terminal ileum (TI) and colon (CO) of 25 healthy controls and 46 CD patients. For active CD patients, biopsies were collected from inflamed as well as non-inflamed regions. Cells were assigned to 65 cell types/states. Differential expression (DE) analysis was conducted by location (TI or CO), cell type, and contrast (inflamed regions in CD patients versus healthy controls, and non-inflamed regions in CD patients versus healthy controls), using MAST86 in either discrete mode (expression rate) or continuous mode (expression level). Supplementary Data 22 (this work) lists, for 556 genes with DAP-matching EAP, the cell types/locations for which CD has a significant effect on gene expression (discrete and/or continuous FDR ≤≤ 0.001; if both FDR ≤≤ 0.001, the best is shown). For each such significant effect, Supplementary Data 22 provides effect (log2 fold change), FDR, cell type, and location. If, for a given gene, the majority sign of the effects (across cell type/locations) corresponds to the majority sign of theta (across cell types) (f.i. CD most often increases the gene’s expression, and the theta between the gene’s EAPs and the DAP is most often positive), the effect is labelled consistent (“TRUE”), otherwise not (“FALSE”).

Effects of IBD on circulating (plasma) protein levels

Were obtained from Supplementary Data 18 in Eldjarn et al.48. Eldjarn et al. analyzed the association with IBD, of concentrations of 2931 circulating proteins (Olink Explore 3072 assay) in ∼~49 K individuals from the UK Biobank (including 900 IBD cases), and of 4,907 circulating proteins (SomaScan v4 assay) in ∼~36 K Icelanders (including 618 IBD cases), using logistic regression. Supplementary Data 22 (this work) lists, for 556 genes with DAP-matching EAP, significant (nominal p-value ≤≤ 0.01) associations between IBD status and plasmatic protein concentrations. For each such significant association, Supplementary Data 22 provides assay, effect (odds ratio), and nominal p-value. If, for a given gene, the direction of the effect(s) corresponds to the majority sign of theta (across cell types) (f.i., CD increases the protein abundance, and the theta between the gene’s EAPs and the DAP is most often positive), the effect is labelled consistent (“TRUE”), otherwise not (“FALSE”).

To identify whether any of the 556 e-genes with IBD-matching EAP were targeted by drugs that are or have been used to treat IBD we consulted the list of IBD drugs that was recently established by Vieujean et al.50.

Identifying non-IBD drugs targeting e-genes with DAP (IBD) matching EAP

To identify whether any of the 556 e-genes with IBD-matching EAP were targeted by drugs that are or have been used to treat diseases other than IBD, we consulted the Open Targets49 database. Open Targets was queried for targeting drugs by e-gene. For each drug, we extracted name, type of drug, mechanism of action, and the most advanced clinical trial by disease. Corresponding data for entrectinib were compiled from references 51–54.
