Design and development of a semi-automated solid target assembly for the indigenous production of medical radioisotopes in the PETrace-800 cyclotron
Authors: Kanchan Kushwaha, Kavindra Pathak, Tejinder P. Sabharwal, Sunil Kamble, Arpit Mitra, Sudeep Sahu, Pravind Maletha, Nand Kishore Prasad, Manish Kumar, Sharmila Banerjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMolecular imaging enables the non-invasive visualization of physiological processes at the cellular and molecular levels and is central to diagnostic nuclear medicine. Among clinically important theranostic radionuclides, 64Cu and 111In are of particular interest due to their favourable decay characteristics and applications in radiopharmaceutical development. Their cyclotron production via the 64Ni(p,n)64Cu and 111Cd(p,n)111In reactions generally require irradiation of enriched solid targets to achieve adequate yields. Although liquid target approaches have been explored, solid targets remain preferable for radiometal production due to higher yields and more efficient utilization of enriched target material.ResultsTo enhance the capability of the two-decade old commercial medical cyclotron (PETtrace 800) at the Radiation Medicine Centre (RMC), originally configured only for liquid and gaseous targets, an indigenously designed and fabricated solid target irradiation assembly was developed and integrated into the Medical Cyclotron Facility (MCF). This work demonstrates the successful routine production of pharmaceutical-grade 64Cu and 111In, achieving yields of 74 MBq µA− 1 h− 1 and 4.88 MBq µA− 1 h− 1, respectively.ConclusionThis development significantly broadens the functional scope of the existing cyclotron infrastructure and establishes a dependable local production pathway for clinically important theranostic radionuclides.

Background

The increasing demand for diverse radiopharmaceuticals in diagnostic and therapeutic nuclear medicine has driven substantial innovation in cyclotron-based radioisotope production. Over the past two decades, the commercial deployment of negative hydrogen ion (H⁻) cyclotrons such as PETtrace 800 cyclotron series (840, 860, 880 and 890 all from GE Healthcare), Cyclone® Kiube (IBA Radiopharma Solutions), TR-19 (Advanced Cyclotron Systems), CYPRIS® HM-20 (Sumitomo Heavy Industries) and Eclipse™ (Siemens) has enabled the routine synthesis of positron-emitting isotopes, especially fluorine-18 (18F), which has long served as the workhorse of positron emission tomography (PET) imaging (Friesel and Antaya 2009; Conti and Eriksson 2016). These systems, typically operating at proton energies between 11–18 MeV and configured for liquid and gaseous targets, have facilitated the consistent production of key PET radioisotopes such as 13N, 15O, 11C, and 18F (Radioisotopes and No 2012).

However, the evolving landscape of molecular imaging, particularly the use of biomolecule-targeted peptides, receptor-specific ligands, and nanobodies, has catalyzed the need for different metallic PET radioisotopes such as 64Cu, 89Zr and 68Ga (Brandt et al. 2018). These radio-metals exhibit favourable coordination chemistry with bifunctional chelators, facilitating their conjugation with a wide range of biologically active molecules for targeted imaging and potential theranostic applications (Sharma and Pandey 2023). To produce these isotopes efficiently and at high activity, solid target irradiation systems capable of operating within the 8–16 MeV proton energy range are essential (Baruta et al. 2022).

While recent cyclotron models offer integrated solid target stations and automated dissolution and purification modules, legacy systems, including many first-generation PETtrace 800 cyclotrons were not originally equipped with such infrastructure. As a result, production of metallic PET radioisotopes in older systems remains limited or non-existent (George et al. 2021). Furthermore, although some metallic PET isotopes can be synthesized using liquid targets, this method suffers from low end-of-bombardment (EOB) and end-of-processing (EOP) yields, along with significant technical drawbacks. The high acidity of irradiated solutions causes corrosion of liquid target filling (LTF) systems and solenoid valves, leading to incomplete transfer, contamination risks, and frequent maintenance (Carmo et al. 2020; Pandey and DeGrado 2021). Although liquid targets offer certain operational advantages, such as ease of automation and rapid turnaround, remote loading and unloading of the target solution and automated separation of the radiometal from the target material within ~ 30–45 min, these benefits are often outweighed by important limitations. These include high-pressure build-up in the target at higher beam currents due to water radiolysis, in-target precipitation of the metal species, and the frequent rupture of the target foil (Pandey and DeGrado 2021).

Worldwide, 64Cu is predominantly produced in cyclotrons via the 64Ni(p,n)64Cu reaction using solid targets, and this approach has been extensively reported in the literature (Alliot, et al. 2011; Avila-Rodriguez, et al. 2007). Most major cyclotron manufacturers have therefore standardized solid target systems (STS) for routine 64Cu production (Fiedler et al. 2018). However, solid target preparation involves several practical challenges, including the time-consuming electrodeposition of enriched 64Ni and detailed morphological characterization of the deposited layer using techniques such as scanning electron microscopy (SEM) or energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (Correia 2022). Although liquid targets have been explored as an alternative to address some of these limitations, their application for 64Cu production remains constrained by relatively lower yields and challenges in optimizing irradiation parameters, which often offset their operational advantages such as automation and faster processing (Correia 2022).

In recent years, solid target irradiation has re-emerged as a robust solution to overcome these limitations. Electroplated or press-fit solid targets have demonstrated significantly improved yields and operational reliability, especially when paired with semi-automated transfer systems that deliver irradiated targets directly to automated chemical processing modules inside shielded hot cells (Lee et al. 2022; Nelson et al. 2020). These advancements not only enhance production efficiency but also reduce radiation exposure and mechanical failure rates.

In this context, the present study aims to bridge the gap between the capabilities of legacy cyclotron systems and modern radiometal production requirements. At our Medical Cyclotron Facility (MCF), PETtrace 800, currently dedicated to the production of 18F via liquid targets, was identified as a candidate for enhancement. To this end, we have indigenously designed, developed, and fabricated a semi-automated solid target irradiation assembly, specifically engineered to fit into the cyclotron's existing but unused liquid target port, without any hardware or mechanical modifications.

The primary goal of this work is to establish a functional, cost-effective platform for the production of clinically relevant radiometals, namely 64Cu and 111In.

The semi-automated nature of the system enables manual loading and unloading of solid targets while integrating automated dissolution and chemical separation modules to minimize radiation exposure and ensure reproducibility. Critical aspects such as heat load distribution during irradiation, target cooling efficiency, and material compatibility were considered during the design phase to ensure both operational safety and machine integrity.

This study highlights the feasibility of retrofitting legacy cyclotrons with indigenous semi-automated solid target systems to expand their utility beyond conventional isotopes. Such upgrades are particularly valuable for institutions in resource-limited settings, enabling the local production of advanced radiopharmaceuticals without the need for full system replacement or extensive infrastructure changes.

Materials

While other parts of the solid target assembly, such as the target base plate (copper disc) and heat sink block, were also fabricated by TPD, and made of electrolytic tough pitch (ETP) copper of purity 99.9%. The solid target assembly needs to be attached to the existing cooling flange, which facilitates the flow of coolant (chilled water and helium gas) through the assembly. The quick-coupling plug, purchased from Staubli Tech Systems, India, enables this attachment. Methods

Medical cyclotron

The H− Cyclotron (PETtrace 800) is an unshielded fixed-energy cyclotron with an incident proton energy of 16.5 MeV. The beam current could be varied between 1 µA–75 µA. It has six beam exit ports with a beam exit valve at each of the ports. During irradiation, the beam exit valve of the used port was opened, and the proton beam came out of the cyclotron tank and was incident on the target (PETtrace Tracer Production System Service Manual—Accelerator). Out of six beam exit ports, three beam exit ports (1, 2, 4) were utilized for the routine production of 18F.

The prototype design of the solid target assembly was carried out considering the compatibility with the present medical cyclotron and the requirements for the production of 64Cu and 111In.

Among the remaining three beam exit ports, port number 3 was considered for mounting the indigenously developed solid target assembly. Target base plate:

The target base plate (a gold-plated copper disc) is a small disc of 25 mm diameter and 2 mm thickness that provides mechanical support for the thin layer of target material. The target material was electroplated at the centre of one side of the disc, forming a circular spot of 8 mm diameter.

An 8 mm target spot size was selected in accordance with the collimator size, which ensures that only an 8 mm diameter proton beam exits the vacuum tank and impinges on the target. However, beam profile analysis performed using the paper burn test indicated that the actual beam size was approximately 10 mm. This suggests that nearly 60% of the incident proton beam was contained within the 8 mm diameter region.

k) Functioning of the solid target assembly:

k) Functioning of the solid target assembly:

Functioning of the solid target assembly:

During irradiation, the proton beam of 16.5 MeV is first incident on the Havar foil. It then travels to the attenuation disc, where its energy gets attenuated. The attenuated proton beam is made incident on the target material supported by the target base plate, and is completely attenuated in the target base plate. The radioisotope production takes place in this layer of target material. After irradiation, the target base plate is removed, and the radioactive target layer is leached out by radiochemical processing. During irradiation, the interlock system assures machine and personal safety, and the cyclotron vault provides adequate radiation shielding.

Thermal analysis for proposed design

The thermal analysis of the solid target assembly with the existing cooling system was carried out to ensure the efficient heat dissipation during irradiation of the solid target assembly, at 25 µA proton current. The proposed design of the solid target assembly shown in Fig. 1 was made with an aim to achieve efficient heat dissipation with the existing cooling system.

Heat load on the solid target:

Heat load on the solid target:

Heat load on the solid target:

When the proton beam hits the target, it deposits heat in different parts of the solid target due to its energy attenuation. This heat has to be dissipated in order to avoid any deformation or

melting of the parts. The existing cooling system of the PETtrace-800 used during irradiation of the dummy target has been used in the present case for the irradiation of the solid target assembly. The Helium gas flows only between the Havar foil and the attenuation disc with a flow rate of 5 L/min, and chilled water (20 °C) flows behind the heat sink block through the fins with a flow rate of 2 L/min. There is no flow of helium gas and water between the attenuation disc and the target.

The heat load on the attenuation disc and the target base plate has been estimated for a proton current of 25 µA. The modes by which heat transfer takes place in the present case are conduction and convection.

Evaluation of steady state temperature during irradiation at 25 μA proton current (Chapman 2016)

When the proton beam hits the solid target, the temperature in different parts starts increasing, thereby resulting in a temperature gradient between the coolant and different parts of the solid target, which, in turn, facilitates the heat transfer. The temperatures keep on increasing till a steady state is achieved, and the rates of heat deposition and that of heat removal become equal. At the steady state, the temperature of the different parts becomes independent of time. These steady-state temperatures in the different parts must be much below the respective melting points. The thermal analysis has been carried out in order to evaluate the steady state temperatures in different parts of the solid target during irradiation, while using a current of 25 µA.

There is no direct water cooling provided to the attenuation disc. Both the attenuation disc and the target base plate containing the target material remain in thermal contact with the heat sink block. Heat generated in the attenuation disc and the target base plate is first transferred to the heat sink block by conduction and is subsequently removed by chilled water through convection. Therefore, both conduction and convection modes of heat transfer were considered in the thermal analysis.

Target preparation for the production of 64Cu and 111In

Target preparation for 64Cu

Electroplating of enriched 64Ni in gold plated (thickness of gold electrodeposition: 1.5E-02 mm) copper disc {dimension: 25 mm (D) × 2 mm (T)} was carried out using an indigenously developed electrolytic cell containing an alkaline electrolytic bath. In brief, the electroplating vessel was a hollow casted Poly methyl methacrylate (PMMA) cylinder (Dimension: 100 mm Diameter × 120 mm Height × 10 mm Thick) with a diameter reduced to 6 cm at the bottom. A Perspex disc (dimension:100 mm Diameter × 10 mm Thick) was used as the lid for the electroplating vessel with three holes to insert the anode (platinum-coated titanium rod – 10 mm Diameter × 145 mm Length), a stirrer rod and a funnel. The stepper motor is mounted at the top of the electroplating vessel, to which the perspex rod is attached for stirring the electrolyte. The speed and the direction of the stepper motor and, consequently, the stirrer, can be programmed. A circular opening of 8 mm diameter at the bottom of the vessel was made to mount the cathode (gold-plated copper disc—25 mm Diameter × 2 mm Thick). The gold-plated copper disc was fitted in such a way that the 8 mm opening was at the centre of the disc and exposed to the electrolytic solution.

The alkaline electrolytic bath containing 500 mg of enriched metallic 64Ni (in the form of powder) was dissolved in 20 mL of 2.3N HNO3. To this solution, NH4OH/NH4Cl buffer (~ 3.2 g ultrapure NH4Cl dissolved in 17 mL ultrapure water and pH is adjusted to 9.2—9.3 using ~ 11 mL of concentrated ultrapure NH4OH) was added to maintain the pH of the electrolytic bath at ~ 9.3. Non-ionic surfactant (100 µL Sodium n-Dodecyl Sulphate, concentration: 10 g/L) was added to the electrolyte bath. Electroplating was carried out using constant current mode (CCE) at 65 mA current (voltage: 4 V) for 5 h. The stirrer rotation speed was set at 150 rpm. During the process, its rotation direction was reversed after each 8 s (Kushwaha and A.M., N. Yuvaraj, K. Pathak, S.R. Kamble, S.R. Patankar, D.A. Dharmik, T. Singh, N.K. Prasad, M. Kumar, A.T. Chaudhari, J. Varshney, J. Haider, P.V. Bhagwat, R.C. Hubli and M.G.R. Rajan. 2014).

Target preparation for 111In

Natural Cd was electrodeposited on a copper disc {dimension: 25 mm (D) × 2 mm (T)} in a non-cyanide electrolytic bath, using the electrochemical deposition method as per our earlier reported methodology (Kushwaha et al. 2021). The typical electroplating process was carried out by maintaining the temperature between 0 to 2 °C, while the deposition potential was maintained at -0.8 V (Kushwaha et al. 2021). The morphological characterization of the electrodeposited enriched 64Ni and natural Cd layer was carried out by using the AFM technique, while the adhesion of the electrodeposited 64Ni and natural Cd layer on the target base plate was evaluated by thermal shock test.

Irradiation parameters for production of 64Cu and 111In

Copper-64

Copper-64 was produced via 64Ni(p,n)64Cu nuclear reaction route. The electroplated enriched 64Ni on a copper disc was irradiated with a proton beam of 12 MeV for 0.5 h with a 10 µA beam current. The proton beam was attenuated from 16 to 12 MeV by the attenuation disc of the indigenous solid target assembly unit. The 12 MeV proton beam from the attenuation disc becomes incident on the gold-plated copper disc, having an electroplated layer of enriched 64Ni (target) (Kushwaha and P.M., A. Mitra, R. Kashid, S. Kamble, J. Babu, K. Pathak, N.K. Prasad, S. Banerjee. Production of 64Cu using indigenously developed solid target assembly in Medical Cyclotron Facility in Radiation Medicine Centre. in EJNMMI Radiopharmacy and Chemistry 2018).

Indium-111

The 111In was produced via 111Cd(p,n)111In nuclear reaction route. The electroplated natural Cd on a copper disc was irradiated with a proton beam of 14 MeV for 1 h with a 5 µA beam current. The proton beam was attenuated from 16 to 14 MeV by the attenuation disc of the indigenous solid target assembly unit. The 14 MeV proton beam from the attenuation disc becomes incident on a gold-plated copper disc with an electroplated layer of natural Cd target (Kushwaha et al. 2021).

Radiochemical separation of 64Cu and 111In from the target matrix

After irradiation, a small quantity of the target material gets converted in to the required radioisotope, which has to be separated from its target material. This separation was carried out by a few steps of radiochemical purification. The 64Cu and 111In were both purified using Ion exchange column chromatography (IECC) method, using different resins.

64Cu

The radiochemical separation and purification of 64Cu were carried out using AG® 1-X8 resin packed in column of dimension 4 cm (length) × 1 cm (diameter). It was preconditioned with 25 mL of 6.0 N HCl, before use. The irradiated target base plate was dissolved in 25 mL 6N HCl. The solution was evaporated to complete dryness and then 3.0 mL of 6N HCl was added. This solution containing [64Cu]Cu2+and [64Ni]Ni2+was loaded on the preconditioned column containing AG® 1-X8 resin. The removal of [64Ni]Ni2+ was achieved by washing the column with 25 mL of 6N HCl, while the trapped [64Cu]Cu2+ was eluted from the resin with 10 mL 0.5N HCl as [64Cu]CuCl2 (Avila-Rodriguez, et al. 2007; Matarrese et al. 2010).

111In

The radiochemical separation and purification of 111In was carried out by employing two different ion exchange resins, viz. (i) acidic alumina oxide and (ii) Dowex-50W-X-4 (Kushwaha et al. 2021). The irradiated target was dissolved in ultrapure 8 M HCl (15 mL). Further, the dissolved target solution was evaporated to dryness, and the residue was reconstituted with.

5 mL of 0.001N HCl. This 0.001 M HCl solution containing 111In was loaded onto a 1.5 gof acidic alumina oxide (Al2O3) column. The acidic Al2O3 column was washed with 0.001N HCl to remove bulk Cd3+ and Cu2+, while the trapped [111In]In3+ was eluted using 15 mL with concentrated HBr (⁓ 8.6 M). The eluted [111In]InBr4− was evaporated to dryness, and the residue was again reconstituted with concentrated HBr (⁓ 8.6 M). The [111In]InBr4− was further loaded in a Dowex-50W-X-4 resin column (resin packed in a polypropylene barrel, dimension: 10 mmdiameter × 25 mmheight). The Dowex-50W-X-4 resin column was further washed with concentrated HBr (⁓8.6 M) to remove traces of Cd3+ and Cu2+. The trapped [111In]In3+ in Dowex-50W-X-4 resin was eluted with 5 mL of concentrated HCl (⁓9.4 M) and evaporated to dryness. The residue was again reconstituted with 2 mL of concentrated HCl (⁓9.4 M) and was evaporated to dryness. This process was repeated for two times. Finally, the [111In]InCl3 was reconstituted with 0.01 M HCl (Kushwaha et al. 2021).

Quality control studies of the produced [64Cu]CuCl2 and [111In]InCl3

The radiochemical purity (RCP) of [64Cu]CuCl2 and [111In]InCl3 was evaluated by thin layer chromatography (TLC, 60Ao silica gel plastic TLC plates) using ethyl acetate (100%) and 0.1 M sodium citrate buffer, pH-5.0 as eluent, respectively.

Post 22.5 h and 38 h of irradiation, RNP for [64Cu]CuCl2 and [111In]InCl3 were determined by HPGe coupled to 64 k MCA, respectively. In-vitro stability of the [64Cu]CuCl2 and [111In]InCl3 was also evaluated. The pH of [64Cu]CuCl2 was adjusted to ~ 5.0 by using sterile pyrogen-free 0.5 M CH3COONa buffer and saline, and its RCP was determined by radio-TLC, at 48 h post radiochemical purification, on storage at room temperature (25 °C–28 °C).

Radiolabelling and quality control studies of [111In]In-Pentetreotide

The preparation of [111In]In-Pentetreotide (⁓7.40 MBq) was carried out using in-house produced pharmaceutical grade [111In]InCl3 (⁓9.25 MBq in 2.0 mL of 0.01N HCl), in sodium citrate buffer containing 30 µg of pentetreotide (1 µg/µL, 21.51 nmoles) and further incubated at room temperature (25 °C) for 0.5 h at pH ~ 4.0. The reaction mixture was further purified using pre-conditioned plus C18 SepPak cartridges (sorbent weight: 360 mg) to remove unlabeled [111In]In3+. The final product, [111In]In-Pentetreotide, was eluted from the SPE column with 800 µL of 100% ethanol. The radiolabelled preparation {[111In]In-Pentetreotide} was stabilized with gentisic acid (concentration: 270 µg/MBq) and further diluted with 10 mL of sterile, pyrogen-free saline, resulting in a radioactive concentration (RAC) of ~ 0.70 MBq/mL, while the ethanol content was < 10% v/v (Kushwaha et al. 2021).

The RCP for [111In]In-Pentetreotide was evaluated by radio-HPLC using RP18 column, (gradient mode: 0–5 min at 100% water, 5–25 min at 100−70% water, 25–35 min at 70% water, 35–40 min at 70—0% water, 40–45 min at 0− 100% water) with eluent as 0.1% TFA in water and acetonitrile at flow rate of 1 mL/minute, while UV was set at 220 nm.

The EL for [111In]In-Pentetreotide was quantified by gel-clot BET assay, while sterility test was carried out by direct inoculation. The In-vitro saline stability of [111In]In-Pentetreotide was also assessed by radio-HPLC on storage at − 20 °C up to 72 h post radiolabelling. The residual ethanol levels in [111In]In-Pentetreotide were detected by gas chromatography using a standardized procedure, with helium as carrier gas and polyethylene glycol capillary cartridge {Dimension: 25 mm (L) × 0.32 mm (ID), Thickness: 0.5 µm}. The cartridge was maintained at a temperature of 70 °C during operation.

After ensuring the heat removal capacity, the design of the solid target assembly, as shown in Fig. 1, was found to be appropriate. The indigenously developed assembled solid target irradiation unit was mounted on the beam exit port and was also attached with the existing four sockets of the cooling flange at port 3 of the PETtrace 800. The existing cooling flange of port 3 was chosen, such that the undisrupted flow of coolant inside the solid target irradiation unit for heat dissipation could be maintained at a flow rate of 2 L/min and 5 L/min for chilled water and helium gas, respectively. Testing of the solid target assembly

The design of the solid target assembly was evaluated broadly based on two types of testing, non-radioactive (without irradiation) or cold testing and radioactive (with irradiation) or hot testing. In cold testing, parameters like (i) stability of pressure in vacuum tank, (ii) leakage and flow rate of coolant, (iii) weight analysis of the solid target assembly and (iv) automated removal of irradiated target were considered, while for radioactive testing, two critical parameters were considered viz. (i) beam profile analysis and (ii) irradiation testing.

After mounting the solid target assembly, the vacuum tank pressure initially fluctuated between 1.2 × 10–7 mbar and 2.2 × 10–2 mbar for approximately 30 s before stabilizing at 1.3 × 10–7 mbar. Leakage and flow rate of coolant

After achieving the stability in vacuum, Helium gas and chilled water were allowed to flow inside the indigenously fabricated solid target assembly for 0.5 h, and the probability of any leakage was observed. Leakage of chilled water and helium gas was not observed during 0.5 h of operation. Therefore, the fabrication and assembly of the solid target were efficient enough to avoid any leakage. Cyclotron & irradiation parameters for production of 64Cu and 111In

Copper-64

The various routes for producing 64Cu are 64Ni(p,n)64Cu, 66Zn(d,α)64Cu, 68Zn(p,αn)64Cu, 64Ni(d,2n)64Cu, 63Cu(n,γ)64Cu, 64Zn(n,p)64Cu. However, the most preferred route for production of 64Cu is 64Ni(p,n)64Cu, due to its high yield, high specific activity and low energy requirement (Niccoli Asabella, et al. 2014). Several studies have been reported on the design of a solid target for producing 64Cu (Jauregui-Osoro et al. 2021; Isolan et al. 2022). However, for the production of 64Cu via nuclear reaction 64Ni(p,n)64Cu, the enriched 64Ni target was bombarded with a proton beam having an incident energy 12 MeV. The 16.5 MeV proton beam of the PETtrace 800 was attenuated to 12 MeV by an attenuation disc, which could be further attenuated to 9 MeV by a 0.12 mm thick electroplated enriched nickel on the gold-plated copper disc (Kushwaha 2022). However, in our methodology, we could able to achieve ⁓0.07 mm thick electroplated enriched nickel on gold plated copper disc, hence on irradiating ⁓0.07 mm thick enriched nickel target with 12 MeV proton beam at 10 µA beam current for 0.5 h, the experimentally measured yield for 64Cu was found to be 74.36 ± 0.4 MBqµA−1 h−1 (n = 6) at the end of bombardment (EOB).

The reported experimental production yield of 140 MBq·µA− 1·h− 1 (Avila-Rodriguez, et al. 2007) is higher than the yield obtained in the present study. The lower yield observed in our work can be attributed to the beam size incident on the target, which was approximately 10 mm, as determined from the beam profile analysis (Fig. 7). Since the enriched 64Ni was electroplated over a circular area of 8 mm diameter, nearly 40% of the proton beam did not impinge on the target surface.

Taking this ~ 40% beam loss into account, the expected yield in our study would correspond to approximately 60% of the reported yield of 140 MBq·µA− 1·h− 1 (Avila-Rodriguez, et al. 2007), which is equivalent to about 84 MBq·µA− 1·h− 1. The experimentally observed yield of 74.36 ± 0.4 MBq·µA− 1·h− 1 obtained in the present study is therefore in reasonable agreement with this estimated value.

The loss of beam utilization could potentially be minimized either by increasing the target deposition area or by reducing the collimator aperture size. Radiochemical purification & quality control studies of [64Cu]CuCl2 and [111In]InCl3

Radiochemical purification of [64Cu]CuCl2

The radiochemical purification of [64Cu]CuCl2 was carried post 12 h of irradiation. Post irradiated, enriched 64Ni target (thickness: ~ 0.07 mm) was decayed for 12 h inside the cyclotron vault. Cooling of the 64Ni target for 12 h, prior to radiochemical purification was necessary to eliminate possible prominent radionuclide impurities namely 61Co (T1/2: 1.65 h) and 63Zn (T1/2: 38.47 min) as expected from the theoretical calculation.

At the end of bombardment (EOB), the produced 64Cu activity was 0.37 ± 0.07 GBq (n = 6). After a decay period of 12 h, the residual activity was 0.19 ± 0.03 GBq (n = 6). Upon completion of the radiochemical purification, the activity of the pharmaceutical-grade [64Cu]CuCl2 obtained at the end of processing (EOP) was 0.14 ± 0.02 GBq (n = 6). The non–decay-corrected radiochemical yield (RCY) was 78.15 ± 0.45% (n = 6), calculated based on the activities measured after 12 h decay and at EOP.

Discussion

Overall, the results demonstrate that the development and implementation of this indigenous solid target irradiation unit provide a practical and effective strategy for expanding the functionality of existing PETtrace 800 cyclotrons, without the need for procuring new systems equipped with proprietary solid-target platforms such as Nirta® STS, ALCEO 4.0™ PTS, PETtrace™ automated STS, HCSTS or QuickCurie™. In contrast to most commercial solid target systems that require dedicated hardware integration, the present approach demonstrates that a compact and adaptable solid-target platform can be implemented by utilizing an unused liquid target port of the cyclotron without mechanical modification to the existing system. This feature allows facilities operating PETtrace 800 cyclotrons to leverage their existing infrastructure more efficiently for PET/SPECT radiometal production, while potentially reducing the overall cost associated with the production of PET radiometals and their corresponding radiopharmaceuticals. The original design of the PETtrace 800 cyclotron was intended for the production of medically useful radioisotopes using liquid or gaseous targets. Consequently, automated transfer provisions, such as the Liquid Target Facility (LTF), were available only for the transport of radioisotopes produced from liquid targets to the radiochemical processing hot cell. Typically, solid targets after irradiation are transported from the irradiation station to the processing hot cell using target carrier systems such as automated pneumatic transfer (rabbit) systems, mechanical tracks, or miniature chain conveyors (Gelbart and Stevenson 1998).

However, due to limitations in the existing infrastructure, the implementation of such systems was not feasible at our facility. Therefore, an alternative pneumatically operated mechanism was designed, installed and tested successfully, as described in Sect. “Proposed design of solid target assembly”(e) and demonstrated in Fig. 6.

The radiological and operational safety aspects of the Medical Cyclotron Facility (MCF) at our institute are governed by the Unit Level Safety Committee for Particle Accelerators (ULSC-PA) under the BARC Safety Council (BSC). The ULSC-PA, operating under the BSC, is responsible for overseeing the radiological and operational safety aspects of the MCF. The facility was originally commissioned to install and operate a commercial 16.5 MeV medical cyclotron (PETtrace 800) for the routine production of medically important positron-emitting radioisotopes, 18F, 13N, 11C, and 15O, using liquid and gaseous targets at proton beam currents up to 75 µA. Following over 16 years of successful operation at 55–65 µA, the licensing authority granted permission to modify the targetry system of the PETtrace 800, enabling the production of medically relevant metallic PET/SPECT radioisotopes 111In, 64Cu, and 89Zr, using an indigenously developed solid target irradiation assembly, within the existing infrastructure, including the cyclotron vault and the console room. Due to the provision of manual transfer of irradiated target system, the maximum yields of 64Cu and 111In production were restricted to 2.22 GBq and 0.74 GBq, respectively. These limits were maintained to ensure continued adherence to radiation and operational safety requirements, particularly since no infrastructural modifications were made to the existing facility.

At the Medical Cyclotron Facility (MCF), radiochemical processing of irradiated targets including separation and purification as well as peptide radiolabelling with cyclotron-produced, clinically relevant radioisotopes and subsequent quality control/characterization of the radiopharmaceuticals, are performed in a dedicated radiochemistry and radiopharmaceutical production laboratory. This facility, equipped with a fume hood incorporating temporary lead shielding, is situated outside the MCF premises.

Radiological safety in these laboratories is governed by the Unit Level Safety Committee for Radiological Operations (ULSC-RO) under the BARC Safety Council (BSC). The ULSC-RO has authorized manual radiochemical separation and purification of irradiated targets (e.g., 64Ni, 111Cd, 89Y, 68Zn) with a maximum permissible dose limit of 100 µSv. In accordance with these regulatory provisions, all procedures were optimized to minimize radiation exposure during both chemical separation and radiolabelling. Accordingly, irradiations of enriched 64Ni (~ 0.07 mm thickness) and natural 111Cd (~ 0.2 µm thickness) targets were conducted at relatively low beam currents, yielding limited amounts of radioactivity sufficient for standardization of separation and radiolabelling protocols, as well as for ensuring pharmaceutical-grade production of 64Cu and 111In. Notwithstanding these constraints, the production yields of 64Cu and 111In can be scaled up to approximately 2.22 GBq and 0.74 GBq, respectively, with the implementation of a dedicated automated synthesis module.

With the growing significance of PET radiometals such as 64Cu, 68Ga, and 89Zr, there is an increasing demand for their production using small to medium energy (≤ 20 MeV) commercial biomedical cyclotrons. In recent years, several manufacturers of such cyclotrons have developed or are in the process of developing solid target irradiation systems for the routine production of these PET radiometals. Some notable examples include, (i) the IBA Nirta® Solid Target Station (STS) for Cyclone® Kiube or Cyclone® 18/9, (ii) the Comecer ALCEO 4.0™ PTS irradiation unit designed for PETtrace 800 series (especially for PETtrace 860 and 880 of GE Healthcare), Cyclone® 18/9 (IBA Radiopharma) and TR-19 (Advanced Cyclotron Systems) (iii) PETtrace™ prototype automated solid target system for PETtrace 860 and 880 (GE Healthcare), (iv) the niobium vessel-based “in-situ production of radiometals” for CYPRIS HM-12 (Sumitomo Heavy Industries), (v) High Current Solid Target Station (HCSTS from Advanced Cyclotron Systems) and (vi) the IBA COSTIS target station for Cyclone 18/9 (Dellepiane et al. 2023; Ometáková et al. 2012; Asp, et al. 2020; Gomez, et al. 2022; Svedjehed, et al. 2022).

Focusing on the feasibility for installing or integrating a solid target station (STS), especially on the GE PETtrace 800 series (PETtrace 840, 860 880 and 890) of cyclotron (proton energy < 20.

MeV) and further adapting those integrated systems for producing radiometals like 99mTc, 64Cu, 68 Ga and 89Zr has been reported in recent years. Among the reported studies, production of 99mTc has been carried out using GE PETtrace 880 with an indigenously developed solid target station (STS) (Fiedler et al. 2018; Lin, et al. 2016). Apart from GE PETtrace 880, Comecer ALCEO 4.0™ PTS has been integrated with GE PETtrace 860 for the production of 68 Ga (Jagodziński, et al. 2025). Recently, GE Healthcare has developed a PETtrace™ prototype automated solid target system, which was integrated with PETtrace 880 and MINItracre (PETtrace 700) and has been utilized for producing 68 Ga (Svedjehed, et al. 2022; Pärnaste, et al. 2023). Presently, of all the commercial developed STS, Comecer ALCEO 4.0™ PTS has the versatility to integrate with various cyclotrons from different manufacturers (PETtrace 800 series of GE Healthcare, Cyclone® 18/9 IBA Radiopharma, TR-19 of Advanced Cyclotron Systems), for producing radiometals (Isolan et al. 2022). Production of 64Cu and 89Zr has been successfully carried out using Comecer ALCEO 4.0™ PTS integrated with Cyclone 18/9 (IBA Radiopharma) and TR-19 (Advanced Cyclotron Systems), respectively (Matarrese et al. 2010; Cocioabă, et al. 2025).

Recent literature has demonstrated the successful production of 64Cu, 68Ga, and 89Zr using indigenously developed, customized solid target irradiation units integrated into older cyclotron models such as Cyclone 18/9 (18 MeV, IBA), TR-19 (19 MeV, Advanced Cyclotron Systems Inc.) and CYPRIS® HM-20 (20 MeV, Sumitomo Heavy Industries) (George et al. 2021; Jeffery, et al. 2012; Williamson 2010). Each of these reported assemblies exhibited distinct design features, as the configuration of a solid target irradiation system primarily depends on several cyclotron-specific parameters, including: (i) the beam exit port design of the existing liquid or gaseous target, (ii) the existing cooling system and its flow rate, (iii) the vacuum system of the cyclotron, and (iv) the in-built interlock parameters (Williamson 2010).

However, major commercial cyclotron manufacturers such as GE Healthcare, IBA Radiopharma Solutions, Advanced Cyclotron Systems, Sumitomo Heavy Industries and Siemens Healthineers have developed their respective solid target systems (STS), including PETtrace™ prototype automated STS, ALCEO 4.0™ PTS, Nirta® STS, HCSTS and Solid Target System, for the irradiation of targets for radiometal production. In most cases, these commercial STS platforms are designed to be compatible only with the cyclotron systems of the respective manufacturers. Consequently, the components of these STS units cannot readily be modified or can only be adapted with limited modification for integration with cyclotrons produced by other manufacturers.

This limitation imposes significant constraints on the utilization of older commercial biomedical cyclotrons particularly systems such as the PETtrace 800 (proton energy: 16.5 MeV) from GE Healthcare for the production of several clinically important radiometals, including 99mTc, 64Cu, 68Ga, 89Zr and 111In. As a result, such older small- to medium-energy cyclotrons, which lack dedicated solid target irradiation facilities, often remain underutilized for routine PET/SPECT radiometal production. This situation was also encountered at our facility, where the PETtrace 800 cyclotron did not have an integrated STS for solid target irradiation.

To the best of our knowledge, there are no previous reports describing the integration of either commercial or indigenously developed STS with the PETtrace 800 cyclotron and its subsequent application for the production of PET/SPECT radiometals. Notably, commercial STS platforms such as ALCEO 4.0™ PTS and PETtrace™ prototype automated STS have primarily been implemented in more advanced versions of the PETtrace series, particularly PETtrace 860 and PETtrace 880, for radiometal production.

In the present work, we demonstrate the successful utilization of a PETtrace 800 cyclotron for radiometal production through the integration of an indigenously developed solid target system at an unused existing liquid target port. The developed system is cost-effective and has been customized to meet user’s requirements, radiation safety considerations and ease of maintenance.

In addition, the solid target design incorporates a provision for attenuation of the proton beam to the desired energy using attenuation discs of varying thickness. The target disc can be loaded simply by pressing it into the target slot, while the removal of the irradiated target disc is performed semi-automatically along the horizontal direction. This design offers a relatively simple loading and unloading mechanism compared with many conventional solid target assemblies. The simplified configuration minimizes the risk of accidental displacement of the target disc during irradiation as well as the possibility of the irradiated disc getting stuck during removal.

Importantly, the existing liquid target port, cooling system and interlock system of the cyclotron were utilized without any modification, thereby ensuring that routine radionuclide production operations remain unaffected.

The PETtrace 800 at our facility is primarily dedicated to the routine production of approximately 6 Ci of 18F per day for the radiosynthesis of [18F]FDG, [18F]NaF and [18F]FET, typically carried out in three production batches. Under such intensive routine production conditions, any mechanical modification to the unused liquid target port could potentially interfere with the regular production schedule of 18F-labelled radiopharmaceuticals. Therefore, modifications to the existing cyclotron infrastructure for integrating an indigenous solid target system (STS) were not considered feasible. In addition, strict adherence to radiological and mechanical safety requirements of the cyclotron system was essential.

Consequently, the development and integration of the indigenous STS posed several technical and operational challenges, particularly in ensuring compatibility with the existing cyclotron architecture while avoiding any disturbance to routine radionuclide production.

In this context, the present work describes the design and successful implementation of an indigenous solid target irradiation unit that can be integrated with the unused liquid target port (Port No. 3) of the PETtrace 800 cyclotron without requiring any mechanical modification or intervention to the existing facility. The system was specifically engineered to utilize the available infrastructure while maintaining operational safety and reliability. The developed setup enabled consistent and reliable routine production of 64Cu and 111In, while studies aimed at extending its application for the production of 89Zr are currently in progress.

Conclusion

A compact solid target irradiation unit was indigenously designed, fabricated and successfully integrated with the existing PETtrace 800 medical cyclotron for the production of metallic PET radioisotopes. Using this system, successful irradiations were performed for the production of 64Cu and 111In alongside routine 18F production, without interrupting the ongoing synthesis of 18F-labelled radiopharmaceuticals such as [18F]FDG, [18F]NaF and [18F]FET. The indigenously developed unit significantly enhanced the operational capability of the PETtrace 800 by enabling the production of clinically relevant quantities of pharmaceutical-grade [64Cu]CuCl₂ and [111In]InCl₃ within the existing facility infrastructure. The radiochemical purity and overall quality of the produced radionuclides indicate their suitability for subsequent radiopharmaceutical formulation and potential clinical application.

Overall, the results demonstrate that the development and implementation of this indigenous solid target irradiation unit provide a practical and effective strategy for expanding the functionality of existing PETtrace 800 cyclotrons, without the need for procuring new systems equipped with proprietary solid-target platforms such as Nirta® STS, ALCEO 4.0™ PTS, PETtrace™ automated STS, HCSTS or QuickCurie™. In contrast to most commercial solid target systems that require dedicated hardware integration, the present approach demonstrates that a compact and adaptable solid-target platform can be implemented by utilizing an unused liquid target port of the cyclotron without mechanical modification to the existing system. This feature allows facilities operating PETtrace 800 cyclotrons to leverage their existing infrastructure more efficiently for PET/SPECT radiometal production, while potentially reducing the overall cost associated with the production of PET radiometals and their corresponding radiopharmaceuticals.

Furthermore, the design principles and operational framework of the present solid-target irradiation platform can be readily extended for the routine production of other medically important radionuclides, such as 68Ga and 89Zr, thereby further broadening its applicability in diagnostic and theranostic nuclear medicine. In this context, the approach described in this work provides a scalable and cost-effective pathway for improving the accessibility of emerging radiometals and their associated radiopharmaceuticals, particularly in facilities equipped with existing medium-energy biomedical cyclotrons, especially the PETtrace 800.
