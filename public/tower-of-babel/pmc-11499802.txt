Perioperative mortality of caesarean section in North-West Ethiopia: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Amanuel Sisay Endeshaw, Eyaya Misgan Asress, Habtu Tsehayu Bayu, Dereje Gashaw Andargie, Misganew Terefe Molla, Eshetu Tesfaye Dejen, Fantahun Tarekegn Kumie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to assess 7-day perioperative maternal mortality and risk factors at Tibebe Ghion Specialized Hospital.DesignA single-centre prospective cohort study.SettingA tertiary hospital in North-West Ethiopia, 01 June 2019 to 30 June 2021.ParticipantsA total of 979 pregnant mothers who underwent caesarean section were enrolled in this study. BackgroundIntroduction

The Sustainable Development Goals’ target is to reduce the global maternal mortality ratio (MMR) to less than 70 maternal deaths per 100 000 live births by 2030.1 However, every year, 300 000 women die during childbirth, and MMRs are significantly higher in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs).2 The number of maternal mortalities in African countries remains unacceptably high. Sub-Saharan Africa and southern Asia account for 86% of all maternal deaths.3 4 Many of these deaths might have been prevented if women had had access to safe surgery, such as caesarean delivery.5

Caesarean section is the most common obstetric surgical procedure. Its global rates (including emergency and elective) range from 5% to 20%, and the rates continue to rise in developed and developing countries.6 A report between 1990 and 2014 shows the global average caesarean section rate increased from 12.4% to 18.6%, rising at an average rate of 4.4% per year. However, the lowest rates were found in Africa (7.3%).7 Since caesarean section rates higher than 15% were not associated with reductions in maternal and newborn morbidity and mortality rates,7 8 the rate should be kept under 15%.9 Caesarean section is associated with complications such as increased blood loss, adjacent organ damage and increased risk of thromboembolism.10

Improved access to emergency caesarean section is associated with decreased maternal deaths in low-income countries where there is inadequate surgical capacity.11 Despite its indisputable importance, this procedure is also associated with a higher risk of maternal mortality as compared with vaginal deliveries.12 13 Maternal mortality after caesarean section is three times higher than vaginal deliveries.14 A systematic review shows that maternal deaths and perinatal deaths following caesarean sections are disproportionately high in LMICs, which account for 7.6 per 1000 procedures, with the highest burden in sub-Saharan Africa.15 Another piece of evidence illustrates that maternal mortality after caesarean delivery in Africa was 5.43 per 1000 operations, making African mothers 50 times more likely to die after caesarean delivery than mothers in high-income countries.16 Furthermore, according to WHO, maternal deaths following caesarean sections in LMICs are 100 times higher than in high-income countries.17

Worldwide, the leading causes of maternal death are still haemorrhage, hypertension, sepsis, obstructed labour and unsafe abortions, while indirect causes are gaining in importance in developed countries.18 Caesarean delivery provision in LMICs is also hampered by a lack of a suitable anaesthetic and surgical workforce and the availability of oxygen, anaesthesia and blood banks.15 19 20

Moreover, women undergoing emergency caesarean section in LMICs were twice as likely to die than those delivered by elective caesarean section, and when the caesarean section was performed in advanced labour at full dilation of the cervix (second stage).16 A third of all deaths following caesarean section were attributed to postpartum haemorrhage (32%), 19% to pre-eclampsia, 22% to sepsis and 14% to anaesthesia-related causes.17 21 22

According to the Ethiopian Demographic Health Survey conducted in 2016, the MMR in Ethiopia was 412 deaths per 100 000 live births indicating the country remains as one of sub-Saharan countries with a higher maternal mortality.23 The Ethiopian government has implemented several interventions such as improving access to caesarean section to reduce high number of maternal deaths.24 However, the national prevalence of caesarean section in Ethiopia is still low and has been found to be 1.96% and 5.44% in two recent Ethiopian Demographic Health Surveys conducted in 2016 and 2019, respectively.25 26 Despite the trends of caesarean section in the Ethiopian population appearing to be increasing,27 few studies have tried to document mortality after caesarean section. Therefore, this study aimed to assess 7-day perioperative maternal mortality and risk factors using a prospectively collected data set in North-West Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design

A hospital-based prospective cohort study was conducted from 01 June 2019 to 30 June 2021. This study was reported in line with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology checklist.

Study setting

This study was conducted at Tibebe Ghion Specialized Hospital (TGSH), a tertiary-level hospital in Bahir Dar City, North-West Ethiopia, affiliated with Bahir Dar University. It serves a wide range of patient catchment areas, including all Amhara regional state zones, the nearby zones of Oromia and the Benishangul-Gumuz regional state.

The hospital, located 508 km from Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia, has more than 500 beds and two dedicated operation rooms for caesarean section and other obstetrics procedures. The Department of Obstetrics and Gynaecology leads the care for pregnant mothers. The team for obstetrics care includes caesarean section procedure composed of senior obstetricians and gynaecologists, midwives and anaesthetists. Partograph and cardiotocography monitors are available to assess fetomaternal well-being. Routine laboratory and imaging facilities are also available for all elective and emergency caesarean sections.

Study population

All pregnant women who underwent caesarean section at TGSH during the study period were eligible for inclusion. Mothers without cellphones and incorrect information (such as wrong phone numbers) for follow-up were excluded from the study.

Variables

Perioperative mortality (died/alive) measured from intraoperative time to 7 days after caesarean section was the outcome variable for this study. Age, district, maternal comorbidity, WHO surgical safety checklist, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status,28 anaesthesia type, length of surgery, blood loss and blood transfusion were the explanatory variables considered to affect 7-day perioperative maternal death.

Data collection tool, procedure and quality control

A data collection tool previously used to monitor anaesthesia care delivery and perioperative mortality in low-resource settings was used to collect data prospectively for this study.29 Data were collected using a Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) tool in which data are entered offline into the application, and then the data entered will be uploaded to the server using the internet.

The attending anaesthesia provider or anaesthesia student collected demographic, maternal, clinical and anaesthetic information by electronic tablets. The mortality status and missing information were collected by a dedicated data manager hired by the research team. In the hospital, the mortality status was collected by checking the hospital logbook during follow-up phone calls (phone calls to the patient or family member until the seventh postoperative day). Finally, the collected data were uploaded to the REDCap database using the internet. The database containing the patient data is restricted to authorised users only. Information technology (IT) personnel, as part of the research team, consistently maintain the overall functionality of the data collection system, including the electronic tablets.

In order to ensure data quality, primary anaesthesia providers, anaesthesia students, data managers, and IT experts received simulation-based training regarding the overall data collection system and ethics of data handling. Multiple phone numbers were obtained to reduce the loss to follow-up during phone calls.

Data analysis

The data retrieved from the REDCap data collection system were examined for completeness and accuracy and then exported to STATA V.17 for further analysis. Descriptive statistics such as frequency, percentage, mean and SD were presented using tables and graphs.

Modified Poisson regression (robust error variance) was used to estimate the relative risk of explanatory variables on perioperative 7-day maternal mortality. The robust error estimate is commonly used to deal with variance underestimation in correlated data analysis.30 Crude and adjusted relative risk with 95% CI were calculated to determine the strength of association between dependent and independent variables. The bivariable modified Poisson regression model was used to select candidate variables with a statistically significant value of p≤0.25 to incorporate into the multivariable modified Poisson regression model. Multivariable modified Poisson regression analysis was used to identify risk factors for perioperative 7-day maternal mortality. A value of p<0.05 was considered statistically significant for all independent variables in the multivariable modified Poisson regression model to identify the independent effects of the different factors responsible for the maternal death.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Cohort profile

During this cohort study period, 1007 mothers who had a caesarean section at TGSH were included. However, 28 records were excluded due to incomplete outcome data, resulting in 979 records being considered for the final analysis.

The mean age of the mothers who underwent caesarean section was 27.4 years, with an SD of 5.2 years. Most of the participants came from the West Gojjam Zone, followed by East Gojjam and South Gondar. Infrequently, mothers also came from Awi, North Gondar and North Wollo. Very few mothers came from South Wollo, Metekel, Waghemra and Oromia Special Zone (figure 1).

Maternal and clinical characteristics

In this investigation, it was found that out of 979 pregnant women, 211 (21.55%) had comorbid health conditions, with hypertension, HIV and diabetes mellitus being the most prevalent comorbidities comprising 52.23%, 9.388% and 5.80% of all comorbidities, respectively. In the context of using the WHO surgical safety checklist, it was commonly employed during most (91.20%) caesarean section procedures. The majority (92.95%) of pregnant women who underwent caesarean sections had ASA physical status II. Spinal anaesthesia emerged as the predominant (93.05%) type of anaesthesia for these procedures. The median procedure duration was 40 min (IQR 35, 50). Nearly two-thirds (63.43%) of the women who had caesarean sections had blood loss below 500 mL, while 27 (2.76%) mothers had to undergo blood transfusions (table 1).

Indication for caesarean section

In this cohort, indications for caesarean section were categorised into five major categories. The most frequent indication for caesarean section was failure to progress, followed by fetal distress and elective caesarean delivery, accounting for 39.63%, 24.31% and 20.33% of all cases, respectively. In addition, maternal bleeding and pre-eclampsia/eclampsia accounted for 6.33% and 9.40% of all caesarean sections, respectively (figure 2).

Seven-day perioperative maternal mortality

In this study, we examined the 7-day perioperative outcomes of 979 pregnant mothers who had undergone caesarean delivery. Out of these mothers, 18 died during the period of follow-up, resulting in an overall mortality rate of 1.83%. From 18 deaths captured during the follow-up time, 17 (1.74%) deaths occurred during emergency caesarean section, and 1 (0.10%) death occurred during elective caesarean section. Of all mothers who underwent caesarean section, hysterectomy was done for eight parturients, and none of them died.

In the multivariable modified Poisson regression model, after adjusting for potential confounders, maternal bleeding (ARR = 11.80; 95% CI 1.12, 124.08) and not using WHO surgical safety checklist (ARR = 6.88; 95% CI 3.14, 15.07) were independently associated with increased risk of 7-day perioperative mortality among mothers who underwent caesarean section in North-West Ethiopia (table 2).

Discussion

This prospective study investigates the maternal outcome and factors associated with 7-day mortality following caesarean section, shedding light on critical aspects that impact perioperative maternal outcomes. Our study found that the proportion of 7-day perioperative death among mothers who underwent caesarean section during the follow-up period is 1.83%. This finding is comparable with the studies conducted in Nigeria31 and Sierra Leone.22 However, this finding is higher than that in a maternal outcome report from the African Surgical Outcome Study (ASOS), which reported that African mothers have a 50 times higher mortality rate after caesarean delivery compared with mothers from high-income countries.16 The possible explanation for this discrepancy might be a difference in sample size and study setting. But our result can be translated as the maternal mortality after caesarean section in this study is more than 50 times higher than that of high-income countries.

Of all deaths recorded in this study, the highest percentage of mortality was recorded among pregnant mothers who underwent emergency caesarean section. This finding is in agreement with ASOS16 and a study conducted in Iran.32 The reason for this link might be an increased risk of postoperative morbidity such as infection, fever, postpartum haemorrhage and postoperative intensive care unit admission among mothers who underwent emergency caesarean section, which significantly increases the risk of death after caesarean section.33 Besides clinical factors, service delivery factors such as poor referral systems might contribute to a higher adverse outcome after emergency caesarean section.34

Maternal bleeding, a common indication for caesarean delivery, poses a substantial risk for poor maternal and neonatal outcomes. In line with several studies,16 22 the findings of this study revealed that those mothers having maternal bleeding for indication of caesarean section were at nearly 12 times higher risk of perioperative death compared with those mothers with another indication. The possible explanation for this link might be that those mothers indicating bleeding for caesarean section have a higher risk of bleeding during and after caesarean section, which increases the risk of perioperative mortality after caesarean section.35 However, caesarean section is considered to be a life-saving procedure to salvage the mother and the fetus in a very critical obstetrics emergency, including uterine rupture, placenta abruption and cord prolapse.36

In this study, in 8.80% of caesarean procedures, there was a failure to adhere to the surgical safety checklist. Nationwide in Ethiopia, studies have reported low adherence to the utilisation of surgical safety checklists.37 38 Our study showed that non-utilisation of the WHO surgical safety checklist is associated with an increased risk of perioperative maternal death. The WHO surgical safety checklist is a well-established tool designed to enhance communication and teamwork, reduce the risk of errors during surgical procedures, and improve patient safety and outcomes. The absence of checklist utilisation in surgical procedures such as caesarean sections may contribute to overlooked safety measures, potentially leading to adverse outcomes.39 40 Due to the complexity of care coordination and communication for the mother and the newborn, to reduce perioperative mortality and morbidity, The Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine has developed and recommends implementing specialised surgical safety checklists for caesarean delivery to address the added complexity.41

Despite multiple improvement strategies implemented in Ethiopia, there is still a gap in access to caesarean section.25 27 Besides the unmet need for caesarean section, the problem is doubly burdened by higher mortality among those who get caesarean section. The reason might be inadequate medical staff and resources, a poor referral system, and a lack of safety protocols.

Our study provides practical insights for healthcare stakeholders in resource-limited settings, stressing the significance of addressing specific risk factors and promoting adherence to safety protocols to enhance maternal care significantly. This research makes a substantial contribution to the limited literature on perioperative maternal mortality in resource-limited countries, guiding future research and interventions aimed at improving the quality of maternal healthcare. Healthcare providers and institutions must recognise the significance of these findings and incorporate evidence-based practices into caesarean section protocols.

The main limitation of this study is we did not assess fetal outcome, which is one indication in assessing the outcome of caesarean section. In addition, this is a single-centre study, which affects its generalisability. Furthermore, we consider deaths until 7 days postoperatively to be related to the caesarean section procedure.

Conclusion

In this caesarean section cohort, resembling other African countries, mortality after caesarean section is higher compared with high-income countries. Maternal bleeding and non-utilisation of the WHO surgical safety checklist are identified as risk factors for perioperative maternal death after caesarean delivery.

Implementing targeted interventions focusing on the prevention and management of maternal bleeding, along with consistent use of the WHO surgical safety checklist, can improve the overall safety and outcomes of caesarean sections.
