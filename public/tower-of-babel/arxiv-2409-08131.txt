𝓞 ⁡ ( 𝒓 𝑵 ) {\mathcal{O}(r^{N})} two-form asymptotic symmetries and renormalized charges
Matteo Romoli

Abstract
We investigate 𝒪 ⁡ ( r N ) \mathcal{O}\left(r^{N}\right) asymptotic symmetries for a two-form gauge field in four-dimensional Minkowski spacetime. By employing symplectic renormalization, we identify N N independent asymptotic charges, with each charge being parametrised by an arbitrary function of the angular variables. Working in Lorenz gauge, the gauge parameters require a radial expansion involving logarithmic (subleading) terms to ensure nontrivial angular dependence at leading order. At the same time, we adopt a setup where the field strength admits a power expansion, allowing logarithms in the gauge field expansions within pure gauge sectors. The same setup is studied for electromagnetism.

Another subject of interest, related to the previous discussion, regards the choice of the expansion for fields and parameters. Early works on this topic considered a

When dealing with higher order asymptotic symmetries, however, a relevant issue occurs: the asymptotic charge turns out to be divergent. These divergences can be understood as ambiguities of the presymplectic potential and therefore renormalized in the spirit of

There are several motivations for this work. To begin with,

And since scalar theories represent the simplest models in physics, these results could help in a deeper understanding of the connection between asymptotic symmetries and physical effects.

Furthermore, it has been recently pointed out in

Additionally, let us observe that the two-form provides the simplest example of a theory possessing a gauge-for-gauge redundancy, namely the gauge parameter has itself a gauge symmetry. We thus might expect some peculiarities that are not present in the electromagnetic case. From our analysis, however, it seems that no particular role is played by the gauge-for-gauge parameter, except that of simplifying some computations.

Preliminaries

2.1
Notation and conventions

We focus on
D=4
and adopt the mostly plus convention for the metric. For the discussion of asymptotic parameter, we employ retarded Bondi coordinates
(u,r,z^{i})
, with
r
being the radial coordinate,
u=t-r
the retarded time and
z^{i}\;(i=1,2)
the angular coordinates, which we parameterise by means of stereographic projection in terms of
(z,{\bar{z}})
, related to the standard angles as

z=e^{i\phi}\cot\frac{\theta}{2},\qquad\bar{z}=e^{-i\phi}\cot\frac{\theta}{2}\,,

(2.1)

with
\theta\in[0,\pi]
and
\phi\in[0,2\pi)
.

The Minkowski metric reads

ds^{2}=-du^{2}-2du\,dr+r^{2}\gamma_{ij}dz^{i}\,dz^{j},

(2.2)

where
\gamma_{ij}
is the unit metric on the two-sphere, which is anti-diagonal in terms of
(z,{\bar{z}})
, with

\gamma_{z\bar{z}}=\frac{2}{(1+z\bar{z})^{2}}.

(2.3)

The covariant derivative with respect to
\gamma_{ij}
is denoted with
D_{i}
and the two-sphere Laplace operator is
\Delta=D_{i}D^{i}
. At the same time, we use
\nabla_{\mu}
for the covariant derivative respect to the metric (
2.2
) and
\Box=\nabla_{\mu}\nabla^{\mu}
for the d’Alembert operator. Divergences are often denoted as
D\cdot V:=D^{i}V_{i}
and
\nabla\cdot V:=\nabla^{\mu}V_{\mu}
.

The non-vanishing Christoffel symbols are

\Gamma^{u}_{ij}=-\Gamma^{r}_{ij}=r\gamma_{ij},\qquad\Gamma^{i}_{rj}=\frac{1}{r}\delta^{i}_{j},\qquad\Gamma^{z}_{zz}={\bar{\Gamma}^{{\bar{z}}}_{{\bar{z}}{\bar{z}}}}=-\frac{2z}{1+z{\bar{z}}}\,.

(2.4)

We often make use of the following commutation relation, which we write here explicitly:

D_{i}\Delta V_{j}=(\Delta-3)D_{i}V_{j}+2\gamma_{ij}D_{k}V^{k}.

(2.5)

Future null infinity
{\cal I}^{+}
is reached by sending
r\to\infty
(or
t\to\infty
) with fixed
u
. It is a null hypersurface with
\mathbb{R}\times S^{2}
topology. Sending
u\to\pm\infty
we reach
{\cal I}^{+}_{\pm}
. Fields and parameter are expanded near
{\cal I}^{+}
. The most general radial expansion we write is

\varphi=\sum_{n_{1}}\frac{\varphi^{(n_{1})}}{r^{n_{1}}}+\sum_{n_{2}}{\hat{\varphi}}^{(n_{2})}\frac{\log r}{r^{n_{2}}},

(2.6)

where the coefficients of the radial expansion are always denoted with a superscript “
(n)
” and, in particular, we use the hat for the logarithmic coefficients.

When discussing charges and renormalization we employ
(t,u,z,{\bar{z}})
coordinates, since we are interested in the large
t-
behaviour around
{\cal I}^{+}
. The expansion coefficients are denoted with a subscript “
(n)
” and

\varphi=\sum_{n_{1}}{t^{n_{1}}{\varphi_{(n_{1})}}}+{\log t}\sum_{n_{2}}{t^{n_{2}}}\,{\hat{\varphi}}_{(n_{2})}.

(2.7)

To describe the behaviour near
{\cal I}^{+}_{-}
we consider only power expansions that we denote as

\varphi^{(n)}=\sum_{m}u^{m}\varphi^{(n,m)}.

(2.8)

In some cases this expansion is valid for all values of
u
and not only for
u\to-\infty
, but we use the same notation, always specifying its meaning.

2.2
Covariant phase space formalism

The most important tool in order to understand whether an asymptotic symmetry has a physical interpretation is the asymptotic charge. We introduce
this notion employing the covariant phase space formalism, whose main idea is to combine the calculus in both spacetime and field space. This technique was introduced in
[
43
,
44
,
45
]
and later refined in
[
46
,
47
,
48
]
. Discussions with a particular focus on asymptotic symmetries can be found in
[
49
,
50
]
. Reviews can be found, for instance, in
[
51
,
52
,
53
]
.

On a differentiable manifold
M
, the space of forms induce the de Rham cohomology, where we denote with
d
and
i
the exterior derivative and interior product on this complex,
respectively. The exterior derivative is assumed to be nilpotent, i.e.
d^{2}=0
. The Lie derivative along
\xi\in TM
is given by

{\cal L}_{\xi}=d\,i_{\xi}+i_{\xi}d.

(2.9)

These notions can be introduced also in the space of all possible field configurations
\Gamma
. In this case, we denote with
\delta
the exterior derivative, assumed to be nilpotent as well, while we use
I_{V}
for the interior product, so that the Lie derivative reads

\mathfrak{L}_{V}:=\delta\,I_{V}+I_{V}\delta\,.

(2.10)

Given a Lagrangian theory, whose action reads

S=\int_{M}L,

(2.11)

we can write the way
L
transforms under a generic field variation
\varphi\to\varphi+\delta\varphi
as

\delta L=\text{EOM}\delta\varphi+d\theta,

(2.12)

with
\theta
being the presymplectic potential. We define the presymplectic (field-space) two-form as

\omega=\delta\theta,

(2.13)

and the symplectic two-form

\Omega=\int_{\Sigma}\omega,

(2.14)

with
\Sigma
being an arbitrary Cauchy surface.

Assuming trivial cohomology in the space of
1
-forms on
\Gamma
, a vector
V\in T\Gamma
is called a symplectomorphism or Hamiltonian vector field if
\mathfrak{L}_{V}\omega=0
. For a gauge symmetry, we can define the current as

J_{V}=I_{V}\theta

(2.15)

and the charge

Q=\int_{\Sigma}J_{V}.

(2.16)

Furthermore, for a gauge symmetry the current is in general an on-shell total derivative and the charge can be written as a integral over
\partial\Sigma
by means of the Stokes theorem.

The presymplectic potential admits two types of ambiguities

\theta\to\theta+\delta\Xi+d\Upsilon.

(2.17)

The first does not change
\omega
, since
\delta
is nilpotent, and corresponds to the addition of a boundary term to the Lagrangian
L\to L+d\Xi
. The second modifies
\omega\to\omega+d\delta\Upsilon
but does not affect
d\theta
in (
2.12
), since
d
is nilpotent.

The existence of these ambiguities is fundamental in order to discuss asymptotic charges. In fact, as we discuss later, some divergences occur when dealing with higher order asymptotic symmetries. Therefore, it is not possible to perform the limit to define asymptotic charges. However, we can absorb the divergent terms in ambiguities of the presymplectic potential, while leaving the finite and non-vanishing parts of the asymptotic charges unchanged; the latter provide the physical content of the asymptotic symmetries.

2.3
Asymptotic charges

Electromagnetism.

Considering the Lagrangian

L=-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}\,,

(2.18)

the presymplectic potential current reads

\theta_{e}^{\mu}=-\sqrt{-g}\,F^{\mu\nu}\delta{\cal A}_{\nu}.

(2.19)

Given a gauge transformation
\delta{\cal A}_{\nu}=\partial_{\nu}\epsilon
the current is an on-shell total derivative

{\cal J}_{e}^{\mu}=-\partial_{\nu}(\sqrt{-g}\,F^{\mu\nu}\epsilon)\,.

(2.20)

We consider a surface
\Sigma_{t}
at constant
t=u+r
and then study the limit
t\to\infty
. This defines the asymptotic charge, evaluated in
(u,t,z,{\bar{z}})-
coordinates, which is

\begin{split}Q_{e}=&\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}-\int_{\Sigma_{t}}dudzd{\bar{z}}\,\partial_{\mu}(\sqrt{-g}\,F^{t\mu}\epsilon)\\
=&\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}-\int_{\Sigma_{t}}dudzd{\bar{z}}\gamma_{z{\bar{z}}}(\partial_{u}-\partial_{r})(r^{2}F_{ur}\epsilon),\end{split}

(2.21)

where in the last step we substituted the expression in terms of Bondi components and neglected a total two-sphere divergence.

At the same time, we can write a dual asymptotic magnetic charge, which is

{\tilde{Q}}_{m}=\int_{\Sigma_{t}}dudzd{\bar{z}}(\partial_{u}-\partial_{r})(\sqrt{-g}\,{\tilde{F}}_{ur}{\tilde{\epsilon}})\,,

(2.22)

where
\tilde{F}
is the dual field strength, defined as through the Hodge dual
\tilde{F}=\star F
, and
{\tilde{\epsilon}}
is the gauge parameter of the dual gauge field
\tilde{A}
, with
\tilde{F}=d{\tilde{A}}
.

However, it should be noted that the definition (
2.22
) is not entirely correct. As studied in detail in
[
54
]
, the charge (
2.22
) is not derived from a well-defined canonical analysis. To obtain its correct form, one should extend the covariant phase space by means of edge modes degrees of freedom. In particular, the correct magnetic charge is different from the one (
2.22
) when the dual gauge parameter has singularities.

We do not perform such an analysis here, as our primary interest lies in the two-form and the comparison with the electric charges. The correct form of the charge is used in
[
28
]
for a
{\cal O}(r^{n})
dual gauge parameter. We leave this analysis for future investigations.

2-form.

The Lagrangian is

L=-\frac{1}{6}H_{\mu\nu\rho}H^{\mu\nu\rho}\,\text{Vol}_{M}\,,

(2.23)

so that the presymplectic potential reads

\theta_{B}^{\mu}=-\sqrt{-g}\,H^{\mu\nu\rho}\delta{\cal B}_{\nu\rho}\,.

(2.24)

Given a gauge transformation
\delta{\cal B}_{\nu\rho}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\nu}\lambda_{\rho}-\partial_{\rho}\lambda_{\nu})
the current takes the form

{\cal J}^{\mu}_{B}=-\partial_{\nu}(\sqrt{-g}\,H^{\mu\nu\rho}\lambda_{\rho}).

(2.25)

Considering again a constant time surface
\Sigma_{t}
, the asymptotic charge is defined as the limit
t\to\infty
of the quantity

Q_{B}=\int_{\Sigma_{t}}dudzd{\bar{z}}\gamma_{z{\bar{z}}}(\partial_{u}-\partial_{r})\left(r^{2}H^{uri}\lambda_{i}\right)

(2.26)

where we are neglecting again a total two-sphere divergence.

For higher-order asymptotic symmetries, there are
t-
divergences which imply that the both
Q_{e}
and
Q_{B}
are not well-defined in the limit. However, we can employ symplectic renormalization in order to neglect these divergences and perform the limit.

Electromagnetism

We start by deriving
{\cal O}(r^{N})
asymptotic symmetries for electromagnetism. Unlike
[

28

[

[
55

. In addition to this, we notice that considering the source of
[

28

j_{\mu}=ie\phi{\cal D}_{\mu}\phi^{*}+c.c

(3.1)

with
{\cal D}_{\mu}\phi=\partial_{\mu}\phi-ie{\cal A}_{\mu}\phi

\phi={\cal O}(\tfrac{1}{r})
and
{\cal A}_{u}={\cal O}(\tfrac{1}{r})

u-

j_{u}^{(2)}=0
.

Furthermore, we cannot treat
A_{\mu}

j_{\mu}

\phi^{3}

[

\phi={\cal O}(\tfrac{1}{r})

A full analysis is left for future investigation, while for the moment we restrict our study to the scenario where all quantities satisfy the vacuum equations of motion, analysed in
Appendix A

[
57

The falloffs analysis starts by asking compatibility with some physical requests about the field strength:

F_{ur}={\cal O}(r^{-2}),\qquad F_{ui}={\cal O}(1),\qquad F_{ri}={\cal O}(r^{-2}),\qquad F_{ij}={\cal O}(1).

(3.2)

Moreover, we propose a key ansatz regards the expansion of the field strength components:

F_{\mu\nu}=\sum_{n}\frac{F_{\mu\nu}^{(n)}(u,z,{\bar{z}})}{r^{n}},

(3.3)

where we add details on the
u-

3.3
) are unnecessary. In fact, they are required in the gauge parameter because otherwise the Lorenz gauge condition does not allow for a non-trivial
{\cal O}(1)
term. Consequently, logarithms are added in the field components or otherwise the falloffs cannot be preserved (see, for instance, section 5 of
[
15
]
). However, this issue is specific to the Lorenz gauge but does not arise, for instance, in retarded radial gauge or in radiation gauge. In these well-studied and somehow simpler cases, the parameter does not require logarithms, therefore there is no need to consider these terms in the gauge field components and, accordingly, no reason to add them in the field strength. And since the field strength is a gauge invariant quantity, we do not expect the expansion considered to be gauge-dependent. This choice is one of the main difference with
[
28
]
.

We work in Lorenz gauge

\nabla^{\mu}{\cal A}_{\mu}=0,

(3.4)

so that the residual gauge parameter
\epsilon
obeys to

\Box\epsilon=0

(3.5)

and the Maxwell equations reduces to

\Box{\cal A}_{\mu}=0.

(3.6)

3.2
Field strength

As shown in (
2.21
), the component
F_{ur}={\cal O}(r^{-2})
enters in the asymptotic electric charge definition. Specifically, the assumption

F_{ur}=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{F_{ur}^{(n)}}{r^{n}}

(3.12)

implies that only the first series of (
3.7
) contributes to it. Using the equations of motion, we find

\begin{split}F_{ur}^{(2)}&=D\cdot A^{(0)}\\
F_{ur}^{(n)}&=-\frac{\Delta}{n-2}A_{r}^{(n-1)}-D\cdot A^{(n-2)}\qquad(n>2),\end{split}

(3.13)

as well as

\begin{split}\partial_{u}F_{ur}^{(2)}&=\partial_{u}D\cdot A^{(0)}\\
\partial_{u}F_{ur}^{(3)}&=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta D\cdot A^{(0)}\\
\partial_{u}F_{ur}^{(n)}&=\frac{\Delta}{2}\left(\frac{\Delta}{(n-2)(n-3)}+1\right)A_{r}^{(n-2)}+\left(\frac{\Delta+(n-2)(n-3)}{2(n-2)}\right)D\cdot A^{(n-3)}\qquad(n>3).\end{split}

(3.14)

We can perform a further gauge fixing and express

only in terms of

These equations, together with the assumption (
3.9
), fixes the
u-

to be

F_{ur}=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{n}}\left[\sum_{m=0}^{n-2}u^{m}F_{ur}^{(n,m)}(z,{\bar{z}})+f_{ur}^{(n)}(u,z,{\bar{z}})\right]

(3.15)

where
f_{ur}^{(n)}(u,z,{\bar{z}})\to 0
for

The dual tensor
{\tilde{F}}_{ur}:=(\star F)_{ur}

{\tilde{F}}_{ur}=\varepsilon_{urij}F^{ij}=\frac{\varepsilon^{ij}}{r^{2}}F_{ij},

(3.16)

where
\varepsilon^{ij}

In order to analyse it, let us introduce a particularly useful splitting which generally valid for every two-sphere vector

&A_{\bar{z}}=D_{\bar{z}}(A-A^{\prime}).\end{split}

3.4.1

4.1
Falloffs and field strength

The two-form analogue of (

{\cal B}_{\mu\nu}=B_{\mu\nu}+\partial_{[\mu}b_{\nu]}=\sum_{n,n_{1},n_{2}}\left[\frac{B_{\mu\nu}^{(n)}}{r^{n}}+\partial_{[\mu}\left(b_{\nu]}^{(n_{1})}\frac{1}{r^{n_{1}}}+{\hat{b}}^{(n_{2})}_{\nu]}\frac{\log r}{r^{n_{2}}}\right)\right]\,,

The field strength falloffs (

{\cal R}_{i}
, can be decomposed again in

{\cal R}_{i}=D_{i}{\cal R}+\varepsilon_{ij}D^{j}{\cal R}^{\prime}

(4.27)

and only
{\cal R}^{\prime}
contributes to the charge, while the existence of
{\cal R}
signals the possibility of performing a further residual gauge fixing.

4.2.2
O\left(r^{N}\right)
asymptotic symmetries

Let us now discuss a
{\cal O}(r^{N})
asymptotic parameter, with the expansion (
4.18
). Using the splitting (
4.13
), the only contribution to the charge comes from the term
\lambda^{\prime}
. In fact, to study the asymptotic symmetries of a two-form we can consistently choose

\lambda_{u}=0,\qquad\lambda_{r}=0,\qquad\lambda=0.

(4.28)

With this choice we cannot appreciate the possible double-copy structure as in the previous discussion, but it is the simplest way to analyse the two-form asymptotic symmetries. This setup implies, for instance, that the falloffs of
B_{ur},B_{r},B_{u}
, where we used the notation introduced in (
4.15
), can be always preserved. The falloffs we need to generalise by means of pure gauge terms are only
B,B_{r}^{\prime},B_{u}^{\prime}
. In particular,
\lambda^{\prime}
acts on these components as

\begin{split}&[\delta B]^{(n)}=\Delta\lambda^{\prime\,(n)},\quad[\delta B^{\prime}_{u}]^{(n)}=\partial_{u}\lambda^{\prime\,(n)},\quad[\delta B^{\prime}_{r}]^{(n)}=-(n-1)\lambda^{\prime\,(n-1)}+\hat{\lambda}^{\prime\,(n-1)}\\
&\widehat{[\delta B]}^{(n)}=\Delta\hat{\lambda}^{\prime\,(n)},\quad\widehat{[\delta B^{\prime}_{u}]}^{(n)}=\partial_{u}\hat{\lambda}^{\prime\,(n)},\quad\widehat{[\delta B^{\prime}_{r}]}^{(n)}=-(n-1)\hat{\lambda}^{\prime\,(n-1)}\end{split}

(4.29)

The wave equation for
\lambda^{\prime}
, which is reported order-by-order in (
4.19
), completely fixes the
u-
dependence on
{\cal I}^{+}
being again of the form

\lambda^{\prime\,(-n)}=\sum_{m=0}^{N-n}u^{m}\lambda^{\prime\,(-n,m)}\,,

(4.30)

where we are now focusing on the
n\geq 1
part, which gives non-vanishing contributions to the charge, and in particular

\lambda^{\prime\,(-n,m)}=\frac{1}{2^{m}}\frac{(n-1)!}{m!(n+m-1)!}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{m}\big(\Delta+(i+n-1)(i+n)\big)\right]\lambda^{\prime\,(-n-m)}_{c}\,,

(4.31)

where the result is identical the case of the scalar wave equation except for a shift
n\to n-1
due to the fact that the equations of motion of the angular component change the
r
-order (see
footnote 8
).

4.3
Charges

4.3.1
Leading and subleading charges.

We study the limit
t\to\infty
of

-\int_{\Sigma_{t}}dudzd{\bar{z}}\gamma_{z{\bar{z}}}(\partial_{u}-\partial_{r})(\gamma^{ij}H_{uri}\lambda_{j})\,.

(4.32)

and consider the
{\cal O}(t^{0})
term. We rewrite the integral using the splitting (
4.13
) and integrating by parts as

\int_{\Sigma_{t}}dudzd{\bar{z}}\gamma_{z{\bar{z}}}(\partial_{u}-\partial_{r})\rho_{H}\,,

(4.33)

where we define

\rho_{H}:=\lambda^{\prime}\varepsilon^{ij}D_{j}H_{uri}\,.

(4.34)

We start considering the leading and subleading contributions to the asymptotic charge, with a
\lambda^{\prime}={\cal O}(r^{2})
. In particular, we have

\begin{split}(\partial_{u}-\partial_{r})\rho_{H}=&r\left[\lambda^{\prime\,(-2)}\varepsilon^{ij}\partial_{u}D_{j}H_{uri}^{(1)}\right]+r^{0}\left[-\lambda^{\prime\,(-2)}\varepsilon^{ij}D_{j}H_{uri}^{(1)}\right.\\
&\left.+\,\partial_{u}(\lambda^{\prime\,(-1)}\varepsilon^{ij}D_{j}H_{uri}^{(1)})+\lambda^{\prime\,(-2)}\varepsilon^{ij}\partial_{u}D_{j}H_{uri}^{(2)}\right]+\dots\end{split}

(4.35)

and the
{\cal O}(t^{0})
term is given by

\partial_{u}\left(-u\lambda^{\prime\,(-2)}\varepsilon^{ij}D_{j}H_{uri}^{(1)}+\lambda^{\prime\,(-1)}\varepsilon^{ij}D_{j}H_{uri}^{(1)}+\lambda^{\prime\,(-2)}\varepsilon^{ij}D_{j}H_{uri}^{(2)}\right),

(4.36)

while the term linear in
t
is neglected by means of symplectic renormalization.

To evaluate this, we need the expressions

\begin{split}&H_{uri}^{(1)}=-D^{j}B_{ij}^{(-1)}\\
&\partial_{u}H_{uri}^{(2)}=\frac{\Delta-1}{2}D^{j}B_{ij}^{(-1)},\end{split}

(4.37)

and, by means of (
4.16
), we find

\begin{split}&\varepsilon^{ij}D_{j}H_{uri}^{(1)}=\Delta B^{(-1)},\\
&\varepsilon^{ij}\partial_{u}D_{j}H_{uri}^{(1)}=-\frac{\Delta^{2}}{2}B^{(-1)}.\end{split}

(4.38)

Finally, using

\begin{split}&\lambda^{(-2)}=\lambda^{(-2)}_{c}(z,{\bar{z}}),\\
&\lambda^{(-1)}=u\frac{\Delta+2}{2}\lambda^{\prime\,(-2)}_{c}(z,{\bar{z}})+\lambda^{\prime\,(-1)}_{c}(z,{\bar{z}}),\end{split}

(4.39)

we arrive to

Q_{B}=Q_{B}^{(0)}+Q_{B}^{(-1)}\,,

(4.40)

with

\displaystyle Q_{B}^{(0)}=\int dudzd{\bar{z}}\gamma_{z{\bar{z}}}\,\lambda_{c}^{\prime\,(-1)}\partial_{u}\Delta B^{(-1)},

(4.41a)

\displaystyle Q_{B}^{(-1)}=\int dudzd{\bar{z}}\gamma_{z{\bar{z}}}\frac{u}{2}\lambda_{c}^{\prime\,(-2)}\partial_{u}\Delta^{2}B^{(-1)}.

(4.41b)

The charge
Q^{(0)}_{B}
matches the one presented in
[
33
]
. It corresponds to the leading soft scalar theorem, originally derived in
[
22
]
, as studied in
[
6

7
]
. The charge
Q^{(-1)}_{B}
, to which we refer as subleading, is presented here for the first time. Interestingly, it has a form identical to its electromagnetic counterpart
Q^{(-1)}_{e}
derived in (
3.37
) which, up to an integration by parts, reads

Q^{(-1)}_{e}=\int dudzd{\bar{z}}\gamma_{z{\bar{z}}}\frac{u}{2}\epsilon^{(-1)}_{c}\partial_{u}\Delta^{2}A^{(0)}

(4.42)

where we used the splitting (
3.17
) to highlight the similarity between the two charges. Our guess is that the conservation of the charge (
4.41b
), up to the identification of
B^{(-1)}
with the scalar mode
\phi^{(1)}
, might correspond to a subleading version of the soft scalar theorem presented in
[
22
]
.

To our knowledge, however, no subleading formulation of the soft scalar theorem of
[
22
]
has been derived yet. Nonetheless, we expect the subleading soft scalar factor to have a similar structure to the photon and graviton analogues. This is motivated, for instance, by the structure of subleading soft factors for dilatons, derived in
[
60
,
61
]
.

Furthermore, we notice the proposal in (3.43) of
[
22
]
for the smeared version of the scalar charges is also motivated by the analogy with electromagnetism and gravity. In this sense, the natural proposal for a subleading version of the charge would be our
Q^{(-1)}_{B}
.

4.3.2
O\left(r^{N}\right)
charges.

Let us now consider the
{\cal O}(r^{N})
charges with arbitrary
N
by repeating the same step of the previous case. We have

\rho_{H}=\sum_{n=-N}^{\infty}\frac{\rho_{H}^{(n)}}{r^{n}}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\hat{\rho}}^{(n)}\frac{\log r}{r^{n}}\,.

(4.43)

We focus on the part with
-N\leq n\leq 0
with

\rho_{H}^{(-n)}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-n}\lambda^{\prime\,(-n-k-1)}\varepsilon^{ij}D_{j}H_{uri}^{(1+k)}\,,

(4.44)

so that the
{\cal O}(t^{0})
term reads

\sum_{n=0}^{N}\partial_{u}\left((-u)^{n}\rho^{(-n)}\right),

(4.45)

with the charge

Q_{B}=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\int_{\Sigma_{t}}dudzd{\bar{z}}\gamma_{z{\bar{z}}}\,\partial_{u}\left((-u)^{n}\rho^{(-n)}\right).

(4.46)

There are again
u-
divergences, but they are understood in terms of ambiguities of the presymplectic potential. We are left only with the
u-
independent terms and a well-defined integral, so that we can perform the limit
t\to\infty
and obtain

Q_{B,\,\text{ren}}=\sum_{n=0}^{N}Q^{(-n)}_{B,\,\text{ren}}=-\int_{{\cal I}^{+}_{-}}dzd{\bar{z}}\gamma_{z{\bar{z}}}\,\lambda^{(-(n+1))}_{c}\varepsilon^{ij}D_{j}H_{uri}^{(n+1,0)}.

(4.47)

The confrontation with the spin-one counterpart is straightforward: we find
N
independent asymptotic charges, parametrised by
N
arbitrary scalar functions of the angular variables, arising from
u-
integrations. Using the expressions obtained in
Appendix B
we find

\begin{split}\qquad Q_{B}^{(\,0\,)}\;=&-\int_{{\cal I}^{+}_{-}}dzd{\bar{z}}\gamma_{z{\bar{z}}}\lambda^{\prime(-1)}_{c}\Delta B^{(-1,0)}\,,\\
\qquad Q_{B}^{(-1)}=&-\int_{{\cal I}^{+}_{-}}dzd{\bar{z}}\gamma_{z{\bar{z}}}\lambda^{\prime(-2)}_{c}\frac{\Delta^{2}}{2}B_{r}^{\prime(1,0)}\,,\\
\qquad Q_{B}^{(-n)}=&\frac{n-1}{2}\int_{{\cal I}^{+}_{-}}dzd{\bar{z}}\gamma_{z{\bar{z}}}\lambda^{\prime(-n-1)}_{c}\Delta B^{(n-1,0)}\,\,\qquad(n\geq 2)\,.\end{split}

(4.48)

5
Renormalization of the charge

Our study of asymptotic charges encounters two types of divergences: those arising from positive powers of

t
and those resulting from the
du
integration over positive powers of
u
. To address these issues, we employ a symplectic renormalization procedure, as proposed in
[
42
]
.

The presymplectic potential admits two types of ambiguities that allow us to define an equivalence relation

\theta^{\mu}\sim\theta^{\mu}+\partial_{\nu}\Upsilon^{\mu\nu}+\delta\Xi^{\mu},

(5.1)

with
\Upsilon^{\mu\nu}
being antisymmetric. Our prescription is to use these ambiguities to cancel the divergences while leaving the rest unchanged.

We do not need to specify the theory to which we are referring, since all the expansions and assumptions apply to both one- and two-form cases, as we prove. This is a consequence of using the same setup for both theories. In
(u,t,z,{\bar{z}})
coordinates, we have

\partial_{t}\theta^{t}\approx\delta{\cal L}-\partial_{u}\theta^{u}-\partial_{i}\theta^{i},

(5.2)

where “
\approx
” denotes an on-shell relation. As
t\to+\infty
,
\theta^{t}
has the general structure

\theta^{t}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}t^{n}\theta^{t}_{(n)}(u,z,{\bar{z}})+\theta^{t}_{(0)}(u,t,z,{\bar{z}}),

(5.3)

where
\theta^{t}_{(0)}(u,t,z,{\bar{z}})
is such that
\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\theta^{t}_{(0)}(u,t,z,{\bar{z}})=\theta^{t}_{(0)}(u,z,{\bar{z}})
. In particular, logarithmic terms such as
t^{-n}\log t
are inside
\theta^{t}_{(0)}(u,z,{\bar{z}})
and, therefore, not relevant. Similar expansions are introduced for
{\cal L},\theta^{u},\theta^{i}
with respective coefficients
{\cal L}_{(n)},\theta^{u}_{(n)},\theta^{i}_{(n)}
.

Let us verify that the expansion (
5.3
) holds in both one- and two-form cases. For the spin-one case, we write

\theta^{t}_{e}=\theta^{u}_{e}+\theta^{r}_{e}=r^{2}\gamma_{z{\bar{z}}}(F^{u\nu}+F^{r\nu})\delta{\cal A}_{\nu}\,.

(5.4)

In our setup, the field strength admits a power expansion, while for the gauge field variation we have

\delta{\cal A}_{\nu}=\delta A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}\delta a,

(5.5)

where
A_{\nu}
admits a power expansion as well, while for
a
we chose to consider a polyhomogeneous expansion. In particular, we used

a=\sum_{n=-N}^{\infty}\frac{a^{(n)}}{r^{n}}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\hat{a}}^{(n)}\frac{\log r}{r^{n}},

(5.6)

with the logarithmic series starting at order
{\cal O}\left(\frac{\log r}{r}\right)
. Summing over indices we find

\theta^{t}_{e}=r^{2}\gamma_{z{\bar{z}}}\left[F_{ur}(\delta{\cal A}_{u}-\delta{\cal A}_{r})-\frac{\gamma^{ij}}{r^{2}}F_{uj}\delta{\cal A}_{i}\right]

(5.7)

and, using the field strength falloffs (
3.2
), we deduce that the first logarithmic term in
\theta^{t}_{e}
is of order
{\cal O}\left(\frac{\log r}{r}\right)
and therefore of order
{\cal O}\left(\frac{\log t}{t}\right)
. The highest order, instead, is of order
{\cal O}(r^{N})
and therefore
{\cal O}(t^{N})
.

Let us repeat the analysis for the two-form case. We have

\begin{split}\theta^{t}_{B}&=r^{2}\gamma_{z{\bar{z}}}(H^{u\mu\nu}+H^{r\mu\nu})\delta{\cal B}_{\mu\nu}\\
&=\gamma_{z{\bar{z}}}\left[\gamma^{ij}H_{uri}(\delta{\cal B}_{uj}-\delta{\cal B}_{rj})-\frac{\gamma^{ik}\gamma^{jl}}{r^{2}}H_{uij}\delta{\cal B}_{kl}\right].\end{split}

(5.8)

Field strength components admit a power expansion with falloffs (
4.3
). The two-form gauge field variation is

\delta{\cal B}_{\mu\nu}=\delta B_{\mu\nu}-\partial_{[\mu}\delta b_{\nu]}

(5.9)

with
b
admitting a polyhomogenoues expansion of the form

\begin{split}&b_{u}=\sum_{n=-N}^{\infty}\frac{b_{u}^{(n)}}{r^{n}}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\hat{b}}_{u}^{(n)}\frac{\log r}{r^{n}},\\
&b_{r}=\sum_{n=-N}^{\infty}\frac{b_{r}^{(n)}}{r^{n}}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\hat{b}}_{r}^{(n)}\frac{\log r}{r^{n}},\\
&b_{i}=\sum_{n=-N-1}^{\infty}\frac{b_{i}^{(n)}}{r^{n}}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\hat{b}}_{i}^{(n)}\frac{\log r}{r^{n}}\,.\end{split}

(5.10)

Substituting these in the equation(
5.8
), we find exactly the expansion (
5.3
).

The equation (
5.2
) implies that every
\theta^{t}_{(n)}
with
n>0
corresponds to an ambiguity, namely

\theta^{t}_{(n)}=\frac{1}{n}\left(\delta{\cal L}_{(n-1)}-\partial_{u}\theta^{u}_{(n-1)}-\partial_{i}\theta^{i}_{(n-1)}\right)\,.

(5.11)

Therefore, we can identify

\begin{split}\Upsilon^{tu}=&\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{n}t^{n}\theta^{u}_{(n-1)}+\Upsilon^{tu}_{(0)}\,,\\
\Upsilon^{ti}=&\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{n}t^{n}\theta^{i}_{(n-1)}+\Upsilon^{ti}_{(0)}\,,\\
\Xi^{t}=&\sum_{n=1}^{N}-\frac{1}{n}t^{n}{\cal L}_{(n-1)}+\Xi^{t}_{(0)}\,,\end{split}

(5.12)

where
\Upsilon^{tu}_{(0)},\Upsilon^{tA}_{(0)},\Xi^{t}_{(0)}
are (free) functions with a well-defined limit for
t\to\infty
. We thus find that

\theta^{t}\sim\theta^{t}_{(0)}+\partial_{u}\Upsilon^{tu}_{(0)}+\partial_{i}\Upsilon^{ti}_{(0)}+\delta\Xi^{t}_{(0)},

(5.13)

implying that only the
t^{0}
order contributes to the charge in the limit
t\to\infty
.

Next, we address the
u-
divergences, which arise from the
du
integration. This is managed using
\Upsilon^{t\mu}_{(0)}
. Near
u\to-\infty
,
\theta^{t}_{(0)}
reads

\theta^{t}_{(0)}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\theta^{t}_{(0,n)}u^{n-2}+\Theta^{t}_{(0)}(u,z,{\bar{z}}),

(5.14)

where we factored out the part that is not
u-
integrable, absent for
N=0
, while
\Theta^{t}_{(0)}(u,z,{\bar{z}})
admits a
u-
power expansion with leading order
{\cal O}(u^{-2})
.

To prove this expansion, we start from the setup used for
{\cal O}(1)
asymptotic symmetries. The
{\cal O}(r^{N})
case would differ from the
{\cal O}(1)
only for overleading powers of
u
. Let us begin with electromagnetism and determine the
{\cal O}(t^{0})
term. The only contribution is from the last term in (
5.7
) and reads

\theta^{t}_{e(0)}=\gamma_{z{\bar{z}}}\gamma^{ij}\partial_{u}A_{i}^{(0)}\delta{\cal A}_{j}^{(0)},

(5.15)

where we substituted
F_{ui}^{(0)}=\partial_{u}A_{i}^{(0)}
. Near
{\cal I}^{+}_{-}
we use the ansatz (
3.9
) for
A_{i}^{(0)}
which, for
u\to-\infty
, means

\partial_{u}A_{i}^{(0)}={\cal O}(u^{-2})\,.

(5.16)

Consistently with this assumption, we find that
\delta{\cal A}_{j}^{(0)}
tends to a well-defined function of the angles for
u\to-\infty
. Combining these two results, we find a
u-
integrable quantity for
\theta^{t}_{(0)}
in the
N=0
case. Considering higher order terms with arbitrary
N
, the setup differs only for
a={\cal O}(r^{N})
, therefore we are adding overleading powers of
u
, finding non-integrable quantities as in the first series of the expansion (
5.14
).

Let us repeat the same computation for the two-form case in the
N=0
setup. We have

\theta^{t}_{B(0)}=\gamma_{z{\bar{z}}}\gamma^{ik}\gamma^{jl}\partial_{u}B_{ij}^{(-1)}\delta{\cal B}_{kl}^{(-1)},

(5.17)

and near
{\cal I}^{+}_{-}

\begin{split}&B_{ij}^{(-1)}=B_{ij}^{(-1,0)}(z,{\bar{z}})+{\cal O}\left(u^{-1}\right)\\
&\partial_{u}B_{ij}^{(-1)}={\cal O}\left(u^{-2}\right).\end{split}

(5.18)

Hence, the presymplectic potential in the
N=0
case is integrable and order
{\cal O}(u^{-2})
. Modifying the setup to arbitrary
N
adds positive powers of
u
.

We now use
\Upsilon^{t\mu}_{(0)}
to cancel the first series. This can be achieved, for instance, employing these expansions

\Upsilon^{tu}_{(0)}=\sum_{n=3}^{N+1}u^{n-2}\Upsilon^{tu}_{(0,n)},\qquad\Upsilon^{ti}_{(0)}=\frac{1}{u}\Upsilon^{ti}_{(0,-1)}

(5.19)

with

\Upsilon^{tu}_{(0,n)}=-\frac{\theta^{t}_{(0,n+1)}}{n-2},\qquad\partial_{i}\Upsilon^{ti}_{(0,-1)}=-\theta^{t}_{(0,1)}.

(5.20)

This solution is not unique and depends on the specific structure of the renormalizing terms. Here we are assuming that
\Upsilon^{tu}_{(0)}
has a
u-
power expansion, making it impossible to cancel a
\tfrac{1}{u}
term with a
u-
derivative, which is why we used
\partial_{i}\Upsilon^{ti}_{(0,-1)}
.

Let us comment on the fact that the expansion (
5.14
) is related to the ansatz (
3.9
), specifically to the assumption that the free data approaches
{\cal I}^{+}_{-}
with a
u-
power expansion. As mentioned in
footnote 3
, other expansions can be considered. This is the case of
[
28
]
, for which the free data approaches a well defined function
A_{i}^{(0,0)}
faster that any
u^{-n}
. This choice, however, would not alter equation (
5.14
), but only the falloff of the integrable quantity, preserving the validity of the renormalization process. A different expansion is proposed in
[
62
]
, namely

A_{i}^{(0)}=A_{i}^{(0,0)}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\hat{A}}_{i}^{(0,n)}\frac{(\log u)^{n-1}}{u^{n}}+\dots\,,

(5.21)

which instead would modify the equation (
5.14
), and therefore would lead to some differences in the renormalization procedure, whose analysis we leave to a future work.

Ultimately, this process leaves us with well-defined quantities, thus showing that it is possible to renormalize the presymplectic potential to make the asymptotic charge finite while accommodating arbitrary powers of the asymptotic parameter.

The symplectic renormalization procedure is a powerful tool that allow us to study higher order asymptotic symmetries and deal with divergences. This approach suggests the possibility of discovering an infinite set of asymptotic charges, broadly meaning an infinity of infinitely-many conserved charges. It is crucial, however, to emphasize that we used a specific prescription: to cancel the divergences by means of ambiguities while leaving unaltered the finite parts, which enter in the definition of asymptotic charges. However, it is clear that there are residual ambiguities that might further constraint the number of physical asymptotic charges. We could find more general forms for
\Upsilon
and
\Xi
to cancel some finite parts of
\theta^{t}
. In particular, we did not use the residual term
\delta\Xi_{(0)}^{t}
.

6
Conclusions

In this work, we analysed
{\cal O}(r^{N})
one- and two-form asymptotic symmetries. We began by revisiting electromagnetism, which was studied in detail in
[
28
]
. We focused only on the soft part of the charges, with the aim of confronting it with the two-form ones, and we chose a slightly different setup w.r.t.
[
28
]
, mostly concerning to the expansion of the quantities involved. At any rate, our results are in agreement with those of
[
28
]
, in all places where comparisons were possible. We then moved to
{\cal O}(r^{N})
two-form asymptotic symmetries. Up to the symplectic renormalization procedure, we found
N
independent asymptotic charges, with each charge parametrised by an arbitrary function of the angles. The result for the
{\cal O}(r)
case is particularly interesting, since it is the charge one would naively expect for the subleading soft scalar theorem in the spirit of
[
22
]
.

These results leave space for several future investigations. First, it would be interesting to repeat our analysis considering sources of various form, thus providing the hard part of the asymptotic charges. As mentioned, we think that this would require particular attention in the falloffs analysis. Nonetheless, let us remark that near spatial infinity, the interacting theory should be well-described by the free theory. In this limit, we expect the charge expressions (
4.48
) to be valid in the interacting theory as well.

Another enticing perspective is to investigate higher-order asymptotic symmetries in other gauges. In this respect, let us remark that
{\cal O}(1)
two-form asymptotic symmetries were analysed in radial gauge in
[
7
]
, providing the surprising result of a vanishing charge. Our interpretation of this fact is that, in order to reach the radial gauge condition
B_{r\mu}=0
, we employ a physical part of the gauge parameter
\lambda_{\mu}
, which would contribute to the asymptotic charge. In fact, let us observe that we are able to find instead a nonvanishing asymptotic charge if working in “subleading radial gauge”, namely

B_{r\mu}=B_{r\mu}(u,z,{\bar{z}})

(6.1)

exactly. In this case, in fact, we gauged away only the subleading part of
B_{r\mu}
, leaving a
{\cal O}(r)
residual gauge parameter, which contributes to the charge
Q^{(0)}_{B}
.
To our knowledge, higher-order asymptotic symmetries in electromagnetism were discussed only in Lorenz gauge. Let us propose here a possible extension to the radial gauge condition, which is reached imposing

A_{r}=0\,,

(6.2)

leaving a
{\cal O}(1)
gauge parameter
\epsilon(u,z,{\bar{z}})
. Then, the
u-
dependent part of
\epsilon
is typically employed to reach the retarded radial gauge, characterised by the extra condition
A_{u}|_{{\cal I}^{+}}=0
. Our opinion is that, to impose retarded radial gauge we are using a physical part of
\epsilon
, corresponding to all the subleading charges. If we consider only the radial gauge condition, we can for instance assume a
u-
expansion for
\epsilon

\epsilon^{(0)}=\sum_{n=0}^{N}u^{n}\epsilon^{(0,n)}+\text{ subleading in }u\,.

(6.3)

This parameter would lead to
u-
divergences in the charge, which require to be renormalized, but also to some new finite terms that could correspond to the subleading asymptotic charges
Q_{e}^{(n)}
. For gravity,
{\cal O}(r^{2})
asymptotic symmetries were studied also in Bondi gauge in
[
63
]
.
In this respect, let us observe that the Lorenz gauge is natural from a double-copy viewpoint
[
55
]
and therefore, given the result
[
33
]
, the comparison between the one- and two-form results is quite natural in this gauge.

Another possible generalisation involves exploring arbitrary spacetime dimension
D
and generic
p-
forms, with the aim of exploring the duality between
p-
and
(D-p-2)-
forms from the perspective of asymptotic symmetries.

Additionally, we seek to better understand the potential connection between the
{\cal O}(r^{n})
asymptotic charge and the sub
n
-leading soft theorem. In this respect, the existing duality between the two-form and a scalar in
D=4
is particularly appealing, given the relative simplicity of scalar theories. To discuss this connection, we need to deal with the interacting theory, and therefore the inclusion of sources becomes crucial. Furthermore, this would also help in clarifying the role of the residual ambiguities. A deeper understanding of these terms is in fact crucial, as it would help to determine the physical significance of asymptotic symmetries at every order and to explore the limitations of symplectic renormalization.

B.1
F_{ur}
.

Let us start employing the complete gauge fixing mentioned in the text. Using the subleading integration functions we reach a setup with

\nabla^{\mu}A_{\mu}=0,\qquad\Box A_{\mu}=0,\qquad A_{u}^{(1)}=0,\qquad A_{r}^{(n,0)}=0,

where

\epsilon

First, we solve the order-by-order equation
\Box A_{u}

(B.2)

and use
A_{u}^{(1)}=0

A_{u}^{(2)}=A_{u,c}^{(2)}(z,{\bar{z}})
and the equation gives

(B.3)

with

A_{u}^{(n,m)}=\frac{1}{2^{m}}\frac{(n-m-1)!}{m!(n-1)!}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left[\Delta+i(i+1)\right]A_{u,c}^{(n-m)}\,.

(B.4)

This result is exact at
{\cal I}^{+}
. Now we can use the other gauge conditions to determine a relation between
A_{u,c}^{(n-m)}
and
A

The equations

and

B.2

Let us now discuss the component
