Paclitaxel hypersensitivity reaction rates in gynecologic and medical oncology patients following slow titration protocol: A quality improvement project
Authors: Riley Miller, Allison R. Walker, Dereje Negatu, Jenna Synnamon, Ashley Graul, Sarah P. Huepenbecker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & objectiveaTo assess whether slow infusion of paclitaxel decreases hypersensitivity reaction (HSR) rates.MethodsThis was a pre/post-intervention quality improvement study at a single academic-community hybrid institution. This study retrospectively reviewed patients receiving standard rate paclitaxel between 10/2023–10/2024, then implemented a paclitaxel slow infusion protocol (1 mL infusions at rates of 10 mL/h, 20 mL/h, and 30 mL/h, followed by standard rate of 167 mL/h) between 2/2025–8/2025. The primary outcome was HSR rate; secondary outcomes were HSR grade and paclitaxel rechallenge. Descriptive statistics, univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted.ResultsA total of 460 patients (n = 320 pre- and n = 140 post-intervention) were analyzed, with 34% (n = 155) treated by a gynecologic oncologist and 66% (n = 305) by a medical oncologist. Sixty-nine percent of patients were female, and 79% of patients were receiving paclitaxel as part of a neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy regimen. Primary malignancies included lung (27%), breast (20%), ovary (17%), and uterus (17%). The paclitaxel HSR rate was 7% in the pre-intervention vs 10% in the post-intervention group (p = 0.042). HSR rates were significantly increased in medical oncology patients (4% vs 10%, p = 0.034), but not gynecologic oncology patients (12% vs 10%, p = 0.62). There was no difference in HSR grade (p = 0.12) or paclitaxel rechallenge (p = 0.31) between cohorts. In multivariate analysis, the post-intervention group had higher odds of HSR (OR = 1.72, 95% CI 1.001–2.96, p = 0.049) as did female patients (OR = 3.02, 95% CI 1.44–6.36, p = 0.004).ConclusionSlow infusion of paclitaxel resulted in significantly more HSRs among all comers. As a result, this institution has resumed standard paclitaxel administration.

Introduction

Paclitaxel is a common cytotoxic chemotherapy used in the management of multiple cancer types including gynecologic cancers. Hypersensitivity reactions (HSRs) are often observed in patients being treated with paclitaxel, with the incidence ranging from 10 to 40% (Dubinsky et al., 2022; Banerji et al., 2014). HSRs are attributable to the Cremophor component of paclitaxel and typically occur within the first two lifetime exposures, often within 30 min of the infusion starting (Dubinsky et al., 2022; Banerji et al., 2014). One systematic review of 25 studies investigating taxane HSRs found that 87.4% of paclitaxel HSRs occurred during the patients' first or second exposures, 7.2% during the third through eighth exposures, and 3.7% after the eighth exposure (Villarreal-González et al., 2023). Symptoms of HSR include flushing, chest and throat tightness, and anaphylaxis (Thangwonglers et al., 2023). Beyond the immediate life-threatening nature of reactions, HSR to paclitaxel can negatively affect patient experiences and can lead to treatment interruption, regimen modification, or permanent discontinuation of chemotherapy (ALMuhizi et al., 2022).

Slower titration of the first two lifetime doses of paclitaxel has been demonstrated to reduce the risk of HSR in some studies, but the literature is mixed (Goldfeld et al., 2026; Lynch et al., 2023; Sefah et al., 2023; Jabaley et al., 2024; Ferrigni et al., 2021). After years of using an institution-wide titrated paclitaxel infusion protocol, Goldfeld et al. studied the impact of a return to non-titrated infusions on paclitaxel HSR rates in adult gynecologic oncology patients. This retrospective study revealed that the titrated paclitaxel infusion protocol was associated with lower HSR rates as well as decreased reaction severity. Specifically, paclitaxel HSR rates in titrated infusions were 11.9% compared to 20.8% in non-titrated infusions (Goldfeld et al., 2026). Given this finding, this study implemented the slow titration protocol that was used by Goldfeld et al.

Currently, there are no standardized recommendations for a slow paclitaxel titration protocol in gynecologic oncology patients. Prior to this study, this institution did not standardly administer paclitaxel with a slow titration protocol. This single-institution quality improvement study aimed to quantify the HSR rates before and after the implementation of a slow paclitaxel titration protocol in gynecologic and medical oncology patients. The study hypothesis was that slow titration of paclitaxel would result in decreased paclitaxel HSR rates.

Materials and methods

A pre/post quality improvement study was completed at one academic-community hybrid institution. This study was approved by the hospital network Institutional Review Board (IRB-FY2025–130). Patient consent was not required per IRB review.

First, patients in the pre-intervention cohort were retrospectively reviewed to quantify the baseline paclitaxel HSR rates in oncology patients. The inclusion criteria were defined as patients with a clinically and pathologically confirmed malignancy, who received their first or second lifetime dose of paclitaxel between 1 October 2023 and 31 October 2024. Patients with indeterminate prior paclitaxel treatment status and patients who received two or more doses of paclitaxel prior to 1 October 2023 were excluded. Baseline paclitaxel HSR rates were calculated as the number of paclitaxel HSRs in the first or second lifetime exposure divided by the total number of first and second lifetime exposures.

Next, the paclitaxel slow titration protocol was designed with multidisciplinary input from gynecologic oncology, medical oncology, and infusion staff, and teaching was provided at all infusion centers. The slow titration protocol was initiated for all oncology patients throughout the hospital network between 3 February 2025 and 3 August 2025, defining the post-intervention cohort. Data on the post-intervention cohort, which included patients with a clinically and pathologically confirmed malignancy who received their first or second lifetime dose of paclitaxel between 3 February 2025 and 3 August 2025, was prospectively collected. The prospective study period was determined by institutional and resource constraints, including the available time and personnel required for prospective data collection and monitoring. Similar to the pre-intervention cohort, patients with indeterminate prior paclitaxel treatment status and patients who received two or more doses of paclitaxel prior to 3 February 2025 were excluded.

The pre-intervention group received their patient-specific paclitaxel dose in 500 mL of 0.9% sodium chloride infused at the standard full rate of 167 mL/h. Infusion lines were not primed. In the post-intervention group, a stepwise slow-infusion protocol was used, consistent with protocol previously utilized by Goldfeld et al. (Goldfeld et al., 2026) This protocol began with sequential 1 mL infusions at rates of 10 mL/h, 20 mL/h, and 30 mL/h, followed by infusion at the full standard rate. All lines were primed with medication at patient-specific concentrations prior to administration of the drug in the slow titration group. In both the pre- and post-intervention groups, institutional premedication protocols were followed which included: intravenous dexamethasone, famotidine 20 mg IV, and diphenhydramine 25 mg IV. For dexamethasone dosing, patients who received paclitaxel every 21 days were pretreated with 20 mg of dexamethasone, while patients receiving paclitaxel every 7 days were given 10 mg. Although this was the institutional premedication protocol, adherence to the specific regimen was not independently verified for each patient, and minor variations in premedication may have occurred.

The primary objective of this study was to compare paclitaxel HSR rates during the first or second lifetime drug exposure before and after implementation of a slow paclitaxel infusion protocol. Secondary outcomes included HSR grade, paclitaxel rechallenge, and HSR treatment. There is no institutional protocol specifying criteria for paclitaxel rechallenge following HSR, and the decision to rechallenge was left to the discretion of the treating provider. Data collected regarding the paclitaxel HSR included whether a reaction occurred, the lifetime exposure that the HSR occurred on, HSR treatment (diphenhydramine, famotidine, steroids, epinephrine, transfer to emergency department, admission to hospital, ICU admission, intubation, or infusion discontinuation as only management), volume of paclitaxel administered prior to HSR, and whether the patient received any other chemotherapy the same day following the HSR. Additional details were recorded including whether chemotherapy was prescribed by a gynecologic oncologist or a medical oncologist, as well as whether the patient experienced a HSR to platinum-based chemotherapy or to aprepitant. Differences in patient populations, chemotherapy regimens, and prescribing patterns may exist between gynecologic and medical oncology practices. Therefore, prescriber specialty was considered a potentially relevant clinical factor that could be associated with the occurrence or management of HSRs. Cancer and chemotherapy demographics including primary malignancy, stage of cancer, disease treatment indication, and number of prior lines of systemic treatment were recorded. Cancer of the ovary included ovarian, fallopian tube, and primary peritoneal cancers. Other cancers included cancers of the cervix, vagina, vulva, tonsil, tongue, thyroid, supraglottis, larynx, oropharynx, nasopharynx, hypopharynx, pharynx, stomach, skin, oral cavity, vocal cords, anus, peritoneum (male), parotid gland, liver, bladder, unknown primary, or not otherwise specified cancers. Patient demographics including age, gender, race, ethnicity, body mass index (BMI), tobacco use, medical comorbidities, and number of non-paclitaxel allergies were also recorded. All patient demographics and data were obtained from the Electronic Medical Record and managed using a secure REDCap database.

In effort to quantify slow infusion protocol compliance by the infusion nursing staff, as well as to obtain staff feedback on this quality improvement study and infusion protocol, a survey was distributed to all of the infusion center nurses and managers in December 2025 after completion of the intervention but prior to data analysis. Distribution included email invites to an online secure, anonymous REDCap survey and posted flyers in the infusion centers with QR codes linked to the survey. Data collected included staff role, perceived benefit of the protocol, estimation of personal adherence to the protocol via a 1 to 100% slider scale, and barriers to adherence to administration of the drug via the protocol (see survey questions in Table 4). Survey respondents could select multiple responses for questions assessing perceived benefits and barriers to adherence; however, staff role and self-estimated adherence were limited to a single response.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. Continuous variables were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR) because normality assumptions were not met, as assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. Associations between demographic and clinical characteristics and the intervention (slow paclitaxel titration) were evaluated using appropriate statistical tests based on variable type and distribution. For continuous variables, the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test; when expected cell counts were insufficient, Fisher's exact test was used. For categorical variables with more than two categories requiring Fisher's exact test, analyses were performed using the Fisher test function in R (version 4.5.1). The effect of slow paclitaxel titration on hypersensitivity reactions (HSR) was first assessed using univariate logistic regression, followed by multivariable logistic regression adjusting for potential confounders. All statistical analyses for univariate and multivariable logistic regression were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 30.0.0.0 (172). A two-tailed p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The cohorts were similar with respect to distribution of age, sex, race, and tobacco use status (Table 1). The median age was 66 years (IQR 59–75 years). Thirty-four percent of patients were receiving treatment with a gynecologic oncologist and 66% with a medical oncologist. Among the two cohorts, primary malignancies included lung (n = 90, 28% pre-intervention vs. n = 33, 24% post-intervention), breast (n = 62, 19% vs. n = 28, 20%), ovary (n = 50, 16% vs. n = 30, 21%), uterine (n = 56, 18% vs. n = 22, 16%), esophageal (n = 33, 10% vs. n = 9, 6%), and other cancers (n = 29, 9% vs. n = 18, 13%) (Table 1). The most common stage of cancer in both the pre- and post-intervention cohorts was stage III (n = 131, 41% pre-intervention vs. n = 54, 39% post-intervention, p = 0.21). Seventy-nine percent of patients (n = 251, 78% pre-intervention vs. n = 110, 79% post-intervention, p = 0.90) were receiving neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy, and most patients had received no prior lines of systemic treatment prior to the paclitaxel regimen (n = 264, 83% pre-intervention vs. n = 112, 80% post-intervention, p = 0.62). There were no significant differences between the two cohorts regarding primary malignancy, cancer staging, disease treatment indication, or prior lines of systemic treatment (Table 1).

The pre-intervention slow titration cohort experienced significantly lower rates of HSRs to paclitaxel compared to the post-intervention group (7% vs. 10%, p = 0.042; see Supplemental Materials for HSR rate calculations). Paclitaxel HSR grade distribution did not differ significantly between groups, with 78% of patients experiencing grade 2 HSRs (n = 30, 77% pre-intervention vs. n = 22, 79% post-intervention; p = 0.12) (Fig. 1). Paclitaxel rechallenge occurred in 36% of patients overall, with no statistically significant difference between cohorts (n = 12, 31% pre-intervention vs. n = 12, 43% post-intervention, p = 0.31). In the pre-intervention cohort, the median paclitaxel volume administered prior to the HSR was 28.4 mL, while the median volume in the post-intervention group was 3 mL (p = 0.001). However, the paclitaxel volume infused prior to HSR was unknown in 59% in the pre-intervention group and 57% in the post-intervention group. Additionally, monthly variations in HSR rates were observed in the post-intervention group, with the highest observed rate occurring in February (Supplemental Materials). On univariate analysis, patients in the post-intervention had significantly higher odds of experiencing a paclitaxel HSR (OR = 1.80, 95% CI 1.06–3.07, p = 0.030) compared to the pre-intervention cohort (Table 3). Furthermore, females had significantly higher odds of HSRs (OR = 3.33, 95% CI 1.60–6.84, p = 0.001) compared to male patients. Older age was also associated with lower odds of paclitaxel HSR (OR = 0.98 per year, 95% CI 0.96–0.998, p = 0.031); however, this association was not maintained in the multivariable analysis. On multivariable logistic regression, the post-intervention group maintained significantly higher odds of HSR (OR = 1.72, 95% CI 1.001–2.96, p = 0.049) even after adjusting for sex, age, race, hyperlipidemia, and atrial fibrillation. Females also maintained higher odds of HSR (OR = 3.02, 95% CI 1.44–6.36, p = 0.004), holding other factors constant. Multivariate analysis determined that age, White/Caucasian race, hyperlipidemia, and atrial fibrillation did not significantly increase a patient's odds of experiencing a paclitaxel HSR.

In regard to the infusion staff survey, there were a total of 35 responses recorded from 8 different infusion centers with a survey response rate of 29% (Table 4). Eighty-nine percent of respondents were infusion nurses, and the remaining 11% were supervisors. Fourteen percent viewed the slow infusion protocol as beneficial, whereas 54% felt it was not beneficial, and the remaining 31% were unsure. Of respondents who felt the protocol was beneficial (n = 5), 90% felt it decreased the number of HSRs, and 60% felt it decreased HSR severity. The median estimate of frequency of protocol adherence was 98% (IQR 50%–100%). Discussion

A paclitaxel slow infusion protocol was successfully implemented; however, the protocol was found to be significantly associated with an increase in paclitaxel HSR rates. Ultimately based on the study results, this institution reimplemented the initial pre-intervention infusion protocol without the slow-titration.

Based on the available literature, this appears to be the first study to directly compare the impact of a slow titration paclitaxel protocol on hypersensitivity reaction rates in gynecologic versus medical oncology patients at a single institution. The difference in HSR rates between gynecologic and medical oncology patients raises the possibility that the effect of a slow-infusion titration may vary according to the clinical characteristics and treatment context of the patient population, rather than being attributable solely to differences in sex. Although the gynecologic oncology cohort consisted only of female patients, whereas the medical oncology cohort included patients of mixed sex, differences between these groups may also reflect variation in tumor types, prior treatment exposures, concomitant medications, chemotherapy regimens, and other clinical factors that may influence the risk of HSRs. This highlights the importance of evaluating infusion protocols within specific clinical contexts rather than assuming uniform effects across all oncology patients.

This study results add to a growing body of literature on paclitaxel infusion protocols and HSR rates, of which prior published studies demonstrate mixed results. The slow infusion protocol was based on a retrospective cohort study exclusively in gynecologic oncology patients (n = 150, 282 infusions) conducted by Goldfeld et al. In this study, paclitaxel HSRs occurred in 11.9% of titrated versus 20.8% of non-titrated infusions. The difference was even more pronounced during the second infusion (8.4% titrated vs. 22.4% non-titrated) (Goldfeld et al., 2026). However, this study differed from the current study in that it included only gynecologic oncology patients, and its sample size was smaller.

Furthermore, Sefah et. al found that a standardized paclitaxel titration protocol using stepwise infusion rates of 10, 25, 50, and 100 mL/h for 5 min each before transitioning to the full rate significantly reduced hypersensitivity reactions (4.8% titrated vs. 18.3% non-titrated) (Sefah et al., 2023). This study was similar in size to the current study, with 451 total patients. While the study included both gynecologic and medical oncology patients, it did not differentiate between the two groups when reporting HSR rates. Similar to Sefah et. al, Jabaley et al. observed a significant reduction in HSRs using a three-step titration protocol compared with non-titrated administration (6% titrated vs. 19% non-titrated) (Jabaley et al., 2024). In their study, taxane chemotherapy was infused at the following stepwise rates: 2.5 mL at 10 mL/h, followed by 5 mL at 20 mL/h, and then continuation at the full infusion rate. The study size of Jabaley et al. was larger than the current study, with 1122 total patients. Additionally, it evaluated the effects of titration on both paclitaxel and docetaxel, while the current study included only paclitaxel. Therefore, differences in protocol design, such as the shorter infusion times at each titration step and fewer escalation steps, may have contributed to the higher hypersensitivity reaction rates observed.

Conversely, one retrospective study by Ferrigni et. al found that a titrated paclitaxel protocol (starting at 25 mL/h, with rate doubling every 20 min until reaching the maximum infusion rate of 167 mL/h) resulted in higher rates of infusion reactions during first two exposures compared to non-titrated protocols (20% titrated vs. 2% non-titrated) (Ferrigni et al., 2021). While this study included both gynecologic and medical oncology patients, all titrated plans involved patients with gynecologic malignancies only. Additionally, the total number of patients was smaller than the current study population (189 patients vs. 460 patients).

In regard to the finding of higher paclitaxel HSR rates in female as compared to male patients, this is congruent with the literature. In a study of patients with lung cancer, females were 2.81 times more likely to develop a paclitaxel HSR than males (95% CI 1.29–6.11) (Puanfai et al., 2022), a finding consistent with the present results. Although the underlying mechanism remains incompletely understood, sex-based differences in pharmacokinetics may contribute to this increased risk. It has been shown that paclitaxel is primarily eliminated from the body via hepatic metabolism and biliary excretion. In a population analysis of 168 solid-tumor patients, male patients were shown to have a 20% higher paclitaxel maximal elimination capacity after accounting for body surface area, age, and bilirubin (Joerger et al., 2006). Earlier peripheral compartment saturation and reduced elimination capacity in women may lead to higher effective tissue exposure to paclitaxel, potentially increasing immune-mediated hypersensitivity reactions (Joerger et al., 2006). These sex-related differences in paclitaxel drug metabolism suggest one possible reason for the higher prevalence of paclitaxel HSRs among female patients.

Strengths of this study include its real-world, network-wide protocol implementation across oncology specialties, which enhances applicability to routine clinical practice. Restricting inclusion to patients receiving their first or second lifetime dose of paclitaxel minimized confounding related to prior exposure and focused the analysis on the period of highest hypersensitivity risk. The cohorts were well characterized and balanced with respect to demographic, clinical, and cancer-related variables, and the use of multivariable logistic regression allowed adjustment for key potential confounders. Additionally, incorporation of an infusion staff survey provided insight into protocol adherence, feasibility, and operational barriers.

However, limitations should be acknowledged. The post-intervention cohort was smaller than the pre-intervention cohort. Additionally, the shorter study window in the post-intervention group raises the possibility of seasonal variation in HSR rates. Documentation of paclitaxel volume administered prior to hypersensitivity reactions was incomplete in a substantial proportion of cases, limiting interpretation of reaction timing and dosage effects. For patients who experienced a paclitaxel HSR but were not rechallenged, subsequent treatment decisions, including whether they were transitioned to an alternative chemotherapy regimen or discontinued chemotherapy altogether, were not recorded. Additionally, among patients who underwent rechallenge, outcomes of rechallenge, including whether the rechallenge was successful or unsuccessful, were not systematically recorded. The lack of this data limits the study's ability to assess the effectiveness of rechallenge strategies in this study population. Furthermore, while standard institutional premedication protocols were followed in both cohorts, premedications could be manually modified within the order set, and variation in the specific premedications administered to individual patients may therefore have occurred. This potential variability may have introduced unmeasured confounding when comparing hypersensitivity reaction rates between cohorts. Lastly, protocol adherence and hypersensitivity reactions were self-reported by staff rather than objectively measured, and variability in implementation across infusion centers may have influenced outcomes.

The goal of this study was to optimize paclitaxel infusion titration rates to decrease hypersensitivity reactions. However, a slow infusion titration protocol resulted in increased HSR rates. Ultimately, this institution reimplemented the initial pre-intervention protocol without the slow-infusion rate. Future directions of this study could include performing subset analyses of the impact of the slow-infusion protocol specifically among women and gynecologic oncology patients to determine whether specific patient populations may benefit from tailored titration protocols and to identify the most appropriate infusion strategies for each population. Given the higher HSR rates observed in women, further investigation into sex-based pharmacokinetic differences may inform individualized infusion or premedication strategies. Additionally, future studies may focus on refining paclitaxel infusion protocols by evaluating alternative titration strategies, including additional titration steps or longer infusion intervals. Improved real-time documentation of infusion volume at reaction onset would enhance understanding of HSR timing and mechanisms. Overall, these findings highlight the need for continued research to better understand and mitigate paclitaxel hypersensitivity reactions, which remain a significant clinical challenge.
