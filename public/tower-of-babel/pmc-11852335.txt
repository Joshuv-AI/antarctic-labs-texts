A successful post-surgical treatment of cardiogenic shock using Impella via brachiocephalic artery
Authors: Ai Sakai, Kenji Iino, Yoshitaka Yamamoto, Hirofumi Takemura
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with low-flow, low-gradient severe aortic stenosis and ischaemic cardiomyopathy are at risk for postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock and have a poor prognosis. Although Impella has emerged as a bridge therapy, traditional approaches for Impella insertion are infeasible in patients with peripheral vascular diseases. We successfully managed postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock in a patient with low-flow, low-gradient severe aortic stenosis and ischaemic cardiomyopathy and limited vascular access by introducing Impella via the brachiocephalic artery. Impella may enable the surgical treatment of high-risk patients.Keywords: Impella, Low-flow, low-gradient severe aortic stenosis, Ischemic cardiomyopathy, Coronary artery bypass grafting, Postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock

INTRODUCTION

Patients with low-flow, low-gradient severe aortic stenosis (LFLG AS) and ischaemic cardiomyopathy (ICM) are at high risk for postoperative cardiogenic shock and poor prognosis, often necessitating perioperative mechanical circulatory support [1]. Impella improves perioperative low cardiac output syndrome by providing haemodynamic support and left ventricular unloading, leading to favourable outcomes in these high-risk patients [2]. However, successful Impella deployment requires careful vascular access planning, especially in those with peripheral vascular diseases [3]. We present successful management of postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock in a patient with LFLG AS and ICM and limited vascular access by introducing Impella via the brachiocephalic artery.

CASE PRESENTATION

Informed consent was obtained for publication. A 72-year-old man with a history of chronic renal failure and multiple percutaneous coronary interventions was admitted for surgery following the detection of coronary artery and valvular diseases.

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed LFLG AS (Fig. 1A) and left ventricular ejection fraction of 18%. Coronary angiography revealed triple-vessel coronary artery disease. Computed tomography revealed stenosis of the bilateral subclavian and iliac arteries (Fig. 1B) and calcification of the distal ascending aorta (Fig. 1C), aortic valve and coronary arteries. Surgical aortic valve replacement and coronary artery bypass grafting were planned, but since weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) was expected to be difficult for postcardiotomy cardiogenic shock, we decided to introduce Impella via the less calcified brachiocephalic artery (Fig. 1D). CPB was commenced by cannulating the superior and inferior vena cava, as along with the ascending aorta (Video 1). Clampless on-pump beating coronary artery bypass grafting with 4 grafts was performed. Right axillary cannulation was performed directly, using a 14F cannula (PCKC-A-14, Senko Medical Instrument Mfg, Co, Ltd, Tokyo, Japan), with the Seldinger technique and connected to a bifurcated circuit. The brachiocephalic artery was clamped proximally and distally during anastomosis of the 10-mm prosthesis, maintaining blood flow to the right vertebral and carotid arteries via the right axillary artery cannula (Fig. 2A). The calcified valve was replaced with a 21-mm Avalus Bioprosthesis (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA). Before CPB weaning, the patient was diagnosed with left heart failure, presenting a high pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 22 mmHg, necessitating the introduction of Impella. An Impella 5.5 (Abiomed, MA, USA) was placed in the left ventricle via the 10-mm prosthesis (Fig. 2B). The circulatory support rate was P6 (3.5 l/min) on Impella 5.5. The patient maintained a pulmonary artery pulsatility index above 1 with inhaled nitric oxide and dobutamine; thus, Impella alone sufficed for weaning off CPB without needing venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO). The Impella flow rate stayed above 3.0–4.2 l/min (P6–P8) until postoperative day (POD) 2, and dobutamine was tapered off. The patient was extubated on POD 2 and weaned off Impella on POD 3. The ICU stay lasted 5 days. Transthoracic echocardiography showed left ventricular ejection fraction improvement to 31%, and CT angiography revealed all patent grafts on POD 10. The patient was transferred for rehabilitation on POD 30 with no complications.

DISCUSSION

Classical LFLG AS represents an advanced stage of aortic stenosis characterized by myocardial fibrosis and dysfunction. Tessari et al. [1] reported that the 30-day and 1-year mortality rates following surgical aortic valve replacement in patients with classical LFLG AS were 14.6% and 43.8%, respectively. Coronary artery disease, extremely low mean gradient and lack of contractile reserve were identified as major risk factors influencing perioperative and long-term outcomes [1]. Our patient had LFLG AS with ICM, wherein the left ventricular ejection fraction was reduced to 18% and the mean pressure gradient was considerably reduced to 19 mmHg, making this a very high-risk case for surgical aortic valve replacement.

Common complications associated with Impella include vascular complications [3]. Our patient’s bilateral subclavian arteries and ascending aorta were highly calcified, rendering insertion through these sites infeasible. Therefore, a less calcified brachiocephalic artery was selected for access, and the patient was treated without peripheral arterial or cerebral complications. Tadokoro et al. [4] reported that single-access venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation and Impella via a branched vascular graft anastomosed to the brachiocephalic artery allowed for the management of patients with narrow peripheral arteries. Our cannulation method to perfuse the axillary artery into the right vertebral and carotid arteries is less time-consuming and allows its removal without leaving a prosthesis.

CONCLUSION

Impella support is beneficial in managing high-risk patients with LFLG AS and ICM. However, careful selection of access sites for Impella is essential to minimize complications. Further case accumulation is needed to validate the benefits of Impella in this high-risk population.
