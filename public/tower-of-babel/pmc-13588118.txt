Toward the HER-WELL Framework: A Qualitative Mind-Map Study of Wellness Dimensions Among Women in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Heba Yaagoub AlNujaidi, Saja A Al-Rayes, Arwa Alumran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study explores the wellness dimensions of Saudi women and their alignment with Swarbrick’s Eight Dimensions of Wellness framework, aiming to develop a provisional, culturally grounded wellness framework that reflects Saudi women’s lived experiences and values.MethodsAn exploratory qualitative design, grounded in a constructivist paradigm, employed participant-generated mind maps as the primary data collection method, with a purposive, maximum-variation sample of 18 Saudi women, ensuring diversity across age groups (18–65 years) and socioeconomic strata. Sessions lasted 45–90 minutes, during which participants collaboratively constructed, with researcher facilitation, pencil-based mind maps to visually represent and prioritize their wellness experiences. Data analysis followed a hybrid thematic analysis approach, which systematically integrates deductive coding, guided by the Eight Dimensions of Wellness framework, with inductive coding to identify themes that emerge beyond the existing framework. The wellness dimensions were then ranked and visualized using heatmaps, with descriptive statistics (median, IQR) calculated for each dimension.ResultsEight wellness dimensions emerged: seven aligned deductively with the framework (physical, emotional, social, spiritual, home environment [culturally adapted from environmental], occupational [incorporating intellectual], and financial), while Mental Wellness was inductively derived as a prominent addition. Islamic/Spiritual Wellness ranked highest, followed by Physical and Emotional Wellness. Participants emphasized family roles, cultural expectations, and the mental load of dual caregiving and occupational responsibilities.ConclusionSaudi women’s wellness is distinctively shaped by spirituality, family centrality, and dual-role burden. These findings are presented as a preliminary, culturally situated wellness framework, the HER-WELL (Holistic Empowerment and Resilience–Wellness) framework, that offers a foundation for future research, multidisciplinary healthcare practice, and empirical validation.Keywords: women’s wellness, wellness dimensions, cultural adaptation, qualitative research, mind mapping, Saudi women, HER-WELL framework, eight dimensions of wellness

Introduction

The importance of women’s wellness is multifaceted, encompassing physical, psychological, and socio-economic dimensions that significantly impact individual women and broader societal outcomes, influencing everything from family dynamics to national demographic trends.1,2 However, health, wellness, well-being, and quality of life are related but distinct constructs and should not be used interchangeably. Health refers to multidimensional physical, mental, and social functioning and is not limited to the absence of disease. Wellness refers to an active, dynamic, and multidimensional process through which individuals engage with personal, social, and environmental resources to pursue balance and a fulfilling life. Well-being concerns subjective experiences and evaluations of life, including happiness, meaning, psychological functioning, and life satisfaction. Quality of life is a broader evaluative construct reflecting individuals’ perceptions of their position in life within their cultural and social contexts, including health, psychological state, social relationships, independence, and environment.3–7 In our previous conceptual review of wellness models, we used a tree metaphor to illustrate these interrelationships: health represents the roots or foundational conditions supporting functioning; wellness represents the trunk and branches, reflecting ongoing practices and balance across life domains; and well-being represents the fruit, reflecting subjective fulfillment and flourishing.8 This distinction highlights that women’s wellness cannot be inferred from health status alone, because it is also shaped by lived experiences, relationships, opportunities, responsibilities, and social environments. This study focuses on wellness specifically because, unlike wellbeing or quality of life, it denotes an active, modifiable process amenable to intervention and policy actions.

Women’s wellness extends beyond reproductive health to encompass women’s overall well-being, particularly in developing countries, where women face additional health challenges and disparities.9 Obstacles persist in providing comprehensive care that extends beyond pregnancy, including psychological well-being, which is influenced by determinants such as family structures, cultural contexts, and socio-economic conditions.2 The implications of women’s wellness on socio-economic factors are considerable. Women’s wellness is crucial to overall societal well-being, affecting fertility rates, early childhood development, and national progress. Notwithstanding advancements, women frequently prioritize familial responsibilities and professional commitments over their well-being, underscoring the necessity for heightened awareness and improved access to mental health resources.10 A multidimensional approach is therefore needed to understand women’s wellness in relation to both individual experiences and the social contexts in which women live.

Saudi Arabia is a deeply religious society in which Islam functions as a comprehensive way of life, shaping daily routines, social expectations, family responsibilities, sources of meaning, and understandings of health and well-being.11 Religious and cultural, family, and socioeconomic contexts influence access to healthcare, education, and community support systems essential for promoting women’s health and wellness.12,13 Habib et al14 reported significant gender differences in healthcare access in Saudi Arabia, further illustrating the importance of examining wellness within its social and structural context. Spiritual wellness may therefore be especially important for many women. Islamic teachings and practices, including prayer, fasting during Ramadan, charitable giving (zakat), reflection, and family connection, may support spiritual, emotional, and social well-being.15–17 Nevertheless, the personal meaning and influence of religion may vary across women and should not be assumed to be uniform. In this study, religion is considered an important contextual influence that may interact with women’s individual circumstances, family relationships, socioeconomic resources, and changing social opportunities.

Saudi culture is strongly influenced by Islamic values that emphasize family ties (silat al-rahim), communal solidarity, and mutual support.18 Family networks may provide security, belonging, practical assistance and emotional support, thereby contributing positively to women’s social and emotional well-being.19,20 At the same time, family roles and obligations may also create demands related to caregiving, household management, social expectations, and negotiating personal autonomy.21 A culturally situated understanding of women’s wellness should therefore consider both the supportive and potentially demanding dimensions of family and social relationships.

Alongside this religious and cultural foundation, Saudi Arabia is undergoing substantial social and economic transformation under its Vision 2030 framework, which seeks to diversify the economic landscape and improve the living standards of its community, with a specific emphasis on advancing women’s roles across multiple sectors.22 The “Vibrant Society” pillar of Vision 2030 emphasizes healthy lifestyles alongside physical, psychological, and social well-being, supported by initiatives that reflect a broader approach to health and wellness.23 Saudi Arabia ranks 34th globally and 2nd in the Middle East-North Africa region in terms of its wellness economy, with the predominant sectors comprising nutritious dietary practices, physical exercise, and personal care.24 It is not geography or economic transformation alone, however, that shapes Saudi women’s wellness, but the intersection of religious, cultural, family, economic, and social factors together. National initiatives and broader economic indicators do not necessarily translate into uniform wellness experiences. Saudi women are a heterogeneous population whose experiences may differ according to age, marital and parental status, employment, education, socioeconomic circumstances, health status, household conditions, geographic location, and access to social support.

To guide this exploration, the present study draws on Swarbrick’s eight dimensions of wellness framework—physical, emotional, social, spiritual, environmental, occupational, financial, and intellectual wellness.25 This framework was selected on the basis of a prior systematic review by the author AlNujaidi et al,8 which compared thirteen wellness frameworks and found Swarbrick’s model to be the most comprehensive in dimensional coverage and among the few with documented empirical validation, while also noting that the framework does not explicitly address dimensions unique to women’s needs, such as reproductive health and caregiving responsibilities, and has not been culturally adapted, a limitation the present qualitative study was designed to address. This enabled us to examine whether Saudi women’s accounts aligned with the original dimensions and whether they identified culturally relevant meanings, combinations, adaptations, or additional dimensions that were not fully represented in the original framework. The framework recognizes wellness as multidimensional encompassing eight domains: physical wellness refers to caring for the body; emotional wellness to recognising and managing feelings; social wellness to supportive relationships and community; spiritual wellness to meaning, purpose, and faith; environmental wellness to healthy and safe surroundings; occupational wellness to satisfaction and balance in paid or unpaid work; financial wellness to security and effective management of resources; and intellectual wellness to curiosity, learning, and cognitive engagement. However, the framework was not used in this study as a fixed universal structure to be statistically tested. Rather, it served as a sensitizing framework and organizing lens for qualitative inquiry.

Although wellness is increasingly recognized as important to women’s health, there remains limited qualitative evidence on how Saudi women themselves conceptualize wellness, prioritize wellness domains, and experience the interaction of spirituality, family life, caregiving, environment, work, and financial circumstances. This study therefore addresses a gap in the literature by developing a participant-informed and culturally situated understanding of wellness among adult Saudi women. Such evidence may contribute to future research and to the development of more contextually appropriate approaches to women’s wellness.

Accordingly, this exploratory qualitative study used a participant-generated mind map approach to examine Saudi women’s conceptualizations of wellness. Mind mapping was selected because it is a participant-centered visual method that enables women to identify, organize, and prioritize the factors they perceive as influencing their wellness. This approach reduces reliance on researcher-imposed categories at the initial stage of data collection and allows participants to represent relationships among wellness factors in their own words and visual structure. Furthermore, unlike interviewer-led formats such as semi-structured interviews, mind mapping allows participants to independently structure and prioritize concepts before elaboration, reducing the sequencing and emphasis effects that an interviewer’s question order can introduce.26,27 This study aimed to identify the factors Saudi women perceive as supporting or undermining their wellness, and to explore how these factors align with, extend, or challenge Swarbrick’s Eight Dimensions of Wellness framework. The findings are presented as a preliminary, culturally situated wellness framework referred to as the HER-WELL (Holistic Empowerment and Resilience–Wellness) framework that requires further conceptual refinement and empirical validation. This qualitative study does not report the development or validation of the HER-WELL Scale; rather, its findings informed subsequent stages of scale development, content validation, and construct validation reported elsewhere,28,29 and may further inform multidisciplinary healthcare practice and policy for women’s wellness in Saudi Arabia.

Research Methodology

Research Design

This exploratory qualitative study is grounded in a constructivist qualitative paradigm, which assumes that wellness is subjectively experienced and socially constructed and that meaning is best understood through participants’ representations of their lived experiences. It aims to examine the phenomenon of wellness among Saudi women, with the goal of (1) identifying the key factors influencing Saudi women’s wellness and (2) examining how these factors align with, extend, or challenge Swarbrick’s Eight Dimensions of Wellness framework. Participant-generated mind maps served as the primary data collection method, employed with adult women across three age groups and socioeconomic strata. Mind maps exhibit two key features that enhance their utility in qualitative data collection. Firstly, they emphasize a central concept while branching out to related ideas and examples. Secondly, they encourage free-form expression and creativity, allowing participants to develop them with limited guidance from the researcher. This approach facilitates the emergence of the participant’s unique creative processes.26 This design was selected because it privileges participant-generated data over researcher-imposed categories, thereby ensuring that wellness dimensions emerged organically from Saudi women’s own conceptualizations rather than being constrained by existing Western frameworks.26

Sampling Strategy

This study’s target population was Saudi women aged 18 to 65, representing a broad spectrum of socioeconomic statuses. Purposive sampling ensured that participants reflected a range of socioeconomic statuses, marital statuses, and age groups.30 Utilizing this sampling matrix, the study aims to comprehensively represent Saudi women across different age groups and societal roles, enabling a rich exploration of wellness factors from multiple perspectives. A sampling matrix was employed to guarantee participant diversity31 concerning age groups (18–29 years, 30–49 years, and 50–65 years) and socioeconomic status (Low, Middle, and High), yielding a 3×3 matrix of nine categories.

Data collection was conducted in two iterative stages. In the first stage, one participant was recruited per matrix category, yielding an initial sample of nine participants (1 per category × 9 categories). Following preliminary analysis, it became evident that thematic saturation, the point at which no new themes or codes emerge from the data, had not been fully achieved across all categories.32 Accordingly, one additional participant per category was recruited in a second stage, bringing the total final sample to 18 participants (2 per category × 9 categories). This iterative approach ensured that no socioeconomic group or age group was underrepresented and that the final dataset provided a comprehensive, saturated exploration of wellness dimensions across the full sampling matrix. This iterative approach was designed to strengthen thematic depth and ensure that no socioeconomic group or age group was underrepresented. Given the modest per-category sample size (n=2), we present this as an enhancement of thematic richness rather than a formal claim of data saturation, which the present design is not powered to establish with confidence.

Data Collection Process

Recruitment Strategies for Different SES Groups

The researchers used a planned sampling matrix to ensure variation in socioeconomic status (SES) among participants. Saudi women were recruited via an online survey hosted on QuestionPro and distributed using snowball sampling: an invitation link was first shared with initial contacts who met the inclusion criteria, and they were asked to forward it to other eligible Saudi women in their social networks. The survey collected sociodemographic and socioeconomic information, enabling the researchers to determine each woman’s age and calculate her SES score. Of the 106 women who completed the survey and agreed to participate, SES levels were determined using the 33rd and 66th percentiles of total SES scores: scores of 10 or lower indicated low SES, 11–15 indicated middle SES, and 16 or higher indicated high SES, based on access to income, education, and housing (Appendix 1).

Eligible women from each SES category were then randomly selected from this matrix and invited to participate in the study. They were contacted using the telephone numbers provided in the recruitment survey, ensuring each eligible woman had an equal chance of being invited. Each selected woman was invited to choose a convenient venue and time for data collection. Mind-map sessions typically lasted 45–90 minutes, depending on participants’ availability and the depth of engagement in the discussion. The first author, a public health researcher with a master’s degree in research methods and prior training and experience in qualitative research and working with women in clinical and community settings, conducted all sessions. Sessions were conducted in Arabic, the primary language of participants; the first author translated all quotations presented in this manuscript into English, and a second team member reviewed them to preserve semantic and cultural meaning.

Data Collection Session Structure

Session Procedure

The data collection process involved conducting mind-map sessions scheduled according to participants’ preferences and time availability. At the agreed-upon venue and time, the first author conducted the session by welcoming the participant, confirming eligibility using basic sociodemographic details (age, marital status, number of children), and briefly explaining the study and the process before proceeding. A semi-structured guide was developed in line with the study aim and the Eight Dimensions of Wellness framework to support the facilitation of the mind-map sessions. The session began with one broad, open-ended question asking participants what factors positively or negatively affect their general wellness. Participants then completed the mind map at their own pace, while the researcher used probing questions to encourage clarification and elaboration, such as asking what type of healthy food they meant and why they believed it positively affected wellness. The session was designed to be flexible, allowing for in-depth discussions while following a structured framework based on the three sections outlined in the session protocol (Appendix 2). Beginning with an open discussion on wellness and mind-mapping, then introducing and ranking the eight wellness dimensions, and ending with brief reflections and closing thanks.

Section 1: Mind Mapping

Mind map data collection enables a thorough exploration of participants’ experiences.33 The mind-map sessions comprised two sequential stages. In the first stage, participants constructed a mind map documenting their own thoughts and reflections directly onto the map with minimal instruction from the researcher, allowing their individual perspectives to emerge freely. In the second stage, the researcher used the participant’s mind map as a guide, asking follow-up questions to explore and elaborate on the participant’s written content, ensuring that the session remained grounded in participant-generated content rather than researcher-imposed questions.27 Sessions were not audio-recorded, as several participants expressed discomfort with being recorded when discussing personal and family-related issues, and the ethics committee recommended a data-minimization approach.34 The mind maps constituted the primary data source for analysis, an approach that has been shown to yield themes consistent with those derived from traditional transcription.26,27

Participants began with a mind-mapping exercise to visually represent their wellness experiences, using a blank template titled “My Wellness”, with branches for positive and negative influences. During the session, the researcher asked open‑ended follow‑up questions (eg, “Why is this important for your wellness?”, “What does this mean for you?”, “Can you explain further”). Participants were provided with pencils, allowing them the freedom to erase and rewrite their thoughts as they reflected on their experiences. I, as the researcher, also held a pencil during the sessions, offering assistance as participants completed the map. For instance, if a participant wrote, “My family affects my wellness”, I would draw an arrow and ask them to explain further, encouraging them to elaborate. This interactive process enabled a deeper exploration of the connections among the themes and sub-themes, enriching the analysis and understanding of Saudi women’s wellness. The evolving map served both as a visual elicitation tool and as a prompt for deeper probing, so that brief written entries (single words or short phrases) were expanded and clarified by participants’ additional written comments, which were later incorporated into the thematic analysis. Participants were invited to review and clarify their maps throughout the session to strengthen the credibility and trustworthiness of the data.35 Consistent with the open-ended nature of the guiding question asking what factors positively or negatively affect participants’ general wellness. Participants were free to draw on past as well as present experiences. As a result, some entries reflect circumstances that differ from a participant’s current status as recorded in Table 1 (eg, a retired participant reflecting on factors from her working life, or a currently married participant recalling a past period of marital separation); these entries were retained as valid data on the basis that lived experience, not only present circumstance, shapes wellness. After completing the mind map, they ranked the eight wellness dimensions (Appendix 3).

Section 2: The 8 Dimensions of Wellness Ranking

In the 8 Dimensions of Wellness Ranking, participants were introduced to the following dimensions of wellness: physical, emotional, social, spiritual, environmental, occupational, financial, and intellectual. Each dimension was explained to the participants using a template titled “Definitions of the eight dimensions” (Appendix 4). After reading the explanation, participants were asked to rank the dimensions from 1 to 8 based on their significance in their lives, with 1 being the most important and 8 the least. If participants believed a particular dimension did not reflect or impact their lives, they were instructed to rank it as 0.

Section 3: Reflections

In this section, participants reflect on their understanding of wellness. After a ranking section and sharing personal experiences, participants provide additional insights into their meaning of wellness.

Data Analysis

This study employed a hybrid thematic analysis approach,36 which systematically integrates deductive and inductive coding within a single analytical process. Grounded in a constructivist paradigm, this approach was selected because it allowed the Eight Dimensions of Wellness framework to serve as an a priori theoretical lens while simultaneously remaining open to themes emerging organically from participants’ own wellness conceptualizations, a dual orientation consistent with the exploratory purpose of this study. The mind map responses collected during the mind map sessions on Saudi women’s wellness were analyzed following this hybrid approach, enabling systematic and in-depth exploration of participants’ experiences.36,37

Deductive Phase

First, the first author repeatedly reviewed each mind map to become familiar with the data, identify participants’ main themes and sub-themes, and note positive and negative influences on wellness. All brief, to-the-point entries (single words or short phrases) (see Appendix 5) were treated as verbatim data and subjected to open/initial coding, in which similar statements were labeled and grouped deductively within each of the eight wellness dimensions as defined by Swarbrick’s (2012) framework. Participants’ written mind map entries were manually transcribed into Microsoft Excel spreadsheets and organized by wellness dimension. The first author then manually grouped entries by conceptual similarity within each dimension, for example, consolidating entries related to physical wellness before any computational assistance.

Inductive Phase

Codes were then compared across mind maps and examined for patterns that extended beyond or diverged from the eight-dimension framework, using inductive analysis to allow new themes to surface from the data itself.36 This inductive phase directly yielded the identification of Mental Wellness as an emergent ninth dimension not captured by the original framework, reflecting a cognitive and psychological burden specific to Saudi women’s lived experiences.

Integration of deductive and inductive findings: Once both phases were complete, deductively derived codes and inductively identified themes were systematically compared, reconciled, and synthesized into a unified thematic structure representing Saudi women’s holistic wellness experience.36 This integration ensured that the final thematic framework was simultaneously grounded in established theory and responsive to participant-generated data. In this integration phase, two conceptual consolidations were made relative to the original eight Swarbrick dimensions and the additional categories considered during early-stage coding (Appendix 6A and B). First, Intellectual Wellness was merged with Occupational Wellness, as participant references to lifelong learning, skill development, and professional growth consistently occurred within occupational contexts rather than as a standalone concern. Second, an early analytic category labelled “Cultural Wellness” capturing participant entries related to family control and interference, customs and traditions, gender-based treatment differences, and freedom in decision-making was consolidated into Social Wellness, as these entries substantively overlapped with participants’ accounts of family dynamics, patriarchal structures, and cultural obligations already coded within the Social Wellness domain (reported in the Results as the “Culture strength and constraints” subtheme). Rather than constituting a distinct dimension, cultural norms and constraints were understood as one substantive facet of the broader social wellness domain, consistent with participants’ own framing of these experiences as embedded in family and social relationships. This yielded the final eight-dimension structure reported throughout the Results and Discussion: Physical, Emotional, Social (incorporating Cultural), Spiritual, Home Environment (adapted from Environmental), Occupational (incorporating Intellectual), Financial, and Mental Wellness.

Once entries were organized by dimension, the grouped, de-identified written content was shared with a generative AI tool (ChatGPT) as a labeling aid, using a prompt asking how many subthemes could be identified in the material. No participant identifiers or raw session data were shared; only the researcher-organized written entries were provided per dimension. The first author then reviewed, selected, adapted, or rejected the suggested subtheme labels, and the research team confirmed all final subtheme structures through discussion.

The use of a generative AI tool as a labeling aid in this study is methodologically analogous to the use of AI-assisted coding functions in established qualitative software such as NVivo, which employs similar language model capabilities to suggest codes and themes from researcher‑organized text.37,38 The key distinction in both cases lies not in whether AI is involved, but in the degree of human oversight applied, a principle increasingly recognized in emerging guidance on AI use in qualitative research. In the present study, AI involvement was deliberately bounded: it was applied only after the researcher-led manual grouping of participant-generated written entries was complete, and all suggested labels were critically reviewed, adapted, or rejected by the research team before finalization. Furthermore, the nature of the data, concise participant-written mind‑map entries rather than complex spoken interview transcripts, rendered sophisticated software such as NVivo unnecessary and disproportionate to the analytical task. Mind‑map entries are inherently structured, brief, and visually organized by participants themselves, meaning that the primary analytical work of identifying conceptual groupings had already been performed by participants when constructing their maps.26,27

Interpretive and analytic decisions therefore remained entirely with the human researchers throughout; ChatGPT functioned solely as a supplementary labeling aid applied after researcher-led grouping and coding had already been completed. These subcategories were iteratively refined into broader themes and subthemes, and the first author critically reviewed and modified all suggestions to ensure that the final themes accurately reflected the participant data. Data were coded and organized in Microsoft Excel spreadsheets for each dimension, facilitating pattern and frequency analyses and enabling identification of the most important dimensions and subthemes.

The first author led the coding and theme development, and a qualitative research supervisor reviewed code groupings and thematic summaries; any disagreements in code or theme placement were resolved through discussion between the first author and the supervisor until consensus was reached, and no disagreements required third-reviewer arbitration. An early illustrative draft of this coding process, prior to final theme and subtheme consolidation, is presented in Appendix 6A and B. Finally, participants’ rankings of wellness dimensions were integrated with the thematic patterns, and visual tools (a combined mind map and a heat map) were created to summarise key themes, dimensions, and their relative importance for Saudi women’s wellness. The identified themes and subthemes were interpreted in direct relation to the central research question, how Saudi women conceptualize and experience wellness across the eight dimensions, ensuring that the analysis remained anchored to the study’s purpose rather than producing generalizable typologies.

Quotation provenance: Illustrative quotations presented throughout the Results were selected to represent the range and diversity of participant responses within each subtheme, prioritizing clarity, directness, and fidelity to participants’ own wording; quotations were not selected to represent statistical frequency or to imply that a given view was held by all or most participants.

Researcher reflexivity: The first author, a Saudi female public health researcher, conducted all data collection sessions and led coding and interpretation. This positionality may have facilitated participant rapport and openness when discussing culturally and personally sensitive topics (eg, family conflict, religious practice, financial strain), while also carrying a risk of shared cultural assumptions going unexamined. To mitigate this, code groupings and thematic summaries were independently reviewed by two research supervisors, and any interpretive disagreements were resolved through discussion, providing a degree of critical distance from the first author’s own cultural and professional perspective.

To enhance trustworthiness, the analysis employed several strategies to support credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability.39 Credibility was supported by a participant-led, mind-map-based data collection process with open-ended follow-up questions and probing, allowing participants to explain their experiences in their own words. Dependability was strengthened by clearly and systematically documenting the recruitment, the SES sampling matrix, the session procedure, and the analytic steps. Confirmability was supported by grounding themes in participants’ mapped responses and applying a structured coding process using the Eight Dimensions of Wellness Framework while still allowing new themes to emerge inductively. Transferability was enhanced by providing a detailed description of the Saudi context, participant characteristics, and recruitment strategy, enabling readers to assess the applicability of the findings to similar settings. Bias was reduced by using a planned sampling matrix, selecting participants from different SES groups, and asking the same broad opening question to all participants; the researcher also used probing questions consistently to avoid leading participants while still allowing elaboration. Data verification was supported by the entire research team, who carefully checked the mind-map entries during analysis, compared codes across participants, and revisited the original responses when refining themes.

Ethical Considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The Institutional Review Board of Imam Abdulrahman bin Faisal University (IAU-IRB) granted ethical approval under reference number IRB-PGS-2024-03-631. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before they engaged in the study. Participants were fully informed of the research objectives, the voluntary nature of their participation, their right to withdraw at any time without consequence, and their right to ask questions before and during the session. Participants’ written informed consent explicitly included permission for the publication of anonymized responses and direct quotations. Confidentiality was maintained by securely storing all identifiable information in password-protected files accessible only to the first author, in accordance with the university’s ethical guidelines. The use of a generative AI tool ChatGPT (GPT‑4o; OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA) as a supplementary labelling aid, described in the Data Analysis section, was applied only to de-identified, researcher-organized written entries; no raw session data, mind maps, or participant identifiers were shared with the tool, consistent with the data-minimization approach approved by IAU-IRB.

Each age group included six participants, equally distributed across low, middle, and high SES levels, as shown in Table 1.

Mind Map Analysis: Exploring Key Themes in Saudi Women’s Wellness

The mind map analysis was utilized to investigate themes affecting the wellness of Saudi women through qualitative data insights. This method enabled the visualization of interrelated themes and enhanced comprehension of wellness-related factors. Given that data were collected through participant-constructed mind maps, extracts presented throughout the results reflect participants’ exact written entries, which are characteristically brief; this conciseness is intrinsic to the method and reflects participants’ own prioritization of meaning rather than a limitation of the analysis.26

The analysis revealed seven themes based on the eight dimensions of wellness and one additional theme identified through inductive reasoning. These emergent themes represent factors not initially accounted for in the existing framework but are critical to understanding the wellness experiences of Saudi women. Theme 1: Physical Wellness

Physical wellness emerged as a recurring and prominent theme in the mind‑map analysis, with participants frequently discussing their health challenges, routines, and overall well-being. This was also reflected in the ranking, where most participants rated it highly important. Many expressed concerns about maintaining physical health and the impact of specific health issues on their wellness. Physical wellness was commonly associated with exercise, diet, sleep patterns, and managing chronic health conditions. As shown in Figure 3.

Physical Wellness.

Exercise and Fitness

Thirteen women emphasized the importance of exercise in maintaining their physical wellness. For example, Participants G and F wrote “Exercise” on their maps as a key component of their wellness routine. Participant I stated, “Exercise is my stress reliever and helps me stay healthy”. Others expressed comparable feelings, with Participant S wrote, “My interest in sports since childhood is an investment in my health and well-being”.

Sleep Quality

Sleep was another primary concern for participants, with around 8 women reporting that poor sleep was affecting their overall health. Participant D mentioned “Sleep disorder” as a critical challenge, while Participant E also reported experiencing “Sleep disorder”. This pattern is reinforced by Participant A, who highlighted how “Few hours of sleep” was a significant contributor to her wellness.

Physical Health

Physical Health: Physical health was a prominent theme, with participants discussing their efforts to maintain well-being and the challenges of ongoing health conditions. Participant M shared, “I am diabetic and have hypertension; I have pain, and I’m not able to get pregnant”, illustrating the impact of chronic conditions on overall wellness. Similarly, Participant R mentioned dealing with “chronic diseases” and how they affect their physical health and daily life. Diet and Nutrition

Some participants also highlighted the role of diet in their wellness. Participant N added, “I try to take care of my health by eating dates, drinking a lot of water, and walking”, showing a proactive approach to maintaining physical health through dietary choices.

Theme 2: Emotional Wellness

Emotional wellness emerged as a prominent theme in the analysis, with participants frequently discussing emotional balance. Seventeen women discussed their emotional wellness. The participants’ experiences reveal a range of positive and negative factors influencing emotional wellness. Family support, personal achievements, and activities for stress relief were identified as significant positive influences. At the same time, loss, self-criticism, emotional depletion, and relationship struggles contributed negatively to their emotional well-being. These contrasting factors highlight the complexity of emotional wellness and the balance individuals must maintain to foster emotional health. As shown in Figure 4.

Theme 3: Social Wellness

Social wellness emerged as a recurring theme, focusing on the quality of relationships and social interactions that influence participants’ emotional and overall well-being. Loyalty to family, especially prioritizing parents’ wishes, is deeply valued. Family dynamics are traditionally patriarchal, with men responsible for income and security while women handle domestic responsibilities. Both the nuclear family and extended family offer emotional and financial support. Elders and parents are highly respected; children typically remain in their family’s home until marriage. These relationships form the basis of social interactions and impact emotional and social wellness. As shown in Figure 5.

Cultural dynamics in Saudi Arabia reveal women’s dichotomy for women between customary practices and an aspiration for autonomy against the backdrop of transforming policy contexts and empowerment efforts. The patriarchal structure historically imposed strict regulations on women’s roles, necessitating male consent for various life decisions. Recent reforms indicate a departure from these entrenched practices.

Several participants described feeling limited by cultural norms, particularly gender roles and societal expectations for women. One participant explicitly expressed her frustration with cultural constraints on her ability to make decisions:

Discussion

This study aimed to identify the key factors influencing Saudi women’s wellness and to examine how these factors align with or diverge from Swarbrick’s Eight Dimensions of Wellness framework. Overall, the findings show that Saudi women’s wellness is structured around eight interrelated dimensions: Physical, Emotional, Social (incorporating cultural norms and constraints identified in early-stage coding), Spiritual, Home Environment (adapted from Environmental), Occupational (incorporating Intellectual), Financial, and Mental Wellness the latter an additional dimension that capture cognitive load and stress related to caregiving, work, and gendered expectations. These findings are presented as a preliminary, culturally situated wellness framework rather than a validated model; claims of establishing or validating a “HER-WELL model” are reserved for the separate construct–validation study reported elsewhere. Islamic/Spiritual, Physical, and Emotional Wellness were the most consistently prioritized domains in participants’ rankings (Table 2), while Social Wellness, though ranked somewhat lower on average, was prominent and recurring across participants’ qualitative accounts of family and relational life.

The hybrid thematic analysis approach employed in this study36 proved well-suited to this dual purpose: the deductive phase confirmed the broad relevance of Swarbrick’s framework to Saudi women’s wellness, while the inductive phase revealed culturally specific adaptations and a novel dimension that the original framework did not anticipate. Moreover, this study’s findings on wellness dimensions for Saudi women highlight both quantitative rankings and qualitative insights, visualized in a tree metaphor. The heatmap rankings reveal the dimensions that participants rated highest, while the mind map (tree metaphor) visually represents how these dimensions interconnect and support holistic wellness.

Taken together, these eight dimensions reflect a wellness landscape distinctively shaped by Islamic values, family-centered social structures, and the sociocultural transitions underway in contemporary Saudi Arabia. This aligns with Alanazi and Alkouatli,40 who found that family, spirituality, and cultural values are key sources of well-being for Saudi women academic leaders.

Islamic/Spiritual Wellness

In Saudi women’s wellness, Spiritual Wellness had the lowest (best) median rank of all eight dimensions (Mdn = 1.5; Table 2), with nearly three-quarters of participants ranking it among their top three priorities, reflecting its central role in participants’ lives. For these participants, spiritual wellness encompasses both Islamic religious practices (prayers, fasting, charity) and broader personal reflection (tafakkur), with no clear separation between religiosity and spirituality. In the mind map, Spiritual Wellness forms one of the primary branches of the wellness tree, symbolizing its foundational role. Islamic practices such as prayer, fasting, and charity are central components of their spiritual wellness. This aligns with Alrukban et al’s41 findings that the rural population’s limitation of spirituality to religious aspects reflects the broader Islamic context, where religious practices are at the core of spiritual wellness. Islamic spirituality is not limited to ritual worship but extends to Meditative Practices; “Tafakkur” Munsoor and Sa’ari42 present a significant viewpoint. They argue that Islamic spirituality is a “living meditation” in which contemplation is fundamental to life. This concept extends beyond traditional worship, integrating silence, contemplation, and mindfulness into daily activities, thereby advancing a unified paradigm for nurturing the soul, intellect, and physical condition. Similarly, in 2024, Bagis et al43 present Islamic spiritual well-being as a unified system that blends ethical practices, self-discipline, and persistent faith engagement to enrich the soul in line with divine principles.

Physical Wellness

Physical Wellness was the second most consistently prioritized dimension (Mdn = 3.0; Table 2), with no participant ranking it below the scale’s midpoint, underscoring its perceived importance in maintaining overall well-being. Consistent with wellness literature, this study found that exercise, sleep, diet, and chronic health management are central components of physical wellness for Saudi women.44,45 Participant quotes vividly illustrate these priorities. Participant I stated, “Exercise is my stress reliever and helps me stay healthy”, while Participant S noted, “My interest in sports since childhood is an investment in my health and well-being”. Sleep concerns were equally prominent, with multiple women (A, C, D, E, F) highlighting “little sleeping hours” and sleep disorders as major wellness threats. Dietary patterns also featured prominently, as seen in Participant N’s comment: “I try to take care of my health by eating dates and drinking a lot of water”. This is supported by the findings of Kaliyaperumal et al,46 which indicate that physical activities like walking and jogging are associated with improved health-related quality of life, reduced stress, and lower anxiety. However, substantial barriers persisted. Participants identified chronic conditions (diabetes, hypertension, hormonal issues; Participants M, A, N) alongside family time constraints and limited women-only facilities. These echo Abdelhay et al,47 reporting barriers among Saudi adults, including lack of facilities (20.2%), long hours (19.6%), family obligations (females OR=4.2), and cultural/religious factors (females OR=4.83, 3.31), aligning with participants’ reports. This aligns with Saudi Vision 2030’s Quality of Life Program, which increased female sports participation from 7.3% (2017) to 20.3% (2021).48 Yet persistent barriers indicate a lack of structural facilitators, underscoring the need for targeted interventions in chronic health management, sleep hygiene, and access to women-only exercise options.

Social and Emotional Wellness

Emotional Wellness (Mdn = 3.0, SD = 2.28) and Social Wellness (Mdn = 5.0, SD = 1.94) differed in both central tendency and variability. Emotional Wellness showed the greatest inter-individual variation of any dimension in this study (Table 2). Qualitative accounts point to a shared underlying mechanism: family relationships. Family emerged as an important factor shaping both emotional and social wellness among Saudi women, as it plays a central role in Saudi society and shapes individuals’ social and emotional support system.49 Family members, children, spouses, and siblings serve as essential sources of joy, support, and emotional stability, fostering social integration. For instance, Fridays are customarily set aside for family gatherings to strengthen connections. Additionally, many households have designated venues, such as a majlis, for discussing vital family issues and hosting social gatherings.50 This aligns with the National Family Strategy in Saudi Arabia, which emphasizes the family as a foundation for societal well-being and personal identity. The strategy highlights the importance of family support systems, grounded in Islamic and cultural values, which nurture social responsibility and cohesion across generations.51 Consistent with this, higher social support has been shown to buffer against psychological distress among Saudi women.52

However, family relationships also present emotional challenges, particularly in cases of loss, illness, or relational strain, highlighting a dual impact: family bonds offer fulfillment, but they can also lead to emotional strain during difficult times. This dual impact of family relationships may help explain the wide variability observed in Emotional Wellness rankings (Table 2): participants whose family relationships were predominantly a source of support may have prioritized this dimension more highly than those experiencing relational strain or loss. Alkhateeb et al and Sulaimani et al53,54 emphasize that family caregiving roles, especially for children with chronic conditions or disabilities, place significant emotional and social strain on caregivers in Saudi Arabia. Moreover, Almehmadi and Alrashed55 support the notion that caregiving responsibilities, especially in Saudi Arabia, can put a significant emotional burden on women. Their study highlights how family caregivers, often women, face challenges when providing informal care for elderly family members. Although friendships offer supplementary support, family remains the predominant source of emotional and social well-being. This cultural focus indicates that wellness frameworks for Saudi women should integrate family-based support systems to harness positive influences while mitigating familial stressors.

Home Environmental Wellness

Environmental Wellness was adapted to Home Environmental Wellness, reflecting its culturally specific significance for Saudi women. In contrast to the broader notion of environmental wellness, which encompasses external and community contexts, Home Wellness primarily emphasizes the home as a fundamental source of solace, emotional equilibrium, and personal identity. This cultural adaptation represents one of the study’s key contributions to the Eight Dimensions framework, demonstrating how a Western-derived dimension requires recontextualization for the Saudi setting. In Saudi culture, housekeeping support plays a distinct role in Home Wellness by alleviating the daily household responsibilities that would otherwise consume women’s time and energy. In Saudi Arabia, around 3,913,925 domestic workers work at home.56 Participants’ accounts suggest that domestic support may reduce household burdens and increase time available for personal and social activities, though this pattern was not universal: participants without housekeeping support did not describe Home Wellness in these terms, indicating that this relationship is likely shaped by socioeconomic access rather than reflecting all Saudi women’s experience equally. Participant H explained: “Having a maid allows me to go out with my friends and attend social gatherings”, while Participant K noted: “Domestic help gives me time for myself and family visits”. With domestic support reducing household duties, women gain more time for personal and social activities.57

Mental Wellness

A notable finding of this study is the emergence of Mental Wellness as a distinct dimension not captured by Swarbrick’s original framework, consistent with the study’s aim of identifying wellness factors prominent in Saudi women’s own accounts, though not necessarily unique to this population, as related constructs (eg, cognitive load, caregiving-related stress) are documented elsewhere in the global wellness literature. Beyond the cognitive and interpretive burden already distinguished in the Results, mental wellness for Saudi women is further shaped by biological and life-stage factors. Expanding on mental wellness, it is essential to recognize that women experience additional stressors that contribute to mood changes, including biological and life-stage-related factors such as postpartum depression,58,59 menopausal depression,60,61 and menstrual-related mood fluctuations.62,63 Moreover, for Saudi women specifically, mental wellness is influenced by various internal and external factors, including family responsibilities, work stress, and societal expectations.40,64 These factors add unique cognitive and emotional burdens that are not fully addressed by emotional wellness alone. Hormonal shifts associated with these phases can affect mental wellness, affecting mood stability, stress levels, and overall mental health.

Financial Wellness

Financial wellness for Saudi women is intrinsically linked to Saudi Vision 2030, which seeks to enhance economic empowerment and workforce participation. Alessa et al65 note that Vision 2030 has implemented policies to expand employment opportunities, reduce income disparities, and increase women’s financial access. This empowerment fosters not only financial independence but also enhances security and autonomy. Further supporting this view, Anandhi and Velmurugan66 emphasize that financial well-being encompasses both objective metrics, such as income and savings, and subjective perceptions of financial security and control. For Saudi women, achieving financial wellness means having a stable income and autonomy in financial decisions, both of which are critical to well-being.

Notably, in 2019, Saudi Arabia permitted women to obtain passports, travel independently, and live alone without permission from male guardian, representing a pivotal advancement in female autonomy.49,67 However, these gains exist in tension with persistent traditional expectations, as some women encounter opposition from conservative family members when exercising newly acquired rights.68 This conflict exemplifies the broader structural negotiation between empowerment and tradition that characterizes Saudi women’s financial and occupational wellness.

Occupational Wellness

The tension between professional participation and familial obligations was particularly prominent in participants’ accounts of Occupational Wellness. This tension was not experienced uniformly: employed participants more often described conflict between work demands and family responsibilities, whereas unemployed and retired participants located comparable pressures within unpaid caregiving and household management rather than paid work, suggesting that Occupational Wellness reflects a broader negotiation of role-based responsibility rather than employment status alone. Greater participation in the workforce, particularly in professional roles, while maintaining household duties, is one of the most visible manifestations of this tension.69 Alotaibi and Aldossry70 examined the challenges working mothers face in high-demand fields like healthcare, where work-family conflicts arise from long hours and job expectations. Al-Hababi71 further emphasizes the need for family-oriented policies to alleviate these pressures. Intellectual Wellness, as articulated by participants through reflections on skill development and professional goals, was closely intertwined with Occupational Wellness in practice. Integrating these two dimensions streamlines the Framework by emphasizing culturally relevant dimensions that affect Saudi women’s holistic well-being.

Implications for Multidisciplinary Healthcare Practice

These findings carry direct implications for multidisciplinary healthcare practice. Recognizing spirituality, family obligation, and mental load as central rather than peripheral wellness dimensions can inform psychosocial assessment, referral pathways, and patient communication that account for religious and family-centered coping strategies among Saudi women. The variability observed within dimensions such as Emotional and Home Environment Wellness further suggests that service design should avoid a one-size-fits-all approach, instead accommodating differences in family circumstance, caregiving burden, and socioeconomic access when structuring assessment tools, referral criteria, and support services. The eight-dimension structure identified here may be useful to multidisciplinary teams as a screening heuristic for holistic assessment; it is not, at this stage, a validated clinical instrument.

Strengths

This study offers several methodological strengths. Sampling was systematic, using defined SES percentiles, with random selection within each category to achieve balance and minimize convenience bias. The pencil-based mind-mapping sessions enabled rich, participant-led data co-construction, capturing nuanced wellness priorities both visually and in writing. Single-group focus (Saudi women) provided deep cultural specificity, while map-based analysis revealed novel hierarchical themes (eg, physical wellness as foundational) not evident in narrative-only methods. These innovations advance qualitative wellness research in Saudi Arabia.

Limitations and Future Directions

Despite thorough design, limitations affect generalizability: Initial snowball sampling to identify participants introduced social network bias due to similarities among recruits. Although SES categories were defined using percentiles with random selection within categories to balance representation, the small non-probability sample limits representativeness beyond participants’ social/geographical networks. Mind mapping risks bias toward women comfortable with writing/visual organization, potentially losing nuance via brief notes. Finally, focusing solely on Saudi women enhances cultural specificity but restricts transferability to other groups or Arab contexts.

Future studies could address these by employing probability-based/stratified sampling across regions/SES or respondent-driven methods; comparing mind‑map-based vs fully narrative/audio-recorded interviews or digital mapping tools; replicating with diverse Saudi women/regions, including men for gender comparisons, and quantitatively validating the refined eight-dimension framework in larger samples. Moreover, Demographic comparisons across age, marital status, employment, and socioeconomic status were beyond the scope of this exploratory qualitative phase; such comparisons will be more appropriately and rigorously addressed in the forthcoming quantitative scale validation study, where a larger and more diverse sample will allow for meaningful subgroup analyses.

Conclusion

This study reveals Saudi women’s wellness as multifaceted, rooted in Islamic spirituality and family ties at its core, with physical wellness foundational, as heatmap/mind map rankings show it is among the most consistently prioritized dimensions (Table 2). Deductive-inductive analysis identified eight interrelated dimensions: Physical, Emotional, Social (incorporating cultural norms and constraints), Spiritual, Home (adapted from Environmental), Occupational (incorporating Intellectual), Financial, and Mental Wellness, reflecting dual caregiving and work roles, cognitive and psychological load, and the influence of Vision 2030-enabled economic opportunity.

Within this structure, empowerment and resilience, the constructs underpinning the HER-WELL name, were evident in participants’ accounts of exercising financial and decision-making autonomy (Financial and Social Wellness) and of actively managing stress and adversity through coping strategies such as faith, exercise, and social support (Mental Wellness). These findings are presented as a preliminary, culturally situated wellness framework requiring further conceptual refinement and empirical validation. As discussed above, the study’s small, non-probability sample and single-network recruitment limit the generalizability of this framework beyond the participants and social networks from which it was drawn.

The HER-WELL framework bridges global wellness theory with Saudi women’s lived realities and may inform rather than prescribe multidisciplinary healthcare practice, including psychosocial assessment, referral pathways, and culturally responsive patient communication, as well as future research and policy discussion on women’s holistic wellbeing in Saudi Arabia. Translation into validated clinical or policy tools awaits construct validation and psychometric testing in subsequent phases of this research program.
