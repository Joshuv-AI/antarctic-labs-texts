Well-posedness and relaxation in a simplified model for viscoelastic phase separation via Hilbertian gradient flows
Moritz Immanuel Gau, Katharina Hopf

Abstract
This article is concerned with a gradient-flow approach to a Cahn–Hilliard model for viscoelastic phase separation introduced by Zhou et al. (Phys. Rev. E, 2006) in its variant with constant mobility. By means of time-incremental minimisation and generalised contractivity estimates, we establish the global well-posedness of the Cauchy problem for moderately regular initial data. For general finite-energy data we obtain the existence of gradient-flow solutions and a stability estimate of weak–strong type. We further study the asymptotic behaviour for relaxation time and bulk modulus depending on a small parameter. Depending on the scaling, we recover the Cahn–Hilliard, the mass-conserving Allen–Cahn or the viscous Cahn–Hilliard equation. A challenge in the well-posedness analysis is the failure of semiconvexity of the appropriate driving functional, which is caused by a phase-dependence of the bulk modulus.



Keywords and phrases:
Cahn–Hilliard equation, viscoelastic phase separation, gradient systems, Cauchy problem, convexity, uniqueness, minimising movements, relaxation limit.

Introduction

Phase separation in a homogeneous binary mixture can occur when the system is quenched to low temperature in a way that a state close to one of the pure phases becomes energetically favourable.
If the constituents of the mixture have different intrinsic time and length scales, the unmixing process can exhibit dynamical asymmetry, a phenomenon typical for
viscoelastic phase separation
(VPS) in polymer–solvent mixtures.
A first phenomenological continuum model for VPS in polymer solutions that adheres to thermodynamic principles was introduced by Zhou et al.
[
ZZE06
]
, building on earlier modelling work by Tanaka and co-workers. For further background on the modelling, we refer to
[
Tan22
]
and the references therein.

In this paper, we study a gradient-flow model for viscoelastic phase separation in polymer solutions, which corresponds to a version of the dissipative system in
[
ZZE06
, Equation (9)]
with a constant mobility (normalised to 1).
Given a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
1\leq d\leq 3
, the equations read as follows:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{u}&=\divv\Bigl(\nabla\Bigl(\frac{\delta\mathscr{E}}{\delta u}-A(u)q\Bigr)\Bigr)\quad&&\text{in }(0,\infty)\times\Omega,\\
\dot{q}&=-\frac{1}{\tau(u)}q-A(u)\divv\Bigl(\nabla\Bigl(\frac{\delta\mathscr{E}}{\delta u}-A(u)q\Bigr)\Bigr)\quad&&\text{in }(0,\infty)\times\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(
VPS
.a)

Here,
\frac{\delta\mathscr{E}}{\delta u}
denotes the variational derivative with respect to
u
of the total free energy

\mathscr{E}(u,q)=\int_{\Omega}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}+F(u)\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}q^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

(
VPS
.b)

where, for simplicity, fixed parameters are normalised.
Denoting by
\mathrm{n}:{\partial{\Omega}}\to S^{d-1}
the outward unit normal to
{\partial{\Omega}}
, system (
VPS.a
) is complemented by no-flux boundary conditions

\nabla\Bigl(\frac{\delta\mathscr{E}}{\delta u}-A(u)q\Bigr)\cdot\mathrm{n}=\nabla u\cdot\mathrm{n}=0\quad\text{on }(0,\infty)\times{\partial{\Omega}}

(
VPS
.c)

and initial values
u(0)=u^{0}
,
q(0)=q^{0}
.

The function
u:[0,\infty)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the
phase field variable
, modelling the concentration of the polymer molecules (i.e.
u\approx 0
indicates solvent-rich regions and
u\approx 1
polymer-rich regions). The other unknown is the bulk stress
qI_{d}
, where we seek for the scalar variable
q:[0,\infty)\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, hereafter referred to as the
scalar bulk stress
. Moreover,
A
models the bulk modulus,
\tau
denotes the relaxation time associated with the bulk stress quantity
q
, and we let
F
be a regular potential with
f:=F^{\prime}
. A typical choice is the double well potential
F(u)=u^{2}(u-1)^{2}
.
Our particular interest lies in the case of phase-dependent
A
and
\tau
, which can cause a dynamic asymmetry between solvent-rich and polymer-rich regions.
This is reflected in (
1.1
) through the concentration flux – which in addition to the gradient of the chemical potential
\nabla(\frac{\delta\mathscr{E}}{\delta u})
has the extra term
\nabla(A(u)q)
– and through the relaxation rate
\frac{1}{\tau(u)}
of the scalar bulk stress.
These simplified systems are able to reproduce key features of VPS related to dynamic asymmetry like
volume shrinking
and
phase inversion
, as confirmed by various numerical simulations
[
ZZE06
,
DLL20
,
Spi+21
,
BEHL24
]
.

This paper is devoted to a rigorous gradient-flow formulation
of (
1.1
) and the development of a well-posedness theory for the associated Cauchy problem, as well as to the derivation of relaxation limits, assuming that
A=A_{\varepsilon}
and
\tau=\tau_{\varepsilon}
depend on a small parameter
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
.
In this context, let us mention the related
viscous Cahn–Hilliard equation
(vCH), introduced by Novick-Cohen
[
Nov88
]
to model polymeric systems with viscous effects and analysed by Elliott and Stuart
[
ES96
]
.
Mathematically, vCH serves as a homotopy interpolating between the Cahn–Hilliard equation (CH) and the mass-conserving Allen–Cahn equation (mAC) proposed by Rubinstein–Sternberg
[
RS92
]
.
The three models CH, vCH, and mAC will reappear in the relaxation limit.

1.1
Related literature

We briefly review the literature on the analysis of related VPS models and the gradient-flow theory relevant to our study, and discuss our contribution in this context.

The existence of global-in-time weak solutions in 2D for a stress-diffusive version of the full fluid VPS system in
[
ZZE06
]
– involving the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations and an evolution equation for the stress tensor – was established by Brunk and Lukáčová-Medvid’ová in
[
BL22a
]
both in the case of regular mobilities and smooth potentials as well as in the case of degenerate mobilities and logarithmic potentials by means of the Faedo–Galerkin technique. (See also
[
BL22
]
for a previous result.)
In the subsequent articles
[
BL23
,
Bru23
]
, a relative entropy method was used to establish weak–strong stability estimates (for
d\in\{2,3\}
), which are of conditional type in the 3D case.
An error analysis via relative entropies of a structure-preserving discrete finite-element scheme for the simplified VPS model without hydrodynamic transport (still in the presence of bulk stress diffusion) was performed in
[
BEHL24
]
under the assumption of a smooth solution to the continuous problem.
We would also like to point out the work by Colli et al.
[
CGLN99
]
, who considered a non-isothermal phase-field system with memory, where temperature accounts for an extra contribution in the concentration flux. Despite the different physical context, this model has a parabolic–ODE structure at the PDE level that is related to that of (
1.1
) (cf. (

VPS
′

) below), even though confined to a phase-independent constant coupling.

Gradient-flow methods have proved a powerful tool in the analysis of Cahn–Hilliard equations and related systems.
The most classical result concerns the global well-posedness of the Cauchy problem for semiconvex, lower semicontinuous driving functionals (or “energies”) in Hilbert spaces (
[
Bré73
]
,
[
Kom67
]
), see also
[
MS20
, Introduction]
for a short review, or
[
ABS24
]
.
In
[
RS06
]
, Rossi and Savaré relax the hypothesis of semiconvexity using, as a key ingredient, Hilbert space-valued Young measures: under a compactness condition on the energy, they develop an existence theory, via minimising movements, for a generalisation of the gradient-flow equation involving a closure of the subdifferential, provided that this limiting subdifferential is either convex-valued or satisfies a suitable chain rule.
In general, this leads to a very weak solution concept, where uniqueness may not be expected. The dissipation mechanism in their approach is still the classical inner product of the underlying Hilbert space.
An extension to state-dependent dissipation in the context of quasi-linear non-degenerate Cahn-Hilliard–Allen-Cahn systems was carried out by Heida
[
Hei15
]
. Again, this work focusses on the existence of generalised solutions.
For an extension of the variational methods in
[
RS06
]
to a Banach space setting and some refinements, we refer to
[
MRS13
]
and to the review in
[
Mie23
]
for further literature.
For some recent developments in the Hilbertian setting, see e.g.
[
HS24
]
in the context of sharp interface evolutions.
Closer in spirit to PDE analysis, there is also an increasing body of literature using gradient-flow techniques for the construction of global weak solutions to Cahn–Hilliard type models both in the non-degenerate case, see e.g.
[
RS17
,
GK20
]
for constant mobility, and in the case of a degenerate mobility
[
LMS12
]
.
Note that, in the degenerate case, the gradient structure is of metric type based on generalised Wasserstein distances.

While, at a purely formal level, the gradient-flow structure of versions of the simplified model
[
ZZE06
, Equation (9)]
was exploited in
[
HKMW24
]
, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, the present work is the first to develop a rigorous gradient-flow interpretation for such a system. At the PDE level, our results imply an (unconditional) well-posedness theory without stress diffusive modification in a setting of moderately low regularity.
With regard to methodology, a specific feature of the present problem lies in the simultaneous failure of compactness and semiconvexity of the energy, which renders the analysis non-standard.
However, observing that semiconvexity only fails to a limited extent, determined by the state-dependence of the bulk modulus, we are able to leverage ideas from
[
AGS08
, Section 4]
and
[
Bré73
]
, extending them to a “slightly non-semiconvex” situation. This is enabled by combining gradient-flow with PDE methods, which provide the required extra regularity.
Outside the semiconvex case, a systematic application of the gradient-flow theory to the study of (unconditional) uniqueness properties and stability estimates does not appear to have been explored extensively in the literature. In a very broad sense, our approach may be seen as reminiscent to the Wasserstein gradient-flow problem
[
BMZ23
]
, where a singular cross-diffusive perturbation is introduced that destroys uniform displacement convexity of the energy, but where exponential convergence rates to equilibrium can still be recovered under a smallness condition.
Let us mention that an analysis of the gradient structure for VPS in the case of a degenerate mobility will be part of a future work.
Our results on the relaxation limit furnish a rigorous link between (
1.1
) and classical models for phase separation, the Cahn–Hilliard, the viscous Cahn–Hilliard, and the mass-preserving Allen–Cahn equation and extend the latter two to dynamically asymmetric versions.

2.1
Basic concepts for Hilbertian gradient systems

We briefly review basic facts and notions, confining ourselves to the aspects most relevant to the present application. For further developments, we refer the interested reader to
[
MRS13
,
Pel14
,
MMP21
,
Mie23
,
SP23
]
.

Definition 2.1

(Gradient system)
.

We call a triple
(\mathcal{H}_{m},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{R})
a
(Hilbertian) gradient system
if

(i)

\mathcal{H}_{m}
is an affine space over a Hilbert space denoted by
H
,

(ii)

\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{H}_{m}\to[0,+\infty]
a functional with proper domain
\dom(\mathcal{E}):=\{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{H}_{m}\mid\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})<\infty\}
,

(iii)

\mathcal{R}:\dom(\mathcal{E})\times H\to[0,\infty)
can be represented as
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{v})=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{H^{*},H}
for a family of linear, bounded, symmetric and positive definite operators
\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}):H\to H^{*}
,
\boldsymbol{u}\in\dom(\mathcal{E})
.

We immediately see that
\boldsymbol{v}\mapsto\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{v})
is a positive bounded quadratic form with Fréchet differential
\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{v})=\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{v}
for
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\in\dom(\mathcal{E})\times H
.

Gradient systems are used to describe dissipative evolution equations. The affine space
\mathcal{H}_{m}
then represents the state space,
\mathcal{E}
is the driving functional, which in our present model will be the physically relevant free energy, and
\mathcal{R}
is called the dissipation potential, describing the dissipation mechanism of the system. The evolution equation associated to a gradient system
(\mathcal{H}_{m},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{R})
is the
gradient-flow equation

\displaystyle-\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(t)\in\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in(0,\infty)

(
GFE
)

for absolutely continuous functions
\boldsymbol{u}:(0,\infty)\to\mathcal{H}_{m}
, where
\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{E}
denotes the Gateaux subdifferential

\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})=\biggl\{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in H^{*}\;\bigg|\;\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in H:\liminf_{t\searrow 0}\frac{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}+t\boldsymbol{v})-\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})}{t}\geq\langle\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{H^{*},H}\biggr\}.

(2.1)

Note that, for
\boldsymbol{u}\in H
fixed,
\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})\subset H^{*}
is a closed and convex (possibly empty) subset.
In classical applications, (
GFE
) is often formulated using the Fréchet subdifferential

\partial^{\mathrm{F}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})=\biggl\{\boldsymbol{\mu}\in H^{*}\;\bigg|\;\liminf_{\boldsymbol{v}\to\boldsymbol{u}}\frac{\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v})-\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})-\langle\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{H^{*},H}}{\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{H}}\geq 0\biggr\}.

(2.2)

However, the analysis of the present manuscript relies on the more general version (
2.1
), which reduces to (
2.2
) if the state
\boldsymbol{u}
enjoys some extra regularity of (cf. Proposition
3.4
(ii)).

Definition 2.2

(Gradient-flow solution)
.

Let
(\mathcal{H}_{m},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{R})
be a gradient system. We call an absolutely continuous curve
\boldsymbol{u}:(0,\infty)\to\mathcal{H}_{m}
a
gradient-flow solution
, in short
GF solution
, if it satisfies (
GFE
).

A desirable feature of gradient-flow solutions, which holds true in the smooth case, is the
energy–dissipation balance
(EDB). A pair
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\mu})\in\mathrm{AC}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}([0,\infty);\mathcal{H}_{m})\times\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}([0,\infty);H^{*})
is said to satisfy the EDB if

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))+\int_{s}^{t}\bigl(\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{u}(r);\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(r))+\mathcal{R}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u}(r);\boldsymbol{\mu}(r))\bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(s)),\quad 0\leq s\leq t<\infty,

(
EDB
)

where
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\mu}):=\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in H}\bigl(\langle\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{H^{*},H}-\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{v})\bigr)
denotes the Legendre–Fenchel conjugate of
\mathcal{R}
, and
\mathrm{AC}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}(I;\mathcal{H}_{m})
for a general interval
I\subseteq[0,\infty)
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{AC}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}(I;\mathcal{H}_{m}):=\bigl\{\boldsymbol{u}:I\to\mathcal{H}_{m}\text{ locally absolutely continuous}\;\big|\;\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}(I;H)\bigr\}.

For instance, (
EDB
) may play the role of the second law of thermodynamics in isothermal systems, so as in our model (
1.1
). From a technical point of view, (
EDB
) (or (
EDI
) below) is a practical identity to derive a priori estimates in certain scenarios, as in the proof of Theorems
2.6
and
2.9
.

To ensure (
EDB
), one typically shows a chain rule of the following type (cf., e.g.
[
MRS13
,
(2.\mathrm{E}_{4})
]
or
[
Mie16
, Definition 3.3.1]
): If
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{AC}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}([0,\infty);\mathcal{H}_{m})
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}([0,\infty);H^{*})
are such that
\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)\in\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))
for almost all
t\in(0,\infty)
, then
\mathcal{E}\circ\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{AC}([0,\infty);\mathbb{R})
and

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(\mathcal{E}\circ\boldsymbol{u})(t)=\langle\boldsymbol{\mu}(t),\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(t)\rangle_{H^{*},H}\quad\text{for a.e. }t\in(0,\infty).

Thus, if
\boldsymbol{u}
additionally satisfies (
GFE
), then such a chain rule can be applied to
\boldsymbol{\mu}:=-\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u})\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}
and then Legendre–Fenchel equivalences
[
Rin18
, Theorem 3.32]

\langle\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u})\dot{\boldsymbol{u}},\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\rangle_{H^{*},H}=\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{u};\dot{\boldsymbol{u}})+\mathcal{R}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{\mu})=\langle\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{\mu}\rangle_{H^{*},H}

(2.3)

imply (
EDB
).

In the absence of smoothness and convexity, an appropriate chain rule may not always be established. However, in such cases, it is often still possible to prove the weaker
energy-dissipation inequality
(EDI)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))+\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{u}(r);\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(r))+\mathcal{R}^{*}(\boldsymbol{u}(r);\boldsymbol{\mu}(r))\bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\leq\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(0)),\quad t\in[0,\infty).

(
EDI
)

It is worth noting that, if a suitable chain rule holds after all, the formulations (
GFE
), (
EDB
) and (
EDI
) are in fact equivalent; see, e.g.,
[
Mie16
, Theorem 3.3.1]
. This is known as the
energy-dissipation principle
, which is based on ideas from De Giorgi
[
DMT80
,
DeG93
]
.

2.2
Our strategy

Motivation

If
A
and
\tau
are constant, we can apply the well-known theory of gradient flows of
\lambda
-convex (
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
), lower semicontinuous functionals in Hilbert spaces mentioned in Subsection
1.1
.
This guarantees global-in-time existence and uniqueness of gradient-flow solutions, also in the sense of evolution variational inequalities (EVI), and the associated
\lambda
-contractive continuous semiflow extends to data in the closure of
\dom(\mathcal{E})
.
To apply the theory to the present situation, consider

{\mathsf{H}}:=\Bigl\{(u,q)\in{(\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{av}}^{1}(\Omega))^{*}}\times(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))^{*}\;\Big|\;Au+q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\Bigr\}

(2.4)

(see (
2.6
) – (
2.8
) for details to
\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{av}}^{1}(\Omega)
and
{(\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{av}}^{1}(\Omega))^{*}}
) and endow
{\mathsf{H}}
with the scalar product

\bigl((u_{1},q_{1}),(u_{2},q_{2})\bigr)_{\mathsf{H}}:=\langle u_{1},(-\Delta)^{-1}u_{2}\rangle_{{(\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{av}}^{1}(\Omega))^{*}},\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{av}}^{1}(\Omega)}+\tau(Au_{1}+q_{1},Au_{2}+q_{2})_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

Moreover, put
\mathscr{H}_{m}:={(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))_{\mathrm{av}}^{*}}+(m,0)
for some
m\in\mathbb{R}
. Then, under suitable conditions on
F
(for instance as in Assumption
2.3
), the energy
\mathscr{E}
defined in (
VPS.b
) takes proper values on the dense subset
(\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{av}}^{1}(\Omega)+m)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\subset\mathscr{H}_{m}
and is semiconvex with respect to
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathsf{H}}
.
With the (state-independent) dissipation potential
\mathscr{R}(\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{v}):=\frac{1}{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{\mathsf{H}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathbb{I}_{\mathsf{H}}\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle_{{\mathsf{H}}^{*},{\mathsf{H}}}
, the associated gradient-flow equation of
(\mathscr{H}_{m},\mathscr{E},\mathscr{R})
becomes
-\mathbb{I}_{\mathsf{H}}\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathscr{E}(\boldsymbol{u})
, which, at the PDE level, coincides with (
1.1
). Existence and semi-contractivity of the gradient flow defined for initial values in the entire space
\mathscr{H}_{m}
is then a consequence of the classical theory.

Change of variables in phase-dependent case

For phase-dependent
A
and
\tau
, the problem still exhibits a formal gradient structure with driving functional
\mathscr{E}
and dissipation mechanism

\mathscr{G}(u)=\begin{pmatrix}(-\Delta)^{-1}+A^{2}(u)\tau(u)&A(u)\tau(u)\\
A(u)\tau(u)&\tau(u)\end{pmatrix}.

However, the question of determining an appropriate functional setting in the variables
(u,q)
is more involved, since the regularity of the cross terms (corresponding to the off-diagonal terms in
\mathscr{G}(u)
) now depends on that of
u
.
To cope with such coupling, we consider the new variable
z:=q+K(u)
, where
K^{\prime}=A,K(0)=0
, i.e. we introduce the change of variables
\Psi(u,z):=(u,z{-}K(u))
. This leads to the new gradient system
(\mathcal{H}_{m},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{R})
with the pulled-back driving functional
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})=\mathscr{E}(\Psi(\boldsymbol{u}))
,
\boldsymbol{u}:=(u,z)
, and dissipation mechanism
\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u})=\mathrm{D}\Psi(\boldsymbol{u})^{T}\mathscr{G}(u)\mathrm{D}\Psi(\boldsymbol{u})
, which amounts to

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u})=\begin{pmatrix}(-\Delta)^{-1}&0\\
0&\tau(u)\end{pmatrix}.

(2.5)

Note that the dissipation mechanism in the new variables is diagonal and induces a simple functional setting
{(\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{av}}^{1}(\Omega))^{*}}\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
whenever
\tau(u)\sim 1
.

In PDE form, the associated gradient-flow equation
\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{u})\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\in-\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})
reads as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{u}&=\divv\bigl(\nabla\bigl(-\Delta u+f(u)-A(u)(z-K(u))\bigr)\bigr)\quad&&\text{in}&&\;(0,\infty)\times\Omega,\\
\dot{z}&=-\frac{1}{\tau(u)}(z-K(u))\quad&&\text{in}&&\;(0,\infty)\times\Omega,\\
0&=\nabla u\cdot\mathrm{n}=\nabla\bigl(-\Delta u+f(u)-A(u)(z-K(u))\bigr)\cdot\mathrm{n}\quad&&\text{on}&&\;(0,\infty)\times{\partial{\Omega}}.\end{aligned}\right.

(
\mathsf{VPS}^{\prime}
)

Thus, instead of developing directly a solution theory for (
1.1
) in the variables
(u,q)
, we seek a pair
(u,z)
satisfying system (

VPS
′

).
The challenge is that, in contrast to the constant-coefficient case mentioned above, we loose the semiconvexity of the energy.
However, as we will show in Section
3
, under extra regularity assumptions on
(u,z)
, we can derive inequalities that mimic the semiconvex case: crucially, we derive a subgradient estimate of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v})\geq\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})+\langle\delta\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}),\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{H^{*},H}

\displaystyle+\lambda(\boldsymbol{u})\lVert\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{u}\rVert_{H}^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbin{\phantom{+}}\lambda(u,z)=-C(1+\lVert A^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert z-K(u)\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)})^{2}

for
\boldsymbol{u}\in\dom(\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{E})
,
\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})=\{\delta\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})\}
, and an analogous semi-monotonicity estimate for
\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})
.
To exploit such “almost
\lambda
-convexity”, we show that the flow propagates a certain mild regularity using the ODE in the second line in (

VPS
′

).
The extra regularity will ensure that, for solution trajectories
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}(t)
, the function
t\mapsto\lambda(\boldsymbol{u}(t))
is locally integrable in
[0,\infty)
, which turns out to be sufficient for establishing key properties such as local Cauchy estimates at the approximate level as well as the chain rule.

Function spaces, hypotheses, and main results

2.3.1
General notations and hypotheses

We now render precise the functional framework for the gradient system
(\mathcal{H}_{m},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{R})
induced by the transformation
\Psi
. First, we define the averaged spaces

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{av}}^{1}(\Omega)&=\bigl\{\phi\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)\;\big|\;\textstyle\int_{\Omega}\phi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=0\bigr\},\\
{(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))_{\mathrm{av}}^{*}}&=\bigl\{\phi\in(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))^{*}\;\big|\;\langle\phi,1\rangle_{(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))^{*},\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}=0\bigr\},\end{aligned}

(2.6)

and equip them with

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (\phi,\psi)_{\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{av}}^{1}(\Omega)}&:=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\psi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,\\

and

which is an isometric isomorphism by the above choice (

) of inner products. Then,

(2.9)

for regular enough

2.3.2
Well-posedness

To formulate our first main result, we recall Definition
2.2
of a gradient-flow solution associated to a given gradient system. Unless specified otherwise, the underlying gradient system is understood to be that introduced in Subsection
2.3.1
.

Theorem 2.4

(Well-posedness)
.

For any initial data
\boldsymbol{u}^{0}=(u^{0},z^{0})\in(\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{av}}^{1}(\Omega)+m)\times\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, there exists a gradient-flow solution

with
\boldsymbol{u}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}^{0}
. Any such solution

enjoys the regularity
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}([0,\infty);\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega))\times\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1}([0,\infty);\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega))
and satisfies the energy-dissipation balance

\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Bigl(\bigl\lvert\nabla(-\Delta u+f(u)-A(u)(z-K(u)))\bigr\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{\tau(u)}(z-K(u))^{2}\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(s))

(2.16)

for all

.

Moreover, any two gradient-flow solutions
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}=(u_{i},z_{i})\in\mathrm{AC}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}([0,\infty);\mathcal{H}_{m})
with initial values
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}(0)\in(\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{av}}^{1}(\Omega)+m)\times\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
,

, satisfy the stability estimate

\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(t)\rVert_{H}\leq\mathrm{e}^{C\int_{s}^{t}(1+(\lVert A^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}+\lVert\tau^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty})\lVert z_{1}-K(u_{1})\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)})^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(s)-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(s)\rVert_{H}

\beta
,
A^{*}
and
\tau_{*}

The proof of Theorem
2.4
is carried out in Subsection

2.3.3
Relaxation

Beyond the well-posedness theory, we are interested in connecting the viscoelastic system to classical models of phase separation, where elastic effects are neglected.
Observe that, so far, the VPS system was formulated in dimensionless variables and inessential multiplicative constants were set equal to unity. To study relaxation limits, however, we need to take the characteristic size
q_{c}
of the bulk stress variable to be of order
o(1)
.
We will therefore consider a rescaled free energy, which at the level of the original physical variables reads as

\displaystyle\mathscr{\widetilde{E}}(u,q)=\int_{\Omega}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}+F(u)\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{q}{q_{c}}\right)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

and an appropriately rescaled dissipation mechanism

\displaystyle\mathscr{\widetilde{G}}(u,q)=t_{c}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}(-\Delta)^{-1}+A^{2}(u)\tau(u)&q_{c}^{-1}A(u)\tau(u)\\
q_{c}^{-1}A(u)\tau(u)&q_{c}^{-2}\tau(u)\end{pmatrix}.

Note that here
t_{c}>0
denotes a characteristic time scale.
Below, we perform the corresponding change of variables directly in the “diagonal” coordinates
(u,z)
.
We will then make the ansatz
q_{c}=\varepsilon
for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and study the asymptotic behaviour, as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
, for suitable choices of
A=\widehat{A}_{\varepsilon}
,
\tau=\widehat{\tau}_{\varepsilon}
, and

.

For the rigorous asymptotics, we first invoke Theorem
2.6
(with

), which provides gradient-flow solutions
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}
to
(\mathcal{H}_{m},\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon},\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon})
, where
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon}
are given via

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}+F(u)\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\bigl(z-\widehat{K}_{\varepsilon}(u)\bigr)^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

(2.20)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v};\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\lVert v\rVert_{(\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{av}}^{1}(\Omega))^{*}}^{2}+(\widehat{\tau}_{\varepsilon}(u)y,y)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\bigr)

with
\widehat{K}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}=\widehat{A}_{\varepsilon},\widehat{K}_{\varepsilon}(0)=0
.
As explained above, before studying the limit
\varepsilon\searrow 0

.
The appropriate transformation of the dependent variables is given by
\widehat{\Psi}(u,z)=(u,q_{c}^{-1}z)=(\widehat{u},\widehat{z}),
where

.
Combined with the time rescaling under the ansatz

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}(u,z)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}+F(u)\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\int_{\Omega}(z-K_{\varepsilon}(u))^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

(2.21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}^{(\gamma,\kappa)}((u,z);(v,y))

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\varepsilon^{\gamma}\lVert v\rVert_{(\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{av}}^{1}(\Omega))^{*}}^{2}+\varepsilon^{\kappa}(\tau_{\varepsilon}(u)y,y)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\bigr),

(2.22)

where

, and
\varepsilon^{\kappa}\tau_{\varepsilon}(u):=\varepsilon^{\gamma-2}\widehat{\tau}_{\varepsilon}(u)

The goal is then to show that, in some sense, the sequence of solutions in the new variables

, determined by
\widehat{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}(t))=\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon^{-\gamma}t)

of some “effective” gradient system

(see also Remark
2.11
below).
Formula (
2.21
) and the definition of
K_{\varepsilon}(u)
suggest to take

for

bounded away from zero and infinity.

.

\displaystyle\leq A_{\varepsilon}(u)\leq A^{*},

\displaystyle\tau_{*}

Remark 2.11

(Relation to EDB- and EDP-convergence)
.

In the study of singular limits of gradient systems, the notions of
EDB-convergence
and
EDP-convergence
are commonly encountered in the literature, both falling under the umbrella of
Evolutionary
\Gamma
-convergence
.
The core idea of EDB-convergence is to establish the
\Gamma
-convergences
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}\overset{\Gamma}{\to}\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{eff}}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}^{(\gamma,\kappa)}\overset{\Gamma}{\to}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{eff}}
to certain effective
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{eff}}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{eff}}
.
Then, assuming that the approximate solutions
\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}
comply with (
EDB
) (or (
EDI
)) and the initial conditions
(\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}(0))_{\varepsilon}
are well-prepared, one can, under additional technical assumptions, extract suitable convergent subsequences.
Combining liminf-estimates in (
EDB
) (or (
EDI
)) with a subdifferential closedness, and assuming an appropriate chain rule holds for
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{eff}}
, the energy-dissipation principle mentioned in Subsection
2.1
guarantees that the limit is indeed a solution to
(\mathcal{H}_{m},\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{eff}},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{eff}})
.
The strategy behind EDP-convergence is similar, but instead of showing
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}^{(\gamma,\kappa)}\overset{\Gamma}{\to}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{eff}}
, one proves a liminf-estimate of the total dissipation functional.

We highlight that our approach in Section
5
is mainly based on the GFE rather than on EDB- or EDP-convergence. One can show a liminf-estimate, which would yield the EDI for
(\mathcal{H}_{m},\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{eff}},\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{eff}})
.
However, in order to conclude
-\mathrm{D}_{\boldsymbol{v}}\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{eff}}(\boldsymbol{u};\dot{\boldsymbol{u}})\in\partial^{\ell}\mathcal{E}_{0}(\boldsymbol{u})
with the limit subdifferential
\partial^{\ell}\mathcal{E}_{0}
determined in Lemma
5.4
, one would need to apply a suitable chain rule whose validity, without extra assumptions, is unclear at this stage.
In the special case
A,\tau\equiv\text{const.}
, one has
\partial^{\ell}\mathcal{E}_{0}=\partial^{\mathrm{F}}\mathcal{E}_{0}
, and a chain rule can be shown through a simple subgradient estimate. Hence, in the constant case, EDP-type arguments can be used.
In the (vCH) case, the dissipation potential
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}^{(\gamma,\kappa)}
is even non-degenerate, and the limit can be taken using the (EVI) formulation.

=\int_{\Omega}\Bigl(\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+\bigl(f(u)+A(u)K(u)-A(u)z\bigr)v+(z-K(u))y\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(3.9)

In particular, considering
\boldsymbol{v}=(0,y)
for
y\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, we deduce
\xi=z-K(u)
from (
3.9
). Furthermore, if we restrict (
3.9
) to functions of type
\boldsymbol{v}=(v,0)
for

, we obtain

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\bigl(\mu-f(u)+A(u)(z-K(u))\bigr)v\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

i.e.
u
is a weak solution of the nonlinear elliptic problem

\displaystyle-\Delta u+h^{\prime}(u)

\displaystyle=\beta u+\mu+A(u)(z-K(u))+\mathfrak{a}(u,z)\quad

in

\displaystyle\;\Omega,

\displaystyle\nabla u\cdot\mathrm{n}

\displaystyle=0\quad

on

\displaystyle\;{\partial{\Omega}}.

Since
\ell:=\beta u+\mu+A(u)(z-K(u))+\mathfrak{a}(u,z)\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
and
h\in\mathrm{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is convex, i.e.
h^{\prime}
monotonically increasing, we can use the Stampacchia method in order to show

. For this argument,
\ell
needs to lie in
\mathrm{L}^{r}(\Omega)
for some

, see, e.g.
[

, Theorem 4.5 and Section 7.2.2]
.

In particular, since

is continuous,
h^{\prime}(u)\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and whence

. The regularity assumptions on
\Omega
therefore imply

(cf.
[
Gri11
, Chapters II and III]
). Finally,

-\Delta u+h^{\prime}(u)-\beta u-A(u)(z-K(u))=\mu+\mathfrak{a}(u,z)\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega),

so that
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{A}
is shown and we have
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\delta\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})
.

(ii)

Let
\boldsymbol{v}=(v,y)\in\mathcal{H}_{m}
, and we may assume
\boldsymbol{v}\in\dom(\mathcal{E})
, otherwise (
3.5
) is trivial. Then, from (
3
) we receive

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle-\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})-\langle\delta\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}),\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{H^{*},H}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\lVert v-u\rVert_{\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{av}}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}-\frac{\beta}{2}\lVert v-u\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\lVert A^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert z-K(u)\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert v-u\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Since
u\in\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega)
and
d\leq 3
, the Sobolev-embedding
\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
ensures
u\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
. If we then apply (
3.1
) to
v-u
(note that
v
and

have the same mean value

) and
\varepsilon
-Young’s inequality, we arrive directly at (
3.5
), noting that
\lVert v-u\rVert_{(\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{av}}^{1}(\Omega))^{*}}\leq\lVert\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{u}\rVert_{H}
.∎

Proposition 3.5

(Monotonicity-type estimate for
\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{E}
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}=(u_{i},z_{i})\in\dom(\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{E})
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}=(v_{i},y_{i})\in\dom(\mathcal{E})
,
i=1,2
, where
z_{i}\in\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
for at least one
i
. Then it holds

\bigl(\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1})\delta\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1})-\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\delta\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{2}),\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\bigr)_{H}\\
\geq-\Lambda(\boldsymbol{u}_{i})\lVert\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\rVert_{H}^{2}-\omega(\boldsymbol{u}_{i})\bigl(\lVert v_{1}-u_{1}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert v_{2}-u_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\bigr),

(3.10)

where
\Lambda(\boldsymbol{u}):=C_{1}\bigl(1+(\lVert A^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}+\lVert\tau^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty})\lVert z-K(u)\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\bigr)^{2}
for some constant
C_{1}\in(0,\infty)
depending only on
\beta,A^{*},\tau_{*}
, and
\omega(\boldsymbol{u}):=\lVert\tau^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\lVert z-K(u)\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}
.

Proof.

After possibly renumbering indices, we may assume
i=1
. Inserting the definitions of
\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{v}_{i})
,
\delta\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{i})
, and rearranging terms, we obtain

\displaystyle\bigl(\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{v}_{1})\delta\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1})-\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\delta\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{2}),\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\bigr)_{H}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\Bigl(-\Delta(u_{1}-u_{2})+f(u_{1})-f(u_{2})-A(u_{1})(z_{1}-K(u_{1}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+A(u_{2})(z_{2}-K(u_{2}))\Bigr)\cdot\nabla\bigl((-\Delta)^{-1}(u_{1}-u_{2})\bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}\Bigl(\frac{z_{1}-K(u_{1})}{\tau(v_{1})}-\frac{z_{2}-K(u_{2})}{\tau(v_{2})}\Bigr)(z_{1}-z_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla(u_{1}-u_{2})\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\underbrace{(h^{\prime}(u_{1})-h^{\prime}(u_{2}))(u_{1}-u_{2})}_{\geq 0\text{ by convexity of }h}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad-\beta\int_{\Omega}(u_{1}-u_{2})^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}A(u_{2})(z_{1}-z_{2})(u_{1}-u_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\Omega}(z_{1}-K(u_{1}))(A(u_{1})-A(u_{2}))(u_{1}-u_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}\underbrace{A(u_{2})}_{\geq A_{*}>0}\underbrace{(K(u_{1})-K(u_{2}))(u_{1}-u_{2})}_{\geq A_{*}(u_{1}-u_{2})^{2}\geq 0}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}(z_{1}-K(u_{1}))\Bigl(\frac{1}{\tau(v_{1})}-\frac{1}{\tau(v_{2})}\Bigr)(z_{1}-z_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\tau(v_{2})}(K(u_{1})-K(u_{2}))(z_{1}-z_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\tau(v_{2})}(z_{1}-z_{2})^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\lVert u_{1}-u_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{av}}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}-A^{*}\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert u_{1}-u_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\quad-\bigl(\beta+\lVert A^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert z_{1}-K(u_{1})\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\bigr)\lVert u_{1}-u_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\lVert\tau^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}}{\tau_{*}^{2}}\lVert z_{1}-K(u_{1})\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert v_{1}-v_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{A^{*}}{\tau_{*}}\lVert u_{1}-u_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)},

(3.11)

where we used that
A
,
K
and
\frac{1}{\tau}
are Lipschitz continuous with respective Lipschitz constants
\lVert A^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}
,
A^{*}
and
\frac{\lVert\tau^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}}{\tau_{*}^{2}}
. Applying Young’s inequality several times and triangular inequality
\lVert v_{1}-v_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\lVert v_{1}-u_{1}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert u_{1}-u_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\lVert v_{2}-u_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}
to (
3
), one obtains a constant

satisfying

\bigl(\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1})\delta\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1})-\mathcal{K}(\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\delta\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{2}),\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\bigr)_{H}\\
\geq\lVert u_{1}-u_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{av}}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}-C\bigl(1+\lVert A^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert z_{1}-K(u_{1})\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\bigr)\lVert u_{1}-u_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\\
-C(1+\lVert\tau^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert z_{1}-K(u_{1})\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)})^{2}\lVert z_{1}-z_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\\
-\lVert\tau^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\lVert z_{1}-K(u_{1})\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{2}\bigl(\lVert v_{2}-u_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\lVert v_{1}-u_{1}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\bigr).

Eventually, applying (
3.1
) and Young’s inequality once more, we arrive at the desired estimate (
3.10
).
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

There exists a constant
C_{2}=C_{2}(\Omega,d,A^{*},\beta)\in(0,\infty)
such that

\lVert u-m\rVert_{\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{2}\bigl(\lVert(u-m,z)\rVert_{H}+\lVert\delta\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})\rVert_{H^{*}}\bigr)

(3.12)

for all
\boldsymbol{u}=(u,z)\in\dom(\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{E})
.

Proof.

From the definition of
\delta\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})
, i.e. (
3.4
), it holds

\lVert\delta\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})\rVert_{H^{*}}^{2}=\int_{\Omega}\Bigl(\lvert\nabla(-\Delta u+f(u)-A(u)(z-K(u)))\rvert^{2}+(z-K(u))^{2}\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

(3.13)

Thanks to (
3.3
), it suffices to show (
3.12
) with left-hand side
\lVert\Delta u\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}
. Multiplying
-\Delta u+f(u)-A(u)(z-K(u))
with
-\Delta u
and integrating by parts twice, we get, by making use of
\nabla u\cdot\mathrm{n}=0
on
{\partial{\Omega}}
and
\nabla(f(u))=f^{\prime}(u)\nabla u
(as

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\lvert\Delta u\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla(-\Delta u+f(u)-A(u)(z-K(u)))\cdot\nabla u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}f(u)\cdot\Delta u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}A(u)(z-K(u))\cdot\Delta u\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u)\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+A^{*}\lVert\Delta u\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\lVert(z-K(u))\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}.

Hence, as

\displaystyle\lVert\Delta u\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\nabla(-\Delta u+f(u)-A(z-K(u)))\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+(1+2\beta)\lVert u-m\rVert_{\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{av}}^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+(A^{*})^{2}\lVert z-K(u)\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{1,(A^{*})^{2}\}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}\rVert_{H^{*}}^{2}+(1+2\beta)C\lVert\Delta u\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}^{4/3}\lVert u-m\rVert_{(\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{av}}^{1}(\Omega))^{*}}^{2/3},

where the last estimate follows from (

, the desired estimate (

) finally follows after one more application of Young’s inequality.
∎

(Closedness of

In the following, we write

,

. From

Proposition 3.10

(Chain rule)
.

Let
T<\infty
. Assume
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathrm{AC}_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2}([0,T];\mathcal{H}_{m})\cap\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega))
and
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;H^{*})
are such that
\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)\in\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))
for almost every
t\in(0,T)
. Then, the function
\mathcal{E}\circ\boldsymbol{u}:[0,T]\to[0,\infty)
is absolutely continuous, and for almost all
t\in(0,T)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))=\langle\boldsymbol{\mu}(t),\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(t)\rangle_{H^{*},H}.

Proof.

As always,
\boldsymbol{u}
is identified with its continuous representative. Moreover, we fix a representative for
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and put the set of “good points”
\Sigma:=\{t\in(0,T)\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)\in\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))\}
. By assumption,
\mathcal{L}^{1}([0,T]\setminus\Sigma)=0
.

We aim to give a self-contained proof by relying on the subdifferential estimate (
3.5
) and following largely the strategy from the lecture notes
[
Mie23
, Theorem 3.12]
, with adaptations where necessary. We start with Step 1 by introducing the arc-length parametrisation
\eta:[0,T]\to[0,L]
of
\boldsymbol{u}
with left inverse
\theta
, so that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}:=\boldsymbol{u}\circ\eta
is 1-Lipschitz continuous. Accordingly, we define
\widehat{E}:=\mathcal{E}\circ\boldsymbol{u}\circ\eta
and a quantity
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
, which will be a slightly modified version of
\boldsymbol{\mu}\circ\eta
. In Step 2 we will show that the “good properties” of
\Sigma
are inherited to
\theta(\Sigma)
for the reparametrised functions
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
. Step 3 is devoted to showing an absolute continuity estimate for
\widehat{E}
on
\theta(\Sigma)
. This will be achieved by employing the subdifferential estimate (
3.5
) and approximating the integral of
g
by Riemann sums, for which the assumed
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
space regularity plays a crucial role. In Step 4 we will then prove that this absolute continuity estimate transfers back to the original variables
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathcal{E}\circ\boldsymbol{u}
on
\Sigma
. As a result, we obtain a function
E\in\mathrm{W}^{1,1}([0,T];\mathbb{R})
that agrees with
\mathcal{E}\circ\boldsymbol{u}
on
\Sigma
. The task is then to show that
E(t)=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))
for
any

t\in[0,T]
, thereby establishing the desired absolute continuity for
\mathcal{E}\circ\boldsymbol{u}
. Finally, in Step 5 we will identify the weak derivative of
\mathcal{E}\circ\boldsymbol{u}
.

Step 1: Reparametrisation to 1-Lipschitz curve.

The following goes back to
[
AGS08
, Lemma 1.1.4]
. For
L:=\lVert\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(0,T;H)}
, we consider the absolutely continuous and non-decreasing surjective function

\theta:[0,T]\to[0,L],\qquad\theta(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\lVert\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(r)\rVert_{H}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r.

Define the left continuous, non-decreasing map

\eta:[0,L]\to[0,T],\qquad\eta(s):=\min\{t\in[0,T]\mid\theta(t)=s\},

which, in fact, is right inverse to
\theta
. We make the following observation: If
\eta(\theta(t))<t
, then
\theta
is constant on
[\eta(\theta(t)),t]
, which means that
\boldsymbol{u}
is constant on
[\eta(\theta(t)),t]
as well. Therefore,
\boldsymbol{u}(\eta(\theta(t)))=\boldsymbol{u}(t)
. Hence, the following properties of
\theta
and
\eta
hold true

\begin{cases}\theta(\eta(s))=s&\text{for all }s\in[0,L],\\
\eta(\theta(t))\leq t&\text{for all }t\in[0,T],\\
\boldsymbol{u}(\eta(\theta(t)))=\boldsymbol{u}(t)&\text{for all }t\in[0,T].\end{cases}

(3.14)

With that, we define
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}:=\boldsymbol{u}\circ\eta:[0,L]\to\mathcal{H}_{m}
, which is 1-Lipschitz continuous as for
0\leq s_{0}\leq s_{1}\leq L

\lVert\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(s_{1})-\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(s_{0})\rVert_{H}\leq\int_{\eta(s_{0})}^{\eta(s_{1})}\lVert\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(s)\rVert_{H}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\theta(\eta(s_{1}))-\theta(\eta(s_{0}))=s_{1}-s_{0},

where the penultimate equality is simply the definition of
\theta
. Using (
3.14
), we also observe
\boldsymbol{u}=\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}\circ\theta
on

. We also put
\widehat{E}:=\mathcal{E}\circ\boldsymbol{u}\circ\eta
and define

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}:[0,L]\to H^{*}

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(s):=\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{\mu}(\eta(s))&\text{if }s\notin D,\\
\delta\mathcal{E}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(s))&\text{if }s\in D,\end{cases}

where

is at most countable due to monotonicity of
\eta
. Note that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(s)
is well-defined for

(i.e.
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(s)\in\dom(\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{E})
), because
\boldsymbol{u}
is constant on

, it must be that
\boldsymbol{u}(t)\in\dom(\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{E})
for all

. We also stress that
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
is Bochner measurable as

By the change of variables formula

and observing that

\displaystyle\int_{\theta(t_{0})}^{\theta(t_{1})}\lVert\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(s)\rVert_{H^{*}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{\theta(t_{0})}^{\theta(t_{1})}\lVert(\boldsymbol{\mu}\circ\eta)(s)\rVert_{H^{*}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}(\eta(\theta(t)))\rVert_{H^{*}}\theta^{\prime}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}(\eta(\theta(t)))\rVert_{H^{*}}\lVert\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(t)\rVert_{H}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)\rVert_{H^{*}}\lVert\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(t)\rVert_{H}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t,

(3.15)

where the first equality uses that
\mathcal{L}^{1}(D)=0
(i.e.
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\boldsymbol{\mu}\circ\eta
almost everyhwere), and the fourth that

almost everywhere in

. Similarly, we obtain

\int_{\theta(t_{0})}^{\theta(t_{1})}\lVert\widehat{z}(s)-K(\widehat{u}(s))\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\lVert z(t)-K(u(t))\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(t)\rVert_{H}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

In particular,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(0,L;H^{*})
and
\widehat{z}-K(\widehat{u})\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(0,L;\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega))
.

Step 2:
\mathcal{L}^{1}([0,L]\setminus\theta(\Sigma))=0
and

for all

Since

\theta([0,T])=[0,L]

For the second claim, we assume

and take

. If in addition

, then
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(s)=\delta\mathcal{E}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(s))\in\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{E}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(s))
already holds by definition of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
. Let us consider the case

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(s)=\boldsymbol{\mu}(\eta(s))\in\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(\eta(s)))=\partial^{\mathrm{G}}\mathcal{E}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(s)).

Step 3: Absolute continuity estimate for

.

Our goal is to show that
\lvert\widehat{E}(t)-\widehat{E}(s)\rvert\leq(1+M)\int_{s}^{t}g(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r
for all

with

\displaystyle g(r)

\displaystyle:=\lVert\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(r)\rVert_{H^{*}}+\frac{1}{2}\lVert A^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\widehat{z}(r)-K(\widehat{u}(r))\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)},

(3.17)

and

by Step 1.

To begin with, we note that for arbitrary
s_{0},s_{1}\in\theta(\Sigma)
with
s_{0}<s_{1}
, Step 2 tells us that estimate (
3.5
) is applicable. Hence, exploiting the 1-Lipschitz continuity of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(s_{0}))\lvert s_{1}-s_{0}\rvert^{2}-\lVert\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(s_{0})\rVert_{H^{*}}\lvert s_{1}-s_{0}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{E}(s_{1})-\widehat{E}(s_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(s_{1})\rVert_{H^{*}}\lvert s_{1}-s_{0}\rvert-\lambda(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(s_{1}))\lvert s_{1}-s_{0}\rvert^{2}.

(3.18)

Let now
s,t\in\theta(\Sigma)
with
s<t
. Then, for a partition
s=s_{0}<s_{1}<\dotsc<s_{N}=t
with

\displaystyle\widehat{E}(t)-\widehat{E}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigl(\widehat{E}(s_{j})-\widehat{E}(s_{j-1})\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Bigl(\lVert\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(s_{j})\rVert_{H^{*}}\lvert s_{j}-s_{j-1}\rvert-\lambda(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}}(s_{j}))\lvert s_{j}-s_{j-1}\rvert^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lVert\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(s_{j})\rVert_{H^{*}}\lvert s_{j}-s_{j-1}\rvert+\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(s_{j}-s_{j-1})^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}\lVert A^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\widehat{z}(s_{j})-K(\widehat{u}(s_{j}))\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}(s_{j}-s_{j-1})\biggr)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\biggl(1+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(s_{j}-s_{j-1})g(s_{j})\biggr)\sum_{j=1}^{n}(s_{j}-s_{j-1})g(s_{j})+\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(s_{j}-s_{j-1})^{2},

(3.19)

where we have used
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\ell^{2}}^{2}\leq\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\ell^{1}}^{2}
in the penultimate inequality.

Using Lemma

for approximating the Lebesgue integral by Riemann sums with partition points from a set of full measure, we get a sequence of partitions
s=s_{0}^{n}<s_{1}^{n}<\dotsc<s_{N(n)}^{n}=t
, indexed by

, such that
\max_{1\leq j\leq N(n)}\lvert s_{j}^{n}-s_{j-1}^{n}\rvert\to 0
as

,
s_{j}^{n}\in\theta(\Sigma)
for every

,

and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{N(n)}(s_{j}^{n}-s_{j-1}^{n})g(s_{j}^{n})=\int_{s}^{t}g(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r.

Hence, applying (
3

\{s_{0}^{n},\dotsc,s_{N(n)}^{n}\}

n\to\infty

\widehat{E}(t)-\widehat{E}(s)\leq(1+\lVert g\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(s,t)})\int_{s}^{t}g(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r.

Using the lower bound in (

\widehat{E}(t)-\widehat{E}(s)\geq-(1+\lVert g\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(s,t)})\int_{s}^{t}g(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

\lvert\widehat{E}(t)-\widehat{E}(s)\rvert\leq(1+M)\int_{s}^{t}g(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r
.

Step 4: Absolute continuity of
\mathcal{E}\circ\boldsymbol{u}
.

Let
t_{0},t_{1}\in\Sigma
,
t_{0}<t_{1}
. Then
\theta(t_{0}),\theta(t_{1})\in\theta(\Sigma)
, hence, by (

), Step 3, (
3

\displaystyle\lvert\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t_{1}))-\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t_{0}))\rvert

\displaystyle=\bigl\lvert\mathcal{E}\bigl(\boldsymbol{u}\bigl(\eta(\theta(t_{1}))\bigr)\bigr)-\mathcal{E}\bigl(\boldsymbol{u}\bigl(\eta(\theta(t_{0}))\bigr)\bigr)\bigr\rvert=\bigl\lvert\widehat{E}(\theta(t_{1}))-\widehat{E}(\theta(t_{0}))\bigr\rvert

\displaystyle\leq(1+M)\int_{\theta(t_{0})}^{\theta(t_{1})}\Bigl(\lVert\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(t)\rVert_{H^{*}}+\frac{1}{2}\lVert A^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\widehat{z}(t)-K(\widehat{u}(t))\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=(1+M)\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)\rVert_{H^{*}}\lVert\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(t)\rVert_{H}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}(1+M)\lVert A^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\lVert z(t)-K(u(t))\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\lVert\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(t)\rVert_{H}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

Since
\mathcal{L}^{1}([0,T]\setminus\Sigma)=0
, we therefore find
E\in\mathrm{W}^{1,1}([0,T];\mathbb{R})
such that
E(t)=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))
for all
t\in\Sigma
. Now, we want to argue similarly as in
[
AGS08
, Theorem 1.2.5]
and
[
Mie23
, Theorem 3.12, Step 2]
to show
E(t)=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))
for all
t\in[0,T]
, albeit with slight adaptations. The actual difference is that
\boldsymbol{u}
is not 1-Lipschitz continuous but certainly
\frac{1}{2}
-Hölder continuous

\lVert\boldsymbol{u}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}(s)\rVert_{H}\leq\int_{s}^{t}\lVert\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(r)\rVert_{H}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\leq\lVert\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(s,t;H)}\lvert t-s\rvert^{1/2},\quad 0\leq s\leq t\leq T,

(3.20)

and that
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-integrable in time, which, in the end, turns out to be enough to carry out the argument.

Lower semicontinuity of
\mathcal{E}
already gives
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))\leq E(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]\setminus\Sigma
. For the reverse inequality, we restrict to
t\in[T/2,T]\setminus\Sigma
and consider the averages for

E_{r}(t):=\frac{1}{r}\int_{t-r}^{t}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s=\frac{1}{r}\int_{t-r}^{t}E(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\xrightarrow{r\searrow 0}E(t),

where the equality is justified since
\{s\mid\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(s))\neq E(s)\}
is a null set, and the limit
r\searrow 0
because
E
is continuous. Hence, in order to get
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))\geq E(t)
, it suffices to show
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))\geq\lim_{r\searrow 0}E_{r}(t)
. To this end, we compute by applying (
3.5
), (
3.20
) and using the fact that
\lvert t-s\rvert\leq r
for

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))-E_{r}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\int_{t-r}^{t}\bigl(\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))-\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(s))\bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{r}\int_{t-r}^{t}\Bigl(\bigl\langle\boldsymbol{\mu}(s),\boldsymbol{u}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}(s)\bigr\rangle_{H^{*},H}+\lambda(\boldsymbol{u}(s))\lVert\boldsymbol{u}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}(s)\rVert_{H}^{2}\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{r}\int_{t-r}^{t}\Bigl(-\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)\rVert_{H^{*}}\lVert\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(s,t;H)}\lvert t-s\rvert^{1/2}+\lambda(\boldsymbol{u}(s))\lVert\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(s,t;H)}^{2}\lvert t-s\rvert\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\lVert\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(t-r,t;H)}\int_{t-r}^{t}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}(s)\rVert_{H^{*}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s+\lVert\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(t-r,t;H)}^{2}\int_{t-r}^{t}\lambda(\boldsymbol{u}(s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\geq-\lVert\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(t-r,t;H)}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(t-r,t;H^{*})}+\lVert\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(t-r,t;H)}^{2}\lVert\lambda(\boldsymbol{u})\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(t-r,t)},

where we used
\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{1}(t-r,t;H^{*})}\leq\sqrt{r}\lVert\boldsymbol{\mu}\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(t-r,t;H^{*})}
in the last inequality. As
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;H^{*})
and
\lambda(\boldsymbol{u})=-\frac{1}{8}(\beta+\lVert A^{\prime}\rVert\lVert z-K(u)\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)})^{2}\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(0,T)
, the right-hand side vanishes in the limit
r\searrow 0
by absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integral. This shows the desired estimate
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))\geq\lim_{r\searrow 0}E_{r}(t)
in the case
t\in[T/2,T]
. For
t\in[0,T/2]
, we instead consider
E_{r}(t)=\frac{1}{r}\int_{t}^{t+r}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
and the analogous argument yields the same result in the end.

Step 5: Identification of derivative
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})=\langle\boldsymbol{\mu},\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}\rangle_{H^{*},H}
.

Let
\Sigma_{0}
denote the set of points
t\in(0,T)
where
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\mathcal{E}\circ\boldsymbol{u}
are differentiable,
\boldsymbol{\mu}(t)=\delta\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))
, and

. By hypothesis and Step 4, we have
\mathcal{L}^{1}([0,T]\setminus\Sigma_{0})=0
. For any

, it then holds for all

that

\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t+h))-\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))\geq\langle\boldsymbol{\mu}(t),\boldsymbol{u}(t+h)-\boldsymbol{u}(t)\rangle_{H^{*},H}+\lambda(\boldsymbol{u}(t))\lVert\boldsymbol{u}(t+h)-\boldsymbol{u}(t)\rVert_{H}^{2}.

Dividing this by

and taking the limit

, the differentiability of

and

yield

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))\geq\langle\boldsymbol{\mu}(t),\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(t)\rangle_{H^{*},H}+0.

Finally, dividing by
h<0
and taking the limit

∎

Remark 3.11
.

We emphasise that the abstract chain rule results
[
MRS13
, Proposition 2.4]
and
[
MR23
, Appendix A, Proposition A.1]
are not directly applicable. As in our case, their proofs rely on a subgradient estimate of the form

\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{v})\geq\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})+\langle\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{u}\rangle_{H^{*},H}+\rho(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\lVert\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{u}\rVert_{H}.

In Mielke et al., the modulus of subdifferentiability
\rho:\mathcal{H}_{m}\times\mathcal{H}_{m}\to[0,\infty)
is assumed to be upper semicontinuous, which guarantees
\sup_{s,t\in[0,T]}\rho(\boldsymbol{u}(s),\boldsymbol{u}(t))<\infty
for continuous curves
\boldsymbol{u}:[0,T]\to\mathcal{H}_{m}
. In our setting, however, the modulus takes the form

\rho(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=\lambda(\boldsymbol{u})\lVert\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{H}

and is not upper semicontinuous.

In fact, the arguments in Mielke et al. would still apply if
\lambda(\widehat{\boldsymbol{u}})\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(0,T)
for the arc-length reparametrisation
(\widehat{u},\widehat{z})
. Yet, as observed in Step 1,
(\widehat{u},\widehat{z})
in general only belongs to
\mathrm{L}^{1}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2}))
, which is not sufficiently regular.

Proof of Theorem

\kappa=T/N
,

\boldsymbol{u}
and a suitable weak convergence of the discrete time derivatives. Applying the closedness properties Corollary

Eventually, we show the regularity

Following our roadmap, we will start constructing discrete approximants satisfying (EL) by solving the minimisation problem

\min_{\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{H}_{m}}\Phi_{\kappa}^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{G}}(\boldsymbol{v};\boldsymbol{u}):=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u})+\frac{\kappa}{2}\mathcal{R}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{v};\frac{1}{\kappa}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v})\Bigr).

(4.1)

Proposition 4.1

(Solvability of the direct problem)
.

For all
\kappa\in(0,\infty)
and

, minimisation problem (

\boldsymbol{u}_{*}\in\mathcal{H}_{m}

fulfils the following (EL) equation (with

-\frac{1}{\kappa}\mathcal{G}(\boldsymbol{v})(\boldsymbol{u}_{*}-\boldsymbol{v})=\delta\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{u}_{*}).

(4.2)

Proof.

Existence of minimisers follows from the direct method of the calculus of variations:
\Phi_{\kappa}^{\mathcal{E},\mathcal{G}}(\boldsymbol{v};\cdot)
is coercive due to (
2.15a
) and

; weak lower semicontinuity holds since

Starting with an initial condition

(

(A priori estimates)

There exists a constant

The proof is split into three steps. In Step 1 we will derive estimates

(ii)

A.1
Approximation of Lebesgue integral by Riemann sums

In the proof of the chain rule, Proposition
3.10
, we approximated the Lebesgue integral by Riemann sums. For this argument to be valid, we need to ensure that the Riemann sums indeed converge to the Lebesgue integral. The specific situation needed in Proposition
3.10
was recently treated in
[
CS24
]
, where it was shown, in particular, that the partition points can be chosen outside a set of Lebesgue measure zero.

Lemma A.1

(
[
CS24
, Lemma A.1]
)
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,
a<b
, and
\Sigma\subseteq[a,b]
such that
a,b\in\Sigma
and
\mathcal{L}^{1}([a,b]\setminus\Sigma)=0
. Moreover, let
g:[a,b]\to[0,\infty]
be Lebesgue measurable with
\int_{a}^{b}g(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t<\infty
. Fix
\sigma\in\{0,1\}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Then, there exists a partition
a=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dotsc<t_{n}=b
such that
t_{i}\in\Sigma
for all
0\leq i\leq n
,
\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\lvert t_{i}-t_{i-1}\rvert<\varepsilon
and

\biggl\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(t_{i-\sigma})(t_{i}-t_{i-1})-\int_{a}^{b}g(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\biggr\rvert<\varepsilon.

A.2
Evolutionary closedness

In the existence and relaxation limit proofs, we employed the evolutionary closedness properties formulated in Corollaries
3.8
and
5.5
. In both cases, we proved a static strong-weak closedness in advance, namely Proposition
3.7
and Lemma
5.4

[

is in fact independent of

S_{\infty}=\partial^{\ell}\mathcal{E}_{0}

Lemma A.2
.

Let
H
be a real, separable Hilbert space, and let
S_{n}:H\to\mathcal{P}(H^{*})
be mappings for
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
from

into the power set of
H^{*}
. We assume that
S_{\infty}(x)
is closed in
H^{*}
and convex for all

. Moreover, assume (
A.1
) holds for all subsequences
(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq(n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

\left.\begin{array}[]{r}(x_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset H,y_{n_{k}}\in S_{n_{k}}(x_{n_{k}}),x_{\infty}\in H,y_{\infty}\in H^{*}\\

(A.1)

Then, we have the following evolutionary closedness for any

\left.\begin{array}[]{r}x_{n},x_{\infty}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;H),y_{n},y_{\infty}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;H^{*})\\

y_{n}\rightharpoonup y_{\infty}\text{ in }\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;H^{*})\text{ for }n\to\infty,\\

\Longrightarrow\quad y_{\infty}(t)\in S_{\infty}(x_{\infty}(t))\text{ a.e. }t\in(0,T).

(A.2)

Proof.

The proof mostly follows
[
LR18
, Proposition 2.7]
. Assume the left-hand side of (
A.2
). From
x_{n}\to x_{\infty}
strongly and the condition
y_{n}\in S_{n}(x_{n})
, we particularly find a subsequence
(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq(n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and a null set
N\subset[0,T]
such that, for all
t\in[0,T]\setminus N
, it holds
x_{n_{k}}(t)\to x_{\infty}(t)
in
H
as
k\to\infty
and
y_{n_{k}}(t)\in S_{n_{k}}(x_{n_{k}}(t))
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

As a weakly convergent sequence,
(y_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;H^{*})
. Applying the fundamental theorem for weak topologies
[
RS06
, Theorem 3.2]
, we find a time-parametrised Young measure
(\nu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
such that
(\nu(t))(L(t))=1
, where

L(t):=\Bigl\{y\in H^{*}\;\Big|\;\exists(y_{n_{k_{\ell}}})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq(y_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\text{ with }y_{n_{k_{\ell}}}\rightharpoonup y\text{ in }H^{*}\text{ as }\ell\to\infty\Bigr\},

and

y_{\infty}(t)=\int_{H^{*}}z\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu_{t}(z)

(A.3)

for all
t\in[0,T]\setminus N
(by making
N
possibly larger, but still
\mathcal{L}^{1}(N)=0
).

Taking
y\in L(t)
, we find a subsequence such that
y_{n_{k_{\ell}}}\rightharpoonup y
in
H^{*}
. As shown above, we also have
x_{n_{k_{\ell}}}(t)\to x(t)
in
H
and
y_{n_{k_{\ell}}}(t)\in S_{n_{k_{\ell}}}(x_{n_{k_{\ell}}}(t))
for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, (
A.1
) implies
y\in S_{\infty}(x_{\infty}(t))
. Hence,
L(t)\subseteq S_{\infty}(x_{\infty}(t))
. This shows that for all
t\in[0,T]\setminus N
, the measure
\nu(t)
is concentrated on the convex and closed set
S_{\infty}(x_{\infty}(t))
, so we infer
y_{\infty}(t)\in S_{\infty}(x_{\infty}(t))
by (
A.3
).
∎

A.3
Auxiliary Sobolev–Bochner chain rule

Lemma A.3
.

Let
T<\infty
, and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
1\leq d\leq 3
, be open and bounded. Assume
u\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(0,T;(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))^{*})\cap\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega))
and
A\in\mathrm{W}^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, and let
K:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the primitive of
A
with
K(0)=0
. Then
K\circ u\in\mathrm{W}^{1,\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))^{*})\cap\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega))
, with distributional time derivative

\langle\partial_{t}(K\circ u)(t),\phi\rangle_{(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))^{*},\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}=\langle\partial_{t}u(t),A(u(t))\phi\rangle_{(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))^{*},\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}

(A.4)

for all
\phi\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
, almost all
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

Since
A
and
K
are Lipschitz continuous, we have
K(u)\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega))
with
\nabla(K(u))=A(u)\nabla u
and
\Hess(K(u))=A^{\prime}(u)\nabla u\otimes\nabla u+A(u)\Hess(u)
by product rule in Sobolev spaces and the chain rule, e.g.
[
MM72
, Lemma 2.1]
.

Next, we show that the right-hand side in (
A.4
) has the regularity
\mathrm{W}^{1,\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))^{*})
. To this end, we let
\phi\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
and estimate for almost all
t\in(0,T)

\ell(t)(\phi):=\langle\partial_{t}u(t),A(u(t))\phi\rangle_{(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))^{*},\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\lVert\partial_{t}u(t)\rVert_{(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))^{*}}\lVert A(u)\phi\rVert_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}.

Since
\nabla(A(u(t))\phi)=A(u(t))\nabla\phi+A^{\prime}(u(t))\phi\nabla u(t)
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the Sobolev embedding
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{6}(\Omega)
entails

\lVert A(u(t))\phi\rVert_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\bigl(\sqrt{2}\lVert A\rVert_{\infty}+\sqrt{2}C_{\mathrm{S}}\lVert A^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\lVert\nabla u(t)\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{3}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}\bigr)\lVert\phi\rVert_{\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}.

Consequently, Hölder’s inequality applied to the exponents
\frac{3}{2}
,
3
leads to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\ell(t)\rVert_{(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))^{*}}^{4/3}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\partial_{t}u\rVert_{(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))^{*}}^{4/3}\bigl(1+\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{3}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}\bigr)^{4/3}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C\biggl(\int_{0}^{T}\lVert\partial_{t}u\rVert_{(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))^{*}}^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\biggr)^{2/3}\biggl(\int_{0}^{T}\lvert 1+\lVert\nabla u\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{3}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}\rvert^{4}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\biggr)^{1/3},

which is finite by interpolation
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{4}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{3}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))}\leq\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))}^{1/2}\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))}^{1/2}
.

It remains to show that for any
\phi\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)
and
\eta\in\mathrm{C}_{c}^{\infty}((0,T))

\int_{0}^{T}\eta^{\prime}(t)(K(u(t)),\phi)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t=-\int_{0}^{T}\eta(t)\langle\partial_{t}u(t),A(u(t))\phi\rangle_{(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))^{*},\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.

By approximation
[
Rou13
, Lemma 7.2]
, we find a sequence
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathrm{C}^{1}([0,T];\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega))
such that
u_{n}\to u
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{2}(\Omega))
and
\partial_{t}u_{n}\to\partial_{t}u
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))^{*})
. Exploiting the Lipschitz continuity of
A
, one finds
A(u_{n})\eta\phi\rightharpoonup A(u)\eta\phi
in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(0,T;\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))
, so that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\eta^{\prime}(t)(K(u(t)),\phi)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\eta^{\prime}(t)(K(u_{n}(t)),\phi)_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\langle\partial_{t}u_{n}(t),A(u_{n}(t))\eta(t)\phi\rangle_{(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))^{*},\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\eta(t)\langle\partial_{t}u(t),A(u(t))\phi\rangle_{(\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega))^{*},\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t.\qed
