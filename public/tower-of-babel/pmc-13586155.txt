The Wor2 phenotypic switching regulator controls biofilm formation in Candida auris
Authors: Weihong Liang, Shuyun Guan, Jian Bing, Han Du, Qiushi Zheng, Li Tao, Bing Li, Guanghua Huang, Haiqing Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe emerging fungal pathogen Candida auris is a serious global public health threat due to its ability to persist in healthcare environments and on human skin. Here, we report a prevalent cluster of clinical C. auris strains with enhanced biofilm formation, a contributor to environmental and skin persistence. Genomic analyses show that the clinical isolates with enhanced biofilm formation ability carry a loss-of-function mutation in the WOR2 locus, which encodes a white–opaque switching regulator in Candida species. Analysis of 13,314 published genomes revealed that 3104 strains (23.3%) harbor nonsense or frameshift mutations in WOR2, indicating frequent clinical occurrence. Deletion of WOR2 in a clinical strain markedly increased biofilm formation, whereas reintroduction of an intact WOR2 significantly attenuated biofilm development. Further analyses show that Wor2 inactivation upregulates GFC1, ALS4, and multiple biofilm-associated genes. Together, these findings reveal a key regulatory mechanism underlying biofilm development, environmental persistence, and transmission of C. auris.Subject terms: Genetics, Microbiology

Introduction

Candida auris poses a serious and growing threat to global public health1. It has attracted considerable attention due to the characteristics of multidrug resistance, high mortality, and easy transmission2,3. Similar to other pathogenic Candida species, C. auris is able to grow as multiple morphologies including yeast, pseudohyphae, aggregates, and biofilms4,5. This phenotypic plasticity not only makes it a successful pathogen but also confers on it enhanced environmental persistence and the ability to cause outbreaks in healthcare settings.

In contrast to related pathogenic Candida species that predominantly inhabit the gastrointestinal tract, C. auris exhibits a particular tropism for the skin6–8. It is capable of persistent colonization in niches such as the axillae and groin, with biofilm formation enhancing its adhesive capacity and colonization efficiency9–11. A study revealed that after forming biofilms, C. auris develops profound resistance to common azole drugs and echinocandins, while the MIC of amphotericin B sharply increases from 0.25–1 mg/L to 2–16 mg/L, an 8–16 folds increase12. This resistance is partly attributed to the increased expression of efflux pumps, including CDR1, SNQ2, and MDR1, which actively export antifungal drugs, thereby undermining their clinical effectiveness13. Besides, mutations in ERG11, the target of azole antifungals, are a major mechanism underlying resistance to fluconazole and other azoles in C. auris3. Additionally, biofilms facilitate persistent colonization on medical devices and human tissues, through adhesins like Als1, Als3, Als4, Als5, and Scf1, which promote robust adhesion and reinforce structural integrity within the community9,13,14. Moreover, the biofilm matrix further enhances environmental survival by providing a barrier against desiccation and disinfectants, thereby enabling long-term persistence on surfaces5,15,16.

Biofilm development in C. auris critically depends on adhesins, which mediate both surface attachment and cell–cell cohesion. Key adhesins include Als1, Als3, and Als4, which facilitate initial colonization and aggregation; Als4112, specifically promotes fungal anchorage by binding to host extracellular matrix proteins such as laminin9,13,17–20. Additional adhesins like Scf1 and Iff4109 are indispensable for attachment to various surfaces14,18. Furthermore, the transcription factor Ume6 promotes pseudohyphae formation (with Hgc1), aggregation (with Als4498 and Scf1), adhesion to inert surfaces (with Scf1), and biofilm formation (with Als4498, Scf1, and Hgc1). Ume6 hyperactivation in biofilms also increased resistance to amphotericin B and micafungin. These findings establish Ume6 as a key regulator controlling distinct pathways of C. auris morphogenesis and antifungal tolerance21. In parallel, the MAP kinase Hog1 maintains cell wall integrity and stress adaptation, supporting both biofilm structural stability and pathogenicity22–24. Additionally, this pathogen shares several key virulence determinants with Candida albicans, such as the conserved transcriptional regulators including Bcr1, Ndt80, Tec1 and Brg125–27. Among these regulators, Bcr1 (referenced Gfc1 in C. auris) serves as a master regulator of biofilm formation27,28. It controls the expression of critical cell wall proteins like Als1, Als3, and Hwp1 in C. albicans, and its deletion severely disrupts cellular cohesion and biofilm integrity, underscoring a functionally conserved role that may extend to C. auris28,29.

Despite recent advances, our understanding of the upstream signaling pathways that regulate adhesins during biofilm formation remains limited. Given the critical role of biofilm in the skin colonization of C. auris, such regulatory pathways are likely widespread in clinical strains and may help explain the rapid global outbreak and widespread transmission of this pathogen. Direct examination of clinical isolates is essential to uncover species-specific pathogenic mechanisms. For example, a study showed that copy number variations in the ALS4 gene in clinical isolates can enhance biofilm formation, highlighting the value of clinical strains in revealing pathogenic mechanisms9. With the growing numbers of clinical C. auris isolates worldwide2,30,31, there is an urgent need to profile their biofilm-forming capabilities and delineate the underlying genetic determinants that govern this critical virulence trait.

In the present study, we identified a subset of clinical strains exhibiting enhanced biofilm-forming capability and linked this trait to inactivating mutations in the transcription factor gene WOR2. Functional studies confirmed that Wor2 acts as a novel negative regulator of biofilm formation. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that the wor2 mutant strains exhibit upregulation of the adhesin gene ALS4 and the transcription factor gene GFC1. Our findings indicate that WOR2 mutations are prevalent in clinical settings and this transcription factor functions as a key repressor of C. auris biofilm formation.

Results

Diverse biofilm-forming abilities of clinical C. auris isolates

In our previous studies30,31, we collected 82 clinical isolates of C. auris from China. To examine the biofilm formation abilities of these strains, we performed quantitative biofilm assays in 48-well polystyrene plates (Fig. 1a). Colony-forming unit (CFU) analysis indicated that a subset of clinical strains exhibited remarkably enhanced abilities of biofilm formation. The biofilm morphologies of representative strains GZ10, GZ108, GZ109, GZ241, and GZ289 are shown in Fig. 1b. Compared to strain GZ10, the other four strains developed notably denser and thicker biofilms in 48-well plates.

Since biofilm formation is often associated with morphological transitions in pathogenic Candida species, we next tested whether the wor2 mutant strains were able to undergo morphological changes under standard laboratory conditions (YPD, 30 °C). Phloxine B, a red dye that stains fungal cells with altered cell morphology, was added to the medium. Of the five strains tested, three tested strains (GZ10, GZ241, and GZ289) formed white colonies, whereas two strains (GZ108 and GZ109) formed the colonies that stained red (Fig. 1c). Only yeast-form cells and no morphological transitions were observed in all five tested clinical isolates (Fig. 1c).

WOR2 mutations are associated with the enhanced biofilm-forming ability of clinical C. auris isolates

To explore the underlying mechanism of the enhanced biofilm-forming ability in clinical strains of C. auris, we performed whole-genome sequencing analysis (WGS). Among the sequenced strains with enhanced biofilm-forming ability (CFU > 5 × 107 cells per well, 11 isolates), 6 strains (6/11, 54.5%) harbored a missense, non-sense, or frameshift mutation in the WOR2 locus (S1 Table). It has been demonstrated that WOR2 encodes a transcription factor required for white-opaque phenotypic switching in C. albicans32,33.

We performed phylogenetic and pairwise SNP analyses of the wor2 mutants (Figs. S1 and S2 Table). The genetic distances within this cluster were notably narrow, with pairwise SNP differences among the eight WOR2 mutant strains ranging from merely 2 to 31 SNPs, strongly supporting their recent common ancestry and clonal expansion. Of note, strains GZ109, GZ82 and GZ126, which carried an identical WOR2 nonsense mutation (p.Q180*), were isolated from the same patient at 20–30 day intervals. Phylogenetic analysis indicated a very close relationship between these three mutant strains and a contemporaneous clinical isolate GZ55, which contained an intact WOR2 gene and could be their potential progenitor. These findings indicate that mutations of WOR2 could directly contribute to the enhanced ability of biofilm formation in clinical isolates of C. auris. Given that C. auris does not undergo white–opaque switching, the role of Wor2 appears to have been evolutionarily repurposed to regulate biofilm formation, a distinct virulence trait.

Global prevalence of WOR2 mutations in clinical isolates of C. auris

Since adhesion and biofilm development are critical for environmental persistence, transmission, and virulence in C. auris, we next sought to determine whether mutation of WOR2 is a general phenomenon in clinical isolates. Taking advantage of the publicly available datasets, we analyzed the genomic sequences of 13,314 strains deposited in the NCBI database and found that 3,104 strains (23.3%) harbored a nonsense or frameshift mutation in the WOR2 ORF (Figs. 2a and S4 Table). Strikingly, the prevalence of these mutations exhibited a strong clade-specific distribution. These mutations were found in 29.7% (1846/6217) of Clade I strains, 0.9% (48/5426) of Clade III strains and 73.7% (1210/1641) of Clade IV strains (Figs. 2b and S4 Table). However, no mutation of WOR2 was found in Clade II strains. The mating type of Clades I and IV strains are MTLa, whereas that of Clades II and III are MTLα. Our findings suggest that loss-of-function mutations in this key transcriptional regulator are indeed a notably frequent in certain clades and outbreak settings. It should be noted, however, that the publicly available genome sequences are heavily biased toward isolates from the United States (96.3% of all wor2 mutant strains, 2989 out of 3104). These isolates originated from outbreak-associated clonal expansion. Therefore, the overall mutation of WOR2 (23.3%) may not reflect the real global frequency of mutation due to sampling bias and regional outbreak dynamics.

Further analysis indicated that there were 108, 16, and 13 distinct mutation types of WOR2 in Clades I, III, and IV strains, respectively. Among these, certain mutations were extraordinarily prevalent, suggesting possible selective advantages or founder effects. For example, p.Q225*, p.Q174*, and p.G274fs were detected in 715, 841, and 1170 strains, respectively. The WOR2 mutated strains of C. auris were identified in 17 countries. However, the vast majority (96.30%, 2989 strains) were found in the United States (S4 Table). These major epidemic clusters could result from transmission and outbreaks in hospital settings.

Introduction of an intact WOR2 into the clinical strains of C. auris results in reduced biofilm development

To validate the impact of WOR2 mutations on biofilm formation in clinical isolates, we amplified the intact WOR2 gene from strain GZ10 and transformed it into the clinical strains with WOR2 mutations. The mutant strains used were: GZ108 and GZ241 with nonsense mutations at amino acid positions 173 and 198; GZ144 with a proline to serine substitution mutation at the 5th amino acid; and GZ289 with a frameshift mutation starting from position 74 (Fig. 3a). The reference strain GZ10 harbored an intact WOR2 ORF and was phylogenetically closely related to strains GZ108, GZ144, GZ241, and GZ289 (Fig. S1). Biofilm development assays were performed on silicone squares (Fig. 3b, c). As expected, SEM assays demonstrated that strains GZ108, GZ144, GZ241, and GZ289 with a WOR2 mutation developed robust biofilms, whereas the WOR2-reconstituted and GZ10 reference strains formed much weaker biofilms. Consistently, quantitative biofilm assays in 48-well plates demonstrated that the WOR2-reconstituted and GZ10 reference strains exhibited comparable CFU levels (Fig. 3d).

Deletion of WOR2 in C. auris leads to enhanced biofilm formation

To further verify the regulatory role of Wor2 in C. auris biofilm formation, we deleted WOR2 using homologous recombination assays in strain GZ10. Similar to the clinical strains with WOR2 mutations, the wor2Δ mutants exhibited a significantly enhanced biofilm formation ability both in 48-well plates and on silicone squares (Fig. 4a, d). As expected, the WOR2-reconstituted strains of the deletion mutant showed a comparable ability to that of the reference strain GZ10 (Fig. 4b, e). However, overexpression of WOR2 under the TDH3 promoter in C. auris (Fig. S2a and S2b) significantly attenuated biofilm formation (Fig. 4c, f). These results were further confirmed by CFU assays performed in 48-well plates (Fig. 4g). These results indicate that deletion of WOR2 leads to enhanced biofilm development, while overexpression of this gene suppresses biofilm development. Together with what was observed in clinical strains, these results suggest that Wor2 functions as an important negative regulator of biofilm development in C. auris.

C. auris wor2 mutants demonstrate an increased skin colonization ability

Since the biofilm-forming ability is associated with host colonization, we next performed skin colonization experiments using a newborn mouse skin infection model. Although the immune microenvironment of newborn mouse skin differs from that of humans, this model allows stable colonization of C. auris without skin injury. As shown in Fig. 5a, b, compared to the WOR2-reconstituted strains, clinical strains GZ108, GZ144, GZ241, and GZ289 exhibited a remarkably enhanced ability to colonize the animal skin. Consistently, compared to the reference strain GZ10 and WOR2-reconstituted strain, the wor2Δ mutant also showed an enhanced colonization ability (Fig. 5c). CFU analysis of the excised infected skin tissues confirmed that the mutants had a higher fungal burden than their corresponding reconstituted strains and reference controls (Fig. 5d). These results demonstrate that Wor2 is a critical regulator of host skin colonization.

Global transcriptional profile of C. auris wor2 mutants

To explore the potential regulatory mechanism of Wor2 in biofilm regulation, we performed a comprehensive transcriptomic analysis. This investigation compared the global gene expression profiles of the wild-type reference strain GZ10 against four distinct wor2 mutant strain: three clinical isolates (GZ108, GZ144, GZ241) harboring distinct natural loss-of-function mutations in the WOR2 open reading frame, and a constructed wor2Δ strain. As expected, all four mutant strains displayed a broadly similar profile of global gene expression, confirming that both natural mutations and a complete gene deletion result in a convergent functional outcome on the regulon (Fig. S3). We thus performed a detailed comparative analysis between the four wor2 mutants and strain GZ10.

A total of 606 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the four wor2 mutants and strain GZ10 were identified (Fold change of FPKM > = 2 and adjust p-Value <= 0.01). Of the DEGs, 145 genes were upregulated and 461 genes were downregulated in the wor2 mutants (S3 Table). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that the DEGs were involved in the regulation of multiple critical biological processes related to biofilm formation, cell wall biosynthesis, and host interactions (Fig. 6a). Although GFC1 and BRG1 did not meet the strict consensus criteria for differential expression across all four wor2 mutants, their expression was consistently upregulated in some mutants (Fig. 6b and Table S3). As shown in Fig. 6b, compared to the reference strain GZ10, a subset of genes associated with adhesion and biofilm development including ALS4, BRG1, NDT80, ECM33, and GFC1, were upregulated in the wor2 mutants, whereas several cell wall-related genes including YWP1, CHS1 and PGA26 were downregulated (Figs. 6b and S3 Table). ALS4 is a member of the Als family, which plays a critical role in the regulation of biofilm formation in C. auris9,13,17,18. BRG1, NDT80, and GFC1 encode three transcription factors that are core regulators of biofilm development in C. albicans27,34. We subsequently performed qRT-PCR assays and observed a consistent expression pattern (Fig. 6c). These results imply that Wor2 could function through direct or indirect regulation of the expression of adhesin and transcription factors required for biofilm development.

We further performed transcriptome analysis and identified a co-expression module (Fig. S4a) containing genes co-expressed with WOR2. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that this module is significantly enriched in protein O-linked glycosylation, including PMT1, PMT2, PMT4, MNN12, MNN14, and MNT1. O-linked glycosylation is a key post-translational modification of cell wall proteins in C. albicans, directly affecting cell adhesion and biofilm formation. The protein O-mannosyltransferases encoded by the PMT family initiate O-glycosylation; their deletion leads to abnormal glycosylation of cell wall adhesins (e.g., Als1, Als3), significantly impairing cell adhesion and biofilm formation35–37. MNT1 and MNT2 encode α-1,2-mannosyltransferases involved in the elongation of O-linked mannan chains; double deletion mutants exhibit reduced adhesion capacity and attenuated virulence38,39. This co-expression module also contains the transcription factors Wor2 and Gfc1, with Gfc1 directly activating the expression of adhesin genes such as ALS3 and HWP140–42. Msb2, a membrane-localized mucin, regulates biofilm formation and cell wall integrity via the Cek1 MAPK signaling pathway43,44. Ste11 is an upstream kinase of the MAPK cascade involved in hyphal development and biofilm regulation45. XOG1 encodes a β-1,3-exoglucanase involved in cell wall remodeling and cell adhesion46. RBT5 and AQY1, regulated by Gfc1 (Bcr1), contribute to biofilm impermeability and drug resistance in C. albicans 41(Fig. S4b). The co-expression pattern of these genes suggests that WOR2 may finely regulate cell wall composition and adhesion properties by coordinating O-linked glycosylation with the biofilm regulatory network.

Gfc1 and Wor2 coordinate to regulate biofilm development

It has been known that the Gfc1 (the homolog of C. albicans Bcr1) transcription factor plays a critical regulatory role in hyphal formation and biofilm development in pathogenic Candida species29,47,48. Although clinical strain GZ126 contained a nonsense mutation in WOR2 (Figs. 7a and S1 Table), we found that this strain exhibited a relatively weak biofilm formation ability (Fig. 7b, d, f). Further analysis indicated that this strain harbored an additional frameshift mutation in GFC1 (Fig. 7a and S1 Table). Given the conserved roles of GFC1 in pathogenic Candida and the fact of its upregulation in the C. auris wor2 mutants (Fig. 6b, c), we presumed that Gfc1 could function downstream of Wor2 in the regulation of biofilm development. To test this hypothesis, we introduced an intact GFC1, WOR2, or both genes into strain GZ126. We then compared the biofilm-forming capacities of strain GZ126 and the reconstituted strains (Fig. 7b, d, f). In both silicone square and 48-well plate assays, strain GZ126, the WOR2-reconstituted and both genes-reconstituted strains exhibited remarkably attenuated biofilm formation (Fig. 7b, d, f), whereas the GFC1-reconstituted strain developed robust biofilms. Notably, three isolates (GZ126, GZ82, GZ109) obtained from the same patient over a 30-days period. The earliest isolate GZ126 carried loss of function mutations in both WOR2 (p.Q180) and GFC1 (Y86fs), resulting in attenuated biofilm formation. The two later isolates, GZ82 and GZ109 with only WOR2 mutation and lacking the GFC1 frameshift mutation. The cells harboring an intact GFC1 gene became dominant during infection, suggesting that Gfc1 has a potential promoting effect on biofilm formation and skin colonization.

To further validate these results, we constructed a wor2Δgfc1Δ strain in GZ10 and generated corresponding reconstituted strains. As shown in Fig. 7, e, the wor2Δgfc1Δ double mutant and WOR2-reconstituted strains exhibited a severely defective biofilm development, while the GFC1-reconstituted strain (wor2Δgfc1Δ + GFC1) formed robust biofilms. Quantitative analysis using CFU assays verified the biofilm-forming capacities of both the natural strains and deletion mutants (Fig. 7f). Taken together, these engineered strains demonstrated a similar capacity to their corresponding natural mutants, further indicating that Wor2 and Gfc1 play critical and coordinated roles in biofilm development in C. auris.

Consistent with the capacities of biofilm formation, qRT-PCR analysis indicated that the expression of ALS4 was remarkably increased in the GFC1-reconstituted strains (Fig. 7g). However, the expression level of ALS4 in the strain reconstituted with both WOR2 and GFC1 remained relatively low, implying the opposing regulatory effects of Wor2 and Gfc1 on the expression of ALS4.

To further evaluate the regulatory role of Wor2 or Gfc1 on ALS4, a fragment containing the upstream promoter of ALS4, the GFP reporter gene, and the downstream terminator sequence of ALS4 was amplified using fusion PCR assays and transformed into the clinical mutant strains, GZ10, as well as the wor2Δ, gfc1Δ, wor2Δgfc1Δ, and WOR2-overexpression strains. The various mutant strains were then cultured in YPD liquid medium at 30°C for two days. The fluorescence intensity of these strains was then analyzed using a confocal laser scanning microscopy (Fig. S5). The results showed that the GFP fluorescence intensity in the clinical wor2 mutant strain was significantly stronger than that in GZ10 (Fig. S5). Similarly, the fluorescence intensity in the wor2Δ strain was also significantly stronger than that in GZ10 (Fig. S5). However, the fluorescence intensities in the gfc1Δ, wor2Δgfc1Δ double mutant, and WOR2-overexpression strains were weaker than that in GZ10 (Fig. S5). These findings are consistent with the proposed regulatory mechanism: Wor2 acts as a negative regulator of biofilm development in C. auris. Inactivating mutations in WOR2 lead to increased expression of the adhesin gene ALS4 and this process is affected by GFC1.

Based on the above results, we propose a regulatory network model for biofilm development in C. auris (Fig. S6). Wor2 acts as a negative regulator of biofilm development in C. auris. Inactivating mutations in WOR2 directly or indirectly increases the expression of GFC1 and ALS4. Increased expression of ALS4 leads to enhanced biofilm development.

Discussion

The characteristics of environmental persistence and easy transmission make the emerging fungal pathogen C. auris a significant threat to public health. In the present study, we report that loss-of-function mutation of WOR2, which encodes a transcription factor required for phenotypic switching in pathogenic Candida species, is prevalent in clinical strains of C. auris and leads to an enhanced biofilm-forming capacity. Since biofilms are often associated with increased persistence on the environmental surfaces and colonization on host skin4,5,11,49, this mutation could contribute to the prevalence and rapid transmission of C. auris. We further found that Wor2 could coordinate with the Gfc1 transcription factor to regulate biofilm development. It still remains to be determined whether Wor2 represses GFC1 directly or through an intermediate regulator.

Despite the surging case numbers of C. auris globally, there are still few detailed mechanistic analyses linking specific genomic mutations in clinical isolates to virulence phenotypes. The prevalence of loss-of-function mutations in WOR2 among clinical isolates is strikingly high, reaching 23.3% overall and exceeding 70% in Clade IV strains (Figs. 2 and S4 Table), implying a potential positive selection in clinical settings.

In C. albicans, Wor2 is required for white-to-opaque switching and maintenance of the opaque cell type under regular culture conditions32,33. Our findings indicate that WOR2 inactivation in natural strains of C. auris promotes the development of biofilms (Figs. 3, 4, and 5). Consistent with the absence of white–opaque switching in C. auris, our findings indicate that Wor2 could have been evolutionarily repurposed to function as a negative regulator of biofilm formation. Given the importance of biofilm formation in pathogenesis, spontaneous mutation of this gene in clinical isolates could benefit C. auris as a pathogen, which perhaps represents an environmental adaptation strategy.

As a key regulator of biofilm development, the Bcr1 transcription factor is required for the expression of several cell wall adhesins in C. albicans28,29,50,51. Our results demonstrate that C. auris Gfc1 (the homolog of C. albicans Bcr1) functions as a positive regulator of biofilm formation downstream of Wor2 (Figs. 6 and 7). The expression of ALS4 is upregulated in the C. auris wor2 mutants (Fig. 7). The cell wall adhesin Als4 plays a central role in biofilm formation in C. auris and is required for adhesion to both biotic and abiotic surfaces11,14,17,50,52. Similarly to C. albicans28,29, the expression of the adhesin-encoding gene ALS4 is regulated by Gfc1 in C. auris. Collectively, these data establish a functionally conserved role for Gfc1 in biofilm formation in C. albicans and C. auris.

Recently, an independent study reported that the transcription factor Wor2 acts as a negative regulator of biofilm formation in C. auris53. The authors showed that overexpression of Wor2 downregulates the adhesins Scf1 and Als4 and thus attenuate biofilmdevelopment in C. auris. These findings are consistent with our observation that Wor2 represses biofilm development in this fungus. In the current study, we demonstrate that loss of function mutations of WOR2 occurred at high frequency (23.3%) among clinical strains, implying that WOR2 mutations are prevalent in natural strains. Collectively, the two complementary studies establish Wor2 as a repressor of biofilm formation in C. auris from two different angles.

In conclusion, our study describes an interesting phenomenon of biofilm development promoted by gene mutations in clinical isolates of C. auris and elucidate the function of Wor2 using genetic and transcriptional analyses in this regulation. This mechanism likely represents a key evolutionary strategy adopted by C. auris to thrive in the healthcare setting. However, our current understanding of the detailed regulatory mechanism remains incomplete. Future investigations are needed to identify the direct binding targets of the Wor2 and Gfc1 transcription factors in C. auris. It would also interesting to determine why WOR2 mutations are genetic clade-dependent.

Methods

Ethics statement

The animal studies were conducted in adherence to the guidelines established and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Fudan University (approval number 2023JS030). This specific project received official approval from the same committee.

Strains and culture conditions

The strains used in this study are listed in S1 Table. All C. auris isolates were identified by sequencing the 28S D1/D2 region of rDNA and the 18S internal transcribed spacer (ITS), and their identity was further confirmed by genomic sequencing. C. auris strains were routinely cultured on YPD medium (2% glucose, 2% peptone, 1% yeast extract). For phenotypic observation of clinical isolates, the solid YPD medium (with 2% agar) was supplemented with 5 μg/mL Phloxine B; cultures were incubated at 30 °C aerobically for 3 days.

Construction of plasmids and C. auris mutant strains

The primers used for PCR amplification and fusion in this study are listed in S5 Table. To construct the deletion plasmid pSFS2a-ko-WOR2, the 5’-flank and 3’-flank of WOR2 were amplified from C. auris GZ10 genome. The 5’-flank was inserted into the ApaI/XhoI sites, and the 3’-flank into the SacII/SacI sites of pSFS2a. To knockout WOR2, the plasmid pSFS2a-ko-WOR2 was linearized by ApaI/SacI and transformed into GZ10. The mutant strain was then cultured in 5% YPM (10 g/L yeast extract, 20 g/L peptone, 50 g/L maltose) medium to induce FLP recombinase expression and excise the SAT1 flipper cassette. To generate wor2Δgfc1Δ double mutant strain, a similar strategy was used to knockout GFC1 in wor2Δ background with the plasmid pSFS2a-ko-GFC148.

To complement WOR2 in clinical strains (GZ108, GZ144, GZ241, GZ289, and GZ126), wor2Δ strain, and wor2Δgfc1Δ double mutant strain, a 5’ fragment containing WOR2 ORF (amplified from GZ10) and a 3’fragment (amplified from GZ10) were fused with a fragment of SAT1(amplified from pNIM1). The fusion fragment was then transformed into the mutant strains. The complementation was validated by PCR and Sanger sequencing.

To complement GFC1 in corresponding strains (GZ126, GZ126 + WOR2, wor2Δgfc1Δ), a 5’ fragment containing GFC1 ORF(amplified from GZ10) and a 3’fragment (amplified from GZ10) were fused with a fragment of HYP1 (amplified from pUC-19T). The fusion fragment was then transformed into the mutant strains. The complementation was validated by PCR and Sanger sequencing.

To overexpress WOR2, we constructed plasmid pTDH3-WOR2. WOR2 was amplified from GZ10 and subcloned into the plasmid TDH3p with One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme). TDH3p-WOR2 was linearized by StuI and was integrated at the RPS1 locus in C. auris. The overexpression was validated by PCR and RT-PCR.

We used fusion PCR to construct a fragment containing the GFP reporter gene. First, using the genomic DNA of C. auris as a template, we amplified the ALS4 promoter region (983 bp) with primers LWH307 and LWH308; using the same genomic DNA of C. auris as a template, we amplified the terminator region (983 bp) downstream of ALS4 with primers LWH309 and LWH310; and using plasmid pNIM1 (details provided in the Table. S1) as a template, we amplified a fragment (~3000 bp) containing GFP and SAT1 with primers LWH311 and LWH312. Subsequently, using the three fragments as templates, we performed fusion PCR with primers LWH307 and LWH312. The purified fusion fragment (~5000 bp) was then transformed into different strains, and the resulting clones were verified using primers LWH313 and LWH314. The mutant strains were cultured under conditions inducing biofilm formation, and GFP signals were detected using laser confocal microscopy. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using Image J.

Whole-genome sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

A single colony was inoculated into liquid YPD medium and cultured at 30 °C under ambient air for 24 h. Cells were collected, washed with ddH2O, and genomic DNA was extracted using the TIANamp Yeast DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Genomic DNA libraries were constructed following the manufacturer’s protocol for the DNBseq platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China). The qualified libraries were then sequenced on a DNBSEQ platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw sequencing data (FASTQ files) underwent quality control using the FASTX-Toolkit v0.0.14 (http://hannonlab.cshl.edu/fastx_toolkit/index.html)‌. Publicly available sequencing reads for the C. auris reference strain B8441 (Accession: GCA_002759435.3) were downloaded and processed alongside our in-house datasets.

Raw sequencing data for 13,314 C. auris genomes were downloaded from the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA). All reads were aligned to the C. auris B8441 reference genome using BWA-MEM (v0.7.17)54. Subsequent processing of the resulting BAM files included sorting, duplicate marking with Picard Tools (v2.18.29)55, and local realignment around indels using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK v3.8)56. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were called using the unified genotyper in GATK. The raw SNP calls were stringently filtered with the following criteria: QD < 2.0, FS > 60.0, MQ < 40.0, and ReadPosRankSum < −8.0. SNP annotation was performed using the ANNOVAR software57. To specifically investigate the prevalence of mutations in the WOR2 gene, we extracted all variants within the WOR2 ORF from the annotated VCF files. Nonsynonymous mutations, frameshifts, and premature stop codons were classified as putative loss-of-function or functional alterations.

For phylogenetic reconstruction, a high-quality SNP set was generated by retaining only biallelic SNPs with less than 10% missing data across all samples. A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was inferred from this SNP alignment using RAxML-NG (v1.2.1)58. under the GTR + GAMMA model, with branch support assessed by 1000 bootstrap replicates.

Biofilm formation assay

For the biofilm formation assay, a single colony of selected strains was inoculated into liquid YPD medium at 30 °C overnight, followed by sub-culturing into fresh medium the next day. Cells were harvested in the late exponential growth phase at an OD600 of approximately 1.8 and used for biofilm formation assays. Cells were washed and resuspended in 600 µL YPD medium per well (OD600 0.1–0.2). The 48-wells plate was incubated at 30°C for two days. Biofilms formed then were used for CFU assays and SEM assays. The biofilms were washed three times with PBS and imaged under a stereomicroscope. Subsequently, cells adhered to the well bottoms were treated with trypsin (T4549, Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at 37 °C and then plated on YPD medium for CFU assay.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Samples were fixed overnight in 2.5% pentanediol fixative at 4 °C, sequentially dehydrated in a graded ethanol series of 50%, 75%, and 90% (10 min for each concentration), and then processed with a 50%–75%–90% tertiary butanol gradient (10 min per step). After freeze-drying, the samples were sputter-coated with gold. The treated samples were observed and imaged using a scanning electron microscope (SEM).

Mouse skin infection virulence assays

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the protocol approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Fudan University (Approval No. 2023JS030) and followed the ethical guidelines of this committee. All experimental procedures complied with the ARRIVE guidelines.

All experiments were conducted using newborn female BALB/c mice (3–4 days old, 2–3 g) that were specific-pathogen-free (SPF) and obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. The animals were maintained under SPF conditions at a temperature of 21 °C, with relative humidity ranging from 50 to 70%, and subjected to a 12-h light-dark cycle. To control for litter effects, each independent experiment included pups from at least 2–3 different dams. Pups from the same litter were randomly distributed across all experimental groups (mutant, reconstituted, WT) to avoid confounding by litter-specific factors. One group used three newborn mice. Aliquots of 1 × 107 cells from different strains were resuspended in 2 µL PBS was inoculated onto the dorsal skin of the mice. The inoculation site was covered with a small piece of sterile filter paper, which was then fixed with medical adhesive tape for 24 h. After being anesthetized, the mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation. Such sacrifice methods aligned with the recommendations of the American Veterinary Medical Association. The infected area of the dorsal skin was excised. Tissue samples were fixed for observation via SEM assays. For fungal burden on the skin, the infected skin tissue was excised, homogenized, and plated on YPD agar plates for CFU determination.

RNA-seq and RT-PCR

For RNA-seq, biofilms formed in 48-well plates were first treated with trypsin, followed by washing the cells with pre-chilled ddH₂O. C. auris cells were lysed using a bead-beating homogenizer, and total RNA was isolated and purified with the GeneJET RNA Purification Kit. Total RNA sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Berry Genomics Co., Ltd., Beijing). High-quality sequencing reads were aligned to the C. auris reference genome (GCA_002759435.2) from NCBI using HiSat2 v2.0.5. Transcript expression levels were then estimated using StringTie v1.3.3b, and differentially expressed genes were analyzed using the DESeq2 package in R. Each strain was analyzed with three biological replicate samples.

For RT-PCR analysis, each biological replicate comprised three independent samples. cDNA was synthesized from 800 ng of total RNA using RevertAid H Minus Reverse Transcriptase. Subsequent transcript quantification was carried out by RT-PCR using SYBR Green master mix on a Bio-Rad CFX96 real-time PCR detection system. Gene expression levels were normalized to the C. auris ACT1 gene. Each biological replicate included three technical replicates. Data were analyzed using Bio-Rad CFX Manager 3.1 software, and all results were based on three biological replicates. Primers used for RT-PCR are listed in S5 Table.

Gene co-expression network analysis

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed to identify modules of co-expressed genes associated with WOR2 mutation in C. auris. The analysis was conducted using the WGCNA R package (version 1.71) following the standard pipeline59. Briefly, the top 500 most variable genes across all samples were selected based on median absolute deviation (MAD) to construct the co-expression network. A soft thresholding power (β) was determined using the scale-free topology criterion to achieve an approximate scale-free network topology (R² > 0.85). The adjacency matrix was calculated by raising the absolute value of Pearson correlation coefficients between gene pairs to the power of β, which emphasizes strong correlations while down-weighting weak correlations. The topological overlap matrix (TOM) was computed from the adjacency matrix to measure the network interconnectedness, and the corresponding dissimilarity (1-TOM) was used as the distance metric for hierarchical clustering with average linkage60. Gene modules were identified using the dynamic tree cut algorithm with the following parameters: minimum module size = 30 genes, deepSplit = 2, and cutHeight = 0.99. Modules with eigengene correlation >0.75 were merged to reduce redundancy. Module eigengenes (MEs), defined as the first principal component of the standardized expression matrix for each module, were calculated to represent the overall expression pattern of each module. For network visualization, the ME5 (black module), which showed the strongest association with biofilm formation phenotypes, was selected for detailed analysis. Hub genes within the module were identified based on intramodular connectivity (kME), which measures the correlation between individual gene expression profiles and the module eigengene. Genes with high kME values represent highly connected nodes that may play central roles in the module’s biological function. The co-expression network was visualized using the igraph and ggraph packages in R, where nodes represent genes and edges represent significant co-expression relationships (correlation threshold > 0.7)61,62. Node size was proportional to the degree (number of connections), and node color indicated functional categories.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise specified, all values in this study are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). To ensure reproducibility, all experiments were performed with at least three independent replicates. Graph generation and statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software. Statistical comparisons were performed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test.
