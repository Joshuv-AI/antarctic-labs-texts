Probing Spin-Lifetime Correlations in Entangled Hyperon-AntiHyperon Pairs
Aihong Tang

Abstract
Quantum entanglement has now been demonstrated in several hadronic systems, revealing that non‑classical spin correlations survive even through the strong‑interaction hadronization process. To date, however, all studies have focused exclusively on angular observables, leaving the possibility untouched that quantum coherence might also influence the decay times of entangled partners. In this work we propose two data‑driven tests—relative and absolute lifetime correlations—for Λ ​ – ​ Λ ¯ \Lambda–\bar{\Lambda} pairs produced in high‑energy collisions. By examining the opening‑angle distribution in slices of Δ ​ t \Delta t and constructing a pair-wise lifetime correlator, we search for deviations from independent exponential decay that align with known spin correlations. Successful observation of lifetime correlations between entangled partners would demand a fundamental revision of quantum mechanics’ framework for entanglement, extending the concept beyond its current theoretical boundaries.

A particle’s decay can be regarded as a generalized quantum measurement. In previous treatments, the decays of
\Lambda
and
\bar{\Lambda}
in an entangled pair were regarded as independent measurement
Wu et al. 2024
, implying no intrinsic correlation in their decay times. Importantly, however, no known symmetry requirements or superselection rule in quantum mechanics forbids correlations between the decay times of two spatially separated entangled particles. In fact, once two particles interact they no longer behave as separate carriers of pre‑written instructions, but share a single entangled wavefunction that cannot be factorized over distance. When we measure one particle and force part of this joint wavefunction to take a definite value, the remainder of the wavefunction must adjust instantly—not because any signal has traversed space, but because the theory simply does not permit that part of the wavefunction to remain in two incompatible branches at once. By contrast, for truly independent decays each hyperon’s survival probability follows its own exponential decay law, so the joint probability factorizes as

\displaystyle P_{0}(t_{1},t_{2})=P(t_{1})\,P(t_{2})

(1)

and any covariance
\langle(t_{1}-\bar{t})(t_{2}-\bar{t})\rangle
vanishes. Moreover, when viewed in isolation each decay time is not a conserved property but arises from the intrinsic, memoryless stochastic nature of an unstable state, yielding the familiar exponential lifetime distribution. Recall that if

P(t)
is the survival probability up to time
t
, and the instantaneous decay rate is a constant
\Gamma
, then

\displaystyle P(t+\mathrm{d}t)

\displaystyle=P(t)\bigl(1-\Gamma\,\mathrm{d}t\bigr)

(2)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\,\,\,\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-\Gamma\,P(t)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\,P(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-\Gamma t}\,.

The corresponding decay‐time density
f(t)=\Gamma e^{-\Gamma t}
is purely exponential, and hence two independent exponentials
P(t_{1})P(t_{2})
carry zero covariance.

For an entangled
\Lambda-\bar{\Lambda}
pair, however, the survival amplitude

\displaystyle A(t_{1},t_{2})=\bigl\langle\Psi\bigr|\,e^{-iH_{1}t_{1}}\,e^{-iH_{2}t_{2}}\,\bigl|\Psi\bigr\rangle

(3)

does not decompose into separate single-particle amplitudes, and the resulting joint decay-time distribution

\displaystyle P(t_{1},t_{2})=\bigl|A(t_{1},t_{2})\bigr|^{2}

(4)

can exhibit genuine correlations without violating any principle of quantum mechanics, allowing genuine
t_{1}
,
t_{2}
correlations to arise. Demonstrating a nonzero lifetime–lifetime covariance in the
\Lambda-\bar{\Lambda}
system would reveal a new facet of quantum coherence, enable direct observation of time-domain entanglement in unstable hadrons, and provide tests of decoherence models. In particular, studying decay‑time correlations directly in the lab frame—where moving hyperons experience time dilation—tests whether entanglement persists even when lifetimes are stretched by relativistic motion. This “time‑domain” approach complements traditional angular measurements by incorporating kinematic effects into the decay‑time observables.

II
Methodology

We begin by recalling the established procedure for measuring spin–spin correlations in entangled
\Lambda
–
\bar{\Lambda}
pairs
Kwak et al. 1969
;
Tornqvist 1981
;
Tornqvist 1986
;
STA 2025
. After acceptance corrections, the distribution of the opening angle
\theta^{*}
between the decay proton and antiproton in their respective parent rest frames is fitted to

\frac{1}{N}\,\frac{dN}{d\cos\theta^{*}}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl[\,1+(\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\,P)\,\cos\theta^{*}\bigr],

(5)

where
N
is the total number of same‐event pairs,
\alpha_{1,2}
are the weak‐decay parameters, and the fitted coefficient
P
quantifies the spin correlation.

To search for spin–lifetime correlations, we extend this framework by adding decay‐time information. For each same‐event
\Lambda
–
\bar{\Lambda}
pair we measure the decay times
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
(computed from the reconstructed decay length and momentum,
t=L\,m_{\Lambda}/(|\mathbf{p}|c)
) and reuse the same opening angle
\theta^{*}
. We then form the raw per‐pair quantity

w_{i}\;=\;\alpha_{1}\,\alpha_{2}\,\cos\theta^{*}_{i},

(6)

whose ensemble average in an uncorrelated (isotropic) sample is

\langle w\rangle_{\rm iso}=0
and variance
\langle w^{2}\rangle_{\rm iso}=(\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2})^{2}/3
.

Detector acceptance and efficiency effects are removed via a mixed‐event (ME) baseline: pairing each
\Lambda
with a
\bar{\Lambda}
from a different event matched in centrality and vertex position. Denoting by
N_{\rm SE}(\cos\theta^{*},\Delta t)
and
N_{\rm ME}(\cos\theta^{*},\Delta t)
the counts in same‐event (SE) and mixed‐event samples for given
\cos\theta^{*}
and
\Delta t\equiv t_{2}-t_{1}
, we define

R(\cos\theta^{*},\Delta t)=\frac{N_{\rm SE}(\cos\theta^{*},\Delta t)}{N_{\rm ME}(\cos\theta^{*},\Delta t)},

(7)

the acceptance‐corrected joint distribution in spin and relative decay time.

Entangled lifetime correlations could appear either as a dependence on the time difference
\Delta t
or as a more general covariance in
(t_{1},t_{2})
. A test of
R(\cos\theta^{*},\Delta t)
projected onto
\Delta t
alone probes the
relative‑time
(marginal) distribution

P(\Delta t)=\int_{-1}^{1}R(\cos\theta^{*},\Delta t)\,d\cos\theta^{*},

(8)

but is blind to any
common‑mode
shift of
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
. Conversely, an
absolute‑time
test on the full joint distribution

P(t_{1},t_{2})\propto R\bigl(\cos\theta^{*},t_{2}-t_{1}\bigr)

(9)

can reveal both
\Delta t
‑dependent and sum‑dependent correlations. We therefore implement two complementary, data‑driven tests:

II.1
Relative‐lifetime (
\Delta t
‐binned) test

We partition the range of
\Delta t
into
K
bins centered at
\Delta t_{k}
. In each slice we extract the acceptance‐corrected angular distribution

\displaystyle R_{k}(\cos\theta^{*})\;\equiv\;R\bigl(\cos\theta^{*},\Delta t_{k}\bigr)

(10)

We then fit

\displaystyle r_{k}(\cos\theta^{*})\;\propto\;1+P(\Delta t_{k})\,\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\,\cos\theta^{*}

(11)

to extract
P(\Delta t_{k})
with uncertainty
\sigma_{k}
. Under independent exponential decays
P(\Delta t)
is constant; we quantify any
\Delta t
‐dependence by

\displaystyle\hskip-28.45274pt\chi^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\bigl[P(\Delta t_{k})-\bar{P}\bigr]^{2}}{\sigma_{k}^{2}},\bar{P}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}P(\Delta t_{k})/\sigma_{k}^{2}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}1/\sigma_{k}^{2}},

(12)

and compare to a
\chi^{2}_{K-1}
distribution. A small
p
‑value signals a non‑flat
P(\Delta t)
and hence a relative‐time spin–lifetime correlation.

II.2
Absolute‐time test

To capture any common‐mode or non‐
\Delta t
‐dependent correlation, we standardize the pair quantity
w_{i}
using the mixed‐event moments:

\displaystyle s_{i}\;=\;\frac{w_{i}\;-\;\langle w\rangle_{\rm ME}}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\rm ME}(w)}},\quad\langle s\rangle_{\rm ME}=0,\;\langle s^{2}\rangle_{\rm ME}=1.

(13)

We then form the lifetime product

\displaystyle\tau_{i}=(t_{1,i}-\bar{t})\,(t_{2,i}-\bar{t}),\quad\bar{t}=\langle t\rangle,\;\sigma_{t}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}(t),

(14)

and compute the weighted correlators in SE and ME:

\displaystyle C_{\tau}^{\rm SE}=\frac{\sum_{i\in\rm SE}s_{i}\,\tau_{i}}{\sum_{i\in\rm SE}s_{i}^{2}\,\sigma_{t}^{2}},\quad C_{\tau}^{\rm ME}=\frac{\sum_{j\in\rm ME}s_{j}\,\tau_{j}}{\sum_{j\in\rm ME}s_{j}^{2}\,\sigma_{t}^{2}}.

(15)

The mixed‐event–subtracted statistic

\displaystyle\Delta C_{\tau}=C_{\tau}^{\rm SE}-C_{\tau}^{\rm ME}

(16)

removes acceptance and baseline biases but does not by itself provide the uncertainty or null distribution of
\Delta C_{\tau}
. To estimate its significance, We perform the permutation test using
N_{\rm perm}
trails (for example,
N_{\rm perm}\sim 10^{4}
) in which we randomly shuffle the spin‐weights
\{s_{i}\}
among the lifetime products
\{\tau_{i}\}
in the same‑event sample, recompute

\displaystyle\Delta C_{\tau}^{(j)}=\frac{\sum_{i}s_{i}^{(j)}\,\tau_{i}}{\sum_{i}(s_{i}^{(j)})^{2}\,\sigma_{t}^{2}}\;-\;C_{\tau}^{\rm ME},

(17)

and build the empirical null distribution of
\Delta C_{\tau}
. The permutation p‑value is then the fraction of trials for which

\displaystyle\bigl|\Delta C_{\tau}^{(j)}\bigr|\;\geq\;\bigl|\Delta C_{\tau}\bigr|.

(18)

This method captures the combined sampling fluctuations of both
C_{\tau}^{\rm SE}
and its subtraction by
C_{\tau}^{\rm ME}
without relying on any parametric assumptions.

These two complementary, data‑driven tests—a binned
\Delta t
–dependence of the spin coefficient and a permutation‐based absolute‐time correlator—together provide a robust, acceptance‐corrected search for any spin‐dependent departure from independent exponential decay in
\Lambda
–
\bar{\Lambda}
pairs.

Additionally, one can also define the simplest lifetime–lifetime covariance
\mathrm{Cov}(t_{1},t_{2})\;=\;\bigl\langle(t_{1}-\bar{t})\,(t_{2}-\bar{t})\bigr\rangle
, which by construction vanishes when the joint decay probability factorizes as
P_{0}(t_{1},t_{2})=P(t_{1})P(t_{2})
for independent
\Lambda
–
\bar{\Lambda}
pairs. Any nonzero value of this covariance—whether arising from spin entanglement or other joint dynamics—would signal genuine time-domain correlations in the decay processes, independent of angular observables. Its simplicity and model‑independence make it a natural first test before moving on to more detailed analyses. That said, because it condenses all decay‑time information into a single number, it can mask subtle
\Delta t
‑dependent or spin‑weighted features that the
\Delta t
‑binned and per‑pair correlator methods are designed to resolve.

III
Discussion and Experimental Prospects

Experiments at both RHIC and the LHC routinely reconstruct large numbers of
\Lambda
–
\bar{\Lambda}
pairs per collision
Agakishiev et al. 2012
;
Abelev et al. 2013
, making per‑event statistics for both our relative‑time and absolute‑time analyses within reach. These per-event yields, combined with well-validated technique in measureing spin-spin correlations make the spin-lifetime entanglement test feasible. Hyperon production also extends to heavier strange and charm‑strange species such as
\Xi^{-}
–
\bar{\Xi}^{+}
and
\Xi_{c}
–
\bar{\Xi}_{c}
, or even cross particle species, where analogous lifetime‑correlation studies are feasible. Spin‑correlation techniques established for
\Lambda
–
\bar{\Lambda}
can be directly applied to these channels, broadening the investigation of time‑domain entanglement across multiple baryon families. Testing lifetime covariance in these additional channels would probe the flavor and mass dependence of time‑domain entanglement through the hadronization process.

Even a small nonzero lifetime covariance would provide fresh insight into how quantum coherence persists in unstable systems and could serve as a test of wavefunction‑collapse models
Pearle 1989
;
Bassi et al. 2013
. A positive observation, conversely, would demand a revision of our understanding of quantum coherence in unstable systems and may inspire related studies in meson interferometry
Ambrosino et al. 2006
;
Go et al. 2007
. In all cases, the proposed measurements build on established analysis frameworks to open a new window on quantum coherence in high‑energy collisions.

IV
Conclusion

We have proposed two complementary, data‑driven tests—a binned
\Delta t
scan of spin–lifetime correlations and a per‑pair lifetime covariator—that extend standard hyperon spin measurements into the time domain. By reconstructing proper times for each hyperon in the pair, applying mixed‑event baselining, and employing permutation testing, this methodology can be adapted to various baryon species. Observing a nonzero lifetime–lifetime covariance would constitute the first direct evidence of time‑domain entanglement in unstable hadrons, while stringent null results would impose novel constraints on wavefunction‑collapse and decoherence models.

Given the large hyperon yields per collision at RHIC and the LHC and the maturity of spin‑correlation frameworks, these measurements can be carried out with existing data sets. We therefore encourage experimental collaborations to pursue these tests, opening a new frontier in the study of quantum coherence in high‑energy collisions.
