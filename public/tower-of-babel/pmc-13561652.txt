Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter-2 Inhibitor-Metformin Therapy in South Asians: From Guideline Adoption to Context-Specific Safety Gaps
Authors: Rabia Nizam, Chanchal Devi, Rameen Shafqat, Maryam Shuja
Published: 2026

The expanding use of sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitor-metformin therapy reflects strong trial-based evidence of cardiovascular and renal benefit. However, its routine application in South Asian populations may overlook important population-specific safety considerations. With recent guidelines supporting earlier and broader use of SGLT2 inhibitors, this gap has become increasingly relevant in routine care. South Asians with type 2 diabetes mellitus carry a disproportionately high cardiometabolic burden [1], yet they remain underrepresented in major outcome trials (e.g., approximately 2% in the EMPA-REG OUTCOME trial) [2].

Beyond underrepresentation, current guidelines provide limited direction on how environmental, cultural, and healthcare-access factors may influence clinical safety in routine practice, highlighting a gap between trial evidence and real-world implementation. South Asians often exhibit higher metabolic risk at lower body mass indices [3]. The osmotic diuretic effect of SGLT2 inhibitors may interact with heat exposure, physically demanding work, and inconsistent access to fluids—conditions common in South Asia but underrepresented in trials. These factors may increase susceptibility to volume depletion, genitourinary infections, and acute kidney injury, particularly in resource-limited settings where delayed care-seeking is common [4,5]. Thus, safety is shaped not only by pharmacologic effects but also by environmental and structural determinants.

Fasting is another important and often underappreciated modifier of risk. In Muslim-majority South Asian populations, prolonged fasting during Ramadan—often undertaken without structured medication adjustment—may increase the likelihood of euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis, even in the absence of marked hyperglycemia [6,7]. Despite increasing clinical awareness, major guidelines provide limited practical direction on SGLT2 inhibitor use during fasting [7], and such real-world behaviors are rarely incorporated into trial designs. This concern is amplified as these agents are increasingly used earlier in the disease course.

Bridging this gap requires moving beyond uniform prescribing toward individualized, context-aware care. A simple, practice-oriented risk assessment that considers hydration reliability, occupational heat exposure, fasting practices, and access to healthcare may support safer use in routine settings. Temporary treatment interruption during prolonged fasting, acute illness, or periods of dehydration risk, along with structured patient education on hydration and sick-day rules, should be considered. Patients with multiple risk factors may benefit from closer follow-up or individualized therapy selection. In parallel, incorporating these contextual variables into guideline updates and strengthening regional pharmacovigilance efforts would help ensure that safe implementation keeps pace with expanding use. Recognizing these context-specific modifiers is essential for translating trial benefits into safe and equitable outcomes in South Asian populations.
