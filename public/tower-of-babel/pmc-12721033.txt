AAV-PHP.eB achieves superior neuronal transduction over AAV9 in pigtail macaques following intracerebroventricular administration
Authors: Michal G Fortuna, Lyle H Nyberg, Naz Taskin, Avery Hunker, Natalie Weed, Kathryn Gudsnuk, Melissa Berg, Boaz P Levi, Ed Lein, Jonathan T Ting
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors are pivotal in gene therapy for neurological disorders due to their ability to enable long-term gene expression in the central nervous system (CNS). However, transducing larger brains, such as those of non-human primates (NHPs), remains challenging, necessitating alternative delivery routes and optimized capsids. This study directly compares the transduction efficiency and biodistribution of the benchmark AAV9 and its engineered derivative, AAV-PHP.eB, following intracerebroventricular (i.c.v.) administration in juvenile Macaca nemestrina. Employing a neuron-specific promoter and nuclear-localized reporter, we systematically quantified transduction across cortical, subcortical, and spinal regions. AAV-PHP.eB demonstrated significantly higher transduction rates in cortical and spinal regions compared to AAV9, despite similar expression patterns. Both vectors exhibited limited subcortical penetration and significant peripheral leakage, highlighting key challenges in CNS targeting. This is the first study to quantitatively compare AAV-PHP.eB and AAV9 in NHPs, providing valuable insights into the advantages and limitations of engineered AAV capsids for CNS gene therapy. These findings lay a critical foundation for optimizing vector designs and delivery strategies to improve outcomes in clinical applications for neurodegenerative and neurodevelopmental disorders.Keywords: adeno-associated virus, non-human primates, gene therapy, central nervous system, intracerebroventricular administration, biodistribution, AAV9

Introduction

Neurological diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease, frontotemporal dementia, epilepsy, and various inherited neurodevelopmental disorders have long posed significant challenges for effective treatment, primarily due to the complex nature of brain physiology and the blood-brain barrier (BBB), which limits the access of systemically delivered therapeutic agents to the central nervous system (CNS). Gene therapy offers an alternative in treating neurological disorders by correcting underlying genetic defects or delivering therapeutic transgene cargos to restore normal brain function.1,2 Adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) have become pivotal tools for gene therapy due to their ability to deliver genetic material safely and with sustained expression.1,3,4

Among all available AAV serotypes, AAV9 has been widely adopted in both preclinical and clinical studies to address CNS disorders due to its ability to traverse the BBB and transduce a broad range of CNS cell types, including neurons and glia.3,4,5 However, AAV9 has relatively low BBB crossing efficiency in large primate brains, necessitating high viral loads when delivered intravenously (i.v.), raising safety concerns due to potential immune responses and off-target effects.6,7,8 Moreover, unlike in mice where AAV9 efficiently transduces neurons following i.v. administration, in non-human primates (NHPs) and humans this route results primarily in glial transduction, with astrocytes and microglia being the most prominently targeted cell types.6,9 Several comparative studies have demonstrated that i.v.-delivered AAV9 yields rather poor neuronal tropism and suboptimal CNS coverage in NHPs, in stark contrast to robust CNS transduction observed in rodents.6,9,10,11,12 For example, Hinderer et al. showed that even at high doses, i.v.-delivered AAV9 resulted in sparse CNS labeling and predominant peripheral organ targeting.10 In Samaranch et al., intracisterna magna (i.c.m.) delivery of AAV9 achieved substantially more widespread and effective brain transduction than i.v.6 These observations underscore the need to explore alternative delivery routes, such as intracerebroventricular (i.c.v.) or i.c.m., and to evaluate novel capsids that might enhance CNS targeting in primates.

Indeed, extensive efforts have been made to engineer AAV capsids to improve their tropism and biodistribution profile and explore alternative delivery routes and methods.1,3,4,12,13 One promising approach is i.c.v. administration, which bypasses the need for BBB crossing by delivering AAVs directly into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). This method, in principle, allows for a larger distribution of viral particles across the CNS, facilitating broader transduction compared to i.v. or intrathecal delivery.6,10,14

AAV-PHP.eB is an engineered variant of AAV9 capsid with enhanced CNS tropism and superior transduction efficiency following i.v. delivery.13,15 However, these effects are species specific as NHPs lack LY6A (SCA-1), the receptor for BBB crossing in rodents.16,17,18,19 Nevertheless, a recent study in adult rhesus macaques showed that AAV-PHP.eB achieved greater CNS transduction than its predecessor, AAV-PHP.B, when administered intrathecally.20 The efficacy of AAV-PHP.eB following i.c.v. administration in NHPs remains untested, leaving open the question of whether it offers superior transduction compared to AAV9 in this delivery context. Using i.c.v. delivery of clinically relevant doses in juvenile Macaca nemestrina and a neuron-specific, nuclear-localized reporter, we systematically quantified neuronal transduction across the brain and spinal cord and show that AAV-PHP.eB achieves superior CNS transduction vs. AAV9.

Results

To facilitate the comparison of neuronal transduction and biodistribution of AAV9 and AAV-PHP.eB in the NHP brain, we designed a viral vector to label neuronal nuclei ubiquitously across the brain. The vector was packaged into both AAV9 and PHP.eB capsids and unilaterally injected i.c.v. at a dose of 4 × 1013 vg/kg in juvenile Macaca nemestrina (Figure 1A). No physiological or behavioral abnormalities were observed in any subject following vector administration. After 6 weeks, the brain, spinal cord, and peripheral organs were collected for molecular and histological analysis (Figure 1B). Necropsy and pathological evaluation revealed no vector-related macroscopic or microscopic findings, indicating no treatment-related adverse effects.

Unilateral i.c.v. delivery results in widespread CNS transduction

We first qualitatively evaluated the relative expression profiles for AAV-PHP.eB vs. AAV9 vectors by immunostaining for the 3 × FLAG tag under matched staining and imaging conditions. Despite performing unilateral injections, we observed no obvious difference in the number of FLAG+ cells between the ipsilateral and contralateral hemispheres across both the groups (Figure 2). Although slightly denser labeling could be observed near the injection site, there was no statistically significant difference in FLAG+ cell density between the ipsilateral and contralateral hemispheres across both the groups (Figure 2; see Figure 4B for quantification). Both vectors labeled the brain parenchyma to varying degrees of completeness, ranging from occasional nuclei and sparse cell groupings to densely transduced areas resulting in irregular coverage. Most FLAG+ nuclei were observed in the cerebral cortex. Areas in the vicinity of the injection site or in direct contact with the lateral ventricle were typically more densely labeled. In contrast, deeper, subcortical structures such as putamen and thalamus regions more distant from the site of CSF delivery showed only a few FLAG+ cells scattered throughout (Figures 2 and 3B). Although both serotypes yielded a similar overall pattern of transgene expression, there was an obvious difference in the number of FLAG+ cells, with the PHP.eB cohort exhibiting higher labeling density compared to AAV9. Despite individual variations in expression pattern across the brain (see Table 1), there was a general trend toward more intense labeling in the posterior cortices (e.g., parietal and visual cortex) and less prominent viral penetration in the temporal lobe and prefrontal areas (Figures 2A2, 2A3, 2B2, and 2B3). Motor and somatosensory cortices were consistently among the areas with the highest levels of transduction.

Mostly sparse or area-specific labeling could be observed throughout the hypothalamus (e.g., paraventricular hypothalamus), parts of the midbrain (e.g., the periaqueductal gray and superior colliculus), and various nuclei of the hindbrain (e.g., pontine and hypoglossal nuclei). Hindbrain structures closer to the fourth ventricle and the medullary surface showed considerable transduction, whereas very few FLAG+ cells could be found in the core of the tissue. The cerebellum, aside from aspects of the deep cerebellar nuclei neighboring the fourth ventricle, showed no appreciable transgene expression (Figures 2A4, 2A5, 2B4, and 2B5). Detailed comparative examination showed that both serotypes displayed similar characteristics in terms of resultant expression pattern, where both groups lacked uniform transduction. For instance, in the context of the same anatomical structure, a region with high density of FLAG+ cells could neighbor areas displaying very few FLAG+ cells (Figure 3A). Additionally, there appeared to be a tendency for labeled cells to occupy the upper cortical layers 2/3 or deeper layer 5 (Figures 3A, 3A′, and 3E), suggesting that excitatory pyramidal neurons may be preferentially transduced. To explore this further, we co-stained the tissue for markers of neocortical GABAergic interneuron subtypes—parvalbumin (PV) and calretinin (Figures 3C and 3D). We found the presence of both FLAG+/PV+ and FLAG+/calretinin+ cells, indicating that both serotypes readily transduce these GABAergic interneuron populations.

Another manifestation of the irregular transduction pattern was the presence of FLAG+ neurons in large clusters. The presence of these clusters was correlated with areas of high transduction, such as the somatosensory or visual cortex (Figures 2A2, 2B2, and 3E). We also noted a higher proportion of clusters in the cortex and often near large descending blood vessels (Figure 3E′). To examine this further, we co-stained the tissue for GLUT1, a marker in endothelial cells that line the BBB (Figure 3F and 3F′). While many clusters were found surrounding or neighboring medium to large vessels, this association was not exclusive. We also examined whether a high concentration of transgene-expressing cells can result in local cellular pathology. We stained the tissue for IBA1, a microglia marker, but no signs of microglial activation were observed near these clusters (Figures 3G1 and 3G2). Furthermore, we did not observe any signs of neuronal loss, gliosis, or astrocyte activation, as assessed by GFAP co-staining (Figure S1).

AAV-PHP.eB yields higher neuronal transduction rates compared to AAV9

We further assessed neuronal transduction and transgene expression resulting from AAV-PHP.eB and AAV9 i.c.v. administration by quantifying FLAG+ and NeuN+ cells in matched cortical regions along the anterior-posterior axis of the brain, namely in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), motor cortex (M1), somatosensory cortices (S1/S2), posterior parietal cortex (PPC), and visual cortex (V1/V2) (see materials and methods for details). Overall, 336 regions of interest (ROIs) (∼1.4 million NeuN+ cells) and 343 ROIs (∼1.3 million NeuN+ cells) were quantified in both the AAV-PHP.eB and AAV9 groups, respectively. Consistent with qualitative observations, the AAV-PHP.eB cohort displayed more expression in all analyzed brain regions compared to the AAV9 group (Figure 4A). There was no statistical difference between regions along the anterior-posterior axis in the PHP.eB group (combined ipsilateral and contralateral sides), with median percentage neuronal labeling as follows: PFC = 8.96% (4.42–14.80, n = 59), M1 = 10.39% (6.55–18.03, n = 69), S1/S2 = 11.99% (9.35–15.29, n = 72), PPC = 10.95% (9.40–17.21, n = 70), and V1/V2 = 8.03% (4.23–14.28, n = 66), p = 0.286. In contrast, in the AAV9 cohort, there was a significant difference between areas (p = 0.003), with visual cortex showing a higher percentage of labeled neurons than M1, S1/S2, and PPC. Median values were as follows: PFC = 2.14% (1.36–3.75, n = 66), M1 = 1.88% (1.09–4.04, n = 66), S1/S2 = 1.57% (0.98–3.17, n = 69), PPC = 1.93% (1.18–2.62, n = 65), and V1/V2 = 4.22% (2.39–7.03, n = 77). The wide range of transduction rates observed across animals likely reflects biological variability in viral particle distribution and uptake, consistent with previous reports using CSF-based AAV delivery in NHPs. When comparing the transduction rate between the hemispheres, no significant differences were found in either group: PHP.eB ipsilateral 10.83% (6.16–14.46, n = 169) vs. contralateral 11.87% (6.59–17.38, n = 167) (p = 0.348) and AAV9 ipsilateral 1.90% (1.34–3.11, n = 171) vs. contralateral 2.46% (1.20–4.23, n = 172) (p = 0.299) (Figure 4B). Importantly, there was a significant difference in cortical neuron transduction rate between the two serotypes, with median PHP.eB value of 11.1% (6.56–15.70, n = 336) compared to 2.2% (1.30–4.09, n = 343) for AAV9 (p < 0.001) (Figure 4C).

Next, we compared AAV9 and AAV-PHP.eB expression in the spinal cord. Cervical, thoracic, and lumbar segments of the spine were stained and quantified (Figures 4D–4G). Both serotypes transduced all levels of the spinal cord, with FLAG+ cells detected in all regions of the gray matter (dorsal and ventral horns, as well as the peri-central canal area) (Figures 4D and 4E). A higher percentage of neuronal labeling was observed in the more distal parts of the cord: median PHP.eB, cervical = 11.22% (8.99–20.16, n = 12), thoracic = 20.83% (14.51–31.22, n = 12), and lumbar = 18.46% (12.17–38.44, n = 12); median AAV9, cervical = 5.48% (3.41–9.55, n = 12), thoracic = 17.01% (9.57–21.19, n = 11), and lumbar = 12.18% (9.21–16.17, n = 11) (Figure 4F). When considering all segments together, AAV-PHP.eB had ∼1.5-fold higher neuronal transduction in the spinal cord compared to AAV9: median rate for AAV-PHP.eB 16.39% (11.48–22.97, n = 36) vs. 10.26% (6.63–16.38, n = 34) for AAV9 (Figure 4G).

Significant portion of the virus leaks from the CNS into the periphery

To complement the histological examination, we also sampled selected brain areas and peripheral organs for viral DNA by droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) (Figure 5). In the motor cortex, the AAV-PHP.eB group exhibited a median value of 5.7 (2.48–8.37, n = 6) vector genome copies/diploid genome (copies/dg), whereas the AAV9 group totaled 0.73 (0.50–2.02, n = 6) copies/dg (p = 0.009) (5.5-fold difference with immunohistochemistry [IHC] vs. 7.8 with ddPCR). Similarly, in the visual cortex, the AAV-PHP.eB group had a median of 2.84 (1.87–4.87, n = 6) copies/dg compared to 1.28 (0.74–1.62, n = 6) copies/dg for AAV9 (p = 0.041) (1.9-fold increase assessed by IHC vs. 2.2 with ddPCR). In agreement with histological observations, both vectors exhibited minimal penetration of the putamen, with no significant difference between the groups: 0.11 (0.07–0.20, n = 6) copies/dg for AAV-PHP.eB vs. 0.13 (0.07–0.29, n = 6) copies/dg for AAV9 (p = 0. 589). The analysis of the peripheral tissues revealed that much of the vector leaks into the general circulation and gets distributed though the body. Mean vector concentration was relatively low in the skeletal muscle (PHP.eB: 0.40 ± 0.015 copies/dg vs. 0.28 ± 0.059 copies/dg for AAV9; p = 0.069) and the kidney (PHP.eB: 0.49 ± 0.24 copies/dg vs. 0.49 ± 0.13 copies/dg for AAV9; p = 0.993), moderate in the heart (PHP.eB: 1.32 ± 0.71 copies/dg vs. 1.62 ± 0.61 copies/dg for AAV9; p = 0.606), and notably high in the liver (PHP.eB: 202.2 ± 134.8 copies/dg vs. 247.8 ± 90.7 copies/dg for AAV9; p = 0.656).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive comparison of neuronal transduction efficiency and vector biodistribution of AAV-PHP.eB vs. AAV9 following unilateral i.c.v. administration in juvenile macaques. Using a neuron-specific promoter and a nuclear-localized reporter gene, we systematically assessed transduction across cortical, subcortical, spinal, and peripheral tissues. While both vectors achieved widespread CNS distribution with similar expression patterns, AAV-PHP.eB exhibited a significantly higher percentage of neuronal labeling throughout the brain and spinal cord. These findings highlight the superior efficiency of AAV-PHP.eB and its potential advantages in CNS-targeted gene therapy.

Key findings

Histological and molecular analyses revealed that AAV-PHP.eB outperformed AAV9 in most assessed brain regions. In cortical areas, AAV-PHP.eB achieved transduction rates six times higher in motor and somatosensory cortices, with an average 4-fold increase across the cortex. In the spinal cord, the magnitude of this difference was not as pronounced, with AAV-PHP.eB averaging ∼1.5-fold increase in neuronal labeling rate compared to AAV9. In contrast, both vectors exhibited minimal transduction in deeper brain structures, highlighting persistent challenges in achieving efficient subcortical delivery with CSF-based routes in NHPs.11,21,22,23

Although AAV-PHP.eB’s enhanced BBB-crossing and improved performance in rodent models is attributed to binding to the LY6A receptor, which is absent in NHPs, AAV-PHP.eB still showed superior transduction when delivered via the i.c.v. route.11,13,16 Interestingly, in mice, i.c.v. administration of AAV-PHP.eB showed no transduction or tropism advantage over AAV9 in either neonates or adults.16 Chatterjee et al. reported enhanced cortical and cerebellar transduction with AAV-PHP.eB over AAV9 following i.c.m. delivery in adult rats.24 The only other study evaluating AAV-PHP.eB’s transduction efficiency in the CNS of NHPs via the CSF route was Arotcarena et al., who compared it with its precursor, AAV-PHP.B, in adult rhesus macaques following a single lumbar intrathecal injection.20 AAV-PHP.eB demonstrated significantly greater and broader CNS transduction than AAV-PHP.B. This suggests that mechanisms beyond BBB-crossing influence AAV-PHP.eB’s enhanced brain penetration and transduction efficacy compared to its predecessors, warranting further investigation.

An important observation in this study was the uneven, patchy distribution of transgene expressing neurons within the cortex, an expression pattern reported before in NHPs.6,10,23 Areas of dense neuronal labeling were often adjacent to regions with sparse transduction, indicating non-uniform viral particle distribution post injection. In highly transduced regions, prominent clustering of labeled cells was frequently observed. The association of these clusters with brain vasculature suggests that penetrance of viral particles deep into the parenchyma is mediated by CSF flow along perivascular spaces, commonly referred to as the glymphatic pathway.25 A critical observation in this respect is that clusters were observed along some, but not all, vessels, indicating that the direction of CSF flow along the glymphatic spaces and viral clearance mechanism might be key factors influencing the spread and extent of parenchymal transduction following direct CSF administration of AAVs.

The extensive histological evaluation and systematic quantification of neuronal transduction across multiple brain areas in this study is noteworthy. The choice of the neuron-specific hSyn1 promoter was essential to distinguish the relative efficiency of AAV-PHP.eB and AAV9 in targeting neurons. Many studies rely on ubiquitous promoters, such as CMV or CAG, and cytosolic markers such as EGFP, which visualize transduced cell populations but hinder accurate quantification due to intense neuropil labeling.6,11,20 Consequently, NHP studies often resort to qualitative or indirect methods for reporter gene quantification (e.g., bulk tissue qPCR/ddPCR or fluorescent signal intensity quantification). Our design enabled robust, cell-type-specific quantification of transduction and revealed clear differences in neuronal targeting between the two serotypes.

Implications for CNS gene therapy

The higher transduction efficiency of AAV-PHP.eB in the cortex and spinal cord suggests its potential utility for CNS gene therapy applications that require broad cortical coverage or spinal delivery. This includes disorders such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), Rett syndrome, or epileptic encephalopathies, where effective gene delivery to upper motor neurons or widespread cortical networks may be beneficial. However, limited transduction in subcortical structures, such as the putamen and thalamus, remains a challenge, and diseases primarily affecting subcortical regions, such as Parkinson’s or Huntington’s disease, are unlikely to benefit from this delivery route in its current form. Indeed, our findings underscore the importance of delivery routes in determining CNS transduction efficiency. While we did not directly compare i.v. administration, multiple studies have shown that i.v. delivery of AAV9 or AAV-PHP.B in NHPs results in poor brain penetration and limited neuronal transduction, even at high doses, making CSF-based delivery routes like i.c.v. potentially more effective and clinically relevant for certain CNS disorders.5,6,10,18,21 Future studies should explore alternative delivery methods or capsid modifications to improve transduction in deep brain regions. Ongoing capsid engineering may yield variants with enhanced BBB-crossing capabilities in NHPs, enabling more uniform distribution via brain vasculature.2,13,26,27,28 Nonetheless, the consistent and robust cortical transduction achieved with AAV-PHP.eB via i.c.v. delivery in juvenile NHPs represents a significant step toward scalable and clinically viable gene therapy strategies for cortical disorders.

Potential limitations and challenges

Despite equal dosing per kilogram body weight (4 × 1013 vg/kg), differences in cohort body weight may have introduced bias, potentially underestimating AAV9 transduction efficiency. Although no physiological or behavioral abnormalities were observed following vector administration, and necropsy revealed no evidence of toxicity, we cannot exclude the possibility of subclinical effects such as transient thrombocytopenia or liver microvascular injury,8 as clinical chemistry parameters (e.g., serum liver enzymes) were not assessed in this study. Lastly, while the neuron-specific promoter and nuclear-localized reporter enabled precise quantification, the exclusion of non-neuronal cell populations (e.g., astrocytes and oligodendrocytes) limits the broader applicability of these findings.

Conclusion and future directions

AAV-PHP.eB achieves significantly higher neuronal transduction efficiency than AAV9 in NHPs following i.c.v. administration, making it a promising candidate for CNS gene therapy, particularly for cases requiring extensive cortical neuron transduction. Limited subcortical transduction and peripheral leakage remain considerable challenges. Future research should focus on optimizing delivery routes and vector designs to enhance deep brain targeting and minimize off-target effects. These findings underscore the need for primate-tailored capsid engineering and rational route selection to ensure efficient neuronal targeting at clinically relevant doses.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and surgery

NHPs

Experiments with macaque monkeys conformed to the guidelines provided by the US National Institutes of Health and were approved by the University of Washington Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). The experiments reported herein were performed on seven juvenile male Macaca nemestrina monkeys weighing 3.05–4.5 kg. All animals used in this study were pre-screened and determined to be seronegative for AAV9-neutralizing antibodies. Three animals received AAV-PHP.eB virus injection (4.4–4.5 kg), three received AAV9 injection (3.05–3.35 kg), and one control subject received saline injection (4.5 kg). Virus-injected animals received a dose of 4e13 vg/kg of respective virus (1.2–1.8 mL total infusion volume).

Viral vectors

Both serotypes carried the same transgene cassette, consisting of a 3× FLAG-tagged histone 2B under the control of the human synapsin 1 promoter (hSyn1). The hSyn1 promoter was selected to drive neuron-specific expression of the reporter transgene, enabling precise assessment of neuronal transduction. The use of a nuclear-localized reporter further enhanced accuracy in detecting individual transduced neurons.

Both vectors were produced in HEK293 cells by transient transfection with plasmids containing the transgene cassette, helper plasmid, and rep/cap sequences.29 Viral particles were purified using POROS CaptureSelect AAVX affinity chromatography, followed by ultracentrifugation in a cesium chloride density gradient to enrich for full capsids.30,31 The collected AAV particles were concentrated and buffer exchanged using a centrifugal filter unit with a 100 kDa molecular weight cutoff.

The final AAV preparation, suspended in 20 mM Tris, 120 mM NaCl, 74 mM trehalose, and 0.1% poloxamer, pH 7.4, was aliquoted and stored at −80°C until further use. ddPCR was used to determine the viral genome (vg) titers, and both vector types were formulated to comparable final titers (1E14 vg/mL). The capsid-to-genome ratios for both AAV9 and PHP.eB were close to 1, indicating a high proportion of full particles and minimal presence of empty capsids. To assess vector integrity and quality, additional biophysical analyses were performed. Dynamic light scattering confirmed that both AAV preparations had particle sizes within the expected range (∼28 and 29 nm, AAV9 and PHP.eB, respectively), with no detectable aggregation. Size-exclusion chromatography analysis showed that both preparations consisted almost entirely of monomeric AAV species (99% and 97% monomers, AAV9 and PHP.eB, respectively). Together, these characterization steps give us confidence that the AAV9 and AAV-PHP.eB vectors used in this study were of high quality and comparable in physical properties and purity.

Surgery and i.c.v. administration

Viral vectors were injected during surgery in an operating room under isoflurane and fentanyl anesthesia. Planning of the injection and corresponding stereotactic coordinates were based on prior anatomical MRI scans of each subject. Once anesthetized, subjects were positioned in the stereotactic frame in prone position, skull was exposed, and a burr hole was drilled few millimeters off the midline at the predetermined anteroposterior (AP) and mediolateral (ML) levels. A needle was mounted on the stereotactic arm, positioned over the opening, and dura was nicked at the site of tip insertion. The needle was lowered through the opening to planned depth, and correct placement in the ventricle was verified by drawing a small amount of CSF into the syringe. Once access to the ventricle was confirmed, virus solution was loaded into a syringe.

AAV9 and PHP.eB vector stocks were prepared at a concentration of 1 × 1014 vg/mL. Each animal received an injection calculated to deliver a total dose of 4 × 1013 vg/kg. For the PHP.eB cohort, animals Z68 (4.5 kg) and Z75 (4.5 kg) each received 1.8 mL, and Z59 (4.4 kg) received 1.75 mL. For the AAV9 cohort, Z24 (3.35 kg) received 1.34 mL, Z13 (3.15 kg) received 1.26 mL, and Z91 (3.05 kg) received 1.22 mL. The control subject, Z05 (4.5 kg), received 1.8 mL of saline. Vector solution was administered manually in intermittent boluses; total volume was divided into six equal parts (0.2–0.3 mL per part), and each part was slowly injected followed by a 10 min pause. Thus, injections were administered over approximately 1 h for each animal. After completing the injection, the needle was removed, and the brain surface was rinsed with sterile saline. A piece of DuraGen (Integra, USA) was fitted into the burr hole to cover the opening. The subcutaneous tissues and skin were sutured closed, tissue adhesive (Vetbond Tissue Adhesive 1469, 3M, USA) was applied, and incisional bupivacaine was administered. After the surgery, animals were placed in individual cages overnight and resocialized after recovery. Surgery and animal husbandry were provided by the staff of the Washington National Primate Research Center (WaNPRC). All animals were monitored daily by veterinary staff and showed no observable adverse effects following vector administration.

Tissue processing

Six weeks post surgery, animals were euthanized with pentobarbital and transcardially perfused with NMDG artificial CSF (NMDG aCSF) solution.32 The brain and spinal cord (sectioned into cervical, thoracic, and lumbar/sacral segments) were collected; spinal segments were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin (Cat# 16004-128, VWR International LLC, USA). The brain was placed in ice-cold NMDG aCSF for transport, then hemisected, and sliced into ∼5 mm coronal slabs (12–14 per hemisphere). Alternate slabs were NBF fixed (48–72 h) or flash-frozen in isopentane.32 Fixed slabs underwent 30% sucrose infiltration and were sectioned into 50 μm coronal slices on a sliding microtome (Leica Camera AG SM2000R). Sections were stored in PBS with sodium azide at 4°C for IHC processing. Peripheral organ samples (liver, kidney, muscle, and heart) were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until further use.

IHC

Complete immunostaining procedure on free-floating sections was described previously.33 The following primary antibodies were used: (1) anti-FLAG (1:500, mouse, M2 from Sigma-Aldrich Inc., USA), (2) anti-NeuN (1:1,000, rabbit, ABN78 from EDM Millipore Corp., USA), (3) anti-PV (1:5,000, mouse, PV235 from Swant), (4) anti-calretinin (1:2,000, rabbit, Cat# CR7697 from Swant), (5) anti-Iba1 (1:1,000, rabbit, Cat# 019-19741, Wako, Japan), and (6) anti-Glut1 (1:1,000, rabbit, Cat# 07-1401 from EDM Millipore Corp., USA). Only for the GFAP and FLAG co-staining, following antibodies were used: Alexa 647 mouse anti-GFAP (1:250, Cat# 560298 from BD Biosciences, USA) and rabbit anti-FLAG (1:200, Cat#902401 from BioLegend, USA). Secondary antibodies included the following (all from Invitrogen/Life Technologies Corp., USA): (1) Alexa 555 donkey anti-rabbit (1:500, A32732), (2) Alexa 568 donkey anti-rabbit (1:500, A10042), (3) Alexa 488 goat anti-mouse (1:500, A11001 and A11029), (4) Alexa 647 donkey anti-mouse (1:1,000, A31571), and (5) Alexa 488 goat anti-rabbit (1:1,000, A11008). DAPI (Cat# 62248 Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added at 1–2 μg/mL for nuclear counterstain. Spinal cord segments were paraffin embedded post fixation, cut into 5 μm sections, and mounted onto glass slides. For IHC, standard deparaffinization and rehydration protocol was used (xylene and ethanol gradient), followed by antigen retrieval using heat-induced step: 10 min wash with TBS at room temperature (RT), 30 min incubation in CC1 reagent (Cat# 950-500, Roche) at 95°C, and washed 3 × 5 min in TBS at RT. After antigen retrieval, standard IHC protocol for FLAG and NeuN staining was followed, with modified antibody concentrations: 1:3,000 for FLAG and NeuN primary antibody and 1:2,000 for secondary antibody.

Molecular biology

For DNA isolation, frozen tissue was moved from −80° to −20° an hour before tissue punching (with “Disposable Biopsy Punch,” Robbins Instruments, Ref# RBP-40), which was done on a dissection table chilled to −20°. For the brain tissue analysis, one tissue punch per hemisphere was taken from each animal per region, yielding two samples per animal and six samples per group. The same approach was applied to the peripheral tissues, where one punch per tissue per animal was collected, resulting in n = 3 biological samples per group. Viral DNA was isolated with DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). After isolation, DNA concentration was measured (NanoDrop, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and diluted to working concentrations ranging from 0.03 to 10 ng/μL. Prior to PCR reaction, template DNA was treated with SmaI restriction enzyme (New England Biolabs, USA). PCR was performed with PTC Tempo Deepwell Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, USA). AAV genome copies were quantified by ddPCR in ddPCR Supermix for probes (Bio-Rad—no. 18630254) with WPRE3 primer/probe (Bio-Rad—no. 10042959) for viral DNA, and MRFAP1 was used as a reference gene (Fw 5′-GAACCGGAGCAAGCTGT-3′, Rv 5′-GGTTCTGGAGGTGGTTGAG-3′, probe 5′-/5HEX/-CACCTGCGTTTTGATCTGGATGAGC/3IABkFQ/-3′; from Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc.). The MRFAP1 gene was selected as the reference for normalization because of its stable genomic copy number across a wide range of tissues, including brain and peripheral organs.34 Droplets were generated with the QX200 Droplet Generator following the manufacturer’s instructions (Bio-Rad, USA). Droplets were read with the QX200 Droplet Reader, and results were analyzed with QuantaSoft Analysis Pro Software (v.1.0.596, Bio-Rad). ddPCR values were averaged across experiments and technical replicates (three independent PCR runs and varying DNA input concentrations) to obtain a single value per DNA sample.

Microscopy, image processing, and cell counting

Fluorescent images were taken with either an Axio Observer 7 epifluorescent microscope controlled by ZEN 2.3 (blue edition, Zeiss, Jena, Germany) or Nikon Ti-Eclipse 2 epifluorescent microscope and further processed with ImageJ. Standard image alterations, including pseudo-coloring, contrast/brightness adjustments, were applied equally across the entire image.

For quantitative analysis, five cortical areas were selected: ventromedial prefrontal (vmPFC), primary motor (M1), somatosensory (S1/S2), parietal (parietal area PE caudal part and parietal area PEa [medial intraparietal area]), and visual (V1/V2) cortices. For each region investigated, at least three different sections were imaged, and at least three different ROIs were selected and quantified per image (for statistical comparison, multiple ROIs from the same section were averaged resulting in one value per slice). The ROIs represented areas of both high and low transduction, and the standard ROI was a full cortical column 1–1.5 mm wide and 2–3 mm deep (average of 4,046 NeuN cells per ROI).

Images were processed with ImageJ following a similar pipeline as proposed by Cross et al.35 All sections and NeuN and FLAG channels were handled separately to accommodate batch-to-batch variation in signal intensity. First, the background was removed until only the FLAG+ nuclei remained after conversion to grayscale and then binarization. Next, watershed tool was used to separate any cells whose boundaries met. Cell counts were then quantified using the Analyze Particles tool with settings size (mmˆ2) = 10–infinity (values were based on an average of cell size and assessed by “measure function” in ImageJ) and circularity = 0.40–1.00 for NeuN and 0.60–1.00 for FLAG, with overlayed masks to visually confirm successful counting. The neuronal transduction rate was then calculated as a fraction of FLAG+ cells to NeuN cell number.

Statistics

For statistical comparison, SigmaPlot (Grafiti LLC, Palo Alto, CA) was used. Results are expressed as a mean ± standard deviation or median with 50% inter-percentile range (25% and 75% values reported). To compare two groups, t test was used (normality test: Shapiro-Wilk; Mann-Whitney rank-sum test if data were not normally distributed). One-way ANOVA was used to compare multiple groups (Kruskal-Wallis one-way ANOVA on ranks) followed by all pairwise multiple comparison procedures (Dunn’s method). Differences were considered significant if p < 0.05.
