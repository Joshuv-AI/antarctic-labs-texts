Entropy from scattering in weakly interacting systems
Duncan MacIntyre and Gordon W. Semenoff Department of Physics and Astronomy, University of British Columbia, 6224 Agricultural Road, Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada V6T 1Z1

Abstract
Perturbation theory is used to investigate the evolution of the von Neumann entropy of a subsystem of a bipartite quantum system in the course of a gedanken scattering experiment. We find surprisingly simple criteria for the initial state and the scattering matrix that guarantee that the subsystem entropy increases. The class of states that meet these criteria are more correlated than simple product states of the subsystems. They form a subclass of the set of all separable states, and they can therefore be assembled by classical processes alone.



I
Introduction

Amongst the “area laws” of fundamental physics, a rather interesting one has emerged in the context of scattering theory. The question which is asked concerns the quantum entanglement between subsets of the particle degrees of freedom that is produced by interactions in the course of a scattering event. It considers a bipartite quantum system that is composed of two subsystems, A and B, and with the incoming particles in an unentangled pure quantum state. The interaction of the particles is assumed to be weak, so that perturbation theory is valid. Then the change in von Neumann entropy of one of the subsystems per unit time and per unit of incoming beam flux is proportional to the total cross-section,
\sigma
, which is measured in units of area
seki1
;
Grignani:2016igg
;
Fan:2017mth
;
Boyanovsky:2018fxl
;
Peschanski:2019yah
;
Low:2024mrk
;
Low:2024hvn
.
We write

\displaystyle\frac{\delta S^{A}}{{\rm(time)(beam\penalty\ flux)}}\penalty\ =\penalty\ \left[\lambda^{2}\ln\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right]\sigma

(1)

The parameter
\lambda
is the coupling constant which is assumed to be of sufficiently small magnitude that perturbation theory is valid. The appearance of its logarithm in the above expression is interesting. It is due to the singular nature of von Neumann entropy in this limit.

In equation (
1
), the entropy of the A subsystem is increased by the scattering process since the cross section is necessarily positive. This is not surprising. Because the incoming unentangled pure state has zero entanglement entropy and because entropy is non-negative, the entropy can only increase.

In this paper we will study situations where the incoming state is more general than an unentangled pure state. Generally, it is specified by an incoming density matrix
\hat{\rho}^{\rm in}
. We will find criteria for this incoming state and for the scattering matrix so that the entropy of the A subsystem is guaranteed to be increasing. In studying this issue, we will derive a refinement of the formula (
1
) for more general incoming states. We will also consider some examples which demonstrate that, when our positivity criteria are not met, that subsystem entropy can either increase or decrease, depending on the fine details of the initial state. Although we will not explore the subject here, our results should be pertinent to the discussions of a “second law of entanglement”
T
.

A recent interesting work
Cheung:2023hkq
studied the case of an incoming state which is still an unentangled product state of the subsystems but where each subsystem can be in a mixed state, so that the incoming state is a direct product of density matrices of the subsystems,
\hat{\rho}=\hat{\rho}^{A}\otimes\hat{\rho}^{B}
. They asked which initial states of this kind must always have non-decreasing

-Tsallis entropy (for
n\geq 2
) of
subsystem A, no matter what the interaction as long as time evolution is unitary, and no matter what the initial state
\hat{\rho}^{B}
of subsystem B.
They found a surprisingly simple characterization of such states:
the
n
-Tsallis entropy of subsystem
A
is always non-decreasing at the second order in perturbation theory if
\hat{\rho}^{A}
is proportional to a projection operator, that is, if
\hat{\rho}^{A}
has finitely many non-zero eigenvalues and all of the non-zero eigenvalues are equal.

In the following we will ask a similar question for the von Neumann entropy. We will assume a more general initial state which is not necessarily a product of the states of the two subsystems. We will retain the assumption of unitarity, in that the
\hat{T}
matrix satisfies the optical theorem and, as in reference
Cheung:2023hkq

\hat{T}
matrix will be our Maxwell’s demon and our goal is to understand how to organize the initial states of the system in such a way that we are guaranteed to thwart the demon’s efforts to decrease the entropy of subsystem A.

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle Tr}\left[\hat{\rho}^{A{\rm in}}\right]=\sum_{m}\rho^{A{\rm in}}_{m}=1,\penalty\ \rho^{A{\rm in}}_{m}\geq 0.

has a nonempty kernel, that is,

\exists\ket{m}\penalty\ {\rm such\penalty\ that\penalty\ }\hat{\rho}^{A{\rm in}}\ket{m}=0;

For any eigenvector

\displaystyle\biggl[{\ket{m}}\bra{m}\otimes{\mathbb{1}}^{B},\;\hat{\rho}^{\rm in}\biggr]=0;\hskip 30.00005pt\text{and}

Density matrices with the property (

Separable states are mixed states of a bipartite quantum system which are distinguished by the fact that

They are defined as any states whose density matrices are convex combinations of product states

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{\rm separable}=\sum_{i}k_{i}\hat{\rho}_{i}^{A}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{i}^{B}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ k_{i}>0,\penalty\ \sum_{i}k_{i}=1,

The projection operator states of reference

II
Perturbative scattering

To facilitate scattering, an incoming quantum state, which we will describe by the density matrix
\hat{\rho}^{\rm in}
, is prepared at an early initial time. It could, for example, contain a number of free relativistic particles whose initial state is characterized by
\hat{\rho}^{\rm in}

\hat{\rho}^{\rm out}

\hat{S}

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{\rm out}=\hat{S}\hat{\rho}^{\rm in}\hat{S}^{\dagger}.

(6)

We will assume that the

\hat{S}\hat{S}^{\dagger}=\mathbb{1}

\displaystyle\hat{S}=\mathbb{1}+i\hat{T}

(7)

The unitarity of
\hat{S}

\displaystyle i\hat{T}-i\hat{T}^{\dagger}=-\hat{T}\hat{T}^{\dagger}

(8)

We assume that the

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{k}\hat{T}^{(k)},

\displaystyle\lambda\to 0.

\displaystyle i\lambda\left[\hat{T}^{(1)},\hat{\rho}^{\rm in}\right]+i\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\left[\hat{T}^{(2)}+\hat{T}^{(2)^{\dagger}},\hat{\rho}^{\rm in}\right]

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ -\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\left[\hat{T}^{(1)},\left[\hat{T}^{(1)},\hat{\rho}^{\rm in}\right]\right]+\mathcal{O}{\left(\lambda^{3}\right)}.

\hat{T}^{(2)}-\hat{T}^{(2)\dagger}=i\hat{T}^{(1)}\hat{T}^{(1)}

The initial and final density matrices must be non-negative and

III
The leading order
\sim\lambda

Let us first examine the contribution of the leading, linear term in
\lambda
in equation (

\lambda\to 0

It will be useful to separate the states

IV
Second order
\sim\lambda^{2}\ln(1/\lambda^2)

We shall now assume that the initial state obeys (
3
) and is of the form (
4
) so that the leading contribution to the shift in eigenvalues of the reduced density matrix is obtained by second order perturbation theory.

If the initial reduced density matrix
\hat{\rho}^{A{\rm in}}

has a nonempty kernel, the zero eigenvalues contribute a non-analytic term in
\lambda
that will dominate
in the limit
\lambda\to 0
. We have

\displaystyle\delta S=\left[\lambda^{2}\ln\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right]

(20)

\displaystyle\times\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\notin\kappa\\
m^{\prime}\in\kappa\\
\tilde{m},\tilde{m}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\biggl(\rho^{{\rm in}}_{m,\tilde{m}}\left|{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\\
\tilde{m}\tilde{m}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\right|^{2}-\frac{\rho^{{\rm in}}_{m,\tilde{m}^{\prime}}\rho^{{\rm in}}_{m,\tilde{m}}}{\sum_{\tilde{m}^{\prime\prime}}\rho^{{\rm in}}_{m,\tilde{m}^{\prime\prime}}}{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\\
\tilde{m}\tilde{m}\end{subarray}}{T}^{(1)*}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\\
\tilde{m}^{\prime}\tilde{m}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\biggr)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}{\left(\lambda^{2}\right)}

where

{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\\
\tilde{m}\tilde{m}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}=\bra{m,\tilde m}\hat{T}^{(1)}\ket{m',\tilde m'}.

For pure initial states, the non-analyticity in
\lambda
of the leading contribution to the von Neumann entropy was noted in some of the early literature on momentum space entanglement in quantum field theory
vanR
although the rest of the above formula is quite different from theirs. The logarithm of the coupling also appears in the computations of entanglement due to scattering of incoming pure states
seki1
;
Grignani:2016igg
. Here, (
20
) is a generalization of that result to include all incoming states which obey (
3
) and where
\hat{\rho}^{A{\rm in}}
has a nontrivial kernel.

The contribution to the change of the A subsystem entropy in equation (
20
) is proportional to a sum over the perturbations of the zero eigenvalues of the reduced density matrix, the leading order of which are now the second order contributions that are displayed there. Being of second order, their signs can no longer be flipped by changing the sign of
\lambda
or
\hat{T}^{(1)}
. Moreover, just as we argued for the first order in perturbation theory, since they are eigenvalues of a density matrix, each individual eigenvalue as well as the sum over the eigenvalues must be non-negative.
Indeed, we can explicitly confirm that the sum over the shifts of zero eigenvalues is non-negative by rewriting the summation in equation (
20

\displaystyle\delta S=\,

\displaystyle\left[\lambda^{2}\ln\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right]

(21)

\displaystyle\times\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\notin\kappa\\
m^{\prime}\in\kappa\\
\tilde{m},\tilde{m}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\rho^{{\rm in}}_{m,\tilde{m}}\biggl|{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\\
\tilde{m}\tilde{m}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}-\delta_{\tilde{m}\tilde{m}^{\prime}}\sum_{\tilde{n}}\frac{\rho^{{\rm in}}_{m,\tilde{n}}}{\rho^{A{\rm in}}_{m}}{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\\

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}{\left(\lambda^{2}\right)}

If the right-hand-side of (

What is more, the conditions for all of the terms in the summand on the right-hand-side of (

\hat{T}

If it were not so, that is, if any of those

\displaystyle{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\\
\tilde{m}\tilde{m}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\tilde{m}\tilde{m}^{\prime}}{T}^{A(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\end{subarray}},

\displaystyle\text{for all }m\notin\kappa,\;m^{\prime}\in\kappa

(22)

whence the elements of the
\hat{T}
matrix that mix the kernel and non-kernel states act trivially, as the unit operator on the B subsystem.

Thus, we can conclude that, if
the criterion of equation (

and the reduced density matrix
\hat{\rho}^{A{\rm in}}
has a non-empty kernel,

If, in addition,
the
\hat{T}
matrix allows transitions between non-kernel and kernel states
and, if in doing so, the
\hat{T}
matrix acts nontrivially
on the B subsystem, the change in the entropy of subsystem A is guaranteed to be positive, of order
\lambda^{2}\ln\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}
in the weak coupling limit, and given by equation (
21
).

V
A simple example

The first term on the right-hand-side of equation (
21
) has a simple interpretation. It contains the transition probability,
\left|{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\\
\tilde{m}\tilde{m}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\right|^{2}

initial non-zero probability states
\left|m,\tilde{m}\right>
to initial zero probability states,
\left|m^{\prime},\tilde{m}^{\prime}\right>

\rho^{\rm in}_{m,\tilde{m}}
that
\left|m,\tilde{m}\right>
occurs in the initial state.

The second term on the right-hand-side of equation (

\hat{T}

A

\left|{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\\

Then the A subsystem is isolated and its time evolution is unitary. In that case its entropy cannot change.
We have already seen that
\delta S

in this case, the role of the second term in (

As an example, consider

and
\ket{\tilde 1}
could be wavepacket states of two distinct particles, for example. In this case, equation (
21
) reduces to

\displaystyle\delta S=\left[\lambda^{2}\ln\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\right]\sum_{m\neq 1,\tilde{m}\neq 1}\left|{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}1m\\
\tilde{1}\tilde{m}\end{subarray}}\right|^{2}+\mathcal{O}{\left(\lambda^{2}\right)}.

(23)

The result is the total transition probability to all states with the condition that the states of both the A and B subsystems must change. If we apply this formula to the scattering of two distinct particles, and change the normalization to entropy production rate per unit of beam flux, we find the area law of equation (
1
).

VI
When
\hat{\rho}^{A{\rm in}}
has full rank

Finally, we might ask the question about the case where the initial state has the property (
3
) but the reduced initial density matrix
\hat{\rho}^{A{\rm in}}
does not have a kernel, that is,
0<\rho^{A{\rm in}}_{m}\leq 1

\forall m
. Then, the order
\lambda^{2}\ln\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}
term is absent from the perturbative expansion of the entropy. The dominant term is of order at least
\lambda^{2}
. We have

\delta S=-\lambda^{2}\sum_{m}\delta\rho^{A(2)}_{m}[\ln\rho^{A{\rm in}}_{m}+1]+\mathcal{O}{\left(\lambda^{3}\right)}

(24)

as
\lambda\to 0
.

Since the trace of a density matrix such as
\hat{\rho}^{A{\rm in}}
must be unity, we must have
\sum_{m}\delta\rho^{A(2)}_{m}=0
. We can easily confirm this by examining the trace of the order
\lambda^{2}
terms in the matrix
\mathcal{M}_{mm^{\prime}}
in equation (
14
). We see that

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\delta\rho^{A(2)}_{m}=\sum_{m}\mathcal{M}_{mm}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,m^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\Biggl\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{m},\tilde{m}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}[\rho^{\rm in}_{m,\tilde{m}}-\rho^{\rm in}_{m^{\prime},\tilde{m}^{\prime}}]\left|{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\\
\tilde{m}\tilde{m}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\right|^{2}

(25)

\displaystyle\hskip 30.00005pt-\frac{1}{\rho^{A{\rm in}}_{m}-\rho^{A{\rm in}}_{m^{\prime}}}\left|\sum_{\tilde{m}}[\rho^{\rm in}_{m,\tilde{m}}-\rho^{\rm in}_{m^{\prime},\tilde{m}}]{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\\
\tilde{m}\tilde{m}\end{subarray}}\right|^{2}\Biggr\}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\mathcal{O}{\left(\lambda^{3}\right)}.

Here we used
\hat{T}^{(1)}=\hat{T}^{(1)\dagger}
. Now,

\left|{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\\
\tilde{m}\tilde{m}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\right|^{2}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ {\rm and}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \left|\sum_{\tilde{m}}[\rho^{\rm in}_{m,\tilde{m}}-\rho^{\rm in}_{m^{\prime},\tilde{m}}]{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\\
\tilde{m}\tilde{m}\end{subarray}}\right|^{2}

are symmetric under
m,\tilde{m}\leftrightarrow m^{\prime},\tilde{m}^{\prime}
and
m\leftrightarrow m^{\prime}
, respectively.
The summand in (
25
) is therefore odd under this interchange and the sum must vanish;

\sum_{m}\delta\rho^{A(2)}_{m}=0.

Then equation (
24
) becomes

\displaystyle\delta S=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,m^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\ln[\rho^{A{\rm in}}_{m}/\rho^{A{\rm in}}_{m^{\prime}}]\biggl\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{m},\tilde{m}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}[\rho^{\rm in}_{m,\tilde{m}}-\rho^{\rm in}_{m^{\prime},\tilde{m}^{\prime}}]\left|{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\\
\tilde{m}\tilde{m}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\rho^{A{\rm in}}_{m}-\rho^{A{\rm in}}_{m^{\prime}}}\left|\sum_{\tilde{m}}[\rho^{\rm in}_{m,\tilde{m}}-\rho^{\rm in}_{m^{\prime},\tilde{m}}]{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\\
\tilde{m}\tilde{m}\end{subarray}}\right|^{2}\biggr\}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}{\left(\lambda^{3}\right)}

(26)

where we have used the antisymmetry of the summand to anti-symmetrize the logarithmic factor under
m\leftrightarrow m^{\prime}
. Rearranging (
26
),

\displaystyle\delta S=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,m^{\prime}\\
\tilde{m}\tilde{m}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\ln\frac{\rho^{A{\rm in}}_{m}}{\rho^{A{\rm in}}_{m^{\prime}}}[\rho^{\rm in}_{m,\tilde{m}}-\rho^{\rm in}_{m^{\prime},\tilde{m}^{\prime}}]

\displaystyle\;\;\times\biggl|{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\\
\tilde{m}\tilde{m}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}-\delta_{\tilde{m}\tilde{m}^{\prime}}\sum_{\tilde{n}}\frac{\rho^{\rm in}_{m,\tilde{n}}-\rho^{\rm in}_{m^{\prime},\tilde{n}}}{\rho^{A{\rm in}}_{m}-\rho^{A{\rm in}}_{m^{\prime}}}{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\\
\tilde{n}\tilde{n}\end{subarray}}\biggr|^{2}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}{\left(\lambda^{3}\right)}.

(27)

To see the indefinite sign of the change in entropy, consider a simple example of two two-level systems that is initially in the product state

\hat{\rho}^{\rm in}=\biggl[x\ket{1}\bra{1}+(1-x)\ket{2}\bra{2}\biggr]\otimes\biggl[y\ket{\tilde 1}\bra{\tilde 1}+(1-y)\ket{\tilde 2}\bra{\tilde 2}\biggr]

where
x,y\in[0,1]
. Here,

\hat{\rho}^{A{\rm in}}=\biggl[x\ket{1}\bra{1}+(1-x)\ket{2}\bra{2}\biggr]

has full rank when
0<x<1
. Let us
assume that the only nonzero components of the
\hat{T}
matrix are

{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}12\\
\tilde{1}\tilde{2}\end{subarray}}={T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}12\\
\tilde{2}\tilde{1}\end{subarray}}={T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}21\\
\tilde{1}\tilde{2}\end{subarray}}={T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}21\\
\tilde{2}\tilde{1}\end{subarray}}=t.

The result for the change in the entropy of the A subsystem is then

\displaystyle\delta S=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}|t|^{2}\ln\frac{x}{1-x}[x(1-y)-(1-x)y]+\mathcal{O}{\left(\lambda^{3}\right)}.

This result can be either positive or negative. It changes sign as
y
varies from zero to one, irrespective of the value of
x
, as long as
x\neq 0,\frac{1}{2},1
.

Another example is the situation where subsystem A is in a thermal state and subsystem B is in a pure state,

\rho^{\rm in}_{m\tilde{m}}=\frac{e^{-\beta E_{m}}}{\sum\limits_{E}e^{-\beta E}}\delta_{\tilde{m},\tilde{1}}

The thermodynamic entropy of the A system is equal to its von Neumann entropy.

Using equation (
26
) to compute the change of entropy due to scattering yields

\displaystyle\delta S=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,m^{\prime}\\
\tilde{m}\tilde{m}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\beta(E_{m^{\prime}}-E_{m})\frac{e^{-\beta E_{m}}}{\sum\limits_{E}e^{-\beta E}}\biggl|{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\\
\tilde{1}\tilde{m}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\biggr|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{3})

Now we can see that the overall sign of the entropy shift will depend on the relative weights of
E_{m^{\prime}}
and
E_{m}
in the summation and the states of
the B system can be set so that it is either positive or negative.

For example, we could assume that the
\hat{T}
matrix conserves energy for the process
m+\tilde{1}\to m^{\prime}+\tilde{m}^{\prime}
, that is, that

E_{m}+E_{\tilde{1}}=E_{m^{\prime}}+E_{\tilde{m}^{\prime}}
. In that case, we
can replace the above with

\displaystyle\delta S=

\displaystyle-\lambda^{2}\sum_{m}\frac{e^{-\beta E_{m}}}{\sum\limits_{E}e^{-\beta E}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m^{\prime}\\
\tilde{m}\tilde{m}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\beta(E_{\tilde{m}^{\prime}}-E_{\tilde{1}})\biggl|{T}^{(1)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}mm^{\prime}\\
\tilde{1}\tilde{m}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\biggr|^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{3})

(28)

The sign of the right-hand-side now depends on the relative magnitudes of the term with
E_{\tilde{m}^{\prime}}
and the term with
E_{\tilde{1}}
.

If, for example,
\tilde{1}
is the lowest energy state of the B subsystem, so that
E_{\tilde{1}}
is the lowest possible energy of the B subsystem, the right-hand-side of (
28
) is negative and entropy of the A system decreases. This is the onset of thermalization, where a thermal state of the A subsystem at temperature
1/k_{B}\beta
is in weak contact with a zero temperature state of the B subsystem.
The thermal state begins to cool, decreasing its thermal entropy.

In order for the entropy of the A subsystem to increase,
E_{\tilde{1}}
in (
28
) would have to be a high energy state so that typical terms which contribute to the sum have
(E_{\tilde{m}^{\prime}}-E_{\tilde{1}})<0
. Then the weak contact of the two subsystems would be the onset of thermalization where the temperature and therefore the entropy of the A subsystem would begin to increase.

We see that, when the incoming density matrix of the A subsystem has full rank, even simple product incoming states of the composite system can lead to A subsystem entropy increasing or decreasing, depending on the states of the B subsystem. We suspect, but we have not proven that this is always the case and that, when the incoming A subsystem density matrix has full rank, the
\hat{T}
matrix and B subsystem states can always be organized so that the A subsystem entropy increases or decreases. An exception may be the state of maximum entropy where
\rho^{A{\rm in}}
is proportional to the unit matrix. In the infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces that are typical of scattering theory such a state would have to be defined by a subtle limiting process.

VII
Conclusion

In conclusion, we restate our central result, that in perturbation theory, the entropy of a subsystem of a bipartite quantum system will be non-decreasing at order
\lambda^{2}\ln\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}
if the initial reduced density matrix of that subsystem has a nonempty kernel and if the initial density matrix of the composite system commutes with the projectors onto the eigenspaces of the
reduced density matrix of the A system, that is, if equation (
3
) or equivalently (
4
) is satisfied.

In addition, the entropy will be strictly increasing if the above conditions hold and if,
\hat{T}^{(1)}
allows for transitions in and out of the kernel of
\hat{\rho}^{A{\rm in}}
and if
\hat{T}^{(1)}
acts nontrivially on the B subsystem.
