α-KG conformationally stabilizes nuclear GLUD1-SNAIL complex to fuel trans-endothelial migration in metastasis
Authors: Jiawen Cui, Nuo Xu, Rongyu Cao, Ruqi Zhang, Ying Yu, Luyao Ao, Feiyi Wang, Qixing Gong, Le Zhen, Yantong Li, Qingqing Zhou, Yue Dang, Guangji Wang, Fang Zhou, Jiali Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Trans-endothelial migration (TEM) is a critical rate-limiting barrier in tumor metastasis, yet its mechanistic basis remains poorly defined. Here we show that α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) and its metabolic enzyme glutamate dehydrogenase 1 (GLUD1) constitute a pivotal regulatory axis governing this process. Mechanistically, GLUD1 translocates to the nucleus, where α-KG binds to its non-catalytic site (K147) to induce a conformational change that stabilizes the GLUD1-SNAIL interaction, promoting assembly of a nuclear ternary complex. Notably, this complex is preferentially enriched in tumors, and specific blockade of K147 disrupts this assembly and abrogates metastatic effects. Our findings reveal a non-canonical role for GLUD1 and α-KG in transcriptional regulation and propose a therapeutic paradigm that selectively interferes with metabolite-dependent complex formation rather than enzymatic activity, offering a precise strategy to target TEM-driven metastatic cells.

Introduction

Intravasation (cross the endothelial barrier from the primary tissue into the circulation) and extravasation (cross the endothelial barrier from circulation into distant tissues) of tumor cells represent the decisive physical barrier during hematogenous dissemination1–3. Although intravasation and extravasation may involve distinct regulatory mechanisms due to microenvironmental differences, it would have significant therapeutic implications to identify shared functional mechanisms that could serve as common intervention points. Tumor trans-endothelial migration (TEM) is a common feature of intravasation and extravasation, yet TEM remains one of the least mechanistically defined steps of metastasis4–7. While the importance of metabolic rewiring in metastatic competent tumor cells is well established8–10, the mechanisms by which this process influences the regulation of TEM are largely unknown.

Glutamate dehydrogenase 1 (GLUD1), a key enzyme that converts glutamate to α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) during glutamine catabolism11, is commonly upregulated in aggressive human cancers12. GLUD1’s metabolic functions, including generating α-KG to replenish tricarboxylic acid cycle13 and maintain redox homeostasis14, also generating ammonia to support biomass synthesis15, have been well documented in tumor growth. Recently, a compelling concept is emerging wherein tumor cells co-opt metabolic enzymes and metabolites for non-canonical, or “moonlighting” functions. Glycolytic enzymes have been shown to translocate into the nucleus and regulate gene expression through kinase activity, transcription factor interactions, or modulation of transcriptional co-regulators16. Recently, GLUD1 was reported to promote hepatitis B virus transcription with KDM4A through modulating histone methylation in the nucleus17; however, the underlying molecular mechanisms of nuclear translocation of GLUD1 and whether it fuels metastatically competent tumors beyond its established metabolic activities remain insufficiently understood.

Here, through multi-cohort clinical databases screening and phenotypic validation, we identified GLUD1 as a critical driver of tumor cell TEM. We further uncovered a previously unrecognized mechanism wherein GLUD1 translocates into the nucleus, where its metabolite α-KG functions as a conformational stabilizer, inducing changes in GLUD1 structure that enhance its interaction with the transcription factor SNAIL. This interaction promotes the assembly of a nuclear ternary complex, α-KG/GLUD1/SNAIL, which directly binds to the promoter of CDH5, the gene encoding VE-Cadherin, and activates its transcription in tumor cells. This transcriptional upregulation enhances tumor cell-endothelial cell interactions and ultimately promotes TEM during both intravasation and extravasation.

Results

GLUD1 was identified as a key mediator of TEM during both intravasation and extravasation

Recent studies have revealed a interesting phenomenon in which tumor cells enhance heterotypic interactions by acquiring features of other cell lineages, thereby promoting cancer progression. For instance, tumor cells can adopt neuronal features to strengthen interactions with central neurons18,19, or acquire endothelial-like properties to facilitate interactions with vascular endothelial cells20,21. Given that trans-endothelial migration (TEM) fundamentally depends on tumor-endothelial cell interactions, we hypothesized that tumor cells exhibiting vasculature-like features possess an enhanced TEM capacity. This phenomenon, in which tumor cells adopt an endothelial-like identity, is commonly referred to as vasculogenic mimicry (VM)22–26. Thus, we isolated high VM, highly metastatic tumor cell subpopulations and analyzed their differentially expressed proteins to identify key mediators of TEM (Fig. 1A). Integration of the MetMap database (Supplementary Fig. 1A) distinguished strongly metastatic (lung cancer, melanoma) from weakly metastatic tumors (thyroid cancer, prostate cancer). VM markers were enriched in aggressive tumors in TCGA public database, supporting the positive correlation of vasculature-like signatures with metastasis26,27 (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Variance analysis revealed 3,743 differentially expressed genes between highly and weakly metastatic groups, designated Cohort 1 (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, additional single-cell RNA-seq data from lung cancer and melanoma, stratifies tumor cells into high-VM and low-VM subgroups based on VM programmers SERPINE2/SLPI expression26. Comparative analysis yielded two additional gene sets (Cohorts 2 and 3). Intersection of all three cohorts identified 102 genes (Fig. 1B). Among these 102 genes, only 10 genes exhibited concurrent significant negative correlations with both overall survival (OS) and failure-free survival (FFS). GLUD1 emerged as the lead candidate, notably the only endogenous metabolic enzyme identified among the hits (Fig. 1C). Clinical validation in 19 lung cancer specimens (cohort 4) confirmed that GLUD1 expression strongly correlated with VM intensity (Fig. 1D, E). Consistent with clinical observations, lung cancer cell lines with higher GLUD1 expression exhibited enhanced in vitro VM phenotype, as assessed by the tube formation assay, a widely used functional in vitro surrogate of VM capacity evaluation (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Although the role of VM in tumor-endothelial heterotypic interactions has been proposed28, and we identified GLUD1 from highly metastatic, VM-positive tumor subpopulations, whether GLUD1 mediates TEM remains unclear. Therefore, we established both in vitro and in vivo experimental systems to address this question. TEM consists of three dynamic phases: adhesion, barrier disruption, and transmigration. We first assessed GLUD1 function using a three-phase co-culture model that recapitulates these steps (Fig. 1F). A549/DTX cells (high-GLUD1/high-VM) outperformed parental A549 cells (low-GLUD1/low-VM) in all phases (Supplementary Fig. 1F–K). This GLUD1-dependent regulation was confirmed through GLUD1 knockdown in A549/DTX cells, which dramatically impaired all three TEM phases (Fig. 1G–L and Supplementary Fig. 1L–Q, showing results from two distinct siRNA sequences). The essential role of GLUD1 in mediating heterotypic interactions was further validated in PC9/OR cells, another established high-GLUD1/high-VM model system (Supplementary Fig. 1R–W). Further, to determine whether GLUD1-mediated TEM contributes to both intravasation and extravasation, we developed a reliable 3D vascular system that reconstructs key structural and barrier features of native vasculature, including physiological tube-like architecture and tight endothelial barriers (Supplementary Fig. 2A–C). This system exhibited a permeability coefficient (Papp) of 10−7 cm/s comparable to that of real blood vessels (Supplementary Fig. 2D, E). Using this model, we simulated both intravasation and extravasation by seeding tumor cells either within the extracellular matrix (for intravasation) and directly into the vascular lumen (for extravasation) (Fig. 1M). Notably, GLUD1 knockdown attenuated both intravasation and extravasation (Fig. 1N).

To more rigorously validate the role of GLUD1-mediated TEM in both intravasation and extravasation, we utilized two distinct in vivo metastasis models that rely on intravasation or extravasation. In the zebrafish yolk sac injection model, tumor cells form a confined aggregate within the yolk sac that serves as a surrogate primary tumor, from which they intravasate into the bloodstream29–32. In the tail vein injection model, tumor cells are administered directly into the circulation and subsequently extravasate to form metastatic lesions in the absence of primary tumor33–35 (Fig. 1O, P). Pan-cell line analysis demonstrated that high-GLUD1/high-VM cells exhibited greater metastatic dissemination in intravasation based zebrafish models (Supplementary Fig. 2F-2G) and formed larger and more metastatic lesions in extravasation based murine models (Supplementary Fig. 2H–L). Notably, these lesions displayed elevated PAS-positive/CD31-negative (PAS⁺/CD31⁻) staining, the histopathological gold standard for identifying endothelium-independent vasculogenic mimicry in vivo. (Supplementary Fig. 2M, N). Conversely, overexpression of GLUD1 in low-GLUD1/low-VM cell lines significantly enhanced metastatic burden in both intravasation based zebrafish models (Fig. 1Q) and extravasation based murine models (Fig. 1R, S and Supplementary Fig. 2O, P), accompanied by upregulated PAS+/CD31− staining (Supplementary Fig. 2Q). Furthermore, given that implanting cancer cells into the intervitelline space represents a more accurate approach that permits both invasion and intravasation36,37, we performed additional experiments using this model. The results showed that GLUD1 similarly regulated tumor cell dissemination in the intervitelline space injection model, consistent with the findings from the yolk sac injection approach (Supplementary Fig. 2R).

Collectively, GLUD1 was selectively identified from high-VM and highly metastatic tumor subpopulations and functionally validated as a key mediator of TEM, influencing both tumor cell intravasation and extravasation.

GLUD1 promotes TEM by regulating VE-Cadherin

VE-Cadherin, EPHA2 and FAK have been implicated as key mediators of VM and invasive behavior in aggressive tumors. Aberrant VE-Cadherin expression promotes tumor cell plasticity and its interaction with vascular endothelial cells. EPHA2 and its phosphorylation-dependent activation cooperate with VE-Cadherin to activate downstream signaling pathways that facilitate motility and matrix remodeling while FAK and its phosphorylation-dependent activation contribute to cytoskeletal dynamics and invasive phenotypes associated with VM21,38. Given that GLUD1 mediates TEM, we first asked whether it acts through these known regulators. VE-Cadherin, a key mediator of tumor-endothelial heterotypic interactions whose knockdown significantly impaired all phases of TEM (Supplementary Fig. 3B–G), was consistently associated with GLUD1 expression in isolated mouse metastatic lesions (Supplementary Fig. 3H, I). Furthermore, A549/DTX and PC9/OR cells derived from their parental counterparts exhibit elevated GLUD1 expression. Notably, high GLUD1 expression in these cells correlated with increased VE-Cadherin protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 3J) and membrane-localized levels (Supplementary Fig. 3K, L). Knockdown of GLUD1 in A549/DTX and PC9/OR cells markedly reduced VE-Cadherin expression across these levels, whereas GLUD1 overexpression in A549 and PC9 cells significantly upregulated it (Fig. 2B–D and Supplementary Fig. 3M–O). These findings suggest that GLUD1 mediates the protein expression of VE-Cadherin. Crucially, CDH5 silencing largely abolished GLUD1-driven adhesion, barrier destruction, and transmigration in 2D co-culture systems (Fig. 2E–K), as well as intravasation and extravasation in 3D organoid models (Fig. 2L–O). Conversely, CDH5 overexpression rescued the impaired TEM phenotypes caused by GLUD1 silencing (Supplementary Fig. 3P–U), confirming that VE-Cadherin is essential for GLUD1-mediated TEM.

GLUD1 activates VE-Cadherin transcription by forming a nucleus complex with SNAIL

Although several TCA cycle enzymes exhibit nuclear localization and exert non-canonical regulatory functions39,40, GLUD1 has long been regarded as a strictly cytoplasmic and mitochondrial enzyme primarily involved in basal metabolic processes15,41. Through nuclear fractionation assays and DAPI co-localization microscopy, we verified that GLUD1 can localize to the nucleus (Fig. 3B), with its nuclear abundance dynamically regulated by genetic knockdown and overexpression (Supplementary Fig. 4B–D). Subcellular fractionation analysis further revealed that GLUD1 was predominantly localized in mitochondria (~60%), while approximately 23% and 17% were distributed in the nucleus and cytoplasm, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4E, F). To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying GLUD1 nuclear translocation, immunoprecipitation (IP)-Mass Spectrometry (MS) screening coupled by co-IP validation was adopted and revealed Importin-8 (encoding by IPO8) as the primary nuclear transporter for GLUD1 (Supplementary Fig. 4G and Fig. 3C), confirming by the reduction of nuclear GLUD1 accumulation upon IPO8 knockdown; specifically, a 54% reduction in Importin-8 expression led to an approximately 32% decrease in the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio (Fig. 3D, E and Supplementary Fig. 4H, I, showing results from two distinct siRNA sequences). Moreover, deletion of the GLUD1 nuclear localization signal (NLS) impaired its nuclear import, resulting in pronounced reduction of VE-Cadherin (Fig. 3F).

Having established GLUD1’s nuclear localization mechanism, we next investigated how nuclear GLUD1 exerts its transcriptional regulatory function, specifically whether nuclear GLUD1 directly binds the CDH5 promoter as an autonomous transcriptional regulator or requires cooperation with other transcription factors42. To address this, we designed and performed a DNA pulldown-coupled MS assay (Fig. 3G). A synthesized biotinylated CDH5 promoter fragment was incubated with A549/DTX cell lysates prepared under either stringent conditions that disrupt protein interactions or native conditions that preserve them. As shown in Fig. 3H, GLUD1 only associates with the biotinylated CDH5 promoter under mild lysis conditions rather than under strong denaturing conditions, demonstrating its indirect DNA binding through protein interactions.

We next analyzed proteins enriched under both lysis conditions to identify those involved in GLUD1’s DNA binding. Among them, SNAIL, a core metastasis-related transcription factor encoding by SNAI1, emerged as the exclusive transcriptional regulator in our screening. The SNAIL binding motif within the CDH5 promoter was predicted using JASPAR, and specific binding sites were validated by chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays and luciferase reporter assays using a promoter variant with mutated binding sites (Fig. 3I, J and Supplementary Fig. 5A–C). In addition, JASPAR also predicted binding motifs for other relative transcription factors in the CDH5 promoter (Supplementary Fig. 5D). However, these were ruled out due to either minimal enrichment after GLUD1 pulldown (e.g., SLUG/ZEB1 and SOX2 in Supplementary Fig. 5E) or unchanged binding site occupancy upon GLUD1 knockdown (e.g., TWIST in Supplementary Fig. 5F). Moreover, no detectable interaction was observed between GLUD1 and SLUG, ZEB1, SOX2, or TWIST (Supplementary Fig. 5G). Sequential ChIP (reChIP) experiments definitively confirmed GLUD1 and SNAIL co-occupy the same CDH5 promoter region (Fig. 3K). Robust nuclear co-localization of GLUD1 and SNAIL in A549/DTX cells was confirmed by Immunofluorescence analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5H). Further Co-IP assay validated that there is an interaction between GLUD1 and SNAIL proteins, and this interaction occurs predominantly in the nucleus which can be modulated by GLUD1 expression levels (Fig. 3L, M and Supplementary Fig. 5I). Critically, SNAI1 knockdown reversed the GLUD1 overexpression-induced enrichment of CDH5 promoter sequences, whereas GLUD1 overexpression failed to rescue the reduced promoter binding and subsequent VE-Cadherin expression caused by SNAI1 depletion (Fig. 3N–P). Consistently, SNAI1 silencing abolished the enhancement of TEM induced by GLUD1 overexpression (Supplementary Fig. 5J–O), whereas SNAI1 overexpression failed to restore the impaired TEM phenotype resulting from GLUD1 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 5P–U). This unidirectional epistatic relationship also further validated that GLUD1 requires pre-existing SNAIL binding to associate with the CDH5 promoter as shown in Fig. 3Q. Furthermore, SNAI1 re-expression experiments following knockdown excluded potential off-target effects (Supplementary Fig. 5V, W), solidifying the mechanistic hierarchy in which GLUD1 depends on forming nucleus complex with SNAIL for promoter recruitment.

Meanwhile, comparative analysis showed that this interaction was markedly enriched in metastatic compared to contralateral normal lung tissues, where it was rarely detected (Fig. 3R, S). Notably, circulating tumor cells (CTCs) exhibited elevated nuclear GLUD1 and SNAIL levels together with increased VE-Cadherin expression, while leptomeningeal metastatic lesions derived from primary lung cancer displayed enhanced GLUD1-SNAIL interaction and VE-Cadherin expression compared to parental cells (Fig. 3T, U and Supplementary Fig. 5X, Y), further supporting the biological relevance of this complex in metastasis.

To test this hypothesis, we first examined the effect of α-KG on GLUD1-SNAIL interaction in purified protein system (Supplementary Fig. 6N). Increasing concentrations of α-KG progressively enhanced complex formation (Fig. 4N, O), with 100 μM identified as the threshold concentration for promoting complex assembly. Notably, this concentration is higher than the estimated local physiological concentrations of α-KG in the nucleus (6–50 μM)43. Using LC-TRAP/MS, we measured nuclear α-KG concentrations in the various cell lines used throughout this study. Notably, cell lines exhibiting stronger GLUD1-SNAIL interaction harbored higher nuclear α-KG levels, and these levels were above 100 μM in those lines (Fig. 4O).

To test whether this correlation reflects a causal role of GLUD1-generated α-KG, we generated cells expressing the catalytically dead mutant GLUD1 R496S44. In R496S-expressing cells, basal GLUD1 catalytic activity was already markedly impaired, and R162 failed to further reduce α-KG-dependent GLUD1-SNAIL complex formation (Fig. 4P and Supplementary Fig. 6O). We next supplemented α-KG in R162-treated A549/DTX cells. Here, we use methyl-α-KG due to its cell-permeable nature and ability to rapidly release α-KG upon intracellular hydrolysis45. α-KG restoration robustly increased nuclear GLUD1-SNAIL interaction (Fig. 4Q) and subsequent VE-Cadherin expression (Fig. 4R). Furthermore, the following GST pulldown and recombinant protein Co-IP assays in cell-free systems confirmed that α-KG is an enhancer in the assembly process of GLUD1-SNAIL complex (Fig. 4S, T).

To determine whether α-KG modulates the binding of the protein complex to the promoter, we synthesized recombinant promoter DNA sequences and incubated them with different combinations of α-KG, recombinant GLUD1, and recombinant SNAIL. After pull-down assays, we quantified the binding affinity of GLUD1/SNAIL for promoter DNA via qPCR (Fig. 4U). As shown in Fig. 4V, neither GLUD1 nor SNAIL alone recruited the CDH5 promoter, whereas α-KG significantly enhanced binding of the GLUD1-SNAIL complex to this promoter. Meanwhile, using the inhibitor of another α-KG upstream cytoplasmic enzyme IDH1, also significantly impaired the nuclear α-KG abundance and complex level in nucleus (Supplementary Fig. 6P, Q), emphasizing the necessity of α-KG for complex assembly. Notably, other endogenous metabolites structurally similar to α-KG, including Glutamate, Succinate, Fumarate, Malate and D/L-2-HG, failed to significantly promote either complex formation or its binding to the promoter, underscoring the specificity of α-KG (Supplementary Fig. 6R–U).

Next, a key question arose: does α-KG influence complex formation by binding to the GLUD1-SNAIL complex? Microscale thermophoresis (MST) demonstrated that α-KG preferentially binds to GLUD1 rather than SNAIL (Fig. 4W). This finding was further supported by cellular thermal shift assays (CETSA, Fig. 4X; Supplementary Fig. 6V) and drug affinity responsive target stability assays (DARTS, Supplementary Fig. 6W). Together, these results suggest that α-KG likely binds to GLUD1, forming an α-KG/GLUD1/SNAIL ternary complex.

α-KG acts as a conformational stabilizer within the α-KG/GLUD1/SNAIL ternary complex

Using Autodock predictions (Fig. 5A) followed by MST validation with point mutant recombinant proteins (Fig. 5B), we identified K147 (Lys), a highly conserved residue across species (Supplementary Fig. 7A), as the primary α-KG binding site in GLUD1. Enzymatic characterization of the K147 mutant confirmed this to be a non-catalytic binding site (Fig. 5C). As predicted by molecular dynamics simulations, α-KG binding induces conformational changes in GLUD1 (Fig. 5D). Circular Dichroism (CD) spectroscopy directly demonstrated this structural effect, with a distinct spectral shift confirming α-KG-driven alterations in secondary structure (Fig. 5E). This structural change induced by α-KG binding enhances the interaction between GLUD1 and SNAIL (Fig. 5F). Through fragment deletion analysis of GLUD1 and SNAIL, residues 1–158 of GLUD1 and 1–100 of SNAIL were identified as protein-protein binding domains (Fig. 5G, H). Mutating these residues in either GLUD1 or SNAIL significantly impaired their protein-protein interaction, in agreement with the molecular dynamics predictions. (Fig. 5J). Competitive inhibition of α-KG engagement blocks ternary complex formation and suppresses TEM-driven metastasis

Glutarate structurally resembles α-KG and has been proposed as a competitive inhibitor of α-KG-dependent metabolic enzymes46. MST assays confirmed that glutarate directly binds to GLUD1, and mutation of K147 abolished this interaction, indicating that glutarate, like α-KG, interacts with the K147 residue of GLUD1 (Fig. 6A). However, unlike α-KG, glutarate did not alter the secondary structure of GLUD1 (Supplementary Figs. 8A vs. 5E), suggesting that it acts as a competitive inhibitor that blocks α-KG engagement and consequently prevents ternary complex formation. For further validation, we synthesized biotinylated α-KG and incubated it with GST-tagged GLUD1, followed by streptavidin bead pull-down. As shown in Fig. 6B, glutarate significantly reduced the interaction between GLUD1 and α-KG. Consistent with this finding, both in vitro recombinant protein system and cellular system demonstrated that glutarate effectively suppresses α-KG-induced formation of the GLUD1-SNAIL complex (Supplementary Fig. 8B, C). For cellular experiments, diethyl glutarate (DEG) was used due to its cell-permeable nature and ability to rapidly release glutarate upon intracellular hydrolysis. Further in vitro DNA promoter-contained pull-down assays revealed that glutarate competitively inhibits transcriptional complex binding at the CDH5 promoter (Fig. 6C, D), concomitant with reduced VE-Cadherin induction (Supplementary Fig. 8D). Finally, we validated the functional consequences of glutarate treatment and found it impaired TEM ability, affecting both the intravasation and extravasation processes. In addition to the step-specific models described above, we also employed an in-situ lung tumor metastatic model to validate our intervention strategy. This model is commonly used to assess the complete metastatic cascade, as it combines both intravasation and extravasation within a single system. As shown in Fig. 6L–O, DEG administration reduced brain metastatic burden (Fig. 6L) and decreased the proportion of mice developing metastasis-associated hindlimb paralysis (0/10 versus 7/10; two-sided Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.0031; absolute risk reduction = 0.70, 95% CI, 0.29–0.89; Fig. 6M). DEG treatment also prolonged survival (Fig. 6N) and attenuated body weight loss (Fig. 6O).

Additionally, we further explored the clinical relevance of the GLUD1/VE-Cadherin axis across multiple cancer types using TCGA datasets. Notably, GLUD1 expression positively correlated with CDH5 gene levels in high-VM breast cancer and melanoma cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 8E, F). Moreover, CDH5 expression was significantly elevated in advanced-stage tumors compared with early-stage tumors, supporting a potential role for this pathway in other malignancies, as we showed in NSCLC models (Supplementary Fig. 8G, H).

Discussion

TEM is a dynamic and essential step in the metastatic cascade, enabling tumor cells to intravasate and extravasate the vasculature and thereby drive tumor dissemination. Yet whether metabolic enzymes, long regarded solely as catalysts of cellular metabolism, can directly moonlight as regulators of the transcriptional programs governing this process remains largely unexplored. Our findings establish α-KG as a conformational stabilizer that enables the formation of a nuclear α-KG/GLUD1/SNAIL complex potentiating the ability of tumors to intravasate and extravasate through vasculature. Furthermore, through detailed mechanistic dissection of the assembly mode of this ternary complex, we uncover previously unreported moonlighting functions of both α-KG and GLUD1 (Fig.7).Fig. 7Schematic model of α-KG-dependent GLUD1-SNAIL complex formation during trans-endothelial migration and metastasis.In the cytoplasm, GLUD1 performs its canonical metabolic function in glutamate catabolism by generating α-KG. α-KG exhibits pleiotropic functions in tumor progression that extend beyond core metabolism. As an essential cofactor for dioxygenases, α-KG regulates epigenetics and epitranscriptomics47,48 and it can also acts as a signaling molecule by directly binding and modulating protein activity49,50. Although previous study has detected GLUD1 and α-KG in the nucleus17,43, where it modulates histone methylation via α-KG-dependent dioxygenases to influence epigenetic remodeling51, the nuclear entry of GLUD1 remains mechanistically unexplained. Our study delineates a coherent mechanistic framework that GLUD1 can translocate into the nucleus via Importin-8 and directly interacts with SNAIL, with α-KG functioning as an essential conformational stabilizer. Structural and mutagenesis analyses revealed that α-KG docks at the hydrophilic K147 site of GLUD1, a non-enzymatic binding pocket that influences GLUD1 conformation, enabling its enhanced interaction with SNAIL. This interaction forms a binding domain comprising Y2, Y4, R13, I132 and V156 in GLUD1, along with P2, F5, and R8 in SNAIL. The resulting α-KG/GLUD1/SNAIL complex binds to the CDH5 promoter, transcriptionally upregulating its expression, thereby promoting tumor cell TEM and metastasis. In addition to SNAIL52, another transcription factor TWIST1 also has been reported to directly bind with the CDH5 promoter and upregulate tumor cells’ transactivation and VM ability in hepatocellular carcinoma53 while our experiments conclusively demonstrated the absence of GLUD1-TWIST1 interaction. Notably, VM has been widely used as a morphological feature associated with aggressive tumor behavior. We therefore employed it as a phenotypic screening marker to identify subpopulations with enhanced TEM capacity, which subsequently led us to identify GLUD1 as a key regulator of this process. It should be emphasized that the GLUD1-VM correlation is associative, and we do not claim a mechanistic connection between VM structures and metastasis. Rather, our study focuses on the role of GLUD1 in regulating TEM-related tumor cell behavior, whereas the mechanistic basis of VM formation and its causal contribution to metastasis remain beyond the scope of the current work.

Compared to normal cells, tumor cells co-opt metabolic enzymes not only to fulfil cellular metabolic demands but also to regulate diverse non-metabolic processes54,55. These non-canonical roles of endogenous metabolic enzymes have garnered increasing attention due to their pivotal regulatory functions in tumorigenesis16, particularly glucose-metabolizing enzymes such as HK56,57, PFK58, PGK159, PKM260,61 and PCK162, which act as protein kinases with phosphorylating a broad spectrum of substrates to drive tumor progression. Beyond modulating post-translational modifications, glucose metabolic enzymes further diversify their functions via protein-protein interactions, altering the activity of binding partners in a catalysis-independent manner63–65. While glutamine catabolism also plays a crucial role in tumorigenesis and progression12, evidence for moonlighting functions of glutamine-related metabolic enzymes remains limited. In the cytosol, phosphorylated GLUD1 (Ser384) interacts with RelA and IKKβ to form an oncogenic complex66. Our current work reveals analogous functionality in the nucleus. GLUD1 engages in protein-protein interactions with SNAIL, while its metabolic product α-KG similarly regulates complex assembly. These findings significantly expand the known scope of GLUD1’s moonlighting functions and suggest that similar to glucose-metabolizing enzymes, glutamine-related metabolic enzymes may possess diverse regulatory capabilities warranting broader investigation. Although the cell lines and animal models used in this study are primarily based on lung cancer models and leptomeningeal metastasis of lung cancer, the initial screening based on clinical samples in Fig. 1 incorporated multiple tumor types. In addition, our TCGA-based analyses suggest that the GLUD1/VE-cadherin axis may have broader relevance beyond NSCLC, although the strength of this association varied across cancer types. The variable correlation strengths between GLUD1 expression and VE-Cadherin across lung cancer, breast cancer, and melanoma may be partly attributable to the nature of bulk TCGA transcriptomic data, which may be influenced by differences in tumor purity, vascular density, stromal composition, endothelial cell abundance, and immune infiltration across cancer types. In addition, cancer type-specific molecular backgrounds, metabolic dependencies and baseline propensities may further contribute to the variable correlation patterns observed among different malignancies. Therefore, the applicability and clinical value of the α-KG/GLUD1/SNAIL axis in other cancer types require further systematic investigation, including assessment of complex abundance across a broader spectrum of malignancies and evaluation of strategies targeting α-KG/GLUD1-mediated TEM and metastasis.

Many disease-associated metabolism enzymes have been targeted through the development of small-molecule compounds for therapeutic intervention, particularly in cancer67. However, conventional approaches to intervention in the canonical catalytic activities of these enzymes unavoidably disrupt systematic metabolism, which is essential for maintaining the homeostasis of normal cells, such as immune cells, and organs, such as the brain. This disruption often leads to severe side effects. The discovery of the moonlighting functions of metabolic enzymes and specific mechanism, underscores alternative approaches to mitigating these non-canonical functions for treating cancer. In this study, we reveal that GLUD1 regulates TEM through a dual function that integrates its canonical metabolic activity with moonlighting functions through both protein-protein interactions and metabolic regulation, the latter mediated by α-KG-dependent modulation of complex assembly. By competitively inhibiting the conformational binding of α-KG with GLUD1, we were able to block the assembly of the ternary complex and suppress the TEM process. α-KG has attracted considerable attention for its well-established roles in anti-aging and maintenance of cellular stemness and it has gained popularity as a daily anti-aging supplement68–73. However, for cancer patients, α-KG supplementation may not yield positive outcomes. Although α-KG has traditionally been considered tumor-suppressive47,48, emerging evidence reveals a contrasting oncogenic role: α-KG accumulation can promote immune evasion50 and confer resistance to disulfidptosis74. Similarly, our study identifies α-KG as a factor supporting tumor metastasis. Unlike previous reports describing α-KG as a cofactor for dioxygenases that modulate epigenetics to promote tumorigenesis, we reveal that α-KG acts as a conformational stabilizer facilitating transcriptional complex assembly, a previously unrecognized mechanism. The addition of α-KG alters the secondary structure while Glutarate act as non-functional ligand. Therefore, Glutarate administration exerts competitive inhibition of this interaction effectively reduces TEM in tumor cells. However, given α-KG’s pivotal physiological functions, systemic inhibition of α-KG using non-targeted metabolic analogs inevitably induces toxic side effects. Meanwhile, glutarate is an endogenous metabolite whose physiological ratio with crotonyl-CoA is tightly regulated. Excessive glutarate accumulation exhibits cytotoxicity, as evidenced in glutaric acidemia type I patients harboring GCDH loss-of-function mutations, who develop severe neurodevelopmental impairments46,75,76. Although glutarate demonstrates anti-metastatic properties, supratherapeutic accumulation due to impaired clearance mechanisms triggers metabolic dysregulation, including feeding abnormalities in murine models77. While we have characterized the complex using AlphaFold predicted protein model and MD simulations, these findings underscore the necessity of developing novel compounds, contingent upon precise structural characterization of the α-KG/GLUD1/SNAIL ternary complex to overcome the inherent uncertainties, for instance, using interface-based peptidomimetics78.

In summary, our study identifies the conformational regulatory role of α-KG and the assembly of the nuclear α-KG/GLUD1/SNAIL complex as a key mechanism mediating the often-overlooked process of TEM during tumor dissemination. Notably, preferential assembly of this complex in tumor tissues provides a mechanistic basis for selective therapeutic intervention. Targeting the non-canonical α-KG-GLUD1 interface, rather than enzymatic activity, offers a strategy to disrupt metastatic progression while potentially sparing normal metabolic functions. Our findings provide a potential therapeutic innovation, underscoring the need for deeper structural insight and structure-guided therapeutic designs into this α-KG-driven nuclear complex. However, further studies, including detailed structural characterization and in vivo evaluation of safety and pharmacological properties, will be required to assess the feasibility of therapeutic development based on this mechanism.

Reagents and tools

All reagents and resources used in this study, including antibodies, chemicals, recombinant proteins, critical commercial assays and software, are listed in Supplementary Data 1.

Cell culture

All tumor cell lines were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Human lung cancer cells (PC9, H460, A549, PC9/OR, H1975, A549/DTX) were maintained in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. Primary Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cell (HUVEC) was purchased from Shanghai Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd and cultured in endothelial cell culture medium (ZQ-1304, Shanghai Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd).

Plasmid transfections, viral infections and RNA interference

For transient plasmid transfection, parental cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 5 × 105 cells per well and transfected with 1 μg plasmid using lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000015) in serum-free RPMI 1640. After 6 h incubation, the medium was replaced with complete RPMI 1640 containing 10% FBS. Transfected cells were harvested 2-4 days post-transfection for subsequent assays. GLUD1 overexpression vectors (wild-type, GFP-tagged, and K147A mutant), FLAG-tagged GLUD1 deletion mutants (wild-type, Δ1-158, Δ159-258, Δ259-358, Δ359-458, Δ459-558), FLAG-tagged SNAI1 deletion mutants (Δ1-100, Δ101-200, Δ201-264), FLAG-tagged GLUD1 point mutants (Y2A/Y4A/R13A/I132A/V156A), FLAG-tagged SNAI1 point mutants (P2A, F5A, R8A) and the CDH5 promoter-driven dual-luciferase reporter system (wild-type and mutant) was constructed as described. Plasmids pCDNA3.1 containing FLAG-tagged constructs were obtained from Genomeditech (Shanghai, China), while pEGFP-GLUD1 variants and luciferase reporters were purchased from Jinbeijin Biotechnology (Nanjing, China).

For stable lentiviral transduction, lentiviral vectors (pLV-CMV-MCS-GFP-luciferase-puro and its GLUD1 variants) were obtained from Jinbeijin Biotechnology. Cells were infected at multiplicities of infection of 50 (A549/DTX, H1975) or 20 (PC9, A549) in serum-free medium, followed by selection with 4 μg/ml puromycin.

The sequences of all relevant constructs are provided in Supplementary Data 2.

Animal treatment

Zebrafish (Danio rerio) were supplied by Hunter Biotech. For the zebrafish xenograft model, tumor cells (1 × 106) were washed twice with PBS, stained with 1 μg CellTrackerTM Cm-DiI (Thermofisher, USA) in 1 mL PBS at 37 °C for 5 min, then centrifuged (1000 × g, 3 min) and harvested. After three PBS washes, cells were injected into the yolk sac or intervitelline space of 48 hpf zebrafish larvae using a capillary needle. At 24 hpi, zebrafish with successful engraftment were selected under a stereomicroscope and maintained at 34 °C. Metastasis was assessed at 96 hpi by fluorescence imaging (stereomicroscope) and quantified using FIJI software (ImageJ). For the zebrafish xenograft experiments, tumor cells were injected into 48 hpf zebrafish larvae, and metastasis was assessed at 96 hpi. During this experimental window, biological sex cannot be reliably determined; therefore, sex was not considered as a biological variable in these experiments.

Female BALB/c nude mice (3–4 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. and housed in individually ventilated cages in the specific pathogen-free facility of the Laboratory Animal Center, China Pharmaceutical University. Mice were maintained at 22 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 10% relative humidity under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. They were provided with an irradiated maintenance diet (XT101WC-009; Jiangsu Synergy Pharmaceutical Bioengineering Co., Ltd., China) and water ad libitum. Cages contained irradiated corncob bedding (8 mesh; catalogue no. 1060012; Jiangsu Synergy Pharmaceutical Bioengineering Co., Ltd., China).

For mouse metastasis model in Figs. 1 and 5, Luciferase-expressing tumor cells (1 × 10⁶ in 200 μL PBS) were intravenously injected into 3–4 week old female BALB/c nude mice. Metastasis was monitored by bioluminescence imaging (IVIS Spectrum, Revvity) at days 2, 7, 14, and 28 post-injection following luciferin administration (150 mg/kg). Lungs were harvested for either fixation (4% PFA) or Western blot analysis. H&E and PAS + /CD31− staining assays were performed by Servicebio (China), with whole-slide scanning (NanoZoomer S210, Hamamatsu) and quantification (NDP.view2).

For drug-treated mouse metastasis model in Fig. 6H–K, A549/DTX cells expressing luciferase with either wild-type or K147A GLUD1 (1 × 10⁶) were injected as above. Treatment groups received DEG (10 mg/kg, i.p.) every other day. Imaging and analysis followed the same protocol.

For mouse metastasis model in Fig. 6L–O, A549/DTX cells expressing GFP-luciferase (5 × 10⁶) were orthotopically injected into mouse lungs. Mice were treated with Saline/DEG (10 mg/kg, i.p., every 3 days) and hindlimb paralysis was recorded as a metastasis-associated neurological outcome.

For the zebrafish xenograft experiments, the endpoint of 96 hpi was prospectively defined to allow assessment of metastatic dissemination within the established experimental window. For all tail-vein metastasis models, including those shown in Figs. 1, 5 and 6H–K, the endpoint of 5 weeks after tumour-cell injection was prospectively defined based on our previous experience with these models and the expected time required for the development of detectable metastatic lesions. For the orthotopic lung tumour model shown in Fig. 6L–O, the experiment was designed to evaluate survival. Animals were followed for survival, and the experiment was terminated on day 26, when cumulative mortality in the saline-treated group reached 90%. All surviving animals were euthanized at this time, and tissues were collected for subsequent analyses. Because the tail-vein and orthotopic lung tumour models involved internally located tumours, caliper-based measurements were not applicable. Tumour burden was monitored by bioluminescence imaging and clinical assessment. No animal exceeded the maximum tumour burden permitted under the approved animal protocol before the respective experimental endpoints.

Tube formation assays

Pre-chill a 96-well plate on ice and coat each well with 50 μL of Matrigel (Corning, #354230). Polymerize the Matrigel by incubating the plate at 37 °C for 2 h. Harvest and count tumor cells from experimental groups, then seed 2 × 10⁴ cells per well in 200 μL of RPMI 1640 supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) onto the Matrigel-coated wells. Incubate the plate at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 4–6 h. Tube formation images were taken using an Agilent BioTek Lionheart™ automated microscope. Quantify tube formation using Fiji (ImageJ) with the Angiogenesis Analyzer2 plugin to automatically assess endothelial network parameters (e.g., tube length, branching points).

Western blot

After 30 min lysis on ice, samples were centrifuged (14,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), and supernatants were collected for BCA protein quantification (Bio-Rad). Lysates were mixed with 4× Laemmli buffer and denatured.

For surface protein preparation, cell lysate was performed with Surface and Cytoplasmic Protein Reagent Kit (P0033, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). In brief, cells were washed with PBS and resuspended in membrane extraction reagent A containing 100 μM PMSF. After 15 min incubation on ice, samples were centrifuged (700 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). The supernatant was further centrifuged (14,000 × g, 30 min, 4 °C) to obtain membrane pellets (solubilized in reagent B). Then the surface protein preparation was mixed with 4× Laemmli buffer and denatured.

For nuclear extraction, cell lysate was performed with Nuclear Protein and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (P0027, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). In brief, cells were resuspended in cytoplasmic extraction reagent A (with 100 μM PMSF), incubated on ice for 15 min, then treated with reagent B for 1 min on ice before centrifugation (14,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). Nuclear pellets were extracted by vortexing in nuclear extraction reagent (30 min, with intermittent mixing), followed by centrifugation to collect nuclear proteins (14,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). Then the nuclear protein preparation was mixed with 4× Laemmli buffer and denatured.

For recombinant protein interactions, tagged proteins (GST/FLAG/GFP/Biotin, 5 μg) were incubated (2 h, RT) before IP with respective antibodies: anti-FLAG (#ab205606, Abcam), anti-GFP (#PA1-980A, Thermo Fisher), or streptavidin/GST magnetic beads (#P2151/P2138, Beyotime). Input (20 μL) and IP samples were processed as above.

Chromatin-immunoprecipitation (Ch-IP) assay

Chromatin immunoprecipitation was performed using the Pierce™ Magnetic ChIP Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #26157) with the following modifications:

For cellular chromatin, tumor cells (1 × 10⁷) were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde (10 min, RT) and quenched with 0.125 M glycine. Chromatin was extracted by nuclear separation and sheared by MNase/sonication to 200–500 bp fragments and immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C with rotation using different antibodies and species-matched IgG controls.

For recombinant chromatin, annealed DNA sequences were incubated with recombinant proteins (2 h, 37 °C) before antibody addition and overnight IP at 4 °C.

Protein A/G magnetic beads were added (4 h, 4 °C), followed by sequential washes and elution. For sequential ChIP (reChIP), eluates were subjected to secondary IP. Crosslinks were reversed (65 °C, 1.5 h with Proteinase K) and DNA purified using kit columns.

Immunoprecipitation coupled with proteomics analysis

Nuclear protein extracts from tumor cells were prepared using a nuclear and cytoplasmic protein extraction kit (Beyotime #P0027). Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay and adjusted to 5 μg/μL in PBS containing 100 μM PMSF. Nuclear lysates were incubated with 5 μg anti-GLUD1 antibody or an equivalent amount of control IgG overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with magnetic beads for 4 h. The beads were washed three times with PBS, and bound proteins were eluted in loading buffer at 95 °C for 5 min. Samples were separated on 10% Omni-PAGE Hepes Plus gels, and the excised gel regions were subjected to bottom-up proteomic analysis by APT Biotechnology. Three independent immunoprecipitation experiments were pooled to generate one LC-MS/MS sample per condition.

Conditional DNA pull-down coupled with proteomics analysis

The target promoter sequence (1000 bp upstream of exon) was retrieved from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and synthesized with 5’ biotin modification (FITGENE, China). Tumor cells were lysed under two conditions: (1) SDS lysis buffer with sonication (strong condition) or (2) IP lysis buffer (mild condition). After centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, the clarified lysates were incubated with the biotinylated DNA probe overnight at 4 °C. DNA-protein complexes were captured using streptavidin magnetic beads for 30 min at room temperature. Bound proteins were eluted in 4× Laemmli buffer at 95 °C for 7 min, separated on 10% Omni-PAGE Hepes Plus gels and subjected to bottom-up proteomic analysis by APT Biotechnology. Three independent DNA pull-down experiments were pooled to generate one LC-MS/MS sample per condition.

LC-MS/MS acquisition and database searching

Excised gel samples were reduced and alkylated and digested with sequencing-grade trypsin at an enzyme-to-protein ratio of 1:50 (w/w) for 20 h at 37 °C. Peptides were desalted, lyophilized and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid. Nano-LC-MS/MS analysis was performed using an Easy-nLC 1000 system coupled to a Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in positive-ion mode. Mobile phase A consisted of water containing 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B consisted of 84% acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid. The LC system was equilibrated with 95% mobile phase A. Peptides were loaded onto an Acclaim PepMap 100 trap column (100 μm × 2 cm, nanoViper C18; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and separated on an EASY analytical column (25 cm × 75 μm inner diameter, 1.9 μm particle size; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a flow rate of 300 nL min−1. The 60-min gradient of mobile phase B was as follows: 5–8% from 0 to 3 min, 8–26% from 3 to 43 min, 26–40% from 43 to 48 min, 40–90% from 48 to 50 min, followed by maintenance at 90% from 50 to 60 min.

Full MS scans were acquired over an m/z range of 300–1800 at a resolution of 70,000 at m/z 200, with an automatic gain control target of 1 × 10⁶ and a maximum injection time of 50 ms. The 20 most intense precursor ions from each full scan were selected for higher-energy collisional dissociation using a normalized collision energy of 27. MS/MS scans were acquired at a resolution of 17,500 at m/z 200 with an isolation window of 2 m/z. Dynamic exclusion was set to 30 s, and the underfill ratio was 0.1%. Raw files were processed using MaxQuant version 1.6.14 and searched against the UniProt Homo sapiens protein sequence database downloaded on 8 January 2024. Trypsin specificity was applied, allowing up to two missed cleavages. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was specified as a fixed modification, and oxidation of methionine was specified as a variable modification. The precursor-ion and fragment-ion mass tolerances were 20 ppm and 0.1 Da, respectively. The false-discovery rate was controlled at 1% at both the peptide and protein levels, and intensity-based absolute quantification was enabled. The minimum peptide length was seven amino acids. No additional minimum unique-peptide filter was applied after MaxQuant processing, and all protein groups passing the specified false-discovery-rate threshold were retained.

Microscale thermophoresis (MST) binding assay

Binding affinities were determined using Monolith NT.115 (NanoTemper Technologies). GFP-tagged recombinant proteins were mixed with 16 serially diluted concentrations (typically spanning 1 nM to 1 mM) of α-Ketoglutarate (α-ketoglutarate, #K105570, Aladdin, China) and Glutarate (#D154294, Aladdin, China)

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

We performed thermal stability profiling using an optimized CETSA protocol. Tumor cells (1 × 10⁸) were harvested in PBS containing 100 μM PMSF and protease inhibitors, then aliquoted into eight PCR tubes for temperature gradient treatment (37 °C, 38.5 °C, 41.3 °C, 45.7 °C, 51 °C, 55.1 °C, 58.1 °C, 60 °C; 3 min each) using a thermal cycler. Following 3 min equilibration at room temperature, samples underwent three freeze-thaw cycles (alternating liquid nitrogen and 25 °C water bath) before centrifugation (20,000 × g, 20 min, 4 °C). Soluble fractions (90 μL supernatant) were collected for western blotting.

Drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assay

The DARTS assay was performed to assess protein-ligand interactions through protease sensitivity profiling. Recombinant GLUD1 (50 ng) was incubated with 5 mM α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) in 20 mM HEPES buffer (pH 7.4) containing 1 mM DTT for 15 min at room temperature. Proteolytic digestion was then carried out using subtilisin (Sigma-Aldrich, #P4860) at final concentrations of ~0.4, 0.2 and 0.1 μg/mL for 30 min at 25 °C. Reactions were terminated by adding 4× Laemmli buffer and heating at 95 °C for 5 min. Finally, separation was performed by Western Blot.

Targeted LC-MS/MS determination of nuclear α-ketoglutarate

Nuclear α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) was quantified using a targeted LC-MS/MS method. A total of three independent biological samples was analyzed per group. Cells were collected in ice-cold 0.25 M sucrose buffer (containing 10 mM Tris-HCl and 3 mM MgCl2, pH 7.4) and homogenized using a pre-cooled homogenizer. The homogenate was filtered and layered onto a 0.34 M sucrose cushion, followed by centrifugation at 700 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to pellet the nuclei. The nuclear pellet was washed twice with 0.25 M sucrose buffer at 1000 × g for 10 min to minimize cytosolic contamination. To ensure metabolic quenching and prevent α-KG leakage, the final nuclear pellet was immediately extracted with 500 μL of pre-chilled 80% (v/v) methanol at −80 °C for 2 hours. After centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 15 min, the supernatant was collected and vacuum-dried for subsequent LC-TRAP-MS/MS analysis. Chromatographic separation was performed on a Shimadzu LC-20 HPLC system (Kyoto, Japan) coupled to a QTRAP 5500 mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA).

Chromatographic separation was performed using a liquid chromatography system equipped with an XSelect HSS T3 column (150 × 4.6 mm, 3.5 μm; Waters, Milford, MA, USA) at 40 °C on the Shimadzu HPLC. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (5 mM ammonium acetate, pH = 9, and 5% acetonitrile) and solvent B (acetonitrile) with the following gradient:0 min, 5% B; 5 min, 5% B; 5.5 min, 75% B; 7.5 min, 75% B; 8 min, 5% B with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min.

α-KG was detected in negative electrospray ionization mode using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). The ESI source conditions were set as follows: Gas1, 33 psi; Gas2, 33 psi; Curtain Gas, 25 psi; Ion spray voltage, −4500 V; Turbo spray temperature, 550 °C. The MRM transition for α-KG was m/z 145.1 → 101.0, with declustering potential (DP) = −20 V and collision energy (CE) = −12 V. 2-Ketoglutaric acid-13C5 was used as an isotopically labeled internal standard and The MRM transition for Ketoglutaric acid-13C5 was m/z 151.06 → 105.0, with declustering potential (DP) = −50 V and collision energy (CE) = −12 V. Data acquisition and analysis were both performed using Analyst v1.5.1 software. Quantification was by comparison with an external standard curve generated using authentic α-KG standards. Further nuclear α-KG concentration was calculated based on the measured α-KG amount normalized to the estimated nuclear fraction volume (1 pL per cell).

MD simulation

Protein structures were predicted using AlphaFold (GLUD1: P00367; SNAIL:O95863). The α-KG structure was retrieved from PubChem (CID:51). Molecular docking was performed using Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) software, generating 30 ligand-protein conformations ranked by the MOE scoring algorithm. The top-scoring conformation was selected for molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. All simulations were conducted using Gromacs 2022 with the following parameters: Force fields, Amber99sb-ildn (protein) and GAFF (ligand); Solvation, SPCE water model in a 10 × 10 × 10 nm³ box (minimum 1.2 nm protein-to-edge distance); Electrostatics, Particle-Mesh Ewald (PME) method with 1.0 nm cutoff; Energy minimization: 50,000 steps using steepest descent algorithm; System equilibration: NVT and NPT ensembles (300 K, 1 bar) using V-rescale and Berendsen coupling methods; Production run: 100 ns MD simulation at 300 K and 1 bar with 10 Å non-bonded interaction cutoff. Trajectory analysis was performed using Gromacs-built-in tools, with visualizations created in PyMOL (v2.5.0).

Public bioinformatics dataset analysis

Publicly available datasets were obtained from the following sources: TCGA PanCancer Atlas (lung adenocarcinoma, melanoma, thymoma, and prostate cancer; https://xenabrowser.net), GEO (NSCLC single-cell RNA-seq: GSE131907; melanoma single-cell RNA-seq: GSE215121; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), Kaplan-Meier Plotter (overall/progression-free survival analysis; https://kmplot.com), and MetMap (metastasis quantification; https://depmap.org/metmap/).

None of the databases applied sex-based screening. Both female and male patients are included. In the TCGA dataset, the lung adenocarcinoma cohort comprised 357 females and 301 males; the melanoma cohort comprised 180 females and 296 males; the thyroid carcinoma cohort comprised 423 females and 157 males; and the prostate cancer cohort (which inherently includes only male patients) comprised 572 males; and the breast cancer cohort included 1241 females and 13 males (which mainly occurs in females). In the GEO single-cell datasets, the lung adenocarcinoma cohort included 17 females and 27 males; the melanoma cohort included 3 females and 5 males. The in-house cohort related to Fig. 1D, E included 11 females and 8 males.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 and R v4.3.2. Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise indicated. Exact sample sizes and the definition of n are provided in the corresponding figure legends. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. Comparisons between two groups were performed using two-sided unpaired Student’s t-tests. Comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way or two-way ANOVA. For single-factor multigroup comparisons, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s or Dunnett’s post hoc testing was applied. For experiments involving two independent variables, two-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s multiple-comparisons test was used. Categorical outcomes, including the incidence of metastasis-associated hindlimb paralysis, were compared using a two-sided Fisher’s exact test. Correlations were evaluated using two-sided Pearson’s correlation. Survival analyses were performed using the two-sided log-rank test. Details of the statistical methods for each figure are included in the respective figure legends, with significance set at P < 0.05. All experiments corresponding to the representative micrographs shown in the figures were independently repeated more than three times.
