Caesalpinia sappan is a potent source of multi-functional bioactive compounds
Authors: Zhishu Li, Qiongqiong Yang, Peiwen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional Chinese medicinal (TCM) plants have become an important source for screening natural bioactive compounds. This study aimed to compare the phenolic profiles, and antioxidant, antiproliferative, and antibacterial activities of sixteen TCM extracts, to evaluate their effects on intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), and to characterize the major bioactive constituents in the most promising plants. Wide ranges of variation were found in methanolic plant extracts for total phenolics (31.3–1247 mg gallic acid equivalent (GAE)/100 g dry extract (DE)), total flavonoids (1.12–346 mg catechin equivalent (CE)/100 g DE), antioxidant activities, antiproliferative effects against four cancer cell lines with IC50 values of 3.74 to more than 200 µg DE/mL, and antibacterial activities against five bacteria with MIC values of 0.313-5.00 mg DE/mL. Caesalpinia sappan L. had the highest total phenolics, flavonoids, antioxidant, antiproliferative and antibacterial activities. It exerted an antiproliferative effect by reducing the intracellular ROS generation. Thirty-two compounds, including two organic acids, two phenolic acids, two chromones, eight flavonoids, four lactones, and 14 other phenolic compounds were tentatively identified from the heartwood of C. sappan. This study’s results indicated that C. sappan’s heartwood is a potential natural source of excellent antibacterial and anticancer compounds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-30175-2.Keywords: Heartwood, Caesalpinia sappan, Phytochemicals, Biological activity, ROSSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Cancer, Chemical biology, Drug discovery, Plant sciences

Introduction

Malignant tumors and microbial contaminations are two major global health challenges. As the environment and our lifestyles change, more and more people are facing risks from these health issues. Conventional anticancer approaches are surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy. However, chemotherapy and radiotherapy can damage normal tissues while killing cancer cells, seriously affecting the quality of life of patients. Antimicrobial agents are usually used to combat the microorganisms, but antibiotic resistance is a serious problem. Therefore, researchers are trying to find drugs with low side effects and good anticancer/antibacterial effects.

Many researchers are engaged in the extraction and separation of bioactive compounds from natural products to find effective anticancer/antimicrobial drugs1,2. Traditional Chinese medicinal (TCM) plants have become an important source for screening these drugs due to their long history of successful clinical use. In recent decades, researchers have isolated and identified some antitumor and antibacterial active ingredients from TCM plants. Among them, paclitaxel, hydroxycamptothecin, vinblastine, resveratrol, and curcumin have been widely used in clinical treatments3,4. These compounds exert anticancer and antimicrobial effects through different mechanisms, reflecting the diversity of natural products, and they can also serve as ideal structural references for synthetic new drugs. Therefore, the discovery of bioactive compounds from TCM plants has important scientific significance and broad market prospects.

Phenolic compounds, such as flavonoids, phenolic acids, coumarins, and tannins, are considered to be the major contributors to the biological activity of TCM plants5. The phenolic compounds in these TCM plants have attracted much attention due to their therapeutic properties such as anticancer6, antimicrobial7, antiviral8, and anti-inflammatory activities9. The types, amounts, and properties of phenolic compounds in these plants vary considerably10. However, these plants have not been fully exploited to unravel their cancer prevention properties and therapeutic potential to inhibit microbial growth.

As part of a research program launched in our laboratory to discover antibacterial and anticancer compounds from TCM plants, the top sixteen TCM plants with the best biological activities were carefully selected based on our preliminary experiments with 82 TCM plants. The selected TCM plants are listed in Table 1. We systematically evaluated the antioxidant, anticancer, and antibacterial activities of these extracts, and screened for potential candidate plants for further chemical characterization. The results of this study will help us understand the bioactivities of these medicinal plants and support their biological applications.

Methods

Chemicals and reagents

Gallic acid and catechin were purchased from Chengdu Derick Biotechnology Ltd. (Chengdu, Sichuan, China). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Folin-Ciocalteu reagent was purchased from Macklin Biotechnology Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Mueller Hinton (MH) broth was purchased from Oxford (Basingstoke, England). Dulbecco’s minimal essential medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin, and streptomycin were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Other chemical reagents were of analytical grade.

Plant materials

Sixteen dried TCM plant samples were purchased from Beijing Tongrentang Pharmacy in Beijing, China, and their Family and Latin names are listed in Table 1. The dried plants were further air-dried in a ventilated oven at 40 °C for 24 h and were milled into powders with sizes less than 500 μm. The powders were stored in a dryer at room temperature (20–25 °C) for further use.

Preparation of methanolic extracts

Sample powders (2.0 g) were extracted with 75% (v/v) methanol (40 mL) in a shaking water bath (150 rpm, 25 °C, 24 h). Thereafter, the mixtures were centrifuged at room temperature (3000 × g, 15 min) and the supernatants were collected and concentrated in a rotatory vacuum evaporator at 40 °C. The concentrated mixtures were further freeze-dried for 48 h. The freeze-dried samples were stored at -20 °C until further use.

Determination of total phenolic content (TPC)

The Folin-Ciocalteu method was used to determine the TPC of TCM plant extracts according to Yang et al.11. Gallic acid was used as a standard and the results were expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalent per 100 gram dry extracts (mg GAE/100 g DE). A standard calibration curve (y = 0.0383x + 0.0917) with an excellent linearity (R² = 0.9993) was established over a concentration range of 1–10 µg/mL.

Determination of total flavonoid content (TFC)

The AlCl3-based colorimetric method was used to determine the TFC of TCM plant extracts according to Yang et al.11,12. Catechin was used as a standard and the results were expressed as milligrams of catechin equivalent per 100 gram dry extracts (mg CE/100 g DE). A standard calibration curve (y = 0.0746x + 0.0382) with an excellent linearity (R² = 0.9983) was established over a concentration range of 1–10 µg/mL.

Determination of antioxidant activities

The antioxidant activities were evaluated with ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay, 2,2-azino-bis-3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulphonic acid (ABTS) assay, and 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) assay according to previous studies11,13.

For FRAP assay, FRAP reagent was prepared by mixing TPTZ solution (10 mM), FeCl3 solution (20 mM), and sodium acetate buffer (300 mM) in a volume ratio of 1:1:10. Before the experiment, the FRAP reagent was kept at 37 °C. FRAP reagent (3 mL) was reacted with a properly diluted sample solution (100 µL) for 4 min, and then the absorbance of the mixture was measured at 593 nm. The results were expressed as mmol Fe2+/g DE.

For ABTS assay, ABTS working solution was prepared by mixing ABTS stock solution (7 mM) with K2S2O8 solution (2.45 mM) in a volume ratio of 1:1 and the mixture was kept in dark for 16 h. Afterward, the absorbance of the mixture at 734 nm was adjusted to 0.70 ± 0.05 with distilled water. Properly diluted sample solutions (100 µL) were reacted with ABTS working solution (3.9 mL) for 6 min and the absorbance at 734 nm was recorded. Trolox was used as a standard, and the results were expressed as mmol Trolox/g DE.

For DPPH assay, DPPH working solution was prepared by dissolving DPPH in 80% methanol and the absorbance of the DPPH solution was adjusted to 0.70 ± 0.05 at 515 nm. Properly diluted sample solutions (100 µL) were reacted with DPPH working solution (3.9 mL) for 2 h in dark at room temperature. The absorbance at 515 nm was recorded and the results were expressed as mmol Trolox/g DE.

Antiproliferative assay

The antiproliferative effects of TCM plant extracts were evaluated against two human breast cancer cell lines (MCF7 and MDA-MB-231) and two human colon cancer cell lines (HCT116 and HT29). All cell lines were gift from Dr. Yueliang Zhao (Shanghai Ocean University, Shanghai, China). All cell lines were maintained in DMEM complete medium at 37 °C in a cell incubator with a humid atmosphere and 5% CO2. Cell counting kit 8 (CCK8) assay was used to evaluate the antiproliferative activity. Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates (5000 cells/well) and incubated for 8 h in the cell incubator. The medium was replaced with new mediums containing different concentrations of TCM plant extracts and the cells were further cultured for 48 h. Thereafter, 10 µL of CCK8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 1 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a multifunction microplate reader SpectraMax® iD3 (Molecular Devices Crop., Sunnyvale, CA), and IC50 values were calculated.

Intracellular ROS level analysis

The intracellular ROS level was assessed using a DCFH-DA detection kit (Yeasen Biotech, Shanghai, China). Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates (5000 cells/well) and cultured in the cell incubator for 24 h. The medium was replaced with a new medium containing 100 µg DE/mL of TCM plant extracts and the cells were further cultured for 4 h. Afterwards, the medium was removed and replaced with DMEM medium containing 10 µM DCFH-DA and the cells were further incubated for 30 min. The cells were washed with PBS twice to remove the DCFH-DA that did not enter into cells, and then immediately analyzed at 480/525 nm using a multifunction microplate reader SpectraMax® iD3. The total fluorescence intensity in each well was recorded and the ROS level was calculated as a fold change compared to the untreated control.

Bacterial strains preparation

Staphylococcus aureus SJTUF 20758, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923, Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, Listeria monocytogenes ATCC 7644, and Salmonella enteritidis SJTUF 10978 were used for antibacterial studies. All the bacterial strains were cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth at 37 °C for 24 h with 160 rpm agitation.

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

The MIC and MBC of the extracts were evaluated by a broth microdilution method according to previous studies14,15 with minor modifications. Briefly, two-fold serial dilutions of the crude extracts were prepared in MH broth (5.00–0.156 mg DE/mL). Thereafter, 100 µL bacterial suspension (1 × 106 CFU/mL) was added to each well and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Freshly prepared resazurin (0.015%, 30 µL/well) was added to each well and further incubated for 2 h to observe any color changes. The blue color indicated bacterial growth inhibition while the pink color indicated bacterial growth. MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of crude extracts that inhibits bacterial growth. Afterward, the contents of wells with concentrations equal to or higher than MIC values were plated into the MH plate and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The lowest concentration at which no bacterial growth was observed on the MH plate was taken as MBC.

UPLC-QTOF-MS analysis

Major compounds in the heartwood of C. sappan were separated using an ACQUITY I class ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography system (Waters, Milford, MA) with a BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters, Milford, MA). Detailed settings were as follows: injection volume, 1 µL; flow rate, 0.4 mL/min; column oven, 45 °C; mobile phase A, ultrapure water containing 0.1% (v/v) formic acid; mobile phase B, acetonitrile containing 0.1% (v/v) formic acid. The gradient elution program was as follows: 0 min, 5%B; 3 min, 5%B; 10 min, 20%B; 15 min, 100%B; 20 min, 95%B.

A quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (QTOF-MS, Waters, Milford, MA) was operated in a negative ionization mode with following settings: acquisition range, 50–1000 m/z; desolvation gas temperature, 450 °C; flow rate of desolvation gas, 900 L/h; source temperature, 115 °C; sample cone voltage, 40 V; flow rate of cone gas, 50 L/h; capillary voltage, 2 kV; collision energy, 6 eV/20–45 eV; scan rate, 0.2 s; retention time tolerance, 0.01 min; peak alignment, 0.1 mDa. The raw data acquired from the mass spectrometer was initially processed by Masslynx 4.1 software (Waters, Milford, MA) obtaining information including retention time (Rt), mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), and MS intensity of each peak. The mass fragments were manually matched with the mass fragments of online database Dictionary of Natural Products (DNP, http://dnp.chemnetbase.com/).

Statistical analysis

All tests were performed in triplicate and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The data were analyzed with one-way ANOVA plus post-hoc Tukey test using statistical software IBM SPSS Statistics 19 (IBM, New York, NY) to compare the mean values, with p < 0.05 defined as statistical significance.

Results

Total phenolic content (TPC) and total flavonoid content (TFC)

The TPC of sixteen TCM plant extracts is shown in Table 1. Among all the tested extracts, C. sappan had the highest TPC (1247 mg GAE/100 g DE), which was lower than Korean C. sappan 70% ethanol extract (17.6 mg GAE/g)16 and Thailand C. sappan 75% ethanol extract (742 mg GAE/g)17. The next highest were A. catechu (864 mg GAE/100 g DE), F. dibotrys (729 mg GAE/100 g DE), and R. palmatum (635 mg GAE/100 g DE), and all significantly higher than other samples (p < 0.05). P. chinensis had the lowest TPC (31.3 mg GAE/100 g DE). There was a 39.8-fold difference in TPC between the highest and the lowest-ranked extracts.

Flavonoids are a major group of phenolics and are considered to be responsible for the health benefits of TCM plant extracts. The TFC of sixteen tested extracts is shown in Table 1. Similar to the TPC trends, C. sappan, A. catechu, F. dibotrys, and R. palmatum had the highest TFC (346, 281, 202, and 172 mg CE/100 g DE, respectively) and P. chinensis had the lowest TFC (1.14 mg CE/100 g DE). There was a 303-fold difference in TFC between the highest and the lowest-ranked extracts. There was a positive correlation between TFC and TPC (r = 0.968, p < 0.0001) (Table 2), indicating that TFC is the major part of phenolics in Chinese medicinal plants.

In vitro antioxidant activity

The antioxidant activity of phenolic compounds is the most extensively investigated chemical property. In this study, the antioxidant activities of Chinese medicinal plants were evaluated with metal ion chelation activity (FRAP assay) and Trolox equivalent antioxidant capacity.

The FRAP values of the 16 tested samples were shown in Table 1, ranging from 486 mmol Fe2+/g DE (P. chinensis) to 12,927 mmol Fe2+/g DE (C. sappan). There was a 26.6-fold difference in FRAP values between the highest and the lowest-ranked samples. Correlation analysis shows that FRAP had a close correlation with TPC (r = 0.889, p < 0.0001) and TFC (r = 0.825, p < 0.0001), indicating that the metal ion chelating activity of Chinese medicinal plants was closely related to phenolics and flavonoids.

The ABTS values of 16 Chinese medicinal plants are shown in Table 1. Among all the tested Chinese medicinal plants, C. sappan and A. catechu had the strongest ABTS free radical scavenging (6526 and 6256 mmol Trolox/g DE, respectively), and were significantly higher than other Chinese medicinal plants (p < 0.05). Again, P. chinensis had the weakest antioxidant activity (284 mmol Trolox/g DE). ABTS was closely correlated with TPC (r = 0.943, p < 0.0001) and TFC (r = 0.908, p < 0.0001), indicating that phenolics and flavonoids contribute to the ABTS free radical scavenging capacity. There was a significant positive correlation between FRAP and ABTS (r = 0.762, p < 0.001), indicating that both assays demonstrated that Chinese medicinal plants have antioxidant activity. The high positive correlations between these methods may be due to the same mechanism between the two assays.

Different from the ABTS results, the highest DPPH free radical scavenging activity was observed in A. catechu (3882 mmol Trolox/g DE), followed by F. dibotrys (2750 mmol Trolox/g DE) and C. sappan (2690 mmol Trolox/g DE). A. officinarum had the lowest DPPH free radical scavenging capability (291 mmol Trolox/g DE). DPPH was closely correlated with TPC (r = 0.7032, p < 0.01) and less correlated with TFC (r = 0.678, p < 0.01), indicating that phenolics contribute to the DPPH free radical scavenging capacity, while TFC contributes less to the DPPH free radical scavenging capacity. There was a significant positive correlation between DPPH and ABTS (r = 0.7072, p < 0.01), indicating that both assays demonstrated that Chinese medicinal plants have free radical scavenging ability. However, DPPH and FRAP have a weak correlation with each other (r = 0.5726, p < 0.01).

Antiproliferative activity

The growth inhibition of breast cancer cells (MCF7 and MDA-MB-231) and human colon cancer cells (HCT116 and HT29) were expressed as IC50 values and presented in Table 3. The results varied strongly among the tested cell lines, indicating a different cellular sensitivity to these TCM plant extracts. For MCF7 cells, nearly all tested extracts provided a strong antiproliferative effect with IC50 values lower than 100 µg DE/mL, except S. miltiorrhiza and P. orientalis. P. chinensis showed the strongest anti-proliferation effect with IC50 of 3.74 µg DE/mL, which was significantly (p < 0.05) higher than other tested extracts. Compared to MCF7 cells, these extracts had less effect against MDA-MB-231. Only three samples exhibited a rather strong inhibition effect with IC50 values lower than 50 µg DE/mL, C. sappan (11.9 µg DE/mL), A. katsumadai (35.3 µg DE/mL), and R. cordifolia (36.6 µg DE/mL). Different from MCF7 cells, the IC50 of A. officinarum against MDA-MB-231 was more than 200 µg DE/mL, which was 3.62-fold higher than MCF7 (55.3 µg DE/mL). There was no significant correlation between MCF7 and MDA-MB-231 (r = 0.161, p > 0.05).

Intracellular ROS level analysis

Intracellular ROS is closely related to cancer cell proliferation19,20. As shown in Fig. 1a, the intracellular ROS levels in MCF7 cells treated with the plant extracts were lower than the control group except for R. cordifolia (9) and Inula britanica L. (10), indicating that the tested extracts inhibited the proliferation of MCF7 partly by scavenging the intracellular ROS. A similar phenomenon was reported in a previous study21. The intracellular ROS levels in MCF7 cells treated with R. cordifolia (9) and I. britanica (10) were not significantly different from those in the control group. Compared to rutin (20 µM) and the control group, C. sappan (1) and A. catechu (2) exhibited stronger scavenging ROS capability. Previous studies also reported that C. sappan decreased intracellular ROS formation22 thereby suppressing the inflammatory response. Unlike with the MCF7 cells, the tested extracts did not significantly affect the intracellular ROS levels in MDA-MB-231 (Fig. 1b), except for C. sappan (1), which significantly decreased the formation of ROS. F. dibotrys, A. officinarum, I. britanica, and P. orientalis increased the formation of intracellular ROS in MDA-MB-231, resulting in apoptosis. Similar phenomena were reported in previous studies23,24.

For HCT116 cells (Fig. 1c), rutin significantly inhibited the formation of intracellular ROS, and C. sappan (1), A. catechu (2), and I. britanica (10) showed similar effects as rutin. In addition, C. sappan (1) exhibited the strongest inhibition effect among the tested extracts (p < 0.05). Eight tested extracts promoted the formation of ROS. Combining these four cancer cell lines and a previous review25, some TCM plant extracts exert an antiproliferative effect by reducing intracellular ROS levels, such as C. sappan (1), while others induce cell apoptosis by generating ROS.

Antibacterial activity

Extracts were screened for their antibacterial activity against S. aureus SJTUF 20,758, S. aureus ATCC 25,923, E. coli ATCC 25,922, L. monocytogenes ATCC 7644, and S. enteritidis SJTUF 10,978 (Table 4). For S. aureus SJTUF 20,758 and S. aureus ATCC 25,923, the MIC of extracts ranged from 0.313 to 5.00 mg DE/mL. C. sappan and A. katsumadai had the lowest MIC value (0.313 mg DE/mL), followed by A. officinarum and P. orientalis (1.25 mg DE/mL). However, F. dibotrys, U. macrophylla, Inula britanica, and P. chinensis did not exhibit antibacterial activity at the concentrations tested (10 mg DE/mL). In contrast, only C. sappan, C. cassia, A. katsumadai, and S. baicalensis were active against S. enteritidis SJTUF 10,978 and the MIC value of the first three were 2.50 mg DE/mL and the latter one was 5.00 mg DE/mL. In addition, E. coli ATCC 25,922 was only sensitive to C. sappan and C. cassia with MIC values of 1.25 and 5.00 mg DE/mL, respectively. For L. monocytogenes ATCC 7644, only seven samples were active against it with MIC values ranging from 0.625 to 5.00 mg DE/mL. Again, C. sappan exhibited the strongest antibacterial effect with the lowest MIC value (0.625 mg DE/mL), followed by A. katsumadai (1.25 mg DE/mL), A. officinarum, and P. orientalis (2.50 mg DE/mL). A previous study also reported that C. sappan exhibited the highest antibacterial activity26.

Phytochemical profiles of heartwood of C. sappan

UPLC-QTOF-MS analysis was used to characterize the phenolic compounds in the heartwood of C. sappan. From the base peak ion (BPI) chromatogram (Fig. S1), compounds were identified by elucidating the parent ion, MS/MS fragmentation pattern, and calculated formula of peaks, and by matching the above data with that in related references and database, Dictionary of Natural Products (DNP, http://dnp.chemnetbase.com/). The detailed information of all identified compounds is shown in Table 5. Notably, 32 compounds were assigned, including two organic acids, two phenolic acids, two chromones, eight flavonoids, four lactones, and 14 other phenolic compounds, and ten of them were proposed from C. sappan for the first time, including dl-umtatin, divaricatol, 3´,8-dihydroxy-methoxyisoflavan, 3’,4’,7-trihydroxyisoflavanone, benzopyran derivative, citflavanone, demethyl auraptenol, osthenol, panial, and dalbergin.

Discussion on the potential mechanisms of the identified phytochemicals

The superior bioactivities demonstrated by the heartwood extract of C. sappan, notably its antioxidant, antibacterial, and antiproliferative effects, can be mechanistically linked to the diverse array of phytochemicals identified by UPLC-QTOF-MS. The presence of flavonoids, phenolic acids, homoisoflavonoids, and brazilin-related compounds suggests a multi-faceted and potentially synergistic mode of action.

The exceptionally high antioxidant capacity observed in the FRAP, ABTS, and DPPH assays is fundamentally rooted in the chemical structures of the identified phenolics. Compounds such as epicatechin (NO. 1), kaempferol (NO. 10), and dihydroquercetin (NO. 5) possess multiple phenolic hydroxyl groups on their aromatic rings. These groups act as potent hydrogen donors, effectively neutralizing free radicals by stabilizing the resulting phenoxyl radicals, which directly explains the extract’s free radical-scavenging capabilities45. Furthermore, the ortho-dihydroxy (catechol) configuration found in the B-ring of compounds like epicatechin confers a strong metal-chelating ability. By sequestering transition metal ions like Fe2+, these compounds inhibit the Fenton reaction, a primary source of highly reactive hydroxyl radicals, thereby contributing to the outstanding FRAP activity46.

The broad-spectrum antibacterial activity of C. sappan, particularly against Gram-positive bacteria, likely involves targeting the bacterial cell membrane and critical enzymes. Hydrophobic compounds like brazilin (NO. 26) and brazilein (NO. 21) may integrate into the lipid bilayer of bacterial membranes, disrupting membrane integrity and leading to increased permeability, efflux of cellular contents, and eventual cell lysis47. Additionally, phenolic compounds can interfere with bacterial virulence by inhibiting enzyme systems. For instance, specific compounds in C. sappan have been reported to inhibit sortase A activity in S. aureus, an enzyme crucial for surface protein anchoring and biofilm formation48. The presence of linoleic acid (NO. 32) may also contribute, as fatty acids are known to disrupt bacterial membrane functions.

The antiproliferative effects of C. sappan are complex and involve a sophisticated interplay with intracellular ROS, which is cell-line dependent. Our data indicates that C. sappan extract significantly reduced ROS levels in MCF-7 and HCT116 cells. This effect can be attributed to its rich antioxidant constituents, which scavenge excess ROS and may consequently dampen ROS-driven proliferative signaling pathways, leading to cell cycle arrest49. However, it is crucial to note that the anticancer effect is not solely due to ROS reduction. Key compounds like brazilein have been extensively documented to induce apoptosis through ROS-independent pathways, including the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway and induction of G2/M cell cycle arrest50. Furthermore, sappanone B (13) has been identified as a specific inhibitor of the NF-κB signaling pathway, a central regulator of inflammation and cell survival, which is often hyperactive in cancers51. This suggests that C. sappan exerts its antiproliferative activity through a combination of antioxidant-driven cytoprotection against genomic instability and direct pro-apoptotic signaling.

In summary, the potent and multi-functional bioactivities of C. sappan heartwood are not attributable to a single compound but are the result of the concerted action of its complex phytochemical profile. These compounds likely operate through a network of mechanisms: direct free radical scavenging and metal chelation for antioxidant effects; membrane disruption and enzyme inhibition for antibacterial action; and dual modulation of ROS levels coupled with direct induction of apoptosis and interference with key signaling pathways for anticancer activity. This multifaceted nature underscores the potential of C. sappan as a valuable source of functional constituents and justifies further investigation into isolating its key active principles for deeper mechanistic studies and therapeutic development.

Conclusions

This study systematically evaluated the total phenolics, total flavonoids, antiproliferative and antibacterial activities of sixteen TCM extracts, and their effects on the intracellular ROS level. C. sappan contained the highest level of phenolics and flavonoids and exhibited the strongest antibacterial and antiproliferative activities. In addition, C. sappan exerts antiproliferative activity by inhibiting the generation of intracellular ROS. Thirty-two major bioactive compounds were identified in C. sappan and ten compounds were proposed from it for the first time. The results of this study contribute to understanding the value of C. sappan in terms of its potential as a source for the development of functional products or lead compounds for antibacterial and anticancer applications. This study also provides phytochemical information for the further biological activity research of C. sappan.
