Microbiological Profiles of Suspected Postoperative Pneumonia After Esophagectomy: A Focus on Gram-negative Pathogens
Authors: TAKESHI MATSUBARA, HIROKI OKAMURA, KANA FUJIHARA, KENTO NAKAJIMA, YUTO YASUDA, KOKI KAWAKAMI, YURIKO YAMADA, KAZUNARI ISHITOBI, SHUNSUKE KAJI, TAKAHITO TANIURA, TAKAYUKI TANAKA, TETSU YAMAMOTO, MASAAKI HIDAKA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimPostoperative pneumonia is an important complication after esophagectomy for esophageal cancer. Although sputum cultures are often obtained when pneumonia is suspected, the microbiological profiles detected in this setting remain insufficiently characterized. This study aimed to describe sputum culture findings at suspected postoperative pneumonia onset, focusing on Gram-negative organisms and targetable pathogens relevant to antimicrobial selection.Patients and MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 150 consecutive patients who underwent esophagectomy for esophageal cancer between January 2014 and April 2026. Patients were classified into early video-assisted thoracoscopic (VATS) esophagectomy, robot-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy (RAMIE), and late VATS esophagectomy groups. Clinically relevant postoperative pneumonia was defined as Clavien-Dindo grade II or higher pneumonia within 30 days after surgery. Sputum cultures obtained at suspected pneumonia onset were reviewed.ResultsA significantly higher incidence of pneumonia was observed in the RAMIE group (44.4%) compared to the early (19.0%) and late (24.2%) VATS groups (p=0.008). Sputum cultures were positive in all 44 pneumonia cases, and 60 organisms were detected. Gram-negative organisms accounted for 41 isolates (68.3%), and at least one Gram-negative organism was detected in 36 patients (81.8%). Targetable pathogens were detected in 33 patients (75.0%). No clear surgical-era-specific microbiological profile was identified among pneumonia cases.ConclusionSputum cultures, after esophagectomy, obtained at suspected postoperative pneumonia onset frequently revealed Gram-negative organisms and targetable pathogens. These findings provide a clinical basis for empirical antimicrobial selection and subsequent de-escalation strategies, but detected organisms should be interpreted as organism distribution rather than definitive causative pathogens.

Introduction

Postoperative pneumonia remains one of the most clinically important complications after esophagectomy for esophageal cancer. Esophagectomy is a highly invasive procedure that involves thoracic manipulation, prolonged operative and anesthetic stress, one-lung ventilation, postoperative pain, impaired cough strength, and delayed mobilization. In addition, recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy, swallowing dysfunction, aspiration, and impaired airway clearance may contribute to postoperative respiratory morbidity. Therefore, even in the era of minimally invasive esophagectomy, prevention and early management of postoperative pneumonia remain major challenges.

The clinical impact of postoperative pneumonia extends beyond the early postoperative period. Previous studies have shown that postoperative pneumonia and infectious complications after esophagectomy are associated with delayed recovery, prolonged hospitalization, impaired functional status, and worse long-term survival (1-4). Tanaka et al. reported that acute-phase postoperative pneumonia was an important prognostic factor in patients with esophageal cancer (1). Booka et al. also demonstrated the negative prognostic impact of postoperative pneumonia and postoperative complications after esophagectomy (2, 3). Tamagawa et al. reported that postoperative pneumonia adversely influenced survival and recurrence after esophagectomy for esophageal cancer (4).

These findings suggest that postoperative pneumonia should be regarded not only as a short-term adverse event but also as a clinically meaningful condition that may influence subsequent recovery and oncological outcomes.

To improve the evaluation of postoperative outcomes, standardized definitions are essential. The Esophagectomy Complications Consensus Group proposed an international framework for reporting complications after esophagectomy, including pulmonary complications (5). Based on such standardized assessment, several risk factors for postoperative pneumonia have been investigated. Patient-related factors, including smoking history, pulmonary comorbidity, poor physical function, sarcopenia, impaired swallowing function, and insufficient oral care, have been associated with postoperative pulmonary complications (6-9). Yoshida et al. further reported that asymptomatic sputum retention in the respiratory tract on preoperative computed tomography was an independent predictor of postoperative pneumonia after esophagectomy (10). These observations indicate that postoperative pneumonia after esophagectomy is a multifactorial condition involving host vulnerability, airway clearance impairment, aspiration risk, and surgical stress.

Microbiological evaluation may provide additional insight into this complex condition. Several studies have investigated the clinical usefulness of perioperative bacterial cultures in patients undergoing esophagectomy. Kaneta et al. reported that positive preoperative bacterial culture was associated with severe postoperative pneumonia; however, the organisms detected preoperatively did not necessarily correspond to those detected after pneumonia onset (11). Matsui et al. showed that sputum culture on postoperative day 1 was useful for predicting early postoperative pneumonia, particularly when target bacterial species, Gram-positive cocci or Gram-negative rods, were detected (12). Kosumi et al. also suggested that monitoring sputum cultures in patients receiving preoperative treatment may help characterize perioperative changes in airway bacterial profiles (13). These studies suggest that sputum culture may be useful for risk stratification and antimicrobial decision-making in selected settings.

However, the clinical meaning of sputum culture results requires careful interpretation. Sputum cultures may reflect not only infection but also colonization, oropharyngeal contamination, aspiration of oral flora, and postoperative airway changes. Therefore, detected organisms should not automatically be regarded as definitive causative pathogens. This distinction is particularly important after esophagectomy, where aspiration risk, secretion retention, hospital exposure, and antimicrobial use may coexist. In this context, sputum culture findings should be interpreted together with clinical symptoms, radiological findings, oxygenation status, inflammatory response, sputum quality, and the treatment course.

Despite previous reports on preoperative or early postoperative screening cultures, the microbiological profiles of sputum cultures obtained specifically at the time of clinically suspected postoperative pneumonia after esophagectomy remain insufficiently described. In daily practice, sputum cultures are often submitted when pneumonia is suspected based on fever, inflammatory response, oxygenation deterioration, increased sputum production, and new pulmonary infiltrates. These culture results may influence empirical antimicrobial selection, escalation, continuation, or de-escalation. However, it remains unclear which organisms are commonly detected in this clinical setting and how frequently Gram-negative organisms or targetable pathogens relevant to antimicrobial selection are identified.

Therefore, the present study aimed to characterize sputum culture findings obtained at the time of suspected postoperative pneumonia after esophagectomy for esophageal cancer. We focused on the distribution of detected organisms, the predominance of Gram-negative bacteria, polymicrobial growth, and the frequency of targetable pathogens relevant to antimicrobial selection. We also explored whether microbiological profiles differed according to surgical era and thoracic approach. Because sputum culture results may reflect both infection and colonization, the findings were interpreted as organism distribution at suspected pneumonia onset rather than as definitive evidence of causative pathogens.

Patients and Methods

Study design and patients. This retrospective single-center cohort study was conducted at the Department of Digestive and General Surgery, Shimane University Faculty of Medicine. We reviewed consecutive patients who underwent esophagectomy for esophageal cancer between January 2014 and April 2026.

Patients were classified according to surgical era and thoracic approach into three groups: early video-assisted thoracoscopic esophagectomy (early VATS), robot-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy (RAMIE), and late VATS. Patients without postoperative pneumonia were included for comparison of pneumonia incidence among surgical eras. The primary microbiological analysis focused on patients who developed clinically relevant postoperative pneumonia within 30 days after surgery and underwent sputum culture at the time postoperative pneumonia was clinically suspected.

Definition of postoperative pneumonia. Postoperative pneumonia was diagnosed based on clinical and radiological findings. Clinical findings included fever, increased inflammatory response, oxygenation deterioration, increased sputum production, or the need for antimicrobial treatment. Radiological findings included new pulmonary infiltrates on chest radiography or computed tomography.

Pneumonia severity was graded according to the Clavien-Dindo classification. Clinically relevant postoperative pneumonia was defined as Clavien-Dindo grade II or higher pneumonia occurring within 30 days after esophagectomy.

Sputum culture assessment. Sputum cultures were obtained when postoperative pneumonia was clinically suspected. They were not performed as routine screening cultures in all patients. Therefore, culture results were analyzed as organism distribution at suspected pneumonia onset, not as a screening test for predicting future pneumonia.

For each pneumonia case, sputum culture results were retrospectively reviewed. Up to two organisms per patient were recorded when multiple organisms were reported. Detected organisms were classified according to organism type and Gram staining category. Polymicrobial growth was defined as detection of two organisms in a single pneumonia episode. Because sputum cultures may reflect infection, colonization, or oropharyngeal contamination, detected organisms were not automatically regarded as definitive causative pathogens.

Definition of targetable pathogens. Targetable pathogens were defined before the final analysis based on organisms generally considered clinically relevant for antimicrobial selection in postoperative or hospital-acquired pneumonia. This classification mainly included clinically important Gram-negative organisms and selected Gram-positive pathogens, such as Staphylococcus aureus. Candida spp., oral commensals, Neisseria spp., alpha-streptococci, gamma-streptococci, and viridans group streptococci alone were not considered targetable pathogens. Some less frequently detected organisms may be clinically relevant depending on the clinical context; therefore, all culture results were interpreted together with clinical findings, radiological findings, oxygenation status, sputum characteristics, prior antimicrobial exposure, and antimicrobial treatment course.

Outcomes. The primary endpoint was the microbiological profile of sputum cultures obtained at suspected postoperative pneumonia onset. This included the distribution of detected organisms, frequency of Gram-negative organisms, frequency of targetable pathogens, and frequency of polymicrobial growth.

Secondary endpoints included the incidence of postoperative pneumonia according to surgical era, differences in culture findings among surgical groups, and the association between postoperative pneumonia and overall survival.

Statistical analysis. Continuous variables are presented as median with interquartile range and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test for two-group comparisons or the Kruskal-Wallis test for three-group comparisons, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as number and percentage and were compared using Fisher’s exact test. Overall survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. A Cox proportional hazards model was used to evaluate the association between postoperative pneumonia and overall survival. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP software (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, Cary, NC, USA). A two-sided p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shimane University Faculty of Medicine. The requirement for written informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design, and patients were provided the opportunity to opt out according to institutional policy.

Results

Study population and postoperative pneumonia. A total of 150 patients who underwent esophagectomy for esophageal cancer between January 2014 and April 2026 were included. According to surgical era and thoracic approach, 63 patients were classified into the early video-assisted thoracoscopic esophagectomy group (early VATS), 54 into the robot-assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy group (RAMIE), and 33 into the late VATS group.

Clinically relevant postoperative pneumonia within 30 days after surgery occurred in 44 patients (29.3%). The incidence of postoperative pneumonia differed significantly among the three groups: 19.0% in the early VATS group (12/63), 44.4% in the RAMIE group (24/54), and 24.2% in the late VATS group (8/33) (p=0.008). Surgical era and microbiological findings. When the entire cohort was analyzed, culture-positive pneumonia cases and targetable pathogen detection were most frequent in the RAMIE group. Among patients with postoperative pneumonia, Gram-negative organisms and targetable pathogens were detected across all surgical eras, and no clear surgical-era-specific microbiological profile was identified.

Overall survival. Patients who developed postoperative pneumonia had worse overall survival than those without postoperative pneumonia. The 5-year overall survival rate was 20.2% in patients with postoperative pneumonia and 54.3% in those without postoperative pneumonia. Overall survival differed significantly between the two groups by the log-rank test. In a Cox proportional hazards model, postoperative pneumonia was associated with increased mortality. Overall survival analysis was performed as an exploratory analysis.

Discussion

This study characterized sputum culture findings obtained at the time postoperative pneumonia was clinically suspected after esophagectomy for esophageal cancer. The main finding was that Gram-negative organisms and targetable pathogens were frequently detected in this clinical setting. Unlike studies evaluating preoperative or early postoperative screening cultures, the present study focused on cultures obtained at suspected pneumonia onset. Therefore, our findings describe the microbiological profile encountered in daily clinical practice when postoperative pneumonia is suspected, rather than the predictive value of routine perioperative cultures.

The predominance of Gram-negative organisms is clinically relevant after esophagectomy. Postoperative pneumonia in this population is unlikely to result from a single mechanism. Instead, it may reflect overlapping factors, including aspiration, impaired swallowing function, recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy, reduced cough strength, secretion retention, prolonged operative stress, postoperative immobility, and exposure to the hospital environment (6-10). The frequent detection of Enterobacterales and non-fermenting Gram-negative organisms is consistent with a postoperative and aspiration-related pneumonia phenotype. These findings suggest that antimicrobial management for suspected post-esophagectomy pneumonia may need to consider organisms beyond those typically expected in community-acquired pneumonia.

Several previous studies have investigated bacterial cultures in patients undergoing esophagectomy, but their timing and clinical purpose differ from those of the present study. Kaneta et al. reported that preoperative bacterial culture could predict severe postoperative pneumonia; however, organisms detected before surgery did not necessarily correspond to those detected after pneumonia onset (11). Thus, preoperative culture may help identify high-risk patients, but it may not be sufficient for determining the microbiological background at pneumonia onset. Matsui et al. showed that sputum culture on postoperative day 1 was associated with early postoperative pneumonia, especially when target bacterial species or Gram-negative rods were detected (12). Kosumi et al. also suggested that monitoring sputum cultures in patients receiving preoperative treatment may help characterize perioperative airway bacterial profiles (13). These studies mainly address risk stratification before or shortly after surgery.

Matsunaga et al. reported that perioperative bacteriological cultures, including intratracheal sputum, mouthwash, and gastric fluid cultures, may be useful for estimating pathogens responsible for postoperative pneumonia and selecting effective antibiotics after esophagectomy (14). Their study is closely related to the present work, but the clinical question differs. Matsunaga et al. evaluated the usefulness of cultures obtained perioperatively for predicting or covering pathogens when pneumonia later developed. In contrast, the present study focused specifically on sputum cultures obtained when postoperative pneumonia was clinically suspected. Therefore, our study does not primarily evaluate the predictive value of routine perioperative cultures; rather, it describes the organism distribution and targetable pathogens detected at the point of clinical decision-making for suspected pneumonia.

The concept of targetable pathogens is central to interpreting sputum culture results. In this study, targetable pathogens were defined as organisms that could reasonably influence antimicrobial selection, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterobacterales, and Staphylococcus aureus. This conservative definition was chosen to avoid overinterpretation of organisms that often represent colonization or contamination. Candida spp., alpha-streptococci, Neisseria spp., viridans group streptococci, and other oral commensals were not considered targetable pathogens when detected alone. This distinction is clinically important because overinterpretation of sputum culture findings may lead to unnecessary broad-spectrum or antifungal therapy, whereas underrecognition of clinically relevant Gram-negative organisms may delay appropriate antimicrobial coverage.

Sputum culture at suspected pneumonia onset may contribute to antimicrobial stewardship. Initial treatment for postoperative pneumonia is usually empirical, and the choice of antimicrobial agents must often be made before culture results are available. Once available, culture results may support escalation when resistant or high-risk organisms are detected, continuation when the empirical regimen provides appropriate coverage, or de-escalation when broad-spectrum therapy is no longer necessary. This approach is consistent with general principles of hospital-acquired pneumonia management, in which microbiological data are used to guide appropriate antimicrobial coverage and de-escalation when feasible (15). In esophagectomy patients, antimicrobial prophylaxis may also influence early postoperative pneumonia; Higaki et al. reported that ampicillin/sulbactam was associated with a lower incidence of early-onset pneumonia than cefazolin after thoracoscopic esophagectomy (16). These findings support the importance of considering both perioperative antimicrobial strategy and culture-based treatment adjustment.

The interpretation of sputum culture should be integrated with broader pneumonia-prevention strategies. Perioperative oral care has been reported to reduce postoperative pneumonia after esophageal cancer surgery, supporting the importance of reducing aspiration of pathogenic oropharyngeal flora (9). Preoperative sputum retention on computed tomography has also been reported as a predictor of postoperative pneumonia, suggesting that impaired airway clearance may already be present before surgery in some patients (10). Therefore, culture results should not be interpreted in isolation, but should be considered alongside aspiration prevention, swallowing evaluation, respiratory rehabilitation, airway clearance, early mobilization, and clinical severity.

The surgical-era findings should be interpreted cautiously. Culture-positive pneumonia cases and targetable pathogen detection were more frequent during the RAMIE era in the overall cohort, but this was largely related to the higher incidence of pneumonia during that period. Among patients who developed pneumonia, no distinct surgical-era-specific microbiological profile was identified. Therefore, the present findings do not indicate that a specific thoracic approach produces a unique bacterial pattern. Rather, they suggest that when postoperative pneumonia occurs after esophagectomy, Gram-negative organisms and targetable pathogens are commonly encountered across surgical eras. Recent studies published in 2026 further highlight the complexity of postoperative pneumonia after esophagectomy. Duff et al. demonstrated substantial inter-observer variability among specialists in the radiographic and clinical diagnosis of post-esophagectomy pneumonia, suggesting that pneumonia diagnosis in the early postoperative period remains partly subjective (17). This diagnostic uncertainty reinforces the need to interpret sputum culture findings together with clinical symptoms, radiological findings, oxygenation status, and inflammatory response rather than relying on microbiological results alone. In addition, Řezáč et al. investigated perioperative oropharyngeal microflora, targeted decontamination, and postoperative nosocomial and opportunistic infections in patients undergoing esophagectomy after chemoradiotherapy (18). Their findings support the clinical relevance of perioperative microbial profiles while also emphasizing that postoperative respiratory infections may arise from complex interactions among colonization, aspiration, hospital-acquired organisms, and host vulnerability. These recent observations are consistent with our finding that Gram-negative organisms and targetable pathogens are frequently detected at suspected pneumonia onset, but that detected organisms should not automatically be regarded as definitive causative pathogens.

Limitations. First, it was a retrospective single-center study with a limited number of pneumonia cases. Second, sputum cultures were obtained only when postoperative pneumonia was clinically suspected and were not performed routinely in all patients; therefore, selection bias and variation in culture timing are unavoidable. Third, sputum sample quality was not uniformly assessed, and culture results may have been influenced by colonization, oropharyngeal contamination, or prior antimicrobial exposure. Fourth, the study described microbiological profiles and did not establish whether each detected organism was a true causative pathogen. Fifth, antimicrobial selection, escalation, and de-escalation were not standardized, limiting evaluation of the direct therapeutic impact of culture results. Finally, because surgical-era groups differed in time period and perioperative context, residual confounding related to institutional practice changes cannot be excluded. A related cohort from our institution has been analyzed in a separate study focusing on thoracic operative time, surgical approach, and postoperative pneumonia. In contrast, the present study was designed to address a distinct clinical question: the microbiological profile of sputum cultures obtained at suspected postoperative pneumonia onset.

In summary, sputum cultures obtained at suspected postoperative pneumonia onset after esophagectomy frequently revealed Gram-negative organisms and targetable pathogens. These findings highlight the potential role of sputum culture as a practical adjunct for understanding the microbiological background of post-esophagectomy pneumonia and supporting antimicrobial stewardship. However, culture results should be interpreted as organism distribution at suspected pneumonia onset, not as definitive evidence of causative pathogens, and should always be integrated with clinical and radiological findings.

Conclusion

Sputum cultures obtained at the time of suspected postoperative pneumonia after esophagectomy frequently revealed Gram-negative organisms and targetable pathogens. These findings suggest that sputum culture may serve as a practical adjunct for understanding the microbiological background of post-esophagectomy pneumonia and supporting antimicrobial selection or de-escalation. However, detected organisms should not be regarded as definitive causative pathogens based on culture results alone, and must be interpreted together with clinical and radiological findings.
