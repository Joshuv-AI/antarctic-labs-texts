Certifying entanglement dimensionality by random Pauli sampling
Changhao Yi

Abstract
We introduce a Pauli-measurement-based algorithm to certify the Schmidt number of n n -qubit pure states. Our protocol achieves an average-case sample complexity of 𝒪 ⁡ ( poly ⁡ ( n ) ​ χ 2 ) \mathcal{O}(\mathrm{poly}(n)\chi^{2}) , a substantial improvement over the 𝒪 ⁡ ( 2 n ​ χ ) \mathcal{O}(2^{n}\chi) worst-case bound. By utilizing local pseudorandom unitaries, we ensure the worst case can be transformed into the average-case with high probability. This work establishes a scalable approach to high-dimensional entanglement certification and introduces a proof framework for random Pauli sampling.



I
Introduction

Entanglement is a hallmark of quantum mechanics and a key resource for quantum information processing. Characterizing and detecting entanglement has been a central focus of the quantum information community for decades
12
. Beyond simple separability, the Schmidt number—or
entanglement dimensionality
—provides a refined measure of entanglement strength
36
. High-dimensional entangled states have recently gained attention for their potential to enhance various tasks, including quantum communication
44
, channel discrimination
2
, quantum key distribution
19
, and linear-optical fusion
42
.

The development of practical protocols for certifying the Schmidt number has recently become a prominent research focus
18
. Existing approaches include fidelity-based methods
18
;
3
;
32
;
24
, correlation-matrix techniques
27
;
41
;
26
, protocols relying on
k
-positive maps
43
;
31
, verification operators
40

16
;
5
.
These certification protocols generally fall into two categories:
basis-dependent
and
basis-independent
. Basis-dependent methods, such as fidelity-based criteria, offer dimension-independent sampling overhead but require prior knowledge of the state’s Schmidt basis, making them sensitive to local rotations. Conversely, basis-independent protocols, like those using correlation matrices, circumvent the need for basis alignment but often suffer from sampling complexities that scale linearly with system dimension. Furthermore, these methods frequently necessitate high-order
unitary designs

45
;
10
, posing significant challenges for experimental implementation.

In this work, we present a basis-independent protocol for certifying the Schmidt number of pure bipartite states, featuring sample complexity with only weak dimension dependence and requiring solely Pauli measurements and two random unitary samples. Our protocol builds on the observation that the rank of a pure state’s correlation matrix equals the square of its Schmidt number. Although full reconstruction demands all
d^{4}-1
matrix entries, where
d
is the dimension of local Hilbert space, random matrix theory guarantees that
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d\chi)
Pauli samples (which corresponds to
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{2}\chi^{2})
measurement setups) suffice to preserve rank in the worst case, where
\chi
is the Schmidt number of the target state. Notably, when the Schmidt basis aligns with columns of a Haar-random unitary, merely
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\chi^{2})
Pauli samples are needed with high probability. These findings highlight the power of
pseudorandom unitary
(PRU) techniques
20
;
13
;
35
;
29
for efficient entanglement certification.

Random Pauli measurement has become a pivotal tool in modern quantum information processing, powering diverse applications including quantum state tomography
11
;
28
, classical shadow tomography
17
;
21

8

14

17
;
6

25

This work uncovers one such fundamental property, demonstrating that carefully chosen random Pauli bases can dramatically reduce the measurement cost for high-dimensional entanglement certification. These findings not only advance practical Schmidt-number certification but also open new avenues for harnessing pseudorandom techniques in randomized measurement protocols, paving the way for scalable characterization of complex quantum systems.

II
Schmidt number certification

Consider a bipartite pure state
|\psi\rangle
supported on
\mathcal{H}_{AB}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
with
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{A})=\dim(\mathcal{H}_{B})=d
and Schmidt rank
\chi
:

|\psi\rangle=\sum_{i=0}^{\chi-1}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\,|l_{i}\rangle\otimes|r_{i}\rangle,\quad\lambda_{i}>0.

The set
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\chi-1}
is the Schmidt spectrum, and
\{|l_{i}\rangle\}_{i=0}^{\chi-1}
,
\{|r_{i}\rangle\}_{i=0}^{\chi-1}
are the Schmidt bases.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{j}\}_{j=0}^{d^{2}-1}
be an operator basis of Hermitian operators on a
d
-dimensional system (e.g., Pauli operators including the identity), and
\mathcal{P}_{0}:=\mathcal{P}/\{I\}
. We define the full correlation matrix (CM) of the state as

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=\sum_{P,Q\in\mathcal{P}}T_{P,Q}\,|e_{P}\rangle\langle e_{Q}|,

\displaystyle T_{P,Q}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi|(P\otimes Q)|\psi\rangle,

where
\{|e_{P}\rangle\}
is an orthonormal vector basis in
\mathbb{C}^{d^{2}}
.

A key property is the following:

Lemma 1
.

If
|\psi\rangle
has Schmidt rank
\chi
, then
\rank(T)=\chi^{2}
.

In our protocol, we randomly select a subset
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{P}_{0}
consisting of
K
Pauli operators and form the projected CM

T_{\mathcal{S}}=\sum_{P,Q\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{I\}}T_{P,Q}\,|e_{P}\rangle\langle e_{Q}|.

Since
T_{\mathcal{S}}
is a principal submatrix of
T
, we have
\rank(T_\mathcal{S})\leq\rank(T)=\chi^{2}
. Thus, observing
\rank(T_\mathcal{S})>\chi^{2}
certifies that the Schmidt number of
|\psi\rangle
exceeds
\chi
.

Our main result characterizes the size of
\mathcal{S}
needed to preserve full rank with high probability.

Theorem 1

(Informal)
.

For a Schmidt number-
\chi
pure state on
d\times d
systems, recovering
\rank(T_\mathcal{S})=\chi^{2}
requires
\mathcal{O}(d\chi^{2})
Pauli operators in the worst case, but only
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\chi^{2})
in typical cases.

In this context, a
“typical”
state implies that the Schmidt bases
\{|l_{i}\rangle\}_{i=0}^{\chi-1}
and
\{|r_{i}\rangle\}_{i=0}^{\chi-1}
resemble the first
\chi
columns of a Haar-random unitary, with Schmidt coefficients
\{\lambda_{i}\}
of comparable magnitude. Under these conditions, the sample complexity is primarily governed by the anticoncentration of the Schmidt vectors. Specifically, if
|\langle l_{i}|P|l_{j}\rangle|
and
|\langle r_{i}|P|r_{j}\rangle|
are
\Omega(d^{-1/2})
for the majority of Pauli operators
P
and index pairs
(i,j)
, then a sample size of
K=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\chi^{2})
is sufficient for recovery. Conversely, if the Schmidt basis vectors are aligned with the computational basis, the system represents a worst-case scenario where the required number of Pauli operators scales as
\mathcal{O}(d\chi)
. We can bypass this worst-case scaling by applying local random rotations. This ensures the Schmidt basis vectors are anticoncentrated, allowing the protocol to work with far fewer measurements.

III
Proof idea

Observe that the projected CM has the following decomposition:

\displaystyle T_{\mathcal{S}}=\widetilde{U}_{R}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{\Psi}\widetilde{U}_{L},

(1)

\displaystyle\widetilde{U}_{L}:=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{I\}}\sum_{i,j=0}^{d-1}\langle l_{i}|P|l_{j}\rangle\left(|e_{i}\rangle\otimes|e_{j}\rangle\right)\langle e_{P}|,

(2)

where
\{|e_{i}\rangle\}_{i=0}^{d-1}
is a vector basis for a Hilbert space of dimension
d
. The other matrix
\widetilde{U}_{R}
can be defined in a similar way. Both
\sqrt{\Lambda_{\Psi}}\widetilde{U}_{L}
and
\sqrt{\Lambda_{\Psi}}\widetilde{U}_{L}
have maximal rank
\chi^{2}
provided that
d\gg\chi^{2}
. If rank
(\sqrt{\Lambda_{\Psi}}\widetilde{U}_{L})=\mathrm{rank}(\sqrt{\Lambda_{\Psi}}\widetilde{U}_{R})=\chi^{2}
, then rank
(T_{\mathcal{S}})=\chi^{2}
. Hence, the question becomes how large
\mathcal{S}
should be to keep rank
(\sqrt{\Lambda_{\Psi}}\widetilde{U}_{L})=(\sqrt{\Lambda_{\Psi}}\widetilde{U}_{R})=\chi^{2}
with high probability. We will focus on
\sqrt{\Lambda_{\Psi}}\widetilde{U}_{L}
henceforth.

Introduce
|e_{ij}\rangle:=|e_{i}\rangle\otimes|e_{j}\rangle
for simplification. Consider the space span by
\{|e_{ij}\rangle\}_{i,j=0}^{\chi-1}
. The following vector forms a frame on this space:

|v_{P}\rangle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i,j=0}^{\chi-1}\langle l_{i}|P|l_{j}\rangle|e_{ij}\rangle\quad P\in\mathcal{P}.

(3)

That is,

\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}|v_{P}\rangle\langle v_{P}|=\mathbb{I}_{\Psi}:=\sum_{i,j=0}^{\chi-1}|e_{ij}\rangle\langle e_{ij}|.

(4)

Moreover,

\mathrm{rank}\left(\sqrt{\Lambda_{\Psi}}\widetilde{U}_{L}\right)=\mathrm{rank}\left(\{|v_{P}\rangle\}_{P\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{I\}}\right).

(5)

Up to this step, the question is reduced to studying the distribution of rank
\left(\{|v_{P}\rangle\}_{P\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{I\}}\right)
. If the rank of these random vectors equal
\chi^{2}
, then the Schmidt number certification protocol succeeds.

Note that
P=I
is special in that we know the value of
|v_{I}\rangle
without computing, and
|v_{I}\rangle\in
span
(\{|e_{ij}\rangle\}_{i,j=0}^{\chi-1})
. Therefore, we define

\displaystyle|\widetilde{v}_{P}\rangle

\displaystyle:=(\mathbb{I}_{\Psi}-|e_{I}\rangle\langle e_{I}|)|v_{P}\rangle,

(6)

\displaystyle|e_{I}\rangle

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\chi}}\sum_{i=0}^{\chi-1}|e_{ii}\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{d}{\chi}}|v_{I}\rangle,

(7)

so that

\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}|\widetilde{v}_{P}\rangle\langle\widetilde{v}_{P}|=\mathbb{I}_{\Psi}-|e_{I}\rangle\langle e_{I}|.

(8)

and rank
(\{|v_{P}\rangle\}_{P\in\mathcal{S}\cup\{I\}})=
rank
(\{|\widetilde{v}_{P}\rangle\}_{P\in\mathcal{S}})+1
almost surely.

The quantitative relation between the rank of random vectors and the size of
\mathcal{S}
can be characterized by the following theorem. The proof can be found in Appendix
D.1
.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
\mathcal{S}
is the set of
K
random Pauli operators, and

\mu_{0}:=d\max_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\sum_{i,j=0}^{\chi-1}|\langle l_{i}|P|l_{j}\rangle|^{2}.

(9)

If
K=\mathcal{O}(\mu_{0}\log(\chi/\eta))
, then with failure probability at most
\eta
, we have

\mathrm{rank}\left(\{|\widetilde{v}_{P}\rangle\}_{P\in\mathcal{S}}\right)=\chi^{2}-1.

(10)

In the worst case, we have
\mu_{0}=\mathcal{O}(d)
. For instance, if
\{|l_{i}\rangle\}_{i=0}^{d-1}
is the computational basis, then
\mu_{0}=\mathcal{O}(d\chi)
and
K=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d\chi)
. We give a detail analysis about the sample complexity of this situation in Appendix
E
.

The next corollary demonstrates that when the Schmidt basis vectors exhibit sufficient delocalization (
\mu_{0}=\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{poly}\log(d)\chi^{2})
), the protocol achieves success with merely
K=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\chi^{2})
copies without requiring additional modifications. The proof can be found in Appendix
D.2
.

Corollary 1
.

Suppose
\mathcal{S}
is the set of
K
random Pauli operators, and
U
is a Haar random unitary.
If

K=\mathcal{O}\left(\chi^{2}\mathrm{poly}\log(d\chi^2/\eta)\log(\chi/\eta)\right),

(11)

then with failure probability at most
\eta
, we have

\mathrm{rank}\left(\{|\widetilde{v}_{U^{\dagger}PU}\rangle\}_{P\in\mathcal{S}}\right)=\chi^{2}-1.

(12)

In cases where delocalization is insufficient (
\mu_{0}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d\chi)
), applying random local unitaries beforehand is necessary to ensure low Pauli sampling cost. See Fig.
1
for the illustration. In practice, instead of generating true Haar-random unitaries, one can employ computationally indistinguishable pseudorandom unitaries (PRUs). Recent results
35
established that PRUs can be constructed on an
n
-qubit system using quantum circuits of depth
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{poly}(\log n))
in one-dimensional architectures.

IV
Robustness to noises

For each
P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
, accurately estimating
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P)
within additive error
\varepsilon
with high probability requires a measurement budget of
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})
. Moreover, the deviation between the empirical mean and the true expectation value is a sub-Gaussian random variable. We therefore denote the measurement-induced error by
\delta T_{\mathcal{S}}
, so that the experimentally obtained CM is

\widetilde{T}_{\mathcal{S}}=T_{\mathcal{S}}+\delta T_{\mathcal{S}},

where each entry of
\delta T_{\mathcal{S}}
is a sub-Gaussian random variable with variance
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
.
The following proposition follows from a standard result in high-dimensional probability
34
.

Proposition 1
.

With high probability, the singular values of
\delta T_{\mathcal{S}}
are bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|}\,\varepsilon)
.

Note that the Frobenius 2-norm of the full CM is
d^{2}
. Consequently, the Frobenius 2-norm of
T_{\mathcal{S}}
scales as
\mathcal{O}(K^{2})
, which aligns with the overall scaling of its singular values. Thus, although a sample complexity of
K=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\chi^{2})
suffices to preserve the rank of
T_{\mathcal{S}}
, increasing the number of samples beyond this threshold further suppresses the impact of measurement noise.

A notable feature of our method is its transparency to depolarizing noise. Specifically, the noisy state
(1-\varepsilon)|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|+\varepsilon I/d^{2}
behaves nearly identically to the pure state
|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
under our protocol for tiny
\varepsilon
. This is because the depolarizing noise
I/d^{2}
contributes exactly one additional rank to
T_{\mathcal{S}}
. As a result, with high probability, the rank of
T_{\mathcal{S}}
increases by at most 1.
This property has both advantages and limitations. On the positive side, it confers robustness against depolarizing noise. On the negative side, the certified Schmidt number can overestimate the true value, as strong depolarizing noise can substantially reduce the Schmidt number of the original noiseless state
36
.

V
Numerical results

To demonstrate the effectiveness of our protocol, we design the following numerical experiments. Consider a bipartite quantum system consisting of 12 qubits, with the full Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{AB}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}

A
and
B
contains 6 qubits. The local dimension is thus
d=2^{6}=64
, and the total number of Pauli operators on the full system is
d^{2}-1=4095

As a first toy model, we take the target pure state to be the maximally entangled state with Schmidt rank
\chi=4

|\phi\rangle=\sum_{i=0}^{3}|ii\rangle/2,

where the Schmidt basis vectors are elements in the computational basis. The full CM associated with
|\phi\rangle
is a
4096\times 4096
matrix (
d^{2}\times d^{2}
) of rank 16. For illustration, we randomly select
32
and
64
random Pauli operators and construct the corresponding projected CM. We then compute and display its singular values.

In the rotated scenario, we apply independent random local unitaries
U_{A}
and
U_{B}
to the subsystems, producing the transformed state

|\widetilde{\phi}\rangle=(U_{A}\otimes U_{B})|\phi\rangle.

Using the same fixed set of
32
and
64
Pauli operators, we construct the projected CM for
|\widetilde{\phi}\rangle
and compute its singular values. For comparison, we show the singular values of the full CM as well. To facilitate visual comparison across matrices of different sizes, all singular values are normalized by dividing by the dimension of the respective matrix.

The numerical results, illustrated in Fig.

\chi^{2}=16

\chi\geq 2
. In contrast, by incorporating the random local unitary layer, just
K=32
measurements recover approximately 12 singular values, which is sufficient to certify that

VI
Conclusion

In conclusion, this work presents a practical, basis-independent protocol for certifying the Schmidt number of high-dimensional bipartite pure states using only random Pauli measurements and two local PRUs. By exploiting the rank-preserving nature of projected correlation matrices and random matrix theory insights, the protocol achieves sample complexity that is nearly independent of local dimension in typical cases — scaling as
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\chi^{4})
with PRU rotations — while requiring
\mathcal{O}(d\chi^{2})
in the worst case. Numerical simulations on toy states and physically relevant ground states confirm the method’s effectiveness and demonstrate the substantial efficiency gains provided by local random unitaries. These results make high-dimensional entanglement certification experimentally viable.

We are interested in extending our methods to related areas, such as stabilizer testing
15
and magic estimation
33
(see also Refs.
4
;
30
). Notably, the parameter
\mu_{0}
bears a conceptual resemblance to the stabilizer Rényi entropy
23
;
22
, a computable measure of nonstabilizerness. While
\mu_{0}
is defined over a set of vectors rather than a single state, potential deeper connections between these quantities deserve further investigation.

The single-bit Pauli operators contain three elements:

X=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\

(18)

Suppose
\mathcal{H}

N

N
-qubit Pauli group is an operator basis for all Hermitian operators on

W_{p,q}=\bigotimes_{n=1}^{N}\mathrm{i}^{p_{n}q_{n}}X_{n}^{p_{n}}Z_{n}^{q_{n}}\quad p,q\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{N},

p\cdot q:=\sum_{n=1}^{N}p_{n}q_{n},\quad(p+q)_{n}\equiv p_{n}+q_{n}(\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2)\quad\forall n.

(20)

W_{p,q}|b\rangle=\mathrm{i}^{p\cdot q}\mathrm{i}^{q\cdot b}|p+b\rangle.

The commutation relation is then described by

W_{p,q}W_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}=(-1)^{qp^{\prime}}W_{p+p^{\prime},q+q^{\prime}}=(-1)^{p\cdot q^{\prime}+p^{\prime}\cdot q}W_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime}}W_{p,q}.

(22)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=0}^{d-1}\sum_{a,b=0}^{d-1}\langle l_{i}l_{b}|\mathrm{SWAP}|l_{j}l_{a}\rangle|e_{ij}\rangle\langle e_{ab}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=0}^{d-1}\sum_{a,b=0}^{d-1}\delta_{i,a}\delta_{b,j}|e_{ij}\rangle\langle e_{ab}|=\sum_{i,j=0}^{d-1}|e_{ij}\rangle\langle e_{ij}|.

\displaystyle U_{L}^{\dagger}U_{L}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i,j=0}^{d-1}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}}\langle l_{i}|P|l_{j}\rangle^{*}\langle l_{i}|Q|l_{j}\rangle|e_{P}\rangle\langle e_{Q}|

(27)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i,j=0}^{d-1}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}}\langle l_{j}|P|l_{i}\rangle\langle l_{i}|Q|l_{j}\rangle|e_{P}\rangle\langle e_{Q}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}}\Tr(PQ)|e_{P}\rangle\langle e_{Q}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{P}}\delta_{P,Q}|e_{P}\rangle\langle e_{Q}|=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}|e_{P}\rangle\langle e_{P}|.

The proof for
U_{R}
is identical. Hence rank
(T)=
rank
(\Lambda_{\Psi})=\chi^{2}
. This completes the proof of Lemma
1
.

Appendix D
Proof of the main theorem

Recall that

\displaystyle|v_{P}\rangle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i,j=0}^{\chi-1}\langle l_{i}|P|l_{j}\rangle|e_{ij}\rangle,\quad\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}}|v_{P}\rangle\langle v_{P}|=\sum_{i,j=0}^{\chi-1}|e_{ij}\rangle\langle e_{ij}|:=\mathbb{I}_{\Psi},

(28)

\displaystyle|e_{I}\rangle:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\chi}}\sum_{i=0}^{\chi-1}|e_{ii}\rangle,\quad|\widetilde{v}_{P}\rangle:=(\mathbb{I}_{\Psi}-|e_{I}\rangle\langle e_{I}|)|v_{P}\rangle.

(29)

D.1
Proof of Theorem
2

Proof.

Introduce a random vector
X
whose distribution is the unform distribution on

\mathcal{X}:=\left\{\sqrt{d^{2}-1}|\widetilde{v}_{P}\rangle:P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}.\right\}.

(30)

Note that

\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{X}}[XX^{\dagger}]=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}|\widetilde{v}_{P}\rangle\langle\widetilde{v}_{P}|=\mathbb{I}_{\Psi}-|e_{I}\rangle\langle e_{I}|,

(31)

which means

X
is isotropic on
\mathbb{C}^{\chi^{2}-1}
.

For all
P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
, we have

(d^{2}-1)\langle\widetilde{v}_{P}|\widetilde{v}_{P}\rangle\leq d^{2}\langle v_{P}|v_{P}\rangle=d\sum_{i,j=0}^{\chi-1}|\langle l_{i}|P|l_{j}\rangle|^{2}\leq\mu_{0}.

(32)

Hence,
\|X\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\mu_{0}}
almost surely. By virtue of Corollary
2
, we can conclude that given
K=\mathcal{O}(\mu_{0}\log(\chi/\eta))
, the rank of
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
is
\chi^{2}-1
.

Each
X_{i}
has a one-to-one correspondence with a Pauli operator denoted as
P_{i}
. The proof is then completed by observing that

\left\{X_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{K}=\left\{\sqrt{d^{2}-1}|\widetilde{v}_{P_{i}}\rangle\right\}_{i=1}^{K}=\left\{\sqrt{d^{2}-1}|\widetilde{v}_{P}\rangle\right\}_{P\in\mathcal{S}},

(33)

where
\mathcal{S}=\{P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}
is a set of
K
Pauli operators draw uniformly from
\mathcal{P}_{0}
.

∎

A concrete example that matches the bound in Theorem
2
is in Appendix
E
.

Let

Proof of Lemma

The proof of the original Lemma

Lemma 4
.

Suppose
f:\mathbb{S}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is an
L
-Lipschitz function, and
v
is a Haar random vector on a
N

\Pr\left\{\left|f(v)-\mathbb{E}[f]\right|\geq\varepsilon\right\}\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{N\varepsilon^{2}}{9\mathbb{I}_{\Psi}^{3}L^{2}}\right).

(37)

Note that
f_{1}(v)=|\langle v|P|v\rangle|
is 2-Lipschitz, and
f_{2}(w)=|\langle v|P|w\rangle|
is 1-Lipschitz.

The Lemma
3
can be proved by constructing proper Lipschitz functions.

Proof.

Suppose
\chi=2
. Then

\frac{\mu_{U}}{d}=\max_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\left(|\langle v_{1}|P|v_{1}\rangle|^{2}+2|\langle v_{1}|P|v_{2}\rangle|^{2}+|\langle v_{2}|P|v_{2}\rangle|^{2}\right),

(38)

where
|v_{1}\rangle,|v_{2}\rangle
are the first two column vectors of a Haar random unitary. Sample two random states
|v_{1}\rangle,|v_{2}\rangle
that are orthogonal to each other is equivalent to sample
v_{1}
form
\mathcal{H}_{d}
, then sample
v_{2}
from
\mathcal{H}_{d-1}
. First, we randomly sample
v_{1}\in\mathcal{H}_{d}
. By virtue of Lemma

\max_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}|\langle v_{1}|P|v_{1}\rangle|^{2}\leq\frac{\mathrm{poly}\log(d/\eta)}{d}.

(39)

Then randomly sample a vector

from the complement space of

, which is a Hilbert space of dimension

. In conjunction with Lemma

, with probability
1-\eta
, we also have

\displaystyle\max_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}|\langle v_{2}|P|v_{2}\rangle|^{2}\leq\frac{\mathrm{poly}\log((d-1)/\eta)}{d-1},

(41)

respectively. Altogether, we have with probability at least

Appendix E
Computational basis scenario

In this appendix, we prove that the sample complexity for the states whose Schmidt basis vectors are computational is
\mathcal{O}(d\chi)
, whic matches with Theorem
2
in the case where
\mu_{0}=\mathcal{O}(d\chi)
.

Recall that

|v_{P}\rangle=\sum_{i,j=0}^{\chi-1}\langle l_{i}|P|l_{j}\rangle|e_{ij}\rangle,\quad|\widetilde{v}_{P}\rangle=\left(\mathbb{I}_{\Psi}-|e_{I}\rangle\langle e_{I}|\right)|v_{P}\rangle,\quad\mathbb{I}_{\Psi}:=\sum_{i,j=0}^{\chi-1}|e_{ij}\rangle\langle e_{ij}|,

(45)

where
\{|l_{i}\rangle,|l_{j}\rangle\}
are elements in the computational basis now. Let
\mathcal{A}:=\{l_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\chi-1}
and use
a,b,\ldots
to denote elements in
\mathcal{A}
henceforth. Introduce

\overline{\mathcal{A}+x}:=\{a+x:a\in\mathcal{A}\},\quad\overline{\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}}:=\{a+b:a,b\in\mathcal{A}\}.

(46)

We also simplify
\mathbb{I}_{\Psi}
as

\mathbb{I}_{\Psi}=\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{A}}|e_{ab}\rangle\langle e_{ab}|.

(47)

If
P=W_{x,z}
, then we can rewrite
|v_{P}\rangle
as

\displaystyle|v_{x,z}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{A}}\langle a|W_{x,z}|b\rangle|e_{ab}\rangle=\mathrm{i}^{x\cdot z}\sum_{a,b\in\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{i}^{z\cdot b}\delta_{a,b+x}|e_{ab}\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{x\in\overline{\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}}}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}\cap\overline{\mathcal{A}+x}}\mathrm{i}^{z\cdot(a+x)}|e_{a,a+x}\rangle.

(48)

Observe that
\langle v_{x,z}|v_{x^{\prime},z^{\prime}}\rangle\neq 0
only if
x=x^{\prime}
. Let
\mathcal{S}_{x}:=\{W_{x,z}:W_{x,z}\in\mathcal{S}\}
. Then we have

\mathrm{rank}\left(\{|v_{x,z}\rangle:W_{x,z}\in\mathcal{S}\}\right)=\sum_{x}\mathrm{rank}\left(\{|v_{x,z}\rangle:W_{x,z}\in\mathcal{S}_{x}\}\right).

(49)

Note that

|e_{I}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\chi}}\sum_{i=0}^{\chi-1}|ii\rangle\propto|v_{0,0}\rangle.

(50)

Hence, our target can be decomposed into

\displaystyle\mathrm{rank}\left(\{|\widetilde{v}_{x,z}\rangle:W_{x,z}\in\mathcal{S}\}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\overline{\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}}/\{0\}}\mathrm{rank}\left(\{|v_{x,z}\rangle:W_{x,z}\in\mathcal{S}_{x}\}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{rank}\left(\{(\mathbb{I}_{\Psi}-|e_{I}\rangle\langle e_{I}|)|v_{0,z}\rangle:W_{0,z}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}\}\right).

(51)

Now, we will look into how many Pauli samples are needed to have

\mathrm{rank}\left(\{|\widetilde{v}_{x,z}\rangle:W_{x,z}\in\mathcal{S}\}\right)=\chi^{2}-1.

(52)

Choose one
x\in\overline{\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}}
. Denote the matrix whose column vectors are
\{|v_{x,z}\rangle\}_{z=0}^{d-1}
as
R^{(x)}
. Hence, the non-zero part of
R^{(x)}
writes

R^{(x)}_{a,z}:=\mathrm{i}^{z\cdot(a+2x)}\quad a\in\mathcal{A}\cap\overline{\mathcal{A}+x},\ z\in\{0,\ldots,d-1\}.

(53)

The rank of
R
is exactly
|\mathcal{A}\cap\overline{\mathcal{A}+x}|
because the row vectors are linearly independent:

\sum_{z=0}^{d-1}(-1)^{z\cdot(a_{1}+2x-a_{2}-2x)}=d\delta_{a_{1},a_{2}}.

(54)

The conclusion is equivalent to

\sum_{z=0}^{d-1}|v_{x,z}\rangle\langle v_{x,z}|=d\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}\cap\overline{\mathcal{A}+x}}|e_{a,a+x}\rangle\langle e_{a,a+x}|.

(55)

By virtue of Corollary
2
, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 5
.

Suppose
x\in\overline{\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}}
, and
\mathcal{Z}
is
N
random bit strings sampled uniformly from
\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
. If
N=\mathcal{O}(\chi\log(\chi/\eta))
, then with probability at least
1-\eta
, we have

\mathrm{rank}\left(\{|v_{x,z}\rangle\}_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\right)=|\mathcal{A}\cap\overline{\mathcal{A}+x}|.

(56)

Proof.

Let
z\sim\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
be a random variable. Therefore, the random vector
|v_{x,z}\rangle
is isotropic on a Hilbert space of dimension
|\mathcal{A}\cap\overline{\mathcal{A}+x}|
:

\mathbb{E}_{z\sim{0,1,\ldots,d-1}}[|v_{x,z}\rangle\langle v_{x,z}|]=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{z=0}^{d-1}|v_{x,z}\rangle\langle v_{x,z}|=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}\cap\overline{\mathcal{A}+x}}|e_{a,a+x}\rangle\langle e_{a,a+x}|.

(57)

The norm of
|v_{x,z}\rangle
is bounded by

\||v_{x,z}\rangle\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}\cap\overline{\mathcal{A}+x}}1=|\mathcal{A}\cap\overline{\mathcal{A}+x}|.

(58)

Note that
|\mathcal{A}\cap\overline{\mathcal{A}+x}|\leq|\mathcal{A}|=\chi
. Hence, the lemma is proved by setting
N=\mathcal{O}(\chi\log(\chi/\eta))
.
∎

A similar result holds for
\{(\mathbb{I}_{\Psi}-|e_{I}\rangle\langle e_{I}|)|v_{0,z}\rangle\}_{z=0}^{d-1}
, except the full rank is now
|\mathcal{A}|-1=\chi-1
. We will skip the details of the proof.

The random Pauli sampling is equivalent to first sampling
(x,z)\sim\mathbb{F}_{2}^{N}\times\mathbb{F}_{2}^{N}
, then letting
P=W_{x,z}
. Hence, the probability of having total rank
\chi^{2}-1
is bounded by that of

\forall x\in\overline{\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}},\quad|\mathcal{S}_{x}|=\Omega(\chi\log(\chi/\eta)).

(59)

Certainly, we need
\Omega(d\chi\log(\chi/\eta))
number of samples to achieve this with high probability.
