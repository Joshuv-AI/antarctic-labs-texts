Diagnostic accuracy of stool based Xpert MTB/RIF ultra assay for diagnosis of pulmonary tuberculosis in children, Northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Habtamu Belew, Adane Tilahun, Abebe Fenta, Samrawit Tefera, Adane Adugna, Desalegn Abebaw, Mekuriaw Belayneh, Zigale Hibstu Teffera, Gashaw Azanaw Amare, Bantayehu Addis Tegegne, Mamaru Getinet, Mulualem Biazen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Diagnosing childhood pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) presents unique challenges, including paucibacillary disease, difficulties in specimen collection, and limited diagnostics. Hence, millions of children with tuberculosis (TB) infection remain underdiagnosed. Therefore, our research team evaluated diagnostic accuracy of stool-based Xpert ultra for detecting PTB in children. Five ml respiratory and 3 g stool specimens were analyzed. Strict adherence to laboratory standard operating procedures were warranted. Diagnostic performance of stool Xpert Ultra was compared against Xpert ultra and culture on respiratory samples, and composite reference standards. Of 557 children recruited; 510 had complete microbiological results. PTB was diagnosed in 52/510 (10.2%) children with presumptive TB. Four had microbiologically unconfirmed PTB, who were clinically diagnosed with positive response to anti-TB, remaining 48 were confirmed microbiologically. Stool specimen Xpert ultra had sensitivity 95.8%(95%CI: 82.8–98.6) and specificity of 99.8% (95%CI: 98.7–100) compared to culture; yet, sensitivity of stool was 88.5% (95%CI: 72-95.6) and specificity 100% (95%CI: 99.2–100) when compared to CRS. Xpert ultra on respiratory specimen had sensitivity and specificity 97.9% (95%CI: 85.8– 99.5) and 99.8% (95%CI: 98.7–100) compared to culture and 92.3%(95%CI: 81.4–97.9) and 100% (95%CI: 99.2–100) compared to CRS. Thus, stool samples present promising alternative for diagnosing PTB in children when respiratory specimens cannot be obtained.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22111-1.

Introduction

Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) is a respiratory pathogen that infects about 25% of the global population and causes the disease TB. This transmissible disease has been responsible for more deaths throughout human history than other microorganisms1. In recent years, approximately 10 million people have been diagnosed with TB annually; with nearly 1/3rd remaining undiagnosed or unreported. In 2020, as reported cases dropped from 7.1 million to 5.8 million, leaving an estimated 4.1 million cases unreported2,3. In children, the case detection gap is more significant than other age groups, as 69% of missed cases expected to be among < 5 years’ old4,5. In 2023, 1.3 million TB deaths, of these, 214,000 children were died from TB (almost 600 each day) in under 15 years6 and above 80% of the deaths were (< 5 years)3. In Ethiopia, there is a decreasement of TB, however, still present in high TB burden country with an estimated 212, 220 new TB patients and 29,874 TB-related deaths and all forms of TB, 4% were children compared to the estimated 11% globally7–9. TB remains a significant challenge in diagnosing and treating infections with childhood TB1. The main obstacles in diagnosing TB in children are the unspecific and paucibacillary nature of the illness presentation and the trouble of obtaining respiratory samples (sputum, induced sputum, gastric aspirate, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), nasophargeal specimen10,11. Early and appropriate TB diagnosis in pediatrics is a key pillar of the World Health Organization’s (WHO) End TB Strategy milestone12, where specific emphasis is placed on the discovery, development, and rapid uptake of new diagnostic tools13. Effective strategies for their implementation and scaling up of non-invasive sample collection method for MTB diagnosis in children is a critical point14.

Gastric aspiration and/or sputum induction, an alternative method of acquiring a sample, is invasive procedure and has a low yield15 and these technique for microbiological diagnosis of TB are uncomfortable, stressful, and painful, and cannot be conducted at the lowest levels of the healthcare system, which restricting children’s access to PTB diagnosis4,16. In many resource-limited settings, including Ethiopia, a pediatric TB score chart is used to aid in the diagnosis of TB despite its widely varying sensitivity and specificity, especially in children. Due to these reason some studies have documented stool as a possible specimen for detecting TB using GeneXpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay (Xpert Ultra assay) (Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) among childhood PTB15,17,18. Stool-based tests for TB diagnosis, in Xpert ultra assay, offer several advantages in children, including ease of collection, non-invasiveness, and the ability to obtain multiple samples over time and have a wide variability of test performance19. As a result, this validation and optimization study for stool Xpert ultra diagnostics of PTB in children with high burden TB sites in the region is critical. The aim of this study was to assess diagnostic accuracy of stool based Xpert Ultra assay for diagnosis of presumptive TB among Children in Hospitals, Northwest, Ethiopia.

Results

A total of 557 children with presumptive PTB were recruited to the study. Out of these, 47 were excluded in the final analysis: due to 29 were not given to stool specimens and, 8 were diagnosed with extra PTB and 10 were unable to give expectorated sputum and refused to give gastric aspirate. We included the remaining 510 children for whom we analyzed 86 expectorated sputa, 423 gastric aspirate, 1 BAL and 510 stool specimens to perform all the required tests (Fig. 1) and for the SRAD checklist of this study was presented in (Suppl Fig. 1). Two hundred eighty-four (284/510; 55.7%) of 510 children were males and the majority 205/510 (41.7%) of the age of the children were between 5 and 9 years old. Three hundred thirty (330/510; 64.7%) of the children were rural dwellers (Table 1).

Furthermore, 41/510 children (8%) had a history of TB contact within their families. The majority of the children, 341/510 (66.9%), were vaccinated with BCG. Sixteen of the children (16/510, 3%) were severely malnourished and 397/510 (77.8%) of children were non suggestive for X-ray investigation and were described in the supplementary information in (Table S1). From those specimens, we were found a positive result in sputum 4, and GA, 44 among 510 and 46 from stool specimen from 479. During the study period, the majority of LJ growths have been moderate bacillary load (100–1000 CFUs/ml). Diagnostic performance of TB in the children

Finally, specimens from the remaining 510 children were used to perform the entire required tests in mycobacteriology laboratory. M. Tuberculosis was detected in 48/510 (9.4%) by solid LJ culture on respiratory specimens and 47/510 (9.2%) on Xpert Ultra assay were confirmed. In one of the children’s gastric aspirate LJ culture was positive while the Xpert Ultra assay from the same specimen was negative. Four among the 86 expectorated sputum specimens were positive by both Xpert Ultra assay and LJ culture. Stool Xpert ultra testing revealed that 46/479 (9.6%) MTB positive cases were detected. Out of the stool specimen examined by diagnostic methods, 2 were LJ culture positive on respiratory samples and one Xpert ultra assay positive whereas stool Xpert ultra and LJ culture negative. The results of respiratory sample Xpert ultra assay and stool Xpert ultra assay as compared to respiratory sample LJ culture amongst study children were explained (Table 3). We actively interviewed children and reviewed the medical records of the remaining 462/510 (90.6%) of children who were microbiologically negative. Of these, 4/462 (0.9%) were clinically diagnosed and/or unconfirmed as TB by the clinicians. Among the 4 clinically diagnosed TB cases, 4/4 (100%) showed clinical improvement after ATT. Out of the 4 cases who have improved clinically, all of them had radiological findings suggestive for PTB and hereafter were classified as “clinical diagnosis or unconfirmed PTB’’. In the study, none of the children with clinically diagnosed TB had a positive stool Xpert result. A bacteriological diagnosis was confirmed by either the Xpert Ultra assay or LJ culture, indicated the presence of TB. In contrast, 458/462 (99.1%) were ruled out as PTB negative and received alternative diagnoses. Treatment was then administered based on these identified conditions, and these children were subsequently classified as “unlikely TB.” (Fig. 1).

Error or invalid results were recorded in 31 out of 510 (6.1%) Xpert Ultra assays performed on stool samples, and these cases were subsequently excluded from the final analysis. Due to different factors the tests didn’t repeat in these invalid and error cases in stool specimen (a shortage of Xpert ultra cartridges and reagents), but these were all negatives in respiratory specimen. Of the 479 stool specimens with valid Xpert ultra results, 46/479 (9.6%, 95%CI; 7.5–13.4) were MTB positive. Stool Xpert ultra was detected 45 respiratory LJ culture confirmed TB cases and two positive cases missed by stool Xpert ultra assay. Furthermore, respiratory Xpert ultra assay also detected one MTB positive cases which was found to be negative on stool Xpert ultra assay (Table 3).

Diagnostic accuracy of PTB using respiratory and stool specimens

Of the 48 children with bacteriologically confirmed PTB, Xpert ultra on respiratory specimen had a diagnostic positivity of 47/510 (9.2%, 95% CI; 7.02–12.28), whereas Xpert ultra on stool identified PTB in 46 (9.6%) children. Compared to Xpert ultra and culture on respiratory specimen demonstrated a higher diagnostic outcome by detecting 48 children among 510 presumptive TB cases. Of the 479 children in the total presumptive TB suspected Xpert ultra assay on stool specimens identified PTB in 46 which was comparative with the other diagnostic procedures. Using culture of the respiratory specimen (gastric aspirate and expectorated samples) as the reference standard, stool Xpert ultra had sensitivity of 95.8% (95% CI: 85.8–99.5) and positive predicative value (PPV) 97.8% (95%CI;86.4–99.7), whereas respiratory sample Xpert ultra assay had sensitivity of 97.9% (95% CI: 88.8–99.9) with PPV of 97.9%(95% CI;86.6–99.7) (Table 3).

Discussion

Diagnosing PTB in children poses significant challenges, particularly in high risk demographics with increased exposure to TB20. Ethiopia faces major contests in controlling TB and TB/HIV co-infection, with many undiagnosed cases hindering progress toward ending TB10,11,20,21. Different studies and national guidelines recommend Xpert ultra assay for childhood TB diagnosis in Stool specimen22–24. Recently, stool specimens have been proposed as a viable initial sample for diagnosing PTB using the Xpert ultra assay23,25. The effectiveness of stool examination for diagnosing childhood PTB has been evaluated in several studies23; however, it has not yet been recognized as a conventional testing methods in Ethiopia. As a result, in the current study, we demonstrated that the stool Xpert Ultra assay yields consistent results compared to the respiratory Xpert Ultra assay and culture in children with presumptive PTB. Other studies have also reported similarly promising diagnostic accuracy outcomes for stool specimens25–27.

We found that the stool Xpert Ultra assay demonstrated a sensitivity of 95.8% and a specificity of 99.7% for detecting MTB in children with microbiologically confirmed cases from respiratory specimens, according to the LJ culture standard. Additionally, the assay showed a sensitivity of 88.5% and a specificity of 100% for children diagnosed with TB, either microbiologically or clinically, compared to the CRS. Similar Studies conducted Ethiopia27, Egypt28, Kenya29, and Pakistan30 reported with high sensitivities and specificities for stool Xpert ultra, ranging from (83.3% −100%) when compared to respiratory LJ culture. The specificity of the stool Xpert ultra assay in our study is consistent with findings from previous studies conducted in Ethiopia27, South Africa31, and Egypt32, where reported specificities ranged from (99.3%- 99.7%). Additionally, the specificity when compared to CRS was consistent with a study from Pakistan26.

In comparison to other specimen types, stool specimens have not been thoroughly evaluated for their effectiveness and optimization in conventional testing with molecular diagnostics techniques. In this study, we assessed the performance of stool-based Xpert ultra for diagnosing TB in children within high TB burden settings in Ethiopia. However, the sensitivity (93.6%) and specificity (99.5%) of stool Xpert ultra assay similar as compared to that of respiratory sample Xpert ultra with sensitivity (95.8%), and specificity (99.8%). As a result, this finding provides guarantee that stool-based testing continues to offer significant benefits, particularly in cases where obtaining respiratory tract samples is impractical. This finding was similar to other studies conducted27,29,33. Hence, the comparable diagnostic effectiveness suggests that both stool and respiratory specimen Xpert ultra assays can be relied upon, enabling healthcare providers to accurately diagnose and treat TB based on the results from stool specimens.

Notably, one culture-positive respiratory specimen and two stool specimens tested but, negative for both the respiratory and stool Xpert ultra assays in this study. This discrepancy may be attributed to the presence of low bacilli and the enhanced ability of LJ culture methods to detect MTB, as LJ culture is considered the gold standard for MTB diagnosis34. In this study, only one stool Xpert ultra result was negative when compared to all respiratory Xpert ultra positive cases, indicating that stool could serve as a viable potential alternative specimen as respiratory specimen for routine PTB diagnosis in every health facility. Whereas gastric aspiration, sputum, and BAL can be employed to gather pulmonary specimens from patients who cannot expectorate sputum, these methods are invasive, painful and necessitate trained healthcare professionals to obtain specimens. However, stool is a non-invasive and stool Xpert ultra employs a streamlined protocol with valid results35.

In the current study, regrettably, both stool and respiratory Xpert ultra did not detect any of the four clinically diagnosed PTB cases. Additional consistent studies have also reported similar poor diagnostic performance of stool Xpert ultra in children with clinically diagnosed (microbiologically negative) TB15,27. This may highlight a limitation of both stool and respiratory Xpert ultra, as patients with clinically diagnosed but unconfirmed TB is more prone to having paucibacillary disease nature, but given positive response for ATT, specimen collection, laboratory diagnostic procedures, long preservation and transportation time and a very low mycobacterial load4. Children with a strong clinical suspicion of TB, even when stool and respiratory Xpert ultra results are negative, should begin ATT with clinical decisions until more effective and sensitive diagnostic tools become available.

In this study, older children are able to expectorate sputum. Consequently, from this sputum 4 (4.7%) of the expectorated sputum samples were positive for M. tuberculosis during the study which contradict from other studies conducted in Ethiopia27. This is different from other studies conducted in the same population group, in this study period was given counseling and instruction to children and their parents to expectorate quality sputum and we did also asses the quality of the sample in the current study. The current study has some limitations. We frequently faced challenges related to the storage of specimens due to a shortage of Xpert ultra cartridges. Additionally, we collected only a single respiratory and stool specimen for testing for this study instead of successive specimens and underdiagnosis could be a factor contributing to the low number of unconfirmed cases this related to study design, particularly laboratory based context. The availability and cost of the Xpert Ultra test and LJ culture and human budget constraint.

Conclusions

This study highlights that stool specimens exhibit similar sensitivity and specificity to respiratory specimens when analysed using Xpert Ultra assays for diagnosing PTB in children. Given its practical advantages, stool specimens offer a promising alternative for diagnosing PTB, especially in children in high TB burden settings. The study emphasizes that stool collection is relatively safe and can be conducted in various settings, including primary health care facilities, where it has shown high diagnostic accuracy compared to respiratory specimens. With strong positive and negative predictive values, stool testing can enhance diagnostic capacity and reduce misdiagnosis of PTB, especially where access to respiratory specimens are limited. We recommend that the Ministry of Health of Ethiopia consider stool as a viable specimen for detecting TB in children and provide guidelines and training for lower health care systems, particularly when respiratory specimen collection is impractical.

Methods

Study settings and period

Ethiopia is a low income country in Africa with 128,513,977 inhabitants https://www.worldometers.info/world-population/ethiopia accessed in February 16, 2024). This study was conducted in Amhara National regional state from four hospitals (Felege Hiwot comprehensive specialized Hospital, Bahir Dar); (Debre Markos comprehensive specialized hospital (DMCSH), Finote selam General Hospital, (FSGH), and Injibara General Hospital). These Hospitals was selected according to the Xpert ultra availability and the flow of high TB load patients in the hospitals and these areas are categorized TB hot-spot area in the region36. The study was conducted from March, 2024 to April, 08/2025.

Study design

An institutional based cross- sectional and diagnostic accuracy study was conducted.

Study population

All children aged 15 years or younger visiting inpatient and outpatient services during the study period with presumptive TB were included if they exhibited a persistent cough of ≥ 2 weeks, fever, weight loss, chest pain, history of TB contact, night sweats, loss of appetite, decreased activity, hemoptysis, previous TB history, pneumonia, living with HIV and malnutrition37,38. Screening for TB was conducted in accordance with standard care guidelines, based on clinical presentations and one or a combination of symptoms. Children who were on ATT, for > 72 h before enrollment, critically ill children who didn’t give respiratory and stool specimens and whose guardians weren’t willing to participate in the study were excluded. Operational definitions regarding the study design were presented in (supplementary file 1).

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using the Buderer’s formula for sensitivity and specificity39. The study conducted by Dubale et al.in similar settings reported the sensitivity of 50% and specificity of 99.3% in the diagnostic accuracy of stool Xpert ultra and a prevalence of 13.2% was used based on a hospital at southern Ethiopia for novel diagnostics for children27. The calculated sample size with an absolute precision of 12% at a 95% confidence level and the required sample size was 506. By adding a 10% non-respondent rate, the final sample size was adjusted to 557. Participants who met the eligibility criteria were enrolled consecutively until the target sample size was reached.

Data collection

On the day of recruitment, trained physicians and pediatrics nurses collected data from children at selected hospitals in northwest Ethiopia using semi-structured questionnaires on sociodemographic and clinical variables. Data collection involved face-to-face interviews with guardians, caregivers, or parents, who provided informed consent and assent for the study. They were also asked to provide respiratory and stool samples for the evaluation of PTB.

Children were included in the study regardless of their ability to respiratory and stool specimen. For individuals unable to produce sputum, gastric aspiration was performed as an alternative method to obtain the required respiratory specimens.

Specimen collection, processing and transport

In this study, laboratory professionals, pediatricians, and trained nurses collected gastric aspirates and induced sputum, which were considered respiratory samples. The details of the sample processing are provided in (Supplementary File 2). A minimum of 5 ml of either induced sputum or gastric aspirate was collected for culture and Xpert Ultra assay. One stool sample (a minimum of 3 g) was collected from children who were satisfying the inclusion criteria by laboratory professional, nurses and pediatrician. The samples were collected early morning gastric aspirates using nasogastric tubes from children who were unable to produce sputum. Specimens were collected in wide-mouthed and screw-capped containers and stored at the health facility. Each respiratory specimen was assessed for its mucoid appearance, presence of epithelial cells (< 10 per low power field) and sufficient amount of volume to ensure the quality of the specimens before the analysis started. The radiological investigation (chest X-ray) was done for all the recruited children. The immunization status was recorded, and all participants were interviewed. Samples were transported into the laboratory and processed immediately under the biosafety guidelines22. In case of delay, samples were processed within 7 days of collection; in case the samples were not processed on the same day of collection, they were stored at 2–8 °C. Laboratory personnel who preform laboratory analysis were blinded to the patients’ clinical status.

Laboratory Xpert assay

The respiratory sample was subjected to Xpert ultra according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Cepheid, Sunnyvale, California, USA) described in the package insert by trained laboratory professionals with standard operating procedures (SOPs). Xpert ultra assay was performed at hospitals laboratory for both respiratory and stool samples as part of routine practice. Briefly, clinical samples were added to sample reagents using a ratio of 2:1 with sample reagent to specimen. Approximately, 4 ml of a respiratory sample (gastric aspirate or induced sputum/sputum) were taken to which twice the volumes, that is, 8 ml of sample reagent were added. The mixture was mixed, vortexed, incubated, and processed according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Stool samples were subjected to stool processing before decontaminating the sample with the NALC–NaOH method21. This mixture was then incubated for 15 min at room temperature to allow the particulate matter to settle. The supernatants from each processed stool sample was collected in another container and mixed with sample reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions (2:1). The mixture from each specimen was vortexed and incubates for a further 15 min at room temperature; 2 ml were transferred to a Xpert cartridge and analyzed using Xpert ultra. Stool Xpert ultra results were interpreted the same way as respiratory specimen Xpert ultra results. Briefly, 1 g to 2 g stool sample were mixed with 3 to 4 ml of phosphate buffer saline and mixed thoroughly with vortexed until homogenized and filtered through a single layer of gauze. The filtrate was decontaminated with the NALC–NaOH method40 (Supplementary file 2).

TB culturing process

Before culturing, the respiratory sample was under go with a deep decontamination and concentration procedure using N-Acetyl-L-cysteine–sodium hydroxide (NALC–NaOH) method before further processing. Stool samples were subjected to stool processing before decontaminating the sample with the NALC–NaOH method (Supplementary file 2).

In this study, culture was used as the gold standard method for diagnosing TB41. Respiratory and stool samples for TB culture were transported to and processed in a reference laboratory, where they were cultivated using LJ media slants, following SOPs in a contained Biosafety Level 3 laboratory. Before culture, the samples were subjected to a harsh decontamination procedure (using 4% sodium hydroxide) that liquefied the organic debris and eliminated the unwanted normal flora. The respiratory samples were inoculated by using LJ solid culture and liquid culture, incubated. LJ, two drops of processed and resuspended pellet was inoculated onto two LJ slopes and incubated at 35–37oC for a maximum of 56 days. The stool LJ culture was performed with the same procedure with (a deep homogenization to remove unwanted debris) as that of respiratory specimen in this study (supplementary file2). The result of LJ culture were identified based on colonial morphology MTB (texture, color, shape and size), microscopic examination and biochemical test (Niacin test in this study was performed). Stool Xpert and LJ culture results were compared with both LJ culture alone and then LJ culture and/or Xpert. Children were classified into three categories based on their clinical, radiological, and microbiological results that were confirmed, unconfirmed and unlikely TB. In the study, the mycobacterial load in LJ culture was categorized into three levels: low (< 100 CFUs/ml), moderate (100–1000 CFUs/ml), and high (> 1000 CFUs/ml). A culture was considered contaminated following observation of overgrowth of microorganisms that were lacking characteristics of mycobacteria.

Data analysis and presentation

Data were coded and entered in the Statistical Package of EpiData v4.2. The data were exported to SPSS V25 for further analysis. Baseline demographic characteristics were analysed. Sensitivity, specificity and predictive values for the stool Xpert were calculated using respiratory samples Xpert assay and culture as gold standard. 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) was determined using one of the following reference standards: (i) LJ culture alone; (ii) LJ culture and/or Xpert (proven tuberculosis); and (iii) CRS (both confirmed and unconfirmed TB). To estimate the tests’ sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values, MedCal statistical software was used (https://www.medcalc.org/calc/diagnostic_test.php). The 95% CI was used to identify the differences and similarities between the two procedures (stool Xpert and respiratory sample Xpert). Non-overlapping 95% CIs showed a difference between the two procedures (stool Xpert vs. respiratory sample Xpert vs. LJ culture), and vice versa.
