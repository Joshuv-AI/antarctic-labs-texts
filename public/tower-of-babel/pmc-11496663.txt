Exploring alterations of gut/blood microbes in addressing iron overload-induced gut dysbiosis and cognitive impairment in thalassemia patients
Authors: Kanokphong Suparan, Kornkanok Trirattanapa, Pokpong Piriyakhuntorn, Sirawit Sriwichaiin, Chanisa Thonusin, Wichwara Nawara, Sasiwan Kerdpoo, Nipon Chattipakorn, Adisak Tantiworawit, Siriporn C Chattipakorn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIron overload causes cognitive impairment in thalassemia patients. The gut-brain axis plays an important role in cognitive function. However, the association between gut/blood microbiome, cognition, and iron burden in thalassemia patients has not been thoroughly investigated. We aimed to determine those associations in thalassemia patients with different blood-transfusion regimens. Sixty participants: healthy controls, transfusion-dependent thalassemia (TDT) patients, and non-transfusion-dependent (NTDT) patients, were recruited to evaluate iron overload, cognition, and gut/blood microbiome. TDT patients exhibited greater iron overload than NTDT patients. Most thalassemia patients developed gut dysbiosis, and approximately 25% of the patients developed minor cognitive impairment. Increased Fusobacteriota and Verrucomicrobiota with decreased Fibrobacterota were observed in both TDT and NTDT groups. TDT patients showed more abundant beneficial bacteria: Verrucomicrobia. Iron overload was correlated with cognitive impairment. Increased Butyricimonas and decreased Paraclostridium were associated with higher cognitive function. No trace of blood microbiota was observed. Differences in blood bacterial profiles of thalassemia patients and controls were insignificant. These findings suggest iron overload plays a role in the imbalance of gut microbiota and impaired cognitive function in thalassemia patients. Introduction

Thalassemia is a hereditary hemoglobinopathy characterized by impaired α-globin in alpha-thalassemia and impaired β-globin in beta-thalassemia, leading to dysfunctional red blood cells (RBC) and severe anemia1,2. Over the decades, although the implementation of iron chelators has prevented life-threatening cardiomyopathy due to iron overload3, the longer life expectancy of patients has extended the spectrum of iron overload complications, one of which is cognitive impairment4. Our previous study along with existing literature has shown that thalassemia patients developed cognitive impairment due to iron overload5–7. Although long-term blood transfusion has been recognized as a cause of cognitive impairment8, the association between iron overload in thalassemia patients with different blood-transfusion regimens, including transfusion-dependent thalassemia (TDT) and non-TDT (NTDT), and cognitive impairment is still unclear.

Not only did rats with iron overload experience cognitive impairment9, but beta-thalassemia mice also exhibited changes in gut microbiota or “gut dysbiosis”10, raising the possibility of an association between iron overload and gut dysbiosis in the pathogenesis of cognitive impairment. In human, evidence regarding the role of gut dysbiosis in cognitive decline among thalassemia patients is still unclear. One of the potential mechanisms underlying the gut-brain axis is the disruption of the gut and blood brain barriers following systemic oxidative stress caused by iron overload9,11. Following this disruption, the circulation transports both beneficial and detrimental gut microbial components and their metabolites from the gut to the brain10. Additionally, Tan et al. (2023) confirmed the existence of blood bacteria in healthy individuals as well as a rise in blood bacteriome – a community of blood bacteria – were observed in different pathological conditions, but the characteristics of blood bacterial profiles in thalassemia patients are still unknown12,13.

Altogether, the associations between the gut and blood microbiome, cognition, and iron overload in thalassemia patients with varying blood-transfusion levels have not been thoroughly investigated. Additionally, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), beneficial gut-microbiota derived metabolites, were found as an essential protective factors for cognitive impairment14. However, evidence supporting whether SCFAs alter due to gut dysbiosis in thalassemia remains unexplored. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the interrelationships between iron overload, changes in the gut/blood microbiome, potential alterations in their SCFAs, and cognitive impairment in TDT and NTDT patients.

Results

Thalassemia patients developed iron overload and gut dysbiosis

This unmatched case-control study comprised three groups: (1) 20 healthy controls, (2) 20 NTDT patients, and (3) 20 TDT patients as summarized in Fig. 1. Demographic characteristics showed that sex and age did not differ among all groups, but the control and NTDT groups had higher education levels compared to the TDT patients (Table 1). Within the thalassemia patient cohort, the TDT group consisted predominantly of individuals with beta-thalassemia/HbE, followed by homozygous beta-thalassemia (Table 1). TDT patients required treatment with iron chelators, particularly combined regimens, in contrast to the NTDT patients (Table 1). Other demographic and clinical characteristics, such as the splenectomy status, iron overload, miscellaneous medications, and disease complications, did not differ significantly between the two groups (Table 1). Compared to healthy controls, thalassemia patients exhibited dysregulated RBC homeostasis, as evidenced by decreased hemoglobin levels, altered RBC morphology, and elevated levels of indirect bilirubin (Table 2). Additionally, differences were observed in white blood cell (WBC) counts and components, creatinine levels, liver-produced proteins, and hepatic enzymes (Table 2). However, no significant differences were found in fecal and plasma levels of all SCFAs between groups (Table 2). The significantly different characteristics were incorporated as covariates in Analysis of the Composition of Microbiomes with Bias Correction (ANCOM-BC).

Iron overload was observed in thalassemia patients compared to healthy controls, as indicated by serum ferritin levels (Fig. 2A). The TDT group exhibited significantly greater severity of iron overload than the NTDT group (Fig. 2B). Firmicutes, Bacteroidota, Actinobacteria, and Proteobacteria were the dominant gut microbial phyla across all groups. The highest corresponding taxa were represented by Bifidobacterium, followed by, Blautia, Collinsella, Faecalibacterium, Bacteroides, and Prevotella (Fig. 2C). Alpha-diversity and beta-diversity indices indicated that thalassemia patients exhibited gut dysbiosis (Fig. 2D-G). Alpha-diversity indices tended to differ between thalassemia patients and healthy controls (Fig. 2D). Notably, when categorizing thalassemia into NTDT and TDT, alpha-diversity indices indicated that the control, NTDT, and TDT groups had their own distinctive gut microbial compositions: decreased Observed Features and Shannon in the NTDT group compared to the control group, increased Observed Features and Shannon in the TDT group compared to the NTDT group, and increased Evenness in the TDT group compared to the control and NTDT groups (Fig. 2E). In terms of beta-diversity, gut microbiota of thalassemia patients and healthy controls as well as NTDT and TDT groups showed significant deviations from each other (Fig. 2F-G). ANCOM-BC showed an increase in Verrucomicrobiota and Fusobacteriota, along with a decrease in Fibrobacterota and Spirochaetota, in thalassemia patients compared to healthy controls (Fig. 2H and Supplementary Table 1). An excessive increase in Verrucomicrobiota and a reduction in Euryarchaeota were observed in the TDT group compared to the NTDT group (Fig. 2I and Supplementary Table 1). Collectively, TDT patients were afflicted with greater iron overload than NTDT patients, and the gut dysbiosis of these two groups were also distinct from each other. This suggests that the severity of iron overload, which depends on the regularity status of therapeutic blood transfusion, influences gut dysbiosis.

Levels of iron overload were correlated with cognitive decline in thalassemia patients

Cognitive assessment was performed using Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and Mental State Examination T10 (MSET10). Nine thalassemia patients (five with NTDT and four with TDT) exhibited minor cognitive impairment (MCI) according to the MoCA criteria for the Thai population, while only one NTDT patient exhibited MCI according to the MSET10 criteria. Thalassemia patients had significantly lower scores on both tests than healthy controls (Fig. 3A and B). A significant decline in scores from both tests was found in the TDT group, while the NTDT group only showed a significant decrease in the MoCA scores compared to the controls (Fig. 3C and D). Although cognitive scores of both tests tended to decline more in the TDT group than the NTDT group, no significant differences in cognitive scores were observed between the two groups (Fig. 3C and D). Cognitive scores were negatively correlated with levels of serum ferritin (Fig. 3E and F), indicating that iron overload may play a role in the development of cognitive decline in thalassemia patients. After excluding healthy controls, the spearman correlations between serum ferritin and cognitive tests were as follows: R = -0.1601 with p = 0.3237 for the MoCA test, and R = -0.3496 with p = 0.0270 for the MSET10 test. The statistical significance was lost in the case of the MoCA scores; however, the significance was still in the case of MSET 10 score. Next, the differences in gut microbiota profiles among thalassemia patients were reevaluated based on the MCI status of the MoCA test. Although there were slight changes in alpha-diversity indices (Fig. 3G) and distance in community in beta-diversity indices (Fig. 3H) between patients with normal cognitive function and MCI, no significant differences were found for all indices. ANCOM-BC also indicated some differentials in the taxa, including an increase in Bifidobacteriaceae, Lactobacillaceae, Anaerofustaceae, Veillonellaceae, Carnobacteriaceae, Aerococcaceae, and Enterococcaceae, and a decrease in Fusobacteriaceae, Pasteurellaceae, UCG-010, Selenomonadaceae, Methanobacteriaceae, and Planococcaceae, in the patients with MCI compared to those without MCI (Fig. 3I and Supplementary Table 1). All findings suggest that thalassemia patients tend to exhibit cognitive decline, the degree of decline correlating with the greater severity of iron overload compared to healthy controls. However, there were no differences in the gut microbiota between the patients with and without MCI.

Gut microbiota was shifted in correlation with clinical parameters, possibly reflecting cause-effect relationships

Regarding ANCOM-BC, many associations were found between gut microbes and clinical parameters in terms of RBC homeostasis, white blood cells, SCFAs, and cognitive status across taxonomic levels. In the case of RBC-related biochemical parameters, an increased level of hemoglobin was associated with a decreased abundance of Negativicutes, a rise in serum ferritin was related to a reduction in Synergistota abundance, and an increase in total bilirubin level, along with indirect bilirubin, was observed alongside a decrease in the Clostridia_vadinBB60_group (Fig. 4A). The overall white blood cell count and the percentage of basophils increased in parallel with the abundance of Acholeplasmatales, Aeromonadales, Peptococccales, RF39, and Verrucomicrobiales (Fig. 4A). Additionally, the percentage of eosinophils increased alongside Euryarchaeota and Synergistota (Fig. 4A). Regarding SCFAs, increased levels of fecal isobutyric, isovaleric, and butyric acid were accompanied by a decrease in abundance of Negativicutes, while levels of fecal propionic acid increased in line with Campilobacterota abundance (Fig. 4A). Increased levels of plasma isobutyric acid were associated with reduced abundance of Caloramatoraceae, and increased levels of plasma acetic acid were related to a rise in Acetatifactor abundance (Fig. 4A). In terms of cognitive status, higher MoCA scores were associated with a decrease in Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 and Paraclostridium, but an increase in UCG-002 (Fig. 4A). Higher MSET10 scores were related to an increase in Marinifilaceae at the family level and Butyricimonas at the genus level (Fig. 4A). Figure 4A and Supplementary Table 2 present the significant differences in taxonomic abundance compared to clinical parameters. Next, Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States 2 (PICRUSt2) indicated that the gut microbiota of thalassemia patients, particularly those with TDT, were predictably reduced in areas of fucose degradation, anaerobic allantoin degradation IV, and the superpathway of seleno-compound metabolism (Fig. 4B). The gut microbiota of patients with NTDT exhibited an increase in pathways related to late cobalt incorporation, specifically adenosylcobalamin biosynthesis II and cob(II)yinate a, c-diamide biosynthesis II (Fig. 4B). Differential pathway abundance is described further in Supplementary Table 3. All findings indicate that both changes in gut microbial composition and the clinical courses of thalassemia may concurrently influence each other in various ways.

Blood bacterial profiles were similar between thalassemia patients and healthy controls

The blood bacterial profiles at the genus level were investigated, as summarized in Fig. 5A. Ultimately, 14 genera were retrieved, comprising Acinetobacter, Actinomyces, Bifidobacterium, Comamonas, Corynebacterium, Cutibacterium, Faecalibacterium, Gemella, Lactobacillus, Neisseria, Rothia, Ruminococcus, Staphylococcus, and Streptococcus (Fig. 5A). Six genera, including Acinetobacter, Actinomyces, Comamonas, Corynebacterium, Cutibacterium, and Staphylococcus, were shared among all groups (Fig. 5B). However, no significant differences in total read counts of blood bacteria were observed between groups (Fig. 5C), or in association with the regularity of blood transfusion (Fig. 5D), or cognitive status of the patients (Fig. 5E). In the individual profiles of blood bacteria, Corynebacterium and Staphylococcus predominated in most subjects, while other genera were dispersed with no specific patterns (Fig. 5F). According to the Disbiome database, oxygen requirement of these genera varied, and they could reside on physical barriers across the human body (Fig. 5F). Subsequently, the comparison between blood and gut read counts of genera present in at least five subjects were illustrated. Most subjects had higher read counts of Corynebacterium in the blood than in the feces, suggesting that there might be other sources of this blood genus apart from the gut (Fig. 5G). In contrast, Staphylococcus (Fig. 5H), Actinomyces (Fig. 5I), and Streptococcus (Fig. 5J) were present in the blood with lower read counts than in feces. Overall, there were no differences in blood bacterial profiles among subjects in terms of thalassemia, regularity of blood transfusion, and cognitive status.

Discussion

We found that, compared to healthy controls, thalassemia patients exhibited systemic iron overload together with gut dysbiosis. Notably, the severity of iron overload determined the level of cognitive impairment in thalassemia patients. Interrelationships among gut microbial taxa with clinical and biochemical parameters were identified. However, no differences were observed in blood bacterial profiles between thalassemia patients and healthy controls.

Evidence of gut dysbiosis in the context of thalassemia, both in humans and animal models, has been scant, with a focus on beta-thalassemia major. Studies conducted in Th3/+ C57BL/6J mice (beta-thalassemia) confirmed that iron overload contributes to gut dysbiosis10,11,15,16. However, dysbiotic profiles among studies varied, possibly due to differences in the age group, dietary composition, and interventions such as doses of iron supplements and chelators. Aside from animal studies, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study delineating gut dysbiosis in adult thalassemia patients, although a few clinical observations have been conducted in pediatric patients17,18. Similar to our taxonomic profiles, the gut microbiota of thalassemia children were also predominated by the family Lachnospiraceae, especially Blautia17. Both the family and genus are recognized as one of the main producers of SCFAs in the gut19,20. Yet, a decrease in Fibrobacter (phylum Fibrobacterota), an SCFA producer, was observed in our thalassemia cohort21. Interestingly, Akkermansia (phylum Verrucomicrobiota), which enhances other gut fermenters to produce SCFAs, was extensively increased in our thalassemia patients, especially those in the TDT group22. This finding resembles the findings of Visitchanakun et al. (2020 and 2021) which showed that Verrucomicrobiota was increased in beta-thalassemia mice with iron supplementation11,16. On the other hand, Siwichaiin et al. (2022) demonstrated that deferiprone beneficially increased levels of this phylum in beta-thalassemia mice10. Of note, most of our thalassemia patients were currently treated with iron chelators, particularly those with TDT. Whether the rise in this phylum in our thalassemia patients is compensatory or influenced by xenobiotics from iron chelators is still speculatory and worth mechanistic exploration. Collectively, these changes could explain why levels of SCFAs in thalassemia patients with iron overload did not significantly decline.

In contrast to the beneficial Verrucomicrobiota, increased abundance of Fusobacteriaceae (phylum Fusobacteriota) possibly represents a pathologic change in thalassemia patients. Colonization by Fusobacterium nucleatum has been widely accepted in driving gut dysbiosis23. Under iron-rich intervention, macrophages co-cultured with Fusobacterium nucleatum express various inflammatory mediators, exemplified by CCL8, a neutrophil-recruiting chemokine24. Accordingly, it is postulated that Fusobacteriaceae may serve as a pathobiont in thalassemia. Apart from the taxa mentioned, whether a reduction in commensal Spirochaetota in thalassemia patients has a negative effect on gut communities necessitates further investigation, as most studies have focused on pathogenic strains instead of spirochete flora25. Furthermore, in terms of metagenome function, fucose utilization is an essential process for gut microbes to establish symbiotic gut microbiota26. A predicted decrease in fucose degradation in the gut microbiota of thalassemia patients corroborates the gut dysbiosis in these patients. Together, our findings imply that gut dysbiosis in thalassemia encompasses complex microbial relationships in terms of both composition and corresponding functions.

Iron accumulation in brain cortices as an aging process contributes to cognitive impairment in the elderly27,28. This excess iron potentiates oxidative stress, causing neuronal ferroptosis in both aging population and thalassemia patients29,30. According to our previous study, we applied the MoCA assessment to evaluate cognitive decline in a thalassemia population with a mean age of 35 ± 11years5, which is relatively similar to mean age of our current study. Moreover, this assessment has been validated to detect early MCI in Turkish migrants in Germany with a mean age of 42.3 ± 11.9 years31. In addition to the MoCA assessment, the MSET10 test was also applied to replicate the downward trend observed with another assessment. In our study, where thalassemia patients have been afflicted with life-long iron overload, albeit properly treated with iron chelators, cognitive impairment at an early age becomes an unavoidable complication of the disease. Although our findings highlight that increased ferritin levels were associated with a greater severity of cognitive impairment, the proportion of patients with MCI in the current cohort (approximately 25%) was lower than that in our previous study (approximately 70%)5. One possible explanation is the age disparity between the thalassemia cohorts5. While MCI in older patients was related to the record of maximal five-year ferritin levels, our younger thalassemia cohort represented a very early level of cognitive decline indicated by their correlation with current ferritin levels instead5. In addition, while increasing age contributed to a decline in cognitive function in several studies, the age range of our thalassemia patients within the current cohort were 20–40 years old, which were in adulthood period, not in elderly period. Furthermore, our data showed that the oldest TDT patient was 40 years old with a normal MoCA score of 27, whereas the second oldest TDT patient was 38 years old with a MoCA score of 20. These findings suggest that disease-driven factors, aside from age, may be underlying the cognitive impairment in our study. These findings led us to focus the pathology of thalassemia by examining serum ferritin levels. To understand the relationships between age, level of iron overload and cognitive function in Thalassemia patients, some important questions need to be answered, including (1) whether cognitive function fluctuates in parallel with the status of iron overload across various age strata, and (2) whether there is a point of no return for serum ferritin levels beyond which cognitive function will progressively impair, regardless of age. Therefore, further studies are still required to investigate in both questions. In terms of the gut microbiota, although there were no obvious differences in the gut microbe composition between the patients with and without MCI, initial findings support the existence of evidence regarding the gut-brain axis. For example, Butyricimonas, a producer of butyric acid, was a protective factor against cognitive impairment32,33. Our findings support this role of Butyricimonas by showing that subjects with higher MSET10 scores tended to have a greater abundance of this genus. Furthermore, increased Paraclostridium, a mucin degrader, was considered as a risk factor due to an association with lower MoCA scores in our cohort. Some reports have indicated that Paraclostridium bifermentans is capable of causing a brain abscess34. However, to date there is no evidence linking this genus to cognitive function.

Given that iron is a vital element for the metabolism of bacteria, it has been hypothesized that a sequential rise in blood bacteria as a consequence of iron overload exploitation is associated with the pathogenesis of various chronic inflammatory diseases13,35. In the case of thalassemia, gut barrier disruption with translocation of microbial components into the circulation of patients has been observed11,36. Although our thalassemia patients exhibited iron overload, blood bacterial profiles between the patients and healthy controls did not differ in terms of the disease, regularity of blood transfusion, or cognitive status of the patients. These findings imply that there were no blood microbiome in our case. Interestingly, most subjects in our study shared the characteristic that Corynebacterium, a commensal of oral and skin microbiota, had markedly higher blood read counts than gut read counts37. Similar to Tan et al. (2023), blood bacteria tend to derive from the oral microbiota instead of the gut microbiota in the healthy population12. Our findings imply that, despite gut dysbiosis, the integrity of gut barriers in thalassemia patients are intact enough to prevent the translocation of gut bacteria.

Given that measuring serum ferritin levels has been implemented to evaluate status of iron overload in thalassemia in clinical setting according to the Thalassemia International Federation38, serum ferritin level has also reported to be associated with inflammatory status via mirroring its degree in various conditions including infection, autoimmune diseases, malignancies39. However, serum ferritin level is not the gold standard for precisely quantifying iron overload compared to liver iron content in clinical setting40 and non-transferrin bound iron in basic research41. Thus, measuring liver iron content or serum non-transferrin bound iron level would further be investigated to make the better reflection for the actual status of iron overload condition in the future study. In terms of cognitive function, an imbalance in educational levels could affect the findings of cognitive tests. Several robust investigations of how the clinical phenomenon of thalassemia, including specific types of thalassemia, abnormal hemoglobin structure, altered hemoglobin F level, immune dysregulation from splenectomy, and each iron overload complication, are necessary to further investigate in longitudinal studies with larger sample size for their associations with changes in gut microbiota. Furthermore, certain functional studies of gut microbiota, such as the potential benefit of Butyricimonas supplementation, as a probiotic, in preventing cognitive decline in thalassemia patients with iron overload, as well as the role of each iron chelator and its route of administration in manipulating the abundance of Verrucomicrobiota in thalassemia with iron overload, also require preclinical experiments and clinical trials with metabolomics in the future study. Aside from iron chelators, effects of diet and other xenobiotics on gut microbiota in thalassemic condition are worth exploring in the future. Additionally, shotgun sequencing needs to be employed for microbiota studies due to its comprehensive characterization of microbial domains at the species level42,43. Notably, microbiota from multiple sites with assessment of barrier disruption and translocation of microbial components into the circulation should be thoroughly investigated to determine the origination of blood bacteria12.

In summary, thalassemia patients with iron overload exhibited gut dysbiosis and also cognitive decline. Harnessing benefits from those identified microbes could transform life-long care for thalassemia patients by preventing the development of complications due to iron overload, at least as regards gut dysbiosis and cognitive decline.

Methods

Study subjects

This unmatched case-control study comprised healthy controls (aged 28–36 years old), NTDT patients (aged 24–38 years old), and TDT patients (aged 20–40 years old). The thalassemia patients in the current study had either alpha- or beta-thalassemia, with the specific type confirmed by High-Performance Liquid Chromatography and molecular testing. TDT was defined as thalassemia patients who require regular blood transfusion for survival44. NTDT patients in our study had not received a blood transfusion for at least six months prior to enrollment. The samples were obtained pre-transfusion. Every participant was enrolled at the Department of Hematology, Maharaj Nakhon Chiang Mai Hospital, Thailand, from February 2022 to January 2023 (with a total of 54 TDT patients and 117 NTDT patients). Cognitive assessment of each subject was conducted by the same physician throughout the study at enrollment using Thai MoCA, with a total score of 30 and a cut-off for MCI set at less than 25, and MSET10, with a total score of 30 and a cut-off for MCI set at less than 2245,46. This study was approved under supervision of the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Chiang Mai University, Thailand, in line with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice Guidelines (Approval Number: 037/2565). All participants provided informed consent.

Collection and processing of biospecimens

Subjects were directed to collect their feces using a fecal collection kit for gut microbiota study and targeted metabolomics of fecal SCFAs. Fecal samples were aliquoted into RNAlater solution (Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA). Fifteen-milliliters of whole blood were collected to investigate basic biochemical profile, blood bacterial characterization, plasma SCFAs, and serum ferritin. Quantification of serum ferritin and the basic biochemical profile were conducted using the Cobas e801 immunoassay analyzer (Roche Diagnosis GmbH, Mannheim, Germany). A level of serum ferritin over 800 and 1,000 µg/L designated significantly clinical iron overload in NTDT and TDT patients, respectively38. Plasma was prepared by centrifuging whole blood at 3000 rpm for 10 min. All biospecimens were then preserved at -80 °C.

Gut microbiota

The QIAamp Power Fecal Pro DNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used to extract bacterial genomic DNA from 0.25 g of feces following the manufacturer’s instructions. The 16S rRNA sequencing of V3-V4 hypervariable region was performed using the 341 F and 805R primers with the paired-end read method on Illumina MiSeq (Novogene, Singapore)47. The sequencing data were analyzed using Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology 2 (QIIME 2, v.2023.5) with a minimum sequencing depth of 77,443 to incorporate all samples for the downstream analysis10,48,49. Taxonomic assignment was performed based on the SILVA database (v.138), followed by diversity analysis50. ANCOM-BC enabled differentiation of distinctive taxa between groups51. PICRUSt2 inferred metabolic pathways based on the MetaCyc database52,53.

Blood bacteria profiles

The DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used to extract bacterial genomic DNA from 200 mL of whole blood regarding the manufacturer’s instruction. The 16S rRNA sequencing of V3-V4 hypervariable region was performed using the 341 F and 805R primers and sequenced on DNBSEQ-G400 (BGI Genomics, Hong Kong)47. As with the gut microbiota analysis, QIIME 2 with the SILVA database was used to analyze blood bacterial profiles with a minimum sequencing depth of 87,099. All assigned structural variants (SVs) were decontaminated and visualized in R (v.4.3.1) as follows: (1) removal of unassigned and unclassified bacterial SVs, and Eukaryota-related SVs, (2) removal of non-human-related assigned genera based on the Disbiome database, and (3) removal of assigned genera not presenting on a list of blood bacterial signatures based on Tan et al. (2023)12,54.

Targeted metabolomics of SCFAs

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry has been primarily designated for use in targeted metabolomics, specifically for short-chain fatty acids55. To quantify the targeted metabolomics of SCFAs in both fecal content and plasma, gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (Agilent Technologies, California, USA) was employed, as described in our previous study10. The actual concentration of SCFAs was calculated by comparison with a standard mixture (Restek, Pennsylvania, USA) using MassHunter Quantitative Analysis Software version 10.1 (Agilent Technologies, California, USA). Subsequently, the concentrations were normalized using MetaboDrift to reduce the multi-batch effect, as previously described56.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using R (v.4.3.1). Clinical data were expressed as median with IQR, mean ± SEM, or numbers with percentages. The Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to determine the normality of numerical data. Corresponding statistical tests included the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test and the Mann-Whitney U test for nonparametric data, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test for parametric data, and the simulated chi-square test for categorical data. A p-value less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.
