The association between health anxiety and frequency of participation in physical education classes and physical activity in Chinese university students
Authors: Biaoqi Xu, Hamid Amini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth anxiety, defined as excessive worry about illness, is increasingly common among university students. It may both reduce engagement in physical activity for some individuals and be alleviated by regular exercise. Psychosocial resources such as self-efficacy, enjoyment, and social support may mediate these relations, but evidence among Chinese university students is limited. This study examined psychosocial pathways linking moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) during physical education (PE) classes and overall activity patterns with health anxiety among Chinese university students.MethodsA cross-sectional study (March–April 2025) sampled 4,256 third-year undergraduates from 16 universities in Guangzhou (mean age 21.01 ± 0.34 years; 57.4% female) using stratified cluster random sampling. Health anxiety (Whiteley-7), self-reported physical activity (IPAQ-short), exercise self-efficacy (SEE-C), enjoyment (PACES), and social support (PASSS-C) were assessed by validated questionnaires. Objective MVPA during PE classes was measured with ActiGraph accelerometers (mean across three classes). Some demographic variables including sex and BMI were also collected for covariate analysis. Structural equation modeling with bias-corrected bootstrapping (5,000 resamples) tested direct and indirect associations.ResultsEnjoyment positively predicted MVPA during PE (β = 0.142, p = 0.007), whereas BMI negatively predicted it (β = −0.092, p = 0.045). Greater MVPA during PE classes was associated with higher overall activity (β = 0.183) and higher self-efficacy (β = 0.157). Higher self-efficacy (β = −0.172, p = 0.003) and social support (β = −0.098, p = 0.015) were associated with lower physical inactivity, while BMI showed a positive association (β = 0.097, p = 0.019). Higher physical inactivity predicted greater health anxiety (β = 0.276, p < 0.001). Also, enjoyment indirectly reduced health anxiety through higher MVPA and lower physical inactivity; self-efficacy and social support also exerted indirect protective effects via decreased physical inactivity. MVPA itself contributed to lower health anxiety by reducing physical inactivity. Indirect effects of gender and BMI were non-significant.ConclusionsPsychosocial resources —including self-efficacy, enjoyment, and social support—mediate the links between MVPA during PE classes, physical inactivity, and health anxiety. Enhancing affective and social engagement in PE classes may help reduce physical inactivity and health anxiety among Chinese university students.Keywords: Lifestyle, Sedentary behavior, Health promotion

Introduction

Health anxiety is defined as excessive worry about having or developing a serious illness, often accompanied by heightened attention to bodily sensations and misinterpretation of normal physiological changes as signs of disease [1]. This condition is increasingly recognized as a significant concern among university students, where academic pressures, lifestyle changes, and limited health literacy may exacerbate vulnerability to anxiety-related symptoms [2].

Although health anxiety has sometimes been associated with avoidance behaviors and reduced engagement in physical activities, a growing body of evidence indicates that regular physical activity is, in fact, associated with reductions in health anxiety. Recent studies suggest that physical exercise may alleviate health anxiety through psychological and psychophysiological mechanisms- such as enhanced emotion regulation, lowered perceived stress, and reduced anxiety sensitivity. For example, Wang et al. (2025) report a chain-mediating effect whereby physical activity improves emotion regulation and diminishes stress perception, ultimately reducing health anxiety among college students [3]. Similarly, some studies provide empirical support for a protective role of exercise against health-related worry across diverse populations [4, 5]. Thus, rather than portraying the relationship as strictly unidirectional, a more nuanced perspective is warranted- one in which health anxiety may lead some individuals to avoid activity, while regular physical activity commonly exerts a protective, anxiety-reducing effect.

Despite these benefits, physical inactivity remains widespread in Chinese universities, where a large proportion of students fail to meet recommended guidelines for physical activity [6]. This problem is compounded by declining participation in structured physical education (PE) classes, which are no longer mandatory in many institutions and are often undervalued compared with academic demands [7]. This decline is concerning given that PE classes not only provide structured activity but also foster psychosocial factors—such as self-efficacy, enjoyment, and social support—that encourage students to adopt more active lifestyles [8].

From the perspective of self-determination theory (SDT), persistent health anxiety may reflect frustration of basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness, which can diminish intrinsic motivation for physical activity [9]. Conversely, autonomy-supportive PE environments may enhance need satisfaction, fostering engagement and emotional well-being. In parallel, cognitive-behavioral theory (CBT) emphasize that maladaptive appraisals—such as catastrophic misinterpretation of normal physiological sensations—sustain health anxiety and avoidance of physical activity [10]. Thus, integrating SDT and CBT perspectives offers a complementary framework to understand how motivational and cognitive processes jointly shape the relationship between physical activity and health anxiety.

Previous research has highlighted the importance of psychosocial mechanisms in shaping health-related behaviors [11], yet their role in the relationship between PE class participation, physical activity, and health anxiety has not been thoroughly investigated in Chinese university students. Most prior studies have examined these factors in isolation, without modeling the potential direct and indirect pathways that link them in a unified framework. Using a structural equation modeling approach, we specifically test the mediating roles of exercise self-efficacy, enjoyment, and social support in these relationships. These proposed mediators are theoretically grounded in complementary frameworks. Within SDT, enjoyment represents intrinsic motivation and satisfaction of basic psychological needs—particularly autonomy and competence [12]—that promote sustained physical activity. Self-efficacy, derived from CBT, reflects confidence in one’s ability to overcome barriers to exercise and is a key determinant of health behavior change [13]. Social support functions both as a contextual facilitator of self-efficacy (through encouragement and modeling) [14] and as an indicator of need satisfaction for relatedness within SDT. Together, these constructs capture affective (enjoyment), cognitive (self-efficacy), and social (support) dimensions of motivation, offering a multidimensional framework to explain how physical activity may alleviate health anxiety.

No prior study has systematically modeled the psychosocial pathways connecting PE participation, physical activity, and health anxiety among Chinese university students. Therefore, the present study aims to clarify the psychosocial pathways linking PE participation and physical activity with health anxiety among Chinese university students.

Methods and materials

Participants and study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted between March 1 and April 29, 2025, in Guangzhou, the capital city of Guangdong Province, China. Guangzhou hosts 84 accredited higher education institutions offering undergraduate programs across diverse disciplines. A stratified cluster random sampling approach was employed to recruit participants. First, eligible institutions in Guangzhou were stratified into three categories: public universities, private universities, and vocational colleges offering bachelor’s degrees. Then, a total of 16 institutions were randomly selected in proportion to their representation within these strata. Within each selected university, one or more classes of third-year students were randomly chosen, and all students present in the selected classes were invited to participate in the survey. Recruitment was conducted in person by trained research assistants who briefly explained the study aims, obtained written informed consent, and supervised the completion of questionnaires. Completed questionnaires were collected immediately in sealed envelopes.

Eligible participants were third-year students actively enrolled in coursework and able to complete the questionnaire independently. These students typically exhibit greater autonomy in academic and lifestyle decisions, including voluntary engagement in physical activity. In addition to their relative autonomy in lifestyle decisions, third-year students were selected to ensure greater homogeneity in academic stage and psychosocial development. Compared with first-year students, they have already adapted to university life and established stable routines related to study, sleep, and physical activity, while not yet facing the graduation-related stress commonly observed in final-year students. This group therefore represents a balanced stage of academic engagement and lifestyle stability, minimizing potential confounding effects of transitional or end-stage academic pressures.

Students were excluded if they were majoring in physical education or sports sciences (due to unusually high levels of baseline physical activity) or if they attended university less than three days per week (e.g., part-time or irregular students).

The required sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1, assuming a medium effect size (f² = 0.15), α = 0.05, statistical power = 0.95, and eight predictors. The minimum required sample size was 1,203 participants. To enhance statistical power and accommodate model complexity, a larger sample than the minimum was recruited.

All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation and all procedures complied with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by Medical Ethics Committee.

Continuous variables (BMI, health anxiety, MVPA%, self-efficacy, enjoyment, and social support) were mean-centered prior to analysis to reduce multicollinearity and simplify interpretation of intercepts.

Assessments

Body mass index

Height and weight were measured by using standardized procedures and BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²) [15]. In the main structural equation model, BMI was analyzed as a continuous variable. For descriptive purposes, we also reported the distribution of BMI categories according to the Chinese CDC guidelines (underweight: <18.5; normal: 18.5–23.9; overweight: 24.0–27.9; obese: ≥28) [15].

Health anxiety

Health anxiety was assessed using the Whiteley Index-7, a validated instrument comprising seven items with a yes/no response format [16]. Total scores range from 0 to 7, with higher scores indicating greater levels of health anxiety. The Chinese version of the scale has demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency and stable test-retest reliability [17]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.77, indicating acceptable reliability.

Physical activity

Physical activity levels were measured using the short version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), which assesses the frequency and duration of walking, moderate, and vigorous-intensity activities [18, 19]. Each activity is assigned a MET value—3.3 for walking, 4.0 for moderate, and 8.0 for vigorous intensity—representing multiples of the resting metabolic rate. Weekly MET scores were calculated by multiplying the MET value by the total minutes spent on each activity, and summing across all activities. Participants with less than 600 MET-minutes per week were classified as physically inactive [20]. The short IPAQ has demonstrated acceptable reliability in Chinese adults (correlation coefficients >0.70) [21], and in this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.79, indicating good internal consistency.

Moderate to vigorous-intensity physical activity (MVPA) during physical education class

To objectively assess MVPA during PE lessons, ActiGraph wGT3X-BT accelerometers were used. Given that longer epoch lengths can underestimate vigorous activity and potentially overestimate MVPA by merging structured activity bouts, data were collected using 1-second epochs [22–24]. Following each measurement session, the raw ActiGraph files were downloaded and processed using ActiLife software (version 6.5). Time spent at or above the moderate-intensity threshold (≥ 2,800 counts per minute) was classified as MVPA [25]. Each participant wore the device on the right hip, secured with an elastic belt, during three separate scheduled PE classes. Devices were initialized immediately before class and removed afterward by trained research assistants. Participants were instructed to wear the device throughout the class period and to engage in activities as they normally would. For each participant, the mean MVPA across the three classes was used in subsequent analyses.

Self-efficacy for exercise

In this study, The Chinese version of the Self-Efficacy for Exercise Scale (SEE-C) was used to assess participants’ confidence in maintaining regular exercise in the face of common barriers. The instrument consists of nine items, each describing a potential challenge (for example, feeling tired or having a busy schedule). Responses are recorded on an 11-point scale ranging from 0 (“not confident at all”) to 10 (“completely confident”). Scores are summed to yield a total between 0 and 90, with higher values reflecting stronger exercise self-efficacy. Previous research has reported satisfactory internal consistency in various Chinese-speaking populations [26, 27]. In the present study, internal consistency was also acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.73), supporting the reliability of the measure for use among Chinese university students.

Physical activity enjoyment

To assess how much participants enjoy engaging in physical activity, we used the Physical Activity Enjoyment Scale (PACES). Originally developed by Kendzierski and DeCarlo in 1991 [28], the full version includes 18 items and is typically administered on a 7-point Likert scale, where responses range from one polar feeling to its opposite (e.g., “I enjoy it” vs. “I hate it”). Total scores span 18 to 126, with higher scores reflecting greater enjoyment. The research confirmed both the reliability and construct validity of the Chinese version of PACES [29]. The internal consistency was also acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.79) in the present study.

Physical activity social support

The Chinese version of the Physical Activity Social Support Scale (PASSS-C) was used to evaluate participants’ perceived social support related to physical activity. This validated instrument consists of 20 items distributed evenly across five dimensions: emotional support (4 items), informational support (4 items), instrumental support (4 items), validation support (4 items), and companionship support (4 items). Each item is rated on a seven-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree), with an additional “does not apply” option scored as 0. The overall score is obtained by summing the ratings for all 20 items, with higher scores reflecting greater perceived social support. The PASSS-C has demonstrated excellent internal consistency in previous research, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.942, supporting its reliability for use in Chinese-speaking populations [30]. The internal consistency was also acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.818) in the present study.

Statistical Methods

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 and AMOS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The significance level was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed) for all tests. Descriptive statistics (means ± standard deviations for continuous variables; frequencies and percentages for categorical variables) were used to summarize characteristics of the participants. Structural equation modeling was performed to examine the direct and indirect relationships among health anxiety, participation in physical education classes, and physical inactivity, controlling for gender, BMI, exercise self-efficacy, physical activity enjoyment, and social support for physical activity.

Prior to model estimation, multivariate normality was evaluated using Mardia’s tests for multivariate skewness and kurtosis, and univariate skewness and kurtosis values were inspected for all observed variables. The results indicated a significant departure from multivariate normality (Mardia’s multivariate skewness = 241.2, p < 0.001; kurtosis = 96.5, p < 0.001). Therefore, model parameters were estimated using the maximum likelihood method with Bollen–Stine bootstrap correction and bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals (5,000 resamples), which provide robust standard errors and adjusted significance levels under non-normal data conditions. Inspection of the dataset indicated no substantial item-level missingness. Therefore, all available cases were retained for analyses using the full information maximum likelihood estimation in AMOS.

Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices, including the Chi-square statistic (χ²), Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI), Adjusted Goodness-of-Fit Index (AGFI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Normed Fit Index (NFI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) with 90% confidence interval, and the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) [31–34]. The following cutoff criteria were applied a priori to indicate an acceptable to good model fit: CFI, TLI, and NFI ≥ 0.95 (≥ 0.90 acceptable); RMSEA ≤ 0.06 (≤ 0.08 acceptable); SRMR ≤ 0.08; GFI and AGFI ≥ 0.90; and χ²/df < 3 [31–34].

Standardized path coefficients for direct, indirect, and total effects were calculated, and statistical significance was determined using 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals. Bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples was used to test the indirect effects. Continuous predictors were mean-centered before estimation to facilitate interpretation of intercepts and interaction terms. All variables were screened for outliers using Mahalanobis distance (p < 0.001), and no influential multivariate outliers were retained.

Results

A total of 4,604 questionnaires were distributed; 4,256 were correctly completed and met inclusion criteria, yielding a valid response rate of 92.4%. Participants were exclusively third-year undergraduates (mean age = 21.01 ± 0.34 years); with 57.4% being female (n = 2,443). Approximately 39.0% of students were overweight/obese (n = 1,659). The mean weekly physical activity was 797.35 ± 542.49 MET-min/week, and nearly half of the sample (47.98%, n = 2,042) were classified as physically inactive (< 600 MET-min/week). The mean health-anxiety score (WI-7) was 1.76 ± 0.78 and students spent about 45% of their PE lesson time engaging in MVPA.

To account for clustering effects and unequal selection probabilities, a design effect of 1.20 was applied, and sampling weights (ranging from 0.85 to 1.30, mean = 1.05) were computed as the inverse of the probability of selection. Harman’s single-factor test indicated that one factor explained only 36.3% of the total variance, below the 50% threshold, suggesting that common method bias was not a serious concern.

In the final SEM, enjoyment emerged as a meaningful predictor of MVPA during PE classes (β = 0.142, p = 0.007), suggesting that students who reported greater enjoyment, engaged more MVPA than their peers. BMI showed a negative association with MVPA during PE classes (β = −0.092, p = 0.045), implying that students with higher BMI tended to be less physically active during PE classes, while gender was not a significant determinant of MVPA during PE classes (β = 0.025, p = 0.301).

Regarding inactivity, both self-efficacy (β = −0.172, p = 0.003) and social support (β = −0.098, p = 0.015) were associated with lower inactivity levels—students who felt more confident and socially supported were less likely to be inactive. Conversely, higher BMI increased the likelihood of inactivity (β = 0.097, p = 0.019). Physical inactivity was a strong positive predictor of health anxiety (β = 0.276, p < 0.001), indicating that students who were less active tended to report substantially higher levels of health-related worry (Table 2).

Bootstrapped mediation analyses (5,000 samples) identified several indirect pathways linking these variables. Greater MVPA during PE classes was indirectly associated with lower health anxiety via reduced inactivity (β = −0.033, 95% CI [− 0.045, − 0.022], p < 0.001). Similarly, students who enjoyed physical activity more tended to engage in more MVPA during PE classes and were less inactive, which collectively contributed to reduced health anxiety (β = −0.039, 95% CI [− 0.070, − 0.016], p = 0.003). Higher self-efficacy (β = −0.051, 95% CI [− 0.084, − 0.022], p = 0.004) and stronger social support (β = −0.028, 95% CI [− 0.057, − 0.009], p = 0.012) also lowered health anxiety indirectly through their effects on inactivity. By contrast, gender and BMI showed no significant indirect effects on health anxiety (p > 0.05).

Discussion

This study examined the interrelationships among moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) during physical education (PE) classes, psychosocial factors (enjoyment, social support, and exercise self-efficacy), overall physical activity patterns, and health anxiety in Chinese college students. The findings illuminate several interconnected pathways that help explain how behavioral and psychological processes converge to influence mental health outcomes.

A key finding was the direct positive association between enjoyment and MVPA during PE classes. The observed pattern of relationship can be understood within both SDT and CBT. In SDT terms, enjoyment can be viewed as indicators of need satisfaction (autonomy, competence, relatedness) [35], which promote autonomous motivation for participation in MVPA. When these needs are met, students are more likely to experience exercise as self-endorsed and emotionally rewarding, buffering against anxiety. Conversely, need frustration—such as perceived pressure or lack of competence—may amplify vulnerability to health anxiety. Also, this is consistent with CBT suggesting that maladaptive appraisal of bodily sensations (e.g., interpreting benign physiological arousal as signs of illness) reinforces anxiety and avoidance [10]. Regular MVPA may serve as behavioral exposure, providing corrective experiences that reduce such maladaptive interpretations and improve interoceptive accuracy.

This study showed that higher self-efficacy acts as a psychological buffer, promoting greater overall physical activity and indirectly mitigating health anxiety. Mechanistically, self-efficacy enhances students’ confidence in their ability to interpret and manage bodily sensations [36], reducing maladaptive perceptions of physical symptoms—a core component of health anxiety. Also, social support showed a significant positive association with overall physical activity levels (i.e., an inverse relationship with physical inactivity). Students who received greater encouragement from peers or family tended to engage more frequently in active behaviors and were less likely to lead sedentary lifestyles. This aligns with social cognitive theory, which posits that supportive interpersonal environments enhance motivation and self-regulatory capacity [37]. Social reinforcement can normalize physical activity, lower perceived barriers, and sustain adaptive behaviors patterns [38]. Importantly, social support may also indirectly influence mental health by facilitating engagement in MVPA during PE classes and encouraging broader participation in leisure-time activity—both of which are known to reduce anxiety symptoms. In this context, social support functions not only as a motivational resource but also as a psychosocial scaffold that promotes behavioral activation and emotional regulation [39].

MVPA during PE classes demonstrated both direct and indirect effects on psychological outcomes. Behaviorally, higher in-class MVPA contributes to healthier activity patterns by replacing sedentary time with active engagement [40]. Psychologically, MVPA during PE classes enhances self-efficacy, which in turn contributes to lower health anxiety [41, 42]. This dual-pathway effect highlights that physical activity—particularly structured MVPA within PE settings—is both a behavioral and psychological mechanism through which resilience is strengthened.

From a neurobiological perspective, regular participation in MVPA may regulate autonomic arousal, decrease interoceptive hypersensitivity, and improve emotional control —all processes relevant to the etiology of health anxiety [43]. Thus, MVPA during PE classes serves not only as a physical training stimulus but also as a psychological regulator.

The association between lower activity levels and health anxiety underscores the potential psychological risks of sedentary behavior. Students who engaged less in physical activity reported greater anxiety about their health, possibly due to increased bodily vigilance and catastrophic interpretation of normal sensations. This observation is consistent with cognitive-behavioral models of health anxiety, which emphasize attentional bias and avoidance as maintaining factors [44].

Furthermore, limited exposure to normal physiological sensations associated with physical activity (e.g., elevated heart rate or muscle fatigue) may prevent corrective experiences that recalibrate threat perception, leaving individuals more prone to misinterpreting benign bodily cues as signs of illness [44].

Gender did not show a significant direct effect on health anxiety. Although gender did not show a significant direct or indirect effect in the current model, psychosocial resources such as self-efficacy, enjoyment, and social support may operate differently across males and females in some cultural contexts. Given the nonsignificant findings in our study, we cannot draw conclusions about gender differences; however, these potential variations merit further investigation using gender-stratified or multi-group analyses in future work. BMI significantly predicted both MVPA during PE classes and overall activity levels. Specifically, higher BMI was associated with lower MVPA and reduced activity participation, aligning with previous evidence linking excess body weight to diminished engagement in exercise [45, 46]. Nevertheless, BMI did not directly predict health anxiety, suggesting that its influence operates indirectly through behavioral mechanisms. This underscores that while BMI is an important correlate of physical activity patterns, psychosocial constructs such as enjoyment, self-efficacy, and social support remain central, modifiable targets for interventions aiming to enhance both physical and mental well-being.

Beyond individual and psychosocial factors, cultural and institutional characteristics of Chinese universities may also shape students’ physical activity and anxiety patterns, potentially moderating the observed relationships. In recent years, many universities in China have reduced the frequency and grading weight of PE courses, often replacing them with theoretical modules or elective formats. This institutional shift limits opportunities for structured MVPA during academic schedules, particularly among upper-year students, who typically face intense academic demands and limited time for exercise. Moreover, the academic culture in Chinese higher education emphasizes achievement and exam performance, which can increase stress and leave little space for recreational or health-promoting activities. In this context, enjoyment, self-efficacy, and social support become especially important psychological resources that help students maintain physical activity despite environmental barriers. Conversely, students lacking these resources may experience compounded effects of academic pressure and inactivity, leading to heightened health-related anxiety.

Future studies should therefore examine how institutional PE policies, campus fitness environments, and cultural attitudes toward exercise interact with psychological factors to influence students’ mental health trajectories. Also, Future studies should explore how intrinsic motivation can serve as the key driver of sustained MVPA among university students. According to SDT, enhancing intrinsic motivation requires creating a need-supportive environment that satisfies autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Some studies proposed a classification system of need-supportive and motivational behaviors, which offers a practical framework for designing and evaluating interventions aimed at promoting autonomous motivation [47]. Applying such frameworks in PE or university-based health programs could help identify effective strategies to increase students’ intrinsic motivation toward physical activity and, consequently, improve both behavioral and psychological outcomes.

Despite the valuable insights provided by this study into the psychosocial mechanisms linking physical education and health anxiety, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference. Although structural equation modeling tests directional hypotheses, longitudinal or experimental designs are needed to confirm causal pathways. Second, apart from objectively measured BMI and MVPA during PE classes, other variables—including enjoyment, self-efficacy, social support, physical activity, and health anxiety—were self-reported, which may introduce recall bias or social desirability effects. Future studies should include behavioral and physiological measures to enhance validity. Third, participants were limited to third-year undergraduate students from Chinese universities, restricting generalizability to other groups. Replication across diverse populations is encouraged. Fourth, while the model focused on three psychosocial resources—enjoyment, self-efficacy, and social support—other potentially psychological constructs such as anxiety sensitivity or coping style may further clarify these relationships.

Conclusion

This study contributes to a growing body of evidence highlighting the psychosocial mechanisms that link physical education experiences to lower health anxiety among university students. By integrating enjoyment, self-efficacy, and social support into a comprehensive model, the findings underscore the importance of fostering positive psychological resources within structured physical activity contexts. The observed indirect pathways suggest that promoting students’ affective and social engagement in PE-based MVPA may protect against physical inactivity and anxiety-related concerns. From a theoretical standpoint, the results support multidimensional models of health behavior, emphasizing the interplay between emotional, cognitive, and behavioral factors. Practically, the findings advocate for the design of PE programs that prioritize enjoyment, competence-building, and peer support to enhance both physical activity participation and mental well-being.

While the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and several variables were self-reported, the inclusion of objectively measured BMI and MVPA during PE classes adds methodological strength. Future longitudinal research should include additional psychological constructs and replicate these mechanisms in broader contexts to further validate and extend the current model.
