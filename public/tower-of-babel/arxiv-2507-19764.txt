Electron-phonon coupled Langevin dynamics for Mott insulators
Rico Pohle, Yukitoshi Motome, Terumasa Tadano, Shintaro Hoshino

Abstract
The Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert (LLG) equations are widely used to study spin dynamics in Mott insulators. However, because energy dissipation is typically introduced phenomenologically, its validity for describing nonequilibrium processes and long-time dynamics in real materials remains questionable. In this paper, we derive a generalized stochastic LLG equation from first principles for spin-orbital coupled Mott insulators, explicitly incorporating the coupling between electronic degrees of freedom and lattice vibrations. Our approach is based on the path-integral formalism formulated along the Keldysh contour, which naturally accounts for dissipation and thermal fluctuations through interactions with a phonon bath and emergent stochastic noise. We benchmark our theoretical framework by numerically integrating the equations of motion for a two-orbital spin chain coupled to Einstein phonons. The resulting energy relaxation mimics realistic cooling dynamics, exhibits nontrivial transient behavior during thermalization, and accurately reproduces thermodynamic properties upon equilibration. This demonstrates the robustness and reliability of our formalism for capturing both equilibrium and nonequilibrium aspects of dissipative spin dynamics in strongly correlated systems. We further show how electron-phonon coupling induces hybridization between electronic and phononic modes in the excitation spectrum. Finally, we demonstrate how the conventional LLG equations can be recovered as a limiting case of our microscopic theory, and discuss their applicability to real materials.

II
Path Integral Formalism on the Keldysh Contour

The goal of this work is to derive real-time equations of
motion for spin-orbital coupled Mott insulators based on a
microscopic treatment of electron-phonon coupling.
To achieve this, we represent localized electrons
using a semiclassical SU(
N
) bosonic coherent state
framework and solve their dynamics via a path integral
formalism on the Keldysh contour.
As illustrated in Fig.
1
, we consider three
coupled subsystems:

1.

localized electrons on a lattice, interacting
via nonequilibriumal exchange,

2.

phonon modes representing lattice vibrations, and

3.

a thermal bath at temperature
T
.

All three components are treated explicitly and are dynamically coupled.
Electrons and phonons interact through electron–phonon coupling,
while the phonons themselves are coupled to a thermal bath.
This phonon–bath interaction introduces both damping and stochastic noise,
modeled microscopically after introducing auxiliary fields in the path integral
formalism.
These terms satisfy the fluctuation–dissipation theorem, ensuring that
the phonons relax toward the correct thermal equilibrium distribution
at temperature
T
.

The resulting equations of motion
[see explicit formulation in Eq. (
69
)
in Sec.
III
] take the form of two
coupled first-order differential equations:

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{\Omega}}={\bm{B}}^{-1}\ \nabla_{\bm{\Omega}}{\bm{\mathcal{O}}}({\bm{\Omega}})\ \Big[{\bm{E}}-{\bm{I}}\ \bm{\mathcal{O}}({\bm{\Omega}})-g\big(\bm{a}+\bm{a}^{*})\Big]\,,

(1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\quantity(1+\frac{\imu\gamma}{\omega})\ \dot{\bm{a}}=\omega{\bm{a}}+g\bm{\mathcal{O}}({\bm{\Omega}})+\bm{\Gamma}\,.

(2)

Here,
{\bm{\mathcal{O}}}({\bm{\Omega}})
denotes the
expectation values of the local electronic operators,
parameterized by the coherent state variables
{\bm{\Omega}}
;

is the phonon displacement;

{\bm{E}}
is the local energy term;

{\bm{I}}

is the Berry curvature matrix.
The phonon dynamics are governed by an
effective electron-phonon coupling strength

and thermal noise

This framework enables a unified description of nonequilibrium

In the following, we set up the generalized nonequilibriumal model

1

The total Hamiltonian is written as

II.3
Imaginary-Time Path Integral

To account for the influence of phonons, one might simply treat them
as classical variables with phenomenological damping.
However, this approach lacks a clear microscopic foundation.
To address this point properly, we begin with a fully quantum mechanical
formulation and derive the equations of motion by taking
the semiclassical limit at a later stage.

We begin by formulating the partition function as an
imaginary-time path integral.
Phonons from electrons and the bath are treated explicitly

(23)

Here,
\bm{\Omega}=\{\Omega_{i}\}
denotes the set of coherent-state
parameters across all lattice sites.
The term
\mathscr{H}_{e}(\bm{\Omega})
is the energy functional corresponding
to the electronic Hamiltonian [second line of Eq. (
18
)].
The second term in Eq. (
22
)
encodes the dissipative effects arising from the phonons and takes the form

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{\rm diss}=\sum_{\nu}\sum_{ij\xi\xi^{\prime}}\mathscr{O}_{i}^{\xi*}(\mathrm{i}\nu)\Sigma_{i\xi,j\xi^{\prime}}(\mathrm{i}\nu)\mathscr{O}_{j}^{\xi^{\prime}}(\mathrm{i}\nu)\,.

(24)

Here, the self-energy
\Sigma
is given by

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69054pt\Sigma_{i\xi,j\xi^{\prime}}(\mathrm{i}\nu)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{k}n}\Big[g_{i,\bm{k}n}^{\xi*}g_{j,\bm{k}n}^{\xi^{\prime}}G_{\bm{k}n}(\mathrm{i}\nu)+g_{i,\bm{k}n}^{\xi}g_{j,\bm{k}n}^{\xi^{\prime}*}G_{\bm{k}n}(-\mathrm{i}\nu)\Big]

(25)

where the dressed phonon Green’s function is

\displaystyle G_{\bm{k}n}(\mathrm{i}\nu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{i}\nu-\omega_{\bm{k}n}-\gamma_{\bm{k}n}|\nu|/\omega_{\bm{k}n}}\,.

(26)

This result captures both the retarded propagation of phonons and
the dissipative influence of the thermal bath, encoded through the
damping rate
\gamma_{\bm{k}n}
.
Physically, this damping term reflects the energy transfer from the system
phonons to the external environment.
While a more detailed treatment would require specifying how the system
couples to a physical reservoir (e.g., a substrate or cryostat), we
adopt this simplified, mode-resolved damping description
[see Eq. (
21
)]
as a practical and commonly used approximation.
For an idealized system, the damping can be turned off by taking
the limit
\gamma_{\bm{k}n}\to 0
.

Using the phonon Green’s function from Eq. (
26
),
we now express the dissipative term from Eq. (
24
)
in imaginary time and rewrite Eq. (
22
)
as

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{\rm eff}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{S}_{0}

\displaystyle+\int\mathrm{d}\tau\mathrm{d}\tau^{\prime}\sum_{ij\xi\xi^{\prime}}\mathscr{O}^{\xi}_{i}(\tau)\Sigma_{i\xi,j\xi^{\prime}}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\mathscr{O}^{\xi^{\prime}}_{j}(\tau^{\prime})\,,

(27)

with time dependence expressed through the
inverse Fourier transform
[cf. Eq. (
20
)].
The time dependence in the self-energy,
\Sigma(\tau-\tau^{\prime})
, captures
memory effects and dissipation
resulting from the coupling to phonons and the bath.

II.4
Real-Time Keldysh Formalism

We now consider the real-frequency representation of the self-energy
in Eq. (
27
).
The retarded self-energy is obtained by analytic continuation of the
Matsubara self-energy via
\mathrm{i}\nu\to\varepsilon+\mathrm{i}0^{+}
, for
\nu>0
,
resulting in

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\rm R}_{i\xi,j\xi^{\prime}}(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{k}n}\Big[g_{i,\bm{k}n}^{\xi*}g_{j,\bm{k}n}^{\xi^{\prime}}G^{\rm R}_{\bm{k}n}(\varepsilon)+g_{i,\bm{k}n}^{\xi}g_{j,\bm{k}n}^{\xi^{\prime}*}G^{\rm A}_{\bm{k}n}(-\varepsilon)\Big]\,.

(28)

The advanced self-energy follows from
\mathrm{i}\nu\to\varepsilon-\mathrm{i}0^{+}

for
\nu<0
, giving

\displaystyle\Sigma_{i\xi,j\xi^{\prime}}^{\rm A}(\varepsilon)=\Sigma_{j\xi^{\prime},i\xi}^{\rm R}(\varepsilon)^{*}\,.

(29)

Its corresponding real-time representation is obtained via Fourier
transform of the retarded and advanced Green’s functions

\displaystyle G^{\rm R}_{\bm{k}n}(t)=G_{\bm{k}n}^{\rm A}(-t)^{*}=-\frac{\mathrm{i}\theta(t)}{z_{\bm{k}n}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\bm{k}n}t/z_{\bm{k}n}}

(30)

with the dimensionless complex number

\displaystyle z_{\bm{k}n}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{\mathrm{i}\gamma_{\bm{k}n}}{\omega_{\bm{k}n}}\,.

(31)

\theta(t)
is the Heaviside step function, defined as

\theta(t)=1
for
t>0
and
\theta(t)=0
for
t<0
.
In the weak damping limit
\gamma_{\bm{k}n}\ll\omega_{\bm{k}n}
, this reduces
to the more familiar form

\displaystyle G_{\bm{k}n}^{\rm R}(t)\simeq-\mathrm{i}\theta(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\bm{k}n}t-\gamma_{\bm{k}n}t}\,,

(32)

confirming that

\gamma_{\bm{k}n}
indeed represents a damping rate.
The presence of
\theta(t)
ensures causality – the future evolution is
influenced only by the past.

In thermal equilibrium, the Keldysh component of the self-energy
satisfies the fluctuation-dissipation relation
Kamenev 2011

\displaystyle\Sigma_{i\xi,j\xi^{\prime}}^{\rm K}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\quantity[ \Sigma_{i\xi,j\xi'}^{\rm R} (\ep) - \Sigma_{i\xi,j\xi'}^{\rm A} (\ep) ]\coth\frac{\varepsilon}{2T}\,.

(33)

We note that this relation relies on the assumption that the phonon bath remains close to thermal equilibrium.
Equation (
33
)
can be expressed
compactly as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{i\xi,j\xi^{\prime}}^{\rm K}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{i}\sum_{\bm{k}n}\Big[g_{i,\bm{k}n}^{\xi*}g_{j,\bm{k}n}^{\xi^{\prime}}F_{\bm{k}n}(\varepsilon)+g_{i,\bm{k}n}^{\xi}g_{j,\bm{k}n}^{\xi^{\prime}*}F_{\bm{k}n}(-\varepsilon)\Big]\,,

(34)

with the spectral function
F_{\bm{k}n}(\varepsilon)
defined as

\displaystyle F_{\bm{k}n}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=-\,{\rm Im\,}G_{\bm{k}n}^{\rm R}(\varepsilon)\,\coth\frac{\varepsilon}{2T}\,.

(35)

We now transform the effective action of Eq. (
27
)
into its real-time representation.
In this form, the action consists again of two parts

\displaystyle S_{\rm eff}=S_{0}+S_{\rm diss}\,.

(36)

where
S_{0}
is the coherent-state action for the
isolated electronic system [c.f. Eq. (
23
)],
and
S_{\rm diss}
captures
dissipation induced by coupling to phonons and the thermal bath
[c.f. Eq. (
24
)].

Figure 2:

Schematic illustration of the Kelydysh contour

\mathcal{C}
as used in the integral of
Eq. (
37
).
The contour extends from
-\infty
to
+\infty

along both the forward and backward branches.

The dissipative part is written as a nonlocal-in-time interaction along the
Keldysh contour
\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle S_{\rm diss}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{C}}\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\sum_{ij\xi\xi^{\prime}}\mathscr{O}^{\xi}_{i}(t)\tilde{\Sigma}_{i\xi,j\xi^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})\mathscr{O}^{\xi^{\prime}}_{j}(t^{\prime})\,,

(37)

where
\tilde{\Sigma}
is the contour-ordered self-energy kernel.
The coherent-state fields are defined along the forward and backward branches
of the contour

\Omega_{i}^{+}
for
(-\infty,\cdots,+\infty)
and

\Omega_{i}^{-}
for
(+\infty,\cdots,-\infty)
as shown in Fig.
2
.

Applying the standard Keldysh rotation
Kamenev 2011
, we define

classical

(\rm cl)
and
quantum mechanical

(\rm qm)
components of the
coherent state variables and local electronic observables

\displaystyle\Omega_{i}^{\rm cl}

\displaystyle=\left(\Omega_{i}^{+}+\Omega_{i}^{-}\right)/2\,,

(38)

\displaystyle\Omega_{i}^{\rm qm}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{i}^{+}-\Omega_{i}^{-}\,,

(39)

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}_{i}^{\xi,{\rm cl}}

\displaystyle=\quantity[ \mathscr O^{\rm\xi} (\Omega_i^+)
+ \mathscr O^{\rm\xi} (\Omega_i^-) ]/2\,,

(40)

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}_{i}^{\xi,{\rm qm}}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{O}^{\rm\xi}(\Omega_{i}^{+})-\mathscr{O}^{\rm\xi}(\Omega_{i}^{-})\,.

(41)

That is, the classical components are defined as the average of
the forward and backward fields on the Keldysh contour,
while the quantum components correspond to their difference.

Using this rotation and the Matsubara-to-Keldysh
correspondence
Kamenev 2011
, we rewrite both the system action
and the dissipative term.
The total effective action becomes

\displaystyle S_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hskip-5.69054pt\mathrm{d}t\sum_{s=\pm}\left[s\sum_{i\alpha}c_{\alpha}^{*}\quantity( \Omega_i^{\rm cl}+ \tfrac 1 2 s\Omega_i^{\rm qm})\partial_{t}c_{\alpha}\quantity(\Omega_i^{\rm cl}+ \tfrac 1 2 s\Omega_i^{\rm qm})+s\mathrm{i}\ {\mathscr{H}}_{e}\quantity(
\bm\Omega^{\rm cl}
+ \tfrac 1 2 s \bm\Omega^{\rm qm}
)\right]

(42)

\displaystyle S_{\rm diss}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{ij\xi\xi^{\prime}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\ \mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{2}\mathscr{O}^{\xi,{\rm cl}}_{i}&\mathscr{O}^{\xi,{\rm qm}}_{i}/\sqrt{2}\end{pmatrix}_{t}\begin{pmatrix}0&\Sigma^{\rm A}_{i\xi,j\xi^{\prime}}\\[5.69054pt]
\Sigma^{\rm R}_{i\xi,j\xi^{\prime}}&\Sigma^{\rm K}_{i\xi,j\xi^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix}_{tt^{\prime}}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{2}\mathscr{O}^{\xi^{\prime},{\rm cl}}_{j}\\
\mathscr{O}^{\xi^{\prime},{\rm qm}}_{j}/\sqrt{2}\end{pmatrix}_{t^{\prime}}

(43)

This Keldysh-rotated form of the action makes causality manifest: the
retarded and advanced components encode dissipative response, while
the Keldysh component governs fluctuations
Kamenev 2011
.
Importantly, this expression is fully quantum mechanical – no approximations
have been made at this stage.

II.5
Equations of Motion in the Semiclassical Limit

We now introduce the semiclassical approximation
in our formalism.
The real-time dynamics are governed by the
partition function

\displaystyle Z=\int\mathscr{D}[\Omega]\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\hbar^{-1}S_{\rm eff}}\,,

(44)

which plays the role of the time-evolution
operator in the Keldysh path-integral formalism.
We rescale the quantum component in the functional
integral as

\Omega^{\rm qm}\to\hbar\Omega^{\rm qm}
.
This makes the expansion in
\hbar

equivalent to an expansion in
\Omega^{\rm qm}
,
as discribed in Ref.
Kamenev 2011
.
We then expand the classical and quantum mechanical
observables for electrons as

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}_{i}^{\xi,\rm cl}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{O}^{\xi}(\Omega_{i}^{\rm cl})+O\quantity( (\Omega^{\rm qm})^{1} )\,,

(45)

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}_{i}^{\xi,\rm qm}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}\frac{\partial\mathscr{O}^{\xi}(\Omega_{i}^{\rm cl})}{\partial\Omega_{ip}^{\rm cl}}\Omega_{ip}^{\rm qm}+O\quantity( (\Omega^{\rm qm})^{2} )\,,

(46)

where the
index
p
labels the

2(N-1)
components of
\Omega_{i}
[see Eq. (
16
)].
Substituting the leading-order terms
from Eqs. (
45
) and (
46
)
into
S_{0}
[Eq. (
42
)], we obtain the
semiclassical form of the action for
the isolated electronic system

\displaystyle S_{0}\simeq\int\mathrm{d}t\sum_{ip}\bigg(\sum_{q}\ \mathrm{i}B_{pq}(\Omega_{i}^{\rm cl})\partial_{t}\Omega_{iq}^{\rm cl}-\sum_{i}\frac{\partial{\mathscr{H}}_{e}(\bm{\Omega}^{\rm cl})}{\partial\Omega_{ip}^{\rm cl}}\bigg)\Omega^{\rm qm}_{ip}\,,

(47)

with the Berry curvature matrix defined as

\displaystyle B_{pq}(\Omega_{i})=\sum_{\alpha}\quantity[ \frac{\partial c^*_\al(\Omega_{i})}{\partial\Omega_{ip}} \frac{\partial c_\al(\Omega_{i})}{\partial\Omega_{iq}} - \frac{\partial c^*_\al(\Omega_{i})}{\partial\Omega_{iq}}\frac{\partial c_\al(\Omega_{i})}{\partial\Omega_{ip}} ]\,.

(48)

We divide the dissipative part of the action,

S_{\rm diss}
[see Eq. (
43
)], into
its retarded/advanced and Keldysh components

\displaystyle S_{\rm diss}=S^{\rm RA}_{\rm diss}+S^{\rm K}_{\rm diss}\,.

(49)

The first term,
S^{\rm RA}_{\rm diss}
, captures
dissipation and involves the real part of the retarded
self-energy

\displaystyle S^{\rm RA}_{\rm diss}=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{ij\xi\xi^{\prime}}\int\mathrm{d}t\ \mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\mathscr{O}_{i}^{\xi,{\rm qm}}(t)\ {\rm Re}\big[\Sigma^{\rm R}_{i\xi,j\xi^{\prime}}(t,t^{\prime})\big]\ \mathscr{O}_{j}^{\xi^{\prime},{\rm cl}}(t^{\prime})\,,

(50)

where we used the complex-conjugate relation
between
\Sigma^{\rm R}
and
\Sigma^{\rm A}

[see Eq. (
29
)].
The quadratic terms in
\Omega^{\rm qm}

give rise to
thermal noise, captured by
the Keldysh part of the action.
The Keldysh component is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}S_{\rm diss}^{\rm K}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\bm{k}n}\int\mathrm{d}\varepsilon\ F_{\bm{k}n}(\varepsilon)|V^{\rm qm}_{\bm{k}n}(\varepsilon)|^{2}\,,

(51)

\displaystyle V_{\bm{k}n}^{\alpha}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\mathrm{d}t\ \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varepsilon t}\sum_{i\xi}g_{i,\bm{k}n}^{\xi}\mathscr{O}_{i}^{\xi,\alpha}(t)\,,

(52)

with
\alpha=\rm\{qm,cl\}
, and

F_{\bm{k}n}(\varepsilon)

defined in Eq. (
35
).

To linearize the Keldysh part
in
\Omega^{\rm qm}
, we apply a
Stratonovich-Hubbard (SH) transformation

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}S_{\rm diss}^{\rm K}}

\displaystyle=\int\mathscr{D}[\zeta]\exp\quantity[ - \sum_{\bm kn} \int\diff\ep\ \frac{|\zeta_{\bm kn}(\ep)|^2}{F_{\bm kn}(\ep)} ]\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\tilde{S}_{\rm diss}^{\rm K}[\zeta]}\,,

(53)

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{\rm diss}^{\rm K}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{k}n}\int\mathrm{d}\varepsilon\big[\zeta_{\bm{k}n}^{*}(\varepsilon)V^{\rm qm}_{\bm{k}n}(\varepsilon)+\zeta_{\bm{k}n}(\varepsilon)V^{{\rm qm}*}_{\bm{k}n}(\varepsilon)\big]\,.

(54)

For convergence of the Gaussian integral,
it is essential that
F_{\bm{k}n}(\varepsilon)>0
,
which can be verified using
Eqs. (
26
) and (
35
).

We define the Fourier transformation of the SH field from
frequency to time

\displaystyle\zeta_{\bm{k}n}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\mathrm{d}\varepsilon\ \zeta_{\bm{k}n}(\varepsilon)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon t}\,.

(55)

With this, the Keldysh action becomes

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{\rm diss}^{\rm K}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\xi}\int\mathrm{d}t\ \mathscr{O}_{i}^{\xi,\rm qm}(t)\sum_{\bm{k}n}\quantity[ g_{i,\bm kn}^\xi\zeta^*_{\bm kn}(t) + {\rm c.c.}]\,.

(56)

We define the average as

\displaystyle\langle\cdots\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\int\mathscr{D}\zeta(\cdots)\exp\quantity[ - \sum_{\bm kn} \int\diff\ep\frac{|\zeta_{\bm kn}(\ep)|^2}{F_{\bm kn}(\ep)} ]}{\int\mathscr{D}\zeta\exp\quantity[ - \sum_{\bm kn} \int\diff\ep\frac{|\zeta_{\bm kn}(\ep)|^2}{F_{\bm kn}(\ep)} ]}\,,

(57)

and obtain the noise spectrum

\displaystyle\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}n}^{*}(\varepsilon)\zeta_{\bm{k}n}(\varepsilon^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=F_{\bm{k}n}(\varepsilon)\delta(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime})\,.

(58)

Let us now combine all components to derive the
semiclassical equation of motion.
The partition function takes the form

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\int\mathscr{D}[\zeta]\exp\quantity[ - \sum_{\bm kn} \int\diff\ep\frac{ |\zeta_{\bm kn}(\ep)|^2}{F_{\bm kn}(\ep)} ]\int\mathscr{D}[\Omega^{\rm cl},\Omega^{\rm qm}]\ \exp[\mathrm{i}\sum_{ip}\int\mathrm{d}t\ \Omega^{\rm qm}_{ip}\ \mathcal{X}_{ip}(t)\Bigg]\,,

(59)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{ip}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{q}\mathrm{i}B_{pq}(\Omega_{i}^{\rm cl})\dot{\Omega}_{iq}^{\rm cl}-\frac{\partial{\mathscr{H}}_{e}(\bm{\Omega}^{\rm cl})}{\partial\Omega_{ip}^{\rm cl}}+\sum_{\xi}\frac{\partial\mathscr{O}^{\xi,{\rm cl}}_{i}}{\partial\Omega_{ip}^{\rm cl}}\quantity{
2 \sum_{j\xi'}
\int\diff t'
\ {\rm Re} \left[ \Sigma^{\rm R}_{i\xi,j\xi'} (t,t') \right] \ \mathscr O_j^{\xi',{\rm cl}} (t')
+ \sum_{\bm kn}\qty( g_{i,\bm kn}^\xi\zeta^*_{\bm kn}(t) + {\rm c.c.})
}\,.

(60)

This form of the partition function reflects an underlying
mathematical structure, similar to the well-known delta function
identity

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}q\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}qx}=2\pi\delta(x)
,
where only the contribution at
x=0
survives.
Analogously, in our case, the functional integral

\int\mathscr{D}[\Omega^{\rm qm}]\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Omega^{\rm qm}\mathcal{X}}
,
is most significant for a configuration where

\mathcal{X}_{ip}(t)=0
.
This condition defines the dominant semiclassical
path within the partition function.
Applying this reasoning, the semiclassical equation of
motion becomes

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\sum_{q}B_{pq}(\Omega_{i})\dot{\Omega}_{iq}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi}\frac{\partial\mathscr{O}^{\xi}_{i}}{\partial\Omega_{ip}}\quantity[
E_i^\xi-
\sum_{j\xi'}
\bigg(
I_{ij}^{\xi\xi'} \mathscr O_j^{\xi'}
+
2 \int_{-\infty}^{t} \diff t'
\ \ {\rm Re}\left[ \Sigma^{\rm R}_{i\xi,j\xi'} (t-t') \right] \ \mathscr O_j^{\xi'} (t')
\bigg)
- \sum_{\bm kn} \qty(g_{i,\bm kn}^\xi\zeta_{\bm kn}^* + {\rm c.c.})
]\,,

(61)

where we have omitted the symbol “cl”
for brevity and display only the
t^{\prime}
-dependence
explicitly.
The single-site energy term
E_{i}^{\xi}

may, in general, be time-dependent, which allows the
treatment of localized electron dynamics in
the presence of time-dependent external fields.
The retarded self-energy
\Sigma^{\rm R}
encodes both
phonon-mediated interactions and dissipation
effects.
The stochastic term involving
\zeta_{\bm{k}n}(t)

corresponds to thermal noise, with statistics
given by Eq. (
58
).

Note that the self-energy
\Sigma^{\rm R}(t-t^{\prime})
in Eq. (
61
)
introduces memory effects, making the dynamics inherently
non-Markovian.
This nonlocality in time poses significant challenges for numerical simulation.
Fortunately, the non-Markovian problem can be mapped onto an
equivalent Markovian process, as we will outline in detail in
the next section.

Finally, we remark that our approach recovers known results in
appropriate limits.
In particular, for coherent states of SU(2), the semiclassical
dynamics reduce to the standard stochastic LLG
equation, as shall be demonstrated in detail in Sec.
V.2
.

III
From Non-Markov to Markov

In actual numerical calculations, Markov processes–whose
evolution depends only on the current state and not on the
full history–are much easier to handle.
However, the
noise spectrum in Eq. (
58
)
and the retarded self-energy in Eq. (
61
)
introduce memory effects, resulting in a non-Markovian process.
For certain classes of stochastic processes (a well-known
example is the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process
Risken 1996
),
it is established that non-Markovian dynamics can be mapped
onto an equivalent Markovian representation by introducing
auxiliary variables
Marchesoni and Grigolini 1983
;
Łuczka 2005
;
Ceriotti et al. 2009
.
A similar strategy can be applied to our equations of motion,
enabling a reformulation in which auxiliary semiclassical
degrees of freedom absorb the memory, as outlined below.

III.1
Retarded interaction

A Markov-type description can be constructed
for the retarded term

\Sigma^{\rm R}
in the equation of motion (
61
).
It can be rewritten as

\displaystyle 2\sum_{j\xi^{\prime}}\int_{-\infty}^{t}\hskip-5.69054pt

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\ {\rm Re}\left[\Sigma^{\rm R}_{i\xi,j\xi^{\prime}}(t-t^{\prime})\right]\ \mathscr{O}_{j}^{\xi^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{k}n}g_{i,\bm{k}n}^{\xi*}A_{\bm{k}n}(t)+{\rm c.c.}\,,

(62)

where we have defined the auxiliary variable

\displaystyle A_{\bm{k}n}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}G^{\rm R}_{\bm{k}n}(t-t^{\prime})V^{\rm cl}_{\bm{k}n}(t^{\prime})\,.

(63)

Here,
V^{\rm cl}_{\bm{k}n}(t)

is the inverse Fourier transform of Eq. (
52
),
giving its time-domain representation

\displaystyle V^{\rm cl}_{\bm{k}n}(t)=\sum_{i\xi}g_{i,\bm{k}n}^{\xi}\mathscr{O}_{i}^{\xi}(t)\,.

(64)

One can verify by direct differentiation that
A_{\bm{k}n}

satisfies the first-order differential equation

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}z_{\bm{k}n}\dot{A}_{\bm{k}n}=\omega_{\bm{k}n}A_{\bm{k}n}+V_{\bm{k}n}^{\rm cl}(t)\,,

(65)

where
z_{\bm{k}n}
and
\omega_{\bm{k}n}
are the same
parameters appearing in the phonon Green’s function
[Eq. (
30
)].
Physically, each auxiliary variable
A_{\bm{k}n}
represents a
dynamical phonon mode that effectively mediates interactions
between electrons.
In this way, the memory integral is replaced by auxiliary
variables, reducing the problem to a Markov process
without explicit dependence on the full history.

III.2
Approximation for noise

III.2.1
Colored noise

Next, we analyze the properties of the noise appearing in
the equations of motion.
In general, the process is non-Markovian because the noise
has long-time correlations

\displaystyle\langle\zeta_{\bm{k}n}^{*}(t)\zeta_{\bm{k}n}(t^{\prime})\rangle=F_{\bm{k}n}(t-t^{\prime})\,,

(66)

where
F_{\bm{k}n}(t-t^{\prime})
is the Fourier transform of
the spectral function defined in Eq. (
58
).
This non-local correlation implies that the noise
is
colored
and depends explicitly on the full time history,
which complicates numerical integration.

Fortunately, in certain limits, the correlation function becomes local
by considering the noise in an alternative representation.
Without loss of generality, the same correlation function is
reproduced by introducing the following auxiliary stochastic
differential equation

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}z_{\bm{k}n}\dot{\zeta}_{\bm{k}n}=\omega_{\bm{k}n}\zeta_{\bm{k}n}+\Gamma_{\bm{k}n}(t)\,,

(67)

where the spectrum of the newly introduced noise term

\Gamma_{\bm{k}n}
is defined by

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma^{*}_{\bm{k}n}(\varepsilon)\Gamma_{\bm{k}n}(\varepsilon^{\prime})\rangle=\frac{\gamma_{\bm{k}n}\varepsilon}{\omega_{\bm{k}n}}\coth\frac{\varepsilon}{2T}\ \delta(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime})\,.

(68)

Since Eq. (
67
) closely resembles Eq. (
65
),

\zeta_{\bm{k}n}
can be interpreted as describing phonon degrees
of freedom associated with coupling to the bath.
Note that the prefactor in Eq. (
68
) is
directly related to the imaginary part of the phonon self-energy,

-{\rm Im\,}\Pi_{\bm{k}n,\bm{k}n}(\varepsilon+\mathrm{i}0^{+})
,
defined in Eq. (
21
).

The equation of motion is then rewritten as

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\sum_{q}B_{pq}(\Omega_{i})\dot{\Omega}_{iq}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi}\frac{\partial\mathscr{O}^{\xi}_{i}}{\partial\Omega_{ip}}\quantity[
E_i^\xi-
\sum_{j\xi'}
I_{ij}^{\xi\xi'} \mathscr O_j^{\xi'}
- \sum_{\bm kn} \qty(g_{i,\bm kn}^\xi a_{\bm kn}^*
+ {\rm c.c.})
]\,,

(69a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}z_{\bm{k}n}\dot{a}_{\bm{k}n}=\omega_{\bm{k}n}a_{\bm{k}n}+V_{\bm{k}n}^{\rm cl}(t)+\Gamma_{\bm{k}n}(t)\,,

(69b)

where we have introduced the effective phonon variable

\displaystyle a_{\bm{k}n}=A_{\bm{k}n}+\zeta_{\bm{k}n}\,,

(70)

and used the complex constant

z_{\bm{k}n}=1+\mathrm{i}\gamma_{\bm{k}n}/\omega_{\bm{k}n}

as defined in Eq. (
31
).
While concise and deceptively simple in form, Eq. (
69b
)
can be shown to reduce to the familiar equation of motion for a
forced damped harmonic oscillator in the limit
\gamma_{\bm{k}n}\ll\omega_{\bm{k}n}
,
as demonstrated in Appendix
A
.
This confirms that solving the above equations yields physically
reasonable and consistent dynamics.
The corresponding Fokker-Planck equation is also derived in Appendix
B
.

III.2.2
White noise

The equation of motion introduced above is still
non-Markovian, and therefore we consider an approximation.
If we explicitly restore the Planck constant
\hbar
,
the semiclassical limit corresponds to
\hbar\to 0
.
In this limit, it is natural to assume high temperatures
Kamenev 2011
.
More precisely, the condition
\hbar\to 0
is equivalent to

\hbar\omega^{*}\ll k_{\rm B}T
, where
\omega^{*}

denotes a characteristic energy scale of the system.
In this regime, the correlation function of the noise

\Gamma
in Eq. (
68
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma_{\bm{k}n}^{*}(t)\Gamma_{\bm{k}n}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{2\gamma_{\bm{k}n}T}{\omega_{\bm{k}n}}\delta(t-t^{\prime})+O(T^{-1})\,,

(71)

in real-time representation.
This expression is local in time, corresponding to a constant
spectrum in frequency space (
white
noise), as in the standard
Langevin equation.
Consequently, the originally non-Markovian problem is mapped
onto a Markovian one for both the retarded and Keldysh components,
and the equation of motion no longer depends on its history.

To summarize, while the original equation of motion in
Eq. (
61
) together with Eq. (
58
)
describes a non-Markovian system that is numerically demanding
to simulate, the problem can be generically reduced to a
Markovian form by employing the auxiliary
equations for the phonon and electronic variables defined in
Eq. (
69
), together with the
high-temperature noise correlation in Eq. (
71
).
Given a Gaussian noise
\Gamma_{\bm{k}n}(t)

one can compute any physical quantities as functions
of
\Omega_{ip}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
and
a_{\bm{k}n}(t)\in\mathbb{C}

at each time step.

A systematic improvement that incorporates quantum fluctuations is,
in principle, straightforward: it can be achieved by retaining
higher-order terms in the quantum component
\Omega^{\rm qm}

defined in Eq. (
39
).
Although including such corrections is practically more complicated,
it allows a more accurate description.
Regarding the noise, one must then consider the full spectrum in
Eq. (
68
) without invoking the high-temperature
approximation.
This spectrum has nontrivial frequency dependence (colored noise),
which partly encodes the quantum fluctuations.
While calculations in this direction are significantly more complicated
due to the history dependence of the dynamics (non-Markov problem),
they are worth pursuing to elucidate the role of quantum fluctuations
more rigorously.
A more detailed discussion and an outlook on future directions
are given in Sec.
VI
.

Numerical benchmark

In the previous section, we reformulated the general electron-phonon
equations of motion in Eq. (
61
) as a Markov process
in the semiclassical limit with high-temperature noise correlation
(white noise) [see Eq. (
69
)].
In this section, we explicitly solve the equations of motion in
Eq. (
69
) to benchmark both the formalism and its
numerical implementation.

IV.1
Two-orbital spin chain

We consider a two-orbital spin chain described by
the following microscopic Hamiltonian for the electronic degrees of
freedom

\displaystyle\hat{\mathscr{H}_{e}}

\displaystyle=-J^{\prime}\sum_{i}\hat{\bm{s}}_{i1}\cdot\hat{\bm{s}}_{i2}-2J\sum_{i}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{2}\hat{\bm{s}}_{i\alpha}\cdot\hat{\bm{s}}_{i+1,\alpha}\,,

(72)

where
\hat{\bm{s}}_{i\alpha}
denotes a
spin–
1/2
operator at
orbital
\alpha=1,2
on site
i
.
We focus on the limit where the intra-site interaction
J^{\prime}>0
is
ferromagnetic and dominant compared to the inter-site
interaction
J
, i.e.,
J^{\prime}\gg|J|
.
In this limit, spin-triplet states are energetically
favored,
making the triplet sector the relevant subspace
for describing the system’s low-energy physics.
Projecting out the singlet states using the projector

P_{t}=\prod_{i}(\frac{3}{4}+\hat{\bm{s}}_{i1}\cdot\hat{\bm{s}}_{i2})
,
we obtain the effective low-energy Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{e}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=P_{t}\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{e}P_{t}=-J\sum_{i}\hat{\bm{S}}_{i}\cdot\hat{\bm{S}}_{i+1}\,.

(73)

Here,
\hat{\bm{S}}_{i}
denotes an effective spin–1 operator acting
within the triplet subspace at site
i
.
It satisfies the spin-length relation
\bm{S}_{i}^{2}=S(S+1)=2
,
and spans a local Hilbert space with SU
(3)
structure.

We consider localized phonons that couple to the
time-reversal symmetric components of the electronic moments at
each site–namely, the quadrupolar degrees of freedom
defined by

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{3z^{2}-r^{2}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left[3(\hat{S}_{z})^{2}-S(S+1)\right]\,,

(74)

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{x^{2}-y^{2}}

\displaystyle=(\hat{S}_{x})^{2}-(\hat{S}_{y})^{2}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{xy}

\displaystyle=\hat{S}_{x}\hat{S}_{y}+\hat{S}_{y}\hat{S}_{x}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{yz}

\displaystyle=\hat{S}_{y}\hat{S}_{z}+\hat{S}_{z}\hat{S}_{y}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{zx}

\displaystyle=\hat{S}_{z}\hat{S}_{x}+\hat{S}_{x}\hat{S}_{z}\,.

The part of the Hamiltonian that contains both the phonon energy and
the spin-phonon coupling is given respectively by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{p}

\displaystyle=\omega_{0}\sum_{i\xi}\hat{a}_{i\xi}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i\xi}\,,

(75)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{ep}

\displaystyle=g\sum_{i\xi}(\hat{a}_{i\xi}+\hat{a}_{i\xi}^{\dagger})\hat{Q}_{i\xi}\,,

(76)

where
\hat{a}_{i\xi}
denote optical Einstein phonon modes at site
i
, each
with constant frequency
\omega_{0}
(no dispersion), and five fluctuating
components labeled by
\xi=\{3z^{2}-r^{2},x^{2}-y^{2},xy,yz,zx\}
.
These phonon modes couple to the five linearly independent quadrupolar
components in Eq. (
74
)
of the electrons via the spin-phonon coupling strength
g
.
For simplicity, we neglect any anisotropy, such that phonons have
only one characteristic eigenfrequency and coupling constant.
A similar
S=1
model coupled to
yz
- and
zx
-type phonons is also studied in Ref.
Sutcliffe et al. 2025
.

In this setup, the generalized equations of motion from
Eq. (
69
),
applied to the effective spin–
1
chain, take the explicit form

\displaystyle\dot{\Omega}_{ip}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\sum_{q=1}^{4}B^{-1}_{pq}(\Omega_{i})\quantity[
J \frac{\partial\bm{S} (\Omega_i)}{\partial\Omega_{iq} }
\cdot\sum_{j\in{\rm NN}} \bm{S} (\Omega_j)
- g \sum_{\xi}
\frac{\partial Q_{\xi}(\Omega_i)}{\partial\Omega_{iq} }
\qty(a_{i\xi} + a_{i\xi}^* )
]\,,

(77a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}z\dot{a}_{i\xi}

\displaystyle=\omega_{0}a_{i\xi}+gQ_{\xi}(\Omega_{i})+\Gamma_{i\xi}(t)\,.

(77b)

These two coupled differential equations describe the real-time
dynamics of the electronic and phononic degrees of freedom.
The damping of the phonons is captured by the complex parameter

z=1+\mathrm{i}\gamma/\omega_{0}
[see Eq. (
31
)].

We model the stochastic temperature fluctuations
\Gamma_{i\xi}(t)

as white noise [see definition in Eq. (
71
)]

\displaystyle\langle\Gamma^{*}_{i\xi}(t)\Gamma_{j\xi^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\rangle=\frac{2\gamma T}{\omega_{0}}\delta_{ij}\delta_{\xi\xi^{\prime}}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\,.

(78)

While we here adopt white noise to mimic thermal fluctuations in their
high-temperature limit, other forms of noise–such as colored noise–can
in principle be implemented as well (see discussion
in Sec.
III.2
).

In numerical simulations, we discretize time as

t_{n}=n\ \varDelta t
and
integrate the deterministic parts of
Eq. (
77
) using
a fourth-order Runge-Kutta method
Hairer et al. 1993
.
The stochastic fluctuations are treated via the Euler method

\displaystyle\Gamma_{i\xi}(t_{n})=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma T}{\omega_{0}\varDelta t}}\left(X_{i\xi n}+\mathrm{i}Y_{i\xi n}\right)\,,

(79)

where
X_{i\xi n}
and
Y_{i\xi n}
are independent real-valued Gaussian
random variables with mean
\mu=0
and variance
\sigma^{2}=1
.

We represent the electronic coherent states
|\Omega_{i}\rangle
[see Eq. (
12
)] using the
parametrization given in Eq. (
13
)
Iwazaki et al. 2023
,
and provide an explicit representation in SU(3) for the present
problem in Appendix
C.2
.
As shown in Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
),
the parametrization of
c_{\alpha}(\Omega)
is restricted to
specific parameter domains which, in general, are not preserved
when numerically solving the equations of motion.
Nevertheless, as we show in Appendix
C.3
,
this does not pose any practical issues in simulations, since different
domains are related by internal gauge transformations and do
not distort the underlying physics.

explored the dynamical consequences of electron-phonon interactions,
we now turn to examine
how they relate to the commonly used
LLG equations in the next section.

V
Relation to LLG equation

Stochastic LLG equations have been widely used to explore
unconventional dynamical phenomena in strongly correlated
electron systems.
It is of general interest to understand how the electron-phonon
Langevin dynamics (epLD) derived in this work relate to
the traditional LLG equations.
In this section, we demonstrate that our epLD framework
recovers
the traditional LLG equations of motion in a
specific limit when localized electrons are
coupled to acoustic phonons.

V.1
Equations of motion for electrons coupled to acoustic phonons

We consider a specific form of the electron-phonon system,
where the
\bm{k}
-dependence of the coupling constant is assumed
to be

\displaystyle g_{i,\bm{k}n}^{\xi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{V}}h^{\xi}_{n}k^{\alpha_{n}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}_{i}}\,,

(92)

where
V
is a system volume, and
k=|\bm{k}|
.

\bm{R}_{i}
denotes the position of site
i
, and
h_{n}^{\xi}
is a constant
characterizing the coupling between the phonon mode
n
and the
electronic observable

\mathscr{O}_{i}^{\xi}
.
The exponent
\alpha_{n}
determines the nature of the electron-phonon coupling in the long-wavelength limit.
The corresponding dispersion relation for the acoustic phonon is

\displaystyle\omega_{\bm{k}n}

\displaystyle=c_{n}k\,,

(93)

describing the low-energy dissipative dynamics with

c_{n}
the speed of the acoustic phonon mode.

We consider the limit of small phonon damping in this section for simplicity (
\gamma_{\bm{k}n}\to 0
).
By performing the angle integral in
\bm{k}
-space, where the wave-vector sum is represented as
\frac{1}{V}\sum_{\bm{k}}=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}
, the retarded part in Eq. (
28
) is then given by

\displaystyle\Sigma_{i\xi,j\xi^{\prime}}^{\rm R}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta(t)}{8\pi^{2}r}\sum_{n}h_{n}^{\xi*}h_{n}^{\xi^{\prime}}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}=\pm}\sigma\sigma^{\prime}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \times\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}k\,k^{2\alpha_{n}+1}\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\sigma c_{n}kt+\sigma^{\prime}kr)}\,,

(94)

where we have defined
r=|\bm{R}_{i}-\bm{R}_{j}|
.
The Keldysh part can be obtained by utilizing the fluctuation-dissipation theorem in Eq. (
33
).

Now we specify the exponent
\alpha_{n}
. For this purpose, we observe that damping in Langevin dynamics usually takes the form of a first-order time derivative of the mechanical variables.
This corresponds to the retarded self-energy of the form

\Sigma^{\rm R}(t)\sim\delta^{\prime}(t)
.
As shown below, this functional form is derived for an
exponent

\alpha_{n}=-1/2
.
Although this choice may not be fully justified in light of the discussion of realistic electron–phonon coupling in Appendix
F
, it enables us to derive standard LLG-like equations and to better understand the structure of our equation of motion.

The retarded self-energy for
\alpha_{n}=-1/2
is evaluated to give

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\rm R}_{i\xi,j\xi^{\prime}}(t-t^{\prime})=\left\{\begin{matrix}\displaystyle\sum_{n}\frac{h_{in}^{\xi*}h_{in}^{\xi^{\prime}}}{4\pi c_{n}^{2}}\delta^{\prime}(t-t^{\prime})&\ \ (i=j)\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{n}\frac{h_{in}^{\xi*}h_{jn}^{\xi^{\prime}}}{4\pi c_{n}|\bm{R}_{i}-\bm{R}_{j}|}\delta\quantity( t-t'- \frac{|\bm R_i-\bm R_j|}{c_n} )&\ \ (i\neq j)\end{matrix}\right.

(95)

The local term (
i=j
) has the same form as the Gilbert damping as it is proportional to
\delta^{\prime}(t-t^{\prime})
.
The non-local term (
i\neq j
) shows that the effect at time
t
is caused by the event at time
t^{\prime}=t-|\bm{R}_{i}-\bm{R}_{j}|/c_{n}
.
If the phonon propagation is much faster than the localized electron dynamics, the retardation effect may be neglected (
c_{n}\to\infty
).
Then the electronic interaction is mediated by phonons and contributes to the interaction parameter
I_{ij}^{\xi\xi^{\prime}}
.

Next, we consider the noise properties.
The Keldysh part at high temperature is associated with the retarded part as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Sigma_{i\xi,j\xi^{\prime}}^{\rm K}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-2\mathrm{i}T\quantity[ \Sigma_{i\xi,j\xi'}^{\rm R}(t-t') - \Sigma_{j\xi',i\xi}^{\rm R}(t'-t) ]\,,

(96)

This is evaluated to give

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\rm K}_{i\xi,j\xi^{\prime}}(t-t^{\prime})=\left\{\begin{matrix}\displaystyle-\frac{\mathrm{i}T}{2\pi}\sum_{n}\frac{h_{in}^{\xi*}h_{jn}^{\xi^{\prime}}}{c_{n}^{2}}\delta(t-t^{\prime})&\ \ (i=j)\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle-\frac{\mathrm{i}T}{2\pi|\bm{R}_{i}-\bm{R}_{j}|}\sum_{n}\frac{h_{in}^{\xi*}h_{jn}^{\xi^{\prime}}}{c_{n}}\theta\big(c_{n}|t-t^{\prime}|-|\bm{R}_{i}-\bm{R}_{j}|\big)&\ \ (i\neq j)\end{matrix}\right.

(97)

The local part (
i=j
) gives a white noise acting on localized electrons.
The non-local part is also regarded as a noise, where the inter-site correlations are limited by
|\bm{R}_{i}-\bm{R}_{j}|<c_{n}|t-t^{\prime}|
due to the phonon propagation with finite velocity
c_{n}
.

If we keep only the spatially local part as a dominant contribution to the electronic dynamics, the
equation of motion is now obtained:

\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\sum_{q}B_{pq}(\Omega_{i})\dot{\Omega}_{iq}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi}\frac{\partial\mathcal{O}^{\xi}_{i}}{\partial\Omega_{ip}}\quantity[
E_i^\xi-
\sum_{j\xi'}
I_{ij}^{\xi\xi'} \mathcal O_j^{\xi'}
- \sum_{\xi'} K_{\xi\xi'} \frac{\partial\mathcal O_i^{\xi'}}{\partial t}
+ \eta_i^\xi(t)
]\,,

(98a)

\displaystyle\big\langle\,\eta_{i}^{\xi}(t)\,\eta_{j}^{\xi^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})\,\big\rangle

\displaystyle=2TK_{\xi\xi^{\prime}}\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime})\,,

(98b)

where
\eta_{i}^{\xi}(t)
is a random Gaussian field described by a real number.
We have defined

\displaystyle K_{\xi\xi^{\prime}}=\sum_{n}\frac{h_{n}^{\xi*}h_{n}^{\xi^{\prime}}}{4\pi c_{n}^{2}}
.
In this way, by employing several assumptions, we have derived the equation of motion for electrons with Markov process.
As demonstrated in the next subsection, this equation can be associated with the standard LLG equation.

Comparison with LLG equation

V.2.1
Equation of motion for SU(2)

For a direct comparison with the LLG equations, we now express

concrete representation.
To facilitate this comparison, recall that in the LLG framework
the classical spin moment is parameterized by two variables: the polar

First of all, we define the damping matrix

V.2.2
LLG equation

The LLG equation is considered for the magnetization (
\bm{M}
) dynamics:

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{M}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{s}\bm{M}\times\bm{H}+\alpha_{s}\bm{M}\times\frac{d\bm{M}}{dt}

(105)

where
\gamma_{s}
is a gyromagnetic ratio and
\alpha_{s}
is a damping coefficient.
We may include the interaction and noise in the effective field
\bm{H}
, and the resulting equation of motion is known as the stochastic LLG
equation
Aharoni 2000
;
Aron et al. 2014
;
Aron et al. 2016
.

The magnetic moment is expressed by polar angle
\theta\in[0,\pi]
and azimuthal angle
\phi\in[0,2\pi)
:
\bm{M}=(M_{x},M_{y},M_{z})=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta)
with the constraint
|\bm{M}|=1
.
We can then rewrite it as

\displaystyle\sin\theta\dot{\phi}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{s}(H_{z}\sin\theta-H_{x}\cos\theta\cos\phi-H_{y}\cos\theta\sin\phi)+\alpha_{s}\dot{\theta}

(106)

\displaystyle\sin\theta\dot{\theta}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{s}\sin\theta(-H_{x}\sin\phi+H_{y}\cos\phi)-\alpha_{s}\sin^{2}\theta\dot{\phi}

(107)

Comparing this set of equations with those in the previous subsection, there is one-to-one correspondence between SU(2)–epLD in the previous subsection and stochastic LLG equation as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\text{\bf SU(2)--epLD}\\[2.84526pt]
\pi-2x\\
\varphi\\
(E^{1},E^{2},E^{3})\\
\gamma\end{matrix}\ \ \longleftrightarrow\ \ \begin{matrix}\text{\bf Stochastic LLG }\\[2.84526pt]
\theta\\
\phi\\
\gamma_{s}(-H_{z},H_{x},H_{y})/\sqrt{2}\\
\alpha_{s}\\
\end{matrix}

(108)

The formal equivalence of SU(2)–epLD and stochastic LLG equation demonstrates the validity of our approach to localized electron dynamics.
However, we note that the SU(2) formalism is not directly applied to spin systems, since spin is a magnetic degree of freedom and cannot be linearly coupled to phonons.
For the application to the spin systems,

N\geq 3
is required.
If we are still interested in the SU(2) case, the corresponding system can be a non-Kramers doublet system
Hart et al. 2025
, where
\mathcal{O}^{1,2,3}
are regarded as quandrupoles and octupole.

VI
Discussion

VI.1
Realistic coupling to bath

It is worth commenting on the nature of thermal contact with the heat bath in realistic experimental conditions.
When maintaining a material at a constant low temperature, various experimental configurations can be employed. For instance, the sample may be immersed in liquid nitrogen or liquid/boiling helium, or it may be mounted under vacuum in thermal contact with a cooled substrate. In all such cases, thermal coupling to the heat bath is established via an interface.
In one- or two-dimensional systems, the entire material is typically in contact with the thermal bath, so a coupling form that preserves translational symmetry, such as that in Eq. (
21
), is likely to be justified.
In contrast, in three-dimensional systems, heat transfer across the interface is mediated by phonons,
making the problem of phonon transmission and reflection at the boundary of fundamental importance. This interfacial thermal resistance is known as the Kapitza resistance
POLLACK 1969
;
Peterson and Anderson 1973
;
Sheard et al. 1973
. While in this work we adopt a phenomenological form for it, i.e., Eqs. (
8
) and (
9
), we anticipate that a truly first-principles theoretical description of the nonequilibrium phenomena will become possible by properly accounting for phonon transfer between the refrigerant and the sample in accordance with the specific experimental setup.
This is one of the interesting future issues to be explored.

VI.2
Outlook for First-Principles Integration

A promising future direction is the integration of first-principles calculations to enable quantitative analysis of real Mott insulating materials. In this study, we have constructed our theoretical framework based on a general formalism, such that the derived equations of motion can be directly applied once the parameters

E_{i}^{\xi},I_{ij}^{\xi\xi^{\prime}},\omega_{\bm{k}n}
, and
g_{i,\bm{k}n}^{\xi}
are specified.

Regarding the determination of electronic parameters, downfolding techniques
Imada and Miyake 2010
based on first-principles calculations allow for the construction of effective tight-binding models within the low-energy subspace near the Fermi level
Marzari and Vanderbilt 1997b
;
Souza et al. 2001
. Within this target space, interaction parameters can be systematically estimated using the constrained random phase approximation (cRPA)
Aryasetiawan et al. 2004
;
Solovyev and Imada 2005
. For Mott insulators, the previous studies
Iwazaki and Hoshino 2021
;
Iwazaki et al. 2023
have established general procedures to derive the effective interaction
I_{ij}^{\xi\xi^{\prime}}
and on-site energy
E_{i}^{\xi}
for localized electrons from multi-orbital Hubbard models.

As for phonons, the dispersion relation
\omega_{\bm{k}n}
can be obtained from first-principles phonon calculations, and the electron-phonon coupling constant
g_{i,\bm{k}n}^{\xi}
within the relevant low-energy window can also be estimated
Nomura et al. 2014
.

Concerning the coupling to a thermal bath, the damping parameter
\gamma_{\bm{k}n}
must be provided. In situations close to thermal equilibrium, it is sufficient to assign
\gamma_{\bm{k}n}
phenomenologically, as the canonical distribution is correctly reproduced. For more detailed descriptions of nonequilibrium dynamics, the coupling to the thermal bath must be explicitly modeled based on the experimental setup, as discussed in Sec.
VI.1
.

In this way, a first-principles-based approach to Mott insulators can enable quasi-classical simulations that cover not only equilibrium properties but also nonequilibrium dynamics. Validation of the method for known materials, followed by its application to unexplored compounds, may pave the way for an efficient computational exploration of functional Mott-insulator materials.

VI.3
Effect of quantum fluctuations

In this work, we have discussed the semiclassical dynamics along a classical trajectory and proposed possible improvements of the approximation in Sec.
III.2
. In this section, we further elaborate on quantum corrections. These can be broadly classified into the following three categories.

First, quantum corrections to the noise term can be considered. In deriving the Langevin equation as a Markovian process, we assumed the high-temperature limit in Eq. (
68
). At lower temperatures, quantum effects can be incorporated by abandoning this assumption. It is known that such quantum corrections enable an accurate description of the dynamics of quantum particles in harmonic potentials
Schmid 1982
;
Weiss 2021
. In practice, simulations become more demanding, since the dynamics acquires memory effects that depend on the system’s trajectory.

Second, as also relevant to the present study, the presence of anharmonicity requires additional quantum corrections from a different perspective. The quantum component
\Omega^{\rm qm}
defined in Sec.
II.4
was truncated at the lowest orders, but in general, higher-order contributions are necessary. In the language of conventional single-particle quantum mechanics, this corresponds to including effects such as anharmonic potentials and quantum tunneling
Kamenev 2011
. As a result, the region where the quantum component becomes significant expands, posing further challenges for both formulation and simulation.

At last but not least, a distinct correction arises from the Berry phase term. The parameters characterizing the coherent state of localized electrons are subject to the curvature of their phase space, which is associated with the Berry curvature defined in Eq. (
48
). This is analogous to the case of classical spins, which are represented by coordinates on a unit sphere, i.e., the polar and azimuthal angles. Remarkably, such geometric effects do not appear in ordinary quantum mechanical system, where the difference between the Hamiltonian
H
and the Lagrangian
L
is of the form
L+H=p\dot{q}
with
q
and
p
being a generalized coordinate and momentum, yielding only first-order quantum components when separated into classical and quantum parts. Furthermore, this correction is independent of the microscopic details of the system and instead reflects the choice of variables representing the physical degrees of freedom. When interpreted as the motion of a particle on a curved phase space, it invites connections to geometric quantities such as the quantum geometric tensor
Törmä 2023
, which has recently attracted considerable attention.

A systematic understanding of these corrections provides a clearer decomposition of quantum effects in systems of localized electrons, thereby deepening our insight into their quantum nature.

VI.4
Simulations of microscopic model Hamiltonians

The epLD formalism introduced in this work provides a
powerful framework for studying dissipation and nonequilibrium
dynamics in magnetic systems with arbitrary spin
S
.
Although the present formulation does not account for long-range
quantum entanglement beyond a single site, it fully captures
the local Hilbert space structure of electrons in terms of
SU(
N
) coherent states, correctly describing all local
multipoles (dipole, quadrupole, etc.).
This makes our approach ideally suited for large-scale
simulations of microscopic model Hamiltonians, allowing for
systematic investigations of finite-size effects and direct
comparisons with experimental results.

As a first step, it would be natural to go beyond the benchmark
example of the spin–1 chain used in Sec.
IV

and apply the formalism to higher-dimensional lattices.
These systems can exhibit finite-temperature phase transitions,
either continuous or first-order, which may be studied in the
presence of dissipation.
A particularly promising direction involves the study of spin
nematic phases, which host a variety of topological excitations.
Their nonequilibrium dynamics–especially in response to external
perturbations–are of great interest for potential applications
in spintronic and quantum information devices.

Another intriguing avenue is the interplay between lattice dynamics
and spin-liquid behavior.
Spin liquids are known to exhibit strong dynamical signatures
even at very low temperatures, and the effect of phonons on
their stability remains an open question.
A notable example is the
S=1
pyrochlore magnet
NaCaNi
2
F
7

Plumb et al. 2019
;
Zhang et al. 2019
,
which has been suggested to relate to a RP
{}^{2}\times
U(1)
spin liquid stabilized by large negative biquadratic
interactions
Pohle and Shannon 2025
.
Such interactions imply significant coupling to lattice degrees
of freedom
Gao 2024
and call for further investigation using the present
formalism.

Previous studies on spin liquids investigated the
stability of spin liquids in presence of bond
phonons
Ferrari et al. 2021
;
Ferrari et al. 2024
.
However, other regimes–particularly those involving site
phonons–may have a stabilizing effect.
While in multi-orbital systems bond phonons are
expected to be
energetically less favorable than site phonons [see
discussion around Eq. (
7
)],
singlet formation on bonds can,
in certain limits, be effectively described using an
SU(2)
\times
SU(2)
\cong
SU(4) framework
Dahlbom et al. 2024
,
offering a potential route to incorporating bond phonons
into this formalism.

Because the equations of motion [Eq. (
69
)]
are general, external
magnetic or electric fields can be incorporated directly, enabling
simulations of driven systems and exotic
topological solitons such as
CP
2
skyrmions
Amari et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2023
,
or defect formation processes described by the
Kibble–Zurek mechanism.
This opens up the possibility of realistically modeling pump–probe
experiments, where energy dissipation and relaxation occur
through microscopically grounded damping mechanisms.

VII
Summary

We have developed a microscopic framework for spin-orbital coupled
Mott insulators with electron-phonon interactions by deriving
generalized equations of motion from a Kugel-Khomskii-type
Hamiltonian.
Using SU(
N
) coherent states within the Keldysh path-integral
formalism, our approach systematically incorporates both
dissipation and stochastic fluctuations arising from coupling
to a phonon bath.
This leads to semiclassical equations of motion featuring
microscopically derived damping and noise terms–providing a
significant improvement over traditional phenomenological models.

We benchmark the formalism numerically using a two-orbital
spin chain coupled to Einstein phonons, and demonstrate the
emergence of hybridized electron-phonon excitations.
In a specific limit, our equations reduce to the well-known
Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert (LLG) form, establishing a direct
connection to existing spin dynamics models.

Overall, our approach enables realistic simulations of both
equilibrium and nonequilibrium dynamics in strongly correlated
systems, and offers a promising route to bridge first-principles
band-structure calculations with microscopic dynamical behavior.

Appendix B
Fokker-Planck equation

B.1
General derivation

Let us consider the probability distribution function by taking the average over the random force.
Unlike the Langevin equation, which tracks individual stochastic trajectories, the Fokker–Planck equation directly provides the time evolution of the probability density, making it well-suited for analyzing ensemble behavior and deriving steady-state distributions.
First of all, we write the equation of motion
in a symbolic way:

\displaystyle\dot{\Omega}_{ip}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{ip}(\bm{\Omega},\bm{q},\bm{p})\,,

(114)

\displaystyle\dot{q}_{\bm{k}n}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}^{(1)}_{\bm{k}n}(\bm{\Omega},\bm{q},\bm{p})-\frac{1}{|z_{\bm{k}n}|^{2}}\quantity( {\rm Im\,}\Gamma_{\bm kn}(t)- \frac{\gm_{\bm kn}}{\omega_{\bm kn}}{\rm Re\,}\Gamma_{\bm kn}(t))\,,

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{\bm{k}n}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{\bm{k}n}(\bm{\Omega},\bm{q},\bm{p})-\frac{1}{|z_{\bm{k}n}|^{2}}\quantity( {\rm Re\,}\Gamma_{\bm kn}(t) + \frac{\gm_{\bm kn}}{\omega_{\bm kn}}{\rm Im\,}\Gamma_{\bm kn}(t))\,,

where the real-number representation is employed:

\displaystyle a_{\bm{k}n}=q_{\bm{k}n}+\mathrm{i}p_{\bm{k}n}\,.

(115)

We have also introduced the notation such as

\bm{\Omega}=\{\Omega_{ip}\}
and
\bm{q}=\{q_{\bm{k}n}\}
.
These first-order equations are just a rewriting of
Eqs. (
69a
) and (
69b
).

We define the probability density by

\displaystyle P(\bm{X},\bm{x},\bm{y},t)=\delta\big(\bm{X}-{\bm{\Omega}}(t)\big)\delta\big(\bm{x}-\bm{q}(t)\big)\delta\big(\bm{y}-\bm{p}(t)\big)\,.

(116)

The time-dependent components, such as
\Omega_{ip}(t)

W(t)=\langle P(t)\rangle

\displaystyle\frac{\partial W}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\bigg[-\mathcal{F}_{ip}\frac{\partial}{\partial X_{ip}}-\mathcal{G}^{(1)}_{\bm{k}n}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\bm{k}n}}-\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{\bm{k}n}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{\bm{k}n}}

\displaystyle\hskip 11.38109pt+\frac{\gamma_{\bm{k}n}T}{2\omega_{\bm{k}n}|z_{\bm{k}n}|^{2}}\quantity( \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^{2}_{\bm kn}}
+ \frac{\partial^2}{\partial y^{2}_{\bm kn}} )\bigg]W\,,

(117)

where the Einstein rule is employed for the summation with respect to the repeated indices (
i,p,\bm{k},n
).
We omit the arguments of
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}^{(1),(2)}
for brevity.

The probability distribution function for the electronic part
is obtained by taking the partial trace over phonons as

\displaystyle W_{\rm el}(\bm{X},t)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\ W(\bm{X},\bm{x},\bm{y},t)\,,

(118)

which describes the statistics of the electronic states at every time

.
The normalization condition
\int\mathrm{d}\bm{X}W_{\rm el}=1

B.2
Explicit Example

Although the Fokker–Planck equation is generally analytically
intractable, we present here an exactly solvable example for
the damped harmonic oscillator.
Let us consider the
\gamma\to 0
limit of the equation of motion
for phonons, without coupling to electrons

\displaystyle\dot{q}

\displaystyle=\omega p-\gamma q-{\rm Im\,}\Gamma\,,

(119)

\displaystyle\dot{p}

\displaystyle=-\omega q-\gamma p+{\rm Re\,}\Gamma\,,

(120)

where we have omitted the index
\bm{k}n
.
Namely, the problem is reduced to the two-coupled differential
equations with damping and noise.
We define

\displaystyle P(x,y,t)

\displaystyle=\delta\big(x-q(t)\big)\delta\big(y-p(t)\big)\,,

(121)

and obtain

\displaystyle\dot{P}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}(\omega y-\gamma x-{\rm Im\,}\Gamma)P-\partial_{y}(-\omega x-\gamma y+{\rm Re\,}\Gamma)P\,.

(122)

Defining
W=\langle P\rangle
, we derive the following Fokker-Planck equation

\displaystyle\dot{W}=\bigg[-\partial_{x}(\omega y-\gamma x)-\partial_{y}(-\omega x-\gamma y)+\frac{\gamma T}{2\omega}(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2})\bigg]W

(123)

\displaystyle\hskip-8.53581pt=\bigg[\omega(x\partial_{y}-y\partial_{x})+\gamma\partial_{x}\quantity(x + \frac{T}{2\omega}\partial_x)+\gamma\partial_{y}\quantity(y + \frac{T}{2\omega}\partial_y)\bigg]W\,.

(124)

Hence, we confirm that

\displaystyle W_{\rm equib}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\exp[-\beta\omega(x^{2}+y^{2})]\,,

(125)

is the solution of the stationary state (
\dot{W}=0
).
This is simply the Boltzmann factor in the canonical ensemble,
as expected.

C.3
Domain of variables in coherent state

We comment here on the domains in the coherent-state variables.
The coherent state is characterized by the coefficients
c_{\alpha}(\Omega)
,
which are expressed in terms of

\cos x_{\alpha}
,
\sin x_{\alpha}
, and
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi_{\alpha}}
,
in the domains

\displaystyle x_{\alpha}\in[0,\pi/2]\,,\quad\ \varphi_{\alpha}\in[0,2\pi)

(136)

[see Eqs. (
13
)–(
15
)].
If we change the variable as

x_{\alpha}^{\prime}=\pi/2-x_{\alpha}
, the sine and cosine
functions are interchanged

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}(\sin x_{\alpha},\cos x_{\alpha})&=&(\cos x^{\prime}_{\alpha},\sin x^{\prime}_{\alpha})\end{matrix}\,,

(137)

with
x_{\alpha}^{\prime}\in[0,\pi/2]
.
The representations in terms of
x_{\alpha}
and
x_{\alpha}^{\prime}

are defined in the same domain and are equivalent.

Next, consider the following ‘gauge’ transformations
of the variable
x_{\alpha}

\displaystyle x_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow x_{\alpha}^{\rm(i)}=x_{\alpha}+\pi/2\,,

(138)

\displaystyle x_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow x_{\alpha}^{\rm(ii)}=x_{\alpha}+\pi\,,

(139)

\displaystyle x_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow x_{\alpha}^{\rm(iii)}=x_{\alpha}+3\pi/2\,.

(140)

Under these shifts, the sine and cosine transform as follows

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\text{(i)}\ \ &(\sin x_{\alpha},\cos x_{\alpha})&\to&(-\sin x_{\alpha}^{\prime},\cos x_{\alpha}^{\prime})\,,\\
\text{(ii)}\ \ &(\sin x_{\alpha},\cos x_{\alpha})&\to&(-\sin x_{\alpha},-\cos x_{\alpha})\,,\\
\text{(iii)}\ \ &(\sin x_{\alpha},\cos x_{\alpha})&\to&(\sin x_{\alpha}^{\prime},-\cos x_{\alpha}^{\prime})\,,\end{matrix}

(141)

resulting in sign changes of the sine and/or
cosine functions.
However, these extra minus signs can be absorbed by shifting
the phase variable:
\varphi_{\alpha}^{\prime}=\varphi_{\alpha}+\pi
,
where, due to the periodicity of
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi_{\alpha}}
,
\varphi_{\alpha}^{\prime}

can still be defined in the interval
[0,2\pi)
.
Therefore, the coherent states defined for
x_{\alpha}\in[0,\pi/2]

are equivalent to those defined in the intervals:

\displaystyle{\rm(i)}\ \ [\pi/2,\pi],\quad{\rm(ii)}\ \ [\pi,3\pi/2],\quad{\rm(iii)}\ \ [3\pi/2,2\pi]\,.

(142)

Any of these representations can be used in practical calculations,
reflecting the fact that the parametrization of coherent states is
not unique.

Appendix D
Efficient Monte Carlo Simulations

In this Appendix, we provide practical details for
performing efficient Monte Carlo simulations of
SU(
N
) coherent states.
Our formalism is based on the representation of
coherent states given in Eqs. (
12
)
and (
13
), which we reproduce here for
convenience

\displaystyle|\Omega\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}c_{\alpha}(\Omega)|\alpha\rangle\,,

(143)

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=e^{i\varphi_{\alpha-1}}\cos{x_{\alpha}}\prod_{\beta=1}^{\alpha-1}\sin{x_{\beta}}\,,

(144)

where
N
denotes the dimension of the local Hilbert space
and
|\alpha\rangle
are the basis states.
The set of continuous variables parametrizing the
state is given by

\displaystyle\Omega=\{\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{N-1},\varphi_{1},\cdots,\varphi_{N-1}\}\,.

(145)

Each site thus carries
2(N-1)
real degrees of freedom.
The phase space element
\int d\Omega
covers the ranges

\xi_{i}\in[0,\pi/2]
and

\varphi_{i}\in[0,2\pi)
, where
variables
\varphi_{0}=0
and
\xi_{N}=0
.

In Monte Carlo simulations, it is essential to sample
configurations uniformly over the phase space measure.
For example, in conventional O(3) Heisenberg spin models,
it is standard practice not to sample the polar
angle
\theta
uniformly from
0
to
\pi
, but rather to
sample
\cos{\theta}
uniformly in

[-1,1]

Metropolis et al. 1953
;
Marsaglia 1972
.
Analogously, in our SU(
N
) coherent-state parametrization,
one must respect the correct volume element

\displaystyle\int d\Omega

\displaystyle=\prod_{\alpha=1}^{N-1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\varphi_{\alpha}\ \int_{0}^{\pi/2}dx_{\alpha}\ \cos{x_{\alpha}}\sin^{2(N-\alpha)-1}{x_{\alpha}}\,.

(146)

Naively sampling
x_{\alpha}
uniformly requires explicitly
normalizing this measure at every Monte Carlo step, which is
computationally inefficient.
To avoid this, we reformulate Eq. (
146
)
by rewriting the integrand as

\displaystyle\int d\Omega

\displaystyle=\prod_{\alpha=1}^{N-1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\varphi_{\alpha}\ \times

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/2}dx_{\alpha}\left[\frac{1}{2(N-\alpha)}\frac{d}{dx_{\alpha}}\sin^{2(N-\alpha)}{x_{\alpha}}\right]\,.

(147)

This transformation expresses the measure in a form where

x_{\alpha}
can be sampled efficiently from a uniform
random variable
r_{\alpha}
, defined as

\displaystyle r_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2(N-\alpha)}\sin^{2(N-\alpha)}{x_{\alpha}}\,,

(148)

which can be inverted to obtain

\displaystyle x_{\alpha}=\arcsin{ \left\{ \left[ 2(N-\alpha)
r_{\al} \right]^{1/(2 (N-\alpha))} \right\} }\,.

(149)

Here,
r_{\alpha}
is drawn uniformly from the interval

\displaystyle r_{\alpha}\in\left[0,\cdots,\frac{1}{2(N-\alpha)}\right]\,.

(150)

This procedure guarantees uniform sampling according to the
correct measure for arbitrary
N
and ensures all components
of
\Omega
are sampled consistently.

Appendix F
Realistic electron-phonon coupling in solids

F.1
Coupling to acoustic phonons

Let us consider the electron-phonon coupling in solids.
We begin with the derivation of the electron-phonon coupling
based on the Coulomb potential.
The electron-phonon interaction is written as

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}\bm{r}\,\rho(\bm{r})\sum_{i}V(\bm{r}-\bm{R}_{i})\,,

(151)

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma}\psi^{\dagger}_{\sigma}(\bm{r})\psi_{\sigma}(\bm{r})\,,

(152)

where
V
is a pseudo-potential from ions at
\bm{R}_{i}
with screening effects.

\psi_{\sigma}(\bm{r})
is the annihilation operator of electron at position
\bm{r}
and spin
\sigma
.
To account for the vibration, we consider deviation from the stable point:
\bm{R}_{i}=\bm{R}_{i}^{0}+\delta\bm{R}_{i}
.
Then the electron phonon coupling is given by

\displaystyle H_{ep}

\displaystyle=-\int\mathrm{d}\bm{r}\rho(\bm{r})\sum_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{r}}V(\bm{r}-\bm{R}_{i}^{0})\cdot\delta\bm{R}_{i}\,.

(153)

The displacement operator is expanded by the Fourier series:

\displaystyle\delta\bm{R}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\bm{k}n}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}_{i}^{0}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2M\omega_{\bm{k}n}}}\ \bm{\epsilon}_{\bm{k}n}\phi_{\bm{k}n}

(154)

with
\phi_{\bm{k}n}=a_{\bm{k}n}+a^{\dagger}_{-\bm{k},n}
.
Here
M
is the ion mass and
N
is the number of ions.

\bm{\epsilon}_{\bm{k}n}
is the polarization vector of the mode
\bm{k}n
, and satisfies

\bm{\epsilon}_{\bm{k}n}^{*}=\bm{\epsilon}_{-\bm{k},n}

Landau and Lifshitz 1980
.

Usually, a continuum approximation is employed for the description of low-energy dynamics.
With the unit cell volume
v_{\rm cell}
, one can write the Hamiltonian as

\displaystyle H_{ep}

\displaystyle\simeq\int\mathrm{d}\bm{r}\rho(\bm{r})\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{R}}{v_{\rm cell}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{R}}V(\bm{r}-\bm{R})

\displaystyle\ \ \ \cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\bm{k}n}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2M\omega_{\bm{k}n}}}\bm{\epsilon}_{\bm{k}n}\phi_{\bm{k}n}

(155)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{v_{\rm cell}\sqrt{N}}\sum_{\bm{k}n}\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\epsilon}_{\bm{k}n}\rho(-\bm{k})V(\bm{k})\sqrt{\frac{1}{2M\omega_{\bm{k}n}}}\phi_{\bm{k}n}\,.

(156)

The screened potential should have a finite decay length, which is an order of the inverse Fermi wavevector for electron gas [
V(\bm{k})\sim 1/(k^{2}+k_{c}^{2})
].
We suppose that the screening length is finite also for the Mott insulator, i.e., the ion potential is suppressed at some finite length, and then
V(\bm{0})
is finite.
We also assume that the dominant contribution for the electron phonon coupling enters through the low-energy branch.
Then, taking
\bm{k}\to\bm{0}
and considering the gapless longitudinal mode (
\bm{\epsilon}_{\bm{k}n}=\mathrm{i}\bm{k}/|\bm{k}|
),
we obtain

\displaystyle H_{ep}

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{v_{\rm cell}\sqrt{N}}V(\bm{0})\sum_{\bm{k}n}\rho(\bm{k})|\bm{k}|\sqrt{\frac{1}{2Mc_{n}|\bm{k}|}}\phi_{\bm{k}n}\,,

(157)

where
c_{n}
is the speed of acoustic phonons.
Hence we obtain the leading-order contribution for the coupling constant defined in Eq. (
6
) as

\displaystyle g_{i\bm{k}}\sim\sqrt{k}\ \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}_{i}^{0}}\,,

(158)

where we have used
\rho(\bm{k})\sim\sum_{i}n_{i}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}_{i}^{0}}
with the electron charge density operator
n_{i}
at site
i
.
This result is reasonable in the sense that the coupling vanishes in the
\bm{k}\to\bm{0}
limit:
the long-wavelength limit physically corresponds to a uniform shift of the system, where any crystal deformation is absent
Khan and Allen 1984
.
For Mott insulators,
the charge density
n_{i}
is replaced by the local electric multipole operators associated with the orbital degrees of freedom.

According to the last subsection,
the coupling to acoustic phonons suggest
\alpha_{n}=1/2
as discussed in the previous section.
By performing the integral in Eq. (

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\rm R}_{i\xi,j\xi^{\prime}}(t-t^{\prime})=\left\{\begin{matrix}\displaystyle-\sum_{n}\frac{h_{n}^{\xi*}h_{n}^{\xi^{\prime}}}{4\pi c_{n}^{4}}\delta^{\prime\prime\prime}(t-t^{\prime})&\ \ (i=j)\\[14.22636pt]

F.3
Comment on optical phonons

We briefly make a comment on the coupling to optical phonon. In Eq. (
94
), the frequency
\omega_{\bm{k}n}=c_{n}k
in exponent is replaced by
\omega_{\bm{k}n}=\omega_{n}={\rm const.}
for optical phonons which is wavevector-independent in the long-wavelength limit.
Then,
\Sigma^{\rm R}(t)
behaves as
\sim\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_{n}t}

In this case, the Markovian numerical simulation with semiclassical phonons (see Sec.
III

We also remark that the coupling constant is enhanced in the long wavelength limit (
q\to 0
)
Verdi and Giustino 2015
;
Sohier et al. 2016
, and therefore the coupling to optical phonon should play an important role in solid state materials including Mott insulators.
