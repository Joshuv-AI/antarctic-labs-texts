Antidepressant prescribing patterns and patient-reported adverse effects: a cross-sectional study from Pakistan
Authors: Sohail Riaz, Fazli Khuda, Abuzar Khan, Asif Jan, Samir Ibenmoussa, Ghallab Alotaibi, Syed Shaukat Ali, Basmah Abdulaziz Albabtain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntidepressant prescribing practices and Adverse-effect profiles are poorly characterised in Pakistan, highlighting the need for systematic evaluation. This study aimed to assess prescribing patterns, validate the Urdu version of the Antidepressant Side-Effect Checklist (U-ASEC), and document patient-reported adverse effects.MethodsA cross-sectional questionnaire-based study was conducted at two psychiatry clinics in Lahore and Rawalpindi. All adult patients diagnosed with MDD according to the DSM-5-TR were invited to participate. Sampling was done through a convenient method. The Urdu-translated Antidepressant Side-Effect Checklist (U-ASEC) was developed through forward–back translation, expert panel review and pilot testing, and its psychometric properties such as internal consistency, construct validity were examined. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. Differences in adverse-effect scores across demographic and clinical groups were assessed with Kruskal–Wallis tests followed by Dunn’s test with Bonferroni adjustment. Multivariable logistic regression models were fitted for each adverse-effect item to explore associations with antidepressant classes, adjusting for age, sex, tobacco use, comorbidity and duration of MDD. Multicollinearity was checked using variance inflation factors (VIF) (< 3 for all variables), and false discovery rate (FDR) control was used to account for multiple testing.ResultsAmong the 457 participants, 54.9% were male and 60% of participants were married. Escitalopram was the most frequently prescribed single agent (12.9%), and Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors as a class accounted for 32.1% of monotherapy prescriptions. Combination therapy was used by 26.7% of participants; typical pairs included mirtazapine + escitalopram (12.2%) and mirtazapine + fluoxetine (5.5%). The U-ASEC demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.78). Factor analysis identified three clusters of adverse effects: autonomic dysfunction, Central Nervous System effects, and metabolic effects. Dry mouth and insomnia were the most frequently reported adverse effects of SSRIs. Adverse effects varied by class: TCAs had higher rates of nausea/vomiting (41.3%); SNRIs were associated with a higher prevalence of urinary difficulties (32.4%); atypicals reduced dry mouth (18.8%) but increased dizziness (10.4%); and dual therapy showed more metabolic effects, including weight gain (29.5%) and increased body temperature (20.5%). Significant associations were identified for dry mouth (p = 0.0059), nausea and vomiting (p = 0.0202), problem with urination (p = 0.0248), feeling like the room is spinning (p = 0.041), and Tremor (p = 0.0382). Multivariable analysis showed TCAs increased odds of nausea (OR 3.96, 95% CI 1.72–9.03, p = 0.001), urination problems (OR 2.77, 1.10–6.63, p = 0.025), orthostatic symptoms (OR 2.59, 1.07–6.01, p = 0.030) and tremor (OR 2.66, 1.22–5.94, p = 0.015). SNRIs were associated with increased odds of urinary difficulties (OR 12.92, 1.19–284.32, p = 0.040). Atypicals reduced dry mouth (OR 0.18, 0.02–0.94, p = 0.050) but increased dizziness (OR 8.42, 1.47–48.41, p = 0.012).ConclusionEscitalopram was the most commonly prescribed antidepressant, with dry mouth and insomnia as the most reported adverse effects. This study provides a culturally relevant tool, U-ASEC, for adverse effect monitoring, addressing critical gaps in Pakistan’s clinical practices. Our findings underscore the importance of tailoring antidepressant choice to patient characteristics.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12991-026-00644-7.

Background

Antidepressants are essential for the treatment of major depressive disorder (MDD) and associated disability, absenteeism [1] and reduced productivity [2]. Global trends indicate a significant increase in antidepressant use across countries, with a 43% overall increase from 2014 to 2019 [3]. The global rise in antidepressant use reflects mental health treatment expansion, yet much of this increase stems from primary care providers prescribing antidepressants off-label and often for milder MDD patients [4–6]. This trend is especially pronounced in lower-middle-income countries (LMICs), where antidepressant usage has increased by an average of 69% [7], highlighting ongoing efforts to improve mental health treatment access in resource-limited settings.​ In contrast to high-income countries (HICs), where up to 17% of adults are prescribed antidepressants [8], in LMICs, the prevalence of antidepressant use is lower, primarily due to limited access to healthcare services and medications, as well as cultural barriers and stigma surrounding mental health treatments [9].

Previous studies support the effectiveness of antidepressants in treating MDD [10–13]. For example, a study showed that second-generation antidepressants such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) reduce depressive symptoms by more than 50% in many cases [14]. Recent studies across various countries highlight SSRIs as the most commonly prescribed antidepressants. In Bangladesh, sertraline is frequently used, aligning with WHO guidelines for rational prescribing [15]. In Malaysia, SSRIs make up 72.9% of prescriptions among depressive patients, with tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) following at 10.3% [16]. In Sweden, antidepressant use among youths has increased significantly, with SSRIs predominantly prescribed for long-term treatment [17]. In Pakistan, a study indicated similar trends, with SSRIs being the primary choice for major depressive disorder patients because of their favourable safety profile and efficacy [18]. A recent Pakistani cohort study also reported that antidepressant treatment response varied across sociodemographic strata, supporting the clinical relevance of demographic correlates in antidepressant therapy [19].

While the efficacy of antidepressants is well supported, treatment outcomes are often impacted by adverse effects [20]. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) have become widely accepted instruments for assessing treatment efficacy [21]. According to the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), “Patient Reported Outcome is any report of the status of a patient’s health condition that comes directly from the patient without interpretation of the patient’s response by a clinician or anyone else.” [22] Two primary areas in which PROMs have been discussed are Adverse effect reporting (PRO-AEs) and quality-of-care evaluation (using PRO-PMs) [23]. PRO-AEs identify adverse effects earlier than clinician reports [24, 25]. Various PRO-AE tools, such as the Maudsley Side-Effects (MSE) measure [26], the Glasgow Antipsychotic Side-Effect Scale (GASS) [27], and the Antidepressant Side-Effect Checklist (ASEC) [28], have been developed to monitor adverse drug reactions among psychiatric patients. Among these, the ASEC is particularly effective for capturing antidepressant-related adverse effects because of its strong psychometric properties [28]. However, its unavailability in Urdu limits its utility in Urdu-speaking countries such as Pakistan.

Research highlights a range of patient-reported adverse effects, from gastrointestinal issues and weight gain to cardiovascular and cognitive disturbances, which contribute to nonadherence and poor clinical outcomes [29]. For example, studies from Ethiopia, the Netherlands and Nepal reported that 85% of patients experienced adverse reactions such as weight gain, insomnia, and anxiety, which reduced adherence​ [30–32]. Another study revealed that nortriptyline and escitalopram often cause persistent adverse effects such as dry mouth and insomnia, which lead to discontinuation​ [28]. Adverse effects on gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea and vomiting are common with SSRIs and SNRIs and may lead to discontinuation early in treatment​ [33]. Moreover, a study showed that 65% of long-term antidepressant users experienced weight increases, which disrupted adherence and ongoing treatment​ [34].

These studies underscore the importance of understanding prescribing trends and associated patient-reported outcomes. Observational evidence has further described antidepressant prescribing patterns alongside patient-reported side-effect profiles in routine care, reinforcing the rationale for structured adverse-effect monitoring during pharmacotherapy [35]. However, there is limited evidence that examines both aspects together; most existing studies focus either on prescribing trends, drug‒drug interactions or the pharmacoeconomic aspects of antidepressants [18, 36–39]. Understanding prescribing patterns and adverse effects not only enhances local mental health care strategies but also contributes to the global discourse on rational antidepressant use, particularly in LMICs.

However, there is a paucity of studies from Pakistan that simultaneously analyse both antidepressant prescribing patterns and patient-reported adverse effects using a culturally validated instrument. This gap hinders our understanding of how prescribing practices translate into real-world patient experiences in this setting. To address this gap, the present study aims to: (1) describe current antidepressant prescribing trends in Pakistan; (2) translate and culturally adapt the ASEC into Urdu and evaluate its psychometric properties; and (3) assess the prevalence and severity of patient-reported adverse effects across antidepressant classes using the validated Urdu ASEC.

Methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional, questionnaire-based study was conducted at Shaikh Zayed Medical Complex, Lahore, and the Healing House Psychiatry Clinic, Rawalpindi, from March 23, 2024, to November 13, 2024.

The study included patients diagnosed with recurrent MDD by a psychiatrist, following the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) criteria, the most current standard in clinical practice in Pakistan. Participants were recruited from outpatient departments (OPDs) at the Shaikh Zayed Medical Complex and the Healing House Psychiatry Clinic. Eligible participants were 18 years or older, fluent in Urdu, and able to understand and complete the questionnaire. Individuals younger than 18 years or those unable to comprehend Urdu were excluded, these include educational level below matric. Only patients who expressed willingness to participate were included, ensuring voluntary and informed participation.

Measures

The questionnaire comprised demographic, clinical, and prescribing patterns, as well as the Urdu version of the Antidepressant Side-Effect Checklist (U-ASEC). The demographic variables included age, sex, marital status, occupation, education level, and monthly income, providing an overview of the sample’s population characteristics. Tobacco use, alcohol addiction, type of MDD episode, duration of MDD, and presence of comorbidities were also noted.

Antidepressant prescription patterns were documented with categories for antidepressant classes such as SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs, and atypical antidepressants. Specific medications were noted within each class to explore patterns in prescription choices. Dual therapy was defined as the concurrent use of two antidepressant agents, regardless of class. In this study, combinations included SSRIs plus atypical antidepressants (e.g., escitalopram + mirtazapine) as well as SSRIs plus TCAs and other combinations.

Adverse effects were evaluated via the Urdu version of the U-ASEC, adapted from the original English ASEC [28]. The ASEC included 21 items covering a range of common symptoms associated with antidepressant use, such as dry mouth, drowsiness, insomnia, and gastrointestinal issues. Each symptom was rated by severity on a scale from 0 (absent) to 3 (severe), with participants also indicating if they believed the symptom was linked to their antidepressant medication. Severity ratings were entirely patient-reported, and clinicians did not verify or modify these responses.

Translation and validation of U-ASEC

The translation and cultural adaptation of the original ASEC into U-ASEC followed rigorous procedural guidelines to ensure linguistic fidelity and cultural relevance [40]. Initially, two translators independently translated the ASEC into Urdu: Translator 1 (T1), with a clinical background, ensured precise medical terminology, whereas Translator 2 (T2), without a medical background, provided accessible, layperson language. These independent translations were synthesised into a unified draft through collaboration with the S.R. (principal investigator), F.K. (supervisor) and A.K. (co-supervisor). Two additional translators, Back Translator 1 (BT1) and Back Translator 2 (BT2), who were both blind to the original ASEC and lacked medical expertise, subsequently conducted back-translations into English, allowing verification of consistency with the source text. An expert panel comprising the principal investigator, a psychiatrist, two pharmacotherapy professors and a psychologist reviewed the U-ASEC for semantic, idiomatic, experiential, and conceptual equivalence. The panel assessed each item’s importance, categorising them as “essential,” “useful but not essential,” or “not useful,” and achieved consensus on the translation’s accuracy and cultural appropriateness. The finalised U-ASEC was subsequently evaluated for face validity in a pilot test with 35 patients diagnosed with MDD, confirming its clarity for the target population.

Sample size and sampling method

The initial sample size was calculated via Raosoft Inc. software [41], yielding a required 385 participants based on a 5% margin of error, a 95% confidence level, and a 50% response distribution. To account for a potential dropout rate of 15%, the sample size was adjusted to approximately 453 participants. For validation of the 21-item U-ASEC, a respondent-to-item ratio of 15:1 was used [42], requiring a minimum of 315 participants. The adjusted sample size of 453 exceeded this threshold, ensuring sufficient power for the primary study and reliable checklist validation. A convenience sampling method was used, and patients attending follow‑up visits during the study period were invited consecutively.

Analyses

This study employed descriptive and inferential statistics to explore the relationships between participant demographics, clinical factors, and antidepressant adverse effects. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarise variables such as age, Sex, marital status, and income.

Reliability and factor analyses were conducted to assess the U-ASEC. Internal consistency was evaluated via Cronbach’s alpha, with values > 0.70 considered acceptable [43]. Factor analysis, guided by the Kaiser‒Meyer‒Olkin (KMO) measure (acceptable values > 0.6) and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < 0.05), was used to identify clusters of related adverse effects. The retained factors met the criterion of eigenvalues > 1, supporting the structural validity of the scale [44].

For group comparisons, the Kruskal-Wallis H test was used to assess differences in adverse effects across demographic and clinical categories, with significant findings followed by Dunn’s test and Bonferroni adjustments to manage multiple comparisons. Logistic regression models were used to evaluate the associations between antidepressant classes and specific adverse effects, with the results reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Predictor variables for each logistic regression model were chosen a priori based on clinical relevance. Predictor variables included antidepressant class and patient characteristics, and comorbidities. All combination therapies were aggregated into a ‘dual therapy’ category for statistical analysis. We did not differentiate between specific combination types (e.g., SSRI + atypical vs. SSRI + TCA) in the regression models. Multicollinearity was assessed by calculating variance inflation factors (VIF) for each predictor. All VIFs were below 3, indicating no problematic multicollinearity. Goodness-of-fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. A p-value threshold of 0.05 was set for all the statistical tests. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.1).

Results

Demographic characteristics of the participants

Among the 480 participants, 457 completed the questionnaires, with a response rate of 95.2%. Most participants were between 26 and 40 years old (n = 192, 42.0%) and were predominantly male (n = 251, 54.9%). Most were married (n = 274, 60.0%), employed (n = 149, 32.6%) or housewives (n = 124, 27.1%). Educational levels varied, with the largest group completing higher secondary education (n = 153, 33.5%). In terms of monthly income, over half of the respondents reported earnings between 20,000 and 40,000 PKR (n = 243, 53.2%). The demographic characteristics are summarised in Table 1.

Social and clinical characteristics

Most participants had no history of tobacco use (n = 279, 61.0%), whereas others reported occasional (n = 86, 18.8%), frequent (n = 62, 13.6%), or regular use (n = 30, 6.6%). Alcohol addiction was absent across the sample, with no participants reporting alcohol use. All participants were diagnosed with recurrent MDD, as none were experiencing a first episode.

Antidepressant prescription patterns

The prescription trends among the study sample (N = 457) revealed that most participants received single-drug therapy (n = 307, 67.2%). Within this group, SSRIs were the most commonly prescribed class, with a total of 147 participants (32.1%) taking SSRIs, including escitalopram (n = 59, 12.9%), paroxetine (n = 28, 6.1%), and fewer fluoxetine (n = 27, 5.9%), sertraline (n = 27, 5.9%), and fluvoxamine (n = 6, 1.3%). Serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) were prescribed to 37 participants (7.3%), primarily venlafaxine (n = 21, 4.6%) and duloxetine (n = 16, 3.5%). TCAs accounted for 16.4% of prescriptions, with the common choices being amitriptyline (n = 27, 5.9%) and nortriptyline (n = 23, 5.0%). Atypical antidepressants, including mirtazapine (n = 29, 6.3%) and bupropion (n = 17, 3.7%), were prescribed to 48 participants (10.4%).

For combination therapy, 122 participants (26.7%) received two antidepressants, with mirtazapine + escitalopram (n = 56, 12.2%) and mirtazapine + fluoxetine (n = 25, 5.5%) being the most common combinations. Three-drug regimens were less common, observed in 27 participants (6.1%), with combinations such as fluoxetine + duloxetine + clomipramine (n = 18, 4.0%) and duloxetine + amitriptyline + agomelatine (n = 9, 2.0%). Translation, reliability and validity of the U-ASEC

The U-ASEC translation maintained the integrity and relevance of the original tool. Forward-back translations ensured consistency. The expert panel review validated all the items as “essential”. All adverse effects were well understood in a pilot test with 35 MDD patients. However, the term “linked to antidepressant” revealed interpretation issues and was excluded from the pilot phase, although it was retained in the final version of U-ASEC for reproducibility testing.

The reliability analysis of the U-ASEC demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.70 to 0.78. The item-total correlations ranged from 0.07 (ASEC8: increased appetite) to 0.44 (ASEC17: increased body temperature), indicating moderate to strong associations for most items, as shown in Table 4. Alpha-if-item-deleted analyses showed minimal improvement (removing ASEC8 increased alpha to 0.79), suggesting retention of all items for content validity. Subscale alphas were: Autonomic Dysfunction (0.82), CNS Effects (0.76), metabolic Effects (0.71), indicating stronger reliability within factors.

The sampling adequacy, evaluated by the Kaiser‒Meyer‒Olkin (KMO) measure, was 0.71, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p < 0.001), confirming the data’s suitability for factor analysis.

The scree plot (Fig. 1) supported a three-factor structure, with an elbow after the third component, indicating minimal explanatory power beyond these three factors (Table 5; Fig. 1).

Patient-reported adverse effects

Dry mouth was most frequently reported among TCA users (45.3%) and those on dual therapy (36.9%), whereas atypical antidepressants showed the lowest prevalence (18.8%) (p = 0.0059). Insomnia was common across all classes, affecting roughly one-third of patients, with the highest rates in triple therapy (40.7%). Nausea and vomiting were significantly more common with TCAs (41.3%) compared to SSRIs (19.7%) and atypical antidepressant (18.8%) (p = 0.0202). Urination problems were especially pronounced with SNRIs (32.4%) and TCAs (22.7%) (p = 0.0248). Tremor was also more frequent in TCAs (28.0%) and dual therapy (21.3%) compared with SSRIs and atypical antidepressant (p = 0.0382). Atypical antidepressants were associated with a lower burden of dry mouth but showed a higher rate of vertigo/room-spinning sensations (10.4%, p = 0.0418). Patients on dual therapy exhibited the highest risk of metabolic effects, with increased body temperature (20.5%, p = 0.0247) and weight gain (29.5%) being more frequent than in other groups. Other adverse effects such as headache, constipation, sweating, sexual dysfunction, and disorientation were reported across all drug classes, but differences between groups did not reach statistical significance. Discussion

This study highlights the prescribing patterns of antidepressants, validation of U-ASEC and patient-reported adverse effects in patients with recurrent MDD in Pakistan. SSRIs are the most commonly prescribed class, reflecting their favourable safety and efficacy profiles. The translation and validation of the U-ASEC further provide a crucial tool for capturing PRO-AEs, ensuring cultural and linguistic appropriateness in diverse patient populations. The prevalence of adverse effects, such as dry mouth, insomnia, and weight gain, was notable​​​. These patient reported adverse effects underscore the importance of systematic monitoring and patient education. The dominance of escitalopram among prescribed antidepressants aligns with some international trends but contrasts with others due to regional variations. Similar to a study in Pakistan, where 67.5% of prescriptions included escitalopram, our study confirms its widespread use, reflecting its perceived efficacy and safety [18]. In contrast, a study in China reported significant use of both citalopram and escitalopram, which may be attributed to broader drug availability or differing prescribing guidelines [45]. Another study in India also identified fluoxetine as an essential antidepressant alongside escitalopram, likely due to differences in cost-effectiveness, accessibility, local formulary access, and prescriber familiarity in that setting [46]. Unlike the uniform SSRI usage reported by Noor Salman Abdulsattar (2024) in Sweden [47]. Our study highlights the significance of single-drug SSRI prescriptions in improving adherence. These findings also reflect the influence of Pakistan’s healthcare infrastructure, drug supply chain, and patient affordability constraints. Healthcare systems with robust insurance coverage, centralized guidelines, and subsidized access may lead to more homogeneous prescribing, while in low- and middle-income settings, local supply, out-of-pocket costs, and clinician experience play a larger role.

The validation and reliability analysis of the U-ASEC align with the original ASEC, which was developed to demonstrate strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.78 vs. 0.74 in the original) [28]. However, while the original ASEC focused on both self-reported and clinician-rated measures, our study relied exclusively on patient-reported outcomes, identifying similar adverse effects. The internal consistency across items was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.70–0.78), and items within each factor showed moderate to strong inter-item correlations, further supporting the coherence of the constructs measured. Moreover, higher adverse-effect scores were observed in participants receiving combination therapy, which lends preliminary support to known-groups validity.

The explained variance (36.88%) is modest, potentially due to cultural differences in symptom reporting, such as underreporting of sexual dysfunction in conservative Pakistani contexts [48]. Exploratory factor analysis of U-ASEC revealed three factors (autonomic dysfunction, CNS effects, metabolic effects), providing a culturally adapted structure for Urdu-speaking populations. As no other validation studies of ASEC have been published, this study significantly extends the tool’s applicability to new contexts, bridging a significant gap in patient-reported outcome research.

Our findings suggest, TCAs were associated with a higher burden of autonomic and neurological effects such as dry mouth, nausea, urinary problems, and tremor, reflecting their anticholinergic and noradrenergic activity [49]. SNRIs, though less frequently prescribed, showed a clear association with urinary difficulties, consistent with their noradrenergic mechanism [50]. Atypical antidepressants were linked with fewer complaints of dry mouth but increased dizziness and vertigo, likely due to sedative and antihistaminergic properties of agents such as mirtazapine [51]. Dual therapy was associated with higher reporting of metabolic adverse effects. This association may reflect greater illness severity, longer treatment exposure, comorbidity, or pharmacodynamic effects [52]. In contrast, SSRIs were associated with relatively lower rates of severe adverse effects, reinforcing their role as first-line agents.

Our study identified SSRIs, particularly escitalopram, as the most commonly prescribed antidepressants associated with dry mouth, insomnia, weight gain, and sweating as prevalent adverse effects. Similar findings have been reported in Germany [53], Turkey [54], Ethiopia [30], India [55], and Nepal [31], highlighting gastrointestinal issues, weight gain, and sleep disturbances as common SSRI-related adverse effects. These alignments may stem from the pharmacodynamics of SSRIs and their widespread use globally [56]. In contrast, a study in Australia/USA [32] reported more severe adverse effects, such as suicidal thoughts and headaches, and in Spain, it emphasized sexual dysfunction and apathy [57]. These differences may reflect variations in patient demographics, genetic predispositions, cultural attitudes toward reporting adverse effects, and healthcare settings, highlighting the need for localized strategies to address patient-specific challenges in Pakistan [58, 59].

Our study also revealed significant associations between demographic and clinical factors and antidepressant adverse effects. For example, income level was linked to dry mouth (p = 0.0235), reflecting disparities in healthcare access [60, 61], whereas tobacco use was associated with dry mouth (p = 0.0058) and insomnia (p = 0.0055), likely due to its pharmacological effects [62]. Insomnia was more frequently reported by certain sex and marital‑status groups (p = 0.0463) and headache (p = 0.0003), highlighting hormonal and psychosocial factors [63, 64]. Reports of dry mouth were less frequent among participants taking atypical antidepressants, (OR = 0.18, p = 0.0505) whereas reports of dizziness were more common (OR = 8.42, p = 0.0124). TCAs were linked to nausea (OR = 3.96, p = 0.00108) and urinary issues (OR = 2.77, p = 0.0252), whereas Metabolic adverse effects were more frequently reported among patients receiving dual therapy (OR = 10.19, p = 0.0247). Some OR values such as the association between SNRIs and urinary problems (OR = 12.92, 95% CI 1.19–284.32), had very wide confidence intervals, reflecting sparse event counts and instability in the estimates. These findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously, and larger samples are needed to obtain more precise estimates. Moreover, symptoms such as dry mouth and dizziness may arise from conditions like diabetes or hypertension rather than from the antidepressant itself. Our regression models adjusted for these comorbidities; nonetheless, residual confounding is possible, and clinicians should consider underlying medical conditions when interpreting patient-reported side effects. These findings emphasize the importance of tailoring antidepressant choices to patient profiles to minimize adverse effect. This study has several strengths, including a comprehensive evaluation of antidepressant prescribing patterns, the culturally adapted validation of the Urdu ASEC, and analysis of demographic and clinical correlates, providing actionable insights for clinical practice in resource-limited settings. A high response rate (95.2%), achieved by inviting patients immediately after consultations, reduces the likelihood of non-response bias, while the diverse sample and rigorous validation process enhance both generalizability and cultural relevance. However, certain limitations should be noted: reliance on self-reported data may have introduced recall bias; the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; and convenience sampling from two psychiatric centers limits representativeness [65]. In addition, convergent and divergent validity testing was not conducted, and exclusion of participants with low literacy may bias results against less educated populations. Despite these constraints, the study contributes three important clinical benefits: (1) a validated U-ASEC tool for systematic monitoring, (2) class-specific adverse-effect insights to guide tailored prescribing, and (3) prescribing pattern data to inform local guideline and training reforms.

Conclusion

This study underscores the critical interplay between antidepressant efficacy and adverse effects, with particular attention given to patient reported outcomes. The U-ASEC emerged as a culturally adapted tool for identifying adverse effects and better understanding patient experiences among the Pakistani population. The results revealed significant prescribing patterns favouring SSRIs, while highlighting prevalent adverse effects such as dry mouth and insomnia. Our findings underscore the need to consider patient‑reported adverse effects when applying existing antidepressant use guidelines.
