The association between MGMT expression and outcome in H3 G34-mutant diffuse hemispheric glioma is robust to adjustment for pre-analytic and technical variables
Authors: Qunyu Zhang, Dana Tlais, Tong Lin, Xiaoyu Li, Arzu Onar-Thomas, Jason Chiang
Published: 2026

We thank Shen and Sheng for their careful reanalysis and for raising the possibility that technical batch effects may confound the MGMT–outcome association we reported in histone H3 G34-mutant diffuse hemispheric glioma (DHG) [8, 9]. The question extends beyond DHG. Cohorts of rare pediatric brain tumors are typically retrospective and multi-institutional, span many years, and mix preservation methods, all potential sources of batch effects [3].

We analyzed RNA-seq data from 110 H3 G34-mutant DHGs, all sequenced centrally at St. Jude on the NovaSeq 6000 platform and including all RNA-sequenced tumors from our original report [9]. The four SJC-DS-1001 samples, the most extreme in Shen and Sheng’s principal component analysis (PCA), were sequenced on an older platform and excluded from both our original and present analyses. As in our original analysis, counts were TMM-normalized (edgeR), converted to log2 counts per million (CPM), and corrected for processing batch (limma removeBatchEffect) [4, 6, 7]. Our original analysis therefore already included batch correction; we regret not describing this step in our Methods. We examined four pre-analytic and technical variables: collection year, preservation method (formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded [FFPE] or fresh/frozen), data release year, and tumor content, estimated from the H3 G34 variant allele frequency with whole exome sequencing and H&E review.

In raw counts, the first two principal components tracked library size, and samples separated by release year (Kruskal–Wallis p < 0.001 for PC1 and PC2; Fig. 1a). After correction, release year no longer separated samples (p = 0.51 and 0.81; Fig. 1d). Shen and Sheng reported that MGMT correlated with CD34 (r = 0.76) and ACTB (r = 0.75) in raw feature counts from 20 tumors [8]. Because all gene counts scale with sequencing depth, raw counts of unrelated genes tend to correlate [7]. In our cohort, both correlations were present but weaker in raw counts (r = 0.29 and 0.42; Fig. 1b, c) and absent after correction (r = −0.04 and 0.01; Fig. 1e, f). Raw ACTB counts were also 4.6-fold higher in fresh/frozen than FFPE samples (medians; p = 0.014), a depth difference removed by normalization.

A confounder must be associated with both MGMT expression and outcome. None of the four variables was significantly associated with MGMT expression, progression-free survival (PFS), or overall survival (OS) in univariable or multivariable models (Fig. 1g); in particular, MGMT expression did not differ by preservation method (p = 0.71), although only five tumors were fresh/frozen. More directly, in the 25 patients of our original report, continuous MGMT expression, adjusted for all four variables, remained associated with PFS (adjusted hazard ratio [HR] per doubling 1.64, 95% CI 1.20–2.24, p = 0.002) and OS (adjusted HR 3.00, 95% CI 1.73–5.21, p < 0.001), independent of the median cutoff used previously [9].

Shen and Sheng also suggested that a shared artifact could inflate the correlation of MGMT expression with gene-body/intronic CpG methylation [8]. Because methylation was measured from DNA on a separate array platform, sequencing-depth artifacts alone cannot explain this correlation. In the 34 tumors with both data types, Spearman ρ between MGMT expression and methylation at cg15441483, cg18453665, and cg15782766 was 0.74, 0.75, and 0.71, and 0.58, 0.78, and 0.75 after partialing out the four variables (all p < 0.001). The association was also site-specific: the two MGMT-STP27 promoter CpGs (cg12434587 and cg12981137) correlated negatively with expression (ρ = −0.48 and −0.36) [9], a pattern also reported across 31 cancer types [1] and supported by demethylation experiments in glioblastoma cells [5].

These results support our original conclusions and indicate that batch effects in retrospective, multi-institutional RNA-seq cohorts can be mitigated. We agree with Shen and Sheng that such cohorts require care, and we suggest that studies (1) record pre-analytic variables; (2) normalize and correct for batch before comparing samples; (3) exclude low-complexity libraries, which correction cannot rescue; and (4) test whether adjustment for these variables changes the association between marker and outcome, and confirm key findings with an independent assay. We also agree that correlation alone cannot establish that gene-body methylation regulates MGMT, and that MGMT expression still requires validation in larger, prospective cohorts before it guides therapy [9].
