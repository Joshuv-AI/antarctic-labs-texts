Depth and season‐related variability in fatty acid composition and lipid nutritional quality indices of farmed Mytilus galloprovincialis
Authors: Meryem Yeşim Çelik, Bayram Köstekli, Gülşen Uzun Gören, Zeynep Hasançavuşoğlu, Mümin Metehan Kılıç, Alihan Bülbül, Beyza Dündar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDLipid quality is one of the most important indicators of the nutritional value of farmed mussels. In open‐coastal environments, factors such as cultivation depth and seasonal changes affect lipid dynamics, but their combined effects on lipid quality remain unclear. This study examined how depth and season influence fatty acids and lipid quality in farmed Mytilus galloprovincialis at 1 m and 10 m depths over a year.RESULTSAcross depths, 37 fatty acids were identified, with polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) dominating the lipid fraction (48.63% at 1 m; 48.88% at 10 m). Long‐chain n‐3 PUFAs, particularly eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), exhibited narrower and more consistent seasonal patterns at 10 m than at 1 m. Lipid quality indices remained within favorable ranges throughout the year. At 1 m, Index of Atherogenicity (IA) and Index of Thrombogenicity (TI) increased sharply in autumn, reaching approximately 0.79 and 0.30, but changes at 10 m were gradual. Flesh Lipid Quality (FLQ), Polyene Index (PI), unsaturation index (UI), and Health Promoting Index (HPI) displayed wider seasonal amplitudes at 1 m but more constrained ranges at 10 m.CONCLUSIONThis study indicates that seasonal variations primarily influence fatty acid composition, with cultivation depth playing a secondary role. Depth mainly impacts the stability of lipid quality indices rather than causing uniform changes in individual fatty acids. These results indicate that depth management could help promote product standardization in open‐coastal mussel farming. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Mussel farming plays an important role in global aquaculture, supporting food security and boosting coastal economies. In addition to the ecological function of mussels (Mytilus spp.) as filter feeders, they are valued as a nutritious seafood source, offering high‐quality protein and beneficial lipids for human consumption. Their minimal input needs and efficient biomass use make them a sustainable choice in modern aquaculture.

There has been a growing focus on the lipid composition of farmed mussels in recent years, especially their fatty acid (FA) profiles, due to their importance for nutrition and consumer health. Mussel lipids are characterized by a high polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) content, particularly eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), which are linked to cardiovascular and anti‐inflammatory benefits. 1 , 2 Consequently, FA composition has emerged as a key indicator of product quality in both food science and aquaculture research. Despite its importance, the extent to which FA composition varies under different farming conditions and the mechanisms behind this variability remain insufficiently understood.

Variability in mussel FA composition has been documented across both temporal and spatial scales within aquaculture systems. Seasonal fluctuations in lipid profiles are often linked to variations in environmental factors and food accessibility. Disparities related to depth have also been documented in suspended longline cultures. 3 , 4 However, these factors are often examined independently, despite representing interacting components of a dynamic environmental system. Consequently, the underlying mechanisms causing the observed variations in FA composition remain uncertain and require further investigation. The potential drivers of these variations include seasonal environmental forcing, depth‐related processes, or a combination of both. Although mussels generally exhibit a lipid profile dominated by PUFAs across species and cultivation systems, 5 , 6 the extent to which environmental variability and depth modulate this composition remains unclear.

Beyond individual fatty acids, various lipid nutritional quality indices have been developed to offer a comprehensive assessment of lipid value in human diets. Indices such as the PUFA/saturated fatty acid (SFA) ratio, n‐6/n‐3 ratio, index of atherogenicity (IA), index of thrombogenicity (IT), hypocholesterolemic/hypercholesterolemic ratio (h/H), health‐promoting index (HPI), unsaturation index (UI), polyene index (PI), and flesh lipid quality (FLQ) are increasingly utilized in seafood research to evaluate dietary suitability and compare product quality. These indices often reflect good nutritional qualities in mussels, supporting their role as a functional food. 2 , 7 However, the temporal variability and responsiveness of these species to environmental gradients remain poorly understood, particularly when considered alongside fatty acid composition.

Despite increasing research, there is still a lack of integrated assessments that evaluate fatty acid composition and lipid nutritional indices across seasonal and vertical gradients in real farming environments. This gap is especially clear in open‐coastal systems characterized by strong stratification and distinct seasonal hydrodynamics, like the Black Sea. In these environments, vertical variations in environmental conditions may interact with seasonal changes, making it harder to interpret lipid dynamics and their effects on product quality.

Based on this framework, this study hypothesized that (i) seasonal environmental changes mainly influence FA composition and lipid nutritional quality, and (ii) cultivation depth plays a secondary role that modulates the extent to which this variability occurs rather than determining the overall lipid quality. The study examined monthly fatty acid composition and lipid nutritional quality indices of farmed Mytilus galloprovincialis cultivated at depths of 1 m and 10 m in the Black Sea. By combining fatty acid profiles with multiple nutritional indices across different seasons and depths, the aim of this work was to clarify the roles of environmental variability and depth in influencing lipid dynamics and to evaluate their implications for maintaining product quality in mussel aquaculture.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and sampling strategy

This study was designed as a depth‐structured investigation of the fatty acid profile of farmed Mytilus galloprovincialis cultivated at two different depths (1 m and 10 m) within a semi‐enclosed brackish marine environment. The research was conducted along the Black Sea coast (41° 54′ 37.093″ N, 35° 09′ 47.145″ E) at a site 35–42 m deep from May 2023 to April 2024. All field and laboratory procedures were conducted at the Mussel and Oyster Aquaculture Research Facility, Faculty of Fisheries, Sinop University, Turkey. A surface longline system was employed, with lantern nets deployed at depths of 1 m and 10 m. At each depth, three lantern nets (diameter: 50 cm; mesh size: 11 mm; tray spacing: 30 cm) were hung on the same longline, spaced approximately 1 m apart horizontally. Each lantern comprised two trays, each stocked with 250 mussels, totaling 500 mussels per lantern and 1500 mussels per depth.

All lantern units were integrated into a single longline system to ensure that mussels at both depths experienced the same broader environmental regime, allowing depth to be evaluated as a within‐system vertical gradient rather than a site‐level treatment. All experimental units were located on the same longline, so lantern nets were not considered fully independent experimental replicates. Instead, the study was structured as a repeated‐sampling design over time, with depth treated as a fixed factor and month as the primary source of independent variation. Sampling was carried out monthly over a year to capture seasonal variability. Each month, ten mussels were gathered randomly from each tray across the three lantern nets, resulting in a total of 60 mussels at each depth. At each depth and sampling month, mussels were randomly divided into three groups. Each group consisted of ten individuals, which had their soft tissues pooled, dried, and processed separately. Each pooled set acted as an individual biological replicate. The soft tissues were removed from the shells, oven‐dried at 105 °C for 12 h, ground into a powder, homogenized, and then stored at −20 °C until analysis. Fatty acid composition was determined from this composite sample. Each sample was analyzed using multiple gas chromatography injections (technical replicates), and the resulting values were averaged prior to statistical analysis to avoid pseudo‐replication.

Environmental parameters

To characterize the environmental conditions at the cultivation site, measurements of seawater temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen (mg L−1), pH, total suspended matter (TSM), inorganic matter (IM), and organic matter (OM) were conducted monthly at 1 m and 10 m depths throughout the study period. In situ temperature and salinity were measured using a YSI 6600 multiparameter probe (YSI Inc., Yellow Springs, OH, USA). Seawater samples were collected using a Nansen bottle. For particulate matter analysis, triplicate 3 L seawater samples were filtered through pre‐weighed GF/C filters (Whatman, Maidstone, UK). Total suspended matter, IM, and OM were determined as described in the literature. 8 These measurements were used to describe environmental conditions co‐occurring with mussel cultivation across depths and seasons.

Fatty acid analysis

Fatty acid profiles were determined by an accredited external laboratory. Mussel samples were preserved at −20 °C until analysis. The extraction of lipids and the preparation of fatty acid methyl esters (FAME) adhered to the guidelines set out in European Commission Regulation EEC/2568/91 (Annexes X.A and X.B). Fatty acid methyl esters were analyzed using an Agilent Technologies gas chromatography system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with an HP‐88 capillary column (100 m × 0.25 mm × 0.20 μm film thickness). Helium served as the carrier gas. Samples of 1 μL were injected in split mode (1:99) at a temperature of 250 °C. The oven temperature program started at 165 °C for 10 min, then increased gradually at 1 °C per minute to 175 °C, followed by an increase at 5 °C per minute to 220 °C, where it was maintained for an additional 10 min. Individual fatty acids were identified by comparing retention times with a 37‐component FAME standard mixture from Supelco (Bellefonte, PA, USA). Each biological replicate was analyzed independently. Repeated GC injections were used only to ensure analytical precision, and mean values were used in all statistical analyses.

Lipid nutritional quality indices

To evaluate the lipid nutritional quality, several established indices were calculated based on fatty acid composition. These included the Index of Atherogenicity (IA), Index of Thrombogenicity (IT), Flesh Lipid Quality (FLQ), hypocholesterolemic/hypercholesterolemic (h/H), Polyene Index (PI), Health_Promoting Index (HPI) Unsaturated Index (UI), Monounsaturated Fatty Acids (MUFA), Polyunsaturated Fatty Acids (PUFA) and Saturated Fatty Acids (SFA). Data use and scope

This research is part of a larger project investigating how depth influences variability in farmed M. galloprovincialis within Black Sea cultivation environments. To maintain clarity, data from this study have been presented in separate manuscripts, each addressing specific research questions. This paper specifically examines fatty acid profiles and lipid nutritional quality indices. Environmental measurements are included only to provide context for the cultivation conditions. All fatty acid data reported are original and have not been published previously.

Statistical analysis

Fatty acid data were treated as compositional and transformed using the centered log‐ratio (CLR) transformation prior to analysis. Environmental variables were standardized (z‐scores). Depth‐related differences in individual fatty acids were evaluated at the monthly level using exact permutation tests based on biological replicates (n = 3 per depth). This non‐parametric method was chosen because of the small sample size and does not assume any specific data distribution. Multivariate patterns in fatty acid composition were examined using principal component analysis (PCA) on CLR‐transformed data. Environmental variables were integrated into the multivariate structure by fitting them onto the PCA ordination with the envfit procedure. To examine the effects of depth, season, and their interaction on overall fatty acid composition, permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was used on the CLR‐transformed data. The relationships between fatty acids and environmental variables were tested using Spearman correlations, focusing on fatty acids with significant depth effects and adjusting P‐values with false discovery rate (FDR). All analyses were performed, and related figures were generated, using R version 4.4.1 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Environmental variability

Environmental variables showed strong seasonal changes at both depths, while vertical differences were usually moderate and depended on the specific parameter (Table 1). Surface waters were warmer than the deeper layers during stratification, showing the largest temperature difference in July (24.54 °C at 1 m compared with 20.83 °C at 10 m). This temperature gradient mostly disappeared during winter. Dissolved oxygen showed clear seasonal variation, increasing from summer and autumn levels to a peak in March at 1 m. Salinity and pH stayed relatively consistent throughout the year, with slightly higher readings typically observed at 10 m. Total suspended matter and its OM displayed marked temporal variability without a consistent vertical pattern. In April, TSM peaked at a depth of 1 m at 7.42 mg L−1, whereas OM exhibited distinct episodic peaks during winter, especially in February. These peaks indicate short‐term boosts in organic material rather than consistent seasonal trends. Overall, environmental conditions were mainly driven by temporal variability, with depth‐related differences remaining secondary and inconsistent across various parameters.

Fatty acid composition and lipid classes

A total of 37 fatty acids were identified in mussel tissues at both depths (Supporting Information, Table S1). Polyunsaturated fatty acids consistently remained the dominant lipid class, surpassing SFAs and MUFAs at both depths (Table 2). Temporal variability in PUFA contribution was greater at 1 m (44.08% to 54.90%) compared to 10 m (46.78% to 50.68%), indicating a wider seasonal range in surface mussels. Monthly patterns showed that the proportion of PUFAs generally increased in winter, whereas those of SFAs and MUFAs were relatively higher during summer and fall. At the level of individual fatty acids, EPA and DHA exhibited distinct temporal changes (P < 0.05). Docosahexaenoic acid levels were highest during late autumn and winter, reaching 20.50% at 1 m in January, whereas EPA peaked in winter–early spring, reaching 12.11% at 1 m in January. These increases coincided with elevated C18:3n3 levels, indicating a coordinated seasonal enrichment of n‐3 PUFA‐rich profiles. In contrast, saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids, especially C16:0 and C18:1n9c, tended to be more prevalent during warmer months, although this pattern was not strictly consistent across all months. Seasonal variability was further supported by significant month‐to‐month differences for multiple fatty acids, as shown by superscript groupings in Supporting Information, Table S1.

Univariate and multivariate analysis

Depth‐related differences in fatty acid composition varied and were strongly influenced by both fatty acid type and season (Fig. 1). Although several fatty acids exhibited significant differences between depths in specific months (P < 0.05), these differences were episodic rather than consistent throughout the year. The ΔCLR heatmap confirmed this, showing compound‐specific but temporally inconsistent depth‐related differences without a persistent vertical structure.

Heatmap showing monthly depth‐related differences in fatty acid composition of Mytilus galloprovincialis. Values represent centered log‐ratio (CLR)‐transformed differences between 1 and 10 m (1–10 m), where positive values (red) indicate higher proportions at 1 m and negative values (blue) indicate enrichment at 10 m. The pattern reveals strong temporal variability, with depth‐related differences changing in both direction and magnitude across months. May, the baseline, was excluded (Δ = 0).

Multivariate analysis confirmed the limited influence of depth. The PERMANOVA showed a significant effect of season (P = 0.001), but depth (P = 0.867) and the interaction between depth and season (P = 0.851) were not significant. Principal component analysis revealed distinct seasonal clustering of samples, with significant overlap across depths (Fig. 2). The first two principal components explained 43.6% and 27.9% of the total variance, respectively. This indicates that the primary variation in fatty acid composition was more affected by temporal gradients than by vertical differences. Environmental fitting indicated that OM (r 2 = 0.197) was the most significant explanatory variable, followed by pH (r 2 = 0.163), IM (r 2 = 0.127), salinity (r 2 = 0.113), and temperature (r 2 = 0.097). Dissolved oxygen and TSM were not significant. These findings suggest that organic and particulate dynamics mainly drove the changes in fatty acid variability, aligning with seasonal variations in lipid composition.

Principal component analysis biplot of fatty acid composition in mussels at 1 m and 10 m, with PC1 and PC2 explaining 71.5% of the total variance (43.6% and 27.9%). Samples were clustered primarily by season, but depth‐related differences were limited. Environmental variables were fitted using envfit, with organic matter (OM), pH, inorganic matter (IM), salinity, and temperature identified as significant drivers.

Depth‐specific correlation analysis revealed a clear restructuring of fatty acid–environment relationships between 1 m and 10 m (Fig. 3). At 1 m depth, the variability in fatty acids was mainly related to dissolved oxygen, showing strong positive correlations for C18:3n3 and C20:5n3. pH and OM also played significant roles, especially for C20:1 and C22:1n9. Negative correlations were found between oxygen and C16:1, temperature and C18:3n3, and salinity and C18:1n9c. At a depth of 10 m, the correlation pattern changed, becoming more influenced by particulate matter and chemical factors. Positive associations included pH with C20:4n6, oxygen with C18:3n3, and TSM with C20:1, whereas IM was positively related to C18:2n6c. Strong negative relationships were observed between IM and C22:1n9, and between OM and both C18:1n9c and C18:2n6c. Several fatty acids showed depth‐dependent changes in their environmental associations. For instance, C22:1n9 was positively associated with OM at 1 m but negatively associated with IM at 10 m, whereas C18:1n9c shifted from a negative association with salinity at 1 m to a negative association with OM at 10 m. Overall, these findings indicate that depth‐related effects on fatty acid composition were secondary and inconsistent over time at the community level but could be significant at the level of individual fatty acids, reflecting depth‐dependent environmental influences rather than a stable vertical pattern in lipid composition.

Lipid quality indices

Lipid quality indices exhibited noticeable seasonal changes, whereas differences related to depth were limited and inconsistent (Table 2). The n3/n6 ratio reached its highest point in winter, particularly at a 1 m depth (up to 4.58), and was more stable at a 10 m depth. Similarly, DHA/EPA ratios increased from autumn to early winter and then declined, showing no clear pattern with depth. IA and IT values showed moderate variation across months but stayed within a narrow range at both depths, suggesting consistent cardiovascular‐related lipid quality throughout the year. The PI and HPI showed more distinct seasonal patterns, with higher values in winter indicating a greater contribution of PUFA. This variation was more noticeable at 1 m than at 10 m. Overall, lipid quality indices were mainly influenced by seasonal changes in fatty acid composition, whereas depth had a secondary effect.

DISCUSSION

Depth‐related variation in fatty acid composition

The fatty acid composition of M. galloprovincialis differed between cultivation depths, with surface (1 m) and deeper (10 m) mussels displaying distinct compositional patterns across the sampling period. However, these differences were not consistent throughout the year and appeared to vary with temporal environmental conditions rather than reflecting a fixed depth‐driven effect. These differences were observed alongside contrasting environmental background conditions characterizing the two depth layers, which together reflect vertical heterogeneity in the Black Sea water column and its influence on food availability, metabolic activity, and physicochemical variability.

Surface waters (1 m) exhibited greater temporal variability in salinity, consistent with the brackish characteristics of the basin. Comparable patterns have been reported in dynamic coastal and estuarine environments, where fluctuations in salinity, temperature, and seston availability increase variability in fatty acid composition. 10 In this study, the increased environmental variability at 1 m was linked to more extensive temporal fluctuations in various fatty acid groups than at 10 m, indicating a greater impact of short‐term environmental forces at that depth.

Consistent differences in pH were also observed between depths, with slightly lower values at 1 m during summer months (P < 0.01). Although these differences were small, pH correlated with changes in fatty acid composition and other environmental factors like temperature and dissolved oxygen, suggesting that they are part of a combined environmental signal rather than acting independently.

Correlation analysis indicated associations between pH and selected fatty acids, including C18:1n9t, as well as with temperature and dissolved oxygen. In multivariate space, pH exhibited a broader and more dispersed association pattern at 1 m, whereas at 10 m its relationships were more limited, especially with DHA (C22:6n3) and C20:4n6, likely indicating decreased environmental variability at greater depth.

These patterns suggest that depth‐related pH differences form part of the environmental background linked with observed FA variability rather than acting as isolated factors. These associations are consistent with reports that fatty acid composition may be sensitive to subtle pH shifts when combined with other environmental factors, particularly under warming conditions. 11 , 12

Across both depths, dissolved oxygen showed positive associations with EPA (C20:5n3) and α‐linolenic acid (C18:3n3). Similar associations have been reported in marine bivalves, 13 , 14 although these are generally interpreted as reflecting linked changes in metabolic activity and dietary inputs rather than oxygen acting as an isolated driver. This study found higher levels of these fatty acids at 1 m during times with greater fluctuations in oxygen and temperature. In contrast, measurements at 10 m showed smaller variations, indicating less short‐term environmental change at greater depths.

Overall, these patterns suggest that fatty acid variability at 1 m depth is affected by changing environmental factors, including temperature, oxygen, and seston dynamics. These changes may boost metabolic activity and influence the availability of lipids from phytoplankton. Conversely, the more stable environment at 10 m appears to buffer these short‐term changes, resulting in more consistent fatty acid profiles. However, because environmental parameters vary strongly together, it is difficult to determine the separate impact of each factor in this analysis. Monounsaturated fatty acid exhibited pronounced seasonal variations at 1 m, with higher proportions recorded during summer months. Similar seasonal increases have been observed in Mytilus spp. in shallow‐water systems, often linked to temperature‐driven metabolic shifts and changes in food quality. 15 , 16 At 10 m, MUFA proportions showed a reduced seasonal amplitude, likely indicating lower environmental variability and more stable feeding conditions at this depth.

Temperature‐related associations with fatty acid groups also varied with depth. At 1 m, temperature was associated with increases in several saturated and monounsaturated fatty acids, whereas relationships with polyunsaturated fatty acids were less pronounced. In contrast, at 10 m, temperature showed weaker associations with individual fatty acids and lipid classes, and multivariate analyses indicated a weaker connection between temperature and fatty acid composition and environmental variables.

These contrasting patterns indicate that temperature effects on fatty acid composition were more pronounced under variable surface conditions whereas at depth they were constrained within a more stable environment. This view aligns with studies showing that bivalves in thermally buffered environments tend to maintain more consistent lipid profiles across seasons. Overall, the results suggest that fatty acid composition is mainly shaped by seasonal environmental changes, with depth serving as a secondary factor that modifies these patterns rather than driving them. From a production standpoint, these findings suggest that cultivation depth may affect the variability, rather than the overall composition, of fatty acid profiles in farmed M. galloprovincialis.

C20:1 and C20:4n6 acids are established components of reproductive lipid pathways during the known spawning period. 17 Previous studies have shown that C20:4n6 levels are closely linked to gonadal development, peaking during spawning or gonadal regression. 18 , 19 , 20 Similarly, C20:1 has been reported as a dominant fatty acid in gonadal tissue during reproductive periods, 20 indicating that some of the fatty acid variability may also reflect physiological and reproductive processes alongside environmental influences.

Depth‐related variation in lipid nutritional quality indices

Lipid nutritional quality indices highlight specific fatty acids because of their physiological importance to human health. These indices combine the relative impacts of multiple fatty acids instead of showing the effect of just one compound. Saturated fatty acids, such as C12:0, C14:0, C16:0, and C18:0, are associated with increased cardiovascular risk, while unsaturated fatty acids, including C18:1n9, C18:2n6, C18:3n3, C20:4n6, EPA (C20:5n3), and DHA (C22:6n3) are linked to anti‐inflammatory effects and enhanced lipid metabolism. Accordingly, these indices provide a composite measure of lipid nutritional quality and cardiovascular relevance. 7

In the present study, the assessment of lipid nutritional indices in mussels cultivated at depths of 1 m and 10 m revealed minor depth‐related differences in lipid quality indicators. Across seasons, indices such as FLQ, PI, HPI, and UI remained slightly higher and more stable at 10 m, especially during the summer. This pattern might indicate the combined impact of decreased environmental variability, thermal buffering, and more consistent food supply at depth, all of which can affect the proportion of n‐3 PUFAs, including EPA and DHA. Similar patterns have been reported in bivalves from colder and more stable environments, where higher EPA + DHA content and n‐3/n‐6 ratios have been observed, 21 , 22 although these responses are mostly connected to combined environmental and dietary effects rather than stability alone. Seasonal peaks in PUFA (50%) content, including EPA (20.68%) and DHA (17.54%), have also been documented, 23 typically associated with phytoplankton dynamics and temperature‐dependent metabolic processes.

Stratev et al. 24 found that the PUFA ratio in Black Sea mussels peaked at 52.54% in October, concurrently with the lowest values for IA (0.68) and IT (0.24) and the highest h/H ratio (1.92). This period also coincided with the seasonal phytoplankton bloom and elevated EPA (22.74%) and DHA (23.89%) levels. Experimental studies have shown that thermal stress can negatively impact PUFA‐based indices in Mytilus galloprovincialis, resulting in reductions in FLQ from 24.6 to 18.4, UI from 186 to 150, PI from 0.88 to 0.58, and HPI from 1.24 to 0.86. 2 Similarly, Tan et al. 22 demonstrated that PUFA/SFA and n‐3/n‐6 ratios declined with increasing temperature in temperate regions. Lipid profiles have also been reported to vary with depth and hydrodynamic conditions, with deeper, more stable sites supporting more consistent PUFA composition across seasons. 25 In the present study, similar trends were observed, as PUFA‐derived indices exhibited peaks in mid‐to‐late summer at a depth of 10 m.

The IA and IT are well‐established markers of cardiovascular risk, influenced primarily by the relative amounts of SFAs, especially C14:0, C16:0, and C18:0. Threshold values of IA ≥1.0 and IT ≥0.5 are generally seen as risk indicators, whereas values below these thresholds are considered beneficial for human health. 2 , 26 In this study, although the annual averages of IA and IT were similar at both depths, the monthly trends exhibited notable differences. Mussels cultivated at 1 m showed a sharp rise in IA and IT in October, peaking in November (IA= 0.79, IT = 0.30), and then rapidly declined in December. This pattern may be associated with post‐spawning physiological shifts and concurrent environmental changes. Conversely, mussels at 10 m exhibited a steady, gradual decline in IA and IT from September to December, which may indicate more stable environmental and feeding conditions at that depth rather than an intrinsic depth‐related effect. These depth‐related fluctuations are consistent with previous observations, where IA and IT values tended to be higher under environmentally variable or stressful conditions. For example, Stratev et al. 24 observed that IA reached 0.94 in June and IT 0.37 in July, declining to IA = 0.68 and IT = 0.24 by October. Similarly, Moniruzzaman et al. 27 reported IA values ranging from 0.74 to 1.74 and IT from 0.5 to 2.6 in various mollusks, with the highest values found in freshwater or environmentally unstable habitats. Biandolino et al. 2 demonstrated that elevated temperatures can drive IA and IT towards critical thresholds, reaching values of 1.18 and 0.50, respectively. Similarly, warm conditions have been linked to SFA accumulation and EPA reduction in Mytilus spp., 28 supporting the idea that thermal variability may affect lipid quality negatively. Overall, these findings suggest that changes in lipid nutritional indices are primarily driven by seasonal environmental factors and the associated metabolic responses, with depth serving as a secondary factor that influences the magnitude of these changes rather than their direction.

CONCLUSION

This research explored how depth and season influence the fatty acid composition and lipid nutritional quality indices of farmed M. galloprovincialis grown in open‐coastal Black Sea environments. Throughout the study period, mussels at both depths showed lipid profiles high in polyunsaturated fatty acids, highlighting their nutritional importance in aquaculture systems. The results show that fatty acid composition and lipid nutritional quality are influenced primarily by seasonal environmental changes, with depth serving as a secondary, context‐dependent factor. Mussels at the surface (1 m) displayed greater temporal variability in fatty acid composition and lipid indices, while mussels at deeper levels (10 m) demonstrated comparatively less variability across seasons. These patterns were reflected in composite lipid nutritional quality indices, including the FLQ, PI, UI, and HPI, which remained within nutritionally favorable ranges at both depths, suggesting that differences were mainly due to variability rather than overall quality. From a production standpoint, these results indicate that cultivation depth likely affects the uniformity of lipid quality rather than its overall nutritional content. Surface cultivation might lead to greater variability and occasional peaks in specific fatty acids, whereas deeper cultivation tends to reduce short‐term fluctuations, producing more consistent lipid profiles over time. This study highlights the potential role of depth in influencing lipid variability. By integrating fatty acid composition and multiple lipid quality indices across seasonal and depth gradients, the results offer a more comprehensive understanding of how environmental variability affects product quality. This research demonstrates that the nutritional quality of lipids in M. galloprovincialis is shaped mainly by seasonal environmental changes, and cultivation depth functions as a modulating factor that influences the stability of fatty acid composition under actual farming conditions.
