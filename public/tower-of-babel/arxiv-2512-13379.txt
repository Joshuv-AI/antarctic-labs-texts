Fundamental bound on entanglement generation between interacting Rydberg atoms
Georgios Doultsinos, Antonis Delakouras, David Petrosyan

Abstract
We analytically derive the fundamental lower bound for the preparation fidelity of a maximally-entangled (Bell) state of two atoms involving Rydberg-state interactions. This bound represents the minimum achievable error E ≥ ( 1 + π / 2 ) ​ Γ / B E\geq(1+\pi/2)\Gamma/B due to spontaneous decay Γ \Gamma of the Rydberg states and their finite interaction strength B B . Using quantum optimal control methods, we identify laser pulses for preparing a maximally-entangled state of a pair of atomic qubits with an error only 1 % 1\% above the derived fundamental bound.



Introduction –

Neutral atoms in arrays of reconfigurable microtraps
1
;
5
;
2
represent one of the leading platforms for physical implementation of quantum computing
22
;
12
;
8
;
9
;
6
;
13
;
20
;
14
.
In these systems, the main mechanism to realize quantum gates or produce entanglement between atomic qubits relies on laser-excitation of the atoms to Rydberg states
18
, i.e., states with high principal quantum number
n
.
Rydberg states possess large (transition or induced) dipole moments
\wp\propto n^{2}

7
, which enables them to interact strongly over mesoscopic distances via van der Waals or dipole-dipole interactions with strength
B\propto n^{11}/x^{6}
or
\propto n^{4}/x^{3}
, respectively, depending on the selected state and the interatomic distance
x
.
Moreover, Rydberg states are relatively stable and, in cryogenic environments, spontaneously decay with small but non-negligible rates
\Gamma\propto n^{-3}
.
This requires quantum gates and entangling operations, such as preparation of a Bell state, to be performed in short times
T\ll\tau=1/\Gamma
, while minimizing the population of the decaying states, in order to achieve high fidelities.

The speed of these operations is limited, in the blockade regime
B\gg\Omega
, by the amplitude
\Omega
of the laser pulse driving the atoms between the ground and Rydberg states, and the time-optimal pulses have been determined in Ref.
10
and employed in most experiments ever since
6
;
13
;
20
;
14
.
But ultimately the error
E=\Gamma T_{r}=\eta\Gamma/B
is determined by the average time
T_{r}
that the atoms spend in the decaying Rydberg states, and is proportional to the ratio
\Gamma/B
and scaling coefficient
\eta
that depends on the laser pulses.
Hence, identifying pulses that minimize
\eta
, and thereby
T_{r}
, is highly desirable and important, as has been highlighted in several works

18
;
19
;
16
, but, to
the best of our knowledge, never satisfactorily addressed.

Here we show that the error
E
of creating a maximally-entangled (Bell) state of two atoms, or qubits, is bounded by
E\geq\eta_{\min}\Gamma/B
where
\eta_{\min}=\left(1+\pi/2\right)
.
Furthermore, we demonstrate that this bound is tight:
We identify laser pulses that generate entanglement with error only about
\sim 1\%
above this theoretical limit. This result refines previous estimates
21
, which (after correcting numerical inaccuracies, see Appendix
B
) yielded a weaker lower bound of
E\gtrsim 1.05\,\Gamma/B
.

Lower bound for decay error –

We consider two atoms,
A
and
B
, each possessing
d\geq 2
internal electronic states, one of which is an excited Rydberg state
\ket{r}
.
The total Hamiltonian of the system
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{int}}+\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}
consists of the interaction and local terms.
We assume dispersive interaction given by (
\hbar=1
throughout this work)

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{int}}=B\,\ket{rr}\bra{rr}.

(1)

The local term
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{B}+\mathbb{1}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
describes single-atom laser driving between the Rydberg state and the remaining stable, non-interacting electronic states. The precise form of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}
is left unspecified at this stage.

At any time
t
, we can use the Schmidt decomposition to express the state of the system as

\ket{\psi(t)}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}(t)\,\ket{u_i(t)}\ket{v_i(t)},

(2)

where
c_{i}(t)\geq 0
are the time dependent Schmidt coefficients satisfying
\sum_{i}c_{i}^{2}(t)=1
, while
\{\ket{u_i}\},\{\ket{v_i}\}
are orthonormal bases in the Hilbert spaces of atoms
A
and
B
, respectively.
Our goal is to transform an initial product state
\ket{\psi(0)}\equiv\ket{\psi_\mathrm{in}}
with
c_{1}(0)=1
and
c_{i>1}(0)=0
into a maximally-entangled (Bell) state
\ket{\psi(T)}\equiv\ket{\psi_\mathrm{f}}
with
c_{1}(T)=c_{2}(T)=1/\sqrt{2}
and
c_{i>2}(T)=0
, with the highest possible fidelity
F=\big|\bra{\psi_\mathrm{f}}\mathcal{U}(T)\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{in}}}\big|^{2}\simeq 1
,
where
\mathcal{U}(t)=\mathcal{T}\exp\{-i\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{H}(t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}\}
is the unitary time-evolution operator and
T
is the process duration.

For
T\ll\tau=1/\Gamma
, decay of the Rydberg states with rate
\Gamma
can be accounted for via the non-Hermitian contribution
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}=\mathcal{H}-i\frac{\Gamma}{2}\,\Pi_{r},
where
\Pi_{r}=\sum_{j=A,B}\ket{r}_{j}\bra{r}
is a projector onto the Rydberg states.
Since entanglement generation necessarily involves populating the interacting Rydberg states, the resulting decay error is given by

E\simeq\Gamma T_{r}\equiv\Gamma\int_{0}^{T}P_{r}(t)\,dt,

(3)

where
T_{r}
is the average time that the system spends in the Rydberg states and
P_{r}(t)=\bra{\psi(t)}\Pi_{r}\ket{\psi(t)}
is the instantaneous Rydberg-state population of the system in state
\ket{\psi(t)}=\mathcal{U}(t)\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{in}}}
.
Hence, our goal is to perform the transformation
\ket{\psi_\mathrm{in}}\to\ket{\psi_\mathrm{f}}
while minimizing the accumulated decay error
E
.
The smallest possible error will determine an upper bound on the entanglement fidelity.

To calculate the bound, we follow an analysis similar to that of Ref.
4
and consider the so-called min-entropy
S=-\log_{2}c_{1}^{2}
, where
c_{1}\equiv c_{\mathrm{max}}
is the largest Schmidt coefficient.
Clearly,
S(\psi_{\mathrm{in}})=0
for the initial state, and
S(\psi_{\mathrm{f}})=1
for the final state.
It is important to note that a state with
S=1
is not necessarily a Bell state, except when
d=2
.
Nevertheless, determining the minimum decay error for attaining states with
S=1
provides a bound that also applies to the special case of Bell-state preparation.
Substituting the Schmidt decomposition (
2
) into the Schrödinger equation
i\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\psi}=\mathcal{H}\ket{\psi}

and omitting the explicit time dependence of variables to simplify notation, we find that
\dot{c}_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}\,\mathrm{Im}\bra{u_1 v_1}\mathcal{H}\ket{u_i v_i}
(see Appendix
B
).
The time derivative of the min-entropy is then

\dot{S}(\psi)=-\frac{2}{\ln{2}}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}\,\mathrm{Im}\bra{u_1 v_1}\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{int}}\ket{u_i v_i}}{c_{1}},

(4)

where we replaced
\mathcal{H}
with
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{int}}
, since for unitary evolution the min-entropy can change only due to interaction-induced dynamics.

We can now determine the bound for the average time
T_{r}
that the atoms spend in the Rydberg states as follows.
During the evolution, the min-entropy does not necessarily increase monotonically from
S=0\to 1
, but using the coarea formula for one-dimensional integrals (see Appendix
A.1
) we can write

T_{r}\equiv\int_{0}^{T}P_{r}(t)\,dt\geq\int_{0}^{1}G(s)\,ds,

(5)

where

G(s)\equiv\min_{\psi_{s}}\frac{P_{r}(\psi_{s})}{|\dot{S}(\psi_{s})|},

(6)

and the minimum is taken over all states
\ket{\psi_s}
satifying
S(\psi_{s})=s
, or, equivalently, all states with
c_{1}=2^{-s/2}
.
A closed-form expression for
G
can be obtained analytically (see Appendix
A.2
) and it leads to
c_{i>2}=0
, thus implying
c_{2}=\sqrt{1-2^{-s}}
.
This yields
G=\frac{\ln 2}{B}\,\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}(c_{1}+c_{2})^{2}
, or, in terms of the min-entropy,

G(s)=\frac{\ln 2}{B}\>\frac{(1+\sqrt{2^{s}-1})^{2}}{2^{s}\sqrt{2^{s}-1}}.

(7)

Integrating
G(s)
over the interval
s\in[0,1]
yields
T_{r}\geq(1+\pi/2)/B
and therefore

E\geq\eta_{\min}\frac{\Gamma}{B},\quad\eta_{\min}=1+\frac{\pi}{2}.

(8)

From the same analysis, we can also determine the time duration
T
of the transformation as

T=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{ds}{\dot{S}},

(9)

where, for the
\ket{\psi_s}
that minimizes the right-hand side of Eq. (
6
), we have

\dot{S}=B\,\frac{2}{\ln 2}\,\frac{2^{s}-1}{(1+\sqrt{2^{s}-1})^{2}}.

(10)

Remarkably, the integral in Eq. (
9
) diverges as
s\to 0
, implying that the optimal evolution that saturates inequality (
8
) can be attained only in the limit of
BT\to\infty
.
Nevertheless, below we identify finite-duration pulses for the preparation of a maximally-entangled (Bell) state with error that approaches the fundamental bound (
8
) to within
\sim 1\%
deviation.

Laser pulse via GRAPE optimization –

The minimal system to exhibit entanglement corresponds to a pair of two-level atoms with the internal states
\ket{g}
and
\ket{r}
that can serve as qubit basis states
\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}
.
For alkali or alkaline-earth atoms commonly used in experiments
12
;
8
;
9
;
6
;
13
;
20
;
14
,
\ket{g}
corresponds to a long-lived lower electronic state, while
\ket{r}
is a highly-excited Rydberg state.
These states are coupled by laser field(s) with the time-dependent Rabi frequency
\Omega(t)
and detuning
\Delta(t)
, see Fig.
1
(a).
The lasers act symmetrically on both atoms
A
and
B
.
The local Hamiltonian is thus

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{loc}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\Omega(t)\sum_{j}\big(\sigma_{rg}^{(j)}+\sigma_{gr}^{(j)}\big)-\Delta(t)\sum_{j}\sigma_{rr}^{(j)},

(11)

where
\sigma_{\mu\nu}^{(j)}=\ket{\mu}_{j}\bra{\nu}
is the transition (
\mu\neq\nu
) or projection (
\mu=\nu
) operator for atom
j=A,B
.

A naive approach to entangle the two atoms initially in state
\ket{\psi_\mathrm{in}}=\ket{gg}
is to
apply a very strong (
\Omega\gg B
) and short resonant
\pi/2
-pulse to both atoms to prepare them in state
\tfrac{1}{2}(\ket{g}+\ket{r})(\ket{g}+\ket{r})
.
Then, with
\Omega=0
, one can wait for time
T=\pi/B
, during which the system accumulates the dynamical interaction phase
\phi_{\mathrm{int}}=BT=\pi
and evolves into the maximally entangled state
\ket{\psi_\mathrm{f}}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\ket{gg}+\ket{gr}+\ket{rg}-\ket{rr})

1
1

1

This state is equivalent to the Bell state
(\ket{gg}+\ket{rr})/\sqrt{2}
, up to a single atom (
A
or
B
) rotation
\ket{g,r}\to(\ket{g}\pm\ket{r})/\sqrt{2}
by a resonant
\pi/2
-pulse.
.
This protocol corresponds to the fastest possible way to generate entanglement between the two atoms, but it involves the Rydberg-state population
P_{r}(t)=1\>\forall\,t
, hence
T_{r}=T
, leading to the decay error
E\gtrsim\pi\,\Gamma/B
that exceeds the fundamental bound (
8
) by a factor of
\sim 1.22
.

We therefore consider dynamical laser driving of the system initially in state
\ket{\psi_\mathrm{in}}=\ket{gg}
and set the target state
\ket{\psi_\mathrm{f}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\ket{gg}+\ket{rg}+\ket{gr}-\ket{rr}\big)
.
Since the Hamiltonian is symmetric under exchange of the atoms, the dynamics remain confined to the symmetric subspace of the two-atom Hilbert space,
H_{\mathrm{sym}}=\mathrm{span}\{\ket{gg},\,\ket{W},\,\ket{rr}\}
, where
\ket{W}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{gr}+\ket{rg})
, and we neglect dephasing that could couple the bright state
\ket{W}
to the dark state
\ket{D}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{gr}-\ket{rg})
.
Within this subspace [see Fig.
1
(a)], the system reduces to a three-level ladder system governed by the effective Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Omega&0\\
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Omega&-\Delta&\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Omega\\
0&\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Omega&B-2\Delta\end{pmatrix}.

(12)

Our task is to prepare the state

\ket{\psi_\mathrm{f}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\ket{gg}+\sqrt{2}\ket{W}-\ket{rr}\big)
using a laser pulse with time-dependent
\Omega(t),\Delta(t)
whereas the interaction strength
B
is fixed, while minimizing the Rydberg-state population
P_{r}
which is the expectation value of operator
\Pi_{r}^{\prime}=\mathrm{diag}(0,1,2)
.
This problem does not admit a trivial solution.

\ket{\psi}

P_{r}(\psi)/\dot{S}(\psi)\simeq G(S(\psi))
of Eq. (

).

To determine the optimal pulses, we employ the gradient ascent pulse engineering (GRAPE) method
11
.
For the numerical optimization, we set the total evolution time
T
and divide it into
N\gg 1
equal time steps
t_{n}=n\,\mathrm{d}t
with
\mathrm{d}t=T/N
. We then optimize simultaneously over the discrete values of the Rabi frequency
\Omega_{n}=\Omega(t_{n})
and the detuning
\Delta_{n}=\Delta(t_{n})
for
n=0,\dots,N

2
2

2

Note that since
B
is finite, the GRAPE optimization must find both the pulse amplitude
\Omega(t)
and phase
\varphi(t)
[or detuning
\Delta(t)=d\varphi(t)/dt
], unlike the case of
B\to\infty
where the time-optimal pulse is the one with maximum
\Omega=\mathrm{const}
for all
t\in[0,T]
and only the phase
\varphi(t)
is a function of time
10
.
.
The parameter space thus consists of
2(N+1)
variables, and we evaluate the gradients of the fidelity
\delta F/\delta\Omega_{n}
and
\delta F/\delta\Delta_{n}
, see Appendix
A.3
.

In addition to maximizing the fidelity
F
, the solution must minimize
T_{r}
and thereby the decay error
E=\Gamma T_{r}
.
We therefore define the cost functional

J(\gamma)=F-\gamma T_{r},

(13)

where
\gamma>0
controls the strength of the penalty.
During the optimization,
\gamma
is gradually reduced from
10^{-3}B
to zero.
This strategy, commonly used in constrained optimization
15
, helps the algorithm to first explore solutions with low Rydberg population and not necessarily optimal fidelity
F=1
.
Once the optimization has converged to a good region of the parameter space, the penalty
\gamma
is progressively relaxed to focus on maximizing the fidelity.
The gradients
\delta T_{r}/\delta\Omega_{n}
and
\delta T_{r}/\delta\Delta_{n}
are also computed (see Appendix
A.3
) and combined with those of the fidelity to construct the overall gradient of the cost function.
The total gradients are then used within the BFGS (Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shannon) algorithm
15
to identify optimal solutions.

Discussion –

To summarize, using general arguments, we have derived the fundamental lower bound (
8
) for the error
E
during the preparation of fully entangled states of a pair of atoms interacting via decaying Rydberg states.
Our derivation of the error bound is mathematically rigorous and can be applied to other similar systems for quantum information processing and provide useful fidelity bounds in the presence of decay or other relaxation processes.
This bound also applies to the average error of all two-qubit Rydberg gates that can maximally entangle four orthogonal input product states, such as the CZ gate, i.e.

\min\bar{E}=\Gamma\int_{0}^{T}\bar{P}_{r}(t)\,dt\geq\eta_{\min}\Gamma/B
. Here

\bar{P}_{r}=\tfrac{1}{4}\sum_{q_{A}q_{B}}\bra{q_A q_B}\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}(t)\,\Pi_{r}\,\mathcal{U}(t)\ket{q_A q_B}
is the instantaneous Rydberg state population averaged over the four input states
\ket{q_A q_B}
subject to the
unitary evolution
\mathcal{U}(t)
.

Next, using GRAPE optimization, we have identified a smoothly-varying laser pulse of finite duration
T
that, starting with the two-atom state
\ket{gg}
, prepares a maximally-entangled (Bell-like) state
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{f}}}
with error
E\simeq 2.575\,\Gamma/B
, very close to the lower bound (
8
).
The corresponding unitary transformation
\mathcal{U}(t)
is, by construction, a perfect entangler with information content
c=(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})\simeq(1.7,0.31,0.31)

23
.
It is, however, not a special perfect entangler
17
, i.e., it does not simultaneously maximally entangle the four orthogonal input product states of two atoms,
while the resulting average decay error is large,
\bar{E}=6.8\Gamma/B
, since
\bar{P}_{r}=1\,\forall\,t\in[0,T]
and
T=6.8/B
.

We were not able to determine a pulse that implements a special perfect entangling gate with an average decay error close to the fundamental bound
\bar{E}\simeq(1+\pi/2)\Gamma/B
.
Instead, for various
d\geq 2
, the optimizer identified pulses implementing quantum gates with the average errors
\bar{E}\gtrsim\pi\Gamma/B
.
This result is straightforward for a pair of two-level atoms (
d=2
) with

\ket{g}\equiv\ket{0}
and
\ket{r}\equiv\ket{1}
:
The best strategy is to simply wait for time
T=\pi/B
until the interacting state
\ket{rr}\equiv\ket{11}
accumulates a dynamical
\pi
-phase, while all the other states
\ket{gg}\equiv\ket{00},\ket{gr}\equiv\ket{01},\ket{rg}\equiv\ket{10}
remain unchanged, corresponding to the CZ gate.
Since the Rydberg state population averaged over all the inputs is
\bar{P}_{r}(t)=1\,\forall\,t
, the resulting average decay error
\bar{E}=\pi\Gamma/B
.
A similar logic applies to a pair of three-level atoms (
d=3
) with two stable ground-state sublevels representing the qubit states,
\ket{g_0}=\ket{0}
and
\ket{g_1}=\ket{1}
.
In this case, the optimal pulse sequence consists of a strong (
\Omega\gg B
) resonant
\pi
-pulse that induces the transition
\ket{1}\rightarrow\ket{r}
, followed by a waiting period of
T=\pi/B
, and concluded by a second strong
\pi
-pulse that de-excites the atoms
\ket{r}\rightarrow\ket{1}
.
We note that the previous best attempt
16
to identify laser pulses for realizing a CZ Rydberg gate and entangling two atoms led to the decay error
\bar{E}\simeq 3.4\Gamma/B
.

Hence, finding experimentally-relevant laser pulses to implement Rydberg quantum gates with minimal error remains a challenge, especially for the strong interaction (blockade) regime
B\gg\max\Omega
.

A.1
Application of the coarea formula

Here we detail the application of the co-area formula for one-dimensional integrals to derive the inequality (
5
).

Let
g(x):[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuously differentiable function (not necessarily monotone) such that
g^{\prime}(x)
is non-zero except possibly at isolated points. Also let
f(x):[a,b]\to[0,\infty)
be integrable. Then the co-area formula states that

\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,|g^{\prime}(x)|\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\sum_{x\in g^{-1}(y)}f(x)\right)dy,

(14)

where the set
g^{-1}(y)=\{x\mid g(x)=y\}
is the inverse image (preimage) of
y
. If
g(x)
is monotone, the co-area formula reduces to the usual change of variables in one-dimensional integrals.

We can now apply this formula for
P_{r}(t)=P_{r}(\psi(t))
and the min-entropy
S(t)=S(\psi(t))
as

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}P_{r}(\psi(t))\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{P_{r}(\psi(t))}{|\dot{S}(\psi(t))|}\,|\dot{S}(\psi(t))|\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\sum_{\psi(t)\in S^{-1}(s)}\frac{P_{r}(\psi(t))}{|\dot{S}(\psi(t))|}\right)ds

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{1}\min_{\psi_{s}}\frac{P_{r}(\psi_{s})}{|\dot{S}(\psi_{s})|}\,ds,

(15)

where
\psi_{s}
is any state satisfying
S(\psi_{s})=s
, and in the last line, we used the fact that the sum over the preimages
\psi(t)\in S^{-1}(s)
is bounded from below by its smallest term.

A.2
Analytic expression for
G(s)

Our goal here is to obtain an analytic expression for the bound
G(s)=\min_{\psi_{s}}\frac{P_{r}(\psi_{s})}{|\dot{S}(\psi_{s})|}
.
We first express the states
\ket{\psi_s}
in Schmidt form as
\ket{\psi_s}=\sum_{i}c_{i}\ket{u_i}\ket{v_i}
, where
c_{1}\equiv c_{\mathrm{max}}=2^{-s/2}
, and define
w_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\left(|\langle r|u_{i}\rangle|^{2}+|\langle r|v_{i}\rangle|^{2}\right)
, which satisfy
0\leq w_{i}\leq 1
and
\sum_{i}w_{i}=1
.
The entanglement rate can be bounded from above as

\displaystyle\lvert\dot{S}(\psi_{s})\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2B}{\ln 2\,c_{1}}\sum_{i>1}c_{i}\lvert\mathrm{Im}\{\bra{u_1v_1}\ket{rr}\bra{rr}\ket{u_iv_i}\}\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2B}{\ln 2\,c_{1}}\sum_{i>1}c_{i}|\bra{u_1v_1}\ket{rr}\bra{rr}\ket{u_iv_i}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2B}{\ln 2\,c_{1}}\sum_{i>1}c_{i}w_{1}w_{i}.

(16)

In the first line, we used the triangle inequality. In the second line, we applied the inequality for complex numbers
|\mathrm{Im}\,\zeta|\leq|\zeta|
, which in this case is saturated when
\arg\langle rr|u_{i}v_{i}\rangle-\arg\langle rr|u_{1}v_{1}\rangle=\pm\pi/2
. In the third line, we used the inequality
w_{i}\geq|\langle r|u_{i}\rangle\langle r|v_{i}\rangle|
, which is saturated if and only if
|\langle r|u_{i}\rangle|=|\langle r|v_{i}\rangle|
.
Moreover, the average population in the Rydberg states can be expressed as
P_{r}=2\sum_{i}c_{i}^{2}w_{i}
. Combining the two expressions, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{P_{r}}{|\dot{S}|}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\ln 2\,c_{1}}{B}\frac{\sum_{i}c_{i}^{2}w_{i}}{\sum_{i>1}c_{i}w_{1}w_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\ln 2\,c_{1}}{B}\frac{c_{1}^{2}w_{1}+\sum_{i>1}c_{i}^{2}w_{i}}{w_{1}\sum_{i>1}c_{i}w_{i}}.

(17)

To simplify the notation and the algebra, we define the variables
x=c_{1}^{2}=2^{-s}
,
y=w_{1}
, where
1/2\leq x\leq 1
since
s\in[0,1]
, and also introduce
a_{i}^{2}=c_{i}^{2}/(1-x)
and
b_{i}=w_{i}/(1-y)
, for which the normalization
\sum_{i>1}a_{i}^{2}=\sum_{i>1}b_{i}=1
holds. Hence, the expression becomes

\frac{P_{r}}{|\dot{S}|}\geq\frac{\ln 2}{B}\sqrt{\frac{x}{1-x}}\frac{xy+(1-x)(1-y)\sum_{i>1}a_{i}^{2}b_{i}}{y(1-y)\sum_{i>1}a_{i}b_{i}}.

(18)

Using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have

\left(\sum_{i>1}a_{i}b_{i}\right)^{2}\leq\sum_{i>1}a_{i}^{2}b_{i}\sum_{j>1}b_{j}=\sum_{i>1}a_{i}^{2}b_{i},

(19)

where we used the normalization of
b_{j}
.
We define
z=\sum_{i>1}a_{i}b_{i}
which satisfies
0\leq z\leq 1
due to the normalization of both
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
. Thus, the expression becomes

\frac{P_{r}}{|\dot{S}|}\geq\frac{\ln 2}{B}\sqrt{\frac{x}{1-x}}\frac{xy+(1-x)(1-y)z^{2}}{z}.

(20)

Since the variable
x=2^{-s}
is fixed, our goal is to minimize the function

f(x;\,y,z)=\frac{xy+(1-x)(1-y)z^{2}}{y(1-y)z}

(21)

with respect to
y
and
z
.
To find the critical points of this function, we solve
\partial_{y}f=\partial_{z}f=0
and obtain the following system of equations:

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\frac{z\sqrt{1-x}}{\sqrt{x}+z\sqrt{1-x}},

(22a)

\displaystyle z^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{xy}{(1-x)(1-y)}.

(22b)

Substituting one equation into the other, we find that the critical point of
f
is

z_{*}=\sqrt{\frac{x}{1-x}},\qquad y_{*}=\frac{1}{2}.

(23)

However, for
x>1/2
it follows that
z_{*}>1
which is false. This indicates that the critical point lies outside the domain of function
f
.
In other words, the minimum must occur on the boundary of the domain, at
z=1
. This corresponds to
a_{2}=b_{2}=1
and
a_{i}=b_{i}=0
for
i>2
.
Equivalently we can choose any other index
i_{*}
so that
a_{i_{*}}=b_{i_{*}}=1
and
a_{i}=b_{i}=0
for
i\neq i_{*}
. Hence,

f_{\mathrm{min}}(x)=\min_{y}f(x;\,y,1)=(\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{x-1})^{2}.

(24)

Upon substituting back
x=2^{-s}
, we obtain

\displaystyle G(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ln 2}{B}\sqrt{\frac{2^{-s}}{1-2^{-s}}}f_{\mathrm{min}}(2^{-s})

\displaystyle=\frac{\ln 2}{B}\>\frac{(1+\sqrt{2^{s}-1})^{2}}{2^{s}\sqrt{2^{s}-1}}.

(25)

This expression has also been verified numerically by minimizing
P_{r}(\psi_{s})/|\dot{S}(\psi_{s})|
over all states
\ket{\psi_s}
for which
S(\psi_{s})=s
, for different values of
s\in[0,1]
.

A.3
Gradients for GRAPE optimization

The gradients of the fidelity with respect to the control parameters, for the discretization
t_{n}=n\,\mathrm{d}t
with
\mathrm{d}t=T/N
and
n=1,\dots,N
, are given by

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F}{\delta\Omega_{n}}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{Im}\{\bra{\chi_n}\mathcal{V}_{\Omega}\ket{\psi_n}\bra{\psi_n}\ket{\chi_n}\},

(26a)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F}{\delta\Delta_{n}}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{Im}\{\bra{\chi_n}\mathcal{V}_{\Delta}\ket{\psi_n}\bra{\psi_n}\ket{\chi_n}\}.

(26b)

Here,
\mathcal{V}_{\Omega}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j}(\sigma_{rg}^{(j)}+\sigma_{gr}^{(j)})
and
\mathcal{V}_{\Delta}=-\sum_{j}\sigma_{rr}^{(j)}
, with
j=A,B
.
The states
\ket{\psi_n}=\ket{\psi(t_n)}
are obtained from the forward Schrödinger evolution
i\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\psi(t)}=\mathcal{H}(t)\ket{\psi(t)}
, with
\ket{\psi(0)}=\ket{\psi_\mathrm{in}}.

The states
\ket{\chi_n}=\ket{\chi(t_n)}
are obtained from solving
i\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\chi(t)}=\mathcal{H}(t)\ket{\chi(t)}
with the boundary condition
\displaystyle{\ket{\chi(T)}}=\ket{\psi_\mathrm{f}}.

For the gradients of the average time spent at the Rydberg states
T_{r}=\int_{0}^{T}P_{r}(t)\,dt
, we introduce auxiliary states
\ket{\xi_n}=\ket{\xi(t_n)}
satisfying the inhomogeneous Schrödinger equation
i\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\xi(t)}=\mathcal{H}(t)\ket{\xi(t)}+\Pi_{r}\ket{\psi(t)}
with
\ket{\xi(T)}=0
, which can be derived using first-order perturbation theory.
The corresponding gradients are then given by

\displaystyle\frac{\delta T_{r}}{\delta\Omega_{n}}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{Re}\{\bra{\xi_n}\mathcal{V}_{\Omega}\ket{\psi_n}\},

(27a)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta T_{r}}{\delta\Delta_{n}}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{Re}\{\bra{\xi_n}\mathcal{V}_{\Delta}\ket{\psi_n}\}.

(27b)

Appendix B
Supplemental Material

Here we revisit the derivation of the optimal scaling for the decay error when generating a unit of entanglement, as originally reported in
21
. There, it was found that
E\geq\eta_{\mathrm{min}}\Gamma/B
with
\eta_{\mathrm{min}}\simeq 2.09
. By carefully following the logic of the original derivation, we find that the corresponding scaling coefficient is actually smaller by a factor of two, i.e.,
\eta_{\mathrm{min}}\simeq 1.05
.

We consider a bipartite system with Hilbert space
H=H_{A}\otimes H_{B}
and write its Hamiltonian in the general form
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{B}+\mathbb{1}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}+\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{int}}
.
The amount of entanglement is quantified by the von Neumann entropy of either subsystem, i.e.,
S_{\mathrm{vN}}=S_{\mathrm{vN}}(\rho_{A})=S_{\mathrm{vN}}(\rho_{B})
, where
\rho_{A,B}=\mathrm{Tr}_{A,B}(\ket{\psi}\bra{\psi})
denotes the reduced density matrix of the corresponding subsystem.

We express the total wavefunction
\ket{\psi}
in its Schmidt decomposition as

\ket{\psi}=\sum^{d}_{i=1}c_{i}\ket{u_i v_i},

(28)

where
c_{i}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i}c_{i}^{2}=1
, and
\ket{u_i},\ket{v_i}
form orthonormal bases in
H_{A}
and
H_{B}
, respectively. Defining
p_{i}\equiv c_{i}^{2}
, the von Neumann entropy reads
S_{\mathrm{vN}}=-\sum_{i}p_{i}\log_{2}(p_{i})
. The rate of entanglement generation is then

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{\mathrm{vN}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i}\frac{dp_{i}}{dt}\log_{2}(p_{i})-\sum_{i}\frac{1}{\ln 2}\frac{dp_{i}}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i}\frac{dp_{i}}{dt}\log_{2}(p_{i}),

(29)

where in the second line we used that
\sum_{i}\frac{dp_{i}}{dt}=0
.

Substituting (
28
) into the Schrödinger equation
i\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\psi}=\mathcal{H}\ket{\psi}
(
\hbar=1
), we obtain

\displaystyle i\left(\sum_{i}\frac{dc_{i}}{dt}\ket{u_i v_i}+\sum_{i}c_{i}\frac{d}{dt}\ket{u_i v_i}\right)=\sum_{i}c_{i}\mathcal{H}\ket{u_i v_i}.

(30)

Multiplying from the left by
\bra{u_j v_j}
and using
\langle u_{i}|u_{j}\rangle=\langle v_{i}|v_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}
gives

\frac{dc_{j}}{dt}+\sum_{i}c_{i}\bra{u_j v_j}\frac{d}{dt}\ket{u_i v_i}=-i\sum_{i}c_{i}\bra{u_j v_j}\mathcal{H}\ket{u_i v_i},

(31)

where

\displaystyle\sum_{i}c_{i}\bra{u_j v_j}\frac{d}{dt}\ket{u_i v_i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}c_{i}\big[\bra{u_j v_j}\ket{\dot{u}_i v_i}+\bra{u_j v_j}\ket{u_i \dot{v}_i}\big]

\displaystyle=c_{j}\big[\bra{u_j}\ket{\dot{u}_j}+\bra{v_j}\ket{\dot{v}_j}\big]\in\mathrm{Im},

since
\frac{d}{dt}\langle u_{j}|u_{j}\rangle=0\iff\langle u_{j}|\dot{u}_{j}\rangle=-\langle u_{j}|\dot{u}_{j}\rangle^{*}
and similarly
\langle v_{j}|\dot{v}_{j}\rangle=-\langle v_{j}|\dot{v}_{j}\rangle^{*}
. Equating the real parts of both sides of eq. (
31
) gives

\frac{dc_{i}}{dt}=\sum_{j}c_{j}\,\mathrm{Im}\langle u_{i}v_{i}\rvert\mathcal{H}\rvert u_{j}v_{j}\rangle.

(32)

Writing
\frac{dp_{i}}{dt}=2c_{i}\frac{dc_{i}}{dt}
, the rate of change of the von Neumann entropy can then be expressed as

\displaystyle\dot{S}_{\mathrm{vN}}

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{ij}c_{i}c_{j}\log_{2}(c_{i}^{2})\>\mathrm{Im}\bra{u_iv_i}\mathcal{H}\ket{u_jv_j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}c_{i}c_{j}\log_{2}\Big(\frac{1}{c_{i}^{2}}\Big)\>\mathrm{Im}\bra{u_iv_i}\mathcal{H}\ket{u_jv_j}

\displaystyle+\sum_{ij}c_{j}c_{i}\log_{2}\Big(\frac{1}{c_{j}^{2}}\Big)\>\mathrm{Im}\bra{u_iv_i}\mathcal{H}\ket{u_jv_j}^{*},

where on the second and third lines we split the sum into two parts and interchanged indices
i\leftrightarrow j
in the last.
Using
\mathrm{Im}\bra{u_iv_i}\mathcal{H}\ket{u_jv_j}^{*}=-\mathrm{Im}\bra{u_iv_i}\mathcal{H}\ket{u_jv_j}
and the properties of the logarithm, we obtain

\dot{S}_{\mathrm{vN}}=2\sum_{ij}c_{i}c_{j}\log_{2}\left(\frac{c_{j}}{c_{i}}\right)\mathrm{Im}\bra{u_iv_i}\mathcal{H}\ket{u_j v_j}.

(33)

Clearly, local terms do not contribute to generating entanglement between the two subsystems, and we may therefore replace
\mathcal{H}
with
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{int}}
.

We now focus on the specific case of atoms interacting via
\mathcal{H}_{\text{int}}=B\ket{r r}\bra{r r}
. Taking the absolute value of Eq. (
33
) and using the triangular inequality
|\sum_{ij}\alpha_{ij}|\leq\sum_{ij}|\alpha_{ij}|\;\forall\;\alpha_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
, as well as
|\mathrm{Im}\,\zeta|\leq|\zeta|\;\forall\;\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
gives

\frac{|\dot{S}_{\mathrm{vN}}|}{B}\leq 2\sum_{ij}c_{i}c_{j}\left|\log_{2}\left(\frac{c_{j}}{c_{i}}\right)\right|\left|\langle u_{i}v_{i}\ket{rr}\right|\left|\langle rr|u_{j}v_{j}\rangle\right|.

(34)

Employing now the inequality
\alpha\beta\leq\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2})
for
(\alpha,\beta)=(\left|\langle v_{i}\ket{r}\right|,\left|\langle u_{i},\ket{r}\right|)
and defining
w_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\left(|\bra{u_i}r\rangle|^{2}+|\bra{v_i}r\rangle|^{2}\right)
, we have

\frac{|\dot{S}_{\mathrm{vN}}|}{B}\leq 2\sum_{ij}c_{i}c_{j}\left|\log_{2}\left(\frac{c_{j}}{c_{i}}\right)\right|w_{i}w_{j}.

(35)

Note here that
\sum_{i}w_{i}=1
, since
\{\ket{u_i}\},\{\ket{v_i}\}
form orthogonal bases in the corresponding Hilbert spaces.
Parametrizing the Schmidt coefficients as
(c_{i},c_{j})=\sqrt{c_{i}^{2}+c_{j}^{2}}\left(\cos\theta_{ij},\sin\theta_{ij}\right)
gives

\displaystyle\frac{|\dot{S}_{\mathrm{vN}}|}{B}\leq

\displaystyle 2\sum_{ij}w_{i}w_{j}\left(c_{i}^{2}+c_{j}^{2}\right)\cos\theta_{ij}\sin\theta_{ij}\left|\log_{2}\left(\tan\theta_{ij}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\Big[\sum_{i}c_{i}^{2}w_{i}\sum_{j}w_{j}\cos\theta_{ij}\sin\theta_{ij}\left|\log_{2}\left(\tan\theta_{ij}\right)\right|

\displaystyle+\sum_{i}c_{i}^{2}w_{i}\sum_{j}w_{j}\sin\theta_{ij}\cos\theta_{ij}\left|\log_{2}\left(\cot\theta_{ij}\right)\right|\Big],

where in the last line we exchanged indices
i\leftrightarrow j
in the second sum and used
\sin{\theta_{ij}}=\cos{\theta_{ji}}
. Using now that
\left|\log\tan\theta_{ij}\right|=\left|\log\cot\theta_{ij}\right|
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{|\dot{S}_{\mathrm{vN}}|}{B}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sum_{i}c_{i}^{2}w_{i}\sum_{j}f(\theta_{ij})w_{j},

(36)

where we defined
f(\theta)=\sin\theta\cos\theta\left|\log_{2}(\tan\theta)\right|
, which satisfies
f(\theta)\lesssim 0.478
.
We note that the factor
4
appearing in Eq. (
36
) differs from the factor
2
reported in Ref.
21
.

Replacing
f(\theta)
with its maximum value and recalling that
\sum_{j}w_{j}=1
, the entanglement rate is bounded by
|\dot{S}_{\mathrm{vN}}|/B\lesssim 1.91\sum_{i}c_{i}^{2}w_{i}
. Moreover, the average population in the Rydberg state is given by

\displaystyle P_{r}

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi}\left(\ket{r}\bra{r}\otimes\mathbb{1}+\mathbb{1}\otimes\ket{r}\bra{r}\right)\ket{\psi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}c_{i}c_{j}\left(\bra{u_i}r\rangle\bra{r}u_{j}\rangle\delta_{ij}+\delta_{ij}\bra{v_i}r\rangle\bra{r}v_{j}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}c_{i}^{2}\left(|\bra{u_i}r\rangle|^{2}+|\bra{v_i}r\rangle|^{2}\right)=2\sum_{i}c_{i}^{2}w_{i},

and therefore

|\dot{S}_{\mathrm{vN}}|\lesssim 0.956\>P_{r}B.

(38)

Integrating the entanglement rate over the interval
[0,T]
such that
S_{\mathrm{vN}}(0)=0
and
S_{\mathrm{vN}}(T)=1
, i.e., we create a unit of entanglement, and multiplying by the decay rate
\Gamma
, we obtain

E=\Gamma\int_{0}^{T}\!P_{r}dt\gtrsim 1.05\frac{\Gamma}{B}.

(39)
