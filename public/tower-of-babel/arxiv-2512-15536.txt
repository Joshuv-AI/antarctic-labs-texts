Mixing induced by Faraday surface waves
Andrés Castillo-Castellanos 1, Benoît-Joseph Gréa 2,3, Antoine Briard 2, Louis Gostiaux 4

Abstract
We investigate how surface waves enhance mixing across the interface between two miscible fluids with a small density contrast. Imposing a vertical, time-periodic acceleration, we excite Faraday waves both experimentally and numerically. In systems with a shallow density gradient, these standing waves advect the interface and can trigger secondary instabilities. When driven beyond the linear regime, large Faraday crests collapse to form cavities, injecting bubbles and lighter fluid deep into the heavier layer. Together, these mechanisms gradually homogenize the upper layer, diminish the interfacial density jump, and drive the interface downward until it decouples from surface forcing. We report a non-monotonic mixing rate—first increasing as the interfacial energy barrier lowers, then decreasing as less energy is injected into the weakened surface—revealing a balance between barrier reduction and energy input. Based on these observations, we introduce a one-dimensional model incorporating a turbulent diffusivity coefficient that depends on depth and the internal Richardson number, which captures the qualitative evolution of the system.

Mixing across fluid density interfaces plays a critical role in both natural
phenomena and industrial operations
(
Chaté et al. 1999
;
Ibrahim 2005
)
. In
nature, breaking ocean surface waves generate turbulence, promoting mixing at
and below the surface
(
Thorpe 2005
;
Mostert et al. 2022
)
. In industrial contexts,
sloshing during the storage and transport of liquefied natural gas (LNG) is
known to increase the evaporation rates by disrupting the stratification of
fluid layers
(
Behruzi et al. 2017
)
. This uncontrolled spontaneous mixing can lead
to a sudden rise in pressure
(
Scurlock 2016
)
. While such mixing is
typically attributed to convective instabilities and double diffusive
convection, this study investigates an alternative mechanism where interfacial
waves, upon interacting with a free surface, break and promote turbulent mixing.

The interaction between a miscible and an immiscible fluid layer offers an
interesting setting, where the properties of the internal layer evolve due to
molecular diffusion and turbulent entrainment.
Linden 1973
argued that
discrete turbulent motions may transfer their kinetic energy into potential
energy by deforming the interface and promoting mixing between the two layers.
For two-layer fluids, the interface acts as a barrier for the turbulence,
transferring energy from the vertical to the horizontal scales (see, for
instance,
Jacobs & Dalziel 2005
;
Poulain-Zarcos et al. 2022
). In this sense, the entrainment
process can be described using parameters local to the interface,
recontextualizing previous experiments by
Turner 1965
.

Over time, the mixing layer becomes both deeper and thicker, driven by two
competing mechanisms. First, the density contrast across the interface decreases
as the near-surface region becomes increasingly homogeneous, reducing the energy
required for irreversible mixing, a process reminiscent of penetrative
convection (see, for instance,
Dorel et al. 2023
). Second, the energy injected
into the interface diminishes with distance from the free surface, analogous to
the behaviour of impinging jets, rising plumes, or oscillating grid turbulence
in stratified fluids
(
Rouse & Dodu 1955
;
Morton et al. 1956
;
Linden 1975
;
Hopfinger & Toly 1976
;
Cotel & Breidenthal 1996
;
Herault et al. 2018
;
Vaux et al. 2021
)
. Eventually, as the available energy
decreases faster than the energy needed for mixing, the interface is bound to
decouple from the surface dynamics and stall. On the other hand, the thickening
of the interface is predominantly caused by the transition to turbulence, with
molecular diffusion contributing to a lesser extent
(
Zoueshtiagh et al. 2009
;
Benielli & Sommeria 1998
)
. Modal competition may lead to chaotic behaviour and generate
mixing at small scales
(
Ciliberto & Gollub 1985
;
Briard et al. 2019
;
Briard et al. 2020
)
, while
secondary instabilities often trigger interfacial wave breaking, enhancing
turbulent mixing
(
Thorpe 1968
;
Cavelier et al. 2022
)
.

In this study, we investigate the interaction between a free surface and a
miscible interface with a small density contrast through laboratory experiments
and direct numerical simulations (DNS) of a simplified configuration shown in
Figure
1
. For this configuration, surface waves are generated
by applying a periodic vertical oscillation—of amplitude
a
and frequency

\omega
—to the container as a result of the Faraday
instability
(
Faraday 1831
)
. In the classical Faraday experiment,
free-surface motion is controlled by the ratio between the forcing frequency

\omega
and the dispersion relation of the interface
\omega_{0}
, and by the
external forcing term
a\omega^{2}/g
(where
g
is the gravity). For this
system, linear stability analysis reveals unstable regions where the response
frequency is either half-frequency, which is the most amplified, or isochronous
with respect to the forcing frequency
(
Benjamin & Ursell 1954
;
Kumar & Tuckerman 1994
;
Ward et al. 2019
)
. Depending on the external forcing term
a\omega^{2}/g
, we may
observe a wavy, unbroken, free surface or a disconnected surface as in a
breaking wave. Wave breaking can also lead to splashing droplets and jets from
the collapse of large cavities, which inject fresh fluid into the stratified
layer (see, for instance, Figure
2
and previous work
presented at the APS/DFD Gallery of Fluid Motion

(
Castillo-Castellanos et al. 2023
)
).

To investigate the effects of these two regimes on the long-term irreversible
mixing, we vary the forcing amplitude and the initial depth of the
stratification, and follow the system for several hundred forcing periods. These
findings motivate the use of numerical simulations to complement the experiments
and provide access to quantities that are difficult to measure otherwise. Based
on these observations we propose a model to characterize the asymptotic regime.
This article is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we
describe the configuration under study and detail the experimental setup. In
Section
3
, we introduce the governing equations and
describe the numerical approach used in the DNS. Section
4

is focused on the dynamics of the free-surface motion and how it drives the
motion of the miscible interface. Section
5
addresses
the long-term evolution of the stratification, where two distinct mixing regimes
are identified. Finally, in Section
6
, we present a diffusion
model based on these observations, which contains the minimum ingredients
required to reproduce the observed behaviour.

2
Experimental Setup

We use a cuboidal tank with internal dimensions of
94.6$\mathrm{cm}$
width,
11$\mathrm{cm}$
depth, and
67$\mathrm{cm}$
height, designed for free surface flow studies at
the Gaztransport and Technigaz® (GTT) Motion Analysis and Testing
Laboratory. The tank is filled up to a height
H
with a mixture of
salt water and fresh water forming a two-layer system: a sharp miscible
interface separating fresh water (mixed with blue dye) and salt water, and a
free surface separating air and water (see, Figure
1
). Salt
diffuses in water with molecular diffusivity
D_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}}
, while the
free surface is characterized by a (constant) surface tension coefficient

\sigma_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{surf}}
. We denote the properties of the fluid below (respectively
above) the diffuse interface by the subscript 1 (respectively 2), while the
properties of the gas phase above the free surface are denoted by the subscript
3.

It is convenient to distinguish between the ‘mixed layer’ — the nearly
homogeneous uppermost region formed by the history of mixing — and the ‘mixing
layer’ — the zone in which mixing is currently active
(
Brainerd & Gregg 1995
)
.
The vertical positions of the interfaces separating fluids 1 and 2 (respectively
2 and 3) are denoted by
\zeta_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}}(x,y,t)
(respectively

\zeta_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{surf}}(x,y,t)
). For convenience, we also define the initial depth and
density of the mixed layer as
h_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{init}}
and
\rho_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{init}}
, with their
time-dependent counterparts given by
h_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mixed}}(t)
and
\rho_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mixed}}(t)
.

For the upper interface, the image intensity was transformed into a
transversally averaged volume fraction field
f(x,y,t)
, which was set to 0
(gas phase) and 1 (liquid phase) using a binary threshold. As mixing progresses,
the intensity in the upper layer decreases over time. To accommodate this, the
threshold values were determined for each frame using the triangle method. The
interface separating the two phases was identified with a contour-tracking
algorithm, representing the interface as a one-dimensional curve embedded in
two-dimensional space. Alternatively, the free surface position

\zeta_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{surf}}(x,t)

column of
f(x,y,t)
, yielding a monotonic function of
x

produce equivalent results for non-breaking waves but may diverge in the
presence of breaking waves (see, for instance, the blue line in Figure

3
b). The lower interface position,
\zeta_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}}(x,t)
,
was estimated using a similar approach. However, the binary threshold was based
on the mean pixel value
(
Glasbey 1993
)
within the liquid phase, while the
gas phase was ignored. Finally, a mean separation distance is estimated from

\displaystyle h_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mixed}}(t)=\langle\zeta_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{surf}}(x,t)-\zeta_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}}(x,t)\rangle

(1)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes the horizontal average.

2.2
External Forcing

The tank is mounted on the platform of a hexapod capable of movement along six
axes (translation and rotation); however, in this study, it is used exclusively
to generate vertical sinusoidal oscillations. The experimental protocol follows
that of
Briard et al. 2020
and
Cavelier et al. 2022
. The platform is initially
positioned at
Z=a
— where
Z
denotes the vertical position of the
hexapod in the laboratory frame. After a 20-second rest period, we impose a
periodic vertical motion
Z=a\cos(\omega t)
. Each experiment typically runs
for
400
to
800
periods to reach the asymptotic regime. Finally, the
platform returns to
Z=a
and rests for 60 seconds. The forcing frequency

\omega=20$\mathrm{rad}\text{\,}{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}$
generates ‘two-dimensional’ waves on the free surface with a
longitudinal wavenumber
k=3\pi/W
and frequency
\omega/2
. This choice
of parameters gives the largest possible sub-harmonic waves, while limiting
contact with the container lid. This choice of parameters also ensures a good
separation from the nearest unstable mode, even for intense forcing.

2.4
Parameters and Dimensionless Numbers

The system dynamics is fully determined by the tank geometry
(W,H,D)
,
the physical properties of the fluids
(\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho_{3},\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3},\sigma_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{surf}},D_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}})
, the filling process
(h_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{init}},L_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{init}})
, the forcing terms
(a,\omega)
, and the gravitational acceleration
(g)
. According to the Buckingham
\Pi
theorem, the system depends on
thirteen dimensionless groups, which include:

— a vibrational Reynolds number,

\mathrm{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Re}

\equiv

{\rho_{1}a^{2}\omega}/{\mu_{1}}\approx 190-3400

— a vibrational Weber number,

\mathrm{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}We}

\equiv

{(\rho_{2}-\rho_{3})a^{3}\omega^{2}}/{\sigma_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{surf}}}\approx 0.16-9.5

— a Schmidt number,

\mathrm{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Sc}

\equiv

\mu_{1}/(\rho_{1}D_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}})\approx 700

— a forcing parameter,

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}F}

\equiv

{a\omega^{2}}/{g}\approx 0.12-0.50

— a density ratio for each interface,

\rho^{*}_{1}

\equiv

{\rho_{2}}/{\rho_{1}}\approx 0.90

\rho^{*}_{2}

\equiv

{\rho_{3}}/{\rho_{2}}\ll 1

— a viscosity ratio for each interface,

\mu^{*}_{1}

\equiv

{\mu_{2}}/{\mu_{1}}\approx 0.91

\mu^{*}_{2}

\equiv

{\mu_{3}}/{\mu_{2}}\ll 1

— a relative width,

\mathcal{W}

\equiv

{W}/{H}\approx 2.82

— a relative depth,

\mathcal{D}

\equiv

{D}/{H}\approx 0.32

— a relative amplitude,

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}a^{*}}

\equiv

{a}/{H}\approx 0.009-0.036

— a relative initial height,

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h^{*}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{init}}}

\equiv

{h_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{init}}}/{H}\approx 0.07-0.67

— a relative initial width,

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L^{*}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{init}}}

\equiv

{L_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{init}}}/{H}\approx 0.02-0.11

Any other possible dimensionless groups can be expressed in terms of these
parameters. We consider this choice to be convenient in the context of
parametric instabilities. For clarity, the density ratio
\rho^{*}_{1}
is
customarily replaced by the nominal Atwood number:

\displaystyle\mathrm{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}At}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{init}}=\frac{\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}}{\rho_{1}+\rho_{2}}\approx 0.047.

(3)

The value of
\omega
is selected to induce a sub-harmonic resonance of the
free surface, with the excited wavenumber
k=3\pi/W=9.96

{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}

being the one closest to the resonance conditions, as quantified by the detuning
parameter
\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Delta}\equiv{gk}/{\omega^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\approx-0.006
.
In this work, we focus on the interaction between the external forcing and the
initial stratification by varying the control parameters
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}F}
and

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h^{*}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{init}}}
. We consider values of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}F}
up to 0.5, while
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}h^{*}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{init}}}
ranges
from 6% (shallow stratification) to 70% (deep stratification). The remaining
dimensionless groups are either held constant or determined by other control
parameters.

As illustrated in Figures
2
and
3
,
the external forcing induces large amplitude surface waves, which are expected
to modify the internal stratification. However, characterizing this process
will require measuring quantities that are challenging to perform in our current
experimental setup. To address this, we have opted to use numerical simulations
to supplement our experimental observations. For clarity, we chose to present
the numerical framework before discussing the experimental and numerical
observations.

3
Governing Equations and Numerical Approach

3.1
Governing Equations

The equations of motion are based on the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations
for two-phase fluids, supplemented by a transport equation for the binary
mixture. In this approach, the governing equations are formulated in terms of
a volume fraction of liquid
f(\boldsymbol{x},t)\in[0,1]
:

\displaystyle{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=0

(4a)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot({\nabla}\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\rho}{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}+\boldsymbol{g}(t)

(4b)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot({\nabla}f)

\displaystyle=0

(4c)

where
\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=(u,v,w)
is the velocity field,
\rho(\boldsymbol{x},t)
is
the density field,
\boldsymbol{g}(t)=-(g+a\omega^{2}\cos(\omega t))\boldsymbol{e_{z}}
is
the modulated gravity, and
\boldsymbol{\tau}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
is the stress tensor:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}=-p\boldsymbol{I}+\mu({\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}+{\nabla}\boldsymbol{u}^{T})+\sigma_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{surf}}\kappa\delta_{s}(\boldsymbol{n}\otimes\boldsymbol{n})

(5)

where
p(\boldsymbol{x},t)
,
\mu(\boldsymbol{x},t)
,
\kappa
,
\delta_{s}
, and

\boldsymbol{n}
represent the pressure field, the dynamic viscosity, the local
curvature, a surface Dirac delta function, and the unit normal vector of the
free surface, respectively
(
Tryggvason 2011
)
. Stratification results from
differences in density due to salt concentration in water. This is included by
means of a (volumetric) concentration field
c(\boldsymbol{x},t)\in[0,1]
, which is
governed by the transport equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial c}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot({\nabla}c)

\displaystyle={\nabla}\cdot\left(D_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}}{\nabla}c-\beta_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}}\delta_{s}c\right)

(6)

where
D_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}}
is the molecular diffusion coefficient of
c
and

\beta_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}}
is a parameter that controls the diffusion across the free surface
(see, for instance,
Haroun et al. 2010
;
Marschall et al. 2012
). In practice, reflecting
the low volatility of salt water in air, we assume
c=0
in the gas phase.
Accordingly,
\beta_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}}
is set to restrict molecular diffusion to the
liquid phase.

The equations above are coupled through the fluid properties:

\displaystyle\mu(\boldsymbol{x},t)

\displaystyle=f_{1}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\mu_{1}+f_{2}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\mu_{2}+f_{3}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\mu_{3}

(7a)

\displaystyle\rho(\boldsymbol{x},t)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{f_{1}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\rho_{1}}_{\mathrm{salt-water}}+\underbrace{f_{2}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\rho_{2}}_{\mathrm{fresh-water}}+\underbrace{f_{3}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\rho_{3}}_{\mathrm{air}}

(7b)

where

is the volume fraction of the component
i
and the liquid phase
is treated as a binary mixture, where fluid properties have a linear dependence
on

. In practice, we take

,
f_{2}=f(1-c)
, and

, which satisfies (

)-(

solute is not allowed into the gas phase. For the simulations, the viscosity of
the liquid phase is simplified by assuming constant, equal pure-component
dynamic viscosities,
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
.

3.2

In the simulations, we may use the method proposed by
Tseng & Ferziger 2001
to
estimate the instantaneous density profiles,
\bar{\rho}(z,t)
, from the
cumulative distribution function (CDF) of
\rho(\boldsymbol{x},t)
in space. This is
the same density profile as the reference state of minimum potential energy
obtained by allowing the fluid to stratify adiabatically
(
Winters et al. 1993
)
.
From these profiles, we take the position of the mixed layer from the peak in

(\bar{\rho}-\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{min}})(\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{max}}-\bar{\rho})
,
while the interface width

is calculated using the classical expression
for a mixing layer width
(

)
:

\displaystyle L(t)=6\int_{z_{\min}}^{z_{\max}}\frac{(\bar{\rho}-\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{min}})(\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{max}}-\bar{\rho})}{(\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{max}}-\bar{\rho}_{\mathrm{min}})^{2}}~\mathrm{d}z.

(8)

This definition of the mixing width

is convenient, in the sense that

We performed numerical simulations using a finite-volume solver implemented in
the open-source code Basilisk
(
Popinet 2009
;
Popinet 2015
)
.
Boundary conditions include a static contact angle of 90°, a no-slip condition,
and impermeability imposed on all boundaries. By using a face-centered velocity
for volume fraction advection — which is not strictly zero even when a zero
velocity is imposed at the solid boundary — the contact line can move due to
what is known as "numerical slip". This numerical slip is linked to the grid
size and tends toward the no-slip limit as the size of the mesh is
decreased
(
Tavares et al. 2024
)
.

The solver has been validated for a variety of complex interfacial
flows
(
van Hooft et al. 2018
;
Berny et al. 2020
;
Rivière et al. 2021
;
Mostert et al. 2022
)
. In this work, the
governing equations are discretized on a regular Cartesian grid, where

\Delta_{x}=\Delta_{y}=\Delta_{z}
, using a second-order scheme in time and space.
Viscous terms are treated implicitly, while a projection method is used to deal
with the velocity-pressure coupling. A compromise in spatial resolution is
required to perform simulations for several hundred periods at a reasonable
computational cost. Here, we use 1024
\times
768
\times
128 grid points
such that
\Delta_{x}
is roughly one third of the size of the capillary
length.

The initial conditions assume the fluid is at rest, and the initial condition
follows (
2
), with thickness comparable to the experiments. The
resulting system is solved using an iterative multigrid cycle with a small
relative tolerance to ensure that
c(\boldsymbol{x},t)
does not diffuse into the gas
phase. Additional details can be found in
Farsoiya et al. 2021
. The source code
developed in this work, as well as the simulation setups are available on the
Basilisk website
(
Castillo-Castellanos 2025
)
.

The numerical investigation comprises three series of 3D simulations:

—

Series A: varying the stratification depth.

—

Series B: varying the forcing term.

—

Series C: ran for several hundred periods to comment on the
asymptotic regime.

These simulations aim at closely reproducing the phenomenology observed in
experiments (see Table
4
for simulation parameters).
However, spatial resolution constraints require setting the Schmidt number to
1—instead of 700, as for salt water. Despite this limitation, we shall see
that the simulations successfully capture the fundamental aspects of the
evolution of the stratification and the development of secondary instabilities
on the interfacial waves.

Low-dimensional description of the interface motion

4.2.1
Formulation of the POD modes

(a)

(b) (c)

Figure 5:

Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD) for the case previously shown in

Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD) is used to decompose the interfaces into a
set of orthogonal functions. POD is optimal in the sense that it captures the
maximum variance of the data with the fewest modes
(
Holmes et al. 2012
)
. This
allows us to remove discontinuities from the reconstructed interfaces and filter
out random noise, by focusing on the most salient and recurrent features. Here,
we apply the POD separately to the free surface
\zeta_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{surf}}
and the miscible
interface
\zeta_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}}
, representing their positions in terms of two distinct
sets of orthogonal modes and their corresponding modal coefficients

\displaystyle\zeta_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{surf}}(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha^{(n)}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{surf}}}(t)~\phi^{(n)}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{surf}}}(x),

\displaystyle\zeta_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}}(x,t)=h_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mixed}}(t)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\alpha^{(n)}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}}}(t)~\phi^{(n)}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}}}(x),

(9)

POD modes constitute a hierarchy of patterns most likely to be observed, and
correspond to solutions of the eigenvalue problem:

\displaystyle\int\mathsfbi{R}_{j}(x,x^{\prime})\phi_{j}^{(n)}(x^{\prime})~dx^{\prime}=\lambda_{j}^{(n)}\phi_{j}^{(n)}(x)

(10)

where

\displaystyle\mathsfbi{R}_{j}(x,x^{\prime})\equiv(1/M)\sum_{m=1}^{M}\zeta_{j}(x,t_{m})\zeta_{j}(x^{\prime},t_{m})

(11)

is the spatial autocorrelation tensor computed from
M
snapshots at times

t_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,M
. By construction, the modal amplitudes have zero mean,
are uncorrelated, and their variance is equal to
\lambda_{j}^{(n)}
.

In practice, the modes are obtained by solving an eigenvalue problem using the
‘scikit-learn’ and ‘LAPACK’ libraries. Additionally, we use only snapshots in
the stationary regime to extract the modes, and we expand each dataset by
applying the reflection symmetry
\eg\zeta_{j}(x,t)\to\zeta_{j}(-x,t)
to
enforce the natural symmetry of the system on the POD modes.

In general, both interfaces are well-suited for dimensionality reduction, with
the first 10 modes containing over 95% of the total variance
(Figure
5
a). The first few modes usually suffice to describe the
free surface qualitatively, although higher-order modes are required to capture
sharp-crested or splashing waves (Figures
5
b-c).

4.3.4
Comparison with weakly nonlinear predictions

Unlike linear theory, where amplitudes increase indefinitely, nonlinear effects
lead to saturation
(
Ockendon & Ockendon 1973
;
Miles 1984
;
Nagata 1989
;
Tsai et al. 1990
;
Douady 1990
;
Nesterov 1995
)
. Consider a nonlinear dispersion relation of the
form
\omega^{\prime}_{0}=\omega_{0}(1+\frac{1}{2}K(k\alpha)^{2})
into the
damped Mathieu equation

\displaystyle\ddot{\alpha}+\gamma\dot{\alpha}+\omega_{0}^{2}(1+\tfrac{1}{2}K(k\alpha)^{2})^{2}(1+F\cos(\omega t))\alpha=0

(15)

where
\alpha
is the wave amplitude,
\dot{\alpha}
and
\ddot{\alpha}

indicate the first and second time derivatives,
\gamma
is the linear damping
coefficient, and
K
a nonlinear detuning parameter, which depends on the
fluid depth. We may use the expression from
Tadjbakhsh & Keller 1960
:

\displaystyle K=\frac{9s^{-4}-12s^{-2}-3-2s}{64},\quad s\equiv\tanh(kH).

(16)

For
kH\ll 1
, this value tends to
K={9}/{64(kH)^{4}}
, while

K=-1/8
if
kH\gg 1
. For the experiments, this value
is close to the deep water limit with
K=-0.124
.

If the damping, forcing, and nonlinear coefficients are small, the free linear
oscillations that are the solutions at leading order have the possibility of
being slowly modulated
(
Rajchenbach & Clamond 2015
)
. At the leading order, solutions
may be written in terms of
\alpha=A(t)\exp{(i\omega t/2)}+\text{c.c.}
,
where

and c.c. indicates the complex
conjugate terms. Furthermore, equation (
15
) is expected to reach a
stationary regime with amplitude

\displaystyle 3Kk^{2}|A_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{sat}}|^{2}=\left(\frac{1}{4}\frac{\omega^{2}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}-1\right)\mp\frac{1}{2}\left[F^{2}-\left(\frac{\omega\gamma}{\omega_{0}^{2}}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2},\quad\sin(2\theta)=\frac{1}{F}\frac{\omega\gamma}{\omega_{0}^{2}}

(17)

Details on the dynamical equation for
A(t)
can be found in

Potential and kinetic energies

A more quantitative comparison between the experiments and simulations can be
made using global quantities, such as the total potential and kinetic energies:

\displaystyle\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}PE}\equiv\iiint\rho(\boldsymbol{x},t)gz~dx~dy~dz,\quad\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}KE}\equiv\iiint\frac{1}{2}\rho(\boldsymbol{x},t)\boldsymbol{u}^{2}(\boldsymbol{x},t)~dx~dy~dz.

(20)

As mentioned in Appendix
C
, by assuming a
piecewise density profile, the potential energy may be approximated as

\displaystyle\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}PE}=\frac{g(\rho_{1}-\rho_{3})}{2}\iint\zeta^{2}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{surf}}\,dx\,dy-\frac{g(\rho_{1}-\rho_{2})}{2}\frac{h_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{init}}}{h_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mixed}}}\iint\left(\zeta^{2}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{surf}}-\zeta^{2}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}}+h_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{init}}h_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}}\right)dx\,dy,

which can be evaluated from the recorded images. Equation (

)
essentially shows the potential energy can be approximated as the potential
energy of an equivalent homogeneous system, in addition to a small correction
due to the stratification. Additionally, since kinetic energy is actively being
transformed into potential energy and vice versa, both quantities are expected
to be comparable and out-of-phase, while the external forcing is well correlated
with the potential energy.

While recovering the global energy is important, our primary interest lies in
the spatial distribution of contributions to the kinetic energy. This
distribution reveals the energy within flow structures reaching the interface.
We analyse this in two ways: by following the time evolution of vertical kinetic
energy profiles, and by separating contributions from large-scale and
small-scale flow features described in the
Appendix
D
. This separation of scales is
performed in an ad hoc manner based on the POD of the velocity field

\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
, where the modes are weighted by the density
\rho
to
ensure the resulting base optimally represents the kinetic energy. At each time

t
, we reconstruct a large-scale velocity field

\boldsymbol{\tilde{u}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
using the first 10 POD modes, which in this
context represent oscillating motion. The small-scale features, associated with
turbulence, are then captured by the remainder,
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
(see
Figures
15
a-b). These small-scale structures tend to be more
energetic just below the wave anti-nodes, where energy injection is most
intense, and may occasionally penetrate the stratification (see Supplementary
Material).

We also investigate the spatial distribution of viscous dissipation,

\epsilon(\boldsymbol{x},t)=2\nu\mathsfbi{S}:\mathsfbi{S}
(where

\mathsfbi{S}=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{T})
is the strain-rate
tensor). In practice, we approximate dissipation within the bulk from a slice in
the transversal mid-plane by assuming the horizontal derivatives are comparable
(
\eg
\partial v/\partial y\approx\partial v/\partial x
and so on). As
expected, contributions are concentrated in the small-scale features and remain
confined to the mixed layer (see Figure
15
c). Here,
large-scale contributions are negligible, and the total dissipation is
equivalent to the dissipation at small scales.

Applying a similar decomposition to the concentration field is more complex due
to the progression of stratification. To address this, we subtract the
short-time averaged concentration field before applying POD, and then re-add it
during the reconstruction of the large-scale concentration field

\tilde{c}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
using the first 10 POD modes (see details in the
Appendix
D
). Again, we consider the
small-scale features are captured by the remainder
c^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
. This
decomposition allows us to compute other quantities, such as the vertical
concentration flux
u_{z}^{\prime}c^{\prime}
(see Figures
15
d).

Three key observations can be made here.
First, regarding the spatial distribution of energy: large-scale flow features
contain most of the kinetic energy. This energy typically peaks at the surface
level and decreases with
\exp{(-2kz)}
, consistent with the characteristics of
the potential solution (Figure
16
a-b). Notably, these large
scales showed little change with the initial stratification; most differences in
energy distribution are concentrated in the small scales.
In contrast, contributions from the small scales also decrease with

\exp{(-2kz)}
but are primarily confined to the mixed layer, as stratification
acts as a barrier (Figure
16
c).
A similar spatial distribution, including confinement to the mixed layer, is
observed in the square of the vertical shear,
S^{2}=(\partial u/\partial z)^{2}+(\partial v/\partial z)^{2}
.

Contributions to viscous dissipation are often larger in the gas phase than in
the liquid phase. Within the mixed layer, these contributions decrease with
something between
\exp{(-2kz)}
and
\exp{(-3kz)}
and quickly vanish inside
the mixing layer (see Figure
16
d).
On the one hand, a decrease with
\exp{(-2kz)}
is consistent with potential
theory, in which viscous dissipation is the dominant term in the dynamic
equation of the turbulent kinetic energy. On the other hand, a decrease with

\exp{(-3kz)}
might suggest the transport of turbulent kinetic energy by the
mean field, which itself decreases with
\exp{(-kz)}
. Additionally, the
vertical concentration flux due to the fluctuating fields,
u^{\prime}_{z}c^{\prime}
, is shown
to decrease at a slower rate inside the mixed and mixing layers and vanish in
the bottom layer (see Figure
16
e).

Second, we observed notable differences between the transient and stationary
regimes: the transient regime is characterized by intense bursts of energy,
whereas the stationary regime maintains a more constant energy level, with
differences up to a factor of two (Figure
14
). Two important
aspects emerge from the above: (i) More energy is available for mixing during
the transient phase than during the stationary regime due to the amplitude
modulation caused by strong non-linearities. (ii) The amount of energy seen by
the interface decreases exponentially as the interface moves downward.
This suggests that mixing is more intense during the transient phase and
decreases over time.

Third, we may use the vertical profiles of the turbulent kinetic energy and
viscous dissipation rate to estimate the typical turbulent length scale from
the Kolmogorov and Prandtl relations:

\displaystyle l_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{turb}}\sim\frac{{K^{\prime}}^{3/2}}{\epsilon}

(22)

If we consider that within the mixed layer both quantities decrease
exponentially with depth, we may write

\displaystyle K^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x},t)=C_{E}(x,y,t)e^{-mkz},~~\epsilon(\boldsymbol{x},t)=C_{\epsilon}(x,y,t)e^{-nkz}

(23)

and obtain the following expression for the turbulent length scale

\displaystyle l_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{turb}}\sim\frac{{K^{\prime}}^{3/2}}{\epsilon}=\frac{C_{E}^{3/2}}{C_{\epsilon}}e^{-(\frac{3}{2}m-n)kz}

(24)

Another way to estimate the turbulent length scale is through the square of the
vertical shear rate
S^{2}
. If we approximate the vertical shear rate as

\displaystyle S^{2}(\boldsymbol{x},t)\approx(u_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{turb}}/l_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{turb}})^{2}=C_{S}(x,y,t)e^{-pkz},

(25)

then, the relation
p=n-\frac{1}{2}m
between the exponents is expected to hold.
Based on data from Figure
16
within the mixed layer, we
estimate the exponents as
m=2
,
n=3
, and
p=2
. These values are consistent
with the derived expression for
p
, and suggest that within the mixing layer

l_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{turb}}
is independent of
z
(see Figure
16
f). We will
revisit these quantities in §
6
in the context of a turbulent
diffusion model.

5
Evolution of the stratification

For small Atwood numbers and equal forcing, the surface dynamics are largely
similar. However, as shown in Figure
17
, the evolution
of the mixed layer and of the mixing layer display a non-trivial dependence on
the stratification depth. We shall see that this dependence arises from the
lowering of the energetic barrier to mixing imposed by the stratification. In
this section, we first describe the short-term evolution of the stratification,
focusing on the mechanisms that destabilize the miscible interface. We then
comment on the long-term evolution of the stratification and the asymptotic
regime.

6
One-dimensional model for the stratification

As shown in both experiments and simulations, the mixing layer displays two
distinct yet connected behaviours. When mixing occurs equally on both sides of
the interface (
double-sided stirring
), the mixing layer thickens.
Conversely, if mixing is predominant on one side (
single-side stirring
),
it causes the interface to shift in that direction
(
Fernando 1991
)
.

In the following, we focus on the long-term evolution of a case with

single-side stirring
, where turbulence generated by the surface dynamics
is confined to the mixed layer, whereas the bottom region is essentially
irrotational. We assume energy is injected at the surface, proportional to wave
amplitude, and decays with depth. This leads to an upper layer that gradually
becomes a homogeneous mixture, while the lower layer remains unmixed, reducing
the concentration gradient and pushing the interface downwards. While secondary
destabilization, in principle, acts equally on both sides of the interface
(reducing density contrast and potentially enhancing entrainment) we expect this
effect to fade over time. Therefore, we may initially neglect this effect.

6.1
One-dimensional model with turbulent diffusivity

One way to illustrate the observed behaviour is to consider a one-dimensional
model that relies on the concept of turbulent
diffusivity
(
Fernando & Hunt 1997
;
Canuto et al. 2001
)
. This approach is inspired by models
used in studies of upper ocean mixing driven by breaking and non-breaking waves,
such as those by
Qiao et al. 2004
and
Ghantous & Babanin 2014
. These models typically
yield a diffusion equation in the vertical direction

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\overline{c}}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(D_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}}\frac{\partial\overline{c}}{\partial z}\right)-\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\overline{u^{\prime}_{z}c^{\prime}}

(31)

where
\overline{c}(z,t)
represents the instantaneous density profile as
defined in §
3.2
, while the right-hand side
terms account for molecular diffusion, which is usually small, and the mass flux
due to (turbulent and oscillating) fluctuations, respectively.

To close this system, a common approach is to approximate these fluxes by
introducing a turbulent diffusivity, such that:

\displaystyle\overline{u^{\prime}_{z}c^{\prime}}=-D_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{turb}}\frac{\partial\overline{c}}{\partial z}

(32)

For the present discussion, we will consider only the most general features of

D_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{turb}}
based on dimensional arguments. In its most general form, the
turbulent diffusivity is proportional to the product of characteristic length
and velocity scales of the turbulent flow within the mixed layer

\displaystyle D_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{turb}}=l_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{turb}}\cdot u_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{turb}}\cdot C_{D}

(33)

where
C_{D}
is a function (to be determined) of four dimensionless groups:

a\omega^{2}/g
,
gk/\omega^{2}
,
kz
, and
\omega^{2}/N^{2}

(
N^{2}=-(g/\rho)(\partial\rho/\partial z)
being the square of the Brunt-Väisälä
frequency), of which only the last one depends on the stratification.

Our observations from §
4.3.5
indicate that
the characteristic velocity scale
u_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{turb}}
is proportional to the surface wave
amplitude and decays exponentially with depth. Additionally, the characteristic
length scale
l_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{turb}}
is assumed to be constant within the mixed layer,
reflecting the size of the largest turbulent eddies generated by the breaking
waves. However, the choice of
C_{D}
is not as straightforward. To gain insight
into its form, we may consider the mixing of a passive tracer in a homogeneous
fluid.

6.1.2
Dynamic evolution of the energetic barrier

The energetic barrier can be understood by examining the Richardson number

\displaystyle Ri\equiv\frac{N^{2}}{(u_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{turb}}/l_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{turb}})^{2}}

(34)

representing the balance between buoyancy and flow shear at the interface. When

Ri
exceeds a critical value
Ri_{\text{crit}}
, it indicates that the flow is
sufficiently stable that turbulence cannot penetrate further. In our scenario,
we observe two competing effects which modify the Richardson number. First,
while the total energy injected at the surface remains constant, the energy
reaching the interface decreases exponentially as the mixed layer progresses.
Second, as the mixed layer progresses, it also weakens the interfacial buoyancy
gradient —
N^{2}
decreases, thereby allowing eddies to engulf fluid more
efficiently. Overall, since the first effect is dominant, the Richardson number
is expected to increase over time.

The issue with using the above definition of the Richardson number with equation
(
31
) is that, since we are prescribing the turbulent velocity

u_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{turb}}
, there is no feedback between the mixing occurring at the interface
and the turbulent diffusivity. Because of this, the model cannot see the
difference between a thick or a thin interface, as both may have the same
N^{2}

but different behaviour. To address this, we propose to use a modified
Richardson number that considers the gradient thickness of the interface, while
still relying on the prescribed turbulent velocity. This modified Richardson
number is defined as

\displaystyle Ri_{\text{inside}}\equiv\frac{N^{2}}{(u_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{turb}}/L^{\prime})^{2}},\quad\text{where}\quad L^{\prime}\equiv\frac{\overline{c}_{\text{max}}-\overline{c}_{\text{min}}}{\max\frac{\partial\overline{c}}{\partial z}}

(35)

where
L^{\prime}
is the local gradient thickness of the interface, estimated from the
horizontally-averaged density profile.

In order to be consistent with the observations from the DNS,
C_{D}
must verify
the following conditions. In the mixed layer,
C_{D}
is expected to remain a
non-zero constant, similar to the behaviour of a passive tracer. Within the
mixing layer,
C_{D}
is a function of the Richardson number. To capture the
barrier effect, we consider that energy is injected from the top to the bottom.
As the turbulent structures move downwards, they will lose energy. The thicker
the interface, the more energy they will lose before reaching the interface.
Based on this reasoning, if at some depth
z_{\text{crit}}
, the critical
Richardson number
Ri_{\text{crit}}
is exceeded, then
C_{D}
is expected to
decrease below
z_{\text{crit}}
. A suitable formulation could be:

\displaystyle C_{D}=\begin{cases}C_{\text{tracer}}&\text{ if }z<z_{\text{crit}}\\
C_{\text{tracer}}\cdot f(Ri_{\text{inside}})&\text{ if }z_{\text{crit}}<z<z_{\text{max}}\\
C_{D}(z_{\text{max}})&\text{ if }z\geq z_{\text{max}}\end{cases}

(36)

Here,
z_{\text{max}}
indicates the depth where the barrier effect is most
pronounced and the function
f(Ri_{\text{inside}})
quantifies the attenuation
attributed to stratification,

\displaystyle f(Ri_{\text{inside}})=\frac{1}{1+C_{\text{barrier}}\left[\frac{Ri_{\text{inside}}}{Ri_{\text{crit}}}\right]^{n}}

(37)

where
C_{\text{tracer}}
is the proportionality term for the advection of a
passive scalar, while the parameters
C_{\text{barrier}}=0.1
and
n=4
, which
control the attenuation intensity where chosen to mimic the trends observed in
the experiments.

This approach recovers the main features of the entrainment process observed in
both experiments and simulations. The model captures the non-monotonic behaviour
discussed earlier, where cases with shallower initial stratification may exhibit
higher entrainment rates and push the interface deeper than those with deeper
initial stratification (Figure
28
d-f).

6.1.3
Discussion and limitations of the model

While equation (
31
) is simple, a model like this can be used to
highlight the coupling between energy injected at the surface, nonlinear
dynamics, and the lowering of the energetic barrier required for mixing. A
decrease in
\mathrm{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}At}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{init}}
or an increase in the initial thickness
L_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{init}}
,
decrease the Richardson number and weakens the barrier effect. This, in turn,
promotes entrainment and increases the mean density in the mixed layer.
Several factors are missing from the model. One is the breaking of interfacial
waves due to the secondary parametric instability or by presence of internal
waves. Another missing element is the oscillating motion exerted by the surface
wave on the miscible interface, which locally stretches and compresses the
interface, also weakening the barrier effect.
Additionally, the model assumes energy is injected at a single scale—that of
the primary wave. In practice, bubbles injected during splashdown are expected
to be highly energetic and operate at smaller scales. Nevertheless, this simple
model includes the basic ingredients required to reproduce the experiments and
simulations, providing a simple way to contextualize the competing dynamics of
this system.

7
Conclusions

In this study, we investigated mixing between two miscible fluids with a small
density contrast, induced by large amplitude standing waves at surface level.
Surface waves were generated using time-periodic vertical acceleration,
triggering the Faraday instability. The resulting standing wave allowed us to
isolate and study the short- and long-term evolution of stratification.

Through a combination of laboratory experiments and direct numerical
simulations, we identified two distinct mixing regimes for the short-term
evolution : (i) one primarily driven by a secondary destabilization of the
miscible interface, and (ii) another where mixing is boosted by the coupling
between the secondary destabilization and surface dynamics.

Secondary destabilization is driven by surface waves that advect the interface,
leading to the development of parametric instabilities and turbulent mixing. As
the forcing amplitude increases, coupling with surface dynamics becomes
increasingly important, drawing heavy fluid parcels from the disrupted interface
upwards into the mixed layer. This process is compounded by the collapse of
large cavities, which results in the injection of bubbles and light fluid deep
into the lower layer, enhancing vertical mixing.

The long-term evolution of the stratification revealed a non-monotonic behaviour
of the entrainment with respect to the forcing amplitude and initial
stratification depth. We identified turbulence injected downwards by breaking
and non-breaking surface waves as the dominant mixing mechanism. In this
context, non-monotonicity arises from the competition between two counteracting
effects: the decreasing energy barrier at the interface, due to the progressive
homogenization of the upper layer, and the exponential decay with depth of the
energy injected at surface level. This leads to the (eventual) saturation of the
mixed layer height. We also observed a progressive decoupling of the interface
from the surface dynamics, a direct consequence of the entrainment process,
consistent with predictions from linear theory.

Qualitatively, DNS effectively replicates experimental observations, capturing
key features of the free surface and interfacial waves. Quantitatively, while
the global energy levels show good agreement (with transient peaks reaching up
to twice the stationary values), DNS exhibited faster entrainment and diffusion
compared to experiments. These quantitative discrepancies are likely
attributable to differences in the Schmidt number. Specifically, the increased
dissipation and the longer time required for Faraday waves to develop
numerically had an unanticipated effect on the interface width. It is also
possible that the spatial resolution influences the droplet size distribution
and the scalar dissipation. These differences, which come from a compromise
between accuracy and computational cost, were considered to be small, but may
accumulate over extended periods of time.

The strong qualitative and quantitative agreement between our direct numerical
simulations and laboratory experiments under the investigated conditions
provides a solid foundation for understanding the dominant mixing mechanisms. An
important area for future investigation is how these results scale to larger
systems and different fluid properties often encountered in industrial
processes. This experimental and numerical setup offers promising avenues for
broader applications, particularly in oceanographic studies. While Faraday waves
and ocean waves differ in their generation and characteristics, our ability to
isolate specific effects could also guide the improvement of existing
parametrizations for wave-driven mixing in geophysical models.

In this section, we present a simple model for the evolution of three-layer

B.1
Mathieu equation with cubic non-linearities

In this section, we apply the method of multiple scales
(
Kevorkian & Cole 1996
;
Bender & Orszag 1999
)
to show the slow amplitude modulation and the typical behaviour
during the stationary and attenuation phases. Consider the Mathieu equation
(
15
) written in terms of
\tau=\omega t
,

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\alpha}{d\tau^{2}}+\frac{2\gamma}{\omega}\frac{d\alpha}{d\tau}+\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}(1+K(k\alpha)^{2}+\cdots)(1+F\cos(\tau))\alpha=0.

(51)

In standard fashion, we introduce a small parameter
\epsilon
and consider
the damping, forcing and nonlinear terms are small. We also consider the
near-resonance case with a small detuning parameter. We introduce the following
variables,

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{\omega}=\epsilon\mu,\quad\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}K=\epsilon\gamma,\quad\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}F=2\epsilon f

(52)

where
\mu
,
\gamma
, and
f
are of order 1 (or smaller). We focus on
the sub-harmonic response with a small detuning parameter

\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Delta}=\epsilon\delta
, such that
{\omega_{0}^{2}}/{\omega^{2}}=\frac{1}{4}+\epsilon\delta
. Introducing these new variables, we may write (
51
) as

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\alpha}{d\tau^{2}}+\frac{1}{4}\alpha=-\epsilon\left\{2\mu\frac{d\alpha}{d\tau}+\gamma k^{2}\alpha^{3}+\delta\alpha+2f\cos(\tau)\alpha\right\}+O(\epsilon^{2})

(53)

with the following initial conditions:
\alpha(0)=R_{0}
and
\dot{\alpha}(0)=0
,
where
R_{0}
is some constant.
If
\epsilon
is small with respect to the other parameters, we may search for
solutions of the form

\displaystyle\alpha=\alpha_{0}+\epsilon\alpha_{1}+\cdots

(54)

which depend on the variables
t_{\mathrm{f}}
and
t_{\mathrm{s}}

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{f}}

\displaystyle=\tau

(55a)

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{s}}

\displaystyle=\epsilon\tau.

(55b)

The chain rule gives

\displaystyle\frac{d\alpha}{d\tau}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\alpha_{0}}{\partial t_{\mathrm{f}}}+\epsilon\left(\frac{\partial\alpha_{1}}{\partial t_{\mathrm{f}}}+\frac{\partial\alpha_{0}}{\partial t_{\mathrm{s}}}\right)+\cdots

(56a)

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\alpha}{d\tau^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}\alpha_{0}}{\partial t_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}}+\epsilon\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\alpha_{1}}{\partial t_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}}+2\frac{\partial^{2}\alpha_{0}}{\partial t_{\mathrm{f}}\partial t_{\mathrm{s}}}\right)+\cdots

(56b)

\epsilon

\displaystyle\text{order $\epsilon^{0}$}:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\alpha_{0}}{\partial t_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}}+\frac{1}{4}\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle=0

(57a)

\displaystyle\text{order $\epsilon^{1}$}:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\alpha_{1}}{\partial t_{\mathrm{f}}^{2}}+\frac{1}{4}\alpha_{1}

\displaystyle=-\left\{2\frac{\partial^{2}\alpha_{0}}{\partial t_{\mathrm{f}}\partial t_{\mathrm{s}}}+2\mu\frac{\partial\alpha_{0}}{\partial t_{\mathrm{f}}}+\gamma k^{2}\alpha_{0}^{3}+\delta\alpha_{0}+f\left(e^{it_{\mathrm{f}}}+e^{-it_{\mathrm{f}}}\right)\alpha_{0}\right\}

\alpha_{0}(0)=R_{0}
and

for

\displaystyle\text{order $\epsilon^{0}$}:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\alpha_{0}}{\partial t_{\mathrm{f}}}(0)

\displaystyle=0,

(58a)

\displaystyle\text{order $\epsilon^{1}$}:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\alpha_{1}}{\partial t_{\mathrm{f}}}(0)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\alpha_{0}}{\partial t_{\mathrm{s}}}(0).

(58b)

∗

Appendix C
Estimation of potential and kinetic energies from the experiment

A practical way to validate the simulations is to consider global quantities
such as the potential and kinetic energies. In this section, we search for
reasonable estimates which can be obtained from the recorded images. By focusing
on the effects of the deepening of the interface and initially ignoring
molecular diffusion, the density field
\rho(\boldsymbol{x},t)
can be approximated
as a piecewise profile:

\displaystyle\rho(\boldsymbol{x},t)=\begin{cases}\rho_{1},&\text{for layer 1 }(z\leq\zeta_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}}(x,y,t))\\
\rho_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mixed}}=\rho_{1}-(\rho_{1}-\rho_{2})\frac{h_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{init}}}{h_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mixed}}},&\text{for layer 2 }(\zeta_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mix}}(x,y,t)<z\leq\zeta_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{surf}}(x,y,t))\\
\rho_{3},&\text{for layer 3 }(z\geq\zeta_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{surf}}(x,y,t))\end{cases}

(69)

where the upper layer is taken as a homogeneous mixture of fresh and salt water,
while the lower layer remains salt water. Replacing this profile in Eq.
(
3

\displaystyle\mathrm{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}At}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mixed}}\approx\frac{\rho_{1}-\rho_{2}}{\rho_{1}(2\frac{h_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mixed}}}{h_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{init}}}-1)+\rho_{2}}

(70)

\mathrm{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}At}_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{init}}

h_{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathrm{mixed}}
increases, less energy is required to mix both layers.

Now consider the change in the global potential energy:

\displaystyle\mathsf{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}PE}\equiv

\displaystyle\iiint\rho(\boldsymbol{x},t)gz\,dx\,dy\,dz.

(71)

Replacing the piecewise profile into Eq. (

(
18a

In §

, we used a POD-based decomposition
to separate the small- and large-scale components of the velocity and
concentration fields. Here, we provide additional details on this decomposition.
The main idea is to be able to decompose the velocity field into two components:
a large-scale component that contains the oscillating motions induced by the
Faraday waves, and a small-scale component that captures the turbulent
fluctuations,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x},t)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\tilde{u}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)+\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x},t),

(79)

since the time-averaged velocity field is assummed negligible.

To obtain the large-scale velocity field,
\boldsymbol{\tilde{u}}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
, we
perform a POD analysis of the velocity field,
\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
. About 60%
of the L2-norm of the velocity field is captured by the first POD modes and
about 75% by the first 10 modes (see figure
31
a). The
leading POD mode is reminiscent of the irrotational velocity field obtained from
the linear theory of Faraday waves, while the second POD mode corresponds to a
spatial and temporal harmonic of the first mode (see
figures
31
b-c). As this is an ad-hoc decomposition, we choose
to reconstruct the large-scale velocity field using the first
N
POD modes that
capture the oscillating component of the flow. We also verified that the viscous
dissipation associated with the large-scale velocity field is negligible
compared to the total viscous dissipation. A typical example of this decomposition is shown in figure
32
a-c.

We also wished to decompose the concentration field into a slow-time,
oscillating and fluctuating components,

\displaystyle c(\boldsymbol{x},t)

\displaystyle=\bar{c}(\boldsymbol{x},t)+\tilde{c}(\boldsymbol{x},t)+c^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x},t).

(80)

This way,
\bar{c}(\boldsymbol{x})
would represent the progression of the mean
stratification over long time scales and is expected to depend only on the
vertical coordinate,
\tilde{c}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
represents the oscillating component
of the concentration field due to the advection by the large-scale velocity
field, and
c^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
represents the small-scale fluctuations due to
turbulent mixing. Note that horizontal gradients in
\tilde{c}
and
c^{\prime}
are expected to contribute to the rotational part of the velocity field.

To obtain this decomposition, we use short-time averaging to separate the
slow-time component,
\bar{c}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
, from the oscillating and fluctuating
components,
\tilde{c}(\boldsymbol{x},t)+c^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
. Then, we perform a POD
analysis of the oscillating and fluctuating components to separate the
oscillating component,
\tilde{c}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
, from the fluctuating component,

c^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
. Once again, we choose to reconstruct the oscillating component
using the first
N
POD modes that capture the oscillating component of the
flow. A typical example of this decomposition is shown in
figure

A similar effect could be obtained by separating the velocity field into
rotational and irrotational components, by applying a band-pass filter in the
frequency domain, or by using phase-averaging. However, we found that the
POD-based decomposition provided a simple and effective way to separate the
different components of the flow.
