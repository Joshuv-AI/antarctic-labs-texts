Convex iso-Delaunay regions in strata of translation surfaces
Sam Freedman, Bradley Zykoski

Abstract
. Strata of translation surfaces are covered by the closures of finitely many iso-Delaunay regions: open subspaces parametrizing surfaces whose Delaunay triangulations are combinatorially equivalent. We prove that the iso-Delaunay regions for triangulations with involutions called triangle matchings are convex with respect to the angle coordinates of the triangles. As a corollary, we show that all iso-Delaunay regions in hyperelliptic stratum components are convex. This work generalizes two directions in the theory of flat surfaces.

1.
Introduction

For
g\geq 1
, let
S_{g}
be a closed surface of genus
g
, and let
Z=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\subset S_{g}
be a set of
n
points.
Every integer partition
\kappa=(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})
of
2g-2
determines a
Teichmüller stratum of translation surfaces

\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
.
This is the space of of
Teichmüller equivalence classes
of translation surfaces
(X,\omega)
, where
X
is a Riemann surface of genus
g
and
\omega\in H^{1,0}(X)
is a nontrivial abelian differential, along with a
marking
homeomorphism
f:(S_{g},Z)\to(X,\omega)
such that
\omega
has a zero of order
k_{i}
at the point
f(x_{i})
.
We will work with the complex projectivization
\mathbb{P}\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
of this space because the objects we study are invariant under the
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action on
\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
.

Every translation surface may be decomposed into a collection of polygons whose opposite sides have been identified by Euclidean translations.
A generic translation surface
(X,\omega)
admits a canonical such decomposition, where every polygon is a triangle, called the
Delaunay triangulation
of
(X,\omega)
.
This triangulation is dual to the classical Voronoi decomposition of
(X,\omega)
with respect to the zeros of
\omega
.
The strata
\mathbb{P}\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
naturally decompose as a countable union of
iso-Delaunay regions

\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}(\tau)
, which are subspaces of translation surfaces whose Delaunay triangulations are combinatorially equivalent to
\tau
.
Masur–Smillie first used these regions in
[
MS91
]
to obtain quantitative bounds on geometric quantities such as the lengths of saddle connections and volumes of strata to analyze degenerations of flat structures.

This work concerns the geometry of iso-Delaunay regions in strata of translation surfaces, and it may be viewed as a common generalization of the study of iso-Delaunay regions in two other contexts.

(i)

Bowman
[
Bow10
]
and Veech
[
Vee11
]
proved that Teichmüller disks intersect iso-Delaunay regions in convex hyperbolic polygons, which they used to algorithmically compute topological statistics of Teichmüller curves.

(ii)

Rivin
[
Riv94
]
and Veech
[
Vee97
]
proved that the iso-Delaunay regions in moduli spaces of complex-affine structures are convex in angle coordinates, yielding results on the local geometry of those moduli spaces.

The rigid nature of Teichmüller disks in (
i
) and the flexible nature of complex-affine structures in (
ii
) are necessary for these results.
Strata of translation surfaces enjoy neither of these features,
and the structure of iso-Delaunay regions in this setting has remained mysterious.
In particular, no iso-Delaunay regions in
\mathbb{P}\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
for
g\geq 2
were known to be contractible prior to this work.

In Theorem
A
, we prove that the iso-Delaunay regions for triangulations admitting involutions called
triangle matchings
(Definition
5.1
) are convex with respect to
angle coordinates
(Definition
4.6
), which parametrize the Euclidean angles in the triangles.

Theorem A

(Theorem
6.6
)
.

Let
\tau
be a topological triangulation of
(S_{g},Z)
such that
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}(\tau)
is nonempty.
If
\tau
admits a triangle matching, then
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}(\tau)
is convex with respect to angle coordinates.
In particular,
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}(\tau)
is contractible.

Furthermore, in §
7
we provide explicit constructions of convex iso-Delaunay regions, from which we obtain the following corollaries.

Corollary B

(Lemma
7.5
)
.

All iso-Delaunay regions in hyperelliptic stratum components are convex with respect to angle coordinates.

Corollary C

(Corollary
7.17
)
.

All components of the projectivized strata
\mathbb{P}\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
contain a contractible iso-Delaunay region.

Outline

An iso-Delaunay region is defined by
holonomy equations
and
Delaunay inequalities
on triangle angles; a crucial difficulty is that while the latter are affine-linear, the former are transcendental and opaque.
Our key insight is that when
\tau
admits a triangle matching
\iota:H\to H
on the
half-edges

H
of
\Gamma(\tau)
, these constraints reduces to an affine-linear system.

We first develop in §§
2
-
3
the necessary background on translation surfaces and trivalent ribbon graphs, respectively.
In §
4
, we define the
angle coordinate map

\Theta
on the
iso-triangulable region

\Delta=\Delta(\tau)
, and we show that its image
\Theta(\Delta)
is the locus of trivial holonomy in
angle space

\mathcal{A}
(Lemma
4.8
).
Next, in §
5
we introduce triangle matchings, identify the affine-linear
\iota
-invariant subspace
\mathcal{A}^{\iota}\subset\mathcal{A}
, and prove in §
6
that
\Theta(\Delta)=\mathcal{A}^{\iota}
(Theorem
6.6
). The affine-linearity of the Delaunay inequalities in angle coordinates implies the convexity of
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
.
Finally, in §
7
we construct examples of convex iso-Delaunay regions: those containing hyperelliptic surfaces (§
7.1
), sums of surfaces (§
7.2
) and
arboreal origamis
(§
7.3
).
In §
8
, we compare our results with the study
L^{\infty}
-Delaunay triangulations and the topology of strata.

2.
Triangulations of translation surfaces

In this section, we establish our notation and recall basic material on translation surfaces and their triangulations.
In particular, we define the iso-triangulable region
\Delta(\tau)
and iso-Delaunay region
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}(\tau)
associated to a triangulation
\tau
of a surface.
A basic reference on translation surfaces is
[
AM24
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
marked translation surface

(X,\omega)

with zero multiplicities

\kappa

on

(S_{g},Z)
is a translation surface along with an orientation-preserving diffeomorphism (a
marking
)
\varphi:S_{g}\to X
such that
\omega
vanishes to order
k_{j}
at each
\varphi(x_{j})
for
x_{j}\in Z
. When
k_{j}=0
, the differential
\omega
is nonvanishing at
\varphi(x_{j})
, and we say that
\varphi(x_{j})
is a
marked point
of
\omega
; otherwise, we say that
\varphi(x_{j})
is a
singularity
of
\omega
.

Definition 2.2
.

The
Teichmüller stratum

\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
is the space of equivalence classes
M=[(X,\omega)]
of marked translation surfaces
(X,\omega)
on
(S_{g},Z)
under the following equivalence relation.
Two marked translation surfaces
(X,\omega)
and
(X^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})
(with marking diffeomorphisms
\varphi
and
\varphi^{\prime}
) are
Teichmüller equivalent
if there is a biholomorphism
\psi:X\to X^{\prime}
with
\psi^{*}\omega^{\prime}=\omega
such that
\psi\circ\varphi
is isotopic rel
Z
to
\varphi^{\prime}
.

Remark 2.3
.

Given a marked translation surface
(X,\omega)
on
(S_{g},Z)
with marking
\varphi
, we obtain a Riemann surface structure on
S_{g}
by precomposing coordinate charts
X\supset U\to\mathbb{C}
with the marking.
In this way, we may regard
X
as a Riemann surface structure on
S_{g}
itself, and
\omega
as a differential form on
S_{g}
with zeroes in
Z
.
We therefore assume without loss of generality that
(S_{g},Z)
is the base topological surface of every marked translation surface that we consider.

For each
\gamma\in H_{1}(S_{g},Z;\mathbb{Z})
, we have a
period map

\Phi_{\gamma}:\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)\to\mathbb{C}
given by
[(X,\omega)]\mapsto\int_{\gamma}\omega
, which is well-defined because the class
\gamma
is invariant under isotopy rel
Z
.
The topology on the space
\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
is the weakest topology that makes the maps
\Phi_{\gamma}
continuous.
Given a basis
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{2g+n-1}
of
H_{1}(S_{g},Z;\mathbb{Z})
, it is a basic fact of Teichmüller theory that the map
\Phi=\prod_{j=1}^{2g+n-1}\Phi_{\gamma_{j}}:\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)\to\mathbb{C}^{2g+n-1}
is a local homeomorphism, called the
period coordinate map
determined by the basis
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{2g+n-1}
.

Definition 2.4
.

The group
\mathbb{C}^{*}
acts on
\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
by rescaling the differential: for
M=[(X,\omega)]\in\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
, we have
\lambda M=[(M,\lambda\omega)]
for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
.
We define
\mathbb{P}\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
as the quotient of
\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
by this action.

Note that there is a natural injective map
\mathbb{P}\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)\to\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
by forgetting the abelian differential.

Remark 2.5
.

Given a translation surface
(X,\omega)
on
(S_{g},Z)
with multiplicities
\kappa
, there is a flat incomplete Riemannian metric on
S_{g}\smallsetminus Z
induced by coordinate charts
\varphi:U\to\mathbb{C}
that satisfy
\varphi^{*}dz=\omega
.
This is the
Euclidean metric
on
(X,\omega)
.
At each point of
x_{j}\in Z
, there is a homeomorphism of a neighborhood of
x_{j}
to a neighborhood of the vertex of a Euclidean cone with cone angle
2\pi(k_{j}+1)
that is an isometry away from
x_{j}
.
We may therefore speak of, for example, line segments and Euclidean polygons on
(X,\omega)
.

Definition 2.6
.

A
topological triangulation

\tau
of
(S_{g},Z)
is a CW decomposition of
S_{g}
such that
Z
is the set of 0-cells, and the resulting complex is a simplicial complex. We will write
V(\tau)=Z,E(\tau),F(\tau)
for the sets of vertices, edges, and faces, respectively, of
\tau
.

Definition 2.7
.

A
geometric triangulation
of a translation surface
(X,\omega)
on
(S_{g},Z)
is a topological triangulation
\tau
such that each
f\in F(\tau)
is a Euclidean triangle on
(X,\omega)
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\tau
be a topological triangulation.
The
iso-triangulable region

\widetilde{\Delta}(\tau)\subset\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
is the set of all Teichmüller equivalence classes
M=[(X,\omega)]\in\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
where
(X,\omega)
a marked translation surface for which
\tau
is a geometric triangulation.
As
\widetilde{\Delta}(\tau)
is invariant under the action of
\mathbb{C}^{*}
, there is a well-defined projectivized
iso-triangulable region

\Delta(\tau)\subset\mathbb{P}\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
.

We now discuss Delaunay triangulations, adapting the presentation in
[
BS07
]
.

Figure 2.
A Delaunay triangulation of an equilaterally-tiled translation surface. Corollary

implies that
\psi+\psi^{\prime}<\pi
.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
(X,\omega)
be a translation surface.
An
immersed empty disk
is a continuous map
d:\overline{D}\to X
, where
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is an open Euclidean disk such that
d(D)\cap Z=\varnothing
, and
d|_{D}
is an isometric immersion with respect to the Euclidean metric on
(X,\omega)
.

Definition 2.10
.

A
Delaunay triangulation

\tau
of
(X,\omega)
is a geometric triangulation such that for each face
f\in F(\tau)
, there is an immersed empty disk
d_{f}:\overline{D}\to X
such that there are three points
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\in d_{f}^{-1}(Z)
such that
f
is the image of the Euclidean triangle with vertices
\{p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\}
. See Figure
2
.

There might be more than three points in
d_{f}^{-1}(Z)
.
If for every
f\in F(\tau)
there are exactly three points in
d_{f}^{-1}(Z)
, we say that the Delaunay triangulation
\tau
is
nondegenerate
.

Remark 2.11
.

If
(X,\omega)
admits a nondegenerate Delaunay triangulation
\tau
, then
\tau
is the unique Delaunay triangulation of
(X,\omega)
. Hence, in this case,
\tau
is uniquely determined by the metric structure of
(X,\omega)
.
See the discussion in
[
BS07
, §2]
.

Definition 2.12
.

Let
\tau
be a topological triangulation of
(S_{g},Z)
.
The
iso-Delaunay region

\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}(\tau)\subset\Delta(\tau)
is the subset of all Teichmüller equivalence classes
M=[(X,\omega)]\in\Delta(\tau)
where
(X,\omega)
is a marked translation surface for which
\tau
is a nondegenerate Delaunay triangulation.

Note that both
\Delta(\tau)
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}(\tau)
are open subsets of
\mathbb{P}\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
.

3.
Ribbon graphs

In §
3.1
, we establish our notation for trivalent ribbon graphs, which will be the basic combinatorial framework for this paper.
In §
3.2
, we describe the combinatorial analogue to the holonomy of a complex-affine structure, which will be used as a bridge between the combinatorial and geometric aspects of triangulations of translation surfaces.

3.1.
Basic definitions

Definition 3.1
.

Let
E
and
F
be finite sets.
A graph
\Gamma
with vertex set
E\cup F
and edge set
H
is a
trivalent ribbon graph
if

(i)

The vertices
e\in E
have valence 2, and the vertices
f\in F
have valence 3.

(ii)

There are
endpoint maps

\varepsilon:H\to E
and
\varphi:H\to F
such
e=\varepsilon(h)
and
f=\varphi(h)
are the vertices of
h
. We write
h=(f,e)
.

(iii)

There is a fixed-point-free action
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright H_{f}
on each fiber
H_{f}=\varphi^{-1}(f)
and therefore an action
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright H
.
Given
n\in\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
and
h=(f,e)\in H_{f}
, we write
(f,e+n)
for the image of
h
under the action of
n
.

We refer to the edges
(f,e)
of
\Gamma
as
half-edges
.

Definition 3.2
.

For each half-edge
(f,e)\in H
, we define the
combinatorial angle
as the set

(f;e,e+1)\coloneqq\{(f,e),(f,e+1)\}.

Let
A
denote the set of all combinatorial angles endowed with the
(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})
-action
(f;e,e+1)+n\coloneqq(f;e+n,e+n+1)
.
We refer to
\mathbb{Z}\langle A\rangle
as the
angle group

\mathbb{Z}\langle A\rangle
, and we write
(f;e+1,e)\coloneqq-(f;e,e+1)\in\mathbb{Z}\langle A\rangle
.

A face
f
of a topological triangulation
\tau
, along with the dual graph
\Gamma(\tau)
and the combinatorial angles
A(\tau)
.

Definition 3.3
.

Given a topological triangulation
\tau
of
(S_{g},Z)
, the
dual graph

\Gamma(\tau)
is the trivalent ribbon graph with vertices
E(\tau)\cup F(\tau)
and a half-edge
(f,e)\in H(\tau)
from
f\in F
to
e\in E
if and only if
e
is an edge of
f
.
The action of
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
on each
H_{f}
is defined so that
(f,e+1)
is the edge of
f
which is the successor of
(f,e)
in the counterclockwise cyclic ordering on the edges of
f
.
We also let
A(\tau)
denote the set of combinatorial angles of
\Gamma(\tau)
.
See Figure
3
.

For any trivalent ribbon graph
\Gamma
, it is easy to construct a unique topological triangulation
\tau(\Gamma)
of some closed surface such that
\Gamma=\Gamma(\tau(\Gamma))
.

Remark 3.4
.

As is implicit in Figure
3
, the graph
\Gamma(\tau)
naturally embeds into
S_{g}\smallsetminus Z
, and there is a deformation retraction
S_{g}\smallsetminus Z\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma(\tau)
.

The group of simplicial
0
-chains
C_{0}(\Gamma)
is the free abelian group
\mathbb{Z}\langle E\cup F\rangle=\mathbb{Z}\langle E\rangle\oplus\mathbb{Z}\langle F\rangle
, and the group of simplicial
1
-chains
C_{1}(\Gamma)
is the free abelian group
\mathbb{Z}\langle H\rangle
.
The following definition establishes our convention for the orientation of half-edges; in particular, we think of the half-edge
(f,e)
as being directed from
f
to
e
(see
(f_{1},e_{1})
and
(f_{2},e_{4})
in Figure
1
.)

Definition 3.5
.

We define the boundary map
\partial:C_{1}(\Gamma)\to C_{0}(\Gamma)
by
\partial(f,e)=e-f
. Let us write
(e,f)\coloneqq-(f,e)\in C_{1}(\Gamma)
. We write
Z_{1}(\Gamma)=\ker\partial
, the group of simplicial 1-cycles.

Remark 3.6
.

Since
\Gamma
is 1-dimensional, no 1-cycle is a nontrivial boundary, and hence
H_{1}(\Gamma;\mathbb{Z})=Z_{1}(\Gamma)\leq C_{1}(\Gamma)
.

Definition 3.7
.

A
simple cycle
in
\Gamma
is an element
\alpha\in C_{1}(\Gamma)
of the form

\alpha=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\left((e_{j-1},f_{j})+(f_{j},e_{j})\right),

where all
e_{j}
and
f_{j}
are distinct and the subscripts are considered modulo
\ell
.

Note that no simple cycle is a nonunit multiple of any other, and a simple cycle may be expressible as a sum of other simple cycles. The following lemma is standard.

Lemma 3.8
.

The subspace
H_{1}(\Gamma)\leq C_{1}(\Gamma)
is spanned by the simple cycles in
\Gamma
.

3.2.
Combinatorial holonomy

In this subsection, we develop notation to express the holonomy formulas of
[
Riv94
]
in the context of ribbon graphs.

Definition 3.9
.

An
angle assignment
is a function
\theta:A\to(0,\pi)
such that

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}}\theta(f;e+n,e+n+1)=\pi.

The
angle space

\mathcal{A}
for
\Gamma
is the set of all angle assignments
\theta:A\to(0,\pi)
.

Note that an angle assignment
\theta
extends naturally to a homomorphism
\mathbb{Z}\langle A\rangle\to\mathbb{R}
. In particular, we may write
\theta(f;e+1,e)=-\theta(f;e,e+1)
.
The following definition is inspired by
[
Riv94
, Definition 6.6]
.

Definition 3.10
.

Given
\theta\in\mathcal{A}
, we define the following homomorphisms. The
rotational contribution to holonomy
is the homomorphism

\displaystyle R^{\theta}:\mathbb{Z}\langle A\rangle

\displaystyle\to S^{1}\subset\mathbb{C}^{*}

\displaystyle(f;e,e+1)

\displaystyle\mapsto\exp(i\cdot\theta(f;e,e+1)).

The
dilational contribution to holonomy
is the homomorphism

\displaystyle D^{\theta}:\mathbb{Z}\langle A\rangle

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}\subset\mathbb{C}^{*}

\displaystyle(f;e,e+1)

\displaystyle\mapsto\frac{\sin\theta(f;e+1,e-1)}{\sin\theta(f;e-1,e)}.

These homomorphisms are defined to make Lemma
4.3
true. The following two definitions give formal relationships between homology cycles, combinatorial angles, and simplicial
1
-chains.
To illustrate this relationship, see the path labeled

Definition 3.11
.

As

is generated by simple cycles, we define the homomorphism

\varphi:H_{1}(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{Z}\langle A\rangle

by extending the following action on simple cycles by linearity:

\varphi\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\left((e_{j-1},f_{j})+(f_{j},e_{j})\right)\right)\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}(f_{j};e_{j-1},e_{j}).

Definition 3.12
.

We define a homormorphism

\displaystyle p:\mathbb{Z}\langle A\rangle

\displaystyle\to C_{1}(\Gamma)

\displaystyle(f;e,e+1)

\displaystyle\mapsto(f,e+1)-(f,e)

that sends an angle to its incident half-edges.

The next lemma tells us that every cycle in
\Gamma
can be understood as a sum of combinatorial angles.

Lemma 3.13
.

The homomorphism
\varphi
is injective.

Proof.

If
\alpha=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\left((e_{j-1},f_{j})+(f_{j},e_{j})\right)

is a simple cycle in

, observe that

\alpha=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\left((f_{j},e_{j})-(f_{j},e_{j-1})\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}p(f_{j};e_{j-1},e_{j})=p\left(\varphi(\alpha)\right),

where arithmetic in the subscript

is understood modulo
\ell
.
In other words,
p
is left inverse to
\varphi
, and
\varphi
is injective.

4.
Complex-affine structures

In this section, we develop geometric analogues to the combinatorial notions developed in §
3
.
In Definition
4.1
, we define a complex-affine structure on
S_{g}\smallsetminus Z
determined by an angle assignment
\theta:A\to(0,\pi)
.
In §

, we describe the relationship between its complex-affine holonomy and combinatorial holonomy.
In §

Let
\tau
be a triangulation of
(S_{g},Z)
, and let
\Gamma=\Gamma(\tau)
be its dual graph.
Given
\theta\in\mathcal{A}
, we will define a complex-affine structure on
S_{g}\smallsetminus Z
with an associated flat connection
\nabla(\theta)
on the tangent bundle
T(S_{g}\smallsetminus Z)
.

Definition 4.1
.

For each
(f,e)\in H
, let

\varphi_{(f,e)}^{\theta}:f\to\{z\in\mathbb{C}~|~\mathrm{Im}(z)\geq 0\}

be the continuous orientation-preserving map whose image is the Euclidean triangle with angles
\{\theta(f;e+n,e+n+1)~|~0\leq n\leq 2\}
such that
\varphi_{(f,e)}(e)
is the line segment
[0,1]
.

Now consider the combinatorial angle
(f;e,e^{\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}\langle A\rangle
, and suppose that the edge
e^{\prime}
belongs to the faces
f
and
f^{\prime}
.
There is a unique complex-affine transformation

\lambda_{(f;e,e^{\prime})}^{\theta}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}

that takes the edge
\varphi_{(f,e)}^{\theta}(e^{\prime})
onto the edge
\varphi_{(f^{\prime},e^{\prime})}^{\theta}(e^{\prime})
in an orientation-reversing manner.
(See Figure

Gluing the family
F
of triangles via the transformations
\lambda_{(f;e,e^{\prime})}^{\theta}
induces a complex-affine structure on the punctured surface
S_{g}\smallsetminus Z
as follows.
Consider small open neighborhoods
U_{f}
of the punctured triangles

.
The maps
\varphi_{(f,e)}^{\theta}
extend to the sets
U_{f}
in such a way that the transition functions
\varphi^{\theta}_{(f^{\prime},e^{\prime})}\circ(\varphi^{\theta}_{(f,e)})^{-1}
are equal to the transformations
\lambda_{(f;e,e^{\prime})}^{\theta}
.
We have thus specified an atlas of charts
\{\varphi^{\theta}_{(f,e)}:U_{f}\to\mathbb{C}\}
for a complex-affine structure
S_{g}\smallsetminus Z
.

Finally, we define
\nabla(\theta)
as the flat connection on
T(S_{g}\smallsetminus Z)
induced by this complex-affine structure.

Remark 4.2
.

Note that, for edges

and

4.1.
The holonomy homomorphism

The following computation is required for the proof of Lemma
4.5
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For all
(f;e,e^{\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}\langle A\rangle
, we have

\frac{d}{dz}\lambda^{\theta}_{(f;e,e^{\prime})}=D^{\theta}(f;e,e^{\prime})\cdot R^{\theta}(f;e,e^{\prime}).

Proof.

For
\zeta\in\mathbb{C}
, we refer to
\zeta/|\zeta|
as the
phase
of
\zeta
.
We will show that the phase and modulus of
\frac{d}{dz}\lambda^{\theta}_{(f;e,e^{\prime})}
are equal to
R^{\theta}(f;e,e^{\prime})
and
D^{\theta}(f;e,e^{\prime})
, respectively.

We first observe that
\lambda_{(f;e,e+1)}^{\theta}
rotates the triangle
\varphi_{(f,e)}^{\theta}(f)
counterclockwise through the angle
\theta(f;e,e+1)
and
\lambda_{(f;e,e-1)}^{\theta}
rotates
\varphi_{(f,e)}^{\theta}(f)
clockwise through the angle
\theta(f;e,e-1)
.
Since
e^{\prime}
is either
e+1
or
e-1
, it follows from either case that the phase of
\frac{d}{dz}\lambda^{\theta}_{(f;e,e^{\prime})}
is equal to
R^{\theta}(f;e,e^{\prime})
.

Now observe that since the segment
\varphi_{(f,e)}^{\theta}(e)
has length
1
, the Law of Sines implies that the length of
\varphi_{(f,e)}^{\theta}(e+1)
is
\sin\theta(f;e-1,e)/\sin\theta(f;e+1,e-1)
, and the length of
\varphi_{(f,e)}^{\theta}(e-1)
is equal to
\sin\theta(f;e,e+1)/\sin\theta(f;e+1,e-1)
.
In particular, we have

\mathrm{length}\left(\varphi_{(f,e)}^{\theta}(e^{\prime})\right)=D^{\theta}(f;e,e^{\prime})^{-1}.

Since the map
\lambda_{(f;e,e^{\prime})}^{\theta}
takes the edge
\varphi_{(f,e)}^{\theta}(e^{\prime})
onto the segment
[0,1]
, it follows that the modulus of
\frac{d}{dz}\lambda^{\theta}_{(f;e,e^{\prime})}
is equal to the inverse of the length of
\varphi^{\theta}_{(f,e)}(e^{\prime})
, which is
D^{\theta}(f;e,e^{\prime})
as desired.
∎

Definition 4.4
.

Fixing an arbitrary basepoint
x\in S_{g}\smallsetminus Z
, we have a holonomy homomorphism
\pi_{1}(S_{g}\smallsetminus Z,x)\to\mathbb{C}^{*}
given by parallel transport along the flat connection
\nabla(\theta)
.
Since
\mathbb{C}^{*}
is abelian, this map factors through the abelianization
H_{1}(S_{g}\smallsetminus Z;\mathbb{Z})
of
\pi_{1}(S_{g}\smallsetminus Z,x)
.
We define
\mathrm{Hol}^{\nabla(\theta)}:H_{1}(S_{g}\smallsetminus Z;\mathbb{Z})\to\mathbb{C}^{*}
to be the induced map.

As in Remark
3.4
, there is a deformation retraction
S_{g}\smallsetminus Z\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma
, and hence the inclusion
i:\Gamma\hookrightarrow S_{g}\smallsetminus Z
induces an isomorphism
i_{*}:H_{1}(\Gamma)\xrightarrow{\sim}H_{1}(S_{g}\smallsetminus Z;\mathbb{Z})
.
The following lemma asserts that the combinatorial holonomy map
\mathrm{Hol}^{\theta}
does indeed compute the holonomy of
\nabla(\theta)
.

Lemma 4.5
.

We have

\mathrm{Hol}^{\nabla(\theta)}\circ i_{*}=\mathrm{Hol}^{\theta}.

Proof.

Let
\alpha=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\left((e_{j-1},f_{j})+(f_{j},e_{j})\right)\in H_{1}(\Gamma)
be a simple cycle.
Then
i_{*}(\alpha)
is the homology class
[\gamma]\in H_{1}(S_{g}\smallsetminus Z;\mathbb{Z})
of the simple closed curve
\gamma
, which is the concatenation of arcs that join the edges
e_{j-1}
and
e_{j}
of
f_{j}
(as in Figure
4
).

Note that
\gamma
passes through the open sets
\{U_{f_{j}}~|~j\in\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}\}
, and the transition function from
U_{f_{j}}
to
U_{f_{j+1}}
is
\lambda_{(f_{j};e_{j-1},e_{j})}^{\theta}
.
By definition,
\mathrm{Hol}^{\nabla(\theta)}([\gamma])
is equal to the derivative of the composition of the transition functions between the charts through which
\gamma
passes.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Hol}^{\nabla(\theta)}(i_{*}(\alpha))=\mathrm{Hol}^{\nabla(\theta)}([\gamma])

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dz}\left(\lambda_{(f_{\ell};e_{\ell-1},e_{\ell})}^{\theta}\circ\cdots\circ\lambda_{(f_{1};e_{0},e_{1})}^{\theta}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}D^{\theta}(f_{j};e_{j-1},e_{j})\cdot R^{\theta}(f_{j};e_{j-1},e_{j})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Hol}_{D}^{\theta}(\alpha)\cdot\mathrm{Hol}_{R}^{\theta}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Hol}^{\theta}(\alpha),

where the third equality follows from Lemma
4.3
.
∎

4.2.
The angle map

Fix a topological triangulation
\tau
, and let
\Delta=\Delta(\tau)
be its associated iso-triangulable region.
We now consider how
\Delta
embeds into the space of complex-affine structures under the
angle map

\Theta
.
Lemma
4.8
uses
\Theta
to express the well-understood fact that a translation surface structure is equivalent (up to the action of
\mathbb{C}^{*}
) to a complex-affine structure with trivial holonomy.

Definition 4.6
.

Let
\widetilde{\Delta}=\widetilde{\Delta}(\tau)\subset\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
be an iso-triangulable region.
For each Teichmüller equivalence class
M\in\widetilde{\Delta}
, choose a translation surface structure
(X,\omega)\in M
such that
\tau
is a geometric triangulation of
(X,\omega)
.
For each combinatorial angle
(f;e,e+1)\in A
, we define the map

\widetilde{\Theta}_{(f;e,e+1)}:\widetilde{\Delta}\to(0,\pi)

such that
\widetilde{\Theta}_{(f;e,e+1)}(M)
is equal to the angle on
(X,\omega)
between the line segments
e,e+1
, measured inside the Euclidean triangle
f
. Note that the angle is independent of the choice of
(X,\omega)\in M
. These
\widetilde{\Theta}_{(f;e,e+1)}
together define the
angle map

\widetilde{\Theta}:\widetilde{\Delta}\to\mathcal{A}
, where
\widetilde{\Theta}(M)
is the angle assignment
(f;e,e+1)\mapsto\widetilde{\Theta}_{(f;e,e+1)}(M)
.

The angle map
\widetilde{\Theta}
is invariant under the action of
\mathbb{C}^{*}
on
\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
, because this action simply rescales and rotates the triangles, and so we have an induced angle map

\Theta:\Delta\to\mathcal{A}

defined on
\Delta\subset\mathbb{P}\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
.

As a consequence of Lemma
4.8
, we may understand
\Theta
as providing a system of coordinates on
\Delta
, which we refer to as
angle coordinates
.

Definition 4.7
.

We define the
locus of trivial holonomy
in
\mathcal{A}
to be the set

\mathcal{V}=\left\{\theta\in\mathcal{A}~\middle|~\mathrm{Hol}^{\theta}(\alpha)=1\text{ for all }\alpha\in H_{1}(\Gamma)\right\}.

That is,
\mathcal{V}
is the preimage under
\mathrm{Hol}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathrm{Hom}(H_{1}(\Gamma),\mathbb{C}^{*})
of the trivial character
H_{1}(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{C}^{*}
.

Lemma 4.8
.

The map
\Theta:\Delta\to\mathcal{A}
is a homeomorphism onto the locus
\mathcal{V}
of trivial holonomy.

Proof.

We first prove that
\Theta
is continuous.
Given
(f;e,e+1)
, let
\gamma
and
\gamma^{\prime}
denote the relative homology classes of the edges
e
and
e+1
of
f
, respectively, oriented outward from their common vertex.
Writing
\Phi_{\gamma}(M)=r\exp(i\varphi)
and
\Phi_{\gamma^{\prime}}(M)=r^{\prime}\exp(i\varphi^{\prime})
, we have
\widetilde{\Theta}_{(f;e,e+1)}(M)=\varphi-\varphi^{\prime}\in(0,\pi)
, where this difference is a well-defined element of
(0,\pi)
because
e+1
follows
e
in the counterclockwise orientation of the face
f
.
It follows that
\widetilde{\Theta}
depends continuously on the period maps
\Phi_{\gamma}
, and so
\widetilde{\Theta}
is continuous by the definition of the topology on
\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
.
Therefore, the induced angle map
\Theta
is also continuous, as desired.

Next, we show that the image of
\Theta
is in
\mathcal{V}
.
Consider
M\in\Delta
, and let
(X,\omega)
be a translation surface structure in the Teichmüller equivalence class
M
such that
\tau
is a geometric triangulation of
(X,\omega)
.
Recall that the transition functions for the atlas that induce the translation structure on
(X,\omega)
are of the form
z\mapsto z+C
for
C\in\mathbb{C}
.
As these maps have derivative equal to
1\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
, Lemma
4.5
implies that
\Theta(M)\in\mathcal{V}
.

Finally, we construct a continuous inverse mapping to
\Theta
that is defined on
\mathcal{V}
.
Fix
\theta\in\mathcal{V}
, and consider the complex-affine structure from Definition
4.1
, which induces the structure of a Riemann surface
X
on
S_{g}
.
Choose arbitrarily a half-edge
(f,e)\in H
, and consider the differential form on the face
f\subset S_{g}
given by
\omega_{(f,e)}\coloneqq(\varphi_{(f,e)}^{\theta})^{*}(dz)
, where the edge
e
of
f
is arbitrarily chosen.
The holonomy of the flat connection
\nabla(\theta)
is precisely the cohomological obstruction to extending this local form to a well-defined global form
\omega
on all of
S_{g}
.
Since
\Hol^{\theta}=\Hol^{\nabla(\theta)}\equiv 1
, there is no obstruction, and therefore we obtain a globally well-defined differential form
\omega
on
S_{g}
.
If another half-edge
(f,e)
had been chosen, the resulting form would differ by a scalar
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
.
The mapping
\mathcal{V}\to\Delta
that sends
\theta
to
[(X,\omega)]
, the Teichmüller equivalence class of
(X,\omega)
, is inverse to
\Theta
by construction, and is clearly continuous.
We conclude that
\Theta
is a homeomorphism onto
\mathcal{V}
.
∎

5.
Triangle matchings

We are now ready to define a
triangle matching

\iota:H(\tau)\to H(\tau)
, the central tool of this paper.
In §
5.1
, we define
\iota
-invariant angle assignments, and we prove in Theorem
5.8
that they determine the same combinatorial holonomy.
In Lemma
5.11
of §
5.2
, we show that a triangle matching induces a symmetry on the relative homology classes of the edges of
\tau
, which will be used to establish Lemma
6.1
and Lemma
7.15
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\iota:H\to H
be a bijection. We say that
\iota
is a
triangle matching
if

(i)

\iota
commutes with the action
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright H
.

(ii)

The induced automorphism
\iota:\mathbb{Z}\langle H\rangle=C_{1}(\Gamma)\to C_{1}(\Gamma)
satisfies
\iota(\alpha)=-\alpha
for all
\alpha\in H_{1}(\Gamma)\leq C_{1}(\Gamma)
.

Remark 5.2
.

The name “triangle matching” was chosen for the following reasons. Since, by Property (
i
),
\iota
commutes with the action
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\curvearrowright H
, and the set of triangles
F
is in one-to-one correspondence with the
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
-orbits of half-edges, the map
\iota
induces a bijection
\iota_{F}:F\to F
.

In fact, the triangles
f
and
\iota_{F}(f)
are “matched” in a stronger geometric sense: they must be congruent in any translation surface structure
(X,\omega)
for which
\tau
is a geometric triangulation. This is an immediate consequence of Lemma
5.11
, because the shapes of the triangles are determined by the periods
\int_{r(f,e)}\omega=-\int_{r(\iota(f,e))}\omega
.

\iota
on the ribbon graph dual to a triangulation of a L-shaped translation surface.
In the figure, we write
\iota(h_{j})=h^{\prime}_{j}
.
Because the half-edges
h_{1}
and
h_{1}^{\prime}
are matched, Lemma
5.11
implies that the arcs
\gamma
and
\eta
(when oriented left to right) are homologous.

Example 5.3
.

(Square L)
In Figure

, we indicate a triangle matching for the dual graph of the standard triangulation of the square L.
From the figure, we can see that the cyclic order about each solid face vertex is preserved.
The reader can also check that
\iota
negates the homology classes of each horizontal and vertical cylinder core curve (which form a basis for
H_{1}(\Gamma)
in this case).
For example, the horizontal core curve for the top square is the simple cycle

\alpha\coloneqq-h_{2}+h_{3}-h_{3}^{\prime}+h_{2}^{\prime}.

Applying
\iota
yields

\iota(\alpha)=-h^{\prime}_{2}+h^{\prime}_{3}-h_{3}+h_{2},

so
\iota(\alpha)
is indeed equal to
-\alpha
.
∎

5.1.
Triangle matchings and holonomy

Every triangle matching naturally determines an automorphism of the angle group
\mathbb{Z}\langle A\rangle
as in Definition
5.4
.
We may therefore consider the set of
\iota
-invariant angle assignments
\mathcal{A}^{\iota}
.
In Lemma
5.6
, we show that
\mathcal{A}^{\iota}
is a convex subspace of the space of angle assignments
\mathcal{A}
.
We study the representation
\theta\mapsto\mathrm{Hol}^{\theta}\in\mathrm{Hom}(H_{1}(\Gamma),\mathbb{C}^{*})
on
\mathcal{A}^{\iota}
in Theorem
5.8
to establish Corollary
5.9
, which is one direction of the containment to determine the image
\Theta(\Delta)
.

Definition 5.4
.

Let
(f^{\prime},e^{\prime})=\iota(f,e)
.
Because
\iota
commutes with the
(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})
-action on
H
, we have
\{\iota(f,e),\iota(f,e)+1\}=\{(f^{\prime},e^{\prime}),(f^{\prime},e^{\prime}+1)\}
.
That is to say,
\iota
induces a well-defined bijection
\iota:A\to A
given by
\iota(f;e,e+1)=(f^{\prime};e^{\prime},e^{\prime}+1)
, and hence an automorphism
\iota:\mathbb{Z}\langle A\rangle\to\mathbb{Z}\langle A\rangle
.

Definition 5.5
.

We say that an angle assignment
\theta:A\to(0,\pi)
is
\iota
-invariant
if
\theta\circ\iota=\theta
. We denote by
\mathcal{A}^{\iota}
the set of all
\iota
-invariant angle assignments
\theta\in\mathcal{A}
.

Note that
\mathcal{A}
can be viewed as the affine-linear subspace of
(0,\pi)^{3n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3n}
, where
n=\#F
, consisting of points
x
that satisfy
x_{3j}+x_{3j+1}+x_{3j+2}=\pi
for all
0\leq j<n
.
Therefore, we may speak of convex subsets of
\mathcal{A}
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\iota:H\to H
be a triangle matching. Then
\mathcal{A}^{\iota}
is a convex subspace of
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

The set
\mathcal{A}^{\iota}
is defined by a system of homogeneous linear equations, namely

\theta(f;e,e+1)-\theta(\iota(f;e,e+1))=0

for all
(f;e,e+1)\in A
.
∎

The holonomy representation restricted to
\mathcal{A}^{\iota}
takes a particularly simple form:

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\theta\in\mathcal{A}^{\iota}
.
Then
\mathrm{Hol}^{\theta}_{D}:H_{1}(\Gamma)\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is trivial, and
\mathrm{Hol}^{\theta}_{R}(\alpha)=\pm 1
for all
\alpha\in H_{1}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

Since
\theta\circ\iota=\theta
, we also have
\mathrm{Hol^{\theta}}\circ\iota=\mathrm{Hol}^{\theta\circ\iota}=\mathrm{Hol}^{\theta}
.
So for
\alpha\in H_{1}(\Gamma)
, we have

\mathrm{Hol}^{\theta}(\alpha)=\mathrm{Hol}^{\theta}(\iota(\alpha))=\mathrm{Hol}^{\theta}(-\alpha)=\mathrm{Hol}^{\theta}(\alpha)^{-1}.

The multiplicative group
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
does not have elements of order 2 other than the identity, so
\mathrm{Hol}^{\theta}_{D}
is trivial.
The elements of order 2 in
S^{1}
are
\pm 1
, and hence the latter claim follows.
∎

Although rotational holonomy has an a priori dependence on the angles, it is surprisingly independent of
\theta\in\mathcal{A}\iota
:

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}^{\iota}
. Then
\mathrm{Hol}^{\theta}=\mathrm{Hol}^{\theta^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

Consider the representation
\mathrm{Hol}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathrm{Hom}(H_{1}(\Gamma),\mathbb{C}^{*})\cong(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{\mathrm{rk}H_{1}(\Gamma)}
sending
\theta
to
\mathrm{Hol}^{\theta}
. From the formulas in Definition
3.10
, we see that
\mathrm{Hol}
is continuous. From Lemma
5.7
it follows that the image of
\mathcal{A}
under
\mathrm{Hol}
is discrete. Finally, Lemma
5.6
implies that
\mathcal{A}^{\iota}
is connected, and so we conclude that
\mathrm{Hol}|_{\mathcal{A}^{\iota}}
is constant.
∎

An
\iota
-invariant angle assignment need not have trivial holonomy, but as an immediate corollary of Theorem
5.8
we have the following.

Corollary 5.9
.

If
\mathcal{\mathcal{}}A^{\iota}\cap\mathcal{V}
is nonempty, then
\mathcal{A}^{\iota}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
.

5.2.
Triangle matchings and relative homology

Every half-edge
(f,e)
determines a unique class
r(f,e)\in H_{1}(S_{g},Z;\mathbb{Z})
that we define in Definition
5.10
.
In Lemma
5.11
, we demonstrate the identity
r(f,e)=-r(\iota(f,e))
, which we will use in Lemma
6.1
to prove that the image
\Theta(\Delta(\tau))
of an iso-triangulable region is
\iota
-invariant whenever
\tau
admits a triangle matching.

Definition 5.10
.

Given
(f,e)\in C_{1}(\Gamma)
, let
r(f,e)\in H_{1}(S_{g},Z;\mathbb{Z})
be the relative homology class of the edge
e
of
\tau
, oriented according to the counterclockwise orientation of the face
f
.

Observe that if
e
belongs to the faces
f
and
f^{\prime}
of
\tau
, then
r(f,e)=-r(f^{\prime},e)
.

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
\iota:H\to H
be a triangle matching. Then
r(f,e)=-r(\iota(f,e))
for every
(f,e)\in H
.

Proof.

Recall that there is a natural isomorphism
i_{*}:H_{1}(\Gamma)\xrightarrow{\sim}H_{1}(S_{g}\smallsetminus Z;\mathbb{Z})
.
Therefore, the algebraic intersection pairing on
H_{1}(S_{g}\smallsetminus Z;\mathbb{Z})\times H_{1}(S_{g},Z;\mathbb{Z})
gives a pairing

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:H_{1}(\Gamma)\times H_{1}(S_{g},Z;\mathbb{Z})\to\mathbb{Z},

which is nondenerate by Poincaré duality.

Let
\alpha=\sum\left((e_{j-1},f_{j})+(f_{j},e_{j})\right)
be a simple cycle.
Letting
[f,e](\alpha)
denote the coefficient of
(f,e)
in this sum, observe that

\langle\alpha,r(f,e)\rangle=[f,e](\alpha).

Since
\iota(\alpha)=-\alpha
, we have that

[f,e](\alpha)=[\iota(f,e)](\iota(\alpha))=[\iota(f,e)](-\alpha)=-[\iota(f,e)](\alpha).

We have thus showed that

\langle\alpha,r(f,e)\rangle=-\langle\alpha,r(\iota(f,e))\rangle

for every simple cycle
\alpha
.
Since
H_{1}(\Gamma)
is spanned by simple cycles and the intersection pairing is nondegenerate, we conclude that
r(f,e)=-r(\iota(f,e))
.
∎

For example, the horizontal arcs
\gamma\coloneqq-r(h_{1})
and
\eta\coloneqq r(h_{1}^{\prime})
in Figure
5
are homologous.

Proof of Theorem

Fix an iso-triangulable region
\Delta=\Delta(\tau)
with a triangle matching
\iota:H(\tau)\to H(\tau)
.
Lemma

and Corollary

together imply that

In Lemma

, we establish the reverse inclusion

6.1.
Iso-triangulable regions

Lemma 6.1
.

If
\tau
has a triangle matching
\iota:H(\tau)\to H(\tau)
, then
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\iota}
.

Proof.

Fix a combinatorial angle

.

If
\tau

.

6.2.
Iso-Delaunay regions

Given
e\in E
with
e\subset f,f^{\prime}
and
\theta\in\mathcal{A}
, we define the
sum of

\theta
-angles opposite

\theta(e)\coloneqq\theta(f;e+1,e+2)+\theta(f^{\prime};e+1,e+2)\in\mathbb{Z}\langle A\rangle.

The following is a fact of Euclidean geometry.

Proposition 6.3
.

Given a triangle
\triangle ABC
in the plane, let
D
be a point such that
A
and
D
lie on opposite sides of the line
\overleftrightarrow{BC}
. Then
D
lies outside the circumcircle of

7.
Examples of convex iso-Delaunay regions

In this section we give infinitely many examples of triangulations that admit triangle matchings, and hence by Theorem

, examples of convex iso-Delaunay regions.
In §

, we show that Delaunay triangulations of translation surfaces in hyperelliptic stratum components admit triangle matchings.
In §

, we define the connected sum

and

In §

7.1.
Hyperelliptic examples

We will show in Lemma

that hyperelliptic involutions acting simplicially on a triangulation yield examples of triangle matchings, from which Corollary

follows.
Note that this family of examples includes the square L.
We start by recalling some definitions.

Definition 7.1
.

An orientation-preserving homeomophism
\iota:S_{g}\to S_{g}
is a
hyperelliptic involution
if
\iota_{*}(\beta)=-\beta
for every
\beta\in H_{1}(S_{g};\mathbb{Z})

Definition 7.2
.

Let
\tau
be a topological triangulation of

We say that

is

induces a simplicial automorphism

Lemma 7.3
.

Suppose that
\tau
is a topological triangulation of
(S_{g},Z)
that is compatible with a hyperelliptic involution
\iota:S_{g}\to S_{g}
.
Then
\iota
induces a graph automorphism
\iota:\Gamma(\tau)\to\Gamma(\tau)
such that
\iota:H(\tau)\to H(\tau)

Proof.

The simplicial automorphism
\iota:\tau\to\tau
induces a graph automorphism
\iota:\Gamma(\tau)\to\Gamma(\tau)
. Thus, there is a well-defined induced map
\iota:H\to H
on the half-edges of
\Gamma
, and this map commutes with the
(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z})
-action on

because

is orientation-preserving.

Suppose
\#Z=1
. Then we have natural isomorphisms

H_{1}(S_{g};\mathbb{Z})\cong H_{1}(S_{g}\smallsetminus Z;\mathbb{Z})\cong H_{1}(\Gamma;\mathbb{Z}).

By Definition
7.1
, we have
\iota(\alpha)=-\alpha
for every
\alpha\in H_{1}(\Gamma;\mathbb{Z})
, and therefore
\iota:H\to H
is a triangle matching.

Now suppose
Z=\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
. Then there is a natural surjection

H_{1}(S_{g}\smallsetminus Z;\mathbb{Z})\overset{\pi}{\twoheadrightarrow}H_{1}(S_{g};\mathbb{Z}),

whose kernel is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}
. Let
\beta_{j}\in H_{1}(S_{g}\smallsetminus Z;\mathbb{Z})
denote the homology class of a small arc

encircling the point

counterclockwise for

.
Then
\beta_{1}=-\beta_{2}
, because

and

co-bound a connected subsurface of

.
In addition,

is equal to the set of integer multiples of

, because

bounds a disk in

centered on

.
Since
\iota
is assumed to nontrivially permute the elements of

, we have
\iota(z_{1})=z_{2}
and also
\iota(\beta_{1})=\beta_{2}
.
Since
H_{1}(S_{g}\smallsetminus Z;\mathbb{Z})\cong H_{1}(\Gamma;\mathbb{Z})
, the fact that
\iota
is a hyperelliptic involution along with the identity
\iota(\beta_{1})=\beta_{2}=-\beta_{1}
implies that
\iota(\alpha)=-\alpha
for every
\alpha\in H_{1}(\Gamma;\mathbb{Z})
.
We conclude that
\iota:H\to H
is a triangle matching.
∎

[

.

Definition 7.6
.

For
j=1,2

\Gamma_{j}

\varepsilon_{j}:H_{j}\to E_{j}
and
\varphi_{j}:H_{j}\to F_{j}

•

Lemma 7.8
.

Let

be trivalent ribbon graphs with triangle matchings
\iota_{j}:H_{j}\to H_{j}
, and let

be nonseparating edges of the graphs

. Then for
\Gamma_{1}\#_{h_{1}^{\prime},h_{2}^{\prime}}\Gamma_{2}

\iota_{1}\#\iota_{2}

Proof.

For each

, let

be a small open neighborhood of

in the graph
\Gamma_{1}\#\Gamma_{2}
, and let

\displaystyle U_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq U_{h^{\prime}_{2}}\cup\bigcup_{h\in H_{1}}U_{h},

\displaystyle U_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq U_{h^{\prime}_{1}}\cup\bigcup_{h\in H_{2}}U_{h}.

Retracting the neighborhoods

along the contractible sets

Moreover, since

retracts onto

By the Mayer–Vietoris Theorem, the diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U_{1}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}

Because

is assumed to be nonseparating, there exists a simple cycle
\alpha_{j}^{\prime}\in H_{1}(\Gamma_{j})
of which

is a nontrivial summand.
Regarding
\alpha_{j}^{\prime}
as an element of

, then because

Claim
.

There is a decomposition

Proof of Claim.

First, consider

, which by exactness is
\ker(i_{*}\oplus j_{*})
.
The map
i_{*}\oplus j_{*}
, viewed as a map from
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
to
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, has matrix

Using the definition of the connecting homomorphism, we compute that

Therefore

is our desired class mapping onto the image of

As a result of the above, we have established a splitting

Exactness at

on all of

Lemma 7.9
.

If
\Gamma
is the dual graph to a geometric triangulation
\tau
of a translation surface
(X,\omega)
, then every half-edge
h\in H
is nonseparating.

Proof.

First observe that the half-edge
h=(f,e)
is separating if and only if
\Gamma\smallsetminus\{e\}
is disconnected, because
h
retracts onto its endpoint
e
. The preimage of the vertex
e
of
\Gamma
under the deformation retraction
S_{g}\smallsetminus Z\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma
is the interior of the edge
e
of the triangulation
\tau
. Therefore,
\Gamma\smallsetminus\{e\}
is disconnected if and only if
(S_{g}\smallsetminus Z)\smallsetminus e
is disconnected, which means that the topological arc
e
bounds a subsurface of
S_{g}
. Since the triangulation
\tau
is geometric, the edge
e
is a saddle connection on
(X,\omega)
, and hence its period
\int_{e}\omega
is nonzero. An arc that bounds a subsurface is nulhomologous, and hence must have trivial period
\int_{e}\omega
. We conclude that if

is an edge of a geometric triangulation
\tau
, then

cannot be separating.
∎

7.3.
Arboreal origamis

In this subsection, we give combinatorial criteria that determine whether the
standard triangulation

\tau(h,v)
of an
origami

X(h,v)
admits a triangle matching (Theorem
7.16
).
Our proof uses Lemmas
7.8
and
7.9
to establish that
\tau(h,v)
admits a triangle matching if and only if the core curves of

form an

as in
[
CS21
, Definition 4.6]
.
Hamenstädt
[
Ham21
, Definition 1.2]
independently employed arboreal networks under the name

.
We begin by recalling some basic definitions.

Definition 7.10
.

For
s\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathrm{Sym}(s)
denote the symmetric group on
s

Given a pair of permutations
(h,v)\in\mathrm{Sym}(s)^{2}
such that
h
and
v
act transitively on
[s]\coloneqq\{1,\dots,s\}
, we take the set
[0,1]^{2}\times[s]
and impose the relations

(1,y,j)\sim(0,y,h(j))\text{ and }(x,1,j)\sim(x,0,v(j)).

The quotient
X\coloneqq([0,1]^{2}\times[s])/\sim
inherits an abelian differential
\omega
from the differential
dz
on
[0,1]^{2}
. The image of every point of the form
(0,0,j)\in[0,1]^{2}\times[s]
at which
\omega
does not vanish is regarded as a
marked point
(cf. Definition
2.1
) of
\omega
.

We denote by
X(h,v)\coloneqq(X,\omega)
the translation surface structure determined by this construction, and we say that
X(h,v)
is an
origami
.
Because
h
and
v
act transitively on
[s]
, the resulting surface is connected.
For each
j\in[s]
, we say that the image of
[0,1]\times\{j\}
in

under the quotient mapping is a
square
of
X(h,v)
.

Definition 7.11
.

We say that a simple closed curve
\beta\subset X(h,v)
is a
horizontal (resp. vertical) cylinder core curve
if every point

is of the form
(x,\tfrac{1}{2},j)
(resp.
(\tfrac{1}{2},y,j)
).
Let
\mathcal{N}(h,v)
denote the collection of all cylinder core curves on

, called the
network
for
X(h,v)
.

Definition 7.12
.

Let
X(h,v)
be an origami. The
standard triangulation

\tau(h,v)
of
X(h,v)
is the geometric triangulation whose faces are the isosceles right triangles with hypotenuses equal to the positively sloped diagonals of the squares of
X(h,v)
.

See Figure
1
for the example of the square L.

Definition 7.13
.

Let
X(h,v)
be an origami.
If the geometric intersection number

is at most 1 for each pair of core curves
\eta,\eta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(h,v)
, we say that
X(h,v)
is

.

Definition 7.14
.

If
X(h,v)
is geometrically simple, let

denote the simple graph with vertices
\mathcal{N}(h,v)
, where we add an edge between
\eta,\eta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}(h,v)
if and only if
\eta
and

and its network

The square L admits an arboreal network.
We give another example in Example

.

Lemma 7.15
.

If
X(h,v)
is a geometrically simple orgami whose standard triangulation admits a triangle matching
\iota:H\to H
, then for all

the half-edges
h
and
\iota(h)
must reside in the same square.

Proof.

Let
h=(f,e)\in H
, and let

.
Recall the map

from Definition

.
Assume that

is a diagonal edge oriented so that it points down and to the left — the other cases will follow from the fact that a triangle matching commutes with the
\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}
action on

.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
denote the core curves of the horizontal and vertical cylinders, respectively, that contain the face

.
By Lemma

we have

are contained in the same horizontal cylinder.

are contained in the same vertical cylinder.
Because

\alpha

∎

Corollary 7.17
.

For
g\geq 3
, every nonhyperelliptic component of the projectivized stratum of
\mathbb{P}\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
contains a contractible iso-Delaunay region.

Proof.

Lemma 6.15 of
[
CS21
]
, or equivalently Theorem 1.3 of
[
Ham21
]
, provides explicit arboreal networks
\mathcal{N}
that, after applying the Thurston–Veech construction (see, for instance,
[
McM22
, §6]
), produce abelian differentials in every nonhyperelliptic component of the moduli stratum
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(\kappa)
.
Replacing all the rectangles in the Thurston–Veech decomposition with unit squares (and choosing appropriate mapping class group lifts) produces origamis
X(h,v)
in each nonhyperelliptic component of
\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
.

Note that the matrix

A=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1/2\\
0&\sqrt{3}/2\\
\end{pmatrix}

maps the triangles with vertices
\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\}
and
\{(1,0),(1,1),(0,1)\}
onto equilateral triangles.
The matrix
A
then acts on
X(h,v)
to give a translation surface
A\cdot X(h,v)
on which every triangle of
\tau(h,v)
is equilateral.
An equilateral triangulation is nondegenerate Delaunay, so the projective class of
A\cdot X(h,v)
belongs to the nonempty iso-Delaunay region
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}(\tau(h,v))
.
Since
\mathcal{N}
is aboreal, Theorem
7.16
implies that
\tau(h,v)
admits a triangle matching and
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}(\tau(h,v))
is contractible by Theorem
6.6
.
∎

8.
Future directions

The structure of the iso-Delaunay regions is of independent interest, as seen in the works
[
MS91
]
,
[
Bow10
]
,
[
Vee11
]
,
[
Vee97
]
, and
[
Riv94
]
mentioned in the introduction.
In addition, if it were known that
all
the iso-Delaunay regions were convex, then these regions could be used to form a cellulation of
\Omega\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(\kappa)
, and also of the moduli stratum
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(\kappa)
.
This is precisely what is done in the case of iso-Delaunay regions for the
L^{\infty}
-metric in
[
Zyk22
]
.

In that context, the induced cellulation may be understood computationally by way of linear programming, but precise analysis of the general combinatorial properties of that cellulation are stymied by its sensitive dependance on the geometry of translation surfaces.
However, the main results of this paper depend almost entirely on the combinatorial structure of surface triangulations.
This gives us optimism that these iso-Delaunay regions may produce a cellulation of
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(\kappa)
about which one can more easily prove structural results, and hence obtain results on the topology of
\Omega\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(\kappa)
, a subject about which few general results are known.
