Intraoperative quantitative angiography predicts midterm left subclavian artery patency after thoracic endovascular aortic repair
Authors: Jiawei Zhao, Kun Fang, Mingyao Luo, Jia Liu, Dong Chen, Chang Shu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) frequently requires coverage of the left subclavian artery (LSA) to achieve an adequate proximal landing zone, which carries the risk of LSA occlusion and associated ischaemic complications. No validated real-time intraoperative tool currently exists to assess LSA hemodynamics during TEVAR. This study aimed to identify hemodynamic predictors associated with midterm LSA patency after TEVAR involving LSA management.MethodsPatients who underwent TEVAR for aortic pathologies between April 2018 and December 2020 were retrospectively enrolled. Quantitative assessment of LSA blood flow before and after TEVAR was conducted using the angiography post-processing software, iFlow. Two key hemodynamic parameters, the differential of delayed time to peak (diff.dTTP) and peak ratio (diff.Pr) in the proximal (P), middle (M), and distal (D) areas of the LSA were extracted. LSA patency was determined through follow-up computed tomography angiography (CTA). Cox regression analysis was used to identify hemodynamic predictors for LSA patency.ResultsA total of 94 patients [median age, 61 years; interquartile range (IQR), 51.00–65.75 years; 83.0% male] were included, with 7 patients (7.4%) experiencing LSA occlusion during follow-up (median duration, 1,242 days; IQR, 968–1,356 days). The diff.dTTPs were identified as independent risk factors for midterm LSA occlusion [diff.dTTP.P: hazard ratio (HR) =30.0, P<0.001; diff.dTTP.M: HR =6.1, P<0.001; diff.dTTP.D: HR =5.1, P<0.001]. These hemodynamic parameters provided incremental prognostic value for midterm LSA occlusion [diff.dTTP.P: area under the curve (AUC) =0.957; diff.dTTP.M: AUC =0.921; diff.dTTP.D: AUC =0.938].ConclusionsiFlow provides a real-time quantitative approach for evaluating LSA hemodynamics during TEVAR. The diff.dTTP values can predict midterm LSA patency and may guide LSA management strategies during TEVAR.Keywords: Aortic pathologies, thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR), left subclavian artery patency (LSA patency), quantitative angiography, hemodynamic predictors

Introduction

Aortic diseases pose a life-threatening risk and are challenging to manage due to their intricate anatomical location and adjacent abnormalities. Thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) has emerged as a minimally invasive treatment option for descending thoracic aortic pathologies (1-3). However, expanding a safe landing zone in TEVAR often necessitates the initial coverage of the left subclavian artery (LSA). Despite its efficacy, this approach has been associated with heightened perioperative risks, including stroke, spinal cord ischemia, left upper extremity ischemia, and even death (4,5). Consequently, LSA revascularization has gained prominence as a strategy to preserve blood flow and mitigate ischemic complications in elective TEVAR (6,7). Previous studies have confirmed that even when LSA revascularization is performed, improper configurations of the branched stent can still cause hemodynamic disturbances in the LSA, potentially promoting thrombus formation (8-10).

To minimize the risk of LSA occlusion following TEVAR, it is crucial to perform comprehensive hemodynamic assessments and identify the hemodynamic risk factors of LSA occlusion during TEVAR. Presently, there is a notable gap in real-time and accurate hemodynamic evaluation for LSA blood flow during TEVAR. Diagnosis of LSA occlusion relies on a multifaceted approach involving symptomatology, digital subtraction angiography (DSA), computed tomography angiography (CTA), and ultrasonography. Delayed diagnosis of LSA occlusion may result in postponed intervention, increasing the risk of upper extremity and central nervous system ischemia (4,5). A real-time, quantitative assessment of LSA hemodynamics could offer an instantaneous intraoperative guide for reintervention before symptom manifestation (11,12). The iFlow stands out as an innovative quantitative hemodynamic analysis tool, which generates real-time time-concentration curves leveraging DSA data for intraoperative analysis of hemodynamics. Previous studies have underscored the feasibility and potential of iFlow in predicting hemodynamic-related prognoses in cerebral and peripheral vascular diseases (13-15).

This study aims to investigate the utility of iFlow in predicting LSA outcomes post-TEVAR. We hypothesize that iFlow-derived hemodynamic parameters predict midterm LSA patency following TEVAR. By validating these predictors, we seek to refine LSA management techniques and reduce the incidence of occlusion-related complications. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1-2565/rc).

Methods

Patients

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This retrospective case-control study was approved by the Ethics Committee and the Institutional Review Board of National Centre for Cardiovascular Diseases, Fuwai Hospital (No. 2021-1525). Informed consent for each individual was waived due to its retrospective nature. Between April 2018 and December 2020, we reviewed the medical records of patients who underwent TEVAR in zone 2 for aortic pathologies at the National Centre for Cardiovascular Diseases, Fuwai Hospital. Only patients with pre- and postoperative aortic angiography images of sufficient quality were included. Exclusion criteria included patients treated with parallel techniques (chimney or periscope), LSA transposition or bypass, those with a history of previous aortic intervention or aberrant subclavian artery, and cases with image artifacts, incomplete imaging fields, or compromised image quality. These exclusions were made due to alterations in LSA anatomical morphology that alter the blood flow path or insufficient data for blood flow analysis. Thus, our study finally enrolled the patients undergoing zone 2 TEVAR combined with the LSA fenestration technique or partial/total LSA coverage.

The clinical events were recorded during hospitalization and follow-up. All patients completed the first postoperative CTA before discharge or within the first month and were scheduled for outpatient follow-up at 3, 6, and 12 months, followed by yearly thereafter. Symptoms and clinical signs were also collected via clinic visits and telephone interviews. The primary endpoint was defined as LSA occlusion revealed by follow-up CTA. Based on the LSA patency in follow-up CTA, patients were categorized into LSA patent and LSA occlusion groups (Figure 1).

Procedure description

TEVAR was performed in a hybrid operating room under general anaesthesia. For patients with inadequate proximal landing zones (<15 mm), LSA revascularization was performed in conjunction with TEVAR. Revascularization methods included in-situ fenestration (ISF) and physician-modified fenestration (PMF). The decision to utilize ISF or PMF was based on a comprehensive assessment of the specific aortic pathology and the anatomical configuration of the arch branches. For ISF, a fenestration of 5–8 mm diameter was created in the stent graft fabric using the needle technique combined with sequential balloon dilation to match the LSA orifice. For PMF, customized fenestrations were pre-created in the stent graft fabric before deployment. Bridging stent grafts (covered or bare stents, typically 7–10 mm in diameter and 30–60 mm in length) were deployed through the fenestration to establish a connection between the aortic lumen and LSA. In emergency cases with right vertebral dominance and preserved collateral vertebrobasilar circulation, partial or total LSA coverage was undertaken. A detailed procedural standard is available in previously published literature (16).

DSA protocol and image postprocessing

As shown in Figure 2, pre- and post-stent-graft deployment DSA was acquired using the Artis Zeego angiographic system (Siemens Healthcare, Forchheim, Germany) with the following settings: tube voltage of 70 kV, radiation dose of 3.000 µGy/frame, pulse width of 36.8 ms, frame rate of 7.5 fps, and a 48 cm field of view, with an anterior oblique projection angle of 46°, covering the aortic arch and supra-aortic branches. A 25 mL injection of Visipaque 320 mgI/mL contrast (GE Healthcare, Cork, Ireland) was delivered through a 5-Fr pigtail catheter in the ascending aorta at a rate of 18 mL/s, with a 0.4 s delay. The acquisition duration (5–10 s) was adjusted to visualize the left axillary artery contrast. To maintain consistency in hemodynamic conditions during DSA acquisition, systolic blood pressure was controlled at around 100 mmHg and heart rate at around 60 beats/min. Temporary interruption of mechanical ventilation maintained stable patient vitals throughout image acquisition to minimize motion artifacts while ensuring adequate oxygenation.

Workflow for LSA hemodynamic evaluation. Intraoperative DSA images were acquired, transferred to the workstation, and post-processed using iFlow. The resultant images were color-coded into a single summation image. ROIs were delineated on the color-coded image, and the corresponding TICs, along with hemodynamic parameters, were calculated for each ROI. DSA sequences were analysed using iFlow on a dedicated workstation (syngo X-Workplace VB21; Siemens Healthineers, Forchheim, Germany). Hemodynamic parameters were calculated for each LSA ROI and included dTTP and Pr for the proximal (dTTP.P and Pr.P), middle (dTTP.M and Pr.M), and distal (dTTP.D and Pr.D) segments. Differential of dTTP and Pr before and after TEVAR were defined as diff.dTTP and diff.Pr, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as means ± standard deviations, medians [interquartile ranges (IQRs)], or frequencies (%), as appropriate. Normality was assessed using Q-Q plots. Comparisons between patient groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), Chi-squared tests, or Fisher’s exact test, as applicable. Changes in hemodynamic parameters were compared using Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression, with Firth’s penalized likelihood correction applied when necessary (17). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to identify optimal cutoffs for hemodynamic parameters. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated to estimate the cumulative probability of LSA occlusion events based on dichotomized diff.dTTP values. Subgroup analyses were performed to assess interactions between dichotomized diff.dTTP and clinical factors, with comparisons using the Z-test for differences in regression coefficients (18).

All analyses were conducted using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corporation), SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute), and R 4.1.3 (R Development Core Team). Statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 94 patients were included in this study, with 87 patients having patent LSA and 7 patients experiencing LSA occlusion. The median age of the cohort was 61 years (IQR, 51.00–65.75 years), with no significant age difference between the patent and occlusion groups (P=0.462). The gender distribution showed a predominance of males (83.0%), with similar distributions in both groups (P=0.340). The distribution of aortic pathologies within the cohort comprised type B aortic dissection in 49 patients (52.1%), thoracic aortic aneurysm in 6 patients (6.4%), pseudoaneurysm in 5 patients (5.3%), penetrating aortic ulcer in 18 patients (19.1%), and intramural hematoma in 16 patients (17.0%).

The present analysis entailed a comparison of the dTTP values and the alterations in dTTP values before and after TEVAR in both the LSA patent and LSA occlusion groups, respectively. Preoperatively, no statistically significant disparities were identified in the dTTPs between the two groups (Figure 4A). Postoperatively, marked differences were observable in the dTTP within the proximal (0.28±0.39 vs. 1.08±0.30, P<0.001), middle (0.77±0.66 vs. 1.88±0.93, P<0.001), and distal (1.84±0.95 vs. 3.83±1.79, P<0.001) regions between the two groups (Figure 4B). Analogously, the diff.dTTP at the proximal (0.03±0.49 vs. 1.01±0.25, P<0.001), middle (−0.10±0.70 vs. 1.28±0.75, P<0.001), and distal (−0.17±0.75 vs. 2.00±1.65, P<0.001) regions also manifested significant dissimilarities between the two groups (Figure 4C).

LSA patency predictors

Cox proportional hazards regression analysis explored predictors of LSA occlusion events. In univariate analysis, LSA coverage (HR =8.2, 95% CI: 1.8–37.0, P=0.006), diff.dTTP.P (HR =29.4, 95% CI: 5.8–148.0, P<0.001), diff.dTTP.M (HR =5.9, 95% CI: 2.6–13.4, P<0.001), and diff.dTTP.D (HR =5.3, 95% CI: 2.4–11.8, P<0.001) were identified as predictors of LSA occlusion events (Table 3). Multivariate analysis, adjusted for age, gender, and LSA management, demonstrated that all diff.dTTPs exhibited significant independent associations with LSA patency (diff.dTTP.P: HR =30.0, 95% CI: 5.2–170.0, P<0.001; diff.dTTP.M: HR =6.1, 95% CI: 2.2–17.0, P<0.001; diff.dTTP.D: HR =5.1, 95% CI: 2.0–13.0, P<0.001) (Table 4). A ROC analysis utilized all significant hemodynamic parameters from the Cox regression model to determine cut-off values associated with LSA patency (Figure 5). All three diff.dTTP parameters showed very high performance. [diff.dTTP.P: cut-off =0.675 s, area under the curve (AUC) =0.957; diff.dTTP.M: cut-off =1.000 s, AUC =0.921; diff.dTTP.D: cut-off =0.730, AUC =0.938]. Based on these cut-offs, patients were stratified into two groups: those with low diff.dTTPs and those with high diff.dTTPs. The high diff.dTTP group exhibited a significantly increased likelihood of LSA occlusion after TEVAR compared to the low diff.dTTP group (P<0.001 for all) (Figure 6).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were stratified by age (≤60 vs. >60 years), gender (male vs. female), and LSA management strategy (revascularization vs. coverage). In these analyses, diff.dTTP.P, diff.dTTP.M, and diff.dTTP.D consistently remained associated with an increased risk of mid-term LSA occlusion. No significant interactions were detected between diff.dTTP.P or diff.dTTP.M and the stratified characteristics in relation to mid-term LSA occlusion (all P for interaction >0.1; Figure 7A,7B). However, gender was found to modify the association between diff.dTTP.D and LSA occlusion (P for interaction =0.038; Figure 7C). Specifically, a high diff.dTTP.D was a significant risk factor in men, whereas this association was not significant in women.

Discussion

LSA occlusion following TEVAR is often asymptomatic in the early stages, which can delay intervention and increase the risk of ischemic complications. This study investigates the relationship between intraoperative LSA hemodynamics and midterm LSA patency, and our key finding is that intraoperative LSA hemodynamic parameters can predict midterm LSA patency following TEVAR. Specifically, delays in LSA blood flow after TEVAR were identified as independent risk factors for midterm LSA occlusion. To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the association between intraoperative LSA hemodynamics and midterm LSA patency.

In nearly 40% of patients undergoing TEVAR for aortic pathologies, coverage of the LSA is necessary to secure an adequate proximal landing zone for the stent graft (19). However, direct coverage of the LSA can lead to severe complications, including arm and spinal cord ischemia, stroke, and even death (20). Despite these risks, LSA coverage, whether partial or complete, is often unavoidable during emergency TEVAR procedures. The decision to perform LSA revascularization depends on both the patient’s anatomy and the urgency of the surgery, yet there are no standardized quantitative criteria to guide this decision-making process (6). Current guidelines recommend routine LSA revascularization in elective TEVAR cases to maintain normal antegrade flow and reduce ischemic complications (6,7). However, even with revascularization, improper branch stent configuration can result in post-implantation hemodynamic disturbances, which may lead to thrombosis and significant stenosis of the LSA (8-10). In our study, three patients who underwent LSA revascularization experienced LSA occlusion during follow-up due to factors such as fenestration misalignment or kinking of the bridging stent. In contrast, twelve patients who underwent partial LSA coverage during emergency TEVAR did not experience LSA occlusion. These findings underscore the importance of LSA hemodynamics in determining the prognosis of TEVAR procedures involving LSA intervention. However, research on intraoperative LSA hemodynamics and its correlation with LSA patency remains limited, highlighting the need for further studies.

Various methods are available to assess LSA hemodynamics, including inter-arm blood pressure differences, CT, MRI, and duplex ultrasound. However, CT and MRI, while effective, are impractical for intraoperative use, and duplex ultrasound is typically performed before or after TEVAR to assess blood flow in the vertebral and carotid arteries (21). In our study, we utilized iFlow for intraoperative LSA hemodynamic evaluation. This technique allows surgeons to assess dynamic blood flow changes within the target vessel intraoperatively, without the need for additional angiography. Prior to this study, iFlow had been used primarily for blood flow assessment in cerebral and peripheral vascular interventions (13-15). Burkhardt et al. found that iFlow parameters, such as TTP and dTTP, could predict symptomatic vasospasm following aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (13). Similarly, Huang et al. observed that prolonged TTP and shortened trans-stenotic TTP after angioplasty and stenting were associated with stroke relapse in patients with intracranial vertebrobasilar atherosclerosis (14). Tang et al. showed that postoperative ankle joint TTP could predict the clinical success of endovascular interventions for peripheral artery disease (15). Beyond cerebral and peripheral applications, iFlow and color-coded DSA quantification have also demonstrated utility in the aortic context. Fang et al. first reported the feasibility of iFlow for intraoperative renal perfusion assessment during TEVAR for type B aortic dissection, confirming that quantitative hemodynamic changes in target vessels can be captured in real time during aortic endovascular procedures (22). Zhou et al. subsequently demonstrated that color-coded quantitative DSA could predict the outcomes of immediate type I and type III endoleaks following endovascular aortic repair, underscoring the prognostic value of intraoperative flow quantification beyond the index vessel (23). Augustin et al. further applied color-coded summation imaging to evaluate blood flow in endovascular aortic dissection fenestration, highlighting its potential to characterize hemodynamic changes associated with complex aortic remodeling (24). Collectively, these studies establish a growing body of evidence supporting iFlow-based assessment across diverse aortic endovascular scenarios, providing the methodological foundation upon which the present study builds. These studies validate iFlow’s potential for intraoperative blood flow assessment and its ability to predict hemodynamic outcomes. Our results confirm that a significant delay in dTTP post-TEVAR is associated with an increased risk of midterm LSA occlusion, which may correlate with higher risks of upper limb ischemia and neurological complications.

Remarkably, we identified diff.dTTP as an independent risk factor for midterm LSA occlusion, with significant sensitivity and specificity. In particular, the parameter diff.dTTP.M, associated with the middle subclavian artery’s anatomical morphology, showed the most delayed cutoff. We hypothesize that dTTP.M may be influenced by vertebral artery blood flow, potentially leading to flow reversal due to arterial insufficiency in the LSA post-TEVAR (25). This highlights the importance of monitoring more pronounced dTTP changes for accurately predicting LSA occlusion risk. An important consideration in our analysis is the inclusion of both LSA coverage and fenestration reconstruction strategies. While these approaches involve different hemodynamic determinants, both represent clinically relevant management options that may result in compromised LSA blood flow. Nevertheless, both multivariable Cox regression and subgroup analyses demonstrated that differential dTTP remained a significant predictor across both management strategies. This suggests that delayed LSA blood flow serves as a common final pathway, indicating increased occlusion risk. Additionally, subgroup analyses revealed that the relationship between diff.dTTP.D and LSA occlusion was influenced by gender, suggesting that gender-specific factors may modify this association. While the low incidence of spinal cord ischemia (1.1%) in our cohort limits the statistical power to evaluate associations between hemodynamic parameters and this specific complication. Future studies should investigate these potential differences to improve LSA patency prediction and validate the prognostic value of intraoperative hemodynamic parameters for neurological complications.

The clinical application of iFlow lies in its potential to guide real-time intraoperative decision-making regarding LSA management. During TEVAR, if post-deployment angiography reveals elevated values (diff.dTTP.P >0.675 s, diff.dTTP.M >1.000 s, or diff.dTTP.D >0.730 s), indicating hemodynamic compromise, surgeons should consider interventions such as optimizing the fenestration position, adjusting the bridging stent, or enlarging the fenestration. In cases where LSA revascularization was not initially planned, the presence of significant hemodynamic impairment should prompt consideration of bailout revascularization using the chimney or ISF technique. Conversely, diff.dTTP values within acceptable ranges suggest adequate collateral circulation, potentially reducing the need for immediate intervention. This quantitative criterion provides a hemodynamic supplement to traditional anatomical assessments and may help reduce the risk of mid-term LSA occlusion.

Our study has several limitations. First, the relatively small number of LSA occlusion events limits the statistical power of our multivariable analyses and may affect the precision of HR estimates. The subgroup analyses should be interpreted with particular caution, given the small event numbers in stratified subsets. Larger multicenter studies with longer follow-up periods are needed to validate our findings. Second, we only included patients who underwent LSA revascularization via fenestration, which does not alter the blood flow path. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings to patients undergoing other LSA preservation techniques (e.g., chimney or surgical bypass) remains to be determined. Third, our cohort comprised a heterogeneous group of aortic pathologies. The hemodynamic differences across various aortic pathologies warrant further investigation to validate their impact on the predictive value of intraoperative parameters. Fourth, the efficacy of iFlow’s hemodynamic assessment depends on systemic blood pressure during DSA acquisition. Standardizing intraoperative blood pressure control is essential to minimize potential errors in iFlow measurements. Finally, while iFlow is a valuable tool for blood flow assessment, its sensitivity and specificity compared to duplex ultrasound for evaluating LSA blood flow remain unclear.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study establishes iFlow as an invaluable real-time quantitative tool for assessing hemodynamic changes during TEVAR, particularly in relation to LSA management. The use of diff.dTTP for intraoperative blood flow evaluation provides predictive insights into midterm LSA patency, offering a promising approach for enhancing patient outcomes.
