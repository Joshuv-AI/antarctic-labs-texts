Deep learning CAT bond valuation
Julian Sester 1 , Huansang Xu 1

Abstract
. In this paper, we propose an alternative valuation approach for CAT bonds where a pricing formula is learned by deep neural networks. Once trained, these networks can be used to price CAT bonds as a function of inputs that reflect both the current market conditions and the specific features of the contract. This approach offers two main advantages. First, due to the expressive power of neural networks, the trained model enables fast and accurate evaluation of CAT bond prices. Second, because of its fast execution the trained neural network can be easily analyzed to study its sensitivities w.r.t. changes of the underlying market conditions offering valuable insights for risk management. Keywords: CAT-bonds, deep learning, importance sampling, sensitivities

Introduction

Hurricane Andrew struck the Gulf Coast of the United States in August 1992, resulting in insurance-related damages of $15.5B and the failure of at least 8 insurance companies. Several insurance companies reassessed their risk exposure to the coastal regions across the US, see also
[
1
]
, and subsequently, risk exposure to catastrophic events in American coastal regions substantially decreased, while demand for catastrophe insurance increased. This shortage paved the way for the creation of
catastrophe (CAT) bonds
in the mid 1990s.
In 1995, the American insurance industry had an estimated capital holding of $240B and the possibility of single-event losses of over $100B, see
[
11
]
. Insurance firms consequently started issuing CAT bonds to transfer risk off its balance sheet to the capital markets where institutional investors sought high yields uncorrelated with equity and fixed income returns, provided they indemnified the issuer if a catastrophe-related or trigger event occurred. Providing evidence for the resultant success of CAT bonds, according to
[
7
]
, the total outstanding market has grown from $1B to $49.1B in June 2024.

When issuing a CAT bond, a trigger event and corresponding payout mechanism are defined in the bond contract. The catastrophe is defined using peril (catastrophe type) and territory (catastrophe location). The trigger event type is usually either indemnity (loss of the sponsor exceeds a predetermined threshold), industry index (the aggregate estimated industry loss, measured by an independent third-party index, exceeds a predetermined threshold) or parametric (measurable characteristic of the defined catastrophe exceeds a predetermined threshold), compare also Table
1
for an overview of the market shares of the different loss types.

.

Consequently, a special purpose vehicle (SPV) is established. This is a legally separate entity that issues the CAT bond to investors. The SPV isolates the bond proceeds from the sponsor’s credit risk and satisfies regulatory requirements. Investors (e.g., hedge funds, pension funds) purchase the bond and pay the principal to the SPV. This capital is held in a collateral account, typically invested in low-risk securities. The sponsor then pays periodic coupon payments (usually above-market rates) to the investors, which are funded by the sponsor and the collateral returns. If no trigger event occurs during the bond’s term, the investors receive full repayment of their principal at maturity. If in contrast a trigger event occurs, some or all of the principal is transferred to the sponsor to cover the specified catastrophic losses, resulting in a loss for the investors.

The pricing of CAT bonds has been a subject of increasing interest in both financial and actuarial literature, particularly due to the complexities introduced by low-probability, high-impact trigger events and the structural features of the bonds themselves. The literature on CAT bond pricing frequently employs stochastic modeling frameworks.
Notably, Baryshnikov et al. introduced in
[
40
]
a framework, in analogy to the pricing of defaultable bonds (compare, e.g.,
[
4
]
), where a compound Poisson process is employed to capture the the cumulated random losses. While their model effectively incorporates the stochastic nature of catastrophic events, it does not yield a closed-form pricing formula. Nevertheless, the study provides a thorough numerical analysis based on simulation techniques.
A simplified valuation framework is presented in Jarrow (
[
24
]
), where a closed-form solution for CAT bond pricing is derived. The model relies on the initial LIBOR zero-coupon bond price curve along with two key inputs: the hazard rate of the catastrophic event and the expected loss severity.

A foundational mathematical treatment was presented by Burnecki and Kukla (
[
10
]
), who subsequently refine the methodology from
[
40
]
, addressing key limitations in the original formulation.
This work is particularly influential in bridging actuarial modeling with applied probability theory, offering a robust and tractable approach for CAT bond pricing. Their enhanced framework is further applied by Härdle and López (
[
20
]
) to examine the calibration of CAT bonds for Mexican earthquakes, demonstrating its practical applicability in real-world catastrophe-linked securities.
Further mathematical refinement was proposed by Nowak and Romaniuk (
[
32
]
), who incorporated interest rate risk and dependence structures into CAT bond valuation. Their model provide a generalized catastrophe bond pricing formula, assuming the one-factor stochastic diffusion form of the risk-free interest rate process, where the interest rate behavior is described by the multi-factor Cox–Ingersoll–Ross (CIR) model. Additional contributions such as Ma and Ma (
[
29
]
) addressed the impact of multiple risk drivers - loss severity distributions, claim arrival intensities, trigger thresholds, and stochastic interest rates - on CAT bond valuation. Recent research has focused on enhancing CAT bond pricing models by incorporating more realistic features of catastrophe risk and financial market dynamics. Hofer et al. (
[
21
]
) developed a risk-neutral pricing framework for CAT bonds, extending the basic structure of compound Poisson models to account for time-varying intensities. Burnecki (
[
9
]
) formalizes the structure of contingent convertible catastrophe bonds (CocoCats), modeling catastrophe losses with a time-inhomogeneous compound Poisson process and interest rates using the Longstaff model.

Several numerical methods to pricing CAT bonds have been developed over the years with Monte Carlo methods and numerical PDE solvers remaining the main tools, especially when analytical tractability is lost due to model complexity.
For example, Reshetar (
[
36
]
) models payoff structures contingent on both catastrophic property losses and catastrophic mortality events, using simulation-based techniques.
Moreover, Siyamah et al. (
[
37
]
) utilized Monte Carlo and quasi-Monte Carlo simulation techniques for CAT bond valuation, highlighting efficiency gains in estimating expected losses under stochastic triggers.

Our contribution to the literature on the pricing of CAT bonds is to establish a novel deep learning-based valuation approach where the relationship between model and contract features and the bond price is learned by a deep feedforward neural network under a compound Poisson framework, akin to the model introduced in
[
40
]
. The input features include both model-specific parameters, such as the intensity and frequency of catastrophic events, and contract specifications, including maturity, trigger threshold, and coupon frequency.

In our approach, CAT bond prices are initially computed using Monte Carlo simulation enhanced with importance sampling to reduce variance and computational efficiency. These simulated prices are then used to train neural networks to approximate the pricing function. Through an extensive empirical study, we demonstrate that our neural network-based pricing method achieves high accuracy and significantly outperforms state-of-the-art techniques, such as the partial integro-differential equation (PIDE) approach from
[
40
]
, particularly in terms of computational efficiency. We back the empirical findings with theoretical results showing that neural networks are universal approximators of the pricing relationship, and also that our importance sampling implementation indeed leads to a reduced variance estimate and thus allowing for more accurate neural network approximations through less noise in the approximated prices.

The remainder of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
we introduce the pricing framework using doubly stochastic Poisson processes.
Section
3
discusses Monte-Carlo simulations and importance sampling to compute CAT bond prices, and Section
4
introduces our approach to pricing CAT bonds with deep neural networks.

2.
Pricing of CAT bonds

In this section, we present an approach to the pricing of CAT bonds. Before elaborating the details of CAT bond pricing in Section
2.2
, we detail the modeling of interest rates through affine models in Section
2.1
which turn out to be crucial for the pricing of CAT bonds.

2.1.
Modeling interest rates

Consider a probability space
(\Omega,\mathbb{G},\mathbb{Q})
, where
\mathbb{Q}
is interpreted as a risk-neutral probability measure, or spot martingale measure, and let
\mathbb{F}=\{\mathcal{F}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
be a sub-filtration of
\mathbb{G}
, describing the evolution of available market information over time. In line with
[
15
,
17
,
16
,
26
,
34
]
, we use a general affine term structure model to model the spot interest rate, i.e., we assume the annual interest rate
r_{t}
has dynamics

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}r_{t}

\displaystyle=(\alpha(t)r_{t}+\beta(t))\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\gamma(t)r_{t}+\delta(t)}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

for
\{W_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
denoting a standard Brownian motion w.r.t.
\mathbb{F}
,
meaning that the drift
\alpha(t)r_{t}+\beta(t)
and variance rate
\gamma(t)r_{t}+\delta(t)
are affine in the spot rate
r_{t}
at all times – explaining the name of this model class.

A non-defaultable zero coupon bond promises the payment of the face value
F
at maturity
T
. Assuming no default risk, by risk-neutral valuation (see, e.g.,
[
8
]
), the price of the zero coupon bond at time
t\leq T
is given by

(2.2)

\displaystyle P_{\rm Z}(F,t,T):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[Fe^{-\int_{t}^{T}r_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s}\Big|\mathcal{F}(t)\right]=Fe^{A(t,T)-B(t,T)r_{t}},

where the last equality follows from our assumption of an affine term structure, see also, e.g.,
[
26
]
.
A(t,T)
and
B(t,T)
are deterministic functions of the model parameters which satisfy the ordinary system of equations:

(2.3)

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\mathrm{d}B(t,T)}{\mathrm{d}t}+\alpha(t)B(t,T)-\frac{1}{2}\gamma(t)B^{2}(t,T)

\displaystyle=0,\qquad B(T,T)=0

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}A(t,T)}{\mathrm{d}t}-\beta(t)B(t,T)+\frac{1}{2}\delta(t)B^{2}(t,T)

\displaystyle=0,\qquad A(T,T)=0

see, e.g.,
[
5
,
26
]
.
In order to ensure the existence of continuous solutions
A
and
B
we impose the following assumption.

Assumption 2.1
.

The coefficient functions
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma
,
\delta
from (
2.1
) are assumed to be continuous.

Lemma 2.2
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, there exist functions
A,B\in C(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2})
that solve the Riccati equations (
2.3
). In particular, the solutions
A(t,T)
and
B(t,T)
are jointly continuous in
(t,T)
.

The above described general types of affine term structure models include, in particular, the widely used Vasicek (
[
39
]
) and Cox–Ingersoll–Ross (CIR) (
[
14
]
) models which describe short-rate dynamics using a single stochastic factor driving the randomness..

2.2.
Pricing of CAT bonds

Given the rare and unpredictable nature of catastrophic events, Poisson processes provide a natural mathematical framework for CAT bond pricing. In this approach, the jumps of the Poisson process correspond to catastrophe occurrences, with the jump frequency parameter capturing the rate at which catastrophes occur. This modeling choice has become the standard approach in the literature, as demonstrated in the comprehensive review by
[
38
]
, which examines various CAT bond pricing methodologies.

To describe these catastrophes mathematically, we model the number of catastrophes by a
\mathbb{F}
-conditional Poisson process

\{M(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
, defined on
(\Omega,\mathbb{G},\mathbb{Q})
, associated with positive intensity
\{\lambda(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
being a
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable process with locally integrable sample path.

This allows to define a
hazard process

\{\Lambda(t):\Lambda(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(s)\,{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}s\}_{t\geq 0}
, which is
\mathbb{F}
-adapted, right-continuous and increasing. For any
t>0
and every
n=0,1,\cdots
, we then have

\mathbb{Q}(M(t)=n|\mathcal{F}(t))=\frac{(\Lambda(t))^{n}}{n!}e^{-\Lambda(t)}.

Next, we model the indemnity loss trigger. The
i
th occurrence of a loss is given by a value drawn from the random variable
X_{i}
. For tractability, we assume that the losses {
X_{i}\}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with cumulative distribution function (cdf)
F_{X}
and independent of
\{M(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
. Then, the aggregate indemnity loss is a compound Poisson process
\{L(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
such that

L(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{M(t)}{X_{i}}.

A trigger event occurs exactly when
L(t)
exceeds some pre-determined threshold
D>0
, i.e., we are considering the stopping time
\tau=\inf\{t:L(t)\geq D\}
indicating a loss event. We define
\{N(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
by
N(t):=I(L(t)\geq D)=I(\tau\leq t)
, where
I
denotes the indicator function
I(\tau\leq t):=1
if
\tau\leq t
, and
0
otherwise, and we observe that
N
is itself a Poisson process, see also
[
40
]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

The process
\{N(t)\}_{t\geq 0}=\{I(L(t)\geq D)\}_{t\geq 0}
is a doubly stochastic Poisson process with intensity
\Bigg\{\lambda(t)\Bigl(1-F_{X}\bigr(D-L(t)\bigr)\Bigr)I\bigl(L(t)<D\bigr)\Biggr\}_{t\geq 0}
.

This result leads directly to the following pricing formulas.

Corollary 2.4
.

Consider a CAT-bond at time
t\leq T
with face value
F
, maturity
T
, and loss threshold
D
.

(i)

The (risk-neutral) price of a zero-coupon CAT bond that pays
F
at maturity only if no trigger event occurs by time
T
is given by

\displaystyle C_{\rm Z}(F,t,T)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[N(T)\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}(t)\right]\right)\cdot P_{\rm Z}(F,t,T)

(2.4)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[1-\int_{t}^{T}\lambda(s)\left(1-F_{X}(D-L(s))\right)I(L(s)<D)\,\mathrm{d}s\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}(t)\right]\cdot P_{\rm Z}(F,t,T).

(ii)

The (risk-neutral) price of a CAT bond with face value
F
, maturity
T
, and coupon payments
\{C_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
at times
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
where
0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}\leq T
, is given by

(2.5)

C_{\rm B}(F,\{C_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},t,T)=C_{\rm Z}(F,t,T)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(t\leq t_{i})\cdot C_{\rm Z}(C_{i},t,t_{i}).

(iii)

If, in the setting of (ii), the CAT bond has a recovery rate
R\in[0,1]
, then its (risk-neutral) price is

(2.6)

\displaystyle C_{\rm RB}(F,\{C_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},t,T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(t\leq t_{i})\cdot C_{\rm Z}(C_{i},t,t_{i})+(1-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[R])\cdot C_{\rm Z}(F,t,T)+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[R]\cdot P_{\rm Z}(F,t,T).

3.
Pricing CAT bonds via Monte Carlo Simulation

This section introduces Monte Carlo methods for pricing CAT bonds. These simulation techniques form the foundation for our deep learning-based valuation framework, which we develop in Section
4
.

3.1.
Monte Carlo integration

According to (
2.4
), prices of CAT bonds can be understood as a combination of zero coupon bond price
P_{\rm Z}(F,t,T)
and the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[N(T)|\mathcal{F}(t)\right]
introducing path dependency, and thus imposing an additional numerical challenge. Due to Lemma
2.2
, an explicit solution for the zero coupon bond price according to Eq. (
2.2
) is readily available, however, in general there is no closed-form analytical solution for
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[N(T)\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}(t)\right]
, so thus
C_{\rm Z}(F,t,T)
. Instead, a numerical approximation, e.g., via Monte Carlo integration is required to evaluate this expectation.
Alternatively, leveraging the Feynman–Kac theorem (see, e.g.,
[
12
]
), integro-PDE methods could be applied (see, e.g.,
[
40
]
), which however turn out to be rather inefficient in our settings as we will discuss in Section
4.3
.

To determine CAT bond prices at time
0
, due to (
2.4
), we need to compute the risk-neutral probability of a trigger event that corresponds to
\theta:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[N(t)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[I(L(t)\geq D)\right]=\mathbb{Q}(L(t)>D)
.

We approximate this probability using Monte Carlo simulation. For a fixed number of simulations
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we generate independent loss realizations
L_{1}(t),\dots,L_{n}(t)
and compute the estimator

\hat{\theta}_{n}^{\rm{MC}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(L_{i}(t)\geq D),

for
L_{i}(t)
denoting the loss in the
i
th simulation.
The variance of the estimated trigger probability is given as

\displaystyle\rm{Var}(\hat{\theta}_{n}^{\rm{MC}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[I(L(t)\geq D)^{2}\big]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[I(L(t)\geq D)\big]^{2}\Big)=\frac{1}{n}(\theta-\theta^{2}).

Algorithm
1
in the appendix outlines the procedure to compute this estimate.

3.2.
Importance sampling

By the law of large numbers,
\hat{\theta}^{\rm{MC}}
converges to
\theta
as the sample size increases. Although the MC estimator converges to
\theta
, plain Monte-Carlo estimation is characterized by slow convergence and a large variance, since for large thresholds
D
, very few samples contribute to the estimate, see also, e.g.,
[
33
]
.
This motivates to apply
Importance Sampling
(IS), a variance reduction technique allowing to sample under a different probability measure making exceedance of the threshold
D
a more frequently occurring event. To this end, we consider a second probability density
g
, whose support is assumed to contain that of original density
f
, and define the
likelihood ratio

R(l)
by
R(l):=f(l)/g(l)
whenever
g(l)>0
, and
R(l)=0
otherwise. Hence, we get
\int I(\ell\geq D)f(\ell){\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\ell=\int I(\ell\geq D)g(\ell)R(\ell){\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}\ell
.

The idea of using IS is to choose the density
g
such that Monte-Carlo simulation under
g
leads to an IS estimator
\hat{\theta}^{\rm{IS}}
with variance being smaller than for the standard Monte Carlo integration.
Then, we estimate
\hat{\theta}_{n}^{\rm{IS}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(L_{i}(t)\geq D)R\left(L_{i}(t)\right)
, where
L_{i}(t)
is simulated according to density
g
,
I(L_{i}(t)\geq D)
is the indicator function associated to the trigger event and
R\left(L_{i}(t)\right)
denotes the likelihood ratio used for the change of measure in the
i
th simulation.
We compute that the variance for the estimated trigger probability equals

\displaystyle\rm{Var}(\hat{\theta}^{\rm{IS}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{g}\big[I(L(t)\geq D)^{2}R\left(L(t)\right)^{2}\big]-\mathbb{E}_{g}\big[I(L(t)\geq D)R\left(L(t)\right)\big]^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{g}\big[I(L(t)\geq D)^{2}R\left(L(t)\right)^{2}\big]-\theta^{2}\Big)=\frac{1}{n}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\big[R\left(L(t)\right)I(L(t)\geq D)\big]-\theta^{2}\Big).

To determine a candidate change of measure for IS in the compound Poisson setting, we follow the procedure outlined in
[
33
, Chapter 7]
,
[
18
, Chapter 11.4 ]
,
[
6
]
, or
[
28
, Section 3]
, by applying
exponential tilting
.
For the aggregated loss
L(t)
, two types of random variables need to be simulated - the number of losses and the size of losses. Firstly, consider the number of losses
M(t)
till time
t
, modelled by a Poisson process with intensity
\{\lambda(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
.
The moment generating function (MGF) of
M(t)
is calculated to be

M_{M}(a)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[e^{aM(t)}\right]=e^{(\Lambda(t))(e^{a}-1)},\quad a\in\mathbb{R},

where
\Lambda(t)
is defined in Section
2.2
.
Applying exponential tilting that changes the probability measure from
\mathbb{Q}
to
\mathbb{Q}_{a}
for
a\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}_{a}(M(t)=n|\mathcal{F}_{t})

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[e^{aM(t)}/M_{M}(a)I({M(t)=n)}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{an}}{e^{\Lambda(t)(e^{a}-1)}}\cdot\frac{(\Lambda(t))^{n}}{n!}e^{-\Lambda(t)}=\frac{(\Lambda(t)e^{a})^{n}}{n!}e^{-\Lambda(t)e^{a}}.

The number of losses
M(t)
under probability measure
\mathbb{Q}_{a}
is thus modelled by a Poisson process with new intensity
\{\lambda(t)e^{a}\}_{t\geq 0}
after having applied exponential tilting.

Next, we address the jump size component. Catastrophic losses typically exhibit heavy-tailed behavior, necessitating appropriate distributional choices. The actuarial literature has identified several continuous distributions suitable for modeling such extreme losses. Among these, the Gamma and Lognormal distributions have emerged as particularly popular choices, though other options include the Pareto and Burr distributions, compare, also
[
10
]
. In the following subsections, we develop specific IS techniques for both the Gamma and Lognormal distributions.

3.2.1.
Importance sampling for Gamma distributed losses

We first consider Gamma distributed losses. Assume the size of losses
\{X_{i}\}_{i}
are i.i.d.  according to a Gamma distribution with shape
k
and scale
\beta
, i.e., its probability density function is given as

f_{X}(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(k)\beta^{k}}x^{k-1}e^{-x/\beta},\quad x>0,k>0,\beta>0,\qquad\text{for }\Gamma(k):=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{k-1}e^{-t}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}t.

Applying exponential tilting, we have that the new density function for the size of losses under the new probability measure
\mathbb{Q}_{a,b}
is given by

\displaystyle f_{a,b}(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\exp(bx)f_{X}(x)}{M_{X}(b)}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(k)}(\frac{1}{\beta}-b)^{k}x^{k-1}e^{-x(\frac{1}{\beta}-b)},

for
b<\frac{1}{\beta}
, see, e.g.,
[
2
]
. This means the jump size satisfies a Gamma distribution with shape
k
and new scale
\frac{\beta}{1-\beta b}
under the probability measure
\mathbb{Q}_{a,b}
.

Recall, that the initial mean value of the losses is usually much smaller than the predetermined threshold
D>0
, i.e., we have
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[L]\ll D
, leading to a high variance of standard Monte Carlo estimates. For IS, we select parameters
a
and
b
such that the expected value of the loss under the modified measure
\mathbb{Q}_{a,b}
approximates the threshold
D
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{a,b}}[L]\approx D
. By this choice the event
\{L(t)>D\}
will occur much more frequently which in turn helps to reduce the variance of the estimators. To this end, note that the expected loss under
\mathbb{Q}_{a,b}
computes as

(3.1)

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{a,b}}[L(t)]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{a,b}}[M(t)]\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}_{a,b}}[X_{1}]=(\lambda te^{a})\cdot k\frac{\beta}{1-\beta b},

where the first equality follows from Wald’s identity.

From Eq. (
3.1
), it is evident that the choice of the importance sampling parameters
(a,b)
plays a critical role in reducing the variance of the estimated trigger probability, which is the primary objective of importance sampling. While one may rely on numerical experimentation to evaluate various combinations of
(a,b)
and select the most effective pair for a given loss distribution, this approach lacks generality and may not guarantee robustness across different scenarios. Therefore, it is important to investigate the regularity and consistency of this parameter selection procedure. Below, we present a result that guides the appropriate selection of
(a,b)
across a broad class of loss distributions allowing for a more systematic and reliable guidance than case-by-case numerical tuning.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let an aggregate indemnity loss
\{L(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
be defined by
L(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{M(t)}X_{i}
, where
M(t)\sim\rm{Poisson}(\lambda t)
and
\{X_{i}\}_{i}
are i.i.d. with
X_{1}{\sim}\rm{Gamma}(1,\beta)
for
\beta\geq 0
. Further, let

a=\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\frac{D}{\lambda t\beta}\right),\qquad b=\frac{1}{\beta}-\frac{\lambda te^{a}}{D}.

Then, the importance sampling estimator

\hat{\theta}_{n}^{\rm{IS}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(L_{i}(t)\geq D)R\left(L_{i}(t)\right)

with

R(L(t))=\exp\left(\lambda t(e^{a}-1)-aM(t)\right)(1-\beta b)^{-M(t)}\exp{\big(-bL(t)\big)}

satisfies

\rm{Var}(\hat{\theta}^{\rm{IS}})\leq\rm{Var}(\hat{\theta}^{\rm{MC}}),\qquad\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}^{\rm{IS}}]=\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}^{\rm{MC}}].

IS is most beneficial when estimating probabilities of rare events, particularly when the expected loss under the original measure is significantly smaller than the threshold, i.e., when
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[L(t)]\ll D
. When the maturity
T
is large, the aggregate indemnity loss
L(T)
increases, leading to a higher trigger probability, making it not necessarily a rare event anymore. In such scenarios, one can use standard Monte Carlo simulation with a reasonable small variance instead.

In the appendix, we provide Algorithm
2
to estimate the trigger probability with Gamma distributed losses by using Monte Carlo simulation with IS.

3.2.2.
Importance sampling for Lognormally distributed losses

Now we consider another frequently used type of loss distribution - the Lognormal distribution. Assume the size of losses
\{X_{i}\}_{i}
are i.i.d. and satisfy a Lognormal distribution with parameters
\mu
(mean of
\ln(X_{i})
) and
\sigma
(standard deviation of
\ln(X_{i})
). The probability density function of the random variable
X_{i}
is

f_{X}(x)=\frac{1}{x\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(\ln(x)-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big)\quad x>0,\sigma>0,\mu\in\mathbb{R}.

The moment generating function of a Lognormal distribution is infinite for positive arguments. Thus, we cannot apply exponential tilting for Lognormal distributions directly. Instead, we follow the idea from
[
3
]
, where the importance sampling density is built as another Lognormal distribution with a larger expected value, denote as
X\sim\text{Logn}(\mu+b,\sigma^{2})
under the probability measure
\mathbb{Q}_{a,b}
, with

4.2.
CAT bond pricing with neural networks

By applying Monte Carlo simulation with importance sampling, we estimate trigger probabilities under different loss distribution assumptions. CAT bond prices then follow from Corollary
2.4

2.4

2.5

Therefore, we develop a NN based approach to compute the CAT bond price, with both zero coupon and coupon settings.
Motivated by Proposition
4.2

We have two independent simulated CAT bond price datasets, considering Gamma and Lognormally distributed losses separately. In these simulated datasets, we assume that the annual interest rate

[

w.r.t.
\mathbb{F}

is the long-term mean level of interest rates, and

Then the price of the non-defaultable zero coupon bond in Vasicek’s model at time

\displaystyle A(t,T)

\displaystyle=\Big(\alpha-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2\alpha^{2}}\Big)(B(t,T)-(T-t))-\frac{\sigma^{2}B(t,T)^{2}}{4\alpha},

5.
Conclusion

In this work, we proposed a novel framework for the valuation of CAT bonds using deep learning techniques. Building on a doubly stochastic Poisson process framework for modeling catastrophe events, in Section
3.2
we first developed efficient Monte Carlo simulation methods enhanced by importance sampling to estimate CAT bond prices under various loss distributions. These simulations provide high-quality training data for fully connected feedforward neural networks that approximate the CAT bond pricing function, see Section
4.2
.

Our empirical results from Section
4.3
demonstrate that neural networks trained on simulated data can predict CAT bond prices with high accuracy and significantly improved computational efficiency compared to traditional pricing techniques, including Monte Carlo even in combination with importance sampling and PIDE solvers. In particular, the neural network models allow for the almost instant computation of prices several orders of magnitude faster than simulation-based approaches while maintaining accuracy. Furthermore, with Proposition
3.1
we provided theoretical justification for the use of neural networks based on the universal approximation theorem and analyzed their sensitivity to key model inputs, offering practical insights into risk assessment and model interpretability.

Overall, our approach offers a scalable and accurate alternative to conventional CAT bond pricing methods, making it especially valuable in real-time risk management and portfolio optimization contexts.

(\lambda,t_{i},C_{i},D)
. We can conclude that
C_{\rm B}(F,\{C_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},0,T)
is continuous in
(r_{0},\lambda,\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},T,\{C_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},F,D)
and the universal approximation theorem from Proposition
4.1
can be applied.
∎
