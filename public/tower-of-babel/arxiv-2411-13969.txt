Continuum of coupled Wasserstein gradient flows
Clément Cancès, Daniel Matthes, Ismael Medina, Bernhard Schmitzer

Abstract
. We study a system of drift-diffusion PDEs for a potentially infinite number of incompressible phases, subject to a joint pointwise volume constraint. Our analysis is based on the interpretation as a collection of coupled Wasserstein gradient flows or, equivalently, as a gradient flow in the space of couplings under a ‘fibered’ Wasserstein distance. We prove existence of weak solutions, long-time asymptotics, and stability with respect to the mass distribution of the phases, including the discrete to continuous limit. A key step is to establish convergence of the product of pressure gradient and density, jointly over the infinite number of phases. The underlying energy functional is the objective of entropy regularized optimal transport, which allows us to interpret the model as the relaxation of the classical Angenent–Haker–Tannenbaum (AHT) scheme to the entropic setting. However, in contrast to the AHT scheme’s lack of convergence guarantees, the relaxed scheme is unconditionally convergent. We conclude with numerical illustrations of the main results.

Introduction

1.1.
Problem setup

Let
X\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be the closure of a bounded and connected open set with smooth boundary, and let
Y\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d^{\prime}}
be compact.
Let
\mathcal{L}
denote the Lebesgue measure on
X
,
\mu
be a
\mathcal{L}
-absolutely continuous probability measure on
X
and
\nu
an arbitrary probability measure on
Y
.

We will denote by
\rho
a probability measure on
X\times Y
, absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathcal{L}\otimes\nu
and with density
r
in
L^{1}(X\times Y,\mathcal{L}\otimes\nu)
. Furthermore, let
V\in C^{2}(X\times Y)
be a non-negative function and
\kappa>0
be a positive parameter.

Motivated on the one hand by multiphase porous media flows —see for instance
[
4
]
for a presentation of the physical models,
[
18
]
for some elements of mathematical analysis in the two-phase context, and for
[
31
,
10
,
11
]
for a variational reinterpretation of the multiphase flow problem (see also
[
30
,
12
,
14
]
)—, and on the other hand by the classical Angenent-Haker-Tannenbaum (AHT) scheme for
L^{2}
optimal transport
[
2
]
— see below for a detailed explanation — we study the following stratified gradient flow:

(1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}r(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=\kappa\Delta_{X}r(t,x,y)+\nabla_{X}\cdot\big(r(t,x,y)\,\nabla_{X}[V(x,y)-\Pi(x)]\big),

(2)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle={\mathbf{n}}\cdot\big(\kappa\nabla_{X}r(t,x,y)+r(t,x,y)\,\nabla_{X}[V(x,y)-\Pi(t,x)]\big)\quad\text{for $x\in\partial X$},

(3)

\displaystyle\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{Y}r(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y),

where
\nabla_{X}
and
\nabla_{X}\cdot
denote the gradient and the divergence with respect to the variable
x\in X
only.

The pressure
\Pi
in (
1
) is implicitly given via the
first marginal
constraint (
3
),
where by slight abuse the notation we use the symbol
\mu(x)
for the (Lebesgue) density of the Lebesgue-absolutely continuous measure
\mu
.
Note that the mass at level
y\in Y
, that we denote
second marginal
, is constant in time thanks to the divergence structure of (
1
) and the no-flux boundary condition (
2
).
Assuming the initial condition has second marginal
\nu
, it will be subsequently preserved over time.

Formally, (
1
)–(
3
) is a
y
-wise Wasserstein gradient flow for the potential energy

(4)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(\rho)=\int_{X\times Y}V(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho(x,y)+\kappa{\mathcal{H}}(\rho)+\iota_{{\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}}(\rho),

where
{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the weighted negative entropy

(5)

{\mathcal{H}}(\rho\mid\nu):=\int_{X\times Y}(r\log r-r+1)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)

(we will write
{\mathcal{H}}(\rho)
whenever
\nu
is clear from the context)
and
\iota_{\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}
is the indicator function of the feasible couplings (or transport plans) between
\mu
and
\nu
, see (
16
) below for a rigorous definition.

1.2.
First motivation: multiphasic flows

(
1
)–(
3
) arises naturally as a generalization of multiphase models such as those studied in
[
11
,
13
]
to the case of a (possibly) infinite number of species.
Indeed, Equations (
1
)–(
3
) boil down to such models when the space
Y
is finite, or equivalently if the measure
\nu
is made of a finite number of Dirac masses:

(6)

\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}\delta_{y_{i}},\qquad\text{with }(y_{i})_{i=1}^{k}\subset Y\text{ and }\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}=1.

The measure
\mu
then corresponds to the porosity of the material, i.e. the local ratio of void space available for the fluid in the solid porous structure, within which the multiphase and incompressible fluid is flowing.
The function
V(\cdot,y_{i})
then denotes the intrinsic potential energy of the
i
-th species.
Condition (
3
) is then interpreted as the constraint that the whole porous space is occupied by the fluid mixture.

In our setting,
\kappa
is a given positive constant that encodes the intensity of the term
\kappa{\mathcal{H}}(\rho)
, which could be interpreted as a capillary energy term. The limiting case of vanishing capillarity
\kappa=0
is known to be highly complex. Even in the seemingly simple two-phase case, the well-posedness of the problem remains widely open. In the one-dimensional case
d=1
, the problem reduces to some Burgers’ type equation
[
31
]
. It is now well understood that in this particularly simple setting, the JKO scheme selects the unique entropy solution in Kruzkov’s sense
[
31
,
26
]
. In larger dimension, tools from convex integration can even be used to construct multiple weak solutions
[
20
,
36
,
24
]
. Moreover when the space dimension
d
is greater or equal to two, there is no available criterion to charaterize the solution captured by the JKO scheme, and up to our knowledge there is also no general existence theorem unless the external potential is identically equal to zero
[
38
]
.

Let us expand on the physical interpretation of (
1
)-(
3
).
Formally, (
1
) can be written as:

(7)

\partial_{t}r_{t}+\nabla_{X}\cdot(r_{t}v_{t})=0,\qquad\text{with}\quad v_{t}(x,y):=\nabla_{X}[\Pi_{t}(x)-V(x,y)-\kappa\log r_{t}(x,y)].

Integrating (
7
) with respect to the
Y
-marginal
\nu
yields:

(8)

0=\partial_{t}\mu=\partial_{t}\left(\int_{Y}r_{t}(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)\right)=-\nabla_{X}\cdot\left(\int_{Y}r_{t}(x,y)v_{t}(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)\right).

Equation (
8
) shows that the
X
-projection of the momentum field
\rho_{t}v_{t}
results in an
X
-divergence-free velocity field, therefore preserving the pointwise
X
-marginal constraint.
In fact, the following decomposition (closely related to the Helmholtz decomposition) holds:

(9)

-\nabla_{X}[V(x,y)+\kappa\log r_{t}(x,y)]=-\nabla\Pi_{t}(x)+v_{t}(x,y).

The left-hand side corresponds to the steepest descend direction for (
4
) once one removes the marginal constraint.
Thus, the velocity field
v_{t}(x,y)
encodes the divergence-free part of the unconstrained velocity field, with
\nabla\Pi_{t}(x)
claiming the gradient component.
Whenever the left-hand side can be written as a sheer gradient of a function on
X
, the velocity field
v_{t}
becomes identically zero, resulting in a stationary point of (
1
)-(
3
).
Intuitively,
v_{t}
will progressively cancel the curl component of the left-hand side until such a stationary point is attained.
This will become clearer when we explore the connections to the AHT scheme
[
2
]
in Section
1.3
.

For future reference, let us conclude by stating the elliptic equation satisfied by
\Pi_{t}
, which is a direct consequence of (
8
) plus the marginal constraints:

(10)

\nabla\cdot[\mu(x)\nabla\Pi_{t}(x)]=\kappa\Delta\mu(x)+\nabla\cdot\left[\int_{Y}\nabla_{X}V(x,y)r(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)\right].

1.3.
Second motivation: Angenent-Haker-Tannenbaum scheme

The field of computational optimal transport offers an alternative interpretation for the evolution equations (
1
)–(
3
).
From this perspective, the energy (
4
) is the entropic transport score of the coupling
\rho
between
\mu
and
\nu
for the cost function
V
and regularization strength
\kappa
. This entropic objective is a common approximation to the classical, unregularized optimal transport problem (corresponding to
\kappa=0
), which has gained increased popularity thanks to the availability of efficient solvers, most notably the Sinkhorn algorithm
[
21
,
33
]
and the connection to stochastic optimal control problems
[
17
,
27
,
29
]
.
From this perspective, the evolution equations (
1
)–(
3
) address the solution of the entropic transport problem by evolving the density of the transport plan in the direction of steepest descent of the entropic score
{\mathcal{E}}
, conditioned to keeping the marginals feasible.

A similar philosophy is central to the Angenent-Haker-Tannenbaum (AHT) scheme
[
2
,
35
]
, a classical algorithm for unregularized optimal transport (i.e.
(
4
)
for
\kappa=0
) and
V
strictly convex. In this regime the minimizers of
{\mathcal{E}}
are very sparse —when
\mu
has a density, each
x\in X
sends mass to a single
y\in Y

[
7
,
25
]

— which motivates the parametrization of the current plan
\rho
as a
transport map
.
Hence, the AHT scheme considers a minimizing flow for
{\mathcal{E}}
in the space of transport maps, progressively removing the curl component of an initial transport map
T_{0}
until only the gradient component remains. Formally, for
T_{0}:X\rightarrow Y
verifying
\nu=T_{0\sharp}\mu
, the AHT evolution is driven by:

(11)

T_{t}=T_{0}\circ s_{t}^{-1}\qquad\text{with}\qquad s_{0}=\text{id}_{X},\qquad\partial_{t}s_{t}=v_{t}\circ s_{t},\qquad\nabla\cdot(\mu v_{t})=0

where
v_{t}
is a velocity field whose divergence constraint ensures
(s_{t})_{\sharp}\mu=\mu
, and therefore —if such
s_{t}
exists—
T_{t}
stays a feasible transport map between
\mu
and
\nu
at all times
t\geq 0
.
v_{t}
is chosen among the feasible velocity fields to realize the steepest descent for the transport cost

E(T)=\int_{X}V(x,T(x))\mu(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x,\qquad\partial_{t}E(T_{t})=\int_{X}\langle\nabla_{X}V(x,T_{t}(x)),v_{t}(x)\rangle\mu(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x

with respect to some metric of choice.
In
[
2
]
the velocity field is chosen as
v_{t}(x)=u_{t}(x)/\mu(x)
, where
u_{t}(x)
is the
L^{2}(X)
projection of
x\mapsto-\nabla_{X}V(x,T_{t}(x))
onto the space of divergence-free velocity fields.
If one chooses instead the
L^{2}(X,\mu)
-metric, the steepest descent is given by setting
v_{t}
to the projection of the vector field
x\mapsto-\nabla_{X}V(x,T_{t}(x))
onto the subspace satisfying
\nabla\cdot(\mu v_{t})=0
in
L^{2}(X,\mu)
.
This projection is given by

(12)

v_{t}(x):=\nabla\Pi(x)-\nabla_{X}V(x,T_{t}(x)),\quad\textrm{with }\nabla\cdot[\mu(x)\nabla\Pi(x)]=\nabla\cdot[\mu(x)\nabla_{X}V(x,T_{t}(x))]

Leaving aside the (challenging) problem of existence of solutions to (
11
)-(
12
), the similarities between (
7
) and (
12
) are striking.
Indeed,
\Pi
satisfies both in (
10
) and in (
12
) the same elliptic equation, once we (formally) allow in the former the choice
\kappa=0
and that
\rho_{t}
is given by a Monge plan
\rho_{t}=(\operatorname{id},T_{t}(x))_{\sharp}\mu
.
Moreover, the AHT velocity field
v_{t}(x)=\nabla\Pi(x)-\nabla_{X}V(x,T_{t}(x))
is also analogous to that in (
7
), once we consider that in the AHT only points of the form
(x,T_{t}(x))
carry mass, and therefore the velocity field can be given as a function of
x
instead of
(x,y)
. For these reasons one may additionally interpret (
1
)–(
3
) as a relaxation of the AHT scheme to entropic optimal transport, which inevitably requires the relaxation from transport maps to transport plans.

Leaving aside the well-posedness issues of the AHT scheme — which largely align with those outlined in Section
1.2
for the limit of vanishing
\kappa
in multiphase flows—, the main drawback of the AHT scheme is that it is unable to escape certain suboptimal configurations. Even though, by construction, the score
E(T)
is non-increasing along solutions of (
11
)-(
12
), the stationary points are all the feasible maps
T
that verify that
x\mapsto\nabla_{X}V(x,T(x))
is curl-free. Unfortunately this characterization does not suffice to grant optimality, and may in some cases include not only the best but also the
worst
transport map, as shown by the following example:

\displaystyle X=Y=[-1,1]^{d},\quad\mu=\nu=\tfrac{1}{2^{d}}\mathcal{L},\quad V(x,y)=|x-y|^{2},\quad T(x):=-x,\quad\nabla_{X}V(x,T(x))=4x.

In this case the optimal transport map would correspond instead to
T^{*}:x\mapsto x
, and
T
thus corresponds to the map
maximizing
the total transport cost.
However, since
\nabla_{X}V(x,T(x))
is curl-free, the velocity field
v_{t}
given by (
12
) is identically zero, and the trajectory
T_{t}
with initial condition
T_{0}=T
remains stationary in an utterly suboptimal configuration.

Except for special cases
[
8
,
35
]
, convergence of the AHT scheme to the optimal transport map thus remains an open question.
Up until now, the question of whether a suitable relaxation can make the AHT scheme unconditionally convergent has remained open as well.
We expect that regularization by diffusion for
\kappa>0
helps the algorithm to escape from such configurations.

1.4.
Outline

Existence of weak solutions.

We dedicate Section
2
to recall the relevant optimal transport background.
Under suitable regularity assumptions, in
Section
3
we build an approximating sequence for the gradient flow of the energy
{\mathcal{E}}
by means of a minimizing movement scheme and discuss the relevant a-priori estimates. Section
4
shows convergence of such approximating sequences to a continuous limit trajectory
\rho
with values on

and a pressure

(13)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X\times Y}\partial_{t}\psi\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X\times Y}\big(\nabla_{X}\psi\cdot\nabla_{X}[V-\Pi]-\kappa\Delta_{X}\psi\big)\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho-\int_{X\times Y}\psi(0)\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{0}

for all
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\times X\times Y)
with
\nabla_{X}\psi\cdot\mathbf{n}\equiv 0
on

, and

(14)

\int_{X}\nabla\xi\cdot\nabla\Pi(t)\,\mu\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\int_{X}\nabla\xi\cdot\left[\int_{Y}\nabla_{X}V(x,y)r(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)\right]\,{\mathrm{d}}x-\kappa\int_{X}\mu\Delta\xi\,{\mathrm{d}}x

for all
\xi\in C^{\infty}(X)
with
\nabla\xi\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
on
\partial X
and a.e.
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
, where

is the density of
\rho
w.r.t
\mathcal{L}\otimes\nu
.

Stability of solutions.

Section
5
shows stability of weak solutions under convergence of the marginals
\mu
and
\nu
and of the initial condition
\rho^{0}
.
This relates the continuous
\nu
limit to previous multiphasic flow studies such as
[
30
,
12
,
14
]
, identifying our dynamics as the limit of the multiphasic case when the mass of each phase
\nu
is not a discrete measure supported on a finite set but rather a general probability measure.

Asymptotic convergence to the minimizer of
{\mathcal{E}}
.

In Section
6
we show that weak solutions converge to the unique minimizer of the energy
{\mathcal{E}}
as
t
goes to infinity. The relevance of this result stems from the non-convexity of
{\mathcal{E}}
in the fibered topology of the minimizing movement scheme. We also discuss implications to computational optimal transport.

The regime
\kappa=0
.

In Section
7
we discuss the
\kappa=0
regime, and whether the results of Section
6
may apply to this limit. As explained in Section
1.2
, existence of solutions remains an open problem even in for the two-phase case, since the only source of regularity (the entropic term) in (
1
)–(
3
) is lost.
However, from the computational optimal transport perspective this regime is extremely interesting, as it would correspond to a relaxation of the AHT scheme to the space of transport
plans
, while staying in the unregularized transport regime.

We show that, even though we cannot provide existence of solutions in the general case, the
\kappa=0
regime shares with the AHT scheme the existence of suboptimal stationary configurations.
For instance, for
V(x,y)=|x-y|^{2}
, all initial conditions of the form
\rho^{0}=(\operatorname{id},\nabla\phi)_{\sharp}\mu
with
\phi\in C^{2}(X)
turn out to be stationary.
The intuition is that, for
\tau
sufficiently small,
\rho^{0}
becomes a stationary point of the minimizing movement scheme, and therefore also a stationary constant weak solution.
We conclude that a relaxation to the space of transport plans does not improve the convergence properties of the AHT scheme significantly, and that some kind of regularization is a necessary component for an unconditionally convergent variant of the AHT algorithm.

Numerical simulations.
Finally, Section
8
exemplifies the stability and convergence results with numerical simulations of the minimizing movement scheme.
Experimentally we observe
linear convergence to the energy
{\mathcal{E}}
, which may open the door to novel numerical methods for the solution of entropic optimal transport in geometric domains.

2.
Preliminaries

In the following chapters we will leverage the theory of optimal transportation to build an approximation sequence for the solutions of (
1
)–(
3
).
For a thorough exposition of optimal transport we refer to
[
35
,
39
]
.

For
Z\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
a compact set, denote by
\mathcal{M}(Z)
the set of Radon measures on
Z
, by
\mathcal{M}_{+}(Z)
the set of positive Radon measures on
Z
and by
\mathcal{P}(Z)
the set of probability measures on
Z
. For
Z_{1}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d},Z_{2}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d^{\prime}}
compact sets and
\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(Z_{1}),\rho_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(Z_{2})
two probability measures,
\Gamma(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
denotes the set of
couplings
or
transport plans
between
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
:

(16)

\Gamma(\rho_{1},\rho_{2}):=\{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(Z_{1}\times Z_{2})\ \mid\ \mathrm{P}_{1}\gamma=\rho_{1},\quad\mathrm{P}_{2}\gamma=\rho_{2}\}

where the maps
\mathrm{P}_{1}
and
\mathrm{P}_{2}
denote the projections of measures on
Z_{1}\times Z_{2}
to its marginals, i.e.

\displaystyle(\mathrm{P}_{1}\gamma)(S_{1}):=\gamma(S_{1}\times Z_{2})\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad(\mathrm{P}_{2}\gamma)(S_{2}):=\gamma(Z_{1}\times S_{2})

for
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(Z_{1}\times Z_{2})
,
S_{1}\subset Z_{1}
,
S_{2}\subset Z_{2}
measurable.

The optimal transport problem between
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
with a continuous cost function
c:Z_{1}\times Z_{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
is then given by

(17)

\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})}\int_{Z_{1}\times Z_{2}}c(z_{1},z_{2})\,{\mathrm{d}}\gamma(z_{1},z_{2}).

For compactly supported marginals and a lower-semicontinuous cost function
c
, Problem (
17
) admits a minimizer.

When
d=d^{\prime}
and
c(z_{1},z_{2})=|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{p}
with
p\in[1,\infty)
, the minimal score of (
17
) is (the
p
-th power of) the
L^{p}

Wasserstein distance
(denoted by
{\mathbf{W}}_{p}
) between
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
. With no subscript,
{\mathbf{W}}
will be understood to denote the
{\mathbf{W}}_{2}
distance.
When
z_{2}\mapsto c(z_{1},z_{2})
is strictly convex for all
z_{1}\in Z_{1}
(as is the case for the
L^{p}
distance with
p>1
) and
\rho_{1}
is Lebesgue-absolutely continuous, the unique minimizer of (
17
) is of the form
\gamma=(\operatorname{id},T)_{\sharp}\rho_{1}

[
7
,
25
]
, and
T
solves the so-called

:

(18)

\min_{T_{\sharp}\rho_{1}=\rho_{2}}\int_{Z1}c(z,T(z))\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{1}(z).

The optimal transport problem (
17
) admits the dual formulation:

(19)

\sup_{\Phi(z_{1})+\Psi(z_{2})\leq c(z_{1},z_{2})}\int_{Z_{1}}\Phi(z_{1})\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{1}(z_{1})+\int_{Z_{2}}\Psi(z_{2})\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{2}(z_{2})

where the supremum runs over continuous functions from
Z_{1}
(resp.
Z_{2}
) to
{\mathbb{R}}
.
If

is uniformly continuous maximizers for (

When
Z_{1}=Z_{2}=:Z
, the
L^{p}
-Wasserstein distance for

takes a particularly convenient form:

(20)

{\mathbf{W}}_{1}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})=\sup_{\text{Lip}(\Phi)\leq 1}\int_{Z}\Phi(z)\,{\mathrm{d}}(\rho_{1}-\rho_{2})(z),

which is known as the
Kantorovich-Rubinstein formula
. On the other hand, for

it is customary to rewrite the dual relation (

) as:

(21)

\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{W}}(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})^{2}=\sup_{\Phi(z_{1})+\Psi(z_{2})\leq\frac{1}{2}|z_{1}-z_{2}|^{2}}\left(\int_{Z_{1}}\Phi(z_{1})\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{1}(z_{1})+\int_{Z_{2}}\Psi(z_{2})\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{2}(z_{2})\right).

This re-scaling allows to identify the optimal transport map

as
T=\operatorname{id}-\nabla\Phi
(
\rho_{1}
-almost everywhere). Besides,
{\mathbf{W}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{W}}_{2}
satisfy an order relation: for
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
optimal transport plans between
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
for
{\mathbf{W}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{W}}_{2}
respectively, it holds:

(22)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{W}}_{1}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})

\displaystyle=\int_{Z^{2}}|z-z^{\prime}|\,{\mathrm{d}}\gamma_{1}(z,z^{\prime})\leq\int_{Z^{2}}|z-z^{\prime}|\,{\mathrm{d}}\gamma_{2}(z,z^{\prime})

i) For a continuous test function

\displaystyle=\int_{X\times Y}\xi(x)r(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)=\int_{X\times Y}\xi(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho(x,y)

3.

The minimizing movement scheme (

\displaystyle\rho^{n}=\mathrm{P}_{13}\gamma^{n},\quad\text{with}

\displaystyle\int_{X\times Y}\left[\frac{1}{\tau}\Phi(x,y)+V(x,y)+\kappa\log r^{n}(x,y)\right]\theta(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{n}(x,y)\geq 0.

Therefore we find that

\displaystyle L\mu

\displaystyle\geq\left|\int_{Y}\nabla_{X}(\kappa\log r^{n}-\Pi^{n})r^{n}\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)\right|=\left|\kappa\int_{Y}(\nabla_{X}\log\sigma)\sigma e^{\Pi^{n}/\kappa}\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)\right|=\left|\kappa\int_{Y}(\nabla_{X}\sigma)e^{\Pi^{n}/\kappa}\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\kappa\nabla_{X}\left(\int_{Y}\sigma\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)\right)e^{\Pi^{n}/\kappa}\right|=\left|\kappa\nabla\left(\mu e^{-\Pi^{n}/\kappa}\right)e^{\Pi^{n}/\kappa}\right|=\mu|\nabla(\kappa\log\mu-\Pi^{n})|.

This shows that the function

is in

We will now show the uniform lower bound of

.
Define

\Psi(x,y)=\Pi^{n}(x)-\kappa\log\mu-\frac{1}{\tau}\Phi(x,y)-V(x,y).

Therefore

On the other hand note that, by the properties of disintegrations (Lemma

(60)

Since the log-sum-exp behaves like a weighted average, (

We conclude that there exists a finite constant

For the estimate (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{W}}_{F}(\rho^{n},\rho^{n-1}\big)^{2}=\int_{X\times Y}\big|\nabla_{X}(V-\Pi^{n}+\kappa\log r^{n})\big|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\big|\nabla\Pi^{n}\big|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu+\int_{X\times Y}\big|\nabla_{X}(V+\kappa\log r^{n})\big|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{n}-2\int_{X\times Y}\nabla\Pi^{n}\cdot\nabla_{X}(V+\kappa\log r^{n})\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{n}.

Rearranging terms, and noting that the second contribution is always non-negative:

(62)

\int_{X\times Y}\big|\nabla\Pi^{n}\big|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu\leq\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}{\mathbf{W}}_{F}(\rho^{n},\rho^{n-1}\big)^{2}+2\int_{X\times Y}\nabla\Pi^{n}\cdot\nabla_{X}\left(V+\kappa\log r^{n}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{n}.

Note that for the entropic term we can write:

\displaystyle\int_{X\times Y}\nabla\Pi^{n}\cdot\nabla_{X}\log r^{n}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{X\times Y}\nabla\Pi^{n}\cdot\frac{\nabla_{X}r^{n}}{r^{n}}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{n}=\int_{X\times Y}\nabla\Pi^{n}\cdot\nabla_{X}r^{n}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}\nabla\Pi^{n}\cdot\nabla\mu\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\int_{X}\nabla\Pi^{n}\cdot\nabla\log\mu\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu.

Thus, summing (

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\tau

\displaystyle\int_{X\times Y}\big|\nabla\Pi^{n}\big|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(x)\leq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\tau}{\mathbf{W}}_{F}(\rho^{n},\rho^{n-1}\big)^{2}+2\tau\int_{X\times Y}\nabla\Pi^{n}\cdot\nabla_{X}\left(V+\kappa\log r^{n}\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{n},

\displaystyle\leq 2{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{0})+\sum_{n=1}^{N}2\tau\int_{X\times Y}\nabla\Pi^{n}\cdot\nabla_{X}V\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{n}+2\tau\kappa\int_{X}\nabla\Pi^{n}\cdot\nabla\log\mu\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu

\Pi^{n}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla_{X}\Phi|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{n}

\displaystyle=\tau\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla_{X}(\kappa\log r^{n}+V-\Pi^{n})|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{n}

\displaystyle\geq\tau\int_{X\times Y}\left[\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2}|\nabla_{X}\sqrt{r^{n}}|^{2}-2|\nabla_{X}V|^{2}r^{n}-2|\nabla\Pi^{n}|^{2}r^{n}\right]\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y).

Using the already established estimate on

Lemma 9
.

For every
\psi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{X}\times Y)
with

,

\displaystyle\int_{X\times Y}\psi\,{\mathrm{d}}\left[\frac{\rho_{\tau}^{n}-\rho_{\tau}^{n-1}}{\tau}\right]=-\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{X}\psi\cdot\nabla_{X}\big[V-\Pi_{\tau}^{n}\big]\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{\tau}^{n}+\kappa\int_{X\times Y}\Delta_{X}\psi\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{\tau}^{n}+\tau\,R_{\tau}^{n}(\psi),

where the remainder is bounded as

\displaystyle\big|R_{\tau}^{n}(\psi)\big|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\psi\|_{C^{2}}S_{\tau}^{n},\quad S_{\tau}^{n}:=\frac{{\mathbf{W}}_{F}(\rho_{\tau}^{n},\rho_{\tau}^{n-1})^{2}}{\tau^{2}}.

In particular, for every
\xi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{X})
with
{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\nabla_{X}\xi(x)\equiv 0
on

,

(65)

\displaystyle\int_{X}\nabla_{X}\xi\cdot\nabla_{X}\Pi_{\tau}^{n}\,\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu-\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{X}\xi\cdot\nabla_{X}V\,\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{\tau}^{n}+\kappa\int_{X\times Y}\Delta_{X}\xi\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(x)=\tau\,R_{\tau}^{n}(\xi).

Integrating
\rho_{\tau}^{n-1}
with respect to
\psi
and using (

\displaystyle\int_{X\times Y}\psi(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{\tau}^{n-1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{X\times Y}\psi(x+\tau\nabla_{X}[V-\Pi_{\tau}^{n}+\kappa\log r_{\tau}^{n}],y)r^{n}_{\tau}(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{X\times Y}[\psi(x,y)+\tau\nabla_{X}\psi\cdot\nabla_{X}(V-\Pi_{\tau}^{n}+\kappa\log r_{\tau}^{n})]r_{\tau}^{n}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)

\displaystyle\ \ +\frac{\tau^{2}}{2}R_{\tau}^{n}(\psi),

where the remainder

\displaystyle|R_{\tau}^{n}(\psi)|

Then (

4.
Existence of weak solutions

Throughout this section we denote by
\bar{\Pi}_{\tau}
and
\bar{\rho}_{\tau}
the piecewise-constant interpolations of the iterates
(\Pi^{n}_{\tau})_{n}
and
(\rho^{n}_{\tau})_{n}
:

(66)

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}_{\tau}(t)

\displaystyle=\rho_{\tau}^{\lfloor t/\tau\rfloor},\qquad\bar{r}_{\tau}(t)=r_{\tau}^{\lfloor t/\tau\rfloor},\qquad\bar{\Pi}_{\tau}(t)=\Pi_{\tau}^{\lfloor t/\tau\rfloor},\qquad\text{for all $t\in[0,\infty)$}.

The main result is their convergence to weak solutions of the evolution equation (
1
)-(
3
).
There is a technical aspect worth noting.
Although most of the terms in the time-integrated version of (
63
) can be shown to converge by sheer weak* compactness of
\bar{\rho}_{\tau}
, the pairing between
\bar{\Pi}_{\tau}
and
\bar{\rho}_{\tau}
poses a special difficulty, since both converge only weakly. The control on the horizontal derivatives of
\bar{\rho}_{\tau}
provided by Lemma
8
is unfortunately insufficient to extract even weak convergence of the pairing
\bar{\rho}_{\tau}\nabla\bar{\Pi}_{\tau}
.

Fortunately, as we shall show in Lemma
10
below, thanks precisely to the horizontal regularity bound shown in Lemma
8
, the
Y
-average of
\bar{\rho}_{\tau}
against a smooth test function yields a sequence converging strongly to the corresponding average of the limiting
\rho
. Since the potential
\bar{\Pi}_{\tau}
is merely a function on
X
, integration against this strongly convergent sequence of averages yields the desired convergence of the joint product. This intuition is made rigorous by the following Lemma:

Lemma 10
.

There are functions
\rho\in C({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0};\Gamma(\mu,\nu))
and
\Pi\in L^{2}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0};H^{1}(X))
such that, in the limit
\tau\to 0
, at least along a suitable sequence and for each
T\in(0,\infty)

(67)

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}_{\tau}(t)

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\rho(t)\quad\text{locally uniformly w.r.t.\ $t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}$ in ${\mathbf{W}}$},

(68)

\displaystyle\bar{\Pi}_{\tau}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\Pi\quad\text{weakly in }L^{2}([0,T];H^{1}(X)).

Moreover, define for any given
\zeta\in C^{1}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\times X\times Y)
the
vertical averages

(69)

\omega_{\tau}(t,x):=\int_{Y}\zeta(t,x,y)\bar{r}_{\tau}(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y),\qquad\omega(t,x):=\int_{Y}\zeta(t,x,y)r(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y).

where
\bar{r}_{\tau}\in L^{1}(X\times Y,\mathcal{L}\otimes\nu)
and

denote respectively the densities of
\bar{\rho}_{n}
and
\rho
with respect to
\mathcal{L}\otimes\nu
.
Then, along the same sequence as above,

(70)

\displaystyle\omega_{\tau}

\displaystyle\to\omega\quad\text{strongly in $L^{2}([0,T]\times X)$},

(71)

\displaystyle\omega_{\tau}\nabla\bar{\Pi}_{\tau}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\omega\nabla\Pi\quad\text{weakly in $L^{1}([0,T]\times X)$}.

The limit (
\rho,\Pi)
satisfies, for all
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times X\times Y)
with
\nabla_{X}\psi\cdot\mathbf{n}\equiv 0
on

(72)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X\times Y}\partial_{t}\psi\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X\times Y}\big[\nabla_{X}\psi\cdot\nabla_{X}[V-\Pi]-\kappa\Delta_{X}\psi\big]\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho-\int_{X\times Y}\psi(0)\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{0}.

The potential
\Pi
is given for a.e. time as the unique solution satisfying
\int_{X}\Pi\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu=0
of the elliptic equation:

(73)

\int_{X}\nabla\xi\cdot\nabla\Pi(t)\,\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu=\int_{X}\nabla\xi\cdot\left[\int_{Y}\nabla_{X}V(x,y)r(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)\right]\,{\mathrm{d}}x-\kappa\int_{X}\Delta\xi\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu

for all
\xi\in C^{\infty}(X)
with
\nabla\xi\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
on
\partial X
.

Proof.

For the convergence of the trajectories (
67
) we employ the refined version of Ascoli-Arzela in
[
1

: we use Lemma
7
for the equicontinuity of
\rho

for

and

compact.
The fact that
{\mathcal{E}}(\bar{\rho}_{\tau})
is uniformly bounded by

and thus

On the other hand, the weak convergence of the potentials (

For (

\zeta\in C_{c}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\times X\times Y)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X}

\displaystyle\psi(t,x)\omega_{\tau}(t,x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X\times Y}\psi(t,x)\zeta(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\bar{\rho}_{\tau}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X\times Y}\psi(t,x)\zeta(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho(t,x,y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X}\psi(t,x)\left[\int_{Y}\zeta(t,x,y)r(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)\right]\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X}\psi(t,x)\omega(t,x)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}t

This shows at least weak* convergence of

and the pseudo-distance is the dual norm in

, i.e.,

\displaystyle g(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=\sup\left\{\int_{X}\xi(x)\big(\omega_{1}(x)-\omega_{2}(x)\big)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,\middle|\,\xi\in C^{1}(X),\,\|\xi\|_{C^{1}(X)}\leq 1\right\}.

Let us first show that
\omega_{\tau}

, it holds:

(76)

|\omega_{\tau}(t,x)|=\left|\int_{Y}\zeta(t,x,y)\bar{r}_{\tau}(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)\right|\leq\|\zeta\|_{\infty}\int_{Y}\bar{r}_{\tau}(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)=\|\zeta\|_{\infty}\mu(x)

for a.e.
(t,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\times X
, and the upper bound on
\mu
completes the claim.
Further,
\omega_{\tau}
inherits from
\bar{\rho}_{\tau}
the regularity of its
X
-gradient:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{X}|\nabla_{X}\omega_{\tau}|\,{\mathrm{d}}x\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{X}\left|\nabla_{X}\int_{Y}\zeta(t,x,y)\bar{r}_{\tau}(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)\right|\,{\mathrm{d}}t\,{\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{X\times Y}\left|\nabla_{X}(\zeta\bar{r}_{\tau})\right|\,{\mathrm{d}}t\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)

\displaystyle\leq T\|\nabla_{X}\zeta\|_{\infty}+\|\zeta\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{X\times Y}\left|\nabla_{X}\bar{r}_{\tau}\right|\,{\mathrm{d}}t\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)

\displaystyle=T\|\nabla_{X}\zeta\|_{\infty}+\|\zeta\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{X\times Y}2\sqrt{\bar{r}_{\tau}}\nabla_{X}(\sqrt{\bar{r}_{\tau}})\,{\mathrm{d}}t\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)

\displaystyle\leq T\|\nabla_{X}\zeta\|_{\infty}+2\|\zeta\|_{\infty}\sqrt{T}\left(\int_{0}^{T}\int_{X\times Y}\big|\nabla_{X}\sqrt{\bar{r}_{\tau}}\big|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}t\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)\right)^{1/2}.

Where we used Cauchy-Schwarz in the last inequality.
In virtue of Lemma
8
, we conclude

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{X}|\nabla_{X}\omega_{\tau}|\,{\mathrm{d}}x\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}t\leq T\|\nabla_{X}\zeta\|_{\infty}+\frac{\|\zeta\|_{\infty}}{2\kappa}\sqrt{T}(C+{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{0})).

In combination with the
L^{\infty}
-bound, we thus have

(77)

\displaystyle\sup_{\tau}\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{F}(\omega_{\tau})\,{\mathrm{d}}t<\infty.

Further, the
Y
-averages
\omega_{\tau}
also inherit the uniform regularity in time from
\bar{\rho}_{\tau}
: testing with
\xi\in C^{1}(X)
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{X}\xi(x)

\displaystyle[\omega_{\tau}(m\tau,x)-\omega_{\tau}(n\tau,x)]\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\int_{X\times Y}\xi(x)[(\zeta\bar{r}_{\tau})(m\tau,x,y)-(\zeta\bar{r}_{\tau})(n\tau,x,y)]\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{X\times Y}\xi(x)[\zeta(m\tau,x,y)-\zeta(n\tau,x,y)]\bar{r}_{\tau}(m\tau,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{X\times Y}\xi(x)\zeta(n\tau,x,y)[\bar{r}_{\tau}(m\tau,x,y)-\bar{r}_{\tau}(n\tau,x,y)]\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)

\displaystyle\leq\|\xi\|_{C^{1}(X)}\|\zeta\|_{C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\times X\times Y)}\big[\tau(m-n)+{\mathbf{W}}_{1}(\rho_{\tau}^{m},\rho_{\tau}^{n})\big]

\displaystyle\leq\|\xi\|_{C^{1}(X)}\|\zeta\|_{C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\times X\times Y)}\big[\tau(m-n)+C\sqrt{\tau(m-n)}\big]

where we used (
20
) in the first inequality, and Lemmas
2
and
7
in the last. In other words, we have that

(78)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T-h}g\big(\omega_{\tau}(t+h),\omega_{\tau}(t)\big)\,{\mathrm{d}}t\to 0\quad\text{as $h\searrow 0$, uniformly in $\tau\in(0,1)$.}

Since (
77
) and (
78
) are verified, it follows by means of
[
34
, Theorem 2]
that
\omega_{\tau}
converges to a limit
\omega_{*}\in L^{1}([0,T]\times X)
, in
L^{1}(X)
, in measure with respect to time.
Moreover, since the limit was already identified in (
75
), in particular we have that
\omega_{*}=\omega
.
Invoking the uniform
L^{\infty}
-bound (
76
) again, we conclude that actually
\omega\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times X)
, and that
\omega_{\tau}\to\omega
strongly in

, for any

.

In particular, the convergence
\omega_{\tau}\to\omega
is strong in
L^{2}([0,T]\times X)
, and so (

) follows by weak convergence of
(\nabla\bar{\Pi}_{\tau})_{\tau}
.

We now turn to the weak formulation.
Let
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times X\times Y)
with
\nabla_{X}\psi\cdot\mathbf{n}\equiv 0
on
\partial X
.
Since
\psi
is compactly supported, for each
\tau
there exists an

such that
\psi\equiv 0
for

. Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X\times Y}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\bar{\rho}_{\tau}(t,x,y)=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\int_{n\tau}^{(n+1)\tau}\int_{X\times Y}\partial_{t}\psi(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{\tau}^{n}(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\int_{X\times Y}\big[\psi((n+1)\tau,x,y)-\psi(n\tau,x,y)\big]\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{\tau}^{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=-\int_{X\times Y}\psi(0,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{0}(x,y)-\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\tau\int_{X\times Y}\psi(n\tau,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\left[\frac{\rho_{\tau}^{n}-\rho_{\tau}^{n-1}}{\tau}\right]

The first term is the last term of (
72
). For the second term we turn to (
63
):

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X\times Y}\partial_{t}\psi(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\bar{\rho}_{\tau}(t,x,y)+\int_{X\times Y}\psi(0,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{0}(x,y)

(79)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\tau\left[-\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{X}\psi(n\tau)\cdot\nabla_{X}\big[V-\Pi_{\tau}^{n}\big]\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{\tau}^{n}+\kappa\int_{X\times Y}\Delta_{X}\psi(n\tau)\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{\tau}^{n}+\tau R_{\tau}^{n}(\psi(n\tau))\right].

Let us first control the remainder:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\tau^{2}R_{\tau}^{n}(\psi(n\tau))

\displaystyle\leq\tau\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\tau R_{\tau}^{n}(\psi(n\tau))\leq\tau\|\psi\|_{C^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\frac{{\mathbf{W}}_{F}(\rho_{\tau}^{n},\rho_{\tau}^{n-1})^{2}}{2\tau}\leq\tau\|\psi\|_{C^{2}}{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{0}).

which yields an

contribution. Lemma

allows to control the rest of integrands, so that replacing

by
\psi(t)
in (

) only incurs in an

):

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X\times Y}\partial_{t}\psi(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\bar{\rho}_{\tau}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{X}\psi(t,x,y)\cdot\nabla_{X}V(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\bar{\rho}_{\tau}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X}\nabla_{X}\bar{\Pi}_{\tau}(t,x)\cdot\left[\int_{Y}\nabla_{X}\psi(t,x,y)\bar{r}_{\tau}(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)\right]\,{\mathrm{d}}t\,{\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X\times Y}\Delta_{X}\psi(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\bar{\rho}_{\tau}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{X\times Y}\psi(0,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{0}(x,y)+o(1).

The first and third lines converge by sheer integration of the weak* convergent
(\bar{\rho}_{\tau})_{\tau}
against continuous functions, while the second line converges in virtue of (
71
), and the last line is constant (up to the

term).

Finally, (
73
) follows from testing (
72
) with
\psi(t,x,y)=\theta(t)\xi(x)
, for

:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X}\partial_{t}\theta(t)\xi(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X\times Y}\partial_{t}\theta(t)\xi(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho(t,x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X\times Y}\theta(t)\nabla\xi(x)\cdot\nabla_{X}[V-\Pi]\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho(t,x,y)-\kappa\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X\times Y}\theta(t)\Delta_{X}\xi(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho(t,x,y).

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X}\theta(t)\nabla\xi(x)\cdot\left[-\mu\nabla\Pi+\int_{Y}\nabla_{X}V(\cdot,y)r(\cdot,\cdot,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)\right]-\kappa\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{X}\theta(t)\Delta_{X}\xi(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}t,

and the claim follows from the arbitrariness of
\theta
.
∎

Lemma 11
.

Let
(\rho_{t},\Pi_{t})_{t}
be a limit trajectory as in Lemma
10
and
r_{t}\in L^{1}(X\times Y,\mathcal{L}\otimes\nu)
denote the density of
\rho_{t}
with respect to
\mathcal{L}\otimes\nu
.
Define the dissipation of the energy
{\mathcal{E}}
as:

(80)

\mathcal{I}(\rho_{t},\Pi_{t}):=\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla_{X}(V-\Pi_{t}+\kappa\log r_{t})|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{t}.

Then for all
0\leq t_{1}<t_{2}\leq\infty
the integral of the dissipation is lower-semicontinuous along the converging subsequences in Lemma
10
, i.e.:

(81)

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathcal{I}(\rho_{t},\Pi_{t})\,{\mathrm{d}}t\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathcal{I}((\bar{\rho}_{n})_{t},(\bar{\Pi}_{n})_{t})\,{\mathrm{d}}t.

In particular,

(82)

0\leq{\mathcal{E}}(\rho_{t_{1}})+\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathcal{I}(\rho_{t},\Pi_{t})\,{\mathrm{d}}t\leq{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{0}).

Proof.

For simplicity in this proof we will remove the bars from
\bar{\rho}^{n}
and
\bar{\Pi}^{n}
; simply note that they refer to the piecewise constant discrete trajectories. Then:

\displaystyle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}((\rho_{n})_{t},(\Pi_{n})_{t})\,{\mathrm{d}}t=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla_{X}(V-\Pi_{n}+\kappa\log r_{n})|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{n}

(83)

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla_{X}(V+\kappa\log r_{n})|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{n}

(84)

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X\times Y}2\nabla_{X}(V+\kappa\log r_{n})\cdot\nabla_{X}\Pi_{n}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{n}

(85)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X}|\nabla\Pi_{n}|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}t\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu.

For (
83
), defining
\sigma_{n}:=r_{n}e^{V/\kappa}
we have

\displaystyle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla_{X}(V+\kappa\log r_{n})|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{n}

\displaystyle=\kappa^{2}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla_{X}\sigma_{n}|^{2}\sigma_{n}e^{-V/k}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)

\displaystyle=4\kappa^{2}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X\times Y}e^{-V/k}|\nabla_{X}\sqrt{\sigma_{n}}|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y).

which is lower-semicontinuous, since
\sigma_{n}
inherits
\rho_{n}
’s regularity properties outlined in Lemma
12
. The next term (
84
) can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X\times Y}

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}(V+\kappa\log r_{n})\cdot\nabla_{X}\Pi_{n}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{n}=

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X}\nabla\Pi_{n}\cdot\left[\int_{Y}\rho_{n}\nabla_{X}V\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)\right]\,{\mathrm{d}}t\,{\mathrm{d}}x+\kappa\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X}\nabla\Pi_{n}\cdot\nabla\log\mu\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu,

where the first contribution converges thanks to Lemma
13
and the second by sheer weak convergence of
\nabla(\Pi_{n})_{t}
(plus the fact that
\log\mu\in H^{1}(X,\mu)
, thanks to the finite Fisher information of
\mu
). The last term (
85
) is lower-semicontinuous under weak convergence of
(\Pi_{n})_{n}
in
L^{2}([t_{1},t_{2}];H^{1}(X,\mu))
.

Finally, (
82
) is a direct consequence of (
51
) and the lower-semicontinuity of both energy and the integrated dissipation.
∎

\displaystyle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X}\big|\nabla\Pi_{t}\big|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}t\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(x)

Remark 4
.

Even though the weak solutions constructed in Lemma

can (a priori) only be shown to be continuous in the
{\mathbf{W}}
metric, a posteriori Lemma
12
yields regularity in time with respect to the
{\mathbf{W}}_{F}
metric.

Proof of Lemma
12
.

For (
86
) we will use the Benamou-Brenier formula fiberwise. For given
t_{1}<t_{2}
, and for each
s\in[0,1]
consider
\varrho_{s}:=\rho((1-s)t_{1}+st_{2})
with density given by
\tilde{r}_{s}\in L^{1}(X\times Y,\mathcal{L}\otimes\nu)
and
\pi_{s}:=\Pi((1-s)t_{1}+st_{2})
. For each
y\in Y
, define the velocity field:

(89)

v_{s}:(x,y)\mapsto(t_{2}-t_{1})\nabla_{X}[V(x,y)-\pi_{s}(x)+\kappa\log\tilde{r}_{s}(x,y)],

The pair
(\tilde{r}_{s},v_{s})
thus constructed is a feasible weak solution for the continuity equation:

(90)

\begin{gathered}\partial_{s}\tilde{r}_{s}(x,y)+\nabla_{X}\cdot(\tilde{r}_{s}(x,y)v_{s}(x,y))=0\\
\nabla_{X}(\tilde{r}_{s}v_{s})=0\quad\text{on }\partial X\end{gathered}

Now, by the Benamou-Brenier formula,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{W}}_{F}(\varrho_{0},\varrho_{1})^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{Y}{\mathbf{W}}(\tilde{r}_{0}(\cdot,y),\tilde{r}_{0}(\cdot,y))\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)\leq\int_{Y}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\int_{X}|v_{s}(x,y)|^{2}\tilde{r}_{s}(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}s\right]\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{X\times Y}|v_{s}(x,y)|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\varrho_{s}(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}s

\displaystyle=(t_{2}-t_{1})^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla_{X}[V(x,y)-\pi_{s}(x)+\kappa\log\tilde{r}_{s}(x,y)]|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\varrho_{s}(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}s,

and after a change of variables we eventually obtain:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{W}}_{F}(\rho(t_{1}),\rho(t_{2}))^{2}

\displaystyle=(t_{2}-t_{1})\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla_{X}[V(x,y)-\Pi_{t}(x)+\kappa\log r_{t}(x,y)]|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{t}(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle\leq(t_{2}-t_{1}){\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{0}),

where in the last inequality we used Lemma
11
.

For (
87
) we test (
73
) with (an approximating sequence of)
\Pi_{t}
and integrate by parts to obtain:

\displaystyle\int_{X}

\displaystyle|\nabla\Pi_{t}|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(x)=\int_{X\times Y}\nabla\Pi_{t}\cdot\nabla_{X}V\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{t}+\kappa\int_{X}\nabla\Pi_{t}\cdot\nabla\log\mu\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(x)

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla_{X}V\|_{\infty}\left(\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla\Pi_{t}|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{t}\right)^{1/2}+\kappa\left(\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla\Pi_{t}|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla\log\mu|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu\right)^{1/2},

where we used Cauchy-Schwartz. Then, using that
\|\nabla\log\mu\|_{L^{2}(X,\mu)}=2\|\nabla\sqrt{\mu}\|_{L^{2}(X)}
and simplifying one

term yields:

(91)

\left(\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla\Pi_{t}|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu\right)^{1/2}\leq\|\nabla_{X}V\|_{\infty}+2\kappa\|\nabla\sqrt{\mu}\|_{2},

and (
87
) follows by integration in time.

To obtain (
88
) we expand (
82
):

(92)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(\rho_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathcal{I}(\rho_{t},\Pi_{t})\,{\mathrm{d}}t=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla_{X}(V-\Pi+\kappa\log r)|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho

(93)

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla_{X}V|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho+\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X}|\nabla\Pi|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu\,{\mathrm{d}}t+\kappa^{2}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla_{X}\log r|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho

(94)

\displaystyle\quad+2\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X\times Y}\left[\kappa\nabla_{X}V\cdot\nabla_{X}\log r-\kappa\nabla_{X}\log r\cdot\nabla_{X}\Pi-\nabla_{X}V\cdot\nabla_{X}\Pi\right]\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho

To bound the Fisher information of
\rho
we solve for the last term in (

\displaystyle 4\kappa^{2}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla_{X}\sqrt{r}|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{0})-2\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X\times Y}\kappa\nabla_{X}V\cdot\nabla_{X}\log r\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho

\displaystyle\quad+2\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X\times Y}\left[\kappa\nabla_{X}\log r\cdot\nabla_{X}\Pi-\nabla_{X}V\cdot\nabla_{X}\Pi\right]\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho

Now all the inner products are straightforward to bound:

\displaystyle-2\kappa\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X\times Y}

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}V\cdot\nabla_{X}\log r\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho

\displaystyle\leq 2\kappa\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left(\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla_{X}V|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{t}\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{X\times Y}|\nabla_{X}\log r|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{t}\right)^{1/2}\,{\mathrm{d}}t

where we used

for

and

encompassing the rest. For the next term:

\displaystyle 2\kappa\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{X}\log r\cdot\nabla_{X}\Pi\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho

\displaystyle\leq 2\kappa\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X}\nabla\mu\cdot\nabla\Pi\,{\mathrm{d}}x\leq 2\kappa\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X}\nabla\log\mu\cdot\nabla\Pi\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu

where we used a similar bound. For the remaining term:

\displaystyle 2\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{X}V\cdot\nabla_{X}\Pi\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\nabla_{X}V\|_{\infty}\sqrt{t_{2}-t_{1}}\left(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{X}|\nabla\Pi|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu\right)^{1/2}

Finally, collecting all the contributions and using (

) yields the desired bound.

\omega_{n}(t,x):=\int_{Y}\zeta(t,x,y)r_{n}(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y),\qquad\omega(t,x):=\int_{Y}\zeta(t,x,y)r(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y).

\rho_{n}

Then, along the same sequence as above,

(98)

\displaystyle\omega_{n}

\displaystyle\to\omega\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}([0,T]\times X),

(99)

\displaystyle\omega_{n}\nabla\Pi_{n}

\displaystyle\rightharpoonup\omega\nabla\Pi\quad\text{weakly in }L^{1}([0,T]\times X).

Proof.

As in Lemma
10
, for the convergence of the trajectories (

[

: we use Lemma
12
for the equicontinuity of
\rho
w.r.t.
{\mathbf{W}}
, while the pointwise compactness follows from the compactness of
(\mathcal{P}(X\times Y),{\mathbf{W}})
for

and
Y
compact. This yields existence of a
{\mathbf{W}}
-continuous limit trajectory
\rho
, to which
(\rho_{n})_{n}
converges locally uniformly. Besides,
\rho_{t}
has marginals
\mu
and
\nu

to
(\mu,\nu)
and continuity of the projection operators
\mathrm{P}_{X}
and
\mathrm{P}_{Y}
.

To show density of

with respect to

note first that, by Lemma

{\mathcal{E}}(\rho_{n}(t)\mid\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\leq{\mathcal{E}}(\rho^{0}_{n}\mid\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\leq C
, since by assumption the energy of the initial datum is uniformly bounded, and by Lemma

the energy is non-increasing.
On the other hand, the joint lower-semicontinuity of the energy with respect to weak* convergence (Lemma

) implies:

(100)

{\mathcal{E}}(\rho(t)\mid\mu,\nu)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{E}}(\rho_{n}(t)\mid\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{E}}(\rho_{n}^{0}\mid\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\leq C,

which shows that

Now, the bounds (

\mu_{n}
,
\kappa
and

imply the weak convergence of the potentials

to a limit trajectory

The remaining step is to show that

\Pi

with

\psi
for the weak solution

:

(101)

\displaystyle\int_{X\times Y}\psi(0)\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{0}_{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\times X\times Y}\left[\nabla_{X}\psi\cdot\nabla_{X}V-\nabla_{X}\psi\cdot\nabla\Pi^{k}-\kappa\Delta_{X}\psi-\partial_{t}\psi\right]\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{n}.

The left-hand side converges by weak* convergence of the initial conditions. Likewise, the first, third and last term of the right-hand side converge by sheer weak* convergence of
\rho^{n}
to
\rho
. The remaining term can be shown to converge in virtue of (

(102)

\mathbf{u}_{n}(t,x):=\int_{Y}\nabla_{X}\psi(t,x,y)r_{n}(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y),\qquad\mathbf{u}(t,x):=\int_{Y}\nabla_{X}\psi(t,x,y)r(t,x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y),

and using (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{X\times Y}

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}\psi\cdot\nabla\Pi_{n}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{n}=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{X}\nabla\Pi_{n}(x)\cdot\mathbf{u}_{n}(t,x)\,{\mathrm{d}}t\,{\mathrm{d}}x

\displaystyle\to\int_{0}^{T}\int_{X}\nabla\Pi(x)\cdot\mathbf{u}(t,x)\,{\mathrm{d}}t\,{\mathrm{d}}x=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{X}\psi\cdot\nabla\Pi\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho.

where

the threshold after which

Finally, the fact that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{X}\nabla_{X}\xi\cdot\nabla\Pi\,\,{\mathrm{d}}t\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu

\displaystyle=\lim_{n}-\kappa\int_{0}^{T}\int_{X}\Delta_{X}\xi\,{\mathrm{d}}t\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{n}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{X}\xi\cdot\nabla_{X}V\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{n}

\displaystyle=-\kappa\int_{0}^{T}\int_{X}\Delta_{X}\xi\,{\mathrm{d}}t\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{X\times Y}\nabla_{X}\xi\cdot\nabla_{X}V\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho

6.
Asymptotic limit of weak solutions

In this section we show that any weak solution
(\rho_{t},\Pi_{t})
converges as
t\to\infty
to the unique minimizer of the energy
{\mathcal{E}}
.
This contrasts with the behavior of the solutions of the AHT scheme (corresponding to
\kappa=0
and
\rho_{t}
being of Monge type, cf. Section
1.2
and also Section
7
further below), which are not guaranteed to converge to the optimizer and can stay stationary in suboptimal configurations.

Lemma 14
.

For a sequence of weak solutions
(\rho_{n},\Pi_{n})
with initial conditions
(\rho^{0}_{n})_{n}
and fixed marginals
\mu
and
\nu
converging to
(\rho,\Pi)
on a time interval
(t_{1},t_{2})\subset[0,\infty)
under the same conditions of Lemma
13
, the integral of the dissipation is lower-semicontinuous, i.e.:

(103)

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathcal{I}(\rho_{t},\Pi_{t})\,{\mathrm{d}}t\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathcal{I}((\rho_{n})_{t},(\Pi_{n})_{t})\,{\mathrm{d}}t.

Proof.

The proof is analogue to that of Lemma
11
, replacing the regularity estimates coming from the minimizing movement scheme by the weak solution estimates in Lemma
12
.
∎

Lemma 15
.

Let
\rho_{\infty}\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
, with a non-negative density
r_{\infty}\geq 0
in
L^{1}(X\times Y,\mathcal{L}\otimes\nu)
, and let
\Pi_{\infty}
be the corresponding pressure given by (
73
). If
\mathcal{I}(\rho_{\infty},\Pi_{\infty})=0
, then
\rho_{\infty}
is the unique minimizer of the energy
{\mathcal{E}}
.

Proof.

By assumption:

(104)

0=\int_{X\times Y}\left|\nabla_{X}\big(\kappa\log r_{\infty}(x,y)+V(x,y)-\Pi_{\infty}(x)\big)\right|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho_{\infty}(x,y).

First we will show that
\Pi_{\infty}
is bounded. Since
r_{\infty}
is non-negative, the gradient in (
104
) must vanish wherever
r_{\infty}
is strictly positive, i.e.

(105)

\nabla\Pi_{\infty}(x)=\nabla_{X}V(x,y)+\kappa\nabla_{X}\log r_{\infty}(x,y)\quad\text{for $\rho_{\infty}$-a.e.~}(x,y).

Multiplying by
\rho_{\infty}
and integrating in
Y
one finds:

(106)

\nabla\Pi_{\infty}(x)\mu(x)=\int_{Y}\nabla_{X}V(x,y)r_{\infty}(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)+\kappa\nabla\mu(x)\quad\text{for a.e.~}x,

or

(107)

\nabla\Pi_{\infty}(x)=\int_{Y}\nabla_{X}V(x,y)\frac{r_{\infty}(x,y)}{\mu(x)}\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)+\kappa\nabla\log\mu(x)\quad\text{for a.e.~}x.

Since
\int_{Y}r_{\infty}(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)=\mu(x)
and
\nabla_{X}V
is bounded, we deduce
\Pi_{\infty}-\kappa\log\mu
is bounded on
X
, and in view of the uniform bounds on
\mu
so is
\Pi_{\infty}
. Now, defining
\sigma:=e^{(V-\Pi_{\infty})/\kappa}r_{\infty}
we can rewrite (
104
) as:

(108)

0=\int_{X\times Y}\left|\kappa\nabla_{X}\log\sigma\right|^{2}\sigma e^{-(V-\Pi_{\infty})/\kappa}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu=4\kappa^{2}\int_{X\times Y}\left|\nabla_{X}\sqrt{\sigma}\right|^{2}e^{-(V-\Pi_{\infty})/\kappa}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu.

And since both
V
and
\Pi_{\infty}
are bounded,
\kappa
is positive and
X
has connected interior,
\sigma(x,y)
must be for each
y
equal to a positive constant
e^{\Psi(y)/\kappa}
.
Rearranging terms yields:

(109)

r_{\infty}(x,y)=\exp\left(\frac{\Pi_{\infty}(x)+\Psi(y)-V(x,y)}{\kappa}\right).

Finally, since the measure
\rho_{\infty}=r_{\infty}\,{\mathrm{d}}x\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)
has marginals
\mu
and
\nu
and its density takes the form of the diagonal scaling (
109
), we conclude by Lemma
4
that it is the unique minimizer of
{\mathcal{E}}
, or analogously, the optimal entropic plan between
\mu
and
\nu
with cost function
V
and regularization strength
\kappa
. The optimal entropic dual potentials are thus
\Pi_{\infty}
and
\Psi
.
∎

Lemma 16
.

The weak solutions
(\rho_{t},\Pi_{t})_{t}
obtained in Lemma
10
tend as
t\to\infty
to the unique minimizer of
{\mathcal{E}}
.

Proof.

Our convergence argument follows a similar rationale to that in
[
28
]
.
Let
(\rho,\Pi)
be a weak solution of (
72
)-(
73
).
There exist cluster points of the trajectory
(\rho_{t})_{t}
by sheer weak* compactness in the space of probability measures on
X\times Y
; let us denote such a cluster point by
\rho_{*}
and the converging subsequence of times by
(t_{n})_{n}
. By weak* continuity,
\rho_{*}
preserves the marginals and positivity of the trajectory
\rho
.

Let us define functions
\rho_{n}(s):=\rho(t_{n}+s)
,
\Pi_{n}:=\Pi(t_{n}+s)
for
s\in[0,1]
. Since each
(\rho_{n},\Pi_{n})
is a weak solution of (
72
)-(
73
) with initial condition
\rho_{n}(0)=\rho(t_{n})\to\rho_{*}
, the stability of weak solutions (Lemma
13
) grants convergence of (a subsequence of)
(\rho_{n},\Pi_{n})_{n}
to a weak solution
(\rho^{\prime},\Pi^{\prime})
with initial condition
\rho_{*}
. Now, since the dissipation
\mathcal{I}
of the original weak solution
(\rho,\Pi)
is positive and summable, the integrals of the form
\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{I}(\rho_{n}(s),\Pi_{n}(s))\,{\mathrm{d}}s
must converge to zero, which combined with Lemma
14
implies that the dissipation vanishes in the asymptotic limit:

(110)

0\leq\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{I}(\rho^{\prime}(s),\Pi^{\prime}(s))\,{\mathrm{d}}s\leq\liminf\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{I}(\rho_{n}(s),\Pi_{n}(s))\,{\mathrm{d}}s=0

Since
\mathcal{I}
is a non-negative quantity (for
\rho\geq 0
), this means that
\mathcal{I}(\rho^{\prime}(s),\Pi^{\prime}(s))
is identically zero, which by Lemma
15
implies that
\rho^{\prime}(s)
and
\rho_{*}
are equal to the unique minimizer of
{\mathcal{E}}
, given by (
109
). Since this convergence holds along any subsequence, the whole trajectory converges to
\rho_{*}
.
∎

7.
Stationarity in the unregularized case

Section
6
showed that solutions to (
1
)-(
3
) converge as
t\to\infty
to the minimizer of
{\mathcal{E}}
.
In contrast, the AHT scheme does not enjoy such a global convergence.
As explained in Section
1.2
, equations (
1
)-(
3
) introduce two kinds of modifications with respect to the AHT scheme: a relaxation (enlarging the space of transport maps to that of transport plans) and a smoothing (adding an entropic regularization term to the energy).
In this section we show that the relaxation step is not sufficient to endow the AHT scheme with convergence properties analogous to those of (
1
)-(
3
), and that some kind of regularization appears to be necessary.

As reviewed in Section
1.2
, the problem of existence of solutions becomes in general much harder for
\kappa=0
, since the a priori estimate on the horizontal gradients of
\rho
(or
r
) is lost.
In fact, for an absolutely continuous
\nu
, weak solutions to (
1
)-(
3
) may be extremely degenerate, possibly giving mass to a single point in each
y
-fiber. Due to these difficulties we will not attempt to provide a comprehensive picture of the
\kappa=0
case. Instead, we will exemplify that, when weak solutions exist, they face the same type of obstacle for asymptotic convergence to the energy minimizer that is observed in the AHT scheme.
The reason is that in the
\kappa=0
regime there exist suboptimal stationary points of the evolution equation. We will demonstrate this by showing that, whenever
V\in C^{2}(X\times Y)
satisfies the
twist condition
(i.e.,
\nabla_{X}V(x,\cdot)
is injective for all
x
), any initial datum
\rho_{0}
of the form

(111)

\rho_{0}=(\operatorname{id},T_{*})_{\sharp}\mu,\qquad\textrm{with $T_{*}(x)=\nabla_{X}V(x,\cdot)^{-1}(\nabla\Pi_{*}(x))$,
\qquad$T_{*\sharp}\mu=\nu$}

with
\Pi_{*}
in
C^{2}(X)
stays stationary by the discrete minimizing movement when
\tau
is small enough.
As a consequence, in this case a limit trajectory exists and is constantly equal to the initialization, which in general will be suboptimal for the energy.

Let us show this in detail.
For
\kappa=0
the minimizing movement (
41
) becomes:

(112)

\rho_{\tau}^{n}\in\argmin_{\rho\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\frac{1}{2\tau}{\mathbf{W}}_{F}(\rho,\rho_{\tau}^{n-1})^{2}+{\mathcal{E}}_{0}(\rho),\qquad{\mathcal{E}}_{0}(\rho):=\int_{X\times Y}V\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho.

Our argument hinges on a primal-dual argument for the minimizing movement. In analogy to Lemma
6
, (
112
) can be rewritten as

(113)

\rho^{n}=\mathrm{P}_{13}\gamma^{n},\quad\text{with $\gamma^{n}$ }\in\argmin_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\rho^{n-1})}\int_{X\times X\times Y}\left(\frac{|x-x^{\prime}|^{2}}{2\tau}+V(x,y)\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}\gamma(x,x^{\prime},y),

with respective (pre)-dual problem

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\sup_{\scriptsize\begin{array}[]{c}\Pi\in C(X)\\
\Psi\in C(X\times Y)\end{array}}\int_{X}\Pi(x)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(x)+\int_{X\times Y}\Psi(x^{\prime},y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{n-1}(x^{\prime},y)\\
\text{s.t. }\Pi(x)+\Psi(x^{\prime},y)\leq\frac{|x-x^{\prime}|^{2}}{2\tau}+V(x,y)\text{ for all }(x,x^{\prime},y)\in X\times X\times Y.\end{gathered}

We can show that
\rho_{0}
is stationary by the minimizing movement —or, equivalently, optimal for the primal problem (
113
) when choosing
\rho^{n-1}=\rho_{0}
— by building a dual certificate. Choosing
\Pi=\Pi_{*}
, we can obtain a feasible
\Psi
by computing the
c
-transform of
\Pi_{*}
with respect to the cost function
c(x,x^{\prime},y):=\tfrac{|x-x^{\prime}|^{2}}{2\tau}+V(x,y)
:

\displaystyle\Psi_{*}(x^{\prime},y):=\inf_{x\in X}\frac{|x-x^{\prime}|^{2}}{2\tau}+V(x,y)-\Pi_{*}(x).

For
\tau
sufficiently small, and for each
(x^{\prime},y)\in X\times Y
, the expression in the infimum is a uniformly convex function of
x
(thanks to the assumed smoothness of
\Pi_{*}
).
So a sufficient condition for
\hat{x}
to attain the infimum is that it satisfies:

(117)

(\hat{x}-x^{\prime})/\tau+\nabla_{X}V(\hat{x},y)-\nabla_{*}\Pi(\hat{x})=0.

Now, for
(x^{\prime},y)
in the support of
\rho_{0}=(\operatorname{id},T_{*})_{\sharp}\mu
, (
111
), one has
y=\nabla_{X}V(x^{\prime},\cdot)^{-1}(\nabla\Pi_{*}(x^{\prime}))
, so choosing
\hat{x}=x^{\prime}
verifies (
117
). Thus, for
\rho^{n-1}
-a.e.
(x^{\prime},y)
,
\Psi_{*}
is given by:

(118)

\Psi_{*}(x^{\prime},y)=V(x^{\prime},y)-\Pi_{*}(x^{\prime}).

The dual score then reads:

(119)

\int_{X}\Pi_{*}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu+\int_{X\times Y}\Psi_{*}\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{n-1}=\int_{X}\Pi_{*}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu+\int_{X\times Y}(V-\Pi_{*})\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{n-1}=\int_{X\times Y}V\,{\mathrm{d}}\rho^{n-1},

which matches the primal score (
113
) for
\gamma_{*}:=(\operatorname{id},\operatorname{id},T)_{\sharp}\mu
. Hence we obtained a primal-dual certificate, showing that for
\tau
sufficiently small
\rho_{0}
is a minimizer of (
113
) and therefore a stationary point of the minimizing movement scheme (
112
).

r(\cdot,y)
and we observe congestion at the boundaries of the bottleneck region; however the relative density
r(\cdot,y)/\mu(\cdot)
remains continuous for all times.
Again, the trajectories show a consistent behavior for all values of
N
considered, which may indicate that the Fisher information bound hypothesis could be further relaxed.

In conclusion, the numerical simulations of this section exemplify the stability of the weak solutions of (
72
)-(
73
) as the second marginal
\nu
tends to a continuum, as well as the asymptotic convergence to the minimizer of the energy
{\mathcal{E}}
as time goes to infinitiy. Moreover, there is some numerical evidence that the convergence rate may be linear.
The simulations provide interesting insights on how a continuum of particles (the species
y\in Y
) find an optimal arrangement of positions (the points
x\in X
) by local exchanges, subject to a volume constraint, that may include obstructions and bottlenecks.

Appendix A
A technical lemma

Lemma 17
.

Let
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(X\times Y)
with respective marginals
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
be given, and assume that
\gamma
possesses a density
u\in L^{1}(X\times Y,\mu\otimes\nu)
with respect to
\mu\otimes\nu
. Further, let
f\in L^{1}(X\times Y,\gamma)
have the following property: for every
\theta\in L^{\infty}(X\times Y,\mu\otimes\nu)
satisfying

(122)

\begin{split}\int_{X}f(x,y)\,\theta(x,y)u(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(x)&=0\quad\text{for $\nu$-a.e.~$y\in Y$},\\
\int_{Y}f(x,y)\,\theta(x,y)u(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)&=0\quad\text{for $\mu$-a.e.~$x\in X$},\end{split}

it follows that

(123)

\displaystyle\int_{X\times Y}f(x,y)\,\theta(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\gamma(x,y)=0.

Then there are
P\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
and
Q\in L^{1}(Y,\nu)
such that
f(x,y)=P(x)+Q(y)
for
\gamma
-a.e.
(x,y)\in X\times Y
.

Proof.

This is an application of the Hahn-Banach separation theorem. Define

U:=\{P(x)+Q(y)\,|\,P\in L^{1}(X,\mu),\,Q\in L^{1}(Y,\nu)\}.

This is a subspace of
L^{1}(X\times Y,\gamma)
, since

\displaystyle\int_{X\times Y}|P(x)+Q(y)|\,{\mathrm{d}}\gamma(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{X}|P(x)|\left(\int_{Y}u(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(x)+\int_{Y}|Q(y)|\left(\int_{X}u(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(x)\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)

\displaystyle\qquad=\|P\|_{L^{1}(X,\mu)}+\|Q\|_{L^{1}(Y,\nu)}.

Further, the subspace
U
is closed since convergence of
f_{n}
with
f_{n}(x,y)=P_{n}(x)+Q_{n}(y)
in
L^{1}(X\times Y,\gamma)
is equivalent to convergence of
P_{n}
and
Q_{n}
in the respective Banach spaces
L^{1}(X,\mu)
and
L^{1}(Y,\nu)
.

Fix some
f_{*}\in L^{1}(X\times Y,\gamma)
that is
not
in
U
; the claim is proven if we can verify the existence of some
\theta_{*}\in L^{\infty}(X\times Y,\mu\otimes\nu)
that satisfies (
122
), but
not
(
123
) with
f=f_{*}
. Since
U
is closed,
f_{*}
has a positive distance to
U
, and the Hahn-Banach theorem thus guarantees the existence of a continuous linear functional
\varphi_{*}:L^{1}(X\times Y,\gamma)\to{\mathbb{R}}
with
\varphi_{*}(U)=\{0\}
and
\varphi_{*}(f_{*})=1
. By
L^{1}
-
L^{\infty}
duality, there exists some
\theta_{*}\in L^{\infty}(X\times Y,\mu\otimes\nu)
such that

\displaystyle\varphi_{*}(f)=\int_{X\times Y}f\,\theta_{*}\,{\mathrm{d}}\gamma\quad\text{for all $f\in L^{1}(X\times Y,\gamma)$.}

Further,
\varphi_{*}(U)=\{0\}
implies for
f(x,y):=a(x)+b(y)
with arbitrary
a\in L^{\infty}(X,\mu)
and
b\in L^{\infty}(Y,\nu)
that

\displaystyle 0=\varphi_{*}(f)=\int_{X}a(x)\left(\int_{Y}\theta_{*}(x,y)u(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y)\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(x)+\int_{Y}b(y)\left(\int_{X}\theta_{*}(x,y)u(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu(x)\right)\,{\mathrm{d}}\nu(y).

Hence
\theta_{*}
satisfies the conditions (
122
). On the other hand, one has

\displaystyle 1=\varphi_{*}(f_{*})=\int_{X\times Y}f_{*}\,\theta_{*}\,{\mathrm{d}}\gamma,

i.e.,
f_{*}
does
not
satisfy (
123
) for the choice
\theta=\theta_{*}
.
∎

Remark 6
.

In case that
\gamma=\mu\otimes\nu
is a product measure, i.e.,
u\equiv 1
, one does not need the abstract machinery of the Hahn-Banach theorem. Then, for any given
f_{*}\in L^{1}(X\times Y,\gamma)
that is not of the form
f(x,y)=P(x)+Q(y)
, a suitable test function
\theta_{*}
satisfying (
122
) but not (
123
) for
f=f_{*}
can be obtained very explicitly. The generalization of that construction to
\gamma
that are not of product form is unclear.
