Impact of data augmentation size on deep learning-based third lumbar vertebra computed tomography skeletal muscle segmentation performance
Authors: Xuzhi Zhao, Yi Du, Xianggao Zhu, Yufei Liu, Yanhui Xiao, Huawei Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVarious deep learning (DL) models have been proposed for skeletal muscle segmentation at the level of the third lumbar vertebra (L3) on computed tomography (CT) images, typically incorporating data augmentation to improve model robustness and mitigate overfitting. However, the impact of augmentation size on model performance remains unclear. This study aims to quantitatively evaluate this impact and provide evidence-based guidance for selecting appropriate augmentation sizes.MethodsA total of 400 patients diagnosed with rectal or cervical cancer were included, each with a pelvic CT series acquired using a clinical CT scanner. For each patient, skeletal muscle regions were manually annotated on three consecutive axial CT images at the L3 level to serve as ground truth. The dataset was divided into training, validation, and test sets comprising 280, 60, and 60 patients, respectively. Ten experimental groups (Labs 1–10) were established by varying the number of augmented images included in the training set to represent different augmentation sizes. Lab 1 contained only the original images. To create Labs 2–10, each original training image was augmented up to nine times using random combinations of horizontal flipping, translation, scaling, and Gaussian noise addition. Specifically, Labs 2–9 incorporated 1–8 randomly selected augmented images per original image, while Lab 10 included all nine augmented images. To enhance experimental reliability, the dataset creation process for Labs 2–10 was repeated ten times. Three representative DL models—U-Net, attention U-Net, and attention V-Net—were trained on each group and evaluated on the same test set using five quantitative metrics: dice similarity coefficient (DSC), precision, recall, 95th percentile Hausdorff distance (HD95), and average surface distance (ASD).ResultsModel performance generally improved with increasing augmentation size, accompanied by enhanced robustness in skeletal muscle morphology and boundary delineation. All three models achieved their best performance within Labs 7–10. The U-Net achieved the best averaged DSC, precision, recall, HD95, ASD values of 97.481%, 97.299%, 98.040%, 1.782 mm, and 0.268 mm, respectively. The attention U-Net achieved corresponding best values of 97.455%, 97.208%, 98.013%, 1.822 mm, and 0.270 mm, while the attention V-Net achieved 97.627%, 97.436%, 97.939%, 1.781 mm, and 0.250 mm, respectively.ConclusionsData augmentation effectively improves DL model performance for L3 CT skeletal muscle segmentation. Based on systematic evaluation across ten augmentation sizes, an augmentation size of 6× or greater per L3 CT image is recommended to ensure high segmentation accuracy and robust generalization.Keywords: Deep learning (DL), data augmentation size, skeletal muscle segmentation, third lumbar vertebra (L3), computed tomography images (CT images)

Introduction

The cross-sectional area of skeletal muscle at the level of the third lumbar vertebra (L3) on computed tomography (CT) imaging is a well-established and widely used biomarker for assessing nutritional status in patients with sarcopenia (1,2), cancer (3,4), cardiovascular disease (5,6), and those recovering from major surgery (7,8). Traditionally, skeletal muscle contours on CT images at the L3 level are manually annotated by board-certified radiologists (9,10). However, manual delineation is time-consuming and labour-intensive, subject to substantial inter-observer variability, and inherently limited in scalability (11,12). Consequently, there is growing interest in developing automated and accurate computational algorithms for skeletal muscle segmentation on CT images at the L3 level (13-41), some of which have already been translated into commercially available solutions (16,34).

Existing segmentation algorithms can be broadly categorized into traditional image-processing techniques (13-20) and deep learning (DL)-based approaches (21-41). Early studies relied on traditional methods, including statistical shape models (13), rule-based expert systems (14,15), non-uniform finite element meshes (16), reference atlas matching (17), morphological operations (18), random forest-based classifiers (19), and divide-and-conquer strategies (20), among others. These methods depend heavily on hand-crafted, task-specific features and are often sensitive to anatomical variability in muscle shape, frequently necessitating manual correction. In recent years, DL models—particularly those based on convolutional neural networks (CNNs)—have been developed for skeletal muscle segmentation at the L3 level (21). Representative architectures include U-Net (22-28), ResUNet (29), FCN (30), FCN-2s-VGG16 (31-33), FCN-UNet (34,35), CDFNet (36), U-shaped Transformer (37,38), and attention U-Net (39,40), attention V-Net (41). Experimental results have demonstrated that DL models can effectively learn end-to-end mappings from L3 CT images to skeletal muscle masks, achieving markedly higher segmentation accuracy than traditional image-processing techniques (21).

In DL-based L3 CT skeletal muscle segmentation studies, data augmentation is widely employed to enhance dataset diversity and representativeness, thereby improving model robustness and mitigating overfitting under limited data conditions. Table 1 summarizes representative prior studies, including their dataset sizes, augmentation sizes, augmentation strategies, and evaluation metrics. Despite the widespread adoption of data augmentation, the augmentation sizes used in existing studies (21-41) have been determined empirically. Importantly, no prior work has systematically investigated how the augmentation size itself influences segmentation accuracy or model performance. As a result, reproducible and generalizable guidance for selecting an appropriate augmentation size remains lacking in the field.

To this end, this study aims to quantitatively evaluate the impact of augmentation size on DL model performance. We construct a CT dataset encompassing two cancer types and two scanning protocols and select three representative segmentation networks—U-Net, attention U-Net, and attention V-Net—as benchmark models. Ten experimental groups are designed by varying the augmentation size from 0× to 9× while keeping all other factors constant. Model performance is assessed using five quantitative metrics across the ten experimental groups, enabling evidence-based recommendations for choosing appropriate augmentation sizes for skeletal muscle segmentation on CT images at the L3 level. Experimental results indicate that an augmentation size of 6× or greater consistently yields optimal performance across all three models.

Methods

Patients and CT image acquisition

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University Cancer Hospital & Institute and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived. A total of 400 patients from Peking University Cancer Hospital & Institute were included, all of whom had a confirmed diagnosis of either rectal cancer or cervical cancer and received radiotherapy between 2015 and 2021. Among them, 91 male patients had rectal cancer, and 309 female patients had cervical cancer. The patients’ ages ranged from 32 to 90 years, with a median age of 63 years.

All patients underwent pelvic CT scans on a Sensation Open CT scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) for radiotherapy simulation. During image acquisition, patients were immobilized in the supine position using customized thermoplastic devices to minimize motion. Two scanning protocols were employed: contrast-enhanced and non-contrast scans. The contrast-enhanced scans were performed following intravenous administration of an iodinated contrast agent, while the non-contrast scans were acquired without contrast injection. In total, 151 patients underwent contrast-enhanced CT scans, and 249 patients underwent non-contrast CT scans.

The pelvic CT scans were acquired using a tube voltage of 120 kVp with automatic tube current modulation ranging from 29 to 294 mA. Axial images were reconstructed using a B20s kernel (42) with a slice thickness of 5 mm. The reconstructed images had a matrix size of 512×512 and an in-plane pixel size of 1.27 mm × 1.27 mm.

image selection and muscle annotation

A senior radiographer with 15 years of experience reviewed the axial CT image series of all patients and selected three consecutive two-dimensional (2D) CT slices at the mid-L3 level for each patient, ensuring clear visualization of skeletal muscle structures while preserving continuous morphological variation across the three slices.

Because manual annotation of skeletal muscle in the selected L3 CT images is labor-intensive, five oncologists with 8, 12, 16, 23, and 25 years of clinical experience were invited to perform the annotations. Prior to the labeling process, the radiographer and the five oncologists jointly discussed and established the standardized annotation guidelines. Each oncologist then independently annotated images from 80 randomly assigned, non-overlapping patients using ITK-SNAP software (version 4.0) (43). After the initial annotations were completed, the radiographer conducted a comprehensive patient-by-patient review and corrected the annotations when necessary to ensure consistency and accuracy. The finalized annotated masks served as the ground truth (GT) for subsequent segmentation tasks. Image preprocessing and data partitioning

The CT images at the L3 level from all patients were preprocessed in two steps. First, the pixel values were windowed to a range of −250 to 250 HU (44), with values below −250 and above 250 HU clipped to these limits. Second, the pixel values were linearly scaled to a range of 0 to 1.

The dataset of 400 patients was randomly divided into three subsets: 280 patients for the training set, 60 for the validation set, and the remaining 60 for the test set. To prevent data leakage, the data partitioning was performed at the patient level (45). Specifically, the training set was used to optimize the learnable parameters of the DL model, the validation set was employed for hyperparameter tuning and to prevent overfitting of the model, and the test set was used to evaluate the model’s generalization performance on unseen data.

Data augmentation

To precisely control the dataset size and ensure reproducibility across experiments, offline data augmentation (46) was applied to the training set. For each original CT image at the L3 level, augmented samples were generated using random combinations of the following four transformations: horizontal flipping, random translation (with x- and y-axis shifts within ±20 pixels), random scaling (with scaling factors ranging from 0.8 to 1.0 or from 1.0 to 1.4), and random Gaussian noise addition (with a mean of 0 and a standard deviation ranging from 0 to 0.004). Each of the four augmentation transformations was applied independently with a triggering probability of 0.5, and all augmented images were ensured to be distinct from their corresponding original images. Examples of the four augmentation transformations are shown in Figure 2.

Examples of four data augmentation transformations applied to an original CT image at the L3 level, including horizontal flipping, random translation, random scaling, and random Gaussian noise addition. CT, computed tomography; L3, third lumbar vertebra.

These transformations were designed to reflect common sources of physical variability encountered in clinical CT imaging. Specifically, the horizontal flipping simulated changes in patient orientation (e.g., from head-first to foot-first positioning); translation simulated slight misalignments of the scanning table relative to the imaging center; scaling simulated variations in the field of view (FOV) during CT acquisition; and Gaussian noise simulated electronic noise introduced by hardware limitations of the CT system (47). All augmented CT images were kept at a matrix size of 512×512, and pixel values below 0 or above 1 were clipped. The corresponding GT masks were adjusted accordingly to maintain pixel-wise alignment.

DL models

In this study, three representative DL models used in previous L3 CT skeletal muscle segmentation research (21-41) were included: U-Net (22-28), attention U-Net (39), and attention V-Net (41). The subsequent subsections present detailed descriptions of the model architectures and the loss function.

Model architecture

Figure 3 shows the architecture of the U-Net model. The model takes a 2D axial CT image at the L3 level as input and generates the corresponding skeletal muscle segmentation mask as output. The model follows an encoder-decoder design, consisting of four down-sampling stages in the encoder and four symmetric up-sampling stages in the decoder. The encoder progressively reduces an input of size H × W × 1 to a latent representation of size (H/16) × (W/16) × 256, while the decoder restores this representation back to the original H × W resolution. Each encoder stage comprises two consecutive 3×3 convolutional layers (zero-padded) with a stride of 1, each followed by a batch normalization (BN) layer and a rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation function. A down-sampling operation is then performed using a max pooling layer with a stride of 2. In the decoder, each stage concludes with an up-sampling operation using a 2×2 transposed convolutional layer (zero-padded) with a stride of 2. Skip connections are employed between corresponding encoder and decoder stages to fuse feature maps, thereby preserving spatial information and alleviating the vanishing gradient problem (48). After the final 1×1 convolutional layer with a stride of 1, a sigmoid activation function is applied to generate pixel-wise confidence scores for skeletal muscle segmentation. During model inference, a threshold of 0.5 was used to classify each pixel, where scores above the threshold were labeled as muscle tissue and those below as background.

Overall architecture of the U-Net model. BN, batch normalization; Conv, convolutional layer; CT, computed tomography; H, height; L3, third lumbar vertebra; ReLU, rectified linear unit; W, width.

Figure 4 shows the architecture of the attention U-Net model. The architectural differences between the attention U-Net model and the standard U-Net model are twofold. First, each encoder and decoder stage consists of a 3×3 convolutional layer followed by a selective kernel (SK) attention block (49). Second, to enhance feature reuse, the feature maps produced by the SK attention blocks in the last three decoder stages are each upsampled to the original H × W resolution using transposed convolutional layers with different kernel sizes and strides. These three upsampled outputs are then combined with the output of the final SK attention block through element-wise summation, and the result is fed into the final 1×1 convolutional layer followed by a sigmoid activation function. Moreover, the SK attention block adaptively adjusts the receptive field through a selective fusion of feature maps obtained from multiple convolutional kernels of different sizes, enabling the network to dynamically focus on the most informative features (49). In our experiments, all SK attention blocks employed a combination of 3×3 and 5×5 convolutional kernels.

Overall architecture of the attention U-Net model. BN, batch normalization; Conv, convolutional layer; CT, computed tomography; H, height; L3, third lumbar vertebra; ReLU, rectified linear unit; SK, selective kernel; W, width.

Figure 5 shows the architecture of the attention V-Net model. Similar to the standard U-Net and attention U-Net architectures, it follows an encoder-decoder design, each composed of four stages. Each encoder or decoder stage contains one, two, or three 3×3 convolutional layers, followed by a squeeze-and-excitation (SE) attention block (50). A residual learning technique is applied within each stage by directly adding the input of the first convolutional layer to the output of the SE block. Furthermore, the cross-stage attention block (41) in the n-th decoder stage operates simultaneously on the outputs of the residual learning modules from both the n-th decoder stage and the (5−n)-th encoder stage. This block incorporates both spatial and channel attention mechanisms, enabling the network to effectively integrate hierarchical contextual information, selectively emphasize informative features, and suppress irrelevant background responses. Finally, after a 3×3 convolutional layer followed by a sigmoid activation function, the model generates the skeletal muscle segmentation mask.

Experimental setup

Experimental groups defined by augmentation size

To investigate the impact of data augmentation size on model performance, ten experimental groups—named Lab 1 through Lab 10—were established. The training set of each group contained the same number of original CT images at the L3 level but differed in the number of augmented images included.

Specifically, the training set in Lab 1 consisted solely of the original L3 CT images without any augmented images, corresponding to an augmentation size of 0×. Three models—U-Net, attention U-Net, and attention V-Net—were trained separately on this training set.

To enhance the reliability of the experiment, the creation and model training process for Labs 2–10 was repeated 10 times.

As a result, a total of 273 models were trained: three models for Lab 1 and thirty models for each of Labs 2–10. It is important to note that the validation and test sets were identical across all experimental groups and consisted exclusively of the original L3 CT images. During model training, the same validation set was used for hyperparameter tuning in all experimental groups. After training, all models were evaluated on the same test set to ensure a fair and consistent comparison across groups.

Model training details

For training the U-Net, attention U-Net, and attention V-Net models, the batch sizes were set to 50, 50, and 40, respectively. The Adam optimizer (51) was used for all three models, with an initial learning rate of 10−3, β1=0.9, and β2=0.999. Convolutional and transposed convolutional kernels were initialized using the Kaiming initialization method (52). To optimize the training process and prevent overfitting, a learning rate scheduler and early stopping strategy were employed, both based on the averaged loss value on the validation set. Specifically, the learning rate was reduced to one-tenth of its current value if the averaged loss on the validation set did not decrease over 10 consecutive epochs, and the training process was halted if there was no decrease in this loss over 20 consecutive epochs. All models were trained for a maximum of 200 epochs.

The experiments were implemented using Python v3.8.18, PyTorch v1.10.1, and CUDA v11.1.74. The computational platform consisted of an Intel® CoreTM i7-10700K CPU @ 3.80 GHz and an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU with 24 GB of memory. To ensure reproducibility, the random seed was fixed across all components (53), including PyTorch, NumPy, and Python’s built-in random module. Additionally, CUDA and cuDNN were configured to use deterministic algorithms (54), ensuring that all stochastic operations produced identical results across multiple runs.

Evaluation metrics

The performance of the U-Net, attention U-Net, and attention V-Net models was quantitatively evaluated using five segmentation accuracy assessment metrics: dice similarity coefficient (DSC), precision, recall, 95th quantile of the Hausdorff distance (HD95), and average surface distance (ASD) (20). Specifically, DSC, precision, and recall were used to measure the pixel-wise overlap between the model-predicted segmentation mask and the GT mask, while HD95 and ASD were employed to estimate the distance between the boundary points of the predicted and GT masks.

First, when the training set contained only the original L3 CT images without data augmentation (Lab 1), all five metrics—DSC, Precision, Recall, HD95, and ASD—exhibited the poorest averaged values across all three models.

Second, after augmented images were incorporated into the training set (Labs 2–10), all three models showed consistent performance improvements compared with Lab 1. Specifically, the U-Net model improved by at least 0.687%, 0.618%, 0.761%, 0.317 mm, and 0.072 mm in averaged DSC, precision, recall, HD95, and ASD, respectively. Similarly, the attention U-Net model improved by at least 0.282%, 0.174%, 0.385%, 0.388 mm, and 0.048 mm, respectively, while the attention V-Net model showed minimum improvements of 0.217%, 0.466%, 0.037%, 0.138 mm, and 0.026 mm, respectively.

Third, the model performance generally improved as the augmentation size expanded. All three models achieved their best performance within Labs 7–10. Relative to Lab 1, the U-Net model exhibited peak improvements of 1.297%, 1.645%, 1.279%, 0.608 mm, and 0.141 mm in averaged DSC, precision, recall, HD95, and ASD, respectively. In comparison, maximum gains for the attention U-Net model were 0.763%, 0.840%, 0.942%, 0.660 mm, and 0.096 mm, respectively, whereas the attention V-Net model achieved maximal improvements of 0.771%, 0.998%, 0.648%, 0.355 mm, and 0.089 mm, respectively.

Fourth, after Lab 6, performance gains for all three models began to plateau, showing a clear diminishing-returns pattern, and some metrics displayed non-monotonic fluctuations. Although the best results obtained in Labs 7–10 were statistically significantly different from those of Lab 6 for most metrics (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P<0.05), the magnitude of these improvements was relatively modest. Compared with Lab 6, additional improvements to the best averaged metrics for the U-Net model resulted in only 0.192%, 0.275%, 0.153%, 0.111 mm, and 0.021 mm in DSC, precision, recall, HD95, and ASD, respectively. The attention U-Net model showed similarly limited gains of 0.066%, 0.235%, 0.168%, 0.094 mm, and 0.006 mm, respectively, while the attention V-Net model exhibited limited gains of 0.145%, 0.295%, 0.083%, 0.037 mm, and 0.019 mm, respectively.

As shown in Figures 6A,7A,8A, the models trained exclusively on the original L3 CT images without any data augmentation (Lab 1) exhibit relatively poor generalization. Specifically, portions of the kidneys are erroneously classified as skeletal muscle, and some skeletal muscle regions are incompletely delineated. Consequently, Lab 1 yields the lowest DSC and the highest ASD values among all experimental groups.

In Figures 6B-6D,7B-7D,8B-8D, as the augmentation size increases (Labs 2–4), the models’ ability to discriminate skeletal muscle from anatomically overlapping tissue intensities and to identify previously under-segmented skeletal muscle regions progressively improves. This improvement is reflected by enhanced morphological robustness, increased boundary fidelity, and corresponding gains in both DSC and ASD metrics.

As depicted in Figures 6E,7E,8E, the models’ segmentation performance shows a non-monotonic dip in Lab 5, where both DSC and ASD metrics slightly deteriorate compared with those observed in Lab 4. This behavior may be attributed to the transitional regime in data augmentation, where the augmentation size has increased but has not yet reached a level sufficient to provide stable and diverse feature representations. At this stage, the introduced variability may not consistently enhance model generalization and may instead introduce noise or mild distributional shifts, leading to temporary performance fluctuations.

Finally, in Figure 6F-6I (Labs 6–9) and Figures 7F-7J,8F-8J (Labs 6–10), segmentation performance re-enters a steady improvement phase, characterized by progressively fewer misclassified pixels and continued gains in DSC and ASD. From Lab 8 onward, the models achieve consistently high segmentation accuracy, effectively labelling the majority of skeletal muscle pixels and clearly distinguishing them from surrounding kidney and intestinal tissues. The highest segmentation fidelity is observed in Lab 9 for the U-Net model and in Lab 10 for the attention U-Net and attention V-Net models, which achieve the best DSC and ASD values across all experimental groups.

Discussion

The cross-sectional area of skeletal muscle measured from CT images at the L3 level is an established biomarker for assessing nutritional status in patients with sarcopenia, cancer, cardiovascular disease, and those recovering from major surgery (1-8). Although numerous previous studies (21-41) have proposed DL models for L3 CT skeletal muscle segmentation and have routinely incorporated data augmentation in an empirical manner to enhance model robustness and generalizability, none have systematically investigated how the augmentation size itself influences model performance. In this study, we isolate augmentation size as an independent experimental variable—while keeping all other factors constant, including validation/test set composition, model architecture and training strategy, and evaluation metrics—to quantitatively evaluate how varying augmentation size affects model behaviour. Our findings offer new insights into the selection of appropriate augmentation sizes for DL-based skeletal muscle segmentation on L3 CT images.

In our experimental design, ten groups were constructed with augmentation sizes ranging from 0× to 9×. The performance of the three representative DL models across these groups (Tables 2-4) reveals several important findings. Across all three models, the absence of augmentation (Lab 1) resulted in the lowest performance, indicating that training solely on original data is insufficient for achieving strong generalization. Introducing even a small number of augmented samples (Labs 2–3) led to consistent improvements, highlighting the importance of augmentation for improving dataset diversity and model generalizability under limited data conditions.

As the augmentation size increased, model performance generally improved but reached a plateau. Across all three models, the best performance was observed in Labs 7–10, while performance gains became relatively modest after Lab 6, suggesting a clear diminishing-returns pattern. This behavior likely reflects the inherent limitation of augmentation as a form of data simulation, where excessive augmentation introduces limited additional variability. Based on these findings, we recommend an augmentation size of 6× or greater per original image as a practical guideline for L3 CT skeletal muscle segmentation. However, excessively large augmentation sizes may incur increased computational cost without substantial performance gains. Therefore, the optimal augmentation size should be determined in a task-specific manner, balancing segmentation performance, training efficiency, and available computational resources.

Beyond the above experimental findings, this study also provides broader implications for both medical image segmentation research and real-world clinical deployment. In recent years, the research community has increasingly emphasized the development of novel DL architectures—such as Transformer-based models (55), and diffusion-based models (56)—in pursuit of higher segmentation accuracy. Although these advanced architectures push methodological boundaries, they also introduce greater model complexity, including larger network parameters, higher computational demands, and longer inference times. Such characteristics pose challenges for clinical translation, as most hospital environments lack sufficient graphics processing unit (GPU) resources to support computationally intensive model development or deployment. Consequently, the gap between cutting-edge academic research and feasible clinical implementation continues to widen. In contrast, our study highlights the value of fundamental DL design considerations—specifically, the size of data augmentation—which can yield meaningful performance improvements without modifying model architectures or increasing inference computational cost. By systematically quantifying how augmentation size influences segmentation accuracy across three representative networks, our findings demonstrate that carefully tuned augmentation strategies provide a practical and resource-efficient pathway for enhancing model robustness and generalization. Importantly, this approach imposes no additional hardware requirements, making it well-suited for clinical settings with constrained computational capacity. These insights underscore that, alongside architectural innovation, foundational investigations such as data augmentation strategy remain essential for advancing the real-world utility and scalability of DL models in clinical applications.

There are three major limitations to this study. First, due to the scope of the IRB approval, the dataset was limited to 1,200 L3 CT images from 400 patients diagnosed with rectal or cervical cancer. Variations in disease types, CT imaging protocols, and body composition distributions may lead to differences in CT image texture, as well as in the contour and morphology of skeletal muscle at the L3 level. In future work, we plan to expand the dataset to include patients with a broader range of diseases, with the inclusion of non-oncologic populations, as well as a wider variety of imaging protocols and body composition profiles. This will enable a more comprehensive investigation of how these factors influence the selection of appropriate augmentation sizes. Second, the optimal augmentation size ≥6× identified in this study was derived from evaluations using only three representative CNN-based DL models for L3 skeletal muscle segmentation on pelvic CT scans. Future research will incorporate additional state-of-the-art segmentation networks from other domains, such as nnU-Net (57) and SegFormer (58), and will also include CT scans from other anatomical regions, such as the abdomen, to further validate the generalizability of our findings. Third, to ensure reproducibility across experimental groups, this study employed an offline data augmentation approach consisting of horizontal flipping, random translation, random scaling and random Gaussian noise addition. The diminishing-returns pattern observed after Lab 6 should be interpreted as specific to this offline augmentation regime. In contrast, on-the-fly (online) augmentation can introduce a broader range of data variations and combinations by applying different augmentation approaches probabilistically during model training. Future work will investigate how varying the application probabilities of different augmentation approaches in an online augmentation pipeline affects model performance and will further compare these results with those observed under offline augmentation settings. Fourth, in the preprocessing steps of the L3 CT images, pixel intensities within the range of −250 to 250 HU were empirically normalized to a range of 0 to 1. In future work, we will refine the windowing strategies (e.g., by adopting task-specific soft-tissue windows) to better facilitate DL-based skeletal muscle segmentation tasks.

Conclusions

In summary, the experimental results indicate that DL models benefit substantially from data augmentation for skeletal muscle segmentation on CT images at the L3 level. Furthermore, to ensure high segmentation accuracy and robust generalization performance, an augmentation size of 6× or greater per L3 CT image is recommended.
