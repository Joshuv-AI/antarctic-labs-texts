Methodological evaluation of the cross-cultural validation of psychiatric scales in Tunisia: about 43 doctoral theses in medicine
Authors: Ghassen Kharroubi, Ameni Ben Said, Hadhemi Cherif, Borhen Elloumi, Ines Cherif, Abir Baatout, Wafa Kabtni, Sami Dhif, Hamdi El Kefi, Abdelaziz Oumaya, Jihene Bettaieb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe cross-cultural validation of psychiatric scales is an essential step to ensure the relevance of assessment tools in specific cultural contexts. In Tunisia, several medical doctoral theses have focused on this field, but their methodological quality has not yet been systematically evaluated. This work aimed to assess the methodology of Tunisian medical theses dealing with the cross-cultural validation of psychiatric scales.MethodsWe conducted a study evaluating Tunisian theses on the cross-cultural validation of psychiatric scales. The analysis focused on translation steps as well as psychometric validation methods, using an evaluation grid designed based on international recommendations.ResultsA total of 43 theses concerning the validation of 50 scales were included in our analysis. For translation, direct translation was performed for all scales, while back-translation was applied to 43 scales. Indeed, during the translation process, an expert committee concerned 28 scales, whereas a pretest was administered for only 17. Regarding sampling to ensure psychometric validity, the size of the studied populations ranged from 58 to 2577 participants, and 37 scales used non-probabilistic sampling. An expert committee evaluated face validity for 22 scales and content validity for 42 scales, whereas a pretest was applied to 10 scales for face validity and 40 for content validity. On the psychometric level, internal consistency was generally satisfactory, with most alpha coefficients above 0.70. Temporal stability was examined in 29 scales, often using the ICC. Internal construct validity was studied for 38 scales, mainly through exploratory factor analysis and principal component analysis. External construct validity was less explored, with convergent validity evaluated in 23 scales and divergent validity in only 5. Finally, only 17 scales resulted in publications in indexed journals.ConclusionsThis study highlighted certain gaps in the scale validation process, particularly in some translation and psychometric validation steps. Standardization of procedures strengthened psychometric training, and better scientific dissemination of results are essential to ensure the reliability and usability of instruments adapted to the Tunisian context.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08236-1.

Background

Psychiatric scales play a major role in the clinical assessment of patients in psychiatry. Indeed, they contribute to the standardization and objectification of symptoms or mental states, allow both a global and specific evaluation of the patient’s condition, and facilitate diagnosis as well as monitoring of clinical progress [1]. They also provide a common language among healthcare professionals to describe the patient’s mental state, thereby improving care coordination [2]. Furthermore, they help to identify patients’ specific needs, which is essential for appropriate management [3]. The cross-cultural validation of psychiatric scales is a rigorous process aimed at ensuring that measurement tools are suitable, reliable, and valid within the targeted cultural context, which often differs from the original one [4]. This process ensures relevant and comparable psychiatric assessments. It takes into account the diversity of representations, expressions, and perceptions of mental illness around the world, ensuring that the tools actually measure the concepts they are intended to assess [5]. Cross-cultural validation generally involves translation followed by back-translation, as well as a process of cultural adaptation [6]. It also requires a rigorous selection of participants to ensure the representativeness of the studied groups. Moreover, the use of appropriate statistical analyses is essential to evaluate the reliability and validity of the validated tool [7]. All these steps aim to guarantee that the tool maintains its psychometric qualities in a cultural context different from that of its initial development.

In Tunisia, several medical theses undertaken within the four medical faculties have been dedicated to the adaptation and validation of standardized psychiatric tools. This dynamic responds to a real need for reliable diagnostic tools that are culturally adapted to the local context. It also aims to improve the quality of patient care while strengthening research capacities in mental health [8, 9]. Although some of these theses have been published in specialized scientific journals, reflecting the growing interest in psychiatric research and the desire to strengthen local knowledge in this field [10, 11], others remain confined to medical faculty libraries and remain largely unknown to mental health professionals. Additionally, there is currently no comprehensive list of validated scales for clinical or research use within medical thesis work. This gap constitutes an obstacle both for clinicians, who may lack appropriate tools adapted to the Tunisian context, and for researchers, whose work sometimes suffers from a lack of uniformity and methodological comparability. Cataloging thesis work on cross-cultural validation could help fill this gap. It would also prevent duplication of validation processes already completed and ensure standardization of diagnostic procedures. However, for these scales to allow rigorous objectification of symptoms or mental states, it is essential to guarantee the methodological quality of their validations [12]. Our work falls within this framework, aiming to assess the methodology of Tunisian medical theses dealing with the cross-cultural validation of psychiatric scales.

Methods

Study design

This study assessed the methodology used in Tunisian medical theses concerning the cross-cultural validation of psychiatric scales defended between 2000 and 2024.

Studied theses

Our study was exhaustive and covered all available medical doctoral theses that met the following eligibility criteria:

Inclusion criteria

The study included all medical doctoral theses that:

•Concerned the cross-cultural validation of psychiatric scales with translation into Modern Standard Arabic or Tunisian dialect

•Were defended between 2000 and 2024

•Belonged to one of the four Tunisian medical faculties (Tunis, Sfax, Sousse, Monastir)

•Were accessible in digital or paper format in faculty libraries

Exclusion criteria

We Excluded medical doctoral theses that:

•Concerned cross-cultural validation of psychiatric scales exclusively for pediatric populations

•Were dedicated to the original design of a psychometric tool

•Concerned the validation, in a Tunisian population, of a non-translated scale (in a foreign language)

•Addressed only one validation step, such as translation alone or validation of a preexisting Arabic version without an initial translation process

•Were defended outside the 2000–2024 period

•Were not available in digital or paper format in faculty libraries

Design of the evaluation grid for the cross-cultural validation process of psychiatric scales validated within the included theses

To strengthen the methodological approach of the analysis (design of the evaluation grid), we conducted a literature review on recommendations related to the different steps of the translation process (forward translation, synthesis of versions, backtranslation, expert committee, pretest, final adjustments) and the psychometric validation process (face validity, content validity, reliability (internal consistency, inter-item correlation, item-total correlation, temporal stability, inter-rater reliability), construct validity (internal and external structural validation)). This allowed the development of an evaluation grid for standardized analysis. Each item in the grid, referring to a process step, was rated with three options: “step done and compliant,” “step done but non-compliant,” and “step not done.” The conformity of each step was judged based on the recommendation cited by the thesis author or, if no citation was provided, on the synthesized widely recognized recommendations and standards. Data collection and analysis method

Data extraction and entry were performed by the research team who had undergone prior training. Each thesis was fully reviewed by one team member before being recorded in the database. To ensure accuracy, a single assessor conducted a double-check by comparing the database records against the source documents and then assessed the conformity of translation and psychometric validation processes for all scales based on the evaluation grid developed for this research.

Data were analyzed using Epi Info™ version 7.2 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Atlanta, GA, USA). First, a descriptive analysis was performed on general thesis information such as language of writing, faculty of origin, defense year, and number of validated scales or questionnaires.

Results

Analysis concerning the theses

A total of 46 theses were initially identified. Of these, two were excluded because they validated scales that had not been translated into Arabic (remaining in French), and one was excluded because it evaluated a pre-existing Arabic version without an initial translation process. No theses were excluded due to inaccessibility, leaving a final sample of 43 theses. Figure 1 represents the flowchart of the selection process of theses. Among the selected theses, 42 (98%) were written in French while only one thesis was written in English. Among the theses studied, 27 (63%) belonged to the Faculty of Medicine of Tunis, 9 (21%) to the Faculty of Medicine of Sousse, 4 (9%) to the Faculty of Medicine of Monastir, and 3 (7%) to the Faculty of Medicine of Sfax. All included theses were defended from 2007 onwards. When analyzing the 2000–2024 timeframe in five-year increments, the number of theses defended shows a steady upward trend. The number grew consistently from zero defenses in the initial period (2000–2004) to 3 (2005–2009), 7 (2010–2014), 15 (2015–2019), and finally peaked at 18 defenses between 2020 and 2024, despite this final period being impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic. Among the 50 scales studied, 25 (50%) were translated into Tunisian dialectal Arabic, 20 (40%) into Literary Arabic, and 5 (10%) into a combination of the two. In the latter, the main statement of the item was generally in Literary Arabic, while explanations or examples were provided in Tunisian dialectal Arabic.

Publication in indexed journals

For seventeen scales (34%), their cross-cultural validation processes were the subject of publications in indexed journals (16 as articles and one as an abstract).

Translation process

Scales with citation of the reference used for the translation process

41 scales (82%) were translated based on the reference cited in the theses. The steps of forward translation and backtranslation were applied to all scales, but with varying degrees of compliance with the cited reference. A synthesis of the translations was carried out only when multiple translators were involved. Sixteen scales (39%) were translated without involving an expert committee, and 14 (34%) were subjected to a pretest. Among the latter, five scales translated according to Beaton et al. and WHO guidelines [4, 6] had an insufficient sample size, as these references require at least 30 participants for the pretest step. Reliability

All the scales validated within the theses included in our study were subjected to a reliability assessment.

Internal consistency

The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated during the validation process for 48 scales (96%), with 7 values (15%) corresponding to a weak internal consistency. This coefficient was not calculated for two scales: for one, due to the presence of open-ended questions, and for the other, the reason was not specified, as inter-rater reliability analysis was prioritized. Among the 41 scales with a compliant alpha coefficient, 11 (27%) had a value ≥ 0.9.

Inter-item and item-total correlations

Inter-item and item-total correlations were calculated during the validation process for 25 (50%) and 20 (40%) scales, respectively. Compliance with recommendations is presented in Table VI. An analysis of correlations between different dimensions was conducted for four scales (8%).

Temporal stability

The study of temporal stability was conducted as part of the reliability study for 29 scales (58%) : a single measure of temporal stability was used for 18 scales (Pearson coefficient (r), Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), Cohen’s Kappa coefficient (κ), or t-test), two measures were used for 9 scales, and three types of measures were applied for one scale. For the temporal stability study of the remaining scale, only the Bland-Altman method was used. The ICC was the most used index (18/29), followed by r (8/29), t-test (8/29). and then κ (5/29). The compliance of the values obtained for these tests is presented in Table 6.

Inter-rater reliability

Inter-rater reliability was evaluated for 11 scales (22%). For ten of these scales, a single reliability measure (ICC, κ, or r) was calculated, whereas for the other scale, it was only indicated in the thesis that this step was completed with 100% identical responses. ICC was the most used index (6/10), followed by r (2/10) and κ (2/10). Compliance of the values obtained from these tests with the recommendations is presented in Table 6.

Construct validity

Construct validity was studied during the validation process of 45 scales (90%).

Internal construct validity

Internal construct validity was assessed during the validation of 38 scales (76%). Principal component analysis alone was applied to 11 scales; exploratory factor analysis alone was conducted for 5 scales and confirmatory factor analysis was used to evaluate the internal construct validity of one scale. For the remain 21 scales, a combination of exploratory factor analysis and principal component analysis was performed.

External construct validity

Convergent validity was evaluated for 23 scales (46%). Nearly half of these scales used a single assessment instrument (12/23), two instruments were used for the validation of 9 scales, and 3 instruments were used for the remaining 2 scales.

Divergent validity was evaluated in the validation process of five scales (10%): a single assessment instrument was used for the validation of 4 scales, while 3 instruments were used to study the divergent validity of the remaining scale.

In addition to the evaluation of convergent and divergent validity, other types of validity were explored such as external criterion validity (including concurrent validity) performed for 9 scales and internal divergent and convergent validities performed for one scale.

Discussion

Our work aimed to evaluate the methodology of medical doctoral theses defended in Tunisian Medicine faculties, focusing on the cross-cultural validation of psychiatric scales between 2000 and 2024. Through a comprehensive and systematic approach, we sought to analyze not only the rigor of the translation process but also the quality of the psychometric validation of the studied instruments. Our results showed that the analyzed theses adhered heterogeneously to international methodological recommendations. Some steps, such as forward translation and back translation, were generally well applied, whereas others, like the creation of an expert committee or conducting a pretest, were often neglected or partially implemented. Regarding psychometric validation, internal consistency measured by Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was the most frequently reported metric, while other essential aspects, such as temporal stability, inter-rater reliability, or construct validity, were less systematically explored. These observations reveal methodological gaps in some stages of the cross-cultural validation of scales.

There was a noticeable trend of a progressive increase in the number of theses on cross-cultural and psychometric validation of psychiatric instruments over the years, especially in the last decade. This trend reflects the growing need for reliable and locally adapted scales, enabling clinicians and researchers to use rigorous tools for assessing various psychiatric concepts [32].

Most theses in our study focused on validating a single scale (38 out of 43), while fewer addressed two or three instruments. This predominance reflects the complexity and time required for rigorous cross-cultural and psychometric validation, including translation, adaptation, and reliability and validity analyses [33]. However, the number of scales validated per thesis does not guarantee methodological quality. Some theses addressing multiple instruments may benefit from enhanced expertise and structured supervision, enabling reliable validation of several scales simultaneously. Often, theses with multiple scales involved tools addressing the same theme, studied comparatively or sharing the same study population. Thus, whether a thesis concerns one or multiple scales, the key factor is the rigor applied at each validation step.

Nearly three-quarters of the validated scales targeted major psychiatric disorders—primarily psychotic, bipolar, depressive, and anxiety disorders. This focus reflects both clinical priorities and the availability of internationally validated tools that facilitate adaptation [34]. Moreover, the “schizophrenic disorders” section of the CIDI was validated twice in different theses. It is important to verify if a scale has already undergone prior validation before initiating a new study. However, two seemingly similar validations may correspond to different processes involving distinct samples, cultural or linguistic contexts, or statistical methodologies. These differences can affect psychometric properties and justify new validation while allowing comparison and consolidation of existing results. Conversely, some frequent and clinically significant disorders remain little explored : no scale was validated in sexology, and only one scale addressed alcohol use disorder among addictive disorders, leaving notable gaps in some psychiatric thematic. Additionally, fewer scales addressed crucial psychosocial dimensions, such as stigma or quality of life, which are essential for comprehensive patient care. These findings highlight the need to broaden research scopes to develop and validate tools covering all psychiatric disorders and psychosocial dimensions.

Analysis of the original and source languages of validated scales showed English predominance. This trend reflects English’s central role in international scientific literature and the availability of competent translators in this language [34]. However, in five cases, the source language differed from the original language, potentially introducing methodological biases. Translation based on an already translated version risks loss of meaning or distortion of the original content.

Regarding target languages, results showed a nearly balanced split: 25 scales were translated into Tunisian Arabic dialect, 20 into formal Arabic, and 5 used a combination of both. Using the Tunisian dialect is particularly relevant, as it facilitates participant understanding and reflects everyday spoken language [35]. In contrast, formal Arabic, more standardized, allows comprehension across the Arab world, promoting intercultural comparability and regional dissemination of validated tools [35]. The combined approach (formal Arabic for items and dialect explanations or examples) found in five scales balances academic rigor and practical readability and could reduce participant misunderstanding. This strategy could be encouraged in future validations, especially for complex psychiatric concepts [36].

Among the 50 analyzed scales, only 17 were published in indexed journals (16 articles, one abstract). The presence of abstracts only suggests some authors presented their work at scientific conferences, constituting initial dissemination but limiting accessibility and lasting visibility. Publication in indexed journals guarantees scientific recognition and promotes tool dissemination beyond the local context. Thus, the fact that only about a third of validations were published underscores the importance of encouraging systematic publication to enhance the value of conducted work, avoid duplication, and strengthen the scientific credibility of cross-cultural validations in Tunisia [37–39].

Our results showed that the forward and back translation process was applied for all scales following specific translation recommendations and nearly all those that did not (7 out of 9). This aligns with most international recommendations [4, 33, 40–63] which usually advocate forward translation followed by back translation to ensure equivalence between original and target versions. Most recommend carrying out these two steps independently. For the two scales without back translation, this was not a methodological error, as some authors suggest direct translation alone or consider back translation optional [53, 64–68]. However, the methodological robustness of scales following both steps remains better [4, 42–44].

Regarding the number of translators, most recommendations suggest at least two translators for forward translation and two for back translation [4, 33, 40, 41, 45, 46, 48–51, 56] or at least different translators for each step [44, 53, 68]. Others recommend three translators forward and one back [43], and some set no minimum [52, 65]. In our study, among scales following specific recommendations requiring two translators and two back translators (Brislin, Beaton, WHO), 16 met the translator number and four met the back translator number out of 26 scales. Non-compliance with translator numbers was the main deviation factor, though using one translator/back translator is not a major methodological error, as most scales involved two different translators at each step. For scales requiring one translator and one back translator (Werner & Campbell, MAPI), compliance was 100%. Scales not mentioning specific recommendations generally complied with classic standards, mostly using different translators and back translators.

Our results showed that all scales with two translators involved a reconciliation step to obtain a final translated version. Most guidelines recommend this after forward translation to identify and resolve discrepancies [4, 33, 45, 49–51, 54, 57, 67]. Some suggest involving a third translator or one or more reviewers to merge versions [41, 42, 45, 46, 50, 60, 67] and providing supplementary material (guides, definitions, examples) to improve comprehension and translation accuracy [54].

Many directives recommend expert committees to ensure semantic, idiomatic, experiential, and conceptual equivalence of items [4, 33, 40, 41, 43, 48–50, 52, 57, 64, 69]. Overall, nearly half of the scales (22/50) were translated without an expert committee, a methodological gap partly explained by following initial recommendations that did not foresee this step. Although strongly recommended, absence of a committee does not invalidate translation but may reduce methodological robustness.

Most guidelines recommend pretests to adapt instruments to the target culture, evaluating item comprehension, detecting ambiguous phrasing, checking relevance, completeness, acceptability, and emotional impact [4, 33, 40–46, 48–54, 56–66, 68–71]. All recommendations on translation of psychometric scales include a pretest step. However, a pretest was performed for only 17 scales. Absence of this step does not invalidate translation but limits verification of item clarity, comprehension, and cultural adaptation, reducing process robustness. No consensus exists on pretest sample size—which ranges from 5 to 8 [45, 48] to 30–40 [4, 41, 46, 57] or even 25–75 participants [65]. In our study, pretest sample sizes ranged from 10 to 84 participants, generally meeting recommendations. Nonetheless, referring to key translation recommendations guiding our evaluation, 7 of 17 scales performing a pretest had insufficient participants, highlighting non-compliance again.

To evaluate sample sizes for psychometric validation, we based on Comrey and Lee [16], and Tabachnick and Fidell [17] recommendations, suggesting at least five subjects per item or a minimum of 100 participants. Literature suggests over 100 participants to assess internal consistency, measurement error, reliability, test construct validity hypotheses, and subgroup comparisons [52]; others recommend 100–200 respondents [65] or at least 200 participants [70]; some suggest a ratio of ten participants per instrument item [33]. Overall, sample sizes in our study align with most international recommendations, indicating methodological rigor and quality.

Most validation processes analyzed used non-probabilistic, mainly convenience sampling (37 of 50). Probabilistic sampling is recommended because it ensures sample representativeness and generalizability [72–74]. However, convenience sampling remains common for practical reasons and is acceptable for exploratory studies if limitations are acknowledged [75, 76]. Although probabilistic sampling was rarely used, convenience sampling does not represent a major methodological flaw but reduces robustness and generalizability.Regarding studied populations, researchers often select students or health professionals rather than the general population due to accessibility, homogeneity, and motivation facilitating data collection and item comprehension. This limits representativeness and generalizability, as sociodemographic, cultural, or clinical characteristics may differ from the target population.

Our study revealed that face validity was checked in 26 of 50 scales. This is relatively low, showing nearly half lacked this crucial step. Most assessments were by expert committees, following classic recommendations emphasizing expert opinion to ensure item relevance, clarity, and cultural fit [4, 18]. In contrast, only 10 scales performed a pretest, which tests understanding and acceptability on a small representative sample to detect ambiguities or inappropriate phrasing [19, 21]. Literature states that if face validity is not assessed (neither by experts nor pretest), methodological robustness is limited as it remains unknown if items are understandable and relevant to the target population [4, 20]. Expert evaluation is the key step: its absence may impair validation, whereas lack of pretest alone does not invalidate but reduces practical reliability. Thus, for nearly half of validations lacking face validity check, the validation process is less robust, highlighting the need to standardize this step.

Content validity was assessed for 42 of 50 scales which is relatively high compared to face validity. In all these scales, content validity was evaluated by an expert committee with expert agreement above 70% for most items, consistent with literature recommendations [73, 77–79]. However, less than half the scales were compliant on the pretest (16 of 40). This was primarily due to small sample sizes, as standard recommendations require at least 30 participants to ensure relevance and representativeness. According to international work, content validity is essential to ensure items comprehensively and relevantly cover the studied concept [21, 79]. Its absence or incompleteness compromises psychometric validation robustness, as omission or misformulation bias impacts item relevance, clarity, and completeness.

All scales in our study evaluated reliability, mainly via Cronbach’s alpha, the reference method for internal consistency assessment [23, 24]. Among 48 scales for which this indicator has been calculated, weak internal consistency was observed for 7 scales. On the other hand, a Cronbach’s alpha ≥ 0.9 was found in 11 cases, indicating excellent internal consistency. However, some authors warn that too high Cronbach’s alpha may reflect excessive item redundancy, meaning some questions measure the exact same aspect [22, 80]. Therefore, very high alpha should be cautiously interpreted. For the two scales without alpha calculation, their methodological validity is not necessarily compromised, as other reliability forms (inter-rater, test-retest) can suffice depending on instrument nature, if properly applied and documented [24, 81].

Inter-item and item-total correlations complement Cronbach’s alpha for internal consistency evaluation. Our study found inter-item correlations in 25 scales and item-total in 20, mostly conforming to recommended thresholds (r ≥ 0.30) [23, 24]. These analyses identify items correlating weakly with total scores or each other, indicating possible redundancy or lack of scale homogeneity [24]. Literature states item-total correlations below 0.30 signal low contribution to construct measurement, warranting revision or exclusion [22, 23]. Conversely, excessively high correlations suggest item redundancy, limiting content diversity [1]. Only 4 scales analyzed correlations between dimensions, a limited number, yet this step is vital in multidimensional instruments to ensure subscales measure distinct but related constructs [30]. Omitting inter-item or item-total correlations could weaken the validation process by missing an important psychometric quality indicator.

Temporal stability or test-retest reliability is crucial as it checks if an instrument produces consistent results when administered repeatedly under similar conditions [1]. This property was evaluated in 29 scales, with variable indices used. Intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC, n = 18) was most used and is the reference measure for stability, accounting for within- and between-subject variability [25]. Pearson’s r (n = 8) and Cohen’s kappa (κ, n = 5) were also used, along with paired t-tests (n = 8). However, r and t-test have limits: r ignores systematic differences; a non-significant t-test does not guarantee good agreement [24]. κ suits categorical variables but is influenced by response distribution. Literature recommends thresholds of 0.70 or higher for ICC, κ, and r to accept stability [25]. Most ICC results met this, confirming good robustness of temporal stability. Variability in methods reflects lack of standardization in validation practices. Indeed, the absence of temporal stability measure, found in 21 of the 50 scales studied, may deprive validation of an essential reliability dimension, especially for clinical repeated use.

Inter-rater reliability was assessed in only 11 scales, a rather limited number. Most studies used a single reliability index (ICC, r, or κ), meeting basic methodological recommendations but limiting robustness [1, 25]. ICC was most used (n = 6), consistent with its status as reference for estimating agreement among raters on continuous data [25, 82]. Cohen’s kappa (n = 2) suits categorical data but has known limitations including sensitivity to prevalence and asymmetrical response distributions. Use of Pearson’s r (n = 2) is methodologically questionable, as r measures linear association rather than true agreement [83]. One study reported perfect concordance (100% identical) without statistical measure, insufficient methodologically as lack of standardized quantitative indices prevents comparison and threatens validity. Overall, reported values met recommendations, indicating good quality standards application. However, the low use of this step (11 of 50) suggests inter-rater reliability remains a frequently neglected but essential aspect when multiple raters are involved.

Internal construct validity was assessed for 38 of 50 scales, showing notable effort to examine theoretical instrument structure. However, methods used were heterogeneous and variably compliant with methodological standards. Principal component analysis (PCA) was frequently performed (alone in 11 scales, combined with exploratory factor analysis (EFA) in 21 scales). Though widely used in validation studies, PCA is primarily a data reduction method rather than true factor analysis and may not adequately verify latent constructs [29, 83, 84]. EFA remains the most appropriate for identifying underlying dimensions but was less applied. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), the gold standard for testing an a priori model and assessing data fit, was used in only one scale. This low utilization marks a major methodological shortcoming, as without CFA, confirming structural validity is difficult [83, 85, 86]. Most theses applied Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO, n = 33) and Bartlett’s test (n = 32) with near-total compliance to recommended thresholds (KMO > 0.6, Bartlett p < 0.05), indicating good data quality for factor analyses [27, 87]. Variance explained was reported in 35 scales, with 15 meeting the >60% recommended threshold, suggesting extracted dimensions often insufficiently capture construct variance.

Convergent validity was evaluated across 23 scales; for nearly half of these (12 out of 23), a single comparison instrument was utilized. Methodological guidelines recommend multiple reference measures to strengthen evidence and reduce bias from single comparator limitations [31, 83, 88]. Using a single tool narrows conclusion scope, especially if that instrument has psychometric weaknesses.

Divergent validity was much less studied: only 5 validations assessed it, mostly with one instrument (4 scales) and only once with three different scales. This under-exploration is common but constitutes a methodological gap, as divergent validity is essential to demonstrate that the tool measures a construct distinct from related variables [89, 90]. Other complementary approaches identified include external criterion validity, including concurrent validity, in nine scales, and internal convergent and divergent validity in one scale. These indicate attempts to enrich psychometric validation but remain minority activities. Overall, external construct validity was partially evaluated, focusing mainly on convergent validity, but with limited single-instrument use weakening evidence robustness. Divergent validity, though complementary and necessary to show specificity, was largely neglected. These findings echo literature emphasizing that lack or limited external validity evaluation reduces generalizability and credibility of psychometric results [88–91]. Nevertheless, some validation studies do not systematically assess divergent validity, particularly in transcultural adaptation processes where the primary objective is often to preserve the psychometric properties of an already established instrument. Although the absence of divergent validity assessment may reduce the methodological robustness of the validation process, it does not necessarily invalidate the adaptation, especially when other psychometric properties such as reliability and convergent validity are adequately demonstrated. In contrast, during the development of a new scale, neglecting divergent validity may constitute a major methodological limitation because it weakens evidence that the instrument captures a unique and theoretically distinct construct [92].

Strengths and Limitations

Our study’s strengths include its exhaustive coverage of all theses meeting eligibility over more than twenty years, and the development of a standardized evaluation grid allowing homogeneous analysis. This grid, based on a synthesis of international recommendations, can serve as a reference for future cross-cultural psychiatric scale validations in Tunisia or elsewhere. In clinical and research practice, using a translated scale requires prior verification of validation rigor and fit with the target population to guarantee result relevance and avoid biased interpretations. Accordingly, our work also produced summary sheets for each studied scale (Additional file 1), a practical tool so clinicians and researchers can quickly access reliable, comparative data to select suitable instruments for their goals and contexts.

However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. Theses in child psychiatry were not considered due to the specific characteristics of the pediatric population, which require tailored instruments and methodologies [93], and because our study focuses on the adult population. Similarly, validation studies of scales conducted outside of medical theses were excluded, as the aim was to concentrate the analysis on theses as a clearly defined unit of study. Furthermore, as there are no universal psychometric rules or strict guidelines, threshold choices may influence compliance ratings. To minimize this impact, we selected the thresholds and criteria most strongly supported by the existing literature. Finally, the evaluation was performed by a single assessor and without proceeding to inter-rater reliability of the evaluation grid and, which may introduce a risk of judgment error, although the use of a systematic checklist helped to minimize this bias.

Conclusions

Our work revealed growing interest in adapting and validating psychiatric tools in Tunisia, but methodological rigor remains improvable. In fact, our findings highlighted certain gaps in the scale validation process, particularly in some translation and psychometric validation steps. These shortcomings call for standardizing approaches, harmonizing practices, and strengthening cross-cultural psychometric validation skills to ensure the reliability and usability of instruments adapted to the Tunisian context.
