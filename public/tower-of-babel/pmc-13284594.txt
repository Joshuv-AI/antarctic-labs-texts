Polypharmacy and Bone Health in Patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus: A Narrative Review
Authors: Israa O. Kashmoola, Shatha H. Mohammad, Mohammad H. Alsaaty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) commonly demonstrate reduced bone quality and elevated fracture risk rates despite having normal or elevated bone mineral density (BMD). Beyond the pathophysiological effect of diabetes, polypharmacy has emerged as a potential, yet underappreciated contributor of skeletal fragility. To explore this association, a thorough search of the literature was conducted using PubMed, Google Scholar, and Web of Science. and Scopus for articles published in English language until October 2025. Studies addressing diabetes, polypharmacy, bone health, and drug-related skeletal outcomes were identified and included. A divergent effect on bone health was evident in patients using antidiabetic agents, with thiazolidinediones, sulfonylurea and some insulin regimens increase fracture risk. Whereas metformin, incretin-based therapies and sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors appear neutral or protective. Concerning non-antidiabetic medication used in T2DM, glucocorticoids, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, proton pump inhibitors and loop diuretics are among the drugs that worsen the bone architecture, causing increased fracture risk. On the other hand, statins, β-blockers, angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors, thiazide diuretics and angiotensin receptor antagonists are bone protective. Such cumulative impact of polypharmacy involves drug-drug interactions, increased fall potential, and attenuation of antiresorptive therapy. Clinical interventions to prevent skeletal deterioration include periodic medications review, deprescribing of high-risk agents, calcium and vitamin D supplementation, and life-style modifications. Personalized and multidisciplinary strategies are essential to balance metabolic status with long-term skeletal preservation in patients with T2DM exposed to polypharmacy.

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic condition that is growing rapidly in prevalence, making it a major global healthcare concern. One of the most common forms of diabetes, type 2 DM (T2DM), affects people of all ages and accounts for more than 90% of cases.[1] Uncontrolled T2DM frequently results in hyperglycemia, which by time extremely harm many bodily systems.[2] Besides the well-known complications of T2DM, risk of bone strength impairment is also increase by 40% to 70%, although having a normal or high bone mineral density (BMD), proposing that other issues besides bone quantity must explain the increased bone fragility.[3] The progressive nature of the disease urges the need for combination therapy to achieve glycemic goal via combining oral agents from different classes or using insulin alongside other medications. Moreover, the majority of patients are presented with comorbidities such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, obesity, cardiovascular problems, liver impairment and kidney problems. Each of these disorders requires its own medications in addition to the antidiabetic agents, which subject the patients to polypharmacy.[4]

Polypharmacy is the continuous use of five or more drugs simultaneously. This involves the patient’s usage of prescription, over-the-counter, conventional, and complementary medications.[5] Importantly, such long-term exposure of diabetic patients to numerous drugs does not only influence metabolic profile, but may also affect organ systems, including skeleton.[6] Despite evident recognition that diabetes itself modulates bone metabolism and fracture risk, much less is known about the role of polypharmacy as a contributing factor in patients with T2DM. Additionally, results from recent studies are conflicting and requires further investigations. This gap of knowledge provides rationale for this review, to examining the biological and clinical pathways linking T2DM, polypharmacy, and bone health. Beside this, the insights that will be provided by this review may guide the clinicians in balancing the benefits of polypharmacy for metabolic and cardiovascular outcomes with its possible unintended consequences on skeletal health.

METHODS

Research databases like PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar were used to search the literature. After then, words or phrases were used to abstract data from these publications and the link between them such as DM, diabetes complications, bone health, polypharmacy, multiple medications, consequences of polypharmacy in diabetes, outcomes of polypharmacy on bone health, current antidiabetic agents, and management of diabetes complications, that were published in English until October 2025. Specific filters were applied to narrow the inclusion, such as research article, review article, systematic review, meta-analysis, and old age. Title and abstract screening were used to determine each paper’s eligibility. We manually searched the reference lists of a few chosen articles in addition to the database search. No formal quantitative synthesis or meta-analysis was conducted due to the narrative design of this review. Instead, the available literature was examined qualitatively to provide an integrated overview of how antidiabetic and non-antidiabetic medications, when used concurrently in patients with T2DM, may collectively influence bone metabolism and overall bone health.

1. Bone status in DM

Fracture is a common complication of DM, especially those with longer diabetes duration, vascular complications, and due to the use of specific medications.[7] BMD is a usual significant indicator of bone health and a primary predictor of fracture risk. A low BMD means weaker bones and is often indicative of osteoporosis.[8] Evidence stares that T2DM patients routinely reveal average or augmented BMD levels compared to those with type 1 DM (T1DM) or those without T2DM; nevertheless, they experience inappropriate risk of fractures. This highlights that skeletal fragility in T2DM is more a matter of bone quality than bone quantity, and indicates an intricate interplay of other factors.[9]

2. Pathophysiological mechanism of bone fragility in T2DM

It is anticipated that patients with T2DM will have fewer osteoblasts, more osteoids, and a slower pace of bone production. Patients with T2DM have reduced bone growth in their cancellous, intracortical, and endocortical surfaces, but not in those with T1DM.[10] However, some data suggest that in addition to the reduction of the activation frequency of the bone remodeling units, an increase in bone mineralization may be present. This complex diabetic paradox may be attributed to factors such as aging, hyperinsulinemia, hyperglycemia, oxidative stress, inflammation, increase advanced glycation end products (AGEs), decreased levels of hormones that promote bone formation, obesity and chronic kidney disease (CKD).[11]

One of the major risk factors for fractures, low bone mass, and bone fragility is aging.[12] On the other hand, hyperinsulinemia is responsible for increased BMD, due to insulin anabolic effect. In hyperinsulinemia, insulin, because of its structural similarity, can interact with the insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) receptor found on osteoblasts. IGF-1 controls skeletal tissue metabolism by regulating the process of osteogenesis, and it has been demonstrated to have a substantial positive association with BMD. [13] In order accomplish this, the IGF axis regulates the activity of various cell types, including osteoblasts, osteoclasts, osteoprogenitor cells and osteocytes. However, as T2DM progresses, the situation often followed with insulin resistance, hyperglycemia and structural deterioration of bone quality.[14]

Regarding reactive oxygen species (ROS), high levels can negatively affect the quality of bone and predispose to fracture risk, through lowering the receptor activator for nuclear factor κ-B ligand (RANKL) expression and enhancement of greater osteoclast formation.[15] Together with ROS, hyperglycemia boosts protein glycation, with the formation of AGEs.[16] The AGEs trigger inflammatory cytokines by binding to a variety of receptors, particularly the receptor for AGEs (RAGE). Additionally, there is proof that AGEs cause osteoblast apoptosis, which lowers osteoblast counts and hinders the production of new bone.[17] Increased expression of proinflammatory cytokines like tumor necrosis factor-α, which decreases osteoblast development and activity and promotes osteoblast death, is another way that hyperglycemia may impact bone. Runt-related transcription factor 2 expression is decreased and bone production is inhibited by elevated glucose levels. Moreover, it hinders the formation of a mineralized matrix, and elevated glucose also reduces osteoblast viability. Another way to decrease bone mass is via increasing peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPAR-γ) activation, which encourages mesenchymal stem cell adipogenesis at the expense of bone production.[18,19]

The prevalence of obesity in T2DM could be another contributor to poor skeletal health.[20] For instance, increased lean soft tissue mass and body weight can have beneficial mechanical loading effects on weight-bearing skeletal locations, despite being linked to obesity and T2DM. However, bone resorption may be accelerated by the concurrent rise in proinflammatory cytokines and circulating adipokines, especially those released from visceral adipose tissue storage.[21] Additionally, buildup of bone marrow adiposity, which happens with obesity, can negatively impacts on bone health.[22] Likewise, in patients with T2DM, CKD disturbs homeostasis of minerals resulting in bone and mineral disorders. This combination leads to microvascular complications, with CKD-bone and mineral disorder; which is distinguished by abnormal mineral metabolism, bone abnormalities and vascular calcification. [23] Th combination of T2DM and CKD may also result in vitamin D deficiency, due to inability of the diseased kidneys to activate it, leading to reduced bone quality.[24]

3. Polypharmacy in diabetic patients

Polypharmacy is common in patients with T2DM for various reasons; including multimorbidity, age-related pharmacokinetic variability, psychiatric disorders and over-thecounter medications and herbal supplements. These medications can lead to higher risk of adverse outcomes, drug-drug interactions, non-adherence, in addition to negative influence health outcomes. Therefore, polypharmacy should be an area of serious concern in T2DM, and healthcare providers can assist in spotting polypharmacy and minimizing drugs to improve the outcome of diabetes care.[25]

4. Effect of antidiabetic drugs on bone health

While most of antidiabetic drugs have neutral or even favorable effects, others may have negative impacts the bone metabolism. By functioning as agonists of PPAR-γ, thiazolidinediones (TZDs) improve insulin sensitivity. PPAR-γ regulates the differentiation of hematopoietic and mesenchymal cells in bone. Unbalanced bone remodeling results from TZD-induced PPAR-γ activation.[26] Additionally, TZDs drive higher bone marrow adiposity, decreased activity of aromatase, and promoted differentiation of osteoclast, all of that increase resorption and fracture of bones.[27] A retrospective study on 198 Chinese people showed that bone loss in postmenopausal women using TZDs was more significant in lumbar spine and right hip. [28] Another study found that adipose content was considerably higher in the fracture callus of rosiglitazone-treated animals compared to control mice that did not receive rosiglitazone, suggesting deleterious effect of rosiglitazone on healing of endochondral fracture through lowering osteogenesis and augmenting adipogenesis.[29]

Metformin, the most frequently used oral antidiabetic, is believed to sensitize cells to insulin in patients with T2DM. Although results of previous studies are not entirely consistent, metformin has been demonstrated to enhance type 1 collagen production in cell culture as well as osteoblast proliferation and differentiation, producing possible protective effect on bone. Moreover, it seems to inhibit differentiation of adipocytes and promote differentiation of osteoblasts.[30] A previous examination of metabolite levels was discovered that metformin restored the levels of glutamate and succinate, which were significantly elevated in diabetic animals, to normal in bone marrow samples taken from healthy and diabetic mice treated with either phosphate-buffered saline or metformin, indicating beneficial effects on bone health.[31] On the other hand, conflicting results were obtained from a meta-analysis systematic review of randomized controlled trials, which studied the bone health of metformin users in a total of 3,394 participants over 15 investigations. Metformin showed little to no impact on BMD, according to the data. Additionally, there was no apparent alteration in bone alkaline phosphatase, osteocalcin, or osteoprotegerin. Metformin also caused reductions in type I collagen’s C-terminal telopeptide and N-terminal propeptide.[32] The Study of Women’s Health Across the Nation (SWAN) looked at the long-term modifications of BMD in midlife women beginning metformin and discovered that these women’s BMD changes were substantially similar to those of other women who did not begin metformin.[33]

Sulfonylureas, work by increasing insulin secretion from the pancreas, are another class antidiabetics. They have been associated with high risk of hypoglycemia, resulting in falls and fractures.[34] The GRADE system suggests that administration of sulfonylureas may heighten the risk of falls and hip fracture in individuals aged more than 65 years and in subjects with established hypoglycemia.[35] While glibenclamide was included in some of these studies, recent research suggests that glibenclamide may protect against aging through metabolic-epigenetic mechanisms, which could be relevant to age-related bone loss. Since aging is a risk factor for osteoporosis, this anti-aging effect may indirectly benefit bone health.[36] Additionally, evaluating the effects of gliclazide on bone loss in male albino Wistar rats, showed decreased bone loss with decreased neutrophils, macrophage and inflammatory response.[37]

Incretin-based therapies (glucagon like peptide 1 receptor agonists [GLP-1RAs] and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors [DPP-4i]) are agents that accelerate glucose absorption by activating incretin hormones, which induce insulin secretion from the pancreas.[38] The anti-osteoporotic effect of sitagliptin, a DPP-4i, was investigated in two recent studies, and revealed anti-osteoporotic effect, with the ability to suppress inflammation while promoting osteoblast formation.[39,40] Additionally, a meta-analysis and systematic review concerning the impacts of GLP-1RAs on bone metabolism in T2DM reported that these agents can improve the density of bone minerals in the femoral neck and lumbar spine of patients with T2DM, beside protection of skeletal health by stopping bone resorption and supporting bone formation.[41]

Concerning selective inhibitor of sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2), they exert their hypoglycemic effects by reducing glucose re-absorption via inhibiting SGLT2 in kidneys’ proximal tubules, thus encouraging the excretion of urine glucose and minimizing blood glucose. [42] SGLT2 inhibitors do not markedly impact BMD or increase fracture risk, with some evidence even suggesting a potential reduction in fracture risk in certain studies. A Korean nationwide cohort study among diabetic population susceptible for bone fragility showed that SGLT2 inhibitors were not connected with the increased risk of fractures when compared with DDP-4i and GLP-1RAs.[43] Evidence from assessing the efficacy of empagliflozin, a SGLT2 inhibitor, in 100 diabetic patients with osteoporosis revealed that after 6 months of therapy, patients presented significantly higher levels of BMD, serum phosphorus, and calcium in comparison to the control group. Moreover, the incidence of fractures was 2% in the intervention group and 16.33% in the control group following a 12-month follow-up, indicating that empagliflozin has a beneficial therapeutic effect in patients with diabetes osteoporosis.[44] The only exception is increased fracture risk associated with canagliflozin treatment, shown by CANVAS patients. However, the patients in this investigation were older, had a history of cardiovascular disease (CVD), and had higher baseline diuretic use and a lower baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate.[45]

Regarding exogenous insulin’s effect on bone, studies showing both potential anabolic effects and increased fracture risk, possibly due to hypoglycemia, falls, weight gain, and the underlying severity of the patient’s diabetes. [46–48]

5. Effects of non-antidiabetic drugs on bone in diabetic patients

Non-antidiabetic agents may have a range of effects on bone. While some agents may protect the skeletal system, others are accompanied with major risk of osteoporosis or fracture.[6] Antidiabetic medications are often taken with multiple other drug classes, such as corticosteroids, statins, antihypertensive agents, antidepressants, anticoagulants, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), and various over-the-counter supplements and herbal products. Concerning corticosteroids, in general they are not prescribed for T2DM because they worsen glycemic control by increasing insulin resistance and stimulating liver glucose production, making diabetes harder to manage.[49] However, they are sometimes necessary to treat other serious inflammatory or autoimmune conditions (like rheumatoid arthritis or asthma) in patients with T2DM, even if it temporarily destabilizes their blood sugar.[50,51] A matched cohort study used two large UK databases to study patients ≥40 years old initiated inhaled corticosteroids or long-acting bronchodilator for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, with their relation to the development and progression of diabetes and osteoporosis, found that the risks of diabetes onset, diabetes progression, and osteoporosis were all significantly increased.[52] Furthermore, a retrospective, cross-sectional study of 477 American patients with T2DM, undergoing oral glucocorticoid therapy, showed that the trabecular bone scores (TBS) of L1–L4 and lumbar spine were significantly less in diabetics than in nondiabetic group.[53]

Statins are widely prescribed to diabetic patients to control dyslipidemia. Although these agents show marked reduction in the risk of CVD, research results are mixed, with some studies showing increased BMD and reduced fracture risk, while others suggest no significant benefit or even a potential decrease in bone quality. However, it is reported that these agents may have positive effects on bone health by promoting bone formation and potentially reducing bone resorption.[54] A recent meta-analysis of 12 studies showed no effect on reducing the fracture rates at the hip region, although the likelihood of osteoporotic fractures in the vertebral region was reduced by 18% with the use of different statins.[55] Granat and colleagues [56] focused on the role of simvastatin on bone tissue metabolism, and revealed the ability of drug in demonstrating effective potential in avoiding osteoporosis and helping in bone defect healing, however only at increased doses (20 mg/kg/day). A Korean population-based cohort study reported that patients who received higher-potency statin (rosuvastatin ≥10 mg/day, atorvastatin ≥20 mg/day or simvastatin ≥40 mg/day within 30 days) had lower risk of osteoporosis and fracture in comparison to lower-potency statin.[57]

Concerning antihypertensive drugs, the choice of medication depends on individual patient factors and may involve combination therapy to achieve blood pressure goals.[58,59] Antihypertensive drugs have varied impacts on bone health in patients with diabetes; thiazide diuretics, β1-selective blockers and RAAS blockers may improve BMD or slow bone loss over time, resulting in lower fracture risk. In contrast, calcium channel blockers (CCBs), α-blockers and some loop diuretics may increase fracture risk, possibly due to their influence on calcium balance and an increased risk of falls.[60–62]

Patients with T2DM are at high need for prescribing antidepressants.[63] Depression undoubtedly increases the chance of developing metabolic problems, such as hyperglycemia and dyslipidemia, because prolonged stress and hypercortisolism disrupt the immune system and neural plasticity. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) have been linked to reduced BMD and increased fracture risk, potentially due to their effect on bone metabolism and increased falls. For individuals with diabetes or a history of osteoporosis, SSRI use warrants caution, prompting a proactive evaluation of BMD by clinicians.[64,65] However, a meta-analysis that evaluated the the relationship between the use of tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) or SSRIs and BMD in women, revealed no association between the use of TCAs or SSRIs and lower or higher BMD.[66] Similarly, identifying individuals that initiated treatment for depression with sertraline, citalopram, or escitalopram between 2007 and 2019 in the Danish nationwide registers revealed no relation between the use of SSRIs and osteoporosis risk.[67]

Regarding anticoagulants, they are routinely used in diabetic patients with atrial fibrillation to prevent thromboembolic events like stroke. Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) like apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran and edoxaban are often preferred over older vitamin K antagonists, such as warfarin, due to their more predictable profiles, fewer drug interactions, and potentially better renal outcomes. [68] While anticoagulants help manage thrombotic risk, it’s crucial to choose the right type and dosage for individuals with diabetes, considering their potential effect on glycemic status, bone and other metabolic and organ health. While warfarin’s effect on bone density and fracture risk has been debated, evidence suggests DOACs, independent of vitamin K, may offer better bone health outcomes, with some studies showing reduced risk of osteoporosis and fracture with DOACs. However, the specific impact of different DOACs can vary, and the overall clinical choice involves balancing fracture risk with other patient factors. [69,70]

PPIs contribute to polypharmacy when they are inappropriately or unnecessarily prescribed to T2DM patients, especially old ages.[71] PPIs are linked to a higher risk of fractures and osteoporosis due to their potential effects on BMD, particularly in patients using glucocorticoids. While the exact mechanisms are debated, proposed causes include reduced calcium absorption, altered osteoclast function, impaired vitamin B12 absorption, and changes in phosphate and magnesium metabolism.[72] For patients with T2DM, a 2020 study found a high risk of hip fracture in PPI users.[73] Recent analyses of 27 observational studies and randomized controlled trials, which assessed the relationship between bone metabolism outcomes and PPIs use, has also highlighted the potential concerns for the relation between long-term PPI use and alterations in BMD, hypomagnesemia, increased fracture risk, and dental implant failure.[74] The impact of antidiabetic and non-antidiabetic agents on bone health with suggested mechanism is summarized in Table 1.

6. Cumulative impact of polypharmacy on bone health

The impact of polypharmacy on bone health in patients with T2DM extends beyond the effect of each drug individually, highlighting a complex interplay of drug interactions, increased risk of fall, masking the effect of bone-protective therapy, medication non-adherence and the liability to frailty and multimorbidity. All these factors augment the possibility of fracture risk, rendering polypharmacy a major concern in the management of diabetic-bone disorders. Understanding how polypharmacy affects T2DM patients is principally important, particularly due to its association with inappropriate multidrug administration, negative quality of life and health outcomes in addition to increased adverse drug reactions.[75]

7. Drug-drug interactions

The increased fracture risk due to concurrent use of multitherapy could be attributed to drug-drug interactions. Cross-sectional data from a prospective single-center cohort (2015–2022) of patients with inflammatory rheumatic and musculoskeletal diseases revealed significant correlation between concurrent use of corticosteroids and PPIs with increased osteoporosis, which could be due to impaired calcium absorption and bone mineralization.[76] Likewise, steroid-induced osteonecrosis has shown to be augmented in Japanese white rabbits receiving intramuscular methylprednisolone acetate and warfarin concomitantly, which could be attribute to reduced vitamin K-dependent osteocalcin activation and accelerated bone resorption.[77] Additionally, analyzing the data of commonly used medications on human osteoblastic cell activity in a list of medications records of old age patients revealed that the use of metformin, metoprolol and rosuvastatin inhibited cell mineralization and affected bone health adversely.[78]

8. Increase risk of fall

Falls, due to hypoglycemia or balance disturbance, represent the most frequent pathway linking polypharmacy to fracture risk in patients with T2DM. Concerning antidiabetic agents, utilization of insulin, sulfonylureas or canagliflozin further increases the risk of fracture due to hypoglycemia.[79] Concomitant use of certain sedative, hypnotic or antidepressant medications was moderately associated with higher risk of bone fractures.[80] Similarly, antihypertensive agent such as CCBs, loop diuretics and α-blockers may be associated with orthostatic hypotension, syncope and increased risk of falls, especially when used with other hypoglycemic agents and sedative-hypnotics.[81,82] Such concomitant use of fall risk medications simultaneously needs further assessment and further medication review.[83]

9. Interference with bone protection therapy

Treatments for osteoporosis include antiresorptive (bisphosphonates; such as risedronate, alendronate, zoledronic acid, pamidronate, ibandronate, and the RANKL protein blocker; denosumab) and osteoanabolic (teriparatide, abaloparatide, romosozumab) agents.[84] PPIs may reduce the GIT absorption of bisphosphonates, by increasing gastric pH which is needed for the drugs to dissolve properly before absorption, attenuating their antifracture effect.[85] Additionally, the antiresorptive therapy may be counteracted during the concomitant use of corticosteroids.[86] Similarly, interactions have been noticed between vitamin D or bisphosphonate supplementations when they are simultaneously used with warfarin. A retrospective cohort study, which examined serum levels of vitamin D in 89 persons receiving a fixed dose of warfarin for more than 3 months, found that vitamin D level might impact the sensitivity of warfarin and maintenance dose requirement, especially in patients with vitamin D deficiency. [87] Additionally, examining the pharmacodynamic effect of bisphosphonates on warfarin kinetics and clinical effects in patients undergoing knee arthroplasty reported an increased warfarin bleeding, indicating an interaction between these two drugs with reduce patient’s adherence to bone protective therapy.[88]

10. Clinical implications and future directions

Routine screening for osteoporosis is advised in patients with T2DM, especially those who are receiving polypharmacy, due to the synergistic effects of diabetes, aging, and multiple medications on skeletal fragility. Standard systems such as dual energy X-ray absorptiometry scan remain the cornerstone for evaluation of BMD. However, in diabetic patients, fracture risk may be underestimated by BMD alone because diabetes impairs bone quality through mechanisms like increased cortical porosity and impaired microarchitecture, even when BMD is normal or high.[89] Factors like poor glycemic control, AGEs, and a higher risk of falls due to complications like neuropathy also contribute to a higher fracture risk that BMD alone does not capture.[90] Adjunct tools such as TBS can improve identification of individuals at risk. A low TBS is partially independent of clinical risk factors and BMD, suggests deteriorated bone microarchitecture, and predicts osteoporotic fracture.[91] Likewise, diabetes-adjusted fracture risk calculators (such as FRAX), could be a good tool for determining fracture in some cases without the need for measuring BMD.[92] On the other hand, deprescribing strategies is recommended in T2DM, to reduce bone risk, by carefully discontinuing or replacing medications with negative effects on bones (Table 2). However, it is essential to ensure adequate glycemic, cardiovascular and other systems control. Regular medication review helps identifying and managing drugs linked to bone fragility, while replacing them with bone-neutral alternatives.[93] If discontinuation is risky, supplementation with vitamin D, calcium or antiresorptive therapy and life style modifications is recommended.[94]

Weight-bearing and resistance exercise are recommended in T2DM patients due to their significant involvement in improvement of bone strength, muscle mass, blood pressure, lipid profiles, insulin sensitivity, and cardiovascular health.[95] Unlike most pharmaceutical interventions to reduce fracture risk, modifying lifestyle through stopping smoking, reducing alcohol and increasing protein intake is essential for further reduction of bone loss.[96] The targeted life style recommendations are essential in patients who have genetic susceptibility to bone fragility, such as in case of epigenetic modification and differences in drug metabolism. Furthermore, integrating genetic screening, biomarkers profiling and advanced risk calculators can guide individualized medicine. Personalized therapy ensures that high-risk patients receive early osteoporosis screening, optimal pharmacological regimens, and long-term skeletal protection.[97,98] It is advised that future longitudinal studies focus on assessing the interplay between diabetes, polypharmacy and bone health, as most of the available studies are cross-sectional or short-term trials, which underestimate delayed effects on bone. Additionally, generating artificial intelligence-driven risk prediction models may offer a promising tool for better bone health. By integrating electronic health records, medication history, genetic data and bone markers, AI may predict fracture risk in patients exposed to polypharmacy. This may support health care providers in making more informed and individualized treatment decisions.

CONCLUSION

Patients with T2DM and polypharmacy are at elevated risk of skeletal fragility, arising from both the metabolic alterations of the disease and the adverse effects of drugs. Comprehensive management should include early screening for osteoporosis, periodic medication review, and bone-protective strategies. Maintaining a balanced therapeutic approach that optimizes glycemic control while protecting bone health, thereby providing short-term metabolic objectives with long-term skeletal preservation. Future studies should focus on long-term clinical trials assessing cumulative drug effects on skeletal outcomes and on developing predictive models for medications-induced bone fragility.
