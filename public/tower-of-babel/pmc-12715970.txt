Enhanced risk of thrombosis in aged insulin-treated diabetic rats exposed to non-severe recurrent hypoglycemia
Authors: Ashish K. Rehni, Sunjoo Cho, Miguel A. Perez-Pinzon, Wenche Jy, Kunjan R. Dave
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntidiabetic treatment is linked with an increased risk of hypoglycemia. Several clinical studies have demonstrated that hypoglycemia exposure elevates the risk of cardiovascular diseases in diabetic patients. Our previous research has shown that non-severe recurrent hypoglycemia (RH) exposure increases the risk of thrombosis in young insulin-treated diabetic (ITD) rats. Given that a substantial subset of diabetes patients who experience cardiovascular complications are elderly and the effect of non-severe RH on thrombosis risk in aged subjects remains unknown, we tested the hypothesis that prior RH exposure enhances thrombosis in aged ITD rats.MethodsAged, insulin-treated streptozotocin-diabetic Wistar rats of both sexes were randomly assigned to either the recurrent hyperinsulinemic euglycemia control or the recurrent hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia group. The risk of thrombosis was quantified using an in vivo model.ResultsRH increases the risk of thrombosis in male ITD rats 7 days after exposure. A twice-weekly hypoglycemia exposure for 6 weeks also elevated the risk of thrombosis in male rats. Similar exposures to RH also increased the risk of thrombosis in aged female rats.ConclusionsExposure to non-severe RH increases the risk of thrombosis in aged ITD rats of both sexes. Nonetheless, it remains uncertain whether aging influences the characteristics of thrombosis observed in RH-exposed young ITD rats as well as its clinical consequences, highlighting the need for further research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01950-y.

Introduction

Diabetes is a prevalent disorder linked to significant long-term complications. Antidiabetic therapy delays the progression of complications arising from hyperglycemia but increases the risk of hypoglycemia [1, 2]. The incidence of diabetes is higher in older adults compared to younger adults [3]. Patients with type 1 and type 2 diabetes experience recurrent hypoglycemia (RH) [4, 5], and hypoglycemia exposure is linked to an increased risk of stroke, transient ischemic attack, and cardiovascular disease [6]. Patients with diabetes have an increased risk of cardiovascular disease and related mortality, and this risk significantly increases with age [7]. Both non-severe and severe hypoglycemia exposure enhance the risk of major adverse and serious cardiovascular events and cardiovascular mortality in subjects with diabetes [6, 8]. However, a few studies did not find a link between hypoglycemia exposure and stroke [9, 10]. Literature reviews indicate that hypoglycemia contributes to cardiovascular disease in diabetes, potentially through pro-thrombotic mechanisms [11]. We have shown that non-severe RH exposure enhances the risk of thrombosis and platelet susceptibility to activation by potent stimuli in young male and female insulin-treated diabetic (ITD) rats [12]. Overall, the literature indicates that severe hypoglycemia increases the risk of cardiovascular complications by increasing thrombosis. There is a gap in knowledge if the risk of thrombosis in non-severe hypoglycemia-exposed aged diabetic animals is similar to that in young animals.

Therefore, we hypothesized that prior exposure to RH enhances the risk or severity of thrombosis in aged diabetic rats treated with insulin. Specifically, we tested if aged ITD rats subjected to a history of RH would exhibit heightened thrombotic responses (increased clot formation) compared to aged ITD rats without prior RH exposure.

Materials and methods

Animals

This study was conducted as per the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health and the protocols approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Miami (IPROTO202000000174, IPROTO202300002261). Middle-aged Wistar rats of both sexes were purchased from Charles River Laboratories International, Inc (Wilmington, MA) and aged in-house.

Diabetes induction, insulin treatment and RH exposure

Aged male (21 ± 0.1 months) and female rats (21 ± 0.1 months; Table 1) were rendered diabetic using a single 27.5–30 mg/kg b.w. (ten males received a dose of 30 mg/kg b.w. and sixteen males received a dose of 27.5 mg/kg b.w.) and 30 mg/kg b.w. intraperitoneal doses of streptozotocin, respectively. The initial studies in male rats were conducted using 30 mg/kg streptozotocin. However, this dose was revised to 27.5 mg/kg to avoid mortality. Blood glucose monitoring was performed twice-weekly throughout the experiment using an Alpha Trak Blood Glucose Monitoring System (Zoetis, Parsippany, NJ). Insulin pellet implantation (Linplant; LinShin, Toronto, Canada) was done two to three weeks after the diabetes induction, and pellets were adjusted until euglycemia was achieved [13, 14]. These rats were considered ITD [12].

RH was started after a period of two to three weeks of insulin pellet treatment using a supplemental subcutaneous insulin injection and food removal [12]. Blood glucose levels were monitored before and during hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia and until recovery (Fig. 1). Blood glucose levels were maintained in the hypoglycemia range (< 70 mg/dL) for a 3 h period. After the intended duration of exposure, hypoglycemia was corrected by replacing food in the cage and subcutaneous dextrose administration. The rats in the hyperinsulinemic euglycemia control (RH + glucose) groups remained euglycemic throughout the procedure by receiving dextrose injections along with the insulin doses.

Determination of thrombosis

The effect of RH exposure on thrombosis was assessed using an in vivo model [12]. Briefly, rats were anesthetized with isoflurane (3:7 oxygen: nitrous oxide). Their femoral artery and vein were cannulated and the rats were then placed on a ventilator. The left jugular vein and the right carotid artery were connected by a shunt with a 4−0 silk suture. After confirming that the physiological parameters (body and head temperature, blood pH, partial pressure of carbon dioxide: pCO2 in blood, partial pressure of oxygen: pO2 in blood, and mean arterial blood pressure: MABP) were within normal range (Table 1), blood was allowed to pass through the shunt for 15 min. The suture was then withdrawn and weighed. The clot weight was calculated by subtracting the weight of the suture from the total weight of the suture with the attached thrombus.

Experimental groups

Rats were randomly assigned to either the RH + glucose or RH group. The risk of thrombosis in rats exposed to either once daily-hypoglycemia for five days (5-day RH) or twice-weekly hypoglycemia for six weeks (6-week RH) was determined on day 7 or 3, after the last hypoglycemia exposure, respectively (Fig. 1A).

Statistical analysis

Grubbs’ test was used to confirm the absence of any potential outliers. Two groups were compared using Student’s t-test. The clot weight of male and female rats in different study groups was compared using two-way and three way ANOVA. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and the results are expressed as mean ± SEM.

Results

We did not observe any major differences in physiological parameters between the RH-exposed ITD groups and their respective control groups (Table 1). We did observe statistically significant differences between groups for two of the parameters. Given the extent of the observed variations, they are unlikely to have a physiologically significant effect.

Diabetes prevalence increases with age [15] and the risk of cardiovascular death in diabetes is high across all age groups, with patients over 70 showing an even higher risk [16]. Therefore, we studied the impact of hypoglycemia exposure on the risk of thrombosis in ITD rats of both sexes. Glucose levels are presented in Fig. 1B, C, F, G, J, K, N, and O. First, we evaluated whether the pro-thrombotic effect observed after the 5-day RH exposure in our earlier study using young animals was also present in male ITD rats. The clot weight in the 5-day RH-exposed male ITD rats determined 7 days after exposure (29 ± 3 mg, n = 7) was 88% higher (p < 0.01) than in the control rats (15 ± 1 mg, n = 6) (Fig. 1D). The clot weight normalized to body weight in the 5-day RH-exposed male ITD (n = 7) rats was also significantly higher by 130% (p < 0.01) than in the control group (n = 6) (Fig. 1E). We also evaluated whether twice-weekly hypoglycemia episodes for six consecutive weeks increased the risk of thrombosis in male ITD rats. The clot weight in the male ITD rats previously exposed to RH (28 ± 3 mg, n = 6) was 66% higher (p < 0.01) than in the control rats (17 ± 1 mg, n = 7) (Fig. 1H). We also observed that clot weight normalized to body weight in the 6-week RH-exposed male ITD rats (n = 6) was also significantly higher by 58% (p < 0.05) than in the control animals (n = 7) (Fig. 1I).

Next, we evaluated the effect of 5-day RH exposure on the increased risk of thrombosis in aged female ITD rats. The clot weights observed in RH-exposed female ITD rats determined 7 days post-exposure (21 ± 1 mg, n = 7) were 47% higher (p < 0.001) than in the control rats (14 ± 1 mg, n = 7) (Fig. 1L). We also noted that the clot weight normalized to body weight in the 5-day RH exposed aged female ITD rats (n = 7) was significantly higher by 41% (p < 0.01) than that observed in the control group (n = 7) (Fig. 1M). Next, we tested if a 6-week RH exposure enhances the risk of thrombosis in female ITD rats. The clot weight in the RH-exposed female ITD rats (22 ± 2 mg, n = 7) was 46% higher (p < 0.01) than in the control rats (15 ± 1 mg, n = 6) (Fig. 1P). The clot weight values normalized to body weight in 6-week RH exposed aged female ITD rats (n = 7) was significantly higher by 41% (p < 0.05) than that observed in the control animals (n = 6) (Fig. 1Q). We did not find any correlation between hematocrit value and clot weight in any of the experimental groups (see supplementary Fig. 1).

Although experiments using male and female animals were not conducted in parallel, we compared the effect of RH on the extent of thrombosis in both male and female groups. In the 5-day RH exposure experiment, we observed that both RH exposure (p < 0.001) and sex (p < 0.05) had a significant effect on clot weight. However, in the 6-week RH exposure experiment, while RH exposure (p < 0.001) significantly affected clot weight, sex did not have any such effect. In the 5-day RH exposure experiment, the clot weight in the RH + Glucose control male group (n = 6) was not significantly different from the clot weight in the RH + Glucose control female group (n = 7). However, the clot weight in the RH-exposed male ITD rats (n = 7) was significantly higher (p < 0.05; 39%) than that observed in the RH-exposed female ITD rats (n = 7). In the 6-week RH exposure experiment, the clot weights in the RH + Glucose male control (n = 7) and RH-exposed male ITD groups (n = 6) were not significantly different from the clot weights observed in the RH + Glucose female control (n = 6) and RH-exposed female ITD groups (n = 7), respectively. In terms of clot weight normalized to body weight, we did not observe a significant difference between the RH-exposed aged male and female ITD rats. However, there was a significant difference (p < 0.05 each) between the male and female control groups. Three-way ANOVA analysis did not show any interaction between RH exposure, duration of RH exposure and sex (Supplementary Table 1). Our results show that RH exposure also exerts a pro-thrombotic effect in aged ITD rats of both sexes.

Discussion

Hypoglycemia exposure increases the chances of cardiovascular disease complications and mortality in diabetes [11]. Acute hypoglycemia exerts a procoagulant effect [17]. Therefore, it is plausible that hypoglycemia-induced activation of pro-thrombotic mechanisms may increase the risk of cardiovascular complications in diabetes. Supporting this hypothesis, we recently demonstrated that prior non-severe RH exposure increases the risk of thrombosis in young ITD rats of both sexes [12]. In the present study, we extended our findings by confirming that prior RH exposure increases the risk of thrombosis in aged male and female ITD rats.

Our data show that a 5-day RH exposure increases the risk of thrombosis for 7 days after exposure in aged male and female ITD rats. These findings are consistent with our previous results in young ITD rats [12] and a prior study that demonstrated a prothrombotic effect 7 days after exposure to two episodes of hypoglycemia in human subjects with diabetes [18]. Therefore, building on the published literature, our current results demonstrate that RH exposure increases the risk of thrombosis in aged ITD rats potentially by activating prothrombotic pathways. This underscores the significance of hypoglycemia as a serious cardiovascular risk factor in both young and aged individuals with diabetes, regardless of sex.

Aging is a significant nonmodifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease [19]. Diabetes prevalence is lowest among adults aged 20–24 years (1.9%) and increases with age, reaching 24.8% among those aged 75–79 years [20]. Older patients are approximately twice as likely to be hospitalized due to hypoglycemia [21]. Older diabetes patients (≥ 65 years old) exhibit increased unawareness to hypoglycemia when compared to middle aged (39–64 years old) patients [22]. As individuals age, the incidence of hypoglycemia as well as cardiovascular complications and mortality tends to rise [23]. Moreover, platelets in elderly subjects are at an enhanced risk of activation [24]. Therefore, in light of the present findings showing an increased risk of thrombosis in aged ITD rats, we may infer that hypoglycemia exposure plays a substantial role in increasing the risk of cardiovascular complications via enhanced thrombosis in the vulnerable aging patient population with diabetes. A study has proposed that acute hypoglycemia activates platelets by stimulation of α-adrenergic receptors [25]. Another study has shown that acute as well as antecendent hypoglycemia promote a hypercoagulable state by impairing fibrinolytic balance, and increasing the levels of coagulation factors, platelet activation, and reduces NO-mediated endothelial inhibition of platelet aggregation [26]. A study has shown that moderate hypoglycemia increases the levels of PAI-1 and may thus inhibit fibrinolysis contributing towards the observed thrombosis [27]. It is plausible that these mechanisms may be responsible for increased thrombosis in RH-exposed aged ITD rats. However, future confirmatory studies are warranted.

Aged men and women have a higher prevalence of diabetes compared to their younger counterparts [28]. The increase in the risk of cardiovascular disease in diabetes is higher in women compared to men, highlighting the importance of studying this risk in both diabetic men and women [29, 30]. Perimenopausal females with diabetes have a higher risk of thrombosis compared to males [31]. The incidence of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism increases in men and women over 60 years old [32]. While glycemic control in diabetes reduces the risk of venous thromboembolism in female patients, the same effect is not seen in male patients [33]. Therefore, it is important to test the effect of hypoglycemia on the extent of thrombosis in both sexes. Healthy and diabetic women exhibit reduced endogenous glucose production and glucose counterregulatory mechanisms during hypoglycemia compared to men, indicating a differential risk of RH in women versus men [34]. Our published data shows that RH exposure enhances thrombosis in young male and female ITD rats [12]. Our data on aged female ITD rats indicates that RH exposure substantially contributes to the enhanced thrombosis observed in aging females. However, the clot weight in the 5-day RH-exposed male ITD rats was substantially higher than that in the female ITD rats. Since sex specific effects of recurrent hypoglycemia on the risk of thrombosis are not well studied and thus underlying mechanisms are unknown, the mechanisms underlying this sex difference remain to be investigated. Further, future studies are required to determine the role of platelet and coagulation cascade activation, and platelet count in enhanced thrombosis observed in RH exposed aged ITD rats. Not conducting studies using male and female animals in parallel and not evaluating effects of RH exposure on markers of platelet activation, inflammation and coagulation are limitations of our study. Since we did not include cohorts of young animals, it is not possible to infer if age has any influence on the RH-induced increased risk of thrombosis.

Conclusion

Diabetes is a significant risk factor of cardiovascular disease [35]. Based on literature [11] and our previous findings in young ITD rats [12], our current findings demonstrate that hypoglycemia may contribute towards diabetes-induced increase in the risk of cardiovascular disease by exacerbating thrombosis in aged individuals. However, studies are needed to identify the mechanisms responsible for increased risk of thrombosis in RH-exposed aged men and women with diabetes.
