Delayed Repair of an Iatrogenic Perimembranous VSD After Redo Aortic Valve Replacement
Authors: Chang Hun Kim, Mi Hee Lim, Chee-Hoon Lee, Min Ho Ju, Yeon Ju Rhee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIatrogenic ventricular septal defect (VSD) is an uncommon complication of redo aortic valve surgery, particularly during prosthesis explantation near the membranous septum.Case SummaryA 71-year-old woman with a prior 19-mm bioprosthetic aortic valve underwent redo aortic valve replacement for severe prosthetic dysfunction. Explantation created a perimembranous VSD that was initially closed transaortically; intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography showed a trivial residual shunt. On postoperative day 5, transthoracic echocardiography revealed a new 0.5-cm VSD with left-to-right shunting. Emergent transtricuspid patch repair failed because of extreme septal tissue friability. Six weeks later, after fibrotic maturation, left ventricular–side patch closure with concomitant sutureless aortic valve replacement achieved complete closure.DiscussionIn stable patients, delayed repair of an iatrogenic VSD may enable durable closure when early repair fails because of friable tissue.Take-Home MessageTiming of repair may determine surgical success in an iatrogenic VSD.

History of Presentation

A 71-year-old woman with prior surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) was referred for reoperation after transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated severe prosthetic aortic stenosis and regurgitation. The findings were consistent with bioprosthetic valve dysfunction due to cusp degeneration, although chronic prosthetic valve infection was also considered. Despite these findings, she remained asymptomatic, without dyspnea or clinical evidence of heart failure.Take-Home Messages•Iatrogenic perimembranous ventricular septal defect can occur during redo aortic valve replacement, and even a trivial residual shunt on intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography may enlarge and become clinically significant early postoperatively.•When early re-repair is limited by extreme septal tissue friability, a staged strategy with delayed reoperation after fibrotic maturation of the defect margins can facilitate durable patch closure.•In complex redo settings with a small annulus, sutureless valves may simplify reimplantation and improve operative feasibility.

Past Medical History

In January 2016, she underwent SAVR via upper sternotomy for severe aortic stenosis using a 19-mm Mitroflow tissue valve (Sorin Group).

Differential Diagnosis

For prosthetic valve dysfunction, structural valve degeneration, pannus, and chronic prosthetic valve endocarditis were considered.

Investigations

TTE (May 21, 2025) showed preserved LV systolic function (58%) with concentric LV hypertrophy and left atrial enlargement (anteroposterior diameter 41.6 mm). The bioprosthetic aortic valve demonstrated markedly increased gradients (maximum velocity [Vmax] 4.5 m/s; peak/mean gradient 81/48 mm Hg) with moderate-to-severe aortic regurgitation (Figure 1A); no prosthetic rocking motion was seen. Preoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) confirmed severe mixed prosthetic aortic stenosis and regurgitation due to a malfunctioning tissue valve with diffusely thickened, restricted cusps, and focal prolapse, without paravalvular leak (Figure 1B). Color Doppler showed leaflet-tip flow acceleration and a large diastolic regurgitant jet occupying approximately half of the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT). A 5-mm hypermobile linear echogenic material on the medial cusp raised concern for possible vegetation.

There were no clinical features suggestive of active infection, including absence of fever or systemic symptoms. Inflammatory markers, including white blood cell count, C-reactive protein, and procalcitonin, were within normal limits. Given the absence of clinical suspicion, preoperative blood cultures were not obtained. Overall, active infective endocarditis was considered unlikely.

Preoperative computed tomography demonstrated a small aortic annulus consistent with the previously implanted 19-mm prosthesis, suggesting limited annular reserve for reoperative valve implantation (Figure 1C).

Management

Redo AVR (September 10, 2025)

Redo median sternotomy was performed for severe prosthetic valve dysfunction. During explantation of the degenerated 19-mm bioprosthesis, an iatrogenic defect was created at the membranous interventricular septum. The defect was repaired transaortically using a double-layer closure with reinforcement, and redo AVR was completed with a 19-mm mechanical aortic valve prosthesis (St. Jude Medical, [now Abbott]). Given the friable tissue, pledgeted valve sutures were used to provide additional reinforcement of the repair site. Intraoperative TEE demonstrated only a trivial residual ventricular septal defect (VSD) shunt with minimal flow, which was expected to seal spontaneously; therefore, the operation was concluded (Figure 2).Figure 2Intraoperative Transesophageal Echocardiography During Redo Aortic Valve ReplacementIntraoperative transesophageal echocardiography (midesophageal 4-chamber view) demonstrating a trivial residual ventricular septal defect shunt after transaortic ventricular septal defect repair.

Early postoperative diagnosis and emergent reoperation (postoperative day 5)

Postoperative TTE performed on postoperative day 5 demonstrated a new perimembranous VSD with left-to-right shunting toward the right ventricle and right atrium (Figure 3A). The defect measured approximately 4.7 mm in diameter, with a peak shunt velocity of 5.0 m/s; therefore, the patient underwent emergent redo sternotomy (third sternotomy) for surgical repair, performed early to minimize the development of dense postoperative adhesions. A transtricuspid approach was used (partial incision of the septal leaflet). A bovine pericardial patch was secured using 4-0 polypropylene interrupted pledgeted sutures placed via the tricuspid valve exposure. The inferior rim of the VSD (muscular septum) was extremely friable and tore with suture tension. After the first aortic cross-clamp release, intraoperative TEE demonstrated a residual VSD shunt at the inferior portion of the patch measuring approximately 7 mm; LV-to–right atrium shunting was not observed (Figure 3B). After a second aortic cross-clamp and additional inferior patch reinforcement, repeat TEE demonstrated persistent residual shunting at the same inferior position. Because the septal tissue was extremely friable, further repair was unlikely to achieve secure closure and carried a high risk of enlarging the defect. In addition, any additional attempts would have prolonged aortic cross-clamp and cardiopulmonary bypass times, potentially increasing operative risk. Given that the patient had been clinically stable and asymptomatic preoperatively, the procedure was concluded with a plan for staged, delayed definitive repair after tissue maturation. After cardiopulmonary bypass weaning, atrioventricular block occurred, and epicardial pacing wires were placed with the initiation of temporary epicardial pacing.

Postoperative course after early reoperation and discharge

Atrioventricular block persisted postoperatively despite temporary epicardial pacing initiated at the time of reoperation. On postoperative day 7, because no recovery of atrioventricular conduction was observed, a permanent pacemaker was implanted. TTE on postoperative day 10 showed a well-functioning mechanical aortic valve prosthesis and a residual interventricular septal defect with left-to-right shunting from the LVOT toward the right ventricle and right atrium, consistent with a perimembranous VSD with a Gerbode-type component (Figure 3C). She remained hemodynamically stable and was discharged with a plan for delayed definitive repair.

Although quantitative shunt measurement, such as pulmonary/systemic flow ratio, was not performed, postoperative TTE after the third operation demonstrated a high-velocity shunt (Vmax 4.92 m/s) with moderate resting pulmonary hypertension (tricuspid regurgitation Vmax 3.9 m/s; peak gradient approximately 60 mm Hg). However, LV systolic function was preserved (ejection fraction 67%), and the patient remained clinically stable without signs of pulmonary hypertension–related symptoms or right-sided heart failure. In addition, no inotropic support was required. These findings supported a strategy of delayed definitive repair.

Planned delayed definitive repair (6 weeks later)

Six weeks after discharge, a planned reoperation (fourth sternotomy) was performed. The 19-mm mechanical aortic valve prosthesis implanted at redo AVR was explanted. The VSD was approached from the LV side through the aortic annulus (transaortic, LV-side exposure). In contrast to the earlier operation, the VSD margins had become firm and fibrotic, permitting secure bovine pericardial patch closure without suture cut-through (Figure 4A). Because the patient had a small aortic annulus and had undergone multiple prior sternotomies, implantation of another conventional prosthesis was technically challenging after VSD patch closure. A 19-mm mechanical valve sizer could not be advanced across the annulus, and root enlargement was considered undesirable in the setting of a heavily scarred and fibrotic annulus. Therefore, a sutureless bioprosthesis (Perceval S, LivaNova) was selected to facilitate valve implantation while minimizing additional operative complexity. Outcome and Follow-Up

On postoperative day 8 after delayed repair, TTE demonstrated a well-functioning aortic bioprosthesis with no residual VSD shunt (Figure 4C). The patient remains in outpatient follow-up.

Discussion

Iatrogenic VSD after SAVR, particularly in redo surgery, is an uncommon but clinically significant complication that may occur during prosthetic valve explantation near the membranous septum. These defects can be technically challenging to repair because the surrounding septal tissue is often fragile and lies adjacent to both the prosthetic sewing ring and the cardiac conduction system.1,2

In our patient, early postoperative echocardiography demonstrated a high-velocity left-to-right shunt originating from the LVOT toward the right-sided chambers, consistent with a perimembranous VSD with a Gerbode-type component. Acquired Gerbode defects have been reported after valve surgery and other cardiac insults and may be under-recognized when the high-velocity jet is misinterpreted as tricuspid regurgitation on echocardiography.3,4

Management should be individualized on the basis of shunt severity, clinical status, prosthetic valve integrity, and anatomic suitability. Although surgical patch closure remains the standard treatment in many patients, percutaneous device closure has been successfully performed in selected cases of iatrogenic perimembranous VSD after prosthetic AVR.2,5,6 Recent reports have also described the feasibility of minimally invasive or endoscopic closure as an alternative approach in complex redo settings when transcatheter closure is not suitable.7

A key learning point from this case is the potential value of delayed reintervention when early repair is limited by extreme tissue friability and recurrent suture tearing. This concept parallels experience in postinfarction ventricular septal rupture, in which delayed repair, when feasible, has been associated with improved outcomes, likely reflecting maturation and fibrosis of necrotic margins.8 In our patient, 6 weeks after the failed early repair, the VSD margins had become firm and fibrotic, allowing secure transaortic patch closure from the LV side.

Valve selection during the final operation also required careful consideration. Implantation of another conventional prosthesis was technically challenging because the small annulus and prior patch repair prevented passage of a 19-mm mechanical valve sizer. In addition, root enlargement was considered suboptimal in the setting of a fourth sternotomy and a heavily fibrotic annulus. In this context, a sutureless bioprosthesis (Perceval S) provided a practical solution that simplified implantation while minimizing additional annular manipulation. Previous reports have demonstrated that sutureless valves may facilitate valve implantation and improve valve hemodynamic performance in selected patients undergoing complex redo surgery with small aortic annuli.9,10

Another notable aspect of this case was the occurrence of atrioventricular block after the early reoperation. The atrioventricular conduction system, including the bundle of His, is located in close proximity to the membranous septum and the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve. Surgical manipulation in this region during transtricuspid exposure may therefore increase the risk of conduction injury. This observation highlights the importance of balancing adequate exposure for repair with preservation of the conduction system in complex redo septal surgery. In this case, both prosthetic valve explantation and subsequent patch suturing near the membranous septum may have contributed to conduction disturbance.

The most important lesson from this case is that immediate surgical reintervention may not always yield durable repair when septal tissue is extremely friable. Repeated suturing in such circumstances may enlarge the defect or lead to recurrent patch dehiscence. When the patient remains hemodynamically stable, a staged strategy allowing time for fibrotic maturation of the septal margins may facilitate more secure and durable repair.

This case illustrates that, in selected stable patients with an iatrogenic VSD after redo AVR, timing of repair may be more important than urgency when septal tissue is extremely friable.

Conclusions

Iatrogenic perimembranous VSD (with possible Gerbode-type component) can complicate prosthetic valve explantation during redo AVR. When early reoperation fails because the septal rim is extremely friable, planned delayed repair after tissue fibrosis can permit durable LV-side patch closure with excellent early echocardiographic results.
