The craniofacial triad of micrognathia, transverse facial cleft, and microtia in trisomy 18: a rare but telling combination
Authors: Jie Zhou, Hong Luo, Jiao Chen
Published: 2026

Introduction

Transverse facial cleft, also known as macrostomia, is an extremely rare congenital craniofacial anomaly resulting from failure of fusion between the maxillary and mandibular prominences during embryogenesis (1). It falls within the spectrum of first and second pharyngeal arch dysplasia (2). The incidence of transverse facial cleft in the general population is very low, and the anomaly typically presents either as an isolated defect or in association with monogenic disorders such as Treacher Collins syndrome or Goldenhar syndrome (3).

Trisomy 18 (T18) is the second most common autosomal trisomy syndrome, with an overall prevalence of approximately 4.08 per 10,000 pregnancies (4). It is characterized by severe intrauterine growth restriction, distinctive craniofacial dysmorphism, limb anomalies, and congenital heart defects (5). Its well-recognized craniofacial features include micrognathia, cleft lip and/or palate, and ear abnormalities; however, transverse facial cleft is exceedingly rare (6). To date, no previous prenatal ultrasound report appears to have documented this complete triad in a single case of T18.

We report a case of T18 diagnosed at 28 weeks of gestation by prenatal ultrasound and confirmed by whole-exome sequencing of fetal tissue following labor induction. The fetus presented with a rare craniofacial triad comprising micrognathia, transverse facial cleft, and bilateral microtia, in addition to multiple systemic anomalies, including ventricular septal defect, single umbilical artery, intracranial abnormalities, and limb deformities. This case expands the known craniofacial phenotypic spectrum of T18 and provides a phenotypic basis for exploring the embryonic developmental significance of this unusual combination of malformations.

Case presentation

A 41-year-old woman, gravida 3, para 2, was referred to the Xizang Region Child Development Clinical Medical Research Center at 28 weeks of gestation for a targeted ultrasound examination due to polyhydramnios detected at an outside facility. She had registered for prenatal care but had not undergone routine screening, including first-trimester nuchal translucency measurement, non-invasive prenatal testing, second-trimester maternal serum screening, oral glucose tolerance testing, or detailed fetal anatomic and cardiac ultrasound examinations. Her medical history was unremarkable, with no family history of genetic disorders, consanguinity, or known teratogen exposure.

Our comprehensive ultrasound examination revealed a fetus with complex, multisystem anomalies. Central nervous system findings included an abnormal cranial shape, abnormal appearance of the sylvian fissure, short and underdeveloped cerebral sulci suggestive of cortical dysplasia, and a cerebellum measuring small for gestational age. Prominent craniofacial anomalies were also noted (Figure 1A), including a transverse facial cleft, visualized as a 1.6-cm discontinuity of the skin extending from the left oral commissure; bilateral microtia with a low-set left ear (right auricular length, 1.4 cm; left auricular length, 1.3 cm); and micrognathia with significant mandibular retrusion and an inferior facial angle of approximately 45°. Additional findings included a fixed hyperextended neck posture; bilateral hand anomalies, including persistent extension of the left thumb, overlapping fingers on the right hand, and preaxial polydactyly; a malalignment-type ventricular septal defect measuring 5 mm, with approximately 20% aortic overriding and bidirectional shunting; a single umbilical artery; and severe polyhydramnios (maximum vertical pocket, 10.2 cm; amniotic fluid index, 32.3 cm).

After counseling regarding the poor prognosis associated with these findings, the patient opted for termination of pregnancy. Postmortem external examination confirmed the prenatal ultrasound findings (Figure 1B), and chromosomal copy number variation analysis of fetal tissue confirmed T18 [seq (GRCh38) dup(18)(p11.32q23)].

Discussion

The most notable feature of this case is the rare craniofacial triad of micrognathia, transverse facial cleft, and bilateral microtia. To date, no previous prenatal ultrasound report appears to have documented this complete triad in a single case of T18. Thus, this case further expands the recognized craniofacial phenotypic spectrum of T18. Admittedly, a single case alone cannot establish a specific molecular pathogenic pathway. Accordingly, the following mechanistic discussion is speculative and hypothesis-generating, based on the phenotypic observations, and requires further validation through the accumulation of additional cases and experimental studies.

From an embryological perspective, all three anomalies are thought to arise from abnormal development of the first and second pharyngeal arches and their derivatives during gestational weeks 6–8 (7). Micrognathia reflects hypoplasia of the mandibular process of the first arch; microtia results from abnormal development of the auricular hillocks of the first and second arches; and transverse facial cleft results from failure of fusion between the maxillary and mandibular processes (4). Classic pathological studies have suggested that most malformations in T18 originate during these critical early developmental windows, with the teratogenic effects thought to primarily affect the mesoderm and manifest as developmental arrest (8). The morphological overlap between this triad and branchial arch dysplasia syndromes suggests a potential association with pharyngeal arch involvement in T18, although this hypothesis remains speculative and requires further validation. Given the fundamentally genome-wide nature of the genetic imbalance in T18, it is difficult to determine whether the pharyngeal arch involvement in this case reflects a regional predisposition or simply a random extension of the systemic developmental disturbance. The latter possibility is particularly relevant given the well-documented multisystemic nature of this aneuploidy (9).

Notably, the facial cleft in this case was left-sided and unilateral, accompanied by smaller ipsilateral auricular and orbital measurements, as well as a left-sided low-set ear, demonstrating clear asymmetry. Three possible interpretations may account for this laterality pattern. First, it may represent a stochastic manifestation of the phenotypic heterogeneity inherent to T18 (9). Second, this unilateral pattern shows phenotypic overlap with oculo-auriculo-vertebral spectrum (OAVS). Rosa et al. reported a similar case in a cohort of patients with T18 (10), suggesting that the overlap may not be entirely coincidental. Whether this case represents an OAVS-like phenotype associated with T18 or reflects shared developmental pathways, such as defects in neural crest cell migration, remains to be elucidated. Third, the possibility of chance co-occurrence of two rare conditions cannot be excluded. All three hypotheses require further validation in additional cases.

Clinically, when prenatal ultrasound reveals a combination of facial anomalies suggestive of first and second branchial arch involvement—specifically the triad of micrognathia, transverse facial cleft, and microtia—T18 should be included in the differential diagnosis, alongside single-gene disorders and sporadic branchial arch syndromes. This distinction is critical for genetic counseling and prognostic assessment, as the recurrence risk and outcomes associated with aneuploidy differ substantially from those of most monogenic conditions (11).

In summary, this case expands the recognized craniofacial phenotypic spectrum of T18, while its unilateral features further raise the possibility of localized pharyngeal arch developmental abnormalities occurring in the context of a systemic genetic imbalance. Whether the chromosome 18 dosage effect specifically interferes with neural crest cell development in the pharyngeal arches remains a hypothesis that requires further validation through larger case series and animal model studies.
