Eschweilenol C and subfractions from Terminalia plants disrupt haemoglobin metabolism, inhibiting Plasmodium falciparum growth at rings and trophozoite stages
Authors: Mariscal Brice Tchatat Tali, Cedric Derick Jiatsa Mbouna, Aafreen Zehra, Steven Collins Njonte Wouamba, Guy Paulin Kemayou Mouthe, Aubin Youbi Kamche, Darline Dize, Simeon Fogue Kouam, Boniface Pone Kamdem, Jean Claude Tchouankeu, Feroz Khan, Fabrice Fekam Boyom
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rising prevalence of artemisinin-resistant malaria parasites in Africa underscores the urgent demand for new and effective therapeutic options. Historically, natural products have demonstrated significant anti-Plasmodium efficacy, indicating their potential as a source for malaria drug discovery. Extracts and fractions from Terminalia ivorensis and Terminalia brownii previously exhibited promising anti-Plasmodium potency; however, a thorough assessment of their pharmacodynamics across all asexual blood stages of Plasmodium falciparum and elucidation of their mode of action remains to be conducted. Products from the active fractions of T. ivorensis (TiWEa) and T. brownii (TbMMeOH:Ea) were investigated throughout the asexual-blood and sexual stages of P. falciparum, focusing on their mechanism of inhibition and interaction with some key targets of haemoglobin metabolism.MethodsBoth active fractions (TiWEa and TbMMeOH:Ea) were fractionated by column chromatography, and the chemical structures of the isolated compound were elucidated through physical and spectroscopic techniques. The resulting compound and subfractions were screened in vitro against P. falciparum strains (Dd2, 3D7, and Dd2-GNF156), stage V gametocytes, and mammalian cells. The pharmacodynamics (stage-specific analysis and killing kinetics) of potent inhibitors were assessed and complemented by the effect on haemozoin formation. The molecular interaction between the potent compound and the two target enzymes was investigated through molecular docking.ResultsThe fractionation of TiWEa yielded five subfractions (Ti01, Ti02, Ti03, Ti04, Ti05), while TbMMeOH:Ea yielded three subfractions (Tb01, Tb02, Tb03) along with one compound (01) characterized as eschweilenol C. All Subfractions demonstrated good activity on susceptible and multidrug-resistant strains of P. falciparum (3D7 and Dd2) with an IC50 ranging from 0.15 to 5.73 µg/ml. eschweilenol C equally displayed an IC50 of 490.74 and 379.20 nM, respectively, on PfDd2 and Pf3D7. Two top hits subfractions (Ti03; Ti04) from TiWEa and eschweilenol C from TbMMeOH:Ea exhibited a perfect antiplasmodial profile (IC50 < 1 µg/ml); good selectivity (SI > 10) and demonstrated no spontaneous loss of efficacy when tested against mutant resistant strain (PfDd2-GNF156) of P. falciparum. Ti03, Ti04, and eschweilenol C reduced haemozoin production, exhibited killing activity on ring and trophozoite stages and showed significant binding affinity to PfM1AAP and PfM17LAP.ConclusionThis study identifies eschweilenol C as a promising candidate for malaria drug discovery efforts and its significance for further exploration in the context of the emergence of artemisinin-resistant parasites in Africa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-025-05445-z.

Background

Efforts to reduce the burden of malaria still face several challenges, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, where the disease is most widespread. Indeed, malaria remains a major global health problem, having caused an estimated 263 million cases and 597,000 deaths in 2023 [1]. The greatest disease burden is in sub-Saharan Africa, which accounts for 95% of all malaria-related deaths each year, with significant morbidity and mortality reported among young children and pregnant women [1]. Among the Plasmodium species that are responsible for this disease, Plasmodium falciparum causes the highest malaria burden [2–4]. The P. falciparum infection is initiated when the sporozoites enter the bloodstream after a female Anopheles mosquito bites. These parasitic forms migrate to the hepatocytes, multiplying and differentiating into merozoites. After initiation of a 48 h cycle of infection in the red blood cells, the parasite develops within a parasitophorous vacuole, evolving from ring stage to trophozoite and then to schizont that replicates asexually to release 6–32 daughter merozoites able to invade new erythrocytes [5]. The switch from asexual proliferation to P. falciparum gametocyte differentiation (0.5–10% of asexual blood stage (ABS) parasites) is crucial for the parasite's survival. Five distinguishable developmental stages exist for gametocytes, ranging from immature stages I–III to late-stage IV gametocytes sequestered in specific tissues such as the spleen or bone marrow. In the final mature stage V, female and male gametocytes leave their sequestered site and enter the circulatory system to be picked up by a mosquito [6]. Of these cells, only 103 must be ingested by the next feeding mosquito to sustain human-to-mosquito transmission of the parasite [7, 8]. Given the complexity (dual stage) of the life cycle of P. falciparum, it becomes essential to identify compounds with dual activities against ABS and gametocytes by detailed transmission-blocking screenings.

Referring to the asexual stages, malarial parasites, residing within erythrocytes, rely heavily on haemoglobin degradation for amino acid acquisition [9]. This process occurs in specialized digestive vacuoles, where haemoglobin is ingested via the cytostome and broken down by specific proteases [10]. The resulting amino acids are crucial for parasite growth and protein synthesis, while the haem byproduct is sequestered as haemozoin [10]. However, haemoglobin lacks isoleucine, an essential amino acid for Plasmodium growth [9]. To overcome this and other nutritional limitations, parasites increase erythrocyte membrane permeability through new permeation pathways (NPPs), allowing the uptake of additional nutrients from the host serum [9].

The haemoglobin degradation pathway and associated structures represent potential targets for antimalarial drug development [9, 10]. Among these, P. falciparum M1 alanyl aminopeptidase (PfM1AAP) and M17 leucyl aminopeptidase (PfM17LAP) are potential targets for novel anti-malarial drug development [11]. These enzymes are believed to function in the terminal stages of haemoglobin digestion, providing free amino acids for parasite growth [12]. Both enzymes are primarily located in the parasite's cytosol and have optimal activity at neutral pH [12]. PfM1AAP exhibits broad substrate specificity, efficiently hydrolyzing neutral and basic amino acids, while PfM17LAP preferentially cleaves bulky, hydrophobic amino acids [13]. Inhibitors of these aminopeptidases have shown efficacy in killing malaria parasites in culture and reducing parasite growth in murine models [11]. Recent biochemical, structural, and functional studies have provided valuable information for the design of specific inhibitors, bringing these enzymes closer to being validated anti-malarial drug targets [12].

The treatment of P. falciparum malaria poses a serious problem because of the development of resistance by parasite populations against several current drugs, such as quinolones [14, 15], atovaquone [16], and artemisinin [17–20], even when used in combination therapies [21, 22]. Notably, artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) fails when both the artemisinin derivative is compromised and the partner drug experience resistance [23]. Resistance occurs when the compound is effectively eluded from the parasite or if more compound is needed to kill the parasite. On the other hand, even if there are influential ACT for malaria, these treatments cannot eliminate mature gametocytes from the blood, thus leaving treated patients infectious to blood-sucking mosquitoes for up to 21 days [24]. To eliminate the possibility of malaria infections, potential compounds that target both sexual and asexual stages of P. falciparum should be the first choice. The use of plant products as scaffolds for the discovery of antimalarial drugs has been undeniable since the advent of quinine from the Cinchona tree and artemisinin from Artemisia annua [25]. Examples of such potential plants that are being used for the treatment of malaria by traditional healers, include Terminalia ivorensis and Terminalia brownii [26]. Thus, scientific validation of the use of T. ivorensis and T. brownii as traditional remedies for malaria is worthwhile.

Previous investigation reported the antiplasmodial activity of crude extracts (IC50: 0.13–1.43 μg/ml; selectivity indices (SI) > 100; Vero and Raw cells) and fractions (IC50: 0.15–1.73 μg/mL; SI > 100; Vero and Raw cells) from T. ivorensis and T. brownii against multidrug-resistant (Dd2) and chloroquine-susceptible (3D7) strains of P. falciparum asexual-blood stage (ABS). The ethyl acetate fraction (TiWEa) from T. ivorensis and the ethyl acetate/methanol fraction [TbMMeOH/Ea (1:1)] from T. brownii were identified as the most active fractions [26]. However, a detailed investigation of both fractions with emphasis on their pharmacodynamics looking at their stage-specific analysis and killing kinetics, transmissible blocking efficacy should afford potentially active compounds that can be prospected as efficient scaffolds for malaria discovery. Thus, the present study sought to research the inhibitory effects of subfractions and isolated compounds from previously identified T. ivorensis and T. brownii-derived fractions (TiWEa and [TbMMeOH/Ea (1:1)]) on different asexual (ring, trophozoite, and schizont) and sexual (gametocytes V) blood stages of P. falciparum. In addition, the molecular interaction of the most promising compounds with two key malarial drug targets (P. falciparum M1 alanyl aminopeptidase and P. falciparum M17 leucyl aminopeptidase) was also elucidated.

Methods

Plant collection, identification, extraction, and fraction preparation

The stem bark of T. ivorensis and T. brownii were collected around Carrefour MEEC-Nkolbisson, Yaounde-Cameroon in August 2017 and authenticated by a botanist: Mr Victor Nana (from the Cameroon National Herbarium). Voucher specimens were previously collected and deposited at the Cameroon National Herbarium under the reference numbers 48878/HNC and 36394/HNC for T. ivorensis and T. brownii, respectively [26]. Plant materials were dried, ground to a fine powder, and extracted as previously described [26]. Briefly, T. ivorensis aqueous extract was suspended in sterile distilled water and then fractionated by liquid–liquid extraction, while T. brownii methanolic extract was fractionated by flash chromatography [26]. The most active fractions viz. TiWEa and TbMMeOH:Ea (1:1 v/v) from T. ivorensis and T. brownii extracts were subjected to silica gel column chromatography (CC). The use of T. ivorensis and T. brownii complies with Cameroon's local regulations. The National Herbarium of Cameroon granted permission to collect samples from a specific plant. Plant materials were gathered following institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation.

Silica gel column chromatography

The TiWEa fraction (5.7 g) and TbMMeOH:Ea (1:1 v/v) fraction (30.6 g) obtained respectively from T. ivorensis and T. brownii exhibited the lowest antiplasmodial IC50 values were subjected to purification through column chromatography (CC). TiWEa fraction was eluted with MeOH:Ea (1:1 v/v) solvent system to yield 105 sub-fractions (150 mL, each), which were combined into 5 main sub-fractions (Ti01–Ti05) based on their TLC profiles. The sub-fraction Ti01 (30.1 mg; EtOAc/MeOH 100:0 → 98:2 v/v) was obtained by combining subfractions 1–34 based on the similarity of their TLC profile; after evaporation of the solvent and freeze-drying, 30.1 mg of a brown paste was obtained. Similarly, Ti02 (20.5 mg; EtOAc/MeOH 97:3 → 95:5 v/v) was obtained as a brown powder by combining subfraction 35–50, whereas Ti03 (25.2 mg; EtOAc/MeOH 94:6 → 9:1 v/v), a whitish powder, was obtained from the grouping of subfraction 51–60. Moreover, Ti04 (100.0 mg; EtOAc/MeOH 9:1 → 85:15 v/v), a brown powder, derived from the gathering of subfractions 61–81, whereas Ti05 (300.8 mg; EtOAc/MeOH 85:15 → 1:1 v/v), a yellow powder, emanated from the grouping of subfraction 82–105.

EtOAc/MeOH (1:1 v/v) fraction was eluted with EtOAc/MeOH/H2O in following a gradient mode of increasing polarity to yield 80 sub-fractions (ca. 300 mL, each), which were subsequently combined into 3 main sub-fractions (Tb01–Tb03) based on their TLC profiles. Tb01 (2.2 g; EtOAc/MeOH/H2O 95:4:1 → 90:9:1 v/v) was obtained by combining subfractions 1–20 based on the similarity of their TLC profiles. Similarly, Tb02 (12.5 g; EtOAc/MeOH/H2O 90:9:1 → 80:18:2 v/v) was obtained as a brown powder by pooling of subfractions 21–60, whereas, Tb03 (10.3 g; EtOAc/MeOH/H2O 80:18:2 → 2:1:1 v/v), a yellow powder, derived from the combination of subfractions 61–80. Furthermore, Tb04 (5.5 g; EtOAc/MeOH/H2O 90:9:1 → 80:18:2 v/v) was separated on a Sephadex LH-20 column eluting with CH2Cl2–MeOH (4:6) to obtain compound 1 (eschweilenol C; 6.0 mg; [27] and a mixture of several compounds after alternating several silica gels and Sephadex LH-20 chromatographies.

Preparation of the drug master plate

Subfractions and compound 1 were dissolved in 100% DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, Munich, Germany) at 10 mg/ml. Afterwards, master plates were prepared by mixing 5 µL of sample solutions with 195 μl of fresh RPMI 1640 incomplete (without HEPES, Albumax I, hypoxanthine and gentamicin) culture medium into a 96-well plate to yield a concentration of 250 µg/ml (2.5% DMSO) followed by a five-fold serial dilution. For each inhibitor, the intermediate concentrations ranged from 0.4 to 250 μg/mL. Chloroquine and artemisinin (Sigma-Aldrich, Munich, Germany), which were used as standard drugs, were prepared at 1 mM in sterile distilled water and 100% DMSO, respectively, and serially diluted in an incomplete RPMI1640 medium.

Plasmodium falciparum growth inhibition assay

Maintenance of P. falciparum asexual-blood stages and induction of gametocytogenesis

Plasmodium falciparum strains 3D7, NF54-E (MRA-1000), Dd2, and Dd2-GNF156, which were obtained from Bei Resources (USA), were maintained in culture as previously described [29] in fresh O-positive human red blood cells suspended at 4% (v/v) haematocrit in complete RPMI 1640 medium (500 mL RPMI 1640 (Sigma, Munich, Germany) supplemented with 25 mM HEPES, 10% Albumax I (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), 1X hypoxanthine (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) and 50 mg/mL gentamicin (Sigma-Aldrich, Munich, Germany) and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The medium was renewed daily to propagate the culture. Parasite growth was monitored by microscopic examination of Giemsa-stained thin blood smears using immersion oil and synchronized at the ring stage by serial treatment with 5% D-sorbitol two days before each experiment [30]. Gametocytogenesis was induced from synchronized asexual P. falciparum parasite strain NF54-E (MRA-1000) and cultured as previously established [31].

Inhibition of P. falciparum asexual blood stages

Subfractions and the isolated compound were evaluated for their inhibitory potential on chloroquine-sensitive (Pf3D7; multi-resistant (PfDd2) and mutant-resistant (PfDd2-GNF156) strains of P. falciparum using PfLDH assay as previously described [32, 33]. In brief, 10 µl of subfractions and the isolated compound and 90 μL of synchronized parasites (ring stages) at 2% parasitaemia and 1% haematocrit were dispensed in each well of a microtiter plate. The test concentrations ranged from 0.04 to 25 μg/mL, and the final concentration of DMSO did not exceed 0.5%, which was found to be non-toxic for the parasite (negative control). Standard drugs were screened at the highest concentration of 1 µM. Plates were then incubated at 37 °C under a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 72 h. Afterwards, 20 µl of each well was transferred to a new plate containing 100 µl of Malstat solution [55 mM Tris, 0.22 M L-lactic acid, 0.17 mM acetylpyridine adenine dinucleotide [APAD; Sigma-Aldrich], 0.2% [v/v] Triton X-100(Sigma-Aldrich), pH 9.0] and 25 µl NBT/PES solution (1.96 mM nitrotetrazolium blue chloride, 0.24 mM phenazine ethosulphate, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany). Plates were left to develop in the dark for 30 min at room temperature before recording the absorbance at 650 nm using a microtiter plate reader (TECAN Infinite M200, USA). The data analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism 5.0, and dose–response curves were drawn to determine the median inhibitory concentrations (IC50s). The degree of resistance (resistance index; RI) was determined by comparing the antiplasmodial activity of the test sample on chloroquine-susceptible vs multidrug-resistant strains of P. falciparum using the following formula: RI: (IC50 multidrug-resistant strain) / IC50 (chloroquine-sensitive strain). The experiments were carried out three times, and the IC50 value was calculated as the mean of the three measurements taken from these trials.

Inhibition of P. falciparum late-stage IV/V gametocyte viability

To evaluate the impact of the isolated compound and subfractions on late-stage NF54-E (MRA-1000) gametocytes (> 90% stage IV/V, 2% gametocytaemia), the experimental method previously reported [31] was carried out using a P. falciparum lactate dehydrogenase (PfLDH)-based assay following 48 h of drug treatment. Dual point inhibition (at 1 and 5 μg/mL, n = 1, in technical triplicates) was used for subfractions and the isolated compound, while methylene blue was used as a positive control in this experiment. Microsoft Excel was used to calculate the percentage of gametocyte inhibition.

Haemolysis assay

A haemolysis assay was performed as previously described [34] against normal O+ human red blood cells to assess the haemolytic effect of the test subfractions and eschweilenol C at concentrations ranging from 100 to 0.16 µg/mL. Briefly, 250 µl of O+ human red blood cells at 2% haematocrit were mixed with 250 µl of the test sample at a concentration ranging from 100 to 0.16 µg/mL in an Eppendorf tube, followed by incubation for 2 h at 37 °C. Triton X-100 0.1% (v/v) and phosphate buffer saline (PBS), which induced 100% and 0% haemolysis in RBCs, were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. After 2 h of incubation at 37 °C, the preparation composed of lysed red blood cells and free haemoglobin was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was separated. Next, the amount of haemoglobin released in each supernatant was quantified spectrophotometrically at 540 nm using a microtiter plate reader (TECAN Infinite M200, USA) and compared to the negative control. Assessment of selectivity on normal cells

A Resazurin based-assay was used to evaluate the in vitro cytotoxicity of active subfractions and eschweilenol C as described [35] against Vero ATCC CRL 1586 (African green monkey kidney) and Raw 264.7 (macrophage) cells. These cells were cultured in a T-25 cm2 flask containing DMEM (Sigma-Aldrich, Munich, Germany) supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich, Munich, Germany) and 10% fetal bovine serum (ATCC), which were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. For each experiment, 80–90% confluent cell culture was trypsinized, counted using a Neubauer counting chamber and seeded onto 96-well flat-bottomed plates treated for cell culture (Costar, USA) at a cell density of 104 cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then treated with serially diluted concentrations of active inhibitors for 48 h. In the assay plates, Podophyllotoxin was included at concentrations ranging from 100 µM to 0.16 µM as positive control while a 0.5% DMSO solution, which demonstrated 100% cell viability, served as the negative control. After incubation, 10 µl of resazurin solution (0.15 mg/mL prepared in PBS) was added to each well, and plates were re-incubated for 4 h to allow the conversion of non-toxic cell-permeable resazurin (weakly fluorescent blue dye) to the highly fluorescent red dye resorufin by viable cells. The level of fluorescence that positively correlates with cell viability was measured using an Infinite M200 plate reader (Tecan, USA) at excitation and emission wavelengths of 530 nm and 590 nm, respectively. The inhibitory percentage of cell proliferation was calculated from the absorbance values of untreated and treated cells. Sigmoidal concentration–response curves were plotted using GraphPad Prism (v5), from which the half-maximal cytotoxic concentration (CC50) values were determined. Selectivity indices (SI) [SI = CC50/IC50] of test samples were determined based on their antiplasmodial activity (IC50) and cytotoxicity (CC50 on Vero and Raw cells). The experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the CC50 value was reported as the average of the three measurements obtained from the three experiments.

Haemozoin inhibition assay

The ability of active antiplasmodial subfractions and the isolated compound to inhibit the haemozoin (β-haematin) produced by P. falciparum in cultures was assessed by using drug concentrations at their IC50 (Ti030.54 µg/mL, Ti040.49 µg/mL, and eschweilenol C490.74 µM) upon incubation for 72 h (assay time) [36]. Artemisinin and chloroquine were used as positive controls. After the incubation time had elapsed, cultures were transferred in an Eppendorf tube and centrifuged for 7 min at 1300 rpm to dispose of the culture medium. The infected erythrocyte pellet, composed of membrane debris and β-haematin, was subjected to lysis by 0.01% saponin at 25 °C for 10 min to release the parasites from red blood cells. The released parasites were further washed thrice with PBS for 5 min at 1500 rpm, re-suspended in 2.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate buffer solution (SDS in PBS), and subjected to spin at 20,000 g for 1 h. The supernatant was disposed of, and the insoluble haemozoin pellet was washed in 2.5% SDS in PBS and then dissolved in 20 mM NaOH. Next, the absorbance of the preparation was read at 405 nm, and a standard curve was generated to determine the quantity of β-haematin, from which the amount of haemozoin crystals was deduced. All assays were performed in triplicate.

Time-kill kinetics

The kinetics of growth of P. falciparum multidrug-resistant strain (Dd2) was monitored upon treatment with the most promising antiplasmodial samples viz. Subfractions Ti03, Ti04, and eschweilenol C according to a previously described procedure [37]. Briefly, a preparation containing an asynchronous parasite blood culture with test samples (Ti03, Ti04, and eschweilenol C) was assessed using a PfLDH-based method to yield corresponding IC50 values. Three exposure times were employed for each test sample, viz.72 (standard assay time), 48, and 24 h. The IC50 ratios, normalized to the 72 h exposure, were subsequently used to classify test samples (Ti03, Ti04, and eschweilenol C) as fast or slow-acting.

Plasmodium falciparum asexual-blood stage-specific analysis and post-drug effect of the most promising antiplasmodial hits

The rationale for evaluating actives against the three developmental stages of the malaria parasite (rings, trophozoites, and schizonts) stems from the intricate life cycle of P. falciparum and the specific biological processes that characterize each stage. Each developmental phase exhibits distinct metabolic demands and vulnerabilities, positioning them as crucial targets for therapeutic intervention. To achieve that, highly synchronized resistant strain of P. falciparum (Dd2) rings, trophozoites, and schizonts stages (2% parasitaemia and 1% haematocrit) were treated with Ti03, Ti04 and eschweilenol C at their 99% inhibitory concentration (IC99) for 48 h (rings), 24 h (trophozoites) and 12 h (schizonts) under standard culture conditions. Giemsa-stained thin smears were prepared at different time intervals (0, 6, 24, and 48 h for rings, 0, 6, 18, and 24 h for trophozoites, and 12 h for schizonts) for microscopic examinations of the intraerythrocytic maturation of the parasite and to determine the effects on the parasite growth [38, 39] and the stage-to-stage transition. After incubation, test samples were removed by three-time media wash, and the cultures were re-suspended in a complete culture medium and maintained under standard culture conditions for 48 h in rings, 24 h in trophozoites, and schizonts. Next, thin smears were prepared from control and treated wells and processed for Giemsa staining. The percentage of stage-specific inhibition by Ti03, Ti04, and eschweilenol was compared to the corresponding drug-free control by counting 3000 cells (10–12 fields) for each parasite stage. Parasitized and non-parasitized cells were counted by microscopy to determine stage-specific growth inhibition, cytostatic or cytocidal effect, and growth suppression following post-sample exposure on P. falciparum Dd2. The parasites with pyknotic morphology were considered nonviable cells (death parasites).

In silico molecular docking and ADME studies of eschweilenol

In silico molecular docking studies were performed to support the pharmacodynamics investigations and inhibition of haemozoin formation. Research has been concentrated on P. falciparum M1 alanyl aminopeptidase (PfM1AAP) and P. falciparum M17 leucyl aminopeptidase (PfM17LAP). These enzymes play a critical role in the final stages of haemoglobin degradation, producing vital amino acids that support parasite growth. Consequently, they present promising opportunities for the development of novel anti-malarial drugs.

Structure drawing, cleaning, and optimization

ChemDraw Professional version 15.0 software (PerkinElmer Informatics, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to clean the drawing and geometry of the antiplasmodial compound eschweilenol C. The two-dimensional (2D) structures were transformed into three-dimensional (3D) structures using Chem3D version 15.0. Afterwards, the energy of the 3D structure was minimized using a molecular mechanics-2 (MM2) force field.

Molecular docking and binding energy studies

The docking analysis of selected targets (P. falciparum M1 alanyl aminopeptidase (3EBG), P. falciparum M17 leucyl aminopeptidase (3KQX)) and the ligand eschweilenol C was accomplished by employing AutoDock Vina v1.5.7 (Molecular Graphics Lab at The Scripps Research Institute, La Jolla, CA92037, USA). 3D crystallographic structures of the target enzymes were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. An expanded version of the PDB format was employed for the coordinate files, including atomic partial charges and PDBQT files. The software automatically transformed the PDB files to PDBQT, which was then used in the docking process. To obtain the appropriate tautomeric and ionized states, polar hydrogen atoms were added to the target proteins for amino acid residues. The native ligand of the co-crystallized complex was first removed and re-docked to its matching binding pocket using AutoDock Vina v1.5.7 to standardize the software. The ultimate binding/docking energy was determined by averaging the three times the ligands and receptors were docked. For each target protein, the binding poses with the lowest docking energy served as the ultimate model for the post-docking study. This analysis was done using PyMOL (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 1.5.0.4 Schrodinger, LLC, USA) and UCSF Chimera v1.5.3 (NCRR, University of California, San Francisco, supported by NIH P41 RR001081).

Virtual prediction of pharmacokinetics properties

The ADMET (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity) properties of drugs are fundamental in assessing their therapeutic potential and safety profile across all human diseases [40]. In this context, the ADME parameters and Lipinski rule of eschweilenol C were analysed using a free online tool, ADMETlab 2.0 (https://admetmesh.scbdd.com/). All the studied substances were transformed into their standard Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System (SMILES) format and then uploaded to ADMETlab 2.0 for analysis [41]. This web server provides the users easy access to the comprehensive, accurate, and efficient prediction of the ADMET profiles for chemicals, including absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, toxicity properties, and some essential physicochemical and medicinal chemistry properties. Significant chemical descriptors, such as polar surface area (PSA), play a crucial role in fraction absorption, and low molecular weight (MW) is essential for oral absorption. Therefore, the bioavailability of the eschweilenol C was assessed using TPSA analysis.

Results

Structural characterization of the isolated compound

Compound 1, trivially named eschweilenol C (Fig. 1), was isolated from T. brownii as a light-yellow amorphous powder. Although this is the first report of compound 1 in this species, its presence is not surprising since plants of the genus Terminalia are rich in ellagic acid derivatives. Its structure was determined using 1D and 2D NMR data and by their comparison with those from the literature [28]. In its 1H-NMR spectrum, signals for five oxymethine protons and a doublet (δ 1.14) for methyl groups were observed (Table 1), indicating the presence of one 6-deoxysugar [27]. Two singlets (δ 7.74 and 7.48) in the aromatic region suggest the ellagic acid moiety [28]. The glycoside was easily determined as rhamnose through analysis of the chemical shifts and coupling patterns of its proton signals and by comparison with the literature [28]. Although the aglycon bears only two singlets (δ 7.72 and 7.46) in the aromatic region, there are 14 carbon signals in the low field region of its 13C-NMR spectrum, including two carbonyl esters and 12 aromatic carbons, of which 6 are oxygenated. This information indicated that the main skeleton consisted of an ellagic acid moiety attached to a rhamnose unit whose signals at δC/H (100.6/5.46 (d, J = 1.2, 1H)). Based on the literature data [28], all carborn signals were assigned and the two main fragments were linked based on the HMBC spectrum, which displayed a strong correlation peak between the signal at δH 5.46 and δC 148.6. This proposed assignment was fully supported by the heteronuclear multiple bond correlation (HMBC) evidence data, as shown in Table 1. Both vinyl proton signals (H-5 and H-5′) had similar patterns for their long-range correlations to carbons signals at δ 114.9 (C-1), 141.6 (C-3), 146.8 (C-4), 107.9 (C-6), 159.5 (C-7) and 112.2 (C-1′), 140.1 (C-3′), 149.1 (C-4′), 108.5 (C-6′), and 159.4 (C-7′). The anomeric configuration was confirmed as R by the small coupling constant of the anomeric proton (J = 1.2 Hz). Subfractions and eschweilenol C selectively inhibit the growth of P. falciparum asexual-blood stages

Overall, eight subfractions were obtained, five (05) from T. ivorensis (Ti01, Ti02, Ti03, Ti04, and Ti05) and three (03) from T. brownii (Tb01, Tb02, and Tb03) along with one compound (eschweilenol C), which was isolated and characterized from another subfraction of T. brownii (Tb04). To assess the selectivity of active antiplasmodial subfractions and eschweilenol C, in vitro cytotoxicity was assayed on Vero, Raw, and red blood cells. Ti03, Ti04, and eschweilenol C stage-specifically arrested Plasmodium growth at rings and trophozoites stages

Using a highly synchronized multi-resistant strain of P. falciparum (Dd2), the growth of ring, trophozoite, and schizont forms in the presence of Ti03, Ti04, and eschweilenol C was measured at their 99% inhibitory concentration (IC99,) after 48, 24 and 12 h of incubation, respectively. For each experiment, parasite growth was monitored at different post-invasion time points (at 12 h intervals) for cell cycle progression. After incubation, stage-specific effects of the test sample were analyzed microscopically using Giemsa staining. As illustrated in Fig. 6, compared to untreated cultures, Ti03, Ti04, and eschweilenol C induced rapid (6hpi) arrest of the parasite development from the early ring and trophozoite stages (Fig. 8) compared to the untreated cultures. Also, at 6 h post-invasion (hpi), Ti03, Ti04 and eschweilenol C arrested parasite stage transition as evidenced by the presence of pyknotic forms of the parasites in the drug-treated cultures compared to the untreated control (Fig. 8). Following 48 h (rings) and 24hpi (trophozoite) parasite incubation with test samples, the percentage of growth inhibition ranged from 73.33% (Ti03) to 82.13% (eschweilenol C) for rings and 72.95% (Ti03) to 85.01% (eschweilenol C) for trophozoite. However, we observed a low inhibition on schizonts stages ranging from 41.59% (Ti03) to 58.88% (eschweilenol C) (Table 5 and Fig. 7). Molecular docking of eschweilenol C with selected targets

Eschweilenol C displayed a strong binding affinity with selected targets

The molecular interactions were carried out between the antiplasmodial compound eschweilenol C and P. falciparum M1 alanyl aminopeptidase (PfM1AAP), and P. falciparum M17 leucyl aminopeptidase (PfM17LAP) as target enzymes. Table 6 summarizes the binding affinities of eschweilenol C with PfM1AAP and PfM17LAP. Eschweilenol C showed low binding energies compared to the values obtained with the standard antimalarial drug chloroquine, thus exhibiting strong affinity with PfM1AAP and PfM17LAP. The complexes eschweilenol C-PfM1AA and eschweilenol-PfM17LAP were stabilized mainly by 02 and 07 hydrogen bonds, respectively, and amino acids that were involved in the binding pockets were GLY460 and ALA46 for eschweilenol C-PfM1AA and SER391, SER554, ARG463, LYS386, LEU487(2), and THR486 for eschweilenol C-PfM17LAP respectively (Table 6 and Fig. 9). Regarding standards control, chloroquine displayed two hydrogen bonds with amino acids (GLY460 and ALA461) in its binding pocking when docked with PfM1AAP. The binding pockets of eschweilenol C, and chloroquine were dominated by hydrophobic and van der Waals interactions, which are well known to increase the binding energies of ligand-receptor connections. Eschweilenol C showed acceptable pharmacokinetics

The physicochemical properties of eschweilenol C were evaluated using the freely available online tool ADMETlab 2.0 (https://admetmesh.scbdd.com/). Table 7 summarizes the physicochemical parameters of the antiplasmodial compound eschweilenol C and the standard drug chloroquine. According to the criteria reported [40], favorable physicochemical properties include compound lipophilicity (LogP) between −0.7 and + 5.0, molecular weight (MW) between 150 and 500 g/mol, topological polar surface area (TPSA) between 20 and 130 Å2, solubility: log S < 6 saturation: fraction of carbons in the sp3 hybridization not less than 0.25, and flexibility: no more than nine rotatable bonds, were retrieved. Consequently, eschweilenol C does not satisfy all the necessary criteria to qualify as an oral medication, particularly since it fails to adhere to Lipinski's Rule of Five (see Table 7). In addition, the TPSA analysis is essential in evaluating the bioavailability of eschweilenol C as a potential antimalarial agent. TPSA measures a compound's polarity and hydrogen bonding potential, which affect its ability to cross biological membranes and reach target sites [40]. Compounds with a TPSA below 140 Å2 typically demonstrate better oral bioavailability, indicating a greater likelihood of effective absorption through the intestinal barrier into the systemic circulation [40]. Therefore, medicinal chemistry investigation is needed to enhance its chemical properties as a starting point for developing an antimalarial drug candidate. Discussion

The emergence of resistant Plasmodium falciparum strains in Africa underscores an urgent need to develop alternative antimalarial therapy that quickly targets multiple malaria parasite stages, including novel mechanisms of action that mitigate drug resistance. This paper reports the extent of multistage potency of T. ivorensis and T. brownii-derived products against stage-specific intra-erythrocytic asexual stages of P. falciparum. In addition, they equally emphasize the molecular interaction between the isolated compound eschweilenol C and key target enzymes involved in haemoglobin metabolism and parasite survival. In prior work, we identified ethyl acetate fraction (TiWEa) from T. ivorensis and methanol/ethyl acetate fraction [TbMMeOH/Ea(1:1)] from T. brownii as a vital source of antimalarial scaffolds that selectively inhibit the growth of P. falciparum with an IC50 less than 1 µg/ml [26]. This study extends prior work by further fractionating the antiplasmodial fractions TiWEa and TbMMeOH:Ea (1:1) using silica gel column chromatography. Afterwards, the derived subfractions and the isolated compound eschweilenol C were assessed in vitro for their activity against asexual-blood stages of multidrug-resistant (PfDd2), chloroquine-susceptible (Pf3D7) and mutant-resistant (PfDd2-GNF156) strains of P. falciparum. Five of the eight subfractions obtained showed good (IC50 < 5 µg/ml) activity on PfDd2, Pf3D7, and PfDd2-GNF156. Interestingly, 02 subfractions (Ti03; Ti04) and eschweilenol C exhibited excellent (IC50 < 1 µg/mL) activity on multi-resistant (PfDd2) and susceptible (Pf3D7) strains of P. falciparum. In addition, Ti03, Ti04, and eschweilenol C showed no reduction in their potency when tested on mutant-resistant (PfDd2-GNF156) strains of P. falciparum. Ti03 and Ti04 were 0.40-fold more potent on the susceptible (3D7) strain compared to the parent fractions, whereas they maintained the same range of activity on the multidrug-resistant (Dd2) strain of P. falciparum. The ability of Ti03 and Ti04 to retain efficacy against mutant strains is a critical advantage, suggesting their potential as viable sources of therapeutic options in the face of growing drug resistance. In contrast, the subfraction Ti01 exhibited a decrease in efficacy against the three strains of P. falciparum. This reduction may be attributed to the uneven distribution of active compounds among the active subfractions. The chemical composition of these extracts, previously examined through LC–MS/MS analysis, included ellagic acid and derivatives, triterpenoids, and various other compounds such as leucodelphinidin, papyriogenin D, and miltiodiol [26]. Interestingly, ellagic acid (EA) and derivatives have been largely reported to inhibit the growth of P. falciparum through a well-known mechanism. This previous chemical profile supports column chromatography results, which for the first time report the isolation of 4-α-rhamnopyranosyl ellagic acid (eschweilenol C) from T. brownii, together with excellent antiplasmodial potency (IC50 < 1 µg/mL). Being a derivative of ellagic acid known to display multistage antiplasmodial activity (IC50 values ranging from 105 to 330 nM, no matter the strains used) [42], 4-α-L-rhamnopyranosyl ellagic is proposed to disrupt critical biological processes within the parasites as ellagic acid, providing a promising avenue for the development of new antimalarial therapies. The difference in activity of eschweilenol C compared to ellagic acid reported in the literature might be explained by the presence of appended sugars at position 4, which is known to increase solubility and hydrogen bonding. [43]. However, this research suggests that the attached sugars are not just affecting the solubility of the compound or its ability to reach the cellular target(s). Instead, it indicates that the sugars may induce significant interactions with cellular components, reducing the antiplasmodial action compared to the original phenolic compound, ellagic acid. Subfractions and eschweilenol C from T. ivorensis and T. brownii were additionally assessed for their ability to block malaria transmission through dual-point inhibition of mature gametocytes stages (IV/V) of P. falciparum.

As a result, all subfractions and eschweilenol C have no activity against later gametocyte stages of falciparum at the tested concentration (IC50 > 5 µg/mL). These results are unsurprising because researchers underscored that most antimalarial compounds usually targeting P. falciparum asexual proliferation failed to inhibit transmissible gametocyte stages [44]. The differences in activity in asexual blood stages compared to sexual stages may be attributed to variations in mitochondrial development and gene expression [45]. Sexual stages exhibit more developed mitochondria, higher respiratory complex activity, and increased cytochrome b gene copy number and expression compared to asexual stages [45]. Moreover, these findings are supported by prior work by Soré et al. [46] on a plant belonging to the Terminalia species. This study illustrated that ethanol and methanol leaf extracts of Terminalia macroptera exhibited activity against mature gametocyte stages of P. falciparum, with respective IC50 values of 32.74 and 36.34 µg/mL, suggesting that Terminalia species-derived products may exert their anti-gametocytocidal effect on later gametocyte stages at higher concentrations.

Previous studies showed that the parent phenolic compound ellagic acid exerts its antiplasmodial action on trophozoites and schizonts stages of the P. falciparum erythrocytic life cycle [42]. These parasitic stages are well-known to produce a high amount of ferriprotoporphyrin IX (FP) during haemoglobin digestion [47]. Of note, FP induces oxidative damage to the Plasmodium parasite, leading to its death, and thus, the Plasmodium needs to detoxify FP formation to remain viable. Therefore, to escape oxidative stress induced by haemoglobin metabolism, FP is detoxified by its biomineralization to haemozoin and degraded via glutathione (GSH) by a radical-mediated mechanism [48, 49]. Therefore, the amount of haemozoin measures the parasite's capacity to neutralize toxic-free haem by forming haemozoin in the presence of a drug, whereas an increase in free heme results in a decrease in parasite survival. Inhibition of haemozoin formation assay was conducted to elucidate the possible mode of action of Ti03, Ti04, and eschweilenol C. As a result, we observed a significant reduction in the production of haemozoin upon treatment with chloroquine, artemisinin, Ti03, Ti04, and eschweilenol C compared to untreated infected erythrocytes, suggesting that the antiplasmodial action of eschweilenol C might be similar to that of the parent phenolic compound, ellagic acid.

The findings from the killing kinetics indicate that all examined subfractions, along with eschweilenol C, demonstrated a slow action in decreasing parasite load. However, in the realm of antimalarial drug development, the pharmacokinetic profile of slow-acting compounds such as eschweilenol C presents both unique challenges and significant opportunities for therapeutic use [50]. While rapid-acting agents are often favored for their immediate effectiveness in reducing parasite load, slow-acting compounds may be invaluable in sustaining treatment regimens. They can play a crucial role in preventing recrudescence and addressing issues of drug resistance [51].

The extended pharmacological action of eschweilenol C enables a gradual reduction in parasite populations, potentially decreasing the risk of resistance emergence that is often associated with fast-acting therapies, which exert intense selective pressures on malaria parasites [51]. Furthermore, the potential application of eschweilenol C and similar compounds as chemoprophylactic agents is particularly compelling; their prolonged action may offer extended protection against malaria in high-risk populations. Consequently, incorporating such slow-acting compounds into combination therapies alongside rapid-acting agents could enhance overall treatment efficacy while simultaneously alleviating risks associated with drug resistance.

The intraerythrocytic cycle of P. falciparum takes approximately 48 h, during which it shows different morphological stages, including ring, trophozoite (the most metabolically active stage), and the multinucleated schizont stage [52]. Current antimalarial drugs such as chloroquine and sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine mainly target trophozoites and schizonts, with only artemisinin targeting all malaria parasite stages [53]. Therefore, analysing the stage-specific action of Ti03, Ti04, and eschweilenol C has helped to understand their targeted stages across the P. falciparum life cycle. Ti03, Ti04, and eschweilenol C were incubated with synchronized rings, trophozoites, and schizonts stages of multi-resistant (Dd2) strains of P. falciparum. Results suggest that T. ivorensis and T. brownii-derived subfractions (Ti03, Ti04) and eschweilenol C stage-specifically arrest the ring and trophozoite stages, thereby inhibiting their transition into schizonts. The treatment did not affect the schizont maturation and merozoite invasion of uninfected erythrocytes. The effect of those plant derivatives on trophozoites could be justified by their ability to inhibit haemozoin formation, as revealed by the haemozoin formation assay. Of note, chloroquine and sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine mainly affect trophozoites by inhibiting the globin catabolism pathway, providing nutrients for the parasite, and folate biosynthesis [54]. Interestingly, a previous study demonstrated that, as eschweilenol C isolated in this study, parent compounds ellagic acid exert its antiplasmodial efficacy by arresting the Plasmodium growth at the trophozoites and schizonts stages [55], suggesting that ellagic acid and derivatives could exert a similar mechanism of action. eschweilenol C has emerged as a promising therapeutic candidate in the battle against malaria, particularly due to its potent inhibitory effects on the early developmental stages of P. falciparum, specifically the rings and trophozoites. However, its inactivity against schizonts and merozoite egress presents challenges that could be leveraged for therapeutic advancements. This stage-specific activity profile suggests that eschweilenol C may be especially effective in disrupting the early development of the parasite within red blood cells, potentially thwarting the establishment of infection during critical phases of the parasite’s life cycle. Such characteristics could position eschweilenol C as a valuable asset in combination therapies aimed at enhancing overall treatment efficacy while reducing the risk of resistance development. Furthermore, the selective inhibition of these early stages may facilitate the strategic application of eschweilenol C in chemoprophylaxis, protecting against initial infections, particularly in endemic regions. By targeting the early stages of the parasite's lifecycle, eschweilenol C has the potential to lower transmission rates and impede the progression of malaria before the manifestation of symptoms.

In silico studies of eschweleinol C revealed low binding energies, thus highlighting solid affinities with the targets PfM1AAP and PfM17LAP, compared to the antimalarial drugs chloroquine. PfM1AAP and PfM17LAP have been identified as key enzymes that are essential for the survival and virulence of P. falciparum. Inhibitors of PfM1AAP and PfM17LAP have shown anti-P. falciparum effects at nanomolar concentrations [56, 57]. Therefore, the high affinity displayed by eschweleinol C vis-à-vis PfM1AAP and PfM17LAP corroborates the observed in vitro antiplasmodial efficacy. Thus, it is not unreasonable to speculate that eschweleinol C might have exerted its antiplasmodial effects by inhibiting the enzymes PfM1AAP and PfM17LAP. Accumulated evidence has also demonstrated the implication of PfM17LAP on the susceptibility of P. falciparum toward several natural products [58, 59]. Although eschweleinol C displayed poor bioavailability, as evidenced by the ADME results, in vitro and in silico studies demonstrate that this compound can be exploited further in antimalarial drug discovery. Nevertheless, more studies, including structural modifications of functional groups complemented with pharmacokinetics, in dept mechanistic basis of the antiplasmodial action are warranted to support the antiplasmodial claim of eschweleinol C.

Conclusion

This study revealed that eschweilenol C, isolated from the stem bark of T. brownii, is a promising antiplasmodial agent targeting multiple asexual blood stages of P. falciparum. Subfractions Ti03 and Ti04 from the stem bark of T. ivorensis were found to possess significant activity against asexual-blood stages of P. falciparum, with preferential killing against rings and trophozoite stages. Moreover, Ti03, Ti04, and eschweilenol C inhibited haemozoin formation significantly, which might explain the observed antiplasmodial action. Further, in silico studies of eschweilenol C vis-à-vis selected targets of P. falciparum (PfM1AAP and PfM17LAP) revealed strong binding affinity as evidenced by their low binding energies. Overall, this study demonstrates for the first time the stage-specific efficacy of subfractions and eschweilenol C derived from T. ivorensis and T. brownii, thus validating the ethnopharmacological use of Terminalia plants in the treatment of malaria and related symptoms. While these results provide mechanistic support for the Terminalia species derivative for malaria drug discovery programmes, the study is limited to in vitro assays and does not exclude other possible mechanism of action or synergistic effects within subfractions. Therefore, additional investigations including in vivo antimalarial efficacy, toxicity profile, pharmacokinetics profiling, and detailed mechanistic analyses are warranted to fully evaluate eschweilenol C potential as a lead compound for antimalarial drug development.
