Investigation of cartilage modification for controlling immune reaction against xenogeneic cartilage
Authors: Sakura Uto, Daisuke Mori, Tomoaki Sakamoto, Satoru Nisizawa, Fumiko Yano, Shinsuke Ohba, Taku Saito, Tsuyoshi Takato, Kazuto Hoshi, Atsuhiko Hikita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAllogeneic cartilages are decellularized when they are transplanted subcutaneously to avoid the immunoreaction in clinical practice. However, transplanting cartilage with chondrocytes would be beneficial because chondrocytes are essential for the maintenance of the cartilage. In this study, we investigated the effect of modifications to cartilage before transplantation for the suppression of immunoreactions. We utilized xenogeneic models because they are often employed in preclinical safety evaluations prior to clinical trials. If xenogeneic immunoreactions can be controlled, allogeneic rejection should be easier to manage.MethodsCartilaginous particles differentiated from human iPS cells were modified in two ways. For coverage with syngeneic chondrocytes, cartilaginous particles were cocultured with murine chondrocytes for 7 days. For decellularization, particles were undergone freeze & thaw with or without DNase cycles of either 5 or 15. After these modifications, cartilaginous particles were filled in meshed tubular PLA scaffolds and subcutaneously transplanted into C57BL/6-Tg (CAG-EGFP) for 8 weeks. Immunoreactions were histologically evaluated by immunostaining against CD3, CD4 and Foxp3.ResultsIn vitro studies showed that chondrocytes successfully covered the cartilaginous particle irrespective of decellularization. In addition, decellularization of cartilage was possible without apparent breakdown of the matrix structure. In transplantation experiments, severe immune reactions were not observed histologically. However, CD4 (+) cells and CD3 (+) cells increased when cartilaginous particles received both chondrocyte coverage and decellularization, while Foxp3 (+) cells were not observed.ConclusionsContrary to our expectations, combination of decellularization and surface coverage of cartilaginous transplants increased T cells, suggesting the complexity of the immunoreaction against xenogeneic cartilages.

Introduction

Regenerative medicine has been developed to recover damaged, deformed, or deficient cartilage because the intrinsic repair ability of cartilage is limited. Our group developed an implant-type regenerative cartilage for rhinoplasty using in vitro-expanded autologous auricular chondrocytes [1,2]. However, the number of cells prepared using this method is insufficient for larger regenerative cartilages such as the auricle. For this reason, we utilized a human iPS cell-originated cartilaginous particle [3,4] and a meshed tubular scaffold to form an ear-shaped regenerative cartilage [5].

Considering the large budget and long time required to induce the cartilaginous particle, the use of allogeneic iPS cells is practical, which raises concerns about immunoreaction. In fact, in our previous study, subcutaneously transplanted allogeneic femoral heads or xenogeneic cartilaginous particles into immunocompetent mice caused immunoreaction [6].

Generally, cartilage is considered to be immune-privileged [7], while the chondrocytes are immunogenic [8,9]. Clinically, allogeneic cartilages are processed to eliminate live cells to control immunoreactions [[10], [11], [12]], although chondrocytes are essential to maintain the extracellular matrix of cartilage [9,[13], [14], [15], [16]]. It may reduce the risk of exposing immunogenic allogeneic chondrocytes if the cartilaginous particle is covered with autologous cartilage while live chondrocytes in the cartilaginous particle are reduced.

In the present study, we examined the effects of modifications of human iPSC-derived cartilaginous particles on the immunoreaction upon implantation into mice. Although such modifications are necessary in allogeneic transplantation in clinical settings, we utilized a xenogeneic model for following reasons: 1) preclinical safety evaluations of the final human regenerative cartilage product are frequently required prior to clinical trials; and 2) achieving effective control over the immunoreaction in a xenogeneic context suggests that avoiding rejection in allogeneic transplantation would be significantly more feasible. Auricular chondrocytes of C57BL/6-Tg (CAG-EGFP) were cocultured with cartilaginous particles to cover the surface of the particles. The procedure to reduce live chondrocytes from the cartilaginous particle was also examined to an extent that does not deteriorate the extracellular matrix. By subcutaneously transplanting these particles into immunocompetent mice, immune reactions and the changes in the graft were examined histologically.

Cartilaginous particles

Cartilaginous particles from human iPS cells induced from an HLA homozygous male donor were provided from Professor Noriyuki Tsumaki's laboratory of Center for iPS Cell Research and Application, Kyoto University approved by ethics committees of both Kyoto University and the University of Tokyo (Fig. 1B). Cartilaginous particles were cultured with differentiation medium provided from the same laboratory (Composition not open to public).

Decellularization of cartilaginous particle

Cartilaginous particles were frozen with liquid nitrogen, thawed at room temperature, and washed with deionized water as previously described [22]. Part of frozen & thawed cartilaginous particles were processed with DNase as previously described [16,23,24]. Briefly, cartilaginous particles were treated with 4 % deoxycholic acid, and with 1000 KU/mL DNase and these procedures were counted as 1 cycle. DNase cycles were either 5, 10, or 15.

Subcutaneous transplantation of modified cartilaginous particles in meshed tubular PLA scaffold

6-week-old male C57BL/6-Tg (CAG-EGFP) were anesthetized with Isoflurane, and were transplanted the sample subcutaneously (Fig. 7A). After 8 weeks, mice were euthanized, and the samples were collected, including skin and muscle sandwiching the graft.

Histological analysis

Samples were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde in PBS, decalcified with Morse solution (10% trisodium citrate dihydrate, 22.5% formic acid), and sectioned. Sections were stained with hematoxylin, eosin, 0.05% toluidine blue, 0.1% safranin O, immunohistochemistry for COL1, COL2, CD3, CD4 and Foxp3, and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) for human XY chromosome.

Antigen retrieval for immunohistochemistry was as follows; COL1 with 0.1 % trypsin, COL2 with 2.5% hyaluronidase, CD3 with Dako real target retrieval solution, CD4 with HistoVTone, and Foxp3 with Universal HIER. Following antigen retrieval, samples were treated with 3 % H2O2 in methanol, Blocking One, primary and secondary antibodies, ABC, and DAB, and counterstained with hematoxylin and mounted with Mount-quick. The negative control was normal rabbit IgG.

For FISH, sections were processed according to the protocol provided by the manufacturer. Briefly, sections were treated with either 2x or 1x SSC, 0.1 % pepsin in 1 N HCl, and 50 % formamide. Sections were mounted with Mounting Medium with DAPI.

Image J analysis of histological findings

GFP positive area in fluorescence image was analyzed with Image J [25]. Immunohistochemical image was separated to DAB and hematoxylin channel by color deconvolution plugin [26] of Image J [25]. DAB positive area was analyzed. Hematoxylin and eosin-stained image was separated to hematoxylin and eosin channel by same matter and the histogram of hematoxylin channel under 254 was analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using either Excel Statistics (Social Survey Research Information Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) or R 4.3.2 [27]. The data were analyzed with either Fisher's test, Tukey-Kramer's test, or Dunnett's test [28]. p-Values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Mouse auricular chondrocytes covered the surface of the human iPS-derived cartilaginous particle in coculture

At first, the auricular chondrocytes of C57BL/6-Tg (CAG-EGFP) (Fig. 1A) and cartilaginous particle [3,4] (Fig. 1B) were co-cultured for 7 days to optimize the condition for the coverage of the particle by mouse chondrocytes (Fig. 2A). The pH of the proliferation medium seemed lower than those of the differentiation medium, as indicated by their colors (Fig. 2B). The GFP (+) areas were broadly observed on the surface of the particles, except for those cocultured with 5 × 104 cells in the differentiation medium, which were statistically lower than those cocultured with 5 × 104 cells in the proliferation medium (Fig. 2B and C) (Dunnett's one-sided test, p = 0.0397). Histologically, GFP (+) cells covered the cartilaginous particle in the proliferation medium, while few GFP (+) cells were seen on the cartilaginous particle in the differentiation medium (Supplementary Fig. S1). In time course observations, the GFP positive area on the cartilaginous particle increased from day 1 to day 7, and there were statistical significances among each time point compared with day 7 (Fig. 3B and C) (Tuley-Kramer's test, day 0 vs day 7; p = 0.0339, day 1 vs day 7; p = 0.0169, day 4 vs day 7; p = 0.0274). Taking these into consideration, coculture of the cartilaginous particle with 5 × 104 cells in the proliferation medium for 7 days was performed for sufficient coverage of the cartilaginous particle with mouse auricular chondrocytes hereafter.

Redifferentiation of human auricular chondrocytes on the cartilaginous particle

Human auricular chondrocytes were also co-cultured with the cartilaginous particle in the proliferation medium for 1 week (Fig. 4A). Thick layers of cells were observed on the surface (Fig. 4B, left panels). In addition, the particles covered by auricular chondrocytes were further cultured in differentiation medium for 3 and 7 weeks. Cell density on the surface gradually reduced and the matrix increased (Fig. 4B, middle and right panels). On the other hand, no apparent change in cartilaginous particle was observed throughout the culture (Fig. 4B). FISH for human XY chromosome showed that the human auricular chondrocyte (female) attached to cartilaginous particle (male) and matured (Fig. 4C).

Decellularization of cartilaginous particle

To seek the possibility for the use of decellularized cartilaginous particles, appropriate DNase cycles for the cartilaginous particle following freeze & thaw were determined (Fig. 5A). The number of empty lacunae increased and hematoxylin dye-affinity seemed to deteriorate over the culture period (Fig. 5B). Statistical analysis of the empty lacunae of the cartilaginous particle suggested that the number of empty lacunae with DNase 15 cycles was greater than that of samples without DNase treatment as early as 1 week culture (Dunnett's one-sided test, 1 week culture, non-decellularized vs DNase 15 cycles; p = 0.0482, Tukey-Kramer's test, 4 weeks culture, non-decellularized vs DNase 15 cycles; p = 0.0269). For the total 8 weeks of culture, DNase cycles of 5 or more increased the number of empty lacunae significantly (Dunnett's two-sided test, non-decellularized vs DNase 5 cycles; p = 0.0426, non-decellularized vs DNase 10 cycles; p = 0.0176, non-decellularized vs DNase 15 cycles; p = 0.0048) (Fig. 5C). Taking these into consideration, further analyses were performed for the particles that were frozen & thawed, and frozen & thawed and 5 cycles or 15 cycles of DNase.

Coculture of human auricular chondrocytes and the decellularized cartilaginous particle

Decellularized particles were also co-cultured with human auricular chondrocytes in the proliferation medium for 1 week and in the differentiation medium for 3 and 7 weeks (Fig. 6A). Auricular chondrocytes attached on the surface of the particles formed cartilaginous matrix as is the case with non-decellularized particles (Fig. 6B). The number of empty lacunae in the particles of each condition seemed unchanged during the observation period. Statistical analysis suggested that the number of empty lacunae in the particles treated by DNase 15 cycles was significantly greater than those of non-decellularized particles at 1 and 4 weeks (Dunnett's one-sided test, 1 week; p = 0.0263, Tukey-Kramer's test, 4 weeks; p = 0.0259) and those of samples that underwent freeze & thaw treatment at 4 weeks (Tukey-Kramer's test; p = 0.0259). The number of empty lacunae in the particles of DNase 5 cycles was greater than those of non-treated particles (Dunnett's one-sided test, 1 week; p = 0.0258, Tukey-Kramer's test, 8 weeks, p = 0.0120) (Fig. 6C). Overall, the temporal change in the number of empty lacunae was not obvious.

Transplantation of multi-stage modified cartilaginous particles

To examine the effect of surface coverage with syngeneic auricular chondrocytes and decellularization of cartilaginous particles on the host immunoreaction and maintenance of the cartilaginous tissue, multi-stage modified human iPS-derived cartilaginous particles were transplanted subcutaneously into the GFP mice (Fig. 7A). As indicated in the histological sections, eosin-stained tissue surrounded the untreated cartilaginous particle (Fig. 7B, upper left). In cocultured samples, murine auricular chondrocytes formed regenerative cartilage either near or attached to the cartilaginous particle (Fig. 7C green arrowheads in HE panels, “m” in immunohistochemistry panels). CD3 (+) and CD4 (+) cells scattered around the cartilaginous particle both in non-cocultured samples and in cocultured samples (Fig. 7B and C). Foxp3 (+) cells were not found in any sample (Fig. 7B and C). Most of the GFP (+) host cells surrounded the cartilaginous particle and the murine regenerative cartilage, while some of them invaded either the cartilaginous particle or the murine regenerative cartilage (Supplementary Fig. S2.) Overall, the cartilaginous particle maintained its cartilage feature indicated by lacuna structures. Samples of non-decellularized particles with or without coculture were BSP (−), indicating there was no sign of ossification (Supplementary Fig. S3.)

When comparing the samples which underwent the same decellularized procedures, there were some differences in the presence of immune cells. Among samples that underwent freeze & thaw, cocultured samples showed significantly greater CD4 positive areas than those of the control group (Fisher's test, p = 0.0276) (Fig. 8B, upper panels). In samples treated with the freeze & thaw process and DNase for 5 cycles, coculture significantly increased the CD3 (+) and CD4 (+) areas (Fisher's test, CD3 (+) area, comparison group vs cocultured; p = 0.0054, not cocultured vs cocultured; p = 0.0425, CD4 (+) area, comparison group vs cocultured; p = 0.00000121, not cocultured vs cocultured; p = 0.00000232) (Fig. 8B, middle panels).

The most frequent counts of the histogram of the hematoxylin channel indicate dye affinity of the extracellular matrix (ECM). Discussion

In this study, the effectiveness of two strategies was examined to alleviate the immune reaction against xenogeneic cartilage. One strategy was to cover the surface of the cartilaginous particle with allogeneic chondrocytes to physically shield it from immune cells. The other strategy was the decellularization of the cartilaginous particle to decrease the immunogen.

Regarding the first strategy, the cartilaginous particle was cocultured with auricular chondrocytes in the proliferation medium or differentiation medium for 7 days (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 1). As a result, the surface coverage of the particle by the proliferation medium was better than that by the differentiation medium (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 1). Considering that fifty thousand cells are enough to cover a large part of a 3 mm diameter sphere (https://www.thermofisher.com/blog/learning-at-the-bench/useful-numbers-for-cell-culture/), the attached cells of the primarily seeded chondrocyte needed to proliferate to cover the whole surface of the particle, and the differentiation medium was not enough to provide a sufficient number of cells. However, the covering of the cartilaginous particle was still partially uneven. The application of chondrocyte cell-sheet [[29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36]] may solve this issue. Additional differentiation culture formed cartilage tissue from the attached chondrocytes, which had been expected to function as a barrier (Fig. 4). In addition, improvements to the evaluation methods for quality control of cell coverage are necessary. For instance, the extent and uniformity of the coverage could be better assessed using 3D images obtained via light-sheet microscopy. Furthermore, cell viability should be evaluated using methods such as TUNEL staining. Post-implantation fate tracking could also be performed by utilizing CAG-GFP mice.

For the second strategy, two procedures were adopted for decellularization. One method is a treatment with freeze & thaw cycles that is reported to be suitable for the decellularization of high-density tissue such as bone or cartilage. In this procedure, although ECM damage is suppressed by avoiding the detergent process, insufficient removal of antigens may cause inflammation [37]. Another decellularization procedure is a treatment with enzyme cycles, which promotes cell and ECM dissection and nuclear antigen removal. However, ECM damage is enhanced in this procedure [37]. In the present study, cell fragments were left within the lacunae of frozen & thawed samples. On the other hand, there were more empty lacunae in DNase cycles-processed cartilaginous particles than those of non-decellularized or frozen & thawed samples (Fig. 5B and C). Apparent ECM damage was not observed in the samples of both treatments (Fig. 5B). The cartilaginous particle is positive for both type I and type II collagen [3]. These two types of collagens are reported to be cell adhesion molecules for chondrocytes [38]. Damage of COL1 and COL2 may affect their function as cell adhesion molecules. However, there was no apparent difference in cell attachment in this study (Fig. 6B).

The aim of decellularization is to suppress immune reaction by reducing the immunogenic cells. However, insufficient antigen removal and damage associated molecular pattern (DAMP) release caused by deteriorated ECM may enhance immune reaction [37]. In this study, human iPS-derived cartilaginous particles with or without cell coverage and decellularization treatment were transplanted into immunocompetent mice, and immune reactions were analyzed by immunohistochemistry of CD3, CD4, and Foxp3. As a result, few CD3 and CD4 positive cells were detected near untreated cartilaginous particles (Fig. 7, Fig. 8B). Among these cells, CD3 (+) CD4 (+) helper T cells and CD4 (+) M1 macrophages are pro-inflammatory and inhibit tissue repair [39]. On the other hand, CD3 (+) CD4 (+) Foxp3 (+) Tregs, CD3 (+) γδ T cells, and CD4 (+) M2 macrophages are anti-inflammatory, and regulate and promote tissue repair [39,40]. These suggest that CD4 immunoreactivity can include non-T-cell populations, and therefore the CD4-positive area should be interpreted cautiously as a composite immune signal. Taking into account that the cartilaginous particles were not covered with syngeneic chondrocytes or not decellularized, xenogeneic cells were exposed. This suggests that there was an immune reaction against xenogeneic cells, indicating that the detected few CD3 and CD4 positive cells are possibly pro-inflammatory helper T cells and M1 macrophages and may indicate slight inflammation. In cartilaginous particles without coverage by chondrocytes, decellularization did not apparently affect the infiltration of CD3-or CD4-positive cells (Fig. 8A and B). These suggest that neither suppression of immune reactions by decreasing xenogeneic cells nor promotion of immune reactions by remaining cell component was observed. On the other hand, broader CD3 positive and CD4 positive areas were observed in decellularized cartilaginous particles with chondrocyte coverage (Fig. 8A and B). These indicate that the combination of decellularization and chondrocyte coverage of cartilaginous particles caused an apparent immune reaction instead of decellularization alone.

Immune reactions against decellularized tissue scaffold are mediated mainly via DAMP released from fragmented DNA and ECM. The DAMP promotes inflammation by releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines and recruiting immune cells. The provoked inflammation destroys the graft. Inhibition of excessive release of DAMP by preserving ECM during decellularization and sufficient removal of antigens is important to avoid graft failure [37]. In this study, apparent damage of the ECM of the decellularized cartilaginous particle was not observed within the adapted DNase cycles. These suggest that the release of DAMP caused by fragmented ECM was suppressed and led to the downregulation of the inflammation. On the other hand, the CD3 and CD4 positive area of the decellularized cartilaginous particle seemed to increase compared to the non-decellularized one, although there was no statistical significance. These suggest that antigens left within the ECM or slightly fragmented ECM caused by the DNase cycle may have provoked slight inflammation. Taking these into account, some of the cells in the non-cocultured group may be pro-inflammatory cells such as T cells, helper T cells or M1 macrophages.

These indicate that stronger immune privilege or anti-inflammation was required for in vivo murine cartilage regeneration to overcome the slight inflammation caused by decellularization. To fully understand the extent of the immune reaction and how further immune privilege or anti-inflammatory measures controlled the inflammation, more detailed analysis including earlier stages is required. Specifically, we should evaluate around 1 to 4 weeks post transplantation and capture immune kinetics including transient Treg responses. In addition, we should expand immune phenotyping beyond CD3, CD4, and Foxp3, to CD8, B cells, macrophage and its M1/M2 polarization, neutrophils, and NK cell by adding cytokine profiling or flow cytometry.

Recent report showed immune reactions against allogeneic cultured chondrocytes seeded on decellularized and laser-damaged xenogeneic cartilage. In this report, seeded chondrocytes modified the immune reaction rather than alleviating it by suppressing T cell-dominant inflammation against damaged cartilage and summoning inflammatory cells that would support the chondrocytes’ process to recover the damaged cartilage ECM [41]. Other studies showed that chondrocytes cocultured in contact inhibited the proliferation and activation of T cells in vitro [42,43]. These studies suggest that the chondrocytes cocultured with decellularized cartilaginous particles may have modified the immune reaction and summoned cells to support their process to re-differentiate and recover the damaged ECM in the cartilaginous particles. Another report showed that articular chondrocytes from osteoarthritis patients proliferated cocultured autologous T cells in vitro [44]. Another report showed that chondrocytes transplanted to allogeneic articular defects were rejected when exposed to bone marrow cells. In the same report, chondrocytes isolated from bone marrow either by re-differentiation before transplantation or by transplanting to intrachondral defect model were not rejected [45]. Taking these together, to avoid unnecessary inflammation, chondrocytes should be isolated from non-inflammatory cartilage. For better precaution, chondrocytes should be re-differentiated before transplantation when transplanting near a large number of immune cells.

However, CD3 and CD4 are expressed not only by pro-inflammatory cells but also by anti-inflammatory ones. During in vivo redifferentiation of chondrocytes, M2 macrophages localize near the regenerative cartilage and maintain immune privilege to support the process [[19], [20], [21],46,47]. Another report showed that M2 macrophages promote anti-inflammatory responses and regulate tissue repair [39]. In this study, CD4 (+) cells localized near murine regenerative cartilage, suggesting that there was immune regulation to support this process. This indicates that some of the CD4 (+) cells localized near murine regenerative cartilage may be anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages.

γδ T cells play an important role in tissue recovery by regulating immune cell recruitment and tissue proliferation [39]. In this study, CD3 (+) cells localized near murine regenerative cartilage, suggesting that there was immune regulation to support this process. This indicates that some of the CD3 (+) cells localized near murine regenerative cartilage may be anti-inflammatory γδ T cells.

Unexpectedly, Tregs were not detected in this study. At the beginning of the wound healing process, Tregs regulate the polarization of the macrophages [39]. After cartilage injury, Tregs and M2 macrophages together promote chondrogenesis of MSCs, anti-inflammation, and repair of injured cartilage [40]. In murine skeletal muscle injury, Tregs were found around 2 to 4 weeks post-trauma [39]. Taking these into consideration, Tregs may have been detected around 2 to 4 weeks post-transplantation in order to control the slight inflammation caused by decellularization and support the in vivo redifferentiation of chondrocytes and may have disappeared at 8 weeks post transplantation. The absence of Treg might reflect the limitation of our study, where only mere snapshots of 8 weeks were examined, potentially missing events at earlier time points.

In the in vivo study, degeneration of cartilage ECM, suggested by a decrease in hematoxylin intensity, was observed in cartilaginous particles treated by freeze & thaw, and freeze & thaw and DNase cycles. Degeneration of cartilage ECM is caused by disruption during decellularization [37], mineralization, and endochondral ossification [48], and the loss of live chondrocytes maintaining cartilage features [9,[13], [14], [15], [16]]. As discussed earlier, antigens left within the ECM or slightly fragmented ECM caused by the DNase cycle may have provoked slight inflammation in this study. Such inflammation provokes an immune reaction that can destroy the graft [37]. Taking these into consideration, decellularization, loss of live chondrocytes, and inflammation provoked by decellularization may be the cause of cartilage ECM degeneration. To strengthen the quality control of decellularization, we should examine more parameters such as residual DNA content, DNA fragmentation, ECM components, and residual deoxycholic acid and active DNase. We performed pilot studies and evaluated DNA fragmentation by TUNEL assay, and observed broader TUNEL (+) area in freeze & thaw sample compared to non-decellularized sample and freeze & thaw and DNase 1 cycle sample. We also evaluated ECM degradation by toluidine blue staining in the pilot study and observed loss of metachromasia by DNase cycles. (data not shown). These methods could be adopted to more detailed evaluation of the effectiveness of decellularization and quality of decellularized cartilages.

In many previous studies, regenerative cartilages were transplanted to immunodeficient animals without a scaffold [[49], [50], [51], [52], [53], [54], [55]]. In our previous studies, cartilaginous particles were transplanted to either immunodeficient animals with a PLA scaffold [5] or immunocompetent animals without a PLA scaffold [6]. On the other hand, in this study, cartilaginous particles are inserted in a meshed tubular PLA scaffold and transplanted to immunocompetent mice. Thus, there may be a foreign body reaction against the PLA scaffold, which might have masked the difference in immunoreaction to the cartilaginous particles. However, in our previous study [6], where iPSC-derived cartilaginous particles were transplanted into immunocompetent mice, the CD3-and CD4-positive areas were less than 0.1 mm2 per field of view, which was comparable to the values observed in the present study using iPSC-derived cartilaginous particles with PLA scaffolds. To further evaluate the impact of the scaffold on the immune response, direct comparisons between groups with and without scaffolds should be conducted in future studies. Further and detailed analysis is required to analyze the immune reaction against the transplant without a scaffold.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we evaluated the modifications of human iPSC-derived cartilaginous particles in a xenogeneic environment, similar to the settings used in preclinical safety evaluations. Our goal was to prove the feasibility of preventing allogeneic rejection by achieving effective control over xenogeneic immunoreaction. On the contrary to our expectation, the combination of decellularization and covering with syngeneic chondrocytes increased T cell accumulations, suggesting the complexity of immunoreactions against xenogeneic cartilages.
