Robust modelling of arterial blood pressure reconstruction from photoplethysmography
Authors: Jiating Pan, Lishi Liang, Yongbo Liang, Qunfeng Tang, Zhencheng Chen, Jianming Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Blood pressure is a crucial indicator of cardiovascular disease, and arterial blood pressure (ABP) waveforms contain information that reflects the cardiovascular status. We propose a novel deep-learning method that converts photoplethysmogram (PPG) signals into ABP waveforms. We used \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {U}^{2}$$\end{document}-Net as a feature extractor and designed a Bi-block to capture individualised time information in encoder feature extraction. We further enhanced the prediction accuracy of the ABP waveforms by applying a combined loss function to each layer of deep supervision. We also propose a total error index (TEI) to measure overall performance. Furthermore, we extended our method from the UCI dataset to the VitalDB dataset, achieving mean absolute error ± standard deviation (MAE ± STD) values of 2.48 ± 1.95, 1.42 ± 1.42, and 1.48 ± 1.36 mmHg for systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and mean arterial pressure (MAP) in UCI dataset, and 2.16 ± 1.53, 1.12 ± 0.59, and 1.35 ± 0.84 mmHg in VitalDB dataset, respectively. The mean ± STD values of the TEI index are 0.29 ± 0.10 in UCI dataset and 0.29 ± 0.15 in VitalDB dataset. These results demonstrate the superiority of the proposed method over existing methods and its robustness to different sampling frequencies and devices.

Introduction

Continuous blood pressure monitoring is essential for maintaining overall health and preventing cardiovascular diseases, such as coronary heart disease, stroke, and kidney failure, as hypertension is a significant risk factor for these conditions1. Research indicates that individuals with prehypertension have a 3.2/1.74 times higher risk of developing cardiovascular diseases than those with normal blood pressure2. In 2015, approximately 10.7 million deaths (19.2% of all deaths) were attributed to all causes with SBP between 110 and 115 mmHg, and 7.8 million deaths (14.0% of all deaths) were associated with SBP of 140 mmHg3. Consequently, blood pressure is a critical indicator of overall health and requires timely intervention when abnormal blood pressure is detected. Furthermore, postoperative blood pressure monitoring is crucial for patient’ recovery following interventions such as percutaneous coronary intervention, as intraoperative blood pressure waveforms can provide a model for postoperative monitoring, ensuring a safe and effective return to work and predicting patients’ quality of life4.

Blood pressure represents the force exerted by the blood on the arterial wall per unit area and is the sum of the haemodynamic, kinetic, and hydrostatic energies5. Blood pressure not only reflects the presence of hypertension but also indicates arterial and cardiac functions. Blood pressure measurement methods include numerical and waveform measurements. The clinical measurement of blood pressure values typically involves using a cuff to measure SBP and DBP. Invasive catheterisation allows continuous blood pressure waveform measurement. Cuff-based blood pressure monitors provide only two parameters of the arterial pressure waveform, SBP, and DBP, which are suitable for the routine diagnosis of hypertension. However, waveform morphology and beat-to-beat variability can reflect a wide range of information, including aortic valve disease and myocardial functional disorders6. For acute patients, interpreting their condition solely based on changes in SBP and DBP can lead to misdiagnosis because of the complexity of various factors that cause changes in ABP and their interrelationships. Blood pressure waveform analysis can provide more physiological information, which is beneficial for diagnosis. In addition to providing beat-by-beat estimates of SBP, DBP, and MAP, blood pressure waveforms can also provide left ventricular function assessment by evaluating the heart rate, systolic time intervals, and other information7. Analysis of ABP waveforms is a key method for obtaining arterial function-related parameters such as peripheral resistance, arterial compliance, and wave reflection8. Therefore, analysing blood pressure waveforms can provide a more in-depth understanding of the haemodynamic state6,9.

Although ABP waveforms can offer valuable physiological insights, the invasiveness of the gold-standard method for measuring ABP limits its utility in daily health monitoring and postoperative recovery10. Consequently, the non-invasive measurement of the ABP waveform is considerably important. Therefore, the development and application of PPG are promising alternatives.

PPG is a non-invasive technique that indirectly measures changes in blood volume using a light source and a photodetector placed on the skin. For decades, this cost-effective method has been acknowledged for its potential in assessing cardiovascular diseases and is widely employed in areas such as blood pressure measurement and vascular assessment11. The rationale for using PPG to predict ABP is as follows. First, as shown in Fig.1, PPG and ABP have a remarkable similarity in morphology, with similar upstrokes, downstrokes, and dicrotic notches. Moreover, ABP and PPG in individuals with normal blood pressure, prehypertension, and hypertension displayed a strong resemblance in both the time and frequency domains, suggesting a consistent and significant causal relationship. These similarities suggest a consistent and significant causal relationship, thereby enabling the prediction of ABP from PPG signals12.Fig. 1Synchronised ABP and PPG signals.

In recent years, substantial advancements have been made in the field of non-invasive blood pressure monitoring through the reconstruction of ABP waveforms from PPG signals. A variety of methods have been proposed to address this challenge. Ibtehaz et al. proposed PPG2ABP, which is an approach for predicting the ABP from PPG signals. This method uses a one-dimensional U-Net approximation network after filtering, and a one-dimensional MultiResUNet refinement network to refine the results13. Cheng et al. introduced ABP-Net, a Wave-U-Net-based architecture that reconstructs ABP waveforms using the PPG, its first-order derivative (VPG), and its second-order derivative (APG) as inputs14. Tang et al. presented a deep learning model with a W-Net architecture comprising of two cascaded U-Net structures for reconstructing ABP from PPG signals15. Additionally, Qin et al. developed a deep autoencoder (DAE) model for reconstructing ABP pressure waveforms from PPG signals16. Although their results were superior to other related experiments, the need for both PPG and ECG signals complicates its application. Athaya et al. used a U-NET to reconstruct ABP signals from PPG signals and predict SBP and DBP, but their data was only a small subset of the MIMIC dataset17.However, they conducted experiments on a single dataset and evaluated the prediction results using the predicted values of SBP, DBP, and MAP. A comprehensive metric is required to measure the numerical prediction accuracy and similarity of the reconstructed blood pressure waveforms.

This study established an end-to-end model for predicting ABP using a one-dimensional signal transformation of PPG signals. This model is based on an improved version of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {U}^{2}$$\end{document}-Net, a deep convolutional neural network with a U-Net structure primarily used for salient object detection (SOD)18. In \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {U}^{2}$$\end{document}-Net, the encoder and decoder have multi-scale feature maps that capture signal features at different scales. To obtain long-term dependencies in the sequence, we integrated the concept of bidirectional Long Short-Term into our model. This integration significantly boosted the capability of the model to predict the ABP from PPG signals. We designed a comprehensive loss function based on existing experiments under deep supervision. We propose a novel integrated evaluation metric that measures the accuracy of the predicted values and assesses the waveform similarity. We also collected data from two different datasets to verify the universality of the model and the experiment. The results showed that the reconstructed ABP waveforms in both datasets were satisfactory in terms of waveform similarity and achieved satisfactory results for SBP, DBP, and MAP indicators.

Data sources

The data used in this experiment were obtained from the UCI dataset19and VitalDB20 dataset.

The MIMIC II dataset contained signals sampled at 125 Hz. In this dataset, the ABP signals were derived from invasive radial artery measurements, whereas the PPG signals were obtained from fingertip measurements. Due to the large size of the MIMIC II dataset and the variability in the quality of signals, some of which contain distortions and artifacts, we opted for the well-organized UCI dataset21, which is also derived from the MIMIC II dataset and has been preprocessed by Mohamad Kachuee and others. UCI dataset comprises three types of signals: PPG, ABP, and electrocardiogram (ECG), totalling 12000 records. For this experiment, only the PPG and ABP signals were required. Considering related studies, we extracted records with durations exceeding 8 mins and excluded records with ABP values greater than 200 mmHg14,15. Ultimately, we selected the first 150 records from the filtered data as the research subjects. Fig.2 shows the distributions of SBP and DBP in the training and prediction sets after segmenting 150 records. After segmentation, 38829 signal segments were obtained.

The VitalDB dataset contained data sampled at 500 Hz. The PPG and ABP signals in this dataset were obtained from a GE Solar8000 monitor, with PPG signals measured from the fingertip and ABP signals derived from invasive radial artery measurements. Initially, we acquired cases containing PPG and ABP signals from a Web Monitoring dataset and downloaded the data for the first twenty cases for experimentation. We also extracted two synchronised signals with more than 8 min duration after signal stabilisation and ABP values below 200 mmHg. Studies have revealed that PPG derivatives, specifically VPG and APG, provide additional heart cycle-related information closely associated with blood pressure22. Moreover, experiments verified that VPG and APG can improve pressure prediction23. Consequently, this study incorporated PPG, VPG, and APG as model inputs to enhance the ABP waveform prediction. In this study, we utilized two different datasets: UCI dataset and VitalDB, with sampling rates of 125 Hz and 500 Hz, respectively. The difference in sampling rates necessitated the use of different mathematical methods for calculating the APG and VPG. We calculated APG and VPG for both datasets using the 2-point method.

Comparison with existing studies

To validate the effectiveness of our model, we extended our dataset to include a VitalDB dataset and compared it with existing related studies.

We not only compared individual experiments related to waveform reconstruction but also compared individual experiments related to the numerical prediction of SBP, DBP, and MAP from PPG. The comparison results are shown in Table 3, including the dataset and the MAE ± STD of SBP, DBP, and MAP. From the table, we can see that in the waveform reconstruction work, Ibtehaz et al., who only used PPG as input, achieved SBP, DBP, and MAP values of 5.73 ± 9.16, 3.45 ± 6.15, and 2.31 ± 4.44 mmHg respectively. Cheng et al., using PPG, VPG, and APG as inputs and employing Wave-U-Net, achieved SBP, DBP, and MAP values of 3.27 ± 3.92, 1.90 ± 2.44, and 1.49 ± 2.10 mmHg respectively. The results of Tang et al., who used W-Net, were the closest to ours. Their results only measured SBP and DBP, which reached 2.60 ± 1.89 and 1.45 ± 1.33 mmHg. In the numerical prediction experiments we compared, Lin et al. used features from PPG, VPG, and APG as input, with the prediction results only measuring SBP and DBP, which reached 4.59 ± 1.78 and 2.47 ± 1.09 mmHg respectively. Wang et al. only used PPG as input, with their results measuring SBP and DBP at 3.95 ± 4.38 and 2.14 ± 2.40 mmHg. Athaya et al. used a U-Net based model with PPG as input, achieving results of 3.68 ± 4.42 mmHg for SBP, 1.97 ± 2.92 mmHg for DBP, and 2.17 ± 3.06 mmHg for MAP. Discussion

In recent years, studies on blood pressure measurements based on PPG signals have mainly included numerical measurement methods for SBP, DBP, and MAP, and methods for reconstructing the ABP waveform through PPG. Current numerical measurement methods for SBP, DBP, and MAP primarily involve blood pressure estimation methods based on pulse wave velocity (PWV), pulse arriving time (PAT), and pulse transit time (PTT)28–30. Some methods predict blood pressure by acquiring features from the PPG, its derivatives, and the ECG31,32, whereas others apply deep learning techniques to predict blood pressure values directly from raw PPG and its derivatives33,34. Existing methods for obtaining the ABP waveform from PPG are primarily based on deep learning. This approach extracts important parameters, such as SBP, DBP, and MAP, from the ABP waveform derived from PPG and provides more physiological information. However, a comprehensive consideration of the numerical values and waveform morphology is lacking in the current waveform reconstruction methods, and related deep learning methods have not yet been extended to other dataset to test the robustness of their models under different sampling frequencies and devices. The main contribution of this study is the design of a feature extractor based on the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {U}^{2}$$\end{document}-Net. A Bi-block was designed to capture the temporal information that emerged after extracting features at different levels from the bidirectional information flow in individual experiments. A Bi-block is used to process the temporal information of the fused features in the deep supervision outputs to avoid information loss and blurring during the fusion process. In addition, this study optimised the loss function for each layer in deep supervision based on existing research and employed a combined loss function. This design enhances feature extraction capabilities, improves model generalisation, and increases the performance and accuracy of the model on unprocessed data. Our results for the two models confirm this, as the models demonstrate good robustness on the two datasets and achieve high accuracy and improved metrics for important parameters such as SBP, DBP, MAP, and the composite index TEI. One limitation of this study is that our model was tailored for individual modelling. The primary reason for this is that PPG and ABP signals vary with the test subject’s age and other variable conditions, and individual differences are expressed differently in the PPG and ABP signals, often leading to larger prediction errors. Despite established healthcare standards prohibiting calibration of testing devices during evaluation16,35, in practical applications, individual blood pressure waveforms are continuously measured and recorded, offering the potential to leverage personal historical data for calibrating generalized models. Our future studies will focus on applying this model to individual devices and extending it to group studies.

Conclusion

In this study, we introduced an innovative end-to-end network designed to predict ABP waveforms from PPG signals. Building on existing research, our approach incorporates a combined loss function at each level of our deeply supervised architecture. A comprehensive TEI was proposed to assess the accuracy of the vital numerical predictions and waveform shape fidelity. To verify the robustness of our model, we expanded our experiment to include a VitalDB dataset from UCI dataset. An important aspect to note is the inherent complexity of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hbox {U}^{2}$$\end{document}-Net used for feature extraction in our model, which comprises 14,698,765 parameters. In the complete architecture of our model, this number increases to 17,629,901, reflecting its enhanced complexity and capabilities. This augmentation inevitably extends the training time of the model. Although this increase in parameters and the associated training duration presents a challenge, there is a trade-off for the enhanced ability of our model to capture and process complex temporal relationships in the data, leading to improved accuracy in ABP waveform prediction. This enhancement was evident in the high accuracy of the key numerical predictions across both the UCI dataset and VitalDB dataset, along with a commendable overall performance in waveform prediction. We plan to deploy our model on medical devices to provide more accurate numerical and waveform information. Future studies will involve optimising the model to balance the increased complexity with efficient training, thereby ensuring practical applicability in real-world settings.
