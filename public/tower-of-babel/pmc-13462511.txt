From traditional to novel: a national cohort study on the predictive value of C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose-obesity composite parameters for cardiovascular disease
Authors: Mengjie Zhao, Xujie Wang, Xuexue Zhang, Yan Yan, Zirong Li, Fang Lu, Qiuyan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of mortality worldwide. The C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose (CTI) index captures metabolic inflammation and insulin resistance, while obesity indices reflect adiposity-related cardiometabolic risk. However, the predictive value of combining CTI with obesity indices for CVD remains unclear.Objectives:To examine the prognostic utility of CTI combined with established and novel obesity indicators for CVD, and to benchmark their predictive efficacy.Design:Nationally representative prospective cohort study.Methods:Using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative prospective cohort, 7847 adults aged 45 years and above without baseline CVD were enrolled. CTI was calculated from C-reactive protein, fasting blood glucose, and triglycerides (TG); each obesity index was combined with CTI to form composite parameters (CTI-body mass index (CTI-BMI), CTI-waist circumference (CTI-WC), CTI-waist-to-height ratio (CTI-WHtR), CTI-body roundness index (CTI-BRI), CTI-weight-adjusted waist index (CTI-WWI), CTI-Chinese visceral adiposity index (CTI-CVAI), CTI-a body shape index (CTI-ABSI)). We employed Cox proportional hazard models, restricted cubic spline analyses, Kaplan–Meier survival plots, ROC curve evaluations, and weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression to examine the links and prognostic utility of each metric with incident CVD across baseline and cumulative exposure tiers.Results:Over a 9-year follow-up period, 1938 new-onset CVD cases were documented. Following multivariable adjustment, each CTI-obesity composite metric showed a significant positive link to CVD risk (p < 0.05). Moreover, all CTI‑obesity composite parameters showed stronger associations with CVD than CTI alone. CTI‑CVAI demonstrated the relatively best predictive accuracy among the evaluated indices, though absolute discriminatory ability remained modest (baseline area under the ROC curve (AUC): 0.596; cumulative AUC: 0.600). Trajectory clustering found that, versus the well-managed group, the poorly managed subgroup had 21%–71% higher CVD risk (max 71% for CTI-WC). WQS regression indicated that CRP and obesity indices (especially CVAI) were the major contributors in the mixed exposure; cumulative exposure WQS analysis showed that cumulative TG and cumulative obesity parameters contributed the most.Conclusion:CTI combined with obesity indices, particularly CTI-CVAI, was associated with modestly improved predictive ability for CVD risk compared with CTI alone. This parameter may offer incremental information for CVD risk stratification by integrating metabolic inflammation and visceral fat markers.Keywords: cardiovascular disease, CTI, inflammation, insulin resistance, obesity

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of mortality and disability worldwide, accounting for approximately 598 million prevalent cases and 20.5 million deaths in 2025, with projections suggesting a 90% increase in prevalence by 2050.1,2 In China, CVD contributed to roughly 44% of total deaths in 2021, affecting an estimated 330 million individuals.3 –5 Timely identification of modifiable risk factors remains a critical priority for reducing this burden.

Insulin resistance (IR) independently accelerates atherosclerosis through inflammatory, metabolic, and oxidative pathways. 6 The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, computed from fasting triglycerides (TG) and glucose, serves as a practical surrogate marker of IR, 7 and elevated TyG levels are associated with increased CVD risk.8,9 High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) reflects chronic low-grade vascular inflammation and predicts incident and recurrent CVD events. 10 The C-reactive protein-triglyceride-glucose (CTI) index, which combines CRP, fasting glucose, and TG, enables simultaneous assessment of inflammation and IR, and has shown predictive value for CVD.11 –13

Obesity contributes to vascular dysfunction through chronic inflammation and oxidative stress,14,15 and is closely linked to IR through a bidirectional pathophysiological relationship. 16 Novel anthropometric indices—including weight-adjusted waist index (WWI), Chinese visceral adiposity index (CVAI), and a body shape index (ABSI)—have been developed to better characterize adipose tissue distribution and cardiometabolic risk. 17 However, the prognostic value of combining these indices with CTI for CVD prediction has not been systematically evaluated.

In this nationally representative prospective cohort study, we examined the associations of CTI combined with seven obesity indices with incident CVD. We hypothesized that CTI-obesity composite indices, particularly those reflecting visceral adiposity, would demonstrate stronger predictive performance than CTI alone.

Methods

Study design and study population

This study used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative prospective cohort of adults aged ⩾45 years. 18 The CHARLS baseline survey was conducted in 2011 (Wave 1), recruiting 17,708 participants from 10,257 households across 150 counties/districts and 450 villages nationwide, with follow-up surveys conducted biennially in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020, respectively.

Study population

Inclusion criteria were: age ⩾45 years. Exclusion criteria were: (1) missing data for TG, fasting blood glucose (FBG), HDL-C, hs-CRP, height, weight, or waist circumference (WC); (2) pre-existing CVD at baseline; or (3) incomplete CVD follow-up data. This yielded a baseline analytic sample of 7847 participants. For cumulative exposure and trajectory analyses, we further excluded participants with CVD diagnosed at Wave 3 or missing physiological indicators at follow-up assessments, resulting in 4536 individuals. Data collection

Multiple types of covariates were extracted from the CHARLS annual questionnaire for this study, including sociodemographic attributes (e.g., age, sex, residence, educational attainment, marital condition, disability status, medical insurance coverage, tobacco use, and alcohol drinking habits), anthropometric indicators (height, body mass, WC), medical history (diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension, hyperlipidemia), drug utilization (glucose-lowering, lipid-lowering, and antihypertensive agents), sleep duration, and blood biochemical parameters. Directed acyclic graphs were utilized to depict the fundamental interrelationships among CTI, obesity-linked indicators, CVD, and a range of covariates (Figure S1).

Overnight-fasting venous blood specimens were obtained by formally trained medical personnel from the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention in accordance with standardized procedures and were uniformly transported to a central laboratory for testing. 20 TG, HDL-C, as well as FBG were assayed via enzymatic colorimetric assays; hs-CRP concentration was determined by immunoturbidimetry. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) was assayed via the urease UV rate assay; serum creatinine (SCR) was quantified with the rate-blanked and compensated Jaffe creatinine procedure; uric acid (UA) was determined through the UA Plus protocol.

Exposure assessment

Exposure variables were operationalized at three levels: baseline (2011), cumulative (time-weighted average 2012–2015), and trajectory (k-means clustering of 2012–2015 values). CTI and CTI-obesity composite indices were computed as described in Supplemental Methods and Figure S2. Briefly, CTI was calculated from CRP, TG, and FBG, 21 combined with seven obesity indices (body mass index (BMI), WC, waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), body roundness index (BRI), WWI, CVAI, ABSI) via simple multiplication. Cumulative exposure was computed using the time-weighted average method. Trajectory analysis identified two or three latent trajectory groups for each index (Figures 2 and S3 and S4).

Outcome measures

The main study endpoint was incident CVD during the follow-up phase (survey rounds 2 through 5). CVD encompassed both cardiac disease and stroke. A diagnosis of heart disease was verified based on the respondent’s response to the survey item, “Have you ever been diagnosed with heart disease, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems?”; stroke was ascertained based on the response to the query, “Have you ever been diagnosed with a stroke?” 22 Even though self-reported ascertainment of cardiovascular events carries inherent limitations, this approach has long been extensively employed in prior research to evaluate population-level risk associations. 23

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared between participants with and without incident CVD using standardized mean differences (>0.1 as clinically relevant) and appropriate parametric or nonparametric tests. Missing data were handled using random forest multiple imputation (Tables S2 and S3). Kaplan–Meier curves with log-rank tests were used to compare CVD cumulative incidence across tertiles of CTI-obesity indices.

Cox proportional hazards models were constructed in three sequential stages: Model 1 adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 additionally adjusted for education, marital status, residence, health insurance, disability, smoking, and alcohol use; Model 3 further adjusted for systolic and diastolic blood pressure, sleep duration, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, BUN, SCR, and UA. Collinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF) and correlation matrices (Figures S5 and S6); all VIF values were <5, indicating no significant multicollinearity. Restricted cubic splines were fitted in the fully adjusted model to examine dose-response relationships.

Predictive performance was assessed using area under the ROC curve (AUC), continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). We computed continuous NRI (category-free) and IDI to assess incremental predictive value. 24 The continuous NRI evaluates the net proportion of individuals whose predicted risk moves in the correct direction after adding the new marker, without relying on predefined risk thresholds. This approach was chosen because no established clinical risk thresholds exist for 9-year CVD probability in this population. The IDI was calculated as the difference in discrimination slopes between models with and without CTI-obesity metrics. Weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression with 1000 bootstrap iterations was used to quantify the relative contributions of individual metabolic components to CVD risk.

Subgroup analyses were stratified by age, sex, smoking, alcohol use, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes. Sensitivity analyses included: (1) analysis using raw unimputed data; (2) exclusion of participants with baseline cancer; and (3) additional adjustment for antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and glucose-lowering medications.

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1), with two-sided p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Ultimately, 7847 participants were enrolled in the current study, consisting of 3699 men (47.14%) and 4148 women (52.86%), with an average age of 59.03 years. The baseline characteristics of the study cohort are comprehensively outlined in Table 1. Compared with participants without CVD, those affected by CVD displayed the following characteristics: advanced age, a greater proportion of female individuals, subpar educational attainment, a heightened number of people living with disabilities, elevated SBP and DBP, reduced sleep duration, as well as a higher incidence of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and DM. In addition, body weight, WC, BMI, FBG, TG, and hs-CRP levels showed significantly higher values in the CVD group versus the non-CVD group (p < 0.05).

Association of CTI‑obesity parameters (baseline and cumulative exposure) with CVD risk

Over the 9-year follow-up period, 1938 new-onset CVD events were documented. The unadjusted model (Table S4) showed that Z‑score‑standardized CTI‑obesity parameters were significantly positively associated with CVD, with CTI‑CVAI, CTI‑WHtR, and CTI‑BRI exhibiting the strongest associations. After adjustment with multivariable Cox regression, these associations were attenuated but remained significant. For cumulative exposure indices, after full adjustment for covariates, all standardized cumulative CTI-obesity parameters showed a significant association with CVD (Table 2). Relative to the first tertile (T1) of cumulative indices, the risk for CVD in the T3 group was significantly elevated: 30% for Cum-CTI, 45% for Cum-CTI-BMI, 43% for Cum-CTI-WC, 36% for Cum-CTI-WHtR, 42% for Cum-CTI-BRI, 24% for Cum-CTI-WWI, 51% for Cum-CTI-CVAI, and 24% for Cum-CTI-ABSI. Notably, whether based on baseline or cumulative exposure, CTI-CVAI showed the most robust association with CVD across all assessed metrics. RCS regression (Model 3) further revealed that CVD risk increased linearly with rising levels of CTI‑obesity parameters and their cumulative exposure (p < 0.05; Figures 3 and S7).

Association of CTI‑obesity parameter trajectory changes with CVD risk

In the trajectory analysis, for indices categorized into three trajectory patterns, employing the stable low-risk group (Class 1) as the reference category, the rise in CVD risk in the high-risk cohort (Class 3) spanned from 30% to 71%: 37% for CTI, 60% for CTI-BMI, 71% for CTI-WC, 53% for CTI-WHtR, 59% for CTI-BRI, and 30% for CTI-CVAI (Table 3). For indices categorized into two trajectory patterns, relative to the stable low-risk group (Class 1), the rise in risk in the high-risk cohort (Class 2) spanned from 21% to 26%, with 21% for CTI-WWI and 26% for CTI-ABSI (Table 3).

Predictive analysis

The ROC curve analysis showed that, compared with the single CTI index, all CTI‑obesity parameters had better predictive performance for CVD events (DeLong tests p < 0.001), with CTI‑CVAI and CTI‑BRI demonstrating the highest predictive accuracy (Figure 5 and Table S5). For cumulative exposure indices, Cum‑CTI‑CVAI, Cum‑CTI‑WC, and Cum‑CTI‑WHtR exhibited the highest predictive accuracy (Figure 5 and Table S5), a finding consistent with the results of the association analysis.

After integrating CTI-obesity metrics into the baseline framework (Model 3), the model’s predictive capacity for CVD showed a modest improvement, as evidenced by statistically significant positive NRI scores (Table S6). Similarly, incorporating cumulative CTI-obesity metrics (excluding Cum-CTI) into the baseline framework further enhanced the predictive performance, with all NRI values exceeding 0 (Table S6). These research outcomes indicate that the incorporation of CTI-obesity metrics yields modest but statistically significant incremental predictive value for the foundational risk assessment framework.

WQS analysis

The WQS regression approach was utilized to assess each individual exposure constituent’s respective impact across the CTI-obesity metrics on CVD. Our findings revealed that the weighting coefficients of CRP and obesity-associated metrics were remarkably greater compared with those of other metabolic indicators (Figure S8). Further WQS regression analysis indicated that all CTI‑obesity parameters were significantly positively associated with CVD (Figure 6 and Table S7). In terms of cumulative exposure metrics, the weighting coefficients of cumulative TG and obesity-associated parameters were remarkably elevated relative to those of other metabolic constituents (Figure 7). Findings from the WQS regression analysis indicated that, with the exception of Cum-CTI and Cum-CTI-ABSI, all remaining CTI-obesity metrics exhibited a notably strong positive correlation with CVD (Figure 6 and Table S7).

Subgroup analysis

To verify the stability of the core correlations and investigate possible moderating effects, we conducted predefined subgroup stratifications based on gender, age category, smoking behavior, drinking pattern, as well as hypertensive history, hyperlipidemia, and DM. Our findings demonstrated that the direct positive correlation linking heightened levels of baseline or cumulative CTI-obesity parameters to an elevated risk of new-onset CVD remained uniformly evident across all subgroup strata (Tables S8 and S9). The point estimations of hazard ratios (HRs) along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals displayed a high degree of consistency across the board.

Sensitivity analysis

Three sensitivity analyses assessed result robustness: (1) exclusion of participants with incomplete covariate data (complete-case analysis, Tables S10 and S11); (2) exclusion of participants with baseline cancer (Tables S12 and S13); and (3) additional adjustment for antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and glucose-lowering medications (Tables S14 and S15). In all analyses, associations between CTI-obesity indices and CVD risk remained directionally consistent and statistically significant.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this research is the first to systematically examine the correlation of multiple CTI-related obesity parameters with CVD risk profile. The results showed that elevated values of CTI-associated obesity parameters were correlated with an elevated risk of CVD, following a positive dose-response pattern, and this observed relationship remained robust throughout per-standard-deviation increments, tertile grouping, cumulative exposure, and trajectory analyses. Furthermore, CTI composite indices derived from both conventional and emerging obesity metrics were statistically significantly associated with new-onset CVD, with effect estimates generally larger than those of CTI alone; CTI-CVAI showed relatively consistent associations across the analyses. These findings suggest a potential adjunctive role for CTI-CVAI in cardiovascular risk stratification, pending further validation in clinical settings.

On a global scale, obesity rates have been rising steadily, evolving into a critical public health burden. Per data from the Global Burden of Disease Study, adiposity prevalence has increased twofold in over 70 nations. 25 With the increase in obesity, the risk of IR and CVD also increases. 26 Abdominal fat comprises subcutaneous and visceral adipose tissue, with the latter being more tightly linked to CVD development and exerting a more harmful effect on cardiometabolic outcomes. 27 To assess visceral obesity, several simple and reliable surrogate indicators have been developed and validated, such as the well-validated visceral adiposity index (VAI), 28 the widely adopted lipid accumulation product (LAP), 29 and CVAI. 30 Conversely, VAI and LAP were initially constructed and verified in Caucasian cohorts, and thus may present inherent constraints when forecasting cardiovascular adverse outcomes in Asian populations. Studies have demonstrated that CVAI (derived from a Chinese cohort) exhibits a more robust correlation with metabolic disorders, CVD, and diabetes-related sequelae among Asian populations relative to VAI and LAP.31,32 Among the evaluated composite indices, CTI-CVAI demonstrated relatively higher AUC values; however, the absolute discriminatory performance was modest. Combining CVAI with CTI enhances the ability to identify individuals with concurrent excess visceral fat and metabolic‑inflammatory disturbances, a high‑risk phenotype closely associated with accelerated atherosclerosis, endothelial dysfunction, and incident CVD. In contrast, traditional obesity‑based CTI combinations (e.g., CTI‑BMI and CTI‑WC) showed relatively weaker predictive ability, suggesting that general obesity or simple WC cannot fully capture the metabolic heterogeneity of adipose tissue. Furthermore, other novel indices such as BRI, WWI, and ABSI also showed statistically significant associations with CVD risk, but their consistency and stability were inferior to those of CTI-CVAI, suggesting that CVAI is likely to be more strongly associated with the cascade of cardiac and vascular injury triggered by metabolic inflammation.

Obesity frequently coexists with chronic low-grade inflammation. Inflammatory signaling can interfere with insulin signal transduction, thereby inducing metabolic IR and subsequent compensatory hyperinsulinemia.33,34 IR and inflammation are jointly associated with endothelial dysfunction, reduced nitric oxide bioavailability, and vascular remodeling,26,35 –37 creating a feedback loop in which obesity, persistent inflammation, and IR may mutually reinforce each other. Lin et al. 38 demonstrated a temporal association between visceral fat accumulation (as reflected by CVAI) and inflammatory burden (hs-CRP), with both variables independently associated with increased cardiometabolic disease risk. These mechanisms are consistent with prospective cohort studies showing that composite indices integrating metabolic and adiposity parameters—such as TyG-BMI, TyG-WHtR, and TyG-CVAI—are significantly associated with incident CVD in middle-aged and older Chinese adults.39,40

The TyG index is a practical surrogate marker of IR. 41 Meta-analytic evidence indicates that elevated TyG levels are associated with arterial stiffness and coronary artery calcification, 42 and TyG predicts CVD risk.8,43 Combining TyG with obesity indices further improves predictive performance.39,43,44 CRP concentrations correlate positively with IR severity, suggesting that chronic inflammation contributes to IR pathogenesis. 45 Cross-national cohort studies have shown that TyG-obesity composite indices predict cardiovascular events, with CRP partially mediating these associations. 46 The CTI index captures both metabolic and inflammatory states.47,48 CTI combined with obesity indices has been used to predict hypertension, stroke, CVD, and cardiometabolic multimorbidity.40,49–51 For example, CTI-WHtR predicted stroke more strongly than CTI alone, 50 and cumulative CTI-WHtR was associated with a 2.76-fold increased risk of cardiometabolic multimorbidity. 40 However, prior studies have limitations: some focused only on standalone CTI, 12 others combined CTI with limited obesity metrics, 51 and studies of obesity indices rarely incorporated inflammatory markers such as CTI.17,52

The AUC values for CTI-CVAI (baseline: 0.596; cumulative: 0.600) indicate only modest discriminatory ability and should be interpreted in context. CVD risk prediction is inherently multifactorial, and composite indices derived from routine clinical parameters in population-based studies typically achieve AUCs in the range of 0.55–0.65—exemplified by TyG-BRI (AUC = 0.58) and TyG-CVAI (AUC = 0.59) in recent Chinese cohorts. 17 Furthermore, although the magnitude of NRI and IDI improvements was modest, the consistent directionality and statistical significance across both baseline and cumulative exposure analyses support the potential utility of CTI-CVAI as a supplementary risk marker, rather than a standalone diagnostic tool.

We used WQS regression to quantify the relative contributions of inflammation, lipid, and adiposity components to CVD risk. In the baseline WQS model, CRP and obesity-related metrics received higher weights than FBG and TG, suggesting that inflammatory and adiposity markers may be more strongly associated with CVD risk than isolated metabolic parameters in the short term. In the cumulative exposure model, cumulative TG and obesity parameters contributed most, consistent with the concept that long-term metabolic disturbances have a more sustained impact on cardiovascular health. This study is the first to apply WQS regression to CTI-obesity composite indices, providing insight into the distinct roles of acute versus chronic exposure patterns.

In clinical practice, CTI-CVAI may have potential as a candidate screening metric in settings where routine biochemical measurements are available. Its component indicators (CRP, TG, FBG, WC, BMI, HDL-C) are standard clinical laboratory parameters; however, the availability of hs-CRP testing varies across healthcare settings, which limits its universal applicability in resource-constrained primary care environments.

Key strengths include the nationally representative prospective design, extended follow-up, comprehensive evaluation of seven CTI-obesity indices using multiple exposure metrics, and robust sensitivity analyses. This is, to our knowledge, the first study to assess CTI-obesity indices with baseline, cumulative, and trajectory approaches.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, CTI and obesity indices were not measured at every follow-up wave, which may have introduced exposure misclassification. Second, residual confounding from unmeasured lifestyle factors (e.g., detailed dietary intake and physical activity) cannot be excluded. Third, the observational design precludes causal inference. Fourth, generalizability to younger individuals or other ethnic groups requires further validation. Fifth, incident CVD was ascertained through self-reported physician diagnoses rather than centrally adjudicated events; the composite outcome combined heterogeneous cardiovascular conditions, and independent verification was not feasible within CHARLS. While such misclassification would likely bias effect estimates toward the null, residual error cannot be excluded. Sixth, the modest magnitude of NRI and IDI improvements suggests limited incremental reclassification value in everyday clinical practice. Seventh, hs-CRP is a non-specific acute-phase reactant susceptible to transient elevations; using single fasting blood samples, we were unable to distinguish acute from chronic inflammatory states. Finally, TG is a shared component between CTI and CVAI, which raises the theoretical concern of mathematical coupling that cannot be entirely excluded.

Conclusion

In the present, nationally representative prospective cohort investigation, we conducted a systematic comparison of predictive value for incident CVD of CTI combined with conventional obesity indicators (BMI, WC, WHtR) and novel adiposity metrics (BRI, WWI, CVAI, ABSI). The results indicated that all CTI-obesity index combinations performed modestly better than CTI alone, with CTI-CVAI demonstrating the strongest effect estimates. These findings suggest that CTI-CVAI could be explored as a candidate screening tool, though its incremental clinical value beyond existing risk scores requires further evaluation.
