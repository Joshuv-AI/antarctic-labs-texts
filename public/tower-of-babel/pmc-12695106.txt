Multiple Risk Factors Controlled and Mortality Risks in Chronic Kidney Disease
Authors: Boxuan Pu, Zun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic kidney disease (CKD) is associated with elevated mortality risk. The study aimed to investigate the extent to which multiple risk factors controlled could attenuate CKD-related excess mortality.MethodsThe study included 2,394 CKD patients and 15,962 non-CKD individuals from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2007–2018. Seven modifiable risk factors included blood pressure, body mass index, hemoglobin A1c, non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, smoking, physical activity, and diet. CKD patients were categorized by the number of controlled risk factors. Cox models were used to estimate associations between risk factors controlled and risks of all-cause and cause-specific mortality.ResultsAmong CKD patients, each additional risk factor control was significantly associated with a 19% lower risk of all-cause mortality (HR [hazard ratio]: 0.81; 95% CI [confidence interval]: 0.71–0.93), with the optimal control of 6–7 risk factors associated with a 59% lower risk of all-cause mortality (HR: 0.41; 95% CI: 0.19–0.90). Furthermore, compared with non-CKD individuals (death rate per 1,000 person-years: 2.69 [95% CI: 1.77–4.09]), CKD patients with 6–7 risk factors controlled had no significantly different risks of all-cause mortality (absolute rate difference per 1,000 person-years: 0.79 [95% CI: −2.65, 6.63]; HR: 1.29 [95% CI: 0.62–2.66]), cardiovascular disease mortality (absolute rate difference: 0.27 [95% CI: −0.80, 3.00]; HR: 1.51 [95% CI: 0.43–5.34]), and cancer mortality (absolute rate difference: 0.27 [95% CI: −0.80, 3.00]; HR: 1.06 [95% CI: 0.28–3.96]).ConclusionsMultiple risk factors controlled were associated with reduced CKD-related excess mortality. Optimal control of 6–7 factors was associated with the elimination of increased mortality risks relative to non-CKD individuals. These findings highlight the need for integrated management strategies targeting biological and lifestyle risk factors in CKD populations.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major public health challenge, affecting over 840 million people and causing 1.2 million deaths globally [1, 2]. CKD is not merely a renal disorder, but a systemic condition intricately linked with metabolic, vascular, and inflammatory pathways [3], probably explaining CKD as a major driver of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) and premature death [4]. Traditional cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia are highly prevalent in CKD patients [5], yet control rates remain suboptimal. Only 8.4% of CKD patients achieve simultaneous control of these 3 factors [6]. Additionally, only 37% of hypertensive CKD patients achieved blood pressure (BP) targets <130/80 mm Hg [7], and only 17.9% achieved low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels within the normal range [8]. Even under treatment, suboptimal risk factor control remains more common in CKD patients than non-CKD individuals [9]. These multiple uncontrolled risk factors jointly exacerbate CKD progression to end-stage renal disease and increase mortality risks [10]. Thus, improving clinical outcomes in CKD requires a holistic strategy.

Current CKD guidelines emphasize comprehensive risk factor management to mitigate CKD progression and reduce associated adverse outcomes [11]. However, some knowledge gaps remain. Few randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have evaluated multifactorial interventions for reducing mortality in CKD; only the Steno-2 trial showed that intensive management of glucose, BP, and lipids reduced all-cause mortality by 46% [12]. RCTs may not fully reflect real-world relationships, underscoring the need for observational studies. While observational research in diabetes and hypertension indicates that controlling multiple risk factors reduces excess mortality [13–16], this association remains underexplored in large, population-based studies of CKD. Only one cohort study in China reported that CKD patients who had systolic BP (SBP) <130 mm Hg, LDL-C <2.6 mmol/L, and fasting glucose <6.1 mmol/L showed no excess all-cause mortality risk compared to the general population [17]. Additionally, no study has yet examined the association between the number of risk factors controlled, including 7 biological and lifestyle domains, and all-cause and cause-specific mortality in CKD. Critically, it also remains unclear whether optimal multifactorial control could attenuate or eliminate CKD-related excess mortality. Accordingly, the study leverages a nationally representative U.S. dataset to (1) investigate associations between multiple risk factors controlled (BP, body mass index [BMI], glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c], non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [non-HDL-C], smoking, physical activity, and diet) and risks of all-cause and cause-specific mortality in CKD patients and (2) assess the extent to which controlling multiple risk factors attenuates CKD-related excess mortality, by comparing CKD patients with non-CKD individuals.

Methods

Study Population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a large-scale, ongoing cross-sectional study to collect data on the health and nutrition from a nationally representative sample of the US population. During a 2-year cycle survey starting from 1999 to 2000, participants were recruited and underwent standardized interviews, physical measurements, and laboratory tests at baseline enrollment. Each participant provided written informed consent. The National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Ethics Review Board approved the NHANES protocols (Protocol #2005-06, #2011-17, and #2018-01). For the current analysis, the study used data from 6 cycles of NHANES from 2007 to 2018 (n = 59,842) and selected adults with complete data about multiple risk factors, mortality, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) or urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) and without cancer and CVD. As shown in Figure S1 of the online supplementary material (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000549538), participants who were <20 years (n = 25,072), were pregnant (n = 372), with prevalent CVD (n = 3,875) or cancer (n = 2,535), had no available mortality data (n = 115), had missing values on any risk factor (n = 9,422), and had missing values on eGFR or ACR data (n = 95) were excluded. Finally, the current analysis included 18,356 participants.

Definition of CKD

CKD was defined as an eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or a urinary ACR ≥30 mg/g at the baseline examination. Participants who did not meet either criterion were classified as non-CKD. eGFR was calculated using the Collaborative Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation [18]. Urinary ACR was measured from the same urine sample, with urinary creatinine quantified by an enzymatic method and urinary albumin measured by fluorescent immunoassay. The urinary ACR (presented as mg/g) was calculated using the formula: ACR = urinary albumin/urinary creatinine.

Assessment of Multiple Risk Factor Control

According to clinical guideline-recommended target levels [19–21], multiple risk factor controls including metabolic, lifestyle, and cardiovascular parameters were defined as consisting of seven components: (1) SBP <130 mm Hg and diastolic BP (DBP) <80 mm Hg, (2) 18.5≤ BMI ≤24.9 kg/m2, (3) HbA1c <6.5%, (4) non-HDL-C <3.4 mmol/L, (5) nonsmoking status (never-smokers or past-smokers), (6) active physical activity (≥150 min of moderate-to-vigorous intensity activity per week), (7) high dietary quality, indicated by a Healthy Eating Index-2015 (HEI-2015) score within the top 40th percentile.

BP was measured by trained researchers following a standardized protocol (see link: https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhanes/public/2019/manuals/2019-Blood-Pressure-Procedures-Manual-508.pdf). After participants rested quietly in a seated position for 5 min and their maximum inflation level was determined, 3 consecutive readings were taken, and the mean of these readings was used for analysis. Height and weight were measured with participants wearing light indoor clothing, using a stadiometer and a calibrated digital scale, respectively. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m2). HbA1c was measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (Trinity Biotech Boronate Affinity method). Serum total cholesterol (TC) was assayed enzymatically on the Cobas 6000 analyzer, and HDL-C was measured directly on the same platform using a homogeneous, third-generation assay. Non-HDL-C was calculated as TC minus HDL-C. Given the limited availability of LDL-C measurements, which were only obtained from morning fasting samples, and the established prognostic relevance of non-HDL-C in CKD [22], non-HDL-C was used as the lipid risk factor in this study. More methodological details are provided in the corresponding laboratory procedures manual (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nhanes/public/2017/manuals/2017_MEC_Laboratory_Procedures_Manual.pdf). Smoking status and physical activity levels were assessed using standardized self-reported questionnaires. Data from two 24-h dietary recall interviews were used to compute HEI-2015 scores for diet quality assessment. The number of risk factors controlled for each participant, ranging from 0 to 7, was computed by counting how many of the 7 criteria were met. Given the limited number of CKD patients in some groups, the number of multiple risk factors controlled was categorized into 5 groups: 0–2, 3, 4, 5, and 6–7.

Covariates

Covariates were self-reported using standardized questionnaires, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), drinking status, hypertension, diabetes, and lipid-lowering drug use. Age was categorized into two subgroups: <65 years and ≥65 years. Race/ethnicity was classified as Mexican American, non-Hispanic black, non-Hispanic white, or other races. Education was categorized into less than high school or high school and above. PIR was categorized into low (<1.3), medium (1.3–3.0), and high (≥3.0) levels. Drinking status was classified as current drinkers or non-current drinkers (never or past drinkers). Hypertension was defined as either self-reported physician diagnosis, current use of antihypertensive medication, or BP ≥140/90 mm Hg. Diabetes was identified by self-reported diagnosis, current insulin or antidiabetic drug use, fasting glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, postprandial 2-h glucose ≥200 mg/dL, or HbA1c ≥6.5%. Lipid-lowering drugs used were defined as self-reported currently taking prescribed lipid-lowering medicines.

Outcomes

The study outcomes were all-cause and cause-specific mortality, including CVD mortality and cancer mortality. Mortality data were ascertained by linking NHANES participant records with the National Death Index, with follow-up through December 31, 2019. Underlying causes of death were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10). CVD mortality was defined by codes I00-I09, I11, I13, I20-I51, and I60-I69 and cancer mortality by codes C00-C97. Follow-up time was calculated from the baseline examination date until death date or last known survival date (December 31, 2019), whichever occurred first.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed in accordance with NHANES analytical guidelines to account for the complex survey design and sample weights, thereby ensuring nationally representative estimates for the US population. We compared the baseline characteristics of CKD patients stratified by the number of controlled risk factors with those of non-CKD individuals. Weighted means (standard errors) were reported for continuous variables, while counts (weighted percentages) were presented for categorical variables. Analysis of variance was used to compare continuous variables, and the chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables.

The Kaplan-Meier method was used to compute the cumulative incidence of mortality for each group, and the log-rank test was used for comparison. To evaluate the association between the number of risk factors controlled and mortality among CKD patients, we employed survey-weighted Cox proportional-hazards models, both unadjusted and multivariable adjusted, to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). The group with 0–2 controlled risk factors was used as the reference. The multivariate model was adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, PIR, drinking status, hypertension, diabetes, and lipid-lowering drug use. Additionally, we assessed the association between each individual risk factor controlled and mortality risk; these models were mutually adjusted for all other risk factors.

The number of controlled risk factors was analyzed both as a categorical variable (0–2, 3, 4, 5, and 6–7) and as a continuous variable (per one additional factor controlled). We used adjusted Cox models to compare mortality risk between CKD patients stratified by level of risk factor control and non-CKD individuals (reference group), evaluating the extent to which risk factor control attenuated the CKD-related excess mortality. Adjusted mortality rates and absolute rate differences per 1,000 person-years with 95% CIs were calculated using Poisson regression models.

We further assessed potential effect modification by including interaction terms between the number of controlled risk factors and age subgroup (<65 vs. ≥65 years) and sex (male vs. female). To examine the robustness of the primary findings, we conducted three sensitivity analyses: (1) exclusion of deaths occurring within the first 2 years of follow-up; (2) exclusion of participants with missing covariate data; and (3) redefinition of the reference group as non-CKD individuals with different numbers of controlled risk factors (0–3, 4 [median], or 5–7) to assess the consistency of results across alternative comparison groups.

Missing values for PIR (missing rate: 8.3%) were handled using multiple imputation. All analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R 4.2.2 (R Foundation, Vienna). A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

The study included 2,394 participants with CKD (weighted percentage: 10.7%) and 15,962 without CKD (89.3%); among CKD patients, the proportions of those with 0–2, 3, 4, 5, and 6–7 controlled risk factors were 2.8% (n = 714), 3.0% (n = 702), 2.6% (n = 567), 1.3% (n = 275), and 1.0% (n = 136) of the total study population, respectively. Compared with non-CKD individuals, CKD patients were older, more likely to be female, less educated, living in poverty, and physically inactive; they also had higher BMI, HbA1c, SBP, non-HDL-C levels, and higher prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, and lipid-lowering drug use (all p < 0.05). Furthermore, CKD patients with higher numbers of multiple risk factors controlled tended to be younger, be female, be white, and have higher educational attainment and lower poverty levels. These individuals also reported healthier behaviors, including higher physical activity, better dietary quality, and nonsmoking status. Clinically, they showed more favorable profiles, with lower HbA1c, BP, and non-HDL-C levels, higher use of lipid-lowering drugs, and lower prevalence of diabetes and hypertension (all p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Risk Factor Controlled and Mortality Risk in CKD

During a median follow-up of 7.0 (IQR: 4.2–10.0) years, 405 all-cause deaths (weighted rate: 12.3%), 110 CVD deaths (3.5%), and 87 cancer deaths (2.9%) occurred among CKD patients. Mortality rates decreased progressively with an increasing number of controlled risk factors. Specifically, all-cause mortality rates were 16.5%, 14.4%, 9.4%, 9.3%, and 5.3% for patients with 0–2, 3, 4, 5, and 6–7 controlled factors, respectively. Corresponding rates for CVD mortality were 4.8%, 3.4%, 3.4%, 2.4%, and 1.6% and for cancer mortality were 3.6%, 4.0%, 1.7%, 1.9%, and 1.6% (Table 2).

After multivariable adjustment, controlling for 4, 5, and 6–7 risk factors was associated with significantly lower risks of all-cause mortality compared with the reference group (0–2 factors), with HRs of 0.59 (95% CI: 0.40–0.86; p = 0.007), 0.59 (95% CI: 0.38–0.91; p = 0.018), and 0.41 (95% CI: 0.19–0.90; p = 0.027), respectively. When analyzed as a continuous variable, each additional controlled risk factor was associated with a 19% lower risk of all-cause mortality (HR: 0.81; 95% CI: 0.71–0.93; p = 0.002) and a 27% lower risk of cancer mortality (HR: 0.73; 95% CI: 0.54–0.99; p = 0.042). A trend toward reduced CVD mortality was also observed, although it was not significant (HR: 0.84; 95% CI: 0.69–1.01; p = 0.063) (Table 2). Additionally, control of individual risk factors, including smoking status, physical activity, and BP, was significantly associated with lower all-cause mortality among CKD patients (Table S1 in the online suppl. material).

Comparative Mortality Risks: CKD Stratified by Risk Factor Controlled vs. Non-CKD

Among non-CKD individuals, there were 585 all-cause deaths (weighted mortality rate: 2.9%), 141 CVD deaths (0.6%), and 167 cancer deaths (0.9%). In multivariable adjusted models, CKD patients exhibited higher risks of all-cause mortality (HR: 2.24; 95% CI: 1.80–2.78), CVD mortality (HR: 2.50; 95% CI: 1.76–3.56), and cancer mortality (HR: 1.86; 95% CI: 1.09–3.19) compared with non-CKD individuals (Table S2 in the online suppl. material).

After stratifying CKD patients by the number of controlled risk factors, the all-cause mortality rate among those with 6–7 controlled factors was not significantly different from that in the non-CKD group (2.69 per 1,000 person-years; 95% CI: 1.77–4.09), with an absolute rate difference of 0.79 per 1,000 person-years (95% CI: −2.65 to 6.63). Similarly, no significant excess risk was observed for CVD mortality (rate difference: 0.27; −0.80–3.00) or cancer mortality (rate difference: 0.05; −1.08–2.52) (Table 3). Kaplan-Meier curves showed similar results (Fig. S2 in the online suppl. material). In multivariable Cox models, CKD patients with 6–7 risk factors controlled had no significantly increased risks of all-cause (HR: 1.29; 95% CI: 0.62–2.66; p = 0.500), CVD (HR: 1.51; 95% CI: 0.43–5.34; p = 0.500), and cancer mortality (HR: 1.06; 95% CI: 0.28–3.96, p = 0.900) compared with non-CKD individuals (Fig. 1).

Discussion

The study is the first, to our knowledge, to use a large, nationally representative sample of the US population to examine the association between control of a comprehensive set of seven metabolic, lifestyle, and cardiovascular risk factors and all-cause and cause-specific mortality among CKD patients. Importantly, it also directly compares mortality risks between optimally controlled CKD patients and the general non-CKD populations. There are two key findings. First, among CKD patients, each additional risk factor control was associated with a 17%, 14%, and 25% lower risk of all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality, with the optimal control of 6–7 risk factors related to a 59% reduction in all-cause mortality risk. Second, CKD patients achieving optimal risk factor control, i.e., 6–7 factors, exhibited mortality risks statistically indistinguishable from those of non-CKD individuals for all-cause, CVD, and cancer mortality. These findings highlight the critical need for comprehensive management strategies that target multiple modifiable risk factors to improve clinical outcomes in CKD.

RCT evidence has shown that targeted management of individual risk factors, such as hypertension [23], diabetes [24], and hyperlipidemia [25], can delay renal function decline and reduce CVD incidence and mortality in CKD patients. However, multifactorial intervention strategies remain underexplored; to date, only a trial reported that joint control of glycemia, BP, and lipids reduced mortality among individuals with diabetes and persistent microalbuminuria [12]. Observational studies have largely examined the control of individual or limited numbers of risk factors (e.g., BP, HbA1c, LDL-C) and their association with mortality in CKD or related complications [13, 15, 26]. For instance, the Kailuan cohort found that controlling BP, fasting glucose, and LDL-C was associated with a 39% reduction in all-cause mortality, exhibiting a dose-response relationship [17]. Similarly, a study using a UK primary care database of patients with both CKD and diabetes showed that achieving four control targets (HbA1c, BP, TC, and smoking) was associated with a 40% lower risk of all-cause and CVD mortality [27]. Our findings are not only consistent with these studies but also expands the existing evidence by comprehensively assessing up to seven modifiable risk factors specifically in CKD, beyond three traditional factors. We also observed a graded reduction in all-cause mortality risk as the number of controlled risk factors increased, underscoring that multifactorial risk management is critically associated with improved survival in this high-risk group.

Crucially, our study directly compared mortality risks of optimally controlled CKD patients with non-CKD individuals, providing a unique perspective on the potential of multiple risk factors controlled to mitigate excess risk. We observed that CKD patients who achieved control of 6–7 risk factors exhibited no significant excess risk in all-cause or cause-specific mortality compared with non-CKD individuals, whether assessed via absolute rate differences or relative HRs. These results remained robust across multiple sensitivity and subgroup analyses. Additionally, the association aligns with previous studies reporting that multifactorial control reduces mortality in diabetes or hypertension [13–16], suggesting the benefit of multidimensional interventions in chronic diseases; however, evidence specific to CKD remains limited. Our results are supported by a prior study from China, which also reported a reduction in CKD-related excess mortality through improved risk factor control, even to levels comparable to non-CKD [17]. Notably, our study extends prior evidence by comprehensively evaluating seven modifiable risk factors, aligning with the American Heart Association (AHA) Life’s Simple 7 (LS7) framework for cardiovascular health [28]. Although previous studies from the UK Biobank and NHANES showed that higher LS7 scores were associated with lower mortality risks in CKD [29, 30], none have evaluated whether optimal LS7 achievement is associated with reduced excess mortality in CKD relative to the non-CKD reference group. Our study helps address this gap and underscores the importance of multidimensional risk management in CKD.

The mechanisms by which multifactorial control reduces mortality in CKD remain unclear; however, potential physiological pathways may explain this protective effect. Hypertension and diabetes synergistically exacerbate vascular and renal injury. Elevated BP accelerates glomerular hyperfiltration damage, while hyperglycemia promotes the deposition of advanced glycation end products, which collectively drives endothelial dysfunction and renal fibrosis. Concurrently, dyslipidemia exacerbates vascular inflammation through oxidized LDL-induced macrophage activation and foam cell formation [4]. Additionally, lifestyle modifications, such as smoking cessation, dietary improvements, and exercise, mitigate systemic inflammation and oxidative stress [31]. Multifactorial interventions may interrupt pathogenic cascades in CKD progression, which stem from the interplay among metabolic disturbances, hemodynamic stress, and chronic inflammation [3], suggesting that multiple risk factors controlled may yield synergistic effects, thereby lowering mortality risk. Nevertheless, further mechanistic studies are warranted.

The study has important implications in clinical practice. The observation that only 1.0% of CKD patients achieved control of 6–7 risk factors underscores a critical gap in current care and emphasizes the urgent need for integrated, multifactorial management strategies to reduce premature mortality in this high-risk population. Suboptimal control of risk factor, despite higher treatment rates, may result from low disease awareness among both patients and clinicians regarding CKD and its associated risk factors [6]. Our findings provide scientific support for implementing multifactorial intervention strategies, reinforcing the importance of managing not just a single factor. Nephrologists collaborating with other specialists in cardiology and endocrinology can better identify patients with high comorbidity burdens and unhealthy lifestyles [6]; thus, multidisciplinary approaches should be integrated into routine CKD management. Our study also supports the treat-to-target concept, i.e., treating patients to achieve predefined targets for both biological and lifestyle factors, as a practical approach for improving prognosis in CKD. These findings also align with the AHA’s 2023 advisory on cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic health, which advocates a shift from isolated risk management toward an integrated, interdisciplinary care model [32].

The strengths of this study include the use of a large, nationally representative sample of US adults, a population-based design, and the systematic evaluation of seven guideline-recommended risk factors spanning both biological and lifestyle domains. However, this study has several limitations. First, the study is observational without causal inference; thus, RCTs are needed to validate multifactorial intervention on survival in CKD. Second, CKD was defined based on measurement of eGFR and UACR at a single baseline timepoint rather than clinical diagnosis, and lifestyle factors were self-reported, which may introduce misclassification. Third, the study did not account for changes in risk factor control over time due to data unavailability. Cohort studies with repeated measurements are warranted. Fourth, residual confounding may persist despite multivariate adjustments, particularly from unmeasured variables such as medication adherence and socioeconomic determinants. Fifth, the selection of risk factor did not incorporate other emerging risk factors; future research should explore additional factors to provide a more comprehensive understanding. Finally, it should be acknowledged that only a small proportion of individuals with CKD achieved optimal control of 6–7 risk factors. This limited sample size resulted in reduced statistical power, particularly for cause-specific mortality outcomes such as CVD and cancer, as shown by wide CIs. Thus, the generalizability of these findings may be limited, and the results should be interpreted with caution. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these findings and further investigate the underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

CKD patients achieving optimal control of seven modifiable risk factors, including BP, BMI, HbA1c, non-HDL-C, smoking status, physical activity, and diet quality, had no significant excess risk of mortality than non-CKD individuals. The finding highlights the necessity for integrated risk factor strategies to improve survival in CKD patients.
