Positive semidefinite and positive semidefinite splitting iteration methods for solving nonsingular positive semidefinite systems
Mohsen Masoudi \comma, \corrauth and Davod Khojasteh Salkuyeh

Abstract
This article introduces an iterative method for solving nonsingular positive semidefinite systems of linear equations. To construct the iteration process, the coefficient matrix is split into two positive semidefinite matrices along with an arbitrary Hermitian positive definite shift matrix. Several conditions are established to guarantee the convergence of method and suggestions are provided for selecting the matrices involved in the desired splitting. We explore selection process of the shift matrix and determine the optimal parameter in a specific scenario. The proposed method aims to generalize previous approaches and improve the conditions for convergence theorems. In addition, we examine two special cases of this method and compare the induced preconditioners with some state-of-art preconditioners. Numerical examples are given to demonstrate effectiveness of the presented preconditioners.

Introduction

Consider the solution of systems of linear equations in the form

\mathcal{A}{u}={b},

(1)

where
{b}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\mathcal{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is large, sparse, nonsingular positive semidefinite (PSD), i.e.,
\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A^{*}}
is Hermitian positive semidefinite (HPSD).
Systems of this type arise in numerous scientific and engineering applications, such as computational fluid dynamics and constrained optimization
[
1
,
11
,
21
]
.
Various methods can be utilized to solve a system of linear equations when
\mathcal{A}
is assumed to be positive definite (PD), i.e.,
\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A^{*}}
is Hermitian positive definite (HPD).
For instance, the Hermitian and skew-Hermitian splitting (HSS) method was introduced by Bai et al. in
[
5
]
.
This method uses the Hermitian and skew-Hermitian splitting of the matrix
\mathcal{A}
as
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{S}
, where
\mathcal{H}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}^{*})
and
\mathcal{S}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{A}^{*})
. The iteration process is then based on this splitting:

\begin{cases}(\alpha\mathcal{I}+{\mathcal{H}}){u}^{k+\frac{1}{2}}=(\alpha\mathcal{I}-{\mathcal{S}}){u}^{k}+{b},\\
(\alpha\mathcal{I}+{\mathcal{S}}){u}^{k+1}=(\alpha\mathcal{I}-{\mathcal{H}}){u}^{k+\frac{1}{2}}+{b},\end{cases}

in which
\alpha>0
,
\mathcal{I}
is identity matrix and
u^{0}
is an arbitrary initial guess.

It is shown in
[
5
]
that the HSS method converges unconditionally to the unique solution of the system (
1
) when A is positive definite.
Due to the method’s efficiency, it has attracted significant attention from researchers, leading to the developing several extensions. The preconditioned version of the HSS method, known as PHSS, was proposed by Bai et al. in
[
7
]
.
The modified HSS (MHSS) and its preconditioned version were introduced by Bai et al. for solving symmetric complex systems
[
2
,
3
]
. Two generalized versions of HSS (GHSS) were introduced in
[
9
,
26
]
.
The positive definite and skew-Hermitian splitting (PSS) method, introduced by Bai et al. in
[
4
]
, was proposed to solve positive definite linear systems. It was proved in
[
4
]
that the PSS method converges unconditionally to the unique solution of such systems. By further generalizing the HSS iteration method, the normal and skew-Hermitian splitting (NSS) iteration method was proposed in
[
6
]
. A generalization of PSS (GPSS) was introduced by Cao et al. in
[
14
]
. An extension of this method was stated in
[
19
]
by Masoudi and Salkuyeh. Huang and Ma in
[
17
]
presented the positive definite and semidefinite splitting method. The shift-splitting (SS) iterative method and the corresponding induced preconditioner was presented by Bai in
[
8
]
.

Similar to the HSS method, we introduce an iterative method, called
Positive semidefinite and Positive semidefinite Splitting (PPS)
method, for solving the system (
1
). The proposed method employs a splitting of the form
\mathcal{A}={\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
, where
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
are two positive semidefinite matrices, combined with an arbitrary HPD matrix
\Sigma
as a shift. Our method encompasses various existing approaches and enhances their convergence theorems. We establish conditions under which the method is guaranteed to converge. An advantage of our method over the other methods is the flexibility in selecting matrices
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
. Some ideas concerning choosing the matrices
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
, and the shift matrix
\Sigma
are discussed.

Throughout this paper, we use the following notations. The set of
n\times m
complex matrices is denoted by
\mathbb{C}^{n\times m}
and
\mathbb{C}^{n}=\mathbb{C}^{n\times 1}
represents the set of

n
-dimensional complex vectors.
The symbol
\mathcal{I}
denotes the identity matrix.
For
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, the notations
\text{{null}}{(A)}
,
\text{{rank}}{(A)}
,
\rho(A)
,
\sigma(A)
,
\left\|{A}\right\|
and
eigvec
(
A
) stand for the null space, rank, spectral radius, spectrum, Euclidean norm and the set of all nonzero eigenvectors of the matrix
A
, respectively. If
Q\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is an HPD matrix and
Q=U^{*}DU
is the spectral decomposition of matrix
Q
, then we define
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}=U^{*}D^{\frac{1}{2}}U
.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the PPS iterative method and its induced preconditioner. Our research, outlined in Section
3
,
focuses on analyzing the necessary and sufficient conditions for the convergence of the method, as well as establishing the specific requirements for its existence. The selection of the matrix
\Sigma
and determination of the optimal parameter are investigated in Section
4
. In Section
5
, we discuss choosing the matrices
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
. To demonstrate the theoretical results, we present numerical examples in Section
6
. Finally, the paper concludes with Section
7
.

2
The PPS method

Assume that the matrix
\mathcal{A}
has a splitting of the following form

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}={\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2},

(2)

where
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
are PSD.
In this case, we say that the matrix
\mathcal{A}
has a
Positive semidefinite and Positive semidefinite (PP)
splitting.
Consider the following two splittings

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=

\displaystyle(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{1})-(\Sigma-{\mathcal{P}}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{2})-(\Sigma-{\mathcal{P}}_{1}),

where
\Sigma
is an arbitrary HPD matrix.
Obviously, both of the matrices
\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
are nonsingular. Now, similar to the HSS method
[
5
]
, we establish the PPS method as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{1}){u}^{k+\frac{1}{2}}=(\Sigma-{\mathcal{P}}_{2}){u}^{k}+{b},\\
(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}){u}^{k+1}=(\Sigma-{\mathcal{P}}_{1}){u}^{k+\frac{1}{2}}+{b},\end{cases}

(3)

where
{u}^{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a given initial guess.

Note that all the PD, HPSD or skew-Hermitian matrices are PSD.
If
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
is a skew-Hermitian and
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
is an HPD (resp. PD), then the PPS method reduces to the PHSS
[
13
]
(resp. Extend of PSS (EPSS) method
[
19
]
). Now, let
\Sigma=\alpha\mathcal{I}
. If
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}=0
, then the PPS method becomes the shift-splitting (SS) method
[
8
]
. If
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
is a skew-Hermitian and
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
is an HPD (resp. PD) then the PPS method turns into the HSS
[
5
]
(resp. PSS
[
4
]
) method. If
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
is PD and normal, i.e.,
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*}={\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*}{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
, and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
is a skew-Hermitian, then the PPS method reduces to the NSS method
[
6
]
. Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{H}_{1}+\mathcal{H}_{2}+\mathcal{S}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
are HPSD and
\mathcal{S}
is skew-Hermitian. Setting
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}=\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}=\mathcal{H}_{2}+\mathcal{S}
, we obtain the generalized HSS (GHSS) method
[
9
]
. Therefore, the PPS method is a generalization of the HSS, GHSS, PHSS, NSS, PSS, EPSS and SS methods.

Eliminating
{u}^{k+\frac{1}{2}}
from (
3
) yields

\displaystyle{u}^{k+1}=\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}}{u}^{k}+{c},

(4)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}}=(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{2})^{-1}(\Sigma-{\mathcal{P}}_{1})(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{1})^{-1}(\Sigma-{\mathcal{P}}_{2}),

(5)

and
{c}=2(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{2})^{-1}\Sigma(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{1})^{-1}{b}
.
It is known that the PPS iteration method is convergent for any initial guess
u^{0}
, if and only if
\rho(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})<1

[
22
]
.

Since the matrix
\Sigma
is HPD, the PPS iteration matrix can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}}

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})\Sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})\Sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{2})^{-1}\Sigma(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{1})^{-1}(\Sigma-{\mathcal{P}}_{1})\Sigma^{-1}(\Sigma-{\mathcal{P}}_{2}),

(6)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{i}:=\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathcal{P}}_{i}\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}},~~\text{for}~~i=1,2.

(7)

If we define

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}_{{\rm{PPS}}}}:=\frac{1}{2}(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{1})\Sigma^{-1}(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{2})\quad\textrm{and}\quad{\mathcal{N}}_{{\rm{PPS}}}:=\frac{1}{2}(\Sigma-{\mathcal{P}}_{1})\Sigma^{-1}(\Sigma-{\mathcal{P}}_{2}),

(8)

then
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{M}_{{\rm{PPS}}}-\mathcal{N}_{{\rm{PPS}}}
, the matrix
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm{PPS}}}
is nonsingular and

\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}}=\mathcal{M}_{{\rm{PPS}}}^{-1}{\mathcal{N}_{{\rm{PPS}}}}
.
Therefore, if we apply a Krylov subspace method such as GMRES
[
23
]
to approximate the solution of the system (
1
), then the matrix
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm{PPS}}}
can be considered as a preconditioner to the system. Since the prefactor
\frac{1}{2}
in the preconditioner
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm{PPS}}}
has no effect on the preconditioned system, we can take the matrix

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{{\rm{PPS}}}:=(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{1})\Sigma^{-1}(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}),

(9)

as the PPS preconditioner for solving (
1
).

We prove that if
\mathcal{A}
is PD and one of the following conditions holds, then the PPS method with any HPD matrix
\Sigma
is convergent:

(i)

If one of the matrices
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
is HPSD.

(ii)

If one of the matrices
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
is skew-Hermitian.

(iii)

If one of the matrices
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
is PD.

(iv)

If
\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{1})\cup\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})=\mathbb{C}^{n}
or
\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{2})\cup\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*})=\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

(v)

If the matrix

{\mathcal{A}}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*}\Sigma^{-1}{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*}\Sigma^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}

is PD.

(vi)

If

{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\Sigma^{-1}{\mathcal{P}}_{2}={\mathcal{P}}_{2}\Sigma^{-1}{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
(e.g. if

{\mathcal{P}}_{1}{\mathcal{P}}_{2}={\mathcal{P}}_{2}{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
\Sigma=\alpha\mathcal{I}
).

3
Convergence of the PPS method

Let
{\mathcal{P}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
.
If
\mathcal{I}+{\mathcal{P}}
is invertible,
then we define the function
f
as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} f({\mathcal{P}}):=\left\|{(\mathcal{I}-{\mathcal{P}})(\mathcal{I}+{\mathcal{P}})^{-1}}\right\|.\end{aligned}

(10)

Then we have

\displaystyle f({\mathcal{P}})

\displaystyle=\max_{x\neq 0}\frac{\left\|{(\mathcal{I}-{\mathcal{P}})x}\right\|}{\left\|{(\mathcal{I}+{\mathcal{P}})x}\right\|}=\max_{x\neq 0}\sqrt{\frac{{x^{*}(\mathcal{I}+{\mathcal{P}}^{*}{\mathcal{P}})x-x^{*}({\mathcal{P}}+{\mathcal{P}}^{*})x}}{x^{*}(\mathcal{I}+{\mathcal{P}}^{*}{\mathcal{P}})x+x^{*}({\mathcal{P}}+{\mathcal{P}}^{*})x}}.

Therefore, we have the following remark.

Remark 3.1
.

Suppose that
-1\notin\sigma({\mathcal{P}})
and
f({\mathcal{P}})
is defined as in (
10
).

1.

If
{\mathcal{P}}
is skew-Hermitian, then for all
x\neq 0
we have
x^{*}({\mathcal{P}}+{\mathcal{P}}^{*})x=0
and so
f({\mathcal{P}})=1
.

2.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a normal matrix, then there exist matrices
U
and
D
such that

\mathcal{P}=U^{*}\mathcal{D}U
,
where
U
is a unitary matrix and
\mathcal{D}
is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{P}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle f({\mathcal{P}})

\displaystyle=\left\|{(\mathcal{I}-{U^{*}\mathcal{D}U})(\mathcal{I}+{U^{*}\mathcal{D}U})^{-1}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|{(\mathcal{I}-{\mathcal{D}})(\mathcal{I}+{\mathcal{D}})^{-1}}\right\|

\displaystyle={\max_{\lambda\in\sigma({\mathcal{P}})}}{\left|\frac{1-\lambda}{1+\lambda}\right|}.

3.

The matrix
{\mathcal{P}}
is PSD if and only if
for all
x\neq 0
,
x^{*}({\mathcal{P}}+{\mathcal{P}}^{*})x\geq 0
if and only if
for all
x\neq 0
,

\frac{{x^{*}(\mathcal{I}+{\mathcal{P}}^{*}{\mathcal{P}})x-x^{*}({\mathcal{P}}+{\mathcal{P}}^{*})x}}{x^{*}(\mathcal{I}+{\mathcal{P}}^{*}{\mathcal{P}})x+x^{*}({\mathcal{P}}+{\mathcal{P}}^{*})x}\leq 1

if and only if

f({\mathcal{P}})\leq 1
.

4.

The matrix
{\mathcal{P}}
is PD if and only if
for all
x\neq 0
,
x^{*}({\mathcal{P}}+{\mathcal{P}}^{*})x>0
if and only if for all
x\neq 0
,

\frac{{x^{*}(\mathcal{I}+{\mathcal{P}}^{*}{\mathcal{P}})x-x^{*}({\mathcal{P}}+{\mathcal{P}}^{*})x}}{x^{*}(\mathcal{I}+{\mathcal{P}}^{*}{\mathcal{P}})x+x^{*}({\mathcal{P}}+{\mathcal{P}}^{*})x}<1

if and only if
f({\mathcal{P}})<1
.

Let
\tilde{\Gamma}:=(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})
. By (

), we have

\sigma(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})=\sigma(\tilde{\Gamma})
and moreover
x\in\text{{eigvec}}(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})

if and only if
\Sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}x\in\text{{eigvec}}(\tilde{\Gamma})
.

To facilitate the proof of the theorems, we will work with the sets
\text{{eigvec}}(\tilde{\Gamma})
and
\sigma(\tilde{\Gamma})
. In the next theorem, we present an upper bound for

.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that the matrix
\mathcal{A}
has a splitting of the form (

).
Then, for every

(12)

in which

is defined in (

\displaystyle\rho(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})\leqslant\max_{x_{\lambda}\in\text{{eigvec}}(\tilde{\Gamma})}\min\left\{m_{1}\left(x_{\lambda}\right),m_{2}\left((\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}\right)\right\}\leqslant 1.

(13)

Proof 3.3
.

Let
\lambda\in\sigma(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})
.
Therefore

\lambda\in\sigma(\tilde{\Gamma})
. Thus,
there exists a vector
x_{\lambda}\in\text{{eigvec}}(\tilde{\Gamma})
such that

\tilde{\Gamma}x_{\lambda}=\lambda x_{\lambda}
.
So,

(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}=\lambda(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}.

Hence

\displaystyle\left\|{\lambda(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|{(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\|{(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})}\right\|\left\|{(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}}\right\|

\displaystyle=f(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})\left\|{(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}}\right\|,

(14)

which is equivalent to

|\lambda|\leq f(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})\frac{\left\|{(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}}\right\|}{\left\|{(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}}\right\|},

which results in

|\lambda|\leqslant m_{1}\left(x_{\lambda}\right)
.

Also, from
\tilde{\Gamma}x_{\lambda}=\lambda x_{\lambda}
, we have
(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})\tilde{\Gamma}x_{\lambda}=\lambda(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}
and so

\displaystyle\left\|{\lambda(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|{(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq f(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})\left\|{(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}}\right\|.

.
Then,
\left\|{\lambda(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})y_{\lambda}}\right\|\leq f(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})\left\|{(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})y_{\lambda}}\right\|

which is equivalent to

|\lambda|\leq f(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})\frac{\left\|{(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})y_{\lambda}}\right\|}{\left\|{(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})y_{\lambda}}\right\|},

and so
|\lambda|\leqslant m_{2}(y_{\lambda})

It is clear that matrices
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and

,

. Similarly,

Theorem 3.5

Proof 3.6
.

Let
\lambda\in\sigma(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})
. By Theorem
3.2
, there exists a vector
0\neq x_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, such that

\displaystyle(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}=\lambda(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}.

(17)

Therefore,
\left\|{(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{A}}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}}\right\|=\left\|{\lambda(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{A}}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}}\right\|
.
Hence,

\displaystyle|\lambda|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\|{(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{A}}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}}\right\|^{2}}{\left\|{(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{A}}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}}\right\|^{2}}={\frac{r_{1}(x_{\lambda})-r_{2}(x_{\lambda})}{r_{1}(x_{\lambda})+r_{2}(x_{\lambda})}},

where
r_{i}(x)
, for
i=1,2
, are defined in (
16
).
By (
11
) and (
12
),

{\frac{r_{1}(x_{\lambda})-r_{2}(x_{\lambda})}{r_{1}(x_{\lambda})+r_{2}(x_{\lambda})}}=|\lambda|^{2}\leq(m_{1}(x_{\lambda}))^{2}\leq 1

and so
r_{2}(x_{\lambda})\geq 0.

Therefore, from

r_{1}(x_{\lambda})-r_{2}(x_{\lambda})=\left\|{(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}}\right\|^{2}\geq 0,

we deduce that
r_{1}(x_{\lambda})\geq r_{2}(x_{\lambda})
.
Also, since
(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})
is nonsingular, we get

r_{1}(x_{\lambda})+r_{2}(x_{\lambda})=\left\|{(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})x_{\lambda}}\right\|^{2}>0,

and so
r_{1}(x_{\lambda})>0
. Hence

\rho(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})=\max_{x_{\lambda}\in\text{{eigvec}}(\tilde{\Gamma})}\sqrt{\frac{r_{1}(x_{\lambda})-r_{2}(x_{\lambda})}{r_{1}(x_{\lambda})+r_{2}(x_{\lambda})}},~~r_{1}(x_{\lambda})\geq r_{2}(x_{\lambda})\geq 0,~~r_{1}(x_{\lambda})>0,

and

\rho(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})<1
if and only if
r_{2}(x_{\lambda})\neq 0
, for all
x_{\lambda}\in\text{{eigvec}}(\tilde{\Gamma})
.

Using Theorem
3.5
, we have following corollary.

Corollary 3.7
.

Suppose that the matrix
\mathcal{A}
has a splitting of the form (
2
) and
K=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
. If
K

is PD, then the PPS method is convergent.

Proof 3.8
.

Suppose that the matrix
K
is PD. Therefore,
K+K^{*}
is HPD and so by
[
16
, Corollary 4.3.15]
,
r_{2}(x)=x^{*}(K+K^{*})x\geq\lambda_{\min}\left(K+K^{*}\right)\|x\|_{2}^{2}>0,
for all
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Thus, by Theorem
3.5
,
\rho(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})<1.

Corollary 3.9
.

Suppose that the matrix
\mathcal{A}
is PD and has a splitting of the form (
2
). If
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}=\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
, then the PPS method is convergent.

Proof 3.10
.

Since
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}=\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle K+K^{*}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}+\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}+\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}+\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}+(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*}(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}+\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*}(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*})\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}+\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*}(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*})\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*}(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}.

Since
{\mathcal{A}}
is PD, we conclude that
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}+\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
is HPD. Therefore
K+K^{*}
is HPD and so

K
is PD. Hence, by Corollary
3.7
, the PPS method is convergent.

The next theorem presents other conditions for the convergence of the PPS method.

Theorem 3.11
.

Suppose that the matrix
\mathcal{A}
is PD and has a splitting of the form (
2
). If one of the following conditions holds, then
\rho(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})<1
:

(1)

If
\text{{eigvec}}(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})\cap\text{{null}}{({\mathcal{P}}_{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})}\subseteq\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{2})
.

(2)

If
\text{{eigvec}}(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})\subseteq\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{2})\cup\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*})
.

Proof 3.12
.

Let
(\lambda,x)
be an eigenpair of the matrix
\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}}
, i.e.,
\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}}x=\lambda x
. Thus,
x\in\text{{eigvec}}(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})
and we get
x_{\lambda}=\Sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}x\in\text{{eigvec}}(\tilde{\Gamma})
. Since
\mathcal{A}
is PD,
x^{*}({\mathcal{A}}+{\mathcal{A}}^{*})x>0
and so
x^{*}({\mathcal{P}}_{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})x>0
or
x^{*}({\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*})x>0
.

(1)

If
x^{*}({\mathcal{P}}_{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})x>0
, then
x_{\lambda}^{*}(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})x_{\lambda}>0
and as a result by Theorem
3.2
, we have
m_{1}\left(x_{\lambda}\right)<1
and so
|\lambda|<1
. On the other hand, if
x^{*}({\mathcal{P}}_{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})x=0
, then
x^{*}({\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*})x>0
.
Moreover, by
[
16
, Observation 7.1.6]
, we have
x\in\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})
and so
x\in\text{{eigvec}}(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})\cap\text{{null}}{({\mathcal{P}}_{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})}
.
By the assumption,
x\in\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{2})
and hence
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}x=0
. Therefore,
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}x_{\lambda}=0
.
So we have
x_{\lambda}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*}=0
. By the definition of
r_{2}
in (
16
), we conclude that

\displaystyle r_{2}(x_{\lambda})=~

\displaystyle x_{\lambda}^{*}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}+\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}+\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{A}})x_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle x_{\lambda}^{*}(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})x_{\lambda}+x_{\lambda}^{*}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}x_{\lambda}+x_{\lambda}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*}(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*}\tilde{\mathcal{A}})x_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle x_{\lambda}^{*}(\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*})x_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle x^{*}({\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*})x>0.

So, by Theorem
3.5
,
\rho(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})<1
.

(2)

Let
\text{{eigvec}}(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})\subseteq\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{2})\cup\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*})
. Since,
\mathcal{A}
is PD, we have
\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*})\cap\text{{null}}{({\mathcal{P}}_{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})}=\{0\}
and so

\displaystyle\text{{eigvec}}(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})\cap\text{{null}}{({\mathcal{P}}_{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})}

\displaystyle\subseteq(\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{2})\cup\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*}))\cap\text{{null}}{({\mathcal{P}}_{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})}

\displaystyle=(\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{2})\cap\text{{null}}{({\mathcal{P}}_{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})})\cup\{0\}.

Obviously
\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{2})\subseteq\text{{null}}{({\mathcal{P}}_{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\text{{eigvec}}(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})\cap\text{{null}}{({\mathcal{P}}_{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})}

\displaystyle\subseteq\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{2}).

Using case (1), we have
\rho(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})<1
.

Using Theorem
3.11
, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 3.13
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a PD matrix with a splitting of the form (
2
).
If
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
is PD, or
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
is HPSD, or
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
is skew-Hermitian, or
\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{2})\cup\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*})=\mathbb{C}^{n}
, then
\rho(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})<1
.

Proof 3.14
.

If
\mathcal{P}_{2}
is PD, then
\text{{null}}{(\mathcal{P}_{2}+\mathcal{P}_{2}^{*})}=\{0\}
, and so

\text{{eigvec}}(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})\cap\text{{null}}{({\mathcal{P}}_{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})}=\{0\}\subseteq\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{2}).

Hence, the result follows from part (1) of Theorem
3.11
.

If
\mathcal{P}_{2}
is HPSD, then
\text{{null}}{(\mathcal{P}_{2}+\mathcal{P}_{2}^{*})}=\text{{null}}{(2\mathcal{P}_{2})}=\text{{null}}{(\mathcal{P}_{2})}
, and

\text{{eigvec}}(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})\cap\text{{null}}{({\mathcal{P}}_{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})}=\text{{eigvec}}(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})\cap\text{{null}}{({\mathcal{P}}_{2})}\subseteq\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{2}).

Therefore, convergence of the method is deduced using part (1) of Theorem
3.11
.

If
\mathcal{P}_{1}
is skew-Hermitian, then
\text{{null}}{(\mathcal{P}_{1}+\mathcal{P}_{1}^{*})}=\text{{null}}(0)=\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and hence

\text{{eigvec}}(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}=\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{2})\cup\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*}).

Thus, convergence of the method is concluded using part (2) of Theorem
3.11
.

If
\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{2})\cup\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*})=\mathbb{C}^{n}
, then
convergence of the method is inferred from part (2) of Theorem
3.11
.

Remark 3.15
.

We have

\displaystyle\sigma(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})

\displaystyle=\sigma\Big((\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})\Big)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left((\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left((\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left((\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})^{-1}(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2})(\mathcal{I}-\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1})\right)

Therefore, by changing
the roles of the matrices
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
, i.e., considering the splitting
\mathcal{A}={\mathcal{P}}_{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
,
if the matrix
\mathcal{A}
is PD and one of the following conditions holds, then the PPS method with any HPD matrix
\Sigma
is convergent:

(i)

If one of the matrices
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
is HPSD.

(ii)

If one of the matrices
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
is skew-Hermitian.

(iii)

If one of the matrices
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
is PD.

(iv)

If
\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{1})\cup\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})=\mathbb{C}^{n}
or
\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{2})\cup\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*})=\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Now, some comments and suggestions are in order. In each of the following cases the PPS method is convergent:

(1)

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{H}+{\mathcal{P}}
is PD, where
\mathcal{H}
is HPSD and
{\mathcal{P}}
is PSD.

(2)

\mathcal{A}={\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
is PD and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
is PSD.

(3)

\mathcal{A}={\mathcal{P}}+\mathcal{S}
is PD, where
{\mathcal{P}}
is PSD and
\mathcal{S}
is skew-Hermitian.
It is worth noting that in the special case that
\Sigma=\alpha\mathcal{I}
, the HSS, NSS, PSS, EPSS, and SS iterative methods are obtained.

Consider the saddle point matrix

\mathcal{A}=\begin{bmatrix}A&B^{*}\\
-B&C\end{bmatrix},

(18)

where
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
and
C\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
. We assume that the matrix
\mathcal{A}
is PD (in this case, the matrices
A
and
C
are PD). Then, from (
iv
) of Remark
3.15
, the PPS method is convergent with the splitting

\mathcal{A}={\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
, in which

{\mathcal{P}}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&C\end{bmatrix},\quad{\mathcal{P}}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}A&B^{*}\\
-B&0\end{bmatrix},

(19)

or

{\mathcal{P}}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad{\mathcal{P}}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&B^{*}\\
-B&C\end{bmatrix}.

(20)

However, when the matrix
\mathcal{A}
is PSD, convergence of the PPS method is not guaranteed.
For instance, consider the nonsingular matrix

\mathcal{A}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right],

with splitting
\mathcal{A}={\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
, in which
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}=\mathcal{A}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}=0
. Letting
\Sigma=\mathcal{I}
, we have
\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}}=\mathcal{A}
. The eigenvalues of the iteration matrix
\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}}
are
\lambda=\pm i
.
So, the spectral radius of
\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}}
is equal to
1
and the PPS method is not convergent.
Therefore, if the matrix
\mathcal{A}
is nonsingular and the convergence conditions are not met, then the PPS method may diverge.
Nevertheless, we can generate a new convergent iterative method for nonsingular PSD system (
1
) as (see
[
10
, page 27]
,
[
24
]
)

\displaystyle\hat{u}^{k+1}=\left((1-\beta)I+\beta\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}}\right)\hat{u}^{k}+\beta\mathcal{M}_{{\rm{PPS}}}^{-1}b,~~~\beta\in(0,1),~~k=1,2,\ldots

(21)

where
\mathcal{M}_{{\rm{PPS}}}
is defined in (
8
) and
\hat{u}^{0}
is initial guess.
Therefore, we can use the preconditioner
{\mathcal{P}}_{\beta}=\frac{1}{\beta}\mathcal{M}_{{\rm{PPS}}}=\frac{1}{2\beta}{\mathcal{P}}_{{\rm{PPS}}}
for the equation (
1
), for all
\beta\in(0,1)
, where
{\mathcal{P}}_{{\rm{PPS}}}
is presented in (
9
).
It is advisable to use a value of
\beta
close to 1.
Since
\beta
is close to 1 and the prefactor
\frac{1}{2\beta}
has no effect on the preconditioned system,
we can continue using the preconditioner
{\mathcal{P}}_{{\rm{PPS}}}
even if none of the provided convergence conditions are met.

4
Choosing the matrix
\Sigma

If
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
is HPD and
\Sigma={\mathcal{P}}_{1}
, then
\rho(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})=\rho(0)=0
.
However, in this scenario, solving the first half-step of the system in (
3
) is equivalent to solving
\mathcal{A}{u}={b}
. Thus, selecting a matrix
\Sigma
close to
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
can lead to faster convergence with the PPS method.
This is because when
\Sigma
is close to
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
, the matrix
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
is also close to the identity matrix, implying that
m_{1}
in Theorem
3.2
approaches zero. Consequently,
\rho(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})\approx 0
, indicating rapid convergence of the PPS method. However, this may increase the computation time.

Since the matrix
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
is PSD, we see that the matrix
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\frac{1}{2}({\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{*})
is HPSD. If
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is nonsingular, then
\Sigma
can be chosen as an approximation of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
. Otherwise,
\Sigma
can be selected as an approximation of the matrix
\epsilon I+\mathcal{H}_{1}
, where
\epsilon
is a small positive number.
The same relationship holds for
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}=\frac{1}{2}({\mathcal{P}}_{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})
.
So, we take
\Sigma
as an approximation of the matrix

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}({\mathcal{H}}_{1}+{\mathcal{H}}_{2})=\frac{1}{4}({\mathcal{A}}+{\mathcal{A}}^{*}).

(22)

In the sequel, we assume that
\Sigma=\alpha Q
, where
\alpha>0
and
Q
is a suitable HPD matrix. We choose the parameter
\alpha
such a way that
{\mathcal{N}}_{{\rm{PPS}}}\approx 0
. In this case, we see that
\mathcal{A}\approx\mathcal{M}_{{\rm{PPS}}}
, and a high speed of convergence is expected. To do so, we set

\frac{1}{2}(\Sigma-{\mathcal{P}}_{1})\Sigma^{-1}(\Sigma-{\mathcal{P}}_{2})=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha Q-{\mathcal{P}}_{1})(\alpha Q)^{-1}(\alpha Q-{\mathcal{P}}_{2})\approx 0,

which results in

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}Q+{\mathcal{P}}_{1}Q^{-1}{\mathcal{P}}_{2}\approx\alpha{\mathcal{A}}.

(23)

Now, we define the function

\phi(\alpha)=\alpha^{2}\left\|{Q}\right\|_{F}+\left\|{{\mathcal{P}}_{1}Q^{-1}{\mathcal{P}}_{2}}\right\|_{F}-\alpha\left\|{{\mathcal{A}}}\right\|_{F},

where
\left\|{\cdot}\right\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm. This function has a minimum at

\displaystyle\alpha_{*}=\frac{\left\|{{\mathcal{A}}}\right\|_{F}}{2\left\|{Q}\right\|_{F}}.

(24)

For this value of
\alpha
, we have

\Sigma=\frac{\left\|{{\mathcal{A}}}\right\|_{F}}{2}\frac{Q}{\left\|{Q}\right\|_{F}},

which demonstrates that if we replace the matrix
Q
by
kQ
where
k
is a positive number, then the matrix
\Sigma
remains unchanged. Therefore, referring to (
22
), we can set
\Sigma=\alpha_{*}Q
, where
\alpha_{*}
is defined by (
24
) and
Q
is an approximation of the matrix
{\mathcal{H}}=\frac{1}{2}({\mathcal{A}}+{\mathcal{A}}^{*})
.

5
Choosing matrices
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}

For a PSD matrix
\mathcal{A}
,
some choices of
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
are as follows:

(i)

Suppose that the matrix
\mathcal{A}
has a splitting of the form
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{L}+\mathcal{D}+\mathcal{U}
, where
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{U}^{*}
are block lower triangular, and
\mathcal{D}
is block diagonal matrix. Moreover, we assume that the matrix

is PD and
\mathcal{L}=-\mathcal{U}^{*}
. Let also

\mathcal{D}_{1}

In this case, a choice for the matrices

and

Thus,
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and

.

(ii)

Let
\mathcal{A}=\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{1}+\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{2}

\mathcal{A}
, where
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{1}=\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{3}+\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{4}
in which
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{3}
and
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{4}
are PSD. Then, we can choose
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}=\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{3}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}=\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{2}+\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{4}
.
For example, let
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{S}
be the
Hermitian and skew-Hermitian splitting of the matrix

\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{G}+\mathcal{K}
(see
[
9
]
), where
\mathcal{K}
is of a simple

\mathcal{K}
can be associated with the skew-Hermitian portion
\mathcal{S}
of
\mathcal{A}
. Hence, we can choose
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}=\mathcal{G}
and

\mathcal{A}

3.11
, if

\mathcal{G}

.

6
Numerical examples

We consider the system of linear equations

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}u=\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}\\
y_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{bmatrix},

(26)

where
\mathcal{A}
is PSD. Suppose that
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A}}
,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}
and
\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{A}}
are the diagonal, strictly lower triangular and strictly upper triangular matrices of the matrix
\mathcal{A}
, respectively, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{BD}_{\mathcal{A}}=\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
0&D\end{bmatrix},~~\mathcal{BL}_{\mathcal{A}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
C&0\end{bmatrix},~~\mathcal{BU}_{\mathcal{A}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&B\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}.

We now consider the splitting
\mathcal{A}={\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
, with the following choices for the matrix
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
:

(1)

{\mathcal{P}}_{1}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}+\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
.

In this case, if
\Sigma=\alpha\mathcal{I}
, then the PPS method coincides with the triangular and skew-Hermitian splitting (TSS)
[
4
]
. If the matrix
\Sigma
is arbitrary, we call the iteration method as extended TSS (ETSS).

(2)

{\mathcal{P}}_{1}=\mathcal{BD}_{\mathcal{A}}+\mathcal{BL}_{\mathcal{A}}+\mathcal{BU}_{\mathcal{A}}^{*}
.

In this case, if
\Sigma=\alpha\mathcal{I}
, then the PPS method corresponds to the block triangular and skew-Hermitian splitting (BTSS)
[
4
]
. For an arbitrary matrix
\Sigma
, we call the corresponding splitting as extended BTSS (EBTSS).
Note that, if
C=-B^{*}
, then the BTSS method turns into the PSS
[
20
]
iteration method.

(3)

{\mathcal{P}}_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathcal{A}+\mathcal{A}^{*})
.

In this case, if we set
\Sigma=\alpha\mathcal{I}
, then the PPS method reduces to the HSS method
[
5
]
.
If the matrix
\Sigma
is arbitrary, we call the corresponding splitting as extended HSS (EHSS).

(4)

{\mathcal{P}}_{1}=\mathcal{A}
.

When
\Sigma=\alpha\mathcal{I}
, the PPS iteration method concurs with the SS method. For
\Sigma
being arbitrary, we call the corresponding splitting as extended SS (ESS).

Each of the stationary iteration methods generated using the above splittings to solve the system (
1
) induces a preconditioner to the system.

In order to improve the performance of the PPS method, we consider two special cases of the PP splitting of the matrix
\mathcal{A}
as
\mathcal{A}={\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
with the HPD matrix
\Sigma
.

First, suppose that the matrix
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\
C&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
is PSD. Let

{\mathcal{P}}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&D\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathcal{P}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
C&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha Q_{1}&0\\
0&\alpha Q_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Hereafter, the corresponding iteration method is called SPPS1 and the induced preconditioner is denoted by
{\mathcal{P}}_{{\rm{SPPS}}1}
. So

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{{\rm{SPPS}}1}^{-1}

\displaystyle=(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{2})^{-1}\Sigma(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{1})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}A+\alpha Q_{1}&B\\
C&\alpha Q_{2}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha Q_{1}&0\\
0&\alpha Q_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha Q_{1}&0\\
0&D+\alpha Q_{2}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
-\frac{1}{\alpha}Q_{2}^{-1}C&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}S_{1}^{-1}&0\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&-B\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
0&(D+\alpha Q_{2})^{-1}\end{bmatrix},

where
S_{1}=A+\alpha Q_{1}-\frac{1}{\alpha}BQ_{2}^{-1}C
. Therefore, an algorithm for computing
[y_{1};y_{2}]={\mathcal{P}}_{{\rm{SPPS}}1}^{-1}[x_{1},x_{2}]
can be written as the following algorithm.

Algorithm 1

Solving
{\mathcal{P}}_{{\rm{SPPS}}1}[y_{1};y_{2}]=[x_{1},x_{2}]
.

1:

Solve
(D+\alpha Q_{2})v_{2}=x_{2}
;

2:

Set
v_{1}=x_{1}-Bv_{2}
;

3:

Solve
(A+\alpha Q_{1}-B(\alpha Q_{2})^{-1}C)y_{1}=v_{1}
;

4:

Solve
y_{2}=v_{2}-(\alpha Q_{2})^{-1}Cy_{1}
;

For the second choice, suppose that the matrix

\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0&B\\
C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right]
is PSD.
we set

{\mathcal{P}}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathcal{P}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&B\\
C&D\end{bmatrix},~~\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha Q_{1}&0\\
0&\alpha Q_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Henceforward, the corresponding iteration method is called SPPS2. For the associated preconditioner
{\mathcal{P}}_{{\rm{SPPS}}2}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{{\rm{SPPS}}2}^{-1}

\displaystyle=(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{2})^{-1}\Sigma(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{1})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha Q_{1}&B\\
C&D+\alpha Q_{2}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha Q_{1}&0\\
0&\alpha Q_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}A+\alpha Q_{1}&0\\
0&\alpha Q_{2}\end{bmatrix}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}I&-(\alpha Q_{1})^{-1}B\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
0&S_{2}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
-C&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}(A+\alpha Q_{1})^{-1}&0\\
0&I\end{bmatrix},

where
S_{2}=D+\alpha Q_{2}-\frac{1}{\alpha}CQ_{1}^{-1}B
.
Using the above factorization we can state the following algorithm for computing
[y_{1};y_{2}]={\mathcal{P}}_{{\rm{SPPS}}2}^{-1}[x_{1},x_{2}]
.

Algorithm 2

Solving
{\mathcal{P}}_{{\rm{SPPS}}2}[y_{1};y_{2}]=[x_{1},x_{2}]
.

1:

Solve
(A+\alpha Q_{1})v_{1}=x_{1}
;

2:

Set
v_{2}=x_{2}-Cv_{1}
;

3:

Solve
(D+{\alpha}Q_{2}-C(\alpha Q_{1})^{-1}B)y_{2}=v_{2}
;

4:

Solve
y_{1}=v_{1}-(\alpha Q_{1})^{-1}By_{2}
;

Considering the discussion given in Section
4
, we may set

\Sigma=\alpha_{*}Q,~~~\text{where}~\quad\alpha_{*}=\frac{\left\|{{\mathcal{A}}}\right\|_{F}}{2\left\|{Q}\right\|_{F}},\quad\text{and}\quad Q\approx\frac{1}{2}({\mathcal{A}}+{\mathcal{A}}^{*}).

In Algorithms
1
and
2
, the matrices
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
should be HPD and the subsystems with them, as the coefficient matrix, should be solved easily. Therefore, if
A
is PD, then we can choose
Q_{1}
as an approximation of
\frac{1}{2}(A+A^{*})
and if
A
is PSD, then we may take
Q_{1}
as an approximation of
\frac{1}{2}(A+A^{*})+\epsilon I
, where
\epsilon
is a small positive number. In the same manner, the matrix
Q_{2}
is chosen using the matrix
D
.

In Algorithm
1
, if the inverse of
Q_{2}
is easily computed, then the computation of the Schur complement involves simple matrix multiplication and subtraction operations.
This can lead to efficient numerical computations and better performance compared to the HSS, SS, TSS and BTSS methods.
In practice, we solve the systems with the Schur complement matrix by an iterative method. So it is not necessary to form the Schur complement matrix explicitly.
Similarly, the matrix
Q_{1}
is selected in Algorithm
2
.

For example, in the cases that the matrices
A
and
C
are, respectively, PD and PSD, then in Algorithm
1
, we may consider
Q_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(A+A^{*})
and
Q_{2}=\frac{1}{2}{\rm{diag}}(C+C^{*})+\epsilon I
, and in Algorithm
2
, we can consider
Q_{1}=\frac{1}{2}{\rm{diag}}(A+A^{*})
and
Q_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(C+C^{*})+\epsilon I
.

We present three numerical examples to show the effectiveness of our preconditioners. To do so, the numerical results of the preconditioners SPPS1 and SPPS2 are compared with those of the TSS, BTSS, HSS, SS, ETSS, EBTSS, EHSS, ESS, and ILUT (incomplete LU factorization) preconditioners with dropping tolerance
droptol=0.1
. Since the system in Example
6.1
can also be solved using the modified HSS (MHSS) iteration method
[
2
]
, we also report numerical results of the FGMRES method in conjunction with the MHSS preconditioner for this example. Moreover, the parameter
\alpha
is estimated
as
\alpha=\sqrt{\gamma_{\min}\gamma_{\max}}
, where
\gamma_{\min}
and
\gamma_{\max}
are the extreme eigenvalues of
W
(see
[
2
]
). With the aim of revealing the superiority of the proposed preconditioners to the other preconditioners,
the elapsed CPU time and the iterations to achieve a solution of the system with a desired accuracy are compared.

Example 6.1

(
[
2
]
)
.

Consider the system of linear equations of the form

\displaystyle(W+iT)x=b,\qquad W,T\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p},\qquad x,b\in\mathbb{C}^{p},

(27)

where

W=K+\frac{3-\sqrt{3}}{\tau}I,\quad\rm{and}\quad T=K+\frac{3+\sqrt{3}}{\tau}I,

in which
\tau
is the time step-size and
K
is the five-point centered difference matrix approximating the negative Laplacian operator

L=-\Delta
with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions,
on a uniform mesh in the unit square
[0,1]\times[0,1]
with the mesh-size

h=\frac{1}{m+1}
.
The matrix
K\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
possesses the tensor-product form
K=I_{m}\otimes V_{m}+V_{m}\otimes I_{m}
,
with
V_{m}=h^{-2}\text{tridiag}(-1,2,-1)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
. Hence,
K
is an
p\times p
block-tridiagonal matrix, with
p=m^{2}
.

System (
27
) can be rewritten in the real form

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}{u}=\begin{bmatrix}W&-T\\
T&W\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re(x)\\
\Im(x)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\Re(b)\\
\Im(b)\end{bmatrix}=\textbf{b},

(28)

We consider this system for our numerical test. The parameter
\tau
is set to be
\tau=h
and the
j
-th entry of the right-hand side vector
b

is set to be

b_{j}=\frac{(1-i)j}{\tau(j+1)^{2}},\qquad j=1,2,\ldots,p.

In this example, we set
m=64
,
128
,
256
and
512.

Example 6.2
.

We consider the Oseen problem

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} -v\Delta\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{w}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla p=\mathbf{f},&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0,&\text{ in }\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

(29)

with suitable boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
, where
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a bounded domain and
\mathbf{w}
is a given divergence free field. The parameter
v>0
is the viscosity, the vector field
\mathbf{u}
stands for the velocity and
p
represents the pressure. Here
\Delta,\nabla\cdot
and
\nabla
stand for the Laplace operator, the divergence operators and the gradient, respectively. The Oseen problem (
29
) is obtained from the linearization of the steady-state Navier-Stokes equation by the Picard iteration where the vector field
\mathbf{w}
is the approximation of
\mathbf{u}
from the previous Picard iteration. It is known that many discretization schemes for (
29
) will lead to a generalized saddle point problems of the form (
26
). We use the stabilized (the Stokes stabilization)
Q_{1}-P_{0}
finite element method for the leaky lid driven cavity problems on uniform-grids on the unit square, with
v=0.001
. We use the IFISS software package developed by Elman et al.
[
15
]
to generate the linear systems corresponding to
32\times 32,64\times 64,128\times 128
and
256\times 256
grids.
In this example, the right-hand side vector
b
is set to be
b=\mathcal{A}e
, where
e=[1,1,\ldots,1]^{T}
.

Example 6.3
.

In this example, we discuss the image restoration problems
KX=F
, where
K\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is a blurring matrix and
F=KX_{\text{org }}+\Phi
is the observed image. Here,
X_{\text{org }}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
and
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
represent the original image and noise matrix, respectively. Consider solving the least squares problems with regularization

\displaystyle\min_{X}\left\{\|KX-F\|_{F}^{2}+\mu\|GX\|_{F}^{2}\right\},

(30)

to restore the original image, where
\mu>0
and
D
is an auxiliary operator so that it reduces the effect of the noise vector and it is often an approximation to a derivative operator.
It is not difficult to see that the problem (
30
) is equivalent to solve the saddle point problem

\begin{bmatrix}I_{q}\otimes I_{p}&I_{q}\otimes K\\
-I_{q}\otimes K^{*}&\mu^{2}I_{q}\otimes\left(G^{*}G\right)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}\\
y_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\operatorname{vec}(F)\\
0\end{bmatrix},

where
\otimes
is the Kronecker product
\operatorname{symbol}
and
\operatorname{vec}(F)=\left[F_{1};F_{2};\cdots;F_{q}\right]
, in which
F_{i}
is the
i
-th column of
F
. In (
30
), let
K=\left(k_{ij}\right)
be the
p\times p
Toeplitz matrix with the entries
(see
[
12
]
)

k_{ij}=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(\frac{-|i-j|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right),\quad 1\leqslant i,j\leq p,

and
G=\left(g_{ij}\right)
,
1\leq i,j\leq p
where
(see [50, pp 92-93])

g_{ij}=\begin{cases}-2;&\text{if}~~~i=j,\\
1;&\text{if}~~|i-j|=1~~\text{or}~~|i-j|=p-1,\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

It is noted that if
\sigma
is close to 2, then the matrix
K
is a highly ill-conditioned
[
12
]
.

In this example, we set
\sigma=2,~\mu=10^{-6}
and noise
\Phi
is set to be the Gaussian white noise with noise-to-signal ratios of 30dB. In addition,
X_{\text{org }}
is the image
cameraman.tft
of

, which is of

pixel image.

7
Conclusion

In this article, we have introduced the PPS method for solving the system of linear equations with nonsingular PSD coefficient matrices. The PPS method encompasses and extends existing methods such as SS, HSS, NSS, and PSS.
In this approach, we split the coefficient matrix
\mathcal{A}
into
\mathcal{A}={\mathcal{P}}_{1}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
, where both
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
are PSD matrices. Based on this splitting, we have introduced the preconditioner
{\mathcal{P}}_{{\rm{PPS}}}=(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{1})\Sigma^{-1}(\Sigma+{\mathcal{P}}_{2})
, where
\Sigma
is an HPD matrix.
We have studied the convergence properties of this method, providing necessary and sufficient conditions for its convergence. It has been observed that when certain conditions are met, such as the positive definiteness or skew-Hermitian or Hermitian of one of the
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
or
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
, or
\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{1})\cup\text{{null}}({\mathcal{P}}_{2}+{\mathcal{P}}_{2}^{*})=\mathbb{C}^{n}
or
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\Sigma^{-1}{\mathcal{P}}_{2}={\mathcal{P}}_{2}\Sigma^{-1}{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
, then this method is convergent. But in general, we have
\rho(\Gamma_{{\rm{PPS}}})\leq 1
.
Nevertheless, the preconditioner
{\mathcal{P}}_{{\rm{PPS}}}

can be effectively applied for solving linear systems with nonsingular PSD coefficient matrices. We also have demonstrated that the matrix
\Sigma
can any arbitrary HPD matrix, significantly influencing the spectral radius of the iteration matrix and the convergence speed of the preconditioning method. When
\Sigma=\alpha Q
, a specific value for the parameter
\alpha
was proposed. Additionally, we provided guidance on the selection of the matrices
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
. Furthermore, we discussed two special cases of the PPS method, showing that these variants are superior to methods like SS, HSS, TSS, and BTSS in terms of performance.

Finally, it is worth noting that one may generalize the PPS method by selecting two different HPD shift matrices
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
in two half-steps of the method. In this case, if the matrix
{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
is skew-Hermitian and
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
is an HPD matrix, then the method reduces to GPHSS presented in
[
25
]
if we set
\Sigma_{1}=\alpha\mathcal{P}
and
\Sigma_{2}=\beta\mathcal{P}
, and to the
GHSS method given in
[
18
]
if we choose
\Sigma_{1}=\alpha\mathcal{I}
and
\Sigma_{2}=\beta\mathcal{I}
.
However, the convergence analysis of the method in the general form would be complicated and currently is under consideration.
