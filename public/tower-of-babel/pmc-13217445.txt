The Efficacy and Safety of Teplizumab in the Treatment of Stage 3 Type 1 Diabetes: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials
Authors: Qiu Sun, Gang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAfter diagnosis of stage 3 type 1 diabetes, β-cell decline persists, and intensive insulin therapy alone still fails to control metabolic fluctuations and acute risks; teplizumab may delay decline, but trial findings are inconsistent, necessitating a systematic review of efficacy and safety.ObjectiveTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of teplizumab for stage 3 type 1 diabetes from randomized controlled trials and to determine the certainty and applicability of the evidence.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science were searched up to October 31, 2025, including parallel RCTs comparing intravenous teplizumab monotherapy with placebo/standard care. The primary outcome was preservation of MMTT C-peptide AUC at 9–15 months (SMD), with assessment at 18–24 months; secondary outcomes were insulin dose and HbA1c (MD), and partial remission (RR, IDAA1c≤9). Risk of bias was assessed using RoB 2; random-effects meta-analysis was performed, and subgroup, sensitivity, and GRADE assessments were conducted.ResultsFive double-blind, placebo-controlled RCTs were included (975 analyzed), with overall low risk of bias. Teplizumab improved C-peptide AUC preservation at 9–15 months (SMD=0.28, 95% CI 0.09–0.48, P=0.016; exceeded MCID 0.20), and showed only a favorable trend at 18–24 months (SMD=0.27, 95% CI −0.02–0.56). It also reduced insulin dose (MD=−0.11 U/kg/day) and HbA1c (MD=−0.22%), and increased partial remission rate (RR=1.39). For safety, immune-related events increased (lymphopenia RR=3.36, infusion-related reactions RR=2.79), whereas infection (RR=0.98) and diabetic ketoacidosis (RR=0.80) showed no apparent difference, and severe hypoglycemia decreased (RR=0.67). GRADE indicated moderate certainty for short-term outcomes, and low certainty for long-term C-peptide and some acute events.ConclusionTeplizumab was associated with preservation of β-cell function and modest metabolic benefit, with immune-related reactions as the main risk and no increase in serious clinical events observed; long-term benefits remain to be confirmed.

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes is a T-cell-mediated autoimmune disease, with onset mainly in children and adolescents.1 Stage 3 is the clinical onset stage; at diagnosis, patients often still retain a certain level of endogenous insulin secretion, but β-cell function declines markedly over the subsequent months to years, and patients rely on exogenous insulin long term.2,3 Even in the context of intensive insulin therapy and continuous glucose monitoring, severe hypoglycemia, diabetic ketoacidosis, and glycemic variability remain difficult to completely avoid.4 Residual β-cell function is associated with more stable blood glucose and lower insulin requirements, making immunomodulation to delay β-cell decline in the newly diagnosed stage a translatable therapeutic goal; the anti-CD3 monoclonal antibody teplizumab has attracted attention because it directly modulates T-cell responses.5,6 Although teplizumab has been approved in some regions to delay progression from stage 2 to stage 3 type 1 diabetes in high-risk individuals, its role in patients with established stage 3 disease remains under investigation. Evidence for teplizumab in the stage 3 population mainly comes from randomized controlled trials, but differences in regimens and endpoints have led to divergent conclusions.7 Dosing strategies include a single course and repeated courses, and some studies have enrolled patients with longer disease duration;8 efficacy assessments include both C-peptide measures and metabolic outcomes at different time points. The interval from diagnosis to treatment, age, and baseline C-peptide reserve may affect the magnitude of benefit, yet integrated results that quantitatively compare outcomes within unified time windows are lacking.9 In terms of safety, lymphopenia and infusion-related reactions are expected signals, whereas serious infection, severe hypoglycemia, and diabetic ketoacidosis are low-frequency outcomes, making it difficult for individual trials to stably estimate their risk boundaries.10 Clinically, a comprehensive evaluation is still needed that centers on β-cell function, quantifies metabolic benefit and safety simultaneously, and provides an assessment of certainty of evidence and the main sources of uncertainty. Compared with previous syntheses, a focused quantitative evaluation in diagnosed stage 3 disease using unified clinically relevant time windows, integrated safety outcomes, and certainty assessment remains needed. This study conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials of teplizumab for stage 3 type 1 diabetes published up to October 31, 2025, with preservation of MMTT-stimulated C-peptide AUC at 9–15 months as the primary outcome and 18–24 months as the long-term outcome, and pooled insulin dose, HbA1c, partial remission, and key safety events. RoB 2 was used to assess risk of bias; pooling was performed using a random-effects model with Hartung–Knapp adjustment, and prespecified subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, and GRADE rating were completed to define the net benefit and applicability boundaries.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study was a systematic evaluation and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials, with the study population limited to patients with stage 3 (diagnosed) type 1 diabetes. The intervention was intravenous teplizumab (including the earlier name hOKT3γ1[Ala-Ala], regarded as the same drug), and the control was placebo or standard care. The study protocol was prespecified before the search was initiated and followed the methodological standards of PRISMA 2020 and the Cochrane Handbook. The analysis was based on publicly published aggregated data, did not involve individually identifiable information, and ethical review was waived.

Literature Search and Study Selection

PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science were systematically searched from inception to October 31, 2025, with language restricted to English. The search strategy comprised subject terms and free-text terms and was adapted to the syntax of each database: the core terms were “teplizumab” OR “hOKT3γ1(Ala-Ala)” OR “anti-CD3 monoclonal antibody”, crossed with “type 1 diabetes”, and combined with trial-related terms “randomized” OR “placebo” OR “clinical trial”. ClinicalTrials.gov was also searched, and reference list screening and citation tracking were performed. Inclusion criteria were: randomized, parallel-group design; participants with diagnosed type 1 diabetes and stage 3; intervention of intravenous teplizumab monotherapy; control of placebo or standard care; reporting at least one prespecified outcome; follow-up covering either the 9–15-month or 18–24-month window. Exclusion criteria were: non-randomized or crossover design; stage 2 (asymptomatic high-risk or prodromal) populations; combined interventions with other immunotherapies; inability to directly compare with the control; lack of quantitative outcomes. Two investigators independently completed title/abstract screening and full-text rescreening, used EndNote for deduplication, and disagreements were adjudicated by a third investigator. For multiple reports from the same trial, the primary report with the most complete information and the longest follow-up was used as the source of effect estimates, and other reports were used to supplement information to avoid double counting.

Data Extraction and Management

A standardized form was used for independent double data entry by two investigators. Extracted items included basic study information (first author, publication year, study region, number of centers, sample size, age, and disease duration), intervention details (total course, whether a repeated course was used, number of dosing days, and initial dose escalation strategy), type of control, follow-up time points, and outcome reporting methods. Outcome data were extracted according to prespecified time windows: the primary window was 9–15 months (using the time point closest to 12 months), and the long-term window was 18–24 months (using the time point closest to 24 months); if the same study reported both windows, they were included in two separate analyses. For continuous outcomes, change from baseline to follow-up was preferred; if only the final follow-up value was provided, the final value was used. When standard deviations were missing, they were estimated in the following order: calculated from standard error (SD = SE×√n), calculated from the 95% confidence interval (SD = √n×[CI upper limit - CI lower limit]/3.92), or estimated from quartiles (mean ≈ [Q1 + median + Q3]/3; SD ≈ [Q3 - Q1]/1.35). C-peptide units were converted uniformly (1 ng/mL = 0.331 nmol/L). For multi-arm trials with multiple teplizumab doses, arms were first combined into a single intervention arm: continuous outcomes were combined using weighted means and pooled variance formulas, and dichotomous outcomes were combined by summing event counts and sample sizes, ensuring that the same control group was not counted repeatedly. Safety events were extracted according to the observation period defined in the trial report; if the report specified “during treatment to 30 days after the last dose”, data were extracted using this time window; if a trial provided an explicit definition different from the above, the trial definition was followed. After data entry was completed, consistency checks were performed and each item was verified against the original text.

Outcome Measures and Definitions

The primary outcome was preservation of mixed meal tolerance test (MMTT) 0–4-hour stimulated C-peptide area under the curve (AUC), defined as the change from baseline to the follow-up time window;11 if reported as a log-transformed value or geometric mean of AUC, analyses were performed after log transformation and back-transformed for presentation when necessary. Key secondary outcomes included daily insulin dose (U/kg/day) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) (%) as change from baseline or final value in the 9–15-month window. Clinical remission was defined as insulin-dose-adjusted HbA1c (IDAA1c) ≤9;12 when the original report did not allow recalculation of IDAA1c based on HbA1c and insulin dose, the original study definition was used and clarified in sensitivity analyses. Safety outcomes included any adverse event, serious adverse event, infection-related events, lymphopenia, and infusion-related reactions; severe hypoglycemia was defined as events “requiring assistance from others”; diabetic ketoacidosis was defined as clinical events adjudicated by the investigators. All outcomes were extracted according to the intention-to-treat principle.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Risk of bias in randomized controlled trials was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool. Two investigators independently evaluated five domains, including the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result, and made an overall judgment. When insufficient information prevented a clear judgment, it was labeled as “unclear risk”, and any disagreements were adjudicated by a third investigator. Risk-of-bias grading was used for sensitivity analyses and GRADE evidence rating.

Statistical Analysis

All primary and secondary outcomes were tested using two-sided tests, with the significance threshold set at 0.05. Considering the limited number of studies and differences in regimens, effect pooling used a random-effects model, with τ2 estimated by REML and confidence intervals adjusted using Hartung–Knapp. For continuous outcomes, mean difference (MD) was used when units were consistent, and standardized mean difference (SMD) was used when units or statistical metrics were inconsistent; C-peptide AUC was prespecified to use SMD. Dichotomous outcomes used risk ratio (RR) and were pooled using the Mantel–Haenszel method. Trials with single-zero events applied a 0.5 continuity correction; trials with double-zero events were not included in the pooled analysis for that outcome but were described qualitatively. Heterogeneity was quantified using I2 and τ2, with I2 interpretation thresholds set at 25%, 50%, and 75%. For the primary outcome, a 95% prediction interval was also reported to reflect the expected range of between-study differences. Prespecified subgroups included timing of administration (diagnosis to first dose ≤100 days vs >100 days), course regimen (single course vs repeated course), age (<18 years vs ≥18 years), and baseline C-peptide (≥0.2 nmol/L vs <0.2 nmol/L). Subgroup analyses were performed only when a given stratum included at least two studies. Because the number of studies was 5, meta-regression was not performed. Sensitivity analyses included: replacing fixed-effect and random-effects models; excluding studies at high risk of bias; substituting final values vs change values; adjusting the change-score correlation coefficient r from 0.5 to 0.3 and 0.7; and substituting a single dose arm for multi-arm trials. Clinical significance thresholds were prespecified: for C-peptide AUC, SMD 0.20 was used as the minimal clinically important effect threshold based on a standardized metric for type 1 diabetes trial outcome interpretation;11 0.30% for HbA1c; 0.10 U/kg/day for insulin dose; and RR 1.20 as the unfavorable threshold for severe hypoglycemia and diabetic ketoacidosis. Statistical analyses were performed using R 4.3.2 (metafor and meta packages), with verification using RevMan 5.4, and results were presented in ways other than forest plots and funnel plots; given the insufficient number of studies, no quantitative test for publication bias or funnel plot was performed.

Certainty of Evidence Assessment

GRADE rating was performed for the primary and key secondary outcomes, evaluating five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. For imprecision, downgrading or maintaining was determined by combining prespecified clinical significance thresholds with whether the 95% confidence interval crossed the threshold; when rare events led to wide confidence intervals crossing the threshold, imprecision was judged. For publication bias, given the insufficient number of studies, no statistical methods were used; only qualitative judgments were made based on search coverage and cross-checking with registration information. Finally, a “Summary of Findings” table was produced, providing the evidence certainty ratings and effect estimates for the primary and key secondary outcomes.

Results

Study Selection and Characteristics of Included Studies

A total of 717 records were retrieved. After deduplication, 523 records were included for title/abstract screening, of which 457 were excluded. Sixty-six articles underwent full-text assessment; 61 were excluded due to ineligible study design, population, or intervention, or insufficient outcome data, and 5 randomized controlled trials were ultimately included in the quantitative synthesis (Figure 1). Five multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled RCTs were included, with 1006 randomized participants and 975 analyzed. Participants were mainly adolescents and young adults; most initiated treatment within approximately 2 months after diagnosis, and one trial enrolled patients with disease duration of 4–12 months. All studies provided outcomes at 9–15 months; three extended to 18–24 months and reported insulin dose, HbA1c, partial remission, and safety outcomes (Table 1). Methodological Quality Assessment

Overall, the 5 RCTs were predominantly at low risk across domains: the randomization process was low risk in 4 trials and had some concerns in 1 trial; all studies were at low risk in the domains of deviations from intended interventions and measurement of the outcome. Some concerns mainly focused on missing outcome data (3 trials) and selective reporting (2 trials), and no high-risk judgments were made (Figure 2). Efficacy Outcomes (β-Cell Function and Metabolic Benefit)

At 9–15 months of follow-up, the pooled analysis showed an overall effect favoring the teplizumab group, with a pooled SMD of 0.28 (95% CI 0.09–0.48; P=0.016), exceeding the prespecified minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 0.20; heterogeneity was moderate (I2=43.36%) (Figure 3A). At 18–24 months of follow-up, 3 RCTs were included, and the pooled point estimate favored the teplizumab group (SMD=0.27, 95% CI −0.02–0.56; P=0.058), with moderate heterogeneity (I2=39.08%) (Figure 3B). Prespecified subgroup analyses showed that the effects in diagnosis ≤100 days, single course, age <18 years, and baseline C-peptide ≥0.2 nmol/L were consistent with the primary analysis (SMD=0.28–0.32). Within-subgroup heterogeneity was generally moderate (I2 approximately 35.47–51.62%), and some strata were not pooled due to an insufficient number of studies (Figure 3C). At 9–15 months of follow-up, daily insulin dose was lower in the teplizumab group than in the control group (MD=−0.11 U/kg/day, 95% CI −0.17 to −0.05), and HbA1c was also lower (MD=−0.22%, 95% CI −0.33 to −0.10). The partial remission rate increased (RR=1.39, 95% CI 1.08–1.79). Heterogeneity across outcomes was low to moderate (Figure 3D). Safety Outcomes

The pooled safety results showed that, compared with control, the teplizumab group had a slight increase in any adverse event (RR=1.06), a decrease in serious adverse events (RR=0.74), higher rates of lymphopenia (RR=3.36) and infusion-related reactions (RR=2.79), and a decrease in severe hypoglycemia (RR=0.67) (all P<0.05). Robustness and Certainty of Evidence

After excluding any single trial one at a time, the pooled effect for C-peptide AUC remained stable (SMD=0.24–0.35), with consistent direction and no abnormal changes in heterogeneity metrics. The pooled effects under different models and parameter settings were consistent with the primary analysis (SMD=0.25–0.29), and the conclusions did not materially change (Figure 5A and B). According to the GRADE assessment, the certainty of evidence for C-peptide AUC (9–15 months), insulin dose, HbA1c, partial remission, and serious adverse events was moderate; long-term C-peptide AUC was downgraded to low certainty due to imprecision and inconsistency, and severe hypoglycemia and diabetic ketoacidosis were downgraded to low certainty due to imprecision caused by rare events (Table 2). Discussion

Within one year after diagnosis, teplizumab showed a reproducible signal of β-cell function preservation in randomized controlled evidence, accompanied by reduced exogenous insulin requirements, a slight improvement in HbA1c, and an increased proportion of partial remission, suggesting that immunomodulatory intervention produced translatable metabolic benefit. At approximately 2 years of follow-up, the direction remained favorable, but uncertainty increased and evidence certainty was downgraded, indicating that boundaries remain for the durability of efficacy and the applicable population. The primary outcome and key metabolic outcomes were directionally consistent, the overall risk of bias was low, and sensitivity analyses did not change the conclusions, making the observed effects more credible. The anti-cluster of differentiation 3 (CD3) monoclonal antibody binds to the CD3 complex on T cells, reshaping the balance of effector T-cell activation, migration, and apoptosis, inhibiting persistent attacks on islet antigens, and may enhance regulatory T-cell-mediated immune tolerance, thereby slowing β-cell function loss in new-onset disease.13,14 C-peptide preservation is consistent with lower insulin dose requirements, and the increase in partial remission defined by IDAA1c indicates that endogenous secretion can still yield detectable overall benefit in the context of intensive insulin therapy, whereas the small magnitude of HbA1c improvement is consistent with the endpoint compression effect under target-driven management.15 Previous anti-CD3 studies generally used C-peptide as the most sensitive pharmacodynamic readout and suggested that efficacy is more sensitive in earlier disease and may wane over time.16 After integrating trials from different eras and regimens within unified time windows, the pattern of short-term benefit reaching the prespecified clinical significance threshold while long-term evidence tends to be uncertain is consistent with previous observations, supporting teplizumab as an immunomodulatory option for newly diagnosed stage 3 patients.

Prespecified subgroup analyses suggested that efficacy was mainly concentrated in patients who initiated treatment earlier after diagnosis, adolescents, and patients with better residual β-cell function at baseline, with effect directions consistent with the overall results; for patients treated later, adults, or populations with low C-peptide reserve, robust estimates could not be provided due to an insufficient number of studies available for pooling. Overall heterogeneity was moderate, but effect directions remained stable under leave-one-out exclusion and multiple methodological settings, indicating that differences were more attributable to effect magnitude rather than whether benefit was present. Immunologically, early stage 3 type 1 diabetes is still within the partial remission window, with a relatively preserved β-cell mass and inflammatory infiltration and antigen spreading not yet fully fixed, making the inhibition of effector T-cell activation and migration by anti-CD3 more likely to translate into measurable C-peptide preservation;17 the higher the baseline C-peptide, the greater the preservation margin, and metabolic benefits are more readily reflected by insulin dose and IDAA1c.18 Age-related differences may be jointly influenced by immune maturity, disease progression rate, and treatment adherence.19 No clear dose-response gradient was observed between a single course and repeated courses, suggesting that the benefit of repeat dosing is constrained by the timing of redosing, the duration of immune rebalancing, and the intensity of background intensive therapy.20 Previous anti-CD3 and other immunomodulatory trials have generally reported that shorter disease duration and higher baseline C-peptide are associated with better C-peptide preservation, whereas the additional benefit of repeated courses has been inconsistent across studies;21 the current results support patient stratification and timing of administration as key for clinical translation, while positioning repeated courses and efficacy in later-stage populations as priorities for verification in future more homogeneous trials.

The pooled safety evidence showed a response profile consistent with anti-CD3 immunomodulation: any adverse event was slightly higher overall, and the changes mainly aligned with increased infusion-related reactions and lymphopenia; serious adverse events were not increased and were directionally lower, suggesting that additional risks largely remained at the level of manageable mild-to-moderate events. Infection-related events and diabetic ketoacidosis risk were close to control; together with the maintenance of optimized insulin therapy during the trial period, no signals of clinically relevant immunosuppression or metabolic imbalance attributable to immunomodulation were observed. Mechanistically, binding to CD3 induces suppression and redistribution of effector T-cell function, and peripheral blood lymphopenia is mostly a reversible phenomenon;22 early cytokine fluctuations during dosing can explain the occurrence of rash, fever, and infusion-related reactions, and risk control can usually be achieved during the dosing period through complete blood count monitoring and infusion monitoring.23 Severe hypoglycemia showed a decreasing direction, consistent with the biological rationale that glycemic variability is reduced after β-cell function preservation.24 However, limited event numbers reduced the certainty of evidence, and diabetic ketoacidosis also showed a wide uncertainty range due to rare events; clinical interpretation should avoid directly extrapolating directional results to a definite reduction in risk. Previous anti-CD3 randomized studies generally reported transient lymphopenia and infusion-related reactions, while serious infection was not a major signal,25 and this pooled analysis yielded a consistent safety profile across different eras and regimens, further supporting the net benefit judgment: the main trade-off between efficacy benefit and safety risk centers on predictable immune-related events and an acceptable risk level of serious clinical events.

Limitations

Only 5 studies were included, and dosing regimens, follow-up time points, and outcome reporting methods were not completely consistent; although data were extracted using prespecified time windows and a random-effects model was applied, unexplained heterogeneity attributable to regimen differences may still remain. Some prespecified subgroups could not be pooled due to an insufficient number of studies, making the results more applicable to populations who initiated treatment earlier after diagnosis, were mainly adolescents, and still had a certain β-cell reserve, while direct evidence is lacking for extrapolation to those treated >100 days after diagnosis, adults, or those with lower residual function. Long-term outcomes were available from only 3 trials, with wide confidence intervals and prediction intervals suggesting that the true effect may cross the null, leading to downgraded certainty of evidence and limiting the strength of conclusions regarding sustained benefit. In terms of safety, severe hypoglycemia and diabetic ketoacidosis were rare, pooled estimates were unstable and sensitive to continuity correction, and the current evidence is more suitable for excluding an apparent risk increase rather than proving risk reduction. Based on aggregated data, individual-level analyses could not be performed; interactions among age, baseline C-peptide, the interval from diagnosis to treatment, and efficacy could not be tested within a unified model, and dose-response relationships for cumulative dosing and repeated courses could not be assessed. The insufficient number of studies prevented quantitative assessment of publication bias, and the findings still need to be verified with subsequent more homogeneous RCTs or real-world data.

Conclusion

In patients with stage 3 type 1 diabetes, teplizumab can achieve clinically meaningful preservation of β-cell function within one year after diagnosis and translate into reduced insulin requirements, modest improvement in metabolic control, and increased partial remission. The safety profile is mechanism-related and manageable, mainly featuring transient lymphopenia and infusion-related reactions, with no increase in serious clinical events observed. Efficacy is more evident in those with shorter time since diagnosis, adolescents, and higher baseline C-peptide; durability within 2 years shows a favorable trend but remains uncertain. Teplizumab can be an immunomodulatory option for early stage 3 patients, should be used under standardized monitoring, and attention should be paid to timing of administration and population stratification.
