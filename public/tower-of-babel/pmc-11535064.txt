Wind velocity and dispersion/advection–diffusion of artificial droplets and droplet nuclei in a domed all-weather multi-purpose stadium
Authors: Naohide Shinohara, Noboru Kurihara, Wataru Naito, Aya Iwai, Tetsuo Yasutaka, Toshihiro Morioka, Toshiyuki Takatsuji, Masayuki Ogata, Koichi Tatsu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
To evaluate the COVID-19 infection risk and the effectiveness of countermeasures at mass-gathering events, we measured the dispersion and advective diffusion of artificial droplets and artificial droplet nuclei at the Tokyo Dome, Japan (capacity 55,000 people). We also measured and evaluated the effectiveness of wearing masks and increasing the space between seating areas. If people were seated facing forward, artificial droplets did not reach the mouths of surrounding people, suggesting low risk of droplet transmission. For an artificially generated cough or sneeze, the volume of droplets deposited on the hair, back of the neck, and back of the human in front, and the backs of the seats in front, decreased by two to three orders of magnitude when a mask was worn, regardless of the type of mask. However, when the mask was worn with the nose out, the amount deposited on the back of the seat in front was reduced by only 17%. Even in seats with the highest particle concentration in the vicinity of the source, only 0.097%–0.24% of the generated droplet nuclei (1.0–3.0 μm) from the source were inhaled. Our results suggest that the infection risk at the Tokyo Dome via droplet and airborne transmission was low.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic had a major impact on how mass sporting and cultural events were organized. Because mass-gathering events (MGEs) have been considered to be a source of the spread of infectious diseases, major MGEs, including the Union of European Football Associations (UEFA) Euro 2020 football championship, and the Tokyo 2020 Olympics, were cancelled, postponed, or held without spectators to contain the spread of COVID-19. Indeed, the spread of COVID-19 infection through mass-gathering events1,2and an association between the cancellation of mass-gathering events and a decrease in cumulative COVID-19 mortality3have been reported. In Japan, in April 2020, the government restricted events to a maximum of 5,000 people or 50% of the venue capacity, whichever was larger, depending on the infection situation in the area4. Restrictions on the number of people attending events gradually eased, but requirements such as mask-wearing and prohibitions on speaking aloud were in place until May 20235.

SARS-CoV-2, the virus that causes COVID-19, is primarily transmitted through three routes: droplets, droplet nuclei (airborne), and contaminated surfaces6. Recently, airborne transmission and droplet transmission have been reported to be particularly important for SARS-CoV-2 transmission7,8. SARS-CoV-2, like other respiratory viruses, is spread from an infected individual to uninfected individuals via virus-laden droplets emitted while breathing, talking, singing, coughing, and sneezing. As the spectator seating area of MGEs is considered an infection-prone environment, the transport and deposition of droplets and droplet nuclei and the effectiveness of transmission prevention countermeasures are of great interest to event organizers and spectators.

Several studies have attempted to understand the spread of droplets and droplet nuclei in the spectator areas of facilities hosting sporting and cultural events9–11. A scientific research project led by Eindhoven University of Technology has investigated how aerosols, and thus possibly coronavirus, spread among spectators in the Johan Cruijff ArenA, a semi-indoor stadium11; however, the results have not yet been reported. Schade et al10., who experimentally studied the dispersion of aerosols (modal diameter 0.3 μm) in a concert hall, demonstrated that no significant enrichment of aerosols was obtained outside a radius of 1.5 m when the concert hall was operating at an air change rate per hour of 3. Although these studies provide valuable insights into understanding aerosol behavior in spectator areas, the results are location- and environment-specific, making generalization difficult. Moreover, these studies were conducted in an outdoor stadium and a medium-sized concert hall, and there have been no experimental studies on how droplets and droplet nuclei disperse in an actual large indoor stadium.

Districts hosting football games (i.e., MGEs), in particular those hosting matches without strict face mask requirements, experienced a significant relative rise in the incidence rates of COVID-19 after matches12. Although several studies have reported on the effectiveness of wearing masks in reducing infection13–15, none have verified the effectiveness of face masks in facilities where MGEs are held.

COVID-19 infection risk assessments have been conducted mainly by the quantitative microbial risk assessment multi-route model16,17and the Wells–Riley model18,19. The former model has been applied to estimate risk in health care centers20,21, offices22, stadiums23, subways24, and buses25. The latter model has been used to assess infection risks in classrooms26, hospitals27, various other types of facilities28, and buses29. However, most previous assessments have used results from laboratory tests or estimates from simulation models; few have evaluated aerosol behavior in real environments based on actual measurements25.

To investigate the dispersion of droplets and droplet nuclei for assessing the risk of COVID-19 infection in the seating areas of a large-scale indoor stadium, we conducted an experimental study at the Tokyo Dome, one of Japan’s most famous large-scale indoor facilities. Specifically, we conducted a series of experiments in the spectator seating areas of the dome to assess how changes in air-conditioning conditions affect the spread of droplets and droplet nuclei, the extent to which the spread of droplets and droplet nuclei is suppressed by wearing masks, and how the distance between spectators (seat spacing) affects exposure to droplets and droplet nuclei. The scientific evidence obtained in this study will be of great value for evaluating the COVID-19 infection risk and the effectiveness of countermeasures among spectators in the seating areas of large-scale indoor stadiums.

Materials and methods

Location and test dates

The tests were conducted in the seating area of the Tokyo Dome in Tokyo, Japan (Fig. 1A, B). The Tokyo Dome is a domed all-weather multipurpose stadium that was built in 1988 and is used for baseball games, music concerts, and product exhibitions. The dome has a building area of 46,755 m2, a volume of approximately 1,240,000 m3, and a capacity of 55,000 people (approximately 43,500 during baseball games)30. The air-conditioning system was renovated (specifically, the air supply was increased by 1.5 times) by the end of 2020, following the COVID-19 outbreak. Tests on the spread of droplet nuclei were conducted on June 10 and 11, 2021, under the conditions before and after the renovation of the ventilation and air-conditioning system, and on the dispersal of droplets on June 22 and 23, 2021, under the conditions after the renovation of the ventilation and air-conditioning system. These tests were conducted at four locations: the seating areas behind the back net; the lower stand of the first base side in the field; the upper stand of the first base side; and the outfield (right stand) (Fig. 1C). In this study, artificial saliva particles of > 4 μm diameter were measured as artificial droplets because of the lower quantitation limit, and artificial saliva particles of diameters 0.3–0.5, 0.5–1.0, and 1.0–3.0 μm were measured as artificial droplet nuclei to take account of the performance of the generator and the concentration of background particles.Fig. 1(A) Exterior and (B) interior views of the Tokyo Dome and (C) diagram of the test site showing the potential spreading sites of droplets and droplet nuclei. Spread of artificial droplets by coughing

The spread of droplets and droplet nuclei resulting from coughing was tested in the seating areas near the center behind the back net and near the center on the first base side in the lower and upper stands (Supplementary Fig. S1). A total of 18 measurement points were selected: the backs of the head (hair), necks, backs, and mouths of mannequins in three seats located in the row in front of the cough simulator (right front, middle front, and left front); the back of the head (hair) of a mannequin in the seat two rows in front of the cough simulator (middle front); and the mouths of mannequins in the seats to the right and left of the cough simulator and in three seats in the row behind the cough simulator (right behind, middle behind, and left behind) (Fig. 2A). Tests were also conducted to assess the effect of mask wearing: the cough simulator was operated without a mask, with a non-woven mask (Soft flat elastic non-woven mask for children (12.5 cm × 8.5 cm), Daiso Industries Co., Ltd., Japan), and with a urethane mask (Smart fit mask (S size), Federation of Japan Apparel Industrial Association, Japan). In the seating area behind the back net, additional tests were conducted with measurement points in a staggered arrangement, located in every alternate seat around the cough simulator (Fig. 2B). These tests were conducted under the conditions before and after renovation of the ventilation and air-conditioning system. Artificial droplets were ejected forward 20 times at 4-s intervals from a height of 70 cm above the seat surface using an artificial cough generation system (cough simulator NSY-02, Seika Digital Image, Japan; Supplementary Fig. S2A). The artificial cough generation system generates cough droplets by blowing compressed air from a compressor when a solenoid valve is momentarily opened in an oral cavity model fed with a test solution using a two-fluid nozzle. Artificial saliva, consisting of 12 g of NaCl and 76 g of glycerin dissolved in 1 L of ultrapure water, was used as a test solution to generate artificial cough droplets31,32. For coughing, the opening time of the solenoid valve was controlled to 0.1 s, the gas pressure from the compressor to 0.4 MPa, and the pumping rate to 10 mL/h, considering the human cough injection time33. The number-average size and mean velocity of the artificial cough droplets, determined with a 2D-PDI system (PDI-200-MD, Seika Digital Image Co., Japan, Tokyo), were 13.9 µm (range 0.6–81.5 µm) and 7.8 m/s (max. 12 m/s) at a distance of 10 cm from the outlet (Supplementary Table S1).

Water-sensitive test strips (20,301-2N, 76 mm × 52 mm, Syngenta AG, Switzerland) were used to assess droplet deposition. This water-sensitive paper develops a blue color when in contact with water. Each color-developed strip was imaged using a scanner (GT-X980, Seiko Epson Co., Japan) at 2400 dots per inch, and the number and area of the dark blue marks caused by the droplets were measured using image analysis software (Image J, Wayne Rasband, National Institutes of Health). The diameters of droplet particles were calculated from the diameters of the dark blue droplet traces on the scanned images of the test strips according to the method described in Ogata et al32. and Shinohara et al25., using a correlation equation for the diameter of the aerosol with the diameter of the trace. The number and area of the dark blue marks were divided by the number of coughs (20 times) to obtain the number and volume of droplet particles deposited at each location per cough.

Although the manufacturer’s instructions for water-sensitive paper state that droplets of 50 µm can be detected, much smaller particles could be detected in the present study. The smallest particle size that could be counted on the water-sensitive paper was 4.1 µm, which is the diameter of an aerosol particle corresponding to a dark blue mark measuring 112 μm2, one pixel of the smallest unit of imaging. However, the numbers of small particles may have been underestimated because the moisture in some artificial droplet particles may have dried out in the air, meaning that dark blue marks may not have been obtained for those particles.

Spread of artificial droplets by sneezing

The spread of droplets and droplet nuclei caused by sneezing was determined for the seats near the center behind the back net (Supplementary Fig. S1). The number of droplets deposited on the top of the backrest of the seat directly in front of and the seat two rows in front of the cough simulator was measured using water-sensitive paper after the simulated sneezing ejection. Three pieces of water-sensitive paper were placed side by side at the top of the backrest of a seat in front of the cough simulator, and three pieces were placed at the top of the backrest of a seat two rows in front of the cough simulator (Fig. 2C). In addition, the degree to which the droplets were dispersed forward was recorded using a continuous wave laser (CDY-5S, Seika Digital Image Co.) and a high-sensitivity high-speed camera (Phantom T1340, Vision Research Inc., Charlottetown, PE, Canada). Three tests were conducted: with no mask, with a non-woven mask, and with a non-woven mask with the nose of the cough simulator exposed. Each test was performed once.

The NSY-02 cough simulator was used for assessment of sneezing. Sneezes were produced 10 times at 10-s intervals. The solenoid valve opening time was 0.4 s, the gas pressure from the compressor was 0.4 MPa, and the pumping rate was controlled at 40 mL/h (Supplementary Table S1). The number-average size and mean velocity of the artificial sneeze droplets were 11.6 µm (range 0.5–101.2 µm) and 10.3 m/s (max. 24.2 m/s) 10 cm from the outlet, respectively34.

Advection–diffusion of artificial droplet nuclei and CO2

To understand how droplet nuclei from infected persons spread, artificial droplet nuclei emitted from a source were measured at 12 locations around the source: at the seats to either side of the source; at the seats in front of and behind the source; at three seats two rows in front of and two rows behind the source; and at the seats three rows immediately in front of and three rows immediately behind the source (Fig. 2D). These tests were conducted on seats near the center behind the back net, near the center of the lower stand of the first base side of the field, near the center of the upper stand of the first base side of the field, and near the center of the right stand of the outfield (Supplementary Fig. S1). The CO2 emitted together with artificial droplet nuclei from the source was measured at 35 points around the source (Fig. 2D). The emission duration was set to 10 min, and the test was repeated three times. Photographs of the measurements are shown in Supplementary Fig. S1.

To evaluate the spread of droplet nuclei from an infected spectator to the surrounding seats, artificial droplet nuclei and CO2 were emitted at a constant volume flow rate from a single location on a seat at a height of 70 cm above the seat surface. Artificial droplet nuclei were generated with artificial saliva (NaCl [12 g] and glycerin [76 g] in water [1 L]) from two atomizers (ATM 226, Topas GmbH, Dresden, Germany; Supplementary Fig. S2B). The flow rate from each atomizer was set to 5 L/min, to give a total flow of 10 L/min. CO2 was emitted at a flow rate of approximately 27.5 L/min from gas cylinders equipped with a primary pressure regulator with heater (YR-510F-2, Yamatosangyo Co. Ltd., Osaka, Japan), a secondary pressure regulator (CKD W2000-8-w, CKD Co. Ltd., Aichi, Japan), and a bonnet needle valve (SS-3NBS4, Swagelok, Ohio, USA). The airflow rate of CO2 monitored using a mass flow meter (CMC0200, Azbil Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) with a data logger was 27.6 ± 0.14 L/min. Particle-containing air and CO2were combined using conductive tubing and a Y-shaped connecting tube, mixed with a static mixer (T8-15R, Noritake Co., Limited, Aichi, Japan), and discharged straight ahead from a height of 70 cm above the seat surface (assumed to represent the position of a spectator’s mouth) through a 20-mm-diameter cylinder as the discharge outlet at approximately 37.5 L/min. The resulting flow rate (linear air flow rate approximately 2.0 m/s) corresponds to talking and breathing during conversation, as reported in previous studies (1 m/s for breathing and 5 m/s for talking)35.

The emission rates of artificial droplet nuclei with diameters of 0.3–0.5, 0.5–1.0, and 1.0–3.0 μm from the two atomizers were 8.9 × 1011 ± 1.3 × 1011, 2.7 × 1011 ± 6.4 × 1010, and 1.3 × 1010 ± 2.8 × 109 particles/h, respectively. The emission rates and concentration decay coefficients for each particle size were obtained by least-squares fitting to exponential function for the measured values of the concentration increase during particle emission from the atomizer and the subsequent concentration decay in the clean booth. These concentrations in the clean booth were measured using handheld particle counters (Model 3889; Kanomax Japan, Inc., Osaka, Japan) one day prior to the study. The temperature and humidity in the clean booth where the emission rates were measured were 23 °C and 42% RH, respectively, whereas in the stadium during the tests their values were 24 °C and 55% RH, respectively. Although there might be uncertainties regarding particle size increases resulting from absorption with humidity, which may have caused differences in the particle size distribution between the clean booth and the stadium, there would not have been a great enough difference to have had a significant effect on particle size because the temperature and humidity in the clean booth and stadium were similar.

The particle concentrations were continuously measured every 10 s using handheld particle counters (Model 3889) at a height of 0.7 m above the 12 seats in the stadium. The CO2 concentration was monitored using CO2/humidity/temperature data recorders (RTR-575; T&D Co., Nagano, Japan) at a height of 0.7 m above the 35 seats. Due to the small number of particle counters available compared to the number of CO2 recorders, artificial droplet nuclei were monitored at only some of the CO2 monitoring sites.

Background air direction/velocity measurement

Air velocities were measured precisely by using ultrasonic anemometry to clarify the background airflow field in each area of the experimental cough simulation. Four sets of three-dimensional ultrasonic anemometers (WA-790 with TR-90 T probe, SONIC; Supplementary Fig. S3) were positioned at every corner of a seven-by-seven seat area, enclosing the positions where the cough simulations had already been performed or were to be performed later (Fig. 2E). An isolated three-dimensional ultrasonic anemometer (DA-700 with a TR-92 T probe, SONIC) was placed in the center seat behind the home plate for continuous reference observation. The ultrasonic anemometers were previously evaluated by the national standard of low airspeed calibration facility (National Metrology Institute of Japan)36 in the range 0.05–1.5 m/s with an expanded uncertainty of 0.02 m/s.

Local coordinates were defined to assess the wind direction (Fig. 1C). Each ultrasonic anemometer probe has its own three orthogonal axes corresponding to the air velocity components. Each probe was mounted on a tripod at a height of 1.0 m above the stand floor, and the velocity components were defined with downwind speeds as follows: horizontally (left to right) Vx; horizontally (forward to backward) Vy; and vertically (upward to downward) Vz. The local velocity observed at each target position was acquired in 15-min intervals at a data rate of 10 Hz, whereas the reference velocity at the seat behind the home plate was acquired continuously at a data rate of 20 Hz. At every measurement, a zero adjustment was performed by covering each anemometer probe with a plastic bag to block the ambient airflow.

When it was necessary to retake measurements, the datasets of acquired local velocity were corrected by using the difference in the velocity change in reference anemometer indications under common conditions.

To visualize the airflow further, helium-filled soap bubbles were generated using an HFSB generator (LaVision GmbH, Germany), illuminated by a CW laser (CW532-5WM) with laser sheet optics, and the helium bubbles in a laser sheet plane were photographed (resolution 1024 pixels × 1024 pixels) at a frame-rate of 50 Hz using a high-sensitivity high-speed camera (FASTCAM Mini AX100, Photron Ltd., Japan). The visualization system was delivered by Kanomax/LaVision.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 20.0 (IBM Co., Armonk, USA). Multiple comparisons among groups were performed by the Kruskal–Wallis test with Bonferroni correction.

Results

Spread of droplets by coughing

The volumes of artificial droplets deposited on the body surface of (test strip on) the mannequin at each seat, in units of total deposited droplet volume per body surface area of each body part in one cough (μm3/cm2/cough), are illustrated in Fig. 3; data are provided in Supplementary Table S2. Because no marked differences were observed among the seating areas near the center behind the back net, near the center of the lower stand of the first base side in the field, and near the center of the upper stand of the first base side in the field, and between the ventilation and air-conditioning conditions before and after the renovation, the combined results are plotted in Fig. 3. No obvious difference in deposition on the mouths of the mannequins around the source was observed with or without masks (Kruskal–Wallis test P = 1.00): the volume of droplets deposited onto the mouths of mannequins around the source was 0.94–7.2 × 102, 1.2–5.9 × 102, and 0.52–5.4 × 102 μm3/cm2/cough for no mask, a non-woven fabric mask, and a urethane mask, respectively. These levels were equivalent to the blank levels. The blank samples were opened on site and transported and measured in the same way as the test samples (2.0 × 102 ± 1.2 × 102 μm3/cm2/cough [n = 3]). Without masks, droplet deposition on the back of the head, back of the neck, and back was markedly higher only in the seat directly in front of the emission seat (position C-4) relative to droplet deposition on the back of the head, back of the neck, and back at the other seats, with values of 1.1 × 106, 3.1 × 106, and 1.7 × 106 μm3/cm2/cough, respectively (for other sheets data were integrated to each group by body region, Kruskal–Wallis test P < 0.01, P = 0.015 for the back at C-4 and back at other seats; P < 0.01 for the other eight pairs). One test detected a deposition volume of 1.0 × 104 μm3/cm2/cough on the back of the mannequin on the left side in front of the source (position D-4); however, for other positions the deposition volume was only 2.3–7.8 × 102 μm3/cm2/cough on the back, back of the neck, and back of the head in the seats other than those directly in front of the source: these values were equivalent to the blank levels (2.0 × 102 ± 1.2 × 102 μm3/cm2/cough). Spread of droplets by sneezing

The distribution of droplets deposited on the upper back of the seat in front of the source after sneezing is shown in Fig. 5. Although the amount of deposition on the seat in front of the source was reduced by almost three orders of magnitude when a non-woven mask was properly worn (3.0 × 103 μm3/cm2/sneeze), the amount was almost the same when a non-woven mask was worn with the nose out (9.1 × 105 μm3/cm2/sneeze) as when a mask was not worn (1.1 × 106 μm3/cm2/sneeze). Without a mask, deposition on the seat two seats in front of the source (4.7 × 106 μm3/cm2/sneeze) was greater than that on the seat immediately in front. When a non-woven mask was properly worn, the amount of deposition on the backrests of the seats in front of and two rows in front of the source was reduced to 0.28% and 0.051% of the value without a mask, respectively. The results are shown as the ratio of the artificial droplet nuclei concentration at each seat to the concentration generated from the atomizer (hereafter referred to as the exposure concentration ratio), which is synonymous with the ratio of exhaled to inhaled viral load assuming that the breathing volume/rate of the person sitting in each seat is the same as those of the infected person (Supplementary Table S5). In the seat behind the back net, the airflow was homogeneous from back to front over the square measurement area. There was little difference between the ventilation before and after the renovation. This back-to-front air flow was also observed in the visualization of airflow using helium-filled soap bubbles (Supplementary Video 4). On the lower stand of the first base-side infield seat, under the pre-renovation ventilation system the airflow was nearly stagnated at the rear seat, and there was a diagonal headwind from right front at the front seat; in contrast, post-renovation the stagnation had vanished and the airflow had changed to a cross-wind at the front seat and diagonal wind at the rear seat. The air around the upper stand of the first base infield seat stagnated. After renovation, the airflow direction changed from heading to crossing at the rear seats. At the spectator seats of the right field side stand, the diagonal headwind from right front was removed and the airflow became stagnated as a result of renovating the ventilation.

Both before and after renovation, the vertical airflows were almost parallel to the slope of the stadium in side view. On the lower stand, a difference from this tendency was observed after renovation: the airflow had become enhanced, as if it was induced from the stream through the concourse of the stands. Prior to the renovation, the airflow was weakened at the same position. This difference indicates that the airflow might have been separated at the rear seat on the lower stand before renovation.

Discussion

In this study, we tested how droplets and droplet nuclei spread in the spectator seating of a large indoor stadium by obtaining measurements in an actual large stadium. As droplet deposition on the mouths of mannequins around the emission source was rarely observed, the risk of droplet transmission is considered to be low if all spectators watch the game facing forward, even if they vocalize without masks. However, if spectators turn their heads backward to talk or vocalize, the risk of droplet transmission may be greater if they do not wear masks. The risk of contact transmission could be markedly reduced by wearing a mask, as the volume of droplets deposited on the hair, neck, and back of people around the emission source was more than three orders of magnitude smaller when a mask was worn. However, the risk of contact transmission for COVID-19 is considered to be low7,8.

When an infected person sneezes, the risk of contact transmission can be reduced by more than two or three orders of magnitude if the infected person wears a mask. However, if the mask is not worn properly (i.e., with the nose poking out), the risk reduction effect may be much smaller.

Regarding the effectiveness of masks, it has been reported that wearing of a surgical mask by an infected person can reduce by 50%–70% the amount of virus reaching a person 50 cm in front of and facing the infected person after a mild coughing15. When surgical masks were worn in the present study, artificial droplet deposition on the back of the head, back of the neck, and back of the person in front of the source was reduced by two to three orders of magnitude, and the effect was greater than in the results of Ueki et al15. This difference may be because in their study, Ueki et al15. used a nebulizer to generate simulated droplets, so 20% of the particles were less than 3 μm diameter and 80% were 3–8 μm diameter, whereas in the present study more than 50% of the particles produced by the cough simulator measured 10 μm or larger and trapping of particles by the surgical masks was greater and leakage through the gaps was smaller. In addition, when a surgical mask is worn, droplets are trapped by the filter of the mask and the initial velocity of the expiratory jet of coughing is reduced to approximately a quarter of that without the mask37. Therefore, we conclude that the artificial droplets of this study that passed through a mask also lost their initial velocity, and many of them failed to reach the seat 70–77 cm in front of the source.

Our results indicate that wearing a mask with the nose out when sneezing has very little effect, with only a 17% reduction in droplet deposition onto the seat in front. When wearing an actual surgical mask, leakage from the gap between the face and the mask is significant, with 40%–50% of particles escaping by side leakage38. Furthermore, when wearing a surgical mask, leakage at the mask edge is estimated to be 35%–50% around the nose39, and when the mask is worn such that it does not cover the nose, leakage from the gap in the nose area is even greater.

From the results of the test using artificial droplet nuclei, in the seating area around the source, the person even with the greatest exposure in each seating area would inhale 0.097%–0.24% of the emitted droplet nuclei. There are uncertainties regarding the absorption ratio owing to the fact that particle size increases with humidity, which may cause differences in the particle size distribution; in addition, fluctuations in the emission rates of particles may have affected the absorption ratio. However, the temperature and humidity during the test in the stadium were 24 °C and 55%RH, respectively, whereas the temperature and humidity during the test in the clean booth were 23 °C and 42%RH, respectively. Although the humidity in the clean booth was slightly lower, the difference was not large enough to have a significant effect on particle size.

The exposure concentration ratio for the particle of 1.0–3.0 μm diameter was used to calculate the risk. In the Tokyo Dome, the average airborne transmission risk around (up to ca. 2 seats from) the infected person was 2.0%–4.4% in each seating area during 2.5 h of game viewing. At the seat with the highest risk around an infected person, the risk was 14%–31%. Considering that at most only one person in a few thousand is infected and that masks are worn during an infectious epidemic, these values suggest that the risk of aerosol transmission in dome stadiums is low.

Retrospective studies in Italy found no association between entering stadiums during the EURO 2020 competition and catching COVID-1941; in addition, no association was found between attending a live indoor concert in France and the risk of COVID-19 infection42. In these venues, strict infection control measures were taken, such as limits on the number of visitors, physical distancing, proper ventilation, masks, and proof of a negative COVID test. In contrast, gatherings outside stadiums during EURO 2020 have been reported to have contributed to an increase in infections41. The lower infection rates inside stadiums partly resulted from fewer infected people being admitted because of the requirement for a negative test; in addition, the risk of airborne transmission was lower because of dilution, and the risk of droplet and contact transmission was probably lower because masks were used inside the stadium. This finding is similar to the results of the present study.

In this work, we confirmed that artificial droplet nuclei and CO2 emitted from a source spread in the direction of measured wind flow. These results suggest that, during MGEs, it is possible to estimate the risk of infection among spectators by measuring the wind direction/speed at multiple points in the seating area, even without generating and monitoring artificial droplets/droplet nuclei or CO2.

Conclusion

In this study, dispersion and advection–diffusion of droplets and droplet nuclei in a dome stadium were evaluated using artificial droplets and artificial droplet nuclei to assess the risk of COID-19 transmission. Even for coughing by a non-mask-wearing infected person, almost no deposition on the mouths of surrounding persons was observed, and the risk of droplet transmission is considered to be low unless an infected and an uninfected person are talking face to face. For contact transmission, the risk of which is considered to be low for COVID-19 transmission, appropriate wearing of a mask can reduce the risk. However, our results also suggest that the risk is not reduced very much if an infected person wears a mask with the nose out. Our measurements of advection–diffusion of droplet nuclei indicate that the ratio of the exposure concentration at the seat near the infected person to the concentration discharged from the infected person is on average 0.018%–0.039%, and even at the position of highest exposure is only 0.097%–0.24%, suggesting that the risk of airborne infection during game viewing in dome stadiums is low.
