Massive Psoas Muscle Bleeding from the Contralateral Lumbar Artery Obscured by a Lumbar Vertebra Fracture: A Case Report
Authors: Jee Yea Im, Sang Hyun Seo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLumbar artery injury is a rare but potentially life-threatening consequence of blunt trauma. We report the case of an 84-year-old man who presented with right-sided chest and back pain and hypotension following a 3-m fall from a bicycle. Contrast-enhanced CT revealed burst fractures of the L2 and L4 vertebrae, massive contrast extravasation in the left psoas muscle, and a smaller blush in the right psoas muscle, suggesting left lumbar artery injury. Angiography revealed active bleeding from the right L3 lumbar artery, with contrast extravasation extending through the fractured vertebral body into the contralateral psoas muscle. Endovascular embolization achieved complete hemostasis and the patient was subsequently stabilized. This case illustrates a rare diagnostic pitfall, in which vertebral fractures may obscure contralateral lumbar arterial bleeding, emphasizing the importance of careful CT evaluation and angiographic confirmation in patients with trauma.Keywords: Lumbar Artery, Lumbar Fracture, Psoas Muscle, Acute Bleeding, Embolization

INTRODUCTION

Accurate localization of a bleeding source is crucial when managing patients with trauma. Multi-detector CT has substantially improved the detection of vascular injuries. Precise delineation of both the side of origin and the vertebral level of the lumbar artery injury is essential to guide appropriate therapeutic interventions.

We present the case of an older adult patient who sustained lumbar trauma following a bicycle fall. The initial multi-detector CT scan suggested a left lumbar artery injury; however, angiography revealed active contrast extravasation from the right lumbar artery. Cases in which contrast leakage extends across the fractured vertebral body can mimic contralateral involvement, which is a rare diagnostic pitfall.

CASE REPORT

An 84-year-old man with no significant medical history presented to the emergency department after a 3-m fall from a bicycle. Upon arrival, the patient had a scalp laceration and complained of right-sided chest and back pain. The primary survey findings were as follows: a patent airway; tachypnea, 37 breaths/min; oxygen saturation in room air, 84%; heart rate, 107 beats/min; and blood pressure, 96/68 mmHg. He was alert, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15 (E4V5M6).

A contrast-enhanced CT scan performed approximately 15 minutes after arrival at the emergency department revealed right hemopneumothorax, multiple right rib fractures (third to seventh ribs), and multiple pulmonary contusions. In addition, burst fractures were identified in the L2 and L4 vertebrae. Large contrast extravasation was observed in the left psoas muscle, with a focal contrast blush in the right psoas muscle, accompanied with a hematoma extending into the retroperitoneum (Fig. 1A, B).

C, D. Abdominal angiography demonstrates active contrast extravasation from the right L3 lumbar artery (arrows) extending across the fractured vertebral body to the contralateral side.

E. Non-contrast abdominopelvic CT shows a burst fracture of the L4 vertebral body.

F. Contrast-enhanced CT (arterial phase) demonstrates enhancing foci within the fractured vertebral body (arrows) with extravasation into the left psoas muscle.

Approximately 47 minutes after arrival, his blood pressure deteriorated to 60/49 mmHg. Angiography was initiated approximately 55 minutes after arrival. During catheterization, a 5 Fr catheter was incidentally engaged in the right L4 lumbar artery, and diagnostic angiography was performed. Multiple sites of contrast extravasation were observed, correlating with the contrast blush at the right psoas muscle shown on the CT scan. Embolization was performed immediately using a gelatin sponge. Further evaluation of multiple left lumbar artery levels revealed no abnormal findings. Abdominal aortography was performed to identify the source of bleeding, which unexpectedly revealed severe active extravasation from the right L3 lumbar artery (Fig. 1C). The contrast agent extended through the fractured vertebral body into the left psoas muscle, mimicking a contralateral arterial injury (Fig. 1D). The entire angiographic procedure was completed within approximately 25 minutes of initiation. Embolization was successfully performed using a 1:3 mixture of NBCA (Histoacryl; B. Barun, Melsungen, Germany) and iodized oil (Lipiodol; Guerbet, Paris, France), which resulted in complete hemostasis. The patient’s blood pressure stabilized after embolization, and no additional complications were observed.

A retrospective review of arterial-phase CT compared to non-contrast scans confirmed enhancing foci within the fractured vertebral body, continuous with contrast extravasation into the left psoas muscle (Fig. 1E, F). On the initial CT evaluation prior to the procedure, the enhancing foci were overlooked because the hyperdense bone fragments of the lumbar burst fracture closely resembled active bleeding, contributing to the diagnostic challenge. Subsequently, surgical fixation of the vertebral and rib fractures was performed, and the patient was discharged after 1 month without further complications.

This report was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our institution, which waived the requirement for informed consent (IRB No. 2025-10-024).

DISCUSSION

Lumbar artery injuries are unusual causes of bleeding after blunt trauma, and are often complicated by associated lesions (1). These are frequently overlooked or underestimated sources of hemorrhage, resulting in high mortality rates (2). The lumbar arteries usually comprise four paired branches that arise from the posterior aspect of the abdominal aorta, encircle the vertebral bodies, and divide into branches supplying blood to the psoas, quadratus lumborum, and radiculomedullary arteries (3). Transverse processes and/or lumbar vertebral fractures may disrupt these vessels, resulting in retroperitoneal hemorrhage or psoas hematoma (4). As the lumbar arteries arise directly from the aorta, high-pressure bleeding may rapidly cause massive retroperitoneal hematoma (5).

In a large trauma registry cohort, angiographically confirmed that blunt lumbar artery injury occurred in only 16 of 3,436 patients (0.47%), underscoring its rarity (2). The reported mortality rate of traumatic retroperitoneal hemorrhage is 5.3% (6). The combination of diffuse vascular injuries and difficulty in achieving proximal control makes surgical hemostasis challenging and contributes to poor outcomes (7). Furthermore, because lumbar artery injuries occur deep within the retroperitoneal space, surgical access is technically demanding, time-consuming, and difficult to achieve (8). Selective endovascular embolization of the lumbar artery, when feasible, has been shown to be a safe and effective treatment for controlling active bleeding (9) and is thus considered the first-line treatment for traumatic lumbar artery injury. The choice of embolic agent (e.g., gelatin sponge, coils, and liquid materials such as n-butyl cyanoacrylate) depends on angiographic findings. In our patient, the combination of a gelatin sponge and Histoacryl achieved successful hemostasis at multiple bleeding sites (10).

Typically, lumbar artery injuries appear as ipsilateral extravasations adjacent to spinal fractures. However, in this case, contrast-enhanced CT imaging suggested left lumbar artery injury, whereas angiography revealed that the true bleeding source was arising from the right L3 lumbar artery. The extravasated contrast traversed the fractured vertebral body and presented as contralateral psoas muscle hemorrhage. To our knowledge, this is the first report of lumbar arterial bleeding obscured by a vertebral burst fracture, underscoring its significance as a diagnostic pitfall. During the diagnostic phase, correlating contrast-enhanced CT images with non-contrast scans is important to distinguish true extravasation from hyperdense fracture fragments. Moreover, the location of contrast leakage should be carefully evaluated in relation to the expected course of the lumbar arteries, and a thin-section image review may help trace subtle vascular continuity across the fracture site. In burst fractures, hyperdense fracture fragments may mimic contrast extravasation and should be interpreted with caution.

In conclusion, this case underscores the need for accurate assessment of trauma patients who require intervention. When a hemorrhage accompanies a vertebral fracture, careful comparison of contrast-enhanced and non-contrast CT images is essential to detect intrasomatic extravasation and localize the injured vessel. If the initially suspected side is negative on angiography, the contralateral side should also be evaluated. In cases without definite contrast extravasation, performing abdominal aortography may be helpful in identifying the bleeding source.
