The CONSTANS-like gene family in wheat is involved in heading date and drought stress response
Authors: Bentolhoda Farokhpouri, Ghader Mirzaghaderi, Adel Siosemardeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The CONSTANS-like (COL) zinc finger transcription factors are central regulators of the flowering pathway, linking the circadian clock to floral induction. However, their roles in abiotic stress responses remain unclear. Here, we identified 51 COL genes in common wheat, unevenly distributed between chromosomes and grouped into nine phylogenetic clades. Promoter analysis revealed abundant light-responsive cis-elements, followed by elements responsive to abscisic acid. Analysis of independent RNAseq datasets revealed that most members of the TaCOL gene family are expressed in multiple tissues and developmental stages, with preferential expression in leaves. Many TaCOLs including TaCOL-12/16/24/51, and their homoeologs, were drought responsive. Under hydroponic conditions, drought resulted in a reduction in TaCOL-03/12/22/27/33 expression, while expression of TaCOL-16/24/45/51 remained unchanged and TaCOL-08 upregulated. We also examined the physiological traits and expression rates of some TaCOLs in an early-maturing synthetic wheat–derived line (R16P1) and its parental cultivar, Pishgam, under drought stress. Drought significantly increased peroxidase and superoxide dismutase activities while reducing chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoid contents in both genotypes. However, these adverse effects were significantly less pronounced in R16P1. qPCR confirmed the drought-induced upregulation of some TaCOL genes, with higher expression in R16P1 line. These findings provide new insights into COL gene family’s potential roles in wheat adaptation, highlighting candidates for genetic improvement of earliness and resilience to drought stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22973-5.

Introduction

CONSTANS (CO) and CONSTANS-like (COL) proteins are members of zinc finger family of transcription factors, characterized by two zinc finger B-box domains at the N-terminus and a CCT (CO, COL, TIMING OF CAB EXPRESSION 1; TOC1) domain (PF06203) at the C-terminus. The central region contains glutamine-rich sequences1,2. The COL proteins are divided into three subgroups. The group I harbors two B-boxes, one CCT domain, and one VP motif, the group II has one B-box and one CCT domain and the group III has one B-box, one CCT domain and a divergent zinc finger structure3.

The B-box zinc finger domain is a short sequence of about 40 amino acids, is responsible for DNA interaction. The B-box domains are capable of interacting with one another, resulting in various oligomeric configurations, mainly tetramers4. Studies have shown that some B-box proteins in Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana) can bind to AtCO through their B-box domains. This suggests that COL proteins create multiunit complexes that operate not only alone but also through heterologous interactions involving B-box domains5,6. The CCT domain is also around 30 amino acids long and was first found in Arabidopsis proteins CO, COL, and TOC1, thus, this region was named CCT domain7. Generally, proteins with CCT domain regulate processes related to flowering such as circadian rhythms, photoperiodic flowering, and vernalization8. The intermediate uninterrupted glutamine-rich sequences between B-box and CCT domains are involved in the transcriptional activation capacity of COL proteins9.

CO proteins are well known for their pivotal role in regulating plant flowering through the circadian rhythm, a process that with the help of day length, enables plants to detect light through photoreceptors and modulate the periodic expression of specific genes10,11. This results in earlier flowering of long-day plants such as Arabidopsis and temperate cereals when day length exceeds a critical threshold. Furthermore, many plant species require prolonged exposure to cold (vernalization) and inductive photoperiods to initiate inflorescence development12.

The CO gene was first recognized and characterized in Arabidopsis, where the circadian clock determines daytime-dependent alternations of its expression, consequently, the expression peak of CO coincide with the light phase under long day2. Long-day conditions stabilize the CO protein, which in turn upregulates FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT/VRN3; harboring PEBP domain Pfam ID: PF01161) and promotes flowering, while short-day condition degrades CO in the dark and suppresses FT activation2,10,13–16. CO further influences other flowering time genes such as SUPPRESSOR OF OVEREXPRESSION OF CO1 (SOC1), TERMINAL FLOWER 1 (TFL1), and affects the expression of P5CS2, ACS10, and NAKR1, which are involved in proline biosynthesis, ethylene biosynthesis, and long-distance movement of FT, respectively. The tropical cereal Oryza sativa harbours a single CO gene named as Hd117, while Brachypodium distachyon, barley, and wheat have two paralogs, namely CO1 and CO218,19.

PRR37 (The pseudo-response regulator also called PPD1 in wheat and Hd2 in rice) and VRN2 are two important flowering genes that, like CONSTANS proteins contain the CCT domain but lack a B-box domain. In temperate grasses, response to photoperiod is determined further by PRR3720–22. The VRN2 protein represses flowering by preventing the induction of FT1/VRN3 before vernalization23–26. Vernalization induces the MADS-box transcription factor VRN1 both in leaf and in the shoot apical meristem (SAM)25,27. Presence of VRN1 in the leaves suppresses VRN2 in the long days of the spring, which allows the FT1 upregulation. Transport of FT1 to the SAM results in further upregulation of VRN1 and induction of GA accumulation, leading to spike development and stem elongation28. The wheat VRN2 is suppressed during the vernalization or cold treatment23. In wheat, CO1 and PPD1 can respond to photoperiod independently, but both of them are required to fine-tune the flowering timing29.

Members of COL gene family have been identified in various plant species. Among monocotyledonous plants, 16 COL members have been reported in rice (Oryza sativa)19, 19 in maize (Zea mays)30, and 9 members in barley (Hordeum vulgare)19. In Arabidopsis, 17 COL genes are present1, while other dicotyledonous species possess between 10 to 25 members31–35. Identification of COL gene family members has advanced our understanding of their roles not only in regulating flowering but also in response to abiotic stresses tolerance. Many COL members exhibit significant variations in expression levels under various environmental stresses3. A systematic genome-wide analysis of the COL gene family in wheat, and an investigation of its role in drought stress response, is still lacking. With the growing availability of publicly accessible RNAseq datasets from diverse experiments, it is now possible to examine COL genes expression patterns throughout plant growth and development, particularly under flowering-inductive and stress conditions. In this study, we performed a genome-wide analysis of the COL gene family in common wheat (Triticum aestivum) using bioinformatics approaches, and investigated their phylogenetic relationships. was analyzed the expression of the wheat COL genes across multiple tissues and developmental stages and in response to drought using public RNAseq data. Furthermore. we assessed the physiological traits and expression rates of the COL genes in two common wheat genotypes including a synthetic wheat derived early maturing line called ‘R16P1’ and the parental common wheat cultivar ‘Pishgam’. The synthetic wheat line was a true euploid selected from the cross (T. turgidum × Aegilops tauschii ‘299’) × T. aestivum ‘Pishgam’, after four generations of selfing36, and significantly shows earlier heading dates, compared to the parental cultivar ‘Pishgam’.

Materials and methods

Identification CONSTANS-like genes in T. aestivum

CONSTANS proteins from Z. mays30, H. vulgare (XP_044960655.1)15, A. thaliana (NP_197089)1, T. turgidum (CO1 = QIS60155.1, CO2 = QIS60157.1)29, O. sativa (NP_001408467.1) and B. distachyon (KQK18616.1) were downloaded (Supplementary Text S1), aligned using the Clustal Omega online tool37 and used to create a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profile by the HMMER package38. To identify the CONSTANS-like proteins, the HMM was used as a query against the protein sequence file of T. aestivum (IWGSC_refseqv1.1, EnsemblPlants website)39 and each of the above-mentioned species at the 0.001 p-value cut-off. The results were further inspected in the CDD (using the Pfam option) and SMART databases. Characteristics of CONSTANS-like gene family include N-terminal B-box1 and/or B-box2 domains (Pfam accession number: PF00643 or PF22586) and a C-terminal CCT domain (Pfam accession number: PF06203). Commonly, PF00643 type of B-box is present. PF22586 is a B-box domain characterized by a C6H2 zinc-binding consensus motif40. The identified wheat COL genes were named TaCOL, followed by a number and the name of the harboring chromosome (Supplementary Dataset S1). TaCO1 and TaCO2 were identified and named based on the homology with their T. turgidum orthologs29.

Physical mapping

TaCOL genes were represented in their positions within the wheat genome on a circular map using shinyCircos241. The chromosomes were banded based on their general FISH patterns of (GAA)10 and pTa535-1 repetitive sequences. For each target gene, homoeologous members were distinguished through the “homoeologous” link in the gene-based display of the Ensembl Plants summary page.

Phylogeny of CONSTANS proteins

Multiple sequence alignment of all the identified COL proteins were done using Clustal Omega37. The output alignment file was trimmed using the kpi-gappy strategy of the ClipKIT software42. The trimmed alignment file was used to infer a maximum likelihood tree using IQTREE tool (v2.0.7)43 with 1000 bootstraps44. The best substitution model was identified using the ModelFinder option based on the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and the JTT + F + G4 was selected45. We used the ‘ggtree’ package (v3.15.0) in R for the visualization of the output newick tree file46.

Identification of the cis-regulatory elements

Cis-acting regulatory elements in the promoter regions of TaCOL genes were predicted using the PlantCARE database47. The 1500 bp upstream region of the start codon corresponding to the promoter region of the TaCOL genes was extracted using the BioMart tool of Ensembl Plants website.

Identification of the COL protein characteristics

Characteristics of the wheat COL proteins, such as sequence length, molecular weight, and isoelectric point were retrieved from the ProtParam website at the Expasy server48. Conserved domains and motifs of the COL protein sequences were searched from Pfam49 and MEME50 websites, respectively and the resulting files were visualized along with the gene structures in TBtools software (v2.210)51.

COL gene expression analysis using RNAseq data

Expression values belonging to the RNAseq data of 51 wheat COL genes were obtained from www.wheat-expression.com52 in transcripts per million (TPM) for all the available wheat tissues and developmental stages53. A heatmap was generated from log2(TPM + 1) transformed values of COL genes over developmental stages using the R package ‘pheatmap’. To analyses the expression of the wheat COL genes under drought stress, we downloaded RNAseq data sets from the SRA database of NCBI for response to heading date and drought stress conditions including SRP287185 Heading date54, SRP045409 heat and drought55, and PRJNA630059 drought and cold56. The quality of the reads was checked using FastQC (v0.12.1), and Trimmomatic (v0.39)57 was used to remove the Illumina adapters and low-quality reads with a Phred score below 20. We made a decoy-aware index by Salmon software (v1.10.1)58 using the wheat reference transcriptome and the genome (masked type) files (IWGSC_RefSeq v1.1) to improve specificity. The reads from each sample were mapped to the reference transcriptome using Salmon with default settings, and the expression level of genes was quantified and extracted based on the TPM value.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks

The protein sequences belonging to the differentially expressed TaCOL genes in RNAseq results59, were used as the query in the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) for predicting protein–protein interactions (default confidence score ≥ 0.40) using the Arabidopsis orthologs with default minimum required interaction score (0.4).

Plant material and experimental conditions

An experiment was set up in a greenhouse under a 16 h light and 8 h dark photoperiod at 23–17 °C day/night temperature to investigate the effect of genotype and drought on the physiological traits and expression rates of TaCOL genes in two wheat genotypes including a synthetic wheat derived early maturing line called ‘R16P1’ and the parental common wheat cultivar ‘Pishgam’. The early maturing synthetic wheat line was selected from the cross (T. turgidum × Aegilops tauschii ‘299’) × T. aestivum ‘Pishgam’, after four generations of selfing36. Selection criteria were true euploidy, early maturity, soft glume, higher plant yield and larger seed size. The selected line demonstrated significant earlier heading dates, compared to the parental cultivar ‘Pishgam’ (unpublished data). We used a factorial experiment based on a completely randomized design with three replicates in which the watering conditions, including control (80–85% of field capacity) and moderate drought (45–50% of field capacity)60, and genotypes (L16P1 line and Pishgam cultivar) were considered as factors. Before planting, the germinated seeds in Petri dishes were vernalized in 4 °C for 4 weeks. Five uniform and healthy seedlings were planted in each pot (25 cm in height and 20 cm in diameter filled with garden soil) in a greenhouse. Drought treatment was applied after 21 days at four-leaf stage. To monitor gene expression trends, sampling of the last leaves was started in four-, five-, six-, and seven-leaf stages (4LS, 5LS, 6LS, and 7LS) during the last hour of light time (three biological replicates) and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until use. To determine physiological traits flag leaves were sampled at the early dough stage (Zadoks Z83)61.

Measurement of antioxidant Enzyme activities

Protein concentration in the extracts was determined spectrophotometrically using the Bradford method62, by measuring the absorbance ratio at 590 nm to 450 nm, with bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard. For enzyme extraction, 500 mg of powdered leaf tissue was homogenized under ice-cold conditions in an extraction buffer containing 1% (w/v) polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and 0.5% (v/v) Triton X-100 in 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). The homogenate was centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 20 minutes at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was used for antioxidant enzyme assays. Peroxidase (POD, EC 1.11.1.7) activity was assayed following guaiacol oxidation by adding 50 μL of enzyme extract to 2950 μL of 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5) containing 9 mM guaiacol and 10 mM H₂O₂. The increase in absorbance at 470 nm over 2 min, due to tetraguaiacol formation, was recorded, and activity was calculated using an extinction coefficient of 26.61 mM⁻1 cm⁻1. Results were expressed as ΔAbs min⁻1 mg⁻1 protein63. For superoxide dismutase (SOD; EC 1.15.1.1) activity assay, the reaction mixture (3 mL final volume) contained 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer, 13 mM methionine, 75 μM nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT), 2 μM riboflavin, 0.1 mM EDTA, and 100 μL enzyme extract64. The tubes were illuminated with fluorescent light (approximately 4000 lx) for 10 min to initiate the photochemical reduction of NBT, while identical tubes kept in the dark served as blanks. Absorbance was measured at 560 nm, and one unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to cause 50% inhibition of NBT photoreduction under the assay conditions. SOD activity was expressed as units per milligram of protein (U mg⁻1 protein), where protein content was determined by the Bradford method.

Measurement of leaf chlorophyll and carotenoid contents

Leaf chlorophyll and carotenoid contents were quantified spectrophotometrically65. Briefly, 0.10 g of fresh leaf tissue was ground into a fine powder and homogenized in 10 mL of 80% (v/v) acetone under dark conditions to prevent pigment degradation. The homogenate was centrifuged at approximately 5000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4 °C using a refrigerated centrifuge (Eppendorf, Germany). The absorbance of the clear supernatant was recorded at 663, 646, and 470 nm using a spectrophotometer (U-1800, UV–VIS, Hitachi, Japan), with 80% acetone as the blank. Pigment concentrations were calculated using the following equations and expressed as milligrams per gram fresh weight (mg g⁻1 FW) where Weight refers to the fresh sample weight in grams:

Chl a = [12.21 (ABS663)—2.81 (ABS646)] × [(10 ml Acetone)/1000].

Chl b = [20.13 (ABS646)—5.03 (ABS663)] × [(10 ml Acetone)/1000].

CAR = [[[1000 (ABS470)—3.27 Chl a – 104Chl b]/227]/Weight (g)] × [(10 ml Acetone)/1000].

Total chlorophyll = Chl a + Chl b.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

We used the acid guanidinium thiocyanate–phenol–chloroform method66 for the extraction of the total RNA. The isolated RNA samples were run in an agarose gel, and their quality and quantity were analyzed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. About 750 ng of RNA from each sample was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a FanAvar kit (Tehran, Iran). The cDNA products were diluted tenfold and kept frozen at −20°C until needed. Based on the analysis of public RNAseq data (described above), ten wheat COL genes were randomly chosen (not necessarily differentially expressed genes) and their expression was analyzed by reverse transcription quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) in three biological replicates. Primers were designed using Primer3Plus tool so that each primer pair could amplify all the homoeologous transcripts from each homoeologous gene group. The common-wheat Actin (AB181991.1)67 was used as an internal reference gene. qPCR was performed on an ABI 7500 (Applied Biosystems) real-time PCR system using SYBR Green with ROX 2X mix. Each reaction mixture was 10 μl containing 5 μl 2X SYBR master mix, 1 μl of the template cDNA reaction mixture, 0.5 μl of each primer (200 μM), and 0.5 μl ROX Reference Dye. The PCR protocol was set to pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 60 s, denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 58 °C for 30 s, amplification at 72 °C for 30 s, 35 cycles in total, and a 55‒95 °C melt curve analysis. All reactions were performed in duplicate. The threshold cycles (Ct) were automatically calculated for each reaction.

Statistical analysis

Bar plots were produced from the mean of expression values of TaCOL genes derived from the RNAseq data and presented along with standard errors using ‘ggplot2’ package in R v4.3.3 (www.R-project.org). Data from each physiological trait were analyzed based on the completely randomized design (CRD) procedure of SAS 9.468. The assumptions of the analysis of variance (ANOVA) were met after checking residuals for normality and homogeneity of variances. The least significant difference (LSD) value was calculated at the 5% probability level for mean comparisons. qPCR data were analyzed using ‘rtpcr’ package (v2.0.3)69 in R, based on the Pfaffl70 method for the analysis of the relative expression ratio between the normal and drought stress conditions or between R16P1 and Pishgam genotypes.

Results

General properties of the wheat CONSTANS genes and proteins

Using the HMM profile from Arabidopsis, barley, maize, rice, and B. distachyon COL proteins (Supplementary Text S1) together with BLASTP searches, we identified 51 COL genes in the common wheat genome. Genes containing both B-box and CCT domains were retained and designated TaCOL-01 to TaCOL-51, followed by the name of the harboring chromosome. Gene IDs, physical location and protein sequences are provided in Supplementary Dataset S1. TaCOL genes were distributed across 15 chromosomes and the unassembled part of the genome, with higher densities on homoeologous groups 6 and 7, and no representatives in groups 1 and 3. Regarding physicochemical properties, the length of TaCOL proteins ranged from 243 (TaCOL-40-7B) to 490 (TaCOL-38-7A) amino acids. Phylogenetic analysis of the CONSTANS proteins

A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was made from 117 COL protein sequences belonging to T. aestivum, O. sativa, B. distachyon, H. vulgare, Z. mays and A. thaliana (Fig. 2). The protein sequences and their gene IDs can be found in Supplementary Dataset S1. The tree classified the COL proteins into 9 groups with unequal numbers of COLs per group. Most clades exhibited high bootstrap support, and in most cases, grass orthologs from different species clustered together within a single clade. Expression profiles from Ramírez-González et al. (2018) showed that most TaCOL genes were expressed in multiple tissues and stages. Most TaCOL genes are differentially expressed during developmental stages, with the highest levels in leaves, leaf sheaths, leaf blades, and flag leaves. Homoeologous groups displayed largely uniform expression patterns. Cis-regulatory elements

Promoter analysis (1.5 kb upstream) identified 1,130 cis-acting elements across 51 TaCOL genes (Supplementary Figure S5), dominated by light-responsive motifs (4 to 18 elements per gene), followed by abscisic acid (1 to 10 elements per gene). Fewer elements were associated with drought (0 to 2 per gene) or low temperature (0 to 3 per gene).

Discussion

CONSTANS and CONSTANS-like (generally referred here to as COL) proteins belong to the family of zinc finger transcription factors, which contain two zinc finger B-box domains at the N-terminal and a CCT domain at the C-terminal. The central region of COLs contain glutamine-rich sequences1,2. COL proteins are known for their involvement in the flowering pathway under day length, mediating the link between the circadian clock and flowering control10–12. However, the specific biological and functional roles of COL gene remain unclear. Here, we conducted a comprehensive identification of COL genes across the common wheat genome and identified 51 COL genes in the common wheat genome. We didn’t list the VRN2 homoeologs (TraesCS5A02G541300.1, TraesCS4B02G372700.1, TraesCS4D02G364500.1) as CONSTANS-like protein, due to the lack of B-box domain, although they include the CCT domain. VRN2 probably translocated from 4 to 5 A in hexaploid wheat71. In addition to B-box and CCT core domains, we identified eight other domains including Atrophin-1 and TIM in TaCOL-41-Un (involving in signal transduction and metabolism), Androgen_receptor (hormone receptors constitute an important super-family of transcription regulators that are involved in diverse physiological functions, including control of embryonic development, cell differentiation and homeostasis) in TaCOL-45-7A and TaCOL-46-7B, Peptidase_C25_C (a hydrolase enzymes that degrade proteins) in WCO2-6D, PHD_SF (associated with chromatin-mediated transcriptional regulation) in TaCOL-12-7B, TaCOL-13-7D, TaCOL-14-7A, GATA-N in TaCOL-14-7A, AAT_I (a putative pyridoxal 5’-phosphate-dependent methionine gamma-lyase enzyme involved in methionine catabolism) in TaCOL-23-6A and AP3B1_C (a short low-complexity, highly serine-rich region lies on clathrin-adaptor complex 3 beta-1 subunit proteins) in TaCOL-37-7A were also detected (Fig. 3).

TaCOL genes distributed unequally between the chromosomes. Such an uneven distribution of the members in a gene family is common in wheat72,73. Close affinity of TaCOL-18/19/20, WCO2-6A/6B/6D and TaCOL-33/34/35 blocks at the homoeologous group 6 and their reverse syntenic order with TaCOL-44/45/46, WCO1-6A/6B/6D and TaCOL-07/08 segments at the homoeologous group 7 (based on gene structure and protein similarity) suggest that gene duplication events might be involved in the evolution of these genes74,75. Higgins, et al.18 stated that a segmental duplication has generated CO1 and CO2 paralogs at the base of the grass evolution, with CO1 located in the ancestral position syntenic with Hd1 of rice, while CO2 was deleted in rice and is perhaps not functional in sorghum.

Based on the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2), each of the TaCOL homoeologous proteins aligned together in the same clade along with their orthologs from related species including barley, maize, rice and B. distachyon. In most cases, the clades exhibited a high bootstrap value, and in the majority of instances, grass orthologs from different species were grouped in a single clade, indicates a reasonable sequence alignment.

Expression data analysis showed that, although the wheat COL genes are differentially expressed in different tissues and developmental stages, they are expressed in leaves at higher rates. Such a preferential expression of COL genes in leaves has been already reported76. TaCOL genes are not or weakly expressed in root, shoot, and root apical meristems and flowering organs such as spike, anther, stigma ovary, and grain. Similarly, there were no COL gene representing a high expression level in root, stem, embryo, seed, and endosperm in maize77.

COL genes play important roles in the regulation of flowering time2,10,13,15,19,30, but little is known about their role in abiotic stresses. Here, we showed that most of the evaluated TaCOL genes including TaCOL-12-7B, TaCOL-15-2D, TaCOL-28-5B, TaCOL-29-4B and TaCOL-49-4A were upregulated by drought in both R16P1 line and Pishgam wheat cultivar especially in the early maturing line R16P1 (Fig. 8). Various studies have reported significant up- or down-regulation of COL genes in response to abscisic acid and abiotic stresses in plants3,71,77–80. Qin, et al.79 observed that COL genes from six homoeologous groups (12 genes in total) are up-regulated under drought stress in cotton. In rice, Ghd2 (Os02t0731700) was significantly down-regulated under drought stress71, which corresponded to TaCOL-33/34/35 in wheat. In Arabidopsis, AtCOL4 (orthologous to the wheat WCO2 homoeologs) was strongly up-regulated under salt and osmotic stress, and the Atcol4 mutant showed increased sensitivity to abscisic acid and salt stress78. From the types and frequencies of cis-acting elements (Supplementary Figure S5), it is obvious that the TaCOL genes have a reasonable number of cis elements for light and abscisic acid, but the drought and low-temperature responsive elements have very low frequencies. Considering that the corresponding COL genes are highly responsive to drought and low temperature (discussed above), one could propose that COL genes might perform their function in abiotic stress response through the abscisic acid-dependent signaling pathway.

To confirm the effects of drought treatment, physiological traits were evaluated, including POD and SOD activities, as well as Chl a, Chl b, Total Chl, and CAR contents. Significant differences were observed between the control and drought, as well as between genotypes (Table 2), generally indicating the superior performance of the synthetic wheat–derived line compared with its parental cultivar, Pishgam. qPCR showed that TaCOL-12/13/14, TaCOL-15/16/17, TaCOL-24/25/29 and TaCOL-49/50/51 are upregulated under drought, a pattern that is in line with the results of the RNAseq data. The results further showed that TaCOL-15/16/17 and TaCOL-49/50/51 have higher expression rates in the early maturing line R16P1 under drought stress. Based on the SRP287185 RNAseq data, TaCOL-12/16/15/22/27/28/49/51 (Fig. 5) and the homoeologs (Supplementary Figure S1) seem to be differentially expressed in leaves of early and late heading date wheat lines and overexpressed in the early heading date line. In agreement with the involvement of the COL genes in heading date, Jiang, et al.81 showed that 4 COL genes are significantly up-regulated in the early-flowering foxtail millet compared to the control cultivar. TaCOL genes might also be involved in flowering time, however, we didn’t evaluate the effect of light on the TaCOL expression.

We observed elevation in the expression of the above mentioned TaCOL genes in R16P1, the genotype that also demonstrated superior physiological resilience under drought stress. This suggests that the upregulation of these COL genes may not only be linked to flowering regulation but could also contribute to stress tolerance by modulating downstream pathways associated with photosynthetic stability and antioxidant defense. For instance, higher TaCOL expression might enhance regulatory networks that sustain chlorophyll biosynthesis or reduce degradation, thereby supporting more stable pigment pools under stress. Similarly, COL-driven transcriptional cascades could indirectly prime antioxidant systems, facilitating higher POD and SOD activities to mitigate oxidative damage. Alternatively, the observed higher expression could be a secondary consequence of R16P1’s improved physiological state, reflecting feedback from preserved chloroplast function and stronger redox homeostasis. Distinguishing whether TaCOL upregulation is a driver or merely a marker of enhanced drought tolerance will require functional validation, such as overexpression or knockout studies3,79,82.

Our analysis using the public RNAseq data showed that most of the COL genes are highly responsive to drought and heading date in common wheat. Some TaCOL genes were differentially expressed in PEG-induced vs. soil-based drought experiments. This is not surprising because soil drying triggers both water‐deficit signals and secondary effects such as changes in soil oxygen, nutrient availability, and rhizosphere signaling, leading to a complex response involving drought, nutrient, and hormonal pathways. In contrast, PEG creates an immediate and uniform osmotic potential without altering nutrient composition or aeration, eliciting a more specific osmotic stress-driven transcriptional profile with fewer confounding signals. Consequently, while both methods activate core drought‐responsive genes, gene expression patterns differ due to the additional environmental cues present in soil‐drying but absent in PEG treatments83.

The data further showed very low transcript expression for the wheat WCO1 and WCO2 relative to the other TaCOL genes (Fig. 5, 6, 7, and 9). However, this gene has an important role in wheat flowering. By gene expression analyses in the newly expanded fourth leaf of relevant mutants, Shaw et al., concluded that CO1 and CO2 are weak repressors of heading time in both long and short days. By contrast, the ppd1 mutant delays heading more than the combined co1 and co2 mutations. In mutants with loss-of-function mutations in both PPD1 homeologs, heading time is accelerated by the wild-type CO1 allele compared to the co1 mutant allele under long day29. Overexpression of CO1 and CO2 in spring barley promoted flowering under both long-day and short-day conditions by upregulating FT1. Conclusion

Overall, we identified and characterized 51 CONSTANS-like (COL) genes in common wheat. Our analysis revealed that a major part of TaCOLs is highly responsive to heading date and drought stress. The results provided here could potentially lead in a better understanding of the role and behavior of the wheat COL gene family and could provide basic information toward future experimental work. Considering the availability of the high-quality wheat genome sequence and the possibility of mutant creation, our study identifies TaCOL-15/16/17 and TaCOL-49/50/51 as high-priority candidates for future functional validation due to their consistent drought responsiveness and association with earliness.
