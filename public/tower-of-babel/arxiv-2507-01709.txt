Entropic optimal transport beyond product reference couplings: the Gaussian case on Euclidean space
Paul Freulon, Nikitas Georgakis, Victor Panaretos

Abstract
The optimal transport problem with squared Euclidean cost consists in finding a coupling between two input measures that maximizes correlation. Consequently, the optimal coupling is often singular with respect to Lebesgue measure. Regularizing the optimal transport problem with an entropy term yields an approximation called entropic optimal transport. Entropic penalties steer the induced coupling toward a reference measure with desired properties. For instance, when seeking a diffuse coupling, the most popular reference measures are the Lebesgue measure and the product of the two input measures. In this work, we study the case where the reference coupling is not necessarily assumed to be a product. We focus on the Gaussian case as a motivating paradigm, and provide a reduction of this more general optimal transport criterion to a matrix optimization problem. This reduction enables us to provide a complete description of the solution, both in terms of the primal variable and the dual variables. We argue that flexibility in terms of the reference measure can be important in statistical contexts, for instance when one has prior information, when there is uncertainty regarding the measures to be coupled, or to reduce bias when the entropic problem is used to estimate the un-regularized transport problem. In particular, we show in numerical examples that choosing a suitable reference plan allows to reduce the bias caused by the entropic penalty.



Keywords:
Optimal transport; multivariate Gaussian measures; entropic regularization; covariance matrix; reference coupling; bias reduction.

Introduction

Background.

Optimal transport allows to compare two probability measures by searching the most efficient way to rearrange the mass of the first measure to recover the second one. Assessing efficiency relies on a bivariate function called the ground cost function. If this ground cost function is a distance or the power of a distance, the optimal transport problem defines a distance on a subset of probability measures. This distance has found many applications in mathematics
[
28
,
1
]
; for instance in the study of geometric inequalities or partial differential equations, among many other topics. In this work we have particular interest toward problems of a statistical nature
[
23
,
8
]
. In this field, the optimal transport distance allows to compare probability measures with non overlapping support. Also, in a statistical problems, one might be interested by actually computing the optimal transport distance through a numerical scheme. In this case, we have to solve a linear programming problem, which can become costly in realistic settings, for example in machine learning contexts. This practical difficulty has motivated the introduction of entropic optimal transport in
[
11
]
. Beyond its considerable impact in computational optimal transport, this regularized version of the problem is of intrinsic interest and thus is being studied for its own sake
[
22
]
, for statistical interest
[
9
,
26
]
, and for it connects to Schrödinger bridge problems
[
16
]
. In this work, we pursue the study of entropic optimal transport by studying one of the few cases where this problem has a closed-form: when the measures are Gaussian on the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this scenario, the optimal transport problem is completely solved since
[
13
]
, and then extended to a separable Hilbert space in
[
10
]
. The optimal transport distance when restricted to centered Gaussian measures is called the Bures-Wasserstein distance, with roots in quantum information theory
[
21
,
4
]
, and the geometric properties of this metric space is an active domain of research
[
27
,
6
]
. Consequently, the Gaussian case is of interest in its own right, beyond its role as a central test case. Accordingly, entropic optimal transport on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has been an object of study in the Gaussian case specifically, particularly when the reference measure is taken to be the product of the two marginals
[
15
,
18
]
(with an extension to Hilbert spaces by
[
20
]
). In this paper we follow this line of work, but we study the impact of choosing a reference coupling which is not necessarily a product measure (whether of the two marginals or otherwise). To motivate why this is worthwhile, we first need to intrioduce some basic notions and notation related to optimal transport and its entropic version.

Entropic optimal transport

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be two probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
\Pi(\mu,\nu):=\{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})~|~\proj_{1}\#\pi=\mu~\text{and}\proj_{2}\#\pi=\nu\}
be the set of all measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
that have
\mu
and
\nu
as first and second marginal respectively. We refer to elements of
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
as transport plans, or couplings between
\mu
and
\nu
. On the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the squared optimal transport problem between
\mu
and
\nu
is

W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu):=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-y\|^{2}d\pi(x,y).

(1.1)

The square root of (
1.1
) is a specific instance of Wasserstein distances that corresponds to the ground cost function
c(x,y)=\|x-y\|^{2}
. Another criterion to compare probability measures is the Kullback-Leibler divergence. This divergence, also called relative entropy, is defined for two measures
\pi
and
\pi_{\refe}
by the formula

\KL(\pi|\pi_{\refe}):=\int\log\left(\frac{d\pi}{d\pi_{\refe}}\right)d\pi,

(1.2)

where
d\pi/d\pi_{\refe}
denotes the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\pi
with respect to
\pi_{\refe}
, if
\pi
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi_{\refe}
. If
\pi
is not absolutely continuous with respect to
\pi_{\refe}
,
\KL(\pi|\pi_{\refe}):=+\infty
. In entropic optimal transport, the Kullback-Leibler divergence (
1.2
) is exploited as a regularizing term for the optimal transport problem (
1.1
). Thus, entropic optimal transport refers to problems of the form

W_{\pi_{\refe}}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu):=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-y\|^{2}d\pi+2\varepsilon\KL(\pi|\pi_{\refe});

(1.3)

where
\varepsilon\geq 0
is a regularization parameter, and
\pi_{\refe}
is a measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
that we call the reference coupling.
To the best of our knowlege, there are two reference couplings have been investigated (indeed, the two are related). First, the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this case, the Kullback-Leibler divergence equals the negative entropy that we denote by
\Hneg
, and defined by
\Hneg(\pi):=\int\log(d\pi(x)/dx)d\pi(x)
if
\pi
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure; and
\Hneg(\pi)=+\infty
otherwise. Notice that, strictly speaking, the Lebesgue measure is not a coupling of
\mu
and
\nu
, but we abuse terminology occasionally and allow “coupling” to signify a measure on the product space. The other popular coupling is the independence coupling of the input measures. This corresponding to choosing
\pi_{\refe}=\mu\otimes\nu
, which yields the regularizing term
\KL(\pi|\mu\otimes\nu)
. These two reference couplings, that we also call reference transport plans, define the two optimization problems

\begin{split}W_{\Hneg}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)&:=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\|x-y\|^{2}d\pi(x,y)+2\varepsilon\Hneg(\pi)\quad\text{and,}\\
W_{\otimes}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)&:=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\|x-y\|^{2}d\pi(x,y)+2\varepsilon\KL(\pi|\mu\otimes\nu).\end{split}

(1.4)

Remark 1.1
.

For
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
, if
\mu
and
\nu
are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, we have the relation

\KL(\pi|\mu\otimes\nu)=\Hneg(\pi)-(\Hneg(\mu)+\Hneg(\nu)).

From this observation, it follows that
W_{\Hneg}^{\varepsilon}
and
W_{\otimes}^{\varepsilon}
relate through the equality

W_{\otimes}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)=W_{\Hneg}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)-2\varepsilon(\Hneg(\mu)+\Hneg(\nu));

(1.5)

and both problems have the same solution
\pi^{\varepsilon}.

This last remark originally led to the tentative conclusion that these two entropic optimal transport problems are equivalent (see e.g.
[
19
, Lem. 1.5]
or
[
25
, Prop. 4.2]
). However, this may be a nuanced statement, depending on the context: for example, in statistical inference problems, one wishes to fit a model (i.e. a collection of probability measures) to empirical observations. Concretely,

let
\hat{\nu}_{n}
be an “empirical probability measure” over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\{\mu_{\theta}~|~\theta\in\Theta\}
be a statistical model with parameter space
\Theta
. Then, as shown by the relation

\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}W_{\otimes}^{\varepsilon}(\mu_{\theta},\hat{\nu}_{n})=\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\left\{W_{\Hneg}^{\varepsilon}(\mu_{\theta},\hat{\nu}_{n})-2\varepsilon\Hneg(\mu_{\theta})\right\},

assessing model fit with
W_{\otimes}^{\varepsilon}
or
W_{\Hneg}^{\varepsilon}
leads to different solutions. More precisely, when relying on
W_{\otimes}^{\varepsilon}
instead
W_{\Hneg}^{\varepsilon}
, the term
-2\varepsilon\Hneg
steers the solution toward an undesirable minimum entropy proxy
\mu_{\tilde{\theta}}
of
\hat{\nu}_{n}
.

This observation shows that choosing the reference measure
\mu\otimes\nu
induces some bias in scenarios where the quantity
W_{\otimes}^{\varepsilon}
is used as loss function. This phenomenon has led to the introduction of a de-biased version called the Sinkhorn divergence
[
14
,
12
,
9
]
.

As stated in the abstract, the optimal transport problem with squared Euclidean cost is equivalent to searching for the coupling between
\mu
and
\nu
that maximizes correlation: expanding the squared Euclidean cost enables to write

\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\|x-y\|^{2}d\pi(x,y)=-2\max_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\langle x,y\rangle d\pi(x,y)+\text{constant}.

(1.6)

And in (
1.6
), the right-hand side problem is a correlation-type term induced by
\pi
. So one expects the solution to be as close as possible to a deterministic linear function, subject to the marginal constraint. Indeed, Brenier’s theorem states that when
\mu
is absolutely continuous, the optimal coupling
\pi^{\star}
is deterministic, in the sense that it is supported on the graph of a function
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
(said differently,
\pi^{*}=(\Id,T)\#\mu
).
On the other-hand, the Kullback-Leibler terms in the regularized versions (
1.4
) favor solutions with minimum (zero) correlation. Thus, considering
\mu\otimes\nu
or the Lebesgue measure yields a regularization term adversarial to the optimal transport problem – which reflects an implicit preference or prior toward diffuse couplings.

That being said, there may well be other qualitative features that the user may want to steer the solution toward, depending on prior beliefs. Furthermore, in statistical contexts, the measures
\mu
and
\nu
are often corrupted or partially accessible, in which case the plug-in reference
\mu\otimes\nu
for the Kullback-Leibler divergence penalty is questionable. The purpose of this article is to precisely study how other reference measures on the coupling might lead to Kullback-Leibler regularizing terms that are more more context-adaptive or more natural with regards to the optimal transport geometry.

Outline and contributions

In Section
2
, we introduce our framework and show that the problem we study is well defined. This first part enables us to introduce our approach based on matrix analysis. The main results of this paper are in Section
3
where we solve the Gaussian entropic optimal transport problem relative to a general reference measure. Our first proof is based on the study of the primal objective function. Then, we recover the result through the derivation and solution of the dual problem. We conclude by studying the specific case where the reference measure is a proper coupling, and thus is only parametrized by a correlation matrix (Section
4
). In the same section, we use the one-dimensional case as an illustration of how the different parameters of our problem impact the solution. More precisely, we show that a well chosen reference correlation parameter enables to reduce the bias induced by the entropic penalty. A separate appendix collects auxiliary results used in the proofs of our main statements (Section
A
).

We use the notation
A:=B
to say that quantity
A
is defined by formula
B
. For
E
and
F
two measurable spaces, if
T:E\rightarrow F
is a measurable map and
\mu
a measure on
E
, we denote by
T\#\mu
the push-forward measure defined for every measurable set
B

by
T\#\mu(B):=\mu(T^{-1}(B))
. The positive integer

denotes the dimension of the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The first coordinate projection
\proj_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined by
\proj_{1}(x,y)=x
. In the same way, for every

\proj_{2}(x,y)=y

. For the subspace of

. The cone of positive-semidefinite matrices, i.e. matrices
M\in S_{d}(\mathbb{R})
such that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},~\langle Mx,x\rangle\geq 0
, is denoted by

. In the case the symmetric matrix

is such that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\},~\langle Mx,x\rangle>0
, we say that
M
positive-definite and use the notation
M\in S_{d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})
. Sometimes, if
A
and

are two symmetric matrices, we write
A\geq 0
and

instead of
A\in S_{d}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
and

. For
A,B
two matrices, we denote by
\langle A,B\rangle_{\HS}:=\tr(AB^{T})
the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product, also often called the Frobenius inner product. We mention that if

. For a positive-semidefinite matrix

, we denote by

the centered Gaussian measure defined on

with covariance matrix

. Similarly, if

is a covariance matrix on

2
Statement of the problem and matrix reduction

2.1
Statement of the problem

Let
\mu=N_{d}(A)
and
\nu=N_{d}(B)
be two centered Gaussian measures with respective covariance matrices
A
and
B
, assumed of full rank. In this work, we investigate the case where the reference measure
\pi_{\refe}
introduced in equation (
1.3
) is an arbitrary centered Gaussian measure (not necessarily having marginals
\mu
and
\nu
; we consider that special case in Section
4.1
). Thus, for a user-chosen positive-definite matrix
\Sigma\in S_{2d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})
and a regularization parameter
\varepsilon>0
, the optimal transport problem we study is

W_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-y\|^{2}d\pi(x,y)+2\varepsilon\KL(\pi|N_{2d}(\Sigma)).

(2.1)

Hence, we are aiming to minimize the objective function

I_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(\pi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-y\|^{2}d\pi(x,y)+2\varepsilon\KL(\pi|N_{2d}(\Sigma)),

(2.2)

where
\pi
belongs to the constraint set
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
. We point out that multiplying the Kullback-Leibler divergence by
2\varepsilon
in (
2.1
) instead of

\varepsilon
is arbitrary, but will remove factors
1/2
in upcoming computations. As observed before us, for instance in
[
19
,
16
]
, the regularized problem (
2.1
) can be reformulated as a (static) Schrödinger bridge problem. Indeed, we have the following equality

W_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)=2\varepsilon\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\KL\left(\pi|e^{-\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}}{2\varepsilon}}\pi_{\refe}(dxdy)\right),

(2.3)

where in this case
\pi_{\refe}=N_{2d}(\Sigma)
. The first questions regarding (
2.1
) relate to the well-posedness of the minimization problem defining the quantity
W_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}
. To address the questions of existence and uniqueness of a solution to problem (
2.1
), we exploit results on Schrödinger bridge problems in the specific case of Gaussian measures.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a full rank covariance matrix acting on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, for any pair of Gaussian measures
\mu=N_{d}(A)
and
\nu=N_{d}(B)
with non-singular covariances
A
and
B
, the regularized optimal transport problem (
2.1
) has a unique solution.

Proof.

Denote by
\mu\otimes\nu\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
the product measure induced by
\mu=N_{d}(A)
and
\nu=N_{d}(B)
.
The transport cost of
\mu\otimes\nu
can be explicitly computed and is given by

\displaystyle I(\mu\otimes\nu)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|^{2}d\mu(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|^{2}d\nu(y)-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle x,y\rangle d\mu\otimes\nu(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|^{2}d\mu(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|^{2}d\nu(y)<+\infty.

We now study the Kulback-Leibler divergence term. As
\mu
and
\nu
are centered Gaussian measures, the product measure
\mu\otimes\nu
is also centered Gaussian, and has covariance matrix
\Sigma_{\otimes}
defined by

\Sigma_{\otimes}:=\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&B\end{pmatrix}.

From this observation and Proposition
A.1
, we deduce the equalities

\displaystyle 2\KL(\mu\otimes\nu|N_{2d}(\Sigma))

\displaystyle=2\KL(N_{2d}(\Sigma_{\otimes})|N_{2d}(\Sigma))

\displaystyle=\tr(\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma_{\otimes}-I_{2d})-\log\det(\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma_{\otimes}).

As
\Sigma
and
\Sigma_{\otimes}
have full rank,
\det(\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma_{\otimes})>0
. Hence
\KL(\mu\otimes\nu|N_{2d}(\Sigma))<+\infty
. These computations of the transport term and the Kullback-Leibler divergence term show that the objective function (
2.2
) of the regularized problem (
2.1
) is finite when evaluated at
\mu\otimes\nu
. Applying
[
22
, Thm. 2.1]
ensures that there exists a unique solution to regularized transport problem (
2.1
).
∎

Remark 2.1

(Connection with the standard entropic optimal transport problem)
.

In regularized problem (
2.1
), when the reference covariance matrix is chosen to be the block diagonal matrix
\Sigma=\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A,B)
, we recover the regularized optimal transport with penalty term
\KL(\cdot|\mu\otimes\nu)
.

2.2
Matrix reduction

When the input measures are centred Gaussian, the set of admissible couplings
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
can be reduced to a set of admissible cross-covariance matrices. Specifically, for
A
and
B
two full rank covariance matrices on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we introduce the convex constraint set

\Pi^{+}(A,B):=\left\{C\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})~|~\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}\geq 0\right\}.

(2.4)

Lemma 2.2
.

Set
\mu=N_{d}(A)
and
\nu=N_{d}(B)
two centered Gaussian measures with covariance matrices denoted by
A
and
B
. The optimal transport problem between
\mu
and
\nu
reduces to minimizing a scalar product. Indeed, the equality

\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-y\|^{2}d\pi(x,y)=\min_{C\in\Pi^{+}(A,B)}\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}&-I_{d}\\
-I_{d}&I_{d}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle_{\rm HS}

(2.5)

holds true. In last equation, the right-hand is a Hilbert-Schmidt inner product, which is defined for two arbitrary matrices
M,N\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
by
\langle M,N\rangle_{\HS}=\tr(MN^{T})
.

Proof.

For
\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
, the transport cost is

\displaystyle I(\pi)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-y\|^{2}d\pi(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x\|^{2}d\mu(x)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|y\|^{2}d\nu(y)-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle x,y\rangle d\pi(x,y)

\displaystyle=\tr(A)+\tr(B)-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle x,y\rangle d\pi(x,y).

This last equation shows that the transport cost depends only on the covariance matrix of the transport plan. Moreover, for every matrix
C\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
such that the matrix

X_{C}:=\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}

is positive-semidefinite, as
\mu
and
\nu
are Gaussian measures, the centered Gaussian measure
\pi_{C}:=N_{2d}(X_{C})
belongs to
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
. Thus, we can parametrize the optimal transport problem as follows

\min_{C\in\Pi^{+}(A,B)}I(\pi_{C}),

(2.6)

where
\Pi^{+}(A,B)
is the constraint set introduced in equation (
2.4
). With this new parametrization, we rewrite by
I(C)=I(\pi_{C})
. That is, the objective function reads

I(C)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\|x-y\|^{2}d\pi_{C}(x,y).

(2.7)

Doing some computations now yields

I(C)=\tr(A)+\tr(B)-2\tr(C)=\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}&-I_{d}\\
-I_{d}&I_{d}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle_{\rm HS}.

(2.8)

∎

Lemma
2.2
is a classic optimal transport result when the two input measures are centered Gaussian measures. Results of this flavor can thus be found in the literature, for instance in
[
6
]
or
[
23
, Sec. 1.6.3]
, as detailed in next Remark. But we explicitly recall this reduction here as it enables us to introduce our approach and notations.

Remark 2.2

(Bures-Wasserstein distance)
.

The problem studied in Lemma
2.2
is the
2
-optimal transport problem between Gaussian measures. This problem was already solved in
[
13
]
, and extended to the case of a separable Hilbert space in
[
10
]
. As pointed out in the more recent work
[
6
]
, an alternative way to formulate the Gaussian optimal transport problem (
2.5
) is

W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)=\min_{C\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})}\tr(A)+\tr(B)-2\tr(C)\quad\text{such that}\quad\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}\geq 0.

(2.9)

In the same reference, one can find the solution to problem (
2.9
) which is given by the non-symmetric matrix
\sqrt{AB}
defined by

\sqrt{AB}:=A^{1/2}\left(A^{1/2}BA^{1/2}\right)^{1/2}A^{-1/2}.

(2.10)

It follows that
2
-Wasserstein distance between Gaussian measures has the closed form expression

W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)=\tr(A)+\tr(B)-2\tr\left(A^{1/2}BA^{1/2}\right)^{1/2}.

(2.11)

The right-hand side of equation (
2.5
) involves a Hilbert-Schmidt inner product between two matrices we will repeatedly manipulate throughout what follow. We thus introduce separate notations for these two important matrices.

Remark 2.3

(Optimal transport matrix)
.

From Lemma
2.2
, the optimal transport problem between Gaussian measures reduces to minimizing the Hilbert-Schmidt scalar product between an admissible covariance matrix
X_{C}
, and another matrix
Y
acting on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This matrix is defined by

Y:=\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}&-I_{d}\\
-I_{d}&I_{d}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.12)

We sometimes refer to
Y
as the optimal transport matrix.

The second matrix involved in the inner product (
2.5
) is a covariance matrix, as explained in the following remark.

Remark 2.4

(Constraints on the covariance)
.

Let
\pi
be an admissible coupling between
\mu=N_{d}(A)
and
\nu=N_{d}(B)
. Then, there exists a squared matrix
C\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
such that we can write the covariance matrix of
\pi
as

X_{C}:=\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}.

(2.13)

The matrix
C
is called the cross-covariance of
\Sigma
, and if a pair of random variable
(Z_{1},Z_{2})
has distribution
\pi
, then
C
can be explicitly written as
C=\mathbb{E}[Z_{1}Z_{2}^{T}]
. We will make use of notation (
2.13
) to denote an admissible covariance matrix parametrized by its cross-covariance matrix.

Parametrizing a covariance matrix through its correlation matrices ensures that the matrix is positive-semidefinite.

Remark 2.5

(Correlation as parameter)
.

For every cross-covariance matrix
C\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, there exists a unique matrix
R\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
with matrix norm less or equal to one, such that
C=A^{1/2}RB^{1/2}.
This matrix
R
is called the correlation matrix and allows to derive the factorization

\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}A^{1/2}&0\\
0&B^{1/2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}&R\\
R^{T}&I_{d}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A^{1/2}&0\\
0&B^{1/2}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.14)

When the correlation matrix
R
parametrizes the covariance matrix of admissible couplings between
\mu
and
\nu
, we use the notation
X_{R}
for

X_{R}:=\begin{pmatrix}A&A^{1/2}RB^{1/2}\\
B^{1/2}R^{T}A^{1/2}&B\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}A^{1/2}&0\\
0&B^{1/2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}&R\\
R^{T}&I_{d}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A^{1/2}&0\\
0&B^{1/2}\end{pmatrix}

(2.15)

instead of
X_{A^{1/2}RB^{1/2}}
.

2.3
The Kullback-Leibler divergence as a regularizing term

Adding a Kullback-Leibler divergence penalty on the optimal transport problem requires some absolute continuity conditions to be satisfied. Assuming to work with full-rank covariance matrices simplifies the matter. We discuss how the invertibility of the covariance matrix
\Sigma
relates to the decompositions (
2.13
) and (
2.15
).

Remark 2.6

(Invertibility of joint covariance matrices)
.

A joint covariance matrix having the block representation (
2.13
) is invertible if and only if the diagonal blocks
A
and
B
are invertible and the Schur complement
B-C^{T}A^{-1}C
is positive-definite. As in equation (
2.14
), when considering the parametrization of a joint coupling by its correlation matrix
R
, the joint covariance matrix is invertible if and only if;
A
and
B
are invertible and
R
has matrix norm less than one. Indeed, if
C=A^{1/2}RB^{1/2}
for some correlation matrix
R
, we have the equality
B-C^{T}A^{-1}C=B^{1/2}(I_{d}-R^{T}R)B^{1/2}
. Hence, the Schur complement
B-C^{T}A^{-1}C
is positive-definite if and only if
R
has matrix norm less than one.

Before computing the regularized objective function (
2.2
), we state a result that will allow to restrict to Gaussian measures.

Lemma 2.3
.

[
24
, p. 34, Ex. 17]
Given a reference centered Gaussian measure
N(\Sigma)
, if
\gamma
is a centered probability measure with covariance matrix
X
, then

\KL(N(X)|N(\Sigma))\leq\KL(\gamma|N(\Sigma)).

(2.16)

In this section, we set a full rank covariance matrix
\Sigma
on the product space
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\varepsilon>0
a parameter tuning the strength of the Kullback-Leibler divergence. With these two regularization parameters, the optimal transport problem we study is

W_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)=\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-y\|^{2}d\pi(x,y)+2\varepsilon\KL(\pi|N_{2d}(\Sigma)).

(2.17)

The following lemma states that we can parametrize our problem through cross-covariance matrices.

Lemma 2.4
.

Denote by
Y\in S_{2d}(\mathbb{R})
the optimal transport matrix introduced in equation (
2.12
) and by
X_{C}
an admissible covariance matrix as in equation (
2.13
). With these notations, the regularized optimal transport problem reduces to the minimization problem

W_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)=\min_{C\in\Pi^{+}(A,B)}\left\{\left\langle Y+\varepsilon\Sigma^{-1},X_{C}\right\rangle_{\HS}-\varepsilon\log\det(X_{C})\right\}+\varepsilon\log\det(\Sigma)-2\varepsilon d.

(2.18)

Proof.

For
C\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
such that
X_{C}
belongs to the cone of positive-definite matrices, we have from Lemma
2.2
that the transport term is

I(X_{C})=\langle Y,X_{C}\rangle_{\rm HS}.

(2.19)

Next, exploiting Proposition
A.1
, we can rewrite

2\KL(N_{2d}(X_{C}),N_{2d}(\Sigma))=\langle\Sigma^{-1},X_{C}\rangle_{\rm HS}-\log\det(\Sigma^{-1}X_{C})-2d.

(2.20)

Thus, the regularized transport loss can be rewritten

I(X_{C})+2\varepsilon\KL(N_{2d}(X_{C}),N_{2d}(\Sigma))=\langle Y+\varepsilon\Sigma^{-1},X_{C}\rangle_{\rm HS}-\varepsilon\log\det(X_{C})+\varepsilon\log\det(\Sigma)-2\varepsilon d.

(2.21)

Finally, this regularized optimal transport problem reads

\displaystyle W_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\min_{C\in\Pi^{+}(A,B)}\{\langle Y+\varepsilon\Sigma^{-1},X_{C}\rangle_{\rm HS}-\varepsilon\log\det(X_{C})\}+\underbrace{\varepsilon\log\det(\Sigma)-2\varepsilon d}_{\text{independent of}~C},

as announced.
∎

Remark 2.7

(Equality constraints)
.

For
X\in S_{2d}^{+}(\mathbb{R})
, we use the block decomposition

X=\begin{pmatrix}X_{11}&X_{12}\\
X_{12}^{T}&X_{22}\end{pmatrix},

where all blocks have same dimension
d\times d
. With this notation, we can rewrite the matrix reduction (
2.18
) of entropic optimal transport (
2.1
) as the optimization with equality constraint

W_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)=\min_{X\in S_{2d}^{+}(\mathbb{R})}\langle Y+\varepsilon\Sigma^{-1},X\rangle_{\rm HS}-\varepsilon\log\det X\quad\text{such that}\quad X_{11}=A,~X_{22}=B,

(2.22)

when forgetting about the additive constant
\varepsilon\log\det(\Sigma)-2\varepsilon d
.

3
Closed form for arbitrary reference Gaussian measures

We may now leverage the matrix reduction of the entropic optimal transport (
2.1
) in order to deduce its solution.

3.1
Primal problem approach

From Lemma
2.4
, the objective function to minimize is

\begin{array}[]{cccc}I_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}:&\Pi^{+}(A,B)&\rightarrow&\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}\\
&C&\mapsto&\left\langle Y+\varepsilon\Sigma^{-1},X_{C}\right\rangle_{\HS}-\varepsilon\log\det(X_{C}),\end{array}

(3.1)

where
\varepsilon>0
.

Remark 3.1

(Restriction to positive-definite matrices)
.

For every
C\in\Pi^{+}(A,B)
such that
X_{C}
is positive-semidefinite but not positive-definite,
\det(X_{C})=0
. This implies that the objective function (
3.1
) takes value
+\infty
when computed at such points
C
. Taking
C=0
, as
A
and
B
are full rank, we have that
X_{0}=\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A,B)
is positive definite. This observation shows that objective function at
X_{0}
is such that
I_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(X_{0})<+\infty
. Hence, if a minimum of our objective function is reached, it is on the subset of
\Pi^{+}(A,B)
of positive-definite matrices. For this subset we introduce the notation
\Pi^{++}(A,B)
. Formally

\Pi^{++}(A,B):=\left\{C\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})~|~X_{C}:=\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}>0\right\}.

(3.2)

Lemma 3.1
.

The objective function
I_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}
introduced in equation (
3.1
) is strictly convex on
\Pi^{++}(A,B)
.

Proof.

To begin, we point out that the objective function
I_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}
that maps
C
to
\left\langle Y+\varepsilon\Sigma^{-1},X_{C}\right\rangle_{\HS}-\varepsilon\log\det(X_{C})
can be written as the composition of two functions. Specifically,
I_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}=\ell\circ f
, where
f
is defined for every
C\in\Pi^{++}(A,B)
by

f(C):=X_{C}=\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix},

and for
X\in S_{2d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})
,
\ell(X):=\left\langle Y+\varepsilon\Sigma^{-1},X\right\rangle_{\HS}-\varepsilon\log\det(X)
. Now, the function
\log\det
is strictly concave on
S_{2d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})
(e.g.
[
3
, p. 42, Cor. 1.4.2]
). From this we deduce that
\ell
is strictly convex on
S_{2d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})
. Setting
t\in(0,1)
and
C_{0},C_{1}\in\Pi^{++}(A,B)
such that
C_{0}\neq C_{1}
, we observe that

\displaystyle f((1-t)C_{0}+tC_{1})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}A&(1-t)C_{0}+tC_{1}\\
(1-t)C_{0}^{T}+tC_{1}^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=(1-t)\begin{pmatrix}A&C_{0}\\
C_{0}^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}A&C_{1}\\
C_{1}^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=(1-t)f(C_{0})+tf(C_{1}).

This last computation enables us to write

\displaystyle I_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}((1-t)C_{0}+tC_{1})

\displaystyle=\ell\circ f((1-t)C_{0}+tC_{1})

\displaystyle=\ell((1-t)f(C_{0})+tf(C_{1}))

\displaystyle<(1-t)\ell(f(C_{0}))+t\ell(f(C_{1}))

\displaystyle=(1-t)I_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(C_{0})+tI_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(C_{1}),

where the inequality comes from the strict convexity of
\ell
. This shows the strict convexity of the objective function on
\Pi^{++}(A,B)
.
∎

We now study the first variation of objective function (
3.1
). For this purpose, we introduce the matrix
\Gamma\in S_{2d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})
to denote the inverse of the reference covariance matrix
\Sigma
. Thus, from now on

\Gamma:=\Sigma^{-1},\quad\text{and we use the block formulation}\quad\Gamma=\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma_{11}&\Gamma_{12}\\
\Gamma_{12}^{T}&\Gamma_{22}\end{pmatrix},

(3.3)

where the blocks
\Gamma_{11},\Gamma_{12}
and
\Gamma_{22}
are all
d\times d
matrices. In what comes next, we will have a particular interest for the off-diagonal block
\Gamma_{12}
and more precisely for the matrix
I_{d}-\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}
. As this matrix will appear often throughout, we will denote it by

M_{\varepsilon}:=I_{d}-\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}.

(3.4)

Remark 3.2
.

When the reference measure is the product of the input measures, we have
\Sigma=\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(A,B)
, i.e.
\Gamma_{12}=0
, so that
M_{\varepsilon}
reduces to the identity matrix
I_{d}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The objective function (
3.1
) is differentiable at every
C\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
such that
X_{C}
is positive-definite. Furthermore, the gradient at
C\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
such that
X_{C}
is positive definite is given by the formula

\nabla I_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(C)=2(\varepsilon A^{-1}C(B-C^{T}A^{-1}C)^{-1}-M_{\varepsilon}),

(3.5)

where
M_{\varepsilon}=I_{d}-\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}
as per definition (
3.4
).

Proof.

The objective function
I_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}
is the sum of a linear term denoted by
L
and the
\log\det
function. We first compute the gradient of the linear term defined by
L(C):=\langle Y+\varepsilon\Sigma^{-1},X_{C}\rangle
. Set
C\in\Pi^{++}(A,B)
. For
H\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
sufficiently small so that
X_{C+H}
is positive-definite, and using the notation
M_{\varepsilon}=I_{d}-\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}
we can write

\displaystyle L(C+H)

\displaystyle=\left\langle Y+\varepsilon\Sigma^{-1},\begin{pmatrix}A&C+H\\
C^{T}+H^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle_{\HS}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}&-I_{d}\\
-I_{d}&I_{d}\end{pmatrix}+\varepsilon\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma_{11}&\Gamma_{12}\\
\Gamma_{12}^{T}&\Gamma_{22}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&H\\
H^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle_{\HS}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}+\varepsilon\Gamma_{11}&-M_{\varepsilon}\\
-M_{\varepsilon}^{T}&I_{d}+\varepsilon\Gamma_{22}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&H\\
H^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle_{\HS}

\displaystyle=L(C)-\langle 2M_{\varepsilon},H\rangle_{\HS}.

From this computation we deduce that the linear part of objective function has gradient
{\nabla L(C)=-2M_{\varepsilon}}
. We now compute the gradient of the
\log\det
function at
C
.

\displaystyle\log\det(X_{C+H})

\displaystyle=\log\det\left(\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&H\\
H^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\det\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}+\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}^{-1},\begin{pmatrix}0&H\\
H^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle_{\HS}+o(H).

To derive last equality, we have used that the gradient of the
\log\det
function at a matrix
X\in S_{2d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})
is
X^{-1}
(see e.g.
[
7
, p. 641]
). We now exploit the formula for computing the inverse of a block matrix. For this purpose we introduce the Schur complement defined by
S:=B-C^{T}A^{-1}C
and write

\displaystyle\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}^{-1},\begin{pmatrix}0&H\\
H^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle_{\HS}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}(-)&-A^{-1}CS^{-1}\\
-S^{-1}C^{T}A^{-1}&(-)\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&H\\
H^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle_{\HS}

\displaystyle=\langle-2A^{-1}CS^{-1},H\rangle_{\HS}.

This last computation shows that
\nabla\log\det(X_{C})=-2A^{-1}CS^{-1}
.
Note that we did not need to compute the off-diagonal blocks of the matrix
X_{C}^{-1}
for the last computation. Collecting the pieces, we deduce that the gradient of our objective function is given by

\nabla I_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(C)=2(\varepsilon A^{-1}C(B-C^{T}A^{-1}C)^{-1}-M_{\varepsilon}).

(3.6)

∎

We have computed the gradient of the objective function
I_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}
in Proposition
3.1
, and now aim to solve the equation
\nabla I_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(C)=0
. To solve this equation, we need the matrix
M_{\varepsilon}:=I_{d}-\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}
to be invertible. In what follows, we discuss sufficient conditions for this matrix
M_{\varepsilon}
to be invertible. For this purpose, we introduce the block decomposition of the reference matrix
\Sigma
given by

\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}A_{\refe}&C_{\refe}\\
C_{\refe}^{T}&B_{\refe}\end{pmatrix},

(3.7)

where
A_{\refe},B_{\refe}
and
C_{\refe}
are squared matrix of dimension
d
with
A_{\refe}
and
B_{\refe}
positive-definite. A classical result of Baker
[
2
, Thm.1.A]
ensures the existence of a matrix
R_{\refe}
of matrix norm at most 1 such that
C_{\refe}=A_{\refe}^{1/2}R_{\refe}B_{\refe}^{1/2}
. This factorization of the reference cross-correlation matrix
C_{\refe}
enables to factorize the joint covariance matrix
\Sigma
as

\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}A_{\refe}^{1/2}&0\\
0&B_{\refe}^{1/2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}&R_{\refe}\\
R_{\refe}^{T}&I_{d}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A_{\refe}^{1/2}&0\\
0&B_{\refe}^{1/2}\end{pmatrix}.

(3.8)

Remark 3.3

(Blocks of
\Sigma
and matrix
M_{\varepsilon}
)
.

In the block representation of
\Gamma
introduced in equation (
3.3
), as
M_{\varepsilon}=I_{d}-\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}
, we will have a particular interest for the off-diagonal block
\Gamma_{12}
. If
\Sigma
has blocks
A_{\refe},B_{\refe}
and
C_{\refe}
as in equation (
3.7
), exploiting Lemma
A.1
to compute
\Gamma_{12}
, we have the equality

M_{\varepsilon}=I_{d}+\varepsilon A^{-1}_{\refe}C_{\refe}(B_{\refe}-C_{\refe}^{T}A_{\refe}^{-1}C_{\refe})^{-1}.

(3.9)

Assumption 3.1

(Invertibility of
M_{\varepsilon}
)
.

The matrix
\Sigma\in S_{2d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})
and the parameter
\varepsilon>0
are chosen such that the matrix
M_{\varepsilon}
introduced in equation (
3.4
) is invertible.

We now give a sufficient condition for Assumption
3.1
to hold, i.e. for the matrix
M_{\varepsilon}=I_{d}-\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}
to be invertible. To state this sufficient condition we follow the notations of equations (
3.7
) and (
3.8
). Moreover, we apply the singular value decomposition to
R_{\refe}
, and the spectral theorem to the blocks
A_{\refe}
and
B_{\refe}
. Hence, there exist
(\sigma_{i}[r],e_{i}[r],f_{i}[r])_{1\leq i\leq d}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
(\lambda_{i}[a],e_{i}[a])_{1\leq i\leq d}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
(\lambda_{i}[b],e_{i}[b])_{1\leq i\leq d}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the equalities

R_{\refe}x=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}[r]\langle f_{i}[r],x\rangle e_{i}[r],\quad A_{\refe}x=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}[a]\langle e_{i}[a],x\rangle e_{i}[a]\quad\text{and}\quad B_{\refe}x=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}[b]\langle e_{i}[b],x\rangle e_{i}[b]

(3.10)

hold true. In the previous decompositions, we suppose that the singular values
(\sigma_{i}[r])_{1\leq i\leq d}
, as well as the spectral values
(\lambda_{i}[a])_{1\leq i\leq d}
and
(\lambda_{i}[b])_{1\leq i\leq d}
, are ordered decreasingly. Thus,
\sigma_{1}[r]
is the largest singular value of
R_{\refe}
, and
\lambda_{d}[a]
and
\lambda_{d}[b]
are the smallest eigenvalues of
A_{\refe}
and
B_{\refe}
respectively.

Lemma 3.2
.

Set
\Sigma\in S_{2d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})
and
\varepsilon>0
and let
A_{\refe}
,
B_{\refe}
and
R_{\refe}
be the same blocks as in factorization (
3.8
) of
\Sigma
. Denote by
\sigma_{1}[r]
the largest singular value of
R_{\refe}
, and by
\lambda_{d}[a]
and
\lambda_{d}[b]
the smallest eigenvalues of
A_{\refe}
and
B_{\refe}
. If the inequality

\varepsilon\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\sigma_{i}[r]}{1-\sigma_{i}[r]^{2}}\right)<\sqrt{\lambda_{d}[a]\lambda_{d}[b]}

(3.11)

holds true, then the matrix
M_{\varepsilon}=I_{d}-\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}
is invertible.

Proof.

We will show that
\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}
has matrix norm less than one. From Remark
3.3
, we can express
\Gamma_{12}
as

\Gamma_{12}=-A^{-1}_{\refe}C_{\refe}(B_{\refe}-C_{\refe}^{T}A_{\refe}^{-1}C_{\refe})^{-1}

Let us introduce
R_{\refe}
the correlation matrix such that
C_{\refe}=A_{\refe}^{1/2}R_{\refe}B_{\refe}^{1/2}
. From this expression of the reference covariance matrix, we derive the equalities

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}

\displaystyle=I_{d}+\varepsilon A_{\refe}^{-1/2}R_{\refe}B_{\refe}^{1/2}(B_{\refe}-B_{\refe}^{1/2}R_{\refe}^{T}R_{\refe}B_{\refe}^{1/2})^{-1}

\displaystyle=I_{d}+\varepsilon A_{\refe}^{-1/2}R_{\refe}(I_{d}-R_{\refe}^{T}R_{\refe})^{-1}B_{\refe}^{-1/2}.

Applying the singular value decomposition to
R_{\refe}
, and following the same notations than in equation (
3.10
), we have for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that

R_{\refe}x=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}[r]\langle f_{i}[r],x\rangle e_{i}[r].

(3.12)

As
R_{\refe}
has matrix norm less than one, every singular value
\sigma_{i}[r]
is smaller than one. The singular value decomposition of
R_{\refe}
implies the following spectral decomposition for
I_{d}-R_{\refe}^{T}R_{\refe}
: for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(I_{d}-R_{\refe}^{T}R_{\refe})x=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(1-\sigma_{i}^{2}[r])\langle f_{i}[r],x\rangle f_{i}[r].

(3.13)

The singular values
\sigma_{i}[r]
being smaller than one, we deduce that
I_{d}-R_{\refe}^{T}R_{\refe}
is invertible and that its inverse has the explicit expression

(I_{d}-R_{\refe}^{T}R_{\refe})^{-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{1-\sigma_{i}^{2}[r]}f_{i}[r]f_{i}[r]^{T}.

(3.14)

Gathering the pieces, we have showed that the matrix
R_{\refe}(I_{d}-R_{\refe}^{T}R_{\refe})^{-1}
can be decomposed such that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

R_{\refe}(I_{d}-R_{\refe}^{T}R_{\refe})^{-1}x=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\sigma_{i}[r]}{1-\sigma_{i}^{2}[r]}\langle f_{i}[r],x\rangle e_{i}[r].

(3.15)

Combining the previous computations, we can derive an explicit expression for the matrix
-\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}
. Indeed, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have that

-\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}x=\varepsilon\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\sigma_{i}[r]}{1-\sigma_{i}^{2}[r]}\langle f_{i}[r],B_{\refe}^{-1/2}x\rangle A_{\refe}^{-1/2}e_{i}[r].

Now set
e_{j}[b]
an arbitrary eigenvector of
B_{\refe}
, which is also an eigenvector of
B_{\refe}^{-1/2}
. From our previous expression of
-\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}
, we deduce that

-\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}e_{j}[b]=\varepsilon\lambda_{j}[b]^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\sigma_{i}[r]}{1-\sigma_{i}^{2}[r]}\langle f_{i}[r],e_{j}[b]\rangle A_{\refe}^{-1/2}e_{i}[r].

(3.16)

In the previous equation, all the vectors
f_{i}[r]
,
e_{i}[r]
or
e_{i}[b]
have unit norm. From this observation and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we derive

\displaystyle\|\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}e_{j}[b]\|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\lambda_{j}[b]^{-1/2}\|A_{\refe}^{-1/2}\|_{\op}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\sigma_{i}[r]}{1-\sigma_{i}^{2}[r]}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\lambda_{d}[b]^{-1/2}\lambda_{d}[a]^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\sigma_{i}[r]}{1-\sigma_{i}^{2}[r]}.

Hence, if inequality (
3.11
) holds true, which means that Assumption
3.1
is satisfied, for every eigenvector
e_{j}[b]
of
B_{\refe}
we have that

\|\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}e_{j}[b]\|<1.

As
(e_{j}[b])_{1\leq j\leq d}
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we deduce that
\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}
has matrix norm less than one. Which implies that
I_{d}-\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}
is invertible.
∎

We now give our main result: the explicit solution to the entropic Gaussian optimal transport problem when the reference coupling is a Gaussian measure on the product space
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mu=N_{d}(A)
and
\nu=N_{d}(B)
two centered Gaussian measures with non-singular covariance matrices
A
and
B
. Assume that the reference measure is the Gaussian measure
N_{2d}(\Sigma)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, where
\Sigma
is a full rank covariance matrix having inverse
\Gamma:=\Sigma^{-1}\in S_{2d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})
. Then, for all
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small (so that Assumption (
3.1
) holds), the regularized optimal transport problem (
2.1
) has a unique solution given by the Gaussian measure

\pi_{\varepsilon}^{\star}:=N_{2d}\begin{pmatrix}A&C_{\varepsilon}\\
C_{\varepsilon}^{T}&B\end{pmatrix},

(3.17)

where the cross-covariance matrix
C_{\varepsilon}
is given by the formula

C_{\varepsilon}=\left[\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right](M_{\varepsilon}^{T})^{-1},

(3.18)

with
M_{\varepsilon}=I_{d}-\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}
defined in equation (
3.4
).

Remark 3.4
.

As the matrix
AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}
is not necessarily symmetric, the matrix
(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\varepsilon^{2}/4I_{d})^{1/2}
is defined by the formula

\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}:=A^{1/2}\left(A^{1/2}M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}A^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}A^{-1/2}.

(3.19)

Note that it matches the square root matrix of
AB
introduced for the case
\varepsilon=0
in equation (
2.10
).

Before proving Theorem
3.1
, we derive as a corollary the value of the entropic optimal transport cost
W_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)
.

Corollary 3.1
.

If
\mu=N_{d}(A)
and
\nu=N_{d}(B)
are two centered Gaussian measures with non singular covariance matrices, the entropic optimal transport cost has the closed form expression

\displaystyle W_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\tr(A)+\tr(B)-2\tr\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right)+\varepsilon\log\det\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon\tr(\Gamma_{11}A)+\varepsilon\tr(\Gamma_{22}B)-\varepsilon\log\det(AB)-\varepsilon d-\varepsilon d\log(\varepsilon)-\varepsilon\log\det(\Sigma^{-1}).

(3.20)

In last equation, the matrix

is defined by

M_{\varepsilon}:=I_{d}-\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}=I_{d}+\varepsilon A^{-1}_{\refe}C_{\refe}(B_{\refe}-C_{\refe}^{T}A_{\refe}^{-1}C_{\refe})^{-1}.

Remark 3.5

(Product measure as reference)

In classic entropic optimal transport, the reference measure is

\displaystyle=\tr(A)+\tr(B)-2\tr\left(\left(AB+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right)+\varepsilon\log\det\left(\left(AB+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right)

Thus, we recover the results established in

We now prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof.

We aim to solve the gradient equation
\nabla I_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(C)=0
, where the gradient of the objective function is computed in Proposition
3.1
. This equation is equivalent to

\displaystyle\varepsilon A^{-1}CS^{-1}-M_{\varepsilon}=0\quad

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\varepsilon A^{-1}CS^{-1}=M_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\varepsilon C=AM_{\varepsilon}S

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\varepsilon C=AM_{\varepsilon}(B-C^{T}A^{-1}C)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow AM_{\varepsilon}C^{T}A^{-1}C+\varepsilon C-AM_{\varepsilon}B=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow M_{\varepsilon}C^{T}(A^{-1}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T})+\varepsilon A^{-1}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}-M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(A^{-1}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T})^{T}A(A^{-1}CM_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon A^{-1}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}-M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}=0.

Introducing the notation
Z=A^{-1}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}
, we can rewrite the last matrix equation

Z^{T}AZ+\varepsilon Z-M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}=0.

(3.23)

Taking the transpose of this new equation, and exploiting that
A
and
B
are symmetric matrices, we derive

Z^{T}AZ+\varepsilon Z^{T}-M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}=0.

(3.24)

Combining these last two equations implies that
Z=Z^{T}
. As
Z=A^{-1}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}
, a matrix
C
solution of the equation
\nabla I_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(C)=0
is such that
M_{\varepsilon}C^{T}A^{-1}=A^{-1}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}
. Using this relation, we can rewrite the equation
\nabla I_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(C)=0
as

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}C^{T}(A^{-1}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T})+\varepsilon A^{-1}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}-M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(M_{\varepsilon}C^{T}A^{-1})CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\varepsilon A^{-1}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}-M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow A^{-1}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\varepsilon A^{-1}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}-M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\varepsilon CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}-AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}=0.

After introducing the matrix
W=CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}
, we reach the equation

W^{2}+\varepsilon W-AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}=0.

(3.25)

A similar matrix equation was studied in
[
15
, Prop. 3]
. We adapt their analysis to solve (
3.25
). First, we rewrite
CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}=A(A^{-1}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T})
. We have noticed that if
C
is solution of the equation
\nabla I^{\varepsilon}(C)=0
, then
A^{-1}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}
is symmetric. Exploiting this observation, we rewrite
CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}
as

\displaystyle CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}

\displaystyle=A(A^{-1}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T})

\displaystyle=A^{1/2}(A^{1/2}(A^{-1}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T})A^{1/2})A^{-1/2}.

As
A^{1/2}(A^{-1}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T})A^{1/2}
is symmetric, there exists
U
orthogonal and
D
diagonal such that

A^{1/2}(A^{-1}CM_{\varepsilon}^{T})A^{1/2}=U^{T}DU.

Introducing the change of basis matrix
P=UA^{-1/2}
we can finally write

CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}=P^{-1}DP.

Plugging this expression in equation (
3.25
), and introducing

the matrix corresponding to
AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}
after change of bases with the matrix
P
, yields

P^{-1}D^{2}P+\varepsilon P^{-1}DP-P^{-1}RP=0.

(3.26)

This last equation implies that the matrix
AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}
is diagonal in the same basis as
CM_{\varepsilon}
. Denoting by
r_{i}
the diagonal coefficients of the matrix
R

d
quadractic real equations

\delta_{i}^{2}+\varepsilon\delta_{i}-r_{i}=0,

(3.27)

with respect to
\delta_{i}
. This equation has two solutions

\delta_{i}^{-}=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}+r_{i}}\quad\text{and}\quad\delta_{i}^{+}=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}+r_{i}}.

Now, recall that the
\delta_{i}
are the coefficients of the diagonal matrix
D_{\varepsilon}
related to
C
through the equation
CM_{\varepsilon}^{T}=P^{-1}D_{\varepsilon}P
, and that
X_{C}=\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}
is a covariance matrix. The condition of

being positive-definite implies that the coefficients of
D_{\varepsilon}
are
\delta_{i}^{+}=\sqrt{r_{i}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
. Finally, exploiting the relation
C_{\varepsilon}:=P^{-1}D_{\varepsilon}P(M_{\varepsilon}^{T})^{-1}:=A^{1/2}U^{T}D_{\varepsilon}UA^{-1/2}(M_{\varepsilon}^{T})^{-1}
we derive

C_{\varepsilon}=\left[A^{1/2}\left(A^{1/2}M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}A^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}A^{-1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right](M_{\varepsilon}^{T})^{-1},

that we write for short

C_{\varepsilon}=\left[\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right](M_{\varepsilon}^{T})^{-1}.

To conclude, one can check that the matrix
C_{\varepsilon}
previously defined is such that
\nabla I_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(C_{\varepsilon})=0
. As
I_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}
is strictly convex on
\Pi^{++}(A,B)
and differentiable on this domain,
C_{\varepsilon}
is the unique minimizer of the objective function
\Pi^{++}(A,B)
. From Remark

,

on the set of admissible cross-covariance matrices
\Pi^{+}(A,B)
.

∎

We now flesh out the computations for deriving Corollary

.

Proof.

As

W_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)=\min_{C\in\Pi^{+}(A,B)}\left\langle Y+\varepsilon\Sigma^{-1},X_{C}\right\rangle_{\HS}-\varepsilon\log\det(X_{C}),

and we have derived the expression of

solution of this minimization problem, we can write

\displaystyle W_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\left\langle Y+\varepsilon\Sigma^{-1},X_{C_{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle_{\HS}-\varepsilon\log\det(X_{C_{\varepsilon}})

\displaystyle=\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}I_{d}&-I_{d}\\
-I_{d}&I_{d}\end{pmatrix}+\varepsilon\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma_{11}&\Gamma_{12}\\
\Gamma_{12}^{T}&\Gamma_{22}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}A&C_{\varepsilon}\\
C_{\varepsilon}^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle_{\HS}-\varepsilon\log\det\begin{pmatrix}A&C_{\varepsilon}\\
C_{\varepsilon}^{T}&B\end{pmatrix}.

We begin by the scalar product and exploit the expression of
C_{\varepsilon}
in Theorem
3.1
. Recalling that the Hilbert-Schmidt scalar product is defined by the trace of the matrix product, and the notation
M_{\varepsilon}=I_{d}-\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}
, we derive

\displaystyle\left\langle Y+\varepsilon\Sigma^{-1},X_{C_{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle_{\HS}

\displaystyle=\langle I_{d}+\varepsilon\Gamma_{11},A\rangle_{\HS}+\langle I_{d}+\varepsilon\Gamma_{22},B\rangle_{\HS}-2\tr(M_{\varepsilon}^{T}C_{\varepsilon})

We now focus on the term
\tr(M_{\varepsilon}^{T}C_{\varepsilon})
in last equation, and rewrite it as

\displaystyle\tr(M_{\varepsilon}^{T}C_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\tr(C_{\varepsilon}M_{\varepsilon}^{T})

\displaystyle=\tr\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right)

\displaystyle=\tr\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right)-\varepsilon\frac{d}{2}.

Thus, we can rewrite the scalar product as

\displaystyle\left\langle Y+\varepsilon\Sigma^{-1},X_{C_{\varepsilon}}\right\rangle_{\HS}=\tr(A)+\tr(B)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon\langle\Gamma_{11},A\rangle_{\HS}+\varepsilon\langle\Gamma_{22},B\rangle_{\HS}

\displaystyle-2\tr\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right)+\varepsilon d.

(3.28)

We then turn to the
\log
-determinant term. For this computation, we will use the identity

\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}=A^{1/2}\left(A^{1/2}M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}A^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}A^{-1/2}.

Thus, we can write the cross covariance block
C_{\varepsilon}
as follows:

C_{\varepsilon}=A^{1/2}\left[\left(A^{1/2}M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}A^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right]A^{-1/2}(M_{\varepsilon}^{T})^{-1}.

We will also exploit the determinant formula

\det(X_{C_{\varepsilon}})=\det(A)\det(B-C_{\varepsilon}^{T}A^{-1}C_{\varepsilon}).

We begin by computing

\displaystyle C_{\varepsilon}^{T}A^{-1}C_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=M_{\varepsilon}^{-1}A^{-1/2}\left[\left(A^{1/2}M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}A^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right]^{2}A^{-1/2}(M_{\varepsilon}^{T})^{-1}

\displaystyle=M_{\varepsilon}^{-1}A^{-1/2}\left[A^{1/2}M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}A^{1/2}-\varepsilon\left(A^{1/2}M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}A^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}I_{d}\right]A^{-1/2}(M_{\varepsilon}^{T})^{-1}

\displaystyle=B+M_{\varepsilon}^{-1}A^{-1/2}\left[\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}I_{d}-\varepsilon\left(A^{1/2}M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}A^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right]A^{-1/2}(M_{\varepsilon}^{T})^{-1}

Next,

\displaystyle B-C_{\varepsilon}^{T}A^{-1}C_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=M_{\varepsilon}^{-1}A^{-1/2}\left[\varepsilon\left(A^{1/2}M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}A^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}I_{d}\right]A^{-1/2}(M_{\varepsilon}^{T})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon M_{\varepsilon}^{-1}A^{-1}\left[A^{1/2}\left(A^{1/2}M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}A^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}A^{-1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right](M_{\varepsilon}^{T})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon M_{\varepsilon}^{-1}A^{-1}\left[\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right](M_{\varepsilon}^{T})^{-1}.

We can now compute the determinant of
X_{C_{\varepsilon}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\det(X_{C_{\varepsilon}})

\displaystyle=\det(A)\det(B-C_{\varepsilon}^{T}A^{-1}C_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\det(A)\det\left(\varepsilon M_{\varepsilon}^{-1}A^{-1}\left[\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right](M_{\varepsilon}^{T})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{d}\det(M_{\varepsilon})^{-2}\det\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right).

Taking the logarithm of last expression yields

\log\det(X_{C_{\varepsilon}})=d\log(\varepsilon)-2\log\det(M_{\varepsilon})+\log\det\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right).

(3.29)

Now, we will exploit the equality

\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right)^{-1}=\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right)(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T})^{-1},

that derives from the identity

\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right)\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right)=AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}.

(3.30)

From this equality we get

\displaystyle\log\det\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right)

\displaystyle=-\log\det\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right)

\displaystyle+\log\det(AB)+2\log\det(M_{\varepsilon}).

Thus, the
\log\det
term of the optimal covariance matrix can be written

\log\det(X_{C_{\varepsilon}})=d\log(\varepsilon)+\log\det(AB)-\log\det\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right).

Collecting the pieces of the previous computations, and recalling the additive constant
-\varepsilon(\log\det(\Sigma^{-1})+2d)
we derive

\displaystyle W_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\tr(A)+\tr(B)-2\tr\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right)+\varepsilon\log\det\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon\left(\langle\Gamma_{11},A\rangle_{\HS}+\ \langle\Gamma_{22},B\rangle_{\HS}-\log\det(AB)-d-d\log(\varepsilon)-\log\det(\Sigma^{-1})\right).

∎

3.2
shows an alternative optimization problem associated to our original optimal transport problem. From now on, we refer the right hand side of equation (
3.2
) as to the dual problem. The objective function
D_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}:S_{d}^{++}\times S_{d}^{++}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
associated to this problem is called the dual function, and defined for every
(F,G)\in S_{d}^{++}\times S_{d}^{++}
by

D_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(F,G):=\langle I_{d}+\varepsilon\Gamma_{11}-F,A\rangle_{\HS}+\langle I_{d}+\varepsilon\Gamma_{22}-G,B\rangle_{\HS}+\varepsilon\log\det\begin{pmatrix}F&-M_{\varepsilon}\\
-M_{\varepsilon}^{T}&G\end{pmatrix}.

(3.43)

Lemma 3.3
.

The dual function (
3.43
) is strictly concave on
\Pi(M_{\varepsilon})
the convex subset of
S_{d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})\times S_{d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})
defined by

\Pi(M_{\varepsilon}):=\left\{(F,G)\in S_{d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})\times S_{d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})~|~\begin{pmatrix}F&-M_{\varepsilon}\\
-M_{\varepsilon}^{T}&G\end{pmatrix}>0\right\}.

(3.44)

Proof.

As the set of positive-definite matrices is convex, the set
\Pi(M_{\varepsilon})
is convex. Regarding the strict convexity of the dual function
D_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}
, up to additive constant, we can rewrite it as

D_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(F,G)=\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}F&-M_{\varepsilon}\\
-M_{\varepsilon}^{T}&G\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}-A&0\\
0&-B\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle_{\HS}+\varepsilon\log\det\begin{pmatrix}F&-M_{\varepsilon}\\
-M_{\varepsilon}^{T}&G\end{pmatrix}+\text{constants}.

Exploiting the strict concavity of the log-determinant function on
S_{2d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})

[
3
, p. 42, Cor. 1.4.2]
allows to conclude that
D_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}
is strictly concave on the set
\Pi(M_{\varepsilon})
that we introduced in equation (
3.44
).
∎

We now study the first variation of the dual function (
3.43
).

Proposition 3.3
.

The dual function (
3.43
) is differentiable at every
(F,G)\in S_{d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})\times S_{d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})
such that the matrix
G-M_{\varepsilon}F^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}^{T}
is positive-definite. For such a couple
(F,G)
, denoting by
S=G-M_{\varepsilon}^{T}F^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}
the Schur complement, the gradient of the dual function is given by the formula

\nabla D_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(F,G)=(\varepsilon(F^{-1}+F^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}S^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}^{T}F^{-1})-A,\varepsilon S^{-1}-B).

(3.45)

Moreover, solving the gradient equation
\nabla D_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(F,G)=(0,0)
is equivalent to solving the matrix equations system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}FAF-\varepsilon F-M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}&=&0\\
\varepsilon B^{-1}+M_{\varepsilon}^{T}F^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}&=&G.\end{array}\right.

(3.46)

Proof.

The dual function is a sum of a linear term and a log-determinant term. The gradient of the linear term is constant and equal to
(-A,-B)
. Regarding the log-determinant term, if
(F,G)
is such that
G-M_{\varepsilon}F^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}^{T}
is positive-definite, from Theorem
A.2
the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}F&-M_{\varepsilon}\\
-M_{\varepsilon}&G\end{pmatrix}

(3.47)

is positive-definite. We now exploit that the log-determinant is differentiable on
S_{2d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})
and that its gradient is given by the inverse matrix. Moreover, in our case, only first variations of the form
H=\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(H_{1},H_{2})
are allowed. More precisely, introducing the function
\widetilde{D}_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}
defined at
F,G
by

\widetilde{D}_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(F,G):=\log\det\begin{pmatrix}F&-M_{\varepsilon}\\
-M_{\varepsilon}^{T}&G\end{pmatrix},

(3.48)

we write

\displaystyle\widetilde{D}_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(F+H_{1},G+H_{2})

\displaystyle=\log\det\left(\begin{pmatrix}F&-M_{\varepsilon}\\
-M_{\varepsilon}^{T}&G\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}H_{1}&0\\
0&H_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\det\begin{pmatrix}F&-M_{\varepsilon}\\
-M_{\varepsilon}^{T}&G\end{pmatrix}+\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}F&-M_{\varepsilon}\\
-M_{\varepsilon}^{T}&G\end{pmatrix}^{-1},\begin{pmatrix}H_{1}&0\\
0&H_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle_{\rm HS}+o(H),

where we used that that the gradient of the log-det function at point
X
is its inverse
X^{-1}
(see e.g.
[
7
, p. 641]
). Now, exploiting the inversion formula for block matrices, we derive that

\displaystyle\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}F&-M_{\varepsilon}\\
-M_{\varepsilon}^{T}&G\end{pmatrix}^{-1},\begin{pmatrix}H_{1}&0\\
0&H_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle_{\rm HS}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}F^{-1}+F^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}S^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}^{T}F^{-1}&(-)\\
(-)&S^{-1}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}H_{1}&0\\
0&H_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle_{\rm HS}

\displaystyle=\langle F^{-1}+F^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}S^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}^{T}F^{-1},H_{1}\rangle_{\HS}+\langle S^{-1},H_{2}\rangle_{\HS},

where
S=G-M_{\varepsilon}^{T}F^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}
. Collecting the pieces together, we get

\nabla D_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(F,G)=(\varepsilon(F^{-1}+F^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}S^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}^{T}F^{-1})-A,\varepsilon S^{-1}-B),

as claimed. Now, the first order optimality condition, that is the equation
\nabla D_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(F,G)=0
is equivalent to the system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\varepsilon(F^{-1}+F^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}S^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}^{T}F^{-1})&=&A\\
\varepsilon S^{-1}&=&B.\end{array}\right.

(3.49)

Exploiting the second equation, we can substitute
S^{-1}
by
\varepsilon^{-1}B
in the first equation to rewrite the first equation

\varepsilon F^{-1}+F^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}F^{-1}=A.

(3.50)

Then, we have the equivalences

\displaystyle\varepsilon F^{-1}+F^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}F^{-1}=A

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\varepsilon F+M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}=FAF

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow FAF-\varepsilon F-M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}=0.

For the second equation, we derive

\displaystyle\varepsilon S^{-1}=B

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\varepsilon B^{-1}=S

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\varepsilon B^{-1}=G-M^{T}F^{-1}M

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow G=\varepsilon B^{-1}+M^{T}F^{-1}M.

We thus have shown that the matrix equations system (
3.49
) is equivalent to the system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}FAF-\varepsilon F-M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}&=&0\\
\varepsilon B^{-1}+M_{\varepsilon}^{T}F^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}&=&G.\end{array}\right.

∎

Theorem 3.2
.

If
\varepsilon>0
, the optimal dual variables
F_{\varepsilon}^{\star},G_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
associated to dual problem (
3.2
) are given by the formulae

\begin{array}[]{ccc}F_{\varepsilon}^{\star}&=&A^{-1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}+\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right)\\
G_{\varepsilon}^{\star}&=&B^{-1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}+\left(BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}AM_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right).\end{array}

(3.51)

We can also express the second optimal dual variable
G_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
as a function of
F_{\varepsilon}^{\star}
through the relation

G_{\varepsilon}^{\star}=\varepsilon B^{-1}+M_{\varepsilon}^{T}(F_{\varepsilon}^{\star})^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}.

(3.52)

Proof.

Our strategy is to solve the system (
3.46
) starting from the first matrix equation

\displaystyle FAF-\varepsilon F-M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow AFAF-\varepsilon AF-AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow Z^{2}-\varepsilon Z-AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}=0,

with the notation
Z=AF
. This last matrix equation is very similar to (
3.25
), that we solved for proving Theorem
3.1
. Adapting the argument, we establish that the equation
{Z^{2}-\varepsilon Z-AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}}
has a unique solution
Z_{\varepsilon}
such that
F_{\varepsilon}:=A^{-1}Z_{\varepsilon}
is positive-definite. This solution is given by the matrix
Z_{\varepsilon}
defined by the formula

Z_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}+\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}.

Then, as
Z_{\varepsilon}=AF_{\varepsilon}
, we have
F_{\varepsilon}=A^{-1}Z_{\varepsilon}
. This yields

F_{\varepsilon}=A^{-1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}+\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right).

(3.53)

Let us now compute the second dual variable. To do so, let us go back to system (
3.46
); that we repeat below for clarity:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}FAF-\varepsilon F-M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}&=&0\\
\varepsilon B^{-1}+M_{\varepsilon}^{T}F^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}&=&G.\end{array}\right.

Notice that we can rewrite the first equation as follows:

\displaystyle FAF-\varepsilon F-M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow FA-\varepsilon I_{d}-M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}F^{-1}=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow M_{\varepsilon}^{T}F^{-1}=B^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(FA-\varepsilon I_{d})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow M_{\varepsilon}^{T}F^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}=B^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(FA-\varepsilon I_{d})M_{\varepsilon}.

This expression of the matrix
M_{\varepsilon}^{T}F^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}
allows us to rewrite the second equation of the system (
3.46
) as

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=B^{-1}(\varepsilon I_{d}+M_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(FA-\varepsilon I_{d})M_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=B^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}^{-1}FAM_{\varepsilon}.

Introducing
F_{\varepsilon}
solution of the first equation, we express it as in equation (
3.53
) to write
G_{\varepsilon}
solution of the system as

G_{\varepsilon}=B^{-1}\left(M_{\varepsilon}^{-1}A^{-1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}+\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right)AM_{\varepsilon}\right).

(3.54)

Now, the identity

M_{\varepsilon}^{-1}A^{-1}\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}AM_{\varepsilon}=\left(BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}AM_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}

yields

G_{\varepsilon}=B^{-1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}+\left(BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}AM_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right).

(3.55)

If the dual function (
3.43
) reaches a maximum, it is on the subset
\Pi(M_{\varepsilon})
introduced in equation (
3.44
). Exploiting Theorem
A.2
, one can show that the dual matrices
F_{\varepsilon}
and
G_{\varepsilon}
are such that the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}F_{\varepsilon}&-M_{\varepsilon}\\
-M_{\varepsilon}^{T}&G_{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}

(3.56)

is positive-definite. This shows that
(F_{\varepsilon},G_{\varepsilon})
belongs to
\Pi(M_{\varepsilon})
. Moreover, as the dual objective function is strictly concave on the set
\Pi(M_{\varepsilon})
, and
\nabla D_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(F_{\varepsilon},G_{\varepsilon})=(0,0)
, we deduce that the couple
(F_{\varepsilon},G_{\varepsilon})
is the unique solution to the dual problem (
3.2
).
∎

From the solutions to dual problem, we recover the regularized optimal transport cost already computed in Corollary
3.1
.

Corollary 3.2
.

For
\mu=N_{d}(A)
and
\nu=N_{d}(B)
two centered Gaussian measures; the regularized optimal transport cost has the closed form expression given by the formula

\displaystyle W_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\tr(A)+\tr(B)-2\tr\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right)+\varepsilon\log\det\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon\tr(\Gamma_{11}A)+\varepsilon\tr(\Gamma_{22}B)-\varepsilon\log\det(AB)-\varepsilon d-\varepsilon d\log(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\log\det(\Sigma).

(3.57)

Proof.

Using the notations from Theorem
3.2
, we can write the optimal transport cost between
\mu
and
\nu
as

W_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)=\langle I_{d}+\varepsilon\Gamma_{11}-F_{\varepsilon}^{\star},A\rangle_{\HS}+\langle I_{d}+\varepsilon\Gamma_{22}-G_{\varepsilon}^{\star},B\rangle_{\HS}+\varepsilon\log\det\begin{pmatrix}F_{\varepsilon}^{\star}&-M_{\varepsilon}\\
-M_{\varepsilon}^{T}&G_{\varepsilon}^{\star}\end{pmatrix}-\varepsilon\log\det(\varepsilon\Sigma^{-1}).

For the scalar product terms, we begin by writing

\displaystyle\langle I_{d}+\varepsilon\Gamma_{11}-F_{\varepsilon}^{\star},A\rangle_{\HS}

\displaystyle=\tr(A)+\varepsilon\langle\Gamma_{11},A\rangle_{\HS}-\tr\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}+\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\tr(A)+\varepsilon\langle\Gamma_{11},A\rangle_{\HS}-\tr\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right)-\varepsilon\frac{d}{2}.

And second we write,

\displaystyle\langle I_{d}+\varepsilon\Gamma_{22}-G_{\varepsilon}^{\star},B\rangle_{\HS}

\displaystyle=\tr(B)+\varepsilon\langle\Gamma_{22},B\rangle_{\HS}-\tr\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}+\left(BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}AM_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle=\tr(B)+\varepsilon\langle\Gamma_{22},B\rangle_{\HS}-\tr\left(\left(BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}AM_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right)-\varepsilon\frac{d}{2}.

Then, the identity

M^{-1}A^{-1}\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}AM_{\varepsilon}=\left(BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}AM_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}

ensures that

\tr\left(\left(BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}AM_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right)=\tr\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right).

We thus have that

\displaystyle\langle I_{d}+\varepsilon\Gamma_{11}-F_{\varepsilon}^{\star},A\rangle_{\HS}+\langle I_{d}+\varepsilon\Gamma_{22}-G_{\varepsilon}^{\star},B\rangle_{\HS}

\displaystyle=\tr(A)+\tr(B)+\varepsilon\langle\Gamma_{11},A\rangle_{\HS}+\varepsilon\langle\Gamma_{22},B\rangle_{\HS}

\displaystyle\quad-2\tr\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right)-\varepsilon d.

Regarding the determinant term, we write it as

\displaystyle\det\begin{pmatrix}F_{\varepsilon}^{\star}&-M_{\varepsilon}\\
-M_{\varepsilon}^{T}&G_{\varepsilon}^{\star}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\det\begin{pmatrix}F_{\varepsilon}^{\star}&-M_{\varepsilon}\\
-M_{\varepsilon}^{T}&\varepsilon B^{-1}+M_{\varepsilon}^{T}(F_{\varepsilon}^{\star})^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\det(F_{\varepsilon}^{\star})\det(\varepsilon B^{-1}+M_{\varepsilon}^{T}(F_{\varepsilon}^{\star})^{-1}M_{\varepsilon}-M_{\varepsilon}^{T}(F_{\varepsilon}^{\star})^{-1}M)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{d}\det(A)^{-1}\det(B)^{-1}\det\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}+\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right).

Taking the logarithm of the determinant yields

\varepsilon\log\det\begin{pmatrix}F_{\varepsilon}^{\star}&-M_{\varepsilon}\\
-M_{\varepsilon}^{T}&G_{\varepsilon}^{\star}\end{pmatrix}=\varepsilon d\log(\varepsilon)-\varepsilon\log\det(AB)+\varepsilon\log\det\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}+\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right).

Recalling the additive constant
-\varepsilon(2d\log(\varepsilon)+\log\det(\Sigma^{-1})
, and collecting the pieces we derive

\displaystyle W_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\tr(A)+\tr(B)-2\tr\left(\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right)+\varepsilon\log\det\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}+\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}\right)

\displaystyle+\varepsilon\tr(\Gamma_{11}A)+\varepsilon\tr(\Gamma_{22}B)-\varepsilon\log\det(AB)-\varepsilon d-\varepsilon d\log(\varepsilon)-\varepsilon\log\det(\Sigma^{-1}).

∎

4
Examples

4.1
Reference plan parametrized by a correlation matrix

In our entropic transport problem, so far, we have addressed the case where the Gaussian prior has arbitrary covariance matrix
\Sigma
. However, the constraint set
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
imposes that the solution to our Gaussian problem has a covariance with diagonal blocks
A
and
B
. Thus, in this section we study the case where the prior covariance matrix also has diagonal blocks
A
and
B
. Thus; it only remains to optimize over the cross covariance matrix
C
. However, every valid cross covariance matrix decomposes as
C=A^{1/2}R_{\refe}B^{1/2}
, with
R_{\refe}
a correlation matrix with matrix norm
\|R_{\refe}\|_{\op}\leq 1
. Thus, the reference coupling has covariance matrix

\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}A&A^{1/2}R_{\refe}B^{1/2}\\
B^{1/2}R_{\refe}^{T}A^{1/2}&B\end{pmatrix},

(4.1)

where
R_{\refe}
acts as the only free parameter of the reference coupling. With these notations; using the inverse block formula
A.5
, we can show that
R_{\refe}
relates to
\Gamma
the inverse of
\Sigma
as follows:

\Gamma:=\Sigma^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}A^{-1/2}(I_{d}+R_{\refe}(I_{d}-R_{\refe}^{T}R_{\refe})^{-1}R_{\refe}^{T})A^{-1/2}&-A^{-1/2}R_{\refe}(I_{d}-R_{\refe}^{T}R_{\refe})^{-1}B^{-1/2}\\
-B^{-1/2}(I_{d}-R_{\refe}^{T}R_{\refe})^{-1}R_{\refe}^{T}A^{-1/2}&B^{-1/2}(I_{d}-R_{\refe}^{T}R_{\refe})^{-1}B^{-1/2}\end{pmatrix}.

Thus,
\Gamma_{12}
the upper-right block of
\Gamma
defining the matrix
M_{\varepsilon}=I_{d}-\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}
is now given by the formula

\Gamma_{12}=-A^{-1/2}R_{\refe}(I_{d}-R_{\refe}^{T}R_{\refe})^{-1}B^{-1/2}.

Applying Theorem
3.1
, we derive the solution of entropic optimal transport (
2.1
) when the reference covariance is of the form (
4.1
).

Corollary 4.1
.

Set
\mu=N_{d}(A)
and
\nu=N_{d}(B)
two centered Gaussian measures with full rank covariance matrices
A
and
B
. For
\varepsilon>0
and
R_{\refe}\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
a correlation matrix with matrix norm smaller than one such that Assumption
3.1
holds true, the entropic transport problem

\min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|x-y\|^{2}d\pi(x,y)+2\varepsilon\KL(\pi|N_{2d}(\Sigma))\quad\text{with}\quad\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}A&A^{1/2}R_{\refe}B^{1/2}\\
B^{1/2}R_{\refe}^{T}A^{1/2}&B\end{pmatrix},

(4.2)

has solution

\pi_{\varepsilon}:=N_{2d}\begin{pmatrix}A&C_{\varepsilon}\\
C_{\varepsilon}^{T}&B\end{pmatrix},

where

C_{\varepsilon}=A^{1/2}\left[\left(NN^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right](N^{T})^{-1}B^{1/2},\quad\text{and}\quad N=A^{1/2}B^{1/2}+\varepsilon R_{\refe}(I_{d}-R_{\refe}^{T}R_{\refe})^{-1}.

(4.3)

Proof.

We now detail the computations that led to the closed form (
4.3
) for the cross covariance
C_{\varepsilon}
.
First, with the parametrization of the prior covariance
\Sigma
given in equation (
4.1
), the matrix
M_{\varepsilon}=I_{d}-\varepsilon\Gamma_{12}
that appears in Theorem
3.1
is now given by

M_{\varepsilon}=I_{d}+\varepsilon A^{-1/2}R_{\refe}(I_{d}-R_{\refe}^{T}R_{\refe})^{-1}B^{-1/2}.

With the purpose of circumventing intricate expressions for
C_{\varepsilon}
, we factorize
M_{\varepsilon}
as

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=A^{-1/2}(A^{1/2}B^{1/2}+\varepsilon R_{\refe}(I_{d}-R_{\refe}^{T}R_{\refe})^{-1})B^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=A^{-1/2}NB^{-1/2},

where
N=A^{1/2}B^{1/2}+\varepsilon R_{\refe}(I_{d}-R_{\refe}^{T}R_{\refe})^{-1}
. Now, from Theorem
3.1
, we know that the cross covariance matrix is given by the formula

\displaystyle C_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\left[\left(AM_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right](M_{\varepsilon}^{T})^{-1}

\displaystyle=A^{1/2}\left[\left(A^{1/2}M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}A^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right]A^{-1/2}(M_{\varepsilon}^{T})^{-1}.

Next, we observe the simplification

A^{1/2}M_{\varepsilon}BM_{\varepsilon}^{T}A^{1/2}=NN^{T}.

(4.4)

From this last observation, we finally reach the expression

C_{\varepsilon}=A^{1/2}\left[\left(NN^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right](N^{T})^{-1}B^{1/2}.

∎

Remark 4.1

(Correlation matrix)
.

We point out that solution
C_{\varepsilon}
given in equation (
4.3
) to regularized problem (
4.2
) is already factorized as
C_{\varepsilon}=A^{1/2}R_{\varepsilon}B^{1/2}
. Indeed, introducing the correlation matrix

R_{\varepsilon}=\left[\left(NN^{T}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}I_{d}\right)^{1/2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}I_{d}\right](N^{T})^{-1},\quad\text{where}\quad N=A^{1/2}B^{1/2}+\varepsilon R_{\refe}(I_{d}-R_{\refe}^{T}R_{\refe})^{-1}

(4.5)

we have
C_{\varepsilon}=A^{1/2}R_{\varepsilon}B^{1/2}
.

4.2
Gaussian measures on the real line

We now set
d=1
. As in the previous Section
4.1
, we assume that the reference coupling
N_{2}(\Sigma)
has the same marginals as the input measures. Hence, if
\mu=N_{1}(a^{2})
and
\nu=N_{1}(b^{2})
with
a,b>0
, the reference covariance matrix
\Sigma\in S_{2}^{++}(\mathbb{R})
is of the form

\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}a^{2}&a\rho b\\
b\rho a&b^{2}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and has inverse}\quad\Sigma^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{a^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}&\frac{-\rho}{ab(1-\rho^{2})}\\
\frac{-\rho}{ab(1-\rho^{2})}&\frac{1}{b^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}\end{pmatrix}.

(4.6)

Thus, on the real line, the correlation matrix
R_{\refe}
with matrix norm less than one reduces to a real number
\rho\in(-1,1)
. And exploiting Corollary
4.1
, the solution of the optimal transport problem is

\pi_{\varepsilon}=N_{2}\begin{pmatrix}a^{2}&c_{\varepsilon}\\
c_{\varepsilon}&b^{2}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{where}\quad c_{\varepsilon}=\left(\sqrt{\left(ab+\frac{\varepsilon\rho}{1-\rho^{2}}\right)^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)\frac{ab(1-\rho^{2})}{ab(1-\rho^{2})+\varepsilon\rho}.

(4.7)

And the value of the entropic optimal transport problem is

\displaystyle W_{\Sigma}^{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=a^{2}+b^{2}-2\left[\left(ab+\frac{\varepsilon\rho}{1-\rho^{2}}\right)^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\right]^{1/2}+\varepsilon\log\left(\left[\left(ab+\frac{\varepsilon\rho}{1-\rho^{2}}\right)^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\right]^{1/2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)

\displaystyle+2\varepsilon(1-\rho^{2})^{-1}-\varepsilon-\varepsilon\log(\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\log(1-\rho^{2}).

(4.8)

In the following examples we display the impact of this parameter
\rho
on our optimal transport problem. In our experiments, we are interested by the impact of the regularizing parameters
\varepsilon
and
\rho
. Thus, we choose the simplest possible scenario for the input measures:
\mu=\nu=N(0,1)
. We begin by reviewing the impact of the parameters
\varepsilon
in the case of classic entropic optimal transport. That is when
\rho=0
. The impact of this parameter
\varepsilon
on the transport plan
\pi_{\varepsilon}
is displayed in Figure
1
. This kind of illustration already exists in the literature; for instance in
[
15
, Fig. 2]
or in
[
19
, Fig. 1]
. However, we aim at comparing the effect of the parameter
\varepsilon
with the effect of the correlation parameter
\rho
. For this purpose, in a second scenario, we set
\varepsilon=2
and study the impact of this parameter
\rho
. In Figure
2
, for
\rho

Appendix A
Auxiliary results

On the space of squared matrices
M_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, the Hilbert-Schmidt (also called Frobenius) scalar product is defined by
\langle A,B\rangle_{\rm HS}:=\tr(AB^{T})
; and reduces to
\langle A,B\rangle_{\rm HS}=\tr(AB)
between symmetric matrices.

Proposition A.1

(Kullback-Leibler divergence)
.

[
24
, p. 33, ex. 11]

For
\mu_{0}=N_{d}(m_{0},\Sigma_{0})
and
\mu_{1}=N_{d}(m_{1},\Sigma_{1})
two Gaussian measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with full rank covariance matrices
\Sigma_{0}
and
\Sigma_{1}
, the Kullback-Leibler divergence has the closed form expression

\KL(\mu_{1}|\mu_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\left[(m_{1}-m_{0})^{T}\Sigma_{0}^{-1}(m_{1}-m_{0})+\tr(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\Sigma_{1}-\Id)+\log\left(\frac{\det(\Sigma_{0})}{\det(\Sigma_{1})}\right)\right].

(A.1)

We point out that this divergence can be rewritten

\KL(\mu_{1}|\mu_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\left[\langle\Sigma_{0}^{-1},(m_{1}-m_{0})\otimes(m_{1}-m_{0})+(\Sigma_{1}-\Sigma_{0})\rangle_{\rm HS}-\log\det(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\Sigma_{1})\right],

(A.2)

which has a more geometric interpretation.

Remark A.1
.

In the case where both Gaussian measures
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
have zero mean and respective full-rank covariance
\Sigma_{0}
and
\Sigma_{1}
, the Kullback-Leibler divergence simplifies to

\KL(\mu_{1}|\mu_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\left[\langle\Sigma_{0}^{-1},(\Sigma_{1}-\Sigma_{0})\rangle_{\rm HS}-\log\det(\Sigma_{0}^{-1}\Sigma_{1})\right].

(A.3)

Theorem A.1
.

[
29
, p. 24, Thm. 1.9]

Let
E
be a separable normed vector space,
E^{*}
its topological dual space, and
f
,
g
two convex functions defined on
E
with values in
\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
. Denoting by
f^{*}
and
g^{*}
the Legendre-Fenchel transform of and
f
and
g
respectively; if there exists a point
z_{0}\in E
such that
f(z_{0})<+\infty
,
g(z_{0})<+\infty
and
f
is continuous at
z_{0}
, then

\inf_{x\in E}\{f(x)+g(x)\}=\max_{z\in E^{*}}\{-f^{*}(-z)-g^{*}(z)\}.

(A.4)

Lemma A.1
.

[
17
, p. 12, eq. 29]

Let
M\in S_{2d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})
be a positive-definite matrix that we can write by blocks as

M=\begin{pmatrix}A&C\\
C^{T}&B\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{where}\quad A,B\in S_{d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})\quad\text{and}\quad C\in M_{d}(\mathbb{R}).

Then, introducing the Schur complement
S:=B-C^{T}A^{-1}C
, that belongs to
S_{d}^{++}(\mathbb{R})
, we can write the inverse matrix of
M
as

M^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}A^{-1}+A^{-1}CS^{-1}C^{T}A^{-1}&-A^{-1}CS^{-1}\\
-S^{-1}C^{T}A^{-1}&S^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.
