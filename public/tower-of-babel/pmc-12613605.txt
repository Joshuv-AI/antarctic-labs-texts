A single-cell multi-omics framework identifies immune cell drivers of migraine and repurposable therapeutics
Authors: Da-Peng Li, Chun-Bo Feng, Li Wang, Ye Tao, Yan-Xun Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMigraine, a complex neurovascular disorder, is closely associated with neuroinflammation and immune dysregulation. However, the high heterogeneity of immune cell populations means that the specific cellular immune mechanisms driving migraine susceptibility remain unclear.MethodsWe integrated single-cell expression quantitative trait locus (sc-eQTL) data by applying single-cell Mendelian randomization (Mendelian randomization applied at single-cell resolution, scMR) and colocalization analyses to systematically explore immune-mediated regulatory mechanisms underlying migraine and to identify potential therapeutic targets.ResultsWe assessed the causal effects of 9,117 unique sc-eQTLs on migraine across 14 immune cell types. Four genes (PRDM11, VIM, FGFRL1, C6orf25) were identified as high-priority targets. Colocalization analysis revealed a high probability (posterior probability PP.H4 > 0.90) that these genes share causal variants with migraine genome-wide association study (GWAS) signals. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analyses revealed differential expression patterns of these genes across cell types and migraine-related states. Safety assessments based on phenome-wide association studies (PheWAS) showed a low risk of off-target effects in multiple body systems, thus supporting their safety as therapeutic targets. Mapping these targets to a database of known drugs identified three repurposable drug candidates (one approved and two investigational) with therapeutic potential for migraine.ConclusionsThis study establishes an extensible multi-omics analytical framework, providing novel insights into the immunogenetic basis of migraine. Furthermore, it successfully identified repurposable candidate drugs targeting key pathogenic genes, offering new perspectives for developing novel immunomodulatory therapeutic strategies for migraine.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-025-02179-w.

Introduction

Migraine, the world’s second leading cause of disability, imposes a substantial burden extending far beyond headache itself, encompassing multidimensional neurovascular, metabolic, and immune dysfunction [1–4]. Although widely used, conventional non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and triptans are significantly limited by systemic adverse events, medication-overuse headache, and insufficient efficacy, substantially reducing patients’ quality of life [5]. In recent years, the role of immune mechanisms in migraine pathophysiology has become increasingly clear [6]. Studies demonstrate abnormal immune function in migraine patients during both ictal and interictal periods [7]. Zhang et al. further confirmed that metabolic dysregulation, particularly lactate accumulation, significantly accelerates disease progression by activating B-cell/Natural killer (NK)-cell-mediated immune responses and adhesion molecule signaling pathways [2, 8]. Notably, migraine exhibits high comorbidity rates with autoimmune diseases (ADs). These conditions share aberrant activation of the NF-κB pathway and neuropeptide axis (CGRP-PACAP)-mediated vascular inflammation, suggesting deep involvement of a “neuro-immune-metabolic” interaction network in the pathological process [9–13].

Current migraine therapeutic strategies are undergoing a paradigm shift from symptomatic analgesia towards immune targeting. Although calcitonin gene-related peptide monoclonal antibodies (anti-calcitonin gene-related peptide monoclonal antibodies [anti-CGRP mAbs]) are effective for migraine prevention, their efficacy is significantly limited in patients with obesity, comorbid ADs, or refractory migraine [14]. Moreover, they fail to reverse the overall dysregulation of the neuro-immune-metabolic axis [15]. Real-world evidence indicates that combining anti-CGRP mAbs with immunomodulators (e.g., TNF inhibitors or methotrexate) reduces monthly migraine days by 44% in migraine patients with comorbid ADs, without exacerbating autoimmune activity [16]. These findings suggest that targeting the neuro-immune-metabolic axis may represent a novel strategy to reverse immune-metabolic reprogramming.

However, immune-targeted therapy currently faces two major challenges: unclear cell-specific mechanisms and a lack of multi-omics integration, which hinder the development of precision therapeutics. To address these gaps, we aimed to systematically identify immune cell type-specific causal genes for migraine and explore their potential as therapeutic targets. Using a multi-omics framework that integrates single-cell expression quantitative trait loci (sc-eQTLs), single-cell Mendelian randomization (scMR), and colocalization analyses, we sought to uncover genetic drivers of migraine across immune cell subtypes and identify repurposable drug candidates. The ultimate aim of this approach is to propel the management of migraine towards a novel paradigm of immunophenotype-driven precision interventions.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a multi-stage integrative analytical framework to investigate the causal influence of immune cell-specific mechanisms on migraine. The overall workflow is depicted in Fig. 1. Leveraging summary-level data from individuals of European ancestry (n = 462,933), we identified cis-expression quantitative trait loci (cis-eQTLs) associated with 14 immune cell types for subsequent scMR analysis. This study strictly adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology using Mendelian Randomisation (STROBE-MR) guidelines, which are designed to ensure that observational studies are reported correctly and transparently (Supplementary Table 1). All analyses were pooled using publicly available sc-eQTLs and genome-wide association studies (GWAS). As no individual level data are involved, no additional ethical approval is required. All analyses in this study were performed in the R 4.3.1 environment.

Exposure and instrument selection

We utilized cis-eQTL data obtained from the OneK1K single-cell dataset provided by Yazar S et al. [17]. This resource comprises single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from 1,267,758 peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) derived from 982 healthy human donors. It identified 26,597 independent cis-eQTLs and 990 trans-eQTLs across the 14 cell types CD4 + naïve and central memory T cells (CD4 NC), CD8 + T cells with an effector memory or central memory phenotype (CD8 ET), classical monocytes (Mono C), CD8 + naïve and central memory T cells (CD8 NC), CD4 + T cells with an effector memory or central memory phenotype (CD4 ET), CD4 + T cells expressing SOX4 (CD4 SOX4), CD8 + T cells expressing S100B (CD8 S100B), Natural killer cells (NK), Natural killer recruiting cells (NK R), Immature B cells (B IN), Memory B cells (B Mem), Non-classical monocytes (Mono NC), Dendritic cells (DC), and Plasma cells (Plasma). Detailed information on all datasets is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

The instrumental variable (IV) selection adhered to the core assumptions of MR [18]: (1) Relevance Assumption: IVs must be strongly associated with the exposure factor; (2) Independence Assumption: IVs must be independent of known confounders; (3) Exclusion Restriction Assumption: IVs affect the outcome solely through the exposure, not via alternative pathways. To satisfy the relevance assumption, we first filtered of independent cis-eQTLs associated with the 14 immune cell types using a significance threshold of P < 5 × 10−08. Using strict thresholds (r2 = 0.001, clumping window: 10,000 kb), we conducted minimize linkage disequilibrium (LD)-based clumping with the 1000 Genomes European reference panel. To mitigate pleiotropic violations of the exclusion restriction assumption, PhenoScanner V2 (http://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/) and literature sources were systematically queried to detect and eliminate IVs statistically linked to established migraine confounders. Furthermore, we harmonized exposure and outcome datasets and excluded palindromic single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) to avoid strand ambiguity issues.

Outcome data

Migraine outcome data were obtained from the IEU OPEN GWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). This GWAS included 462,933 individuals of European ancestry, consisting of 13,597 migraine cases (defined by ICD-10 diagnosis codes) and 449,336 controls (Supplementary Table 2). Data collection occurred to 2018. To ensure consistency with the exposure dataset, we restricted our analysis to individuals of European descent.

sc-eQTL MR analysis

We performed scMR analysis to estimate the causal effect of immune cell-specific gene expression on migraine. Multiple MR methods were employed: Inverse-Variance Weighted (IVW), Weighted Median (WM), Weighted Mode, MR-Egger, and Wald Ratio (WR). For instruments with a single SNP (N.SNP = 1), the WR method was applied directly calculating the ratio of the SNP-outcome association to the SNP-exposure association to infer the causal effect [19]. When N.SNP was 2, the IVW method was applied to derive a weighted average of the causal effects estimated from each individual variant [20]. When N.SNP was >2, we primarily relied on the IVW method but complemented it with the WM method to mitigate potential bias inherent to any single approach [21]. Bonferroni correction was implemented to mitigate false positives from multiple comparisons [22].

Colocalization analysis

To validate the reliability of causal associations identified by scMR, we performed Bayesian colocalization analysis on causal genes (PBonferroni < 0.05). This analysis tests whether the sc-eQTL signals and migraine GWAS signals within a given genomic region share the same underlying causal variant. Bayesian colocalization, implemented primarily using the Coloc R package, is a statistical framework that integrates GWAS summary data with molecular QTL data to infer regulatory effects at GWAS risk loci [23]. The analysis evaluates five mutually exclusive hypotheses: (1) H0: No association with either trait; (2) H1: Association with trait 1 (gene expression) only; (3) H2: Association with trait 2 (migraine) only; (4) H3: Association with both traits, but driven by distinct causal variants; 4) H4: Association with both traits, driven by a shared causal variant. We focused primarily on the posterior probability for H4 (PP.H4). A PP.H4 exceeding 0.90 was considered to provide strong statistical support that the gene’s association with migraine is driven by a shared causal genetic variant.

Single-cell transcriptomic validation

We investigated subcellular positioning and dynamic expression profiles for scMR-colocalization-derived candidate genes using human whole-blood single-cell transcriptomes. Analyses leveraged the GSE269117 dataset, comprising scRNA-seq profiles from 4 migraine patients and 4 healthy controls (all without diagnosed allergies or ADs) [24, 25]. Identified clusters encompassed major immune-relevant populations, including macrophages, B cells, T cells, and their associated subsets. Transcriptional abundance of prioritized targets was profiled in diverse cellular subtypes and states. Feature plots enabled graphical representation of expression signatures, quantifying signal intensity and cellular expression fractions. Expression patterns of the candidate genes were visualized across cell types and states.

PheWAS analysis for safety profiling

To systematically evaluate the potential pleiotropy and off-target effects of candidate genes, we conducted a phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) using exome data from approximately 500,000 individuals in the UK Biobank. This data, publicly accessible via the AstraZeneca PheWAS Portal (https://azphewas.com/), integrates genetic association results for ~ 13,000 binary phenotypes and ~ 5,000 continuous clinical measurements. For each candidate gene, we retrieved all phenome-wide significant associations to exclude any unexpected systemic effects potentially impacting their suitability as therapeutic targets. We controlled the risk of false positives by applying Bonferroni correction to the results of each test.

Pharmacological repurposing and PPI analysis

Given that most therapeutic drugs target proteins, we prioritized candidate genes exhibiting strong colocalization signals with migraine as potential druggable targets. We systematically assessed the repurposing potential of approved or clinical-stage compounds by integrating evidence from DrugBank (https://go.drugbank.com/) and the Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb, https://dgidb.org/). This approach focused on candidates with established safety profiles to accelerate their translation into migraine therapeutics.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) plays a critical role in biological processes, including cellular function, signaling, and disease mechanisms. Exploring PPIs within the context of biopharmaceutical design can reveal complex networks and interdependencies among drug targets. To elucidate potential interactions between candidate gene products and established migraine therapeutic targets, we analyzed validated drug target information using the GeneMANIA platform (https://genemania.org/). By constructing a PPI network, we aimed to identify functional connections between proteins encoded by candidate genes and proteins targeted by existing migraine treatments. This approach not only reveals complex target networks but also provides a platform for investigating the roles of biological signaling and energy metabolism responses in migraine pathophysiology.

Results

Selection of instruments for immune cell-specific gene analysis

We first acquired gene expression profiles across 14 immune cell types, using donor samples spanning 643 to 982 individuals. We observed that CD4 NC (77%), CD8 ET (47%), and Mono C (53%) accounted for the highest proportions within their respective cell type compartments (Fig. 2). Other innate and adaptive immune cell types exhibited relatively smaller proportions, including CD8 NC, CD4 ET, CD4 + T cells expressing SOX4 (CD4 SOX4), CD8 S100B, NK, NK R, B IN, B Mem, Mono NC, DC and Plasma. cis-eQTLs associated with gene expression in these cell types were selected. A total of 26,597 unique genes harbored cis-eQTLs meeting the initial screening criteria. To construct robust and reliable instrumental variables (IVs), we applied stringent quality control: a genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5 × 10−08) and linkage disequilibrium (LD)-based clumping (r² < 0.001, clumping window: 10,000 kb). This process identified 9,117 independent immune cell-specific IVs for downstream analysis (Supplementary Table 3). Most genes were linked to one IV, whereas a minor proportion exhibited several independent IVs, strengthening the robustness of causal inference.

Causal associations between candidate gene expression and migraine

To investigate causal relationships between immune cell type-specific gene expression and migraine, we performed scMR analysis. Significant causal associations were identified for four genes following Bonferroni correction. Increased expression of PR/SET domain 11 (PRDM11) in NK was associated with a higher risk of migraine (Odd ratio [OR]: 1.001, 95% confidence Interval [CI]: 1.001, 1.002, PBonferroni < 0.001). Increased expression of chromosome 6 open reading frame 25 (C6orf25) in B IN was also associated with a higher migraine risk (OR: 1.002, 95% CI: 1.001, 1.003, PBonferroni < 0.001). Conversely, increased expression of vimentin (VIM) in CD8 ET demonstrated a protective effect against migraine (OR: 0.998, 95% CI: 0.995, 0.999, PBonferroni < 0.001). Increased expression of fibroblast growth factor receptor like 1 (FGFRL1) in CD4 ET was also associated with reduced migraine risk (OR: 0.998, 95% CI: 0.996, 0.999, PBonferroni < 0.001. More results for all tested associations are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Co-localization analysis of candidate genes with migraine

To further validate the identified candidate genes and determine if they share causal genetic variants with migraine, we performed Bayesian colocalization analysis. The posterior probability for a shared causal variant (PP.H4) was 0.996 for PRDM11, 0.996 for VIM, 0.985 for FGFRL1, and 0.990 for C6orf25, exceeding the threshold of 0.90 for all four genes (Supplementary Tables 5 & Fig. 3).

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling of migraine-colocalized targets

To explore the spatial and cellular expression patterns of candidate genes implicated in migraine, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from migraine patients and healthy controls using the GSE269117 dataset. UMAP-based clustering identified five major cell populations: macrophages, B cells, T cells (predominantly CD4 + T cells), NK cells, and plasma cells. These clusters were subsequently annotated (Fig. 4A). Using established quality control (QC) criteria, we selected and filtered cells before normalizing, scaling, and identifying highly variable features (Fig. 4B). This process identified a subset of features exhibiting high intercellular variability across the dataset (Fig. 4C). Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed for dimensionality reduction, focusing on cell cycle-related genes. Principal components (PCs) were extracted (Fig. 4D), allowing us to retain maximal information from the original data while reducing the dimensionality for downstream analysis. Furthermore, we identified the primary sources of heterogeneity within the dataset to determine the most informative PCs for subsequent biological interpretation (Supplementary Fig. 1A-D).

PheWAS analysis identifies safety profile of candidate genes

We conducted a PheWAS to evaluate the potential off-target effects and pleiotropic associations of the four candidate genes. This analysis assessed whether these genes exhibited significant associations with adverse health outcomes across a broad spectrum of clinical conditions. Following Bonferroni adjustment, no statistically significant associations linking the four prioritized genes to adverse clinical outcomes were observed (P < 1.25 × 10−02). Fig. 6 displays all tested associations, and the visual grouping into OR > 1 and OR < 1 was used for clarity to distinguish between risk-increasing and protective associations. This indicates a low potential risk for unexpected adverse effects if these genes are targeted therapeutically.

Drug repurposing for candidate genes and PPI network analysis

Database interrogation of DrugBank and DGIdb revealed approved agents; investigational candidates targeting prioritized genes were also detected. For VIM, the approved drug Artenimol and the investigational compound Phenethyl isothiocyanate (currently in clinical trials) were identified. For FGFRL1, the clinical-stage compound Brivanib was identified (Supplementary Table 6). A PPI network was constructed between the candidate gene products and the targets of approved migraine therapeutics. This analysis revealed 35 genes potentially involved in interactions, interconnected through various association types: co-expression (10.38%), shared protein domains (31.70%), co-localization (5.12%), and physical interactions (43.31%) (Supplementary Tables 7 & Supplementary Fig. 2). Functional analysis of the PPI network suggested potential functional links between the candidate genes and molecular mechanisms underlying migraine pathogenesis.

Discussion

This study first establishes an integrative framework combining single-cell multi-omics data, scMR, colocalization, and drug repositioning strategies. We systematically identified four high-priority target genes (PRDM11, VIM, FGFRL1, and C6orf25) causally linked to migraine susceptibility across 14 immune cell types, sharing common causal variants with migraine GWAS signals. scRNA-seq analysis revealed distinct expression patterns of these genes within key immune cell populations (NK, CD8 + T, CD4 + T, B cells) in migraine patients. Phenome-wide analysis revealed minimal off-target risks, affirming the safety of these targets. Critically, VIM and FGFRL1 were linked to approved or clinical-stage compounds, highlighting their immediate therapeutic potential. Although PRDM11 and C6orf25 currently lack direct drug repurposing candidates, their strong genetic associations with migraine underscore their roles in distinct immunopathological pathways. PRDM11 may regulate NK cell-mediated neuroinflammation, while C6orf25 could influence B cell maturation and autoantibody production. These findings provide novel biological insights and may guide future investigations into targeted immunomodulatory strategies, such as antibody-based therapies or small molecule screening, even in the absence of existing repurposable drugs. Collectively, this work provides novel insights into migraine’s immunogenetic basis and proposes a precision immunophenotype-driven drug repositioning strategy.

While our study identifies peripheral immune mechanisms, the core pathophysiology of migraine is undoubtedly centered on the trigeminovascular system and central nervous system. A legitimate question is whether findings from peripheral blood can elucidate central disease processes. We argue that they can, as peripheral and central immune compartments are not isolated. Systemic immune activation can sensitize the trigeminal nociceptive pathway, alter blood-brain barrier permeability, and facilitate pain signaling [26, 27]. Clinical studies consistently report elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines and altered immune cell profiles in the blood of migraine patients, supporting the biological plausibility of our approach [28, 29]. Thus, our findings likely capture systemic immune contributors that interact with central mechanisms to influence migraine susceptibility. While not a direct mirror of intracranial processes, peripheral immune profiling provides a feasible and powerful paradigm for identifying novel, therapeutically targetable pathways in a non-invasive manner.

PRDM11, a PR domain-containing transcriptional regulator implicated in epigenetic modulation and cancer [30], plays a critical role in lymphocyte differentiation [28]. It negatively regulates Th1/Th17 differentiation by suppressing enhancer activity of pro-inflammatory genes (e.g., TNF-α, IFN-γ). Our findings suggest a potential role in NK cell function regulation. Elevated serum neuron-specific enolase levels during migraine attacks [31] align with the hypothesis that PRDM11 overexpression may upregulate NK cytotoxicity genes, potentially contributing to trigeminal ganglion neuron damage and chronic neuropathic pain.

VIM, a cytoskeletal protein acting as a critical neuro-immune interface, emerges as a potential therapeutic target in various neurological disorders (spinal cord injury, stroke, meningitis, glioma) [32, 33]. It may exert neuroprotective effects by regulating effector memory T cell migration, signaling, and neurovascular inflammation. Within neuroinflammatory contexts, VIM stabilizes intracellular CXCL10/CXCR3 signaling, enhancing T cell chemotaxis [33, 34]. Furthermore, VIM-high macrophages promote an immunosuppressive microenvironment via interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) secretion and Treg enhancement [35]. Delineating VIM’s cell-specific mechanisms offers promise for precise immune interventions in headache and neurodegenerative disorders.

FGFRL1, a unique truncated receptor lacking the intracellular tyrosine kinase domain, functions as a decoy receptor [36]. Its upregulation in SNCAA53T models of Parkinson’s disease (PD) and prior evidence link it to migraine, Alzheimer, and PD [37–39]. Future research must bridge the gap between these mechanistic insights and clinical translation, advancing FGFRL1 as a therapeutic target for immune and neurological disorders.

C6orf25 (G6B/MPIG6B), an immunoglobulin superfamily member, is implicated in platelet inhibition and megakaryocyte signaling [40]. Emerging genomic and proteomic evidence underscores its role in immune regulation, particularly hematopoiesis and B cell maturation [41, 42]. Aberrant B cell maturation driven by C6orf25 dysregulation could break self-tolerance, generating autoantibodies targeting neuronal or vascular antigens, potentially activating meningeal nociceptors or complement-mediated inflammation [28]. While direct evidence linking C6orf25 to headache disorders is lacking, future studies should elucidate its tissue-specific interactions within migraine-relevant B cell subsets [43].

Our scMR analysis reveals highly specific gene-cell type associations, moving beyond viewing the immune system as a monolithic entity in migraine pathophysiology. Instead, we pinpoint discrete immune subpopulations exerting distinct mechanistic roles. Strong colocalization evidence supports a direct causal link between migraine risk variants and cis-regulation of gene expression within precisely defined immune cell contexts. scRNA-seq further implicates these genes in critical intercellular communication pathways, suggesting they mediate key signals within the neuro-immune-metabolic axis. Thus, these four genes represent high-confidence, genetically validated therapeutic targets, providing a robust foundation for developing cell type-selective immunomodulatory strategies for migraine.

Several limitations need to be considered. First, our findings primarily derive from European-ancestry GWAS data, which limits the extrapolation of the findings to other populations. Second, although peripheral blood provides an accessible and informative tissue for studying systemic immunity, it does not fully capture the intricate immune processes within the trigeminal ganglion, meninges, or brain parenchyma—sites considered core to migraine pathogenesis. The causal relationships we identified between peripheral immune gene expression and migraine risk likely operate through intermediary mechanisms, such as cytokine signaling or cellular migration. Future studies integrating data from migraine-relevant tissues (e.g., from animal models or bio-specimens like cerebrospinal fluid) are crucial to bridge this gap and validate the central connections of our peripheral findings. Third, although cell communication analysis revealed potential interactions, deeper mechanistic understanding is needed to elucidate how the identified genes specifically regulate these complex intercellular networks. Additionally, the databases used in this study do not differentiate between migraine patients’ attack and interictal (between attacks) phases. This distinction is crucial as immune system dynamics may differ between these two states. Future efforts should integrate spatial transcriptomics, functional perturbations, and detailed pathway analysis to establish causal links between target gene dysregulation in specific immune cells, altered signaling networks, and migraine pain pathways. Specifically, we note that ICD-10 codes are administratively assigned and may not always reflect diagnoses confirmed by neurologists or headache specialists, which could introduce misclassification bias. Despite this limitation, the use of ICD codes is standard in large-scale GWAS due to the breadth of data available, and reference prior validation studies supporting their use in genetic analyses.

Conclusions

This study successfully establishes a multi-omics integrative framework that identifies immune cell type-specific genetic drivers of migraine. We identified several mechanistically novel targets with high therapeutic potential and pinpointed repurposable drug candidates. These findings lay a solid foundation for developing next-generation, immunophenotype-guided precision therapeutic strategies for migraine.
