The Loring–Schulz-Baldes Spectral Localizer Revisited
Gregory Berkolaiko

Abstract
The spectral localizer, introduced by Loring in 2015 and Loring and Schulz-Baldes in 2017, is a method to compute the (infinite volume) topological invariant of a quantum Hamiltonian on ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} , as the signature of the (finite) localizer matrix. We present a direct and elementary spectral-theoretic proof treating the d = 1 d=1 and d = 2 d=2 cases on an almost equal footing. Moreover, we re-interpret the localizer as a higher-dimensional topological insulator via the bulk-edge correspondence.



1
Introduction

In the study of topological insulators
[
8
]
, quantum mechanical Hamiltonians (or their associated Fermi projections) are labelled by a topological index: a continuous map from the space of such systems into a discrete group. The most well-known example being the two-dimensional Chern number, which also corresponds to an experimentally measurable quantity, the Hall conductivity. Mathematically, these indices arise in various forms: as winding numbers of functions, as Chern characteristic classes of vector bundles, as index-pairings between K-theory group elements and a Dirac operator, or as Fredholm indices of certain operators on a Hilbert space. With no exception, all of these topological indices are zero if the system is truncated to a finite volume in real space. Physically, the intuition for this is provided by a finite-width Hall insulator: it will have edge currents running in opposite directions on its two opposite boundaries, producing zero in total. Mathematically, one perspective on this problem is afforded by the Fredholm index being zero for any finite square matrix: by the rank-nullity theorem, the dimension of the kernel equals the dimension of the cokernel. Another way to view this issue is to note that all topological indices may be written in the form
\operatorname{tr}([A,B])
for appropriate operators
A,B
(for instance, the Chern number may be written as
2\pi\operatorname{i}\operatorname{tr}([P\Lambda_{1}P,P\Lambda_{2}P])
with
P
the Fermi projection and
\Lambda_{j}\equiv\chi_{\mathbb{N}}(X_{j})
,
j=1,2
). If these are truncated to finite space so that
A,B
are matrices, then
\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(BA)
and the expression vanishes.

The issue just described poses a problem when trying to run numerical calculations on topological insulators. To be sure, most toy models in physics are
translation invariant
, in which case the indices reduce to winding of vector bundles, which can be evaluated numerically despite the system being infinite volume. However, for
disordered
systems, it is not really clear how to proceed. To that end, in pioneering studies
[
14
,
10
]
, Loring, and later, Loring and Schulz-Baldes introduced a new way to run finite-volume calculations:
the spectral localizer
. In this context, one should also mention
[
19
,
22
,
9
]
which offer alternative perspectives on this question.

The spectral localizer construction is as follows:

•

Add to the Hamiltonian an
unbounded
self-adjoint perturbation which however keeps the gap open due to its special structure.

•

Truncate the result to a finite Hermitian matrix. The truncation is performed where the perturbation is large; since the low energy eigenfunctions are exponentially suppressed in area of large perturbation, truncation has a vanishingly small effect on the gap states.

•

Compute the signature (the number of positive minus the number of negative eigenvalues) of the truncated matrix.

The result is twice the topological index of the infinite-volume Hamiltonian one had started with. The first proof used K-theory of fuzzy spheres (for
d=1
using the eta invariant)
[
10
,
13
]
and the second proof
[
23
,
12
]
used Phillips’ theorem connecting index pairings and the spectral flow
[
17
]
.

Our goal here is to re-derive the Loring–Schulz-Baldes result, motivate it via the bulk-edge correspondence and provide an elementary spectral flow-based argument that treats both odd and even dimensions in a unified way (cf.
[
11
,
15
]
which separate into even and odd
d
). Moreover, our choice of the perturbation is slightly different from the original one, leading to more convenient estimates and a bounded infinite-volume localizer.

1.2
The Loring–Schulz-Baldes spectral localizer construction

We describe now the spectral localizer construction and the theorem that relates
the signature of its finite volume truncation to the Zak phase

\operatorname{Zak}(H)
and to the Chern number
\operatorname{Chern}(H)
.

For any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\ell}:=\Set{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}{\left\lVert x\right\rVert\leq\ell}

be the box of radius
\ell

and

\mathcal{H}_{\ell}:=\mathbb{C}^{{\mathcal{B}_{\ell}}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{N}

(1.6)

be the finite-volume Hilbert space based on
{\mathcal{B}_{\ell}}
. Viewing

\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
as a subspace of
\mathcal{H}
, let

\displaystyle J_{\ell}:\mathcal{H}_{\ell}\to\mathcal{H}

be the inclusion operator (i.e.,
J_{\ell}

extends a wave-function by zero outside the finite box). Then

J_{\ell}^{*}J_{\ell}:\mathcal{H}_{\ell}\to\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
is the identity and

J_{\ell}J_{\ell}^{\ast}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is the orthogonal projector onto

\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
. With this notation,

\displaystyle H_{\ell}:=J_{\ell}^{\ast}HJ_{\ell}

(1.7)

is
the finite-volume (Dirichlet) truncation of
H
, a finite
matrix. We similarly define the truncation
S_{\ell}
, a
finite matrix.

Let
X_{\ell}
be the position operator on the finite box, amended to be
invertible. More precisely, in
d=1
,

X_{\ell}:\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{B}_{\ell}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{N/2}\to\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{B}_{\ell}}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{N/2}\qquad X_{\ell}(\delta_{x}\otimes v)=\begin{cases}x(\delta_{x}\otimes v),&\ x\neq 0,\\
\delta_{0}\otimes v,&x=0.\end{cases}

(1.8)

In
d=2
we use the

complex-valued position operator
,

X_{\ell}:\mathcal{H}_{\ell}\to\mathcal{H}_{\ell}\qquad X_{\ell}(\delta_{x,y}\otimes v)=\begin{cases}(x+\operatorname{i}y)(\delta_{x,y}\otimes v),&\ x+\operatorname{i}y\neq 0,\\
\delta_{0,0}\otimes v,&x+\operatorname{i}y=0.\end{cases}

(1.9)

We define the finite-volume localizer by

\displaystyle L_{\kappa,\ell}:=\begin{cases}\left(1-\kappa\right)\begin{bmatrix}0&S_{\ell}^{\ast}\\
S_{\ell}&0\end{bmatrix}+\kappa\begin{bmatrix}X_{\ell}&0\\
0&-X_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}&d=1\\
\left(1-\kappa\right)\begin{bmatrix}H_{\ell}&0\\
0&-H_{\ell}\end{bmatrix}+\kappa\begin{bmatrix}0&X_{\ell}^{\ast}\\
X_{\ell}&0\end{bmatrix}&d=2\end{cases}

(1.10)

on
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
or
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
respectively. Note
that in the
d=2

the original degrees of freedom.

Theorem 1.1

(Loring–Schulz-Baldes)
.

Let
H
be a Hamiltonian obeying the locality condition

Equation

1.1
with locality constants
C<\infty
,
\mu>0
. Assume it is
gapped:

\operatorname{gap}(H):=\left\lVert H^{-1}\right\rVert^{-1}>0,

(1.11)

and, in
d=1
, assume further it is
chiral. Let

\kappa_{\star}:=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\operatorname{gap}(H)^{2}}{\operatorname{gap}(H)^{2}+D^{d}},\qquad D:=\frac{C}{\cosh(\mu)-1}.

(1.12)

Then, for all

\ell>\frac{\left\lVert H\right\rVert}{\kappa_{\star}}\max\Set{2,1+\frac{8\left\lVert H\right\rVert}{\operatorname{gap}(H)}},

(1.13)

we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Signature}(L_{\kappa_{\star},\ell})=\begin{cases}\operatorname{Zak}(H)&d=1,\\
\operatorname{Chern}(H)&d=2.\end{cases}

(1.14)

We recall that for any invertible Hermitian matrix
M=M^{\ast}
,
\operatorname{Signature}(M)
is defined as the number of positive minus the number of negative eigenvalues.

1.3
Discussion and possible extensions

The main contribution of this work is to review the proof of the Loring–Schulz-Baldes correspondence between bulk topological indices and the signature of the spectral localizer, in the chiral one-dimensional and integer quantum Hall settings, using only elementary functional analysis and basic properties of the spectral flow. In particular, the proof does not rely on
K
-theory or on abstract tools employed in
[
10
,
13
,
12
]
. Instead, the argument is based on three ingredients: (i) locality estimates for commutators with (functions of) the position operator, (ii) quantitative lower bounds on spectral gaps of block operators, and (iii) the stability and homotopy invariance of the spectral flow. This makes the spectral localizer construction more accessible to readers with a standard analysis background, while at the same time yielding explicit bounds on the admissible parameters
\kappa_{\star}
and
\ell
in
1.1
in terms of the locality constants and the bulk gap.

A second conceptual point is the use of a bounded “flattened” position operator
f_{\ell}(X)
in place of the unbounded position operator employed in the original localizer of
[
14
,
10
]
. On the infinite-volume level, the resulting localizer
L_{\kappa,\ell}^{\infty}
is a bounded perturbation of the chiral block operator or of the doubled Hamiltonian, which is technically convenient when invoking spectral-flow arguments and when passing to finite-volume truncations. The price to pay is a slightly more elaborate truncation scheme, but the boundedness of
f_{\ell}(X)
allows us to keep uniform control of commutators and gaps as
\ell\to\infty
, which is crucial for obtaining quantitative estimates.

From the point of view of applications, our results justify the use of the spectral localizer as a robust finite-volume numerical diagnostic for one-dimensional chiral phases and two-dimensional Chern insulators. The fact that the signature of
L_{\kappa_{\star},\ell}
reproduces the bulk invariant for all sufficiently large boxes shows that no extrapolation to infinite volume is required, provided one works in the regime prescribed by
1.1
. While our bounds on
\ell
are probably far from optimal, they make the dependence on the gap and locality parameters completely explicit and may serve as a starting point for further optimization tailored to concrete models.

Let us now comment on two natural extensions that are not treated in the present work.

The mobility gap regime.
As recalled above, in the absence of a spectral gap but under a mobility gap (dynamical localization) assumption one can still define the bulk index in terms of the polar part
U=\operatorname{pol}(S)
of the Fermi unitary, both in the chiral
d=1
case and in the IQHE case
[
5
,
20
,
2
]
. In this regime the operator
\Lambda S\Lambda+\Lambda^{\perp}
need not be Fredholm, so one is forced to work with
\Lambda U\Lambda+\Lambda^{\perp}
instead. Our proof of
1.1
already passes through the index formula for
U
via
Proposition

2.4
, and the locality assumptions on
H
imply suitable Schatten-class bounds on commutators
[U,f_{\ell}(X)]
(compare
A.2
and
A.3
). It is therefore plausible that the argument can be extended to the mobility-gap setting, replacing the spectral gap by dynamical localization estimates in the sense of
[
5
,
20
]
. The main task would be to make sense of the notion of a spectral flow in the presence of dense pure point spectrum of Anderson localized states within the mobility gap. See also
[
21
]
for a somewhat different approach.

Alternative boundary conditions and truncations.
Throughout we work with Dirichlet-type truncations implemented by the partial isometries
J_{\ell}
, which amount to cutting the system to the box
\mathcal{B}_{\ell}
and extending wave-functions by zero outside. This choice is convenient because it decouples the interior and the exterior up to finite-rank errors, so that the infinite-volume localizer splits into a finite-volume part and a uniformly gapped complement. From a numerical perspective, however, periodic or twisted boundary conditions, as well as smoother spatial cut-offs, are often preferable. Our proof suggests that the specific choice of truncation is not essential: the key property is that the finite-volume localizer can be realized as a finite-rank perturbation of a uniformly gapped operator acting on the complement, with commutator bounds that are uniform in
\ell
. It should therefore be possible to adapt the argument to periodic boxes (with flux twists), to more general shapes, or to window functions with soft edges, at the cost of a slightly more involved bookkeeping of the off-diagonal terms. We do not attempt to develop such generalizations here.

Further directions.
Beyond these two immediate extensions, several other directions appear promising. One is to treat higher spatial dimensions and symmetry classes beyond the complex chiral and unitary classes considered here, in particular real symmetry classes with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
indices, where spectral localizers for
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
invariants have already been constructed in
[
18
,
12
]
. Another is to adapt the present approach to Floquet systems, in which the fundamental object is a unitary time-evolution operator rather than a static Hamiltonian, and where bulk indices are again naturally expressed in terms of Fredholm indices of half-space compressions. Finally, it would be interesting to investigate whether the bulk-edge correspondence interpretation developed in
Section

3
can make sense in higher dimensions, in particular two dimensions.

using the double-commutator formula for the Chern number:

\operatorname{Chern}\left(\begin{bmatrix}0&U^{\ast}\\
U&0\end{bmatrix}\pm\kappa\sigma_{3}\right)=-\frac{1}{8}\int_{\kappa\in\mathbb{R}}\operatorname{d}\!{\kappa}\operatorname{tr}_{\ell^{2}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)}\left(\Sigma_{\kappa}^{\pm}\left[\left[\Lambda,\Sigma_{\kappa}^{\pm}\right],\partial_{\kappa}\Sigma_{\kappa}^{\pm}\right]\right)\,.

We have

\displaystyle\left[\Lambda,\Sigma_{\kappa}^{\pm}\right]\equiv\left[\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda&0\\
0&\Lambda\end{bmatrix},\Sigma_{\kappa}^{\pm}\right]=\begin{bmatrix}0&\left(\kappa^{2}+1\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left[\Lambda,U^{\ast}\right]\\
\left(\kappa^{2}+1\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left[\Lambda,U\right]&0\end{bmatrix}

and

\displaystyle\partial_{\kappa}\Sigma_{\kappa}^{\pm}=\begin{bmatrix}\pm(\kappa^{2}+1)^{-3/2}&-\kappa(\kappa^{2}+1)^{-3/2}U^{\ast}\\
-\kappa(\kappa^{2}+1)^{-3/2}U&\mp(\kappa^{2}+1)^{-3/2}\end{bmatrix}

and so

\displaystyle\left[\left[\Lambda,\Sigma_{\kappa}^{\pm}\right],\partial_{\kappa}\Sigma_{\kappa}^{\pm}\right]=\begin{bmatrix}-\left(\kappa^{2}+1\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\kappa\left(\left[\Lambda,U^{\ast}\right]U-U^{\ast}\left[\Lambda,U\right]\right)&\mp 2\left(\kappa^{2}+1\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\left[\Lambda,U^{\ast}\right]\\
\pm 2\left(\kappa^{2}+1\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\left[\Lambda,U\right]&-\left(\kappa^{2}+1\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\kappa\left(\left[\Lambda,U\right]U^{\ast}-U\left[\Lambda,U^{\ast}\right]\right)\end{bmatrix}\,.

But
\left[\Lambda,U^{\ast}\right]U-U^{\ast}\left[\Lambda,U\right]=2U^{\ast}\left[U,\Lambda\right]
and similarly with
U^{\ast}
,
and hence

\displaystyle\left[\left[\Lambda,\Sigma_{\kappa}^{\pm}\right],\partial_{\kappa}\Sigma_{\kappa}^{\pm}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left(\kappa^{2}+1\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\begin{bmatrix}\kappa U^{\ast}\left[\Lambda,U\right]&\mp\left[\Lambda,U^{\ast}\right]\\
\pm\left[\Lambda,U\right]&\kappa U\left[\Lambda,U^{\ast}\right]\end{bmatrix}\,.

From this we find

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\kappa}^{\pm}\left[\left[\Lambda,\Sigma_{\kappa}^{\pm}\right],\partial_{\kappa}\Sigma_{\kappa}^{\pm}\right]=2\left(1+\kappa^{2}\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\begin{bmatrix}\pm U^{\ast}\left[\Lambda,U\right]&0\\
0&\mp U\left[\Lambda,U^{\ast}\right]\end{bmatrix}\,.

When we now integrate over
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
, we obtain, using

\int_{\kappa\in\mathbb{R}}2\left(1+\kappa^{2}\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\operatorname{d}\!{\kappa}=4\,,

that

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}=\pm\frac{-1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(U^{\ast}\left[\Lambda,U\right]-U\left[\Lambda,U^{\ast}\right]\right)=\mp\operatorname{tr}\left(U^{\ast}\left[\Lambda,U\right]\right)=\pm\operatorname{index}\left(\mathbb{\Lambda}U\right)\,.

(3.4)

We learn that

\displaystyle\operatorname{index}\left(\mathbb{\Lambda}S\right)=\pm\operatorname{Chern}\left(H\pm\kappa\sigma_{3}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\operatorname{Chern}\left(H+\kappa\sigma_{3}\right)-\operatorname{Chern}\left(H-\kappa\sigma_{3}\right)\right]\,.

(3.5)

3.2
The bulk-edge correspondence

Using the bulk-edge correspondence
we know that the difference of the Chern numbers between the left
and the right of
L_{\kappa}^{\pm}
should yield the edge index of the interface domain-wall operator
L_{\kappa}
.
Since we have the special case of
L_{\kappa}
a fiber operator in

\kappa
, the edge index is the spectral flow
[
7
]
of
\mathbb{R}\ni\kappa\mapsto L_{\kappa}
, i.e.,

\operatorname{i}\operatorname{tr}_{x,\kappa}\left(\mathbb{R}\ni\kappa\mapsto g^{\prime}\left(L_{\kappa}\right)\partial_{\kappa}L_{\kappa}\right)\,.

We have thus established

\displaystyle\operatorname{index}\left(\mathbb{\Lambda}S\right)=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Sf}\left(\mathbb{R}\ni\kappa\mapsto L_{\kappa}\right)\,.

Finally one may use symmetry to go from the flow on
\mathbb{R}
to the flow on
[0,\infty)
: first note that

\displaystyle\sigma_{3}L_{\kappa}\sigma_{3}=-H+\kappa\operatorname{sgn}(X)\sigma_{3}=-\left(H-\kappa\operatorname{sgn}(X)\sigma_{3}\right)=-L_{-\kappa}.

But the spectral flow is unitarily invariant and odd under overall sign, we find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Sf}\left(\mathbb{R}\ni\kappa\mapsto L_{\kappa}\right)=\operatorname{Sf}\left([0,\infty)\ni\kappa\mapsto L_{\kappa}\right)

and hence the proof of
3.1
is now complete.

Appendix A
Locality estimates

A.1
Commutator estimates

Lemma A.1
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{d})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{N})
be exponentially local as in

\displaystyle\left\lVert A_{xy}\right\rVert\leq C\exp\left(-\mu\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert\right)\qquad(x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d})\,.

Then for all
j=1,\cdots,d
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left[A,X_{j}\right]\right\rVert\leq C\frac{\coth\left(\frac{\mu}{2\sqrt{d}}\right)^{d-1}}{\cosh\left(\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{d}}\right)-1}<\infty\,.

Proof.

We use the Holmgren-Schur bound

\displaystyle\left\lVert A\right\rVert\leq\sqrt{\sup_{i}\sum_{j}\left|A_{ij}\right|}\sqrt{\sup_{j}\sum_{i}\left|A_{ij}\right|}

(A.1)

to estimate

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left\lVert\left[A,X_{j}\right]_{xy}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left|x_{j}-y_{j}\right|C\exp\left(-\mu\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\left|x_{j}-y_{j}\right|C\exp\left(-\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{d}}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=C\frac{\coth\left(\frac{\mu}{2\sqrt{d}}\right)^{d-1}}{\cosh\left(\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{d}}\right)-1}\,.

∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{N})
be exponentially local as in

\displaystyle\left\lVert A_{xy}\right\rVert\leq C\exp\left(-\mu\left|x-y\right|\right)\qquad(x,y\in\mathbb{Z})\,.

Let
f_{\ell}
be defined as in (
2.1
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}\ni x\mapsto\begin{cases}1&x=0,\\
x&1\leq|x|<\ell,\\
\ell\operatorname{sgn}(x)&|x|\geq\ell,\end{cases}

(A.2)

with

\displaystyle\operatorname{sgn}(x)\equiv\begin{cases}1&x\geq 0\\
-1&x<0\end{cases}\qquad(x\in\mathbb{R})\,.

(A.3)

Then

\displaystyle\sup_{\ell}\left\lVert\left[A,f_{\ell}(X)\right]\right\rVert\leq\frac{C}{\cosh\left(\mu\right)-1}

(A.4)

and

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left[A,f_{\ell}(X)\right]\right\rVert_{1}\leq\ell\left\lVert\left[A,\operatorname{sgn}(X)\right]\right\rVert_{1}+2N\left\lVert A\right\rVert\ell\left(2\ell+1\right)+4N\left\lVert A\right\rVert\,.

(A.5)

Proof.

We estimate again via Holmgren
eq.

A.1

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|f_{\ell}(x)-f_{\ell}(y)\right|C\exp\left(-\mu\left|x-y\right|\right)\,.

Now note that
\left|f_{\ell}(x)-f_{\ell}(y)\right|\leq\left|x-y\right|
uniformly in
\ell
, so using the proof above we find an upper bound on the above expression as

\displaystyle\frac{C}{\cosh\left(\mu\right)-1}\,.

To get the trace-class bound, we see that

\displaystyle f_{\ell}(X)=\ell\operatorname{sgn}(X)+\chi_{[-\ell,\ell]}(X)\left(X-\ell\operatorname{sgn}(X)\right)+P_{0}

where
P_{0}:=\left(\delta_{0}\otimes\delta_{0}^{\ast}\right)\otimes\mathds{1}_{N\times N}
. We know that
\left\lVert\left[A,\operatorname{sgn}(X)\right]\right\rVert_{1}<\infty

[
6
, Lemma 2 (b)]
and

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left[A,\chi_{[-\ell,\ell]}(X)\left(X-\ell\operatorname{sgn}(X)\right)\right]\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2N\left\lVert A\right\rVert\ell\left(2\ell+1\right)\,.

∎

Lemma A.3
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})\otimes\mathbb{C}^{N})
be exponentially local as in

\displaystyle\left\lVert A_{xy}\right\rVert\leq C\exp\left(-\mu\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert\right)\qquad(x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{2})\,.

Let
f_{\ell}(X)
be defined via

\displaystyle f_{\ell}(X)=\ell L+\chi_{[-\ell,\ell]^{2}}(X)\left(X-\ell L\right)+P_{0}

(A.6)

with
L:=\operatorname{e}^{\operatorname{i}\arg\left(X_{1}+\operatorname{i}X_{2}\right)}
.
Then

\displaystyle\sup_{\ell}\left\lVert\left[A,f_{\ell}(X)\right]\right\rVert\leq\left(\frac{C}{\cosh\left(\mu\right)-1}\right)^{2}

and

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left[A,f_{\ell}(X)\right]\right\rVert_{3}\leq\ell\left\lVert\left[A,L\right]\right\rVert_{3}+2N\left\lVert A\right\rVert\ell\left(2\ell+1\right)^{2}+4N\left\lVert A\right\rVert\,.

The proof is identical to the one above and is thus omitted, here we
use that
\sup_{\ell}\left|f_{\ell}(x)-f_{\ell}(y)\right|\leq\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert
and that
\left\lVert\left[A,L\right]\right\rVert_{3}<\infty
(see e.g.
[
2
, Lemma A.1]
).

Appendix B
Estimates on gaps

For any (not necessarily self-adjoint) invertible operator
A
,

\operatorname{gap}(A)\equiv\left\lVert A^{-1}\right\rVert^{-1}=\mathrm{dist}(0,\spec(|A|))\,.

If
A
is self-adjoint, this is equivalent to
\operatorname{gap}(A)=\mathrm{dist}(0,\spec(A))
. For non-invertible operators
A
we set
\operatorname{gap}(A)=0
and note that
\operatorname{gap}(A)>0
if and only if
A
is invertible.

Lemma B.1

(Basic estimates on the gap)
.

The spectral gap satisfies the following estimates:

1.

If
A
is invertible then

\operatorname{gap}(A)=\sqrt{\operatorname{gap}(A^{\ast}A)}.

(B.1)

2.

If
A,B
are both invertible,

\operatorname{gap}(A)\operatorname{gap}(B)\leq\operatorname{gap}(AB)\leq\operatorname{gap}(A)\|B\|.

(B.2)

3.

\operatorname{gap}(A)-\|B\|\leq\operatorname{gap}(A+B)\leq\operatorname{gap}(A)+\|B\|.

(B.3)

4.

If
A
and
B
are non-negative definite,

\operatorname{gap}(A+B)\geq\operatorname{gap}(A)+\operatorname{gap}(B).

(B.4)

5.

\operatorname{gap}(A\oplus D)=\min\{\operatorname{gap}(A),\operatorname{gap}(D)\}.

(B.5)

Proof.

One has

\|(AB)^{-1}\|=\|B^{-1}A^{-1}\|\leq\|B^{-1}\|\|A^{-1}\|=\frac{1}{\operatorname{gap}(A)\operatorname{gap}(B)},

(B.6)

establishing the left side of (
B.2
). The right side
follows by using the left side to note that

\operatorname{gap}(AB)\operatorname{gap}(B^{-1})\leq\operatorname{gap}(ABB^{-1})=\operatorname{gap}(A).

To show the left side of (
B.3
), we note that the case
\operatorname{gap}(A)\leq\|B\|
is vacuous, therefore we assume
\operatorname{gap}(A)>\|B\|
, which implies that
A
is invertible and
\|A^{-1}B\|<1
.
The estimate (valid for
\|C\|<1
)

\left\|(\mathds{1}+C)^{-1}\right\|\leq\frac{1}{1-\|C\|}

(B.7)

gives

\operatorname{gap}\left(I+A^{-1}B\right)\geq 1-\|A^{-1}B\|\geq 1-\|A^{-1}\|\|B\|=1-\frac{\|B\|}{\operatorname{gap}(A)}.

(B.8)

The left side of (
B.3
) now follows by applying (
B.2
) to
A+B=A(\mathds{1}+A^{-1}B)
. Exchanging
A\mapsto A+B
,
B\mapsto-B
in the left side of (
B.3
) yields the right side of (
B.3
).

∎

Lemma B.2

(Gaps of block operators)
.

Let a self-adjoint
H
on
\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}^{\perp}
be given by

\displaystyle H=\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
B^{*}&D\end{bmatrix}\,.

(B.9)

Then

1.

We have a lower bound on the gap of the full operator in terms of the gaps of the diagonal blocks:

\operatorname{gap}(H)\geq\min\{\operatorname{gap}(A),\operatorname{gap}(D)\}-\|B\|.

(B.10)

2.

We have an upper bound on the gap of the full operator in terms of the Schur gap:

\operatorname{gap}(H)\leq\min\left(\Set{\operatorname{gap}(D),\operatorname{gap}\left(A-BD^{-1}B^{*}\right)}\right)\left(1+\left\|BD^{-1}\right\|\right)^{2}.

(B.11)

3.

If a self-adjoint
H
given by (
B.9
) satisfies
\operatorname{gap}(A)\leq\operatorname{gap}(D)
,

\operatorname{gap}(H)\geq\operatorname{gap}(A)-\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{gap}(A)}{\operatorname{gap}(D)}}\|B\|

(B.12)

We remark that the estimate (
B.10
) is useful if
B
is small, while (
B.11
) and (
B.12
) come into play when
D
has large gap.

Proof.

To show (
B.10
), we note that

\operatorname{gap}\left(\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
0&D\end{bmatrix}\right)=\min\{\operatorname{gap}(A),\operatorname{gap}(D)\},\qquad\left\|\begin{bmatrix}0&B\\
B^{*}&0\end{bmatrix}\right\|=\|B\|,

(B.13)

and apply (
B.3
).

To show (
B.11
), we write

H=\begin{bmatrix}\mathds{1}&BD^{-1}\\
0&\mathds{1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}A-BD^{-1}B^{*}&0\\
0&D\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathds{1}&0\\
D^{-1}B^{*}&\mathds{1}\end{bmatrix},

(B.14)

and use the right estimate in (
B.2
). According to (
B.5
), the gap of the middle matrix is bounded by
\operatorname{gap}\left(A-BD^{-1}B^{*}\right)
. The norms of the left and right matrices are bounded by
1+\left\|BD^{-1}\right\|
.

Finally, writing

H=\begin{bmatrix}\mathds{1}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathds{1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}A&\varepsilon B\\
\varepsilon B^{*}&\varepsilon^{2}D\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathds{1}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathds{1}\end{bmatrix},

(B.15)

we estimate (assuming
0<\varepsilon<1
and therefore the norm of the outside factors is
1
),

\operatorname{gap}(H)\geq\min\{\operatorname{gap}(A),\varepsilon^{2}\operatorname{gap}(D)\}-\varepsilon\|B\|.

(B.16)

Setting

\varepsilon=\sqrt{\frac{\operatorname{gap}(A)}{\operatorname{gap}(D)}}

(B.17)

yields (
B.12
).
∎

Lemma B.3
.

For any
s\in[0,1]
, let a self-adjoint operator
H_{s}
on
\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}^{\perp}
be given by

H_{s}=\begin{bmatrix}A&sB\\
sB^{*}&D\end{bmatrix}.

(B.18)

If

\displaystyle\operatorname{gap}(D)>\left\lVert B\right\rVert\max\left(\Set{1,\frac{\left\lVert B\right\rVert}{\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{gap}(H_{1})}}\right)

(B.19)

then
H_{s}
is invertible for all
s\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

Conditions (
B.19
) imply that
D
is invertible.
We can then write

H_{s}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathds{1}&sBD^{-1}\\
0&\mathds{1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}A-s^{2}BD^{-1}B^{*}&0\\
0&D\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathds{1}&0\\
sD^{-1}B^{*}&\mathds{1}\end{bmatrix}.

(B.20)

The matrices on the left and on the right are invertible because from (
B.19
) we get that
\|sBD^{-1}\|<1
.

From inequality (
B.11
) we get

\operatorname{gap}\left(A-BD^{-1}B^{*}\right)\geq\frac{\operatorname{gap}(H_{1})}{\left(1+\left\|BD^{-1}\right\|\right)^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{gap}(H_{1}),

and therefore by
Equation

B.3
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{gap}\left(A-s^{2}BD^{-1}B^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{gap}\left(A-BD^{-1}B^{*}\right)-\big(1-s^{2})\left\|BD^{-1}B^{*}\right\|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{gap}(H_{1})-\frac{\|B\|^{2}}{\operatorname{gap}(D)},

which is positive by (
B.19
). We conclude that the middle matrix in (
B.20
) is invertible for all
s\in[0,1]
, and so is
H_{s}
.
∎

Lemma B.4
.

Let a self-adjoint operator
H_{s}
on
\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}^{\perp}
be given by

\displaystyle L:=\begin{bmatrix}A&B^{\ast}\\
B&-A\end{bmatrix}

where
A=A^{\ast}
and
B
is general. Then

\operatorname{gap}(L)\geq\sqrt{\operatorname{gap}(A)^{2}+\operatorname{gap}(B)^{2}-\left\lVert[A,B]\right\rVert}.

(B.21)

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle L^{2}=\begin{bmatrix}A&B^{\ast}\\
B&-A\end{bmatrix}^{2}=\begin{bmatrix}A^{2}+\left|B\right|^{2}&0\\
0&A^{2}+\left|B^{\ast}\right|^{2}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0&[A,B^{\ast}]\\
-[A,B]&0\end{bmatrix}\,.

We use
B.1
to estimate

\displaystyle\operatorname{gap}\left(L^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{gap}\left(\begin{bmatrix}A^{2}+\left|B\right|^{2}&0\\
0&A^{2}+\left|B^{\ast}\right|^{2}\end{bmatrix}\right)-\left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}0&[A,B^{\ast}]\\
-[A,B]&0\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{gap}(A)^{2}+\operatorname{gap}(B)^{2}-\left\lVert[A,B]\right\rVert.

The conclusion for
\operatorname{gap}(L)
now follows by (
B.1
).
∎

Appendix C
A short review of the spectral flow

We give a brief review of the properties of the spectral flow we need; more details may be found in e.g. the textbook
[
4
]
.

Definition C.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable Hilbert space. Let
[0,1]\ni t\mapsto A_{t}
be an analytic family of self-adjoint Fredholm operators on
\mathcal{H}
such that
A_{0},A_{1}
are moreover invertible. We call such a family
a spectral-flow family
. Let
\Delta\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be the essential gap of the family, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(A_{t})\cap\Delta=\varnothing,\qquad t\in[0,1]\,.

We assume further that
\sigma(A_{i})\cap\Delta=\varnothing
for
i=0,1
and that
\Delta
contains an open interval about zero; let
g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\to[0,1])
be so that
g=1
above
\Delta
,
g=0
below
\Delta
and
\operatorname{supp}(g^{\prime})\subseteq\Delta
. Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{Sf}(A):=\int_{t\in[0,1]}\operatorname{tr}_{\mathcal{H}}\left(g^{\prime}(A_{t})\partial_{t}A_{t}\right)\operatorname{d}\!{t}\,.

(C.1)

Informally we interpret

\operatorname{tr}\left(g^{\prime}(A_{t})\partial_{t}A_{t}\right)=\partial_{t}\operatorname{tr}(g(A_{t}))
and apply the
fundamental theorem of calculus to get

\displaystyle\operatorname{Sf}(A)=\operatorname{tr}\left(g(A_{1})-g(A_{0})\right),

which counts the number of eigenvalues which have
transitioned from below to above the gap as we interpolate between
A_{0}
and

A_{1}
. We refer the reader to
[
16
]
or

[
4
, Sec. 4.1]
) for the extension of the
spectral flow to norm-continuous families. We also remark that the
interval
[0,1]
can be replaced with any other interval.

In this work we use the following fundamental properties of the
spectral flow.

Theorem C.2
.

For any spectral flow family
t\mapsto A_{t}
, the spectral flow
\operatorname{Sf}(A_{\cdot})
is:

1.

Well-defined (i.e., it is independent of the choices of
\Delta
and
g
above).

2.

Integer-valued:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Sf}(A)\in\mathbb{Z}\,.

3.

Additive
[
4
, Thm. 4.2.1(v)]
: If
A,B
are
two spectral flow families, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{Sf}(A\oplus B)=\operatorname{Sf}(A)+\operatorname{Sf}(B)\,.

4.

Concatenation-additive

[
4
, Thm. 4.2.1(ii)]
: If

[0,1]\ni t\mapsto A_{t}
is norm-continuous with
A_{0}
,

A_{0.5}
and
A_{1}
invertible, then

\operatorname{Sf}\big([0,1]\ni t\mapsto A_{t}\big)=\operatorname{Sf}\big([0,0.5]\ni t\mapsto A_{t}\big)+\operatorname{Sf}\big([0.5,1]\ni t\mapsto A_{t}\big).

(C.2)

5.

Homotopy invariant
[
4
, Thm. 4.2.2]
: Let

[0,1]\times[0,1]\ni(t,s)\mapsto h(t,s)
be a norm-continuous
family of bounded Fredholm operators. Consider two continuous paths

\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}:[0,1]\to[0,1]^{2}
with

\gamma_{1}(0)=\gamma_{2}(0)
and
\gamma_{1}(1)=\gamma_{2}(1)
. If

h\big(\gamma_{1}(0)\big)
and
h\big(\gamma_{1}(1)\big)
are
invertible, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{Sf}\Big([0,1]\ni t\mapsto h\big(\gamma_{1}(t)\big)\Big)=\operatorname{Sf}\Big([0,1]\ni t\mapsto h\big(\gamma_{2}(t)\big)\Big).
