Automated detection of cerebral microbleeds on ex-vivo MRI scans of community-based older adults
Authors: Grant Nikseresht, Arnold M. Evia, Gady Agam, David A. Bennett, Julie A. Schneider, Konstantinos Arfanakis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) are small hemosiderin deposits visible on T2*-weighted MRI that have been associated with cerebrovascular pathology, cognitive decline, and increased stroke risk. While CMBs have been studied extensively in living populations, their relationship to neuropathology assessed at autopsy remains incompletely understood. Large-scale MRI-pathology studies are needed to clarify these associations, but manual annotation of CMBs on ex-vivo MRI is time-consuming and labor-intensive, creating a critical bottleneck. Automated detection of CMBs on ex-vivo MRI in community-based older adults is particularly challenging due to low CMB prevalence, abundant mimics (e.g. air bubbles), and limited training data. We present the first comprehensive automated detection algorithm for CMBs on ex-vivo T2*-weighted MRI from community-based older adults. Our approach combines a novel multi-echo synthesis algorithm with self-supervised pretraining using fuzzy segmentation and confidence-aware learning to address data scarcity and class imbalance. The method successfully captures 90% of definite CMBs (unambiguous hypointensities clearly within brain tissue) and 83% of all CMBs (definite and possible CMBs combined) at 15 false positives per scan in a dataset of 287 community-based older adults. This represents a 46% improvement in average precision over the baseline approach using only real data and establishes a benchmark for this challenging detection problem. The proposed system enables partially automated annotation workflows that reduce manual review burden by 5 to 20-fold compared to feature-based approaches, making large-scale MRI and pathology studies feasible.

Introduction

Problem background

Cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) are small hemosiderin deposits that appear as round or ovoid hypointensities on T2*-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Greenberg et al., 2009; Wardlaw et al., 2013; Puy et al., 2021). CMBs on in-vivo MRI have been shown to be associated with cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) (Martinez-Ramirez et al., 2015; Guidoux et al., 2018; Pasi et al., 2019), infarcts (Graff-Radford et al., 2017), white matter hyperintensities (Graff-Radford et al., 2017; Wiegman et al., 2014), lower brain volume (Su et al., 2019), physical frailty (Chung et al., 2016), increased risk of stroke (Akoudad et al., 2015; Lau et al., 2017), cognitive decline (Akoudad et al., 2016; Ding et al., 2017), and increased mortality (Charidimou et al., 2018). CMBs occurring in individuals receiving anti-amyloid therapy, known as amyloid-related imaging abnormalities with hemosiderin deposition (ARIA-H), can present with clinical symptoms such as headache, confusion, nausea, and, in severe cases, death, and are considered sufficient to warrant interruption or discontinuation of treatment (Sperling et al., 2011). With three anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies now approved by the FDA for Alzheimer’s disease treatment, accurate CMB detection has become clinically urgent, as baseline CMB burden is a key predictor of ARIA risk (Greenberg et al., 2025). In the ex-vivo setting, a recent ex-vivo MRI and pathology study in 289 community-based older adults investigated the neuropathologic correlates of CMBs at autopsy and demonstrated an association between CMBs in the cerebrum and evidence of CAA as well as arteriolosclerosis (Nikseresht et al., 2023). Another ex-vivo MRI study on 3 false-positive CAA cases corroborated that arteriolosclerosis can generate lobar CMBs, and observed that these CMBs tended to appear in juxtacortical or subcortical white matter (Perosa et al., 2023). Furthermore, ex-vivo MRI research showed that CMBs in CAA patients are predominantly located in the cortical ribbon (van Veluw et al., 2016). Complementary in-vivo work has shown that CMB topography carries diagnostic value: intracortical lobar CMBs are strongly associated with CAA (OR=18.9), while subcortical lobar CMBs associate with hypertensive arteriolosclerosis (OR=10.9), suggesting that precise anatomical localization can help differentiate underlying microangiopathies (Kuo et al., 2024). Despite these findings, further studies examining the links between CMBs and neuropathology assessed at autopsy are still required to shed light on the role of CMBs in cerebral small vessel disease (SVD). It is essential to conduct these studies using ex-vivo rather than in-vivo MRI in conjunction with neuropathological assessment. Ex-vivo MRI offers two key advantages: (a) it eliminates the potential for additional pathological changes that may occur between in-vivo imaging and death, thereby reducing the risk of data mismatch; and (b) it mitigates the selection bias inherent to in-vivo MRI research, which tends to favor less-frail individuals. However, the process of manually annotating CMBs on ex-vivo MRI is labor-intensive and prone to error, posing a significant obstacle to the development of future MR-pathology studies.

Automated detection of CMBs has been proposed as a solution to reduce annotation time and increase sensitivity when compared to labeling without algorithmic assistance. Recent in-vivo methods have achieved impressive performance, with state-of-the-art approaches now reaching sensitivities above 90% with fewer than 1 false positive per scan on clinical populations (Kim et al., 2025; Tsuchida et al., 2024). Despite these advances, ex-vivo CMB detection in community-based older adults remains significantly more challenging for several reasons (Nikseresht et al., 2022). Pockets of air, caused by imaging the brain outside the skull, appear with high prevalence near the edge of the brain and in sulci and can cause dark, spherical partial volume effects on T2*-weighted MRI that often resemble cortical CMBs. Residual blood in brain vasculature ex-vivo introduces in many vessels intermittent, slightly elongated hypointensities on T2*-weighted MRI, which are less pronounced than the continuous vessel hypointensities seen on in-vivo T2*-weighted MRI due to blood flow. Both classes of mimics greatly outnumber CMBs in community-based older adults where there are typically fewer than 2 CMBs per scan (Nikseresht et al., 2023), which makes training a high-sensitivity deep learning classifier challenging due to a shortage of real CMB examples and an overwhelming number of potential false positives. This contrasts with in-vivo detection methods that tend to evaluate on participants taken from clinical populations with many more CMBs per scan caused by conditions such as severe cerebral amyloid angiopathy (Charidimou et al., 2017), traumatic brain injury (Koschmieder et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2019), stroke (Dou et al., 2016), or induced by radiation therapy (Bian et al., 2013). The use of susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI), which enhances hypointense structures such as CMBs (Ayaz et al., 2010; Shams et al., 2015), has been shown to improve CMB detection performance (Chesebro et al., 2021). However, SWI is less suitable for ex-vivo CMB annotation where the enhancement of mimics, particularly air bubbles, outweighs any advantages gained from the enhancement of CMBs. Related work in CMB detection

Early approaches to CMB detection utilized feature-based methods (Bian et al., 2013; Fazlollahi et al., 2015; Kuijf et al., 2012; Seghier et al., 2011) such as fast radial symmetry transform (FRST) (Bian et al., 2013; Kuijf et al., 2012) for localizing and identifying CMBs on MRI scans often combined with machine learning techniques such as support vector machines (SVM) (Bian et al., 2013) or random forest (Fazlollahi et al., 2015) for false positive reduction. Despite the fact that feature-based approaches lack the complexity of deep learning-based models, they are still useful as pre-processing techniques and in situations where training data is scarce such as community-based populations. For instance, in a recent paper by Chesebro et al., 2021, the authors propose a classification pipeline based on 2D features, such as Canny edge detection and Hough transforms followed by 3D Frangi and local entropy analyses, that achieves a sensitivity of 93% with 9.1 false positives per participant on in-vivo SWI scans in a community-based cohort of older adults.

More recent approaches to CMB detection have relied on variants of convolutional neural networks (CNNs). A two-stage algorithm is often utilized with an initial candidate selection step followed by a false positive reduction step (H. Chen et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2019; Dou et al., 2016; Sundaresan et al., 2023; al-Masni et al., 2020, Xia et al., 2024). Single-stage approaches have also shown promise, with early work using 2D U-Net on QSM and SWI reporting high correlation (r = 0.99) with ground truth on 24 participants (Rashid et al., 2021), and more recent methods incorporating hard sample prototype learning to reduce false positives from 14.7 to 0.56 per scan while maintaining 94.7% sensitivity (Kim et al., 2025). Multi-source training has emerged as a key strategy for improving generalization, with SHIVA-CMB achieving F1=0.74 and fewer than 1 false positive per scan using a 3D U-Net trained on 450 scans across 7 acquisition protocols from 6 cohort studies (Tsuchida et al., 2024). Transformer-based architectures such as UNETR++ have also been applied to CMB segmentation (Kwon et al., 2024a), though their effectiveness on small datasets remains limited. The synthesis of hypointense lesions onto MRI has also been shown to improve performance significantly in scenarios where access to real CMB examples is limited using both manually defined synthesis models (Nikseresht et al., 2022; Momeni et al., 2021a) and generative adversarial neural networks (GANs) for synthesis (Nikseresht et al., 2022; Momeni et al., 2021b), with recent work validating CycleGAN-based mimic generation for QSM-based detection (Xia et al., 2024). Transfer learning through pre-training on auxiliary tasks has also proven effective, including CSF segmentation (Dadar et al., 2022) and anatomically guided proxy tasks leveraging clinical prior knowledge about CMB spatial distributions (Kwon et al., 2024b). The current state-of-the-art approaches for in-vivo CMB detection achieve sensitivities between 88–95% with 0.56 to 2.9 false positives per scan (Kim et al., 2025; Tsuchida et al., 2024; Xia et al., 2024).

However, direct comparison of algorithm performance across studies is limited by the heterogeneity of validation datasets caused by differences in manual rating standards, the MRI modality used, and the clinical background of the study participants, among other factors. The only existing work on CMB detection on ex-vivo MRI in community-based older adults reported a sensitivity of 87% at 30 false positives per scan using 3D residual neural networks combined with iterative refinement of synthetic examples in a set of 120 participants (Nikseresht et al., 2022). The lack of availability of a general purpose CMB detection algorithm that can be used ex-vivo is a key obstacle in the development of future neuropathology studies examining the correlations between CMBs and pathologies found at autopsy.

Contributions

In this work, we develop an end-to-end CMB detection algorithm that leverages data-efficient learning techniques to address challenges inherent to ex-vivo MRI and community-based populations with low CMB prevalence. To begin, we present the first comprehensive deep learning framework for CMB detection on ex-vivo T2*-weighted MRI data from community-based older adults, where CMB prevalence is low and mimics are abundant, establishing a benchmark for this underexplored domain and enabling assisted annotation workflows that substantially reduce manual review burden. Second, we introduce a physics-informed CMB synthesis algorithm that leverages multi-echo signal modeling to generate realistic lesions while preserving T2* decay characteristics, demonstrating the value of domain-specific data augmentation for rare pathology detection. Third, we propose fuzzy segmentation (FuzzSeg), a self-supervised pretraining task derived from the synthesis algorithm, where the model learns to reconstruct the implantation kernel from synthetic examples, creating a unified framework linking pretraining to the downstream detection objective. Finally, we demonstrate the first application of confidence-aware learning to CMB detection.

Materials and methods

Participants

Older adults participating in four longitudinal cohort studies of aging, the Rush Memory and Aging Project (MAP), the Religious Orders Study (ROS) (Bennett et al., 2018), the Minority Aging Research Study (MARS) (Barnes et al., 2012), and the Clinical Core of the Rush Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center (Barnes et al., 2015), were included in this work. All studies were approved by an institutional review board at Rush University Medical Center. All participants provided written informed consent and signed an anatomical gift act. At the time of this project, 4808 participants of the parent studies had completed the baseline clinical evaluation. Of these, 1876 participants were autopsied and 1004 underwent ex-vivo MRI. Images of the first 320 participants to undergo ex vivo MRI were reviewed, 33 participants were excluded because of image artifacts or large abnormalities (large hemorrhages, tumors, or large infarcts), and the remaining 287 were included in this work. Demographic, clinical, and neuropathologic characteristics of the participants are shown in Table 2. The remaining 684 persons with ex vivo MRI data were not considered because CMB annotation was not completed by the time of this project.

Brain hemisphere preparation

After removal of the brain at autopsy, the cerebral hemisphere that contained the most visible pathology was selected for ex-vivo MRI and neuropathologic examination, while the contralateral hemisphere was frozen and stored. Within 30 min of removal from the skull, the chosen hemisphere was submerged in phosphate-buffered 4% paraformaldehyde solution and then refrigerated at 4 °C. Prior to ex-vivo MRI, the hemisphere was placed in a container of 4% paraformaldehyde solution with the medial aspect facing the bottom of the container and allowed to return to room temperature (Arfanakis et al., 2020; Kotrotsou et al., 2015). A detailed neuropathologic examination of the hemisphere was performed within 2 weeks of the ex-vivo MRI scan (Kotrotsou et al., 2015).

Ex-vivo MRI data acquisition

Collection of ex-vivo MRI data was done approximately 30 days postmortem using a Philips 3 Tesla MRI scanner using a three dimensional multi-echo gradient echo (GRE) sequence with 8–10 echoes per scan, TE1/ΔTE/TR=4.6/4.63/49.5ms, flip angle = 22 degrees, 65 sagittal slices, an acquired voxel-size of 1 × 1 × 1 mm3, and scan time = 8 m 29 s. The data used in this work can be accessed by submitting a request to www.radc.rush.edu.

Ex-vivo MRI data pre-processing

N4 bias field correction was applied by extracting the bias field from the first echo and applying it across all echoes. Raw images, I, were z-score normalized using the mean, μI, and standard deviation, σI, of the signal within tissue from the fourth echo as a reference using IN=I-μIσI. Additional feature maps were generated from the raw images to assist in CMB annotation and candidate selection. A T2* map, T^, was estimated for each scan by taking a mono-exponential fit of the signal from the first four even echoes to the MR signal equation given by Sei=S0ie−TEeT2*, where Sei is the raw signal (no N4 correction or z-score normalization) at echo e for voxel i,S0i is the raw signal at time zero, and TEe is the echo time at echo e. Modified Frangi filters (Frangi et al., 1998) were also applied independently to each echo in the raw images to enhance small, hypointense, spherical structures using the magnitude and direction of the ordered Hessian eigenvalues, λ1≤λ2≤λ3. The relative magnitude of drops in signal were summarized using R′B=λ′1+λ′2+λ′3 where λ′i=max0,λ1, which is high for structures with a large drop in local signal intensity in all three directions. Spherical structures were highlighted using RA′=λ′1/λ′3, which is high when the smallest and largest eigenvalues are both large and non-negative.

Manual CMB annotation

Manual annotation of CMBs was done on T2*-weighted ex-vivo MRI by an experienced rater blinded to all pathologic and clinical data. On these images, CMBs were defined as approximately spherical hypointensities (2 to 5 mm in diameter) that were predominantly surrounded by brain parenchyma on T2*-weighted images from earlier echoes (to distinguish them from air bubbles at the brain boundary) and exhibited a ‘blooming effect’ on later echoes. Voxels within the CMB region were also required to have relatively low estimated T2*-values compared to the surrounding tissue and a low T2* fitting error. CMBs were also characterized by having high values in both RA′ and RB′ Frangi components, which indicate a high degree of sphericality and relative hypointensity when compared to neighboring voxels. Histological validation of CMBs identified with this manual annotation approach was previously published (Nag et al., 2021). Each CMB was further classified by the rater as definite or possible. Definite CMBs exhibited unambiguous hypointensity clearly within brain tissue. Possible CMBs exhibited similar characteristics but with some degree of ambiguity, either in the degree of hypointensity relative to surrounding tissue or in their proximity to the brain boundary. Technical background on data synthesis, self-supervised learning, and uncertainty estimation

In domains where labeled data is scarce, data synthesis is a popular approach for increasing the size of labeled training sets (Nikolenko 2021). Data synthesis approaches can compensate for class imbalances by synthesizing rare cases (Beery et al., 2020; Fernández et al., 2011; Khan et al., 2019), be tuned to incorporate given feature or context information (Yang et al., 2018; Shrivastava et al., 2017; Momeni et al., 2021b; Sela et al., 2017), and, in the case of CMBs, can be developed without the need for training data (Nikseresht et al., 2022; Momeni et al., 2021a). A recent review of synthetic data in neuroimaging validated physics-based simulation approaches for generating synthetic brain MRI with lesions, demonstrating that models trained on synthetic data can generalize across scanners and acquisition protocols (Gopinath et al., 2024). Generative adversarial networks (GANs) have been proven to be effective for a variety of synthesis tasks in medical imaging including skin lesion synthesis (Behara et al., 2023), brain MRI synthesis (Bermudez et al., 2018), and histopathology synthesis (Xue et al., 2021), with recent work applying CycleGAN to generate CMB mimics for false positive reduction training in QSM-based detection pipelines (Xia et al., 2024). However, GANs are difficult to train without access to sufficient labeled data, making non-deep learning techniques based on a priori domain knowledge more desirable for CMB synthesis in data-scarce settings. Complementary approaches such as imbalance-aware loss functions have also been shown to improve minority class detection by up to 51% on 3D MRI classification tasks (Scholz et al., 2024), though these do not address the fundamental data scarcity problem.

Self-supervised learning involves learning useful embeddings from a pretext task where labels can be automatically generated to improve performance on a downstream task (Jing and Tian, 2019). Techniques based on self-supervised learning have been shown to be effective in the field of medical imaging (Azizi et al., 2021; Haghighi et al., 2024; Taleb et al., 2020), where data scarcity is common, and network inputs do not resemble natural images. Typical pretext tasks for medical images include reconstruction (Tajbakhsh et al., 2019), distance estimation (Spitzer et al., 2018), and data context restoration (L. Chen et al., 2019). Recent work has shown that restorative pretext tasks, which require predicting or reconstructing local image content, preserve fine-grained spatial features particularly important for small lesion detection, while discriminative tasks tend to learn more global representations (Haghighi et al., 2024). Augmentation-based pretext tasks such as inpainting and rotation prediction have also been shown to be effective for medical segmentation (Tang et al., 2022), with domain-specific multi-task pretraining on brain MRI outperforming single-task approaches (Kim et al., 2024).

Uncertainty estimation is the problem of calibrating a model’s estimate of its own confidence in the model’s prediction, both to improve performance and enhance model interpretability. A recent comprehensive review categorizes approaches into probabilistic and non-probabilistic methods, highlighting that real-world adoption in medical imaging remains limited despite substantial research progress (Huang et al., 2024). Bayesian methods include Bayesian neural networks (Blundell et al., 2015), Monte Carlo dropout (Gal and Ghahramani, 2016), and variational inference (Graves, 2011), but are computationally expensive due to posterior estimation requirements. Non-Bayesian methods such as deep ensembles provide valuable confidence heuristics but do not regularize models directly (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017). Conformal prediction has recently emerged as a model-agnostic, distribution-free framework that constructs statistically rigorous uncertainty sets with finite-sample guarantees (Chen et al., 2024). Confidence-aware learning via correctness ranking loss (CRL) relies on using the fraction of correct predictions on a sample accrued throughout training as a proxy for model confidence to rank the difficulty of examples to regularize probabilities throughout training (Moon et al., 2020).

CMB synthesis

Given a healthy background patch, xh, of size 16 × 16 × 16 sampled from a GRE scan and centered within tissue, the objective of CMB synthesis is to implant a hypointense region near the center of the patch that resembles a real CMB (Fig. 1). Background patches are sampled to avoid existing hypointense regions caused by air bubbles, large vessels, partial volume effects, and true CMBs by thresholding on Frangi filter, tissue mask, and ground truth mask values. Hypointensity structure is modeled using a randomly generated 11 × 11 × 11 voxel three-dimensional kernel, k~K(θ), where the elements ki,j∈[0,1] are 1 at background voxels and approach zero towards the center according to a roughly Gaussian decay function meant to simulate the blooming effect of CMBs. The synthesis parameters, θ, control the range of possible kernels to sample from and are tuned to produce kernels that are approximately spherical and at least moderately hypointense for CMBs. This kernel is then multiplied with the central 11 × 11 × 11 voxels of the T2* map of xh to implant a hypointense region at the center of the T2* patch. The final simulated signal values across all echoes are generated from the modified T2* values at the modified voxels using the MR signal equation to produce the final synthetic patch, xs. Negative examples are also generated using this same procedure with modified parameters. Blood vessels can be generated using this process by increasing the standard deviation of the Gaussian kernel in one or two of the three directions and by decreasing the intensity of the hypointensity drop. Air bubbles can be generated by sampling background voxels outside of the brain and adding noise to the regenerated signal values within the implanted region. A comparison between real and synthetic CMBs is shown in Fig. 2.

CMB detection algorithm

Candidate selection

As a means of screening conspicuous mimics and background voxels from latter stages of evaluation, a candidate selection algorithm is used to distinguish between CMB candidate voxels and background on brain images. In an initial pre-selection step, Frangi filter and tissue mask values are used to eliminate non-hypointense voxels and voxels that lie outside of tissue. The remaining pre-selected voxels are then filtered using a weak AdaBoost classifier that has been trained to discriminate between real CMB and non-CMB voxels using a set of voxel-wise features that include Frangi values, tissue mask values, and second order eigenvalues at various scales. This classifier is trained with a high class weight on the CMB class to alleviate class imbalance issues and retain a high sensitivity. Signal patches are then sampled at each candidate before being passed to the detection model for final CMB determination.

Patch-based CMB detection

CMB detection is done using a ResNet20 (He et al., 2016) network architecture that has been modified to accept 3D volumes as input (Fig. 3). The initial pre-residual convolutional layer is altered from the original to use a 2 × 2 × 2 voxel filter size with a stride of 1 to avoid obscuring local features relevant to CMB detection early in the encoder. This is followed by 4 residual blocks, a global average pooling layer, and two fully connected layers. In the case where self-supervised pre-training is used, weights in the feature encoding layers are initialized from the pre-trained networks. Network input volumes are size 16 × 16 × 16 × 4 with 4 stacked gradient echo image channels. CMB patches are defined as patches where the darkest voxel in a CMB overlaps with the central 3 × 3 × 3 region.

Self-Supervised pre-training

Before training on the primary detection task, the feature encoder component of the neural network is pre-trained on two self-supervised auxiliary tasks, fuzzy segmentation (FuzzSeg) and rotation prediction, to improve feature learning and task generalization (Fig. 4). Fuzzy segmentation is a self-supervised auxiliary task derived from the CMB synthesis algorithm where the objective is to predict the synthesis kernel, k, used to generate a given synthetic example patch, xs. The term fuzzy segmentation refers to the fact that each kij is interpreted as the relative scalar intensity drop in T2* at a particular voxel compared to healthy background, rather than as a simple probability. The objective of the rotation prediction task is to determine the number of 90-degree rotations r∈{0,1,2,3} along the z-axis applied to a given rotation augmented input image.

The fuzzy segmentation loss term is given by LFS=LR+γLC, where LR is the elementwise mean squared error between the ground truth kernel and the predicted kernel, LC is a localization loss term defined as a binary cross-entropy loss term that penalizes the model for predicting non-zero voxels disconnected from the central hypointense region, and γ is a weight term between 0 and 1. The final combined joint loss term to be minimized is LSSL=LRot+LFS where the rotation loss, LRot, is cross-entropy loss based on the differences between the true rotation labels and the predicted ones.

All layers prior to the final pooling layer in the 3D ResNet architecture are used as the feature encoder backbone for self-supervised pretraining. Two separate decoder heads are then used for fuzzy segmentation and rotation prediction and loss terms are computed separately on each output. For fuzzy segmentation, a decoder network with three residual blocks containing one upsampling layer and two convolutional layers each is used to output a predicted kernel that is the same size as the image input. For rotation prediction, a pooling and flatten layer followed by a sequence of fully convolutional layers equivalent to the layers used for CMB prediction is used to output a predicted class. The weights of the feature encoder are used to initialize the feature encoder in the CMB detection model at the start of training.

Confidence-aware learning

Confidence-aware learning (Moon et al., 2020) is used to enforce that probabilities output by the detection model can be interpreted as estimates of the model’s relative confidence in each prediction. This is achieved by optimizing the detection model using an additional loss term, called correctness ranking loss (CRL), that enforces agreement between the ordering of the predicted probabilities and the relative difficulty of correct prediction for examples in each mini-batch. The CRL loss for any given pair of examples is defined by, LCRLxi,xj=max0,−gci,cjκi−κj+ci−cj where ci is the number of times the model correctly predicted example i over the total number of epochs. The indicator function g is 1 if ci>cj, 0 if ci=cj, and −1 otherwise. κi is the confidence score for example i computed by taking the negative entropy of the predicted probability, pi. In other words, CRL is high for a pair of examples in the case where the difference in model confidence does not agree with the difference in ordinal ranking according to correctness. The final CMB detection loss for a given batch of size n is given by, LCMB=∑xi,yi∈DLCEpi,yi+ω∑xi,xj∈DCLCRLxi,xj where D is the set of all image class pairs and Dc=xi,xi+1i=2…n−1∪x1,xn is a subset of all possible pairs of examples in the batch and ω is a weight term.

Implementation details

Network architecture

Our detection system employs a modified 3D ResNet-20 architecture adapted for volumetric patch classification (Fig. 3). The network accepts 16 × 16 × 16 × 4 input patches corresponding to four echo times (9.6, 19.6, 29.6, and 39.6 ms) from multi-echo gradient echo (GRE) sequences. The architecture consists of three residual blocks with [3, 3, 3] layers and channel dimensions progressing as [16, 32, 64], using strided convolutions for spatial downsampling. The network employs batch normalization, ReLU activations, global average pooling, and a final fully-connected layer (64 units) for binary classification.

Training configuration

All models were trained for 150 epochs using stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with momentum (0.9) and weight decay (10−4). The initial learning rate was set to 0.1 with a step decay schedule (γ=0.1) at epochs 50 and 100. Training used a batch size of 64 with early stopping patience of 10 epochs (minimum improvement threshold: 10−4). For methods incorporating self-supervised learning (SSL), we initialized the encoder with weights from a FuzzSeg model pre-trained for 250 epochs on a combination of FuzzSeg and rotation prediction tasks, with encoder weights fine-tuned (not frozen) during detection training. 48 scans out of the 287 present in the dataset were held out for pre-training the fuzzy segmentation model. For correctness-ranking loss (CRL), we applied ranking loss with weight λ=1.0 to encourage confidence calibration. All models were implemented in PyTorch 2.6.0 and trained on Tesla A40 GPUs. Inference was performed with batch size 64.

Data augmentation and synthetic data

All input images underwent N4 bias field correction followed by z-score normalization. For methods utilizing synthetic data augmentation, we augmented the training set by randomly swapping negative patches with implanted synthetic CMBs with probability p=0.125 (12.5% of training patches). Synthetic CMBs were generated using our kernel-based synthesis algorithm that preserves the multi-echo signal characteristics and spatial morphology of real CMBs. The “Real Only” baseline used only real CMBs from the training set with no synthetic augmentation (equivalent to p=1.0 for real CMB retention).

Candidate selection

We employed a two-stage pre-selection and refinement approach. First, a candidate pre-selection generated by thresholding on tissue masks and RA′ and RB′ Frangi masks was used to identify hypointense and spherical objects and eliminate voxels that lie outside of tissue. Second, a shallow AdaBoost classifier (50 estimators, maximum depth 1) refined candidates using 27 voxel-wise features including modified Frangi sphericality scores (RA′andRB′), tissue probability, and Hessian eigenvalues at various scales. This classifier was trained with class weights (CMB weight: 2000) to handle severe class imbalance and promote high recall, using a CMB probability threshold of 0.5. Signal patches were then sampled at each candidate before being passed to the detection model for final CMB determination.

Cross-Validation and ensembling

We evaluated all methods using stratified 5-fold cross-validation repeated across 4 runs yielding 20 independent training runs per method. Each set of 4 runs used the same set of random seeds for each experiment making the permutation of subject splits equivalent across runs. Each training set consisted of 192 subjects, with 48 subjects held out for validation. For ensemble prediction, we averaged probability maps across all 4 runs (1 validation fold per run per scan) and applied non-maximum suppression (NMS) with IoU threshold of 0.1 to generate final detections.

Evaluation metrics

We evaluated detection performance using multiple complementary metrics to characterize system behavior across different operating points. Our primary metric was average precision (AP). AP provides a single summary measure that is particularly suitable for highly imbalanced detection tasks such as CMB identification.

To assess performance at clinically relevant operating points, we computed sensitivity (recall) and precision at three target false positive rates: 1, 5, and 15 false positives per scan. Sensitivity measures the proportion of ground truth CMBs correctly detected, while precision measures the proportion of detections that correspond to true CMBs. We also computed the F1 score (harmonic mean of precision and sensitivity) at these same operating points. Additionally, we report the false positive rate achieved at 50% and 80% sensitivity thresholds, providing insight into the practical trade-offs between detection completeness and false positive burden.

A predicted detection was considered a true positive if its center fell within 3 mm Euclidean distance of a ground truth CMB center. Detections not matching any ground truth CMB were classified as false positives, while ground truth CMBs with no matching detection were counted as false negatives. We applied non-maximum suppression (NMS) with an intersection-over-union (IoU) threshold of 0.1 to eliminate redundant detections before evaluation.

For cross-validation metrics, we aggregated results across all individual fold evaluations (5 folds × 4 random seeds = 20 runs), reporting mean and standard deviation of AP and other metrics. For ensemble metrics, we take the average of all holdout CMB probability maps (4 per scan), then applied detection thresholds to generate final predictions.

All metrics were computed using free-response receiver operating characteristic (FROC) analysis, where we swept detection probability thresholds to generate sensitivity-versus-false-positive-rate curves.

Results

CMB detector performance

Table 4 summarizes the overall detection performance of the proposed CMB detection method both before and after applying the ensemble method and across different categories of CMBs. The proposed method utilizing synthetic training data, SSL, and CRL (Real + Synth + CRL + SSL) achieves a mean AP of 0.305 before ensembling and final AP of 0.351 after ensembling, compared to 0.168 and 0.241 for the baseline using only real data. For possible CMBs and definite CMBs, the proposed method achieves AP improvements from 0.087 to 0.109 and from 0.209 to 0.410, respectively (Table 5). At key operating points, sensitivity improved from 0.291 to 0.377 at 1 false positive per scan, from 0.595 to 0.667 at 5 false positives per scan, and from 0.758 to 0.831 at 15 false positives per scan. The pre-ensemble results also showed greater training stability with lower standard deviation across runs compared to the baseline (Fig. 5). These results establish a new state-of-the-art baseline for the ex-vivo CMB detection problem.

Impact of training enhancements

Fig. 6 shows FROC curves for all evaluated methods including pairwise comparisons showing the effect of each method enhancement. To evaluate the contribution of each method enhancement, we compared models trained with different combinations of synthetic data augmentation, self-supervised learning (SSL), and confidence-aware learning (CRL). Models trained on real data combined with synthetic examples (Real + Synth) achieved an ensemble AP of 0.333 compared to 0.241 for models trained on real data alone (Real Only), representing a 38% relative improvement. Adding SSL pretraining using fuzzy segmentation and rotation prediction (Real + Synth + SSL) improved ensemble AP to 0.340 for all CMBs and 0.394 AP for definite CMBs, 2% and 5% gains over the baseline without SSL (Real + Synth), respectively. Incorporating correctness ranking loss (Real + Synth + CRL) improved ensemble AP to 0.344 for all CMBs and 0.410 for definite only CMBs, 3% and 9% gains over baseline without CRL (Real + Synth), respectively, while also enhancing the interpretability of model predictions. The full method using all three techniques (Real + Synth + SSL + CRL) achieved the best performance across all runs both after applying ensemble prediction with an AP of 0.351 and when averaged across all runs with a mean AP of 0.305. The proposed method achieved the highest performance on possible CMBs with an AP of 0.109, while the model with CRL but without SSL (Real + Synth + CRL) achieved the best performance on definite CMBs with an AP of 0.410 (Table 5).

Discussion

This work establishes a baseline for the challenging problem of detecting CMBs on ex-vivo T2*-weighted MRI taken from community-based older adults. This is the first paper to both establish the difficulty and importance of the ex-vivo CMB detection problem, and the first to propose a comprehensive detection algorithm that significantly reduces ex-vivo CMB annotation times for manual raters. We also demonstrate the effectiveness of a variety of techniques including data synthesis, self-supervised learning, and confidence aware learning as promising methods for enhancing performance in this domain which is characterized by small datasets and extreme class imbalance. Our best performing model achieves an average precision of 0.351 which establishes a new benchmark for the ex-vivo CMB detection task.

In addition to achieving the highest average precision score, our top model demonstrates high functional performance in terms of reducing the number of false positives at key sensitivity thresholds, capturing 83% of all CMBs and 90% of definite CMBs at 15 false positives per subject. On a practical level, this allows for the generation of candidate masks with an order of 5 to 20 times fewer potential candidates when compared to pure feature-based thresholding techniques and more well-calibrated probabilities, both crucial tools for manual raters to label more CMBs faster and with higher accuracy. These results are also an improvement over preliminary work on ex-vivo CMB detection which showed an average precision of 0.294 on a smaller dataset of 120 participants without robust cross-validation, ensemble prediction, and confidence calibration (Nikseresht et al. 2022). While the detection performance is lower than results reported in-vivo CMB detection studies using clinical populations (Kim et al., 2025; Tsuchida et al., 2024), this work establishes the key differences in CMB detection between in-vivo studies in clinical populations and ex-vivo studies in community-based older adults where CMBs are much harder to detect.

The automated detection method proposed in this work addresses a critical bottleneck in MRI and neuropathology research. Manual annotation of CMBs on ex-vivo MRI is time-consuming and requires expert knowledge, limiting the scale of studies that can investigate the relationship between CMBs and cerebrovascular pathologies. Large-scale studies are needed to understand how CMBs relate to cerebral amyloid angiopathy, arteriolosclerosis, and other pathologies found at autopsy that have been previously validated in smaller scale studies (Nikseresht et al., 2023). While the proposed method also requires expert knowledge for reviewing and confirming potential CMB candidates, it enables researchers to screen large numbers of scans efficiently by presenting a small set of high-confidence candidates for review. Even with its current limitations, the proposed system provides substantial practical value in real-world annotation workflows, enabling manual raters to review a small fraction of potential candidates while maintaining high detection rates.

The three methodological enhancements proposed in this work, i.e. synthetic data augmentation, self-supervised learning, and confidence-aware learning, each address specific challenges in the ex-vivo CMB detection problem. Synthetic data augmentation produced the largest performance gain, improving average precision by 38% over models trained on real data alone. This substantial improvement addresses the fundamental challenge of data scarcity in community-based populations. The synthesis algorithm generates realistic multi-echo CMBs by modeling the blooming effect and signal decay characteristics observed in real examples. The ability to generate unlimited training examples helps the model learn robust features despite the severe class imbalance. Importantly, the synthesis approach requires no additional labeled data and can be tuned based on domain knowledge of MRI physics and CMB appearance.

Self-supervised learning through fuzzy segmentation (FuzzSeg) and rotation prediction provided more modest but consistent improvements. FuzzSeg pretraining improved ensemble AP by 2% for all CMBs and 5% for definite CMBs. This is consistent with recent findings that restorative pretext tasks preserve fine-grained features critical for small lesion detection compared to discriminative approaches that tend to learn global representations (Haghighi et al., 2024). This auxiliary task leverages the same synthetic data generation process that produces training examples, creating a natural connection between pretraining and the downstream detection task. Confidence-aware learning via correctness ranking loss (CRL) improved performance by 3% overall and 9% for definite CMBs. More importantly, CRL encourages probability estimates that better reflect prediction correctness, as the ranking loss penalizes confident errors. This calibration is valuable for practical annotation workflows where raters need to prioritize which detections to review or hedge their uncertainty when reviewing ambiguous cases. The combination of all three techniques achieved the best overall performance after ensembling, suggesting that they address complementary aspects of the detection problem.

Few limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of this study. The dataset consists of 287 participants from a single site with a specific ex-vivo MRI protocol, which limits the generalizability of the findings to other sites with different imaging protocols or tissue handling procedures. External validation on independent datasets acquired at different sites would be needed to assess how well the model transfers to new imaging conditions. The relatively small dataset size and low CMB prevalence present ongoing challenges for training deep learning models. With an average of fewer than 2 CMBs per scan, the total number of positive examples available for training is severely limited. While synthetic data augmentation helps address this limitation, the model still struggles with rare CMB presentations and borderline cases. The false positive rate remains high compared to in-vivo detection methods, though this is explained by the greater number and variety of mimics present in ex-vivo scans. The proposed method still misses 10% of definite CMBs and 23% of possible CMBs at the operating point of 15 false positives per scan. Most missed detections were either very faint CMBs that are difficult to distinguish from background noise or CMBs located near the edge of the brain where air bubbles create additional confusion. In older adults with substantial metal deposits in the basal ganglia, the reduced T2* values increase the likelihood of false positives presenting additional challenges. Qualitative review of detections revealed cases where the model identified ambiguous CMB candidates that fall within the expected range of inter-rater disagreement, suggesting that some model detections classified as false positives may represent true CMBs missed by the rater during manual CMB annotation. These findings suggest that future studies evaluating the model against multi-rater annotations are needed to properly assess model performance on ambiguous cases.

There are several future research directions that are enabled by this baseline work. On a practical level, the proposed method has the potential to dramatically expand the scale of MRI-pathology studies examining the relationship between CMBs and neuropathological findings. Beyond the ex-vivo setting, automated CMB detection methods developed here could be extended to in-vivo MRI, further broadening the scope of CMB research. The incorporation of data sources from multiple protocols and scanning sites along with data from in-vivo scans would prove useful for enhancing model generalizability across diverse imaging domains. Future architectural enhancements will focus on the development of end-to-end detection pipelines, instead of two-stage approaches, to alleviate issues caused by optimizing the candidate selection and false positive reduction steps independently. While variants of vision transformers failed to converge on the current dataset due to their tendency to overfit to small datasets and weak performance on small objects, recent work on domain-aware pretraining for brain MRI suggests transformer architectures may become viable as dataset sizes and pretraining strategies mature (Kim et al., 2024).

Conclusion

This work establishes the first comprehensive baseline for automated detection of cerebral microbleeds on ex-vivo MRI in community-based older adults. We have demonstrated that partially automated CMB annotation is achievable despite the significant challenges posed by low CMB prevalence, abundant mimics, and limited training data. The proposed detection system achieves a final average precision after ensembling of 0.35, capturing 83% of all CMBs with 15 false positives per scan, representing a substantial improvement over preliminary work in this domain. By combining novel synthesis techniques, self-supervised learning, and confidence-aware training, we address the fundamental data scarcity problem that has limited progress on this task. This work establishes both the importance and feasibility of the ex-vivo CMB detection problem, providing a foundation for future algorithmic improvements and, more importantly, enabling the large-scale neuropathology studies needed to understand the role of CMBs in cerebrovascular disease and cognitive decline in aging populations. As ex-vivo MRI protocols become more widely adopted and datasets grow, the baseline established in this work will serve as a reference point for advancing automated detection methods and accelerating discovery of CMB-pathology associations.
