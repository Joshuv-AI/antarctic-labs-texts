Digital Evaluation of Pathologic Root Resorption in Primary Mandibular Molars and Its Implications on Succedaneous Unerupted Premolars
Authors: Ramya Ramanathan, Paul Chalakkal, Siya Gajanan Dukle, Harsh U Manerkar, Mubasshir Ahmed Shaikh, Sayali Jain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulpal infection from primary molars may have periapical implications resulting in pathological root resorption (PRR). There is little clarity regarding its effects on succedaneous teeth. Therefore, the aim of the study was to evaluate the effects of PRR on the root formation and eruption of their succedaneous premolars.Materials and methodsDigital orthopantomograms (OPGs) were obtained of subjects aged between 6 and 9 years. A split-mouth study design was planned, wherein the affected side had to contain a carious mandibular primary molar (MPM) showing PRR. The corresponding tooth on the contralateral side had to be unaffected, showing physiologic root resorption alone. Premolar root formation, premolar eruption, and MPM root resorption pattern were measured digitally. A sample size of 56 OPGs were used for the study. Statistics were carried out using STATA version 17.0 (StataCorp). Shapiro–Wilk, Cohen's d, and Student's t-tests were also used.ResultsGreater amounts of premolar root formation and intra-alveolar eruption had occurred resulting from PRR of the overlying primary molars. A greater amount of PRR was observed on the distal and mesial roots of the first and second molars, respectively.ConclusionPRR in primary molars enhances the root formation and intra-alveolar eruption of their succedaneous premolars.Clinical significanceThis study objectively demonstrates that PRR in primary mandibular molars accelerates root formation and intra-alveolar eruption of succedaneous premolars. The split-mouth design minimizes interindividual variability, strengthening the evidence for the proposed space replacement theory of eruption. These findings clarify previous inconsistencies and enhance clinical understanding of premolar eruption patterns in pediatric dentistry.Keywords: Digital evaluation, Pathological root resorption, Premolar development, Premolar eruption, Premolar formation

Introduction

Pulpal infection from primary molars often results in pathological root resorption (PRR) and pathologies such as apical periodontitis, granulomas, abscesses, and radicular cysts. The effects of periapical infection on premolar crowns, based on the level of crown completion during their exposure to the insult, is known.1 The possibility of apical periodontitis resulting in irregular eruption of succedaneous teeth has been reported.2 The possible role of periapical inflammation in affecting the formation or development of succedaneous teeth has also been proposed.3,4 However, there is little clarity in the literature regarding the effects of PRR in primary molars on the root formation and eruption of their succedaneous premolars, and the pattern of PRR. Therefore, this study was carried out to evaluate these factors, and the null hypothesis assumed was that PRR had no such implications.

Materials and Methods

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee, Approval number: GDCH/IEC/I-2024 (09) and the study was carried out in the Department of Pedodontics and Preventive Dentistry. Digital orthopantomograms (OPGs) were obtained of subjects aged between 6 and 9 years, such that the unerupted mandibular premolars had completed Nolla's stage 6 of formation. A split-mouth study design was planned, wherein the affected (experimental) side had to contain a carious mandibular primary molar (MPM) with pulpal involvement, showing PRR with periapical radiolucency, discontinuation in lamina dura, or widened periodontal ligament space. The corresponding tooth on the contralateral (control) side had to be unaffected, showing physiologic root resorption alone (Fig. 1). OPGs with distorted images, congenitally missing premolars, and endodontically treated MPMs were unconsidered. All OPGs had been obtained using the same machine (Planmeca ProMax® 3D Classic machine containing a Toshiba D-054SB X-ray tube), and the following parameters were evaluated on the basis of measurements made using the Romexis Viewer Win application.

Each of the measurements were made again by the same observer after a period of 1 month to determine intra-observer reliability (ICC = 0.9995, p < 0.001). A third set of measurements were made by another observer and compared with the previous 2 sets in order to obtain interobserver reliability (Kappa = 0.9222, p < 0.001). The minimum sample size was found to be 44 (44 OPGs). The a priori power analysis (probability >0.9) showed that a sample size of 44 was reliable to detect an effect size of δ ≥ 0.5, assuming a 2-sided criterion for detection, that allows for a maximum type I error rate of a = 0.05. However, a total of 56 OPGs were obtained, and the above-mentioned procedures were carried out.

The data gathered were entered in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet and analyzed using STATA version 17.0 (StataCorp). Data analysis was carried out using descriptive statistics and other relevant tests of significance. Shapiro–Wilk normality tests were conducted to check whether the samples followed a normal distribution, so that it could be decided whether to use parametric or nonparametric tests. A p-value < 0.05 and 0.01 were considered significant and highly significant, respectively. The Student's t-test was used for intergroup comparison.

The greater amount of intra-alveolar eruption of the premolars on the experimental side was found to be statistically significant (Tables 3 and 4).

A greater amount of PRR was observed on the distal and mesial roots of the first and second molars, respectively, when compared with the control sides. The difference was found to be statistically highly significant (Tables 3 and 4).

Discussion

Mandibular teeth were examined in this study because of the clarity in comprehending tooth structures on the OPG. OPGs were chosen of subjects aged between 6 and 9 years because root formation and intra-alveolar eruption of premolars occurs during these years, and the MPMs are yet to exfoliate.4 The split-mouth study design eliminated interindividual variability that could have otherwise occurred due to differences in genetics, physiology, and growth between individuals. The reduction in variance increased the statistical power of the study, thereby reducing sample size. The split-mouth study design reduced the logistical difficulties of evaluating a larger sample size.

Two previous studies had found that PRR does not affect the root formation of premolars.5,6 It may be noted that the study by Haralabakis et al. was on conventional OPGs without any digital measurements. However, 2 studies had reported that PRR delays the formation of premolars.2,7 In this study, it was found that PRR resulted in greater amounts of root formation in unerupted premolars, which is in accordance with the finding made by Den-feng and Shao-min.8

It has been reported that bone resorption secondary to periapical infection speeds up the eruption of succedaneous teeth.9–11 In this study, it was found that a greater amount of intra-alveolar eruption was observed for premolars beneath primary molars exhibiting PRR. This finding is similar to the observation made by Fanning.10 Studies have claimed that exfoliation or extraction of MPM secondary to periapical infection resulted in the premature eruption of premolars with incomplete roots.2,10 The results of this study could not be compared with this fact because this study assessed the intra-alveolar eruption of premolars prior to the exfoliation or extraction of their corresponding primary molars. Therefore, whether the premolars would have erupted with incomplete roots cannot be commented upon. The eruption of succedaneous teeth being affected by the formation of a fibrous scar tissue barrier secondary to periapical infection has also been suggested.4,10 However, it could not be ascertained from this study whether scar tissue had formed following periapical infection. In this study, PRR was observed mostly on the distal and mesial roots of the first and second molars, respectively.

During the meticulous evaluation of relevant literature, we came across studies where authors had wrongly quoted studies that were irrelevant to their findings. Patil et al. quoted the study by Winter while stating that degradation of the overlying osseous tissue due to periapical infection expedites the emergence of the succedaneous tooth, without mentioning that the latter was a study on kittens.7,12 Wahono et al. quoted a study by Vieira-Andrade et al. while stating that PRR caused early eruption and disruption in the growth of the succedaneous tooth.4,13 Mulia et al. also quoted the study by Vieira-Andrade et al. while stating that the rate of root resorption of primary teeth affects the eruption rate of the succedaneous teeth.6,13 However, the study by Vieira-Andrade et al. was about the prevalence and associated factors of inflammatory root resorption in primary molars, with no mention of its implications on premolars.13 Li et al. quoted a study by Chalakkal et al. while stating that periapical inflammation of primary teeth could accelerate the eruption of permanent teeth, although there is no mention of this fact in the article by Chalakkal et al.1,3 Li et al. also quoted an article by Bardellini et al. while stating that PRR slows down the eruption of succedaneous teeth, although the article by Bardellini et al. was about anomalies in permanent teeth after trauma to primary teeth.3,14

It was found that PRR enhances the formation and eruption of succedaneous teeth. Therefore, a theory entitled space replacement theory of eruption of succedaneous teeth is proposed, since it was observed that PRR results in bone resorption, which in turn creates a space that gets occupied by the developing premolar. Moreover, this is the only study that carried out digital measurements to evaluate all the parameters. None of the previous articles related to this topic have used digital measurements for evaluation. Therefore, it is logical to state that the findings in this study are accurate. In this study, it was not possible to diagnose the exact cause for PRR on the OPGs, except that they had developed due to pulpal disease, which was confirmed from the presence of loss of crown structure with pulpal involvement. Future studies are suggested with longer follow-up periods to check for premature intraoral eruption of premolars after being subjected to overlying MPMs with PRR.

Conclusion

PRR in primary molars enhances the root formation and intra-alveolar eruption of their succedaneous premolars.
