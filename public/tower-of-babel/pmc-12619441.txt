Clinical efficacy and impact on sexual function of percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy in the treatment of male lumbar disc herniation complicated with cauda equina syndrome
Authors: Juyi Lai, Yuanfei Fu, Huangsheng Tan, Pengwei Deng, Hualong Feng, Zhiming Lan, Jun Tao, Shenghua He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate the surgical efficacy of Percutaneous Endoscopic Lumbar Discectomy (PELD) versus Interlaminar Fenestration Decompression (IFD) in treating male patients with Lumbar Disc Herniation (LDH) complicated with Cauda Equina Syndrome (CES), and to evaluate their impact on sexual function.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on male patients with LDH complicated with CES who underwent PELD or IFD at Shenzhen Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital from January 2014 to December 2022. The patients were divided into PELD group and IFD group based on the surgical procedure. Surgical efficacy was assessed using Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score, and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI). Sexual function was evaluated using the International Index of Erectile Function-5 (IIEF-5), Premature Ejaculation Diagnostic Tool (PEDT), and the International Spinal Cord Injury Male Sexual Function Basic Data Set.Results A total of 98 patients were included (47 in the PELD group and 51 in the IFD group). Perioperative Indicators: The PELD group showed significant advantages over the IFD group in intraoperative blood loss (15.84 ± 5.31 ml vs. 42.56 ± 12.79 ml, P < 0.001), incision length (0.74 ± 0.17 cm vs. 4.53 ± 0.81 cm, P < 0.001), and time to ambulation (24.52 ± 6.28 h vs. 46.84 ± 10.57 h, P < 0.001), and did not require drainage. Clinical Efficacy: At 1 month postoperatively, the PELD group had lower low back pain and leg pain VAS scores (3.12 ± 1.24/3.57 ± 1.36 vs. IFD group), higher JOA scores (15.84 ± 2.81), and lower ODI indices (35.57 ± 5.81%) (P < 0.05). There was no significant difference in pain and function scores between the two groups at 2 years postoperatively (P > 0.05). Impact on Sexual Function: At 3 months postoperatively, the PELD group had significantly higher IIEF-5 scores (15.88 ± 3.21), psychogenic erection normal rate (38.30%), and orgasm normal rate (40.43%) than the IFD group (P < 0.05). Sexual function recovered to similar levels in both groups at 2 years postoperatively (IIEF-5 score > 20, P > 0.05).ConclusionPELD demonstrates advantages of minimal trauma, faster early pain relief, and quicker functional recovery compared with IFD in the treatment of LDH complicated with CES, and exerts more significant early protective effects on male sexual function, while the long-term efficacy is comparable between the two procedures. This technique can be an option for male CES patients seeking rapid recovery and sexual function protection.

Introduction

Lumbar disc herniation (LDH) complicated with Cauda Equina Syndrome (CES) is an emergency in spinal diseases, in which the herniated nucleus pulposus compresses the cauda equina nerve, leading to saddle anesthesia, urinary and fecal incontinence, and lower limb motor dysfunction [1]. The cauda equina contains parasympathetic nerve fibers from the S2-S4 spinal segments, which directly regulate male sexual functions such as erection and ejaculation [2, 3]. Studies indicate that 70%−81% of spinal cord injury patients suffer from erectile dysfunction, while research on the impact of CES caused by lumbar disc herniation on sexual function and therapeutic outcomes after endoscopic surgical intervention remains insufficient [4, 5].

Currently, the core principle for clinical treatment of LDH complicated with CES is “emergent surgical decompression,” aimed at swiftly relieving cauda equina nerve compression to salvage neurological function. The mainstream surgical approaches can be broadly categorized into open surgery and minimally invasive techniques. The former, represented by laminectomy and interlaminar fenestration decompression (IFD), offers the advantage of fully exposing the surgical field and thoroughly removing the herniated nucleus pulposus and osteophytes. However, it is associated with drawbacks such as significant trauma, potential disruption of spinal stability due to paraspinal muscle dissection, and laminectomy. Minimally invasive techniques, centered around percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy (PELD), boast advantages including smaller incisions, reduced bleeding, and minimal disruption to spinal stabilizing structures [6, 7]. Nevertheless, for CES cases complicated by severe spinal stenosis or multi-level herniation, the adequacy of decompression achieved with PELD remains a subject of debate.

Despite ongoing advancements in treatment technologies, several critical unresolved issues persist in the diagnosis and management of LDH complicated by CES. Firstly, clinical evaluation systems predominantly focus on the recovery of lower limb muscle strength and urofecal function (e.g., residual urine volume, anal sphincter tone), while sexual function (erectile and ejaculatory function) has not been incorporated into routine postoperative follow-up indicators. Consequently, some patients continue to experience a decline in quality of life and psychological anxiety due to sexual dysfunction, even after the recovery of motor and urofecal functions [8, 9]. Secondly, existing studies predominantly explore the application of PELD in CES independently, lacking comparative analyses of long-term functional outcomes among different surgical approaches, particularly with respect to the quantitative correlation between PELD and sexual function recovery. Thirdly, there is a lack of standardized criteria for surgical approach selection in complex cases. For CES patients with mild lumbar instability or calcified herniation, whether minimally invasive PELD can serve as an alternative to open IFD or necessitate combined spinal fusion remains unclear, with no unified diagnostic and treatment guidelines currently available.

This retrospective analysis compares clinical data of male CES patients treated with PELD versus Interlaminar Fenestration Decompression (IFD) to explore the mechanism and prognostic factors of this procedure on sexual function, providing references for clinical practice.

Methods

Patient selection

A retrospective analysis was conducted on male patients with LDH complicated with CES who underwent PELD or IFD at Shenzhen Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital from January 2014 to December 2022. The patients were divided into PELD group and IFD group based on the surgical procedure. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital (No: K2014016), and all patients provided informed consent.

Inclusion criteria

Patients were included if they met the following criteria: (1) diagnosis of LDH complicated with acute CES, with both magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-confirmed single-segment LDH and clinical manifestations of CES (saddle anesthesia, anal sphincter dysfunction, lower limb radicular pain, and numbness); (2) non-calcified herniation; (3) treatment with PELD or IFD; (4) male gender aged 18–50 years with normal preoperative sexual function evaluated by scales such as IIEF-5; and (5) voluntary signing of informed consent and ability to cooperate with postoperative follow-up.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with lumbar spondylolisthesis (Grade ≥ I) or lumbar instability; (2) Severe cardiovascular or cerebrovascular diseases, liver/kidney failure, malignant tumors, or other diseases affecting sexual function or survival; (3) History of lumbar surgery, spinal cord injury, conus medullaris syndrome, or peripheral neuropathy; (4) Mental disorders precluding scale assessment, long-term use of drugs affecting sexual function without discontinuation, or inability to cooperate with the study; (5) Loss to follow-up or inability to complete the study.

Surgical technique

All surgeries were performed by a chief physician with over 10 years of experience in spinal endoscopy, who had performed more than 1,000 cases of PELD and over 1,000 cases of IFD. The selection of PELD or IFD followed the principle of individualized assessment and indication matching, as detailed below: Type of disc herniation: IFD was selected for severe free or extruded herniation. Anatomical characteristics of the involved segment: ① For L5/S1 herniation, PELD via the transintervertebral approach was prioritized; IFD was chosen for cases complicated by interlaminar space stenosis. ② For L1-L5 disc herniation, PELD via the transforaminal approach was used in the absence of significant foraminal stenosis, while IFD was adopted for those with obvious foraminal stenosis. ③ IFD was selected for the involved segment complicated by bony spinal canal stenosis. ④ IFD was chosen when secondary spinal canal stenosis caused by herniation hindered PELD cannula placement. ⑤ IFD was selected for cases with severe ligamentum flavum hypertrophy and thickening. Regarding symptoms: PELD was used for slowly progressive symptoms to minimize trauma, while IFD was preferentially selected for rapidly progressive symptoms to ensure urgent and thorough decompression. Patients’ own preference for the surgical approach was also considered.

PELD

All patients underwent epidural anesthesia. After successful anesthesia induction, patients were placed in a prone position with hips and knees flexed, followed by routine disinfection and draping of the surgical field.

Translaminar Approach: A 10 ml syringe needle was used for percutaneous puncture localization 2 cm from the posterior median line of the responsible intervertebral space, with C-arm fluoroscopy confirming the target disc. A skin incision of approximately 0.7 cm was made at the puncture site, followed by pencil casing dilation and placement of the intervertebral foramen mirror working channel. Degenerated nucleus pulposus tissue was removed microscopically, and dynamic burr was used for spinal canal decompression when necessary, with foraminoplasty, radiofrequency ablation of the annulus fibrosus, and release of nerve root adhesion. After thorough decompression, the floating test was checked. Methylprednisolone sodium succinate 40 mg was injected through the channel to prevent postoperative nerve root edema.

Transforaminal Approach: An 18-gauge puncture needle was inserted into the posterior third of the intervertebral space through the foraminal area 8–10 cm from the midline under C-arm guidance, with ioparol contrast injection to confirm annulus fibrosus rupture and disc herniation. A 0.7 cm skin incision was made, followed by dilation and placement of the working channel. Herniated nucleus pulposus was removed microscopically. After decompression, the floating test was checked, and 0.5 ml of compound betamethasone was injected to prevent nerve root edema. The incision was sutured.

Postoperative Management: Antibiotics were not routinely administered. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs were administered in cases of significant low back pain. Patients ambulated with a lumbar brace. IFD

Under general anesthesia, the patient was positioned prone on a radiolucent spinal frame with hips and knees flexed to minimize lumbar lordosis. A standard posterior midline incision was made, followed by sequential dissection of the skin, subcutaneous fascia, and paraspinal muscles. Subperiosteal dissection was performed to expose the interlaminar window at the target level. Using Kerrison rongeurs and pituitary curettes, partial laminectomy and excision of the hypertrophied ligamentum flavum were completed to establish an adequate operative corridor on the symptomatic side. Neural decompression was achieved by resecting herniated nucleus pulposus and osteophytes compressing the dural sac and traversing nerve roots. After copious irrigation, a closed-suction drain was placed deep to the fascia. Closure proceeded with layered reapproximation of the lumbodorsal fascia, subcutaneous tissue, and skin under direct vision.

Postoperative Management: Antibiotics were used for 24 h. Closed wound drainage was routine, with removal when daily drainage was < 50 ml. Patients ambulated at 48 h with a lumbar brace for 3 months. Evaluation indicators

Perioperative Indicators: Operation time (from skin incision to closure), intraoperative blood loss was measured using a combined method of suction bottle metering and gauze weighing: (Total fluid in the suction bottle-Volume of irrigation saline) + (Weight gain of gauze ×1 ml/g, as 1 g of gauze weight gain ≈ 1 ml of blood), incision length, hospital stay, drainage time, time to first ambulation, and complication rate (infection, hematoma, nerve injury, dural tear).

Surgical Efficacy: Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) [10], Japanese Orthopaedic Association Scores (JOA) [11], and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) [12] were recorded preoperatively, at 1 month, 6 months, and 2 years postoperatively. VAS: 10 cm visual scale for subjective pain intensity (0–10 points). JOA Score: 29-point scale evaluating lumbar function, including subjective symptoms (9 points), objective signs (6 points), and activity ability (14 points). ODI : 10-question scale (0–50 points) evaluating disability, with percentage calculated as (raw score/50)×100%.

Sexual Function: International Index of Erectile Function-5 (IIEF⁃5) [13], Premature Ejaculation Diagnostic Tool(PEDT) [14], and the International Spinal Cord Injury Male Sexual Function Data Set [15] were used to evaluate sexual function before onset, 3 days preoperatively, and at 3 months, 6 months, and 2 years postoperatively.

IIEF-5: 5-question scale (0–25 points): confidence in erection, number of insertions, maintenance of erection, difficulty in maintaining erection, and satisfaction. Scores: 1–7 (severe), 8–11 (moderate), 12–21 (mild), 22–25 (normal).

PEDT: 5-question scale (0–20 points): difficulty delaying ejaculation, probability of premature ejaculation, ejaculation with minimal stimulation, frustration, and partner dissatisfaction. Scores: ≤8 (normal), 9–10 (tendency), ≥ 11 (premature ejaculation).

International Spinal Cord Injury Data Set: Willingness to discuss sexual issues, psychogenic erection, reflex erection, and orgasm (normal/abnormal).

Statistical methods

SPSS 26.0(IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for analysis. Quantitative data were tested for normality (Shapiro-Wilk test), expressed as mean ± SD, and compared using independent sample t test. Categorical data were analyzed by chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Repeated measures ANOVA was used for longitudinal data (VAS, JOA, ODI), with Bonferroni correction for post hoc tests. Categorical variables related to sexual function were analyzed by chi-square test, and ordinal variables by Cochran-Armitage trend test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with two-sided tests.

Results

Patient characteristics

From January 2014 to December 2022, a total of 3,862 male patients with LDH were admitted to the inpatient department at Shenzhen Traditional Chinese Medicine Hospital. Among them, 125 cases (3.24%) presented with concomitant cauda equina syndrome (CES). After excluding 27 cases based on predefined criteria, 98 patients were ultimately enrolled: comprising a PELD cohort (n = 47) and an IFD cohort (n = 51). Perioperative indicators

Discussion

LDH-induced CES is regarded as a ‘critical condition of LDH, primarily involving the upper cauda equina (L2-S2). The reported incidence varies in the literature, ranging from 0.4% to 10.6%, with most studies indicating 1% to 2% [16–19]. Some research suggests that surgery should be performed within the optimal therapeutic window (within 48 hours) [20, 21], while other studies have shown no significant correlation between surgical timing and nerve function improvement [22]. In the past, surgeons often used laminectomy decompression, which could fully expose the surgical field, completely remove the intervertebral disc tissue compressing the cauda equina, and thoroughly decompress the spinal canal and root canal. However, this approach is associated with large trauma, slow recovery, and a high incidence of complications [23, 24].

With the comprehensive development of percutaneous full-endoscopic spinal techniques, many surgeons have begun to use this technology for treating discogenic CES [25, 26]. Our results show that at 1 month postoperatively, the lumbar VAS score in the PELD group (3.12 ± 1.24 points) was 2.24 points lower than that in the IFD group, indicating a more rapid relief of cauda equina compression. This may be related to the reduction of secondary nerve injury under endoscopic direct vision, especially suitable for acute CES caused by soft herniation. Additionally, the lower VAS scores for back pain in the PELD cohort may be attributable to: (1) its minimal tissue disruption of requiring only a 0.7 cm portal incision without extensive paraspinal muscle dissection while preserving posterior tension band complexes (supraspinous ligament/facet joints), compared to IFD necessitating laminar fenestration and muscular detachment with greater soft tissue injury; and (2) significantly earlier ambulation (mean 22 h faster than IFD), reducing disuse-induced myofascial stiffness and immobilization-related pain. Notably, these intergroup differences in short-term back pain resolve progressively with functional recovery during follow-up. Regarding changes in leg VAS scores, although IFD provides more thorough decompression, its requisite nerve root retraction during laminotomy may induce transient postoperative radicular edema. Conversely, PELD achieves targeted nucleus pulposus excision under endoscopic visualization without neural manipulation, resulting in milder early-stage inflammation. Consequently, the PELD cohort demonstrated significantly lower initial leg VAS scores. By the 2-year follow-up, edema resolution eliminated intergroup pain differences (p > 0.05), indicating that IFD’s decompressive advantage was offset by acute surgical trauma, whereas PELD’s minimally invasive superiority yielded more pronounced short-term benefits. In addition, this study exclusively enrolled cases of non-calcified single-segment LDH complicated with cauda equina syndrome (CES), where PELD achieved adequate decompression. In contrast, some previous studies did not exclude calcified herniation or multi-segment degeneration cases, leading to incomplete decompression with PELD and an underestimation of its efficacy. On the other hand, this study adopted an individualized strategy of “interlaminar approach for L5/S1 segment and transforaminal approach for other segments” for PELD, combined with endoscopic direct visualization to minimize nerve traction injury. However, some previous studies uniformly used a single approach, which could result in inadequate decompression or nerve stimulation in specific segments. Finally, patients in the PELD group were able to ambulate with a lumbar brace 24 h postoperatively, reducing brake-related low back pain. In contrast, some previous studies adopted a protocol of ambulation after 48 h postoperatively for both groups, failing to highlight the minimally invasive advantages of PELD. Notably, the results of this study are not isolated, as multiple high-quality studies provide supporting evidence: The meta-analysis showed that the PELD group had significantly better postoperative low back pain VAS scores, incision lengths, and intraoperative blood loss compared to the microendoscopic discectomy (MED) group [27]. The matched cohort study confirmed that the PELD group had significantly lower low back pain VAS scores at 1 day and 3 months postoperatively compared to the open lumbar microdiscectomy (OLMD) group [28]. The retrospective matched cohort study by Ahn targeting young patients found that the PELD group had advantages in postoperative back pain VAS scores and hospital stay [29]. The study by Choi focusing on large-volume LDH also demonstrated that the PELD group had a significantly greater improvement in postoperative back pain compared to the OLMD group [30].

The technique of PELD applicable to CES caused by soft compression, where the herniated or extruded intervertebral disc tissue can be exposed under endoscopic direct vision [31]. During decompression, fine operation is required to avoid iatrogenic compression injury to the cauda equina. The yellow ligament and part of the articular process should be thoroughly removed, and the compressive substances should be excised to ensure sufficient decompression. The safety of spinal endoscopy lies in reducing traction on the nerve roots and dural sac, minimizing disturbance to the cauda equina, and using bipolar radiofrequency electrocoagulation to reduce bleeding and postoperative tissue adhesion. Therefore, it can achieve clinical efficacy equivalent to traditional open surgery while featuring minimal trauma, rapid recovery, maximum preservation of spinal structural stability, and a significantly reduced complication rate [32]. In the results of this study, the intraoperative blood loss in the PELD group (15.84 ± 5.31 ml) was only one-third of that in the IFD group, and the incision length (0.74 ± 0.17 cm) was significantly shorter, avoiding extensive dissection of paraspinal muscles and laminectomy, and reducing damage to the spinal stability structures (such as the supraspinous ligament and facet joint complex) [33]. Additionally, studies have shown that this “channel technology” can reduce the release of local inflammatory factors (such as IL-6 and TNF-α) postoperatively, alleviate perineural fibrosis, and create conditions for early functional recovery. The postoperative ambulation time was advanced by 22 h (24.52 h vs. 46.84 h), further confirming the rapid improvement of activity ability with the minimally invasive approach [34].

Due to the narrow disc space and intervertebral foramen at L5/S1, an interlaminar approach is recommended for L5/S1 cases to avoid a transforaminal approach. On the other hand, the spinal canal volume at L5/S1 is the largest in the entire spine, and the width of the interlaminar space is also the largest, which is sufficient to safely introduce the working channel, thus avoiding the potential risk of iatrogenic nerve injury. As the water content and elasticity of the herniated or extruded intervertebral disc tissue decrease, it is more prone to fragmentation than normal tissue. If a large herniated tissue is directly removed, small fragments are likely to remain. Therefore, when removing large tissues, the surrounding tissues should first be released, including the surrounding annulus fibrosus, posterior longitudinal ligament, and scar tissue. Then, the nucleus pulposus tissue should be clamped, and the wrist should be used to gently shake the nucleus pulposus tissue to facilitate its complete removal. Violent pulling can easily rupture the nucleus pulposus tissue, complicate the surgery, and even damage the nerve roots or dura mater [35]. Whether the nucleus pulposus is completely removed needs to be comprehensively judged based on the size of the nucleus pulposus tissue in imaging, the amount removed during the operation, the relaxation degree of the nerve roots and dural sac, and the patient’s subjective feelings. Once discogenic CES is clearly diagnosed, immediate surgery or emergency surgery is the best choice to maximize the chance of cauda equina nerve function recovery. In this study, 68 patients underwent emergency surgery within 24 h, and their cauda equina nerve function basically recovered, indicating that time is extremely important for the recovery of cauda equina nerve function [36].

The current controversies regarding the use of endoscopic techniques for treating CES mainly focus on surgical efficacy, risks, and scope of application [34]. As a spinal emergency, the treatment of CES needs to balance nerve decompression and functional recovery. Although endoscopic techniques have been widely explored due to their minimally invasive characteristics, their clinical value remains controversial. Some studies have shown that endoscopic surgery and traditional open surgery have comparable nerve function recovery at 6 months and 1 year postoperatively, both of which can effectively relieve symptoms such as saddle anesthesia and urinary/fecal dysfunction [37]. However, the controversy lies in that the thoroughness of decompression by endoscopic techniques for complex cases (such as calcified herniations, multi-segment degeneration, or severe spinal stenosis) is still questioned, and open surgery with direct vision may be more conducive to completely relieving nerve compression. In terms of risks, although endoscopic surgery has the advantages of minimal trauma, less blood loss, and better preservation of segmental stability, it has a high technical threshold. Intraoperative risks of vascular, nerve, or spinal cord injury may exist, especially for surgeons with insufficient experience. Operational errors may exacerbate nerve function damage. Regarding the scope of application, some believe that endoscopic techniques have a wide range of indications and can be used for CES patients with single-segment herniation [38], while opposing views emphasize that for patients with severe cauda equina nerve adhesion, multi-segment lesions, or those requiring simultaneous spinal fusion, open surgery is still a more reliable choice [39]. In addition, endoscopic techniques have high requirements for surgeons’ operational precision and equipment. When promoted in primary hospitals, the application may be limited by technical conditions, leading to uneven treatment effects, which has also become part of the controversy.

In current orthopedic clinical practice, the diagnosis and treatment of lumbar disc herniation complicated with CES often focus on motor function recovery and urinary/fecal function reconstruction, while sexual function assessment has long been in a state of neglect [40]. Although the cauda equina contains S2-S4 parasympathetic nerve fibers that directly regulate male physiological processes such as erection and ejaculation, clinical physicians lack systematic attention to the incidence, impact mechanisms, and intervention strategies of sexual dysfunction after CES. This neglect has led to a large number of patients still facing the hidden trouble of decreased sexual life quality after “clinical cure” of nerve function, especially for young and middle-aged male groups aged 18–50. Sexual dysfunction may further trigger psychological anxiety and social role disorders [41]. This study quantitatively compared the impact of PELD and interlaminar fenestration decompression (IFD) on sexual function in male CES patients using scales such as IIEF-5, PEDT, and the International Spinal Cord Injury Sexual Function Database Collection Form. It was found that PELD showed significant advantages in early postoperative recovery: at 3 months postoperatively, the normal rate of psychogenic erection (38.30% vs. 23.53%, P = 0.040), normal rate of orgasm (40.43% vs. 25.49%, P = 0.041), and IIEF-5 score (15.88 ± 3.21 vs. 13.17 ± 3.42, P = 0.057) in the PELD group were significantly better than those in the IFD group. This advantage stems from the minimally invasive characteristics of PELD. In addition, the PELD group could ambulate at 24.52 h postoperatively, 22 h earlier than the IFD group. This early functional recovery may indirectly promote sexual function recovery by reducing the inhibition of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis by somatic pain. It is worth noting that sexual function in both groups recovered to similar levels at 2 years postoperatively (IIEF-5 score >20 points), suggesting that the thorough decompression of IFD can compensate for the early trauma disadvantage in the long-term efficacy. However, the normal rate of willingness to discuss sexual issues at 3 months postoperatively (72.34% vs. 56.86%, P = 0.048) was significantly higher, reflecting the positive impact on patients’ sexual psychological reconstruction.

This single-center retrospective study has several limitations: selection bias (due to surgeon experience and exclusion of calcified herniation cases), lacks female patients, and has limited follow-up (2 years). Subjective scales (IIEF-5, PEDT) may miss subtle differences. Multicenter RCTs with larger samples, especially calcified CES and female cohorts, are needed for validation.

Conclusion

PELD demonstrates significant advantages over IFD in minimal trauma, early pain relief, and faster functional recovery for LDH complicated with CES, with more pronounced early protection of male sexual function. While long-term efficacy is comparable, PELD represents a suitable surgical option for male CES patients who prioritize rapid recovery.
