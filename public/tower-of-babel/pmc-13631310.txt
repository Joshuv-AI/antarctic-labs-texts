National genomic surveillance reveals global plasmid and chromosomal dissemination routes of blaNDM-1
Authors: Ricardo Gutiérrez, Manuel Ramírez-Cardoce, Tábata Elizondo-Alvarez, Francisco Duarte-Martínez, Estela Cordero-Laurent, Elvira Segura-Retana, Daniel Cascante-Serrano, Romein Walters-Mais, Cristian Pérez-Corrales, Cristina García-Marín, Heylin Estrada-Murillo, Ernesto Amuy-Morera, Ruth Arce-Bejarano, Maria José Rodríguez-González, Christine Seah, Roberto G Melano, María Antonieta Jiménez-Pearson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe global spread of New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase (NDM)–producing Gram-negative bacteria poses a major threat to healthcare systems worldwide, yet the processes driving their long-term establishment at national scales remain poorly understood. Here, we integrate a decade of nationwide surveillance with high-resolution genomics to reconstruct the emergence, dissemination, and evolution of blaNDM in Costa Rica, from its first detection in 2014 to a major hospital outbreak during the COVID-19 pandemic. National surveillance confirmed hundreds of blaNDM-positive isolates, revealed widespread dissemination across hospitals, and identified a temporal shift in dominant blaNDM hosts from 2020 onwards. During the pandemic, increased NDM detection coincided with a large intrahospital outbreak involving 247 patients with a 51% case-fatality rate. Genomic analysis of 40 representative isolates revealed both plasmid- and chromosome-associated blaNDM-1-carriage and heterogeneous dissemination through globally disseminated high-risk and locally emerging clones. Plasmid-resolved analyses showed that the dominant IncA/C2 blaNDM-1-carrying plasmid derived from a globally conserved backbone and diversified into multiple circulating variants and fusion events with IncF elements, generating multidrug-resistant megaplasmids. Together, these findings highlight how global connectivity, local population dynamics, and plasmid plasticity interact to drive the national persistence of blaNDM and underscore the importance of sustained genomic surveillance.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Genetics, Microbiology

Introduction

The New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase (NDM) is an enzyme that hydrolyzes nearly all β-lactam antibiotics, including carbapenems, and is encoded by the blaNDM gene family. It was first described in 2008 in a Klebsiella pneumoniae isolate recovered from a Swedish patient with a recent history of travel to New Delhi, India1. Retrospective analyses revealed that NDM variants had already been present in Acinetobacter species isolated from India and Turkey, often embedded in potentially mobilizable elements2,3. The ancestral genetic platform of blaNDM is believed to be a Tn125-like composite transposon flanked by two copies of ISAba125, originally identified in Acinetobacter baumannii4,5. Although Tn125 mediated the initial mobilization of blaNDM from Acinetobacter, evidence suggests that it was soon overtaken by other transposons contributing to its dissemination across diverse bacteria, including several Enterobacterales species6,7.

The blaNDM gene family comprises a growing number of variants that contribute to carbapenem resistance among Gram-negative bacteria, reflecting its ongoing and rapid molecular evolution. As of December 2025, 95 blaNDM variants have been reported (NCBI Pathogen RefGene), with blaNDM-1 and blaNDM-5 being the most prevalent globally8. These variants have been identified on more than 33 distinct plasmid replicon types and, in some cases, integrated into the bacterial chromosome, and were found across over 27 bacterial genera7. Within Enterobacterales, the blaNDM gene is most frequently associated with IncX3 plasmids, followed by IncFII/IncFI, IncC and IncN2 backbones. Importantly, blaNDM-positive isolates frequently harbor additional clinically significant resistance determinants, including extended-spectrum β-lactamases (e.g., blaCTX-M), oxacillinases (blaOXA), 16S rRNA methyltransferases conferring high-level aminoglycoside resistance (e.g., rmtC), plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance (qnr) and tetracycline resistance (tet) genes, resulting in multidrug-resistant (MDR) phenotypes9,10.

In Costa Rica, the National Reference Center for Bacteriology (NRCB) coordinates the nationwide surveillance of antimicrobial-resistant bacteria through the National Bacteriology Laboratory Network (NBLN). In 2014, NRCB confirmed the first NDM-producing isolates, corresponding to an Escherichia coli from a pediatric patient and a K. pneumoniae, both isolated from patients from Nicaragua and associated with urinary tract infections. These reports were followed by the first autochthonous case, an Acinetobacter sp. isolated in the Atlantic zone of the country. By the end of that year, four additional Acinetobacter cases were reported from different regions of the Country.

Following the initial detections in 2014, NDM-producing isolates continued to emerge sporadically in various healthcare institutions across Costa Rica and were referred to the NRCB for confirmation. However, in 2021, coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic, the number of NDM-positive organisms rose sharply, alongside the first reported outbreak at the country’s largest public hospital. This study investigates the emergence and progression of the outbreak through detailed genomic analyses of clinical and environmental isolates and places it within the broader context of nationwide dissemination. We provide evidence of the spread of blaNDM IncA/C2 plasmids among Enterobacterales, driven by clonal expansion of globally high-risk and locally emerged clones, along with interspecies transfers. In contrast, A. baumannii, initially carrying blaNDM on a plasmid, exhibited clonal expansion in 2021 through chromosomal integration of the blaNDM ISAba125 element. Furthermore, we identified fusion events between IncA/C2-blaNDM-1 and IncF-blaCTX-M-15 plasmids, resulting in the formation of large, MDR megaplasmids. Our findings underscore the complex evolutionary dynamics driving the persistence and transmission of blaNDM genes within and across healthcare settings in Costa Rica.

Results

Intrahospital NDM-outbreak description

Hospital San Juan de Dios (HSJD), a general hospital in San José, provides specialized care to approximately one million residents of Costa Rica’s capital. According to national antimicrobial resistance surveillance by the NRCB, the first NDM-producing isolate at HSJD was detected in June 2016, occurring two years after the initial report of NDM in Costa Rica, and corresponded to an A. baumannii isolate. Within the same week, an NDM-producing K. pneumoniae was identified in a second patient, and three months later a subsequent K. pneumoniae isolate carrying blaNDM was confirmed, totaling three NDM-positive cases reported in 2016. Between 2017 and 2020, only sporadic cases were reported, ranging from 3 to 11 isolates annually (Fig. 1A), suggesting limited circulation without evidence of sustained transmission prior to 2021.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, HSJD experienced a significant increase in patient admissions, in response to a national health system reorganization that temporarily eliminated geographic catchment areas, allowing unrestricted inter-hospital patient transfers from any region to the major hospitals. These events correlated with an exponential rise in the detection of NDM-producing bacteria at the HSJD. Accordingly, during the period from January 13, 2021, to the end of 2022, the hospital documented 122 cases of NDM-producing infections, confirmed by isolation and molecular methods in their clinical laboratory. The epicenter of the outbreak was the central intensive care unit (ICU Central), which housed patients with severe COVID-19, along with Picado ward, a unit temporarily repurposed to manage additional critically ill patients. Consequently, 63% (77/122) of the affected patients were diagnosed with SARS-CoV-2 infection prior to the detection of blaNDM-positive bacteria. As shown in Fig. 1B, both ICU Central and Picado units were epidemiologically linked to most other wards where NDM-positive cases emerged (i.e., connected wards presented new NDM cases within the same week). During this period, four patients were transferred to HSJD from other healthcare centers (HCC) already infected with NDM-positive carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (Fig. 1B). The most frequently isolated organisms were Enterobacterales (n = 116), including K. pneumoniae (n = 82, 67%), Enterobacter spp. (n = 21, 17%), Serratia marcescens (n = 8, 7%), E. coli (n = 4, 3%), and Morganella morganii (n = 1, 1%). Non-Enterobacterales NDM-positive isolates included Acinetobacter spp. (n = 5, 4%), detected for the first time on March 29, 2021, representing case #19, and a Pseudomonas aeruginosa (case #38) in May 2021. The isolates were obtained mainly from sputum (n = 60, 49%), followed by blood (n = 23, 19%), urine (n = 17, 14%), and other tissues (n = 15, 12%; Supplementary Table S1). Among the infected patients, the case-fatality rate was 51% (62/122). Within the COVID-19 patient subgroup, the mortality rate was even higher (46/77, 60%). These findings highlight the convergence of COVID-19 critical care pressures and NDM dissemination, with the ICU and Picado ward serving as central hubs for transmission within the hospital.

Through a structured weekly surveillance program aimed at early detection of NDM-producing bacterial colonization, a total of 71 carriers were identified, including 20 in 2021 and 51 in 2022 (Fig. 1C). In parallel, targeted environmental screenings conducted during epidemiological weeks 23 and 26 detected NDM-producing bacteria in both the Volio and Picado wards. In the Volio ward, an A. baumannii complex isolate was recovered from a bed surface, and a K. pneumoniae isolate was isolated from a nightstand. In the Picado ward, two K. pneumoniae isolates were obtained from distinct ventilator components (keyboard and screen). These findings highlight widespread silent carriage and persistent environmental reservoirs, likely contributing to sustained transmission during the outbreak.

Species distribution and resistance profiles of NDM-positive isolates

Of 247 blaNDM-positive isolates obtained at HSJD during 2021–2022, 113 were submitted to the NRCB for surveillance and further molecular characterization. These included samples from infected and colonized patients, as well as environmental sources. Species distribution was as follows: K. pneumoniae (72; 63.7%), Enterobacter spp. (16; 14.2%), A. baumannii complex (8; 7.1%), and E. coli (7; 6.2%). The remaining 10 isolates (9.6%) comprised Lelliottia amnigena, M. morganii, Proteus mirabilis, P. aeruginosa, and Serratia spp. Among carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), 63.5% exhibited multidrug resistance (MDR; non-susceptibility to ≥3 antimicrobial classes), while 36.5% were resistant to one or two classes. Colistin and fosfomycin showed the highest in vitro activity against CRE (Fig. 1D). Within the A. baumannii complex, four isolates were MDR and four showed resistance to one or two classes, with the greatest susceptibility to doxycycline, minocycline, and colistin (Supplementary Fig. S1). All isolates referred by HSJD were confirmed NDM-positive by PCR at the NRCB. Notably, 83.7% (87/104) of CRE isolates also carried blaCTX-M, explaining the high aztreonam resistance observed (Fig. 1D). These findings underscore the predominance of K. pneumoniae among NDM producers, the frequent co-occurrence of blaCTX-M, and the severely limited therapeutic options for these infections.

Nationwide surveillance expansion and species shift of NDM-producing bacteria

During 2021, the number of NDM-positive Gram-negative bacilli referred to the NRCB increased by 153.3%, rising from 137 isolates in 2020 to 347 by the end of 2021 (Fig. 2A). Although HSJD submitted the highest number of NDM-positive isolates that year, other HCCs also made a significant contribution to the number of submissions. The “Hospital Tony Facio Castro” (HTFC), the primary referral center for the Atlantic region, submitted the highest number of NDM-positive isolates prior to 2021. Additionally, the “Hospital México” (HMex), the second largest hospital in the Greater Metropolitan Area of San José, exhibited a growing number of NDM-positive referrals, reaching a peak in 2022. By the end of 2022, a total of 29 HCCs had submitted NDM-positive isolates to the NRCB. These nationwide surveillance data demonstrate that the dissemination of NDM-producing organisms was not restricted to HSJD and that extensive inter-hospital patient movements during the pandemic likely facilitated their spread across multiple HCCs.

Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, NDM-positive Acinetobacter spp. were the most frequently referred pathogens to the NRCB (Fig. 2B). A marked epidemiological shift occurred in 2020, when Klebsiella spp. became the predominant NDM-positive isolates nationwide, as reflected by isolates submitted from multiple HCCs across different regions of the country (Fig. 2C). From 2020 onward, most referred isolates were Klebsiella spp., including K. pneumoniae, K. quasipneumoniae, K. variicola, K. oxytoca, and K. aerogenes. In 2021, Klebsiella spp. accounted for 51% (177/347) of referred isolates, followed by Enterobacter spp., E. coli, and Acinetobacter spp. This trend continued in 2022, alongside an increase in the number of hospitals submitting isolates (Supplementary Fig. S2). Together, these findings highlight the nationwide expansion of NDM-producing Enterobacterales during the pandemic, with Klebsiella spp. becoming the dominant reservoir across HCCs.

Lineage diversity and dissemination of NDM producers across healthcare centers

Multiple lineages of NDM-producing species and strains associated with the HSJD outbreak, as well as others circulating in HCCs across Costa Rica, were identified from whole-genome sequences of 40 isolates (Table 1; Supplementary Tables S2–S3). High-quality assemblies generated from Illumina and Nanopore data enabled precise taxonomic assignment, detailed resolution of plasmid backbones, and reliable reconstruction of mobile genetic elements. This genomic resolution provided a robust framework for strain identification, plasmid evolution analyses, identification of shared resistance platforms, and contextualization of NDM dissemination across the national healthcare network.

Klebsiella spp

Among the sequenced Klebsiella isolates, twenty-one were confirmed as K. pneumoniae (98.9–99.2% identity to reference strain HS11286, CP003200.1), while one was identified as K. quasipneumoniae (99.0% identity to FDAARGOS_1503, CP083633.1; Table 1). The earliest NDM-positive K. pneumoniae isolate from 2014 was assigned to sequence type ST147, a recognized global high-risk clone11. This genome showed striking similarity (99.95% WGS identity) to a ST147 K. pneumoniae recovered in July 2021 from “Hospital Monseñor Sanabria” (HMS), the major referral center in Costa Rica’s Pacific region. Their chromosome lengths differed by only ~91 kbp and exhibited an ANI of 99.97%. Correlating with their high ANI values, both genomes showed identical genotyping profiles (i.e., siderophore, capsular and O antigens; Supplementary Table S4), underscoring their shared genetic background and the epidemiological relevance of this high-risk lineage.

Within the HSJD outbreak, two major K. pneumoniae STs were identified: ST342 (n = 9) and ST37 (n = 3). ST342 isolates were recovered between case 2 (15 January 2021) and case 39 (5 July 2021), as well as two environmental samples isolated in Picado ward (Table 1). This ST was additionally detected at other hospitals, including the HTFC (April–May 2021), the “Hospital Calderón Guardia” (HCG) (May 2021), and the “Specialized Center for COVID-19 Patients” (CEACO) (August 2021). All ST342 isolates showed identical genotyping profiles (Supplementary Table S4). The ST37 isolates were only detected early from HSJD (February 2021, cases 3 and 4) and from both patients and environmental sources (Table 1; Supplementary Table S4). Additionally, three unrelated K. pneumoniae STs were identified during 2021 in other HCCs: ST48 (HMex), ST882 (HTFC), and ST2098 (CEACO). Finally, only one K. quasipneumoniae isolate was identified, from HMex, classified as ST696-3LV (Supplementary Table S4).

The ANI-based phylogenetic reconstruction revealed that Costa Rican NDM-positive K. pneumoniae isolates were distributed across multiple internationally disseminated lineages, with distinct clustering patterns (Fig. 3A). The 2014 and 2021 ST147 isolates clustered closely with genomes from North America, Europe, and Asia, confirming the circulation of a globally disseminated high-risk clone in Costa Rica over multiple years. Although the Costa Rican ST147 genomes were highly similar, they did not appear to be strictly clonal (Fig. 3A), suggesting repeated introductions or independent dissemination events involving ST147-associated lineages within the Costa Rican healthcare system. In contrast, the ST342 isolates formed a highly compact lineage composed predominantly of Costa Rican genomes from multiple healthcare centers, supporting local dissemination and intra-country expansion during the 2021 outbreak period.

Other sequence types showed variable patterns of international relatedness. ST37 isolates clustered with genomes from the USA collected between 2015 and 2020, suggesting circulation of related lineages across the Americas during the past decade (Fig. 3A). ST48 and ST882 isolates also grouped with geographically diverse international genomes; however, evident sequence divergence suggested more distant phylogenetic relationships (Fig. 3A). In contrast, ST2098 isolate appeared to represent a rare lineage with limited genomic representation, as only a single related genome from the United Kingdom (2015) was identified in the PubMLST database. These ANI-based relationships were largely consistent with the population structure inferred using PopPUNK clustering analysis with 1,360 reference genomes from the European Survey of Carbapenemase-Producing Enterobacteriaceae (EuSCAPE; Supplementary Fig. S3).

Overall, these results demonstrate the co-circulation of diverse NDM-positive Klebsiella lineages across Costa Rica, including globally disseminated high-risk clones and locally emerging sequence types.

Enterobacter xiangfangensis

All Enterobacter isolates (n = 7) were identified as E. xiangfangensis (99.8% ANI to strain LMG27195), following the speciation criteria described by Wu et al.12. All HSJD isolates (n = 6) belonged to ST143 (Table 1). The earliest ST143 isolate corresponded to case 5 (epidemiological week 5) from the Picado ward (Table 1). Subsequent isolations were recovered from the ICU Central and Picado wards, although reports of NDM-producing Enterobacter were documented across additional HSJD wards (as shown in Fig. 1B). Notably, two ST143 isolates were obtained from patients with co-infections involving NDM-producing K. pneumoniae (cases 23 and 39; Table 1). The remaining Enterobacter isolate, recovered from the urine of a patient at HMex, was identified as ST90, which showed 97.0% ANI relative to the HSJD ST143 genomes, indicating a genetically distinct lineage circulating outside HSJD.

The ANI-based phylogenetic reconstruction of E. xiangfangensis revealed substantial genomic diversity among Costa Rican NDM-positive isolates (Fig. 3B). The Costa Rican ST143 genomes formed a compact cluster that was distinct from nearby international genomes from Europe, Africa, and Asia, represented by multiple STs. This pattern supports local expansion of ST143 as a dominant clone within the Costa Rican healthcare system, rather than dissemination of an already globally expanded ST143 lineage. The Costa Rican ST90 genome clustered closely with genomes from Europe, the Americas, and Asia. The clustering patterns observed in the ANI-based reconstruction were largely consistent with the population structure inferred using PopPUNK analysis with 836 E. xiangfangensis-like genomes (Supplementary Fig. S4).

E. coli

Sequence analyses revealed the presence of three distinct lineages among the isolates included in the study. The 2014 isolate (originating from the National Children’s Hospital, “HNN”) was classified as ST410, while the 2021 isolates were identified as ST1193 (HTFC) and ST156 (HCG). Remarkably, the 2021 isolates were obtained from the same patient, collected from different anatomical sites (urine and tissue) three months apart, who had been hospitalized in two separate HCCs (Table 1). The virulence gene profiles also differed between these isolates (Supplementary Table S5). Together, these findings demonstrate that the 2014 and 2021 NDM-producing E. coli isolates detected in Costa Rica belonged to genetically distinct lineages with divergent virulence profiles and unrelated evolutionary origins.

The ANI-based phylogenetic reconstruction of E. coli revealed the distribution of the Costa Rican genomes across multiple distinct phylogenetic branches and internationally related lineages (Fig. 3C), reflecting multiple independent introductions into the Costa Rican healthcare system. The ST410 isolate from 2014 clustered closely with genomes from the USA, Europe (France and Germany), Asia (China, Lebanon, India, Myanmar, and Thailand), and Australia, isolated between 2005 and 2016, supporting its association with a globally disseminated high-risk lineage. Similarly, the ST156 isolate grouped with genomes from multiple geographic regions, and especially to a USA isolate recovered in 2020. The ST1193 isolates formed a distinct cluster with isolates from China (2015) and the United Kingdom (2016). The clustering patterns observed in the ANI-based reconstruction were largely consistent with the population structure inferred using PopPUNK analysis based on 4,364 E. coli reference genomes from Horesh et al.13 (Supplementary Fig. S5), which similarly demonstrated substantial genomic diversity and separation among major E. coli lineages.

P. mirabilis

Two genetically distinct P. mirabilis isolates were identified, one from HSJD and one from HCG. The isolates showed an ANI of 99.0% and were classified as P. mirabilis ST99 and ST185, respectively (Fig. 3D). Global phylogenetic contextualization showed that the HSJD ST99 isolate clustered closely with isolates from the USA (2013-2021) and Brazil (2015-2017), supporting its circulation in the Americas. In contrast, the HCG ST185 isolate formed a more divergent branch, displaying greater sequence distance from other publicly available international ST185 genomes. Population clustering analysis based on 1,144 P. mirabilis genomes from the PubMLST database supported these findings (Supplementary Fig. S6).

A. baumannii

All six Acinetobacter isolates were identified as A. baumannii (100% support by rMLST). The isolate from 2014 was classified as ST105, whereas all the 2021 isolates were classified as ST250 (Table 1). The chromosomes from 2021 isolates were 100% identical to each other and were distantly related to the 2014 isolate (97.7% ANI). The first A. baumannii associated with the HSJD outbreak appeared only at case 19 (epidemiological week 19) in the Volio ward (Table 1). Thirteen days later (epidemiological week 21), an A. baumannii (ST250) was also detected in the HTFC. In that same week, case 23 from HSJD yielded an A. baumannii bloodstream infection in a patient who had presented an infection with a K. pneumoniae NDM-producer three days earlier and had been transferred from HCG ten days before. By epidemiological week 23, two additional ST250 isolates were identified at HSJD: one from the bloodstream of a patient in the ICU-SX ward and another from the Volio ward obtained through environmental sampling. Together, these findings indicate the emergence of a single, highly clonal ST250 lineage disseminating across wards and institutions within a short time frame.

The ANI-based phylogenetic reconstruction and population clustering analyses further supported these observations (Fig. 3E; Supplementary Fig. S7). The 2021 ST250 isolates clustered closely with an A. baumannii genome from Honduras collected in 2013 and two isolates from the USA collected in 2015 and 2019, respectively, suggesting association with a lineage circulating across the Americas over at least the past decade. In contrast, the 2014 ST105 isolate grouped with a distinct genome recovered in the USA in 2010. These findings indicate that the A. baumannii clones detected in Costa Rica belonged to genetically distinct regional lineages, with the 2021 ST250 lineage likely representing a recent introduction followed by rapid local dissemination.

Characterization of plasmid cargo

The extrachromosomal content of the isolates comprised 79 validated plasmids, ranging from 20 to 386 kb in length and averaging 2.0 ± 0.9 plasmids per organism (Table 1; Supplementary Table S3). These included 72 from Enterobacterales and seven from A. baumannii. Large plasmids ranging from 130–170 kb were the most abundant (Supplementary Fig. S8A). MobSuite analysis (mob_typer)14 characterized these plasmids predominantly as conjugative (81.0%, 64/79), encompassing nearly all Enterobacterales plasmids (83.3%, 60/72) and four of the seven Acinetobacter plasmids (Fig. 4A). An additional 13 plasmids were classified as mobilizable and only two highly similar non-mobilizable plasmids were detected. The latter corresponded to 112 kb and 116 kb plasmids recovered from ST147 K. pneumoniae isolates and shared 99.9% ANI.

Genomic context of the blaNDM

The blaNDM gene was found on conjugative plasmids (n = 35) in all Enterobacterales and one A. baumannii (2014 isolate), while all 2021 A. baumannii isolates harbored it in their chromosomes. All blaNDM alleles were classified as blaNDM-1, showing 100% identity to the reference sequence NG_049326.1.

The blaNDM-1-plasmids displayed considerable sequence diversity, with sizes ranging from 138,891–386,821 bp (Supplementary Table S3) and were classified into nine “pCR groups” (Fig. 4B). pCR-20 (141-145 kbp, N = 22) was the most widely disseminated plasmid among Enterobacterales in 2021, being identified from all HCCs and in three different bacterial species (Fig. 4B; Supplementary Fig. S8B). This plasmid corresponded to an IncA/C2 replicon and displayed a conserved topology consisting of three antimicrobial resistance (AMR) islands, named according to their proximity to oriC (Fig. 4C): “AMR_I”, a putative composite transposon carrying florR, tet(A), aph(6)-Ib, aph(3”)-Ib, and sul2 genes, flanked by IS26 (IS6-like) and ISVsa3 (IS91-like) insertion sequences; “AMR_II”, carrying a blaCMY-6 gene downstream of an IS1380 transposase and surrounded by tra/mob genes; and, “AMR_III” harboring the blaNDM-1 gene downstream of aac(6’)-Ib11, sul1, rmtC, bleomycin resistance genes, all near a complete class 1 integron (hereafter named “integron-AMR_III”) and Tn3 and IS110 transposases.

The first NDM-Enterobacterales identified in Costa Rica from 2014 carried blaNDM-1-harboring plasmids classified into a single CD-HIT-EST cluster (“pCR-21”), an IncA/C2 replicon detected in E. coli ST410 (HNN) and K. pneumoniae ST147 (HMex). The same plasmid was also identified in a 2021 K. quasipneumoniae recovered from a bronchial aspirate sample collected at HMex (Table 1). pCR-21 was closely related to pCR-20, with representative sequences showing an overall 99.5% identity (ANI). The plasmids differed primarily in the region near the origin of replication and by the absence of the AMR_I region in pCR-21 (Fig. 4D). This high similarity suggests that pCR-21 was potentially the parental plasmid from which pCR-20 evolved through subsequent acquisition of the AMR_I region.

The remaining nine blaNDM-1 harboring plasmids from Enterobacterales were classified into six independent clusters (Fig. 4B). All these plasmids encoded the blaNDM-1 within homologous AMR_III regions, located downstream of the integron-AMR_III, as previously described in pCR-20 and pCR-21. The larger blaNDM-1 plasmids ( > 280 kb, pCR-0, -2, -3 and -5) contained additional AMR determinants, including a class 1 integron (hereafter referred to as “integron-OXA”) carrying catB3, blaOXA-1, and aac(6’)-Ib, which was annotated as a complete integron in three plasmids and as a CALIN (i.e., Cluster of AttC sites Lacking Integrase16) region in five. Near this integron, the genes aac(3)-IIe and qnrB1 were found in four and seven plasmids, respectively. Another conserved region contained blaCTX-M-15, blaTEM-1, aph(6)-Id, aph(3”)-Ib, and sul2, forming a multi-gene resistance locus embedded within a genomic island rich in IS elements (IS1, IS3, IS5, IS6, IS110, IS1380, and Tn3). Except for pCR-5, this island was consistently located near the integron-OXA. Additionally, all nine plasmids carried the trimethoprim-resistance gene dfrA14, either within a distinct class 1 integron (herein referred to as “integron-dfrA”) or immediately downstream of the integron-OXA. The smallest plasmids of this group, pCR-14 (187 kbp) and pCR-17 (165 kbp), presented an integron, carrying blaOXA-1, blaTEM-1 and blaCTX-M-15, dfrA14, qnrB, among other genes, inserted in the AMR region I homolog of pCR-20.

The first NDM-A. baumannii identified in Costa Rica in 2014 carried the blaNDM-1 in a large plasmid (i.e., “pCR-8”, 218 kbp), with no similar element identified in 2021. Instead, all the A. baumannii isolates from 2021 harbored the blaNDM-1 in their chromosome. Despite the differences in genomic location, the blaNDM-1 genetic environment was identical and corresponded to the classical Tn125-like composite transposon flanked by two copies of ISAba125 (Supplementary Fig. S9), highlighting the high mobility and successful dissemination capacity of this transposon.

Sequence variation among blaNDM plasmids

Global sequence analysis of the most widely disseminated blaNDM-1-carrying plasmid type (pCR-20) revealed the presence of four circulating variants during 2021 (Fig. 5A). “pCR-20 variant 1” (141 kbp) was identified in 15 isolates (68%, 15/22), recovered from K. pneumoniae (ST342, ST37, ST48) and E. xiangfangensis (ST143, ST90). This plasmid was detected from case 2 to case 86 of the HSJD outbreak and was also present in isolates from other HCCs (namely, HCG, CEACO, and HMex) between May and December 2021. The “pCR-20 variant 2” (145 kbp) was identified in five isolates from early 2021 cases (January to May), including HSJD outbreak cases 2, 4, and 5, as well as isolates from HCG and HTFC. This variant was present in K. pneumoniae (ST342, ST37), E. xiangfangensis (ST143), and P. mirabilis. pCR-20 variants 1 and 2 differed by only 4,607 bp within the AMR_I region, as pCR-20 variant 1 carried an additional IS91 sequence (Fig. 5A). Remarkably, both variants were identified in two different isolates recovered from the same patient (HSJD case 2), namely from a K. pneumoniae (hematoma origin) and a P. mirabilis (blood origin) (Table 1). “pCR-20 variant 3” (142 kbp) was recovered from a K. pneumoniae isolate (tissue origin) at HMS. This variant differed from the others by lacking the floR gene within the AMR_I region. Finally, “pCR-20 variant 4” (144 kbp), detected in a K. pneumoniae isolate (urine origin) from CEACO, harbored an additional IS5 element and lacked an IS3 transposase (Fig. 5A). These findings demonstrate the sequence diversification of pCR-20 plasmids during the outbreak and their dissemination across multiple species, sequence types, and HCCs.

Genetic connectivity and recombination between resistance platforms

In addition to the blaNDM-1-carrying plasmids, we identified 44 other plasmids harboring AMR genes (38 from Enterobacterales and six from A. baumannii isolates). These plasmids ranged in length from 19,569 to 331,864 bp. Enterobacterales non-NDM-harboring plasmids harbored several replicon types, being the IncF-types the most common (Supplementary Table S3). Notably, several Enterobacterales plasmids carried blaCTX-M variants (Table 1), indicating convergence of extended-spectrum β-lactam and carbapenem resistance within the same bacterial population. We also detected a rare double-carbapenemase genotype in E. xiangfangensis, where blaIMP-18 was located on a 36-kbp IncP mobilizable plasmid distinct from the blaNDM-1 backbone (Supplementary Fig. S10), highlighting the potential for independent acquisition of high-priority resistance determinants. In contrast, non-NDM A. baumannii plasmids contributed minimally to the broader resistance landscape.

Pairwise comparisons among all validated plasmids revealed sequence similarities between the blaNDM(+) IncA/C2 and some blaNDM(-) plasmids from other incompatibility groups (Supplementary Fig. S8D). In total, 31 blaNDM(-) plasmids harbored homologous sequences, covering a range of 8.06-62.00% of the IncA/C2 plasmid sequences, with ANI values ranging from 81.96-99.99%. Among these, IncFIB(K) plasmids, from groups pCR-7, -16, -18, and -19, exhibited the highest ANI and coverage values. These plasmids carried blaCTX-M-15 and qnrB1, genes that were also identified in the larger blaNDM(+) plasmids.

Structural clustering using the pling pipeline17, which incorporates rearrangement-aware comparisons to evaluate plasmid relatedness, further supported the interconnectivity between blaNDM(+) and blaNDM(-) plasmids (n = 15; Fig. 5B). Specifically, “IncFIB(K) blaNDM(-)/blaCTX-M(-)” plasmids were structurally linked exclusively to “IncFIB(K) blaNDM(-)/blaCTX-M(+)” plasmids, whereas “double replicon IncFIB(K)-IncA/C2 blaNDM(+)/blaCTX-M(+)” plasmids formed a structural bridge connecting the “IncFIB(K) blaNDM(-)/blaCTX-M(+)” and the “IncA/C2 blaNDM(+)/blaCTX-M-15(-)” plasmids.

Among the 40 outbreak isolates sequenced, 15 harbored blaNDM-1 and blaCTX-M-15 on independent plasmids, whereas six isolates harbored the “double replicon IncFIB(K)-IncA/C2 blaNDM(+)/blaCTX-M(+)” (Table 1). Alignment of these elements revealed that the fusion of the independent plasmids resulted in a highly conserved double-replicon megaplasmid, without major rearrangements or sequence disruptions (Fig. 5C). The annotation of the overlapping sequences showed the IS91- and IS1-family insertion sequences, together with aph(6)-Id, aph(3”)-Ib, and sul2 genes at the putative recombination junctions between the IncA/C2 and IncFIB(K) plasmid backbones. Together, these findings support the occurrence of modular recombination and plasmid fusion events linking IncFIB(K) and IncA/C2 backbones during the evolution of multidrug resistance megaplasmids. A schematic model summarizing the proposed IncFIB(K)-IncA/C2 plasmid fusion process is shown in Fig. 5D.

Outbreak-associated IncA/C2-blaNDM plasmids align with global reference plasmids

Comparative analysis revealed strong structural similarity between outbreak-associated blaNDM(+) plasmids and reference plasmids deposited in the NCBI RefSeq database. Among 893 NCBI deposited plasmids analyzed, 16 of them were found closely related by nucleotide identity and structure to the study Enterobacterales pCR-20 and pCR-21 plasmids (Fig. 6A). All of them carried the blaNDM-1 variant and belonged to the IncA/C2 incompatibility group (Supplementary Table S6). These included elements described from the USA, Canada, Australia, Denmark, Kenya, and China (Fig. 6A). The pCR-21 2014-plasmids from our study were virtually identical to plasmids described in Argentina (Fig. 6B), two of them also collected from 2014 (Supplementary Table S6).

blaNDM-positive megaplasmids were also identified among 16 related reference plasmids retrieved from NCBI, with sizes ranging from 267 to 335 kb. In agreement with our findings, these plasmids carried multiple incompatibility replicons, including IncF, IncR, and IncU, in addition to IncA/C2 (Supplementary Table S6). The NCBI blaNDM-megaplasmids similarly displayed composite resistance architectures, co-harboring blaNDM together with blaCMY-6 and blaCTX-M-15. Notably, the largest plasmid in the NCBI dataset exhibited a topology comparable to that of the largest plasmid characterized in this study, although substantial structural differences were observed (pCR-0; Fig. 6C).

The closest reference plasmid to the 2014 A. baumannii plasmid (pCR-8) was a plasmid from A. baumannii strain 13A297n (CP129246.1), isolated from a human urine sample and carrying blaNDM-9 in France18. The two plasmids shared an ANI of 98.33% but presented several structural differences (Fig. 6D). Notably, the backbone of pCR-8 also showed high structural similarity to plasmid pAba7804b (CP022285.1), recovered from a human patient in Mexico, with 99.98% identity by BLASTn. However, pAba7804b lacks the blaNDM-1 resistance locus present in pCR-8 (Fig. 6D). These findings suggest that the outbreak-associated pCR-8 evolved through insertion of the blaNDM-1 locus into a preexisting worldwide disseminated A. baumannii plasmid backbone.

Discussion

This study illustrates how blaNDM can progress from sporadic detection to nationwide dissemination when high-risk bacterial hosts, adaptable plasmid backbones, and healthcare system stressors converge. Integrating nationwide surveillance with detailed outbreak-associated genomics, we show that the Costa Rican NDM outbreak was driven primarily by plasmid-mediated spread across diverse Enterobacterales species and lineages, rather than by the expansion of a single clone. The identification of IncA/C2 plasmids as central vectors, capable of interspecies transfer, rapid diversification, and fusion with IncF plasmids to generate multidrug-resistant megaplasmids, underscores the pivotal role of plasmid evolution in amplifying carbapenem resistance under pandemic-associated selective pressures. Collectively, these findings demonstrate how pandemic conditions can accelerate the expansion of pre-existing resistance determinants, transforming localized carbapenemase circulation into a nationwide outbreak and highlighting the need for genomic surveillance strategies that prioritize high-risk mobile genetic platforms alongside high-risk clones.

Before the COVID-19 pandemic, the Costa Rican Social Security System (CCSS) operated through sectorized healthcare networks with defined hospital catchment areas. This structure was suspended during the pandemic following the establishment of specialized COVID-19 units, the CEACO facility, and a centralized bed-allocation system, enabling unrestricted inter-hospital transfers nationwide. Consequently, HSJD experienced a marked increase in patient volume and diversity. During this period, blaNDM was likely introduced into the central ICU through unrecognized colonized patients, triggering an outbreak with an initial 100% mortality rate. As ICU capacity was subsequently reallocated for new COVID-19 admissions, NDM-colonized or infected but COVID-recovered patients were transferred to other wards, driving hospital-wide dissemination. Our analysis identified the ICU Central and Picado wards as the primary epicenters of intrahospital transmission, consistent with prior reports of carbapenem-resistant organism (CRO) outbreaks in critical care settings19–23. Furthermore, the outbreak at HSJD was intensified by further admissions of patients previously infected with NDM-positive bacteria from other HCCs. Remarkably, environmental screening demonstrated contamination of high-touch surfaces (mattresses, nightstands, ventilator machine panels) with CRO carrying the same NDM determinants and vehicles, underscoring the significance of environmental reservoirs in facilitating hospital transmission. These findings suggest that compromised infection control practices during pandemic-related healthcare disruptions may have significantly exacerbated the transmission dynamics observed24–26, emphasizing the necessity for stringent infection prevention measures during healthcare crises.

Surveillance data indicate a significant shift in the predominant pathogens carrying the blaNDM-1 gene, transitioning from A. baumannii to Enterobacterales, notably Klebsiella species, from 2020 onwards. Genomic data revealed the initial introduction of Enterobacterales carrying blaNDM from Nicaraguan patients in 2014, aligning with global reports of the international dissemination of antimicrobial-resistant organisms6,27. Moreover, the main IncA/C2 blaNDM-plasmid was found to be nearly identical to elements described across the world, especially from Argentina, also described in 2014. On the other hand, in Acinetobacter strains, the chromosomal integration of a blaNDM carrying fragment may have acted as a limiting factor, influencing the epidemiological shift towards plasmid-mediated dissemination in Enterobacterales. These findings underscore the dual routes of blaNDM introduction into Costa Rica, reveal regional differences in dissemination patterns, and highlight the urgent need for reinforced infection control measures and national AMR containment strategies.

Genomic analyses revealed substantial taxonomic and sequence type diversity among NDM-producing organisms circulating in Costa Rican healthcare settings. We identified globally disseminated high-risk clones such as K. pneumoniae ST14711, as well as locally emerging lineages like ST342 and ST37. Thus, this study provides clear evidence of both clonal expansion and marked horizontal gene transfer (HGT) events among CRO, spanning across species and sequence types, phenomena increasingly reported in global AMR outbreaks28–30. In Enterobacterales, a central finding is the identification of IncA/C2 pCR-20 as the most widely disseminated plasmid during the outbreak, detected in multiple species, including K. pneumoniae, E. xiangfangensis, and P. mirabilis, at many HCCs. Comparative genomic analyses suggested that pCR-20 is an evolved variant of the 2014 IncA/C2 plasmid pCR-21, sharing a conserved backbone with additional acquired AMR genes, a pattern consistent with previously described modular evolution of IncA/C2 plasmids31,32, including broad host range and high dissemination potential driven by a conserved conjugative backbone and a modular architecture that facilitates rapid acquisition of resistance determinants. A striking example of interspecies plasmid transfer was observed in the earliest recorded HSJD outbreak case 2, in which two different Enterobacterales isolates (K. pneumoniae from a hematoma and P. mirabilis from blood) harbored the same pCR-20 plasmid. This finding highlights the rapid and flexible dissemination potential of this plasmid backbone, emphasizing its role in shaping the local epidemiology of blaNDM-1 in Costa Rica.

Plasmid analysis revealed extensive diversity in size, structure, and mobility potential among blaNDM-1 -carrying elements. All blaNDM-1-plasmids were classified as conjugative or mobilizabled, aligning with the demonstrated elevated horizontal dissemination between species and strains in this study. Moreover, evolutionary evidence was clearly observed, with four distinct variants of the major pCR-20 plasmid identified, all circulating during the 2021 outbreak. These variants differed by insertions of transposable elements or accessory resistance regions but maintained a conserved backbone. Beyond pCR-20 intra-plasmid variation, multiple large plasmids displayed evidence of fusion between blaNDM-1- and blaCTX-M-15-bearing elements. These megaplasmids ( > 287 kbp) carried dual replicon types (e.g., IncA/C2 and IncF types) and multiple resistance islands, including additional integrons and transposons harboring many AMR genes. Structural and phylogenetic analyses demonstrated that plasmids containing both blaNDM-1 and blaCTX-M genes acted as bridges between clusters of plasmids originally carrying either gene alone, suggesting stepwise fusion events29,33,34. This configuration is clinically significant: while NDM enzymes hydrolyze nearly all β-lactams except monobactams, the acquisition of blaCTX-M-15 overcomes aztreonam susceptibility35, thereby significantly expanding the resistance profile within the β-lactam group. These observations suggest a selective advantage under therapeutic pressure and highlight the contribution of plasmid fusion to the evolution of extensively drug-resistant phenotypes.

Comparative genomic analysis of the outbreak-associated blaNDM-1-carrying plasmids revealed strong structural and sequence similarity with reference plasmids deposited in international databases. The pCR-21 plasmid, first identified in 2014 from K. pneumoniae and E. coli, displayed the highest sequence identity to global IncA/C2 plasmids, including the initial blaNDM-plasmid detected from USA, namely the pNDM-US, from K. pneumoniae in 201036. pCR-21 plasmid also showed high sequence identity to variants recovered in Argentina in 2014 from Enterobacterales37,38, and from a 2019 K. pneumoniae isolate in Canada (GenBank accession number PV023092.1), and from isolates from Australia39 and China (NZ_MH263652.1), reflecting its global dissemination. In contrast, the evolved pCR-20 variant from 2021 showed slightly lower similarity to these references, reflecting its adaptation through the acquisition of additional resistance genes and mobile genetic elements during the outbreak in Costa Rica. Furthermore, structural comparisons indicated that many of the outbreak plasmids, including megaplasmids, shared conserved backbones and recombined AMR regions with international counterparts, particularly those carrying blaNDM-1 and blaCTX-M-15. These parallels support either a common ancestral origin or convergent evolutionary trajectories driven by selection for multi-resistance configurations. Notably, several large reference plasmids also harbored dual replicon types and multiple integrons, consistent with the fusion events observed in the Costa Rican plasmid dataset.

The emergence of A. baumannii harboring blaNDM, identified in 2014 within a 218-kbp plasmid (pCR-8), lacked evidence of foreign introduction to the country, suggesting independent but dual introduction of blaNDM-1 determinants in Costa Rica. The pCR-8 backbone showed high structural similarity to a plasmid recovered from an A. baumannii isolate in France in 2013, but carrying the blaNDM-9 gene variant18, as well as to an NDM-negative plasmid recovered from an A. baumannii isolate in Mexico in 200640,41. This Mexican isolate carried a blaOXA-58 gene in another plasmid within a ISAba3 sequence. Interestingly, in 2021, a private hospital in Costa Rica reported the appearance of four cases of A. baumannii (ST126) NDM-producing isolates, carrying the blaNDM-1 and blaOXA-58 genes within a 73 kbp-plasmid42, further highlighting the convergent evolution and adaptation of A. baumannii resistance plasmids within a relatively short time frame.

In conclusion, the outbreak illustrates how compromised infection control during COVID-19 overburdened ICUs facilitated the rapid propagation of blaNDM-1-positive organisms, primarily through environmental contamination and horizontal plasmid transfer between species25,26. WGS proved instrumental, associating patient and colonized bacterial strains with those of environmental origin, as well as distinguishing clonal spread from horizontal gene transfer, enabling precise mapping of transmission networks20. The detection of colonized patients and contaminated environments supports expanding surveillance beyond clinical infections to include systematic screening and environmental sampling. Finally, the integration of plasmid genomics into national AMR surveillance frameworks is essential to preempt future outbreaks driven by mobile resistance determinants43.

Methods

Study design and case definition

This study was conducted as a retrospective, nationwide analysis of carbapenem-resistant, blaNDM-positive Gram-negative bacilli isolates referred to the NRCB from 2014 to 2022 across the NBLN, as part of the national laboratory-based AMR surveillance. Confirmed clinical cases or colonization isolates were defined by the demonstration of blaNDM, detected via PCR and confirmed through phenotypic carbapenem resistance at the NRCB.

CRO case detection and colonization screening at HSJD

Each case was defined as a single patient with at least one clinical associated isolate positive for blaNDM by phenotypic and molecular testing, irrespective of species, specimen type, or clinical syndrome.

As a response to the raise in the detection NDM-producing bacteria, a weekly carbapenem-resistance surveillance was conducted at HSJD using a structured screening protocol for colonization with CRO. Rectal swabs were collected from patients in designated high-risk areas, including intensive care units (ICUs), burn units, transplant wards, and hematology-oncology services. Additional screening was performed for all patients transferred from other healthcare centers, with rectal swabs and, when applicable, samples from invasive devices (e.g., endotracheal tubes, urinary catheters). Swabs were streaked directly onto selective chromogenic Carba agar (bioMérieux), and suspected CRO colonies were subjected to confirmatory testing. Phenotypic detection of carbapenemase production was performed using NG-Test CARBA 5 (NG Biotech), and molecular confirmation of resistance genes was conducted using the Xpert Carba-R assay (Cepheid). Species identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were performed using the VITEK 2 system (bioMérieux) and matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization–time of flight mass spectrometry, MALDI-TOF (microflex LT, Bruker, BD, MA, US).

Surveillance in predefined wards with prior detection of blaNDM-positive cases was conducted weekly until three consecutive weeks without new isolations were achieved. Colonization status was defined by isolation of CRO from rectal swabs or other non-sterile body sites in the absence of clinical signs of infection. In cases where isolates were recovered from other sites (e.g., urine, respiratory samples) without corresponding symptoms, patients were classified as colonized rather than infected. Data from colonization screening were incorporated into the broader genomic and epidemiological analyses.

Environmental sampling

To evaluate the role of the nosocomial environment as reservoir in the dissemination of NDM-producing bacteria at HSJD, the laboratory implemented an environmental screening pilot test in Picado and Volio wards. Accordingly, sterile swabs were collected from frequently touched surfaces in these two hospital units. Sampling sites included a patient’s bed (mattress) in the Volio ward, where the first Acinetobacter case had been identified, the nightstand adjacent to this bed, as well as a ventilator unit and its monitor screen at a bed in the Picado ward. All environmental isolates were recovered using ChromID® CARBA agar as described above.

National surveillance and epidemiological metadata

Bacterial isolates were referred to the NRCB-INCIENSA by the participating NBLN institutions. All isolates were processed for taxonomic identification by Vitek 2 C and MALDI-TOF systems and confirmed as blaNDM-positive by conventional PCR, as described previously44. Data collected included the hospital of origin, sample source, date of collection, anonymized patient demographics, and ward location. Epidemiological trends were analyzed in relation to healthcare-associated clusters and national alert bulletins. Geographical distribution of cases according to submitting HCC was plotted in Microsoft Power BI (Azure Maps visual).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

ASTs were performed on all isolates included in this study, including disk diffusion tests according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI), M0245, the broth microdilution method for colistin, following CLSI, M0746, and Sensititre system (ThermoFisher Scientific, MA, US) using ARGNF-panel, following the manufacturer´s guidelines. Moreover, to identify carbapenemase activity, the modified carbapenem inactivation method (mCIM) was performed47. All AST results were interpreted according to CLSI M100, Ed. 23, 2023, except for TGC (tigecycline), colistin and fosfomycin, for which EUCAST (https://www.eucast.org/clinical_breakpoints) and ReLAVRA48 breakpoints were employed.

WGS and bioinformatic analyses

To investigate the genetic context of blaNDM during the HSJD intrahospital outbreak and within the broader circulation of NDM-producing bacteria in Costa Rica since 2014, a representative panel of isolates was selected for WGS, including multiple species and HCCs. The panel comprised: (i) the first three NDM-positive isolates reported in 2014 (E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and an A. baumannii); (ii) 23 isolates from the HSJD outbreak, spanning case 2 (15 January 2021) to case 86 (27 September 2021); and (iii) 14 additional isolates collected in 2021 from five other hospitals (HTFC, HCG, CEACO, HMex, and HMS). Overall, the dataset included 22 Klebsiella spp., seven Enterobacter, six A. baumannii, three E. coli, and two P. mirabilis isolates.

All isolates (N = 40) were sequenced using a hybrid approach combining Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) and Illumina MiSeq platforms. Genomic DNA was extracted from fresh cultures grown on Columbia blood agar (bioMérieux) at 36 °C for 18 h using the Maxwell RSC Cultured Cells DNA Kit (Promega, WI, USA) on a Maxwell automated DNA extractor, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Illumina libraries were prepared using 100–500 ng of input DNA and sequenced as paired-end 250-bp reads on a MiSeq instrument with 500-cycle v2 chemistry (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Long-read sequencing was performed on the ONT MinION device following previously described methods49, using flow cells FLO-MIN106 version R9.4. Libraries were prepared with the Rapid Barcoding Kit SQK-RBK004 starting with 400 ng of high molecular weight DNA from each isolate, and according to the Oxford Nanopore protocol (RBK_9054_V2_revE_23jan2018). Base calling was performed using the Guppy protocol from ONT (https://nanoporetech.com/document/Guppy-protocol). A sequencing coverage depth of 75x to 100x was obtained.

Hybrid genome assembly

Hybrid genome assemblies were generated with Unicycler v0.4.850. Genome quality was assessed with CheckM v1.2.351. and assembly-stats v1.0.1. https://github.com/sanger-pathogens/assembly-stats). When assembled large contigs ( > 200 kbp) resulted in linear contigs, autocycler pipeline was applied52, and the output was cleaned using Polypolish v.0.6.153 with the trimmed Illumina reads. Finally, all contigs' starting position were set with dnaapler v4.1.3.054.

Chromosomal and Plasmid prediction

Chromosomes and plasmid sequences were identified from the final assembled genomes using a consensus approach combining PlasFlow v1.155, PlasmidFinder v2.1.656, PlasmidVerify (via ViralVerify with the -p flag for plasmid mode) v1.157, MOB-suite (mob_recon v3.1.9)14, and MOBFinder (v1.0)58. A contig was classified as chromosome or plasmid if at least two algorithms supported the prediction. Chromosomes were validated by circularization in a single contig. Validated plasmids were those that were circular and had a contig length >10 kbp. Contigs <10 kbp were excluded from the analyses. To predict the mobility class, MOBtyper was used14.

Gene annotation

Genes were annotated using Bakta v1.9.459. AMR genes were detected using AMRFinderPlus v4.0.360. Plasmid replicon types were assigned according to the outputs of PlasmidFinder and MOB-suite (mob_typer). Comparative analysis of plasmid sequences was performed using BLASTn61. Virulence profiling for K. pneumoniae was performed using Kleborate and Kaptive via the Pathogenwatch platform. For E. coli, virulence-associated genes were identified using AMRFinderPlus with a minimum identity and coverage threshold of 95%. Class 1 integrons and attC arrays were identified with IntegronFinder v2.062 and annotated for cassette content.

Plasmid grouping and structural analyses

Plasmid sequences were first clustered using CD-HIT15 in two steps. First, a local identity-based clustering analysis was performed (with parameter -G 0) using the default nucleotide identity threshold (-c 0.9) together with -s 0.9 and -aL 0.5 to identify broadly related plasmids sharing substantial sequence similarity despite moderate size variation or partial structural conservation. Subsequently, a global identity-based clustering analysis (-G 1) was performed using the same identity threshold and clustering parameters to further explore intragroup diversity. Then, ANI was calculated with FastANI v1.3363 and Mash v2.364 distances were computed to estimate global plasmid relatedness. Neighbor-Joining trees were generated in R v4.3.1. Plots were generated using the ggplot2 and ggrepel packages. To assess gene content similarity and structural conservation, a plasmid network analysis was conducted using the pling pipeline v1.0.217. Plasmid and chromosomal sequences were compared using BLASTn, with only alignments >1000 bp considered for downstream analyses. Synteny and structural comparisons between representative plasmids were visualized using genoplotR65 or MAUVE66. Structural rearrangements, fusion events, and modular acquisition of resistance genes were identified based on conserved backbone regions, replicon content, and shared resistance genes. All complete plasmid sequences from this study were compared against a curated dataset of 858 publicly available blaNDM-positive plasmids from NCBI RefSeq. Additional targeted sequence comparisons were performed using Samtools v1.10.267, BLAST, and RStudio v2024.04.168.

Species identification and MLST

Species assignment was confirmed using ANI. Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) was performed using fastmlst69 and through upload of genomes to the Pathogenwatch (https://pathogen.watch/) and pubMLST (https://pubmlst.org/). Identification at the species level for Enterobacter spp. was performed through ANI pairwise comparisons of the assembled genomes against the updated Enterobacter spp. catalog described by Wu and colleagues12, as described earlier44.

Population structure of study genomes within global genomic contexts

Core genome clusters were generated using PopPUNK (Population Partitioning Using Nucleotide K-mers70) to delineate bacterial lineages and assess population structure based on core and accessory genome content. Outputs were visualized using GrapeTree (https://achtman-lab.github.io/GrapeTree/MSTree_holder.html). Nodes represent individual genomes, with reference strains from public databases in light blue and study isolates in red. Node size reflects the number of genomes represented in each node. For Klebsiella pneumoniae, E. coli, and Acinetobacter baumannii, we downloaded and used the poppunk databases available (https://bacpop.org/poppunk-databases/). For Enterobacter and Proteus, we downloaded all PubMLST genomes and created a database using popPUNK (–create-db). In the case of Enterobacter, we filtered the database for all closely related E. xiangfangensis genomes, using fastANI63. With the popPUNK outcomes, we identified the clusters and closest genomes for each isolate, and we downloaded the relevant STs from the BIGdb from either PubMLST, NCBI Genbank, or PasteurMLST (https://bigsdb.pasteur.fr/) as corresponded. Then, ANIclustermap was run to generate a NJ dendrogram based on the ANI distances, using the representative genomes for each ST. The resulting tree was then incorporated into iTOL71 for annotations and illustration. Metadata of the reference sequences can be found in Supplementary Table S7.
