Background

Early-onset neonatal sepsis (EOS), occurring within the first week of life, remains a significant cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality [1]. Group B Streptococcus (GBS) is a predominant pathogen responsible for EOS, typically acquired through vertical transmission from a colonized mother during labor or delivery [2]. The implementation of universal late-trimester maternal GBS screening and intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (IAP) has markedly reduced the incidence of early-onset GBS disease [3–6]. However, cases can still occur in infants born to mothers with negative screening results, due to factors such as false-negative tests [7], rapid intrapartum colonization [8], or a high maternal bacterial inoculum [7, 9]. We present a case of a term neonate with culture-proven GBS sepsis and meningitis born to a mother with a documented negative GBS screen.

Case report

A male infant was born vaginally at 39 weeks and 6 days of gestation to a 28-year-old G2P2 mother. The mother’s blood type was O, Rh-positive. Her pregnancy was complicated by gestational diabetes mellitus, well-controlled by diet. She had no history of hypertension, pre-eclampsia, or overt infectious diseases. Prenatal care was regular. A rectovaginal GBS culture performed at 39 weeks of gestation was reported as negative. Multiple urine analyses during pregnancy showed 0–5 white blood cells per high-power field. There was no history of prolonged rupture of membranes; amniotic fluid was clear.

The infant’s birth weight was 3750 g. Apgar scores were 8 at 1 min (points deducted for tone and cry), 9 at 5 min (improved tone), and 9 at 10 min. At 38 min of life, he developed tachypnea, grunting, and generalized hypotonia and was admitted to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit.

On admission, vital signs were: temperature 36.6 °C, heart rate 134 bpm, respiratory rate 50 bpm, blood pressure 71/35 mmHg. Physical examination revealed grunting, coarse breath sounds with rales, and significantly decreased muscle tone. Capillary refill time was > 3 s. Initial arterial blood gas under oxygen therapy showed metabolic acidosis (pH 7.188, base excess − 10.5 mmol/L, lactate 4.9 mmol/L). Initial complete blood count showed leukocytosis (24.56 × 10⁹/L). C-reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin (PCT) were initially normal.

Empirical antibiotic therapy with intravenous penicillin and ceftazidime was initiated for suspected sepsis. The authors acknowledge that this regimen deviates from standard early-onset sepsis guidelines (e.g., AAP/ACOG 2019), which recommend ampicillin and an aminoglycoside. Ceftazidime was chosen empirically due to the attending physician’s concern for possible healthcare-associated Gram-negative coverage given the infant’s early presentation in the neonatal unit; however, this decision is not supported by standard protocols and should be considered a deviation from the standard of care [8].

On the second day of life, the infant developed fever and increased work of breathing. Repeat labs revealed a sharp drop in white blood cell count (4.99 × 10⁹/L) and elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 9.5 mg/L, PCT 13.54 ng/mL). Blood culture drawn on admission yielded Gram-positive cocci, later identified as Group B Streptococcus. Lumbar puncture was performed: CSF was faintly yellow, with weakly positive Pandy’s test, 22 × 10⁶/L white blood cells, and elevated protein (562 mg/L). CSF culture was sterile, likely due to prior antibiotic administration.

Antibiotic therapy was escalated to intravenous vancomycin based on: (1) confirmed GBS bacteremia with concurrent meningitis (elevated CSF protein and white blood cells), (2) the need for reliable central nervous system penetration, as vancomycin achieves therapeutic CSF levels in inflamed meninges, and (3) clinical deterioration with persistent fever and worsening respiratory status despite 48 h of penicillin therapy. Ceftazidime was discontinued as it provided no additional coverage against GBS.

The infant’s clinical course was complicated by hypotension, oliguria, and anemia requiring fluid resuscitation and packed red blood cell transfusion. He also developed unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia treated with phototherapy. Cranial ultrasound and subsequent MRI revealed small hemorrhagic foci in the left centrum semiovale and periventricular area, managed conservatively with vitamin K.

The infant showed gradual clinical improvement. Antibiotics were de-escalated back to penicillin after clinical stabilization and normalization of inflammatory markers. A follow-up lumbar puncture on day 14 showed persistent CSF abnormalities: Pandy’s test remained positive, and protein level increased from 562 mg/L to 840 mg/L (0.84 g/L). Although the protein level worsened numerically, this finding is not necessarily indicative of clinical deterioration. In the resolution phase of bacterial meningitis, CSF protein may remain elevated or even increase transiently due to ongoing intrathecal inflammation, protein exudation, and impaired blood-brain barrier repair, while the white blood cell count normalizes. Importantly, the infant’s clinical status had significantly improved by day 14, with resolution of fever, respiratory support, and normalization of peripheral inflammatory markers. Therefore, the persistent CSF protein abnormality was interpreted as a resolving, rather than worsening, process. A repeat cranial MRI showed resolution of the previous hemorrhagic foci. The infant completed a total of 17 days of intravenous antibiotic therapy and was discharged home in good condition.

Discussion

This case illustrates early-onset GBS disease in a term neonate born to a mother with a documented negative GBS screening test. Current guidelines recommend universal GBS screening between 36 and 37 weeks of gestation. A negative screening result close to term is highly predictive of the absence of GBS colonization at delivery and is the basis for withholding IAP [9]. However, false-negative results can occur due to laboratory error, low colonization burden, or recent acquisition of GBS after screening [10]. Several case series have reported neonatal GBS sepsis despite negative maternal screening. Zhao et al. described 10 such cases in China [11], and Pulver et al. documented continued early-onset GBS infections in the era of intrapartum prophylaxis [12]. In this case, the mother had several urine analyses showing trace leukocytes, which, although not diagnostic, may indicate low-grade genitourinary tract inflammation or colonization not detected by a single swab.

The infant’s presentation with respiratory distress, lethargy, and hypotonia is classic for neonatal GBS sepsis. The rapid progression to hypotension and the presence of meningitis underscore the virulence of this organism. The initially normal CRP and PCT levels illustrate the nonspecific and evolving nature of early sepsis markers, adding to the diagnostic challenge [13]. Definitive diagnosis relied on blood culture, emphasizing its critical role [14].

The development of intracranial hemorrhagic lesions, though uncommon, has been reported in severe neonatal sepsis and may be related to coagulation disturbances, vascular endothelial injury, or thrombocytopenia [15]. Their resolution on follow-up imaging is reassuring.

An important aspect of this case is the sterile CSF culture despite clinical and biochemical findings consistent with meningitis (elevated white blood cells and protein). This discrepancy is most likely attributable to the administration of empirical antibiotics prior to lumbar puncture. Even a single dose of penicillin can rapidly sterilize the CSF, making culture confirmation difficult while inflammation persists. This phenomenon underscores a critical diagnostic principle: a negative CSF culture does not rule out meningitis, particularly when antibiotics have been initiated. Therefore, lumbar puncture remains essential in neonates with positive blood cultures or strong clinical suspicion of systemic infection. Diagnosing meningitis, even without a positive CSF culture, has significant implications for antibiotic duration and the potential need for agents with better CNS penetration.

A limitation of this case report is the lack of microbiological characterization beyond species identification. Serotyping (e.g., Ia, III) and antibiotic susceptibility testing including MICs were not performed on the isolated GBS strain, as these tests are not routinely available at our institution. Nevertheless, published data from Chinese neonatal GBS cohorts indicate that serotype III is the predominant type associated with invasive disease [16]. This case also highlights the inherent limitations of a screening-based prevention strategy. Compared with traditional culture, rapid polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays have a sensitivity of 97% and specificity of 100% [17]. Maternal colonization status is a snapshot in time, and false negatives can occur. Furthermore, IAP does not prevent late-onset disease or GBS-related stillbirths. These gaps have renewed interest in developing an effective GBS vaccine [18]. A maternal vaccine administered during pregnancy could provide passive immunity to the neonate at birth, protect against both early- and late-onset disease, and circumvent false-negative screening results.

Conclusion

Despite advances in prenatal screening and prophylaxis, GBS remains a threat to newborns. This report of a term infant with early-onset GBS sepsis and meningitis born to a GBS-screen-negative mother serves as a critical reminder of the limitations of screening and the importance of clinical judgment. Neonatologists and obstetricians must maintain a high index of suspicion for neonatal sepsis based on infant clinical signs, prompting immediate diagnostic evaluation and empirical therapy, even with negative maternal GBS screening. Lumbar puncture is essential in neonates with positive blood cultures or strong clinical evidence of sepsis to exclude meningitis.
