Background

Choristoma is as a tumor-like mass of histologically normal cells that are located in an abnormal location [1]. Gingival salivary gland choristoma (GSGC) is a very rare and atypical heterotopic lesion and was first reported and described in 1964 [2]. In a few exceptional cases, most of the GSGC are reported as asymptomatic, smooth-surfaced enlargements of salivary glands on the gingiva [3].

To support the diagnosis of GSGC histopathologically, an excisional biopsy is required. However, a biopsy usually creates a residual mucogingival defect in periodontium which may further cause functional and aesthetic problems. Although excision is considered the primary treatment for GSGC, periodontal soft tissue reconstruction of the residual defect after excision of GSGC has not yet been clearly described in the literature due to the rarity of this lesion. Therefore, the aim of this case report is to present the diagnosis of GSGC and to describe a successful periodontal reconstructive approach for the management of the residual soft tissue defect in a pediatric patient.

Case presentation

A 14-year-old healthy female patient was referred to the Department of Periodontology, Marmara University Faculty of Dentistry, with a preliminary diagnosis of ameloblastoma from another clinic following completion of orthodontic treatment. The patient’s chief complaint was a gingival overgrowth on the mandibular right buccal region. Findings from the extraoral clinical examination were normal. During the intraoral examination, a pink, polypoid nodule, measuring 0.5 × 0.8 × 0.3 cm was found on the buccal side of tooth #43 (Fig. 1a). Inadequate and thin keratinized mucosa was also detected. Radiographic examination revealed no osseous or dental abnormalities (Fig. 1b).

a. Clinical appearance of GSGC. b. Radiographic image without any bone involvement

Based on the clinical presentation of a localized gingival overgrowth, the differential diagnosis included reactive gingival lesions such as irritation fibroma, peripheral ossifying fibroma, and peripheral giant cell granuloma. In addition, odontogenic lesions, particularly peripheral ameloblastoma, were considered given the initial referral diagnosis.

After conducting a full thickness excisional biopsy of the lesion, minimal ostectomy and osteoplasty was performed to eliminate the possible invasion areas in the cortical bone (Fig. 2a-b). At the mesial and distal aspects of the defect, split-thickness flap preparations were performed using a tunnel technique, with preservation of the interdental papillae. Apically, additional split-thickness dissection was carried out to achieve tension-free coronal advancement of the flap, combining laterally closed tunnel (LCT) technique with a coronally advanced flap (CAF). A free gingival graft (FGG) was harvested from the right premolar region of the palate and partially de-epithelized (PDFGG) (Fig. 2c). The PDFGG was inserted into the prepared tunnel, positioning the epithelized portion on the coronal part of the defect to cover the exposed root and bone surfaces and stabilized with 5/0 polyglycolic acid-co-lactide absorbable sutures from the mesial and distal aspects (Fig. 2d). The adjacent papillae were de-epithelialized to facilitate flap adaptation, and the laterally mobilized tissues were approximated over the defect with tension-free 5/0 polyglycolic acid-co-lactide absorbable sutures. The apically released mucosa was then coronally advanced and secured using suspensory sutures at the papillary regions (Fig. 2e). The patient was prescribed ibuprofen-based analgesic anti-inflammatory medication (twice daily, 5 days), amoxicillin-based antibiotic (twice daily, 1 week) and a chlorhexidine mouthwash (twice daily, 1 week). The excised lesion was buffered in 1/10 formalin and sent for histopathological examination. At the 10-day follow-up, sutures were removed, and healing was still in progress (Fig. 2f).

Histopathologically, a lobular arrangement of salivary glands, comprising of ducts and acini was identified in the hyalinized connective tissue, beneath the normal-appearing surface epithelium. Additionally, nerve-vessel sections and fatty tissue were detected surrounding these structures (Fig. 3a-b). The diagnosis of GSGC was confirmed. At the 3-month follow-up, soft tissue adaptation was satisfactory; however, mild edema and bleeding were observed at the buccal margin (Fig. 4). The patient was re-instructed on proper oral hygiene practices. No recurrence was observed at the 2-year follow-up; however, a 2 mm gingival recession was noted on the buccal aspect of the tooth (Fig. 5). At the 4-year follow-up, the recession was completely covered, and a thick band of keratinized attached gingiva was present, with no evidence of recurrence (Fig. 6). The patient and her parents reported satisfaction with the aesthetic outcome and no discomfort. Discussion and conclusions

Salivary gland choristomas are extremely rare in the gingiva [1]. Clinically, GSGC presents as soft tissue masses without any bone invasion. Histopathologically, they consist of lobules, ducts and acini of normal salivary glands. Two possible origins were described in the literature; the first is due to the multipotent feature of the gingiva, where metaplasia of the tissues could occur; the second suggests they originate from entrapped salivary gland cells during the embryological branchial development of the oral cavity [3].

A review of the literature reveals only a few cases of these unusual lesions, with only three cases reported in pediatric patients [2, 4, 5] including this present case. Among these reported cases, information about the surgical treatment procedure for GSGC was very limited [1, 3, 5]. To the best of our knowledge surgical reconstruction of the damaged soft tissues beyond conventional excision of GSGC has not yet been reported in the literature (Table 1). When encountering such lesions, appropriate surgical reconstruction of the affected region is important for the patients’ comfort and long-term success of the treatment.

The first step of the surgical approach to gingival lesions are usually an excisional biopsy to remove the entire lesion including its clinical margins and periosteum. This step is crucial for reducing the incidence of recurrence but it could compromise the integrity of the periodontium, leaving a large mucogingival defect, lost keratinized gingiva, exposed alveolar bone and cement surface [13]. Specifically, inadequate keratinized gingiva can lead to reduced vestibular depth, challenges in maintaining oral hygiene and negatively impact the further functionality of the teeth especially in pediatric cases. The literature outlines several surgical methods for managing residual mucogingival defects such as FGG, subepithelial connective tissue grafts (SCTG), CAF, laterally displaced flaps and platelet-rich fibrin, either individually or in various combinations [13].

In this present case, differing from the other cases reported in the literature, surgical reconstruction of the residual soft tissues after the biopsy of GSGC was presented for the first time. After a full thickness excisional biopsy, minimal osteotomy/osteoplasty was performed. Although osteotomy/osteoplasty is not routinely indicated for GSGC, the surgical approach in the present case was influenced by the initial diagnostic considerations, as the patient had been referred with a preliminary diagnosis of ameloblastoma. Despite the absence of radiographic bone involvement, ameloblastoma may exhibit subclinical infiltration beyond radiographic boundaries and is associated with relatively high recurrence rates when treated conservatively [14, 15]. Therefore, minimal osteoplasty and ostectomy were performed as cautious adjunctive procedure to reduce potential recurrence risk.

Following lesion excision, a combination of the laterally closed tunnel (LCT) technique and a CAF was employed to achieve passive flap adaptation in the surgical area. The CAF technique combined with a SCTG is widely considered the gold standard for root coverage procedures [16]. The LCT technique was first introduced by Sculean and Allen and it has been reported as a predictable approach for the treatment of deep, isolated mandibular gingival recessions, particularly in Miller Class I, II, and III defects [17]. In the present case, split-thickness flap preparations were performed at the mesial and distal aspects of the defect using LCT technique, with preservation of the interdental papillae. Additional apical split-thickness dissection was carried out to achieve tension-free coronal advancement of the flap, effectively combining the principles of LCT and CAF. Through the passive release of the flap from both lateral and apical aspects, a relatively large defect area was successfully covered using this modified approach, while preserving the vestibular depth.

To reconstruct the lost gingiva in the defect area a FGG was harvested from the palatal aspect of the premolar region. The graft was subsequently partially de-epithelialized, leaving the epithelial layer intact at the collar portion. The palate is the most commonly used donor site for free gingival and connective tissue grafts, typically extending from the distal aspect of the canine to the molar region. Previous studies have reported that connective tissue harvested from the anterior palate presents a relatively looser structure compared to posterior regions [18]. Conversely, the posterior palate may carry an increased risk of vascular complications due to the proximity of the greater palatine artery. In the present case, considering the young age of the patient and the relatively limited palatal dimensions, the premolar region was selected as the donor site. This approach was preferred to minimize the risk of intraoperative bleeding and postoperative discomfort, while still providing adequate graft quality and ensuring patient comfort.

In this present case a PDFGG was placed under the tension-free flaps, positioning the epithelized portion on the coronal part of the defect to cover the exposed root and bone surfaces and form a sufficient and thick keratinized gingiva. The PDFGG technique was first introduced by Cortellini et al. as a modification of the conventional FGG technique for augmenting keratinized gingiva [19]. They suggested that PDFGG offers better results than FGG in terms of root coverage potential and aesthetics, which were considered close to the performance of CAF-based techniques. In CAF-based techniques, using a SCTG under CAF is considered the gold standard for root coverage [16]. Additionally, some reports in the literature described using a SCTG with an epithelial collar (ECTG) on the most coronal part [20]. Zucchelli et al. stated that using these both types of grafts with CAF was effective for root coverage, but ECTG provided slightly more gingival thickness than SCTG [21]. In a recent report by Park et al., a submerged PDFGG was utilized in conjunction with a CAF to cover multiple gingival recessions in a patient with very thin gingival and palatal biotypes. They reported effective root coverage and visible increase in the width of keratinized gingiva after a 34-year follow-up [22]. In the present case, since no keratinized gingival tissue was present at the defect site, the PDFGG technique was selected to augment the keratinized gingiva and achieve the most favorable soft tissue reconstruction. Findings in the literature are consistent with this case, demonstrating that the combination of PDFGG with a modified LCT-CAF technique offers functional and aesthetic outcomes for long-term periodontal reconstruction of residual soft tissue defects.

A potential limitation of the present case is the minor increase in gingival recession on tooth number 44, observed at the 2nd and 4th year follow-ups. This finding may be partially influenced by the patient’s periodontal phenotype and prior orthodontic treatment, considering that slight baseline recession was already present on multiple teeth including #44. Furthermore, the surgical approach, which required removal of the entire gingival tissue within the defect area, may have contributed to minor recession in adjacent teeth during the healing and tissue remodeling process. This potential outcome was explained to the patient preoperatively as part of the informed consent.

In conclusion, this case report has demonstrated an effective surgical option for an exceptionally rare heterotropia, GSGC, by successfully reconstructing the lost periodontal tissues in a 14-year-old patient. By utilizing PDFGG together with CAF and LCT after the surgical excision of GSGC, sufficient keratinized tissue was provided, and the aesthetic outcome was enhanced with a long-term stability over a 4-year follow-up period.
