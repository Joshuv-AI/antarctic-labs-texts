Calibration without Ground Truth Authorship follows alphabetical order.
Yuqing Kong, Mingyu Song, Yizhou Wang, Yifan Wu

Abstract
46 predicts that publicly available human text will be exhausted within the next decade. Thus, improving models without access to ground-truth labels becomes increasingly important. We propose a label-free post-processing framework that improves a strong but miscalibrated model using a weaker yet better-calibrated reference. Our framework guarantees a strict performance improvement under any proper loss. Our approach is based on a characterization of when strict improvement is possible: when the strong and reference models are not mutually calibrated. We formalize this condition, connect it to arbitrage and no-trade results from economics, and develop an efficient Bregman projection algorithm that guarantees worst-case loss reduction without labels. Experiments on representative LLMs across varying scales demonstrate that our label-free method significantly reduces proper losses and calibration errors, achieving performance competitive with supervised baselines.

Introduction

Recent advances in AI, especially in large language models (LLMs), have been largely improved by the scaling law
(
26
)
and, in particular, by the availability of training data. However, this scaling of training data is not sustainable.
46
predicts that LLMs will be trained on datasets comparable to the stock of public human text between 2026 and 2032. As we enter this regime where training data is exhausted, the improvement of models demands methods without relying on ground truth data.

In this regime without ground truth, one solution is to use an existing weak model to improve a strong model.
10
show a weak-to-strong generalization: a student model in a stronger hypothesis class can outperform a fine-tuned weak reference model. This weak-to-strong generalization suggests that the weak reference can be valuable even when the model is imperfect, which motivates exploring additional ways to extract transferable information from a weak reference.

Our work proposes
calibration
as a transferable property that can improve a strong model with the outputs of a weak model, without ground truth labels. Calibration requires that model outputs can be reliably interpreted as probabilities
(
15
)
.
As an example, your fund manager might state there is a
20\%
chance that a high-risk investment will double in value. This confidence is calibrated if, among all the times your manager predicts a
20\%
probability, the investments actually succeed roughly
20\%
of the time.

Calibration has become increasingly important for modern neural networks and large language models. Despite high accuracy, state-of-the-art models are often miscalibrated and systematically overconfident
(
22
;
37
)
. In real-world deployments, this overconfidence can lead to high-profile failures. For example, in a recent federal court case
1
1

1

Court filing available at
https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.575368/gov.uscourts.nysd.575368.54.0_3.pdf
.
, attorneys relied on an LLM to generate legal citations that were presented with high confidence but later found to be entirely fictitious, ultimately resulting in court-imposed sanctions. Crucially, the errors are difficult to detect when reported confidence is unreliable. As a result, calibration has emerged as a key notion of trustworthiness: well-calibrated predictions communicate not only what a model predicts, but also when its outputs should or should not be relied upon for downstream decision-making. Moreover, improving calibration can enhance performance under probability-sensitive objectives and decision rules, as calibration aligns predicted probabilities with true conditional likelihoods rather than merely optimizing accuracy.

A practical motivation for improving model calibration without ground truth is that a well-calibrated weak model is relatively easy to obtain. For instance, the GPT-4 Technical Report notes that the pre-trained model is well-calibrated, while post-training makes the model less calibrated
(
1
)
. In many settings,
fine-tuned
models resemble aggressive star traders: they are highly capable of finding “alpha”, but they often become overconfident. On the contrary,
base
pretrained checkpoints resemble conservative index analysts: they may be less skilled at picking individual winners, yet they are more grounded in statistical reality, resulting in a more calibrated prediction. This pattern is common.
22
observe that more complicated modern neural networks are better in accuracy but worse in calibration. For another example,
13
show that a general-purpose text model may achieve higher accuracy on a forecasting or classification task, yet produce less calibrated confidence estimates than a simpler model trained on structured signals (e.g., tabular features), which is less accurate but more reliable.

This gap suggests a unique opportunity. Fixing a weaker but better-calibrated
reference
model (e.g. base), we can improve the calibration of a stronger model (e.g. fine-tuned), which “distills” the reference. We name the stronger model as the
primary
model we wish to improve.
In standard model distillation, the primary model is typically trained to match the reference’s predictions
(
25
)
. When the reference is weaker than the primary model, such imitation can unnecessarily constrain the primary model and may degrade performance. Instead of distilling the reference model, we treat the weak reference model as a reliable reference and restrict our attention to post-processing the strong primary model,
preserving the primary model’s predictive information.

Formally, our goal is to design a post-processing algorithm that maps the primary model’s output to an (strictly) improved predictor, which comes with a provable worst-case improvement guarantee of a proper loss. The guarantee assumes access only to the joint distribution of the primary model’s and reference model’s outputs. The worst case is taken over all possible ground-truth label distributions consistent with this joint distribution. This provides a guarantee when no labeled ground truth is available.
Crucially, our restriction to post-processing preserves the information of the primary model. The restriction to transformation uses the primary output as a sufficient statistic and never pools it into a coarser summary. Thus, our theoretical guarantee ensures that this transformation cannot degrade the loss in the worst case, even though an arbitrary remapping could, in principle, hurt accuracy.

We characterize the equivalent condition when
strict
improvement is possible, which connects to the calibration of the two models. Intuitively, improvement requires a contradiction between the strong model’s prediction and the calibrated reference. We formalize this as a property of
mutual calibration
: two predictors are
mutually calibrated
if there exists a joint distribution over both predictions and the ground truths, such that both predictors are calibrated with respect to the truth under this distribution. Our characterization shows that strict improvement is possible precisely when the two predictors are
not
mutually calibrated, i.e., when no joint distribution exists under which both predictors are calibrated. This characterization connects the improvement of the strong model to the transferrable property of calibration. We give an example of contradicting predictions in
Example

1.1
.

Our algorithm exploits the contradiction between the two predictors, based on the characterization of strict improvement. First, since strict improvement is impossible for predictors that are mutually calibrated with the calibrated reference, we define a
reference-compatible set
as the set of predictors that are mutually calibrated with the reference. This reference-compatible set is specified by a set of linear constraints over the joint distribution, where the linear constraints correspond to the calibration of the two models.

Second, we project the primary predictor onto this reference-compatible set using the geometry induced by the training loss. Our algorithm selects the model that minimizes the expected Bregman divergence to the original strong model.
Here, the Bregman divergence is generated by the convex function corresponding to the chosen proper loss (e.g., squared loss yields a squared divergence). Importantly, this is a Bregman projection in
functional model space
: the optimization ranges over all predictors as functions (or conditional distributions) rather than over pointwise predictions on a fixed dataset. In this view, our projection then chooses, among all mutually calibrated predictors compatible with those constraints, the one that minimally moves the strong model. This yields a principled post-processing rule that changes the model only as much as needed to restore mutual calibration while preserving the strong model’s information under the corresponding Bregman divergence.

The above results also reveal a connection between machine learning and economics. If we view a predictive model as a fund manager reporting subjective beliefs about an investment, mutual calibration then parallels the existence of a common prior between two fund managers. The two managers may receive different information, but there exists a single underlying world in which both reported beliefs can be simultaneously rational.
When mutual calibration fails, the implied worldviews are inconsistent. In economics, this inconsistency induces arbitrage opportunities
33
;
5
;
35
, where an arbitrageur can trade with the two managers and profit without new information. We bring this view into machine learning and show that the inconsistency guarantees the existence of a transformation that strictly reduces expected loss. Arbitrage and model improvement are thus two sides of the same principle, which extracts value from incoherent probabilistic beliefs.

Our Contributions

Theory

We formalize the problem of improving a miscalibrated primary model using a (approximately) calibrated reference, and develop an efficient Bregman-projection-based algorithm.

•

Condition for Strict Improvement.
We characterize when strict improvement is possible: precisely when the strong and reference models are
not
mutually calibrated. We link mutual calibration to the No-Trade Theorem in economics, and establish a correspondence between maximal extractable arbitrage profit and calibration error metrics (
Appendix

D
).

•

Worst-Case Improvement Guarantee.
The algorithm outputs a transformation that reduces the strong model’s expected loss at least its Bregman divergence to the reference-compatible set (the “distance” between the red and green points in
Figure

1
),
for all
ground-truth distributions under which the reference model is calibrated (
Theorem

3.3
). Furthermore, we relax the assumption of a perfectly calibrated reference, quantifying how the guarantees degrade in relation to the reference’s calibration error (
Theorem

B.1
).

•

Generality of the Framework.
Our framework applies broadly to:

–

Diverse Metrics:
Optimization with respect to any proper loss, including Brier Score, Log Loss, and Confidence Loss.

–

General Elicitable Properties:
Extensions beyond full predictive distributions to elicitable reports such as means, medians, and top-one classification with confidence.

Experiment

We evaluate our method on mainstream open-source LLM families across different sizes. We set the better-calibrated Base models as
reference
predictors to improve their
primary
Instruct counterparts. Experimental results show significant reductions in both the Brier Score and Expected Calibration Error (ECE) comparing to the Instruct models. Despite being label-free, our method achieves calibration performance competitive with supervised baselines. Unlike standard post-hoc calibration only modifies confidence scores, our approach re-estimates the full probability distribution, allowing us to preserve the high prediction accuracy of Instruct models while offering the potential for marginal gains in certain cases (
Section

5
).

1.2
Related Work

Model Distillation

Similar to our idea, model distillation trains a model with the output of reference model and aims at a better performance than training the model on ground truth labels (see, e.g.,
25
;
42
;
21
;
2
). Our work is similar to model distillation in the sense that we “distill” the calibration of the weak model. Distillation is most commonly used with a reference model at least as good as the trainable model. As discussed in the introduction, our work is closer to the work on weak-to-strong generalization that uses a weak model to improve a strong model
(
10
)
.

Calibration under Distribution Shift

The classical literature of calibration focuses on post-processing model outputs with ground truth
(
39
;
51
;
28
)
. The closest work to ours improves model calibration under distribution shifts. The goal of this literature is similar to our work, which is to measure or to improve calibration when there is no ground truth labels of a shifted distribution. This literature usually makes assumptions of the distribution shift. The proposed solution relies on the assumptions. For example,
3
;
40
assume that only the marginal distribution of the true outcomes is shifted while the conditional distribution of the features remains the same.
38
assumes that the shifted distribution is sufficiently close to the training data. Our work is orthogonal to the work on distribution shift. We use a weak calibrated model as a reference and correct contradictions in predicted probabilities.

Bregman Projection

Bregman projection has been widely studied in a number of learning theory problems, e.g, mamximum entropy models
(
11
;
16
;
4
;
7
;
18
;
23
;
36
;
12
)
, boosting
(
11
;
27
)
, clustering
(
8
)
, Bayesian estimation
(
17
)
. Similar to our work,
34
apply this Bregman projection to the self-improvement of language models. However,
34
adopt a different focus. Rather than addressing calibration transfer, they project a baseline model to the space of coherent models, where coherence means the answers to paraphrases stay the same.

Mutual Calibration and the No-Trade Theorem

The notion of
mutual calibration
closely parallels the Common Prior Assumption and rationality in the economic literature. Seminal work by
6
shows that Bayesian agents who share a common prior cannot “agree to disagree”. This insight was extended to market settings by the No-Trade Theorem
(
33
)
, which states that risk-averse agents with common priors cannot profit from trade in an equilibrium. More recently,
5
and
35
characterize the set of
feasible
joint distributions of posterior beliefs as those that admit no arbitrage, formalizing feasibility through arbitrage-free constraints. This notion of feasibility aligns precisely with our definition of mutual calibration. We leverage this connection to show that violations of mutual calibration correspond to exploitable arbitrage opportunities, which in turn can be harnessed in a learning context to systematically refine model predictions and improve performance.

Problem Formulation

Setup.

We consider a prediction setting with an outcome variable
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
and two predictive models. We treat the output of the predictive models as random variables:

•

\bm{Q}_{1}
: The
primary predictor
(e.g., a strong, but miscalibrated model), which we aim to improve.

•

\bm{Q}_{0}
: The
reference predictor
(e.g., a weak, but calibrated model).

We first consider the standard case where predictors output full probability distributions over outcomes,
\bm{Q}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
. We assume the existence of a calibrated reference predictor.

Assumption 2.1

(Calibrated Reference)
.

We assume the reference predictor
\bm{Q}_{0}
is
calibrated
, meaning
\Pr(Y=y\mid\bm{Q}_{0}=\bm{q})=\bm{q}(y)
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.

Assumption relaxation

We relax the assumption that the reference predictor is perfectly calibrated, providing a rigorous analysis of how the theoretical guarantees depend on the reference’s calibration error (
Theorem

B.1
). Empirically, we observe that even when the reference is only better calibrated than the primary, our method successfully exploits this relative advantage to reduce error, demonstrating robustness to approximate calibration.

We assume access to the joint distribution over
\bm{Q}_{0}
,
\bm{Q}_{1}
, denoted by
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},\bm{Q}_{1}}
, estimated by the empirical joint distribution of two predictors outputs on an
unlabeled
dataset. We focus on the setting where predictors have finite supports
\mathcal{Q}_{0},\mathcal{Q}_{1}
.

Problem Statement.

We aim to learn a transformation map
h:\mathcal{Q}_{1}\mapsto\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
solely based on
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},\bm{Q}_{1}}
such that the transformed predictor
h(\bm{Q}_{1})
strictly outperforms the original
\bm{Q}_{1}
in terms of a chosen proper loss, “transferring” calibration information from
\bm{Q}_{0}
to
\bm{Q}_{1}
without requiring
\bm{Q}_{0}
during subsequent deployment.

Formally, we evaluate performance using a
(strictly) proper loss

\ell:\Delta(\mathcal{Y})\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
, such that the expected loss is (uniquely) minimized when the predicted distribution matches the true posterior. Specifically, for any true distribution
\bm{p}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
of the outcome
y
, it holds that
\E_{y\sim\bm{p}}\big[\ell(\bm{p},y)\big]\leq\E_{y\sim\bm{p}}\big[\ell(\bm{q},y)\big]
for any forecast
\bm{q}
. Common examples include the Log-Loss and the Brier Score (Squared Error).

Definition 2.2

(Strictly Improving Transformation)
.

Given access to the joint distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},\bm{Q}_{1}}
over the primary predictor and the reference predictor, transformation
h
strictly improves model if
1) for all distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},\bm{Q}_{1},Y}
extending
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},\bm{Q}_{1}}
where
\bm{Q}_{0}
is calibrated, applying
h
weakly improves the loss:
\E[\ell(h(\bm{Q}_{1}),Y)]\leq\E[\ell(\bm{Q}_{1},Y)];

and
2) there exists a
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},\bm{Q}_{1},Y}
, where the inequality is strict.

We aim to find a strictly improving transformation. This is not guaranteed in all cases. For instance, if both predictors
\bm{Q}_{0}
and
\bm{Q}_{1}
are already providing the ground truth, no transformation will improve the performance.
Thus, we will quantify the lower bound of improvement as a function of
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},\bm{Q}_{1}}
, and explore the conditions on
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},\bm{Q}_{1}}
that make a strictly improving transformation possible.

We will solve this problem with an even stronger result: if a strictly improving transformation exists, it will lead to a strict improvement for
all
valid ground truth distributions
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},\bm{Q}_{1},Y}
, not just in the case of specific distributions.

2.1
Extension to General Elicitable Properties

In many practical applications, models do not output full probability distributions but rather specific summary statistics (e.g., an investing advice with a confidence score). To accommodate this, we generalize our framework from full distributions to
elicitable properties
. Here elicitable properties are those for which a reporter can be incentivized to reveal truthfully through a proper loss
29
. While common statistical measures such as the mean and median are elicitable, others—such as variance—are not elicitable on their own.

Let
\mathcal{R}
denote the report space (e.g.,
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{Y}
for mode estimation, or
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{R}
for mean estimation). A property
\Gamma:\Delta(\mathcal{Y})\to 2^{\mathcal{R}}
maps a probability distribution to a set of valid reports.

Generalized Definitions

We extend our core concepts to this setting as follows:

•

Property Calibration:
A predictor is calibrated with respect to property
\Gamma
if its report matches the property of the conditional distribution given that report:

r\in\Gamma(\mathcal{D}_{Y\mid R=r}).

for all possible report
r
.

•

Proper Loss for Properties:
A loss function
\ell:\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
elicits property
\Gamma
if truthful reporting minimizes the expected loss. Formally, for any distribution
\bm{p}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
:

\Gamma(\bm{p})\subseteq\operatorname*{argmin}_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\E_{Y\sim\bm{p}}[\ell(r,Y)].

The loss is
strictly proper
if the inclusion is an equality.

Let
R_{0}
and
R_{1}
be reports from the reference and primary predictors, corresponding to possibly distinct properties
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
. Analogous to the full distribution setting, the objective remains to find a transformation
h:\mathcal{R}_{1}\mapsto\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
that strictly reduce the proper loss associated with
\Gamma_{1}
, under the assumption that the reference
R_{0}
satisfies property calibration. Here
\mathcal{R}_{1}
denotes the finite support of primary predictor
R_{1}
.

3
Solution and Theoretical Guarantees

This section presents our core methodology for improving a primary predictor given a calibrated reference. We adopt a geometric perspective, viewing transformed predictors as points. The set of transformed predictors that are mutually calibrated with the reference constitutes a convex region. If a primary predictor is not mutually calibrated with the reference predictor, it must lies outside this region. Our approach projects such a predictor back onto this reference-compatible set, thereby reducing prediction error by eliminating the inconsistencies while preserving maximal information from the original primary predictions.

3.1
The Condition for Improvement: Mutual Calibration

We first introduce the central concept determining the possibility of strict improvement:
Mutual Calibration
. This concept characterizes the consistency of the joint distribution of two predictors without requiring access to the ground truth outcome.

Definition 3.1

(Mutual Calibration)
.

Given a joint distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},\bm{Q}_{1}}
, a primary predictor
\bm{Q}_{1}
and a reference predictor
\bm{Q}_{0}
are
mutually calibrated
if there exists a joint distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},\bm{Q}_{1},Y}
extending
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},\bm{Q}_{1},}
such that both predictors
\bm{Q}_{0}
and
\bm{Q}_{1}
are simultaneously calibrated with respect to the outcome
Y
.

We will demonstrate that strict improvement is achievable if and only if the two predictors violate mutual calibration condition (
Theorem

3.3
).

Connection to the No-Trade Theorem

If two predictors are mutually calibrated, they can be interpreted as Bayesian agents updating their beliefs from a common prior distribution, where the common joint distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},\bm{Q}_{1},Y}
serves as the shared prior distribution. The celebrated No-Trade Theorem
33
establishes that under the common prior assumption, no external agent can construct a “zero-trade” contract that yields a risk-free profit against them. Recent work in information economics characterizes the space of
reference-compatible
joint distributions
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},\bm{Q}_{1}}
through this lens, aligning with the definition of mutual calibration
(
5
;
35
)
. Their results imply that the mutual calibration condition can be viewed as providing a
no-arbitrage guarantee
. A failure of mutual calibration implies the existence of arbitrage opportunity, making an improvement in predictions possible. We provide a formal mapping between the maximal arbitrage space and specific calibration errors in
Appendix

D
.

3.2
Algorithm: Projection to Reference-Compatible Set

When mutual calibration does not hold, an algorithm can be designed to exploit these discrepancies to systematically decrease forecasting loss.

Given a joint distribution between the primary and reference predictors, the algorithm has two steps: first, define a
reference-compatible set
of predictors as the set of transformations of the primary predictor that are mutually calibrated with the reference. Second,
project
the primary predictor onto this feasible set by finding the closest reference-compatible predictor in terms of Bregman divergence.

3.2.1
Step 1: The Reference-Compatible Set

Let
\mathcal{H}=\{h\mid h:\mathcal{Q}_{1}\mapsto\Delta(\mathcal{Y})\}
denote the space of all functions mapping the primary predictions to a full prediction over outcome space.

Given the joint distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},\bm{Q}_{1}}
, the reference-compatible set of transformation map is defined as the set of predictors that are mutually calibrated with the reference.

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}=\Big\{h\in\mathcal{H}\mid h(\bm{Q}_{1})\text{ and }\bm{Q}_{0}\text{ are mutually calibrated}\Big\}.

We restrict the reference-compatible set to mutually calibrated transformations because we will show that any violation of the mutual calibration condition allows for a further reduction in forecast loss.

The reference-compatible set is specified by linear constraints over the joint distribution
\mathcal{D}_{h(\bm{Q}_{0}),\bm{Q}_{1},Y}
, implying the convexity of the set.

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},h(\bm{Q}_{1}),Y}}\left[Y\mid h(\bm{Q}_{1})=\bm{q}\right]=\bm{q}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}_{\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},h(\bm{Q}_{1}),Y}}\left[Y\mid\bm{Q}_{0}=\bm{q}\right]=\bm{q}

Observation 3.2
.

The reference-compatible set
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}
is a convex set.

Convexity is vital because it guarantees that a strictly improving transformation exists and can be found efficiently using standard optimization techniques.

3.2.2
Step 2: Bregman Projection

Our algorithm improves the primary predictor by projecting it onto the set of reference-compatible transformations. To define this projection, we require a notion of “distance”. Since we aim to reduce proper loss, the natural choice is the Bregman divergence generated by the proper loss
\ell

43
. Formally, the divergence between a true distribution
\bm{p}
and a prediction
\bm{q}
is the expected regret:

D_{\ell}(\bm{p},\bm{q})=\E_{Y\sim\bm{p}}[\ell(\bm{q},Y)-\ell(\bm{p},Y)].

We seek the reference-compatible transformation
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}
that remains closest to the primary predictor
\bm{Q}_{1}
. Crucially, this projection is equivalent to minimizing the expected divergence (or loss gain) relative to the primary predictor:

\displaystyle h^{*}

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{argmin}_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}}\E_{\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1}}}\left[D_{\ell}(h(\bm{Q}_{1}),\bm{Q}_{1})\right]

(2)

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{argmin}_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}}\E_{\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1}}}\left[\E_{Y\sim h(\bm{Q}_{1})}\left[\ell(\bm{Q}_{1},Y)-\ell(h(\bm{Q}_{1}),Y)\right]\right]

Note that this formulation differs from a standard Bregman projection on the probability simplex because we operate on the space of transformations (functions). We demonstrate that this objective induces a valid Bregman divergence on this function space, thereby generalizing the standard projection framework.

3.3
Theoretical Guarantees

We show that the aforementioned projection-based transformation is strictly improving, guaranteeing a reduction in expected loss
regardless of
the underlying ground-truth distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2},Y}
. Specifically, we show that the reduction is precisely equal to the Bregman ‘distance’ from the primary predictor to the reference-compatible set, by extending the definition of Bregman divergence to the functional space of transformations.

We impose mild conditions on proper loss. Every proper loss function
\ell
is linked to a convex generator function
G
, where
G(\bm{q})=-\E_{y\sim\bm{q}}[\ell(\bm{q},y)]
represents the amount of uncertainty of a distribution. We focus on proper losses induced by strictly convex, differentiable generator functions, which we refer to as differentiable proper loss. While a rigorous derivation is deferred to
Section

A.1
, standard ones (such as the Brier or Log loss) are all induced by strictly convex, differentiable generator functions.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\ell
be a differentiable, strictly proper loss. Consider a joint distribution over primary and reference predictors
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},\bm{Q}_{1}}

Specifically, when they are not mutually calibrated, for any ground truth distribution

\displaystyle\E\big[\ell(\bm{Q}_{1},Y)\big]-\E\big[\ell(h^{*}(\bm{Q}_{1}),Y)\big]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}}\E\big[D_{\ell}(h(\bm{Q}_{1}),\bm{Q}_{1})\big].

The core of this proof is a generalized Pythagorean Theorem for Bregman Divergence defined on the functional space of transformations. The total error can be split cleanly into two parts: the unavoidable error and the “distance” between the current prediction and the reference-compatible set. The projected predictor will eliminate the “distance” error, and the improvement is lower bounded by this “distance” error.

Assumption relaxation

We relax the assumption that the reference predictor is perfectly calibrated in
Theorem

B.1
. Our analysis shows that the change in loss achieved by

decomposes into two competing terms: a guaranteed geometric “distance” gain and a potential miscalibration risk. The risk accounts for the cost of propagating the reference predictor’s own errors. We establish that strictly positive improvement is maintained as long as the gain from alignment outweighs the reference’s error.

6
Discussion

Limitations.

Our framework relies on two assumptions. First, it assumes the reference predictor is well-calibrated. While pre-trained Base models generally satisfy this, non-negligible miscalibration could weaken our theoretical guarantees. Second, accurate estimation of the joint distribution between two predictors requires sufficient unlabeled data, which may be challenging for rare events. Additionally, although our framework is compatible with various proper losses and elicitable properties, there is no universal transformation optimal for all proper losses simultaneously.

Future Work.

Currently, our approach requires a batch of unlabeled data for joint distribution estimation. A promising direction is extending this to online settings, allowing for dynamic updates as predictions arrive sequentially. Furthermore, while we validate our method on multiple-choice tasks, future work could adapt this framework to token-level text generation.

A.1
More Preliminaries: Proper Loss and Bregman Divergence

To analyze general proper losses, we utilize the property that any proper scoring rule is generated by a convex function, and the regret is equivalent to a Bregman divergence.

Lemma A.1

(Convex Representation of Proper Losses)
.

A loss
\ell
is proper if and only if there exists a convex function

G:\Delta(\mathcal{Y})\to\mathbb{R}

and a (possibly subgradient) mapping

\psi(\bm{q})\in\partial G(\bm{q})

such that, for all forecasts
\bm{q}
and outcomes
y
,

\ell(\bm{q},y)=-\left(G(\bm{q})+\langle\psi(\bm{q}),\mathbf{e}_{y}-\bm{q}\rangle\right),

where
\mathbf{e}_{y}
is the one-hot vector corresponding to outcome
y
. If
\ell
is strictly proper,
G
is strictly convex.

Remark A.2

(Regret as Bregman Divergence)
.

The expected regret of using prediction
\bm{q}
instead of the true probability
\bm{p}
is given by the Bregman divergence defined by the generator
G
:

\E_{y\sim\bm{p}}[\ell(\bm{q},y)]-\E_{y\sim\bm{p}}[\ell(\bm{p},y)]=G(\bm{p})-G(\bm{q})-\langle\psi(\bm{q}),\bm{p}-\bm{q}\rangle=:D_{\ell}(\bm{p},\bm{q}).

Similar as
34
, we extend this divergence to the space of transformations
\mathcal{H}
, but take expectations over the distribution of predictions of the reference.

Observation A.3
.

A Bregman divergence on
\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
induces a Bregman divergence on the space of transformations
\mathcal{H}
:

D_{\ell}(h,g)=\E_{\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1}}}[D_{\ell}(h(\bm{Q}_{1}),g(\bm{Q}_{1}))].

If
G
is strictly convex and differentiable on
\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
, then the induced divergence
D_{\ell}(\cdot,\cdot)
is also a Bregman divergence with a strictly convex and differentiable generator function.

Proof of
A.3
.

Let
h,g\in\mathcal{H}
be two transformations. By definition of the Bregman divergence
D_{\ell}
for any fixed point
\bm{q}\in\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
, we have:

D_{\ell}(h(\bm{q}),g(\bm{q}))=G(h(\bm{q}))-G(g(\bm{q}))-\langle\psi(g(\bm{q})),h(\bm{q})-g(\bm{q})\rangle.

By taking the expectation over the distribution of the random variable
\bm{Q}_{1}
, we define the induced divergence on
\mathcal{H}
:

\displaystyle D_{\ell}(h,g)

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1}}}\left[D_{\ell}(h(\bm{Q}_{1}),g(\bm{Q}_{1}))\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1}}}\left[G(h(\bm{Q}_{1}))-G(g(\bm{Q}_{1}))-\langle\psi(g(\bm{Q}_{1})),h(\bm{Q}_{1})-g(\bm{Q}_{1})\rangle\right].

If
G
is the generator for the point-wise divergence, the extended generator
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as:

\mathcal{G}(h)=\sum_{\bm{q}\in\mathcal{Q}_{1}}G(h(\bm{q}))\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1}}(\bm{q}).

\mathcal{Q}_{1}
is the finite support of
\bm{Q}_{1}
. Thus, if
G
is strictly convex and differentiable on
\Delta(\mathcal{Y})
, then
\mathcal{G}
is strictly convex and differentiable on
\mathcal{H}
.

Crucial to our proof is the projection property of Bregman divergences. The Generalized Pythagorean Theorem is a standard property of Bregman divergences

Lemma A.4

(Generalized Pythagorean Theorem)

Given a Bregman divergence

\Pi_{\ell,\mathcal{C}}(v)\;=\arg\min_{v\in\mathcal{C}}D_{\ell}(v,v_{0}).

Then, for any

and any point

, the following inequality holds:

D_{\ell}(v,v_{0})\;\geq\;\;D_{\ell}(v,\Pi_{\ell,\mathcal{C}}(v_{0}))+D_{\ell}(\Pi_{\ell,\mathcal{C}}(v_{0}),v_{0})\;.

Appendix B
Performance Guarantees under Imperfect Calibration of Reference

Theorem B.1
.

Let
\ell
be a strictly proper scoring rule generated by a strictly convex, differentiable function
G
. Consider a joint distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},\bm{Q}_{1}}
and a ground truth
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{0},\bm{Q}_{1},Y}
.
The change in expected loss achieved by the optimal reference-compatible transformation
h^{*}
decomposes into a guaranteed structural gain minus a penalty induced by the reference predictor’s miscalibration:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\E\big[\ell(\bm{Q}_{1},Y)\big]-\E\big[\ell(h^{*}(\bm{Q}_{1}),Y)\big]}_{\text{Total Improvement}}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\E\big[D_{\ell}(h^{*}(\bm{Q}_{1}),\bm{Q}_{1})\big]}_{\text{Geometric Gain (Always }\geq 0)}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\E\big[\langle\nabla G(h^{*}(\bm{Q}_{1}))-\nabla G(\bm{Q}_{1}),h_{\mathrm{comp}}(\bm{Q}_{1})-h^{*}(\bm{Q}_{1})\rangle\big]}_{\text{Compatibility Bonus (Always }\geq 0)}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\E\big[\langle\nabla G(h^{*}(\bm{Q}_{1}))-\nabla G(\bm{Q}_{1}),h(\bm{Q}_{1})-h_{\mathrm{comp}}(\bm{Q}_{1})\rangle\big]}_{\text{Miscalibration Risk (Can be $\leq 0$)}},

(4)

where
h(\bm{Q}_{1})=\E[\mathbf{Y}|\bm{Q}_{1}]
is the true conditional expectation, and
h_{\mathrm{comp}}
can be arbitrary element the reference-compatible set. Furthermore, by choosing the specific candidate
h_{\mathrm{comp}}=h-\E[\mathcal{E}(\bm{Q}_{0})|\bm{Q}_{1}]
where
\mathcal{E}(\bm{Q}_{0})=\E[\mathbf{Y}|\bm{Q}_{0}]-\bm{Q}_{0}
measures the miscalibration of the reference predictor, we obtain the explicit upper bound for the risk:

\displaystyle\left|\text{Miscalibration Risk}\right|\leq\underbrace{\|\nabla G(h^{*}(\bm{Q}_{1}))-\nabla G(\bm{Q}_{1})\|_{2}}_{\text{Transformation Magnitude}}\cdot\underbrace{\|\mathcal{E}(\bm{Q}_{0})\|_{2}}_{\text{Reference Calibration Error}},

(5)

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
of random variables denotes the
L_{2}
norm
\sqrt{\E[\|\cdot\|^{2}]}
.

Proof of Theorem
B.1
.

The improvement in loss is given by the difference in Bregman divergences from the truth conditional expectation
h
:

\Delta L=\E\left[D_{\ell}(h(\bm{Q}_{1}),\bm{Q}_{1})-D_{\ell}(h(\bm{Q}_{1}),h^{*}(\bm{Q}_{1}))\right].

Like the proof for the main theorem, using the Bregman divergence defined for the space of transformations, we can express it as:

D_{\ell}(h,h_{0})-D_{\ell}(h,h^{*})

Here
h_{0}
is the identical map that maps every prediction to itself.

Unlike the proof for the main theorem, the true conditional expectation
h
may not be in the reference-compatible set
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}
.

Thus, we apply a more delicate analysis:

D_{\ell}(h,h_{0})-D_{\ell}(h,h^{*})=D_{\ell}(h^{*},h_{0})+\langle\nabla G(h^{*})-\nabla G(h_{0}),h-h^{*}\rangle.

The first term is always greater than zero, referred as the Geometric Gain. To analyze the second term (the inner product), we decompose the residual vector
h-h^{*}
by introducing
h_{\mathrm{comp}}
:

h-h^{*}=(h-h_{\mathrm{comp}})+(h_{\mathrm{comp}}-h^{*}).

Substituting this splits the inner product into two components:

1. The Compatibility Bonus:
Consider the term
\E[\langle\nabla G(h^{*})-\nabla G(h_{0}),h_{\mathrm{comp}}-h^{*}\rangle]
.
Recall that
h^{*}=\Pi_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}}(\bm{Q}_{1})
is the projection of
\bm{Q}_{1}
onto the convex set
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}
. By definition,
h_{\mathrm{comp}}
also lies within
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}
. The variational inequality characterizing Bregman projections states that for any
f\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}
,
\langle\nabla G(h^{*})-\nabla G(h_{0}),f-h^{*}\rangle\geq 0
. We can set
f=h_{\mathrm{comp}}
, which shows the term is non-negative.

2. The Miscalibration Risk:
The remaining term is
\E[\langle\nabla G(h^{*})-\nabla G(h_{0}),h-h_{\mathrm{comp}}\rangle]
.
This term captures the cost of the reference predictor’s calibration errors.

Since the theorem holds for any
h_{\mathrm{comp}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}
, we construct a specific candidate
h^{\dagger}
defined as:

h^{\dagger}(\bm{Q}_{1})\coloneqq h(\bm{Q}_{1})-\E[\mathcal{E}(\bm{Q}_{0})\mid\bm{Q}_{1}],

where
\mathcal{E}(\bm{Q}_{0})=\E[\mathbf{Y}|\bm{Q}_{0}]-\bm{Q}_{0}
. We first verify
h^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}
. Checking the constraint condition:

\displaystyle\E[h^{\dagger}(\bm{Q}_{1})\mid\bm{Q}_{0}]

\displaystyle=\E[h(\bm{Q}_{1})\mid\bm{Q}_{0}]-\E\big[\E[\mathcal{E}(\bm{Q}_{0})\mid\bm{Q}_{1}]\big|\bm{Q}_{0}\big]

\displaystyle=\E[Y\mid\bm{Q}_{0}]-\E[\mathcal{E}(\bm{Q}_{0})\mid\bm{Q}_{0}]

\displaystyle=(\bm{Q}_{0}+\mathcal{E}(\bm{Q}_{0}))-\mathcal{E}(\bm{Q}_{0})=\bm{Q}_{0}.

Since
h^{\dagger}
is a valid member of the set, we substitute it into the risk term. The residual becomes
h-h^{\dagger}=\E[\mathcal{E}(\bm{Q}_{0})\mid\bm{Q}_{1}]
. Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

|R|\leq\E\left[\|\nabla G(h^{*}(\bm{Q}_{1}))-\nabla G(\bm{Q}_{1})\|_{2}\cdot\|\E[\mathcal{E}(\bm{Q}_{0})\mid\bm{Q}_{1}]\|_{2}\right].

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for expectations (
\E[XY]\leq\sqrt{\E X^{2}}\sqrt{\E Y^{2}}
):

|R|\leq\|\nabla G(h^{*}(\bm{Q}_{1}))-\nabla G(\bm{Q}_{1})\|_{2}\cdot\|\E[\mathcal{E}(\bm{Q}_{0})\mid\bm{Q}_{1}]\|_{2}.

Finally, applying Jensen’s inequality for conditional expectations implies that
\|\E[X\mid Y]\|_{2}\leq\|X\|_{2}
. Thus:

\|\E[\mathcal{E}(\bm{Q}_{0})\mid\bm{Q}_{1}]\|_{2}\leq\|\mathcal{E}(\bm{Q}_{0})\|_{2}.

Combining these yields the final bound:

|R|\leq\|\nabla G(h^{*}(\bm{Q}_{1}))-\nabla G(\bm{Q}_{1})\|_{2}\cdot\|\mathcal{E}(\bm{Q}_{0})\|_{2}.

∎

C.4
The Generalized Pythagorean Theorem in Non-smooth Case

We provide the lemma for the non-smooth approximation case mentioned in the proof above.

Lemma C.2

(Generalized Pythagorean Theorem (Non-Smooth Case))
.

Let
G:U\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function on a bounded domain
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq U
be a convex set.

(A) Differentiable Case:
For all point
v_{0}\in U
, if there exists
v^{*}\in\Pi_{\ell,\mathcal{C}}(v_{0})
such that
G
is differentiable at
v^{*}
, then for any

D_{\ell}(v,v_{0})\;\geq\;D_{\ell}(v,v^{*})\;+\;D_{\ell}(v^{*},v_{0}).

(B) Non-Smooth Case:
If
G
is bounded and continuous, for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists a finitely supported random variable
v^{*}_{\epsilon}
(an
\epsilon
-random Bregman projection) such that for all

:

D_{\ell}(v,v^{\prime})\;\geq\;\inf_{v^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}D_{\ell}(v,v^{\prime\prime})\;+\;\mathbb{E}_{v^{*}}\!\big[D_{\ell}(v^{*},v^{\prime})\big]\;-\;\epsilon.

Proof of
Lemma

C.2
.

We first prove the differentiable case (A). The proof mainly relies on the first-order optimality condition for the convex minimization.

Recall the Bregman divergence

D_{\ell}(p,q)=G(p)-G(q)-\langle\psi(q),p-q\rangle,\qquad\psi(q)\in\partial G(q).

Fix
v_{0}\in U
. Consider
v^{*}\in\Pi_{\ell,\mathcal{C}}(v_{0})
where
G
is differentiable at
v^{*}
. Because

\Pi_{\ell,\mathcal{C}}(v)=\arg\min_{v\in\mathcal{C}}\big\{G(v)-\langle\psi(v_{0}),v\rangle\big\},

the minimization problem is convex in
v
. The first-order optimality condition for the convex minimization (and differentiability of
G
at
v^{*}
) yields

\big\langle\nabla G(v^{*})-\psi(v_{0})\,,\,v-v^{*}\big\rangle\geq 0\qquad\text{for all }v\in\mathcal{C}.

Compute the combination of Bregman divergences:

\displaystyle\;D_{\ell}(v,v_{0})-\big(D_{\ell}(v,v^{*})+D_{\ell}(v^{*},v_{0})\big)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\;[G(v)-G(v_{0})-\langle\psi(v_{0}),v-v_{0}\rangle]-[G(v)-G(v^{*})-\langle\nabla G(v^{*}),v-v^{*}\rangle]

\displaystyle\;-[G(v^{*})-G(v_{0})-\langle\psi(v_{0}),v^{*}-v_{0}\rangle]

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\;\langle\nabla G(v^{*})-\psi(v_{0}),\,v-v^{*}\rangle.

By the first-order condition the right hand side is
\geq 0
. Hence for every

D_{\ell}(v,v_{0})\;\geq\;D_{\ell}(v,v^{*})+D_{\ell}(v^{*},v_{0}),

which proves the differentiable case.

We provide the proof for the more complex non-Smooth Case (B).

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq U
be convex and fix
v\in U
. Denote

. Fix
\varepsilon>0
. We define a zero-sum game to find the randomized projection.

Step 1: The Zero-Sum Game.

Define the payoff

Player I (minimizer) chooses

, Player II (maximizer) chooses

.
Observe that
\inf_{v\in\mathcal{C}}\max_{w\in\mathcal{C}}\Phi(v,w)=\inf_{v\in\mathcal{C}}\{D_{\ell}(v,v_{0})-\min_{w\in\mathcal{C}}D_{\ell}(v,w)\}
.

Step 2: Discretization.

Since
G
is continuous on a compact set, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a finite
\epsilon
-net
N=\{n_{1},\dots,n_{N}\}\subset\mathcal{C}
such that every point in
\mathcal{C}
is within
\epsilon
Bregman divergence of some point in
N
(
Lemma

C.4

Step 3: Minimax.

We analyze the game restricted to Player II using strategies in
N

Using the
\epsilon
-net property, we will show:

m-\epsilon\ \leq\ \inf_{v\in\mathcal{C}}\max_{n_{i}\in N}\Phi(v,n_{i})\ \leq\ m.

In detail, for the upper bound:

\displaystyle\inf_{v\in\mathcal{C}}\max_{n_{i}\in N}\Phi(v,n_{i})

\displaystyle=\inf_{v\in\mathcal{C}}\left\{D_{\ell}(v,v_{0})-\min_{n_{i}\in N}D_{\ell}(v,n_{i})\right\}

(6)

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{v\in\mathcal{C}}D_{\ell}(v,v_{0})=m

(7)

For the lower bound, we have:

\displaystyle\inf_{v\in\mathcal{C}}\max_{n_{i}\in N}\Phi(v,n_{i})

\displaystyle=\inf_{v\in\mathcal{C}}\left\{D_{\ell}(v,v_{0})-\min_{n_{i}\in N}D_{\ell}(v,n_{i})\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{v\in\mathcal{C}}D_{\ell}(v,v_{0})-\sup_{v\in\mathcal{C}}\min_{n_{i}\in N}D_{\ell}(v,n_{i})

\displaystyle=m-\sup_{v\in\mathcal{C}}\min_{n_{i}\in N}D_{\ell}(v,n_{i})

(10)

\displaystyle\geq m-\epsilon

(11)

where the last inequality follows from the

-net property of the discretization

By the Minimax theorem, there exists a mixed strategy

(a distribution) over

Let

Lemma C.4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be compact and let

\mathcal{C}
.
For each

\psi(x)\in\partial G(x)

D_{\ell}(y,x):=G(y)-G(x)-\langle\psi(x),y-x\rangle.

Then for every

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0

Finite subgradient

\psi(x)

For each
x\in\mathcal{C}
with a finite subgradient

\psi(x)

U_{x}:=\{y\in\mathcal{C}:D_{\ell}(y,x)<\varepsilon\}.

We start to show that the set

is open in the

\mathcal{C}

(i.e.,
U_{x}=O\cap\mathcal{C}
for some open set

Consider the function

y\mapsto D_{\ell}(y,x)=G(y)-G(x)-\langle\psi(x),y-x\rangle.

Since
G
is continuous and
y\mapsto\langle\psi(x),y\rangle
is linear as

is finite, this map is continuous on
\mathcal{C}
. Moreover,
D_{\ell}(x,x)=0
, so the set

U_{x}:=\{y\in\mathcal{C}:D_{\ell}(y,x)<\varepsilon\}

(-\infty,\varepsilon)
under a continuous map

the set
U_{x}
is open in the
relative topology
on
\mathcal{C}
.

Infinite subgradient

\psi(x)

The above analysis requires
\psi(x)
to be finite. It remains to justify that every point
x\in\mathcal{C}
is covered
by at least one of the sets
U_{x^{\prime}}
, even if
\psi(x)
is not finite.

We begin by selecting a point
x_{0}\neq x
from the relative interior of
\mathcal{C}
. Consider the line segment from
x_{0}
to
x
. Since
G
is differentiable almost everywhere, we will show that there exists a sequence of differentiable points

We use

When

\langle\nabla G(x_{n}),x-x_{n}\rangle

Appendix D
Mutual Calibration is Equivalent to No-Arbitrage

We first rephrase the result in
5
,
35
. Given
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}}
,
\bm{Q}_{1}
and
\bm{Q}_{2}
, are said to be
mutually calibrated
if there exists
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2},Y}
extending
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}}
such that both
\bm{Q}_{1}
and
\bm{Q}_{2}
are calibrated.

In the literature, this framework is referred to as the common prior assumption combined with rationality, where
\mathcal{D}
represents the common prior.

This concept admits an appealing interpretation through the lens of a
no-trading theorem
:
if forecasts are mutually calibratable, then no external agent can exploit their joint distribution to construct arbitrage opportunities.

D.1
Mutual Calibratability = No-Arbitrage

Economic interpretation

Consider a prediction market for an uncertain outcome
y
. A data-rich outsider—think of a market-making institution or platform operator—observes each predictor’s full predictive distribution and can offer customized contracts to each predictor conditional on their own prediction. After observing predictor
i
’s prediction
\bm{q}_{i}
, the outsider commits to a contract that pays
t_{i}(\bm{q}_{i},y)
when the outcome realizes as
y
. Predictors are free to accept or decline any such offer. A rational predictor with belief
\bm{q}_{i}
will accept any contract whose expected value under their own belief is nonnegative.

To guarantee acceptance for every possible report, the outsider can restrict attention to zero-mean contracts—what we call zero-trades—defined by

\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\bm{q}_{i}}\big[t_{i}(\bm{q}_{i},Y)\big]\;=\;0\quad\text{for all reports }\bm{q}_{i}.

When two predictors predict
(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2})
, the platform may offer different zero-trade contracts to each. Its realized profit upon outcome
y
is the negative of the total payout,

-\,t_{1}(\bm{q}_{1},y)\;-\;t_{2}(\bm{q}_{2},y).

Let
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2},Y}
denote the common prior over the two predictions and the outcome. The outsider’s expected profit under a given zero-trade scheme
(t_{1},t_{2})
is

-\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\left[t_{1}(\bm{Q}_{1},y)\;+\;t_{2}(\bm{Q}_{2},y)\right].

The arbitrage space between the predictors, represented as the maximal extractable profit, can be written as

\max_{\text{zero-trade }(t_{1},t_{2})}-\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\left[t_{1}(\bm{Q}_{1},y)\;+\;t_{2}(\bm{Q}_{2},y)\right]\;=\;-\,\min_{\text{zero-trade }(t_{1},t_{2})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\!\left[t_{1}(\bm{Q}_{1},y)\;+\;t_{2}(\bm{Q}_{2},y)\right].

The no-trade theorem
33
implies that the arbitrage space is zero when predictions and outcomes are drawn from a common prior: there is no profitable way to make everyone accept a zero-trade and still earn positive expected profit.

In the context of mutual calibration and the setting of
5
,
35
, we can not access the ground truth
y
. We only observe the joint distribution of reports,
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}}
, rather than the full prior
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2},Y}
. If the outsider evaluates profits using some belief
K(Y\mid\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2})
about the outcome conditional on the reports, then the expected profit of a zero-trade scheme is

-\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}}}\Big[\,\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim K(\cdot\mid\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2})}\big[t_{1}(\bm{Q}_{1},Y)\;+\;t_{2}(\bm{Q}_{2},Y)\big]\,\Big].

Mutual Calibratability = No-Arbitrage

A lower bound on the arbitrage space—given only
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}}
and no access to
y
—is obtained by replacing the outsider’s belief with the worst-case state realization:

\text{Arb}(\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}})\;=\;-\,\min_{\text{zero-trade }(t_{1},t_{2})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}}}\!\left[\max_{y}\,\big\{t_{1}(\bm{Q}_{1},y)\;+\;t_{2}(\bm{Q}_{2},y)\big\}\right].

Recent results show that if the two predictors are mutually calibrated, then
\text{Arb}(\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}})=0
for binary case
5
and non-binary extension
35
.

Proposition D.1
.

35

Given
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}}
, there exists a joint distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2},Y}
such that both forecasts
\bm{Q}_{1}
and
\bm{Q}_{2}
are calibrated with respect to
y
if and only if
\text{Arb}(\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}})=0
.

Economically, mutual calibration can be interpreted as the condition that eliminates any guaranteed arbitrage between the predictors. Though this result has been proved, we provide a proof here for completeness.

Proof.

Consider a zero-sum game played between an Outsider and Nature: The Outsider’s goal is to make a positive profit. The Outsider has access to the joint distribution over the two forecasts,
\bm{Q}_{1}
and
\bm{Q}_{2}
. Based on this, the Outsider chooses zero trades,
t_{1},t_{2}
, that apply to both predictors. The outsider’s payoff is
-t_{1}(\bm{Q}_{1},y)-t_{2}(\bm{Q}_{2},y)
when predictors report
\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}
and the outcome is
y
. Nature’s goal is to prevent the Outsider from making a profit. Nature does this by choosing
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2},Y}
.

-\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{zero trades:}t_{1},t_{2}\end{subarray}}\max_{K(\cdot|\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2})}\E_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}},Y\sim K(\cdot|\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2})}[t_{1}(\bm{Q}_{1},y)+t_{2}(\bm{Q}_{2},y)]

(12)

Nature must choose
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2},y}
that extends
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}}
. This is equivalent to choosing the conditional probability distribution
K(\cdot|\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2})
.

Because the objective is bilinear and the strategy domains are convex and bounded, we can use the Minimax Theorem to exchange the operators. This allows us to analyze the game as if Nature moves first. If
\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}
are mutually calibrated, then nature can pick their common prior such that no zero trade can profit. If
\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}
are not mutually calibrated, regardless what nature chooses, the outsider can always make positive profit by playing after nature.

Notice that, fix trades
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
, the optimal strategy for nature is to concentrate the probability mass
\mathcal{D}(y\mid\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2})
to a single outcome. This simplification reduces the original min-max problem to the following form:

-\,\min_{\text{zero-trade }(t_{1},t_{2})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}}}\!\left[\max_{y}\,\big\{t_{1}(\bm{Q}_{1},y)\;+\;t_{2}(\bm{Q}_{2},y)\big\}\right].

∎

D.2
A Quantified Relationship

Definition D.2

(MinECE)
.

Let
\bm{Q}_{1}
and
\bm{Q}_{2}
be two predictions with joint distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}}
. Define the minimal sum of absolute calibration errors as

\text{MinECE}(\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}})=\frac{1}{2}\min_{K(\cdot\mid\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2})}\E_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}},Y\sim K(\cdot|\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2})}\Big[\,\|\bm{Q}_{1}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{Q}_{1}]\|_{1}+\|\bm{Q}_{2}-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{Q}_{2}]\|_{1}\,\Big].

We use
\mathbf{y},\mathbf{Y}\in\{0,1\}^{|\mathcal{Y}|}
to denote the one-hot vector representations of state
y
and random state
Y
, respectively. When
\mathcal{Y}=\{s_{1},s_{2},\cdots\}
. For a state
y=s_{i}
, the vector
\mathbf{y}
contains a 1 at index
i
and 0 elsewhere. This representation maps the categorical state space to a vector space where the probability distribution is simply the expectation
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{Y}]
.

MinECE represents the minimal sum of absolute calibration errors, minimized over all possible joint distributions
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2},Y}
that extend
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}}.

We introduce a geometric interpretation of MinECE.
\mathcal{Q}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
denote the sets of all possible realizations of the predictors
\bm{Q}_{1}
and
\bm{Q}_{2}
, respectively.
Any pair of transformations

h_{1}:\mathcal{Q}_{1}\to\Delta(\mathcal{Y}),\qquad h_{2}:\mathcal{Q}_{2}\to\Delta(\mathcal{Y})

can be represented as an
\big(|\mathcal{Q}_{1}|+|\mathcal{Q}_{2}|\big)|\mathcal{Y}|
-dimensional vector. We use
h_{0}^{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}
to denote the identical transformation that maps every element in
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
to itself.

Given a fixed marginal distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}}
,
define the feasible set of transformations as

\mathcal{H}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{feas}}=\Big\{(h_{1},h_{2}):h_{1}(\bm{Q}_{1})\text{ and }h_{2}(\bm{Q}_{2})\text{ are mutually calibrated under }\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}}\Big\}.

The induced
\ell_{1}
distance between two pairs of transformations

(h_{1},h_{2})
and
(h^{\prime}_{1},h^{\prime}_{2})
is defined by

\|(h_{1},h_{2})-(h^{\prime}_{1},h^{\prime}_{2})\|_{1}=\E_{\mathbb{P}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}}}\!\left[\|h_{1}(\bm{Q}_{1})-h^{\prime}_{1}(\bm{Q}_{1})\|_{1}+\|h_{2}(\bm{Q}_{2})-h^{\prime}_{2}(\bm{Q}_{2})\|_{1}\right].

Then,

2\,\mathrm{MinECE}(\mathbb{P}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}})=D_{\ell_{1}}\big((h_{0}^{\mathcal{Q}_{1}},h_{0}^{\mathcal{Q}_{2}}),\mathcal{H}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{feas}}\big):=\min_{(h_{1},h_{2})\in\mathcal{H}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{feas}}}\E_{\mathbb{P}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}}}\!\left[\|h_{1}(\bm{Q}_{1})-\bm{Q}_{1}\|_{1}+\|h_{2}(\bm{Q}_{2})-\bm{Q}_{2}\|_{1}\right]

can be interpreted as the
\ell_{1}
distance between the identical mapping and the feasible set

\mathcal{H}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{feas}}
;
that is, it equals the minimal
\ell_{1}
distance from the pair of identical transformations to any
feasible pair
(h_{1},h_{2})\in\mathcal{H}^{(2)}_{\mathrm{feas}}
.

We subsequently connect this value to the arbitrage value, showing that a non-zero MinECE implies a discrepancy in market beliefs
that can be exploited.

Proposition D.3

(Arbitrage value & total absolute calibration error)
.

Let
\bm{Q}_{1}
and
\bm{Q}_{2}
be two predictions with joint distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}}
, and let zero trades be bounded in
[-1,1]
. We have

\text{MinECE}(\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}})\leq\text{Arb}(\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}})\leq 2\text{MinECE}(\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}})

In particular,
\text{Arb}(\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}})=0
if and only if
\bm{Q}_{1}
and
\bm{Q}_{2}
are mutually calibrated.

The proof relies on the following lemma that bridges
\ell_{1}
distance to maximal inner product.

Lemma D.4
.

For any distribution vectors
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
, the following inequality holds:

\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\|_{1}\leq\max_{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}\leq 1,\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{p}\rangle=0}\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{q}\rangle\leq\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\|_{1},

where
\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{q}\rangle
denotes the dot product of
\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{q}
,
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}=\max_{i}|\mathbf{v}_{i}|
, and
\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\|_{1}
is the
\ell_{1}
-norm of the difference between
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
.

Proof of
Proposition

D.3
.

Recall the zero-sum game between nature and outsider:

-\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{zero trades:}t_{1},t_{2}\end{subarray}}\max_{K(\cdot|\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2})}\E_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}},Y\sim K(\cdot|\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2})}[t_{1}(\bm{Q}_{1},y)+t_{2}(\bm{Q}_{2},y)]

(13)

For the arbitrage value, we begin by interchanging the min–max operators. This swap is justified by von Neumann’s minimax theorem and the convex–concave structure of the payoff function. Under this formulation, Nature moves first by selecting
K(\cdot|\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2})
, that is equivalent as selecting joint distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2},Y}
that extend the given marginal
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}}
. The Outsider responds by choosing zero trades.

For a fixed joint law
\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2},Y}
(shorthand:
\mathcal{D}
), the Outsider’s payoff can be written as

V(\mathcal{D})\;:=\;\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{zero trades }t_{1},t_{2}\end{subarray}}\E_{\mathcal{D}}[-t_{1}(\bm{Q}_{1},y)-t_{2}(\bm{Q}_{2},y)].

(We use max operator here because we have put the negative sign inside.)

By definition of
Arb
,

\text{Arb}(\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}})=\min_{K(\cdot\mid\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2})}V(\mathcal{D}).

Thus it suffices to show, for every fixed
\mathcal{D}
,

\text{MinECE}(\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}})\;\leq\;V(\mathcal{D})\;\leq\;2\,\text{MinECE}(\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}}).

Fix a law
\mathcal{D}
. For each realized value of
\bm{Q}_{1}
define

\mathbf{p}_{1}=\bm{Q}_{1},\qquad\mathbf{q}_{1}=\E[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{Q}_{1}],

and for any zero trade
t_{1}
set

\mathbf{v}_{1}(y)=-t_{1}(\bm{Q}_{1},y).

Since
t_{1}
is a zero trade we have
\langle\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{1}\rangle=0
. Moreover,

\langle\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{q}_{1}\rangle=\E_{\mathcal{D}}[-t_{1}(\bm{Q}_{1},y)\mid\bm{Q}_{1}],\qquad\|\mathbf{p}_{1}-\mathbf{q}_{1}\|_{1}=\|\bm{Q}_{1}-\E[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{Q}_{1}]\|_{1}.

Applying Lemma
D.4
(with
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{q}_{1},\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}_{1}
) yields, for each
\bm{Q}_{1}
,

\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{Q}_{1}-\E[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{Q}_{1}]\|_{1}\;\leq\;\max_{t_{1}}\E_{\mathcal{D}}[-t_{1}(\bm{Q}_{1},y)\mid\bm{Q}_{1}]\;\leq\;\|\bm{Q}_{1}-\E[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{Q}_{1}]\|_{1}.

Taking an expectation over
\bm{Q}_{1}
gives

\frac{1}{2}\E_{\mathcal{D}}\big[\,\|\bm{Q}_{1}-\E[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{Q}_{1}]\|_{1}\big]\;\leq\;\max_{t_{1}}\E_{\mathcal{D}}[-t_{1}(\bm{Q}_{1},y)]\;\leq\;\E_{\mathcal{D}}\big[\,\|\bm{Q}_{1}-\E[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{Q}_{1}]\|_{1}\big].

An identical argument for
\bm{Q}_{2}
yields

\frac{1}{2}\E_{\mathcal{D}}\big[\,\|\bm{Q}_{2}-\E[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{Q}_{2}]\|_{1}\big]\;\leq\;\max_{t_{1}}\E_{\mathcal{D}}[-t_{1}(\bm{Q}_{2},y)]\;\leq\;\E_{\mathcal{D}}\big[\,\|\bm{Q}_{2}-\E[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{Q}_{2}]\|_{1}\big].

Because the Outsider can choose
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
independently,

\E_{\mathcal{D}}\big[\,\|\bm{Q}_{1}-\E[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{Q}_{1}]\|_{1}+\|\bm{Q}_{2}-\E[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{Q}_{2}]\|_{1}\,\big]\;\leq\;V(\mathcal{D})\;\leq\;2\,\E_{\mathcal{D}}\big[\,\|\bm{Q}_{1}-\E[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{Q}_{1}]\|_{1}+\|\bm{Q}_{2}-\E[\mathbf{Y}\mid\bm{Q}_{2}]\|_{1}\,\big].

Finally, minimizing the left- and right-hand sides over Nature’s choice
K(\cdot\mid\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2})
yields

\text{MinECE}(\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}})\;\leq\;\text{Arb}(\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}})\;\leq\;2\,\text{MinECE}(\mathcal{D}_{\bm{Q}_{1},\bm{Q}_{2}}),

which is the desired bound.
∎

Remark D.5
.

Here the definition of ECE is slightly different from the commonly used one in machine learning model calibration literature. The most commonly used definition of ECE only cares about the confidence of the model’s predictions. The definition of MinECE we use above is more related to the concept of
multi-class calibration
proposed by
28
, which states that the proportion of all possible outcomes among all possible instances getting the same forecast
\bm{q}
matches the prediction vector
\bm{q}
. This is a stronger requirement.

Proof of
Lemma

D.4
.

The upper bound follows straightforwardly from Hölder’s inequality:

\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\rangle\leq\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\|_{1}.

Since
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, we have

\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{q}\rangle\leq\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\|_{1},

proving the upper bound.

To prove the lower bound, consider a vector
\mathbf{u}
such that
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
and
\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{q}\rangle=\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\|_{1}
. Next, define
\mathbf{v}^{*}
as

\mathbf{v}^{*}=\frac{\mathbf{u}-\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{p}\rangle\mathbf{1}}{2}.

\mathbf{1}
denotes the vector whose coordinates are all one.
Notice that
\|\mathbf{v}^{*}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
and
\langle\mathbf{v}^{*},\mathbf{p}\rangle=0
, as required.

Now, we can compute the value of
\langle\mathbf{v}^{*},\mathbf{q}\rangle
:

\langle\mathbf{v}^{*},\mathbf{q}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{q}\rangle-\langle\mathbf{u},\mathbf{p}\rangle\right)=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\|_{1}.

Thus, we have

\max_{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\infty}\leq 1,\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{p}\rangle=0}\langle\mathbf{v},\mathbf{q}\rangle\geq\langle\mathbf{v}^{*},\mathbf{q}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}\|_{1}.

This completes the proof.
∎

E.2.1
Prompting Strategy and Confidence Extraction

We describe the prompting strategies and the subsequent procedure for extracting confidence scores.

Prompting Strategy.

Following previous studies
24
,
47

Base

Instruct
models typically generate reasoning contents before the final answer, making direct next-token probability extraction unreliable. To employ their CoT capabilities
48
, we adopt a two-step approach widely used in instructing LLMs to verbalize confidence scores
31
,
45
. We first prompt the model to generate a reasoning process using the standard zero-shot prompts. Subsequently, we append this reasoning process along with an answer-triggering phrase to extract the next-token probability. Specific prompt templates are detailed in Figure
2
and Figure
3
.

Confidence Extraction.

After extract the log-probabilities from LLMs, we construct the (answer, confidence) pair through the following renormalization process. Let
\mathcal{Y}
denote the set of valid answer tokens (e.g.,
\mathcal{Y}=\{\text{A},\text{B},\text{C},\text{D}\}
). We denote the raw log-probability of a token
y\in\mathcal{Y}
assigned by model
\mathcal{M}
given input
\mathbf{x}
as
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}(y|\mathbf{x})
. Since
\mathcal{Y}
is a subspace of the full vocabulary, we re-normalize the distribution over
\mathcal{Y}
using a restricted softmax:

\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{M}}(y|\mathbf{x})=\frac{\exp(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}(y|\mathbf{x}))}{\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\exp(\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{M}}(y^{\prime}|\mathbf{x}))},

The token with the highest re-normalized probability is selected as the predicted answer, with
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{M}}(y|\mathbf{x})
serving as the associated confidence score.

E.2.2
Joint Distribution Estimation

To compute the recalibration map, we approximate the joint distribution
\mathcal{D}_{R_{0},R_{1}}
using the empirical distribution derived from the dataset. Let the report from a predictor be denoted as a pair
r=(\widehat{y},\widehat{c})
, consisting of an answer
\widehat{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
and a continuous confidence score
\widehat{c}\in[0,1]
.

Given the continuous nature of confidence scores, direct estimation of the joint distribution on finite samples is theoretically sparse. Therefore, we utilize a discretization process with a step size
s\in[0,1]
. We define a quantization function
\psi_{s}:[0,1]\to\mathcal{C}_{s}
that maps a continuous confidence score to the nearest value on the discrete grid
\mathcal{C}_{s}=\{0,s,2s,\dots,1\}
. Specifically, for an observed report
r_{i}=(\widehat{y}_{i},\widehat{c}_{i})
, the discretized report is given by
\widetilde{r}_{i}=(\widehat{y}_{i},\psi_{s}(\widehat{c}_{i}))
.

Let
\{(\widetilde{r}_{0i},\widetilde{r}_{1i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
denote the set of discretized report pairs generated by the
Base
and
Instruct
models for
N
input samples in the evaluation dataset. The estimated joint distribution is then formulated as the normalized frequency of these discretized pairs:

\widehat{\mathcal{D}}_{R_{0},R_{1}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{(\widetilde{r}_{0i},\widetilde{r}_{1i})},

(14)

where
\delta_{(\cdot)}
is the Dirac delta mass. In our main experiments, we set
s=0.01
, ensuring a fine-grained yet computationally tractable grid for probability estimation.

E.3
Baselines Details

We compare our
label-free
method against several established
supervised
calibration techniques. Unlike our method, these baselines require a labeled validation set to learn a calibration map
\mathcal{T}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
. This map transforms the original confidence
\widehat{c}
into a calibrated score
\widetilde{c}
while preserving the original model prediction
\widehat{y}
.

Although these baselines are originally developed for full probability distributions over the outcome space
\mathcal{Y}
, we adapt them to our (answer, confidence) setting by formulating calibration as a binary classification task. Specifically, we define the ground truth for the
i
-th sample as
z_{i}=\mathbb{I}(\widehat{y}_{i}=y_{i})\in\{0,1\}
, indicating whether the predicted answer
\widehat{y}_{i}
matches the true label
y_{i}
. Consequently, the calibration dataset is constructed as pairs of initial confidence and correctness indicators:
\{(\widehat{c}_{i},z_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{val}}}
. Then the supervised baselines can be formulated as:

•

Temperature Scaling (TS)

22
.
Originally designed for multi-class logits, TS is adapted to binary calibration by scaling the log-odds of the confidence, which can also be viewed as a special case of
Platt Scaling

39
with no bias. It optimizes a scalar temperature
T>0
to minimize the Negative Log Likelihood (NLL) on the calibration set:

\min_{T}-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{val}}}\Big[z_{i}\log(\sigma(\phi_{i}/T))+(1-z_{i})\log(1-\sigma(\phi_{i}/T))\Big],

where
\phi_{i}=\text{logit}(\widehat{c}_{i})=\log(\frac{\widehat{c}_{i}}{1-\widehat{c}_{i}})
denotes the uncalibrated log-odds, and
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid function. The resulting calibrated confidence score is given by:

\widetilde{c}=\sigma\left(\frac{1}{T}\log\left(\frac{\widehat{c}}{1-\widehat{c}}\right)\right).

•

Histogram Binning (HB)

50
.
This non-parametric method partitions the confidence interval
[0,1]
into
B
disjoint bins defined by boundaries
0=a_{1}<\dots<a_{B+1}=1
. For any sample falling into bin
B_{m}=[a_{m},a_{m+1})
, the calibrated score
\theta_{m}
is estimated as the average empirical accuracy (i.e., the mean of
z_{i}
) within that bin. The calibration map can be viewed as minimizing the bin-wise squared loss:

\min_{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{B}}\sum_{m=1}^{B}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{val}}}\mathbb{I}(\widehat{c}_{i}\in B_{m})(\theta_{m}-z_{i})^{2},

assigning
\widetilde{c}=\theta_{m}
wherever
\widehat{c}\in B_{m}
. We use a uniform partition with
B=10
.

•

Isotonic Regression (IR)

51
.
IR fits a piecewise constant, non-decreasing function
f
to transform uncalibrated confidences. It solves a similar optimization problem to Histogram Binning, but jointly optimizes the bin boundaries and bin predictions under strict monotonicity constraints. Specifically, we can write the optimization problem as:

\displaystyle\min_{f}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{val}}}(f(\widehat{c}_{i})-z_{i})^{2}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\forall i,j:\widehat{c}_{i}\leq\widehat{c}_{j}\implies f(\widehat{c}_{i})\leq f(\widehat{c}_{j}).

The calibrated confidence is obtained via
\widetilde{c}=f(\widehat{c})
.

E.4
Evaluation Metrics Formula

Let
\mathcal{D}=\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
denote the evaluation dataset. For each sample
\bm{x}_{i}
, the model predicts an answer
\widehat{y}_{i}
with an associated confidence
\widehat{c}_{i}\in[0,1]
. We evaluate the model’s performance from three perspectives:

•

Prediction Accuracy (Acc).
This metric measures the correctness of the LLM’s predictions:

\displaystyle\text{Acc}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}(\widehat{y}_{i}=y_{i}),

where
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
is the indicator function.

•

Calibration Metrics.
We assess calibration performance using
Expected Calibration Error (ECE)

22
and the
Brier Score (BS)

9
. Brier Score measures the mean squared error between the confidence and the prediction correctness:

\displaystyle\text{BS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\widehat{c}_{i}-\mathbb{I}(\widehat{y}_{i}=y_{i}))^{2}.

For ECE, we uniformly partition the confidence interval
[0,1]
into
B=10
bins, with
B_{m}
containing samples with confidence within the
m
-th interval
(\frac{m-1}{M},\frac{m}{M}]
. ECE is calculated as the weighted average of the absolute difference between the accuracy and confidence of each bin:

\displaystyle\text{ECE}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{|B_{m}|}{N}\left|\text{acc}(B_{m})-\text{conf}(B_{m})\right|,

(15)

where
|B_{m}|
is the number of samples in bin
m
. The accuracy and confidence within each bin
B_{m}
are defined as:

\displaystyle\text{acc}(B_{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|B_{m}|}\sum_{\bm{x}_{i}\in B_{m}}\mathbb{I}(\widehat{y}_{i}=y_{i}),

\displaystyle\text{conf}(B_{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|B_{m}|}\sum_{\bm{x}_{i}\in B_{m}}\widehat{c}_{i}.

(16)

•

Optimization Objective:
We also report the
Confidence Loss (CL)
as defined in Equation
1
, which serves as our primary optimization target:

\text{CL}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(-\mathbb{I}(\widehat{y}_{i}=y_{i})+(\widehat{c}_{i}-\mathbb{I}(\widehat{y}_{i}=y_{i}))^{2}\right).
