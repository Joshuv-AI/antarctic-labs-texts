Stochastic growth marks in Crocodylus niloticus
Authors: Anusuya Chinsamy, Maria-Eugenia Pereyra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSkeletochronology combined with growth curve reconstruction is routinely used to assess the age and growth dynamics of extinct and extant vertebrates. Here we performed in vivo labelling studies of the bone histology of four 2 years-old Crocodylus niloticus individuals. We found that all the crocodiles have more growth marks in their compacta than expected for their age, i.e., they deposited stochastic growth marks in their bones. Using the fluorochrome markers we determined that these stochastic growth marks were deposited during their favourable season of growth. The variable preservation of growth marks in the crocodile bones highlights developmental plasticity in their growth, which can be extrapolated to extinct archosaurs, and other reptiles. We caution the use of growth marks in fossil bones as a reliable estimator of age and discuss the far-reaching implications this has for growth curve reconstruction and life history assessments of extinct vertebrates, such as nonavian dinosaurs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31384-5.Subject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Evolution, Zoology

Introduction

Many extant vertebrates grow in a cyclical manner with alternating deposits of zones, and annuli, which result in growth marks (GM) in the compacta of their bones1–4. Seasonality and other environmental and internal physiological factors appear to be the driver for the formation of these GM5,6. Experiments using fluorochrome stains, such as tetracycline have permitted the verification that a zone is formed during the fast-growing season, whereas an annulus is deposited during the slow growing period e.g.,7–10. Annuli are often associated with a Line of Arrested Growth (LAG) that indicates a cessation of bone growth11. Thus, by counting the number of cycles of annuli/zones in a bone, an estimate of the minimum age of the animal can be deduced e.g.,2. Since remodeling processes in the bone often removes growth rings formed early in ontogeny retro-calculation is necessary to provide a more accurate estimate the age of the animal (e.g.,4,12). Through the application of skeletochronology in a growth series of a species, statistically supported growth curves can be deduced, thereby permitting a host of information about their life history traits (e.g.,5). Skeletochronology and its utility in growth curve assessment, has been widely applied to various vertebrates that grow in a cyclical manner, such as, amphibians (e.g.,13,14, chelonians e.g.,9,15–21, lepidosaurs (e.g.,5,22–25), and crocodilians (e.g.,7,26–30). It has also been applied to mammals (e.g., Microcebus31; hedgehogs32, and long-lived birds, such as the kiwi33, and extinct giant birds (e.g., Aepyornithids34 and Dromornithids35. The application of this methodology has permitted important deductions about various aspects the life history of a wide range of extinct reptiles (e.g., Neogene tortoises20; extinct crocodilians27,36–38, including nonavian dinosaurs (e.g.,39–48).

The precise histological nature of the cyclically formed anulus and/or LAGs appears to be rather complex. Sometimes double or accessory lines are recorded (e.g.,9,16,24). In some cases, these are thought to be in response to aestivation or other environmental49stress, such as in Newts living in high altitudes13, where they are closely associated with the annulus, and do not influence the age estimation of the animals. In the leatherback turtle, Dermochelys coriacea, a couplet of dark and light LAGs in ossicles were counted as a single annual event, with the dark lines linked to annual migrations to foraging areas, and the light lines attributed to additional migrations16. Sometimes, a single LAG splits into multiple lines in different part of the cross section, and at other times they appear to merge, making it extremely difficult to understand if a group of LAGs represent one incident of slowed growth or multiple events e.g.,46,49. To complicate matters even further, there are also noncyclical marks that occur in the compacta of vertebrates: hatching lines (HL) have been recognised in reptiles24,50, and in mammals, a line marking birth, the so-called neonatal line or Birth Line (BL) is recognised51,52. In the latter instances, there is a change in the nature of the bone before and after this life history event, making these growth marks readily identifiable. A weaning line (WL), marking reduced growth perhaps from food shortage after weaning, has been reported in the jaws of young hedgehogs32, although such lines are not always deposited, especially when the transition to independent feeding occurs smoothly. WL have been reported in captive red deer53, and perhaps for the same reason as hedgehogs, not in wild individuals where weaning is not traumatic. Sometimes the WL has been referred to as an “adhesion” line54 or a “discordance line”31. In the hand-raised red deer, and in the modern leporid, L. europaeus55, the pre- to post-weaning transition is indicated by a change in the rate of bone deposition53, and this is often (though not always associated with a LAG, or partial LAG or annulus. A study by Schucht et al.,49 on a range of known aged vertebrates found that skeletochronology could not reliably estimate the age of the individuals, irrespective of whether petrographic or stained microtomised sections were used.

Here we studied known age juveniles of Crocodylus niloticus. We conducted in vivo fluorescent labelling to validate the use of growth marks for age estimation, and to assess which long bones of the skeleton reliably preserved a track record of growth.

Methods

Four one year old Crocodylus niloticus individuals held in captivity at the Le Bonheur Reptiles and Adventures farm, near Cape Town in the Western Cape (South Africa) were injected with fluorochrome markers on three occasions. Note that for all individual, the fluorochrome dosage was specifically measured according to their body weights8. The crocodiles were born between March and April 2011. They were fed regularly during summer but were not fed during winter. On the farm, they were kept in enclosures with small pools, in groups of about 10 crocodiles of similar age. Individuals in our study were marked by using colour tags, a “Short Red” tag, and a “Long Red” tag for the two males, and a “Short Green” tag and a “Long Green” tag for the two females. These specimens are referred to according to their tags. In general, these individuals experienced the same environmental conditions. The treatment began when they were one year old, and the staining was done in the following sequence: Engemycin (April 2012); Alzarin red (November 2012), Engemycin (May 2013) (Table 1). When each injection was administered, the mass and length of each specimen was collected (Table 1). Upon death at just over 2 years of age (June 2013), mid-shaft, un-demineralised thin sections of various long bones (humerus, femur, ulna, radius, tibia and fibula) of each of the crocodiles were prepared using standard methods57. The sections were examined and photographed using a petrographic microscope (Zeiss, E200 at the Department of Biological Sciences, at the University of Cape Town (UCT). To study the fluorochrome stains in the bone, we used a confocal microscope (ZEISS LSM 880 with Airyscan at the Confocal and Light Microscope Imaging Facility of the Faculty of Health Sciences at UCT). All the histological slides will be deposited in the Vertebrate Comparative Collection of Iziko Museums of Cape Town.

Results

Since the crocodiles were sacrificed in their second year of life, we expected to see two growth marks in their compacta, and three fluorescent stains in their bones. More specifically we expected to see one annulus before the first Engemycin injection (green stain), and another annulus between the green and the red stain. The Alizarin red was expected following the annulus (i.e. the unfavourable season of growth) which should than have been followed a final green marker close to the periosteal surface (Fig. 1A). However, we observed several more GMs recorded in the bones of these two-year-old crocodiles (Fig. 1B). Additionally, it is worth mentioning that the last injection, which should have left a green line close to the surface of the bone, was not observed in any of the appendicular bones of the four specimens. Interesting, the number of GMs also varied in the different bones (Table 2). In several of the skeletal elements, we were able to identify that the earliest GM, and hence slowdown in growth represented a “hatching” line (HL), that overlaid embryonic bone tissue (Fig. 1C, F). Contrary to our expectations of two GMs, we found as many as five LAGs in the radius, ulna and fibula of one of the females, with most of the other crocodiles showing at least 2 extra GMs, as well as a HL (see Table 2; Fig. 1). Only one of the female crocodiles maintained an accurate number of GMs in most of its bones, with only its ulna and fibula recording additional GMs; Table 1). From the GM data obtained, it is evident that different bones from the same skeleton express variable numbers of GMs in the compacta (Table 1). In most of the individuals the ulna, fibula and radius recorded more than one GM during the first year of life and it is impossible to distinguish which of these is the seasonal GM or stochastic GM. The deposition of stochastic GMs in their second year of life is discernible because of the fluorochrome stains and allows us to deduce that they were deposited during the favourable season of growth (Fig. 2). The humerus, femur and tibia also revealed more GMs than expected but with less frequency (Table 2). We also found that the period of slow growth takes the form of an annulus (accompanied by a LAG) in the femora and humeri (Fig. 2), but in the fibula, radius and ulna, just LAGs were deposited (Fig. 3), indicating more abrupt pauses in the growth of these bones.

Discussion

Our results demonstrate additional GMs preserved in the compacta of multiple bones of the skeletons of known age crocodiles. In most cases we were able to identify one GM as a HL, since the preceding tissue was clearly embryonic bone and differed in structure and texture from the post-embryonic bone tissue (e.g.,28,29,58). However, in addition to the hatching line, most the bones of the individuals studied show at least two additional growth marks before the deposition of the first growth cycle, and several bones show the deposition of an additional growth mark in the second year of growth (see Table 1). These results suggests that these juvenile crocodiles, were susceptible to external environmental factors, possibly related to competition for food, dominance or physiological factors (e.g.,7,59,60).

Growth marks exceeding the age of the animal have been previously reported in various known aged vertebrates28,61–64. In these studies these “growth marks” have variously been referred to as “accessory”, “extra”, “accidental”, “supernumerary” growth marks4,22. Finding three additional GM (besides the hatching line) in an 8-month-old caiman, led Pereyra et al.,28 to suggest that perhaps the first year of life is harsh and that the formation of the GM may be related to temperature, food acquisition and competition. Extra growth marks in the 5-year-old North Island brown kiwi (A. mantelli), led Heck and Woodward63 to suggest that perhaps hatching, egg-laying or captivity induced their formation. In the latter study, the consistent mismatch of the number of LAGs in the bone and the known age of the two kiwi specimens studied, led these authors to suggest that there was a decoupling of age and GM in the bones. This concurs with labelling studies done by Collins and Rodda64 on captive Boiga irregularis, where the formation of additional growth marks led them to conclude that the GM preserved in the mandible of the snakes were not annually formed, and more likely related to ecdysis.

Although additional growth marks have been recognised for several decades understanding precisely which of the growth marks were authentic annual marks and which were “extra” GM has been elusive. The main reasons for this is twofold: (i) there appears to be no histological difference in the GM formed annually and those formed stochastically (e.g.,2,5,28,63). Although in Loggerhead Sea Turtles, Caretta caretta, Zug et al.,64reported accessory LAGs to be less intense or more diffuse or incomplete, and Avens et al.16 also found this to be the case for accessory lines in the ossicles of leatherback turtles; (ii) Although several earlier labelling studies have been done, their focus has been on assessing bone depositional rates e.g.,8,10,31,65–72 and validation of the seasonal formation of zones and annuli (and LAGs) e.g.,7. Starck and Chinsamy68 combined bone labelling studies with food restriction and biomechanical loading experiments on Japanese quail (Coturnix japonica) to assess whether LAG formation could be induced, but they found that neither treatment triggered the formation of LAGs in these birds.

The current study is novel in that it has permitted an assessment of when the stochastic growth marks form. We demonstrate that several of the bones showed a noncyclical slowdown in growth during what would have been their favourable season of growth. We are uncertain as to the cause/s of these unseasonal slowdowns in growth, but it could be nutritional stress because of competition, or perhaps physiological conditions such as in response to heat stress, as observed in kangaroos73. Irrespective of the reason for their deposition, the mere presence of the stochastic growth marks demonstrate an inherent plasticity in growth, and we propose that the same is true in other vertebrates in which they occur68.

It is well known that many extant vertebrates change their relative growth rates in response to hostile environmental conditions, which may be an adaptive response. For example, Jacobsen and Kushlan74 found that alligators from the Everglades in southern Florida grew more slowly than ones from more temperate areas, which may have been because of increased metabolic costs and limited resources or perhaps due to social dominance75. Brito et al.,76 found that relic crocodiles in the Sahara Desert exhibit a retardation of growth perhaps because of ephemeral water bodies limiting feeding and food availability. Interpopulation differences between populations of the newt, Triturus marmoratus at high and low latitudes in Portugal showed differences in life history events, i.e. individuals from high altitudes reached older ages than those from lower altitudes but both reached older ages than ones from further south. Southern newts also reached sexual maturity at smaller mean sizes than northern ones77. In a study of Gallotia species in the Canary Islands, Castanet and Baez24 found a variable expression of LAGs in the different species and in different populations, which they attributed to varying local climatic conditions on the different islands. They specifically noted that more distinctive LAGs occurred in the high-altitude species (G. stehlini), probably because of strong seasonality at 1800 m. In a more extreme case, Webb et al.,78 found that changes in the availability of food along the McKinlay River area in the Northern Territory of Australia led to changes in the rates of growth of Crocodylus johnstoni i.e., the upstream population grew at slower rates as compared to downstream populations. They also reported substantial variations in growth of individual members of each population. A study of known aged vertebrates also documented excess number of growth marks in the lissamphibian, Bombina orientalis and the lepidosaur, Paroedura picta50. Although most birds do not experience cyclical protracted growth, the kiwi, Apteryx mantelli is noteworthy in that its compacta is regularly interrupted by growth rings33,64. A recent study of Apteryx found more LAGs than the known age of the individual64.

. The consistent expression of LAGs in stem Mesozoic birds (i.e. basal to the nonornithurine euornithians), and their poor development in later birds, led Starck and Chinsamy68 to propose that this reflected the loss of developmental plasticity in the evolution of birds. Among sauropodomorph dinosaurs, Chinsamy39 reported different numbers of growth rings in similar sized Massospondylus femora, and developmental plasticity has been explicitly invoked in Plateosaurus79, and Massospondylus80. Intriguingly, Mussaurus, an intensely histologically sampled nonavian dinosaur shows that although most of them have GM, there is a dramatic complete absence of GM in some specimens48 which suggests individual responses to local conditions. Furthermore, in a study of the Mesozoic bird, Confuciusornis, Chinsamy et al.,81 noted that the number of LAGs in the bones did not correlate with body sizes of the birds, which they suggested indicated developmental plasticity in the growth of this early bird, In a study of the crocodylomorph, Terrestrisuchus, it was reported that two similar sized individuals of had different numbers of LAGs, whilst a larger individual had no LAGs in its compacta82. More recently, a study of the alligatoroid crocodilian Diplocynodon, Hoffman et al.,38 found that a femur 50% the size of the largest, recorded 21 GM with most of these being relatively closely stacked together. They proposed that perhaps this indicated sexual dimorphism in their sample or represented developmental plasticity.

Implications for assessing the growth dynamics and life history traits of extinct animals

Skeletochronology has been applied to a wide range of extinct animals (e.g.,20,27,42,45,83–89). For many of these animals, growth curves have been developed using skeletochronology to assess the age of the animal. When applying skeletochronology, researchers are usually aware of the fact that in most species, bone tissue and GMs formed during early ontogeny are often obliterated because of the remodeling processes of growth. Therefore to account for these “missing GMs”, researchers have proposed various methods to back-calculate or retro-calculate the number of GMs that were removed e.g.,16,24,39. As such, it is well recognised that the count of GMs in the compacta of extinct animals provides an indication of the “minimum” age of the individual24,90. However, these earlier studies have not considered that the occurrence of “stochastic” growth marks in the bones of their study animals could lead to an overestimation of the age of the animal.

The current study demonstrates that by simply counting the number of GMs in the bones of 2-year-old crocodiles, their age would have been estimated as 6 years (if the HL was unrecognised), and as 5 years (with the HL discerned). Although our study records stochastic GM in juvenile crocodiles, we propose that the inherent plasticity most likely still occurred in larger individuals, especially considering that aggression and competition become increasingly common with increasing age. Furthermore, since the extant phylogenetic bracket of dinosaurs, birds64 and crocodilians, have the innate ability to deposited “stochastic” GMs, it is likely that nonavian dinosaurs and perhaps other archosaurs also had this flexible growth strategy (plasticity).

There are currently two issues that need to be considered when reconstructing growth curves for dinosaurs and other extinct animals: (i) the problem of the obliteration of early formed GMs, (ii) the issue of “stochastic” or extra GMs. In very young dinosaurs and other vertebrates, if embryonic bone is preserved, one can readily identify the HL91, but currently there is no way to distinguish between the stochastic GMs and “normal” GMs formed during the unfavourable season of growth5. This is further complicated by intraskeletal variation in that different bones preserve different numbers of GM19,28,29,64,92, which can lead to different age estimates, which would directly impact growth curves being reconstructed. Furthermore, in multi-elemental studies it is usually good practice to utilise the skeletal element that preserves the highest number of GM since this is the bone that is considered to best represent the track record of growth19,29, but given the results of our study, it turns out that this may not be the case. Considering all these factors regarding the application of skeletochronology, it is imperative that the issue of stochastic GMs is considered when developing growth curves for extinct animals, since they directly influence deductions concerning various life history events, such as age of sexual maturity and skeletal maturity. The presence of stochastic GMs also directly impacts calculations of daily/annual bone appositional rates of extinct and extant vertebrates.

Given this real issue of stochastic GMs, we propose that GMs in the compacta of bones should not be considered as annually formed (i.e. reflecting age) but rather be recognised as intermittent cycles of growth that reflect how the animal grew through ontogeny. This means that instead of saying an animal was 5 years old, one would refer to the fact that the animal experienced 5 cycles of growth irrespective of whether 1 or all 5 cycles occurred in one year. The same applies to growth curves deduced using perimeter measurements, i.e., they would still give a reasonable account of the dynamics of growth of the bone, with the caveat that the cycles need not be annual. It must be recognised that different bones within a single skeleton each provide a unique history of growth and do not reflect the overall growth of the animal. Thus, extreme caution is necessary when comparing growth dynamics across taxa in time and space and even between individuals within a population.

Limitations of the current study

Although this study offers some unique perspectives for our understanding of the growth dynamics of Crocodylus niloticus, with some serious implications for palaeobiological research we are aware of what might be considered limitations of our study. Firstly, our studied sampled juveniles held captive at a crocodile farm in the Western Cape of South Africa i.e., further South as compared to their natural Northern distribution. During the summer, the mean annual maximum and minimum temperatures registered at the farm are 28 °C and 16 °C as compared to 30 °C and 21 °C in their northern South African range, whereas, during winter, the maximum and minimum temperatures are between 17 and 6 °C at the farm, as compared to 24 °C and 10 °C in their wild distribution in South Africa (Weather Spark: accessed 5 June 2025, https://weatherspark.com/). Since ambient temperature plays such a vital role in the metabolism of poikilothermic ectotherms, it is quite likely that their captive environment posed challenges for them and may have contributed to the deposition of stochastic growth marks. In terms of rainfall, we note that although the mean high and low values of rainfalls are similar at the farm and in their normal distribution, they experienced higher rainfall at the farm in winter, whereas this occurs in the summer in their wild range. However, since rainfall is related to the depth of the water which impacts food availability78, since our crocodiles were captive, the pools in which they occurred would have been maintained at a consistent level and food was regularly provided. Secondly, since our sample consists only of juveniles, it may well be that older crocodiles will not form stochastic growth marks, although there is no reason to suspect that this is the case, especially since as crocodiles mature they face stress due to competition for various resources (e.g.,15). Thirdly, we wondered if the act of handling the juveniles during the administration of the injections may have been stressful and resulted in the formation of the stochastic GM. However, we note that there is an additional GM prior to the administration of the first injection. Additionally, although Hutton7 applied a similar fluorochrome study on C. niloticus, he did not report any associated additional growth marks. Furthermore, several of the earlier studies found GM that exceeded the age of the animal, even when the individuals were not handled in any way (e.g.,49,63). Fourthly, territorial behaviour and competition are other stress factors that crocodilians experience (e.g.,93), both in the wild and in captivity. In captive situations, it has been observed that larger individuals tend to be aggressive and dominate others in the population by restricting their access to food and to the pools for thermal regulation which may limit their growth93,94. It is therefore noteworthy that in our study we observed stochastic growth marks in both the smallest and largest of our individuals.

Despite being on captive juveniles, our study demonstrates the ability for flexible growth in C. niloticus. To improve and expand on this study, future studies could consider working on wild populations of crocodilians in their normal distribution and include a wider ontogenetic range of individuals.

Conclusions

This study documents the occurrence of multiple stochastic GM in several skeletal elements of known aged crocodiles. We demonstrate that these GM formed during the favourable season of growth. These results have far-reaching consequences for using skeletochronology for growth curve reconstruction and deductions concerning the life history traits of extinct vertebrates. Like findings of other studies19,28,29,94, here we also document that different bones within single skeletons have unique records of growth, which complicates how comparisons of growth dynamics are made intra-specifically, as well as interspecifically. It is also noteworthy that all four crocodiles in our study were subjected to the same environmental conditions, but each experienced unique skeletal response. Overall, our results indicate plasticity in the growth of the crocodiles, which has direct implications for assessing various aspects of the paleobiology of archosaurs and other extinct vertebrates.
