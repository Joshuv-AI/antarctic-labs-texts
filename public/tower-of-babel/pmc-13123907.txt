Reply to Zhabagin and Sabitov: Novel knowledge is gained through fresh data and new analytical methods, not through paraphrasing
Authors: Ayken Askapuli
Published: 2026

A letter (1) has been addressed to the editor of our recent PNAS paper (2). The authors of the letter claim to have clarified the branching patterns of the Y-chromosomes of three Golden Horde elites (CKZ001, CKZ002, and CKZ003) at higher resolution using markers they suggest we failed to analyze.

The primary critique in the Letter (1) is that CKZ001 and CKZ002 should belong to two separate branches. However, we investigated this possibility extensively prior to the publication of our manuscript (2). In collaboration with the Y-chromosome team at Family Tree DNA (FTDNA) (3), we analyzed the aDNA data together with modern Y-chromosome sequences available in the FTDNA database.

Consequently, the FTDNA team identified several variants that could possibly differentiate the three individuals (Runfeldt 2025, personal communication). While CKZ001 was positive for C-FT442440* and negative for BY68504, CKZ002 was positive for C-MF691989*, but lacked coverage for C-FT442440. Furthermore, while CKZ002 showed a derived allele for C-BY68504, the signal was extremely weak, with a single C-to-T read at the very tail end of the read. It is well established that the termini of sequencing reads are highly prone to errors; therefore, the derived allele at C-BY68504 was likely a false positive. CKZ003 was positive for C-ZQ369 but lacked coverage at ZQ372, MF691989, and FT442440.

Given the missing calls and the resulting ambiguities, we purposefully omitted these markers from the manuscript. We defined the Kenggir Lineage based on the reliable terminal marker ZQ369, for which all three individuals possessed a derived allele without any ambiguity.

In essence, the Letter paraphrases existing public knowledge. The phylogenetic tree provided by Zhabagin and Sabitov (1) is effectively a mirror image of the tree publicly available on the FTDNA platform (4) (Fig. 1). Not only the tree topologies, but the number of samples in the respective trees is identical.

In conclusion, I acknowledge the authors of the Letter (1) for revealing the clan identities of the modern Kazak samples belonging to the Kenggir Lineage. Based on publicly available genetic genealogy databases, members of various ethnic groups—including Kazaks, Karakalpaks, Uzbeks, and some other ethnic groups in China and Russia—trace their ancestry to this lineage. However, the identification of modern descendants of the lineage was beyond the scope of our original study (2). The ambiguities regarding the terminal branching of these Golden Horde elites will only be resolved through resequencing and the acquisition of high-coverage aDNA data.
