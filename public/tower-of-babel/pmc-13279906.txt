Effect of marbofloxacin and cefazolin concentrations and bacterial count on lipopolysaccharide (LPS) release by Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae isolated from bovine clinical mastitis
Authors: Shunsuke MORI, Tomomi KURUMISAWA, Makoto KAWAI, Yasunori SHINOZUKA, Kazuhiro KAWAI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The amount of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) released by Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae isolated from bovine clinical mastitis and cultured with marbofloxacin (MBFX), cefazolin (CEZ), ceftazidime (CAZ), and imipenem was quantified. The relationships between antimicrobial type, concentration, and bacterial count in relation to the amount of LPS released were evaluated. Bacterial morphology, which is closely associated with LPS release, was also investigated. At an initial inoculum of 105 CFU/mL, the amount of LPS released after 4 hr of incubation with MBFX and CEZ was significantly lower than that for the negative control (NC) group at concentrations higher than 5× the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) for E. coli and higher than 50× MIC for K. pneumoniae. These findings suggest that exposure to high-dose antimicrobials results in reduced LPS release associated with bacterial growth and lysis. Conversely, at an initial inoculum of 108 CFU/mL, incubation with MBFX and CEZ at concentrations of 50× MIC and 250× MIC for 4 hr resulted in an amount of LPS released that was comparable to that for the NC group for both E. coli and K. pneumoniae. Both MBFX and CEZ also induced bacterial filamentation, but the morphological changes were less pronounced than those induced by CAZ and were associated with lower LPS release. These suggest that early administration of high concentrations of antimicrobials at low bacterial loads may mitigate the adverse effects associated with LPS release in cases of mastitis caused by E. coli and K. pneumoniae.

INTRODUCTION

Bovine mastitis is the most prevalent infectious disease affecting dairy cows, causing significant economic losses to dairy producers due to reductions in milk yield and quality [6]. The disease is caused by infection of the mammary gland by pathogenic microorganisms, most commonly bacteria, via the teat canal, although non-bacterial pathogens have also been reported [32]. Among gram-negative bacteria, Escherichia coli or Klebsiella spp. are the most common isolates from acute mastitis [7]. Klebsiella spp. cause the most severe cases of mastitis, followed by E. coli, whereas Serratia spp. and Enterobacter spp. typically cause relatively mild cases [20]. Clinical signs of mastitis caused by gram-negative bacteria arise not only from the bacteria themselves, but from the overproduction of inflammatory cytokines triggered by lipopolysaccharides (LPS). LPS are bacterial cell wall components released as endotoxins during bacterial growth or lysis. The resultant overproduction of inflammatory cytokines often induces excessive systemic inflammation, referred to as endotoxin shock [21]. Once E. coli reaches the teat cistern, the organism multiplies rapidly, increasing 8–10-fold every 2 hr and releasing large amounts of LPS [2]. LPS elicits a variety of physiological reactions, including fever, fluctuations in circulating white blood cell counts, complement activation, macrophage activation, increased vascular permeability, and altered plasma levels of metabolites, minerals, acute-phase reactants, and hormones [12, 13].

In cases of severe mastitis caused by E. coli and characterized by systemic symptoms, treatment with fluoroquinolones or third- or fourth-generation cephalosporins is recommended to limit unrestricted bacterial growth in the udder and reduce the risk of bacteremia [25]. However, several in vitro and in vivo medical studies have reported antimicrobial-induced release of endotoxins from gram-negative bacteria [15, 18, 19]. This clinical deterioration following the initiation of antimicrobial treatment is attributed to a systemic inflammatory response triggered by endotoxins released during bacterial lysis. Therefore, while systemic administration of antimicrobials can improve outcomes in mastitis caused by E. coli and Klebsiella spp. [16, 24], the use of an inappropriate antimicrobial may increase mortality.

Imipenem (IPM) is a carbapenem antimicrobial that significantly reduces endotoxin release from Pseudomonas aeruginosa compared with ceftazidime (CAZ) [8, 9, 28, 30, 33, 34]. Previous studies have attributed the low level of endotoxin release observed during IPM treatment to bacterial spheroidization [8, 9, 28, 33, 34]. A close relationship appears to exist between antimicrobial-induced endotoxin release and morphological changes in bacteria. Specifically, antimicrobial agents such as IPM that induce spheroidization of bacterial cells reduce LPS release, while agents that promote filamentation, such as CAZ, increase LPS release.

These observations suggest that the antimicrobial agents selected to treat bovine mastitis caused by gram-negative bacteria, including E. coli and Klebsiella spp., should induce minimal LPS release; however, few studies have examined the relationship between endotoxin release and the morphological changes induced by antimicrobial agents used specifically to treat bovine mastitis. In Japan, cefazolin (CEZ) is the most widely used antimicrobial agent for the treatment of bovine mastitis [10]; for severe acute cases of mastitis caused by gram-negative bacteria, fluoroquinolones are typically administered with careful consideration for prudent use [23]. Therefore, this study investigated the amount of LPS released from E. coli and K. pneumoniae isolated from clinical cases of bovine mastitis in the presence of CEZ and marbofloxacin (MBFX). The study characterized the relationship between the type and concentration of the antimicrobial agent, bacterial density, and LPS release. Furthermore, bacterial morphology, which is hypothesized to be closely linked to LPS release, was also investigated. The aim was to identify antimicrobial agents and dosing conditions that minimize LPS release, thereby contributing to the selection of optimal treatment strategies for bovine mastitis and reducing the risk of excessive inflammation and endotoxin shock.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Bacteria

A total of six E. coli strains (originating from Hokkaido, Tochigi, and Kumamoto) and five K. pneumoniae strains (originating from Hokkaido and Saitama), isolated from cases of bovine clinical mastitis in Japan in 2015, were used in this study. These strains were previously identified by PCR and DNA sequencing in our laboratory in 2023 and were stored at −80°C until use in the present study [11].

Antimicrobials

The antimicrobial agents used in this study included MBFX (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan), CEZ (Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), IPM (Sigma-Aldrich Co., LLC., St. Louis, MO, USA), and CAZ (LKT Laboratories, Inc., St. Louis, MO, USA). Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of these antimicrobial agents against the E. coli and K. pneumoniae strains were determined using the agar dilution method according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) recommendations [3].

Growth conditions and preparation of test samples

Bacterial strains were subcultured overnight at 37°C under aerobic conditions on tryptic soy agar supplemented with 5% sheep blood. Colonies were picked, suspended in sterile saline (0.9% NaCl), and adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland turbidity using a calibrated densitometer (Waken B Tech Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan). To measure antimicrobial-induced LPS release, the bacterial suspension was washed twice with sterile saline by centrifugation at 1,300 × g for 10 min at 4°C to remove residual LPS prior to antimicrobial exposure. The washed bacterial pellet was inoculated into trypticase soy broth (TSB) to reach the target concentration. The antimicrobial solution was adjusted to 10 times the final concentration and added to a 10-fold volume of TSB containing bacteria. Incubation, which was initiated immediately after adding the antimicrobial solution, was performed for 4 hr at 37°C.

The relationship between antimicrobial concentration and LPS release was evaluated in Experiment 1. The initial inoculum density was set at 105 CFU/mL, and the MBFX and CEZ concentrations tested were 0 μg/mL (NC: negative control), 0.1× MIC, 5× MIC, 50× MIC, and 250× MIC. IPM and CAZ, which reportedly exhibit low and high LPS-induction activity, respectively [29], were evaluated at concentrations of 5× MIC and 50× MIC as comparative controls. IPM and CAZ analyses were performed for E. coli only.

The relationship between inoculum density and LPS release was evaluated in Experiment 2. The initial inoculum densities were set to 105 and 108 CFU/mL based on the classification of Nagasawa et al. [17], and LPS release was measured at 50× MIC and 250× MIC of MBFX or CEZ. Experiments 1 and 2 were conducted using five strains each of E. coli and K. pneumoniae. For E. coli, four of the five strains used in Experiments 1 and 2 were identical, whereas one strain differed between experiments.

Immediately following incubation, the culture was centrifuged at 1,300 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred to an LPS-free tube (Seikagaku Corp., Tokyo, Japan) and stored at −80°C until LPS measurement. The resulting pellet was suspended in a volume of sterile saline equivalent to the supernatant volume, serially diluted 10-fold to 10−6, and 0.1 mL of each dilution was spread onto nutrient agar and incubated at 37°C overnight. Colonies were counted to determine the number of viable bacteria. The viable bacterial count at 0 hr was determined using the same procedure but without an incubation step.

Determination of antimicrobial-induced LPS

Cryopreserved samples were thawed in a mini cooler (Major Science Co., Ltd., Taoyuan, Taiwan) at 4°C. LPS release was quantified using a chromogenic Limulus amoebocyte lysate (LAL) assay (Pyrochrome®, Associates of Cape Cod, Inc., Falmouth, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 50 μL of control standard endotoxin and diluted sample were dispensed into wells of LPS-free microplates, and an equal volume of lysate was added and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Following incubation, stopping solution and diazo coupling reaction reagents were added, and absorbance was measured at 545 nm using a Powerscan spectrophotometer (DS Farma Biomedical, Tokyo, Japan). A standard curve was generated based on the absorbance of the standard solution, and the LPS concentration of each sample was calculated. Spike and recovery tests were performed on all samples to confirm recovery rates within the 50–200% range.

Examination of bacterial morphology

Bacteria were incubated in antimicrobial-containing TSB at a concentration of 5× MIC. After supernatant collection for LPS quantification and viable bacterial counting, the bacterial suspension was Gram stained and bacterial morphology was observed under a light microscope (DM500, Flexacam i5, Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany).

Statistical analysis

The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to evaluate differences in LPS release at different antimicrobial concentrations in Experiment 1. Viable bacteria counts were compared using paired t-tests in both Experiments 1 and 2. In Experiment 2, the Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare LPS release between different inoculum densities, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for comparisons between antimicrobial concentrations within the same inoculum density. Differences were considered significant at P<0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.1; R Core Team, 2020) [26].

RESULTS

Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of each antimicrobial agent

The MICs of MBFX, CEZ, IPM, and CAZ were determined for six E. coli strains and five K. pneumoniae strains isolated from clinical cases of bovine mastitis. The MBFX MIC was 0.03 μg/mL for all E. coli strains and 0.06 μg/mL for all K. pneumoniae strains. The CEZ MIC ranged from 1 to 2 μg/mL for E. coli and was 1 μg/mL for all K. pneumoniae strains. The MIC of IPM was 0.125 μg/mL for all E. coli strains and ranged from 0.125 to 0.25 μg/mL for K. pneumoniae. The CAZ MIC ranged from 0.125 to 0.25 μg/mL for both E. coli and K. pneumoniae.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that MBFX and CEZ induced significantly lower LPS release than the NC group when the initial inoculum was 105 CFU/mL at >5× MIC for E. coli and >50× MIC for K. pneumoniae, suggesting that exposure to higher doses of antimicrobial agents results in reduced LPS release. However, when the initial inoculum density was 108 CFU/mL, LPS release was comparable to that in the NC group for both E. coli and K. pneumoniae at 50× MIC and 250× MIC for MBFX and CEZ. Both MBFX and CEZ induced bacterial filamentation, yet the amount of LPS released was lower than that induced by CAZ.

Effect of antimicrobial concentration on LPS release

Several studies have examined the effects of antimicrobial agents on bacterial LPS release across various combinations of antimicrobial classes, concentrations, and bacterial species. Crosby et al. examined the effects of cefotaxime (CTX), ciprofloxacin (CPFX), piperacillin (PIPC), and tobramycin (TOB), each at 100× MIC, on LPS release by cultures of Enterobacter cloacae and E. coli at an initial density of 104 CFU/mL. TOB exposure resulted in lower LPS release than the control group (without antimicrobials), whereas CTX, CPFX, and PIPC induced the release of significantly higher amounts of LPS than the control or TOB [4]. Conversely, Eng et al. reported that exposure of E. coli and K. pneumoniae to gentamicin, fluoroquinolones (CPFX, ofloxacin, and temafloxacin), and IPM resulted in lower LPS release than in the control group (without antimicrobials) or following exposure to CAZ, aztreonam, or chloramphenicol [5].

These findings indicate that LPS release varies depending on the specific combination of antimicrobial agents used, exposure concentration, and the bacterial species [4, 5]. In this study, we investigated the effects of MBFX and CEZ on LPS release from bovine mastitis–causing bacteria. When the initial inoculum was adjusted to 105 CFU/mL, the viable counts of both E. coli and K. pneumoniae declined significantly following 4 hr of incubation with MBFX and CEZ at concentrations of 5× MIC, 50× MIC, and 250× MIC compared with preincubation (0 hr). The amount of LPS released was significantly lower than that for the NC group for E. coli at >5× MIC and for K. pneumoniae at >50× MIC. Thorsted et al. reported that endotoxin release over 24 hr was lowest when antimicrobial exposure rapidly eradicated the bacteria and highest when bacterial growth and antimicrobial bacterial activity occurred simultaneously [27]. Therefore, exposure to high concentrations of CEZ and MBFX at an inoculum of 105 CFU/mL likely rapidly reduced viable bacterial counts and minimized LPS release. In K. pneumoniae, the amount of LPS released at 50× MIC and 250× MIC of CEZ was significantly lower than that at 5× MIC, suggesting that exposure to higher concentrations of the antimicrobial agent suppresses LPS release. This is consistent with the study by Thorsted et al. [27], who reported that exposure to cefuroxime at >2× MIC with an initial inoculum of 105 CFU/mL increased endotoxin levels by approximately 1 log fold relative to pre-exposure, and that the control (0 MIC), 0.5× MIC, and 1× MIC conditions resulted in a significant increase in endotoxin release. These results suggest that, at an initial inoculum of 105 CFU/mL, exposure to MBFX at >5× MIC for both E. coli and K. pneumoniae, and exposure to CEZ at >5× MIC for E. coli and >50× MIC for K. pneumoniae, minimizes the amount of LPS released due to bacterial growth and lysis.

Effect of initial inoculum density on LPS release

Previous studies have reported that, under high inoculum conditions, CPFX treatment promotes greater LPS release than under low inoculum conditions [31], suggesting that initial inoculum density influences LPS release. In addition, because the intramammary bacterial load in cows with mastitis varies among cases, differences in bacterial burden within the mammary gland may affect the therapeutic efficacy of antimicrobial treatment. In this study, when the initial inoculum density was adjusted to 108 CFU/mL, the viable counts of both E. coli and K. pneumoniae declined significantly after 4 hr of incubation with MBFX and CEZ at concentrations of 50× MIC and 250× MIC compared with preincubation (0 hr). Conversely, LPS release was comparable to that of the NC group, and no significant difference was observed. A comparison of LPS release at an initial inoculum of 105 CFU/mL or 108 CFU/mL revealed that significantly more LPS was released at 108 CFU/mL than at 105 CFU/mL, regardless of the bacterial species or antimicrobial agent concentration. These results were consistent with those reported by Van Den Berg et al. [31]. The increased release of LPS is considered to be due to the simultaneous growth of bacteria and the bactericidal activity of the antimicrobial agents, as reported by Thorsted et al. [27]. These results suggest that at high initial inoculum densities, although exposure to high antimicrobial concentrations reduces the bacterial count to some extent, the bacteria are not completely eradicated; the amount of LPS released due to bacterial growth and/or lysis is thus greater than at low initial inoculum densities. Furthermore, Nagasawa et al. reported that the coliform bacterial load in milk is associated with the clinical severity score of bovine mastitis, with higher clinical severity scores corresponding to greater bacterial loads [17]. Therefore, initiating antimicrobial therapy at an early stage, when clinical symptoms are mild and the bacterial load in the mammary gland is presumed to be low, is considered important for limiting LPS release. Shinozuka et al. suggested that mammary irrigation regimens may be effective for treating acute E. coli mastitis in cows by eliminating both microbes and LPS, and inhibit LPS production within infected udder tissue [22]. In cases involving high bacterial densities in the udder, combining mammary irrigation regimen with antimicrobial therapy may mitigate the effects of LPS.

Effect of antimicrobial agents on bacterial morphology

Several previous studies have reported a close relationship between antimicrobial-induced endotoxin release and morphological changes in bacteria treated with β-lactam antibacterial agents [8, 9, 28, 33, 34]. These morphological changes depend on the affinity of antimicrobial agents for specific penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs). Since PBP2 plays a role in cell shape maintenance and elongation, its inhibition results in the loss of rod shape, and consequently, spheroidization of bacterial morphology and reduced cell wall destruction. Inhibition of PBP3 also prevents cell wall synthesis, so the bacteria are unable to divide, leading to filamentation and lysis. IPM, a carbapenem antimicrobial, selectively acts against PBP2, thus inhibiting bacterial elongation and causing spheroidization [9, 33]. By contrast, the cephalosporin CAZ acts against PBP3, leading to bacterial filamentation. Because endotoxin is a component of the outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria, the amount of endotoxin released in response to IPM, which induces spheroidization, is low, whereas the amount of endotoxin released in response to CAZ, which induces bacteria to become filamentous, is high. Fluoroquinolones, such as CPFX, induce the SOS response and promote filamentation in E. coli via SulA-mediated inhibition of cell division, a mechanism of morphological change distinct from that of β-lactam agents [1].

In this study, both E. coli and K. pneumoniae exhibited filamentation upon exposure to MBFX, CEZ, and CAZ. However, when the initial inoculum was adjusted to 105 CFU/mL, exposure to MBFX and CEZ resulted in significantly lower LPS release than that in the NC group for E. coli at >5× MIC and for K. pneumoniae at >50× MIC, which differs from previous reports [8, 9, 28, 33, 34]. For CAZ, consistent with previous reports [8, 9, 28, 33, 34], LPS release increased under the 5× MIC and 50× MIC conditions. For IPM, also consistent with previous reports [8, 9, 28, 33, 34], E. coli underwent spheroidization and the amount of LPS released at 50× MIC was low. Regarding the degree of filamentation observed in microscopic images, CAZ induced the greatest cell elongation, followed by CEZ and MBFX, suggesting that CAZ is a stronger inducer of cell elongation and may thereby lead to greater LPS release through enhanced cell wall disruption. However, since the extent of elongation was not quantified in this study, it was not possible to determine the mechanistic basis for the differences in LPS release among the drugs. Furthermore, despite the observation of filamentation with MBFX and CEZ, LPS release was significantly lower than that in the NC group, indicating that factors other than filamentation may also influence LPS release. Although there are few reports on the affinity of CEZ for PBPs, it has been reported that CEZ induces filamentation without inhibiting septum formation [14], which may account for its relatively low level of LPS release. In addition, because MBFX, similar to CPFX, is considered to induce filamentation through a mechanism distinct from that of β-lactam antimicrobials, this difference in mechanism may also account for the lower levels of LPS release observed with MBFX. Further studies are needed to clarify the relationships among PBP affinity, bacterial morphology, and LPS release.

In conclusion, the findings of the present study suggest that, in the treatment of mastitis caused by coliform bacteria, administering antimicrobials at high concentrations early in the course of disease, when clinical symptoms are mild and the bacterial load in the mammary gland is presumably low, may mitigate the adverse effects associated with LPS release. The present study revealed that exposure of E. coli and K. pneumoniae to high concentrations of MBFX and CEZ under low-inoculum conditions reduces the amount of LPS released; however, further studies are required to determine how these findings relate to host pathology and clinical prognosis in cases of bovine mastitis. Furthermore, this study did not investigate the relationship between bacteriological characteristics, such as surface antigens and molecular epidemiological features, and the amount of LPS released. Therefore, future studies utilizing a larger number of isolates will be necessary in order to more precisely characterize how strain-specific factors influence LPS release.
