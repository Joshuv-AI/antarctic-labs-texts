Unexplored Opportunities for Automatic Differentiation in Astrophysics
Marc Bara Iniesta

Abstract
We present a systematic analysis of automatic differentiation (AD) applications in astrophysics, identifying domains where gradient-based optimization could provide significant computational advantages. Building on our previous work with GRAF (Gradient-based Radar Ambiguity Functions), which discovered optimal radar waveforms achieving 4× computational speedup by exploring the trade-off space between conflicting objectives [ 4 ] , we extend this discovery-oriented approach to astrophysical parameter spaces. While AD has been successfully implemented in several areas including gravitational wave parameter estimation and exoplanet atmospheric retrieval, we identify nine astrophysical domains where, to our knowledge, gradient-based exploration methods remain unexplored despite favorable mathematical structure. These opportunities range from discovering novel solutions to the Einstein field equations in exotic spacetime configurations to systematically exploring parameter spaces in stellar astrophysics and planetary dynamics. We present the mathematical foundations for implementing AD in each domain and propose GRASP (Gradient-based Reconstruction of Astrophysical Systems & Phenomena), a unified framework for differentiable astrophysical computations that transforms traditional optimization problems into systematic exploration of solution spaces. To our knowledge, this is the first work to systematically delineate unexplored domains in astrophysics suitable for automatic differentiation and to provide a unified, mathematically grounded framework (GRASP) to guide their implementation.

1.1
From Optimization to Discovery

The key insight from GRAF extends beyond computational efficiency: gradient-based exploration of parameter spaces discovers solutions that elude human intuition. In radar waveform design, the joint optimization of conflicting objectives (detection performance vs. stealth characteristics) revealed Pareto-optimal solutions in regions of parameter space that traditional design approaches never explore. This paradigm shift—from optimizing human-conceived designs to discovering the full landscape of optimal solutions—motivates the extension to astrophysical domains.

In astrophysics, many fundamental problems involve exploring vast parameter spaces subject to physical constraints:

•

What spacetime geometries minimize exotic matter while permitting superluminal motion?

•

Which stellar models simultaneously satisfy asteroseismic frequencies and spectroscopic constraints?

•

What antenna gain configurations optimize interferometric calibration across multiple observing conditions?

These questions require not just optimization of known solutions, but systematic discovery of all possible solutions within the constraints of physics.

1.2
The Power of Automatic Differentiation: From Forward Models to Discovery

The transformative insight underlying both GRAF and GRASP is deceptively simple: if we can compute something, automatic differentiation lets us optimize it through iterative refinement. This principle, familiar from neural network training, extends powerfully to physical systems.

Consider the current workflow in astrophysics when dealing with complex, non-convex optimization landscapes:

1.

Forward model
: Given parameters
\theta
, compute observables (e.g., given stellar mass, compute oscillation frequencies)

2.

Comparison
: Evaluate how well the model matches data or satisfies constraints

3.

Population-based search
: Use genetic algorithms, particle swarm, or similar methods that require hundreds to thousands of forward evaluations to estimate good parameter updates

These methods work but are computationally expensive because they treat the forward model as a black box, learning about the landscape only through repeated sampling.

Automatic differentiation transforms this into an efficient discovery process:

1.

Forward model
: Same as before - compute observables from parameters

2.

Loss function
: Quantify the mismatch or constraint violation as
\mathcal{L}(\theta)

3.

Backward pass
: AD automatically computes
\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}
- the exact direction to improve

4.

Parameter update
:
\theta_{new}=\theta_{old}-\eta\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}

5.

Iterate
: Repeat until convergence

The crucial advantage: while genetic algorithms require hundreds of forward evaluations to estimate a single good update direction, AD provides the exact gradient at a cost comparable to just one or two forward passes. This represents a 100-1000× reduction in computational cost per iteration.

This efficiency enables a paradigm shift. Rather than being limited by computational budget to explore only small regions of parameter space, we can:

•

Start from many random initializations to explore diverse regions efficiently

•

Navigate through million-dimensional parameter spaces (e.g., neural network weights)

•

Discover solutions in regions traditional methods never reach due to computational constraints

•

Find optimal trade-offs between competing objectives in minutes instead of days

Just as gradient descent in neural networks discovers feature representations no human would design, gradient-based exploration in physics discovers configurations no scientist would conceive—yet they satisfy all physical laws and optimize our objectives.

1.3
Technical Foundations of Automatic Differentiation

While the discovery paradigm drives our approach, we briefly review the mathematical machinery that makes it possible. AD computes exact derivatives by decomposing functions into elementary operations and applying the chain rule systematically.

Forward Mode
: For a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
, forward mode computes directional derivatives:

\dot{y}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\dot{x}

(1)

where
\dot{x}
is a seed vector. This is efficient when
n\ll m
.

Reverse Mode
: More relevant for optimization where typically
m=1
(scalar loss), reverse mode computes:

\bar{x}=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)^{T}\bar{y}

(2)

where
\bar{y}
is the adjoint. This requires one forward pass and one backward pass, regardless of
n
.

Complex Differentiation
: For complex-valued functions, the Wirtinger derivatives provide:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}-i\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right),\quad\frac{\partial f}{\partial z^{*}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}+i\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right)

(3)

where
z=x+iy
. For holomorphic functions,
\frac{\partial f}{\partial z^{*}}=0
, recovering standard complex differentiation. This formalism is essential for radio interferometry and other complex-valued problems where functions are generally not holomorphic.

2
Mathematical Framework for Astrophysical Applications

2.1
General Discovery Framework

Most astrophysical problems can be formulated as exploration of solution spaces subject to constraints:

\mathcal{S}^{*}=\{\theta:\mathcal{C}(\theta)=0,\mathcal{L}(\theta)\leq\mathcal{L}_{max}\}

(4)

where
\mathcal{S}^{*}
represents the set of all valid solutions,
\mathcal{C}(\theta)
represents physical constraints (e.g., Einstein equations, conservation laws), and
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
represents objectives to minimize (e.g., exotic matter, fitting residuals).

The discovery process involves:

1.

Exploration
: Systematically sampling the parameter space
\theta

2.

Constraint satisfaction
: Ensuring physical validity through differentiable constraints

3.

Pareto frontier identification
: Finding trade-offs between competing objectives

4.

Solution characterization
: Identifying qualitatively different solution families

This transforms single-point optimization into comprehensive mapping of solution landscapes.

2.2
Key Differentiable Operations in Astrophysics

Several mathematical operations appear repeatedly across astrophysical applications:

Integration
: Many forward models involve integrals

I(\theta)=\int_{\Omega}f(x;\theta)dx

(5)

These can be differentiated using Leibniz’s rule when the integrand is smooth.

Differential Equations
: Stellar structure, orbital dynamics, and field evolution often involve ODEs/PDEs:

\frac{dy}{dt}=f(y,t;\theta),\quad y(0)=y_{0}(\theta)

(6)

The sensitivity equations or adjoint methods provide gradients.

Fourier Transforms
: Essential for interferometry and time series analysis:

\tilde{f}(k)=\int f(x)e^{-ikx}dx

(7)

The FFT is differentiable, enabling end-to-end optimization.

Matrix Operations
: From Jones calculus to metric tensors:

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}(\theta)\mathbf{x},\quad\text{where }\frac{\partial\mathbf{y}}{\partial\theta}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial\theta}\mathbf{x}

(8)

Unexplored Applications for Automatic Differentiation

We now present nine astrophysical domains where, to our knowledge, automatic differentiation has not been applied for systematic exploration of solution spaces. For each application, we demonstrate how gradient-based discovery can reveal solutions beyond human intuition.

3.1
General Relativistic Applications

The Einstein field equations and related spacetime metrics constitute inherently differentiable systems, yet optimization approaches for exotic solutions remain largely unexplored.

3.1.1
Alcubierre Metric Optimization

The Alcubierre metric
[
11
]
represents a solution to the Einstein field equations permitting apparent faster-than-light travel through spacetime distortion:

ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+[dx-v_{s}f(r_{s})dt]^{2}+dy^{2}+dz^{2}

(9)

where
f(r_{s})
is the shape function and
r_{s}=[(x-x_{s}(t))^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}]^{1/2}
.

The stress-energy tensor components require:

T^{00}=\frac{v_{s}^{2}}{8\pi}\left[\frac{(x-x_{s})^{2}}{r_{s}^{2}}\left(\frac{df}{dr_{s}}\right)^{2}+f^{2}\frac{d^{2}f}{dr_{s}^{2}}\right]

(10)

This expression assumes a static bubble configuration where
\dot{x}_{s}=0
. For a moving bubble, additional terms arise from the time dependence of
r_{s}
through
x_{s}(t)
. In our optimization framework, we consider static snapshots where the bubble position is fixed during each gradient computation, making the above expression valid.

Discovery Problem
: Explore the landscape of all spacetime geometries that satisfy Einstein’s equations while minimizing
\int|T^{\mu\nu}|dV
, subject to boundary conditions
f(0)=1
,
f(\infty)=0
.

Differentiable Formulation

We parameterize the shape function using a neural network or smooth basis functions to ensure differentiability:

f(r_{s};\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}\phi_{k}(r_{s})

(11)

where
\phi_{k}
are basis functions (e.g., radial basis functions, B-splines) and
\theta
are learnable parameters.

For neural parameterization:

f(r_{s};\theta)=\sigma(W_{L}\cdots\sigma(W_{2}\sigma(W_{1}r_{s}+b_{1})+b_{2})\cdots+b_{L})

(12)

where
\sigma
is a smooth activation function and
\theta=\{W_{i},b_{i}\}
. Unlike traditional analytical forms that constrain the search to human-conceived geometries, neural parameterization enables the discovery of novel spacetime configurations that may violate geometric intuition while better satisfying the physical constraints.

Gradient Computation

The key challenge is computing gradients through the stress-energy tensor. We decompose the computation:

1.

Shape function derivatives
: For the basis function parameterization:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial r_{s}}=\sum_{k}\theta_{k}\frac{\partial\phi_{k}}{\partial r_{s}},\quad\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial r_{s}^{2}}=\sum_{k}\theta_{k}\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{k}}{\partial r_{s}^{2}}

(13)

For neural network parameterization, we compute these derivatives directly through automatic differentiation of
f(r_{s};\theta)
.

2.

Stress-energy components
: Each component is differentiable:

\frac{\partial T^{00}}{\partial\theta}=\frac{v_{s}^{2}}{8\pi}\left[\frac{(x-x_{s})^{2}}{r_{s}^{2}}\cdot 2\frac{df}{dr_{s}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\frac{df}{dr_{s}}\right)+2f\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\frac{d^{2}f}{dr_{s}^{2}}+f^{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\frac{d^{2}f}{dr_{s}^{2}}\right)\right]

(14)

3.

Volume integral
: We discretize on a spatial grid. For simplicity, we use Cartesian coordinates with uniform spacing:

E[\theta]=\sum_{i,j,k}|T^{\mu\nu}(x_{i},y_{j},z_{k};\theta)|\Delta x\Delta y\Delta z

(15)

Note: For the logarithmic radial spacing mentioned in Eq. 16, appropriate volume weights would be needed.

Numerical Considerations

•

Spatial discretization
: The grid must extend sufficiently far to capture the asymptotic behavior. We use logarithmic spacing near the origin:

r_{i}=r_{\min}\exp\left(i\frac{\log(r_{\max}/r_{\min})}{N_{r}}\right)

(16)

•

Boundary conditions
: Enforce constraints using penalty terms:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{boundary}}=\lambda_{1}|f(0)-1|^{2}+\lambda_{2}|f(r_{\max})|^{2}+\lambda_{3}\int_{0}^{r_{\max}}\max(0,-f(r))^{2}dr

(17)

•

Gradient stability
: The term
(x-x_{s})^{2}/r_{s}^{2}
can cause numerical issues near
r_{s}=0
. We regularize:

\frac{(x-x_{s})^{2}}{r_{s}^{2}}\rightarrow\frac{(x-x_{s})^{2}}{r_{s}^{2}+\epsilon}

(18)

with
\epsilon\sim 10^{-4}
for single-precision (float32) or
\epsilon\sim 10^{-8}
for double-precision (float64) computations.

•

Efficient implementation
: Vectorize all operations over the spatial grid:

# Pseudocode
r_s = torch.sqrt(X**2 + Y**2 + Z**2 + eps)
f = shape_network(r_s)
df_dr = torch.autograd.grad(f, r_s, create_graph=True)[0]
d2f_dr2 = torch.autograd.grad(df_dr, r_s, create_graph=True)[0]
T_00 = compute_stress_energy(f, df_dr, d2f_dr2, v_s)
loss = torch.sum(torch.abs(T_00)) * dx * dy * dz

Geometry Space Exploration

The neural parameterization transforms the problem from optimizing a single predetermined geometry to systematically exploring the entire landscape of physically valid spacetime configurations. We can implement several exploration strategies:

Multi-start optimization
: Initialize multiple neural networks with different random weights to explore diverse regions of geometry space:

\{\theta_{i}^{*}\}=\{\arg\min_{\theta_{i}}E[\theta_{i}]\},\quad i=1,...,N_{starts}

(19)

Geometry generation pipeline
: Train a generative model to produce diverse initial geometries:

\theta_{init}\sim p_{\text{generator}}(\theta|\text{constraints})

(20)

Population-based exploration
: Combine gradient descent with evolutionary strategies to maintain diversity while optimizing:

\theta_{i}^{(t+1)}=\theta_{i}^{(t)}-\eta\nabla E[\theta_{i}^{(t)}]+\epsilon_{i}^{(t)}

(21)

where
\epsilon_{i}^{(t)}
introduces stochastic exploration.

This approach enables discovery of multiple solution families, characterization of the Pareto frontier between competing objectives (e.g., exotic matter vs. bubble size), and identification of qualitatively different geometric configurations that satisfy the same physical constraints. The framework transforms spacetime engineering from a problem of optimizing human-conceived designs to one of discovering the full spectrum of physically realizable geometries.

Cosmic String Network Evolution

Cosmic strings
[
12
]
evolve according to the Nambu-Goto action:

S=-\mu\int d^{2}\sigma\sqrt{-\det(\gamma_{ab})},\quad\gamma_{ab}=\partial_{a}X^{\mu}\partial_{b}X_{\mu},

(22)

where
X^{\mu}(\sigma,\tau)
describes the string worldsheet in spacetime coordinates
\mu=0,1,2,3
and
\sigma^{a}=(\sigma,\tau)
are worldsheet coordinates.

The equations of motion derived from (24) are:

\partial_{a}\left(\sqrt{-\gamma}\gamma^{ab}\partial_{b}X^{\mu}\right)=0,

(23)

with
\gamma=\det(\gamma_{ab})
and
\gamma^{ab}
the inverse induced metric.

Discovery Problem
: Given observational constraints on the stochastic gravitational wave background
\Omega_{GW}(f)
, find the string network parameters
\theta=(\mu,p_{reconnect},\xi_{initial})
that minimize:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\int\left|\Omega_{GW}^{\text{model}}(f;\theta)-\Omega_{GW}^{\text{obs}}(f)\right|^{2}df+\lambda\mathcal{R}(\theta),

(24)

where
\mathcal{R}(\theta)
penalizes unphysical configurations (e.g., negative string tensions).

Differentiable Formulation
:

•

Worldsheet Parameterization
: Represent
X^{\mu}(\sigma,\tau;\theta)
as a spectral decomposition:

X^{\mu}(\sigma,\tau;\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}^{\mu}\phi_{k}(\sigma,\tau),

(25)

where
\phi_{k}
are Fourier basis functions for periodic strings or B-splines for non-periodic segments.

•

Gradient Computation
: The adjoint method computes gradients through the PDE constraint (
23

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta}=\int\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial X^{\mu}}\frac{\partial X^{\mu}}{\partial\theta}d^{2}\sigma+\text{adjoint terms},

(26)

with the adjoint field
\lambda^{\mu}(\sigma,\tau)
satisfying:

\partial_{a}\left(\sqrt{-\gamma}\gamma^{ab}\partial_{b}\lambda^{\mu}\right)=-\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}}{\delta X^{\mu}}.

(27)

(30)

z=0

\frac{d^{2}\phi}{dz^{2}}=\lambda\phi(\phi^{2}-\eta^{2}),\quad\phi(-\infty)=-\eta,\ \phi(+\infty)=+\eta.

(31)

Discovery Problem
: Find field configurations
\phi(z;\theta)
that minimize the total energy

E[\theta]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{d\phi}{dz}\right)^{2}+V(\phi)\right]dz,

(32)

[
21
]

Parameterize
\phi(z;\theta)
using a spectral decomposition:

\phi(z;\theta)=\eta\tanh\left(\frac{z}{\delta_{0}}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}\psi_{k}(z),

(33)

where
\delta_{0}=\sqrt{2/\lambda}\eta^{-1}
and
\psi_{k}(z)
are compactly supported basis functions (e.g., B-splines).

The adjoint field
\xi(z)
for gradient computation satisfies:

\frac{d^{2}\xi}{dz^{2}}-\lambda(3\phi^{2}-\eta^{2})\xi=\frac{\delta E}{\delta\phi},\quad\xi(\pm\infty)=0.

(34)

3.1.4
False Vacuum Decay and Bubble Nucleation

The nucleation rate of bubbles in a metastable vacuum
[
14
]
is determined by the bounce solution minimizing the Euclidean action:

S_{E}=2\pi^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dr\,r^{3}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{d\phi}{dr}\right)^{2}+V(\phi)\right]

(44)

The bounce equation derived from
\delta S_{E}=0
is:

\frac{d^{2}\phi}{dr^{2}}+\frac{3}{r}\frac{d\phi}{dr}=\frac{dV}{d\phi}

(45)

with boundary conditions
\phi(0)=\phi_{\text{top}}
(field value at the potential barrier) and
\phi(\infty)=\phi_{\text{false}}
(false vacuum state).

Discovery Problem
: Find all field configurations
\phi(r)
that minimize
S_{E}
while satisfying quantum tunneling constraints, determining the decay rate
\Gamma\sim e^{-S_{E}}
.

Differentiable Formulation

We parameterize the bounce solution using a neural network with radial symmetry:

\phi(r;\theta)=\phi_{\text{false}}+(\phi_{\text{top}}-\phi_{\text{false}})\sigma(W_{L}[...\sigma(W_{1}r+b_{1})...]+b_{L})

(46)

where
\sigma
is a smooth activation function (tanh) and
\theta=\{W_{i},b_{i}\}
are learnable parameters. This automatically satisfies
\phi(0)\approx\phi_{\text{top}}
through initialization and
\phi(\infty)=\phi_{\text{false}}
via activation function asymptotics.

Gradient Computation

The AD workflow requires:

1.

Forward solution of the ODE (Eq. 2) using the parameterized
\phi(r;\theta)

2.

Evaluation of
S_{E}[\theta]
via adaptive quadrature

3.

Backpropagation through the ODE solver using adjoint methods

4.

Constraint enforcement via penalty terms:

\mathcal{L}=S_{E}[\theta]+\lambda_{1}|\phi(0)-\phi_{\text{top}}|^{2}+\lambda_{2}|\phi(R_{\max})-\phi_{\text{false}}|^{2}

(47)

3.2
Radio Astronomy and Interferometry

Radio interferometry fundamentally involves complex-valued measurements and calibration challenges that are naturally suited to gradient-based optimization, yet current methods rely on alternating least squares or stochastic sampling without exploiting gradient information.

3.2.1
Interferometric Calibration

Radio interferometers measure complex visibilities that encode spatial Fourier components of the sky brightness distribution. The measurement process introduces complex gains that must be calibrated to recover accurate astronomical information
[
15
]
.

The measurement equation for baseline
ij
is:

V_{ij}^{obs}(t,\nu)=g_{i}(t,\nu)g_{j}^{*}(t,\nu)V_{ij}^{true}(t,\nu)+n_{ij}(t,\nu)

(54)

where
g_{i}
are complex antenna gains and
n_{ij}
is thermal noise.

For direction-dependent effects (ionosphere, primary beam), the equation becomes:

V_{ij}^{obs}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{dir}}\mathbf{J}_{i}^{k}\mathbf{V}_{ij}^{k}\mathbf{J}_{j}^{k\dagger}+\mathbf{n}_{ij}

(55)

where
\mathbf{J}_{i}^{k}
are
2\times 2
Jones matrices and
\mathbf{V}_{ij}^{k}
are visibility coherency matrices.

Discovery Problem
: Explore the space of calibration solutions that minimize:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\sum_{ij,t,\nu}\frac{|V_{ij}^{obs}-\mathcal{M}(V_{ij}^{model};\theta)|^{2}}{\sigma_{ij}^{2}}+\mathcal{R}(\theta)

(56)

where
\theta=\{g_{i},\phi_{ion},\mathbf{J}_{i}^{k}\}
includes all calibration parameters and
\mathcal{R}
enforces physical constraints.

Differentiable Formulation

We parameterize the complex gains and ionospheric phases using smooth functions:

g_{i}(t,\nu)=a_{i}(t,\nu)\exp(i\phi_{i}(t,\nu))

(57)

where amplitudes and phases are parameterized as:

a_{i}(t,\nu)=\exp\left(\sum_{k=0}^{K_{a}}\theta_{a,k}^{i}B_{k}(t)P_{k}(\nu)\right)

(58)

\phi_{i}(t,\nu)=\sum_{k=0}^{K_{\phi}}\theta_{\phi,k}^{i}B_{k}(t)P_{k}(\nu)+\phi_{ion}^{i}(t)

(59)

with
B_{k}
being B-spline basis functions in time and
P_{k}
polynomials in frequency.

The ionospheric contribution is modeled as a 2D phase screen:

\phi_{ion}^{i}(t)=\frac{8.45\times 10^{9}}{\nu}\int_{LOS_{i}}n_{e}(x,y,z,t)dl

(60)

We parameterize the electron density using a neural network:

n_{e}(x,y,z,t;\theta_{ion})=\text{NN}_{\theta_{ion}}(x,y,z,t)

(61)

Gradient Computation

The complex-valued nature requires Wirtinger derivatives:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial g_{i}}=\sum_{j}\frac{g_{j}^{*}V_{ij}^{model}(V_{ij}^{obs}-g_{i}g_{j}^{*}V_{ij}^{model})^{*}}{\sigma_{ij}^{2}}

(62)

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial g_{i}^{*}}=\sum_{j}\frac{g_{j}V_{ij}^{model*}(V_{ij}^{obs}-g_{i}g_{j}^{*}V_{ij}^{model})}{\sigma_{ij}^{2}}

(63)

For the ionospheric screen, we use the chain rule:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta_{ion}}=\sum_{i}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\phi_{ion}^{i}}\frac{\partial\phi_{ion}^{i}}{\partial n_{e}}\frac{\partial n_{e}}{\partial\theta_{ion}}

(64)

3.2.2
Black Hole Image Reconstruction

Very Long Baseline Interferometry (VLBI) observations of black holes
[
16
]
present an extreme sparse sampling problem in the Fourier domain. The Event Horizon Telescope’s success relied on regularized maximum likelihood methods that could benefit from gradient-based exploration of the image and hyperparameter space.

The forward model relates the image
I(x,y)
to observed visibilities:

V(u,v)=\int\int I(x,y)\exp[-2\pi i(ux+vy)]dxdy

(71)

where
(u,v)

For black hole imaging, relativistic effects modify the relationship:

I_{obs}(\alpha,\beta)=\int_{\gamma}j_{\nu}g^{3}e^{-\tau_{\nu}}d\lambda

(72)

where the integral follows null geodesics
\gamma
in the Kerr metric,
j_{\nu}
is the emission coefficient,

is the gravitational redshift factor, and
\tau_{\nu}
is the optical depth.

Discovery Problem
: Explore the space of physically valid black hole images that satisfy:

\mathcal{L}(I,\lambda)=\chi^{2}_{data}(I)+\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}\mathcal{R}_{k}(I)

(73)

where
\mathcal{R}_{k}
are regularizers encoding physical priors.

Differentiable Formulation

We parameterize the image using a continuous representation:

I(x,y;\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_{k}\phi_{k}(x,y)+I_{NN}(x,y;\theta_{NN})

(74)

where
\phi_{k}
are fixed basis functions (e.g., shapelets, wavelets) and

is a neural network component for capturing fine structure.

The data fidelity term with closure quantities:

\chi^{2}_{data}=\chi^{2}_{vis}+\chi^{2}_{amp}+\chi^{2}_{cphase}+\chi^{2}_{logcamp}

For closure phases (which cancel station-based errors):

\chi^{2}_{cphase}=\sum_{ijk}\frac{|\arg(V_{ij}V_{jk}V_{ki})-\arg(\tilde{V}_{ij}\tilde{V}_{jk}\tilde{V}_{ki})|^{2}}{\sigma_{cphase}^{2}}

Gradient Computation

The key challenge is differentiating through the Fourier transform and closure quantities:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial I}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\tilde{V}}\right]

2.

\frac{\partial\chi^{2}_{cphase}}{\partial V_{ij}}=\sum_{k}\frac{2(\Delta\phi_{ijk})}{\sigma_{ijk}^{2}}\frac{\partial\arg(V_{ij}V_{jk}V_{ki})}{\partial V_{ij}}

\frac{\partial\arg(z)}{\partial z}=\frac{1}{2i}\left(\frac{1}{z}-\frac{1}{z^{*}}\right)

\frac{d^{2}x^{\mu}}{d\lambda^{2}}+\Gamma^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}\frac{dx^{\alpha}}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^{\beta}}{d\lambda}=0

3.3
Stellar and Planetary Astrophysics

Stellar and planetary systems present optimization problems spanning vast scales—from stellar cores where nuclear fusion occurs to planetary surfaces where thermal forces alter orbits. Despite smooth parameter dependencies and well-posed forward models, these domains remain largely unexplored by gradient-based methods.

3.3.1
Asteroseismic Inversion

Stars pulsate in global oscillation modes that probe their internal structure
[
17
]
. These acoustic and gravity waves satisfy the stellar pulsation equations, with frequencies encoding information about density stratification, sound speed profiles, and rotation. Current inversion methods rely on grid searches or genetic algorithms without exploiting gradient information.

The stellar oscillation equations in the adiabatic approximation are:

\frac{d\xi_{r}}{dr}=\left(\frac{2}{r}-\frac{1}{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{d\ln p}{dr}\right)\xi_{r}+\frac{1}{\Gamma_{1}p}\left(\frac{S_{\ell}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}-1\right)P^{\prime}

(88)

\frac{dP^{\prime}}{dr}=\rho\left(\omega^{2}-N^{2}\right)\xi_{r}+\frac{d\ln p}{dr}P^{\prime}

(89)

where
\xi_{r}
is the radial displacement,
P^{\prime}
is the Eulerian pressure perturbation,
S_{\ell}
is the Lamb frequency, and
N
is the Brunt-Väisälä frequency.

The eigenvalue problem
\mathcal{L}\xi=\omega^{2}\xi
yields discrete frequencies
\{\omega_{n\ell}\}
that depend on stellar structure parameters
\theta=\{M,R,Y_{0},Z,\alpha_{MLT},f_{ov},...\}
.

Discovery Problem
: Given observed frequencies
\nu_{n\ell}^{obs}
with uncertainties
\sigma_{n\ell}
, explore the space of stellar models that minimize:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\sum_{n,\ell}\frac{(\nu_{n\ell}^{obs}-\nu_{n\ell}^{model}(\theta))^{2}}{\sigma_{n\ell}^{2}}+\sum_{k}\lambda_{k}\mathcal{R}_{k}(\theta)

(90)

where
\mathcal{R}_{k}
encode constraints from spectroscopy, evolutionary tracks, and physical consistency.

Differentiable Formulation

The key challenge is differentiating through the eigenvalue problem. We use the Hellmann-Feynman theorem:

\frac{\partial\omega_{n\ell}^{2}}{\partial\theta_{i}}=\langle\xi_{n\ell}|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta_{i}}|\xi_{n\ell}\rangle

(91)

where
|\xi_{n\ell}\rangle
is the normalized eigenfunction.

For the stellar structure, we solve the equations of hydrostatic equilibrium:

\frac{dm}{dr}=4\pi r^{2}\rho,\quad\frac{dP}{dr}=-\frac{Gm\rho}{r^{2}},\quad\frac{dL}{dr}=4\pi r^{2}\rho\epsilon

(92)

\frac{dT}{dr}=-\frac{3\kappa\rho L}{16\pi acT^{3}r^{2}}\quad\text{(radiative)},\quad\frac{dT}{dr}=\left(1-\frac{1}{\gamma}\right)\frac{T}{P}\frac{dP}{dr}\quad\text{(convective)}

(93)

These ODEs are differentiable, allowing gradients via the adjoint method:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\theta}=\int_{0}^{R}\lambda^{T}(r)\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}(y(r),r,\theta)dr

(94)

where
\lambda(r)
is the adjoint variable satisfying a backward ODE.

Gradient Computation

We decompose the gradient calculation into three stages:

1.

Structure Derivatives
: For parameter
\theta_{i}
, solve the linearized structure equations:

\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial\theta_{i}}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta_{i}}

(95)

where
y=(m,P,L,T)^{T}
and
f
represents the RHS of the structure equations.

2.

Eigenvalue Derivatives
: With perturbed structure, compute:

\frac{\partial\omega_{n\ell}^{2}}{\partial\theta_{i}}=\int_{0}^{R}\xi_{r}^{2}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}(\rho\omega^{2}-A)\right]dr

(96)

where
A
contains the coefficient functions in the pulsation equations.

3.

Surface Correction
: Account for near-surface errors:

\nu_{n\ell}^{corrected}=\nu_{n\ell}^{model}+a_{0}\left(\frac{\nu_{n\ell}}{\nu_{max}}\right)^{a_{1}}

(97)

with
a_{0},a_{1}
as additional parameters.

3.3.2

The radial velocity method
[
18
]
detects exoplanets through stellar wobble induced by gravitational interaction. Despite being mathematically smooth with well-defined derivatives, current analyses use Markov Chain Monte Carlo or genetic algorithms without gradient exploitation.

The stellar radial velocity due to

v_{r}(t)=\sum_{p=1}^{N_{p}}K_{p}[\cos(f_{p}(t)+\omega_{p})+e_{p}\cos(\omega_{p})]+v_{sys}+v_{inst}(t)

(104)

where
K_{p}
is the semi-amplitude,
f_{p}
is the true anomaly,
\omega_{p}
is the argument of periastron, and
e_{p}
is eccentricity.

The true anomaly relates to time through Kepler’s equation:

M=E-e\sin E,\quad M=\frac{2\pi}{P}(t-T_{0})

(105)

where
M
is mean anomaly and
E
is eccentric anomaly.

Discovery Problem
: Explore the parameter space
\theta=\{K_{p},P_{p},e_{p},\omega_{p},T_{0,p}\}_{p=1}^{N_{p}}
that minimizes:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{obs}}\frac{(v_{r}^{obs}(t_{i})-v_{r}^{model}(t_{i};\theta))^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}+\mathcal{R}(\theta)

(106)

discovering both the number of planets and their orbital configurations.

Differentiable Formulation

The key challenge is differentiating through Kepler’s equation. We solve it iteratively:

E_{n+1}=M+e\sin E_{n}

(107)

converging to tolerance
|E_{n+1}-E_{n}|<\epsilon
.

For gradients, we use implicit differentiation. At convergence,
E
satisfies:

g(E,M,e)=E-e\sin E-M=0

(108)

By the implicit function theorem:

\frac{\partial E}{\partial M}=\frac{1}{1-e\cos E},\quad\frac{\partial E}{\partial e}=\frac{\sin E}{1-e\cos E}

(109)

The true anomaly is:

f=2\arctan\left(\sqrt{\frac{1+e}{1-e}}\tan\frac{E}{2}\right)

(110)

with derivatives following from the chain rule.

Gradient Computation

For each orbital parameter, we trace gradients through the complete model:

1.

Period Gradient

\frac{\partial v_{r}}{\partial P}=K\frac{\partial}{\partial P}[\cos(f+\omega)]=-K\sin(f+\omega)\frac{\partial f}{\partial P}

(111)

where:

\frac{\partial f}{\partial P}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial E}\frac{\partial E}{\partial M}\frac{\partial M}{\partial P}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial E}\frac{1}{1-e\cos E}\frac{-2\pi(t-T_{0})}{P^{2}}

(112)

2.

Eccentricity Gradient
: More complex due to appearance in multiple terms:

\frac{\partial v_{r}}{\partial e}=K\cos\omega+K\frac{\partial}{\partial e}[\cos(f+\omega)]

(113)

3.

Multi-Planet Coupling
: Account for gravitational interactions:

\mathcal{L}_{stability}=\sum_{i<j}\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta a_{ij}}{R_{Hill}}\right)

(114)

GRASP’s power lies in its ability to discover rather than merely optimize. Modern AD frameworks provide the infrastructure to discover not just local optima, but the full spectrum of physically permitted solutions. This establishes a new paradigm: instead of relying on initial guesses or incremental refinements, GRASP systematically discovers what the universe fundamentally permits. While GRASP currently provides a theoretical scaffolding, its primary contribution is establishing the viability and urgency of gradient-based discovery as a new computational paradigm in astrophysics.
