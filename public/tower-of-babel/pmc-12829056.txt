A pilot study of the plasma and urinary neurotransmitters in Chinese children with autism spectrum disorder
Authors: Yingying Xin, Yifan Yang, Qiaoqiao Qian, Ting Xia, Wanqing Zhang, Taoying Dai, Ruizhen Li, Zhisheng Liu, Chuanjie Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent studies have indicated that there are imbalances in neurotransmitter levels in children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Alterations in peripheral blood and urinary neurotransmitter levels may serve as biomarkers for ASD.MethodsThis study aims to explore the relationship between neurotransmitter levels and the severity of ASD. Totally, forty sex-, age-, and ethnically matched typically developing (TD) children were recruited to compare the neurotransmitter levels with ASD aged over 3 years. Differences in plasma and urinary neurotransmitter levels between TD children and children with ASD were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. For the indicators that showed significant differences, further multivariate regression analysis was conducted to assess the association between individual neurotransmitters and Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition (ADOS-2) Calibrated Severity Score (CSS). Finally, a restricted cubic spline (RCS) model was applied to explore the dose-response relationship between neurotransmitter concentrations and ADOS-2 CSS.ResultsPlasma levels of dopamine (DA), normetanephrine (NMN), 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), γ-aminobutyric acid (γ-GABA) levels in ASD were significantly higher than those in TD children while plasma levels of norepinephrine (NE), tyrosine (Tyr) were significantly lower. Urinary levels of NMN were significantly lower, while epinephrine (E), NE, vanillylmandelic acid (VMA), homovanillic acid (HVA) were significantly higher in children with ASD compared to TD. After adjusting for confounders of age, sex and BMI, plasma GABA was negatively associated with ADOS-2 CSS, while urinary NE and NMN were positively associated with ADOS severity scores (P ＜ 0.05). Multiple regression analysis indicated that plasma GABA and urinary NE and NMN levels explained 26.9%, 24.8% and 15.8% of the variability in ADOS-2 CSS for ASD. The RCS analysis results were largely consistent with the linear regression analysis results. Plasma GABA and urinary NE, and NMN exhibited a linear correlation with ADOS-2 CSS. Plasma NE and Tyr showed a U-shaped curve relationship with the ADOS-2 CSS (P< 0.05).ConclusionsA significant negative correlation was observed between plasma GABA neurotransmitter concentrations and ASD severity. In contrast, NE and NMN levels in urine showed significantly positive correlations with ASD severity. Both excessively low and excessively high plasma NE and Tyr levels may be associated with more severe autism symptoms, suggesting a disruption in peripheral neurotransmitter levels in children with ASD. Future studies with larger sample sizes are required for prediction and validation. The peripheral neurotransmitters may serve as potential auxiliary biomarkers for assessing the severity of autism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07682-7.Keywords: Autism spectrum disorder, Neurotransmitter, Autism diagnostic observation schedule, Calibrated severity score

Background

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is an early-onset neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by persistent deficiency of social communication and interaction, repetitive, and stereotypical behaviors [1]. The overall prevalence of ASD in Chinese children is estimated at approximately 1% [2], with a male-to-female ratio of 3–5:1 [3]. The prevalence of ASD has progressively increased, and early identification and diagnosis are crucial for improving prognosis [4]. However, the pathogenesis of ASD remains poorly understood. Recent research considers ASD as a complex and multifactorial disease involving environment, heredity, disturbance of gut microbiota, neuroimmunology or oxidation, immunological factors and so on [5]. However, clinical diagnosis primarily relies on behavioral observations and Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS) assessments, lacking objective, quantifiable biological markers. Research on the association between peripheral neurotransmitter levels and autism severity is relatively scarce. This study is designed to explore a non-invasive, potential biological tool that can aid in assessing the severity of ASD symptoms.

There has been growing interest in finding disrupted metabolism of neurotransmitters in ASD. Glutamic Acid (Glu) and γ-aminobutyric acid (γ-GABA) are the primary excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters in the brain, respectively. The homeostasis and balance between them are crucial for the function of the central nervous system (CNS). An imbalance between glutamatergic and GABAergic systems has been proposed as a factor contributing to the behavioral characteristics of ASD [6, 7]. Al-Otaish H et al. [8] found significant elevations in plasma GABA levels and glutamate (Glu)/glutamine ratio, as well as significantly lower levels of plasma glutamine and Glu/GABA ratios compared to TD children. Although Glu cannot cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB), it has been reported that Glu levels in the blood and CNS are closely correlated [9]. Thus, previous research has proposed that the plasma levels of Glu could reflect the Glu levels in the brain to some extent. Aldred et al. [10] found that the plasma Glu levels in ASD and their parents was increased.

Compelling evidence implicates dysregulation of the neuroendocrine and stress response systems in ASD. Monoamine neurotransmitters primarily include catecholamines and 5-HT (indoleamine). Catecholamines, acting as central mediators of these systems, offer a direct window into physiological stress status via their peripheral plasma concentrations [11]. This may provide a biological basis for explaining the anxiety, emotional distress, and behavioral issues frequently observed in children with ASD. However, findings from previous research on catecholamine neurotransmitters have been inconsistent and the evidence was limited. An early study from 1987 reported that some children with autism had significantly elevated plasma levels of adrenaline and noradrenaline, suggesting potential overactivation of the stress response system in children with ASD [12]. In contrast, the levels of NE, E, and DA were decreased in platelets. Recently, data analysis revealed higher urinary levels of DA and its metabolite HVA in ASD compared with TD children, probably due to the reduced dopamine β-hydroxylase enzyme activity [13]. Consequently, urinary levels of NE, E and VMA were decreased due to less biotransformation of DA to NE.

Although the BBB restricts the free passage of certain substances, the “brain-gut axis” provides multiple communication pathways. Measuring neurotransmitter levels in peripheral blood and urine helps in understanding the functional state of the overall “brain-gut axis” system and serves as a surrogate indicator for reflecting the function of central neural circuits [14]. Compared to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) collection, blood and urine detection is relatively non-invasive for children. Therefore, this study measured the levels of monoamine neurotransmitters and amino acid neurotransmitters in peripheral plasma and urine, in an attempt to explore their correlation with the severity of autism symptoms and to identify biomarkers for supplementary assessment of symptom severity.

Methods

Ethical approval of the study protocol

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuhan Children’s Hospital, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (No:2024R017–E01). Informed consent was obtained from the guardians of the children involved in this study, which was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Ethical Review Methods for Biomedical Research involving Humans adopted by the National Health and Family Planning Commission of the People’s Republic of China. This research was registered in China National Medical Research Registration and Filing Information System, the Clinical Trial Number is MR-42–24-023506.

Experimental subjects and typically developing children

In this prospective study, a total of 40 children with ASD and 40 typically developing (TD) children were enrolled. The children with ASD were recruited from outpatient Child Health department of Wuhan Children’s Hospital between August 1, 2023, and September 2024. All subjects were over 3 years and under 8 years old (Given that TD children who undergo routine physical check-ups are typically of preschool or school age), the children with ASD enrolled in this study were all older than 3 years. All enrolled ASD children underwent a series of examinations, including diagnostic hearing tests, blood amino acid analysis, electroencephalogram (EEG), cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), thyroid function tests, and chromosomal analysis, to rule out hearing impairment, epilepsy, hypothyroidism, inherited metabolic diseases, and other organic disorders. ASD diagnosis excluded cases such as global developmental delay, intellectual disability, developmental language disorder, reactive attachment disorder and the known syndromic autism. Additionally, assessments using the Gesell Developmental Schedules, the Wechsler Intelligence Scale and Achenbach Child Behavior Checklist or the Conners Rating Scale were completed to evaluate the presence of any comorbidities.

TD children aged 3 to 8 years were recruited from the health check-up children. The final group of TD children was randomly selected from the health check-up children and matched 1:1 to the ASD group based on gender and age. TD children Inclusion criteria: ① children aged between 3 and 8 years; ② Chinese nationality; ③ normal hearing and vision; ④ all domains of the Chinese Developmental Scale for Children Aged 0–6 Years were above 85 (CDSC), the assessments were administered by nurses certified in using CDSC. Exclusion criteria: ① tumor; ② acute infectious diseases; ③ developmental delay, intellectual disability, epilepsy, and other neurological or psychiatric disorders, as well as a history of drug administration within the past two weeks. The TD children group was fully matched to the ASD group in terms of age and sex. Inclusion criteria for children with ASD: ① adherence to the diagnostic criteria outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th edition (DSM-5) [1], and diagnosis by at least two deputy chief physicians; ② the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition (ADOS-2) was conducted by one professional reviewer, the Calibrated Severity Score (CSS) ≥ 4; ② 3–8 years old; ③ the children were drug naïve within the past two weeks; ④ diagnostic tests for hearing were normal. Exclusion criteria: ① children with syndromic autism (e.g., Fragile X syndrome, Rett syndrome, etc.); ② hypothyroidism and other inherited metabolic diseases.

Plasma and urine sample collections

All ASD patients and TD children were required to fast and undergo testing between 8:00 AM and 10:00 AM. Subjects rested in a seated position for at least 30 minutes prior to blood and urine collection to avoid excitation. Approximately 2–3 mL of random EDTA-anticoagulated blood was collected from participants. Samples were immediately centrifuged (centrifugal radius: 85 mm; 10,000 rpm; duration: 5 min) to obtain 1 mL of plasma. Plasma levels of 10 neurotransmitters: dopamine (DA), epinephrine (E), norepinephrine (NE), metanephrine (MN), normetanephrine (NMN), 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT, serotonin), glutamate (Glu), tyrosine (Tyr), tryptophan (Trp）, and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) were detected. Random urine specimens from ASD children were preserved in acetic acid-containing tubes and analyzed for 8 neurotransmitters/metabolites via Ultra-Performance Liquid Chromatography - Tandem Mass Spectrommetry (UPLC-MS/MS): DA, E, NE, MN, NMN, vanillylmandelic acid (VMA), homovanillic acid (HVA), and 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA). Internal Standards (IS) in this study consisted of Isotopically labeled IS compounds DA-D4, NE-D6, and NMN-D3 were employed. The solid-phase extraction (SPE) method was used to isolate plasma DA, E, NE, MN, NMN, and 3-methoxytyramine (3-MT). While protein precipitation was performed to extract plasma 5-HT, GABA, Glu, Tyr, Trp, and urinary neurotransmitters. All collected samples were stored under cryogenic conditions and immediately transferred for analysis. The concentration analyses were conducted in the mass spectrometry laboratory of Wuhan Kindstar Diagnostics Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China. Sample processing followed established protocols from previous studies with minor modifications [15, 16].

Quantification and analytical method

The quantitative analysis of neurotransmitters was performed using Shimadzu ultra-performance liquid chromatograph coupled with a Shimadzu LCMS-8050 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer system (Kyoto, Japan) equipped with an autosampler, high-performance liquid chromatography unit, column oven, and electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Detection was carried out in positive or negative ion multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode using ESI. Transfer 40 μL of the filtered supernatant into an autosampler vial and inject 10 μL into the detection system. Concentrations were determined using the IS method. Calibration curves were constructed with linear regression weighting (w = 1/C2 or w = 1/C), achieving R2 values > 0.99. Intra-day and inter-day precision measurements showed relative standard deviations (RSD) < 10%, demonstrating good stability.

Evaluation of disorder severity by CARS

CARS including 15 items, is a behavioral rating scale used for diagnosing ASD in conjunction with clinical judgment. The 15 items are: relating to people; imitation; emotional response; body use; object use; adaptation to change; visual response; listening response; taste, smell, touch response and use; fear or nervousness; verbal communication; nonverbal communication; activity level; level and consistency of intellectual response and general impressions. Research has shown that CARS is a reliable and stable indicator of ASD in children over 2 years of age as well as in adolescents [17]. Each item is scored from 1 (no pathology) to 4 (severe pathology). Severity assessment is classified as follows: a total score of 15–29.5 indicates “nonautistic”; a score of 30–35.5 indicates “mild to moderate”; and a score of 36–60 indicates “moderate to severe” ASD.

Diagnosis by ADOS-2

ADOS is a semi-structured assessment tool designed to evaluate communication, social interaction, and play or imaginative use of materials in individuals suspected of having ASD. It consists of four modules that are administered according to participant’s language development and age. Each module contains standard activities that allow the examiner to observe the behaviors consistent with a diagnosis of ASD or other developmental disorders. The ADOS-2 is an updated version of ADOS, which evaluates ASD symptoms in aspects of social affects, play and creativity, and repetitive behaviors [18]. The ADOS-2 CSS provides a quantitative assessment of increasing severity of autism related symptoms (1–2: “minimal-to-no evidence”, 3–4: “low”, 5–7: “moderate”, and 8–10: “high”), with a score of 4 or above meeting criteria for an ASD diagnosis [19].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). GraphPad Prism Version 10.0 was used for graphics. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed data are presented as the interquartile range (25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles). For continuous data, a T-test was used to compare normally distributed data, while non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test to compare two groups. Due to the non-normal distribution of the neurotransmitter concentration data, log transformation was applied for all subsequent statistical analyses. Pearson correlation analysis was used for correlation analysis, with P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance. Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine the correlations among the 6 log-transformed plasma and 6 log-transformed urinary neurotransmitters. The multivariate linear regression analysis was used to evaluate the relationship between neurotransmitter levels (plasma/urine) and ADOS severity scores of ASD, adjusting for age, sex and BMI. In linear regression analysis, gender was treated as a categorical variable through dummy coding. To further explore the dose-response relationships between neurotransmitter concentrations and outcomes using the restricted cubic spline (RCS) model. In the RCS models, the median concentration of metals was used as the reference point, with the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles setting as knots. The red line represents the dose-response relationship curve, and the shaded area indicates the 95% confidence interval. All the above models were adjusted for the three covariates: age, sex, and BMI.

Neurotransmitter levels in plasma and urine were not significantly different between males and females within the ASD and TD children groups. The mean age of the children with ASD was 4.17 ± 1.10 years (mean ± standard deviation, SD). A total of 40 plasma samples and 38 urine samples were collected from children with ASD. Among the 40 ASD participants, classified according to ADOS-CSS severity classification, 1 ASD child was mild, 29 children were moderate, and 10 children were severe. Discussion

Recently, evidence has indicated that the disturbances of neurotransmitters in peripheral fluids and the CNS are involved in the pathogenesis of ASD. Acquiring CSF via lumbar puncture is an invasive and challenging procedure, particularly in pediatric populations. The “periphery as a window to the brain” concept has led to an increasing number of clinical studies assessing plasma, urine and saliva neurotransmitter levels in ASD [20–22]. This study focused on detecting monoamine and amino acid neurotransmitters in peripheral plasma and urine, and analyzing their correlations with ADOS severity scores. This study demonstrated that plasma GABA levels exhibit a negative linear relationship with ASD severity scores. In contrast, urinary NE and its metabolite NMN showed a positive linear relationship with ASD severity scores. Plasma NE and Tyr levels demonstrated a non-linear relationship with ASD severity scores. Alternatively, measuring neurotransmitters in peripheral plasma and urine may serve as a non-invasive, rapid, and straightforward biological tool to assist in assessing the severity of ASD.

Amino acid neurotransmitters in plasma GABA

GABA is an amino acid neurotransmitter and serves as the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the CNS. A major hypothesis concerning ASD is that dysregulated GABAergic pathways disrupt the normal excitatory/inhibitory (E/I) balance in the brain, leading to social and interpersonal difficulties [23]. The initial univariate analysis in this study showed that plasma GABA levels were significantly higher in children with ASD compared to the TD children. Previous studies have frequently reported elevated plasma GABA levels, as well as significantly lower levels of plasma glutamine and Glu/GABA ratios in individuals with ASD compared to TD children [8, 24, 25]. To more precisely evaluate the independent association between GABA and the severity of ASD symptoms, we further constructed multiple linear regression models and RCS models, incorporating potential confounding factors such as age, gender, and BMI as covariates. A significant negative correlation was found between GABA levels and ASD severity scores. This shift in results indicates that the initially observed intergroup difference in plasma GABA may have been driven by the aforementioned confounding factors. Specifically, lower plasma GABA levels were associated with more severe ASD symptoms. Previous studies have reported controversial conclusions regarding central E-I imbalance, with findings suggesting either increased or decreased levels [7]. This controversy is influenced by multiple factors, such as detection methods, the heterogeneity of ASD, and the existence of various ASD subtypes (e.g., high-functioning vs. low-functioning). From the perspective of reduced E-I balance, enhanced activity of central GABAergic neurons may lead to a compensatory decrease in peripheral GABA levels, which correlates with the severity of ASD symptoms—the lower the peripheral GABA levels, the more severe the ASD.

Monoamine neurotransmitters—plasma and urinary catecholamines

The level of NE in the blood reflects the excitability of the sympathetic nerve. Some studies suggest increased peripheral noradrenergic activity in individuals with ASD, manifested as elevated plasma E and NE levels, as well as alterations in urinary metabolites of various catecholamine neurotransmitters. Other studies have failed to identify significant abnormalities in NE levels [18]. However, the clinical procedures for blood collection and urine sampling may induce heightened stress responses in children with ASD, which could potentially lead to increased E and NE levels. In this study, the levels of NE in the plasma of children with ASD were significantly lower than that of TD children, which was consistent with Luo LR et al.’s findings [26]. In this study, we found that plasma Tyr levels were significantly lower in children with ASD compared to TD children. The decreased plasma Tyr levels lead to impaired peripheral NE synthesis. On the other hand, we found elevated plasma NMN levels (an intermediate metabolite of NE), this discrepancy is related to the source of NMN. Plasma NMN primarily originates from the metabolism of NE into NMN at sympathetic nerve terminals, which then enters the bloodstream [27]. The significant decrease in urinary NMN may be due to an overall deficiency in catechol-O-methyl transferase (COMT) enzyme activity in ASD patients. Consequently, the metabolism of a large amount of NE is impaired, leading to increased excretion of unmetabolized NE in urine. This conclusion requires further validation by measuring the intermediate metabolite 3-Methoxy-4-hydroxyphenylglycol (MHPG)- Monoamine oxidase (MAO)-dominated intraneuronal metabolic pathway of NE [27]. While plasma NE decreases, its precursor DA increases, consequently resulting in an increase in the ultimate urinary metabolite HVA. Correspondingly, the terminal metabolite of NE (urinary VMA) was significantly higher in ASD children than in the TD children. Given that plasma NMN—largely derived from the local metabolism of NE at sympathetic nerve terminals—is more stable and has a longer half-life than NE itself, it is a superior biomarker for overall sympathetic activity [28]. The observed elevations in both plasma NMN and the ultimate urinary metabolites (HVA and VMA) lead us to conclude that children with ASD exhibit enhanced peripheral sympathetic activity.

Further investigation of the dose-response relationship using an RCS model revealed that plasma NE levels, after adjusting for sex, age, and BMI, exhibited a non-linear relationship with ADOS CSS. This suggests that the relationship between neurotransmitter levels and ASD severity may be complex, potentially attributable to the substantial heterogeneity of ASD. Furthermore, the log-transformed levels of urinary NE and NMN showed a positive linear trend with ADOS severity scores. The parallel linear trends observed between both urinary markers and ASD severity indicate the presence of sustained activation of the noradrenergic system in children with ASD. Currently, in clinical practice, α2-adrenergic receptor agonists—which inhibit NE release at the presynaptic site—have been shown to improve hyperactivity, impulsivity, hyperexcitability, and social interactions in individuals with ASD, as verified in double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trials [29]. The drug mechanism is highly consistent with the biological changes identified in this study. However, ASD is a condition with high clinical heterogeneity and complex phenotypes. The imbalance in the noradrenergic system may not merely be overactivation, warranting more in-depth investigation and discussion in the future.

Monoamine neurotransmitters—plasma 5-HT

5-HT participates in regulating the proliferation, migration, synaptogenesis, and circuit maturation of brain neurons. This study found that plasma 5-HT levels were significantly higher in children with ASD compared to TD children. This is consistent with the conclusion reported before: A study involving 24 children with ASD and 24 TD children found that the ASD group exhibited a hyperserotonergic state, characterized by elevated plasma serotonin [14]. Following supplementation with probiotics and fructo oligosaccharides, serotonin levels were significantly reduced. The latest study indicated that children with ASD, especially boys, presented higher serum 5-HT levels compared with TD children (2–7 years) [22]. Additionally, increased 5-HT content is considerably positively associated with core ASD symptoms and negatively associated with neurodevelopment in boys with ASD.

Monoamine neurotransmitters in plasma Tyr

In this research, plasma Tyr was lower in ASD group than TD group, which was consistent with Randazzo M et al.’s conclusion [30]. Recent research suggests that tyrosine metabolism is associated with disruptive behaviors in ASD children without neurodevelopmental regression [31]. One study has indicated that plasma tyrosine metabolism is linked to the autism-risk poor communication scores measured on the Ages and Stages Questionnaire (ASQ) [32]. After adjusting for covariates including age, gender, and BMI, the RCS model revealed a nonlinear relationship between plasma log-transformed Tyr levels and ADOS-2 CSS. Specifically, both excessively high and excessively low plasma Tyr levels were associated with more severe autism symptoms. This suggests the complexity of the pathophysiological mechanisms of ASD: When plasma tyrosine levels decrease, the entry of tyrosine into the brain relies on competitive transport via the Large Neutral Amino Acid Transporter (LAT) at the BBB [33]. This leads to reduced central tyrosine levels, thereby decreasing the synthesis of central catecholamines (such as DA and NE). These neurotransmitters are closely associated with deficits in social motivation, impaired motor coordination, inattention, sensory abnormalities, and executive dysfunction in children with ASD. When plasma Tyr levels are too high, the competitive balance of other large neutral amino acids that cross the BBB may be disrupted, indirectly altering the cerebral amino acid profile and neurotransmitter environment [30]. The tyrosine hydroxylase inhibitor L1-79, a racemic formulation of α-methyl-para-tyrosine, is currently being under evaluation as a treatment for the core symptoms of ASD (NCT02947048) [34]. In clinical practice, appropriate supplementation of tyrosine-rich foods can be considered for children with ASD who are picky eaters, but excessive intake should be avoided.

The limitations of this research include the followings: First, this was a cross‑sectional study involving a relatively small number of ASD patients; a large sample size of participants may improve the statistical power of the results. Second, this study lacked data of ASD children less than 3 years old, limiting the generalizability of the results. Meanwhile, this study was performed as exploratory investigations and have not yet been independently replicated. Thirdly, multiple regression analysis indicated that plasma GABA, urinary NE and NMN levels explained 26.9%, 24.8% and 15.8% of the variability of ADOS severity scores for ASD, suggesting that additional confounding factors need to be explored. Only age, gender, and BMI were included as covariates, while other potential confounding factors such as parental education level, children’s dietary status, living environment, and ASD subtypes were not thoroughly examined. Furthermore, peripheral level measurements cannot serve as substitutes for direct assessments of brain tissue or CSF. There is insufficient evidence to confirm a strong correlation between peripheral and central neurotransmitter levels. Future studies are expected to uncover the underlying biological mechanisms through more extensive and in-depth research involving animal models, CSF, brain tissue, and saliva.

In conclusion, plasma GABA and urinary NE and NMN levels were identified as potential biomarkers significantly associated with ASD severity. Future studies should focus on increasing patient sample sizes and incorporating additional covariates to further develop models for predicting ASD severity. This will provide a more practical and robust scientific basis for supporting the assessment of ASD severity. Additionally, more in-depth investigations into the underlying biological mechanisms should be conducted through animal models and analyses of tissues such as cerebrospinal fluid.
