Global trends and inequalities in appendicitis burden among youth: Analysis of GBD 1990 to 2021
Authors: Wei Luo, Yongqing Zhao, Yuqi Liu, Xiaoliang Zhu, Shaozhen Rui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Appendicitis is a common digestive system disorder worldwide. This study aims to assess the global pattern and trends of appendicitis burden in adolescents and young adults as well as its health inequalities from 1990 to 2021. In this descriptive cross-sectional ecological study, we downloaded data on the incidence, death, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) due to appendicitis among people aged 10 to 24 years from the Global Burden of Disease 2021. We evaluated the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), average annual percentage change (AAPC), and sociodemographic index (SDI). The slope index and the concentration index were calculated to assess cross-national health inequalities. From 1990 to 2021, the global age-standardized incidence and DALY rates of appendicitis in adolescents and young adults showed downward trends, with an AAPC of −0.18 (−0.2 to −0.17) and −2.4 (−2.47 to −2.32), respectively. An increasing trend in incidence was observed in the 10 to 14 age group (AAPC = 0.06). At the regional level, the highest ASIR was observed in High-income Asia Pacific (741.8) in 2021, and the highest age-standardized DALY rate was observed in Andean Latin America (32.5). There was a trend at the national level of increasing ASIR along with increases in SDI, and the relationship between DALY and SDI followed an inverse U-shaped pattern. The slope index decreased from −19.98 (95% confidence interval [CI] = −23.79 to −16.17) in 1992 to −12.13 (95% CI = −14.35 to −9.91) in 2021. The concentration index also decreased from −0.33 (95% CI = −0.39 to −0.26) in 1992 to −0.19 (95% CI = −0.23 to −0.14) in 2021. While the global age-standardized incidence and DALY rates of appendicitis in adolescents and young adults declined from 1990 to 2021, an increasing trend of ASIR was observed in the 10 to 14 age group. Although the cross-national inequalities have diminished over time, they remain a public health concern.

1. Introduction

Appendicitis, characterized by inflammation of the appendix, is one of the most common surgical emergencies.[1] Without timely diagnosis and intervention, it can progress to severe complications such as appendiceal perforation, peritonitis, and sepsis, posing a significant threat to individual health. Although appendicitis can occur at any age, its epidemiological distribution shows a distinct age-specific pattern, with adolescents and young adults exhibiting the highest disease burden.[2]

Adolescence and early adulthood represent a critical period of human development, characterized by rapid physical and psychological changes.[3] The onset of disease during this phase may lead to disrupted education, psychological distress, and diminished quality of life.[4] The burden from childhood noncommunicable diseases has mounted, especially in low- and middle-income countries.[5] The health status of adolescents and young adults is now included within the “Countdown to 2030” towards Sustainable Development Goals.[6] As a high-incidence noncommunicable disease, appendicitis constitutes a significant health resource and economic burden. Despite the increasing recognition of high disease burden among young individuals, comprehensive data regarding burden and cross-country health inequalities of appendicitis in these cohorts remains scarce.

In this study, we utilized data from the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) 2021 to systematically analyze the temporal trend and cross-country health inequalities of appendicitis burden in adolescents and young adults aged 10 to 24 years between 1990 and 2021. The findings aim to provide critical scientific evidence for optimizing health resource allocation, formulating early intervention strategies, and informing efforts to reduce global health inequalities.

2. Methods

2.1. Data sources and definitions

Data were downloaded from the GBD 2021, coordinated by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool). The GBD 2021 provides comprehensive and comparable estimates of the incidence, prevalence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for 371 diseases and injuries in 5 sociodemographic index (SDI) quintiles, 21 GBD regions and all countries and territories, stratified by age, sex, and year.[7] The GBD data were derived from a wide range of sources, including vital registration systems, hospital records, surveys, and published scientific literature. This is a descriptive cross-sectional ecological study utilizing secondary data.

In the GBD 2021, appendicitis is defined as the inflammation of the appendix, generally presenting with nausea, vomiting, and sharp pain in the right lower abdomen.[7] To isolate the relevant cohort for this study, our inclusion criteria for data extraction from the GBD Results Tool were defined as follows: age demographic strictly filtered to adolescents and young adults aged 10 to 24 years; disease etiology specifically selected as “appendicitis,” encompassing the 10th International Classification of Diseases codes K35-K35.3, K35.8, K35.80, K35.89, K35.9, K36, K36.0, K37, K37.0, K37.9, and K38.3[7]; and the temporal range set from the year 1990 to 2021.

DALY measures the total health loss due to both fatal and nonfatal disease burden, which was calculated as the sum of years of life lost (quantifying premature mortality by combining the number of deaths with standard life expectancy at age of death) and years lived with disability (estimating nonfatal health loss by integrating prevalence and duration of a disease or injury weighted by a measure of disease severity).[8] The SDI is a comprehensive indicator that can measure the overall development status of a region or a country. It synthesizes 3 dimensions of human development: average income per capita, years of schooling, and total fertility rate in women under the age of 25.[9]

2.2. Joinpoint regression model

The Joinpoint regression model was employed to analyze the temporal trends of appendicitis incidence and DALY, from 1990 to 2021. The model enables the calculation of the average annual percentage change (AAPC) over the entire period, providing a nuanced understanding of the direction and magnitude of trends.[10] The analysis was conducted using the Joinpoint Regression Program software developed by the National Cancer Institute.[11] The AAPC, along with its 95% confidence interval (CI), provides a summary measure of the average trend across the entire study period, with statistical significance determined by whether the 95% CI excludes zero. If the AAPC and the lower bound of its 95% CI are >0, it indicates a statistically significant upward trend; if the AAPC and the upper bound of its 95% CI are <0, it reflects a statistically significant downward trend.[12]

2.3. Health inequality analysis

To quantify the magnitude and direction of socioeconomic inequality in disease burden, we employed 2 well-established indicators: the slope index of inequality and the concentration index.[13] The slope index is an absolute measure of inequality, which can represent the difference in disease burden between the most advantaged (country with the highest SDI) and most disadvantaged (country with the lowest SDI) countries via an appropriate regression model. The concentration index is a relative measure of inequality. It assesses the degree to which a health outcome is concentrated across the socioeconomic distribution. The larger the absolute value of the slope index and concentration index are, the more severe the degree of inequality is.[14]

2.4. Data analysis

Data analysis and visualization are accomplished in the R software (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Uncertainty for all estimates was quantified using 95% uncertainty intervals, which were calculated for every metric using the 25th and 975th ordered values from a 1000-draw posterior distribution.[15] The age-standardized rate was calculated based on the world standard population to allow for comparable estimates across populations with different age structures.[16] The change in the absolute number of incident cases and DALYs over the study period was assessed by calculating the percentage change between the initial year (1990) and the terminal year (2021). All rates are expressed per 100,000 population.

2.5. Ethics statement

This study used publicly available, de-identified data from the GBD 2021 study. Therefore, institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required.

3. Results

3.1. Overall burden of appendicitis in people aged 10 to 24 years

To assess the global pattern, temporal trends, and associated health inequalities of appendicitis from 1990 to 2021, we first evaluated the overall disease burden across the 10- to 24-year-old demographic before stratifying by region and SDI. In 2021, the global incidence of appendicitis in adolescents and young adults of 10 to 24 years was 6,336,988 (4,610,260–8,716,450). Among these cases, 3,023,423 (2,170,915–4,208,852) were males and 3,313,565 (2,443,860–4,505,472) were females. The global age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) was 353.0 in 1990, which slightly decreased to 334.7 in 2021 (AAPC = −0.18, 95% CI = −0.2 to −0.17). Among adolescents and young adults of 10 to 24 years, there were 345,441 (283,864–420,023) total DALYs in 2021, including 150,130 (122,915–182,101) in males and 195,311 (149,472–243,071) in females. There was a 41.4% decrease in the number of DALYs globally from 1990 to 2021, decreasing from 590,067 (461,824–749,636) DALYs for both sexes combined in 1990. The age-standardized DALY rate decreased from 38.0 in 1990 to 18.3 in 2021, with an AAPC of −2.4 (−2.47 to −2.32; Fig. 1).

In the global population, people aged 20 to 24 years had the highest incidence rate of appendicitis, and people aged 15 to 19 years had the highest DALY rate (Table 1). The largest decrease in appendicitis incidence from 1990 to 2021 was found in individuals aged 20 to 24 years (AAPC = −0.3, 95% CI = −0.32 to −0.29), higher than the decrease in incidence in people aged 15 to 19 years (AAPC = −0.2, 95% CI = −0.23 to −0.18). Conversely, an increasing trend in incidence was observed in the 10 to 14 age group, with an AAPC of 0.06 (95% CI = 0.03 to 0.08). Over the same period, the DALY rate showed a declining trend across all 3 age groups.

3.2. Burden at regional and national level

Stratified by regional levels, the most pronounced significant increase in ASIR of appendicitis in adolescents and young adults of 10 to 24 years was observed in East Asia, with an AAPC of 1.1 (95% CI = 1.03 to 1.16). Conversely, Andean Latin America experienced the most substantial decline, with an AAPC of −0.96 (95% CI = −1.01 to −0.91). Figure S1, showed the number of incident cases and DALYs of appendicitis in adolescents and young adults in 2021. In 2021, the highest ASIRs were observed in High-income Asia Pacific (741.8), Andean Latin America (567.5), and East Asia (534.3; Fig. S2. From 1990 to 2021, among the 21 GBD regions, only the age-standardized DALY rate in Southern Sub-Saharan Africa showed an upward trend (AAPC = 0.16, 95% CI = 0.05 to 0.3). Seven (35%) of the remaining 20 regions had DALY rates in 2021 in the upper tercile of the distribution (Fig. 2), which represents the regions with the highest burden in 2021. Furthermore, 3 of those 12 countries are in the upper tercile of the distribution of the AAPC for 1990 to 2021, representing the group with the greatest reduction in burden: Andean Latin America, South Asia, and Southeast Asia. Eastern Europe is located in the lower tercile of the distribution in both DALY rates in 2021 and AAPC from 1990 to 2021 combined, representing the situation with the lowest disease burden in 2021 and the greatest reduction in disease burden over the past 32 years.

At the national level, the highest ASIRs of appendicitis in people aged 10 to 24 years in 2021 were observed in Japan (769.7), Belgium (768.1), and Switzerland (746.9), and the highest age-standardized DALY rates were observed in Guatemala (54.9), Pakistan (47.7), and Cambodia (44.2; Fig. 3). From 1990 to 2021, the largest increase in ASIR of appendicitis in adolescents and young adults of 10 to 24 years was observed in Czechia, followed by Slovakia and Slovenia. During the same period, the most pronounced increase in age-standardized DALY rate was found in Tokelau and Zimbabwe.

3.3. Burden by SDI

The age distributions of the number of incident cases and DALYs due to appendicitis in 5 SDI quintiles are shown in Figure S3. In 2021, the highest ASIR of appendicitis was observed in countries with high SDI (469.9), and the lowest was observed in countries with low SDI (196.8). The appendicitis incidence significantly decreased in countries in low, low-middle, and middle SDI quintiles from 1990 to 2021. The largest decrease in ASIR was observed in the low-SDI quintile countries (AAPC = −0.67, 95% CI = −0.69 to −0.66). During this period, the countries in the high-middle SDI quintile showed an increasing trend in ASIR of appendicitis, with an AAPC of 0.69 (0.66–0.71). As for the age-standardized DALY rate, low, low-middle, and middle SDI quintiles showed greater decline.

Figure 4 demonstrates the association between age-standardized incidence and DALY rates and SDI across all countries and territories in 2021. There was a trend at the national level of increasing ASIR along with increases in SDI. Some high-income countries, such as Japan, Belgium, and Switzerland, were located above the fitted curve. The relationship between DALY and SDI followed an inverse U-shaped pattern: as the SDI increased, the DALY rate first rose and then declined, with a turning point occurring near an SDI of 0.4. 3.4. Cross-country health inequalities

The slope indexes were −19.98 (95% CI = −23.79 to −16.17) and −12.13 (95% CI = −14.35 to −9.91) DALYs per 100,000 population in 1990 and 2021, respectively (Fig. 5). This finding indicates that the inequality in the burden of appendicitis in adolescents and young adults of 10 to 24 years between high- and low-SDI countries decreased during this time. The concentration index also decreased between 1990 (−0.33, 95% CI = −0.39 to −0.26) and 2021 (−0.19, 95% CI = −0.23 to −0.14).

Sociodemographic index-related health inequality in the DALY of appendicitis in adolescents and young adults between 1990 and 2021. DALY = disability-adjusted life year.

In summary, while the absolute number of incident cases of appendicitis among youth increased from 1990 to 2021, the global age-standardized incidence and DALY rates generally declined. Despite this overall progress, notable disparities remain: the highest incidence is concentrated in High-income Asia Pacific and other high-SDI regions, whereas the highest DALY burden persists in Andean Latin America and lower-SDI regions, reflecting an inverse U-shaped relationship between DALYs and SDI. Finally, cross-national health inequalities related to appendicitis burden have measurably decreased over the 32-year study period.

4. Discussion

This study reveals that over the past 30 years, the global age-standardized incidence and DALY rates of appendicitis have decreased in adolescents and young adults aged 10 to 24 years. Notably, an increasing incidence trend was observed in the youngest age group, and was observed in East Asia. Although the cross-country health inequality related to SDI has decreased, the DALY for appendicitis is still concentrated in countries with low SDI. These findings require us to pay more attention to the health security and inequalities among adolescents and young adults.

Our study is consistent with previous studies, which indicates that the incidence of appendicitis is higher in countries with higher SDI.[2,17] Dietary habits are believed to be one of the possible causes of appendicitis.[18,19] A study showed that children with higher fiber intake levels were estimated to have 30% lower risk of appendicitis than children with lower fiber intake.[20] Air pollution and smoking were also potential risks for appendicitis.[21,22] In addition, advanced diagnostic intensity and healthcare access in high-SDI countries likely contribute to accurate recorded incidence rates rather than underestimated incidence rates.

There was an inverted U-shaped relationship between SDI and DALY rates of appendicitis in adolescents and young adults aged 10 to 24 years. In low-income countries, the overall provision of surgical care is poor.[23] Countries with low SDI had lower incidence, but DALYs are higher than in countries with high SDI due to elevated case-fatality from limited healthcare access, such as delay in diagnosis and poor surgical facilities. As regions develop (middle-SDI), incidence rises due to environmental and lifestyle changes, while healthcare systems are not yet fully robust enough to manage the increased caseload effectively, leading to a peak in DALYs. In high-SDI regions, although incidence remains high, exceptionally low case-fatality rates, driven by excellent surgical and emergency care, result in the lowest DALY rates. Furthermore, even if surgical procedures are available, the risks of postoperative complications and mortality in low-SDI countries are often much higher than those in high-income countries.[24] This finding underscores the critical need to enhance diagnostic capacity for appendicitis in adolescents and young adults and to advance surgical facilities in low-SDI regions.

The improvement in health inequality, reflected by the slope index and concentration index, indicates great progress in global health, suggesting that the absolute and relative gaps in appendicitis burden in adolescents and young adults of 10 to 24 years between the most and least advantaged countries are narrowing. This convergence is likely driven by significant improvements in surgical infrastructure, healthcare access, and public health awareness in many low- and middle-SDI countries. This also explains the comparatively rapid decline in the age-standardized DALY rate observed in low and low-middle SDI regions over the past 32 years. Although our analysis mainly focuses on health inequality among countries, it must be recognized that such inequality may also be significant within countries. There may be significant differences among urban and rural areas, different genders, and different socioeconomic status groups of teenagers in terms of access to timely diagnosis and high-quality surgical procedures. The existing health inequality highlights the continued need for targeted interventions, such as strengthening emergency surgical systems, improving diagnostic capacity, and ensuring timely access to treatment, in order to sustain and further accelerate the narrowing of this gap. Future research should also aim to reveal these intragroup differences to ensure that public health policies can precisely cover the most vulnerable groups, thereby reducing the gap between countries while striving to eliminate inequality within countries.

At the public health level, healthy diet and nutrition education should be prioritized in countries with low SDI. At the same time, investment and upgrading of the emergency surgery capabilities and imaging diagnostic equipment in grassroots medical institutions can effectively reduce diagnostic delays. At the clinical practice level, for medical workers in low SDI areas, promoting standardized diagnosis and surgical plans for appendicitis can significantly reduce postoperative complications and mortality rates. In addition, exploring safe and effective nonsurgical antibiotic treatment plans in these environments may also serve as a supplementary management strategy when surgical resources are insufficient. Moreover, assistance should also be provided to strengthen the surgical system construction in low- and middle-income countries, including training local medical personnel, donating necessary surgical equipment and medicines.[25]

This study, using the GBD 2021 data, comprehensively depicted the disease burden trend and health inequality pattern of appendicitis among individuals aged 10 to 24 worldwide over the past 30 years. However, our study has limitations inherent to the GBD framework. Firstly, estimates rely on modeling and imputation for regions with sparse data, which may introduce uncertainty. Secondly, despite statistical adjustments, underreporting and misclassification of appendicitis, particularly in regions with limited health infrastructure, are probable, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true burden in low-SDI settings. Third, due to limitations in the database, we were unable to differentiate between acute appendicitis and chronic appendicitis. In the future, more attention should be paid to specific registration systems for appendicitis, especially in countries with low SDI. It is also necessary to further improve the health economics assessment to determine the most cost-effective intervention measures.

5. Conclusion

Over the past 3 decades, the global age-standardized incidence and DALY rates of appendicitis in adolescents and young adults (aged 10–24) have steadily declined. However, an opposing trend of increasing incidence was observed within the youngest cohort (10–14 years) and specifically in the East Asia region. Furthermore, while cross-country health inequalities have narrowed, the overall DALY burden remains disproportionately concentrated in lower-SDI countries. The persistent inverse U-shaped relationship between DALY rates and SDI highlights a critical vulnerability in developing healthcare systems. This underscores that resolving global appendicitis disparities relies heavily on equitable access to safe, timely surgical care. To consolidate global health progress, targeted interventions are imperative. Public health strategies must prioritize nutritional and lifestyle education to address incidence drivers in at-risk populations. Future research should investigate granular, intranational inequalities to ensure that health policies effectively reach the most vulnerable youth populations.
