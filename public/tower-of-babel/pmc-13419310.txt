Primary hyperparathyroidism presenting as atraumatic bilateral hip fractures: a case report

Background

Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is the third most common endocrine disorder after diabetes and thyroid disease [1] and is estimated to affect about 1.5% of older adults (>70 years) [2]. With an increasing life expectancy, the proportion of older adults with PHPT is predicted to grow [2]. However, the diagnosis is often missed or delayed [2], as most cases (80%) are asymptomatic and usually only identified after blood tests confirm an elevated serum calcium and a high or inappropriately high parathyroid hormone (PTH) level [2, 3].

In PHPT, continuously high PTH levels induces osteoblast expression of the receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL), which stimulates osteoclasts and increases bone resorption. PTH also induces a decrease in osteoprotegerin (OPG), a decoy receptor that functions to block RANKL. The increased RANKL/OPG ratio is the main mechanism that explains bone loss in PHPT [4]. While there is an increased activation of bone modeling units (BMUs), in cancellous bone the resorption depth is shallower and the bone formation period is longer. However, in the cortical bone, cortical porosity and endocortical bone resorption are enhanced. For this reason, there is a greater loss of bone at cortical sites, where fracture risk is especially increased [4], such as the neck of femur, distal radius and humerus. However, atraumatic fractures resulting from PHPT are rare [5]. We report the case of an 82-year-old woman who presented with atraumatic bilateral hip fractures and was diagnosed with PHPT.

Case presentation

An 82-year-old White woman was referred to the emergency department with severe left hip pain and was not able to weight bear. She had a history of progressive left-sided thigh pain radiating to her knee over the previous two months with a decline in her mobility. There was no history of trauma, falls, prior fractures nor a diagnosis of osteoporosis. Risk factors for bone loss included an early menopause (in her thirties) and distant steroid use with chemotherapy. Medical history included esophageal cancer (15 years prior) treated with surgery and chemo-radiotherapy, cholecystectomy, osteoarthritis of lumbar spine, right total knee replacement, and left ventricular failure. Medications were frusemide, pantoprazole, paracetamol, and codeine.

X-ray of hips showed a displaced left neck of femur fracture and non-displaced right neck of femur fracture (see Fig. 1). Computed tomography of the pelvis was performed to rule out a pathological cause and confirmed X-ray findings. It also showed sclerosis and small volume callus formation bilaterally suggestive of healing subacute fractures, with no evidence of destructive lesions. Blood tests revealed hypercalcemia (corrected calcium 2.76 mmol/l), normal renal, liver profile and serum electrophoresis, an elevated PTH of 172 pg/ml and vitamin D deficiency (25-hydroxyvitamin D of 12 nmol/l). Thyroid function tests revealed a normal T4 of 18.8 pmol/l and suppressed thyroid stimulating hormone (< 0.01) consistent with subclinical hyperthyroidism. 24-hour urinary calcium was normal at 5.88 mmol. There was no evidence of malignancy on femoral biopsies. For her vitamin D deficiency, she was given 1600 IU vitamin D3 daily. This was also supplemented with a loading dose of 50,000 IU initially followed by another 50,000 IU one week later. 25-Hydroxyvitamin D level improved to 65 nmol/l at day 27 post admission (Table 1) and her vitamin D dose was then lowered to 800 IU daily.

For her left hip fracture, she had a cemented bipolar hemiarthroplasty and was allowed to partially weight bear on her right hip for two weeks after which she had a right hip arthroplasty. Densitometry (DXA) revealed a T-score of − 4.0 (0.607 g/cm2) at lumbar spine and − 4.9 (0.401 g/cm2) at distal forearm with normal vertebral fracture assessment. Single photon emission computed tomography (SPECT)/sestamibi for work up of PHPT showed a focus of intense tracer uptake in the superior right thyroid lobe suggesting a parathyroid adenoma. However, an ultrasound confirmed this as a U3 nodule which was found to be hyperfunctioning on a pertechnetate scan and is being followed up by endocrinology. Her PHPT was managed conservatively with cinacalcet (30 mg daily) to control hypercalcemia, and she received zoledronic acid (5 mg) for osteoporosis. However, five days after starting both treatments, her serum calcium dropped to 2.20 mmol/l and cinacalcet was stopped. Romosozumab was also considered given her very low T-scores, though was avoided as she had a diagnosis of heart failure. We plan to follow up her treatment with lifelong denosumab.

Discussion

Patients with PHPT have an increased risk of both hip and other major osteoporotic fractures [6] with up to a third having a history of at least one fracture at the time of diagnosis [7]. Fractures of the neck of femur after falls have been reported in younger males (aged 25 and 33 years) [8, 9] and females (aged 28 and 35 years) with PHPT [10, 11]. Tang et al. also reported bilateral femoral neck fractures after a fall in a 25-year-old male who was diagnosed with normocalcemic PHPT [12] while a subtrochanteric fracture after minor trauma was reported in a 54-year-old female with PHPT [13].

However, atraumatic fractures in patients with PHPT are rare, though have been reported at various sites including tibia [14], pubic rami [15], pelvis [16] and neck of femur [5, 17]. An even rarer occurrence is that of atraumatic bilateral neck of femur fractures as identified in our patient. To our knowledge, the only comparable case is that described by Kalender et al., who reported spontaneous bilateral femoral neck fractures in a 33-year-old postpartum woman with PHPT, although transient osteoporosis of pregnancy may also have contributed [18].

In our case, PHPT may have been present for some time. Apart from a blood test done two months previously showing mild hypercalcemia, we had no record of other blood tests that were done many years ago. Given that our patient had no symptoms to suggest PHPT and no prior history of fracture or DXA assessment, it was not possible to ascertain the duration of disease.

Our patient did have other risk factors for osteoporosis that are associated with an increased risk of hip fracture including premature menopause [19] prior steroid use [20], vitamin D deficiency [21] and a new diagnosis of subclinical hyperthyroidism [22]. For this reason, her hip fractures are not entirely attributable to her diagnosis of PHPT.

Vitamin D deficiency is common in PHPT and can exacerbate the condition (as was likely in our patient) resulting in higher PTH levels [23]. Vitamin D deficiency in PHPT has also been associated with higher bone turnover markers and lower bone mineral density [24]. Furthermore, supplementation of vitamin D deficiency in PHPT improves 25(OH)D status without worsening hypercalcemia or hypercalciuria [25] and is associated with lower PTH levels. For this reason, it is important to correct vitamin D deficiency in PHPT aiming to achieve a recommended level of > 50 nmol/l [23]. We achieved this by supplementing initially with 1600 IU vitamin D3 daily and bolus doses followed by close monitoring of her serum calcium.

For her severe osteoporosis (T-score − 4.0 in spine and − 4.9 distal radius), we administered zoledronic acid while awaiting work up for a possible parathyroidectomy. Oral bisphosphonates are shown to improve BMD in PHPT [3], though zoledronic acid has a more rapid onset of action so was used initially. Romosozumab was a possible therapeutic option though no study has specifically examined its effect in non-surgically treated PHPT. However, it was avoided as she had left ventricular failure and was at increased cardiovascular risk.

To manage her hypercalcemia (> 0.25 mmol/l above the upper limit), she was prescribed cinacalcet (30 mg daily). This has been shown to reduce serum PTH and calcium in PHPT and with dose titration results in normocalcemia in up to 73% of patients. It has not been proven though to prevent bone loss [3]. However, five days later serum calcium dropped to 2.20 mmol/l likely due to the antiresorptive effect of zoledronic acid in addition to the cinacalcet. For this reason, cinacalcet was stopped. A previous study identified a rapid and sustained decrease (over four weeks) of serum calcium in patients with PHPT after zoledronic acid therapy [26]. While data on longer term effects of zoledronic acid in this regard are lacking, serum calcium remained only marginally elevated three months later (2.55 mmol/l) in our patient.

A SPECT/sestamibi scan demonstrated avid uptake, but this was a false positive due to a hyperfunctioning thyroid nodule as is reported elsewhere [27]. This is being followed up by the endocrinology service and would account for her subclinical hyperthyroidism, which is also associated with increased hip fracture risk [28]. While its treatment appears to reduce bone loss no effect on fracture risk has been established [28].

We were not able to localize a parathyroid adenoma on CT SPECT or U/S, though sensitivity of these modalities for single gland disease in pooled analyses has been reported as 78.9% and 76.1%, respectively [29]. False-negative scans may also occur when parathyroid glands are small or hyperplastic and in adenomas with a paucity of oxyphil cells. Our patient declined further imaging with 4D CT and other possible investigations such as 18 F-fluorocholine positron emission tomography (PET)/computed tomography (CT) or parathyroid hormone venous sampling [29] were not available at our unit. Without localizing a parathyroid adenoma, minimally invasive parathyroidectomy was not an option.

Parathyroidectomy in older adults (aged > 75 years) is associated with a lower risk of hip and any fracture with benefits noticed as early as one year [30]. However, surgical risk is increased in those with frailty, advanced age and with non-minimally invasive procedures [2, 30]. Indeed, fewer than one third of older adults in the US are treated with parathyroidectomy [30]. This was an important consideration in our patient who was aged 82, had left ventricular failure and who declined explorative parathyroid surgery, instead opting for medical management.

Despite not having surgery, serum PTH and BMD may remain stable for up to 8–10 years [3, 31]. In a small case series of asymptomatic patients with PHPT, hypercalcemia or hypercalciuria did not worsen over 10 years. However, about one quarter that entered menopause lost BMD [31]. For medical treatment of PHPT, oral bisphosphonates or denosumab can decrease bone turnover markers and increase lumbar spine and femoral neck BMD over 12–24 months [3, 32]. No data exist on the impact of zoledronic acid on BMD in PHPT, though as a more potent antiresorptive than oral bisphosphonates may be more effective. Interestingly, in postmenopausal women with PHPT, denosumab therapy for 24 months resulted in greater increases in lumbar spine and total hip BMD compared to that achieved in those with primary osteoporosis [33]. However, longer term effects (> 2 years) of these therapies on BMD or fracture risk in PHPT are unknown. Given the very low T-scores, we decided for ongoing denosumab therapy, as it is the most potent antiresorptive and in postmenopausal osteoporosis produces sustained and greater rises in BMD than bisphosphonates [34].

For follow up, we plan to measure serum calcium, 25(OH)D, PTH and creatinine clearance every 12 months and assess her BMD every 1–2 years as recommended [35]. Where there is clinical suspicion for kidney stones or vertebral fractures, appropriate imaging to identify same will be considered. If there is worsening BMD, new fractures, a decline in kidney function or kidney stones, the option of parathyroidectomy will be rediscussed with the patient.

Conclusions

To conclude, this case highlights the impact of PHPT on bone loss and fracture risk. Despite this, it is often undiagnosed, especially in older adults [2] where it can lead to substantial loss of BMD at the hip and in rare cases atraumatic femoral fractures. This emphasizes the importance of screening for osteoporosis in older adults. In particular, in patients with atypical fracture presentations (for example with minimal or no trauma or at sites less common for fragility fractures) it is important to consider secondary causes such as PHPT.
