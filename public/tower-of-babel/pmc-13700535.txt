Preoperative ultrasound assessment of gastric volume in diabetic patients scheduled for elective surgery: a prospective, observational, comparative cohort study
Authors: Mustafa Gökhan Bayram, Bengü Gülhan Köksal İncegül, Merve Sena Baytar, Çağdaş Baytar, Keziban Bollucuoğlu, Rahşan Dilek Okyay, Dinçer Fırat Şeker, Özcan Pişkin, Hilal Ayoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic gastroparesis is a chronic complication that increases the risk of perioperative aspiration. Despite adherence to current fasting guidelines, gastric emptying remains difficult to predict in patients with diabetes. However, comparative data on ultrasound-defined gastric contents and residual gastric volume in fasting patients with and without diabetes scheduled for elective surgery remain limited. This knowledge gap provides the rationale for the present study. Therefore, this study aimed to assess gastric contents and volume using gastric ultrasonography in patients with diabetes scheduled for elective surgery and to investigate clinical variables associated with a full stomach, indicative of an increased risk of aspiration.MethodsIn this prospective observational study, 39 patients with diabetes and 39 control participants, all of whom had fasted for at least 8 h, were enrolled. Preoperative gastric ultrasonography was performed to assess the Perlas grade, antral cross-sectional area (CSA, cm²), and estimated gastric volume (mL). High aspiration risk was defined using ultrasound-based surrogate markers, rather than clinically observed aspiration events, and was identified by the presence of solid or thick fluid contents (Perlas grade 3) or an estimated clear fluid volume exceeding 1.5 mL/kg. Gastric aspirate volume (mL) and pH were subsequently recorded. Among patients with diabetes, the associations of glycemic control (HbA1c), disease duration, and diabetes-related complications (ascertained from pre-existing medical records) on gastric parameters were evaluated. Statistical analyses included the independent-samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for group comparisons, chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate for categorical variables, Spearman correlation analysis, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsIn the diabetes group, antral CSA was significantly greater than that in the control group (10.91 ± 9.49 cm² vs. 5.11 ± 2.01 cm²; p < 0.001), as were estimated gastric volume (94.21 ± 110.65 mL vs. 41.04 ± 29.78 mL; p = 0.008), aspirated gastric fluid volume (19.67 ± 14.49 mL vs. 8.69 ± 5.68 mL; p < 0.001), Perlas score (median 0 [range 0–3] vs. 0 [range 0–0]; p < 0.001), and gastric pH (2.56 ± 0.54 vs. 2.14 ± 0.85; p = 0.011). A full stomach was identified in 17.94% of patients in the diabetes group (7/39) and in none of the control participants (0/39). Among participants classified as having high aspiration risk, the prevalence of diabetes-related neuropathy and the volume of aspirated gastric fluid were significantly higher. An internal exploratory ROC analysis indicated that a CSA threshold of ≥ 10 cm² may have descriptive value within this cohort (AUC: 0.862; 95% CI: 0.728–0.995, estimated using the DeLong method); however, as the outcome was partly derived from CSA, this threshold is descriptive and should not be interpreted as an independently validated diagnostic cutoff. No significant correlation was observed between HbA1c levels and gastric volume or gastric fluid pH, and no significant association was found between diabetes duration and gastric parameters.ConclusionDespite adherence to standard fasting protocols, patients with diabetes exhibited higher residual gastric volumes and a greater prevalence of a full stomach than control participants. Among patients with diabetes, neuropathy was more prevalent in those classified as having ultrasound-defined high aspiration risk than in those with low aspiration risk (57.1% vs. 9.4%; p = 0.012). No significant correlation was identified between HbA1c levels and gastric volume, and no statistically significant association was found between glycemic control and ultrasound-defined aspiration risk. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory rather than conclusive. Preoperative bedside gastric ultrasonography may provide additional information for aspiration risk assessment in selected patients with diabetes, but these findings require confirmation in larger studies.Trial registrationThe trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06195488 first posted January 8, 2024). Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a major global health challenge affecting approximately 537 million adults worldwide. This growing burden is characterized not only by its rising prevalence, which is projected to reach 783 million adults by 2045, but also by its substantial economic impact and its role as a leading cause of systemic complications [1, 2]. Diabetic gastroparesis is a chronic complication that poses significant challenges to perioperative management.

Standard preoperative fasting protocols are designed to ensure adequate gastric emptying and thereby reduce the risk of pulmonary aspiration. However, autonomic neuropathy and impaired gastrointestinal motility can make gastric emptying unpredictable in patients with DM. Not all patients with diabetes carry equivalent gastric emptying risk. Delayed gastric emptying is primarily driven by autonomic dysfunction, and its presence can be objectively confirmed through autonomic nervous system testing or scintigraphy. In routine preoperative practice, however, these investigations are not feasible, and patients at highest risk, particularly those with established diabetic autonomic neuropathy, cannot be reliably identified through fasting history alone. In the present study, autonomic neuropathy status was ascertained from pre-existing clinical diagnoses in medical records rather than formal autonomic testing, which represents a recognised limitation of this and similar observational studies. Consequently, patients who strictly adhere to conventional fasting guidelines may present with substantial residual gastric contents. The 2023 American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Practice Guidelines for Preoperative Fasting emphasize that although standard fasting times apply to healthy patients, certain comorbidities, including diabetes, warrant individualized clinical assessment. The guidelines specifically acknowledge that these conditions may delay gastric emptying, suggesting that standard intervals may not be sufficient for all patients [3–5]. Despite this updated guidance, no definitive clinical consensus has been reached regarding the optimal fasting duration or standardized screening tool for this high-risk population. By providing a non-invasive and objective assessment of gastric contents, gastric ultrasonography may improve the evaluation of aspiration risk beyond that afforded by time-based fasting guidelines alone [5–7].

While previous studies have investigated preoperative residual gastric volumes in patients with type 2 diabetes scheduled for elective surgery using gastric ultrasonography [8, 9], many have been limited by small sample sizes, the absence of formal autonomic neuropathy assessment, or a lack of correlation with objective glycaemic control markers such as HbA1c. Notably, Sastre et al. demonstrated in a bicentric prospective study of 289 patients that solid gastric residue was significantly more prevalent only in diabetic patients with confirmed dysautonomia compared with controls (12% vs. 3.5%; p = 0.03), underscoring the importance of autonomic status in perioperative gastric risk stratification [10]. Historically, high-risk diabetic patients have been identified using clinical symptom-based screening, scintigraphy-based gastric emptying studies, and autonomic nervous system testing [10]. However, these methods are not routinely available in the preoperative setting. Gastric ultrasonography offers a practical, non-invasive, bedside alternative enabling real-time qualitative and quantitative assessment of gastric contents [5–7]. HbA1c was included as a surrogate marker of long-term glycaemic control to explore its potential association with gastric parameters, given the inconsistent findings reported in the literature. Nasogastric aspirate volume was incorporated to provide an objective complementary measure of residual gastric contents beyond ultrasonographic estimation alone.

In this prospective study, we hypothesized that patients with diabetes would have greater gastric volumes compared with control participants despite adherence to standard fasting protocols. To address this hypothesis, the present study assessed the prevalence of ultrasound-defined full stomach using bedside POCUS in patients with type 2 diabetes scheduled for elective surgery, complemented by objective post-induction nasogastric aspirate measurements, an approach not employed in previous studies, while also examining the association between glycaemic control markers and gastric parameters in this population.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

After approval was obtained from the Faculty Ethics Committee and permission for the clinical study was granted (Meeting date: May 10, 2022; Protocol no: 2022/17; ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06195488), this prospective observational study was conducted at the Department of Anesthesiology and Reanimation, Zonguldak Bülent Ecevit University Faculty of Medicine, between November 1, 2022, and October 31, 2024. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study included 78 participants (39 with diabetes and 39 without diabetes) aged 40–65 years who were classified as ASA physical status I–III, scheduled to undergo elective surgery under general anesthesia, and had fasted at least 8 h. The minimum required sample size was 68 participants; to account for potential losses, 39 participants were included in each group (see Statistical Analysis). Participants were excluded if they were younger than 40 years or older than 65 years, had a BMI ≥ 40 kg/m², were pregnant, had an ASA physical status of IV or higher, or declined participation. Participants with renal or hepatic failure, sepsis, diabetic ketoacidosis, hiatal hernia, or a history of abdominal surgery were also excluded. Participants with hiatal hernia were excluded because this condition independently affects antral ultrasound assessment and may confound gastric volume estimation irrespective of diabetes status. Exclusion was based on documented prior clinical diagnosis; therefore, asymptomatic or previously undiagnosed cases could not be fully excluded, which is acknowledged as a limitation. In addition, patients undergoing emergency or gastrointestinal procedures and those using opioids, proton pump inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, or other medications known to affect gastrointestinal motility were excluded.

Data collection

Demographic characteristics, comorbidities, fasting duration, and diabetes status were recorded for all participants in the preoperative area. All participants were prospectively screened for eligibility according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Participants without a prior diagnosis of diabetes mellitus who met all study criteria were assigned to the control group (Group C). Control participants presented with normal fasting blood glucose values on preoperative assessment, reducing the likelihood of undiagnosed diabetes. However, as HbA1c was not routinely measured in this group, undiagnosed prediabetes could not be fully excluded. For participants in the diabetes group (Group D), diabetes duration, antidiabetic medications, fasting blood glucose (FBG), postprandial plasma glucose (PPG), HbA1c values, and the presence of previously diagnosed diabetes-related complications were recorded. The presence of previously diagnosed diabetes-related complications, including neuropathy, was ascertained from pre-existing clinical diagnoses documented in participants’ medical records. No additional neurological examination, nerve conduction study, or standardised screening instrument (e.g., Michigan Neuropathy Screening Instrument) was administered as part of this study. Patients with a prior clinical diagnosis of gastroparesis were excluded from participation; therefore, gastric ultrasound findings reflect subclinical or unrecognised delayed gastric emptying rather than clinically established gastroparesis. All participants in the diabetes group had type 2 diabetes mellitus. No participant in the diabetes group was receiving a GLP-1 receptor agonist at the time of study enrollment, consistent with the prespecified exclusion criteria. Preoperative anxiety was assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) before the ultrasound examination, and the results were documented. Fasting duration was determined primarily from patient reports during the preoperative assessment and cross-checked against medical records when available. The reported fasting duration referred to the time since any oral intake, including clear liquids.

Gastric ultrasound assessment

Participants were examined in the supine position using a low-frequency (2–5 MHz) convex probe (Esaote MyLab310). The probe was placed in a parasagittal orientation in the epigastrium to identify the gastric antrum, using the liver and abdominal aorta as anatomical landmarks. The imaging depth was adjusted to visualize the posterior vessel borders or vertebral bodies. The gastric antrum was then scanned by sliding the probe transversely to assess and document the nature of the gastric contents, which were categorized as solid, thick, or clear fluid.

Following the initial assessment, participants were repositioned into the right lateral decubitus (RLD) position, and gastric ultrasonography was repeated. In the RLD position, the gastric antrum was reevaluated using the same anatomical landmarks, and the nature of the gastric contents (empty, clear fluid, thick fluid, or solid) was recorded. Gastric content was graded using the Perlas grading system, with an additional qualitative classification for thick fluid or solid content. For clarity, solid or thick fluid content was classified as high risk/full stomach. In this system, grade 0 indicates an empty antrum in both positions; grade 1 indicates clear fluid visible only in the RLD position; grade 2 indicates clear fluid present in both the supine and RLD positions; and grade 3 indicates the presence of thick fluid or solid material. The antral CSA was measured at the level of the aorta using a standardized approach. Gastric volume was subsequently estimated using a previously validated formula [Volume (mL) = 27.0 + (14.6 × RLD CSA) − 1.28 × age (years)] [11]. For risk stratification, the stomach was considered empty when no gastric contents were visualized or when the estimated clear fluid volume was < 1.5 mL/kg. Conversely, the presence of solid content or clear fluid exceeding this threshold was classified as a full stomach. Participants were subsequently categorized into low- and high-risk aspiration groups according to their gastric fullness status. High aspiration risk (full stomach) was defined using ultrasound-based surrogate markers, namely the presence of solid or thick fluid gastric content (Perlas grade 3) or an estimated clear fluid volume > 1.5 mL/kg, rather than clinically observed aspiration events [6, 11]. All ultrasound examinations were performed by the same investigator (M.G.B.), who had prior experience with at least 50 gastric ultrasound assessments. To ensure partial blinding, all clinical and diabetes-related data (including HbA1c level, medications, and complications) were collected and recorded by a separate investigator who was not involved in the ultrasound assessments. The sonographer performed all gastric ultrasound examinations before induction of anesthesia and did not have access to participants’ medical records or study forms at the bedside.

Anesthesia management and gastric volume and ph measurement

Standard monitoring, including pulse oximetry, ECG, non-invasive blood pressure, temperature, and bispectral index (BIS), was established for all patients. Following preoxygenation (6 L/min, 80% O2), anesthesia was induced with lidocaine (1 mg/kg), propofol (2–3 mg/kg), and fentanyl (1 µg/kg). Muscle relaxation was achieved with rocuronium bromide (0.6 mg/kg), and endotracheal intubation was performed after 2 min. In participants identified as having a full stomach on gastric ultrasonography, rapid sequence induction (RSI) was performed using rocuronium bromide (1–1.2 mg/kg) and the Sellick maneuver. Following intubation, a 14 F nasogastric tube was inserted. Correct tube placement was confirmed by auscultation and aspiration of gastric contents. Gastric contents were aspirated manually with repeated suction attempts until no additional fluid could be obtained. Participants were maintained in the supine position during aspiration. However, no additional imaging confirmation (e.g., ultrasound verification of antral collapse) was performed. The volume and pH of aspirate, measured using a digital pH meter, were recorded. Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane, adjusted to 1 MAC and BIS < 60, and remifentanil infusion (0.05–0.2 µg/kg/min). Postoperative complications, including hypertension, hypotension, tachycardia, bradycardia, agitation, confusion, dizziness, nausea, and vomiting, were also recorded.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the prevalence of a full stomach as defined by gastric ultrasonography findings. Secondary outcomes included estimated gastric volume, aspirated gastric volume, and pH, as well as exploratory analyses examining associations between ultrasound parameters and clinical variables.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was estimated using G*Power software. Assuming an alpha level of 0.05, 80% power, and an effect size of d = 0.50 based on between-group differences in gastric volume reported by Sabry et al. [8], the minimum required total sample size was 68 participants. As the sample-size calculation was therefore based on a continuous secondary outcome (gastric volume) rather than the primary binary outcome (full-stomach prevalence), analyses of full-stomach prevalence and within-diabetes subgroup comparisons should be considered exploratory. To account for potential losses, 39 participants were included in each group. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 20.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), non-normally distributed continuous variables as median (minimum–maximum), and categorical variables as number (%). Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t-test for normally distributed continuous variables (with Welch’s correction applied when Levene’s test indicated unequal variances) and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Fisher’s exact test was used when expected cell counts were small. Associations between continuous variables were assessed using Spearman correlation analysis. An internal exploratory ROC analysis was performed to describe the relationship between CSA and ultrasound-defined high aspiration risk; as the outcome was partly derived from CSA, the resulting threshold is descriptive and should not be interpreted as an independently validated diagnostic cutoff. The Youden index was used to identify the optimal descriptive threshold. No imputation was performed, and all analyses were based on the available data. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Given the exploratory nature of the secondary analyses, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied, and secondary analyses were interpreted as hypothesis-generating. As an exploratory sensitivity analysis, linear regression models adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and fasting duration were used to evaluate the independent association between diabetes status and antral CSA and estimated gastric volume.

No significant difference in fasting duration was observed between the groups (Group C: 9.82 ± 0.56 h; Group D: 10.00 ± 1.43 h; p = 0.10). The observed fasting durations exceeded the minimum recommended by current preoperative fasting guidelines (Table 1).

No statistically significant differences were observed between the groups in Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) scores. The median anxiety scores were 4.00 (range 0–16) in the control group and 5.00 (range 0–15) in the diabetes group (p = 0.344). Depression scores were 5.00 (range 0–11) and 3.00 (range 0–15), respectively (p = 0.059).

Statistically significant between-group differences were observed for all gastric parameters. The Perlas score distribution differed significantly between the groups (p < 0.001). All participants in the control group were classified as grade 0, whereas higher grades were observed only in the diabetes group. The mean antral CSA was significantly higher in the diabetes group than in the control group (10.91 ± 9.49 vs. 5.11 ± 2.01 cm², p < 0.001). Similarly, the estimated gastric volume was significantly greater in the diabetes group (94.21 ± 110.65 vs. 41.04 ± 29.78 mL, p = 0.008). Aspirated gastric fluid volume was also significantly higher in the diabetes group (19.67 ± 14.49 vs. 8.69 ± 5.68 mL, p < 0.001). In addition, gastric pH was higher in the diabetes group than in the control group (2.56 ± 0.54 vs. 2.14 ± 0.85, p = 0.011) (Table 2).

Among participants in the diabetes group, the mean FBG level was 142.20 ± 36.38 mg/dL, the mean HbA1c level was 7.78 ± 1.51%, and the mean PPG level was 247.12 ± 53.09 mg/dL. Regarding diabetes-related complications, most participants (82.05%, n = 32) had no complications, whereas neuropathy was observed in 17.94% (n = 7).

Full stomach was observed in 7/39 participants with diabetes (17.90%; exact 95% CI: 7.5%–33.5%) and 0/39 controls (0%; exact 95% CI: 0.0%–9.0%), corresponding to a risk difference of 17.9% points (95% CI: 5.3–32.7; Fisher’s exact p = 0.012). Of the 39 diabetic participants, 32 (82.10%) were classified as having low aspiration risk and 7 (17.9%) as having high aspiration risk. The Perlas grade distribution differed markedly between the groups. All participants in the control group were classified as Perlas grade 0. In the diabetes group, 23 participants (59.00%) were classified as grade 0, 11 (28.20%) as grade 1, three (7.70%) as grade 2, and two (5.10%) as grade 3. Among the seven participants with diabetes classified as having a full stomach, two were categorized as high-risk because of grade 3 findings indicating solid or thick fluid content, whereas the remaining five were classified as high-risk because the estimated clear fluid volume exceeded 1.5 mL/kg. When groups were compared in terms of aspiration risk, diabetes-related complications, aspirated gastric fluid volume, gastric volume, and CSA were significantly higher in high-risk participants. No significant differences in HADS scores were observed between low- and high-risk participants with diabetes mellitus (Table 3).

An internal exploratory ROC analysis was performed to describe the relationship between CSA and ultrasound-defined high aspiration risk (Fig. 2). The AUC for CSA was 0.862 (95% CI: 0.728–0.995, estimated using the DeLong method; p < 0.001). A descriptive threshold of ≥ 10 cm² was identified using the Youden index, at or above which participants were more likely to be classified as high-risk, with a sensitivity of 85.71% and specificity of 81.25% (Table 4). However, as the outcome was partly derived from CSA, and given the small number of events (n = 7), these findings should be considered descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than indicative of independently validated diagnostic performance.

No statistically significant difference in HbA1c levels was observed between patients with low and high aspiration risk (7.57 ± 1.29 vs. 8.74 ± 2.13; p = 0.065; Table 3).

Antidiabetic medication type did not differ significantly between aspiration risk groups (p = 0.236; Table 3).

Spearman correlation analysis revealed no significant association between HbA1c levels and either gastric volume (r = 0.063, p = 0.702) or gastric fluid pH (r = − 0.158, p = 0.337). No significant association was found between the duration of diabetes mellitus and CSA, gastric volume, aspirated gastric fluid volume, or gastric fluid pH. Similarly, no significant association was observed between gastric volume and gastric fluid pH. In contrast, a strong positive correlation was identified between gastric volume and aspirated gastric fluid volume (r = 0.693, p < 0.001; Table 5).

In the exploratory adjusted sensitivity analysis, diabetes status remained an independent predictor of both antral CSA (β = 6.159, 95% CI: 2.986–9.332; p < 0.001) and estimated gastric volume (β = 54.967, 95% CI: 18.078–91.855; p = 0.004) after adjustment for age, sex, BMI, and fasting duration (Table 6). However, the models explained only 17–20% of the total variance (R² = 0.172–0.198), and these findings should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size.

Discussion

In this prospective observational study, patients with diabetes exhibited significantly greater residual gastric volumes, antral CSA, and Perlas scores than control participants despite similar fasting durations. These findings suggest that standard preoperative fasting protocols may not reliably ensure gastric emptying in all patients with diabetes, though this interpretation should be considered in the context of the observational design and relatively small sample size. Notably, the present study complemented ultrasonographic estimates with objective post-induction nasogastric aspirate measurements, and ultrasound-defined full stomach, as a surrogate marker of aspiration risk, was identified exclusively in the diabetes group.

Diabetes mellitus is associated with delayed gastric emptying, primarily as a result of autonomic dysfunction, and may increase the risk of perioperative aspiration-related complications [12]. Published data indicate a higher prevalence of residual gastric contents among patients with diabetes, although findings vary across populations and study designs [13, 14]. Furthermore, standard fasting intervals may not consistently guarantee gastric emptying, as demonstrated in both diabetes and non-diabetes populations [15]. In the present study, all participants with an ultrasound-defined full stomach were in the diabetes group, supporting the notion that fasting guidelines alone may be insufficient in this population. However, aspiration risk was defined using ultrasound-based surrogate markers rather than clinically observed aspiration events. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as reflecting the preoperative risk stratification rather than the actual incidence of perioperative aspiration.

Existing evidence demonstrates that antral CSA is influenced by factors such as BMI and diabetes status, with higher values reported in obese and diabetic populations [16–18]. In the present study, the mean CSA was greater in patients with diabetes than in previously reported cohorts. This difference may be attributable to the relatively higher mean BMI and potential differences in disease characteristics, including diabetes duration, within our study population.

The literature reports variable findings regarding gastric volume in patients with diabetes following preoperative fasting. Garg et al. [14] reported relatively low fasting gastric volumes in patients with and without diabetes, whereas other studies identified clinically relevant residual volumes in a subset of patients, the majority of whom had diabetes [19]. In the present study, the mean gastric volume in the diabetes group was 94.21 mL, and increased to a mean of 234.81 mL in patients classified as high risk. Notably, aspiration of volumes as low as 50–70 mL has been associated with aspiration pneumonitis in adults [20–25]. These findings suggest that the gastric volumes observed in our cohort may be clinically relevant. Differences in patient characteristics, including BMI and diabetes-related factors, may partially explain these differences.

In addition to ultrasonographic estimation, nasogastric aspiration has been used to quantify residual gastric volume [26, 27], with comparable aspiration volumes reported between patients with and without diabetes, although higher volumes were observed in subgroups with poor glycemic control [27]. In the present study, aspirated gastric volume was significantly higher in the diabetes group than in the control group (19.67 mL). Furthermore, among patients classified as high risk, a strong positive correlation was observed between ultrasound-derived gastric volume and aspirated volume. These findings support the complementary roles of ultrasonography and direct aspiration in assessing gastric contents. However, nasogastric aspiration is known to underestimate true gastric volume because of several technical factors; therefore, it should not be regarded as a reference standard.

Gastric acid secretion is known to be reduced in patients with diabetes, likely because of autonomic dysfunction and delayed gastric emptying [28–30]. Previous studies have reported higher gastric pH values in patients with diabetes than in those without, although findings regarding its association with glycemic control have been inconsistent [29–31]. In the present study, gastric pH was higher in the diabetes group (2.56) than in the control group (2.14), consistent with previous reports. This observation may be related to diabetes-associated alterations in gastric physiology, including impaired acid secretion and delayed gastric emptying.

Factors associated with delayed gastric content and volume have also been evaluated to minimize aspiration risk in patients with diabetes [32]. Demirel et al. [33] reported that age, BMI, fasting duration, and CSA were significantly greater in patients with a full stomach (p < 0.001) and that neuropathy and longer diabetes duration (> 8 years) were associated with an increased likelihood of a full stomach. In the present study, demographic characteristics, diabetes duration, antidiabetic medications, fasting duration, and HbA1c levels did not differ significantly between patients with diabetes who had high and low ultrasound-defined aspiration risk. However, diabetic neuropathy was more prevalent among high-risk patients, who also had greater aspirated gastric fluid volume and larger CSAs. It should be noted that neuropathy status was ascertained from pre-existing clinical diagnoses documented in participants’ medical records rather than through formal neurological assessment or standardised screening instruments; therefore, the observed association between neuropathy and high aspiration risk should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. ROC curve analysis also indicated that a CSA threshold ≥ 10 cm² had discriminatory value for ultrasound-defined high aspiration risk in this cohort. However, because only seven patients with diabetes met the criteria for a full stomach, robust multivariate analysis was not feasible. Accordingly, these findings should be considered preliminary, and the proposed CSA threshold should not be interpreted as a clinically validated decision threshold for routine perioperative practice until it is confirmed in larger studies with external validation. An exploratory adjusted regression analysis further suggested that the association between diabetes and greater antral CSA and gastric volume persisted after controlling for age, sex, BMI, and fasting duration, although the limited explanatory power of the models and the small sample size preclude definitive conclusions. A methodological limitation of the present study should be acknowledged regarding the ROC analysis. Because ultrasound-defined high aspiration risk included estimated gastric volume > 1.5 mL/kg, and estimated gastric volume was calculated from antral CSA, the ROC analysis of CSA for high-risk classification is partially circular. Therefore, the ROC-derived cutoff values should be interpreted as internal descriptive thresholds rather than independently validated diagnostic cutoffs. Future studies employing an independent reference standard for aspiration risk would be needed to externally validate the discriminatory performance of CSA.

These findings should be interpreted alongside those of Sastre et al. [10], who demonstrated in a bicentric prospective study of 289 patients that solid gastric residue was significantly more prevalent only in diabetic patients with confirmed dysautonomia compared with controls (12% vs. 3.5%; p = 0.03), with no significant difference in those without dysautonomia. Unlike Sastre et al., the present study incorporated post-induction nasogastric aspirate measurements and gastric pH assessment, providing complementary objective data not reported in their study. However, as formal autonomic testing was not performed in our cohort, it remains unclear whether the elevated gastric volumes observed were confined to participants with subclinical dysautonomia.

Previous studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding the association between HbA1c levels and residual gastric volume, with some reporting no association and others suggesting a possible relationship with poor glycemic control (HbA1c > 7) [34, 35]. In the present study, no significant correlation was observed between HbA1c level and gastric volume (r = 0.063, p = 0.702), or diabetes duration and gastric parameters. No statistically significant association was identified between glycemic control and ultrasound-defined aspiration risk. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted with caution and should be considered exploratory rather than indicative of causal relationships. These findings suggest that HbA1c alone may not be a reliable predictor of residual gastric volume in patients with type 2 diabetes scheduled for elective surgery.

As anxiety and depression scores did not differ significantly between groups or between aspiration risk subgroups, the observed differences in gastric parameters are unlikely to be attributable to preoperative psychological stress.

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, complete blinding of the sonographer to diabetic status may not have been fully achievable in a real-world clinical setting, potentially introducing measurement bias. Additionally, because all ultrasound examinations were performed by a single investigator and no interobserver reliability assessment was conducted, measurement reproducibility could not be evaluated. Second, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size. Multicenter studies with larger and more diverse populations are needed to validate and extend these findings. In particular, only seven patients with diabetes met the predefined full-stomach criterion, substantially limiting the precision of subgroup comparisons and precluding robust multivariable analysis. Another important limitation was the highly selective nature of the study population. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were designed to improve internal validity and minimize potential confounding factors. However, these criteria may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader perioperative populations commonly encountered in routine clinical practice. Furthermore, hiatal hernia exclusion was based on prior clinical diagnosis; asymptomatic or undiagnosed cases could not be fully excluded. The lower age limit of 40 years was selected to enrich the study population for patients with established type 2 diabetes and a sufficient disease duration for clinically relevant autonomic dysfunction, consistent with published evidence that autonomic neuropathy prevalence increases substantially in T2DM patients aged 40 years and above [36, 37]. However, as no validated biological threshold supports this specific cut-off, this criterion represents a source of selection bias and limits the generalisability of findings to younger adult patients with type 2 diabetes. Furthermore, diabetic neuropathy was ascertained from pre-existing clinical diagnoses documented in medical records rather than through standardised prospective assessment tools. This approach may have led to underdiagnosis of neuropathy in some participants, potentially attenuating the observed association between neuropathy and high aspiration risk. The present results may not be directly applicable to several patients, including individuals with morbid obesity, type 1 diabetes, emergency surgery, or gastrointestinal procedures, or those using opioids, proton pump inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists or other medications that may affect gastric motility. The trial was retrospectively registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (first posted: January 8, 2024), as enrollment had commenced prior to registration (October 15, 2022), which is acknowledged as a limitation. Finally, fasting durations exceeded the minimum recommended by the current guidelines and were determined primarily from patient self-reports, which were cross-checked against medical records. Consequently, further studies are warranted to evaluate the prevalence of a full stomach among patients who have fasted for the minimum recommended duration before surgery.

Conclusion

In this study, patients with diabetes had greater antral CSA, gastric volume, and aspirated gastric fluid volume than control participants despite similar fasting durations. A full stomach was identified exclusively in the diabetes group, and diabetic neuropathy was more prevalent among patients classified as ultrasound-defined high aspiration risk compared with low-risk patients (57.1% vs. 9.4%; p = 0.012), though this finding should be interpreted with caution given the small number of high-risk cases (n = 7). No significant association was observed between diabetes duration and gastric parameters. No significant correlation was observed between HbA1c levels and gastric volume, though the study was not specifically powered to detect small associations between glycemic control markers and gastric parameters, and glycemic control was not significantly associated with ultrasound-defined aspiration risk. These findings suggest an association between diabetes and increased ultrasound-estimated gastric volume despite fasting, but these results should be interpreted with caution given the observational design and small sample size. Larger adjusted studies are needed to confirm whether diabetes-related factors independently predict full stomach, accounting for potential confounders such as BMI and comorbidities.
