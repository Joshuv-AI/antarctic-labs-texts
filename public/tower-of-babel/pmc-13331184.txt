Frontobasal bridging vein rerouting for microsurgical clipping of a posterior cerebral artery aneurysm through anterior temporal approach: A technical note
Authors: Masafumi Segawa, Tomohiro Inoue, Ryotaro Mizuno, Tosai Sho, Mao Yamamoto, Yoshinobu Iwaki, Hiroshi Matsufuji, Sho Tsunoda, Ryuichi Noda, Mariko Kawashima, Atsuya Akabane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Posterior cerebral artery (PCA) aneurysms are extremely rare and challenging to treat because of their deep location and complex morphology. We describe a case of an enlarging thrombosed PCA aneurysm successfully treated with direct neck clipping through an anterior temporal approach (ATA) incorporating frontobasal bridging vein (FBBV) sacrifice and venous rerouting.Methods: A 48-year-old man was incidentally found to have a right PCA aneurysm during routine magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Over an 8-year period, the aneurysm progressively enlarged and developed perifocal edema in the right cerebral peduncle, prompting surgical intervention. Direct neck clipping was performed using the ATA with FBBV sacrifice and venous rerouting through side-to-side anastomosis between sylvian veins to maintain venous drainage and secure adequate exposure.Results: Postoperative MRI demonstrated no infarction or venous congestion, and angiography confirmed complete aneurysm obliteration with preserved right PCA patency and a reconstructed venous drainage route. The patient was discharged with a modified Rankin Scale score of 0 and remained symptom-free at 3-year follow-up.Conclusion: Direct neck clipping of a thrombosed PCA aneurysm through the ATA with FBBV sacrifice and venous rerouting was safe and effective. The sphenoparietal sinus transposition technique, combined with venous rerouting, provided optimal surgical exposure while minimizing the risk of complications.

INTRODUCTION

Posterior cerebral artery (PCA) aneurysms account for only 0.7–2.3% of all cerebral aneurysms.[2,5,12] Most of these lesions occur in the P1–P2 segments of the Zeal and Rhoton classification, with 40–60% occurring in the P2 segment.[16] PCA aneurysms are challenging to treat because of their deep location near the brainstem and their tendency to be large or dissecting. Surgical approaches, including the transsylvian fissure, subtemporal, and occipital interhemispheric approaches, should be tailored to the aneurysm’s location.

Here, we report a case of successful neck clipping of a thrombosed PCA aneurysm through an anterior temporal approach (ATA) with frontobasal bridging vein (FBBV) sacrifice and venous rerouting. An edited high-definition video with detailed subtitles is provided to illustrate this technique and its nuances [Video 1].

CLINICAL PRESENTATION

A 48-year-old man presented with an incidental aneurysm in the right PCA during routine follow-up magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which had enlarged over 8 years. MRI revealed worsening cerebral perifocal edema in the right cerebral peduncle around the aneurysm, indicating the need for surgical treatment [Figure 1a]. Preoperative vertebral angiography revealed a saccular aneurysm on the right P2a segment with mild arterial stenosis near the proximal neck of the aneurysm [Figure 1b]. Preoperative gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted MRI showed a large, partially thrombosed P2a aneurysm (3.5 × 10 mm) with a “bull’s eye” appearance in the right cerebral peduncle [Figure 1c]. The aneurysm was positioned relatively high, 11 mm above the interclinoid line, with the aneurysmal dome protruding posteriorly, complicating identification of the perforating PCA branch. The eccentric thrombus did not involve the aneurysmal neck, making it suitable for direct clipping. Given the presence of mass effect and the desire for decompression, we favored direct microsurgical clipping over endovascular flow diversion. In addition, flow-diverter placement would have required dual antiplatelet therapy and would not have promptly relieved mass effect. Bypass with parent-vessel trapping was considered as a contingency; therefore, the superficial temporal artery was preserved within the skin flap to allow a possible extracranial–intracranial bypass if intraoperative assessment had shown the parent vessel to be unsuitable for preservation.

The patient was positioned supine with the head elevated 15° and turned 30° to the left, secured in a Mayfield skull clamp. A standard frontotemporal skin incision was made, and the skin flap was reflected in a two-layer fashion. The temporalis muscle was detached and retracted posteriorly, exposing the temporal side of the sphenoid wing. A standard frontotemporal craniotomy with partial zygomectomy was performed because of the patient’s high neck position [Figure 1d]. The medial sphenoid ridge and temporal base wing were flattened extradurally to the level of the lateral rim of the superior orbital fissure using a high-speed drill. The meningo-orbital band was coagulated and cut, and the outer dural layer was lifted backward to expose the anterior clinoid process. Extradural anterior clinoidectomy was accomplished by drilling the superior wall of the superior orbital fissure, optic nerve canal, and optic strut. The dura was then opened and reflected at its base.

Under high-magnification microscopy, the Sylvian fissure (SF) was meticulously split. The frontal superficial Sylvian vein (fSSV) and temporal superficial Sylvian vein (tSSV) converged immediately before entering the sphenoparietal sinus (SPS), with the FBBV merging with the fSSV near the base of the frontal lobe. These veins entered the SPS at a low position on the sphenoid ridge, hindering the surgical corridor to the highly positioned P2 aneurysmal neck [Figure 2a]. A tough arachnoid membrane was incised from the distal portion of the SF for a wide opening. After confirming the presence of the internal carotid artery, the dura mater was incised toward the outer edge of the optic canal and split.

The FBBV merged with the fSSV just before entering the SPS, and this confluence was located near the lateral rim of the superior orbital fissure, where the SPS entered the cavernous sinus. The short bridging segments of the FBBV and tSSV limited temporal lobe retraction, restricting lateral retrocarotid space widening. The FBBV appeared too large to sacrifice, and its cisternal portion was too short to allow adequate frontal lobe retraction to visualize the P2a segment. Attempts to retract the temporal lobe and the SPS posteriorly resulted in partial tearing of the FBBV wall and venous bleeding, making further retraction of the temporal lobe difficult [Figure 2b]. Hemostasis was achieved using a single 10-0 nylon stitch in the injured FBBV. Therefore, to enable greater posterior traction of the FBBV, a venous outflow tract was created from the base of the frontal lobe [Figure 2c]. The tSSV and fSSV distal to the FBBV junction were freed from the arachnoid to allow temporary clipping for side-to-side anastomosis. After temporary clamping and venotomy for bypass preparation, the internal lumen was flushed with heparinized saline, and the outer membranes were stained with methylrosaniline chloride (pyoctaninum blue). The first stitch pierced the wall from the outside to the inside, then from the inside to the outside; thereafter, the running sutures were continued to approximate the back wall using 10-0 nylon. The final stitch pierced the wall from the inside to the outside; thus, both ends of the back-wall running suture thread were outside. Next, stay stitches were added at both ends [Figure 2d]. Each stay suture was tied using a continuous back-wall suture thread. Subsequently, the anterior wall was sutured using interrupted stitches. After completing the anastomosis, vascular patency was confirmed using a microvascular Doppler flowmeter and indocyanine green (ICG) videoangiography.

The fSSV was cut just proximal to the SPS confluence to preserve venous outflow from the FBBV to the fSSV. The temporal uncus was retracted posteriorly using an ATA, exposing the PCA P1-P2 segments and the aneurysm in the carotid-oculomotor triangle. Neck clipping was successfully performed through the widened retrocarotid space. However, owing to the high location of the aneurysm, the dome of the thrombosed aneurysm could not be fully visualized during thrombectomy.

Postoperative MRI showed no infarction or venous congestion [Figure 3a], and angiography confirmed complete obliteration of the aneurysm with preserved patency of the right PCA and the reconstructed venous route [Figure 3b and c]. The patient was discharged with a modified Rankin scale score of 0. No evidence of aneurysmal recurrence was observed over a 3-year outpatient follow-up period, and the patient has remained symptom-free.

DISCUSSION

PCA aneurysms are relatively rare and among the most challenging aneurysms to treat. Approximately 50% of PCA aneurysms are larger than 10 mm, with an increased propensity for enlargement at the P1-P2 junction and P2 segment.[11] The transsylvian fissure approach (TSA), also known as the subtemporal approach, is often used for PCA aneurysms involving the P1 and P2a segments. The ATA, a modification of the TSA, was initially developed for midline lesions such as distal basilar artery aneurysms. In this approach, posterior retraction of the temporal uncus allows lateral visualization of the posterior segment of the internal carotid artery. The ATA offers excellent exposure of the interpeduncular and crural cisterns, P1, P2a, and the medial posterior choroidal artery; however, P2p remains difficult to access. In the present case, we adopted the ATA to achieve adequate exposure up to the P2a segment.

The FBBV is a small vein draining from the base of the frontal lobe into the SPS. It can drain into the SPS as a single trunk or as a common stem of the insular vein (common vertical trunk). To obtain a clear surgical view, sacrificing small FBBVs may sometimes be necessary. Sacrificing intracranial veins, such as FBBVs, should be avoided whenever possible because of the potential for serious neurological complications such as venous infarction or contusion.[6,8,14,15] Excessive retraction of the frontal lobe in conjunction with sacrificing a prominent FBBV could lead to venous complications at the frontal base.[4]

Intraoperative ICG video angiography and Doppler ultrasonography should be used to evaluate venous flow distal to a sacrificed vein. If normal perfusion congestion is observed, venous reconstruction or rerouting should be actively considered.[3,10,13] Preservation of the FBBV cannot be reliably determined based solely on preoperative imaging. The basal vein of Rosenthal classification predicts the pattern of the deep Sylvian venous system, including the insular veins and FBBV,[1] and may help anticipate the difficulty of preserving these veins.

The length and position of bridging veins affect temporal lobe mobility and lateral retrocarotid space widening, particularly near the sphenoid wing.[9] The SPS transposition technique was performed to displace the bridging segments of the tSSV and temporopolar vein at the dural entrance, allowing safe temporal lobe retraction. In the present case, transection of the FBBV with venous sacrifice and rerouting further facilitated wider lateral retraction of the temporal lobe without causing venous congestion. Our approach involved performing an anterior clinoidectomy to extend the dural incision near the internal carotid artery, enabling posterior displacement of the dural entrance of the bridging veins and widening of the retrocarotid surgical space.

Thrombosed aneurysms can be classified into six types based on thrombus and lumen morphology. Most thrombotic aneurysms that cannot be directly clipped may benefit from alternative strategies, such as bypass and aneurysm occlusion.[7] However, direct neck clipping, with or without thrombectomy, remains the most effective method for treating thrombosed aneurysms (type 2). In the present case, the thrombus was located away from the aneurysmal neck, allowing direct clipping of the aneurysm.

CONCLUSION

Direct neck clipping of a thrombosed PCA aneurysm through the ATA, combined with FBBV sacrifice, venous rerouting, and SPS transposition, was extremely effective. This approach enabled optimal securing of the surgical field laterally, accommodating clipping, and avoiding perioperative complications, such as venous congestion.
