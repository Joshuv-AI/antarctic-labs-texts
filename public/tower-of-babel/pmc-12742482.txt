Comparison of multimodal return-to-sport testing and force plate-based balance testing among anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction patients
Authors: Dhruv S. Shankar, Heather A. Milton, Ciara Ostrander, Luilly Vargas, Kirk A. Campbell, Guillem Gonzalez-Lomas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The purpose of our study was to identify force plate-derived balance metrics that were predictive of multimodal return-to-sport test findings in a sample of anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction patients.Methods:We conducted a cross-sectional study of anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction patients who were at least 6 months postoperative. Subjects completed our multimodal Institutional RTS testing battery which included testing of hip and knee range of motion (ROM), isokinetic quadriceps strength, and core trunk muscle strength (Bunkie test), as well as balance testing on a force plate. Balance metrics predictive of Institutional RTS test findings for the involved leg were identified using multivariable regression with stepwise selection. Model significance was assessed at α = 0.002.Results:Sixteen subjects (7M, 9F) were enrolled in our study with a median age of 29 years (range 20–47). Multivariate multiscale entropy (MMSExyz) of the involved leg was predictive of knee flexion active ROM (P = .02) and Bunkie stabilizing test score (P = .03), MMSExyz of the uninvolved leg was predictive of Bunkie lateral and medial test scores (P = .02 and P = .02), and interlimb symmetry of MMSExyz was predictive of triple jump test score (P = .007). Interlimb symmetry of the COPx shift balance metric was predictive of hip internal rotation active and passive ROM (P = .008 and P = .03) and step-down test score (P = .004).Conclusion:Force plate-based balance testing metrics are predictive of some return-to-sport test findings including knee flexion ROM and Bunkie test scores, but further analysis is needed to identify balance metrics predictive of quadriceps isokinetic testing and muscle flexibility testing results.

INTRODUCTION

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) ruptures are among the most common orthopedic injuries observed in the athletic population, with an estimated incidence of 68.6 per 100,000 person-years in the United States.1 Although return-to-sport (RTS) rates following ACL reconstruction (ACLR) surgery are generally high,2,3 ACL reinjuries may affect up to 35% of younger athletes.4 Consequently, many sports medicine clinics offer RTS testing, which typically refers to a sequence or “battery” of physical assessments conducted under the supervision of a physical therapist and/or exercise physiologist to gauge the stability and function of the operated knee, determine readiness to RTS, and reduce the risk of ACL reinjury upon RTS.

Although the multimodal nature of RTS testing is intended to provide a comprehensive assessment of knee function and physical performance, this also creates significant limitations in that specialized personnel and equipment are often needed to perform all the components of the testing battery. Force plate technology has been investigated as a novel tool for RTS testing among ACLR patients5 and may provide a simpler alternative to some of the more cumbersome aspects of conventional RTS testing. Force plates may have particular applications to balance testing,6 which is a crucial component of the RTS assessment since deficits in postural control following ACL injury may negatively affect performance across a wide range of sports.7

The purpose of our study was to compare RTS testing using a multimodal Institutional RTS Assessment Protocol (IRTS) vs. a force plate-based balance testing protocol among ACLR patients who were at least 6 months postoperative. We aimed to identify which balance testing metrics were significantly predictive of IRTS assessment findings. We hypothesized that Bunkie test results would be predicted by force plate balance metrics since the Bunkie tests assess the strength of core trunk muscles which are critical to maintaining balance.

Methods

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the New York University Langone Health institutional review board (New York, NY) on January 31, 2020 (approval # 19-01832).

Subject consent

All subjects gave their informed consent for participation in the research study.

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional study to identify force plate balance testing metrics that were predictive of IRTS testing findings (eg, isokinetic testing results, knee range of motion measurements) in a sample of ACLR patients at a single point in time.

Subject recruitment

We recruited patients who underwent primary ACL reconstruction with 1 of 5 sports fellowship-trained orthopedic surgeons at a single urban academic medical center and were at least 6 months postoperative. This time cutoff was used since our center's ACLR rehabilitation protocol permits RTS sport-specific training at 6-month follow-up at the earliest with the goal of return to competitive play between 8 and 12 months. Subjects were included if they were age 18–60 years at the time of enrollment and if they were cleared by their surgeon to participate in RTS testing. Subjects were excluded if they were less than 6 months out from surgery, were pregnant, or had experienced a multiligamentous knee injury. Upon enrollment, subjects were scheduled at our institution's sports rehabilitation clinic for a 2-hour RTS assessment consisting of the IRTS assessment and a force plate-based balance assessment. Since fatigue has been reported to impact performance on RTS testing among ACLR patients,8 the order of the 2 assessments was randomized for each patient. A computer program was used to randomize subjects 1:1 to undergo either (1) force plate testing followed by IRTS or (2) IRTS followed by force plate testing.

Study procedures

Demographic data collection

Demographic data including age, sex, body weight, height, and body mass index (BMI) were recorded at the time of RTS testing. Perioperative data were abstracted from electronic medical records and included American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification, preoperative Kellgren-Lawrence grade of knee osteoarthritis, ACL graft type, and concomitant procedures (eg, meniscus repair).

Postoperative rehabilitation protocol

Subjects completed a 4-phase postoperative rehabilitation protocol. During phase I (weeks 0–4), subjects were weight bearing as tolerated with crutches and are placed in a hinged knee brace locked in full extension for the 1st week and unlocked for ambulation in the subsequent weeks. Subjects could discontinue brace use as soon as they were able to achieve a straight leg raise without extensor lag. During phase II (weeks 4–6), subjects progressed to mild strengthening, balance, and flexion range of motion (ROM) exercises. During phase III (weeks 6–16), subjects were fully weight bearing and were allowed full ROM as tolerated. They participated in closed chain strengthening exercises and could begin straight-ahead running at 12 weeks. During phase IV (months 4–6), subjects could participate in sports-specific drills. Subjects could gradually RTS at 6–8 months per the surgeon's evaluation and RTS testing results, with the goal of return to full competitive play at a minimum of 8 months that was influenced by graft choice and type of sport. Subjects who underwent a concomitant meniscus repair followed a modified protocol for phases I and II in which they were partial weight bearing instead of weight bearing as tolerated.

Institutional return-to-sport assessment

The IRTS assessment included measures that are known to be contributors of dynamic knee valgus and/or ACL stress, including hip and knee joint range of motion, knee stability, manual muscle testing of the hip stabilizers, isokinetic quadriceps and hamstring strength, dynamic lumbopelvic stability, hop testing, core stability, and drop jump analysis were completed by each subject according to evidence-based protocols.9–15 Each test was completed under instruction from 1 of 2 American College of Sports Medicine–certified clinical exercise physiologists. Force plate balance testing

Balance testing was performed on all subjects using the Sparta Science model SSFP01 force plate (Sparta Science, Menlo Park, CA). Subjects were first guided through a standard warmup and thorough overview of the testing procedure. Subjects were asked to stand on the force plate with weight evenly distributed on both feet. When prompted by the software system, subjects shifted weight to the right foot to balance for 20 seconds. Subjects then were instructed to step off the plate, step back on, and repeat the procedure on the left foot. This was completed twice on both the right and left sides.

The force plate system software generated a sway path plot (Figure 1) indicating the trajectory of the subject's center of pressure (COP) on the force plate over time. The sway path time series was resolved into an x-axis component (COPx) representing movement in the medial-lateral direction and a y-axis component (COPy), representing the anterior-posterior direction. In addition, the force plate measured a time series of the resultant vertical force (Fz) based on the magnitude of the ground reaction force over time. Balance metrics were derived using the sway path, COPx, COPy, and Fz time series data. Metrics were measured or calculated separately for the left and right legs. A “symmetry” measure was calculated for each pair of left- and right-sided metrics which indicated the degree of relative similarity between the 2 sides. Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in SAS Studio version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Continuous variables were assessed for normality with the Shapiro-Wilk test and most were found to be non-normally distributed. Due to the small sample size and non-normal distribution, continuous variables were reported with medians and ranges. Categorical variables were reported with counts and percentages. Force plate jump testing metrics that were significant predictors for IRTS test findings for the involved leg were identified using multiple linear regression for continuous outcomes (eg, quadriceps strength on isokinetic testing at 60 deg/second) and multivariable logistic regression for binary outcomes (eg, succeeding or failing to achieve normal quadriceps strength at 60 deg/second). To account for the small sample size and multiple comparisons involving 21 separate predictor variables, stepwise selection was applied to each regression model to eliminate predictor variables that did not contribute significantly to model fit. Model fit was assessed using the Akaike information criterion. For predictor variables, significance level for entry into the model was set at 0.10, and significance level of staying in the model was set at the Bonferroni-corrected value of 0.002 (α = 0.05 divided by 21). Predictor variables associated with an individual P-value <.05 and a regression model P-value <.002 were considered significant. Significant predictors were reported with regression coefficient (β) or odds ratio with 95% confidence interval.

Results

Cohort demographics

Sixteen subjects were enrolled in this study (Table 3). All subjects participated in some form of physical activity (ex. stretching, weightlifting) before their ACL injury. Ten subjects (62.5%) self-reported as athletes, of whom 3 engaged in at least 1 full-contact or limited-contact activity as a primary sport (eg, football, basketball) and 7 only engaged in noncontact sports (eg, cycling, running). The cohort had a median age of 29 years (range 20–47), was majority female (9 subjects, 56%), and had a median body mass index of 23.1 (range 18.1–44.7). Only 1 subject had radiographic signs suspicious for knee osteoarthritis (Kellgren-Lawrence grade 1). Most subjects (14 subjects, 88.9%) received a bone-patellar-tendon-bone autograft. Nine subjects were randomized to undergo IRTS testing first while 7 were randomized to undergo force plate testing first.

Knee and hip range of motion

Median active and passive ROM for knee extension, knee flexion, and hip external rotation were all lower for the involved leg compared with the uninvolved leg. By contrast, median active and passive ROM for hip internal rotation was higher for the involved leg compared with the uninvolved leg. Multivariate multiscale entropy (MMSExyz) of the involved leg was positively predictive of knee flexion active ROM for the involved leg (β = 33.50, P = .02). Symmetry of the sway velocity mean (Symm SVM) was positively predictive of knee flexion passive ROM for the involved leg (β = 83.92, P = .006). Symmetry of the COPx shift (Symm COPx shift) was positively predictive of both hip internal rotation active ROM (β = 41.83, P = .008) and passive ROM (β = 48.37, P = .03). There were no significant balance metric predictors for knee extension ROM or hip external rotation ROM for the involved leg.

Flexibility testing

Median hamstring flexibility was higher for the involved leg (16°) than the uninvolved leg (10.5°). By contrast, quadriceps tightness and TFL tightness were observed at higher rates in the involved leg (63% and 50%, respectively) compared with the uninvolved leg (56% and 38%, respectively). Hip flexor tightness was observed at the same rate for both legs (50%). No balance metrics were found to be significant predictors for flexibility test findings.

Gluteus medius strength

Gluteus medius weakness was more commonly observed in the involved leg (81%) vs. the uninvolved leg (63%). No balance metrics were found to be significant predictors for gluteus medius weakness.

Lachman test

All subjects had a negative Lachman test for both legs. Due to the absence of any subjects with positive Lachman tests, multivariable analysis could not be performed.

Quadriceps strength on isokinetic testing

Quadriceps strength was below the normal range (ie, test failure) at a higher rate for involved legs vs. uninvolved legs at 60 deg/second (81% vs. 38%), 180 deg/second (81% vs. 38%), and 300 deg/second (63% vs. 19%). Most subjects exhibited an interlimb difference in quadriceps strength >10% at all 3 testing speeds (69%–81%). By contrast, involved legs were more likely to have a quadriceps-to-hamstrings strength ratio within normal range (ie, test pass) than uninvolved legs at 60 deg/second (69% vs. 13%) and 300 deg/second (50% vs. 0%). Symmetry of the multiscale vertical entropy (MVE) was positively predictive of quadriceps strength at 60 deg/second for the involved leg (β = 274.58, P = .04) but was not a significant predictor of the odds of achieving a passing strength value at 60 deg/second. There were no significant balance metric predictors of quadriceps strength at 180 or 300 deg/second, interlimb difference in quadriceps strength at any testing speed, quadriceps-to-hamstring strength ratio at 60 or 300 deg/second, or the odds of achieving a passing value for any of these isokinetic strength tests.

Step-down, triple jump, and crossover jump tests

Passing rates for the step-down test were lower for the involved leg (50%) compared with the uninvolved leg (81%). Most subjects failed the triple jump test (62%) and the crossover jump test (69%). Symm COPx shift was negatively predictive of step-down test score for the involved leg (β = −5.98, P = .004) but was not a significant predictor of the odds of passing the test. Symmetry of MMSExyz was negatively predictive of triple jump test score (β = −0.90, P = .007) but was not a significant predictor of the odds of passing the test. There were no significant balance metric predictors of crossover jump test score or the odds of passing the test.

Bunkie tests

Passing rates for the Bunkie stabilizing test were the same for the involved and uninvolved legs (13%). However, passing rates for the Bunkie lateral test and medial test were slightly higher for involved legs (33% and 13%, respectively) vs. uninvolved legs (20% and 0%, respectively). MMSExyz of the involved leg was positively predictive of Bunkie stabilizing test score for the involved leg (β = 56.10, P = .03) but was not a significant predictor of the odds of passing the test. MMSExyz of the uninvolved leg was positively predictive of Bunkie lateral test score for the involved leg (β = 90.15, P = .02) and Bunkie medial test score for the involved leg (β = 89.23, P = .02), but was not a significant predictor of the odds of passing either test.

Landing error scoring system

SVM of the uninvolved leg was positively predictive of landing error scoring system score (β = 109.47, P = .006) but was not significant predictor of the odds of achieving a passing score.

Discussion

Summary of main findings

We identified several force plate-derived balance testing metrics that were significantly correlated with specific components of a conventional RTS battery. The MMSExyz balance metric had several notable associations with IRTS test findings for the involved leg: MMSExyz of the involved leg was predictive of knee flexion active ROM and Bunkie stabilizing test score, MMSExyz of the uninvolved leg was predictive of Bunkie lateral and medial test scores, and interlimb symmetry of MMSExyz was predictive of triple jump test score. Interlimb symmetry of the COPx shift balance metric was also predictive of several IRTS test findings for the involved leg including hip internal rotation active and passive ROM and step-down test score. Although interlimb symmetry of the MVE balance metric was predictive of quadriceps strength on isokinetic testing at 60 deg/second, we did not identify any other balance metrics that were predictive of any other quadriceps isokinetic test findings. We also did not identify any balance metrics that were predictive of findings on flexibility testing of the hamstrings, quadriceps, hip flexors, or TFL. Finally, we did not identify any balance metrics that were significantly associated with the odds of achieving a pass or passing value on any of the tests in the IRTS battery based on established cutoff values.

Discussion of the literature

Force plates can capture subtle fluctuations in a patient's COP during balance testing and the granularity of the resulting time series data can be analyzed using complex statistical methods such as the multiscale entropy method.16 However, there is a lack of consensus around how metrics should be calculated from COP data and which metrics have the strongest association with postural control.6 Masani et al.17 reported that smaller COP velocity (analogous to SVM in this study) was associated with better postural control and that this metric was the most sensitive measure of the efficiency of the postural control system across different age groups and neurological conditions. In our own analysis, we found that a higher degree of symmetry in SVM between the involved and uninvolved sides was associated with higher knee flexion active ROM for the involved leg while higher SVM of the uninvolved leg was predictive of higher landing error scoring system score.

Several clinical studies have applied balance testing using force plates and related technologies to assess physical function among ACL injury patients. Salavati et al.18 studied the test-retest reliability of COP-based metrics in a sample of 33 patients with various musculoskeletal disorders, including 12 patients (all male) with nonoperated ACL injuries. The authors found the mean total velocity of the COP signal (analogous to SVM in our study) to have high reliability as measured by the intraclass correlation coefficient (range 0.74–0.91) and concluded that this metric was a good candidate for quantifying balance performance among patients with musculoskeletal disorders. Another study by Fernandes et al.19 evaluated dynamic and static postural control among 28 athletes, 14 of whom had a history of nonoperated ACL injury to the dominant leg and 14 who did not, using the AccuSwayPlus force plate (Advanced Mechanical Technology, Inc., Watertown, MA). The authors found that the ACL injury group exhibited greater mediolateral displacement (analogous to COPx shift in this study) on the eyes-closed single-leg test compared with the control group. Interestingly, we found a higher degree of symmetry in COPx shift between the involved and uninvolved sides to be associated with greater active and passive hip internal rotation ROM, which implies that mediolateral displacement on balance testing may be strongly associated with hip function.

Studies in the rehabilitation literature have suggested that effective prevention of ACL injury and reinjury involves a combination of balance and strengthening exercises. White et al.20 found that balance training and assessment can provide insight into lower extremity strength and limb asymmetries and that both variables are potential risk factors for ACL injury. Force plate-based balance testing has been used to quantitatively demonstrate these interlimb imbalances in postural control among ACL-deficient patients. Sahoo and Sahu21 conducted a controlled trial using the HUMAC Balance System (Computer Sports Medicine, Inc., Stoughton, MA) in 33 subjects to compare postural balance between patients with chronic ACL injury and healthy controls. The authors found that within the ACL-deficient group, there was reduced overall stability based on the COP signal (P = .02), increased body sway COP path length (P = .002), and decreased dynamic balance (P < .001) compared with controls. Interestingly, the uninjured limb in the ACL-deficient group exhibited lower stability and higher body sway scores than the dominant limb in the control group, suggesting a compensatory mechanism by the ACL-intact limb in unilateral ACL-deficient patients.

Potential advantages of the force plate-based balance testing protocol over the multimodal IRTS testing protocol include the force plate's relative ease of setup and operation, shorter time needed for testing and reduced need for specially trained personnel to observe and evaluate the results of the assessment. Some previous studies have demonstrated the utility of balance metrics in RTS assessments, though many of these studies did not obtain these metrics using force plate technology. Kerr et al.22 found that a multimodal assessment of postural stability could contribute to successful RTS in athletes after sports-related concussions, Plisky et al.23 described the physical assessment-based Star Excursion Balance Test as a reliable predictor of lower extremity injury in basketball players, and Saper et al.24 discussed use of the Lower Quarter Y-Balance Test to predict RTS time among adolescents who underwent medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction.

Limitations

We note several limitations of our study design. First, we used a cross-sectional design such that subjects only underwent RTS testing at a single point in time postoperatively. Since ACLR patients are expected to experience functional improvement over time, performing a second or third round of testing at a later time point would allow us to determine whether associations between IRTS findings and balance metrics hold over time. Second, our sample size was limited which necessitated the use of stepwise selection methods and a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold (α = 0.002) in our regression analysis to avoid overfitting models and obtaining spurious results. Consequently, some balance testing metrics that were predictive of IRTS findings may have been eliminated in the selection process. Third, there was a considerable proportion of the cohort who underwent a concomitant meniscal repair (6 patients, 38%) and were instructed to follow a delayed weight bearing protocol. This raises the possibility that differences in rehabilitation protocols between subjects who underwent ACLR with vs. without meniscal repair may have potentially confounded our results.
