Safe General Anesthesia in a Pediatric Patient With 1p36.33 Duplication Syndrome: A Rare Case Report
Authors: Michael McNally, Esha Thakkar, Adrienne Hutton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT1p36.33 Duplication Syndrome is an extremely rare autosomal dominant multi‐gene disorder, including the disruption of the ATAD3 gene cluster, which encodes for mitochondrial membrane proteins. As of 2025, there is only one publicly available article highlighting the characteristic findings in patients with this condition; however, there is no existing data on safe anesthetic management. We present a case highlighting the successful and uneventful care of a pediatric patient with this condition.Keywords: 1p36.33 duplication syndrome, cardiomyopathy, mitochondrial disorder, pediatric anesthesia, pharmacology, rhabdomyolysis

Introduction

1p36.33 Duplication Syndrome is not strictly a mitochondrial disorder; however, it is rather a multi‐gene disorder with a strong correlation of mitochondrial dysfunction due to its disruption in the ATAD3 gene cluster. Patients may exhibit clinical features consistent with primary genetic mitochondrial diseases, such as lactic acidosis. Generally, conditions with mitochondrial dysfunction affect approximately 1 in 4000 individuals and often present challenges in anesthetic planning due to limited research and case reports. These disorders commonly involve dysfunction of the cardiac, central nervous, and musculoskeletal systems, raising concerns over the use of certain medications during the course of anesthetics that can cause dysregulation as a result of mitochondrial dysfunction [1]. Despite a comprehensive literature review of various disorders featuring mitochondrial dysfunction, there is only one published report specifically addressing 1p36.33 Duplication Syndrome.

The first documented case of 1p36.33 Duplication Syndrome involved 5 unrelated neonates, all of whom died within the first 6 weeks of life [2]. Common findings included bradycardia, hypertrophic or dilated cardiomyopathy, and ocular anomalies such as cataracts or corneal anomalies. Additional findings included fetal edema or hydrops in three patients, lactic acidosis and increased urinary excretion of organic acids in two, and white matter abnormalities—such as reduced brain volume, cerebellar atrophy, and hypoxic brain damage—in three patients. Genitourinary anomalies such as micropenis, hypospadias, and cryptorchidism were also observed [2].

This case report describes the general anesthetic management of a 4‐month‐old patient with 1p36.33 Duplication Syndrome undergoing exploratory laparotomy for true abdominal wall evisceration. It contributes valuable insight to the literature by demonstrating an example of safe and effective anesthetic care in a patient with this rare condition.

Case Examination

A 4‐month‐old, 3.49 kg, ventilator‐dependent female presented for an exploratory laparotomy due to true abdominal wall evisceration. The diagnosis of 1p36.33 Duplication Syndrome had been made at 3 months of age via GeneDx ultraRapid Genome Sequencing during her stay in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU).

Her medical history is notable for extreme prematurity (born at 24 weeks gestation), pulmonary hypertension, acute kidney injury, anemia of prematurity, thrombocytopenia, and moderately sized patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) with mild left heart enlargement. Additional concerns included suspected dysplastic kidneys and a risk of adrenal insufficiency.

Her past surgical history includes multiple abdominal interventions: percutaneous gastrostomy tube placement, three prior exploratory laparotomies, two abdominal wall repairs for dehiscence/evisceration, ileostomy takedown, as well as a catheter insertion.

At the time of this surgery, she remained ventilator‐dependent and was receiving several medications including dexmedetomidine, midazolam, hydromorphone, furosemide, and total parenteral nutrition (TPN). There are no known allergies, and no family history was available.

Physical exam findings included a patent airway, low‐set large ears, a small mouth, and macrocephaly—her head size appeared disproportionately large relative to her body, spanning the full width of her torso. Due to her small mouth, the treating physician anticipated a potentially difficult airway requiring a modified position should re‐intubation be required.

Laboratory evaluation revealed: elevated nucleated red blood cells (NRBC) at 0.6, presence of white blood cells (WBC) in the urine (1.0), mild hypokalemia (serum potassium of 3.9 mmol/L), and arterial blood gas (ABG) results indicating elevated CO2 (53 mmHg), O2 (124 mmHg), elevated HCO3 (30 mmHg), and full oxygen saturation (100%).

The patient had previously tolerated general anesthesia safely with sevoflurane, dexmedetomidine (Precedex), hydromorphone (Dilaudid), rocuronium and a stress dose steroid during a gastrostomy tube placement prior to an exploratory laparotomy.

Investigations and Treatment

On the day of surgery, the patient was transported from the NICU while intubated and sedated on continuous infusions of dexmedetomidine (Precedex) and hydromorphone, along with a recent dose of midazolam (Versed). Due to limited literature on 1p36.33 Duplication Syndrome—but known mitochondrial involvement—propofol and succinylcholine were intentionally avoided in the anesthetic plan.

NICU medications were continued in the operating room, including dexmedetomidine at 2 mcg/kg/h, hydromorphone at 0.35 mg/kg/h, furosemide at 0.1 mg/kg/h, and midazolam at 0.02 mg/kg/h. Sevoflurane was used for both induction and maintenance; however, end‐tidal concentrations were not displayed during the first 30 min due to the time taken to stabilize the gas analyzer. During maintenance, end‐tidal concentrations ranged from 1.5%–1.6% and inspired concentrations of 1.6%–1.9%, with the total cumulative sevoflurane consumption being 12 mL, and a single bolus of rocuronium (4 mg) for muscle relaxation. Prophylactic cefoxitin (100 mg) was administered intravenously. The hydromorphone infusion was paused due to loss of IV access.

Total anesthesia time was 1 h and 16 min. Following surgery, the hydromorphone infusion was resumed at the original dose (0.35 mg/kg/h) and transported back to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) for ongoing management No intraoperative complications were observed, and no new metabolic or neurologic deterioration was documented in the days following this procedure.

Conclusion and Results

Although there have been no reported cases of anesthesia administration in patients with this disorder, it is recommended to avoid depolarizing neuromuscular blocking agents to avoid adverse effects such as rhabdomyolysis. Additionally, it is important to be cautious when using any agents that may have an effect on mitochondrial function, such as propofol and volatile agents, which can also lead to hypersensitivity reactions.

This case in particular is incredibly valuable in developing proper treatment plans moving forward for those with this condition due to the extremely limited research. It underscores the importance of individualized planning and consideration of metabolic vulnerabilities when managing anesthesia in patients with mitochondrial dysfunction. The only article published on this condition offers insight into the symptoms and causes of the 1p36.33 Duplication Syndrome; however this case will serve as the only published research documenting the proper care and treatment plan when undergoing surgery.

Discussion

Due to the mitochondrial dysfunction associated with 1p36.33 Duplication Syndrome, affected individuals are at high risk for multisystem involvement, including cardiomyopathy, neurologic deficits, and metabolic derangements due to a severe reduction in metabolic rate. Our patient had a PDA with mild left heart enlargement, consistent with reported cases of hypertrophic or dilated cardiomyopathy in this population. This highlights the need for careful cardiovascular evaluation when developing an anesthetic plan due to the increased risk of myocardial complications.

Disorders with mitochondrial dysfunction are associated with increased sensitivity to several anesthetic agents. Propofol [1] has the ability to interfere with mitochondrial function and uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation, essentially disrupting the electron transport chain to prevent the production of ATP. As a result, the administration of propofol in patients with mitochondrial disorders puts the patient at risk of becoming more susceptible to propofol's inhibitory properties [3]. Because of this finding, it is recommended to be used sparingly in patients with mitochondrial disorders.

Dexmedetomidine, an α2‐adrenergic agonist [1], was selected as a safe sedative due to its minimal action on the electron transport chain. Current research recommends this agent for sedation [1] and as an alternative to propofol. It has been successfully used for this purpose in patients with mitochondrial disease and was well tolerated in this case.

Volatile anesthetics like sevoflurane have been associated with several potential risks in patients with mitochondrial dysfunction, including malignant hyperthermia, hypersensitivity reactions and developmental delay [1]. Numerous studies have discovered the link between volatile agents and their interaction with mitochondrial complexes, suggesting these agents can suppress oxidative phosphorylation, specifically at complex I and coenzyme Q [4]. Furthermore, animal studies have demonstrated significantly increased sensitivity to volatile agents in organisms with mutations in mitochondrial proteins [5]. However, in this case, sevoflurane was used in this patient for both induction and maintenance, with the observed end‐tidal concentrations (1.5%–1.6%) being below the usual minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) for a 4‐month‐old patient (roughly 3.2%) [6]. These findings are consistent with the patient's stable intraoperative course, and the patient did not experience any adverse effects during prior procedures, during this operation, or in the following days.

Succinylcholine was avoided due to the potential risk of rhabdomyolysis and hyperkalemia, given the potential for underlying myopathy in patients with mitochondrial dysfunction that can result in musculoskeletal dysfunction [7]. Rocuronium, a non‐depolarizing neuromuscular blocker, was safely used based on prior tolerance.

Midazolam, a type of benzodiazepine, is often a preferred hypnotic drug used for induction in patients susceptible to malignant hyperthermia, such as those with mitochondrial dysfunction. However, midazolam is found to decrease Complex I activity in the electron transport chain in a dose dependent manner [4]. Although midazolam has been linked with some success in various cases, its use must be cautioned in patients with mitochondrial dysfunction [8]. The patient in this case previously tolerated midazolam and was continued on this medication after her procedure.

Opioids have not been linked to any significant reduction in mitochondrial activity; however, they have been found to pose a concern among spontaneously ventilating patients with mitochondrial disorders. Without proper titration, opioids can lead to respiratory depression and subsequent hypoxia and hypercarbia, leading to respiratory acidosis [9]. For patients with mitochondrial dysfunction, lactic acidosis is highly linked, and so being compounded with new respiratory acidosis can lead to severe health outcomes.

Lactated Ringer's is important to avoid reducing the risk of lactic acidosis [9]. In the case of our patient, there was no lactic acidosis present, and there was no risk of respiratory acidosis due to the patient being dependent on mechanical ventilation. Normal saline was utilized in this case.

Furosemide has been linked with various rat studies suggesting inhibition of mitochondrial activity, specifically Complex II and state 3 (ADP‐dependent respiration) [10]. No negative outcomes have been reported in human patients with mitochondrial disorders. Further research is warranted to evaluate its clinical significance.
