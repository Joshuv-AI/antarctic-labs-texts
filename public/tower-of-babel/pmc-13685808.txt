Can autonomous Agentic-AI systems accelerate drug discovery and development in the next decade?
Authors: Navaneet Chaturvedi, Shafiul Haque, Vijay Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe pharmaceutical industry is experiencing a profound transformation driven by rapid advancements in artificial intelligence (AI), increasing research and development complexity, and the expiration of key patents. Agentic-AI systems, which enable autonomous reasoning, decision-making, and adaptive learning beyond traditional data-driven automation, represent a paradigm shift in this context. This review examines the impact of Agentic-AI across the pharmaceutical value chain, including supply chain orchestration, manufacturing optimization, early-stage drug discovery, process development, and regulatory intelligence. These systems enhance operational resilience, reduce development timelines, and accelerate the identification of new therapeutic candidates by fostering dynamic human-AI collaboration. However, the widespread adoption of Agentic-AI introduces significant challenges related to workforce integration, ethical accountability, governance, and regulatory compliance. Addressing these challenges is essential for reliable and scalable deployment. This study identifies key factors shaping Agentic-AI’s influence over the next decade and provides a forward-looking perspective on its evolving role in pharmaceutical innovation.

Introduction

The global pharmaceutical sector is at a point of critical tumult. There are a number of demands upon its structure which are working simultaneously to disrupt well-established approaches to drug discovery and development. One of note is an impending patent cliff, with many high-end therapies set to exit market exclusivity between 2025 and 2030. This would seem to risk financially the basic business models of all of the largest pharmaceutical enterprises and heighten generic and biosimilar competition. Meanwhile, there are still prolonged timescales for drug development, research and development spending is growing all the time, and global pharmaceutical chains are subject to an increasing number of disruptions from geopolitics and global economic trends [1, 2].

Considering the pharmaceutical domain, conventional applications of artificial intelligence (AI) have largely focused on pattern recognition, data analytics, and predictive modeling to support established processes such as target identification, virtual screening, and clinical trial design. While these approaches have generated measurable improvements, they have not fundamentally altered the long-standing bottlenecks that challenge pharmaceutical research and development (R&D) and commercialization [3]. Traditional AI systems primarily function as analytical or predictive tools that operate under human supervision, requiring users to interpret outputs, make decisions, and execute subsequent actions [4]. Consequently, the promise of AI to significantly reduce timelines and costs without compromising scientific precision has not been fully realized.

On the contrary, the emergent capabilities inherent to Agentic-AI, described as having autonomous and proactive agents with the capacity to monitor complex scenes, decide independently, and engage in complex workflows without the constant oversight of humans, marks an important step change over the current state of the art represented by the existing capabilities of AI technology itself [5]. Such technology is capable of information integration across disparate sources, independently conducting experiments, optimizing clinical trial paths in real-time, and managing cross-functional business activities like the supply chain and regulatory issues. Additionally, the integration of Agentic-AI in pharmaceutical applications is much broader and encompasses organizational change and competitive strategy. The life sciences companies that choose to leverage such technologies might be able to accelerate innovation, improve operational efficiencies, and provide new and innovative solutions in precision medicine, trial adaptability, and drug regulatory interfaces [2, 6]. Nevertheless, realizing this vision requires addressing critical challenges related to data quality, governance, regulatory oversight, workforce readiness, and ethical deployment to ensure the safe and responsible integration of autonomous systems across the pharmaceutical lifecycle [7].

While the emerging literature has increasingly recognized the transformative potential of Agentic-AI in healthcare and life sciences, current discussions remain largely focused on conceptual frameworks and individual use cases [8–12]. This review extends beyond these perspectives by providing a comprehensive analysis of Agentic-AI across the entire pharmaceutical value chain, from drug discovery and manufacturing to regulatory sciences, commercial operations, and organizational transformation. By integrating technological advances with regulatory, ethical, and workforce considerations, the review offers a holistic framework for understanding the opportunities, challenges, and future trajectory of Agentic-AI-driven pharmaceutical innovation.

Patent cliff as a catalyst for innovation in pharma

The ‘patent cliff’ is the colloquial name given to the sharp drop-off in revenues resulting from the expiration of market exclusivity of bestseller medications, which then face competition from generic and biosimilar medications sold for much lower costs [13]. In the current decade, this situation is expected to affect an unprecedented number of high-priced therapies and is expected to impact around 190 drugs making more than $1 billion every year, set to face the loss of market exclusivity between 2025 and 2030, resulting in the potential loss of around $236 billion for the industry. The scope of these impending events poses a threat to the established models of the pharmaceutical industry and simultaneously triggers the forces of innovation. Unlike traditional patent cliff situations where participants primarily consisted of small molecule products with simpler generics as direct substitutes, the current patent cliff involves numerous biologic products whose loss of exclusivity may fail to be substituted directly by generics owing to the technicality required in biosimilarity, that is on the rise and is exerting pricing and market-share pressures even on biosimilars themselves. For biosimilars to become possible as direct substitutes for the mentioned products, it may take considerable time as manufacturers require time to conduct extensive clinical trials. For instance, top-selling cancer and immunology brands like Merck’s Keytruda, Bristol-Myers Squibb’s Eliquis, and Johnson & Johnson’s Stelara face patent expiry in the next few years and promise to extremely alter the bottom-line performance outlooks for the top pharmaceutical firms [9]. In such scenarios, the projected drop in revenue by firms because of the loss of patent exclusivity may reach up to 50%–60% in as short a time as a few years [14].

The implications of revenue erosion due to the expiration of patents are far-reaching. Traditionally, the existence of a patent granted a certain number of years of monopoly power during which the pricing of the drug could be set at a certain level to recoup the high costs incurred during research and development of the drug. After the expiry of the patent, the sales volume and the pricing of the innovator drug collapse sharply as competing companies enter the scene and drive down the price of the innovator drug to as low as 30% to 90%, depending on the companies concerned for some drugs such as small molecules. This rapid revenue contraction places significant pressure on research and development budgets, complicates long-range financial planning, and underscores the vulnerability of relying on a narrow portfolio of blockbusters for sustainable growth. Although daunting, the patent cliff also functions as a transformative impetus for innovation. The looming loss of revenue resources motivates companies to reposition themselves strategically and adopt new technological capabilities capable of driving the next generation of therapeutics. Among these, advanced AI and autonomous Agentic-AI systems are emerging as critical components of a reimagined innovation infrastructure. Rather than serving as tools for incremental efficiency gains, autonomous AI agents can integrate heterogeneous datasets, design experiments, execute simulations, and adapt clinical trial parameters in real time capabilities that directly address some of the most entrenched bottlenecks in drug discovery and development [15, 16].

Amid pressures from the patent cliff, pharmaceutical companies are turning to new approaches that help reduce potential risks. While traditional approaches such as lifecycle management approaches like label extensions, formulation optimization, and secondary patents are being taken, such approaches generally offer limited extensions of product life. At the same time, fast-paced mergers and acquisitions (M&As), which are an inevitable result of patent expirations, are also now in progress, with big pharma giants looking to fill their pipelines with new and innovative products and fill potential revenue drains in the future. Big pharma companies are now pursuing big acquisitions of late-stage biotech that harbor innovative products, as there is a growing need to fill potential product life drains. For instance, big M&As for improving cancer and specialty pharmaceuticals product portfolios are indicative of an overall shift in the pharmaceutical industry trend, which now seeks big returns from big growth sectors, unlike before, when companies generally sought big improvements in big products.

Beyond M&A, AI-driven innovation is increasingly central to life science research paradigms. Advanced computational models are rapidly accelerating key stages of the drug development pipeline, including target identification, lead optimization, preclinical safety assessments, and even early clinical trial design. According to industry analyses, AI-enabled platforms are projected to contribute hundreds of billions of dollars in value by streamlining discovery processes, reducing development costs, and enabling pharmaceutical companies to explore therapeutic modalities that were previously cost-prohibitive. These developments suggest that the patent cliff, while disruptive, is also fostering a competitive imperative for companies to adopt transformative technologies that may ultimately lead to more resilient innovation ecosystems. Additionally, the urgency imposed by patent expirations thus reframes the narrative of crisis into an engine for strategic evolution. Firms that effectively harness autonomous intelligence and AI-augmented R&D are better positioned to uncover novel therapeutic opportunities, reconfigure pipelines around innovative modalities, and navigate the increasingly complex regulatory and commercial terrain of the 2030s. The patent cliff, therefore, not only reveals the limitations of legacy pharmaceutical models but also accelerates the transition toward digitally driven, data-centric innovation processes that could define the industry’s next era.

Regulatory transformation: FDA’s integration of autonomous and AI-driven intelligence

The United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has managed to develop the changing paradigm within its regulatory framework to ensure the accommodation of the revolutionary power of AI, hence marking the beginning of a new era in the regulation of the drug life cycle, including the development, evaluation, and control of drugs. Traditionally, the regulatory evaluation of drugs in FDA was performed through human expertise and traditional approaches of statistical and mechanism-based evidence. The increase in the use of AI and ML in the application of analytical power in the life sciences has forced the FDA, among other regulatory agencies, to accept the paradigm change in their approaches aimed at accommodating innovation and providing assurance of public safety. The paradigm change in the use of the proactive power of AI in the FDA regulatory environment within the life of drugs demonstrates a regulatory revolution, not only seeing AI as an innovation tool within industries but rather as partners within regulatory science. One of the major foundations of this revolution is FDA’s release of draft guidance documents outlining the application of AI models in regulatory decision-making in drug and biological product applications [17]. This guidance, the first of its kind, articulates a risk-based framework for evaluating the credibility of AI models based on their context of use that is, the specific role the model plays in supporting safety, effectiveness, or quality assessments. It emphasizes that sponsors should define and justify how an AI model’s output will be used in regulatory submissions, and what credibility evidence is required to support that use [17]. By fostering early engagement between sponsors and the agency on AI credibility plans, the guidance aims to improve transparency, reduce uncertainty, and promote robust, trustworthy modeling practices that align with regulatory expectations (Table 1).

Parallel initiatives within the FDA underscore this commitment to AI integration. For example, the Centre for Drug Evaluation and Research has established an internal AI Council to consolidate and coordinate AI policy, governance, and implementation across the agency, ensuring consistent regulatory perspectives on AI use in drug lifecycle activities ranging from preclinical modeling to postmarket surveillance and manufacturing [18]. This institutional readiness reflects a recognition that AI will play increasingly prominent roles in interpreting real-world data, enhancing predictive safety assessments, and augmenting decision support for complex regulatory evaluations. In addition to policy guidance, the FDA has begun deploying generative and Agentic-AI technologies internally to support regulatory functions. In 2025, the agency announced a phased, agency-wide rollout of Elsa, a generative AI tool designed to assist scientific reviewers with reading, summarizing, and organizing safety data, clinical protocols, and regulatory documentation within a secure GovCloud environment (Fig. 1). The tool is already being used to accelerate internal review processes, enabling reviewers to focus on higher-order judgment tasks while reducing time spent on repetitive or administrative work. This operational AI adoption is part of a larger strategy to enhance efficiency without compromising the agency’s public health mission.

The FDA’s AI strategy is moving into more advanced territory. By adopting agentic systems, the agency is now able to handle intricate, multi-step workflows that go far beyond simple data processing. These tools are currently being applied to the heavy lifting of pre-market reviews and compliance tracking, and even the daily administrative tasks that often slow things down. Of course, this isn’t an automated free-for-all; the systems run within secure, air-gapped environments and are kept under strict human oversight to ensure total accountability. What we’re seeing here is a fundamental shift in regulatory culture. Instead of replacing human judgment, these autonomous tools are being used to clear out bottlenecks, allowing the agency to be much more responsive and efficient in a fast-moving market [19].

Despite such progress, there are not unencountered challenges related to integrating AI into regulatory workflows. Stakeholder input on the FDA’s draft guidance about AI raised questions about how responsibilities are divided up, the risk assessments, and monitoring performance of AI models used in submissions to clarify responsibilities, risk assessments, and performance monitoring for AI models used in submissions. It is important that transparency and accountability of AI outputs, along with explainability, will be ensured in order not to compromise public trust and the integrity of regulation. Moreover, the cautious approach of the FDA, which requires that AI output not replace human judgment but rather enhance the capability of reviewers, underlines the requirement to balance innovation with stringent oversight, including ethical consideration. Nevertheless, the FDA’s regulatory innovation also extends to the broader implications for the pharmaceutical sector. With the development of frameworks outlining the responsible application of AI in submissions and the use of AI to accelerate reviews in the FDA, a clear message regarding a shift in the culture towards co-evolution in emerging technologies is demonstrated. This not only eliminates bottlenecks in the approval process for drugs, but it also encourages the development in the pharmaceutical sector of effective and FDA-aligned approaches in AI to improve the efficiency for drugs in the development, predictive, and safety sectors. With the evolving nature of the AI environment, the FDA’s continuing development in the issuance of guidelines, improvement in risk-based frameworks, and the application of autonomous AI in the regulatory environment portrays a responsive regulatory environment.

Economic disruption: the trillion-dollar transformation

The pharma and healthcare industry is currently facing a massive economic transformation due to AI, and Agentic-AI systems have become a very important catalyst for change and improvements here. Rising costs and timelines for research and development, as well as high rates of attrition, have pushed conventional, traditional, and current efforts for drug discovery and development into unsustainable territory and timelines. The estimated cost for bringing a novel molecular entity into the market has risen above USD 2.3 billion, and development timelines have crossed ten years, with attrition rates above 90% for most projects [26, 27]. ML and deep learning (DL) techniques have shown tangible economic value in enhancing target identification, improving lead optimization, and preventing late-stage clinical development failures [28, 29]. Agentic-AI takes advantage of these improvements in economic value and, in addition, allows systems to self-direct experiments, perform multi-step processes, assess their own results, and learn and improve decision paths throughout the pharmaceutical development value chain. This paradigm shift from human-in-the-loop to human-on-the-loop analytical intelligence fundamentally changes economic models by eliminating redundancies, accelerating decision cycles, and eliminating capital-intensive trial and error. At an industry scale, economic benefits approach saving hundreds of millions of dollars per program (Table 2).

Capital markets and investment strategies increasingly reflect this transformation. Redefining pharmaceutical workforces: human Agentic-AI co-intelligence in drug innovation

Although an Agentic-AI model is still awaiting global acceptance in the pharmaceutical industry, the developing agentic frameworks (AutoGPT, and so on) and pharmaceutical applications of AI (like AlphaFold, and so on) are rapidly finding their way into the pharmaceutical industry to be utilized as self-governing decision-making and execution assistants throughout the pharmaceutical life cycle [3]. Agentic frameworks and pharmaceutical applications of AI, in total, provide the foundation for new human co-intelligence models based on human-AI co-performing, which is transforming workforce organization, and not replacing it. Unlike automation, Agentic-AI brings new self-governing computer assistants to pharmaceuticals with decision-making, execution, and ongoing learning functions in R&D, production, and commercial roles. Given the pharmaceutical industry is faced with R&D expenses and lack of R&D productivity, and patent life challenges, organizational work force models are inherently tied to pharmaceutical industry adoption of Agentic-AI solutions [32]. The latest estimates from the Chief Human Resource Officers foretell a sharp increase in the use of AI agents, which may rise from the current level of 15% to more than 60% in the coming years. This changeover has been linked to labor productivity gains of nearly 30% per person and labor cost savings of nearly 20% due to the use of digital labor to supplement human intelligence, and not merely to replace it [42, 43]. This has particularly valuable applications in the pharmaceutical industry, where highly specialized knowledge work has hitherto been limited owing to human limitations of overload, fragmented data, and decision cycles (Table 3).

More importantly, the labor impact of Agentic-AI spans the efficiency frontier into a transformation of human cooperation with machines. There is evidence that the strategic leadership of the workforce now places digital labor integration on the agenda of their roles and responsibilities. Agentic-AI systems are now operating in the same space as researchers and developers in the industry, including regulatory professionals, dealing with data-driven tasks such as hypothesis development, process optimization, monitoring, and scenario simulation. There is a transfer of cognitive labor that will enable human professionals to concentrate on the task of using their strategic judgments and human problem-solving skills in areas that are still hard to automate tasks [44]. Despite early fears of widespread job losses, the evidence points toward a shift in workforce roles rather than a simple replacement of human staff. In the pharmaceutical sector, the rise of Agentic-AI is more likely to trigger a broad move toward redeployment and professional evolution. Most employees will likely stay in their current positions but will find themselves working in much closer coordination with AI systems. At the same time, we are seeing the emergence of entirely new career paths, such as AI product owners, model validators, and specialists in human-AI interaction. This evolution is particularly visible in R&D settings; while autonomous systems can now handle the heavy lifting of exploring chemical space or analyzing complex omics data, the human scientist’s role has become even more critical for interpreting those results, setting strategic priorities, and ensuring the final clinical relevance of the work.

The industry’s dependence on domain-specific expertise presents a set of challenges in terms of workforce transformation that are different from what has been observed in most automation-driven industries, where workforce reduction has been noticed, and this has partly led to a workforce increase in data science, bioinformatics, AI governance, and digital sales and medical affairs, and workforce reduction in more routine functions such as administration and operation [33, 34]. AI literacy has emerged as an essential skill set that cuts across all workforces, and this not only includes technical know-how but also skills to interpret AI model results and limitations.

Despite the scale of this transformation, adoption remains uneven. Many pharmaceutical organizations have yet to fully operationalize Agentic-AI, and a significant proportion of employees report limited awareness of how autonomous systems will affect their roles. This awareness gap presents dual risks: unprepared organizations may experience workforce resistance, skills mismatches, and loss of institutional knowledge, while early adopters gain competitive advantages by attracting AI-literate talent and embedding human AI collaboration into organizational culture [44]. Resistance to change frequently cited as a primary barrier to AI innovation further amplifies disparities between leaders and laggards in the emerging agentic economy. The workforce revolution driven by Agentic-AI is not a technological disruption alone but a socio-organizational transformation. Pharmaceutical companies that successfully navigate this shift will be those that invest simultaneously in digital infrastructure, continuous reskilling, transparent governance, and trust-building mechanisms that position AI as a collaborator rather than a competitor. In doing so, they will redefine productivity, innovation capacity, and professional identity in an industry where human judgment and scientific integrity remain paramount.

Autonomous manufacturing and intelligent supply networks in pharma

Traditional constraints for drug manufacturing include manual workflows, rigid schedules of production, siloed architectures of data, and limited visibility into operations in real time. These challenges have contributed to productivity losses, capacity underutilization, delayed product launches, and vulnerability to external shocks. With increasingly diversified product portfolios and rising regulatory complexity, traditional operational models cannot respond effectively to demands related to speed, flexibility, and resilience at state-of-the-art pharmaceutical production.

Agentic-AI presents a paradigm shift in the operational process, where the agents are constantly sensing, reasoning, and acting in the manufacturing domain. In the new product introduction domain, there is the possibility of self-governing the process of taking the knowledge from the development domain and executing the good manufacturing practice (GMP)-compliant process, almost automatically. Autonomous agents would be able to self-govern the act of setting up the production equipment and documenting quality in the manufacturing domain, which would reduce the new product introduction process cycle from the error-prone process of checklists to the standardized process of checklists and, eventually, the process of ‘one-click’ implementation [46]. Moreover, laboratory and production processes, and additional benefits in Agentic-AI based predictive maintenance and scheduling. Through its real-time monitoring of various data sources, including sensors and environment, Agentic-AIs can predict potential equipment failures and perform automatic scheduling, thus reducing equipment downtime, maintaining equipment life, and minimizing costs associated with production. On the other hand, scheduling Agentic-AIs perform dynamic task allocation according to real-time constraints, thus enhancing production flow and ensuring there are minimal idle capacities, especially in cell and gene therapy production and other precious biologics [49, 50].

Of note among various fields of operation, pharmaceutical industry supply chain management stands out as an extremely compelling and mature use of Agentic-AI. This is because autonomous agents in pharmaceutical supply chain activities would have the ability to monitor inventory levels and other activities around-the-clock and adjust accordingly. This would help overcome various flaws in pharmaceutical supply chain activities. Autonomous discovery pipelines: redefining pharmaceutical R&D from target identification to clinical translation

The traditional R&D pipelines involving sequential decision-making, manual hypothesis evaluation, and laborious cycles of iteration are being progressively replaced by adaptive AI agents continuously sensing, reasoning, and acting on the entire drug development pipeline. This is a paradigm shift towards static computational models and self-governed exploration environments that run at an unprecedented scale and speed [55, 56]. In the initial stages of drug discovery, Agentic-AI brings about a paradigm shift in target identification and validation. Interestingly, AI agents begin with manually crafted hypothesis statements, as AI agents learn to independently probe multi-omics profiles, biological literature, pathway databases, and real-world data to automatically identify relevant disease targets and mechanically craft hypothesis statements [57]. AI agents automatically validate targets using known biomarkers, genetically validated data, and disease networks. These agents rank continuously through cycles of target validation as progressively new information becomes available. This strategy minimizes the risk of false-positive target nomination and improves confidence before making substantial preclinical investments, which is one of the dominant modalities affecting attrition in pharmaceutical R&D efforts [29, 58–60]. A key limitation of many conventional AI approaches in drug discovery is their reliance on reductionist representations of biological systems, where individual biomarkers, molecular features, or surrogate endpoints are often treated as independent predictors of therapeutic outcomes. Such approaches may suffer from proxy failure when predictive variables correlate with outcomes in training datasets but fail to capture the underlying biological mechanisms required for successful clinical translation. Agentic-AI offers a systems-level alternative by integrating multi-omics datasets, biological interaction networks, pathway dynamics, real-world evidence, and experimental feedback into continuously evolving decision frameworks. Rather than optimizing a single predictive objective, autonomous agents can evaluate relationships across multiple biological scales and iteratively refine hypotheses as new evidence emerges. Through closed-loop validation, agentic systems continuously compare predictions with experimental and clinical outcomes, enabling adaptive updating of models, prioritization strategies, and mechanistic interpretations. This capacity for network-aware reasoning and continuous learning may improve translational robustness by reducing dependence on static proxies and fostering a more holistic representation of disease biology and therapeutic response [58–62].

Compound discovery and screening represent another domain where Agentic-AI delivers transformative gains. High-throughput virtual screening historically constrained by computational cost and manual oversight is now orchestrated by autonomous agents capable of evaluating billions of compounds across evolving chemical spaces. These agents continuously integrate assay outputs, toxicity predictions, and structure activity relationships to reprioritize compound libraries in real time. If adverse signals emerge, agentic systems dynamically reconfigure screening strategies without human intervention, enabling adaptive exploration rather than fixed experimental pipelines [63, 64]. Additionally, predictive modeling and simulation highlight a clear move away from old, static analytics toward systems that learn in real-time. Instead of just running numbers, Agentic-AI builds models that forecast how a compound will behave in a living system covering everything from how it’s absorbed to its potential toxicity. The real value here is that these simulations aren’t set in stone. They evolve constantly as new data from the lab or clinical trials comes in, creating a ‘closed loop’ where the system gets smarter at every stage. This allows teams to spot potential failures much earlier and makes it far easier to get dosing and trial designs right the first time [65].

The role of Agentic-AI is becoming increasingly central to clinical research. We are seeing a shift where autonomous systems take the lead on everything from refining trial designs to tracking patient safety in real-time. These agents excel at parsing through fragmented clinical and observational data to identify the best patient cohorts and anticipate potential enrollment delays before they derail a study. By integrating these tools with real-world data, the industry is moving toward a model of existing evidence generation. This doesn’t mean we are moving away from randomized trials; rather, we are enhancing them with the kind of adaptive, high-density data that regulators now expect in modern submissions [66].

Beyond operational efficiency, a critical measure of value for Agentic-AI is its potential to improve translational success from preclinical discovery to clinical development. Traditional drug development frequently suffers from poor concordance between preclinical findings and human clinical outcomes, contributing to high attrition rates during Phase II and Phase III studies [21, 67]. By integrating mechanistic biological knowledge, multi-omics datasets, real-world evidence, and emerging clinical observations within a continuous learning framework, Agentic-AI may improve the biological relevance of target selection and candidate prioritization [20, 21]. In contrast to conventional workflows, where preclinical and clinical decision-making often occur in separate stages, agentic systems can continuously update predictive models using newly generated evidence, thereby strengthening the link between preclinical predictions and observed clinical responses [24]. Similarly, AI-supported adaptive clinical trials differ from traditional fixed trial designs by enabling dynamic patient stratification, cohort enrichment, dose optimization, and protocol refinement based on interim evidence [45]. These capabilities may improve trial efficiency while increasing the probability of identifying clinically meaningful treatment effects. Nevertheless, although early studies suggest reductions in late-stage failure risk and improved decision quality, robust prospective evidence demonstrating sustained improvements in overall clinical success rates remains limited, underscoring the need for further validation in real-world pharmaceutical development programs [17, 20].

Agentic-AI assumes a pivotal function in filling the gap between discovery and clinical manufacturing through autonomous optimization of development schedules, process, and evidence bundles, thus shrinking timescales between preclinical success and clinical development. By melding discovery output, manufacturing, and regulatory constraints into comprehensive decision-making agents, life sciences companies can enhance filing quality, facilitate approvals, and eliminate costly rework in later development stages. Practical implementation examples illustrate the maturity and viability of R&D with Agentic-AI. In-silico Medicine has illustrated the potency of autonomous discovery by propelling an AI-developed drug candidate targeting idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis from target discovery to clinical development within 18 months, which significantly differs from traditional R&D cycles. Also, atom-wise uses AI decision-making agents to rapidly screen enormous chemical databases by collaborating with leading pharmas to rapidly augment initial R&D, thus expanding their pipeline and R&D output [29, 68]. These instances emphasize that R&D instead of being augmented by Agentic-AI, has been transformed by it itself in terms of structure, rhythm, and risk.

Recent advances in Agentic-AI have expanded the role of AI from predictive analytics to autonomous execution of complex pharmaceutical workflows. Emerging agentic frameworks, including AutoGPT [46], LangGraph [69], CrewAI [70], and Microsoft AutoGen [71], demonstrate how LLM-driven agents can autonomously plan tasks, coordinate multi-agent interactions, access external tools, and iteratively refine outputs toward predefined objectives. In pharmaceutical research, such systems offer opportunities to automate hypothesis generation, experimental design, candidate prioritization, literature synthesis, and decision support across the drug development continuum. Unlike conventional AI approaches that primarily generate recommendations, agentic systems can execute and adapt multi-step workflows with limited human intervention, thereby supporting a more autonomous and integrated R&D ecosystem.

Case study: Agentic-AI for autonomous drug discovery

Several emerging examples illustrate the practical potential of agentic and semi-agentic frameworks in pharmaceutical research. Insilico Medicine using its Pharma.AI platform has developed a small-molecule TRAF2- and NCK-interacting kinase (TNIK) inhibitor, rentosertib (formerly ISM001–055/INS018–055) for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), which progressed from target discovery to clinical translation in a substantially shortened timeframe compared with conventional approaches [29, 72].

Pharma.AI does not represent a single independent tool, but rather represents a modular and coordinates pipeline of purpose-built AI engines. PandaOmics, a target-discovery engine performed the mining of multi-omics data, scientific literature, and patents using deep feature synthesis, causal inference, and pathway reconstruction methods [73] was used to nominate and prioritize anti-fibrotic targets. TNIK was ranked as a top candidate among anti-fibrotic targets [74]. In turn, the generative chemistry engine called Chemistry42 was employed to design a series of inhibitors of TNIK to perform iterative optimization and evaluate potency and selectivity of the synthesized series of analogs, resulting in INS018–055 being nominated as a preclinical candidate [74]. Medicine42/inClinico, a separate predictive module, was then used to help forecast clinical trial outcomes.

The project advanced from target hypothesis to the identification of a preclinical candidate in a period of about 18 months [74]. On the other hand, the downstream clinical efficacy of this program has been independently peer-reviewed and clinically validated as evidenced by a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled phase IIa trial (GENESIS-IPF; 71 IPF patients across 22 sites), in which the 60 mg once daily dosing achieved a mean change in FVC of +98.4 mL after 12 weeks compared with a mean reduction of −20.3 mL for placebo [75].

The rentosertib program is best classified as a semi-agentic, AI-enabled pipeline rather than a fully agentic system. Its constituent modules (PandaOmics, Chemistry42, inClinico) each perform autonomous reasoning within a bounded task and are coordinated in sequence, but the system does not autonomously execute physical experiments, does not independently authorize advancement to synthesis or clinical trial, and does not close the prediction-to-outcome loop without human mediation at each transition. This is distinct from a fully agentic architecture, in which autonomous agents would plan, execute, and evaluate multi-step experimental workflows (e.g., automated synthesis and assay execution) with minimal human oversight.

It must be noted that the rentosertib program is best classified as a semi-agentic, AI-based pipeline than an agentic pipeline. The components of this pipeline (PandaOmics, Chemistry42, and inClinico) do some independent reasoning in a limited task and operate sequentially. However, the system does not independently conduct physical experimentation, authorize further steps toward synthesis or clinical trials, and complete the loop of prediction to result without human intervention in the process. It is different from an entirely agentic design, where the agents would independently plan, conduct, and evaluate multistep experiment protocols (e.g., synthesis and assay execution).

Recent general-purpose AI agent architectures like AutoGPT, CrewAI, LangGraph, and Microsoft AutoGen demonstrate the technological building blocks for such autonomy, including task decomposition, tools utilization, and iterative planning, but currently the applicability of these technologies for closed-loop experimentation in drug discovery is not experimentally confirmed but rather hypothetical in the context of a peer-reviewed program. The rentosertib case therefore represents the current state of practice: several specialized AI modules working together to significantly reducing the time required for specific discovery steps under human supervision, whereas agentic drug discovery is still futuristic.

Similarly, Recursion Pharmaceuticals combines large-scale biological datasets, automated experimentation, and machine-learning-driven prediction systems to create closed-loop discovery workflows that continuously refine biological hypotheses and compound prioritization strategies [65]. Such platforms exemplify the transition toward agentic drug discovery ecosystems in which autonomous agents interact with experimental and computational resources, evaluate outcomes, and adapt subsequent actions based on newly generated evidence.

Future implementations of Agentic-AI are expected to further integrate laboratory automation, digital twins, real-world evidence, and regulatory intelligence systems into unified multi-agent architectures. These capabilities could enable autonomous orchestration of drug discovery activities from target identification through candidate selection and translational decision-making while maintaining human oversight at critical decision points.

Agentic-AI in commercial strategy and market engagement

Conventionally, end-to-end commercial planning spanning forecasting, segmentation, budget allocation, and execution has required six to eighteen months, limiting responsiveness to dynamic market conditions. Agentic-AI systems compress this cycle to a matter of months by autonomously integrating market intelligence, real-world evidence, and performance feedback into continuously updated decision frameworks [76]. Additionally, one of the most impactful applications of Agentic-AI lies in budget optimization and channel strategy. Autonomous agents evaluate historical sales performance, promotional effectiveness, competitive activity, and regional market dynamics to recommend optimal allocation across digital, in-person, and omnichannel engagement pathways. Unlike static annual planning models, these systems simulate multiple scenarios in parallel and dynamically reallocate resources as market conditions evolve, enabling brand teams to move from retrospective analysis to proactive, data-driven decision-making [33, 34].

The segmentation and targeting of healthcare providers are yet another area that has been completely transformed using Agentic-AI. Traditional methods of segmentation use intermittent clustering carried out manually because of the volume of prescriptions and specialty. But what the AI agents do on a continuous basis is process large data sets of prescribing habits, demographics of patients, evidence-based clinical practices, and engagement data to optimize micro-targeting strategies on a near-real-time basis, allowing pharmaceutical companies to engage patients more precisely [77]. Furthermore, Agentic-AI advances commercial effectiveness through predictive modeling of physician behavior and prescribing trajectories. By learning from longitudinal market data, AI agents forecast treatment adoption patterns, identify early adopters, and anticipate shifts driven by new evidence or guideline updates. Outreach strategies are autonomously adjusted based on feedback signals, allowing sales and medical affairs teams to focus on high-impact interactions rather than broad, low-yield coverage models [40].

In the areas of marketing and sales, there is a growing use of ‘next best action’ agents, which bring together behavioral analysis and market feedback. These agents suggest and, in the case of the most advanced ones, enact optimal subsequent actions, e.g., educational, peer-to-peer, and evidence-sharing initiatives, according to the individual preferences and behavior of health care professionals. This dynamic form of engagement stems from the issue of data overload without actionable insights, a paradigm that has existed in the commercial space for a considerable period [78]. Rare disease commercialization is where the strengths of Agentic-AI become even more distinctive for outlier detection in complicated data sets. By automatically examining electronic health records, diagnostic codes, referral data, and genomic markers, Agentic-AI components are capable of automatically pointing to possible undiagnosed groups. The implications allow for better education outreach and patient access solutions, which represent an important and under-addressed market information gap where conventional market research is not possible because of the low prevalence and lack of data comprehensiveness for these rare disease markets [79, 80].

Global realignment of pharmaceutical power in the Agentic-AI era

The accelerating adoption of Agentic-AI is changing the global competitive landscape, with far-reaching geopolitical and economic consequences. As the increasingly autonomous AI system effects on discovery, development, manufacturing, and commercialization become more pronounced, national and regional capabilities related to AI infrastructure, data governance, and regulatory agility now are paramount factors in defining the direction and cadence of biopharmaceutical innovation leadership. The United States presently possesses ~ 40% of the worldwide pharmaceutical market and roughly half of international sales revenue. It, therefore, is strategically situated at a critical juncture, where continued mastery of Agentic-AI will solidify-or potentially chip away at-its perennial dominance [80].

Agentic-AI introduces a democratizing force into drug innovation by lowering traditional barriers associated with scale, capital intensity, and legacy infrastructure. Countries with advanced computational expertise but comparatively smaller pharmaceutical sectors are increasingly able to compete through AI-enabled discovery and development pipelines. This trend is particularly evident in China, where biotechnology firms have rapidly expanded their global footprint. In 2024, approximately one-third of in-licensed drug candidates originated from Chinese biotechs, reflecting a strategic shift in global innovation flows and signaling a redistribution of early-stage discovery leadership toward Asia.

China’s growing influence is further reinforced by its expanding pharmaceutical market, projected to increase total volume by ~ 20% over the next five years. The country’s integrated approach combining state-supported AI research, large-scale health data access, and rapid clinical execution creates a competitive environment distinct from Western innovation models (Fig. 5). However, China’s regulatory framework for AI in healthcare differs substantially from that of the United States and the European Union (EU), potentially generating asymmetric advantages or risks depending on how global standards for transparency, validation, and accountability evolve [81].

Conversely, the EU has taken a precautionary and more organized way of regulation via the AI Act. This act has provided adequate protection for high-risk applications for AI technology, such as life sciences and healthcare. By acting on transparency, risk, and the protection of fundamental rights, the EU’s regulations for AI seek to ensure that innovation and trust in society are properly balanced. This could result in limiting fast-tracking for its applications, yet at least there has been clarity and potential for sustainable developments for AI-based pharmaceutical systems in the EU in the longer term [76]. The United States, being at the cutting edge technologically, does not yet have comprehensive regulations for AI technology and its applications in the healthcare industry. While the FDA has already provided guidelines for the credibility and lifecycle management of AI models, there has yet to be comprehensive federal regulation for their application in the healthcare industry. The end of a federal working group for the governance of healthcare AI could potentially create volatility for U.S. companies while also providing for short-term innovation flexibility [40].

This regulatory divergence across major markets is creating distinct innovation pathways and competitive strategies. Pharmaceutical companies operating globally must increasingly navigate heterogeneous governance regimes while maintaining interoperable AI systems across jurisdictions. Organizations capable of aligning Agentic-AI development with multiple regulatory frameworks while preserving data integrity, explainability, and auditability are likely to gain durable competitive advantages. Further, at the industry level, Agentic-AI is expected to accelerate consolidation, as firms with robust AI capabilities and data assets outperform competitors reliant on traditional R&D and operational models. While consolidation may enhance efficiency and capital deployment, it also raises concerns about reduced diversity in innovation. As a result, many organizations are strategically redirecting AI-driven investments toward therapeutic areas characterized by high unmet medical need and limited generic competition, including gene therapies, cell therapies, and rare diseases, where Agentic-AI can deliver disproportionate value by optimizing complex, data-intensive development pathways [79, 82]. These dynamics suggest that the global pharmaceutical industry is entering a period of structural realignment, in which leadership will be defined not only by scientific excellence or market size, but by the ability to deploy Agentic-AI responsibly, at scale, and across regulatory boundaries.

Regulatory approaches to AI are evolving differently across major pharmaceutical markets. The U.S. FDA follows a risk-based, product-oriented framework focused on model credibility, validation, transparency, and context-of-use for AI-supported drug development and regulatory decision-making [17]. In contrast, the EU AI Act establishes a comprehensive legal framework that categorizes AI systems according to risk levels and mandates requirements for transparency, accountability, and human oversight across multiple sectors, including healthcare [83]. China has adopted a more centralized governance approach that combines regulatory oversight of algorithms and generative AI systems with strong national initiatives to accelerate AI innovation and deployment [84]. India, meanwhile, is pursuing a sector-driven and innovation-focused strategy through initiatives such as the National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence and the IndiaAI Mission, emphasizing responsible AI development while fostering technological growth [85]. These differing regulatory approaches are likely to influence the pace of Agentic-AI adoption, cross-border collaboration, and regulatory harmonization across the global pharmaceutical industry.

Future prospects and challenges

Agentic-AI and the expansion of therapeutic possibilities

These systems will likely serve as self-updating decision engines in light of the integration of multi-omics data, pathway discoveries, and clinical evidence. These, therefore, have the potential to reshape drug discovery and development in real time [21, 40, 61]. Agentic-AI is likely to have a transformative role in enabling adaptive precision medicine within oncology. Future systems may iteratively refine treatment strategies at the level of individual patients through continuous analyses of genomic, transcriptomic, imaging, and longitudinal clinical data. In contrast to static protocols, Agentic-AI platforms may be able to autonomously optimize combination regimens of drugs, dosing schedules, and treatment sequencing in response to tumor evolution and patient-specific responses. This would also support the transition toward continuously adaptive cancer therapy [62, 86, 87]. Additionally, the role of integrated continuous biomonitoring is also expected to further extend the clinical reach of Agentic-AI. By virtue of its capability to synthesize the information obtained from blood testing, tissue profiling, and wearable biosensors, the system is expected to identify subtle manifestations that may exist before the actual clinical event, a capability which would still be difficult for human-only decision making and traditional-AI [40, 88–90].

Rare disease research may represent an important future application area for Agentic-AI despite the inherent challenge of limited patient data. Rather than overcoming data scarcity directly, agentic systems could facilitate the integration of heterogeneous evidence sources, including genomic data, biomedical literature, clinical records, disease ontologies, and knowledge graphs [79, 91]. By leveraging foundation models, transfer-learning strategies, and autonomous hypothesis-generation workflows, Agentic-AI may help identify disease mechanisms, prioritize therapeutic targets, and guide experimental design in data-constrained settings. Nevertheless, the effectiveness of such approaches will remain dependent on data quality, domain expertise, and rigorous validation before clinical translation [33, 34, 92].

Advances in virtual screening and mechanistic simulation are likewise expected to redefine the scale of early-stage discovery. Future Agentic-AI systems may autonomously conduct large-scale in silico experiments, simulating molecular interactions, pathway perturbations, and systems-level biological responses across expansive chemical spaces. By prioritizing promising candidates before physical experimentation, these systems could substantially reduce experimental burden while expanding exploration into novel regions of chemical and biological space [29, 93–95]. These forward-looking developments suggest that Agentic-AI will not merely enhance existing workflows but will enable qualitatively new modes of therapeutic innovation. As these systems mature, their capacity for autonomous adaptation across discovery, development, and clinical optimization is likely to redefine how medicines are conceived, evaluated, and personalized, positioning Agentic-AI as a foundational pillar of next-generation pharmaceutical innovation.

Strategic readiness for the agentic transition

First and foremost, the relevant question facing top executives in the pharma industry is no longer whether Agentic-AI is coming to alter the business, but rather how soon the industry can position itself to absorb the effects. It is believed over the next two to three years, patent lives, accelerating competitive forces, regulatory changes, and changes in the workforce will come together in such a way that strategic choices made over this period have disproportionate effects over time. Industry reports have clearly shown that first movers in the adoption of top AI technology have a productivity advantage over time that is hard to overcome when norms become established. Secondly, the identification of high-value pilot areas where Agentic-AI is able to show demonstrable impact must be done on a priority basis. Research and development, clinical operations, and supply chain resilience have thus turned out to be lead domains for early adoption because autonomous agents are already showing an ability to accelerate hypothesis creation, optimize trial workflows, and respond dynamically to operational disruptions. Strategic pilots allow firms to validate value creation while simultaneously building in internal governance and trust mechanisms around autonomous systems [76]. Third, workforce readiness is a make-or-break facilitator of meaningful adoption. Agentic-AI does not replace isolated functions; rather, it reshapes workflows across disciplines requiring shared literacy among data scientists, clinicians, regulatory professionals, and commercial teams alike. Research in AI transformation underlines the fact that organizations which invest in cross-functional AI education and change management tend to outperform those that bank solely on technical specialists, especially in regulated industries such as healthcare and pharmaceuticals [96].. Fourthly, robust data infrastructure must be treated as a strategic asset rather than a technical prerequisite. Agentic-AI systems depend on high-quality, interoperable, and continuously updated data streams to support autonomous reasoning and execution. Without strong data governance, standardisation, and integration across R&D, clinical, and commercial functions, the benefits of Agentic-AI remain constrained and operational risks increase [97]. Finally, proactive engagement with regulators is essential during this formative phase. Regulatory frameworks governing autonomous and adaptive AI systems in healthcare are still evolving, and early engagement allows companies to help shape validation standards, transparency expectations, and risk management practices. Organisations that establish credibility with regulators at this stage are more likely to secure long-term flexibility and first-mover advantages as Agentic-AI becomes embedded in pharmaceutical development and commercialisation pathways [98, 99]. These steps underscore that Agentic-AI must be approached as a strategic transformation rather than an experimental technology. Companies that act decisively now will be better positioned to lead the industry through the next phase of innovation, while those that delay risk being structurally disadvantaged as autonomy becomes a baseline capability.

Temporal advantage and strategic lead in the Agentic-AI transition

The window for decisive strategic intervention is rapidly narrowing, as competitive advantage today is defined less by incremental optimization and more by the early adoption of disruptive core technologies. Industry foresight identifies Agentic-AI as the primary driver of long-term value creation within the life sciences, largely due to its unique capacity for autonomous experimentation and independent decision-making. This shift has already precipitated a surge in merger and acquisition activity as organizations move to secure the foundational data and specialized talent essential for maintaining relevance in a shifting market. Historically, periods of regulatory recalibration and economic stimulus have served as catalysts for industry consolidation, and Agentic-AI represents the clear focal point of the current cycle. Far beyond a mere cost-saving measure, this technology necessitates a fundamental reimagining of the drug discovery and development lifecycle, where companies are no longer just acquiring software, but are instead competing for control over the proprietary models and intelligence frameworks that will govern the next generation of medicine.

Those organizations which start the usage of Agentic-AI before their competition are expected to create long-run structural advantages. This allows organizations to create organizational capability related to human AI collaboration, set up their own rules of governance within the company on autonomous systems, as well as set up their own regulatory credibility on AI-enabled submissions. This increases exponentially as data networks build up on their own experimenting and clinical data through the usage of Agentic AI, making the decision-making process even more complex for the late starters. Most importantly, this paradigm shift from traditional machine learning towards Agentic-AI has already begun. Autonomous systems have begun executing their operations within pharmaceutical research environments. These systems not only design experiments but also perform virtual screening as well as optimize molecule structures independently. This shift has marked a structural shift within knowledge production and is initiating a shift from sequential human-guided knowledge production towards its autonomous production [100].

Early adoption also delivers significant advantages in human capital acquisition and retention. As AI literacy emerges as a core professional competency in the agentic economy, highly skilled researchers, computational scientists, and clinicians are increasingly attracted to organisations offering advanced autonomous platforms. Empirical evidence from prior technological transitions suggests that such talent concentration accelerates innovation cycles and reinforces competitive divergence between early movers and laggards [100]. These dynamics suggest that Agentic-AI functions as a temporal differentiator rather than a neutral efficiency tool. Firms that act decisively during this formative period are positioned to shape scientific standards, regulatory norms, and organisational models, securing leadership roles in the next phase of pharmaceutical innovation.

Pharmaceutical innovation in 2030: the Agentic-AI driven Enterprise

By 2030, the pharmaceutical landscape will likely look fundamentally different. A ‘perfect storm’ of expiring patents, shifting regulations, and the rise of Agentic-AI is pushing the industry toward a sharp, discontinuous break from its traditional roots. We are moving away from incremental updates and toward a reality where autonomous AI serves as the core infrastructure for life sciences, fundamentally changing how value is created across a drug’s entire lifecycle. In this new era, success won’t be determined by a company’s size or budget, but by how well it orchestrates the partnership between human intuition and machine intelligence. The winners will be firms that weave agentic systems into their scientific reasoning and clinical strategies, freeing up human experts to focus on the high-level ethics and creative problem-solving that machines can’t touch. Essentially, we are seeing a pivot from labor-heavy models to intelligence-first organizational designs [101].

The industry is also likely to consolidate. We expect to see a few global leaders emerge who have mastered end-to-end AI capabilities from the lab bench to the pharmacy shelf. These organizations will operate with lean cost structures that make it finally possible to invest in ‘unviable’ areas like ultra-rare diseases or complex, multimodal therapies [3]. At the same time, regulatory bodies would not be standing still; they will co-evolve with the tech. By the end of the decade, we expect standard frameworks for AI validation and governance to be the norm. Companies that help shape these rules while staying transparent will win through faster approvals and deeper trust with regulators [18].

At its core, this shift is about finding a new equilibrium between people and machines. We are moving toward a model where AI takes over the exhausting work of large-scale data processing and routine task execution, which finally allows human experts to focus on what they do best: high-level strategy and patient-centered care. In this new landscape, the strength of a company won’t be judged by its total headcount. Instead, ‘organizational health’ will be defined by a leadership team’s ability to manage, train, and ethically steer this dual workforce.

Challenges in operationalising Agentic-AI

Empirical studies demonstrate resistance to change is the most pervasive challenge, with over half of pharmaceutical companies listing cultural resistance to change as the main hindrance to innovation around AI. This is bespoken in adoption rates, where very few companies report having fully operational Agentic-AI systems in place, highlighting the divide between planning and implementation. One area where resistance to change is especially evident is in concerns about data readiness and system integration. Agentic-AI requires quality and continually synchronized and integrated data flows to be able to work independently. At the current state of the pharmaceutical industry, pharmaceutical data infrastructure is very fragmented, with data silos ranging from discovery platforms through clinical trial systems, and through to manufactured product and commercial systems. This integration and ensuring consistency in meaning, auditability, and system governance is a very challenging task and is imperative for Agentic-AI to be able to work without posing safety issues [3]. Additionally, regulatory compliance further complicates large-scale implementation. AI systems involved in drug discovery, clinical development, manufacturing, or medical communications must satisfy stringent regulatory expectations related to transparency, validation, auditability, and lifecycle management. For Agentic-AI capable of adaptive behaviour this introduces additional complexity, as regulators traditionally assess static models rather than continuously learning systems. The absence of fully harmonized global standards for autonomous AI creates uncertainty, slowing deployment and increasing compliance costs [18].

Human trust represents another critical barrier. The prospect of delegating high-stakes scientific or clinical decisions to autonomous agents generates understandable concern among domain experts. Researchers, clinicians, and regulators often require clear explanations of how AI systems arrive at conclusions, particularly when patient safety or regulatory approval is at stake. As a result, XAI techniques and human-in-the-loop frameworks are increasingly viewed as essential components for adoption, enabling oversight while preserving the efficiency gains of autonomy [102, 103]. Moreover, data security and privacy risks also intensify as agentic systems gain broader access to sensitive patient, genomic, and proprietary research data. Ensuring compliance with regulations such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) requires robust cybersecurity architectures, access controls, and continuous monitoring. Autonomous agents that act across organisational boundaries amplify the importance of secure-by-design AI infrastructures [104].

However, apart from technology, the scale of the change required is essentially organisational. For the successful implementation of the change, there is a requirement for reskilling, redeployment, and change management. Industry research indicates that a majority of pharmaceutical company executives regard reskilling and redeployment as being less expensive than recruiting new staff, recognising the irreplaceable value of human intelligence, which must keep pace with the development of AI technology [105]. For the successful implementation of Agentic-AI, there is a requirement for a paradigm shift not only in technology but also a requirement for a different way of working, different mindsets, and different governing structures that will be accountable, equitable, and optimize the use of AI technology. The problem of successfully implementing Agentic-AI is no longer a matter of the maturity of the technology but the maturity of organisations to successfully leverage the technology. Organisations that start early and build the foundation for data, regulatory, people, and governing structures will be better positioned to unlock the transformative potential of AI technology.

Conclusion

The pharmaceutical and life science landscape is experiencing the transformative impact of AI, including generative and agent-centric AI, and is expected to broaden further. The recent efforts of the regulatory bodies, the FDA and the EU, highlight the increasing focus on the use of the efficiency and prediction capabilities of AI while maintaining the validity and safe delivery of healthcare to the patient. The industry studies and international reports demonstrate the ability to overcome the timeline issues and workforce and productivity concerns with the help of AI, provided the validation and integrity of the data are ensured. As the regulatory policies become more mature and practical experiences become common, the successful application of AI technology into the pharmaceutical industry would require the participation of various stakeholders and the responsible use of the technology.
