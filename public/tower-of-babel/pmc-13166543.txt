An analysis of HIV-related behaviors and attitudes among college students in eastern China: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Weiyong Chen, Xin Zhou, Zhongrong Yang, Qiaoqin Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recently, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection among young university students has garnered significant global attention. This study aimed to compare the demographics, behaviors, and knowledge of male and female college students, in addition to investigating the self-reported HIV-related behaviors and attitudes of sexually active college students in Zhejiang Province, eastern China. This cross-sectional study was conducted using stratified cluster sampling. A self-developed web-based questionnaire was used to collect demographic and sexual behavior data, and those only sexually active students were analyzed. The χ2 test was then used to compare the characteristics of the participants across different genders. A total of 3873 students who reported engaging in sexual activity were evaluated in this study, representing 12.2% of the total student population. Among them, 2734 were men, accounting for 19.1% of all male students (2734/14,320). Sexually active college students had an average age of 20.18 ± 1.37 years. Significant statistical differences were found between male and female students about age, grade, place of household registration, monthly living cost, family relationships, acquired immunodeficiency syndrome-themed lectures or health education courses, HIV testing publicity, HIV risk self-assessment, voluntary counseling and testing for HIV, acceptance of one-night stands and commercial sex, condom use self-efficacy, and casual sex. The findings highlight significant gender disparities in sexual behaviors and HIV-related awareness among sexually active students, suggesting the need for gender-tailored sexual health education programs that address socioeconomic factors (e.g., household registration disparities) while strengthening condom use self-efficacy interventions, particularly for male students exhibiting higher-risk sexual practices. Sexually active college students displayed high levels of sexual openness and knowledge regarding HIV testing, but low testing rates, condom use, and condom use self-efficacy. In addition, a higher proportion of male students engaged in casual sex.

1. Introduction

Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS) is a major global infectious disease that poses a significant social and public health challenge for countries worldwide.[1,2] The human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) is primarily transmitted through sexual activity, leading to numerous challenges in the prevention and control of AIDS. In 2022, approximately 30% of the daily reported new cases of HIV infection globally, amounting to 3600 cases, were aged 15 to 24 years, with AIDS-related deaths being the leading cause of mortality in this age group.[3] Active engagement in sexual behaviors among college students makes them a priority population for HIV/AIDS prevention and control efforts.[4] Owing to their inadequate knowledge of HIV prevention and lack of awareness regarding self-protection, college students often engage in high-risk behaviors such as one-night stands, having multiple sexual partners and inconsistent condom use during sexual activity, which increase the risk of HIV transmission.[5,6]

HIV infection among young university students has garnered significant global attention.[7] The increased occurrence of premarital sexual activity among college students has led to risky behaviors such as commercial sex, casual sex, and sexual activities following alcohol consumption, elevating the risk of acquiring and transmitting HIV.[8] Studies indicate a higher prevalence of sexual activity among male college students compared to females,[9,10] yet many sexually active students lack a comprehensive understanding of the associated risks. This is compounded by insufficient HIV/AIDS education in universities,[11,12] resulting in poor knowledge, low self-protection awareness, and deficiencies in health education. These factors significantly increase HIV risk among sexually active students.[13] Research on same-sex sexual behavior and interventions among male students highlights the challenge of ensuring consistent condom use, which contributes to rising sexually transmitted diseases and unplanned pregnancies.[14,15] Therefore, a comprehensive investigation into attitudes and behaviors is crucial, and promoting safe sexual practices through education and behavioral modifications is essential for effective HIV/AIDS prevention among college students in Zhejiang Province.

Understanding the characteristics, behaviors, and attitudes of college students who engage in sexual activities is crucial for developing effective intervention measures against HIV/AIDS to prevent further transmission within this population, but few large-scale provincial studies in China have analyzed gender differences using validated instruments. This study focused on college students in Zhejiang Province as the target population, with a particular emphasis on those who engaged in sexual activities. By analyzing baseline survey data collected from 13 universities/colleges, this study aimed to provide a better understanding of the knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors of college students of different genders who self-reported engaging in sexual activities related to HIV/AIDS, providing a scientific foundation for the development of intervention strategies.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Sampling method and participants

In October and November 2018, a cross-sectional survey was conducted among university and college students across 13 universities located in 11 prefecture-level cities in Zhejiang Province. The local Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommended the universities, including 3 in Hangzhou and 1 in each of the remaining 10 cities. The survey employed a stratified cluster sampling method, initially randomly selecting 3 departments from each university or college using a random number table. The departments were subsequently divided into 4 grade levels, and the class groups were randomly selected using a random number table. There are a total of 32,500 students from 13 universities, and after excluding those with incomplete personal information, there are 31,674 students left, with an effective response rate of 97.5%. Among them, there are 14,320 males and 17,354 females. Three thousand seven hundred eighty-three college students who reported having engaged in sexual activity were selected as the participants, while others were excluded. A survey questionnaire was used to gather self-reported data on the sexual behavior of college students for statistical analysis.

2.2. Questionnaire design

The survey questionnaire was developed through a literature review,[16,17] research team discussions, and a pre-survey conducted among students in a particular class in a selected school. The survey questions covered general demographic characteristics, AIDS awareness and attitudes, sexual behaviors, and intervention experiences as the main topics. The 3 knowledge-based questions about HIV/AIDS were “AIDS is an incurable and highly contagious disease,” “By appearance, one can judge if a person is infected with HIV,” and “Consistently using condoms can reduce the risk of HIV infection and transmission.” The correct responses of these questions were “AIDS is an incurable and highly contagious disease,” “By appearance, one cannot judge if a person is infected with HIV,” and “Consistently using condoms can reduce the risk of HIV infection and transmission.” Casual sexual activity referred to sexual activity that the participant has engaged in with other people in the past year, apart from their fixed sexual partners. A fixed sexual partner was defined as a participant’s self-reported relatively fixed sexual partner in the past year and not a temporary sexual partner.

The self-efficacy of condom use was measured using a scale developed by Hanna et al,[17] which included 3 questions: do you have the confidence to discuss condom use with a sexual partner before engaging in sexual activity, do you have the confidence not to engage in sexual activity if a partner refuses to use or does not have a condom, and do you have the confidence to prepare a condom in advance before engaging in sexual activity. Each question offered 5 response options: very confident, confident, somewhat confident, not confident, and not at all confident, corresponding to scores of 3, 2, 1, 0, and −1, respectively. The measurement scores were categorized into 3 groups: ≥9, 5 to 8, ≤4. This measurement had a Cronbach alpha coefficient of 0.868.

2.3. Data collection

This study used a cross-sectional survey design. The teachers collectively administered an online electronic survey questionnaire to in-school students. Outside the school, students received an online link to the survey questionnaire and were instructed to complete it independently by following the instructions stated at the top of the questionnaire. The investigation team consisted of professionals from the local CDC and counselors from various university classes. They underwent standardized training and subsequently completed an anonymous survey using a uniform questionnaire. Before initiating the survey, the investigators explained the survey’s purpose, significance, methods, and privacy protection policies to the participants. This information was included in the introductory section of the questionnaire. The participants were notified that the survey aimed to develop strategies for preventing HIV-related diseases among students. They were assured of the survey’s anonymity and informed that only aggregated data, not individual data, would be analyzed. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Zhejiang Provincial CDC (approval no. 2018-036), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2013. All participant details were de-identified in this study, which conformed to STROBE guidelines.[18] Commercial sexual behavior refers to individuals who have engaged in commercial sexual activities involving monetary transactions in the past year (such as prostitution).

2.4. Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using the IBM SPSS Statistics version 21.0 software (IBM). Categorical variables were presented as percentages (%), while continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation. The demographic and sexual behavioral characteristics of the participants of different sexes were compared using the χ2 test. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

3. Results

3.1. General demographic characteristics of the participants

A total of 3873 students who reported engaging in sexual activities were included, representing 12.2% of the total student population. Among them, 2734 were men, accounting for 19.1% of all male students (2734/14,320), and 1139 were women, accounting for 6.6% of all female students (1139/17,354). Regarding monthly living expenses, 24.4%, 39.8%, and 35.8% had budgets of ≤1000, 1001–1500, and ≥1501 yuan, respectively. In addition, 72.0% reported having harmonious family relationships. The average age of the sexually active male college students was 20.21 ± 1.39 years. There were statistically significant differences (P < .05) between the 2 groups regarding age, grade, registered residence, monthly living expenses, and family relationships (Table 1). 3.2. AIDS awareness and attitude of participants

Among the 3873 sexually active college students, the 3 knowledge-based questions about HIV/AIDS: “AIDS is an incurable and highly contagious disease,” “By appearance, one can judge if a person is infected with HIV,” and “Consistently using condoms can reduce the risk of HIV infection and transmission,” yielded accuracy rates of 74.7%, 87.0%, and 96.2%, respectively. Furthermore, 42.1% students engaged in one-night stands, and 24.8% engaged in commercial sex. Among these, 35.0% participants reported condom use self-efficacy scores of 9. In addition, 90.2% participants were aware that disease control centers provided HIV testing services.

The correct response rate of the male student group for the knowledge question “AIDS is an incurable and highly contagious disease” was higher than that of the female student group (P = .024). Similarly, the correct response rate of the female student group for the knowledge question “By appearance, one can judge if a person is infected with HIV,” as well as the proportion of respondents who know that the CDC provides HIV testing services, were higher than those of the male student group (P < .05). There was no statistically significant difference in the correct response rate for the knowledge question “Consistently using condoms can reduce the risk of HIV infection and transmission” between the male and female student groups (P > .05). The proportion of male students who engaged in one-night stands, commercial sex, and had a self-efficacy measure of 9 for condom use was higher than that in the female student group, and the difference was statistically significant (P < .05). 3.3. Analysis of the sexual behavior characteristics and related interventions

Over the past year, 64.9% of the 3873 participants attended AIDS-themed lectures or health education classes conducted in schools. Of these, 58.2% received school promotion for HIV testing, and 33.0% participants had undergone an AIDS risk self-assessment conducted by the school. A total of 5.1% participants underwent voluntary counseling and testing for HIV. Within the past year, 71.6% had engaged in sexual intercourse with regular partners. In total, 52.7% participants consistently used condoms during sexual encounters. In addition, 17.8% had participated in casual sexual activities in the past year.

The proportion of male students who had attended AIDS-themed lectures or health education courses, HIV testing publicity, self-assessment of the AIDS risk, and voluntary HIV counseling and testing within the past year was significantly higher than that of the female students (P < .05). A significantly higher proportion of male students (21.8%) engaged in casual sexual activities in the past year compared to female students (8.3%; P < .05). Female students had a significantly higher likelihood of engaging in sexual activities with a regular partner and having regular partners who were social personnel compared to male students (P < .05). However, there was no statistically significant difference in condom usage during sexual activities with regular partners between male and female students (P > .05). 4. Discussion

This study was a cross-sectional survey conducted among college students to better understand the demographics, behaviors, and knowledge levels of male and female college students who self-reported engaging in sexual activity. In addition, we investigated the self-reported HIV-related behaviors and attitudes of sexually active college students. The study results revealed that 12.2% of college students self-reported engaging in sexual activity, with a higher proportion of male students. This finding suggests that as sexual attitudes become more open, a significant proportion of college students may engage in unsafe sexual behaviors, leading to a major risk of HIV transmission.

Compared to female students, more male students exhibited characteristics such as discordant family relationships, a high proportion of freshmen, and low monthly living expenses. Previous studies have indicated that the onset of sexual behavior among college students is occurring at increasingly younger ages, with factors such as high monthly living expenses, poor family relationships, and male sex influencing the sexual behavior.[19,20] Consequently, these findings suggest that male students may benefit from educational interventions related to condom use and HIV testing awareness.

The study findings demonstrated that the prevalence of casual sexual behavior among male students in Zhejiang Province was 21.8%, with male students exhibiting a greater degree of openness to sexual behavior than their female counterparts. Prior research has indicated that a higher level of openness towards attitudes regarding sexual behavior can contribute to unsafe practices, such as having multiple sexual partners, thereby elevating the risk of contracting HIV as well as other reproductive health conditions and unintended pregnancies.[21] Male students exhibited a higher propensity to engage in risky sexual activities,[22] which increased their vulnerability to physical and psychological harm. Concurrently, the accuracy rates for the statements “AIDS is an incurable and highly contagious disease” and “By judging the appearance, one can determine if an individual is infected with HIV” were 74.7% and 87.0% respectively, thereby revealing a shortfall in meeting the newly established 95% awareness threshold for college students at the national level. This study suggests that the proportion of correct responses regarding AIDS awareness and attitudes of participants was suboptimal. Many studies have indicated that bolstering HIV/AIDS awareness within the population and fostering consistent knowledge and behavior are vital measures for safeguarding vulnerable populations.[4,23,24] Hence, it is necessary to promote HIV/AIDS prevention and educational initiatives in schools with a specific focus on fostering sexual ethics and attitudes among male students to mitigate the risk of HIV transmission, and ameliorate the associated physical and psychological health hazards faced by students.

The low uptake of HIV voluntary counseling and testing (5.1%) observed in this study may be attributed to several factors. First, persistent stigma associated with HIV remains a significant barrier, as individuals may fear discrimination and social rejection if their status is disclosed.[25,26] Second, concerns about confidentiality and potential breaches of privacy – particularly in settings where protective mechanisms are perceived as weak – may deter individuals from seeking testing.[27,28] In addition, lack of awareness about the testing process, the implications of test results, and available posttest support may also contribute to low participation rates.[29] Future efforts to increase HIV voluntary counseling and testing should address these barriers through stigma reduction interventions, strengthening confidentiality protocols, and improving health education and communication. We found that 58.2% of the students had received promotions for HIV testing in the past year. In addition, male students outperformed female students in attending HIV-themed lectures, receiving school-based HIV testing promotions, and participating in HIV risk self-assessment. However, 90.2% were aware of the HIV testing services provided by the disease control center. The results of this study provide important information for designing new strategies to increase the uptake of HIV counseling and testing, and ultimately decrease the prevalence of HIV and other sexually transmitted infections. Given the epidemiological and economic costs associated with these diseases, it is critical to propose new interventions to curb their spread.[30] Several studies have demonstrated that being knowledgeable about the availability of HIV testing services at disease control centers, increasing awareness of HIV testing, and engaging in discussions about HIV infection with sexual partners can promote safe sexual behavior among college students, potentially reducing the risk of HIV transmission.[4,24,30,31] Therefore, during the health education process for HIV/AIDS prevention in schools, it is crucial to enhance public awareness of the importance of HIV testing and the availability of services.[32,33] Strengthened cooperation and promotion between disease control centers and universities should be emphasized. Highlighting the significance of informed dating among students, which involves knowing the HIV-infection status prior to entering a relationship, can increase their awareness of HIV testing and improve their ability to protect themselves.

The results of this study show that among college students, the proportion of condom use during sexual activities with a fixed partner was 52.7%, and no significant gender differences were found; however, the self-efficacy measurement for condom use was only 35.0%. The prevalence of HIV risk factors was high among the students, highlighting the importance of consistent condom use as an effective method for reducing the risk of HIV transmission.[34] Therefore, in situations where students lack awareness of risks, it is crucial to emphasize their individual health responsibilities. Increasing condom use among students is an effective measure for mitigating the risk of HIV infection. Furthermore, our findings regarding the low rate of voluntary HIV testing (5.1%) among students highlight a critical gap in preventive healthcare that is not unique to our setting. Comparative data from international studies indicate similar challenges in engaging young students in routine screening. For instance, a large-scale survey in the United States found that approximately 42% of college students reported getting tested for HIV.[35] Similarly, a study in Europe reported that around 7.6% had been tested for HIV among university students.[36] These figures suggest that despite varying public health infrastructures, the reluctance of students to seek diagnostic services remains a persistent global issue.

This study has several limitations. First, owing to its cross-sectional nature, it was not possible to establish causality or determine causative relationships among the identified factors. Second, reliance on self-reported data from participants may introduce potential biases, such as recall bias, which can affect the interpretation of the findings, and same-sex behaviors were not assessed in this study. In the questionnaire design of this study, although the core scales (such as the condom use self-efficacy scale) have been validated for reliability and validity, the overall questionnaire has not undergone large-scale psychometric testing (such as construct validity or cross-cultural adaptation analysis). Future research could adopt a more systematic questionnaire development process, including more comprehensive predictive testing and reliability and validity evaluation. Furthermore, we did not perform additional analyses to compare the sexual behaviors of men with and without HIV lecture knowledge to assess the mediation or moderation structure of knowledge among male participants, and the survey questionnaire used in this study did not include variables related to the age at first sexual intercourse, which may impact the research outcomes. Consequently, it is crucial to validate the results of this study in large-scale, multicenter surveys.

5. Conclusions

Sexually active college students who disclosed their sexual behavior generally accepted diverse sexual attitudes and understood testing procedures, yet demonstrated low actual testing rates and inconsistent condom use. Notably, male students reported a higher incidence of casual sexual encounters and greater behavioral inconsistency. Our findings indicate that tailored educational programs may help improve specifically for the characteristics of this demographic. Findings suggest that educational programs may benefit from enhanced HIV testing awareness, promotion of safe socialization, and dissemination of relevant information. By doing so, it will be possible to raise HIV testing rates and increase the understanding of potential health risks among college students, emphasizing the importance of condom use.
