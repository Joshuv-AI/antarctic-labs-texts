Construction and implantation of a modified trileaflet polytetrafluoroethylene valved conduit for pulmonary valve replacement
Authors: Abel Getachew, Nikita Zadneulitca, Ashok Muralidaran, Castigliano M Bhamidipati, Yoshio Ootaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesHandmade expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE) valved conduits have emerged as a durable and cost-effective option for pulmonary valve replacement. We have reported excellent results of trileaflet ePTFE valved conduit and the longest follow-up exceeded 10 years. Technical precision during valve construction and implantation is critical to optimize valve function and conduit geometry, and we recently modified the technique. We report our updated technique and clinical outcomes.MethodsThe conduit was constructed intraoperatively on a sterile back table while the patient was being prepared by the anesthesia team. Leaflet dimensions were calculated based on conduit diameter, and leaflets were fashioned with 1 mm extensions at each end to reinforce commissural attachment, then sutured to an ePTFE tube graft using 3 CV-5 sutures with external knot tying. The graft was rotated during construction to ensure accurate leaflet alignment and valve competence was confirmed prior to implantation. During implantation, the main pulmonary artery was divided to optimize conduit geometry, minimizing the posterior conduit length and the graft trimmed in a trapezoidal fashion to reduce tension and improve alignment.ResultsFifty-four patients received ePTFE valved conduit. Conduit sizes were 18 mm (n = 7), 20 mm (n = 9), 22 mm (n = 1), 24 mm (n = 36), and 28 mm (n = 1). Median follow-up was 1.4 years (maximum, 4.3 years). There was 1 mortality during follow-up due to infectious pseudoaneurysm of the aortic root after Ross procedure. One patient developed severe pulmonary insufficiency after surgery. Recent echocardiography showed no significant pulmonary stenosis, pulmonary insufficiency, or valve deterioration in the remaining patients, and no catheter-based or surgical reinterventions have been required to date.ConclusionsThis refined technique for the construction and implantation of a trileaflet ePTFE valved conduit is simple, reproducible, and yields excellent early hemodynamic results. Attention to leaflet design, commissural security, and conduit geometry may contribute to low early failure rates and favorable valve performance. Longer-term follow-up is ongoing to assess durability.Key Words: expanded polytetrafluoroethylene, pulmonary valve replacement, right ventricular outflow tract, valved conduit

Central Message

A modified handmade trileaflet ePTFE valved conduit is a reproducible and effective option for pulmonary valve replacement with excellent early durability.

Perspective

Current pulmonary valve replacements are limited by structural deterioration and a finite lifespan. This modified trileaflet ePTFE conduit offers intraoperative customization, excellent early valve performance, and a high rate of freedom from reintervention. These results support further evaluation of this approach as a potentially durable reconstructive option for younger patients.

Pulmonary valve replacement (PVR) is among the most frequently performed surgical procedures in younger people with congenital heart disease, particularly in patients with repaired right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) abnormalities.1 Even with improved treatments, many patients require multiple reinterventions over their lifetime as the result of progressive conduit dysfunction, including stenosis, regurgitation, or both.

A variety of options are currently available for PVR, including autologous pericardial valves, neopulmonary valves using atrial appendage tissue, allografts, bioprosthetic valves, and transcatheter pulmonary valve replacement (TPVR). However, none of these approaches have an optimal outcome for long term. Bioprosthetic valves are prone to structural valve degeneration; allografts and xenografts are limited by calcification and variable durability, and mechanical valves are rarely used in the pulmonary position because of the need for lifelong anticoagulation.2, 3, 4, 5 Although TPVR has expanded treatment options, it remains constrained by anatomic suitability and does not eliminate the need for future interventions.6,7 These limitations are especially important in younger patients, in whom long-term durability and avoiding repeat interventions are important.

Expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE) valved conduits (VCs) have emerged as alternatives for surgical PVR since 2005. Favorable biocompatibility, resistance to calcification, and the ability to tailor conduit geometry have contributed to increasing adoption, with successful implantation reported in more than 1000 patients in the world.8 Our group previously described a simplified trileaflet ePTFE VC and demonstrated successful midterm outcomes, with sustained valve function and low rates of reintervention.9 Continued follow-up has now extended beyond 10 years in a subset of patients, supporting the durability of this approach.1

In this study, we describe an updated technique for the construction and implantation of a trileaflet ePTFE VC and evaluate its clinical outcomes. We hypothesized that these modifications would result in improved durability and sustained valve competence, reducing the need for reintervention.

Methods

This research study was approved by the institutional review board at Oregon Health & Science University (#24166) on March 16, 2022.

The conduit was constructed on the day of surgery while the patient was being prepared by the anesthesia team. Commercially available ePTFE tube grafts (W.L. Gore & Associates, Inc) with diameters of 18, 20, 22, or 24 mm were selected on the basis of patient body size, RVOT dimensions, and anticipated postoperative hemodynamic requirements, as assessed using preoperative cardiac imaging. Because a 28-mm ePTFE tube graft is not commercially available, we constructed a 28-mm VC using a 24-mm graft by making a longitudinal incision and inserting a rectangular ePTFE strip approximately 17 mm wide (4 × π mm + 4 mm for suturing) to increase the graft circumference (Table 1). A 0.1-mm thick ePTFE membrane was used for leaflet construction. The VC was assembled on a sterile back table using a standardized tricuspid valve construction technique as we described previously.9

For conduit preparation, a 24-mm ePTFE conduit (W. L. Gore & Associates) was typically trimmed to approximately 7 cm in length, because excessive conduit length may complicate valve construction and distort final geometry. The conduit was initially inverted to facilitate leaflet construction on the external surface, improving visualization and precision during assembly. A rectangular segment of 0.1-mm ePTFE membrane (W. L. Gore & Associates, Inc) was then tailored according to a standardized geometric design to create 3 symmetric leaflets (Figure 1). For a 24-mm conduit, each leaflet has a length of 24 mm and height of 16.8 mm (70% of leaflet length). Each leaflet has an additional 1-mm suturing margin, which calculates to a 26 mm length and 17.8 mm height. To obtain 3 leaflets of equal size, 1-mm “wings” were added at the left and right edges of the 3-leaflet construct (not to each individual leaflet). These extensions strengthen commissural fixation and help distribute mechanical stress along the suture line.

Leaflet design for 24-mm valved conduit. Dots show the suturing location for attaching to the conduit.

Therefore, the membrane measured approximately 17.8 mm in height and 80 mm in total length, corresponding to 3 leaflet segments of 26 mm each. The membrane was folded into thirds to ensure symmetric cusp geometry, and the inferior margin was trimmed into a smooth semicircular contour while preserving an approximately 5-mm superior margin for coaptation area of the valve leaflets. In the tricuspid configuration, leaflet length corresponds approximately to twice the conduit radius plus 2 mm, resulting in shorter leaflet dimensions that facilitate more physiologic coaptation geometry and improved valve competence (Figure 1).

The leaflet membrane was positioned approximately 1.5 cm below the superior edge of the inverted conduit. The leading edges of the commissures were approximated and secured symmetrically using 3 CV-5 sutures (W. L. Gore & Associates, Inc). The CV-5 sutures were secured to the ePTFE graft with 5 knots, which will be outside after inverting the graft. After circumferential wrapping of the leaflet membrane around the conduit, the remaining commissural fixation points are secured sequentially to preserve equal spacing and geometric symmetry. At the commissure regions within the coaptation area, each suture is placed vertically. Each leaflet was then continuously sutured to the conduit wall using CV-5 sutures. The CV-5 sutures are secured at the nadir of the leaflet and tied with multiple knots inside the ePTFE graft, which will be outside after reinverting the graft. After completion of leaflet attachment, the conduit is reinverted to restore the leaflets to the luminal position. All knots are tied on the external surface of the conduit to minimize intraluminal flow disturbance and prevent thrombus formation.

Commissural reinforcement sutures using 6-0 polypropylene sutures (Johnson & Johnson) were subsequently placed in a vertical mattress fashion at each commissural junction to facilitate sinus like leaflet geometry, enhance structural stability, and prevent regurgitation from the commissure regions (Figure 2).

Vertical sutures to the commissure regions.

Valve competence was assessed by filling the VC with sterile saline, allowing for immediate identification of leaflet prolapse, asymmetry, or valvular insufficiency before implantation. A sterile surgical marking pen was used to indicate conduit orientation and direction of flow. The conduit was specifically oriented so that the primary commissural suture junction would ultimately lie in the posterior position after implantation, thereby minimizing localized mechanical stress at the commissural interface. Construction of the tricuspid ePTFE VC typically takes approximately 40 minutes.

During implantation, the main pulmonary artery was divided to improve exposure and increase the effective distance between the right ventricle and distal pulmonary arteries, facilitating tension-free conduit placement. Posterior anatomic distances were measured using silk sutures to guide conduit length and orientation. Particular attention was paid to conduit rotation and commissural alignment to avoid distortion of leaflet geometry after implantation. Distal anastomosis was accomplished using CV-5 continuous sutures.

The posterior aspect of the conduit was additionally tailored into a trapezoidal configuration to improve spatial geometry, reduce graft redundancy, and facilitate better alignment with the distal pulmonary artery. The anterior aspect of the conduit was adjusted depending on the distance between the main pulmonary artery and RVOT incision (Figure 3). Final conduit positioning was adjusted to achieve minimal tension, optimal orientation, and unobstructed RVOT (Figure 4, Video 1). Patients require the use of aspirin for at least 6 months after implantation on the basis of current American Heart Association recommendations for bioprosthetic valves.

Results

Between March 2022 and May 2026, 54 consecutive patients underwent surgical PVR with a handmade trileaflet ePTFE VC at our institution. The median age at the time of implantation was 9.3 years (range, 6 months to 37 years). The median body weight was 32.7 kg (range, 6.7-140 kg). Conduit sizes were 18 mm in 7 patients, 20 mm in 9 patients, 22 mm in 1 patient, 24 mm in 36 patients, and 28 mm in 1 patient. Thirty-seven patients (69%) had a history of median sternotomy. Seventeen patients (31%) had tetralogy of Fallot. Three patients (6%) had tetralogy of Fallot with an absent pulmonary valve. Seven patients (13%) had truncus arteriosus. Twenty patients (37%) had right ventricle-to-pulmonary artery conduits, and 1 patient (2%) had a bioprosthetic valve in a pulmonic position. Ten patients (19%) underwent a Ross or Ross-Konno procedure as concomitant surgery.

Among the 54 patients, there was 1 hospital death (1.9%) after implantation. The patient, who had tetrasomy 18p, severe aortic insufficiency, and underwent hemiarch repair with a reinforced Ross procedure using an ePTFE VC in the pulmonary position, died 42 days postoperatively from an aortic root abscess. One additional patient with peripheral pulmonary artery stenosis developed severe pulmonary insufficiency after surgery. One patient showed increased pressure gradient to 42 mm Hg 4.3 years after the surgery while her body weight doubled. The remaining 51 patients (94%) demonstrated no significant pulmonary stenosis (less than 30 mm Hg peak pressure gradient) or pulmonary insufficiency less than mild range on follow-up echocardiography. A total of 145 follow-up echocardiograms from 54 patients demonstrated favorable peak pressure gradients, indicating sustained valve hemodynamic performance with no evidence of clinically significant pulmonary stenosis (Figure 5).

Comment

PVR in children and young adults with congenital heart disease remains challenging because currently available conduits and prosthetic devices are limited by structural degeneration, calcification, thrombosis, and the need for repeated intervention. Particularly, younger patients frequently outgrow implanted conduits or develop progressive conduit dysfunction over time, resulting in multiple reoperations throughout their lifetime.10, 11, 12, 13 In this study, we describe a modified technique for the construction and implantation of a handmade trileaflet ePTFE VC and demonstrate favorable early and midterm clinical outcomes with preserved valve competence and low rates of reintervention.

The present technique incorporates several modifications intended to improve leaflet geometry, commissural stability, and long-term durability. Although our previous handmade ePTFE conduit performed well clinically, we added 1-mm commissural “wings” at the left and right edges of the 3-leaflet construct to create more symmetric cusps and improve valve durability under greater pulmonary blood flow and pressure that may develop over time, particularly in growing pediatric patients. First, construction of symmetric trileaflet ePTFE cusps using a standardized geometric design allows reproducible valve configuration and physiological coaptation. The leaflet height, corresponding to approximately 70% of leaflet length, creates a broad coaptation zone while avoiding excessive cusp redundancy. The commissural wings, together with vertical mattress reinforcement sutures, distribute mechanical stress more evenly at the commissural junctions, recognized sites of high stress concentration and potential valve failure, similar to the commissural stabilization concept used in the Ozaki procedure.14 Second, we use CV-5 rather than CV-6 sutures for leaflet construction. CV-5 sutures have demonstrated long-term durability as artificial chordae in mitral valve repair.15

Although ePTFE sutures require multiple throws, all knots are placed on the external surface of the conduit, minimizing intraluminal irregularities and potentially reducing thrombus formation and flow disturbance. Preservation of a smooth luminal surface may also improve hemodynamic performance. Third, vertical commissural sutures create small sinus-like spaces behind each leaflet, promoting reverse flow during diastole to facilitate valve closure. Collectively, these modifications may improve valve competence and reduce commissural regurgitation.

Our clinical outcomes are encouraging. Except for 1 hospital mortality unrelated to conduit dysfunction, nearly all patients demonstrated satisfactory valve competence without significant pulmonary stenosis or insufficiency during follow-up. There were no conduit-related reoperations. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating favorable durability and resistance to calcification of ePTFE VC.1,9 The absence of structural valve degeneration during the current follow-up period may reflect the intrinsic biocompatibility and noncalcifying properties of ePTFE material.

Compared with bioprosthetic valves and homografts, handmade ePTFE VCs offer several potential advantages. The conduit can be customized intraoperatively to accommodate individual patient anatomy and hemodynamic requirements, making it suitable for patients across a wide range of sizes. In addition, ePTFE is widely available and is not limited by donor availability, unlike homografts. Although a larger homograft can be used in toddlers and adults, their use is constrained by several drawbacks, including early structural deterioration within 1 to 5 years, high cost (approximately $25,000 per valve), and limited availability across different valve sizes.1,16,17 The largest handmade ePTFE VC implanted at our institution to date is 28 mm. In general, we select the largest conduit that can be safely accommodated by the patient's anatomy to facilitate future TPVR and potentially delay the need for conduit reintervention. At our institution, 16- to 28-mm ePTFE VCs are generally used for right ventricular-to-pulmonary artery reconstruction in patients who are at least approximately 6 months of age and weigh more than 6 kg. Although smaller ePTFE conduits (8-16 mm) have demonstrated acceptable durability, Miyazaki and colleagues reported a freedom from conduit reintervention of 77.2% at 5 years in this patient population.17 Because current TPVR technologies are generally not applicable to conduits smaller than approximately 15 mm, younger infants and neonates who require these smaller conduits rely on durable conduit function until they become candidates for future transcatheter or surgical intervention.

Unlike mechanical valves, lifelong anticoagulation is not required, and the incidence of infective endocarditis also appears to be low.18 The ePTFE VC additionally offers a cost advantage over other valve substitutes.1 Moreover, compared with right atrial appendage valves, ePTFE conduits provide a favorable landing zone for future TPVR. Although no patients in the present cohort have undergone TPVR after conduit implantation, our previous study demonstrated its feasibility, with 5 of 55 patients (9.1%) successfully undergoing TPVR without procedure-related complications.1,9 Collectively, these characteristics make the ePTFE VC a suitable alternative for surgical PVR, particularly in younger patients, who are likely to require future reinterventions.

The current study also demonstrates the feasibility and reproducibility of conduit construction. Although handmade valve fabrication may initially appear technically demanding, the standardized construction method allowed consistent conduit preparation within approximately 40 minutes. With increasing experience, the learning curve appears acceptable, and reproducible surgical outcome can be achieved.

Longer follow-up will remain essential to determine the ultimate durability of this modified conduit design. Progressive conduit stenosis, leaflet stiffening, or calcification may still develop over time, particularly in pediatric patients, who are rapidly growing. However, the current results support the concept that a carefully constructed trileaflet ePTFE VC can provide stable valve function and favorable hemodynamic performance in the pulmonary position.17

This study has a few limitations. First, it represents a single-center retrospective experience with a relatively small number of patients. Although the early clinical outcomes were favorable, larger multicenter studies are necessary to validate the reproducibility and generalizability of these findings. Second, the duration of follow-up remains limited for many patients. Although our previous experience with earlier ePTFE conduit designs demonstrated durability beyond 10 years in selected patients, the current modified technique requires long-term evaluation to determine rates of structural valve degeneration, conduit stenosis, calcification, and freedom from reintervention. The exact mechanism of valve insufficiency in the one patient who developed pulmonary insufficiency is uncertain. We speculate that severe peripheral pulmonary artery stenosis resulted in chronically elevated right ventricular afterload and altered hemodynamic conditions, which may have contributed to the development of pulmonary insufficiency. Our in vitro bench testing demonstrated satisfactory valve function under elevated afterload conditions. However, the long-term durability and performance of the valve under chronically elevated afterload in vivo remain unknown. Finally, handmade conduit construction remains operator dependent. Although the technique was standardized, subtle variation in leaflet geometry, commissural alignment, and implantation techniques may influence long-term outcomes.

In conclusion, the handmade trileaflet ePTFE VC represents a feasible and effective option for PVR in patients with congenital heart disease requiring RVOT reconstruction. The modified construction technique described in this study allows reproducible creation of symmetric valve geometry with favorable early valve competence and low rates of conduit related reintervention. Technical refinements including commissural reinforcement, optimized leaflet design, and individualized conduit tailoring may contribute to improved durability and hemodynamic performance. Although longer term follow-up and larger comparative studies are necessary, the current results support the continued use of trileaflet ePTFE VC as a promising alternative for surgical PVR, particularly in younger patients requiring durable reconstruction.
