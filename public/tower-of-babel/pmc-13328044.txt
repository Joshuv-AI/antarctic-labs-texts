Exploring the role of alpha-tocopherol in pain and inflammation: interplay with TRPV1, PKA, and PKC pathways
Authors: Sahar Jaffal, Sawsan Oran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlpha-tocopherol (vitamin E) is a fat-soluble antioxidant with neuroprotective properties. There is a gap in research regarding the anti-nociceptive and anti-inflammatory effects of alpha-tocopherol in different tests of nociception as well as its signaling pathways. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the anti-nociceptive and anti-inflammatory effects of alpha-tocopherol in vivo and examine the potential involvement of transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) and downstream kinase pathways, particularly protein kinases protein kinase A (PKA) and protein kinase C (PKC).MethodsMale Swiss albino mice were used in acetic acid-induced writhing, immersion tail-flick, and hot-plate tests for nociception while male Wistar rats were used in carrageenan-induced paw edema assay for inflammation. Alpha-tocopherol (50 mg/kg, i.p.), alone or in combination with TRPV1 antagonist (capsazepine) or PKC/PKA inhibitors (Go 6976, H89, respectively) was administered, followed by conducting the behavioral tests. TRPV1 expression in brain and skin tissues was examined using immunohistochemistry.ResultsAlpha-tocopherol significantly reduced writhing responses and extended heat latency in thermal tests. The TRPV1 was involved in the hot-plate and immersion tail-flick assays, while PKC and PKA pathways were implicated in the supraspinal analgesic effect (the hot plate test). Alpha-tocopherol (20 μg) did not exert any significant anti-inflammatory effect in the carrageenan model. Intense TRPV1 immunolabeling was observed in the alpha-tocopherol/carrageenan condition, particularly in the skin.ConclusionsAlpha-tocopherol possesses a strong anti-nociceptive effect through TRPV1 modulation via PKC/PKA-dependent mechanisms. These findings suggest that alpha-tocopherol can be used as an adjuvant in multimodal pain management protocols.Keywords: Alpha-tocopherol, Immunohistochemistry, Inflammation, Nociception, Protein Kinase A, Protein Kinase C, TRPV Cation Channels

INTRODUCTION

The most bioavailable form of vitamin E, alpha-tocopherol, is a lipid-soluble antioxidant that is essential for preventing oxidative damage to cell membranes by scavenging free radicals [1]. Its neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory properties were reported in a number of chronic conditions such as diabetes and neurodegenerative diseases [2]. Several lines of evidences showed that there is an interaction between oxidative stress and nociceptive signaling pathways [3]. Furthermore, the authors have shown previously, through molecular docking, that alpha-tocopherol, β-sitosterol, and ursolic acid fit in the same pocket of the transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) channels receptor—a key pain receptor—in a similar way to the TRPV1 antagonist (capsazepine) suggesting that these compounds can be good candidates for TRPV1 inhibition [4]. Thus, it is expected that alpha-tocopherol can have a potential anti-nociceptive effect.

Regarding pain mechanisms, it is well-known that TRPV1, protein kinase A (PKA), and protein kinase C (PKC) are important mediators of inflammation and pain perception [5,6]. TRPV1 is an ion channel that is activated by various stimuli, including capsaicin, protons, and noxious stimuli, and is associated with pain perception. This non-selective cation channel which is primarily expressed in sensory neurons, is essential for identifying noxious heat and chemical stimuli [7]. Furthermore, the TRPV1 receptor is highly expressed in different areas of the central nervous system [8]. In fact, several lines of evidence have shown that TRPV1 activation can modulate the excitatory synaptic transmission and that this effect is enhanced by the use of PKC activators [5]. Previous reports demonstrated that various inflammatory mediators trigger protein kinases which phosphorylate TRPV1. In this regard, it was found that TRPV1 phosphorylation plays a crucial role in the mechanisms of pathological pain response [9]. In addition, Yüksel et al. [10] reported that the TRPV1 receptor is implicated in apoptosis, oxidative stress, and hyperalgesia in a fibromyalgia model. Phosphorylation via PKA and PKC pathways regulates its activation and increases neuronal sensitivity leading to hyperalgesia and allodynia [6,11]. Also, changes in TRPV1 function and/or expression were implicated in multiple disorders such as irritable bowel syndrome, insulin resistance, asthma, cancer, atherosclerosis, over-activity in the bladder, obesity, neuropathic pain, epilepsy, inflammation, and itching [12–20]. Although preclinical models have demonstrated the potential of pharmacological inhibition of TRPV1 and related kinases in relieving pain and inflammation, systemic toxicity and side effects have limited their clinical application. Accordingly, there is an ultimate need for safer substitutes or adjuvants that can indirectly alter these pathways. Thus, the aim of this study was to assess alpha-tocopherol’s in vivo anti-nociceptive and anti-inflammatory properties and to determine the pathways that are involved in producing this effect, and also to evaluate the effect of alpha-tocopherol on TRPV1 expression.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Alpha-tocopherol in the form of softgel capsules with known label purity (400 IU, ≥ 98%) was obtained from Geltec Private Limited. Capsazepine was purchased from Tokyo Chemistry Industry, while acetic acid was obtained from Lab Alley. Diclofenac sodium was brought from Novartis, Basel, Switzerland and λ-carrageenan was from Santa-Cruz Biotechnology. Diethyl ether, ethanol, dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO), phosphate buffer saline (PBS), paraformaldehyde, tris-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and tween-80 were acquired from Scharlau. TRPV1 antibody was purchased from Fine Test. Tablets of morphine sulphate were provided by Thermo Fisher Scientific. Naloxone, H89 and Go 6976 were purchased from Tocris Bioscience. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) materials were obtained from Roche Diagnostics.

1. Preparation of drugs

A stock solution of 10 mM capsazepine was prepared in absolute ethanol. The oil of an alpha-tocopherol softgel capsule (400 IU, ≥ 98% purity) was dissolved in a vehicle consisting of 1% tween-80 in normal saline. λ-carrageenan was prepared in hot saline at 80°C and was allowed to cool before injection. H89 and Go 6976 were dissolved in DMSO. Diclofenac sodium, naloxone and morphine were directly dissolved in saline. All drugs were freshly prepared from stock solutions in sterile saline before use.

2. Animals

Male Swiss albino mice (20–25 g, 6–8 weeks old) were used in the acetic acid-induced writhing test, the immersion tail-flick test, and the hot plate test while male Wistar rats (250–300 g, 8–10 weeks old) were employed in a carrageenan-induced paw edema model. All animals were maintained at a temperature-controlled environment with a 12-hour light and 12-hour dark cycle in the animal facility at the University of Jordan, Amman, Jordan. The animals were randomly assigned into treatment groups. Food pellets and water were available ad libitum. The animals were allowed to acclimatize to the experimental room before conducting the experiments. All procedures were conducted following the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) guidelines for animal research and were approved under the ethical approval number 235/2020/19 from the University of Jordan.

3. Acetic acid-induced writhing test

In the acetic acid-induced writhing test, the mice were divided into control and treatment groups (n = 6). Investigators performing behavioral scoring were blinded to the treatment allocation. Diclofenac sodium was administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) at a dose of 30 mg/kg as a positive control while alpha-tocopherol was given at 50 mg/kg (i.p.) pursuant to Kabay et al. [21]. In the groups that received TRPV1 antagonist, PKC inhibitor and PKA inhibitor (capsazepine, Go 6976 and H89, respectively), the drugs were administered 30 minutes prior to alpha-tocopherol at doses of 5 mg/kg capsazepine, 1 mg/kg Go 6976 and 2 mg/kg H89 pursuant to previous studies [22–24]. After 30 minutes from the administration of the vehicle, alpha-tocopherol or diclofenac sodium, the animals received an i.p. injection of 1% acetic acid (10 mL/kg) to induce visceral pain. The number of writhes displayed by the animals was recorded ten minutes after receiving acetic acid.

4. Immersion tail-flick test

In the immersion tail-flick test, the time between treatments as well as the time between different treatments and behavioral testing was 30 minutes. In more detail, the animals received i.p. injections of either the vehicle, 50 mg/kg alpha-tocopherol, or 10 mg/kg morphine and were monitored for 30 minutes followed by the conduction of the immersion tail-flick test. In the groups that received 5 mg/kg TRPV1 antagonist, 1 mg/kg PKC inhibitor, and 2 mg/kg PKA inhibitor, the drugs were i.p. administered 30 minutes prior to alpha-tocopherol’s injection. Morphine antagonist (naloxone) at 5 mg/kg was administered prior to morphine in the positive control group. The immersion tail-flick test was conducted 30 minutes after the treatments. For this test, 5 cm of the mouse’s tail was immersed in hot water maintained at 55°C. The time was calculated from the moment of placing its tail in hot water till flicking the tail. The latency time between immersion and withdrawal was recorded in seconds using a stopwatch. A cut-off time of 20 seconds was set to prevent tissue injury. Investigators performing behavioral scoring were blinded to the treatment allocation.

5. Hot plate test

In the hot plate test, the time between treatments as well as the time between the treatments and behavioral testing was 30 minutes. The animals received i.p. injections of either the vehicle, 50 mg/kg alpha-tocopherol, or 10 mg/kg morphine and were monitored for 30 minutes. In groups that received the TRPV1 antagonist, PKC inhibitor, or PKA inhibitor, capsazepine (5 mg/kg), Go 6976 (1 mg/kg), and H89 (2 mg/kg) were administered i.p. 30 minutes prior to alpha-tocopherol. Naloxone (5 mg/kg) was administered i.p. 30 minutes prior to morphine in the positive control group. The hot plate test was conducted 30 minutes after treatments by placing each animal separately on a hot plate that was kept at 55°C. The time was calculated from the moment of placing the animal on the hot plate until the animal’s jump. The animal’s reaction time was measured in seconds from the moment it was placed on the hot plate. A cut-off time of 20 seconds was applied to avoid paw damage. Investigators performing behavioral scoring were blinded to the treatment allocation.

6. Carrageenan-induced paw edema

The anti-inflammatory effect of 20 µg alpha-tocopherol was assessed using a carrageenan-induced paw edema model in rats. Paw circumference was measured before and after carrageenan injection by wrapping a thread around the paw, then measuring the thread’s length with a metric ruler. The rats were divided into control and experimental groups. The experimental group received an intraplantar (ipl) injection of 20 µg alpha-tocopherol in the left hind paw. The positive control group received an ipl injection of 2.5% diclofenac sodium. After the administration of different treatments, the animals were monitored for 30 minutes, followed by an injection of a freshly prepared λ-carrageenan (1% w/v in 100 µL/paw) into the left hind paw of each rat under ether anesthesia [25]. Paw size was measured at baseline, 2, and 4 hours post-carrageenan injection. The images of the plantar surface were captured before and after treatment. Investigators performing behavioral scoring were blinded to the treatment allocation.

The percentage of edema was calculated using the formula: [(Cac – C0) / C0] × 100%, where Cac is the paw thickness after carrageenan injection and C0 is the baseline paw thickness prior to injection.

7. Immunohistochemistry

IHC materials were obtained from Roche Diagnostics. IHC was performed pursuant to reference [26]. Dissection of rats was conducted 4 hours after the ipl injection of carrageen under ether anesthesia. Skin and brain sections were obtained from animals and were preserved in 10% paraformaldehyde dissolved in 0.1 mol/L PBS, PBS pH 7.4. Coronal brain sections covering supraspinal pain-processing regions were evaluated for TRPV1 immunoreactivity. Brain sections from the contralateral hemisphere to the injected paw the were evaluated to connect the molecular results of TRPV1 to nociceptive data. The sections were embedded in paraffin to be prepared for histology. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded rat brain and skin tissues were deparaffinized in xylene (10 minutes) and rehydrated using graded ethanol (100%, 95%, and 70%) into distilled water. Heat-induced epitope retrieval was carried out in Tris-EDTA buffer (10 mM Tris, 1 mM Tris-EDTA, pH 9.0) at 95°C for 20 minutes, followed by cooling to room temperature. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 minutes. Non-specific binding was blocked with 5% normal goat serum for 30 minutes at room temperature. Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with rabbit anti-TRPV1 primary antibody diluted in antibody diluent (1:500). After three PBS washes, sections were incubated with a biotinylated goat anti-rabbit (IgG secondary antibody) for 30 minutes, followed by avidin-biotin complex reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine as chromogen, and sections were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin, dehydrated and cleared in xylene. The negative control group included slides without the primary antibody. Investigators performing IHC interpretation were blinded to the treatment allocation.

8. Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard error of mean. Normality tests for all groups were conducted using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For parametric analyses, differences between groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. All graphs and statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 6 (GraphPad). All experiments were performed in triplicate.

RESULTS

Fig. 1 presents the effect of alpha-tocopherol and its mechanisms on the acetic acid-induced writhing test. The results showed that alpha-tocopherol significantly decreased the number of writhings compared to the control group (P < 0.0001). The effect of alpha-tocopherol was comparable to the positive control group treated with diclofenac sodium. The administration of TRPV1 antagonist as well as the inhibitors of PKC and PKA didn’t reverse the effect of alpha-tocopherol in the acetic acid induced writhing test. Furthermore, two thermal tests were used to assess the anti-nociceptive effects of alpha-tocopherol. The findings revealed that the administration of alpha-tocopherol caused a significant increase in latency time in the immersion tail-flick test (Fig. 2). There was no reversal for the effect of alpha-tocopherol by using PKC and PKA inhibitors in this test. Only the TRPV1 antagonist abolished the effect of alpha-tocopherol in the immersion tail-flick test. The anti-nociceptive effect of morphine, a positive control drug, was completely blocked by the use of naloxone. Similarly, alpha-tocopherol lengthened the latency time in the other thermal test (the hot plate test). The administration of PKC and PKA inhibitors as well as the TRPV1 antagonist reversed the effect of alpha tocopherol. The greatest prolongation in latency time was observed in morphine-treated group, an effect that was antagonized by naloxone (Fig. 3).

Figs. 5 and 6 illustrate TRPV1 expression in sections of the animals’ brains in different treated groups. It is obvious that significant variations in the patterns of TRPV1 expression were found between the experimental groups based on IHC staining of the brain and skin sections, respectively. Only a small number of neurons in the control group showed faint and sparse brown immunoreactive signals, indicating low TRPV1 expression in the brain and skin sections. The sections from carrageenan-treated animals revealed an increase in TRPV1 staining. However, the effect of carrageenan on increasing TRPV1 expression was local (in skin but not the brain sections). DISCUSSION

According to the current study, alpha-tocopherol has a significant anti-nociceptive effect in mice as shown in the writhing and thermal latency tests. The effect can be due to interference with pain-modulating signaling cascades as well as antioxidant properties. The TRPV1 receptor was involved in the effect of alpha-tocopherol in the immersion tail-flick and hot plate tests but not the writhing test. In fact, the writhing test is a general test for pain occurring due to the activation of acid-sensitive ion channels or other channels in the afferent neurons [27]. Under oxidative stress, the TRPV1 receptor is known to be upregulated and sensitized, leading to an increase in calcium influx and nociceptor activity [27]. By lowering the levels of reactive oxygen species, alpha-tocopherol may change the phosphorylation of TRPV1 or stabilize its function [28]. In this regard, previous studies reported that the phosphorylation of TRPV1 can be affected by many factors including oxidative stress. Furthermore, the lack of activity of alpha-tocopherol in the groups that were pre-treated with H89 or Go 6976 in the immersion tail-flick test suggests that it engages PKC/PKA-dependent pathways in supraspinal pain modulation (the hot plate test) while engages the PKC/PKA-independent mechanism in spinal analgesic action (the immersion tail-flick test). Therefore, alpha-tocopherol may have dual-site mechanisms of analgesia: kinase-dependent in the brain, and kinase-independent in the spinal cord. Similarly, Javed et al. [29] revealed that PKC and PKA inhibitors reversed the effect of morphine in supra-spinal tests. Furthermore, the use of alpha-tocopherol (at 20 µg) had no discernible effect on carrageenan-induced paw edema in this study, suggesting that its anti-inflammatory properties are weak in models of acute inflammation where prostaglandin synthesis and neutrophil infiltration predominate. In contrast, the non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs that have strong anti-edematous properties, such as diclofenac sodium, directly inhibit cyclooxygenase enzymes. Another possibility is that the used dose was not potent in exerting an anti-inflammatory effect in the carrageenan paw edema model. According to these results, alpha-tocopherol may not be useful as a stand-alone anti-inflammatory drug in acute situations, but it can be effective in neuropathic pain or chronic inflammatory diseases marked by its persistent oxidative damage. Together, the authors’ results suggest that alpha-tocopherol primarily reduces pain by attenuating TRPV1 activation through inhibiting downstream kinases supraspinally. Regarding TRPV1 expression, it is well-known that the TRPV1 receptor is expressed in nociceptors, keratinocytes, sebocytes, and various immune cells in the skin [30]. Targeting TRPV1 expression on keratinocytes can be used to treat pain in the skin [31]. Because carrageenan is a known pro-inflammatory agent, it produces a strong boost in TRPV1 antibody staining, indicating significant inflammation or immune activation. Similarly, Elitt et al. [32] reported that the overexpression of artemin increased TRPV1 and TRPA1 expression in cutaneous sensory neurons leading to changes in the sensitivity to cold and heat. The increase in TRPV1 expression by alpha-tocopherol can indicate tissue-specific regulatory effects rather than anti-inflammatory effects. The changes in TRPV1 immunoreactivity—especially the increased staining in the alpha-tocopherol treated group—probably result from the dynamic regulation of TRPV1 expression or trafficking, as opposed to being a direct measure of the anti-inflammatory effects. The increased expression or availability of TRPV1 doesn’t necessarily mean that TRPV1 activity is increased. Alpha-tocopherol did not change TRPV1 expression significantly in the brain and this can be due to the used dose or treatment period. Other reasons can include the higher baseline oxidative and inflammatory signaling in the skin caused by exposure to variable stimuli compared to the brain, whereby TRPV1 expression is restricted to certain areas. In addition, the brain is enclosed in a more stable microenvironment compared to the skin. Thus, carrageenan could interact with these pathways and paradoxically trigger compensatory upregulation of TRPV1 as part of adaptive responses. Alpha-tocopherol likely increased TRPV1 in skin but not brain due to tissue-specific expression patterns, differences in oxidative/inflammatory signaling, local bioavailability, presence of co-regulatory factors, and distinct physiological needs. In conclusion, alpha-tocopherol possesses a strong anti-nociceptive effect through TRPV1 modulation and kinase-dependent mechanisms as well as kinase-independent mechanisms are not active as an anti-inflammatory compound. These findings suggest that alpha-tocopherol can be utilized as an adjuvant in multimodal pain management in which oxidative stress plays a role in the nociceptive mechanism [33].

Notably, while IASP discourages the use of diethyl ether due to disadvantages related to safety such as inflammability, irritancy, and personnel safety, IASP regulation allows the use of diethyl ether under exceptional situations where scientific justification exists and where other useful experimental designs are not readily available to produce an adequate degree of reliability in experimental results. The justifications for the use of diethyl ether in the current study are as follows. First, the minimal effects of ether on TRPV1-dependent nociceptive signaling (physiological effect). This study aimed at studying the effects of alpha-tocopherol and the involvement of TRPV1, PKA, and PKC pathway(s) in its effects. Previous studies showed that some known anesthetics (i.e., isoflurane and sevoflurane) can modify many members of the TRP receptor family [34], with effects on the phosphorylation dependent sensitization of TRPV1 receptors. The same applies to injectable anesthetics, including ketamine/xylazine and pentobarbital that can alter the nociceptive thresholds and inflammatory biomarkers. Second, diethyl ether was only used briefly and not for prolonged use, surgical manipulation, or survival surgery. The limited exposure to ether therefore reduces animal distress.
