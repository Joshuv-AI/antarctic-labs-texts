A penalized least squares estimator for extreme-value mixture models
Anas Mourahib, Anna Kiriliouk, Johan Segers

Abstract
Estimating the parameters of max-stable parametric models poses significant challenges, particularly when some parameters lie on the boundary of the parameter space. This situation arises when a subset of variables exhibits extreme values simultaneously, while the remaining variables do not—a phenomenon referred to as an extreme direction in the literature. In this paper, we propose a novel estimator for the parameters of a general parametric mixture model, incorporating a penalization approach based on a pseudo-norm. This penalization plays a crucial role in accurately identifying parameters at the boundary of the parameter space. Additionally, our estimator comes with a data-driven algorithm to detect groups of variables corresponding to extreme directions. We assess the performance of our estimator in terms of both parameter estimation and the identification of extreme directions through extensive simulation studies. Finally, we apply our methods to data on river discharges and financial portfolio losses.

Introduction

Consider a random vector
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
of risk factors and suppose the focus is on rare events. In this regime, neither parametric nor nonparametric methods are satisfactory: parametric models rely on distributional assumptions that can be dominated by the bulk of the data, and thus tend to underestimate extreme outcomes, while nonparametric techniques demand large sample sizes—often unavailable when studying extremes. Extreme value theory offers a rigorous framework for extrapolating beyond observed levels and has found applications in finance, hydrology, weather forecasting, and other fields.

Univariate extreme value theory, that is, when
d=1
, has been extensively studied
[
8
,
1
,
7
]
and modeling rare events in this case is done via a finite-dimensional parametric family of distributions.
Multivariate modeling of
d\geqslant 2
risk factors is a more challenging topic, since risks may exhibit some sort of dependence. The goal is not only to model rare events of each risk factor independently but to capture the extremal dependence between these risk factors. Two main approaches appear in this context: the block-maxima approach and the threshold exceedances approach. In the first approach, standardized componentwise maxima of
\boldsymbol{X}
are asymptotically modeled using a max-stable random vector
\boldsymbol{Z}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d})
. In the second approach, asymptotically, observations where at least one risk factor is extreme are modeled using a multivariate (generalized) Pareto random vector
\boldsymbol{Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d})

[
30
]
.

For both approaches, many parametric families have been proposed; popular examples include the Hüsler–Reiss model and the logistic model
[
35
,
19
]
. Estimation methods for such models are numerous, for example, through censored likelihood
[
28
]
, weighted least squares
[
12
]
, or based on neural networks
[
25
,
29
]
.

A drawback of most parametric models is that they force all components of
\boldsymbol{X}
to be extreme simultaneously. Figure
1
(left) shows a log-transformed simulation from a max-stable Hüsler–Reiss model. Observations are close to the diagonal line
x=y
, suggesting that both components are large simultaneously. However, in practice, it may happen that only certain subsets
J\subset\{1,\dots,d\}
of variables are jointly extreme while the others are not. Such a subset

is called an
extreme direction

[
27
,
32
]
.

:
Log-transformed sample of size

from a max-stable Hüsler–Reiss model (left) and a max-stable mixture Hüsler–Reiss model (right).

Both the max-linear model
[
15
,
11
]
and the asymmetric logistic model
[
35
]
can accommodate multiple extreme directions. However, the first model has a singular distribution (not discrete nor absolutely continuous), thus making statistical inference complicated. The second one is too specific in the sense that it cannot represent a large variety of max-stable distributions.

To capture multiple extreme directions,
Mourahib et al. 2024
introduce the
mixture model
that extends the classic max-linear model and can represent
any
max-stable distribution. It is identified via two sets of parameters:

•

a
(d\times r)
coefficient matrix
A
with entries in
[0,1]
, whose columns correspond to distinct extreme directions of
\boldsymbol{X}
. Zero entries in
A
indicate which variables are not contained in a given extreme direction
[
27
, Proposition 4.5]
;

•

a second set of parameters that governs the dependence structure

A simulation example of this model is given in Figure
1
(right), where we can see that this model, in fact, captures not only the extreme direction

and
\{2\}
.

A natural question is how to estimate the model parameters. The weighted least-squares estimator of
Einmahl et al. 2018
requires the true parameters to be in the interior of the parameter space. In contrast, the mixture model’s reliance on zero entries in
A
pushes parameters to the boundary of the space, violating this condition.

We propose an approach for the estimation of the parameters of max-stable distributions possessing any collection of extreme directions, using a penalized version of the least squares estimator proposed in
[
12
]
. While the focus in
Mourahib et al. 2024
is on the multivariate generalized Pareto density, the estimator studied in this work is based on the stable tail dependence function, since estimating the mixture model parameters from the former is computationally burdensome in high or even moderate dimensions because one would need to censor components that are below the threshold.

The proposed penalized least squares estimator is capable of identifying extreme directions
and
estimating the mixture model parameters simultaneously. This differentiates our approach from current literature which focuses solely on the identification of extreme directions
[
17
,
32
,
26
]
.

Outline of the paper.

The paper is structured as follows. Section
2

]
, the mixture model

Background

2.1
Multivariate extreme value distributions

For
i=1,\ldots,n
, let
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}=\left({X_{i1},\ldots,X_{id}}\right)
be i.i.d. copies of
\boldsymbol{X}
, a random vector on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with joint cumulative distribution function (cdf)
F
and margins
F_{1},\ldots,F_{d}
. We say that
F
is in the (max-)domain of attraction of a
max-stable
distribution

if there exist sequences
\boldsymbol{a}_{n}\in(0,\infty)^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{b}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\dfrac{\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{b}_{n}}{\boldsymbol{a}_{n}}\rightsquigarrow G,\qquad n\to\infty.

(2.1)

The margins of
G
, denoted
G_{1},\ldots,G_{d}
, are univariate extreme-value distributions themselves,
and any max-stable distribution
G
admits the representation

G(\boldsymbol{x})=\exp\left\{{-\ell\left({-\ln G_{1}(x_{1}),\ldots,-\ln G_{d}(x_{d})}\right)}\right\},

where
\ell:[0,\infty)^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is the
stable tail dependence function (stdf)
. Then the distribution of
\boldsymbol{X}^{*}=(X_{1}^{*},\ldots,X_{d}^{*})
with
X_{j}^{*}=1/\left\{{1-F_{j}(X_{j})}\right\}
for
j\in D
is in the domain of attraction of
G^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})=\exp\left\{{-\ell\left({1/x_{1},\ldots,1/x_{d}}\right)}\right\}
, a max-stable distribution with unit-Fréchet margins.

The stable tail dependence function can be retrieved via

\ell(\boldsymbol{x})=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}t^{-1}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}}\left[{F_{1}(X_{1})\geqslant 1-tx_{1}\text{ or }\ldots\text{ or }F_{d}(X_{d})\geqslant 1-tx_{d}}\right].

(2.2)

The function
\ell
is convex and homogeneous in the sense that for
c\geqslant 0
and
\boldsymbol{x}\in[0,\infty)^{d}
, we have
\ell(c\boldsymbol{x})=c\ell(\boldsymbol{x})
. Moreover, unit-Fréchet margins of
G^{*}
imply that
\ell(0,\ldots,0,x_{j},0,\ldots,0)=x_{j}
for
x_{j}\geqslant 0
and
j\in D
. Finally,
\ell
can be bounded by

\max(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\leqslant\ell(x)\leqslant x_{1}+\cdots+x_{d},

(2.3)

where the lower bound corresponds to perfect tail dependence and the upper bound to asymptotic independence.

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{Z}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{d})
follows a max-stable distribution with unit-Fréchet margins. For non-empty
J\subseteq D
, let
\ell_{J}
be the stdf associated with
\boldsymbol{Z}_{J}
, that is,

\ell_{J}(\boldsymbol{x}_{J})=\ell\left({\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}\right),\qquad\boldsymbol{x}_{J}\in[0,\infty)^{\lvert J\rvert},

with
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}=\sum_{j\in J}x_{j}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
and
\boldsymbol{e}_{j}
the
j
-th canonical unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The
extremal coefficients

[
31
]
are defined via
\zeta_{J}:=\ell(\sum_{j\in J}\boldsymbol{e}_{j})
, for set
J\subseteq D
with
|J|\geqslant 2
. From (
2.3
), we get that
1\leqslant\zeta_{J}\leqslant|J|
. The value
\zeta_{J}
can be interpreted as the effective number of tail independent variables among
\boldsymbol{Z}_{J}
.

The
exponent measure

\mu
of
G^{*}
is the unique Borel measure concentrated on
\mathbb{E}=[0,\infty)^{d}\setminus\left\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\right\}
such that

\mu\left({\mathbb{E}\setminus\left[\boldsymbol{0},1/\boldsymbol{x}\right]}\right)=\ell(\boldsymbol{x}),\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\infty})^{d}.

(2.4)

The measure
\mu
is finite on Borel sets that are bounded away from the origin. Moreover,
\mu\left({\mathbb{E}\setminus\left[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}\right]}\right)=\infty
as soon as
y_{j}=0
for some
j\in D
.

2.2
Extreme directions and the mixture model

For non-empty
J\subseteq D

The following definition was introduced in

The set

2.3
Least squares estimator of tail dependence

Let again
\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{X}_{n}
denote i.i.d. random vectors in the domain of attraction of a max-stable distribution with stdf
\ell
. We will first recall how to estimate
\ell

k=k_{n}\in(0,n]

k\to\infty\quad\text{and}\quad k/n\to 0,\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

(2.11)

The

determines the fraction of the data that is considered as extreme and that will be used for inference. We will refer to the quantity

as the

. A straightforward non-parametric estimator of the stdf is

\hat{\ell}_{n,k}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\left\{R_{i1}^{n}>n+1/2-kx_{1}\text{ or }\ldots\text{ or }R_{id}^{n}>n+1/2-kx_{d}\right\},

(2.12)

where
R_{ij}^{n}
denotes the rank of
X_{ij}
among
X_{1j},\ldots,X_{nj}
for
j=1,\ldots,d
. Note that (
2.12
) is a minor modification of the standard empirical stdf
[
10
]
. Alternatives are the bias-corrected estimator proposed in
Beirlant et al. 2016

[
24
]

Suppose now that
\ell
belongs to a parametric family
\left\{{\ell(\,\cdot\ ;\boldsymbol{\theta}):\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}\right\}
, with
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
for some integer

. Furthermore, assume the existence of a unique parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\in\Theta

Einmahl et al. 2018

\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n,k}:=\operatornamewithlimits{\arg\min}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}\sum_{m=1}^{q}\left({\hat{\ell}_{n,k}(\boldsymbol{c}_{m})-\ell(\boldsymbol{c}_{m};\boldsymbol{\theta})}\right)^{2}.

(2.13)

Here,
\boldsymbol{c}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{c}_{q}\in[0,\infty)^{d}
, with
\boldsymbol{c}_{m}=(c_{m1},\ldots,c_{md})
for

are the

are evaluated. The estimator (

[

Minimization algorithm and choice of

.

To solve the minimization problem (

), we use the bounded limited-memory modification of the BFGS quasi-Newton method (

), see
Byrd et al. 1995
. This iterative method has the update step

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m+1}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m}-\alpha_{m}H_{m}^{-1}\nabla L\left({\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m}}\right),\qquad m\geqslant 0,

where

is the loss function in (

\alpha_{m}>0
is the step-size,

is a
(d\times r+v)\times(d\times r+v)
approximate curvature positive definite matrix capturing second-order information based on the gradients observed during optimization, and finally,
\nabla L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m})
is the gradient of
L
evaluated at
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{m}
. Here, at each step, the gradient is approximated using finite differences method.

Remark 3.3
.

An additional motivation for our choice of penalization exponent
p\in(0,1]
is the fact that the update direction in (
3.4
) is opposite to the gradient. It follows that for a fixed parameter
a_{js}
, the penalization term in (
3.1
) contributes to the gradient proportionally to

T(p)=a_{js}^{p-1}\left({\sum_{t\in R}a_{jt}^{p}}\right)^{1/p-1}.

(3.5)

Recall that
p<1
and thus
p-1<0
. Consequently, if the true value of
a_{js}
is zero, then as the corresponding coefficient approaches zero at some step of the minimization algorithm L-BFGS-B, the term

in Equation (

, and since the update direction in (

.

Stopping criterion.

The stopping criterion in Step

\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\operatorname{new}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\operatorname{old}}\|_{d\times r+v}<\epsilon,

where

is a predefined small threshold,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\operatorname{new}}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\operatorname{old}}
are as defined in Algorithm

\max
.

The

Our procedure is tailored to max-stable mixture models, since

\displaystyle\operatorname{ED-S}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\operatorname{D}(\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{l},\mathcal{J}),\qquad\operatorname{D}(\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{l},\mathcal{J})=1-\frac{\lvert\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{l}\cap\mathcal{J}\rvert}{\lvert\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{l}\cup\mathcal{J}\rvert}=\frac{\lvert\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{l}\symdif\mathcal{J}\rvert}{\lvert\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{l}\cup\mathcal{J}\rvert}

(4.3)

where
\mathcal{J}
and
\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{l}
denote the signatures of
A
and
\hat{A}^{(l)}
, respectively, as defined in (
2.6
).

Second, we assess the performance of our estimator by measuring its proximity to the true parameter

\boldsymbol{\theta}=\left({A,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{Z}}\right)
of the mixture model. In the following, for
l=1,\ldots,N
, we reorder the columns of
\hat{A}^{(l)}
so as to minimize the difference
\operatorname{diff}\left({\hat{A}^{(l)},A}\right)
, as defined in (
4.5
), but without the denominator term. For simplicity, we continue to denote the resulting matrix by
\hat{A}^{(l)}
. To construct a robust performance metric and ensure that the marginal components of our estimator are equally scaled, we mitigate the disproportionate influence of their magnitudes. This is achieved by normalizing each coefficient of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
using the maximum value computed over the
N
estimates corresponding to that component.
More precisely, let
\hat{m}_{A}=(\hat{m}_{A,js})_{j\in D,\,s\in R}
denote the
(d\times r)
matrix where the
(j,s)
-th entry represents the maximum value calculated based on the coefficients
\hat{a}_{js}^{(l)}
obtained from the matrices
\hat{A}^{(l)}
, for
l=1,\ldots,N
. Similarly, recall from Section
3
the dimension
v
of the dependence parameter vectors
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{Z}
. Let
\hat{\boldsymbol{m}}_{Z}=(\hat{m}_{Z,t})_{t=1,\ldots,v}
denote the
v
-dimensional vector where the
t
-th entry represents the maximum value calculated based on the coefficients
\hat{\theta}_{t}^{(l)}
obtained from the vectors
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{Z}^{(l)}
, for
l=1,\ldots,N
.
We define a standardized version of the mean-squared error as

\displaystyle\operatorname{SMSE}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\left[{\operatorname{diff}_{m_{A}}\left({\hat{A}^{(l)},\,A}\right)+\operatorname{diff}_{m_{Z}}\left({\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{Z}^{(l)},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{Z}}\right)}\right],\quad\text{ where}

(4.4)

\displaystyle\operatorname{diff}_{m_{A}}\left({\hat{A}^{(l)},\,A}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in D}\sum_{s\in R}\frac{\left({\hat{a}_{js}^{(l)}-a_{js}}\right)^{2}}{\hat{m}_{A,js}^{2}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\operatorname{diff}_{m_{Z}}\left({\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}_{Z}^{(l)},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{Z}}\right)=\sum_{t=1}^{v}\frac{\left({\hat{\theta}_{Z,t}^{(l)}-\theta_{Z,t}}\right)^{2}}{\hat{m}^{2}_{Z,t}}.

(4.5)

Note that the maximum of a coefficient across
N
estimates is zero if and only if all estimates of that coefficient are equal to zero. This occurs only for the null coefficients of
A
. In such cases, by convention, we define
0/0=0
.

5.1
River discharge data

We illustrate our method using river discharge data from
31
stations along the Danube River. The dataset consists of daily river discharge measurements from the summer months between 1960 and 2010. To mitigate temporal dependence, the data were declustered by
Engelke et al. 2024
, resulting in approximately seven to ten observations per year and yielding
n=428
observations in total. The declustered dataset is available in the
GraphicalExtremes
package in
R
; see
Engelke et al. 2024
.

To reduce the dimensionality and thereby lower the computational cost of identifying extreme directions, we apply a spatial clustering technique. Specifically, we partition the stations into
K
clusters using the Partitioning Around Medoids (PAM) algorithm
[
20
,
4
]
, adapted to threshold exceedances as in
Kiriliouk and Naveau 2020
. As a pseudo-distance between two stations
s
and
t
, we use

\hat{d}_{st}=\frac{1-\hat{\chi}_{st}}{2\cdot(3-\hat{\chi}_{st})},\qquad\hat{\chi}_{st}:=2-\hat{\ell}_{n,k,st}(1,1),

(5.1)

where
k/n
represents the tail fraction and
\hat{\ell}_{n,k,st}
the pairwise empirical stdf in (
2.12
) for the margin
\left\{{s,t}\right\}
.

On the one hand, the number
K
of clusters balances model simplicity (fewer clusters) and intra-cluster homogeneity. Various methods exist for selecting an appropriate value of
K
, such as minimizing within-cluster inertia
[
20
]
. On the other hand, as explained in Section
4
, the choice of the tail fraction
k/n
is a trade-off between bias and variance. Here, we simply choose
K=5
and
q=0.05
which gives spatially coherent results as displayed in Figure
7
. The cluster centers are given then by the five stations
\left\{{7,18,24,27,30}\right\}
.

Results.

At each step of Algorithm
3
, we fit a mixture logistic model to the five-dimensional river discharge dataset. While in the simulation study, the initial dependence parameter
\alpha
of the mixture logistic model was randomly initialized within the interval

As indicated in Table

[
27

. Specifically, for a mixture logistic model with column coefficients
\left({\boldsymbol{a}_{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.5}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}J}:J\in\mathcal{J}}\right)
, where
\mathcal{J}

\alpha\in[0,1]

w_{\tilde{J}}=\frac{\left(\sum_{j\in\tilde{J}}a_{j\tilde{J}}^{1/\alpha}\right)^{\alpha}}{\sum_{J\in\mathcal{J}}\left(\sum_{j\in J}a_{jJ}^{1/\alpha}\right)^{\alpha}}

(5.2)

can be interpreted as the weight corresponding to the extreme direction
\tilde{J}\in\mathcal{J}
.

Results make sense given the hydrological nature of the dataset. The standard extreme direction

The first cluster corresponds to the extreme directions
\left\{{24}\right\}
,
\left\{{24,27}\right\}

\left\{{7,24}\right\}

[
14

The second cluster corresponds to the extreme direction
\left\{{7,18,30}\right\}

[
14

6
Conclusion

Given a vector of
d
risk factors, extreme directions refer to groups of factors
J\subset\left\{{1,\ldots,d}\right\}
that are large simultaneously while those outside this group are not.
Mourahib et al. 2024
propose a construction method of multivariate generalized Pareto distributions, or equivalently max-stable distributions, with multiple extreme directions. Extreme directions are a consequence of parameters of the mixture model being on the border of the parameter space
[
27
, Proposition 4.5]
. Parameter estimation is therefore a challenging topic. We proposed a penalized least-squares estimator (
3.1
) based on the estimator from
[
12
]
. The penalization term in this estimator is based on a “quasi-norm”
L_{p}
with
p\leqslant 1
. We motivated the choice of
p\leqslant 1
both geometrically and heuristically. Next, we use this estimator to propose a data-driven algorithm for extreme-directions identification. Using a simulation study, we show that although the algorithm relies on a parametric assumption for the mixture model (e.g., mixture Hüsler–Reiss graphical model or mixture logistic model), the specific choice of the model is not crucial. In addition, the algorithm involves three tuning parameters; again through simulations, we demonstrate that the tuning essentially reduces to selecting two parameters: one that controls the proportion of data considered as extreme observations and the other that controls the penalization strength, chosen using
K

We compare our Algorithm with the

Goix et al. 2017

We obtain good simulation results for our penalized least-squares estimator proposed in (
3.1
). However, no results on consistency or asymptotic normality are provided. Two main difficulties prevent us from establishing asymptotic theoretical results:

1.

when the penalization exponent of the penalization term is such that
p\leqslant 1
, our PLS estimator is the solution of a non-convex minimization problem;

2.

some of the true parameters lie on the boundary of the parameter space. Under certain conditions, this is treated in
Kiriliouk 2020
for the unpenalized version of our estimator, i.e., the weighted least-squares estimator in
Einmahl et al. 2018
. However, these conditions seem difficult to verify for the parametric mixture models.

In the simplest case where the true parameters are in the interior of the parameter space and no penalization is needed, asymptotic results are proved in
Einmahl et al. 2018
under certain conditions. However, as pointed out in the same paper, these conditions are either difficult to verify, for instance with the Hüsler–Reiss parametric model, or are not verified at all, for instance with the max-linear model.
