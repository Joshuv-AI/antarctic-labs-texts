Sparse graphs and their Benjamini-Schramm limits: a spectral tour
Charles Bordenave

Abstract
Sparse graphs with bounded average degree form a rich class of discrete structures where local geometry strongly influences global behavior. The Benjamini–Schramm (BS) convergence offers a natural framework to describe their asymptotic local structure. In this note, we survey spectral aspects of BS convergence and their applications, with a focus on random Schreier graphs and covering graphs. We review some recent progress on the spectral decomposition of the local operators on graphs. We discuss the behavior of extreme eigenvalues and the growing role of strong convergence in distribution, which rules out spectral outliers. We also give a new application of strong convergence to the typical graph distance between vertices in Schreier graphs.



1
Introduction.

Sparse graphs with bounded average degree form a rich class of discrete structures where local geometry strongly influences global behavior. The Benjamini–Schramm (BS) convergence offers a natural framework to describe their asymptotic local structure through random rooted graphs. The first instances of BS limits were motivated by combinatorial optimization problems on random structures
[
2
]
and by questions on the recurrence of random walks on planar graphs
[
14
]
. Over the years, this notion has become a central component of graph limit theory
[
63
]
. It has proved particularly fruitful for random graphs (such as Erdős–Rényi graphs), for Cayley and Schreier graphs arising from group actions, and for random or deterministic graph coverings. There are now numerous applications and extensions of BS limits to other discrete or geometric structures, including hypergraphs and manifolds.

Since the early applications of BS limits to the study of spectra of local operators on graphs (such as
[
25
]
), many developments and advances have taken place. In this note, we survey some of these recent results, with a special focus on random Schreier graphs and random covering graphs, which provide a rich class of examples.

We begin in Section
2
by recalling the definition and basic examples of BS convergence and presenting Schreier graphs and covering graphs. In Section
3
, we introduce local operators, such as adjacency and non-backtracking operators, and describe the
*
-algebra they generate. We explain how BS convergence translates into convergence in non-commutative distribution and review recent progress on the spectral decomposition of these operators. Section
4
focuses on the behavior of extreme eigenvalues and on the growing role of strong convergence in distribution, which rules out spectral outliers. Within this framework, we revisit the celebrated Alon–Boppana bound and present convergence results for extreme eigenvalues of classical random graphs, as well as of random Schreier and covering graphs. We also discuss a new application of strong convergence to the typical graph distance between vertices in Schreier graphs. Along the way, we highlight several open problems that illustrate the richness of the field.

2.1
Marked graphs.

A
graph

G=(V,E)
will be a pair formed by a countable vertex set
V
and a countable edge set
E
. An edge
e\in E
has an origin
e_{-}\in V
and a destination
e_{+}\in V
. The graphs here are undirected meaning that
E
is equipped with an involution without fixed point
e\mapsto e^{-1}
such that

(2.1)

(e^{-1})_{+}=e_{-}\hbox{ for all $e\in E$}.

The quotient of
E
by the involution is the set of unoriented edges of
G
. Our graphs may have multiple edges, that is edges
e,f
with the same end vertices (
e_{\pm}=f_{\pm}
) and self-loops, that is edges
e
with
e_{+}=e_{-}
.
A graph is
locally finite
if for all
v\in V
,
\deg(v)<\infty
, where the degree
\deg(v)
is the number of
e\in E
such that
e_{-}=v
(or
e_{+}=v
by (
2.1
)). A
marked graph

G=(V,E,\xi)
on a set
\mathcal{Z}
is a graph
(V,E)
and a map
\xi:E\to\mathcal{Z}
.

Example 2.1
(Conductance graphs).

Let
G=(V,E,\xi)
be a marked graph with a mark function
\xi:E\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
\xi(e^{-1})=\xi(e)
for all
e\in E
. The marks could measure a conductance or strength of an edge.

Example 2.2
(Cayley graphs).

Let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated group and a generating set
S
which is symmetric, that is
S^{-1}=S
. The (left) Cayley graph
{\mathrm{Cay}}(\Gamma,S)
is the
S
-marked graph with vertex set
\Gamma
, edge set
E=S\times\Gamma
with, if
e=(s,g)\in E
,
e_{-}=g
,
e^{-1}=(s^{-1},s.g)
and mark
\xi(e)=s\in S
. In words, an element
g\in\Gamma
is connected to
s.g
with an edge marked by
s
. The degree of a vertex is equal to
|S|
.

Example 2.3
(Schreier graphs).

We continue Example
2.2
and assume that
\Gamma
acts on a countable set
V
, that is there exists a representation
\rho:\Gamma\to\mathrm{S}_{V}
where
\mathrm{S}_{V}
is the symmetric group over
V
. The Schreier graph
{\mathrm{Sch}}(\Gamma,S,\rho)
is the
S
-marked graph with vertex set
V
, edge set
E=S\times V
with, if
e=(s,x)\in E
,
e_{-}=x
,
e^{-1}=(s^{-1},\rho(s)(x))
and mark
\xi(e)=s\in S
. In words:
x\in V
is connected to
\rho(s)(x)
with an edge marked by
s
.

2.2
The local topology.

We assume from now on that
\mathcal{Z}
is a complete separable metric space. To define a topology on marked graphs, we take a reference vertex. A
rooted marked graph

g=(G,o)
is the pair formed by a connected marked graph and a distinguished vertex
o\in V
, the root. Two rooted marked graphs
(G,o)
and
(G^{\prime},o^{\prime})
are
isomorphic
if there exists a graph isomorphism between
(V,E)
and
(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
which sends
o
to
o^{\prime}
and leave the marks invariant.
Let
\dot{\mathcal{G}}=\dot{\mathcal{G}}(\mathcal{Z})
denote the space of isomorphism classes of locally finite rooted marked graphs. In combinatorial language,
\dot{\mathcal{G}}
is the set of unlabeled locally finite rooted marked graphs. It is fine to write for
g\in\dot{\mathcal{G}}
,
g=(G,o)
where
G=(V,E,\xi)
is a marked graph as long as the properties we consider are class properties (that is, do not depend on the choice of the representative of
g
).

The
local topology
on
\dot{\mathcal{G}}
is the product topology inherited from projections around the root. Concretely, two rooted marked graphs are close if, we can find a large radius
r
and a small
\delta>0
such that the subgraphs spanned by balls around the roots are isomorphic and the marks are
\delta
-close. The set
\dot{\mathcal{G}}
equipped with the local topology is a complete separable metric space. We denote by
\mathcal{P}(\dot{\mathcal{G}})
the set of probability measures on
\dot{\mathcal{G}}
equipped with the topology of weak convergence (called the local weak topology). This space
\mathcal{P}(\dot{\mathcal{G}})
plays a key role in the sequel. An element
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\dot{\mathcal{G}})
is the law of a random rooted marked graph
(G,o)
. We will use the probability notation, for an integrable function
f
in
L^{1}(\dot{\mathcal{G}},\mu)
,

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f(G,o)=\int f(G,o)d\mu(G,o).

2.4
First examples of BS convergence.

We give a few examples of graphs converging in the BS sense. In subsections

-

Example 2.5
(Box in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
).

For integer

, let
\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\xi}=(\mathbb{Z}^{d},\xi)
be the random marked graph where on each unoriented edge of

be the restriction

\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\xi}\cap B_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathrm{BS}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\xi}

(independently each edge is open with probability

converges toward the connected component of
0
in the bond percolation in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

(Regular graphs with large girth).

Along a subsequence of integers, for integer

, let

be a

Example 2.7
(Erdős-Rényi random graph and percolation in growing dimension).

For real

where each undirected edge

2.5
Unimodularity.

The neighborhood distribution
U(G)
satisfies a reversibility assumption called
unimodularity
. An
edge-rooted marked graph

(G,\varrho)
is a connected marked graph
G
with a distinguished
\varrho\in E
. The above isomorphisms for rooted marked graphs extend to edge-rooted marked graphs and we may speak of unlabeled edge-rooted marked graphs. We denote by
\vec{\mathcal{G}}
the set of unlabeled edge-rooted locally finite marked graphs. The local topology extends to this setting as well. A probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\dot{\mathcal{G}})
is
unimodular
if for every non-negative measurable functions
f
on
\vec{\mathcal{G}}
, we have

(2.3)

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\sum_{e\in E^{-}_{o}}f(G,e)\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\sum_{e\in E^{+}_{o}}f(G,e)\right],

where
E^{\pm}_{v}=\{e\in E:e_{\pm}=v\}
is the set of out/in-going edges at
v\in V
. Property (
2.3
) first appeared in Aldous
[
2
]
.
As explained in
[
3
, Proposition 2.2]
, this is equivalent to the definition of unimodularity introduced in
[
14
]
where it was presented as an extension of unimodularity in group theory. For
\mu
a Dirac mass at a Cayley graph, Property (
2.3
) boils down to the fact that countable groups are unimodular.

It is easy to check that for any finite marked graph
G=(V,E)
,
U(G)
is unimodular. Indeed,

|V|\cdot\mathbb{E}_{U(G)}\left[\sum_{e\in E^{-}_{o}}f(G,e)\right]=\sum_{u\in V}\sum_{e\in E^{-}_{u}}f(G,e)=\sum_{e\in E}f(G,e)=\sum_{u\in V}\sum_{e\in E^{+}_{u}}f(G,e)=|V|\cdot\mathbb{E}_{U(G)}\left[\sum_{e\in E^{+}_{o}}f(G,e)\right].

It is also not hard to check that the set of unimodular measures is closed in
\mathcal{P}(\dot{\mathcal{G}})
. Therefore, all BS limits of finite marked graph sequences are unimodular (the converse was recently proven to be false in a breakthrough paper
[
29
]
). This property is crucial in many applications of BS convergence.

2.6
BS convergence of Schreier graphs.

Due to the underlying group action, the BS convergence is somewhat simpler to define for sequence of Schreier graphs. As in examples
2.2
-
2.3
, we consider a finitely generated group
\Gamma
with unit
\mathrm{e}
. We assume that along a subsequence
n\to\infty
,
\Gamma
acts on a finite set
V_{n}
of cardinal
n
through a representation
\rho_{n}:\Gamma\to\mathrm{S}_{V_{n}}
. The next folklore lemma asserts that the convergence of Schreier graphs to their Cayley graph is equivalent to the asymptotic triviality of characters
{\mathrm{Tr}}(\rho_{n}(g))=|\{x\in V_{n}:\rho_{n}(g)(x)=x\}|
.

Lemma 2.8
.

The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

For some finite symmetric generating set
S
of
\Gamma
,
{\mathrm{Sch}}(\Gamma,S,\rho_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathrm{BS}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathrm{Cay}}(\Gamma,S)
.

(ii)

For all finite symmetric generating sets
S
of
\Gamma
,
{\mathrm{Sch}}(\Gamma,S,\rho_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathrm{BS}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}{\mathrm{Cay}}(\Gamma,S)
.

(iii)

For all
g\neq\mathrm{e}
,
\frac{1}{n}{\mathrm{Tr}}(\rho_{n}(g))\to 0
.

Lemma
2.8
notably implies that the convergence of Schreier graphs is the property of the representations
\rho_{n}
, it does not depend on the specific choice of the generating set. In (
3.2
), we will see that
(iii)
is the convergence in distribution of
\rho_{n}
toward the regular representation.

Example 2.9
(Congruence subgroups).

The group
\mathbb{Z}
acts on
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
by congruence mod
n
. Consider the groups
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
or
\Gamma=\mathrm{SL}(d,\mathbb{Z})
for example, if
\rho_{n}
is the action by congruence mod
n
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{n}
or
\mathrm{SL}(d,\mathbb{Z}_{n})
then
(iii)
is easily satisfied. Note that in this case, the Schreier graph is itself be a Cayley graph.

Example 2.10
(Random actions of the free group).

Let
\mathrm{F}_{d}
be the free group with
d

g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}
. For integer

, a
d

(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d})

defines uniquely a permutation representation

by setting
\rho_{n}(g_{i})=\sigma_{i}
. By taking independent and uniformly distributed permutations on

, we obtain a random permutation of

that with probability tending to one as

of

and references therein.

3
Operators on unimodular graphs.

We now introduce operators defined on marked graphs. BS convergence offers a natural framework to study spectral properties of large graphs and their limits.

3.1
Local operators for Schreier and covering graphs.

As above let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated group with unit
\mathrm{e}
.
Recall that the
group algebra

\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
is the set of formal finite weighted sums of group elements:

a=\sum_{g\in\Gamma}a_{g}g,

where the support of
a
is finite and
a_{g}\in\mathbb{C}
.
\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
defines a unital
*
-algebra with unit
\mathrm{e}
by setting, for
a,b\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]

a^{*}=\sum_{g}\overline{a_{g}}g^{-1}
and
a.b=\sum_{g,h}a_{g}b_{h}(gh)
.
The left-regular representation
\lambda:\Gamma\to\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\Gamma))
is defined by, for
g,h\in\Gamma
,
\lambda(g)\delta_{h}=\delta_{gh}
where
\delta_{g}\in\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
is the Dirac delta function. This is a faithful unitary representation. For
a\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
, the operator
\lambda(a)\in\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\Gamma))
is

(3.1)

\lambda(a)=\sum_{g}a_{g}\lambda(g).

If
S
is the union of supports of
a
and
a^{*}
minus
\mathrm{e}
, the operator
\lambda(a)
can be interpreted as a local operator on
{\mathrm{Cay}}(\Gamma,S)
where the edge
e=(s,g)\in S\times\Gamma
receives a weight
a_{s}
. For example, if
a=1\!\!{\sf I}_{S}=\sum_{g\in S}g
then
\lambda(a)
is the adjacency operator of
{\mathrm{Cay}}(\Gamma,S)
. If
a\in\mathbb{R}_{+}[\Gamma]
and
\sum_{g}a_{g}=1
, then
\lambda(a)
is the transition kernel of the random walk on
{\mathrm{Cay}}(\Gamma,S)
where the random walker jumps from
g
to
s.g
with probability
a_{s}
.

Similarly, if
\rho:\Gamma\to\mathrm{S}_{V}
is a permutation representation then

\rho(a)=\sum_{g}a_{g}\rho(g)\in\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(V))

is the corresponding local operator on
{\mathrm{Sch}}(\Gamma,S,\rho)
. We have that
\rho(a)
and
\lambda(a)
are self-adjoints when
a=a^{*}
.

The positive faithful state
\tau
on
\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
is
\tau(a)=a_{\mathrm{e}}=\langle\delta_{\mathrm{e}},\lambda(a)\delta_{\mathrm{e}}\rangle
. If along a subsequence of integers,
\rho_{n}
is a permutation representation on
V_{n}=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, then we can reinterpret Lemma
2.8
(iii)
as the
convergence in distribution
of
\rho_{n}
toward
\lambda
:

(3.2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}{\mathrm{Tr}}(\rho_{n}(a))=\tau(a),\quad\hbox{ for all $a\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]$.}

Let
G=(V,E,\xi)

3.3
BS Continuity of the average spectral measure.

Fix a symmetric continuous function
a:\ddot{\mathcal{G}}\to\mathbb{C}
with bounded support. Let
G=(V,E,\xi)
be a locally finite marked graph. Assume that
A=A_{G,a}
defined by (
3.6
) extends to a self-adjoint operator with domain
D(A)\subset\ell^{2}(V)
. Recall from the spectral theorem that for any
\varphi\in D(A)
of unit norm, the exists a probability measure
m_{A}^{\varphi}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
, the
spectral measure at vector
\varphi
such that for any bounded continuous functions
f
,

\langle\varphi,f(A)\varphi\rangle=\int f(\lambda)dm_{A}^{\varphi}(\lambda).

If
E
is the resolution of the identity for
A
, then
dm_{A}^{\varphi}(\lambda)=\langle\varphi,dE(\lambda)\varphi\rangle
. If
G
is finite then

m_{A}^{\varphi}=\sum_{k}\delta_{\lambda_{k}}|\langle\varphi,\psi_{k}\rangle|^{2},

where
(\lambda_{k})
are the eigenvalues (counting multiplicities) and
(\psi_{k})
is orthornormal basis of eigenvectors of
A
and we denote the empirical distribution of eigenvalues by

m_{A}=\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{k=1}^{|V|}\delta_{\lambda_{k}}.

The spatial average of spectral measures is the empirical distribution of eigenvalues:

\mathbb{E}_{U(G)}[m_{A}^{\delta_{o}}]=\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{v\in V}m_{A}^{\delta_{v}}=m_{A}.

More generally, if
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\dot{\mathcal{G}})
is unimodular, in view of Lemma
3.1
, we can define the
average spectral measure
as

m_{\mu,a}=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[m^{\delta_{o}}_{A_{G,a}}].

In particular, for finite
G
,
m_{U(G),a}=m_{A_{G,a}}
. In the group case
\Gamma
with unit
\mathrm{e}
and symmetric
a\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
, we simply write
m_{a}
for the spectral measure
m_{\lambda(a)}^{\delta_{\mathrm{e}}}
. The following theorem asserts that the average spectral measure is continuous for the BS convergence.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
a:\ddot{\mathcal{G}}\to\mathbb{C}
be a symmetric continuous function with bounded support. Let
(G_{n})
be sequence of finite marked graph such that
(G_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathrm{BS}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\dot{\mathcal{G}})
. Then, for the weak topology on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})
,

m_{A_{G_{n},a}}\to m_{\mu,a}.

Moreover, if
a
takes values in
\mathbb{Z}
, we have for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

m_{A_{G_{n},a}}(\{\lambda\})\to m_{\mu,a}(\{\lambda\}).

See Figure
3.1
for an illustration. A proof of the first claim of this theorem can be found in
[
15
,
23
]
(with slightly different assumptions). It relies on the observation that
(G,v)\mapsto m_{A_{G,v}}^{\delta_{v}}
is continuous for the local topology when
G
has uniformly bounded degrees (since
\int\lambda^{k}dm_{A_{G,a}}^{\delta_{v}}(\lambda)=\langle\delta_{v},A_{G,a}^{k}\delta_{v}\rangle
is function of a finite neighborhood of
(G,v)
). The second claim is an instance of Lück approximation theorem due in this context to
[
1
]
(see
[
15
]
for graphs with non-uniformly bounded degrees). It has the striking consequence that if
(G_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathrm{BS}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\dot{\mathcal{G}})
and
a
takes values in
\mathbb{Z}
then
m_{\mu,a}(\{\lambda\})>0
implies that
\lambda
is a totally-real algebraic integer.

For classical random graphs sequences, there are many developments which go much beyond Theorem

3.4
Spectral decomposition.

Theorem

calls for a general study of the spectral decomposition of the operator

when

is a unimodular random rooted graph.

For countable groups

or

Without surprises, for random unimodular graphs even fewer results are known. The study of the spectral decomposition of the adjacency operator of bond percolation in

3.5
Non-backtracking operator.

We conclude this section by the introduction of a new operator which plays an important role in spectral graph theory. Let
G=(V,E)
be a locally finite graph. The
Hashimoto non-backtracting operator
of
G
is the operator defined on compactly supported
\varphi\in\ell^{2}(E)
by the formula:

(3.9)

B\varphi(f)=\sum_{e\in E:e\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathrm{NB}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}f}\varphi(e),

where
e\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathrm{NB}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}f
means
e_{+}=f_{-}
and
e\neq f^{-1}
(the non-backtracking condition). The operator
B
is not symmetric.

More generally, assume that
G=(V,E,\xi)
is a locally finite graph and that the mark space is
\mathbb{C}
. Its non-backtracking operator
B
is defined on compactly supported
\varphi\in\ell^{2}(E)
by:

(3.10)

B\varphi(f)=\sum_{e\in E:e\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathrm{NB}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}f}\xi(e)\varphi(e),

We have
B=\bar{B}D,

where
D
is the diagonal operator
D\varphi(e)=\xi(e)\varphi(e)
and
\bar{B}
is the non-backtracking operator of
\bar{G}=(V,E)
.
B
is a bounded operator on
\ell^{2}(E)
when
\sup_{v\in V}\sum_{e\in E^{+}_{v}}|\xi(e^{-1})|^{2}<\infty
.

There are correspondences between the spectra of weighted adjacency operators as defined in (
3.6
)-(
3.7
) and non-backtracking operators (
3.10
). For simplicity, let us first discuss the case of finite graphs without marks. There is a celebrated determinant identity originating from Ihara
[
56
]
and later formalized in this form
[
11
,
57
]
.

Theorem 3.4
(Ihara-Bass Formula).

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite graph, and let
A
,
B
and
D
be its adjacency, non-backtracking and degree diagianol operators (
D\varphi(v)=\deg(v)\varphi(v)
). For all
z\in\mathbb{C}
, we have:

(3.11)

\det(z1_{E}-B)=(z^{2}-1)^{\chi-1}\det(z^{2}1_{V}-zA+D-1_{V}),

where
1_{V}
and
1_{E}
are the identity operators on
\mathbb{C}^{V}
and
\mathbb{C}^{E}
, respectively, and
\chi=|E|/2-|V|+1
is the Euler characteristic of the graph.

Ihara was developing Selberg’s zeta function theory for discrete groups. The function
\zeta(w)=\det(1_{E}-wB)^{-1}

is called the
Ihara zeta function
of the graph
G
. It admits an expression in terms of closed geodesic, see
[
80
]
.

Note that if
G
is a
d
-regular graph then
D=d1_{V}
. From Theorem
3.4
, it easily follows that

(3.12)

\sigma(B)=\{\pm 1\}\cup\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\text{there exists }\mu\in\sigma(A)\text{ such that }\lambda^{2}-\mu\lambda+d-1=0\right\},

where
\sigma(A)
and
\sigma(B)
are the sets of eigenvalues of
A
and
B
, respectively. One can also express
\sigma(A)
in terms of
\sigma(B)
. There is even an explicit description of the eigenspaces of
B
and the corresponding eigenspaces for
A
; see
[
57
]
. Let us sketch a direct proof of (
3.12
) and this correspondance. Suppose
B\varphi=\lambda\varphi
for some non-zero vector

\varphi\in\mathbb{C}^{E}
and
|\lambda|\neq 1
. We introduce the divergence vector
\hat{\varphi}\in\mathbb{C}^{V}
defined for every
x\in V
by

\hat{\varphi}(x)=\sum_{e\in E_{x}^{+}}\varphi(e).

Let
e\in E
with
e_{-}=x
,
e_{+}=y
, we have
\hat{\varphi}(x)=(B\varphi)(e)+\varphi(e^{-1})
. Writing
(B\varphi)(e)=\lambda\varphi(e)
for
e
and
e^{-1}
, we arrive at the inversion fomula
\varphi(e)=(\lambda\hat{\varphi}(x)-\hat{\varphi}(y))/(\lambda^{2}-1)
. Substituting this identity into
(B\varphi)(e)=\lambda\varphi(e)
, we find

(A\hat{\varphi})(x)=\mu\hat{\varphi}(x)
with
\lambda^{2}-\mu\lambda+d-1=0
as requested.

The Ihara-Bass identity has several extensions and variants. For example, one can write a determinant identity of the type (
3.11
) relating the characteristic polynomial of
A
,
\det(z1_{V}-A)
, to a determinant of the form
\det(1_{E}-BD(z))
, where
D(z)
is an analytic diagonal operator on an unbounded domain of
\mathbb{C}
; see
[
83
,
5
]
. The operator
BD(z)
is a weighted non-backtracking operator as in (
3.10
). These spectral correspondences also extend to complex weights, infinite graphs and even matrix-valued weights
[
19
,
20
,
18
]
.

4
Edge eigenvalues of large marked graphs.

We now present some results on the extreme eigenvalues of local operators of large sparse marked graphs.

4.1
Lower bound from BS convergence.

As in Theorem
3.2
, let
a:\ddot{\mathcal{G}}\to\mathbb{C}
be a symmetric continuous function with bounded support and
(G_{n})
be subsequence of finite marked graph such that
G_{n}
has
n
vertices and
(G_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathrm{BS}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mu
for some
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\dot{\mathcal{G}})
. We consider the sequence of local self-adjoint operators
A_{n}=A_{G_{n},a}
. We order the eigenvalues of
A_{n}
as
\lambda_{n}(A_{n})\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{1}(A_{n})
. By Theorem
3.2
, all but
o(n)
eigenvalues are in a small neighborhood of
\mathrm{supp}(m_{\mu,a})
. In particular, if
k_{n}/n\to 0
then

(4.1)

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\lambda_{k_{n}}(A_{n})\geq\sup\big\{\lambda:\lambda\in\mathrm{supp}(m_{\mu,a})\big\}.

Eigenvalues of
A_{n}
which are not in a small neighborhood of
\mathrm{supp}(m_{\mu,a})
are called the
spectral outliers
. They capture some geometric information on
G_{n}
which cannot be read from the local topology.

4.2
Alon-Boppana lower bound.

A lower bound of the form (
4.1
) holds for all covering graphs under a non-negativity assumption on the local operator. We shall present this inequality in a general group setting. Let
\Gamma
be finitely generated with unit
\mathrm{e}
. The
\ell^{\infty}
-norm in
\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
is

\|a\|=\lim_{p\to\infty}\tau\left((aa^{*})^{\frac{p}{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

It is equal to the operator norm of
\lambda(a)
on
\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
which we also denote
\|\lambda(a)\|
. The closure of
\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
under this norm is the reduced
C^{*}
-algebra of the group, denoted by
C^{*}_{\mathrm{red}}(\Gamma)
.

In
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
we also denote by
\|\cdot\|
the operator norm (it is also the
\ell^{\infty}
-norm in
(M_{n}(\mathbb{C}),\frac{1}{n}{\mathrm{Tr}})
). Let
\rho_{n}:\Gamma\to\mathrm{U}_{n}
be a unitary representation of
\Gamma
of dimension
n
. Recall that a subspace
H
of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is
invariant for
\rho_{n}
if for all
g
,
\rho_{n}(g)H=H
. We say that
\rho_{n}
has
non-negative characters
if for all
g\in\Gamma
:

{\mathrm{Tr}}(\rho_{n}(g))\geq 0.

This condition holds for example if
\rho_{n}(g)\in\mathrm{S}_{n}
which is the case relevant for graphs but also if
n=m^{2}
and
\rho_{n}(g)=\pi(g)\otimes\bar{\pi}(g)
is the tensor product of a unitary representation
\pi
of dimension
m
(this last case is relevant in quantum channels).

Next, we consider
a=\sum_{g}a_{g}g\in M_{k}(\mathbb{C})[\Gamma]
which has
non-negative joint moments
in the sense that for any
g_{1},\ldots,g_{l}
in
\Gamma
and
\epsilon,\ldots,\epsilon_{l}
in
\{1,*\}
, we have

{\mathrm{Tr}}\left(a^{\epsilon_{1}}_{g_{1}}\cdots a^{\epsilon_{l}}_{g_{l}}\right)\geq 0
. Recall finally the operators
\rho_{n}(a)\in M_{k}(\mathbb{C})\otimes M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and
\lambda(a)\in M_{k}(\mathbb{C})\otimes\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}(\Gamma))
defined by (
3.5
).

Proposition 4.1
(Alon-Boppana bound, non quantitative).

Let
a\in M_{k}(\mathbb{C})[\Gamma]
with support
S
and having non-negative joint moments. Let
\rho_{n}:\Gamma\to\mathrm{U}_{n}
be a representation of
\Gamma
with non-negative characters Then, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exist
n_{0}=n_{0}(\Gamma,S,k,\epsilon)
and
\delta=\delta(\Gamma,S,k,\epsilon)
such that if
n\geq n_{0}
and
H_{n}
is an invariant subspace of codimension at most
\delta n
, we have

\|\rho_{n}(a)_{|\mathbb{C}^{k}\otimes H_{n}}\|\geq(1-\epsilon)\|\lambda(a)\|.

Moreover, if
C^{*}_{\mathrm{red}}(\Gamma)
is exact, then
n_{0},\delta
can be made uniform over all
k
(but still depend on
(\Gamma,S,\epsilon)
).

For the definition of exactness see
[
31
]
(all usual groups are exact). Note that, if
a=a^{*}
, by applying the lemma to
a_{t}=a+t\mathrm{e}
for
t
large enough, we also obtain a lower bound on the rightmost point in the spectra of
\rho_{n}(a)_{|\mathbb{C}^{k}\otimes H_{n}}
. This lemma has many variants since its original statement on adjacency operators of
d
-regular graphs
[
4
]
and their universal covering tree. In this case,
\|\lambda(a)\|=2\sqrt{d-1}
as can be seen from the support of
m_{T_{d}}
in (
3.8
). Finite regular graphs such that the second largest eigenvalue of their adjacency matrix is below
2\sqrt{d-1}
are called
Ramanujan graphs
.

Proof 1
(Proof of Proposition
4.1
).

Since
\|\rho_{n}(a)\|^{2}=\|\rho_{n}(a)\rho_{n}(a)^{*}\|=\|\rho_{n}(aa^{*})\|
, we may assume that
a
is symmetric. Let
Q
be the orthogonal projection onto
\mathbb{C}^{k}\otimes H_{n}
and
m=\mathrm{codim}(H_{n})
. For any integer
l\geq 1
,

\|\rho_{n}(a)_{|\mathbb{C}^{k}\otimes H_{n}}\|^{2l}=\|\rho_{n}(a)Q\|^{2l}=\|\rho_{n}(a^{2l})Q\|\geq\frac{1}{nk}{\mathrm{Tr}}(\rho_{n}(a^{2l})Q),

where have used the invariance of
H_{n}
. Expanding the trace,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{nk}{\mathrm{Tr}}(\rho_{n}(a^{2l})Q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{nk}{\mathrm{Tr}}(\rho_{n}(a^{2l}))-\frac{1}{nk}{\mathrm{Tr}}(\rho_{n}(a^{2l})(1-Q))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{nk}\sum_{g}{\mathrm{Tr}}((a^{2l})_{g}){\mathrm{Tr}}(\rho_{n}(g))-\frac{m}{n}\|\rho_{n}(a^{2l})\|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{k}{\mathrm{Tr}}((a^{2l})_{\mathrm{e}})-\frac{m}{n}\|\rho_{n}(a^{2l})\|,

where we have used at the last line the non-negativity assumptions on
\rho_{n}
and
a
. For
b\in M_{k}(\mathbb{C})[\Gamma]
, let

\|b\|_{2}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{1}{k}{\mathrm{Tr}}\otimes\langle\delta_{\mathrm{e}},\cdot\delta_{\mathrm{e}}\rangle\right)(\lambda(bb^{*}))}=\sqrt{\sum_{g}\frac{1}{k}{\mathrm{Tr}}(b_{g}b_{g}^{*})}.

Note that
\frac{1}{k}{\mathrm{Tr}}((a^{2l})_{\mathrm{e}})=\|a^{l}\|_{2}^{2}
and by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have, if the support of
b
in
\Gamma
has
s
elements,

\|\rho_{n}(b)\|\leq\sum_{g}\|b_{g}\|\leq\sqrt{sk}\|b\|_{2}.

Using a rough estimation on the size of support of
a^{2l}
, we arrive at

\|\rho_{n}(a)_{|\mathbb{C}^{k}\otimes H_{n}}\|^{2l}\geq\|a^{l}\|_{2}^{2}\left(1-\frac{|S|^{l}\sqrt{k}m}{n}\right).

Finally, since
\|a^{l}\|_{2}^{1/l}\to\|\lambda(a)\|
, playing with quantifiers, we obtain the first claim. If
C^{*}_{\mathrm{red}}(\Gamma)
is exact, the claim follows as in the end of proof of
[
18
, Theorem 7.3]
.

It is possible to be quantitative in the proof provided that we have a rate of convergence for
\|a^{l}\|_{2}^{1/l}\to\|\lambda(a)\|
. The
rapid decay property
is a property which allows precisely this, refer to
[
33
]
. The group
\Gamma
has the rapid decay property if for all
a\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
we have for some finite symmetric generated set
S
and constants
C_{1},C_{2}\geq 1
,

(4.2)

\|\lambda(a)\|\leq C_{1}\mathrm{diam}^{C_{2}}_{S}(a)\|a\|_{2},

where
\mathrm{diam}_{S}(a)
is the diameter of the support of
a
in
{\mathrm{Cay}}(\Gamma,S)
and
\|a\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{g}|a|^{2}
. This is a group property which does not depend on the choice of
S
(only the constant
C_{1}
does). All Gromov-hyperbolic groups and Abelian groups satisfies this property but not
\mathrm{SL}_{d}(\mathbb{Z})
for
d\geq 3
. For
a\in M_{k}(\mathbb{C})[\Gamma]
, we find
\|\lambda(a)\|\leq\sqrt{k}C_{1}\mathrm{diam}^{C_{2}}_{S}(a)\|a\|_{2}
. For the free group, the rapid decay is known as Haagerup inequality. Applying those bounds for the free group generators, we could obtain the following quantitative bound in
[
18
]
:

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(a_{i})_{-d\leq i\leq d}\in M_{k}(\mathbb{C})^{2d+1}
with non-negative moments. For some numerical constant
c>0
, for any permutation matrices
(S_{1},\ldots,S_{d})
of dimension
n
, setting
S_{0}=1_{n}
and
S_{-i}=S^{*}_{i}
, we have

\left\|\left(\sum_{i=-d}^{d}a_{i}\otimes S_{i}\right)_{|\mathbb{C}^{k}\otimes 1\!\!{\sf I}^{\perp}}\right\|\geq\left\|\sum_{i=-d}^{d}a_{i}\otimes\lambda(g_{i})\right\|\left(1-c\frac{\ln(2d)}{\sqrt{\ln n}}\right),

where
(g_{1},\ldots,g_{d})
are the free generators of the free group
\mathrm{F}_{d}
,
g_{0}=\mathrm{e}
its unit and we have set
g_{-i}=g_{i}^{-1}
.

The bound is universal as it does not depend on the dimension
k
(there are better bounds for small
k
). It is stated here for a linear expression in the permutation matrices, for more general polynomials, see
[
18
, Section 8]
.

4.3
Classical random graphs.

We present here some known asymptotics for adjacency and non-backtracking operators of classical random graph ensembles.

Random regular graph.
It was conjectured by Alon
[
4
]
and proved by Friedman
[
41
]
that almost all regular graphs are almost Ramanujan, see Figure
3.1
(left). For integers
n,d\geq 1
, let
\mathbb{G}(n,d)
denote the set of simple
d
-regular graphs with vertex set
V=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. For
2\leq d\leq n-1
and
nd
even,
\mathbb{G}(n,d)
is non-empty.

Theorem 4.3
(Friedman’s Theorem, adjacency version).

Let
d\geq 3
be an integer,
G
a random graph distributed according to the uniform measure on
\mathbb{G}(n,d)
, and let
d=\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\mu_{n}
be the eigenvalues of its adjacency operator
A
. Then for every
\epsilon>0
, the event

\max(\mu_{2},-\mu_{n})\leq 2\sqrt{d-1}+\epsilon,

has probability tending to
1
as
n
tends to infinity (and
nd
is even).

Simpler proofs now exist
[
16
,
34
]
and the fluctuation of order
n^{-2/3}
has been recently proved in
[
54
]
. In
[
41
,
16
]
, one first proves the corresponding statement for the non-backtracking matrix
B
and then deduces it for
A
via the Ihara–Bass formula (
3.12
). For a finite graph
G=(V,E)
with
|E|=2m
, we denote the complex eigenvalues of
B
by
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{2m}
indexed abritarily by their absolute values:

(4.3)

|\lambda_{2m}|\leq\cdots\leq|\lambda_{2}|\leq\lambda_{1}.

If
G
is a
d
-regular graph,
\lambda_{1}=d-1
and the non-backtracking operator of its universal covering tree has spectral radius
\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}
. An equivalent formulation of Theorem
4.3
is the following, see Figure
4.1
for an illustration.

Theorem 4.4
(Friedman’s Theorem, non-backtracking version).

Let
d\geq 3
be an integer,
G
a random graph distributed according to the uniform measure on
\mathbb{G}(n,d)
, and
d-1=\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots
the eigenvalues of its non-backtracking operator
B
indexed as in (
4.3
). Then for every
\epsilon>0
, the event

|\lambda_{2}|\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}+\epsilon,

has probability tending to
1
as
n
tends to infinity (and
nd
is even).

Erdős–Rényi graph.
For fixed
d>0
, we now consider the Erdős-Rényi random graph with
n
vertices and edge probability
\min(1,d/n)
, see Example
2.7
. The extreme eigenvalues of the adjacency operator
A
diverge with
n
, and their eigenvectors are concentrated on vertices with largest degrees and their neighbors
[
58
,
12
,
51
]
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
d>0
,
G
be an Erdős–Rényi graph on
n
vertices and edge probability
\min(1,d/n)
. Let
\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}\ldots\geq\mu_{n}
be the eigenvalues of its adjacency operator
A
. Then, for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, with probability tending to one as
n\to\infty
, for all
1\leq k\leq n^{1-\epsilon}
, we have

{{\left|\mu_{k}-\sqrt{d_{k}}\right|}}\leq\epsilon\sqrt{d_{1}}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad{{\left|\mu_{n+1-k}+\sqrt{d_{k}}\right|}}\leq\epsilon\sqrt{d_{1}},

where
d_{1}\geq d_{2}\geq\cdots
is the ordered degree sequence of
G
.

For all
1\leq k\leq n^{1-\epsilon}
, we have
d_{k}\sim\ln(n/k)/\ln\ln(n/d)
. Theorem
4.5
is extracted from
[
12
]
(which considers inhomogeneous random graphs). A sharper result has been proved in
[
51
]
which also implies the localization of the top eigenvectors. As a corollary of Theorem
4.5
there is no spectral gap in the sense that
\mu_{k}/\mu_{1}
converges in probability to
1
for every
k=n^{o(1)}
(Figure
3.1
does not show this claim). For the non-backtracking operator
B
, however, there exists an analogue of Theorem
4.4
, proved in
[
26
]
; see Figure
4.1
for an illustration.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
d>0
,
G
be an Erdős–Rényi graph on
n
vertices and edge probability
\min(1,d/n)
. Let
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots
the eigenvalues of its non-backtracking operator
B
indexed as in (
4.3
). Then for every
\epsilon>0
, the event

|\lambda_{1}-d|\leq\epsilon\quad\text{and}\quad|\lambda_{2}|\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{1}}+\epsilon

has probability tending to
1
as
n\to\infty
.

This theorem is proved using much more probabilistic tools than Theorem
4.4
. For instance, the BS limit is no longer deterministic, unlike in the regular case. There are analogs of Theorem
4.6
for inhomogeneous random graphs (such as the stochastic block model) and for weighted graphs, see notably
[
26
,
21
,
79
]
. These results were motivated by the study of spectral algorithms in community detection and compressed sensing problems.

Figure 4.1:
Left: eigenvalues in the complex plane of
B
for a uniform
d
-regular graphs with
n=500
vertices,
d=4
. Right: eigenvalues of
B
for an Erdős-Rényi graph with
n=500
vertices and edge probability
d/n
,
d=4
.

An interesting open problem is to establish the convergence of the empirical distribution of the eigenvalues of
B
in Theorem
4.6
. Also, beyond adjacency and non-backtracking operators, a systematic study of the edge eigenvalues of local operators is still missing for the Erdős-Rényi random graph and other inhomogeneous random graphs. As we explain next, much more is known for random Schreier and covering graphs.

4.4
A strenghtening of BS convergence.

As usual let
\Gamma
be finitely generated group with left regular representation
\lambda
and
\rho_{n}:\Gamma\to\mathrm{U}_{n}
be a subsequence of unitary representations of dimension
n
.

Strong convergence in distribution.
Recall from (
3.2
) that
\rho_{n}
converges in distribution toward
\lambda
if the normalized characters converge. We say that
\rho_{n}

converges strongly (in distribution)
toward
\lambda
if, in addition,

(4.4)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\rho_{n}(a)\|=\|\lambda(a)\|,\quad\hbox{for all $a\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]$},

where
\|\cdot\|
is the operator norm and is also the
\ell^{\infty}
-norm defined in (
4.2
) in the relevant
*
-algebras (ie
(M_{n}(\mathbb{C}),\frac{1}{n}{\mathrm{Tr}}))
and
(\mathbb{C}[\Gamma],\tau)
). Strong convergence in distribution appeared in
[
47
,
37
]
in the more general context of matrix
*
-algebras generated by finitely many matrices. We refer to the surveys
[
65
,
81
]
.

For any even integer
1\leq p<\infty
, the convergence in distribution implies the convergence of the
\ell^{p}
-norm
(\frac{1}{n}{\mathrm{Tr}}(\rho_{n}(aa^{*})^{p/2}))^{1/p}
of
\rho_{n}(a)
, toward the
\ell^{p}
-norm of
\lambda(a)
. Strong convergence allows
p=\infty
and it has important consequences. In particular, if
a=a^{*}
then strong convergence rules out outliers: all eigenvalues of
\rho_{n}(a)
are within vanishing distance from the spectrum of
\lambda(a)
.

There are various implications between convergence in distribution and strong convergence or, more precisely, between (
3.2
) and (
4.4
). First of all, arguing as in Subsection
4.1
, (
3.2
) implies

(4.5)

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\|\rho_{n}(a)\|\geq\|\lambda(a)\|,\quad\hbox{for all $a\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]$}.

Therefore, strongly converging representations are asymptotically spectrally optimal. Also, if
\Gamma
is of subexponential growth (e.g. amenable),
then convergence in distribution implies strong convergence. For non-amenable groups, the difficult part in establishing strong convergence is the converse inequality to (
4.5
):

(4.6)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|\rho_{n}(a)\|\leq\|\lambda(a)\|,\quad\hbox{for all $a\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]$}.

It even turns out that the upper bound (
4.6
) often implies both (
4.5
) and (
3.2
). We say that a group has the unique trace property if
C_{\mathrm{red}}^{*}(\Gamma)
has a unique trace, namely the canonical trace
\tau(a)=a_{e}=\langle\delta_{e},\lambda(a)\delta_{e}\rangle
. By
[
30
, Theorem 1.3]
a discrete group has the unique trace property if and only if it is has no non-trivial amenable normal subgroup. If
\Gamma
has the unique trace property, then (
4.6
) implies (
4.5
) and (
3.2
), see
[
81
, Lemma 2.13]
.

For permutation representations, we have seen in Lemma
2.8
that convergence in distribution is BS convergence. Strong convergence is thus a reinforcement of BS convergence. However, a clear obstruction appears. The subspace
H_{n}=\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{1})
of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
of vectors with constant coordinates is left invariant by all permutation matrices. In particular
H_{n}
is an invariant subspace of
\rho_{n}
. Therefore, the best we can hope for is the strong convergence of the subrepresentation
(\rho_{n})_{|H_{n}^{\perp}}
toward
\lambda
:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\rho_{n}(a)_{|H_{n}^{\perp}}\|=\|\lambda(a)\|,\quad\hbox{for all $a\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]$}.

Depending on the representation, we may sometimes aim at establishing strong convergence on the orthogonal of a larger invariant subspace considered as trivial (as in
[
18
, Theorem 9.3]
for a random permutation representation of Cartesian products of
\mathrm{F}_{d}
).

The linearization trick.

There are many variants of this
linearization trick
. In a nutshell, it asserts that for
(V_{1},\ldots,V_{d})\in M_{k}(\mathbb{C})
, we can replace the study of non-commutative
*
-polynomials
P(V_{1},\ldots,V_{d})
with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}
by the study of non-commutative
*
-polynomials
P(V_{1},\ldots,V_{d})
with matrix-valued coefficients which are linear in the
V_{i}^{\prime}s
. We thus have traded linearity for matrix coefficients. See
[
73
,
70
,
22
]
for three different instances of this trick. The most relevant for us here is due to Pisier
[
73
]
:

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
S
be a finite symmetric generating set of
\Gamma
such that
\mathrm{e}\in S
. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

\lim_{n}\|\rho_{n}(a)\|=\|\lambda(a)\|
for all
a\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
.

(ii)

\lim_{n}\|\rho_{n}(a)\|=\|\lambda(a)\|
for all
k\geq 1
and
a\in M_{k}(\mathbb{C})[\Gamma]
.

(iii)

\lim_{n}\|\rho_{n}(a)\|=\|\lambda(a)\|
for all
k\geq 1
and
a\in M_{k}(\mathbb{C})[\Gamma]
with support in
S
.

The key implication is
(iii)\Rightarrow(i)
. It is proved by showing that for any
a\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
, there exist
k\geq 1
,
b\in M_{k}(\mathbb{C})[\Gamma]
with support in
S
and a continuous real function
f
such that
\|\rho(a)\|=f(\|\rho(b)\|)
for all unitary representations
\rho
(it is enough to prove this for
\Gamma=\mathrm{F}_{d}
and its free generators). See
[
59
,
18
,
81
]
for quantitative and algorithmic versions of this claim. The linearization trick is an important ingredient in most proofs of strong convergence. It is interesting to notice that elements
a\in M_{k}(\mathbb{C})[\Gamma]
in (iii) are precisely the operators which appeared in (
3.5
) as local operators of
(\Gamma,S)
-covers.

4.5
Applications of strong convergence.

In their seminal paper
[
47
]
, Haagerup and Thorbjørnsen have proven the almost sure strong convergence of independent random matrices of dimension
n
sampled according to the Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE). Their original motivation was to establish that the Ext-invariant for the reduced C*-algebra of the free group on
2
generators was not a group but only a semi-group, answering an old question in operator algebra. Since then, strong convergence has found many other interesting applications in operator algebras
[
48
]
but in various other fields such as in spectral geometry
[
50
]
or in minimal surfaces
[
78
]
to cite a few. We will discuss in subsections
4.6
-
4.8
two applications in graph theory. Also related to BS convergence
[
24
]
: the strong convergence of permutation representations implies the cutoff for the random walks on Schreier and covering graphs of non-amenable groups
\Gamma
satisfying the rapid decay property (
4.2
).

We expect that there will be further applications of strong convergence and refer to the surveys
[
65
,
81
]
. Note however that after more than twenty years of active research in the area, random matrix theory is still currently the only access to strong convergence: there are no known non-trivial deterministic examples of strong convergence of matrix algebras.

4.6
Typical graph distance.

We illustrate the strong convergence phenomenon by giving a nice yet elementary application in geometric graph theory. As usual let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated group with left regular representation
\lambda
. We assume that
\Gamma
is non-amenable. If
S
is a finite generatoring set of
\Gamma
, we denote by
\beta_{S}>0
the exponential growth rate of
{\mathrm{Cay}}(\Gamma,S)
, that is
\beta_{S}=\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{r}\ln|B_{S}(r)|
, where
B_{S}(r)
is the ball of radius
r
in
{\mathrm{Cay}}(\Gamma,S)
. Let
\rho_{n}:\Gamma\to\mathrm{S}_{V_{n}}
be a sequence of permutation representations on
|V_{n}|
with
|V_{n}|\to\infty
. The ball of radius
r
in
{\mathrm{Sch}}(\Gamma,S,\rho_{n})
around any point contains at most
|B_{S}(r)|
elements. It follows that the eccentry of any point is at least
(1+o(1))\ln|V_{n}|/\beta_{S}
. More precisely,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\min_{v\in V_{n}}\max_{u\in V_{n}}\frac{d(u,v)}{\ln|V_{n}|}\geq\frac{1}{\beta_{S}},

where
d(u,v)
is the graph distance in
{\mathrm{Sch}}(\Gamma,S,\rho_{n})
. This bound is actually reached for almost all pairs of points if
\rho_{n}
converges strongly on
1\!\!{\sf I}^{\perp}
(orthogonal of vectors with constant coordinates).

Lemma 4.8
.

Assume that
\Gamma
is non-amenable and has the rapid decay property (
4.2
). Let
\rho_{n}:\Gamma\to\mathrm{S}_{V_{n}}
be a sequence of permutation representations which converge strongly toward
\lambda
on
1\!\!{\sf I}^{\perp}
. Then, for any finite generating set
S
of
\Gamma
, for all
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\max_{v\in V_{n}}\frac{\left|\left\{u\in V_{n}:d(u,v)\geq(1+\epsilon)\frac{\ln|V_{n}|}{\beta_{S}}\right\}\right|}{|V_{n}|}=0,

where
d(u,v)
is the distance between
u
and
v
in
{\mathrm{Sch}}(\Gamma,S,\rho_{n})
.

Proof 2
.

Fix
0<\beta<\beta_{S}
. There exists
\delta>0
such that
\beta(1+2\delta)<\beta_{S}
. Then, for all
r
large enough, we have
|B_{S}(r)|\geq e^{\beta(1+2\delta)r}
. For such
r
to be defined later, let
p=\frac{1}{|B_{S}(r)|}\sum_{g}1\!\!{\sf I}_{g\in B_{S}(r)}g\in\mathbb{C}[\Gamma]
,
P_{n}=\rho_{n}(p)
and
P=\lambda(p)
:
P_{n}
and
P
are the transition kernels of the simple random walks on
{\mathrm{Sch}}(\Gamma,S,\rho_{n})
and
{\mathrm{Cay}}(\Gamma,S)
respectively. We have
P_{n}1=P_{n}^{*}1=1
. Thus, for any
v\in V_{n}
and integer
l\geq 1
,

\sum_{u}\left|P_{n}^{l}(v,u)-\frac{1}{|V_{n}|}\right|\leq\sqrt{|V_{n}|}\sqrt{\sum_{u}\left|P_{n}^{l}(v,u)-\frac{1}{|V_{n}|}\right|^{2}}=\sqrt{|V_{n}|}\|(P_{n}^{l})_{|1\!\!{\sf I}^{\perp}}(v,\cdot)\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{|V_{n}|}\|(P_{n})_{|1\!\!{\sf I}^{\perp}}\|^{l}.

Strong convergence implies
\|(P_{n})_{|1\!\!{\sf I}^{\perp}}\|\leq\sqrt{2}\|P\|
for
n
large enough, while rapid decay (
4.2
) implies
\|P\|\leq C_{1}r^{C}_{2}\|p\|_{2}=C_{1}r^{C}_{2}|B_{S}(r)|^{-1/2}\leq(e^{-\beta(1+\delta)})^{r/2}
if
r
was chosen large enough. We find

\sum_{u}\left|P_{n}^{l}(v,u)-\frac{1}{|V_{n}|}\right|\leq\left(2|V_{n}|e^{-rl\beta(1+\delta)}\right)^{1/2}.

If
rl\geq\ln(2|V_{n}|)/\beta
then (i) the right hand side goes to
0
and (ii)
d(u,v)>\ln(2|V_{n}|)/\beta
implies
P_{n}^{l}(v,u)=0
. Use

\left|\left\{u\in V_{n}:d(u,v)>\frac{\ln(2|V_{n}|)}{\beta}\right\}\right|\leq|V_{n}|\sum_{u}\left|\frac{1}{|V_{n}|}-P_{n}^{l}(v,u)\right|,

and the lemma follows since
\beta
can be taken arbitrarily close to
\beta_{S}
.

Remark that
S
need not be symmetric in this lemma. Also, the proof guarantees the existence of many different paths (replace
B_{S}(r)
by any other set of radius
r
and size at least
e^{r\beta}
, e.g. an half-sphere of radius
r
). This joint use of strong convergence and rapid decay is very efficient, see
[
24
]
for a more elaborate use.

4.7
Strong convergence for random representations of the free group.

As already mentionned, each family
(V_{1},\ldots,V_{d})
of unitary matrices in
\mathrm{U}_{k}
uniquely defines a unitary representation of dimension
k
of the free group
\mathrm{F}_{d}=\langle g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}\rangle
by setting
\rho(g_{i})=V_{i}
, for
1\leq i\leq d
.

Then, let
(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
be random unitary matrices of dimension
n
that are Haar distributed on
\mathrm{U}_{n}
,
\mathrm{O}_{n}
or
\mathrm{S}_{n}
(this last case is relevant for graphs). From what precedes this defines a random representation of
\mathrm{F_{d}}
of dimension
k_{n}=n
by setting
\rho_{n}(g_{i})=V_{i}=U_{i}
. More generally, consider a unitary representation
\pi_{n}
of dimension
k_{n}
of
\mathrm{U}_{n}
,
\mathrm{O}_{n}
or
\mathrm{S}_{n}
. Then,
we can define a random representation of
\mathrm{F_{d}}
of dimension
k_{n}
by setting
\rho_{n}(g_{i})=V_{i}=\pi_{n}(U_{i})
. The above case
\pi_{n}(u)=u
corresponds to the standard representation. Below, we consider the tensor product representations defined as, for a pair of integers
(q_{+},q_{-})
as

(4.7)

\pi_{n}(u)=u^{\otimes q_{+}}\otimes\bar{u}^{\otimes q_{-}}.

This is a representation of dimension
k_{n}=n^{q}
with

q=q_{+}+q_{-}.

Note that, there is no real loss of generality as all representations of
\mathrm{U}_{n}
are sub-representations of some tensor product representation.

Finally, let
H_{n}
be the vector subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{k_{n}}
of the vectors that are left invariant by
\pi_{n}(u)
for all
u
in
\mathrm{U}_{N}
,
\mathrm{O}_{N}
or
\mathrm{S}_{N}
. Note that
H_{n}
depends on whether we take
\mathrm{U}_{n}
,
\mathrm{O}_{n}
or
\mathrm{S}_{n}
. Importantly, for
q=1
,
H_{n}
is trivial for
\mathrm{U}_{n}
or
\mathrm{O}_{n}
while
H_{n}=\mathrm{span}(1\!\!{\sf I})
for
\mathrm{S}_{n}
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
be Haar distributed on
\mathrm{U}_{n},\mathrm{O}_{n}
or
\mathrm{S}_{n}
, and let
\rho_{n}
be the random representation of
\mathrm{F}_{d}=\langle g_{1},\ldots,g_{d}\rangle
of dimension
k_{n}=n^{q}
defined by
\rho_{n}(g_{i})=\pi_{n}(U_{i})
, with
\pi_{n}
as in (
4.7
). Then, as
n\to\infty
,
\rho_{n}
restricted to
H_{n}^{\perp}
converges strongly toward
\lambda
in probability. More precisely, for any
a\in\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{F}_{d}]
,

(4.8)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{k_{n}}{\mathrm{Tr}}(\rho_{n}(a))=\tau(a)\quad\hbox{ and }\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\rho_{n}(a)_{|H_{n}^{\perp}}\|=\|\lambda(a)\|.

The LHS of (
4.8
) is the convergence in distribution. For
q=1
and
\mathrm{U}_{n}
or
\mathrm{O}_{n}
this is due to Voiculescu
[
82
]
, for general
q\geq 1
, see
[
36
]
. For
\mathrm{S}_{n}
, the LHS of (
4.8
) is implied by
[
71
]
. The RHS of (
4.8
) is the improvement to strong convergence. It was first established for
\mathrm{U}_{n}
and
\mathrm{O}_{n}
and
q=1
in
[
37
]
, and for general
q\geq 1
including
q
growing with
n
but not too fast, in
[
20
,
66
,
35
]
. The strongest current result is
[
35
]
where
q
can be taken as large as
n^{1/3-\varepsilon}
for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
. Theorem
4.9
for
\mathrm{S}_{n}
and
q=1,2
is first proven in
[
19
]
, tensor product permutations for
q\geq 1
are treated in
[
34
,
32
]
. The strongest current result being
[
32
]
with
q\leq n^{1/12-\varepsilon}
for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
. Theorem
4.9
is stated in asymptotic form. There are some quantitative results, see notably
[
18
,
35
]
.

Let us mention a few common ingredients in the proofs of strong convergence in
[
19
,
20
,
18
]
. The first step is to use the linearization trick (Proposition
4.7
(iii)) with the free generators
S
which reduces the task to prove that for any integer
k\geq 1
and
a=(a_{i})_{-d\leq i\leq d}\in M_{k}(\mathbb{C})^{2d+1}
,

\|(A_{n})_{|\mathbb{C}^{k}\otimes H^{\perp}_{n}}\|\leq(1+o(1))\|A_{\mathrm{F}}\|\quad\hbox{with}\quad A_{n}=\sum_{i=-d}^{d}a_{g}\otimes V_{i},\quad\hbox{and}\quad A_{\mathrm{F}}=\sum_{i=-d}^{d}a_{i}\otimes\lambda(g_{i}),

where
g_{-i}=g_{i}^{-1}
,
g_{0}=\mathrm{e}
,
U_{-i}=U_{i}^{*}
,
U_{0}=1_{n}
and
V_{i}=\pi_{n}(U_{i})
. The second step is to use Ihara-Bass-Selberg type formulas, as in Theorem
3.4
, to transfer spectral properties of the operator
A_{n}
in terms of a family of matrix-valued non-backtracking operators (in
[
18
]
we express directly the powers of
A_{n}
in terms of sums of matrix-valued non-backtracking operators). We then estimate the trace of a high-power of such non-backtracking operator. The combinatorics is greatly simplified because non-backtracking paths in
{\mathrm{Cay}}(F_{d},S)
are trivial. The main probabilistic part is the Weingarten calculus to perform integration with the respect to Haar measures. We prove sharp upper bounds on expressions of the form, for
T
of order up to
n^{\alpha}
for some
0<\alpha<1
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{t=1}^{T}\left(V_{x_{t},y_{t}}-\mathbb{E}V_{x_{t},y_{t}}\right)\right],

where
U
is Haar distributed on
\mathrm{U}_{n},\mathrm{O}_{n}
or
\mathrm{S}_{n}
,
V=\pi_{n}(U)=U^{\otimes q_{-}}\otimes\bar{U}^{\otimes q_{+}}
and
x_{t},y_{t}\in\{1,\ldots,n\}^{q}
are coordinates. We refer to
[
81
]
for a survey on an alternative and powerful strategy known as the polynomial method.

4.8
Applications to spectral graph theory.

It is an exercise to check that if (
4.4
) holds then for any
a=a^{*}\in M_{k}(\mathbb{C})
, the spectrum of
\rho_{n}(a)
converges to the spectrum of
\lambda(a)
in Hausdorff distance (i.e. no outliers). As consequences of Theorem
4.9
for
q=1
and
\mathrm{S}_{n}
, we notably obtain the following two corollaries.

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
S=S^{-1}
be a finite generating subset of
\mathrm{F}_{d}
and let
A_{\mathrm{F}}=\lambda(1_{S})
be the adjacency operator of
\mathrm{Cay}(\mathrm{F}_{d},S)
. Let
A_{n}=\rho_{n}(1_{S})
be the adjacency operator of a uniformly sampled Schreier graph on the vertex set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
with generator
S
.
Then, in probability, the Hausdorff distance between the spectrum of
A_{\mathrm{F}}
and
(A_{n})_{|\mathrm{1}^{\perp}}
tends to
0
as
n\to\infty
.

The case where
S
is the set of free generators of
\mathrm{F_{d}}
and their inverse is Friedman’s Theorem
[
41
]
, see also Theorem
4.3
. The second corollary of Theorem
4.9
for
q=1
and
\mathrm{S}_{n}
is the generalized Alon’s conjecture
[
40
]
.

Corollary 4.11
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite graph, and let
G_{n}
be a uniformly sampled
n
-lift of
G
. Let
A_{n}
be as the adjacency operator of
G_{n}
and
A_{\mathrm{F}}
be the adjacency operator of the universal covering tree of
G
. Then, in probability, the Hausdorff distance between the spectrum of
A_{\mathrm{F}}
and
(A_{n})_{|\mathbb{C}^{V}\otimes\mathrm{1}^{\perp}}
tends to
0
as
n\to\infty
.

In the two above corollaries, we can replace the adjacency operator by any other symmetric local operator.
