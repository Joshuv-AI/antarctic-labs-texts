Data-Driven Control via Conditional Mean Embeddings: Formal Guarantees via Uncertain MDP Abstraction
Ibon Gracia, Morteza Lahijanian

Abstract
. Controlling stochastic systems with unknown dynamics and under complex specifications is specially challenging in safety-critical settings, where performance guarantees are essential. We propose a data-driven policy synthesis framework that yields formal performance guarantees for such systems using conditional mean embeddings (CMEs) and uncertain Markov decision processes (UMDPs). From trajectory data, we learn the system’s transition kernel as a CME, then construct a finite-state UMDP abstraction whose transition uncertainties capture learning and discretization errors. Next, we generate a policy with formal performance bounds through robust dynamic programming. We demonstrate and empirically validate our method through a temperature regulation benchmark.



1.
Introduction

As autonomous dynamical systems continue to be deployed in real-world environments, providing guarantees on their performance becomes paramount. These systems are typically subject to uncertainty, e.g., random external disturbances, and accurately modeling their dynamics is often infeasible due to complex physics and environmental factors.
Consequently,
data-driven control
, which seeks to synthesize controllers (policies) using trajectory data to compensate for the lack of accurate models, has emerged as an essential research direction. However, ensuring that such policies satisfy
safety
requirements remains a major challenge.
This work aims to address this challenge by developing an end-to-end formal framework for data-driven control synthesis with complex requirements that provides rigorous guarantees on system requirements.

Kernel methods are powerful tools for data-driven decision-making. They are especially attractive for safety-critical settings because they can quantify the gap between learned and true models, allowing designers to provide formal statements about the safety of unknown dynamical systems.
A popular class of such methods is
Gaussian process
(GP) regression, which is employed by
many recent works, e.g.,
(
23
;
22
;
20
)
, to certify systems with unknown vector fields and additive noise.
Another important class is
conditional mean embeddings
(CMEs), which learn the transition kernel of an unknown stochastic system by learning its embedding into a
reproducing kernel Hilbert space
(RKHS).
Understanding the convergence of the empirical CME to the true one is an active area of research
(
15
;
11
;
24
)
.
Specifically,
(
15
)
shows that the empirical CME converges to the true one at a rate
\mathcal{O}(\log(N)/N)
), with
N
being the number of samples.
Unfortunately, existing convergence results are either asymptotic
(
18
)
, rely on quantities that are hard to bound
(
15
;
24
)
, or assume finite state spaces
(
11
)
.

Existing control-synthesis methods based on CMEs generally lack formal guarantees. Specifically,
(
21
;
25
)
approximate value functions via kernel regression. That approach also face fundamental limitations: when specifications involve reachability or temporal logic, the initial value function is an indicator function, which cannot be uniformly approximated by functions in an RKHS induced by a continuous kernel.
Other works
(
17
)
consider distributionally robust optimization using ambiguity sets derived from samples. To ensure soundness,
(
17
)
employs a
maximum mean discrepancy
(MMD) ambiguity set and a
conditional-value-at-risk
relaxation. It however introduces irreducible conservatism.

A CME-based work that provides rigorous guarantees for unknown systems is
(
22
)
. It uses data to construct a CME and verifies safety via barrier certificates by ensuring that the true CME lies within an ambiguity set and by bounding approximation error through a GP surrogate.
However, it is limited to safety verification and does not extend to control synthesis under complex objectives.

A common method to specify complex system requirements is through
linear temporal logic
(LTL)
(
1
)
and its variants,
and significant success has been achieved in synthesizing policies for such specifications using
finite-abstraction
techniques for stochastic systems with complex dynamics
(
8
)
, uncertain dynamics
(
23
)
, and unknown disturbance
(
16
;
7
;
9
)
.
These abstractions are typically variants of
Markov Decision Processes
(MDPs), such as interval MDPs
(
13
)
and
uncertain MDPs
(UMDPs)
(
12
)
.
In kernel methods, IMDP abstractions have been used for GP regressed models
(
23
;
22
;
20
)
, but
no formal approach exists for abstracting CMEs.

In this work, we present a novel data-driven control (policy) synthesis framework based on CMEs and UMDP abstraction with formal guarantees. We first learn the empirical CME of the system’s transition kernel and then abstract it into a finite UMDP, quantifying the learning gap. Then, we compute a policy on the UMDP through
robust dynamic programming
(RDP), yielding performance guarantees for the underlying (unknown) system. Our contributions are
4
-fold:
(i) the
first
data-driven control synthesis framework via CMEs that provides formal guarantees,
(ii) a novel method of UMDP abstraction of a CME model,
(iii) a concentration inequality for the empirical CME with explicit constants,
and
(iv) relaxation of the common assumption that samples must be obtained at prescribed points, instead allowing sampling from an arbitrary distribution.

4.
Preliminaries

4.1.
Conditional Mean Embeddings

In this section we give a brief overview of the relevant kernel learning topics. We show how to embed the transition kernel
\mathcal{T}
into a Hilbert space and how to empirically estimate this embedding from data, ensuring “closeness” to the true embedding. This allows us to learn a sound model of the unknown
\mathcal{T}
for policy synthesis.

Let
k:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

be a measurable, bounded, and symmetric function that satisfies the positive semidefinite condition, i.e., for all
x_{1},\dots,x_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{l}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\sum_{i,j=1}^{l}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k(x_{i},x_{j})\geq 0
. Then, we say that
k
is a positive semidefinite (PSD) kernel, and it gives rise to a
reproducing Kernel Hilbert space
(RKHS)
(
2
)
:

\mathcal{H}_{k}:=\overline{\mathrm{span}}\{k(\cdot,x):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\mid x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\},

i.e., the closure of the span of the vectors of the form
k(\cdot,x)
. The RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{k}
has inner product
\langle k(\cdot,x),k(\cdot,x^{\prime})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=k(x,x^{\prime})
which extends to all of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
via linearity and completion. The RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{k}
exhibits a reproducing property,
according to which function evaluation is equivalent to inner product in the RKHS, and therefore a linear and continuous functional, i.e., for all
h\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it holds that
h(x)=\langle h,k(\cdot,x^{\prime})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
. The RKHS inner product induces the norm
\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}:=\sqrt{\langle h,h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}}
.

While RKHSs are often used as a framework for learning a function as an element of the RKHS, we need a
vector-valued RKHS
(vRKHS)
(
5
)
to learn a transition kernel. Being a common choice in the literature, we consider the operator-valued kernel
K
such that
K(x_{1},x_{2}):=k(x_{1},x_{2})\mathrm{Id}
, where
\mathrm{Id}
is the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
. Nevertheless, we highlight that other choices are also possible. Given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
h\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
,
K(\cdot,x)h
defines a function from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\mathcal{H}_{k}
. The operator-valued kernel
k
defines the vRKHS

\mathcal{H}_{K}:=\overline{\mathrm{span}}\{K(\cdot,x)h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{k}\mid x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},h\in\mathcal{H}_{k}\}

equipped with the inner product

\langle K(\cdot,x_{1})h_{1},K(\cdot,x_{2})h_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}:=\langle h_{1},K(x_{1},x_{2})h_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
, which extends to all of
\mathcal{H}_{K}
via linearity and completion, and with norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}:=\sqrt{\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}}
. Similarly to
\mathcal{H}_{k}
,
\mathcal{H}_{K}
exhibits the following reproducing property: for all
H\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
,
h\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
, and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it holds that
\langle H(x),h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\langle H,K(\cdot,x)h\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}
.

Leveraging the theory on RKHSs and vRKHSs we can embed the transition kernel
\mathcal{T}
into the RKHS
\mathcal{H}_{K}
in order to learn the embedding from the system’s trajectory data. We denote by
\Psi:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{k}
the
kernel mean embedding
(ME) operator, which maps every
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to an RKHS function
\Psi(p):=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}[k(\cdot,x)]\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
. Let

\mathrm{MMD}(P,P^{\prime}):=\|\Psi(P)-\Psi(P^{\prime})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
denote the
maximum mean discrepancy
(MMD) between two probability distributions
P,P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, which metrizes the weak topology of probability measures if
\Psi
is injective
2
2

2

This is the case for a wide variety of kernels, including the Gaussian one.
. We also define the conditional mean embedding (CME) of
\mathcal{T}
under
u\in U
as the function
\mu_{u}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{k}
with

\displaystyle\mu_{u}(x)(\cdot):=\Psi(\mathcal{T}(\cdot\mid x,u))=\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{x}_{+}\sim\mathcal{T}(\cdot\mid x,u)}[k(\cdot,\boldsymbol{x}_{+})]\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

For
P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
u\in U
, let

4.2.
Uncertain MDPs

We use UMDP as an abstraction of the stochastic process.

Definition 4.1 (UMDP).

A UMDP is a tuple

\mathcal{U}:=(S,A,\Gamma)

in which
S
and
A

are respectively finite set of states and actions, and
\Gamma:=\{\Gamma_{s,a}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(S):s\in S,a\in A\}
, where
\Gamma_{s,a}
is the set of transition probability distributions, or
ambiguity set
, from state
s
under
a
.to the states in
S
.

A finite
path
of UMDP
\mathcal{U}
is a sequence
\omega=s_{0}\xrightarrow{a_{0}}s_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}\ldots\xrightarrow{a_{T-1}}s_{T}

of states
s_{t}\in S
and actions
a_{t}\in A
such that there
exists a distribution
\gamma\in\Gamma_{s_{t},a_{t}}
with
\gamma(s_{t+1})>0
for all
t\in\{0,\dots,T-1\}
. We denote by
\Omega
the set of all finite paths. Given path
\omega\in\Omega
,
\omega(t)=s_{t}
is the state of
\omega
at time
t
, and we denote its last state by
\mathrm{last}(\omega)
. A
strategy
of a UMDP
\mathcal{U}
is a function
\pi_{s}:\Omega\rightarrow A
that maps each finite path to the next action. We denote by
\Pi_{s}

the set of all strategies of
\mathcal{U}
.
Given path
\omega\in\Omega
and
\pi_{s}\in\Pi_{s}
, the process evolves from
s_{t}=\mathrm{last}(\omega)
under
a_{t}=\pi_{s}(\omega)
to the next state according to a probability distribution in
\Gamma_{s_{t},a_{t}}
. An adversary is a function that chooses this distribution
(
6
)
. Formally, an
adversary
is a function
\xi:S\times A\times\big(\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\big)\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(S)
that maps each state
s_{t}
, action
a_{t}
, and time step
t\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
to a transition probability distribution
\gamma\in\Gamma_{s_{t},a_{t}}
, according to which
s_{t+1}
is distributed. We denote the set of all adversaries by
\Xi
. Given an initial condition
s_{0}\in S
, a strategy
\pi_{s}\in\Pi_{s}
and an adversary
\xi\in\Xi
, the UMDP collapses to a Markov chain
with a unique probability distribution
Pr_{s_{0}}^{\pi_{s},\xi}
over its paths.

be

-Lipschitz in its first argument,
k
be the Gaussian kernel. Let
\widetilde{X}
be a discretization of
X
, i.e., a set of
M:=|\widetilde{X}|
points
\tilde{x}\in X
, with parameter
\eta
. Let the dataset in Problem
1
be of the form
\mathcal{D}:=\{(\tilde{x}_{j},\boldsymbol{x}_{i,j}^{\prime})\}_{i,j=1}^{N,M}
, namely, where the successor of each
\tilde{x}\in\widetilde{X}
is sampled
N
times by starting at
\tilde{x}
. Pick
\delta\in(0,1)
and let

\varepsilon:=(\sigma_{f}/\sigma_{l})\Big(L\eta+\sqrt{1/N}+\sqrt{2\log(M/\delta)/N}\Big).

Then, with probability great than or equal to
1-\delta
,

\mathrm{MMD}(\mathcal{T}(\cdot\mid x),\mathcal{T}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x}))\leq\varepsilon\;\quad\forall x\in X,\forall\tilde{x}\in\widetilde{X}\;\text{ s.t. }\;\|x-\tilde{x}\|\leq\eta.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{x}\in\widetilde{X}
and
x\in X
such that
\|x-\tilde{x}\|\leq\eta
, and let
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x})
denote the empirical distribution corresponding to the samples
\{(\tilde{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}_{i,j})\}_{j=1}^{N}
, with
\tilde{x}_{i}=\tilde{x}
. By
(
10
;
17
)
,
\mathrm{MMD}(\mathcal{T}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x}),\widehat{\mathcal{T}}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x}))\leq\sigma_{f}/\sigma_{l}\big(1/\sqrt{N}+\sqrt{2\log(M/\delta)/N}\big)
, with confidence not smaller than
1-\delta/M
. By Lemma
5.1
,
\mathrm{MMD}(\mathcal{T}(\cdot\mid x),\mathcal{T}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x}))\leq(\sigma_{f}/\sigma_{l})L\eta
for all
x\in X
s.t.
\|x-\tilde{x}\|\leq\eta
. The triangle inequality yields
\mathrm{MMD}(\mathcal{T}(\cdot\mid x)),\widehat{\mathcal{T}}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x})))\leq\mathrm{MMD}(\mathcal{T}(\cdot\mid x),\mathcal{T}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x}))+\mathrm{MMD}(\mathcal{T}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x}),\widehat{\mathcal{T}}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x}))\leq(\sigma_{f}/\sigma_{l})L\eta+\epsilon
. By picking
\varepsilon=(\sigma_{f}/\sigma_{l})L\eta+\epsilon
, this result implies that for all
\tilde{x}\in\widetilde{X}
and
x\in X
with
\|x-\tilde{x}\|\leq\eta
,
\mathrm{MMD}(\mathcal{T}(\cdot\mid x)),\widehat{\mathcal{T}}(\cdot\mid\tilde{x})))\leq\varepsilon
holds with confidence
\geq 1-\delta/M
. Taking the union bound, the statement is extended to all
x\in X
with an overall confidence
\geq 1-\delta
.
∎

We highlight that, even though this bound is slightly different from the one in Assumption
1
, it is sufficient to obtain a sound abstraction. Additionally, while this bound might be conservative and requires the strict assumption that the samples are prompted at deterministic points
\tilde{x}
, it provides intuition on what kind of dynamics satisfy Assumption
1
and it gives an explicit bound.

5.2.
Uncertain MDP Abstraction

Here, we rely on the empirical CME and Assumption
1

to construct a sound finite UMDP abstraction.
This construction is not limited to the explicit bounds derived in Section
5.1
; it generalizes to any system that satisfies Assumption
1
.

Our abstraction is based on a
\varphi_{x}
-conservative partition of set
X
.

Definition 5.3 (
\varphi_{x}
-Conservative Partition).

A finite partition
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{|S|-1},s_{\text{avoid}}\}
of
X
is
\varphi_{x}
-conservative if
(i)
\cup_{s\in S_{\mathrm{safe}}}s\subseteq X_{\mathrm{safe}}
, where
S_{\mathrm{safe}}:=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{|C|-1}\}
,
(ii)
s_{\text{avoid}}\supseteq X_{\text{avoid}}
and
(iii) there exists a maximal subset
S_{\text{reach}}\subseteq S_{\mathrm{safe}}
s.t.
\cup_{s\in S_{\text{reach}}}s\subseteq X_{\text{reach}}
.

Definition 5.4 (UMDP Abstraction).

A UMDP abstraction of System (
1
)
is
\mathcal{U}:=(S,A,\Gamma)
, where

S
is a
\varphi_{x}
-conservative partition of set
X
,

A:=U
, and
\Gamma:=\{\Gamma_{s,a}\mid s\in S,a\in A\}
such that

(5)

\Gamma_{s,a}:=\big\{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(S)\mid\big\|\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\gamma(s^{\prime})k(\cdot,c_{s^{\prime}})-\hat{\mu}_{a}(c_{s})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq\varepsilon\big\},

where
c_{s}\in X
denotes the center point of the region
s
and
\varepsilon:=\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}+\varepsilon_{3}
with
\varepsilon_{1}>0
,

(6a)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{2}

\displaystyle:=\max\big\{\|\hat{\mu}_{a}(x)-\hat{\mu}_{a}(c_{s})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\mid x\in s,s\in S\big\},

(6b)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{3}

\displaystyle:=\max\big\{\sqrt{k(c_{s},c_{s})+k(y,y)-k(c_{s},y)}\mid y\in s,s\in S\big\}.

In the next subsection we describe an exact procedure to obtain
\varepsilon_{2}
and also analyze the tightness of
\varepsilon_{3}
.

5.2.1.
Computation and Analysis of
\varepsilon_{2}
and
\varepsilon_{3}

First, we show how to compute
\varepsilon_{2}
in
Equation (
6a
) exactly.

Proposition 5.5.

Let
\mathcal{U}=(S,A,\Gamma)
be an abstraction of System (
1
) per Definition
5.4
and
k
be the Gaussian kernel. Then,

(7)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{2}=\max_{x\in s,s\in S}\sqrt{(\beta(x)-\beta(c_{s}))^{T}K^{(3)}(\beta(x)-\beta(c_{s}))},

where
K^{(3)}_{i,j}:=k(\hat{x}_{+}^{(i)},\hat{x}_{+}^{(j)})
, and the objective function
in (
7
) is
\|\hat{\mu}_{a}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}\sigma_{f}/\sigma_{l}
-Lipschitz.

Proof.

By the definition of the empirical CME in (
3
),
\|\hat{\mu}_{a}(x)-\hat{\mu}_{a}(c_{s})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\|\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\beta_{i}(x)-\beta_{i}(c_{s}))k(\cdot,\hat{x}_{+}^{(i)})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
, which yields the objective in Problem (
7
), that we denote by
g_{s}(x)
. Next, we obtain the Lipschitz constant of
g_{s}
. Note that
\|\hat{\mu}_{a}(x)-\hat{\mu}_{a}(c_{s})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\sup_{\|v\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq 1}\langle\hat{\mu}_{a}(x)-\hat{\mu}_{a}(c_{s}),v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
and

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\mu}_{a}(x)-\hat{\mu}_{a}(c_{s}),v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\langle\hat{\mu}_{a}(x),v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}-\langle\hat{\mu}_{a}(c_{s}),v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{\mu}_{a},K(\cdot,x)v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}-\langle\hat{\mu}_{a},K(\cdot,c_{s})v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}=\langle\hat{\mu}_{a},K(\cdot,x)v-K(\cdot,c_{s})v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{\mu}_{a},(K(\cdot,x)-K(\cdot,c_{s}))v\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}\leq\|\hat{\mu}_{a}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}\|(K(\cdot,x)-K(\cdot,c_{s})v\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}.

Next, we leverage the
isometric isomorphism
between
\mathcal{H}_{K}
and the
tensor product space

\mathcal{H}_{k}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{k}
, where the norm of some
f\otimes g
, with
f,g\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
, is
\|f\otimes g\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{k}}:=\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
. By this isomorphism,

\displaystyle\|(K(\cdot,x)-K(\cdot,c_{s}))v\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}=\|v\otimes(k(\cdot,x)-k(\cdot,c_{s}))\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{k}}

\displaystyle=\|v\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\|k(\cdot,x)-k(\cdot,c_{s})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}

Putting these results together, we obtain

(8)

\begin{split}g_{s}(x)&\leq\|\hat{\mu}_{a}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}\sqrt{k(x,x)+k(c_{s},c_{s})-2k(x,c_{s})}\\
&\leq\|\hat{\mu}_{a}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}\sigma_{f}/\sigma_{l}\|x-c_{s}\|,\end{split}

and thus
\|g_{s}(x)-g_{s}(x^{\prime})\|\leq\|\hat{\mu}_{a}\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}\sigma_{f}/\sigma_{l}\|x-x^{\prime}\|
by the triangle inequality.
∎

Despite
g_{s}
being nonlinear in
x
, knowing its Lipschitz constant allows us to employ a
global optimization
algorithm, like the
Piyavskii–Shubert
one
(
19
)
, which obtains a tight upper bound on the the global maximum. To obtain
\epsilon_{2}
, this algorithm needs to be used
|S|
times. Alternatively, we could have bounded
\varepsilon_{2}
by plugging expression (
8
) into (
7
), which yields a convex optimization problem. However, since this bound relies on Cauchy-Schwartz’s inequality, it is more conservative than the optimal value in (
7
).

Next, we discuss the tightness of
\varepsilon_{3}
.

Remark 1.

Note that the bound
\varepsilon_{3}
is tight, as it can be attained. For example, let
\mathcal{T}(\cdot\mid x,a)=\delta_{x^{\prime}}
with
x^{\prime}
maximizing
\big(k(c_{s^{\prime}},c_{s^{\prime}})+k(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})-2k(c_{s^{\prime}},x^{\prime})\big)^{1/2}
over all
x^{\prime}\in X
. Let
s^{\prime}\ni x^{\prime}
. Then,
P_{\gamma}=\delta_{c_{s^{\prime}}}
, and the MMD distance between the two distributions is exactly
\varepsilon_{3}
, as

\|\Psi(\delta_{c_{s^{\prime}}})-\Psi(\delta_{x^{\prime}})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\sqrt{k(c_{s^{\prime}},c_{s^{\prime}})+k(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})-2k(c_{s^{\prime}},x^{\prime})}.

5.3.
Soundness of The UMDP Abstraction

We now formalize soundness of abstraction
\mathcal{U}
in Definition
5.4
, i.e., that
\mathcal{U}
contains all the behaviors of System (
1
) (w.r.t.
\varphi_{x}
).

Definition 5.6 (Soundness).

Let
\mathcal{U}:=(S,A,\Gamma)
be a UMDP abstraction of System (
1
). For each
s\in S\setminus\{s_{\mathrm{avoid}}\}
,
a\in A
, and
x\in s
, define
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(S)
such that
\gamma(s^{\prime}):=\mathcal{T}(s^{\prime}\mid x,a)
. UMDP
\mathcal{U}
is a
sound abstraction
if
(i)
\gamma\in\Gamma_{s,a}
for all
s\in S\setminus\{s_{\mathrm{avoid}}\}
and
a\in A
,
and
(ii)
\Gamma_{s_{\mathrm{avoid}},a}=\{\delta_{s_{\mathrm{avoid}}}\}
for all
a\in A
.

Theorem 5.7 (Soundness).

Consider Assumptions
1
and
2
, and let
\varepsilon_{1}
in Definition
5.4
be equal to
\varepsilon
in Assumption
1
. Then, the UMDP abstraction
\mathcal{U}:=(S,A,\Gamma)
constructed per Definition.
5.4

is sound.

Proof.

First we pick
s\in S
,
a\in A
,
x\in s
and
s^{\prime}\in S\setminus\{s_{\mathrm{avoid}}\}
, and define
P_{\gamma}:=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\gamma(s^{\prime})\delta_{c_{s^{\prime}}}
. Then, note that
\gamma\in\Gamma_{s,a}
holds if and only if
\|\Psi(P_{\gamma})-\hat{\mu}_{a}(c_{s})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq\varepsilon
. We now show this is the case by upper bounding
\|\Psi(P_{\gamma})-\hat{\mu}_{a}(c_{s})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
by
\|\Psi(P_{\gamma})-\Psi(\mathcal{T}(\cdot\mid x,a))\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}+\|\Psi(\mathcal{T}(\cdot\mid x,a))-\hat{\mu}_{a}(x)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}+\|\hat{\mu}_{a}(x)-\hat{\mu}_{a}(c_{s})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}
via the triangle inequality. By definition of the ME operator and Assumption
1
, the second term is bounded by
\|\Psi(\mathcal{T}(\cdot\mid x,a))-\hat{\mu}_{a}(x)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\|\mu_{a}(x^{\prime})-\hat{\mu}_{a}(x)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq\varepsilon_{1}
. Similarly, since
x\in s
, we obtain that the third term is upper bounded by
\varepsilon_{2}(s)
. Finally, we show that
\|\Psi(P_{\gamma})-\Psi(\mathcal{T}(\cdot\mid x,a))\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq\varepsilon_{3}
. Note that, by definition of the ME map and linearity of the Bochner integral,

\displaystyle\|\Psi

\displaystyle(P_{\gamma})-\Psi(\mathcal{T}(\cdot\mid x,a))\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\int_{X}k(\cdot,x^{\prime})P_{\gamma}(dx^{\prime})-\int_{X}k(\cdot,x^{\prime})\mathcal{T}(dx^{\prime}\mid x,a)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}k(\cdot,c_{s^{\prime}})\gamma(s^{\prime})-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\int_{s^{\prime}}k(\cdot,x^{\prime})\mathcal{T}(dx^{\prime}\mid x,a)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}k(\cdot,c_{s^{\prime}})\int_{s^{\prime}}\mathcal{T}(dx^{\prime}\mid x,a)-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\int_{s^{\prime}}k(\cdot,x^{\prime})\mathcal{T}(dx^{\prime}\mid x,a)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}

\displaystyle\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\int_{s^{\prime}}\|k(\cdot,c_{s^{\prime}})-k(\cdot,x^{\prime})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\mathcal{T}(dx^{\prime}\mid x,a)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\mathcal{T}(s^{\prime}\mid x,a)\max\big\{\|k(\cdot,c_{s^{\prime}})-k(\cdot,x^{\prime})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\mid x^{\prime}\in s^{\prime}\big\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max\big\{\sqrt{k(c_{s^{\prime}},c_{s^{\prime}})+k(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})-2k(c_{s^{\prime}},x^{\prime})}\mid x^{\prime}\in s^{\prime},s^{\prime}\in S\big\}=\varepsilon_{3},

where
(a)
follows from the triangle and Jensen’s inequalities.
∎

6.
Control Synthesis

Here, we focus on generating a strategy over the sound abstract
\mathcal{U}
that is optimally robust w.r.t. all uncertainties in the abstraction. We then refine it to a policy for System (
1

\varphi_{x}

6.1.
Robust Dynamic Programming (RDP)

Consider UMDP
\mathcal{U}
, a reach-avoid specification
\varphi=(S_{\text{reach}},\{s_{\text{avoid}}\})
with
S_{\text{reach}},\{s_{\text{avoid}}\}\subseteq S
, a strategy
\pi_{s}\in\Pi_{s}
, and an adversary
\xi\in\Xi
. With a slight abuse of notation, we denote by
\text{Pr}_{s}^{\pi_{s},\xi}[\varphi]
the probability that the paths of
\mathcal{U}
starting at
s\in S
satisfy
\varphi
under
\pi_{s}
and
\xi
, which is defined analogously to (
2
).

Proposition 6.1 (RDP
(
7
, Theorem 6.2)
).

Given a UMDP
\mathcal{U}=(S,A,\Gamma)
, a reach-avoid specification
\varphi=(S_{\text{reach}},\{s_{\text{avoid}}\})
, and
s\in S
,
define the optimal robust reach-avoid probability of
\mathcal{U}
as

\underline{p}(s):=\sup_{\pi_{s}\in\Pi_{s}}\inf_{\xi\in\Xi}\text{Pr}_{s}^{\pi_{s},\xi}[\varphi]
. Consider also the recursion

(9)

\displaystyle\underline{p}^{t+1}(s)=\begin{cases}\max\limits_{a\in A}\min\limits_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{s,a}}\sum\limits_{s^{\prime}\in S}\gamma(s^{\prime})\underline{p}^{t}(s^{\prime})&\text{if }s\in S\setminus S_{\text{reach}}\\
1&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

where
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, with initial condition
\underline{p}^{0}(s)=1
if
s\in S_{\text{reach}}
otherwise
0
. Then,
\underline{p}^{t}
converges to
\underline{p}
.

The most computationally intensive part when iterating on (
9
) is solving the inner minimization problems over the sets
\Gamma_{s,a}
. The following theorem shows that these minimizations are quadratically-constrained linear programs (QCLPs), which can be solved using off-the-shelf convex solvers.

Theorem 6.2.

For every
s\in S\setminus\{s_{\mathrm{unsafe}}\}
and
a\in A
, the inner minimization problem over the ambiguity set
\Gamma_{s,a}
in (
9
) is equivalent to the finite-dimensional convex program

(10a)

\displaystyle\min_{\gamma\geq 0}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{|S|}\gamma_{i}\underline{p}^{k}(s_{i})

(10b)

s.t.

\displaystyle\gamma^{T}K_{1}\gamma-2\gamma^{T}K_{2}\beta+\beta^{T}K_{3}\beta\leq\varepsilon^{2},\sum_{i=1}^{|S|}\gamma_{i}=1

with
K_{i,j}^{(1)}=k(c_{s_{i}},c_{s_{j}})\>\forall i,j\in\{1,\dots,|S|\}
,
K_{i,j}^{(2)}=k(c_{s_{i}},\hat{x}_{j})\>\forall i\in\{1,\dots,|S|\},j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and
K_{i,j}^{(3)}=k(\hat{x}_{i},\hat{x}_{j})\>\forall i,j\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.

Proof.

Note that the simplex constraints, i.e., non-negativity of
\gamma
and
\sum_{i=1}^{|S|}\gamma_{i}=1
are equivalent to
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(S)
in (
5
). Furthermore, pick an arbitrary
\gamma\in\Gamma_{s,a}
. Then,
\|\sum_{s^{\prime}\in S}\gamma(s^{\prime})k(\cdot,c_{s^{\prime}})-\hat{\mu}(c_{s})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\leq\varepsilon
, which is
equivalent to the quadratic constraint in (
10
). Finally, since
K^{(1)}
comes from a positive semi-definite kernel is itself positive semi-definite we conclude that the constraint is convex.
∎

Obtaining optimal strategy and policy from RDP.

Based on the RDP in (

(

by iterating on the recursion in
(

9

\pi_{s}^{*}
and the
\min

8.
Conclusion

We presented a framework for formal data-driven control of stochastic systems under complex specifications, via CMEs and UMDP abstractions. Our approach is the first one to obtain a sound finite abstraction of a CME. We empirically validated our method through a temperature regulation benchmark, obtaining better results than state-of-the-art CME-reliant approaches. A promising future research direction involves leveraging deep kernels to increase tightness of the abstraction to yield better results with finer discretizations.
