Analytic Misjudgment of Drug Safety Evidence and Causality: From the Prosecutor’s Fallacy and Simpson’s Paradox to Artificial Intelligence
Authors: Tarek A. Hammad, Justine Rochon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Drug safety assessment, particularly in the post-marketing setting, is especially vulnerable to analytic misjudgment because it relies on heterogeneous evidence streams, incomplete data, infrequent events, and decisions made under substantial uncertainty. Recurring sources of error include misinterpretation of conditional probabilities, conflation of association with causation, inappropriate denominator and comparator selection, inadequate consideration of background incidence and confounding, aggregation artifacts such as Simpson’s paradox, and overinterpretation of exploratory findings arising from multiplicity or repeated testing. Misjudgment may be further amplified by spontaneous reporting data that lack explicit exposure denominators and are susceptible to reporting bias, by fragile or incomplete meta-analyses, and by premature regulatory or public responses to weak or incompletely contextualized signals. Using selected real-world case studies and conceptual examples, this narrative review illustrates how such errors arise and propagate across clinical, regulatory, and public domains, and how they can materially influence causality assessment and decision making. The paper also discusses how artificial intelligence (AI), if implemented without transparency, bias assessment, and clinical oversight, may amplify rather than reduce these vulnerabilities. Greater analytic discipline, clearer communication of uncertainty, triangulation across evidence streams, and careful governance of emerging AI-enabled tools are needed to support more reliable drug safety evaluation.

Key Points

Drug safety decisions can be distorted when early evidence is misread, taken out of context, or treated as stronger than it really is. This paper shows how errors in interpreting probabilities, study comparisons, spontaneous reports, and pooled evidence can lead to flawed conclusions, unnecessary concern, or delayed recognition of true risk. It argues that stronger analytic discipline, clearer communication of uncertainty, and careful use of emerging tools such as artificial intelligence are essential for more reliable causality assessment and better drug safety decisions.

Introduction

Isaacs and Fitzgerald satirically described in their paper ‘Seven Alternatives to Evidence-Based Medicine’, including approaches driven by eminence, vehemence, or confidence, to show how, in the absence of disciplined appraisal, clinical decisions may be driven by authority, rhetoric, fear, or over-confidence rather than systematic integration of evidence [1]. Although written humorously, their framework underscores a serious point: without explicit methodological safeguards, the interpretation of evidence can be distorted by cognitive, social, and institutional pressures.

Errors in analytic reasoning and evidence interpretation can have profound consequences in high-stakes settings. Lucia de Berk, a Dutch nurse, was wrongfully convicted on the basis of deeply flawed analytic reasoning. Prosecutors argued that the probability of her presence during multiple patient deaths was 1 in 342 million and treated that figure as near-certainty of guilt [2]. This reasoning error, known as the Prosecutor’s Fallacy [3], ignored the broader context of background mortality in intensive care units, clustering in high-risk clinical settings, selective classification of events as suspicious, reporting and confirmation bias, and the number of nurses working similar shifts. De Berk was sentenced to life imprisonment and fully exonerated several years later in 2010, highlighting the potentially devastating consequences of analytic misjudgment.

Drug safety decision making, particularly in the post-marketing signal assessment, operates under similarly high stakes and depends on the systematic generation and interpretation of data across multiple evidence streams. Data and analytics are therefore central to pharmacovigilance (PV), underpinning signal detection, causality assessment, and regulatory decision making [4]. At the same time, this setting is especially vulnerable to analytic misjudgment because it relies heavily on incomplete data, heterogeneous evidence sources, and methodologically complex assessments conducted under uncertainty. Regulatory urgency, public health crises, and media amplification can further heighten susceptibility to error by compressing the time available for thorough evaluation. As in other high-stakes settings, misjudging causal inference can lead to flawed conclusions with real-world consequences, particularly when analytic outputs are treated as definitive rather than as inputs that still require contextual clinical and scientific judgment.

This paper is a narrative, selective synthesis rather than a systematic review of all analytic pitfalls relevant to drug safety. The cases and examples were chosen to illustrate recurrent forms of analytic misjudgment that are especially pertinent to drug safety decision making. The goal is not to provide a comprehensive catalogue, but to highlight representative mechanisms by which analytic misjudgment can distort causality assessment and subsequent decisions. The sections that follow illustrate how these vulnerabilities arise and why rigorous contextualization is essential to sound drug safety decision making.

Key Sources and Manifestations of Analytic Misjudgment in Drug Safety

We define analytic misjudgment as errors in reasoning that arise from the misinterpretation or misapplication of probability, statistical findings, methodological assumptions, or data used to assess drug risk, leading to flawed conclusions. These errors can result in premature actions, misperceptions of drug risks, unwarranted product withdrawals, misplaced public concern, distorted real-world utilization patterns, or misallocation of healthcare resources. Throughout this paper, we distinguish among three related but non-identical problems: (A) formal probabilistic fallacies, (B) broader analytic reasoning errors arising from flawed design or interpretation, and (C) communication failures that amplify misinterpretation of uncertain evidence. Some examples discussed below are direct illustrations of formal probabilistic error, whereas others are presented as conceptual analogues that illuminate similar mechanisms of misinterpretation in drug safety.

The Prosecutor’s Fallacy: Misreading Conditional Probabilities

The Prosecutor’s Fallacy classically arises in legal settings when the probability of the evidence given guilt, P(Evidence | Guilt), is wrongly assumed to be equal to the probability of guilt given the evidence, P(Guilt | Evidence). Analogous misinterpretations can arise in drug safety when the probability of an adverse event (AE) given drug exposure, P(AE | Drug Exposure), is confused with the probability of drug exposure given an AE, P(Drug Exposure | AE). In this setting, the inversion error consists of interpreting the proportion of AE cases exposed to the drug as evidence of risk, rather than estimating the incidence of AEs among exposed patients. Figure 1 illustrates a theoretical example of the Prosecutor’s Fallacy [5] in a population of 100, in which 25 individuals were exposed to the drug and 9 experienced an AE, 4 of whom took both the drug and experienced an AE. In this example, P(Drug Exposure | AE) is 4/9, or 44%, whereas P(AE | Drug Exposure) is 4/25, or 16%. Treating the 44% as if it were the risk of the AE among exposed patients would therefore misstate the safety question and could lead to incorrect causal interpretation. This error may be further compounded by differential observability, if AEs were less likely to be detected, attributed, or reported among unexposed patients, inflating the apparent association.Fig. 1The prosecutor’s fallacy in drug safety visualized. A similar inversion error can arise in PV when regulatory outcomes are used as the conditioning event. In a frequently cited example that can invite a Prosecutor Fallacy-type misinterpretation, US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) authors conducted a retrospective review of safety-related labeling updates for 22 new molecular entities approved in 2008 and catalogued 189 newly identified safety issues added to labeling over the subsequent 10 years. They reported that the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) was the most frequent triggering source in the first year after approval, accounting for 69% of issues in Year 1 and approximately 15% overall [6].

This result is sometimes interpreted as evidence of the high value of spontaneous reporting. However, the reported percentage corresponds to the conditional probability P(FAERS involvement | labeling change), that is, the proportion of labeling updates in which FAERS contributed as a triggering source. The safety question of interest is different: it concerns P(labeling change | FAERS signal), namely the probability that a signal identified in FAERS ultimately reflects a true safety issue warranting regulatory action. Substituting the former for the latter constitutes an inversion error analogous to the Prosecutor’s Fallacy and can lead to overestimation of the evidentiary value of spontaneous reports. To estimate P(labeling change | FAERS signal), one would need the full denominator of signals detected in FAERS over the same period, along with their subsequent classification as substantiated, refuted, or indeterminate, and comparison with other signal sources while accounting for reporting bias, notoriety bias, and differential follow-up. Without this information, citing the proportion of labeling changes that involved FAERS overstates what these results can support about predictive performance or causal reliability. The correct interpretation is narrower: among the subset of safety issues that ultimately resulted in labeling updates in this specific cohort, FAERS was one of several data streams contributing to their identification or prioritization.

An additional pertinent nuance is that some labeling actions are precautionary under uncertainty [7] rather than the endpoint of definitive causal proof reflecting a regulatory posture that prioritizes patient protection even when evidence is incomplete or evolving, and where delaying action until full certainty is achieved may itself carry risk. Many decisions about labeling changes therefore represent risk management judgments under uncertainty rather than confirmation of causality and may be revised as new evidence accumulates. In parallel, labeling changes often reflect convergence across multiple evidence streams rather than confirmation from a single source [8]. As a result, the presence of a particular data source in a labeling decision should not be interpreted as establishing its independent predictive value or causal reliability, but rather as part of a broader, integrative evidentiary process.

Case Studies

A real-world example involves the early safety concerns surrounding COVID-19 mRNA vaccines and myocarditis. Initial reports of myocarditis following vaccination led to widespread alarm, particularly among younger populations. Because vaccine uptake and media coverage were both high, a considerable proportion of reported myocarditis cases occurred in vaccinated individuals, which may have inflated the perceived vaccine-associated risk even though some cases would have occurred by chance in vaccinated people. More rigorous studies such as that by Barda and colleagues [9] clarified that although there was a small increased risk of myocarditis following vaccination, estimated at approximately 2.7 excess cases per 100,000 persons, the risk was substantially higher following actual SARS-CoV-2 infection, which was associated with 11.0 additional cases per 100,000 persons. A similar pattern was observed with the rotavirus vaccine and intussusception. Early clustering suggested a possible causal relationship. Later, a large multinational study by Patel and colleagues [10] confirmed a small increased risk, approximately 1 excess case per 51,000 to 68,000 infants vaccinated but also demonstrated that vaccination prevented more than 80,000 hospitalizations and 1300 deaths annually.

Although neither case is a textbook example of the Prosecutor’s Fallacy, both illustrate conceptually related inferential errors. In each instance, particularly in public discourse and early reactions, temporal or statistical associations were implicitly treated as evidence of causality without adequate consideration of the likelihood of observing the reported pattern in the absence of a causal effect. At a minimum, this reflects a tendency to conflate the probability of observing a pattern under the null with the probability that the drug caused the event.

Spontaneous Reporting and Disproportionality: When Numerators Masquerade as Risk

Spontaneous AE reports submitted to regulatory agencies, such as the US FDA through FAERS or the World Health Organization’s VigiBase, are a cornerstone of post-marketing PV and play a critical role in early detection of potential safety signals. These reports are commonly used to identify signals of disproportionate reporting (SDRs), which are statistical signals generated by comparing how often a specific drug-AE pair appears in a reporting system relative to all other drug-event combinations. However, data from these systems are frequently misinterpreted, particularly when raw AE counts are taken at face value and then propagated into downstream analyses, a practice that some experts consider a threat to the principles of evidence-based medicine [11]. Drugs with large exposure may generate high numbers of AE reports simply because they are widely used, whereas infrequently prescribed products may appear comparatively safer because they generate fewer reports, despite a higher incidence among exposed individuals.

Reporting may also be amplified by regulatory actions, media attention, or litigation, leading to differential stimulated or notoriety-driven reporting that reflects heightened awareness of AEs rather than a true increase in risk [12, 13]. In these settings, elevated raw report counts may inflate SDRs and reinforce spurious signals. Regulatory authorities and pharmaceutical companies therefore rely on broader signal evaluation frameworks that incorporate biological plausibility, preclinical data, clinical trials, and epidemiologic studies within structured causality assessment processes [8].

These interpretive challenges are compounded by inherent limitations of spontaneous reporting systems. In addition to reporting bias, AE datasets often contain duplicate reports and reports with incomplete clinical information. Common disproportionality metrics, such as the reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), and information component (IC), can also be sensitive to sparse data, rare events, and small-cell corrections, although the IC is often considered somewhat more stable in sparse-data settings because Bayesian shrinkage partially stabilizes the estimates [14]. Despite these limitations, many published disproportionality analyses overstate conclusions and even claim subgroup differences in risk, for example, by age and sex. Publications have identified frequent deficiencies in reporting quality and methodological transparency [11, 15], and evidence of spin and overinterpretation has also been documented in disproportionality research reporting [16, 17].

Case Studies

A notable example of a spurious safety signal involved statins and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). Disproportionality analyses of FAERS identified apparently elevated reporting of ALS-like conditions associated with statin use [18]. However, subsequent epidemiologic and meta-analytic studies found no consistent evidence of a causal association between statin use and ALS incidence [19]. In this case, stimulated reporting, diagnostic uncertainty, and high background exposure may have contributed to disproportionate reporting without establishing causality.

Similarly, early disproportionality analyses suggested an association between sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and acute kidney injury (AKI) [20]. However, when active-comparator–restricted disproportionality analyses were applied, no AKI signal was detected [21]. Other studies and clinical trial evidence showed that SGLT2 inhibitors are not associated with increased AKI risk and may in fact confer a protective effect compared with other glucose-lowering therapies [22, 23]. In this case, early findings may have been influenced by confounding by indication, intercurrent illness, baseline renal vulnerability in the treated population, and the use of unrestricted background comparators rather than active comparators.

Another example involves spontaneous reports of neuropsychiatric events associated with varenicline use for smoking cessation [24, 25], which led to the addition of a boxed warning [26]. Later, a large randomized clinical trial demonstrated no significant increase in serious neuropsychiatric AEs [27], and the boxed warning was subsequently removed [28, 29]. In this case, substantial baseline neuropsychiatric vulnerability in the smoking-cessation population, together with intense regulatory and media attention, likely amplified reporting.

Ultimately, misreading signals of disproportionate reporting is best understood as a broader inferential error with potential downstream consequences for regulatory decision making. The core mistake is to treat a reporting pattern, or even a disproportionality signal, as evidence of causal risk without adequate consideration of reporting bias, background reporting patterns, the absence of exposure denominators, comparator structure, patient mix, and other alternative explanations. The examples above illustrate how this can occur for different reasons. Collectively, these cases show that disproportionality findings may arise from the interaction of reporting behavior, patient characteristics, and analytic design rather than from true drug-specific toxicity.

Simpson’s Paradox and Aggregation-Induced Analytic Misjudgment in Drug Safety

Simpson’s Paradox refers to the phenomenon in which the direction or magnitude of an association between an exposure and an outcome observed in aggregated data changes, or even reverses, after stratification by a relevant confounding variable. This reveals that the apparent crude association was driven by inappropriate aggregation rather than by true causal effect [30].

Case Studies

A well-documented example of analytic misjudgment arising from inappropriate aggregation of heterogeneous risk groups is the controversy surrounding hormone replacement therapy (HRT) and cardiovascular risk. For years, observational studies reported that HRT use in postmenopausal women was associated with a substantial reduction in the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD) [31–33]. These findings strongly influenced clinical practice and prescribing behavior. However, the Women’s Health Initiative (WHI) later reported that HRT increased the risk of CHD, particularly during the early years of treatment [34–36]. This apparent contradiction initially undermined confidence in observational research and created uncertainty about the true cardiovascular effects of HRT. Subsequent methodological analyses clarified that the divergence between observational and randomized findings was largely attributable to differences in risk stratification and aggregation rather than to flaws inherent to observational studies. Specifically, WHI compared women at the point of treatment initiation with non-users and was therefore able to capture the elevated CHD risk that occurs in the early phase after starting HRT. In contrast, many observational studies compared prevalent users of HRT, women who had already initiated and continued therapy, with never-users. By design, these analyses excluded women who may have experienced early AEs and discontinued treatment, and therefore disproportionately reflected later phases of use among individuals who had already “survived” the early high-risk period. When observational analyses were restructured to emulate randomized experiments and align time since initiation between groups, their findings converged with those of the WHI [37, 38].

This pattern represents a Simpson’s Paradox–like reversal in drug safety, driven by inappropriate aggregation across time since treatment initiation and selection into prevalent user populations. At the aggregated level, HRT appeared cardioprotective. After appropriate stratification by age and time since treatment initiation, however, the direction of the association reversed, revealing an early excess risk that had been obscured by inappropriate pooling of heterogeneous populations. In practical terms, combining early initiators, who experience higher short-term risk, with longer-term users, who represent a selected and more stable population, produced an average effect that masked the true temporal pattern of risk. The apparent protective effect was therefore not causal, but an artifact of aggregation across strata with fundamentally different baseline risks. In drug safety, such aggregation-induced reversals can have substantial regulatory and public health implications, particularly when early observational findings are interpreted as causal without adequate stratification.

A similar phenomenon was observed for HRT and breast cancer risk. Observational studies reported higher risk estimates than those initially observed in the WHI, largely because observational cohorts included women who initiated HRT closer to menopause, whereas the WHI predominantly enrolled women several years after menopause. When WHI results were stratified by time since menopause, breast cancer risk estimates aligned more closely with previous findings [38]. Although this pattern does not constitute a textbook example of Simpson’s Paradox because it reflects convergence rather than reversal of effect direction, it nonetheless illustrates how inappropriate aggregation across biologically distinct patient strata can produce misleading comparisons and apparent contradictions across evidence sources.

Inappropriate Comparator Selection: Lessons from COX-2 Inhibitors

Analytic misjudgment in drug safety can also arise from inappropriate comparator group selection. A prominent example is the early evaluation of cardiovascular risk associated with cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) selective inhibitors, particularly rofecoxib.

The early evaluation of cardiovascular risk associated with COX-2- selective inhibitors illustrates how comparator choice initially obscured true drug-related harm. The first cardiovascular safety signal for rofecoxib emerged from the VIGOR randomized controlled trial, which compared rofecoxib with naproxen and reported a higher incidence of myocardial infarction (MI) in the rofecoxib arm [39]. At the time, this excess risk was largely attributed to a putative cardioprotective effect of naproxen rather than to a prothrombotic effect of rofecoxib, delaying recognition of an intrinsic cardiovascular hazard associated with COX-2 inhibition. Subsequent observational studies that used broader comparator frameworks and population-based designs challenged this interpretation. A study by Graham and colleagues [40] demonstrated a significantly increased risk of acute MI and sudden cardiac death among users of rofecoxib compared with non-users and with other non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), with clear dose-response and duration-response relationships. Importantly, this excess risk was observed even when naproxen users were excluded, undermining the hypothesis that the VIGOR findings were driven solely by a cardioprotective effect of naproxen. Randomized evidence subsequently converged with the observational study findings. The APPROVe trial, which compared rofecoxib with placebo, demonstrated a clear increase in thrombotic cardiovascular events with longer-term exposure, providing definitive confirmation that the risk was intrinsic to rofecoxib rather than an artifact of comparator selection [41]. Similarly, Solomon and colleagues [42] showed elevated cardiovascular risk associated with celecoxib, confirming that the signal persisted across other drugs in the class. Integrated regulatory analyses by Hammad and colleagues [43] using data from electronic medical record data further supported the genuine cardiovascular risk associated with COX-2 inhibition.

The cumulative body of evidence ultimately led to the voluntary withdrawal of rofecoxib from the market [44] and illustrated a critical lesson in drug safety analytics: comparator choice is a foundational determinant of causal inference, not a technical afterthought. Even when an active comparator is used, analytic misjudgment can arise if the comparator itself has, or is believed to have, independent effects on the outcome of interest. Additionally, from a safety perspective, an analysis framed primarily as “comparative safety” can be misleading if it obscures the absolute risk burden, because relative differences against an active comparator do not by themselves establish whether the observed event rate is clinically acceptable for the exposed population.

Challenges in Identifying Population Subgroups at Risk

Refined risk characterization in drug safety often requires identifying population subgroups with differential susceptibility based on age, sex, comorbidities, genetic predisposition, or treatment context. When subgroup-specific denominators cannot be appropriately defined, often because event counts are sparse, risk may be incorrectly assumed to be uniform across all users, leading either to dilution of true safety signals or to overgeneralization of risk [45].

Case Studies

Early evaluations of clozapine-associated agranulocytosis initially suggested a broadly uniform risk across treated patients. Subsequent research demonstrated that susceptibility is genetically mediated, involving variants in genes such as HLA-DQB1, HLA-B, and SLCO1B3/SLCO1B7, enabling more precise identification of patients at elevated risk [46]. Similarly, studies of HRT showed that cardiovascular and cancer risks, as noted earlier, varied substantially by age and time since menopause, reinforcing the need for stratified analyses [38]. A more recent illustration is myocarditis following COVID-19 vaccination. Initial population-level analyses underestimated risk among adolescents and young adults. Subsequent age-stratified analyses revealed higher baseline incidence rates in this subgroup, enabling a more accurate and balanced understanding of vaccine-associated risk [9, 47].

Errors in identifying subgroups at risk, for example by age and sex, are particularly pronounced in disproportionality analyses (DA) based on spontaneous reporting systems such as FAERS and VigiBase, adding to the challenges already mentioned with these systems. On the one hand, subgroup-agnostic conclusions may misrepresent true risk and impair effective decision making. On the other hand, subgroup stratification in DA is highly susceptible to differential reporting bias. The absence of structured subgroup-specific exposure data further limits interpretability [14].

Impact of Study Design Nuances: Oral Bisphosphonates and Esophageal Cancer

An instructive example of how analytic framework and study design influence drug safety conclusions is reflected in the studies examining oral bisphosphonate use and esophageal cancer risk. Despite addressing the same research question using similar data sources and overlapping time periods, three major studies reached materially different conclusions.

The first case-control study, published in BMJ, reported a statistically significant increase in esophageal cancer risk among long-term bisphosphonate users, particularly those exposed for more than three years (odds ratio [OR] 2.24; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.47–3.43) [48]. The study used data from the UK Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) spanning 1995 to 2005. In contrast, a retrospective cohort study, published in JAMA, using the same CPRD data from a comparable period, 1996 to 2006, found no evidence of increased esophageal cancer risk (hazard ratio [HR] 1.07; 95% CI 0.77–1.49) [49]. A third case-cohort study, extending the same CPRD data follow-up through 2009, reported a modest, borderline-significant association (risk ratio [RR] 1.3; 95% CI 1.0–1.7) and noted sensitivity to assumptions regarding exposure lag and censoring [50]. The author explicitly cautioned against interpreting small effect estimates as definitive evidence of causality.

The divergence in findings likely reflects differences in study design and analytic assumptions that can materially influence observational safety results. Potential sources of variation include how exposure was defined, how duration and latency were handled, how follow-up and censoring were specified, and how risk windows were operationalized. The fact that these studies drew on the same database over overlapping time periods yet reached different conclusions underscores the vulnerability of observational findings to design nuances and analytic choices. It also serves as a reminder that some operational details relevant to the study design and interpretation may not be fully visible to readers because of publication space limitations. Collectively, these cases show that analytic misjudgment sometimes reflects methodological choices rather than data quality, and that interpreting risk estimates without careful attention to analytic context can lead to inconsistent regulatory or clinical responses.

Regulatory Urgency, Public Communication, and the Risk of Premature Conclusions

Regulatory agencies and public health authorities often face pressure to act swiftly in response to emerging safety concerns, even when evidence is incomplete or evolving. That pressure can lead to premature action despite the uncertainty that characterizes many early safety signals. The demand for rapid decision making is especially pronounced during perceived potential for public health crises or situations involving widespread exposure, where regulators must balance precautionary action against the risk of undermining public confidence. The following examples illustrate how early regulatory communications, when disseminated publicly before the evidentiary and benefit-risk assessment landscape is sufficiently mature, can reshape real-world prescribing behavior in ways that complicate subsequent safety evaluation.

Case Studies

This dynamic was illustrated during the temporary suspension of the AstraZeneca COVID-19 vaccine following concerns about thrombosis with thrombocytopenia syndrome (TTS) [51]. Rare events temporally associated with vaccination were initially interpreted as evidence of causality rather than evaluated against background incidence and underlying risk factors [52]. Although later analyses showed that vaccine-related TTS incidence was extremely low and that the benefits outweighed the risks, the initial precautionary actions contributed to public concern and reduced uptake in some regions [53].

The experience surrounding paroxetine, one of the selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), provides another example of how premature public exposure of an incompletely characterized safety concern, amplified by regulatory actions and media coverage [54], can lead to unintended prescribing shifts and channeling. Following the June 2003 FDA Paroxetine Public Health Advisory (PPHA) about suicidality risk, substantial changes in antidepressant prescribing were observed [55]. Pamer and colleagues demonstrated that paroxetine use declined across age groups immediately after the PPHA, whereas use of non-paroxetine SSRIs increased over the same period, particularly among children and adolescents. This pattern is consistent with channeling away from the named product toward alternatives in the same drug class perceived as safer. Importantly, these shifts occurred in the absence of evidence demonstrating differential risk among SSRIs at that time. Later analyses showed that suicidality risk applied broadly across antidepressants rather than being specific to paroxetine [56, 57]. Aligning timing, uncertainty framing, and evidentiary strength is therefore critical to minimizing unintended channeling and preserving interpretability of drug safety data.

Mainstream and social media amplification can further distort risk [58, 59]. The controversy surrounding isotretinoin (Accutane) and reports of depression and suicidality illustrates how case reports and sensationalized narratives can outpace more rigorous evidence [59]. When isolated reports are presented as definitive proof of harm without appropriate context, public perception and decision making may be misled. Large-scale studies have failed to establish a clear causal relationship [60, 61], and evidence suggests that acne itself may contribute to mental health risk [62, 63].

The Role of Multiple Comparisons and Repeated Testing

Multiple comparisons and repeated testing increase the likelihood of encountering false-positive safety signals and represent another driver of analytic misjudgment. This risk is particularly pronounced when numerous potential drug-AE associations are evaluated simultaneously. Repeated interim analyses, multiple subgroup evaluations, and sensitivity analyses, all common features of both clinical trials and observational studies, further increase the opportunity for chance findings. Without careful interpretive discipline, such findings may be mistaken for evidence of harm if their probabilistic nature is not explicitly acknowledged [64].

This issue has long been recognized in clinical trial methodology [65] and is particularly relevant to adaptive study designs, in which accumulating data are repeatedly examined to inform decisions on early stopping or protocol modification. Regulatory guidance and methodological frameworks therefore emphasize the importance of pre-specification and error control in these settings to prevent misleading conclusions [66, 67].

From a statistical standpoint, the rationale for adjustment is straightforward. As the number of tests increases, the probability of observing statistically significant results purely by chance also increases, even in the absence of any true causal effect [68]. However, despite this methodological risk, rigid adjustment for multiple comparisons is not universally applied in drug safety, and for defensible reasons. Overcorrection may increase the likelihood of false-negative findings and thereby obscure serious adverse drug reactions. In exploratory drug safety analyses, early signal detection often prioritizes sensitivity over specificity, with the understanding that initial findings require subsequent validation rather than immediate causal interpretation. As a result, many subgroup and sensitivity analyses are intentionally left unadjusted, provided that their exploratory nature is clearly acknowledged.

Taken together, these considerations indicate that the principal risk associated with multiple comparisons and repeated testing in drug safety research lies less in the statistical procedures themselves than in how results are interpreted, emphasized, and acted upon. As highlighted by Boulesteix and Hoffmann [69], analytic misjudgment is most often driven by selective reporting and exaggerated interpretation of isolated statistically significant findings rather than by multiplicity itself, an issue that is particularly salient in PV, where exploratory analyses are frequently misconstrued as confirmatory evidence. Greenland [70] similarly cautions that undue fixation on statistical significance can obscure more consequential considerations, including study design, data quality, and biological plausibility, whereas Hooper [71] warns against the mechanical application of correction methods in settings where flexibility, transparency, and contextual interpretation are more appropriate safeguards.

Fragility of Meta-analysis in Drug Safety

Meta-analysis is often viewed as a powerful tool for evidence synthesis, particularly in drug safety, where individual studies may be underpowered to detect rare AEs. When appropriately designed and transparently conducted, meta-analyses can provide valuable insights and inform regulatory and clinical decision making [56]. Consequently, safety-focused meta-analyses have influenced high-stakes decisions, including those involving cardiovascular risk with rosiglitazone [72] and tiotropium [73], mortality risk with cefepime [74], and suicidality risk with antidepressants [55–57, 75].

Despite their appeal, the validity of meta-analyses in drug safety is highly contingent on the completeness and quality of the underlying evidence base [76]. A prerequisite is comprehensive identification of all relevant studies assessing the safety outcome of interest. However, some trials may be silent with respect to AEs, not because events did not occur, but because safety outcomes were not systematically collected or reported, or because publications space constraints limited what was presented [77]. As a result, pooled estimates may reflect reporting practices and data availability as much as true drug-related risk.

Analytic decisions within the meta-analysis further shape interpretation. Choices regarding heterogeneity handling, use of fixed-effect versus random-effects models, and selection of risk metrics embody different assumptions and can yield materially different conclusions, particularly for rare events and variable follow-up durations [78]. Additional challenges arise when meta-analytic methods are applied to observational studies. These studies often differ substantially in design, endpoint and exposure definitions, confounder adjustment, and analytic strategies. Pooling them may yield an average of heterogeneous biases rather than a more accurate estimate of causal risk. Publication bias and selective outcome reporting further complicate interpretation and may not be reliably detected through statistical diagnostics alone.

Collectively, these considerations underscore that meta-analysis, although important for evidence synthesis, is not necessarily always definitive for drug safety assessment [76, 79]. Recognizing this gap, Zorzela and colleagues [80] introduced the PRISMA harms extension, and CIOMS X [81] emphasized structured appraisal of evidence synthesis and uncertainty when meta-analyses are used to inform drug safety decisions. It is therefore not surprising that several high-profile examples of analytic misjudgment exist in which published meta-analyses of safety findings, appearing in influential journals, generated substantial concern and unintended negative consequences but ultimately failed to reflect the true safety profile of the drug under study.

Case Studies

In 2007, a meta-analysis published in The Lancet Infectious Diseases reported higher all-cause mortality among patients treated with cefepime compared with other beta-lactam antibiotics, including elevated risk in patients with febrile neutropenia [74]. The findings prompted an FDA Early Communication acknowledging the concern [82]. However, following an extensive re-evaluation incorporating both published and unpublished trial data, the FDA concluded that the totality of evidence did not support an increased mortality risk and that cefepime remained appropriate for its approved indications. Expanded trial-level analyses by the FDA reviewers showed no meaningful mortality differences compared with comparator antibiotics [83]. Subsequent critiques highlighted limitations of the original meta-analysis, including heterogeneity of patient populations and indications, inconsistent outcome attribution, incomplete capture of all trials, and sensitivity of subgroup findings to analytic assumptions [84]. Nevertheless, the initial signal generated prolonged regulatory scrutiny and institutional caution.

A similar pattern emerged in 2008, when a meta-analysis published in JAMA reported an increased risk of cardiovascular events associated with inhaled anticholinergic agents for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [73]. The analysis was limited by incomplete AE capture, lack of systematic vital-status follow-up, incomplete inclusion of trials, and failure to account for differential exposure duration using person-time methods. Shortly thereafter, results from the large UPLIFT randomized trial, which enrolled more than 6000 patients and followed them for up to four years, did not confirm these concerns and even suggested potential reductions in cardiovascular events and mortality [85]. Subsequently, FDA concluded that the available data did not demonstrate excess risk [86]. Despite this, the initial meta-analytic signal prompted regulatory communications that led to measurable changes in prescribing behavior [87].

The case of rosiglitazone (Avandia) represents another consequential example of meta-analytic influence on regulatory decision making. In 2007, a meta-analysis published in The New England Journal of Medicine suggested an increased risk of MI and a numerically higher risk of cardiovascular death with the use of rosiglitazone [72]. These findings triggered rapid regulatory action, including FDA safety communications, advisory committee deliberations, boxed warnings, and prescribing restrictions [88], followed by a dramatic decline in the drug’s use [89]. Subsequent re-evaluation incorporating more comprehensive evidence, including reanalysis of the RECORD trial, did not confirm the magnitude of cardiovascular risk suggested by the original meta-analysis [90]. On this basis, the FDA ultimately removed prescribing restrictions and the REMS program [91]. However, a clinically meaningful rebound in prescribing has not been observed.

Taken together, these cases demonstrate how analytic misjudgment can arise when early meta-analytic findings are interpreted as confirmatory evidence of causality. In each instance, influential publications precipitated regulatory actions and changes in clinical practice that were later revised as more definitive evidence emerged. These examples highlight the particular vulnerability of drug safety decision making to premature conclusions, especially when outcomes such as mortality or cardiovascular events heighten perceived urgency.

Artificial Intelligence as a Potential Amplifier of Analytic Misjudgment

In this section, we use “alert” to refer to an initial model-generated pattern that has not yet undergone expert review, “signal” to refer to information that warrants further investigation [92], and “validated signal” to refer to a concern that has undergone preliminary clinical and scientific assessment [93]. Artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled systems may generate outputs that are richer than simple alerts because they can integrate multiple data sources during the detection phase [94]. Even so, such outputs remain hypothesis-generating until they undergo structured human review and formal signal validation. This distinction becomes increasingly important in the current high-dimensional data environment. Modern PV systems operate at a scale that encompasses millions of electronic medical records and similarly large volumes of spontaneously reported AEs. In such settings, correlation-driven AI models are almost certain to identify large numbers of statistically detectable patterns, most of which are likely to lack clinical meaning or causal plausibility [95]. Without an embedded causal perspective in the AI modeling process [96], signal detection tools risk generating overwhelming numbers of false-positive findings, exceeding available expert capacity, and degrading the quality, efficiency, and timeliness of downstream safety decision making. More importantly, automation changes the scale and speed at which such patterns are generated, increasing the risk that weak or spurious associations are not only detected but rapidly propagated and acted upon with unwarranted confidence. This challenge is not merely theoretical. It echoes earlier implementation experiences, such as the initial decision not to deploy signal detection immediately within the FDA Sentinel System despite the statutory mandate, reflecting recognition of the risks associated with large-scale, association-driven analyses without sufficient causal framing.

For these reasons, smart AI-enabled signal detection tools should incorporate at least a preliminary qualitative layer of causality-informed prioritization as part of their core design. This does not mean rendering definitive causal conclusions at the point of detection. Rather, it means encoding structured reasoning that supports triage, filtering, and prioritization in ways that are more consistent with ways in which experienced safety assessors judge plausibility, alternative explanations, and coherence across case-level and aggregate evidence [8]. Embedding this level of causal-informed prioritization promotes continuity and coherence across the signal management lifecycle, from detection through validation and confirmation. Early causal filtering can reduce unnecessary downstream rework, support more proportionate escalation decisions, and improve alignment between automated outputs and subsequent human scientific assessment. In this way, causality-informed AI models do not simply enhance efficiency at the front end of signal detection. They may also strengthen the scientific integrity and decision quality in PV. The objective is not to automate causality assessment, but to define the scientific guardrails required for systems that prioritize, filter, and escalate signals in a manner consistent with core PV principles. At the same time, the development and implementation of such systems may also reveal areas in which current pharmacovigilance practice can be refined, standardized, or improved. Published methodological work on Bayesian evidence evaluation [97], structured evidence aggregation [98], and precision PV [99] is relevant here because it makes assumptions about evidentiary weighting, uncertainty, and contextual relevance more explicit. These approaches may help AI-enabled systems support more disciplined prioritization and evidence appraisal rather than opaque escalation of weak associations. Without such explicit structuring, AI systems risk implicitly weighing heterogeneous evidence sources as equivalent, blurring distinctions between anecdotal reports, observational findings, and controlled clinical data. Their value, however, ultimately depends on transparent assumptions, source-specific bias assessment, traceable reasoning, and continued expert oversight.

The considerations discussed here are intended primarily as forward-looking design concerns and plausible failure modes, rather than as claims that such failures have already been demonstrated extensively in routine regulatory practice. Although AI in drug safety may create important opportunities for evidence integration, it may also introduce new pathways for analytic misjudgment if deployed without appropriate safeguards. The core concern is that AI methods do not transcend the limitations of their underlying data and may inherit, reproduce, or amplify biases arising from missing, incomplete, or structurally imbalanced data sources [100]. Risks of analytic misjudgment may emerge at multiple stages of the signal management lifecycle. At the detection stage, AI models trained on spontaneous reports, electronic health records, or claims data may preferentially identify signals in well-documented populations while failing to detect true risks in underrepresented subgroups. Such imbalances can generate both false reassurance and spurious alarms. Artificial intelligence-driven evidence synthesis, if not designed thoughtfully, may obscure confounding, misrepresent background incidence, or overweigh statistically salient but clinically fragile patterns. When layered onto already complex probabilistic reasoning, AI outputs interpreted without transparency, bias auditing, and expert clinical judgment may reinforce the very forms of analytic misjudgment outlined in this paper. It is worth noting that a recent scrutiny of AI disease-prediction models trained on dubious datasets, some of which appear to have been used in clinical settings, reinforces the point that AI may not overcome weaknesses in underlying data and may instead propagate or amplify them [101]. Adoption of AI in drug safety might be further constrained by unresolved issues in model robustness, interpretability, and reliability, limitations that are particularly consequential when outputs are used in GxP-regulated decision contexts [102].

Discussion and Implications for Drug Safety Decision Making

In drug safety, no single data source is typically sufficient to establish drug-related risk. More reliable inference emerges from disciplined integration of mechanistic plausibility, clinical trials, observational studies, and structured causality assessment [8]. Analytic misjudgment occurs when isolated findings, such as a statistically significant association, a disproportionality signal, or a pooled estimate, are detached from that broader evidentiary context and treated as dispositive. Established work on risk communication and statistical reasoning has shown that even trained clinicians and researchers misinterpret probabilistic information, particularly when risks are presented in relative terms or non-transparent formats, leading to distorted perception of magnitude and clinical relevance [103]. The consequences extend beyond methodological debate. They may include disproportionate regulatory action, erosion of public trust, misallocation of investigational capacity, and suboptimal patient outcomes. Across the examples in this paper, the central theme is that error often arises not only from the existence of uncertainty, but also from how evidence is framed, synthesized, and communicated under uncertainty. By identifying recurring pitfalls and illustrating their consequences, this paper aims to support more robust, evidence-based causality assessment and decision making in drug safety.

High-profile safety signals, especially those involving serious AEs, create strong incentives to treat early findings as definitive. Yet drug safety operates in a setting where early evidence is often hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Spontaneous reports lack exposure denominators, some observational studies are vulnerable to confounding and design choices and even published clinical trial evidence may be limited by incomplete reporting [104], heterogeneous designs, and analytic fragility. Misjudgment arises when associative or screening outputs are interpreted as causal confirmation rather than provisional findings that require further evaluation.

Importantly, these vulnerabilities do not end with individual analyses. They are often propagated and amplified when such findings are subsequently synthesized through meta-analytic frameworks, where selective inclusion, outcome aggregation, and implicit assumptions of comparability can further magnify inaccurate safety signals. The practical difficulty is that early communications, whether regulatory, media-driven, or litigation-amplified, can convert provisional signals into durable beliefs, making later evidence-based corrections appear to be reversals rather than updates [105]. This is one mechanism by which analytic misjudgment becomes operationally consequential, because it can potentially affect prescribing, channeling, adherence, and the interpretability of subsequent safety data. Minimizing analytic misjudgment therefore requires safeguards matched explicitly to where failures occur. Safety evaluations should maintain correct probabilistic framing and clearly separate hypothesis generation, signal validation, and causal inference. Denominator and comparator discipline should be treated as a foundational quality criterion across evidence streams, supported where appropriate by exposure-adjusted incidence rates, absolute risk estimates, and benchmarking against relevant background rates. In observational analyses, confounding and channeling bias should be addressed through fit-for-purpose design choices, including active comparator designs, propensity score methods, and sensitivity analyses for residual or unmeasured confounding. Multiplicity, repeated testing, and data-driven signal generation require pre-specification where feasible, transparent reporting, clinical contextualization, replication across independent data sources, and interpretation rules that prevent selective emphasis on chance findings, including careful attention to interim and adaptive decision rules. Safety communications should preserve uncertainty through calibrated language, explicit limitations, and clear distinction between signals under investigation and final conclusions, so that iterative learning is understood as expected rather than suspicious. Finally, drug safety assessments also benefit from multidisciplinary scrutiny that integrates expertise in epidemiology, biostatistics, preclinical science, toxicology, clinical medicine, pharmacology, and many other relevant disciplines. Advisory committees, independent monitoring boards, and regulatory review bodies can provide structured forums to challenge assumptions, identify statistical fallacies, and ensure that safety-related actions remain proportionate to evidentiary strength.

Conclusions

Drug safety assessment is particularly vulnerable to analytic misjudgment because it relies on heterogeneous evidence, sparse outcomes, evolving data, and decisions that often must be made before causal certainty is attainable. Across the examples discussed here, the recurring vulnerabilities are not merely statistical or methodological. They are also amplified by how evidence is communicated, operationalized, and increasingly automated. Reducing analytic misjudgment therefore requires disciplined analytic approaches, triangulation across evidence streams, and careful oversight of emerging AI-enabled tools. Strengthening these safeguards can improve causality assessment and support more proportionate regulatory responses, enabling precautionary action where warranted while avoiding unnecessary restrictions that may ultimately compromise patient access to effective therapies.
