The value of proteinuria for the administration of rituximab in patients with PLA2R-associated membranous nephropathy: a single-centre retrospective analysis
Authors: Xueting Li, Yinghui Zhang, Chengyun Xu, Ben Ke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveStudies have shown that the efficacy of rituximab (RTX) in patients with M-type phospholipase A2 receptor (PLA2R)-associated primary membranous nephropathy (PMN) is related to proteinuria. However, definitive evidence to guide the initiation of RTX therapy based on proteinuria excretion levels in PMN is still lacking.MethodsA total of 123 patients with PMN, confirmed by positive PLA2R staining in renal biopsy and/or serum anti-PLA2R antibody positivity, were enrolled in this study. All patients received RTX therapy. The patients were categorized into two groups based on their baseline proteinuria: 4–6 g and >6 g. Compared the outcomes and adverse events of patients between different groups after 12-month follow-up.ResultsThis study revealed statistically significant differences in composite and complete remission rates after 12-month follow-up among PMN patients with different baseline proteinuria levels following RTX treatment. At 12-month follow-up, we found that the partial remission (PR) rate in the 4–6 g group was significantly higher than that in the >6 g group at the 1st and 3rd months (p = 0.002, p < 0.001), the complete remission (CR) rate in the 4–6 g group was significantly higher than that in the >6 g group at the 9th and 12th months (p = 0.024, p = 0.013), the composite response rate in the 4–6 g group was also significantly higher than that in the >6 g group at the 1st, 3rd, and 9th months (p = 0.003, p = 0.002, p = 0.037). Moreover, ROC curve analysis found that the predictive cutoff values for proteinuria determined by the Youden index were 4 g/d and 12 g/d, suggesting that the higher baseline proteinuria level was associated with a poorer therapeutic response.ConclusionsOur findings show that lower baseline proteinuria was associated with a better response to RTX in PLA2R-associated PMN patients, whereas higher baseline proteinuria, particularly >12 g/day, was associated with lower remission rates.

Introduction

Primary membranous nephropathy (PMN) is widely recognized as one of the most common etiologies of nephrotic syndrome among adults. Its pathogenesis involves multiple autoantibodies targeting podocyte antigens, leading to immune complex deposition on the glomerular basement membrane and resulting in clinical manifestations, such as proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, and edema [1]. In recent years, the discovery of target antigens, such as M-type phospholipase A2 receptor (PLA2R), has revolutionized the diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for PMN [1]. Rituximab (RTX) is a monoclonal antibody targeting the B cell antigen CD20. The 2021 KDIGO clinical practice guidelines advocate for the administration of RTX in PMN patients stratified as moderate to high risk, specifically those presenting with nephrotic-range proteinuria (urinary protein > 3.5 g/d) [2]. However, a recent study has demonstrated that the remission rate of RTX in patients with PMN is only 88.1% [3]. There are many factors that affect the clinical efficacy of RTX. Recent studies identified several factors contributing to RTX resistance, such as urinary rituximab loss, reduced bioavailability (lower doses of rituximab), the development of anti-rituximab antibodies, high immunological activity (high anti-PLA2R titer), and chronic kidney damage. Interestingly, low residual rituximab levels at month 1 and at month 3 were described in association with lower eGFR, higher non-selective proteinuria, and urinary rituximab loss, and also high anti-PLA2R1 titres and high proteinuria [4–8]. Current research suggests that the more protein present in the urine, the more urinary RTX is lost, which leads to a lack of direct clinical evidence. Consequently, it remains unclear whether the baseline level of proteinuria can guide the initial treatment of RTX for patients with PMN.

In this retrospective cohort study, 123 qualified PMN patients received RTX treatment and were recruited and stratified into two groups by baseline proteinuria levels. The study investigated clinical remission rates, which included composite remission, partial remission (PR), and complete remission (CR) rates, and adverse events, and identified a baseline proteinuria threshold to guide prioritization of RTX treatment in PMN patients.

Methods

Study patients

This study is a single-center retrospective analysis. From July 2020 to July 2024, 279 PMN patients confirmed by kidney biopsy were recruited from the Department of Nephrology, The Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Figure 1). This research was conducted in compliance with the ethical standards set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Approval No.: EFY20250621). Informed consent was secured in writing from every participant.

Flowchart of the study.

Eligibility Requirements: (i) subjects with a confirmed diagnosis of PLA2R-associated membranous nephropathy, as verified by positive PLA2R immunostaining in renal biopsy specimens and/or serum anti-PLA2R antibody titers of ≥14 RU/mL; (ii) patients meeting the diagnostic criteria for nephrotic syndrome and proteinuria over 4 g/d; (iii) no history of glucocorticoid or immunosuppressive agent treatment before kidney biopsy; (iv) all patients received RTX treatment.

Criteria for non-participation: (i) patients with type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus; (ii) secondary membranous nephropathy of any aetiology; (iii) active infection, pregnancy, or lactation; (iv) recent treatment with glucocorticoids or immunosuppressive agents within the past 3 months; (v) complications of severe hepatic insufficiency or significant cardiovascular disorders.

Sample size calculation

Sample size estimation was performed using PASS version 15 software to assess the association between PSI and remission in patients with PMN. Based on a previous study [9], assuming a remission rate of 53% in the low-PSI group, an odds ratio (OR) of 4.5, a two-sided α of 0.05, and a power of 0.80, the minimum required sample size was 84 patients.

Intervention

Based on the 2021 KDIGO clinical practice guidelines for risk stratification of disease progression in membranous nephropathy, the patients were categorized into two groups according to the baseline level of 24-hour urinary protein excretion: the 4–6 g group and the >6 g group. All patients received RTX treatment recommended by the guidelines and were followed up for at least 12 months. According to the 2021 KDIGO guideline recommendations [10], the RTX regimen for PMN involves the administration of two intravenous infusions of 1,000 mg each, separated by a 2-week interval or 375 mg/m2 weekly for 4 weeks. Premedication with 5 mg intravenous dexamethasone and 50 mg intramuscular diphenhydramine was administered 30 min before each RTX infusion. All patients were managed with optimal supportive therapy, which encompassed treatment with an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI) or an angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB), titrated to the highest tolerated dose (e.g. valsartan initiated at 80 mg/day and increased up to 320 mg/day, or irbesartan initiated at 150 mg/day and increased up to 300 mg/day), along with diuretics, lipid-lowering medications, and necessary prophylactic antithrombotic therapy, with specific regimens determined by the attending physician based on clinical conditions.

Data collection and follow-up

The clinical information of age, biological sex, blood pressure, proteinuria, serum albumin (ALB), serum creatinine, anti-PLA2R antibody titre data, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was collected through the electronic medical record at the following time points: baseline, 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months. eGFR was calculated using the CKD-EPI equation [11]. Serum anti-PLA2R antibodies were detected via an ELISA kit (Euroimmune, Lubeck, Germany). All baseline assessments were completed before immunosuppressive therapy. All severe adverse events (SAEs) were addressed and documented throughout both the inpatient and follow-up periods. Notably, every enrolled patient completed a follow-up period of no less than 12 months.

Outcomes

The primary clinical outcome was composite remission, which was defined as complete remission (CR) or partial remission (PR) within 12 months. CR: urinary protein excretion ≤ 0.3 g/d, a serum albumin level of at least 35 g/L, and a stable or improved eGFR. PR: urinary protein excretion between 0.3 and 3.5 g/d, with a reduction of ≥50% from baseline and serum albumin level ≥ 30 g/L. Composite remission comprises CR or PR. Relapse was defined as a renewed and sustained increase in proteinuria to >3.5 g/day, accompanied by a decrease in serum albumin, upon achieving complete or partial remission (CR/PR). Primary refractoriness was defined as the failure to attain at least a PR after a 12-month standard therapeutic course. A decline in renal function was defined as either a ≥ 50% reduction in the eGFR from baseline or progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD), which was characterized by a persistently low eGFR (<15 mL/min/1.73 m2) or the need to initiate long-term dialysis.

Severe adverse events, including infection, leukopenia, and diabetes, were documented as secondary clinical outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Datasets with non-normal distributions were summarized as median values (25th and 75th percentiles), and intergroup comparisons of the corresponding continuous variables were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. By contrast, normally distributed data were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and intergroup comparisons of such continuous variables were conducted using the independent samples t-test. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and intergroup comparisons of these nominal data were conducted using Chi-square (χ2) tests. The cumulative treatment response rate was estimated with the Kaplan–Meier method, and intergroup differences in survival curves were compared using the log-rank test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of target indicators, thereby determining the optimal urinary protein cutoff value. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant. All statistical analyses were completed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL).

Results

Study participants

A total of 123 participants with PMN were included in the study, of whom 60 were in the 4–6 g group and 63 in the >6 g group. The mean age in the 4–6 g group was 53.54 years, the mean proteinuria level was 4381.12 mg/day, and the mean albumin level was 26.59 g/L. In the >6 g group, the mean age was 56.33 years, the mean proteinuria level was 10300.78 mg/day, and the mean albumin level was 23.41 g/L. There was a statistically significant difference in the levels of proteinuria and serum albumin between the two groups.

No significant differences were observed between the two groups in other baseline clinical indices, including age, biological sex, blood pressure, serum creatinine, positive rate of anti-PLA2R antibody, anti-PLA2R titres, and eGFR. Detailed data were presented in Table 1.

During follow-up, both groups exhibited a significant decrease in 24-hour proteinuria levels, an increase in ALB levels, and stable renal function compared to baseline. The intergroup comparison showed that during the 12-month follow-up, there were no statistically significant differences in key indicators such as proteinuria, ALB, serum creatinine, and eGFR between the two groups of patients (Figure 2).

As summarized in Table 2, the composite remission rates, PR rates, and CR rates were compared between the two groups at different time points. We found that the composite remission rates at the 1st, 3rd, and 9th months were significantly different between the two groups (p = 0.003; p = 0.002; p = 0.037). After completion of a 3-month RTX intervention, the PR rate in the 4–6 g group was 62.5% (38/60), significantly higher than the 33.3% (21/63) in the >6 g group (p < 0.001). At 9 months and 12 months, the CR rate in the 4–6 g group (33.3%, 20/60; 45.8%, 27/60) remained higher than that in the >6 g group (15.9%, 10/63; 23.8%, 15/63) (p = 0.024; p = 0.013). Notably, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in composite remission and PR rate at the 6 months and 12 months of follow-up. Furthermore, we compared the baseline level of anti-PLA2R antibody titres between the two groups in the CR/PR subgroup and the non-response subgroup. In the 4–6 g group, we found that there were no significant differences in baseline level of anti-PLA2R antibody titres between the CR/PR subgroup and the non-response subgroup at the 1st, 3rd, 6th, 9th, and 12th months. In the >6 g group, there were significant differences in baseline level of anti-PLA2R antibody titres between the CR/PR subgroup and the non-response subgroup at the 1st and 9th months (p = 0.040; p = 0.023) (Table 3). These findings indicated that baseline level of anti-PLA2R antibody titres influenced the efficacy of RTX, specifically, higher anti-PLA2R antibody titres in PMN patients are associated with poorer responses to RTX, which is consistent with the results of our previous study [12].

We used logistic regression analysis to examine the factors influencing the therapeutic efficacy of the PMN patients included in this study. Univariate regression analysis showed that the baseline level of proteinuria was a significant factor affecting disease remission (OR = 0.915, 95% CI: 0.843–0.994), suggesting that a higher baseline level of proteinuria was associated with a lower remission rate in PMN patients receiving RTX therapy. After including the variables with p < 0.1 (including proteinuria, ALB, and anti-PLA2R antibody titers) from the univariate analysis into the multivariate regression model, none of these variables showed statistical significance (Table 4).

Adverse events

In Table 5, all the side effects that occurred during the 12-month follow-up in the PMN patients were included in the study. Adverse events were not different between the two groups. A total of 7 patients (11.1%) in the >6 g group developed adverse events, among which were 3 episodes of pulmonary infection, 3 cases of abnormal liver function, and 1 case of herpes zoster. In the 4–6 g group, two cases of urinary tract infection were reported.

ROC curve analysis

We performed ROC curve analysis to evaluate the predictive cutoff values of baseline level of 24-hour proteinuria for treatment response in PMN patients at different time points. The areas under the curve (AUC) were as follows: for predicting 3-month composite remission (AUC 0.679; 95% CI: [0.568, 0.791], p = 0.004), 9-month composite remission (AUC 0.630; 95% CI: [0.506, 0.754], p = 0.042), 1-month PR (AUC 0.713; 95% CI: [0.599, 0.827], p = 0.004), and 3-month PR (AUC 0.690; 95% CI: [0.580, 0.800], p = 0.003) (Figure 5). Based on current data, the predictive cutoff values for 24-hour proteinuria determined by the Youden index were 4 g/d and 12 g/d, which means that PMN patients with the baseline level of proteinuria below 4 g/d achieved the highest composite remission and PR rates, whereas those with baseline proteinuria >12 g/d achieved the lowest remission rates after RTX administration.

Based on our above-mentioned results, we found that the remission rate for PMN patients after RTX treatment began to increase significantly when baseline proteinuria was less than 6 g/d. Therefore, 6 g/d may represent a more clinically actionable threshold. Furthermore, our study demonstrates a significant negative correlation between baseline proteinuria levels and response to RTX treatment, suggesting that higher baseline proteinuria may predict poorer therapeutic response to RTX.

Discussion

At present, RTX has been established as a first-line therapeutic regimen for PMN, with its efficacy and safety validated by multiple pivotal randomized controlled trials [13,14]. In the 2021 KDIGO clinical practice guidelines, RTX has been advocated for PMN patients with moderate to high risk and with proteinuria levels exceeding 3.5 g/d [2]. However, some studies have found that in patients with PMN, proteinuria is one of the important factors affecting the clinical efficacy of RTX [4–6,15], which means that for PMN patients with proteinuria exceeding 3.5 g/d, the efficacy of RTX varies. In this study, our data demonstrated that a higher baseline level of proteinuria in PMN patients predicted poorer therapeutic response to RTX, especially for those with baseline level of proteinuria exceeding 12 g/d. Moreover, the composite remission rate of the PLA2R-associated PMN with baseline level of proteinuria of 4–6 g/d was significantly higher than those with baseline level of proteinuria exceeding 6 g/d at 1, 3, and 9 months during follow-up. Therefore, we recommend that RTX alone is applied for PLA2R-associated PMN patients with baseline level of proteinuria ≤6 g/d, but not routinely for those with baseline level of proteinuria >12 g/d, even not for those with baseline proteinuria >6 g/d.

With the widespread application of RTX in patients with PMN, the personalized approach to RTX treatment has gradually been specified. Currently, it is believed that the remission rate of patients with PMN when treated with RTX alone is 88.1% [3]. As our understanding deepened, various factors that might affect the efficacy of RTX were discovered, such as proteinuria, anti-rituximab antibodies, and PLA2R1 epitope spreading status [6,16]. The severity of proteinuria is correlated with an increased risk of renal function decline [5]. In particular, sustained proteinuria exceeding 8 g/day for over six months serves as a significant predictor of long-term kidney impairment [17]. Thus, achieving PR or CR in patients with moderate to high risk of PMN as soon as possible is an issue that urgently needs to be addressed in the individualized treatment approach of RTX. Prior research has demonstrated that proteinuria is a critical factor influencing the achievement of partial remission (PR) in patients with PMN following RTX monotherapy [3]. Mechanistically, higher proteinuria is associated with greater loss of RTX in urine, which leads to a decrease in the bioavailability of RTX [4]. A study reported that patients with urinary rituximab ≥2 µg/ml had lower serum rituximab peak levels at 1 month, possibly reflecting drug underexposure, and significantly lower response rates [4]. Consistent with previous studies, our results also demonstrated that a higher baseline level of proteinuria in PMN patients predicted poorer therapeutic response to RTX, especially for those with baseline level of proteinuria exceeding 12 g/d. However, there is another issue that needs to be resolved: whether the baseline level of proteinuria can guide the initial treatment of RTX for patients with PMN.

In this study, our data suggest that among PMN patients positive for anti-PLA2R antibodies, the composite remission rate in patients with baseline proteinuria of 4–6 g/d was superior to that in those with proteinuria >6 g/d at 1, 3, and 9 months of follow-up. And for those with baseline proteinuria >12 g/d, or even for those with >6 g/d, RTX alone is not recommended.

A number of limitations in this study should be considered. First, being a single-center retrospective analysis, this study may face inherent limitations of selection bias and unaccounted confounding factors. Second, the relatively small sample size may introduce some bias into the results. Finally, the follow-up period of 12 months is relatively short. Therefore, a retrospective cohort study with a larger sample size and prolonged follow-up periods is needed to validate the generalizability of the findings presented herein.

Conclusions

Our findings show that lower baseline proteinuria was associated with a better response to RTX in PLA2R-associated PMN patients, whereas higher baseline proteinuria, particularly >12 g/day, was associated with lower remission rates.
