Intercostal nerve blocks should set the standard for regional analgesia in video-assisted thoracic surgery
Authors: Steve Coppens
Published: 2026

We thank Dr. Juan Cata and colleagues for their thoughtful appraisal of our randomized trial comparing thoracoscopy-guided intercostal nerve block (ICNB) with erector spinae plane block (ESPB) for analgesia after uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) (1,2). We appreciate the opportunity to further discuss the interpretation of our findings and their implications for perioperative analgesic strategies in minimally invasive thoracic surgery.

The authors correctly emphasize the mechanistic differences between these two techniques. Thoracoscopy-guided ICNB directly targets the intercostal nerves under visual control, allowing reliable deposition of local anesthetic around the neurovascular bundle. In contrast, ESPB is a fascial plane block that relies on cranio-caudal spread of local anesthetic beneath the erector spinae muscle and indirect diffusion toward the paravertebral space. Increasing anatomical and imaging evidence suggests that anterior spread to the ventral rami after ESPB is variable and often limited, which may contribute to the heterogeneous clinical results reported in the literature (3). From a mechanistic standpoint, it is therefore not surprising that a technique providing direct blockade of the intercostal nerves may offer more consistent analgesia for thoracic incisional pain. The incorporation of ESPB into several procedure-specific guidelines, including those for caesarean delivery and VATS, may also partly reflect a positive publication effect. Recent publications evaluating the clinical effectiveness of ESPB have been less favorable than earlier reports, with several randomized trials demonstrating limited or inconsistent analgesic benefit (4-6). Furthermore, a number of the systematic reviews informing these recommendations are based on small, heterogeneous studies and variable methodological quality, which limits the strength and reliability of the resulting conclusions (7). We agree that the duration of analgesic effect is an important consideration. Our trial was primarily designed to assess early postoperative opioid consumption, and we did not specifically investigate the temporal profile of block resolution. Nonetheless, the persistence of opioid requirements in both groups beyond the early postoperative period suggests that single-injection techniques alone may be insufficient to provide prolonged analgesia after thoracic surgery, further supporting the role of multimodal strategies. We plan to investigate this more in the future.

It is also important to consider the pharmacological duration of single-injection regional techniques when interpreting these findings. The analgesic effects observed in our study had largely dissipated by 12 hours, which is entirely consistent with the expected pharmacokinetics of long-acting local anesthetics such as ropivacaine when administered as a single-shot block. This limitation is not unique to intercostal nerve blocks but applies equally to ESPB and most single-injection regional techniques used in thoracic surgery. Attempts to prolong the duration of analgesia through catheter techniques or the use of prolonged-release formulations, such as liposomal bupivacaine, have yielded inconsistent or modest clinical benefits (8). These observations may suggest that the principal role of regional anesthesia in this context is to facilitate analgesia during the immediate postoperative period—when pain intensity and opioid requirements are highest—while subsequent pain control is effectively maintained through multimodal systemic analgesia within enhanced recovery pathways.

The authors also correctly note that current guidelines did not incorporate the findings of our trial or several other recently published randomized studies. As higher-quality evidence accumulates, including double-blind randomized trials such as ours, it is likely that these data will inform future updates of perioperative analgesia recommendations. As the evidence base continues to evolve, it may therefore be appropriate to re-evaluate the role of surgeon-performed intercostal nerve block within enhanced recovery pathways for thoracic surgery.

Some have suggested that positioning the needle more laterally along the transverse process may improve the clinical effect of the ESPB. However, in our opinion, this approach likely represents an intertransverse process (ITP) block rather than a classical ESPB. As such, it should be evaluated and reported as a distinct technique in future studies rather than being interpreted as a modification of the ESPB (9).

We agree with the authors that further pharmacological investigations focusing on the systemic uptake and pharmacokinetic profile of local anesthetics after ESPB are required to better elucidate the mechanisms responsible for its analgesic effects. In the present study, pharmacokinetic measurements were performed in a limited subset of participants because these analyses were undertaken without dedicated grant funding

Finally, we agree that additional studies—including on the ITP block—are warranted, most importantly to better define the comparative effectiveness of regional techniques, their duration of action, and their impact on longer-term outcomes such as persistent postsurgical pain. In light of the ongoing discussion regarding the correct needle tip position for the ESPB, we therefore plan a new multicentre trial including local anesthetic plasma sampling to investigate the claims surrounding the ITP block and clarify potential differences in spread, mechanism, and pharmacokinetics.

We thank the authors for their insightful comments and welcome continued discussion on optimizing analgesia for patients undergoing minimally invasive thoracic surgery.
