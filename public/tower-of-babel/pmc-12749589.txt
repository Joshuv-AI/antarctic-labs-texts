In Türkiye, very few internal medicine research assistants consider becoming nephrologists in the future
Authors: Nazife Nur Ozer Sensoy, Sinan Kazan, Ozkan Güngör
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe global population of patients with kidney disease is steadily increasing; however, the number of nephrologists available to treat this patient group is concurrently declining. Although the underlying causes of this trend have been investigated in various countries, no such study has been conducted in Türkiye. Therefore, in this survey study, we aimed to examine the perspectives of internal medicine research assistants in our country regarding the nephrology subspecialty. This cross-sectional survey targeted all internal medicine research assistants working in 80 university and training/research hospitals across seven geographical regions of Türkiye. Data were collected through an online questionnaire developed by the researchers, created using Google Forms (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA), and distributed to participants via e-mail or text message. A total of 2161 research assistants participated in the study, corresponding to a response rate of 60%. Of the participants, 52.1% were female and 47.9% male; 50.4% worked in university hospitals; and 89.6% had never worked in another specialty. The majority reported that they either did not wish to pursue nephrology (50.7%) or were undecided (27.7%). The three most frequently cited reasons for not choosing nephrology were: the high frequency of emergency cases (64.1%), the intense workload during regular working hours (51.7%), and the complexity and severity of the patient population (45.5%). Among those not planning to choose nephrology, 83.7% stated they might reconsider if on-call duties and shifts were less demanding, 44.3% if economic conditions improved, and 19.9% if they had the opportunity to attend a nephrology congress during residency. A heavy daytime workload, demanding on-call duties, limited financial incentives, and the presence of a chronically ill and complex patient population contribute to the low preference for nephrology in Türkiye. Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is defined by the presence of kidney damage or a sustainable reduction in the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) to below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 for a duration of at least three months, regardless of the underlying cause1. The 2012 Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) classification system categorizes CKD into five stages based on GFR and further stratifies each stage according to three levels of albuminuria, determined by the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio2. The global and national (Türkiye) prevalence of CKD and glomerular diseases has been steadily rising, largely attributable to increased life expectancy, enhanced diagnostic capabilities, and the growing burden of diabetes, hypertension, and obesity. Currently, an estimated 840 million individuals are affected by CKD worldwide3. CKD is a progressive condition that may ultimately lead to end-stage renal disease, necessitating renal replacement therapy (RRT). The distribution of RRT modalities varies across countries. Although kidney transplantation represents the optimal form of therapy, hemodialysis remains the most commonly utilized modality in many regions. According to the 2023 Registry Reports of the Turkish Society of Nephrology, 10,238 patients initiated in-center hemodialysis, 1362 patients started peritoneal dialysis, and 2001 underwent kidney transplantation. By the end of 2023, a total of 89,527 patients in Türkiye were receiving RRT4.

Considering its broad spectrum of patients and the escalating global burden of chronic kidney disease, nephrology constitutes a discipline of critical importance to healthcare systems. Despite this significance, nephrology has, over the past 15 years, become one of the least frequently selected internal medicine subspecialties, both nationally and internationally5–7. This trend represents a pressing concern for current and future workforce capacity. Reports from the World Health Organization and other international agencies indicate that the supply of nephrologists in many countries is insufficient to meet population needs. According to the Global Kidney Health Atlas, over 70% of countries reported a shortage of nephrologists8. While Western, Eastern, and Central Europe have approximately 25 nephrologists per million population (pmp), the figure declines to 9.8 pmp in Latin America, 8.1 pmp in the Middle East, 1.2 pmp in South Asia, and as low as 0.6 pmp in Africa8. In Türkiye, approximately 500 adult nephrologists are currently in active practice, corresponding to 5.8 pmp.

The declining interest in nephrology is multifactorial. Contributing factors include the perception of the field as complex and challenging to master, the limited range of interventional procedures, extended working hours, reluctance among internal medicine specialists to manage nephrology patients, and relatively low expected financial remuneration9,10. Additionally, nephrology training during medical school is often brief and predominantly theoretical, limiting opportunities for students to form a strong connection with the specialty. Such findings have been documented in surveys conducted among medical students, internal medicine research assistants, and physicians pursuing non-nephrology subspecialties in the United States and other countries9,10.

The training pathway for nephrology in Türkiye comprises six years of medical education, four years of internal medicine residency, and a subsequent three-year subspeciality training program in nephrology. However, to date, no empirical data have been available regarding the perceptions of internal medicine research assistants in Türkiye toward the nephrology subspecialty, nor the reasons underlying its waning appeal.

Accordingly, the present study aims, for the first time in the literature, to comprehensively assess the perspectives of internal medicine research assistants from all regions of Türkiye on the nephrology subspecialty and to elucidate the key factors influencing their interest or disinterest.

Methods

This research was designed as a cross-sectional survey study. The target population included internal medicine research assistants from all hospitals providing internal medicine residency training across the seven geographical regions of Türkiye. Participation was entirely voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kahramanmaraş Sütçü İmam University Faculty of Medicine (Approval date: 30 June 2025, Decision no: 23) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection

Data were collected using an online questionnaire developed by the researchers. The survey was created using Google Forms (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA) and distributed via e-mail or text message to participants (questions are presented in Table 1). The survey questions were developed based on information derived from previous studies conducted on this topic in various countries. The final version was prepared following consultations with members of the Board of Directors of the Turkish Society of Nephrology, several faculty members from different institutions, and research assistants. Open-ended responses were permitted for questions addressing the reasons for choosing or not choosing nephrology. A pilot test was conducted with five residents at a single center to ensure clarity and feasibility. Responses to the Yes/No/Undecided items were analyzed separately, and the No and Undecided categories were not combined.

All participants read and approved the informed consent statement located at the beginning of the form before proceeding. Respondents who failed to answer these control items correctly were excluded from the analysis to enhance data quality.

Statistical analysis

The survey dataset was analyzed using SPSS version 27.0.1 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). As the survey data, including age, were categorical in nature, results were presented as frequencies and percentages. Categorical variables were compared between groups using the chi-square test. When the assumptions of the chi-square test were not met, Fisher’s exact test was applied. All reported p-values were two-tailed, and values of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

This cross-sectional survey study initially aimed to include 3,620 internal medicine research assistants from 80 hospitals providing internal medicine research assistant across the seven regions of Türkiye. The final study cohort comprised 2161 research assistants (response rate: 60%) who agreed to complete the questionnaire. The completion rate for survey items was 99.5%. A total of ten assistants were excluded from the analysis.

Among the participants, 52.1% (n = 1,126) were female and 47.9% (n = 1,035) were male. A total of 95.7% (n = 2067) were Turkish citizens, while 4.3% (n = 94) held other nationalities. The median duration for completing medical school was 6 years (range: 6–8 years). Approximately half of the participants (50.4%, n = 1,090) were employed in university hospitals, while 49.6% (n = 1,071) worked in training and research or city hospitals.

Regarding foreign language proficiency, 17.4% (n = 375) reported advanced proficiency, 56.5% (n = 1,221) indicated intermediate proficiency, and 26.1% (n = 565) reported inadequate proficiency.

A total of 89.6% (n = 1,936) of research assistants had never previously worked in another specialty. In terms of years in residency, 40.3% (n = 870) were in their 3rd–4th year, 23.1% (n = 500) in their 2nd–3rd year, 20.7% (n = 447) in their 1st–2nd year, and 15.9% (n = 344) had less than one year of training. Overall, 59.5% (n = 1,285) expressed satisfaction with being internal medicine research assistants, 11.5% (n = 248) were dissatisfied, and 29.1% (n = 628) were undecided.

It was determined that 74.5% (n = 1,611) had no intention of leaving internal medicine training, whereas 7.2% (n = 155) were considering leaving and 18.3% (n = 395) were undecided. The responses of the participating research assistants to the survey questions are presented in Table 2.

Among participants interested in nephrology fellowship (n = 466), the most cited reasons were suitability and self-confidence in performing nephrology (54.5%, n = 254), positive influence from nephrology faculty (43.1%, n = 201), and the opportunity to utilize internal medicine knowledge more extensively (37.6%, n = 175). Among those not interested in nephrology fellowship (n = 1096), the top three deterrents were frequent emergencies and challenging on-calls (64.1%, n = 702), high workload intensity (51.7%, n = 567), and difficult patient profiles (45.5%, n = 499). When asked what might increase their interest, the leading factors were more manageable on-calls and shifts (83.7%, n = 917), equitable earnings with other subspecialties (44.3%, n = 485), and opportunities to attend nephrology congresses during residency (19.9%, n = 218).

Discussion

Nephrology has increasingly become a less preferred specialty worldwide in recent years. This survey study, conducted for the first time in our country, represents the most comprehensive study on this subject in the literature. Unfortunately, the study revealed that the majority of internal medicine research assistants in our country are either reluctant or undecided about choosing nephrology as a subspecialty.

If we begin by asking the question, “What motivates Us to become a nephrologist?”

A study conducted in the United Kingdom sought to answer this question. The presence of role models and prior exposure to nephrology appeared to have a significant influence on the choice9. Indeed, the existence of role models is a highly important issue. Previous studies have also shown that mentorship and role models are among the most important factors in choosing nephrology10,11. In one study, 31% of participants reported not having a mentor who could guide them toward nephrology. A U.S. survey also found that access to mentors (70%) was a critical factor in the decision to pursue a nephrology career9. In our study, 43.1% of those who stated they would choose nephrology as a subspecialty indicated that this decision was influenced by taking their nephrology professors as role models. Personally, I decided to become a nephrologist one month after starting internal medicine residency, largely due to my professors’ close involvement with patients, frequent patient follow-up, comprehensive physical examinations, and the fact that they performed interventional procedures themselves (such as dialysis catheter insertion, kidney biopsy, etc.). My residency continued in this way, and I eventually became a nephrologist. Now, I know that some of our research assistants have also chosen nephrology because they saw me as a role model. Therefore, the development of active mentorship programs at both the medical school and residency levels—covering both clinical and research areas—may increase interest in nephrology.

Previous surveys have shown that most nephrology fellows made the decision to enter this field during medical school or residency9–11. Presenting nephrology topics in a more engaging and appealing manner could increase interest. In our study, a comparison on this issue could not be made, as we examined factors influencing preferences during the internal medicine residency period rather than those relevant to the medical school period. In one survey, approximately one-quarter of participants stated that they might have considered nephrology if it had been taught more effectively12.

The field of nephrology involves establishing long-term relationships with patients, continuity of care, and working in a multidisciplinary environment with dietitians, nurses, and other healthcare professionals (such as urology, general surgery, interventional radiology, pathology, etc.)13. It is an appealing specialty that spans a broad spectrum of medical practice, from managing acute conditions to providing long-term holistic care. A study in the UK found that enthusiasm and commitment to the field were important factors. In our country, subspecialty selection is determined by an exam following four years of internal medicine residency. Among all subspecialties, nephrology seems to be the one in which theoretical and practical knowledge acquired during internal medicine residency is most applicable. In our study, 54.5% of those interested in nephrology reported that they found nephrology more suitable and attractive for themselves, and 37.6% stated that they considered nephrology because it allowed them to utilize their internal medicine knowledge more extensively. The findings are consistent with those reported in previous studies14.

Do nephrologists recommend becoming a nephrologist? According to the American Society of Nephrology, the percentage of nephrologists recommending the field has increased over the years. In 2015, 67.7% of participants recommended nephrology to future research assistants, and by 2019, this rate had risen to nearly 80%15. In our country, no clear data exists on this matter. However, our observations suggest that nephrologists generally recommend nephrology to research assistants who truly have a passion for it.

If we look at the demotivating factors for becoming a nephrologist

In our study, 50.7% of participants stated that they would not choose nephrology in the subspecialty exam, while 27.7% reported being undecided. This shows that, overall, internal medicine research assistants in our country are not very enthusiastic about becoming nephrologists. Is this phenomenon unique to our country? No. A review of the literature reveals that in the United States, the United Kingdom, and several other countries, the proportion of trainees choosing nephrology has steadily declined over the past 15 years. According to a report from the United States, the number of applicants per nephrology fellowship position decreased from 1.6 in 2008 to 1.1 in 201215. According to the Global Kidney Health Atlas, the number of nephrology fellows per million population was 3.7 in high-income countries, 1.2 in upper-middle-income countries, 0.6 in lower-middle-income countries, and only 0.1 in low-income countries. In our country, this figure is around 0.828.

When examining survey studies on speciality selection in nephrology, a U.S. study involving 714 fellows from non-nephrology subspecialities reported that only 12% would choose nephrology if given another opportunity5. Another study, which surveyed 644 medical students (years 4–6) and internal medicine residents, found that 154 participants (23%) indicated that they might consider nephrology in the future9. In our study, this proportion was 21.6%, which is comparable. This rate cannot be considered particularly low, given that internal medicine graduates have several alternative subspeciality options-including endoncrinolgy, rheumatology, gastroenterology, hematology, oncology, immunology, geriatrics, and intensive care. Therefore, it is not unexpected that approximately one in five participants expressed an interest in nephrology.

Why is nephrology no longer preferred, and how can we encourage research assistants to choose it?

Previous studies have cited reasons such as: unengaging or difficult renal pathophysiology lectures in medical school; limited nephrology electives during medical school or residency; insufficient mentorship; perception of heavy workload and low pay in nephrology; negative interactions with dissatisfied nephrology fellows or faculty; the complexity of dialysis and transplant patient care; and the limited number of interventional procedures12. Other studies have emphasized the complexity of kidney patients, the lack of adequate procedural training, and the absence of role models or mentors13,14. Additional factors reported include the difficulty of the subject matter, long working hours (poor work-life balance), low income, and insufficient teaching of nephrology during medical school and residency. Concerns regarding chronic disease management have also been highlighted. In another study, lack of interest in the field was cited as the most important reason for giving up nephrology training, followed by concerns about insufficient financial compensation post-fellowship (43%), poor work-life balance (38%), and inadequate early exposure to the field (32%)15. Similar reasons have been reported in different studies15,16. In our study, 64.1% of respondents stated that nephrology involves too many emergency cases and demanding on-call duties, 51.7% reported that it is an intense speciality during regular working hours as well, and 45.5% noted that the patient population is socioeconomically disadvantaged and their management is challenging. Other commonly cited factors included limited financial rewards and fewer opportunities for professional advancement. Overall, the reasons for the recent decline in interest in nephrology in our country are consistent with those reported in the literature.

In our country, the pathway to becoming a nephrologist involves six years of medical education, succesful competion of the national speciality examination, four years of intensive internal medicine residency, and 1.5-2 years of compulsory service, followed by three years of subspeciality training in nephrology and an additional 1.5-2 years of mandatory service. By the time physicians complete their final compulsory service as nephrologists, they are typically around 37–38 years of age. This extended training trajectory, particularly when compared with European countries, apperars to negatively influence both the rate of pursuing subspeciality training and the willingness to choose a demanding field such as nephrology. After becoming a nephrologist, one faces a chronically ill and complex patient population, requiring 24/7 commitment. In addition to a heavy daytime workload, nephrologists are frequently called in for emergencies during evenings, nights, and weekends. As the kidneys are affected by various systemic disorders nephrology consultations are commonly sought even in cases where the underlying problem is not primarily nephrological, leading to a considerable increase in workload. General internists are often unwilling to manage kidney patients, leaving the small group of nephrologists to work at an intense pace. In terms of financial return, procedural performance points (e.g., catheter placement, dialysis) are relatively low, leading to lower earnings compared to other internal medicine subspecialties such as oncology or gastroenterology. Therefore, except for those who genuinely find nephrology suitable for themselves, the rate of choosing the field continues to decline.

Other findings in our study: When comparing the intention to choose nephrology in the subspecialty exam according to the duration of nephrology rotations, the groups were found to be similar. However, since previous studies did not examine this issue in detail, it should not be concluded that the duration of rotations is unimportant. We believe that longer rotations in nephrology increase the chances of making research assistants more interested in the field.

Participation in nephrology congresses: When comparing research assistants who attended nephrology congresses with those who did not, 30.6% of attendees stated that they might choose nephrology as a subspecialty, compared to 19.7% among non-attendees (p < 0.001). This finding is consistent with our observations. Research assistants we involved in nephrology-related clinical studies, supported in presenting at congresses, and exposed to research opportunities often went on to choose nephrology17.

When examining the order of preference for internal medicine residency after the medical specialty exam, the desire to choose a nephrology subspecialty was found to decrease as the preference order for internal medicine residency increased. This finding was expected. It’s not surprising that someone who had previously placed a challenging specialty like internal medicine at the bottom of their list of preferences, meaning they hadn’t chosen it with great enthusiasm, would be interested in a challenging specialty like nephrology.

Regional and institutional differences

Our country consists of seven geographical regions. Established university hospitals in Marmara, Central Anatolia, and the Aegean region have been training research assistants for many years. When analyzing subspecialty preferences by region, the rates were generally similar. However, the highest proportion of research assistants reporting a potential preference for nephrology was observed in the Aegean region (26.8%), while the lowest rate was in Eastern Anatolia (13.1%). The higher rate in the Aegean region may be attributed to the presence of well-established hospitals and a motivating academic environment. In addition, no significant differences were found between research assistants working in university hospitals and those in other training hospitals. We initially hypothesized that university-based research assistants would show a higher preference for nephrology, but in our country, higher financial incentives in non-university hospitals may also attract faculty members to these institutions, indirectly motivating research assistants toward nephrology as well.

How can nephrology be made more attractive? Insights from previous studies suggest several approaches

In one study on this topic, Sozio et al. identified four key factors: (1) the degree of interaction between nephrology faculty members and medical students; (2) clinical exposure to nephrology and the perceived clinical relevance of renal pathophysiology teaching; (3) the incorporation of innovative educational methods; and (4) early exposure to the broad spectrum of nephrology practice18. In another study, Hopkins et al. reported that nephrology was poorly recognized among medical students (27%) and emphasized the need for Greater curricular focus on the field19. Based on the findings of these studies, the necessary interventions can be categorized under the following heading:

-Educational quality: This issue is quite important. Sozio et al. also highlighted this issue in their study. Presenting nephrology topics in a clearer and more engaging way may increase interest18. -Technology and e-learning: Using online education tools aligned with modern learning habits can facilitate learning16,17. -Exposure and clinical experience: Adequate exposure to all aspects of nephrology during medical school and residency is essential18,19. -Interventional procedures: Interventional procedures such as temporary or permanent hemodialysis catheter placement, peritoneal dialysis catheter insertion, and kidney biopsy are frequently performed within the field of nephrology. However, in some nephrology clinics, these procedures are carried out by general surgeons or interventional radiologists. Previous studies have highlighted the limited involvement of nephrologists in interventional procedures as a contributing factor to the declining interest in the speciality15,16. -Expanding training and exp osure to interventional nephrology may attract research assistants interested in procedural work20. -Mentorship: This factor is also of great importance. As demonstrated in multiple studies, mentorship and the presence of strong role models remain central, placing considerable responsibility on faculty members18,19 -Lifestyle and work-life balance: Nephrologists are often perceived as among the busiest specialists. Increasing the number of nephrology training positions, ensuring that general internists manage cases not requiring direct nephrology input, and reducing the nephrologists’ workload could help20,21. Economic factors: we cannot make an objective comparison between the income of nephrologists in our country and that of nephrologists abroad, as no reliable data sources are available. However, in our country, high inflation and lower financial compensation compared to other subspecialties discourage interest in nephrology. Policy changes that allow nephrologists to establish private dialysis units or outpatient clinics may help improve the specialty’s attractiveness.

Our findings also highlighted that participation in nephrology research and congresses, as well as exposure to academic activities, were associated with greater interest in nephrology. Research assistants who indicated they would not choose nephrology cited three main conditions that could change their decision: lighter on-call duties, financial compensation comparable to other subspecialties, and opportunities to attend nephrology congresses during residency. Addressing these reasonable expectations could increase the number of nephrologists in our country.

There are several limitations to our study; the most notable among these are self-reporting bias, the cross-sectional design, and the lack of instrument validation. However, as mentioned earlier, this is the first study to draw attention to this issue in our country.

In conclusion, not only in our country but worldwide, the population of kidney patients is steadily increasing while the number of nephrologists continues to decline. The underlying reasons and potential solutions have been demonstrated in multiple studies. While the current workforce is sufficient for the short term, in the near future, governments may face a serious shortage of physicians capable of managing this growing patient population. This is a critical issue that requires urgent attention.
