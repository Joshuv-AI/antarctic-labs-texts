Gender disparities in random blood glucose levels among Pakistani adults with type 2 diabetes: a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Ruby Khan, Salma Rashid, Sumbal Khan, Bakht Pari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated gender disparities in random blood glucose (RBS) levels among Pakistani adults with Type 2 Diabetes (T2D), examining biological and sociocultural determinants. A cross-sectional analysis of 300 age-matched adults with T2D (150 men, 150 women; age 35–60 years) from four tertiary hospitals in Peshawar, Pakistan (February–July 2023). RBS was measured via the Microlab-300 system (Beer–Lambert Law). Multivariate regression and machine learning models (Ridge Regression, Random Forest, Support Vector Regression (SVR), Neural Network, Polynomial Regression) with nested cross-validation were used to analyze associations between demographic factors and RBS. Women had significantly higher mean RBS than men (243.6 vs. 210.8 mg/dL, p < 0.001) and a higher prevalence of severe hyperglycemia (260 mg/dL: 38.7% vs. 12.0%). Gender alone explained 16.5% of RBS variance in simple linear regression. Age showed a moderate positive correlation with RBS (r = 0.587, p < 0.001). In multivariate analysis, female gender ( = 24.76, p < 0.001), age ( = 3.01 per year, p < 0.001), and BMI ( = 0.88, p = 0.034) were significant predictors, while family history showed a protective effect ( = –13.36, p < 0.001). Machine learning models using only demographic variables achieved moderate predictive performance (R² = 0.421–0.470), with Ridge Regression performing best (R² = 0.470, MAE = 23.68 mg/dL). Feature importance analysis identified age (70.9%), gender (17.8%), and BMI (8.9%) as the dominant predictors. Significant gender disparities exist in random blood glucose among Pakistani adults with T2D, with women exhibiting higher mean values and greater prevalence of severe hyperglycemia. Age, gender, BMI, and family history are important demographic determinants, but demographic factors alone explain less than half of RBS variance. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) represents a growing public health crisis in South Asia, with Pakistan reporting among the highest prevalence rates globally (approximately 19% of adults)1. While global research has established the importance of lifestyle factors, obesity, and aging in T2D pathogenesis2,3, emerging evidence suggests that gender may play a critical modifying role in glycemic control, particularly in culturally conservative regions where sociocultural norms differentially impact men’s and women’s health behaviors4,5.

Gender disparities in diabetes outcomes have been documented across multiple settings, with women often exhibiting poorer glycemic control than men6–8. Proposed mechanisms include biological factors (estrogen effects on insulin sensitivity, differences in body fat distribution) and behavioral factors (dietary patterns, physical activity, healthcare-seeking behaviors). However, the relative contribution of these factors likely varies across cultural contexts. In Pakistan, region-specific practices may exacerbate gender disparities: women often eat after serving family members (potentially consuming leftover, higher-carbohydrate foods); may have limited opportunities for outdoor exercise due to cultural considerations; and may prioritize family health over personal medical care1,9. Despite these observations, few studies have systematically examined gender differences in glycemic control among Pakistani adults with T2D.

The present study addresses this gap by investigating gender disparities in random blood glucose (RBS) levels among adults with T2D in Peshawar, Pakistan. Specifically, we aimed to: (1) quantify differences in mean RBS and prevalence of severe hyperglycemia between men and women; (2) examine the contribution of demographic factors (age, BMI, family history, socioeconomic status, physical activity) to observed disparities; and (3) evaluate whether machine learning approaches could improve prediction of RBS using demographic variables alone. By focusing on an underrepresented region and explicitly considering cultural factors, this study provides region-specific evidence to inform gender-sensitive diabetes management strategies in South Asia.

The scope of this work is limited to the district of Peshawar and focuses on the adult population aged 35–60 years. We acknowledge that findings may not be directly generalizable to other regions of Pakistan or South Asia due to significant heterogeneity in dietary practices, socioeconomic conditions, healthcare access, and cultural norms across different areas. Rather than claiming broad generalizability, this study aims to provide region-specific insights that can inform local interventions and serve as a comparison for studies in other South Asian populations.

Literature review

Global overview of type 2 diabetes

The global rise of Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) is a well-documented phenomenon, with significant attention from both clinical and public health researchers. The landmark classification of diabetes by Atkinson et al.2 set the stage for much of the subsequent research. In their seminal work, they established a clear distinction between Type 1 and Type 2 Diabetes, categorizing the latter as a metabolic disorder primarily influenced by lifestyle and environmental factors. This distinction laid the groundwork for epidemiological studies that explored the rapid global rise in T2D cases. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF)10, T2D accounts for over 90% of diabetes cases worldwide, with a projected increase of 51% by 2045 if current trends continue. These statistics underscore the urgency of understanding and addressing the risk factors contributing to this global health crisis.

A considerable body of research has identified key risk factors for T2D, including obesity, aging, and physical inactivity. Chatterjee et al.3 emphasized that the increasing prevalence of these risk factors is largely driven by urbanization and the adoption of Western dietary habits, particularly in developing countries. Their work points to the urgent need for lifestyle interventions, particularly in low- and middle-income regions, to curb the rising incidence of T2D. While this research provides a global perspective, it often lacks the granularity needed to understand how these risk factors vary across different socio-economic and cultural contexts, particularly in South Asia.

South Asian context and the case of Pakistan

Research specific to South Asia reveals additional complexity in the epidemiology of T2D. Studies by Misra and Khurana4 highlight the unique susceptibility of South Asians to T2D, even at lower body mass index (BMI) levels compared to Western populations. This heightened risk has been attributed to a combination of genetic, dietary, and socio-economic factors that distinguish the South Asian population from other ethnic groups. The work of Anjana et al.11 further reinforced these findings by showing that even modest increases in body weight among South Asians significantly elevate their risk for developing T2D. However, while these studies underscore the importance of region-specific research, they tend to generalize findings across South Asia, often failing to account for the heterogeneity within countries like Pakistan.

In Pakistan, the prevalence of T2D has risen sharply, with recent studies showing that nearly 19% of the adult population is affected1. Research by Shera et al.9 identified several region-specific risk factors, including a high prevalence of central obesity, low levels of physical activity, and poor dietary practices. These findings point to significant differences within the population, influenced by socio-economic conditions, urbanization levels, and regional dietary habits. However, most studies have focused on urban centers like Karachi and Lahore, with limited research exploring the dynamics of T2D in semi-urban and rural areas like Peshawar. This represents a critical gap in the literature, as rural populations may exhibit different risk profiles and health outcomes due to differences in lifestyle, access to healthcare, and socioeconomic conditions.

Specific dietary factors contributing to T2D risk in Pakistan include: high consumption of refined carbohydrates (white rice, refined wheat flour in naan/roti); frequent use of ghee (clarified butter) for cooking; limited vegetable intake; consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages, particularly during social gatherings; and traditional sweets (mithai) high in sugar and saturated fat. Socioeconomic factors further compound these dietary patterns and include: rapid urbanization leading to sedentary occupations; limited health literacy, particularly among women; economic constraints limiting access to quality healthcare and diabetes-friendly foods; food insecurity leading to consumption of inexpensive, calorie-dense, nutrient-poor foods; and occupational shifts where manual labor, once more common among lower socioeconomic groups, is decreasing with mechanization. These factors interact with genetic susceptibility, often termed the ’South Asian phenotype’ of higher insulin resistance at lower BMI, to create elevated T2D risk.

These observations highlight that regional risk factors differ from global risk factors in three key ways:

Prevalence differences While obesity is a global risk factor, central obesity (measured by waist-hip ratio) may be a stronger predictor of T2D in South Asians than BMI, which remains the global standard.

Thus, regional risk factors represent the intersection of universal T2D determinants with locally specific contexts that modify their expression and impact. Understanding these intersections is essential for developing effective, culturally appropriate prevention and management strategies.

Genetic and environmental interactions

Beyond lifestyle factors, genetic predisposition plays a significant role in the development of T2D. The study by Herder et al.12 provided compelling evidence that individuals with a family history of diabetes are more likely to develop the disease, particularly when combined with lifestyle risk factors such as obesity and physical inactivity. However, the interplay between genetics and environmental factors in specific populations remains underexplored. In Pakistan, where consanguinity rates are high, there is a pressing need to understand how genetic predispositions interact with lifestyle and environmental factors to contribute to the rising incidence of T2D. The current study seeks to address this gap by examining the combined influence of genetic, lifestyle, and socio-economic factors in the district of Peshawar.

Gaps in the existing literature

While the global literature on T2D provides a robust foundation for understanding the disease’s risk factors, significant gaps remain, particularly in the context of region-specific studies. As noted by Basit et al.1, much of the existing research in Pakistan has focused on urban centers, neglecting the unique challenges faced by semi-urban and rural populations. Moreover, there is a lack of comprehensive studies that examine the interaction between socioeconomic status, genetics, and lifestyle in shaping T2D risk. Existing studies tend to generalize findings across populations, often overlooking cultural and environmental factors that may significantly influence disease prevalence in regions like Peshawar. By focusing on this under-researched area, the present study aims to provide a more nuanced understanding of how local conditions contribute to the growing burden of T2D.

Contribution to the field

This study will contribute to the existing body of knowledge by providing region-specific data on the prevalence and risk factors associated with T2D in the district of Peshawar. In doing so, it will fill a critical gap in the literature, offering insights that can inform public health policies and intervention strategies tailored to the local population. Additionally, by exploring the interaction between genetic, socio-economic, and lifestyle factors, this research will offer a more holistic understanding of T2D in low- and middle-income countries, where the disease is becoming increasingly prevalent.

Methodology

Study location

The study was conducted at four prominent healthcare institutions in Peshawar, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan: Hayatabad Medical Complex, Nasrullah Babar Teaching Hospital, Lady Reading Hospital, and Khyber Teaching Hospital. These institutions were selected due to their advanced medical facilities, experienced staff, and diverse patient populations, ensuring a representative sample for investigating diabetes prevalence and risk factors.

Study duration

The research was carried out over a period of six months, from February 2023 to July 2023. This extended duration allowed for comprehensive participant recruitment, data collection, and analysis, ensuring a robust exploration of the research objectives.

Estimation of blood metabolites

Random blood glucose (RBS) was measured from venous samples using the Microlab-300 clinical chemistry analyzer (Germany). The normal reference range was defined as 70–160 mg/dL, in accordance with standard clinical guidelines.

A single venous RBS measurement was obtained per participant at the time of enrollment. While this approach reflects the pragmatic reality of diabetes care in many low-resource clinical settings, where access to continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) or even HbA1c may be limited,it also introduces known limitations. Random glucose levels exhibit substantial intra-individual variability due to prandial state, recent physical activity, and acute stress13. Consequently, a single measurement provides only a snapshot and may not capture an individual’s typical glycemic control.

Importantly, the fact that significant gender disparities (p < 0.001) were detected despite this inherent measurement noise underscores the robustness of the observed differences. The unexplained variance in our models ( 53%) can be partially attributed to this variability, in addition to unmeasured physiological and behavioral factors. This study therefore establishes a lower bound for the true magnitude of gender disparities; more precise measures such as HbA1c or CGM-derived metrics (time-in-range, glycemic variability) would likely reveal even larger or more nuanced differences. Our findings provide a strong empirical justification for prioritizing advanced glycemic monitoring in future South Asian diabetes cohorts.

Recognizing that random blood glucose measurements are influenced by prandial state, fasting status (fasting 8 hours vs. non-fasting) was recorded for all participants at the time of blood draw. All regression models described above were adjusted for fasting status as a binary covariate to account for its potential confounding effect.

Data quality control

Missing data, comprising 3.7% of total observations, were addressed through multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) with five imputations. Continuous variables employed predictive mean matching, while categorical variables used logistic regression imputation. For outlier detection, we implemented a two‑stage process combining Mahalanobis distance thresholding () with robust principal component analysis, followed by Winsorization at the 2.5% and 97.5% percentiles to mitigate the influence of extreme values while preserving data structure.

Discussion

Summary of key findings

This cross-sectional study of 300 Pakistani adults with type 2 diabetes (150 men, 150 women) recruited from four tertiary hospitals in Peshawar revealed significant gender disparities in random blood glucose (RBS) levels. Women exhibited substantially higher mean RBS than men (243.6 vs 210.8 mg/dL, ). The prevalence of severe hyperglycemia ( mg/dL) was markedly higher in women (38.7% vs 12.0%), while a greater proportion of men fell within the moderate hyperglycemia category (80.7% vs 60.0%). Age showed a moderate positive correlation with RBS (, ), and this effect remained significant in multivariate models ( per year, ). Machine learning models incorporating demographic variables achieved moderate predictive performance; Ridge Regression yielded the highest coefficient of determination () and lowest mean absolute error (23.68 mg/dL) in nested cross-validation. Feature importance analysis identified age (70.9%), gender (17.8%), and BMI (8.9%) as the dominant predictors.

Gender differences and potential mechanisms

The observation that women have significantly higher mean RBS and a threefold higher prevalence of severe hyperglycemia warrants explanation. As outlined, several interconnected factors may contribute:

Biological factors Estrogen influences hepatic glucose production and insulin sensitivity, potentially creating a U‑shaped glucose distribution in women6,7. Differences in body fat distribution (gynoid vs android) and unrecorded menstrual cycle phase at blood draw may also affect glucose levels.

Behavioral and cultural factors While our study did not directly measure behavioral or cultural practices, we hypothesize that several gender-specific factors documented in the broader literature may contribute to the observed disparities. These include: differential dietary patterns (e.g., women potentially eating after family members, possibly consuming leftover foods)5; gender-specific physical activity norms (e.g., lower rates of outdoor exercise among women due to cultural considerations)4; and healthcare-seeking behaviors (e.g., women potentially prioritizing family health over personal care). These mechanisms are speculative and require direct investigation through studies incorporating detailed dietary assessments, accelerometer-based physical activity measurement, and qualitative interviews to understand healthcare-seeking patterns.

Healthcare factors Differential access to diabetes education, medication adherence patterns, and prioritization of family health over personal care may further modulate these outcomes.

These hypotheses underscore the need for future research integrating detailed behavioural, dietary, and longitudinal data to disentangle the complex interplay of biology and culture in shaping gender disparities (Table 9).

Comparison with previous work

Our findings align with studies demonstrating gender‑specific differences in glycemic control in South Asian populations7,8. The markedly higher prevalence of severe hyperglycemia in women (38.7% vs 12.0%) contrasts with some previous reports that found comparable rates6, highlighting the importance of region‑specific factors. The moderate predictive performance of demographic‑only machine learning models ( up to 0.47) is consistent with the understanding that glucose regulation is a multifactorial process influenced by biomarkers, medication adherence, and lifestyle factors not captured in this study. This reinforces calls for incorporating direct biomarkers (e.g., HbA1c, fasting insulin) and longitudinal assessments in future predictive modeling efforts14.

Study strengths and limitations

Key strengths of this study include its balanced gender representation, rigorous power analysis, and explicit consideration of cultural factors often overlooked in diabetes research. Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged (see the limitation section for a full list). Most notably, the reliance on single‑timepoint RBS measurements precludes assessment of glycemic variability, and the hospital‑based sample may not represent community‑dwelling populations. Unmeasured confounders, including detailed medication adherence, dietary composition, physical activity intensity, and hormonal status, likely contribute to the unexplained variance in our models.

Limitations

While this study provides novel insights into gender disparities in glycemic control among Pakistani adults with type 2 diabetes, several important constraints must be acknowledged. Rather than simply cataloging weaknesses, we contextualize each limitation to clarify how it shapes interpretation of our findings and identifies priorities for future research.

Single RBS Measurement A Pragmatic Choice That Reveals Robust Disparities The use of a single random blood glucose measurement, while consistent with routine clinical practice in resource-limited South Asian settings, inherently limits the precision of our glycemic characterization. RBS exhibits high intra-individual variability influenced by prandial state, time of day, physical activity, and acute stress, with coefficients of variation reportedly ranging from 20–30% in diabetic populations13. Unlike HbA1c which integrates glycemic exposure over 2–3 months or CGM-derived metrics (time-in-range, coefficient of variation, mean amplitude of glycemic excursions), a single RBS cannot distinguish between chronic hyperglycemia and transient fluctuations. This measurement noise likely contributes to the moderate predictive performance of our models (R² 0.47) and may attenuate the true magnitude of gender differences. Crucially, however, this limitation also yields a novel and counterintuitive insight: the detection of highly significant gender disparities (p < 0.001) despite substantial measurement noise argues compellingly for the robustness and clinical irreducibility of these differences. If the true gender effect were small or clinically trivial, it would likely be obscured by the variability inherent in single RBS measurements. That we observed a 32.8 mg/dL unadjusted difference and a threefold higher prevalence of severe hyperglycemia in women, despite this noisy metric, suggests that the underlying biological and sociocultural drivers are powerful enough to overcome substantial measurement error. The unexplained variance () represents an upper bound on the combined contribution of unmeasured factors (insulin resistance, medication adherence, dietary patterns) and measurement error; future studies employing more precise tools will likely reveal even larger effect sizes. Our findings provide the necessary effect size estimates (gender coefficient mg/dL in adjusted models) to power definitive investigations using HbA1c or CGM.

Source code: A Python script (analysis.py) that performs all statistical and machine learning analyses reported in the paper, including: Data preprocessing (normalization, one-hot encoding).
