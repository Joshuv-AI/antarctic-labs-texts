Endometriosis, subclinical cardiac remodeling, and cardiovascular risk: mechanistic insights from cardiac magnetic resonance and mediation analysis
Authors: Yanzhi Feng, Kebing Nie, Mulun Xiao, Xingyu Liu, Tong Wu, Jinjin Zhang, Shixuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndometriosis is a systemic gynecological disorder affecting ~ 10% of reproductive-aged women and shares pathophysiological features with cardiovascular disease (CVD). However, its associations with cardiac remodeling and CVD risk, as well as underlying mechanisms, remain unclear.MethodsThis study utilized data from the UK Biobank, comprising 6,158 women diagnosed with endometriosis and 229,453 women without the condition. To validate our results, we employed a hospital cohort from the Second Affiliated Hospital of the University of South China, which included 612 women with laparoscopically confirmed endometriosis and 612 age-matched controls. We applied multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards models to investigate the association between endometriosis and the risk of CVD. Additionally, generalized linear models were used to evaluate cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) metrics. Mediation analyses were conducted to quantify the contributions of 32 biomarkers, with a particular emphasis on metabolic indices (e.g., triglyceride-glucose (TyG)-related indices, lipid accumulation product (LAP)), inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein (CRP), inflammation score (INFLA)), oxidative stress indicators (urate, bilirubin), and hormonal status markers (sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG)).ResultsOver a median 13-year follow-up, 23,239 CVD events occurred. Endometriosis was associated with increased risks of CVD (HR 1.18, 95% CI 1.09–1.29) and coronary heart disease (CHD) (HR 1.25, 95% CI 1.12–1.40). These findings were robust across multiple sensitivity analyses and consistently replicated in the external Chinese cohort, with an additional significant association observed for stroke (HR: 1.19, 95% CI: 1.03–1.40). CMR imaging revealed subclinical concentric remodeling, including increased interventricular septal thickness (1.11%, 95% CI 0.13–2.11), relative wall mass (1.11%, 95% CI 0.05–2.17), and septal-to-lateral wall thickness ratio (0.64%, 95% CI 0.00–1.29). Mediation analyses identified metabolic dysfunction as the primary pathway, with TyG-related indices (15.4%–17.3%), LAP (11.8%), and triglycerides (8.9%) accounting for the largest proportions, followed by inflammation (CRP: 11.1%; INFLA: 3.7%) and oxidative stress (urate: 6.0%).ConclusionsEndometriosis is associated with increased CVD risk and early subclinical cardiac remodeling, largely mediated by metabolic and inflammatory pathways. These findings underscore the necessity of cardiovascular risk stratification and metabolic monitoring in the clinical management of women with endometriosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12958-026-01564-7.Keywords: Endometriosis, Cardiovascular disease, Coronary heart disease, Stroke, Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, Mediation analysis

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) continues to be the leading cause of mortality among women, accounting for 35% of female deaths globally [1]. In the United States and Europe, CVD results in more deaths among women than the combined impact of breast cancer, lung cancer, and chronic lung disease [1]. The majority of cardiovascular-related deaths in women are attributed to CVD and myocardial infarction (MI), as well as heart failure and stroke [2]. Despite ongoing efforts to raise awareness and improve cardiovascular health among women, progress in mitigating the impact of CVD has stagnated over the past decade [3–5]. Although traditional risk factors contribute to a woman's vulnerability to clinically significant CVD, conventional risk stratification methods, which employ various scoring systems, have proven insufficient in accurately assessing both short-term and long-term or lifetime risk in women [6]. Moreover, emerging research highlights the importance of female-specific conditions and reproductive history in shaping long-term cardiovascular health [7].

Endometriosis is a chronic inflammatory gynecologic condition affecting ~ 10% of women of reproductive age, often characterized by delayed diagnosis and inadequate treatment [8–10]. Traditionally viewed as a pelvic disorder, it is now increasingly recognized as a multisystem disease [11]. Mounting evidence suggests that women with endometriosis may be at greater risk for CVD later in life [7, 9, 12–15]. Further, treatments for endometriosis, such as hormonal medications, hysterectomy, and oophorectomy, may modify CVD risk [9, 16, 17]. Despite growing evidence linking endometriosis to CVD, it remains uncertain whether endometriosis independently associates with adverse cardiac structural and functional phenotypes. Prior investigations have primarily targeted isolated cardiovascular outcomes, often lacking comprehensive scope and mechanistic exploration. Addressing this gap is crucial for developing integrated approaches that consider both reproductive and systemic health in women with endometriosis.

In this study, we leveraged the population-based UK Biobank (UKB) and a hospital-based cohort of laparoscopically confirmed cases to investigate the relationship between endometriosis and CVD. Specifically, our aims were to: 1) systematically examine the prospective associations between endometriosis and incident CVD risk; 2) investigate the associations between endometriosis and subclinical cardiac structural alterations as measured by cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging; and 3) elucidate underlying mechanisms by evaluating the potential mediating roles of metabolic dysfunction, systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and hormonal imbalance in the relationship between endometriosis and CVD.

Methods

Study population

UK Biobank cohort

The UK Biobank is a prospective cohort study that gathered data from more than 500,000 participants aged 40 to 69, sourced from 22 assessment centers across England, Scotland, and Wales. From 2006 to 2010, participants completed a series of questionnaires, underwent diverse physical measurements, and provided biological samples. Participants provided informed consent for linking their data to national records of hospital admissions, cancer diagnoses, and death records. UK Biobank has approval from the North West Multicenter Research Ethics Committee (https://www.ukbiobank. ac.uk/learn-more-about-uk-biobank/about-us/ethics). This study utilizes data from the UK Biobank baseline assessment conducted between 2006 and 2010. Among the 502,128 participants enrolled in the UK Biobank, 273,155 women aged 40 to 69 years with available information on endometriosis were identified. Initially, 36,206 women with a history of coronary heart disease (CHD), myocardial infarction (MI), heart failure (HF), atrial fibrillation (AF), or stroke at baseline were excluded. Subsequently, 1,338 women diagnosed with endometriosis during follow-up were excluded. Ultimately, 235,611 women were included in the main and mediation analyses, among whom 6,158 were diagnosed with endometriosis and 229,453 without endometriosis (Fig. 1).

Study design

External validation hospital cohort

To address potential misclassification of endometriosis in the UK Biobank due to reliance on self-reported and registry-based diagnoses, we conducted an external validation study using a retrospective-prospective hospital-based cohort from the Second Affiliated Hospital of University of South China, a tertiary referral center for gynecologic diseases in Hengyang, China. This hospital-based cohort included women who underwent laparoscopic surgery for suspected endometriosis between January 2008 and December 2012. Eligibility criteria included: (i) women aged ≥ 20 years at the time of diagnosis, (ii) laparoscopically confirmed endometriosis based on intraoperative visual inspection and histopathological examination, and (iii) absence of clinically evident cardiovascular disease prior to laparoscopic confirmation. Women were excluded if they were pregnant, had pathologically or clinically confirmed leiomyoma, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, kidney disease, cancer, or autoimmune disorders, or had previously undergone hysterectomy or oophorectomy. The control group comprised women from the same hospital and time period who had no evidence of endometriosis on transvaginal ultrasound or laparoscopy. Controls were frequency-matched to cases in a 1:1 ratio by age and met the same exclusion criteria as cases. In total, 621 women with laparoscopically confirmed endometriosis were included as cases, and 621 matched controls comprised the comparison group (Figure S1). Participants were followed up until May 2025. Because imaging and biomarker data were unavailable in this cohort, validation was limited to the association between endometriosis and incident cardiovascular events. The hospital-based study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of University of South China (Approval No. 2025006), and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Definition of endometriosis

To maximize case ascertainment, we defined endometriosis using both UK Biobank electronic health records (EHR) diagnoses and self-reported physician diagnoses. Specifically, EHR diagnoses were based on ICD-10 codes N80.x, which are commonly used for identifying endometriosis in large-scale epidemiological studies and have been validated in prior research [18]. Additionally, self-reported cases were obtained during a structured, nurse-led interview at baseline assessment [19]. This dual approach of combining clinical records with self-reported diagnoses has been shown to improve case identification and is consistent with established methodologies in the literature [18]. A total of 6,158 women with endometriosis were identified: 3,627 through health records, 3,384 through self-report, and 853 by both sources.

While laparoscopic confirmation with histological verification remains the diagnostic gold standard [20], such information is not systematically available in population-based cohorts. Previous validation studies have shown that registry- and self-report–based definitions of endometriosis, although less sensitive, have high specificity, and thus are more likely to result in under-ascertainment rather than false-positive misclassification [21]. To strengthen diagnostic validity, we further conducted an external validation study in the Second Affiliated Hospital of University of South China, where all diagnoses were confirmed by laparoscopy and histopathology. This external dataset not only corroborated the accuracy of our case definition but also enabled a sensitivity analysis restricted to laparoscopically confirmed cases, thereby ensuring the robustness of our findings.

Definition of cardiovascular disease

The primary outcome in both the UK Biobank and the hospital-based validation cohort was incident CVD, which includes CHD, MI, HF, AF, and stroke. In the UK Biobank, CVD events were ascertained through linkage to national hospital admission databases in England, Wales, and Scotland, with follow-up from baseline assessment until the first occurrence of a CVD event or October 31, 2022, whichever occurred first. The definition of CVD events, including CHD, MI, HF, AF, and stroke, is based on ICD-10 codes. In the hospital-based cohort, CVD events were identified through electronic medical records and clinical follow-up up to the most recent available date. All events in both cohorts were classified according to ICD-10 codes: CHD (I20–I25, including fatal ischemic heart disease and nonfatal MI I21–I23), AF and related arrhythmias (I48–I49), HF (I50), and stroke encompassing all cerebrovascular diseases (I60–I64, I69) (Table S1). The ICD-10 diagnostic codes for all outcomes have been validated in the UK Biobank and other large cohorts, demonstrating their reliability as diagnostic tools for identifying relevant health conditions [22–25].

CMR outcomes

A detailed CMR protocol and analytical methodology have been previously described [26]. Of the ~ 500,000 original UKB participants, four imaging centers conducted a CMR enhancement study for individuals within travel proximity. By August 2023, over 48,000 participants had undergone CMR imaging, with ~ 40,000 scans included in this analysis, performed on average 9.0 ± 1.7 years after baseline assessment.

The CMR scans were primarily utilized to evaluate cardiac anatomy and biventricular function, offering comprehensive assessments of cardiac structure, function, tissue characteristics, and prognostic markers. Metrics were indexed to body surface area (BSA) and, given BSA's limitations in obesity, also to height^1.7 due to ~ 30% of the UKB cohort having a BMI > 30 kg/m2. The post-processed parameters included left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV), end-systolic volume (LVESV), stroke volume (SV), ejection fraction (LVEF), and myocardial mass. Relative wall mass was defined as the ratio of left ventricular mass to LVEDV. Myocardial volume (MV) was calculated assuming a myocardial density of 1.05 g/mL, and myocardial contraction fraction (MCF) was determined as the ratio of SV to MV. Indexed volumes and mass resulted in the derivation of LVEDV index (LVEDVi), LVESV index (LVESVi), and left ventricular mass index (LVmassi). Wall thickness (WT) across the 16 segments defined by the American Heart Association (AHA) was measured at end-diastole using a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) model. Both mean and maximal WT were computed, alongside interventricular septal WT (segments 2, 3, 8, 9, 14), lateral wall WT (segments 5, 6, 11, 12, 16), and the septal-to-lateral wall thickness ratio (SLWR); an SLWR greater than 1.3 or less than 0.7 was considered indicative of abnormality.

Given the myocardial fiber architecture (oblique, circumferential, longitudinal), LV deformation was assessed via circumferential, radial, and longitudinal strains, both globally and regionally, indexed to LVmassi. Strains were derived from a 3D deformable myocardial model. Regions were defined as basal (segments 1–6), mid (7–12), and apical (13–16); however, mid/apical longitudinal strain data were unavailable at the time of analysis. Absolute strain values were used, with lower values indicating impaired myocardial mechanics.

Peripheral biomarkers

Biological samples from participants were collected at the assessment center using 4 mL EDTA vacuum containers. These samples were subsequently stored at 4 °C and transported overnight to the UK Biocentre in Stockport, UK, utilizing a temperature-controlled container to ensure sample integrity. Hematological analyses of whole blood were conducted using the Beckman Coulter LH750 System hematology analyzers to quantify hemocytes. At baseline, twenty biochemical markers in serum were selected for assay using immunoassay analyzers (DiaSorin Liaison XL and Beckman Coulter DXI800) and clinical chemistry analyzers (Beckman Coulter AU5800 and Siemens Advia1800).

For further analysis, we categorized blood biomarkers into four groups: metabolic disorders, inflammation, hormones, and oxidative stress. The metabolic biomarkers included triglycerides (TG), total bilirubin (TBIL), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), total cholesterol (TC), the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG), TyG-body mass index (TyG-BMI), TyG-waist circumference (TyG-WC), waist-to-height ratio (WtHR), and lipid accumulation product (LAP). Composite indices were calculated based on established formulas: TyG = ln [triglycerides (mg/dL) × fasting blood glucose (mg/dL)/2], TyG-BMI = TyG × BMI (kg/m2), TyG-WtHR = TyG × waist-to-height ratio, TyG-WC = TyG × waist circumference (cm), LAP = (waist circumference [cm] – 58) × triglycerides (mmol/L). For inflammatory biomarkers, except for single indicators, i.e., C-reactive protein (CRP), counts and percentages of leukocyte, lymphocyte, monocyte, neutrophil, and platelet, five systemic inflammation biomarkers were considered and calculated as follows: neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) = neutrophils/lymphocytes, monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) = lymphocytes/monocytes, platelet to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) = platelets/lymphocytes, systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) = (neutrophils * platelets)/lymphocytes, systemic inflammation response index (SIRI) = (neutrophils * monocyte)/lymphocytes, and the low-grade inflammation score (INFLA-score). The INFLA-score integrates four key inflammatory biomarkers, including WBC count, PLT count, CRP, and NLR. Each biomarker was categorized into deciles, and scored from –4 to + 4 based on its distribution: values in the lowest four deciles received scores from –4 to –1, while values in the highest four deciles were assigned scores from + 1 to + 4. The INFLA-score was then calculated by summing the scores of the four biomarkers, yielding a total score ranging from –16 to + 16, with higher values indicating greater levels of low-grade inflammation. Hormones consisted of sex hormone binding globulin (SHBG). The urate, bilirubin, albumin, and oxidative balance score (OBS) as alternative biomarkers of oxidative stress, were also included in our analysis. Details for calculating the OBS are provided in the Supplementary Methods (Table S2).

Measurement of covariates

UK Biobank cohort

We included the following factors in the analyses as covariates according to evidence from previous studies [9, 27]: (1) Sociodemographic variables assessed at baseline, including age, ethnicity, educational level, and the Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI); (2) Lifestyle factors, such as alcohol consumption, smoking status, physical activity level, and a composite healthy diet score; (3) CVD risk factors, including the use of relevant medications (i.e., antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, glucose-lowering agents, and aspirin); (4) Reproductive and hormonal factors, including age at menarche, use of oral contraceptives (OC) and hormone replacement therapy (HRT). The TDI is a composite measure based on unemployment, non-car ownership, non-home ownership, and household overcrowding, with negative values indicating higher socioeconomic status.

Self-reported information on smoking status and alcohol intake was collected. Smoking status was categorized as never, former, and current smokers. Alcohol intake was categorized into the following four groups: never, former, < 1 drink/week, 1–2 drinks/week, and ≥ 3 drinks/week. Physical activity was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire and categorized as low (< 600 metabolic equiva lent task/min per week), moderate (≥ 600 metabolic equiva lent task/min per week), or high (≥ 3000 metabolic equiva lent task/min per week metabolic equiva lent task/min per week) [22]. A healthy diet score was established based on total fruit intake (≥ 4.5 pieces per week), total vegetable intake (≥ 4.5 servings per week), total fish intake (≥ 2 servings per week), processed meat intake (< twice per week), and red meat intake (≤ 5 times per week) [28]. Each favorable diet factor was coded as 1 and unfavorable diet factor was coded as 0, and the total diet score was calculated by summing the individual scores of the five factors, with a range of 0–5. Further details regarding covariates are provided in Table S2.

External validation hospital cohort

Sociodemographic information was obtained from medical records and patient interviews. Lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol, physical activity, diet) were collected via standardized questionnaires administered at baseline. Medication use and reproductive factors (age at menarche, contraceptive or hormone therapy use) were abstracted from medical records. Measurement categories were harmonized as closely as possible with the UKB cohort.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD or median with interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. To achieve normality and minimize heteroscedasticity, all continuous variables underwent log-transformation and subsequent Z-score standardization. Multiple testing was rigorously controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method.

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between endometriosis and incident CVD. We implemented a hierarchical modeling strategy: 1) Model 1: Adjusted for age at baseline; 2) Model 2: Further adjusted for Townsend deprivation index, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and healthy diet score; 3) Model 3: Further incorporated reproductive history, including age at menarche, and use of oral contraceptives (OC) and hormone replacement therapy (HRT). The proportional hazards assumption was verified via Kolmogorov-type supremum tests, with no violations observed (all P > 0.05).

To strengthen our findings, we conducted five sensitivity analyses: 1) Excluding early-incident cases (< 5 years of follow-up) to mitigate potential reverse causation; 2) restricting case definition to ICD-10 codes only to reduce misclassification; 3) adjusting for menopausal status to account for reproductive aging; 4) excluding participants with prior hysterectomy or oophorectomy to isolate the effect of surgically induced hormonal shifts; 5) cross-geographic validation in an independent, international hospital-based cohort (outside of the UK) using only laparoscopically confirmed cases to ensure maximum diagnostic precision.

Given the right-skewed distribution of CMR metrics, generalized linear models (GLM) with a gamma distribution and log link were utilized to evaluate cardiac structural and functional phenotypes. For mediation analysis, we employed a causal mediation framework using the R package “mediation” to quantify the proportions of the endometriosis-CVD association explained by identified biomarkers. Significance was determined via non-parametric bootstrapping with 5,000 iterations, adjusting for all aforementioned covariates. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.3).

Results

Participant characteristics

Tables 1 and S4 summarize the baseline characteristics of participants from the UK-based Biobank cohort and the independent China-based hospital cohort, respectively. In the UK Biobank cohort (n = 235,611; mean age 55.7 years), women with endometriosis were younger and less likely to have a college education. Also, they exhibited a slightly elevated prevalence of current smoking, an increase in low to moderate alcohol consumption, and diminished levels of physical activity (all P < 0.05). Moreover, these women experienced menarche at an earlier age and more frequently utilized oral contraceptives and hormone replacement therapy (all P < 0.05).

In the hospital-based cohort (n = 1,224; 612 cases and 612 controls; mean age 42.3 years), women with laparoscopically confirmed endometriosis similarly had lower college education rates (34.6% vs. 40.0%, P = 0.025), engaged in less physical activity (low activity: 49.0% vs. 43.1%, P < 0.001), experienced earlier menarche (P = 0.004), and more frequently used oral contraceptives (74.7% vs. 66.0%, P = 0.001) and HRT (4.2% vs. 2.0%, P = 0.030). Prospective association between endometriosis and CVD risk

During a median follow-up of 13.0 years, women with endometriosis had higher risk of CVD than those without endometriosis after fully adjustment for age, ethnicity, education level, Townsend deprivation index, alcohol consumption, smoking status, physical activity, healthy diet score, age at menarche, OC and HRT use (HR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.09–1.29; Fig. 2A). With further in-depth analyses examining subtypes of cardiovascular outcomes, endometriosis was associated with an increased risk of CHD (HR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.12–1.40; Fig. 2A). However, no significant associations were observed for myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, or stroke after full adjustment (Fig. 2A). Sensitivity analyses, such as excluding participants with less than 5 years of follow-up, focusing on ICD-10 endometriosis diagnoses, and adjusting for menopausal status, showed no significant change in associations (Figs. S2-S4). Even after excluding women with previous hysterectomy or oophorectomy, endometriosis was still significantly linked to heart failure (HR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.01–2.03; Fig. S5). Similarly, in the hospital-based external validation cohort of laparoscopically confirmed cases, endometriosis was associated with higher risks of composite CVD (HR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.08–1.36; Fig. S6) and CHD (HR = 1.25, 95% CI: 1.08–1.40; Fig. S6). While associations with myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, and heart failure were not significant, stroke risk remained elevated (HR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.03–1.40; Fig. S6), highlighting the consistency of key cardiovascular findings across both cohorts.

Associations between endometriosis and the CMR phenotypes

To further explore the cardiovascular effects of endometriosis, we used CMR imaging to compare cardiac structure and function. In the fully adjusted model, participants with endometriosis had significantly greater IVST (% difference = 1.11, 95% CI: 0.13–2.11; Fig. 3 and Table S5), LV RWM (% difference = 1.11, 95% CI: 0.05–2.17; Fig. 3 and Table S5), and SLWR (% difference = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.00–1.29; Fig. 3 and Table S5). In addition, LV (% difference = 0.82, 95% CI: −0.04–1.68; Fig. 3 and Table S5) was marginally elevated in the endometriosis group, suggesting a possible trend toward concentric remodeling.

Potential mechanism contributing to the associations between endometriosis and CVD risk

A linear regression model revealed significant links between endometriosis and various biomarkers, including 11 metabolic, 16 inflammatory, 4 oxidative stress, and 1 hormonal (Table S6). Endometriosis was significantly associated with a pro-atherogenic metabolic profile. Specifically, significant positive associations were observed for triglycerides (β: 0.14, 95%CI: 0.11–0.16), TyG indices (β: 0.11–0.13, 95%CI: 0.08–0.16), LAP (β: 0.11, 95%CI: 0.08–0.13), and LDL-C (β: 0.09, 95%CI: 0.06–0.11). Conversely, HDL-C (β: −0.07, 95%CI: −0.09–−0.04) and total bilirubin (β: −0.05, 95%CI: −0.07–−0.02) showed a significant inverse relationship. The inflammatory markers were also markedly elevated in women with endometriosis, as evidenced by higher levels of CRP (β: 0.11, 95%CI: 0.09–0.14), lymphocyte count (β: 0.04, 95%CI: 0.01–0.06), and the composite INFLA score (β: 0.04, 95%CI: 0.02–0.07). Regarding oxidative stress and hormonal pathways, endometriosis was positively linked to urate concentration (β: 0.07, 95%CI: 0.04–0.10), while significantly lower levels of DBIL (β: −0.06, 95%CI: −0.09–−0.03) and SHBG (β: −0.05, 95%CI: −0.07–−0.02) were observed.

We further used the Cox proportional hazards model to examine the relationship between peripheral biomarkers and CVD risk (Tables S7-S8). Metabolic dysfunction emerged as a robust predictor of adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Specifically, each SD increment in TyG indices (CVD: HR: 1.13–1.28, 95%CI: 1.12–1.30; CHD: HR: 1.26–1.34, 95%CI: 1.23–1.37) and LAP (CVD: HR: 1.17, 95CI%: 1.16–1.18; CHD: HR: 1.36, 95%CI: 1.33–1.39) was associated with a substantially higher risk of CVD and CHD. Similarly, elevations in triglycerides (CVD: HR: 1.11, 95%CI: 1.10–1.13; CHD: HR: 1.23, 95%CI: 1.21–1.26) and FPG (CVD: HR: 1.09, 95%CI: 1.07–1.10; CHD: HR: 1.12, 95%CI: 1.10–1.14) conferred increased risks for both outcomes. In terms of the lipid profile, HDL-C exhibited a consistent protective association (CVD: HR 0.86, 95% CI: 0.85–0.88; CHD: HR 0.80, 95% CI: 0.78–0.82), while higher LDL-C (CHD: HR: 1.08, 95%CI: 1.06–1.10) and total cholesterol (CHD: HR: 1.04, 95%CI: 1.02–1.06) were specifically linked to increased CHD risk. For inflammatory markers, CRP (HR 1.20, 95% CI: 1.18–1.21) and the composite INFLA score (HR 1.16, 95% CI: 1.15–1.18) showed potent associations, with each SD increase corresponding to a 20% and 16% higher risk of incident CVD, respectively. Furthermore, a spectrum of leukocyte-derived indices, including SIRI (HR: 1.13, 95%CI: 1.11–1.14), NLR (HR: 1.09, 95%CI: 1.08–1.11), and SII (HR: 1.07, 95%CI: 1.06–1.09), consistently signaled increased cardiovascular vulnerability. similar markers were linked to CHD. Conversely, a higher lymphocyte percentage (CVD: HR: 0.92, 95%CI: 0.91–0.93; CHD: HR: 0.93, 95%CI: 0.92–0.95) was associated with reduced risks of both CVD and CHD. In addition, markers of oxidative stress and hormonal status also provided significant prognostic value. Urate levels were positively correlated with increased CVD (HR: 1.17, 95%CI: 1.15–1.18) and CHD (HR: 1.19, 95%CI: 1.17–1.22) risk. In contrast, serum albumin (CVD: HR: 0.91, 95%CI: 0.90–0.93) and SHBG (CVD: HR: 0.93, 95%CI: 0.91–0.94; CHD: HR: 0.86, 95%CI: 0.84–0.88) appeared to be independent protective factors.

Mediation analyses showed that the association between endometriosis and cardiovascular outcomes was primarily driven by metabolic pathways, with additional contributions from inflammation, oxidative stress, and hormonal factors (Fig. 4 and Tables S9-S10). For incident CVD, TyG-related indices were the dominant mediators, including TyG-WC (17.3%), TyG-BMI (17.2%), and TyG-WHtR (15.4%), followed by LAP (11.8%) and triglycerides (8.9%), while HDL-C showed an inverse effect (5.6%). Inflammatory markers, particularly CRP (11.1%) and the INFLA score (3.7%), also contributed, along with oxidative stress markers such as urate (6.0%). Similar patterns were observed for CHD, with metabolic markers again accounting for the largest mediation proportions (e.g., TyG-WC 13.5%, CVAI 13.4%, TyG-WHtR 13.1%). CRP (9.0%) and INFLA score (3.3%) remained key inflammatory mediators, while urate (5.0%) and SHBG (2.8%) contributed to oxidative stress and hormonal pathways, respectively.

Discussion

Principal findings

In this large population-based analysis with external validation in an independent hospital cohort from China, endometriosis was associated with an elevated risk of cardiovascular disease, particularly coronary heart disease. These associations were robust across multiple sensitivity analyses and were consistently replicated in a laparoscopically confirmed cohort outside the UK Biobank. Beyond clinical outcomes, our study provides novel evidence via cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging that women with endometriosis exhibit distinct subclinical phenotypes of concentric cardiac remodeling, characterized by increased interventricular septal thickness and relative wall mass. Furthermore, integrative mediation analyses identified metabolic dysfunction as the predominant pathway linking endometriosis to cardiovascular risk, with additional contributions from inflammatory and oxidative stress processes, providing mechanistic insight into this association.

Interpretation of study findings and comparison with existing literature

To date, a limited number of observational studies have examined the relationship between endometriosis and CVD, often constrained by small sample sizes, inconsistent definitions of CVD, and inadequate adjustment for key confounding variables. For example, Mu et al. (2016) identified an increased risk of CHD, primarily mediated by hysterectomy and oophorectomy; however, their analysis did not account for comorbidities and hormone therapy [17]. Subsequent studies associated endometriosis with stroke and arrhythmias, but their findings were limited by heterogeneous definitions of CVD and insufficient control of confounding factors [29]. A recent study corroborated increased risks of myocardial infarction, stroke, arrhythmia, and heart failure, yet was limited by its focus on a predominantly Danish population [7, 9]. Our study addresses key limitations of prior research by integrating a large population-based cohort with an external hospital-based cohort comprising laparoscopically confirmed cases, while comprehensively adjusting for sociodemographic, lifestyle, and reproductive factors. Across both settings, endometriosis was consistently associated with an increased risk of CVD, particularly coronary heart disease, independent of major confounders, with evidence suggesting that cardiovascular medications may partially attenuate this risk.

However, results were not fully consistent across cardiovascular subtypes. In the UK Biobank cohort, no significant associations were observed between endometriosis and myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, or stroke after full adjustment, whereas in the external Chinese hospital-based cohort, endometriosis was associated with a higher risk of stroke. Several factors may explain these differences. First, differences in case ascertainment across cohorts may have contributed to heterogeneity in the observed associations. The UK Biobank relies primarily on registry-based ICD codes and self-reported diagnoses, which may be subject to misclassification, whereas the hospital-based cohort included laparoscopically confirmed endometriosis and clinically adjudicated outcomes, potentially reducing diagnostic uncertainty. Second, limited statistical power in the UK Biobank for relatively rare outcomes such as myocardial infarction and stroke, compared with large-scale cohort studies such as the Nurses’ Health Study II [9], may have reduced the ability to detect modest associations. Third, differences in study populations and case definitions may also contribute to heterogeneity in effect estimates. In particular, the stricter clinical definition of endometriosis in the hospital-based cohort may have identified a subgroup with different baseline risk profiles. Finally, although adjustment for a wide range of potential confounders was applied, residual confounding and differences in covariate distributions between cohorts cannot be excluded.

Utilizing CMR, the gold standard imaging modality for evaluating cardiac structure and function, our study identified an independent association between endometriosis and an adverse cardiac remodeling pattern. Notably, we observed significant increases in IVST and RWM, alongside a marginal elevation in SLWR, collectively indicating the presence of subclinical concentric remodeling. In the context of endometriosis, this early structural adaptation likely represents a maladaptive response to chronic low-grade systemic inflammation and cyclic oxidative stress [30]. Persistent inflammatory signaling can induce cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and extracellular matrix deposition, which are precursors to concentric hypertrophy [31, 32]. Clinically, such remodeling is not benign. Increased RWM and IVST are recognized markers of enhanced ventricular stiffness and impaired diastolic filling [17, 33–36]. This subclinical evolution of cardiac geometry serves as a critical mechanistic link to long-term cardiovascular risks, reflecting the role of concentric remodeling as a predictive marker for myocardial ischemia and eventual heart failure [37–39]. By identifying these early phenotypes, our study underscores that the cardiovascular impact of endometriosis initiates far before the onset of symptomatic heart disease, suggesting that cardiac structure may be an important surrogate endpoint for early intervention.

While endometriosis has been linked to CVD, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. This study explores four potential mediators, including metabolism, inflammation, hormonal factors, and oxidative stress, highlighting metabolic dysfunction, particularly through TyG indices and the LAP, as key pathways connecting endometriosis to increased cardiovascular risk. Our findings align with growing clinical evidence suggesting that endometriosis is a systemic disorder, characterized by adverse metabolic profiles including type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, hypercholesterolemia, and hypertension [33, 40–43]. We propose that chronic low-grade inflammation in endometriosis may contribute to peripheral insulin resistance and impaired glucose metabolism, with TyG and LAP potentially serving as surrogates for subclinical metabolic inflexibility [41, 44–47]. Moreover, the atherogenic lipid profile in our cohort, marked by elevated triglycerides and low HDL-C, reflects the inflammatory environment of endometriosis, which can promote endothelial dysfunction and accelerate early atherogenesis [48–51]. Collectively, these metabolic shifts establish a potent mechanistic bridge between endometriosis and incident coronary heart disease by promoting endothelial dysfunction and early plaque formation.

Beyond metabolic pathways, our study reaffirms that endometriosis acts as a systemic inflammatory condition rather than a confined pelvic disorder. The significant mediation by CRP and the INFLA score suggest that persistent systemic inflammation, already known to precipitate endothelial dysfunction and atherogenesis [52–54], acts as a continuous stimulus for cardiac remodeling. This inflammatory signaling may directly induce cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and extracellular matrix deposition, as evidenced by our CMR findings of increased relative wall mass. Concurrently, oxidative stress markers, such as urate, offer a more nuanced landscape of the endometriosis-CVD axis. Beyond serving as metabolic byproducts, these markers signify a state of systemic oxidative imbalance that can directly impair vascular integrity and cardiomyocyte function [55, 56].

Clinical and research implications

Our findings highlight the importance of managing and preventing CVD in women with endometriosis. The connection between endometriosis and increased CVD risk, particularly CHD, calls for enhanced cardiovascular monitoring. Early risk assessments using metabolic, inflammatory, and oxidative stress markers can identify high-risk individuals for targeted prevention. Furthermore, the subclinical myocardial remodeling captured by CMR highlights the critical role of non-invasive imaging in detecting early cardiac phenotypes. Beyond gynecological care, therapeutic strategies focusing on metabolic optimization and anti-inflammatory interventions are warranted to mitigate the long-term cardiovascular burden in this population.

Strengths and limitations

This study combines a large population-based cohort from the UK with an independent, international hospital-based validation cohort recruited in China, integrating cardiac magnetic resonance imaging and peripheral biomarkers, to comprehensively evaluate associations and potential mechanisms linking endometriosis with cardiovascular remodeling and disease risk. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, despite using both self-reported and registry-based diagnoses in the UK Biobank, misclassification of endometriosis remains possible, potentially attenuating the observed associations; we mitigated this concern by conducting external validation with laparoscopically confirmed cases. Second, the hospital-based validation cohort only included clinical cardiovascular outcomes and lacked imaging and biomarker data, limiting validation to the association between endometriosis and incident cardiovascular events. Additionally, this cohort was smaller, which reduced statistical power for some cardiovascular outcomes and subgroup analyses. Third, although multiple sociodemographic, lifestyle, reproductive, and cardiovascular risk factors were adjusted for, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded, particularly for unmeasured factors such as disease severity, long-term medication adherence, or other dietary components beyond flavonoids. Finally, the generalizability of our findings may be restricted to populations similar to those in the UK Biobank or the hospital-based cohort, given differences in age, ethnicity, and healthcare access.

Conclusions

This large population-based study with external validation provides compelling evidence that endometriosis is not just a localized gynecological disorder, but a systemic condition that increases cardiovascular risk, particularly for coronary heart disease. Our findings, supported by CMR imaging, reveal a distinct subclinical phenotype of concentric cardiac remodeling in women with endometriosis, highlighting early cardiac changes before the onset of overt cardiovascular disease. We also identify metabolic dysfunction, chronic low-grade inflammation, and oxidative stress as key mechanisms linking endometriosis to adverse cardiovascular outcomes. These findings underscore the importance of recognizing endometriosis as a systemic precursor to cardiovascular risk. Implementing targeted preventive strategies, including metabolic monitoring and early cardiovascular risk assessment, is crucial to mitigating the long-term cardiovascular burden in women with endometriosis.
