Deep eutectic solvent extraction and AB-8 resin adsorption for rambutan shell polyphenol: Profiling and bioactivity assessments
Authors: Xiaonan Bian, Chaozheng Wang, Jiong Mei, Zhiheng Zhao, Kaijie Shang, Xianquan Zhong, Kun Cai, Weimin Zhang, Xiaoping Hu, Xue Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rambutan shell, a major agro-industrial by-product, is a promising source of bioactive polyphenols. This study aimed to optimize polyphenol extraction from the rambutan shell of Baoyan NO.7 (BY-7) using deep eutectic solvent (DES) and to purify the extract with AB-8 resin. The adsorption mechanism was elucidated, and the polyphenol profile was characterized via next-generation metabolomics (NGM). The optimized extract showed high total phenolic (178.89 mg GAE/g), flavonoid (152.93 mg RE/g), and proanthocyanidin (41.89 mg CE/g) contents, along with potent antioxidant (DPPH: 7061.49, FRAP: 6925.34 μmol TE/g) and anti-glycemic activities (α-amylase IC50: 2.62 mg/mL; α-glucosidase IC50: 70.78 μg/mL). Adsorption followed Langmuir and pseudo-second-order kinetic models, indicating a spontaneous process. To the best of our knowledge, proanthocyanidin B1 was identified in the rambutan shell of BY-7 for the first time. Significant correlations were found between 11 polyphenols and the bioactivities. This work provides a theoretical foundation for valorizing rambutan shell resources.

Introduction

Rambutan (Nephelium lappaceum L.), a tropical fruit of the Sapindaceae family, is widely cultivated in Southeast Asia, with a global production reaching 2.5 million tons in 2018 (Mota et al., 2020). The yellow-shell cultivar Baoyan NO.2 (BY-2) and red-shell cultivars BY-4, BY-5, BY-6, and BY-7 are dominant in the Chinese market, while BY-7 features the highest sweetness, superior public recognition, and the largest cultivation scale (Deng et al., 2023; Tahir et al., 2026). Rambutan is primarily consumed fresh but is also processed into products like wine, canned goods, and juice (Afzaal et al., 2023). This processing generates a significant amount of waste, as the inedible shell, which constitutes 48%–55% of the fruit's total weight, is typically discarded (Hernández-Hernández et al., 2019; Solís-Fuentes et al., 2010). However, this by-product is not merely waste; it contains valuable components such as cellulose, lignin, hemicellulose, and bioactive compounds like polyphenols and anthocyanins, as well as essential minerals including Na, Cu, Mn, Fe, Zn, and Mg (Ma et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2022).

The utilization of such plant by-products holds immense economic potential, driven by the potent bioactivity and safety of their extracts for applications in food, health supplements, and cosmetics (Chai et al., 2019; Mota et al., 2020). Currently, the extraction of bioactive compounds predominantly employs conventional methodologies involving organic solvents, water, or more advanced techniques like ionic liquids, typically enhanced by ultrasound, microwave, or enzyme-assisted pretreatments (Nguyen, Nguyen, et al., 2024; Nguyen, Phan, et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2024; Thitilertdecha et al., 2010; Wang, Liu, et al., 2024; Wang, Zhu, et al., 2024). However, organic solvents (e.g., petroleum ether, hexane) exhibit high volatility, toxicity, and poor biodegradability, posing significant threats to ecosystems and operational personnel. Furthermore, seemingly eco-friendly alternatives frequently necessitate harsh operational conditions (e.g., high temperature and long time) and substantial equipment investments, consequently escalating process complexity and production costs (Ozel & Eliboll, 2024). In response, deep eutectic solvents (DES) are a sustainable alternative, synthesized through hydrogen-bonding interactions between two or three safe, economical, and biodegradable components with melting point lower than those of their individual constituents (Abbott et al., 2004; Ozel & Eliboll, 2024; Xu et al., 2019). DES also demonstrate thermal stability, non-volatility, and non-flammability, presenting a viable eco-friendly alternative for extraction processes (Abbott et al., 2004; Fu et al., 2021). DES has also been widely used in the treatment of heavy metal pollution, pharmaceutical separation and purification, and the detection of trace environmental pollutant. For example, hydrophobic DESs demonstrated outstanding removal performance for hexavalent chromium (Cr(VI)), offering a sustainable solution for industrial wastewater treatment (Huynh et al., 2025; Huynh et al., 2026); DES-based aqueous two-phase systems have emerged as a promising green separation technology for pharmaceutical ingredients (Vessally & Rzayev, 2024); DESs coupled with different chromatography-mass spectrometry techniques enabled rapid, sensitive and green determination for key components or pesticide residues in complex food matrices (Dung et al., 2025; Nguyen, Nguyen, et al., 2024; Nguyen, Phan, et al., 2024).

Extensive studies have explored the biological activities and polyphenolic constituents of polyphenol extracts (PE) from rambutan shell (RS). Both aqueous and ethanolic extracts have demonstrated potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-glycemic, anti-osteoporotic, and anti-photo-aging properties (Mota et al., 2020; Palanisamy et al., 2011; Phuong et al., 2020; Zhuang et al., 2020). Moreover, the 80% ethanol extract exhibited broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pyogenes, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Salmonella enterica (Asghar et al., 2021). Through high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) or liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analyses, various polyphenolic compounds were identified in RS polyphenol extract (RSPE), including ellagic acid, gallic acid, corilagin, catechin, (−)-epicatechin, 3-O-gallate epicatechin, quercetin, and rutin (Hernández-Hernández et al., 2019; Phuong et al., 2020).

However, critical knowledge gaps persist regarding the optimization of DES -based extraction processes for polyphenol from RS, as well as the adsorption mechanisms of RSPE on AB-8 macroporous resin. Notably, the polyphenol profile of RSPE obtained via this extraction method remains uncharacterized using new generation metabolomics (NGM) technique. To address these gaps, this study systematically: (1) screened optimal DES formulations; (2) optimized extraction parameters through one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT) experiments, factorial screening experiments, and response surface methodology (RSM); (3) elucidated adsorption mechanisms of AB-8 resin via kinetic and thermodynamic analyses; (4) characterized RSPE using NGM and HPLC, while evaluating its in vitro antioxidant and antiglycemic activities; and (5) established structure-activity relationships via Pearson correlation analysis. The findings of this study provide novel insights into the valorization of by-products from rambutan processing, advancing sustainable resource utilization in the food and pharmaceutical industries.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Choline chloride (≥98% purity), malic acid (≥99%), citric acid (anhydrous, ≥99.5%), lactic acid (≥85%), oxalic acid (≥98%), glycerol (≥99%), and glucose (≥99.5%) were obtained from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Pectinase (food grade, 10000 U/mL) and cellulase (food grade, 21000 U/mL) were supplied by Unson (Beijing, China). Methanol (≥99.9%), hydrochloric acid (36–38%), ethanol (≥99.5%), and sodium hydroxide (≥96%) were purchased from Xilong Scientific Co., Ltd. (Guangdong, China). Vanillin (≥98%), AB-8 macroporous resin, ferric chloride (FeCl₃, ≥99.9%), and 2,4,6-tris(2-pyridyl)-s-triazine (TPTZ, ≥99%) were acquired from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). 1,1-Diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH, ≥98%), Trolox (≥98%), α-glucosidase (50 U/mg), and α-amylase (4000 U/g) were provided by Shanghai Yuanye Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Folin & Ciocalteu's phenol reagent, 4-nitrophenyl-β-D-glucopyranoside (pNPG, ≥98%), and 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) reagent were sourced from Beijing Solarbio Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All HPLC-grade standards, including ellagic acid, gallic acid, salicylic acid, corilagin, catechin, (−)-epicatechin, 3-O-gallate epicatechin, quercetin, rutin, kaempferol, isoquercetrin, naringenin, proanthocyanidin B1, proanthocyanidin B2, proanthocyanidin C1, and tyrosol (all >97% purity), were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Stock solutions (0.2 mol/L) of each standard were prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, ≥99.9%) and stored at −20 °C.

Preparation of rambutan shell powder

Ripe rambutan fruits were harvested from 5-year-old BY-7 rambutan trees in a plantation located in Baoting County, Hainan Province, China (19.8754° N, 109.2786° E) in July 2023. The fresh fruits were transported to the laboratory within 4 h. The shells were immediately collected and frozen at −20 °C for 12 h, and then freeze-dried at −50 °C under 5 Pa for 48 h with a LGJ-10 freeze-dryer (Beijing Songyuanhuaxing Technology Development Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Subsequently, the dried shells were grinded twice for 1 min using an MM400 grinder (Verder Shanghai Instruments and Equipment Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) and the dried powder was sieved through a 120-mesh screen.

Preparation and screening of DESs

Six distinct DESs were synthesized by combining choline chloride with glucose, glycerol, malic acid, oxalic acid, citric acid, and lactic acid at molar ratios of 5:2, 1:2, 1:1, 1:1, 1:1 and 1:2, respectively (Table S1). Firstly, the two components of each DES were mixed at its specific molar ratio in a reagent bottle equipped with a GL45 screw cap. Subsequently, each mixture was heated and stirred at 80 °C and 50 rpm for 40–60 min with an H05–1 constant temperature magnetic stirrer (Shanghai Meiyingpu Instrument and Meter Manufacturing Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) until a transparent and homogeneous solution was obtained. After cooling to room temperature, the synthesized DESs were stored for subsequent use. The research procedure for the following steps is shown in Fig. S1. The polyphenol content of RSPE was used to screen the optimal DES for the polyphenol extraction of RS.

DES-based polyphenol extraction of RS

In order to obtain the optimal RSP extraction protocol, a DES-based combined enzymatic and ultrasound method was employed. Firstly, 1.000 g of lyophilized powder of RS was hydrolyzed by pectinase and cellulase at 50 °C for 1 h; secondly, the above mixture was thoroughly mixed for a period using the screened DES with a certain water content in a water bath; then the mixed system was subjected to ultrasonication at a specified power for a set duration using a SCIENTZ-750F ultrasonic disperser (Ningbo XinZhi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Zhejiang, China); finally, centrifugation was performed at 15,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was the RSPE. The RSPE extracted with distilled water or 80% (v/v) ethanol was used as a control.

One-factor-at-a-time (OFAT), factor screening, and response surface method-ology (RSM) experiments for DES-based polyphenol extraction of RS

DES water content (factor A), liquid-to-solid ratio (factor B), DES contact time (factor C), DES contact temperature (factor D), pectinase activity (factor E), cellulase enzyme activity (factor F), ultrasonic power (factor G), and ultrasonic treatment time (factor H) were investigated as single factors in extraction process. The levels for each factor were set as follows: factor A, 30%, 40%, 50%, 60%, 70%; factor B, 1 g:10 mL, 1 g:15 mL, 1 g:20 mL, 1 g:25 mL, 1 g:30 mL; factor C, 20 min, 30 min, 40 min, 50 min, 60 min; factor D, 40 °C, 50 °C, 60 °C, 70 °C, 80 °C; factor E, 630 U/mL, 840 U/mL, 1050 U/mL, 1260 U/mL, 1470 U/mL; factor F, 660 U/mL, 880 U/mL, 1100 U/mL, 1320 U/mL, 1540 U/mL; factor G, 200 W, 300 W, 400 W, 500 W, 600 W; factor H, 3 min, 6 min, 9 min, 12 min, 15 min. The standard parameters were: factor A at 40%, factor B at 1:15, factor C at 40 min, factor D at 60 °C, factor E at 1050 U/mL, factor F at 1100 U/mL, factor G at 400 W, and factor H at 9 min. Each experiment was repeated three times.

Factor screening experiments were conducted using a Plackett-Burman (PB) design to identify significant factors from the above eight factors. The details of each factor and its corresponding levels were listed in Table S2. On the basis of OFAT and PB, Box-Behnken design was used to optimize the parameters of polyphenol extraction of RS. The specific experimental design information was shown in Table S3. Design-Expert 12 software (Stat-Ease Inc., Minnesota, USA) was used to design the experiments and analyze the results.

Determination of total polyphenol content (TPC), Total flavonoids content (TFC)and total proanthocyanidins content (PC)

The TPC was determined according to the method described by Cao et al. (2023), with minor modifications. Briefly, the lyophilized powder of RS was extracted with various solvents and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was collected to obtain the RSPE, which was diluted with distilled water to an appropriate concentration for analysis. For the assay, 100 μL of the RSPE was mixed with 50 μL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. After 5 min, 300 μL of 20% Na₂CO₃ was added, and the mixture was incubated at 30 ± 2 °C in the dark for 2 h. The absorbance was measured at 765 nm using a Jasco V-630 spectrophotometer (Jasco Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Quantification was performed using a standard curve prepared with gallic acid (0–1 mg/mL), which followed the equation: Y = 74.228X + 2.009 (R2 = 0.9994).

The TFC was measured following the method of Chen et al. (2023), with slight modification, using an aluminum trichloride colorimetric assay. Results were expressed as rutin equivalents (RE) per gram of dry weight (DW). In brief, 100 μL of the RSPE was mixed with 100 μL of distilled water and 50 μL of 0.05 g/mL NaNO₃ solution and incubated for 15 min. Subsequently, 50 μL of 10% AlCl₃ solution was added, the reaction was allowed to proceed for 6 min. Thereafter, 300 μL of distilled water and 400 μL of 4% NaOH solution were added, followed by a 15-min incubation. The absorbance at 510 nm was recorded, and the TFC was calculated using the regression equation: Y = 126.6X + 59.375 (R2 = 0.9971).

The PC was evaluated based on the protocol by Zhang et al. (2020), with minor modification. In summary, 50 μL of the RSPE was combined with 300 μL of 4% (w/v) vanillin-methanol solution and 150 μL of HCl. The mixture was incubated for 20 min under light-protected conditions. The absorbance was measured at 500 nm, and PC was quantified using the regression curve Y = 275.9×- 19.0281 (R2 = 0.9961). Results were expressed as mg catechin equivalent (CE) per gram of DW.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The microstructure morphology of the RS powder treated with different extraction methods (DES, water, 80% ethanol) was observed with a Phenom ProX scanning electron microscope (Funa Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 5600 × magnification. The procedure followed previously described methods (Cao et al., 2023; Fu et al., 2021) with some modifications. Briefly, each extracted RS powder was freeze-dried at −50 °C under 5 Pa conditions for 48 h and subsequently mounted onto a sample stage. Each sample was sprayed with gold using a SBC-12 sputter coater (KYKY Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) at 1.0 mbar and 10 mA for 100 s before SEM imaging was performed.

Analysis of polyphenol composition of RSPE

Qualitative analysis of RSPE

A slightly modified version of the NGM metabolomics method described by Zhou et al. (2022) was employed for qualitative analysis of the polyphenol composition in RSPE. Liquid chromatography (LC) analysis was performed on a Vanquish UHPLC System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) equipped with a Phenomenex Kinetex C18 (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 2.6 μm; Phenomenex Inc., CA, USA). The mobile phases consisted of 0.01%(v/v) acetic acid in ultrapure water (A) and 1:1(v/v) mixture of isopropanol and acetonitrile (B). The column temperature was maintained at 25 °C, and injection volume were set to 2 μL. Mass spectrometric detection was conducted on Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). MS/MS spectra were acquired on information-dependent acquisition (IDA) mode using the 4.4 Xcaliburs software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The electrospray ionization (ESI) source parameters were as follows: sheath gas flow rate, 50 Arb; aux gas flow rate, 15 Arb; capillary temperature, 320 °C; sweep gas, 1 Arb; vaporizer temperature, 350 °C; full MS resolution, 60000; MS/MS resolution, 15000; collision energy, SNCE 20/30/40; spray voltage, 3.8 kV (positive) or − 3.4 kV (negative).

Quantitative analysis of RSPE

The gradient elution program was referenced to the method reported by Chen et al. (2023). Quantification of polyphenol compounds of RSPE was performed using an Agilent 1200 HPLC system (Agilent Technologies Inc., CA, USA) coupled with a Zorbax Eclipse Plus C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm; Agilent Technologies Inc., CA, USA). The mobile phase consisted of (A) 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in ultrapure water and (B) 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in acetonitrile. The injection volume was 10.0 μL, and detection wavelength was set at 280 nm. The elution gradient was as follows: 0 min, 15%B; 0–5 min, 15% B; 5–23 min, 15–25% B; 23–36 min, 25–35% B; 36–40 min, 35–50% B; 40–43 min, 50% B, 43–49 min, 50–80% B; 49–52 min, 80% B; 52.01 min, 15% B; 52.01–60 min, 15% B.

Stock solutions of each standard were prepared at a concentration of 0.2 mol/L with DMSO. A series of standard solutions were diluted with 70% ethanol to generate a calibration curve ranging from 0 to 1500 μmol/L. The concentration of each phenolic compound was expressed as μg/g DW.

Adsorptive purification of crude RSPE and fundamentals of the adsorption process

Determination of adsorption isotherm

Extraction solution of RSP with the screened DES was diluted into 1/8, 1/4, 3/8, 1/2, 5/8, 3/4, 7/8 concentrations of the original concentration. Subsequently, 25 mL of each diluted solution above and the original extraction solution were taken in eight conical flasks and 0.2 g of the optimal resin was added to each flask. The conical flasks were placed into a water bath shaker at 30 °C, 35 °C or 40 °C and 120 rpm for 5 h. The adsorption isotherms were fitted using Langmuir, Freundlich and Temkin models, respectively.(1)Langmuir model:Ceqe=1KLqm+Ceqm

At equilibrium, Ce represents the concentration of RSP in solution, which is expressed in mg GAE/mL; qe denotes the equilibrium adsorption amount of RSP at equilibrium, represented as mg GAE/g (mass of the resin); qm is the peak theoretical adsorption capacity of RSP, represented as mg GAE/g (mass of the resin); KL serves as the associative Langmuir constant, indicating the affinity of resin for RSP.

RL is another parameter in Langmuir model. It is named as resolution factor, and expressed as:(2)RL=1KLC0where C0 is the maximum initial concentration of RSP (mg GAE/mL).(3)Freundlich model:lnqe=1nlnCe+lnKFwhere qe and Ce share the same definitions as those in the Langmuir model, it should be noted that 1/n and KF represent the constants in the Freundlich equation.(4)Temkin model:qe=BTlnKT+BTlnCewhere qe and Ce are the same as in Langmuir model, KT and BT are temperature-dependent constants of the Temkin isotherm model.

Antioxidant activities of purified RSP

The antioxidant capacity of purified RSP was evaluated using two methods: (1) the DPPH radical scavenging assay and (2) the ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) analysis, with vitamin E serving as the reference antioxidant. The experimental protocols were adapted from established procedures (Chen et al., 2023; Nakagawa et al., 2021), with some modifications in reaction incubation time and detection wavelengths. For the DPPH assay, 100 μL of sample and different concentrations of Trolox were combined with 800 μL of DPPH solution (100 μmol in methanol) and incubated in the dark for 30 min. DPPH scavenging capacity was determined using the regression curve (Y = −0.2283X + 0.4911, R2 = 0.9931) and measured at 517 nm, with results expressed as millimoles of Trolox equivalent per gram of RSPE dry weight (mM TE/g DW). In the FRAP assay, the reagent was prepared by mixing acetate buffer (300 mM, pH = 3.6), FeCl3 solution (20 mM), and TPTZ solution (10 mM) in a 10:1:1 (v/v/v) ratio, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. Then, 50 μL of sample and varying concentrations of Trolox were added to 900 μL of FRAP reagent and incubated in the dark for 30 min. The FRAP results were expressed as mM TE/g DW, derived from the regression curve (Y = 0.6146×-0.0157, R2 = 0.9968) and absorbance measured at 593 nm.

Antidiabetic activity of purified RSP

α-glucosidase inhibition activity

The α-glucosidase inhibitory activity was determined using a slightly modified version of a previously reported method (Zheng et al., 2020). In brief, 50 μL of α-glucosidase (1 U/mL) was mixed with 50 μL of RSP solution and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. Subsequently, 100 μL of pNPG (1%) was added, followed by a further incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. The reaction was then terminated with 500 μL of Na2CO3 (1 mol/L), and the absorbance was measured at 405 nm. Acarbose served as a positive control.

α-amylase inhibition activity

The inhibitory activity of α-amylase was assessed following a slightly modified method described by Zheng et al. (2020). Briefly, 0.1 mL of polyphenol extract was mixed with 0.1 mL of amylase solution and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. Next, 0.2 mL of 1% soluble starch solution was introduced, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. The reaction was then stopped by adding 0.2 mL DNS, followed by heating in a boiling water bath for 15 min. After cooling in an ice-water bath, the mixture was diluted with 1 mL of distilled water, and the absorbance was recorded at 540 nm.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Prior to statistical analysis, the normality of the data and the homogeneity of variance were evaluated using Shapiro-Wilk test (p > 0.05) and Levene test (p > 0.05), respectively. Statistical significance of differences between means of different groups was evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in SPSS statistics 20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), with post hoc confirmation via Duncan's multiple range test (p < 0.05).

Analysis of variance for PB and RSM designs and their outcomes was carried out using Design Expert 13 (Design Expert 13, Minneapolis, USA). The adequacy of the models was evaluated via the coefficient of determination (R2), adjusted R2 (Adj R2), and lack-of-fit tests. Adsorption-related data were fitted using Origin version 23 (Northampton, MA, USA) and the adequacy of the fitted models was determined based on R2 and Adj R2. Pearson correlation analysis was employed to assess the relationships between polyphenolic constituents and biological activity. The analysis was conducted with SPSS statistics 20.0, and the correlation heatmap was visualized with R 4.2.1 (ggplot2 package).

Results and discussion

Screening of DESs

The contents of RSPE extracted by different solvents ranged from 70 to 150 mg GAE/g, as shown in Fig. S2. The extracted amounts by all DESs were much higher than those extracted by water and 80% ethanol. Notably, the highest RSPE content was obtained using the choline chloride-glycerol (ChCl-Gly) formulation.

Phenolic can form hydrogen bonds with the hydrogen bond donor present in DES, leading to an improvement in their solubility and extraction efficiency (Cao et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023). Glycerol has the most hydrogen bonds among all the DESs formulas in this study. Moreover, compounds with low polarity are more likely to be extracted by the DES with low polarity (Tang et al., 2023). ChCl-Gly exhibited relatively low polarity among the six DESs, thus demonstrating strong potential as an effective solvent for RSP extraction. This optimal DES formulation was selected for further process optimization.

OFAT, factor screening, and response surface methodology (RSM) experiments for DES-based polyphenol extraction of RS

The effect of single factor on the polyphenol extraction yield of RS

Liquid-to-solid ratio

The initial OFAT experiments were conducted to offer preliminary insights into the influence of each factor, thereby facilitating the design of more intricate experimental frame. The liquid-to-solid ratio, as illustrated in Fig. S3A, had a significant effect on the efficiency of extracting RSP using a ChCl-Gly-based DES. The maximum yield was obtained at a liquid-to-solid ratio of 15 mL/g. The extraction efficiency initially increased and then decreased as the liquid-to-solid ratio increased. This phenomenon can be ascribed to contact area between DES and RS powder at a higher ratio, thereby facilitating the diffusion and dissolution of RSP. Conversely, an excessive ratio may reduce RSP extraction due to the co-dissolution of pigments or other soluble substances. Moreover, the excessively low ratio may increase the viscosity of the extraction system, thereby impairing the extraction efficiency (Duan et al., 2016). The present findings were consistent with those reported by Cao et al. (2023) for the polyphenol extraction from broccoli leaves, as well as the findings of Sukor et al. (2020) regarding the phenolic acids extraction from oak galls.

Water content

Fig. S3B illustrated effect of water content in the extraction system on the polyphenols extraction rate from RS. The highest RSP extraction rate was observed at a water content of 40%, exhibiting a trend analogous to that observed for the liquid-to-solid ratio. This result can be attributed to the decreased viscosity of the extraction solvent at higher water content, thus enhancing the solubility of polyphenols (Zhou et al., 2018). However, at excessively high water contents, water molecules form hydrogen bonds with hydrogen bond donors (HBD) and hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA) of DES, disrupting its internal hydrogen bonding network and altering its structure, consequently decreasing the polyphenol extraction yield (Duan et al., 2016). Moreover, an excessive increase in water content elevated the solvent polarity (Ling & Hadinoto, 2022), thus reducing the extraction rate.

Extraction temperature

Fig. S3C demonstrates the influence of extraction temperature on polyphenol yield. The maximum extraction rate was recorded at 70 °C, above and below which the extraction efficiency decreased; specially, a sharp decline in yield occurred between 70 °C and 80 °C. This observation can be due to the reduction in solvent surface tension and viscosity at moderately elevated temperatures, thereby augmenting its fluidity and intensifying molecular diffusion (Taweekayujan et al., 2023), which promotes the RSP solubilization. Furthermore, it has been indicated that DES and polyphenols may be susceptible to chemical degradation at 75 °C (Pavlić et al., 2022), thus leading to a sharp decline in the extraction efficiency in the range of 70–80 °C. The findings in this study aligned with prior investigations employing DES for the polyphenol extraction from Carya cathayensis Sarg. and from Mentha pulegium (Fu et al., 2021; Soukaina et al., 2024).

Extraction time

Fig. S3D illustrates the effect of the water bath duration on the polyphenol extraction yield from RS. The extraction yield initially increased and subsequently decreased with prolonged water bath duration, peaking at 40 min. This trend can stem from the gradual release and diffusion of polyphenols from RS powder into the solvent as extraction duration extended from 20 to 40 min. Before reaching equilibrium, the concentration gradient of the RSP facilitated diffusion, consistent with Fick's second law of diffusion (Moccia et al., 2022), thereby elevating the extraction efficiency. Nonetheless, an excessive extraction time may induce polyphenol degradation, thus diminishing the extraction rate (Yang et al., 2023).

Pectinase activity, cellulase activity, ultrasonic treatment time, and ultrasonic power

As depicted in Fig. S3E–H, variations in pectinase and cellulase activities, ultrasonic duration, and ultrasonic power, did not significantly influence the extraction yield of RSP. As illustrated in Fig. S4, the application of ultrasound in conjunction with enzymatic treatment significantly enhanced the extraction efficiency of RSP by 15% with 80% (v/v) ethanol and by 27.84% with distilled water, in comparison the non-sonicated and non-enzymatic treatments. These findings suggested that the combination of ultrasound and enzymatic extraction promoted the recovery of polyphenols from RS powder when utilizing 80% ethanol or distilled water. Notably, the extraction rate of RSP using ChCl-Gly combined with ultrasound and enzyme did not significantly differ from the rate obtained with ChCl-Gly alone. Previous study confirmed that DES, enzyme, and ultrasound can deconstruct plant cell walls through distinct mechanisms (Vo et al., 2023). Nevertheless, the three treatments do not always exhibit synergistic couplings, and even can display mutual inhibitions (Vo et al., 2023). The inherent high viscosity of DES universally impedes the collapse of cavitation bubbles, reduces the intensity of micro-jets and shear forces, thus restricts cell structure disruption and the release of target bioactive compounds (Xu et al., 2025). Moreover, in specific DES-based systems, the changes in material particle size and surface morphology caused by enzymolysis can influence the cavitation effect, offsetting the auxiliary advantages of ultrasonication (Vo et al., 2023).

In this study, Plackett-Burman (PB) design was conducted to identify significant factors from the above eight factors. The PB experiments enables rapid identification of factors significantly affecting RSP extraction yield by evaluating only the high and low levels of each factor. As indicated in Fig. 3B–D and Table 1, the correlation coefficients (R2) of the three isotherm models are high, with all values exceeding 0.9. The Langmuir isotherm equation depicts a monolayer adsorption process, indicating on interaction between adsorbate molecules. The Temkin model proposes a multilayered adsorption process, assuming that the heat of adsorption diminishes linearly with increasing surface coverage (Nimibofa et al., 2017).

In this study, the Langmuir model exhibited a higher R2 value than the Freundlich and Temkin models, potentially indicating that the adsorption process of RSPE onto AB-8 macroporous resin follows a monolayer adsorption mechanism more closely. These results confirm the suitability of the Langmuir model for describing the resin-polyphenol interaction.

Adsorption thermodynamics analysis

Commonly the thermodynamic parameters-specifically the Gibbs free energy change (ΔG), enthalpy change (ΔH), and entropy change (ΔS)- are fundamental to assess adsorption mechanism. Negative ΔG values, frequently observed in adsorption systems, indicate a thermodynamically favorable processes, while the magnitude of ΔH offers insight into the predominant interaction forces between adsorbate and adsorbent (Sen et al., 2023). Calculation of ΔG at various temperatures requires prior determination of adsorption equilibrium constant (K) at different temperatures (Liu et al., 2023). As in Fig. 5A, K is derived from the linear relationship between ln(qe/ce) and qe, extrapolated to the y-axis intercept. As shown in Fig. 5B, the ΔH and ΔS of the adsorption process can be determined from the slope and intercept based on a linear fit of the Van't Hoff equation (Eq. 9 in 2.9.4). ΔH < 0 denotes an exothermic adsorption process, while ΔH > 0 indicates endothermic behavior; ΔS > 0 reflects an increase in the system disorder, whereas ΔS < 0 indicates a reduction in disorder.Fig. 5Plots of ln(qe/ce) vs. qe (A) and Van't Hoff plot of ln(kc) vs.1/T (B) for RSPE at 30 °C, 35 °C, and 40 °C.Fig. 5

Plots of ln(qe/ce) vs. qe (A) and Van't Hoff plot of ln(kc) vs.1/T (B) for RSPE at 30 °C, 35 °C, and 40 °C.

Since ΔG < 0, ΔH > 0 and ΔS > 0, this adsorption is a spontaneous, endothermic process accompanied by increased disorder. Moreover, the absolute value of ΔG rises with increasing temperature, indicating that the adsorption process is thermally enhanced (Munagapat & Kim, 2016). In addition, usually the adsorption processes are classified as physisorption when ΔG ranges between −20 and 0 kJ·mol−1, whereas chemisorption predominates when ΔG is less than −80 kJ·mol−1 (Wu et al., 2014). Moreover, physical adsorption process is generally characterized by ΔH values below 40 kJ·mol−1, while chemisorption involves ΔH values between 40 and 120 kJ·mol−1 (Alkan et al., 2004). As shown in Table S7, ΔG is negative with an absolute value below 14 kJ·mol−1 and ΔH is 15.65 kJ·mol−1, indicating that the adsorption of RSP by AB-8 resin occurred via a physical adsorption.

In summary, the adsorption process of RSP onto AB-8 macroporous resin is well described by the Langmuir and pseudo-second-order kinetic models. The negative ΔG confirms the spontaneity of the process. These findings are consisting with previous studies. For instance, Hou et al. (2019) employed AB-8 macroporous resin to purify flavonoids from Sophora tonkinensis Gagnep., following the Langmuir and pseudo-second-order kinetic models. Similarly, Liu et al. (2023) demonstrated that the adsorption of Glycyrrhiza glabra onto LSA-21 resin followed the Langmuir adsorption and pseudo-second-order kinetic models, with a negative ΔG value.

This superior extraction efficiency primarily stems from two synergistic mechanisms. On the one hand, ChCl-Gly treatment induced the most pronounced changes in the surface morphology of RS powder, characterized by extensive fragmentation and dense pore structures, as shown in Fig. 2. In contrast, 80% ethanol caused moderate morphological alterations, while distilled water had the least effect. On the other hand, the Cl− in the ChCl-Gly system formed hydrogen-bonds with the hydrogen atoms of the hydroxyl groups in both polyphenols and glycerol. This interaction reduced steric hindrance between solvent molecules and polyphenols (Fu et al., 2022), thereby enhancing the solubilization and diffusion of the RSP.

The polyphenolic composition of RSPE has been extensively investigated in previous studies, generating a wealth of positive and promising findings. For example, Torgbo et al. (2024) determined the polyphenolic composition of the RSP from the central province of Thailand extracted with 95% ethanol by using HPLC, reporting that anthocyanins, ellagic acid and geraniin were the main components, collectively accounting for 69.3% of the total RSP weight. Albuquerque et al. (2023) used HPLC-DAD-ESI/MS to analyze the composition of the RSP from the northeastern part of Brazil extracted with 60% ethanol, identifying the following concentrations: geraniol isomer I (14 mg/g), geraniol isomer II (4.9 mg/g), ellagitannin I (6.322 mg/g), ellagitannin II (7.7 mg/g) and delphinidin glycoside ligands (4.13 mg/g). Zhuang et al. (2017) extracted RSPE using 80.8% ethanol from locally purchased rambutan in Kunming, and identified compounds such as corilagin, catechin, ellagic acid, and ellagic acid pentoside through UPLC analysis. However, NGM was firstly applied to identify polyphenols in RS. It is a higher level metabolomics technique based on retention time, MS, MS/MS, and reference standards to enhance both the accuracy and breadth of compounds identification (Zhou et al., 2022). Meanwhile, to the best of our knowledge, proanthocyanidin B1 was identified for the first time in BY-7 RS. In a recent study, Wang, Liu, et al. (2024) and Wang, Zhu, et al. (2024) identified proanthocyanidin B2 and C1 in the shells of two rambutan cultivars (BY-2 and BY-7) using metabolomic analysis, whereas proanthocyanidin B1 was only detected in BY-2 shells. The ChCl-Gly-extracted RSP exhibited higher levels of ellagic acid, gallic acid, proanthocyanidin B1 and proanthocyanidin B2, which partially diverged from previous findings. These discrepancies may be due to the differences in rambutan varieties and geographical origins, extraction solvents, and particularly the use of NGM as the primary detection technique.

Moreover, as shown in Table 3, purification significantly increased the concentrations of all substances except salicylic acid, with a total of 15 phenolic acids, flavonoids, and proanthocyanidins identified. Notably, proanthocyanidin B1, proanthocyanidin B2, ellagic acid, and proanthocyanidin C1 were present at relatively high levels, collectively accounting for 62.03% of the total weight. This result was consistent with previous reports indicating a substantial increase in the polyphenolic content following the purification of litchi peel extract (Tan et al., 2024). Collectively, combination of ChCl-Gly extraction and AB-8 resin purification is a novel strategy, which can significantly enhance both the diversity and abundance of polyphenols in RSP.

In vitro antioxidant and antiglycemic activities of RSPE and the correlation analysis of its polyphenolic composition and its bioactivities

In vitro antioxidant and antiglycemic activities of RSPE

TPC, TFC, PC, and in vitro antioxidant and antiglycemic capacities of the RSPEs prepared with different solvents as well as AB-8 resin-purified RSP, are summarized in Table S8. A consistent order was observed across all samples for TPC, DPPH radical scavenging capacity, and ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP): the purified RSP > the RSPE with ChCl-Gly > the RSPE with 80% ethanol> the RSPE with distilled water. Relative to the ChCl-Gly-derived RSPE, purification yielded significant enhancements in purified RSP: TPC (2.18-fold), TFC (2.05-fold), PC (1.44-fold), DPPH radical scavenging capacity (1.36-fold) and FRAP reducing power (1.45-fold). This finding aligned with the significant enhancement of antioxidant capacity of purified kiwi berry polyphenol reported by Qiao et al. (2025).

As shown in Table S8, the purified RSP exhibited the most potent α-glucosidase inhibition, ChCl-Gly and 80% ethanol-extracted RSPE did not show significant differences for the inhibitory activity, while water-extracted RSPE had the weakest α-glucosidase inhibitory ability despite an IC50 value approaching that of acarbose (the positive control). Therefore, RSPE exhibit α-glucosidase inhibitory effects in vitro, warranting further investigation for diabetes management application. However, ChCl-Gly-extracted RSPE showed the strongest α-amylase inhibitory activity, followed by purified RSP, 80% ethanol-extracted RSPE, and distilled water-extracted RSPE. All extracts had larger IC50 values than acarbose. In this study, RSPEs possessed a higher α-glucosidase inhibition than α-amylase inhibition. This trend aligned with Zheng et al. (2020), who reported greater α-glucosidase vs. α-amylase inhibition mung bean peel, yet contrary to proanthocyanidins extract from Malay cherry (Zhang et al., 2020).

Notably, the purified RSP exhibited reduced α-amylase inhibition (IC50 = 5.05 mg/mL) compared to the crude ChCl-Gly extract (IC50 = 2.62 mg/mL, Table S8). Previous studies have shown that α-amylase inhibition is primarily attributed to specific phenolic compounds and synergistic interactions, rather than TPC (Tan et al., 2017). Macroporous resin purification may lead to the loss of auxiliary components, including polyphenols and other substances that form functional complexes with polyphenols. A study by Ma et al. (2015) demonstrated that the polyphenol extract from Sphallerocarpus gracilis stems exhibited significant α-glucosidase inhibitory activity after resin purification, yet almost completely lost its α-amylase inhibitory capacity. In addition, it has been confirmed that for proanthocyanidins, especially B-type proanthocyanidins, the mean degree of polymerization (mDP) significantly affects their α-amylase inhibitory activity, with a higher mDP corresponding to stronger inhibition (Dai et al., 2018). However, AB-8 resin purification commonly leads to a reduction in the mDP of proanthocyanidins (Song et al., 2025). The above results provide potential explanations for the decrease in α-amylase inhibitory capacity observed in the purified RSP, although the specific mechanisms require further investigation.

The correlation analysis of its polyphenolic composition and its bioactivities

Correlation heatmap (Fig. S5) revealed that 11 polyphenols (except for epicatechin, naringenin, salicylic acid and kaempferol) in RSPE, along with TFC, TPC and PC exhibited statistically significant positive correlations with their DPPH radical scavenging and FRAP reducing abilities (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01). Conversely, these compounds showed statistically significant negative correlations (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01) with their IC50 values of α-amylase and α-glucosidase inhibition, indicating statistically significant positive correlation (p < 0.05 or p < 0.01) with the enzymatic inhibition. Moreover, there were no significantly positive correlations between the contents of epicatechin (p = 0.2772 and 0.2351), naringenin (p = 0.2154 and 0.2996) and salicylic acid (undetected) in RSPE with DPPH/FRAP activities. And there were no significantly negative correlations between epicatechin (p = 0.2151 and 0.2265), naringenin (p = 0.1621 and 0.1611), and salicylic acid (undetected) with IC50 values of α-amylase and α-glucosidase. Kaempferol was significantly (p < 0.05) positively correlated with the FRAP activity and inhibition of α-amylase and α-glucosidase, but no significant correlation was observed with DPPH activity (p = 0.0582).

Conclusion

In summary, the extraction of RSPE using the ChCl-Gly (1:2, molar ratio) was systematically optimized via OFAT, PB and RSM. Subsequently, RSPE was purified with AB-8 resin, and the polyphenolic composition was characterized for the first time by NGM. Under optimal extraction conditions, RSPE exhibited TPC, TFC, PC, DPPH and FRAP activities, as well as α-amylase and α-glucosidase IC50 values of 178.89 ± 2.41 mg GAE/g DW, 152.93 ± 4.28 mg RE/g DW, 41.89 ± 0.37 mg CE/g DW, 7061.49 ± 105.37 μmol TE/g DW, 6925.34 ± 79.37 μmol TE/g DW, 2.62 ± 0.06 mg/mL, and 70.78 ± 3.38 μg/mL, respectively-all significantly exceeding those obtained with 80% ethanol or water extraction. Moreover, the adsorption process conformed to the Langmuir model and pseudo-second-order kinetics, indicating a spontaneous and dynamic mechanism. Notably, NGM was firstly applied to identify polyphenols in RS and proanthocyanidin B1 was identified in BY-7 RS for the first time. However, this study has several limitations. The high viscosity of ChCl-Gly system presents challenges for industrial-scale applications. To address this issue, subsequent research can focus on developing ternary or multicomponent DES systems, which reduce the viscosity by introducing a third component to disrupt the original strong hydrogen-bonding network. Alternatively, edible DESs can be constructed using organic acids and sugar-based HBDs such as citric acid and fructose. These edible DESs do not require rigorous removal and can even be retained as functional ingredients in the final products, thereby significantly reducing processing complexity. Moreover, bioactivity assessments were restricted to in vitro assays and low-abundance compounds may have remained undetected due to the analytical sensitivity and ionization efficiency constraints of NGM. In conclusion, this study provides novel insights into the valorization of RS waste, highlights its potential as a source of bioactive compounds.
