Safety of fruquintinib mono- and combo therapy in metastatic colorectal cancer: real-world subgroup analysis from China
Authors: Zhiqiang Wang, Haijun Zhong, Yifu He, Chen Zhang, Zuoxing Niu, Shujun Yang, Tao Zhang, Liangjun Zhu, Yongqian Shu, Jianjun Peng, Yan Song, Jian Li, Ying Yuan, Haibo Zhang, Gengsheng Yu, Yunqi Hua, Jianjun Xiao, Jianfei Fu, Yulong Zheng, Jingyu Zhu, Hua Xue, Xian Luo, Weiguo Su, Shukui Qin, Jin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFruquintinib, a highly selective vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 1, 2, 3 inhibitors, has demonstrated significant efficacy in phase III trials for metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC). However, real-world evidence is essential to confirm its safety profile across diverse treatment settings.AimThis subgroup analysis of a phase IV study aims to evaluate the safety of fruquintinib both as monotherapy and combination therapy in a real-world setting.MethodsPhase IV-study data were categorized by treatment regimen into fruquintinib monotherapy and combination therapy groups. Safety profiles including TEAEs and TRAEs were recorded.ResultsOverall, 2798 patients were enrolled in the phase IV study (2019–2022) and received fruquintinib 5 mg/day or per physician’s choice. Among them, 1888 patients received monotherapy, whereas 775 patients received combination therapy: fruquintinib + chemotherapy (n = 359), fruquintinib with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) (n = 493), and chemotherapy and ICI (n = 77), with overlap between combination groups. Drug exposure was the shortest with monotherapy (2.50 months) and longest with fruquintinib + ICI + chemotherapy (4.70 months). Monotherapy subgroup reported the lowest incidence of TEAEs (74.47%) and grade ≥3 TEAEs (23.94%), while the fruquintinib + chemotherapy subgroup reported the highest incidence (85.52%).ConclusionFruquintinib combination therapy demonstrated a satisfactory safety profile within the treatment groups.KEYWORDS: Colorectal cancer, fruquintinib, combination therapy, safety, third-line treatment

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common malignancy globally, accounting for 9.6% of all cancer cases and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths (9.3%) [1]. In China, CRC ranks as the second most prevalent cancer, with approximately 0.5 million new cases (10.7%) reported in 2022. It is also the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths in China, with an estimated 0.24 million CRC-related deaths (9.3%), according to the Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) 2022 report [2].

With an increasing number of patients with metastatic CRC (mCRC) eligible for third-line (3L) treatments [3], there is a growing need for effective third-line treatment options, especially in China [4]. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Clinical Practice Guidelines in Oncology (NCCN Guidelines®) recommend the following 3L treatment for mCRC: fruquintinib, regorafenib, and trifluridine/tipiracil, either alone or in combination with bevacizumab [5]. Guidelines from the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) 2024 also recommend the use of TAS-102 in combination with bevacizumab for the 3L palliative treatment of mCRC [6]. Reflecting these updates, there has been a gradual shift from monotherapy to combination therapy strategies. In particular, the combination of TAS-102 and bevacizumab has emerged as a new standard, highlighting the advantages of combination therapy in improving patient survival outcomes [7].

Fruquintinib, a potent and highly selective small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitor of vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) 1, 2, and 3 [8], was approved in China in 2018 [9] and later by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2023 [10], the European Commission in June 2024 [11], and, most recently, by the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare in September 2024 [12]. Fruquintinib is indicated for the treatment of adult patients with mCRC who have previously received fluoropyrimidine, oxaliplatin, and irinotecan and who have either received or unsuitable for anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and anti-epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) therapies (for wild-type rat sarcoma) [13].

Clinical studies have consistently demonstrated the efficacy of fruquintinib. The FRESCO phase III study demonstrated the improvements in both overall survival (OS) (9.3 months vs 6.6 months) and progression-free survival (PFS) (median PFS; 3.7 months vs 1.8 months) compared with the placebo group [4]. Similarly, the FRESCO-2 trial confirmed the efficacy of fruquintinib (median OS; 7.4 months vs 4.8 months) compared to the placebo group [14].

In addition, phase III FRESCO trial reported that the most common treatment-related grade ≥3 adverse events of special interest (AESIs) were hypertension (21.2%), hand-foot skin reaction (HFSR) (10.8%), and proteinuria (3.2%) in the fruquintinib group [15]. A recent phase IV real-world study involving 2798 patients with CRC further validated the safety profile of fruquintinib, demonstrating a favorable profile [16], consistent with previous studies [15,17,18]. Furthermore, fruquintinib demonstrated a lower risk of adverse events (AEs) compared with other VEGF/VEGFR inhibitors such as regorafenib [19].

Despite these advancements, there remains an unmet need for more potent and efficacious treatment strategies. Although phase III trials such as FRESCO and FRESCO-2 have established the efficacy and safety of fruquintinib under controlled conditions, these findings may not fully reflect the complexity of routine clinical practice, in which patient characteristics, treatment patterns, and overlapping regimens vary widely. Real-world studies provide complementary evidence by evaluating safety in heterogeneous populations and informing practical tolerability considerations for oncologists.

The CRC tumor microenvironment (TME) is highly heterogeneous, affecting angiogenesis, immune evasion, and treatment response. Fruquintinib normalizes the vasculature and reduces hypoxia, enhancing immune cell infiltration and the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [20,21]. VEGFR blockade and ICIs act synergistically – one reduces immunosuppressive signaling, the other restores T-cell activity – a rationale supported by preclinical and early clinical data. Furthermore, combination therapies have been reported to be superior to monotherapy in various types of cancers [22]. For instance, the combination of trifluridine/tipiracil and bevacizumab has demonstrated superior efficacy compared with monotherapy [23]. Similarly, a study by Deng et al. demonstrated longer mPFS and mOS with fruquintinib combined with anti-programmed death-1 (PD-1) therapy than with fruquintinib alone [24]. A phase Ib/II trial conducted in China also reported promising efficacy with fruquintinib and sintilimab in patients with mCRC [25].

As more patients receive fruquintinib both as monotherapy and in combination therapies, understanding its safety profile in real-world settings is crucial. In this subgroup analysis of the phase IV study, we aimed to evaluate the safety profile of fruquintinib when used as monotherapy or in combination with other treatments in patients with mCRC from China. We hypothesized that combination therapy may have a different safety profile compared to fruquintinib monotherapy in this real-world study.

Methods

Study design and patients

This is a subgroup analysis from a phase IV study of fruquintinib (NCT04005066), the detailed inclusion criteria described in the primary phase IV study [16]. The study included patients who were currently receiving fruquintinib or intended to initiate treatment within the next 7 days, and those deemed eligible for fruquintinib as determined by their physician. Fruquintinib was administered orally once daily at a dose of 5 mg/d for 3 weeks, followed by a 1-week treatment break in the 4-week cycle, in accordance with the package insert. All enrolled patients receiving at least 1 dose of fruquintinib were included in the safety analysis set (SS).

All data from patients with mCRC were extracted from the phase IV database, and the patients were categorized into the fruquintinib monotherapy group and the combination therapy group based on the use of concomitant medications, which were administered based on physician’s discretion. The combination therapy group was further subdivided into several categories: combination chemotherapy, combination ICI, and the combination of both chemotherapy and ICI. Chemotherapy administered in combination with fruquintinib included oral drugs such as capecitabine, TAS-102, and tegafur/gimeracil/oteracil (S-1) and intravenous medications such as oxaliplatin, raltitrexed, fluorouracil, and irinotecan. The immunotherapies administered in combination with fruquintinib included PD-1 inhibitors such as sintilimab, camrelizumab, and tislelizumab and Programmed Death Ligand 1 (PD-L1) antibodies such as durvalumab and atezolizumab.

Ethical statement

The authors are accountable for all aspects of the work and ensure that questions related to the accuracy or integrity of any part of the work are appropriately investigated and resolved. The phase IV study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the ethics/committee of the respective study centers, and informed consent was obtained from all individual participants.

Follow-up and safety outcomes

The enrolled patients were scheduled for three follow-up visits at baseline, 1 month, and 6 months after providing informed consent or 30 days after the administration of the last dose, whichever occurred first to collect patients’ characteristics and safety data. The primary endpoint of the study was safety assessment, which included treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs), treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs), the incidence of overall serious adverse events (SAEs), dose adjustment and discontinuations, and the severity of AEs evaluated according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 4.03.

Statistical analyses

A safety analysis was performed for all patients with CRC who received at least 1 dose of fruquintinib from the SS. Subgroup analyses were performed based on the type of treatment received (monotherapy or combination therapy), comparing baseline clinical characteristics, drug exposure, dose adjustments, and AEs across these subgroups. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers (%), whereas numerical variables are expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR). Continuous variables were summarized as medians with IQR. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 or later.

Results

Patients

This subgroup analysis included 2798 patients with CRC enrolled between April 2019 and September 2022 in the fruquintinib phase IV study. The median age of patients was 60 years (IQR, 52.0–67.0), and male patients accounted for 59.3% of the total CRC population. Among the 2798 patients, 1888 patients received fruquintinib as monotherapy, and 910 received fruquintinib combination therapy, among them 775 patients received fruquintinib combined with ICI or chemotherapy (combination therapy), which is our focus in this present analysis, and the other 135 patients received fruquintinib combined with other anti-cancer therapy (Chinese traditional medicine or target therapy). In the combination therapy group, 359 patients received fruquintinib combined with chemotherapy, 493 patients received it in combination with ICIs, and 77 patients received both chemotherapy and ICIs because of overlapping treatment regimens. Exposure to fruquintinib and dose intensity across the treatment groups

The median duration of exposure for all patients with CRC was 2.70 months. Within the treatment groups, the fruquintinib monotherapy subgroup had the shortest median exposure time of 2.50 months. In contrast, in the fruquintinib combination therapy group, the fruquintinib + chemotherapy + ICI subgroup had the longest median duration of exposure (4.70 months), followed by the fruquintinib + chemotherapy subgroup (3.80 months). The relative dose intensity (RDI) was 85.3% for the fruquintinib monotherapy group and 82.7% for the fruquintinib combination therapy group. Among the fruquintinib combination therapy subgroups, the fruquintinib + chemotherapy + ICI subgroup had the highest relative dose intensity at 90.70%, and the fruquintinib + ICI subgroup had the lowest RDI at 80%.

Overall, 1923 patients with CRC initiated the treatment at a dose of 5 mg/d. A greater proportion of patients in the fruquintinib monotherapy group received the 5-mg initial dose (67.0%) compared with those in the fruquintinib combination therapy group (61.4%). Within the combination therapy group, the fruquintinib + chemotherapy + ICI subgroup reported the highest proportion of patients receiving the 5-mg initial dose (71.4%). Dose adjustments because of AEs were the lowest in the fruquintinib monotherapy group (21.0%). In the fruquintinib combination therapy group, 25.3% of the patients required dose adjustments because of AEs, with the highest being reported in the fruquintinib + chemotherapy subgroup (25.6%; Table 2).

Safety

The overall incidence of any grade TEAEs and grade ≥3 TEAEs in patients with CRC was 77.52% and 24.66%, respectively. Within the treatment groups, the fruquintinib monotherapy group demonstrated the lowest proportion of TEAEs and grade ≥3 TEAEs (74.47% and 23.94%, respectively). The proportion of TEAEs and grade ≥3 TEAEs in the fruquintinib combination therapy group was 83.87% and 26.06%, respectively. Among the combination therapy subgroups, the fruquintinib + chemotherapy subgroup reported the higher proportion of TEAEs (85.52%), whereas the fruquintinib + chemotherapy + ICI subgroup reported the higher proportion of grade ≥3 TEAEs (36.36%) (Figure 2(A)).

Overall, the TRAEs were reported in 64.76% of patients with CRC. The fruquintinib monotherapy group reported the lowest incidence of any grade and grade ≥3 TRAEs (61.97% and 13.82%, respectively) among all treatment groups. In the fruquintinib combination therapy group, 70.71% and 15.87% of cases reported any grade and grade ≥3 TRAEs. The highest incidence of any grade and grade ≥3 TRAEs was observed in the fruquintinib + chemotherapy subgroup (71.59% and 18.11%, respectively) (Figure 2(B)). The incidence of TRAE-associated dose adjustments and treatment discontinuations varied across treatment groups, with 18.54% and 13.56% of patients in the fruquintinib monotherapy group, respectively, and 22.71% and 8.65% of patients in the fruquintinib combination therapy group, respectively. The fruquintinib + chemotherapy subgroup reported the highest incidences of 23.40% for dose adjustments and 10.58% for treatment discontinuations. The most common TRAEs (≥10%) in both the fruquintinib monotherapy and combination therapy groups were palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia (PPES; 19.70% and 22.84%, respectively) followed by hypertension (17.00% and 16.52%, respectively), and fatigue (10.06% and 11.35%, respectively). PPES and hypertension remained the top two most common TRAEs for the fruquintinib + ICI, fruquintinib + chemotherapy, and fruquintinib + chemotherapy + ICI subgroups, and the third most common TRAEs were dysphonia (10.55%), fatigue (14.76%), and decreased appetite (12.99%), respectively, for these subgroups (Table 4). Overall, grade ≥3 AEs were observed in 24.66% of patients, and hypertension (6.86%) and PPES (2.39%) were the most common TRAEs across all the treatment groups (Table 5). SAEs were reported in 11.33% of patients overall, with comparable rates in the monotherapy group (12.02%) and combination therapy (9.68%). Treatment discontinuation because of AEs occurred in 12.04% of patients, with rates of 17.06% in the monotherapy group and 12.39% in the combination therapy group (Table 3). Treatment-related deaths were not frequent, occurring in 8 patients (0.29%) overall, with 3 in the monotherapy group and 5 in the combination therapy group (Table 3).

Discussion

The evolution of palliative 3L therapy for mCRC in the CSCO guidelines over the past 5 years reflects a paradigm shift toward molecularly stratified and increasingly effective treatment strategies. Initially, regorafenib was recommended as the sole category I single-agent multi-kinase inhibitor in 2018, followed by incorporation of fruquintinib in 2019, and trifluridine/tipiracil (TAS-102) in 2020. The CSCO 2024 guidelines also recommend the use of TAS-102 in combination with bevacizumab for the 3L palliative treatment of mCRC [6]. This shift from single-drug treatment to combination therapy strategies, especially TAS-102 combined with bevacizumab, which has become the new standard, reflects the advantages of combination therapy in improving patient survival outcomes. The 2024 CSCO guidelines updated the immunotherapy strategies for patients with pMMR/MSS mCRC who have failed standard treatment, including exploratory treatment with TKIs (regorafenib or fruquintinib) combined with PD-1 inhibitors. In parallel, the NCCN guidelines also reinforce the value of combination approaches, recommending biologic agents (e.g., bevacizumab, cetuximab, and panitumumab) in combination with chemotherapy even in later lines of therapy [5].

As more potent combination regimens are increasingly adopted in clinical practice, this trend justifies the subgroup analysis in our study. This analysis specifically contributes real-world evidence on the safety and dosing characteristics of fruquintinib-based combination therapies in the 3L settings. Specifically, the inclusion of overlapping combination subgroups, such as patients receiving both chemotherapy and ICIs, reflects real-world treatment variability and enhances the applicability of these findings to routine clinical practice. These patterns highlight the need for flexible treatment strategies and the importance of evaluating safety in heterogeneous clinical settings.

Baseline characteristics

In the present study, the median age of all patients with CRC was 60 years. In routine clinical practice, the treatment choice between fruquintinib monotherapy and combination therapy was primarily influenced by patient age, performance status, disease burden, and previous treatment history. This trend was reflected in our study, in which 70.3% of patients receiving combination therapy were aged <65 years compared with 62.5% of patients in the monotherapy group, whereas monotherapy had a higher proportion of patients aged >65 years (37.5%) (Table 1). These data suggest that combination therapy is more commonly prescribed for younger patients who often present with aggressive tumor biology and with higher chances of lymph node metastases and poorly differentiated tumors [26], which might necessitate a more intensive treatment strategy. Consistent with this observation, only 29.7% of the elderly patients (aged ≥65 years) received combination therapy, whereas 70.3% of the younger patients (aged <65 years) received combination therapy. Overall, younger patients with better functional status and aggressive disease were more frequently prescribed combination regimens, while monotherapy was generally favored for elderly patients or those with comorbidities. In addition, our previous subgroup analysis from this phase IV study focused on the safety of fruquintinib across different age groups (<50 years vs. ≥75 years). The safety profile was comparable between subgroups, and combination therapy showed a longer treatment duration than monotherapy in both younger and late-elderly subgroups (4.10 vs 3.90 months) [27]. These findings support that the younger patients are more tolerable for combination regimens compared to elderly patients.

Treatment patterns

The total drug exposure duration was longer in the combination therapy groups than in the monotherapy group (3.6 months vs 2.5 months), with the fruquintinib + chemotherapy + ICI subgroup demonstrating the longest exposure time (4.7 months). As a surrogate efficacy endpoint, drug exposure time reflects whether the disease remains stable or in remission, which indicates that combination therapies particularly fruquintinib + chemotherapy + ICI may offer improved outcomes compared with monotherapy. With increased options for patients with mCRC, combination therapy could enhance the prognosis of this heterogeneous disease [28]. Consequently, various models of combination therapy could prove more effective in optimizing treatment benefits for patients with mCRC. The RDI was comparable between the monotherapy and combination therapy groups (85.3% vs 82.7%). This finding suggests that the tolerability of the combination therapy was comparable with monotherapy regimen. This could be attributed to the demographics of the younger population in the combination group with a median age of 58 years, likely experiencing fewer comorbidities and possessing more favorable baseline health status, enabling them to tolerate relatively higher doses. In contrast, the monotherapy group primarily consisted of the elderly population (median age, 61 years), who may experience age-related comorbidities that can hinder their ability to recover from aggressive treatments, necessitating dose reductions or delays and resulting in lower RDIs. The inclusion of overlapping combination subgroups, such as patients receiving both chemotherapy and ICIs, reflects real-world treatment variability and enhances the applicability of these findings to routine clinical practice. These patterns underscore the need for flexible treatment strategies and highlight the importance of evaluating safety in heterogeneous clinical settings. Dose strategy plays a pivotal role in optimizing treatment outcomes. Conventionally, fruquintinib is administered at a standard dose of 5 mg once daily for 21 days in a 28-day treatment cycle [13]. In combination therapy, various dosage strategies exist for fruquintinib. For instance, in combination with sintilimab, a PD-1 antibody, the recommended phase II dose of fruquintinib was 5 mg/d for 2 weeks on and 1 week off in a 3-week cycle, as evaluated in a phase Ib/II study [25]. In the FRUTIGA study, a randomized, double-blind, phase III trial, fruquintinib was combined with paclitaxel at a dosage of 4 mg/d for 3 weeks on and 1 week off in a 4-week cycle [29]. In the present real-world study, the proportion of patients receiving the standard drug dose of 5 mg was largely comparable between the combination therapy group and the monotherapy group (61.4% vs 67%). In addition, we observed a comparable proportion of patients who initiated treatment at a dose of 3 mg in both the monotherapy group and combination therapy group (21.6% vs 25.3%). Previous research has indicated that commencing treatment at a dose of 3 mg/d and gradually escalating the dose according to patients’ tolerability can improve clinical outcomes [30]. In the real-world clinical practice, physicians often adopt personalized dosing strategies for individual patients based on their specific conditions. Notably, in our study, the combination therapy group had a higher incidence of dose adjustment compared with the monotherapy group. This suggests that more diverse and flexible dosage strategies are required when implementing combination therapy.

Although this analysis provides comprehensive safety data across monotherapy and combination regimens, it did not stratify AEs by specific immunotherapy class (PD-1 vs PD-L1) or chemotherapy backbone. Given the small sample size for PD-L1 inhibitors and variability in chemotherapy regimens, such comparisons were not feasible without risking statistical bias.

Safety

Although, the overall TEAEs were lower in the monotherapy group compared with the combination therapy group (74.47% vs 83.87%), and grade ≥3 TEAEs were comparable between the groups (23.94% vs. 26.06%), both groups had a comparable incidence of dose adjustment (20.60% vs 24.90%). Furthermore, although the TEAEs rates were higher in the combination therapy groups, these did not substantially impact treatment continuation, as reflected by comparable relative-dose intensity (82.7% vs 85.3%) and longer median exposure duration compared with monotherapy (3.6 months vs 2.5 months). The fruquintinib monotherapy group reported an incidence of 61.97% for TRAEs and 13.82% for grade ≥3 TRAEs, whereas the fruquintinib + ICI combination therapy group reported incidences of 69.78% and 14.40% for TRAEs and grade ≥3 TRAEs, respectively. Another study by Guo et al. assessed the safety of fruquintinib (5 mg) + sintilimab (200 mg) in patients with mCRC and reported TRAEs in 100% of patients and grade ≥3 TRAEs in 36.4% of patients. Among all grade TRAEs, PPES and hypertension were observed in 50.0% and 31.8% of patients, respectively. The two most commonly reported grade ≥3 TRAEs were hypertension (13.6%) and PPES (9.1%) [25]. Similar TRAEs were observed in our study, in which the fruquintinib monotherapy and fruquintinib + ICI groups reported PPES in 19.70% and 24.14% of patients, respectively. Hypertension was reported in 17% and 15.82% of patients in the fruquintinib monotherapy and fruquintinib + ICI groups, respectively.

The incidence of AE in the current study was also lower than that reported in a recent real-world retrospective study by Qu et al. [31], in which the efficacy and safety of regorafenib monotherapy and regorafenib + ICI combination therapy were assessed in patients with mCRC. Qu et al. [31] reported that the incidence of any grade AEs in the regorafenib monotherapy group was 66.3%, and grade 3 or 4 TRAEs was 15.2%. For patients administered with regorafenib + ICI, any grade AEs and grade 3 or 4 TRAEs were reported in 87.8% and 13.7% of patients, respectively. The most common TRAE was PPES, reported in 17.7% of patients in the regorafenib monotherapy group and 26.6% of patients in the regorafenib + ICI group. Similarly, in the REGONIVO study [32], PPES was reported as the most common TRAE across all dose groups of regorafenib (80, 120, and 160 mg) with an overall incidence of 70%, followed by hypertension (48%) in patients with gastric cancer and CRC. These findings were consistent with our study, in which PPES and hypertension were the most common TRAEs across the groups; however, the incidences were lower than those reported in the REGONIVO study.

Furthermore, in this study, SAEs occurred in 11.33% of patients overall, with comparable rates in the monotherapy group (12.02%) and in the combination therapy group (9.68%). Rare fatal AEs were reported in 8 patients (0.29%) across all treatment groups: monotherapy group (n = 3) and combination therapy group (n = 5). Most fatal events were attributed to disease progression or severe complications, clinical management measures for SAEs primarily involved hospitalization, dose interruption, and supportive care interventions.

Although the present study did not formally assess the temporal patterns of AEs, findings from the Phase III FRESCO trial offer relevant context regarding AEs onset and management. In the FRESCO study, the median time to onset of hypertension, PPES/ HFSR, and proteinuria was 10, 21, and 20 days, respectively. The incidence of hypertension and proteinuria remained relatively consistent across treatment cycles, whereas HFSR increased during the first three cycles and remained relatively stable in the following cycles. Although the frequency of HFSR generally increased in the first three cycles in FRESCO, the frequency of HFSR (grade ≥3) in different cycles did not significantly increase by treatment cycle. These observations highlight the importance of proactive monitoring and early intervention, particularly during the initial treatment cycles [15].

Despite higher rates of AEs in the combination therapy groups compared with the monotherapy group, toxicity remained generally manageable across all subgroups. This is supported by the comparable relative-dose intensity (82.7% for combination therapy vs 85.3% for monotherapy) and the longer median exposure duration observed in combination regimens, indicating that AEs can be effectively managed through dose adjustments and supportive care.

Compared with other VEGFR inhibitors, fruquintinib offers a more selective profile, contributing to lower rates of PPES/hand-foot syndrome and fatigue than regorafenib, while maintaining similar risks of hypertension [4,32,33]. In addition, fruquintinib‑based combinations demonstrated lower incidences of common AEs such as fatigue, nausea, neutropenia, anemia, and hepatic dysfunction when compared with other third‑line combination regimens [34,35]. In contrast, fruquintinib-based combinations demonstrated manageable toxicity and sustained dose intensity in our study, supporting their use in younger, patients seeking prolonged disease control. Treatment selection should consider patient age, performance status, comorbidities, and prior therapy exposure to optimize outcomes.

Overall, this study provides valuable real-world evidence on fruquintinib-based combination therapies, supported by a large sample size and the inclusion of diverse combination regimens. These strengths enhance the relevance of our findings for current clinical practice. However, it is necessary to acknowledge the limitations of this study. In this study, subgroups were defined to reflect real-world prescribing patterns, including monotherapy, combinations with chemotherapy or ICIs, and triple therapy regimens. Although these strategies are supported by preclinical synergy and early clinical data, treatment choices were physician-driven, influenced by age, ECOG status, and prior therapies, with younger, and fitter patients more likely to receive combination regimens. Molecular markers (e.g., MSI, KRAS/BRAF) were not consistently available, introducing selection bias, and toxicity grading was according to routine clinical assessments and PROs, which lacked centralized review. In addition, overlap among 77 patients receiving both chemotherapy and ICIs may complicate subgroup comparisons. Sensitivity analysis was not feasible, but it is recommended for future studies.

The study primarily focused on safety outcomes, and efficacy endpoints such as PFS or OS were not included, which may be explored in future analyses. However, in this study, drug exposure time was considered as surrogate indicator of treatment efficacy, and AEs were graded according to routine clinical practice and PROs which reflex patients’ tolerability. Treatment decisions were made at the physician’s discretion, reflecting real-world practice but potentially introducing variability and selection bias. As a prospective observational analysis, the study is subject to inherent bias, and AEs were graded according to routine clinical practice and PROs without centralized review, which may lead to variability in toxicity assessment. Some patients received overlapping regimens, reflecting clinical complexity; this may limit subgroup interpretability. Additionally, while offering robust regional data exclusively from the Chinese population, validation in broader populations would strengthen generalizability. Follow-up duration was relatively short, and longer-term safety assessments could provide further insights. Despite these factors, the large sample size and a comprehensive safety evaluation enhance the clinical relevance of these findings. Future randomized studies incorporating efficacy outcomes and extended follow-up are needed to further validate these findings. In addition, real-world evidence complements randomized controlled trial data by capturing heterogeneity and practical tolerability in routine practice. Integrating findings from both sources can inform clinical guidelines and optimize treatment decisions for mCRC in global settings.

Conclusions

Fruquintinib demonstrated a good safety profile in patients with mCRC. This large-sample real-world study demonstrates that the combination therapy involving fruquintinib extended the duration of therapy with acceptable tolerability, suggesting a potentially improved prognosis in this patient population. This supports their use in appropriately selected patients, for whom the potential for improved efficacy may outweigh the incremental risk of toxicity. Clinicians should individualize treatment decisions by balancing anticipated efficacy benefits with tolerability considerations and proactive management of AEs. The safety profiles associated with fruquintinib treatment were consistent across the monotherapy and combination therapy groups. Further investigation is required to demonstrate the efficacy and safety of fruquintinib containing regimens in real-world settings.
