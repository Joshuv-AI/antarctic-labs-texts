Patient-Reported Barriers to Mammography Screening Among Women at a Community Health Clinic in Southeastern Michigan: A Cross-Sectional Pilot Study
Authors: Miranda Goodson, Karen Abdelmalek, Dina Bayachou, Rhea Sehgal, Dea Kanani, Whitney Bowyer, Alexis Kralovich, Anna Lundh, Sarah Qureshi, Nina Agemy, Laura J Benjamins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: The objective of this cross-sectional pilot study was to explore patient-reported barriers to mammography screening and women's knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors regarding mammography among women receiving care at a university-affiliated community health clinic in Southeastern Michigan (SEM).Methods: A 47-item investigator-developed exploratory questionnaire informed by the Health Belief Model (HBM) constructs was distributed via Qualtrics from August to December 2025 to women receiving care at a local community health clinic. The questionnaire assessed the participants' awareness, perceptions, attitudes, and comfort regarding mammogram screening. Descriptive statistics were calculated for completed questionnaires, and patient-reported barriers were categorized using prespecified response categories.Results: Twenty-one adult women aged 18 and older completed the questionnaire. Findings demonstrated that most participants were unaware of affordable or free mammogram services (81%, n = 17), while 28.6% (n = 6) reported language as a barrier in healthcare. The perception of mammography costs also varied across insurance groups.Conclusion: This community-focused pilot study provides preliminary insights into the perceptions, beliefs, attitudes, experiences, and potential barriers related to mammography screenings among women receiving care at a community health clinic in SEM. Although these preliminary findings should be interpreted with caution due to their exploratory study design and small convenience sample, they may inform future community-oriented research examining barriers to mammography screening and the development of region-specific educational and outreach strategies.

Introduction

Breast cancer remains the most commonly diagnosed cancer among American women [1]. Mammography is the most widely used screening modality for breast cancer and can detect early-stage, non-palpable malignancies, often before symptoms develop [1]. Current US Preventive Services Task Force guidelines recommend biennial screening mammography for average-risk women aged 40-74 years [2]. Widespread adoption of mammography has contributed to a substantial decline in breast cancer mortality in the United States between 1987 and 2017 [3].

Despite these well-established benefits, screening adherence remains suboptimal. National public health data indicate that only about 65% of US women are up to date with recommended mammography screening guidelines [4]. Numerous factors contribute to the underutilization of mammography, including lack of health insurance or underinsurance, out-of-pocket costs, limited access to healthcare facilities, transportation difficulties, competing work or caregiving responsibilities, and limited knowledge about screening [5,6]. Psychological and social barriers, such as fear of pain or diagnosis, mistrust of the healthcare system, and lack of an adequate healthcare provider recommendation, further reduce screening participation [5,7].

Importantly, mammography compliance varies significantly by race and socioeconomic status. Women of color are less likely to receive routine mammograms than White women, despite having similar or higher rates of breast cancer incidence and higher mortality rates [8]. Structural inequities, including neighborhood-level resource disparities, reduced access to preventive care, implicit bias in healthcare delivery, and historical and ongoing mistrust of medical institutions, contribute to these differences [5,9].

The health belief model (HBM) provided the conceptual framework for examining preventive health behaviors in this study. This model contextualizes health behaviors through an analysis of how individuals perceive a health threat and make decisions to prevent or respond to that threat [10]. The HBM constructs considered in this study included perceived barriers, perceived benefits, cues to action, and self-efficacy [10]. The objective of this cross-sectional pilot study was to explore patient-reported barriers to mammography screening and women's knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors regarding mammography among women at a university-affiliated community health clinic in Southeastern Michigan (SEM).

Materials and methods

This exploratory cross-sectional pilot study was conducted at a university-affiliated community health clinic in SEM from August to December 2025 (Figure 1). Women aged 18 years or older were invited to voluntarily complete the anonymous survey either through direct recruitment by a research team member during a clinic visit or by accessing the survey link or QR code provided on study flyers distributed throughout the clinic. Eligibility criteria included being a US citizen, identifying as a woman, being 18 years of age or older, and receiving care at the participating clinic. Women younger than the recommended age for routine screening were included because the broader pilot sought to explore mammography knowledge, attitudes, and barriers across the clinic population, including perspectives relevant to future screening. The number of eligible patients who were approached but declined participation was not recorded; therefore, a recruitment response rate could not be calculated. Surveys were available in English, Arabic, and Spanish. The English questionnaire was translated to Arabic and Spanish by bilingual research team members with relevant medical and cultural backgrounds to support cultural and linguistic appropriateness. Prior to participation, respondents reviewed an IRB-approved study information sheet, and completion of the survey indicated consent to participate. The study protocol (IRB-24-07-6999) was reviewed and approved by the Wayne State University Institutional Review Board.

The 47-item descriptive questionnaire collected demographic and background information and assessed mammography history, screening behaviors, awareness, perceived cost, healthcare access, and patient-reported barriers, as seen in Appendix 1. Although there are validated mammography screening instruments available, none completely aligned with the exploratory objectives and specific community context of this pilot study. Questionnaire items were developed by the research team based on the study objectives, relevant mammography screening literature, HBM constructs, and issues considered pertinent to the clinic population. The questionnaire was developed and distributed using Qualtrics (Qualtrics, Provo, UT) through the 2024-2025 platform version provided by Wayne State University School of Medicine. Data were collected, organized, and analyzed in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft® Corp., Redmond, WA).

Survey questions were optional, allowing the participants to omit questions based on their comfort level. Participants who achieved 100% survey progress in Qualtrics were included in the analytic sample. Participants who did not progress through the entire survey or had insufficient responses to barrier-related questions were excluded. A total of 35 survey attempts were recorded, with 21 participants meeting the inclusion criteria for this study's analysis, and 14 excluded due to incomplete survey attempts (Figure 2). Missing responses among the included 21 participants were retained and reported as "no response", where applicable, instead of being excluded from the overall analytic sample.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant responses. For any subgroup analyses, percentages were calculated using the corresponding subgroup denominator. A summary of patient-reported barriers was obtained using prespecified response categories included throughout the questionnaire. Barrier-related items were summarized using predefined response options, so no qualitative coding was performed. Since this was an exploratory pilot study designed to collect preliminary descriptive data, rather than test a hypothesis, no formal power calculation was conducted.

Discussion

Our pilot exploratory study identified several patient-reported elements pertinent to mammography screening amongst women receiving care at a community health clinic in SEM. Self-reported data demonstrated that 81.0% of participants were unaware of affordable or free mammography services, 28.6% reported language as a healthcare barrier, and perception of mammography cost differed across insurance types (Tables 3, 5). Despite these reported barriers, participants expressed general willingness to engage in mammography screening, with 57.1% of participants reporting that they were extremely likely to get their annual mammograms and 42.9% reporting that they were extremely likely to utilize affordable or free mammography services if available. With most participants identified as Middle Eastern/North African (MENA) women, our findings should be interpreted in this context. Previous research focused on Arab American women in metropolitan Detroit, the largest city in SEM, reported that 61.9% adhered to biennial mammography screening recommendations [11]. Similarly, 57.1% of participants in our cohort reported being extremely likely to obtain routine mammography screening (Table 3); of the 12 participants with this response, eight identified as MENA. Although direct comparisons cannot be made due to the small convenience sample and differences in outcome measures, these findings provide regional context for screening attitudes reported in our cohort.

The findings that 81.0% of participants in our study were unaware of affordable or free mammography services may represent an opportunity for more community-partnered education and outreach initiatives (Table 3). The Community Preventive Services Task Force recommends the addition of community health worker interventions (CHWI) to increase breast cancer screening. Across multiple studies, CHWI increased screening participation by 12.7%, with greater improvements when interventions addressed educational and access barriers, including the addition of translation services, resulting in a 30.2% increase in participation [12]. In the present study, 28.6% of participants reported language as a barrier to receiving healthcare, indicating that language-related obstacles were present within this cohort (Table 3). Previous CHWI in SEM have demonstrated improvements in breast cancer screening knowledge among Arab American women (p < 0.001) [13]. These interventions provided educational materials in participants' preferred language, with 68% of the Arab American participants requiring translated materials to participate [13].

Screening mammograms are generally covered at no out-of-pocket cost for Medicare beneficiaries and for most individuals with private insurance and Medicaid under the Affordable Care Act (ACA) [2,14]. Among the five Medicare participants, all reported a perceived mammography screening cost of $0 (Table 5). In contrast, only 40.0% of Medicaid participants and 22.2% of privately insured participants believed screening mammograms cost $0, while 66.7% of privately insured participants believed they cost more than $100 (Table 5). These findings may reflect differences in participant understanding of mammography costs and insurance coverage rather than actual out-of-pocket screening costs. Given the small subgroup sizes, these findings are presented descriptively and should not be interpreted as representative of differences between insurance groups. Previous studies have shown that many patients remain unaware of the ACA's zero cost-sharing requirement and often confuse screening with diagnostic mammography, which may involve additional out-of-pocket costs [2,14]. Indirect costs, including transportation, childcare, and time away from work, may further reduce screening participation [15]. Increasing awareness of insurance coverage and community-based screening programs may help reduce these misconceptions and improve mammography utilization [12,16].

The constructs of the HBM provided a theoretical framework for interpreting these descriptive findings. Participant-reported language challenges and cost misconceptions may reflect perceived barriers. Participants' willingness to obtain mammography and utilize affordable or free screening services may be related to perceived benefits and self-efficacy. Awareness of affordable screening programs, translation services and materials, and CHWI may function as cues to action [10].

This study has several limitations. The small sample size, recruitment from a single community healthcare clinic, and use of convenience sampling limit the generalizability of the findings and present the possibility of selection bias. The developed questionnaire and translated version were not previously validated. This may limit measurement reliability and comparability with studies using validated instruments. Inclusion of participants younger than the recommended age for routine breast cancer screening may have influenced screening-specific responses, while the reliance on self-reported data has the potential for recall and social desirability bias. The number of eligible patients who declined participation was not recorded; therefore, the recruitment response rate could not be calculated. Furthermore, no participants were older than 65 years of age. Subgroup analyses contained even smaller sample sizes, especially between insurance and demographic categories, and should be considered as descriptive only, since the study was not designed or powered to evaluate associations between participant characteristics and mammography behaviors.

As the first phase of an ongoing research initiative, these findings will be used to refine future iterations of the questionnaire by optimizing survey length, improving completion rates, expanding questions about mammography behaviors and cost perceptions, and incorporating additional cultural and socioeconomic items. The next version of our questionnaire will go through a formal evaluation and implement standardized procedures for monitoring recruitment and nonresponse.

Conclusions

This community-based pilot study serves as a foundational step toward understanding mammography screening experiences among women receiving care at a community health clinic in SEM. The findings include self-reported language barriers, limited awareness of affordable and accessible mammography screening services, and possible misconceptions regarding mammography costs and insurance coverage within this study population. Given the exploratory design, small convenience sample, and single-site recruitment, these findings should be interpreted with caution and are not intended to be generalized beyond this study's population. Future studies with larger and more representative samples across SEM can further evaluate these barriers and inform culturally responsive educational initiatives and community-centered interventions aimed at improving mammography utilization.
