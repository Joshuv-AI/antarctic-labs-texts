Inflammation and Immune Dysregulation Across Respiratory Diseases: From Cellular Mechanisms to Therapeutic Targets
Authors: Ashish Kumar, Patricia Silveyra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung inflammation and immune dysregulation are central to the pathogenesis of a broad spectrum of respiratory diseases, yet the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying these processes remain incompletely understood. This review examines mechanisms of pulmonary inflammation across major respiratory diseases, including asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, acute lung injury/acute respiratory distress syndrome, and pulmonary fibrosis. We discuss the roles of dysregulated innate and adaptive immunity, persistent inflammation, tissue remodeling, and impaired resolution in the pathogenesis of respiratory diseases, thereby highlighting mechanisms that are broadly conserved across these conditions as well as those that diverge in a disease-specific manner. This synthesis offers a framework for understanding pulmonary immune dysregulation and identifying new biomarkers and therapeutic strategies to restore pulmonary immune homeostasis. We also discuss how endotyping, single-cell transcriptomics, spatial biology, and multi-omics approaches are refining mechanistic understanding and enabling precision immunomodulatory interventions.

Introduction

Respiratory diseases collectively represent a major cause of morbidity and mortality, imposing a substantial burden on global health systems. Common chronic respiratory diseases (CRDs), including asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and occupational lung diseases, are important contributors to the rising burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) globally, with nearly 545 million individuals living with a chronic respiratory condition, representing 7.4% of the world’s population.1 Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) disproportionately bear a high burden of respiratory disease-related deaths and infections affecting individuals of all ages.2 As a highly specialized organ, the lung requires a tightly regulated immune surveillance system that balances antimicrobial defense with immunological tolerance to innocuous inhaled antigens.3 The pulmonary immune system comprises an intricate network of resident and recruited immune cells, including alveolar macrophages (AMs), dendritic cells (DCs), innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), mast cells, natural killer (NK) cells, neutrophils, T lymphocytes, and B lymphocytes, each executing specialized roles in homeostatic immune and inflammatory responses.4 Disruption of this equilibrium through factors like genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, or pathogen-driven immune activation underlies the pathogenesis of a broad spectrum of inflammatory pulmonary diseases. Emerging evidence suggests that immune dysregulation in respiratory diseases extends beyond classical inflammatory pathways, involving complex epithelial-immune crosstalk, tissue-resident immunity, cytokine imbalance, and microbiome-driven signaling that collectively shape disease onset and progression in asthma, COPD, pulmonary fibrosis, and lung injury.5–7 These observations have expanded our understanding of lung inflammation from a traditional consequence of infection or allergen exposure to a multifaceted process orchestrated at cellular, molecular, and spatial levels, thereby opening new avenues for precision-targeted immunotherapies and biomarker-driven interventions.

Chronic pulmonary inflammation contributes not only to immune dysregulation but also to structural remodeling of the airways. Emerging evidence suggests that intracellular signaling pathways regulating airway processes may influence remodeling responses and represent potential therapeutic targets for chronic inflammatory lung diseases.8,9 Epithelial cells, together with alveolar macrophages and DCs, serve as key sentinels of the respiratory tract by expressing a diverse repertoire of pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs), including toll-like receptors (TLRs), NOD-like receptors (NLRs), RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs), and C-type lectin receptors (CLRs), which initiate innate immune responses and contribute to pulmonary inflammation following microbial exposure.10,11 Engagement of these receptors by pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or endogenous damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released during inflammatory conditions activates downstream signaling through nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and interferon regulatory factor (IRF) pathways, driving the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and type I interferons.11,12 Adaptive immune responses mediated by T lymphocytes are central determinants of disease phenotype in chronic respiratory conditions. In allergic asthma, cluster of differentiation 4 (CD4+) T helper 2 (Th2) cells producing interleukin (IL)-4, IL-5, and IL-13 orchestrate immunoglobulin E (IgE) class switching in B cells, eosinophil recruitment, goblet cell metaplasia, and airway smooth muscle hyperresponsiveness.13,14 In severe corticosteroid-refractory asthma and COPD, Th1 and Th17 cells assume greater prominence; IL-17A and IL-17F secreted by Th17 and ILC3 populations promote neutrophil recruitment and activate airway epithelial cells to amplify inflammatory signaling.15 Regulatory T cells (Tregs), characterized by forkhead box P3 (Foxp3) expression and secretion of IL-10 and transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-β), serve as critical immunological brakes on effector T cell responses; quantitative or qualitative defects in Treg function have been documented in asthma, COPD, and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), suggesting that impaired immunoregulation is a shared pathogenic feature across these diseases.16,17

In this review, we provide a comprehensive synthesis of the cellular and molecular pathways driving pulmonary immune responses, inflammation, and remodeling. Specifically, we integrate current understanding of innate and adaptive immune mechanisms, cytokine networks, and molecular interactions across the major categories of respiratory disease. Our goal is to provide a conceptual framework for understanding pulmonary immune dysregulation and informing the rational design of immunomodulatory therapies for these conditions.

Cellular Drivers of Lung Inflammation

Innate Immune Cells

The innate immune system constitutes the first line of defense against pulmonary insults from allergens, pathogens, pollutants, cigarette smoke, and other inhaled or injury-associated stimuli. Resident and recruited innate cells collectively orchestrate the early inflammatory response in the lung, releasing cytokines, chemokines, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) that shape subsequent adaptive immune responses.18,19 Understanding the distinct contributions of these innate immune cell populations is essential for elucidating the cellular mechanisms that drive pulmonary inflammation and immune dysregulation in respiratory diseases.

Alveolar Macrophages

AMs are the predominant immune cells in the bronchoalveolar space, which act as sentinel cells that maintain tolerance to inhaled antigens and remain poised to respond to pathogenic stimuli.20 Upon activation, AMs can adopt classically activated macrophages (M1)-like or activated M2-type phenotypes, representing a shared polarization axis shaped by local cytokines, epithelial-derived signals, metabolic cues, and tissue injury.21 M1-like macrophages produce pro-inflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-12, driving acute inflammatory responses, while M2 macrophages secrete IL-10, TGF-β, and arginase-1 to promote resolution and tissue repair.22 Dysregulated AM function contributes to the pathogenesis of several chronic lung diseases. In COPD, AMs exhibit enhanced release of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and elastases that degrade the extracellular matrix, contributing to alveolar destruction and emphysema. In asthma, alternatively activated macrophages promote type 2 inflammation and airway remodeling.23,24 In ALI/ARDS, early M1-like activation drives epithelial cell death, while a later shift toward M2-like macrophages promotes alveolar epithelial proliferation and repair, illustrating that the same polarization axis can be protective or injurious.25 Thus, AMs emerge not merely as scavengers of the pulmonary microenvironment but as highly plastic immune cells that help orchestrate pulmonary immune homeostasis and disease-specific inflammatory responses.

Neutrophils

During pulmonary injury or infection, neutrophils are rapidly recruited by chemokines, particularly C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL1), CXCL2, CXCL5, and CXCL8/IL-8. As the most abundant circulating leukocytes, they are among the first cells to arrive in inflamed lung tissue. Their primary roles include phagocytosis of pathogens, release of cytotoxic mediators, and formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs).26 Neutrophil-derived mediators, including myeloperoxidase (MPO), neutrophil elastase (NE), and ROS, represent a shared arsenal critical for pathogen killing but can cause substantial collateral damage to host lung tissue when released in excess. Excessive or dysregulated neutrophil accumulation is a hallmark of acute lung injury (ALI) and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).27 In asthma, neutrophils are particularly associated with severe, steroid-resistant, and type 2-low (T2-low) phenotypes. Airway epithelial cells and resident immune cells release chemokines, which promote neutrophil recruitment through CXCR1/CXCR2 signaling pathways.28 Activated neutrophils release elastase, myeloperoxidase, MMPs, and NETs, which contribute to epithelial injury, mucus hypersecretion, airway remodeling, and persistent inflammation.28,29 In COPD, neutrophils represent a dominant inflammatory cell population within the airways and lung parenchyma. Exposure to cigarette smoke and other noxious stimuli induces the production of neutrophil chemoattractants, resulting in sustained neutrophilic inflammation.30 Distinguishing this recruitment machinery from its disease-specific downstream consequences is essential for identifying the most selective therapeutic target in each disease context.

Dendritic Cells

Pulmonary DCs form an extensive surveillance network spanning the airway epithelium, lung interstitium, and alveolar walls. They exist as two main subsets in the lung: conventional DCs (cDC1 and cDC2) and plasmacytoid DCs (pDCs), each with distinct but shared functional specializations across respiratory disease. Conventional DCs specialize in antigen capture and presentation, while plasmacytoid DCs are potent producers of type I interferons in response to viral infections.31,32 In allergic asthma, cDC2s play a central role in sensitization to aeroallergens by promoting Th2 polarization, while specific inflammatory contexts may also support Th17-skewed responses. In contrast, dysregulated pDC function has been linked to impaired antiviral immunity and exacerbation of inflammatory lung diseases. Tolerogenic DCs, which produce IL-10 and express programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), are critical for maintaining peripheral tolerance to innocuous inhaled antigens.33 In COPD, cigarette smoke exposure promotes myeloid DC accumulation and impaired maturation, driving CD4+ T cell differentiation toward Th1/Th17 responses rather than the Th2 polarization seen in asthma.34

Mast Cells

Mast cells are tissue-resident innate immune cells strategically positioned at mucosal surfaces, including the airway epithelium and areas surrounding pulmonary blood vessels, placing them at the interface between the environment and the host immune system. They express a wide repertoire of pattern-recognition receptors, including TLRs, as well as the high-affinity IgE receptor (FcεRI), a sensing machinery that mast cells deploy across allergic and non-allergic lung diseases alike, though the downstream consequences differ by disease context.35,36 In asthma, mast cells are key effector cells that contribute to both early- and late-phase allergic responses. Activation of FcεRI-bound IgE triggers mast cell degranulation, resulting in bronchoconstriction, mucus secretion, airway edema, and recruitment of eosinophils and Th2 cells.37 Mast cells are frequently localized within the airway smooth muscle bundles in asthma, where they promote airway hyperresponsiveness and remodeling through the release of proteases, growth factors, and type 2 cytokines.35 In COPD, mast cells are less prominent than neutrophils and macrophages but may contribute to chronic inflammation through the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, proteases, and angiogenic factors.38 Increased mast cell numbers have been reported in the small airways and lung parenchyma of some patients with COPD, where they have been associated with airway remodeling, mucus hypersecretion, and tissue destruction.38,39 In IPF, mast cells accumulate within fibrotic lesions and produce profibrotic mediators, including TGF-β, fibroblast growth factor (FGF), and proteases that promote fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix deposition.40,41 Mast cell activation is therefore a recurring feature of pulmonary inflammation, but the balance of mediators released and the tissue consequences that follow differ across respiratory diseases to warrant disease-specific therapeutic strategies.

Regulatory Innate Lymphocytes

Regulatory innate lymphocytes (ILCregs) are a subset of innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) that restrain excessive immune activation while supporting tissue homeostasis and repair. Regulatory ILC programs are generally associated with production of IL‑10 and/or TGF‑β and with suppression of inflammatory ILC1/ILC3 activity, a shared regulatory mechanism proposed to operate across lungs; however, their phenotype, developmental origin, and stability remain context- and tissue-dependent. Thus, defining regulatory innate-lymphocyte states in inflammatory disease may identify mechanisms that restore immune balance while avoiding broad immunosuppression.42,43 In allergic asthma, airway epithelial injury induced by allergens such as house dust mite (HDM) triggers release of IL-33, IL-25, and TSLP, which activate conventional ILC2s to produce IL-5 and IL-13. These effector ILC2 responses drive eosinophil recruitment, mucus hypersecretion, airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR), and type 2 inflammation. In contrast, IL-10-producing regulatory ILC2s are proposed to function as a counter-regulatory population: IL-10 inhibits ILC2 proliferation and type 2 cytokine production, thereby reducing eosinophil accumulation, and may limit the amplification of Th2-cell responses. Importantly, this mechanism is supported mainly by experimental and ex vivo evidence; the phenotype, frequency, and functional stability of regulatory ILC2s in human asthma require further validation.44,45 Overall, the underlying mechanism regulated by ILCregs may represent a potential mechanism for limiting excessive inflammation in the lung. Their phenotype and disease-specific functions in asthma, COPD, and other respiratory diseases remain largely unexplored, which remains a high-priority knowledge gap rather than an established mechanism.

Adaptive Immune Cells

The adaptive immune system provides antigen-specific responses that are essential for pathogen clearance, immune memory, and regulation of chronic inflammation in the lung. T and B lymphocytes are the principal effectors of adaptive immunity, each contributing distinctly to pulmonary inflammation.46,47 Consequently, alterations in adaptive immune function, activation, and intercellular signaling significantly contribute to the immune dysregulation and chronic inflammatory processes that characterize many respiratory diseases. Therefore, understanding the roles of these cells is critical for elucidating the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying pulmonary inflammation and immune dysregulation in respiratory diseases.

T Lymphocyte

T helper cells, or CD4⁺ T cells, are central orchestrators of pulmonary immune responses. Naive CD4⁺ T cells differentiate into distinct functional subsets in response to antigen stimulation and the cytokine environment shaped by innate immune cells, a shared differentiation program whose outcome and pathological consequence depend on which subset dominates in a given disease context.48 Th1 cells, driven by IL-12 and interferon gamma (IFN-γ) signaling and regulated by the transcription factor T-box transcription factor TBX21 (T-bet), are the principal mediators of host defense against intracellular pathogens such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis. They produce IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2, which activate macrophages and cytotoxic T cells. However, exaggerated Th1 responses are implicated in granulomatous lung diseases such as sarcoidosis and hypersensitivity pneumonitis.49 Th2 cells are defined by the transcription factor GATA-binding protein 3 (GATA-3) and the cytokines IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13. IL-4 drives IgE class switching in B cells, IL-5 promotes eosinophil differentiation, survival, recruitment, and activation, and IL-13 induces goblet cell hyperplasia and mucus hypersecretion. Th2 responses are the immunological hallmark of allergic asthma and atopic diseases, driving airway inflammation, hyperresponsiveness, and structural remodeling.50,51 Th17 cells, which develop under IL-6, TGF-β, IL-1β, and IL-23 signaling and are regulated by the transcription factor retinoic acid receptor-related orphan receptor gamma t (RORγt), produce IL-17A, IL-17F, and IL-22. These cytokines recruit neutrophils, enhance mucosal barrier function, and contribute to defense against extracellular bacteria and fungi. Dysregulated Th17 responses are associated with neutrophilic asthma, bronchiectasis, and autoimmune lung diseases.52

Tregs, characterized by the transcription factor Foxp3, are essential for maintaining immune homeostasis and preventing excessive lung inflammation. They exert their suppressive effects through IL-10, TGF-β, and IL-35 secretion, as well as direct cell-contact-dependent mechanisms. Defects in Treg function or number are associated with asthma severity, and Tregs are impaired in the lungs of patients with COPD, contributing to uncontrolled inflammation.48 Evidence suggests Tregs play a dual role in pulmonary fibrosis, suppressing Th1, Th17, and fibroblast CXCL12/CXCR4 signaling, while also promoting a Th2-associated reparative phenotype through secretion of TGF-β and Platelet-Derived Growth Factor-B (PDGF-B). The same study notes that Treg-derived TGF-β further triggers Wnt/β-catenin signaling, promoting Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT) as a source of myofibroblasts in fibrosis.53 Recent studies using distinct markers reinforce a protective role for Tregs in pulmonary fibrosis. CD103 expression distinguishes pathogenic effector CD4+ T cells from immunosuppressive Treg populations in the lung, with CD103hi Tregs constraining the fibrotic activity of CD103lo pathogenic T cells, while in a separate mouse model, Treg depletion worsened fibrosis and increased TGF-β and inflammatory cytokines, with Tregs instead expressing Th2-associated markers and reparative factors like amphiregulin and osteopontin that supported epithelial-fibroblast crosstalk. Together, these findings converge on a common theme that marker-defined Treg subsets restrain fibrotic progression through both suppressive and reparative mechanisms, underscoring their dual protective role across different models of lung fibrosis.54,55 Further refining our understanding of subset-specific function in the lung, additional Treg markers like Suppression of Tumorigenicity 2 (ST2), T-cell Immunoglobulin and Mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3), Killer Cell Lectin Like Receptor G1 (KLRG1), and Trefoil Factor 1 (Tff1) have been identified. ST2+ Tregs secrete amphiregulin and Keratinocyte Growth Factor (KGF) to drive Alveolar Type 2 (AT2) cell proliferation, while TIM-3+ Tregs limit lung injury by regulating macrophage polarization. KLRG1 marks a more terminally differentiated, activated Treg subset often co-expressed with CD103, and a newly described Tff1+ Treg population, induced via IL-33/ST2 signaling, has been shown to restrain exacerbation of bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis. Together, these markers reveal a functionally diverse lung Treg compartment that balances suppression, repair, and fibrosis control.56,57

B Cells and Antibody-Mediated Inflammation

B cells contribute to pulmonary inflammation through both antibody-dependent and antibody-independent mechanisms. In the context of lung infection and allergy, B cells differentiate into plasma cells that produce antigen-specific immunoglobulins, including IgG, IgA, and IgE. Secretory IgA is particularly important for mucosal immunity in the airways, where it neutralizes pathogens at the epithelial surface before systemic invasion occurs.58 IgE produced in response to allergen exposure binds to FcεRI receptors on mast cells and basophils, sensitizing them for rapid degranulation upon allergen re-exposure. This IgE-mediated mechanism is the molecular basis of type I hypersensitivity reactions in allergic asthma. Elevated serum total IgE and allergen-specific IgE are therefore cardinal biomarkers of allergic airway disease.59 Beyond IgE-mediated responses, B cells can form tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) within chronically inflamed lung tissue, a shared pattern of ectopic lymphoid neogenesis across chronic respiratory diseases. In IPF, these ectopic lymphoid organs, which contain germinal center-like B cell zones and follicular helper T cells, facilitate local antigen-driven antibody responses and are associated with autoimmune-like features in chronic lung disease.60

Tissue-Resident Memory T Cells

Tissue-resident memory T (Trm) cells are a population of memory T cells that reside in tissues and contribute to frontline immune protection. Lung Trm cells are important for local immunity against respiratory pathogens; however, their function has been studied predominantly in mouse models, in which they undergo rapid attrition, thereby highlighting the importance of local immunological memory in the human lung.61 A study in a chronic HDM-exposure mouse model shows that allergen-specific CD4+ Trm cells persisted around the airways and were rapidly reactivated locally after HDM re-exposure, independently of circulating T cells. Their activation induced IL-4, IL-5, IL-17, IFN-γ, and TNF-α production and was associated with dendritic-cell activation/recruitment and rapid airway hyperresponsiveness, supporting a mechanism by which CD4+ Trm cells perpetuate recurrent allergic airway inflammation.62 In COPD, Trm cells are increased in the lung, particularly within small-airway walls, and CD8+ Trm cells may contribute to persistent local inflammation and disease progression. In IPF, single-cell RNA-sequencing studies have shown increased CD4+ and CD8+ Trm cells, with enrichment of IFN-γ-response, T-cell activation, and signaling pathways; however, whether these cells directly drive fibrosis or reflect ongoing pulmonary inflammation remains to be determined.63 In conclusion, Trm cells are critical mediators of site-specific immunological memory in the lung. Although their protective role in respiratory infection is well established, the mechanisms that convert Trm-mediated protection into pathogenic inflammation in asthma, COPD, pulmonary fibrosis, and other chronic lung diseases remain incompletely understood.

Structural Cells

Structural cells of the lung, including epithelial cells, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts, actively participate in the initiation, amplification, and perpetuation of lung inflammation and remodeling through the production of cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and extracellular matrix components.64,65 Accordingly, understanding the diverse functions of structural cells is essential for elucidating the cellular and molecular mechanisms that drive pulmonary inflammation, immune dysregulation, and tissue remodeling across respiratory diseases.

Epithelial Cells

The airway and alveolar epithelium constitute the primary physical and immunological barrier against inhaled pathogens, pollutants, and allergens. The pseudostratified airway epithelium is composed of ciliated cells, goblet cells, club cells, and basal progenitor cells, while the alveolar epithelium consists of type I pneumocytes, which mediate gas exchange, and type II pneumocytes, which produce surfactant and support epithelial regeneration.64,66 Alveolar type I (AT1) and type II (AT2) epithelial cells have distinct but complementary roles in maintaining alveolar homeostasis and responding to lung injury. Loss or dysfunction of AT1 cells compromises alveolar-capillary barrier integrity, increases epithelial permeability, and contributes to alveolar flooding characteristic of lung injury. In contrast, AT2 cells actively participate in pulmonary immune regulation by sensing tissue injury and producing cytokines and chemokines that influence inflammatory cell recruitment and activation. Thus, AT1 cells primarily maintain barrier integrity and gas exchange, whereas AT2 cells integrate epithelial repair with surfactant production and inflammatory signaling, highlighting their complementary contributions to both the injury response and the amplification of inflammation.67 Epithelial cells are primary sensors of environmental insults, expressing pattern-recognition receptors, including TLRs, NLRs, and Retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I)-like receptors. Upon activation, they release an array of innate cytokines and alarmins, including IL-25/IL-17E, IL-33, and thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP). These alarmins are pivotal amplifiers of type 2 immunity, activating type 2 ILC2s, mast cells, and DCs to initiate or perpetuate allergic inflammation.65,68 Epithelial barrier dysfunction, resulting from cigarette smoke, viral infection, or allergen-derived proteases, allows increased penetration of antigens into the submucosa, exacerbating immune activation. Repeated injury and aberrant epithelial repair lead to EMT, goblet cell metaplasia, and subepithelial fibrosis, which are key features of airway remodeling in asthma and COPD.66,68 During ARDS, classically activated (M1) macrophages preferentially induce AT1 cell death while promoting AT2 expansion, and the subsequent AT2-to-AT1 differentiation represents a rate-limiting checkpoint for restoring alveolar structure and resolving inflammation.25 Epithelial cells therefore function not merely as a passive barrier but as active determinants of disease trajectory, and defining the mechanisms that govern their injury and repair is a necessary first step toward the therapeutic targets discussed in later sections.

Endothelial Cells

The pulmonary vascular endothelium plays a critical role in regulating leukocyte trafficking into inflamed lung tissue. Under homeostatic conditions, endothelial cells maintain an anti-inflammatory, antithrombotic state. During inflammation, exposure to cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β activates endothelial cells and induces the expression of adhesion molecules, including E-selectin, Intercellular Adhesion Molecule 1 (ICAM-1), and Vascular Cell Adhesion Molecule 1 (VCAM-1).69,70 These adhesion molecules mediate the sequential steps of leukocyte recruitment: rolling, firm adhesion, and transendothelial migration. Chemokines produced by activated endothelial cells, including CXCL8, CXCL10, and C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2), establish haptotactic gradients that direct neutrophil and monocyte extravasation into inflamed lung parenchyma. This endothelial-leukocyte crosstalk is a central amplification step in acute and chronic pulmonary inflammation.71 In severe lung inflammation, such as ARDS, endothelial barrier disruption leads to increased permeability, protein-rich edema accumulation in the alveolar space, and impaired gas exchange. Endothelial injury also triggers procoagulant changes, including tissue factor expression, platelet activation, and fibrin deposition, contributing to microvascular thrombosis observed in severe Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) and other forms of severe respiratory failure.71–73

Fibroblasts

Pulmonary fibroblasts are mesenchymal cells residing in the lung interstitium that maintain extracellular matrix (ECM) structure and support tissue repair. They synthesize collagen (primarily types I and III), fibronectin, elastin, and proteoglycans, providing the mechanical scaffold essential for lung architecture and function. Under normal conditions, fibroblast activity is tightly regulated to balance ECM synthesis and degradation.74,75 In response to inflammatory mediators, particularly TGF-β, PDGF, and IL-13, fibroblasts undergo differentiation into myofibroblasts, characterized by α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) expression and contractile properties. Myofibroblasts produce excessive ECM components, driving progressive fibrosis. This pathological fibroblast activation is a central mechanism underlying IPF and fibrotic sequelae of acute lung injury.75–77 Fibroblasts are not merely passive effectors but actively modulate lung inflammation. They produce cytokines including IL-6, IL-8, and CCL2; express co-stimulatory molecules that interact with lymphocytes; and respond to IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-1β with further amplification of inflammatory signaling.74 Fibroblast heterogeneity in the lung has emerged as a critical determinant of disease-specific remodeling patterns. Single-cell transcriptomic studies distinguish at least four spatially defined subpopulations: adventitial fibroblasts surrounding airways and large vessels, peribronchial fibroblasts beneath the airway epithelium, alveolar fibroblasts within alveolar walls, and ductal myofibroblasts at alveolar ducts, each occupying a distinct anatomic niche with a corresponding functional role in repair versus fibrosis.78 Alveolar fibroblasts in particular have been identified as the principal lineage giving rise to pathological myofibroblasts in pulmonary fibrosis, directly linking a specific spatial subpopulation, rather than fibroblasts as a uniform population, to disease-driving ECM deposition and inflammatory signaling.79 This spatial and functional heterogeneity indicates that fibroblast contribution to inflammation and fibrosis cannot be captured by a single activation state, and resolving which subpopulations drive pathological remodeling in each disease context is necessary for identifying fibroblast-directed therapeutic targets, a point returned to in later sections.

Airway Smooth Muscle Cells

Airway smooth muscle cells (ASMCs) are increasingly recognized as key contributors to pulmonary inflammation, immune dysregulation, and airway remodeling, with roles extending beyond their traditional regulation of airway tone and luminal diameter. By controlling airway contractility, airway smooth muscle (ASM) cells directly influence airway narrowing and airflow limitation, particularly in obstructive airway diseases such as asthma. However, accumulating evidence demonstrates that ASM cells also function as active immunomodulatory cells that respond to inflammatory stimuli and participate in airway hyperresponsiveness, inflammatory mediator production, and structural remodeling processes.80 ASMCs also contribute directly to immune dysregulation by producing inflammatory mediators such as IL-6, IL-8 (CXCL8), CCL2, CCL5, and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF). These factors regulate recruitment and activation of eosinophils, neutrophils, macrophages, and T lymphocytes, establishing reciprocal communication between structural and immune compartments of the airway.81 Beyond immune cell regulation, ASMCs are central mediators of airway remodeling, a pathological process characterized by increased airway wall thickness, fibrosis, and irreversible airflow limitation. Chronic inflammatory stimulation induces ASMC proliferation and phenotypic switching from a contractile phenotype toward a synthetic phenotype. This phenotypic transition is regulated by multiple inflammatory and growth factor signaling pathways, including NF-κB, MAPK, and TGF-β signaling, which promote transcriptional activation of remodeling-associated genes.82 Recent evidence suggests that impaired corticosteroid responsiveness contributes to persistent airway inflammation in severe asthma. Inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-17, and IFN-γ, can disrupt glucocorticoid receptor (GR) signaling by increasing GRβ expression, reducing GRα nuclear translocation, and impairing glucocorticoid-mediated suppression of inflammatory genes. These alterations promote sustained activation of pathways such as NF-κB, contributing to steroid-insensitive inflammation and persistent airway remodeling.83

Molecular Mechanisms of Inflammation

Cytokine and Chemokine Networks

Primary Pro-Inflammatory Cytokines

Primary proinflammatory cytokines, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, are central modulators of pulmonary inflammation, acting through receptor-mediated classical signaling as well as through non-canonical NF-κB and the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain, Leucine-rich repeat, and Pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) signaling axis, to orchestrate downstream cytokine cascades implicated in lung injury and broader pulmonary pathology.84 In ARDS, uncontrolled cytokine-driven inflammation activates alveolar macrophages and neutrophil recruitment. Persistent activation of these cellular effectors engages Janus kinase/Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription (JAK/STAT) and NF-κB signaling pathways, perpetuating lung injury and aberrant extracellular deposition.85 In chronic inflammatory airway diseases like COPD, bronchiectasis, and allergic asthma, elevated cytokine levels correlate inversely with lung function. IL-1β and IL-6 have been specifically implicated in COPD, associated with increased mortality and distinct phenotypic subtypes, while TNF-α has been shown to synergistically augment macrophage IL-8 production, contributing to asthma development.86 IL-1β and IL-6 also play a significant role in the development and progression of pulmonary fibrosis, driving inflammation, promoting EMT, stimulating fibroblast proliferation, and tissue remodeling. These overlapping mechanisms underscore the importance of examining the crosstalk among immune cells, structural lung cells, and fibrotic mediators in disease pathogenesis.87 Among the key pro-inflammatory cytokines implicated in lung disease, IL-33 and its receptor ST2 form a signaling axis with wide-ranging effects on airway inflammation and remodeling, contributing to tissue repair through modulation of fibroblast activity and extracellular matrix production while also influencing adaptive immunity via effects on T cell subsets.88 In COPD specifically, excessive activation of the IL-33/ST2 pathway can drive an exaggerated inflammatory response along with hallmark symptoms like airway wall thickening and fibrosis, increasing the risk of acute exacerbations.89 Given this mechanistic role, Astegolimab, a selective anti-ST2 monoclonal antibody, has been evaluated in Phase 2b and 3 trials in patients with COPD and a history of frequent exacerbations, regardless of baseline blood eosinophil counts.90 Targeting individual cytokines within these pro-inflammatory networks has therefore become an active area of therapeutic development, representing promising future targets for disease-modifying therapy.

Type 2 Inflammatory Cytokines

Type-2 asthma is the predominant asthma subtype, characterized by elevated IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, and activation of type-2 inflammatory pathways. The interaction between these Th2 cell-derived cytokines and airway epithelial cells (AECs), and DCs amplifies type 2 inflammation and promotes neutrophil infiltration, collectively driving disease progression toward severe, uncontrolled asthma.91 IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 contribute distinctly to the development of bronchial asthma. IL-5 mediates eosinophil differentiation and B-cell activation and differentiation, augmenting allergic inflammation, while IL-13 induces goblet cell differentiation in structural cells, such as epithelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and fibroblasts, promoting mucus hypersecretion and airway remodeling. IL-4 complements these effects by driving IgE biosynthesis and Th2 cell differentiation, reinforcing the structural and immunological alterations that characterize asthmatic airways.51,92 Alarmin signals represent important upstream drivers of the type 2 inflammatory axis. In allergic asthma, cells like Th2, ILC2s, mast cells, basophils, and eosinophils collectively produce pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-4 and IL-13, thereby promoting IgE synthesis and mast cell granulation. Concurrently, IL-5 released by Th2 cells and ILCs drives eosinophil activation, underlining a distinct inflammatory niche of asthma endotype.86,91

Chemokine Signaling

Chemokines translate cytokine-driven alarm signals into the direct migration of inflammatory cells to sites of lung injury. In pulmonary infections and acute lung injury, the glutamic acid–leucine–arginine motif-positive CXC chemokines (ELR⁺ CXC) chemokine subfamily is the principal mediator of neutrophil recruitment. CXCR2, expressed on neutrophils and some non-hematopoietic lung cells, contributes to neutrophil recruitment into the lung by regulating the availability of its cognate chemokines in the alveolar space.93 In humans, CXCL8 is a potent neutrophil activator under inflammatory conditions, and its biological activities are mediated through binding to two G protein-coupled receptors, CXCR1 and CXCR2, located on the cell surface.94 The CC chemokine family contributes to both monocyte recruitment and, under specific conditions, neutrophil chemotaxis. CCL2 and CCL7 protein levels are elevated in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid from patients with ARDS, and neutrophil chemotaxis toward ARDS BAL fluid can be partly attributed to these chemokines alongside CXCL8. Together, the CXCL and CCL axes constitute an integrated chemokine network that amplifies and directs the cellular inflammatory response in acute and chronic lung diseases.95

Pattern Recognition and Innate Signaling

Toll-Like Receptors

TLRs are members of the PRRs family that serve as primary activators of the innate immune response through recognition of PAMPs and DAMPs. TLRs are found in cells like macrophages, DCs, B lymphocytes, and T lymphocytes and tend to regulate the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and interferons by activating key transcription factors, thereby playing a crucial role in the regulation of pulmonary inflammation.96 TLRs selectively recruit adaptor proteins like myeloid differentiation primary response protein 88 (myD88), TIR-domain-containing adaptor inducing IFN-β (TRIF), TIR-domain-containing adaptor protein, also known as Mal (TIRAP), and TRIF-related adaptor molecule (TRAM) to activate functionally distinct signaling pathways that result in the production of proinflammatory cytokines and type I interferons. TLR4 is distinctive in its capacity to engage with myD88-TIRAP and TRIF-TRAM signaling axes, enabling robust inflammatory cytokine production.93,97 Downstream signaling of TLRs is mainly through the cytoplasmic Toll/IL-1 receptor (TIR) domain, which interacts with myD88-TRIF proteins, thereby activating interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase (IRAK) and TRAF6 to drive pro-inflammatory cytokine production. Simultaneously, TLRs potentiate the TAK1 (TGF-β-activated kinase 1) complex, which serves to activate downstream signaling MAPK cascade leading to c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) and p38 amplification, further amplifying inflammatory transcriptional pathway.11 In inflammatory diseases like asthma, TLRs function as both protective and determinant. The activation of two of its subtypes TLR2/4 on airway epithelial cells and antigen-presenting cells drives the release of inflammatory mediators, thereby worsening airway inflammation and hyperresponsiveness. However, activation of TLR9 may lead to the production of class I IFNs that counteract Th2-biased immune responses.81,98 In COPD, sustained exposure to cigarette smoking and environmental hazards alters TLR expression and downstream signaling, with TLR2/4 and −9 unleashing activation of proinflammatory cytokine mediators that recruit neutrophils and macrophages into the airways, thereby sustaining the cycle of inflammation, structural tissue damage, and disease progression of the airway.99

The NLRP3 Inflammasome

NLRP3 is a tripartite protein that gets induced through PAMPs and DAMPs, leading to NF-κB activation and gene transcription. The NLRP3 inflammasome complex activates a caspase-1-dependent pathway that results in the release of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18, while simultaneously engaging gasdermin D-mediated downstream effects.100 NLRP3 inflammasome activation is proposed to be a two-signal model: the priming signal and activation signal. Caspase-8 and FAS-mediated death domain protein (FADD) and Nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-containing protein 1/2 (NOD1/2) are involved in the priming step by regulating the NF-κB pathway, leading to post-translational activation of NLRP3. The activation phase involves molecular and cellular events like ionic flux, mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS generation, and lysosomal damage to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome cascade pathway.101 Under the pathological conditions that characterize ALI, NLRP3 activation sets off a caspase-1-dependent cascade that drives the release of IL-1β and IL-18, compounding lung injury. Elevated IL-18 levels have been documented in ARDS patients and carry prognostic significance, correlating with adverse outcomes. Through this sustained pro-inflammatory signaling, the NLRP3 inflammasome functions as a key driver of ALI progression, positioning it as an attractive target for therapeutic intervention.102,103 A recent study has also shown that NLRP3 activation is the key link that bridges the initial infection trigger and the downstream inflammatory cascade that ultimately shapes the clinical manifestations of acute lung-related exacerbations.104 The NLRP3 inflammasome complex is also a central mediator of chronic inflammation in both asthma and COPD. In asthma, dysregulated NLRP3 activation drives the release of inflammatory cytokines, IL-1β and IL-18, promoting downstream T-helper cell polarization and amplifying allergic airway inflammation. In COPD, repeated exposure to cigarette smoke and environmental pollutants functions as a persistent NLRP3-activating signal, sustaining caspase-1-dependent cytokine release and perpetuating airway neutrophilia and tissue destruction. Across both diseases, aberrant NLRP3 inflammasome activation represents a shared pathological mechanism rooted in dysregulated innate immune responses to genetic and environmental triggers.105

Key Inflammatory Pathways and Therapeutic Targets in Respiratory Diseases

Recent advances in respiratory immunology have identified several inflammatory pathways that contribute to pulmonary inflammation, immune dysregulation, and tissue remodeling while also serving as potential therapeutic targets. Type 2 cytokines, particularly IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, play central roles in eosinophilic inflammation, airway hyperresponsiveness, and mucus production, leading to the successful development of biologic therapies targeting these pathways in asthma and related airway diseases.106,107 This approach has produced clinically relevant therapeutics, such as mepolizumab and benralizumab (anti-IL-5/IL-5Rα) and dupilumab (anti-IL-4Rα), which are approved for severe asthma. Depemokimab, an ultra-long-acting anti-IL-5 biologic dosed twice yearly, received US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval in December 2025 as the first biologic in this class with a twice-yearly dosing interval.108 Beyond immune dysregulation, structural remodeling processes play a critical role in the progression of chronic pulmonary diseases. Consequently, molecular pathways regulating airway structural cells have emerged as promising therapeutic targets for mitigating pathological tissue remodeling and preserving lung function.109 The JAK-STAT signaling pathway has also emerged as an important mediator of cytokine-driven inflammatory responses and represents a promising target for precision immunomodulation.107 This finding has prompted the relevance of inhaled lung-selective JAK1 inhibitors, including londamocitinib (AZD4604) and AZD0449, which remain in early-phase clinical development specifically for asthma.110,111 NF-κB functions as a master regulator of inflammatory gene transcription and contributes to the sustained production of cytokines, chemokines, and adhesion molecules that characterize chronic inflammatory lung diseases.112 Given its established role in disease pathogenesis, lung-targeted NF-κB inhibitors such as CKD-497 remain at the preclinical stage in models of asthma and particulate-matter-induced airway inflammation.113 In parallel, activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome promotes the maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18, thereby amplifying innate immune responses and contributing to the pathogenesis of asthma, COPD, ARDS, and pulmonary fibrosis.114,115 Canakinumab, an anti-IL-1β antibody, is marketed for autoinflammatory conditions;116 DFV890, an oral NLRP3 inhibitor, has been tested directly in a Phase 2a trial in patients with COVID-19 pneumonia and impaired respiratory function.117 Beyond cytokines and immune mediators, intracellular signaling regulators are increasingly recognized as therapeutic targets in pulmonary disease, representing a potential strategy for mitigating airway hyperresponsiveness.118 Oxidative stress further perpetuates pulmonary inflammation through the activation of redox-sensitive signaling pathways and has been implicated in corticosteroid resistance and progressive tissue injury.112 Given its increasing recognition, N-acetylcysteine is a marketed antioxidant used clinically in COPD,119 whereas Nrf2 activators aimed at reversing cellular senescence and corticosteroid resistance remain largely at the preclinical and early-phase clinical stage.120,121 Additionally, epigenetic mechanisms, including histone deacetylases, have emerged as important regulators of inflammatory gene expression and immune-cell function, providing new opportunities for therapeutic intervention.122 Histone Deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors, such as RGFP966, are used to restore NRF2 activity in models of pulmonary fibrosis, a strategy that remains at the Future Directions

Respiratory diseases exhibit substantial clinical and biological heterogeneity. Increasing evidence suggests that patients with similar clinical presentations may have distinct underlying molecular and immunological mechanisms, highlighting the limitations of traditional disease classifications based solely on symptoms and lung function measurements.124,125 Increasing recognition of asthma heterogeneity has prompted efforts to classify patients into distinct phenotypes and endotypes, facilitating the development of precision medicine approaches.126 The successful implementation of biologic therapies targeting type 2 inflammation in asthma demonstrates the clinical value of endotyping. Biomarkers such as blood eosinophils, serum IgE, and fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) have facilitated the identification of patients who are most likely to benefit from therapies targeting IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, or IgE signaling pathways.127,128 Future studies integrating genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic datasets may further refine disease stratification and improve the selection of targeted therapies across a broader range of inflammatory respiratory disorders.125,129 The development of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has transformed the study of lung biology by enabling transcriptomic profiling at single-cell resolution. This technology has revealed substantial cellular heterogeneity within both healthy and diseased lungs and has facilitated the identification of previously unrecognized epithelial, immune, endothelial, and stromal cell populations.130,131 Large-scale efforts such as the Human Lung Cell Atlas have generated comprehensive cellular maps of the human respiratory system, providing important reference datasets for understanding disease-associated alterations in cellular composition and function.130 In inflammatory and fibrotic lung diseases, scRNA-seq studies have identified disease-associated macrophage populations, activated fibroblast subsets, and aberrant epithelial cell states that may contribute to persistent inflammation and tissue remodeling.132,133 Furthermore, single-cell analyses have improved our understanding of intercellular communication networks that regulate immune responses within the lung microenvironment, thereby facilitating the identification of novel therapeutic targets.131,132 Although scRNA-seq provides high-resolution transcriptional information, the tissue dissociation process required for cell isolation results in the loss of spatial information. Spatial transcriptomic technologies overcome this limitation by enabling gene expression analysis while preserving tissue architecture and cellular localization.134,135 Recent studies have demonstrated the utility of spatial transcriptomics in characterizing inflammatory and fibrotic microenvironments within diseased lungs. In IPF, spatial transcriptomic analyses have identified distinct pathological niches containing aberrant epithelial cells, activated fibroblasts, and profibrotic macrophages that colocalize within regions of active fibrosis.135 Looking beyond single-cytokine blockade, the current biopharmaceutical landscape in COPD and asthma is increasingly shaped by a shared set of upstream and downstream type 2 inflammatory targets. A growing number of biologic therapies have been developed targeting the IL-4 receptor, IL-5 and its receptor, IL-33, ST2, and TSLP, with newer molecules such as JKN2401, tezepelumab, and TQC2731 now entering trials alongside more established agents, thereby necessitating better patient stratification.88 In this context, Itepekimab, an anti-IL-33 antibody developed by Regeneron and Sanofi, was evaluated in the Phase 3 AERIFY-1 and AERIFY-2 trials in former smokers with moderate-to-severe COPD who had not smoked for at least six months, following earlier phase 2 data showing reduced exacerbations and improved lung function in this subgroup, thereby providing important insights into the therapeutic potential of IL-33 inhibition in COPD.136 In parallel with biologic therapies, small-molecule approaches targeting intracellular inflammatory signaling are also advancing in asthma. Londamocitinib (AZD4604), a highly selective inhaled JAK1 inhibitor developed by AstraZeneca, is being evaluated in the phase 2a AJAX trial, a study in adults with moderate-to-severe asthma.137 These findings provide important insights into how cellular interactions are organized within diseased tissues and how local microenvironments contribute to disease progression. The integration of spatial transcriptomics with single-cell sequencing, proteomics, and imaging technologies is expected to generate increasingly detailed cellular atlases of respiratory diseases and enhance our understanding of lung immune dysregulation.134,135 Collectively, advances in endotyping, single-cell transcriptomics, and spatial biology are reshaping our understanding of lung inflammation and immune dysregulation. Continued integration of these technologies into translational and clinical research will be essential for developing more effective and personalized therapeutic strategies for respiratory diseases.

Conclusion

Lung inflammation is a highly coordinated and dynamic process involving complex interactions among immune cells, structural cells of the respiratory tract, and diverse molecular signaling pathways. While acute inflammatory responses are essential for host defense and tissue repair, persistent or dysregulated activation of these mechanisms can lead to chronic inflammation, immune dysregulation, tissue remodeling, and progressive loss of pulmonary function. Across respiratory diseases such as asthma, COPD, ARDS, pulmonary fibrosis, and respiratory infections, aberrant communication between epithelial cells, macrophages, neutrophils, eosinophils, and lymphocytes drives disease initiation, progression, and exacerbation. These cellular responses are further regulated by intricate molecular networks involving cytokines, chemokines, pattern-recognition receptors, oxidative stress pathways, and intracellular signaling cascades. As highlighted throughout this review, many respiratory diseases share common inflammatory and immune-dysregulatory mechanisms despite their distinct clinical manifestations, underscoring the value of examining pulmonary inflammation through an integrated cellular and molecular framework. Figure 3 illustrates this convergence, depicting how epithelial injury, immune cell activation, and fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition drive the fibrotic and remodeling endpoints common to these conditions. Advances in our understanding of these pathways have provided important insights into disease heterogeneity and revealed novel therapeutic opportunities. The identification of specific inflammatory endotypes and immune signatures has facilitated the development of targeted therapies, including biologics directed against key cytokines and signaling pathways. Furthermore, emerging high-resolution technologies and multi-omics approaches are rapidly expanding our understanding of the cellular interactions and molecular networks that govern pulmonary inflammation, making this an especially timely area of investigation. Emerging strategies that modulate immune cell function, restore epithelial barrier integrity, reduce oxidative stress, or promote the resolution of inflammation hold significant promise for improving clinical outcomes. Continued integration of mechanistic studies with translational and clinical research will be essential for advancing precision medicine strategies and developing more effective interventions for inflammatory respiratory diseases. A comprehensive understanding of lung immune and airway dysregulation will be critical for preventing disease progression and improving respiratory health worldwide.
