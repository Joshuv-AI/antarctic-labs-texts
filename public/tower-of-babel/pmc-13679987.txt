With a primary goal of protecting the physical and mental health of children and youth in Canada, the federal government introduced Bill C-34, the Safe Social Media Act, on June 10, 2026;1 as of September 2026, it is under parliamentary consideration. Through this legislation, the government seeks to minimize exposure of children and youth to harmful online content, promote safe digital platform design, and ensure child sexual abuse material and nonconsensual intimate imagery are inaccessible at any age, while respecting people’s privacy and freedom of expression. Through fall 2026, members of parliament and senators will study the bill, hear from expert witnesses — including clinicians and other interested parties — and consider relevant evidence before finalizing the legislation for a vote.

We examine leading international approaches to online safety policy through a child health lens and assess the extent to which Canada’s Safe Social Media Act contains the essential elements of a successful framework for online safety. We argue that Canada has an opportunity to catch up with other jurisdictions and build on their experience by establishing a regulator with sufficient scope, authority, and adaptability to address current and emerging digital harms. Because digital environments transcend national borders, Canada’s response will form part of a broader international effort through the Global Online Safety Regulators Network to protect children online. We also outline a whole-of-society approach that builds on the legislation as a core component of a comprehensive strategy for online safety, including collaboration across levels of government, funding for digital literacy initiatives, and active engagement from parents, educators, clinicians, and child advocates.

What does the Safe Social Media Act include?

If passed into law, the Safe Social Media Act would introduce a digital safety framework setting out rules for online services, including social media platforms, artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots, and other specified services, and establish the Digital Safety and Data Protection Commission of Canada. As proposed, this commission would have powers to hear complaints, investigate platform companies, and order compliance, including by imposing financial penalties.

Although public discourse has focused on the proposed social media age restrictions for people younger than 16 years, the legislation would not broadly prevent children and youth from accessing the Internet or viewing publicly available online content. Instead, social media platforms would be required to restrict people younger than 16 years from creating accounts unless those platforms meet specific safety standards. Therefore, Canada’s legislation is a variation of the age restrictions that are gathering momentum globally to support safe engagement. Following passage of the legislation, required mechanisms for age assurance, child safety design standards, and other operational details would be determined through regulation or by cabinet directive.

What benefits and harms do social and interactive online platforms have for children and youth?

Interacting with online services can provide young people with opportunities for learning, social connection and support, creativity, and exploring their identity, particularly for those experiencing social exclusion in offline settings.2 Some digital spaces expand access to educational resources,3 civic and cultural participation,4 health information, and forms of peer engagement3 that may be difficult to access in a young person’s immediate offline environment. Yet these same environments can expose children and youth to harmful content, including material that promotes self-harm, disordered eating, violence, hate, or other risks to health and well-being.2 Marginalized young people may face additional risks, as online spaces can reproduce social hierarchies through bullying, harassment, and other forms of exclusion.2

Studies have consistently identified associations between digital media use and adverse health outcomes among young people, including depression, anxiety, suicidality, and sleep disruption.5,6 A recent randomized controlled trial involving youth in Canada with elevated emotional distress demonstrated that limiting social media use to 1 hour per day over 3 weeks resulted in significant reductions in symptoms of depression and anxiety, alongside improvements in sleep, which is required for good physical and mental health.7 However, digital benefits and harms are complex and shaped not only by screen time but also by the context of engagement and design of the digital platform.

For many digital platforms, advertising-based revenue models create a structural incentive to maximize user attention and increase time spent on the platform. This incentive structure is particularly consequential for children and youth whose developmental stage is characterized by heightened responsiveness to reward, increased salience of peer feedback, and still-maturing cognitive control systems.8

Puberty initiates a period of high neuroplasticity, which includes synaptic pruning and myelination in neural systems central to emotion, motivation, and reward processing.9,10 During this period, limbic circuits become increasingly responsive to emotionally salient and rewarding experiences, while the pre-frontal cortex — responsible for decision-making, impulse control, and long-term planning — develops more gradually.9,10 This developmental imbalance may increase young people’s susceptibility to digital platforms that are engineered to capture attention through persuasive design features, such as highly curated and reinforcing personalized algorithms, infinite scrolling, intermittent rewards (e.g., “likes,” “streaks”), and other forms of quantified social validation (e.g., follower counts, view metrics).11 When successful, these design features increase time spent on a platform, potentially contributing to problematic patterns of use associated with sleep disruption, symptoms of depression and anxiety, social isolation, inattention, sedentary behaviour, and other adverse health outcomes.5,6

Digital platforms can also facilitate interaction between users outside a young person’s existing social network. Design choices such as direct messaging, live chats, and friend suggestions increase young people’s accessibility and opportunities for interaction with unknown adults, making unsolicited contact routine rather than exceptional, and facilitating online grooming, exploitation, and other forms of predation. When examined through a child health lens, the convergence of engagement-driven business models, developmental vulnerabilities, and ready discoverability of children by unknown users creates a compelling rationale for regulatory oversight of online platforms.

What can Canada learn from other jurisdictions?

Over the last decade, the United Kingdom, the European Union (EU), and Australia have enacted comprehensive online safety legislation and established regulators with the authority and expertise to oversee digital platforms. These jurisdictions offer valuable comparisons for Canada, particularly with regard to their commitment to instilling democratic values into policies and developing fully independent regulators. Although their legislative frameworks differ in important respects, they share a common philosophy: online safety requires reactive content moderation alongside proactive governance that codifies enforceable safe design standards, promotes transparency, and holds companies accountable for the design and operation of their services.12

Within these parameters, jurisdictions have adopted different approaches to regulation. For example, legislative definitions predating the emergence of consumer-facing AI platforms leave ambiguity about whether these definitions extend to AI chatbots; thus, jurisdictions may regulate services that enable user-to-user communication while not explicitly including AI systems that interact directly with users. 13,14 This problem was highlighted when users of X prompted its integrated chatbot, Grok, to generate nonconsensual intimate imagery and child sexual abuse material.15 Under the UK’s Online Safety Act, regulators could investigate illegal content generated and disseminated on the X platform but, controversially, could not investigate illegal content generated through Grok’s standalone website or app.

These jurisdictions have also diverged in their implementation approaches, but regulators in all 3 areas have developed standards for digital platforms, which translate broad legislative obligations into specific, enforceable criteria that protect children and youth online. Specifically, platforms must set minors’ accounts to private by default, disable addictive features, and modify their algorithms so that children are exposed to less harmful content.16–18

Since the implementation of online safety frameworks in the UK and EU, regulation in both jurisdictions has been credited with driving more than 100 safety-focused design changes across 70 online platforms.19 A UK report found that approximately 70% of parents and children indicated seeing more safety features and around half of children reported seeing age verification and more age-appropriate content.20 The European Commission has demonstrated a willingness to investigate major technology companies, opening 16 formal investigations from mid-2024 to mid-2026,21 including inquiries into whether X has adequately assessed and mitigated the risks of child sexual abuse material associated with AI integration into its platform; whether Snap-chat has adequately protected children from grooming, sexual exploitation, and criminal recruitment; and whether Meta has implemented sufficient measures to prevent children younger than 13 years from accessing their platforms.22–24

However, experience across jurisdictions demonstrates that legislation and regulation must evolve and adapt.13 Perhaps the clearest lesson emerging internationally is the need for greater emphasis on safety by design — an approach by which operators take proactive responsibility for the safety of platform users and ensure risks to children are assessed.25 Since 2024, only 31% of changes made to 3 major platforms addressed the default platform design.20 Instead, companies continue to favour content moderation, warning labels, parental controls, or educational tools while leaving the underlying architecture that drives excessive engagement largely unchanged.19 Consequently, Australia proposed reforms to its original legislation to incorporate a statutory duty of care explicitly requiring platforms to identify and address unsafe design features before harms occur.26,27

In 2024, Australia was the first country to legislate a national minimum age for social media access. As of mid-2026, more than 15 nations have enacted, have passed, or are actively working toward laws setting a minimum age for social media use, demonstrating global concern for the impact of social media on the well-being of young people. Countries have taken different approaches to age minimums, ranging from complete bans to parental consent laws to platform-specific restrictions. Preliminary data highlight important implementation challenges. For example, 4 months after Australia’s social media ban took effect a large-scale poll of teenagers aged 12 to 15 years in the country indicated that 61% continue to access age-restricted platforms.28

Canada’s Safe Social Media Act reflects many emerging best practices. It establishes an independent commission, applies to both social media platforms and AI chatbots, establishes age restrictions on platforms, and imposes a duty to protect children.1 The bill also requires companies to incorporate safety by design.

What should Canada include in its whole-of-society approach?

To build on international experiences, Canada should consider several issues as it seeks to advance the Safe Social Media Act.

The effectiveness of Canada’s online safety framework will depend on an independent commission with the authority, specialized expertise, investigative powers, and resources to administer, monitor, and enforce compliance. The proposed Digital Safety and Data Protection Commission is, therefore, a fundamental component of the bill. The commission must be appropriately structured, staffed, and funded to fulfill its mandate.

The commission should prioritize safety by design to limit harmful content, attention-maximizing designs, and features that allow a child to be discovered by users outside the child’s existing social network. In implementing the legislation, the commission should be empowered to mandate structural changes to platform architecture, ensuring that safety by design requirements go beyond protective tools to address platform features that create foreseeable risks to children. This reflects a growing international consensus that, as with other consumer products, digital platforms used by young people should not wait for harm to occur; safety should be built into their design from the start.

Canada should ensure that the commission has the scope, mandate, and tools needed to respond to emerging risks as technologies evolve. A future-oriented commission will need to assess harmful functionalities across digital services, regardless of the digital platform’s stated purpose. For example, gaming platforms that expose children to systems that use algorithmic recommendations, unsolicited direct messaging, or other engagement-maximizing design elements can create similar risks to those of social media platforms. Therefore, regulatory practice should be guided by functionality and associated risk rather than how a platform self-describes its primary purpose.

Requiring companies to demonstrate that their platforms meet enforceable child safety standards before they allow people younger than 16 years to create accounts is a notable policy in the Canadian bill. Unlike permanent minimum-age prohibitions, it functions less as a ban, which may have implications for rights under the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, and more as a licensing requirement pending compliance with child-safety standards. Effective implementation will require consistent enforcement across platforms, effective privacy protections for age-verification technologies, and embedding the legislation within a broader regulatory framework focused on overall platform safety over age restrictions alone. The commission must ensure that age-assurance measures preserve privacy while minimizing the collection, retention, and disclosure of personal information for all people in Canada.

Lastly, international experience demonstrates that legislation must be assessed in practice, informed by evidence, and adapted as technologies and patterns of harm evolve. Rigorous evaluation will be essential to determine whether the Safe Social Media Act improves the social and interactive online services in which children and youth learn, connect, and develop. A comprehensive evaluation plan should be developed to assess platform compliance, child health outcomes, social connection, exposure to online harms, and unintended consequences for children, youth, and families. Canada’s approach to age restrictions and the effectiveness of its implementation should be measured and adapted based on empiric data. Successful implementation of the legislation will require sustained investment in digital literacy and ongoing public engagement with parents, educators, clinicians, and young people themselves.

Canada has an opportunity to align with and potentially move beyond similar jurisdictions in regulating online platforms used by young people. To do so, Canada must build a child-centred framework for online safety that reflects Canadian legal, regulatory, and health-policy contexts, while drawing on the strongest elements of international practice. Canada’s participation will advance an international commitment to making digital environments safe by design. In a digital environment that transcends national borders, children and youth will be better protected when jurisdictions act with ambition, coordination, and a shared commitment to child and youth well-being.
