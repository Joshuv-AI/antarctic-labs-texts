Pain Persisting After Bone Marrow Aspiration and Biopsy: Clinical Features, Pain Phenotypes, and Prospective Outcomes
Authors: Fadime Ersoy Dursun, Bilinc Doğruöz Karatekin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the severity and pain phenotypes of chronic pain following bone marrow aspiration (BMA) and/or bone marrow biopsy (BMB).Patients and MethodsIn this prospective observational study, 228 adult patients who had undergone BMA and/or BMB were screened at least three months after the procedure. Pain characteristics were assessed using the McGill–Melzack Pain Questionnaire, Visual Analog Scale, pain DETECT questionnaire (PD-Q), and Douleur Neuropathique 4 (DN4). Patients reporting pain underwent standardized physical examination focusing on sacroiliac joint provocation tests and pressure pain threshold (PPT) measurement using digital algometry.ResultsChronic pain persisting for ≥3 months was identified in 13 patients (5.7%). Although nociceptive pain features were most common, neuropathic pain components were identified in a substantial subset of patients based on PD-Q and DN4 scores. Physical examination suggested sacroiliac joint–related pain in some patients. PPT values were reduced at the biopsy site in patients with chronic pain, indicating increased local pain sensitivity.ConclusionIn this prospective observational study, chronic pain following BMA and BMB is an underrecognized but clinically relevant complication with heterogeneous pain characteristics. Although neuropathic pain features and findings suggestive of local sensitization were identified in a subset of patients, these observations should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of affected individuals and the exploratory nature of the analysis. These findings nevertheless support the need for systematic post-procedural pain assessment after BMB.

Introduction

Bone marrow aspiration (BMA) and bone marrow biopsy (BMB) are commonly used invasive diagnostic methods for the diagnosis, prognosis, and evaluation of treatment response in hematological, oncological, and some metabolic diseases.1,2 The posterior superior iliac crest is the most frequently used anatomical site for the procedure. It is usually performed under local anesthesia and without sedation.3 However, bone marrow procedures are not entirely risk-free. The most frequently reported acute complications include pain, bleeding, infection, and hematoma; although rare, serious complications (eg, nerve injury, osteomyelitis, long-term hematoma) can also occur.4–6 Post-procedure pain is one of the most common complications, occurring in approximately 30–50% of patients in the early period.7 Recent studies have highlighted chronic pain (defined as pain lasting longer than 3 months) following bone marrow aspiration and biopsy.8

This pain is usually dull in character, increases with palpation, and sometimes exhibits neuropathic features.9,10 The reported prevalence of chronic pain ranges from 5% to 15%.11 In particular, advanced age, female sex, obesity, long procedure time, and a history of multiple biopsies have been found to be associated with the development of chronic pain.12,13 In terms of the type of pain, while somatic pain is dominant in some cases, neuropathic pain components (burning, stinging, electric sensation) may be prominent in others.14 Although post-procedural pain following BMA and BMB is generally conceptualized as nociceptive or neuropathic in origin, emerging pain science suggests that chronic pain states may also involve nociplastic mechanisms characterized by altered central nociceptive processing. The traditional distinction between nociceptive and neuropathic pain may not fully capture the complexity of chronic pain experiences after BMA and BMB. Contemporary pain frameworks increasingly recognize nociplastic pain as a third mechanistic descriptor, particularly in patients with persistent pain without sufficient structural or neurological explanation. These symptoms may be related to irritation of peripheral nerves or periosteal structures during the biopsy procedure.15 Persistent pain may negatively affect quality of life and future procedural compliance.16,17

Procedural factors including biopsy technique, periosteal manipulation, and operator experience may influence long-term pain outcomes. In particular, the posterior superior iliac spine, the most commonly used biopsy site, contains richly innervated periosteal structures, and repetitive mechanical stimulation or tissue trauma in this region may contribute to peripheral sensitization and subsequent chronic pain development. Persistent pain after minimally invasive procedures cannot always be fully explained by ongoing tissue injury or identifiable nerve damage. However, the potential contribution of nociplastic pain mechanisms after BMA and BMB has not been adequately explored.18,19

Although complications following BMA and BMB have been widely reported, studies specifically investigating chronic pain remain limited. Therefore, in this prospective observational study, we aimed to evaluate the duration, severity, and phenotypic characteristics of chronic pain in patients undergoing BMA and BMB at our center using both clinical assessments and questionnaire-based pain evaluation methods.

Materials and Methods

Participants

This prospective study was conducted on patients attending the Hematology-1 outpatient clinic of Istanbul Medeniyet University, Prof. Dr. Süleyman Yalçın City Hospital for routine check-ups due to hematological diseases between 2014 and 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Prof. Dr. Süleyman Yalçın City Hospital Ethics Committee (approval no: 2020/0142) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Before the study began, patients were given detailed information about the study and those who agreed to participate signed an informed consent form. The study was conducted using a questionnaire-based approach in patients who had undergone BMB and/or BMA at least 3 months previously. A total of 300 patients aged 18–85 years were initially enrolled. Of these, 72 patients were excluded for various reasons (death, additional chronic diseases causing pain). The final analysis included 228 patients (Figure 1). All BMA and BMB procedures were performed under local anesthesia by the same hematologist, who had more than 5 years of experience. A Jamshidi needle (11 G, Co. Becton Dickinson BD, Heidelberg, Germany) (JAM) was used in the procedure. All procedures were performed from the right posterior iliac crest. Patients were followed up for at least one year after BMB and/or BMA. Those whose pain persisted after three months were considered to have chronic pain. Study Procedure

In this study, pain assessment relies on screening questionnaires rather than diagnostic tools. These findings were evaluated by correlating them with clinical findings. Exclusion criteria included unwillingness to participate; psychiatric and/or neurologic disorders impairing pain reporting/perception; diabetes mellitus; rheumatologic disease; chronic substance and/or alcohol dependence; use of medications associated with neuropathy; postherpetic neuralgia; and prior surgery or trauma at the biopsy site for other reasons. Analgesic use and any biopsy-related complications were recorded.

Physical Examination

All participants reporting pain were examined by the same Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation specialist. These maneuvers were used to screen for alternative or overlapping musculoskeletal sources of pain, particularly hip joint or sacroiliac joint (SIJ) origin.26

Flexion–Abduction–External Rotation (FABER) Test; Patrick Test

The participant’s hip was sequentially placed into flexion, abduction, and external rotation (Figure 3C). Pain location and intensity were noted. This test helps differentiate hip-related anterior pain from sacroiliac joint-related posterior pain.

Gaenslen Test

The participant was positioned near the edge of the examination table One leg was extended off the table into hip extension while the contralateral hip and knee were flexed toward the abdomen (Figure 3D). The examiner applied a gentle downward force to both legs to provoke sacroiliac joint pain. This test aids in detecting sacroiliac joint dysfunction or pain reproduction. Sacroiliac compression test: The participant was positioned in side-lying, and the examiner applied a steady downward force to the uppermost iliac crest (toward the examination table) to compress the sacroiliac joint. Pain reproduction in the posterior pelvis/buttock region on the tested side was recorded as a positive response.

Interpretation Rule

A clinical examination was considered positive when at least 2 of the 3 sacroiliac joint provocation maneuvers (FABER, Gaenslen, and SIJ compression tests) were positive. Additionally, discordant findings across different directions/sides (eg, positivity elicited in opposite directions or on different sides across maneuvers) were not classified as a positive clinical examination; only concordant positivity meeting the predefined criterion (≥2 of 3 tests positive on the same side) was accepted as examination positive.

Pressure Pain Threshold Assessment (PPT; Algometry)

PPT represents the minimum pressure that induces pain and is used to quantify local mechanical pain sensitivity, reflecting nociceptive threshold in deep tissues. In this study, PPT provided an objective complement to subjective pain questionnaires. PPT was measured using a digital pressure algometry (Storz Medical F-Meter-SWZ). Measurements were taken at the biopsy site with the device calibrated to N/cm2 and pressure increased at 10 N/s until the first pain sensation. Three readings were obtained with 5-second intervals, and the mean value was recorded as the PPT. All measurements were conducted by the same examiner under standardized environmental conditions.27

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were evaluated for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. Because of the limited sample size in the chronic pain subgroup (n=13), non-parametric statistical methods were preferred for inferential analyses. The frequency of chronic pain after BMA and/or BMB was calculated descriptively as the proportion of patients reporting persistent pain for at least 3 months after the procedure. For subgroup analyses, patients with chronic pain were classified according to neuropathic pain features based on DN4 scores (DN4 ≥4 vs DN4 <4). Additional descriptive phenotypic classification was performed using PD-Q thresholds (PD-Q ≥19 indicating likely neuropathic pain). Comparisons between neuropathic-feature and nociceptive-predominant subgroups were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous variables because of the small sample size and non-normal distribution assumptions. Exact p-values were reported when appropriate. Associations between neuropathic pain features and clinical examination findings were evaluated descriptively and inferentially. PPT values were additionally compared between pain phenotype subgroups to assess differences in local mechanical pain sensitivity. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The results of the questionnaire, clinical tests, and physical examination of the 228 patients who continued the study were evaluated. At the end of the evaluation, chronic pain lasting for at least 3 months after BMA and/or BMB was detected in 13 patients out of 228 patients. Accordingly, the prevalence of chronic pain was 5.7%. Evaluation of the Neuropathic Pain Component

As a result of the PD-Q and DN4 questionnaires applied to evaluate the neuropathic pain component, it was determined that neuropathic features were prominent in some patients. PD-Q scores ranged from 4 to 23 (Figure 2B), while DN4 scores ranged from 1 to 8. When patients with a DN4 score ≥4 were evaluated, it was observed that some patients with chronic pain had an accompanying neuropathic pain component. Among the 13 participants who reported chronic pain at follow-up, 9 (69.2%) had a DN4 score ≥4, indicating prominent neuropathic pain features. When pain phenotypes were additionally classified using the Pain DETECT questionnaire, only 3/13 (23.1%) met the threshold for “likely neuropathic pain” (Pain DETECT ≥19) (Table 3). The remaining participants were categorized as having predominantly nociceptive pain or mixed/uncertain pain characteristics.

Physical Examination Findings

All patients reporting chronic pain were evaluated by physical examination. In the evaluation, which was performed using the criterion of at least two positive FABER, Gaenslen, and sacroiliac compression tests on the same side, findings consistent with sacroiliac joint-related pain were detected in some patients. Notably, patients with DN4 ≥4 commonly showed concordant sacroiliac joint involvement on clinical examination, with positivity in at least two sacroiliac provocation tests on the symptomatic side. All patients with DN4 ≥4 (9/9, 100%) met the predefined criterion for a positive, concordant SIJ examination (≥2 of 3 provocation tests positive on the same side). In contrast, none of the patients with DN4 <4 (0/4, 0%) met the concordant SIJ criterion (Fisher’s exact test, two-sided p=0.0014) (Table 4).

Pressure Pain Threshold Measurements

Pressure pain threshold values recorded over the biopsy region ranged from 22 to 30 N/cm2 (Figure 2) with an overall mean of 25.9 N/cm2; mean PPT was comparable between groups classified as having neuropathic features versus nociceptive-predominant pain (approximately 26.4 vs 24.8 N/cm2, respectively). Mean PPT values are presented descriptively in Table 3, whereas median (IQR) values and inferential comparisons showed no significant between-group difference in PPT (Mann–Whitney U, exact two-sided p=0.330) (Table 4).

Comparison of Neuropathic and Nociceptive Pain Subgroups

In the subgroup analysis based on PD-Q and DN4 scores, patients with chronic pain were divided into two groups: those with and without a neuropathic pain component. In the subgroup classified as having a neuropathic pain component (n=9), all patients had DN4 ≥4 (9/9, 100%), and 3/9 (33.3%) also met the Pain DETECT threshold for likely neuropathic pain (PD-Q ≥19). In contrast, none of the patients in the nociceptive-predominant subgroup (n=4) had DN4 ≥4 or PD-Q ≥19 (0/4, 0%). PD-Q values were statistically significantly different between the nociceptive and neuropathic groups (p=0.0028). Overall, subgroup allocation and threshold-based descriptive classification are summarized in Table 3, whereas distributional summaries (median [IQR]) and inferential comparisons, including sacroiliac joint examination positivity and questionnaire outcomes, are presented in Table 4.

Neuropathic-feature pain was typically described as burning or electric-like, whereas nociceptive-predominant pain was dull and pressure-sensitive. While PPT values were variable and overlapped between subgroups, concordant sacroiliac joint provocation test positivity was consistently observed among patients with neuropathic features. This sub-analysis shows that chronic pain developing after bone marrow procedures is not homogeneous and requires patient-specific assessment.

Discussion

In this prospective study, we comprehensively evaluated the severity and qualitative characteristics of chronic pain developing after BMA and/or BMB. Our results demonstrate that although chronic pain following bone marrow procedures is relatively uncommon, it represents a clinically relevant complication with heterogeneous features, encompassing both nociceptive and neuropathic pain components. In this study, bone marrow biopsy was performed in 12 out of 13 patients diagnosed with chronic pain. Our findings suggest that chronic pain may be more strongly associated with biopsy procedures than with aspiration alone. However, due to the small number of patients, we cannot draw a definitive conclusion on this matter.

The observed prevalence of chronic pain in our cohort was 5.7%, which is in line with previously reported rates ranging between 5% and 15%.11–13 The variability across studies is likely multifactorial and may reflect differences in procedural techniques, operator experience, patient-related factors (such as pain sensitivity and comorbidities), follow-up duration, and methodological differences in pain assessment. Importantly, chronic pain is often under recognized in clinical practice, particularly when symptoms are mild to moderate. By defining chronic pain as pain persisting for at least three months and combining subjective questionnaires with objective assessment tools, our study aimed to provide a more robust and clinically meaningful evaluation.

Analysis of pain intensity in patients undergoing BMA and/or BMB has consistently shown that most individuals report mild-to-moderate pain on VAS measurements, whereas severe pain is relatively uncommon.1,27 Nevertheless, even low-to-moderate persistent pain may have clinically meaningful consequences. Chronic or subacute pain—even at modest intensity—has been associated with impairments in daily functioning, reduced mobility, sleep disturbance, and diminished psychological well-being, underscoring that pain-related disability does not correlate solely with numerical intensity scores.28,29 In procedural settings, inadequate recognition and follow-up of post-procedural pain may therefore lead to underestimation of its functional impact, particularly in vulnerable or chronically ill populations.1,28 These findings support the need for systematic post-procedural pain assessment and longitudinal follow-up in undergoing bone marrow procedures, even when reported VAS scores fall within the mild-to-moderate range.

Qualitative pain assessment further refines understanding of the pain experience after invasive procedures. Use of the McGill–Melzack Pain Questionnaire has demonstrated that procedure-related pain is frequently described using sensory descriptors consistent with somatic nociceptive mechanisms; however, substantial inter-individual variability in descriptors and affective components has also been observed.30,31 Such heterogeneity likely reflects multiple contributing factors, including differences in the extent of periosteal and soft tissue trauma during needle insertion, variability in operator technique, and patient-specific differences in pain modulation and central processing.32 Moreover, peripheral tissue injury may trigger central sensitization, amplifying pain perception and altering qualitative descriptors over time.29,32 Together, these findings support the concept that post–bone marrow procedure pain is predominantly somatic but mechanistically heterogeneous, and that multidimensional pain assessment tools are essential for capturing its full clinical impact.

Notably, a subset of patients demonstrated elevated PD-Q and DN4 scores, suggesting the presence of neuropathic pain features. This finding indicates that chronic pain after BMA and BMB may not be explained solely by local tissue trauma or inflammation. However, the small number of patients included in the study suggests that these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Therefore, studies with larger patient numbers may allow us to better illustrate the phenotype of pain. Previous studies have suggested that periosteal irritation, soft tissue injury, or involvement of small peripheral nerve branches during needle insertion may contribute to neuropathic pain development.14,15 Although direct evidence of nerve injury was not demonstrated in our study, the coexistence of neuropathic features and variable PPT findings suggests that persistent post-procedural pain may involve overlapping nociceptive, neuropathic, and sensitization-related mechanisms. These findings also support the possibility that some patients may exhibit nociplastic pain-related features associated with altered pain processing. However, given the exploratory nature of the analysis and the limited sample size, these interpretations should be considered hypothesis-generating. Future studies incorporating quantitative sensory testing and broader multidimensional pain assessments are needed to better characterize the mechanisms underlying chronic pain after bone marrow procedures.

Physical examination findings further emphasize the multifactorial nature of post-procedural chronic pain. The posterior iliac crest—commonly used for BMA and BMB—is anatomically adjacent to the sacroiliac joint complex, including ligamentous and periosteal structures richly innervated by nociceptive fibers.33,34 In our cohort, patients with DN4 scores suggestive of neuropathic pain more frequently demonstrated positive sacroiliac joint provocation tests. While this observation may indicate a relationship between neuropathic pain-related symptoms and secondary musculoskeletal dysfunction around the biopsy site, the finding should be interpreted with caution. It is therefore plausible that local periosteal trauma, needle passage through cortical bone, and subsequent protective muscle guarding may contribute to secondary musculoskeletal pain patterns after biopsy.35 Given the very small number of patients included in the analysis, the observed association may have been influenced by sample-size effects and should not be considered evidence of a causal or robust clinical relationship. Moreover, the DN4 is a screening instrument designed to identify neuropathic pain-related features rather than establish a definitive diagnosis of neuropathic pain.32,36 Therefore, the coexistence of positive DN4 scores and sacroiliac joint findings may reflect overlapping pain mechanisms, shared regional sensitization processes, or statistical variation within a limited cohort rather than a specific pathophysiological link. Larger studies are required to determine whether this association is reproducible and clinically meaningful.

Objective assessment with pressure algometry has been widely used to quantify mechanical pain sensitivity, and reduced PPT are considered markers of peripheral and/or central sensitization.37,38 Although PPT values tended to be lower in some patients with chronic pain, no statistically significant differences were observed between pain phenotype subgroups of subjective pain reports and suggest enhanced local nociceptive processing. Experimental and clinical studies have demonstrated that sustained nociceptive input following tissue injury can induce peripheral sensitization—characterized by lowered activation thresholds of nociceptors—as well as central sensitization, in which spinal and supraspinal neurons exhibit amplified responsiveness.32,39 Importantly, such neuroplastic changes may persist beyond the period of overt tissue healing, contributing to ongoing pain despite resolution of the initial injury.

Taken together, these data support a model in which chronic pain after BMA and BMB results from a complex interaction among local tissue trauma (including periosteal and ligamentous irritation), possible neural involvement, and altered peripheral and central pain processing mechanisms. Recognition of this multifaceted pathophysiology is critical for improving pre-procedural counseling, refining procedural techniques to minimize tissue trauma, and identifying patients at risk for persistent pain. Larger prospective studies with extended follow-up are warranted to better delineate risk factors, characterize mechanistic subtypes, and evaluate preventive strategies aimed at reducing long-term pain after bone marrow procedures.36

Early identification of symptomatic patients may facilitate timely intervention and potentially reduce pain-related disability and deterioration in quality of life. Current evidence supports an individualized and multimodal management approach, incorporating patient education, pharmacological treatment when appropriate, physical rehabilitation strategies, and referral to specialized pain services for selected patients. Patients exhibiting neuropathic pain-related features on screening questionnaires may warrant further evaluation and consideration of evidence-based neuropathic pain therapies, whereas those with predominant musculoskeletal findings may benefit from targeted physical therapy and functional rehabilitation programs. Importantly, treatment goals should extend beyond pain reduction alone and include restoration of physical function, sleep quality, and overall well-being. Recent reviews on persistent post-surgical pain emphasize the importance of early recognition, multidimensional assessment, and multidisciplinary management to improve long-term outcomes and prevent pain chronification. These considerations highlight the need for greater awareness among hematologists and clinicians performing bone marrow procedures regarding persistent post-procedural pain as a potentially treatable long-term complication.40,41

The strengths of this study include its prospective design, multidimensional pain assessment using validated instruments, incorporation of objective algometric measurements, and standardized examination procedures performed by a single experienced examiner.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study was conducted at a single center, and the relatively small number of patients with chronic pain reduced the statistical power of subgroup analyses. Neuropathic pain classifications were based on screening questionnaires rather than confirmatory diagnostic methods, and quantitative sensory testing beyond PPT measurements was not performed. In addition, the absence of a control group without pain precluded direct comparison of pressure pain threshold values and other pain-related characteristics between symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals. Therefore, mechanistic interpretations should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

Conclusion

In conclusion, chronic pain after BMA and/or BMB appears to be an uncommon but clinically relevant complication. Although most patients reported mild-to-moderate pain intensity, pain characteristics were heterogeneous, and some patients demonstrated neuropathic pain-related features based on screening questionnaires. The combined use of validated pain questionnaires, physical examination, and objective algometric measurements provided a broader characterization of persistent post-procedural pain, although the underlying mechanisms could not be definitively established. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of recognizing persistent pain as a potential long-term outcome after bone marrow procedures and support the need for individualized patient assessment when symptoms persist. However, the small number of patients included in the study suggests that we should approach our results with caution. Larger prospective studies incorporating comprehensive pain phenotyping and quantitative sensory testing are needed to better clarify the mechanisms and clinical implications of chronic pain after bone marrow procedures.
