Introduction

1.1
the main results

The main subject of this note is to give a iterated logarithm bound for the constant in Bourgain’s slicing problem. The Bourgain’s slicing problem is one of the central questions in high-dimensional convex geometry. It is about a universal regularity of the convex sets in

\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A standard form of this problem is whether for any convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with volume one, there exists a hyperplane
H\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\mathrm{Vol}_{n-1}(K\cap H)\geq c,\ \ \ \ n\geq 2,

where
c>0
is a universal constant, and
\mathrm{Vol}_{n-1}

refers to the standard
(n-1)
-dimensional volume measure on
H
.
This motivates us to estimate the constant
L_{n}
defined by

(L_{n})^{-1}=\inf_{K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sup_{H\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathrm{Vol}_{n-1}(K\cap H),\ \ \ \ n\geq 2,

where
H
runs over all hyperplanes and
K
runs over all convex sets with volume one. The main result of this note is the following bound of
L_{n}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that
L_{n}\leq C\log(\log n)

for
n\geq 3
.

Applying a well known connection established in
Eldan and Klartag
[
14
]
, we prove the bound above via proving the same kind of bound for the thin cell constant
\sigma_{n}
.
The thin cell parameter
\sigma_{\mu}
of a log-concave probability measure
\mu
is defined by

\displaystyle n\sigma_{\mu}^{2}=\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(|x|^{2}),

(1.1)

where
\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(u)=\int u^{2}d\mu-(\int ud\mu)^{2}
is the variance of a function
u
.
Set

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}=\sup\{\sigma_{\mu}:\ \mu\ \mathrm{is\ an\ isotropic\ \textrm{log-concave}\ probability\ measure\ on}\ \mathbb{R}^{n}\}.

Here
\mu
is called isotropic
if its barycenter is zero and its covariance matrix is the identity.
The connection between
L_{n}
and
\sigma_{n}
in

[
14
]
is

\displaystyle L_{n}\leq C\sigma_{n},\ \ \ \ n\geq 1,

(1.2)

where
C>0
is a universal constant.

Whether
\sigma_{n}
can be bounded by a universal constant is called the thin cell conjecture. The thin cell conjecture
comes from the works of Anttila, Ball and Perissinaki

[
1
]
and Bobkov and Koldobsky
[
5
]
, which is later than
the slicing problem originated in Bourgain

[
6
,
7
]
. There is still a more stronger conjecture called the KLS
isoperimetric conjecture given in Kannan,
Lov
\acute{\mathrm{a}}
sz, and Simonovits
[
16
]
.
More and more connections have been discovered gradually among these three conjectures although they may have different motivations.

The isoperimetric constant of
\mu
with density
\rho
is define via

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\psi_{\mu}}=\inf_{A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big\{\frac{\int_{\partial A}\rho}{min\{\mu(A),1-\mu(A)\}}\Big\},

where
A
runs over all open sets with smooth boundary satisfying
0<\mu(A)<1
.
Set
\psi_{n}=\sup_{\mu}\psi_{\mu}
, where
\mu
runs over all isotropic, log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The KLS
isoperimetric conjecture states that
\psi_{n}\leq C
for a universal constant
C>0
.

The best bound of
L_{n}

before this note is
C\sqrt{\log n}
,
n\geq 2
, which is a consequence of (
1.2
) and the recent breakthrough
\psi_{n}\leq C\sqrt{\log n}
in Klartag
[
20
]
.
The bound of
L_{n}
given in Theorem
1.1
above is a direct consequence of this isoperimetric constant estimate, (
1.2
) and the following relation.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that
\sigma_{n}\leq C\log\psi_{n}
.

Combing (
1.2
) and Theorem 1.2, we get
L_{n}\leq C\log\psi_{n}
, which improves the previous results of
L_{n}\leq e^{C\psi_{n}^{2}}
in Ball and Nguyen
[
3
]
and
L_{n}\leq C\psi_{n}

by Eldan and Klartag
[
14
]
. This result has an unexpected character that
a weak control of the isoperimetric constant
\psi_{n}
by the slicing constant
L_{n}
or the thin cell constant
\sigma_{n}
would lead to the truth of these three conjectures.

\mathbf{A})
Let
\mu
be a log-concave probability measure with density
\rho
.
In the last ten years, a driven force in the study of the high-dimensional convex sets is the Eldan’s
stochastic localization started in Eldan
[
13
]
, which is a kind of partition of
\mu
through a random procedure.
Unlike the
determined localization, in which the dimension of a
fragment finally goes to one, this stochastic version
is a kind of partition
from dimension
n
to dimension
n
, in which a fragment may keep more information of the original one than the previous method. In fact, a fragment in
this stochastic partition is the original measure
\mu
weighted by
a Gaussian function, which makes it even more regular than
\mu
in some aspect.

More concretely, the Eldan’s stochastic localization of a probability measure
\mu
is a probability distribution valued stochastic process

(p_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.
Here
p_{t}
refers to the density of the corresponding distribution and
p_{0}=\rho
.
In
a simplified version by Lee and Vemplala
[
26
]
, it is the solution of the following stochastic differential equation with the initial condition
p_{0}
:

\displaystyle dp_{t}(x)=p_{t}(x)\langle x-a_{t},dB_{t}\rangle,\ \ \ \ \ \ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(1.3)

where
a_{t}
is the barycenter of
p_{t}
and
(B_{t})=(B_{k,t})_{1\leq k\leq n}
is a standard Brownian motion in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Denote, respectively, the components of the barycenter
a_{t}
and the covariance matrix
A_{t}
of
p_{t}
by

\displaystyle a_{i;t}=

\displaystyle\int x_{i}p_{t}(x)dx,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ 1\leq i\leq n,

\displaystyle A_{ij;t}=

\displaystyle\int(x_{i}-a_{i;t})(x_{j}-a_{j;t})p_{t}(x)dx,\ \ \ \ 1\leq i,j\leq n.

In
[
26
]
, it follows from (
1.3
) that

\displaystyle da_{t}=\int x\langle x-a_{t},dB_{t}\rangle p_{t}(x)dx=A_{t}dB_{t}.

In Klartag and Lehec
[
22
]
, it shows further by It
\mathrm{\hat{o}}
formula that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}|a_{t}|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Tr}(A_{t}^{2}),\ \ \ \ t>0.

(1.4)

As the original version in Eldan
[
13
]
, there is another way to construct
(p_{t})
, which involves the Gaussian function mentioned above. For
t\geq 0
and

\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define the
probability density function

\displaystyle p_{t,\theta}(x)=Z(t,\theta)^{-1}e^{\langle\theta,x\rangle-\frac{t|x|^{2}}{2}}p_{0}(x),\ \ \ \ \ \ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(1.5)

where
Z(t,\theta)=\int e^{\langle\theta,x\rangle-\frac{t|x|^{2}}{2}}p_{0}(x)dx
.
Set
a(t,\theta)=\int xp_{t,\theta}(x)dx
. In Lee and Vemplala
[
26
]
, it shows that

(p_{t,\theta_{t}})
is a solution of (
1.3
) when
(\theta_{t})
is the solution of the stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle d{\theta}_{t}=a(t,\theta_{t})dt+dB_{t},\ \ \ \theta_{0}=0.

In Klartag and Putterman
[
21
]
, it shows further that the stochastic process

(\theta_{t})
is the same as the process

(tX+B_{t})
in the sense of distribution. Here
X
is a random vector with distribution
\mu
, independent of
(B_{t})
.
Moreover,
p_{t,\theta}
can be taken as the density of
X
under the condition that
tX+B_{t}=\theta
.

\mathbf{B})
Notice that
(p_{t})
does not have a semigroup structure on a finite dimensional space. In Klartag and Putterman
[
21
]
, the semigroup picture of
(p_{t,\theta})
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is revealed through a transformation.
Set
Q_{0}=Id
. When
u\in L^{1}(\mu)
, the following relation
is given in
[
21
]
:

\displaystyle Q_{s}u(y)=\int u(x)p_{t,\theta}(x)dx,\ \ \ \ \ with\ s=1/t,\ y=\theta/t,\ \ s,t>0,

(1.6)

where
(Q_{s})
represents part of a nonhomogeneous heat semigroup which can be constructed independently from
(p_{t,\theta})
.

Write
\mu_{0}=\mu
,
\rho_{0}(x)=\rho(x)
and denote

\displaystyle\mu_{s}=\mu\ast\gamma_{s},\ \ \ \ \ d\mu_{s}=\rho_{s}(x)dx,\ \ \ \ s>0,

where
\gamma_{s}
is the Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with mean zero and covariance
s\cdot Id
.
Let
(P_{s})
be the standard heat semigroup on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.,
P_{0}=\mathrm{Id}
and

\displaystyle P_{s}u=u\ast\gamma_{s},\ \ \ \ s>0.

In
[
21
]
, it shows that
P_{s}
is a contraction operator from
L^{2}(\mu_{s})
to
L^{2}(\mu)
, and
Q_{s}
can be defined as the adjoint operator of
P_{s}
from
L^{2}(\mu)
to
L^{2}(\mu_{s})
. It has the following form

\displaystyle Q_{s}u(y)=\frac{P_{s}(u\rho)}{\rho_{s}},\ \ \ s\geq 0.

(1.7)

Since the distribution of
\theta_{t}/t
is the same as that of
X+\frac{B_{t}}{t}
, for
u\in L^{1}(\mu)
we get by (
1.6
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big|\int udp_{t}\Big|^{2}=\int\big|Q_{s}u\big|^{2}d\mu_{s},\ \ \ \ \ \ where\ s=1/t,\ s,t>0.

(1.8)

See Lemma 2.2 in Klartag and Lehec
[
22
]
for more details.

The differential of
(Q_{s})
with respect to
s
is the box operator

\displaystyle\Box_{s}=\frac{\Delta}{2}+\nabla\log\rho_{s}\cdot\nabla.

In Klartag and Putterman
[
21
]
, with operators
(\Box_{s})
, the
\Gamma
-calculus of Markov semigroup (see e.g.
[
2
]
) is extended to this nonhomogeneous setting.
For
s\geq 0
and functions
u,v
, which are smooth for example, set
\Gamma_{0,s}(u,v)=uv
and define inductively

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k,s}(u,v)=\Box_{s}\Gamma_{k-1,s}(u,v)-\Gamma_{k-1,s}(\Box_{s}u,v)-\Gamma_{k-1,s}(u,\Box_{s}v)-\frac{d\Gamma_{k-1,s}}{ds}(u,v),\ \ \ k\geq 1.

It shows in Proposition 2.3
[
21
]
that for
k=0,1
,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\int\Gamma_{k,s}(Q_{s}u,Q_{s}v)d\mu_{s}=-\int\Gamma_{k+1,s}(Q_{s}u,Q_{s}v)d\mu_{s},\ \ s>0,

(1.9)

where
u
and
v
both have subexponential decay relative to
\rho
of which the definition is stated in (
4.5
) below.

\mathbf{C})
In this part we turn to the spectral analysis for the Laplacian of
\mu
which is defined by

\displaystyle L=\Delta+\langle\nabla\log\rho,\nabla\rangle.

(1.10)

A crucial property of the formula (
1.5
) is that
p_{t}
is a
t
-uniformly log-concave probability measure.
A probability measure
\mu
with density
\rho
is called
t
-uniformly log-concave if
\rho(x)=e^{-\varphi(x)-t|x|^{2}}
for some
\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
valued convex function
\varphi
.
The log-concave probability measure is just the
0
-uniformly log-concave probability measure. It does not include the degenerate case here.

Denote by
C_{p}(\mu)
the Poincar
\acute{\mathrm{e}}
constant of
\mu
which is the smallest constant
C
such that for any locally Lipschitz function
u\in L^{2}(\mu)
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(u)\leq C\int|\nabla u|^{2}d\mu.

When
\mu
is
t
-uniformly log-concave for some
t>0
, we have
the log-concave Lichnerowicz inequality (see e.g.
[
2
]
)

\displaystyle C_{p}(\mu)\leq\frac{1}{t}.

(1.11)

This estimate is also a consequence of the Brascamp-Lieb inequality in
[
9
]
.
Applying Bochner formula to an eigenfunction corresponding to the eigenvalue
C_{p}(\mu)^{-1}
, (
1.11
) is improved by the following
log-concave spectral-variance inequality
in Theorem 1.3. Klartag
[
20
]
,

\displaystyle C_{p}(\mu)\leq\sqrt{\frac{\|Cov(\mu)\|_{op}}{t}},\ \ \ \ \ t>0.

(1.12)

For an arbitrary isotropic log-concave probability measure, it may not be
t
-uniformly log-concave for any
t>0
.
However, when
\rho
is positive and smooth, in (
4.3
) below we show that it is
1
-uniformly log-concave in an average sense.

Another notion related to the spectral of
L
is the
H^{-1}(\mu)
norm. For
u\in L^{2}(\mu)
with
\int ud\mu=0
, define

\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{-1}(\mu)}=\sup\{\int ufd\mu:\ f\in L^{2}(\mu)\ is\ locally\ Lipshcitz\ with\ \int|\nabla f|^{2}d\mu\leq 1\}.

According to Proposition 10 in Barthe and Klartag
[
4
]
, for any smooth function
u
such that
u,|\nabla u|\in L^{2}(\mu)
and
\int\nabla ud\mu=0
,

\displaystyle Var_{\mu}(u)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\partial_{i}u\|_{H^{-1}(\mu)}^{2}.

(1.13)

The formula above is fundamental in Klartag
[
18
]
and Klartag and Lehec
[
22
]
to estimate the thin cell parameter.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let

\displaystyle f(r)=

\displaystyle e^{D_{0}(r-r_{0})},\ \ \ \ \ r\leq r_{0}-D_{0}^{-1};

(2.2)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ f(r)=

\displaystyle br^{2},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ r\geq r_{0},

(2.3)

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{20}\leq b\leq\frac{1}{5};\ \ \ \ \ \ \ |f^{\prime\prime}(r)|\leq D_{0}^{2}f(r),\ \ \ \ r\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.4)

Proof
Let

and

Set

\displaystyle h(r)=

\displaystyle D_{0}^{2}e^{D_{0}(r-r_{0})},\ \ \ \ \ r<r_{0}-D_{0}^{-1}.

\displaystyle\int(L_{s}u)^{2}d\mu_{s}=\int\Big(\|\nabla^{2}u\|_{HS}^{2}-\langle\nabla^{2}\log\rho_{s}\cdot\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle\Big)d\mu_{s},\ \ \ u\in\mathcal{F}_{\mu_{s}},\ s>0.

(4.9)

For
\lambda>0
, denote by
E_{\lambda}
the spectral projection below level
\lambda
for
-L
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mu
be a regular log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Let
g_{k}(x)=x_{k}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
1\leq k\leq n
.
Assume also that for some
c_{1}\in(0,e^{-1})
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int|Q_{s}g_{k}|^{2}d\mu_{s}\leq c_{1}^{-1}(s^{-1}\wedge 1)n,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ s>0.

(4.10)

Then for
\lambda\in(0,e^{-1})
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int|g_{k,\lambda}|^{2}d\mu\leq 10^{2}c_{1}^{-2}\lambda|\log\lambda|n,\ \ \ \ \ \ \

(4.11)

where

\displaystyle g_{\lambda,k}=E_{\lambda}g_{k},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ 1\leq k\leq n.

Proof
Let
\lambda\in(0,e^{-1})
and assume without loss of generality that the left hand side of (
4.11
) is positive. Define
c_{0}=c_{0}(\lambda)
by

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int|g_{k,\lambda}|^{2}d\mu=c_{0}^{-1}\lambda n.

(4.12)

Assume further that
c_{0}<e^{-4}
. Otherwise, the Lemma holds automatically from
\lambda,c_{1}\in(0,e^{-1})
.
Since
\mu
is regular, we know from Lemma 2.2 in
[
20
]
that
g_{k,\lambda},g_{k}-g_{k,\lambda}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mu}
for
1\leq k\leq n
.
By Lemma 2.2 in
[
21
]
, we also have
Q_{s}g_{k,\lambda}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mu_{s}}
for
1\leq k\leq n
and
s>0
.

Set
s_{1}=c_{0}^{1/2}\lambda^{-1}
. Since
E_{\lambda}
is a spectral projection, we have by (
1.9
) with
k=0

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int\big(Q_{s_{1}}(g_{k}-g_{k,\lambda})\big)Q_{s_{1}}g_{k,\lambda}d\mu_{s_{1}}\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s_{1}}ds\int\langle\nabla Q_{s}(g_{k}-g_{k,\lambda}),\nabla Q_{s}g_{k,\lambda}\rangle d\mu_{s}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s_{1}}ds\int\langle\nabla Q_{s}g_{k},\nabla Q_{s}g_{k,\lambda}\rangle d\mu_{s}\Big|+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s_{1}}ds\int|\nabla Q_{s}g_{k,\lambda}|^{2}d\mu_{s}.

(4.13)

In what below we estimate the first and the second terms in (
4.13
), respectively.

For
s>0
,
it follows from
(
4.8
), (
4.9
) and the right hand side of (
4.2
) that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int|L_{s}(Q_{s}g_{k,\lambda})|^{2}d\mu_{s}\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int\Gamma_{2,s}(Q_{s}g_{k,\lambda},Q_{s}g_{k,\lambda})d\mu_{s}.

(4.14)

Then, integration by parts, Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and (
4.10
) give

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int\langle\nabla Q_{s}g_{k},\nabla Q_{s}g_{k,\lambda}\rangle d\mu_{s}\Big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int(Q_{s}g_{k})L_{s}(Q_{s}g_{k,\lambda})d\mu_{s}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int|Q_{s}g_{k}|^{2}d\mu_{s}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int|L_{s}(Q_{s}g_{k,\lambda})|^{2}d\mu_{s}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{1}^{-1/2}\big(s^{-1}\wedge 1)^{1/2}n^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int\Gamma_{2,s}(Q_{s}g_{k,\lambda},Q_{s}g_{k,\lambda})d\mu_{s}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{1}^{-1/2}\big(s^{-1/2}\wedge 1)n^{1/2}\Big(-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{d}{ds}\int\Gamma_{1,s}(Q_{s}g_{k,\lambda},Q_{s}g_{k,\lambda})d\mu_{s}\Big)^{1/2},

(4.15)

where (
1.9
) is used with
k=1
in the last step above.
Applying (
4.12
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int\Gamma_{1,0}(g_{k,\lambda},g_{k,\lambda})d\mu=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int|\nabla g_{k,\lambda}|^{2}d\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int(E_{\lambda}g_{k})(LE_{\lambda}g_{k})d\mu

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda\int|g_{k,\lambda}|^{2}d\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{0}^{-1}\lambda^{2}n.

(4.16)

Notice that
\log s_{1}+2\leq|\log\lambda|
by
c_{0}<e^{-4}
.
Then, it follows from (
4.15
), (
4.16
) and Lemma
4.2

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{s_{1}}ds\int\langle\nabla Q_{s}g_{k},\nabla Q_{s}g_{k,\lambda}\rangle d\mu_{s}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{1}^{-1/2}n^{1/2}\int_{0}^{s_{1}}\big(s^{-1/2}\wedge 1)\Big(-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{d}{ds}\int\Gamma_{1,s}(Q_{s}g_{k,\lambda},Q_{s}g_{k,\lambda})d\mu_{s}\Big)^{1/2}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{0}^{-1/2}c_{1}^{-1/2}\lambda|\ln\lambda|^{1/2}n.

(4.17)

The gradient inequality in the proof of Lemma 2.5 of
[
21
]
shows that for
u\in H^{1}(\mu)
,

\displaystyle\int|\nabla Q_{s}u|^{2}d\mu_{s}\leq\int|\nabla u|^{2}d\mu,\ \ \ \ \ s>0.

(4.18)

When
u\in\mathcal{F}_{\mu}
, this inequality is also a consequence of (
1.9
) with
k=1
, (
4.8
) and the right hand side of
(
4.2
).
We have by (
4.16
) and (
4.18
)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s_{1}}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int|\nabla Q_{s}g_{k,\lambda}|^{2}d\mu_{s}\Big)ds\leq

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s_{1}}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int|\nabla g_{k,\lambda}|^{2}d\mu\Big)ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{0}^{-1}\lambda^{2}n\int_{0}^{s_{1}}ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{0}^{-1/2}\lambda n.

(4.19)

Applying (
4.13
), (
4
), (
4
) and
\lambda\in(0,e^{-1})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int\big(Q_{s_{1}}(g_{k}-g_{k,\lambda})\big)Q_{s_{1}}g_{k,\lambda}d\mu_{s_{1}}\Big|\leq

\displaystyle 2c_{0}^{-1/2}c_{1}^{-1/2}\lambda|\ln\lambda|^{1/2}n.

This estimate and
(
4.10
) show that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int|Q_{s_{1}}g_{k,\lambda}|^{2}d\mu_{s_{1}}\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int|Q_{s_{1}}g_{k}|^{2}d\mu_{s_{1}}+2\Big|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int\big(Q_{s_{1}}(g_{k}-g_{k,\lambda})\big)Q_{s_{1}}g_{k,\lambda}d\mu_{s_{1}}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{0}^{-1/2}c_{1}^{-1}\lambda n+2c_{0}^{-1/2}c_{1}^{-1/2}\lambda|\ln\lambda|^{1/2}n.

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 3c_{0}^{-1/2}c_{1}^{-1/2}\lambda|\ln\lambda|^{1/2}n.

Since the Rayleigh quotient of
g_{k,\lambda}
at time zero is no more than
\lambda
for
1\leq k\leq n
, we have by (
4.7
), (
4.12
) and
c_{0}<e^{-4}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int|Q_{s_{1}}g_{k,\lambda}|^{2}d\mu_{s_{1}}\geq e^{-c_{0}^{1/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int|g_{k,\lambda}|^{2}d\mu\geq(2c_{0})^{-1}\lambda n.

Combing the two estimates above, we get

\displaystyle c_{0}^{-1}\leq 6c_{0}^{-1/2}c_{1}^{-1}|\ln\lambda|^{1/2},

which implies the conclusion together with (
4.12
).
∎

\mathbf{Proof\ of\ Theorem\ \ref{theorem2}}

Let
\mu
be an isotropic, log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Since
\psi_{n}\geq\psi_{1}\geq\sqrt{\pi/2}
, to prove
Theorem
1.2
, we only need to show that for some universal constant
C>0
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu}\leq C|\log\psi_{\mu}|+C,\ \ \ \ n\geq C.

(4.20)

Denote by
\lambda_{\mu}
the
first nonzero eigenvalue of the self adjoint operator
-L
on
L^{2}(\mu)
. By the Buser-Ledoux inequality from
[
11
]
,
[
25
]
,

\psi_{\mu}^{2}\leq 9\lambda_{\mu}^{-1}
. Therefore,
to prove (
4.20
), it is enough to show that for some universal constant
C>0
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu}\leq C|\log\lambda_{\mu}|+C,\ \ \ \ n\geq C.

(4.21)

Applying the approximation procedure in
[
20
]
, we can assume also that
\mu
is regular.

Noticing that
a_{0}=0
, we have by (
1.4
) and (
2.1
)

\displaystyle\frac{tn}{2}\leq\mathbb{E}|a_{t}|^{2}\leq 8tn,\ \ \ \ \ t\in[0,e^{-10^{3}}],\ \ n\geq C_{3},

(4.22)

where
C_{3}>0
is a universal constant.
It follows from the fact that
p_{t}(x)
is a martingale and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|a_{t}|^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\int|x|^{2}p_{t}(x)dx=\int|x|^{2}p_{0}(x)dx=n,\ \ \ \ t\geq 0,\ n\geq 1.

Since
\mathbb{E}|a_{t}|^{2}
is a increasing function of
t
by (
1.4
), the estimate above, the upper bound in (
4.22
)
and (
1.8
) show that (
4.10
) holds for
n\geq C_{3}
and some universal constant
c_{1}\in(0,e^{-1})
. Then
Lemma
4.3
yields

\displaystyle F(\lambda)\leq 10^{2}c_{1}^{-2}\lambda|\log\lambda|,\ \ \ \ \ \lambda\in(0,e^{-1}),\ n\geq C_{3},

(4.23)

where

\displaystyle F(\lambda)=n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int|E_{\lambda}x_{k}|^{2}d\mu.

The following formula is from (48) in
[
22
]
which is a direct consequence of the
H^{-1}
-inequality (
1.13
):

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle 4\int_{\lambda_{\mu}}^{\infty}\lambda^{-2}F(\lambda)d\lambda.

(4.24)

Notice that
F(\lambda)\leq 1
for
\lambda>0
. Then, for
n\geq C_{3}
, we have by (
4.23
) and (
4.24
)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle 4\int_{e^{-1}}^{\infty}\lambda^{-2}d\lambda+4\cdot 10^{2}c_{1}^{-2}\int_{\lambda_{\mu}\wedge e^{-1}}^{e^{-1}}\lambda^{-1}|\log\lambda|d\lambda\leq 2\cdot 10^{2}c_{1}^{-2}|\log(\lambda_{\mu}\wedge e^{-1})|^{2},

which implies (
4.21
).
∎
