Faculty Recruitment and Retention in Academic Plastic Surgery
Authors: Abdulaziz Elemosho, Amy M. Moore, Jeffrey E. Janis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Academic plastic surgery continues to face challenges in recruiting and retaining faculty. Although prior studies have reported rising faculty attrition, updated national data in academic plastic surgery reflecting recent workforce and institutional changes are lacking.Methods:A 29-item electronic survey was distributed in March to April of 2025 to chairs and chiefs of Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education-accredited plastic surgery programs. The survey assessed trends during the past decade in faculty hiring, resignation, compensation, mentorship, benefits, and retention strategies.Results:Thirty-five programs (43.8% response rate) were included. Faculty recruitment was concentrated at the assistant professor level, with an average of 1.7, 4.3, and 6.5 faculty added during the past 1, 5, and 10 years, respectively. Concurrently, faculty losses averaged 0.4, 2.2, and 3.0, with turnover rates of 4.2%, 29.1%, and 38.9% during the past 1, 5, and 10 years, respectively. Retention rates declined over time, from 95.8% at 1 year to 61.1% at 10 years, with only 8.6% of programs having a formal retention committee. Family considerations (54.3%) and inadequate compensation (34.3%) were the most cited reasons for faculty departure. Reported retention strategies included competitive compensation packages (80.0%), professional development initiatives (74.3%), mentorship programs (65.7%), and work-life balance accommodations (45.7%), awards and recognition (37.1%), and flexible scheduling (28.6%).Conclusions:Despite continued faculty growth, long-term retention remains a significant challenge in academic plastic surgery. Compensation alone does not seem sufficient to ensure faculty longevity. Broader institutional strategies, including formal retention committee creation, are needed to sustain faculty engagement and ensure workforce stability in the decade ahead.

Takeaways

Question: How have faculty recruitment, retention, and turnover evolved in academic plastic surgery, and what strategies are programs using to address ongoing workforce challenges?

Findings: A national survey of 35 programs showed high 10-year turnover (39%) and declining retention (61% at 10 y). Leading reasons for departure were family needs and inadequate compensation. Although competitive pay, mentorship, and professional development were cited as effective retention strategies, few programs implement them systematically, highlighting the need for comprehensive institutional approaches.

Meaning: Despite increasing hiring, academic plastic surgery faces persistent long-term retention issues. Most programs lack formal retention committees, and compensation alone does not guarantee faculty longevity.

INTRODUCTION

Recruiting and retaining faculty remains a challenge in academic plastic surgery. Mandel et al1 recently reported that nearly 90% of graduating plastic surgery residents pursue careers in private practice, leaving only a small fraction entering academic roles, where they are expected to train future generations and contribute to the scientific advancement of the field. Maintaining a pool of educators and academic leaders is essential to uphold the rigor of residency training, sustain innovation, and support the missions of academic medical centers.2,3 Yet, recent reports suggest a declining interest in academic careers among new graduates, with fewer than 9% of all physicians ultimately remaining in academic practice.4,5 Even among those who enter academic practice, faculty retention remains a major issue. Nationwide attrition rates across surgical specialties remain high.6–9 The cited drivers of attrition are multifactorial, ranging from compensation disparities to lack of institutional support.2

In a 2014 study, Chen et al2 performed a national evaluation of faculty recruitment and retention in academic plastic surgery, identifying rising attrition rates and relatively stagnant faculty growth during the decade preceding their analysis. However, in the 11+ years since their study, the field has undergone significant changes, including increased interest in aesthetic practice, rising student debt burdens, and shifting workforce expectations that may influence the dynamics of faculty recruitment and retention today.

Although Chen et al2 provided foundational data on faculty growth and attrition from 2004 to 2014, no updated national study has assessed how these trends have evolved since then. Given the pace of institutional, economic, and workforce changes in academic medicine, contemporary benchmarking of faculty recruitment, retention, and development strategies is needed.

In this study, we aimed to examine current faculty recruitment, retention, strategies, and incentives during the past decade among academic plastic surgery programs.

METHODS

Survey Instrument Design

A 29-item electronic survey was developed to assess faculty recruitment, retention, compensation structures, mentorship, benefits, and departmental organization within academic plastic surgery programs in the United States. (See survey, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the survey questions, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E809.)

Prior to distribution, the survey underwent face validation with senior authors, who are academic leaders. Feedback was used to refine question clarity, internal consistency, and response burden. The final survey design ensured consistency with contemporary academic metrics and language used in institutional reporting structures. No pilot testing was conducted.

Survey Distribution and Data Collection

Between March 2025 and April 2025, the final survey was distributed electronically to department chairs or division chiefs of Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education-accredited plastic surgery residency programs in the United States, whose email addresses were publicly available. Survey invitations were sent a total of 4 times via personalized emails with embedded SurveyMonkey links by both senior authors. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and no financial incentives were provided. Institutional review board exemption was granted by The Ohio State University.

Inclusion Criteria

Responding programs were included in the final analysis if they completed at least 75% of the survey and provided their total faculty complement and hiring and resignation trends.

RESULTS

Survey Responses

The survey was sent to 80 chairs/chiefs of Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education-accredited plastic surgery programs, and a total of 35 responses were received (with an initial response rate of 43.8%). After reviewing all responses, all 35 responses met the inclusion criteria.

Basic Structure of Academic Plastic Surgery Services

Of the 35 responding academic plastic surgery programs, 82.4% (n = 29) operate as divisions within broader departments of surgery, whereas only 17.6% (n = 6) reported stand-alone departmental status. On average, programs use 11 core faculty members (range, 3–32) and 8 noncore or community-based faculty members (range, 0–93).

The financial flow within these practices predominantly follows a faculty group practice model (85.3%, n = 30), although other arrangements were reported, including hospital budget (8.7%, n = 3), funds flow budget (3.0%, n = 1), and corporate health system-based models (3.0%, n = 1).

Recruitment and Faculty Growth

During the past 1, 5, and 10 years, programs added an average of 1.7 ± 1.4, 4.3 ± 2.7, and 6.5 ± 3.5 new faculty members, respectively. The assistant professor rank represented the majority of these additions: 82.4% (1 y), 83.7% (5 y), and 84.6% (10 y), respectively.

During the same time intervals, academic programs reported faculty losses of 0.4 ± 0.7, 2.2 ± 1.9, and 3.0 ± 2.5 faculty members, respectively. These losses most frequently occurred at the assistant professor level, accounting for 70.6% (1 y), 54.4% (5 y), and 56.7% (10 y) (Fig. 1). The average academic faculty growth rate was 14.8% ± 18.3%, 17.5% ± 31.2%, and 31.4% ± 30.2% during the past 1, 5, and 10 years, respectively.

Faculty Loss

The most commonly cited reasons for faculty departure included family considerations (54.3%, n = 19), inadequate compensation (34.3%, n = 12), university environment (25.7%, n = 9), and insufficient access to the operating room (OR) (14.3%, n = 5). Other contributing factors are shown in Figure 2.

Reasons for faculty departure.

Programs cited several strategies as effective in retaining faculty, including competitive compensation packages (80.0%, n = 28), professional development initiatives (74.3%, n = 26), mentorship programs (65.7%, n = 23), and work-life balance accommodations (45.7%, n = 16). Other tactics are shown in Figure 3. Additionally, only 8.6% (n = 3) of programs reported having a formal faculty retention committee.

Retention strategies.

Mentorship and Development

Formal mentorship programs were available at 68.6% of institutions. Most programs supported mentorship for both assistant and associate professors (45.7%, n = 16), whereas 22.9% (n = 8) offered mentorship exclusively for assistant professors. Among mentorship models, mentee-driven matching and chair-appointed pairings were equally common (28.6%, n = 10 each). Key components of mentorship initiatives included continuous feedback (48.6%, n = 17), knowledge transfer (45.7%, n = 16), goal tracking (20.0%, n = 7), and emphasis on diversity and inclusion (11.4%, n = 4).

Faculty development programs were common and included leadership training (88.6%, n = 31), research funding opportunities (68.6%, n = 24), networking events (60.0%, n = 21), grant writing support (54.3%, n = 19), and career counseling (45.7%, n = 16). Eligible participants were assistant (91.4%, n = 32), associate (88.6%, n = 31), and full professors (82.9%, n = 29).

Recognition strategies included leadership roles (91.4%, n = 32), teaching and research awards (80.0%, n = 28), public acknowledgments at departmental meetings (42.9%, n = 15), access to professional development funds (37.1%, n = 13), and annual performance reviews (34.3%, n = 12).

Reported Retention Strategies

Compensation

The average midpoint salary reported for newly hired assistant professors was $413,243 with a range spanning from $300,000 to $640,000 (Fig. 4A). (Midpoint salary refers to the median salary within the reported range, aligned with the Association of American Medical Colleges [AAMC] benchmarks for regional and national averages.)

Faculty base salaries were most commonly determined based on clinical productivity (67.7%, n = 23), followed by academic rank (64.7%, n = 22), AAMC regional midpoint data (35.3%, n = 12), and AAMC national averages (26.5%, n = 9). Less commonly, base salaries were influenced by a fixed medical school pay scale (5.9%, n = 2), individual negotiation (2.9%, n = 1), or chair discretion (2.9%, n = 1) (Fig. 4B).

Faculty bonuses were awarded in the majority of programs and were most frequently based on productivity: either as a variable (79.4%, n = 27) or a flat bonus (5.9%, n = 2). Additional bonus criteria included citizenship contributions (variable, 38.2%, n = 13; flat, 8.8%, n = 3), group metrics (29.4%, n = 10), and departmental collections (20.6%, n = 7). Only 2 programs (5.9%) reported offering no bonus structure at all (Fig. 4C).

Sources of Salary Support

Ongoing faculty salaries were most often supported by clinical revenue generated by individual surgeons (65.7%, n = 23), shared division/department revenue (57.1%, n = 20), and hospital funding (62.9%, n = 22). Additional sources included endowment accounts (20.0%, n = 7), medical school support (11.4%, n = 4), and research grants (8.6%, n = 3).

Faculty Benefits

General Benefits

All academic programs (100%, n = 35) offered medical, dental, vision, malpractice insurance, and retirement plans (eg, 401(k)). Additional benefits included life insurance (91.4%, n = 32), short-term disability (88.6%, n = 31), long-term disability (85.7%, n = 30), flexible spending account/dependent care accounts (60.0%, n = 21), continuing medical education stipends (2.9%, n = 1), and health savings accounts (2.9%, n = 1).

Extra Benefits for New Faculty Hires

Among new hires, 88.6% (n = 31) received paid relocation expenses and 71.4% (n = 25) received signing bonuses. For research-focused hires, programs provided protected time (60.0%, n = 21), start-up funds (51.4%, n = 18), laboratory space (40.0%, n = 14), laboratory equipment (28.6%, n = 10), research coordinators (31.4%, n = 11), and research fellows (25.7%, n = 9).

Clinical support at onboarding included administrative assistants (85.7%, n = 30), surgical schedulers (77.1%, n = 27), medical assistants (62.9%, n = 22), and advanced practice providers (60.0%, n = 21).

Sources of Benefits

Funding for benefits most frequently came from clinical revenue of the plastic surgery division (65.7%, n = 23) and hospital support (65.7%, n = 23). Other sources included medical school support (40.0%, n = 14), surgery departments (34.3%, n = 12), endowments (22.9%, n = 8), and grant funding (11.4%, n = 4).

DISCUSSION

In this national survey of academic plastic surgery programs, we found that although faculty recruitment continues to rise, especially at the assistant professor level, long-term retention remains a persistent challenge, as last reported in 2014. Programs reported adding an average of 6.5 faculty members during the past decade but also losing 3 during the same period, with the majority of both recruitment and attrition occurring at the junior faculty level.

Despite modest improvements in overall faculty growth rates during the past decade (2014–2025) compared with prior reports (2004–2014), faculty turnover remains high and has, in fact, increased, with 39% of faculty departing within 10 years. Although compensation remains a central issue, with 34.3% of departures citing salary dissatisfaction, family consideration was the most cited reason for most faculty departures. These findings suggest that although the academic plastic surgery workforce has grown and formal support structures have expanded, retention issues remain unresolved.

This is consistent with work from the prior decade (2000–2013) that noted similar growth among junior faculty, with 81% of new hires at the assistant level.2,10,11 This pattern of early-career hiring reflects strategic investments in long-term faculty development, improved cost-efficiency, and adaptability of early-stage academic surgeons to evolving institutional cultures.12 More broadly, national faculty data from the AAMC confirm this expansion. Between 2012 and 2022, the number of full-time surgical faculty increased by 38.5%, with assistant professors representing the fastest-growing demographic.13 Successful recruitment hinges on comprehensive support packages that signal institutional investment. In our study, new faculty hires were offered a median salary of $413,243, with ranges extending as high as $640,000. These figures reflect a continued rise in entry-level compensation, previously reported at a mean of $307,000 in 2014.2 Further, in a 2022 AAMC Faculty Forward survey, compensation was cited as the top consideration among new faculty hires across all surgical departments.14 Beyond salary, the competitiveness of recruitment packages was further enhanced by signing bonuses, relocation allowances, benefits, and research support.15–18

Despite this faculty expansion, long-term retention in academic plastic surgery remains a significant challenge. In our study, faculty retention declined from 95.8% at 1 year to just 61.1% at 10 years, with a corresponding 10-year turnover rate rising to 38.9% from the previously reported 27%.2 Most turnover occurred at the assistant professor level, accounting for over half of all departures. Compared with other surgical specialties, such as general surgery, where attrition rates have been reported as high as 20%–30% during 5 years, plastic surgery’s 10-year turnover rate of 38.9% seems similarly challenging, though direct comparisons are limited by differing methodologies. This turnover, especially among early-career faculty, has been widely reported in academic medicine and is attributed to a combination of inadequate support, compensation dissatisfaction, family pressures, and unclear promotion pathways.6,7,19–26 To improve retention, programs should consider evidence-based strategies such as regular salary reviews tied to promotion, enhanced leadership development, and targeted support for work-life balance, including flexible scheduling and family accommodations; based on published data, career development programs for women and underrepresented groups have shown success in reducing attrition.

In our cohort, the top-cited reasons for departure included family considerations and inadequate compensation. Despite the prevalence of this problem, proactive retention mechanisms remain scarce. Only 8.6% of surveyed programs reported having a formal faculty retention committee. This is especially concerning given the evidence that structured retention initiatives could significantly reduce faculty loss and improve engagement.21,25 The high 10-year turnover seen in our survey may carry serious implications. Each departure imposes financial and administrative burdens and may also erode institutional memory, mentorship continuity, and clinical capacity. Faculty turnover costs including recruitment, onboarding, lost revenue, and patient diversion have been estimated at more than $400,000 per surgical faculty member.19,27

Our thematic analysis showed that compensation was the most frequently cited strategy to improve retention, including salary increases, productivity-based pay, bonuses, retention incentives, and professional education. This emphasis is consistent with prior work indicating that compensation and benefits remain a meaningful factor in faculty engagement and retention in academic surgical settings.28,29 For example, Wai et al found that compensation and benefits were statistically significant predictors of retention among surgical faculty (though less strongly than governance and collegiality).29 Because compensation models in academic surgery often intertwine clinical productivity, research, and teaching expectations, our findings reinforce that transparent and competitive compensation is foundational but may alone be insufficient without complementary supports. Consistently, a recent analysis noted that academic surgeon salaries rose by only approximately 2.9% annually from 2017 to 2023, below inflation, and emphasized that compensation gaps “often factor heavily into graduating residents’ decisions” about academia.30,31 Second, the culture and environment theme highlighted the importance of department and division climate: supportive leadership, fairness and transparency, and a positive work atmosphere. The literature strongly corroborates that workplace culture, collegiality/collaboration, and voice in decision‐making are stronger predictors of intent to stay than salary alone.11,29 For instance, the same AAMC survey study identified department governance and collegiality/collaboration as more powerful predictors of satisfaction than compensation.29 In another review of academic surgical attrition, poor institutional culture and lack of structural support emerged as barriers to retention.6,32 Thus, our data affirm that culture and environment remain a principal domain for intervention. Flexibility and scheduling centered on flexible work arrangements, work-life balance, and dedicated time/resources for research or clinical growth were also among the most common strategies cited. Although much of the prior research on burnout focuses on trainees rather than faculty, our results suggest that academic faculty similarly value scheduling flexibility and protected time, and they potentially contribute to attrition.33

Program leadership is the primary determinant of faculty well-being, job satisfaction, and intent to stay. Across large multi-institutional cohorts, physicians’ ratings of their immediate leaders’ behaviors (eg, listening, clear vision, professional development support, recognition, and fairness) are strongly associated with lower burnout and higher job satisfaction over time; these leadership effects are cross-sectional and predict subsequent changes in physician well-being.34,35 In academic surgery specifically, engagement and retention correlate more with governance, collegiality, and the quality of the work environment than with compensation alone, underscoring the outsized role of program chairs/chiefs in setting transparent rules, distributing work equitably, enabling scholarly activity, and ensuring faculty voice in decision-making.31 The literature also emphasizes promotion pathways and leadership development as key to retaining faculty, especially in preventing the “stagnation” effect of junior faculty.19,36 Further, mentorship has long been identified as a vital component of academic surgery career success and retention; individualized mentoring and sponsorship correlate with advancement, satisfaction, and less attrition.11,31 In reality, most programs functioned as divisions within broader departments of surgery rather than stand-alone departments.37–39 Divisional structure may influence the degree of local control over clinical resources (eg, block time allocation, clinic space, staffing models), compensation plan flexibility, and the ability to formalize protected academic time or recruitment packages.37–39 It may also affect governance and faculty voice in institutional decision-making, which has been linked to physician engagement and intent to stay.37–39 These organizational differences provide a plausible framework for why retention strategies may not be “one-size-fits-all” across institutions.

Turnover is not uncommon in academic medicine, and national data demonstrate meaningful rates of physicians leaving or moving between practices over time.19–26 However, our study provided insights that program leaders can reduce preventable mismatch by incorporating a realistic job preview during recruitment (including explicit expectations for clinical volume, academic output, call frequency, and the operational pathway to obtain OR time). Institutions can also treat OR access, call equity, and administrative support as retention infrastructure particularly for junior faculty who comprised the majority of both hires and departures. Finally, protected academic time should be paired with defined resources and measurable milestones (including mentorship and sponsorship plans, access to research staff, and transparent promotion criteria).

This study is not without limitations. As with the prior survey by Chen et al,2 our results are limited by the self-reported nature of program responses, which may be subject to bias or incomplete reporting. Further, a formal nonresponder analysis could not be performed due to the strict anonymous nature of the survey sent. Because our respondents were program chairs/chiefs, the reported reasons for departure reflect leadership perception rather than the departing faculty member’s self-reported rationale. In particular, “family or personal considerations” may represent a true primary driver for some faculty, but it may also reflect downstream effects of modifiable workplace conditions such as call burden, schedule predictability, compensation structure, and OR access. Similarly, we did not capture the subsequent destination of departing faculty (including local transition to private practice versus academic appointment elsewhere), which limits the ability to distinguish dissatisfaction with the role from geographic preference, promotion opportunities, or career-stage transitions. These gaps highlight the need for future faculty-level studies that directly ascertain motivations and postdeparture trajectories. Finally, the cross-sectional, self-reported nature of the survey limits inferences to associations rather than causality, as unmeasured confounding and perception bias may influence observed relationships and reported drivers of retention.40

CONCLUSIONS

Although faculty growth in academic plastic surgery has remained strong, turnover and retention are worsening. The relative stagnation in departmental advancement also raises questions about the specialty’s institutional influence. Given these trends, institutions should consider forming dedicated retention committees, refining mentorship models, and advocating for departmental status to ensure faculty autonomy, identity, and resource access.
