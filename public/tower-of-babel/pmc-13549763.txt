Online Presentation of No‐, Low‐ and Full‐Strength Alcohol in Three New Zealand Online Alcohol Retailers: Implications for Harm‐Reduction Policy
Authors: Julia Vajda De Albuquerque, Kate Kersey, Natalie Walker, Anna Rolleston, Bodo Lang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe availability of no‐ and low‐alcohol (NOLO) products could lead to reduced consumption of full‐strength alcohol, although concerns exist about their marketing and links to full‐strength alcohol brands. This study examines how NOLO beer and cider products are structured, categorised and presented, relative to their full‐strength alcohol counterparts, within online alcohol retailers in New Zealand.MethodsBetween July–September 2024 an observational audit was conducted of three nationwide online alcohol retailers (two supermarkets, one liquor retailer). Retailer websites were assessed under relevant search and browsing conditions, examining the availability, visibility and co‐presentation of NOLO and full‐strength alcohol products, the proportion of NOLO brands with a full‐strength alcohol variant and the visibility of information about alcohol content.ResultsFull‐strength alcohol products appeared on two of the three retailers' home pages, whereas NOLOs were absent. NOLOs comprised 7%–9% of beers/ciders offered. Between 2% and 11% of full‐strength alcohol beers/ciders were miscategorised as NOLOs. Searches using NOLO‐related terms returned 11%–100% full‐strength alcohol products. NOLO products were less prominent than full‐strength alcohol products across browsing and search conditions, and usually lower cost. Most NOLOs (83%–92%) were line extensions of full‐strength alcohol brands. Between 14% and 33% of NOLO products lacked clear alcohol content information.Discussion and ConclusionInconsistencies in the categorisation, presentation and labelling of NOLO and full‐strength alcohols were observed in all three online alcohol retailers. These features shape how consumers will encounter alcohol products online and may contribute to consumer confusion. Policy measures to improve the clarity and consistency of online alcohol retail presentation are warranted.

Introduction

Alcohol is a Class 1 carcinogen and a leading cause of death, disease and social harm in many countries, including Aotearoa New Zealand (NZ) [1]. However, alcohol consumption in many countries remains relatively normalised. In NZ, 75% of those aged ≥ 15 years consumed alcohol in 2024/2025 [2]. In 2022, the World Health Organization highlighted products with lower alcohol by volume (ABV) as a potential strategy to reduce alcohol consumption [3]. A category of no‐ and low‐alcohol products (NOLO) had been marketed by alcohol companies for many years prior to the World Health Organization announcement, although definitions for these products vary across jurisdictions. The NZ Sale and Supply of Alcohol Act 2012 [4], which governs ‘alcohol’ products with ≥ 1.15% ABV, contains only passing reference to ‘low‐alcohol’ products (defined as not more than 2.5% ethanol) and ‘non‐alcoholic’ (not defined). Further detail is found in the NZ alcohol labelling rules set out in the legally binding Food Standards Australia New Zealand Code, with alcohol similarly defined as a product containing ≥ 1.15% ABV [5, 6]. The Code defines NOLOs as containing ≤ 1.15% ABV, with the products further differentiated: ‘alcoholic drinks’ are considered ≤ 1.15% ABV, and a ‘drink’ is considered 0.5%–1.15% ABV [6]. Drink that contains > 1.15% ABV are not permitted to be called ‘low‐alcohol’. All alcoholic drinks with < 1.15% ABV must state ‘contains not more than X% alcohol by volume,’ and those with > 0.5% ABV must reference the number of ‘standard drinks’. These delineations are similar to those used in the United Kingdom (UK) [7].

A more recent and rapid growth in the NOLO market is evident, and is a result of significant investment by alcohol companies, as witnessed in the UK [8]. The NOLO market is anticipated to grow substantially in many countries [9], enabling the industry to now present itself as actively addressing ‘alcohol‐related harm’, while also creating a profitable new market. Although some NOLO products are marketed by standalone brands, many NOLOs are line extensions of existing alcohol brands (e.g., Heineken‐ > Heineken Zero), which leverage the symbolic value of a brand and apply it to a product variant to target a new segment of consumers [8, 10]. A report for Great Britain (with NOLOs defined as < 1.2% ABV) found that among the 100 best‐selling products by volume within each beverage type, 94% of NOLO beer sales and 99% of NOLO cider sales came from companies with full‐strength alcohol products [11].

As with many industries, the alcohol industry tailors marketing based on various factors, including consumer characteristics and demand. While there is potential for NOLOs to reduce alcohol consumption, debate exists around whether NOLOs substitute full‐strength alcohol consumption (thus reducing harm) or are consumed in addition to these products [12]. It is also important to consider how a substitutive effect varies across different socio‐demographic cohorts, with some research showing NOLOs are more likely to be consumed by those with higher incomes [13].

Concerns have been raised that NOLOs could encourage the sale and consumption of full‐strength alcohol products. Marketing and promotion of NOLOs may allow alcohol brands to gain access to on‐ and off‐line audiences that would otherwise be off‐limits to them, either legally or because of the potential public disapproval of alcohol associations (e.g., Formula One) [14, 15]. For example, in NZ there are no restrictions on where alcohol branded NOLOs can be sold [4]. Therefore, NOLOs are often found in supermarkets and grocery outlets, including being co‐located with soft drinks [16]. Such promotion increases the visibility of alcoholic beverages in general and could foster brand awareness and loyalty. In Australia, where alcohol products are not usually allowed to be sold in supermarkets, in 2024, 59% of zero alcohol products sold in supermarkets were alcohol line extensions [17]. Research conducted on alcohol marketing of zero alcohol products in Australia and NZ has also shown that rather than being promoted as a substitute for usual drinking occasions, the majority of campaigns promoted drinking in new and novel situations (e.g., at the gym or driving) [18]. It is argued that in addition to creating alcohol brand awareness, this also reinforces the idea that drinking alcohol of any kind is integral to everyday life and socialising, thus helping to normalise drinking [13, 19]. Furthermore, similarities in branding between full‐strength alcohol and line extension NOLOs may confuse consumers as to the alcoholic content [10]. Research has shown that being exposed to NOLOs, especially line extensions of full‐strength alcohol products, may trigger alcohol consumption for those in alcohol recovery [20]. Concerns remain that consumption and increased visibility of NOLOs may be a gateway to earlier consumption of full‐strength alcohol by adolescents and young people, especially where there are no age restrictions on the purchase of NOLOs [21, 22]. Some parents view these products as a viable alternative to full‐strength alcohol for under‐age drinkers [21]. However, recent experimental research found that zero alcohol products (irrespective of whether they are full‐strength alcohol line extensions) were more likely than soft drinks to make Australian adolescents think of alcohol [23] and that those who see and like zero alcohol advertising have increased intentions to drink full‐strength alcohol [24].

Although NOLO research independent from the alcohol industry has been increasing internationally [8, 11, 25, 26], there is limited research on NOLOs in NZ. A NZ Master's thesis explored the knowledge and perceptions of 25 people using zero alcohol products as a replacement for binge drinking [27]. The author found that such products were ‘not considered as important as regular alcohol and were only useful for temporary substitution’ [27]. A NZ experimental study found that aisle placement of zero‐alcohol products had little impact on social norms across ethnic groups [28]. In Australia, research has explored how adolescents and parents perceive zero alcohol products, how these products are used in sponsorship and, more recently, how these products are promoted to and perceived by consumers [14, 17, 18, 21, 22, 23, 29]. However, there is a lack of research in both countries on how these products are advertised and promoted. Given NZ's permissive regulatory environment for the sale of NOLOs, and growing concerns by some about how the visibility and accessibility of NOLOs may drive consumption of full‐strength alcohol, we sought to understand how NOLOs are presented in the NZ retail environment. This information is particularly important considering international research has shown that increasing the proportion of NOLOs available in a simulated online supermarket may reduce alcohol purchasing behaviour [30].

We conducted an exploratory audit to obtain information on the availability, visibility and presentation of NOLOs relative to full‐strength alcohol products, within NZ online alcohol retailers. We focused on the online supermarket environment given the small but growing popularity of such services in NZ [31], and evidence that NZ youth under 18 are easily able to access and purchase alcohol via online retailers [32, 33]. We sought ABV information, as research undertaken in NZ and the UK in 2021 found some NZ online alcohol retailers did not enable customers to easily view mandatory health warning labels, such as ABV [34]. We focused on NOLO beer and cider as research indicates these often have full‐strength alcohol parent brands, and international research shows that in 2023 these products comprise the majority of the NOLO market [8, 11]. The audit had three research questions: (i) How are full‐strength alcohol and NOLO products structured, categorised and presented by online retailers?; (ii) What is the relationship between NOLO line extensions and their full‐strength counterparts on online retailer websites? Specifically, what proportion of NOLOs also have a full‐strength alcohol variant visible, how consistent is the branding of NOLOs with the parent full‐strength alcohol brand, and do prices differ?; and (iii) How prominent is information indicating alcohol strength (e.g., no alcohol: < 0.5% ABV, low alcohol: 0.5%–1.15% ABV or full‐strength alcohol)?

Methods

An observational audit of three NZ online alcohol retailers was undertaken by one author (J.V.D.A.), with oversight from two authors (B.L., N.W.). Of the two chosen supermarket brands, one was operated by a NZ‐owned company and the other was Australian‐owned. The chosen alcohol‐only retailer was NZ‐owned and had the greatest number of stores throughout the country. Due to the dynamic nature of grocery retailer websites, each website was searched within a short time‐frame: Supermarket 1 between 17 and 18 July 2024; Supermarket 2 between 15 and 16 August 2024; and the Alcohol Retailer between 5 and 6 September 2024. Screenshots were taken to record the information viewed.

To address the first research question, products shown on each retailer's home page were reviewed to ascertain if beer and cider NOLOs were listed on each website, and if so, where (e.g., under a special category within the alcohol section, co‐located with other beverages, etc.). Categories where beer and cider NOLOs might be listed (either specific to NOLOs or a beverage type, e.g., beer) were then browsed to identify the total number of NOLOs, and to ascertain whether any full‐strength alcohol products were shown. Full‐strength alcohol was defined as all alcohol products over the limit for NOLOs, so may include lighter versions of products (e.g., alcohol content above the limit for NOLOs, such as 2%, 2.5%). The following terms were then used to search the site: ‘low alcohol’, ‘alcohol free’, ‘zero alcohol’ and ‘no alcohol’. The absolute and relative number of beers and ciders incorrectly presented as NOLOs in each browse and search was documented. The alcohol brand name ‘Heineken’ was then used as a search term to determine what full‐strength alcohol and NOLO options would appear. This beer brand was randomly selected using a computer‐generated random list of most consumed beer brands in NZ (number 1 was selected). Finally, three occasion‐based search terms (‘chips’, ‘party’ and ‘BBQ’) were applied individually to see if consumers would be exposed to full‐strength alcohol and/or NOLOs in searches related to alcohol (in NZ, alcohol tends to be present in parties and barbeques, and served with chips).

To address the second research question, a count was made of the number of NOLOs offered by the retailer that also had a full‐strength alcohol variant visible on the website. Product pages were then reviewed to check the similarity of the different product categories. Three NOLO beer brands that were full‐strength alcohol line extensions (among the most popular NZ beer brands) were chosen from Supermarket 1, with prices and website images recorded. The beer brands were randomly selected using the previously mentioned computer‐generated random list of NZ popular beer brands (numbers 1, 2 and 3 were selected). A systematic guide was developed and used to determine similarities between products, with products categorised as: virtually identical, very similar, moderately similar or having low similarity (Appendix 1).

To address the third research question, websites were searched for relevant products that appeared when searching and browsing for NOLOs. Once identified, products were ‘clicked on’, which opened the product page (the text and image were saved). Website text was inspected for information about ABV. If this information was missing, the image was searched for ABV information. The absolute and relative numbers of NOLOs that provided ABV as written text or required image inspection were ascertained.

Results

How Full‐Strength Alcohol and NOLO Products Were Structured, Categorised and Presented

None of the three online retailers displayed NOLOs on their home pages. However, full‐strength alcohol products appeared on two of the three retailers' home page: Supermarket 1 showed two full‐strength alcohol products: one beer and one wine (representing 4% of the 50 products shown). All 13 products on the Alcohol Retailer home page were full‐strength alcohol. NOLOs were visible in both special categories (e.g., ‘No and Low Alcohol Beers’) and general alcohol categories, e.g., ‘Beer and Cider’ (Appendix 2). Each retailer's website listed NOLOs in different browsing categories.

Supermarket 1 correctly categorised its NOLO products, as all were no‐alcohol products (< 0.5% ABV) and included in the ‘Alcohol Free’ category. Supermarket 2 was less accurate with its ‘Low and No Alcohol Beers’ category, as it included six full‐strength alcohol products (18% of the 35 products shown). The alcohol retailer did not provide separate categories for NOLOs apart from ‘Beer—Lighter’ which could include full‐strength alcohol products with lower ABVs but still > 1.15% ABV (i.e., not NOLOs). While the ‘Beer—Lighter’ section included the majority of NOLOs identified in the ‘Beer and Cider’ category (it excluded two but included an additional one), it also included 40 full‐strength alcohol products, often designated as ‘low‐carb’ or products with lighter alcohol content, but still higher than NOLOs (> 1.15% ABV).

The search terms ‘no alcohol’, ‘alcohol free’ and ‘zero alcohol’ most often retrieved full‐strength alcohol products and did not retrieve all available no‐alcohol products. Of 12 searches (four searches for each of the three retailers), only 17% (2 searches; ‘zero alcohol’ and ‘alcohol free’ in Supermarket 1) exclusively retrieved NOLOs. The eight other searches (83%) retrieved full‐strength alcohol products. All ‘low alcohol’ searches retrieved full‐strength alcohol products, including those that would be considered lighter versions of full‐strength alcohol products (e.g., ‘low carb’ or ‘low sugar’ versions, but not NOLOs). Overall, when searching for the brand name ‘Heineken’ across all retailers, NOLO‐specific search terms were more likely to return full‐strength alcohol than NOLOs. Of the nine searches (3 retailers × 3 terms: zero, low and no alcohol) that should have returned only NOLO products, only three did so; 67% (6/9) returned full‐strength alcohol (Appendix 4). Overall, full‐strength alcohol accounted for 60% (199/331) of NOLO search results. This finding indicates that using NOLO‐specific search terms virtually guarantee exposure to full‐strength alcohol.

When using the three occasion‐based search terms (i.e., ‘chips’, ‘party’ and ‘BBQ’) no full‐strength alcohol or NOLOs were identified for supermarkets 1 and 2. The alcohol retailer had full‐strength alcohol products (beers, liqueur and wine) show‐up when searching for ‘chips’ (n = 472 products), 14 full‐strength beers and one full‐strength spirit when searching for ‘party’ (n = 27 products) and two full‐strength spirits when searching for ‘BBQ’ (n = 24 products). All alcoholic beverages identified in the last two searches had either ‘party’ or ‘BBQ’ in their name. None of the searches returned any NOLOs.

The Relationship Between NOLO Line Extensions and Their Full‐Strength Counterparts on Online Retailer Websites

Most identified NOLO beers and ciders were a line extension of a full‐strength alcohol brand available on retailers' websites: 83% (15/17) of NOLOs for Supermarket 1, 91% (31/34) of NOLOs for Supermarket 2 and 92% (34/37) of NOLOs for the Alcohol Retailer (Appendix 8). Across the three retailers, all NOLO products that were not a line extension of a full‐strength alcohol brand were beers. Overall, the sampled NOLOs had consistent branding (container size, number of individual bottles, shape, logo, with colour variations) with their parent brand, and were considered moderately similar or very similar visually (Figure 1). Using normal retail prices, NOLOs were cheaper than their full‐strength alcohol versions. However, when full‐strength alcohol was on sale, the NOLO version was more expensive (Figure 1).

The Prominence of Information Indicating Alcohol Strength

When searching the websites of the three retailers', it was possible to add a product to the shopping trolley without clicking through to the actual product page (where the product description and alcohol content should be displayed). Overall, 14% of beers and ciders categorised as a NOLO by Supermarket 1 did not present the ABV in the product description and required careful examination of the image to reveal this information (or confirmation, for those which had the ABV stated in the title). This metric was 33% for Supermarket 2 and 27% for the Alcohol Retailer. In addition, 8% of products categorised as NOLO on Supermarket 2's website and 13% of those on the Alcohol Retailer's website did not disclose the ABV and required searches outside the retailer's website to find this information (Appendix 5).

Discussion

This exploratory audit of three NZ online alcohol retailers found that NOLO variants represented a very small proportion of the beers and ciders offered by each retailer. The categorisation of NOLOs on retailer websites was frequently inaccurate, and search terms associated with NOLOs also retrieved full‐strength alcohol products. NOLOs were primarily line extensions of full‐strength alcohol brands, with similar packaging and identifiable branding. Marketing of both NOLOs and full‐strength alcohol products also utilised lifestyle framing words, such as ‘low carb’ or ‘low sugar’. Although NOLOs were predominately lower cost than their full‐strength alcohol counterparts, this did not always hold true when full‐strength alcohol products were on sale. Finally, some reviewed products were in breach of NZ alcohol labelling laws [6], as nowhere on the product image or in the website text was the alcohol content stated. Given the above, it is clear that people wishing to access only NOLO products on the reviewed websites at the time of the study are likely to have been exposed to advertising and promotion of full‐strength alcohol products. This finding means shoppers may end up purchasing a higher alcohol content product than originally planned. Furthermore, the marketing and sale of both NOLOs and full‐strength alcohol products using health claims could also lead consumers to perceive that such full‐strength alcohol products also have lower alcohol content [35, 36]—especially if ABV information is not visible.

Moving forward, the NZ government needs to pay more attention to the online promotion of alcohol and NOLOs, to strike the right balance between harnessing NOLOs' potential as a ‘step‐down’ for current users of alcohol, while minimising any potential harms as a ‘step‐in’ for non‐users of alcohol. Importantly, further monitoring of the marketing and presentation of alcohol in online environments is needed. Ideally online retailers selling alcoholic beverages alongside other groceries (e.g., supermarkets) should not have full‐strength alcohol and NOLOs displayed on the home page. Retailers specialising in alcoholic beverages (e.g., liquor stores), should also have NOLOs visible on the home page. On‐going monitoring and enforcement are needed to ensure alcohol content is clearly stated, visible and shown in a consistent format for both full‐strength alcohol and NOLOs (in the online product title and product description). Furthermore, clear and enforced recommendations are needed around the display, listing and retrieval of NOLOs in online environments, to ensure: (i) online shoppers can locate NOLOs without being exposed to any area containing or mentioning full‐strength alcohol; (ii) all NOLOs and full‐strength alcohol are correctly categorised in accordance with NZ's legal definitions of NOLOs; (iii) NOLOs are presented in categories separate from ‘lighter/lower alcohol’ products (e.g., a 2% ABV beer or a 7% ABV wine) and these products are not incorrectly labelled as ‘low alcohol’ (0.5% to 1.15% ABV); (iv) full‐strength alcohol (including low carb/sugar products) do not appear when searching for NOLOs; (v) use of the NZ categories for no‐alcohol and low‐alcohol NOLOs [5]; and (vi) alcohol retailers don't utilise associative search terms (e.g., party, BBQ, chips) to increase shoppers' potential exposure to full‐strength alcohol.

Additional research involving consumers is required to confirm or refute the above, with a focus on those at most risk of alcohol related harms, such as youth under 18 years, pregnant women, people in alcohol recovery, people wishing to reduce their alcohol consumption and consumers who struggle to utilise product information [24]. For these groups, it is essential that the ABV of a product is clearly visible, so that they can make informed choices about the product they are purchasing. As an example, between 2020 and 2024, two population surveys undertaken in Great Britain found increasing consumption of NOLOs (from 35% to 44%) among those consuming alcohol at increasing and risky levels, particularly for women [37]. A 2023 report from Great Britain [11] had similar findings to the NZ study, in respect to NOLOs often having full‐strength alcohol line extensions, and price differentials existing between full‐strength and NOLO alcohol products.

Strengths and Limitations

This study is one of the first to explore the NZ online retail environment for NOLOs and full‐strength alcohol. Although the audit only reviewed three online retailers at one point in time, we included two of the largest supermarkets and one of the largest alcohol retailers in the country. Therefore, at the time of our study, many shoppers browsing or searching for alcohol in these online retailers would have been affected by how NOLOs and full‐strength alcohol were promoted and sold online by these retailers. In terms of limitations, we are unable to determine how generalisable our findings are to these retailers at different times of the year and/or to other alcohol retailers, given retailer websites are dynamic, and products, promotions and price change over time. Australian research has shown that online alcohol retail environments can also push product and price promotions just before the point of purchase and/or via retailer apps [38]—our audit did not capture such information.

Conclusion

Our findings are a reminder to NZ alcohol retailers to improve the way they promote and sell alcohol online. NZ alcohol policy makers also need to closely monitor online alcohol promotions and sales, plus monitor and enforce legal labelling requirements regarding alcohol content. To be an effective harm reduction tool, NOLOs should be substitutive, not additive to full‐strength alcohol and relevant NZ policy and regulations must support this.
