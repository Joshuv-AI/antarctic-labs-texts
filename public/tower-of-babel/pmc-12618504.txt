Integrated metabolomics and lipidomics reveal fatty acid dysregulation in aortic dissection independent of hypertension
Authors: Xuanxuan Bai, Hongbin Lin, Weiliang Zheng, Lijun Sun, Shuangkun Chen, Lin Lu, Lulu Chen, Xijie Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAortic dissection (AD), characterized by rupture of the aortic wall, presents significant diagnostic challenges due to its rapid onset and nonspecific clinical symptoms. Although conventional imaging techniques are clinically useful, they typically lack sensitivity for early AD detection, are often unavailable in resource-limited settings, and cannot elucidate the mechanistic basis of AD pathogenesis. In this study, we used metabolomics and lipidomics to analyze serum samples from healthy controls, hypertensive AD patients, and normotensive AD patients, revealing distinct metabolic and lipidomic perturbations associated with AD, independent of blood pressure status. Notably, mitochondrial dysfunction was a pivotal factor in AD pathogenesis, and key metabolites such as acetylcarnitine served as both an AD biomarker and an indicator of disrupted lipid metabolism. Lipidomic profiling further revealed a consistent accumulation of specific fatty acids in AD patients, including elevated levels of FA(16:1), FA(20:1), FA(22:5), and FA(20:2) in both hypertensive and normotensive subgroups. Additionally, the sphingolipid signaling pathway was the most markedly altered lipidomic pathway between the two AD groups. These findings offer new insights into the complex lipidomic and metabolic mechanisms underlying AD pathogenesis, paving the way for improved diagnostics and targeted therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23667-8.Keywords: Aortic dissection, Metabolomics, Lipidomics, Mitochondria metabolism, Acetylcarnitine, Fatty acidsSubject terms: Chemical biology, Biomarkers, Cardiology

Introduction

Aortic dissection (AD) is a life-threatening cardiovascular condition where blood enters the aortic wall through an intimal tear, creating a false lumen between the vessel layers1,2. This pathological condition can swiftly progress, leading to fatal complications such as aortic rupture, organ failure, or death. AD is clinically stratified into two primary categories based on the tear’s anatomical location3: Stanford Type A dissections occur in the ascending aorta and may extend to the aortic arch and descending aorta, whereas Stanford Type B dissections are confined to the descending aorta distal to the aortic arch. Type A dissections occur more frequently than Type B4. The etiological factors associated with AD include hypertension2,5,6, genetic defects7,8, age9,10 and gender11. Hypertension is a well-established risk factor for AD, with clinical studies documenting its prevalence in 65%-75% of cases12. Hypertension increases AD risk by elevating vascular wall tension and intimal stress. While hypertension remains a key risk factor, substantial evidence now demonstrates AD incidence in normotensive populations13,14, indicating that currently unrecognized molecular or biomechanical mechanisms contribute to disease etiology. Therefore, investigating normotensive AD patients could both elucidate disease pathophysiology and identify novel biomarkers for targeted therapies. A comprehensive risk assessment incorporating genetic, lifestyle, and environmental factors may improve predictive models and enable personalized approaches to AD prevention and management.

AD diagnosis primarily utilizes imaging techniques including echocardiography, cardiac computed tomography, and cardiac magnetic resonance angiography15. While these imaging modalities are essential for anatomical diagnosis, they lack sensitivity for early molecular changes. Modern mass spectrometry-based metabolomics and lipidomics now serve as essential complements to traditional methods, quantitatively analyzing the complete set of endogenous metabolites generated through cellular metabolic pathways16,17. Metabolomic and lipidomic profiling of biofluids (e.g., blood, urine) provides critical insights into disease mechanisms, physiological perturbations, and therapeutic responses. In AD research, these approaches detect molecular changes preceding structural abnormalities on imaging, enabling biomarker discovery and advancing early diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Mitochondrial dysfunction merits special focus in AD research, given its central role in ATP production, oxidative stress regulation, and apoptotic signaling18. These mitochondrial functions are essential for preserving aortic wall homeostasis and vascular integrity19,20. Mitochondrial dysfunction, primarily induced by oxidative stress, disrupts energy production and causes cellular damage. As key regulators of apoptosis, dysfunctional mitochondria promote vascular smooth muscle cell apoptosis, leading to aortic medial degeneration and predisposition to dissection21. Mitochondria also regulate calcium homeostasis, a critical secondary messenger in cellular signaling pathways. Dysfunctional mitochondrial calcium regulation may cause cytotoxic calcium overload, inducing cell death and aortic wall degeneration22,23. Mitochondria serve as the central hub for fatty acid β-oxidation, establishing an essential coupling between lipid metabolism and mitochondrial function24. Disruptions in lipid metabolism, such as decreased fatty acid oxidation, can exacerbate mitochondrial dysfunction by increasing oxidative stress and impairing mitochondrial efficiency to induce AD25. These metabolic alterations exacerbate cellular injury and compromise aortic wall integrity26, potentially accelerating AD progression. However, the mechanistic interplay between mitochondrial bioenergetics and fatty acid oxidation in this pathological cascade remains incompletely understood.

In this study, we present, for the first time, an analysis of potential biomarkers for Type A AD (TAAD) based on blood pressure status. We performed serum metabolomics comparing TAAD patients stratified by hypertension status and healthy controls. Furthermore, we conducted a secondary in-depth lipidomics analysis to validate and further support the metabolomics findings. Our study aims to improve the understanding of the underlying pathophysiology of TAAD by identifying key metabolic alterations—particularly mitochondrial dysfunction and decreased fatty acid catabolism—independent of hypertension. These findings will advance research by revealing novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets, ultimately guiding more precise clinical management strategies to prevent dissection complications.

Materials and methods

Human specimens and selection criteria

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Cardiovascular Hospital Affiliated to Xiamen University (approval 2022 YLK-25). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study encompassed three distinct groups (Table 1): individuals with TAAD accompanied by hypertension (HP-AD, n = 16), individuals with TAAD without hypertension (NP-AD, n = 14), and a healthy control population (Ctr, n = 14). The sample size for this study was determined based on practical considerations, especially the scarcity of non-hypertensive patients with TAAD. There were no overall differences in age or gender among the three groups. Patients’ TAAD was diagnosed according to medical standards, and hypertension was assessed based on medical history and clinical manifestations. Patient samples were collected from Department of Cardiac Surgery at Xiamen Cardiovascular Hospital of Xiamen University (collected from 2018 to 2023). Patients presenting the following conditions were excluded: Marfan syndrome; genetic syndromes; haemodialysis; neoplasm; inflammatory systemic disease; infectious diseases; autoimmune; venous thrombosis; long-term use of immunosuppressive drugs; other non-traumatic aortic diseases (aortic aneurysm, etc.). The healthy population consisted of individuals who voluntarily participated in this research, with all samples having been verified through health examinations in Department of Cardiac Surgery at Xiamen Cardiovascular Hospital of Xiamen University (collected from 2022 to 2023). Serum was collected from three groups of individuals for subsequent studies.

Serum sample collection and preparation of omics samples

Whole blood was collected into coagulation tubes with gray caps (KDL, China) and refrigerated at 4 °C to induce clotting. The samples were then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at room temperature. The upper, clear serum obtained was stored at -80 °C until analysis. The subsequent targeted metabolomics and lipidomics studies were entrusted to Applied Protein Technology, Shanghai, China. The complete list of measured metabolites from metabolomics is included in Table S1. The complete list of measured lipids from lipidomics is included in Table S2.

In metabolomics, to isolate metabolites from the samples, a 400 µL solution of cold methanol/acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) was mixed with 200 µL serum both to precipitate proteins and to extract metabolites, followed by thorough vortex mixing. Concurrently, to enable precise quantification of metabolites, solutions of stable-isotope internal standards including glycoursodeoxycholic acid-d₅, lithocholic acid-d₄, taurolithocholic acid-d₅, glycolithocholic acid-d₄, melatonin-d₄, 8-iso-prostaglandin F₂α-d₄, creatinine-d₃, L-carnitine-d₉, L-glutamic acid-d₅, L-tyrosine-¹³C₆, L-histidine-¹³C₆, L-threonine-d₅-¹⁵N, cholic acid-d₄, L-lysine-d₄, L-arginine-¹³C₆, L-serine-¹³C₃-¹⁵N, docosahexaenoic acid-d₅, and glycocholic acid-d₅, were incorporated into the extraction solvent. The resultant mixture was transferred to a fresh centrifuge tube and spun at 14,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to separate the supernatant. This supernatant was then dried using a vacuum centrifuge. For subsequent LC-MS analysis, the dried extracts were redissolved in 100 µL of acetonitrile/water (1:1, v/v), centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the clear supernatant was then ready for injection.

In lipidomics, lipid extraction was performed using the Methyl-tert-Butyl Ether (MTBE) method. Initially, 22 µL serum were combined with 200 µL of methanol. Subsequently, 10 µL of internal lipid standards including 15:0–18:1-d₇-phosphatidylcholine, 15:0–18:1-d₇-phosphatidylethanolamine, 15:0–18:1-d₇-phosphatidylserine (sodium salt), 15:0–18:1-d₇-phosphatidylglycerol (sodium salt), 15:0–18:1-d₇-phosphatidylinositol (ammonium salt), 15:0–18:1-d₇-phosphatidic acid (sodium salt), 18:1-d₇-lysophosphatidylcholine, 18:1-d₇-lysophosphatidylethanolamine, 18:1-d₇-cholesteryl ester, 18:1-d₇-monoacylglycerol, 15:0–18:1-d₇-diacylglycerol, 15:0–18:1-d₇-15:0-triacylglycerol, d₁₈:1–18:1-d₉-sphingomyelin, and cholesterol-d₇ and 800 µL MTBE were added to the mix. This mixture was then thoroughly vortexed and subjected to sonication for 20 min at 4℃, followed by a 30-minute rest at room temperature. Afterwards, 200 µL of MS-grade water was incorporated, and the mixture was again vortexed and spun down at 14,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 ℃. The top layer of organic solvent was collected and evaporated under a stream of nitrogen. For LC-MS analysis, the residues were redissolved in 200 µL of an isopropanol (IPA) / acetonitrile (9:1, v/v) solution and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 ℃. The clear supernatant was then ready for injection.

LC-MS/MS method and data processing

For metabolomics, experiments were conducted using a UHPLC system (1290 Infinity LC, Agilent Technologies) connected to a QTRAP MS (6500+, Sciex) at Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd. Analytes underwent separation via hydrophilic interaction chromatography (HILIC) on a Waters UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) and C18 columns (Waters UPLC BEH C18-2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm). For the HILIC procedure, the column temperature was maintained at 35 ℃ with an injection volume of 2 µL. The composition of mobile phase A included 90% H2O, 2 mM ammonium formate, and 10% acetonitrile, while mobile phase B contained 0.4% formic acid in acetonitrile. A gradient program was applied starting with 85% B for the first minute, adjusted to 80% B during minutes 3 to 4, 70% B at 6 min, and reduced to 50% B from 10 to 15.5 min, returning to 85% B from 15.6 to 23 min, all at a flow rate of 300 µL/min. For reversed phase liquid chromatographic separation, the column temperature was set at 40 ℃, with an injection volume of 2 µL. Mobile phase A consisted of 5 mM ammonium acetate in water, and mobile phase B was 99.5% acetonitrile. The gradient protocol began with 5% B at 0 min, increasing to 60% B at 5 min, peaking at 100% B between 11 and 13 min, then reverting to 5% B from 13.1 to 16 min, all at a flow rate of 400 µL/min. Samples were kept at 4 ℃ throughout the analysis. Mass spectrometry data were captured using a 6500+ QTRAP (AB SCIEX) in both positive and negative ion modes. The electrospray ionization (ESI) source conditions for the positive mode were set at a source temperature of 580 ℃, with ion source gas1 (GS1) at 45, ion source gas2 (GS2) at 60, curtain gas (CUR) at 35, and an ion spray voltage (IS) of +4500 V. Similar settings were used in the negative mode, with an IS of -4500 V. A multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) method facilitated quantitative data acquisition, and details on MRM ion pairs are provided in the supplementary file. Pooled quality control (QC) samples prepared by pooling equal volumes from all experimental groups to create a representative mixture were included in the sequence to assess the system’s stability and repeatability.

For lipidomics, the examination utilized a UHPLC system (LC-30AD, Shimadzu) connected to a QTRAP MS (6500+, Sciex). Analytes underwent separation employing HILIC (Phenomenex, Luna NH2, 2.0 mm × 100 mm, 3 μm) and a C18 column (Phenomenex, Kinetex C18, 2.1 × 100 mm, 2.6 μm). The temperature of the C18 column was maintained at 45 °C, employing a mobile phase A consisting of 70% acetonitrile, 30% H2O, and 5 mM ammonium acetate, and a mobile phase B of IPA solution. A gradient protocol was applied, starting with 20% B, increasing to 60% B over 5 min, reaching 100% B at 13 min, and returning to 20% B from 13.1 to 17 min, at a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min. Samples were kept at 10 ℃ throughout the analysis. In the amino separation process, the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, with mobile phase A comprising 2 mM ammonium acetate, 50% methanol, and 50% acetonitrile, and mobile phase B consisting of 2 mM ammonium acetate, 50% acetonitrile, and 50% water. A gradient was initiated at a flow rate of 400 µL/min, starting with 3% B for the first 3 min, escalating to 100% B from 3 to 13 min, maintaining 100% B until 17 min, and reducing to 3% B from 17.1 to 22 min. The 6500+ QTRAP (AB SCIEX) operated in a switching mode between positive and negative. The ESI positive conditions were set with a source temperature of 400℃, GS1 at 50, GS2 at 55, CUR at 35, and an IS of +3000 V. Correspondingly, the ESI negative conditions were identical except for an IS of -2500 V. Mass spectrometry quantitative data were collected using an MRM method, with ion pairs detailed in an appended file. Pooled QC samples prepared by pooling equal volumes from all experimental groups to create a representative mixture were incorporated into the sample sequence to assess the system’s stability and repeatability.

Multiple QC metrics were employed to ensure data reliability, including total ion chromatogram comparison, principal component analysis of QCs, inter-QC correlation analysis, multivariate control charts, feature relative standard deviation (RSD) values (with most metabolites showing RSD < 30%), and isotopic internal standard stability monitoring. All QC assessments met our predefined acceptance criteria, demonstrating adequate system stability throughout the analysis.

MultiQuant or Analyst was used for metabolomics quantitative data processing. Sciex OS was used for lipidomics quantitative data processing. The QCs were processed together with the biological samples.

Statistical analysis

Characteristics of study participants were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and t-test for numerical variables, and chi-square test for categorical variables, respectively. The processing of the two expression matrix datasets was subsequently conducted as follows: remove metabolites with more missing values (50%); missing data imputation with KNN methods using impute (version = 1.66.0); data filter for keeping metabolites with QC_RSD < 20%; data normalization using Pareto Scaling of IMIFA (version = 2.1.10); PCA analysis using stats (version = 4.1.0); significantly different metabolites identified by t-test with p value < 0.05 using stats. After the differential metabolites were obtained, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler27 (version = 4.0.5) package in conjunction with the KEGGREST (version = 1.36.0) package. Intracellular organelle localization was achieved through comparison with the human metabolome database (HMDB)28. Detailed mitochondrial change pathways were derived by integrating the HMDB, KEGG, and Virtual Metabolic Human databases29. Additionally, receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis was performed using the pROC (version = 1.18.0) package in R. The total lipid concentration represents the sum of all absolutely quantified lipid species measured by lipidomics. P values < 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant.

Results

Metabolomics uncovered significant disparities between both NP-AD and HP-AD versus healthy control population

To ascertain the etiological significance of hypertension in AD, omics analyses were performed on serum from Ctr (n = 14), HP-AD (n = 16), and NP-AD (n = 14). Demographic analysis revealed no significant differences in age or gender between the three cohorts, with metabolic disorder variations primarily associated with hypertension status (Table 1). We employed metabolomics to analyze water-soluble metabolite profiles within three divergent subject cohorts. In total, 251 metabolites were detected in the metabolomics. PCA revealed clear metabolic separations between both AD groups (NP-AD and HP-AD) versus controls, while showing minimal distinction between NP-AD and HP-AD cohorts (Fig. 1A). The comprehensive volcano plot revealed that, in the comparison between NP-AD and Ctr, a total of 69 metabolites were upregulated, whereas 11 metabolites were downregulated in NP-AD. Among these, the subclasses alpha hydroxy acids and derivatives; amino acids, peptides, and analogues; amines; carbohydrates and carbohydrate conjugates; and fatty acids and conjugates encompassed the highest number of altered metabolites. Specifically, the five metabolites with the most pronounced downregulation in NP-AD included beta-hyodeoxycholic acid (β-HDCA), apocholic acid, glycolithocholic acid (GLCA), 2-furoic acid and n-acetylasparagine, while the top five substances exhibiting the most significant upregulation in NP-AD were 3-hydroxybutyric acid, hydroxypyruvic acid, citric acid, cis-aconitic acid and alpha-hydroxyisobutyric acid (Fig. 1B, Table S3). In the comparative analysis between HP-AD and Ctr, both upregulation of 110 metabolites and downregulation of 9 metabolites in HP-AD were documented, exhibiting a high degree of similarity to prior assessments. The metabolic alterations showed consistent patterns across categories, differing only in the complete absence of alpha hydroxy acids and derivatives and the unique presence of purine-related disturbances. Moreover, the pattern of significant metabolic shifts remained consistent, with identical increases from HP-AD in metabolites 3-hydroxybutyric acid and hydroxypyruvic acid, supplemented by theophylline, caffeine, and glucosamine, and parallel decreases in HP-AD for metabolites 2-furoic acid and β-HDCA, with deviations extending to metabolites threonine, uridine and glycolithocholic acid-3-sulfate (Fig. 1C, Table S3). NP-AD and HP-AD comparisons showed distinct metabolite profiles, with only 13 metabolites decreased in NP-AD. The top differential categories included purines and purine derivatives, fatty acids and conjugates, among others, among which the five metabolites with the most significant alterations from NP-AD were theophylline, caffeine, indoleacetic acid, 2-hydroxyphenethylamine and 11z,14z,17z-eicosatrienoic acid (Fig. 1D, Table S3). In conclusion, metabolomics revealed significant concordance between HP-AD and NP-AD, with both groups showing distinct profiles from Ctr. These findings suggest that the observed metabolic perturbations in AD may occur independently of hypertension status, though further studies with hypertensive controls are needed to fully elucidate hypertension’s role in AD pathogenesis.

Mitochondrial metabolism was a pivotal determinant in AD etiology

The metabolic similarity between NP-AD and HP-AD suggested limited hypertension effects in AD pathogenesis, while their shared differences from controls revealed new disease mechanisms. Comparative metabolomic analysis of two hypertensive cohorts versus healthy controls revealed 72 intersecting differential metabolites (IDM) via Venn analysis (Fig. 2A). Regression of log2-transformed IDM fold changes identified consistent dysregulation patterns across AD types: 66 metabolites were upregulated and 6 downregulated in both groups (Fig. 2B). Notably, amines; amino acids, peptides, and analogues; carbohydrates and carbohydrate conjugates; beta hydroxy acids and derivatives; and fatty acids and conjugates emerged as subclasses with significant shifts. Predominantly, the metabolites exhibiting the largest increases in both AD types were identified as 3-hydroxybutyric acid, hydroxypyruvic acid, among others, and those with the most significant decreases in both different AD types were classified as β-HDCA, 2-Furoic acid, among others. KEGG enrichment analysis of IDM revealed that the top 10 pathways - including the TCA cycle and glycine/serine/threonine metabolism - were predominantly mitochondrial-related (Fig. 2C, Table S4). This conserved metabolic signature was observed across both AD types. The subcellular localization analysis of IDM revealed a broad distribution of metabolic alterations across cellular compartments. While the widespread changes in cytosolic and extracellular metabolites were expected, the striking predominance of mitochondrial disturbances was particularly notable - with mitochondria ranking as the top organelle displaying differential IDM accumulation with 9 upregulated and 2 downregulated metabolites in both AD conditions (Fig. 2D, Table S3). This mitochondrial signature was followed by peroxisomes (7 IDM), endoplasmic reticulum (4 IDM), lysosomes (3 IDM), and nucleus (2 IDM) (Table S3). The selective vulnerability of mitochondria suggests a predominant modulatory role in AD pathogenesis, as in comparison to the metabolite alterations observed in other organelles.

Acetylcarnitine emerged as a crucial biomarker for AD

To investigate biomarkers involved in the pathogenesis of AD, scatter plots of the area under the ROC curve (AUC) values derived from each IDM were constructed across two comparisons. Using an AUC threshold of 0.9, we identified six candidate biomarkers common to both AD subtypes: glucosamine, alpha-hydroxyisobutyric acid, 2-hydroxybutyric acid, sebacic acid, N-acetylputrescine, and acetylcarnitine (Fig. 2E). ROC analysis of acetylcarnitine demonstrated strong discriminative power (NP-AD vs. Ctr: AUC = 0.934; HP-AD vs. Ctr: AUC = 0.906), supporting its biomarker potential (Fig. 2F, Table S5). Metabolomic analysis revealed distinct acetylcarnitine concentrations across cohorts (Fig. 2G). Acetylcarnitine levels were significantly elevated in both NP-AD and HP-AD versus controls, but did not differ between AD subtypes. These findings establish acetylcarnitine as a promising early AD biomarker, indicative of mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction.

Lipidomics elucidated lipid metabolism distinctions among NP-AD, HP-AD and healthy control population

Carnitine plays a crucial role in fatty acid transport and energy metabolism30. Accumulation of the derivative acetylcarnitine, along with the accumulation of cis-aconitic acid and citric acid in the TCA cycle, suggested a significant impairment in lipid metabolism in AD patients. This was further supported by the notable enrichment in the category of fatty acids and conjugates in the IDM analysis (Fig. 2B), and the significant enrichment of KEGG pathways for fatty acid biosynthesis and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids (Fig. 2C). Therefore, we conducted lipidomics with the same samples to further elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

The lipidomics analysis identified a total of 985 lipids. PCA showed overall similarity among Ctr, NP-AD, and HP-AD groups (Fig. 3A). A more detailed analysis revealed distinctions among the three groups at the individual lipid level. In the comparative lipidomic analysis between NP-AD and Ctr, volcano plot visualizations elucidated an upregulation of 16 lipids and a downregulation of 112 lipids in NP-AD (Fig. 3B, Table S6). The five most significantly altered lipid classes were fatty acid (FA), lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC), phosphatidylcholine (PC), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), and triacylglycerol (TG). Notably, FA species, particularly FA(16:1), FA(20:1), FA(22:5), FA(20:2) and FA(22:6), demonstrated the most significant upregulation in NP-AD, whereas TG compounds including TG(54:8)-FA18:3, TG(54:8)-FA18:2, TG(50:5)-FA14:0, TG(48:4)-FA18:2 and TG(54:7)-FA18:3 showed marked downregulation. Upon comparison between HP-AD and Ctr, a distinct pattern was observed in lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE) levels: while an ensemble of 116 lipids demonstrated upregulation, LPE(20:5) exhibited significant downregulation (Fig. 3C, Table S6) in HP-AD. Among these, the lipid classes cholesteryl ester (ChE), FA, PC, PE, and phosphatidylinositol (PI) were identified with the most pronounced elevations from HP-AD, with FA(20:1), FA(22:5), TG(48:4)-FA16:0, FA(20:2) and TG(48:1)-FA16:1 registering the most substantial increase in HP-AD. Comparative lipidomic profiling between NP-AD and HP-AD revealed significant dysregulation, with 200 lipid species demonstrating substantial reductions in NP-AD (Fig. 3D, Table S6). ChE, diacylglycerol (DG), PC, PE and TG were identified as the most significantly reduced lipid classes in NP-AD. Notably, TG(54:8)-FA18:3, TG(50:5)-FA14:0, TG(54:8)-FA18:2, MG(18:3), and TG(50:5)-FA18:3 exhibited the most pronounced depletion in NP-AD.

Fatty acid family was identified as key altered lipids in AD pathogenesis

To identify conserved lipid perturbations across AD subtypes, we conducted systematic lipid class profiling among the three experimental cohorts. Comparative analysis revealed FA as the only lipid class demonstrating consistent dysregulation in both NP-AD and HP-AD compared to controls, while no significant intergroup differences were observed between NP-AD and HP-AD (Fig. 4A). Specifically, FA levels from lipidomics were elevated by 1.25-fold in NP-AD and 1.36-fold in HP-AD (Fig. 4B). To better understand potential metabolic influences on these FA alterations, we performed additional correlation analyses. While BMI showed a statistically significant association with FA levels (r = 0.52, p = 0.003), the relationship with systolic blood pressure was less pronounced (r = 0.36, p = 0.05). These observations suggest that multiple metabolic factors may contribute to the FA changes observed in AD. Subsequent analysis revealed a discernible trend towards differences in the NP-AD vs. Ctr and HP-AD vs. Ctr comparisons, with a mere 16 lipids showing conserved dysregulation patterns, representing proportions of approximately 12.5% and 13.7% in each respective comparison (Fig. 4C). Regression analyses confirmed consistent upregulation of all 16 lipids including PA, PC and PE classes in both AD subtypes (Fig. 4D). FA displayed the strongest conserved dysregulation across AD subtypes, with 16 lipids exhibiting coordinated increases, suggesting central roles in AD progression. These data demonstrated that elevated FA lipids, rather than hypertension, were a more significant factor in the pathogenesis of AD.

Differential alterations in sphingolipid signaling pathway were found between NP-AD and HP-AD

Lipidomic profiling revealed significant separation between AD groups and controls from lipid classes with distinct clustering of NP-AD versus HP-AD subtypes, demonstrating both shared and subtype-specific lipid perturbations. Lipidomic analysis revealed significantly elevated levels of eleven lipid classes in HP-AD compared to NP-AD, including ceramide (Cer), DG, dihydroceramide (DHCer), and LPC (Fig. 5A). KEGG analysis identified the sphingolipid signaling pathway as most significantly altered between NP-AD and HP-AD, with NP-AD showing reduced levels of Cer, DG, DHCer, and sphingomyelin (SM) (Fig. 5B, Table S7). Collectively, hypertension induces significant alterations in AD-associated lipid metabolism, with the sphingolipid signaling pathway showing the most prominent dysregulation.

Discussion

AD represents a critical challenge in cardiovascular medicine, where current diagnostic reliance on imaging techniques faces limitations in early detection and mechanistic insight31. As shown in Fig. 6, our integrated metabolomic and lipidomic analysis reveals profound metabolic dysregulation underlying AD pathogenesis, providing both diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. The observed mitochondrial metabolic disturbances substantiate and extend previous reports of mitochondrial dysfunction in vascular diseases32–34. Notably, the accumulation of acetylcarnitine—a key mediator of fatty acid oxidation - suggests a previously underappreciated metabolic vulnerability in AD pathogenesis24,35,36. This finding builds upon emerging evidence of metabolic disturbances in cardiovascular diseases, while specifically implicating impaired mitochondrial energetics as a central feature of AD, distinct from other vascular pathologies like atherosclerosis where acetylcarnitine may play protective roles37,38.

Graphical summary: Highlights of perturbed fatty acid metabolism in aortic dissection independent of hypertension.

Our integrated metabolomic and lipidomic approach, conducted on the same set of clinical samples, provided a comprehensive profiling of metabolic alterations in AD. This parallel analysis enabled direct correlation and reciprocal validation between the two omics datasets—where metabolomics identified broad disturbances in fatty acid metabolism, while lipidomics offered molecular specificity by characterizing detailed lipid species alterations. Specifically, our integrated study reveals profound dysregulation of FA metabolism in AD, with selective accumulation of FA(16:1), FA(20:1), FA(22:5), and FA(20:2) that corroborates previous reports of lipid imbalances in AD39–41. These FA alterations likely contribute to AD pathogenesis through multiple mechanisms, including serving as precursors for pro-inflammatory lipid mediators41, inducing endothelial dysfunction via oxidative stress pathways32, and altering vascular smooth muscle cell membrane properties34 The observed metabolic disturbances are particularly pronounced in mitochondrial energetics, with acetylcarnitine accumulation suggesting impaired fatty acid oxidation24,35,36, consistent with but extending beyond prior observations of mitochondrial dysfunction in vascular diseases.

The observed alterations in lipidomic profiles in NP-AD patients, characterized by decreased levels of specific LPC, TG, PE, and PC species, likely represent a complex metabolic adaptation to disease pathophysiology. Current evidence suggests these changes exhibit distinct biological effects depending on molecular species and metabolic context. While elevated LPC levels are known to promote pro-inflammatory responses through TLR4/NF-κB activation, certain LPC species demonstrate anti-inflammatory properties via G2A receptor signaling42. The reduced TG levels parallel experimental models showing that excessive myocardial TG accumulation induces lipotoxicity through diacylglycerol-mediated pathways43, suggesting a potential compensatory mechanism. Similarly, the decreased PC/PE ratio mirrors findings using a mouse model where altered hepatic phospholipid composition affected lipoprotein metabolism44, while epidemiological studies associate specific PE species with plaque stability45. These coordinated lipid modifications appear to represent an integrated metabolic response to maintain cellular homeostasis under chronic metabolic stress, though their precise functional consequences warrant further mechanistic investigation.

The hypertension-dependent activation of sphingolipid signaling pathways represents a key mechanistic insight. Our data demonstrate significant elevation of ceramide and dihydroceramide species in hypertensive AD patients, building upon fundamental work in sphingolipid biology46,47 while providing AD-specific signatures. These changes likely mediate vascular injury through smooth muscle cell apoptosis48 and endothelial barrier disruption49, offering a molecular explanation for the clinical variability50. The sphingolipid signature’s specificity to hypertensive patients suggests it may represent the missing link between chronic hemodynamic stress and acute aortic injury, addressing a key gap in current understanding31. Further investigation will confirm these observations.

Our integrated multi-omics approach provides transformative insights into AD pathogenesis while revealing important limitations and future directions. The study’s most significant strength lies in its ability to simultaneously capture mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction and hypertension-specific lipid disturbances—two interconnected features of AD that have not been comprehensively studied in relation to each other. While the sample size in this study has certain limitations, it remains sufficient to support our primary hypothesis regarding metabolic disturbances in AD. The identified biomarkers demonstrate potential clinical utility for both diagnostic applications and therapeutic targeting. The acetylcarnitine accumulation we identified not only serves as a potential diagnostic biomarker but also points to fundamental defects in energy metabolism that could be therapeutically targeted. Similarly, the sphingolipid signature in hypertensive patients offers both prognostic value and opportunities for pharmacological intervention using existing lipid-modifying agents. However, these findings must be interpreted considering several limitations, including the cross-sectional design which cannot establish causality (particularly regarding BMI and lipid interactions), the need for validation in larger cohorts and animal models, and the sample size constraints affecting subgroup analyses. Additionally, our cohort had limited female representation (< 35% in all groups), which may affect the generalizability of these findings. This is a particularly important consideration given the established sex-based differences in the presentation and management of AD51. The complex relationship between acylcarnitines and cardiovascular outcomes observed across different studies—often influenced by BMI—also suggests that the diagnostic and therapeutic potential of our findings will require careful validation in more diverse populations, particularly with balanced sex distribution.

Looking ahead, several critical research avenues emerge from this work. Mechanistic studies should investigate whether pharmacologically modulating the identified metabolic pathways can prevent AD progression in experimental models. The development of rapid clinical assays for acetylcarnitine and sphingolipid measurements could enable earlier diagnosis and more precise risk stratification. Longitudinal studies tracking these metabolic changes in at-risk populations will be essential to determine their predictive value. Importantly, future work should explore how these metabolic insights can be integrated with existing diagnostic and therapeutic approaches to develop comprehensive management strategies. The true translational potential of this research lies in its ability to bridge fundamental metabolic discoveries with clinical applications. By demonstrating that AD involves characteristic and potentially modifiable metabolic disturbances, our findings pave the way for shifting the management paradigm from emergency intervention to targeted prevention. This multi-omics perspective not only advances our understanding of AD but also provides a framework for studying other complex cardiovascular disorders where metabolic dysregulation may play an underappreciated role.
