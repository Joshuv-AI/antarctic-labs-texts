Knowledge and Attitudes Toward Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder Among Physicians and Senior Medical Students in Jordan [Letter]
Authors: Sara Majeed, Shakib Hussain Chowdhury, Maleeha Pandit
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We read with great interest the article by Al-Ghawanmeh et al (2026), titled “Knowledge and Attitudes Toward Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder Among Physicians and Senior Medical Students in Jordan.”1 While prior work from Jordan, such as Al-Habashneh et al, looked at knowledge among students alone, this study fills a genuine gap in the regional literature: it is the first to combine physicians and senior students in a single national sample and examines attitudes alongside knowledge rather than knowledge in isolation.2 The sample size (n=553) comfortably exceeds the authors’ own power calculation, and the use of multivariable ordinal regression to separately model predictors for students and physicians is a methodologically ambitious choice. The resulting finding that knowledge and attitude, while statistically associated, are not predicted by the same experiential factors is a genuinely interesting and clinically relevant observation. That being said, several concerns related to statistical reporting, interpretive alignment between the study’s central claim and its own data, and sampling and theoretical considerations limit how far these conclusions can be pushed.

The authors state that knowledge scores were organized into “poor, fair, and good levels based on predefined cutoffs,” but never explicitly state the thresholds for these values. Moreover, no rationale is offered for the net (+1/-1/0) scoring system, which effectively treats a confidently wrong answer the same as an uncertain one. Readers are unable to independently check the reported 55.2%/37.6%/7.2% distribution without this information. A larger issue sits in the student regression model. The authors report that the test of parallel lines was significant (x2(4) = 20.73, p < 0.001), while also noting that the proportional odds assumption was violated. Despite this, they still interpret a single adjusted odds ratio per predictor, adding an offhand “estimates should be interpreted cautiously.”1 Once this assumption fails, the standard fix is typically a partial proportional odds or generalized ordinal model, which relaxes the constraint only where it is violated, not a caveated proportional odds estimate.3 Several of the headline findings also sit on thin ice statistically. The pediatric-rotation result (OR = 3.37, 95% CI: 1.08–10.56) comes from a subgroup with just two “poor” knowledge students among 23 currently in rotation, and the parenthood finding among physicians (OR = 3.22, 95% CI: 1.00–10.36) is built on an equally small parental subgroup. Both multivariable models were also built by entering variables significant at p < 0.05 on bivariate chi-square testing, a stepwise-style approach known to distort coefficient estimates and standard errors, with no sensitivity analysis to check whether a pre-determined, theory-driven covariate set would alter the overall picture.4

The paper’s central claim is that a “knowledge-attitude gap” exists because factual knowledge does not reliably translate into positive attitudes. The authors report a strong, highly significant global association between knowledge level and attitude (x2 = 18.29, p < 0.001). We believe the data support a narrower claim than this. What the multivariable models show is something more specific: the experiential factors that predict knowledge (pediatric rotation, personal ADHD experience, parenthood, workplace) are not the same factors that predict attitude. That is a difference in which predictors were captured, not proof that knowledge fails to shape attitude. A simpler explanation is that both outcomes share drivers that the study did not measure, such as specific curricular content, mentorship quality, informal clinical culture, that the more abstract experiential proxies used here could not pick up. The paper’s title conclusion leans heavily on this divergence in predictors, without fully reconciling it against the study’s own knowledge-attitude correlation, and we believe the disconnect is somewhat overstated as a result.

The authors acknowledge convenience recruitment through overlapping social media and professional channels as a limitation, but its consequences for the attitude findings specifically deserve more attention than they were given. Furthermore, while an exact response rate may be unobtainable in open web recruitment, the authors omitted basic survey funnel metrics such as unique site visits, completion ratios, or anti-duplicate safeguards recommended by standard reporting frameworks like the CHERRIES guidelines.5 Reporting these proxy metrics is essential for readers to evaluate the extent of self-selection bias. Non-probability “river” sampling of this kind is known to produce self-selection; that is, people with strong views on a survey’s subject matter are more likely to respond than those with mild or indifferent views.6 Attitude, unlike factual knowledge, is exactly the kind of construct this bias distorts, so the reported 52.8%/47.2% split will not hold even within Jordan, and the true prevalence of negative attitudes could run higher or lower than reported. There is also a theoretical gap worth naming. The discussion reaches for stigma and the “hidden curriculum” only after the fact, to explain the knowledge-attitude gap post sampling, but the study was never designed around a theoretical model of how attitudes form. The theory of planned behavior, which separates attitude into beliefs, subjective norms, and perceived control, has been combined with stigma theory in comparable health settings and would have let the attitude items map onto distinct, testable constructs rather than one composite score.7 Without that structure, it is hard to say which part of “attitude” is actually driving the gap, which in turn limits how specific the paper’s call for “stigma-focused educational strategies” can be. This construct ambiguity is further compounded by selective item interpretation. For instance, the authors interpret agreement with parental medication reluctance as an informed clinical awareness of real-world barriers, yet take items regarding ADHD as a “fashionable diagnosis” at face value as unprompted stigma. Single closed-ended survey items cannot distinguish informed clinical judgment from bias without qualitative follow-up or cognitive interviewing to verify participant intent.

Al-Ghawanmeh et al have made a timely and well-powered contribution to understanding how ADHD knowledge and professional attitudes relate to one another in Jordan, and the study’s central question is one the field genuinely needs answered. Our concerns are about how far the current analysis can support that answer: the scoring methodology is not transparent, the violated proportional odds assumption was flagged but not corrected, the leap from divergent predictor sets to a knowledge-attitude gap conclusion outruns what the data show, and the attitude construct itself was never anchored to a theoretical model. Reanalysis with a partial proportional odds model, full disclosure of the scoring thresholds, and future work built around an established attitude-formation framework would go a long way toward strengthening these conclusions and helping medical educators design interventions that actually close the gap the authors describe.
