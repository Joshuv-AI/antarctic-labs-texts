Abstract
. As wearable technologies continue to evolve—becoming smaller, more powerful, and more deeply embedded in daily life—their integration into diverse user contexts raises critical design challenges. There remains a notable gap in large-scale empirical data on where users actually wear or carry these devices throughout the day, systematically examining user preferences for wearable placement across varied contexts and routines. In this work, we conducted a questionnaire in several countries aimed at capturing real-world habits related to wearable device placement. The results from n = 320 n=320 participants reveal how wearable usage patterns shift depending on time of day and context. We propose a set of practical, user-centered guidelines for sensor placement and discuss how they align or diverge from assumptions seen in existing ISWC work. This study contributes to ongoing efforts within the community to design more inclusive, adaptable, and context-aware wearable systems.



1.
Introduction

Wearable technology has become increasingly ubiquitous, offering features supporting health monitoring, fitness tracking, and communication. Continued advancements in miniaturization and computing-power driven by trends such as Moore’s Law
(
Moore 1965
)
, are enabling the creation of devices that are smaller, more powerful, and more seamlessly integrated into daily life
(
Culhane et al. 2005
;
Sun et al. 2018
)
. However, the success of these technologies hinges not only on functionality but also on how individuals choose to integrate them into their lived experiences.
While considerable Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) research focuses on device features and application development (e.g.,
(
Bentvelzen et al. 2023
;
Consolvo et al. 2008
)
), a comprehensive understanding of how individuals integrate wearables into their daily lives, including their preferred wearing locations, their potential motivations for those choices, perceived accompanying usability implications, and how cultural norms and individual preferences influence these choices, remains limited.

Individuals exhibit diverse perceptions of their bodies, influencing how they adopt and use wearable technology, and vice versa (e.g.,
(
Elsayed et al. 2020
;
Spelmezan et al. 2009
;
Turmo Vidal et al. 2024a
;
Turmo Vidal et al. 2024b
;
D’Adamo et al. 2024
)
).
Where users wear their technologies on their bodies are shaped by social norms (e.g., fashion trends), environmental constraints (e.g., climate), personal preferences, age, and body shape (e.g.,
(
Del Din et al. 2016
)
). Although
Zeagler 2017
provided a set of guidelines for the positioning of on-body wearable through literature, there is a lack of empirical HCI knowledge on cross-cultural preferences, hindering the development of user-centered wearable designs that are comfortable, effective, socially acceptable, and adaptable to diverse contexts.
Consequently, there is a discernible disconnection between the requirements of inclusive design practices and the state-of-the-art wearable devices. In a field where each millimeter and gram counts in making wearables as comfortable as possible, designing wearables for a multitude of wearing locations can affect the device’s usability.

Accurate on-body localization of wearable devices remains a persistent challenge for algorithmic models, particularly in the domain of Human Activity Recognition (HAR)
(
Del Din et al. 2016
)
. Although deep learning approaches have shown promising performance when utilizing data from a single or limited number of devices
(
Fortes Rey et al. 2022
;
Lago et al. 2021
;
Yang et al. 2022
)
, their effectiveness is highly dependent on sensor placement
(
Del Din et al. 2016
;
Suh et al. 2023
)
.
Consequently, there is a need to understand contextual wearable habits among users to provide tailored experiences. To construct such a dataset, we conducted the first culturally-diverse, online questionnaire deployed in multiple countries with the aim to acquire insights from a wide array of wearable users on how they wear their devices and in what contexts. Furthermore, given the challenges posed by domain variability
(
Chen et al. 2020
;
Suh et al. 2023
)
, our survey highlights demographic differences (e.g., age, gender, country of origin and residence, level of influence by parents), thereby supporting the refinement of algorithms for broader applicability in real-world scenarios.
From our findings, we derived a set of guidelines to inform the design of more inclusive and effective wearable technologies.

To this end, we make the following contributions: (1) an empirical survey with
n=320
valid responses conducted in four languages worldwide; (2) insights into key user attributes influencing bodily wearable placement habits; and (3) design considerations for more inclusive designs of future wearable developments.

2.
Related Work

Figure 1.
Flowchart depicting the structure and contents of the questionnaire used in the study.

Existing research has extensively examined mobile phone carrying habits across different cultural contexts.
One large-scale study conducted by
Cui et al. 2007
in the pre-smartphone era, analyzed phone carrying behavior through street interviews across 9 countries with over 1,500 participants. They identified gender and cultural influences, showing that women primarily used bags, while men favored trouser pockets
(
Cui et al. 2007
)
. Additionally, hand-carrying was common in Delhi, Seoul, Jilin, and Los Angeles
(
Cui et al. 2007
)
. However, it is worth noting that since the conclusion of that study, the size of mobile phones have drastically changed with the rise of smartphones. A 2005 survey completed by approximately 600 participants from Poland, examined potential correlations between mobile phone usage intensity and symptoms such as headaches, fatigue, and warmth around the ear
(
Szyjkowska et al. 2014
)
. Findings suggested that frequent and prolonged phone use increases the occurrence of these symptoms
(
Szyjkowska et al. 2014
)
. However, the study did not assess the perceived usefulness of mobile devices, leaving gaps in understanding user experience beyond health effects.

Redmayne 2017
conducted an online questionnaire with fewer than
200
women on their smartphone carrying preferences, revealing that
86\%
carried their phones off-body, while
15\%
stored them against the breast. A complementary study by
Zeleke et al. 2022
investigated mobile phone carrying locations among
356
men aged
18
–
72
, aiming to identify correlations between carrying positions and radiofrequency electromagnetic radiation (RF-EMR) risk perception. These studies contribute to a broader understanding of gender-based differences in device placement and perceived health risks, but do not account for potential behavioral variations across different kinds of wearable technology.

Wearable technology studies further examined smartwatch usage patterns, considering daily routines and influencing factors. A longitudinal analysis of 50 college students required participants to wear smartwatches for over
200
days while tracking daily trends
(
Jeong et al. 2017
)
. Three smartwatch user groups were identified:
work-hour wearers
,
active-hour wearers
, and
all-day wearers

(
Jeong et al. 2017
)
, revealing context-driven smartwatch adoption, offering implications for usability optimization in wearable technology design. Generally, it has also been argued that proxemics (how people perceive and use space, and how that space impacts their communication and social interactions), weight distribution and user movement ability have to be taken into account for on-body wearables
(
Zeagler 2017
;
Gemperle et al. 1998
)
, given the risks of wear and tear to the device (see
(
Stein et al. 1998
)
), user discomfort
(
Sullivan 2016
)
, and movement obstruction
(
Tilley 2001
)
. These concerns highlight the importance of appropriate wearable placement on the body.

Building on prior research, our study expands its scope to investigate a diverse range of wearable devices, including smartphones, smartwatches, and other wearables. To enhance inclusivity, we aim to diversify our participant pool in terms of culture, gender, education, and profession.

3.
Methodology

Our inquiry was guided by two research questions:

•

RQ1:

How are on-body placements for wearable technologies influenced by user attributes?

•

RQ2:

How do contextual factors influence users’ on-body wearable placements?

To answer those questions, we conducted a cross-cultural, online questionnaire. Our study was approved by the [removed for review] ethics board of the primary researcher’s affiliation.

3.1.
Survey Content

Our survey was offered in several languages [languages removed for anonymity] to increase accessibility and to ensure broad geographical coverage across countries. The time to complete the survey ranged from 2 minutes 40 seconds to 27 minutes 19 seconds, with an average duration of 9 minutes 32 seconds. The survey was divided into four sections, as seen in
Figure 1
: (1) smartphone usage; (2) smartwatch usage; (3) other wearable devices, and (4) demographic questions. The first and second sections of the questionnaire had the same structure, differing only in the type of device being addressed. For each device, participants were asked where they typically carry it. For each reported location, they were then asked during which times of the day or night (e.g., at home, outside, while sleeping) they use that placement, as well as how tightly the device fits and how close it sits to the skin. The third section followed the same structure but was designed to repeat automatically, allowing participants to report additional wearable devices as desired. In the forth section, demographic questions are asked.

3.2.
Participants

To reach a broad international audience, we used mailing lists, advertisements in public spaces, and word-of-mouth to recruit users of wearable technology. Participants were compensated by getting a chance to win prizes totaling 200 euros in a raffle.

In total,
n=449
participants were recorded, of which we included
n=320
in our final analysis. Reasons for exclusion included failing to complete the survey or to provide valid answers to mandatory fields.
The gender distribution of our sample consisted of 160 females (50%), 151 males (47%), 3 non-binary (1%), 1 other (less then 1%), and 5 whom preferred not to share (1.56%).
The participants were between the ages of 18 - 80 (
M=31.9
,
SD=12.12
). In our sample, 307 (96%) used smartphones, 98 (31%) smartwatches, 86 (27%) earphones, 18 (6%) headphones, 11 (3%) location trackers, 4 (1%) cardiac monitors, 2 (0.5%) tablets, 2 (0.5%) smart rings, 2 (0.5%) smart glasses, and 1 (0.25%) Coros pod. It is to note that some people use more than one device at time.

Besides basic demographic information, we also asked participants about their background, like highest achieved education, handedness, country of residence, and occupation, to collect insights into whether contextual and environmental factors influenced their wearable use.
Our sample had the following education levels: 21 (7%) practical, 61 (19%) high school, 103 (32%) bachelors, 108 (34%) masters, and 27 (8%) PhDs.
Participants originated from, i.e., Germany, Poland, Rwanda, the United States, India, Egypt, France, Japan, Australia, China, Sweden, and Indonesia.

3.3.
Analysis

To identify associations between demographic characteristics and wearable device usage patterns, we employed an automated exploratory data analysis approach. Given the large number of variables included in our survey, a systematic evaluation of all possible pairwise combinations was conducted.

3.3.1.
Variable Selection

Our independent and dependent variables (IVs, DVs) were selected from ‘Device’, ‘Age’, ‘Gender’, ‘Education Level’, ‘Work Status’, ‘Country of Origin’, ‘Country or Residence’, ‘Parental Education Group’, ‘Friend Group Influence’, ‘Environment Score’, ‘Place’ of wear, ‘Time’ of use, ‘Tightness’ preference, and ‘Clothes’ worn during usage.

3.3.2.
Combination Generation

All unique combinations of one, two and three IVs, and DVs were generated. This resulted in a total of 5655 distinct combinations of combinations for analysis (assumming
C^{i}_{I}
is number of combinations of independent variables, and
C^{i}_{D}
is a number of combinations of dependent variables:
Sets_{1}=C^{1}_{I}*C^{1}_{D}
,
Sets_{2}=C^{2}_{I}*C^{1}_{D}
,
Sets_{3}=C^{3}_{I}*C^{1}_{D}
which in total gives:
Sets=C^{1}_{I}*C^{1}_{D}+C^{2}_{I}*C^{1}_{D}+C^{2}_{I}*C^{1}_{D}
).
Combinations with more than three IVs were also evaluated but did not yield any statistical significance.

3.3.3.
Data Preprocessing & Filtering

Prior to testing, categorical variables were consolidated where appropriate to improve statistical power and interpretability. For several IVs, we filtered out conditions that had a lack of responses, to ensure generalizability of our findings. For instance, for the ‘Device’ IV, we solely included smartphones, smartwatches, and headphones. We decided to create a combined category of earphones and headphones due to their similarities in functionality and usage.
For the IV ‘Gender’, only data from participants identifying as male or female were included.

3.3.4.
Statistical Analysis

The association between each IV and DV pair was assessed using a Chi-Square Test of Independence (
\chi^{2}
). Contingency tables were constructed to summarize observed frequencies across all combinations of categorical values.
To ensure the validity of the test results, we evaluated expected cell frequencies within each contingency table. Cells with an expected frequency less than 5 were considered potentially problematic. If more than 10% of cells had an expected frequency below this threshold (min. expected threshold = 5), the corresponding chi-squared test result was excluded from further consideration.
A p-value threshold of 0.001 was applied to identify statistically significant associations.

Our statistical analysis revealed that most demographic factors, including age, education, work background, country of origin and residence, and social influence (parents, friends, environment), significantly affect what type of wearable device people tend to use, where they carry their device, and usage time. Though, our results suggest that gender solely influences where devices are kept.

Age Differences:

Out of all the wearable device options, smartphones are the most used wearable device across all age groups and genders. However, younger users (under 28 years old) show a stronger preference for earphones compared to older individuals. Older individuals use smartwatches more frequently than other age groups. We also observed that age influenced the device placement: younger people frequently carry phones in their hand, while older users—especially women—prefer bags or purses as storage places. Smartwatches are typically worn on the left wrist, though younger users show more variability.

Handedness, Clothes and Furniture:

Regarding smartwatches, there is relatively little variation in storage or placement throughout the day. The vast majority of participants wear the device on their left wrist, likely due to right-handed dominance.
The data also suggest that users have a preference for backpacks, waist-level jacket pockets, front pants pockets, purses or back pants pockets to store their smartwatches. The small subgroup size precludes broader generalizations.

Similar conditions apply to wearing habits for cardiac monitors, wherein the trend is as follows: Users initially prefer to wear it on the left wrist, then furniture (like putting the wearable on a table, etc.), and lastly on their body.

Tablets were typically reported to be kept in waist-level pockets or backpacks. Unsurprisingly, users reported wearing smart glasses on their head, and otherwise in a backpack or a purse.
Smart rings were worn on the hand, in front pants pockets or in a backpack. Only one person reported the usage of a coros pod and keeping it on the furniture.

Gender Differences Throughout the Day:

One of the main recurring themes in the data was that gender seemingly influences how users carry their wearables on their body, as well as how placement changes throughout the day depending on context.

Smartphones:

Patterns for smartphone usage are presented in
Figure 2
. Throughout the day, men mostly store their smartphones in their front pants pocket, while women prefer the back pants pocket or a waist-level jacket pocket. At the same time, both genders also have their phones in their hand throughout the day. Though, this hand-held behavior appears to be driven primarily by younger users, as noted earlier, and may not be as prevalent among participants over the age of 28. A similar trend persists when considering the broader category of “not in bed” (which is all the time a person is not spending in bed), where men still primarily use the front pants pocket. However, women show a more balanced distribution across multiple locations, suggesting greater variability in placement habits.

During working hours, this gender divergence becomes more pronounced. Men maintain the habit of storing their phone in the front pants pocket, while women often place it in a purse or backpack. The distinction continues during physical activity: men again favor the front pants pocket, followed by holding the phone in hand or placing it in a jacket pocket. Women, on the other hand, more frequently store their phones in backpacks or purses, although hand and jacket pockets are also used to a lesser extent.

At night, while in bed, both genders commonly hold the smartphone in their hands.

Earphones:

Patterns of earphone use show more variability than smartwatches, particularly in terms of storage when the device is not in use (
Figure 3
). Most participants report using earphones actively during the day and while engaging in sports, with work hours often overlapping with these usage patterns. When not in use, earphones are most commonly stored in backpacks, regardless of gender. Generally, men tend to use front pants pockets, while women prefer waist-level jacket pockets. For earphones, backpacks are the most common storage, but gendered-preferences like purses for women and pants pockets for men still appear.

5.
Discussion

We propose several design guidelines for on-body wearables based on our key findings. In addition, we situate our research within prior work on data collection and algorithm application.

5.1.
Design Considerations

Design Consideration 1: No uniform device placement across users

Our findings show that demographic factors, such as gender, age, education, and cultural influences, significantly shape how and where users carry wearable devices. For example, men predominantly use front pants pockets, while older users demonstrate more conservative and consistent placement patterns (
RQ1
).

As public datasets often overrepresent young, male, STEM-affiliated participants
(
for Science and NCSES
)
, overreliance on these datasets introduces biases when developing wearables or training HAR models. However, collecting new data is also costly and time-consuming
Liu et al. 2018
;
Reiss and Stricker 2012
. These limitations may lead to unrealistic assumptions.

For instance,
Leng et al. 2024
developed an emotion recognition system using front pants pocket sensors—an impractical location for many women.
Liu et al. 2022
, in food intake monitoring system, targeted older adults but did not include them in testing, and placed devices under the breast, which may be uncomfortable for some women. Other systems, such as chest-worn panic detectors
(
Rubin et al. 2015
)
or headphone-based activity monitors
(
Yatani and Truong 2012
)
, face compatibility issues with user behavior and anatomy.

To ensure real-world relevance, we recommend that future wearable designs
consider diverse carrying preferences
. Avoid defaulting to male-centric placements, and instead design for flexibility and inclusivity across genders and age groups.

Design Consideration 2: Smartphone availability during sports and throughout the day

Our findings show that smartphone placement varies significantly by gender and context. While men often use front pants or jacket pockets, women frequently store phones in purses or backpacks (
RQ1
). During physical activity or certain daily routines, users may not carry their phones at all, leading to gaps in sensing coverage (
RQ2
).This variability can hinder systems that assume consistent on-body smartphone availability.

Other examples include IMU sensors placed in front pants pockets
(
Leng et al. 2024
)
—an approach unsuitable for many users.
Bian et al. 2024
propose custom earables and smartwatches to reduce smartphone dependence, though adoption may be limited due to comfort and hardware constraints. Similarly, while shoe-mounted sensors
(
Hardegger et al. 2014
)
improve motion capture during sports, they are rarely used outside those contexts. As
Mirtchouk et al. 2016
note, accuracy gains from multi-sensor setups often come at the expense of wearability.

We recommend designing systems that
do not assume smartphones are always on-body
. Incorporating complementary wearables or fallback strategies in case of data gaps can improve sensing reliability across diverse real-world scenarios.

Design Consideration 3: Align with users’ dominant wrist preferences

We found that most users wear smartwatches on their non-dominant wrist (typically the left), a consistent behavior across age and gender groups driven by comfort, ease of use, and minimal interference with daily activities (
RQ1
). Systems that require wear on the dominant wrist can disrupt these habits, potentially lowering adoption, increasing discomfort, or degrading interaction.

An example is provided by
Scholl et al. 2015
, wherein a lab-based system required the smartwatch to be on the dominant wrist. Similarly,
Villa et al. 2021
presents a haptic feedback system with a custom wrist device that may face adoption challenges unless it integrates with users’ existing smartwatch setups. Our findings reinforce that users have established wrist preferences, and disregarding them can conflict with existing routines. While some systems, like the energy-aware design developed by
Korpela et al. 2015
, are more adaptable to common smartwatch use, they do not always explicitly consider wrist dominance. Emotion tracking systems (e.g.,
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
) illustrate how using standard form factors and locations supports comfort and long-term use.

To promote adoption,
wrist-based systems should respect dominant wrist-wearing habits
, offer wrist-agnostic designs, or ensure compatibility with prevailing smartwatch ecosystems.

Design Consideration 4: Evolving user preferences driven by fashion and technology trends

User behavior regarding wearable device placement is not static; it evolves alongside broader changes in fashion, lifestyle, and technological development (
RQ2
). Our findings indicate that while older users tend to exhibit consistent, conservative placement habits, younger users are more likely to experiment and adapt based on current trends (
RQ1
).

For instance, comparing our results to earlier studies reveals significant shifts. In 2007,
Cui et al. 2007
reported that 14% of men used a belt clip to carry their phone, whereas in our study, only 1.9% reported doing so. Similarly, 11.5% of women in 2007 carried their phones in hand or around the neck—today, 40% report carrying phones in hand, and none around the neck. Device placement in skirts, once reported by 16.5% of women, is now non-existent in our study. Some patterns remain relatively stable. For example, in both our study and the one by
Zeleke et al. 2022
, men frequently reported using front pants pockets while at work, outside, or in vehicles. However, even these patterns are subject to change with shifting device form factors, clothing styles, and cultural expectations.

Therefore, designers should avoid relying solely on historical norms or current standards. Instead, they must consider how emerging trends and generational differences may influence user behavior over time.
Wearables should be designed with flexibility and adaptability in mind
, ensuring long-term usability across diverse and evolving contexts.

Design Consideration 5: Build benchmarks that reflect real-world device placements

Benchmarks and datasets used in wearable sensing research—especially in applications like HAR—often rely on fixed or researcher-assigned device placements (e.g., front pants pocket or wrist). While this simplifies data collection and model development, it fails to account for the wide variation in how people actually carry devices in everyday life. As our findings show, factors such as gender, age, fashion, and comfort significantly influence on-body device placement (
RQ1
,
RQ2
).

This mismatch between controlled benchmarks and real-world behavior can lead to reduced performance or generalizability when algorithms are deployed outside lab settings. Fortunately, some existing benchmarks have already moved toward more ecologically valid practices. For instance,
Sztyler and Stuckenschmidt 2016
and
Fujinami 2016
have proposed datasets that include multiple on-body positions, better reflecting users’ natural habits.

To support fairer and more effective model evaluation, we recommend that
future benchmarks prioritize capturing these real-world variations
. This includes allowing participants to place devices where they typically would and annotating those locations. Benchmarks should also strive for demographic diversity to capture the full spectrum of carrying habits. Doing so will lead to more inclusive, realistic, and robust wearable sensing systems.

5.2.
Limitations

Although our study methodology allowed us to collect data on a larger scale, we could not follow up on participants’ initial answers to inquire further about the contexts in which they used their wearables. We see our work as a stepping stone towards an understanding of the influence of user attributes for on-body wearable location and we recommend future work to delve deeper into factors like the type of activity (e.g., sports) and social interactions through exploratory interviews, focus groups, and more detailed questionnaires. Particularly, future work should attempt to obtain more balanced samples in terms of age, occupation, education levels, and cultures as our sample was relatively homogeneous in terms of field of work and country of residence.

6.
Conclusion

We present a dataset containing wearable habits for users (
n=320
) from over ten countries collected through an online questionnaire. We examined how user demographics influence commercial wearable devices’ on-body placement during real-world usage. Our findings highlight substantial variability in wearable location habits across gender, age, and lifestyle. While men predominantly store smartphones in front pants pockets, women reported a broader range of placements, including purses and backpacks. We also found that smartwatches are typically worn on the non-dominant wrist, a behavior rooted in traditional watch-wearing. This behavior serves as an example of how tradition can play a key role in the adoption of wearables.
We also performed a systematic statistical evaluation of all possible combinations of factors, revealing (often counter-intuitive) associations between demographics and usage habits.

These insights challenge the uniform device placement in wearable system design and highlight the limitations of benchmarks and datasets that fail to capture this diversity.
We argue that systems trained or evaluated on such biased datasets risk reduced generalizability and adoption of wearables in real-world contexts.
We emphasize the need for inclusive, demographically representative benchmarks that reflect natural user behavior and support robust algorithm performance across populations.
