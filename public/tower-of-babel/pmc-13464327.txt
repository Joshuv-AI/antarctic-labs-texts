The impact of a diversified training framework on work-related stress and depressive and anxious emotions among newly hired nurses after training
Authors: Tixin Gu, Qi Zhang, Pengyu Wang, Man Zhang, Min Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNewly hired nurses frequently experience significant work-related stress and emotional distress during their transition into clinical practice. This study aimed to investigate the impact of a diversified training framework on work-related stress, depressive emotions, and anxious emotions among this population.MethodsA total of 241 nurses with less than three years of experience were stratified by work experience and randomly assigned to either an observation group (n = 122) receiving a diversified training framework (incorporating micro-lessons, flipped classroom, scenario simulations, PBL, and CBL) or a control group (n = 119) receiving traditional training. Stress (SNSI), depression (PHQ-9), and anxiety (GAD-7) levels were assessed before training, immediately after training, and at 3, 6, and 12-month follow-ups.ResultsThe observation group demonstrated significantly lower scores in work-related stress, depressive emotions, and anxious emotions compared to the control group at all post-training and follow-up assessments (all P < 0.05), with no significant differences at baseline. Correlation analysis indicated a consistent negative association between the diversified training framework and these negative emotional outcomes over time.ConclusionsA diversified training framework significantly reduces work-related stress, depressive emotions, and anxious emotions among newly hired nurses, with sustained benefits observed over a 12-month period. These findings highlight the value of innovative, interactive training strategies in supporting the psychological well-being of novice nurses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09174-y.

Introduction

The transition from student nurse to a registered professional nurse is a critical period characterized by numerous challenges, including the acquisition of clinical skills, adaptation to the healthcare environment, and the management of work-related stress and emotional well-being [1, 2]. Newly hired nurses often experience high levels of stress, depressive emotions, and anxiety as they navigate the complexities of clinical practice, potentially impacting their performance and overall job satisfaction [3]. The prevalence of depressive symptoms among nurses in the USA was between 35 and 41%, but another study reported it to be 18% [4], 11%–80% in Iranian nurses, 35% in Chinese nurses [5], 17% in Australian Midwives [6], and 51% in Brazilian nurses [7]. Additionally, approximately 33% of French nurse managers and 10% Canadian nurses were found to suffer from depressive symptoms as well [8]. Addressing these challenges is crucial not only for the well-being of the nurses themselves but also for the quality of patient care they provide. In this context, the development and implementation of effective training programs tailored to the specific needs of newly hired nurses are essential. Nevertheless, the most efficacious training methodologies to mitigate occupational stress and promote emotional well-being among this demographic remain a subject of active investigation.

The demands of clinical practice, patient care responsibilities, and the steep learning curve inherent to their roles can contribute to feelings of inadequacy, self-doubt, and emotional distress [9, 10]. Left unaddressed, these emotions can impact job performance, interpersonal relationships, and the overall psychological well-being of nurses [11]. Furthermore, depressive and anxious emotions among healthcare professionals have been linked to decreased quality of patient care and increased healthcare costs [12]. Therefore, interventions aimed at addressing these emotional challenges are warranted.

Traditional training programs for nurses often rely on didactic lectures, skill demonstration, and clinical preceptorship [13]. While these methods are valuable, they may not sufficiently address the diverse learning needs and emotional well-being of newly hired nurses [14]. Innovative training approaches, such as micro-lessons, flipped classrooms, scenario simulations, problem-based learning (PBL), and case-based learning (CBL), have the potential to offer a more holistic and supportive training experience for novice nurses [15]. These methods prioritize active learning, critical thinking, and the application of knowledge in real-life clinical scenarios, aligning with the specific needs and challenges faced by newly hired nurses as they transition into professional practice [16]. However, the impact of such diversified training frameworks on work-related stress and emotional well-being among this population remains a subject of ongoing investigation [17].

Given the aforementioned challenges and the potential of innovative training approaches, the current study aimed to investigate the impact of a diversified training framework on work-related stress, depressive emotions, and anxious emotions among newly hired nurses after training. Specifically, the study sought to compare the outcomes of nurses who underwent traditional training approaches with those who were exposed to a diversified training framework encompassing micro-lessons, flipped classroom experiences, scenario simulations, PBL, and CBL. The study aimed to contribute to a better understanding of effective training strategies tailored to the unique needs of newly hired nurses by examining the effects of such training on work-related stress and emotional well-being. The findings from this study are expected to inform the development of evidence-based interventions aimed at supporting the psychological well-being and professional competence of healthcare professionals in the early stages of their careers.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

A total of 241 nurses with less than 3 years of work experience at our Hospital between June 2020 and June 2023 were selected using a comprehensive sampling method. This study stratified 241 eligible nurses based on their work experience years, dividing them into three groups: those with less than 1 year, 1–2 years, and 2–3 years of work experience. Subsequently, the random number table method was employed to randomly assign nurses from each group into either the control group or the observation group. They were then randomly assigned to either the control group (n = 119) or the observation group (n = 122) (Fig. 1). Inclusion criteria: ① Newly hired graduate nurses; ② Nurses who have passed the professional qualification examination; ③ Volunteers for participation; ④ Complete personal information. Exclusion criteria: ①Nurses who have not completed pre-job training; ③ Nurses who drop out midway during training; ④ Nurses with at least 1 year of experience prior to entry. This study was approved by the First Hospital of Hebei Medical University Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee (Approval number: SKH-2024-0828-0017). Informed consent was waived for this study due to the exclusive use of de-identified research subject data, which had no potential harm or impact on the research subjects. This waiver was approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee in accordance with regulatory and ethical guidelines for research studies.

Training methods

Control group

In accordance with the “New Nurse Training Program” requirements, the nursing department formulated and distributed the “Standardized Nurse Training Manual,” with the head nurses and educational team leaders diligently implementing the training content and assessment criteria. The specialized theoretical and practical training was conducted by the educational team leaders and senior nurses using a centralized training approach, including monthly teaching rounds and difficult case discussions once, teaching lectures four times, and four nursing procedure training sessions. Additionally, quarterly nursing rounds were conducted. Clinical training was conducted through one-on-one mentoring, with N3 level or higher nursing teachers assigned to mentoring tasks.

Observation group

According to the nursing department’s “New Nurse Training Program” requirements, departmental teaching plans were developed, and a learning model encompassing “micro-lessons, flipped classroom, scenario simulations, PBL, and CBL” was implemented for practical and theoretical training. The specific training process included the following steps. Firstly, learning tools: All novice nurses used smartphones for convenient internet access, and the vice director of the nursing department responsible for teaching created WeChat and QQ groups for collaborative micro-lesson development and selection by head nurses and educational instructors from each department. Additionally, head nurses, educational instructors, and all novice nurses from the respective departments formed student-teacher groups to receive the finalized micro-lessons for independent study. Secondly, selection of teaching content and micro-lesson production: In accordance with the nursing department’s training plan, head nurses selected essential nursing professional knowledge and skills that novice nurses needed to master. Using a hierarchical model of head nurse—educational instructor—night shift leader—nurse, they cooperated to plan, organize, and implement the training, with the head nurse responsible, educational instructors designing, and night shift leaders implementing specific tasks. Clinical educational instructors employed various methods such as demonstrations, images, and simulation teaching aids to produce concise, contextual, and case-based micro-lesson videos of specialized theoretical knowledge and operational procedures. Each micro-lesson video was recorded twice, and the best version was selected. The number of micro-lessons in each department was determined by the department’s training plan and accompanied by relevant assignments. Educational instructors adjusted and updated the micro-lessons based on novice nurse feedback. Lastly, problem-oriented scenario simulated flipped classroom implementation: novice nurses designed scenarios based on standard specialized nursing procedures, hospital emergency nursing protocols, and common critical patient rescue processes, acted out realistic scenarios, wrote scripts, and played role-play based on the scripts. The head nurses and educational instructors from each department observed, guided, and provided feedback. These sessions were conducted twice a month, with each lasting 30 min (Fig. 2).

Data collection

This includes the collection of data on the age, gender, education level, work experience, previous training, and department of all participating nurses.

Stressors and stress levels

The stressors and stress levels of the student nurses were measured before the training, after the training, and during the follow-up at 3, 6, and 12 months. The Student Nurse Stress Index (SNSI), developed by Jones and Johnston, is a psychometric instrument specifically designed to assess perceived stressors and stress intensity levels among nursing students. This 22-item multidimensional scale encompasses four distinct domains: Academic Workload (course requirements and time management), Clinical Practice Challenges (skill acquisition and patient care responsibilities), Interpersonal Communication Competence, and Personal Adjustment Factors. A Likert 5-point scale ranging from 1 to 5 was used, representing “no stress” to “extreme stress,” with a total score range of 22 to 110; higher scores indicated higher stress levels. The Cronbach’s alpha for reliability was calculated to be 0.90 [18].

Depressive symptoms

The assessment of depressive symptoms in student nurses was conducted using the PHQ-9 before the training, after the training, and during the follow-up at 3, 6, and 12 months. The PHQ-9 utilizes a scoring system ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), yielding a total score range of 0 to 27. Based on the scoring criteria, the scores were divided into 5 groups: 0–4, 5–9, 10–14, 15–19, and 20–27, corresponding to no depressive symptoms, presence of depressive symptoms, presence of significant depressive symptoms, moderate to severe depressive symptoms, and severe depressive symptoms, respectively. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale in this study was calculated to be 0.89 [19].

Anxiety

The GAD-7 was an effective tool for identifying generalized anxiety disorder. This scale consists of 7 items, employing a scoring system ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), resulting in a total score range of 0 to 21. Based on the scoring criteria, the scores were divided into 4 groups: 0–5, 6–9, 10–14, 15–21, corresponding to no anxiety, mild anxiety symptoms, moderate anxiety symptoms, and severe anxiety symptoms, respectively. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale in this study was calculated to be 0.93 [20].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 29.0 statistical software (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical data were presented as [n (%)] and analyzed using the chi-square test based on the basic statistical analysis was performed using Fisher’s exact probability method. The Shapiro-Wilk method was employed to assess the normal distribution of continuous variables. For normally distributed continuous variables, the format was used to represent the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and the t-test with corrected variance was applied. Non-normally distributed data were presented in the form of median (25th percentile, 75th percentile) and analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Pearson correlation analysis was used for continuous variables.

Discussion

The core value of this study lies not only in verifying the effectiveness of a diversified training framework, but more importantly, in systematically comparing the differential effects of ‘training types’ (diversified training vs. traditional training) on the three key psychological outcomes of work stress, depression, and anxiety among newly hired nurses, deepening our understanding of how to construct a healthy nursing work environment. Previous studies often focused on improvement in a single dimension, but this study provides more comprehensive and powerful evidence for ‘training type’ as an modifiable and systematic protective factor by horizontally comparing these three closely related but distinguishable emotional disorders.

The core hypothesis of this study is that ‘training type’ (i.e. diversified training framework vs. traditional training) is the main factor determining the level of ‘stress, depression, and anxiety’ among newly hired nurses. Our data supports this hypothesis, indicating a significant and direct negative correlation between the ‘training type’ itself and outcome variables after controlling for confounding factors (i.e. diversified training significantly reduces negative emotions). The findings of this study have significant implications for the development of effective training programmes to support the mental well-being of newly hired nurses, ultimately enhancing their overall job performance and contributing to the delivery of high-quality patient care. The significant reductions in SNSI observed in the observation group following the implementation of the diversified training framework were of particular significance. This indicates that compared to traditional training that focuses on knowledge transfer, a diversified framework that integrates elements such as micro lessons and scenario simulations is more effective in reducing the immediate and sustained stress perception of new nurses when dealing with clinical complexity. The reduction of stress perception is the cornerstone of building a healthy work environment. The post-training and follow-up assessments consistently demonstrated lower stress levels in the observation group compared to the control group. This suggests that the incorporation of innovative training methods, such as micro-lessons and scenario simulations, may contribute to a more comprehensive and effective acquisition of professional knowledge and skills, thereby reducing the stress experienced by newly hired nurses in the workplace. These findings align with previous research highlighting the potential of interactive and scenario-based training approaches in reducing stress and enhancing learning outcomes among healthcare professionals [21, 22].

The results of this study showed that there were statistically significant differences in stress, anxiety, and depression scores between the control group and the observation group after training and at 3-month, 6-month, and 12-month follow-up. This indicates that the diversified training group was significantly better than the control group at all follow-up time points. This means that the differences in training types not only affect nurses’ perception of work pressure, but also have a deeper impact on their emotional regulation ability and psychological resilience. The alleviation of depressive emotions may be attributed to the increased sense of control brought about by the problem-solving abilities cultivated by PBL and CBL. The reduction of anxiety may be attributed to the reduced uncertainty of facing unknown clinical situations through scenario simulation and flipped classroom. This long-term effect further strengthens the causal relationship between ‘training type’ and ‘mental health’. Therefore, a diversified training framework is not only an immediate learning tool, but also a long-term psychological support mechanism that can help nurses continuously resist the invasion of stressors after entering the actual work environment. This significant correlation (Training Type → Outcome) can be explained by the Cognitive Load Theory and Self Efficacy Theory. Diversified training reduces the intrinsic cognitive load during learning through micro lessons and scenario simulations, providing more ‘Mastery Experiences’ and effectively alleviating anxiety and depression caused by uncertainty and helplessness. This theoretical mechanism explains why the associations we observe are not only significant, but also clinically significant. These findings resonate with existing literature emphasizing the importance of comprehensive training programs in promoting the psychological well-being of healthcare professionals, particularly in high-stress environments [23, 24].

The significant negative correlations identified between the diversified training framework and work-related stress, depressive emotions, and anxious emotions at various time points after the training further support the beneficial effects of innovative training approaches on the psychological well-being of newly hired nurses. The strength and consistency of these correlations underscore the potential of the diversified training framework in not only enhancing professional competence but also in fostering a supportive and less emotionally taxing work environment for novice healthcare professionals. These findings contribute to the growing body of evidence indicating the significance of addressing the emotional well-being of healthcare professionals, as it was found to be closely linked to job satisfaction, retention, and the quality of patient care.

Several factors may have contributed to the observed positive outcomes associated with the implementation of the diversified training framework. Firstly, the use of technology, such as smartphones for internet access and the creation of collaborative learning groups, facilitated accessible and interactive learning experiences for novice nurses [25]. The integration of technology-based learning tools may have enhanced engagement, knowledge retention, and self-directed learning, thereby supporting the acquisition of skills and knowledge in a less stressful and more empowering manner [26]. Secondly, the emphasis on scenario simulations, PBL, and CBL may have provided practical and experiential learning opportunities that were essential for building confidence and competence among newly hired nurses [27, 28]. These active learning methods not only offer a more engaging learning environment but also replicate real-life clinical situations, preparing novice nurses for the demands of their professional roles [29].

The diversification of training frameworks, incorporating progressive methods such as micro-lessons, flipped classrooms, scenario simulations, PBL, and CBL, has shown promise in mitigating work-related stress and enhancing the emotional well-being of newly recruited nurses post-training. The potential impact of this approach stems from several underlying factors. Firstly, micro-lessons, characterized by their succinct and targeted nature, facilitate the gradual and manageable acquisition of knowledge and skills [30]. This method addresses the common sentiment of being overwhelmed experienced by novice nurses when confronted with the extensive learning demands of their roles, by breaking down complex information into easily digestible segments, thereby reducing the cognitive load and promoting effective retention of information [31]. The flipped classroom model, which involves conducting traditional learning activities outside the classroom and interactive activities within, fosters a dynamic and participatory learning environment [32]. By engaging in active learning experiences during face-to-face sessions, such as problem-solving exercises and collaborative discussions, newly hired nurses can benefit from enhanced engagement and interaction, potentially alleviating the stress and anxiety associated with the initial learning curve of their profession [33]. Additionally, scenario simulations offer a safe environment for nurses to apply their knowledge and skills by replicating real-life clinical situations. Through these simulations, nurses develop confidence in decision-making and clinical acumen, effectively reducing anxiety and self-doubt often experienced in the early stages of their careers [34]. Similarly, PBL and CBL methods underscore active learning, critical thinking, and self-directed exploration of clinical problems, empowering nurses to develop resilience, adaptability, and confidence in their clinical reasoning abilities. This structured yet interactive nature of PBL and CBL positively influences the emotional well-being of newly hired nurses, fostering a more confident and composed approach to their professional responsibilities [35]. Overall, the integration of bite-sized learning modules, interactive classroom experiences, realistic scenario simulations, and PBL serves to address the specific psychological and educational requirements of newly hired nurses, promoting their well-being and resilience as they transition into professional nursing practice.

It is worth noting that the levels of stress, depression, and anxiety in the observation group were still significantly lower than those in the control group at 3, 6, and 12 months of follow-up. This phenomenon can be explained by the Spaced Effect in learning psychology. Due to the emphasis on the repeated practice mechanism of Micro Lisson and Flipped Classroom in the diversified training framework, new nurses still retained high-frequency knowledge review and situational exercises during the follow-up period. This high-frequency ‘consolidation exercise’ significantly enhances long-term memory and skill retention, thereby continuously alleviating the risk of occupational burnout for new nurses during a one-year follow-up period.

Despite the promising outcomes observed in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the study was conducted at a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings. Replication of the study in diverse clinical environments was warranted to confirm the robustness of the observed effects. Secondly, the follow-up period of 12 months may not capture long-term changes in work-related stress and emotional well-being among newly hired nurses. Future studies with extended follow-up periods were needed to assess the sustainability of the observed benefits over time. In addition, the study focuses on self-reported stress, depression, and anxiety emotions, and does not include objective measures of performance or patient outcomes, as well as the collection of external factors such as nurses’ clinical exposure experiences, personal learning styles, and differences in teacher guidance. Finally, this study mainly relies on cognitive load theory and psychological self-efficacy theory to explain the impact of diversified training on emotions. However, these theories mainly focus on individual level cognitive processing and self-efficacy, and may not fully explain the direct effects of nurse burnout or systemic stressors such as organizational culture and workload on emotions. Future research may need to combine the Job Demands Resources Model or Emotion Regulation Theory to more comprehensively analyze the interaction between complex work environments and emotional responses. Incorporating objective performance assessments and patient-related outcomes in future research provide a more comprehensive understanding of the impact of the diversified training framework on healthcare quality.

Based on the findings of this study, we suggest conducting in-depth exploration in the following three key areas for future nursing training research: firstly, it is necessary to strengthen the validation of theoretical mechanisms. It is recommended to further test the specific roles of “Micro Lisson” in alleviating cognitive load and “Scenario Simulation” in enhancing self-efficacy through rigorous experimental design, and clarify the mediating pathways of these mechanisms in stress and anxiety relief. Secondly, it is necessary to conduct a breakdown analysis of the training components. Future research could attempt to evaluate the effectiveness of each training module separately (such as implementing only Micro Lisson or only Scenario Simulation) to quantify the independent contribution of different teaching methods to emotional improvement (depression, anxiety). Finally, it is necessary to integrate objective performance indicators. Suggest introducing quantitative indicators such as misdiagnosis rate, nursing quality score, or patient satisfaction to verify whether the improvement in emotional state translates into actual improvement in clinical behavior.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings of this study indicate that a diversified training framework, incorporating micro-lessons, flipped classroom, scenario simulations, PBL, and CBL, has the potential to reduce work-related stress and improve the emotional well-being of newly hired nurses after training. The study underscores the importance of innovative and interactive training strategies in not only enhancing professional competence but also in supporting the psychological well-being of healthcare professionals. Future research exploring the long-term effects and broader applicability of such training approaches was warranted to advance the development of effective interventions for promoting the well-being of healthcare professionals and optimizing patient care.
