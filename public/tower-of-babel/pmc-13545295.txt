Paratesticular epithelioid sarcoma in a pediatric patient
Authors: Thomas Robert Ashley, Avery Wolfe, Zhongxin Yu, Adam Rensing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHere we describe the first case of paratesticular epithelioid sarcoma in an adolescent male. Paratesticular epithelioid sarcoma is an exceedingly rare entity with an aggressive clinical course and poor prognosis. The patient underwent wide hemiscrotectomy with radical inguinal orchiectomy and adjuvant chemoradiotherapy for inguinal nodal metastasis. Subsequent retroperitoneal lymph node dissection revealed metastasis to the para-aortic lymph nodes. Paratesticular sarcomas can be challenging to diagnose and treat. This report outlines the pathology and management of the first pediatric paratesticular epithelioid sarcoma in the English literature and highlights the need for multidisciplinary collaboration.

Introduction

Paratesticular tumors (PTTs) are rare, accounting for 7-10% of intrascrotal tumors.1 They can arise from the spermatic cord, epididymis, ductus deferens, tunica vaginalis, and other tissues adjacent to the testis.1 Paratesticular sarcomas (PTS) are even more rare, representing only 2% of genitourinary masses and 30% of PTT. PTS typically present as indolent, firm, irreducible scrotal or groin masses 1, 2, 3. Spermatic cord sarcoma is thought to represent the majority (75%) of cases, but identification of specific tissue of origin may be difficult 2, 3, 4. PTS specific mortality ranges from 5% to 32% in localized disease.5,6 Management of paratesticular sarcoma is primarily surgical with radical inguinal orchiectomy, and the role of adjuvant chemotherapy is debated1.

Epithelioid sarcoma (ES), first described by Enzinger in 1970, is a rare malignant mesenchymal neoplasm accounting for 1% of soft tissue sarcomas.7,8 ES is characterized by inactivation of the SMARCB1 gene, which regulates gene transcription via chromatin remodeling. ES is divided into two distinct subtypes: a more aggressive proximal type, and a less aggressive distal type.7 ES typically has an indolent presentation as painless and indurated nodules in the digits or deep soft tissues, and most commonly occurs in young adult males.9,10 ES is managed with wide excision and adjuvant chemoradiation.8,9 Although ES exhibits slow growth, it is known for both local recurrence and distant metastasis, and disease-specific mortality approaches 45%.7,9

Both ES and PTT are challenging entities to diagnose and treat owing to their aggressive clinical course and lack of existing guidelines. ES may be mistaken for benign or inflammatory processes, delaying diagnosis, and carries an unpredictable clinical course with high rates of recurrence and metastasis.1,2 Here we describe a case of paratesticular ES in a 14-year-old male. To our knowledge, this represents the first reported case of paratesticular ES in a pediatric patient, and the sixth case of paratesticular ES in the literature 3, 4, 5, 6, 7.

Case presentation

Our patient is an otherwise healthy 14-year-old male who presented to the emergency department with two-day history of intermittent severe right-sided testicular pain. However, on questioning, he admitted to noting a firm but painless mass posterior to the right testicle which had been enlarging over the past year. Physical exam was significant for a firm bilateral, fixed inguinal lymphadenopathy and a large, firm paratesticular mass posterior and inferior to the right testis. Scrotal ultrasound showed a heterogenous 4.3 x 4.2 × 3.2 cm paratesticular mass concerning for oncologic process and normal venous flow via doppler. Computed tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis revealed bulky inguinal lymph nodes, the largest measuring 2.5 × 2.9 cm, suspicious for metastasis. White blood cell count, and tumor markers (lactate dehydrogenase, beta-human chorionic gonadotropin, and alpha-fetoprotein) were within normal limits. The patient was admitted to the pediatric intensive care unit for inguinal node biopsy by interventional radiology and oncologic workup by pediatric oncology. He was discharged home while awaiting biopsy results; however, inguinal lymph node biopsy was complicated by large right sided hematoma formation necessitating readmittance. Biopsy of the right inguinal lymph node resulted while inpatient, and revealed epithelioid sarcoma, proximal type. The patient was scheduled for right inguinal orchiectomy and hemiscrotectomy with concurrent port placement in anticipation of future systemic therapy. PET scan was delayed until after primary resection due to scheduling restraints unrelated to clinical status.

Intraoperatively, the testicle and spermatic cord were delivered via an inguinal approach. The paratesticular mass was densely adherent to the posterior scrotal skin, the right corpora cavernosum and bulbar urethra with surrounding lymphadenopathy. The mass was carefully dissected off the adjacent structures and was noted to be separate from the base of the penis. Margins from the right-sided urethra and right corpora were taken. The right testicle was removed and sent en bloc with the mass along with several scrotal lymph nodes for pathology. Inguinal lymphadenectomy was deferred until end of therapy surgical management due to the high associated morbidity and low therapeutic benefit in setting of metastatic disease.

Pathology findings

The initial core biopsy of the right inguinal lymph node revealed sheets of large polygonal epithelioid cells that nearly completely replaced the underlying lymph node architecture. The tumor cells exhibited abundant, lightly eosinophilic cytoplasm and round, eccentrically placed nuclei. Some cells demonstrated rhabdoid features, characterized by intracytoplasmic inclusions (Fig. 1A and B). Marked cytologic atypia and brisk mitotic activity were present. Immunohistochemically, the tumor cells showed diffuse, strong positivity for epithelial markers, including pan-cytokeratin (Fig. 1C) and EMA, with complete loss of nuclear INI1 expression (Fig. 1D). The tumor cells were negative for S-100 protein, GFAP, p63, desmin, myogenin, and MyoD1. Nuclear expression of BRG1 was retained. The overall histopathologic features, together with the loss of INI1 expression, were diagnostic of metastatic epithelioid sarcoma. The primary tumor location and large-cell morphology were consistent with the proximal type of epithelioid sarcoma.

The subsequent resection specimen demonstrated a well-circumscribed soft tissue mass in the paratesticular region. Grossly, the mass measured 6.0 × 4.0 × 4.0 cm. On sectioning, the tumor had yellow-tan cut surfaces with extensive necrosis and hemorrhage (Fig. 1E). Microscopically, the tumor exhibited morphology similar to that seen in the biopsy, consisting of large polygonal epithelioid cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and round, eccentrically placed nuclei. Marked cytologic atypia, brisk mitotic activity, and extensive tumor necrosis were present. Immunohistochemical studies again demonstrated strong positivity for epithelial markers and loss of nuclear INI1 expression, confirming the diagnosis of proximal-type epithelioid sarcoma. The testis was uninvolved and showed no significant histopathologic abnormalities or evidence of tumor invasion.

Postoperative course

Following primary resection, the patient underwent staging positron emission tomography (PET) scan which showed uptake in the surgical bed and bilateral inguinal lymph nodes, classifying him as Stage IV ES. He underwent six cycles of chemotherapy with ifosfamide-doxorubicin and radiation. Sperm banking was performed prior to initiation of chemotherapy. Mid therapy CT scan showed an interval decrease in size of the right inguinal node to 2.2 × 1.6 cm from 2.9 × 2.6 cm, an increase in the left inguinal node to 2.6 × 2.8 cm from 2.1 × 2.2 cm, and a newly enlarged left external iliac lymph node. End-of-therapy PET scan showed no uptake in the surgical bed and decreased size and uptake in the bilateral inguinal lymph nodes and left external iliac lymph node. After counseling with oncology and urology, the patient and his family elected to undergo end of therapy surgical resection with robotic lymph node dissection of bilateral iliac nodes, open dissection of bilateral inguinal lymph nodes, and cystoscopy with exploration of the urethra and possible urethral/corporal resection and urethrostomy with curative intent.

Robotic lymphadenectomy of the pelvis was performed first to evaluate disease burden. Lymph node dissection along the left external iliac vein and left common iliac vein was commenced using the split-and-roll technique. The left vas deferens was excised and submitted for pathologic examination, which was negative for tumor involvement. A visually concerning left deep paraaortic lymph node was submitted for intraoperative frozen section evaluation and was negative for metastatic tumor. Lymphadenectomy on the right side was performed with similar technique. During the procedure, a large, visually concerning right external iliac node, which had not been identified on previous scans, was visualized and resected. Frozen section evaluation of this lymph node was positive for metastatic epithelioid sarcoma.

After extensive intraoperative discussion between urologic oncology and medical oncology regarding the high residual active disease burden, the decision was made not to proceed with inguinal node dissection to facilitate earlier initiation of salvage systemic therapy. Inguinal node dissection was anticipated to delay systemic therapy by several months due to poor wound healing in a previously radiated area. Post-surgical PET scan showed decreased size and uptake in the inguinal and iliac lymph nodes and no activity in the surgical bed. Salvage chemotherapy with doclataxel and gemcitabine was initiated that was complicated by sigmoid colitis with perforation necessitating ileostomy. Given this patient's complications arising from chemoradiation and dismal prognosis of metastatic and refractory epithelioid sarcoma, the patient is now continuing therapy with palliative intent.

Discussion

This is the first case of pediatric paratesticular epithelioid sarcoma described in the literature, as well as the first case of PTT-ES presenting with nodal metastasis. ES is a rare and aggressive tumor with an unpredictable clinical course and poor prognosis which commonly presents with lymph node metastasis, distant metastasis, and eventually local recurrence.2 Proximal-type ES commonly arises in perineum pelvis and genital tract and carries a worse prognosis than distal-type.7 Wide excision with negative margins followed by adjuvant chemotherapy and radiation to prevent local recurrence is the mainstay of therapy 8, 9, 10, 11. Primary resection and subsequent surgical management of this patient were complicated by involvement of the base of the penis, conferring a low likelihood for negative surgical margins. Known metastatic disease evidenced by bulky inguinal lymphadenopathy at presentation necessitated systemic chemotherapy, thus inguinal dissection was deferred until chemotherapy was completed. Following discovery of positive deep paraaortic lymph nodes during end of therapy surgery, inguinal dissection was again deferred to facilitate earlier initiation of salvage systemic therapy.

PTS carry an uncertain prognosis, although most are not associated with nodal spread or metastasis at time of diagnosis [1, 2, 6, 16]. The majority of PTS are not clinically suspected preoperatively, potentially necessitating reoperation.1 Rhabdomyosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, and liposarcoma represent most of the malignant histopathological diagnoses 8,11, 12, 13. Early hemiscrotectomy with wide clear margins and radical orchiectomy has been shown to improve long-term outcomes in PTS.10,11

Diagnosis and management of both PTT and ES present a clinical challenge due to the lack of guidelines and standardized techniques, as well as heterogenous reporting.14 However, a recent risk-based management algorithm for PTT has bene proposed by Jhang et al.9 PTS may be initially misdiagnosed as benign scrotal conditions such as inguinal hernia, epididymal cyst, or testicular malignancies. Malignant paratesticular processes should be suspected in all scrotal masses, including painless irreducible inguinal hernias, and imaging should be utilized to aid in diagnosis.14 Given the increased risk of long-term sequelae of radiation and chemotherapy on pediatric patients, early initiation of survivorship discussions should be paramount. In young patients desiring future fertility, fertility counseling and sperm banking should be a priority before undergoing adjuvant chemotherapy Ongoing efforts to raise public awareness of urogenital cancer presenting as an inguinoscrotal mass should be supported, especially among youths. Most studies of PTS to date consist of single center retrospective case series with older adult patients, limiting their utility for prospective clinical care of pediatric patients with newly diagnosed PTS. Multicenter collaboration is needed to better management and outcomes for patients with pediatric PTS.

Conclusion

Paratesticular epithelioid sarcoma is a rare tumor requiring aggressive surgical and oncological treatment, described here for the first time in a pediatric patient. Early recognition and treatment of paratesticular sarcoma represents a challenge in diagnosis and management, warranting a coordinated multidisciplinary approach.
