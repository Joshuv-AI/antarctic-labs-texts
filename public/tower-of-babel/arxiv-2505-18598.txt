Abstract
Understanding the spectrum and dynamics of excited nucleon states ( N ∗ N^{*} resonances) remains a central challenge in hadron physics, as these resonances emerge in intermediate states of π ​ N \pi N and γ ​ N \gamma N interactions. The dynamical coupled-channel (DCC) models addressing this problem rely critically on experimental inputs for partial wave analyses (PWA). However, polarization observables of hyperons, which serve as sensitive probes of reaction mechanisms, have so far been limited in precision. To end this, we aim to provide high-precision measurements of the polarization of the Λ \Lambda hyperon produced in the π − ​ p → K 0 ​ Λ \pi^{-}p\to K^{0}\Lambda reaction. The data were obtained with the J-PARC E40 experiment at the K1.8 beamline of the J-PARC Hadron Experimental Facility. The polarization of the Λ \Lambda was precisely measured in the angular range 0.6 < cos ⁡ θ C ​ M K ​ 0 < 1.0 0.6<\cos{\theta^{K0}_{CM}}<1.0 with a fine bin width of d cos θ K ​ 0 C ​ M = 0.05 d\cos{\theta^{K0}_{CM}}=0.05 . The observed average polarization in the region 0.60 < cos ⁡ θ C ​ M K ​ 0 < 0.85 0.60<\cos{\theta^{K0}_{CM}}<0.85 was 0.932 ± 0.058 ​ ( stat ) ± 0.028 ​ ( syst ) 0.932\pm 0.058(\text{stat})\pm 0.028(\text{syst}) . These results provide essential input for PWAs of DCC models, contributing to a deeper understanding of N ∗ N^{*} resonance dynamics. Furthermore, the strong Λ \Lambda polarization observed indicates the feasibility of developing a highly polarized Λ \Lambda beam, opening prospects for future Λ ​ p \Lambda p scattering experiments such as J-PARC E86.

Introduction

The nucleon resonances,
N^{*}
, that appear as the intermediate states in
\pi N
and
\gamma N
reactions are direct manifestations of the non-perturbative dynamics of quarks and gluons (in the low-energy regime of QCD), providing crucial insights for understanding hadron structure and the strong interaction. The systematic investigation of the mass, width, and decay branching ratios of
N^{*}
is termed “baryon spectroscopy.” By comparing these results with theoretical predictions from models such as the quark model and lattice QCD, it is possible to test the internal degrees of freedom within hadrons
[
1
]
.

However, analysing a single channel (for example, only
\pi N\to\pi N
) presents the problem that the nature of the resonance cannot be correctly extracted
[
2
]
since the
N^{*}
observed in experiments is often coupled to various channels, not only
\pi N
but also
\eta N,\ K\Lambda,\ K\Sigma,\ \pi\pi N
, and others.

The dynamical coupled-channel (DCC) model is a theoretical framework that approaches this challenge by performing a “dynamically coupled” analysis of all major channels through a partial wave analysis (PWA) using experimental data, such as spin observables of the product particle
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
. Regarding the
\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}\Lambda
reaction, the available experimental data, especially
\Lambda
polarization data
[
28
,
29
]
, have large uncertainties, resulting in the mechanism of the
N^{*}
emerging not being fully understood yet. This paper reports the high-precision experimental data of
\Lambda
polarization in the
\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}\Lambda
reaction. Our data could be an essential input for the DCC models, which typically perform simultaneous fits to experimental data of spin observables in a wide range of reaction channels (e.g.,
\pi N\to\eta N,K\Lambda,K\Sigma
, and
\gamma N\to\pi N
) to extract the model parameters.

This paper is also relevant to the study of hyperon-nucleon (
YN
) interactions, second only to nucleon-nucleon (
NN
) interactions.
The
NN
interaction is classified as part of the baryon-baryon (
{\rm B}_{8}{\rm B}_{8}
) interaction. Since this classification also includes interactions with hyperons, studying
{\rm B}_{8}{\rm B}_{8}
interactions leads to a more general understanding of the nuclear force as a baryon-baryon force. Furthermore, as the
{\rm B}_{8}{\rm B}_{8}
interaction is the basis for describing and understanding nuclear systems containing hyperons, such as hypernuclei and neutron stars, theoretical and experimental studies on the
{\rm B}_{8}{\rm B}_{8}
interaction have been intensively carried out.
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
. Unlike abundant
NN
scattering data,
YN
scattering data are still scarce owing to experimental difficulties due to the short lifetime of hyperons. Instead, the energy level structures of
\Lambda
hypernuclei have been used as probes to investigate the
\Lambda N
interaction
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
. Here, the
\Lambda
hyperon can penetrate the nuclear interior and form deeply bound hypernuclear states since it is not subject to Pauli blocking by other nucleons. Using hypernuclear data, two-body
\Lambda N
interaction theories have been updated
[
17
]
.

However, current theoretical models incorporating the current
\Lambda N
interaction fail to explain the existence of recently observed massive neutron stars
[
18
,
19
]
(known as the “hyperon puzzle” of neutron stars). To establish more realistic
YN
interaction models, it was necessary to obtain the precise two-body
YN
interactions by scattering experiments. We (J-PARC E40 experiment
[
20
,
21
]
) have obtained high-statistic
\Sigma p
scattering data at the Japan Proton Accelerator Research Complex (J-PARC). Here, the differential cross-sections for
\Sigma^{-}p
scattering
[
22
]
,
\Sigma^{-}p\to\Lambda n
inelastic scattering
[
23
]
, and
\Sigma^{+}p
scattering
[
24
]
have been precisely measured. Ref.
[
24
]
also derived the phase shifts for
\delta_{{}^{3}S_{1}}
and
\delta_{{}^{1}P_{1}}
of the
\Sigma^{+}p
channel for the first time.

The modern description of the
YN
interactions based on the chiral effective field theory (
\chi
EFT) constructed by
J. Haidenbauer
[
25
]
has recently updated their low-energy constants (LECs) with our data. The
YN
interaction with strangeness
S=-1
has advanced to the next-to-next-to-leading order (NNLO)
[
26
]
. However, the LECs have not yet been uniquely determined, and
\Lambda N
scattering data, particularly differential observables containing angular information such as differential cross-sections and spin observables, are indispensable. Therefore, we plan the next-generation
\Lambda p
scattering experiment (J-PARC E86 experiment
[
27
]
) to collect
\Lambda p
scattering data, including spin observables. As the spin observable, we will measure the analyzing power and depolarization of the
\Lambda
particle using a spin-polarized
\Lambda
beam produced by the
\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}\Lambda
reaction. Although the experimental design of J-PARC E86 is based on
\Lambda
polarization data in the
\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}\Lambda
reaction, measured in the 1980s
[
28
,
29
]
, these data have rather large uncertainties, so this polarization should be reconfirmed with more precise data. Additionally, establishing a reliable method to measure
\Lambda
polarization is essential for the future spin observables analysis. To accurately verify the feasibility of J-PARC E86, we measured the
\Lambda
polarization using the
\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}\Lambda
reaction data collected during the
\Sigma^{-}p
scattering data collection in J-PARC E40.

N^{*}

\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}\Lambda
reactions was described in Sec.
11
.

2
Experiment

J-PARC E40 was conducted at the K1.8 beamline in the J-PARC Hadron Experimental Facility. Its data taking for
\Sigma^{-}p
scattering was performed in February 2019, where a
\pi^{-}
beam with a momentum of 1.33 GeV/
c
was used for
\Sigma^{-}
production. The beam intensity was
2.0\times 10^{7}
/spill, where the spill is a beam cycle of 5.2 seconds with a beam extraction of 2 seconds. The experimental setup is shown in Fig.
1
.

The
\pi^{-}
beam was transported to an experimental target through the K1.8 beamline, the most downstream of which is the K1.8 beamline spectrometer, composed of a QQDQQ magnet and position detectors, to measure the momentum of the beam particles. The QQDQQ magnetic system is designed to utilize point-to-point optics from the upstream reference (40 cm to the edge of the most upstream Q magnet) and downstream (30 cm from the pole edge of the most downstream Q magnet) points, so that the multiple scattering at the entrance and exit of the QQDQQ does not affect the momentum resolution to the first order. The momentum resolution is expected to be
3.3\times 10^{-4}
(FWHM)
[
30
]
.

The cylindrical detector cluster (CATCH) surrounding the liquid hydrogen target (LH
2
) and a forward magnetic spectrometer (KURAMA) were placed downstream of the K1.8 beamline spectrometer. CATCH
[
31
]
, consisting of the LH
2
target, a cylindrical fiber tracker (CFT), a BGO calorimeter, and a scintillation fiber hodoscope (PiID), measures the kinetic energy of the scattered particles. The energy calibrations of CFT and BGO were performed using the
pp
elastic scattering data with the momentum range of 0.45-0.85 GeV/
c
, referring to the scattering angle and energy deposit of protons
[
22
]
. The efficiencies of CFT and BGO were also estimated using these
pp
scattering data.

The KURAMA spectrometer consists of a dipole magnet (KURAMA magnet), tracking detectors (SFT, SDC1, SDC2, SDC3, FHT1, and FHT2), and trigger counters (SAC, SCH, and TOF). Trajectories of scattered particles at the entrance and exit of the KURAMA magnet were measured using these tracking detectors. The flight path of scattered particles in the magnetic field was determined through numerical iteration using the Runge-Kutta method
[
32
]
. The estimated momentum resolution of the KURAMA spectrometer is
\Delta p/p\sim 10^{-2}

[
30
]
with a magnetic field of 0.78 T at the center. The time-of-flight (TOF) of the outgoing particle, along the flight path of
\sim 3
m, was also measured.

An aerogel Cherenkov counter (SAC) with a refractive index of 1.10 was installed behind SDC1 to reject scattered
\pi^{+}
s at the trigger level. Its efficiency was about 99%, which allowed
\sim
1% of all
\pi
s scattered to the KURAMA spectrometer mixed in its hit pattern. In addition, we also collected data requiring hits of SAC and TOF with a factor of 1/5000. These factors led to the
\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}\Lambda
reaction, in which
K^{0}
decays into
\pi^{+}\pi^{-}
, accumulating during
\Sigma^{-}p
scattering data acquisition.

]
) surrounding the liquid hydrogen (LH

Analysis Flow

The
\Lambda
polarization (hereafter referred to as
P_{\Lambda}
) in the
\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}\Lambda
reaction can be measured using the emission angle distribution of the decay proton from the
\Lambda\to p\pi^{-}
decay at the
\Lambda
rest frame for the
\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}\Lambda
reaction plane. The angular distribution of the decay proton is defined as

\frac{dN}{d\cos{\theta_{p}}}=\frac{N_{0}}{2}(1+\alpha P_{\Lambda}\cos{\theta_{p}}),

(1)

where
\cos{\theta_{p}}
is the emission angle of the proton,
N
is the counts in each bin (
d\cos{\theta_{p}}
),
N_{0}
is the total counts integrating over all
\cos{\theta_{p}}
, and
\alpha
is the asymmetry parameter of
\Lambda\to p\pi^{-}
decay (
\alpha=0.750\pm 0.009\pm 0.004

[
34
]
). When the
\cos{\theta_{p}}
distribution was measured, we could obtain the value of
P_{\Lambda}
by fitting it with the function corresponding to Equation (
1
). The analysis procedures we used are briefly summarized as follows.

First,
K^{0}
in the
\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}\Lambda
reaction was reconstructed by detecting
\pi^{+}
and
\pi^{-}
from
K^{0}\to\pi^{+}\pi^{-}
decay. Subsequently,
\Lambda
was identified using the missing mass method using
\pi^{-}
beam momentum information. In the
K^{0}
reconstruction,
\pi^{+}
was detected using KURAMA with reference to the correlation between mass sqaured and momentum (Fig.
2
), and
\pi^{-}
was detected using CATCH with reference to the correlation between energy deposit measured using CFT (
\Delta E
) and total energy measured using BGO (Fig.
3
). Details are given in Sec.
4
.

Second, the
\Lambda\to p\pi^{-}
decay was identified by detecting the decay proton and
\pi^{-}
using CATCH. Here, we checked the kinematic consistency for the magnitude and the direction of the
\Lambda
momentum vector, assuming the production (
\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}\Lambda
) and decay (
\Lambda\to p\pi^{-}
) kinematics. Finally, we identified
3.56\times 10^{4}

\Lambda
s (Fig.
11
). The remaining background was primarily caused by the multiple
\pi
production (
\pi^{-}p\to\pi^{-}\pi^{-}\pi^{+}p
). Details are given in Sec.
5
.

Third, we obtained the
\cos{\theta_{p}}
distribution and estimated background contribution contained therein. Here, we referred to the
K^{0}
flight length to separate the
\Lambda
production and background. The flight length should be distributed following its lifetime. In contrast, the flight length should distribute around zero if it was mistakenly calculated using
\pi^{+}
and
\pi^{-}
from the
\pi^{-}p\to\pi^{-}\pi^{-}\pi^{+}p
reaction. We subtracted the estimated background from
\cos{\theta_{p}}
of events within the
\Lambda
peak range in the missing mass spectrum. Details are available in Sec.
6
.

Fourth, we corrected the extracted
\cos{\theta_{p}}
using CATCH acceptance estimated using a Geant4-based Monte Carlo simulation. Subsequently, the original
\cos{\theta_{p}}
distribution for the
\Lambda
decay was obtained in the production angular range of
0.6<\cos{\theta^{K0}_{CM}}<1.0
with an angular bin width of
d\cos{\theta^{K0}_{CM}}=0.05
. Details are given in Sec.
7
.

Fifth, we optimized the angular range, which is to be used for the fitting, through a chi-square test. Details are given in Sec.
8
.

Finally, we fitted
\cos{\theta_{p}}
with a linear function corresponding to Equation (
1
) to derive
P_{\Lambda}
. The systematic uncertainties are explained in Sec.
9
.

The present results and discussion are presented in Sec.
10
and Sec.
11
.

Analysis II: Identification of the
\Lambda\to p\pi^{-}
decay

This section explains the second component of the
P_{\Lambda}
analysis,
\Lambda\to p\pi^{-}
decay identification. To correctly determine the cut conditions for the
\Lambda\to p\pi^{-}
decay identification, we simulated the
\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}\Lambda
reactions, normalized to the actual yield, followed by the
\Lambda\to p\pi^{-}
decays without the main background (i.e., multiple
\pi
production). This simulation satisfied the following features; (1) all components of CATCH and KURAMA spectrometers were installed, (2) the measured energy resolution was applied to CFT and BGO, (3) the electromagnetic and hadronic processes were included as physics processes, and (4) The
\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}\Lambda
reaction was generated according to the angular distribution of previous experimental data
[
28
]
. Again, it is reasonable that only the
\Lambda
production reaction (i.e., the
\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}\Lambda
reaction followed by the
\Lambda\to p\pi^{-}
decay) was simulated, as the aim of this simulation is only to study the cut condition for
\Lambda
event selection.

The kinetic energy of the decay
\pi^{-}
cannot be measured because the BGO thickness is insufficient, as mentioned in the explanation of
K^{0}
momentum reconstruction, whereas that of the decay proton can be. Therefore, the
\pi^{-}
momentum magnitude was determined such that the invariant mass of the proton and
\pi^{-}
becomes
\Lambda
mass using the momentum vector of the decay proton and the direction of
\pi^{-}
momentum. Subsequently, the
\Lambda
momentum was obtained by summing the momentum vectors of the decay proton and
\pi^{-}
.

The
\Lambda\to p\pi^{-}
decays were identified by checking the kinematic consistency for magnitude and direction of the
\Lambda
momentum vectors constructed from two independent analyses, that is, the production (
\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}\Lambda
) and decay (
\Lambda\to p\pi^{-}
) analyses. The angular difference (
\Delta\theta
) of the same momentum vectors obtained using these different analyses was used as the valuation index.
\Delta\theta
is calculated as

\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\cos{\Delta\theta}=\frac{\bm{p_{production}}\cdot\bm{p_{decay}}}{|\bm{p_{production}}|\cdot|\bm{p_{decay}|}},

(3)

where
\bm{p_{production}}
is the
\Lambda
momentum vector obtained as the missing momentum of the
\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}X
reaction, and
\bm{p_{decay}}
is the
\Lambda
momentum vector obtained using the above CATCH analysis.
\cos{\Delta\theta}
should ideally be 1, as we here calculate the same momentum (
\Lambda
) using two different analyses. The difference between
\bm{p_{production}}
and
\bm{p_{decay}}
,
\Delta p
, was used as another valuation index and calculated as

\Delta p=|\bm{p_{decay}}|-|\bm{p_{production}}|.

(4)

\Delta p
should ideally be 0.

,

, and

\Delta p

Analysis III: Background Subtraction in the
\cos{\theta_{p}}
Distribution

To measure
P_{\Lambda}
using Equation (
1
), we obtained the
\cos{\theta_{p}}
distribution for the events within the range of
1.07<M_{\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}X}<1.16
GeV/
c^{2}
(indicated by the red solid lines in Fig.
11
), as shown in Fig.
12
. We can easily observe a clear asymmetry in this
\cos{\theta_{p}}
distribution attributed to the decay mechanism of
\Lambda
. The acceptance of CATCH led to a decrease in
\cos{\theta_{p}}=0
. The problem is that this
\cos{\theta_{p}}
distribution still contains unwanted angular distribution due to background contamination in the range of
1.07<M_{\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}X}<1.16
GeV/
c^{2}
. We should remove this background contribution from the
\cos{\theta_{p}}
distribution of Fig.
12
.
The background is the
\pi^{+}\pi^{-}
pair, from multiple
\pi
production (
\pi^{-}p\to\pi^{-}\pi^{-}\pi^{+}p
). To subtract the background contribution, we use the difference of the reaction vertex in the beam direction between multiple
\pi
production and
K^{0}
production. Here, we define the
K^{0}
flight length (
\Delta z_{K^{0}}
) as the distance between the production (
z_{production}
) and decay (
z_{decay}
) points of
K^{0}
with the following expression:

\Delta z_{K^{0}}=z_{decay}-z_{production}.

(5)

Figure 12:
The
\cos{\theta_{p}}
distribution for the events within the range of
1.07<M_{\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}X}<1.16
GeV/
c^{2}
(indicated by the red solid lines in Fig.

).

For a true
K^{0}

\Delta z_{K^{0}}
distribution is asymmetric with an exponential tail toward larger values, corresponding to the
K^{0}
lifetime (the
K^{0}_{s}
lifetime is
8.95\times 10^{-11}
s; thus,
c\tau\sim 2.69
cm). In contrast, for the background events, the same distribution is symmetric with a peak around 0, as the production and decay points should be identical for events induced by the strong interaction.

Fig.
13
shows
\Delta z_{K^{0}}
obtained from the simulation for the background events (red) and
\Lambda
production (blue) with normalized total counts.

The events selected in Fig.

still include both
\Lambda
productions (peak in Fig.
11
) and the
\pi^{+}\pi^{-}
pair from background events (blue shade in Fig.
11
). The
\Delta z_{K^{0}}
distribution calculated for these events includes both the exponential distribution representing true
K^{0}
flight distance and a symmetric distribution around zero due to the background, as schematically shown in Fig.

. The important point is that the background distribution has the symmetric structure for the peak position (hereafter referred to as
\mu_{BG}
). As shown in Fig.
15
, the
\cos{\theta_{p}}
distribution for
\Delta z<\mu_{BG}
(
\cos{\theta_{p}}(\Delta z<\mu_{BG})

\cos{\theta_{p}}(\Delta z>\mu_{BG})

\cos{\theta_{p}}(\Delta z<\mu_{BG})
from
\cos{\theta_{p}}(\Delta z>\mu_{BG})

\Lambda
can be obtained. This subtraction also removes parts of the
\Lambda

To show this analysis is valid, we must confirm that the
\Delta z
distribution is symmetric for background events. In addition, the peak position should be determined from the analysis. To achieve this, we obtained the

.
Since the background events are dominant in this missing mass range, this

f(x)=\frac{p_{0}}{2}(1+p_{1}x),

(9)

where the fitting parameter

corresponds to the total counts of the corrected
\cos{\theta_{p}}
(i.e.,
N_{0}
in Equation (
1

p_{1}
corresponds to
\alpha P_{\Lambda}
in Equation (

In contrast, analysis for the
K^{0}
angular region of
0.8<\cos{\theta^{K0}_{CM}}<0.95
is not as straightforward, because the unexpected enhancement can be observed around central regions from
\cos{\theta_{p}}=0
to
\cos{\theta_{p}}=\pm 0.4

\cos{\theta_{p}}

8.1
Data selection using
\chi^{2}
test

In Fig.
21
, the uncertainty of CATCH acceptance correction made the
\cos{\theta_{p}}
distribution deviate from the expectation, especially in the
0.8<\cos{\theta^{K0}_{CM}}<0.95
range. To select datapoints optimal for fitting to, a
\chi^{2}
test was performed in each
\cos{\theta^{K0}_{CM}}
range with an interval of
d\cos{\theta^{K0}_{CM}}=0.05
.

In general, if
N
independent variables
x_{i}
are each normally distributed with mean
\mu_{i}
and variance
\sigma_{i}^{2}
, then the quantity known as
\chi^{2}
is defined by

\chi^{2}\equiv\frac{(x_{1}-\mu_{1})^{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}+\frac{(x_{2}-\mu_{2})^{2}}{\sigma_{2}^{2}}+\dots+\frac{(x_{N}-\mu_{N})^{2}}{\sigma_{N}^{2}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{(x_{i}-\mu_{i})^{2}}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}.

(10)

In our case, we re-defined that
x_{i}
is the corrected
\cos{\theta_{p}}
counts (
N_{i}
),
\mu_{i}
is the fitting result, and
\sigma_{i}
is the error of corrected
\cos{\theta_{p}}
yield (
\sigma(N_{i})
). The largest degree of freedom where the
\chi^{2}
falls within the 95% confidence interval was selected. Here, we changed the degree of freedom by reducing the data points by two from either side of the center. Since the number of fitting parameters is two as in Equation (
9
), the optimized degree of freedom (
N_{dof}
) can be defined by the number of bins for fitting (
N_{bin}
) as

N_{dof}=N_{bin}-2.

(11)

The
\chi^{2}
with each
N_{bin}
is shown in Figure
22
. The red square point represents the obtained
\chi^{2}
, and the blue-shaded band represents the 95% confidence interval. The numerical values of the optimized
N_{bin}
in each
\cos{\theta^{K0}_{CM}}
range are summarized in Table
1
. Since
P_{\Lambda}
is synonymous with the slope of the
\cos{\theta_{p}}
distribution,
P_{\Lambda}
can be determined more accurately with this optimization. Fig.
23
shows the accepted data points (black) and rejected ones (magenta), with the fitting functions (red solid line).

Consequently,
P_{\Lambda}

\displaystyle P_{\Lambda,\ j}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{1,\ j}}{\alpha},

\displaystyle\delta_{stat,\ j}

\displaystyle=P_{\Lambda,\ j}\sqrt{\left(\frac{\delta(p_{1,\ j})}{p_{1,\ j}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\delta(\alpha)}{\alpha}\right)^{2}},

(13)

where
\delta(p_{1,\ j})
is the fitting error of
p_{1}
and

Systematic Uncertainty

The systematic uncertainties of
P_{\Lambda}
are primarily caused by the following factors:

•

The cut range dependence for the
\Lambda\to p\pi^{-}
decay selection

We identified the
\Lambda\to p\pi^{-}
decays by investigating whether the
\Lambda
momenta obtained by production and decay kinematics are identical. Here, we used the index “
\Delta p
”, as described in Sec.
5
. The systematic uncertainty from the selection of the
\Lambda\to p\pi^{-}
decay was evaluated by changing the cut value for
\Delta p
from (-0.06, 0.04) to (-0.05, 0.03). As mentioned in Sec.
5
, since the central value of the
\Delta p
distribution is off due to the effect of insufficient energy calibration of BGO and CFT, an interval of 0.1 GeV/
c
was chosen to apply the
\Delta p
resolution symmetrically from the actual center.

•

The uncertainty of background cancelation in
\cos{\theta_{p}}
distribution

We subtracted the background in
\cos{\theta_{p}}
, primarily derived from multiple
\pi
production, by referring to the
K^{0}
flight length in the beam axis (
\Delta z_{K^{0}}
). Here, we estimated the mean value (
\mu_{BG}
) of the
\Delta z_{K^{0}}
distribution for the background events in the missing mass spectrum. Thereafter, we subtracted the
\cos{\theta_{p}}(\Delta z<\mu_{BG})
events from the
\cos{\theta_{p}}(\Delta z>\mu_{BG})
events, as shown in Fig.
15
. This background cancellation performance depends on the
\mu_{BG}
value. The systematic uncertainty due to the imperfection of background cancelation was evaluated by changing the value of
\mu_{BG}
to
\mu_{BG}\pm\sigma
, where
\sigma
is the standard deviation of the fitting function in Fig.
16
. Therefore, it could be said that the systematic error from varying the fitting range of
\Delta z
distribution is a conservative estimate.

•

The data sample dependence used at the
\cos{\theta_{p}}
fitting

We selected the data sample in the fitting of
\cos{\theta_{p}}
distribution to remove the overcorrected data point. The systematic uncertainty depending on the data selection was evaluated by fitting
\cos{\theta_{p}}
with all data points and remeasured
P_{\Lambda}
.

We estimated the relative error
\delta_{rel}
caused by each factor above using the absolute error
\delta_{abs}
of each as

\displaystyle\delta_{rel}

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{\Lambda,\ nominal}-P_{\Lambda,\ variation}}{P_{\Lambda,\ nominal}},

(14)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{abs}}{P_{\Lambda,\ nominal}},

(15)

where
P_{\Lambda,\ nominal}
is the final result,
P_{\Lambda,\ variation}
is the polarization value derived by changing each value of systematic uncertainty factors. Table
2
shows the estimated relative errors in each
\cos{\theta^{K0}_{CM}}
bin. Here,
\delta_{rel,\ purity}
,
\delta_{rel,\ \mu_{BG}+\sigma}
,
\delta_{rel,\ \mu_{BG}-\sigma}
,
\delta_{rel,\ fitting}
represent the relative systematic errors contributing to the purity of the
\Lambda\to p\pi^{-}
decay events, the uncertainty in the background estimatation of the
\cos{\theta_{p}}
distribution, and the uncertainty in the fitting of the
\cos{\theta_{p}}
distribution, respectively. The reason why
\delta_{rel,\ fitting}
s in the ranges of
0.6<\cos{\theta^{K0}_{CM}}<0.65
,
0.65<\cos{\theta^{K0}_{CM}}<0.7
,
0.7<\cos{\theta^{K0}_{CM}}<0.75
,
0.75<\cos{\theta^{K0}_{CM}}<0.8
, and
0.95<\cos{\theta^{K0}_{CM}}<1.0
are zero is that we used all data points for the fitting in these ranges according to the
\chi^{2}
test described in Sec.
8
.

The total systematic error in the
j
-th
\cos{\theta^{K0}_{CM}}
bin
\delta_{sys,\ j}

\displaystyle\delta_{sys,\ j}=\sqrt{\delta_{abs,\ purity,\ j}^{2}+\delta_{abs,\ \mu_{BG}+\sigma,\ j}^{2}+\delta_{abs,\ \mu_{BG}-\sigma,\ j}^{2}+\delta_{abs,\ fitting,\ j}^{2}}

(19)

(20)

where

Discussion

11.1
Polarization mechanism in the
\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}\Lambda
reaction

In the following, the discussion is based on the scattering theory described in Ref.
[
35
]
. Considering the scattering of a spin 0 system and a spin 1/2 system, such as the scattering of
\pi^{-}
mesons and protons, the spin matrix (scattering matrix) can be written as

M=f(\theta)+{\bm{\sigma}}\cdot{\bm{n}}g(\theta),

(21)

where
{\bm{\sigma}}
is the Pauli matrices, and
{\bm{n}}
is the normal vector of the reaction plane defined by the momentum vectors (
{\bm{p}}
and
{\bm{p}^{\prime}}
) in the initial and final states:

{\bm{n}}=\frac{{\bm{p}}\times{\bm{p}^{\prime}}}{|{\bm{p}}\times{\bm{p}^{\prime}}|}.

(22)

In Equation (
21
), the first term is called “non-spin flip term”, and the second is called “spin flip term”, respectively.

If the initial state is not polarized, the final state polarization vector
{\bm{P}_{f}}
can be written as

\displaystyle{\bm{P}_{f}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm Tr}(MM^{{\dagger}}{\sigma})}{{\rm Tr}(MM^{{\dagger}})}

(23)

\displaystyle=\frac{(f^{*}(\theta)g(\theta)+f(\theta)g^{*}(\theta)){\bm{n}}}{|f(\theta)|^{2}+|g(\theta)|^{2}}.

(24)

From the above, it can be said that the polarization of
\Lambda
produced by the
\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}\Lambda
reaction can be described as an interference between a non-spin flip term and a spin flip term. Equation (
24
) clearly shows that the polarization vanishes when either
f(\theta)
or
g(\theta)
is zero, as no interference is possible. Conversely,
{\bm{P}}_{f}
is maximized when both amplitudes are non-zero, of comparable magnitude, and have a relative phase that results in a large real part of the product
f^{*}(\theta)g(\theta)
. For example, if
f(\theta)
and
g(\theta)
are real and equal in magnitude, the polarization reaches its theoretical maximum of unity.

Therefore, the observed high polarization of the
\Lambda
hyperon in specific angular regions is not due to the dominance of the spin-flip amplitude alone, but rather to the constructive interference between the spin-flip and non-spin-flip components. This mechanism underscores the importance of partial-wave interference effects and highlights the sensitivity of hyperon polarization to the underlying resonance structure and angular momentum dynamics of the reaction.

11.2
Comparison with theoretical models

The Bonn-Gatchina (BnGa) multichannel PWA group
[
36
]
has indicated that one possible reason for the polarization of
\Lambda
in the
\pi^{-}p\to K^{0}\Lambda
reaction is the presence of the following nucleon resonances:
N(1710)\frac{1}{2}^{+}
and
N(1900)\frac{3}{2}^{+}
. The theoretical calculation from Ref.
[
5
]
, which investigated the nucleon resonances within a DCC model of
\pi N
and
\gamma N
reactions, shows a similar trend to our data. The DCC model they used was parameterized in simultaneous fits to the data of
\pi N\to\eta N,\ K\Lambda,\ K\Sigma
and
\gamma N\to\pi N,\ \eta N,\ K\Lambda,\ K\Sigma
up to
W=2.1
GeV and that of
\pi N\to\pi N
up to
W=2.3
GeV. While our
P_{\Lambda}
data are vital for fixing model parameters in these PWA studies, more spin observable data are necessary to discuss the specific reactions.
