The effectiveness of large language models in medical AI research for physicians: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yuanjun Shang, Yuanfan Lin, Ruiyang Li, Yuanrui Shang, Mingyuan Li, Lanqin Zhao, Ling Jin, Andi Xu, Dong Liu, Qianni Wu, Mingjie Luo, Jianyu Pang, Shaowei Bi, Yuchun He, Miaohong Xu, Xinwei Chen, Zizheng Cao, Sijia Yu, Jiaman Zhao, Yunxi Lai, Wenben Chen, Haotian Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryPhysicians offer invaluable clinical insights, but their involvement in medical AI research is hindered by limited technical expertise. We conduct a superiority, open-label, randomized controlled trial involving 64 junior ophthalmologists to undertake a 2-week project on “automated cataract identification” under minimal engineering assistance, with (intervention, n = 32) or without (control, n = 32) ChatGPT-3.5. The overall project completion rate is higher in intervention group than controls (87.5% vs. 25.0%; difference 62.5%, p = 9.42e−7), and the unassisted completion rate likewise (68.7% vs. 3.1%; difference 65.6%, p = 5.70e−8). The intervention group demonstrates better project planning and faster completion times (p < 0.01). After a 2-week washout, 41.2% of successful intervention participants complete a new project without the support of large language models (LLMs). A survey shows that 42.6% of participants fear regurgitating information without understanding and 40.4% worry about fostering lazy thinking, indicating potential dependency. Therefore, LLMs can help physicians overcome technical barriers, although long-term risks require further study.Trial registration: This study was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06015178).Keywords: large language models, LLMs, AI-augmented medical research, medical education, randomized controlled trial

Introduction

In recent years, interdisciplinary studies that foster collaborations across diverse scientific fields, such as biology, chemistry, physics, materials science, computer science, and engineering,1,2,3 have driven breakthroughs and sparked new avenues of growth across a wide range of scientific domains.4,5,6 A prime example can be seen in digital medicine, where the integration of knowledge and techniques from clinical practice, computer science, and other disciplines has led to remarkable advancements in enhancing healthcare delivery, increasing patient engagement, improving clinical outcomes, and optimizing healthcare systems.7,8,9 Although techniques like AI have shown enormous potential for applications in biomedical fields, their widespread adoption is greatly limited by technical barriers.10,11 While physicians contribute invaluable clinical insights and first-hand experience, their participation in problem-oriented research involving AI techniques can be greatly hindered by a lack of essential multidisciplinary expertise or skills, as well as limited access to engineering manpower.12,13 This challenge is particularly evident for small research groups or clinical teams situated in isolated hospital or university settings and for young physicians with limited access to research resources, interdisciplinary collaboration, and technical assistance.

Previous studies have investigated democratizing AI-augmented medical research by lowering the barriers to entry and making these advanced techniques more accessible to individuals or teams with limited technical expertise. For example, automated machine learning tools were introduced to simplify the process of deep learning model training for physicians without requiring coding skills.14,15 While these efforts to lower technical barriers do not imply that physicians can overlook teamwork or collaboration with engineering experts, they provide more opportunities and possibilities for clinical teams with limited access to engineering and technical resources, to explore, understand, and apply technologies such as AI in problem-oriented clinical scenarios. Moreover, they enable limited engineering resources to concentrate on complex tasks like developing new algorithms and architectures. This, in turn, promotes the adoption of innovative AI and digital techniques in biomedical research fields. However, beyond specific scenarios or tasks such as model training, real-world studies require the integration of cross-domain knowledge, comprehensive problem reasoning and analysis, protocol design, execution, and refinement, along with precise and scientifically rigorous reporting and presentation.16 Encompassing a vast decision-making space, these open-ended and exploratory tasks are often beyond the capabilities of many current tools or strategies designed for a relatively fixed pipeline.

The past few years have witnessed rapid innovations in generative AI with large language models (LLMs).17,18,19 LLMs can provide responses to queries across a broad spectrum of topics using text that approaches near-human quality, benefiting from the extensive volumes of free-text data available for model development. The ability of these generalized systems to complete complex and exploratory tasks highlights their substantial potential in supporting research, especially for physicians engaged in problem-oriented medical AI research. However, there is still a lack of evidence on the effectiveness of LLMs in this context. Additionally, it is crucial to explore and address the risks associated with the application of these innovative technologies.

In this superiority, open-label, randomized controlled trial, we aimed to validate the effectiveness of LLMs in supporting physicians with medical AI research. We enrolled 64 junior ophthalmologists without prior experience in research involving AI techniques or coding skills, to perform a 2-week project on “automated cataract identification” using slit-lamp images from scratch. This task involved developing a model for classifying whether a patient has cataracts based on the eye images. All participants were allowed to utilize traditional methods of information retrieval including search engines, literature, and books, for their projects, with (intervention group) or without (control group) the use of ChatGPT-3.5. Following a 2-week washout period with voluntary participation, participants in the control group who initially failed their projects were required to retry using ChatGPT-3.5, whereas successful participants in the intervention group embarked on a new project involving “automated detection of cloudy lenses” without the use of ChatGPT-3.5. This new task involved developing a model for identification of cataract and localization of cloudy lens region within the image through bounding boxes. An expert committee provided sequential and minimal assistance to participants in a blinded manner when participants encountered issues perceived as beyond their ability to resolve independently, aiming to assess the level of teamwork and engineering support required. The results of this study indicate that LLMs have substantial potential in supporting physicians and clinical teams to gain a deeper understanding of medical AI research and to overcome technical barriers in this process. Subsequently, these findings were utilized to develop corresponding guidelines for efficient LLM utilization. However, we also observed associated risks such as a potential tendency among physicians to rely on LLMs, necessitating further studies to better understand these behaviors.

Results

Participants and study workflow

We designed a superiority, open-label, randomized controlled trial to examine the effectiveness of LLMs in supporting junior ophthalmologists in conducting medical AI studies. Our predefined protocol was prospectively registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06015178), and the trial design is illustrated in Figure 1. Sixty-four participants were recruited from the Departments of Ophthalmology at four affiliated hospitals of the Sun Yat-sen University. None were excluded according to the eligibility criteria. A total of 64 junior ophthalmologists as training residents without prior experience in medical AI study or coding skills were randomly assigned at a 1:1 ratio (stratified by gender and intelligence scores), with all randomized participants included in the intention-to-treat (ITT) population (Figure 2). The overall mean age of the enrolled participants was 26.3 years (SD, 1.2 years), and 47% were females. The mean number of education years was 18.7 years (SD, 1.3 years), and the mean duration of clinical experience was 22.3 months (SD, 1.5 months). The baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1.

All participants were tasked with conducting a 2-week project from scratch with the goal of “automated cataract identification” using a slit-lamp image dataset, for which the participants were involved in project planning, execution, and results reporting (Figure S1) with (intervention group) or without (control group) the use of ChatGPT-3.5. Throughout the project, both groups were permitted to utilize search engines (such as Google), literature, and books for information retrieval. An expert committee blindly followed participants to provide sequential assistance when the participants were perceived to encounter issues that could not be resolved independently and remained stuck at a certain step for 2 days (Table S1). The primary outcome was the project completion rate (including the overall project completion rate and unassisted project completion rate), defined as the ratio of participants who completed the project in 2 weeks. Secondary outcomes assessed included levels of assistance, the feasibility of the project plans, the readability of the project reports, the performance in automated cataract identification, and the duration of the project. The trial endpoints were (1) completion of the project within 2 weeks, regardless of the total time spent or (2) failure to complete the project despite spending more than 20 h over a period exceeding 2 weeks. All participants completed the trial and adhered to the intervention.

Project completion rate

Regardless of the requirement for expert assistance, the overall project completion rate was 87.5% for the intervention group (28 of 32 participants), which was significantly superior to the 25.0% rate for the control group (8 of 32 participants), with a difference of 62.5% (95% confidence interval [CI], 43.6%–81.4%; p = 9.42e−7; Table 2; Figure 3A). Considering the participants who completed the project without assistance from the expert committee, the unassisted completion rate was 68.7% for the intervention group (22 of 32 participants), significantly superior to the 3.1% rate for the control group (1 of 32 participants), with a notable difference of 65.6% (95% CI, 48.4%–82.8%; p = 5.70e−8; Table 2; Figure 3A). Both differences surpassed the 20% superiority margin at their lower limits (Figure 3B). The distribution of participants across each step completion and assistance level is depicted in Figure S2. Among the 41 participants who sought any assistance during the trial, 65.9% requested help during the project plan design phase, and 92.7% sought assistance during model development. The majority of assistance requests came from the control group, accounting for 92.6% of the project plan design assistance and 76.3% of the model development assistance. Among the participants who did not complete the project (4 in the intervention group and 24 in the control group), 75.0% of the intervention participants believed that they could complete it with enough time (estimated mean, 33.3 h), whereas 79.1% of the control participants doubted their ability to complete the project even without time constraints.

Furthermore, we investigated the potential causes of project incompletion within the intervention group, and issues such as AI hallucinations (Figure S3) and a lack of effective questioning strategies for using LLMs (Figure S4) were observed. Consequently, we solicited advice on the effective utilization of LLMs from the successful participants in the intervention group via Delphi-like methods.20 We subsequently synthesized “CPGI” guidelines after extensive deliberations by the expert committee (Figure 4) to provide a logical and structured procedural framework to guide physicians in crafting effective prompts: briefly introduce the detailed background information as “context,” describe the “problem” clearly and accurately, break complex issues down into multiple “goals,” and provide “iterations” of the prompt until useful responses are obtained.

Plan feasibility and report readability scores

To comprehensively assess the role of LLMs throughout the project, the participants were tasked with designing a project plan at the beginning, then executing the plan, and compiling a report upon project completion. The feasibility of the project plans and the readability of the reports were blindly assessed by two members of the expert committee, and in the case of discrepancies, the scores were arbitrated by a third expert (see Tables S3 and S4 for criteria). Compared to the control group, the intervention group demonstrated significantly higher feasibility scores (median, 3.0; interquartile range [IQR], 3.0–4.0 vs. median, 2.0; IQR, 1.6–3.0; p = 1.49e−7) and a greater proportion of feasible plans (difference, 56.2%, 95% CI, 35.9%–76.5%; p = 8.75e−6) (Table 2). There was no significant difference in the report readability scores between the intervention and control groups (median, 6.0; IQR, 4.0–7.0 vs. median, 5.0; IQR, 4.3–6.0; p = 0.61; Table 2), indicating comparable logical correctness and precise expression of their reports.

Duration of project completion

Among the participants who completed the project, the intervention group had a significantly shorter project duration than the control group (mean, 17.23 h; SD, 6.90 h vs. mean, 25.31 h; SD, 4.33 h; p = 0.0013 Table 2). Both groups allocated more time to project plan design and model development (Figures 3D and S5). Compared with the control group, the intervention group spent less time on project plan design and data processing (project plan design: mean, 3.50 h; SD, 0.46 h vs. mean, 5.88 h; SD, 0.40 h; p = 8.39e−7; data processing: mean, 1.93 h; SD, 0.27 h vs. mean, 3.88 h; SD, 0.55 h; p = 8.04e−8). When comparing model training duration, no significant difference emerged between the intervention and control groups among all the participants who completed the project (mean, 10.82 h; SD, 5.46 h vs. mean, 14.43 h; SD, 4.47 h; p = 0.093). In contrast, for those without expert assistance, the intervention group (n = 22) completed model training in 10.05 ± 6.01 h, which was shorter than the control group’s sole unassisted completer (17.00 h), confirming the potential improvement of LLM on novice model training efficiency. Report writing duration showed no significant difference between groups (mean, 0.98 h; SD, 0.10 h vs. mean, 1.12 h; SD, 0.21 h; p = 0.51; Figures 3D and S5).

Performance of automated cataract identification

The participants who completed the project all developed AI algorithms or models. For the intervention group (n = 28; Figure S6; Table S5), accuracies ranged from 0.891 to 0.991 and area under the curves (AUCs) ranged from 0.966 to 0.998. For the control group (n = 8; Figure S7; Table S6), accuracies ranged from 0.873 to 0.996 and AUCs ranged from 0.947 to 1.000. While no statistically significant performance differences were observed between the two groups (accuracy p = 0.45, AUC p = 0.89; Figure 3G), these comparisons should be interpreted with appropriate caution given the limited number of completers in the control group (n = 8).

Additional trial

To further assess the benefits of using LLMs for medical AI studies by physicians, an additional trial was conducted with voluntary participation after a 2-week washout period. The baseline characteristics of the participants involved in this trial are provided in Table S2. Fifteen participants from the control group, who initially failed to complete the project, attempted it again using ChatGPT-3.5 and achieved an unassisted project completion rate of 66.7% and an overall project completion rate of 80.0%, with accuracies ranging from 0.883 to 0.992 and AUCs ranging from 0.958 to 1.000 (Figure S8; Table S7). Seventeen participants from the intervention group, who had successfully completed the initial project, undertook a new medical AI project focused on “automated detection of cloudy lenses” without the use of ChatGPT-3.5 (Figure 2), achieving an unassisted project completion rate of 41.2% and an overall project completion rate of 58.8%, with mean average precision (mAP) ranging from 0.975 to 0.985 (Table S8). This indicates that some participants in the intervention group gained an understanding of the principles and procedures for conducting medical AI research with LLM assistance and were able to apply them to a new project. However, while more than half of the participants completed the new project, 41.2% failed to complete it without LLM assistance, and all of them were stuck in the model training step. This may be because the new project involved a different task with a higher level of complexity, which was both unfamiliar and challenging for participants after only a short-term learning. Additionally, this finding suggests that some of the participants may have merely replicated the responses from the LLM instead of fully understanding the principles and procedures for performing medical AI research, indicating a potential tendency to depend on the LLM. To evaluate this tendency, a questionnaire survey was conducted among the 47 participants who used ChatGPT-3.5 during both the main and additional trials (Figure 5). A total of 82.98% of participants agreed that LLMs helped them learn new knowledge and skills in digital medicine and complete medical AI tasks previously considered unfeasible. However, 42.55% expressed concerns that using LLMs might lead to simply regurgitating responses without understanding or learning key points and 40.43% worried that using LLMs could encourage lazy thinking, resulting in a decline in their independent learning and work abilities.

Discussion

We conducted a superiority, open-label, randomized controlled trial, to explore the effectiveness of LLMs in supporting physicians with medical AI research. The initial phase of the trial demonstrated a significant improvement in unassisted project completion rate, with intervention physicians showing a 65.6% improvement over those who solely relied on traditional methods for information retrieval. With minimal technical assistance from human experts, the completion rate of the intervention group further increased from 68.7% to 87.5%. Additionally, participants from the control group who initially failed to complete their projects achieved an unassisted project completion rate of 66.7% and an overall completion rate of 80.0% using ChatGPT-3.5. Our findings demonstrate that LLMs can empower physicians to conduct problem-oriented medical AI research, providing clinical experts, especially those with limited access to engineering and technical resources, with more opportunities to explore, understand, and apply technologies such as AI in clinical scenarios.

Despite the advancements in digital medicine driven by AI technologies, increasing technical barriers have hindered many physicians and clinical teams from fully participating, leading to a technological divide. Previous efforts, such as model training without requiring coding skills or engineering expertise, aimed to broaden opportunities for physicians in AI-augmented research.14,15 While these efforts allow for the derivation of AI models without a deep understanding of mathematical, statistical, and programming principles, translating these technological successes into meaningful clinical impact requires concerted efforts and a careful stepwise approach to avoid bias. Furthermore, real-world studies necessitate comprehensive decision-making, planning, and execution based on interdisciplinary knowledge and expertise, beyond just programming or model training. To date, no flexible strategy or method has been reported that can work throughout or apply to the entire medical AI research project. In this study, we selected cataract identification, which encapsulates the fundamental workflow of medical AI research, as our evaluation task. Clinicians without prior AI experience were required to autonomously select technical approaches and resolve practical implementation hurdles with varying levels of expert committee support. Our results reveal that LLMs significantly facilitate physicians in conducting real-world medical AI research, enhancing the entire process from initial design through execution to result analysis and reporting. Notably, even without LLM assistance, nearly half of the participants successfully undertook new projects independently. This suggests that their gains may stem not only from the mechanical application of LLM responses but also from substantial learning and a deepened understanding of digital medicine and medical AI. Building on these advancements, future research should explore the role of LLMs in more demanding real-world scenarios requiring advanced exploration and optimization to comprehensively reveal their potential in fostering conceptual innovation and sustainable medical-engineering partnerships.

The application of LLMs could be pivotal in democratizing advanced algorithmic modeling and other digital techniques in healthcare, especially for young physicians and small research teams in isolated hospitals or universities. These groups often face significant barriers due to limited access to technical support, interdisciplinary collaboration opportunities, and research resources, which can hinder their involvement in medical AI research and innovation. Importantly, LLMs enable many clinical teams, especially young physicians, who previously had limited opportunities to engage in digital medicine, to make meaningful contributions. With strong access to clinical data within their own institutions, these researchers can develop models and techniques for internal use, such as research, triage, and customized care delivery. By democratizing these processes, LLMs may also reduce the need for costly external validation across multiple devices, patient populations, and clinician practices.14 This contrasts with the traditional paradigm of digital medicine, where large teams dominate the development of intelligent technologies and standardized models that require extensive or even global validation. Small-scale, context-specific data approaches, tailored to particular patient populations in individual clinics, are equally valuable but often overlooked in favor of larger, more generalized studies. Our study highlights how LLMs can lower technical barriers for researchers, particular young physicians, and empower them to overcome systemic challenges, allowing them to actively contribute to medical AI development. This could be the niche where LLMs play a transformative role in the field of digital medicine. Furthermore, it is important to emphasize that while the current study focused on young physicians, future research should aim to expand the participant pool to ensure the broader applicability and generalizability of these findings.

However, this advancement does not mean that we can overlook the importance of teamwork and collaboration among clinical, engineering, and algorithm experts. Digital medicine studies, such as biomedical AI projects, require more than simply patching together ideas, perspectives, and techniques from various fields. The advent of LLMs not only opens doors for teams that previously lacked resources but also enhances the technological expertise of clinical experts already engaged in the games. This improvement facilitates a more effective application of digital technologies from a clinical perspective, leading to more efficient and productive teamwork and collaboration with engineering experts. Additionally, it allows engineering and AI professionals to focus on more challenging tasks, such as technological breakthroughs and innovation. This enhancement is likely to boost the teamwork and cooperation, further promoting the widespread adoption of digital techniques in healthcare.

Although the scope of LLM applications is gradually increasing, bringing vast prospects, some potential risks and concerns were observed during our trial, as evidenced by the percentages of failed cases and questionnaire surveys regarding LLM usage. First, AI hallucinations were a potential issue, with participants encountering outputs containing false information generated by the LLMs. Previous studies have shown that AI tools can produce nonexistent references or even fabricate and falsify data.21,22 These hallucinations are often attributed to insufficient or biased training data and overfitting within AI models.23 Although few cases of AI hallucinations were observed in this study, larger-scale studies are needed to examine the prevalence and impact. In addition, improving the quality of training data, enhancing training algorithms, implementing robust verification methods, and integrating human supervision to detect and correct erroneous outputs in a timely manner are necessary steps for future development. Second, LLMs might lead to the degradation of human capabilities.24 A study by Ahmad et al. indicated that using AI could increase the loss of human decision-making capabilities and induce human laziness.25 We investigated whether LLMs might cause a dependency tendency, and participants expressed concerns. Our results suggest that some participants might not have truly grasped the principles and processes of medical AI projects and merely replicated responses from ChatGPT-3.5, indicating a possible dependency on LLMs. Future research is needed to assess dependency related to the long-term use of LLMs and to develop relevant usage regulations or policies. Third, we found that ineffective or poorly crafted prompts and questioning strategies are likely to yield unsatisfactory results. To instruct physicians, we synthesized some strategies for crafting effective prompts into “CPGI” guidelines, which include incorporating detailed context (C), clearly articulating the problem (P), breaking down complex issues into manageable goals (G), and engaging in iterative optimization (I). The strength of these guidelines lies in its structured interaction framework, which simplifies complex issues into simple goals, thereby enhancing the precision and relevance of LLM responses and consequently improving their ability to address intricate challenges. Nevertheless, the CPGI guideline should be regarded as a preliminary output requiring future validation rather than a proven tool, as comprehensive validation of this framework is beyond the scope of this work and remains an avenue for future research.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, our use of ChatGPT-3.5—a general-purpose rather than domain-specific or newer-generation LLM—reflects a deliberate trade-off between model capability and real-world accessibility in clinical settings. While this choice ensured broad applicability for resource-constrained medical researchers, it may constrain the upper bound of technical performance achievable. Importantly, the robust improvement in project completion and demonstrated workflow transferability suggest that these benefits represent fundamental advantages of LLM-assisted research frameworks rather than artifacts of specific model capabilities. Looking ahead, next-generation models (e.g., GPT-4o, DeepSeek-R1) with enhanced medical domain alignment and reasoning capacities may amplify both the potential benefits (e.g., deeper conceptual understanding) and risks (e.g., more sophisticated hallucinations) of such approaches, highlighting the need for ongoing evaluation as the technology evolves. Second, although we used a general model, the tasks we utilized in this study can be included in the training process of the model, posing a risk of data leakage and potentially exaggerating the effectiveness of the LLM. However, we show that physicians in the intervention group can complete new tasks and achieve a 41.2% unassisted project completion rate even after the withdrawal of ChatGPT-3.5, which supports the transferability of the expertise and skills acquired from LLM. While “automated cataract identification” data may have been included in ChatGPT-3.5’s training data, it was trained only on textual data and lacks the crucial image data needed for cataract identification. To verify this, we further validated ChatGPT-3.5’s sustained effectiveness by masking label information in dataset descriptions across three different pathophysiological states, and our results still support the effectiveness of LLMs (Figure S9). Third, while the 2-week experimental period can present challenges for completing a comprehensive AI research project—potentially leading to underestimated completion rates—this duration was carefully determined through preliminary testing and empirically validated by our findings: 87.5% of LLM-assisted participants successfully completed their projects, whereas 79.1% of control group non-completers reported inability to finish even without time constraints. These results demonstrate that while the time frame was demanding, it remained achievable with appropriate LLM support, and the control group’s difficulties primarily reflected lack of guidance rather than insufficient time. Furthermore, although our additional trials identified potential LLM dependency patterns, the brief observation period highlights the need for more extensive longitudinal studies to fully characterize these effects. The current findings, nevertheless, provide meaningful and initial evidence of LLMs’ capacity to accelerate medical AI research competency development within clinically feasible time frames, and more rigorous and long-term studies are required to explore these findings further. Fourth, although our primary focus on young physicians within a similar age range may limit the generalizability of the findings, this approach allowed us to isolate the effect of the intervention without the confounding variable of age. As such, the main conclusion regarding the effectiveness of LLMs remains valid. In future studies, it is necessary to expand the participant pool to include a more diverse range of individuals to further assess the generalizability of our findings. Fifth, we acknowledge that the open-label design, an inherent requirement for LLM intervention studies, can introduce potential motivational bias due to participants’ awareness of ChatGPT-3.5 access. However, our effort calibration analyses showed no significant difference in total time invested by non-completers between the groups (intervention group: mean, 22.38 ± SD, 1.80 h; control group: mean, 23.25 ± SD, 1.76 h, p = 0.355). This finding suggests that awareness of LLM availability did not substantially influence participant motivation or effort expenditure in our study. Moreover, strict one-on-one communication between clinical research coordinators and participants prevented cross-participant information exchange, and expert committee members remained blinded to participant group assignments, thereby minimizing motivational bias.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that LLMs can effectively assist physicians in overcoming the expertise and technical barriers related to the design, execution, and reporting of medical AI projects. However, we also observed certain risks associated with the use of LLMs, such as AI hallucinations and potential tendency to depend on LLMs. Further research is needed to assess the risks of dependency formation related to the long-term use of LLMs.

Experimental model and study participant details

We designed a superiority, open-label, randomized controlled trial to examine the effectiveness of LLMs in supporting junior ophthalmologists in conducting AI-augmented research. This study was approved by the ethics committee of ZOC and prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06015178). The trial was conducted in Guangzhou, China, in compliance with the principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration. Written informed consent was obtained from participants before trial. No commercial interests were involved in the trial, and the authors verified the accuracy and completeness of the data as well as the fidelity of the trial to the predefined protocol.

Between September 8, 2023, and September 28, 2023, participants were recruited through social media channels and campus communication at the Department of Ophthalmology in four affiliated hospitals of Sun Yat-sen University, including Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center, the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University, and the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. Participants were screened for eligibility through self-completion of an online questionnaire, including demographic information, clinical experience in ophthalmology, experience in projects involving digital medicine, and coding skills. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 20–28 years old, regardless of gender; 12–36 months of clinical experience in ophthalmology; no prior experience in AI-augmented research or coding; self-reporting as having no less than 20 h available for participation during the trial period; agreeing to participate in this study and sign informed consent. The exclusion criteria encompassed individuals with reading difficulties or disorders that could impede trial completion. Before the start of the study, written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Method details

Randomization and blinding

Before the study commenced, each participant completed Raven’s Advanced Progressive Matrices (APM) test (Raven, Court, & Raven, 1983)27 to obtain baseline intelligence information. Participants were categorized based on their scores, with the top 50% designated as high scorers and the bottom 50% as low scorers. The randomization, conducted anonymously by a statistician, involved pre-stratifying participants based on gender and APM scores (high or low) using IBM SPSS Analytic Server 3.5 and then allocating them at a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group or the control group at the individual level.

Participants in the intervention group were required to develop automated cataract identification models with the use of ChatGPT-3.5 and through other traditional information retrieval methods, including search engines (such as Google), literature, and books, as needed. Participants in the control group were required to develop the models through traditional information retrieval methods without the use of ChatGPT-3.5 (Figure 1). An expert committee blindly followed participants to provide sequential assistance when the participants were perceived to encounter issues that could not be resolved on his or her own and remained stuck at a certain step for two days (Extended Data Figure 1A).

Each participant was contacted and managed separately by a CRC, had no direct interaction with each other, and was unaware of the other participants’ names or contact details. Two CRCs were each responsible for contacting half of the participants from both the intervention and control groups. The CRCs provided the participants’ materials to the expert committee after they were anonymized, and the assisting expert committee was blinded to the group assignment throughout the study. However, the CRCs could not be fully masked to the intervention as they might be notified of the allocation through participant contact or by reviewing the materials submitted.

Procedures

All participants were tasked with conducting a two-week project from scratch with the goal of “automated cataract identification” using a slit-lamp image dataset. Before the trial, the participants in the intervention group were instructed to register their ChatGPT-3.5 accounts and access the platform through a web page for assistance. They were also allowed to utilize traditional methods of information retrieval including search engines (such as Google), literature, or books as needed. In the control group, the participants were prohibited from using LLMs and were limited to using traditional information retrieval methods. Any tools or resources not mentioned above were required to report to the CRCs before use and could only be used after evaluation by the expert committee to verify that they would not contradict the intervention protocols.

The slit-lamp image dataset used for this study was sourced from an ongoing national cataract screening initiative facilitated by the CMAAI.26 Data annotations were independently completed by two ophthalmologists with more than five years of experience, and labels were determined if both ophthalmologists came to agreement, whereas a senior ophthalmologist with more than 10 years of experience arbitrated any inconsistencies. Two separate datasets were employed, one for the participants to conduct the project and the other for the engineers to test the results. The dataset for participants included 3,064 slit-lamp photographs, comprising 776 images of normal lenses, 1,144 images of cataracts with varying severity and etiology, and 1,144 images of postoperative eyes. These images were initially assigned random numerals without any label information and shuffled within a folder when provided to participants. The test set for the engineer included 778 slit-lamp photographs of normal lenses (188), cataracts (295), and postoperative eyes (295), that was independent of the dataset provided to the participants.

Before the trail, the participants underwent standardized one-on-one training on generic tool operation without any task-specific guidance (e.g., cataract AI concepts). The intervention group received 30 min of training on the effective utilization of ChatGPT-3.5, including understanding the capabilities of ChatGPT-3.5, and learning how to formulate effective queries, and another 30 min of training on the effective utilization of search engines such as Google, focusing on understanding search strategies, using advanced search operators, and evaluating search results. The control group took 30 min of training on the effective utilization of search engines only. All the participants were then given a practical test of the training content unrelated to digital medicine; any participants who failed the test were able to receive a second training session prior to taking the test again.

After training, the participants were required to complete the project from scratch in three steps: 1) devising a structured project plan, 2) executing the project plan to develop an “automated cataract identification” model capable of diagnosing whether a patient has cataract based on the eye image, and 3) writing a structured abstract to report the results (Figure S1). The participants devised their project plans on the basis of a structured template in Tencent Docs (an online document application) including three parts: the main techniques used, the necessary resources or tools needed, and the key steps. The participants who mentioned computing power requirements for the necessary resources or tools would obtain remote access to the Tianhe-2 supercomputer. Notably, this access excluded pre-configured deep learning frameworks or coding environments on the server. To simulate the data annotation process in a practical project, participants who mentioned data annotation or labeling (or other synonyms) received an updated dataset with images divided into three categories and stored in corresponding folders: “normal”, “cataract”, and “post-surgery”. Participants recorded the progress of the daily experiments in the Tencent Docs each day. When participants completed the project with an AUC >0.8 and no errors according to a blind evaluation by engineers, they wrote a structured summary of 600 words or less, including the purpose, methods, results, and conclusion, to report their results.

During the trial, the CRCs separately communicated with each participant via WeChat (a chat software) and monitored their progress on the basis of the materials they reported in their Tencent Docs, which were private and viewable only by the participants themselves and their corresponding CRCs. The CRCs followed the participants and tracked any updates of experimental records via Tencent Docs three times a day at 09:00, 15:00, and 21:00. After anonymizing the updated experimental records, the CRCs provided them to the three-member assisting expert committee for blinded assessment. The expert committee comprised an ophthalmic professor, a digital medical expert, and a senior engineer. If a participant was assessed by the expert committee to encounter issues that could not be solved on his or her own and remained stuck on a task step for up to two days, an assistance program managed by the expert committee would be immediately triggered. Throughout the assistance process, the expert committee refrained from direct communication with the participants. Instead, the CRCs provided information regarding the issues encountered by the participants, along with their experimental records, to the expert committee. This information was provided after masking the participant’s group and personal details. The expert committee was then tasked with providing a response within two hours. The assistance was divided into two levels: level-1, which entailed theoretical guidance, including explanations of concepts and background knowledge, and level-2, which offered more hands-on support, such as providing code for setting up a deep learning environment configuration or revising the participant’s code (specifically tailored to the project’s protocol). At the beginning of the assistance program, the expert committee provided level-1 assistance; if the participant was still unable to solve the problem within two days after level-1 assistance, the expert committee provided level-2 assistance.

After the main trial, we investigated the potential causes of project incompletion within the intervention group and used a Delphi-like method20 to gather advices on how to efficiently utilize LLMs in medical AI research from the successful participants in the intervention group. In the first round, all participants were required to submit their advices independently, which were then compiled, anonymized, and redistributed to the group as new materials. In the second round, the participants reviewed the materials, revised the advices, and resubmitted them. The third round followed the same procedure as the second round. After extensive deliberations of these advices by the expert committee, we synthesized guidelines for a logical and structured procedural framework to guide physicians for efficient LLM utilization.

Endpoint

The trial endpoints were 1) completion of the project within two weeks, regardless of the total time spent, or 2) failure to complete the project despite spending more than 20 h over a period exceeding two weeks.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the project completion rate (including overall project completion rate and unassisted project completion rate), defined as the proportion of participants who successfully completed the project in two weeks. The overall completion rate refers to all participants who finished the project, while unassisted completion rate includes only those who finished without expert assistance. Project completion was defined by the successful development of a model capable of identifying cataracts without errors, as verified by engineers in the test set (AUC >0.8). The secondary outcomes included assistance levels, plan feasibility scores, report readability scores, performance of automated cataract identification, and project duration.

Assistance levels were graded according to the degree of expert committee assistance required during the trial, sequentially from low to high as level-0 (none), level-1 (minimal), and level-2 (significant).

The plan feasibility and the report readability were independently scored by two experts on anonymous and randomly ranked plans or reports. The scores were determined in cases of consistency, whereas a third expert arbitrated the inconsistent scores. If the project plan was theoretically infeasible, the score was set to zero. Otherwise, a score of 1–4 points was assigned on the basis of the correctness and completeness of the key steps and details, with higher scores indicating greater feasibility; plans with scores of three or more were categorized as feasible plans (Table S3). The report consisted of four sections: the purpose, methods, results, and conclusions. Each section was separately scored and the sum constituted the report readability score. For each section, a score of zero was assigned for disorganized logic, a score of one was assigned for minor logical errors or imprecise expressions, and a score of two was assigned for correct logic and precise expression (Table S4).

The performance measures of automated cataract identification, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV) and AUC, was evaluated by engineers in the test set.

The project duration was obtained from the time recorded and reported by the participants. Each time when the participants performed the experiment, the duration was recorded using a unified timing software named Pomotodo and reported in their Tencent Docs.

Additional trials

To further verify the effectiveness of ChatGPT-3.5 in facilitating knowledge and skill acquisition, we conducted additional trials after a two-week washout period immediately following the main trial, adhering to the principle of voluntary participation. In the control group, the participants who failed to complete the project even after level-2 assistance attempted to retry the project with the assistance of ChatGPT-3.5. In the intervention group, the participants who had successfully completed the project were assigned a new project on “automated detection of cloudy lenses” (requiring identification of cataract presence and localization of cloudy lens region within the image through bounding boxes), without LLM assistance. The dataset utilized for this new project was generated from the same dataset employed in the main trial, which was annotated by ophthalmologists by marking the pupil area of the images with rectangular boxes. For evaluating performance on the “automated detection of cloudy lenses” task, multiple metrics including mAP at 0.75 intersection over union (IoU) threshold (mAP@0.75), recall, and precision were employed. The additional trials followed the same experimental methodology as the main trial. After the additional trials, we administered a questionnaire to participants who used ChatGPT-3.5 during the trial (32 participants in the intervention group of the main trial and 15 participants in the control group of the additional trials) to assess their tendency to depend on ChatGPT-3.5 when conducting interdisciplinary projects such as those encountered in digital medicine.

Quantification and statistical analyses

The trial was designed as a superiority study with a prespecified superiority margin of 20%. Based on the preliminary study results, the overall project completion rate was anticipated to be 80% in the intervention group and 20% in the control group, while the unassisted completion rate was expected to be 60% and 5%, respectively. A continuity-corrected Z-test for superiority was conducted with 80% power and a one-sided alpha level of 0.025. Required sample sizes for both primary outcomes were calculated independently, and the larger result was selected as the final sample size—60 participants in total (30 per group). Sample size estimation was performed using PASS (version 16).

The study analyses followed a comprehensive, prespecified statistical analysis plan by two of the coauthors (Ling Jin and Lanqin Zhao, both statisticians), who were unaware of the group assignment. Baseline characteristics are summarized descriptively without comparison. Statistical analyses were performed on an ITT basis, and all statistical tests had a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. The primary outcomes of the overall and unassisted project completion rate, according to the superiority tests, were assessed by calculating two-sided 95% CIs of the difference between the two groups. If the lower limit of the interval exceeded the superiority margin by 20%, the intervention was regarded as superior. For baseline characteristics in both groups, continuous variables that followed a normal distribution are presented as the mean ± standard deviation, nonnormally distributed continuous variables are presented as the median (25th to 75th percentile), and categorical values are presented as counts (percentages). Kaplan‒Meier curves were generated and plotted to depict the completion rates of both groups at each time point, accounting for both individuals who completed the projects and all participants. Comparisons of numerical variables between groups were performed with the t-tests (normally distributed) or rank-sum tests (non-normally distributed). For dichotomous variables, comparisons were performed using Fisher’s exact tests. Multiple comparisons are corrected with Benjamini-Hochberg correction. All analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Analytic Server 3.5.
