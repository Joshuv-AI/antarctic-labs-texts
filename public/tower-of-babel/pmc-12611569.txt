Moyamoya Disease in an Eight‐Year‐Old Female Child—A Case Report
Authors: Disha Thapa, Sai Priya Nimmagadda, Betsy Annie Philip, Shreya Milind Kawade, Abhisar Rohila, Mihirkumar P. Parmar, Saleh Mohammed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAn eight‐year‐old girl presented with a first episode of focal seizure and transient loss of consciousness. Brain MRI revealed a large subacute infarct in the left fronto‐parieto‐temporal region, and MR angiography showed bilateral supraclinoid internal carotid and proximal posterior cerebral artery narrowing with extensive basal collateral formation, consistent with Moyamoya disease. The absence of aneurysm or arteriovenous malformation excluded other vascular anomalies. She was managed with hypertonic saline, intravenous antibiotics, and antiplatelet therapy, followed by stabilization and referral for surgical revascularization. This case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for Moyamoya disease in children presenting with new‐onset seizures or stroke‐like episodes. Early neuroimaging and multidisciplinary management are essential to prevent further ischemic injury and optimize neurological outcomes.

Introduction

Moyamoya disease (MMD) is a chronic occlusive cerebrovascular disorder characterized by progressive narrowing of the terminal internal carotid arteries and their proximal branches, leading to impaired cerebral perfusion. This hypoperfusion is compensated by the development of fragile basal collateral vessels, which produce the characteristic “puff of smoke” appearance on cerebral angiography [1, 2]. Anterior circulation is more commonly affected than the posterior, and bilateral involvement is typical, although unilateral disease may also occur. According to the 2021 international diagnostic criteria, both unilateral and bilateral cases are now classified as MMD, whereas Moyamoya syndrome refers to similar angiographic changes secondary to other conditions such as sickle cell anemia or cranial irradiation [3]. Contralateral progression has been reported in up to 40% of initially unilateral cases [4].

The exact etiology remains unclear, but genetic predisposition plays a key role. Variants in the RNF213 gene, particularly p.Arg4810Lys, are strongly associated with MMD and partly explain its higher prevalence in East Asian populations compared to Western cohorts [5, 6]. Epidemiological studies report the highest incidence in Japan (approximately 3 per 100,000), while the incidence in the United States is far lower (0.086 per 100,000) [2]. MMD shows a bimodal age distribution, with a pediatric peak between 5 and 15 years and an adult peak between 30 and 40 years [7, 8].

MMD can be misdiagnosed or detected late, particularly in children because of its rarity and nonspecific clinical manifestations, including seizures, stroke, or transient ischemic attacks. Here, we present the case of an 8‐year‐old girl with Moyamoya disease, reported in accordance with the CARE guidelines [9].

Case Presentation

An eight‐year‐old girl with no significant past medical history was brought to the emergency department with a two‐day history of fever, dry cough, and an episode of focal seizure involving the left side of her body, lasting approximately 20 min, followed by transient loss of consciousness. There was no prior history of seizures, cerebrovascular disease, or head trauma. Family history was negative for epilepsy, hypertension, or similar neurological disorders.

On arrival, the patient was afebrile and hemodynamically stable but exhibited reduced responsiveness (GCS 10/15). Neurological examination revealed upper motor neuron facial weakness, increased tone, brisk reflexes, and extensor plantar responses on the right side, with normal cranial nerve and sensory findings. Other systemic examinations were unremarkable except for mild splenomegaly.

Investigations

Initial blood investigations demonstrated severe microcytic hypochromic anemia (hemoglobin 4.5 g/dL, serum iron 8 μg/dL, ferritin 13.77 ng/mL) and elevated SGOT 54 IU/L, CRP positivity, and serum creatinine 0.35 mg/dL. Thalassemia screening, hemoglobin electrophoresis, sickling test, and TB GeneXpert were all negative. Urine analysis was normal.

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed elevated protein (112.98 mg/dL) with normal glucose and cell counts, excluding meningitis.

Chest X‐ray and abdominal ultrasonography revealed mild splenomegaly but no pulmonary or hepatic abnormalities.

Neuroimaging

CT brain revealed an ill‐defined hypodense area in the left fronto‐parieto‐temporal lobe and insular cortex, consistent with a subacute infarct, and gliotic changes with volume loss in the right high fronto‐parietal cortex, indicating an old ischemic insult.

MRI brain confirmed a large subacute infarct involving the left fronto‐parieto‐temporal lobe and gangliocapsular region (Figure 1).

MR angiography (MRA) demonstrated bilateral supraclinoid internal carotid artery and proximal posterior cerebral artery narrowing, with multiple basal collateral vessels replacing the circle of Willis, characteristic of Moyamoya disease. No aneurysm or arteriovenous malformation was seen.

Diagnostic Assessment

Based on imaging and clinical findings, the patient fulfilled the 2021 international diagnostic criteria for Moyamoya disease. There was no evidence of an underlying systemic disorder, thus excluding Moyamoya syndrome.

Management and Outcomes

The patient was treated with hypertonic saline (3% at 6 mL/h) for cerebral edema, intravenous ceftriaxone and amoxiclav for empirical coverage, intravenous phenytoin for seizure control, and antiplatelet therapy (aspirin 75 mg once daily) as secondary stroke prophylaxis. A packed red blood cell transfusion was given to correct severe anemia. The patient's condition improved progressively, and she was discharged in stable condition with GCS 15/15.

At follow‐up after 3 months, she remained asymptomatic with no recurrent neurological episodes. Long‐term follow‐up every 3 months was planned, and referral for surgical revascularization (encephalo‐duro‐arterio‐synangiosis) was made to prevent future ischemic events.

Interventions

Upon admission, the patient received hypertonic saline (3% NaCl at 6 mL/h) to manage cerebral edema and maintain osmotic balance. Empirical broad‐spectrum intravenous antibiotics were initiated: Ceftriaxone (75 mg/kg/day) and amoxicillin‐clavulanate (30 mg/kg every 8 h) to prevent secondary infections in the context of fever and elevated inflammatory markers. Intravenous phenytoin was administered for acute seizure control and continued as maintenance therapy.

Because of severe microcytic anemia (Hb 4.5 g/dL), a packed red‐blood‐cell transfusion was performed, followed by nutritional counseling and iron supplementation. The patient started on aspirin 75 mg once daily as an antiplatelet agent for secondary stroke prophylaxis, in accordance with pediatric dosing recommendations. Antipyretics were used to control fever, and supportive care including hydration, head elevation, and continuous neurological monitoring was provided throughout hospitalization.

After stabilization, the child was referred to a tertiary neurosurgical center for evaluation of indirect revascularization surgery (encephalo‐duro‐arterio‐synangiosis, EDAS) to enhance cerebral perfusion and prevent recurrence of ischemic events.

Follow‐Up and Outcomes

During the initial inpatient course, the patient demonstrated progressive neurological improvement, regaining full orientation with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15/15 at discharge. No recurrent seizures or new deficits were noted.

At follow‐up visits conducted monthly for 3 months, the patient remained clinically stable, attending school with normal physical activity and no new focal neurological signs. Subsequent follow‐ups were scheduled every 3 months. Continued low‐dose aspirin and physiotherapy were advised, with periodic neuroimaging planned to monitor cerebral circulation and collateral development.

The neurosurgical team recommended elective EDAS once hematological parameters normalized. Long‐term follow‐up aims to evaluate surgical outcomes and neurocognitive recovery.

Discussion

Moyamoya disease (MMD) is a progressive occlusive cerebrovascular disorder characterized by stenosis of the terminal internal carotid arteries and the circle of Willis, with the subsequent development of fragile basal collateral networks. The condition shows a distinct female predominance, with a female‐to‐male ratio between 1:1.8 and 1:2.2, consistent with our patient, an eight‐year‐old girl [10]. MMD displays a bimodal age distribution, with incidence peaks in children aged 5–9 years and adults aged 45–49 years [11]. Although its precise etiology remains unclear, genetic susceptibility, particularly RNF213 gene variants, is implicated, while our patient demonstrated no familial involvement [12].

MMD may occur as an isolated idiopathic entity or in association with other systemic conditions such as tuberculosis, sickle cell disease, neurofibromatosis type 1, Down syndrome, Turner syndrome, Marfan syndrome, cranial irradiation, or chronic meningitis. When associated with these disorders, the term Moyamoya syndrome is used [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. In our case, hematological and imaging evaluations ruled out secondary causes, confirming Moyamoya disease.

Clinical manifestations vary widely, leading to diagnostic delay. Pediatric patients typically present with ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attacks (TIAs), while adults more often develop hemorrhagic events. Seizures, as in our case, may occur secondary to cortical ischemia or infarction [11, 12, 18]. Such nonspecific symptoms often mimic epilepsy or infectious encephalopathy, emphasizing the need to consider MMD in children with new‐onset focal neurological deficits or seizures.

Diagnosis requires demonstration of unilateral or bilateral steno‐occlusive changes in the distal internal carotid arteries and proximal branches of the circle of Willis [10]. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) remains the diagnostic gold standard, but its invasive nature limits its use in children. Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) and computed tomography angiography (CTA) are preferred noninvasive alternatives and, together with MRI, effectively identify vascular stenosis and compensatory collateral networks [11]. High‐resolution vessel wall MRI is an emerging tool for detecting early pathological vessel‐wall changes [10, 19].

In our case, MRI revealed subacute infarction involving the left fronto‐parieto‐temporal region, while MRA showed bilateral supraclinoid internal carotid and proximal posterior cerebral artery narrowing with multiple basal collaterals, fulfilling the 2021 diagnostic criteria for MMD. The absence of aneurysms or arteriovenous malformations excluded other vascular pathologies.

Management primarily focuses on preventing recurrent ischemic events and improving cerebral perfusion. Antiplatelet therapy, particularly low‐dose aspirin, is recommended to reduce stroke risk, although its efficacy remains debated as MMD lacks endothelial damage typical of atherosclerotic disease [11]. Surgical revascularization remains the most effective intervention, with options including indirect, direct, or combined techniques. Indirect methods, such as encephalo‐duro‐arterio‐synangiosis (EDAS), promote collateral vessel formation by placing vascularized tissue over ischemic cortex, whereas direct bypass connects branches of the external carotid artery to cortical vessels, providing immediate reperfusion [17, 18, 20, 21, 22].

Our patient was stabilized medically and referred for elective EDAS surgery following hematological correction. Early surgical intervention in pediatric patients has been associated with improved functional outcomes and lower recurrence rates compared to conservative management [11, 21, 23]. Nevertheless, prognosis varies depending on the extent of collateralization and timing of intervention. This case highlights the importance of early suspicion, prompt neuroimaging, and multidisciplinary collaboration to optimize outcomes in children with Moyamoya disease.

Conclusion

This case shows the diagnostic challenge of pediatric Moyamoya disease presenting initially with seizures and ischemic infarction. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for MMD when evaluating unexplained neurological deficits in children. Prompt neuroimaging (particularly MRI and MRA) is crucial for early identification, while surgical revascularization remains the cornerstone for preventing recurrence and long‐term neurological impairment. Early recognition, multidisciplinary management, and regular follow‐up can markedly improve outcomes in this rare but treatable cerebrovascular disorder.
