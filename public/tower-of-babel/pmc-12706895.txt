Resilience, physical activity and life satisfaction in Greek nursing students: the mediating role of body appreciation
Authors: Georgios Manomenidis, Dimitrios Kosmidis, Thalia Bellali, Savvato Karavasileiadou, Giannis Mavrogiannakis, Vasiliki Georgousopoulou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNursing students are more likely to experience psychological distress and face emotional challenges due to early exposure to clinical settings and direct patient care. Resilience and life satisfaction are critical for academic success and future occupational longevity. This study examined the relationship between resilience, physical activity, and life satisfaction, focusing on the mediating role of body appreciation.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 324 nursing students from all accredited nursing departments in Greece. Participants completed a battery of questionnaires, including the Body Appreciation Scale (BAS-2), the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-10), the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), and the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS). Data were analyzed using structural equation modeling (SEM).ResultsBody appreciation (β = 0.46) and resilience (β = 0.38) were positively associated with life satisfaction. Body appreciation partially mediated the association between resilience and life satisfaction (indirect effect: β = 0.22). In contrast, higher physical activity was weak but significantly associated with lower life satisfaction both directly (β = −0.14, p = 0.006), and indirectly through lower body appreciation (indirect effect: β = -0.12). Fit index values according to the model were χ² = 1018.582, p < 0.001; CMIN/DF = 3.525; CFI = 0.879 and RMSEA = 0.088 (90% CI: 0.083 to 0.094), suggesting an acceptable but not optimal fit.ConclusionsResilience and body appreciation were positively associated with life satisfaction among nursing students, whereas the role of physical activity proved negative and more complex than expected. Interventions that foster body appreciation and resilience, together with strategies that cultivate intrinsic motivation for physical activity should be integrated into nursing curricula and student support programs, rigorously evaluated and further examined in future longitudinal and intervention studies.Clinical trial number Not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04168-5.

Background

University students encounter various challenges in college related to academic performance, employment, self-perception, test anxiety, and relationship issues, which can heighten vulnerability to mental health problems [1, 2]. Within modern positive psychology, psychological constructs such as resilience, life satisfaction, and body image are viewed as central “building blocks” of psychological well-being [3, 4]. These constructs are highly interconnected and are influenced by a range of cultural, social, and developmental factors [5].

Life satisfaction defined as “an individual’s overall cognitive assessment of their life quality” [6], is particularly relevant for students in demanding professional programs such as nursing. Nursing students encounter high academic workloads, early exposed to clinical settings, and emotionally challenging patient care situations, all of which compromise their well-being and future professional sustainability [7]. Maintaining satisfactory levels of life satisfaction is therefore essential for sustaining motivation, preventing burnout and supporting long-term occupational engagement in healthcare [8].

Body image is a multidimensional concept involving perceptions, thoughts, and feelings about one’s body [9]. Within this framework, positive body image, or body appreciation, has gained empirical support as a psychological construct. It reflects respect for and acceptance of the body, irrespective of societal beauty ideals [10, 11]. Individuals with high levels of body appreciation demonstrate greater emotional resilience, exhibit healthier eating behaviors, and face a lower risk of internalizing feelings that could lead to disorders such as depression and anxiety [5, 12–14].

Resilience, conceptualized within Lazarus and Folkman’s Transactional Stress and Coping Model [15], reflects a dynamic capacity to adapt successfully to stress and adversity by influencing how individuals appraise and cope with demands. Higher resilience is consistently associated with better mental health and greater life satisfaction in emerging adulthood [2, 16]. In health care students and professionals, resilience is increasingly recognized as a key protective factor against stress and training related strain [17].

Physical activity is another essential contributor to well-being. Regular participation in physical activity not only enhances physical health but also helps regulate psychological well-being, reducing stress symptoms and improving self-perceptions [18, 19]. Physical activity has been shown to boost resilience, life satisfaction, and overall mental health, often through improvements in body image and emotional self-regulation [20, 21].

Despite growing evidence in students’ mental health, most research has focused on individual constructs, such as resilience, physical activity, and life satisfaction separately, or in general student populations [22]. In Greece, existing research has highlighted high levels of academic and psychosocial stress among university students [23], yet, to our knowledge, no previous research has systematically explored the interaction between body appreciation, physical activity, resilience, and life satisfaction in Greek nursing students.

To address this gap, we adopted a theory-driven structural equation modeling (SEM) approach to examine how resilience and physical activity relate to life satisfaction, and how body appreciation may operate as a key psychological mechanism in these relationships. SEM allows a simultaneous examination of both direct and indirect effects among latent and observed variables, providing an integrated test of a theoretically specified model that reflects the multifaced nature of nursing students’ psychological well-being.

Theoretical framework

The present study is grounded in two complementary perspectives. Positive Body Image Theory emphasizes body appreciation as a core component of well-being that can buffer sociocultural appearance pressures and support healthier health related behaviors [10, 11]. Lazarus and Folkman’s Transactional Stress and Coping Model conceptualizes resilience as a dynamic process through which individuals appraise and cope with stressors in ways that promote adaptation and life satisfaction [15]. Integrating these frameworks, we conceptualize body appreciation as an embodiment -related resource that may translate resilience and health behaviors such as physical activity, into greater life satisfaction among nursing students.

From this integrative perspective, body appreciation can be viewed as a proximal embodiment- related mechanism linking internal resources and behaviors to broader evaluations of life. Resilient individuals may be better able to cope with appearance-related pressures and maintain a respectful, accepting stance toward their bodies. Similarly, when physical activity is seen as enjoyable and health-focused, it can promote a functional appreciation of the body (i.e., strength, endurance) rather than self- critical appearance-based comparisons [12, 19]. In turn, such positive embodiment is expected to contribute to higher life satisfaction by reducing appearance-related distress and supporting self-acceptance.

We specified a theory-driven model in which resilience and physical activity were expected to relate to life satisfaction both directly and indirectly via body appreciation. At the same time, given the high academic and clinical demands faced by nursing students, we recognized that some relationships- particularly those involving physical activity-could be weaker, non- significant, or even negative in this population. The following research hypotheses therefore represent theoretically informed expectations that are tested empirically rather than assumed.

Hypotheses

H1

Body appreciation is positively associated with life satisfaction.

H2

Resilience is positively associated with life satisfaction.

H3

Physical activity is expected to be associated with life satisfaction, with a positive association hypothesized based on previous work in university students.

H4

Resilience is positively associated with body appreciation.

H5

Physical activity is expected to be associated with body appreciation, with a positive association hypothesized in line with literature emphasizing functional body focus.

H6

Body appreciation mediates the relationship between resilience and physical activity with life satisfaction.

Methods

Aim

The purpose of this study was twofold: (a) to examine the relationship between body appreciation, resilience, physical activity, and life satisfaction, and (b) to explore the mediating role of body appreciation in the connection between resilience, physical activity, and life satisfaction.

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted from February 2025 to March 2025 and involved a convenience sample of students from all nursing departments in Greece. This study was reported following the STROBE reporting checklist. An invitation to participate was uploaded using university student platforms and social media. These platforms included official university forums, groups, and social media pages to encourage participation from a diverse group of students. Students from the first to final year who were willing to participate in the study and were enrolled in a nursing program in Greece were considered eligible. For the study, an electronic questionnaire using Google Forms was constructed. On the first page, an informed consent form explaining the aim of the study, along with the first author’s contact information, was included. To reduce evaluation apprehension and socially desirable responding, participation was anonymous, no identifying information was collected, and students were informed that there were no “right or wrong” answers and that they could discontinue at any time. The number of students who viewed the survey invitation could not be determined; thus, a conventional response rate could not be calculated, and the possibility of self-selection bias (i.e., over- presentation of more engaged or health- conscious students) cannot be excluded. The completion time was approximately 10–15 min.

Sample and sample size

Sample size estimation was performed using the online calculator (http://www.raosoft.com/samplesize.html). The calculation was based on an assumed population size of 3000 nursing students, a margin of error of 5%, a confidence level of 95%, which yielded a minimum sample size of 249; we enrolled 324 to improve precision and mitigate non-response.

Measurements

A questionnaire battery was used to assess (a) demographic characteristics, (b) the Body Appreciation Scale (BAS-2), (c) the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale 10-item (CD-RISC-10), (d) the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), and (e) the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS).

Demographic characteristics

The demographic characteristics questionnaire, developed for the study, included questions about gender, age, year of study, and living arrangement.

The Body Appreciation Scale (BAS-2)

The Body Appreciation Scale (BAS-2), developed by Tylka and Wood-Barcalow [10], was used to evaluate participants’ body appreciation. This scale contains 10 items that assess the level of acceptance, respect, and positive regard individuals have for their physical appearance. Respondents rate each item on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (never) to 5 (always), with higher scores reflecting greater body appreciation. To determine an individual’s overall body appreciation score, item responses are summed, resulting in a score ranging from 10 to 50. The BAS-2 has shown strong internal consistency, reliability, and validity in measuring positive body image. It also has validated psychometric properties and has been translated and validated in the Greek population [24]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.947.

The Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale 10-item (CD-RISC-10)

The Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-10) was used to assess participants’ level of resilience. This 10-item scale, developed by Connor and Davidson [25], measures an individual’s ability to cope with stress, adapt to adversity, and recover from challenges. Respondents rate each item on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 (not true at all) to 4 (almost true nearly all the time), with higher scores reflecting greater resilience. The CD-RISC-10 has demonstrated good reliability and validity across various populations, making it a widely recognized tool for measuring resilience. The CD-RISC-10 has been validated in Greek HPs [26]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.899.

The international physical activity questionnaire

The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [27] assessed participants’ PA levels. The IPAQ is a widely used self-report tool that evaluates PA frequency, duration, and intensity over the past week in MET-minutes·week− 1. The Metabolic Equivalent of Task (MET) is the ratio of the work metabolic rate to the resting metabolic rate [28]. Participants are asked to report the duration of each activity. The responses are classified into PA intensity levels, which can then be categorized as low (Total PA score < 600 MET-minutes·week− 1), moderate (Vigorous PA score ≥ 480 MET-minutes·week− 1 or Total PA score ≥ 600 MET-minutes·week− 1), or high (Vigorous PA score ≥ 1500 MET-minutes·week− 1 or Total PA score ≥ 3000 MET-minutes·week− 1). For the purposes of SEM, the total MET-minutes·week-1 score was used as a continuous indicator of physical activity. The IPAQ has been validated in various populations, demonstrating reliability and consistency in assessing physical activity patterns. Moreover, the IPAQ has been standardized for the Greek population [29]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.768.

The Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS)

The Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS), developed by Diener et al. [30], assessed participants’ overall life satisfaction. This scale comprises five items that assess an individual’s global cognitive judgments of their life. Respondents rate each item on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater life satisfaction. The SWLS has demonstrated reliability and validity in assessing subjective well-being across diverse populations and has been standardized for the Greek population [31]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was reported to be 0.872.

Data analysis

We used IBM SPSS Statistics 22.0 and AMOS 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-tailed). The online questionnaire required completion of all items before submission; consequently, there was no item-level missing data in the analyzed dataset. Descriptive statistics included mean, standard deviation, frequency, and proportions. Following tests of normality, we found that the variables did not meet assumptions of normal distribution; therefore, non-parametric statistical tests were selected, including Spearman’s ρ for bivariate associations between body appreciation, resilience, life satisfaction, and physical activity. Then, Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) using maximum likelihood estimation was performed to test the interrelationships between and among Body appreciation, Resilience, Life satisfaction, and Physical activity.

The statistical analysis used the Covariance-Based Structural Equation Modeling (CB-SEM) approach, which offers a theory-driven framework for studying relationships between latent and observed variables [32]. In addition, CB-SEM provides global fit indices that were central to evaluating the conceptual model, whereas Partial Least Squares SEM (PLS-SEM) is more prediction oriented and does not offer the same set of global fit statistics. For this reason, we retained CB-SEM. Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation was applied to estimate model parameters by minimizing the difference between observed and model-implied covariance matrices [33]. Although ML assumes multivariate normality, it performs adequately with samples above 200 and mild-to moderate non normality, particularly when combined with bootstrap procedures [34–36]. In this study, bootstrap resampling (2,000 iterations) was used to estimate indirect effects robustly.

The fit of the hypothetical and emerging models was assessed using several parameters: the Chi-Square Minimum Discrepancy to Degrees of Freedom ratio (CMIN/df), the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), the Parsimonious Normal Fit Index (PNFI) and the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI). CMIN/DF values between 3 and 5, CFI and TLI values ≥ 0.90 and RMSEA values ≤ 0.08 were considered indicative of acceptable model fit [37].

Ethical considerations

The present study followed ethical guidelines and ensured that participants’ rights, privacy, and confidentiality were maintained throughout the research process. Participants received a consent form that explained the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, and the expected time commitment. Participants were also informed at the beginning of the survey that they could withdraw from the study at any time. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki [38]. Approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Democritus University of Thrace (Approval No: 38841/331, 29-1-2025).

The SEM analysis produced borderline to acceptable fit indices: X² = 1018.582, p < 0.001; CMIN/DF = 3.525; CFI = 0.879; and RMSEA = 0.088 (90% CI: 0.083 to 0.094). According to commonly used cut-offs, these values indicate an acceptable but not optimal representation of the data. To avoid capitalizing on chance, we retained the original theory-driven model and did not modify paths based solely on modification indices; therefore, the findings should be interpreted with corresponding caution. Body appreciation significantly influenced life satisfaction (β = 0.46), emphasizing its strong link to overall well-being. Similarly, resilience was positively associated with life satisfaction (β = 0.38, p < 0.001), indicating that higher resilience levels contribute to greater life satisfaction. In contrast, physical activity showed a weak but significant adverse effect on life satisfaction (β = -0.14, p = 0.006), suggesting that increased physical activity does not necessarily improve overall satisfaction. It was also negatively correlated with body appreciation (β = -0.12), implying that individuals who engage in more physical activity may have lower body appreciation, possibly due to societal pressures or self-comparisons. Mediation analysis revealed that body appreciation played a key role in the relationship between physical activity and life satisfaction (indirect effect: β = -0.12, 95% [–0.22, − 0.04]). Additionally, resilience mediated the link between body appreciation and life satisfaction (indirect effect: β = 0.22, 95% CI [0.11, 0.36]), indicating that part of the beneficial association between resilience and life satisfaction operates through more positive body evaluations. The indirect (mediated) effect of physical activity on life satisfaction was not statistically significant when resilience and body appreciation were included in the model, whereas the direct path for physical activity to life satisfaction remained weak but significant (β= -0.14, p = 0.006). The BAS-2 items (BD1–BD10) showed strong standardized loadings on the body appreciation latent factor (highest: BD6, β = 1.168). Similarly, the CD-RISC-10 items (RS1–RS10) were strongly associated with life satisfaction, especially RS8 (β = 1.820), suggesting a strong connection between resilience and well-being (Fig. S2).

Discussion

The present study’s findings offer significant insights into the relationships among body appreciation, resilience, physical activity, and life satisfaction in a sample of nursing students. The used structural model supported most of the proposed hypotheses, revealing several notable direct and indirect effects. Below, we examine each finding in the context of previous literature.

Body appreciation and life satisfaction

H1

Body appreciation is positively related to life satisfaction.

This hypothesis was confirmed. The results indicated that body appreciation was a significant predictor of life satisfaction (β = 0.46), aligning with prior studies that demonstrate individuals with favorable body perceptions tend to report greater subjective well-being [10, 39].

This finding holds particular significance for nursing students. Nursing students face unique pressures during their academic and clinical training, including high academic demands, emotionally challenging clinical experiences, and strong sociocultural expectations regarding health, fitness, and professional appearance [40, 41]. These factors can heighten vulnerability to body dissatisfaction and stress.

Positive body appreciation may serve as a vital psychological resource for nursing students, helping them sustain resilience and life satisfaction despite the demanding nature of their training. Previous research has shown that psychosocial resources, such as a positive body image and resilience, are essential for promoting well-being in young adults, particularly in stressful educational environments [42]. In this regard, nurturing body appreciation among nursing students could be crucial for supporting their mental health and life satisfaction throughout their education and professional development.

Resilience and life satisfaction

H2

Resilience is positively related to life satisfaction.

This hypothesis was also supported, with resilience displaying a significant positive association with life satisfaction (β = 0.38, p < 0.001). This supports the notion that resilience is a protective psychological factor, aiding individuals to cope with stress and feel more satisfied with life [30].

Previous studies have explored the role of resilience and life satisfaction among students and healthcare professionals. For example, research with resident physicians showed that psychological resilience acts as a key mediator, protecting against the adverse effects of sleep disturbances on mental health and life satisfaction [43]. Similarly, among nursing professionals, resilience has been demonstrated to mediate the link between workplace bullying and professional quality of life, emphasizing its protective role in demanding healthcare settings [44]. These results highlight the importance of resilience and psychological well-being in healthcare populations. However, although resilience and life satisfaction have been examined separately in medical and nursing contexts, no research has looked at the combined effects of body appreciation, resilience, physical activity, and life satisfaction among nursing students. Since nursing students face emotional and clinical challenges early in their training, such as direct patient contact and exposure to hospital environments—investigating these interconnected psychological resources in this vulnerable group is both new and essential.

Physical activity and life satisfaction

H3

Physical activity is expected to be associated with life satisfaction.

Unexpectedly, this hypothesis was not supported. Contrary to much of the literature that corroborates the positive association between physical activity and life satisfaction—due to better self-esteem [14], enhanced quality of sleep [45], social interaction, especially in group activities [46], and improved physical health [47]—the findings showed a negative relationship between physical activity and life satisfaction (β = -0.14, p = 0.006). This counterintuitive finding may reflect contextual factors specific to nursing students. Intense academic demands, clinical exposure, and emotional stressors characterize nursing education, which may compromise students’ overall well-being despite their engagement in health-promoting behaviors [40]. Therefore, the buffering effect of physical activity on their well-being may be diminished or insufficient to enhance life satisfaction if stress and academic burnout remain high [43, 44]. Future analyses could include exercise motivation (appearance vs. intrinsic) as a moderator.

Resilience and body appreciation

H4

Resilience is positively related to body appreciation.

This hypothesis was supported. Resilience positively affected body appreciation (β = 0.38). Nursing education requires high academic and clinical achievement and promotes ideals related to health, professionalism, and physical appearance, which may heighten body-related pressures. In today’s high-pressure environment, resilience can serve as a crucial psychological resource that helps protect students from internalizing unrealistic appearance ideals and supports the development of a more positive body image [48]. Furthermore, previous research has shown that psychosocial resources like resilience influence young adults’ health and life satisfaction, underscoring the importance of nurturing these capacities, particularly among nursing students, to support their overall well-being [49].

Physical activity and body appreciation

H5

Physical activity is expected to be associated with body appreciation.

Surprisingly, this hypothesis was not supported. A small but significant negative relationship was observed between physical activity and body appreciation (β = -0.12).

One possible explanation relates to why students engage in physical activity. Prior studies show that when exercise is motivated primarily by appearance-focused goals rather than intrinsic motives such as enjoyment or health, it can be associated with lower body appreciation and greater self-objectification [50]. In Greek university settings, nursing students additionally face high academic workloads, commuting and clinical placements that compete with leisure-time physical activity [23]. Under these conditions, physical activity may be experienced less as freely chosen health behavior and more as another external demand, particularly when coupled with sociocultural expectations about the “healthy” and “fit” appearance of future nurses. This combination of time pressure, academic workload and appearance-based expectations could help explain why, in our sample. Higher physical activity co-occurred with lower body appreciation and, indirectly, lower life satisfaction.

H6

Body appreciation mediates the relationship between resilience, physical activity, and life satisfaction

Partial support was found for this hypothesis. Body appreciation mediated the relationship between resilience and life satisfaction (indirect β = 0.22) suggesting that resilience may be associated with higher life satisfaction in part through the link with body appreciation. Individuals who maintain a positive and accepting view of their bodies are more likely to demonstrate greater resilience and employ more adaptive coping strategies and emotional regulation, thereby improving their life satisfaction. Moreover, body appreciation can foster self-acceptance, reduce appearance-related stress, encourage health-promoting behaviors, and act as a psychological bridge to facilitate the translation of resilience into greater overall well-being. However, a more complex pattern emerged regarding physical activity. Physical activity negatively impacted life satisfaction through body appreciation (β = -0.12).

Taken together, the findings suggest that resilience and body appreciation function as complimentary psychological resources that are closely tied to nursing students’ life satisfaction. Resilience appears to support more adaptive appraisals of academic and clinical stressors, while body appreciation may buffer appearance- related pressures and foster a more accepting relationship with one’s body. Physical activity, in turn, may contribute to well-being when it is experienced as self-determined and health-focused, but may be less beneficial- or even associated with more self-criticism- when driven by external appearance norms or undertaken under substantial time pressure [51, 52]. This highlights the importance of considering not only whether students are physically active, but also how and why they engage in physical activity.

Limitations

Several limitations should be recognized when interpreting these findings. Because of the cross-sectional study design, causality cannot be established. The generalizability of the results is restricted by the sample’s representativeness, which may not fully mirror the broader population of nursing students. Although the sample size met our a priori power analysis, it was relatively modest for estimating a structural complex SEM with multiple latent variables. This raises the possibility that some pathways, particularly weaker or subgroup-specific effects, may have been underpowered. Moreover, we did not conduct subgroup analyses (i.e., by gender, year of study or living arrangements), so potential heterogeneity in the observed relationships remains unexplored. Given the online volunteer sampling method, self-selection bias cannot be ruled out. Additionally, relying on self-report questionnaires introduces biases such as social desirability and participants’ subjective perceptions. These biases could affect the validity of the reported physical activity levels, resilience, and body appreciation. Moreover, the absence of control over other psychological or environmental variables—such as academic stress, cultural norms, or media exposure—may result in confounding factors. Although the instruments used were validated through rigorous statistical methods, further psychometric validation in diverse Greek student populations would strengthen the reliability and applicability of these findings. Future research should employ longitudinal designs to examine causal relationships and monitor changes over time. Using objective measures of physical activity (i.e., accelerometer-based physical activity) or observer-rated psychological assessments could help reduce bias. Expanding the sample to include students from other disciplines or countries may also improve generalizability and enable cultural comparisons. Lastly, exploring mediators and moderators like motivation for physical activity, self-compassion, or social support could provide deeper insights into the complex pathways linking resilience and body image with life satisfaction.

Implications

The results partly support the proposed model. Body appreciation and resilience significantly contribute to life satisfaction, while the role of physical activity is more complex. The findings indicate that, among nursing students, physical activity might be linked to body dissatisfaction if driven by societal ideals rather than intrinsic motivation. This underscores the importance of creating interventions that encourage positive embodiment and self-acceptance. Promoting resilience and a healthy, respectful view of the body may offer more sustainable ways to improve student well-being. Short body-appreciation exercises, psychoeducation on functional body focus, and resilience skills (like cognitive reappraisal) could be incorporated into nursing curricula and counseling services. This study provides valuable insights into how body appreciation, resilience, and life satisfaction are interconnected, especially in academic settings. Future research should use longitudinal designs and examine gender differences and motivational factors to better understand these relationships.

Conclusions

The present study examined how body appreciation, resilience and physical activity relate to life satisfaction in Greek nursing students. A more positive body image and higher resilience were consistently associated with greater life satisfaction, whereas the association between physical activity and life satisfaction was negative and more complex than expected. Given the cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted as correlational and hypothesis generating rather than causal.

From an educational perspective, nursing curricula and student support services could incorporate activities that promote body appreciation (i.e., functional body focus, media literacy), resilience-building skills (i.e., cognitive reappraisal, problem-focused coping) and reflective discussions about healthy, intrinsically motivated physical activity. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal ordering and to test whether changes in body appreciation and resilience are prospectively associated with changes in life satisfaction. Research that examines gender differences, motivational profiles for physical activity and broader sociocultural influences within Greek and international nursing programs would further refine understanding of these relationships and inform targeted interventions.
