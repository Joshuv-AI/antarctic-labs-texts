Diagnostic and prognostic value of T2 mapping in cardiac sarcoidosis
Authors: Mio Uno, Hisanori Kosuge, Masatake Kobayashi, Yasuhiro Fujita, Satoshi Hida, Kazuhiro Satomi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Cardiac sarcoidosis, T1 mapping, T2 mapping, FDG-PET, Myocardial inflammation

Introduction

Cardiac sarcoidosis (CS) is associated with a poor prognosis if left untreated, primarily due to arrhythmia, heart failure, and sudden death [1], [2], [3]. Immunosuppressive therapy, particularly corticosteroids, is the mainstay of medical treatment for CS. Patients who initiate steroid therapy after the progression of fibrosis and deterioration of cardiac function have worse outcomes than those treated at an early disease stage with preserved cardiac function [4]. Accurate assessment of disease activity and early therapeutic intervention are therefore essential.

A positive myocardial biopsy result provides a definitive diagnosis of CS; however, its sensitivity remains low [5]. 18F-labeled fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (18F-FDG PET) is currently considered the noninvasive gold standard for detecting active inflammation and diagnosing CS [6], [7], [8], [9]. Nevertheless, routine follow-up with 18F-FDG PET is burdensome for patients because of prolonged fasting requirements. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging is also valuable for diagnosing CS through late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) and tissue characterization using T1 and T2 mapping. Although LGE is highly sensitive for identifying fibrosis, it is relatively insensitive to myocardial inflammatory processes, limiting early disease detection and assessment of disease activity [10]. T1 and T2 mapping enable estimation of myocardial inflammation by identifying fibrosis and edema without radiation exposure; however, these parameters are not specific to CS [11], [12]. The diagnostic and prognostic utility of T1 and T2 mapping in CS has not yet been fully established.

In this study, we evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of T1 and T2 mapping on CMR for detecting CS and assessed their prognostic value in patients with CS.

Methods

Study population

We retrospectively enrolled 39 patients with suspected CS. All patients underwent both CMR and 18F-FDG PET within 36 days (interquartile range: 16–57 days) between October 2019 and September 2025. Indications for imaging included screening for cardiac involvement in patients with extracardiac sarcoidosis (n = 11), evaluation of unexplained heart failure (n = 11), investigation of left ventricular systolic dysfunction or focal wall asynergy (n = 11), and assessment of arrhythmia, including atrioventricular block or ventricular tachycardia (n = 6). Patients with an estimated glomerular filtration rate < 30 ml/min/1.73 m2 and those receiving corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapy at the time of PET or CMR were excluded. This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tokyo Medical University, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

CMR protocol

CMR was performed using a 3 T system (Magnetom Skyra, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Steady-state free precession cine images were acquired in three long-axis planes and contiguous short-axis slices covering the entire left ventricle. Cine imaging parameters were as follows: repetition time (TR) 28.2 ms, echo time (TE) 1.6 ms, flip angle 60°, field of view (FOV) 360 × 270 mm, acquisition matrix 224 × 224, and slice thickness 6 mm. Native T1 and T2 mapping were obtained in three short-axis slices (basal, midventricular, and apical) using a modified Look-Locker inversion recovery sequence (T1 mapping: TR 349 ms, TE 1.1 ms, flip angle 35°, FOV 360 × 306 mm, acquisition matrix 256 × 169, slice thickness 8 mm) and a T2-prepared TurboFLASH sequence (T2 mapping TR: 251.5 ms, TE 1.32 ms, flip angle 12°, FOV 288 × 360 mm, acquisition matrix 144 × 192, slice thickness 8 mm). Ten minutes after intravenous administration of gadobutrol (Gadovist, Bayer Healthcare, Leverkusen, Germany), postcontrast T1 mapping and LGE imaging were performed using the same protocol as native T1 mapping and an inversion-recovery gradient echo sequence (LGE: TR 700 ms, TE 1.9 ms, flip angle 12°, FOV 360 × 270 mm, acquisition matrix 192 × 157, slice thickness 6 mm). The same standardized imaging protocol and acquisition techniques were followed throughout the study period.

18F-FDG PET protocol

Patients fasted for ≥18 h before imaging to minimize physiologic myocardial 18F-FDG uptake. 18F-FDG PET/computed tomography (CT) scans were performed using a Discovery MI system (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI). CT scanning was performed first (slice thickness, 3.75 mm; pitch, 2.79 mm; voltage, 120 kV; auto mA, 20–580 mA depending on patient body mass with a noise index of 15; reconstructed matrix size, 512 × 512). The transaxial and cranio-caudal FOV of 70 cm and was used for attenuation correction of PET images.

After CT scanning, the patients received an intravenous injection of 3.7 MBq/kg 18F-FDG and underwent whole-body scans 60 min later. PET data were acquired in 3-dimensional mode for 2 min per bed position. Motion correction, including body and respiratory motion, was not applied. PET images were reconstructed using a 128 × 128 matrix with a Bayesian penalized likelihood reconstruction algorithm incorporating point spread function as the default setting (Q.Clear, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI), with a beta value (penalization factor) of 600 and a transaxial FOV of 50 cm.

Image analysis

CMR images were analyzed by two experienced cardiologists using commercially available software (Ziostation 2, Ziosoft, Tokyo, Japan). Left ventricular ejection fraction, volume, and mass were measured by manual tracing of endocardial and epicardial borders on end-diastolic and end-systolic short-axis cine images. Native T1 and T2 values were measured in each segment using a 16-segment model. Regions of interest were carefully placed in each segment while avoiding the blood pool and adjacent tissues. The reference range of T1 and T2 values in our institute are 1220 ± 44 ms and 39.4 ± 4.8 ms, respectively.

The extracellular volume fraction (ECV) for each segment was calculated using the following formula: ECV = (1 − hematocrit) × (1/post-contrast T1 of the myocardium − 1/native T1 of the myocardium)/(1/post-contrast T1 of the blood − 1/native T1 of the blood). Hematocrit values were obtained before CMR imaging. Segments with insufficient image quality for reliable native T1 or T2 measurement were excluded from analysis. Mean T1, T2, and ECV values were calculated by averaging all segments, whereas maximum T1, T2, and ECV values were defined as the highest values among the 16 segments. The presence or absence of LGE was assessed visually.

Myocardial 18F-FDG accumulation on 18F-FDG PET/CT was evaluated by two nuclear medicine radiologists who were blinded to clinical data. Uptake patterns were classified into four categories: 1. no uptake, 2. diffuse uptake, 3. focal uptake, and 4. focal on diffuse uptake [13]. A ‘‘focal’’ or ‘‘focal on diffuse’’ pattern of myocardial 18F-FDG uptake was defined as the CS-positive group (n = 21, focal pattern: 13 patients, focal on diffuse pattern: 8 patients). No patients in the CS-positive group showed a diffuse uptake pattern. The maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) in the heart was measured. Eighteen patients without abnormal uptake on FDG-PET were classified as the CS-negative group. One patient in the CS-positive group had histologically confirmed sarcoidosis on endomyocardial biopsy. The remaining patients in the CS-positive group met the diagnostic criteria for CS or isolated CS according to the Japanese Circulation Society (JCS) 2016 Guideline, whereas patients in the CS-negative group did not [14]. The CS-negative group included four patients with dilated cardiomyopathy, eight with non-dilated left ventricular cardiomyopathy, one with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, one with hypertensive heart disease, and four with normal cardiac function.

Follow-up study

Follow-up information was obtained from hospital records. The primary outcome was a composite of all-cause mortality, sustained ventricular tachycardia (duration > 30 s), and worsening heart failure requiring intensification of diuretic therapy.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 29.0, Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies, and continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Continuous variables were compared between CS-positive and CS-negative groups using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. The diagnostic performance of T1 and T2 mapping for discriminating CS-positive and CS-negative patients was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic analysis. Primary event rates were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and group differences were assessed using the log-rank test. Associations between T1 and T2 mapping parameters and primary outcomes were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards model. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Diagnostic performance of T1 and T2 mapping for detecting CS

Maximum T2 values demonstrated the highest diagnostic accuracy for discriminating between CS-positive and CS-negative groups (Fig. 1A; area under the curve [AUC] = 0.817). The optimal cutoff value for maximum T2 was 49.6 ms, yielding a sensitivity of 61.9 % and a specificity of 83.3 %. The correlation coefficient between SUVmax and maximum T2 values was 0.366. The diagnostic performance of mean T2, mean ECV and maximum ECV values was moderate (Fig. 1B–D; mean T2: AUC = 0.714, mean ECV: AUC = 0.710, maximum ECV: AUC = 0.786), whereas the performance of maximum T1 values was low (Fig. 1E; AUC = 0.684).

Association between T1 and T2 mapping and primary outcome in CS-positive patients

During a median follow-up of 609 days, 7 patients in the CS-positive group experienced the primary composite outcome: one sudden cardiac death, two sustained ventricular tachycardia events, and four cases of worsening heart failure requiring diuretic intensification. Corticosteroid therapy was initiated in nine patients in the CS-positive group during follow-up, three of whom experienced the primary composite outcome. Primary outcomes were compared between groups stratified by the median values of T1, T2, and ECV (median values: maximum T2, 50.0 ms; mean T2, 42.5 ms; maximum T1, 1387 ms; mean T1, 1281 ms; maximum ECV, 43.1 %; mean ECV, 31.0 %). Baseline characteristics of each subgroup are shown in Supplement Tables S1–S3. Patients with maximum T2 values above the median had a lower proportion of hypertension than those with values at or below the median. Patients with maximum ECV values above the median had higher BNP levels, and those with mean ECV values above the median had a higher proportion of smoking, compared with their respective reference groups.

Patients with maximum T2 or mean T2 values above the median had a significantly higher risk of the primary outcome than those with values at or below the median (Fig. 2A and D). No significant differences in the primary outcome were observed between groups stratified by T1 or ECV values (Fig. 2B, C, E, and F). In univariate Cox proportional hazards analysis, maximum T2 and mean T2 values were significantly associated with the primary outcome (maximum T2: hazard ratio [HR], 1.134; 95 % confidence interval [CI], 1.017–1.264; p = 0.023; mean T2: HR, 1.457; 95 % CI, 1.138–1.867; p = 0.003; Table 3). These associations remained significant after multivariate adjustment for age, sex, BNP, and eGFR (maximum T2: HR, 1.156; 95 % CI, 1.009–1.325; p = 0.036; mean T2: HR, 1.525; 95 % CI, 1.107–2.100; p = 0.01; Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the diagnostic accuracy of T1 and T2 mapping in patients with CS and examined the associations between T1 and T2 mapping and primary events. The main findings were as follows: (i) maximum T2 values demonstrated good diagnostic accuracy for distinguishing patients with CS from those without CS, including patients with cardiomyopathies such as dilated cardiomyopathy, regardless of preserved or impaired cardiac function, and (ii) higher maximum T2 and mean T2 values in patients with CS were associated with an increased risk of primary events.

The JCS 2016 Guideline is mainly used for the diagnosis and management of CS and includes both histological and clinical diagnostic categories [14]. Histological diagnosis is based on myocardial biopsy; however, positive findings are reported in only approximately 20 % of cases. The clinical diagnosis is established using eight criteria, including 18F-FDG accumulation and left ventricular LGE on CMR. Previous studies have reported that 18F-FDG PET has a sensitivity of 84 % and a specificity of 82 % for diagnosing CS [7]. In addition, 18F-FDG PET is useful for identifying isolated CS which lacks extracardiac involvement [8], [14]. Shelke et al. demonstrated that serial 18F-FDG PET imaging can be used to monitor responses to steroid therapy [9]. Therefore, 18F-FDG PET is valuable not only for detecting CS but also for assessing disease activity, prognosis, and treatment response. However, prolonged fasting requirement and radiation exposure impose a substantial burden on patients, limiting its suitability for routine follow-up assessments [15], [16].

LGE is helpful for CS diagnosis and is strongly associated with poor prognosis [17], [18], [19]. Nevertheless, no specific LGE pattern is unique to CS, and LGE has limited ability to distinguish active inflammation from chronic inflammation [20]. Native T1 values reflect signals from both cellular and interstitial compartments and are influenced by myocardial edema and regional or diffuse fibrosis, whereas ECV values primarily represent expansion of the interstitial space and reflect myocardial fibrosis [21]. Native T2 values are used to detect myocardial edema and active inflammation, whereas LGE reflects irreversible fibrosis and may be limited in detecting early disease and assessing disease activity [22]. SUV reflects cellular metabolic activity. Therefore, whereas these parameters are associated with inflammatory processes, they represent different disease stages of myocardial injury and provide complementary information regarding myocardial involvement. Previous studies have shown that native T1, T2, and ECV values are higher in patients with CS than in healthy controls or patients without CS, findings that are consistent with the present results [11], [23], [24], [25]. In this study, maximum T2 values showed good diagnostic performance for discriminating CS-positive and CS-negative groups. Furthermore, maximum T2 and mean T2 values were significantly associated with a higher risk of the primary outcome in CS-positive patients. By contrast, Puntmann et al. reported accurate discrimination between patients with CS and controls using both native T1 and T2 values [11], and Cheung et al. found that T1 and T2 values were associated with clinical events in patients with suspected CS [24]. Treiber et al. demonstrated that ECV values in LGE-positive segments could differentiate CS from myocarditis, whereas T1 values could not [25]. In the present study, patients were assigned into the CS-positive group or the CS-negative group based on FDG-PET. The CS-negative group included patients with poorer cardiac function and a higher prevalence of LGE than the control populations in previous reports, suggesting the predominance of chronic myocardial changes such as focal and diffuse fibrosis, whereas the CS-positive group likely included more patients with active inflammation. In addition, prognostic analyses were limited to CS-positive patients. These differences may explain the lower diagnostic accuracy and prognostic value of T1 mapping compared with T2 mapping in this cohort.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective study with a relatively small sample size, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Although a multivariable analysis was performed, the small number of patients and events limit the reliability of the results. Therefore, our findings should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. Second, parametric mapping measurements were obtained from only three slices, and areas with more severe or localized inflammation may have been missed. Third, CMR and 18F-FDG PET were not performed on the same day, and the interval between these modalities might have introduced temporal discrepancies in disease activity. The moderate correlation between SUV and T2 values may be attributed to this interval and the differences in related underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. Fourth, histological confirmation of CS was available in only one patient, and most patients were diagnosed according to the JCS Guideline; however, previous studies have demonstrated the diagnostic utility of the JCS Guideline [26], [27]. Larger prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusion

T2 mapping effectively distinguished patients with CS from those without CS and may provide prognostic information in patients with CS; however, these findings should be validated in larger prospective studies.
