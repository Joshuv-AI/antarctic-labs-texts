Multi-site validation of an AI-based biomarker test for determining ER, PR, and HER2 status from H&E-stained breast cancer slides
Authors: F Ntelemis, S Wolf, C Bass, A Geraldes, J Schmidt, N Kumar, S Singhal, D Mehrotra, A Marcia, J Blackwood, O Maiques, A Ironside, JJ Staves, D Leff, IH Um, J Loane, G Bryson, DJ Harrison, SA Khurram, L Spary, R Clarkson, JN Kather, P Pandya, S Arslan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe assessment of estrogen/progesterone receptors (ER/PR) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) is essential for managing breast cancer (BC) patients, as these biomarkers guide targeted therapies. Traditional methods such as immunohistochemistry (IHC) and in situ hybridisation (ISH) are widely used, but they are resource-intensive and time-consuming. To address these challenges, we developed PANProfiler Breast (PPB), an artificial intelligence (AI)-based digital test to analyse images of haematoxylin–eosin (H&E)-stained slides for BC biomarker assessment.Materials and methodsPPB was validated on a large, diverse cohort of 3138 unique BC samples from five sites across the UK and United States. The validation included both biopsy and resection specimens, digitally scanned using multiple imaging platforms. Standard performance characteristics were assessed by comparison with IHC and ISH reference assays.ResultsPPB consistently demonstrated high performance across multiple cohorts, confirming its effectiveness for establishing the status of ER/PR and assessing HER2 negativity for BC patients. HER2 achieved an average sensitivity of 90.6% (±0.6) and a concordance of 93.7% (±7.8%) when determining the negative status. For ER, we measured an overall sensitivity and specificity of 98.2% (±0.3%) and 62.0% (±7.3%), averaged over all sites, respectively. For PR the averaged sensitivity and specificity across all sites were 97.9% (±1.0%) and 45.7% (±1.6%), respectively.ConclusionsIntegrating PPB into routine clinical workflows could enable rapid and accurate assessment of BC biomarkers, improving access to targeted therapies and enhancing clinical decision making.Key words: breast cancer, precision medicine, diagnostic biomarkers, artificial intelligence, digital pathology

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) continues to be a major global health challenge, with 2.3 million new cases and 666 000 deaths reported worldwide in 2022.1 The choice of treatment of BC relies heavily on the accurate assessment of three key biomarkers, estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2).2 While these biomarkers play a central role in determining targeted therapies, the proliferation marker Ki67, typically evaluated alongside histological grade, is also crucial for decision making in hormone receptor-positive/HER2-negative tumours, despite the lack of global standardisation.3

The current standard practice for assessing ER, PR, and HER2 status relies on immunohistochemistry (IHC) to evaluate protein expression.2 For HER2, IHC results are reported as 0, 1+, 2+, or 3+, with equivocal (2+) cases requiring additional in situ hybridisation (ISH) to determine over-amplification.4,5 ER and PR status is assessed using IHC alone, with samples interpreted as positive when ≥1% of tumour cell nuclei stain positively.6 Final biomarker results are determined by pathologist review. These tests are labour-intensive and impose a significant workload on pathology departments,7,8 contributing to delays in diagnosis and treatment planning,9 with ongoing workforce shortages further intensifying this burden.7,10 In addition, interobserver variability can limit diagnostic accuracy.11,12 Collectively, these challenges highlight the need for alternative diagnostic approaches that can maintain or improve accuracy while alleviating pressure on clinical pathology services.

Digital pathology (DP) and artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning (DL), present promising solutions to current challenges. AI has the potential to function as a test replacement tool, evaluating biomarker expression directly from whole-slide images (WSIs) of haematoxylin–eosin (H&E)-stained tissue. This enables the immediate identification of cases with definitive biomarker status, limiting traditional biomarker testing to only those requiring further confirmation.13 AI enhances efficiency by streamlining diagnostic workflows and shortening turnaround times, enabling more consistent and scalable BC testing across clinical settings. While upfront investment and steps such as slide digitisation are required, these costs can be outweighed by long-term efficiency and operational savings associated with DP and AI-assisted workflows.14 AI can identify clinically actionable biomarkers from histology images, supporting accurate and reproducible cancer diagnostics and potentially enhancing consistency and standardisation in routine clinical workflows.15,16

In this article, we report results from a comprehensive multi-site validation of PANProfiler Breast (PPB), an AI-based biomarker assessment tool that determines ER, PR, and HER2 status directly from WSIs of H&E-stained pathology slides. The study included samples from five sites across the UK and United States, subdivided into cohorts to capture variability in scanner and tissue types (biopsy versus resection), reflecting the range of real-world clinical scenarios. Overall, the results demonstrate the robustness and effectiveness of PPB across multiple sites with diverse settings.

Materials and methods

Study cohorts and eligibility criteria

The study used digitally scanned WSIs of H&E-stained biopsies and resected specimens from invasive breast carcinomas. Metaplastic carcinomas, non-epithelial malignancies, salivary gland type tumours, neuroendocrine neoplasms and other rare malignancies, multiple or multifocal carcinomas, non-invasive lobular neoplasia or ductal carcinoma in situ alone, and metastasis of breast carcinomas were excluded. Frozen section images and slides deemed unsuitable for histological assessment by a pathologist were removed during quality control. Images were captured using four different scanners and stored in three distinct image formats, reflecting real-world inter-site variability. Ethics Statement and Patient Consent

This study only used retrospective or publicly available data. No patient-impacting intervention is done as part of the study. Ethics approval has been gained for the retrospective data acquired as part of the validation.

Overview of PANProfiler Breast

PANProfiler Breast (PPB) is a collection of AI-based image analysis algorithms designed for ER, PR, and HER2 testing using digitally scanned H&E-stained slides of formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) breast adenocarcinoma specimens. PPB leverages intrinsic visual features of H&E-stained slides, particularly cellular morphology, as a proxy for underlying biological processes. It classifies ER and PR status as ‘positive’, ‘negative’, or ‘indeterminate’, and HER2 status as ‘negative’ or ‘indeterminate’. An ‘indeterminate’ result means a conclusive call cannot be made and the case is flagged for standard-of-care testing (IHC or ISH). This version of PPB does not explicitly report a ‘positive’ result for HER2. Instead, any case that cannot be confidently classified as ‘negative’ is returned as ‘indeterminate’. As illustrated in Figure 1, integrating PPB into routine pathology workflows streamlines the diagnostic process, shortens turnaround times from days to minutes, and reduces dependence on conventional biomarker tests.

Artificial intelligence for establishing biomarker results

PPB is powered by AI and DP. The system relies on a series of DL methods, a subset of AI that enables computers to learn patterns from large datasets. Specifically, it can recognise complex histomorphological features in relevant tissue regions of H&E-stained WSIs of BC. These features are then used to determine the status of ER/PR and HER2 negativity. PPB is part of an AI platform that enables end-to-end-learning for determining breast biomarkers using only pathology images.17 Figure 1B illustrates the core preprocessing and DL components that underlie PPB and how they are connected in a streamlined pipeline.

A specific DL model was trained for each biomarker separately. In the first step of the pipeline, the input WSIs were subdivided into image patches (i.e. tiles) of 256 × 256 pixels at a resolution of 1.0 micron per pixel. Proprietary filtering algorithms were used for eliminating the tiles without any relevant information, where factors like tumour content and tissue composition were taken into account. A WSI was discarded from analysis if it contained fewer than 10 tiles after the filtering process. Colour normalisation18 was applied to the remaining tiles before they were assigned with a reference biomarker status. The normalised tiles were passed through a series of proprietary DL models to select the most biologically relevant images, such as invasive tumours, and remove those that contain non-relevant tissue.

The selected pre-processed tiles were then used in our end-to-end DL pipeline. This pipeline consists of the following components: (i) a smart sampling module that is used for extracting groups of tiles that are spatially adjacent (referred to as ‘windows’); (ii) a feature extractor utilising a residual convolutional neural network (ResNet) architecture19 for learning unique feature representations for each tile; (iii) an attention module that aggregates the tile embedding into a slide-level embedding, weighting the features based on their relevance (using the attention scores) for the target biomarker. The attention module is finally connected to a classification layer that makes the final decision on the biomarker status.

Performance characteristics

PPB’s performance was evaluated by comparing its results to those of conventional pathology tests and using standard performance metrics, including concordance, sensitivity, specificity, negative/positive predictive values, and test replacement rate (TRR). A formal definition of all performance metrics is given in the Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100702.

PPB’s performance was determined relative to the biomarker results obtained with conventional pathology tests, i.e. IHC for ER/PR and a two-tiered approach involving IHC and ISH for HER2. While neither IHC nor ISH is considered the gold standard for diagnostic accuracy, both are routinely used in clinical practice and serve as reference tests. In our analysis, concordance (i.e. accuracy) represents the overall percentage agreement with the conventional pathology tests. Similarly, sensitivity and specificity are used to report the positive and negative percent agreement, respectively.

Development and validation strategies

Base model training

We employed a two-stage training strategy consisting of self-supervised pre-training followed by supervised fine-tuning. Data for both stages were pooled from The Cancer Genome Atlas breast invasive carcinoma cohort (TCGA-BRCA) and proprietary in-house datasets.

In the first stage, base models were pre-trained using a self-supervised learning (SSL) framework20 to learn robust feature representations from unlabelled data. This pre-training was carried out on a development cohort comprising 1751 WSIs of FFPE tissue sections.

In the second stage, the SSL-pre-trained model served as a backbone for supervised fine-tuning. Separate models were trained for each biomarker using labelled data from the same sources. The training sets included 2407 WSIs for ER (1808 ER-positive, 599 ER-negative), 2325 WSIs for PR (1510 PR-positive, 815 PR-negative), and 1684 WSIs for HER2 (372 HER2-positive, 1312 HER2-negative). Model performance was then evaluated separately for each validation cohort.

Model evaluation with cross-validation

PPB was evaluated across multiple cohorts using cross-validation. Each cohort was divided into three equal-sized folds. For each iteration, one fold was held out as the test set while the model was fine-tuned and calibrated on the remaining two folds. This process was repeated three times, cycling through all possible fold combinations, so that every fold served once as the test set and every sample was evaluated exactly once. Final performance metrics were computed by aggregating the results across these test folds, representing the model’s capability to generalise across the entire cohort.

‘Calibration’ refers to finding the most optimal decision thresholds for a model. These thresholds help the model operate safely by allowing an ‘indeterminate’ output when no definitive result can be provided. PPB’s safety and effectiveness were maintained across different sites by calibrating cohort-specific thresholds for each biomarker. The decision boundaries identified during the calibration process were applied to compute the final model outputs, which were then used to calculate performance metrics on the test set for each cohort (see the section ‘Performance characteristics’ for details).

Explainability and interpretability analysis

Understanding the internal mechanisms of DL models provides insights into how they link visual tissue patterns to biological signals. To interpret these typically ‘black-box’ systems, we employed spatial heatmaps that overlay tile-level attention scores on to WSIs. These heatmaps reveal the tissue regions that have the strongest influence on the model outputs, highlighting morphological features that correlate with the existence (or absence) of biomarker expression. Consequently, a primary objective in our model design was to implement an attention-based architecture that inherently supports this interpretability. This design choice allows us to visually verify that decisions are driven by biologically relevant histology rather than potential artefacts.

Results

Cohort characteristics

In total, 2719, 2294, and 2976 WSIs were included for validation of ER, PR, and HER2 status, respectively, across five different cohorts sourced from the UK and United States. The prevalence of ER-/PR-positive cases ranged from 80% to 88% and 62% to 78%, respectively. HER2-positive prevalence showed greater variability, ranging from 9% to 15% in the UK cohorts, whereas the United States cohort exhibited a prevalence of 64%. This difference reflects intentional enrichment of HER2-positive cases in the United States cohort to support algorithm development for treatment response prediction.21 Detailed cohort composition and clinical characteristics are summarised in Table 1 and Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100702.

For ER, PPB demonstrated an overall sensitivity of 98.2% (±0.3%) across all sites, with the highest sensitivity observed in S1-UK-BC-ISY at 98.8%. Average specificity was measured as 62.0% (±7.3%), peaking at 67.3% for L1-UK-BC-CZI and L1-UK-BC-SVS. The TRR averaged 77.1% (±5.9%), ranging from 69.6% to 83.9% across cohorts. The average sensitivity across all sites for PR was 97.9% (±1.0%), reaching up to 99.3% for L1-UK-BC-SVS. Specificity averaged 45.7% (±1.6%) across all cohorts, with the highest value of 48.1% observed in G1-UK-BC-ISY. The average TRR was 69.3% (±4.2%), ranging from 65.8% to 75.2%.

PPB returned results of HER2 assessment as either ‘negative’ or ‘indeterminate’. The overall negative predictive value (NPV) of PPB across all sites was 93.7% (±7.8%), reaching up to 97.8% for G1-UK-BC-ISY. The average sensitivity was 90.6% (±0.6%) with a mean false negative rate (discrepancy with wet-lab assays) of 9.4% (±0.6%) across all cohorts. On average, the TRR was 34.6% (±7.0%) ranging from 27.0% to 44.3%. For the United States cohort (Y1-US-BC-SVS), which was intentionally enriched for HER2-positive cases, the NPV was lower than in other cohorts, consistent with the prevalence-dependent nature of this metric. Similarly, TRR was reduced in this cohort because PPB only assesses HER2 negativity, resulting in more indeterminate predictions in a HER2-enriched population.

Discussion

We have presented the comprehensive results from a multi-site validation study to assess the performance of PPB, an AI-based image analysis tool for biomarker profiling of BC. PPB determines ER/PR status and HER2 negativity using only images of H&E-stained BC tissue specimens. These biomarkers are critical in informing treatment decisions for BC management.

We evaluated PPB by comparing its results with ER, PR, and HER2 status obtained from standard tests, such as IHC and ISH. While a direct, one-to-one comparison was not possible due to differences in study settings, PPB’s performance was found to be non-inferior to several commercially available IHC assays for ER and PR testing, including those developed by Dako, Leica, and Ventana. Published studies assessing these assays have reported ER testing sensitivity and specificity ranging from 97.1% to 99.4% and 31.8% to 44.0%, respectively.22 For PR, the specificity and sensitivity of the assays ranged from 96.2% to 97.4% and 22.5% to 25.0%, respectively.23 Similarly, PPB’s performance was comparable to several standard tests used for HER2 assessment.24,25 According to large, prospective clinical trials,24,25 the sensitivity of multiple commercial assays, including those from Dako and Ventana, as well as fluorescence ISH, ranged from 71.42% to 97.6%. Another study evaluating HER2 testing methods using different detecting antibodies, including SP3, 4B5, and Herceptest, reported a sensitivity range of 83.33%-96.15%.24,25 While these comparisons provide valuable insights into PPB’s relative performance against conventional tests, the lack of direct one-to-one evaluations and the practical limitations in including all commercial tests are important factors to consider when interpreting the results.

The primary benefit of integrating PPB into pathological workflows is that it could improve clinical management by reducing the need for time-consuming assays currently being used as the standard for biomarker testing. The reduction in these tests can help users (e.g. pathologists) manage increasingly complex workloads and staff shortages. Currently, obtaining biomarker results through standard laboratory assays can take days, with turnaround times heavily influenced by laboratory scheduling and workload, potentially delaying clinical decision making.8 In contrast, PPB can deliver biomarker results within minutes (see Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100702). For the significant proportion of patients with definitive results (as reflected by TRR), this supports a workflow in which biomarker information can be issued alongside the histological diagnosis or used to prioritise subsequent IHC testing, accelerating the diagnostic pathway, alleviating workforce pressure, and facilitating faster referral to oncology. PPB also reduces cognitive load for pathologists and laboratory staff, allowing greater focus on complex cases. In addition, some patients may receive results more quickly, allowing them to start treatment in a more timely manner. By decreasing the number of laboratory tests required, PPB may also generate cost savings for health care providers.

While PPB demonstrates significant promise, certain aspects merit further exploration. At this stage, PPB could be useful for ER/PR evaluation and for HER2 screening in negative cases, potentially reducing the need for confirmatory IHC. Identifying and reporting HER2-positive results, currently lacking in PPB, would further enhance its applicability to treatment decisions for all patients. Furthermore, the current PPB version adheres to standard binary definitions, grouping IHC 0, 1+, and ISH-negative cases as HER2-negative, and defining ER positivity as ≥1%. Recognising the clinical necessity of identifying HER2-low and ER-low (1%-10%) subgroups for emerging therapies, future model iterations will aim to specifically stratify these populations. In parallel, efforts will focus on reducing the proportion of indeterminate results, further enhancing PPB’s practical utility by streamlining workflows and providing time savings for pathologists. While implementation requires investments such as slide digitisation and scanner acquisition, these upfront costs can be offset by long-term efficiency gains and operational benefits associated with AI-assisted DP. The study’s patient cohorts were primarily from the UK and United States, limiting global representation; future validation should include samples from other regions. Finally, as this was a retrospective study, prospective evaluation in clinical environments is essential to assess PPB’s performance, its integration into routine pathology workflows, and its potential impact on patient outcomes.

In summary, this multi-site validation of PPB demonstrated the performance of an AI-based solution for determining ER/PR status and HER2 negativity directly from WSIs of H&E-stained BC specimens. By integrating this solution into routine pathological workflows, it has the potential to enable rapid, accurate, and systematic screening of patients for targeted therapies.
