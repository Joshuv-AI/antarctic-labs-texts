Iterative Negotiation and Oversight: A Case Study in Decentralized Air Traffic Management Journal: Transportation Research Part C
Jaehan Im, John-Paul Clarke, Ufuk Topcu, David Fridovich-Keil

Abstract
Achieving consensus among noncooperative agents remains challenging in decentralized multi-agent systems, where agents often have conflicting preferences. Existing coordination methods enable agents to reach consensus without a centralized coordinator, but do not provide formal guarantees on system-level objectives such as efficiency or fairness. To address this limitation, we propose an iterative negotiation and oversight framework that augments a decentralized negotiation mechanism with taxation-like oversight. The framework builds upon the trading auction for consensus , enabling noncooperative agents with conflicting preferences to negotiate through asset trading while preserving valuation privacy. We introduce an oversight mechanism, which implements a taxation-like intervention that guides decentralized negotiation toward system-efficient and equitable outcomes while also regulating how fast the framework converges. We establish theoretical guarantees of finite-time termination and derive bounds linking system efficiency and convergence rate to the level of central intervention. A case study based on the collaborative trajectory options program , a rerouting initiative in U.S. air traffic management, demonstrates that the framework can reliably achieve consensus among noncooperative airspace sector managers, and reveals how the level of intervention regulates the relationship between system efficiency and convergence speed. Taken together, the theoretical and experimental results indicate that the proposed framework provides a general mechanism for decentralized coordination in noncooperative multi-agent systems while safeguarding system-level objectives.

3
Preliminaries: Decentralized noncooperative negotiation via
TACo

We employ the
trading auction for consensus
(
TACo
) as a baseline mechanism for noncooperative negotiation
[
18
]
.

TACo
enables self-interested stakeholders to agree on a common decision while pursuing different objectives and withholding private information.

TACo
operates as a structured
give-and-take
process, where agents iteratively trade a secondary asset (
e.g.
, emission credits or monetary tokens) to persuade others to accept their preferred choice. It provides a fully decentralized procedure through which consensus naturally emerges from agents’ self-interested behavior and yields an outcome that remains incentive-aligned for all participants, without centralized coordination or direct bilateral communication.

Unlike standard single-round voting-based consensus mechanisms,
TACo
introduces a dynamic trading structure that aligns incentives through self-interested behavior. Although there is no formal guarantee, this mechanism often yields outcomes with a lower overall system cost and improved fairness compared to voting, as trading redistributes the burden among agents with differing valuations.

Mechanism overview

TACo

2.

At each step, agent

Upon this selection, the common offer and pay units are updated according to

\displaystyle P_{ij_{i}^{*}}

\displaystyle\leftarrow P_{ij_{i}^{*}}+nd,\quad\forall i\in\mathbf{N},

(2)

\displaystyle O_{ij_{i}^{*}}

\displaystyle\leftarrow O_{ij_{i}^{*}}+d,\quad\forall i\in\mathbf{N},

A

[
18
]
.

3.2
Properties of
TACo

The properties summarized below make
TACo
a practical foundation for decentralized coordination:

Incentive-aligned process

Each agent acts solely in its self-interest, and consensus emerges as the natural consequence of individual trading decisions rather than cooperative agreement. The resulting outcome also remains incentive-aligned for all agents.

Privacy preservation

Agents never reveal their private valuations (
b_{i}
) or intrinsic costs (
C_{ij}
); only aggregate preference expressions are publicly observable.

Decentralized mechanism

TACo
does not require either auctioneer nor central coordinator. All agents follow identical public rules and rely solely on broadcast information, enabling a fully decentralized implementation.

Finite termination guarantee

The algorithm provably converges within a finite number of steps.

For these reasons, we adopt
TACo
as the foundation of our negotiation framework.

3.3
Limitations of noncooperative negotiation without oversight

While
TACo
enables self-interested agents to reach consensus in a fully decentralized manner, it cannot change the underlying choices available to the agents, some or all of which may be poor.
Furthermore,
TACo
does not optimize for any concept of system optimality, and is only guaranteed to find a solution that is unilaterally acceptable to all agents.

In safety-critical domains, these limitations motivate the need for an additional oversight mechanism that guides the negotiation toward system-level objectives. The following section introduces our main contribution; an iterative framework that embeds such an oversight into the
TACo
-based negotiation.

4

Oversight role and safeguard

As the shared term in the effective cost
Equation

and thereby controls the amount of assets exchanged during negotiation, influencing how quickly the reserve constraints are satisfied. For example, a smaller
\kappa
(larger asset values) induces larger trading steps, which means that agents can reach consensus while trading fewer assets (not depleting their reserves), and ultimately converge more quickly.

Theorem 2

(Sufficient condition for termination in terms of
\kappa
)
.

If
\alpha_{r}\geq\kappa B_{\max}
at some round
r
, then every payer respects its asset reserve constraint (
c_{j}^{(r)}\leq R_{j}
for all
j\in W_{r}
), and the process terminates.

The proof is provided in

.
∎

Theorem

2
indicates that termination is guaranteed once the normalization factor
\alpha_{r}
exceeds a threshold determined by the product of the tax parameter
\kappa
and the upper bound on individual transfer values
B_{\max}
defined in
Lemma

1
.
This provides a direct link between
\kappa
and
r_{\mathrm{term}}
, the number of rounds required for convergence.

Corollary 2

(Upper bound on termination round)
.

With
\alpha_{1}=1
and
\alpha_{r}=r
, the process must terminate once
r\geq\kappa B_{\max}

r_{\mathrm{term}}\;\leq\;\big\lceil\kappa B_{\max}\big\rceil.

(16)

Equivalently, for a desired iteration budget
r_{\mathrm{des}}\in\mathbb{N}

\big\lceil\kappa B_{\max}\big\rceil\;\leq\;r_{\mathrm{des}}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\kappa\;\leq\;\frac{r_{\mathrm{des}}}{B_{\max}}.

(17)

The proof is provided in

.
∎

This result explicitly shows that
\kappa
acts as a tuning parameter that regulates the convergence rate: smaller
\kappa
values yield faster termination, while larger
\kappa
values provide finer equilibrium adjustments that take place over more rounds.

5.3
The relationship between the taxation parameter
\kappa
and system efficiency

We now analyze how
\kappa
affects the
system-level efficiency
of the resulting consensus,
that is, the gap between the achieved outcome and the global optimum. Intuitively, a larger
\kappa
leads to a greater number of rounds and increases the effect of the coordination factor. This promotes improved fairness among agents, which in turn leads to outcomes closer to the system optimum, at the expense of more rounds.

We first formalize the definition of
system-optimal outcome
.

Definition 1

(System-optimal outcome)
.

Let
u=\tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}^{n}
denote a uniform weight vector, where
\mathbf{1}^{n}=[1,\cdots,1]\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We define the
system-level cost
as

J_{\mathrm{sys}}(o):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{i}^{\mathrm{ind}}(o)=nS_{u}(o),

(18)

where
S_{u}(o)=u^{\top}J^{\mathrm{ind}}(o)
as defined in
Equation

5
. A corresponding
system-optimal choice
is

o^{\mathrm{opt}}\in\arg\min_{o\in\mathcal{O}}J_{\mathrm{sys}}(o).

(19)

Thus, the objective of the oversight is to steer the negotiation outcome
o_{r}^{\texttt{TACo}}
in a way that makes
J_{sys}(o_{r}^{\texttt{TACo}})
closer to
J_{sys}(o^{\mathrm{opt}})
.

Step 1: Selection error

The following lemma bounds the
selection error
, that is, the deviation between the selected choice and the one minimizing the normalized shared score
S_{\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{(r)}}(o)
.

Lemma 3

(Selection error bound)
.

Fix round
r
and let
o^{\dagger}\in\arg\min_{o}S_{\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{(r)}}(o)
. Then

0\leq\underbrace{S_{\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{(r)}}(o_{r}^{\texttt{TACo}})-S_{\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{(r)}}(o^{\dagger})}_{\displaystyle\eta_{r}}\leq\frac{B_{\max}}{\alpha_{r}}.

(20)

Proof.

The proof is provided in
A
.
∎

As
\alpha_{r}
increases, the selection error
\eta_{r}
shrinks to zero.

Step 2: System optimality gap

Building on the selection error bound in
Lemma

3
, we now bound the system–optimality gap as a function of the normalization factor
\alpha_{r}
.

Lemma 4

(Relationship between
\alpha
and system–optimality gap bound)
.

Recall that
J_{\mathrm{sys}}(o)=nS_{u}(o)
with
u=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}^{n}
and
\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{(r)}=\mathbf{w}^{(r)}/\alpha_{r}
. Let
\delta_{r}:=\|\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{(r)}-u\|_{1}
, then

0\leq J_{\mathrm{sys}}\big(o_{r}^{\texttt{TACo}}\big)-J_{\mathrm{sys}}(o^{\mathrm{opt}})\leq n\left(B_{\max}\,\delta_{r}+\eta_{r}\right)\leq n\left(B_{\max}\,\delta_{r}+\frac{B_{\max}}{\alpha_{r}}\right).

(21)

Proof.

The proof is provided in
A
.
∎

This lemma shows that the overall system inefficiency is governed by two quantities:
the
weight misalignment

\delta_{r}
between agents’ effective coordination weights and the uniform distribution,
and the
selection error

\eta_{r}
from
Lemma

3
.
As
\alpha_{r}
increases, the selection error term diminishes, while the misalignment term decreases as
\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{(r)}
approaches the uniform weight
u
.
Ultimately, as
\alpha_{r}
diminishes the
\frac{B_{\max}}{\alpha_{r}}
, the system efficiency is bounded by
nB_{\max}\delta_{r}
.

Step 3: A necessary
\kappa
for a target gap

We finally translate the previous
\alpha_{r}
–optimality gap relationship into a necessary condition on
\kappa
to satisfy a target optimality gap,
\tau
. From
Equation

6
, note that the
\alpha_{r}
increases by one at each round and is initialized as
\alpha_{1}=1
, so
\alpha_{r}=r
.

Theorem 3

(Necessary
\kappa
for a target optimality gap)
.

Let
\alpha_{r}=r
and
r_{\max}:=\lceil\kappa B_{\max}\rceil
, cf.
Corollary

2
. Assume
\delta_{r}\leq\bar{\delta}
for all
r\leq r_{\max}
and let
\tau>0
denote the desired optimality gap. To certify at termination that

J_{\mathrm{sys}}\big(o_{r_{\mathrm{term}}}^{\texttt{TACo}}\big)-J_{\mathrm{sys}}(o^{\mathrm{opt}})\leq\tau,

(22)

it is
necessary
that
\tau>nB_{\max}\bar{\delta}
and

r_{\max}\;\geq\;\frac{nB_{\max}}{\ \tau-nB_{\max}\bar{\delta}\ },\quad\text{equivalently}\quad\kappa\;\geq\;\frac{1}{B_{\max}}\left\lfloor\frac{nB_{\max}}{\ \tau-nB_{\max}\bar{\delta}\ }\right\rfloor.

(23)

Proof.

The proof is provided in
A
.
∎

Corollary 3

(Eliminating
\bar{\delta}
to obtain a practical bound)
.

Since
\delta_{r}=\|\bar{\mathbf{w}}^{(r)}-u\|_{1}\leq\sigma_{\max}
with
\sigma_{\max}=2(1-\tfrac{1}{n})
, plugging
\bar{\delta}=\sigma_{\max}
into the previous theorem (valid whenever
\tau>2(n-1)B_{\max}
) yields

\kappa\;\geq\;\frac{1}{B_{\max}}\left\lfloor\frac{nB_{\max}}{\ \tau-2(n-1)B_{\max}\ }\right\rfloor.

(24)

Proof.

The proof is provided in
A
.
∎

System efficiency improves with (i) better weight alignment (
\delta_{r}\downarrow
)—reflecting the balance in agents’ coordination influence—and (ii) more rounds (
\alpha_{r}\uparrow
).
In practice, misalignment often stems from uneven reserve distributions: agents with larger reserves tend to experience smaller shortfalls and thus contribute less influence in
\mathbf{w}
, whereas those with smaller reserves contribute more, amplifying the misalignment across rounds.

5.4
Concise guide for the central coordinator

We combine the analytical results derived in this section to provide a quick-reference guide
for central coordinators to decide which
\kappa
value to select in order to achieve a desired system behavior.

1.

To ensure that the negotiation process terminates within
r_{\mathrm{des}}
rounds,
choose
\kappa\leq\frac{r_{\mathrm{des}}}{B_{\max}}
, cf.
Equation

17
.

2.

To enable the possibility of achieving a target optimality gap
\tau
,
choose
\kappa\geq\frac{1}{B_{\max}}\left\lfloor\frac{nB_{\max}}{\ \tau-2(n-1)B_{\max}\ }\right\rfloor
, cf.
Equation

24
.

3.

To guarantee a system optimality gap smaller than
\tau
,
enforce the number of rounds to satisfy

r_{\mathrm{des}}\geq\frac{nB_{\max}}{\tau-nB_{\max}\delta_{r}}
, cf.
Equation

21
.

Practical remarks

1.

The target optimality gap
\tau
cannot be smaller than
nB_{\max}\delta_{r}
.

2.

There exists an intrinsic trade-off between convergence speed and system efficiency.
Once
r_{\mathrm{des}}
is fixed, it limits the range of achievable
\tau
,
and vice-versa.

Having established the theoretical framework for decentralized negotiation and oversight,
we now demonstrate its practical application to the
collaborative trajectory options program
(CTOP).
This case study illustrates how the proposed framework can decentralize the existing air traffic management operation paradigm, which traditionally involves centralized decision-making. The overall flow of both the conventional and decentralized CTOP frameworks
is summarized in
Figure

.

6.1
Collaborative trajectory options program (CTOP)

The CTOP is a traffic management initiative designed in the United States to mitigate airspace congestion by coordinating trajectory options for multiple flights
[
14
,
5
,
27
]
. In CTOP, each airline submits a set of alternative flight trajectories, referred to as
trajectory options sets
(TOS), where each flight is associated with
q
trajectory options that differ in route, altitude, or estimated arrival time. A centralized decision-making entity evaluates these submitted trajectories based on predicted sector demand, weather conditions, and sector capacity constraints. The central controller then assigns a trajectory to each flight, balancing global efficiency with equity among airlines.

6.2
Decentralized CTOP

While CTOP enhances operational flexibility by considering the preferences of each individual airline, it does not account for the preferences of the individual airspace sector managers—or
air route traffic control centers
(ARTCCs). Thus, the current paradigm still presents a potential for further decentralization, given the scalability demands of CTOP and the practical limits on cross-center information sharing
[
8
]
. Building on the negotiation and oversight framework introduced in
Section

4
, we develop a decentralized formulation of the CTOP,
where each airspace sector, ARTCC, is modeled as a self-interested
agent
responsible for assigning trajectory options for all flights traversing its region to minimize its own operational congestion.
Unlike the centralized CTOP, where a single decision maker determines the global schedule, the decentralized CTOP allows sectors to suggest their preferred bundles of trajectory options and negotiate to reach a feasible agreement.

6.2.1
Candidate Trajectory Bundle Generation

Each ARTCC
i\in\mathbf{N}
corresponds to an agent in the proposed framework.
All agents operate over the same set of
p
flights,
and each agent
i
constructs a
candidate trajectory bundle
(CTB)
that represents its preferred combination of trajectory options for the flights.
A CTB for agent
i
is expressed as a vector
o_{i}=[o_{i1},\ldots,o_{ip}]\in\{1,\dots,q\}^{p}
,
where each element
o_{if}
denotes the trajectory option selected for flight
f
.
The common set of all possible CTBs is denoted by
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\{1,\dots,q\}^{p}
, and the collection of CTBs selected by all agents at round
r
is
\mathcal{O}^{(r)}=\{o_{1}^{(r)},\ldots,o_{n}^{(r)}\}
.

Each agent
i
evaluates the congestion cost of a bundle
o_{i}\in\mathcal{O}
using an individual cost function
J_{i}^{\mathrm{ind}}:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
:

J_{i}^{\mathrm{ind}}(o_{i})=\texttt{EigenComplexity}(o_{i}).

(25)

\texttt{EigenComplexity}(\cdot)
is a scalar congestion metric computed
from the leading eigenvalue of the sector traffic covariance matrix
[
26
]
,
providing a quantitative measure of sector-level traffic complexity. Each agent constructs its CTB
o_{i}^{(r)}
under the effective cost formulation defined in
Equation

7
.

6.2.2
Noncooperative Negotiation and Asset Valuation

Given the set of candidate bundles
\mathcal{O}^{(r)}=\{o_{1}^{(r)},\ldots,o_{n}^{(r)}\}
at round
r
,
the
n
ARTCC agents engage in a
TACo
-based negotiation to determine
a mutually agreed combination of trajectory options.
Each agent participates in
TACo
backed by its asset reserves
R_{i}
,
which represent a pool of abstract credits, such as monetary tokens or emission credits.
Recall that the asset valuation is defined as

b_{i}=\frac{1}{\kappa R_{i}},

(26)

where
\kappa
is the global taxation parameter broadcast by the oversight mechanism per
Equation

8
. After each negotiation round, the oversight mechanism evaluates reserve shortfalls (cf.
Equation

9
) and updates the coordination factor
\mathbf{w}^{(r)}
according to the shortfall-based rule described in
Section

4.4
.

7.2
Baselines

We compare the proposed framework against three baselines:

1.

Centralized CTOP (C-CTOP):

A centralized optimizer determines the system-wide optimal combination of TOS assignments by minimizing the following cost function
J:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
:

J(o)=\sum_{i\in\mathbf{N}}J_{i}^{\mathrm{ind}}(o),\quad o\in\mathcal{O}.

(27)

This represents the upper performance bound under perfect information and coordination.

2.

First-Come-First-Served CTOP (F-CTOP):
A sequential, priority-based centralized approach. Flight plans are processed in order of their scheduled departure times; the coordinator selects the most efficient trajectory option of the earlier flights first, while later flights are processed in a sequential way.

3.

Voting-based coordination:
A one-shot decentralized mechanism in which each ARTCC votes on the CTBs generated without coordination (
i.e.
,
\mathbf{w}=[0,\dots,0]\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
). The option receiving the majority of votes is selected, and ties are resolved randomly.

8
Conclusion

We propose an iterative negotiation and oversight framework that enables coordination among self-interested agents while safeguarding system-level performance through taxation-like intervention.
We establish theoretical guarantees of finite-round convergence and derive explicit bounds linking the level of central intervention to both convergence rate and system efficiency.
These results provide practical guidance for a central coordinator in selecting the intervention level to balance convergence speed, efficiency, and fairness.

To validate the framework, we developed a decentralized variant of the
collaborative trajectory options program
(CTOP)—a traffic management initiative currently implemented in the United States national airspace system—and evaluated it in a realistic sector-level coordination scenario.
Simulation results confirm that the framework converges in all cases and that adjusting the intervention level effectively regulates the convergence rate, efficiency, and fairness.
The oversight does not directly optimize the system but implicitly guides agents toward coordinated behavior, thereby preserving autonomy while maintaining system-level safety.
Overall, the results demonstrate a practical pathway for realizing noncooperative, decentralized air traffic management.

The same principle can be generalized to other multi-agent systems that require reliable noncooperative coordination beyond air traffic management.
Future work may focus on tightening the theoretical bounds, extending the framework to multi-asset trading schemes, and developing decentralized safeguard mechanisms that ensure fairness and efficiency without centralized oversight or taxation.

Proofs for the Theorems and Lemmas

Proof of
Lemma

1
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
denote the finite set of all feasible choices, and let

\mathcal{O}^{(r)}\subseteq\mathcal{O}
denote the set of choices selected by at least one agent at round
r
. Recall that at outer iteration
r
the effective cost for agent
i
is

J_{i}^{w^{(r)}}(o)=\frac{1}{\alpha_{r}}\Big(J_{i}^{\mathrm{ind}}(o)+S_{w^{(r)}}(o)\Big),\quad S_{w^{(r)}}(o):=(w^{(r)})^{\top}J^{\mathrm{ind}}(o),

(28)

where
\alpha_{r}=\|w^{(r)}\|_{1}+1
and
\alpha_{r+1}=\alpha_{r}+1
.

Since

, we have

|S_{w^{(r)}}(o)-S_{w^{(r)}}(o^{\prime})|\leq\|w^{(r)}\|_{1}\,\max_{i}|J_{i}^{\mathrm{ind}}(o)-J_{i}^{\mathrm{ind}}(o^{\prime})|\leq\alpha_{r}B_{\max},

(29)

where

B_{\max}:=\max_{i}\max_{o,o^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}}|J_{i}^{\mathrm{ind}}(o)-J_{i}^{\mathrm{ind}}(o^{\prime})|<\infty,

(30)

using boundedness of intrinsic costs.

Hence, for any

\big|J_{i}^{w^{(r)}}(o)-J_{i}^{w^{(r)}}(o^{\prime})\big|\leq\frac{1}{\alpha_{r}}\Big(|J_{i}^{\mathrm{ind}}(o)-J_{i}^{\mathrm{ind}}(o^{\prime})|+|S_{w^{(r)}}(o)-S_{w^{(r)}}(o^{\prime})|\Big)\leq\frac{B_{\max}}{\alpha_{r}}.

(31)

Thus,

\max_{o,o^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}^{(r)}}|J_{i}^{w^{(r)}}(o)-J_{i}^{w^{(r)}}(o^{\prime})|\xrightarrow[r\to\infty]{}0,

(32)

since
\alpha_{r}\to\infty
.
This shows that the effective cost spread within
\mathcal{O}^{(r)}
shrinks to zero as
r
increases.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

2
.

and let

Proof of
Theorem

1
.

By
Corollary

1
, every payer
j\in W_{r}
satisfies

|t_{j}^{(r)}|\;\leq\;\frac{B_{\max}}{\alpha_{r}}.

(42)

Transfers are quantized as

|t_{j}^{(r)}|=c_{j}^{(r)}b_{j},\qquad b_{j}=\frac{1}{\kappa R_{j}},

(43)

so

c_{j}^{(r)}\;\leq\;\frac{B_{\max}}{\alpha_{r}}\,\kappa R_{j}.

(44)

Since
\alpha_{r}
increases by one each round, there exists
\bar{r}
such that

\alpha_{r}\geq\kappa B_{\max}
for all
r\geq\bar{r}
.
For these rounds,

c_{j}^{(r)}\;\leq\;R_{j},

(45)

so no payer exceeds its reserve constraint.
Thus, the process must terminate in finite rounds.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

From the proof of
Theorem

1
, we have for any payer
j

and round
r
that

c_{j}^{(r)}\;\leq\;\frac{B_{\max}}{\alpha_{r}}\,\kappa R_{j}.

(46)

Therefore, whenever
\alpha_{r}\geq\kappa B_{\max}
, it follows that

c_{j}^{(r)}\;\leq\;R_{j}

(47)

for all payers
j
, so every reserve constraint is satisfied and the process
terminates.
∎

Proof of
Corollary

2
.

Since
\alpha_{1}=1
and
\alpha_{r}
increases by one at each round, we have
\alpha_{r}=r
.
By
Theorem

2
, the process terminates once
\alpha_{r}\geq\kappa B_{\max}
,
that is, once
r\geq\kappa B_{\max}
.
Hence the termination round satisfies

r_{\mathrm{term}}\;\leq\;\big\lceil\kappa B_{\max}\big\rceil.

(48)

Equivalently, for a desired iteration budget
r_{\mathrm{des}}
, termination is guaranteed if

\kappa\;\leq\;\frac{r_{\mathrm{des}}}{B_{\max}}.

(49)

∎

Proof of
Lemma

3
.

Let
o^{\dagger}\in\arg\min_{o}S_{\bar{w}^{(r)}}(o)
and let
i^{\star}
be the supporting
player for the
TACo
outcome
o_{r}^{\texttt{TACo}}
, i.e.,

S_{\bar{w}^{(r)}}(o_{r}^{\texttt{TACo}})+\frac{1}{\alpha_{r}}J_{i^{\star}}^{\mathrm{ind}}(o_{r}^{\texttt{TACo}})\;\leq\;S_{\bar{w}^{(r)}}(o^{\dagger})+\frac{1}{\alpha_{r}}J_{i^{\star}}^{\mathrm{ind}}(o^{\dagger}).

(50)

Rearranging yields

0\;\leq\;S_{\bar{w}^{(r)}}(o_{r}^{\texttt{TACo}})-S_{\bar{w}^{(r)}}(o^{\dagger})\;\leq\;\frac{J_{i^{\star}}^{\mathrm{ind}}(o^{\dagger})-J_{i^{\star}}^{\mathrm{ind}}(o_{r}^{\texttt{TACo}})}{\alpha_{r}}.

(51)

Since intrinsic costs differ by at most
B_{\max}
over
\mathcal{O}
,

0\leq S_{\bar{w}^{(r)}}(o_{r}^{\texttt{TACo}})-S_{\bar{w}^{(r)}}(o^{\dagger})\;\leq\;\frac{B_{\max}}{\alpha_{r}}.

(52)

Thus the selection error satisfies
\eta_{r}\leq B_{\max}/\alpha_{r}
.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

4
.

Recall
J_{\mathrm{sys}}(o)=nS_{u}(o)
where
u=\tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}
.
For any weight vector
\bar{w}^{(r)}=w^{(r)}/\alpha_{r}
, we may write

S_{u}(o_{r}^{\texttt{TACo}})-S_{u}(o^{\mathrm{opt}})=\big(\bar{w}^{(r)}-u\big)^{\top}\big(J^{\mathrm{ind}}(o_{r}^{\texttt{TACo}})-J^{\mathrm{ind}}(o^{\mathrm{opt}})\big)+\eta_{r},

(53)

where
\eta_{r}=S_{\bar{w}^{(r)}}(o_{r}^{\texttt{TACo}})-S_{\bar{w}^{(r)}}(o^{\dagger})

and
o^{\dagger}\in\arg\min_{o}S_{\bar{w}^{(r)}}(o)
.
Taking absolute values and using

\|J^{\mathrm{ind}}(o_{r}^{\texttt{TACo}})-J^{\mathrm{ind}}(o^{\mathrm{opt}})\|_{\infty}\leq B_{\max}
and

\delta_{r}=\|\bar{w}^{(r)}-u\|_{1}
gives

0\leq S_{u}(o_{r}^{\texttt{TACo}})-S_{u}(o^{\mathrm{opt}})\leq B_{\max}\,\delta_{r}+\eta_{r}.

(54)

Multiplying by
n
yields

0\leq J_{\mathrm{sys}}(o_{r}^{\texttt{TACo}})-J_{\mathrm{sys}}(o^{\mathrm{opt}})\leq n\big(B_{\max}\,\delta_{r}+\eta_{r}\big),

(55)

which proves the claim.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

3
.

Since
\alpha_{r}=r
and
r_{\max}=\lceil\kappa B_{\max}\rceil
,
assume
\delta_{r}\leq\bar{\delta}
for all
r\leq r_{\max}
.
From
Lemma

4
,

J_{\mathrm{sys}}(o_{r}^{\texttt{TACo}})-J_{\mathrm{sys}}(o^{\mathrm{opt}})\;\leq\;n\!\left(B_{\max}\bar{\delta}+\frac{B_{\max}}{r}\right).

(56)

To achieve a target system gap
\tau
, it is necessary that

\tau>nB_{\max}\bar{\delta}
and

r\;\geq\;\frac{nB_{\max}}{\tau-nB_{\max}\bar{\delta}}.

(57)

Because
r_{\mathrm{term}}\leq r_{\max}=\lceil\kappa B_{\max}\rceil
,
the above condition requires

\lceil\kappa B_{\max}\rceil\;\geq\;\frac{nB_{\max}}{\tau-nB_{\max}\bar{\delta}},

(58)

which yields the stated lower bound on
\kappa
.
∎

Proof of
Corollary

3
.

Since
u=\tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}
and
\bar{w}^{(r)}\in\Delta^{n}
, the weight
misalignment satisfies

\delta_{r}=\|\bar{w}^{(r)}-u\|_{1}\;\leq\;2\!\left(1-\tfrac{1}{n}\right)=:\sigma_{\max}.

(59)

Substituting
\bar{\delta}=\sigma_{\max}
into the bound of

Theorem

3
(valid when
\tau>2(n-1)B_{\max}
) gives

\kappa\;\geq\;\frac{1}{B_{\max}}\left\lfloor\frac{nB_{\max}}{\tau-2(n-1)B_{\max}}\right\rfloor,

(60)

which yields the stated geometric lower bound on
\kappa
.
∎
