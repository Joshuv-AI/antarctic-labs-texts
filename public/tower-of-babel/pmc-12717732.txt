Clinical and toxicological epidemiology of intoxications by new psychoactive substances in Reunion Island
Authors: Adrien Maillot, Joris Guyon, Maeva Ma-Tsi-Leong, Laetitia Berly, Flore Weisse, Aurore Mahé, Gael Le Roux, Frédérique Nativel, Amélie Daveluy, Bérénice Puech, Adrien Marteau, Marion Leroy, Olivia Plé, Olivier Maillard, Sophie Bastard, David Mete
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNew psychoactive substances (NPS) represent a growing concern for emergency medicine due to their unpredictable toxicity and limited detectability in routine screening. Reunion Island, French territories in the Indian Ocean, NPS-related intoxications have been suspected for several years, but biological confirmation has remained scarce. This study aimed to characterise confirmed cases of acute NPS intoxication and describe their clinical features, toxicological profiles, and implications for emergency care and public health surveillance.MethodsA retrospective multicentre study was conducted in four public hospitals of Reunion Island between August 2022 and March 2024. Patients presenting with suspected NPS intoxication were mainly identified in emergency and intensive care units. Biological samples were analysed using untargeted high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS). Clinical, demographic, and toxicological data were extracted from medical records through a standardised electronic case report form. Confirmed NPS were grouped by pharmacological class, and their clinical manifestations were compared with non-NPS cases.ResultsAmong 176 suspected cases, 152 (86%) had biological analyses performed. The median age was 29 years, and 77% were men. Most exposures were intentional (77.3%), but unintentional cases—more frequent among women—were also reported. Fourteen intoxications were biologically confirmed, involving synthetic cathinones (dimethylpentylone, hexedrone), cannabinoids (MDMB-4en-PINACA, HHC), a novel opioid (protonitazene), and NM-2AI. Clinical severity ranged from agitation and sedation to cardiovascular collapse, rhabdomyolysis, and respiratory failure. Inhalation was the predominant route of exposure. Alcohol co-exposure was less common in confirmed NPS cases than in conventional drug intoxications.ConclusionThis first series of toxicologically confirmed NPS intoxications on Reunion Island highlights the clinical diversity and potential severity of these emerging substances. For emergency departments, early recognition of unexplained toxidromes, systematic biological sampling, and close collaboration with toxicology laboratories are essential. Strengthened analytical capacity and coordinated toxicovigilance networks are urgently needed to improve detection, guide patient management, and anticipate emerging psychoactive threats.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12245-025-01055-3.

Introduction

New psychoactive substances (NPS) have emerged over the past two decades as a major public health challenge worldwide [1]. Designed to mimic the effects of classical drugs while evading legal control [2]; NPS often display unpredictable pharmacological properties due to structural modifications that significantly influence their pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic characteristics [3]. They may also be sought for their capacity to produce stronger or more intense psychoactive effects than classical substances [4].

By the end of 2023, over 950 NPS had been identified by the European Monitoring Centre for Drugs and Drug Addiction (EMCDDA) [5], with rapid proliferation facilitated by the Internet and illicit markets since the early 2000s [6]. In France, 86 NPS were detected between 2008 and 2018 [7], predominantly synthetic cathinones (SC), synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists (SCRAs) [8], and new synthetic opioids (NSOs).

In the French Indian Ocean islands, substance use has historically involved cannabis, diverted medicines such as trihexyphenidyl and clonazepam [9], and occasional cocaine [10]. SCRAs emerged in Mayotte in 2013 [11], intensifying among young men [10]. In Reunion Island, SCRAs—locally known as “tabac chimique” or “chamane”—appeared in 2016, with marked intensification from 2018 [10]. In 2019, health alert highlighted rising suspected SCRA intoxications [12];, yet limited toxicological confirmation hampered targeted harm-reduction measures.

Objective

Despite growing clinical suspicion, no study has yet provided biologically confirmed evidence of NPS intoxications in Reunion Island. The lack of local toxicological data limits both clinical management and public health responses.

This study therefore aimed to characterise biologically confirmed cases of acute NPS intoxication between August 2022 and March 2024 on Reunion Island, describing the substances involved, clinical presentations, and implications for toxicovigilance and emergency care.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective case series including all patients with suspected NPS intoxication admitted to public hospitals on Reunion Island between August 2022 and March 2024. The study involved four participating hospitals: Hospital West Reunion, University hospital North, University hospital South and Hospital East Reunion. Each site followed identical inclusion and reporting criteria. As the study funding covered the otherwise unaffordable cost of NPS analyses, patient inclusion was monitored in real time.

Case inclusion criteria

Cases were included if an NPS toxicological analysis was prescribed within 24 h of suspected exposure. Suspected cases were defined as acute clinical presentation suggestive of NPS intoxication (e.g. agitation, altered consciousness, sympathomimetic or sedative toxidrome) not explained by conventional toxicology or declared psychoactive substance use.

Data collection

Clinical data were extracted from medical records into an electronic case report form (e-CRF) by trained personnel, validated through automated checks and reviewed by the study coordinator and data management team using Ennov Clinical software. Data collection procedures were harmonised across all participating hospitals to ensure methodological consistency. Data completeness exceeded 95% for all core variables; missing data were not imputed.

Definitions

Medicine misuse: detection of a pharmaceutical analytically but not prescribed, administered, or self-reported.

Intentional use: voluntary self-administration for recreational purposes.

Unintentional use: exposure without knowledge or consent (e.g., accidental or chemical submission).

Classification relied on clinical history, exposure circumstances, and patient declarations.

Biological analysis

Samples collected within 24 h were stored at − 20 °C and shipped to the toxicology laboratory of Bordeaux University Hospital, along with a standardised data sheet. Targeted or non-targeted analyses were conducted based on clinical context using a validated method previously reported [13]. Mass spectrometry, including high-resolution (HRMS), was used. Identification relied on reference spectra or expert interpretation for emerging compounds not yet included in standard libraries (more details on the analytical method and interpretation Appendix 1).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean or median (IQR), categorical as frequency (%). Comparisons used Student’s t-test, Mann–Whitney U, Chi-square, or Fisher’s exact test; p ≤ 0.05 was significant. Analyses were performed with Python 3.10.

Ethical approval

Approved by CNIL (n°921274, Feb 2022) and an institutional review board (AP 2022-19). Patient non-opposition was obtained via information leaflet.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between August 2022 and March 2024, a total of 176 suspected cases of acute intoxication involving new psychoactive substances (NPS) were included in the study (Fig. 1). Of these, 152 patients (86%) underwent biological analysis, and 14 cases (9.2%) were confirmed positive for NPS.

The median age of all cases was 27 years [Q1-Q3: 21.0–34.0] and comprised 110 males (62.5%) and 66 females (37.5%) (Table 1). Young adults aged 18–34 years represented the largest group (60.8%). A history of alcohol consumption was reported by 38.1% of all patients (n = 67) (Table 2). Alcohol use was significantly less common among patients with confirmed NPS exposure (n = 1) compared to the non-NPS group (40.7%, n = 66) (p = 0.018). Previous use of psychoactive substances (excluding alcohol and tobacco) was reported by 46.0% of all patients (n = 81). This prevalence was significantly higher in the NPS group (92.9%) than in the non-NPS group (42.0%) (p = 0.001). Regarding substance categories, most patients with a history of substance use had used illicit drugs. Among those with confirmed NPS exposure, 64.3% had previously used illicit drugs, and nearly 30% reported misuse of medicines, either alone or in combination. These data highlight a significant association between NPS involvement and complex patterns of prior substance use, particularly polydrug use (p = 0.001).

Toxicological findings

Of the 176 cases included in the study, 86% benefited from toxicological biological sampling. The most frequently detected molecule classified under the “Illicit drug” category was tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), identified in 51 cases (Fig. 2). Other psychoactive substances included 14 detections of cocaine, 11 of MDMA, 7 of MDA, and 3 of amphetamine. A separate category, labelled “Cutting agent”, was observed exclusively in association with cocaine.

NPS accounted for fewer identifications (n = 15) involving 14 patients but showed notable chemical and pharmacological diversity, as described below.

The “Misuse medicine” category comprised a highly heterogeneous group of substances. This category included molecules such as clonazepam (n = 6), codeine (n = 4), tramadol (n = 5), trihexyphenidyl (n = 6), and risperidone (n = 3). Other identified substances included opioid analgesics (e.g. morphine, oxycodone, oxycodone, tilidine), ketamine, demoxepam, and various antihistamines, among others.

Clinical manifestations

Neuropsychiatric symptoms were the most prevalent (Table 5), notably impaired consciousness (51.7%) and agitation (40.9%). Other frequent signs included memory impairment (18.2%), anxiety (17.0%), hallucinations (14.8%), seizures (11.4%), and psychotic episodes (5.7%). Cardiovascular effects such as tachycardia (31.3%), hypotension and bradycardia (4.6% each) were also observed. Gastrointestinal symptoms, including vomiting (16.6%), nausea (8.0%) were commonly reported. Asthenia was present in 13.1% of cases.

Confirmed NPS intoxications by substance class (Table 6)

Dimethylpentylone: 6 cases (7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 13)

Six patients tested positive for dimethylpentylone. Presentations ranged from mild symptoms (e.g. asthenia, anxiety, headaches) to severe complications. Case 10 developed life-threatening features including arterial hypotension (80/30 mmHg), mitral valve insufficiency, infective endocarditis, pulmonary oedema, GCS = 3, and cardiopulmonary arrest, requiring an 88-day hospital stay including 11 days in ICU. Other cases involved myoclonus (Case 7), epigastralgia and peripheral tremor (Case 9), and acute kidney injury with urinary retention (Case 11).

MDMB-4en-Pinaca: 4 cases (3, 4, 6 and 14)

Two clustered cases (3 and 4) involved shared use of a joint obtained from the same dealer; Case 4 also tested positive for protonitazen. Case 3, who smoked one-quarter, developed bradypnea, GCS = 13, miosis, and rhabdomyolysis (CPK 695 mmol/L). Case 4, who smoked three-quarters of the same joint, experienced more severe symptoms, including hypothermia (35.8 °C), arterial hypotension (88/40 mmHg), bradypnea (RR 6), miosis, and acute kidney injury (creatinine 114 µmol/L). This patient required ICU admission for five days and developed aspiration pneumonia.

Two other cases (Cases 6 and 14) were also positive for MDMB-4en-Pinaca. Case 6 reported voluntary use of SCRAs mixed with tobacco and presented bradycardia (50 bpm), mild psychomotor agitation, and aggressive behaviour, requiring a brief ICU stay (1 day). Case 14 exhibited asthenia, tachycardia, limb stiffness, dyskinesia, muscle cramps, and hypokalaemia (2.6 mmol/L).

Discussion

Overall results

This multicentre study provides the first biologically confirmed overview of NPS intoxications in Reunion Island from August 2022 to March 2024. Among 176 suspected cases, 14 were confirmed via toxicological analysis.

Unsurprisingly, we identified misused medicines common in local patterns, such as trihexyphenidyl and clonazepam [9], and illicit substances like MDMA and cocaine, consistent with increased seizures over the past five years [10, 14]. Cannabis remains a major contributor to hospital admissions.

Alcohol was often co-detected, especially with THC, MDMA, trihexyphenidyl, and clonazepam. However, alcohol was significantly less frequent among confirmed NPS cases, suggesting different use profiles. Alcohol co-consumption differed between intentional and unintentional exposures: 17.0% vs. 54.5%, reflecting contexts where alcohol may facilitate chemical submission [15].

Intentional use predominated (77.3%), mainly in males (92.7%), whereas unintentional exposure was more frequent in females (48.5% vs. 7.3%, p < 0.001), echoing prior studies on gendered vulnerabilities [16].

NPS

This study was initiated after repeated reports of SCRAs intoxications in Reunion Island [17]. Synthetic cathinones were detected early, SCRAs later, consistent with customs reports noting few SCRA seizures at study start [14, 18]. The NPS market is highly unstable and opportunistic [18]. Most confirmed cases had prior psychoactive substance use, often polydrug [19].

Synthetic cathinone

Synthetic cathinones are central nervous system stimulants structurally related to cathinone, the active compound in khat. These substances can induce agitation, paranoia, tachycardia, and violent behaviour, and are among the leading NPS implicated in hospital emergencies across Europe [20].

In our study, biological analyses identified four NPS families, with synthetic cathinones being the most frequently detected. Dimethylpentylone was the predominant compound, found in six cases. This identification also revealed “B13” as the local street name for dimethylpentylone on Reunion Island [18].

Dimethylpentylone typically induces a sympathomimetic toxidrome — including agitation, tachycardia, and hyperthermia — which may be exacerbated in polydrug contexts [21]. In our cases, clinical severity ranged from mild (asthenia, anxiety) to critical. Case 10 notably developed arterial hypotension and cardiopulmonary arrest, illustrating the potential severity and coherence with known toxicological profiles [22].

Hexedrone caused major psychomotor agitation in one case, aligning with known stimulant toxicity [23].

SCRAs

SCRAs are among the most commonly encountered NPS. They mimic THC but bind more strongly to CB1 and CB2 receptors, resulting in more potent and unpredictable effects. Their use has been associated with agitation, hallucinations, cardiovascular instability, psychosis, and in severe cases, death [24]. Their appeal as a cheaper and more accessible alternative to cannabis, combined with their frequent adulteration in herbal products, contributes to their proliferation across Europe [5].

In our study, five cases involved SCRAs: four with MDMB-4en-PINACA and one with HHC. MDMB-4en-PINACA has been increasingly implicated in severe intoxications across Europe since 2020, and its detection in cannabis-type products — often without users’ knowledge — raises major concerns about inadvertent exposure. The European Risk Assessment Report has linked MDMB-4en-PINACA to numerous cases of acute toxicity, including fatalities and polydrug overdoses [25, 26]. Our cases reflect this severity, with clinical manifestations including bradypnea, psychomotor agitation, aggression, acute kidney injury, rhabdomyolysis, and aspiration pneumonia. Notably, carbamazepine was co-detected in several cases; while its antiepileptic properties could hypothetically modulate seizure risk, its presence as a possible adulterant remains speculative.

The case involving HHC, a semi-synthetic cannabinoid derived from hydrogenation of THC, presented with hypothermia, convulsions, agitation, and vomiting — findings compatible with the clinical spectrum described in emerging toxicological reports [27].

Due to their toxicity and potential for abuse, both MDMB-4en-PINACA and HHC were classified as narcotic substances by the French Medicines Agency in December 2021 and June 2023, respectively.

NSOs

NSOs, particularly fentanyl analogues and benzimidazole derivatives, rank among the most lethal NPS worldwide [28]. These substances exhibit potency several times higher than morphine, with a narrow therapeutic index and high risk of fatal respiratory depression, coma, and death [29]. Naloxone administration is often required, sometimes in repeated or high doses. In Europe, the increasing presence of NSOs in the illicit drug market contributes to the ongoing opioid overdose crisis, prompting heightened public health surveillance [30].

Protonitazene, a potent benzimidazole opioid, is of particular concern. Estimated to be 3 to 10 times more potent than fentanyl [31], it acts primarily on µ, κ, and δ opioid receptors [32]. Clinical intoxications typically mirror fentanyl overdoses, characterised by unconsciousness, respiratory arrest, and extreme sedation, often requiring high-dose naloxone to reverse symptoms. Numerous cases of overdose and even death show just how dangerous this product is [29, 32].

In our study, two cases involved protonitazene (detailed in a ICU case series by Negrault et al. [33]), both demonstrating profound opioid toxicity with features such as bradycardia, cardiogenic shock, and multi-organ dysfunction.

Due to multiple national intoxication clusters, the French Medicines Agency classified this family of substances as narcotics in July 2024, reflecting the urgent need to contain their spread.

N-Methyl-2-Aminodindane (NM-2AI)

NM-2AI is a synthetic psychoactive compound from the aminodane family, structurally related to amphetamines [34]. In our series, one patient tested positive for NM-2AI and exhibited signs of severe sympathomimetic toxicity, including tachycardia (138 bpm), major agitation, convulsions, respiratory distress, and haemoptysis, with complications such as aspiration pneumonia and global left ventricular hypokinesia. These findings illustrate the potential for severe cardiovascular and respiratory involvement, even after single exposures, and underscore the need for further pharmacotoxicological characterisation.

Study limitations

Rapid tests in emergency departments lack specificity, detecting only substance classes [35]. NPS identification requires chromatography with mass spectrometry: targeted methods detect known compounds but depend on reference standards [36], while non-targeted HRMS allows broader screening but needs sophisticated equipment. Toxicokinetics complicate detection—potent substances in microdoses may yield low plasma concentrations and be rapidly metabolised [37], causing false negatives. For example, in our MDMB-4en-Pinaca cluster (Cases 3 and 4), both patients consumed the same product, yet only the severe case tested positive for both SCRAs and protonitazene [38].

The small number of confirmed cases (n = 14) prevented robust multivariate analyses; larger studies are needed to identify predictors of NPS intoxication.

NPS particularities and public health perspective in Reunion Island

Although no comparable biologically confirmed study exists in mainland France, Reunion Island data complement national surveillance and reveal distinct local dynamics. NPS use appears opportunistic, shaped by local supply. Integrating overseas territories into national and European early warning systems (e.g., French Addictovigilance Network, EMCDDA) would enhance responsiveness. A local sentinel network linking hospitals, labs, and customs could improve detection, information sharing, and prevention.

A notable local pattern, also reported in Mayotte [39], is the strong preference for inhalation of psychoactive substances (SCRAs, NSOs). Inhalation increases risk, particularly via unintentional exposure or chemical submission, supporting targeted harm reduction campaigns [40, 41].

Despite challenges—underreporting, high costs, delayed confirmatory testing, and evolving substances—this study enabled effective analytical monitoring and fostered multidisciplinary collaboration across clinical, toxicological, and public health domains.

Implications for emergency practice

Recognition of NPS intoxications in emergency settings remains challenging due to variable presentations and the absence of rapid screening tools. Clinicians should suspect NPS exposure in cases of unexplained agitation, sedation, or mixed toxidromes inconsistent with standard drug screens. Early recognition relies on clinical assessment, awareness of local trends, and collaboration with toxicologists.

Management is mainly supportive: airway protection, haemodynamic monitoring, and benzodiazepines for agitation or seizures. Sympathomimetic presentations require cardiac and temperature monitoring, with cooling if needed. Naloxone should be administered in suspected opioid-like cases, noting that higher or repeated doses may be required for potent synthetic opioids [30].

Given frequent polydrug use, clinicians should anticipate interactions, particularly with alcohol or stimulants. Early biological sampling (within six hours) optimises toxicological confirmation. Strengthened coordination between emergency services, toxicologists, and public health authorities is essential to improve detection, reporting, and prevention. These observations naturally lead to broader implications for surveillance and prevention in Reunion Island.

Conclusion

This study provides the first toxicologically confirmed overview of NPS-related intoxications on Reunion Island. Beyond the persistence of conventional substances such as cannabis, MDMA, and cocaine, the emergence of synthetic cathinones, cannabinoids, and opioids signals a changing drug landscape. These compounds can cause severe, sometimes life-threatening effects, while remaining difficult to detect due to rapid metabolism and evolving formulations.

Enhanced surveillance and analytical capacities are essential to document emerging compounds and guide clinical and public health responses. Based on our findings, three key actions are recommended:

Clinical practice: promote early recognition of atypical toxidromes, prompt biological sampling, and collaboration with toxicologists.

Toxicovigilance networks: strengthen coordination and data sharing between hospitals, laboratories, and customs authorities.

Public health: develop targeted harm reduction campaigns addressing inhaled and adulterated products, particularly among young and vulnerable populations.
