On the interpretation of pleural clearance in imaging pathology correlation studies
Authors: Ayşegül Güler, Arif Hakan Önder, Nilay Çavuşoğlu Yalçın
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

We read with great interest the recent article by Zhou and colleagues,1 in which they evaluated imaging predictors of pleural response in stage IV non–small cell lung cancer. The authors should be commended for addressing a clinically important and underexplored question, namely whether radiologic and metabolic response can inform pleural disease status and guide local consolidative therapy. However, we believe that the central conclusion of the study suggesting that the imaging response of the primary tumor and nodal disease predicts pleural “sterilization” is limited by a fundamental issue in end-point definition.

In this study, undetectable pleural disease (UPD) is defined not only by negative pleural pathology or cytology but also by the absence of gross intraoperative disease, even in cases in which no pleural sampling was performed. Notably, a substantial proportion of patients classified as UPD did not undergo pleural biopsy or cytologic evaluation and were instead categorized on the basis solely of an intraoperative visual assessment. Given the well-recognized microscopic and patchy nature of pleural dissemination, such an approach introduces a significant risk of misclassification.

As a result, the study does not consistently measure true pleural disease status but rather a composite end point that combines histologically verified findings with visually inferred absence of disease. This distinction is critical. Without systematic and comprehensive pleural sampling, it is not possible to establish whether imaging correlates with actual pathologic clearance or merely with an incompletely verified surrogate.

Therefore, the observed association between imaging response and UPD should be interpreted with caution. The findings may reflect concordance with macroscopic disease regression rather than true pleural sterilization. This limitation does not diminish the clinical relevance of the study, but it does constrain the strength of its central inference.

Future studies aiming to validate imaging-based predictors of pleural response should incorporate standardized and systematic pleural assessment to ensure that the end point being predicted is accurately and uniformly defined.
