Penetrating Cranio-Cerebral Gunshot Injury with Retained Intracranial Bullet Adjacent to the Superior Sagittal Sinus
Authors: Liban Hussein Ahmed, Tadese Kebede Nadew, Esmail Husein Mohamud, Ifrah Hassan Nur, Abdirafi Said Elmi, Abdullahi Ali Muse, Mohamed Osman Alim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPenetrating gunshot injuries to the head are among the most severe forms of traumatic brain injury and are associated with high morbidity and mortality. This case report describes a 40-year-old female who presented to the emergency department 2 hours after sustaining a penetrating cranio-cerebral gunshot injury. The patient experienced loss of consciousness at the time of injury. On admission, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 10/15 and no focal neurological deficits. Vital signs were stable.Non-contrast head CT of the brain revealed a retained bullet in the parietal lobe without evidence of intracranial hemorrhage, mass effect, or midline shift, although image interpretation was limited by metallic artifact. The patient underwent an emergency craniotomy for removal of the retained intracranial bullet. Extreme caution was exercised to preserve the superior sagittal sinus and avoid vascular injury during the procedure. The surgery was completed without complications, and the patient was subsequently monitored in the intensive care unit). Follow-up CT imaging confirmed complete bullet removal and preservation of the superior sagittal sinus. Clinically, the patient showed neurological improvement, with her GCS increasing to 15/15.ConclusionThis case highlights the importance of early clinical stabilization, rapid neuroimaging, and timely neurosurgical intervention in improving outcomes following penetrating cranial gunshot injuries. Favorable neurological recovery can be achieved through surgical planning and timely intervention, even when a retained intracranial bullet is located in close proximity to critical neurovascular structures.Keywords: penetrating traumatic brain injury, retained intracranial bullet, superior sagittal sinus injury, craniotomy

Introduction

Civilian gunshot wounds to the head refer to brain injuries caused by projectiles such as bullets and fragments produced by explosions or explosive devices. Gunshot wounds to the head represent the most lethal type of firearm injury.1–3 Gunshot injuries accounted for approximately 251,000 deaths worldwide, representing 5.4% of all injury-related deaths.4 Gunshot injuries occur worldwide in both industrialized and developing countries, with varying incidence and causes. In Africa, particularly in sub-Saharan regions, trauma is a significant contributor to injury-related morbidity and mortality, partly due to the widespread circulation of firearms outside formal military or law enforcement control.5 Brain injuries from gunshot wounds to the brain (GSWB) in civilians are uncommon during peacetime in most societies, with an incidence of less than 1 per 100,000 person-years. However, such injuries still occur, most often due to suicide attempts, homicidal attacks, or accidental shootings. Worldwide, deaths related to firearm violence are also rising.6 The presence of a foreign body violating the skull and brain is the hallmark of penetrating traumatic brain injury (pTBI), a catastrophic primary injury mechanism that occurs in both military and civilian contexts.7–9 Primary brain injury depends on the ballistic characteristics of the projectile as well as secondary projectiles, such as bone or metallic fragments, that may penetrate brain tissue.5 Globally, traumatic brain injuries (TBIs) are increasing, particularly in countries with unstable political conditions and widespread access to firearms. TBIs are classified as either penetrating or closed injuries. Compared with closed TBIs, penetrating TBIs are less common, typically more severe, and associated with a poorer prognosis.10 The energy of a bullet determines the extent of brain injury. Low-velocity gunshot wounds are generally less destructive than high-velocity injuries. Low-velocity bullets typically cause localized penetrating damage rather than extensive perforation of brain tissue.3,11 Furthermore, high-velocity injuries were significantly more frequently linked to severe traumatic brain injury (TBI). Bilateral penetration of the injury path was also observed more often in high-velocity cases, reflecting the typical pattern of firearm-related trauma and its association with poorer clinical outcomes.12 In general, wounds that penetrate the skull are frequently associated with intracranial hemorrhage, depressed bone fractures, and cerebral edema. These injuries can lead to intracranial hypertension and malignant cerebral edema. Similar to the previous century, the management of these conditions through pharmacological and surgical means remains a topic of debate.13 After admission for penetrating head trauma, the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is considered the best predictor of survival.9 Aggressive resuscitation should be the initial management for all patients with gunshot wounds to the head. All patients with a GCS score greater than 5 should be considered for intensive surgical management.1 Early aggressive treatment may be beneficial for patients with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score greater than 8, normal pupillary responses, and single-lobe brain injury.1 Patients with penetrating brain injuries should undergo head CT scanning as soon as possible after initial hemodynamic stabilization, in accordance with current neurosurgical guidelines.9 CT imaging helps determine the entry and exit points of the bullet, the size of intracranial fragments, their proximity to the ventricles and major blood vessels, and the degree of cisternal compression. CT findings also help determine whether surgery is necessary and assist in refining the surgical treatment plan.9 Neuroimaging is essential, particularly for determining the type of operation, as well as the size and location of the surgical approach and the route for foreign body extraction. In addition to standard axial images with bone and brain parenchymal windows, brain CT scanning is commonly recommended for the assessment of patients with penetrating head trauma. Coronal reconstructions may be useful for evaluating lesions at the skull base and cranial convexity.9 Early and late complications may occur. Intracranial hematomas, infections, seizures, and cerebrospinal fluid leaks are among the early complications. Late complications include brain abscess, hydrocephalus, seizures, psychiatric disorders, and bullet migration.11

Case Presentation

A 40-year-old female presented to the emergency department two hours after sustaining a penetrating gunshot injury to the head. She reported loss of consciousness immediately after the incident. Moderate bleeding from the scalp entry wound was noted at the scene of injury. She had no history of chronic medical illness, previous surgical procedures, regular medication use, or known drug allergies. On admission with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 10/15. Neurological examination revealed no focal neurological deficits, and Her vital signs were stable, with a blood pressure of 110/75 mmHg, pulse rate of 91 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute, temperature of 36.4°C, and oxygen saturation of 97% on room air. Local examination demonstrated bleeding from the scalp at the bullet entry site without evidence of active hemorrhage or hemodynamic instability. The remainder of the systemic examination, including cardiovascular, respiratory, and abdominal assessments, was unremarkable (Figure 1). Following neurosurgical evaluation, the patient underwent emergency craniotomy and bullet removal under general anesthesia. An inverted U-shaped scalp incision was made across the midline. Two burr holes were created on either side of the superior sagittal sinus and connected to fashion a mini-craniotomy flap, allowing careful exposure of the sinus. The retained bullet was complete removal with preservation of the superior sagittal sinus with extreme caution to avoid vascular injury. Adequate hemostasis was achieved, the bone flap was replaced, and the scalp was closed in a single layer. The surgical procedure was completed without intraoperative complications, and the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit(ICU) for close postoperative monitoring. Discussion

Gunshot wounds to the head are associated with high rates of morbidity and mortality. Reported mortality rates in the literature range from 23% to 92%, with significantly higher rates of 87–100% observed among patients presenting with poor neurological status on admission.2,7 Gunshot injuries are a major global public health challenge, imposing significant burdens on healthcare systems and the economy. In sub-Saharan Africa, countries affected by internal conflicts, such as Sudan, experience a higher incidence of firearm-related injuries and deaths due to ongoing violence and instability.3,5,14 Pre-hospital mortality following penetrating head injuries may reach up to 90%, and only approximately half of patients who arrive alive at the hospital survive.3 Admission Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score is considered the most important prognostic indicator of survival, in addition to factors such as pupillary size and reactivity, as well as the trajectory of the bullet.3,12,14 During the initial assessment, once the patient arrives at the emergency department, management should follow Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS®) guidelines.15 A structured ABCDE approach is used to rapidly evaluate the patient and promptly detect any immediately life-threatening conditions.15 Additional prognostic factors include CT neuroimaging findings, the number of cerebral lobes involved, lesion location, and patient demographic characteristics. Moreover, the severity of injury is strongly influenced by the bullet trajectory, projectile fragmentation, and the amount of kinetic energy transferred to the brain tissue.,15,16 Craniocerebral missile injuries were first classified by Harvey Cushing based on his experience during World War I and were later refined by Matson during World War II; this classification system remains one of the most commonly used today,9 Intracranial pressure exceeding 20 mmHg and a presenting Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 3–4 are associated with increased mortality. In contrast, patients with a GCS score greater than 8, preserved pupillary reactions, and injury confined to a single cerebral lobe, as well as those with GCS scores between 8–13 and intact ventricles, may achieve favorable outcomes with early and aggressive surgical management.3 There is broad consensus among neurosurgeons that patients with traumatic brain injuries should undergo urgent head CT scanning upon arrival to the hospital.1 Although plain skull radiographs may assist in identifying bullet trajectory, retained foreign bodies, and intracranial air, they are not routinely recommended when CT imaging is available, as CT provides more comprehensive diagnostic information.1 A head CT scan is essential for delineating the bullet trajectory, identifying entry and exit points, and assessing the extent of intracranial fragmentation. It also provides important information regarding the proximity of the injury to major blood vessels and ventricular system, as well as any compression of critical structures such as the basal cisterns. Furthermore, CT imaging plays a key role in determining the necessity for surgical intervention and in guiding the operative management strategy, Current literature provides limited evidence regarding traumatic brain injuries caused by low-velocity bullets, which are generally considered less severe and are often associated with relatively favorable clinical outcomes.10 The objective of this case report is to present a Penetrating Cranio-Cerebral Gunshot Injury with Retained Intracranial Bullet Adjacent to the Superior Sagittal Sinus that was managed surgically, resulting in a favorable neurological recovery. Early and aggressive management in patients with severe penetrating gunshot-related traumatic brain injury appears to be a reasonable treatment strategy, favorable clinical outcomes may be achievable when optimal surgical intervention is provided.3 Penetrating cranial gunshot injuries are associated with high mortality and significant neurological morbidity. Previous reports have demonstrated that outcomes are influenced by initial neurological status, projectile trajectory, vascular involvement, and the presence of intracranial hematomas. In our patient, despite the presence of a retained intracranial bullet adjacent to the superior sagittal sinus, the absence of major hemorrhage, single-lobe injury pattern, stable physiological condition, and timely surgical intervention. Additionally, careful removal of the retained bullet while preserving the superior sagittal sinus may have contributed to the favorable neurological outcome. Limitations include as a single case report, limited generalizability and the inability to establish causal relationships. Further studies involving larger patient populations are required to better understand prognostic factors and guide management strategies for penetrating cranial gunshot injuries.

Conclusion

Penetrating cranial gunshot injuries are life-threatening and are often associated with significant morbidity and mortality. However, this case demonstrates that favorable neurological recovery can be achieved in selected patients, even when a retained intracranial bullet is located adjacent to critical neurovascular structures such as the superior sagittal sinus. In previous literature demonstrate favorable outcome was associated with several factors, including preserved neurological status, absence of major intracranial hemorrhage, a single-lobe injury pattern, timely neurosurgical intervention. This case highlights the importance of early assessment, appropriate neuroimaging, individualized surgical planning, and careful management in optimizing outcomes following penetrating cranial gunshot injuries.
