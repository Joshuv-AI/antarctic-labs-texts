Distal Vessel Occlusion Stroke: Lessons from Recent Randomized Trials and Implications for Future Study Design
Authors: Presaad Pillai, Anand R Warrier, Moyinul Haq, Shakir H Hakim
Published: 2026

Introduction

We read with great interest the recent publications of the Endovascular therapy plus best medical treatment versus best medical treatment alone for medium vessel occlusion stroke (DISTAL) and Endovascular treatment to improve outcomes for medium vessel occlusions (ESCAPE-MeVO) randomized controlled trials[12] as well as the Evaluation of mechanical thrombectomy in acute ischemic stroke related to a distal arterial occlusion (DISCOUNT) trial[3] presented at the International Stroke Conference 2025. These studies add to a growing body of thrombectomy trials that have reshaped the endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) landscape, most recently through large core infarct trials that expanded therapeutic boundaries. Despite the promise of extending EVT to smaller vessels, the collective message from these distal vessel occlusion (DVO) trials has been sobering. Although up to 40% of ischemic strokes are attributed to DVOs,[4] investigators concluded that EVT has not demonstrated a clear benefit in this subgroup.[123] Notably, DISCOUNT was halted at interim analysis after best medical treatment (BMT) demonstrated superiority over EVT.[3]

While several studies continue to evaluate mechanical thrombectomy for distal occlusions, two fundamental questions remain unanswered: (1) Do the available randomized data sufficiently exclude a role for EVT in DVO management? (2) Is further investigation of EVT in DVOs justified, and if so, under what conditions?

Diagnostic accuracy of DVO and classification of the M2 segment

Before assessing the therapeutic effect of EVT in DVO, accurate detection of the occlusion and consistent nomenclature are essential. Interpretation of acute neurovascular imaging remains challenging, particularly for clinicians with limited experience in evaluating computed tomography angiography (CTA) and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA). M2 segment occlusions are more than five times more likely to be missed compared with distal internal carotid artery or M1 occlusions (OR 5.69; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.44–22.57; P = 0.01).[5]

In the HERMES collaboration, which analyzed five randomized controlled trials of large vessel occlusions (LVOs), two trials included M2 occlusions. A substantial proportion of lesions initially classified as M1 by treating interventionists were subsequently reclassified as M2.[6] Such misclassification highlights the limitations of single modality vascular imaging. Distal occlusions may instead be detected indirectly through perfusion deficits, with Tmax delay demonstrating a sensitivity exceeding 95% for DVO detection, prompting closer scrutiny of CTA findings.[7] Accordingly, perfusion imaging in addition to CTA or MRA plays a critical role in diagnosis and treatment planning.[7]

The definition of the M2 segment is heterogeneous, encompassing anatomical, caliber-based, and functional classifications.[8] Among these, functional classification based on the M1 bifurcation offers greater reproducibility and clinical relevance in time-sensitive decision-making.[8] Standardizing nomenclature and integrating perfusion imaging are necessary prerequisites for accurately determining the true treatment effect of EVT in DVO.

Natural history of distal arterial occlusions and the role of collaterals

Studying the natural history of untreated stroke has become increasingly challenging in the era of intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) and EVT, both of which have demonstrated substantial improvements in outcomes.[9] Nevertheless, available observational data suggest that distal occlusions are associated with more favorable outcomes than proximal occlusions. Among untreated patients, good outcomes at six months (modified Rankin scale [mRS] 0–2) were observed in 38.5% of internal carotid artery occlusions, 38.5% of M1 occlusions, and 54.2% of M2 occlusions. This gradient parallels patterns of leptomeningeal collateralization, with poor collaterals present in only 12.5% of M2 occlusions compared with 23.1% of internal carotid artery and 25% of M1 occlusions. Collateral strength was independently associated with good outcome (OR 2.37; 95% CI 1.08–5.20).[9]

Additional indirect evidence arises from studies of dissecting aneurysms treated with parent vessel occlusion, which may serve as a physiological analog of acute arterial occlusion. In proximal circulations, including the internal carotid and vertebral arteries, early ischemic events occurred in nearly one-third of patients despite technically successful occlusion.[10] In contrast, DVOs involving M3, P1, and P2 segments were consistently well tolerated, with no immediate or long-term neurological deficits reported.[1112] This contrast highlights a key physiological principle: distal territories are often supported by robust leptomeningeal collateral networks capable of maintaining perfusion after loss of antegrade flow. Collectively, these observations suggest that collateral status, rather than occlusion location alone, plays a dominant role in determining outcome and should be central to therapeutic decision-making in DVO.

Therefore, systematic collateral assessment using multiphase CTA or perfusion-based imaging (computed tomography perfusion [CTP] or magnetic resonance perfusion [MRP]) is necessary to provide important information on the temporal and spatial behavior of collateral flow in DVOs, supporting treatment decisions and outcome prognostication beyond clinical severity alone.[7]

DISTAL, ESCAPE-MeVO, and discount: Major lessons from neutral trials

Baseline characteristics – limitations

The DISTAL, ESCAPE-MeVO, and DISCOUNT trials provide important insights into DVO management, but several design features may have influenced their neutral findings [Table 1]. All three trials enrolled older patients with mild-to-moderate neurological deficits. The median age ranged from 74 to 77 years, exceeding the approximately 68 years reported in LVO trials.[1] Advanced age is a recognized predictor of poorer outcomes after thrombectomy.[1]

Median National Institutes of Health stroke scale (NIHSS) scores ranged from 6 to 8, with many patients enrolled on the basis of isolated aphasia or other non-motor deficits.[123] In contrast, registry data frequently report median NIHSS scores between 12 and 14 for DVO.[131415] Lower baseline NIHSS limits the capacity to demonstrate meaningful improvement, while language deficits are not well captured by mRS, which reflects motor disability. A population-based study of M2 occlusions identified an NIHSS threshold of 9 as predictive of poor outcome.[16] Thus, the neutral outcomes observed may reflect patient selection rather than absence of treatment effect.

Heterogeneity of inclusion and the M2 problem

Another significant limitation lies in the heterogeneity of inclusion criteria, particularly regarding M2 occlusions. The three trials varied considerably in how these lesions were defined and categorized. DISCOUNT excluded proximal M2 occlusions entirely, whereas DISTAL included only non-dominant or co-dominant M2 occlusions and excluded dominant M2 lesions, which are more hemodynamically significant and more likely to benefit from mechanical thrombectomy.[1234] Prior observational and meta-analytic data consistently demonstrate that proximal or dominant M2 occlusions derive the greatest benefit from MT, with outcomes favoring intervention over BMT, particularly for proximal M2 occlusions (adjusted OR 2.39, 95% CI 1.08–5.28) and dominant M2 occlusions (adjusted OR 4.08, 95% CI 1.08–15.48).[1] Consequently, the patient populations in these trials predominantly represented non-dominant or distal M2 occlusions, limiting the generalizability of their findings to the broader M2 spectrum.[4] This dilutes the apparent treatment effect, as smaller or non-dominant M2 occlusions are less likely to show measurable functional improvement following EVT. Importantly, this heterogeneity was not confined to the M2 segment. ESCAPE-MeVO excluded the first segments of the anterior and posterior cerebral arteries, while both DISCOUNT and ESCAPE-MeVO excluded M4 occlusions.[123] In contrast, DISTAL adopted a more inclusive approach, enrolling all occlusions not meeting the criteria for LVO.[3] Such inconsistencies in anatomical inclusion across an already small patient cohort make it exceedingly difficult to draw unified or definitive conclusions regarding the efficacy of thrombectomy in DVO.

Impact of high thrombolysis rates

Another important confounder across all three studies was the high rate of IVT use in both intervention and control arms. IVT alone can achieve recanalization in approximately 41% of patients and functional independence in up to 67%—considerably higher than rates typically observed in LVOs.[17] Moreover, a meta-analysis including 2,063 patients across 26 studies reported partial or complete recanalization in 52% of M2–M3 MCA occlusions treated with intravenous alteplase alone.[4] These findings highlight the efficacy of IVT in distal occlusions compared with more proximal sites. MeVO registry data[4] demonstrate IVT use of approximately 44%, while nearly 60% of patients in DISTAL, ESCAPE-MeVO, and DISCOUNT received thrombolysis. This high overlap in effective reperfusion therapy reduced the incremental margin for detecting additional benefit from EVT. Consequently, it is plausible that this masked any potential advantage of thrombectomy, shifting trial outcomes toward apparent equivalence between EVT plus BMT and BMT alone.

Are we technologically ready and skilled enough to go distal?

As thrombectomy extends into smaller and more tortuous vessels, questions remain regarding device suitability and operator experience. Although procedural feasibility has improved, current devices may be suboptimal for distal navigation and clot retrieval. Reperfusion rates in ESCAPE-MeVO and DISTAL ranged from 75% to 77%, lower than those reported in LVO trials.[14]

Procedural heterogeneity further complicates interpretation. ESCAPE-MeVO employed stent retrievers alone, DISTAL favored combined techniques, and DISCOUNT permitted either aspiration or stent retriever.[123] Operator experience also influences outcomes, with a suggested proficiency threshold of approximately 100 procedures.[18] Distal thrombectomy likely carries a steeper learning curve due to vessel fragility and tortuosity.[19] These observations highlight the need for device refinement, training, and technique standardization before broader adoption.

Interpreting neutral trial results: Design limitations versus true biological effect

While limitations in trial design, patient selection, and imaging heterogeneity may have influenced the neutral outcomes observed in DISTAL, ESCAPE-MeVO, and DISCOUNT, these considerations should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive explanations. It is equally plausible that the true benefit of endovascular therapy in many DVOs is limited, particularly in patients with a robust collateral circulation, smaller at-risk territories, or a low propensity for infarct expansion. In such cases, successful angiographic reperfusion may not translate into measurable functional improvement. Distal occlusions frequently involve vascular territories with greater collateral redundancy and lower hemodynamic dependence on antegrade flow. Consequently, a substantial proportion of patients may achieve favorable outcomes with BMT alone, and recanalization, even when technically successful, may offer little incremental clinical gain. Acknowledging this possibility is essential to avoid over-attribution of neutral trial results to methodological shortcomings alone and to maintain appropriate clinical equipoise as further data emerge.

Looking ahead – ongoing DVO trials

While several randomized and registry-based studies continue to evaluate EVT for distal occlusions, future trial designs must evolve to more accurately assess its true efficacy.

First, raising the NIHSS inclusion threshold (e.g., ≥10) is a pragmatic enrichment strategy that may increase the likelihood of demonstrating treatment effect. Patients with more severe deficits are more likely to have clinically meaningful impairment and greater potential for measurable functional improvement, allowing neurological recovery to translate into observable gains on mRS. Such an approach may therefore improve statistical power and enhance the probability of a positive trial signal.

However, higher NIHSS thresholds risk systematically excluding patients with disabling but lower-scoring syndromes, including language, visual, or cognitive deficits, which are often underrepresented in global disability scales. This raises ethical and clinical considerations, as eloquent cortical infarcts may produce functional impact despite modest NIHSS scores.

Accordingly, an alternative or complementary strategy may be to retain broader NIHSS eligibility while refining outcome assessment. Future trials could incorporate domain-specific neurological scales, and patient-centered quality-of-life measures to better capture the heterogeneous impact of distal infarcts. Such an approach may allow clinically meaningful benefit to be detected without restricting enrollment to motor phenotypes, thereby preserving inclusivity while improving sensitivity to treatment effects.

Second, the high efficacy of IVT in DVOs complicates the isolation of an incremental treatment effect from EVT. Given the substantial rates of spontaneous or IVT-mediated recanalization in DVOs, concurrent thrombolysis may narrow the measurable treatment gap between EVT and BMT, obscuring the benefit in selected subgroups. While outright exclusion of IVT-eligible patients would raise significant ethical and generalizability concerns, future trials may instead consider alternative design strategies to address this confounding effect. These include stratified randomization by IVT use, evaluation of IVT-refractory cohorts, or delayed or rescue EVT paradigms in patients demonstrating early neurological deterioration despite thrombolysis. Such approaches preserve access to proven therapy while allowing a more rigorous assessment of EVT-specific benefit.

Finally, the standardization of DVO definitions, particularly for the M2 segment, remains imperative. A consistent, functionally based classification should be universally adopted in future studies. Uniform inclusion criteria, whether encompassing all M2 occlusions or excluding proximal segments entirely, will be essential to reliably determine the therapeutic value of thrombectomy in distal occlusions. Until these refinements are implemented, the role of EVT in DVO should be interpreted with cautious optimism rather than finality. The neutral results of DISTAL, ESCAPE-MeVO, and DISCOUNT likely reflect a confluence of evolving definitions, patient selection challenges, and methodological heterogeneity rather than a definitive absence of benefit. As the field progresses, a unified framework for classification and trial design will be critical to defining the true boundaries and potential of thrombectomy in DVOs.

Distal vessel occlusion in the recent American Heart Association/American Stroke Association guideline

In light of the neutral trial results discussed above, the recently published Guideline for the Early Management of Patients with Acute Ischemic Stroke by the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association formally recognizes the medium and distal vessel occlusion (MeVO/DVO) as a distinct clinical entity, while emphasizing that the evidence base remains evolving. For the first time, the guideline acknowledges that endovascular therapy may be reasonable in carefully selected patients with MeVO/DVO, particularly when there is a disabling neurological deficit and favorable imaging characteristics.[20] However, these recommendations carry a lower strength of evidence compared with LVOs, reflecting the absence of definitive randomized trial data at the time of guideline formulation.

Importantly, the guideline cautions against relying solely on NIHSS when assessing patients with distal occlusions, noting that even small infarct territories can produce clinically disabling symptoms depending on eloquence and collateral dependence. Advanced imaging is therefore central, with multiphase CTA and perfusion-based imaging (CTP or MRP) endorsed to assess collateral status, tissue viability, and hemodynamic risk, particularly in patients with apparently mild clinical deficits who may nevertheless be at risk of infarct progression.[20]

A more dynamic methodology – Our viewpoint

Building on these guideline recommendations and trials, we propose a more dynamic, physiology-informed framework that goes beyond trial-defined criteria and NIHSS thresholds to identify patients most likely to benefit from EVT in DVOs. While technology and operator expertise have advanced, the next evolution in distal thrombectomy lies not in how we treat, but in whom we choose to treat. Collateral circulation is often stronger in acute DVOs than in proximal occlusions; however, this apparent advantage complicates the interpretation of clinical severity and treatment thresholds. In DVOs, cerebral perfusion relies predominantly on leptomeningeal collaterals in contrast to LVOs, where additional collateral pathways via the circle of Willis and external carotid–ophthalmic channels may be recruited. This relative dependence on a single collateral system renders DVOs physiologically vulnerable.

When collateral supply is absent or compromised, infarction may occur rapidly, limiting the benefit of revascularization. Conversely, patients with tenuous or intermediate collateral support may harbor unstable penumbral tissue and are at increased risk of early neurological deterioration, representing a subgroup in which timely endovascular intervention may be most impactful. Trial designs that rely solely on NIHSS or occlusion site risk oversimplifying this complex cerebrovascular physiology.

A more dynamic, physiology-based framework is therefore needed, perhaps one that integrates clinical severity, angiographic collateral grading, and cortical territory involvement defined on digital subtraction angiography (DSA). Collateral status reflects hemodynamic reserve, while DSA-based lobar topographical mapping, correlated with clinical findings, helps define the eloquence of the affected territory and the functional stakes of reperfusion. When considered together, these parameters allow a more precise identification of patients most likely to benefit from EVT. Adopting such an approach would move the field beyond broad, empiric inclusion criteria toward individualized, physiology-informed decision-making.

While angiographic collateral grading and DSA-based eloquence mapping are conceptually attractive, their limitations must be acknowledged. Collateral assessment is still operator dependent, variably defined, and may not be uniformly reproducible across centers, and therefore, not scalable in large trials. To address these challenges, future studies should consider standardized operational strategies, such as tiered collateral grading systems on multiphase CTA, predefined perfusion thresholds on CTP or MRP to identify hemodynamically unstable tissue, and the integration of automated imaging tools for DSA-based lobar topographical mapping to reduce interobserver variability.

Importantly, this framework is relevant in resource-limited settings, where access to EVT may be constrained and procedural risk must be carefully weighed against potential benefit. Prioritizing patients with the highest likelihood of meaningful recovery ensures judicious use of limited infrastructure and expertise.

Ultimately, dynamic trial designs based on these principles are essential to delineate where the true benefit of EVT in DVOs begins and to define the subset in which the risk–benefit ratio justifies intervention.

Conclusions

Rather than signaling the end of endovascular therapy in distal occlusions, recent trials highlight the difficulty of translating complex cerebrovascular physiology into clinical practice. The path forward lies in refining definitions, patient selection, and outcome measures, rather than in simple trial repetition. Such refinement is essential to determine whether the current detour in DVO thrombectomy reflects a transient recalibration or a genuine inflection point in the evolution of stroke intervention.

As impactful as endovascular therapy has been for acute LVOs, its extension into progressively smaller and more fragile vascular territories demands greater restraint and precision. In this context, primum non nocere must remain integral to stroke revascularization strategies, reminding us that intervention without clear physiological and clinical justification risks harm without benefit. The challenge ahead is not to broaden access indiscriminately, but to define the limits of benefit, where careful, physiology-informed patient selection, technical discretion, and procedural endpoints are most likely to yield meaningful clinical impact. This approach is especially critical in resource-limited settings, where careful prioritization ensures that scarce EVT capabilities are directed to patients most likely to achieve meaningful recovery.
