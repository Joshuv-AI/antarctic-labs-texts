Analysis of blasting characteristics in interbedded rock masses based on particle expansion method
Authors: Ruizong Xu, Liangfu Xie, Yongshou Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Although rock mass structure exerts a critical influence on blasting outcomes, current research efforts remain predominantly focused on blasting within homogeneous rock masses of uniform properties. Much less attention has been paid to the inherent influence of complex geological formations, such as interbedded rock masses. This study employs the Particle Expansion Method to investigate how interbedding affects blasting through multiple aspects: reflected stress evolution, crack propagation patterns, and contact force dynamics. The results indicate that both the blasting environment and layer thickness exert considerable influence on blasting effectiveness, whereas the interlayer angle has minimal impact. This finding is consistent with experimental blasting results. Specifically, in hard rock environments, reflected stress initially increases and then attenuates with greater layer thickness, and the average contact force converges under larger thicknesses. In soft rock environments, reflected stress shows little variation with thickness, but crack propagation changes significantly even at small layer thicknesses, with contact forces stabilizing early under thin-layer conditions.

Introduction

In underground mining blasting operations, complex rock formations are frequently encountered. These include interbedded, fractured, and faulted rock masses. Such rock masses exhibit significant heterogeneity and anisotropy in their internal structure. These structural characteristics directly alter the propagation path of stress waves during blasting. They also affect the distribution of blast energy and the patterns of crack propagation. Consequently, rock fragmentation effectiveness is significantly influenced. If these specific attributes of the rock layers are not adequately considered, various problems can arise. These may include inefficient blasting, non-uniform fragment size distribution, excessive damage to the surrounding rock, or deviations in excavation profile control. Ultimately, such issues can prevent the achievement of project objectives.

Current research on rock blasting mechanisms largely depends on elastic assumptions and focuses on homogeneous rock masses. While these studies offer useful insights into dynamic responses under blast loading, they often fail to accurately capture the effects of complex geological structures such as interbedded, fractured, or faulted rock masses1. Crack dynamic propagation remains a key research topic. Li et al.2 used experiments and simulations to show that blast stress waves disturb the stress field near crack tips, changing both propagation paths and velocities. Liu et al.3 further emphasized that the relative orientation between the wavefront and crack surface plays a critical role in crack deflection. Another study by Liu et al.4 differentiated the functions of different stress components. It indicated that shear stress controls crushed zone formation near the borehole, while tensile stress governs far-field crack propagation4. The peak blast load drives crack initiation and growth—higher peaks facilitate crack formation5. In contrast, rock heterogeneity influences crack morphology. Less homogeneous materials tend to develop more tortuous and branched crack paths5. Although these studies have advanced blasting theory, field experiments remain limited. Their high cost and safety risks, stemming from explosive sensitivity and instantaneous detonation, restrict widespread use6,7. As a result, numerical simulations have become an essential tool. They provide safety, repeatability, and full-field mechanical data. Among these, the Particle Expansion Method (PEM)8, based on the Discrete Element Method (DEM), has proven effective in modeling dynamic fracture in discontinuous media. Unlike laboratory tests or continuum methods like the Finite Element Method, PEM captures mesoscale processes such as grain contact, separation, and dynamic crack initiation and coalescence. This capability offers deeper understanding of internal rock behavior and failure mechanisms during blasting, attracting broad research attention8.

During the development and application of the Particle Expansion Method (PEM), researchers first validated its accuracy through systematic comparison with physical experiments. These comparisons confirmed PEM’s capability to reliably simulate blast stress wave propagation, crack growth, and fracture zone morphology8,9. This work established a solid foundation for further studies. Subsequently, Yuan et al.10,11 demonstrated that the decoupling coefficient of the charge influences the ratio between stress wave and gas pressure peaks. This ratio directly affects crack initiation and propagation. A coefficient of 1.10 was found to produce the highest stress wave pressure, promoting more initial cracks and supporting the formation of an extensive fracture network. In the context of uranium mining, studies show that the equivalent permeability coefficient initially increases and then decreases as the decoupling coefficient rises. Optimal permeability occurs near 1.10, where crack density and aperture are maximized12. This provides a theoretical basis for enhancing permeability in low-permeability sandstone uranium deposits. To further improve blasting outcomes, Zhang et al.13 investigated short-delay blasting mechanisms. They found that stress wave superposition from sequential detonation increases peak particle velocity. This offers theoretical guidance for selecting delay times. Yuan et al.14 expanded this research by examining how delay time influences crack development under different borehole spacings. They observed that while delay time has little effect on peak velocity or vibration frequency, it significantly enhances the connectivity of the resulting fracture network. Other researchers have also explored the use of guide holes for crack control. Within specific spacing ranges, guide holes alter the local stress field and amplify the maximum principal stress. This helps guide cracks along desired paths15,16, offering a useful strategy for controlling blast-induced damage. In summary, PEM has become a key tool for advancing the study of meso-scale rock blasting mechanisms8.

Interbedded soft-hard rock masses represent a common geological formation in hydroelectric, transportation, and mining projects. Previous research has mainly focused on their static and dynamic properties17–20, slope stability21,22, and tunnel deformation control23,24. In contrast, studies on their dynamic response under blasting loads remain limited. The alternating rock layers cause complex stress wave behavior at bedding interfaces. This includes repeated reflection, transmission, and energy dissipation. Such interactions often lead to uneven blast energy distribution. As a result, crack propagation becomes hindered or deflected. These factors make blasting outcomes difficult to predict accurately.

To address this gap, our study applies the Particle Expansion Method (PEM)8 based on the Discrete Element Method. We systematically examine how interbedded structures affect rock breakage. The analysis evaluates crack propagation length and density. It also monitors internal peak stress distribution. Additionally, we track the evolution of contact force chains. This work aims to provide theoretical support for engineering practice.

Methods and theories

Theoretical analysis of the particle expansion method

The Particle Expansion Method8 is a discrete element-based numerical approach for simulating rock blasting. Compared to experimental and finite element methods, it offers more intuitive analysis of crack development and meso-scale interactions between particles, demonstrating significant advantages at both macro and micro levels. This study employs the Particle Expansion Method to conduct blasting simulations.

In this method, the blast-induced stress wave is modeled as the wave generated by the interaction between explosive particles and surrounding rock particles. As shown in Fig. 1c, the blasting process is predominantly controlled by the initial explosive shock wave (dynamic Crushing Stage), given that the secondary rock-breaking effect of detonation gas is negligible25. This secondary effect is further reduced in rock masses. Thus, by considering only the shock wave’s impact, a simplification is introduced that does not compromise the validity of the conclusions. Boundary condition theory

In blasting simulations, large model sizes and excessive particles can impose significant computational burden and reduce efficiency. To address this issue, researchers have proposed the dispersion effect26 and viscous boundary conditions27, which help prevent stress reflection at boundaries and eliminate the influence of artificial boundary-reflected stresses on blasting results, thereby achieving a simulation closer to an infinite medium. Stress wave incidence law

When an explosive stress wave encounters free surfaces, interfaces between different media, joints, or faults during propagation, reflection and refraction phenomena occur. This study focuses on blasting in interbedded rock masses, specifically examining the transmission of stress waves from one rock medium to another. In the case of normal incidence, the incident wave is a longitudinal wave, and both the reflected and refracted waves are also longitudinal. This paper primarily discusses scenarios involving normal incidence.

Under normal incidence conditions, the angles of incidence, reflection, and refraction are all zero. As a result, only reflected and refracted longitudinal waves are generated. Model development and method validation

Research workflow

As illustrated in Fig. 3, this study focuses on the blasting behavior of interbedded rock masses. First, the theoretical foundations are systematically elucidated, including the particle expansion blast simulation method, the flexible boundary theory, and stress wave incidence theory, providing essential support for the subsequent investigation. Subsequently, the discrete element contact model employed in this work and the crack generation mechanism following contact fracture are introduced. The parallel bond model is selected as the contact model in the discrete element simulation, which is widely used for simulating interactions between rock particles and is particularly suitable for the numerical modeling of rock-like materials. On this basis, the corresponding micromechanical parameters and explosive property parameters applied in the discrete element model are calibrated according to the macroscopic physical and mechanical parameters of sandstone. After determining the rock mass and explosive parameters, a numerical model for interbedded rock blasting is established. Finally, with the blasting environment, layer thickness, and bedding dip angle as influencing factors, the effects of interbedded structures on blasting outcomes are systematically investigated from multiple perspectives, including crack propagation extent and number, distribution of contact force chains, and orientation of contact forces. A sensitivity analysis of the various parameters is also conducted.Fig. 3Schematic representation of the numerical simulation process.

Model development

The interbedded model constructed in this study consists of commonly encountered sandstone and mudstone layers32–34, representing a typical soft-hard layered rock structure. To simulate an in-situ deep rock mass environment, a servo-control mechanism proposed by Cundall et al.35 was applied to impose a confining stress of 5 MPa32–34. The model has a length L = 12 m and a width H = 12 m. It comprises 74,803 uniformly distributed particles with radii ranging from 6 to 10 mm. Contact force chain analysis

Following the explosion, the contact forces between rock particles are closely related to the blasting outcomes. Higher contact forces indicate stronger particle interactions and facilitate crack initiation, making the analysis of contact force effects highly significant. As shown in Fig. 11, under an in-situ stress of 5 MPa32,33,35, the contact forces before the explosion are influenced solely by the in-situ stress (in the force chain visualization, darker blue represents higher contact forces). After the blast, the combined action of in-situ stress and blast-induced stress causes intense compression among particles near the borehole, significantly increasing contact forces and leading to the formation of a crushing zone under such high compression.Fig. 11Schematic of contact forces before and after blasting.

Schematic of contact forces before and after blasting.

Based on the stress wave incidence principles described in the Sect. “Stress wave incidence law” and the model shown in Fig. 12a. Upon detonation in the hard rock layer (Medium 1), the stress wave propagates to the interface with the soft rock (Medium 2). Given that the wave impedance of hard rock Z₁ exceeds that of soft rock Z₂, the reflection coefficient kpp is negative according to elastic wave theory, resulting in a reflected tensile stress σR1. The transmission coefficient lpp lies between 0 and 1, resulting in a reduced-amplitude transmitted stress σl1 in the soft rock. Both σR1 and σl1 depend strongly on the incident stress σi, which is governed by the thickness Hc of the hard rock layer. When Hc is small (Hc < 2.5 m, approximately three times the crushing zone radius), σi is relatively high, but interfacial structural damage due to stress concentration reduces σR1. Contact force chains in the hard rock are shallow and weak, while more blast energy transmits into the soft rock, producing a larger σl1 and slightly deeper contact force chains. At the critical thickness (Hc = 6.0 m, about seven times the crushing zone radius), interfacial damage decreases, significantly enhancing reflection and σR1, with deepened and intensified contact forces in the hard rock. Meanwhile, increased attenuation in the hard rock reduces σl1, resulting in shallower, weaker contact force chains in the soft rock. Beyond this critical thickness, geometric attenuation and damping cause a notable drop in σi, weakening both σR1 and σl1. More blast energy is consumed within the hard rock, inducing intense compression near the borehole and deeper, stronger contact force chains, approaching the stress state typical of blasting in pure hard rock.Fig. 12Diagram of contact force changes of different layer thicknesses.

Diagram of contact force changes of different layer thicknesses.

Based on the theory in the Sect. “Stress wave incidence law” and the model as illustrated in Fig. 12. Detonation within the soft rock (Medium 2) presents a scenario distinct from the hard rock case. Given that the wave impedance of the overlying hard rock (Medium 1) is greater than that of the soft rock (Z₁ > Z₂), the reflection coefficient kpp becomes positive, generating a reflected compressive stress σR2 at the interface. The transmission coefficient lpp ranges between 1 and 2, allowing a compressive transmitted wave to propagate into the hard rock. The defining characteristics of the soft rock are its low wave impedance and high damping, which cause the incident stress wave to attenuate severely before reaching the interface, significantly reducing σi. According to the relations σR = kpp·σi and σl = lpp·σi, this severely weakened σi renders the reflected stress σR2 less sensitive to layer thickness variations compared to the hard rock scenario, and it also limits the magnitude of the refracted stress σl2 transmitted into the hard rock. When the soft rock layer is thin (e.g., less than 0.8 m, or about one crushing zone radius), its consumption of explosive energy is limited, allowing more stress waves to be transmitted and resulting in a relatively larger blast-disturbed zone. In contrast, a thicker soft rock layer (exceeding one crushing zone radius) absorbs substantial energy, leading to rapid attenuation of the reflected stress and a further reduction in energy transmitted into the hard rock, which significantly confines the blast-disturbed zone.

In summary, during blasting in hard rock, where its wave impedance exceeds that of soft rock, the interface generates reflected tensile stress and a refracted stress of reduced amplitude. When the hard rock layer is thin (below three times the crushing zone radius), the interface is prone to damage, resulting in low reflected stress and a relatively larger refracted stress. As the thickness increases to a critical value (seven times the crushing zone radius), reflection strengthens while transmission weakens. Beyond this critical thickness, the incident stress attenuates, diminishing both reflected and refracted stresses, with more energy being consumed within the hard rock. In contrast, during blasting in soft rock, its high damping characteristic causes significant attenuation of the incident stress, leading to notably lower levels of both reflected and transmitted stresses. A thin soft rock layer results in a relatively larger blast-disturbed zone; however, as thickness increases, the soft rock absorbs substantial energy, rapidly reducing the extent of blast-induced disturbance.

Crack state analysis

As shown in Fig. 13a, the extent of crack propagation in hard rock initially increases and then decreases with increasing layer thickness. When the thickness is small (Hc < 2.5 m, i.e., less than three times the crushed zone radius), the crack range gradually expands with thickness. During this stage, severe damage occurs at the lithological interface (as seen in Figs. 12a and 13a), resulting in relatively low reflected stress. Combined with the limited thickness of the soft rock layer, the overall crack propagation remains restrained. As thickness increases (2.5 m ≤ Hc ≤ 8.0 m, where 8 m is about 9 times the crushed zone radius), less damage is inflicted on the interface due to energy dissipation within the thick hard rock layer. This enhances the contribution of reflected stress to blast performance (Fig. 12a), and together with the increased soft rock thickness, the crack propagation range continues to expand. When Hc exceeds 8.0 m up to 12.0 m, the hard rock layer absorbs most of the energy, leaving minimal stress to reach the interface. The reflective effect diminishes accordingly, and along with reduced soft rock thickness, the crack extent gradually decreases.Fig. 13Schematic of cracks with different layer thicknesses.

Schematic of cracks with different layer thicknesses.

In soft rock, thinner layers correspond to larger crack extents—a trend opposite to that in hard rock. As illustrated in Fig. 13b, when Hc is small (Hc ≤ 0.8 m, about one times the crushed zone radius), the crack range decreases sharply with increasing thickness. This is because thin soft rock layers absorb little energy, allowing the stress wave to reach the interface. However, due to the high stiffness of the adjacent hard rock, the remaining energy is insufficient to cause severe interface damage (as shown in Fig. 12b for Hc = 0.5 m, where the interface remains relatively intact compared to the hard rock case). Thus, under small Hc conditions, reflected stress still enhances cracking, leading to extensive fracture within the soft rock layer. Moreover, minimal energy absorption in thin layers enables stress waves to propagate across multiple layers, further extending the crack range. As Hc increases (0.8 m < Hc ≤ 2.0 m, where 2 m is about twice the crushed zone radius), the crack extent continues to shrink. For Hc > 2.0 m, the influence of reflected stress becomes negligible, and the crack propagation behavior converges to that observed in pure soft rock.

Crack quantity analysis

As shown in Fig. 14a, the layer thickness Hc significantly influences the number of cracks generated after detonation in hard rock. Tensile cracks dominate within the hard rock layer, while both tensile and shear cracks are present in the soft rock layer (refer to Fig. 13a, where deep blue represents tensile cracks and dark green indicates shear cracks). When Hc ≤ 2.5 m (3 times the crushed zone radius), the number of cracks increases rapidly at an approximate rate of 68% per meter with increasing thickness. This trend is attributed to two main factors: the growing contribution of reflected stress enhancing crack initiation, and the increased thickness of the soft rock layer, which itself develops more cracks. For thicknesses between 2.5 m < Hc ≤ 12 m, the number of cracks decreases gradually at a rate of about 6.5% per meter. When Hc reaches 12 m, the absence of reflective stress amplification results in the minimum number of cracks.Fig. 14Schematic diagram of the number of cracks.

Schematic diagram of the number of cracks.

As shown in Fig. 14b, the variation in crack quantity with increasing layer thickness Hc in soft rock occurs in three distinct stages. Initially, when Hc ≤ 0.8 m, the number of cracks decreases sharply to a minimum at a rate of approximately 112% per meter. This reduction occurs because although more cracks form within the soft rock layer containing the explosive, the blast-induced stress fails to propagate effectively into adjacent layers, resulting in fewer cracks there and an overall decline in total crack number. When 0.8 m < Hc ≤ 2.0 m, the number of cracks increases slowly at about 10% per meter. In this stage, cracks in adjacent layers gradually diminish until none are observed, but the increase in cracks within the blast layer outweighs this reduction. Once Hc > 2.0 m, as indicated in Figs. 12b and 14b , most energy is dissipated within the thick soft rock layer, making the blasting environment resemble pure soft rock. Under these conditions, the crack quantity remains largely constant.

In summary, layer thickness significantly influences crack quantity, with the crushed zone radius in pure hard and soft rock serving as the reference. In hard rock environments, when the layer thickness is less than three times the crushed zone radius, the number of cracks increases at a rate of 68% per meter with increasing thickness; in contrast, beyond this threshold, it decreases at a rate of 6.5% per meter. In soft rock environments, for layers thinner than one times the crushed zone radius, crack quantity declines sharply at 112% per meter, while for thicknesses between one and two times the reference radius, it increases slowly at 10% per meter. Once the layer thickness exceeds twice the crushed zone radius, the number of cracks stabilizes. A notable difference in crack quantity evolution is observed between hard and soft rock conditions.

Peak stress state

According to the measurement circles set in Fig. 6 for peak stress monitoring, Fig. 15a shows that when the explosive is detonated in a hard rock environment, the layer thickness Hc has a considerable influence on the peak stress. The peak stress essentially pivots around Hc = 6 m (7 times the crushed zone radius). When Hc ≤ 6 m, the peak stress tends to increase with greater thickness, with the vertical peak stress demonstrating a more pronounced upward trend. This behavior is primarily due to the enhanced effect of reflected stress and the increasingly stiff hard rock environment, which intensifies interparticle compression. When Hc > 6.0 m, the thicker rock layer dissipates most of the stress wave before it reaches the interface, resulting in reduced reflected stress. Consequently, both the horizontal and vertical peak stresses remain largely stable.Fig. 15Schematic diagram of peak stress variation.

Schematic diagram of peak stress variation.

As shown in Fig. 15b, the variation of peak stress in the soft rock environment can be divided into two main phases: a declining phase followed by a stable phase. When Hc ≤ 2.0 m (twice the crushed zone radius), only a small portion of the energy is dissipated within the soft rock layer after detonation, and the interlayer interface remains relatively intact (as indicated in Fig. 12b for Hc = 0.5 m). Owing to stress wave reflection and the restraining effect of the hard rock, the peak stress is higher under smaller layer thicknesses. As the thickness of the soft rock increases, more energy is absorbed, leading to a rapid reduction in peak stress. When Hc > 2.0 m, further increase in soft rock thickness results in a blasting environment approximating that of pure soft rock (evident in Fig. 12b for Hc = 3.3 m and Hc = 5.0 m), where the peak stress remains generally stable.

In summary, the peak stress evolves in opposing trends between hard and soft rock environments. In hard rock, with a demarcation point at seven times the crushed zone radius, the peak stress initially increases and then stabilizes as layer thickness grows. In soft rock, using twice the crushed zone radius as the threshold, the peak stress first decreases and then plateaus with increasing thickness. A substantial difference is observed in the peak stress near the borehole between these two rock environments.

Parameter sensitivity analysis

To deeply analyze the influence mechanisms of various factors on experimental results, this study adopted a multi-output sensitivity analysis method based on Random Forest. Thickness, dip angle, and rock type (blasting environment) were used as input features. Separate Random Forest regression models were built for three output variables: crack number, contact force, and peak stress. All 46 experimental cases were utilized. Each model contained 100 decision trees. The importance score of each feature was calculated within the models. These scores were normalized so that the sum of all feature importances equaled one. This normalization quantifies each feature’s influence degree on the different output variables. Subsequently, the importance scores of each feature across all outputs were aggregated and averaged. This produced an overall sensitivity ranking. The analysis results are presented through multi-dimensional visualization. A heatmap reveals the association strength between features and output variables. A bar chart of average importance displays the overall ranking. Together, these visualizations clarify both the individual and combined effects of each influencing factor on the experimental outcomes.

As shown in Fig. 22. The results demonstrate that rock type is the dominant factor in crack formation. Its importance score reaches 0.743. Layer thickness shows the strongest influence on contact force. This factor achieves an importance of 0.590. For peak stress, both thickness and rock type show considerable importance. Their scores are 0.419 and 0.375 respectively. In contrast, the dip angle has relatively minor effects. Overall, rock type ranks highest in average importance at 0.509. Thickness follows at 0.421. The dip angle contributes only 0.071. These findings indicate that rock type and layer thickness are the core factors affecting rock fragmentation. The interlayer angle has limited influence. This conclusion is consistent with the numerical simulation results.Fig. 22Sensitivity analysis diagram.

Discussion

Based on the above research results, this study provides certain insights and approaches for blasting in complex rock formations. However, there are some limitations. First, the discrete element method primarily uses the parallel bond model to simulate contacts between rock particles. This bond model has strong limitations. For example, after the parallel bond is broken, only sliding friction is considered for contacts between rock particles. New contacts between fractured rock particles are not accounted for. The influence of differences in bond moments is neglected. Future studies could develop new contact models to address this limitation. Second, this study used the homogeneous modeling method inherent in PFC2D. In reality, homogeneous rock masses are relatively rare. Subsequent research could consider using random field modeling to assign random physical and mechanical parameters. This would better reflect real conditions.

Additionally, to improve computational efficiency, this study used a two-dimensional model to analyze the impact of rock formation structures on blasting outcomes. Actual rock formations are three-dimensional. Future research should focus on three-dimensional models. As illustrated in Fig. 23, the particle expansion blasting simulation method is inherently extensible to three-dimensional conditions. Based on the superposition principle, this method represents a cylindrical charge as an assembly of multiple spherical explosive units. The cumulative dimensions of these spherical units are designed to match those of the original cylindrical charge, enabling their simultaneous expansion to accurately simulate the detonation effects of a cylindrical explosive in three-dimensional space. However, computational efficiency poses a significant challenge in 3D simulations. As shown in Fig. 24, constructing a 3D model with the same model and particle size results in 366,998 particles even at a thickness of 0.02 m. Computational demand increases exponentially. Future work will require improved algorithms and upgraded computer hardware to address these challenges.Fig. 23Schematic of particle expansion blast simulation method under three-dimensional Conditions.Fig. 24Three-dimensional blasting model.

Conclusion

Based on the discrete element method with a particle expansion method, this study investigates interbedded soft-hard rock masses, considering blasting conditions, interlayer thickness, and inclination angles as influence factors. The effects of these factors on blasting outcomes are examined through crack propagation patterns and quantity, reflected stress, and contact forces. The main conclusions are as follows.

Reflected Stress: During blasting in hard rock, where its wave impedance exceeds that of soft rock, the interface generates reflected tensile stress and a refracted stress of reduced amplitude. When the hard rock layer is thin (below three times the crushing zone radius), the interface is prone to damage, resulting in low reflected stress and a relatively larger refracted stress. As the thickness increases to a critical value (seven times the crushing zone radius), reflection strengthens while transmission weakens. Beyond this critical thickness, the incident stress attenuates, diminishing both reflected and refracted stresses, with more energy being consumed within the hard rock. In contrast, during blasting in soft rock, its high damping characteristic causes significant attenuation of the incident stress, leading to notably lower levels of both reflected and transmitted stresses. A thin soft rock layer results in a relatively larger blast-disturbed zone; however, as thickness increases, the soft rock absorbs substantial energy, rapidly reducing the extent of blast-induced disturbance.

Crack Propagation Behavior: Stratum thickness and the blasting environment (soft rock or hard rock) significantly influence crack propagation efficiency, whereas the bedding angle exerts a comparatively minor influence. In hard rock environments, as the stratum thickness increases, the number of cracks exhibits a trend of initial rapid increase followed by a gradual decrease. Conversely, in soft rock environments, an increase in stratum thickness leads to a trend characterized by an initial rapid decrease in crack number, followed by a slow increase, and ultimately stabilization.

Peak stress state: The peak stress evolves in contrasting trends within hard rock and soft rock environments. In hard rock environments, the peak stress demonstrates an initial increase followed by a plateau as the stratum thickness increases. Conversely, in soft rock environments, the peak stress shows a decrease with increasing stratum thickness before eventually stabilizing.

Contact force: In hard rock environments, the average contact force exhibits a trend of initial increase followed by convergence as the stratum thickness increases, with convergence occurring at greater thicknesses. In soft rock environments, the average contact force demonstrates a trend of decrease followed by convergence with increasing stratum thickness, achieving convergence even at relatively small thicknesses. In both hard and soft rock environments, as the bedding angle increases uniformly in a counterclockwise direction around the central origin, the direction of the contact force shifts correspondingly in a clockwise manner. The magnitude of this angular shift is essentially consistent with the change in the bedding angle.

Parameter sensitivity analysis reveals that both layer thickness and rock type (soft/hard environment) exert significant influence on blasting outcomes, whereas the interlayer angle plays a relatively minor role. This finding is consistent with experimental blasting results. This study addresses the gap in research on the impact of stratigraphic structure on blasting, providing new ideas and methods for subsequent blasting studies in complex strata. In addition, the use of simulation methods effectively mitigates the potential risks and uncertainties during the blasting experiment process, offering valuable insights for numerical experimental research on blasting.
