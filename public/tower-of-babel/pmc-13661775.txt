Introduction

The interconnected crises of climate change, biodiversity loss and pollution are collectively termed the Triple Planetary Crisis (Passarelli et al. 2021; United Nations Environment Programme 2021). These are accelerating global ecological collapse, with profound consequences for human health. The impacts of the Triple Planetary Crisis are not evenly distributed. Persistent inequities mean women and gender‐diverse populations are disproportionately affected and often excluded from decision‐making processes that shape their futures (Solar and Irwin 2010; World Health Organization 2024). Similar inequities are evident within the health sector, where gender continues to shape the professional landscape broadly, and nursing in particular, as a highly gendered profession (Langer et al. 2015).

This scoping review examines how gender justice, broadly defined as the pursuit of equity and equality through a gendered lens, (UN Women 2010) is conceptualised in the international healthcare literature concerning the Triple Planetary Crisis. The review focuses on the experiences of women and gender‐diverse populations, and the representation of nurses and other healthcare professionals dominated by women in the extant literature on this topic.

Background

Beyond the Tipping Point A Planet in Crisis

Across the planet, the stability of interconnected ecological systems and human and non‐human systems—collectively referred to as ‘more‐than‐human’—are being disrupted by unprecedented levels of environmental degradation. Among the most pressing of these threats are climate change, biodiversity loss and pollution (the Triple Planetary Crisis), which collectively undermine the foundations for all life on Earth (Passarelli et al. 2021; United Nations Environment Programme 2021).

The Triple Planetary Crisis

The scientific evidence reveals a rapidly changing Earth system that is moving beyond safe environmental limits that support human life (Caesar et al. 2024; Rockström et al. 2009; Steffen et al. 2015). Greenhouse gas emissions continue to rise beyond safe limits trapping heat (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change 2023), and biodiversity is declining rapidly, as species vanish and ecosystems lose their ability to function and flourish (Romanelli et al. 2015). These problems are further exacerbated by pollution from plastics (Dimassi et al. 2022; Yates et al. 2021) and synthetic chemicals (Lin et al. 2023) along with wildfires and dust storms that reduce air quality and place further strain on living systems. Each separate, systemic environmental threat interacts and amplifies the others (Caesar et al. 2024). Land clearing, for instance, drives biodiversity loss while simultaneously decreasing the atmospheric capacity to sequester carbon dioxide emissions and filter pollutants (Roy et al. 2022). These interconnected, accelerating environmental threats not only undermine planetary stability but are causing and risk further impacts on human health and wellbeing (Whitmee et al. 2015).

The consequences of the Triple Planetary Crisis have significant and protracted health and wellbeing impacts (Romanello et al. 2024). For example, worsening air pollution perpetuated by global emissions and exacerbated by rising temperatures impacts respiratory and cardiovascular health (Porter and Heald 2019). Meanwhile, extreme heat days continue to grow in severity and frequency, placing populations, and especially pregnant people, children, outdoor workers, older people and those with comorbidities at risk of heat‐related illness and death (Romanello et al. 2024).

Biodiversity loss and extreme weather events are disrupting water supplies and increasing the risk of waterborne diseases; changing vector habitats are impacting the range of diseases like malaria and dengue fever; and food security is being compromised by altered crop yields, resulting in malnutrition and foodborne diseases (Robinson et al. 2024). The health impacts of the Triple Planetary Crisis extend beyond individual physiology, however, and collide with entrenched inequities, meaning that health burdens are not experienced homogenously across populations.

Intersecting Inequities

The Triple Planetary Crisis also perpetuates and exacerbates existing social inequalities, disproportionately affecting particular groups. These disproportionate inequalities are not incidental but are shaped by structural, social and cultural determinants of health (Redvers et al. 2022; Solar and Irwin 2010). Social determinants, including gender norms, income, education, housing, political participation and environmental conditions, are the underlying drivers of health inequities between different population groups (Solar and Irwin 2010; World Health Organization 2024). These determinants intersect to produce cumulative disadvantage (Crenshaw 1991).

Distinct from social determinants of health, which refer to structural and socioeconomic conditions, are cultural determinants, which shape wellbeing through connection to culture, land, language, kinship systems and are equally important drivers of health (Redvers et al. 2022).

Gender Justice

A growing body of research demonstrates that women and gender‐diverse (people whose gender identity is not exclusively male or female or whose gender identity differs from the sex they were assigned at birth) populations (van Daalen et al. 2024), particularly those from low‐resource countries and communities, face disproportionate impacts from climate change, pollution and biodiversity loss (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change 2023). Persistent biased social norms are recognised as a major obstacle in achieving gender equality and the empowerment of women and girls (Sustainable Development Goal 5) as they ‘constrain women's choices and opportunities by regulating behaviour and setting the boundaries of what women are expected to do and be.’ (United Nations Development Programme 2023).

Gender justice is closely linked to both intersectionality and ecofeminism, providing complementary lenses for understanding and addressing systemic inequalities. An intersectional approach as defined by Crenshaw (1991) recognizes how overlapping systems of oppression such as gender, race, class, sexuality and disability compound disadvantage. Similarly, ecofeminist perspectives connect gender justice to environmental and planetary health, highlighting how patriarchal and extractivist systems underpin both gender inequities and ecological degradation (Djoudi et al. 2016; Sultana 2022).

There are multiple definitions and interpretations of gender justice across policy, law, and feminist scholarship. UN Women (2010) defines gender justice as ‘ending the inequalities between women and men that are produced and reproduced in the family, the community, the market and the state’. Gender justice extends to recognising that dominant gender‐based social norms and biases reproduce patterns of differentiation in respect to roles and responsibilities, status and authority, voice, agency, and access to resources and opportunities (Hawkes et al. 2025). In this scoping review, we thus define gender justice as ‘the fair and equitable treatment of all genders, achieved by dismantling structural inequalities and ensuring accountability for gender‐based harms’.

The Triple Planetary Crisis and Gender Justice

Emerging scholarship demonstrates that gendered norms shape differential exposure to climate change, pollution, and biodiversity loss, by structuring power relations that often inequitably determine who performs unpaid care labour, who controls and accesses resources, and whose knowledge and priorities are reflected in decision‐making (Global Health 50–50 2024). The World Health Organization (2024) highlights that reducing exposure to health inequities and enhancing resilience in the face of the planetary crisis requires targeted action. Addressing gender justice is therefore a critical dimension of addressing the collective environmental challenges that society presently faces. While the Triple Planetary Crisis produces deeply gendered impacts that exacerbate inequities, advancing gender justice may represent a structural pathway to address these imbalances by addressing inequity.

A Critical Gap

Despite growing recognition of the impacts of environmental change on women and gender‐diverse populations, significant gaps remain in the evidence base connecting the domains of the Triple Planetary Crisis with gender justice. While much has been written on the topic of environmental justice and gender, relatively little research appears to have been conducted and reported. Many articles are non‐empirical commentaries, editorials or discussion papers (Afifi et al. 2024; Barkha 2022; Djoudi et al. 2016; Enarson 2013).

Based on a preliminary search of the literature, existing scoping and systematic reviews focus primarily on climate change‐related health impacts, overlooking the systemic, interconnected effects of the Triple Planetary Crisis and broader social environmental determinants of health. For example, a recent literature review conducted by Keenan et al. (2024) examined the health impacts of climate change on women; however, its focus was limited to climate change and pregnancy‐related outcomes, leaving broader systemic gender justice issues underexplored. Similarly, a scoping review by Stone et al. (2022) found that there were various mental health effects experienced by women—again within the narrowed focus of climate change. While the systematic review by Khine and Langkulsen (2023) expanded the examination of broader determinants of health and marginalised populations (including women), again, this paper was limited to climate change‐related effects.

Finally, to the authors' knowledge, it is unclear what focus, if any, has been placed on the gendered experiences of healthcare professionals, including nurses, associated with the cascading effects of the Triple Planetary Crisis. Not only are nursing and many health professions dominated by women (often around 90%) (World Health Organization 2025), nurses' frontline roles provide insight into how environmental changes differentially impact populations. Their commitment to person‐centred care equips them to advocate for inclusive and equitable responses. By integrating principles of gender justice into practice and advocacy, nurses might influence research, policy and health system transformation. This positions the profession as a vital force in advancing gender‐equitable solutions to the Triple Planetary Crisis (Hopkins‐Walsh et al. 2023).

The Review

Aims

This scoping review aimed to identify and report how gender justice is conceptualised and discussed in contemporary health literature in relation to the Triple Planetary Crisis of climate change, pollution and biodiversity loss, with a particular focus on the experiences of women and gender‐diverse populations and the representation of nurses and other healthcare professionals dominated by women.

It sought to answer the primary question: ‘What is known about gender justice in the context of the Triple Planetary Crisis in relation to the representation of women, gender‐diverse populations, nurses and those working in health professions, often dominated by women?’

Supplementary research questions include: Which structural and systemic factors impacting gender equity have been identified?Which pathways to empowerment, agency, and redistribution of power for women and gender‐diverse populations have been identified?What outcomes have been reported from efforts to advance gender equity in the context of the Triple Planetary Crisis?How are nurses and other health professionals, women, and gender‐diverse individuals represented in this body of literature?

Methods

Design

This review was conducted following the JBI methodology for scoping reviews (Peters et al. 2020). An apriori protocol for this review was prepared and registered with the Open Science Framework. This Scoping Review is reported in accordance with the PRISMA‐ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews) checklist (Tricco et al. 2018).

Search Methods

Following an initial search to identify any preexisting relevant reviews and to establish search terms and concepts, a comprehensive search was conducted across MEDLINE (Ovid), Scopus, CINAHL, Embase, and ProQuest on 23 September 2024, focusing on studies published 2000–23 September 2024. Search terms were initially discussed by the entire research team and developed by identifying key terms related to the overarching columns ‘Women and Gender diverse people’, and ‘The Triple Planetary Crisis’. Free text search and subject headings were included under each column. Search terms were then piloted in MEDLINE (Ovid) and refined with assistance from a senior research librarian (Scoping Review Question and Analytical FrameworkKP). Given how extensive the subject topic is, over 48 search terms were used to search databases. The MEDLINE (Ovid) database search strategy has been presented in Appendix A. This was adapted for the other databases as necessary.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Following the JBI recommendation for scoping reviews, the Participants–Concept–Context (PCC) framework was applied to develop inclusion/exclusion criteria and guide the selection of studies.

Participants

Participants in the studies included women and gender‐diverse people as well as health professionals including nurses. We adopted an inclusive understanding of ‘women and gender‐diverse people’ to encompass all individuals who self‐identify as women, as well as those whose gender identity falls outside the binary. Gender itself refers to a socially and culturally constructed set of roles, behaviours and identities, distinct from biological sex and inclusive of diverse experiences such as transgender and non‐binary gender identities. Studies involving mixed populations were included in the review where data relating specifically to women and gender‐diverse people could be disaggregated and meaningfully interpreted. Definitions of women and gender‐diverse people are provided in the protocol.

Concept

To be eligible, sources must include information in relation to at least one element of the Triple Planetary Crisis and gender justice. Further details of the conceptual framework and definitions for these concepts are explained in the review protocol.

Context

The review adopted a broad inclusion of sources and did not restrict the inclusion of sources based on country or field of research.

Sources of Evidence

Studies of qualitative, quantitative, and mixed methods methodologies were included in the review. In line with the objectives of this scoping review, the search strategy was restricted to peer‐reviewed empirical literature. Grey literature was not included, as the review sought to map how planetary health concepts have been empirically investigated within the health literature, rather than to capture policy, educational resources or non‐peer‐reviewed commentary. The year 2000 represents a critical juncture in global environmental and gender equity efforts. It marks the launch of the Millennium Development Goals, which positioned gender equality as a global priority, and coincided with a growing shift in environmental discourse that began to frame ecological degradation as a human rights and justice issue. As such, to maintain contemporary relevance, only papers published after 1 January 2000 were considered, with older publications excluded.

Source Screening and Selection

The Source Screening and Selection was performed in Covidence Software (Covidence 2024). This screening was conducted independently by two researchers (x and x), with any conflicts resolved by a third researcher (x). Articles retained from this then underwent full text retrieval and screening. Full text screening was performed by two researchers (x and x) with disagreements resolved by a third researcher (x).

The search retrieved 2768 articles. Data was then extracted from the remaining 39 articles. All exclusions were documented with a rationale into Covidence (Covidence 2024).

Critical Appraisal

The Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) (Hong et al. 2018) was applied for standardised appraisal across dynamic studies and to enhance rigour. However, in keeping with JBI scoping review methodology and the PRISMA‐ScR reporting guideline, no papers were excluded based on the MMAT score. Likewise, the authors did not modulate interpretation of the included studies' findings based on the results of quality appraisal.

Data Extraction

Data Abstraction

Prior to data extraction, two researchers (x and x) read and synthesised pertinent theory from the fields of gender justice, ecofeminism, and intersectionality to establish a data extraction template relevant to the research aims. This template was guided by theoretical concepts from the fields of intersectionality and ecofeminism to inform the categorisation of data in relation to gender justice (D1‐D6) (Table One). Once reviewed and refined by the entire research team, the framework formed the deductive codebook used to guide the extraction of relevant data and identification of key themes from the retained articles. Full texts were uploaded into NVivo Version 14 (QSR International 2023) and Covidence (Covidence 2024) software, which were used to extract the data using the pre‐established extraction template. Rationales for including findings from each text were recorded as top‐level ‘codes’, which consolidated related findings into broader categories.

In keeping with Peters et al. (2020), the extracted data were analysed descriptively. Frequency counts, sourced from NVivo, were used to summarise key concepts, populations and study locations.

Synthesis

The findings were initially mapped against the extraction framework (Table 1) and then discussed with the research team. Consensus on the extracted data was reached through discussion, comparison, and synthesis. The results are presented in a summary table outlining study characteristics and Figures (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6) to illustrate the distribution and analytic focus of the included literature. Figures are based on NVivo coding frequency counts. ‘Number of studies’ refers to the number of unique articles in which a theme was identified at least once. ‘Times referenced’ refers to the total number of coding references across all included studies. Themes are not mutually exclusive, and a single study may contribute multiple references to one or more themes.

Results

Study Characteristics

Study Design

Of the 39 included studies, 17 (43.6%) employed qualitative designs, 17 (43.6%) were quantitative, and 5 (12.8%) used mixed methods approaches. Within the qualitative studies, 10 (25.6%) used interviews, 4 (10.3%) adopted case study designs, and 3 (7.7%) employed ethnographic or field research methods. Quantitative studies were primarily cross‐sectional analyses (9, 23.1%) or econometric/modelling studies (7, 17.9%), with one (2.6%) using an ecological time‐series design. Mixed‐methods studies (5, 12.8%) combined qualitative techniques such as interviews or focus groups with quantitative surveys or statistical analyses to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the phenomena investigated.

Phenomena of Interest

The phenomena of interest varied across the 39 studies. The largest proportion of studies (where n = number of times each concept was mentioned in the included articles) (n = 12, 30.8%) examined gendered experiences of climate change impacts, including vulnerability, adaptation, and coping strategies. Eight studies (20.5%) investigated women's roles in climate‐related decision‐making, governance and policy. Health and wellbeing outcomes were addressed in seven studies (17.9%), while six studies (15.4%) explored women's economic participation, labour, and resource access under environmental stress. Three studies (7.7%) examined cultural and spiritual connections to land and environment, and a further three (7.7%) focused on intersectionality and human rights in climate contexts. Although the studies were categorised by their primary phenomenon of interest, several reported incidental findings that overlapped with other aspects of the extraction framework were identified, which are presented in Appendices B–E. None of the included articles reported on gender diverse groups (including transgender individuals and individuals who identify as gender non‐binary).

Cultural Context (D1)

The included papers reflect the different ways in which women are positioned in their local, dominant societies. To highlight the vastly different socio‐cultural, political, and environmental landscapes that the suite of studies are situated within, the UNDP (2023) Human Development Index (HDI) has been adopted as a proxy indicator to provide a broad, global context. The HDI, which is a composite measure that reflects development in three key dimensions: health (life expectancy), education (mean and expected years of schooling), and standard of living (gross national income per capita).

Research Question 1

Which structural and systemic factors impacting gender equity have been identified?

Systemic Oppression and Power Dynamics (D2)

The literature consistently highlighted a convergence of structural and social forces that sustain and reinforce the oppression of women (Figure 3). Systemic oppression and power dynamics that impact women's resilience to endure environmental threats were reported in 76% of the retained articles. Common codes elucidated in the literature included financial exclusion and lack of access to material resources (n = 19), underrepresentation in governance and decision‐making (n = 14), prescriptive gender norms (n = 11), participation in and expectations of domestic and market labour (n = 12), education access and lack of access to information (n = 8), underlying health issues (n = 5), restricted mobility (n = 4), rural living and isolation (n = 4), motherhood (n = 4) and colonialism (n = 2) (Appendix B).

As an example, access to education was a challenge for many women (Hlahla et al. 2022), which in some instances perpetuated barriers to harnessing economic opportunities (Cameron et al. 2021), reinforcing systemic limitations on their personal freedoms and capacity to adapt and respond to climate change (Floro et al. 2019; Hlahla et al. 2022; Imran and Ozcatalbas 2020; Jannat and Kusakabe 2024; Pinho‐Gomes and Woodward 2024) (Appendix B).

Differing, Layered Impacts of Environmental Crises on Women (D3)

Extending from structural factors previously described, articles reported how gendered social and economic inequalities intersect with environmental threats to produce distinct and sometimes disproportionate impacts for women (Figure 4). The impacts of environmental threats on women were reported in 61% of articles (Appendix C) and included health impacts (n = 16), financial impacts (n = 13), safety and security (n = 10), impacts on women as mothers and carers (n = 8), and impacts on holistic systems of wellbeing (n = 4), which encompass relational values and connection to culture, traditions and land, which can be diminished by the loss of species, practices or access to significant places.

Links between key findings in the literature.

Health impacts identified in the literature included increasing mental health issues including anxiety and stress; illnesses from contaminated water (particularly during floods); malnutrition from food insecurity; fatigue; gynaecological disease and sexually transmitted diseases; heat stress; physical injury and sleeplessness (Chapola et al. 2024; Ghosh and Dutta 2024; Goli et al. 2023; Hlahla et al. 2022; Monk et al. 2019; Ojakorotu and Olajide 2018; Vésier and Urban 2023).

Impacts on spiritual wellbeing were also identified, particularly among Indigenous women (Liddell et al. 2024). Environmental degradation is disrupting cultural practices, sacred rituals and connection to Country (Chapola et al. 2024), undermining women's spiritual resilience and cultural continuity (Chapola et al. 2024). This too presents considerable health risks, deeply affecting Indigenous social and emotional wellbeing as an integral aspect of overall health. Additionally, access to the health system was further constrained during extreme weather events, perpetuating further challenges (Shuaib et al. 2019).

Research Question 2

Which pathways to empowerment, agency and redistribution of power for women and gender‐diverse populations have been identified?

Pathways Toward Gender Justice (D4)

Pathways toward greater gender justice were reported in 64% of the retained articles (64%) (Appendix D). Factors that were grouped as a pathway included gender‐equitable governance and decision making (n = 18); representation of women in adaptation and resilience activities and planning (n = 16); building inclusive communities (n = 13); improving access to education as well as individual capacity building more generally (n = 10); and increasing women's financial freedom, agency and autonomy (n = 9) were all pathways that may promote a redistribution of power and agency toward greater gender equity (n = 25) (Appendix D).

Many studies emphasised the importance of building inclusive communities as a means of advancing equality and gender justice. Key aspects included improving women's access to government services (Floro et al. 2019), particularly social protection schemes, which are vital during climate‐related disruptions (Ghasemi et al. 2021). The literature also pointed to the value of strengthening women's social networks (Obossou et al. 2023) through sustained investment in community groups (Ghasemi et al. 2021), communities of practice (Hlahla et al. 2022), and women‐led organisations (Chen and Hansen 2022). Actions to reduce violence against women (Munala et al. 2023) and shift power dynamics within families (Allen et al. 2022; Ghasemi et al. 2021), thereby enhancing women's decision‐making agency and social status, were identified as important. Reducing the caregiving burden placed on women was also linked to improved climate resilience (Allen et al. 2022).

Improving women's participation in governance and decision making was also highlighted as being critical. Strategies that promoted changes to governance and decision‐making included: distributive governance, which encompassed gender inclusive (Allen et al. 2022) multi‐level governance (Ojakorotu and Olajide 2018); women's leadership (Chapola et al. 2024; Chen and Hansen 2022; Goli et al. 2023; Hlahla et al. 2022) sharing power and decision making (Ghasemi et al. 2021; Pinho‐Gomes and Woodward 2024; Wood et al. 2021) and equality in policy‐making (Detraz and Peksen 2017; Hlahla et al. 2022; Jannat and Kusakabe 2024; Ojakorotu and Olajide 2018).

Research Question 3

What outcomes have been reported from efforts to advance gender equity in the context of the Triple Planetary Crisis?

Outcomes of Gender Justice (D5)

Outcomes of improved gender justice were reported in 38% of the articles (Appendix E). Key outcomes that were reported related to women's leadership (n = 8); the promotion of women's financial agency (n = 7), and improved education (n = 2). The literature identified that investing in women's participation in governance can contribute to the development of more climate‐considerate policies (Asongu et al. 2022; Dutta et al. 2024; Eunsoo Kim 2022; Goli et al. 2023). Progress toward gender equity through enhanced leadership opportunities for women was also seen to support the creation of more resilient and adaptive systems (Asongu et al. 2022) and was associated with reduced societal vulnerability to climate‐related impacts (Asongu et al. 2022; Das 2024).

Research Question 4

How are nurses and other health professionals, women and gender‐diverse individuals represented in this body of literature?

Health Professionals (D6)

No articles reported on the gendered experiences of healthcare professionals in the Triple Planetary Crisis. Three articles explored the health system dimensions of the Triple Planetary Crisis, highlighting that health needs are shifting (see above domain on layered impacts), requiring new responses from health workers (Ghosh and Dutta 2024; Patrick and Capetola 2011).

Discussion

This scoping review sought to identify and report how gender justice is conceptualised and discussed in contemporary health literature in relation to the Triple Planetary Crisis, with a particular focus on the experiences of women and gender‐diverse populations and the representation of nurses and other healthcare professionals dominated by women. This exposure is mediated by intersecting structural and social factors which compound existing forms of disadvantage and produce layered and mutually amplifying vulnerabilities. Across the reviewed studies, gender justice is consistently identified as a critical pathway for improving outcomes for women. Notably, while gender justice was consistently positioned as a pathway to improved outcomes, the reviewed studies operationalise the concept in diverse ways, emphasising different structural, social and relational dimensions, which contributes to heterogeneity in the conceptualisation of gendered impacts and pathways. In some cases, these pathways were associated with more equitable and effective outcomes. These findings are elaborated in the sections below and synthesised visually in Figure 7.

Structural Barriers—Women's Access to Education and Economic Rights

A key finding from this review was that structural barriers are shaping women's access to opportunities, impacting their adaptive capacity in the face of the Triple Planetary Crisis. Financial barriers, identified in almost half the retained articles, were a critical factor in shaping how women respond to the Triple Planetary Crisis. There were examples of women, particularly young women, not having equitable access to finances, including access to their own personal finances as well as their ability to take out loans or credit (Chapola et al. 2024; Floro et al. 2019; Obossou et al. 2023). Although the literature did not establish a causal link, several articles reported that limited financial autonomy was a factor that exacerbated women's marginalisation, thereby increasing their vulnerability to exposure. For example, a qualitative study conducted on women's perceptions of and responses to climate change‐induced food insecurity in Southern Mali identified that ‘young women [were] not granted the power to use their earnings to respond to their households' food insecurity’ (Wood et al. 2021).

Financial barriers not only limit women's capacity to respond to the Triple Planetary Crisis but are also aggravated by the crisis, reinforcing and widening pre‐existing social and economic disparities. For example, Detraz and Peksen (2017) found that during severe disasters, women's economic rights were much less likely to be protected or valued. They contextualised their findings with the example after Hurricane Katrina, when the Bush administration suspended minimum wage laws in affected areas, a move that disproportionately disadvantaged women concentrated in low‐paid work (Detraz and Peksen 2017).

In many instances, these structural barriers culminated in heightened susceptibility to adverse health outcomes from environmental shocks and stressors. The literature highlighted that health impacts spanned from physical illness and injury, heat stress and malnutrition, to mental health issues such as anxiety and stress (Chapola et al. 2024; Ghosh and Dutta 2024; Goli et al. 2023; Hlahla et al. 2022; Monk et al. 2019; Ojakorotu and Olajide 2018; Vésier and Urban 2023). This pattern is consistent with the broader literature, which similarly documents that climate‐related disasters exacerbate both physical and mental health burdens, while deepening pre‐existing health inequities for women (Keenan et al. 2024; Stone et al. 2022).

Gendered Norms—Care Giving

Beyond structural disadvantages, women experience the Triple Planetary Crisis through the added emotional and practical burden of caregiving, shaped by cultural and societal expectations that position them as primary carers responsible for the needs of their children and families. By and large, the literature highlighted how entrenched gendered roles and social norms are impacting how women live and work (Mari‐Dell'Olmo et al. 2022; Saville and Adams 2021; Shuaib et al. 2019). In several contexts, women were socialised to absorb primary caretaking responsibilities for children and the elderly (Allen et al. 2022; Ghosh and Dutta 2024; Puwajarernpong et al. 2023; Shuaib et al. 2019). This finding is unsurprising in the context of the broader literature, which frequently highlights that women's caregiving roles are often socially and contextually prescribed, with their own needs often conflated with those of children and the elderly (Langer et al. 2015).

This review also identified that women reported feeling a strong sense of responsibility for protecting children from environmentally‐linked harms such as food insecurity (Ghosh and Dutta 2024) and poor air quality (Allen et al. 2022). This can place an undue emotional burden on women who often absorb these responsibilities, causing them to experience heightened anxiety, guilt and emotional distress (Allen et al. 2022; Ghosh and Dutta 2024). For example, Allen et al. (2022) outlined how the public health messaging to ‘stay inside’ to minimise smoke inhalation during the Australian Black Summer bushfires resulted in distress for some mothers. These women reported experiencing intense guilt either for having to take their children out on errands or, conversely, for restricting them from playing outdoors (Allen et al. 2022).

Women's Work

Limited recognition of domestic work and restricted access to decision‐making opportunities are key concepts for interrogation, to advance gender justice (Global Health 50/50, 2024). Not only do gendered norms drive women's caring responsibilities, they also continue to impact women's working conditions as well as opportunities for leadership and influence on decision‐making. Women's work in both domestic and labour markets continues to be reported as being underrecognized (Goli et al. 2023), unpaid (Obossou et al. 2023), in some instances exploited (Cameron et al. 2021), and a key barrier to inclusion in community and political decision‐making (Andajani‐Sutjahjo et al. 2015; Das 2024).

Environmental shocks and stressors were found to exacerbate these challenges: impeding women's ability to work and generate income (Ojakorotu and Olajide 2018). In some instances, the shocks and stressors of extreme weather compelled women to take on additional labour, often under unsafe or exploitative conditions where protections were weakened or absent (Goli et al. 2023). The literature thus reflects that uniform, ill‐informed neo‐liberal responses to the Triple Planetary Crisis may not be equitably accessible or practical across diverse socioeconomic and cultural contexts (Obossou et al. 2023). Instead, recognising, reducing and compensating women's labour (Puwajarernpong et al. 2023; Shayegh and Dasgupta 2024) and addressing financial instability and/or poverty (Goli et al. 2023; Puwajarernpong et al. 2023; Wood et al. 2021) are critical factors for effective policymaking in the context of the Triple Planetary Crisis.

Displacement

In addition to financial impacts, women are increasingly facing displacement arising from the Triple Planetary Crisis. The number of people internally displaced by climate‐related disasters such as cyclones, storms, wildfires, droughts and floods is increasing each year. In 2024, 45.8 million people were internally displaced by climate disasters, with 29 countries reporting their highest number of internal displacements to date (IDMC 2025). Each of the studies examined in this review was from countries that reported internal displacements as a result of climate‐related disasters (IDMC 2025), emphasising the compounding and eroding impacts of ongoing climate change.

Case studies from across the world describe how expanded insecurities associated with displacement are creating multi‐layered sites of vulnerability for women, girls and gender‐diverse individuals (Ayeb‐Karlsson 2020; Oyilieze et al. 2022; Schuster et al. 2024; Singh‐Peterson et al. 2025). The IDMC (2025) report that across countries, internally displaced households with higher levels of vulnerability were most often headed by women or included people with disabilities. Other studies report how women are most likely to be displaced following extreme weather events, citing the 2022 displacement of 8.2 million people in Pakistan following extensive flooding events, of which 80% were women (UHRO 2022).

These impacts are multi‐faceted and nuanced, anchored in local socio‐cultural contexts and socio‐economic realities, yet at a global scale the movement of people escaping from untenable living situations is rapidly increasing. The other significant cause of internal displacement is conflict, which can follow in the aftermath of climate‐related disasters. In slow‐onset climate events, competition for water, fuel and food can intensify, exacerbating existing precarity (IDMC 2024). Accordingly, future‐focused resilience and adaptation planning needs to consider the very real prospect of ongoing and concurrent sites of displacement and potential for conflict should adaptation efforts fail.

Cultural Context

Because socio‐cultural contexts are diverse and locally nuanced, the findings of this review cannot reasonably be extrapolated and applied evenly across different settings. The social, economic and political conditions that shape women's lives, including their rights and freedoms, manifest differently across countries and cultures. This review drew on the Human Development Index (HDI) and the Gender Development Index (GDI) to provide a broad indicator of these variances in health, education and living standards between women and men across countries. For instance, two studies were analysed from Thailand, which has a GDI greater than one, indicating that women have higher living standards, higher levels of education and longer lives than men. In contrast, three studies were located in India, where women are disadvantaged in the HDI in comparison to men (GDI of 0.849). This indicator is intended to reflect biased social norms which produce gendered patterns of land ownership, where only 13% of rural women in India own land, although 85% of rural women are actively engaged in agricultural work (UN Women 2022).

Other studies were located in countries like Mozambique and Mali, which are greatly impacted by compounding extreme weather events and civil conflicts. In Mozambique, for example, up to 1.3 million people have been internally displaced since 2020. Up to 25% of the 580,000 internally displaced people in 2024 reported feeling fearful of gender‐based and sexual violence, whilst 25% of those displaced reported having insufficient water for basic needs (IDMC 2024). These sobering statistics portray the vastly different and complex contexts described in the included studies. As such, while this scoping review has identified familiar themes and topics across diverse, socio‐political, economic, environmental and cultural contexts, it does not attempt to extrapolate and globalise key trends in the literature.

Progressing Toward Gender Justice

Addressing the growing impacts of the Triple Planetary Crisis requires solutions that embrace gender justice. What is required is not a new or separate agenda to advancing gender equity, but a renewed commitment to implementing the existing one with urgency and within the context of the Triple Planetary Crisis. Many of these solutions require further amplification.

As a critical first step toward gender justice, women must be better represented in leadership and governance in responding to the Triple Planetary Crisis. For example, negotiations at the Climate Change Conference of the Parties must be decolonised and prioritise the lived experiences of women (van Daalen et al. 2024). Women's integration in natural disaster planning (Puwajarernpong et al. 2023), investing in women‐led organisations and social networks (Chen and Hansen 2022; Goli et al. 2023; Puwajarernpong et al. 2023), and respecting and integrating First Nation's women's knowledge (Chapola et al. 2024; Datta et al. 2024) demonstrate how gender inclusive adaptation and resilience might be enacted. The latter is particularly important. For Indigenous Peoples and culturally diverse communities—who were not a specified focus population in the present review—the degradation of ecosystems and biodiversity not only disrupts physical environments but can also erode cultural continuity, spiritual wellbeing and collective identity, amplifying the gendered and racialised impacts of environmental harm (van Daalen et al. 2024). For example, in rural Fiji, the loss of important totem species, due in part to a succession of cyclone events, has far‐reaching intergenerational impacts as cultural traditions that involve gendered rituals are lost, and related collective identities are impacted (Singh‐Peterson 2025).

A recent study on the climate justice experiences of nurses and community partners found that a colonial view of humanity's separation from nature leads to desensitisation to gendered violence against Indigenous Peoples and all life on Earth (LeClair et al. 2025). Participants noted that colonialism and cis‐heteropatriarchy are interconnected systems of domination, particularly in Indigenous communities that respect gender‐diverse Two‐Spirit individuals and struggle against Eurocentric gender and sexual binaries, cultural erasure, genocidal policies and land loss. In the context of the Triple Planetary Crisis, recognising and responding to both social and cultural determinants is essential to advancing equity, environmental justice, and gender justice in an interconnected and sustainable way.

Nursing Role

In response to the Triple Planetary Crisis, nurses are uniquely positioned to advance gender justice for two interrelated reasons. Firstly, nurses are the largest and most geographically dispersed health workforce globally. Nursing's long‐standing tradition of advocacy positions the profession not only as a witness to environmental injustice but as credible and experienced advocates for gender‐responsive systemic change. Secondly, nursing is a profession whose workforce predominantly identifies as women, historically shaped by structural gendered inequities including the undervaluation of care work, barriers to leadership, constrained professional autonomy, and limited influence in policy decision‐making (Salvage and White 2020). These dynamics mirror the gendered power imbalances exacerbated by the Triple Planetary Crisis presented in this review (Figure 7).

Operationalising this role requires nurses to move beyond a narrow biomedical framing of environmental impacts and engage with the gendered social determinants of vulnerability and health. In practice, nurses can integrate gender‐sensitive environmental risk assessments into routine care, accounting for factors such as unpaid care burdens, occupational exposure, food and water insecurity, geographical displacement, and heightened risks during pregnancy and disaster contexts. In education, nursing curricula at all levels should embed environmental and gender justice as core competencies (Levett‐Jones et al. 2023; Nicholas and Breakey 2017). At the policy level, nursing professional bodies and unions can leverage their collective authority to advocate for gender justice in policy, including recognition of care work, workforce protections and equitable representation in decision‐making forums. Guided by nursing's history and underpinned by the International Council of Nurses Code of Ethics (International Council of Nurses 2021) and recently updated definition of nursing and nursing work (White et al. 2025), these strategies position nurses as central, and effective actors in advancing gender justice amidst Triple Planetary Crisis.

Gaps in the Literature

Two notable gaps were identified in the literature, which constrained the extent to which all research questions could be fully addressed. First, none of the included studies reported on the experiences of gender diverse populations, including transgender and non‐binary individuals in the context of the Triple Planetary Crisis. This should not be taken as evidence that such impacts do not exist. On the contrary, emerging reports highlight that LGBTQIA+ communities often face heightened risks due to poverty, housing insecurity and discrimination in disaster response (Mann et al. 2024). Thus, the lack of academic discourse might reflect a gap in research funding and processes, rather than the absence of a problem.

This scoping review also identified a significant gap in the literature exploring the gendered experiences of health professionals in the context of the Triple Planetary Crisis. The experience of nurses, in particular, has been underexplored in contemporary literature. This is surprising, as nurses may indeed have considerable insight into how women are experiencing the Triple Planetary Crisis because they themselves are a part of a gendered profession (World Health Organization 2025). While efforts to achieve greater gender balance in the nursing profession are essential, it remains crucial to acknowledge that nursing is inherently shaped by gendered dynamics. Approximately 87% of the global nursing workforce identify as women (World Health Organization 2025). Further, the Triple Impact of Nursing report suggests that the way nurses are treated within health settings often reflects how women are treated in society (All‐Party Parliamentary Group on Global Health 2016). The report highlights how broader gender norms, particularly across cultures where health labour work is often undervalued, seen as low‐status work, or poorly paid (Langer et al. 2015), mirror how women's economic and social contributions are valued (All‐Party Parliamentary Group on Global Health 2016; Lingel et al. 2022).

Existing policy and societal responsiveness to environmental threats may further reveal how little women's lives and labour are valued (Crenshaw 1991; Gaard 2015). For example, although health workers are expected to lead the frontline response and are often victims of extreme weather events themselves, less than 5% of climate change adaptation funding is directed to health, with under 1% of multilateral climate finance specifically supporting health‐related projects (World Health Organization 2018).

While women make up the bulk of the health workforce, they are also consistently underrepresented in executive or management roles across the health system (Langer et al. 2015). Nurses in particular are underrepresented in climate adaptation and resilience policymaking, detrimentally impacting their adaptive capacity (Richards 2025). This sentiment parallels a key finding of this Scoping Review, which was that women were reportedly unrepresented or subdued in domestic (Jannat and Kusakabe 2024; Obossou et al. 2023; Wood et al. 2021), local community (Andajani‐Sutjahjo et al. 2015; Goli et al. 2023; Obossou et al. 2023), national (Hlahla et al. 2022) and international decision‐making (Hlahla et al. 2022). The exclusion of women and nurses from leadership and strategic planning not only compromises workforce protection and preparedness, it positions women healthcare workers at the frontline of risk without adequate voice, agency, or structural support (Langer et al. 2015). As women (including many health workers) face increasing impacts from environmental degradation, the lack of focused attention may suggest a gender bias that warrants further exploration.

Strengthening women's property rights (Segnestam 2017; Sommer et al. 2022; Wood et al. 2021) is an important goal for advancing gender equity. Investments in women's access to financial resources and strengthened property rights were associated with both greater empowerment (Ghasemi et al. 2021) and enhanced environmental conservation (Ghasemi et al. 2021) – illustrating how supporting women can simultaneously drive social and environmental gains (Sommer et al. 2022).

Recommendations

Advancing gender justice responses requires first acknowledging that women, gender‐diverse people and those working in female dominated healthcare roles remain marginalised within discourses on gender equity in the context of the Triple Planetary Crisis (Langer et al. 2015; van Daalen et al. 2024). There is a need for research that illuminates and examines these experiences more fully, in order to identify solutions that promote gender justice.

Secondly, pathways toward gender equity must continue to be promoted. This is not a separate or new agenda, but rather a refreshed commitment under the auspices of effective environmental policy. The findings of this scoping review should not be taken as a blanket prescription; they must be interpreted within the context in which they are set. Countries should therefore pursue independent research into culturally specific impacts and pathways to inform more nuanced gender justice responses.

Finally, healthcare workers, including nurses, can support gender‐responsive environmental action by staying abreast of the health challenges experienced by women and gender‐diverse people, seeking to improve women's access to care (Dutta et al. 2024), and advocating for inclusive environmental policies (Ghosh and Dutta 2024; Patrick and Capetola 2011). Strengthening gender‐sensitive communication, data collection and service delivery may contribute meaningfully to more equitable health systems. Additionally, strengthening public health systems by improving infrastructure (Austin et al. 2021), increasing staffing (Austin et al. 2021) and considering gender‐responsive planning (Hlahla et al. 2022; Puwajarernpong et al. 2023) are critical to protecting women and gender‐diverse people from the health impacts of the Triple Planetary Crisis.

Limitations

Climate change dominated the evidence base relative to pollution and biodiversity loss, which may constrain the breadth of cross‐domain interpretation.

Studies reporting incidental health findings based on sex or gender were excluded unless they explicitly engaged with gender justice, as defined by our guiding framework. This may have narrowed the scope of included evidence and limited capture of implicit or emergent gendered inequities; however, inclusion of such granular evidence from a vast and disparate body of literature would have been beyond the limited time and resources of our project. Further, due to the nonspecific and often amorphous or undetailed mention of such evidence, cataloguing and deriving cogent conclusions would have been extremely challenging and unlikely to yield clear results or potentially impossible. Additionally, much of the existing literature focuses on individuals assigned female at birth, reflecting primarily the experiences of cisgender women. Consequently, the intersectional experiences of transgender and non‐binary individuals are thus underrepresented. This constrains the applicability of findings across diverse gender identities.

While not constrained by geographic limitations, this review did not seek to specifically include or report on Indigenous Peoples and people from culturally and linguistically diverse groups, or those from migrant backgrounds. These diverse groups, as noted above, are often unequally impacted by the Triple Planetary Crisis, and individual capacities to cope, adapt and transition away from risks vary greatly from setting to setting.

Another limitation is that while temporality was originally included as a component of our gender justice framework, it was removed during the review process due to a lack of findings related to this concept. This restricts understanding of how gendered impacts and pathways may evolve over time. Furthermore, there were several studies that could not be retrieved during the search. While targeted retrieval strategies such as author contact or interlibrary loan may increase completeness, such approaches were not undertaken due to time constraints. Given the small number of unretrieved records relative to the total screened, the impact on the overall mapping of evidence was deemed limited by the research team. In addition, this review did not include grey literature and was limited to English‐language publications, consistent with its focus on mapping peer‐reviewed empirical evidence; however, the inclusion of grey literature may be valuable for future analyses seeking more localised, practice‐based, or policy‐specific insights.

Finally, the geographical origins of the included studies are predominantly from low‐income countries which may limit generalisability. This may bias interpretations toward contexts with distinct socio‐economic and governance conditions. Thus, the findings must be considered in the context of these constraints and further work should be conducted in contexts where evidence is currently limited.

Conclusion

This scoping review elucidated the intersecting dynamics that continue to reinforce systemic gender inequities, restricting women's full participation across social, economic and political spheres. These entrenched inequalities not only heighten vulnerability to environmental crises but also constrain their capacity to adapt. The perspectives of nurses and gender diverse people, whilst currently missing in the literature, are critical to understanding the gendered impacts of the Triple Planetary Crisis and moving toward gender justice. Until the voices of women, gender‐diverse people and nurses are meaningfully recognised and integrated, achieving gender justice within healthcare will remain an aspirational rather than attainable goal.
