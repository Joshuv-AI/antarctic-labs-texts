Comparison of the Effects of Incubator and Radiant Environments on Physiological Criteria and Pain-Stress Levels during Heel Lance in Neonates in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Sezin Gürsu, Birsen Karaca Saydam, Oğuzhan Kalkanlı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectives:To compare the effects of incubator and radiant heat on physiological criteria and pain-stress levels during heel prick procedures in newborns in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU).Methods:This randomized controlled quasi-experimental study was conducted between June and December 2023 with newborns diagnosed with IHB hospitalized at a NICU in Turkey. Prior to the heel prick procedure, 72 newborns (gestational age: 37–41 weeks) were randomly and equally assigned to either a radiant heater or an incubator setting. Physiological parameters (temperature, SpO2, heart rate [HR]) and pain-stress scores of the newborns were measured and compared in both settings.Results:No statistically significant differences were found in the descriptive characteristics of newborns between the incubator and radiant heater groups. When comparing physiological parameters and pain-stress scores, there were no significant differences in SpO2, and HR, or pain-stress levels between the groups; however, body temperature was significantly higher in the radiant heater group. During the heel prick procedure, both groups showed significant changes in body temperature, SpO2, HR, and pain-stress scores across the three time points (before, during, and after the procedure).Conclusion:Although body temperature increased significantly in the radiant heater environment compared with the incubator environment, this difference did not appear to play a vital role in affecting the physiological parameters of the newborn.

Introduction

The neonatal period, the first 28 days after birth, is among the most sensitive and dynamic life stages [1]. After birth, the temperatures of newborns tend to drop significantly due to body heat loss through various pathways, including conduction, convection, radiation, and evaporation. Prolonged cold stress in newborns can lead to oxygen deprivation, hypoglycemia, metabolic acidosis, and rapid glycogen store depletion [2,3]. Importantly, maintaining body temperature within normal limits has been shown to significantly reduce mortality and morbidity rates in newborns in intensive care. Thermoregulation requires an environment in which the baby uses minimal energy and oxygen. Therefore, incubators and radiant-heated bed systems are used to help maintain body temperature [4,5].

Neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) provide care for newborns requiring intensive medical support, including continuous life support when necessary. Within these units, incubators and radiant body heaters are commonly used to maintain body temperature [6]. Newborns in NICUs often undergo various invasive procedures that can cause pain and stress. Studies report that each newborn may undergo an average of 7–17 invasive procedures per day, and without evidence-based pain management, these interventions may lead to short- and long-term neurodevelopmental complications [7,8,9]. One of the most frequently performed and potentially painful procedures is the heel prick, commonly used for blood sampling in newborns with indirect hyperbilirubinemia (IHB), a leading cause of neonatal hospitalization [10,11].

While previous studies have examined body temperature and skin fluid loss in preterm infants cared for in incubators and radiant body heaters, there is a lack of research on how these devices affect body temperature, pain, and stress levels in term infants. Moreover, existing literature highlights the need for further investigation into the differences in body temperature and fluid loss between these 2 warming methods [12,13,14]. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of heel prick blood sampling in term infants placed in incubators versus radiant heaters, focusing on physiological parameters as well as pain and stress levels.

Methods

This randomized controlled experimental study using quantitative methods was conducted between June and December 2023 with newborns diagnosed with IHB hospitalized at Dr. Behçet Uz Children's Hospital in Turkey. Newborns weighing between 2500 and 4000 grams, without any congenital chronic diseases or anomalies, and diagnosed with IHB were included. Those who were using any medication that could affect vital parameters were excluded from the study. Term newborns were randomized by the researcher based on their order of admission to the NICU. The first patient admitted was assigned to the radiant (R) group by coin toss, while the second was assigned to the incubator (I) group. Equal numbers of newborns were included in each group. Heel-prick blood collection was performed under a radiant body heater for the R group and in an incubator environment for the I group. Both groups received routine clinical care. The groups were similar in terms of gestational age and phototherapy status.

The dependent variables were body temperature, SpO2, and HR and pain and stress levels assessed by the Acute Pain and Stress Scale for Neonates (ALPS-Neo). The independent variables were the radiant body heater and incubator environments.

A power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.2 based on an effect size of d = 0.64 reported by Göktürk and Sarıalioğlu [15]. With a 2-sided hypothesis, type 1 error of 0.05, and power of 0.80, the minimum sample size was calculated as 31 newborns per group. Allowing for 10% potential data loss, the target sample size was 68 newborns (34 per group). During data collection, 76 newborns with IHB were admitted. Four were excluded due to additional interventions that did not meet inclusion criteria, resulting in a final sample of 72 newborns: 36 in the R group and 36 in the I group (Fig. 1).

Institutional ethical approval was obtained from Dr. Behçet Uz Children's Hospital prior to the study. Written and verbal consent was obtained from parents before enrolling newborns. Body temperature, SpO2, and HR, along with pain and stress levels, were measured for 2, 2, and 3 minutes before, during, and after the heel prick, respectively. Physiological parameters were recorded using a monitor. Heel-prick procedures were performed when newborns first experienced pain, using a sterile lancet as per ward routine. Pain and stress were assessed using the ALPS-Neo Scale based on simultaneous video recordings of the newborns during the procedure. Videos, capturing the newborns from head to toe, were recorded with a mobile phone. Two experienced observers, a nurse with 6 years of NICU experience, and a NICU specialist physician, scored the videos independently and were blinded to the newborns’ environments to reduce observer bias. Video recording began immediately before the heel prick (considered minute 0) and continued for 5 min to complete the ALPS-Neo assessment.

Data collection tools included an informed consent form, a newborn descriptive information form, a newborn follow-up form, and the ALPS-Neo Neonatal Pain and Stress Assessment Scale. The descriptive information form collected 9 items: gender of the newborn, mode of delivery, birth weight, gestational age, postnatal age, feeding method, type of feeding, fluid intake status, and phototherapy status [16]. The newborn follow-up form was designed to score physiological parameters (body temperature, SpO2, heart rate [HR]) during the heel prick procedure. The ALPS-Neo scale, developed to assess newborn pain and stress based on individualized developmental care, is a 3-point Likert-type scale with 5 items: facial expression, breathing pattern, extremity tone, hand and foot activities, and activity level [17,18]. Measurements were made through observation. Higher scores indicate higher levels of stress and pain; scores of 3–5 indicate mild pain and stress, and scores >5 indicate severe pain and stress. The scale's reliability was previously reported with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.95 [18].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0, and the R program. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square or Fisher's exact tests. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) evaluated differences between groups, measurement times, and their interaction for numerical variables. The Bonferroni test was used for pairwise comparisons across time points. For variables with significant interaction effects, repeated-measures ANOVA was performed separately within each group. The first measurement was compared between groups using an independent samples t-test, and analysis of covariance was used for the second and third measurements. The Brunner-Langer F1-LD-F1 model (in the R program) was used to analyze pain scores. Benjamini correction was applied for pairwise comparisons between time periods after Brunner-Langer. Observer differences in pain scoring were evaluated using the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank test, and interobserver agreement was examined using Weighted Kappa analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Because the interaction between environment and time was significant (p < 0.001) in the in the repeated measures ANOVA, indicating that the changes in body temperature across the 3 time points differed between the 2 groups, comparisons were made between the groups at each time point separately. In addition, within-group comparisons were conducted across the 3 time points.

When body temperature levels were compared, no significant difference was found between the 2 groups before the heel prick procedure (p > 0.05). However, a significant difference in favor of the R group was observed during the procedure (p < 0.001) and also after the procedure (p < 0.05). When the body temperature levels of the newborns in the I group were compared across the procedure time points, a significant decrease was observed during and after the heel prick compared to before the procedure (p < 0.05). However, no statistically significant difference was found between the body temperature levels measured during and after the heel prick (p > 0.05). In the R group, body temperature decreased significantly during the procedure compared to before the heel prick (p < 0.05). However, no significant differences were found between the measurements taken before and after the procedure, or between those taken during and after the procedure (p > 0.05).

The SpO2 levels measured before, during, and after the heel prick did not differ significantly between the I and R groups (p > 0.05). When SpO2 values were analyzed over time within groups, a significant decrease was observed during and after the procedure compared to before (p < 0.05). Additionally, SpO2 levels significantly increased from during to after the procedure (p < 0.05).

The HR levels also showed no significant difference between groups at any time point (p>0.05). Within-group comparisons revealed a significant increase in HR during and after the heel prick compared to before (p < 0.05). A significant decrease in HR was also observed from during to after the procedure (p < 0.05). Pain and stress scores during the heel prick procedure for both groups are presented in Fig. 2.

No significant difference was found between the I and R groups at any of the three time points (before, during, and after; p > 0.05. However, within-group comparisons showed a statistically significant change in pain and stress scores over time in both groups (p < 0.001). Pain and stress scores increased significantly from before to during, and remained elevated after the heel prick (p < 0.05).

To assess the validity of the pain and stress evaluation method, two independent observers conducted the measurements. Because the pre-procedure pain and stress scores were consistently recorded as 0 by both observers, no statistical analysis was performed for that time point. Interobserver agreement was evaluated using Kappa analysis. Moderate agreement was found during the procedure (κ = 0.512), and substantial agreement was observed after the procedure (κ = 0.770). Weighted Kappa [2] analysis indicated no significant differences between the 2 observers (p > 0.05). Therefore, subsequent statistical analyses were based on the scores provided by the second observer.

Discussion

This study compared the effects of incubator and radiant heaters on physiological parameters (body temperature, SpO2, and HR) and pain and stress levels during heel prick blood collection in newborns in the NICU. Previous studies have examined body temperature regulation in preterm infants under different thermal environments [19,20]. A systematic review reported no proven advantage of incubators over radiant warmers in maintaining body temperature on the first day of life, although incubators may be preferable for infants weighing less than 1600 g due to reduced evaporative heat loss [13]. In our study, baseline body temperatures before the heel prick were similar between groups (incubator and radiant warmer environments). However, temperatures during and after the procedure were significantly higher in the radiant warmer group (p < 0.05). This may may be due to the radiant warmer's infrared rays heating objects directly, unlike incubators, which warm the air. Radiant warmers have been found effective in neonatal resuscitation and other invasive interventions such as intubation and IV insertion [21].

In our study, newborns in the R group maintained higher body temperatures across the procedure. In contrast, a significant temperature drop was observed in the I group during the heel prick, though post-procedure differences were not significant. This could be explained by the practice of opening the incubator lids prior to the procedure, leading to heat loss. A study has shown that the temperature in the incubator is determined by factors such as uneven heat transfer, inadequate air distribution, different sensor placements at each point, and non-standard room dimensions [22]. Although some decline in body temperature occurred post-procedure in the R group, it was not statistically significant. This could reflect the newborn's transient thermoregulatory response to pain. Literature supports the use of radiant warmers during invasive procedures to prevent hypothermia [21]. When the radiation distribution is correctly implemented, radiant heaters are a safe alternative for regulating infant body temperature [23]. A radiant heater can create a clinically acceptable environment for thermoregulating newborns during heel prick blood collection. Therefore, radiant heaters may be effective in maintaining newborn body temperature in healthcare facilities where incubators are not available.

Every newborn experiences pain during the heel prick procedure; thus, vital parameters are affected simultaneously. Here, when HR measured during the heel prick procedure was analyzed, there was no difference between the newborns in the incubator and radiant heater groups (p > 0.05). However, HR increased and decreased but remained higher during and after the procedure, respectively. All differences between the measurement times were significant. No studies have been found comparing heart rates between infants in incubators and those under radiant warming. One study compared the 60-minute cardiorespiratory system (SCRIP) scores of mid- to late-preterm neonates receiving maternal physical care at birth and those under radiant warming [24]. The findings indicated that the scores were similar in the 2 groups of neonates and that maternal physical care did not lead to better cardiorespiratory stability compared to neonates under radiant warming. Interventional studies aiming to reduce the pain and stress experienced by newborns in NICUs were conducted; when evaluating the effects of oral glucose and foot reflexology on HR during heel prick, a significant increase was found in HR averages after the procedure [25]. Additionally, in the NICU, newborns exposed to classical music or exposed to therapeutic touch did not exhibit significant HR changes (p > 0.05) [26]. The study examining the effect of swaddling on HR during newborn weight-weighing, HR significantly decreased in the swaddled group of 19 very low birth weight newborns compared to the unswaddled group [26]. Because our study compared 2 different environments (incubator and radiant heater), the “heel prick studies with therapeutic touch” may corroborate our findings. In a newborn who has been traumatized by a painful procedure, such as a heel prick, more time may be required to calm down and for the HR to reach its pre-procedure value after the procedure.

Another vital parameter that can be affected during painful procedures in newborns is SpO2. Here, when SpO2 levels measured during heel prick were compared between the incubator and radiant heater groups, no significant difference was found (p > 0.05). Because SpO2 remained lower following the procedure compared to the pre-procedure values in both groups, all differences between the measurement times were significant (p < 0.05). The statistically significant, albeit minimal, decrease in SpO2 in our study suggests that it would be appropriate for healthcare professionals to make the necessary preparations in advance to administer oxygen to the newborn for a period. Previous research partially supports our findings. Vital signs (heart rate, respiratory rate, SpO2, and body temperature) of term and preterm newborns were compared with and without an incubator cover. No significant difference was found between heart rate and respiratory rate measurements in term and preterm newborns with and without an incubator cover, while SpO2 values in term and preterm newborns with an incubator cover were found to be significantly higher than those in term and preterm newborns without an incubator cover [27]. Aydın, in a study examining the effects of classical music played to preterms in the NICU on the stress symptoms, growth, oxygen saturation level, and hospital stay of newborns, found no significant difference in the saturation levels of newborns between the music-listened and non-music-listened groups (p > 0.05) [8]. Furthermore, Ekinci et al. (2024) examined the effect of Yakson touch on newborn pain and physiological parameters, demonstrating no difference between the 2 groups in terms of oxygen saturation, HR, and pain scores before, during, and after the procedure (p > 0.05) [26]. In a study evaluating the effects of oral glucose and foot reflexology applied during the heel prick procedure on SpO2, no significant difference was found in this measure [25]. Considering the findings of these studies, including ours, although different procedures, such as listening to music, therapeutic touch, oral glucose administration, reflexology, and heel prick, produce different responses in newborns, no adverse effects related to blood flow and oxygen levels are evident.

Although the literature on the subject is limited, the lack of significant difference between the groups in terms of heart rate variability and SpO2 levels obtained here can be due to newborns included in the sample not having any health problems, other than IHB, that would affect heart rate and blood oxygenation.

Currently, nonpharmacological methods are important alternatives for controlling pain in newborns, especially for minor invasive procedures [28]. In a study evaluating the effects of oral glucose and foot reflexology applied during heel prick sampling on pain and stress, the mean ALPS-Neo scores of newborns who received glucose and reflexology after the painful procedure decreased significantly depending on the order of the procedure [25]. In another study of newborn weighing, swaddling significantly reduced pain and stress scores in 19 very low birth weight newborns compared with no swaddling [29]. Sound, light, tactile stimuli, and radiant heaters in neonatal intensive care units can increase physiological and behavioral stress responses. Accordingly, we anticipated that environmental differences might influence pain levels. The lack of between-group differences in our study can be explained by the fact that the behavioral pain symptoms of the newborns ceased 2 min after the heel prick, and the quick return to pre-procedure stability in the absence of additional environmental factors.

Furthermore, a previous study reported that factors affecting pain in newborns include sex, gestational age, and delivery mode [30,31]. The homogeneity of the incubator and radiant heater groups in our study may explain the lack of significant difference in pain and stress scores. While the ALPS-Neo scores close to 6 in our study did not necessitate pharmacological pain management, they do indicate the presence of pain and the need for monitoring during the heel prick procedure in newborns housed in incubators and radiant heaters. Nevertheless, pain is subjective and influenced by several factors. Aydın reported that classical music played to preterm infants in the NICU effectively reduced stress behaviors in preterm newborns, while Ekinci et al. found that newborns who received Yakson touch exhibited shorter crying times compared to the control group [8,26]. Both studies included an intervention (listening to music, therapeutic touch); however, here, we only compared the environments, finding no significant difference in pain and stress scores (p > 0.05). Therefore, our study differs from the literature in this respect.

This study was limited to newborns with gestational ages between ≥37 and ≤41 weeks at the Behçet Uz Children's Hospital Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) in Izmir, Turkey; therefore, the results can only be generalized to this group. Furthermore, when evaluating the results, it should be kept in mind that pain is a highly abstract concept and is influenced by many factors. Although the sample was created by considering factors that could cause extra pain or affect pain in newborns, it should not be overlooked that the results may be affected. Since performing each heel-prick blood collection by a different healthcare professional could alter physiological parameters and pain and stress scores in the newborn, the heel-prick blood collection was performed only by the researcher. In this regard, the study could not be conducted as a double-blind trial.

In conclusion, when physiological parameters (body temperature, SpO2, HR) and pain and stress scores after heel-prick blood collection were compared between incubator and radiant warmer environments, no significant differences were found in SpO2, HR, pain, or stress, whereas body temperature differed significantly between environments. Although body temperature increased significantly in the radiant warmer environment relative to the incubator, this difference did not play a vital role in affecting other physiological parameters. The incubator and radiant warmer environments were also evaluated before, during, and after the heel prick blood collection procedure, and, in both groups, all differences between the measurement times for physiological parameters and pain and stress levels were significant. When interpreting these findings, it should be noted that the study was limited to 72 newborns aged ≥37 to ≤41 weeks with a diagnosis of HBI in a single NICU in Turkey.
