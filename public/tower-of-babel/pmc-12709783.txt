Endovascular therapy versus medical management in posterior cerebral artery stroke: neurological gains without functional superiority: a meta-analysis
Authors: Mostafa Hossam El Din Moawad, Ahmed Farid Gadelmawla, Luay A Abu Alia, Hamza A Abdul-Hafez, Ibraheem M Alkhawaldeh, Mohammed Khaled Mohammed, Mohammad Abualnadi, Attalla F El-kott, Mohammed A AlShehri, Sally Negm, Ali GadKarim A Salih, Mohamed Abouzid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPosterior cerebral artery (PCA) infarctions represent 5–10% of all acute ischemic strokes, often manifesting with visual and cognitive deficits that can substantially impair quality of life. Although endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) is an established treatment for large-vessel occlusions, its role in isolated PCA occlusion remains uncertain, with limited evidence and heterogeneous outcomes across studies. We conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis to compare the effictiveness and safety of EVT versus medical management (MM) in patients with acute PCA occlusion.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to PRISMA guidelines. PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science were searched until July 2025. Eligible studies included randomized or cohort studies comparing EVT with MM in PCA occlusion. Primary outcomes were excellent (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] 0–1) and favorable (mRS 0–2) functional outcomes at 90 days. Secondary outcomes included change in National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH), and all-cause mortality. Pooled risk ratios (RR) and mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using random-effects models.ResultsNine high-quality cohort studies including 57,287 patients (EVT = 2475; MM = 54,812) were analyzed. EVT was not associated with significant improvement in excellent (RR = 1.05; 95% CI, 0.91–1.21) or favorable (RR = 0.94; 95% CI, 0.84–1.05) functional outcomes compared to MM. Mortality (RR = 1.33; 95% CI, 0.99–1.80) and sICH (RR = 1.60; 95% CI, 0.87–2.93) rates were comparable between groups. However, EVT was associated with short-term neurological improvement on NIHSS at discharge (MD=-1.21; 95% CI, -1.96 to -0.46; p = 0.002).ConclusionsEVT for PCA was associated with short-term neurological improvement versus MM, with mortality and sICH remaining comparable. However, these benefits did not translate into superior long-term functional outcomes as measured by mRS. The unique clinical features of PCA stroke, particularly visual and cognitive impairments not adequately captured by global disability scales, may contribute to this discrepancy. High-quality randomized trials incorporating domain-specific outcomes are warranted to define the role of EVT in PCA occlusion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04512-x.Keywords: Posterior cerebral artery occlusion, Ischemic stroke, Endovascular therapy, Medical management, Thrombectomy, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Posterior cerebral artery (PCA) infarctions account for approximately 5–10% of all acute ischemic stroke (AIS) patients [1, 2]. Clinical manifestations in patients with PCA occlusion are widely varied, including vision field defect, cognitive disturbances, hemisensory loss or hemiparesis in case of more proximal occlusion, and rarely consciousness impairment when the infarction involves the artery of Percheron [3, 4].

While patients with acute PCA occlusion often have low National Institute of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score and exhibit mild to moderate deficit compared to anterior circulation or basilar artery strokes [1, 5], they can lead to significant long-term consequences. Persistence visual or cognitive impairments can interfere with driving, reading, work, and other daily activities, that significantly affect quality of life [1, 4, 6]. Moreover, more proximal PCA occlusions affecting thalamic nuclei, cerebral peduncle, or primary visual cortex are associated with greater morbidity and risk of disability in these patients [1, 4, 6].

Endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) is considered as a standard effective treatment for patients with AIS of anterior circulation large vessel occlusions and basilar artery occlusions within early and late timeframe [7, 8]. Recent advance in thrombectomy devices and techniques have extended the EVT use for medium- and distal-vessel occlusions, including PCA occlusion [9, 10]. Despite being EVT often achieves higher rates of arterial recanalization compared to medical management (MM) alone, but its safety and clinical benefit compared to MM in PCA occlusions remain underexplored. Multiple studies regarding EVT effectiveness in patients with PCA occlusion have reported heterogenous results and no randomized trial has focused exclusively on PCA territory occlusions [4, 11–16].

Several single center and multicenter cohorts have described outcomes of patients with PCA occlusion managed with MM, intravenous thrombolysis (IVT), and EVT [12, 14]. Some studies suggest that selected patients undergoing EVT may show earlier neurological or visual improvements despite modest baseline NIHSS scores; others find no clear advantage in 3‑month functional independence and note possible trends toward higher symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH) without definitive outcome benefit [13–16]. A systematic review conducted by Berberich et al. [5] pooled available data and found that no significant differences in the clinical outcomes, sICH rates, and mortality rates between EVT + MM compared to MM alone in PCA occlusion patients.

Therefore, the optimal management strategy for acute PCA infarction remains uncertain. Given the potential impact of PCA stroke sequelae on quality of life and the growing interest in distal vessel thrombectomy, further comparative investigation is needed. In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we aim to compare EVT versus MM in patients with acute PCA occlusion, assessing clinical progression, and safety parameters.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [17] and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [18]. The protocol of this study was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251113803). A PRISMA checklist of this study is provided in Table S1 in the supplementary materials.

Searching strategy

We conducted a comprehensive search in the major databases, including PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, to find all eligible and relevant published articles that could be included in our study. From inception until July 2025, the search was conducted using the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) keywords: “Posterior Cerebral Artery” OR “Posterior Cerebral Artery Stroke” OR “Posterior Cerebral Artery Occlusion” AND “Thrombectomy” OR “Endovascular Thrombectomy” OR “Drug Therapy” OR “Medical management” without language restriction. We searched using the title and abstract on PubMed, as well as abstract on Scopus and Web of Science. A detailed search strategies for all databases are emphasized in Table S2 on the supplementary materials. Articles from the search were sent to EndNote to remove duplicated articles and start the screening process.

Eligibility criteria and screening

After duplicate removal, articles were uploaded to Rayyan for title, abstract, and full-text screening. We included studies meeting the following criteria (Table S3):

◦ Comparator (C): Medical management (MM); defined as best medical therapy with or without intravenous thrombolysis (IVT)

◦ Comparator (C): Medical management (MM); defined as best medical therapy with or without intravenous thrombolysis (IVT)

◦ Outcomes: mRS 0–1, mRS 0–2, NIHSS change from baseline to discharge, sICH, and all-cause mortality

◦ Study design: Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and cohort studies

We excluded case reports, reviews, and grey literature, such as unpublished theses, conference proceedings, government reports, white papers, and technical reports. Two independent authors screened titles and abstracts, then reviewed full texts to select included studies. Any discrepancies were resolved by the senior author.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted from the included studies by two independent authors using standardized Microsoft Excel sheets. We extracted the baseline and general characteristics of the included studies, including study design, sample size, age, gender, and baseline NIHSS score. Moreover, clinical outcomes were also extracted, including the proportion of patients achieving mRS scores of 0–1 and 0–2, as well as the rates of sICH, all-cause mortality, and NIHSS change from baseline. Discrepancies between authors were resolved by the senior author.

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

Two authors independently assessed quality and risk of bias, reconciling differences by consensus or via discussion with the senior author. For all included studies, we assessed risk of bias in non-randomized studies using the ROBINS-I tool, rating seven domains (confounding, participant selection, intervention classification, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting) [19]. We rated certainty of evidence using GRADE across five domains (risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication bias), classifying each outcome as high, moderate, low, or very low [20].

Statistical analysis

Meta-analyses employed an available-case approach for each outcome which is why sample sizes (N) differ for each outcome based on the studies that reported that endpoint. In cases of multiple publications and overlapping datasets, we chose one non-overlapping cohort for each analysis to avoid double counting. We performed statistical analysis using Review Manager version 5.3. For continuous outcomes (NIHSS change), we calculated mean difference (MD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). When a study reported medians and interquartile ranges, we estimated means and standard deviations based on median, IQR, and sample size as per Hozo et al. [21]. For dichotomous outcomes (mRS 0–1, mRS 0–2, sICH, and mortality), we used risk ratio (RR) with 95% CI. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with thresholds of 0–30% (low), 30–70% (moderate), and 70–100% (high). A P-value < 0.05 indicated significant heterogeneity. Random-effects model was applied to account for between-study variability. Moreover, publication bias couldn’t be calculated because number of included studies in each outcome were less than 10 studies.

Results

Search results and study selection

The comprehensive search across the aforementioned databases yielded a total of 429 articles, including 256 duplicates. These studies were then screened by title and abstract screening to determine relevancy and yielded 15 studies which were then thoroughly screened after retrieval of full text to determine compliance with inclusion criteria and 9 studies were eligible for data extraction, analysis and overall inclusion in this systematic review and meta-analysis [15, 22–29] (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram of searching and screening processes. *: Where multiple publications or overlapping datasets existed, we selected a single non-overlapping cohort to prevent double counting

Baseline characteristics

All included studies in this analysis utilized a cohort study design. The studies varied in setting, center size and patient populations but collectively provided a broad overview and valuable insight of the clinical presentations of PCA patients. The pooled sample was comprised of 2475 patients treated with EVT and 54,812 with MM. Study populations varied in size, with EVT cohorts ranging from 21 to 745 patients and MM cohorts from 85 to 34,150 patients. Across studies, isolated PCA occlusions were typically extracted as a subgroup from larger registries. Mean age of patients across included studies ranged from 69.4 to 73.3 years in the EVT arm and 66.4 to 76.2 years in MM arms. Gender distribution showed a higher proportion in favor of males in both groups with the EVT arm ranging from 34.1% to 64%, and the MM arm from 40.1% to 68%. Median NIHSS scores ranged from 7 to 10.21 in EVT arms compared to 3 to 10.82 in MM arms (Table 1).

Quality and assessment

According to the ROBINS-I quality assessment tool, four studies showed low risk of bias, four showed moderate risk of bias and one registry-based study (Dicpinigaitis 2024) showed serious risk of bias due to potential presence of confounding (Fig. 2).

Risk of bias assessment of included studies using ROBINS-I tool

Meta analysis

Modified rankin scale (mRS 0–1)

The analysis demonstrated no statistically significant in achieving excellent functional outcomes (mRS 0–1) within 90 days in EVT group compared to MM groups across the 2635 patients (EVT = 949, MM = 1686) with RR = 1.05, 95% CI [0.91, 1.21], p = 0.53. Non-significant heterogeneity was observed (Heterogeneity: Tau² = 0.01; Chi² = 9.66, df = 6 (P = 0.14); I² = 38%) (Fig. 3).

Effect of EVT and MM on mRs 0–1 (Excellent functional outcome)

Modified rankin scale (mRS 0–2)

The analysis, including 57,104 patients (EVT = 2420, MM = 54684) demonstrated no significant difference in achieving favorable functional outcome within 90 days (mRS 0–2) between the two groups (EVT and MM) (RR = 0.94, 95% CI [0.84, 1.05], p = 0.29). Substantial heterogeneity (Heterogeneity: Tau² = 0.02; Chi² = 25.41, df = 7 (P = 0.0006); I² = 72%) was observed. (Fig. 4). In sensitivity analyses, we excluded Brake 2024 and Dicpinigaitis 2024 together because both are large registry-based cohorts with disproportionately large sample sizes. Heterogeneity became minimal (Heterogeneity: Tau² = 0.00; Chi² = 4.02, df = 5 (P = 0.55); I² = 0%) (Fig. 5).

Effect of EVT and MM on mRs 0–2

Sensitivity analysis of the effect of EVT and MM on mRs 0–2

Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH)

Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage is defined as any intracranial hemorrhage on follow-up neuroimaging that is accompanied by a neurological deterioration attributable to the hemorrhage. The meta-analysis, including a total of 2718 patients (EVT = 998, MM = 1720) showed no statistically significant difference in risk of sICH between the EVT and MM groups (RR = 1.60, 95% CI [0.87, 2.93], p = 0.13). Non-significant heterogeneity (Heterogeneity: Tau² = 0.24; Chi² = 10.04, df = 6 (P = 0.12); I² = 40%) was observed (Fig. 6).

Effect of EVT and MM on Symptomatic Intracranial Hemorrhage (sICH)

Mortality rates

Mortality rate was comparable in the MM group and the EVT group (RR = 1.33, 95% CI [0.99, 1.80], p = 0.06). Non-significant heterogeneity (Heterogeneity: Tau² = 0.06; Chi² = 10.31, df = 6 (P = 0.11); I² = 42%) was observed (Fig. 7).

Effect of EVT and MM on mortality rate

National institutes of health stroke scale (NIHSS)

Upon analysis of the NIHSS score across 656 patients (EVT = 276, MM = 380), a notable statistically significant improvement in NIHSS scores were found in the EVT group in comparison to the MM group (MD=−1.21, 95% CI [−1.96, −0.46], p = 0.002) and with negligible heterogeneity observed (Heterogeneity: Tau² = 0.00; Chi² = 0.61, df = 3 (P = 0.89); I² = 0%) (Fig. 8).

Effect of EVT and MM on National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS)

Certainty of evidence

On GRADE assessment, certainty started with Low (observational cohorts) and no upgrade criteria were met. For functional outcomes, certainty was Low for mRS 0–1 (no serious concerns beyond design) and Very low for mRS 0–2 due to serious risk of bias and inconsistency (substantial heterogeneity). The safety outcome (sICH) was assigned Very low certainty, downgraded for imprecision, since the confidence interval included both substantial harm and no effect. Mortality was Low certainty (no additional downgrades beyond design) and NIHSS change was Low certainty with no serious concerns across domains other than study design. Important indirectness and publication bias signs were also absent across outcomes. The overall evidence base, primarily influenced by the risk of bias, heterogeneity, and imprecision, remains predominantly Low to Very low certainty and should be approached carefully (Table 2).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis study was conducted to compare EVT and MM for patients with PCA ischemic stroke in light of different clinical outcomes, including the proportion of patients achieving mRS scores of 0–1 and 0–2, rates of sICH, mortality, and NIHSS change from baseline. As pooled from the included studies, only the NIHSS score change showed a statistically significant difference favoring EVT over MM for patients with PCA ischemic stroke. Our synthesis indicates that early neurologic gains are achieved with EVT without superior 90-day functional outcomes or a mortality advantage. Such findings are noteworthy and temper claims of efficacy, which underscore the need for domain-specific endpoints for PCA.

The mRS is a physician-reported assessment of global disability following the occurrence of acute stroke, with a score ranging from zero for those with no symptoms to 6 for death [30]. An mRS score of 0–1 at the end of follow-up indicates excellent functional outcomes with nonsignificant disability, and an mRS score of 0–2 indicates favorable functional outcomes with slight disability [31, 32]. In this analysis, no significant differences were observed between EVT and MM for both mRS 0–1 and 0–2. The same findings were observed by a previous meta-analysis comparing EVT and MM for PCA occlusions, whereas no significant differences in excellent and favorable functional outcomes at three months were found [33]. In keeping with prior pooled analyses, we did not observe a clear EVT advantage for 90-day functional independence [33]. However, these observations contradict those of a previous meta-analysis investigating EVT vs. MM for patients with medium vessel occlusion in the anterior circulation, where a statistically significant difference was found for excellent functional outcomes favoring EVT over MM [34]. The reason could be the fact that the mRS is a measure of global functional disability; thus, it does not strictly cover the unique impairments of PCA strokes, including visual and cognitive impairments in addition to complex visual processing deficits. As a result, the use of mRS for PCA strokes does not result in an accurate assessment of the stroke burden in this territory, which could have underestimated the following treatment effects and recovery [35, 36].

EVT is associated with hemorrhagic complications owing to the mechanical manipulation of intracranial vessels and reperfusion injury, leading to sICH, subarachnoid hemorrhage, or distal embolization [37]. Our safety profile is compatible with earlier meta-analytic estimates, which suggested a possible but imprecise increase in sICH with EVT relative to MM [33]. A previous meta-analysis study based on patients with acute basilar artery occlusion reported statistically significant higher rates of sICH for patients receiving EVT compared to those on MM [38]. A similar observation was found by another meta-analysis conducted on patients with mild anterior circulation large vessel occlusion, with higher rates of sICH in those treated with EVT [39]. Medium-sized and distal vessels are longer with thinner arterial walls and more tortuous courses; thus, when occluded, they are susceptible to perforation, dissection, and spasm during EVT [10]. In this analysis, no significant differences were observed for the risk of sICH between EVT and MM groups. This nonsignificant difference could be due to discrepancies in protocols regarding the diagnosis of hemorrhagic transformations and differences in the baseline characteristics of the patients included across the studies. Moreover, the more distal nature of the occlusions, together with advances in endovascular manipulations and technical precision, could have reduced the risks for these symptomatic complications when undergoing EVT.

As pooled from seven studies, no significant differences were found in mortality rates between EVT and MM. This could be due to the less severe nature of PCA strokes as compared to anterior circulation strokes. Anterior circulation strokes are associated with greater impact on motor functions, consciousness, and autonomic centers within the brainstem [40, 41]. PCA strokes often spare motor and brainstem functions, thus contributing to less mortality and morbidity [42]. As EVT did not show an increase in mortality risks, this could highlight the safety of this intervention for managing PCA strokes. Among the pooled studies, the highest mortality risk ratio for EVT compared to MM was 2.03 (95% CI: 1.29–3.19), as reported by Nguyen et al. [15]. This was attributed to the fact that the EVT group was composed of patients with a higher degree of illness severity as detected by the baseline NIHSS scores and with more proximal occlusion compared to those offered MM. Thus, patient-specific characteristics are important for better assessment of mortality risks, with the importance of individualized decision-making for each case presentation.

NIHSS is used to quantify the severity of stroke, provide prognostic measures, and help in guiding management strategies, with the NIHSS value being one of the most important factors for choosing between mechanical thrombectomy or IV thrombolytic treatment [43, 44]. Despite being a widely used scoring system for stroke patients, the NIHSS has a potential weakness regarding its uneven scoring for different lesion-specific neurological deficits, as the scale is more weighted to anterior circulation deficits, including motor functions and cortical signs, with posterior circulation deficits being less represented, causing a difference in NIHSS cutoff scores between anterior and posterior circulation strokes for predicting chronic outcomes [45, 46]. NIHSS scores less than or equal to 4 were used for defining minor strokes and predicting excellent outcomes; however, a previous study found that 15% of posterior circulation stroke patients with an NIHSS score of less than or equal to 4 had death or disability as a direct consequence of this stroke at 3 months [47]. Moreover, for stroke patients with posterior circulation infractions, a baseline NIHSS score of 0 could still result in neurological and muscular complications; thus, baseline NIHSS scores cannot be used alone to rule out the risk of complications in posterior circulation infarcts [46, 48]. In this review, baseline NIHSS scores ranged from 7 to 10.2 in the EVT arm compared to 3 to 10.8 in the MM arms. Moreover, as pooled from four studies, a statistically significant difference in NIHSS scores was found favoring EVT for PCA stroke patients compared to those receiving MM. These findings are consistent with a recent meta-analysis of isolated PCA occlusions, which also observed greater early neurological improvement with EVT versus MM [33]. This finding suggests that EVT may offer better functional recovery, especially in the context of PCA stroke manifestations being less weighted on the NIHSS. This could be explained by the earlier revascularization in EVT, which could limit residual functional impairments. This is comparable with a finding of a previous meta-analysis in which early EVT for large-vessel ischemic strokes was associated with lower degrees of disability at 3 months compared to standalone MM [49].

The NIHSS improvement and mRS results mismatch can be explained by mRS limitations in PCA strokes [50]. Key sequelae such as homonymous hemianopia, alexia without agraphia, prosopagnosia, topographic disorientation and higher-order visual deficits are poorly represented in the global disability scales that concentrate on motor disability [51, 52]. Consequently, numerous PCA stroke survivors are mRS 0–2, though they have significant visual and cognitive disabilities [53]. Subsequent research and clinical work needs to address PCA clinical frameworks that include visual fields (perimetry), shortened neuropsychological instruments in the stroke-affected visuospatial/executive area, and PCA-visual quality-of-life scales.

Clinical implications and directions for future research

The results of this meta-analysis have several implications for clinical practice and future research. First, Pooled NIHSS changes show that EVT is associated with early neurological improvement in PCA ischemic stroke without a notable increase in mortality or sICH. This, notably, might reduce remaining domain-specific deficits, especially in visual field, visuocognitive deficits, and most likely without durable gains in global function as assessed by mRS, where global function is still likely. Second, as PCA strokes often present with visual and cognitive impairments in addition to high-level visual processing deficits, focusing on these presentations during clinical assessment would provide a better understanding of the magnitude of functional disability instead of using mRS, which doesn’t accurately represent the magnitude of these impairments. Thus, assessments of visual field, neuropsychological evaluation, and quality of life questionnaires might provide a better understanding of the stroke burden with timely and accurate diagnosis, which would enable early interventions and better functional outcomes. Third, as this analysis was based on cohort studies, future high-quality trials are needed to confirm the effectiveness and safety of EVT for managing strokes in this territory with a comparison of different EVT techniques, such as stent retrievers and direct aspiration, to determine the best management plan for these distal vessels.

Several included cohorts reported substantial IVT use in one or both treatment arms, and IVT can produce relatively rapid partial or complete recanalization that translates into short-term NIHSS improvements even when longer-term structural injury and domain-specific deficits persist. In registry and single-center series where IVT rates differ between EVT and MM groups, early NIHSS gains attributed to EVT may thus be partially mediated by preceding thrombolysis or by a combined effect of IVT plus mechanical reperfusion. Conversely, combined IVT + EVT may also influence safety signals (for example, reperfusion hemorrhage) in ways that single-modality comparisons do not capture. When possible, future reports should present IVT-stratified outcomes (EVT with IVT, EVT without IVT, IVT alone) or perform subgroup analyses by thrombolysis status to disentangle the independent contributions of IVT and EVT to early neurologic improvement and safety.

Strengths and limitations

In this meta-analysis, we provided up-to-date evidence regarding the effectiveness and safety of EVT as compared to MM for PCA ischemic stroke patients. The Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses and PRISMA guidelines were followed. Only studies conducted on patients with PCA strokes were included. Across all the included studies, adjustments for baseline differences, including age, stroke severity, and the use of intravenous thrombolysis, were performed using propensity score matching or multivariable regression when available. However, this meta-analysis has several limitations. First, all the included studies were observational in design, with no clinical trials comparing EVT and MM for PCA ischemic strokes. Second, there were variations across the included studies regarding selection criteria, outcome diagnostic protocols, stroke severity, and timing of interventions, all of which could have yielded to heterogeneity on some of the assessed outcomes. The substantial heterogeneity observed for some pooled outcomes and its marked attenuation when large registry cohorts were excluded likely reflects a mixture of clinical and methodological differences between registry-based studies and single-center or multicenter cohorts. Registry datasets tend to be larger and more heterogeneous with broader inclusion criteria, variable imaging and treatment protocols, less granular documentation of occlusion site and IVT timing, and greater risk of unmeasured confounding; by contrast, single-center cohorts often apply more uniform imaging selection, procedural techniques, and outcome adjudication but may select patients more strictly (for example, more proximal PCA occlusions or higher NIHSS). Other contributors include differences in operator experience and device selection, variable definitions of sICH and outcome measurement timing, incomplete adjustment for baseline severity or comorbidities, and statistical weighting where very large registries dominate pooled estimates. Our sensitivity analysis (excluding the two largest registry cohorts) drove I² from 72% to 0%, illustrating how much the pooled estimate can be influenced by registry versus cohort designs. These observations argue for cautious interpretation of pooled effects across mixed study types and for the value of stratified/sensitivity analyses and harmonized outcome definitions in future studies. Third, although EVT is recommended for stroke patients with high NIHSS scores, differences in baseline NIHSS scores between patients undergoing EVT and MM could be a contributing factor to score changes after 3 months. Fourth, mRS doesn’t accurately represent the unique clinical manifestations of PCA strokes, which could have underestimated the treatment effects. Sixth, We were unable to perform subgroup meta-analyses by occlusion site (proximal vs. distal) or by EVT technique (stent retriever vs. aspiration) because the included studies variably mixed segments, rarely reported site-stratified outcomes, and used non-comparable device categories. Seventh, two of included cohorts analyzed isolated PCA occlusions as subgroups within larger registries rather than PCA-dedicated studies, which may limit generalizability to PCA-exclusive populations. Moreover, the pronounced MM≫EVT arm imbalance reflects real-world practice patterns (non-protocolized allocation) and may influence study weights and residual confounding.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis demonstrates that even though EVT in PCA ischemic stroke is associated with early neurological improvement on the NIHSS in comparison with MM, this did not lead to better 90-day functional outcomes on the mRS. This highlights the need to appreciate the unique visual and cognitive features of PCA strokes and the need to assess idiographic outcomes that might better reflect clinically relevant advantages. No significant differences were found regarding the risk of sICH and mortality, highlighting the safety of these interventions. Differences in diagnostic protocols and stroke severity, together with the heterogeneity for some of the reported outcomes, highlight the importance of future high-quality interventional studies with consistent diagnostic criteria and standardized procedural techniques in order to provide a better understanding of the effectiveness and safety of these interventions for PCA ischemic stroke patients.
