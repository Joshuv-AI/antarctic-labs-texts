Kubo-Martin-Schwinger states of Path-structured Flow in Directed Brain Synaptic Networks
Elkaïoum M. Moutuou, Habib Benali

Abstract
The brain’s synaptic network, characterized by parallel connections and feedback loops, drives interaction pathways between neurons through a large system with infinitely many degrees of freedom. This system is best modeled by the graph C*-algebra of the underlying directed graph, the Toeplitz-Cuntz-Krieger (TCK) algebra, which captures the diversity of path-structured flow connectivity. Equipped with the gauge action, the TCK algebra defines an algebraic quantum system , and here we demonstrate that its thermodynamic properties provide a natural framework for describing the dynamic mappings of potential flow pathways within the network. Specifically, the KMS states of this system represent the stationary distributions of a non-Markovian stochastic process with memory decay, capturing how influence propagates along exponentially weighted paths, and yield global statistical measures of neuronal interactions. Applied to the C. elegans synaptic network, our framework reveals that neurolocomotor neurons emerge as the primary hubs of incoming path-structured flow at inverse temperatures where the entropy of KMS states peaks. This finding aligns with experimental evidence of the foundational role of locomotion in C. elegans behavior, suggesting that functional centrality may arise from the topological embedding of neurons rather than solely from local physiological properties. Our results highlight the potential of algebraic quantum methods and graph algebras to uncover patterns of functional organization in complex systems and neuroscience.

Due to multiple chemical synapses, reciprocal electrical channels via gap-junctions, and self-synapses,

G
contains directed cycles and recurrent patterns, resulting in interactions with infinitely many degrees of freedom. Algebraic quantum mechanics (AQM)
[
8
]
and graph C*-algebras
[

]
offer sophisticated formalism to describe such a system. The main idea behind AQM methods

[
10
,
8
]
is that physical states are represented by
states
of a C*-algebra
\mathcal{O}
, unlike standard quantum mechanics, which uses a Hilbert space of state vectors and typically deals with systems with finite degrees of freedom.
Observables are self-adjoint elements in
\mathcal{O}
, and time-evolution is represented by a
C^{*}
–dynamics
\alpha_{t}
on
\mathcal{O}
. The pair
(\mathcal{O},\alpha)
is referred to as a
C*–dynamical system
or an
algebraic quantum system
(see Appendix
A
).

Given a state
\omega
and an observable
U\in\mathcal{O}
, the number
\omega(U)
is the expectation value of
U
in state
\omega
. The observable
U
is
analytic
w.r.t.
\alpha
if
t\mapsto\alpha_{t}(U)
extends to an analytic function
\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathcal{O},\ z\mapsto\alpha_{z}(U)
, where
z=t+i\beta
. Equilibrium states of
(\mathcal{O},\alpha)
at inverse temperature
\beta
are states
\omega
satisfying the Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS) condition
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
:

\displaystyle\omega(U\alpha_{i\beta}(V))=\omega(VU),

(1)

for all analytic elements
V,U
.
Such
\omega
is called a
KMS state
. This formalism is an algebraic extension
[
8
]
of classical quantum statistical mechanics
[
13
]
, where observables are represented by self-adjoint matrices, time-evolution is generated by a Hamiltonian
H
via
\alpha_{t}(U)=e^{itH}Ue^{-itH}
, and equilibrium states are given by the Gibbs state
\omega_{\beta}(U)=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho U)
, with
\rho=e^{-\beta H}/\operatorname{Tr}(e^{-\beta H})
.

Here, the use of this formalism is motivated by its ability to rigorously describe the dynamics of path-structured flow, though our framework does not involve traditional quantum phenomena.
\mathcal{O}
is the
Toeplitz-Cuntz-Krieger (TCK) algebra

[
9
]
of
G

Equipped with its natural dynamics, the KMS states of the resulting algebraic quantum system are generated by probability distributions, interpreted as stationary states of a generalized non-Markovian stochastic process with exponentially decaying memory (see Appendix
B
), that describe the interaction profiles of all neurons. These KMS states provide a rigorous foundation for uncovering the mechanisms through which network structure drives the dynamic states of functional interactions. While this work focuses on establishing and illustrating the conceptual foundations of our AQM-based framework, its functional implications and detailed applications are presented in a companion study
[
14
]

.1.1
Algebraic operations

We define algebraic operations on paths to describe concatenation and scaling. Let
\mathcal{H}
be the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space
of complex-valued sequences

indexed by synaptic walks
\gamma
, such that
\sum|\xi_{\gamma}|^{2}<\infty

\mathcal{H}
are expressed as formal sums

, where
\delta_{\gamma}
is the Dirac measure on path
\gamma
, and

are path scaling factors. To neuron

Q_{i}
onto the subspace

generated by all

. And to synapse

, we associate a partial isometry

from

to
\mathcal{H}_{i}
, defined by
S_{a}(\delta_{\gamma})=\delta_{a\gamma}
if
\operatorname{\tt post}(\gamma)=\operatorname{\tt pre}(a)
, and
0
otherwise. The adjoint is given by
S_{a}^{*}(\delta_{a\gamma})=\delta_{\gamma}
(see Appendix
A
). These representations are illustrated in Fig.

.2
The graph algebraic quantum system

The algebra
\mathcal{O}
carries a C*-dynamics
\alpha_{t}
, obtained from the
gauge action
:

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}(S_{\gamma}S_{\theta}^{*})=e^{it(|\gamma|-|\theta|)}S_{\gamma}S_{\theta}^{*},

(3)

defining the algebraic quantum system
(\mathcal{O},\alpha)
(see Appendix). Any state
\omega
on
(\mathcal{O},\alpha)
defines a probability distribution
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}_{i}
over neurons via
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}_{i}=\omega(Q_{i})
, since
\omega(Q_{i})\geq 0
and
\omega(\sum Q_{i})=1
, as
\sum_{i}Q_{i}
is the unit in
\mathcal{O}
(see Appendix
A
). Now by Eq.
1
and (
3
), a state
\omega
of
(\mathcal{O},\alpha)
is KMS at inverse temperature
\beta
iff

\displaystyle\omega(S_{\gamma}S_{\theta}^{*})

\displaystyle=\omega(S_{\theta}^{*}\alpha_{i\beta}(S_{\gamma}))

\displaystyle=e^{-\beta|\gamma|}\omega(S_{\theta}^{*}S_{\gamma})

\displaystyle=e^{-\beta|\gamma|}\omega(S_{\gamma}\alpha_{i\beta}(S_{\theta}^{*}))

\displaystyle=e^{-\beta(|\gamma|-|\theta|)}\omega(S_{\gamma}S_{\theta}^{*}).

Hence,
\omega(S_{\gamma}S_{\gamma}^{*})=e^{-\beta|\gamma|}\omega(Q_{\operatorname{\tt pre}(\gamma)})
as a consequence of the second line of this equation, while the last line yields
\omega(S_{\gamma}S_{\theta}^{*})=0
if
|\gamma|\neq|\theta|
. In particular, for a synapse
a
, the expected value of
S_{a}S_{a}^{*}
in a KMS state
\omega
satisfies

\displaystyle e^{\beta}\omega(S_{a}S_{a}^{*})=\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}_{\operatorname{\tt pre}(a)},

(4)

which, thanks to Eq. (
18
) combined with Eq. (
22
), implies
\sum_{\operatorname{\tt post}(a)=i}\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}_{\operatorname{\tt pre}(a)}\leq e^{\beta}\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}_{i}
. Using the adjacency matrix
A
,
\sum_{j}A_{ij}\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}_{j}\leq e^{\beta}\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}_{i}
; i.e.,
A\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}\leq e^{\beta}\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}
. If
\beta>\log r
, where
r
is the spectral radius of
A
,
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}
is not an eigenvector of
A
and
1-e^{-\beta}A
is non-singular and the components of
\Psi^{\omega}=(1-e^{-\beta}A)\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}
are non-negative, so:

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}=(1-e^{-\beta}A)^{-1}\Psi^{\omega}.

(5)

To characterize the vectors
\Psi
associated with KMS states as in Eq.
27
, define the
partition vector

Z^{\beta}
, where
Z^{\beta}_{j}=\sum_{\operatorname{\tt pre}(\gamma)=j}e^{-\beta|\gamma|}
, which counts all paths starting at
j
weighted by the propagation scale
e^{-\beta}
. Since
\beta>\log r
, the expansion
(1-e^{-\beta}A)^{-1}=\sum_{n}e^{-\beta n}A^{n}
converges (see Appendix
A
), and using the fact that
A^{n}_{ij}
counts all walks from
j
to
i
, we have

Z_{j}^{\beta}=\sum_{n}\sum_{i}e^{-\beta n}A^{n}_{ij}=\sum_{i}(1-e^{-\beta}A)_{ij}^{-1}.

Then,
\Psi^{\omega}
satisfies:

\displaystyle\langle\Psi,Z^{\beta}\rangle=1.

(6)

Conversely, if a non-negative vector
\Psi
satisfies Eq. (
6
), then
\operatorname{{\bf x}}=(1-e^{-\beta}A)^{-1}\Psi

is a probability distribution and defines a KMS state
\omega^{\Psi}
via
\omega^{\Psi}(S_{\gamma}S_{\theta}^{*})=e^{-\beta|\gamma|}\operatorname{{\bf x}}_{\operatorname{\tt pre}(\gamma)}
if
\gamma=\theta
, and
0
otherwise
[
17
]
. Thus, for
\beta>\log r
, the KMS state space
\Omega_{\beta}
is determined by Eq. (
6
) and the values
\omega(Q_{i})
(see Appendix
A
for details).

.2.1
Neuronal interactions

For
\beta>\log r
, the KMS state space
\Omega_{\beta}
is a convex set
[
8
]
, and its extreme points are the
pure KMS states
. These are computed explicitly: for fixed neuron
j
,
\Psi^{j|\beta}=\left(0,...,0,(Z_{j}^{\beta})^{-1},0,...,0\right)
solves Eq. (
6
), and defines the pure KMS state
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}=(1-e^{-\beta}A)^{-1}\Psi^{j|\beta}
. Thus, there is a ’critical’ inverse temperature
\beta_{c}=\log r
above which
\Omega_{\beta}
is the
(N-1)
–dimensional convex hull of the pure states
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}
. The
i
-component of
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}
has the explicit form:

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}_{i}=\frac{1}{Z_{j}^{\beta}}\sum_{n}e^{-\beta n}A^{n}_{ij}=\frac{1}{Z_{j}^{\beta}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\operatorname{\tt post}(\gamma)=i\\
\operatorname{\tt pre}(\gamma)=j\end{subarray}}e^{-\beta|\gamma|},

(7)

giving the probability of interaction from neuron
j
to neuron
i
at inverse temperature
\beta
.

Thus,
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}
is the outgoing path-structured
interaction profile
of
j
at a fixed
\beta
. Varying
\beta
from larger
\beta
toward the critical value
\beta_{c}
draws the dynamic mapping of all potential interactions originting from
j
. Specifically, for sufficiently large
\beta
,
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}\sim(0,...,0,1,0,...,0)
, where
1
is at the
j
-th component, indicating purely local self-interaction. As
\beta
approaches
\beta_{c}
, the profile spreads, gaining more and more nonzero components, while the self-interaction weight
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}_{j}
decreases.

.2.2
Mixed states and dynamic flow patterns

Mixed KMS states are obtained as statistical superpositions of the
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}
via

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf x}}=\sum_{j}\operatorname{\bf p}_{j}\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta},

(8)

where
\operatorname{\bf p}=(\operatorname{\bf p}_{j})_{j}
is a probability distribution over neurons, representing relative quantities such as gene expression, external stimuli, or topological measures. A mixed KMS state thus represents the expected interaction profile of the network under a given distribution
\operatorname{\bf p}
, capturing how relative concentrations influence the dynamics of neural interactions.

For instance, let
C
be a subset of
k
neurons and define
\operatorname{\bf p}
as the uniform distribution on
C
(i.e.,
\operatorname{\bf p}_{j}=1/k
if
j\in C
, and
0
otherwise). Then
\langle C\rangle^{\beta}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{C}\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}
is the average outgoing interaction profile of
C
at inverse temperature
\beta
. Varying
\beta
and visualizing
\langle C\rangle^{\beta}
as weighted directed networks reveal the dynamic flow patterns emerging from
C
.

.2.4
KMS states entropy quantifies flow selectivity

Eq. (
8
) naturally defines the
entropy of the system in equilibrium
at inverse temperature
\beta
via Shannon entropy:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\bullet|\beta},\operatorname{\bf p})=\mathcal{S}(\operatorname{{\bf x}})=-\sum_{i}\operatorname{{\bf y}}_{i}^{\beta}\log\operatorname{{\bf y}}_{i}^{\beta},

(9)

where
\operatorname{{\bf y}}_{i}^{\beta}=\sum_{j}\operatorname{\bf p}_{j}\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}_{i}
. This entropy quantifies uncertainty in neuronal interactions at scale
\beta
under the distribution
\operatorname{\bf p}
. High entropy reflects ”non-selective”, diffusive interactions; low entropy indicates stronger selectivity in outgoing pathways.

In particular, the entropy of
\langle C\rangle^{\beta}
, denoted
\mathcal{S}(\langle C\rangle,\beta)
, quantifies the average interaction selectivity of the group
C
. When
C=\{j\}
, we recover the entropy of the single neuron’s profile
\mathcal{S}(\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta})=\mathcal{S}(\langle\{j\}\rangle,\beta)=-\sum_{i}\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}_{i}\log\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}_{i}
, which quantifies how selectively neuron
j
distributes its outgoing flow.

For a fixed topology, this selectivity depends only on
\beta
and
\operatorname{\bf p}
. Indeed,
\mathcal{S}(\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\bullet|\beta},\operatorname{\bf p})
is maximal if
\operatorname{{\bf y}}^{\beta}_{i}=1/N
for all
i
, yielding
\mathcal{S}(\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\bullet|\beta},\operatorname{\bf p})=\log N
. As shown in Fig.
6
a, this occurs when
\operatorname{\bf p}
is the uniform distribution and at sufficiently low temperatures (larg values of
\beta
), where the profiles
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}
approximate the unit vectors corresponding to trivial self-interactions.

.2.5
Phase transitions and Symmetry breaking

For large
\beta
, the system has different states generated by
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}
. In contrast,
(\mathcal{O},\alpha)
has no KMS states at
\beta<\beta_{c}
, and its KMS state at
\beta_{c}
is obtained as limit of pure states as
\beta\to\beta_{c}

[
17
]
. Specifically, the profiles
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}
converge to a common state as
\beta
approaches
\beta_{c}
where disorder is predominant, and differentiate as temperature decreases to allow for more order. This behaviour reflects a symmetry breaking in path-structured flow: at high temperature near
\sim T_{c}=1/\beta_{c}
, pure KMS states yield symmetric flow networks, while low temperatures lead to increasingly asymmetric interaction profiles. To quantify this asymmetry, we consider the
transition probability
introduced by Uhlmann
[
18
]
and later used by Jozsa
[
19
]
to define ’fidelity’ between quantum states. In our context, the transition between two profiles
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}
and
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j^{\prime}|\beta}
is given by:

\displaystyle P(j,j^{\prime},\beta)=\left(\sum_{i}\left(\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}_{i}\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j^{\prime}|\beta}_{i}\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2},

(10)

This quantity
P(j,j^{\prime},\beta)\in[0,1]
measures the similarity between flow patterns driven by neurons
j
or
j^{\prime}
. A higher value indicates that the corresponding flow networks are nearly indistinguishable, while lower value signals significant structural differences. Statistically,
P(j,j^{\prime},\beta)
is the probability of correctly identifying the system’s state at inverse temperature
\beta
, given that it is in one of the pure states
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}
or
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j^{\prime}|\beta}
. In physical terms, it quantifies the symmetry between flow profiles of two neurons.

.4
Conclusion and discussion

Over the past three decades, graph algebras have been extensively developed in operator algebras and non-commutative geometry
[
27
,
28
]
. However, their potential in interdisciplinary fields such as complex networks and neuroscience has remained largely unexplored due to their abstract nature. In this work, we showed that KMS states on graph C*-algebras provide a rigorous framework for modeling path-structured flow and analyzing their dynamics. Using empirical data from
C. elegans
, we demonstrated how KMS states generate probability distributions that capture neuronal interactions and their modulation by concentration parameters. This approach is novel in bridging algebraic methods from quantum statistical physics
[
8
,
11
]
, graph C*-algebras
[
9
]
, and network (neuro)science
[

, while also introducing a natural entropy measure for quantifying selectivity in flow and linking offering structure to function.

Notably, the centrality of the locomotion circuitry in Fig.

Appendix A
Algebraic Quantum Mechanics

We present here an overview about Algebraic Quantum Mechanics (AQM) and the Toeplitz-Cuntz-Krieger (TCK) algebra of directed networks
[
11
,
34
]
and provide details about our KMS states formalism for quantifying interaction properties in directed networks.

A.1
Basics of C*-algebras

A
C^{*}
–
algebra
is Banach algebra
\mathcal{O}
over
\mathbb{C}
equipped with an involution

\mathcal{O}\longrightarrow\mathcal{O},\ A\mapsto A^{*},

such that

(i)

\|UV\|\leq\|U\|\|V\|
for all
U,V\in\mathcal{O}
, and

(ii)

\|U^{*}U\|=\|U\|^{2}
for all
U\in\mathcal{O}
.

We say
\mathcal{O}
is
unital
if it has a unit element
1
. An element
U\in\mathcal{O}
is
self-adjoint
if
U^{*}=U
. And
U
is said to be
positive
if
U=V^{*}V
for some
V\in\mathcal{O}
. In such a case, one writes
U\geq 0
. Moreover, we write
U\geq V
if
U-V
is positive in
\mathcal{O}
.

For instance the matrix algebra
\mathcal{O}=M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is a unitial
C^{*}
-algebra where the involution is given by the matrix transpose
A^{*}=A^{\top}
, for
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, and
1
is the identity matrix. More generally, the algebra
B(\mathcal{H})
of bounded linear operators on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is a
C^{*}
–algebra with respect to the operator norm
\|\cdot\|_{op}
given by

\|T\|_{op}=\sup\{\|T\xi\|:\xi\in\mathcal{H},\ \|\xi\|\leq 1\}.

An operator
T\in B(\mathcal{H})
is positive if and only if
\langle{T\xi},{\xi}\rangle\geq 0
for all
\xi\in\mathcal{H}
.

A.1.1
States

A linear functional
\omega:\mathcal{O}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
is said to be
positive
if its values on positive elements are all positive; that is,
\omega(U^{*}U)\geq 0
for all
U\in\mathcal{O}
. A
state
on a
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{O}
is a positive linear functional
\omega
such that
\|\omega\|=\sup_{U}|\omega(U)|=1
. A
trace-state
on
\mathcal{O}
is a state
\omega
satisfying
\omega(UV)=\omega(VU)
.

For example, the usual trace of a matrix is a trace-state on
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, showing that the notion of state is, in fact, a generalization of the trace functional.

A.1.2
C*-dynamical systems

The mathematical formalism of quantum statistical mechanics in the realm of
C^{*}
-algebras
[
11
,
8
,
36
]
and noncommutative geometry
[
37
]
can be summarized as follows. A quantum system is represented by a pair
(\mathcal{O},\alpha)
–called a
C^{*}
–
dynamical system
– consisting of a
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{O}
and the time evolution
(\alpha_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
of the system, which is a one-parameter group of
*
-automorphisms of
\mathcal{O}
; that is,
\alpha
is a continuous map from
\mathbb{R}
to the automorphism group of
\mathcal{O}
that respects the C*-algebra structure
[
38
]
. Self-adjoint elements of
\mathcal{O}
usually referred to as the
observables
of the system.

In the finite dimensional case where
\mathcal{O}
is a matrix algebra
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, the one-parameter group of
*
-automorphisms in a
C^{*}
–dynamical system
(M_{n}(\mathbb{C}),\alpha)
is an action of the form

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}(A)=e^{itH}Ae^{-itH},\ t\in\mathbb{R},A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C}),

(11)

where
H\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
is a self-adjoint matrix, and we recover the usual Hamiltonian of a quantum system
[
13
]
.

Now given a state
\omega
on
\mathcal{O}
, one might think of the element
\omega(U)
as the expectation value of the observable
U
when the system is in state
\omega
. By analogy, the physical interpretation of
\omega(\alpha_{t}(\cdot))
is that the system is in state
\omega\circ\alpha_{t}
at time
t
if it was in state
\omega
at time
0
.

A.1.3
KMS states

The thermal equilibrium states of the system at inverse temperature
\beta=\frac{1}{T}
are mathematically characterized by the
Kubo-Martin-Schwinger (KMS)
conditions
[
10
,
39
]
. Specifically, a state
\omega
on
\mathcal{O}
is a
KMS state
at inverse temperature
\beta
on
(\mathcal{O},\alpha)
, or a
\beta
–
KMS state
in short, iff for all
U,V\in\mathcal{O}
, there is an analytic function
F_{U,V}(z)
, bounded and continuous on the strip
0<Im(z)<\beta
, such that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} F_{U,V}(t)&=\omega(U\alpha_{t}(V)),\ \forall t\in\mathbb{R}\\
F_{U,V}(t+i\beta)&=\omega(\alpha_{t}(V)U),\ \forall t\in\mathbb{R}.\end{aligned}

(12)

These conditions are equivalent to
\omega
satisfying

\displaystyle\omega(U\alpha_{i\beta}(V))=\omega(VU),

(13)

for all analytic elements
U,V
in
(\mathcal{O},\alpha)
, where an element
U\in\mathcal{O}
is said to be
analytic
in the
C^{*}
-dynamical system if the function
t\mapsto\alpha_{t}(U)
extends to an analytic function

\mathbb{C}\longrightarrow\mathcal{O},\ t+i\beta\mapsto\alpha_{t+i\beta}(U).

It is immediate that a
\beta
-KMS state
\omega
is necessarily time-invariant with respect to the dynamics
\alpha
; that is,
\omega(\alpha_{t}(U))=\omega(U)
for all
U\in\mathcal{O}
.
In particular,
0
-KMS states are
trace-states
(see Supplementary Note 1) that are time-invariant, and they represent the equilibrium states of the system at infinite temperature. And at the other extreme,
\infty
-KMS states correspond to the
ground states
of the systems
[
11
,
10
]
.

A.1.4
Relation to Gibbs states

In the finite-dimensional case of a matrix algebra where the dynamics is defined by a Hamiltonian
H
, for any non-negative inverse temperature
\beta
, the
C^{*}
-dynamical system
(M_{n}(\mathbb{C}),\alpha)
has a unique
\beta
-KMS equilibrium state given by the Gibbs state
[
13
]

\displaystyle\omega_{\beta}(A)=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(e^{-\beta H}A)}{\operatorname{Tr}(e^{-\beta H})},

(14)

for
A\in M_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

A.2
Directed networks as algebraic quantum systems

A.2.1
Directed networks

A directed complex network is represented by a directed graph
G
consisting of a finite set of nodes
V=\{i,j,...\}
, and a finite set of directed edges
E=\{a,b,...\}
between pair of nodes. If
a:i\leftarrow j
is an edge from
j
to
i
, we write
\operatorname{\tt pre}(a)=j
and
\operatorname{\tt post}(a)=i
. We allow
G
to be a ’
multigraph
’; that is, there might be more than one edge from a node to another (parallel edges), and it is possible to have edges from one node onto itself (self-loops).
Figure

2
shows a toy example of such graphs. A
walk
of length
n
is a sequence
\gamma=a_{n}\cdots a_{1}
of edges
a_{1},...,a_{n}
such that
\operatorname{\tt pre}(a_{l+1})=\operatorname{\tt post}(a_{l})
for
l=1,...,n-1
. For a walk
\gamma
, we write
|\gamma|=n
,
\operatorname{\tt pre}(\gamma)=\operatorname{\tt pre}(a_{1})
, and
\operatorname{\tt post}(\gamma)=\operatorname{\tt post}(a_{n})
. We let
N=\#V
be the number of nodes of
G
, and define the
adjacency matrix

A\in M_{N}(\mathbb{N})
of
G
by

\displaystyle A_{ij}:=\#\{a:\operatorname{\tt pre}(a)=j,\ \operatorname{\tt post}(a)=i\}.

(15)

Observe that for a non-negative integer
n
,
A_{ij}^{n}
is the number of directed walks of length
n
from
j
to
i
.

A.2.2
The TCK algebra

An efficient and elegant way to represent and study the combinatorial and topological properties of a directed network is to view its nodes and edges as
projections
and
partial isometries
on an infinite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
to generate a C*-algebra encoding all possible routes within the graph.

We first recall that a
projection
on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is a self-adjoint bounded linear operator
Q
such that
Q^{2}=Q
, and a
partial isometry
is a bounded linear operator
S
on
\mathcal{H}
such that
S^{*}S
is a projection (which is equivalent to requiring
SS^{*}
to be a projection)
[
38
]
. For instance, let
\mathcal{H}
be the Hilbert space of all complex-valued sequences
h=(\xi_{\gamma})_{\gamma}
indexed on walks of finite lengths in
G
such that
\|h\|^{2}=\sum_{\gamma}|\xi_{\gamma}|^{2}<\infty
. Note that the scalar product
\langle,\rangle
of
\mathcal{H}
is given by
\langle\xi,\xi^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{\gamma}\xi_{\gamma}\bar{\xi^{\prime}}_{\gamma}
for
h=(\xi_{\gamma})
and
h^{\prime}=(\xi_{\gamma}^{\prime})
. Now, for each node
i
in
G
, define the map
Q_{i}:\mathcal{H}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}
such that for
h=(\xi_{\gamma})_{\gamma}\in\mathcal{H}
,

\displaystyle Q_{i}(h)=(\xi_{\gamma})_{\operatorname{\tt post}(\gamma)=i}.

(16)

The intuition here is that, given a (possibly infinite) sequence of (re)scaled walks,
Q_{i}
is the operation that consists of selecting only the scaled walks that end at node
i
. Namely,
Q_{i}
is the projection of
\mathcal{H}
onto the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{i}
of all sequences indexed over all walks ending at node
i
. Indeed, it is immediate that
Q_{i}^{2}=Q_{i}
and that

\langle Q_{i}h,h^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{\operatorname{\tt post}(\gamma)=i}\xi_{\gamma}\bar{\xi}_{\gamma}=\langle h,Q_{i}^{*}h^{\prime}\rangle,

hence
Q_{i}^{*}=Q_{i}
. Observe that these projections are mutually orthogonal; that is,
Q_{i}Q_{j}=0
for
i\neq j
.

On the other hand, for each edge
a:i\leftarrow j
in
G
, let
S_{a}
be the bounded linear operator defined on
\mathcal{H}
by

\displaystyle S_{a}(h)=S_{a}\left((\xi_{\gamma})_{\gamma}\right)=\left(\xi_{a\gamma}\right)_{\operatorname{\tt post}(\gamma)=j},

(17)

for a sequence
h=(\xi_{\gamma})_{\gamma}\in\mathcal{H}
. That is,
S_{a}
is the operation that first select all scaled walks ending at
j
and then extend them to walks ending at
i
scaled at the same factors. Using the inner product of
\mathcal{H}
, we then get

\displaystyle\langle S_{a}(h),h^{\prime}\rangle=

\displaystyle\langle(\xi_{a\gamma})_{\operatorname{\tt post}(\gamma)=j},(\xi^{\prime}_{\gamma^{\prime}})_{\gamma^{\prime}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\operatorname{\tt post}(\gamma)=j}\xi_{a\gamma}\bar{\xi}^{\prime}_{a\gamma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle h,S_{a}^{*}(h^{\prime})\rangle,

therefore, the adjoint operator
S_{a}^{*}
is given by

S_{a}^{*}(h^{\prime})=\left(\xi^{\prime}_{\gamma}\right)_{\operatorname{\tt post}(\gamma)=i,\gamma=a\gamma^{\prime}}.

This means that
S_{a}^{*}
is the operation that select all walks that end at
i
passing through
a
and ’reduce’ them into walks ending at
j
. Now one can check that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} S_{a}^{*}S_{a}&=Q_{j},\ {\rm for\ all\ }a:i\leftarrow j,\\
\sum_{\operatorname{\tt post}(a)=i}S_{a}S_{a}^{*}&\leq Q_{i},\ {\rm for\ all\ node\ }i.\end{aligned}

(18)

In particular, the operators
S_{a}
are partial isometries. The relations (
18
) are referred to as the
TCK conditions
in the literature
[
40
,
9
]
. The
TCK algebra
(or the graph algebra) of
G
is the
universal
C*-algebra
\mathcal{O}_{G}
generated by the family
\{Q_{i},S_{a}\}
and the TCK conditions. Wherever the graph
G
is understood, we will drop the subscript and just write
\mathcal{O}
. The universal property here means the construction of
{\mathcal{O}}_{G}
does not depend on the specific choice of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
neither the particular definitions of the projections
Q_{i}
and partial isometries
S_{a}
. More precisely, any family
\{Q_{i},S_{a}\}
of mutually orthogonal projections
Q_{i}
and partial isometries
S_{a}
satisfying the TCK conditions generate a C*-algebra that is isomorphic to
{\mathcal{O}}_{G}

[
40
]
.

Note that, by definition,
{\mathcal{O}}_{G}
is the completion of the space spanned by projections of the form
S_{\gamma}S_{\theta}^{*}
such that
\operatorname{\tt pre}(\gamma)=\operatorname{\tt pre}(\theta)
, where for a walk
\gamma=a_{n}\cdots a_{1}
, we set
S_{\gamma}:=S_{a_{n}}\cdots S_{a_{1}}
. Moreover, denoting
Q=\sum_{i}Q_{i}
, we have for
S_{\gamma}S_{\theta}^{*}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{G}
and
h=(\xi_{\mu})_{\mu}\in\mathcal{H}
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} Q(S_{\gamma}S_{\theta}^{*})(h)=&Q\left((\xi_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}})_{\gamma^{\prime\prime}=\theta\gamma^{\prime},\operatorname{\tt post}(\gamma^{\prime})=\operatorname{\tt pre}(\gamma)}\right)\\
=&Q_{i}\left((\xi_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}})_{\gamma^{\prime\prime}=\theta\gamma^{\prime},\operatorname{\tt post}(\gamma^{\prime})=\operatorname{\tt pre}(\gamma),i=\operatorname{\tt post}(\gamma)}\right)\\
=&\left((\xi_{\gamma\gamma^{\prime}})_{\gamma^{\prime\prime}=\theta\gamma^{\prime},\operatorname{\tt post}(\gamma^{\prime})=\operatorname{\tt pre}(\gamma)}\right)\\
=&S_{\gamma}S_{\theta}^{*}(h).\end{aligned}

(19)

With similar calculations, one can show that
(S_{\gamma}S_{\theta}^{*})Q(h)=(S_{\gamma}S_{\theta}^{*})(h)
, which implies that
{\mathcal{O}}_{G}
is a unital C*-algebra with unit element
Q
.

A.2.3
The
C^{*}
–dynamics

There is a natural one-parameter group of *–automorphisms
(\alpha_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
on
{\mathcal{O}}_{G}
given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \alpha_{t}(Q_{i})&=Q_{i}\ i\in V,\\
\alpha_{t}(S_{a})&=e^{it}S_{a},\ a\in E,\end{aligned}

(20)

thus defining an algebraic quantum system represented by the C*–dynamical system
({\mathcal{O}}_{G},\alpha)
.

A.2.4
KMS states on the TCK algebra

From the KMS condition (
13
), a state
\omega
of
{\mathcal{O}}_{G}
is KMS at inverse temperature
\beta>0
iff:

\displaystyle\omega(S_{\gamma}S_{\theta}^{*})=\delta_{\gamma,\theta}e^{-\beta|\gamma|}\omega(Q_{\operatorname{\tt pre}(\gamma)}),

(21)

for all walks
\gamma
and
\theta
with
\operatorname{\tt pre}(\gamma)=\operatorname{\tt pre}(\theta)
. In particular, for an edge
a:i\leftarrow j
, we get

\displaystyle\omega(S_{a}S_{a}^{*})=e^{\beta}\omega(Q_{j}).

(22)

It follows from
18
and
22
that if
\omega
is a
\beta
-KMS state on
{\mathcal{O}}_{G}
, then for all node
i
, we have
\sum_{\operatorname{\tt post}(a)=i}e^{-\beta}\omega(Q_{\operatorname{\tt pre}(a)})\leq\omega(Q_{i})
; in other words,

\displaystyle\sum_{\operatorname{\tt post}(a)=i}\sigma(Q_{\operatorname{\tt pre}(a)})\leq

\displaystyle e^{\beta}\omega(Q_{i}).

(23)

Moreover, by observing that

\displaystyle\sum_{\operatorname{\tt post}(a)=i}\omega(Q_{\operatorname{\tt pre}(a)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\sum_{a:i\leftarrow j}\omega(Q_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}A_{ij}\omega(Q_{j}),

and defining the vector
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
by the non-negative numbers
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}_{i}:=\omega(Q_{i})
, the inequality (
23
) yields

\displaystyle A\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}\leq e^{\beta}\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega},

(24)

for a
\beta
-KMS state
\omega
, where the inequality is coordinate-wise. One might then think of
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\sigma}
as a ’
sub-eigenvector
’ of
A
for
e^{\beta}
.

Now since the unit element of
{\mathcal{O}}_{G}
is given by
\sum_{i}Q_{i}
, we obtain
\omega(\sum_{i}Q_{i})=1
. Hence,

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}\|_{1}:=\sum_{i}\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}_{i}=1,

(25)

which implies that
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}
is a probability distribution on the nodes set
V
.

A.2.5
The partition vector

Le
b_{c}=\log r
, where
r
be the spectral radius of
A
; i.e.

r=\max\{|\lambda|,\ \lambda\ {\rm eigeinvalue\ of\ }A\}.

As a consequence of Equation (
24
), if
\beta>\beta_{c}
and
\omega
is a
\beta
-KMS state, then
e^{\beta}
is not an eigenvalue of
A
and
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}
is not an eigenvector of
A
. Hence, the inequality becomes strict. We call
\beta_{c}
the
critical inverse temperature
of
G
. It follows that for
\beta>\beta_{c}
the vector

\displaystyle\operatorname{\bf\Psi}^{\omega}:=(1-e^{-\beta}A)\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}

(26)

is strictly positive, and

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}=(1-e^{-\beta}A)^{-1}\operatorname{\bf\Psi}^{\omega}

(27)

Moreover, since
e^{-\beta}r<1
, it follows from the theory of functional analysis (see for instance
[
41
, Chap. 1 & 5]
that

\displaystyle(1-e^{-\beta}A)^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-\beta n}A^{n}.

(28)

Hence,
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}=\sum_{n}e^{-\beta n}A^{n}\operatorname{\bf\Psi}^{\omega}
. Now consider the coordinate-wise non-negative vector
Z^{\beta}
indexed over the nodes set
V
and given by

Z^{\beta}_{j}=\sum_{\operatorname{\tt pre}(\gamma)=j}e^{-\beta|\gamma|}.

To see that these quantities are well defined, observe that the right-hand side can be written as

\displaystyle\sum_{\operatorname{\tt pre}(\gamma)=j}e^{-\beta|\gamma|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b}\sum_{|\gamma|=n,\operatorname{\tt pre}(\gamma)=j}e^{-\beta n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\sum_{i}e^{-\beta n}A^{n}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\sum_{n}e^{-\beta n}A_{ij}^{b},

and, thanks to (
28
), this infinite series converges to

\sum_{i}(1-e^{-\beta}A)^{-1}_{ij}

when
\beta>\beta_{c}
. Therefore the
partition vector

Z^{\beta}
can be expressed in terms of the adjacency matrix by

\displaystyle Z^{\beta}_{j}=\sum_{i}(1-e^{-\beta}A)^{-1}_{ij}.

(29)

The number
Z^{\beta}_{j}
represents the total volume of all flow pathways coming from node
i
at inverse temperature
\beta
, given that the functioning of each link in the network is affected by a factor of
e^{-\beta}
.

A.2.6
From the TCK algebra to the underlying network

Notice that
\langle\operatorname{\bf\Psi}^{\omega},Z^{\beta}\rangle=\|\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}\|_{1}=1
. Indeed, using (
28
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\operatorname{{\bf x}}_{i}^{\omega}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\sum_{n}\sum_{j}e^{-\beta n}A^{n}_{ij}\operatorname{\bf\Psi}_{j}^{\omega}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\operatorname{\bf\Psi}_{j}^{\omega}\left[\sum_{i}\sum_{n}e^{-\beta n}A^{n}_{ij}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\operatorname{\bf\Psi}_{j}^{\omega}\left[\sum_{\operatorname{\tt pre}(\gamma)=j}\sum_{n}e^{-\beta|\gamma|}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\operatorname{\bf\Psi}_{i}^{\omega}Z^{\beta}_{i}

\displaystyle=\langle\operatorname{\bf\Psi}^{\omega},Z^{\beta}\rangle

\displaystyle=1.

This proves that the distribution
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\omega}
associated with the
\beta
–KMS state
\omega
is completely determined, through (
27
), by the vector
\operatorname{\bf\Psi}^{\omega}
that is a solution of the following equation.

\displaystyle\langle\operatorname{\bf\Psi},Z^{\beta}\rangle=1.

(30)

Conversely, it was proved by an Haef
et al.

[
17
]
that for any non-negative vector
\operatorname{\bf\Psi}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
satisfying (
30
), with
\beta>\beta_{c}
, there is a
\beta
-KMS state
\omega=\omega_{\operatorname{\bf\Psi}}
given by

\omega(S_{\gamma}S_{\theta}^{*})=\delta_{\gamma,\theta}e^{-\beta|\gamma|}\operatorname{{\bf x}}_{\operatorname{\tt pre}(\gamma)},

where
\operatorname{{\bf x}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{N}
is given by
\operatorname{{\bf x}}=(1-e^{-\beta}A)^{-1}\operatorname{\bf\Psi}
as in formula (
27
). Furthermore, this process defines a one-to-one correspondence between the set of such vectors
\operatorname{\bf\Psi}
and the
\beta
-KMS states on
{\mathcal{O}}_{G}
.

A.2.7
KMS states matrices

For a fixed node
j
and
\beta>\beta_{c}
, define the vector
\operatorname{\bf\Psi}^{j|\beta}\in[0,\infty)^{N}
as

\displaystyle\operatorname{\bf\Psi}^{j|\beta}=\left(\frac{1}{Z^{\beta}_{j}}\delta_{i,j}\right)_{i\in V}.

(31)

It is obvious that these vectors are solutions to Eq. (
30
). Hence, we get for each fixed node
j
, a
\beta
-KMS state
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}
given by

\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}=\left((1-e^{-\beta}A)^{-1}\operatorname{\bf\Psi}^{j|\beta}\right)_{i}=\frac{1}{Z^{\beta}_{j}}\sum_{n}e^{-\beta n}A^{n}_{ij}.

Or in a more compact form,

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}=\frac{1}{Z^{\beta}_{j}}(1-e^{-\beta}A)^{-1}_{ij}.

(32)

It can be seen that the space
\Omega_{\beta}
of all
\beta
-KMS states on
G
is the simplex of dimension
N-1
generated by the vectors
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}
; that is,

\displaystyle\Omega_{\beta}=\left\{\sum_{j}\operatorname{\bf p}_{j}\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}|0\leq\operatorname{\bf p}_{j}\leq 1,\ \sum_{j}\operatorname{\bf p}_{j}=1\right\}.

(33)

Thus the vectors
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}
are the
pure (
\beta
–KMS) states
of the system, while their convex combinations are
mixed states

[
11
]
. In a sense, the states
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}
are the analog of eigenvectors and the simplex
\Omega_{\beta}
plays the role of eigenspace. It follows that every
\beta
-KMS state
\operatorname{{\bf x}}
can be written as a matrix product

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\bf x}}=[\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\bullet|\beta}]\operatorname{\bf p},

(34)

where
\operatorname{\bf p}=(\operatorname{\bf p}_{j})_{j}
is a probability distribution on the nodes and
[\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\bullet|\beta}]
is the column stochastic
N\times N
-matrix with non-negative entries whose columns are the the pure KMS states
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\bullet|\beta}
; that is,

[\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\bullet|\beta}]_{ij}:=\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}_{i}.

Appendix B
KMS matrices as non-Markovian processes

It is straightforward to check that, for a fixed
\beta>\beta_{c}
, the KMS matrix
[\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{\bullet|\beta}]
, formed by the pure states
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}
, naturally corresponds to a generalized stochastic process that can be represented as a
non-Markovian random walk with memory decay

\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
, where transitions are influenced by weighted sums over the entire history of walks, but with exponentially fading influence of older steps where the memory decay is governed by
\beta
(unlike a standard one-step Markov chain, where transitions depend only on the current node). This process is defined as follows:

•

a walker starts at node
j\in V
;

•

at each time step, the walker proceeds with probability
e^{-\beta}
, or stops with probability
1-e^{-\beta}
;

•

The probability of reaching node
i
after
n
steps is therefore weighted by
(1-e^{-\beta})e^{-n\beta}
and is the same as the probability of reaching
i
through an arbitrary path of length
n
; more precisely, we get

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} P(X_{n}=i|X_{0}=j)=&\frac{(1-e^{-\beta})e^{-n\beta}A_{ij}^{n}}{\sum_{k\in V}\sum_{m\geq 0}(1-e^{-\beta})e^{-m\beta}A^{m}_{kj}}\\
=&\frac{e^{-n\beta}A_{ij}^{n}}{\sum_{k\in V}\sum_{m\geq 0}e^{-m\beta}A^{m}_{kj}}.\end{aligned}

(35)

It follows that the total probability that a walker starting at
j

eventually
reaches
i
is

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{n\geq 0}e^{-n\beta}A_{ij}^{n}}{\sum_{k\in V}\sum_{n\geq 0}e^{-n\beta}A^{n}_{kj}}=\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}_{i}.

(36)

Therefore, under this model, the pure KMS state
\operatorname{{\bf x}}^{j|\beta}
is the
stationary distribution
over end-nodes starting from node
j
, considering all possible walk lengths with exponentially decreasing weight. In other words, these distributions measure the long-term behavior of the dynamics, capturing how information, influence, or flow propagates and accumulates in the directed network over time when paths of all lengths, redundancies and feedback are taken into account with an exponentially decaying memory.

Moreover, this perspective gives a better physical intuition of the fact that high temperatures (
\beta
small) favor longer paths, since the walker is more likely to continue at each step; while low temperatures (large
\beta
) will
localize
the dynamics by making the walker more likely to stop after one or a few steps (shorter paths).

By interpreting the KMS states this way, we show that our model does capture a kind of structurally embedded propagation, even though it is not time-dependent in the traditional dynamical systems sense. The “flow” arises as an emergent pattern over path ensembles, not from explicit dynamics over time.
