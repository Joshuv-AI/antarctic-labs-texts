Lateral decubitus positioning was associated with improved efficiency and functional outcome compared with traction table positioning for cephalomedullary nailing of subtrochanteric femur fractures: a retrospective comparative study
Authors: Hamit Çağlayan Kahraman, Emre Bilgin, Onur Gültekin, Öykü Merve Yavuz, Olcay Yavuz, Barış Yılmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSubtrochanteric femur fractures are challenging to treat due to complex anatomy and high mechanical stresses. Cephalomedullary nailing is the standard treatment, often performed with the patient supine on a traction table, but this can prolong setup and cause complications (pressure or nerve injuries). Lateral decubitus positioning without a traction table may facilitate reduction and reduce traction-related risks, yet comparative data in subtrochanteric fractures are limited. This study compared outcomes of lateral decubitus versus traction table positioning for subtrochanteric fracture nailing.MethodsA retrospective cohort study included 68 patients with subtrochanteric femur fractures treated with cephalomedullary nails. Patients underwent surgery supine on a traction table (n = 37) or in lateral decubitus without a traction table (n = 31). Operative metrics (entry point determination time, operative time, fluoroscopy duration, and blood loss) and postoperative outcomes (transfusion requirements, time to mobilization, complications, and final Harris Hip Score) were compared between groups.ResultsLateral decubitus positioning was associated with shorter entry-point determination time (median 2 vs. 7 min; p = 0.05), shorter operating-room time (80.3 ± 23.6 vs. 108.2 ± 23.2 min; p = 0.03), reduced fluoroscopy time (151.2 ± 19.2 vs. 178.2 ± 16.1 s; p = 0.03), and lower estimated blood loss (752 ± 346 vs. 1459 ± 611 mL; p = 0.01) compared with traction-table positioning. No statistically significant differences were observed in transfusion requirements, time to mobilization, final Harris Hip Score, or complication rates (all p > 0.05).ConclusionCompared with traction-table positioning, lateral decubitus cephalomedullary nailing was associated with better functional outcome, shorter operative and fluoroscopy times, and lower estimated blood loss, while union and complication rates were similar between groups. These findings suggest that lateral decubitus positioning is a viable alternative for subtrochanteric fracture fixation, particularly when traction-table setup may compromise operating-room efficiency. Given the observational design and potential confounding related to reduction techniques and surgeon factors, these findings should be interpreted as associations; prospective studies are warranted.

Introduction

Subtrochanteric femur fractures are defined as those occurring from about 5 cm distal to the lesser trochanter to 5 cm proximal to the femoral isthmus [1]. These injuries can happen when someone falls from a height or has a high-energy accident (car collision). They are difficult to treat because the area has complex anatomy, high mechanical stresses, and significant muscle forces working across the fracture [2]. Anatomic reduction and stable internal fixation are essential for optimum outcomes in this fracture pattern [3]. Long intramedullary nailing is currently regarded as the gold-standard therapy for the majority of subtrochanteric fractures due to its biomechanical benefits and minimally invasive characteristics in comparison to plate fixation [4].

Patient position during intramedullary nailing affects reduction quality, surgical time, fluoroscopy exposure, and complications [2]. Traditionally, patients remain supine on a traction table for continuous longitudinal traction and rotational control to reduce fractures and maintain alignment during nail insertion. Traction tables help reduce and allow single-surgeon surgery, but they have downsides. Time-consuming traction table setup can cause perineal nerve compression, soft-tissue pressure injuries, and fracture alignment issues [5]. Furthermore, the traction apparatus adds cost and bulk to the operating room setup [6]. Given these considerations, there has been increasing interest in alternative positioning methods that do not require the use of a traction table.

In recent years, performing proximal femoral nailing in the lateral decubitus position (on a standard radiolucent table, using manual or assistant-applied traction) has gained popularity, particularly for intertrochanteric and pertrochanteric hip fractures [7–9]. The lateral decubitus position can offer improved surgical exposure of the proximal femur, easier manipulation for fracture reduction (especially by allowing limb adduction and flexion), and avoidance of traction-table-related complications. Several comparative studies in intertrochanteric fractures have found that lateral decubitus positioning yields similar radiographic and functional outcomes to the traditional supine/traction table position [10]. Notably, some have reported that the lateral decubitus position can shorten operative time and reduce fluoroscopy use without compromising fracture reduction [11, 12]. However, these investigations largely involved peritrochanteric and shaft fractures, and their findings may not be directly generalizable to the subtrochanteric region, which has unique biomechanical challenges. Caution is therefore warranted when extrapolating results from intertrochanteric fractures to subtrochanteric fractures.

There is currently a gap in the literature regarding the optimal patient position specifically for subtrochanteric femur fractures [13]. Only a few reports directly compare lateral decubitus positioning versus use of the traction table in this context [4, 14, 15]. Published descriptions of techniques for intramedullary nailing in the lateral decubitus position for subtrochanteric fractures are also limited to small series or technical notes [15]. Given the paucity of data, surgeons lack clear evidence-based guidance on whether one positioning method confers advantages in subtrochanteric fracture fixation.

This study aimed to compare the lateral decubitus position without a traction table with the standard supine position on a traction table for cephalomedullary nailing of subtrochanteric femur fractures. The study evaluated differences in patient and injury characteristics, along with key intraoperative and postoperative outcomes, between the two positioning methods. The primary outcomes of the study were operative metrics, particularly total operative time, fluoroscopy exposure, and the time required to establish the nail entry point. Secondary outcomes included transfusion requirements, postoperative analgesia needs, time to mobilization, length of hospital stay, complication rates, radiographic healing time, and functional scores. It was hypothesized that intramedullary nailing performed in the lateral decubitus position would enhance intraoperative efficiency—reflected by shorter operative and fluoroscopy times and faster reduction—while achieving clinical outcomes comparable to traction table positioning, including similar union rates and functional recovery.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted as a single-center retrospective comparative cohort study at the Istanbul Fatih Sultan Mehmet Training and Research Hospital, Department of Orthopaedic Traumatology. Ethical approval for this retrospective study was obtained from the Fatih Sultan Mehmet Education and Research Hospital Ethics Committee (FSMEAH-KAEK 2022/117; approval date: 12/01/2023). Written informed consent was obtained from the patients. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The study included consecutive adult patients with subtrochanteric femur fractures treated with long cephalomedullary nails (CMN) between 2021 and 2024. Patients were identified from the institutional trauma registry and electronic medical records and then divided into two groups based on intraoperative positioning: (1) supine position on a traction table (Fig. 1) and (2) lateral decubitus position on a standard radiolucent table (Fig. 2).

Internal fixation protocol

All patients received standard preoperative care in accordance with the institution’s protocols, including appropriate resuscitation, intravenous antibiotic prophylaxis, and thromboprophylaxis. In the traction table group, patients were placed in the supine position on a traction table with a perineal post. After induction of anesthesia, the affected limb was secured in a foot/boot attachment, and traction with internal rotation was applied to achieve a preliminary reduction before draping and skin incision. In the lateral decubitus group, patients were positioned in the lateral decubitus position on a standard radiolucent operating table. Pressure points were padded, and the patient was stabilized with supports. Fracture reduction was achieved by the surgical assistant using manual traction and additional manipulation as required, including flexion, adduction, and internal or external rotation of the hip. In both groups, fixation was performed using a contemporary-design long cephalomedullary nail (Aries ® Orthopedics, PFNA [INT-Type]- Proximal Femoral Nail Locking Nails Systems) inserted through a standard trochanteric entry point. All procedures were performed by surgeons from the same institution who had been trained within the same surgical framework each of them is experienced in both techniques, rather than by a single surgeon. According to the standardized surgical protocol, all patients were treated with an Aries long proximal femoral nail with two distal screws, one of them locking. Proximal fixation was achieved with one PFNA Antirotation subtrochanteric lag screw and one PFNA recon compression locking screw. Intraoperative reduction quality was evaluated based on predefined radiographic targets, with the aim of achieving a tip–apex distance of < 25 mm and a collodiaphyseal angle between 125° and 140° in each patient. Reaming was performed only when nail advancement could not be achieved, and routine reaming was not undertaken. Adjunctive reduction techniques were applied when necessary (Fig. 3). All patients received 2 g cefazolin sodium as preoperative antibiotic prophylaxis. Postoperative thromboprophylaxis consisted of enoxaparin sodium 0.4 mL administered once daily for 30 days.

For consistency, certain time intervals and technical terms were defined. In particular, “entry-point determination time” was defined as the interval from patient positioning after completion of anesthesia and confirmation of surgical readiness by the anesthesia team and skin incision to the successful placement of the guidewire at the correct trochanteric entry point, as confirmed fluoroscopically, during nail insertion. This time was calculated from operating room logs and fluoroscopy device timestamps. This metric was used to quantify the time required for the critical initial steps of the procedure (positioning the C-arm, locating the entry site, and achieving the entry portal). All time measurements were manually extracted retrospectively from operating room logs and fluoroscopy timestamps by the study investigators. This parameter was included as a measure of procedural setup and technical efficiency rather than as a clinical outcome. All procedures were performed or supervised by attending orthopaedic trauma surgeons experienced in both techniques. Wounds were closed in layers in standard fashion. Postoperatively, patients in both groups followed similar protocols for rehabilitation: mobilization and weight-bearing were allowed as tolerated based on the surgeon’s judgment of fixation stability, with all patients receiving standardized physiotherapy beginning on postoperative day 1 or 2. Thromboprophylaxis was continued in the postoperative period according to institutional guidelines.

Patient demographics and perioperative data were independently collected by two researchers using a standardized, predefined data extraction form. Demographic variables included age, sex, and side of injury. The injury mechanism was categorized as low-energy fall, motor-vehicle/traffic accident, sports-related, or other high-energy trauma. Comorbid conditions were recorded and summarized with the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). Each patient’s smoking status (never, former, or current smoker) and any history of alcohol use were recorded. Preoperative laboratory values were noted, including the neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, and platelet count from the complete blood count closest to the time of surgery (usually within 24 h prior); from these, the Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII) was calculated as (platelet count × neutrophil count) ÷ lymphocyte count (expressed in 10^9/L). Fracture morphology was classified on preoperative radiographs and computed tomography (CT) scans according to the AO/OTA classification (31A1, A2, A3 subtypes for trochanteric involvement, with 31A3 typically indicating a subtrochanteric extension).

Estimated intraoperative blood loss (EBL) was extracted from the anesthetic record. EBL represents the attending anesthesiologist’s intraoperative estimate and was used as recorded in routine care. To mitigate misinterpretation, we explicitly refer to this variable as ‘estimated’ blood loss throughout the manuscript.

Primary endpoints were defined as outcomes most directly related to intraoperative efficiency, including total operative time, entry-point determination time, and fluoroscopy time. Secondary outcomes comprised the need for conversion to open reduction, intraoperative complications, postoperative complications (classified as mechanical or biological, with major complications defined as those requiring reoperation), postoperative narcotic analgesic use, time to mobilization, length of hospital stay, time to radiographic union, unplanned revision surgery, functional outcomes at final follow-up, and radiographic quality measures as defined below. Postoperative analgesia use was quantified as the total opioid consumption in the first 48 h, converted to morphine milligram equivalents (MME) for standardization. Time to first mobilization was defined as the interval (in hours or days) from arrival in the recovery room to the patient’s first assisted ambulation with a walker; for analysis, this was recorded as the postoperative day on which mobilization began (postoperative day 1, 2, or 3). Length of stay was measured both as the preoperative hospital days (admission to surgery interval) and total hospital days post-injury. Patients were followed at routine intervals (approximately 2, 6, and 12 weeks, 6 months, 12 months, and annually thereafter as feasible). Functional outcomes at final follow-up were assessed using the Harris Hip Score (HHS), as originally described by Harris [16–18]. The total follow-up duration for each patient was obtained from hospital records. Patients with insufficient follow-up to assess fracture union (lost to follow-up before 4–6 months) had already been excluded as noted above.

All immediate postoperative radiographs were reviewed to assess reduction quality and implant position in both groups using the same set of criteria. Reduction was evaluated on anteroposterior and lateral radiographs according to the criteria described by Chang et al. This Chang Reduction Quality Criteria (CRQC) grades fracture reduction as excellent, acceptable, or poor based on alignment in both planes, cortical apposition, and presence of any varus or malrotation deformity [19]. Tip–apex distance (TAD) for the cephalic screw was measured on calibrated postoperative radiographs and was defined as the sum of the distances (in millimeters) from the tip of the implant to the apex of the femoral head on anteroposterior and lateral views. The collodiaphyseal angle (CDA) (also called neck-shaft angle) was measured on the AP radiograph as the angle between the femoral neck axis and the femoral shaft axis. Fracture healing was assessed from follow-up radiographs. Radiographic union was defined as the presence of bridging callus across at least 3 of 4 cortices on orthogonal views accompanied by painless full weight-bearing. Delayed union was defined as lack of complete union by 4–6 months post-op, and nonunion as failure to unite by 9 months or the need for a secondary procedure to achieve union.

Two independent observers, blinded to group allocation, performed the radiographic measurements to ensure objectivity: one orthopaedic surgery resident and one radiology resident (referred to as R1 and R2). The observers initially recorded their measurements separately; any discrepancies in measurements or CRQC grading were then reviewed in a consensus meeting with a senior orthopaedic surgeon to arrive at a final agreed value.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (Version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data were checked for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed, or as median with interquartile range (IQR) if they show a non-normal distribution. Categorical variables are summarized as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were conducted based on data type and distribution: an independent-samples t-test was used for continuous variables meeting normality and equal variance assumptions, while the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed continuous data. Categorical variables were compared with Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test (two-tailed) when expected cell counts were small. Given the retrospective design and fixed available sample size, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed; all eligible cases in the study period were included. Inter-observer reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for continuous variables and Cohen’s kappa for categorical variables. Results were considered statistically significant at an alpha level of 0.05.

Results

A total of 68 patients met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed, with 37 patients in the traction table group and 31 patients in the lateral decubitus group. The mean age of the overall cohort was 66.3 ± 20.8 years, and 53% of patients were female. A significant proportion had medical comorbidities; the median CCI was 2 (mean 1.6). The majority of injuries in both groups resulted from low-energy falls, though about one-third were due to high-energy trauma. No statistically significant between-group differences were observed in the patient demographic or fracture characteristics presented in Table 1, and the fracture patterns were similarly distributed between the groups, with approximately half of the cases in each group classified as AO/OTA 31A3.3 (Table 2).There were no statistically significant differences between the traction table and lateral decubitus groups in terms of age, sex distribution, injury mechanism (high- vs. low-energy), fracture classification, SII values, or comorbidity burden at presentation (all p > 0.05). This indicates that the two groups were well matched in patient and injury characteristics.

Key intraoperative timing parameters differed between the two positioning methods. Entry point determination time was shorter in the lateral decubitus group, with a median of 2 min (IQR, 2–3) compared with 7 min (IQR, 2–7) in the traction table group (p = 0.05) (Fig. 4). Total operative time, defined as operating room entry to exit, was also lower in the lateral decubitus group (80.3 ± 23.6 min) than in the traction table group (108.2 ± 23.2 min; p = 0.03). Similarly, fluoroscopy time was reduced in the lateral decubitus group (151.2 ± 19.2 s) compared with the traction table group (178.2 ± 16.1 s; p = 0.03). Intraoperative estimated blood loss differed between groups, with mean estimated values of 752 ± 346 mL in the lateral decubitus group and 1459 ± 611 mL in the traction table group, a difference that reached statistical significance (p = 0.01; Fig. 5).

The need for open or percutaneous reduction aids was frequent in both groups and did not differ significantly, occurring in 62.2% of patients in the traction table group and 61.3% in the lateral decubitus group (p = 0.94). The distribution of adjunctive reduction techniques did not differ significantly between the traction table and lateral decubitus groups (χ²=6.782, df = 5, p = 0.237) (Table 3). In the traction table group, the most frequently used technique was the Hook/Weber clamp (37.8%), whereas cerclage cable was more commonly used in the lateral decubitus group (32.3%). Nevertheless, no intraoperative complications, such as iatrogenic fractures or implant malposition necessitating revision, were observed in either group during the nailing procedures.

The need for blood transfusion was somewhat higher in the traction table group, but this did not reach significance: 62.2% of patients in the traction group received at least one unit of blood, compared to 45.2% in the lateral decubitus group (p = 0.25). The average number of units transfused (among those who received transfusion) was approximately 1 unit in both groups (no significant difference, p > 0.05). Postoperative analgesic requirements were likewise similar. In the traction table group, 51.4% of patients required narcotic analgesia in the first 48 h (mean dosage 0.9 ± 1.1 MME), versus 48.4% of patients (mean 0.9 ± 1.1 MME) in the lateral group; neither the rate of usage (p = 0.81) nor the average opioid dose (p = 0.77) differed significantly. Time to mobilization was equivalent: in both groups, the median time to first assisted ambulation with a walker was postoperative day 2 (with most patients mobilizing between day 1 and 4), and there was no significant difference (p = 0.18). No difference in preoperative waiting time and total length of stay was observed between the groups.

Time to radiographic union was comparable between groups, with a median of approximately 4 months in both; mean union times were 4.2 ± 2.1 months and 4.1 ± 2.4 months, respectively (p = 0.73). Final HHS was significantly higher in the lateral decubitus group than in the traction table group. The mean final HHS was 64.9 ± 18.1 in the lateral decubitus group and 55.2 ± 21.2 in the traction table group. The between-group difference in final HHS was 9.65 points (95% CI 0.16 to 19.15), indicating better functional outcome in the lateral decubitus group (Welch’s t-test, p = 0.046).

Postoperative radiographic evaluations showed no significant differences in implant alignment or positioning between the two techniques. The mean TAD was lower in the lateral decubitus group (22.4 ± 11.4 mm) than in the traction table group (26.5 ± 10.7 mm); however, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.07). The mean CDA did not differ between groups (133.9° ± 4.8° in the traction table group vs. 135.0° ± 2.6° in the lateral decubitus group; p = 0.25) (Fig. 6). Reduction quality assessed using the CRQC was similar between groups, with no statistically significant difference observed (Fig. 7).

The overall rate of major mechanical complications or revision surgery was 8.8% (6 of 68 patients). By group, mechanical complications or revision procedures occurred in 4 patients (10.8%) in the traction table group and in 2 patients (6.5%) in the lateral decubitus group. No positioning-specific complications, such as pudendal nerve neuropraxia or severe pressure sores related to the perineal post, were identified, and no iatrogenic neurovascular injuries were recorded in either group.

Interobserver reliability

The agreement between the two independent researchers on radiographic measurements was analyzed. TAD measurements showed excellent interobserver reliability. The Pearson correlation between R1 and R2 for TAD was r = 0.994 (95% CI: 0.990–0.996), and the ICC was 0.997 (95% CI: 0.995–0.998). In contrast, CDA measurements demonstrated poor interobserver agreement and weak correlation (Pearson r = 0.279 (95% CI: 0.043–0.485) and an ICC of 0.086 (95% CI: -0.063 to 0.428), likely due to sensitivity to radiographic projection and patient positioning, and should therefore be interpreted with caution. For the categorical reduction quality grades (CRQC), good interobserver agreement was observed, indicating that both observers generally concurred.

Discussion

In this comparative study of subtrochanteric femur fractures, cephalomedullary nailing in the lateral decubitus position was associated with a shorter operative time, lower fluoroscopy exposure, and reduced estimated blood loss, while maintaining comparable reduction quality, union rates, and clinical outcomes relative to traction table positioning.

Successful antegrade nailing in subtrochanteric femur fractures depends heavily on accurate selection of the starting point and trajectory, which should be individualized according to nail design, patient positioning, trochanteric anatomy, and fracture pattern [20]. Our findings are consistent with previous reports suggesting that lateral decubitus positioning facilitates entry point identification, improves operative efficiency and shorter operation time in proximal femur fracture nailing [15, 21, 22]. Extending these findings, our direct comparison with the conventional traction table method showed that lateral decubitus positioning reduced operative time by approximately 28 min on average without compromising reduction quality or clinical outcomes. This clinically meaningful difference is unlikely to be explained solely by surgeon hand speed and more likely reflects the combined effects of surgical logistics and anatomical positioning. Recent consensus recommendations favor a position-specific strategy in subtrochanteric fractures, supporting greater trochanteric entry in the supine traction-table position while reserving lateral positioning for selected cases, such as younger patients or more complex proximal fracture patterns [23]. Our findings suggest that neither method is exactly superior and that the advantages of each approach are likely context-dependent.

On a traction table, strenuous manual pressure by the surgeon medializes the proximal fragment against the muscle forces to establish a correct trochanteric entry portal. Conversely, in the lateral decubitus position, the natural vector of gravity assists in neutralizing the abduction deformity. Furthermore, unhindered adduction of the limb in the lateral position brings the greater trochanter superficially directly in line with the surgeon’s working trajectory, bypassing the obstructing gluteal mass [24]. From a technical perspective, the lateral decubitus position may facilitate reduction and nail insertion by allowing freer manipulation of the injured limb, easier access to the trochanteric entry point and practical advantages [13, 14]. It also appears to simplify conversion to adjunctive open reduction maneuvers when required, particularly in technically demanding cases or in patients with a larger body habitus [7, 21]. In our study, these practical advantages were reflected by numerically lower TAD values and comparable reduction quality in the lateral decubitus group, although these differences did not reach statistical significance. In general, these results suggest that positioning the patient on their side may help surgeons reach the technical goals of intramedullary nailing more quickly without lowering the quality of the reduction.

One of the most important differences was that the lateral decubitus group had a shorter fluoroscopy time, which probably meant less radiation exposure for both the patient and the surgical team. This finding is consistent with previous reports suggesting lower C-arm utilization with off-table techniques and may reflect easier fracture alignment and more efficient image acquisition in this position [12]. A commonly cited limitation of the lateral decubitus technique is the potential difficulty in obtaining orthogonal fluoroscopic views, particularly of the proximal femur. However, with modern imaging equipment and increasing surgical experience, this challenge appears to be manageable [14]. In our study, fluoroscopy time was not increased in the lateral decubitus group; rather, it was lower, indicating that these theoretical imaging disadvantages did not translate into impaired reduction quality or suboptimal implant positioning. Additionally, with experienced surgeons and modern imaging systems, true lateral hip views can be obtained easily in the lateral decubitus position by manually rotating the limb 15–20° internally or externally, thereby avoiding the additional time required to reposition fluoroscopy around a leg fixed on a traction table.

Because operative time was defined from operating-room entry to exit, it necessarily included setup-related steps in the traction-table group, such as transfer onto the traction table, placement of the perineal post, careful boot fixation of both limbs, and table adjustment. Recent literature has shown that traction-table use in proximal femoral nailing is associated with longer setup and overall operative times, with the setup phase alone reportedly adding approximately 15 to 25 min while providing comparable reduction-quality measures [7]. By contrast, lateral decubitus positioning on a standard radiolucent table generally requires simpler stabilization and allows more rapid progression to draping and incision, which may partly explain the shorter overall operating-room time observed with this approach.

The more constrained limb position on the traction table may hinder optimal entry-point access, while setup and entry preparation are generally more time-consuming [9]. This is consistent with our findings and with recent evidence showing longer setup times with traction-table use [25]. These considerations suggest that the routine use of a traction table may not provide a clear advantage in subtrochanteric nailing [9, 25]. In addition, the shorter operative time observed with the lateral decubitus approach may also have economic relevance, as reduced operating room time is directly associated with lower procedural costs [6]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the lateral decubitus approach may offer practical advantages from both radiation safety and resource utilization perspectives.

Although operative duration and positioning-related variability during intramedullary fixation have previously been reported to be associated with estimated blood loss, the greater blood loss observed in the traction table group in the present study should not be attributed to positioning alone [7]. Operative duration, traction time, and increased soft tissue tension may also have contributed to this difference and should be considered potential confounding factors when interpreting the blood loss findings.

Although previous studies on lateral decubitus nailing have been limited by heterogeneous cohorts and the absence of direct control groups [24], this study allows for a clearer evaluation of its procedural advantages. Prior reports have suggested that easier access to a more medial trochanteric entry point may improve implant alignment and early radiographic reduction [26], while more recent evidence indicates that traction-table assistance does not consistently improve key surgical outcomes such as blood loss, fluoroscopy time, or union rates [9]. In our study, radiological union time was comparable between the traction table and lateral decubitus groups, while the traction table group had significantly longer fluoroscopy time and greater estimated blood loss. Taken together, these findings suggest that routine traction-table use may not be necessary in all subtrochanteric fracture cases.

The traction table provides stable traction and maintenance of length and rotation without continuous manual assistance, which may be advantageous in less experienced teams or limited-staff settings [9, 27]. However, in our series, it did not reduce the need for adjunctive reduction techniques, as open/percutaneous reduction assistance was similarly frequent in both groups. The selective use of adjunctive techniques, particularly cerclage wiring, remains a potential confounder because, although cerclage may facilitate fracture reduction and enhance fixation stability [28], its use may also reflect greater fracture complexity and prolong operative time [29]. Nevertheless, although cerclage use has been associated with longer surgery and greater blood loss in complex subtrochanteric fractures [30], operative time remained shorter in the lateral decubitus group in our cohort even when cerclage was used. This may partly relate to the easier lateral and posterior femoral access provided by the lateral decubitus position during limited cerclage application. Although the overall requirement for reduction assistance was comparable between groups, the potential effect of these non-randomized intraoperative interventions cannot be entirely excluded.

Comparable clinical outcomes between the two positioning methods in our study are consistent with prior comparative literature on proximal femoral fractures. No major traction-related complications were observed in this cohort, the potential for traction-associated soft tissue and neurovascular complications remains a recognized limitation [9, 31]. Tong et al. reported no significant differences in union or functional outcomes between lateral decubitus and traction-table positioning in subtrochanteric fractures, despite noting practical advantages of the lateral position [13]. Evidence from intertrochanteric fracture studies likewise suggests that patient positioning does not substantially influence healing or final clinical outcomes, aside from longer setup times with traction-table use [22, 25]. These findings, the comparable union times, and complication rates observed in our cohort further support that lateral decubitus positioning does not compromise biological healing or postoperative recovery.

Although some reports have suggested that traction-table positioning may provide slightly better radiographic reduction parameters, particularly in terms of TAD and CDA [22], our findings did not demonstrate a significant disadvantage for the lateral decubitus position in either TAD or CAD. Acceptable implant positioning was achieved in both groups, indicating that lateral decubitus positioning can provide radiographic reduction quality comparable to that of traction-table positioning when performed by experienced surgeons.

It has been determined that fracture complexity, age, pre-fracture functional status, and comorbidities affect the ability of hip fracture patients to perform daily living activities after surgery and that there are large and heterogeneous patient groups [32, 33]. In this study at follow-up, HHS demonstrated a broad distribution of functional outcomes in both groups, but a significantly higher HHS score in lateral decubitus group, likely attributable to patient heterogeneity in age and comorbidity burden, with many elderly patients failing to achieve full functional recovery irrespective of surgical positioning.

The available evidence suggests that neither positioning method is categorically superior in terms of long-term outcomes, and that comparable stability and fracture union can be achieved with either technique when surgeons are familiar with the technical nuances of the chosen approach [25].

Limitations

This study has several limitations related to its retrospective, single-center design. First, selection bias cannot be excluded, as patient positioning may have been influenced by fracture morphology, body habitus, operating-room logistics, or surgeon preference. Second, although all procedures were performed or supervised by trauma surgeons experienced in both techniques and a standardized implant and perioperative protocol was applied, the participation of multiple surgeons may still have introduced inter-operator variability and possible learning-curve effects that could have influenced operative efficiency outcomes. Third, adjunctive reduction measures used in difficult reductions—such as cerclage wiring, blocking screws, and other percutaneous or open reduction aids—may prolong surgery and act as confounding factors; although these interventions were recorded, residual confounding remains possible. Fourth, operative time was defined as the interval from operating-room entry to exit, thereby reflecting total workflow efficiency but also incorporating anesthesia- and setup-related factors, which may limit comparability with studies reporting skin-to-skin time only. Fifth, estimated blood loss was obtained from routine anesthetic records and may therefore be imprecise, while transfusion practices may also vary according to patient characteristics and institutional thresholds. Finally, radiation exposure was evaluated using fluoroscopy time alone, since direct dosimetry data for patients and operating-room staff were not available. Additionally, entry-point determination time is operator-dependent and may reflect surgeon experience and workflow factors; we therefore present it as an exploratory efficiency metric. These issues should be considered when interpreting the observed associations, and prospective studies incorporating standardized reduction algorithms together with direct radiation dose measurements are needed.

Despite these limitations, the study also has important strengths. It provides a direct comparison of two positioning strategies within a relatively homogeneous subtrochanteric fracture cohort. Patient characteristics were comparable between the groups, and a broad set of outcomes was collected systematically, including operative parameters and functional results. In addition, this series addresses a meaningful gap in the literature, since previous studies have predominantly focused on intertrochanteric fractures or have not included a comparative control group.

Conclusion

Cephalomedullary nailing performed in the lateral decubitus position was associated with higher HHS, shorter operating-room time, reduced fluoroscopy time, and lower estimated blood loss compared with traction-table positioning, while union rates and complication profiles were comparable. Lateral decubitus positioning may represent an acceptable alternative in appropriately staffed operating rooms, particularly when traction-table setup is expected to prolong workflow. However, because this study is retrospective and susceptible to residual confounding (including surgeon-related factors and the use of adjunct reduction techniques), causal inferences should be avoided, and confirmation in prospective, ideally multicenter studies is recommended.
