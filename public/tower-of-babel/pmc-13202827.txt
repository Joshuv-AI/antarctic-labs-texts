Application of extracellular vesicles in the CRISPR-based diagnosis and treatment: possibilities and challenges
Authors: Eunhye Cho, Jehyung Lee, Jiyu Kim, Jaewon Choi, Mikyung Kang, Jayeon Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe CRISPR–Cas system has revolutionized molecular diagnostics and gene editing, yet its clinical translation is hindered by delivery barriers, off-target activity, immunogenicity, and manufacturing challenges. Compared with viral vectors and synthetic non-viral carriers such as lipid nanoparticles, extracellular vesicles (EVs) offer a biologically derived delivery platform with superior biocompatibility, reduced immunogenicity, intrinsic cargo protection, and natural barrier-crossing capability. Engineered EVs can further achieve cell- or tissue-specific targeting. In diagnostics, endogenous EV proteins and nucleic acids provide disease-informative signatures that can interface with CRISPR nuclease readouts for highly sensitive detection. This review summarizes the therapeutic and diagnostic potential of EV-CRISPR platforms, covering strategies for loading CRISPR cargos (producer-cell engineering, post-isolation methods), cargo formats, and surface targeting approaches. We evaluate preclinical performance with attention to biodistribution, safety, innate and adaptive immune responses, and genomic integrity, as well as analytical assays and scalable manufacturing considerations essential for clinical translation. Finally, we discuss emerging opportunities, including AI-guided optimization of EV-CRISPR design and integrated EV platforms that combine disease detection with therapeutic intervention, highlighting their promise for advancing precision medicine.Keywords: CRISPR technology, Extracellular vesicles, Diagnostics, Treatment, Genome editing, AI-driven CRISPR-EV platforms, Integrated platform of EV with CRISPR technology

Background

Clustered regularly interspaced palindromic repeats (CRISPR) and CRISPR-associated (Cas) protein, initially identified as a bacterial adaptive immune mechanism, have transformed modern molecular biology by enabling highly specific and programmable genome editing [1]. This system precisely targets specific DNA or RNA of interest by binding to complementary sequences by hybridization, then directs the Cas protein to identify and cut its specific nucleic acid target. Utilizing its sequence-specific endonuclease activity, CRISPR-Cas system has emerged as a powerful tool in both diagnostics and therapeutics (Fig. 1) [2]. CRISPR-based diagnostic platforms, such as SHERLOCK and DETECTR, have significantly enhanced the detection of pathogens, genetic mutations, and disease biomarkers [3, 4]. Meanwhile, CRISPR-mediated gene editing offers promising treatments for genetic disorders, cancer, and infectious diseases, further advancing precision medicine [5].

Despite these advancements, the efficient and safe delivery of CRISPR components remains a major hurdle. Conventional delivery methods, including viral vectors, synthetic nanoparticles, and electroporation, suffer from limitations such as immunogenicity, toxicity, and inefficient cargo release [6, 7]. Recently, extracellular vesicles (EV) have emerged as a promising non-viral alternative, owing to their biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and ability to cross biological barriers [8]. Naturally secreted by cells, EVs transport proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids, playing a critical role in intercellular communication and therapeutic cargo delivery [9]. Their ability to encapsulate and deliver CRISPR components makes them an ideal gene-editing platform [6]. Beyond delivery, EVs reflect the molecular composition of their parent cells and serve as rich biomarker sources in bodily fluids, enabling non-invasive liquid biopsy applications and early disease detection when coupled with CRISPR-based detection platforms [10].

Unlike conventional EV drug loading, which typically focuses on small molecules, proteins, or nucleic acids, CRISPR–EV integration is distinguished by two key dimensions: loading CRISPR components into EVs for direct targeting and therapeutic delivery to diseased cells, and targeting endogenous biomarkers within EVs for CRISPR-based diagnostic detection [6]. The distinctive value of this integration lies in the dual role of EVs—serving simultaneously as delivery vehicles for CRISPR therapeutics and as biomarker-rich substrates for CRISPR-based diagnostic detection—thereby establishing a unified platform that bridges therapeutic intervention and molecular diagnostics in precision medicine. Compared with viral vectors, lipid nanoparticles, and electroporation, EV-mediated delivery exploits the endogenous secretory pathway, avoids pre-existing immunity to viral capsids, and preserves natural tissue tropism and biocompatibility [6, 7, 11–28].

Despite growing interest in CRISPR–EV integration, most studies have focused on either diagnostic or therapeutic aspects, lacking a unified view. This review aims to bridge that gap by providing a comprehensive analysis of both EV-based CRISPR diagnostics and therapeutics. The following sections outline CRISPR technology and EV biology, discuss EV isolation and CRISPR-based EV diagnostics, and examine engineered EVs as CRISPR delivery carriers. Clinical applications, advantages and limitations of EV-mediated CRISPR delivery, and emerging directions including AI integration and combined theranostic platforms are also covered. By elucidating the synergistic potential of the CRISPR–EV integration, this review explores how these technologies may reshape precision medicine and drive next-generation strategies for diagnosis and treatment.

Genome editing based on CRISPR technology

CRISPR-induced gene knockouts

The CRISPR–Cas system enables highly targeted modification of genetic material by guiding nuclease activity to specific DNA sequences through RNA–DNA base pairing. This combination of precision and programmability makes it an ideal platform for efficient and versatile gene editing. Over the past decade, CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene knockouts have revolutionized genetic research, agriculture, and drug discovery by enabling precise and efficient gene disruption [11, 13]. This approach introduces double-stranded breaks (DSBs). These breaks are typically repaired through non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), resulting in gene inactivation. In contrast, homology-directed repair (HDR) enables precise sequence replacement but with lower efficiency [20]. This strategy has been broadly applied to model various human diseases. In metabolic and muscular disorders, Li et al. generated a Hereditary Tyrosinemia Type 1 (HT1) rabbit model by deleting fumarylacetoacetate hydrolase (FAH) [15], and Young et al. developed a Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) mouse model lacking exon 45 [19]. In cancer studies, knockout of Trp53 and Brca2 accelerated tumor development and enhanced sensitivity to poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors [18]. In neurodegenerative diseases, Duan et al. employed adeno-associated virus (AAV)-delivered CRISPR to correct APP Swedish mutations (APPswe), significantly reducing amyloid-β accumulation in Alzheimer’s models [29]. Similarly, Dash et al. established a human immunodeficiency virus/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (HIV/AIDS) mouse model characterized by persistent viral load and CD4+ T-cell depletion [30]. Beyond preclinical models, clinical trials have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of CRISPR knockouts. For instance, C–C chemokine receptor type 5 (CCR5) editing in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) led to stable engraftment in HIV-1 treatment [31], while BCL11A knockout in CD34+ HSPCs induced fetal hemoglobin expression in sickle cell disease (SCD) and transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia (TDT) [32]. In cancer immunotherapy, programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) knockout in T cells has been explored as a strategy for treating non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [33]. Technological advances have further strengthened the platform, as demonstrated by Esmaeili Anvar et al.’s development of a CRISPR/Cas12a system for efficient high-throughput screening [34] and Ito et al.’s optimization of gene editing protocols in T cells [35]. Collectively, these advances position CRISPR-based gene knockout as a cornerstone technology in both disease modeling and therapeutic genome engineering.

CRISPR screens

Advancements in CRISPR have extended its use beyond knockouts to systematic genetic screening for disease targets and drug discovery [13]. CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) and activation (CRISPRa) enable reversible gene silencing or activation (Table 1) [36]. CRISPRi, using dead Cas9 (dCas9) fused to repressor domains, has been applied in HEK293 cells and rat hippocampal cultures to study cytokine and neuronal regulators [27], while CRISPRa, employing transcriptional activators, identified immune and melanoma resistance genes such as PD-L1, MCL1, and JUNB [37]. These tools now extend to organoids, animals, and plants. Liu et al. (2020) used CRISPRi in brain organoids to identify long noncoding RNA glioma resistance-specific 1 (lncGRS-1) as a glioma target [38] and Deutsch et al. (2021) silenced hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated channel 4 (HCN4) in mouse neurons using AAV-delivered CRISPRi [39]. Zárate-Chaves et al. (2023) applied CRISPRi in Xanthomonas to reduce virulence [40]. High-throughput platforms like Procode and Perturb-seq now integrate proteomic, epigenomic, and transcriptomic data [41]. These advances promise deeper insight into gene networks and accelerated therapeutic development.

Base editing as a precise alternative to knockouts

Base editing provides a precise alternative to CRISPR knockouts by enabling targeted point mutations without generating DSBs. By fusing dCas9 or Cas9 nickase (nCas9) with deaminase enzymes, it modifies DNA or RNA bases without requiring HDR templates [46]. Cytosine base editors (CBEs) catalyze the conversion of cytosine (C) to thymine (T), while adenine base editors (ABEs) deaminate adenine (A) to inosine (I), which is interpreted as guanine (G) during replication and translation. As a result, ABEs effectively mediate A > G substitutions. However, increasing editing precision—particularly for challenging conversions such as C > G—remains an ongoing goal [47]. Base editing has demonstrated therapeutic potential in multiple preclinical and clinical settings. For example, Whisenant et al. (2022) corrected the lamin A/C (LMNA) c.1824C > T mutation in a progeria mouse model, reducing progerin accumulation and improving skin pathology [48]. Lim et al. (2020) applied CBEs to amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) models, suppressing SOD1 expression and delaying disease progression [49]. In clinical trials, the ABE-based therapy VERVE-101 lowered low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels by 55% in patients with familial hypercholesterolemia [50], and BEAM-101 increased fetal hemoglobin expression in individuals with sickle cell disease [51]. In 2023, base-edited CAR7 T cells induced molecular remission in two of three pediatric T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) patients [52]. Despite these advances, further improvements in specificity, efficiency, and safety are essential to enable broader clinical adoption.

Genetic sequence insertions

Recent CRISPR innovations have enabled accurate genetic insertions, expanding applications in gene therapy and functional genomics [53]. Among these approaches, HDR plays a central role by utilizing a donor DNA template flanked by sequences homologous to the target site, enabling precise integration of new genetic material into specific genomic loci. This mechanism is particularly valuable for introducing therapeutic genes, reporter tags, or corrective sequences with high fidelity. HDR remains a primary strategy, although its activity is restricted to dividing cells due to its dependence on the S and G2 phases of the cell cycle [53]. Li et al. (2021) employed HDR to correct HT1 mutations in rabbits, restoring liver and kidney function [15], and Selvaraj et al. (2024) achieved a 50-fold increase in HDR efficiency in human primary cells [54]. HDR also enables precise fluorescent tagging of genomic loci [55]. To overcome HDR’s cell cycle limitations, prime editing has emerged as a versatile alternative, allowing targeted insertions without generating double-stranded breaks and functioning in both dividing and non-dividing cells. Jang et al. (2022) applied it to correct mutations in HT1 and Leber congenital amaurosis (LCA) models [56]. Everette et al. (2023) used it in sickle cell disease hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), reducing red blood cell sickling [57]. Though less efficient than HDR, prime editing offers greater precision. RNA-guided DNA transposition (CAST) is promising for large insertions but remains confined to prokaryotes [58]. Recombinase–Cas fusions are also being developed to further expand CRISPR’s genome engineering capabilities, facilitating site-specific insertions with potentially reduced reliance on HDR.

Strategies to enhance the specificity and precision of CRISPR-Based gene editing

As CRISPR technologies advance, ensuring high specificity and precision is critical for clinical safety. High-fidelity Cas9 variants (e.g., Sniper2L, HypaCas9, SpCas9-HF1) and guide RNA chemical modifications (2′-OMe, 2′-F) have demonstrated enhanced specificity with FDA-compliant assays detecting no measurable off-target activity (Table 2) [59–68]. Still, Cas9-independent deaminases and reverse transcriptases can introduce unintended edits, requiring refinement of effector domains and delivery systems [69]. Achieving precise edits is also challenged by DNA repair pathway competition; Cas9 often induces insertions/deletions (indels) that reduce precision [70]. Small molecules like nocodazole and irinotecan enhance HDR [53, 71–73], while NHEJ inhibitors (AZD7648, M3814) bias repair toward HDR. DSB-free tools like base and prime editors (CBEs, ABEs) improve single-base editing and minimize indels [74]. Despite limitations such as bystander edits and narrow targeting scopes, newer editors aim to improve specificity and broaden applicability [75].

Natural and artificial EVs

Natural EV isolation methods

Because EVs reflect the molecular profile of their cells of origin, they have emerged as promising biomarkers for disease diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic monitoring, as well as potential vehicles for targeted drug delivery. Therefore, efficient and reproducible EV isolation is essential to ensure accurate downstream analyses and preserve their therapeutic functionality (Table 3).

Ultracentrifugation—the most widely used method—separates vesicles by density under forces exceeding 100,000 × g, enabling the isolation of exosomes, microvesicles, and apoptotic bodies from biological fluids such as blood, urine, and cell culture supernatants [120]. However, it is time-consuming and may damage EVs or co-isolate contaminants such as proteins and lipoproteins, compromising analytical accuracy. Size Exclusion Chromatography (SEC) and Ion Exchange Chromatography (IEX) offer gentler alternatives. SEC employs porous beads to achieve size-based separation while preserving EV structure and function, making it suitable for therapeutic applications. IEX exploits charge differences to enrich EV subpopulations from plasma and serum, improving analytical precision. Tangential Flow Filtration (TFF), which uses cross-flow filtration, enables efficient large-scale EV isolation, prevents membrane clogging, and yields up to 23-fold higher EV recovery than ultracentrifugation while maintaining purity and structural integrity [121]. Moreover, optimizing flow rate and nanochannel width in TFF can enhance drug-loading efficiency without compromising EV stability [117, 122]. These isolation methods are essential for both CRISPR-based EV diagnostics—enabling enrichment of EV biomarkers for Cas12a/Cas13a detection—and therapeutic applications, as high-purity EVs serve as carriers for CRISPR cargo delivery [123].

Artificial synthesis of EVs

While natural EVs possess unique biological properties, their low yield and batch-to-batch variability limit large-scale clinical and research applications. Artificial EV synthesis aims to overcome these constraints by enabling precise control over vesicle size, composition, and cargo loading, while mimicking the structural and functional characteristics of natural EVs. The underlying principle involves the physical or chemical assembly of lipid bilayers or polymer-based nanostructures to replicate the membrane architecture and bioactivity of natural EVs.

Extrusion generates EV mimetics, specifically artificially cell-derived vesicles (ACDVs) by forcing cells or cellular membranes through polycarbonate membranes, allowing size-controlled vesicle production [124]. By adjusting pore size and the number of extrusion cycles, reproducible vesicle batches can be produced for therapeutic or research purposes. Microfluidic technology further enables precise vesicle fabrication by controlling lipid and aqueous flows within microchannels, facilitating efficient cargo loading, surface modification, and scalable production.

EV mimetics include synthetic vesicles (SVs), such as lipid-based liposomes, which are designed to replicate the functional properties of natural EVs. In contrast, non-vesicular polymer-based nanoparticles [e.g., PLGA (poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid)), PEG (polyethylene glycol)] are considered EV-inspired synthetic nanocarriers rather than EV mimetics. Microfluidics can fine-tune particle size and composition, while loading strategies such as passive diffusion, electroporation, and membrane permeabilization enhance the encapsulation efficiency of therapeutic molecules.

Genetically engineered donor cells can be used to produce tailored EVs with enhanced therapeutic functionality. For instance, the Late domain-based exosomal antibody surface display (LEAP) platform demonstrated that donor cells can be genetically modified to generate EVs displaying αPD-L1 and αCD3 single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) on their surface. These engineered EVs redirected T cells toward PD-L1+ tumor cells and promoted T-cell activation and anti-tumor immune responses [125]. This approach highlights how donor-cell engineering enables precise control of EV surface functionality for immunotherapeutic applications.

Various sources of EVs

The biological origin of extracellular vesicles (EVs) directly influences their delivery efficiency, targeting specificity, immunological characteristics, and manufacturing feasibility. Although EVs derived from the same cell type as the intended target cells are generally considered ideal, technical and economic limitations often necessitate the use of alternative cell sources.

Among various producer cells, HEK293 cells—human embryonic kidney–derived cells—are widely used due to their rapid proliferation rate and high transfection efficiency. For these reasons, HEK293 cells are extensively employed in EV-based CRISPR delivery studies, with their derivatives such as HEK293T [124, 125] and HEK293F [126] being particularly popular in recent research.

Beyond HEK293 cells, multiple cell types are utilized for EV production according to their distinct advantages. For instance, mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived EVs [127] exhibit strong anti-inflammatory and regenerative properties, making them particularly attractive for degenerative diseases associated with chronic inflammation. Red blood cell (RBC)-derived EVs [128] offer safety-related advantages because RBCs lack nuclei and genomic DNA, thereby reducing the risk of horizontal gene transfer. In cancer therapy, EVs derived from tumor cells [129] are often employed to maximize targeting efficiency toward homologous tumors; however, the presence of tumor-associated proteins and nucleic acids raises potential safety concerns related to tumor-promoting effects, necessitating careful risk evaluation.

Immune cell-derived EVs possess the inherent advantage of naturally homing to inflamed tissues or pathological microenvironments. However, their broader application remains limited due to challenges in large-scale production. To overcome this limitation, alternative strategies have been explored, including the differentiation of other cell types into immune cell–like phenotypes to harness similar functional advantages [130].

EV as a CRISPR-based diagnostic platform

EVs contain proteins and nucleic acids such as miRNAs, mRNAs, DNA, and other noncoding RNAs. Since the initial discovery that EVs contain many biomarkers, intense interest has been focused on using EV biomarkers as diagnostic markers for diseases (Fig. 2) [131].

Biogenesis and composition of EVs. EVs pass through the forms of early endosome and multivesicular bodies, and are released either as microvesicles, where multiple EVs are contained within a single vesicle, or as the EVs themselves. EVs consist of diverse proteins, RNA, and DNA. On the outer membrane, they express certain proteins including transmembrane proteins, lipid-binding proteins, tetraspanins, and adhesion molecules. Inside, their cargo includes cytosolic proteins, cytoskeletal components, molecular chaperones, metabolic enzymes, and ribosomal proteins. Analysis for EV proteins

EV proteins mainly originate from the plasma membrane and cytosol, with minimal input from organelles like the Golgi apparatus or nucleus. EV membrane proteins include tetraspanins (CD9, CD63, CD81) in exosomes and integrins, selectins, and CD40 ligands in microvesicles, serving as biomarkers in health and disease. Internally, EVs carry cytosolic proteins (TSG101, ALIX, annexins), cytoskeletal components, enzymes, and ribosomal proteins, which can be transferred to recipient cells for therapeutic applications [132, 133]. EV protein analysis is crucial for understanding biogenesis, cargo sorting, and disease markers. Western blotting, though time-consuming, is widely used to confirm EV-associated proteins (Table 4) [134–136]. EV surface proteins such as CD63 and CD81 also serve as capture targets for CRISPR-coupled assays (e.g., aptamer-CRISPR/Cas12a, AID-Cas), enabling sensitive protein-based diagnostics that integrate EV biomarkers with Cas nuclease readouts [137, 138]. ELISA offers faster, high-throughput quantification using antibody-based detection [134, 139, 140]. Mass spectrometry (MS) enables high-throughput, quantitative profiling of EV proteins through enzymatic digestion, peptide fractionation, and ionization, revealing functional protein networks involved in intercellular communication [141–143]. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) is commonly employed to verify sample integrity and support the interpretation of proteomic data [142]. SERS-based platforms facilitate the direct identification of exosomal surface proteins without labeling for cancer diagnostics [143].

Analysis for nucleic acids

EVs carry nucleic acids, including microRNA (miRNA), transfer RNA (tRNA), long noncoding RNA (lncRNA), fragmented messenger RNA (mRNA), and DNA [161, 162]. EV-derived RNA modulates recipient cell function by enabling mRNA translation into proteins and regulating mRNA stability via miRNA [162]. EV-associated DNA, including internal double-stranded fragments (100–2,500 bp) and larger external fragments, reflects parental tumor genetics, aiding in mutation detection and disease monitoring [161]. Because EV nucleic acids are present at low abundance, efficient extraction and highly sensitive detection methods are essential (Table 6) [163]. Phenol-chloroform extraction ensures high-purity RNA [164], while spin columns enable rapid purification [165]. PCR-based methods, including RT-PCR for target quantification and NGS for genomic profiling, facilitate analysis [166]. Advanced detection technologies, such as Droplet PCR, ion-exchange nanodetectors, LSPR-based assays, and FRET-based assays improve high-throughput screening [167–169].

Clinical applications of EV analyses

With growing evidence that EVs carry disease-specific biomolecules, their clinical applications have broadened, especially in oncology (Fig. 3), where the integration of CRISPR-based detection platforms with EV biomarkers has expanded the diagnostic potential of liquid biopsies by enabling highly sensitive and specific identification of EV-derived nucleic acids and proteins. CRISPR-based EV biomarker diagnosis platforms have been actively explored across various cancer types, including glioblastoma, ovarian, pancreatic, prostate, lung, breast, and bladder cancers [192–198]. For instance, Figueroa et al. identified EGFR mutations in cerebrospinal fluid-derived EVs from glioblastoma patients, reflecting tumor-specific RNA profiles [192]. Sinha et al. reported proteomic signatures from ovarian cancer-derived EVs [198], while Madhavan et al. highlighted exosomal CD44v6, Tspan8, EpCAM, and miRNAs in pancreatic cancer [196]. In prostate cancer, urine EVs containing specific miRNA isoforms offered diagnostic value [195]. He et al. developed a fluorescence-based method to detect lung cancer EVs [193], and Morasso et al. found elevated CD9+/CD63+ EVs in early breast cancer [197]. Igami et al. also demonstrated the diagnostic potential of urinary EV-derived CEACAM proteins in bladder cancer [194]. CRISPR-based platforms such as SHERLOCK (Cas13a) and DETECTR (Cas12a) enabled isothermal detection of EV RNA and DNA at sub-attomolar levels, thereby enhancing sensitivity for EV-derived biomarker detection [3, 191, 199]. The SCOPE assay achieved mutational profiling of EV mRNA with single-nucleotide resolution, facilitating precision oncology applications including somatic mutation detection, resistance profiling, and tumor recurrence monitoring [190]. Liposome–EV fusion assays allowed direct CRISPR-based nucleic acid detection in patient blood without time-consuming EV isolation, supporting rapid cancer and infectious disease diagnostics [200, 201]. Orthogonal Cas12a/Cas13a platforms enabled multiplexed exosome protein phenotyping in clinical serum samples [182]. Beyond cancer, EVs serve as emerging biomarkers for neurodegenerative diseases. Misfolded proteins associated with Alzheimer’s, ALS, and Parkinson’s disease have been shown to spread via EVs [202, 203]. For example, exosomal Aβ peptides contribute to Alzheimer’s pathology by promoting plaque propagation [203], while EV-mediated transmission of misfolded SOD1 and α-synuclein is implicated in ALS and Parkinson’s, respectively [202]. Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease have been investigated using microfluidic technologies that isolate and analyze EVs carrying disease-specific biomarkers [204].

Furthermore, EVs aid in monitoring organ injury. Kamińska et al. found that urinary EV size and density correlate with diabetic kidney damage [205], while Hinzman et al. identified radiation-induced metabolic alterations in urinary EVs [206]. In drug-induced liver injury, Zhao et al. observed reduced expression of CD26 and CD81 in EVs [207]. Urinary EV-derived CD2AP mRNA levels were also linked to CKD progression [208], and Miller et al. demonstrated that microRNAs in urinary EVs correlate with acute kidney injury following cardiac surgery [209]. These findings support EVs as robust, non-invasive tools for disease monitoring and prognosis.

EVs play a crucial role in infectious disease diagnostics, enabling early detection, immune response monitoring, and antibiotic resistance tracking [210]. EVs carry viral RNA or proteins, aiding in the diagnosis of SARS-CoV-2, HIV-1, HCV, and TTV [200, 211]. To harness this, liposome–EV fusion coupled with CRISPR–Cas detection has been applied to detect EV-encapsulated viral RNA for SARS-CoV-2 diagnosis in patient plasma. Nguyen et al. (2025) developed the Biochip Antigen and RNA Assay (BARA) to improve SARS-CoV-2 detection in asymptomatic patients [212], while Zhao et al. (2023) found Zika virus E proteins in EVs, facilitating infection monitoring [213]. Additionally, Kim et al. (2020) identified antibiotic resistance proteins in MRSA-derived EVs, highlighting their role in antimicrobial resistance detection [214].

In forensic science, EVs aid in crime scene analysis, DNA identification, and toxicology due to their persistence in blood, saliva, and urine (Fig. 4) [215, 216]. Kim et al. (2022) found that exosomal biomarkers (CD9, CD63, miR-21, miR-126) remain stable post-mortem, supporting forensic investigations [215]. Severtsev et al. (2024) identified EV-associated addiction biomarkers, demonstrating their potential in toxicological analysis [216]. EVs also serve as bioindicators in environmental science, reflecting water quality, soil health, and pollutant exposure [217]. Pal et al. (2023) demonstrated that CRISPR/Cas-based assays detect algal bloom toxins, providing a cost-effective tool for environmental monitoring [218]. The integration of EV diagnostics with CRISPR technology offers a promising approach for enhanced disease surveillance, forensic analysis, and environmental management.

Engineered EV as a CRISPR delivery carrier

Limitations of conventional CRISPR carriers

Delivery strategies for CRISPR components into cells can be broadly categorized into three major approaches: physical methods, viral vectors, and non-viral vectors. Representative physical methods include electroporation and microinjection, both of which are mainly used for ex vivo applications. However, their direct application in vivo remains challenging, as electroporation may induce significant cellular damage while microinjection suffers from low throughput [27].

Viral vectors comprise several commonly used platforms. Adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) exhibit relatively low immunogenicity but are limited by small cargo capacity [25]. In contrast, adenoviruses provide larger packaging capacity but pose a higher risk of immune responses [21]. Lentiviral and retroviral vectors were developed to overcome some of these limitations; however, issues related to controllability and genomic integration remain significant concerns [6].

Among non-viral vectors, most systems are synthetically engineered, with lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) being the most widely used delivery vehicle. Owing to their relatively low immunogenicity and the ease with which functional properties can be tailored, LNPs have been extensively applied in gene therapy research, including CRISPR-based applications [27]. Nevertheless, while LNP-mediated delivery predominantly accumulates in the liver and bone marrow, their targeting specificity toward other tissues or cell types remains limited [25]. Additionally, concerns have been raised regarding prolonged persistence in vivo, which may lead to adverse effects associated with excessive protein expression [219].

Therapeutic potential of EV-mediated CRISPR/Cas9 delivery

EVs offer a biocompatible and efficient platform for CRISPR/CRISPR-associated protein 9 (Cas9) delivery, overcoming major challenges in genome editing. McAndrews et al. demonstrated that exosomes protect plasmid DNA from DNase degradation, enhancing therapeutic stability. Banks et al. reported that different exosome subpopulations traverse the BBB at varying rates, underscoring their potential for targeted gene therapy [220]. These inherent advantages have positioned natural EVs as attractive carriers for CRISPR-based therapeutics. In addition to natural EVs, artificially synthesized EVs and EV mimetics produced by extrusion or microfluidic methods (see Artificial Synthesis of EVs) offer scalable, cargo-loadable platforms for CRISPR delivery [124, 221].

Genetically engineered donor cells can be used to produce tailored EVs capable of delivering gene-editing or therapeutic agents. For example, Kalani et al. (2021) et al. delivered CRISPR-Cas9 mRNA via EVs, and Liang et al. (2020) co-delivered CRISPR-Cas9 with anti-PD-1 (anti-programmed cell death protein 1) antibodies to enhance cancer immunotherapy. Furthermore, stimulating donor cells with cytokines or growth factors can customize EV cargo composition, expanding applications in immune modulation and gene therapy [222].

To improve delivery efficiency and specificity beyond production, EVs can be engineered with various modification strategies (Fig. 5). For instance, conjugating targeting ligands such as peptides, antibodies, or aptamers to the EV surface enables selective delivery to specific tissues or cells, reducing off-target effects. Genetic engineering of donor cells to express EV-enriched membrane proteins (e.g., Lamp2b, CD63) fused with targeting motifs can further enhance tissue-specific distribution. Cargo can be loaded into EVs using physical and microfluidic loading strategies, including electroporation, sonication, and microfluidic mixing, to efficiently incorporate plasmid DNA, messenger RNA (mRNA), or ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes.

Cargo forms and target cell types of surface-modified CRISPR-EVs. Surface-engineered extracellular vesicles (EVs) carrying plasmid DNA, mRNA, or ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes represent versatile therapeutic modalities for precision genome editing. By enabling efficient and cell type–specific delivery, CRISPR-EVs hold promise in treating cancer, genetic disorders, and inflammation. Their potential targets encompass a broad spectrum of cells, including tumor cells, leukocytes, stem cells, myoblasts, chondrocytes, hepatocytes, and neurons

EV-mediated CRISPR/Cas9 delivery enables multiplex genome editing, allowing simultaneous regulation of multiple genes, thereby enhancing therapeutic outcomes such as tumor suppression and metastasis reduction. By combining rational EV engineering, optimized cargo loading methods, and precise targeting strategies, challenges related to scalability, specificity, and delivery efficiency can be addressed.

CRISPR delivery via EVs: pDNA, mRNA, and RNP

Plasmid DNA (pDNA) remains a widely used method for delivering CRISPR/Cas9 due to its cost-effectiveness and ability to sustain long-term expression. However, its reliance on nuclear transcription and translation delays gene editing, and its persistence increases the risk of off-target effects. Despite these limitations, pDNA is effective for applications requiring sustained Cas9 expression. McAndrews et al. demonstrated its therapeutic potential by delivering CRISPR/Cas9-loaded exosomes targeting KrasG12D, reducing tumor proliferation in pancreatic cancer models [124]. Similarly, Kim et al. employed exosomal delivery of CRISPR/Cas9 to inhibit PARP-1, significantly reducing tumor volume in xenograft models [129]. However, electroporation-based pDNA loading into exosomes remains inefficient, with only ~1.75% loading efficacy. To address this, Lin et al. developed an exosome-liposome hybrid system with superior delivery efficiency [223], while Xu et al. engineered CAR-expressing HEK293T-derived EVs to enhance tumor-targeting specificity [224]. Dubey et al. developed the safeEXO platform, an sEV-based delivery system largely devoid of endogenous RNA that enables cell-specific CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing when engineered with tissue-targeting moieties such as integrin alpha-6 for lung epithelium [225]. Liang et al. further advanced this by designing peptide-modified EVs for chondrocyte-targeted MMP-13 knockdown in osteoarthritis, reducing cartilage degradation [226].

Delivering Cas9 as mRNA offers advantages over DNA-based approaches, including faster translation, reduced immunogenicity, and lower carcinogenic risk. However, mRNA’s inherent instability and short half-life limit its therapeutic efficacy, and its large size presents challenges for encapsulation in EVs. Despite these limitations, red blood cell-derived EVs (RBCEVs) have successfully delivered Cas9 mRNA in leukemia and breast cancer models, achieving nuclear localization in ~50% of target cells without cytotoxic effects [128]. Li et al. enhanced mRNA loading by fusing the exosomal membrane protein CD9 with the RNA-binding protein HuR, enabling targeted suppression of C/ebpa expression and demonstrating the potential of EVs for precise gene regulation [227].

CRISPR ribonucleoproteins (RNPs), pre-assembled Cas9-sgRNA complexes, offer a rapid and transient editing approach, minimizing off-target effects while improving precision. RNPs bypass transcription and translation steps, leading to faster genome modifications with greater stability than mRNA. Wan et al. successfully loaded RNPs into LX-2-derived exosomes via electroporation, targeting liver disease-related genes like PUMA and KAT5, showing strong therapeutic potential [228]. However, the large size of Cas9 (160 kDa) and high production costs pose scalability challenges. To improve delivery efficiency, Elsharkasy et al. utilized MS2 aptamer-based RNA binding to enhance sgRNA loading into EVs and engineered VSV-G-expressing EVs to promote cellular uptake and endosomal escape [229]. Zheng et al. developed engineered EVs incorporating mini-intein self-cleavage and VSV-G fusogenic protein that achieved high-efficiency Cas9-RNP delivery with robust genome editing in vitro and in vivo, including Cre recombination in brain after intracerebroventricular infusion [230]. Whitley and Cai demonstrated EV loading of CRISPR RNP via membrane-anchoring strategies to reduce off-target effects [231]. These advances underscore the growing potential of EV-based RNP delivery in next-generation genome editing therapies.

Precision oncology: using EVs for targeted CRISPR/Cas delivery in cancer therapy

In cancer models, CRISPR-loaded EVs have shown remarkable potential for precisely disrupting oncogenic mutations and restoring the function of tumor suppressor genes. For instance, CRISPR-Cas9 systems delivered via EVs have successfully targeted KRAS mutations in pancreatic cancer, leading to 58% reductions in tumor cells [124]. This precision targeting holds substantial promise for overcoming the limitations of conventional cancer therapies.

Advances in surface engineering techniques are further improving the targeting specificity and stability of CRISPR-loaded EVs. Strategies such as antibody-functionalized exosomes and aptamer-conjugated vesicles have demonstrated enhanced binding efficiency to tumor cells while simultaneously protecting CRISPR components from degradation in systemic circulation. As a result, these engineered EVs are paving the way for future clinical applications in precision oncology, offering a more effective and targeted approach to cancer treatment.

Harnessing EVs for CRISPR-Based solutions to genetic disorders

CRISPR-loaded EVs show great potential in correcting genetic mutations, particularly in Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), where engineered EVs enable exon-skipping to restore dystrophin expression in muscle tissues, as demonstrated by serum EV-mediated delivery of CRISPR-Cas9 RNP to modify the dystrophin gene [12, 232]. This localized approach overcomes delivery inefficiencies and toxicity challenges associated with systemic therapies. Furthermore, CRISPR-based therapeutic strategies have been investigated for a wide range of genetic disorders, including phenylketonuria, cystic fibrosis, and hemophilia. Clinical studies have also reported the transplantation of CRISPR-edited hematopoietic stem cells into patients for the treatment of sickle cell disease and β-thalassemia. Although these approaches do not involve EVs, the underlying CRISPR strategies could be adapted to EV-based delivery systems, offering potential advantages in target specificity and minimal invasiveness [32].

Advancing regenerative medicine with CRISPR-Loaded EVs

CRISPR-loaded EVs show promise for tissue repair by targeting inflammation and extracellular matrix remodeling. In osteoarthritis models, EVs delivering CRISPR-Cas9 against MMP-13 reduce cartilage degradation and improve joint function [226], while targeting pro-inflammatory cytokines aids spinal cord injury recovery [233]. Recent advancements, such as dsDNA-RNP nanostructures for controlled gene activation and tissue-specific peptides for precise delivery, enhance therapeutic potential [234]. Small EVs have also been engineered for cardiac-specific CRISPR-Cas9 RNP delivery to improve myocardial infarction outcomes [235]. These innovations extend CRISPR-EV applications to conditions like myocardial infarction, establishing them as a powerful tool in regenerative medicine.

Future directions

Advantages and limitations of EV diagnostics with CRISPR technology

CRISPR-integrated EV diagnostics enable highly sensitive and non-invasive detection of a wide range of diseases, including cancers, infectious diseases, and neurodegenerative disorders. A major advantage of this approach is its ability to identify rare biomarkers from minimal biological samples by harnessing CRISPR’s robust signal amplification capabilities, thereby facilitating early diagnosis [187] and effective treatment monitoring [222].

Despite these advantages, key challenges remain. These include the intrinsic heterogeneity and low nucleic acid content of EVs, and potential off-target effects of CRISPR-based assays. Addressing these issues requires standardized EV isolation protocols, effective enrichment techniques, and scalable, cost-efficient diagnostic platforms. Robust bioinformatics pipelines are also essential to manage and interpret the complex datasets derived from CRISPR readouts and EV nucleic acid profiles, particularly in platforms like DETECTR and SHERLOCK [236].

Future developments should prioritize: (1) standardized EV isolation (e.g., SEC-based methods [143], (2) AI-assisted biomarker discovery [113], (3) nanomaterial-based biosensors, and (4) miniaturized CRISPR platforms (e.g., HUDSON, SHERLOCK [237]) for point-of-care diagnostics. These efforts will be critical to enhancing scalability, specificity, and clinical utility in CRISPR-EV diagnostics, ultimately advancing their role in next-generation precision medicine.

Advantages and limitations of EV treatment with CRISPR technology

EV-mediated CRISPR delivery offers advantages over viral and synthetic vectors, including cargo protection by the lipid bilayer [124], barrier-crossing ability for neurological applications [220], and engineerable tissue tropism [224]. Despite these advantages, several technical constraints hinder clinical translation. Heterogeneous EV surface composition and insufficient ligand density limit tissue-specific delivery and contribute to off-target uptake [6, 124]. Large-scale production faces batch-to-batch variability, low yield, and lack of standardized GMP protocols; heterogeneity in EV size and composition results in variable editing efficiency across preparations [6]. EVs also exhibit low zeta potential and susceptibility to aggregation, while in vivo release kinetics and endosomal entrapment of CRISPR cargo are poorly characterized. Although recent advances such as the SEAL method, exosome–liposome hybrids, and MS2 aptamer–based loading have improved efficiency [223, 224, 229], scalable manufacturing for clinical doses remains unmet.

Safety and regulatory hurdles further impede translation. CRISPR-related risks—off-target effects, inefficient HDR, and potential oncogenic insertions—apply to EV delivery and require careful monitoring [8, 238]. No CRISPR-EV therapies have received FDA or EMA approval, and translational barriers include establishing pharmacodynamic endpoints, defining release criteria for EV-CRISPR products, and navigating regulatory pathways that overlap with both cell-derived therapeutics and gene-editing agents [239, 240]. Addressing these challenges will require GMP-compatible production, standardized quality control, and rigorous preclinical safety evaluation to advance EV-CRISPR therapies toward clinical trials (Fig. 6, Table 8).

Conclusions

Over the past three decades, CRISPR-based technologies have revolutionized disease diagnosis and treatment, enabling applications in gene knockout, screening, base editing, and sequence insertion. However, conventional CRISPR delivery methods—including viral, synthetic nanoparticle, and physical techniques—face limitations in packaging capacity, immunogenicity, and efficiency [6, 7].

To address these challenges, engineered EVs improve CRISPR-based genome editing by ensuring targeted delivery with reduced off-target effects. In oncology, EV-mediated CRISPR/Cas9 delivery offers a promising gene-editing strategy, while in diagnostics, CRISPR-based EV platforms enhance early disease detection, metastasis tracking, and therapeutic response evaluation. Beyond oncology, these platforms extend to neurodegenerative disease and infectious disease detection, forensic science, and environmental biosurveillance.

Despite its potential, EV-CRISPR clinical translation faces challenges, including large-scale EV production, functional consistency, efficient cargo loading, and in vivo stability [6]. Addressing these issues requires standardized EV isolation, advanced bioengineering, and improved stability strategies. Although no CRISPR-EV therapies have received FDA approval, preclinical studies show promise. For example, EVs delivered CRISPR-Cas9 to reverse sorafenib resistance in HCC models [253], edited the dystrophin gene in Duchenne muscular dystrophy mice [16], and targeted miR-34a to improve myocardial infarction recovery [235]. While clinical trials are yet to begin, preclinical success suggests future advancements in clinical trials and precision medicine.

In conclusion, CRISPR-EV technologies hold transformative potential, though challenges in efficiency and safety must be overcome. With ongoing research and engineering advancements, these next-generation diagnostic and therapeutic platforms could progress toward clinical trials, regulatory approval, and widespread medical application.
