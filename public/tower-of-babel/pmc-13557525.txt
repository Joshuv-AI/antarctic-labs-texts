ABSTRACT
Background:Breast cancer remains one of the leading causes of mortality among women worldwide, highlighting the urgent need for accurate and efficient diagnostic tools. Histopathological image analysis plays a critical role in diagnosis by enabling cellularlevel tissue examination. However, manual assessment is often timeconsuming, subjective, and prone to variability, driving the need for automated solutions.Methods:This study proposes DenseUNet169, a deep learning framework that integrates established DenseNet and UNet for systematic evaluation in breast cancer segmentation. The model improves gradient flow, facilitates efficient training, and enhances segmentation accuracy by focusing on visually salient regions within histopathological images. Performance is evaluated using key metrics, including Intersection over Union, Dice Coefficient, Pixel Accuracy, Modified Hausdorff Distance, Surface Dice Overlap, Log Loss, and Jaccard Index.Results:Experiments conducted on a publicly available breast cancer segmentation dataset demonstrate competitive performance compared to alternative backbone configurations.Conclusion:Given the limited dataset size, the results should be interpreted as a proof of concept evaluation of the proposed architectural integration rather than evidence of largescale clinical deployment readiness.

Introduction

Histopathology remains a cornerstone in modern medical diagnostics, providing a vital means for individualized assessment, therapeutic planning, and disease prognosis.[1] The process involves a series of intricate steps, including fixation, tissue processing, sectioning, and staining, each essential to preserving cellular integrity and enabling detailed microscopic evaluation.[2] Fixation stabilizes tissue architecture by halting enzymatic degradation, whereas tissue processing replaces water content with a firm medium to facilitate fine slicing. Sectioning transforms these prepared blocks into ultra-thin slices, and staining imparts contrast to cellular structures, enabling pathologists to identify pathological features with precision.[345] Mastery of histopathological techniques is thus essential for the reliable preparation and interpretation of tissue samples.[6]

In recent years, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into histopathological image analysis has revolutionized diagnostic workflows, particularly in breast cancer screening and monitoring,[78] Given that early-stage breast conditions often present asymptomatically, the deployment of semi-automated and scalable screening tools has become increasingly critical. AI-driven methods, especially those employing convolutional neural networks (CNNs), offer the potential to not only streamline image classification but also augment the diagnostic accuracy of clinical experts.[9]

Deep learning, and CNNs in particular, have demonstrated significant promise in computational histopathology.[1011] Yet, existing models often remain narrowly focused on isolated tasks such as segmentation, lacking the holistic capacity to simultaneously identify spatial locations and morphological features of breast cancer tissues. Addressing this gap requires the development of advanced models that combine robust performance with interpretability two pillars essential for clinical adoption.

To this end, we propose DenseUNet169, a deep learning framework that integrates established DenseNet and U-Net for segmentation of cancerous regions in hematoxylin and eosin (H and E)-stained histopathological images. This model is structured around two synergistic components: a segmentation module and a shared DenseNet169-based backbone network. By jointly optimizing these components, DenseUNet169 achieves superior feature extraction and end-to-end segmentation capabilities. Evaluations on breast cancer datasets demonstrate that our model consistently outperforms existing architectures, offering improvements in both detection accuracy and segmentation fidelity. It should be emphasized that the proposed framework does not introduce new learning principles; rather, it investigates the empirical impact of integrating DenseNet-based encoders within a U-Net segmentation paradigm.

Related Works

Accurate segmentation of cancerous tissues is a cornerstone in histopathological image analysis, serving as a foundation for evaluating tissue morphology, quantifying pathological features, and facilitating the precise localization of malignant regions. Over the years, a diverse array of deep learning-based methodologies has emerged to address inherent challenges in this domain, particularly those arising from complex tissue structures and overlapping cells.

A seminal contribution by Amgad et al.[12] introduced fully CNNs to generate pixel-wise segmentation masks for joint region-and nucleus-level analysis in breast cancer tissue, enabling robust delineation of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. By unifying multiple segmentation tasks within a shared framework, their model achieved enhanced generalization and improved predictive performance on the PanNuke dataset. Subsequent studies have continued to refine segmentation accuracy, particularly in the context of overlapping or densely clustered nuclei.

For instance, Zhao et al.[13] proposed a hematoxylin-aware color decomposition technique to isolate nuclear components from H and E images within a Triple U-Net architecture. This approach enhanced boundary localization of nuclei by leveraging optical properties of H and E staining, improving segmentation accuracy at the cellular level without requiring color normalization. In a related advancement, Zhou et al.[14] introduced the Contour-aware informative aggregation network, which integrates specialized decoders for nuclei instance segmentation and contour detection. This dual-decoder architecture significantly mitigated the impact of cell overlap through multilevel feature aggregation, leading to more precise segmentations on multiple public benchmarks.

Recent efforts have increasingly prioritized both interpretability and computational efficiency in cancer segmentation models. van Rijthoven et al.[15] introduced HookNet, a multi-resolution encoder-decoder network that effectively integrates global context (low resolution) and local details (high resolution) via “hook” connections between parallel branches. This architecture yielded superior performance compared to conventional single-resolution frameworks on whole-slide image (WSI) segmentation tasks while maintaining clinical feasibility. To improve decision transparency, Gu et al.[16] implemented attention-guided CNNs with spatial-channel dual attention mechanisms that dynamically focus on visually salient regions within histopathological images. This design not only aligns the model’s focus with expert pathologists’ assessments but also enhances trust and interpretability in clinical settings. Similarly, Gour et al.[17] developed DeepRNNetSeg, a deep residual network tailored to learn discriminative nuclear features through skip connections and batch normalization, thereby boosting segmentation accuracy in heterogeneous histopathological environments.

Schmitz et al.[18] explored multiscale fully convolutional networks with deep feature fusion, explicitly mimicking the diagnostic strategies of human pathologists by integrating fine-grained nuclear details and global tissue architecture. These models demonstrated high accuracy while maintaining a compact memory footprint compared to ensemble approaches, making them suitable for clinical deployment.

Beyond segmentation, recent studies have proposed comprehensive systems for diagnosis and prognosis. Khaliliboroujeni et al.[19] presented an end-to-end computer-aided diagnosis system for detecting breast cancer metastases in WSIs. This approach integrates attention modules with regression and classification tasks, enabling dense tumor region prediction from low-resolution inputs. The system achieved higher accuracy than expert pathologists on the CAMELYON16/17 benchmarks and operated up to seven times faster.

López-Pérez et al.[20] addressed annotation variability by proposing a novel crowd-sourced learning framework for histopathological segmentation. Their method involves two interacting networks: a segmentation network trained on consensus annotations, and an annotator network that models individual labeling tendencies. This dual-network system enhances scalability and accuracy by leveraging annotator behavior modeling via global image features, representing a substantial innovation in consensus-based segmentation.

Complementing these efforts, recent frameworks have also targeted broader diagnostic automation. Boumaraf et al.[21] proposed a two-branch deep CNN architecture that facilitates robust classification of breast histopathology images into benign, malignant, or normal classes. This architecture addresses vanishing gradient issues through residual connections and offers improved efficiency compared to traditional manual feature engineering methods.

Further, Drioua et al.[9] introduced dual approaches for semantic segmentation of breast cancer histopathology images: (1) an autoencoder architecture for unsupervised representation learning, and (2) an enhanced U-Net architecture with residual blocks for supervised segmentation. Evaluated on the BreakHis dataset, their supervised variant achieved Dice scores exceeding 0.89 for tumor region delineation.

Collectively, these studies underscore a consistent trend: the convergence of accuracy, efficiency, and interpretability in CNN-based breast cancer segmentation. Methods

The proposed DenseUNet169 architecture is designed with a tailored encoder–decoder structure that enhances segmentation accuracy in histopathological breast cancer imaging. This section outlines the methodological workflow, from data preprocessing and augmentation to model training and evaluation techniques.

To standardise the input for deep learning, all histopathological images are resized to 256 × 256 pixels. To increase dataset diversity and mitigate overfitting, a comprehensive data augmentation pipeline is applied. Each image undergoes horizontal and vertical flipping, rotations at 90° and 180°, saturation adjustment, and central cropping, generating seven augmented samples per image. This expands the original dataset of 58 images to a total of 406 samples. The same augmentations (excluding saturation adjustment) are applied to the corresponding ground truth masks to preserve label fidelity.

To avoid data leakage, cross-validation splitting was performed at the level of the original 58 images before any augmentation. Data augmentation was applied exclusively to the training subset within each fold, ensuring that augmented versions of the same image did not appear in both training and validation sets.

The DenseUNet169 is optimised to leverage dense connectivity and enabling effective feature propagation and focusing on visually salient regions within histopathological images. During training, the model adjusts its weights and biases using the Adam optimiser with a learning rate of 0.001, and the ReLU activation function is used throughout the network. Performance is continuously monitored using a validation set, with the IoU metric guiding model checkpointing. The model achieving the highest IoU score is preserved for final deployment.

ReLU: In the proposed architecture, the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) is employed as the primary activation function throughout the hidden layers, defined mathematically as f(x)=max(0,x). The selection of ReLU over traditional saturating functions (e.g., Sigmoid or Tanh) is driven by three critical theoretical advantages. First, its non-saturating linear behavior for positive inputs (x>0) ensures a constant gradient of 1, which effectively mitigates the vanishing gradient problem and accelerates convergence during backpropagation. Second, the piecewise linear formulation requires only simple thresholding, yielding superior computational efficiency. Third, by outputting exact zeros for negative inputs, ReLU induces sparse activations, which enhances the network’s representational capacity and provides an implicit regularization effect. To circumvent the potential ’dying ReLU’ issue where neurons may become inactive and output zero for all inputs the network weights are initialized using the He (Kaiming) initialization scheme, which is specifically designed to maintain the variance of activations and gradients in ReLU-based deep networks.

In the segmentation phase, the best-performing model is used to infer segmentation masks on unseen test data. The output predictions are quantitatively compared against ground truth annotations using several metrics, including IoU, Dice Coefficient, Pixel Accuracy MHD, Surface Dice Overlap, Jaccard Score, and Log Loss. These comprehensive metrics provide a robust assessment of segmentation performance across spatial, surface, and probabilistic dimensions.

To demonstrate the efficacy and robustness of the proposed DenseUNet169, its performance is benchmarked against six state-of-the-art segmentation methods. Comparative analysis is performed using the same evaluation metrics described earlier. This pipeline provides a controlled experimental framework for evaluating segmentation performance under consistent training conditions.[22]

Implemented architecture

The implemented DenseUNet169 follows a U-Net style encoder–decoder framework in which the encoder is replaced by a DenseNet169 backbone pretrained on ImageNet. Skip connections are established between corresponding encoder and decoder stages. The encoder consists of the standard DenseNet169 convolutional blocks (dense blocks and transition layers), excluding the final classification layer. The decoder is composed of sequential upsampling blocks, each consisting of bilinear upsampling followed by convolution, batch normalization, and ReLU activation. The final segmentation output is generated using a 1 × 1 convolution with sigmoid activation for binary segmentation.

Model complexity analysis

The proposed DenseUNet169 architecture consists of a DenseNet169 encoder backbone integrated within a U-Net style decoder. The total number of model parameters is approximately 20.3 million, of which 20.1 million are trainable, and 0.2 million correspond to nontrainable parameters (primarily Batch Normalization statistics).

For an input resolution of 256 × 256 pixels, the computational complexity of the network is approximately 9.2 Giga Floating-Point Operations (GFLOPs) per forward pass. GFLOPs serves as a standard metric to quantify the computational cost and inference efficiency of deep learning models. A complexity of 9.2 GFLOPs indicates a highly efficient architecture that strikes an optimal balance between representational power and computational overhead. This relatively low computational footprint ensures that the proposed model is not only capable of capturing complex spatial features but is also highly suitable for deployment in resource-constrained environments or real-time clinical applications. While the majority of the parameters originate from the DenseNet169 encoder to ensure robust feature extraction, the decoder modules are deliberately designed to be lightweight, contributing additional representational capacity without significantly inflating the overall GFLOPs.

Given the relatively small dataset size (58 original images), the model capacity may increase the risk of overfitting despite ImageNet pretraining. Therefore, performance results should be interpreted as proof-of-concept rather than large-scale deployment validation.

For comparison, the DenseUNet121 configuration contains approximately 13.2 million parameters, representing a 35% reduction in model capacity relative to DenseUNet169 (DenseNet121 backbone ≈ 8M, Full DenseUNet121 ≈ 12-14M).

Transfer learning and fine-tuning strategy

The DenseNet169 encoder was initialized with ImageNet pretrained weights to leverage general visual feature representations. To mitigate overfitting risk given the limited dataset size (58 original images), a staged fine-tuning strategy was adopted.

During the initial training phase, the encoder layers were frozen, and only the decoder modules were optimized. This allowed the segmentation-specific layers to adapt without perturbing pre-trained feature representations.

After convergence of the decoder, the upper dense blocks of the encoder were unfrozen and fine-tuned using a reduced learning rate (〈 = 1e-3), whereas earlier layers remained frozen. This partial fine-tuning approach balances adaptation to histopathological features with regularization against overfitting.

All experiments were conducted using cross-validation, and early stopping based on validation IoU was applied to further reduce overfitting.

Dataset description

The breast cancer segmentation project utilized 58 histopathological images with expert annotations. This dataset included 58 hematoxylin and eosin (H and E) stained histopathology images of breast cancer cells, along with corresponding ground truth data. H and E staining is commonly used in routine histology to improve visibility, as most cells are naturally transparent with little intrinsic pigment. Specialized stains that selectively bind to specific components are used to highlight biological structures such as cells. The main difficulty in these images is segmenting cells for classification into benign and malignant categories. The images were saved at a resolution of 256 × 256 pixels in Portable Network Graphic format, with no image compression applied during the steps of capturing, annotating, and compiling datasets.

Various augmentations were implemented, including right-left flips, up-down flips, image rotations (90° for k = 1 and 180° for k = 2), saturation adjustments, and central cropping, resulting in a total of 406 data points. Within this dataset, 81.2% was allocated for training purposes, whereas the remaining 18.8% was designated for testing.

This step plays a vital role in tasks like measuring cell characteristics, studying cell shapes, and aiding in the diagnosis and treatment of breast cancer. Consequently, there remains a potential risk of overfitting, and the findings should be interpreted with caution. Surface Dice overlap is a variant of the Dice coefficient but focuses on boundary regions. The score of 76.8% proves that the model-predicted boundaries are much closer to the ground truth boundaries. Therefore, acceptable accuracy for delineating the contours of objects was confirmed.

Log Loss (cross-entropy loss) was computed as the cumulative pixel-wise loss across the full image resolution rather than as a normalized per-pixel average. Given the 256 × 256 resolution (65,536 pixels per image), the absolute value of Log Loss appears numerically large. Therefore, Log Loss values reported in this study should be interpreted comparatively across models rather than in absolute magnitude terms. Future work may report normalized per-pixel cross-entropy for improved interpretability.

The value of 333.07 shows where the model can be improved in terms of the confidence calibration of its predictions, since the smaller the log loss value, the better.

The Jaccard score characterizes the overlap, like IoU, but considers penalties for over-prediction and under-prediction. This score of 0.771 also agrees with IoU, strengthening the fact that this model performs very well in segmenting regions quite accurately.

To provide an estimate of performance variability, we computed the mean and standard deviation across the five cross-validation folds for each evaluation metric. Given the limited number of folds (n = 5), statistical comparisons between closely performing models should be interpreted with caution, as small numerical differences may fall within expected sampling variance.

Discussion

This study presents a structured integration and comparative evaluation of established deep learning architectures, DenseNet169 and U-Net, for breast cancer histopathological image segmentation. Rather than proposing a novel theoretical framework, the work focuses on examining how specific architectural design choices interact under controlled experimental conditions and how these interactions influence segmentation performance.

The DenseNet169 encoder facilitates dense feature propagation and multi-scale contextual representation, which is particularly beneficial for capturing subtle morphological variations in histopathological imagery. When combined with the U-Net decoder structure, the architecture enables effective spatial localization of tumor regions through hierarchical feature reconstruction.

Importantly, the performance improvements observed in this study should be interpreted as incremental architectural refinements rather than fundamental methodological advances. The primary contribution lies in the systematic benchmarking of these configurations under identical preprocessing, augmentation, and evaluation protocols. By reporting multiple quantitative metrics and maintaining consistent experimental settings, this study provides a structured empirical reference for future investigations in medical image segmentation.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, although 5-fold cross-validation was employed to improve robustness of performance estimation, the limited dataset size (58 original images) restricts statistical power. Differences observed between closely performing backbones, such as DenseNet121 and DenseNet169, may reflect expected experimental variability rather than statistically significant superiority. Larger-scale evaluations would be necessary to draw definitive comparative conclusions.

Second, despite the use of ImageNet pre-trained weights to stabilize optimization, the relatively high model capacity (approximately 20 million parameters) introduces potential overfitting risk in small-data regimes. While cross-validation and regularization strategies were applied, the results should be interpreted as proof-of-concept evidence of technical feasibility rather than confirmation of broad generalizability.

Finally, it is essential to emphasize that this study evaluates segmentation performance at a technical level and does not constitute a clinical validation study or a diagnostic decision-support assessment. No downstream clinical tasks were performed, and no claims regarding diagnostic reliability or real-world deployment readiness are made. Future work should involve evaluation on larger and more diverse datasets, as well as formal clinical validation, to assess translational applicability.

In summary, the present work contributes a disciplined empirical analysis of architectural integration strategies for histopathological image segmentation. Its value lies in methodological transparency, controlled benchmarking, and cautious interpretation of results within the constraints of limited data availability.

Conclusion

This study presented a DenseUNet-based segmentation framework integrating a DenseNet169 encoder within a U-Net architecture and evaluated its performance under controlled experimental conditions using a publicly available histopathological dataset. The work was intentionally positioned as a technical investigation into architectural integration strategies for semantic segmentation, rather than as the introduction of a novel theoretical construct or a clinically validated diagnostic system.

Under the current experimental setting, the DenseNet169 backbone demonstrated segmentation performance comparable to other high-capacity backbone configurations, with incremental improvements observed in selected evaluation metrics. These findings suggest that dense feature propagation combined with structured decoder reconstruction can provide stable and competitive performance in histopathological tumor segmentation tasks.

However, it is essential to interpret these results within the context of dataset constraints. The study was conducted on a dataset comprising 58 original images. Although data augmentation and cross-validation were employed to mitigate overfitting and improve robustness of performance estimation, the limited sample size restricts the strength of conclusions regarding generalization capability and real-world applicability. The reported results should therefore be understood as proof-of-concept evidence illustrating the empirical behavior of the proposed architectural integration under small-data conditions.

Future research should focus on evaluation across larger, multi-institutional, and more diverse datasets to assess external validity. Additional studies incorporating rigorous statistical testing, external validation cohorts, and clinical workflow assessment will be necessary to determine translational potential and practical deployment readiness.

In summary, this work contributes a disciplined empirical analysis of architectural configurations for breast cancer histopathological image segmentation, emphasizing methodological transparency, controlled benchmarking, and cautious interpretation of findings within data-limited scenarios.
