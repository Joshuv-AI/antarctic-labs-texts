Detecting active Lévy particles using differential dynamic microscopy
Mingyang Li (李明洋), Yu’an Li (李聿安), H. P. Zhang (张何朋), Yongfeng Zhao (赵永峰)

Abstract
Detecting Lévy flights of cells has been a challenging problem in experiments. The challenge lies in accessing data in spatiotemporal scales across orders of magnitude, which is necessary for reliably extracting a power-law scaling. Differential dynamic microscopy has been shown to be a powerful method that allows one to acquire statistics of cell motion across scales, which is a potentially versatile method for detecting Lévy walks in biological systems. In this article, we extend the differential dynamic microscopy method to self-propelled Lévy particles, whose run-time distribution has a algebraic tail. We validate our protocol using synthetic imaging data and show that a reliable detection of active Lévy particles requires accessing length scales of one order of magnitude larger than its persistence length. Applying the protocol to experimental data of E. coli and E. gracilis , we find that E. coli does not exhibit a signature of Lévy walks, while E. gracilis is better described as active Lévy particles.

Introduction

Microorganisms exhibit various patterns of motility to navigate in a complex environment
Taktikos et al. 2013
: Run-and-tumble motion of
Escherichia coli

Berg and Brown 1972
and
Euglena gracilis

Tsang et al. 2018
, run-reverse-flip pattern of several marine bacteria
Taktikos et al. 2013
, and run-reverse-wrap motion of
Pseudonmas putida

Alirezaeizanjani et al. 2020

;
Wadhams and Armitage 2004

;
Angelani 2013
;
Cates and Tailleur 2013
;
Santra et al. 2020
;
Datta et al. 2024

Solon et al. 2015
, the particle runs at constant speed and orientation. Tumbles happen randomly with a constant rate over time, which results in an exponential distribution of run time. Following the term in the literature, we use ”run-and-tumble particles (RTP)” specifically for the model with exponentially distributed run time
Solon et al. 2015
;
Kurzthaler et al. 2024
.

However, experimental evidence suggests that some microorganisms and cells may walk in the Lévy way

;
Harris et al. 2012
;
Ariel et al. 2015
;
Reynolds 2018
;
Figueroa-Morales et al. 2020a
;

;

E. gracilis

Nevertheless, identification of algebraic scaling from experimental data is a notoriously challenging problem

;
Petrovskii et al. 2011
. Power laws suggest scale invariance. Its identification requires measurements over the order-of-magnitude variation in spatiotemporal scales, which is generally a challenging experimental task. Indeed, the consequence of the Lévy walk of

\upmu

Differential dynamic microscopy (DDM) is a high-throughput method that can simultaneously access scales across 2 orders of magnitude
Wilson et al. 2011
;
Martinez et al. 2012
;
Kurzthaler et al. 2024
;
Zhao et al. 2024

f(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\tau)=\langle e^{-i\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(\tau)}\rangle\;.

(1)

\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(\tau)
is the displacement of the particle during the lag time
\tau
in a steady trajectory, and
\bm{\mathrm{k}}
is the wave vector in Fourier space. ISF is the probability density of particle displacement in Fourier space, and thus contains the full information on particle motion. Although ISF is defined from the trajectories of particles, it can be measured from the autocorrelation of density fluctuations of particles without the need to resolve particle trajectories. The field of view usually contains statistics of
10^{4}\sim 10^{6}
particles. The advantage of acquiring high-throughput data across scales may help detect Lévy walk.

In this article, we extend differential dynamic microscopy to detect active Lévy particles. Firstly, we use simulation-generated data to validate the spatial-temporal scales that allow distinguishing an active Lévy particle and an RTP. Next, we analyze the experimental results of

E. gracilis

E. coli

E. gracilis

10^{1}\ \upmu
m to
10^{3}\ \upmu
m.

We consider a paradigmatic model of run-and-tumble-like particles

. The particle switches between running and tumbling states. A running particle moves at a constant speed
v

\tau

\varphi_{R}(\tau)
. A tumbling particle diffuses passively and resumes running after a random tumbling time

\varphi_{T}(\tau^{\prime})

P(v;\bar{v},\sigma_{v})=\frac{v^{Z}}{\Gamma(Z+1)}\left(\frac{Z+1}{\bar{v}}\right)^{Z+1}e^{-(Z+1)v/\bar{v}}\;,

(2)

where
Z=\bar{v}^{2}/\sigma_{v}^{2}-1

D

The two models we consider differ in the asymptotic behavior of
\varphi_{R}(\tau)
. As in the literature
Tailleur and Cates 2008
;
Solon et al. 2015
;
Zhao et al. 2024
, we refer to as run-and-tumble particles (RTPs) the model with exponentially distributed run and tumble time.

\varphi_{R,T}^{\rm RTP}(\tau)=\frac{1}{\tau_{R,T}}\exp\left(-\frac{\tau}{\tau_{R,T}}\right)\;,

(3)

where
\tau_{R,T}
are the mean run and tumble time, respectively.

By contrast,
\varphi_{R}(\tau)
of the active Lévy particles (ALPs) have a power-law tail at large

. The power law must be truncated at small
\tau
to ensure the normalization of
\varphi_{R}(\tau)
. For convenience, we use a Lomax distribution

;
Zaburdaev et al. 2015
;
Figueroa-Morales et al. 2020b
;
Li et al. 2025
to introduce a continuous cutoff at small
\tau
.

\varphi_{R}^{\rm ALP}(\tau)=\frac{\mu-1}{\tau_{0}}\left(1+\frac{\tau}{\tau_{0}}\right)^{-\mu}\;,\ \varphi_{T}^{\rm ALP}(\tau)=\frac{e^{-\tau/\tau_{T}}}{\tau_{T}}\;,

(4)

where
\tau_{0}
is the cutoff time scale. The mean run time is finite for
\mu>2

\tau_{R}=\frac{\tau_{0}}{\mu-2}\;,\ \text{for }\mu>2\;.

II.2
Renewal theory

The ISF (
1
) of RTPs and ALPs can be calculated using renewal theory
Detcheverry 2015
;
Zhao et al. 2024
. We briefly review the renewal theory for self-containing in this section. The stochastic trajectory of a run-and-tumble-like particle is fully determined by the definition of run and tumble time distributions,
\varphi_{R,T}(\tau)
, and run and tumble propagators,
\mathbb{P}_{R,T}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\tau)
. The propagators
\mathbb{P}_{R,T}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\tau)
measure the probability that a particle travels a distance
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
during a time
\tau
in a running or a tumbling phase, respectively. The two models we considered share the same expressions of
\mathbb{P}_{R,T}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\tau)
, which are expressed in Fourier space as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{R}^{\rm 3D}(k,\tau)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}P(v;\bar{v},\sigma_{v})\exp(-Dk^{2}\tau)\frac{\sin(vk\tau)}{vk\tau}\,dv\;,

(6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{R}^{\rm 2D}(k,\tau)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}P(v;\bar{v},\sigma_{v})\exp(-Dk^{2}\tau)J_{0}(vk\tau)\,dv\;,

(7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{T}(k,\tau)=

\displaystyle\exp(-Dk^{2}\tau)\;.

(8)

We note that
\mathbb{P}_{R}
depends on the spatial dimension and
J_{0}(x)
is the 0th-order Bessel function.

Then we denote
P_{R}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\tau)
and
P_{T}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\tau)
as the probability density of the particle displaced by a distance
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
after a delay time
\tau
, which being in the running and tumbling state, respectively. We denote
P_{R,T}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\tau)
as their Fourier transforms. Since the system is isotropic, we drop the angular dependence, and the ISF can then be expressed as

f_{\rm RT}(k,\tau)=P_{R}(k,\tau)+P_{T}(k,\tau)\;,

(9)

where
k=|\bm{\mathrm{k}}|
. Following Ref.
Zhao et al. 2024
,
P_{R,T}(k,\tau)
are given by a set of integral equations.

\displaystyle P_{R}(k,\tau)

\displaystyle\!=\!P_{R}^{0}(k,\tau)\!+\!\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\,R(k,\tau\!-\!t)\varphi_{R}^{0}(t)\mathbb{P}_{R}(k,t)\;,

(10)

\displaystyle R(k,\tau)

\displaystyle\!=\!R^{1}(k,\tau)\!+\!\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\,T(k,\tau\!-\!t)\varphi_{T}(t)\mathbb{P}_{T}(k,t)\;,

(11)

\displaystyle P_{T}(k,\tau)

\displaystyle\!=\!P^{0}_{T}(k,\tau)\!+\!\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\,T(k,\tau\!-\!t)\varphi^{0}_{T}(t)\mathbb{P}_{T}(k,t)\;,

(12)

\displaystyle T(k,\tau)

\displaystyle\!=\!T^{1}(k,\tau)\!+\!\int_{0}^{\tau}dt\,R(k,\tau\!-\!t)\varphi_{R}(t)\mathbb{P}_{R}(k,t)\;.

(13)

\varphi_{R,T}^{0}(\tau)=\int_{\tau}^{\infty}d\tau^{\prime}\,\varphi_{R,T}(\tau^{\prime})
are the probabilities that the run or tumble time exceeds
t
, respectively.
R(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\tau)
and
T(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\tau)
are probabilities that a particle starts to run or tumble at displacement
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
and lag time
\tau
, with
R(k,\tau)
and
T(k,\tau)
being their Fourier transforms.
P^{0}_{R,T}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\tau)
is the probability that the particle reaches
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
at time
\tau
without having tumbled and run in
[0,\tau]
, respectively. We assume that the initial time and position of the particle is arbitrary in its steady trajectory, so that the fraction of running particles
p_{R}=\tau_{R}/(\tau_{R}+\tau_{T})
. Then their Fourier transforms are given by

\displaystyle P^{0}_{R}(k,\tau)

\displaystyle=p_{R}\mathbb{P}_{R}(k,\tau)\int_{\tau}^{\infty}dt\,\varphi_{R}(t)(t-\tau)/\tau_{R}\;,

(14)

\displaystyle P^{0}_{T}(k,\tau)

\displaystyle=(1-p_{R})\mathbb{P}_{T}(k,\tau)\int_{\tau}^{\infty}dt\,\varphi_{T}(t)(t-\tau)/\tau_{T}\;.

(15)

R^{1}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\tau)
and
T^{1}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\tau)
are the probabilities of starting the first run or tumble in displacement
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
at time
\tau
, respectively. Their Fourier transforms are given by

\displaystyle R^{1}(k,\tau)

\displaystyle=(1-p_{R})\mathbb{P}_{T}(k,\tau)\int_{\tau}^{\infty}dt\,\varphi_{T}(t)/\tau_{T}\;,

(16)

\displaystyle T^{1}(k,\tau)

\displaystyle=p_{R}\mathbb{P}_{R}(k,\tau)\int_{\tau}^{\infty}dt\,\varphi_{R}(t)/\tau_{R}\;.

(17)

Then equations (
10
)-(
13

\tilde{s}=s\tau_{0}
and

\displaystyle W^{\rm 2D}(x,y;\nu)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\frac{e^{-xt}J_{0}(yt)}{(1+t)^{\nu}}\;,

(20)

\displaystyle W^{\rm 3D}(x,y;\nu)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\frac{e^{-xt}\sin(yt)}{yt(1+t)^{\nu}}\;.

(21)

III.1
Asymptotic behavior in large scales

Firstly, we analyze the asymptotic behavior of
\tilde{f}(\tilde{k},\tilde{s})
on large scales. We note that
W(x,y;\mu)
is not an analytic function at
x=y=0
, and the asymptotic behavior requires specifying the path of the limit
(x,y)\to(0,0)

Schmiedeberg et al. 2009
. We seek a following rescaling of the temporal and spatial coordinates to larger scales.

\displaystyle\tilde{s}\mapsto b\tilde{s}\;,

\displaystyle\tilde{k}\mapsto b^{\xi}\tilde{k}\;,

(22)

where
1>b>0
is a scaling factor and
\xi>0
is the exponent of spatial coordinates. Because
f(k,\tau)
is invariant upon rescaling,
\tilde{f}
rescales as
\tilde{f}\mapsto b\tilde{f}
. The asymptotic form
g(\tilde{k},\tilde{s})
of the dimensionless ISF is then given by a proper choice of
\xi
such that

\displaystyle g(\tilde{k},\tilde{s}):=\lim_{b\to 0}b\tilde{f}(b^{\xi}\tilde{k},b\tilde{s})

(23)

has a non-trivial dependence on
s
and
k
.

We then expand
W(bx,b^{\xi}y;\nu)
with respect to
b
at finite
x,y>0
. The leading terms are given by

\displaystyle W(bx,b^{\xi}y;\nu)\sim

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\nu-1}-\frac{xb}{(\nu-1)(\nu-2)}+A_{\nu}y^{\nu-1}b^{\xi(\nu-1)}

\displaystyle-\frac{y^{2}b^{2\xi}}{d(\nu-1)(\nu-2)(\nu-3)}+\cdots\;,

(24)

where
\cdots
represents higher-order terms in
b
, and
A_{\nu}
is a factor dependent on spatial dimension
d
.

\displaystyle A_{\nu}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(1/2-\nu/2)}{2^{\nu}\Gamma(1/2+\nu/2)}\;,&d=2\;,\\[12.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2\Gamma(\nu+1)\cos(\pi\nu/2)}\;,&d=3\;.\\
\end{array}\right.

Next, we plug the expansion (
24
) into Eq. (
18
). We denote

\tilde{f}(\tilde{k},\tilde{s})=\frac{N(\tilde{k},\tilde{s})}{D(\tilde{k},\tilde{s})}\;,\ D(\tilde{k},\tilde{s}):=1-(\mu-1)W(\tilde{s},\tilde{k};\mu)\;.

(27)

Letting
\nu=\mu
,
x=\tilde{s}
, and
y=\tilde{k}
in Eq. (
24
), the leading terms in the denominator are

D(\tilde{k},\tilde{s})\sim\frac{\tilde{s}b}{\mu-2}+\frac{\tilde{k}^{2}b^{2\xi}}{d(\mu-2)(\mu-3)}-(\mu-1)A_{\mu}\tilde{k}^{\mu-1}b^{\xi(\mu-1)}\;.

(28)

Similarly, the leading term in the numerator
N(\tilde{k},\tilde{s})
is

N(\tilde{k},\tilde{s})\sim\frac{1}{\mu-2}\;.

(29)

For
\lim_{b\to 0}bN/D
to be finite and non-trivial, one needs non-vanishing
\tilde{k}
-dependent terms in Eq. (
28
). The condition for the leading term in
\tilde{k}
to survive is

\xi=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle 1/(\mu-1)\;,&2<\mu\leq 3\;,\\
\displaystyle 1/2\;,&\mu>3\;.\\
\end{array}\right.

(30)

Then the limit (
23
) gives the following.

g(\tilde{k},\tilde{s})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tilde{s}+K_{\mu}\tilde{k}^{\mu-1}}\;,&2<\mu<3\;,\\[12.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tilde{s}+\tilde{k}^{2}/[d(\mu-3)]}\;,&\mu>3\;,\\[12.0pt]
\end{array}\right.

(31)

where
K_{\mu}:=-(\mu-1)(\mu-2)A_{\mu}
. We note that the scaling function
g(\tilde{k},\tilde{s})
takes a form similar to that of the propagator of 1D Lévy walk particles
Zaburdaev et al. 2015
.

The asymptotic function
g(k,\tau)
can now be calculated by performing the inverse Laplace transform from
\tilde{s}
to
\tilde{t}
and recovering spatial-temporal units,

\log g(k,\tau)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle-K_{\mu}\frac{(v_{0}\tau_{0})^{\mu-1}}{\tau_{0}}k^{\mu-1}\tau\;,&2<\mu<3\;,\\[12.0pt]
\displaystyle\left(-\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\gamma}{2}+\log 2\right)v_{0}^{2}\tau_{0}k^{2}\tau\;,&\mu=3\;,d=2\;,\\[12.0pt]
\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{9}-\frac{\gamma}{3}\right)v_{0}^{2}\tau_{0}k^{2}\tau\;,&\mu=3\;,d=3\;,\\[12.0pt]
\displaystyle-\frac{v_{0}^{2}\tau_{0}}{d(\mu-3)}k^{2}\tau\;,&\mu>3\;,\\[12.0pt]
\end{array}\right.

(32)

where
\gamma\simeq 0.5772
is the Euler’s constant.

We test the asymptotic form (
32
) in particle simulations in Fig.
2
e. We find that at small enough

, the ISFs measured from particle simulations using Eq. (
1
) resemble an exponential function
\exp(-\lambda(k)\tau)
. Fitting
\lambda(k)
of the ISFs with varying

, we find that it follows a scaling

over a wide range of

. However, we note that obtaining the asymptotic function is based on a special rescaling (

) on both temporal and spatial coordinates, which destroys the information on the mean squared displacement (MSD) of particles if
\xi\neq 1/2
for
2<\mu<3
. One can refer to Ref.
Schmiedeberg et al. 2009
for a more detailed discussion on this issue. Thus, the asymptotic function (
32
) fails in the vicinity of
k=0
if
2<\mu<3
and cannot predict the MSD of particles, which we will calculate in Sec.

.
K_{\mu}
in Eq. (

III.2
Differences between ALPs and RTPs reveal in large length scales

We first note that on length scales smaller than
\ell_{p}
, the reorientation of the particles does not take effect, and the ISFs of both ALPs and RTPs converge to that of a straight swimmer, which equals the swimming propagator
\mathbb{P}_{R}
. With
D=0
,
\sigma_{v}=0
, and
\tau_{T}=0
,
\mathbb{P}_{R}=J_{0}(kv_{0}\tau)
in a 2D space (Fig.
2
a-d), which is an oscillating function in
\tau
. With decreasing
k
,
f(k,\tau)
transits from an oscillatory function
\mathbb{P}_{R}
to an exponentially decaying function
g(k,\tau)
. With
\mu
approaching 2 in ALPs, the transition occurs on a larger length scale (Fig.
2
a-d). The qualitative difference in ISFs of ALPs and RTPs is exhibited by the way the oscillation in
f(k,\tau)
decays with an increasing length scale. Thus, the asymptotic behavior of the ISF of ALPs reveals qualitative differences from that of RTPs.

To demonstrate the difference between ALPs and RTPs, let us review the asymptotic behavior of RTP ISF
Martens et al. 2012
. With the same simplification
D=0
,
\sigma_{v}=0
, and
\tau_{T}=0
, and dimensionless variables
\hat{s}:=s\tau_{R}
and
\hat{k}:=kv_{0}\tau_{R}
, the ISF of RTPs in the Fourier-Laplace domain is
\hat{f}_{\rm RTP}(\hat{k},\hat{s}):=f_{\rm RTP}(k,s)/\tau_{R}
and
Martens et al. 2012
;
Zhao et al. 2024

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{\rm 2D}_{\rm RTP}(\hat{k},\hat{s})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{(\hat{s}+1)^{2}+\hat{k}^{2}}-1}\;,

(33)

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{\rm 3D}_{\rm RTP}(\hat{k},\hat{s})=

\displaystyle\frac{\arctan(\hat{k}/(\hat{s}+1))}{\hat{k}-\arctan(\hat{k}/(\hat{s}+1))}\;.

(34)

With the same scaling (
22
), the asymptotic function
g(k,\tau)
of
f_{\rm RTP}(k,s)
exists in the limit
b\to 0
if
\xi=1/2
,

g_{\rm RTP}(k,\tau)=\exp(-v_{0}^{2}\tau_{R}k^{2}\tau/d)\;,

(35)

which takes the form of an ISF of Brownian particles with a
k^{2}\tau
scaling and an effective diffusivity
D^{\rm RTP}_{\rm eff}=v_{0}^{2}\tau_{R}/d
. Fig.
2
d shows that
f_{\rm RTP}(k,\tau)
quickly converges to
g_{\rm RTP}(k,\tau)
on length scales longer than
10^{1}\ell_{p}
, where the persistence length
\ell_{p}=v_{0}\tau_{R}
for both ALPs and RTPs.

ALPs with
2<\mu<3
exhibit an asymptotic behavior distinct from that of RTPs. Despite the scaling
k^{\mu-1}\tau
instead of the scaling
k^{2}\tau
in
g(k,\tau)
, the ISF of ALPs converges to an exponential function on a length scale much longer than that of RTPs (Fig.
2
e). From Fig.
2
a-b, the oscillation in
f(k,\tau)
persists to a length scale of the order
10^{2}\ell_{p}
, one order of magnitude longer than that of RTPs. We note that the oscillations are stronger when
\mu
approaches 2. But on length scales of the order
\ell_{p}\sim 10\ell_{p}
, the ISFs of ALPs are qualitatively different from those of RTPs.

If
\mu>3
, ALPs are like diffusive Brownian particles on large scales. Compared to the ISF of Brownian particles,
\exp(-Dk^{2}\tau)
, it scales as
k^{2}\tau
with an effective diffusion constant

D^{\rm ALP}_{\rm eff}=\frac{v_{0}^{2}\tau_{0}}{d(\mu-3)}=\frac{v_{0}^{2}\tau_{R}(\mu-2)}{d(\mu-3)}\;,\ \ \ \mbox{for}\ \mu>3\;.

(36)

Thus, ALPs with
\mu>3
share the asymptotic behavior similar to that of RTPs. However, compared to RTPs with the same mean run time
\tau_{R}
, we first note that
D^{\rm ALP}_{\rm eff}>D^{\rm RTP}_{\rm eff}
, indicating that ALPs can achieve faster diffusion with the same particle speed and persistence length. Although the ISFs of ALPs with
\mu>3
will finally converge to
g(k,\tau)
in Eq. (
32
) (Fig.
2
e) with
k\to 0
, it occurs on longer length scales than RTPs (Fig.
2
c-d). In length scales of the order
\ell_{p}\sim 10\ell_{p}
, the oscillation in ISFs of ALPs is more profound than that of RTPs.

In summary, ISFs of ALPs and RTPs differ in asymptotic behavior on large scales. In particular, on the length scales of the order
10\ell_{p}
, which is measurable in experiments, ALPs and RTPs already show a significant qualitative difference. Thus, an analysis of ISF on various length scales ranging from the order of
\ell_{p}
to
10\ell_{p}
is expected to detect ALPs from RTPs.

III.3
Mean-squared displacement of ALPs

Eq. (
18
) allows us to calculate the exact mean squared displacement of the simplest ALPs. Conditioned that the time
t=0
is arbitrary in a stable trajectory of a particle, the Laplace transform of the MSD of any spatial coordinate is related to the ISF via

\langle\Delta x^{2}(s)\rangle=-\left.\frac{\partial^{2}f(k,s)}{\partial k^{2}}\right|_{k=0}\;.

(37)

Substituting Eq. (
18
), direct calculation gives

\frac{d}{2}\cdot\frac{\langle\Delta x^{2}(s)\rangle}{v_{0}^{2}\tau_{0}^{3}}=\frac{s\tau_{0}+2-\mu+(\mu-2)(\mu-1)W(\tilde{s},0;\mu)}{(s\tau_{0})^{4}}\;.

(38)

Its inverse Laplace transform can be calculated as

\frac{d}{2}\cdot\langle\Delta x^{2}(t)\rangle=\frac{(1+t/\tau_{0})^{4-\mu}-1}{(\mu-3)(\mu-4)}\left(v_{0}\tau_{0}\right)^{2}+\frac{v_{0}^{2}\tau_{0}t}{\mu-3}\;.

(39)

Eq. (
39
) is exact at any
t
for
\mu>2
and captures the crossover between the short-time and long-time scaling of ALPs. For a short time
t
, we have the expansion for
\mu>2
that

(1+t/\tau_{0})^{4-\mu}-1\sim\frac{4-\mu}{\tau_{0}}t+\frac{(4-\mu)(3-\mu)}{2\tau_{0}^{2}}t^{2}\;,

(40)

so that in the limit
t\to 0
, the particle is ballistic and

d\langle\Delta x^{2}(t)\rangle\underset{t\to 0}{\sim}(v_{0}t)^{2}\;.

(41)

In large time
t
,
(1+t/\tau_{0})^{4-\mu}-1\sim(t/\tau_{0})^{4-\mu}
, and the scaling is dominated by the larger exponent between
4-\mu
and 1. Thus, for
t\to\infty
we have

\frac{d}{2}\cdot\frac{\langle\Delta x^{2}(t)\rangle}{(v_{0}\tau_{0})^{2}}\underset{t\to\infty}{\sim}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\mu-3)(\mu-4)}\left(\frac{t}{\tau_{0}}\right)^{4-\mu}\;,&2<\mu<3\\[12.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{t}{\tau_{0}}\log\left(1+\frac{t}{\tau_{0}}\right)\;,&\mu=3\\[12.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu-3}\left(\frac{t}{\tau_{0}}\right)\;.&\mu>3\\
\end{array}\right.

(42)

For
\mu>3
, the MSD is diffusive, with effective diffusivity
D^{\rm ALP}_{\rm eff}=v_{0}^{2}\tau_{0}/[d(\mu-3)]
, consistent with Eq. (
36
). For
2<\mu<3
, the ALPs are superdiffusive and the MSD scales as
t^{4-\mu}
, which is consistent with the results in the literature
Zaburdaev et al. 2015
. The generalized diffusion constant
\mathcal{D}_{\mu}
, defined as
\langle\Delta x^{2}(t)\rangle=2\mathcal{D}_{\mu}t^{4-\mu}
, is

\mathcal{D}_{\mu}=\frac{v_{0}^{2}\tau_{0}^{\mu-2}}{d(\mu-3)(\mu-4)}\;.

(43)

We note that unlike RTPs and ALPs with
\mu>3
,
\mathcal{D}_{\mu}
is different from the factor in the asymptotic function (
32
). The difference stems from the non-analytic nature of
f(k,s)
at
(k,s)=(0,0)
when
2<\mu<3
. Eq. (
42
) agrees exactly with the particle simulations in all time
t
and
\mu>2
(Fig.
3
).

IV.1
Differential dynamic microscopy

Now we provide a protocol for using differential dynamic microscopy to characterize the motion of cells. We take bright-field time-lapse images of the cells swimming in the liquid. Cells can swim in a quasi-2D or 3D environment. Assuming
I(\bm{\mathrm{r}},t)
is the intensity of pixels at position
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
and time
t
, we calculate

\Delta I(\bm{\mathrm{r}},t)=I(\bm{\mathrm{r}},t)-\langle I(\bm{\mathrm{r}},t)\rangle_{t}\;,

(44)

where
\langle I(\bm{\mathrm{r}},t)\rangle_{t}
is the average intensity of the pixels over time. Then we calculate the Fourier transformation of
\Delta I(\bm{\mathrm{r}},t)
and average over the direction of the wave vector
\bm{\mathrm{k}}
. We assume
\Delta I\propto\Delta\rho
where
\rho
is the density of cells. Instead of calculating the differential intensity correlation function as in Ref.
Wilson et al. 2011
;
Martinez et al. 2012
;
Zhao et al. 2024
, we directly calculate the intermediate scattering function as the autocorrelation function of
\Delta I(k,t)
,

f(k,\tau)=\frac{\langle\Delta I(k,t)\Delta I^{*}(k,t+\tau)\rangle_{t}}{\langle|\Delta I(k,t)|^{2}\rangle_{t}}\;.

(45)

Note that this approach is equivalent to that adopted in Ref.
Wilson et al. 2011
;
Martinez et al. 2012
;
Zhao et al. 2024
but does not require reaching the plateau at
\tau=0
and
\tau\to\infty
. Then the experimentally measured
f(k,\tau)
is fitted by its theoretical prediction

f(k,\tau)=\alpha f_{\rm RT}(k,\tau)+(1-\alpha)\exp(-Dk^{2}\tau)\;,

(46)

where we add a fraction
1-\alpha
of nonmotile diffusive cells with the same diffusion constant
D
as motile cells, and
f_{\rm RT}(k,\tau)
is the ISF of ALPs or RTPs calculated using Eq. (
9
) and the renewal theory.

To characterize the motion of cells across scales, we fit simultaneously
f(k,\tau)
with multiple
k
values that range across scales. The choices of
k
should include those on small length scales
\sim\ell_{p}
, which encode swimming properties, and those on large length scales
\sim 5\ell_{p}
, which capture the asymptotic behavior of
f(k,\tau)
on large scales. We note that the smallest
k
accessible experimentally is determined by the image size in quasi-2D systems or the depth of fields in 3D systems
Martinez et al. 2012
, which should be orders of magnitude larger than
\ell_{p}
. The largest
k
is restricted by the pixel size of the images. As visualization of a single cell is not necessary, using a pixel size between the body size of the cell and
\ell_{p}
should be sufficient.

IV.2
Validations of the method using simulation-generated data

We first validate our protocol using simulations, where we know the ground truth of cell motion. To simulate active Lévy particles, we sequentially sample the time
\tau
of the next run or tumble event, according to the corresponding probability distributions
\varphi_{R,T}(\tau)
. The particle position is then updated towards the next snapshot time or
\tau
. We assume that the particles are initially stationary and distributed uniformly in the simulation space. We note that unlike RTPs where the run and tumble processes are Poissonian, the initial run-time distribution of ALPs is not the Lomax distribution
\varphi_{R}^{\rm ALP}(\tau)
(
4

Detcheverry 2015

\varphi_{R,0}^{\rm ALP}(\tau):=\int_{\tau}^{\infty}\frac{\varphi_{R}^{\rm ALP}(t)}{\tau_{R}}dt=\frac{\mu-2}{\tau_{0}(1+\tau/\tau_{0})^{\mu-1}}

(47)

Then we generate artificial imaging data using the same method as in Ref.
Zhao et al. 2024
.

IV.1
.

2.05

3.5

L=4096

while

VI
Summary and discussion

In this article, we generalized the differential dynamic microscopy method to detect and characterize active Lévy particles. We first analyze the asymptotic behavior of the intermediate scattering function of active Lévy particles. We show that the ISF follows a scaling
k^{\mu-1}\tau
over a wide range of
k
with
2<\mu<3
, which differs qualitatively from the scaling
k^{2}\tau
of classical run-and-tumble particles at small wave numbers. The oscillation of the ISF of ALPs persists at length scales of the order
10\ell_{p}
, where the ISF of RTPs converges to an exponential function. This allows us to detect the difference between ALPs and RTPs in experiments. To demonstrate an application of ISF, we calculate the mean squared displacement of ALPs from its analytical ISF, which is exact for all time
t
and exponent
\mu>2
. The exact analytical expression of MSD clearly shows the crossover from a ballistic
t^{2}
regime and a superdiffusive
t^{4-\mu}
regime for
2<\mu<3
.

Then we propose a protocol to measure and analyze the ISFs of active particles from experiments. To capture the asymptotic behavior of ISFs, data with multiple wavenumber
k
ranging over lengths scales from
1\ell_{p}
to
10\ell_{p}
need to be fitted simultaneously. We test the protocol using synthetic image data and show that it can reliably detect ALPs and is robust under the particular range of length scales used in fitting. The method can extract the kinetic parameters within 10% relative error from the ground truth.

Finally, we apply our protocol to the experimental data of
E. coli
and
E. gracilis
. We find that although the ALP model can fit the ISFs of
E. coli
in certain cases, the estimates of the exponent
\mu
is unstable and unreasonably larger than 3. In contrary, the ISFs of
E. gracilis
are better fitted by the ALP model than the classic RTP model. These findings support the previous reports in Ref.
Kurzthaler et al. 2024
and
Li et al. 2025
:
E. coli
enters a diffusive regime on a length scale of the order
400\ \upmu
m, while
E. gracilis
exhibits Lévy walk at least up to a length scale of the order
10^{3}\ \upmu
m.

Our method provides a high-throughput alternative for detecting and characterizing active Lévy particles. We highlight the importance of acquiring and analyzing data across scales. The characteristics of Lévy walk are encoded in the asymptotic behavior of the intermediate scattering function of the particles, which can be captured only if ISFs of a wide range of wavenumbers are measured and analyzed simultaneously. The differential dynamic microscopy provides a versatile method for accessing data on a large length scale.
