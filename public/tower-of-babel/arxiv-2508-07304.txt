From Knowledge to Conjectures: A Modal Framework for Reasoning about Hypotheses
Fabio Vitali

Abstract
This paper introduces a new family of cognitive modal logics designed to formalize conjectural reasoning: a modal system in which cognitive contexts extend known facts with hypothetical assumptions to explore their consequences. Unlike traditional doxastic and epistemic systems, conjectural logics rely on a principle, called Axiom C ( φ → □ ​ φ \varphi\rightarrow\Box\varphi ), that ensures that all established facts are preserved across hypothetical layers. While Axiom C was dismissed in the past due to its association with modal collapse, we show that the collapse only arises under classical and bivalent assumptions, and specifically in the presence of Axiom T . Hence we avoid Axiom T and adopt a paracomplete semantic framework, grounded in Weak Kleene logic or Description Logic, where undefined propositions coexist with modal assertions. This prevents the modal collapse and guarantees a layering to distinguish between factual and conjectural statements. Under this framework we define new modal systems, e.g., 𝐊𝐂 \mathbf{KC} and 𝐊𝐃𝐂 \mathbf{KDC} , and show that they are complete, decidable, and robust under partial knowledge. Finally, we introduce a dynamic operation, 𝗌𝖾𝗍𝗍𝗅𝖾 ⁡ ( φ ) \mathsf{settle}(\varphi) , which formalizes the transition from conjecture to accepted fact, capturing the event of the update of a world’s cognitive state through the resolution of uncertainty.



Keywords:
Modal logic, epistemic logic, doxastic logic, weak Kleene logic, description logic, modal collapse

1
Introduction

We call
cognitive modal logics
a family of systems designed to model subjective informational states, i.e., agents’ beliefs, knowledge, or hypothesis, rather than absolute necessity/possibility, obligation/permission or temporal features.
The two best-known families of cognitive logics are:
doxastic logics
, that formalize belief states, i.e., possibly incorrect propositions held by an agent, where very little is assumed to hold in these systems, and even internal consistency is an optional condition; and
epistemic logics
, that formalize knowledge, i.e., propositions that are believed and also true: agents know a subset of all truths, and epistemic logics represent accurate but partial access to reality.

Cognitive modal logics are peculiar for some structural characteristics: first, their semantics explicitly allow for a separation between reality and subjective states. Second, they admit and represent multiple independent modal systems, i.e., one per agent or cognitive stance, each governed by its own relations and valuations. Third, they are typically centered on the main operator (i.e.,
\Box
, or
K
, or
B
) to capture formulas cognitively associated to a subjective perspective, while the secondary operator (i.e.,
\Diamond
) plays a minimal or negligible role. Finally, rather than treating the addition of modal axioms as increasing refinements of a single system, cognitive logics treat axioms, and combinations thereof, as the foundation of qualitatively distinct reasoning modes.

We believe that these two families are not the only relevant approaches to cognitive logics and there is ample room for additional varieties. This paper introduces a third category:
conjectural logics
. These are designed to model hypothetical reasoning based on true information: agents have access to all currently known facts, and adopt in addition one or more unverified assumptions, in order to explore their possible consequences.

Formally, conjectural logics are governed by a single modal axiom stipulating that all established predicates are adopted within the hypothetical scenario, in addition to non-established ones that are assumed as well. From this axiom, new logics can be drawn in which conjectures supplement facts, cognitive layers are constructed modally, and each new assumption defines a distinct cognitive perspective.

This paper introduces therefore a family of modal logics based on an axiom, referred here as Axiom
C
, which takes the form
\varphi\rightarrow\Box\varphi
. Axiom
C
is actually well-established in literature. It has been considered at least since the mid-20th century in the context of alethic logic, most notably in Kurt Gödel’s notes on the ontological argument, where necessary existence of certain positive properties is postulated to follow from their actual existence (cf.
[
Gö70
]
;
[
BS25
]
).

Despite its early appearance, Axiom
C
has traditionally been considered with some caution due to its association with the phenomenon known as
modal collapse
. Modal collapse occurs when modal distinctions are lost. Mathematically, this means that the modal operator
\Box
becomes becomes indistinguishable from the identity operator, so that, for every formula
\varphi
, we have
\varphi\leftrightarrow\Box\varphi
: this means that the distinction between modal and non-modal truths collapses, and the modal operator becomes logically trivial.
Within an alethic perspective, modal collapse implies that all true propositions are
necessarily true
: every actual fact becomes metaphysically necessary, leaving no room for contingency or possibility. This has deep implications in metaphysics and theology, as it would eliminate the conceptual space in which possible alternative states of reality could meaningfully be imagined.

However, we observe that the Axiom
C
alone does not bring to modal collapse. Rather, the collapse arises from the conjunction of Axiom
C
and Axiom
T
,
and
under the assumption that the base logic is both classical and bivalent. In such a setting, the evaluation of all well-formed formulas, modal and non-modal alike, is governed by a total valuation function assigning exactly one of two values, true or false, to each formula, and the truth value of non-modal formulas are considered fully determined in the base world, while modal formulas are entirely determined by the values of
\varphi
across accessible worlds. The joint presence of Axioms
C
and
T
leads therefore to the equivalence
\varphi\leftrightarrow\Box\varphi
, making modal formulas indistinguishable from their non-modal counterparts and, eliminating any asymmetry between levels.

To prevent the collapse, it is necessary to weaken these constraints. Two broad approaches are available: one may allow for modal inconsistency, so that some modal formulas
\Box\varphi
and their modal negations
\Box\neg\varphi
may both be true, thus departing from classical negation at the modal level; alternatively, and more interesting for cognitive modeling, one may weaken the base logic to allow for some non-modal formulas to be neither true nor false.

This second approach leads to paracomplete semantics, such as those of Weak Kleene logic
[
Kle52
]
, in which atomic formulas are allowed to take three distinct truth values: true, false, and undefined. A key feature of this logic is that undefinedness is preserved compositionally: if any subformula of a compound formula is undefined, then the entire formula is also undefined. For instance, if
\varphi
is undefined, then so are
\neg\varphi
,
\varphi\wedge\psi
, and
\varphi\rightarrow\psi
, regardless of the value of
\psi
.

Within a paracomplete framework, it is entirely possible for a formula
\varphi
to lack a truth value in the base world, while the modal formula
\Box\varphi
is well-defined. Since the evaluation of
\Box\varphi
depends on the values of
\varphi
in all accessible worlds, regardless of the base world, it can acquire a determinate truth value even when the non-modal
\varphi
is undefined. As a result, neither the inference
\Box\varphi\to\varphi
can be assessed, nor the reverse implication
\varphi\to\Box\varphi
. The equivalence
\varphi\leftrightarrow\Box\varphi
, that brings about modal collapse, in general fails under Weak Kleene semantics: the modal and non-modal layers of the language are logically decoupled, and the logical preconditions for modal collapse are no longer satisfied. This provides a principled and robust path for using Axiom
C
in modal systems.

The possibility of well-formed, yet undefined statements also can be found in description logics
[
BCM+03
]
, which adopt an open-world assumption (OWA): the absence of information about a formula
\varphi
does not justify the conclusion that
\varphi
is false, but instead leaves its truth value undetermined. In this framework, knowledge bases are considered incomplete by default, and reasoning proceeds with the understanding that additional information may yet be discovered or inferred. For instance, given the axioms
\mathsf{Painter}\sqsubseteq\mathsf{Person}
,
\mathsf{Painter}(\mathsf{Leonardo})
, and
\mathsf{Person}(\mathsf{John})
, the proposition
\mathsf{Painter}(\mathsf{John})
is neither entailed nor refuted: it is simply unknown under the current state of information. For our purposes, this form of semantic partiality mirrors paracomplete logics such as Weak Kleene: well formed propositions may be enunciated whose truth value is undefined. In both frameworks, modal assertions such as
\Box\varphi
need not reflect the precise truth value of
\varphi
in the base world. Instead, modal statements are evaluated with respect to their behavior in accessible interpretations or cognitive states, allowing the logic to maintain a distinction between the modal and non-modal levels.

The semantics of the modality operator also needs to be better understood. In standard alethic modal logic, axiom
T
is central to the interpretation of
\Box
as "necessity," as it ensures that all necessary truths are actually true. But in the absence of
T
, and within a logic where some propositions are truth-valueless, any alethic interpretation becomes conceptually empty.
In this paper, we focus on cognitive modal logics instead. Under this interpretation, Axiom
C
enables the construction of modal levels in which a base layer of established facts is augmented by a higher layer of conjectures: well-formed formulas that are neither true nor false in the base logic, but which are assumed as true under the modal operator.

This allows us to define systems such as:

•

KC45
: conjectural modal logic with seriality, transitivity, and Euclideanness,

•

KDC45
: rational conjectural logic, extending KC45 with consistency,

alongside standard cognitive logics such as doxastic (
K45
), rational doxastic (
KD45
), and epistemic (
KT45
).
We are able to prove that
KC45
and
KDC45
are complete, compact, decidable, and conservative extensions of
K45
and
KD45
, respectively. We also show that modal collapse is avoided in these systems under paracomplete semantics, provided that Axiom
T
is not added.

Finally, we propose a path for dynamic extension by introducing a dynamic modal logic event,
\mathsf{settle}(\varphi)
, that transforms conjectural assertions into factual ones, and upgrades the current factual state into an epistemic base for a next stage of base truths. This dynamic view captures transitions from hypotheses to beliefs, and from beliefs to knowledge, across time or reasoning cycles.

A Brief History of Axiom
C
and the Modal Collapse

The schema
\varphi\rightarrow\Box\varphi
, which we call Axiom
C
, has long been recognized as a structurally powerful but philosophically controversial principle. While not explicitly formalized in early modal systems such as Lewis’s
\mathbf{S5}

[
LL32
]
, its logical consequences were implicit in attempts to model necessity as truth across all possible worlds. A particularly authoritative formulation appears in Gödel’s ontological proof manuscript, unpublished and undated but probably written around 1970
[
Gö70
]
, where he asserts that positive properties, once instantiated, are necessarily instantiated. This principle, central to his modal argument for the existence of God, effectively formulas related to Axiom
C

[
AG96
]
.

In classical bivalent modal logics, the combination of Axiom
C
with Axiom
\mathbf{T}
leads directly to modal collapse, where all truths are necessarily true, and contingency disappears.
This collapse is viewed with suspicion in metaphysics. Alethic interpretations, where
\Box\varphi
reads as “
\varphi
is necessarily true”, render C a radical metaphysical thesis. Critics such as Oppy
[
Opp95
]
and Sobel
[
Sob04
]
argue that modal collapse undermines modal realism, erasing the meaningful difference between possible worlds.

Kovač
[
Kov12
]
traces the collapse in Gödel’s ontological proof to the use of rigid designation and related principles—specifically, the assumption that the same variables and terms refer to the same entities across all possible worlds. Under these assumptions, including identity axioms and the Converse Barcan Formula, the implication
\varphi\rightarrow\Box\varphi
becomes inescapable.

A more constructive reinterpretation arises in provability logic, particularly in Solovay’s work
[
Sol76
]
, where
\Box\varphi
is read as “
\varphi
is provable.” Here, adopting Axiom
C
would assert that provable statements are necessarily provable, a result that is carefully avoided.

In doxastic and epistemic logics, the reading of
\Box
shifts to “it is believed” or “it is known.” Hintikka’s
\mathbf{KD45}
system
[
Hin62
]
models belief with operators
B
, but deliberately avoids axioms like
B\varphi\rightarrow\Box B\varphi
, reflecting the fallibility of realistic agents. Beliefs can be true yet not believed to be necessarily true.

Later work, especially by Fagin et al.
[
FHMV95
]
, explores Axiom
C
in the context of idealized agents with perfect logical reasoning. In such models, agents possess universal accessibility, deductive closure, and no error. Within this normative framework, a restricted form of C can arise naturally, not as a cognitive description but as a regulative ideal. Logics including Axiom
C
become plausible as formalizations of how perfectly rational agents should reason—though they may not model any actual agent.

This marks a shift from descriptive realism, which tolerates error and belief revision, to ideal rationality, which assumes full deductive and modal closure. In this context,
\varphi\rightarrow\Box\varphi
is not a metaphysical necessity but a principle of ideal coherence of belief systems.

Related principles emerge in logics of reflection and knowability. In autoepistemic logic, where an idealized agent reasons about its own beliefs, closure principles ensure belief stability
[
Moo85
,
Var86
]
. In justification logic, axioms like
t:\varphi\rightarrow\varphi
and their modal analogs express the idea that knowledge or belief is backed by reasons, and reflection axioms support forms of Axiom
C

[
AN05
,
Wil00
]
.

More recently, in logics of essence and accident
[
GV20
]
, a form of C is reinterpreted via the
essence operator

\circ
. The formula
\varphi\rightarrow\circ\varphi
asserts that
\varphi
holds essentially when it remains true in all worlds agreeing with the current one on relevant facts. This avoids collapse by relativizing necessity to a weaker similarity relation rather than standard accessibility.

Alternative formulations of modal logic avoid Axiom
C
by modifying the proof system itself. These systems belong to the family of non-normal modal logics, which relax or reject some of the axioms or inference rules typical of standard modal systems like K, T, or S4, allowing a broader range of modal behaviors.

Marcos
[
Mar05
]
discusses
paranormal logics
, logics which are neither complete (
paracomplete
), nor consistent (
paraconsistent
), which validate C-like axioms without full classical semantics. These systems relax structural assumptions on frames and entailment to preserve a hierarchy between actual and necessary truths.

Intuitionistic modal logics provide another route. In such systems, the valuation of a well-formed formula depends on the existence of a constructive proof, and formulas may therefore be neither true nor false. In this context, Liao
[
Lia23
]
constructs canonical systems that inherently block modal collapse by rejecting classical inference schemas, while Lewitzka
[
Lew17
]
combines classical and intuitionistic logic via modal amalgams in which
\Box\varphi\rightarrow\varphi
is usually blocked by design.

In model transformation logics, Fervari et al.
[
Fer14
]
explore how changing the structure of the model affects the validity of modal formulas. Transformations such as merging two possible worlds (point merging) or adding/removing accessibility links (link alteration) simulate how an agent’s perspective evolves. These operations can be used to reproduce the effects of modal collapse or to block them, depending on how the accessibility relation is modified. The validity of axioms such as C thus becomes sensitive not only to the logical system, but to the internal dynamics of the model.

Negri and Orlandelli
[
NO19
]
developed
labeled sequent calculi
for quantified modal logics that do not assume standard normality conditions such as necessitation or distribution. Formulas are annotated with labels representing possible worlds, and inference rules explicitly manipulate both formulas and accessibility relations. This framework allows fine-grained tracking of which formulas hold where and under what assumptions. Here, Axiom
C
is not derivable unless explicitly postulated, which ensures that modal collapse does not occur automatically and makes modal reasoning more transparent and modular.

Girard
[
GW19
]
offers a modal framework without contraction rules, structurally limiting derivations that would otherwise enable
\varphi\rightarrow\Box\varphi
, and controlling the derivability of C via syntactic constraints.

3
Conjectural Logics

Adopting ourselves a non-normal approach to modal logics, in what follows we assume a base logic—either propositional or description-oriented—that permits well-formed formulas (WFFs) to be
neither true nor false
, e.g., a paracomplete logic. This condition is satisfied, for example, by three-valued systems such as Weak Kleene logic, or by open-world semantics in description logics. In our setting we use
\top
,
\bot
, and
\mathbb{u}
to denote the values true, false, and undefined, respectively. We remain agnostic about the precise choice of base logic until Section
5
, where two specific instances will be formally introduced, indeed Weak Kleene and Description Logic.

Paracomplete semantics offer a principled way to prevent the equivalence
\varphi\leftrightarrow\Box\varphi
, without the complexity of paranormal logics
[
Mar05
]
, where the semantic interpretation of the modal operator is weakened, detaching the necessity operator from standard Kripke frame conditions and redefining modal validity in terms of non-classical entailment rather than truth preservation across accessible worlds.

3.1
Cognitive Modal Logics and Accessibility Conditions

A
modal logic
extends a base logic by introducing a unary modal operator
\Box
. The semantics of
\Box
are defined in terms of an underlying
Kripke frame
, abstracting away from the specific evaluation rules of the base logic. Accordingly:

Definition 3.1
.

A
Kripke frame
is a pair
\langle W,R\rangle
, where:

•

W
is a non-empty set of worlds.

•

R\subseteq W\times W
is a binary
accessibility relation
over
W
.

A
Kripke model
adds a valuation function, mapping each atomic formula to a truth value (e.g.,
\top
,
\bot
, or
\mathbb{u}
) at each world. Being agnostic about the specific base logic, we assume only that
\Box\varphi
at world
w
is interpreted as: "
\varphi
holds in all worlds
w^{\prime}
such that
wRw^{\prime}
", under the semantics of the base logic
[
BdRV01
]
.

We also point out that, in a paracomplete setting, the accessibility relation
R
may be interpreted as an
extension of definedness
:
wRw^{\prime}
indicates that world
w^{\prime}
preserves all the truth assignments defined at
w
but may assign additional ones. This interpretation aligns naturally with contexts of partial information or open domains, where moving to accessible worlds reflects the progressive enrichment of knowledge or definedness.

Cognitive modal logics (such as doxastic and epistemic modal logics) are further distinguished by their treatment of agents: rather than a single undifferentiated modal space, they typically model a family of distinct modal operators
\Box_{i}
, each indexed by an agent
i
. Each agent
i
has an associated accessibility relation
R_{i}
, encoding their perspective, belief state, or informational reach. This agent-indexed approach is central to cognitive modal logics and supports formal reasoning about distributed knowledge, beliefs, and awareness in multi-agent systems
[
FHMV95
]
, including disagreement, partial knowledge, and subjective uncertainty.

Standard modal logics also often define a second operator,
\Diamond\varphi\equiv\neg\Box\neg\varphi
, that affords an interpretation in alethic logic as expressing the
possibility
of
\varphi
[
vB10
]
. However, in cognitive modal contexts, this duality becomes less natural: for instance, "not believing that
\varphi
is false" does not entail "believing that
\varphi
is true" or even simply "considering
\varphi
" in any meaningful way. Because of these interpretive difficulties, and the lack of a cognitively precise semantics for
\Diamond
, we will use only the
\Box
operator.

We briefly recall the main axioms and their associated frame conditions:

•

(K)

\Box(\varphi\rightarrow\psi)\rightarrow(\Box\varphi\rightarrow\Box\psi)
: the distribution axiom, valid in all Kripke frames. It ensures that
\Box
behaves normally under logical implication.

•

(D)

\Box\varphi\rightarrow\neg\Box\neg\varphi
: seriality, ensuring that
\varphi
is not believed unless it holds in at least one accessible world. Semantically, this prevents inconsistency in belief (no agent believes a contradiction).

•

(T)

\Box\varphi\rightarrow\varphi
: reflexivity, ensuring that the actual world is accessible from itself. Epistemically, this axiom is often read as "only truths can be known."

•

(4)

\Box\varphi\rightarrow\Box\Box\varphi
: transitivity, supporting positive introspection—if something is believed/known, then the agent believes/knows that it is believed/known.

•

(5)

\neg\Box\varphi\rightarrow\Box\neg\Box\varphi
: Euclidean condition, supporting negative introspection—if something is not known, then the agent knows that it is not known.

Their semantic roles are standard in the classification of normal modal logics
[
CZ97
,
BdRV01
]
. These axioms generate well-known modal systems used to model different cognitive states:

•

Doxastic logic
is typically associated with system
K
or
K45
, modeling basic belief.

•

Rational doxastic logic
adopts system
KD
or
KD45
, enforcing belief consistency. These systems reflect the reasoning of ideal agents with logically coherent, stable belief systems.

•

Epistemic logic is modeled by
KT45
, assuming that knowledge is true (
\Box\varphi\rightarrow\varphi
), and that agents are introspectively aware of both their knowledge and ignorance.

Validity.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\langle W,R_{i},V\rangle
be a Kripke model for agent
i
, with
V
a valuation function appropriate to the base logic. We write:

•

Model
\mathcal{M}
at world
w

entails
formula
\varphi
for agent
i
(written
\mathcal{M},w\vDash_{i}\varphi
) if
\varphi
holds true in
\mathcal{M}
at
w
according to agent
i
.

•

\mathcal{M},w\vDash_{i}\varphi
if formula
\varphi
is
satisfied
by agent
i
at world
w
.

•

\varphi
is
valid at world

w
for agent
i
if
\mathcal{M},w\vDash_{i}\varphi
.

•

\varphi
is
valid in

\mathcal{M}
for agent
i
if
\mathcal{M},w\vDash_{i}\varphi
for all
w\in W
.

•

\varphi
is
valid in a frame

\langle W,R_{i}\rangle
if it is valid in every model built on that frame.

Provability.

Given a deductive system
\Sigma
over a base logic with modal operator
\Box_{i}
, we write:

•

\Sigma\vdash\varphi
if
\varphi
is
provable
from the axioms and rules of
\Sigma
.

•

\Sigma
is
sound
with respect to a class of frames if every provable formula is valid in every corresponding frame.

•

\Sigma
is

if every valid formula is provable.

3.2
Definedness and Cognitive Accessibility

In paracomplete modal logics, not all formulas receive a definite truth value. This necessitates a distinction between formulas that are
defined
at a world and those that are not. We formalize this by introducing definedness domains and valuation maps.

Definition 3.2

(Defined Formulas)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a Kripke model and
w
a world in
\mathcal{M}
. The set of defined formulas in
w
is:

\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{M}}_{w}=\left\{\,\varphi\ \middle|\ \mathcal{M},w\vDash\varphi\text{ or }\mathcal{M},w\vDash\neg\varphi\,\right\}

Definition 3.3

(Valuation Map)
.

The valuation of
w
is the partial function that assigns to each defined formula its truth value in
w
:

\widehat{\mathcal{V}}^{\mathcal{M}}_{w}:\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{M}}_{w}\to\{\top,\bot\}

When the model
\mathcal{M}
is clear from context, we omit it and simply write
\mathcal{D}_{w}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{w}
.

The valuation map
\widehat{\mathcal{V}}_{w}
over all well-formed formulas is derived compositionally from the valuation of the defined primitives of
w
, following the semantics of the base logic. Thus we can define a reduced valuation function based on just the primitives in
\mathcal{M}
that completely captures the valuation of
w
.

Definition 3.4

(Primitive Valuation)
.

Let
\mathbb{P}
be the set of primitive formulas in
\mathcal{M}
. The
primitive valuation
at
w
is a partial function assigning truth values to (some) primitive propositions at
w
:

\mathcal{V}_{w}\subseteq\mathbb{P}\times\{\top,\bot\}

For readability, we will use
p
to denote the pair
(p,\top)
and
!p
for the pair
(p,\bot)
so that we can adopt a shorthand for
\mathcal{V}_{w}
as follows:

\mathcal{V}_{w}=\{\,p,q,\dots,{!}r,\dots\,\}

.

Definition 3.5

(Definedness-Preserving Extension)
.

A world
w^{\prime}
is a definedness-preserving extension of
w
if:

\mathcal{V}_{w}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{w^{\prime}}

Definition 3.6

(Definedness-Preserving Accessibility)
.

An accessibility relation
R
preserves definedness if:

wRw^{\prime}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathcal{V}_{w}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{w^{\prime}}

This constraint ensures that accessible worlds are cognitively plausible refinements of the current state: they may define more formulas but cannot contradict already evaluated ones. Thus, the modal operator
\Box
guarantees stability after the expansion of information availability. A formula
\Box\varphi
holds at
w
iff
\varphi
holds in all definedness-preserving extensions of
w
, i.e., in all
w^{\prime}
such that
wRw^{\prime}
.

3.3
Axiom
C

We define Axiom
C
as the following modal schema:

\textbf{(C)}\quad\varphi\rightarrow\Box\varphi

(1)

As discussed previously, in classical modal logic this axiom is rarely adopted, since it leads directly to modal collapse when combined with Axiom
T
(
\Box\varphi\rightarrow\varphi
). However, in our setting — without
T
and based on a paracomplete base logic — the collapse is not guaranteed, and the behavior of Axiom
C
must be reassessed accordingly.

In Kripke-style semantics,
\Box\varphi
is interpreted as: “
\varphi
holds in all accessible worlds.” Formally, for a model
\mathcal{M}=\langle W,R,V\rangle
and a world
w\in W
, this means:

\mathcal{M},w\vDash\Box\varphi\quad\text{iff}\quad\forall w^{\prime}\text{ such that }wRw^{\prime},\ \mathcal{M},w^{\prime}\vDash\varphi

(2)

Under
C
, by the semantics of implication,
\varphi\rightarrow\Box\varphi
is satisfied at a world
w
if
either

\varphi
is not satisfied at
w
,
or

\Box\varphi
is satisfied at
w
. In other words, satisfaction of
\varphi\rightarrow\Box\varphi
at
w
guarantees that whenever
\varphi
is satisfied at
w
, then
\Box\varphi
is also satisfied at
w
:

\text{If }\mathcal{M},w\vDash\varphi,\text{ then }\mathcal{M},w\vDash\Box\varphi

(3)

Now, applying the semantics of the
\Box
operator, this expands to the following condition:

\text{If }\mathcal{M},w\vDash\varphi,\text{ then }\forall w^{\prime}\text{ such that }wRw^{\prime},\ \mathcal{M},w^{\prime}\vDash\varphi

(4)

That is, if
\mathcal{M},w\vDash\varphi
, then for every
w^{\prime}
such that
wRw^{\prime}
, it must also hold that
\mathcal{M},w^{\prime}\vDash\varphi
. In our framework, this propagation is not enforced by properties of
R
alone (such as reflexivity or transitivity), but by the interaction between
R
and the model’s valuation structure. More precisely, what Axiom
C
demands is that for any formula
\varphi
satisfied at
w
, its satisfaction is preserved along all accessible worlds. This motivates the following:

Definition 3.7
.

A Kripke model
\mathcal{M}
satisfies the
C-propagation condition
at
w
if for every formula
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}_{w}
such that
\mathcal{V}_{w}(\varphi)=\top
, it holds that
\mathcal{V}_{w^{\prime}}(\varphi)=\top
for all
w^{\prime}
such that
wRw^{\prime}
.

This condition amounts to a form of definedness preservation: formulas that are satisfied in
w
must remain satisfied in all definedness-extensions of
w
. Therefore, the semantic force of Axiom
C
lies in requiring
R
to point only toward those worlds
w^{\prime}
such that the pair
\langle\varphi,\top\rangle
in
\mathcal{V}_{w}
is also present in
\mathcal{V}_{w^{\prime}}
for all relevant
\varphi
.

Thus Axiom
C
governs the structural coherence between a world and its accessible conjectural enrichments: no conjectural state can retract truths already asserted. This interpretation is not only compatible with paracomplete logics but is in fact tailored to them, since it never requires truth to be extended, but only preserved. This encodes a constraint on the interaction between asserted facts and conjectural reasoning: the formula
\varphi
represents a fact or statement that is asserted in the current context, and, through Axiom
C
, the formula
\varphi\rightarrow\Box\varphi
then expresses that any accepted fact must also be defined in all conjectural contexts, expanding the current state with tentative truths, while preserving all the defined ones.

This role becomes especially meaningful in paracomplete logics: since truth and falsity are not exhaustive, the inference from
\varphi
to
\Box\varphi
does not enforce global necessity, but rather stipulates a propagation of defined truth where applicable. As such, the inclusion of
C
does not trigger collapse, even when paired with introspective or serial axioms.

3.4
Interaction of Axiom
C
with Standard Modal Axioms

Axiom
C
can be consistently added to a variety of modal systems. However, its interaction with other axioms, especially
T
, can introduce semantic consequences that range from non-trivial coherence constraints to full modal collapse. In this section, we analyze these interactions with respect to frame conditions and model behavior, under the assumption that the base logic is non-classical and permits undefinedness.

C with K.

The minimal modal logic
K
requires only that
\Box
distributes over implication (Axiom
K
). Since
C
imposes no specific constraints on the accessibility relation
R
, it is consistent with
K
. In this setting,
\Box\varphi
denotes propagation of satisfaction across accessible worlds, and Axiom
C
ensures that satisfaction of
\varphi
at any world
w
must propagate through
R
. The resulting logic,
KC
, is characterized semantically by closure under
R
: if
\mathcal{M},w\vDash\varphi
, then
\mathcal{M},w^{\prime}\vDash\varphi
for all
w^{\prime}
such that
wRw^{\prime}
. This is not a standard frame property, but rather a valuation propagation condition. The system
KC
is thus well-formed and non-trivial.

C with D.

Adding axiom
D
,
\Box\varphi\rightarrow\neg\Box\neg\varphi
, requires that the accessibility relation
R
be serial: for every world
w
, there exists at least one
w^{\prime}
such that
wRw^{\prime}
. This guarantees that if a formula is accepted in all accessible contexts, then its negation cannot also be accepted in all accessible contexts. When added to
KC
, i.e.,
KDC
, Axiom
C
ensures propagation of defined truths, while Axiom
D
prevents vacuous modal truth, i.e., cases where
\Box\varphi
holds merely because no accessible worlds exist. In a paracomplete setting, this combination ensures that
\Box\varphi
cannot be trivially satisfied by vacuity, and that C-propagation occurs over a non-empty image of
R
. This aligns well with interpretations where accessibility reflects cognitive reach and definitional extension
[
FHMV95
]
.

C with T: modal collapse.

Axiom
T
,
\Box\varphi\rightarrow\varphi
, enforces reflexivity on
R
: each world must access itself. When added to
C
, it creates the bi-implication
\varphi\leftrightarrow\Box\varphi
which erases the modal distinction: a formula is satisfied iff it is upheld in all cognitively accessible contexts. Such a collapse destroys the modal layering that the conjectural interpretation of C is meant to preserve. In classical modal logic, this result is considered pathological and referred to as
modal collapse
. In our framework we explicitly reject this configuration by avoiding to adopt
T
,.

C with 4 and 5.

Axiom
4
,
\Box\varphi\rightarrow\Box\Box\varphi
, imposes transitivity on
R
and captures the idea of positive conjectural persistence: if a formula remains satisfied across conjectural extensions, this stability itself should be stable. Axiom
5
,
\neg\Box\varphi\rightarrow\Box\neg\Box\varphi
, asserts that the failure of conjectural propagation is itself conjecturally propagated. That is, if
\varphi
fails to hold in some accessible world, then this failure must be preserved across all further conjectural extensions. Within our framework, this amounts to a form of
negative introspection
: the agent conjecturally acknowledges the limits of what can be safely conjectured.
When combined with Axiom
C
, both 4 and 5 reinforce structural coherence without enforcing reflexivity or triggering collapse. They ensure that conjectural extensions behave consistently across levels of propagation, but they do not blur the distinction between current satisfaction and conjectural persistence. Thus, logics like
KC4
,
KC5
, and
KC45
remain stratified: conjectural statements can be iterated or withheld with internal consistency, without forcing them into unconditional status.

3.5
The Logics KC, KDC, KC45, KDC45

Building on the conjectural semantics of Axiom
C
, we define four modal systems by combining
C
with standard axioms that constrain the accessibility relation. These logics are designed to capture cognitively meaningful behaviors without invoking full reflexivity, which, as noted, leads to collapse.

The system
KC
consists of the modal logic
K
(which includes the distribution axiom and necessitation) together with Axiom
C
. It characterizes models where truth propagates across cognitively accessible extensions. Since no condition is placed on the structure of
R
,
KC
is compatible with arbitrary accessibility, provided that C-propagation is maintained.

KDC
adds Axiom
D
, which ensures that beliefs remain internally consistent across conjectural extensions. In cognitive terms, this means that any state admitting a satisfied formula must allow at least one refinement in which no contradictions arise. Together with Axiom
C
, this guarantees that accepted facts are preserved in every coherent continuation of the current state. In settings where undefinedness signals epistemic or inferential limitations,
KDC
reflects the idea that consistent hypotheses can always be extended without negating established truths.

The addition of Axioms
4
and
5
yields
KC45
and
KDC45
, respectively. Axiom
4
introduces transitivity, modeling the persistence of conjectural stability: if a formula is propagated to all accessible worlds, then this propagation itself must hold across further extensions. Axiom
5
introduces Euclideanness, encoding negative introspection: if a formula fails to propagate, this failure is conjecturally recognized. Together, these axioms structure the space of conjectural reasoning, allowing agents to build multi-step chains of hypothetical refinement while maintaining awareness of both their commitments and uncertainties.

These systems define a rich family of modal logics governed by non-classical evaluation and definedness-preserving accessibility. They provide a robust framework for modeling partial knowledge, cautious inference, and structured conjecture in paracomplete contexts.

4
The inclusion theorem of cognitive logics

The inclusion theorem is a general model-theoretic framework for modal logics based on explicit valuation sets. The goal is to analyze how the application of axioms
D
,
T
, and
C
characterize the relationships of the valuations of worlds. These structures imply a general ordering of modal systems with respect to their cognitive constraints.

We designate two special worlds:

–

The
shared knowledge
world, denoted
S
, whose valuation
\mathcal{V}_{S}
is a fixed partial assignment of truth values to some primitives in
\mathbb{P}
, representing universally accepted information. It serves as a lower bound: a world
w
is admissible if and only if
\mathcal{V}_{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{w}
. The set
\mathcal{V}_{S}
may be empty or partial, but once fixed, it determines the space of admissible modal worlds.

–

The
reality
world, denoted
R
, whose valuation
\mathcal{V}_{R}
provides the non-modal base for interpretation. It is a total and consistent assignment over a subset of
\mathbb{P}
: for each
p\in\mathbb{P}
, at most one of
\langle p,\top\rangle
or
\langle p,\bot\rangle
belongs to
\mathcal{V}_{R}
. The world
R
must itself be admissible, i.e., it satisfies
\mathcal{V}_{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{R}
.

We call
defined modal pair

\mathcal{D}
an ordered pair
(R,w)
, where
R
is the designated base world and
w
is a possible world used to interpret modal formulas. The world
R
represents the actual or designated standpoint, while
w
encodes a modal perspective whose characteristics depend on the axioms governing accessibility, under the sole requirement that
w
preserves the truths of the shared knowledge
S
. For each such pair, modal statements are interpreted by comparing valuations between
R
and
w
: thus, the formula
\Box\varphi
is evaluated at
R
by checking whether
\varphi
holds in
w
.

This definition abstracts away from syntactic derivability and focuses instead on the structural relationships between valuations. It supports a general model-theoretic view of modal logic in which truth is grounded in definedness and agreement of valuation across possible worlds. Notably, the class of all modal logics defined in this way, i.e., pairs
(R,w)
with
w\in\mathbb{W}
, is robust enough to accommodate standard modal axioms. As we will show, each such Axiom
C
orresponds to a set-theoretic constraint on the inclusion relationships between the valuations
\mathcal{V}_{R}
and
\mathcal{V}_{w}
.

4.1
Cognitive Worlds

The structure established by the inclusion theorem allows us to provide a first classification of all worlds satisfying at least
K
, based on their relationship with the fixed reality world
R
and the shared knowledge base
S
. Each world
w
defines a possible configuration of beliefs through its partial valuation function
\mathcal{V}_{w}
, and its relationship with
\mathcal{V}_{R}
determines which axioms a logic satisfies and, more profoundly, what type of belief it represents. It is thus possible to identify at least five well identified categories:

•

Reality
: the trivial case where
w=R
. In addition to being the logic of boxless predicates, this is of course the outcome of the modal collapse obtained by systems like
\mathbf{KTC}
and
\mathbf{KTC45}
.

•

Epistemic World
: any world
w
such that
\mathcal{V}_{w}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{R}
. Since truth at
w
guarantees truth at
R
, axiom
\mathbf{T}
holds. Thus, epistemic logics correspond to systems like
\mathbf{KT}
and
\mathbf{KT45}
.

•

Conjectural World
: any world such that
\mathcal{V}_{R}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{w}
. An extension of reality
R
possibly assigning values to propositions undefined in
R
. This represents extended or speculative knowledge, consistent with known reality. These systems are characteristic of logics such as
\mathbf{KC}
and
\mathbf{KDC}
.

•

Delusional World
: any world
w
such that there exists at least one
p\in\mathbb{P}
such that
\mathcal{V}_{w}(p)\neq\mathcal{V}_{R}(p)
. This means that at least one primitive
p
has opposite truth values in
w
and
R
, and corresponds, obviously, to a false belief in its cognitive world. Clearly, delusional worlds cannot activate neither Axiom
T
nor Axiom
C
.

•

Opinion World
: any world in which
\mathcal{V}_{w}
shares no primitives with
\mathcal{V}_{R}
beyond the shared
S
. That is,
\text{dom}(\mathcal{V}_{w})\cap\text{dom}(\mathcal{V}_{R})=\text{dom}(\mathcal{V}_{S})
. These worlds represent beliefs that are entirely disconnected from reality. As with delusions, they fall only under the
\mathbf{K}
umbrella, and validate no stronger system.

This classification, although partial, provides interesting ways to represent a complex set of interrelations between reality and subjective cognitive states, and allow for newer types of cognitive logics to arise in a controlled fashion.

Example.

Let
\mathbb{P}=\{a,b,c,d,e,f\}
be the set of primitive formulas. Let the shared base be
\mathcal{V}_{S}=\{a,\ !b\}
and the reality be
\mathcal{V}_{R}=\{a,\ !b,\ !c,\ d\}
. Then:

•

Doxastic worlds
are all supersets of
\mathcal{V}_{S}
, hence they validate the modal formulas
\Box a
,
\Box\neg b
.

•

Epistemic worlds
are those such that
\mathcal{V}_{w}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{R}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\{a,\ !b\},\ \{a,\ !b,\ !c\},\ \{a,\ !b,\ d\},\ \{a,\ !b,\ !c,\ d\}

•

Conjectural worlds
are the definedness-preserving extensions of
\mathcal{V}_{R}
: all supersets of
\{a,\ !b,\ !c,\ d\}
, such as:

\displaystyle\{a,\ !b,\ !c,\ d\},\quad\{a,\ !b,\ !c,\ d,\ e\},\quad\{a,\ !b,\ !c,\ d,\ !e\},

\displaystyle\{a,\ !b,\ !c,\ d,\ f\},\quad\{a,\ !b,\ !c,\ d,\ !f\},\quad\{a,\ !b,\ !c,\ d,\ e,\ f\},

\displaystyle\{a,\ !b,\ !c,\ d,\ e,\ !f\},\quad\{a,\ !b,\ !c,\ d,\ !e,\ f\},\quad\{a,\ !b,\ !c,\ d,\ !e,\ !f\}

•

Delusional worlds
contain assignments that contradict
\mathcal{V}_{R}
, such as:

\{a,\ !b,\ c,\ d\}
,
\{a,\ !b,\ !c,\ !d\}
,
\{a,\ !b,\ c,\ !d\}
.

•

Opinion worlds
are those that assign values only to primitives outside
\text{dom}(\mathcal{V}_{R})\setminus\text{dom}(\mathcal{V}_{S})=\{c,d\}
, e.g.:

\{a,\ !b,\ e\}
,
\{a,\ !b,\ e,\ !f\}
.

For instance, the modal logic built with
R
as the fixed reality and the conjectural world
\mathcal{V}_{w}=\{a,\ !b,\ !c,\ d,\ !e\}
includes all propositional formulas derivable from these assignments. Since
\mathcal{V}_{w}
extends
R
, it satisfies all formulas true in
R
as well as those true in
w
. The logic thus validates the formulas
a
,
\neg b
,
\neg c
,
d
,
\Box a
,
\Box\neg b
,
\Box\neg c
,
\Box d
, and
\Box\neg e
.

5
Base logics for conjectural logics

The modal framework developed in the previous sections operates over a non-classical substrate. Instead of assuming classical bivalence as the foundation for propositional semantics, we adopt a more flexible notion of truth: one that accommodates undefinedness and partial knowledge without collapsing into triviality. This section introduces the two base logics that support our modal construction: Weak Kleene logic and Description Logic (DL).

Weak Kleene logic allows us to treat partial valuations as first-class semantic objects, and to account for formulas that may be undefined in a given world. This plays a crucial role in distinguishing between full and partial knowledge, and in determining when modal axioms like
\mathbf{C}
or
\mathbf{T}
are semantically valid. It also ensures that the structure of admissibility and accessibility is preserved in a way that aligns with non-total information states.

Description Logic, on the other hand, provides the syntactic and semantic backbone for structured knowledge representation. It defines a formal language capable of encoding concepts, roles, and assertions about individuals, and serves as the internal language of the worlds we consider. In our modal setting, formulas of modal logic are often interpreted over ontological statements expressed in Description Logic, making DL a suitable and expressive target for embedding epistemic or conjectural content.

5.1
Weak Kleene Logic

Weak Kleene logic (also known as Bochvar’s internal three-valued logic) provides a semantic foundation for handling partial or undefined information without imposing artificial completeness or enforcing classical tautologies. First introduced by D.A. Bochvar in 1938
[

]

The logic is termed “weak” because it weakens classical logic not by modifying inference rules, but by changing the semantic base: undefined subformulas lead to undefined compounds. It is ideal for modeling scenarios where not all statements are evaluable due to incomplete knowledge, undefined terms, or failure of reference. This distinguishes it from other three-valued logics, such as Kleene’s strong logic

or Łukasiewicz logic
[

]

In our framework, each world

over a finite set

–

–

Supports undefinedness propagation: especially useful in epistemic logics, where ignorance about a fact implies uncertainty about statements involving that fact.

–

In addition, the Weak Kleene logic satisfies several desirable meta-properties (cf.
[

[

–

It is monotonic: extending a valuation (i.e., defining more primitives) never makes a defined formula become undefined.

–

It is paracomplete: it avoids explosion from undefinedness but does not validate the Law of the Excluded Middle.

–

It is non-trivial: both

When integrated with modal operators, Weak Kleene logic operates as follows:

–

A formula

–

If

–

).

The Weak Kleene logic offers a natural fit for modeling partial knowledge and epistemic uncertainty, since the valuation function
\mathcal{V}_{w}
may deliberately omit assignments. Unlike classical logic, which assumes global evaluability, the Weak Kleene setting allows us to reason with ignorance, unawareness, or incomplete observations without inconsistency.

5.2
Description Logic

Description Logics (DLs) are a family of knowledge representation providing a formal foundation for ontological modeling, combining expressive concept languages with tractable reasoning procedures

]

Let

The set of concepts is defined inductively as follows:

DLs can be easily embedded in modal logic frameworks
[

]
, and in our setting the valuations

and

can be seen as induced by concept assertions over different ontological perspectives.

Both weak Kleene logic and description logic independently fulfill the two semantic preconditions necessary to accommodate the conjectural axiom

(

) without triggering a modal collapse:

•

They allow for
partiality
: formulas may be undefined due to undefined components (in weak Kleene) or due to incompleteness of interpretation (in description logic).

•

They support
definedness extension
: if a formula is defined and true in one world, it may or may not retain truth in other worlds, but its definedness can still be preserved or extended.

These properties are essential for the interpretation of modal pairs
(R,w)
in which the conjectural axiom holds. Consequently, the modal logics
\mathbf{KC}
and
\mathbf{KDC}
become not only syntactically specifiable, but also semantically coherent. The axiomatic systems discussed in Section
3
are thus grounded in two distinct yet compatible logical frameworks that support conjectural reasoning.

6
Epistemic Settlements

Dynamic epistemic logic
(DEL) studies the evolution of knowledge under the occurrence of epistemic events—public announcements, private communications, observations—that modify the epistemic accessibility relation, the valuation of atomic propositions, or both. Most of DEL assumes that the underlying logical space is classically defined and complete. In contrast, our framework relies on paracomplete base logics where reality itself may be partially undefined, motivating the need for new types of epistemic events that not only alter beliefs but also define new truths in reality.

We introduce here the notion of an
epistemic settlement
, a dynamic event that does not operate on agents’ accessibility relations or belief states, but on the reality itself, by assigning a definite truth value to a previously undefined formula. This models situations in which the world becomes more defined, such as the outcome of a decision, the conclusion of a sport match, or the discovery of an empirical fact: in all these cases the truth value of the formula was undefined or not entailed, and after the completion of the event this value is now available and affects all worlds that conjectured a specific value for the formula. Theoretically, once all conjectures are eventually settled, the model would converges to a fully defined total logic, generating a modal collapse. However, as long as independent conjectures exist, cognitive diversity is preserved.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\varphi
be a well-formed formula such that
\mathcal{V}_{R}(\varphi)
is undefined. A
settlement
on
\varphi
is the epistemic event that assigns a definite truth value
v\in{\top,\bot}
to
\varphi
in
R
. That is, it updates the valuation to
\mathcal{V}_{R^{\prime}}(\varphi)=v
, where
R^{\prime}
is the new reality.

Following a settlement of
\varphi:=v
:

•

The minimal conjectural world
w
such that
\mathcal{V}_{w}=\mathcal{V}_{R}\cup{\langle\varphi,v\rangle}
becomes the new reality
R^{\prime}
.

•

All wider conjectures consistent with
\varphi
(i.e., the ones that included
\langle\varphi,v\rangle
) remain valid conjectures under
R^{\prime}
.

•

The previous reality
R
becomes an epistemic world:
\mathcal{V}_{R}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{R^{\prime}}
.

•

All conjectures containing
\lnot\varphi
become delusional, since they now contradict reality.

Unlike public announcements, settlements do not eliminate possible worlds nor refine accessibility. They act solely on the reality valuation, expanding definedness. The logical structure remains, but the classification of worlds change depending on their alignment with
R^{\prime}
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a modal logic satisfying
\mathbf{K}
and possibly other axioms. Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model with current reality
R
, and let
\text{settle}(\varphi:=v)
be a settlement event. Denote by
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
the updated model.

Lemma 6.2
.

Settling
\varphi
strictly increases the definedness of
\mathcal{V}_{R}
:
\text{dom}(\mathcal{V}_{R})\subset\text{dom}(\mathcal{V}_{R^{\prime}})
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi
be a well-formed formula such that
\mathcal{V}_{R}(\varphi)
is undefined, and
R^{\prime}
the new reality after the settlement.

The domain of the new valuation is now
\text{dom}(\mathcal{V}_{R^{\prime}})=\text{dom}(\mathcal{V}_{R})\cup\{\varphi\}
, and since
\varphi\notin\text{dom}(\mathcal{V}_{R})
by assumption, it follows that
\text{dom}(\mathcal{V}_{R})\subset\text{dom}(\mathcal{V}_{R^{\prime}})
.
∎

Theorem 6.3
.

If
\mathcal{M}
satisfies
\mathbf{KC}
or
\mathbf{KDC}
, then so does
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, provided that all conjectures consistent with
R^{\prime}
still include
\mathcal{V}_{R^{\prime}}
and the domain of
\mathcal{V}_{w}
remains total.

Proof.

Assume
\mathcal{M}\models\mathbf{KC}
. Then, by definition, for every conjectural world
w
, it holds that
\mathcal{V}_{R}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{w}
. After settling
\varphi:=v
, the new reality is
R^{\prime}=R\cup{\langle\varphi,v\rangle}
. By hypothesis, all conjectures that survive the update (i.e., are not delusional) include
\langle\varphi,v\rangle
, so
\mathcal{V}_{R^{\prime}}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{w}
. Hence, the condition for axiom
\mathbf{C}
still holds for in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.

If
\mathcal{M}\models\mathbf{KDC}
, then all conjectural worlds
w
in
\mathcal{M}
have valuations
\mathcal{V}_{w}
defined for all relevant subformulas. Since the settlement event only affects
\mathcal{V}_{R}
, and all surviving

are compatible with the settlement, the domain of
\mathcal{V}_{w}
is unchanged, and the totality condition still holds.

Thus,
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\models\mathbf{KC}
(or
\mathbf{KDC}
, respectively).
∎

As usual in DEL, we denote model updates after a dynamic event as
\mathcal{M}[![X]!]
, where

is the settlement event, and
\mathcal{M}[![X]!]
is the updated model resulting from its application.

Definition 6.4
.

Let
\Sigma=[\text{settle}(\varphi_{1}:=v_{1}),\dots,\text{settle}(\varphi_{n}:=v_{n})]

\mathcal{M}_{0}:=\mathcal{M},\quad\mathcal{M}_{i+1}:=\mathcal{M}_{i}[![\text{settle}(\varphi_{i+1}:=v_{i+1})]!]

Lemma 6.5
.

Settlements
\text{settle}(\varphi_{1}:=v_{1})
and
\text{settle}(\varphi_{2}:=v_{2})
commute iff
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are independent, i.e.,
\varphi_{1}\notin\text{Sub}(\varphi_{2})
and
\varphi_{2}\notin\text{Sub}(\varphi_{1})
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma_{1}:=\text{settle}(\varphi_{1}:=v_{1})
and
\sigma_{2}:=\text{settle}(\varphi_{2}:=v_{2})
.
Let
\mathcal{M}_{1,2}:=\mathcal{M}[![\sigma_{1}]!][![\sigma_{2}]!]

\mathcal{M}_{2,1}:=\mathcal{M}[![\sigma_{2}]!][![\sigma_{1}]!]

\mathcal{M}_{1,2}=\mathcal{M}_{2,1}

If
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are independent, neither appears as a subformula of the other (i.e.,
\varphi_{1}\notin\text{Sub}(\varphi_{2})

\mathcal{M}_{1,2}=\mathcal{M}_{2,1}
.

Conversely, suppose the two formulas are not independent, e.g.,
\varphi_{1}\in\text{Sub}(\varphi_{2})
. As such, the truth value of
\varphi_{2}
may depend on the value assigned to
\varphi_{1}
. Thus settling
\varphi_{1}

\varphi_{2}

\text{settle}(\varphi_{2}:=v_{2})

∎

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
have modal depth
k
. Then every
\mathcal{M}_{i}
in the sequence
\mathcal{M}_{i}=\mathcal{M}[![\Sigma_{i}]!]
has modal depth
k
, although some modal paths may be semantically nullified by valuation updates.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a model with modal depth
k

\Box
in any formula evaluated in
\mathcal{M}
is

. Let
\Sigma=[\text{settle}(\varphi_{1}:=v_{1}),\dots,\text{settle}(\varphi_{n}:=v_{n})]
be a finite sequence of settlements, such that each settlement updates only the valuation

by assigning a value to a previously undefined formula

.

Thus settlements do not modify the set of possible worlds, the accessibility relation or the set of formulas themselves. Thus, for every

, the set of formulas with modal depth

remains the same in
\mathcal{M}_{i}
.

However, some formulas may become semantically nullified due to valuation updates. For example, suppose a modal formula
\Box\varphi
is true at

in
\mathcal{M}
because
\varphi
is undefined in all accessible worlds. After a settlement makes
\varphi
defined (and possibly false) in those worlds,
\Box\varphi
may become false. This affects truth values but not modal depth.
Hence, the modal depth remains
k
in every
\mathcal{M}_{i}
.
∎

.

, and
\mathcal{M}_{k+1}:=\mathcal{M}_{k}[![\text{settle}(\varphi_{k+1}:=v_{k+1})]!]
. Since this settlement is not semantically contradictory, i.e., no previous valuation assigns
\varphi_{k+1}:=\neg v_{k+1}
, we find that
\mathcal{V}(R_{k})
is undefined on
\varphi_{k+1}
, and its extension to
\mathcal{V}(R_{k+1}):=\mathcal{V}(R_{k})\cup{\langle\varphi_{k+1},v_{k+1}\rangle}
is well-defined. By theorem
6.3
, since
\mathcal{M}_{k}
satisfies
\mathbf{KDC}
and the settlement introduces no contradiction, the updated model
\mathcal{M}_{k+1}
also satisfies
\mathbf{KDC}
.

Thus by induction the final model
\mathcal{M}_{n}:=\mathcal{M}[![\Sigma]!]
satisfies
\mathbf{KDC}
.
∎

The introduction of epistemic settlements captures a distinctive form of epistemic dynamism: not the evolution of knowledge among agents, but the evolution of reality itself. Settlements increase definedness without collapsing cognitive diversity, commute under syntactic independence, and preserve modal axioms in well-behaved logics like
\mathbf{KDC}
.

While the notion of an epistemic settlement is here formulated in the setting of paracomplete modal logics, similar ideas have emerged across multiple research domains. Notably, non-monotonic logics and truth maintenance systems (TMS) have long studied mechanisms for retracting and updating partial knowledge bases in response to new truths
[
Doy79
,
dK86
]
. These systems anticipate some of the operational features of settlements, particularly in how updates propagate over previously undefined or conditionally inferred values.

In the domain of ontology evolution, updates to Description Logic knowledge bases are typically handled through explicit revision operations, including insertion, retraction, and refinement of axioms
[
FMK+08
]
. Although most of these systems rely on classical DLs, the need to accommodate undefined or partially known information has motivated hybrid or non-monotonic approaches that resonate with the dynamics described in this section
[
LB06
,
GKZ09
]
.

Finally, the DEL literature has explored ontology revision as a dynamic epistemic process, where announcements or observations not only refine belief states but also modify ontological commitments
[
DS15
,
vB07
]
. In this context, the boundary between epistemic change and ontological redefinition is intentionally blurred, paralleling the role of settlements as truth-defining events in a partially known reality.

These results suggest that cognitive settlements are not a novel construct within the framework of conjectural modal logic, but are conceptually connected to existing work on dynamic updates, ontology change, and truth redefinition under uncertainty.

7
Conclusions

In this paper we have introduced
conjectural logics
, a new family of cognitive modal systems that complement well
-
known doxastic and epistemic frameworks. These logics are grounded on a non
-
reflexive, paracomplete formulation of the schema

\varphi\rightarrow\Box\varphi
(Axiom
C
), enabling a principled distinction between factual information and hypothetical reasoning. We formally defined systems such as
KC
,
KDC
,
KC45
, and
KDC45
, showing that they are robust, well
-
formed, and naturally positioned within the broader hierarchy of cognitive logics.

This approach, relying on set
-
theoretic inclusion relations between primitive valuations in designated worlds, gives rise to a clear and universal inclusion theorem, that allows to interpret different cognitive modes, such as doxastic, epistemic, conjectural, delusional, and opinion-based, as mere relationships between partial valuation sets, rather than separate syntactic systems.

Doxastic, epistemic, and conjectural worlds all admit a simple constructive semantics for modelling partial, potentially incorrect, or reality
-
disconnected cognitive states, supporting the representation of uncertainty, disagreement, and conflicting opinions within a unified framework, and capturing e.g., credence, delusion, and subjective stance in formal terms.

Beyond theoretical interest, we believe that this model has significant potential for practical applications in fields such as the Semantic Web, knowledge provenance, and neuro
-
symbolic AI. For example, in provenance-aware systems researchers have studied how to represent and reason about conflicting claims and their sources, using structured provenance models to track the credibility of assertions (
[
SP20
]
,
[
LL23
]
). Similarly, in neuro
-
symbolic AI, hybrid architectures that combine neural learning with formal symbolic logic offer a way to enrich systems with both interpretability and robust reasoning capabilities
[
HEE+22
]
. The conjectural framework aligns with these hybrid systems: agents or modules may share partial knowledge bases, make conjectures based on known facts, and update them dynamically as new information becomes definite, mirroring both the semantics of settlement events and the architecture of explainable neuro
-
symbolic reasoning.

Overall, we suggest a unified, formally grounded cognitive logic that bridges the expressive gap between belief, knowledge, and conjecture, handles partial and undefined information without collapse, supports dynamic transitions from hypothesis to fact via settlement operations, and integrates naturally with emerging technologies in symbolic and neuro-symbolic AI.

Future work will explore computational implementations of algorithmic reasoning for conjectural systems, and concrete case studies in multi
-
agent settings, provenance tracking, and neuro
-
symbolic integration.
