Shifting From Anatomical to Functional Abnormalities
Authors: Yung-Tsai Lee, Wei-Hsian Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Severe aortic stenosis (AS) is increasingly prevalent in Asia. Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), initially established for patients at high surgical risk, has rapidly expanded to intermediate- and low-risk populations, fundamentally changing the management of AS.1 More recently, the EARLY TAVR trial (NCT03042104) demonstrated that early intervention was superior to clinical surveillance in asymptomatic severe AS, supporting a shift toward earlier treatment before irreversible cardiac damage develops.2 This concept is supported by previous evidence showing that myocardial fibrosis is strongly associated with adverse long-term outcomes after aortic valve replacement.3 Building on these observations, Généreux et al4 proposed a 4-stage cardiac damage classification that extends the assessment of AS beyond the aortic valve to encompass progressive injury involving the left ventricle, left atrium, pulmonary circulation, and right heart. This framework emphasizes that the prognosis of AS depends not only on the severity of valve obstruction but also on the extent of secondary cardiac remodeling. Accordingly, recent studies have shifted their focus from anatomical abnormalities and conventional clinical parameters to functional markers of cardiac damage. Left ventricular global longitudinal strain (LVGLS), an afterload-dependent measure of myocardial function, is closely associated with myocardial fibrosis and detects subclinical left ventricular dysfunction earlier than left ventricular ejection fraction.5 Likewise, right ventricular-pulmonary artery (RV-PA) coupling has emerged as an important indicator of right ventricular adaptation and has been consistently associated with clinical outcomes after TAVR.6

However, subsequent investigations have suggested that the original 4-stage classification does not fully capture the diversity of cardiac injury patterns observed in contemporary TAVR populations. Gutiérrez-Ortiz et al7 reported that a substantial proportion of patients could not be adequately classified using the original staging system, highlighting its limitations in routine clinical practice. Consequently, several groups have proposed revised functional classifications based on patterns of cardiac injury, including normal cardiac function, reversible left-sided injury, irreversible left-sided injury, and end-stage right heart damage.7

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Liu et al8 further refined this concept by classifying patients into 4 phenotypes: normal function, isolated left-sided injury, isolated right-sided injury, and combined left- and right-sided injury. Their findings demonstrated that most patients followed the progressive cardiac damage hypothesis, with those exhibiting combined left- and right-sided dysfunction experiencing the worst clinical outcomes. Interestingly, they also identified a distinct subgroup with isolated right-sided injury whose prognosis was comparable to that of patients with early left-sided injury rather than advanced disease. These patients were more likely to have atrial fibrillation, moderate-to-severe mitral regurgitation, concomitant aortic regurgitation, and impaired RV-PA coupling despite relatively preserved LVGLS. This observation suggests that not all patients with AS follow the conventional sequence of cardiac damage proposed by the original staging model. Mixed valvular disease or other pathophysiological mechanisms may result in predominant right-sided dysfunction independent of advanced left ventricular injury. Therefore, right ventricular dysfunction should not be interpreted in isolation. Instead, an integrated functional assessment incorporating both LVGLS and RV-PA coupling may provide a more comprehensive characterization of cardiac remodeling and improve risk stratification beyond traditional clinical variables or left ventricular ejection fraction alone.

Collectively, these studies illustrate an important paradigm shift in the evaluation of AS—from anatomical abnormalities to comprehensive functional assessment of cardiac injury. Functional imaging biomarkers may identify patients who would benefit from earlier intervention before irreversible myocardial damage occurs, thereby providing additional support for an earlier TAVR strategy in selected patients.
