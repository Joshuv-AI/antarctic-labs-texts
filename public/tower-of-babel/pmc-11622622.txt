Locus-specific differential expression of human satellite sequences in the nuclei of cancer cells and heat-shocked cells
Authors: Christina Rabeler, Nicholas Paterna, Rajiv Potluri, Lia R D’Alessandro, Anusha Bhatia, Shu Yi Chen, Johanna Lee, Bereketab Abeje, Benjamin Lipchin, Benjamin R Carone, Dawn M Carone
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman satellitess(HSats) are pericentric, tandemly repeating satellite DNA sequences in the human genome. While silent in normal cells, a subset of HSat2 noncoding RNA is expressed and accumulates in the nucleus of cancer cells. We developed a FISH-based approach for identification of the distribution of three subfamilies of HSat2 (A1, A2, B) sequences on individual human chromosomes. Further, using the HSat subfamily annotations in the T2T completed centromere satellite (CenSat) sequence, we isolated, defined and mapped differentially expressed sequence variants of nuclear-restricted HSat2 and HSat3 RNA from cancer cell lines and heat-shocked cells. We identified chromosome-specific and subfamily-specific expression of HSat2 and HSat3 and established a computational pipeline for differential expression analysis of tandemly repeated satellite sequences. Results suggest the differential expression of chromosome-specific HSat2 arrays in the human genome may underlie their accumulation in cancer cells and that specific HSat3 loci are upregulated upon heat shock.KEYWORDS: Satellite DNA, pericentromere, centromere, Cancer, repetitive DNA, heat shock, nucleus, RNA-seq

Introduction

Satellite DNAs are defined by tandemly repeating units of DNA. They are localized to the centric or pericentric regions and the telomeres of chromosomes, yet despite their conserved location, the sequences of satellite DNAs are poorly conserved [1]. Originally discovered over 60 years ago by density centrifugation, satellite DNA was described as the minor fraction of DNA in multiple species [2] that underwent higher rates of renaturation and thus was composed of highly repetitive sequences [3]. Further analysis led to the discovery of four main fractions of human satellite DNA [4,5]: HSATI, HSATII, HSATIII, and HSATIV, which are now known to be resident within the pericentric heterochromatin on a subset of human chromosomes.

Early attempts to localize these four human satellite DNAs (HSATs) on human metaphase chromosome spreads led to the discovery of differential localization of each of these distinct satellite fractions, with the bulk of satellite sequence resident on chromosomes 9 and Y [6]. Yet, smaller sites of signal were detected on twelve additional chromosomes and the hybridization patterns displayed a unique signature for each HSAT. Illustrating this, HSat2 (the DNA sequence constituting satellite fraction HSATII [7]) was found to be located in the pericentromere of Chr 16 despite the absence of other HSATs at this locus [6]. Further, biochemical studies into the composition of HSAT sequences using restriction enzyme digestion revealed some sequence homology of HSAT classes, with more similarity between HSat2 and HSat3 than HSat1 [8]. HSat2 was later found to consist of a tandemly repeated, ~26bp monomer sequence that is highly divergent, yet composed of the pentamer sequence ATTCC, which also forms the basic monomer unit of HSat3 [9].

Early studies of DNA methylation indicated repeat sequences, including HSATs, are highly methylated in normal cells, yet are targets of early demethylation in cancer cells and this demethylation is correlated with tumor progression [10]. The 1q12 locus on Chr 1 harbors more than 13Mb of HSat2 DNA that is demethylated in hepatocellular cancer carcinoma and multiple myeloma, which is correlated with a 1q copy gain due to a breakpoint within the 1q12 HSat2 DNA region [11,12], suggesting that hypomethylation of HSat2 DNA leads to the formation of fragile sites in cancer. Analysis of the genomes of individuals with ICF syndrome, characterized by mutations in DNMT3B, demonstrates high levels of demethylation of HSat2 DNA at 1q12 and 16 which results in chromosomal abnormalities and breakage events involving these chromosome regions [13]. Thus, proper regulation of the chromatin environment within pericentric satellite sequences such as HSat2 is critical to maintaining genomic stability.

Further illustrating the consequences of pericentric satellite misregulation, HSat2 is transcriptionally silent in normal cells, yet HSat2 RNA is expressed in many cancer types, producing long non-coding RNA [14] that accumulates in nuclear foci in cancer cells [15]. This expression is thought to be triggered by HSat2 DNA hypomethylation [15] and to result in further instability within these pericentric regions including the expansion of HSat2 DNA arrays [16]. Ectopic HSat2 expression induces cell cycle defects and leads to the recruitment of the regulatory protein MeCP2 [17], thus indicating that HSat2 RNA nuclear accumulation has downstream effects that may lead to further misregulation and genomic instability in cancer. Blocks of HSat2 DNA have been identified on 8 human chromosomes and are most abundant on human chromosomes 1 and 16 in the pericentric region [18]. Despite this broad distribution of HSat2 DNA in the genome, HSat2 RNA is only expressed in cancer cells from a subset of loci on distinct chromosomes, the most notable of which lies on chromosomes 7 and 10 [15]. In the same cancer cells where some HSat2 loci are expressed, other HSat2 DNA loci (Chr 1, 16) recruit Polycomb (PcG) repressive proteins and do not express HSat2 RNA [15]. Recruitment of PcG proteins follows DNA demethylation, suggesting that PcG proteins may be recruited to sites of HSat2 DNA demethylation in order to further repress and/or condense these genomic regions upon hypomethylation.

Very little has been known about the sequence diversity of HSATs resident at individual chromosomes. Past findings indicate that when HSat2 is digested with HinfI, several 10–80 bp fragments are produced and contain the pentamer repeat 5’ A-T-T-C-C in a highly diverged form [9]. Study of HSATs is complicated by the fact that HSat2 and HSat3 are very closely related and HSat3 DNA also contains sequences of varying repeat lengths on different chromosomes, with the largest block of HSat3 resident on chromosomes 9 and Y [19,20]. HSat3 sequences are also typically transcriptionally silenced, but expression of HSat3 is specifically induced upon heat shock by recruitment of the HSF1 transcription factor [21,22], where this noncoding RNA accumulates in the nucleus to form nuclear stress bodies (nSBs) that sequester RNA-binding proteins [23,24]. HSat3 acts as an architectural ncRNA (arcRNA) that is induced upon heat shock, where it reversibly sequesters hundreds of RNA binding proteins, which results in modulation of gene expression and intron retention [25].

Previous unbiased, pairwise-classification of HSat2 and HSat3 from whole genome shotgun sequencing allowed for the classification of HSATs into subfamilies, with three defined subfamilies for HSat2 (HSat2A1, HSat2A2, HSat2B) and 11 for HSat3 [26]. Despite a rigorous classification of these sequences, exploration of the diversity of HSat2 sequences has been hindered by the difficulty in mapping repetitive elements to the human genome [27]. Improvement of the human genome assembly compared to that of GRCh38 has recently been conducted using long-read sequencing and assembly methods and includes a telomere-to-telomere assembly of the human genome via a haploid CHM13hTERT(T2T-CHM13) cell line [28]. This genome assembly also includes the first complete sequence through human centromeres, which are densely populated with satellites and have been assembled into a CenSat annotation track [18]. However, given the challenges of mapping divergent repeats in silico, inherent human genetic variation, and the prevalence of structural rearrangements in pericentric and centric heterochromatin, there is a need to further confirm and refine mapping of satellite sequence subfamilies via in situ methodology using HSAT subfamily-specific probes.

Here, we present the development of mapping tools for HSat2 subtypes using fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) of the HSat2 subfamilies in male fibroblast cells to create a cytogenetic map of subfamilies HSat2A1, HSat2A2, and HSat2B on individual human chromosomes. Using this in situ approach, we demonstrate the ability to design highly specific satellite subfamily oligonucleotide probes for FISH, and use these probes to identify HSAT2 subtype-specific RNA accumulations in cancer cells by RNA FISH. We also isolate and sequence nuclear restricted, ribo-depleted RNA from cancer cell lines and heat-shocked cells in order to develop a computational pipeline for differential expression analysis of CenSat repeat sequences. Our results suggest the presence of HSat2-specific subfamilies on specific chromosomes may contribute to locus-specific HSat2 misregulation in cancer cells and generate a new resource of differentially expressed HSAT sequence variants that can be used for further functional analysis of HSat2 transcripts in cancer cells and HSat3 transcripts in heat shock conditions.

Materials and methods

Human cell lines

Human male fibroblast cells (Coriell, AG04449) were grown in Eagle’s MEM (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) supplemented with 100 units/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2 mm L-glutamine (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 15% FBS (HyClone, GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Marlborough, MA; Avantor Seradigm, VWR, Radnor, PA) in 5% CO2 at 37°C. HeLa and U2OS cells were grown in Eagle’s MEM (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) supplemented with 100 units/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2 mm L-glutamine (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 10% FBS (HyClone, GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Marlborough, MA; Avantor Seradigm, VWR, Radnor, PA) in 5% CO2 at 37°C. PC3 cells (ATCC, CRL-1435) were grown in F-12K (Kaighn’s Modification of Ham’s Medium) Medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with penicillin, streptomycin and 10% FBS as above. HeLa cells were heat shocked for 1 hour at 42°C followed by a 1 hour recovery at 37°C prior to pelleting for nuclear RNA extraction or fixation for RNA FISH. Standard mammalian cell culture protocols and sterile techniques were utilized.

Chromosome spreads

Chromosome spreads were prepared as previously described [29], with the following modifications. Cells in flasks were treated with thymidine for 12 hours, allowed to recover for 24 hours, and then treated with colcemid for 6 hours. Mitotic cells were then monitored, at which point the cells were harvested, treated with a hypotonic solution (0.075 M KCl) and fixed with Carnoy’s Fixative (3:1 ratio of methanol: glacial acetic acid). Fixed cells were then dropped onto clean glass slides under humid conditions to produce chromosome spreads. Slides were serially dehydrated with ethanol and stored at −20°C. Thawed slides were dehydrated in 100% ethanol prior to DNA FISH experiments.

Image acquisition and analysis

Slides were imaged using a ZEISS Axio Observer Z1 epifluorescent microscope equipped with a ZEISS Axiocam 702 mono camera under a 100 × oil objective. Single plane images were captured using ZEISS ZEN2 imaging software.

Images were analyzed in Adobe Photoshop. Image contrast was adjusted in the DAPI and GFP channel images to eliminate background and to obtain clearer banding for karyotyping of metaphase chromosomes. HSat2 subfamily signal presence was assessed by assigning signal scores (S) to each chromosome in the image. Clear signal on both homologous chromosomes received a score of 1, clear absence of signal on both homologous chromosomes received a 0 and unclear signal on one or both homologous chromosomes or clear signal on only one homologous chromosome received a score of 0.5. FISH signal was considered strong when greater than 75% of scored chromosome spreads had consistent signal on both homologous chromosomes (Figure 1, S1).

Quantification of RNA focal accumulations

Analysis of nuclei and focal accumulation counts were performed in Imaris Version 10.2 on slides processed and hybridized concurrently with images taken at the same exposure time (60 nuclei per probe). Processing was performed manually through the creation of surfaces to define and mask both the nuclei and the focal accumulations. The creation of nuclei surfaces was assisted by machine learning segmentation aimed to help automatically identify nuclear signal from background, whereas focal accumulations were classified through a background subtraction method. All images were analyzed with original thresholds and intensities to help distinguish any background signal.

Nuclei were assigned a surface grain size of 0.133 µm and an estimated diameter of 13.0 µm. Focal accumulations were assigned a 0.0564 µm surface grain size and were estimated at 0.350 µm in diameter. Minor adjustments were made from this initial setting as required per image to ensure all focal accumulations were included.

All nuclei surfaces touching the image border were excluded only to include whole nuclei in analysis. Additionally, all focal accumulation surfaces outside the boundary of a nuclear surface were excluded. Individual focal accumulations were subsequently assigned to their parent nuclei by importing both the nucleus surface and focal accumulation surface to a cell module. This allowed for accurate focal counts per nuclei (see example in Figure S2).

Data was compiled to create a list of parent nuclei with their corresponding number of focal accumulations. Nuclei with inaccurate surface creations were excluded from the analysis.

RNA extraction and nuclear isolation

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and treated with a TURBO DNA-free™ Kit (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to remove any residual genomic DNA. Samples were quantified using a Qubit RNA BR Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Each nuclear RNA sample was prepared from 4 150 mm cell culture dishes grown to 100% confluency and pooled after trypsinization. Harvested cells were washed with 1× PBS and pelleted at 1000 rpm, then resuspended in 1 mL cell lysis buffer (10 mm Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 10 mm NaCl, 3 mm MgCl2, 0.5% NP-40, supplemented with cOmplete™, Mini Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich)). Cells were homogenized using 20 strokes of a Type B dounce (Kimble) and lysate was centrifuged for 5 minutes at 5,000 rpm at 4°C. The pelleted cell homogenate was resuspended in 0.5 mL cell lysis buffer and layered over 1 mL STRB (2 M sucrose, 10 mm HEPES, 25 mm KCl, 0.5 mm spermidine, 1 mm EDTA, 10% glycerol), then centrifuged at 16,000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4°C. Intact nuclei were stained and visualized with Trypan Blue. RNA was extracted from the resulting nuclear pellet with TRIzol (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific), following manufacturer’s instructions and then treated with TURBO DNA-free™ (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to remove contaminating DNA prior to sequencing.

RT-qPCR

RNA was reverse transcribed using iScript™ Reverse Transcription Supermix (Bio-Rad) and qRT-PCR was conducted using PerfeCTa® SYBR® Green FastMix® Reaction Mix (QuantaBio, VWR). All reactions were performed in triplicate, using a CFX96 Real-Time C1000 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad). Analysis of relative enrichment was calculated using the ΔΔC(t) method [31], where reported values are normalized to β-actin expression. RNA was analyzed using TapeStation, rRNA depleted with Illumina Ribo-Zero rRNA removal kit, and paired-end sequenced (2× 150 bp) on Illumina HiSeq. Results

In situ chromosome-specific distribution of HSat2 subfamilies

In order to confirm the in silico mapping of HSat2 subfamilies to individual human chromosomes [18] and to demonstrate the ability to design and hybridize highly specific HSat2 subfamily-specific oligonucleotide probes, we performed DNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) for HSat2 subfamilies using subfamily-specific oligonucleotide sequences previously defined (A1, A2, B) [26] on primary human male fibroblast (AG04449) metaphase chromosome spreads. Oligonucleotide probes consisted of either a mix of LNA and DNA bases (A1, A2) or just DNA bases (B). Hybridizations were performed under very stringent conditions using high temperature (42ºC) and low salt washes to decrease potential cross-hybridization between HSat2 subfamilies. Metaphase chromosomes were karyotyped and the distribution of HSat2 subfamily signals on each chromosome was analyzed (Figure 1). Notably, the chromosome distributions differed for each of the hybridized probes (A1, A2, B) (Figure 1a) and all HSat2 FISH signals were found in the pericentric and centromeric regions of the chromosomes (Figure 1b-d) for all probes, indicating high specificity of subfamily detection.

Chromosomal distributions of each subfamily were analyzed by scoring presence of the subfamily-specific FISH signal to homologous chromosomes (see Methods, Figure S1) for 30 metaphase spreads per probe (Figure S1). We then compared our FISH subfamily chromosome distributions to the T2T-CHM13 CenSat annotations [18] to compare chromosome-specific distributions of each subfamily to the identified HSat2 arrays of the CHM13 genome (Figure 1a). Notably, we observed strong FISH signal consistently for all three subfamilies on chromosomes 1 and 16. These results likely indicate these largest contiguous loci of HSat2 allowed for strong probe hybridization and our FISH data corroborate the T2T-CHM13 mapping that indicates that HSat2 sequences on chromosome 1 are diverse in sequence composition [18]. HSat2B also appeared to be the most represented subfamily on multiple chromosomes by FISH analysis (Figure 1a), (e.g. Chrs 2, 7, 9, 10, 17, 22), supporting its annotated CHM13 genomic distribution [18]. While Chr16 is annotated in CHM13 to solely consist of the B subfamily, we saw signal for all 3 probes (A1, A2, B) on Chr 16 by DNA FISH in the AG04449 cell line. We also observed FISH signal for A1 and A2 on some of the smaller HSat2 arrays on Chrs 2, 9, 10, and 17 while these subfamilies are missing from the T2T-CHM13 CenSat track. These results may indicate that there is potential cross-hybridization of probes A1 and A2 to the large genomic arrays of HSat2, or that these subfamilies exist in small amounts on these chromosomes that have previously gone uncharacterized within larger arrays of HSat2B. The potential for probe cross-hybridization is not unexpected due to the strong sequence similarity between HSat2A1, HSat2A2, and HSat2B oligo sequences (see Methods). The highly stringent hybridization conditions employed here, including hybridization and washes at 42°C, did decrease considerable cross-hybridization to other genomic sites of HSat2 compared to hybridization at 37°C (not shown) and each oligo probe displayed unique DNA hybridization patterns under the highly stringent conditions shown here (Figure 1). Thus, our DNA FISH results for HSat2 subfamilies indicate the possibility to design and use highly specific HSat2 subfamily probes to interrogate subfamily expression differences even among highly similar, tandemly repeated HSat2 sequences. Therefore, we utilized the same highly specific HSat2 subfamily oligo probes to identify differences in expressed sequence variants by RNA FISH in the U2OS cell line (Figure 2), which has previously been shown to express high levels of HSat2 [15].

RNA FISH with HSat2A1, HSat2A2 and HSat2B LNA probes revealed a similar average number of RNA focal accumulations, with an average of 8.3, 8.9 and 9.3, respectively (n = 60 nuclei for each) (Figure 2d). However, the median number of focal accumulations for HSat2B was lower (5) than the median for HSat2A1 (7) and HSat2A2 (8) and there was a broader distribution of nuclei that had either very low numbers of focal accumulations or very high numbers of focal accumulations. For example, some HSat2B nuclei had around 50 focal accumulations per nucleus, while others had none (Figure 2c,d). There was overall much more consistency both in the number of focal accumulations and the percentage of nuclei with accumulations for HSat2A1 and HSat2A2 compared to HSat2B. This suggests that a small number of nuclei in U2OS cells have very high levels of expression of HSat2B. These RNA FISH results demonstrating differential expression of HSat2 subtypes prompted us to further investigate the specific genomic locations of HSat2 subtypes that are expressed in cancer cells by RNA sequencing and differential gene expression analysis.

Isolation of nuclear-enriched RNA to identify expressed HSat2 variants

Previous annotation of HSat2 sequences within the T2T-CHM13 genome [18] and our FISH data for HSat2 subfamilies confirm that each HSat2-containing chromosome harbors a unique signature of HSat2 subfamilies. This is intriguing given previous observation that only a subset of HSat2 loci are expressed in a given cancer cell line or tissue [15] coupled with data presented here that highly specific subfamily probes for HSat2 display different numbers of focal accumulations in the U2OS cell line. Therefore, we set out to isolate and map expressed centric/pericentric satellite sequences in cell lines previously observed to express and accumulate nuclear HSat2 RNA by RNA FISH [15](Table 1). PC3 prostate cancer cells typically harbor 2–4 HSat2 RNA accumulations per nucleus [15], while U2OS osteosarcoma cells average 8–9 RNA accumulations per nucleus (Figure 2). In contrast, HeLa cervical cancer cells do not have HSat2 RNA accumulations [15] and serve here as a negative control for HSat2 expression. However, HeLa cells do express high levels of HSat3 upon heat shock [21] that are observed as focal RNA accumulations that remain in cis and form nuclear stress bodies that are detectable by RNA FISH with an HSat3-specific probe (Figure 3a). RT-qPCR with primers designed to amplify HSat3 confirmed induced expression of HSAT3 upon heat shock (Figure 3b). Given that HSat3 is very closely related in sequence and structure to HSat2 [7] and HSat3 RNA is induced and accumulated in the nucleus upon heat shock (Figure 3), we used heat shock of HeLa cells (HeLa HS) as a positive control to test whether we could both isolate nuclear satellite RNA accumulations and map them to specific genomic loci.

Mapping of nuclear pericentric satellite RNA

Nuclear RNA isolated for each cell line (Table 1) was subjected to Illumina paired end sequencing and trimmed, quality controlled reads were mapped to the human reference transcriptome and the T2T-CHM13 genome using Kallisto [33] and Bowtie2 [32]. In order to determine the optimal mapping parameters for highly repetitive HSat2/3 sequences, we optimized the parameters for alignment using the pseudoalignment program Kallisto [33], as described in the Supplementary Information (Figure S4). Ribo-depleted total RNA from the U2OS cell line was also sequenced in order to confirm enrichment of nuclear transcripts. Differential gene expression (DGE) analysis of T2T-CHM13 CenSat mapping transcripts revealed a subset of CenSat transcripts were highly upregulated in nuclear compared to total RNA from U2OS cells (Figure 4c), confirming the expected nuclear enrichment of CenSat transcripts. When comparing U2OS to HeLa nuclear RNA fractions (Figure 4d), a significantly skewed upregulation of HSat2/3 mapped reads was observed, which supports previous RNA FISH results [15] and demonstrates that the HSAT subset was sufficiently represented in the nuclear RNA fractions.

Comparison of HeLa nuclear RNA sequencing with and without heat shock showed upregulation of annotated heat shock genes and heat-shock associated nuclear transcripts, as expected (Table S2). Differential expression analysis of heat-shock and control HeLa nuclear RNA also showed the greatest upregulation of HSat3 transcripts compared to other CenSat satellite sequences (Figure 5a), providing further confirmation of the specificity of our mapping methods to specific satellite families. In particular, HSat3 sequences mapping to Chr9 showed significant upregulation of mapped transcripts across the entirety of the HSat3 array on Chr9q12 in HeLa HS compared to all other cell lines (Figure 5b). This is of interest because the HSat3 at Chr9q12 locus has previously been shown to be activated upon heat shock [21]. The 10 most significantly upregulated CenSats overwhelmingly belonged to the HSat3 family and one bsat locus (bsat_9_4), which is located embedded within the 9q12 hSat3 region, and one HSat2 locus (HSat2_17_1 (B)) which is located immediately adjacent to a HSat3 locus on Chr 17 (Figure 5c). These results, taken together, demonstrate the ability to identify differentially expressed nuclear CenSat transcripts that map to unique genomic locations and to examine the distribution of satellite-derived transcripts within these genomic regions.

Differential expression of nuclear RNA

Pairwise comparisons were performed for sequences mapping to the Human Reference Transcriptome for the following: 1) HeLa HS vs. HeLa Control, 2) U2OS Nuclear vs. Total RNA, 3) U2OS vs. PC3, 4) U2OS vs. HeLa (Figure 6a,b). These comparisons were performed with the prediction that these were conditions under which HSat2 and/or HSat3 sequences would be expected to be upregulated based on previous observations [15,21,22,35]. Overall, within the Human Reference transcriptome mapping, there were very few shared upregulated genes between these comparisons (Figure 6a). For example, out of a total of 3705 upregulated genes in the comparison of U2OS to HeLa nuclear, there are 1206 that are uniquely upregulated in U2OS nuclear RNA compared to 55 that are shared in all 4 comparisons. In contrast, out of 565 CenSat transcripts upregulated in U2OS compared to HeLa nuclear RNA, only 69 are uniquely upregulated in U2OS compared to 50 that are shared in all 4 comparisons (Figure 6b). This supports that the differential expression observed for CenSats is less cell-type specific and suggests that a large proportion of shared CenSat transcripts are upregulated in all 4 comparisons. In contrast, there is little shared differential expression of protein-coding genes among these same comparisons, which suggests that global transcriptional changes in these cell lines do not correlate with shared CenSat upregulation. U2OS cells express the highest levels of HSat2 of the cell lines examined (Table 1, Figure 2); therefore, we set out to characterize the CenSats most highly upregulated in the comparison of U2OS vs HeLa. CenSat-derived transcripts were first examined in bulk, which revealed that a smaller subset of sequences are highly upregulated relative to others (Figure 6c). When further broken down by CenSat subtype, HSat2, followed by HSat3 sequences were the most upregulated CenSat mapping sequences in the comparison of U2OS to HeLa nuclear RNA (Figure 6d). These results support our previous observations of large nuclear accumulations of HSat2 RNA in U2OS cells [15] and demonstrate that HSat2/3 sequences are specifically upregulated in U2OS cells.

Differential expression of HSAT sequence variants

We then applied the same T2T-CenSat differential expression parameters (Table S1) to analyze the chromosomal locations from which HSat2 transcripts are expressed in U2OS cells. In order to be categorized as an expressed sequence, we established a minimum threshold and required that a minimum of mapped RNA-seq reads (see Methods) were present within one cell type in triplicate. This filtered the expressed HSat2 regions to 13 (out of 61 total HSat2 genomic locations) (Figure 7a, Table S3). The remaining HSat2-containing regions were not considered to be ‘expressed’, although there are some mapped reads within these regions, and thus there may be low-level expressed transcripts emanating from these regions that are not included in this analysis. Of note, the regions identified as expressed were from several different chromosomes and the majority of HSat2 expressed regions contained HSat2B subtype sequences. Examination of the expressed HSat2 genomic regions with mapped RNA-seq profiles showed that the RNA-seq reads typically map along the entirety of the HSat2 containing region, rather than demonstrating peaks of distinct transcript lengths within these regions. For example, there is significant upregulation of HSat2-mapping RNA-seq reads in U2OS cells compared to all other cell lines along the entirety of the length of the HSat2_7_6 (B) locus located on Chr 7 (Figure 7b). The HSat2-containing region on Chr 7 is of particular interest because it was previously found to be expressed [15] and a Chr7-derived HSat2 sequence that was ectopically expressed has been previously shown to accumulate in cis and induce cell division defects [17]. Further examination of a large HSat2/3 containing region in the Chr 7 pericentromere showed a high level of expression across both HSat2 and HSat3 sequences, suggesting this entire locus may be misregulated in the U2OS cell line (Figure 7c). While some HSat2/3 expression was cell-line specific, there was also evidence of other shared loci that were highly expressed in both U2OS and PC3 cell lines (Figure 7d, Table S1), which supports the finding that some satellite containing regions tend to be coordinately misregulated in multiple cell types.

Discussion

Complete genome sequencing using long-read sequencing technology has finally closed the large gaps within the centromere and pericentromere regions of chromosomes, now revealing a complete Telomere-to-Telomere assembly of the human genome [36]. This has presented an exciting opportunity to examine the functional role of sequences residing within these regions. Human centromeres and pericentromeres consist of large arrays of satellite sequences that are often tandemly repeated and are composed of many different classes of centromere satellite RNAs (CenSats, Table S1) that have recently been fully assembled [18]. Analysis of the distribution of HSat2 sequence variants has revealed a differential distribution of specific HSat2 subfamilies A1, A2, and B resident on individual human chromosomes [18,26]. Here, we designed and carried out FISH mapping of these HSat2 subfamilies by designing highly specific oligonucleotide probes to each of the HSat2 subfamilies. Our DNA FISH results in primary male fibroblasts indicate differences in both the number and distributions of HSat2 subfamilies in the human genome, which are largely in agreement with the CenSat annotations in the fully assembled T2T-CHM13 genome.

While subfamily HSat2A2 is abundantly present in Chr 1q12, HSat2B demonstrates a broader distribution on a larger number of chromosomes, including Chrs 7, 9, 10, 16, 17 and 22. Overall, subfamilies A2 and B are more highly represented on different chromosomes compared to A1 (Table 1). These chromosome-specific differences in subfamily distribution are likely to be of consequence due to the abundance of evidence supporting chromosome-specific misregulation of HSat2 expression in cancer [14,15,17] and the sequence-specific hypomethylation of HSat2 sequences in cancer [10] and ICF syndrome [13]. While some HSat2 loci (e.g. Chr 1, 16) recruit repressive polycomb proteins and remain transcriptionally silent, in the same cancer cells other HSat2 loci such as those on Chr 7 and 10 are prone to expression and do not recruit polycomb proteins [15]. Results presented here support that HSat2 subfamilies resident on these chromosomes are composed of different HSat2 subfamilies. For example, while HSat2A2 is enriched on Chr 1, HSat2B is enriched on Chr 7, which is devoid of HSat2A1 and A2 subfamilies. These sequence-specific signatures of HSat2 resident on individual chromosomes may underlie the differential regulation of these HSat2 loci in cancer cells. Supporting this, the HSat2B subfamily displays a distinct expression pattern compared to HSat2A1 and HSat2A2 (Figures 2 and 7).

The design of highly specific HSat2 subfamily-specific probes was possible with 24-mer oligonucleotides representing the subfamily definition defined from previous pairwise clustering of k-mers [26]. The amount of subfamily represented on a chromosome correlates with the feasibility of binding for the probe, which influences the amount and quality of hybridized signal detected. While largely in agreement, close comparison of our stringent subtype-specific probe hybridization in the AG04449 cell line to T2T-CHM13 CenSat annotations [18] revealed some discrepancies. In particular, the large Hsat2 arrays on Chrs 1 and 16 were detected by all HSat2 subfamily FISH probes. While each subfamily is included in the T2T-CHM13 annotation for Chr 1, Chr 16 appears to be homogeneous for HSat2B in CHM13, whereas all three of our subfamily FISH probes hybridized to Chr 16 in AG04449 (Figure 1). This may be due to the extremely large size of the Chr 16 HSat2 array (12.7Mb) [18] and potential for cross-hybridization of probes to similar sequences despite the high stringency of the hybridization conditions. This may also indicate that different individuals harbor different HSat2 subfamilies on individual chromosomes, but similar hybridization results were also obtained for Chr 16 on a female cell line (data not shown), thus our FISH analysis suggests that the HSat2 locus on chromosome 16 may have more diverse sequence composition than previously identified. A more detailed analysis of the HSat2 sequences resident on each of these chromosomes in multiple individuals can be used to design more specific and effective FISH probes in the future. Moving away from the use of consensus sequences to define and detect satellite subfamilies to stretches of more unique, contiguous sequences holds promise toward the development of chromosome-specific HSat2 sequence probes for detection and targeting. Future work, including analysis of the diversity of satellite sequences resident within centromeres and pericentromeres between individuals in the human population, is likely to make this an exciting reality.

Results presented here represent, to our knowledge, the first characterization of differentially expressed, nuclear-restricted centromere satellite-derived RNA. We adapted an open-source differential gene expression analysis platform [37] based on the Kallisto pseudoalignment program and R/Bioconductor to specifically analyze centromere satellite-derived expression in cell culture conditions that had been previously demonstrated to induce satellite expression. While normally subject to strict epigenetic repression, including DNA demethylation and repressive histone modifications, pericentric satellites are highly expressed in some cell types and conditions. For example, normal human fibroblasts treated with 5-aza-2’-deoxycytidine leads to demethylation followed by HSat2 locus-specific expression [15] and HSat2 bidirectional expression is induced by expression of DUX4 in FSHD [38]. Derepression of satellite transcripts also correlates with overexpression of long interspersed nuclear element (LINE-1) retrotransposons and the expression of neuroendocrine-related genes in close proximity to LINE-1 insertion [14]. Upon heat shock, HSat3 expression is known to be rapidly induced from the G-rich strand [22], where these transcripts accumulate and form nuclear stress bodies that recruit RNA binding proteins [39]. This is correlated with upregulation of heat-shock related proteins, intron retention, and ultimately, transcriptional repression of protein-coding genes (reviewed in [40]. Our results present a comprehensive analysis of the satellite sequences differentially expressed upon heat shock and demonstrate that the pseudoalignment-based mapper Kallisto can be used to identify differentially expressed tandem repeat sequences when the T2T-CHM13 CenSat track is used as the reference transcriptome.

Differential expression analysis revealed that upon heat shock, HSat3 transcripts are dynamically and globally upregulated in the nucleus. This increased expression emanates from the entire HSat3 locus on Chr 9 (Figure 5b) and other HSat3 genomic locations, while other CenSat-derived transcripts remain mostly unchanged (Figure 5a). These results demonstrate that pericentric satellite sequences are regulated in a locus-specific manner upon specific cellular conditions. Further analysis of the specific HSat3 transcripts that are upregulated has the promise to reveal the regulatory mechanisms responsible for this, including the identification of promoter regions within these loci and the epigenetic changes that allow for this rapidly induced expression. Characterization of the HSat3 transcripts within the nucleus will also allow for the further identification of RNA binding partners and potential modifications of HSat3 transcripts [41] mediating heat-shock induced nuclear stress body formation and heat stress recovery.

The analysis of HSat3 transcription upon heat shock allowed us the opportunity to develop and validate our differential expression pipeline for nuclear restricted, tandemly repeated satellite transcripts derived from centric and pericentric regions of the genome and to apply this to the study of HSat2 transcription in cancer cells. The previous observation that not all HSat2 loci are expressed in a given cancer cell line or tissue [15] suggests there may be cancer-specific signatures of HSat2 expression. This is further supported by different amounts of HSat2 RNA that can be detected in the nuclei of cancer cell lines such as PC3 and U2OS (Table 1). Here, we identified the HSat2 loci most highly expressed in U2OS and PC3 cells compared to HeLa cells, which do not express HSat215. In the comparison of U2OS to HeLa cells, HSat2 sequences, followed by HSat3, are highly upregulated while other CenSat transcripts remain mostly unchanged (Figure 6d). We identified 13 locations from which HSat2 sequences are most highly expressed across all cell types (Figure 6a). While HSat2 is expressed from several different chromosomes, these transcripts are differentially expressed in the cell types we examined (Table S1). We previously demonstrated expression of Chr7-derived HSat2 sequences [15] and found that RNA from a Chr7-derived HSat2 transgene accumulated in the nucleus of primary cells and led to cell division defects [17]. Three of the most highly expressed HSat2 genomic locations are located on Chr 7 and examination of the distribution of RNA-seq reads across this region suggests that the entire region is highly expressed in U2OS cells compared to all other cell lines (Figure 7c) supporting that there is specific misregulation of this locus in U2OS cells. The mechanism leading to expression of sequences on Chr7 is currently unknown, but this could be due to chromatin changes and/or lack of DNA methylation across this region in this cell line. Notably, this Chr7 HSat2 region is composed entirely of HSat2B sequences, thus it may also be the case that this subtype of HSat2 is more prone to expression independently of chromatin changes, which is supported by our RNA FISH data indicating some U2OS nuclei express very high levels of HSat2B (Figure 2). Further, all but one of the highly expressed HSat2 variants are composed of the HSat2B subtype (Figure 7a).

Transcription of HSat2 sequences has been shown to be induced by binding of the DUX4 transcription factor in an FSHD model [38], which leads to the accumulation of DNA damage via sequestration of PRC1 protein components [42]. Thus, further analysis of the HSat2-proximal genomic regions, identification of chromatin changes at individual HSat2B-containing regions, and testing of the ability of each of the different HSat2 subtypes to accumulate and recruit proteins in the nucleus will be required to shed light on the cause and consequence of differential expression of specific HSat2 variants. Results presented here are a significant first step in identifying subtype-specific HSat2 sequences that are differentially expressed and generate a rich dataset of nuclear-retained, centromere-satellite derived transcripts for further study. This also represents a significant step forward in developing computational methods to specifically map and define differentially expressed satellite sequences that was made possible by the completion of a fully assembled telomere-to-telomere human genome [36]. With the identification of HSat2 expressed sequence variants mapped to specific genomic loci, we now have a resource with the exciting potential to design highly specific therapeutic tools, to perturb interactions with specific-binding partners, and to further our understanding of how HSat2 expression contributes to cancer progression.
