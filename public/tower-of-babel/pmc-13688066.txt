ABSTRACT
In this post hoc analysis of the ANNEXa‐I trial (n = 434), we evaluated non‐contrast computed tomography (NCCT) markers of hematoma expansion (HE) in factor Xa inhibitor (FXai) –associated intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH). At least one NCCT marker was present in 72.1%, and HE (≥12.5 mL or ≥ 35% increase in hematoma volume) occurred in 31.8%. Heterogeneous density, hypodensity, and the black hole sign were independently associated with HE. The effect of andexanet on HE was generally consistent across NCCT marker subgroups. These findings support the utility of NCCT markers for identifying patients at high risk of HE in FXai‐associated ICH. ANN NEUROL 2026;100:979–983

Non‐contrast computed tomography (NCCT) markers, including density and shape heterogenous markers, were validated to predict patients at high risk of hematoma expansion (HE) in patients with antithrombotic‐associated intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH). 1 Factor Xa (FXa) inhibitors (FXai) substantially increases the risk of HE in patients with ICH. 2 , 3 Recent reports have demonstrated that andexanet shows radiologic hemostatic efficacy in patients with FXai‐associated intracranial hemorrhage. 4 , 5 , 6 Given an increased risk of thrombotic events, 7 identifying a subgroup of patients who are most at risk of HE and would benefit from andexanet might aid in clinical decision‐making.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the predictive value of NCCT markers of HE in patients with acute FXai‐associated ICH and to determine whether the presence of these markers modifies the treatment effects of andexanet, using baseline imaging data from the ANNEXa‐I.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This study was a post‐hoc secondary analysis of the ANNEXa‐I trial who met the qualifying criteria for ICH. 6 Patients with the most recent FXai taken within 15 h and presenting within 6 h from symptom onset were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive andexanet or usual care. Follow‐up imaging was performed within 15 h from randomization. We used the CONSORT reporting guideline for reporting the findings of this post‐hoc analysis. 8

Imaging Analysis

Baseline and follow‐up hematoma volumes were quantified by trained physicians at a central imaging core laboratory who were blinded to treatment assignment using Quantomo software (Cybertrials Inc, Calgary, Canada). NCCT markers were evaluated on the intraparenchymal hemorrhage. Concomitant intraventricular, subarachnoid, or subdural hemorrhage could be present but were not assessed for NCCT markers. Baseline NCCT markers of HE (heterogenous density [Barras density scale III–V], swirl sign, hypodensity, black hole sign, blend sign, fluid level, irregular shape [Barras shape scale III–V], island sign, and satellite lesions) were independently assessed by two of three experienced stroke neurologists (K.T., U.P., and M.A.) who were blinded to treatment allocation and outcome. An instruction manual was provided to assist with the interpretation of each of these imaging findings (Table S1). 9 All NCCT markers were evaluated on the axial NCCT thickest slices. The coronal images were used when available to confirm the presence or absence of continuity between any hypodensity and the surrounding tissue, thereby discriminating between the swirl sign and hypodensity. Representative images of the evaluated NCCT markers are shown in Figure S1. We used a three‐step agreement process: (i) two readers read the baseline imaging of each patient independently, (ii) in case of initial disagreement between them, the consensus was reached by discussion between the two readers or by the involvement of the third reader to produce majority decision, (iii) for challenging cases or crucial discordance, a panel review with an Expert Committee (A.M.D., A.S., A.H.K., M.S., P.B., S.K., and S.R.) was arranged for consensus.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was a significant HE, defined as ≥12.5 ml absolute or ≥ 35% relative increase in hematoma volume between baseline and follow‐up imaging. 10 The secondary outcome was severe HE, defined as ≥12.5 ml or ≥ 66% increase. 11

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with SAS software (Version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Multivariable analysis was performed by logistic regression for patients without missing variables. Each NCCT marker was adjusted with time from symptom onset to scan, baseline hematoma volume, baseline anti‐FXa activity, and baseline diastolic blood pressure, based on previous post‐hoc analyses of ANNEXa‐I that explored independent clinical predictors of hemostatic efficacy. 10 The treatment effect of andexanet versus usual care on outcomes was investigated by examining the frequency of HE in both arms, and an interaction term of the treatment effect was calculated by determining the presence or absence of each NCCT marker. A similar analysis was performed for severe HE. A p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Patients' Characteristics

Among 530 patients in the intention‐to‐treat population, 434 (197 female, mean age 79.3 ± 8.1 years) had qualifying ICH, baseline NCCT, and follow‐up imaging available for assessment of HE and were included in this study (Figure S2). HE was observed in 138 patients (31.8%). The characteristics of the patients are shown in Table S2. Slice thickness was mainly 3 mm (32.0%) or 5 mm (28.3%; Table S3). The coronal NCCT image was available in 50.5% of patients.

Associations between NCCT Markers and HE

A total of 313 patients (72.1%) had at least 1 marker, including swirl sign (63.4%), satellite lesion (39.6%), irregular shape (35.0%), hypodensity (34.1%), island sign (20.5%), heterogenous density (17.1%), black hole sign (15.2%), blend sign (7.8%), and/or fluid level (1.6%). Percent agreement between the two reviewers exceeded 80% for all the NCCT markers (Figure S3). Heterogenous density (odds ratio [OR] 2.49, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.34–4.60), hypodensity (OR 2.20, 95%CI 1.36–3.57), and black hole sign (OR 2.63, 95% CI 1.45–4.78) were significantly associated with HE (Table 1).

Treatment Interaction of Andexanet

Figure 1 shows the subgroup analysis of NCCT markers and the treatment effect of andexanet. The absolute reduction in HE with andexanet appeared greater in patients with hypodensity (19.5% vs. 8.0%) than those without. A treatment interaction was observed for the blend sign (OR 0.03, 95% CI 0.00–0.29 for those with blend sign; and OR 0.60, 95% CI 0.38–0.96 for those without, p for interaction = 0.01).

Discussion

In this study, heterogeneous density, hypodensity, and the black hole sign were associated with significant HE and severe HE in patients with acute FXai‐associated ICH. These density‐based markers likely reflect recent bleeding, characterized by fluid blood with lower attenuation compared with the surrounding more mature hematoma. 12 Our findings are consistent with the previous ANNEXa‐I secondary analysis, 13 which found that the relative effect of andexanet on HE was generally consistent across subgroups defined by baseline hematoma volume, time from onset to treatment, prescan hematoma growth rate, and baseline diastolic blood pressure. These findings suggest that the effect of andexanet may be broadly preserved across a range of clinical and imaging characteristics. Prior studies have shown that the predictive value of these markers is greater when imaging is performed within an early time window (i.e., within 3 hours of symptom onset). 14 , 15 In our study, the median time from symptom onset to baseline imaging was 2.3 hours, which may have strengthened the observed associations between these markers and HE. While relative treatment effects were generally consistent across NCCT marker subgroups, patients with NCCT markers might derive greater absolute benefit from andexanet because of their higher baseline risk of HE. This finding may be clinically relevant when considering hemostatic treatments in patients with FXai‐associated ICH. A treatment interaction was observed for the blend sign, another density‐based marker reflecting recent bleeding and clot retraction. 16 However, given the small sample size, multiple interaction tests performed, and the exploratory nature of the analysis, this finding should be considered hypothesis‐generating. Our findings support the utility of early NCCT imaging to identify patients with FXai‐associated ICH who are at high risk for HE and may benefit from intensive monitoring or enrich patient selection for enrollment in future hemostatic treatment clinical trials. Given the low frequency of some NCCT markers in our cohort, larger studies are needed to evaluate whether NCCT markers of HE can mark those patients who will specifically benefit from andexanet.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, and some NCCT markers were infrequent, which may have introduced selection bias and limited the statistical power to detect certain associations. Second, the study included patients with ICH regardless of location, presence of concurrent subdural, subarachnoid, or intraventricular hemorrhage, and underlying etiology. Although intraventricular hemorrhage is associated with mortality, 17 the relationship between NCCT markers and intraventricular hemorrhage growth is still unclear. Moreover, most NCCT markers evaluated in this study were originally developed and validated in patients with spontaneous intraparenchymal hemorrhage and the performance of these markers has not been extensively studied in traumatic hemorrhage. Third, imaging acquisition parameters were not standardized across cases. Last, the study did not explore the association between NCCT markers and thromboembolic events and the ANNEXa‐I study was not designed to assess the effect of andexanet on long‐term clinical outcomes.

In conclusion, heterogeneous density, hypodensity, and black hole were associated with significant HE and severe HE in patients with acute FXai‐associated ICH. These findings suggest that NCCT markers may help identify patients with FXai‐associated ICH who are at high risk of HE. Larger studies are needed to determine whether NCCT markers can identify patients who derive greater benefit from hemostatic treatments.
