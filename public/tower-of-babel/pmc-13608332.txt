Not Just a Strategy, But the Heartbeat of Our Work: The Importance of Youth Engagement in Drug-Free Communities Coalitions
Authors: Chris Taylor, Jerria Martin, Wendy Cummings, Luke Jarvis, Olga Costa, Afra Hussain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTContext:The role of youth engagement within Drug-Free Communities (DFC) coalitions, strategies used by coalitions, and actionable recommendations for new and expanding DFC programs.Program:Youth engagement in DFC coalitions is most effective when coalitions position youth as co-creators, leaders, and drivers of environmental change.Implementation:Exploring youth engagement from the perspective of empowerment, youth-adult partnerships, youth as active co-constructors, focus on environmental change, and skill enhancement. Each focus area will draw on examples from coalitions receiving DFC funding.Discussion:Findings draw on DFC National Cross-Site Evaluation data and qualitative examples from DFC coalitions. Coalitions using these strategies and values experience stronger engagement, sustainability, and community impact.

Introduction

A core strategy of the Drug-Free Communities (DFC) Support Program to prevent youth substance use is youth engagement, an approach in which young people are actively involved as leaders or partners at all stages of decision making for community substance use prevention efforts.1 Research by Campbell and Erbstein2 has shown that engaging youth in community change efforts can result in significant benefits, such as youth bringing unique insight about their own lives, energy and creativity, and access to networks that can enhance the effectiveness of efforts. It also has the potential to build the next generation of leaders, increase credibility among decision makers, and create positive relationships with adults in the community, which is associated with improved health outcomes for youth.2 Although this approach may require more time to build trusted relationships and more resources to ensure youth involvement, prevention efforts that embrace youth as true partners—valuing their generational perspectives and lived experiences—result in stronger youth engagement, longer-term sustainability, and deeper community impact.

Context

The DFC Program is the nation's leading effort to mobilize communities to prevent youth substance use.1 The program provides grant awards each year to community-based coalitions to use strategies that limit access to substances, change the culture and context around how decisions about substance use are made, shift the consequences associated with substance use to promote healthy options, and address barriers to achieving optimal health and well-being.1

Although DFC coalitions are required to bring together community representatives from 12 sectors, which are essential coalition partners,3 youth play a critical role in DFC coalitions and are actively involved in leading the development and implementation of activities to prevent youth substance use. As a cornerstone of the DFC Program, coalitions promote youth engagement through 5 critical concepts and activities: empowerment, youth-adult partnerships, youth as active co-constructors, connecting youth to their surrounding environment, and skill enhancement. This manuscript describes each of these focus areas, provides examples of how DFC coalitions involve youth not merely as participants but as co-creators, leaders, and active drivers of community change, and offers considerations for coalitions interested in engaging youth.4–6

Empowerment

Empowerment is “the process of becoming stronger and more confident, especially in controlling one's life and claiming one's rights.”7 As children grow into adolescents and young adults, they become more independent in their ability to make decisions about themselves and their place in the world. Often through trial and error, young people may make decisions that put their health and safety at risk.

A common example of risk taking in youth is experimenting with substances. Because of the prevalence of these behaviors, it is important and timely for substance use prevention efforts to begin before children and adolescents decide to experiment with substances.8,9 This is why many prevention programs, including DFC, focus efforts on young people under the age of 18 to empower them to make healthier decisions.1

Adolescent empowerment is associated with greater prosocial engagement, responsible decision making, supportive peer interactions, and with lower levels of delinquency.10 In addition to DFC coalitions' efforts to influence decisions like experimenting with substances, they provide an excellent training ground to empower young people by practicing communication, priority setting, decision making, and collaboration.3

A common engagement and empowerment activity among many DFC coalitions is hosting a youth-led coalition, with 69% of coalitions hosting a youth coalition in 2024.3 In these coalitions, youth participants are highly involved in the planning of prevention activities in their communities.3 Youth coalitions not only empower youth to make healthy decisions for themselves but also foster the development of leadership to ultimately drive change in schools and communities.3

DFC coalitions frequently host youth coalitions as a strategy for empowering youth. For example, Wilkes Youth Life Development (WYLD) (Wilkes County, NC) convenes the Teen Action Council, a youth-led coalition serving middle and high school students ages 12–18. Youth help identify community needs, plan and implement programs, speak at public meetings, and lead peer-focused efforts. Since launching in mid-2022, approximately 50 youth have served as core coalition members, with many remaining involved for multiple years and advancing into leadership roles. This approach has resulted in consistent participation, growth of leadership capacity, and a culture in Wilkes County where youth are recognized as essential partners in prevention and community change.4

The Drug-Free Communities of Dallas County (Selma, Alabama) also empowers youth to shape prevention activities in their community. Those involved in the program are referred to as “Hope Dealers,” as they are integral in spreading hope and equipping young people and their peers with the skills needed to avoid substance use. Youth Hope Dealers serve as peer educators, content creators, media hosts, and community ambassadors. The program gives voice to young people through these roles and empowers them to lead prevention projects that will have a positive influence on their peers.5

Youth-Adult Partnerships

The coalition model provides opportunities for youth-adult partnerships to take hold by creating environments where youth are seen as capable, critical contributors, and equal decision makers.11 Youth attending coalition meetings can voice their perspective on the issues affecting their peers, the realities of young people's lives, and effective ways to reach them, whereas adults can use their experience to guide effective outcomes. As previously discussed, youth-led coalitions are a common component of the DFC Program and are shown to effectively engage young people.3 Here, adults often serve as mentors and facilitators for youth-led activities, providing structure, safety, and technical support.3,4

Examples from DFC coalitions highlight the benefit of partnerships for both youth and adults in achieving the coalition's goals. For instance, as discussed earlier, Drug-Free Communities of Dallas County elevates youth into true leadership roles to ensure youth participation is genuine.5 WYLD's Teen Action Council intentionally leaves decision making and creative direction to young people whenever possible. In both coalitions, adults serve as supportive guides, allowing youth voices to shape messaging, tone, and program design. This approach builds authentic ownership and ensures youth engagement is meaningful, not just symbolic.4

In addition, the Oyate Prevention Coalition (Rapid City, SD) involves Lakota traditions to help youth build confidence and provides a transformative experience for Indigenous youth. The experience fosters meaningful youth engagement and builds relationships with trusted adults while celebrating cultural traditions. A powerful testimonial from an adult attendee stated: “In the heart of our community, under the wide-open skies, I observed children finding healing and strength through the wisdom of their ancestors. They are reconnecting youth with their heritage… families gather around the fire, sharing stories and laughter, strengthening the bonds that form the foundation of their culture. With every sunset, the children witness the beauty of a community united, and with every sunrise, they are empowered to continue the journey of their ancestors. This event breathes life and love into traditions and makes it possible for the children to witness this living spirit.”6

Active Co-constructors

It is important for youth to serve as active co-constructors alongside adults in the planning and implementation of DFC activities.3 This involves not just youth attending coalition meetings but being active participants in addressing the challenges facing their peers. Often, programs serving young people will create activities, resources, and tools for, rather than with, the youth they serve. By not having youth involved as active co-constructors or providing their valuable lived experience, the activity or product may miss the mark.5 Involving youth as active co-constructors creates a greater sense of ownership among youth and potential for improved substance use prevention outcomes.11

Through their youth members, Drug-Free Communities of Dallas County creatively engages young people in a rural county where youth often feel unseen. They strive to create prevention projects that resonate with their peers. Examples of these projects include youth-led music videos featuring local youth, school leaders, clergy, and law enforcement; a youth-led cooking show teaching substance-free living, life skills, and positive coping strategies; youth-designed social-media challenges, PSA videos, and billboards; and peer-support campaigns that feature young people speaking in their own voice.5 This approach ensures that prevention messaging is not top-down; it is peer-generated, relatable, and deeply embedded in youth culture.

WYLD has intentionally worked to model healthy relationships, build positive environments, and provide meaningful experiences that not only educate young people but inspire them to take ownership of community well-being.4 Youth have stepped into leadership roles, designed and delivered prevention messaging, and participated in community events that highlight healthy decision making. Their involvement has strengthened a sense of community by elevating youth voices, increasing awareness about mental health, and increasing awareness of local resources.

Young people drive this progress. Their energy, creativity, and insight have transformed the coalition's work and strengthened prevention efforts across Wilkes County. WYLD's commitment to building resilience and protective factors has not only supported youth development; it has contributed to a stronger, healthier rural community overall.4

Connecting Youth to the Community

The concept of person-in-environment is a theoretical framework for understanding how individuals are deeply connected to the environments in which they live, learn, and work.12 Whether that is their physical, cultural, or social surroundings, the concept acknowledges that both internal and external factors influence who a person is and their response to the world around them.12 A more specific aspect of person-in-environment is systems theory, which views a person as part of an interconnected system including family, neighborhood, and society.13 It is important to consider these concepts when exploring the design of substance use prevention programming, as the reasons people may choose to use substances are multifactorial.

It is also important to acknowledge that young people's families, social networks, schools, and communities all play a role in their development of skills, critical thinking, and access to the tools needed to make informed decisions about their lives. The young people who are part of Drug-Free Communities of Dallas County have transformed how they interact in their community. They are no longer passive recipients of prevention; they are leading it by drawing on their life experiences. They are shaping their own narrative, using their own creativity, and building a healthier culture for their peers. Their leadership has brought pride, visibility, and healing to a community that has faced generations of challenges, making Dallas County a powerful example of what happens when you put youth at the center of prevention.5

WYLD encourages youth to volunteer with other programs across the organization and connects them to the broader community. This allows them to make an impact across different prevention aspects while reinforcing empathy, responsibility, and leadership. This approach has resulted in consistent participation, growing leadership capacity, and a culture in Wilkes County where youth are recognized as essential partners in prevention and community change.4

Skill Enhancement

Although it is important for youth to understand the dangers of substance use, knowledge alone is not always enough to prevent initial experimentation, especially in situations involving peer pressure.14 Combining knowledge with social, emotional, and cognitive skills that support everyday decision making is a more effective strategy for preventing substance use. These skills foster resilience, strengthen decision making, and reduce susceptibility to peer influence.15 Youth can then apply what they have learned and, through their leadership, encourage positive decision making within their peer groups. Although the primary aim of the DFC Program is to prevent or reduce youth substance use, youth can use and promote these skills in all aspects of their lives.

Examples of youth-led skill enhancement activities among DFC coalitions include delivering training programs (eg, Mental Health First Aid), leading mentoring groups, peer modeling, providing space for people who have experienced trauma, grief, and loss, and engaging in activities that reconnect people with their cultural history and traditions.4–6 Nearly 800 ninth-grade students attended a teen Mental Health First Aid training, an ongoing effort organized by WYLD, Wilkes County Schools, and Wilkes Health.4 This important training provided young people in the community with skills on how to recognize signs of mental health and substance use challenges among their peers.4 WYLD also led community-focused efforts such as creating and presenting proposals to the Town of North Wilkesboro for clearer social district signage (areas where alcoholic beverages can be carried in to-go containers). After approval from the Town, WYLD youth helped train local law enforcement on social district boundaries and safety considerations, demonstrating their ability to contribute meaningfully to public safety and policy implementation.4

Drug-Free Communities of Dallas County uses a peer-led model for promoting positive youth development by teaching both life skills, such as coping, and practical skills including building community awareness through public messaging. This model has increased youth engagement and contributed to the growth of the youth coalition from 1 small coalition to a full youth-led movement that has reshaped the culture of an entire rural county in Alabama. The program now has over 150 Hope Dealers across every middle and high school in the county, with plans to expand into elementary schools.5

Discussion

Youth engagement is essential to preventing youth substance use, and the DFC Program values adults and youth working together to create drug-free communities. This manuscript describes key components of meaningful youth engagement and provides examples from DFC coalitions of these concepts in practice. Information gained from DFC coalitions regarding their youth engagement efforts and strategies reveals a broad range of creative strategies to 1) empower participants, 2) encourage youth-adult partnerships, 3) nurture youth as active co-constructors, 4) connect youth to their community, and 5) identify opportunities for skill enhancement.

Consistent with these qualitative examples, DFC National Cross-Site Evaluation data indicate that youth engagement is both widespread and meaningful.3 For example, DFC coalitions hosting a youth coalition reported, on average, significantly higher involvement from the youth sector than those not hosting a youth coalition.3 Youth engagement data from the DFC Program show overall high rates of
