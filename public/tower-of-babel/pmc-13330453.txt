Cross-population validation of European GWAS loci for metabolic syndrome in Chinese schizophrenia
Authors: Yangyang You, Ning Qiao, Yao Chen, Zhou Wang, Lirong Zhuang, Xinyu Fang, Xiaowei Tang, Xiangrong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLong-term use of second-generation antipsychotics (SGAs) increases the risk of metabolic syndrome (MS) in patients with schizophrenia (SCZ). Using susceptibility loci identified by European Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) as entry points, we conducted a case–control study to verify their association with antipsychotic-induced MS in Chinese Han SCZ patients.MethodsClinical and metabolic data were collected from 528 chronically SCZ patients who had been treated with SGAs for ≥ 12 months. Patients were divided into MS (n = 232) and non-MS (n = 296) groups. Forty tag single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) selected from European GWAS data were genotyped; inter-group comparisons and risk analyses for MS-related factors were performed.ResultsCompared with the non-MS group, the MS group exhibited significantly elevated waist circumference, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), triglycerides (TG), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), and body-mass index (BMI), alongside significantly reduced age and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels (all P < 0.05). Allele-based analysis revealed that the G allele of G-protein–coupled receptor 98 (GPR98) rs1967256 was more prevalent in MS patients (χ² = 4.049, P = 0.046), whereas the T allele of Tudor domain-containing protein 15 / Long intergenic non-protein coding RNA 1822 (TDRD15/LINC01822) rs1117324 showed reduced frequency (χ² = 6.639, P = 0.011). Genotype analysis further indicated an over-representation of the rs1117324 T/T genotype in the MS group (χ² = 10.833, P = 0.004). Background

Schizophrenia (SCZ) is a common yet etiologically unresolved mental disorder characterized by a wide range of clinical symptoms. It typically exerts severe impairment on patients’ thought, emotion, and behavior, carries a high risk of long-term disability, and markedly compromises social functioning and quality of life, constituting a major global public-health challenge [1–3]. Patients with SCZ face a higher all-cause mortality risk than the general healthy population, with an average life-expectancy reduction of about 20% [4]. Glucose and lipid metabolic abnormalities substantially elevate mortality in SCZ; our group recently reported in MBC Medicine that SCZ patients have a 3.87-fold higher risk of death from diabetes than the general population [5]. Therefore, investigating the biological mechanisms underlying glucolipid metabolic disturbances in SCZ is of great importance.

Pharmacotherapy remains the cornerstone of SCZ management. Compared with first-generation antipsychotics (FGAs), second-generation antipsychotics (SGAs) offer superior clinical efficacy, a markedly lower incidence of extrapyramidal side-effects (EPS) [6], and better medication adherence, and are therefore used far more extensively in everyday practice. Accumulating evidence indicates that SGAs can induce metabolic disturbances, including weight gain, dyslipidemia, and elevated blood pressure [7, 8]. Epidemiological data show that the global prevalence of metabolic syndrome (MS) among patients with SCZ is as high as 41.3%—meaning that nearly one in two patients is affected [9]. Moreover, a domestic study reported that the prevalence of MS reached 44% in patients with schizophrenia receiving olanzapine monotherapy for 2 consecutive years [10]. Consequently, these patients face a markedly increased risk of developing MS [11–13]. Therefore, greater attention must be paid to preventing and reducing metabolic-syndrome-related morbidity and mortality in people with SCZ.

Despite the high risk of MS induced by SGAs in SCZ, not all patients develop metabolic disturbances, indicating marked inter-individual variability. Identifying the mechanisms underlying antipsychotic-related obesity is therefore essential, as it could guide personalized prescribing. In recent years, accumulating evidence has confirmed a genetic contribution to SGA-induced MS [14–16]. In addition, the Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS) approach has been introduced and applied to investigations of drug response [17–19].

International work has already examined the heritability of antipsychotic-induced weight gain in SCZ. Daniel E. Adkins et al. performed a GWAS on 738 SCZ participants from the Clinical Antipsychotic Trials of Intervention Effectiveness (CATIE), successfully genotyping 492 K SNPs and systematically screening for loci associated with antipsychotic-related MS [19]. In China, single-gene association studies have already linked polymorphisms in genes such as ANKK1 to second-generation antipsychotic-induced MS [20].

To date, no systematic GWAS has been conducted on antipsychotic-induced MS in Chinese Han populations. Given the significant genetic heterogeneity and differences in linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns across ethnicities [21–25], it remains to be elucidated whether the susceptibility loci identified by previous Western GWAS are significantly associated in this population. Tag single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) that are highly correlated in European populations may not serve as optimal markers in Asian populations, or their allele frequencies may differ significantly. Furthermore, Asian populations often exhibit unique phenotypic characteristics, typically developing metabolic disturbances at relatively lower body-mass index (BMI) levels [26, 27]. Among the candidate loci, G-protein–coupled receptor 98 (GPR98) encodes a receptor implicated in synaptic function and cardiovascular risk [28–31], whereas the Tudor domain-containing protein 15 (TDRD15) region is associated with lipid metabolism and adipose tissue regulation [32–35].

Therefore, we used susceptibility loci identified by previous foreign GWAS as entry points and conducted a case–control study to verify their association with antipsychotic-induced MS in Chinese Han patients with SCZ. We aimed to validate the association of previously reported genetic loci with antipsychotic-induced metabolic syndrome in Han Chinese patients with schizophrenia. We hypothesized that the genetic loci underlying antipsychotic-related glucolipid metabolic abnormalities may be either shared or distinct between Han Chinese patients with schizophrenia and Western populations.

Methods

Subjects

A total of 528 Han-Chinese in-patients with SCZ were recruited from Changzhou Dean Hospital and Yangzhou Wutaishan Hospital of Jiangsu Province, between January 2024 and April 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (1) a primary diagnosis of SCZ according to International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10) criteria; (2) age ≥ 45 years, both sexes eligible; patients < 45 years must have no family history of hereditary metabolic disorders and documented absence of glucose/lipid abnormalities before antipsychotic treatment. These criteria were applied to minimize the confounding effects of physiological aging and genetic predisposition on metabolic profiles. This design ensures that subsequent metabolic disturbances in patients are primarily attributable to long-term administration of second-generation antipsychotics, thereby reducing sample heterogeneity and improving the statistical power of our genetic association analysis; (3) continuous treatment with one or more SGAs (e.g., clozapine, olanzapine, quetiapine) for ≥ 12 months; (4) illness duration ≥ 2 years and clinical stability for > 6 months. Clinical stability for more than 6 months, which was defined as no change or escalation in antipsychotic dosage over the 6 months prior to enrollment. Exclusion criteria were: pregnancy or lactation; any comorbid organic brain disorder (e.g., Parkinson’s disease, epilepsy); dependence on or abuse of psychoactive substances; and any additional primary psychiatric diagnosis other than SCZ. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Changzhou Dean Hospital. All procedures were strictly performed in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Methods

Clinical data collection and grouping procedure

General clinical data were collected for all enrolled patients, including age, sex, illness duration, antipsychotic types and dosages, and history of metabolic disorders. Height, body weight, blood pressure and waist circumference were measured, and BMI was calculated. On the morning after enrollment, fasting venous blood was drawn to measure fasting plasma glucose (FPG), triglycerides (TG), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), with all assays performed on a Beckman Coulter AU5800 fully automated chemistry analyzer (Beckman Coulter, USA). MS was defined as the presence of ≥ 3 of the following five criteria, in accordance with the U.S. National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (ATP-III) [36]: (1) Abdominal (central) obesity: waist circumference ≥ 90 cm in men or ≥ 85 cm in women; (2) Hyperglycaemia: FPG ≥ 6.1 mmol/L, or 2-h post-load glucose ≥ 7.8 mmol/L, and/or established diabetes; (3) Hypertension: blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg (1 mmHg = 0.133 kPa), and/or diagnosed hypertension; (4) Fasting TG ≥ 1.7 mmol/L; (5) Fasting HDL-C < 1.04 mmol/L.

Genotyping of candidate polymorphisms

(1) Locus selection: Forty tag SNPs were chosen on the basis of published international data (Table S1) [19]. Pairwise evaluation revealed two sets in linkage disequilibrium: rs1967256 with rs11954387 and rs1117324 with rs1991126. To avoid redundancy, rs1967256 and rs1117324 were retained as proxies for their respective bins and used in all subsequent analyses. (2) DNA extraction and genotyping: Two milliliters of peripheral venous blood were drawn from each participant, collected in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)-anticoagulant tubes. One milliliter was used for DNA extraction, and the remaining 1 mL was stored at − 80 °C as a backup. DNA extraction was performed using the CWE960 Blood DNA Extraction Kit (Jiangsu Cowin Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The 40 selected loci were genotyped using the improved multiplex ligation detection reaction (imLDR) assay [37–39]. The main steps comprised: designing allele-specific and fluorescence-labeled probes for each SNP; multiplex PCR amplification of the target regions containing the SNPs, purification of amplicons with Exonuclease I and Shrimp Alkaline Phosphatase (ExoI/SAP) enzymes followed by multiplex ligation; separation of ligation products on an ABI 3730XL capillary electrophoresis platform, and genotype calling with GeneMapper 4.1 software. For quality control, the genotyping call rate for all 40 tag SNPs was > 98%. Furthermore, the genotype distributions of all 40 polymorphisms in the non-MS group were in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (P > 0.05).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 29.0. Continuous variables were compared between groups with Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and categorical variables with the χ² test. Independent risk factors for glucolipid-metabolic abnormalities were identified by binary logistic regression. A two-tailed α of 0.05 was set; P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of baseline characteristics between patient groups

Among the 528 patients, 232 fulfilled the criteria for MS group and 296 non-MS group. The groups were comparable in age at illness onset and chlorpromazine-equivalent antipsychotic dose (both P > 0.05). As expected, the MS cohort exhibited significantly higher values for waist circumference, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), TG, FPG and BMI, together with lower age and HDL-C levels (all P < 0.05) (Table 1).

Differences in SNP genotype and allele distribution between the MS and Non-MS groups

The genotyping call rate for all 40 tag SNPs was > 98%. Furthermore, the genotype distributions of all 40 polymorphisms in the non-MS group were in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (P > 0.05). Allele-based analysis showed that the frequency of the G allele of GPR98 rs1967256 was significantly higher in the MS group than in the non-MS group (χ² = 4.049, P = 0.046), whereas the frequency of the T allele of TDRD15/LINC01822 rs1117324 was significantly lower in the MS group (χ² = 6.639, P = 0.011). Genotype analysis further revealed that, compared with the C/T and C/C genotypes of TDRD15/LINC01822 rs1117324, the T/T genotype was significantly more frequent in the MS group than in the non-MS group (χ² = 10.833, P = 0.004) (Table 2).

Independent associations of SNPs, sex, and age with hyperglycemia and low HDL-C

Given the above results suggesting that rs1967256 and rs1117324 may be related to the development of hyperglycaemia and low HDL-C, binary logistic regression analyses were further performed with these two variables as the dependent outcomes. A binary logistic regression model with hyperglycaemia as the dependent variable showed that older age at illness onset (OR = 0.975, 95% CI: 0.957–0.994) and lower BMI (OR = 1.120, 95% CI: 1.062–1.181) reduced the risk of hyperglycaemia. Discussion

In the presnt study, patients with SCZ were enrolled and stratified according to the presence or absence of MS, and we examined the associations of tag SNPs with MS and other relevant clinical indices. The results showed that the MS group had larger waist circumference, higher FPG and BMI, and lower HDL-C levels, all consistent with the clinical profile of MS [40]. In addition, carriers of the GPR98 rs1967256 G/C genotype had an earlier age at illness onset. A binary logistic regression model with hyperglycaemia as the outcome indicated that a later age at illness onset reduced the risk of elevated fasting glucose. Previous studies have confirmed that [41], studies have shown that an earlier age at onset and a longer illness duration are associated with a higher prevalence of MS; therefore, patients carrying the rs1967256 G/C genotype may be at increased risk of developing MS. A higher BMI raises the likelihood of both low HDL-C and hyperglycaemia, thereby increasing the overall risk of MS.

GPR98 encodes very-large G-protein–coupled receptor-1 (VLGR1), which is localized at synaptic junctions and cooperates with other proteins to regulate synaptic function [28, 29]. It is also known as the human adhesion G protein-coupled receptor V1 (ADGRV1) gene, the monogenic audiogenic seizure susceptibility 1 (MASS1) gene, or the VLGR1 gene. Located at chromosome 5q14.3–q21.3 and spanning 91 exons, the gene encodes a protein that contains seven trans-membrane receptor domains, couples with Ca2+, and is abundantly expressed in the central nervous system. This gene is implicated in Usher syndrome, a disorder characterized by hearing loss, progressive visual impairment, and familial febrile seizures [42, 43]. GPR98 has also been reported to be associated with cancer [44], osteoporosis [45]and tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) [30]. Studies have shown that GPR98 rs111033530 is significantly associated with DBP and is a potential candidate gene underlying the genetic risk for preeclampsia and cardiovascular disease [31].

For the GPR98 rs1967256 locus, international studies have already demonstrated that it mediates the effect of olanzapine on glycated haemoglobin [19]. Our findings indicate that male patients carrying the C allele or C/C genotype at rs1967256 have a significantly higher risk of low HDL-C. Since HDL-C is a protective component of MS, its reduction increases the overall prevalence of MS in this population. In summary, these data indicate that the rs1967256 C allele acts as a risk allele for metabolic dysregulation in SCZ. GPR98 rs1967256 is associated with fasting glucose and HDL-C levels and plays an important role in the development of MS; however, no previous study has reported a relationship between this variant and blood pressure. To date, there is no direct evidence demonstrating that GPR98 rs1967256 itself participates in lipid or glucose metabolism. Our results show that males are more prone to low HDL-C than females, thereby increasing their risk of developing MS. It has been reported that the protective effect of estrogen on lipid-metabolism disorders is an important contributing factor [46]. Other studies have also indicated that sex differences in HDL levels are attributable more to sex chromosomes than to sex hormones [47]; consequently, women, who possess an additional X chromosome, exhibit higher HDL concentrations and thus have a lower risk of developing MS than men.

Previous research on LINC01822 is limited; the variant rs4611672 has only been marginally linked to microbial composition in the oesophagus [48], and no study has yet associated this locus with glucose metabolism. TDRD15 encodes the Tudor domain-containing protein 15, studies have revealed that TDRD15 is highly expressed in brown adipose tissue and ranks among the most up-regulated proteins in subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), suggesting it may play a pivotal role in the development of obesity and its associated complications such as insulin resistance (IR) and type 2 diabetes (T2D) [32, 33]. Additional reports have shown that genetic variation in TDRD15 is significantly associated with cardiometabolic traits; the gene is located within a cardiovascular-disease (CAD)-linked locus and, together with apolipoprotein B (APOB) and others, participates in the regulation of low-density lipoprotein (LDL)-associated CpG sites, implying a role in determining LDL-cholesterol levels [34, 35]. Studies have also linked TDRD15 to spermatogenesis [49], the development of atopic dermatitis (AD) [50], and even to the promotion of breast cancer when mutated [51]. Our findings demonstrate that the TDRD15/LINC01822 rs1117324 C/C genotype is a risk factor for hyperglycaemia, whereas the C/T genotype exerts a protective effect against elevated fasting glucose. These data indicate that rs1117324 is an independent determinant of hyperglycaemia. Hyperglycaemia is a core component of MS; consequently, patients carrying the rs1117324 C/C genotype are more prone to MS, whereas those with the C/T genotype have a lower risk. This observation challenges the traditional assumption that heterozygotes must always display an intermediate phenotype and indicates that, for certain metabolism-related SNPs, the heterozygous state itself may confer protection. Previous studies have linked TDRD15 to glucose and LDL levels, but no report has specifically addressed rs1117324; its association with hyperglycaemia therefore awaits independent confirmation.

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, the sample was recruited from a specific geographical region; therefore, the findings require replication in other regions of China. Second, due to the cross-sectional study design, this investigation could only report statistical associations between GPR98 and TDRD15/LINC01822 polymorphisms and SGA-induced MS, and cannot establish a causal relationship between the identified SNPs and metabolic disturbances. Finally, comprehensive lifestyle factors (such as dietary habits, physical activity levels, smoking, and alcohol consumption history), family history of metabolic diseases, and medication adherence were not included as covariates in the analysis. These confounding factors may have cumulative effects on glucose and lipid metabolism, leading to potential residual confounding that could influence the results. Future longitudinal cohort studies must strictly control for these lifestyle and clinical variables to further validate our findings.

Conclusions

In summary, among the 40 MS susceptibility loci previously identified by European GWAS, we validated two loci—GPR98 rs1967256 and TDRD15/LINC01822 rs1117324—as being significantly associated with antipsychotic-induced MS in Chinese Han patients with SCZ. Specifically, the G allele of rs1967256 and the T/T genotype of rs1117324 were enriched in MS patients, suggesting shared genetic susceptibility across ethnic populations. Furthermore, our findings demonstrate that these genetic variants modulate distinct metabolic phenotypes: rs1117324 influences fasting glucose levels and hyperglycaemia risk in a genotype-dependent manner (C/T protective vs. C/C risk), while rs1967256 C/C genotype contributes to reduced HDL-C. These results highlight the potential of incorporating genetic profiling into personalized risk assessment and prevention strategies for metabolic complications in SCZ patients undergoing long-term SGA treatment.
