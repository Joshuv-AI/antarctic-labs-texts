Sophorolipids: multifunctional microbial glycolipids with diverse bioactivities and applications
Authors: Chaozhi Wang, Chunyang Zhou, Yazheng Cao, Mohd Sadeeq, Ke Yu, Feifei Hou, Peng Xiong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sophorolipids (SLs) are glycolipid biosurfactants produced by non-pathogenic yeasts and represent a sustainable, biocompatible alternative to synthetic surfactants. Their amphiphilic architecture—comprising a hydrophilic sophorose headgroup and a hydrophobic fatty acid tail—confers excellent surface activity, emulsifying capacity, and a broad spectrum of biological effects. This review synthesizes advances since 2020 in understanding SL bioactivities, including antibacterial, antifungal, antibiofilm, antiviral, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer effects. We examine structure–activity relationships that govern functionality and survey strategies to enhance efficacy, such as chemical modification to generate novel derivatives and incorporation into nanostructures for targeted delivery. The review also evaluates application potential across sectors—agriculture (biopesticides, soil amendments), food (emulsifiers, preservatives), healthcare (therapeutics, coatings), and environmental remediation (oil and heavy-metal removal). Despite promising developments, challenges remain in production scaling, mechanistic clarity, and comprehensive characterization of novel SL. Addressing these issues will facilitate the integration of SLs into sustainable, high-value bioproducts aligned with green chemistry principles.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Surfactants are compounds that reduce interfacial tension and provide wetting, emulsifying, dispersing, and solubilizing functions. They are important across agriculture (e.g., pesticide formulations, post-harvest treatments), food processing (emulsification, antimicrobial preservation), industry, medicine, personal care, and environmental remediation [1]. Conventional synthetic surfactants, however, often persist in the environment, show poor biodegradability, require high energy for production, and pose ecological and health risks [2]. Against this backdrop, biosurfactants have attracted interest because they align with green chemistry and sustainability principles. Biosurfactants are amphiphilic microbial molecules with hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions that partition at liquid–liquid, liquid–gas, and liquid–solid interfaces, enabling emulsion stabilization, foam formation, cleaning action, and dispersion—properties useful across many industries [3]. Furthermore, they exhibit remarkable stability under extreme environmental conditions, such as high salinity, temperature fluctuations, and varying pH levels. These unique properties position biosurfactants as a viable and sustainable solution for diverse industrial applications [4, 5].

Among biosurfactants, sophorolipids (SLs) and rhamnolipids represent two extensively studied classes, both exhibiting significant bioactivities and broad application potential [6]. However, SLs present distinct advantages in several critical areas. From a production safety perspective, SLs are synthesized by the non-pathogenic yeast Starmerella bombicola, whereas rhamnolipids are predominantly produced by potentially pathogenic Pseudomonas species. In addition, acidic SLs have been demonstrated to possess lower cytotoxicity and a wider safety margin compared to their rhamnolipid counterparts [7]. Structurally, the lactonic forms of SLs exhibit greater hydrophobicity than rhamnolipids [8], a characteristic that enhances their efficacy in specialized applications, such as cosmetics and antimicrobial formulations. These combined advantages—including a unique molecular architecture, potent surface activity, diverse biological effects, and a well-established fermentation process utilizing hydrophobic carbon sources (e.g., vegetable oils, alkanes) and sugars (e.g., glucose)—render SLs particularly promising for industrial scale-up and application [9–11]. In terms of molecular structure, an SL molecule consists of a hydrophilic sophorose disaccharide (2-O-β-d-glucopyranosyl-β-d-glucopyranose), which is β-glycosidically linked at the C1’ position to a hydrophobic hydroxylated fatty acid. This fatty acid is typically attached at the ω or ω–1 carbon, although internal linkage variants also exist (Fig. 1A1–D) [12–14]. The fermentation process yields a complex mixture of structurally related SLs, including both acidic (Fig. 1A1–2) and lactonic (Fig. 1B1–2) forms, collectively encompassing up to approximately 40 different congeners and isomers [15]. Recently identified bolaform SLs bear two sophorose units attached to both ends of a single fatty acid; the hydrophilic ends increase water solubility, while the amphiphilic architecture still permits micelle, vesicle, and lamellar assembly [12, 16, 17]. Fatty acid chains in SLs are typically C16–C18, although short-chain (C10–C14) and long-chain (C20–C22) variants have been reported [18–23]. Acidic SL (ASL) can form very-long-chain derivatives by esterification with an additional fatty acid molecule (Fig. 1E) [24]; polymeric (dimeric, trimeric) and glyceride-esterified SLs have also been described (Fig. 1F1–G4) [25, 26]. This structural diversity—variations in lactonization, chain length, unsaturation, acetylation, and oligomerization—modulates key physicochemical parameters (e.g., CMC, emulsifying capacity) and underpins the broad range of biological activities observed for SLs [10, 12, 27, 28]. Generally, lactonic SLs exhibit pronounced surface activity and biological functionality, whereas acidic SLs offer greater water solubility and superior foaming. As sophorolipids are a class of structurally diverse mixtures, their separation and purification are essential prerequisites for an in-depth study of their physicochemical properties and applications. Lactonic sophorolipids, which have relatively low water solubility, can spontaneously settle at the bottom of the fermentation broth during the fermentation process, enabling preliminary separation [30]. Water–ethyl acetate extraction is a commonly used method for sophorolipid extraction, through which the more hydrophilic bolaform sophorolipids are partitioned into the aqueous phase, while the more hydrophobic sophorolipid components remain in the ethyl acetate phase [20, 31]. Acidic sophorolipids contain carboxyl groups in their molecular structure, making their water solubility highly dependent on pH: solubility is higher in alkaline conditions above their pKa and lower in acidic conditions below their pKa. This property can be utilized for the selective purification of acidic sophorolipids [32, 33]. To obtain sophorolipids of higher purity, traditional chromatographic techniques such as column chromatography, flash chromatography, and preparative chromatography are required [20, 34–36]. In addition, for certain sophorolipid components, low-temperature crystallization is also an effective method for obtaining high-purity products [21, 37].

SL biosynthesis proceeds through a series of sequential—and sometimes overlapping—enzyme-catalyzed steps (Fig. 2). In S. bombicola, genes encoding the SL biosynthetic machinery (except the extracellular SL esterase SBLE) are co-localized in a single cluster on chromosome 2 (Fig. 2A). The pathway begins with ω- or ω-1 hydroxylation of exogenous or endogenous fatty acids by the cytochrome P450 monooxygenase CYP52M1, yielding hydroxy fatty acids [38, 39]. Two UDP-glucosyltransferases, UGTA1 and UGTB1, then sequentially transfer glucose units to the hydroxyl group to form an acyl-glycosidic glycolipid (the acidic SL) [40–42]. Further glycosylation at the carboxyl terminus produces bola-type SLs linked via ester glycosidic bonds [43, 44]. The precise order of the two glucosylation events has not been definitively established. Bola SLs may be acetylated to varying degrees (1–4 acetyl groups) by an acetyltransferase (AT) [44, 45], and are exported extracellularly via a multidrug resistance (MDR) efflux transporter [46, 47]. Recent studies indicate that SLs, particularly specific structural congeners, possess broad-spectrum biological activities—including antibacterial (against bacteria and fungi), antiviral, anti-inflammatory, antitumor, antibiofilm, and immunomodulatory effects [10]. These multifunctional bioactivities underpin diverse application prospects in agriculture, food preservation, medicine, and personal care formulations. Beyond their versatile functional properties, SLs have attracted considerable commercial interest. The global market for SLs is experiencing significant growth, driven by increasing consumer demand for sustainable and bio-based products, alongside strict environmental regulations. According to market data, the global SL market was valued at approximately USD 409.84 million in 2021 and is projected to reach USD 644.21 million by 2030, reflecting a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 5.17% during the forecast period. This growth is largely attributed to their expanding applications in industries such as personal care and cosmetics, household detergents, and food processing. Notable companies, including BASF SE, Evonik Operations GmbH and Actera Ingredients, are actively involved in the commercial production and development of innovative SL-based formulations, further advancing market penetration [48]. This review systematically summarizes recent advances in SL research, with particular emphasis on their diverse biological activities—antibacterial, antiviral, anti-inflammatory, and antitumor—and the molecular mechanisms that underlie these effects. We examine SL synergisms, chemical modifications, and nanostructured formulations, and evaluate current and prospective applications in food, agriculture, medicine, cosmetics, environmental remediation, and industrial cleaning. The review concludes by identifying key challenges and outlining future research and development directions.

Bioactive properties and applications of native sophorolipids

This section reviews the bioactivities of native SLs, referring to the core SL structures—whether present in fermentation-derived mixtures or as isolated congeners—prior to any discussion of their synergistic combinations, deliberate chemical modifications, or integration into complex nanostructures, which are covered in subsequent sections.

Antimicrobial activity

The antimicrobial activity of SLs represents one of their most significant functional properties and has become a major research focus in recent years. Extensive studies have demonstrated that SLs exhibit potent inhibitory effects against the growth of various pathogenic microorganisms, including both bacteria and fungi, while also effectively disrupting biofilm formation. SLs also potentiate conventional antimicrobials and emerging antibacterial materials, producing reproducible synergistic effects that support new combination strategies for microbial control. Consequently, elucidating SLs’ bacteriostatic/bactericidal and antibiofilm mechanisms, and characterizing their synergistic interactions with other agents, are active and high-priority research areas [28].

Recent fermentation-based studies show that feedstock and yeast strain determine SL composition and antimicrobial spectrum, with crude extracts exhibiting activity against a range of bacterial and fungal pathogens. Suparna Sen et al. used distillers dried grains with solubles, a byproduct of the ethanol industry, as the sole carbon source for SL production. The resulting SLs inhibited 10 of 12 tested pathogens, including Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria and fungi [51]. Kumari et al. reported SL mixtures produced by three novel Metschnikowia strains. All contained SL components with C10–C18 chain lengths and varying degrees of saturation, demonstrating favorable inhibitory activity against Gram-positive bacteria. Two of these SLs also demonstrated comparable antifungal activity, although efficacy against phytopathogenic fungi was generally lower than against human pathogenic bacteria [19, 20]. Pal et al. utilized refinery byproducts (palm fatty acid distillate, soybean fatty acid distillate) and sunflower oil as hydrophobic substrates for SL production through fermentation; the crude extracts inhibited Salmonella Typhimurium, Bacillus cereus, and Staphylococcus epidermidis, with minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values ranging from 50 to 200 μg/mL [52].

Studies on purified SL congeners reveal species-dependent antibacterial potency, with certain lactonic and acidic C18 SLs demonstrating high efficacy against pathogens. Park et al. focused on the activity of purified SLs against Clostridium perfringens. In vitro experiments showed that all tested SLs reduced C. perfringens viability in a dose-dependent manner. Notably, C18:1 lactonic SL and C18:1 acidic SL both achieved over 95% inhibition of C. perfringens at 125 μg/mL concentration, indicating the potential application value of SLs in animal health management [49]. Further research by Ma et al. systematically compared the antimicrobial efficacy and mechanisms of purified lactonic SLs against several pathogenic bacteria. They reported species-dependent activity: the MIC for Staphylococcus aureus was 0.05 mg/mL, whereas that for Pseudomonas aeruginosa was 0.20 mg/mL. No significant inhibition was observed against Escherichia coli or Lactobacillus sp., suggesting that LSL antibacterial activity is species-specific and may relate to differences in bacterial cell-wall structure [50].

Long-chain SLs also show distinctive profiles. Solaiman et al. isolated a long-chain (C22) SL (22:0-SL) from Pseudohyphozyma bogoriensis and systematically evaluated its antimicrobial activity through a multidimensional testing system. Results demonstrated that its 6’-monoacetylated form showed significantly superior inhibitory activity against Cutibacterium acnes (formerly Propionibacterium acnes) compared to the diacetylated form, even exceeding that of the lactonic 6',6"-Ac2-18:1-SL, contrary to conventional expectations. The study also highlighted the distinct species- and strain-specific antimicrobial characteristics of SLs [21]. Leyton et al. also investigated 22-carbon SLs, including methyl ester acidic SL (AS C22:1) and monoacetylated acidic SL (AS C22:0-1Ac); at 0.20 g/L concentration, these compounds exhibited inhibition rates of 21.1% against S. aureus and 24.4% against E. coli. Notably, contrary to the typical pattern where SLs show stronger activity against Gram-positive bacteria, these compounds demonstrated slightly higher inhibition rates against the Gram-negative E. coli than the Gram-positive S. aureus [22].

Hirlekar et al. investigated the relationship between SL acid chain length and antimicrobial activity. Results indicated that shorter fatty acid chains correlated with stronger antimicrobial activity (manifested as lower MIC values) and reduced cytotoxicity. For instance, C12-LASL had an MIC of 0.05 mg/mL against S. aureus, while C18-SASL exhibited significantly higher MIC values. This discrepancy may be attributed to how fatty acid chain length influences the hydrophobicity of SL molecules and their ability to penetrate bacterial cell membranes [18].

Studies indicate that SL mixtures produced by diverse microbial sources (such as novel Metschnikowia yeast strains, Starmerella riodocensis GT-SL1R, and Starmerella bombicola) or using different hydrophobic substrates (e.g., palm and soybean fatty acid distillates, sunflower oil (SO), or waste oils) typically contain molecules with C10–C18 chain lengths and varying degrees of saturation, which effectively inhibit multiple fungal species [19, 31, 52, 55, 57]. Data suggest that specific structural types often exhibit superior activity. For example, SLs dominated by diacetylated lactonic forms show marked inhibition of phytopathogenic fungi (e.g., Botrytis cinerea, Sclerotium rolfsii, Rhizoctonia solani, and Pythium ultimum), with minimum effective concentrations as low as 2 mg/mL, showing enhanced efficacy in preventive applications [31]. Similarly, single-component C18:1 lactonic SLs exhibit potent activity against multiple food spoilage fungi, with MIC and minimum fungicidal concentrations (MFC) generally below 1 mg/mL [53].

The inhibitory effects of SLs vary between yeasts and filamentous fungi, with Candida species generally more susceptible. For example, specific SL mixtures can nearly completely inhibit hyphal formation in Candida albicans at 500–1000 μg/mL [55], whereas crude SL extracts produced from industrial byproducts inhibited mycelial growth by 56% at 1 mg/mL [52]. Candida tropicalis is even more sensitive to certain SL components, with MICs as low as 0.125 mg/mL [51, 56]. In contrast, inhibition against Aspergillus species is comparatively weaker, with significantly lower inhibition rates at equivalent concentrations (1 mg/mL) than those observed for Candida [52]. Acid-type and lactonic C18:1 SL mixtures produced from waste oils demonstrate promising inhibitory effects against Mucor species, suggesting potential utility against Mucorales infections such as mucormycosis [57]. Regarding dermatophytes, specific SL mixtures strongly inhibit Trichophyton mentagrophytes in vitro (85% inhibition at 2 mg/mL; MIC = 1 mg/mL) and have shown therapeutic efficacy when applied topically (1 mg/mL) in a mouse model of cutaneous dermatophytosis [51, 56].

Antibiofilm activity

Biofilms are complex microbial communities in which cells are embedded in a self-secreted extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix and adhere to one another and to surfaces. These structures are heterogeneous and dynamic, enabling adaptive responses to environmental changes. Biofilms play crucial roles in natural ecosystems, industrial processes, and chronic infections; their intrinsic properties present substantial challenges for both scientific study and control [58].

SLs markedly inhibit fungal biofilm formation. Treatment of Trichophyton mentagrophytes biofilms with a SL mixture (containing both acidic and lactonic forms, C11–C18) at 2 × MIC (2 mg/mL) concentration resulted in a dose-dependent reduction of biofilm biomass as quantified by crystal violet staining. Microscopic analyses further elucidated the underlying mechanisms: scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed disrupted biofilm architecture with disorganized hyphal networks and diminished extracellular matrix, while confocal laser scanning microscopy detected enhanced propidium iodide fluorescence, indicating SL-induced cell death within the biofilms [56].

For bacterial biofilms, particularly Staphylococcus species, SLs demonstrate superior inhibitory activity. Comparative studies of SLs produced from different oil byproducts—sunflower oil (SO), soybean fatty acid distillate (SFAD), and palm fatty acid distillate (PFAD), yielding SOSL, SFADSL, and PFADSL—found SFADSL to be the most potent against Staphylococcus epidermidis biofilms, with a minimum biofilm inhibitory concentration (MBIC) of 12.5 μg/mL and minimum biofilm eradication concentration (MBEC) of 50 μg/mL, significantly outperforming SOSL [52]. These findings suggest that fatty acid composition critically influences SL antimicrobial efficacy.

Different SL configurations display distinct characteristics and potency against Staphylococcus aureus biofilms. Evaluation of acidic (SLA), lactonic (SL18), and mixed (SLV) SLs demonstrated that all forms could effectively disrupt mature biofilms on medical-grade silicone surfaces at concentrations > 0.1% w/v, (~ 1000 μg/mL). Notably, acidic SL (SLA) exhibited superior antimicrobial activity at lower concentrations (0.05% w/v, ~ 500 μg/mL), potentially mediated through surface tension reduction and enhanced microbial cell interaction [59].

Innovative application strategies have been explored for catheter-associated biofilm prevention. A covalent immobilization approach (“antiadhesive strategy”) employing acidic SL mixtures achieved approximately 90% biofilm reduction. Alternatively, a sustained-release system (“release strategy”) using purified lactonic SL not only significantly inhibited biofilm formation without host cell toxicity but also demonstrated more durable efficacy compared to the anti-adhesion approach [35]. These results highlight the promising potential of lactonic SLs for long-term antibiofilm applications.

The aforementioned studies demonstrate that SLs are a promising class of antibiofilm agents. Their antimicrobial activity is concentration-dependent and species-specific (e.g., SFADSL shows exceptional efficacy against S. epidermidis) [52]. Biofilm maturity significantly influences SL performance, with younger biofilms generally being more susceptible to inhibition or eradication than mature biofilms [60]. The molecular configuration of SLs (acidic vs. lactonic vs. mixed forms) critically determines potency, effective concentration ranges, and likely mechanisms of action. For instance, acidic forms may act via surfactant activity at lower concentrations, while lactonic configurations demonstrate prolonged efficacy in sustained-release systems [35]. The primary antibiofilm mechanisms include: (1) extracellular matrix reduction, (2) disruption of biofilm architecture through mycelial structure loosening, (3) induction of cellular death, and (4) inhibition of initial bacterial adhesion [56].

Antialgal activity

SLs offer a promising solution for degrading harmful algal blooms (HABs). Baek et al. were the first to examine the effects of SL treatment on the growth, motility, sedimentation, and recovery of HAB organisms in controlled lab experiments [61]. Their findings highlighted the significant algicidal properties and superior biodegradability of SLs compared to conventional surfactants [62]. In addition, a novel strategy has been proposed that combines SLs with yellow clay to mitigate HABs [63, 64]. In recent studies, Xia et al. systematically compared the degradation effects of acidic and lactonic SLs specifically on cyanobacterial harmful algal blooms (CyanoHABs). Their results demonstrated that lactonic SLs exhibited superior degradation capabilities, with a concentration of 5 mg/L resulting in cyanobacterial cell rupture within just 30 min. Moreover, through experiments aimed at optimizing the ratio of the two types of SLs, they identified the optimal degradation effect at a ratio of 8.25:1.75 for lactonic to acidic SL [65].

Mechanisms of antimicrobial action​​

The primary antimicrobial mechanism of SLs is interaction with microbial cell membranes, resulting in increased membrane permeability, cytoplasmic leakage, and cell death. Multiple studies support this mechanism, reporting that SLs induce structural alterations or rupture of target membranes, resulting in efflux of intracellular components and enzymes [18, 49, 66–68].

The membrane-disruptive effects of SLs vary markedly among bacterial species, largely because of differences in cell wall and membrane architecture. SEM shows that SL-treated Staphylococcus aureus (characterized by a thick peptidoglycan and teichoic acid-containing cell wall) predominantly displays surface “leakage” phenomena, whereas Pseudomonas aeruginosa (with a thinner, lipid-rich cell wall/membrane) demonstrates more pronounced “bursting” effects [50]. These findings suggest differential penetration capacity and disruptive intensity of SLs against structurally distinct bacterial membranes, with thinner, lipid-rich structures (e.g., P. aeruginosa) potentially being more susceptible to severe damage.

The molecular architecture of SLs, particularly their fatty acid chain length, significantly influences membrane-disruptive capability and mode of action. Research indicates that short-chain fatty acid SLs (e.g., C12-SL) generally exhibit stronger membrane disruption activity compared to their long-chain counterparts (e.g., C18-SL) [18]. This effect may reflect an optimal balance of hydrophobicity and amphiphilicity in shorter chains that facilitates insertion into and disruption of lipid bilayers. However, Solaiman et al. reported superior antimicrobial activity for C22 long‑chain SLs relative to C18 variants, indicating that, beyond chain length, other structural features—such as glycosylation pattern, degree of acetylation, and additional functional groups—modulate efficacy by affecting hydrophobicity, charge distribution, and specific interactions with membrane components [21].

Although membrane disruption remains the principal antimicrobial mechanism, emerging evidence points to additional, possibly strain‑specific pathways. Solaiman et al. observed marked, strain‑dependent differences in Streptococcus mutans susceptibility to identical SL treatments, implying potential molecular‑target interactions beyond nonspecific physical membrane damage [21]. Moreover, some studies propose secondary mechanisms, including modulation of enzymatic activity or inhibition of extracellular polymer synthesis [31], but the prevalence and significance of these pathways require further validation.

Molecular-level investigations using model phospholipid membranes have provided further mechanistic insights. Acidic SLs at sub-critical micelle concentrations (2.5 μM) can gradually but completely induce fluorescent probe leakage in liposome models [69]. This phenomenon, interpreted as membrane permeabilization rather than high-concentration dissolution effects, supports the permeability-enhancing mechanism hypothesis.

The antifungal action of SLs parallels their antibacterial effects. SEM shows untreated fungal hyphae with intact morphology, whereas SL-treated samples (1800 μg/mL) display marked deformation and surface damage, consistent with membrane disruption [53]. Confocal laser scanning microscopy further confirms that SLs compromise fungal membrane integrity and lead to cell death [20]. In addition, acidic SLs may promote cellular interactions via micelle formation [59].

In summary, SLs principally exert antimicrobial activity by compromising membrane integrity, increasing permeability, and causing cytoplasmic leakage. The extent and pattern of membrane disruption depend on (1) structural features of the target cell envelope (e.g., thickness, lipid content) and (2) molecular features of the SL (notably fatty‑acid chain length, glycosylation, and acetylation). Observed strain-specific differences and reports of secondary effects (e.g., impacts on extracellular polymer synthesis or enzymatic function) indicate that antimicrobial action can involve multiple targets. Clarifying these structure–activity relationships and the specific interactions between defined SL structures and microbial membrane components will facilitate the rational design of more selective and effective SL‑based antimicrobials.

Anticancer activity

SLs have demonstrated notable antitumor activity in preclinical studies. Their reported mechanisms include induction of tumor cell death, inhibition of migration and clonogenicity, and modulation of key signaling pathways.

Regarding cell‑death induction, LSLs display selective cytotoxicity toward certain tumor cell types. For example, in melanoma SK‑MEL‑28 cells, LSL at 100 µg/mL induced ~ 42 ± 5% necrosis, accompanied by morphological changes such as rounding and detachment, whereas the same concentration produced minimal toxicity in normal HaCaT keratinocytes (viability > 85%) [70]. ASL significantly increases the proportion of early apoptotic cells (≈3.2-fold) in colorectal carcinoma cells [19].

Inhibition of tumor cell migration and colony formation is also reported. ASLs reduced the migratory capacity of A549 lung cancer cells by ~ 78%, concomitant with decreased secretion of matrix metalloproteinase‑9 (MMP‑9) [71]. In colon cancer models, ASL treatment suppressed colony formation to ≈30% of control, suggesting effects on tumor subpopulations with stem‑like properties [19].

At the molecular and model‑system level, Roberge et al. evaluated two SL derivatives (diacetylated LSL and diacetylated DSL) in breast cancer models. Both compounds showed efficacy comparable to doxorubicin in 2D cultures but reduced potency in 3D spheroids. Although toxicity to normal cells was low, the compounds did not demonstrate cancer‑selective cytotoxicity. Notably, diacetylated LSL retained activity in 3D models (IC50 = 33 µg/mL) despite the increased drug resistance of spheroids [72].

Overall, current evidence indicates that LSLs and ASLs can induce necrosis or apoptosis, suppress migration and clonogenicity, and interfere with metabolic or signaling pathways (e.g., STAT3/HIF‑1α). Their variable activity across tumor types and model systems, and instances of selective cytotoxicity toward malignant versus normal cells, warrant further investigation.

Anti-inflammatory activity

SLs exhibit anti‑inflammatory activity in diverse experimental models, primarily by modulating key inflammatory signaling pathways, notably the nuclear factor kappa B (NF‑κB) cascade. Current research delineates several multifaceted anti‑inflammatory mechanisms.

In intestinal models, SLs modulate inflammatory cytokine balance and improve barrier function. Kwak et al. reported in broiler chickens that SLs reduced pro‑inflammatory interleukin‑6 (IL‑6), increased anti‑inflammatory interleukin‑10 (IL‑10), and elevated short‑chain fatty acid concentrations, thereby indirectly strengthening intestinal barrier integrity [73]. This protective effect was corroborated in dextran sulfate sodium-induced murine colitis, where SL treatment upregulated mucin‑2 (MUC2) and anti‑inflammatory cytokines (IL‑10, transforming growth factor‑β [TGF‑β]) and markedly ameliorated colonic histopathology, collectively restoring gut homeostasis [74].

At the cellular level, using lipopolysaccharide‑stimulated murine RAW264.7 macrophages, Xu et al. showed that both acidic and lactonic SLs suppress activation of the canonical NF‑κB pathway, evidenced by inhibition of p65 nuclear translocation and reduced phosphorylation of IκBα. Consequently, the release of pro‑inflammatory mediators—including tumor necrosis factor‑α (TNF‑α), IL‑6, and nitric oxide—was significantly decreased, indicating direct action on immune cells to curb excessive inflammatory responses [75].

Protective effects have also been reported in pulmonary fibrosis and hepatotoxicity models. Mousa et al. and Abdel‑Latif et al. demonstrated that SLs inhibit the pro‑fibrotic TGF‑β1/Smad‑3 signaling pathway and downregulate NF‑κB and its regulated cytokines (e.g., interleukin‑1β [IL‑1β] and IL‑6), while concurrently activating endogenous antioxidant defenses, including the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway [57, 76]. This dual capacity to suppress inflammation while enhancing antioxidant defense contributes to synergistic protection against inflammatory tissue damage and oxidative stress.

Antioxidant activity

SLs exhibit antioxidant activity through direct free‑radical scavenging and by activating endogenous antioxidant defenses. Studies show that SLs reduce oxidative‑stress markers such as malondialdehyde [57, 77], while enhancing the levels of key antioxidant substances including glutathione and antioxidant enzymes (superoxide dismutase, catalase, and glutathione S‑transferase) [57, 76].

In vitro DPPH assays of SLs from various sources reported dose‑dependent radical‑scavenging rates of ~ 52.4–63.0% [20, 78]. The antioxidant effects of SLs often act synergistically with their anti‑inflammatory properties; for example, SLs can mitigate ROS‑mediated inflammatory damage by activating the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway while concurrently suppressing NF‑κB signaling [54].

Applications of sophorolipids

As natural biosurfactants, SLs have potential applications across agriculture, the food industry, healthcare, and environmental remediation owing to their physicochemical properties and bioactivities. The following sections summarize recent advances.

Agricultural and livestock applications

In agriculture, SLs show in vitro antifungal activity against phytopathogens (e.g., Botrytis cinerea, Rhizoctonia solani), with efficacy influenced by environmental pH [31, 79]. Beyond direct antimicrobial effects, SLs act synergistically with Trichoderma harzianum to enhance rice seed germination and seedling growth [80]. In livestock, dietary SLs improve growth performance in broilers and piglets by modulating gut microbiota composition (e.g., increasing Lactobacillus and Akkermansia muciniphila), elevating short‑chain fatty acid levels, and strengthening intestinal barrier function [73, 74]. Safety assessments indicate minimal sub-lethal effects on fish at low concentrations, suggesting favorable environmental compatibility [81].

Food industry applications

SLs are promising natural bioemulsifiers and antimicrobial agents for food applications. They inhibit food-spoilage fungi (e.g., Aspergillus, Fusarium) with MIC as low as 225 μg/mL, suggesting potential as natural preservatives [53]. In food processing, SLs can replace conventional vegetable fats as emulsifiers in baked goods (e.g., cakes), maintaining product quality while exhibiting high thermal stability (tolerance up to 180 °C) and antioxidant activity (DPPH radical scavenging: 52.42%) [78]. Regarding safety as food additives, SL intake alters gut microbiota composition (e.g., reducing butyrate‑producing bacteria) but exhibits lower cytotoxicity than synthetic emulsifiers; therefore, optimal dosing should be established [82].

Healthcare applications

SLs exhibit multiple bioactivities relevant to healthcare. They effectively disrupt biofilms formed by Staphylococcus aureus and Candida albicans, inhibiting colonization on medical-grade silicone surfaces [59, 83]. Among these, Diacetylated L‑C18:1 lactonic SLs show promise as bioactive coatings to prevent catheter‑related infections because of potent antimicrobial activity and favorable cytocompatibility [35]. In anticancer studies, lactonic SLs selectively inhibit proliferation of malignant melanoma (SK‑MEL‑28) and hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG2) cells, inducing apoptosis via caspase‑3‑dependent pathways while exhibiting low toxicity toward normal cells [70, 84]. Mechanistic work also indicates that SLs alleviate histamine‑dependent pruritus by modulating the phospholipase/inositol 1,4,5‑trisphosphate receptor signaling axis and transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 activity [85]. In addition, acidic SLs act as low‑toxicity cryoprotectants by inhibiting ice‑crystal formation and mitigating osmotic damage during freezing [86].

Environmental and industrial applications

SLs enhance solubilization of petroleum hydrocarbons and improve microbial biodegradation of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons; for example, phenanthrene degradation by Pseudomonas stutzeri LSH‑PAH1 increased from 21.4 to 91.7%, and SLs can mitigate toxicity of metabolic intermediates (e.g., 1‑naphthol) by modulating gene expression [87]. SLs also facilitate metal leaching from mineral slags (e.g., Cu and Zn), with reports of 53% copper extraction from sulfide slag, offering greener alternatives to conventional acidic‑oxidative chemical leaching [88]. In industrial applications, SLs achieved a 19.2% heavy-oil recovery rate in microbial-enhanced oil recovery tests [89]. Encapsulation of SLs within γ-cyclodextrin metal–organic frameworks increases water solubility by 10.9-fold, enabling 85.82% oil-removal efficiency [90]. In mineral flotation, SLs exhibit pH‑responsive behavior: under acidic conditions, they adsorb to copper sulfide and increase particle hydrophilicity, whereas at neutral-alkaline pH, Cu(II)-SL complexes precipitate via dissolution–precipitation, enabling efficient metal separation at low SL concentrations (~ 1 mM) [91].

Sophorolipid derivatives and their properties

The inherent bioactivity of SLs, particularly their antibacterial properties, can be significantly enhanced and functionally expanded through diverse chemical modification strategies, which represent a critical research direction. Such modifications aim to optimize physicochemical properties, target specificity, and mechanisms of action to meet application‑specific requirements.

Acid-catalyzed modification

Acid-catalyzed modification is a fundamental and effective approach (Fig. 3A). Crude SLs can be converted into glucolipids (GLs) by deglycosylation and de-esterification reactions (1–4). Amination modification

Nitrogen functionalization of SLs—principally by amination, amidation, or introduction of quaternary ammonium groups—is a key strategy to enhance bioactivity, especially antimicrobial properties. These modifications introduce nitrogen‑containing moieties that alter molecular charge, amphiphilicity, and interactions with biological targets, thereby optimizing performance. Amination effectively incorporates amino groups into the SL scaffold (Fig. 3B), producing structurally diverse SL amines (5–16). Compared with unmodified SLs, many aminated derivatives display broader spectrum antibacterial activity, including efficacy against Gram‑negative strains [93].

Amidation modification

Amidation enables the introduction of functional side chains at specific sites (e.g., carboxyl groups) of SLs (Fig. 4). For example, amidation of GLs with arginine methyl ester (D‑Arg‑OMe) yields GLs‑D‑Arg‑OMe derivatives (20a–20d) that show substantially enhanced antibacterial activity against Escherichia coli, with bactericidal concentrations as low as 200 mg/L and a favorable therapeutic index [94]. Sophorolipid-D‑arginine (SL‑D‑Arg) derivatives (21–24), synthesized by amidation with D‑arginine, improved inhibition of Staphylococcus aureus, achieving MIC of ~ 63 mg/L. The guanidinium group in SL‑D‑Arg can form multiple hydrogen bonds with membrane phospholipids, enhancing antimicrobial potency; notably, derivative 22 caused < 10% hemolysis even at concentrations up to 500 mg/L [33]. Amide conjugation of SLs with toluidine blue (SL‑TB, 25) generated a photosensitizer for antimicrobial photodynamic therapy; under irradiation, SL‑TB showed greater bactericidal activity against Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus than free toluidine blue [95]. Quaternization

Quaternization—introduction of quaternary ammonium groups—imparts a permanent positive charge to SLs (Fig. 5). Combined with hydrophobic alkyl chains, quaternized derivatives (25–36) exhibit potent antimicrobial activity; some (35, 36) have MICs as low as 0.014 mg/mL against selected pathogens. Hydrogenation of quaternized derivatives to increase alkyl‑chain saturation can further enhance activity (e.g., ~ threefold improved activity against Listeria monocytogenes), indicating that hydrophobic‑chain saturation modulates efficacy [93]. Synergistic effects and applications of sophorolipid combinations

SLs possess valuable individual properties, but their combinations and composites often produce synergistic effects that broaden application potential and enhance performance. This synergy is evident across antimicrobial applications, material modification, and environmental remediation.

Enhancing antibacterial activity

Combining SLs with other antimicrobials markedly enhances antibacterial efficacy. Formulations that include lactic acid, citronella essential oil, or cinnamaldehyde reduce MICs against pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus and Enterococcus faecalis and often lower viable counts by > 50% [101, 102]. This synergy principally reflects SL‑mediated disruption of biofilm structure and increased membrane permeability, which facilitate entry of co‑administered agents. SLs can also potentiate conventional antibiotics: combinations with tetracycline altered the cellular morphology and mechanical properties of methicillin‑resistant S. aureus, increasing bacterial susceptibility [103]. In medical‑device applications, 3D‑printed coatings of SLs and chitosan increased the surface contact angle to 105° (reduced wettability) and decreased bacterial attachment by > 90%, demonstrating an effective surface‑modification strategy [104].

Optimizing material properties

Incorporation of SLs improves physicochemical and functional material properties. For example, combining SLs with soy protein isolate at a 1:50 mass ratio lowered the surface tension to 32 mN/m and approximately doubled foam half‑life [105]. Cotton fabric modified with SLs and the crosslinker 1,2,3,4‑butanetetracarboxylic acid acquired durable antibacterial activity, retaining > 85% efficacy after 50 standard washing cycles [106]. Low‑concentration mixtures of SLs and sodium dodecyl sulfate (0.1 × their respective CMCs) effectively disrupted Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilms, suggesting routes to efficient, low‑consumption antibacterial materials and cleaning agents [107].

​​Improving environmental remediation efficiency

SL combinations also enhance environmental remediation performance. SLs mixed with rhamnolipids at optimized ratios (e.g., 4:6) achieved oil‑removal efficiencies up to ~ 90% for oil‑contaminated sludge [108, 109]. In enhanced oil recovery, SLs combined with selected nanofluids modified the wettability of oil‑wet carbonate rocks and increased heavy‑oil recovery to 29.3% [89]. SL‑modified fly ash materials simultaneously adsorbed heavy metals and antibiotic pollutants, achieving maximum adsorption capacities of ~ 138 mg/g [110]. These results indicate promising strategies for remediation at co‑contaminated sites.

​​Synergistic mechanisms and application outlook​​

Synergy likely arises from complementary mechanisms, including intermolecular hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, interfacial‑property modification, and biofilm disruption. Understanding these interactions provides a theoretical basis for designing multifunctional, environmentally benign SL‑based composites and processes. Future research should explore combination patterns with novel nanomaterials, biodegradable biopolymers, and other components and should evaluate scalability and long‑term safety to facilitate large‑scale implementation in biomedicine (e.g., antibacterial dressings, drug‑delivery systems), green chemical engineering, and environmental remediation. Current evidence supports SL combination technology as a promising pathway toward green‑chemistry and sustainability goals.

Sophorolipid-involved nanostructures

Nano-construction strategies​​

The construction of SL nanostructures exploits their amphiphilicity and functional groups and leverages synergistic interactions with other materials. This approach yields several core design strategies.

Self-assembled nanocarriers

Self‑assembly is the principal route to SL nanostructures. The amphiphilic SL scaffold spontaneously forms ordered nanoassemblies in aqueous or oil phases via hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and related intermolecular forces, facilitating the preparation of pure SL micelles and emulsions. Examples include drug-loaded nanomicelles formed by the self-assembly of sophorolipid/egg yolk lecithin [111], honokiol-loaded SL nanomicelles (HK-SL Ms) [112], and stable water-in-oil nanoemulsions assembled from SL and eugenol [113]. SLs also synergistically assemble with biomacromolecules: composite nanoparticles formed by SL-zein self‑assembly substantially increase loading capacity for hydrophobic drugs such as curcumin and 7,8‑dihydroxyflavone [114, 115]. Addition of polysaccharides (e.g., chondroitin sulfate) to form ternary zein/CS/SL systems further enhances particle stability and functionality [116].

Eco-friendly nanoparticle synthesis

SLs can enable green synthesis of metal nanoparticles because they combine reducing functionalities (e.g., hydroxyl groups) with surface‑active moieties (e.g., carboxylates). In a single process, SLs can act as reductants for metal precursors (Au3+, Ag+, Zn2+) and simultaneously stabilize nascent nanoparticles to prevent aggregation. Successful examples include SL-modified gold nanoparticles synthesized using SL as both reductant and stabilizer [117, 118], SL-coated silver nanoparticles [119], and SL-modified zinc oxide nanoparticles [120].

Hybrid nanodelivery systems

Hybrid systems integrate SLs with other functional materials (synthetic polymers, natural lipids, or polysaccharides) to create composite nanocarriers that overcome physiological barriers such as poor solubility and limited targeting. Representative composite systems include: Nanocapsule systems formed by PEGylated poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) encapsulating SLs [121]; lipid-polymer hybrid nanoparticles constructed using SL modification (SL‑stabilized phospholipid nanoparticles) [122]; SL‑based membrane‑biomimetic nanoparticles (e.g., phosphatidylcholine–PLGA hybrids) [71]; and ternary complexes integrating various biomaterials, for example, drug-loaded nanoparticles composed of gliadin, hyaluronic acid, and SL [123]. These hybrid designs exploit synergistic material properties to improve stability, loading, and delivery performance.

Functional enhancement mechanisms of sophorolipid nanostructures

The amphiphilic architecture of SLs enables self‑assembly into diverse nanostructures (e.g., micelles, vesicles, and nanoparticles) [12, 124, 125]. Serving as drug carriers or active agents, these nanostructures enhance therapeutic efficacy and bioactivity via multiple mechanisms.

Enhanced solubility and stability of insoluble drugs

SL‑based nanocarriers markedly improve solubility and stability of hydrophobic drugs by forming hydrophobic cores or drug-carrier complexes. For example, zein/chondroitin sulfate/SL increased curcumin aqueous solubility ~ 246‑fold and protected its stability in simulated gastrointestinal fluids [116]. 7,8-DHF loaded zein-sophorolipid nanoparticles achieved encapsulation efficiencies up to 98.16% for 7,8‑dihydroxyflavone [114]. Honokiol‑loaded SL micelles (HK‑SL Ms) effectively improved honokiol water dispersibility and stability [112].

Smart drug delivery capabilities

SLs confer stimuli‑responsive and targeting capabilities via ionizable or reactive functional groups and composite architectures. Carboxylate moieties impart pH‑responsive behavior (e.g., controlled release from curcumin-loaded zein/SL nanoparticles at intestinal pH) [115]. Biomimetic membrane designs, such as phosphatidylcholine‑containing sophorolipid-associated hybrid nanoparticles, enhance mucus penetration and tumor accumulation [71]. Integration with manufacturing technologies (e.g., 3D printing and hot‑melt extrusion) permits tailoring of release profiles for agents such as celecoxib [126].

Synergistic bioactivity enhancement

SL nanostructures amplify biological effects through complementary mechanisms: (1) membrane disruption—SL/eugenol nanoemulsions increase bacterial membrane permeability and exert antibacterial activity [113]; (2) ROS induction—SL‑stabilized gold nanoparticles (AuNPs‑SL) trigger reactive‑oxygen‑species bursts that kill Vibrio cholerae [117]; (3) virulence‑gene modulation—SL-copper complexes downregulate enhanced filamentous growth protein 1 and secreted aspartic protease 8, suppressing Candida albicans’ virulence [127]; and (4) drug potentiation—SL carriers can enhance apoptotic signaling in tumor cells (e.g., caspase‑3 activation) [70, 84].

Application-specific nanostructures

Capitalizing on amphiphilicity, biocompatibility, and biodegradability, SLs are attractive biomaterials for engineering functional nanostructures. These nanomaterials exhibit distinctive properties and versatile application prospects across three principal domains: antimicrobial/antibiofilm platforms, drug‑delivery vehicles, and environmental/industrial applications.

Advanced antimicrobial and antibiofilm platforms

AuNPs‑SL effectively eradicated Vibrio cholerae biofilms via ROS‑induced DNA damage [117, 118]. SL-silver nanoparticle composites incorporated into packaging films substantially inhibited foodborne pathogens such as Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus [119], while ZnO‑SL hybrids displayed concentration‑dependent broad‑spectrum activity against S. aureus, E. coli, and Candida albicans [120]. Self‑assembled SL-eugenol water‑in‑oil nanoemulsions maintained stability across ionic‑strength and pH variations and showed synergistic bactericidal effects against both Gram‑positive and Gram‑negative bacteria [113]. Acidic SL complexes with the antimicrobial peptide LL‑37 eliminated > 70% of bacteria within 1 h and suppressed S. aureus biofilms [128]. For resilient biofilms, glycyrrhizin-SL-egg yolk lecithin micelles penetrated exopolysaccharide barriers and inhibited srtA expression [111], whereas HK‑SL Ms effectively disrupted mature biofilms [112].

Efficient drug delivery systems​

SL‑based nanoparticles enhance oral bioavailability, stability, and controlled release of poorly soluble agents. Zein/SL nanoparticles increased curcumin bioaccessibility ~ sixfold [115] and enabled amorphous, highly bioavailable encapsulation of 7,8‑dihydroxyflavone, with sequential encapsulation improving storage stability and release kinetics [114]. SL‑based membrane‑mimetic nanoparticles modeled on intestinal membranes enhanced luteolin oral absorption ~ 3.2‑fold and reduced breast‑cancer metastasis by ~ 65.2% [71]. SL‑stabilized phospholipid nanoparticles significantly improved oral bioavailability of model drugs [122], and PEGylated PLGA nanocapsules incorporating SLs enabled targeted colon‑cancer delivery [121]. Formulation advances—such as SL‑stabilized niclosamide nanocrystals that match synthetic surfactant performance [129], and hot‑melt‑extrusion/3D‑printing strategies to tailor celecoxib release—illustrate translational potential [126].

Environmental remediation and industrial applications​

For environmental remediation, SLs achieved 95.66% crude oil removal from contaminated sludge [108]. Enhanced oil recovery systems employing SL/polyetheramine/wellan gum ternary formulations increased yield by 22.66% [130], and ZnO NPs/SL-rhamnolipid nanofluids exhibited synergistic reduction of oil–water interfacial tension [131]. γ-Cyclodextrin metal–organic frameworks incorporating SLs enhanced water solubility 10.9-fold and attained 85.82% oil-washing efficacy [90]. Agricultural applications included flash nanoprecipitation-optimized SL-based nanopesticides for superior foliar adsorption [132]. Water treatment utilizing SL-nano zero-valent iron activated persulfate to degrade 93.5% of norfloxacin [133], while SL/polylactic acid films in food packaging inhibited Listeria monocytogenes and S. aureus proliferation [134].

Conclusion and perspectives

Multifaceted bioactivities and application prospects​

SLs possess diverse bioactivities and tunable properties that support applications in pharmaceuticals, agriculture, the food industry, and environmental remediation. Their most intensively studied function is antimicrobial activity, with efficacy against numerous Gram‑positive bacteria, selected Gram‑negative bacteria, and various fungi. SLs act against biofilm‑associated infections by disrupting biofilm architecture, increasing membrane permeability, and inducing cell death; they also potentiate conventional antibiotics and natural antimicrobials, offering strategies to mitigate drug resistance. Beyond antimicrobial effects, SLs display antitumor activity (induction of apoptosis or necrosis, suppression of migration and clonogenicity, interference with signaling pathways) and exert anti‑inflammatory and antioxidant effects via modulation of inflammatory cascades and activation of endogenous defense systems (Fig. 7).Fig. 7Applications of sophorolipids

Applications of sophorolipids

Structure–activity relationships

SL bioactivity is tightly linked to molecular structure. Key determinants include structural type (acidic versus lactonic), fatty‑acid chain length, degree of unsaturation, and acetylation pattern. In general, shorter chains, greater acetylation, and lactonic forms correlate with stronger antibacterial potency, although species‑specific exceptions occur (e.g., certain C22 congeners show enhanced activity against Cutibacterium acnes [21]). Bolaform SLs remain underexplored; their symmetric, bola‑amphiphilic architecture may impart distinct interfacial and biological properties and therefore warrant systematic study. From an application perspective, crude SL extracts (mixtures) frequently deliver broader or stronger overall activity due to synergistic or complementary interactions among congeners and offer economic advantages. Nevertheless, isolation and testing of purified congeners are essential to define precise mechanisms and robust structure–activity relationships (SARs).

Formulation strategies and functional enhancement

Formulation and targeted modification extend SL functionality. Co‑formulation with surfactants, antimicrobials, polymers, or nanomaterials can enhance antibacterial and antibiofilm efficacy, improve emulsification and cleaning performance, and increase remediation efficiency while reducing required dosages. Chemical (e.g., amidation, quaternization) and enzymatic (e.g., regioselective esterification) modifications permit directional tuning of hydrophobicity, charge, and targeting. Exploiting SL amphiphilicity enables self‑assembly or hybridization with other materials to produce nanostructured carriers that increase drug loading, improve delivery of insoluble therapeutics, potentiate antimicrobial/antitumor effects, and expand environmental applications.

Challenges and prospects

The commercialization and practical deployment of SLs are constrained by interrelated scientific, technological, and economic barriers. Addressing these barriers requires coordinated advances across fundamental mechanistic studies, strain and process engineering, formulation science, and translational validation under application-relevant conditions. Based on our review of the current literature, we propose the following priority research directions that could facilitate the maturation of SL technologies.

Elucidate structure–activity relationships

Systematic, quantifiable investigations are needed to determine how defined structural parameters (e.g., glycosylation pattern, acetylation, fatty‑acid chain length and saturation, lactonization, bolaforms) govern physicochemical properties and biological activities. This requires access to well‑characterized, homogeneous homologs via advanced separation and synthetic strategies, coupled with high‑throughput bioassays, computational modeling, and integrated multi‑omics to establish mechanistic hypotheses and predictive structure–function models.

Characterize understudied variants

Certain classes of SLs—most notably bolaform congeners and atypical glycoforms—remain poorly described. Comprehensive characterization of their surfactant behavior, self‑assembly, phase behavior, stability under relevant environmental conditions, and bioactivity is necessary to identify unique functional niches and application windows.

Quantify synergistic and composite effects

Molecular‑level studies of interactions between SLs and conventional antimicrobials, natural products, polymers, or material matrices are required to elucidate synergistic, additive, or antagonistic outcomes. Mechanistic understanding will enable rational co‑formulation and composite design for medical, agricultural, and industrial applications, with attention to efficacy, safety, and environmental fate.

Advance production biology and scalable processes

Apply metabolic engineering and synthetic‑biology tools to construct robust production strains with improved titers, yields, and volumetric productivities, and to enable precise control of biosynthetic enzymes for targeted structural diversification and component enrichment. Fermentation R&D should emphasize scalable bioprocess design, low‑cost renewable feedstocks, integrated downstream purification, and techno‑economic and life‑cycle assessments aligned with circular‑economy principles.

Develop targeted modifications and delivery systems

Guided by validated structure–activity relationships, pursue selective chemical or enzymatic modifications and advanced formulation strategies (e.g., SL‑based nanosystems, polymeric composites) to enhance potency, stability, bioavailability, targeting, and ecotoxicological profiles. Robust physicochemical and ecotoxicological evaluation frameworks should accompany formulation development.

Collectively, these directions call for multidisciplinary collaborations, standardized analytical frameworks, and early engagement with regulatory and end‑user stakeholders to ensure that research progress translates into safe, effective, and economically viable SL products.
