The risk prediction models for deep venous thrombosis in perioperative patients with lower limb fractures: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ruixin He, Xiaoyan Li, Jie He, Huizhuo Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe quality of model development and its applicability are still unknown, even though numerous studies have developed or validated predictive models to estimate the risk of perioperative deep venous thrombosis (DVT) in lower limb fractures. The aim of the study was to systematically assess the DVT risk prediction model in lower limb fracture patients in the perioperative phase.MethodsPubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) were assessed regarding studies on risk prediction models for perioperative DVT in limb fractures. The search period spanned from the database establishment to December 20, 2024. Two investigators independently screened the articles and extracted the data, and the quality of the included articles was evaluated using the PROBAST assessment tool.ResultsA total of 21 studies describing 25 predictive models were included, and the acquired data indicated that the incidence of DVT ranged from 1.2% to 72.5% in the perioperative period of lower limb fractures. The most commonly used predictors were D-dimer, age, time from injury to admission, and time from injury to surgery. The areas under the curve (AUCs) for all models ranged from 0.676 to 0.979. While twenty-three models demonstrated good predictive performance, many exhibited biases, often stemming from unreported methods for handling missing data, some models lacked external validation. The combined AUC value for the training models was 0.82 (95% confidence interval: 0.78–0.87), suggesting a moderate level of discrimination.ConclusionsThe existing model exhibits good overall prediction performance and a low applicability risk, however the bias risk is high. To enhance the robustness of these findings, further research should encompass prospective studies, external validation of the current model, and refinement of the statistical analysis aspects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09457-3.

Introduction

According to the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (GBD) that lower limb fractures were the most common and burdensome fracture worldwide in 2019 [1]. With the aging population recently, the incidence of lower limb fractures has risen notably in the elderly due to osteoporosis, decreased balance ability, and diminished vision [2]. Venous thromboembolism (VTE), encompassing deep venous thrombosis (DVT) and secondary pulmonary embolism (pulmonary embolism, PE), is a notable concern following fractures [3]. Particularly, DVT is a common complication following lower limb fractures, especially in the hip and knee, during the perioperative period. The incidence of DVT in hospitalized patients with multiple injuries accounted for 26.19%, of which postoperative deep vein thrombosis accounted for 72.73%, and bilateral lower extremity thrombosis accounted for 30.91% [4]. DVT development predisposes the individual to lower extremity swelling, and dysfunction of associated organ tissues, and is significantly associated with the development of PE, heightened risk of recurrence and mortality [5, 6]. It has been reported [7] that approximately 70% of patients with acute venous thromboembolic events predominantly develop lower extremity DVT, while 30% develop PE. While the clinical detection rate of DVT is low, and often there are no apparent clinical manifestations, either because the symptoms of fracture are masked or due to the absence of obvious clinical signs [8]. This delay in diagnosis results in increasing the risk of mortality due to PE. Therefore, it is important to strengthen perioperative nursing intervention for patients with lower limb fractures.

At present, DVT still lacks specific treatment in clinic, emphasizing the importance of prevention. In order to detect DVT early, orthopedic experts have made substantial efforts to address this issue, such as identifying patient- or injury-related risk factors [9, 10], and exploring potential biomarkers (e.g., neutrophils or platelet-to-lymphocyte ratios) [3, 11]. Prevention of DVT is crucial when lower limb fractures, especially hip fractures. Patients with these fractures tend to have poor general conditions, such as advanced age, reduced organ reserve systems, and compromised vascular conditions [12]. Additionally, they exhibit impaired hindlimb locomotion, making them more prone to developing DVT [13]. Furthermore, DVT serves as a predisposing factor for acute hemodynamic instability, further exacerbating the complexity of surgical treatment [14]. Despite the clinical relevance of these findings, studies on existing topics vary widely in terms of methodologies, approaches, and results [15, 16], which do not necessarily apply to populations in different settings.

The goal of developing or validating predictive models to assess the perioperative risk of DVT in patients who have suffered lower limb fractures has drawn more attention in recent years. The predictive model can predict the prevalence of DVT in the perioperative population of lower limb fractures, provide more accurate diagnosis and prognosis information, improve the accuracy of clinical decision-making, and help medical staff to monitor and treat high-risk groups more closely, so as to enhance the targeted preventive intervention and treatment. This approach also has the potential to reduce DVT-associated morbidity, mortality, and disability by facilitating early detection and prevention. In addition, some specific laboratory indicators are also mentioned in the prediction model, which can not only improve the clinical value of hematological tests, but also reduce the cost of regular imaging examinations. However, when focusing on the prediction of DVT in patients with lower limb fractures, we found it difficult to find a clinical prediction model with high accuracy and specificity, and the quality and results of these studies were inconsistent, resulting in uncertain predictive performance and clinical applicability of the model. First, some current prediction models do not consider factors specific to the factors that may influence the occurrence of DVT in patients with lower extremity fractures, including high-risk time periods, such as some prediction models focus on the time after surgery [17, 18], while some studies focus on the time before surgery [19, 20], and the time of collection of clinical data in postoperative patients, ranging from 7 to 30 days after surgery [8, 21, 22], with poorer accuracy of the models. In addition, the predictive factors in the models ranged from 1 to 10 [23, 24], and some studies focused on the effect of single/fewer risk factors on DVT formation in patients with lower extremity fracture, whereas thrombosis is a relatively complex and multifactorial process, so the ability of prediction by single/fewer factors is limited, and the models are less judgmental and less specific.

Furthermore, there were no systematic review on perioperative DVT risk prediction models for lower limb fractures. Although a number of predictive models for DVT in patients with lower extremity fractures exist and provide corresponding predictors, the role of these identified factors does not seem to be sufficiently emphasized by clinicians in clinical practice, mainly because they are viewed in isolation. Therefore, it is almost impossible to use these “isolated” risk factors to determine the “intuitive” and “real” likelihood of DVT in a patient. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to evaluate the perioperative DVT risk prediction model for lower extremity fractures, to provide an effective reference for clinicians to identify and intervene in perioperative DVT at an early stage, and to provide a reference for the construction, application and optimization of the DVT risk prediction model and its personalized prevention and treatment.

Material and methods

Data source and inclusion criteria

From the creation of the database until December 20, 2024, a thorough search was conducted for Chinese and English language articles through PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) to find pertinent articles. The retrieval of these articles was performed using a combination of subject headings and free words. English retrieval words mainly included: “fractures OR hip fractures OR femoral neck fracture OR intertrochanteric fracture OR subtrochanteric fractures OR trochanteric fractures OR knee fractures OR ankle fractures OR foot fractures OR tibia fractures OR fibula fractures OR calcaneal fractures”, “venous thrombosis/phlebothrombosis/venous thromboses/deep vein thrombosis/deep vein thromboses/deep-venous thrombosis/deep-venous thromboses” and “prediction model/prediction/predict model/risk prediction/risk factors/risk assessment/prognostic model/model”. Articles that were not in either Chinese or English were disregarded. To ensure no pertinent articles were overlooked, the references of the included articles were tracked down.

The eligibility of prospective or retrospective observational studies was based on whether they developed or validated predictive models or scoring systems for the incidence of perioperative DVT in lower limb fractures. Literature screening and data extraction

Two investigators independently screened the literature according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria by reading the titles and abstracts. In the event of a dispute, a third party was consulted or engaged in discussion in order to aid in decision-making and resolution. Following the exclusion of irrelevant literature, the full text was assessed to identify the ultimately included studies. In the process of data extraction, we first clarified the data to be included and the extraction principles, conducted a pre-experiment, and modified and improved the data accordingly. Data were extracted by two people independently. In case of dispute, the decision was made by two people through consultation or a third person participated in the dispute to reach an agreement. Data, including publication time, country, study design, data source, sample size, number of models, DVT outcome indicators, candidate variables in the DVT risk prediction model, the area under the curve (AUC) value (AUC value is the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve surrounded by the coordinate axis, which is a comprehensive evaluation method widely used in clinical diagnosis), or C-index, treatment and population screening studies, and the diagnostic efficiency of diagnostic tests can be judged by calculating the AUC. Generally, we consider AUC = 0.5–0.7 as poor discrimination, 0.7–0.8 as moderate discrimination, 0.8–0.9 as good discrimination, 0.9–1.0 as excellent discrimination.) or C-statistics, modeling methods, etc., were extracted and cross-checked.

Evaluation of the risk of bias and applicability of the included studies

The risk of bias and suitability of the included articles were evaluated by two investigators (RH and XL) using the PROBAST risk prediction model assessment tool [26], which is the definitive tool for assessing the risk of bias in predictive modeling studies specifically. PROBAST was developed by 38 experts in accordance with the Delphi method, a set of questions dedicated to evaluation development, validation, and updating, which consists of two parts: risk of bias assessment and applicability assessment. The risk of bias assessment consists of four domains (study object, predictor, result, and statistical analysis) with a total of 20 landmark questions, each containing 2–9 questions with 3 options: yes/probably yes, no/probably no, and lack of information [26]. The evaluator assessed the level of risk of bias in each domain by the results of all questions in that domain, which were categorized as low risk, high risk and unclear.

Statistical analysis

First, the risk and applicability of bias in each model were assessed via the PROBAST evaluation tool [26] using the descriptive analysis method and strict grid. Basic features, predictors, modeling techniques, and other information from the literature were then extracted. Next, Stata software 12.0 was used to perform a meta-analysis of the AUC values and predictive factors from the validated models. In each model fixed effects analysis was used when each study was homoplasmic (P > 0.1 and I2 < 50%). If the heterogeneity between studies (P ≤ 0.1 and I2 ≥ 50%), random effects analysis was used and the sources of heterogeneity were analyzed via sensitivity analysis. The combined effect was then calculated for each study after excluding those contributing to heterogeneity. The combined effect measures were expressed as AUC or ORs with 95% confidence interval (CI). A subgroup analysis was conducted, excluding influential or outlier studies, to identify heterogeneity among the included studies. Sensitivity analyses employed influence analysis to detect outliers and publication bias was assessed with contour-enhanced funnel plot analysis. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

In these models, eleven [12, 17–19, 22–24, 27, 28, 30, 33, 36] focused on patients with hip fractures (included femoral, intertrochanteric, and acetabular fractures); one [20] on calcaneal fracture patients, one [34] on tibial fracture patients, and six [8, 21, 29, 31, 32, 35, 37] on patients with fractures at multiple sites such as hip, knee, ankle, foot, and shaft. The total number of modeling samples in each model was 104 to 15,342, and the mean age of patients was 68.6 years. The predictors of these models varied from 1 to 10, and the total incidence of DVT in the perioperative period ranged from 1.2% to 72.5% (A study-Zeng G [27] included all patients were diagnosed with DVT, excluding non-DVT patients, the prevalence was not calculated). In four of the studies [8, 12, 31] logistic regression methods were used to establish predictive models, whereas 17 studies [17–24, 27–30, 32–34, 36, 37] used a nomogram to build the prediction model. And one study [35] established five predictive models, including XGBoost, logistic regression, random forest, multilayer perceptron, and Support Vector Machine.

Predictors included

Among the 25 models of 21 studies, the number of included predictors ranged from a minimum of 1 to a maximum of 10. D-dimer level (the most commonly used clinical indicator for the diagnosis of DVT and PE [38]), age, the time from injury to admission, the time from injury to surgery, diabetes, fibrinogen (FIB), and systemic immune inflammation index (SII, a popular immunoinflammatory index in recent years) were the most frequently utilized predictors, with D-dimer and age appearing in twelve and eleven models, respectively, and the other the time from injury to admission/surgery and diabetes in six models each. The FIB, and SII were also used five and four times, respectively.

Model validation

A summary of the model validation for each of the included prediction models is presented in Supplementary Table 2. Most of the models underwent validation; three underwent internal and external validation, twelve underwent internal validation, one underwent external validation, and five underwent no validation. The AUCs of the 21 models was 0.648 to 0.979 for modeling; five studies reported AUCs was 0.545 to 0.912 for model validation. Exception for the study by Chen X, et al. [23] and Lin Z, et al. [32], the AUCs of other studies were all higher than 0.7, indicating good performance.

Risk of bias and applicability

The results from the risk of bias and applicability assessment, conducted with the Prediction model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST), were detailed in Table 2. The study evaluation indicated that all DVT risk prediction models had some risk of bias, except one study Xing J [29]. The primary issue was the inappropriate data sources, as well all models were derived from retrospective studies. Additionally, two models used improper methods for selecting predictive factors, seven models had insufficient sample sizes (< 100 cases for training group) [39], eight models did not report missing data, and five models did not evaluate the models. As for the applicability, seven of the studies had a high risk of applicability and fourteen had a low risk. Mainly because most of the studies did not focus on lower limb surgery in all age groups, as well as did not explicitly report uniform prediction times, and there were concerns about the timing of predictor measurements. Meta-analysis

Meta-analysis of validation models included in the review

Of the models in the included studies, 21 studies were eligible for synthesis. Among these, Wei et al.'s [35] research involved multiple methods for model development, all were based on the same sample, so only model developed using logistic regression was included. The pooled AUC was calculated using a random effects model, resulting in a value of 0.82 (95% CI: 0.78–0.87) (Fig. 2). The I2 value was 96% (p < 0.001), indicating a high degree of heterogeneity among the studies. To explore potential sources of the high heterogeneity, six subgroups according to modeling method, fracture site, country, study design, prediction time, and mean age were conducted. Subgroup analysis of pooled AUCs did not identify any discrete sources of the heterogeneity observed between studies (Table 3). In terms of Model method, the model performance of nomogram (AUC = 0.83) was more consistent with that of logistic regression (AUC = 0.82). Most of the studies were on older adults, however model performance for young adults (AUC = 0.87) was better than older adults (AUC = 0.82) and unclear mean age (AUC = 0.76). In terms of prediction time, model performance for pre-operative (AUC = 0.85) was superior to post-operative (AUC = 0.84) and perioperative period (AUC = 0.78). The model performance of pre-operative and post-operative was significantly better than that of perioperative period, and therefore pre-operative and post-operative prediction tools are recommended as future screening tools for DVT of lower extremity fractures, respectively. Sensitivity analyses and publication bias

To further explore potential sources of the high heterogeneity, the sensitivity analyses using leave-one-out method to identify any significant source of heterogeneities in all studies. The sensitivity analysis showed that this meta-analysis was stable (Supplementary Fig. 1). A funnel plot was used to evaluate the publication bias among the included studies regarding the risk prediction models for DVT in perioperative patients with lower limb fracture. The results revealed no noticeable publication bias (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

DVT is a common complication of traumatic fractures, especially those involving the lower extremities. Multiple studies have shown that DVT is preventable [40, 41], and prevention is the most effective way to minimize its incidence and associated negative consequences. Multimodal intervention strategies, involving a combination of measures, are widely used to prevent perioperative DVT in lower limb fractures [41, 42]. However, these strategies require adequate resources. Predictive models help stratify the risk of perioperative DVT, enabling healthcare providers to implement preventive measures in high-risk patients. Twenty-one studies—of which twenty-five models were described—were included in this systematic review of predictive models associated with perioperative DVT in patients with lower limb fractures. The included models generally showed moderate to high predictive performance in internal or external validation with values ranging between 0.648 to 0.979. Nonetheless, PROBAST deemed the majority of studies were noted of high concern and a high risk of bias. Strict predictive models have limited availability and more high-quality research is needed to drive development in this area.

Insights of great value can be obtained from how the included models were developed. For example, Xing J et al. [29] used a large sample size cohort design with both internal and external validation, but it was a retrospective study with a high risk of bias. Brennan J et al.'s [8] study, although also with a large sample size and data from the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program, was deficient in the treatment of missing data and lacked external validation, failing to address the issue of missing data. The study was deficient in the handling of missing data, model calibration, and differentiation, and lacked external validation, failing to address the problem of model overfitting [43]. It is important to mention that Wei C et al. [35] employed both logistic regression and various machine learning techniques during the model development. It has been demonstrated that machine learning algorithms usually offer superior accuracy compared to logistic regression [44]. A number of the studies faced issues related to sample size, the management of continuous variables, and predictor selection. Applying machine learning strategies in model development can address some of these issues. A disadvantage of machine learning models is the existing shortage of suitable representation tools. Consequently, researchers should determine the right model development approach for each specific situation. In general, despite all models demonstrating moderate or good performance, the risk of bias is still high. In our assessment of bias using PROBAST for the included studies, all studies were rated as having a high risk of bias except Xing J [29], and it was observed that model validation requires further exploration. Seventeen models with AUCs > 0.7 observed in all models except those by Chen X et al. [23] and Lin Z et al. [32], indicating their effectiveness in predicting DVT in perioperative lower limb fractures. There is room for improvement in terms of data sources (cohort or cross-sectional studies), time interval between predictor assessment and outcome determination, number of events, treatment of continuous variables, predictor selection methods, data complexity, and model calibration and fitting.

Although subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses were performed, the heterogeneity of the meta-analyses was still large, possibly due to the following reasons: first, although the prediction time for the occurrence of DVT in the included studies was perioperative, the prediction time varied considerably, e.g., the prediction time in the postoperative period ranged from 5 to 30 days postoperatively, and thus the degree of occurrence of DVT in the studies may be inconsistent. However, in terms of model performance, preoperative and post-operative prediction tools, respectively, are recommended as future screening tools for DVT of lower extremity fractures. Second, although subgroup analyses were performed based on demographic data, including modeling method, fracture site, country, study design, time to prediction, and mean age, they did not standardize all confounders. Third, most of the subjects included in the study were elderly, and most of them had multiple comorbidities, each affecting the others. Finally, differences in quality control of the study process may have led to greater heterogeneity.

Due primarily to the varying ages of the target population included in the study, reported incidences varied greatly (from 1.2% to 72.5%). Research on senior citizens has revealed a high incidence of DVT [12, 15]. Perioperative DVT is a common complication and can occur in patients of any age, making screening of patients of all ages necessary. However, only six models [23, 29–32, 34] focused specifically on perioperative lower limb fracture patients of adults. The models in our systematic review include a variety of predictors because perioperative DVT is influenced by several factors. The most frequent predictor in these models was D-dimer and it has the advantage of high sensitivity, however D-dimer lacks specificity. Studies suggest that the main factors affecting D-dimer are infection, pregnancy, persistent blood loss, trauma, stress status after surgical anesthesia, tumors, etc. [45]. Additionally, the hypercoagulable state in patients at the time of fracture may be one of the main reasons for the increase in D-dimer [38, 45]. Therefore, the D-dimer test is primarily employed for exclusionary diagnosis and is not sufficient as the sole basis for confirming the diagnosis of deep venous thrombosis or pulmonary embolism, which requires multifactorial comprehensive assessment. Notably, the results of this study showed that age was another well-documented risk factor and one of the most common predictors. Some studies [46, 47] revealed that the age-related metabolites glutamine, phenylalanine, and proline significantly affect the vein wall and E-selectin, contributing to venous thrombosis. In addition, the duration from injury to operation and from hospitalization to operation may represent immobility time, which is a transient risk factor and has been identified as a factor predisposing patients to thrombosis [48, 49]. This emphasizes that timely medical attention and shortening the waiting time after admission are particularly important.

There are some clinical implications to the current prediction models. Firstly, the predictors in these models may serve as predictors in future studies when developing new models. Furthermore, evaluating the availability of predictive models is crucial, as predictors from various populations may affect how biased the outcomes are. Second, limited studies have evaluated the clinical benefit of existing prediction models, which should facilitate clinical promotion and application. However, the included models also have certain shortcomings, primarily in the following aspects: 1) In terms of the included population, most of the models were established based on the lower limb fracture population of the study site and the Affiliated Hospital of Medical College. The lack of patients in primary care centers limits the extrapolation of the models. 2) In terms of missing data, all studies were retrospective studies and there was a risk of missing data. Only three studies reported missing data, one of which came from studies in the Department of Orthopaedics of hospitals in different economic development provinces of China. A multiple filler method was used to supplement the data for missing data. 3) In terms of predictor selection, most studies conducted their selection based on multiple factor analysis results, without combining clinical expertise and other independent variables. This approach introduces the risk of bias. 4) In terms of model validation, a complete model build should include internal validation and external validation of the model to test the validity of the model, and the datasets validated by the two should be completely heterogeneous [50]. In this study, out of the models of 21 studies, 6 were not internally validated, and 17 were not externally validated. The incomplete evaluation of model effectiveness hinders its further clinical translation, and its external efficacy remains to be explored. Furthermore, some studies do not specify when the predictive model should be used, this could limit the applicability and usability of the model. Nonetheless, future studies can use prospective studies, multicenter studies and prediction models validated in different populations, or develop new prediction models to address the identified limitations and explore DVT risk prediction models that are practical, generalizable, and suitable for lower extremity fracture patients at different time points. At the same time, avoiding the factors affecting the model conversion and promoting the translation of prediction models to clinical practice, so as to provide a high-quality DVT risk assessment tool for lower limb fracture patients, is also the direction of our unremitting efforts.

Conclusions

Twenty-one perioperative DVT risk prediction models for lower limb fractures were included in this study, with D-dimer level, age, time from injury to admission, and time from injury to surgery, as the most common used predictors. The overall predictive performance of the model was good and the risk of applicability was low. However, according to PROBAST criteria, the risk of bias in developing or validating the predictive model was high in most studies included. This study also has shortcomings, firstly, the included studies focused on patients with lower limb fractures in the perioperative period, and did not evaluate the prediction results of non-surgical patients; secondly, most models lacked external validation, and the stability and applicability of the models need to be further explored. The PROBAST criteria are not met by the clinical models currently used to predict lower limb fractures and perioperative DVT. Therefore, future research should concentrate on strengthening the predictive models currently in use or rigorously developing new models. At the same time, multicenter, large-sample prospective studies can be carried out to include patients from primary care centers, taking full account of the extrapolation of the model.
