Diagnostic Value of Choroid Plexus-to-Lateral Ventricle Volume Ratio (CLVVR) in First-Trimester Screening (11–13⁺6 Weeks) for Fetal Autosomal Aneuploidies
Authors: Shanshan Huang, Chushuang Zhang, Yanqiu Ding, Guorong Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditional first-trimester screening for chromosomal abnormalities using nuchal translucency (NT), serum biomarkers, and cell-free DNA-based screening still has limitations, particularly for morphology-based early risk enrichment and for abnormalities outside the main autosomal trisomies. This study evaluated a novel 3D ultrasound marker—the choroid plexus-to-lateral ventricle volume ratio (CLVVR)—for detecting fetal autosomal aneuploidies at 11–13⁺6 gestational weeks.MethodsIn this prospective, case-enriched cohort study, 142 singleton pregnancies (100 euploid and 42 aneuploid) underwent 3D volumetry using Virtual Organ Computer-Aided Analysis (VOCAL). CLVVR was calculated from choroid plexus volume and lateral ventricle volume. Diagnostic performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and compared with a baseline Fetal Medicine Foundation (FMF)-style screening model and an updated model incorporating CLVVR.ResultsCLVVR was significantly lower in trisomy 21 (0.317) and trisomy 18 (0.270) than in euploid fetuses (0.458; P < 0.001), whereas no significant differences were observed in sex chromosome or structural chromosomal abnormalities. CLVVR showed good discriminatory performance for autosomal aneuploidies (AUC = 0.928, sensitivity = 91.0%, specificity = 77.5%) and outperformed choroid plexus volume alone. In the external validation analysis, incorporation of CLVVR improved the AUC of the baseline FMF-style model from 0.927 to 0.959. Intra- and interobserver reproducibility was high (ICC > 0.90).ConclusionCLVVR appears to be a reproducible, non-invasive morphometric marker for trisomy 21 and trisomy 18 in the first trimester. Its integration into an established screening framework improved discrimination in an external validation analysis, although broader validation is still needed before routine implementation.Keywords: first trimester screening, 3D ultrasound, choroid plexus volumetry, autosomal trisomy, trisomy 21, trisomy 18, prenatal diagnosis

Introduction

Chromosomal abnormalities, occurring in approximately 0.5% of live births, are a leading cause of structural malformations, miscarriage, and perinatal mortality.1 Current first-trimester screening combines nuchal translucency (NT), maternal serum biomarkers, and non-invasive prenatal screening (NIPS/cfDNA), but important limitations remain, particularly for morphology-based early phenotyping and for abnormalities not fully captured by standard screening frameworks.2–5 Additional ultrasound-based morphometric markers may therefore provide complementary information for early identification of autosomal aneuploidies.

The choroid plexus (CP), a key structure in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) production,6 has increasingly been explored as an imaging marker of early brain development. Prior first-trimester studies based on 2D choroid plexus/lateral ventricle length or area relationships suggested that ventricular-choroid disproportion may be associated with aneuploidy, but their diagnostic performance has been inconsistent and limited by the inability of 2D measurements to capture irregular ventricular geometry.7,8

In the present study, we used 3D ultrasound with VOCAL to quantify choroid plexus volume (CPV), lateral ventricle volume (LVV), and the choroid plexus-to-lateral ventricle volume ratio (CLVVR; a study-specific shorthand retained for consistency across the manuscript, tables, and figures) at 11–13⁺6 weeks. We further evaluated whether this ratio added discriminatory value beyond an FMF-style first-trimester screening framework in an independent external validation cohort.9–12 In this framework, patient-specific risks for trisomies are estimated using maternal age, gestational age determined by crown-rump length (CRL), fetal nuchal translucency (NT), fetal heart rate (FHR), and maternal serum biochemical markers, including free β-hCG and pregnancy-associated plasma protein A (PAPP-A). In the present study, this baseline trisomy risk framework was used to assess whether the addition of CLVVR could improve discrimination of autosomal aneuploidies.

3D Ultrasound Acquisition and Volumetric Analysis

Ultrasound examinations were performed using a GE Voluson E8 system (GE Healthcare) equipped with a transabdominal RAB6-D volume transducer (5.0–8.0 MHz). Volume datasets were acquired using a standardized trans-temporal approach. Stored 3D volumes were reconstructed offline, and VOCAL-based measurements were performed on the midsagittal plane. For each case, three high-quality volumes meeting predefined criteria (complete choroid plexus and lateral ventricle visualization and minimal motion artifact) were stored for offline analysis.

Choroid plexus volume (CPV) and lateral ventricle volume (LVV) were measured using the Virtual Organ Computer-Aided Analysis (VOCAL™) module in 4D-View software (GE Healthcare). Manual tracing was performed as follows:

LVV: Hypoechoic CSF-filled lateral ventricles were measured from the medial choroid plexus margin to the lateral ventricular wall (Figure 2A).

LVV: Hypoechoic CSF-filled lateral ventricles were measured from the medial choroid plexus margin to the lateral ventricular wall (Figure 2A).

CPV: Hyperechoic choroid plexus boundaries were traced at 30° rotational intervals, excluding adjacent hypoechoic cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) spaces (Figure 2B).

CPV: Hyperechoic choroid plexus boundaries were traced at 30° rotational intervals, excluding adjacent hypoechoic cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) spaces (Figure 2B).

VOCAL-based measurement of lateral ventricle volume (LVV) and choroid plexus volume (CPV). Dotted or outlined tracing regions denote the manual segmentation boundaries used for VOCAL calculations. (A) LVV tracing of the hypoechoic cerebrospinal fluid space from the medial choroid plexus margin to the lateral ventricular wall. (B) CPV tracing of the hyperechoic choroid plexus boundaries with exclusion of adjacent cerebrospinal fluid spaces.

All measurements were performed on stored volume datasets. Reproducibility testing used blinded repeat analysis as described below; formal per-case acquisition time was not prospectively recorded.

External Validation Based on the FMF First-Trimester Trisomy Screening Framework

The external validation cohort was sourced from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University. External cases were limited to the same gestational window and were selected using the same inclusion and exclusion criteria as the derivation cohort. The external set comprised 103 abnormal fetuses and 397 normal fetuses and was used to compare the performance of an FMF-style baseline screening model with that of an updated model incorporating CLVVR. Volumetric analyses in the external cohort were performed using the same offline workflow and measurement definitions as in the derivation cohort; however, formal inter-center protocol harmonization and blinded re-reading were not independently re-audited.

For external validation, baseline patient-specific risk for fetal aneuploidy was estimated using an FMF-style first-trimester trisomy screening framework incorporating maternal age, gestational age determined by crown-rump length (CRL), fetal nuchal translucency (NT), fetal heart rate (FHR), and maternal serum free β-hCG and pregnancy-associated plasma protein A (PAPP-A). CLVVR was then added as an additional predictor to generate updated model estimates.

To assess the incremental value of CLVVR, we compared the baseline FMF-style model with the updated model using ROC analysis, including area under the curve (AUC), detection rate (DR), false-positive rate (FPR), positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). Because this external analysis was designed primarily as a discrimination comparison, formal calibration and overfitting assessment were not performed and are acknowledged as limitations.

Results

Comparison of Baseline Characteristics and Ultrasound Parameters Between Normal and Chromosomal Abnormality Groups

The study cohort comprised 142 fetuses, categorized into a normal karyotype group (n=100) and a chromosomal abnormality group (n=42). Maternal age, gestational age, fetal crown-rump length (CRL), and maternal body mass index (BMI) did not differ significantly between the two groups (P >0.05 for all), indicating balanced baseline characteristics. However, the chromosomal abnormality group exhibited a significantly higher median nuchal translucency (NT) thickness compared to the normal group (1.6mm [IQR: 1.4–2.0] vs 1.3mm [IQR:1.1–1.5], P <0.001).

Ultrasound parameter analysis revealed markedly reduced choroid plexus volume (CPV) and choroid plexus-to-lateral ventricle volume ratio (CLVVR) in the chromosomal abnormality group (CPV: 0.255 cm3 [IQR: 0.212–0.294] vs 0.305 cm3 [IQR: 0.260–0.382], P <0.001; CLVVR: 0.361 [IQR: 0.284–0.522] vs 0.458 [IQR: 0.377–0.561], P <0.001). In contrast, lateral ventricle volume (LVV) showed no significant difference between the groups (0.586 cm3 [IQR: 0.423–0.888] vs 0.650 cm3 [IQR: 0.459–1.012], P = 0.313) (Table 1).

DeLong’s test confirmed significant AUC differences (ΔAUC=0.189, Z=3.200, P<0.05).

Bootstrap validation (1,000 iterations) supported the stability of CLVVR discrimination, yielding a mean AUC of 0.915 (95% CI: 0.882–0.941). For the trisomy 18 subgroup (n = 5), bootstrap analysis yielded an AUC of 0.903 (95% CI: 0.821–0.962), but these estimates should be interpreted cautiously because of the very small subgroup size.

Measurement Reproducibility and Agreement

ICC analysis demonstrated excellent intraobserver reproducibility for CPV, LVV, and CLVVR measurements: 0.982 (95% CI = 0.966–0.990), 0.996 (95% CI = 0.992–0.998), and 0.963 (95% CI = 0.932–0.980), respectively. Interobserver ICC values were 0.977 (95% CI = 0.957–0.988), 0.985 (95% CI = 0.972–0.992), and 0.908 (95% CI = 0.834–0.951) for CPV, LVV, and CLVVR, respectively. Bland–Altman analysis showed that >95% of measurement differences fell within the 95% limits of agreement for both intra- and interobserver assessments (Figure 4), supporting good technical reproducibility under standardized study conditions.

Diagnostic Performance Before and After the Addition of CLVVR to the FMF Baseline Trisomy Screening Model

The baseline patient-specific risk for fetal aneuploidy was estimated using the FMF-style first-trimester framework based on maternal age, gestational age determined by CRL, fetal nuchal translucency thickness (NT), fetal heart rate (FHR), serum free β-hCG, and pregnancy-associated plasma protein A (PAPP-A). Each external validation case was classified as screen-positive or screen-negative at the prespecified threshold. We then compared this baseline model with an updated model that additionally incorporated CLVVR.

At the prespecified threshold, the baseline model yielded 120 screen-positive calls, corresponding to a DR of 76.17%, an FPR of 10.41%, a PPV of 66.15%, and an NPV of 92.61%. After addition of CLVVR, the updated model yielded 106 screen-positive calls, with a DR of 83.94%, an FPR of 7.15%, a PPV of 79.15%, and an NPV of 95.41%. Thus, incorporation of CLVVR was associated with fewer positive calls overall, a higher detection rate, a lower false-positive rate, and improved predictive values (Table 4).

Discussion

Current prenatal diagnostic pathways increasingly integrate first-trimester ultrasound, biochemical screening, and cfDNA-based approaches, yet each modality has distinct strengths and limitations. First-trimester ultrasound remains essential for early anatomical assessment, whereas cfDNA and related molecular approaches provide strong screening performance for common aneuploidies but do not replace diagnostic testing or detailed morphological evaluation.14 Within this context, a complementary ultrasound-derived marker such as CLVVR is best viewed as an adjunctive early phenotyping tool rather than a stand-alone replacement for established screening strategies.

Our findings are consistent with prior studies showing that disproportion between the choroid plexus and the lateral ventricle may be informative in the first trimester.7,8,15 The present study extends this literature by applying 3D volumetry and by examining both derivation-cohort discrimination and incremental value in an external validation setting. The observation that CPV was lower in abnormal fetuses whereas LVV did not differ significantly at the group level does not preclude improved performance of CLVVR over CPV alone. A ratio may enhance discrimination by normalizing CP volume to the available ventricular space and thereby reducing between-fetus anatomical variation that is not directly related to aneuploidy. In other words, the added discriminatory value of the ratio does not require LVV itself to differ significantly at the group level; rather, it may arise from improved normalization of CP size to ventricular context at the individual-fetus level. Chromosomal abnormalities are closely associated with central nervous system (CNS) developmental defects. Studies report a 7% incidence of chromosomal abnormalities in fetuses with ventriculomegaly (VM) (95% CI: 5%–9%),16 with trisomy 21 being the most prevalent numerical aberration.17 Over 100 genes linked to hydrocephalus pathogenesis—including L1CAM, AP1S2, MPDZ, and CCDC88C—have been identified, with mutations disrupting cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) homeostasis. For instance, L1CAM mutations are a hallmark of X-linked hydrocephalus.18

Recent studies have suggested an association between first-trimester choroid plexus (CP) and lateral ventricle (LV) morphometrics and chromosomal abnormalities. Manegold-Brauer et al7 reported elevated choroid plexus-to-lateral ventricle length ratios (PLVLR) in aneuploid fetuses compared with euploid controls (0.53 vs 0.40, P < 0.05), whereas Loureiro et al8 found reduced choroid plexus-to-LV area ratios (CLR) in trisomy 18 and trisomy 13, but not in trisomy 21, despite smaller LV and CP dimensions. These heterogeneous 2D findings may partly reflect methodological limitations in capturing irregular ventricular geometry and complex spatial relationships in early fetal brain structures. Against this background, our use of 3D volumetry was intended to provide a more comprehensive assessment of ventricular–choroid proportionality. Although autosomal trisomies may plausibly influence early neurodevelopmental growth trajectories and choroid plexus maturation, the mechanisms linking these abnormalities to ventricular–choroid disproportion remain incompletely understood. Accordingly, the biological interpretation of CLVVR should be regarded as supportive and hypothesis-generating rather than as a mechanism directly established by the present clinical dataset.

Baseline characteristics, including maternal age and gestational age, showed no significant differences between groups (P >0.05).The elevated nuchal translucency (NT) thickness in the chromosomal abnormality group (P <0.001) aligns with prior evidence linking increased NT to genetic defect risk.19 The diagnostic data support moderate-to-good discrimination rather than an unqualified claim of “excellent” performance. CLVVR achieved an AUC of 0.928 with high sensitivity but only moderate specificity (77.5%), indicating that false-positive results remain clinically relevant. Accordingly, CLVVR should be interpreted alongside established first-trimester markers rather than as an isolated decision tool. The same caution applies to the external validation analysis: although the updated FMF-style model showed improved AUC, DR, FPR, PPV, and NPV after addition of CLVVR, the analysis was exploratory and should not be overinterpreted as proof of immediate clinical implementation.

The specificity of autosomal aneuploidies may reflect distinct pathological cascades, including genomic dosage imbalance, epigenetic dysregulation, and aquaporin-mediated CSF perturbations, which can influence neural crest migration and CP secretory function.20–28 In comparison, sex chromosome anomalies primarily affect gonadal development and transcriptional networks.29–33 The observed improvement in diagnostic performance of 3D CLVVR over conventional 2D parameters (eg, a 32% sensitivity gain for trisomy 21) suggests its potential utility as an early biomarker, although it does not establish causality. Divergence from L1CAM-associated hydrocephalus phenotypes in sex chromosome variants (such as 45, X and 47, XXY) points to tissue-specific CSF regulatory mechanisms, warranting further investigation at the transcriptomic level.8,15,34–37 Sensitivity analyses excluding NT ≥ 3.0 mm indicate that the ratio signal may persist even in fetuses without markedly enlarged NT, but small subgroup sizes—particularly for trisomy 18 (n = 2)—limit the generalizability of this finding. Similarly, reproducibility data demonstrate technical reliability under standardized conditions, but routine application in non-expert settings remains uncertain, especially as measurements were obtained on a single ultrasound platform using vendor-specific offline software.

Several limitations merit explicit emphasis. First, the derivation cohort was case-enriched and therefore subject to spectrum bias; predictive values from this internal dataset should not be extrapolated to routine screening prevalence. Second, the trisomy 18 subgroup was very small, limiting precision. Third, the external validation model incorporated CLVVR as an additional predictor, but formal calibration analysis and overfitting assessment were not performed. Fourth, the study did not directly compare CLVVR with contemporary cfDNA/NIPS-based screening performance. Fifth, the single-center derivation design, operator expertise, and platform-specific VOCAL workflow limit generalizability. Finally, formal per-case acquisition time was not prospectively recorded, so feasibility in routine workflow can only be inferred indirectly.

These caveats notwithstanding, the study suggests that a volumetric first-trimester marker may complement evolving prenatal diagnostic pathways that increasingly combine morphological assessment with genome-wide and molecular screening approaches.36,37 Future work should prioritize multicenter validation, calibration-aware model development, automated or semi-automated measurement workflows, and head-to-head comparison against contemporary cfDNA/NIPS-informed pathways before routine implementation is considered.
