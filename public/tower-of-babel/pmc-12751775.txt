Is the HPV vaccine lottery system equitable under constrained vaccine supply? A cross-sectional study in China
Authors: Bo Yan, Zilian Yu, Shuting Kong, Yulian Yang, Xiaomin Wang, Hui Zhu, Xudong Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLimited supply has led to poor accessibility of high-priced imported human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccines, resulting in a vaccine lottery system in China. Few studies explore the inequities of vaccine lottery based on real-world environments, leaving uncertainty about whether the lottery actually achieved equal distribution in practical application. This study aims to take the HPV vaccine lottery system in China as an example to investigate the potential factors associated with participation in HPV vaccine lottery and the association between participation in HPV vaccine lottery and HPV vaccination.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in a developed county that have piloted the HPV vaccine lottery system between April and June 2024. The study respondents were mothers of 7th grade students and the primary outcome variables were mothers’ participation in vaccine lottery and HPV vaccination status. Logistic regression models were used for analysis.ResultsAmong the 2436 mothers of 7th grade students, mothers with high school, bachelor’s degree or higher educational level, a monthly household income exceeding 20,000 RMB (US$2752) and better critical health literacy were more likely to have ever participated in the vaccine lottery. The above significant disparities still existed in the subgroups of medical education background, residence and functional health literacy. Mothers who used to participate in the vaccine lottery were 5.54 times more likely to have received the HPV vaccine.ConclusionsEducational attainment, household income, and health literacy are key determinants of both HPV vaccine lottery participation and vaccination. Our results suggested potential inequities generated by the vaccine lottery system, indicating that consideration should be given to further optimizing the vaccine lottery and combining it with vaccine subsidies, improving the health literacy of the population, and enhancing the accessibility and comprehensibility of vaccine-related information.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01771-5.

Introduction

Vaccination plays a crucial and effective role in mitigating illness, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), thereby averting millions of deaths from vaccine-preventable diseases [1]. Despite a number of global initiatives aimed at reducing vaccine inequities over the past decades, immunisation coverage varies widely across countries [2, 3]. Among them, progress in Human Papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine promotion is particularly unevenly distributed [4]. Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer affecting women globally, with 90% of the total deaths caused by cervical cancer occurring in LMICs [5, 6]. However, many LMICs have been unable to incorporate HPV vaccine into their national immunisation schedules due to financial and supply constraints. As a result, a significantly lower proportion of LMICs have introduced HPV vaccination compared to high-income countries (HICs) [7], leaving the populations with the highest incidence and mortality rates largely unprotected [8, 9].

Among the diverse ways of allocating scarce vaccines, prioritisation and consequentialism are often the overriding goals in emergencies. None of the contingency plans implemented by many developed countries in response to influenza pandemics recognizes the equal right of all citizens to be vaccinated [10–13]. Nevertheless, lottery can introduce randomness into the decision-making process and can be used to allocate the resources when faced with competing demands, especially when it is difficult to discern which criterion is more valuable or carries a greater degree of weight [10, 11]. Furthermore, lottery is considered more ethical and applicable in non-emergency situations where efficiency goals cannot be sustained [12, 13]. There have been various proposals advocating the use of the lottery as a means of allocating scarce medical resources, particularly in relation to the influenza vaccine and the COVID-19 vaccine [14–17]. However, most previous studies have been solely based on the construction and evaluation of virtual lottery models rather than real-world studies, leaving uncertainty about whether the lottery actually achieved equal distribution in practical application. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct real-world studies on the equity of vaccine lottery to assess their actual effectiveness in narrowing the gap between privileged groups and the most vulnerable groups [18]. Previous limited studies have found that lottery tend to favour those who are well off, well educated, well informed and those living in urban areas [10, 19], but these studies were conducted in developed countries and were not specifically focused on HPV vaccines.

China contributes to 18% of cervical cancer cases globally, with cervical cancer ranking as the second most prevalent cancer among Chinese women [20, 21]. The Chinese government has launched the National Action Plan for Accelerated Elimination of Cervical Cancer (2023–2030) in 2023, aiming to enhance the cervical cancer prevention and control service system and strengthen HPV vaccination among eligible women. In China, HPV vaccination is recommended for all females aged 9–45 years. Women can typically access vaccination information through online media platforms supported by local governments or at community health centers [22, 23]. Currently, China has approved five HPV vaccines, including three bivalent HPV vaccines, one 4-valent HPV (4vHPV) vaccine, and one 9-valent HPV (9vHPV) vaccine. The bivalent HPV vaccine protects against infection with HPV types 16 and 18, which are the primary causative agents of cervical cancer. In contrast, the 4-valent vaccine offers additional protection against HPV types 6 and 11, while the 9-valent HPV vaccine extends protection to HPV types 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58 [24]. Accordingly, the prices of the bivalent, 4-valent, and 9-valent vaccines are approximately 600 yuan/dose (US$84/dose), 800 yuan/dose (US$111/dose), and 1,300 yuan/dose (US$181/dose), respectively. In recent years, with support from local authorities, several regions have prioritized free bivalent HPV vaccination programmes for girls aged 9–14 years [22]. However, instead of choosing the effective domestically produced 2-valent HPV vaccines which are readily available in China, women prefer the imported 4-valent HPV and 9-valent HPV vaccines due to their broader antiviral effects [25]. Nevertheless, the dearth of available supply has rendered the acquisition of imported high-priced vaccines a challenging endeavour, culminating in the emergence of a vaccine distribution system dubbed the vaccine lottery, which was dedicated to imported 4-valent HPV and 9-valent vaccines [25]. Once the requisite personal data has been submitted and the type of vaccine selected, each applicant will be assigned an exclusive code for a random ballot. Successful applicants will be duly notified via a short message and should complete the booking for the specific vaccination clinic and vaccination time within the specified period. Having success in the vaccine lottery does not impact the price of the vaccine. However, after several years of trial implementation of the vaccine lottery policy, there is still a lack of research evaluating its impact on HPV vaccination rates. It is necessary to confirm its effectiveness to ensure that optimizing this policy is not a futile effort.

Eight years after the HPV vaccine was introduced into the Chinese market, the coverage of at least one-dose of HPV vaccination for adult women aged 27–35 and 36–44 in China was only 22.0% and 20.2%, respectively. This coverage rate is more than four times that of the girls and adolescents, implying that adult women were the main audience of the current HPV vaccination in China [26, 27]. Moreover, as mothers of girls aged 9–14 years, who were the primary target group for HPV vaccination, they were found to have a significant influence on their daughters’ HPV vaccination uptake [27]. Thus, these women who have participated in the vaccine lottery constitute an ideal population to investigate the equity issue within this vaccination allocation process.

Consequently, this study aims to take the HPV vaccine lottery system in China as an example to investigate the potential factors associated with mothers’ participation in HPV vaccine lottery and the association between participation in HPV vaccine lottery and mothers’ HPV vaccination.

Methods

Study design

The data for this study were derived from a cross-sectional survey conducted between April and June 2024 in Zhejiang province in China, a developed eastern coastal province with the fourth-highest per capita gross domestic product (GDP). This cross-sectional survey was designed to investigate the willingness of mothers of 7th grade students to participate in the HPV vaccine lottery and to receive the HPV vaccine. The study site selected was Yinzhou County, Ningbo city, where the HPV vaccine lottery system has been piloted.

Participants and data collection

The respondents were mothers of 7th grade students. The inclusion criteria for this survey were as follows: (1) mothers of 7th grade students, (2) responsible for making vaccination decisions for their children, and (3) able to complete the online survey. Only parents of students with contraindications to the vaccine were excluded.

Two-stage stratified cluster random was adopted. Ten urban districts and ten rural towns in Yinzhou county were randomly chosen as sampling sites. At each site, all the middle schools were selected to guarantee the minimum sample size. All these schools agreed to participate. At each school, a certain number of classes from grade 7 were randomly selected. All parents of children in these selected classes were invited to participate.

Approval to conduct this study in schools was obtained from the local Bureau of Education and the Center for Disease Control and Prevention. The first page of the questionnaire provided a short introduction to ensure respondents’ anonymity and their right to withdraw from the study at any time, as well as a consent form requiring the signatures of all participants. Participants were informed that their involvement was voluntary and that completing the questionnaire would take approximately 10 to 15 min. The questionnaire was administered with the help of teachers to the parents responsible for making vaccination decisions for their children and those who agreed to participate then signed the consent form and completed the questionnaire online. A trap question was used as an attention check to identify valid questionnaires.

In this cross-sectional analysis, based on our pilot study, a total of 2112 mothers were required, based on a 9% participation rate among parents of 7th grade students in the vaccine lottery, a 95% confidence interval, a 90% survey response rate, and an 83% validity rate. After excluding respondents who answered the trap question incorrectly (N = 632) and in which fathers were the questionnaire completers and primary guardians (N = 565), 2576 valid samples of mothers were screened. Furthermore, to analyse the situation of vaccines of shortage categories, those who selected the domestic bivalent vaccine in the lottery system (N = 14), who could not remember the type of vaccine that had been given (N = 25), and who had received was domestic bivalent (N = 101) were also excluded (Fig. 1). Ethical approval was obtained from Zhejiang University School of Public Health (no. ZGL202303-2).

Variables and measurement

Socio-demographic variables

Socio-demographic characteristics collected for this study included: educational attainment (middle school or less/senior high school/bachelor’s degree or above), medical education background (yes/no), monthly household income, ethnicity (han/minority), and residence (rural/urban).

Health literacy

This study comprised questions on health literacy to reflect the concepts and levels of functional health literacy and critical health literacy. Two items based on information acquisition and understanding of HPV vaccination were used to evaluate functional health literacy [28]: To what degree do you agree or disagree with the following statements: (1) “It is difficult for me to find correct and comprehensive information about the HPV vaccine.” (2) “It is difficult for me to understand the information I got about the HPV vaccine.” A Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) was applied to measure the responses. The scores of these two items were added and sorted, and then divided into dichotomous variables based on the median, with greater than the median defined as a low level and vice versa as a high level.

The Chinese version of a widely used scale that fits with the concept of critical health literacy, capturing key information about HPV transmission, sequelae and prevention, was used to measure critical health literacy [29]. This scale consists of 10 items to assess the ability to critically evaluate different sources and information, such as “Only women can get infected with HPV” and “A person could have HPV for many years without knowing it”. 1 point was awarded if the answer was correct, whereas 0 point if participants made the wrong choice between “Yes” and “No”, or chose “I don’t know” directly. Similarly, the total score for these 10 questions was grouped into two levels, where below the median was equivalent to a low level and no less than the median was equivalent to a high level. The Cronbach’s alpha for the critical health literacy demonstrated relatively high internal consistency in the current study sample (α = 0.80).

Outcome variables

The primary outcome variables were mothers’ participation in vaccine lottery and HPV vaccination status. All age-appropriate women were eligible to participate in the vaccine lottery. Mothers were asked whether they had ever participated in the HPV vaccine lottery (“yes” vs. “no”) and whether they had received at least one dose of the HPV vaccine (“yes” vs. “no”). The type of vaccine administered was also reported according to the categorisation of imported bivalent, imported 4-valent and imported 9-valent.

Statistical analysis

The characteristics of the study participants were described across participation in HPV vaccine lottery and HPV vaccination status, and differences between groups, respectively, were tested by χ2 test for categorical variables. The primary analysis aimed to explore the potential factors associated with mothers’ participation in HPV vaccine lottery, using multiple logistic regression models. The potential factors were accomplished via two models: (1) Model 1, included educational attainment, medical education background, monthly household income, ethnicity, and household residence; (2) Model 2, additionally included functional health literacy and critical health literacy. Analysis was then conducted on the association of factors showing statistical significance in the regression model with participation in HPV vaccine lottery stratified by other factors.

Furthermore, we used binary logistic regression to assess the association between mothers’ participation in HPV vaccine lottery and HPV vaccination status. To test the robustness and potential variations in different subgroups, we repeated all analyses stratified by educational attainment, medical education background, monthly household income, household residence, functional health literacy, and critical health literacy. Sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the findings by incorporating additional samples who had received domestic bivalent vaccines. Statistical analysis was carried out using SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, NC). No missing data were found in the analyzed data. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of respondents

Among the 2436 mothers of 7th grade students, as presented in Table 1, nearly half (49.0%) had a monthly household income less than 5000RMB (US$688), and 44.2% attained an educational level of bachelor’s degree or above. Most were Han (96.9%) and lived in urban areas (69.1%). Only 9.3% had a medical education background. Respondents who participated in the vaccine lottery were more likely to live in urban areas, have higher levels of educational attainment, household income, and critical health literacy. HPV vaccination status was predicted by ethnicity, medical background and better functional health literacy score, in addition to the same factors that predicted vaccine lottery participation.

Vaccination status of survey respondents

Table S1 showed vaccination status of survey respondents who participated in the vaccine lottery. Among the 2,436 surveyed individuals, 269 had participated in the HPV vaccine lottery, of whom 152 were successfully selected. Among these 152 individuals, 144 had received at least one dose of the HPV vaccine, with 118 vaccinated through the lottery mechanism and 26 through other channels. Regarding vaccine types, 89 individuals received the imported 9-valent vaccine. Of the 117 respondents who had participated in the lottery but were not selected, 31 had received the imported HPV vaccine through alternative channels.

Mothers’ participation in HPV vaccine lottery and HPV vaccination

Model 1 (Table 3) indicated that mothers who had participated in the vaccine lottery were 5.54 times more likely to have received the HPV vaccine (95%CI [4.16, 7.38]). Those with a bachelor’s degree or above (aOR = 2.13, 95%CI [1.59, 2.86]), a monthly household income exceeding 15,000RMB (US$2064) (aOR = 1.97, 1.84, respectively), and a high level of functional health literacy (aOR = 1.25, 95%CI [1.01, 1.54]) and critical health literacy (aOR = 1.70, 95%CI [1.36, 2.14]) were also more likely to have been vaccinated. When further including respondents that had received the domestic bivalent (Model 2), the results of sensitivity analyses were similar to those of the main analyses. Furthermore, despite significant differences within some subgroups (p for interaction < 0.05), highly significant positive correlations mothers’ participation in HPV vaccine lottery and HPV vaccination were presented in all subgroups, consistent with the total population (appendix Fig. S1).

As Table S2 presented, participation in the vaccine lottery programme was significantly associated with higher odds of receiving both 4-valent (aOR = 3.00, 95%CI [2.10, 4.29]) and 9-valent (aOR = 15.66, 95%CI [11.00, 22.32]) HPV vaccines, relative to remaining unvaccinated. Furthermore, higher education attainment (bachelor’s degree or above), greater household income, and advanced functional health literacy also emerged as significant facilitators for uptake of higher-cost vaccine options.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the inequity of HPV vaccine lottery among women under the constrained vaccine supply condition in China. Substantial gaps were found across educational, household income, and critical health literacy level groups. Mothers with higher level of educational attainment, income, and critical health literacy had a higher probability of participating in the lottery. Furthermore, a significant positive association was observed between lottery participation and HPV vaccination. Higher educational attainment, income, and health literacy were also found to facilitate HPV vaccination. Our study reveals the inequities of HPV lottery participation in the context of insufficient vaccine supply.

We found significant differences in lottery participation rates among mothers with different levels of educational attainment, which could be attributed to disparities in health information seeking behavior. According to the knowledge gap theory, as an important component of socioeconomic status (SES), education showed positive correlations with information-seeking behavior [30]. For example, college-educated individuals were observed to demonstrate a higher tendency to seek online health information [31], and people with a higher educational level had significantly more online cancer information-seeking behavior in the United States [32]. Consistently, people with lower education may pay little attention to the online or offline health information such as routine vaccine lottery. Although the vaccine lottery is open to all females of appropriate age, low-education groups may not be able to access the information in a timely manner or understand the policy and rules, suggesting that it exists an urgent need to expand the coverage of health information among low educational level groups in order to improve the timeliness of information access.

Income inequalities contributed most to the unbalanced distribution of health service use [33, 34]. Our results echoed previous income equity studies that socioeconomically advantaged groups tended to have greater ease in accessing healthcare services and adopt new effective services earlier compared to disadvantaged groups [35]. A survey conducted in 18 countries showed that people with higher incomes were more likely to consult a doctor than those with lower incomes for the same health care needs [36]. Since the vaccine lottery is only used for imported quadrivalent and nine-valent vaccines, the high price may make the low-income group give up participating in the vaccine lottery. However, due to the blind inclination towards higher-valent vaccines, these people who did not participate in the lottery also did not choose to receive the domestically produced bivalent vaccine [25]. Therefore, in order to solve the inequality caused by income, on the one hand, the government should reduce the price of high-valent vaccines moderately, and even consider incorporating them into the National Immunisation Programme (NIP). On the other hand, publicity on the effectiveness of bivalent vaccines could be strengthened, so that groups of low socio-economic status, who have not participated in the lottery of imported vaccines due to their prices, can achieve the prevention of HPV infections by using domestic bivalent vaccines, which have a better price-performance ratio.

It is estimated that the additional burden caused by limited health literacy on the healthcare system accounts for approximately 3% to 5% of the total annual health expenditure, which serves to highlight the importance of health literacy [37]. Our findings observed a higher participation rate of HPV vaccine lottery among mothers with higher level of critical health literacy than those with lower level (13.6% vs. 7.4%). People with poor health literacy were more likely to use different types of health services including curative, emergency, and hospital care, but less often use preventive services, which might result from the lack of awareness of disease prevention [36, 38, 39]. Hence, as a route to preventive vaccination, lottery might be overlooked by females with low health literacy, who tend to focus on treatment after illness. Another reason might be the difficulties in understanding and processing health-related information. Low health literacy has been shown to be associated with poor understanding of health information [40], and the utility of information materials is diminished when they necessitate a level of health literacy that is beyond the reach of a significant proportion of the population [41]. Consequently, policymakers ought to be cognizant of the informational needs of the general population and ensure that the information they disseminate is sufficiently accessible and pertinent to their intended audience, with the ultimate objective of fostering favorable behavioral modifications.

Our findings indicate that participating in vaccine lotteries has a significant positive impact on enhancing HPV vaccination rates, suggesting that lottery is an effective measure worthy of further optimization and promotion. Fair random allocation of scarce resources can help to enhance enthusiasm. Previous studies have found that introducing random selection mechanisms into organisational design can produce a less-is-more effect, meaning that by identifying and eliminating biases, greater effectiveness can be achieved [42]. However, the inherent inequities of lottery participation may make this positive effect only apply to those who are more likely to participate. In line with the findings of previous studies, the main drawback of lottery is that they are blind to many seemingly relevant factors [8]. Consideration should be given to further optimizing the vaccine lottery system and making it more accessible to the population groups identified in this study that were less likely to be vaccinated. Future lottery mechanisms could incorporate a weighted multi-principle system with stratification based on key factors identified in our study. This approach would ensure equal probability of resource access within each stratum while allowing for resource allocation preferences toward specific strata containing disadvantaged groups. Such design would simultaneously address equity expectations among the general population and priority needs of less advantaged groups [8, 13]. Our findings aim to inform future policy adjustments and advance the establishment of a more equitable, multi-principle lottery framework to better protect the vaccination priorities of disadvantaged groups.

Positive correlations were also observed between educational attainment, household income and health literacy with HPV vaccination behavior. The results were consistent with previous studies conducted in Germany [43] and Italy [44], which showed the beneficial impact of high SES and health literacy on HPV vaccination among young women. The current HPV vaccination rate among Chinese women is unsatisfactory.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, data collection relied on a self-reported questionnaire, which may introduce recall bias. Second, our survey was conducted solely in one of the more economically developed counties, raising concerns about the generalizability of the results to less developed counties. Third, we measured vaccination through whether respondents had received at least one dose of HPV vaccine, potentially resulting in an overestimate of vaccination rates. However, single-dose HPV vaccine has been proved to offer solid protection against cervical cancer, which contributes to the plausibility of the outcome measure [45]. Finally, we included only mothers of secondary school pupils rather than surveying a broader female population, which may limit the generalisability of our findings. Future large-scale studies should further investigate the circumstances of different female groups.

Conclusions

We assessed the inequities in the HPV vaccine lottery allocation system with Chinese characteristics and identified several influencing factors. Educational attainment, household income, and health literacy were key determinants of both HPV vaccine lottery participation and vaccination. Although several researchers argued that upstream interventions focusing on social or policy level determinants are more likely to reduce socioeconomic inequities in health than downstream interventions based on individual factors [46], our results suggested potential inequities generated by the vaccine lottery system as a national policy. The HPV vaccine lottery needs to be further expanded in the future and more heavily weighted towards the disadvantaged groups identified in this study.
