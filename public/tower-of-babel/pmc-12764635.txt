Aesthetic medicine treatments in reducing the effects of chronic demodicosis
Authors: Renata Przydatek-Tyrajska, Katarzyna Bartosik, Patrycja Tokarzewska, Aleksandra Sędzikowska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Humans mites Demodex folliculorum are found in the hair follicles and in pilosebaceous units, while D. brevis favours areas with high levels of sebaceous glands. Demodex mites present in excessive numbers can lead to demodicosis. The aim of the study was to investigate the incidence of Demodex mites infestation in patients and determine the effectiveness of selected aesthetic procedures in alleviating the consequences of chronic inflammation caused by the infestation. The study group consisted of 100 patients (81 women, 19 men), and Demodex infestation was noted in 44%. The patients infested with Demodex required anti-parasite therapy and post treatment observation. The most frequently-performed aesthetic treatments that improved skin condition and improved appearance were medical peelings, mesotherapy and botulinum toxin. In cases where Demodex spp. infestation has impaired the condition of the skin, supplementing antiparasitic treatments with Aesthetic Medicine can help achieve skin recovery, long-lasting aesthetic benefits and improve patient satisfaction.

Introduction

The Demodicidae are permanent parasites of humans and other mammals. They have adapted to life in pilosebaceous units, i.e. the narrow hair follicles and their associated ducts and glands; as such, they are smaller than other mites, and some of their external structures are strongly reduced (Mullen and Durden 2018). Two species have been identified to infest humans: Demodex folliculorum (Simon, 1842) and Demodex brevis Akbulatova, 1963.

Demodex folliculorum is typically found in the hair follicles of eyelashes (Fig. 1) and in pilosebaceous units located in other parts of the body, while D. brevis favours areas with high levels of sebaceous glands (Bitton and Aumond 2021) The presence of higher numbers of Demodex mites in the skin can lead to symptoms known collectively as demodicosis; despite it usually occurring as a chronic disease, acute onsets have been noted (Gerkowicz et al. 2005; Guzman et al. 2020). Demodicosis can occur as a primary form, i.e. as a disease sui generis (including pityriasis folliculorum, papulo-pustular, nodular and cystic subtypes), and a secondary form associated mainly with immunosuppression (Chen and Plewig 2014). The mites are transferred through close and prolonged contact with an infested person, most commonly via family members (Palopoli et al. 2015).Fig. 1Five adults, a larval form (black arrow) and an egg of Demodex folliculorum (red arrow) in an eyelash preparation (original magnification 200×)

The prevalence of Demodex infestation in humans may vary significantly depending on the characteristics of the study group. Cheng et al. (2021) reported a prevalence of 68.9% among seniors (mean age: 65.7 years), among whom D. folliculorum was detected in 80.7% and D. brevis in 18.4%. Lower percentages of Demodex infestation were noted among Turkish women (mean age: 41.9 years), i.e. 39.3% (Enginyurt et al. 2015) and in a Polish population, i.e. 41%−47% (Wesołowska et al. 2014; Sędzikowska et al. 2018). Although the relationship between infestation and patient sex remains unclear, studies indicate that the prevalence of Demodex mites is positively correlated with patient age, which may be related to immunosenescence (Sędzikowska et al. 2018; Lee et al. 2023; Rychlik et al. 2024).

Colonization of pilosebaceous units by Demodex spp. may be associated with various ophthalmological conditions, such as anterior and posterior blepharitis, meibomian gland dysfunction, keratitis, chalazion, basal cell carcinoma of the eyelid, as well as certain dermatological conditions. e.g. rosacea or seborrheic dermatitis (Erbagci et al. 2003; Liu et al. 2010; Tarkowski et al. 2015; Fromstein et al. 2018; Aktaş Karabay & Aksu Çerman 2020). Demodex mites are associated with symptoms of rosacea, acne vulgaris, seborrhoeic dermatitis (Aktaş Karabay & Aksu Çerman 2020; Karincaoglu et al. 2009) or perioral dermatitis. In the above-mentioned cases, the increase in the number of Demodex mites in the pilosebaceous units seems to be secondary to the local immunotolerance/immunosuppression induced by the use of topical steroid therapy (Dolenc-Voljc et al. 2005; Douglas and Zaenglein 2019).

The clinical picture of demodicosis includes various dermatological symptoms that may prompt the patient to seek help from a specialist in Aesthetic Medicine, the most common being papules, spots, erythema, hyperpigmentation, burning skin, dry skin or skin with an oily T-zone. In recent decades, both the availability and range of treatments offered by Aesthetic Medicine have significantly increased. Data from the American Society for Esthetic Plastic Surgery (ASAPS) indicates that the number of treatments has increased by 197% since records began in 1997–2011 (Krueger et al. 2013). The discipline has seen a shift away from treatments that require permanent interference with the skin structure in favour of repair treatments that support natural regeneration processes (Zarbafian et al. 2022).

The goal of Aesthetic Medicine is not only to care for patients looking to slow the ageing process or to improve their appearance. It also aims to significantly improve their quality of life by minimizing and mitigating the aesthetic impact of conditions such as skin diseases (Hoffman and Fabi 2022).

The aim of the study was to investigate the incidence of Demodex infestation in patients reporting to a specialist in Aesthetic Medicine, and determine the effectiveness of selected aesthetic procedures in alleviating the consequences of chronic inflammation caused by the infestation.

Materials and methods

All experimental protocols were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by Bioethics Committee of the Medical University of Lublin (MUL). Consent from the Bioethics Committee of the MUL was obtained prior to the research (consent number: KE-0254/122/2018). All participants gave their written informed consent to take part in the study by completing Informed Consent to Participate in a Research Study.

Recruitment for the study took place through an announcement placed on the online profile of an Aesthetic Medicine clinic. The inclusion criteria comprised adults, of either sex, with full legal capacity, complaining of chronic or recurrent skin and/or eye problems, e.g. seborrhoeic dermatitis, rosacea, erythema or blepharitis. Demodex diagnostics were performed at the RenoMed medical clinic in Warsaw (Poland).

To determine the presence of Demodex mites, samples were collected from the eyebrows and eyelashes, and a scraping was taken of the skin from the face. Eyelash samples were collected using sterile tweezers, three from each eye, and the face scrapings were collected using disposable sterile scalpel blades (size 10). The obtained material was placed on a glass slide and covered with a few drops of Hoyer’s solution as described by Cielecka et al. (2009). The preparation was examined under a light microscope at 100-400x magnification. A positive result was indicated by the presence of adult or juvenile Demodex specimens, or their eggs, in the tested material. In patients with a positive result, after obtaining prior consent, the facial skin lesions visible in the course of demodicosis were photographically documented.

The patients were qualified for aesthetic procedures after the completion of antiparasitic treatment depending on the following:

Results

Characteristics of the study group

The study group consisted of 100 patients (81 women and 19 men). The mean age in the studied group was 45.2 ± 12.2 years (range 25–81 years) for the female patients, and 44.8 ± 13.8 years (range 18–72 years) for the male patients.

Demodex folliculorum infestation was noted in 44% (44/100) of the examined patients, the majority of whom, i.e. 65.9% (29/44), were women. No significant difference in the prevalence of Demodex infestation was noted between the sexes, i.e. women 35.8% (29/81), men 78.9% (15/19); (χ2 = 1.31, p = 0.2528; Pearson’s chi-square test). The prevalence of Demodex infestation significantly increased with age (χ2 = 6.0; p = 0.0498); the greatest percentage of infested was noted in patients aged over 50 years, i.e. 38.6% (17/44).

In 30 patients out of 44 infected, antiparasitic treatment gave satisfactory results both in elimination of Demodex mites and the skin condition after treatment. Antiparasitic treatment applied was based on 1% ivermectin cream in case of skin demodicosis and on 0.5–2.5% metronidazole preparations in case of ocular demodicosis. To complement the therapy, eyelid and skin hygiene preparations based on tea tree oil (TTO), Spanish sage extract or aloe were also recommended (Polish Ophthalmological Society guidelines; Li et al. 2023). In 14 patients, despite the fact that the antiparasitic treatment was successful, further therapeutic measures were necessary due to the skin condition after chronic inflammation due to Demodex mites infestation.

The most frequently-performed treatments, which resulted in improved skin condition and the restoration of a satisfactory appearance, were medical peelings, mesotherapy and botulinum toxin treatment (Table 1). The patients infested with Demodex required long-term treatment which, depending on the individual characteristics of the patient, lasted from 3 to 12 months. The longest treatment periods were needed by patients in whom Demodex was detected in more than one preparation, and in cases where more than five mites were detected in a single preparation. After antiparasitic treatment, the patient underwent a one-month observation period during which they were monitored before starting any aesthetic treatments. Case 1

A 44-year-old female patient presented for consultation due to exacerbation of facial skin lesions that had continued for several weeks. Physical examination revealed erythematous papules and pustules, located mainly in the central part of the face (Fig. 2A). Female patient, 44 years old, with chronic skin inflammation and a positive test result for Demodex sp.; photo before treatment (A1 right cheek, A2 left cheek); patient after antiparasitic treatment and mesotherapy with non-cross-linked hyaluronic acid (B1 right cheek, B2 left cheek)

Demodex sp. was confirmed in all sample preparations, viz. eyebrow hairs (one adult), eyelashes (one egg) and epidermal scrapings from the nasolabial folds (nine adults).

The patient was offered the following treatment: prescription facewash with 0,5% metronidazole (Metronidazoli 0,5, Aquae Calcis ad 100) twice a day for 7 to 14 days, 1% ivermectin face cream, applied once a day for at least three months, eyelid skin hygiene wipes, prescription ointment with metronidazole (0.2% metronidazole, 1.0 glycerine, white petrolatum) (Juchnowicz 2016) twice a day for six weeks. It was also recommended that the patient use a cream with high sun protection, and refrain from using make-up for the duration of the treatment; she should also replace her cosmetics and make-up accessories, such as brushes, mascara, powders and fluids. The patient was referred for an ophthalmological and dermatological consultation to provide more comprehensive specialist care.

During the first follow-up examination, after four weeks, the patient was found to have a reduced number of inflammatory papules and pustules. Also, samples taken from the three facial areas were found to be negative for Demodex sp. based on microscope examination.

The next follow-up took place eight weeks later. During this visit, further improvement in the appearance of the facial skin was observed. As the skin around the eyes and cheeks was dry, the patient was offered three mesotherapy treatments consisting of non-cross-linked hyaluronic acid 10 mg/ml, one treatment every three weeks. The treatment yielded improved facial skin hydration (Fig. 2B). The patient continued to use 1% ivermectin cream for the next four weeks.

Discussion

Demodex mites may play a role in the pathogenesis of seborrhoeic skin lesions (Rufli and Mumcuoglu 1981; Gothe 1989; Karincaoglu et al. 2009; Forton 2012). Cutaneous demodicosis typically manifests as papules, vesicles or pustules, or as ocular demodicosis, i.e. redness of the conjunctiva and eyelids, peeling of the eyelid epidermis, tearing of the eyes, burning, itching and loss of eyelashes. In some patients with demodicosis, the skin becomes dry or desiccated. Redness and characteristic scaling may occur, or red spots and eczema (Erdal and Albayrak 2022; Silny and Czarnecka-Operacz 2004; Paichitrojjana and Chalermchai 2024).

In our study group, the majority of patients were women (81%). The number of procedures performed on men continues to grow, and minimally invasive procedures dominate like botulinum toxin or hyaluronic acid. According to the latest study, they will constitute 14.5% of the total (Triana et al. 2024). According to Matera et al. (2018), the most important factors that make men interested in cosmetic procedures are peer influence, dissatisfaction with body fat, and social comparison. However, due to the smaller number of men using aesthetic treatments, there is still a lack of information on the acceptance, motivation and obstacles that men face in this topic (Buhiliga et al. 2024).

In this study, patients with ocular demodicosis were treated with a prescription preparation with 2% metronidazole for approximately 5–6 weeks. Due to reported dry eyes, patients were offered moisturizing and regenerating preparations usually based on dexpanthenol (2%) and hyaluronic acid (0.15%) or vitamin A (250 IU/g). Sędzikowska et al. (2021b) found Demodex infestation to disrupt the tear film, which may contribute to the occurrence of dry eye syndrome.

When treating ocular and cutaneous demodicosis, patient education has been found to play a key role in optimizing treatment and reducing the risk of re-infestation. In this case, many patients were advised to change their make-up and cosmetics, as these provide a favourable environment for the mites outside the host and thus can prolong infestation. Sędzikowska et al. (2021a) report that Demodex mites can survive in cosmetics with an oily consistency; for example, they can remain viable in mascara for a mean period of 24.1 h, and lipstick for 38.5 h (max. 56 and 260 h, respectively). Although the effect of education on the prevalence of Demodex sp. infestation is unknown, health education has been found to reduce the incidence of parasitic diseases caused by other permanent parasites of human skin, such as scabies (Reid and Thorne 1990; Gezmu et al. 2020).

In some cases, long-term inflammation may result in various aesthetic defects; these can be addressed by an individual repair program prepared by a specialist in Aesthetic Medicine. In the present study, some patients demonstrated persistent skin changes after inflammatory infiltration, particularly scars and postinflammatory hyperpigmentation. Such changes required additional support, such as fractional CO2 laser, peels, or micro-dermabrasion. These were used to lighten or remove hyperpigmentation and reduce scarring by rebuilding and regenerating damaged tissue. Hyperpigmentation also been described previously in demodicosis cases in both immunocompetent and immunodeficient patients (Al Harbi et al. 2023; Dash et al. 2023).

Our findings demonstrate that any erythema remaining after antiparasitic treatment for demodicosis, can be alleviated with a pulsed light source. In turn, telangiectasia can be effectively removed with diode laser procedures.

In the described cases, causal treatment was followed by aesthetic treatments to improve the quality of the skin or reduce any imperfections that may have occurred following Demodex sp. infestation. The most common procedures involved the use of botulinum toxin to reduce excessive sebum production (5–10 Speywood units per area) and narrow the dilated openings of the sebaceous glands, as well as mesotherapy to improve skin hydration and firmness, and medical peelings to regulate sebaceous gland function, reduce post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and improve skin tone. Less frequently-used treatments include micro-dermabrasion to reduce seborrhoea (Fąk et al. 2018) and reduce scarring (Karimipour et al. 2010), or a diode laser to reduce facial telangiectasia (Mohammed et al. 2020) in addition, a fractional CO2 laser can be used to reduce post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, smooth the skin, shrink the dilated openings of the sebaceous glands and reduce scars (Borzęcki and Kwaśny 2022) and a pulsed light source was employed to reduce erythema (Tirico et al. 2020).

When used as part of the Aesthetic Medicine armamentarium, botulinum toxin type A (BTX-A) is used to treat dermatological conditions such as hyperhidrosis, flushing, seborrhoeic dermatitis and dilated sebaceous glands, localized skin itching and hypertrophic scars (Sterodimas et al. 2003; Bansal et al. 2006; Dayan et al. 2012; Friedman et al. 2019). Botulinum toxin type A inhibits the release of inflammatory mediators, such as substance P and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), and reduces the expression of transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV-1), whose activation may affect the release of inflammatory mediators (Lucioni et al. 2008; Fan et al. 2017). Overall, treatment was found to reduce local subclinical inflammation and erythema (de Almeida et al. 2013; Khan et al. 2013; Luque et al. 2021; Hanna et al. 2022).

BTX-A has been found to have beneficial effects, including reducing seborrhea, improving skin structure and reducing enlarged sebaceous gland openings (Ahmed El Attar and Nofal 2021). A single treatment with BTX-A improved skin structure and reduced sebum secretion in 87.2% of patients; the mean reduction in sebaceous gland orifice size was 3.7 points on a four-point scale, and mean patient satisfaction was 4.7 points, measured on a five-point Likert scale (Ahmed El Attar and Nofal 2021).

Neodymium-YAG and yellow 577 nm lasers have been tested as treatments for demodicosis. However, while they have been found to reduce the number of Demodex mites in the skin, the treatment did not guarantee long-term improvement of skin condition (Yalici-Armagan and Elcin 2020; Altunisik et al. 2021; Temiz et al. 2022). As such, conventional pharmacological therapy appears to be an effective option. In some cases, a higher number of mites in the skin is associated with folliculitis. This is characterized by, among others, hair loss, erythema, pustules and scaly skin, and neutrophilic and/or the presence of mononuclear folliculitis with sporadic granulomas in the histological preparation (Helou et al. 2016; Altemir et al. 2022). In some patients with ocular demodicosis, treatment resulted in eyelash weakness and thinning. This problem also occasionally affected eyebrow hair, resulting in an unfavourable aesthetic effect in a very visible area (Sędzikowska et al. 2016; Harrar et al. 2018; Rivera Pérez de Rada et al. 2021).

Both primary and secondary demodicosis (Akilov et al. 2005; Álvarez-Salafranca et al. 2017; Przydatek-Tyrajska et al. 2021) may present as U- or T-shaped lesions on the skin, or may have a diffuse form (Yun et al. 2017) in which skin lesions cover various parts of the face. Therefore, when demodicosis is suspected, it is advisable to collect samples from various areas, such as the eyebrows, eyelashes, the skin of the nasolabial folds and the forehead or cheeks, if skin lesions are present (Porta Guardia 2015; Dzika et al. 2020).

Some ophthalmic patients have experienced simultaneous infestation of the pilosebaceous units and eyelash follicles (Huang et al. 2024). In these cases, combining 1% ivermectin cream with a TTO preparation as ophthalmic treatment eliminated mites from the eyelashes in 76% of treated patients, compared to only 16% of those who received ophthalmic treatment alone (Huang et al. 2024). In the present study, treatment was performed according to the 2017 recommendations of the Polish Ophthalmological Society, according to which, ocular and cutaneous demodicosis should be treated simultaneously to prevent re-infestation. This approach was also previously found to yield better therapeutic effects when treating ocular lesions (Koo et al. 2012).

Studies have identified Demodex mites in 71.4% of nurses taking care of patients in hospital settings (Firat et al. 2010), and in 30 out of 75 (40%) tested health care workers, who were aware of the occupational risk associated with the transmission of aetiological factors of infectious diseases (Wesołowska et al. 2014). As demonstrated by Palopoli et al. (2015), Demodex mite transmission is facilitated by close physical contact between individuals. Therefore, working in close contact with an infested patient may increase the risk of infestation in cases where hygiene standards are not followed. Although the main route of transmission of Demodex spp. is through direct contact, it is likely that the eggs are also spread in population centres, including medical facilities, with dust (Czepita et al. 2007). In the present study, Demodex infestation was found in 44% of the examined patients (44/100), which may suggest that the Aesthetic Medicine specialist is at significant risk of infestation. Our findings hence confirm the need to expand knowledge regarding the prevention of parasitic skin diseases in this area.

Study limitations: Research should be continued on a larger group of patients of both sexes. The therapeutic effects of specific methods alone as well as their combinations should be verified. Therefore, the study should be continued so that in the future we can offer patients even more effective treatment that also takes into account the aspect of skin regeneration and aesthetics.

Conclusion

Testing for Demodex spp. can be an important complement to the medical interview during Aesthetic Medicine treatments. It can also support the implementation of antiparasitic treatment and the subsequent use of aesthetic treatments tailored to the skin condition. In cases where Demodex spp. infestation has impaired the condition of the skin, supplementing antiparasitic treatments with Aesthetic Medicine can help achieve long-lasting aesthetic benefits and improve patient satisfaction. Due to the prevalence of Demodex folliculorum observed in the patients, and the close contact required during treatment, medical professionals should be aware of the potential risk of Demodex infestation occurring through occupational exposure. The most effective preventive measure remains education, for both the doctor and the patients themselves.
