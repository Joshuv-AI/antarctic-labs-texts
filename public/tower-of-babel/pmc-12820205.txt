Transparent and dynamic network-based risk assessment for development of hepatic encephalopathy
Authors: Zixing Wang, Suzhen Jiang, Feng Liu, Xiao Xiao Wang, Guangchuan Wang, Rui Huang, Xiangsha Kong, Ran Fei, Xiaohe Li, Jinming Cao, Shuo Yang, Nan Wu, Dongbo Chen, Shaoping She, Wei Han, Yaoda Hu, Xin Wen, Tianyi Xue, Guangjun Song, Huiying Rao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatic encephalopathy is a debilitating and resource-intensive complication of cirrhosis, with high prevalence, frequent hospitalizations, and poor prognosis. Precise and dynamic identification of at-risk individuals remains a major clinical challenge.MethodsCombining expert knowledge with data-driven methodologies, we proposed a network-based risk assessment model by analyzing the inter-connections between cirrhotic complications and using routinely available blood test results. The model was developed (n = 2789) and validated (n = 698) in multi-etiology cirrhosis cohorts.ResultsHere we show hepatic encephalopathy as a pivotal nexus in the cirrhotic complication cascade. The presence of ascites (adjusted relative risk: 4.8), gastroesophageal varices (3.6), peritonitis (2.2), hepatorenal syndrome (2.1), gastroesophageal variceal hemorrhage (1.9) and hepatocellular carcinoma (1.5) is related with subsequent hepatic encephalopathy; all p < 0.05. Nine of 980 tests (ammonia, international normalized ratio, red cell distribution width standard deviation, fibrinogen, triglycerides, mean corpuscular hemoglobin, absolute neutrophil count, sodium, and total CO2) are selected through a stringent process encompassing clinical utility, expert agreement, risk direction clarity, and information non-redundancy. The network-based model accurately predicts hepatic encephalopathy risk in both cross-sectional and longitudinal settings, AUROC = 0.926 (95% confidence interval: 0.882-0.962) and 0.962 (0.933-0.984), respectively. It is compatible with missing data (i.e., using partially observed information) and offers flexible clinical implementation through a simple tool-free method and smart device integration. Clinical utility spans patient risk stratification, dynamic risk monitoring, optimized screening, and hepatic encephalopathy-related complication prevention.ConclusionsThis network-based approach provides transparent, precise and dynamic risk assessment in hepatic encephalopathy management, with potential to improve clinical outcomes in cirrhotic patients.Subject terms: Liver cirrhosis, Epidemiology

Introduction

Hepatic encephalopathy (HE) is the leading cause of hospital readmission in liver cirrhosis patients and a key factor in liver transplantation prioritization1,2. Its clinical presentation ranges from subtle cognitive impairments to complete loss of consciousness, with varying courses that can be episodic, recurrent, or persistent3. Epidemiological surveys show that minimal HE affects up to 80% of cirrhosis patients4, yet it remains largely undetected in routine care5,6. As HE progresses to overt encephalopathy, which occurs in one-third to one-half of patients annually7, it severely impairs mental, physical and social functioning, imposing significant socioeconomic burdens that disproportionately affect working-class families8. The reported median survival time after HE onset is less than one year even in developed countries9.

The twisting course of illness and poor prognosis of HE underscore the need for early risk assessment10. However, predicting the onset of HE remains a significant challenge11, primarily due to its multifactorial pathogenesis that involves intricate interactions between multiple organs and biological systems7,12. Impaired ammonia metabolism and systemic inflammation are considered central contributors12,13, yet the precise mechanisms driving and extending beyond these processes remain actively under investigation7,14. Previous attempts to link HE to alterations in a single metabolic or signaling pathway have thus far been inconclusive7,15. As a result, the identification of a reliable, singular biomarker for HE remains elusive16.

Clinically, HE is influenced by a broad range of predisposing and precipitating factors. These include systemic disturbances such as malnutrition and electrolyte imbalances17,18, as well as cirrhotic complications like ascites, peritonitis, gastrointestinal bleeding, and hepatorenal syndrome19,20. While these triggers are relatively easy to identify, their specific roles in HE onset have not been rigorously explored7,21. Furthermore, HE rarely develops in isolation during its course, with numerous comorbidities and cirrhotic complications often coexisting10, thereby adding layers of complexity to dynamic risk assessment. The interactions among these factors within the broader HE pathophysiological network remain poorly understood.

In this study we explore the inter-connections among cirrhotic complications and integrate real-world clinical data with expert insights on HE through network analysis, applied to both pre- and post-HE risk assessment. We develop a network-based tool for forecasting HE risk, which enables the timely selection of screening candidates for HE and supports dynamic risk assessments. Its transparency and dynamism offer an adaptive framework for managing this complex disease, providing essential insights for personalized care of cirrhotic patients at risk of HE.

Methods

Patient Selection

This study was based on a dynamic cohort in Beijing, China. Inpatients or outpatients, aged 18-85 years old, diagnosed with liver cirrhosis from January 1, 2010 to December 31, 2023, were eligible for inclusion. The diagnosis of cirrhosis was based on histology, characteristic imaging findings (including CT, MRI, and ultrasound), or a combination of clinical and laboratory features, as per guidelines22. Patients were excluded if they had previously experienced episode(s) of HE, or had established neurological or psychiatric disorders, including intracranial diseases, such as subarachnoid or epidural hemorrhage, cerebral infarction, brain tumors, intracranial infections, and epilepsy; other metabolic encephalopathies, including ketoacidosis, hypoglycemia, and hyponatremia; Wernicke’s encephalopathy, resulting from thiamine deficiency; and alcohol-related encephalopathy.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University People’s Hospital (No. 2024PHB408-001). A waiver of the need for informed consent was granted, as only anonymized data were used for analysis.

Clinical Outcomes

Clinical follow-up of the patients extended until April 2024. The primary outcome was onset of overt HE (ICD-10 code: K72.903). The diagnosis criteria included the presence of underlying severe liver disease, identifiable neuropsychiatric symptoms, and signs, while excluding other conditions that may cause neuropsychiatric abnormalities, as per guidelines42,43. In sensitivity analyses, we further explored two alternative definitions: (1) a stricter definition, which excludes patients diagnosed with HE only in an outpatient setting; and (2) a more general definition, which includes patients with newly diagnosed neuropsychiatric disorders after the diagnosis of cirrhosis but before the diagnosis of HE. Impacts of the two alternative definitions were minimal (a decrease of 25 cases with the stricter definition and an addition of 44 cases with the more general definition). Other cirrhotic complications were considered as secondary outcomes and were confirmed by image, pathology, or laboratory findings. They included, in alphabetical order, ascites (ICD-10 code: K74.653), gastroesophageal variceal hemorrhage (GEVH, I85.000×001, I86.400×011, I86.800×014), gastroesophageal varices (GEV) (GEV, I85.901, I86.401), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC; C22.001), hepatorenal syndrome (HRS, K76.701, 702), peritonitis (K65.005-009, 013, 051, 808-809), and sepsis (A40, A41).

Classification of Cirrhosis Etiology

We classified the patients according to the primary etiology of cirrhosis, ranked in order of significance, as follows: HBV, HCV, alcohol, autoimmune, drug-induced, metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), hereditary (Wilson’s disease, hemochromatosis, hepatic amyloidosis, hereditary hyperbilirubinemia, α1-antitrypsin deficiency, hepatic porphyrias, glycogen storage diseases, galactosemia, and tyrosinemia), and other causes (combined as one group) including parasitic infections, circulatory disturbances (Budd-Chiari syndrome and right heart failure) and cryptogenic causes.

Laboratory variable selection

We extracted 980 blood test candidates from the institute-based laboratory information system for risk signaling of HE. We implemented a random forest model to analyze the significance of laboratory tests. This choice was driven by the model’s ability to effectively capture nonlinear relationships and complex interactions among variables, making it well-suited for variable selection for network analysis. The model was configured with 500 decision trees and standard hyperparameters, including a maximum depth of 30, a minimum node size of 1, and Gini impurity as the splitting criterion. To effectively illustrate the relevance of each variable, we calculated the mean decrease Gini scores. These scores were then ranked to pinpoint the most influential variables (mean decrease Gini score>6.5), thereby enhancing feature selection for the subsequent development of a Bayesian network. We utilized the XGBoost algorithm to explore interactions among the selected laboratory tests. The model was tuned with key hyperparameters, including a learning rate of 0.05, a maximum depth of 7, and a subsample rate of 0.6, aiming to balance model complexity and prevent overfitting. The XGBoost model was trained for 100 rounds, with early stopping after 8 rounds of no improvement. This approach further illuminated the complex interactions between risk signals, offering deeper insights into HE onset.

These laboratory candidate variables also underwent a stringent selection process encompassing clinical utility, expert agreement, risk direction clarity, and information non-redundancy. Clinical utility was defined as indicators tested in over one-third of the cirrhosis patients, and with an average of three tests per individual during follow-up. Expert agreement required consensus from three hepatologists on the predictive value of the test for HE. Risk direction clarity was determined by indicators whose alterations (either elevated or decreased) correlated with more than a 20% risk of HE. Lastly, information non-redundancy mandated that the Spearman correlation coefficients between selected indicators be less than 0.60, leading to the retention of only one among highly correlated candidates based on expert agreement.

Network Construction

Prior to network construction, several data preprocessing steps were implemented. Laboratory indicators were categorized according to standardized normal reference values, with binary classification based on primary risk direction (e.g., ammonia levels were classified as elevated or non-elevated). To ensure data authenticity, missing laboratory results were not imputed but instead categorized as “Unknown,” with status updates based on available follow-up data. Comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and CVD were classified into “present” and “not present” categories based on clinical diagnoses. Additionally, an optimal age threshold of 75 years was identified for discriminating HE risk, with individuals categorized as either “aged” or “non-aged.” These preprocessing steps aimed to enhance the network’s operability while ensuring clarity and consistency in data interpretation.

The study cohort was divided into training (n = 2789) and validation (n = 698) sets using complete randomization (4:1 ratio) for model development and evaluation. The initial structure of the network was developed using the Max-Min Hill-Climbing algorithm applied to the training set, integrating expert-defined whitelists and blacklists. These constraints were essential for ensuring clinical applicability and minimizing the risk of spurious or irrelevant findings. To evaluate the stability of the learned structure, we implemented a bootstrapping technique, generating 1000 samples to assess the strength and direction of the edges. We filtered these edges to retain only those with a strength greater than 0.75 and a directional score of at least 0.5.

The final Bayesian network structure was constructed using an averaged network approach based on the filtered bootstrapped results and validated by two hepatologists through an interactive layout, enhancing the interpretation of relationships among clinical variables. Subsequently, we fitted the Bayesian network using the Maximum Likelihood Estimation method on the training dataset. This network was then utilized for probabilistic queries regarding the primary and secondary outcomes for risk assessment purposes.

Calculation of MELD and BABS scores

We identified two risk scoring systems for liver cirrhosis and HE that could be evaluated within this cohort, which were then directly compared to the network assessment results. The MELD score (a widely used clinical tool for assessing the severity of the hepatic disease and prognosis23) was calculated using the standard formula: MELD = 3.78 × ln [serum bilirubin (mg/dL)] + 11.2 × ln [international normalized ratio (INR)] + 9.57 × ln[serum creatinine (mg/dL)] + 6.43. For the BABS score (a HE-pertinent risk assessment tool developed in a population-based cohort of patients with cirrhosis24), the following parameters were employed: total bilirubin (mg/dL), categorized into 8 discrete levels (<0.5, 0.5-, 1-,1.5-, 2-, 2.5-, 3-, 4-), albumin (g/dL), divided into 6 discrete levels (<2, 2-, 2.5-, 3-, 3.5-, 4-), and medication use of beta-blockers and statins (each with 2 levels, yes or no). The scoring system assigned weightings for each parameter that differ between baseline and longitudinal models24. Additionally, these scores were standardized to a 0–1 scale for decision curve analysis and comparison.

Statistics and reproducibility

The cumulative incidence of cirrhosis complications was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method to account for censored data. Using log-binomial regression, we evaluated the bidirectional risk relationship between HE and other complications, calculating relative risk adjusted for age, sex, and cirrhosis etiology. We did not adjust for additional complications due to their collinear nature and temporal non-alignment. We employed both conventional receiver operating curve (ROC) analysis and time-dependent ROC analysis to evaluate the model’s predictive performance for any time point (regardless of HE occurrence time) and specific time points of interest (short-term: 3-month and 6-month; long-term: 1-year, 2-year, 3-year, and 5-year). The confidence intervals for AUC values were estimated using 1000 bootstrap resamples. To assess the robustness of our model against missing laboratory data in real-world clinical settings, we performed computational simulations examining varying degrees of data absence (5–45%), with 1000 bootstrap replicates conducted for each missingness level to evaluate its impact on both baseline and longitudinal predictive performance.

Results

HE Amidst Cirrhotic complications

The baseline clinical characteristics of the cirrhosis cohort (n = 3478) are presented in Supplementary Table 1. At enrollment, the prevalence of cirrhotic complications vary substantially, ranging from 0.1% (95% CI: 0.05–0.3%) for sepsis to 28.4% (26.9–30.0%) for GEV. During a maximum follow-up period of 14 years (median [IQR]: 2.62 [0.93–7.63] years), 2215 patients (63.5%) had at least one major complication. The 1-year cumulative incidence, ordered by frequency, were: GEV: 28.8% (27.0–30.6%), ascites: 17.5% (16.0–19.2%), HE: 10.2% (9.3–11.3%), spontaneous bacterial peritonitis: 5.0% (4.3–5.8%), HCC: 4.5% (3.8–5.3%), GEVH: 2.4% (1.9–2.9%), HRS: 2.1% (1.6–2.6%), sepsis: 0.7% (0.4–1.0%). Additional incidence estimates for shorter (3-month, 6-month) and longer-term (2-year, 3-year, 5-year) follow-up periods are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Notably, there is a high prevalence of overlap among these complications, with 44.1% of patients presenting two or more types. This clustering is illustrated in Fig. 1a, where HE is situated in the most intersected region. To be specific, among 398 (11.4%) patients who experienced HE, the proportion of overlap with other complications is highest for GEV (91.2%) and ascites (89.2%), followed by peritonitis (37.2%), HCC (21.9%), GEVH (15.8%), HRS (14.1%), and sepsis (5.0%). Supplementary Fig. 1 provides a more detailed view of the overlapping spectrum of cirrhotic complications.

Crucially, these complications do not manifest simultaneously. The frequency of each complication being the initial one is depicted in Fig. 1b. Although HE typically develops after other complications (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Table 3), it is the most common initial decompensating event in nearly half of patients with autoimmune (46.8%) and MAFLD-related cirrhosis (45.5%), and about one-third of those with alcohol-related cirrhosis (36.4%) (Supplementary Table 4). The onset of HE often coincides with additional complications or serves as an intermediary between them (Fig. 1c). These cascadic inter-connections strongly suggest the presence of a “domino” effect. In particular, the presence of ascites (adjusted relative risk, aRR: 4.8), GEV (aRR: 3.6), peritonitis (aRR: 2.2), HRS (aRR: 2.1), GEVH (aRR: 1.9) and HCC (aRR: 1.5) is related with heightened risk of subsequent HE (Fig. 1d). Conversely, the onset of HE is related with increased risk of developing sepsis (aRR: 5.7), HRS (aRR: 5.1), GEVH (aRR: 3.4), peritonitis (aRR: 2.7), GEV (aRR: 1.4) and ascites (aRR: 1.2); all p < 0.05 (Fig. 1e). This interplay highlights the crucial role of HE within the multifaceted clinical landscape.

Risk signaling of HE by laboratory tests

Nine out of 980 tests meet all the variable selection criteria, ranked by their variable importance according to the random forest model: ammonia, prothrombin international normalized ratio (INR), red cell distribution width standard deviation (RDW-SD), fibrinogen, triglycerides, mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), absolute neutrophil count (NEUT), sodium, and total carbon dioxide (TCO2). Supplementary Data 1 summarizes the physiological, and hepatic and neurological pathological significance of these indicators, along with their clinical relevance with HE. In brief, these markers provide insights into the extent of liver dysfunction and systemic disturbances, providing biological signals of HE risk through abnormalities in ammonia metabolism, inflammation, coagulation, electrolyte balance, and acid-base regulation.

Notably, the signal value differences between patients experiencing HE and no HE widen after baseline, leading to a general improvement in predictive performance in longitudinal follow-up (Table 1). For instance, the area under ROC (AUC) of ammonia increases from 0.71 to 0.77, while the INR rises from 0.68 to 0.72, highlighting the importance of dynamic monitoring of these risk signals. However, none of these indicators, when assessed in isolation, is adequate for reliably predicting the risk of HE (maximum AUC < 0.80). Furthermore, the mechanisms involved are interrelated, as evidenced by the interactions between these signals (Supplementary Fig. 2). For instance, both ammonia and INR levels independently affect HE, but they also exhibit a significant interaction at both baseline and longitudinal assessments. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of adopting a comprehensive approach to understanding HE development and highlight the need for precise predictive tools to manage this complex condition.

Network risk prediction of HE

The network is constructed to account for the cascading effect of cirrhotic complications and the multifaceted mechanisms underlying HE. Its construction integrates expert knowledge with data-driven insights, which is briefly illustrated in Fig. 2a. To be specific, the network maps the probabilistic connections (edges) between various clinical inputs (nodes), combining expert-established associations with the frequency and intensity of data connections derived from a large number of repeated simulation experiments yielding stable results. The network thus provides visualization of the independence pathways inclusive of the complication sequences (Fig. 2b).

The network comprises 31 nodes, including three basic factors (age, sex, etiology of cirrhosis), four comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, cardiovascular disease [CVD]), nine laboratory findings (hyperammonemia, neutrophilia, elevated INR, MCH, and RDW-SD, hyponatremia, hypocapnia, hypofibrinogenemia, and hypotriglyceridemia), and eight cirrhotic complications (differentiating those occurring before and after HE). A total of 42 edges connect these nodes, forming a comprehensive network centered on HE. The probabilities of strength and direction are presented in the Supplementary Table 5.

In both training and validation sets, the network exhibits strong predictive capacity for HE onset at any future time (Fig. 3) and within selected time points of interest (3 m, 6 m, 1 y, 2 y, 3 y and 5 y; Supplementary Table 6). In head-to-head comparisons with the MELD score and the BABS score, AUCs (validation set) at baseline are 0.926 (network) versus 0.670 (MELD) and 0.745 (BABS); both p < 0.001. More importantly, the predictive accuracy improves as subsequent test results becomes available, yielding AUCs (validation set) of 0.962 (network) versus 0.707 (MELD) and 0.734 (BABS); both p < 0.001 (Fig. 3b). This highlights the clinical value of the network as a tool for precise and dynamic risk assessment of HE. At a given risk threshold of 11.4% (the average HE risk for all patients), the sensitivity and specificity of the network (validation set) are 0.864 and 0.982, respectively, while in longitudinal follow-up, they are 0.932 and 0.938, respectively. The network’s predictive capacity offers greater net benefits over MELD and BABS at each risk threshold, making it a valuable tool for enhancing risk-based decision-making. (Fig. 3d).

The network’s performance is consistently high regarding alternative definitions of outcomes and in diverse age groups and sexes (Supplementary Table 7), indicating a robust generalizability in inpatient/outpatient settings and across demographics. However, the etiology of cirrhosis emerged as a significant factor influencing predictive accuracy. Notably, for patients with Hepatitis B Virus (HBV)-related, drug-induced, and autoimmune cirrhosis, the network achieves a prediction accuracy of 100% at baseline. In contrast, longitudinal follow-up shows a marked improvement in prediction accuracy for patients with alcohol-related cirrhosis, with the AUC rising from 0.860 at baseline to 0.976. Similarly, in patients with HCV-related cirrhosis, AUC improves from 0.819 to 0.894, reflecting the network’s evolving capacity to track disease progression over time. Supplementary Table 8 examines the variations in abnormal blood test results across different cirrhosis etiologies, which may help explain the observed differences in predictive performance. Moreover, we observed progressive declines in model accuracy with increasing proportions of missing laboratory data in both baseline and longitudinal scenarios (Supplementary Fig. 3), highlighting the need for complete clinical data collection to preserve optimal predictive performance.

Application of the network in clinical context

A unique characteristic of the network is its dynamism, meaning it can assess HE risk using partially observed information (compatible with missing data) and be updated as new test results and prior complications become available. This feature provides clinical flexibility in balancing accuracy and simplicity based on resource availability11. In this section, we explore the translation value of the network into clinical practice, for tool-free use during patient admission or more precisely by implementing user-friendly technology such as computers and smartphones.

For tool-free usage, we develop a key-nodes approach, that is, using the key nodes identified from the network for convenient risk assessment for HE (Fig. 4a). The key nodes are determined through probabilistic reasoning via the network and include advanced age (≥75 years), hyponatremia, hyperammonemia, elevated INR and elevated RDW-SD. Notably, the risk of HE escalates almost linearly with the number of the key nodes, from 6.5% (0 nodes), 11.3% (1 node), 20.4% (2 nodes) to 33.1% (3 or more nodes). Through visual scoring (Supplementary Fig. 4), the key nodes can also be used for rapid bedside risk assessment.

To illustrate the application of computer- and smartphone-assisted monitoring, consider the case of a 60-year-old female patient with ascites, diagnosed with primary biliary cholangitis and cirrhosis. Initial laboratory results reveal elevated INR and hypofibrinogenemia, prompting the predictive network to assess her risk of HE at 7.7%. At a follow-up visit 120 days later, while fibrinogen levels normalized, there was decreased TCO2 and sodium levels, coupled with an increase in ammonia levels, though other clinical parameters remained unchanged. As a result, the network recalibrates her HE risk to 13.2%, a value notably higher than the average risk for patients (Fig. 4b), corroborating the diagnosis of HE. This reevaluation triggers an updated HE status and a comprehensive reassessment of other cirrhotic complications, revealing an increased risk for GEV, GEVH, and HRS relative to baseline averages (Fig. 4c). The identification of GEV at 126 days, followed by the development of HRS at 448 days, highlights the potential role of the network in guiding timely preventive and therapeutic interventions.

The network can also be utilized for planning of the timing of screening for HE. This is achieved by establishing two risk thresholds for two future time points of interest (3 months and 12 months): those assessed by the network to have a risk of developing HE exceeding 12.5% within the next three months are recommended for immediate testing; conversely, patients with a risk of 2.5% or less over the next twelve months are advised to undergo annual screening. For the remaining patients, screening within three months is suggested. As illustrated in Fig. 4d, this strategy of timing screening based on detailed risk assessment has demonstrated favorable outcomes both in terms of overall performance and individual case applications.

Disucssion

HE is a typical condition involving interactions across multiple systems. Our interdisciplinary team, which brings together expertise in basic sciences, clinical medicine, and data science, is uniquely equipped to carry out this study, supported by a vast and continuously growing patient population with cirrhosis in China25. This large cohort enables us to undertake one of the most comprehensive investigations into the risk assessment of HE.

Our network model demonstrates superior predictive accuracy compared to existing predictive tools, such as the MELD score24, BABS score25, and others4,26. A key innovation in our approach lies in the integration of inter-connected complications, which, as our findings suggest, provide enhanced predictive value for HE risk. Furthermore, we emphasize model transparency, clearly illustrating the network of clinical factors and their interrelationships. This not only incorporates HE as a critical element within a high-dimensional, interactive network—thereby improving predictive accuracy through probabilistic constraints on each individual factor—but also offers insights into the underlying mechanisms.

In our cohort, 20% of the patients experience a duration of > 1 year from inclusion to the diagnosis of HE. This highlights that while our model demonstrates high predictive accuracy, we must temper our expectations regarding its diagnostic value. Effective translation of our findings hinges on two interrelated factors: the risk of misdiagnosis and the potential benefits of early diagnosis. Given that minimal HE is prevalent in patients with cirrhosis27,28, regular screening and close monitoring are essential, which is a critical component of implementing our network in clinical practice. Importantly, while current guidelines recommend regular screening for minimal HE29, routine implementation is not common in China due to variability in screening tool performance, limited human and time resources for implementation, and uncertainty regarding implementation pathways30,31. Our tool is positioned to address this gap in several ways: first, its high predictive accuracy can help identify patients who require screening; second, it allows for a more thorough assessment and monitoring of those who test negative, thus mitigating the limitations of existing screening tools; and finally, it can inform tailored screening frequencies, moving away from a one-size-fits-all approach to a more precise and personalized patient management strategy.

The results concerning patients with liver cirrhosis of varying etiologies warrant closer attention. In our cohort, HE frequently emerges as the first decompensating event in patients with autoimmune, MAFLD, and alcohol-related cirrhosis, a pattern also observed in a dedicated cohort of MAFLD patients32. These findings are particularly relevant in the context of the shifting spectrum of liver disease, especially with the global goal to eliminate viral hepatitis as a public health threat by 203033. The high prevalence of alcohol-related liver disease in developed countries, coupled with the globally expanding burden of other non-virus liver etiologies and cirrhosis8,34, suggests that HE will play an increasingly significant role in the identification, prognosis, and management of decompensated cirrhosis. Moreover, while certain etiologic groups—such as HBV, autoimmune, and drug-induced cirrhosis—demonstrated strong predictive value for HE risk at baseline, alcohol-related cirrhosis exhibited substantial improvement during follow-up. In contrast, HCV-related cirrhosis showed relatively lower predictive performance. These findings underscore the variable reliability of HE risk assessment based on specific etiologies and application scenarios. In particular, we found that patients with HCV and alcohol-related cirrhosis demonstrated complex patterns of comorbidities and complications, emphasizing need for more tailored approaches in patient evaluation and management35.

In our study, we deliberately constructed the network model based solely on patients’ intrinsic clinical status, without incorporating prophylactic medications (e.g., lactulose, rifaximin, L-ornithine-L-aspartate, or non-selective beta-blockers) that might alter HE risk. This decision was based on three principal considerations: First, we observed significant prescribing selectivity among physicians regarding HE prophylaxis, and the observed medication-HE risk association may be confounded by both identified and unidentified factors (Supplementary Tables 9–13); second, the temporal patterns and dosage variability of these medications would substantially complicate dynamic prediction; and third, regional variations in prescribing practices could compromise the model’s generalizability. While the complex interplay between dynamic risk assessment and corresponding therapeutic decision-making presents significant challenges, investigating this relationship (such as developing dynamic regime schemes via reinforcement learning) holds considerable promise for optimizing patient outcomes and should be regarded as a key future research direction.

The network is open for adaptation and improvement in several key areas. First, while our primary focus is on HE in patients with cirrhosis, HE also arises in contexts such as acute liver failure, acute-on-chronic liver failure, spontaneous or artificial portal shunts, and/or pediatric patients36–39, where the underlying mechanisms may vary substantially. Investigating the applicability of our network to these scenarios presents a compelling opportunity for future research. Second, the variables integrated into our network do not encompass all known and unknown risk factors associated with HE. The potential of emerging biomarkers and imaging modalities for enhancing our understanding of HE merits additional investigation22,40. Third, hepatitis B is the leading cause of cirrhosis in China, accounting for 39% of our cohort. However, the small sample sizes of specific etiologies (e.g., HCV-related, drug-induced, MAFLD, and hereditary) in the validation set compromise both the feasibility and statistical validity of the corresponding analyses. Therefore, further calibration will be essential when applying these findings to regions where other etiologies predominate. Last, we employ standardized test procedures, reference values for laboratory results. As we embark on multicenter studies, variations in these aspects could influence the accuracy of risk assessments, underscoring the necessity for validation and potential adjustments prior to broader implementation.

Conclusion

By combining expert knowledge with data-driven methodologies, we developed a network-based risk assessment tool that effectively synthesizes various complications, risk factors, and relevant clinical signals. This open framework that renders multiple clinical applications, particularly in dynamic risk assessment, optimal screening, and prevention of post-HE complications. The network model demonstrates potential to improve both clinical decision-making and personalized patient management in cirrhosis care. Broader validation across diverse ethnic and clinical populations remains necessary to establish its generalizability.
