Immune-pressure redistribution in resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade: mechanisms, biomarkers, and therapeutic design
Authors: Xiaodong Wang, Jiayi Liu, Alifujiang Hairulajiang, Junjie Wang, Qianqian Wang, ZhiHong Li, Bingwen Zou, Yeqian Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPD-1/PD-L1 blockade can produce durable tumor control, yet primary, adaptive, and acquired resistance remain common. Existing accounts often catalogue resistance by cellular compartment, obscuring the coordinated nature of tumor adaptation. Here, we introduce immune-pressure redistribution as a treatment-oriented framework that complements cancer immunoediting by asking where therapeutic immune pressure is diverted after checkpoint release. Resistance is organized into three coupled routes: transfer into tumor-intrinsic escape through antigen-presentation loss, interferon-response defects, oncogenic rewiring, and lineage plasticity; weakening through defective priming, terminal T-cell differentiation, compensatory checkpoints, metabolic constraint, and chronic cytokine signaling; and unloading into stromal, vascular, myeloid, regulatory, microbial, and systemic host compartments. We integrate clinically validated mechanisms with emerging evidence, including the temporal duality of interferon–JAK signaling, the role of tumor-draining lymph nodes in sustaining progenitor-exhausted T cells, and the limited translation of TIGIT, IDO1, TGF-β, and CSF-1R targeting. We further propose a biomarker-guided strategy that combines tumor visibility, immune-cell state, spatial architecture, systemic inflammation, and early treatment dynamics to identify the dominant resistance topology. Introduction

Antibodies targeting PD-1 or PD-L1 have transformed the treatment of several advanced malignancies, producing survival benefits across melanoma, non-small-cell lung cancer, renal-cell carcinoma, and other tumor types [1–3]. Durable tumor control, including prolonged treatment-free intervals in a subset of patients, demonstrates that PD-1 pathway blockade can restore clinically effective endogenous antitumor immunity [4, 5]. Nevertheless, clinical experience has also defined the limits of checkpoint monotherapy: many tumors fail to regress, some achieve only transient disease stabilization, and others recur after months or years of response [6–8]. These trajectories should not be conflated. Primary resistance usually reflects a baseline defect in tumor immunogenicity, T-cell priming or trafficking, or effector function, whereas acquired resistance emerges after an initial objective response or durable disease control [9, 10]. Adaptive resistance denotes treatment-induced immune engagement followed by counter-regulatory programs that attenuate or terminate the emerging antitumor response before durable tumor control is established [11, 12].

Resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade is frequently classified according to anatomical or cellular compartment. Tumor-cell alterations, T-cell dysfunction, suppressive myeloid populations, cancer-associated fibroblast-mediated exclusion, metabolic competition, and systemic host factors are consequently presented as parallel categories [9, 13, 14]. Although useful for descriptive purposes, this organization can obscure the coordinated evolution of resistance across compartments. Longitudinal studies indicate that checkpoint therapy can simultaneously reshape tumor clonal composition, antigen-presentation programs, T-cell states, and the local immune microenvironment [15, 16]. Interferon-γ illustrates this temporal and spatial interdependence. Baseline interferon-related antigen-presentation and chemokine programs are associated with response, whereas sustained interferon signaling can induce multigenic inhibitory states; under continued immune selection, defects in interferon responsiveness or antigen presentation may subsequently emerge [17–19]. Lactate exerts a similarly distributed effect by suppressing effector T- and natural killer-cell activity while supporting the metabolic fitness of intratumoral regulatory T cells and promoting suppressive macrophage polarization [20, 21]. A resistance mechanism therefore cannot be interpreted fully by identifying only the cell type in which it is detected.

We use the term immune-pressure redistribution to describe this coordinated adaptation to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. The model comprises three interconnected routes. First, tumors may transfer immune pressure into cell-intrinsic adaptations that reduce immune recognition or susceptibility, including loss of major histocompatibility complex class I, defective interferon signaling, oncogenic rewiring, and lineage plasticity [15, 17, 22]. Second, tumors may weaken immune pressure by limiting T-cell priming, maintaining terminally exhausted differentiation states, or imposing metabolic constraints on effector cells [23, 24]. Third, tumors may unload immune pressure into stromal, vascular, and suppressive myeloid circuits that exclude effector lymphocytes or blunt their function [25, 26]. Metabolic and regulatory programs, including lactate-supported regulatory T cells and suppressive macrophages, can further stabilize this ecosystem [20, 21]. These routes are neither mutually exclusive nor intended to imply a literal conservation law. Rather, they provide an operational map of where therapeutic immune activity is diverted, attenuated, or rendered ineffective, and therefore indicate which compartment is most likely to become rate limiting and therapeutically actionable.

This framework complements rather than replaces cancer immunoediting. Immunoediting describes how immune selection drives tumor-cell elimination, equilibrium, and escape over time [27–29]. Immune-pressure redistribution addresses a more treatment-oriented question: once PD-1/PD-L1 blockade releases inhibitory signaling, where is the resulting immune pressure diverted, and which compensatory compartment becomes rate limiting? This distinction has direct clinical implications because different resistance topologies are expected to require different biomarkers, combination strategies, and treatment sequences. Accordingly, mechanisms supported by prospective or interventional clinical evidence should be distinguished explicitly from those derived predominantly from retrospective associations or preclinical models.

Clinical phenotypes and the logic of immune-pressure redistribution

Primary, adaptive, and acquired resistance

For the purposes of this framework, primary resistance denotes progressive disease, or stable disease that fails to meet a prespecified durability threshold, as the best response to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade. Baseline resistance can be organized around failure of one or more non-redundant requirements: sufficient tumor antigenicity, productive T-cell priming and trafficking, and a tumor-reactive T-cell compartment capable of effector differentiation [11, 30, 31]. Low clonal neoantigen fitness and impaired HLA-I-restricted antigen presentation can limit clinical benefit from immune-checkpoint blockade [32–34]. Tumor-intrinsic WNT/β-catenin activation and STK11/LKB1-associated programs can generate non-inflamed, T-cell-poor states linked to primary resistance [22, 35], whereas TGF-β-activated stroma and suppressive myeloid programs can reinforce T-cell exclusion and attenuate the activity of PD-1/PD-L1 blockade [25, 36]. Thus, similar radiographic outcomes may arise from mechanistically distinct constraints. An antigen-presentation-deficient tumor and a T-cell-excluded tumor represent different limiting states and would therefore be expected to require different rescue strategies [17, 25, 37].

Adaptive resistance denotes treatment-induced counter-regulatory programs that emerge after measurable immune activation and attenuate the developing antitumor response [11, 12, 38]. PD-1 blockade can expand tumor-reactive CD8 T-cell populations, whereas residual tumors may upregulate alternative inhibitory receptors and broader immune-resistance programs [11, 16]. Because treatment-induced immune changes can be detected within the first weeks of therapy, radiographic nonresponse alone cannot determine whether antitumor immunity failed to initiate or was rapidly counter-regulated [39–41]. Paired baseline and early on-treatment tissue or blood profiling can therefore help distinguish failure to initiate an antitumor response from failure to sustain it.

For the purposes of this framework, acquired resistance denotes disease progression after an initial objective response or durable disease control with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade [8, 15, 17]. Paired analyses of responding and relapsing lesions have identified B2M loss, JAK1/2 defects, impaired HLA-I antigen processing, neoantigen loss, and persistently altered interferon-associated immune states [17, 42]. Acquired resistance is therefore not simply delayed primary resistance. It reflects treatment-driven evolution in which susceptible clones are eliminated and genetic or phenotypic states that were absent, rare, or clinically silent at baseline become enriched [15, 16]. Spatially distinct metastases may harbor divergent genomic and immune states, providing a biological basis for mixed progression and limiting the inferences that can be drawn from a single-lesion biopsy [43, 44].

Hyperprogressive disease should be considered separately from conventional primary resistance. It denotes disease progression accompanied by a marked acceleration in tumor growth relative to the pretreatment trajectory, although its reported incidence varies substantially according to tumor type and the operational definition applied [45–47]. Exploratory studies have associated hyperprogression with MDM2/MDM4 amplification or EGFR alterations, expansion of PD-1-positive effector regulatory T cells, and macrophage Fc/Fc-receptor interactions, but none of these candidate mechanisms has been prospectively validated for routine prediction [48, 49]. Classifying hyperprogression as a universal form of checkpoint resistance would therefore conflate a variably defined and incompletely understood growth phenotype with better-characterized patterns of primary and acquired immune escape.

How redistribution differs from immunoediting

Cancer immunoediting is fundamentally temporal: immune surveillance eliminates susceptible transformed cells, adaptive immunity can maintain residual disease in equilibrium, and immune-selected escape variants subsequently emerge [27, 28, 50]. By contrast, we define immune-pressure redistribution as a spatial and functional framework. Longitudinal tumor profiling, tumor-draining lymph-node studies, and systemic immune mapping support an integrated analysis of antitumor immunity across tumor cells, lymphoid tissues, the local microenvironment, and systemic immune compartments [16, 24, 51]. Within this model, transfer, weakening, and unloading are non-exclusive routes that may arise concurrently or sequentially and may reinforce one another.

Loss of antigen presentation exemplifies transfer. Under cytotoxic T-cell selection, tumors may undergo B2M loss or allele-specific HLA loss, reducing the presentation of immunogenic peptides and shifting the limiting step from T-cell activation to tumor-cell recognition [17, 52, 53]. Compensatory activation of alternative inhibitory pathways exemplifies weakening: antigen recognition may remain intact, yet the expansion and effector function of responding T cells become progressively constrained [16, 54]. Stromal TGF-β programs and tumor-driven emergency myelopoiesis exemplify unloading by establishing fibroblast-rich barriers and immunosuppressive myeloid compartments that restrict cytotoxic T-cell access or function [25, 36]. The framework therefore connects mechanisms that are often considered separately and makes their distinct therapeutic implications explicit.

Resistance as a coordinated and time-dependent state

The temporal dimension of redistribution is clearest when a pathway that supports immune initiation acquires suppressive functions under sustained signaling. Acute type I or type II interferon signaling can increase tumor-cell MHC-I expression, support dendritic-cell-dependent priming, and induce CXCL9/CXCL10 programs associated with T-cell recruitment [19, 55, 56]. By contrast, prolonged interferon signaling can establish multigenic inhibitory programs and epigenetic inflammatory memory in cancer cells, while sustained type I interferon signaling can suppress progenitor-like T-cell states and favor exhausted differentiation [18, 57]. High tumor glycolytic activity can deplete extracellular glucose and generate a lactate-rich, acidic microenvironment, thereby constraining the metabolism and effector function of tumor-reactive T and natural killer cells [21, 58, 59]. The same metabolic environment can favor suppressor populations: intratumoral regulatory T cells use lactate to preserve their fitness and suppressive activity, whereas tumor-derived lactate promotes an M2-like macrophage program [20, 60](Fig. 1). Loss or downregulation of classical MHC-I can reduce CD8 T-cell recognition while increasing susceptibility to missing-self natural killer-cell responses [61, 62]. Tumors that retain β2-microglobulin and non-classical HLA-I expression may counter this pressure through HLA-E–CD94/NKG2A or other NK-inhibitory pathways [63, 64].

Collectively, these observations support representing resistance as a dynamic topology of interacting bottlenecks rather than as the consequence of a single dominant lesion. The practical objective is to determine which route is currently rate limiting, whether the constraint remains reversible, and which intervention is most likely to restore productive immune pressure.

Reading the resistance topology: biomarkers that assign mechanism to intervention

Baseline markers of tumor visibility and immune competence

PD-L1 immunohistochemistry enriches for response to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in several clinical settings but is neither necessary nor sufficient for benefit [1, 2, 65]. PD-L1 expression on tumor or immune cells may mark an IFN-γ-driven, pre-existing T-cell response that is being restrained through adaptive PD-1/PD-L1 signaling [11, 12]. Accordingly, responses can occur in PD-L1-low or PD-L1-negative tumors, whereas high PD-L1 expression enriches for, but does not determine, clinical benefit. PD-L1-high tumors may nevertheless remain resistant when tumor-cell antigen presentation, T-cell access, or the differentiation state of tumor-reactive T cells is unfavorable [25, 31, 53].

High tumor mutational burden and mismatch-repair deficiency enrich for checkpoint benefit, whereas selected pathogenic POLE/POLD1 alterations identify additional hypermutated tumors with potential sensitivity to PD-1 blockade [66, 67]. However, mutation count does not establish clonal expression, HLA presentation, or T-cell recognition. Incorporating neoantigen clonality, predicted immunogenic fitness, and HLA genotype can therefore refine prediction beyond tumor mutational burden alone [32–34].

Direct assessment of tumor visibility should include MHC-I and B2M expression, allele-specific HLA loss, defects in antigen-processing machinery, and intact JAK1/2–interferon signaling. Abnormalities in these pathways define settings in which CD8-focused checkpoint reinvigoration alone is unlikely to suffice [17, 52, 53]. By contrast, T cell-inflamed IFN-γ/CXCL9/CXCL10 programs and pre-existing intratumoral CD8 T cells indicate an ongoing, although potentially restrained, antitumor response [11, 19]. cDC1-rich tumors and mature tertiary lymphoid structures further mark organized immune niches associated with successful checkpoint responses [68, 69].

Oncogenic context modifies the interpretation of these immune features. Tumor-intrinsic STK11/LKB1 loss, PTEN loss, and WNT/β-catenin activation can each generate non-T-cell-inflamed or immune-excluded states and reduce checkpoint sensitivity [22, 30, 70]. EGFR-mutant non-small-cell lung cancer generally derives limited benefit from PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy, including in some PD-L1-positive tumors, although activity varies with molecular subtype, treatment history, and immune context [71, 72]. These alterations should therefore be interpreted within a composite immune state rather than used as deterministic exclusion criteria for checkpoint therapy.

Spatial, systemic, and dynamic biomarkers

Bulk tissue profiling can obscure spatially organized immune states. Spatially resolved histologic, multiplex-imaging, and transcriptomic analyses can distinguish immune-infiltrated tumors from excluded lesions in which CD8 T cells are confined to fibroblast- or myeloid-rich interfaces [25, 73, 74]. The abundance and spatial co-localization of antigen-presenting-cell or cDC1 niches, TCF1-positive stem-like CD8 T cells, and PD-L1-positive myeloid cells provide information that is not captured by total leukocyte density alone [69, 75]. When available, tumor-draining lymph-node profiling can determine whether tumor-specific TCF1-positive or progenitor-exhausted CD8 T cells are generated and retained upstream of the tumor, and whether PD-1/PD-L1 signaling or impaired trafficking limits their contribution to intratumoral immunity [24, 76, 77].

Blood-based measurements complement tissue by capturing systemic inflammatory and tumor-burden signals. Elevated derived neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, lactate dehydrogenase, and circulating IL-8 are associated with inferior outcomes during checkpoint therapy, but these associations are substantially prognostic and are not sufficiently treatment-specific to nominate a rational combination partner in isolation [78, 79]. Serial circulating tumor DNA provides a dynamic measure of treatment response: early decline or clearance correlates with radiographic response and survival, whereas persistent or recurrent ctDNA can identify molecular nonresponse or relapse before conventional imaging in some cohorts [80–82]. Early peripheral expansion of Ki-67-positive PD-1-positive CD8 T cells and tumor-associated T-cell receptor clonotypes provides pharmacodynamic evidence that PD-1 blockade has generated an immune response; however, its clinical significance depends on the magnitude of reinvigoration relative to tumor burden [41, 83]. Changes in TCF1-positive populations or alternative inhibitory receptors are more appropriately assessed in paired tumor or tumor-draining lymph-node samples, where they may help localize the compartment in which an initially generated immune response subsequently weakens.

Microbiome biomarkers remain investigational. Akkermansia muciniphila, Faecalibacterium, Ruminococcaceae, and other taxa have each been associated with checkpoint benefit in individual cohorts [80, 81]. However, cross-cohort analyses demonstrate substantial geographic, clinical, dietary, medication-related, and methodological effects, with limited reproducibility of species-level signatures [84]. No taxonomic profile is currently sufficiently reproducible for treatment selection. Future biomarker development should therefore prioritize standardized sampling and prospectively test whether community-level functions, microbial metabolites, and coupled host immune phenotypes generalize more reliably than species-level inventories.

Matching biomarkers to the dominant route

A biomarker becomes clinically actionable when it changes treatment selection, trial stratification, or the timing of intervention. Outside selected mismatch-repair-deficient contexts, fixed B2M loss or broad HLA-I loss defines a tumor-visibility defect that is unlikely to be corrected by adding another T-cell checkpoint inhibitor [17, 53, 85]. Reversible suppression of antigen processing may justify strategies that restore tumor visibility, whereas genetically fixed presentation defects provide a rationale for MHC-independent effector approaches, most directly NK-cell-based therapies; prospective treatment selection based on B2M or HLA-I status nevertheless remains unvalidated [61, 62]. An inflamed tumor containing progenitor-like T cells and coexpressing PD-1 and LAG-3 provides a biologically plausible context for dual PD-1/LAG-3 blockade, although LAG-3 expression alone is not a validated treatment-selection biomarker [54, 86, 87]. By contrast, a TCF1-low, terminally exhausted compartment is less likely to respond to additional inhibitory-receptor blockade alone and may motivate renewed priming or cellular replacement [23, 88].

A TGF-β-high, CAF-rich, immune-excluded lesion identifies a stromal barrier and provides a rationale for stromal modulation or vascular-normalizing combinations; however, no TGF-β- or CAF-based biomarker has been prospectively validated for routine treatment selection [25, 26, 36]. IL-8-high and SPP1-high myeloid states identify myeloid-dominant suppression and are better regarded as trial-enrichment variables than as established selectors of a specific myeloid-directed agent [78, 89]. Evidence of CD73–adenosine pathway activity may similarly justify biomarker-enriched trials of A2A-receptor or CD73 blockade, but current clinical evidence remains early and context dependent [90, 91]. Persistent inflammatory signaling after initial PD-1 exposure may define an investigational setting for treatment-sequenced JAK inhibition; this must be distinguished from tumor-cell JAK1/2 loss, which abolishes interferon responsiveness and can mediate checkpoint resistance [17, 92, 93] (Table 1).

Weakening immune pressure: priming failure, T-cell dysfunction, and metabolic constraint

T-cell differentiation and compensatory checkpoints

PD-1 expression alone does not define irreversible exhaustion because it is shared by progenitor-like and terminally exhausted tumor-reactive CD8 T-cell states [23, 121]. Within the exhausted lineage, these cells form a developmental hierarchy comprising TCF1-positive progenitor states with self-renewal capacity, transitory effector-like intermediates, and TCF1-low, TOX-high terminal states with limited reinvigoration capacity [23, 122, 123]. The proliferative response to PD-1 pathway blockade is generated predominantly by the TCF1-positive progenitor-exhausted compartment, which self-renews and supplies downstream effector and terminally exhausted progeny [23, 88, 121]. Accordingly, PD-1 abundance alone does not distinguish a responsive from a refractory infiltrate. Responsiveness is favored when PD-1-positive cells retain TCF1 expression, proliferative competence, and access to antigen-presenting-cell niches, whereas dominance of TCF1-low terminal states is associated with limited reinvigoration [31, 69, 75].

Alternative inhibitory receptors can coexist with PD-1 at baseline or become further upregulated during PD-1-directed therapy, thereby imposing overlapping constraints on residual T-cell function [38, 124, 125]. LAG-3 is frequently coexpressed with PD-1 on dysfunctional or chronically stimulated tumor-reactive T cells, and dual blockade can restore cytotoxic programs; in untreated advanced melanoma, nivolumab plus relatlimab prolonged progression-free survival relative to nivolumab alone [54, 125]. CTLA-4 blockade acts on both effector and regulatory T-cell compartments. Fc-dependent depletion of intratumoral regulatory T cells contributes to efficacy in selected mouse models, although consistent depletion has not been demonstrated across human tumors [125–127]. Clinically, CTLA-4 blockade is established, but the incremental efficacy of combined PD-1/CTLA-4 blockade is accompanied by a substantial increase in high-grade immune-related toxicity [128, 129]. Beyond CTLA-4 and LAG-3, inhibitory pathways such as TIM-3, TIGIT, and A2A-receptor signaling remain investigational, with clinical maturity varying substantially by target and tumor context [38, 90].

TIGIT illustrates the limitations of extrapolating from receptor expression and early-phase activity. The favorable randomized phase II CITYSCAPE signal was followed by divergent phase III outcomes across tumor types and treatment backbones, indicating that TIGIT-directed benefit is context dependent rather than uniform [130–132]. Because TIGIT competes with CD226 for shared ligands, the activity of TIGIT blockade depends on an intact CD226 costimulatory axis; CD155 abundance should therefore be interpreted together with CD226 expression and functional competence rather than as a stand-alone marker of pathway dependence [133, 134]. Antibody architecture introduces an additional source of biological heterogeneity. Fc competence can alter myeloid-cell engagement, regulatory T-cell depletion or reprogramming, and the relative contribution of direct CD8 T-cell reinvigoration, although the clinically optimal Fc configuration remains unresolved [135–137]. TIGIT expression alone should therefore not be regarded as evidence of TIGIT dependence or as a validated treatment-selection biomarker. Evaluation of TIGIT-directed combinations should incorporate CD226 competence, T-cell differentiation state, antigen-presentation integrity, and non-lymphoid exclusion programs.

Immunometabolic weakening and support of suppressor cells

Checkpoint reinvigoration can remain metabolically constrained when nutrient limitation or suppressive metabolite accumulation persists within the tumor microenvironment. In some tumors, high glycolytic flux lowers extracellular glucose availability and increases lactate and proton export, generating a metabolically restrictive and acidic microenvironment [21, 59, 138]. Low glucose and high lactate can suppress T- and natural killer-cell effector function while promoting immunosuppressive macrophage polarization [20, 21]. Lactate, however, is not merely an inhibitory by-product. Tumor-infiltrating regulatory T cells can import and utilize lactate to preserve metabolic fitness and suppressive function under glucose-poor conditions [60, 139, 140]. Tumor glycolysis can therefore weaken effector immunity while redistributing metabolic advantage toward regulatory compartments.

Tryptophan catabolism imposes a parallel constraint. IDO1 and TDO2 initiate tryptophan catabolism along the kynurenine pathway, increasing kynurenine availability; kynurenine–aryl hydrocarbon receptor signaling can directly constrain antitumor T-cell function and reinforce immune-regulatory programs [141–143]. The phase III ECHO-301/KEYNOTE-252 trial failed to improve progression-free or overall survival with epacadostat plus pembrolizumab despite encouraging activity in the preceding non-randomized, open-label ECHO-202/KEYNOTE-037 development program [97, 144]. The development program demonstrated systemic modulation of kynurenine metabolism but did not establish a validated intratumoral pharmacodynamic threshold or select patients for demonstrable IDO1 dependence [97, 145]. Redundancy through TDO2 or downstream kynurenine–AHR signaling remains biologically plausible, but it has not been shown to account for the ECHO-301 result [97, 145]. ECHO-301 therefore argues against unselected single-node IDO1 inhibition, but it does not test—and thus cannot exclude—dependence on the broader tryptophan–kynurenine–AHR axis. Future studies should incorporate intratumoral pharmacodynamic confirmation, test pathway redundancy explicitly, and evaluate functional kynurenine-pathway activity rather than relying on IDO1 expression alone.

Additional metabolic constraints arise through arginine depletion, adenosine signaling, lipid stress, and hypoxia. Arginine depletion by ARG1-positive myeloid cells and CD73–A2A adenosine signaling can independently suppress antitumor T-cell function [146, 147], whereas lipid accumulation and hypoxia can promote dysfunctional or exhausted CD8 T-cell states within tumors [148–150]. Because nutrient acquisition and metabolite utilization are partitioned differently among malignant, myeloid, regulatory, and effector lymphocyte populations, the consequences of systemic metabolic intervention are likely to be cell-type and context dependent [60, 151]. Metabolic therapy should therefore be viewed as ecological engineering: the aim is not simply to suppress a metabolite or enzyme, but to shift competitive fitness toward antitumor effectors and away from malignant and suppressive populations.

Interferon signaling as a temporal switch

Acute type I interferon signaling is required for cDC1-dependent cross-priming of tumor-specific CD8 + T cells and thereby supports the initiation of antitumor immunity [55, 152]. IFN-γ reinforces the effector phase by increasing tumor-cell MHC-I expression, inducing CXCL9 and CXCL10 within the tumor microenvironment, and exerting direct growth-inhibitory effects on IFN-responsive cancer cells. Accordingly, loss-of-function lesions in JAK1/2 or the MHC-I antigen-presentation machinery can mediate primary or acquired checkpoint resistance by uncoupling tumor cells from IFN-dependent growth control and/or CD8 + T-cell recognition [17, 108, 153].

By contrast, prolonged interferon exposure can establish a multigenic inhibitory state, including PD-L1 and other immune-regulatory programs, and can imprint cancer cells with interferon-associated epigenetic memory that amplifies type I IFN signaling and sustains T-cell dysfunction [18, 57, 154]. Paired NSCLC studies further show that acquired resistance is heterogeneous: relapsing lesions may retain a CD8-rich, PD-L1-high or persistently IFN-active state, whereas others lose immune infiltration or antigen-presentation capacity [8, 53]. At baseline, T-cell-inflamed IFN-γ gene-expression programs are associated with response to PD-1 blockade, whereas prolonged interferon signaling can accompany or drive resistant states, underscoring the temporal dependence of pathway function [19, 57]. In parallel, chronic type I interferon signaling can act directly on CD8 + T cells to promote lipid-peroxidation-associated terminal exhaustion and limit the efficacy of PD-1 blockade [155].

Two 2024 proof-of-concept studies support a sequence-dependent therapeutic interpretation: in metastatic NSCLC, adding the JAK1 inhibitor itacitinib after an anti-PD-1 lead-in yielded a 67% response rate and increased CD8 + T-cell plasticity, whereas in checkpoint-refractory Hodgkin lymphoma, ruxolitinib plus nivolumab achieved a 53% objective response rate and reduced suppressive myeloid features [92, 93]. These observations should not be conflated with tumor-cell JAK1/2 loss: genetic loss of IFN sensing abolishes a tumor-intrinsic effector pathway, whereas transient JAK inhibition introduced after initial PD-1 priming is intended to attenuate chronic host or microenvironmental JAK–STAT signaling. Thus, the biological effect of JAK–STAT modulation is conditional on cellular compartment and treatment sequence.

Tumor-draining lymph nodes as an upstream resistance node

Although PD-1 blockade has traditionally been framed as reinvigorating exhausted T cells within tumors, TDLNs harbor stem-like tumor-reactive CD8 + T-cell populations that can replenish intratumoral responses and materially contribute to immune-checkpoint blockade efficacy [76, 77, 156]. Migratory cDC1s transport tumor antigens to TDLNs in a CCR7-dependent manner, where dendritic-cell-mediated priming supports the activation and maintenance of TCF1 + stem-like tumor-specific CD8 + T cells [157, 158]. Within TDLNs, PD-1/PD-L1 interactions between tumor-reactive T cells and PD-L1 + dendritic cells restrain nodal T-cell activation and the generation or expansion of progenitor populations that subsequently contribute to intratumoral immunity [24, 77]. Preclinical studies further indicate that TDLN-derived stem-like T-cell populations and their egress into tumors contribute substantially to checkpoint efficacy [77, 156].

Stem-like tumor-specific CD8 + T cells persist in TDLNs and provide clonally related precursors for intratumoral populations. These cells undergo initial activation in draining nodes while retaining a TCF1 + precursor program, with full effector differentiation occurring predominantly after entry into the tumor [76, 158, 159]. Human single-cell and TCR-resolved studies across solid tumors further identify clonally related TCF1 + or progenitor-exhausted populations in TDLNs and tumors, supporting a nodal precursor pool that contributes to intratumoral T-cell populations during checkpoint therapy [156, 158]. More recently, antigen-specific profiling of patients with melanoma receiving neoadjuvant PD-1 blockade showed that T-bet and CD39 expression in lymph nodes already distinguished CD8 + T-cell differentiation trajectories associated with treatment response [160]. Prospective validation of TDLN-derived biomarkers and TDLN-directed interventions across tumor types nevertheless remains limited.

TDLN dysfunction can arise through impaired cDC1-dependent antigen trafficking and priming, local PD-1/PD-L1-mediated suppression of tumor-reactive T cells, or failure to generate and maintain a stem-like CD8 + T-cell pool capable of supplying the tumor [24, 156, 161]. Local treatment can also alter nodal immune function: preclinical studies show that elective or concomitant irradiation of TDLNs can attenuate antigen-specific systemic immunity and the efficacy of combined radiotherapy–checkpoint blockade, whereas delayed nodal irradiation may partly preserve therapeutic benefit [162, 163]. These observations are insufficient to justify withholding or modifying standard nodal surgery or radiotherapy outside prospective studies, but they provide a rationale for incorporating nodal immune function and treatment timing into future trials.

Proof-of-concept studies support lymph-node-directed immune modulation: locoregional checkpoint delivery can increase antibody exposure in TDLNs while retaining efficacy at reduced doses, lymph-node-targeted vaccination can elicit robust tumor-specific T-cell responses in patients, and Flt3L-mediated cDC1 expansion can enhance checkpoint efficacy through TDLN priming [157, 164, 165]. TDLN-directed therapy therefore remains investigational rather than a clinically validated treatment strategy; nevertheless, TDLNs should be regarded as active sites of tumor-specific T-cell priming, maintenance, and checkpoint response rather than as passive conduits [24, 165].

Transferring immune pressure: tumor-intrinsic escape and phenotypic plasticity

Antigen-presentation and interferon-response defects

Loss of tumor-cell MHC-I antigen presentation is a clinically documented mechanism of both primary and acquired resistance to immune-checkpoint blockade [17, 108]. Because β2-microglobulin is required for stable surface expression of classical MHC-I, biallelic B2M inactivation can eliminate tumor-cell HLA-I expression and abrogate conventional CD8 + T-cell recognition [53, 108]. Tumors can further reduce HLA-I-restricted visibility through allele-specific HLA loss or immunoediting of neoantigens, thereby removing restriction elements or antigenic targets required for recognition by selected tumor-reactive T-cell clones [42, 52, 166]. Defects or dysregulation of TAP, immunoproteasome, and endoplasmic-reticulum aminopeptidase components can alter peptide supply and reshape the MHC-I immunopeptidome, thereby impairing recognition by tumor-reactive CD8 + T cells [53, 167]. Tumor-cell loss-of-function lesions in JAK1/2 can abolish IFN-γ responsiveness, blunting inducible antigen-presentation programs and IFN-dependent growth inhibition without necessarily eliminating basal MHC-I expression [17, 153]. B2M and JAK1/2 lesions have been identified in paired relapse specimens, but longitudinal series indicate that these recurrent genetic events account for only a subset of acquired resistance [17, 53].

Therapeutic implications depend on whether antigen presentation is reversibly suppressed or genetically abolished. Some non-genetic antigen-presentation defects are reversible: epigenetic repression can suppress MHC-I antigen-processing programs, whereas epigenetic therapy or ionizing radiation can restore antigen-presentation and immune-recognition programs in preclinical models [168–170]. Outside selected mismatch-repair-deficient tumors, in which HLA-I-independent effector responses can preserve checkpoint sensitivity, fixed biallelic B2M loss is expected to limit the benefit of further CD8-focused checkpoint intensification [108, 171]. Classical HLA-I loss can instead shift immune pressure toward HLA-I-independent effectors: NK cells can exploit reduced inhibitory MHC-I signaling, whereas γδ T cells can mediate antitumor responses in selected HLA-I-deficient tumors [171, 172]. Surface-antigen-directed cellular and bispecific platforms can also bypass classical peptide–MHC recognition, although B2M or HLA-I loss is not yet a validated treatment-selection biomarker for these modalities [173, 174]. In tumors that retain non-classical HLA-I expression, the HLA-E–NKG2A axis can restrain NK- and CD8-mediated immunity, providing an additional inhibitory route as classical HLA-I-dependent immune pressure is remodeled [64, 175].

Oncogenic and epigenetic reprogramming

Tumor-intrinsic oncogenic programs can remodel antitumor immunity by altering chemokine output, immune-cell recruitment, and the susceptibility of malignant cells to immune attack [70, 117, 175]. Tumor-intrinsic WNT/β-catenin activation is associated with a non-T-cell-inflamed phenotype and, in mechanistic models, impairs dendritic-cell recruitment and downstream T-cell activity [117, 176]. PTEN loss activates PI3K–AKT signaling and can promote immune exclusion, including reduced intratumoral T-cell trafficking and infiltration [70, 177]. In lung adenocarcinoma, STK11 and KEAP1 alterations identify subsets with depleted cytotoxic T-cell infiltration and reduced sensitivity to PD-(L)1-based therapy, although the magnitude of these effects depends on genomic context and treatment backbone [30, 178]. VEGF-driven angiogenic signaling contributes to immune exclusion by impairing dendritic-cell maturation and establishing abnormal endothelial barriers to effector-cell entry; antiangiogenic therapy can partially reverse these features [179, 180]. The clinical relevance of this axis is supported by randomized phase III trials combining PD-1/PD-L1 blockade with VEGF-pathway inhibition in renal-cell carcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma [181, 182]. Accordingly, these oncogenic genotypes should be interpreted as context-dependent modifiers rather than deterministic biomarkers, because their immune and clinical effects vary with tissue lineage, co-mutation structure, clonality, and treatment backbone [176, 178] (Fig. 2).

Epigenetic and post-transcriptional remodeling can generate immune-evasive states that remain potentially reversible in at least a subset of tumors [168, 170]. DNA methylation and Polycomb/EZH2-mediated repression can suppress antigen-presentation or interferon-responsive programs, whereas perturbation of SWI/SNF/PBAF components can alter IFN-γ sensitivity and T-cell-recruiting chemokines; these chromatin effects are therefore context dependent rather than uniformly suppressive [168, 170]. Tumor-intrinsic RNA-regulatory pathways can also support immune escape through distinct mechanisms: YTHDF1 promotes MHC-I degradation, ALKBH5 shapes lactate-dependent accumulation of suppressive immune cells, and NAT10 restrains tumor-intrinsic type I interferon signaling; current evidence for these mechanisms remains predominantly preclinical [183, 184]. Therapeutic manipulation of epigenetic and epitranscriptomic pathways is complicated by compartmental pleiotropy, because related chromatin- and RNA-regulatory machinery also governs T-cell differentiation and dendritic-cell activation [185, 186]. This compartmental pleiotropy provides a rationale to test transient, sequenced, or tumor-directed epigenetic priming rather than presuming that continuous systemic inhibition will be optimal [168, 170].

Lineage plasticity and evolutionary escape

Tumor cells can acquire immunologically distinct phenotypes through transcriptional and epigenetic state transitions without requiring newly acquired coding mutations [187, 188]. In melanoma, lineage plasticity can suppress melanocytic differentiation antigens and generate neural-crest-like or NGFR-high states with altered susceptibility to T-cell attack [187, 188]. Mesenchymal and EMT-associated states can accompany checkpoint resistance through immune exclusion or reduced susceptibility to T-cell-mediated killing; in experimental pancreatic cancer, EMT directly lowers sensitivity to TNF-dependent cytotoxicity [189–191]. Tumor-cell stress and death-response programs can further reduce susceptibility to immune attack by increasing resistance to IFNγ- or TNF-mediated cytotoxicity or by promoting an immune-excluding microenvironment [192, 193]. Such phenotypic transitions can be induced or selected by immune-derived cytokines and therapeutic pressure: sustained IFN-γ exposure can remodel melanoma chromatin and differentiation state, whereas repeated cytotoxic T-cell pressure can enrich resistant NGFR-high populations [187, 188]. Because some plastic states are inducible, reversible, and dynamically selected during therapy, pretreatment archival tissue may not faithfully represent the phenotype present at resistance [35, 188].

Checkpoint blockade acts on a heterogeneous tumor population and can reshape clonal composition, including loss of immunogenic neoantigens and enrichment of genetically or phenotypically resistant states [16, 42]. Spatially distinct metastases within the same patient can harbor divergent genomic and immune states and exhibit heterogeneous responses to checkpoint blockade, consistent with parallel but non-identical routes of immune escape [43, 44]. Multiregion and longitudinal sampling can reveal branch-, lesion-, and time-specific resistance states that may be missed by a single pretreatment biopsy [16, 194]. Some phenotype-switching states remain experimentally reversible, whereas genetically fixed loss of antigen presentation leaves a downstream recognition defect that re-priming alone would not be expected to correct [17, 53, 188].

Unloading immune pressure: local ecosystems and systemic host programs

Stromal and vascular exclusion

In immune-excluded tumors, CD8 + T cells can accumulate within stromal regions while remaining spatially separated from malignant-cell nests [25, 195]. CAF-associated extracellular-matrix remodeling can impose physical barriers to immune-cell access: organized collagen-rich matrices constrain T-cell migration, whereas hyaluronan-rich desmoplasia can increase intratumoral pressure and compress the microvasculature [195–197]. CAF-derived CXCL12 and stromal TGF-β are experimentally established mediators of T-cell exclusion and resistance to PD-(L)1 blockade in selected tumor contexts [25, 36]. TGF-β further couples fibroblast-state programming to suppression and spatial exclusion of antitumor T-cell responses, linking stromal remodeling directly to immune dysfunction [25, 36].

VEGF-driven angiogenesis can reinforce immune exclusion by impairing dendritic-cell maturation and establishing an endothelial barrier to effector T-cell entry; antiangiogenic treatment can partially reverse these features in preclinical models [179, 180]. Randomized phase III trials establish the clinical efficacy of combining PD-1/PD-L1 blockade with VEGF-pathway inhibition in renal-cell carcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma, consistent with—but not proving—the vascular and immune mechanisms defined preclinically [181, 182].

Therapeutic translation of stromal modulation has nevertheless been inconsistent: bintrafusp alfa failed to outperform pembrolizumab in PD-L1-high NSCLC, pegvorhyaluronidase alfa failed to improve survival in hyaluronan-high metastatic pancreatic cancer, and broad depletion of αSMA+ fibroblasts accelerated pancreatic cancer in preclinical models [198–200]. CAFs comprise functionally distinct states, and selected stromal compartments can restrain rather than promote tumor progression; indiscriminate stromal depletion can therefore produce paradoxical biological effects [201, 202]. These findings provide a rationale to test CAF-state- or biomarker-guided stromal modulation rather than assuming that broad stromal depletion will be beneficial; such strategies remain investigational [201, 203].

Myeloid–treg networks

Tumor-associated macrophages, suppressive neutrophil and monocytic populations, regulatory T cells, and dysfunctional dendritic-cell states can participate in coordinated immunosuppressive ecosystems rather than acting as isolated compartments [204–206]. Regulatory T cells can restrict local effector responses through IL-2 consumption and CTLA-4-dependent attenuation of CD80/CD86 costimulation on dendritic cells [206, 207]. Suppressive myeloid states inhibit T-cell activity through context-dependent effector programs, including prostaglandin E2 production and hypoxia-induced HIF-1α–PD-L1 signaling [208, 209]. Lactate provides a cross-compartmental link between metabolic constraint and immune suppression: tumor-derived lactate can polarize macrophages toward suppressive states, whereas regulatory T cells adapt metabolically to glucose-poor, lactate-rich conditions and retain suppressive function [20, 139, 140].

Within tumors, SPP1-high macrophage states and related myeloid programs have been associated with immune suppression, disease progression, or checkpoint resistance, although their biomarker value remains context dependent [89, 204]. CSF1R-directed macrophage targeting has produced context-dependent preclinical effects and modest clinical activity in unselected solid tumors, while adaptive microenvironmental resistance can emerge during pathway inhibition [210, 211]. CSF1R blockade need not simply deplete macrophages: surviving TAMs can undergo functional state changes, whereas stromal adaptation can recruit PMN-MDSCs and other compensatory myeloid populations [210, 212]. These observations provide a rationale to test functional myeloid reprogramming rather than assuming that broad macrophage depletion will be sufficient. Myeloid- and antigen-presenting-cell–directed strategies include CD40 agonism, intratumoral innate-receptor activation, and blockade of inhibitory receptors such as LILRB2/ILT4; each has shown biological activity, although clinical maturity remains heterogeneous [213–215] (Fig. 3).

Suppressive myeloid and Treg programs are spatially organized: macrophage-rich stromal regions can restrict CD8 + T-cell access to tumor nests, whereas tumor-associated Treg cells can engage dendritic cells locally and attenuate CD80/CD86 costimulation through CTLA-4 [74, 216]. Accordingly, bulk myeloid abundance cannot distinguish functionally divergent cell states or resolve their organization within cellular neighborhoods and tumor–stroma interfaces [74, 204]. These spatial observations provide a rationale to test locoregional or intratumoral immunomodulation in spatially restricted suppressive niches, but superiority over systemic treatment has not been established.

Systemic inflammation, microbiota, and host physiology

Tumors can remodel hematopoiesis at distant sites, with tumor-derived cytokines and cancer-induced bone-marrow stromal programs skewing myeloid output toward neutrophil and monocyte states with tumor-promoting or immunosuppressive properties. Liver metastases can impose systemic immune suppression and reduce checkpoint efficacy through hepatic immune-regulatory circuits, including macrophage-mediated loss of activated CD8 + T cells [217–219]. Elevated circulating IL-8 and NLR-based inflammatory indices are associated with inferior outcomes during checkpoint therapy, but they remain predominantly prognostic and do not identify a validated partner therapy [78, 79]. Two proof-of-concept studies further suggest that JAK inhibition can enhance PD-1-directed immunity in selected inflammatory states: itacitinib introduced after an anti-PD-1 lead-in in metastatic NSCLC and ruxolitinib combined with nivolumab in checkpoint-refractory Hodgkin lymphoma produced clinical activity accompanied by immune remodeling [18, 92, 93].

Gut microbial communities can modulate systemic and intratumoral immunity and influence the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 blockade through effects on antigen-presenting and effector-cell programs [220, 221]. Concordant patient cohorts, responder-to-mouse transfer experiments, and interventional FMT studies support a causal contribution of gut microbiota to checkpoint sensitivity, although the responsible organisms and functions are not universal [220, 221]. Early clinical trials have shown that FMT can modulate checkpoint responsiveness in melanoma and other solid tumors, but donor selection, microbial engraftment criteria, and predictive community features remain insufficiently standardized for routine practice [222, 223]. Microbiome–response associations show limited reproducibility at the species level, with geography, cohort structure, diet, medication exposure, and analytical methodology contributing materially to between-study heterogeneity [84, 224]. Routine probiotic supplementation has not been shown to improve checkpoint efficacy; in one melanoma cohort accompanied by preclinical validation, commercial probiotic use was associated with lower gut-microbiome diversity and impaired anti-PD-1 activity in mice [84, 224, 225]. Microbiome-directed therapy therefore remains investigational: current data justify standardized prospective evaluation of FMT, defined microbial communities, dietary interventions, and functional microbial outputs rather than taxon-based treatment selection [226, 227].

Host physiology can modify checkpoint responses, but the direction and magnitude of these associations are context dependent. Obesity can increase PD-1-associated T-cell dysfunction yet has paradoxically correlated with greater benefit from PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in some cohorts; age-related remodeling can alter the intratumoral effector-to-regulatory balance, whereas sympathetic β1-adrenergic signaling can directly promote CD8 + T-cell exhaustion [119, 228, 229]. Most clinical associations between host factors and checkpoint outcomes remain observational and susceptible to residual confounding, limiting their use as stand-alone treatment-selection biomarkers. Associations between immune-checkpoint infusion timing and outcome likewise remain retrospective and inconsistent and are not sufficient to guide routine treatment scheduling [120, 230] (Fig. 4).

Reassembling effective immunity: combination design, sequencing, and clinical translation

Clinically validated strategies for restoring immune pressure

Within this framework, combination therapy is most rational when its components address distinct, mechanistically non-redundant constraints. In advanced melanoma, combined PD-1/CTLA-4 blockade produces durable long-term disease control and survival, but substantially increases high-grade treatment-related toxicity relative to PD-1 monotherapy [95, 128, 231]. Combined PD-1/LAG-3 blockade also improves progression-free survival over PD-1 monotherapy in untreated advanced melanoma, with a comparatively modest within-trial increase in high-grade treatment-related toxicity [54, 86].

VEGF-pathway inhibition can remodel tumor endothelium and increase effector-cell trafficking, providing a mechanistic basis for combination with PD-(L)1 blockade [26, 180]. Randomized phase III trials have established survival benefits for PD-1/PD-L1 blockade combined with VEGF-pathway inhibition in renal-cell carcinoma and hepatocellular carcinoma [181, 182]. Selected cytotoxic regimens can increase tumor immunogenicity and T-cell infiltration, whereas radiotherapy can engage type I interferon-dependent priming; these immune effects depend on drug choice, radiation dose and target volume, and treatment timing rather than reflecting a uniform “immunogenic cell death” effect [163, 232, 233].

Adoptive cellular therapy can reduce dependence on endogenous priming by supplying ex vivo expanded or engineered tumor-reactive effectors [234, 235]. Autologous TIL therapy has demonstrated clinically meaningful activity in advanced melanoma after PD-1 failure, including with the centrally manufactured TIL product lifileucel [104, 235, 236]. CAR T cells and surface-antigen-directed bispecific T-cell engagers can recognize tumor-cell surface antigens without classical peptide–MHC presentation [173, 174]. NK-cell approaches can exploit reduced HLA-I-mediated inhibition, whereas conventional engineered αβ TCR therapies remain peptide–HLA restricted and therefore should not be considered a general bypass strategy for fixed HLA-I loss [171, 172].

Personalized neoantigen vaccines can generate or expand polyclonal neoantigen-specific T-cell responses in patients, providing a strategy to broaden the tumor-reactive repertoire when antigen presentation remains competent [237, 238]. Intratumoral oncolytic virotherapy can increase local T-cell infiltration and generate an inflamed tumor microenvironment, although randomized combination trials show that such biological activity does not guarantee additional clinical benefit [239, 240]. Priming-focused interventions are unlikely to restore conventional CD8 + T-cell control when tumor cells have fixed loss of HLA-I-mediated antigen presentation. Checkpoint intensification likewise presupposes a tumor-reactive T-cell population capable of proliferative or effector reinvigoration [23, 88, 171].

What negative trials reveal about resistance biology

Late-stage negative trials can be mechanistically informative when interpreted against earlier biological and pharmacodynamic evidence [97, 200]. Clinical development of TIGIT blockade illustrates that compelling target biology and early randomized activity do not establish uniform pathway dependence: outcomes have diverged across tumor types and treatment backbones [130–132]. The ECHO-301/KEYNOTE-252 failure likewise showed that systemic IDO1 inhibition could not be assumed to establish therapeutically sufficient control of intratumoral tryptophan–kynurenine signaling; early studies documented robust peripheral kynurenine suppression, whereas TDO2–kynurenine–AHR signaling provides a biologically plausible, but unproven, route of pathway redundancy [97, 141, 145]. Stromal targeting is similarly context dependent: bintrafusp alfa failed to outperform pembrolizumab in PD-L1-high NSCLC, broad αSMA + CAF depletion accelerated pancreatic cancer in preclinical models, and single-cell analyses reveal functionally distinct fibroblast states linked to immunosuppression and immunotherapy response [198, 200]. CSF1R blockade need not simply deplete macrophages: surviving TAMs can undergo functional state changes, whereas adaptive resistance can emerge through microenvironmental remodeling and compensatory recruitment of PMN-MDSCs [210, 212].

Collectively, these trials caution against empirical addition of mechanistically plausible agents to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in the absence of evidence for pathway dependence or relevant-compartment target engagement. Accordingly, we propose that mechanism-informed combination development prospectively address four linked questions: whether the putative resistance route is active in the enrolled tumor; whether the partner drug engages that route in the relevant compartment; whether an early on-treatment readout demonstrates the intended biological shift; and whether compensatory escape emerges. Platform and window-of-opportunity designs can incorporate on-treatment tissue sampling and parallel mechanistic arms, allowing pharmacodynamic activity to be assessed before commitment to large confirmatory trials.

Dynamic sequencing rather than uniform escalation

Treatment timing can materially alter the biological and clinical effects of checkpoint blockade. In resectable melanoma, randomized trials show that initiating checkpoint blockade before surgery can improve event-free survival relative to postoperative-only therapy, whereas neoadjuvant studies demonstrate substantial pathological responses and treatment-associated immune remodeling; the specific contributions of preserved antigen burden, lymphatic drainage, and tumor-draining lymph nodes have not been isolated clinically [86, 241, 242]. Limited-duration CTLA-4 exposure is clinically feasible: dose-optimized or short-course ipilimumab–nivolumab regimens can reduce severe toxicity while retaining substantial antitumor activity, although the optimal duration and subsequent maintenance strategy remain undefined [243, 244]. A sequence-dependent JAK-inhibition strategy is supported most directly by the NSCLC study in which itacitinib was introduced after an anti-PD-1 lead-in; the Hodgkin lymphoma data support JAK–PD-1 combination activity but do not establish that JAK inhibition must universally follow a defined priming interval [92, 93]. Preclinical and early clinical studies provide a rationale to test finite or sequenced epigenetic therapy, radiotherapy, and intratumoral innate agonists as conditioning interventions, but superiority over continuous combination therapy has not been established [163, 170].

Within this framework, longitudinal monitoring is intended to inform escalation by capturing treatment-induced changes that baseline profiling cannot resolve. Serial ctDNA kinetics can provide an early molecular measure of checkpoint response and, in some cohorts, precede conventional radiographic assessment [80, 82]. Paired on-treatment or relapse biopsies can resolve distinct resistance trajectories, including persistent immune remodeling, impaired antigen presentation, and heterogeneous tumor–microenvironment evolution [16, 53]. Peripheral immune profiling can detect PD-1-responsive T-cell proliferation and longitudinal circulating immune states associated with checkpoint outcome, but these signals remain pharmacodynamic or correlative rather than complete mechanistic diagnoses [41, 245]. We therefore propose route-matched escalation as a trial strategy rather than a validated treatment algorithm. Dual PD-1/LAG-3 blockade is clinically validated in advanced melanoma, but neither LAG-3 expression nor a progenitor-rich T-cell state is a prospectively validated treatment-selection biomarker [54, 86]. For immune-excluded disease, VEGF-pathway combinations have randomized clinical validation in renal-cell and hepatocellular carcinomas, whereas biomarker-guided TGF-β/CAF modulation remains investigational [181, 182]. Fixed HLA-I antigen-presentation loss provides a mechanistic rationale for MHC-independent effector strategies, but B2M/HLA-I status is not a validated treatment-selection biomarker [53, 171]. Myeloid-directed escalation remains investigational because suppressive myeloid states are heterogeneous and adaptive remodeling can emerge after pathway inhibition [89, 210]. Combination checkpoint regimens can produce substantial high-grade immune-related toxicity, and exploratory blood-, genomic-, and T-cell-based signatures have been associated with irAE risk; however, no toxicity biomarker is validated for routine prospective treatment selection [246, 247].

The objective of this framework is an adaptive treatment system rather than a universal resistance regimen. We propose that baseline profiling define the initial topology, early sampling determine how therapy reshapes it, and subsequent intervention target the newly dominant bottleneck. In this formulation, resistance becomes a measurable treatment-evolution process rather than only a post-progression category (Table 3).

Conclusion

Resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade is often termed multifactorial, but that label is useful only when relationships among mechanisms are defined. The immune-pressure redistribution framework organizes resistance into three coupled outcomes: tumor cells transfer pressure by reducing immune visibility or susceptibility; antitumor responses are weakened through defective priming, terminal differentiation, compensatory checkpoints, metabolic restriction, or persistent cytokine signaling; and pressure is unloaded into stromal, vascular, myeloid, regulatory, microbial, and systemic compartments. The framework complements immunoediting by identifying where treatment-induced pressure is diverted and linking each destination to a therapeutic strategy.

It also explains observations that otherwise appear contradictory. Interferons are required for priming yet can promote resistance when signaling becomes chronic. Tumor-cell JAK loss impairs immune recognition, whereas timed JAK inhibition may relieve persistent inflammatory dysfunction. Lactate suppresses effector lymphocytes while sustaining regulatory T cells, and an inflamed tumor may remain resistant when inflammation is spatially or temporally misdirected. These are not anomalies, but manifestations of redistribution across compartments and treatment stages.

Clinical progress will require topology-matched intervention rather than empirical combination therapy. Antigen-presentation defects, compensatory checkpoint signaling, and angiogenic exclusion already have substantial clinical support, whereas tumor-draining lymph-node dysfunction, epigenetic memory, metabolic competition, myeloid plasticity, and microbiome effects require stronger prospective validation. Composite biomarkers, paired tissue and blood sampling, spatial profiling, and circulating tumor DNA-guided adaptation may reveal when pressure has shifted and whether treatment restores productive tumor killing. The objective is not indiscriminate immune activation, but sustained alignment among tumor visibility, effector competence, and a permissive tissue ecosystem.
