Integrated Bioinformatics Analysis Identifies FCER1G, IL1R2, and BST1 as Diagnostic Biomarkers and Therapeutic Targets in Sepsis
Authors: Junjie Li, Guanghui Xiu, Ping Liu, Xianzhong Chen, Kailv Zhou, Yunfeng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSepsis remains difficult to diagnose since blood culture has low sensitivity and current biomarkers lack sufficient specificity. We sought to identify robust diagnostic and therapeutic targets for neonatal sepsis through an integrated bioinformatics and experimental workflow.MethodsThe GSE69686 dataset (108 neonates; 48 sepsis, 60 controls collected ≤ 3 days after birth) was analyzed using a multi‐step pipeline: WGCNA to identify sepsis‐associated modules; limma‐based differential expression; feature selection by LASSO and SVM‐RFE; immune deconvolution (CIBERSORT); and pathway interrogation (GSEA). Drug enrichment (Enrichr/DSigDB) and molecular docking (AutoDock) supported targetability. Experimental validation employed a THP‐1 sepsis‐like model (1 μg/mL LPS, 24 h) with qRT‐PCR, shRNA‐mediated BST1 knockdown, Western blot, ELISA (IL‐6, TNF‐α, IL‐1β), CCK‐8, and Annexin V/PI flow cytometry.ResultsIntersecting the outputs of LASSO and SVM‐RFE yielded three robust biomarkers—FCER1G, IL1R2, and BST1. These genes are associated with broad shifts in inferred immune infiltration, and GSEA linked their high expression to INFLAMMATORY RESPONSE; BST1 and FCER1G were additionally enriched for TNFα/NF‐κB signaling. In THP‐1 cells, LPS upregulated all three genes; focusing on BST1, shRNA knockdown partially rescued proliferation (0–72 h), reduced IL‐6/TNF‐α/IL‐1β secretion, and lowered apoptosis relative to sh‐NC. Docking analysis suggested artesunate as a candidate modulator with a binding energy of −6.27 kcal/mol to BST1.ConclusionOur integrated pipeline nominates FCER1G, IL1R2, and BST1 as biologically coherent biomarkers of sepsis and highlights BST1 as a plausible therapeutic target warranting further investigation. These findings support external validation in multicenter cohorts and mechanistic extension—particularly functional testing of predicted modulators—in primary cells.Keywords: artesunate, bioinformatics analysis, biomarkers, immune infiltration, molecular docking, sepsis, WGCNA

Introduction

Sepsis is a systemic inflammatory syndrome occurring within the first 28 days of life due to infection with bacterial, viral, or fungal pathogens [1, 2]. More than 3 million cases of sepsis have been reported worldwide [3, 4], with a significant mortality rate due to sepsis, especially in underdeveloped countries [5, 6]. Clinically, sepsis is categorized into early‐onset (< 72 h after birth) and late‐onset (> 72 h) [7, 8]. Early‐onset sepsis typically arises from vertical transmission during labor or delivery and is most often caused by Group B Streptococcus and Escherichia coli [9, 10]. Late‐onset sepsis is often hospital‐ or community‐acquired, with pathogens including Gram‐negative bacilli, coagulase‐negative staphylococci, and fungi [11, 12]. Several perinatal and clinical factors—such as maternal infection, prolonged rupture of membranes, low birth weight, prematurity, invasive procedures, and NICU admission—have been identified as major risk factors for disease development [13].

Despite its prevalence, sepsis remains difficult to diagnose accurately [14]. Blood culture, while considered the gold standard, is limited by poor sensitivity and long processing time [15]. Biomarkers such as white blood cell count, IL‐6, and presepsin have been investigated for their diagnostic value, but none demonstrate sufficient specificity or timeliness for early detection [16, 17]. Empirical use of broad‐spectrum antibiotics remains the mainstay of treatment, but increasing antimicrobial resistance and variation in individual pathogen profiles challenge standardized clinical management [18, 19].

With the advancement of bioinformatics and transcriptomic technologies [20, 21], increasing attention has been directed toward elucidating the molecular mechanisms of sepsis [22, 23]. Recent studies have shown that disease progression is not solely determined by pathogen invasion but also involves complex host responses, including immune dysregulation, inflammatory cascades, and signal transduction pathways [24, 25]. Key regulatory genes and therapeutic targets were identified by systematic analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), functional enrichment pathways, and protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks.

Therefore, we identified sepsis‐related DEGs based on publicly available gene expression datasets, to explore underlying molecular mechanisms, and to predict potential therapeutic agents by integrating drug–gene interaction data. This research is expected to enhance our understanding of the pathophysiology of sepsis and provide a theoretical basis for the development of improved diagnostic tools and personalized treatment strategies.

Materials and Methods

Data Source

Gene expression data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database [26]. Specifically, we retrieved the sepsis‐related dataset GSE69686. To ensure clinical homogeneity and analytical relevance, we selected a total of 108 samples collected within the first three days after birth, including 48 samples from neonates diagnosed with sepsis and 60 healthy controls. These data were used for subsequent differential expression analysis and molecular investigations.

Weighted Gene Co‐Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA was performed using the “WGCNA” package in R [27] to identify gene modules significantly associated with sepsis. Initially, hierarchical clustering was conducted to assess sample quality and remove outliers. A soft‐thresholding power was then selected to ensure a scale‐free topology with a fit index of 0.90. During module construction, the minimum module size was set to 100 genes, and modules with a merge cut height of 0.2 were merged. The module exhibiting the highest correlation with the sepsis phenotype was identified as the key module and selected for further analysis.

Differential Expression Analysis

To identify DEGs between the sepsis group and healthy controls, the “Limma” package in R [28] was employed to perform differential expression analysis. Linear models were constructed for each gene, followed by empirical Bayes moderation to enhance statistical robustness. Genes that conformed to the criteria of |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 and padj < 0.01 were regarded as significant DEGs. The resulting DEGs were subjected to subsequent functional annotation and enrichment analyses.

Functional enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis was performed using the KOBAS‐i platform. To control the false discovery rate, the resulting p values were adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Only GO terms and KEGG pathways with an adjusted p < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched and used to outline the functional profile of genes associated with sepsis.

PPI Network Construction and Hub Gene Identification

To pinpoint key genes that may participate in the central regulatory network of sepsis, the overlapping gene set was imported into the STRING database to obtain both experimentally validated and predicted protein–protein interactions. The resulting interaction map was then rendered in Cytoscape software (version 3.8.0) [29]. Within Cytoscape, the cytoHubba plugin was applied to assess the topological prominence of nodes in the PPI network using six complementary algorithms: Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC), Maximum Neighborhood Component (MNC), Closeness and Radiality, Degree, Edge Percolated Component (EPC). For each algorithm, the 10 highest‐scoring genes were extracted, and those shared across all six rankings were defined as hub genes and carried forward for downstream functional characterization and drug‐target analysis.

Identification of Candidate Biomarkers Using LASSO and SVM‐RFE

To identify specific biomarkers associated with sepsis, we extracted the expression profiles of intersecting hub genes and used sample groupings (sepsis vs. control) as the classification variable. Two machine learning–based feature selection methods (LASSO regression and SVM‐RFE) were applied.

The “glmnet” package in R was used to perform LASSO regression on the candidate genes [30], and the model corresponding to the minimum cross‐validated error was selected as the optimal LASSO solution. SVM‐RFE is a backward feature elimination algorithm based on support vector machines, which recursively removes the least informative features according to the magnitude of their weights in the SVM classifier. SVM‐RFE was implemented using the “e1071” package in R [31], and the feature set with the lowest classification error was selected.

Common feature genes selected by both analyses were considered to represent robust candidate biomarkers for sepsis, and these genes were subjected to further validation and modeling.

Immune Infiltration Analysis

To characterize the immune microenvironment in sepsis, immune cell infiltration levels were estimated using the CIBERSORT algorithm implemented in R [32]. CIBERSORT is a support vector regression (SVR)–based deconvolution tool that infers the relative proportions of 22 immune cell types from bulk gene expression profiles. The reference expression matrix (LM22), representing 22 predefined immune cell phenotypes, was obtained from the official CIBERSORTx platform.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)

To elucidate the transcriptome‐wide biological processes and signaling pathways associated with the key genes, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis was performed using GSEA_4.2.2 [33]. The HALLMARK collection from the MSigDB was used as the reference, encompassing 50 non‐overlapping gene sets that summarize prototypical cellular states and pathways. Genes were ordered according to their differential expression between the sepsis and control groups, and an enrichment score was computed for each gene set. Gene sets with a nominal p value < 0.05 were regarded as significantly enriched.

Prediction of Targeted Therapeutics and Molecular Docking

To identify candidate therapeutic compounds targeting the identified biomarkers of sepsis, we conducted drug prediction using the Enrichr package in R [34], leveraging the Drug Signature Database (DSigDB) for enrichment analysis. This approach allowed for the identification of small molecules significantly associated with the input gene set. The 3D crystal structures of receptor proteins were retrieved from the UniProt database, following strict selection criteria: [1] structures derived from X‐ray crystallography; [2] structures with the lowest possible resolution values (measured in Ångströms, where smaller values indicate higher accuracy); and [3] preference for entries with longer amino acid sequences to ensure sufficient binding site coverage.

The corresponding 3D conformations of the predicted drugs were downloaded from the PubChem database and used as ligands. If no 3D structure was available or if the drug was intended to modulate gene expression (e.g., overexpression or knockdown), alternative compounds were selected based on ranked enrichment p‐values.

Prior to docking, all ligands and receptors were preprocessed. PyMOL software was used to remove water molecules, eliminate small molecule contaminants, and add hydrogens to the receptor proteins. Ligand structures were subjected to energy minimization using ChemBioOffice. Molecular docking was then performed using AutoDockTools. Binding interactions were evaluated, and compounds with binding energies less than −5 kcal/mol were considered as potential candidates for stable interactions with the target protein.

Cell Culture and Transfection

This study used the human monocytic cell line THP‐1 (SNL‐044, Sunncell, Wuhan, China) (https://www.sunncell.com.cn/). Cells were cultured in PRMI‐1640 (PM150 110, Procell, Wuhan, China) supplemented with 10% FBS (164210, Procell), 0.05 mM β‐mercaptoethanol (M3148, Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The incubator (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was maintained at 37°C with a 5% CO2 concentration under saturated humidity. All experiments were performed using THP‐1 cells between passages 3 and 10 after revival. We have performed STR identification on the cells, and the mycoplasma detection results turned out to be negative. PBS, trypsin, and freezing medium were used for routine handling as needed.

Liposome‐mediated transfection was used to deliver two BST1‐targeting short hairpin RNAs (sh‐BST1#1 and sh‐BST1#2) and a non‐targeting negative‐control shRNA (sh‐NC) into THP‐1 cells. All oligonucleotides were obtained from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The sequences were sh‐BST1#1 (5′‐CCAAGTCTTTATACAGAACAA‐3′), sh‐BST1#2 (5′‐GCAGCATGAAAGTCCTGGAAA‐3′), and sh‐NC (5′‐TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT‐3′). Following the manual, transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000150, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

In Vitro Sepsis‐Like Cell Model (LPS Stimulation)

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were exposed to lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 1 μg/mL, dissolved directly in culture medium; Sigma‐Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) for 24 h to model sepsis‐related inflammatory injury [35]. Unless otherwise specified, cells receiving an equal volume of culture medium without LPS served as controls. After the 24 h stimulation, cells were collected immediately for downstream assays.

Quantitative Reverse Transcription PCR (qRT‐PCR)

Following the protocol, total RNA from THP‐1 cells was isolated using TRIzol reagent (15596026CN, Invitrogen, USA). RNA concentration and purity were measured with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). First‐strand cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (D7178S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). qRT‐PCR was subsequently performed with SYBR Green qPCR Mix (D7260, Beyotime) on an Applied Biosystems real‐time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Billings, MT, USA). The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 30 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 30 s and 72°C for 1 min, and a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. Relative mRNA expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the endogenous control, and Table 1 shows the primer sequences.

Western Blot

RIPA lysis buffer added with protease and phosphatase inhibitors was used to isolate total protein from THP‐1 cells. After placing the cell lysates on ice for 30 min, the cells were centrifuged at 12,000× g for 15 min at 4°C to remove debris. Using a BCA assay kit (P0010, Beyotime), protein concentrations were measured. Equal amounts of protein (20–30 μg per lane) were mixed with 5× loading buffer, boiled at 95°C for 5 min, separated on 10%–12% SDS–PAGE gels, and subsequently transferred onto PVDF films (0.22 μm; FFP19, Beyotime). The films were blocked with 5% skimmed milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies against BST1 (ab235786, 1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and β‐actin (ab8226, 1:5000, Abcam) as the loading control. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature (goat anti‐rabbit IgG, ab205718, 1:5000–1:10,000; goat anti‐mouse IgG, ab205719, 1:2000–1:20,000). Protein bands were visualized using an ECL substrate on a Bio‐Rad chemiluminescence imaging system, and band intensities were quantified with ImageJ, with BST1 expression normalized to β‐actin. All experiments were repeated independently three times at least.

Cell Proliferation Assay

THP‐1 cells stably expressing BST1‐targeting shRNA or a non‐targeting control shRNA were seeded into 96‐well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well in 100 μL of complete DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Three technical replicates were set up for each condition. Cells were then treated according to the corresponding experimental protocol. At 0, 24, 48, and 72 h, 10 μL of CCK‐8 solution (C0037, Beyotime) was supplemented for 30‐min incubation at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The absorbance at 450 nm was subsequently recorded using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and readings from medium‐only wells were used for background correction.

ELISA

Cell culture supernatant from THP‐1 cells was collected at the indicated time points (typically 24 h after 1 μg/mL LPS stimulation). Supernatants were clarified by centrifugation (300× g, 5 min, 4°C) to remove cells, followed by 10,000× g for 10 min to remove debris, aliquoted, and stored at −80°C until analysis (single freeze–thaw). Human IL‐6, TNF‐α, and IL‐1β were quantified using pre‐coated ELISA kits in accordance with the manufacturers' protocols. Samples were diluted in assay buffer as required, and standards were prepared by serial dilution to generate calibration curves. All measurements were performed in duplicate on a microplate reader at 450 nm with a 570–630 nm reference wavelength. Background‐corrected absorbance values were converted to concentrations (pg/mL) using a four‐parameter logistic fit. Data from at least three independent experiments are reported as mean ± SD.

Flow Cytometry

THP‐1 cells were collected, washed twice with ice‐cold PBS and resuspended at a final density of 1 × 106 cells/mL in 1× Annexin V binding buffer. An aliquot of 100 μL cell suspension from each sample was then incubated with 5 μL Annexin V–FITC and 5 μL propidium iodide (PI) from the Annexin V–FITC/PI detection kit (C1062S, Beyotime) in the dark for 15 min at room temperature. Subsequently, 400 μL binding buffer was added, and samples were subjected to flow cytometric analysis within 1 h. Unstained and single‐stained tubes were used to adjust instrument settings and compensation. For each sample, at least 10,000 singlet events were acquired after exclusion of debris (based on FSC/SSC) and doublets (FSC‐A vs. FSC‐H). Data from at least three independent experiments were analyzed using FlowJo software, and results were expressed as the percentage of total cells.

Statistical Analysis

All tests were two‐sided with α = 0.05. Data are shown as mean ± SD (or median [IQR] if non‐normal; Shapiro–Wilk for normality). Two‐group comparisons used unpaired Student's t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests; ≥ 3 groups used one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test or Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn's post hoc test. Time‐course/factorial experiments were analyzed by two‐way ANOVA with post hoc multiple comparisons. Where applicable, p values were adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate. Technical replicates were averaged within each biological replicate. Analyses were performed in R and GraphPad Prism; exact tests, n, and p values are provided in the figure legends.

Results

Identification of a Key Module Associated With Sepsis by WGCNA

To identify gene co‐expression modules associated with sepsis, Weighted Gene Co‐expression Network Analysis was performed on the GEO dataset. A soft‐thresholding power of 20 was selected to construct a scale‐free topology network, based on the criterion that the scale‐free topology fit index exceeded 0.90 (Figure 1A). With a minimum module size set to 100 genes and a module merging threshold of 0.2, a total of seven distinct modules were identified (Figure 1B).

The minimum soft‐thresholding power of 20 was selected based on the criterion of R 2 > 0.90 to ensure a scale‐free network. (B) Gene dendrogram and module assignment. The top panel displays the gene clustering dendrogram; each branch corresponds to a gene. The color bars below represent module assignment before and after merging. (C) Module‐trait relationship heatmap. The x‐axis indicates the clinical trait (sepsis status), and the y‐axis lists the identified modules. Each cell shows the Pearson correlation coefficient and corresponding p‐value. Red indicates positive correlation and blue indicates negative correlation.

Correlation analysis between module eigengenes and the clinical phenotype revealed that the brown module (MEbrown) exhibited the strongest positive association with sepsis (correlation coefficient = 0.60, p = 6e−12), suggesting its potential biological relevance (Figure 1C). A total of 398 genes within the MEbrown module were extracted for subsequent analyses.

Identification of DEGs Associated With Sepsis

To screen for genes with markedly altered expression in sepsis, samples in the GEO dataset were classified into a sepsis group and a control group. Differential expression was analyzed in R using the “limma” package. Linear models combined with empirical Bayes shrinkage were fitted, and genes satisfying |log2 FC| ≥ 1 together with an adjusted p‐value < 0.01 were defined as DEGs. Under these thresholds, 28 DEGs were obtained, all showing increased expression in the sepsis group, and the overall distribution of changes was displayed in a volcano plot (Figure 2A).

Gray dots denote non‐significant genes, red dots represent significantly upregulated genes, blue dots indicate significantly downregulated genes (threshold: |log2FC| ≥ 1, padj < 0.01). (B) Upset plot showing the intersection between DEGs and WGCNA hub module genes. The left bar chart shows the size of each gene set, while the top bar chart indicates the number of intersecting genes. (C) Heatmap of expression levels for the 27 intersecting genes. Blue indicates low expression, and red indicates high expression, reflecting differential gene expression between sepsis and control groups.

To narrow down the candidate set, these DEGs were intersected with hub genes from the WGCNA brown module, yielding 27 overlapping genes (Figure 2B). A heatmap was then constructed to depict the expression patterns of these 27 genes across all samples (Figure 2C). Most of the overlapping genes consistently showed higher expression in neonates with sepsis, implying a potential role in the development and progression of the disease.

Functional Enrichment Analysis Reveals Inflammation‐Related Pathways

To explore the potential biological roles of the 27 overlapping genes, GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed. The results indicated that these genes were significantly enriched in inflammation‐related biological processes and signaling pathways, including inflammatory response, interleukin‐18‐mediated signaling pathway, and positive regulation of NF‐κB transcription factor activity (Figure 3A–D). These pathways are known to play key roles in immune and inflammatory regulation, suggesting that the identified genes may contribute to the pathogenesis of sepsis by modulating inflammatory signaling networks.

Construction of PPI Network and Hub Genes Identification

To identify core regulatory genes potentially involved in sepsis, a PPI network was constructed using the 27 overlapping genes via the STRING database (Figure 4A). The resulting interaction data were imported into Cytoscape software for visualization and further analysis.

PPI network analysis and hub gene selection. (A) PPI map of the 27 intersecting genes. Nodes correspond to proteins encoded by the selected genes, and edges denote predicted protein–protein associations. The network was generated using the STRING database and rendered in Cytoscape, highlighting the interaction landscape among candidate proteins. (B) Upset plot summarizing the overlap of top‐ranked genes obtained from six cytoHubba ranking methods (MCC, MNC, Degree, EPC, Closeness, and Radiality).

Using the cytoHubba plugin, six distinct topological algorithms—MCC, MNC, Degree, EPC, Closeness, and Radiality—were applied to rank the importance of genes within the network. The top 10 genes from each algorithm were identified, and the intersection of these six gene sets yielded eight hub genes, including S100A12, FCGR1A, IL18RAP, FCER1G, IL1R2, IL18R1, FCGR1B, and BST1 (Figure 4B), which were considered core candidates for further investigation.

Identification of Disease‐Associated Feature Genes by Two Machine Learning Methods

To identify representative diagnostic feature genes from the eight hub candidates, two machine learning algorithms were employed. First, LASSO regression was performed using the “glmnet” package in R. Based on cross‐validation, the optimal model was obtained at λ = 0.0029, which yielded six feature genes (Figure 5A,B).

Subsequently, SVM–RFE was conducted using the “e1071” package in R. The model achieved the minimum classification error when the number of selected features was four, resulting in four candidate genes (Figure 5C).

By intersecting the results from LASSO and SVM‐RFE, three overlapping genes—FCER1G, IL1R2, and BST1—were identified (Figure 5D). These genes were considered robust diagnostic biomarkers and were selected for further functional and therapeutic evaluation.

Correlation Between Biomarkers and Immune Cell Infiltration

To investigate the relationship between identified biomarkers and the immune microenvironment in sepsis, we applied the CIBERSORT algorithm to estimate immune cell infiltration levels across samples. The analysis revealed that nine immune cell types exhibited significantly different infiltration levels between the sepsis and control groups (Figure 6A), suggesting an altered immune landscape in sepsis.

Furthermore, Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess the association between the expression levels of the three candidate biomarkers and the infiltration scores of 22 immune cell types. The results indicated that these biomarkers were significantly correlated with the majority of immune cell populations, implying their potential involvement in immune regulation during the pathogenesis of sepsis (Figure 6B).

GSEA Identifies Inflammation‐Related Pathways Associated With Biomarkers

To explore the functional roles of the three candidate biomarkers in sepsis, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis was performed using GSEA_4.2.2 software. Samples were stratified into high‐ and low‐expression groups based on the median expression level of each gene. The HALLMARK gene sets were used as the reference set, and a FDR < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The results showed that high expression of all three biomarkers was significantly associated with enrichment in the INFLAMMATORY RESPONSE pathway (Figure 7A–C). Additionally, BST1 and FCER1G high‐expression groups exhibited significant enrichment in the TNFA_SIGNALING_VIA_NFKB pathway, a classic NF‐κB–mediated inflammatory signaling cascade known to play a central role in the pathogenesis of sepsis. These findings suggest that the identified biomarkers may contribute to sepsis by modulating key inflammation‐related pathways.

Experimental Validation in THP‐1 Cells

To corroborate the in silico findings, we validated the three candidate biomarkers (FCER1G, IL1R2, BST1) in a THP‐1 sepsis‐like model (1 μg/mL LPS, 24 h). qRT‐PCR showed increased mRNA levels of all three genes versus untreated control (Figure 8A). For mechanistic interrogation, we focused on BST1 and generated knockdown cells using two independent shRNAs; both reduced BST1 expression, with shBST1#1 achieving the strongest decrease at the mRNA and protein levels (Figure 8B,C). Functionally, LPS reduced cell viability, whereas BST1 knockdown partially rescued proliferation across 0–72 h (p < 0.0001; Figure 8D). ELISA further demonstrated that LPS robustly elevated IL‐6, TNF‐α, and IL‐1β, and these increases were significantly attenuated in shBST1 cells compared with sh‐NC (Figure 8E). Consistently, Annexin V/PI flow cytometry indicated that LPS increased apoptosis, while BST1 silencing lowered the apoptotic fraction (Figure 8F). Together, these data confirm the transcriptional signal and support BST1 as a driver of LPS‐induced inflammatory injury in THP‐1 cells.

Drug Prediction and Molecular Docking Validation

To identify potential therapeutic compounds targeting the identified biomarkers, we performed drug enrichment analysis using the Enrichr package in R, with reference to the DSigDB database. Given the stringent safety considerations in pharmacotherapy, we further refined candidate compounds based on existing literature. Artesunate, an antimalarial agent with reported use, was selected for molecular docking [36].

For docking analysis, the crystal structure of BST1 was used as the receptor protein. AutoDockTools was employed to assess binding affinity and molecular interactions. The results showed a binding energy of −6.27 kcal/mol between Artesunate and BST1, suggesting a stable interaction. In addition, the bond lengths of all hydrogen bonds fall within the reasonable range of 1.5–3.5 Å (Figure 9), supporting the structural plausibility of Artesunate as a candidate therapeutic agent for sepsis.

Discussion

Sepsis is characterized by abrupt onset, marked heterogeneity, and high mortality, with a disproportionate burden in low‐ and middle‐income countries. Current diagnostic approaches centered on blood culture and biomarkers such as CRP/PCT have suboptimal sensitivity and specificity, limiting early recognition and risk stratification [37, 38]. In this study, we combined systems‐level discovery (WGCNA to prioritize sepsis‐associated modules, differential expression to define robust signal, immune deconvolution by CIBERSORT, and pathway interrogation by GSEA) with feature selection (LASSO and SVM‐RFE) to converge on a three‐gene axis—FCER1G, IL1R2, and BST1. The signature aligned with innate/inflammatory programs enriched at the transcriptome level (including TNF/NF‐κB–related pathways) and with shifts in the inferred immune landscape. Therapeutic exploration via enrichment against drug signatures and structure‐based docking further suggested small molecules with favorable binding energetics to the targets. Closing the loop experimentally, we validated the transcriptional upregulation of all three genes in an LPS‐induced THP‐1 model and, focusing mechanistically on BST1, showed that shRNA knockdown (confirmed by qRT‐PCR and Western blot) tempered the LPS‐evoked cytokine output without compromising viability or inducing apoptosis. Together, these multilayer results nominate FCER1G/IL1R2/BST1 as biologically coherent and potentially translational markers for sepsis.

As the signaling adaptor for Fc receptors, FCER1G (FcRγ) functions not only in adaptive immunity but also directly in innate immune recognition and phagocytic responses to pathogens. Recent reviews have suggested that several Fc receptors can interact with bacteria independently of immunoglobulin ligands, thereby acting as innate immune receptors in infection and sepsis [39]. A 2024 Nature Communications study further demonstrated in mucosal ILC3s that FcεR1γ stabilizes partner membrane proteins and promotes anti‐infective immunity, mechanistically aligning with our observations of inflammatory enrichment and immune‐cell infiltration in sepsis samples [40]. In addition, multi‐cohort bioinformatic studies of adult sepsis have repeatedly nominated FCER1G as a diagnostic/subtyping candidate, indicating a conserved role in systemic inflammatory responses [41, 42]. IL1R2, a “decoy receptor” within the IL‐1 pathway, has been consistently implicated in the immune dysregulation of sepsis: integrated high‐dimensional flow cytometry and transcriptomic analyses show selective upregulation of IL1R2 in patient monocytes, associated with disease severity and markers of immunosuppression [43]; mechanistic work at cellular and animal levels indicates that IL1R2 can attenuate cytokine storm and improve survival by restraining glycolysis/pyroptosis (the ENO1–GSDMD axis) [44]. Contemporary reviews have accordingly proposed IL1R2 as a target for precision intervention in sepsis [45]. Regarding BST1, this adhesion/migration‐related NAD‐metabolizing ectoenzyme and receptor is predominantly expressed in myeloid and endothelial lineages and regulates neutrophil transendothelial migration [46]. In infection‐ and inflammation‐related disorders, BST1 contributes to neutrophil adhesion, migration, and NET‐associated processes; pediatric sepsis studies have also included BST1 among robust diagnostic candidates, suggesting a reproducible signal in childhood systemic inflammation [47, 48]. These external data accord with our findings of inflammation/NF‐κB enrichment and immune‐infiltration correlations, providing biological plausibility for the three genes in early‐life anti‐infective responses.

Cross‐disease evidence links all three genes to the tumor immune microenvironment (TME), particularly to the infiltration and functional states of myeloid cells. In pan‐cancer analyses and tissue/cohort studies of clear cell renal cell carcinoma, high FCER1G expression associates with increased macrophage infiltration, activation of immune‐related pathways, and altered outcomes [49, 50, 51]. IL1R2 has been shown to shape an immunosuppressive TME via cancer‐associated fibroblast (CAF)‐mediated regulation of MHC‐II and PD‐L1, augmenting Treg infiltration [52]; blockade of IL1R2 mitigates immunosuppression and can synergize with immune checkpoint pathways in preclinical models [53]. BST1 has been associated across multiple solid and hematologic malignancies with tumor cell adhesion, invasion, and poorer prognosis, and it participates in endothelium–immune crosstalk and leukocyte diapedesis—processes that naturally modulate immune‐cell infiltration patterns [54]. Together with our sepsis observations linking the three genes to myeloid/lymphoid composition and inflammatory signaling, these data suggest that an “immunity–adhesion–inflammation” axis may represent a shared program bridging acute infection and chronic tumor‐associated inflammation: by tuning the recruitment, activation, and effector functions of innate immune cells, this axis reshapes the immunologic makeup and inflammatory tone of tissues, thereby influencing clinical outcomes. Such cross‐context concordance strengthens the case for these genes as immune phenotype sensors and potential intervention points.

Our work has both strengths and limitations. First, all bioinformatics analyses were based solely on a single neonatal dataset and lacked external independent validation; therefore, the study cannot provide evidence for sepsis in other age groups. Future studies should conduct external validation across multiple independent adult and pediatric sepsis cohorts to assess the cross‐population diagnostic performance of the three genetic biomarkers. Second, the experimental validation focused solely on in‐depth functional studies of BST1 knockdown, while FCER1G and IL1R2 were assessed only at the transcriptional level; their functional contributions were not verified in the same model. In future studies, FCER1G and IL1R2 knockdown or overexpression cell lines should be established separately to systematically examine their effects on inflammatory factors, apoptosis, and proliferation. Third, the current data do not clarify whether FCER1G, IL1R2, and BST1 exert synergistic or combined effects in sepsis. Future studies should design multi‐gene co‐intervention experiments (such as dual knockdown or combined drug treatment) and combine them with co‐expression network disruption to elucidate the functional relationships among these three genes. Fourth, the molecular docking prediction of the binding between artesunate and BST1 is based solely on computational models and lacks functional validation. Future studies should verify physical binding through surface plasmon resonance (SPR) or cell‐based thermal displacement assays, and evaluate its pharmacological efficacy at the cellular and animal levels. Finally, the in vitro experiments utilized only the THP‐1 monocyte cell line, which cannot fully represent the complex in vivo immune environment. Future studies should conduct further validation using primary human monocytes, neutrophils, and animal sepsis models. Through the systematic implementation of the above measures, the true value of FCER1G, IL1R2, and BST1 as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets for sepsis can be more comprehensively evaluated.

Conclusion

This study integrates systems‐level bioinformatics with cell‐based validation to nominate a compact three‐gene axis—FCER1G, IL1R2, and BST1—as relevant to innate immune activation in sepsis. Using a pipeline that combines WGCNA, differential expression (limma), immune deconvolution (CIBERSORT), pathway analysis (GSEA), and feature selection (LASSO/SVM‐RFE), we defined a coherent signature and linked it to drug‐repurposing hypotheses via enrichment and molecular docking. In an LPS‐induced THP‐1 model, qRT‐PCR confirmed upregulation of all three genes, and shRNA‐mediated BST1 knockdown—validated by qRT‐PCR and Western blot—attenuated IL‐6, TNF‐α, and IL‐1β release without compromising viability or increasing apoptosis. Collectively, these findings position BST1, alongside FCER1G and IL1R2, as promising candidates for biomarker development and targeted modulation in sepsis, motivating external validation in multicenter cohorts and primary cells, as well as functional testing of predicted modulators.
