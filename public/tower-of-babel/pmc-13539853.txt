Updated ENIGMA recommendations for reporting germline variants in cancer susceptibility genes and their translation into twenty languages
Authors: Arcangela De Nicolo, Diana M Eccles, Kirsimari Aaltonen, Pia Alhopuro, Sarah Louise Ariansen, Michela Biancolella, Sandrine M Caputo, Olivier Caron, Pietro Cavalli, Jianbang Chiang, Kathleen B M Claes, Edgar Christian S Cuaresma, Miguel de la Hoya, Antoine De Pauw, Orland Díez, Mev Domínguez-Valentin, Hans Ehrencrona, Magnhild K Fjeldvær, Florentia Fostira, Marie Belle D Francia, Javier Galego-Carro, Encarna B Gómez García, Nur Tiara Hassan, Jan Hauke, Akira Hirasawa, Xin Huang, Elaine Marisse H Ilagan-Cargullo, Issei Imoto, Sowmya Jonnagadla, Manasadevi Karthikeyan, Petra Kleiblova, Irene Konstantopoulou, Artur Kowalik, Anders Kvist, Fabienne Lesueur, Shao-Tzu Li, Adrià López-Fernández, Eva Machackova, Alexandra Martins, Arjen R Mensenkamp

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenetic testing for cancer susceptibility underpins precision cancer prevention and care. Gaps in the healthcare providers’ genetic literacy and an ambiguous lexicon for variant description may hinder proper delivery and clinical application of consistently trustworthy test results. The Evidence-based Network for the Interpretation of Germline Mutant Alleles (ENIGMA) international consortium supports controlled terminology and recommends a framework for reporting germline variants in cancer susceptibility genes, using breast cancer as an exemplar. Moving forward towards terminological coherence across disciplines and borders, the ENIGMA Clinical Working Group launched a multinational effort to release consortium-approved translations of the published recommendations. The herein reported Vocabulary Translation Project offered an opportunity to reappraise and align the reference text to the recent BRCA1 and BRCA2 specifications to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology rules by the ENIGMA Variant Curation Expert Panel and to highlight country-specific differences in breast cancer risk assessment and management. The updated recommendations and their 20 translations are now provided as easy to handle documents, covering 11 of the most widely spoken languages in the world. They will contribute to minimised erroneous inferences, more informed decision-making, improved health outcomes and equity in the use of genetic testing for cancer predisposition and in translational oncology.

Precision cancer prevention and care is a rapidly growing discipline in the field of medicine.1 In the last decade, genetic testing for cancer susceptibility in high-risk individuals (and families) has been implemented in many countries worldwide. The advent and widespread application of massively parallel sequencing technologies, including multigene panels,2 has further contributed to large-scale DNA testing for cancer diagnosis, treatment and risk prediction. Notably, cancer is a heterogeneous disease, characterised by a complex genetic architecture encompassing both somatic and germline (constitutional) variants; for breast cancer, the latter were categorised based on the level of their associated (high, moderate or low) cancer risks.3 Nevertheless, the terminology employed to describe these germline variants largely derives from a lexicon that is traditionally used for genetic diseases with classical Mendelian inheritance. This often leads to major communication hurdles among the differently specialised healthcare professionals, who are involved in the identification, counselling and clinical management of at-risk individuals, and it can make the conversation between providers and carriers of disease-associated variants challenging.

To address this issue, the Evidence-based Network for the Interpretation of Germline Mutant Alleles (ENIGMA) international consortium (https://enigmaconsortium.org/) published a position statement that included recommendations on how to report variants in cancer predisposition genes, focusing on breast cancer.4 The effort was prompted by the ENIGMA members’ awareness that the terms used to report gene variants may have different meanings in multiple contexts and may be used interchangeably, with potential for erroneous inferences and thus inappropriate clinical interventions (see supplemental table 1 in 4). Incorrect interpretation of the clinical actionability of sequence variants can lead to their improper use as risk biomarkers to guide clinical decision-making and risk-reduction measures.5 6 A critical issue pertains to bioinformatic prediction tools, which often describe the effect of sequence variants on protein function using the very same terms that are employed to define the association of variants with cancer risk.4 7

The recommendations included in the ENIGMA position article were compiled and published in English.4 However, as acknowledged by the compilers themselves, identical problems of misinterpretation and miscommunication occur also in other languages. As a step forward towards consolidated terminology for variant reporting, and to avoid further heterogeneity (and potential misunderstandings) caused by non-rigorous translations of the proposed English lexicon, the ENIGMA Clinical Working Group launched an initiative aimed at translating the published recommendations into the various languages spoken by the international membership. First developed into an ENIGMA study concept in 2020, the Vocabulary Translation Project evolved over time in its scope to accommodate an increasingly important update and alignment step (as described below), reaching its full maturity and final shape in 2024.

We established 20 teams covering the languages listed in box 1. All teams but one (see box 1 footnote for specifics) included three to four individuals with expertise in clinical and/or molecular genetics. In total, 70 clinicians and researchers, based in 25 countries across four continents at the time of the translation, participated in the project.

As a first step, through collegial discussions and electronic surveys, we reached a majority consensus on the sections of the original position article4 that warranted translation. We aimed at a concise, easy to handle document, which eventually included: (a) a glossary of terms and general recommendations for describing and reporting variants in cancer predisposition genes; (b) the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC)8 and American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP)9 five-tier schemes for disease gene variant classification; (c) the recommended terminology and descriptors for five-tier disease gene variant classification, considering variant pathogenicity in the multigene panel testing era and (d) suggestions for multitier reporting of cancer gene variants conferring high or moderate disease risk. We collated (a) based on excerpted passages and we derived sections (b)–(d) from tables 2-4 of the source article.4

At the time of the original publication, ENIGMA served as an external panel for Clinical Genome Resource (ClinGen, https://clinicalgenome.org/), providing classifications based on the original in-house ENIGMA quantitative and qualitative criteria, which followed the IARC terminology and class assignment8 (https://enigmaconsortium.org/enigma-classification-criteria/, ENIGMA rules v.2.5.1 29 June 2017). ENIGMA has, since, sought and received approval as a Food and Drug Administration-aligned ClinGen internal Variant Curation Expert Panel (VCEP) for BRCA1 and BRCA2. The VCEP now follows ClinGen ACMG/AMP guidelines and it has released BRCA1 and BRCA2 specifications to the ACMG/AMP rules (https://cspec.genome.network/cspec/ui/svi/affiliation/50087), available in the ClinGen Criteria Specification Registry. We used the opportunity of this translation work to update the excerpts from the published recommendations4 and ensure their alignment with the current ClinGen-approved variant classification framework.10

The 20 teams worked independently on the assigned tasks via a two-step process, namely translation followed by back-translation—the latter serving as a verification step, by team members who were not involved in the translation, to confirm proper rendition of the original text/concepts. Our choice to opt for a so-structured, non-automated translation exercise proved useful to highlight concepts and sections that were particularly challenging, transversely across languages or in a language-specific manner, and it offered an invaluable opportunity for lively discussions, and for reappraisal and improvement of the reference text.

Specifically, trans-national issues prompted revision of the excerpts to emphasise that the original article used breast cancer as an exemplar, with thresholds of risk categories derived from Easton et al,11 and actionability per National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines-based risk stratification (https://www.nice.org.uk/Guidance/CG164)—we note that the NICE guidelines were chosen as a model because they explicitly link (cancer) risk to clinical action, helping to illustrate the importance of clarity about risk when genetic variants are interpreted and reported. This clarification was deemed necessary considering that breast cancer risk stratification cut-offs and management may vary depending on the guidelines used. Moreover, for other cancer types, the same relative risk may entail different actionability, depending on the absolute risk and the severity of the disease. Furthermore, we now specify (online supplemental appendix 1, glossary) that the terms risk and penetrance were used interchangeably in the reporting template of the original publication. We also point out that the terms penetrant/penetrance are preferred to indicate the probability of disease occurrence when reporting cancer predisposing variants in isolation, that is, when additional factors that contribute to cancer risk (other than gender and age) are not accounted for.

Country-specific differences in breast cancer risk assessment (eg, different absolute/relative breast cancer risk cut points) and management were noted by the translators. Moreover, even though we asked that the translated text be otherwise as faithful as possible to the reference source, certain concepts required language-specific elaboration for proper rendition, and, occasionally, sentences were rephrased or words inserted to underscore the translational slant of the effort. Any such adjustment(s), when considered major, were also marked as notes of the translators.

Online supplemental appendix 1 includes a glossary of terms and tables from the published article selected for translation, with amendments and corrigenda to the original English text made to comply with the current recommendations10 and to address any issues that emerged during the translation process. All 20 translations, available as online supplemental 20 translations, reflect this revised version of the reference source. On completion of the translation process, via electronic survey, we asked the participating teams to elaborate on the perceived significance and anticipated clinical utility of the translations. Overall, the feedback we collated recognises a high value of the translated recommendations for the wide medical community and for patients, and an expected positive impact on more consistent clinical management of carrier individuals, regardless of the English proficiency of the local, scientific or general, population.

We used multiple-choice questions to inquire about the outreach strategies (How do you plan to use/promote the use of the translation in your country?) and the target audience (Whom do you envision as the ideal target audience/users of this translation in your country?). The delineated outreach programmes include the use of the translations for education and training purposes (75%), as well as dissemination via local (ie, institutional, including websites), regional and especially national (eg, scientific societies, consortia, working groups) networks (35%, 25% and 70%, respectively) (figure 1A). The identified target audience encompasses primarily physicians of other specialties who participate in multidisciplinary teams, but also medical geneticists, genetic counsellors and other professionals involved in the counselling process, primary care physicians, members of diagnostic laboratories, as well as non-healthcare professionals, for example, journalists and science communicators (figure 1B).

Fostered terminological coherence will be especially relevant to health professionals who might not be well acquainted with cancer genetics and/or work in community settings.12 13 It will also facilitate mutual understanding and exchange among providers from different disciplines and help individuals who carry variants in cancer susceptibility genes to gain clearer knowledge of the risks and clinical consequences associated with their status and to appreciate the value of report updating.14 This will, in turn, guide tested individuals and their blood relatives towards more appropriate choices and ensure better adherence to (national or international) guidelines.13

Pitfalls in sequence variant interpretation and communication have been reported as a potential cause of incorrect or missed diagnoses, unnecessary treatments and interventions, heightened anxiety and distress for patients and family members.13 Ideally, in countries where English is not the official language used for written and/or verbal communication with patients, the documents herein presented could improve reporting of test results on cancer predisposition. This would ultimately lead to more consistent translation of genetic testing findings into the most clinically appropriate cancer prevention and care.

Considering the estimated ~2.2 billion native speakers of the 20 languages covered by this project, along with the recorded ~400 million English native speakers (Ethnologue, https://www.ethnologue.com, editions 22–24), the updated ENIGMA recommendations for reporting germline variants in cancer susceptibility genes and their translations could altogether benefit a wide audience of about one third of the current ~8.2 billion global population (United Nations, World Population Prospects, https://population.un.org/wpp/). Moreover, because our collection covers 11 of the 45 most widely spoken languages in the world (Ethnologue, edition 25), including English, Spanish, French and Portuguese, which are (co)official languages in a number of countries and territories, the reach of this project may extend to an even greater proportion of the world population (figure 1C).

By overcoming the language barriers across disciplines and countries, the translated controlled vocabulary and framework are provided here to advance towards more equity in the use of genetic testing for cancer predisposition and in (access to) translational oncology and precision medicine. We hope that harmonised communication of germline genetic test results will contribute to reduced misdiagnoses and to more coherent clinical management, eventually improving health outcomes. In keeping with the original report, the updated recommendations and their translations are not intended to provide guidance for clinical treatments. The latter remains an oncologist’s decision, which our documents may assist by ensuring correct reporting of the detected variants and associated risks.

As previously stated,4 because knowledge of the genetic bases of cancer predisposition is constantly evolving, and so is the related lexicon used for description, we predict that our recommendations (and their translations) will require periodic revision and amendment. In this regard, the use of ‘atypical penetrance’ is currently under consideration for a more general description of any variant displaying disease penetrance features that may be actionable, yet distinct from those of average pathogenic variants in that disease gene (eg, as described in references 15 16).

We used breast cancer as a blueprint. Future iterations of this exercise might extend to include other cancer types, on proper calibration of the cancer-specific absolute and relative risks. The latter direction, along with a focus on the evolving terminology and communication issues related to the interpretation and actionability of reduced penetrance variants, aligns with the mission of the ENIGMA Clinical Working Group and will be pursued under its umbrella. Because we expect that the ENIGMA consortium membership, which currently comprises 221 officially enrolled affiliates from 37 countries (as of 31 December 2025), will continue to expand, it is likely and desirable that an exercise like ours will encompass additional languages in the future.
