Unveiling the emission mechanism in analog-doped carbazole-based organic afterglow materials
Authors: Zesen Lin, Peng Lan, Zheng Yin, Xiaochun Fan, Jie Kong, Xianhe Zhang, Wei Zhang, Rengo Yoshioka, Xun Tang, Zhu Wu, Youichi Tsuchiya, Ryota Kabe, Meng Zhou, Chihaya Adachi, Bin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Afterglow from purely organic solids has drawn increasing attention over the past decade. In carbazole-based systems, recent studies revised earlier aggregation-state explanations and implicated trace carbazole analog impurities as the origin. However, the widely assumed charge-trapping mechanism cannot account for exponential decay kinetics, lacks robust experimental validation and leaves some photophysical features unexplained. Here we establish the mechanism by revisiting two representative series of carbazole–analog host–guest systems. Quantitative spectral decomposition and comparative photophysics reveal that the afterglow is dominated by guest phosphorescence, accompanied by an extremely weak power-law long-persistent luminescence. The analog guests are confirmed to act as deep triplet exciton traps and shallow hole traps, the latter of which can enhance hole-transport-type long-persistent luminescence. These insights clarify previously unresolved mechanisms and establish a unified framework for luminescence that integrates energy transfer, exciton diffusion, and three afterglow pathways (delayed fluorescence, room-temperature phosphorescence and long-persistent luminescence), offering design principles for brighter and more persistent purely organic afterglow materials.

Introduction

In recent years, the afterglow phenomenon of organic solids, encompassing thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF)1–3, room-temperature phosphorescence (RTP)4–10, and organic long-persistent luminescence (OLPL)11–18, has opened a new frontier in luminescence research. Conventional wisdom holds that in purely organic solids, long-lived excitons are highly susceptible to various emission quenching processes (collectively referred to as aggregation-caused quenching, ACQ), making afterglow at room temperature nearly unobservable19. Over the past decade, growing reports have described ultralong RTP from purely organic solids20–26, notably in carbazole (Cz) derivatives5,27–30. However, recent work revealed that such RTP often originates from trace Cz analog impurities31–33. Similar impurity-induced afterglow has been confirmed in other organic systems, e.g. derivatives of dibenzofuran, dibenzothiophene, triphenylamine, and boronate esters34–40. These findings prompted a re-examination of the true origin of afterglow in purely organic solids and suggested that analog doping is a general and important mechanism for organic afterglow41–44.

Afterglow in doped organic solids (host–guest systems) generally follows two mechanisms (Fig. 1). The first is the emissive triplet-exciton trapping mechanism (triplet confinement), where low-concentration guests with much lower energies of the first triplet (T1) state than those of the hosts (ΔETT ≥ 0.5 eV) confine triplets (Fig. 1a)45,46. This prevents triplet migration to quenching sites and yields exponential decays similar to those of isolated molecules in a rigid matrix. The second is the carrier trapping-detrapping mechanism (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Note 1, and Supplementary Fig. 1): guests act as deep electron or hole traps of hosts when the offset in either the lowest-unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) or the highest-occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) (ΔELUMO or ΔEHOMO) is ≥ 0.8 eV11,12,14–16,18,49. Photoexcited excitons may dissociate, forming geminate pairs where one carrier is trapped on the guest and the opposite carrier on other traps in the host, giving long-lived charge-separated states (CSS). Unlike excitons with intrinsic decay pathways, separated charges are not true excited states and can only disappear through recombination; consequently, charge-separated states can persist far longer than excitons. Charge recombination requires thermal detrapping, and the distribution of trap depths leads to power-law decays, analogous to inorganic long-persistent luminescence (LPL)50. Neat organic solids also contain intrinsic traps (tail states, defects), but because most separated charges recombine rapidly, the filling of deep traps is inefficient (Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Fig. 2), giving only weak LPL and thermally stimulated luminescence51–54. This weak LPL is hard to detect by conventional setups at room temperature (Supplementary Note 1, and Supplementary Fig. 2). In contrast, host–guest systems where the intermolecular charge transfer state (inter-CT) is the lowest excited state (Type-I OLPL) can facilitate efficient charge separation, suppress fast recombination of paired charges, and thus enhance trap filling to yield observable LPL11,15.

For Cz-derived host–guest solids, many recent studies have attributed the observed afterglow to long-lived CSS31,33,40,55,56. However, these decays are exponential and spectra dominated by guest phosphorescence, more consistent with confined triplets than CSS (Supplementary Note 1, Supplementary Fig. 3). Certain methodological limitations may also contribute to uncertainty. Some studies compared pristine and doped hosts without contrasting the guest behavior in rigid inert matrices31,34,55, or estimated LUMO levels from HOMO plus optical gaps without accounting for exciton binding energies31,57. The paramagnetic nature of triplet excitons58,59 may complicate interpretation of ESR signals, leaving ESR signals potentially misassigned to radicals42,60. Delayed fluorescence (DF) observed under intense excitation (e.g., 365 nm UV flashlights with power >10 mW cm⁻2) was occasionally attributed to TADF without detailed excitation-power- or temperature-dependent analyses43,55,57. Moreover, most reports treated the host–guest system as a simple two-molecule model, without fully considering exciton diffusion and multi-exciton processes, which may critically influence emission. Consequently, a quantitative, self-consistent model linking triplet confinement, carrier trapping, energy transfer, and multi-exciton dynamics to long-lived luminescence is still lacking.

Here we show that the afterglow in Cz-based analog host–guest solids is dominated by guest phosphorescence arising from deep triplet trapping, whereas carrier trapping-detrapping contributes only a minor LPL component. To establish this, we perform quantitative studies on two representative model series: the mCBP-host/analog-guest series (mCBP series), characterized by locally excited (LE) states, and the DPhCzT-host/analog-guest series (DPhCzT series), exhibiting intramolecular charge-transfer (intra-CT) character (Fig. 2a). Accurate HOMO/LUMO levels were measured under identical electrochemical conditions, and fluorescence/phosphorescence were compared with guest-doped polystyrene (PS) references to isolate host–guest-specific effects. Using a lab-built ultrasensitive setup, we observed a weak LPL tail emerging after the exponentially decaying afterglow under weak excitation conditions, confirming the minor role of CSS. Comparison of spectra obtained by transient absorption spectroscopy (TAS) and spectroelectrochemistry, which provides better distinguishability than ESR, verified that the exponential afterglow originates from deep triplet trapping rather than charge trapping-detrapping. Excitation-power- and temperature-dependent measurements clarified DF pathways. Together with quantitative analysis of elementary photophysical processes, energy transfer and exciton diffusion, and afterglow, we establish a self-consistent mechanistic framework for photo-induced emission in Cz derivatives. Results

Selection and redox properties of Cz-derived host/analog-guest systems

To clarify the emission mechanism, we built two representative families of Cz-based host/analog-guest pairs (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Note 2, Supplementary Figs. 4–7; Supplementary Tables 1, 2) that vary in three key dimensions—molecular structure, excited-state character, and solid-state phase. The guests were based on 1H-benzo[f]indole (Bd)31,32, and 5H-benzo[b]carbazole (BCz)33,56,57, both of which were isolated from commercial Cz and could induce afterglow when doped into Cz. The mCBP series represent amorphous LE-type systems, whereas the DPhCzT series comprise crystalline compounds with a pronounced intra-CT character. If similar photophysical behaviors arise in both host–guest and inert-matrix (e.g., PS) systems doped with Bd- and BCz-based guests, the resulting mechanism can be considered more universal rather than a consequence of the unique properties of the host-guest system. This design allows us to assess whether the observed afterglow mechanisms are influenced by molecular packing or phase and clarify the role of the intra-CT character. The mCBP series were prepared as melt-cast amorphous films (Supplementary Note 3.1, Supplementary Fig. 8), whereas the DPhCzT series were obtained as solution-grown crystalline powders.

The LUMO and HOMO values were obtained by differential-pulse voltammetry (DPV) conducted in rigorously de-aerated electrolytes (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Note 3.2, Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10). Cz-substituted compounds and their Bd- or BCz-substituted analogs display nearly identical LUMO levels (~ − 2.05 eV for mCBP series compounds, −2.61 eV for DPhCzT series compounds), consistent with the quantum-chemical calculations (Supplementary Fig. 5 in ref. 61. for mCBP; Supplementary Fig. 37 in ref. 31. for DPhCzT). In contrast, parent Cz possesses a shallower LUMO ( − 1.61 eV) than Bd ( − 1.89 eV) and BCz ( − 2.06 eV, Supplementary Fig. 9). Hence, the LUMO energy offset (ΔELUMO) between the Cz-derived host and analog guest is negligible (between −0.08 and 0.03 eV, Supplementary Table 3), indicating that the guest cannot function as an effective electron trap. Measuring all members of each series under identical conditions minimized systematic errors from the singlet exciton binding energy (Eb; Supplementary Table 4). Calculated Eb values reveal that Bd-based compounds possess much smaller Eb (~ 0.00 eV) than their Cz isomers (Cz: 0.24 eV; mCBP: 0.07 eV; DPhCzT: 0.20 eV; Supplementary Figs. 11–13; Supplementary Table 4).

In most host–guest pairs, the singlet energy difference, ΔESS, is <0.3 eV (Supplementary Table 3; Supplementary Figs. 14, 15), particularly for systems that use Bd derivatives as the guest. This indicates that the optical gaps between the host and guest are highly similar. Consequently, selective guest excitation is unfeasible at low concentrations. In contrast, the lowest triplet energy offset (ΔETT) always exceeds 0.5 eV, confirming that the guests act as deep triplet-exciton traps.

Comparison of the fundamental photophysical properties between the host-guest systems and corresponding guest-doped PS systems

To determine whether additional pathways, specifically inter-CT and CS, enhance the RTP of the host–guest systems, we compared their fundamental photophysical properties with those of PS films doped with the same guests (Fig. 3, Supplementary Figs. 16–28; and Supplementary Tables 5–9). In 1 wt%-doped PS films, the guest spectrum is almost identical to that in the dilute toluene solution, indicating that the molecules are essentially dispersed as isolated monomers within the PS matrix (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17). For the 1 mol% doped mCBP series, both fluorescence and phosphorescence originate almost exclusively from the guest, even under host-selective excitation ( ≤ 340 nm), and the spectra are virtually indistinguishable from those of the corresponding PS films (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Figs. 16a–f and 18). In contrast, the 1 mol% doped DPhCzT series show pronounced host–guest emission mixing: prompt fluorescence originates from both the host aggregate state and the guest molecule, while the phosphorescence spectrum matches that of the guest-doped PS film, indicating that RTP originates exclusively from the guest (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Figs. 16g–i, 19 and 20). The overall photoluminescence quantum yields remain modest (ΦPL < 35 %) and the phosphorescence quantum yields are very low (ΦPhos < 2 %) across all samples (Fig. 3g, h; Supplementary Note 3.5, Supplementary Tables 6–9). ΦPhos was estimated based on the spectral contribution of the phosphorescence component in the total photoluminescence (see Section 4.4, Supplementary Figs. 27–29, Supplementary Tables 6–9). In PS, DPhCzT analogs (DPhBdT and DPhBCzT) exhibit lower ΦPL ( < 10%) but higher ΦPhos (≈ 1.6%) than the LE-type mCBP analogs (mCzBdBP, mBBP, and mBCzBP) (ΦPL > 17%, ΦPhos < 0.6% for mCBP analogs; Supplementary Tables 6). This is consistent with the intersystem crossing (ISC) efficiencies of DPhCzT analogs possessing an intra-CT state being 3–4 times higher than those of the LE-type mCBP analogs (e.g., ΦISC: 2.2% for DPhBdT, 0.6% for mCzBdBP, Supplementary Table 8). However, in host–guest solids, ΦPhos values decreases by a factor of 1–6 compared with the PS films (Fig. 3g, h; Supplementary Table 10), indicating the presence of extra excited-state quenching pathways (kq). For instance, although DPhCzT as a host exhibits highly efficient ISC (kISC = 8.2 × 107 s-1; Supplementary Note 3.6, Supplementary Table 7; Supplementary Fig. 30a, b), the ΦPhos of DPhBdT and DPhBCzT in DPhCzT falls to just 20–30 % of that of the PS reference (Fig. 3h; and Supplementary Table 10). Thus, the hosts do not enhance ΦPhos of the guests relative to inert rigid matrices.

Overall, the host–guest systems (Supplementary Table 3) do not significantly improve guest RTP, and possible host aggregation (e.g., H-aggregates5) also shows no detectable contribution to RTP, such as prolongation of the lifetime or improvement in the quantum yield. However, the crystalline host–guest systems (DPhCzT series) retained visible RTP after prolonged air exposure, in contrast to the rapid quenching observed in the PS films (Supplementary Note 3.7, Supplementary Figs. 31, 32), largely due to the suppression of oxygen diffusion within the crystals.

High-sensitivity afterglow measurements and time-resolved absorption/emission spectroscopy

We first probed the afterglow of neat hosts (mCBP, DPhCzT) and host–guest solids (mCzBdBP/mCBP, mBCzBP/mCBP, DPhBdT/DPhCzT, DPhBCzT/DPhCzT) under single-photon excitation with our home-built, high-sensitivity setup (Fig. 4a,b, Supplementary Note 3.8, and Supplementary Figs. 33–36). The noise level was approximately 30 fW cm–2; consequently, any optical signal exceeding this threshold could be reliably detected. All neat hosts and host–guest systems exhibited weak LPL (Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Fig. 37). The LPL emission in host-guest systems emerged after a strong RTP that lasted for approximately 10 seconds. Because the excitation intensity was kept low (100 µW cm⁻²), the observed LPL occurred entirely under single-photon conditions. In contrast, the doped PS films show RTP only, with no detectable LPL. All guests have shallower HOMO levels than their hosts, suggesting hole-trapping ability. However, the LPL intensities of doped systems remain comparable to those of the neat hosts and show no significant dependence on the doping concentration (Fig. 4c). Therefore, the observed LPL is mainly attributed to charge separation within the host aggregate itself, with minor guest contribution, when the excitation wavelength falls within the absorption range of the hosts (Supplementary Fig. 38a, b). The weak guest contribution to LPL stems from two main factors. First, classical OLPL requires large host–guest offsets (ΔEHOMO or ΔELUMO ≥ 0.8 eV) to immobilize one type of charge carrier11,14–16,18. However, here ΔEHOMO and ΔELUMO are below 0.5 eV (Supplementary Table 3), so trapped carriers readily escape and recombine at room temperature, which reduces the population of long-lived, charge-separated states. Second, all systems exhibit pronounced guest phosphorescence, with a large singlet–triplet energy gap of the guest (ΔEST,guest > 0.5 eV, Supplementary Table 4, Supplementary Fig. 17), indicating that fewer excitons dissociate to populate host traps compared with classical inter-CT or TADF systems, thereby limiting LPL generation12,63.

To pinpoint the true metastable species influencing the millisecond-to-second exponential afterglow, we compared the millisecond-scale excited-state absorption (ESA) spectra of the analog-doped solids with (i) the radical-ion spectra of hosts and guests obtained electrochemically and (ii) triplet absorption features captured by nanosecond TAS (ns-TAS; Fig. 4d, e; and Supplementary Figs. 39–47). No characteristic radical-ion peaks are detected in the ms-TAS spectra of any of the host–guest systems (e.g., 1.4 eV peak for mCzBdBP•+, 1.7 eV peak for DPhBdT•+, 1.8 eV peak for DPhCzT•−; Fig. 4c, d, and Supplementary Figs. 40–47). The absence of CSS signals likely stems from their extremely low population, rendering them undetectable by TAS. Instead, the ms-TAS spectra of all host–guest systems closely match those of the corresponding guest-doped PS films, with the ESA bands aligning with the guest triplet signatures observed on the nanosecond timescale (Fig. 4d, e and Supplementary Fig. 39). Additionally, the lifetimes of the ms-ESA match the phosphorescence lifetimes (Supplementary Note 3.9, Supplementary Figs. 40–47). These observations collectively demonstrate that the guest-molecule triplet state is responsible for the exponential afterglow, rather than the CSS.

The presence of inter-CT states can enhance ISC without compromising the radiative rate of localized triplet states63. Therefore, we assessed whether pronounced inter-CT populations exist in our host–guest solids by combining time-resolved emission spectra (TRES; Fig. 4f, g, and Supplementary Figs. 18–20) with femtosecond TAS (fs-TAS; Fig. 5 and Supplementary Figs. 48, 49). For the DPhCzT-based host–guest series (Fig. 4f, g), TRES cleanly resolves aggregated host fluorescence, guest prompt fluorescence, guest DF, and guest RTP, but shows no discernible inter-CT emission. Fs-TAS control experiments comparing the neat host, host-guest blends (1 mol% and 20 mol%), and guest-doped PS references (1 wt%) reveal no signals attributable to inter-CT species. Instead, the results provide clear evidence of host-to-guest energy transfer (Fig. 5; and Supplementary Figs. 48, 50, 51). Taking the mBBP/mCBP system as an example, the 20 mol% system exhibits an excited state absorption (ESA) band near 500 nm, attributed to the guest’s lowest singlet excited state (S1). This signal rises within 0.1–3 ps concomitantly with the decay of the host ESA, whereas a similar spectral feature in the 1 mol% mBBP/mCBP sample emerges only after ~100 ps. To disentangle these coupling signals, singular-value decomposition (SVD) followed by multivariate curve resolution alternating least squares (MCR-ALS) analysis64 was performed, revealing only two clear signal components (Supplementary Fig. 50). The associated spectra indicate that the first component is characteristic of the host S1 absorption, whereas the second is consistent with the guest S1 absorption (Supplementary Figs. 50b, c). Notably, the concentration profiles show that the decay of the host component over the 1–100 ps time window is accompanied by a concomitant rise of the guest component, a kinetic signature characteristic of energy transfer (Supplementary Figs. 50d, e). Moreover, as inter-CT states are typically associated with radical-ion absorption features, the absence of such signatures in the fs-TAS data further excludes the involvement of inter-CT processes in the present system (Supplementary Fig. 51).

These observations indicate that the contribution of inter-CT processes is negligible, which is consistent with the ΔEG–interCT values summarized in Supplementary Table 3. When ΔEG–interCT is a negative value (<−0.1 eV; e.g., mBCzBP/mCBP and DPhBCzT/DPhCzT), the lowest singlet excited state (S1) of the system is localized on the guest molecule, and no inter-CT processes are detectable. When ΔEG–interCT is nearly zero (e.g. mCzBdBP/mCBP, DPhBdT/DPhCzT), a rapid equilibrium is established between the guest S1 (S1,G) and inter-CT states. Given that emission from inter-CT states is typically slower than that from locally excited singlet states, the inter-CT excitons preferentially relax to the S1,G state. Consequently, the population of inter-CT states remains extremely low and thus spectroscopically unresolvable under our experimental conditions.

Analysis of delayed fluorescence in the Cz-derived host/analog-guest systems

In current studies of organic solid-state RTP materials, researchers often use high-power 365 nm UV flashlights ( > 10 mW cm⁻2) to preliminarily screen for visible RTP emission, with the corresponding afterglow spectra recorded by CCD. As a result, in addition to RTP, a long-lived DF signal is often observed. This DF is typically attributed to a triplet–triplet annihilation (TTA) process. In our mCBP host–guest systems, where large ΔEST ( > 0.6 eV) excludes TADF, the DF indeed follows TTA characteristics: bimolecular kinetics with superlinear excitation intensity dependence at low excitation power (ideal slope = 2 in a log–log plot; Fig. 6a, b, and Supplementary Note 3.10, Supplementary Figs. 52, 53). However, due to additional quenching processes in organic solids, such as singlet–singlet annihilation (SSA) or triplet–polaron annihilation (TPA), the slope in the log–log plot often deviates from 2, resulting in an observed slope of ~1.5 (Fig. 6b)65,66. Since RTP is a single-photon process, the relative contributions of RTP and TTA-DF to the afterglow spectrum at a fixed time delay depend on excitation intensity: RTP dominates at low power, whereas the rapidly rising TTA-DF prevails at higher power (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 52a, b). In contrast, the DF observed in the DPhCzT series is neither TTA-DF nor TADF, because it shows a single-photon characteristic (Fig. 6c, d and Supplementary Fig. 52c, d), shows no thermal activation behavior (Fig. 6e), and is guest concentration-independent (Supplementary Fig. 55). This DF is also not the recombination fluorescence (RF) of photoinduced geminate pairs67, as no distinct CSS was observed in the prior ps–ns TAS spectra. In organic solids, RF is essentially equivalent to LPL: both involve charge separation and typically show power-law decay67, because the distribution of HOMO, LUMO, and trap levels is broad rather than discrete in disordered materials52, unlike ideal single crystals at low temperature. The distinction is mainly semantic: LPL usually refers to afterglow lasting beyond a second, whereas RF denotes DF in the μs–ms range. However, the DF of the DPhCzT series follows a power law only up to ~0.1 s before switching to exponential decay (Figs. 4f and 6c), indicating that this DF does not originate from charge separation.

With these possibilities excluded, we attribute the DF of the DPhCzT series to a phosphorescence resonance energy transfer (PRET) process from the host T1 state to the guest S1 state (Supplementary Fig. 56a) for the reasons outlined below68–71. (i) The DPhCzT series possess efficient PRET (e.g., efficiency ΦPRET = 55.4%, rate constant kPRET = 0.24 s-1 for 1 mol% DPhBdT/DPhCzT, Supplementary Tables 11, 12), with the Förster integral for PRET (JPRET) about one order of magnitude smaller than the corresponding integrals of fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET; JFRET), in contrast to the mCBP-based systems where JPRET is three orders of magnitude smaller than JFRET (Supplementary Fig. 56b; and Supplementary Table 11). (ii) The DF follows a power law in the μs–ms range but becomes exponential beyond ~0.1 s. This can be readily explained by the dependence of kPRET on the concentration of triplet excitons on the host, which influences the distance between the excited host and guest molecules, r. Immediately after excitation, r follows a normal distribution, so PRET-DF initially exhibits power-law behavior. Hosts with smaller r values have larger kPRET, leading to preferential triplet transfer to the guest. As host triplets decay, the remaining triplets are farther from the guest with a narrower r distribution, yielding exponential decay characteristics for DF on longer timescales. (iii) Because both PRET and FRET originate from the same pool of excited host molecules, their initial energy donor–acceptor distance (r) distributions are identical. Therefore, the ratio of their rate constants calculated from the average distance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\langle r\right\rangle$$\end{document}r), kFRET / kPRET, remains constant (e.g., 5.8 × 109 for the DPhBdT/DPhCzT pair) and is independent of guest concentration (Supplementary Table 12), explaining the concentration-independent DF-to-RTP intensity ratio in the DPhCzT series.

Analysis of energy transfer processes

The steady-state photoluminescence spectra evolve systematically with increasing analog guest concentration: the host fluorescence diminishes, while the guest fluorescence (including DF) and guest phosphorescence increase correspondingly (Fig. 7a,b, Supplementary Figs. 28 and 29). This reciprocal spectral evolution is consistent with efficient host-to-guest energy transfer (Supplementary Fig. 56e). Quantitative distance analysis shows that at 1 wt% guest doping, the calculated average donor-acceptor distance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left\langle r\right\rangle$$\end{document}r (mCzBdBP/mCBP: 1.02 nm; DPhBdT/DPhCzT: 0.71 nm. Supplementary Table 12) remains smaller than the Förster radius (R0. mCzBdBP/mCBP: 1.73 nm, DPhBdT/DPhCzT: 0.87 nm. Supplementary Table 11). Therefore, a significant fraction of photoexcited host molecules statistically fall within the regime r < R0, supporting near-field Förster resonance energy transfer ( < 10 nm) as the dominant pathway, although far-field radiative energy transfer ( > 10 nm) remains a possibility52. In mCBP series, host fluorescence nearly vanishes at 1 mol% guest, whereas in DPhCzT series significant host emission remains. At the 10 mol% guest-doping level, the increased guest self-absorption suppresses the higher-energy shoulder peak in the photoluminescence spectra. According to the TRES results, the photoluminescence spectra at different doping levels can be well reproduced by a linear combination of host fluorescence, guest fluorescence, and guest phosphorescence (Fig. 7c, Supplementary Note 3.11, Supplementary Figs. 28, 29 and 56c, d; see Section 4.4 for the method). As typical heavy-atom-free materials, both hosts and guests exhibit low ISC efficiencies (e.g., 10.8% for mCBP and 15.1% for DPhCzT, Supplementary Table 7), indicating that singlet excitons dominate under steady-state conditions. Consequently, FRET is expected to be the primary energy transfer mechanism, while Dexter energy transfer is expected to be rare at low doping67. If PRET from the host T1 state occurs, it would further suppress Dexter energy transfer owing to competition. ΦFRET and kFRET were extracted for each host–guest sample by linearly deconvoluting the steady-state photoluminescence spectra and quantifying the loss of host fluorescence relative to that of the undoped films (Fig. 7d,e; Supplementary Tables 9, 11 and 12; see Section 4.6.1 for method). Quantitative analysis confirms highly efficient FRET in mCBP series: ΦFRET > 90% at 1 mol% (kFRET ≈ 3.6 × 109 s⁻1) and ~60% even at 0.01 mol% (Fig. 7f,g and Supplementary Fig. 56e–g).The Förster radii (R0) calculated from host-emission/guest-absorption overlap fall in the approximately 1.8 nm (Supplementary Fig. 56h). The fs-TAS confirms fast FRET: the host-singlet ESA band decays within ~100 ps after excitation (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 50). In contrast, for PS films co-doped with both mCzBdBP and mCBP, (mCzBdBP+mCBP)/PS, with a total concentration of 1 wt% (Supplementary Note 3.12, Supplementary Fig. 57, and Supplementary Table 13, 14), FRET efficiency remains moderate even at high guest loadings (Fig. 7g). For instance, the PS film co-doped with mCzBdBP and mCBP in an equimolar ratio (0.5:0.5) exhibits a ΦFRET of 53%, which is lower than that of the 0.01 mol% mCzBdBP/mCBP small-molecule solid. Given that exciton diffusion is suppressed in the diluted PS matrix, the superior FRET in the mCzBdBP/mCBP system is attributed to robust Förster-type singlet-exciton diffusion (Förster-ED) facilitated by dense molecular packing.

In DPhCzT series, ΦFRET only reaches 70–80% at 1 mol% (kFRET ≈ 1.4 × 109 s⁻1) and falls below 50% at ≤0.3 mol%. The corresponding R0 values are approximately 0.8 nm (Supplementary Fig. 56h). Collectively, these data quantitatively explain the efficient host-to-guest FRET in mCBP series and the mixed host–guest emission observed in lightly doped DPhCzT series. Overall, R0 values for the isomer-doped systems are smaller than those in classical efficient FRET systems (R0 > 3 nm)52, likely due to quenching caused by exciton migration among host molecules. Notably, the JFRET between host molecules (S1H → S1H’) is only about one order of magnitude lower than host-to guest JFRET (S1H → S1G) (Supplementary Table 11), indicating that singlet exciton migration within the host matrix is a non-negligible factor.

Unlike fluorescence, no host phosphorescence is detected even at low guest concentrations; the host-guest systems still exhibit only the guest phosphorescence (Supplementary Fig. 55). This is mainly because the long-lived host triplet excitons (τP of mCBP/PS: 1.57 s; DPhCzT/PS at 50 K: 5.08 s) can migrate over long distances, greatly increasing the probability of encountering quenchers such as non-emissive impurities and other excitons. Importantly, even at 100 ppm doping, the resulting weak phosphorescence can still be recorded with a standard CCD spectrometer and is visible by naked eye in the dark under intense UV excitation (Supplementary Figs. 31, 32 and 55).

Mechanistic analysis of the Cz-derived host/analog-guest systems

Based on the above results, we discuss the emission mechanism of the host–guest systems on three hierarchical levels: (i) molecular photophysics of host and guest molecules (Fig. 8a, b, and Supplementary Note 3.13, Supplementary Fig. 58a–c), (ii) exciton diffusion and trapping in the host matrix (Fig. 8c,d), (iii) global exciton dynamics involving charge separation and recombination (Fig. 9). Without inter-CT processes, direct guest excitation leads to photophysical behavior similar to that in PS-doped systems. However, because the host and isomeric guest share similar optical gaps (ΔESS < 0.3 eV), selective guest excitation at low concentrations is impractical, especially under commonly used excitation sources, e.g., 365 nm UV lamp. Likewise, host triplet excitons can diffuse through the matrix via successive host-to-host Dexter energy transfers, a process referred to as Dexter-type triplet-exciton diffusion (Dexter-ED). Host triplets can in principle diffuse farther via Dexter-ED because of long-lived triplets, but low host ISC efficiency keeps this flux minor, so exciton migration is still dominated by Förster-ED. In TADF-active DPhCzT, a fraction of triplets regenerated to singlets through reverse ISC (RISC) are efficiently delivered to the guest by FRET or Förster-ED, while the rest can reach the guest S1 directly via PRET.

As excitons diffuse among the host molecules, they may (i) transfer to guests, (ii) be annihilated non-radiatively by other excitons or polarons (SSA, TTA, TPA, singlet–triplet annihilation, singlet-polaron annihilation, etc.) or (iii) be quenched by non-emissive traps, e.g., structural defects (Fig. 8c, d). For convenience, we define all species that quench host excitons other than the guest as quenchers (Q), encompassing other excitons, polarons, and intrinsic defects. Because triplet Dexter-ED enables much longer diffusion length than singlet Förster-ED, host triplets are more likely quenched, so host phosphorescence is completely suppressed in neat hosts and host–guest mixtures. In the mCBP series, high excitation power further raises the chance of radiative TTA between host and guest, making TTA-DF evident.

Figure 9 schematically depicts the global exciton dynamics of the host–guest system with LPL (DF omitted). Under 365 nm excitation, nearly all excitons form on the host. Apart from intrinsic deexcitation and ISC, most initial S₁,H excitons diffuse outward through rapid FRET: some S₁,H excitons migrate to guests, while the remainder are quenched by annihilation (e.g., SSA) or non-emissive impurities (Förster-ED,HQ). Only a small fraction dissociates into charge pairs that are immobilized by two types of carrier traps (e.g., shallow guest HOMO and host LUMO tailed state), producing long-lived CSS. Most initial host triplets from ISC migrate via Dexter-ED, with some captured by guests as deep triplet traps and others quenched. All the above processes unfold within the first ∼1 µs after photoexcitation.

Most guest triplets—the secondary T1—originate from the guest's own ISC after FRET, with only a minor fraction transferred from host triplets via Dexter-ED. As deep traps, guest T1 states confine excitons, and in the rigid host matrix their suppressed nonradiative decay together with intrinsically slow radiative rates gives rise to ms–s RTP lifetimes. Nonetheless, some guest triplets can be quenched by diffusing host triplets, lowering ΦPhos. Consequently, the guest phosphorescence-to-fluorescence ratio in host matrices is even lower than in PS films (Supplementary Fig. 58d).

After ~10 s, carriers released from deep traps recombine predominantly on hosts, since the low guest content and their predominant role as triplet traps leave only a small fraction available as carrier traps. With high energy transfer efficiency, these regenerated excitons are again transferred to guests and emitted as fluorescence or phosphorescence, producing a faint but long-tailed LPL component. Once all excitons and carriers are exhausted, the system fully relaxes to the ground state, completing the photophysical cycle.

Discussion

In this work, we systematically unraveled the photophysical mechanisms governing afterglow in two series of Cz-based analog host–guest solids. The pronounced exponential afterglow originates from guest molecules acting as deep triplet traps (ΔETT > 0.5 eV), ensuring exciton confinement and long-lived emission. Although CSS can form under single-photon excitation, their population is extremely small ( ≈ 10⁻4–10⁻3 of triplets) and contributes only to an ultra-weak LPL rather than the main RTP.

Because of small singlet-energy gaps and low guest concentrations, host-to-guest energy transfer critically impacts guest emission efficiency, while exciton diffusion within the host adds further energy loss. Consequently, host–guest systems with inefficient energy transfer show lower \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\varPhi }_{{{\rm{phos}}}}$$\end{document}Φphos than guest-doped PS films. As host triplet emission is completely quenched in the solid state, the solids display only weak guest phosphorescence even at trace guest-doping levels ( ≈ 100 ppm), and under strong excitation this faint RTP is still discernible. No aggregate-state enhancement was observed in the DPhCzT series. Nonetheless, compared to PS samples, the structural similarity enables guests to embed into host crystals with minimal disruption, forming dense packing that suppresses oxygen diffusion and allows visible afterglow in air.

DF arises via distinct pathways in the two series. In mCBP host–guest solids, DF shows the superlinear intensity-dependence of TTA, becoming prominent only under strong excitation. In contrast, DPhCzT-based systems display single-photon, temperature-independent DF, attributable to phosphorescence resonance energy transfer (PRET) from host T1 to guest S1.

Current Cz-isomer host–guest architectures still show only low afterglow efficiencies, leaving room for improvement through molecular design and host–guest pairing engineering, e.g., adopting phosphorescent-OLED design strategies to optimize host–guest energy transfer52. Overall, our findings resolve the mechanism dispute in Cz-based analog afterglow and highlight two outstanding challenges for organic solid-state afterglow: (i) establishing purification and diagnostic protocols that rule out trace triplet-trap contaminants (ppm level at least); (ii) clarifying whether aggregate-specific states (e.g., H-aggregates72,73, self-trapping74,75) contribute to afterglow, and if so, how they form, distribute, and evade exciton-diffusion quenching.

Methods

Preparation of materials and samples

Chemicals

9H-carbazole (Lab-Cz) was synthesized by Lab route 3 in ref. 32. 9H-carbazole (TCI-normal-Cz, C0032; TCI-HP-Cz, C3722), 1H-Benzo[f]indole (Bd, B6283), 5H-Benzo[b]carbazole (BCz, B6453), 2-Chloro-4,6-diphenyl-1,3,5-triazine (DPhClT, C2703) were purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry (TCI) Co., Ltd. 3,3’-Dibromo-1,1’-biphenyl (mBrBP, QJ-1017) was purchased from Combi-Blocks Inc. All the other reagents and solvents were purchased from commercial sources including TCI, Aldrich, Alfa Aesar, and BLD Chemical. They were of analytical grade and used without purification except as specified.

Synthesis and characterization

Detailed synthetic procedures and characterization data are provided in Supplementary Note 2. Unless noted, the host materials were synthesized from laboratory-synthesized carbazole (Lab-Cz) following our previously reported procedures32, and were subsequently purified by an additional sublimation step.

Sample fabrication

Thick films of the mCBP series for optical measurements were fabricated on quartz substrates with a groove (10 × 10 × 0.5 mm) using the melt-casting method under a nitrogen atmosphere at a temperature 10 °C above the melting point of the host material, followed by rapid cooling. The fabricated films were then covered with a quartz cover glass (1 mm thick) and sealed using encapsulation adhesive. The DPhCzT series were obtained as solution-grown crystal powder5,31. Samples of the DPhCzT series for photoluminescence measurements were fabricated by sandwiching the sample powder between two quartz substrates at room temperature. Thin films for the transient absorption test were fabricated by sandwiching the melted samples between two quartz substrates at a temperature 10 °C above the melting point of the host material. All doped PS films were made by the drop-casting method.

Characterization and measurement

Basic characterization

Proton and carbon nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR and 13C NMR) spectra for the compounds were attained from a Bruker ARX 400 NMR spectrometer with Chloroform-d (CDCl3) as solvent. High-resolution mass spectra (HRMS) with ESI ionization were recorded on an Agilent 7200 GC-QTOF. Thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) were performed with a SHIMADZU DTG-60AH and NETZSCH DSC 204 instrument, respectively, under nitrogen at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. Thermal decomposition temperatures (Td) were determined at 5 % weight loss.

Electrochemistry

The HOMO and LUMO energy levels of the compounds were derived from their redox potentials measured according to the differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) method described in ref. 15. DPV measurements were carried out using a Pine Research WaveNow wireless potentiostat (AFTP4) inside a nitrogen-filled glovebox. The measurements were conducted in desiccated and oxygen-free dichloromethane (DCM) or dimethylformamide (DMF), with 0.1 mol/L tetrabutylammonium hexafluorophosphate (TBAPF6) as the supporting electrolyte. A platinum wire served as the working electrode, a glassy carbon electrode was used as the counter electrode, and an Ag/Ag⁺ electrode (0.01 mol/L AgNO3 in 0.1 mol/L TBAPF6 acetonitrile solution) was employed as the reference electrode. Redox potentials were referenced against ferrocene/ferrocenium (Fc/Fc+). The DPV curves were obtained with a pulse width (ΔEpulse) of 0.05 V. Before each DPV curve measurement, the working electrode was polished, cleaned, dried and then transferred into a glovebox to ensure a clean surface and eliminate any interference from adsorbed water.

The same electrolyte solution was used to measure the redox potentials of the host and guest for HOMO/LUMO comparison. Dilute solutions in the same solvent were prepared to determine their absorption and emission peaks and estimate the optical gap. The electrolyte was chosen to ensure all redox events occurred within its electrochemical window. DMF was selected for most compounds due to its low volatility, good electrolyte solubility, and high ion dissociation. For DPhCzT, whose redox peak exceeds the DMF window, DCM was used instead for both redox and optical measurements.

Time-resolved emission spectra (TRES)

Time-resolved transient photoluminescent decay profiles are recorded using an Edinburgh Instruments Spectrofluorometer (FLS 1000) and a time-resolved spectroscopy setup76, composed of a third harmonic wave generation, a Nd:YAG/YVO lasing source (EKSPLA PL-2250, the excitation wavelength of 355 nm, and a pulse width of 30 ps), and a streak camera (C10910-01, Hamamatsu Photonics). The sample was placed in a cryostat (PS-HT-200, Nagase Techno-Engineering) connected to a turbo molecular pump (HiPace 80, Pfeiffer Vacuum), and the temperature was controlled from 10 K to 300 K.

Femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy measurements (fs-TAS)

The fs-TAS was performed on a home-built pump−probe set-up. The laser pulse (800 nm, 35 fs pulse width, 1 kHz repetition rate) was generated by a regeneratively amplified Ti:sapphire laser (Coherent Astrella-Tunable-USP, USA). The output of the pulse is divided into two beams with a beam splitter. The 330 or 370 nm pump beams were produced by TOPAS, and the power of the pump pulse was set as 100 or 120 μW. The probe pulse was delayed by a computer-controlled optical delay line and then focused on a thin sapphire plate to generate the white light supercontinuum which was split into two beams by using a broadband 50/50 beam splitter as the signal and reference beams (450 - 750 nm). The focused pump and probe pulses overlapped in a sample film. No photodegradation was observed after fs-TA experiments by checking the steady-state absorption spectra. A transparent amorphous film of mCzBdBP/mCBP was fabricated by melt-casting the blend between two quartz slides. Because this system crystallizes very rapidly, an amorphous film of DPhBdT/DPhCzT could not be obtained. Instead, after melting, a temperature gradient was applied to induce the formation of a semi-transparent crystalline film of DPhBdT/DPhCzT.

Millisecond transient absorption spectroscopy measurements (ms-TAS)

The transient absorption measurements in the millisecond regime were conducted following the approach described in ref. 11. This method utilizes two light sources: a monochromatic excitation source and a broadband white-light probe. Since the powder samples are optically opaque, we adapted the method from transmission to reflection mode31. Specifically, a BaSO4 reflector was placed behind the sample, and white light was introduced and collected via a reflection fiber probe (R600-125F). The UV excitation light source was positioned at the side of the sample (at an angle of approximately 60°), as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 60.

A key consideration in this setup is the distance between the reflection fiber probe and the sample. On one hand, this distance affects the illumination area and intensity of the white-light probe, which diverges from the fiber tip. On the other hand, it also impacts the intensity of the reflected signal. Additionally, the material exhibits phosphorescence emission around 600 nm within the 300–1100 nm detection window of ms-TAS, which may interfere with the absorption signal in that spectral region. Although the UV-LED excitation sources (310 nm and 340 nm) used in our setup are collimated and can uniformly cover the entire sample area, the distance between the fiber probe and the sample significantly influences the balance between excited-state absorption (ESA) and phosphorescence signals in the ms-TAS spectra. To minimize these effects, we optimized the probe–sample distance to ensure that the white-light spot fully covers the sample while maintaining sufficient signal intensity without saturating the detector.

Afterglow measurements

The afterglow decay profiles and spectra were recorded using a home-built, high-sensitivity measurement setup (Supplementary Fig. 33a) including the following detectors: a photon counting head (H11890, Hamamatsu Photonics) without further wavelength sensitivity calibration for measuring decay signals of afterglow and either a multichannel photodetector (PMA-12, C10027-02, Hamamatsu Photonics) or a multi-channel spectrometer (QE-Pro, Ocean) for recording afterglow spectra. To maximize the sensitivity of the afterglow signal, the distance between the sample and photon-counting head, and the optical path of the side of the photon-counting head is simplified (e.g., no wavelength-resolving components are included in the optical path for afterglow decay signal measurement). Samples were made on a quartz substrate with a groove (10 × 10 × 0.5 mm) placed in a dark chamber of this measurement setup for room temperature measurements. Samples were excited by various wavelengths of LEDs (M280L6, M340L5, M365LP1, M455L4, Thorlabs) with bandpass filters (280 ± 5 nm, 340 ± 5 nm, 365 ± 5 nm, 450 nm ± 5 nm, Thorlabs or Edmund Optics) with a specific excitation intensity and duration. LED excitation intensity was measured with a power meter (S120VC, Thorlabs). The ND filter wheel contained five filters: OD0.3, OD1, OD2, OD3, and OD4.

The intensity calibration curve was obtained using a UV-VIS spectrometer (ULS2048CL-EVO, Avantes). The optical fiber probe equipped with a cosine corrector was placed at the same position as the photon counting head. To construct the calibration curve, we measured the light intensity at six levels using a xenon lamp and five sets of ND filters. For each intensity level, the signal from the photon counting head (equipped with additional OD3 and OD4 filters) was corrected using the manufacturer-provided sensitivity curve to obtain the integrated intensity (counts s−1) over the 400–861 nm range. In parallel, the UV-VIS spectrometer was used to record the spectral irradiance (μW cm−2 nm)), which was then integrated over the same 400–861 nm range (μW cm−2). By comparing the two sets of values under identical experimental conditions, the calibration curve was obtained.

All afterglow signals were baseline-corrected using instrument readings recorded before and after the emission measurements, ensuring that the baseline of all displayed afterglow intensities is zero. Specifically, after placing the sample in the setup, the system was kept in the dark for 5 minutes. Prior to excitation, a baseline reading (Baseline 1) was recorded with the ND filter wheel positioned at an open (non-ND) setting to capture the instrument’s dark signal. Following afterglow acquisition (30 minutes to 1 hour, or longer depending on the duration of the sample’s afterglow), a second baseline reading (Baseline 2) was taken. When the setup is properly light-tight, the detector dark current is stable, and the sample emission has decayed below the detection limit, the average of Baselines 1 and 2 is expected to be consistent. This average value was then used to correct the afterglow signal intensity.

Moreover, it is not applicable to different emissive species with highly similar spectral profiles, such as fluorescence and TADF. In such cases, their relative contributions can be determined using time-resolved emission decay profiles (Supplementary Fig. 19c)78. Furthermore, this method does not consider distortions in the onset region of steady-state spectra caused by self-absorption. In this work, self-absorption is relatively weak, and neglecting it has little impact on the linear regression results. However, for materials with significant self-absorption, proper correction is necessary to obtain reliable fitting results. Notably, self-absorption has a negligible effect on transient spectral shapes.

Calculation of rate constant of resonance energy transfer, Förster integral and radius

In this study, the absorption spectra of guest molecules are red-shifted relative to the host. If the excitation wavelength only excites the guest without affecting the host, the system behaves similarly to guest-doped PS, with the host merely providing a rigid environment. However, if the excitation wavelength falls within the absorption range of the host, the host molecules will predominantly absorb the energy and subsequently transfer it to the guest molecules. Therefore, it is necessary to evaluate the energy transfer efficiency from host to guest under such conditions. Since the phosphorescence contribution in the emission spectrum of our system is minimal, the ISC process of host is inefficient, indicating that energy transfer occurs mainly via fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET).

The difference is that we evaluate this change via the donor’s fluorescence quantum yield, which is experimentally easier to determine. Note that this method yields the overall energy transfer efficiency from the S1,H to the S1,G via RET, including processes where excitons first undergo Förster-type diffusion among host molecules and are then transferred to the guest S1,G via FRET. The presence of Förster exciton diffusion can effectively shorten the donor–acceptor distance by enabling excitons to migrate toward acceptors, thereby increasing the probability of energy transfer. Once the exciton migrates within the Förster radius, direct FRET from host to guest becomes the dominant pathway. As a result, the average donor–acceptor separation distance between energy donors and acceptors (<r>) calculated by this method is expected to be smaller than the average initial separation between excitons in the host and the guest molecules. However, since exciton migration on the host occurs in a random manner, at low guest concentrations—especially under high photon flux excitation—direct FRET still dominates, while diffusion primarily contributes to exciton quenching. Moreover, this method assumes that the ISC process of the host remains unchanged upon guest doping, which holds true at low guest concentrations but may not be valid at higher doping levels.

Quantum chemical calculations (for Supplementary Fig. 21c–f)

Quantum chemical calculations were conducted using the Gaussian 16 Rev. B.01 package86. The geometry and electronic properties of ground states were optimized using density functional theory (DFT) at the B3LYP/6-311 G level of theory. The excited states were computed using time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT) at the same B3LYP/6-311 G level and then analyzed using Multiwfn87 and VMD programs88.
