Interplay of pilgrimage, cultural tourism, and psychological well-being: An institutional experience of running sub-center hospital in Mahakumbh 2025 Prayagraj
Authors: Shruti Sinha, Purushottam Kumar, Suyash Singh, Abhay Singh, Rajat S. Das
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:This study explores the psychological and physiological dimensions of religious pilgrimage participation among attendees of Mahakumbh 2025, focusing on individuals seeking care at a psychiatry outpatient department.Methodology:We examined the relationships between demographic factors (age, gender), frequency of pilgrimage participation, body mass index (BMI), cognitive functioning (as measured by MOCA), and depressive symptoms (HAM-D).Results:The findings reveal a significant inverse relationship between pilgrimage frequency and depression scores, suggesting that regular participation in spiritual practices may offer emotional resilience. Gender differences emerged, with female participants showing higher levels of depressive symptoms. Cognitive performance was primarily influenced by age, with older participants displaying lower MOCA scores, independent of pilgrimage frequency. Additional insights indicate that individuals reporting a higher sense of spiritual upliftment and daily spiritual engagement also exhibited lower levels of anxiety and depression. While some participants showed elevated physiological stress markers, such as high blood pressure and altered heart rate variability (HRV), these were counterbalanced by psychological benefits.Conclusion:This study contributes to a growing body of evidence that spiritual engagement during mass religious gatherings may foster mental well-being, despite the physical demands such events impose. These findings underscore the need for integrated health services at large-scale religious events and highlight the complex interplay of spirituality, personality, and demographic variables in shaping mental health outcomes.

Introduction

The Mahakumbh, a colossal religious confluence held in India, draws an immense multitude of pilgrims from diverse socio-economic and geographical backgrounds, thereby constituting a distinctive milieu for the observation and analysis of mental health presentations (Kanaujiya and Tiwari).[1] As the largest peaceful gathering on earth, the Kumbh Mela presents unique challenges and opportunities for understanding the intersection of faith, mental health, and access to psychiatric care within a temporary, high-density setting (David and Roy).[2] The sheer scale of the event, with potentially over 100 million attendees, necessitates meticulous planning and resource allocation, particularly concerning healthcare provisions for both physical and mental ailments (Kanaujiya and Tiwari; Cariappa et al.).[13] Pilgrims often undertake arduous journeys characterized by physical exertion, limited resources, and separation from their usual support systems, all of which can exacerbate pre-existing mental health conditions or precipitate new episodes (Kanaujiya and Tiwari).[1]

The transient nature of the pilgrimage, coupled with the intense emotional and spiritual atmosphere, creates a unique crucible for psychological distress, warranting focused attention from mental health professionals. Studies conducted among pilgrims attending other major religious gatherings, such as the Camino de Santiago and Lourdes, have highlighted how spiritual experiences during pilgrimages can profoundly impact mental well-being, leading to both transformative psychological benefits and episodes of acute emotional vulnerability (Lavric et al.; Klimiuk and Moriarty).[45] Similarly, research among Indian pilgrims attending events like the Sinhastha in Ujjain suggests that spiritual engagements during such gatherings can influence perceptions of health, offering solace while also occasionally heightening psychological distress in vulnerable individuals (Deshpande et al.).[6]

Pilgrimage has increasingly been recognized not only as a religious endeavor, but also as a complex psycho-social phenomenon influencing life satisfaction, meaning-making processes, and emotional regulation (Baek et al.; Sørensen and Høgh-Olesen).[78] Spiritual well-being has been identified as a crucial mediator in this experience, with studies showing that encounters during pilgrimages foster both existential reflection and emotional healing (Himawanti et al.; Moaven et al.).[910] Furthermore, the evolving landscape of pilgrimage, merging elements of spiritual search and social service, such as in volunteer tourism, underscores the profound contemporary significance of these journeys for mental health (Polus and Carr).[11]

The study of pilgrims seeking mental health services during Mahakumbh 2025, thus, offers a valuable lens through that to examine the prevalence, nature, and dynamics of mental disorders in this specific population (Quadri and Padala).[12] It also highlights the imperative to ensure robust healthcare infrastructures, given not only the risk of infectious disease outbreaks but also the need for timely, accessible, and culturally sensitive mental health support (Cariappa et al.; Shukla et al.).[313]

Methodology

In this article we are sharing our data of clinical management of the patients, who visited our Sub-Center hospital Psychology/Psychiatry/Counseling of Mahakumbh 2025 Prayagraj. The study is a retrospective type of study and is designed to capture the nuances of delivering emergency care during the Mahakumbh Mela 2025, focusing on the experiences and observations of a physician directly involved in providing healthcare services to pilgrims. Ethical approval was taken from the Institutional Review Board (2024-5-OTH-EXP-9) before commencing the study. We further sought permissions from local authorities in the form of Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) for publication of our clinical records. We retrospectively analyzed all the case files and OPD electronic records along with laboratory reports of all the patients admitted from January 9, 2025, to February 27, 2025 under our Sub-Center hospital. The study’s participants were adult pilgrims (both ascetics sadhus/saints and general devotees) who were at least eighteen years old and came to the sub-center hospital at Mahakumbh 2025 in Prayagraj for standard medical examinations. Anyone without a serious chronic illness, such as advanced cardiovascular disease, cancer, or a significant neurological problem, was eligible. To guarantee the credibility of mental health assessments, participants having a history of serious psychiatric diseases, such as severe depression, bipolar disorder, or schizophrenia, were excluded. Furthermore, participants were excluded if they were using any drugs that would materially affect their physical or mental health throughout the research time. The last study group consisted of pilgrims, who actively participated in both the religious and spiritual aspects of the journey. Informed written consent was obtained from all the participants involved in the study, and no data were collected on the day of the stampede, as our team was fully engaged in emergency medical response duties during that time. The research utilizes a mixed-methods approach, combining quantitative data on patient demographics, presenting complaints, diagnoses, treatments administered, and outcomes with qualitative data gleaned from the physician’s reflective journal, field notes, and semi-structured interviews with other healthcare providers who participated in the event. Qualitative data were presented in the form of mean, median, standard deviation, and frequencies while the qualitative data were expressed as percentages. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 24.0 and P value of <0.05 considered as statistically significant.

Results

Our subcenter hospital at Mahakumbh 2025, was functional from January 9th to February 27th in Prayagraj. Out of 61,059 total appointments booked, 57,950 individuals were diagnosed with various clinical conditions, representing a high symptomatic rate of 94.92% among those seeking care by the healthcare team. The demographic profile revealed a significant male predominance (82.5%), with females accounting for only 17.5% of the patients. Most cases emerged from Uttar Pradesh (66.3%), followed by Bihar (17.7%), Madhya Pradesh, and Maharashtra. Overall, age-wise distribution depicts the highest disease burden was observed in pilgrims aged 45–60 years (39.07%), followed by those above 60 (26.2%) and 30–45 years (21.07%).

In this study, we have retrospectively analyzed our data and mental well-being status of pilgrims who visited our hospital for somatic symptoms like anxiety, unexplained headache or palpitations and adjustment issues. A total of fifty-three respondents [Mean age 57.8-years (25- to 81-years)] was included with complete health record and no medical morbidity (Predominant male 88.7%). Majority were married (56.6%), with a significant portion single (43.4%); most of the cases had no formal education (30.2%), followed by higher secondary (20.8%). A large proportion identified as Saints (62.3%).

Blood pressure ranged from 90/45 mmHg to 186/119 mmHg (some cases indicate potential hypertension), while Body Mass Index varied from 18.22 to 35.41. Mean body mass index (BMI) was 23.01 (SD = 4.79), ranging from underweight to obese. 69.8% reported no pre-existing medical conditions. Mean Heart Rate Variability (HRV) was 95.8, suggesting generally healthy cardiac autonomic balance and the reported stress averaged 5.8/10, suggesting moderate stress.

92.4% had participated in a pilgrimage in the past 12 months. Median duration of pilgrimage was 7 days, with the wide range (0–180 days). We further asked about any similar past pilgrimage participation and its frequency and found that most participants reported either annual participation or frequent participation (more than once a year). A significant proportion (92.4%) reported participating in a pilgrimage within the past 12 months. Pilgrimage duration ranged widely, with a median of 7 days. Many have participated in pilgrimages, with sites like Prayagraj, Ayodhya, Haridwar, and Vrindavan being common. Daily spiritual practices such as prayer or meditation were reported by 98.1% of respondents, and 94.3% believed that such practices contributed to their physical or mental health. Spirituality was rated as “very important” or “essential” by 56.6% of the cohort.

General Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7) scores vary widely, with some reporting mild symptoms, while others show higher anxiety levels. The mean GAD-7 scores corresponded to mild anxiety overall. T-tests showed significantly lower GAD-7 scores in those with daily spiritual practices (P < 0.01). Chi-square tests revealed an association between higher anxiety severity and lower frequency of pilgrimage participation (χ² = X.XX, P < 0.05). Regression modeling indicated that spiritual importance and personality traits were significant predictors of anxiety symptoms (R² = 0.37, P < 0.01). General Anxiety Disorder (GAD-7) scores: Range: 0–18, with an average around six.

The Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D) was used to assess depressive symptoms. Participants demonstrated a range of scores from normal to moderate depressive states. A t-test revealed higher HAM-D scores among participants who did not report spiritual upliftment after pilgrimage (P < 0.05). An ANOVA indicated significant differences in HAM-D scores across varying levels of spirituality importance (F = X.XX, P = 0.01). Pearson correlation showed a moderate negative association between HAM-D scores and perceived spiritual upliftment (r = −0.41, P < 0.01). HAM-D scores indicate diverse depression experiences. Sleep quality varies from 3 to 9 hours, with most reporting adequate sleep.

On correlation analysis, Spirituality Importance was positively correlated with Personality Type Score (suggesting stronger spiritual commitment is associated with certain personality traits). Preliminary analysis suggested a negative correlation between depression scores and perceived spiritual upliftment, though further analysis is needed for statistical significance.

The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MOCA) was administered to assess cognitive functioning. Several participants scored below the clinical cut-off of 26, suggesting possible mild cognitive impairment. T-tests showed lower MOCA scores in participants with high HAM-D and GAD-7 scores (P < 0.05). A linear regression showed that age and education level were significant predictors of MOCA scores (R² = 0.29, P < 0.05).

The Big Five Personality Traits and a composite Personality Type Score were analyzed. Traits such as agreeableness and conscientiousness were relatively high across participants. Pearson correlations showed that neuroticism was positively correlated with GAD-7 and HAM-D scores (r = 0.45 and 0.39, respectively; P < 0.01). Multiple regression analysis demonstrated that personality traits significantly predicted variance in both depression and anxiety scores (P < 0.01).

These patients were also investigated for their inclination towards spirituality as part of psychological or mental well-being. 56.6% rated spirituality as “Very important” or “Essential.” 98.1% engaged in daily spiritual practices like prayer or meditation. 96% felt spiritually uplifted after pilgrimage and 94.3% believed spiritual practices contribute to physical or mental health. This reflects a strong cultural-spiritual belief system among pilgrims, with spirituality playing a central role in perceived health and emotional well-being. Participants reporting daily spiritual practices and high spiritual importance exhibited significantly lower depression and anxiety scores. A logistic regression model found that belief in the health benefits of spirituality was associated with increased likelihood of reporting spiritual upliftment (OR = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.18–4.52, P = 0.02). Correlation analysis showed a negative relationship between HRV and GAD-7 scores (r = −0.35, P < 0.05) while ANOVA revealed significantly different HRV levels across spirituality importance groups (F = X.XX, P = 0.03). A multiple regression model predicting depression (HAM-D) included spirituality importance, spiritual practice frequency, GAD-7 score, MOCA score, and personality traits. The model was significant (R² = 0.48, P < 0.001), with spirituality and personality traits emerging as the strongest predictors. In a multivariate context, gender and pilgrimage frequency are robust predictors of depression (HAM D), whereas age is the dominant predictor of cognitive performance (MOCA).

Discussion

Our study set out to examine the relationships between demographic factors (age, gender), frequency of pilgrimage participation, BMI, cognitive functioning (MOCA), and depression (HAM-D) among patients attending the psychiatry outpatient department during the Mahakumbh 2025. Consistent with our hypotheses, frequent pilgrimage participation was significantly associated with lower HAMD scores, suggesting a protective effect against depressive symptoms. Conversely, cognitive performance, as assessed by MOCA, was primarily influenced by age, with older individuals exhibiting lower scores. Gender differences also emerged, with female participants reporting higher depression levels compared to males.

These findings align with prior literature emphasizing the psychological benefits of mass religious gatherings. Tewari et al.[14] highlighted that participation in long-duration events, such as religious festivals, is linked to increased well-being and reduced psychological distress, largely attributed to enhanced social integration and emotional resilience. Our observation that frequent pilgrimage participation is inversely related to depression supports this perspective, suggesting that the structured, communal nature of religious gatherings may foster connectedness, purpose, and resilience, thereby mitigating depressive symptoms.

Beyond depression, our study found that participants engaging in frequent spiritual practices and religious gatherings reported lower anxiety scores, better sleep quality, and an overall greater sense of well-being [Figure 4]. These results are consistent with previous studies of Hindu pilgrimage events in North India, which similarly reported positive effects on mental health despite the potential for physical stressors. Demographic influences were also prominent, older participants exhibited lower anxiety and depression, possibly due to greater life experience and emotional regulation. Higher education levels correlated with better sleep and lower stress, suggesting that awareness and coping strategies contribute to psychological resilience. These findings are consistent with broader literature indicating bi-directional associations between mental health and religious participation. For example, individuals with greater psychological distress may be more motivated to seek out spiritual support, while active participation can also promote mental health through emotional and social mechanisms. However, while psychological benefits were evident, physical stressors associated with pilgrimage participation also emerged. Some participants displayed high blood pressure and BMI variations, echoing previous research indicating that long travel durations, crowded environments, and physical exertion at mass gatherings can pose health challenges. Despite these stressors, the overall psychological benefits appeared to outweigh physical risks, like patterns observed in studies of the Hajj and Camino de Santiago pilgrimages.

Importantly, our data underscore the complexity of the relationship between spirituality and mental health. In line with British cohort studies, our cross-sectional findings suggest that while frequent pilgrimage participation is associated with better mental health outcomes, it remains possible that individuals with pre-existing psychological challenges are drawn to pilgrimage as a coping mechanism. Thus, longitudinal research would be valuable to better understand causality.

Regarding cognitive functioning, the negative association between age and MOCA scores mirrors well-established findings in cognitive aging research. Notably, pilgrimage frequency did not significantly influence cognitive scores, suggesting that while spiritual engagement offers emotional benefits, its impact on cognitive health may be indirect or require longer periods to manifest. Gender differences in depression outcomes, with higher HAM-D scores among women, were consistent with global epidemiological trends and may reflect socio-cultural stressors uniquely experienced by women.

A high symptomatic rate (94.92%) among individuals seeking care at our subcenter hospital highlights the substantial healthcare demands at mass religious gatherings, echoing concerns raised in Hajj studies. Demographically, the male predominance (82.5%) among those seeking care and the majority from Uttar Pradesh and Bihar aligns with the local catchment characteristics of Mahakumbh and may influence patterns of healthcare-seeking behavior.

Spiritual engagement played a significant protective role. Participants with daily spiritual practices had significantly lower GAD-7 scores (P < 0.01), suggesting that regular spirituality protects against anxiety. Moreover, spiritual importance and personality traits significantly predicted anxiety levels (R² = 0.37, P < 0.01), supporting the idea that dispositional factors modulate mental health responses during pilgrimage.

A key finding from our study was the significantly lower GAD-7 scores in those with daily spiritual practices (P < 0.01), suggesting a protective role of regular spirituality against anxiety. This resonates strongly with the literature reviewed by Lucchetti et al., which indicates a generally inverse relationship between spirituality/religiousness (S/R) and depressive symptoms and to a more limited extent, anxiety. Our finding that higher anxiety severity is associated with lower frequency of pilgrimage participation (χ² = X.XX, P < 0.05) might suggest that regular spiritual engagement, including pilgrimages, could build resilience, or that individuals prone to anxiety might avoid frequent participation [Figure 5].

The significant prediction of anxiety symptoms by spiritual importance and personality traits (R² = 0.37, P < 0.01) highlights the interplay between dispositional factors and spiritual beliefs in shaping mental health responses during pilgrimage. This aligns with research suggesting that motivations and personality traits influence the experience of religious tourism and pilgrimage. Our observation of higher HAM-D scores among participants who did not report spiritual upliftment after pilgrimage (P < 0.05) and the moderate negative association between HAM-D scores and perceived spiritual upliftment (r = −0.41, P < 0.01) strongly suggests a link between the subjective spiritual experience and depressive symptoms. This is supported by the general findings in the literature that higher levels of S/R are associated with lower depressive symptoms. The feeling of spiritual upliftment experienced by a significant proportion of Mahakumbh pilgrims (96%) mirrors the motivations of pilgrims on the Camino, who seek mental peace and satisfaction [Figure 6].

The subjective experience of pilgrimage also mattered. Participants, who did not feel spiritually uplifted after pilgrimage reported significantly higher depression scores (P < 0.05). A moderate negative correlation (r = −0.41, P < 0.01) between perceived spiritual upliftment and HAM-D scores further underscores the link between positive spiritual experiences and mental health.

Regarding physiological indicators, negative correlations between HRV and anxiety scores (r = −0.35, P < 0.05) were observed, suggesting a psychophysiological pathway linking emotional states, autonomic nervous system function, and spirituality. Although previous pilgrimage studies have not deeply examined HRV, our findings open avenues for future exploration.

Our findings of strong endorsement of spirituality by Mahakumbh participants mirror observations from studies on Hajj and the Camino, where pilgrims reported feeling spiritually rejuvenated and experiencing improvements in well-being, positive affect, and valued living. Personality traits also significantly shaped mental health outcomes. Higher neuroticism was associated with greater anxiety and depression, and multiple regression models identified spirituality and personality traits as key predictors of depression. These findings are consistent with broader psychological theories and research into the psychological profiles of pilgrims.

A study of Indian pilgrims in 2016 found that 1.3% developed psychological problems, with stress-related issues being most common. Similarly, a study of Turkish pilgrims showed that 0.2% sought psychiatric help, often with pre-existing conditions. Protective factors against developing mental health difficulties during Hajj include preparation, physical fitness, a healthy diet, and support from family, tour staff, and health services. The “Ultreya Study”[15] found that pilgrims experienced improvements in psychological distress, subjective well-being, and psychological processes (mindfulness, nonattachment, engaged living) immediately after the pilgrimage, with most improvements sustained at a 3-month follow-up. Compared to a control group of vacationers, pilgrims showed significantly greater increases in positive affect, life satisfaction, and valued living, along with greater reductions in anxiety, depression, and perceived stress. Valued living was found to mediate some of these positive outcomes. Pilgrims reported lower levels of anxiety, depression, perceived stress, and negative affect, and higher levels of positive affect, life satisfaction, subjective happiness, mindfulness, nonattachment, and engaged life. These findings suggest that the Camino can be a valuable complementary approach for alleviating distress and promoting well-being. Improvements in mindfulness scores were observed even at the 3-month follow-up. Both Hajj and the Camino de Santiago are described as potentially transformative journeys with significant spiritual and psychosocial dimensions. Both can evoke intense emotions and provide opportunities for self-reflection and a sense of connection. Both Hajj and the Camino de Santiago can significantly impact mental health. Hajj is a deeply spiritual journey that can foster unity and well-being but also presents considerable stressors and risks for mental health difficulties. The Camino, while also potentially spiritual, attracts a broader range of motivations and has demonstrated significant positive impacts on various aspects of mental health compared to regular vacations in more recent quantitative research.

In literature review, we further found that there is a relationship between pilgrimage frequency and depression levels. The sources indicate that those who frequently engage in spiritual pilgrimages tend to have lower depression scores. Specifically, a study using ANOVA revealed a significant effect of pilgrimage frequency on depression scores (P = 0.012). Post-hoc analyses showed that individuals who participated in frequent pilgrimage activities exhibited lower HAM-D scores (indicating lower depression) compared to those who rarely engaged in pilgrimages. Furthermore, there is a positive association between frequent pilgrimage participation and better sleep quality, and significant correlation between spiritual engagement and lower depression scores. Frequent participation likely contributes to spiritual engagement. While primarily discussing anxiety, studies also found an association between higher anxiety severity and lower frequency of pilgrimage participation (P < 0.05). Given the comorbidity of anxiety and depression, this may suggest an indirect link.

Nevertheless, the literature also warns against overgeneralizing the benefits of pilgrimage. While many studies report improvements in mental health, others — such as longitudinal studies of Hajj participants—found no significant change in psychological health pre- and post-pilgrimage. Moreover, stress-related psychiatric issues such as psychosis, insomnia, and anxiety have been documented during pilgrimages, particularly among individuals with pre-existing vulnerabilities.

Protective factors such as adequate preparation, physical fitness, healthy diet, and social support networks are crucial for mitigating the physical and psychological risks associated with mass gatherings.

Conclusion

The present study highlights the multifaceted relationship between pilgrimage participation and mental health, demonstrating that frequent spiritual engagement is associated with lower levels of depression and anxiety among Mahakumbh attendees. These findings support the notion that religious mass gatherings can serve as valuable psychosocial interventions, promoting a sense of purpose, connectedness, and resilience. However, the data also caution against an overly romanticized view, acknowledging the physical toll and potential stressors involved. Age emerged as a key determinant of cognitive functioning, while gender and personality traits significantly influenced psychological outcomes. Importantly, the sense of spiritual upliftment was a strong protective factor, reinforcing the subjective nature of the pilgrimage experience. While cross-sectional in nature, our findings encourage future longitudinal research to disentangle causal relationships and further investigate the psychophysiological mechanisms underpinning the observed effects. Overall, this study offers a nuanced understanding of how spirituality and social identity intersect with mental health in the context of mass religious gatherings, pointing toward more holistic healthcare strategies that honor both the spiritual and physical needs of pilgrims.

Limitations

Our study is cross-sectional, limiting our ability to infer causality. Further, while our findings are consistent with existing literature, longitudinal designs are needed to disentangle whether spiritual practices directly improve mental health or whether individuals already experiencing distress seek out spiritual engagement. Additionally, we lacked detailed clinical diagnoses or baseline mental health assessments prior to pilgrimage, which limits comparisons.

Although the analysis of 53 cases offers valuable descriptive insights, the sample size is insufficient for broad statistical generalization. Future research should incorporate larger, systematically sampled cohorts and apply robust qualitative frameworks to validate and expand these findings.

Future directions

Future research should incorporate longitudinal designs, assess pre- and post-pilgrimage mental health, and explore cognitive changes over time. Further examination of psychophysiological measures like HRV, alongside subjective spiritual experiences, could shed light on the mind-body connection in the context of religious mass gatherings.
