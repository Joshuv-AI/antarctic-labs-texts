Rescuing TP53 from nonsense: novel triazoles for translational readthrough via optimized drug design
Authors: Davide Ricci, Michele Menditto, Giulia Culletta, Marco Tutone, Carla Rizzo, Andrea Pace, Laura Lentini, Ivana Pibiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nonsense mutations introduce premature termination codons (PTCs), leading to mRNA degradation and the production of truncated, non-functional proteins. These mutations account for approximately 10% of TP53 alterations, a key driver in over 50% human cancers. Effective therapeutic strategies to restore full-length functional p53 remain limited. Here, using an optimized drug design workflow that integrates in silico approaches, we identified and characterized a novel class of 1,2,4-triazole-based translational readthrough-inducing drugs (TRIDs) capable of restoring full-length p53 protein in cells harboring the R213X nonsense mutation. We synthesized and screened four compounds (IP14, IP15, IP17, IP18) with favorable in-silico absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) profiles. All four compounds successfully rescued p53 expression in H1299 R213X cells, outperforming Ataluren and matching G418 at significantly lower concentrations. The restored p53 exhibited nuclear localization upon genotoxic stress and induced transcription of canonical targets. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of these compounds for treating TP53 nonsense mutations in cancer and lay the groundwork for the development of targeted nonsense mutation-specific treatment for a wide range of pathologies, including new emergent p53 related diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48790-y.

Introduction

Nonsense mutations introduce premature stop codons (UAA, UAG or UGA) that disrupt protein synthesis and are one of the leading causes of genetic diseases1.

The formation of a premature termination codon (PTC), located upstream of the natural termination codon (NTC), triggers nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD), a highly conserved surveillance mechanism, leading to reduced transcript levels and the production of a truncated and non-functional polypeptide2.

These mutations are associated with a threefold higher risk of developing severe conditions, often characterized by high mortality rates3.

Diseases caused by nonsense mutations are highly heterogeneous. They may affect specific tissues, as in Choroideremia4 and Leber’s Amaourosis5, which primarily impact the ocular district, be systemic, as in Cystic Fibrosis6,7, or even contribute to cancer development8.

Overall, they account for ~ 11%9 of all genetic diseases and ~ 12% of tumor suppressor gene mutations, including TP53, one of the most frequently mutated and functionally critical genes in cancer suppression10.

TP53 is located at position p13.1 on the short arm of chromosome 17 and encodes the protein p53, a transcription factor known as the “guardian of the genome” that is currently the most extensively studied cancer-related protein by the scientific community. Human p53 comprises 393 amino acids organized into five domains: the N-terminal Transactivation Domain (TAD), the Proline Rich Domain (PRD), the central DNA-Binding Domain (DBD), the Tetramerization Domain (TD) and the C-Terminal Regulatory Domain (CRD)11.

Initially thought to be an oncogene involved in cell transformation, TP53 was later classified in 1989 as a tumor suppressor gene. Today p53 and its associated protein network are considered the major stress responder in cells, capable of promoting adaption to a broad range of stress stimuli, including oncogene activation, genotoxic stress, ribosomal stress, hypoxia, and nutrient deprivation, impacting the expression of over 60 genes12.

To respond to such wide range of stress signals, p53 can activate and influence a broad spectrum of metabolic pathways, such as cell-cycle arrest via induction of CDKN1A (p21), extrinsic apoptosis through upregulation of death receptor genes, and intrinsic apoptosis via activation of GADD45A, PUMA and NOXA. It also regulates ferroptosis, glycolysis, oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid metabolism, immune responses and ageing13,14 (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Comprehensive schematic overview of P53 related metabolic pathways.

Given this wide regulatory role, the loss or impairment of p53 function, often caused by nonsense mutations in TP53, has been linked to the onset of different diseases, such as cardiovascular15, type II diabetes16, neurodegenerative disorders17, and, mainly, cancer18.

It’s estimated that more than half of the cases of cancer in humans involve TP53 mutations, with ~ 10% being nonsense mutations. The most frequent nonsense hotspots include R196, R213, R306 and R34218,19.

Although TP53-related diseases were long considered undruggable, since most of efficient anticancer drugs act as inhibitors of the excessive and dysregulated activities of proteins instead of recovering their functionality, recent advances have led to the development of new therapeutic strategies to treat p53 loss of function12.

One particularly promising approach for nonsense-mutated TP53 treatment involves the induction of translational readthrough (TR). This process enables suppression of PTC thanks to a mismatch between it and a near-cognate tRNA that successfully competes with the release factor eRF1, leading to the insertion of an amino acid in the nascent polypeptide chain instead of the translation termination20.

TR happens physiologically at a basal level at low frequencies: < 0.1% at NTCs and up to ~ 1% at PTCs, probably due to a difference in termination mechanisms, as supported by several lines of evidence1. Pharmacological enhancement of TR is possible using translational readthrough-inducing drugs (TRIDs)21, which offer key advantages over other approaches: they preserve endogenous gene regulation and bypass issues of gene size or splicing.

Furthermore, since the TR-based approach is not gene-specific, the same molecules could, in principle, be used to treat different diseases that share a common cause22.

While aminoglycoside antibiotics were the first TRIDs identified, and are currently used as positive control in in vitro studies to evaluate the efficacy of TR-based therapies in nonsense mutation disorders, Ataluren (PTC124), a 1,2,4-oxadiazole derivative, is the only translational readthrough-inducing drug (TRID) that has ever been approved for clinical use, specifically for treating Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy caused by nonsense mutations23,24.

Ataluren promotes TR without altering transcript stability or interfering with NMD25. Notably, our research group recently demonstrated that Ataluren and its analogues act selectively on PTCs and do not affect NTCs of essential housekeeping genes like TP53, suggesting that the TR-based approach is intrinsically selective26.

Although Ataluren obtained conditional approval from the European Medicines Agency (EMA), continuing uncertainty regarding its clinical efficacy led to non-renewal of its authorization in 202527. Patients already receiving treatment were permitted to continue therapy to ensure continuity of care, while PTC therapeutics submitted a request for EMA reconsideration, maintaining ongoing discussion of its clinical utility.

The uncertainty surrounding Ataluren, along with the potential impact of TR-based therapies on nonsense mutation–pathologies, particularly in treating TP53 mutations in cancer, in terms of therapeutic efficacy and number of patients who could potentially benefit from treatment, highlights the urgent need for new TRIDs. In this context, the aim of this study is to identify and evaluate novel TRIDs able to restore p53 expression.

To achieve this objective, we adopted an optimized developing workflow extensively using CADD (Computer Aided Drug Design) methodology, organized in three steps: (i) pharmacophore modeling , virtual screening and new hits identification and synthesis of the hit compounds, (ii) preliminary in vitro biological screening for the evaluation of the therapeutic effect (iii) in-silico pharmacokinetic e toxicity (ADMET) properties determination.

This iterative workflow enables efficient selection of active compounds and refinement of pharmacophore models, enhancing future TRID discovery. Furthermore, we incorporated in silico green toxicology principles to support sustainable early-stage compound evaluation.

Results

Pharmacophore model design and hits synthesis

The pharmacophore model was generated using a “ligand-based” approach. The library used for model construction and validation consisted of 61 molecules with known, well-established TR activity, sourced from “in-house” and public libraries reported in Table S1 of Supplementary data.

We focused on Ataluren analogues28–32 and other non-aminoglycosidic33–35 small molecules, as these compounds have demonstrated superior TR activity at lower doses with reduced toxicity compared to aminoglycoside antibiotics, which were excluded from the library.

During the molecule’s conformers generation and alignment of known TRIDs emerge that the so-called “ataluren-like” and “chromenopyridine” derivatives, derived from Ataluren and EX 1.37 respectively, exhibit similar features disposition that align to the same pharmacophore model.

Based on this observation, the pharmacophore model (Model 1) was built considering only these two classes of compounds and consists of nine features: three hydrophobic interactions, two aromatic interactions, three hydrogen bond acceptors, and one hydrogen bond donor, as shown in Fig. 2a. The pharmacophore model was validated using a validation database. The enrichment factor (EF) and the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve were subsequently calculated (Fig. 2b).

ROC curve analysis revealed an AUC of 1.0, accompanied by an enrichment factor (EF) of 28 within the top 1% of ranked results. At full coverage (100%), the AUC was 0.79, with an EF of 7.0 resulting predictive in identifying new valid hits. Moreover, Model 1 was able to recognize the parent compounds of both the chromenopyridine (Fig. 2c) and the ataluren-like (Fig. 2d) classes of TRIDs.

This model was used as a query to screen an in-house database to identify candidate compounds for TR-inducing activity. We selected four hits, IP14, IP15, IP17, and IP18 (Fig. 2e), based on their high pharmacophore fit scores and synthetic accessibility. These hits, detected by the pharmacophore model, present a 1,2,4-triazoles core and were synthesized as reported in the methods section.

Preliminary in vitro biological screening for the evaluation of the therapeutic effect

To assess the potential of newly synthesized 1H-1,2,4 triazole derivatives as TRIDs, we first performed in vitro toxicity testing of the four selected compounds through two independent experiments: a cell count at 24, 48, and 72 h of treatment, and MTT assay.

Cells treated with the compounds showed no loss of viability or proliferation at any time point, with results comparable to controls (Fig. 3a). These findings were confirmed by MTT assay, which showed no significant change in metabolic activity (Fig. 3b). To assess the ability of the novel triazole derivative to restore expression of a nonsense-mutated p53 protein, H1299 lung adenocarcinoma cells stably expressing the p53R213X variant were treated with 12 µM of the compound for 24 h. This triazole concentration was selected as a starting point based on previous in vitro and in vivo studies conducted with Ataluren and the NV848, NV914, and NV930 compounds28. It has been shown to be non-toxic to cells while significantly enhancing readthrough activity.

DMSO was used as a negative control, while G418 (630 µM) and Ataluren (12 μM) served as positive controls for translational readthrough. HCT116 cells were included as a positive control for endogenous p53 expression, and H1299 p53-null cells (p53-/-) were used as baseline controls for the reporter system.

Immunofluorescence analysis, after 24 h of treatment, revealed that HCT116 cells exhibited expression of p53, while both H1299 p53-/- and H1299 p53R213X cells showed no detectable signal, confirming the specificity and reliability of the reporter system (Fig. 3c).

In the same conditions, treatment with the 1H-1,2,4-triazole derivatives led to a significant increase in p53 fluorescence in H1299 p53R213X cells, with both cytoplasmic and nuclear localization, consistent with the induction of full-length p53 protein (Fig. 3c, d). Overall, fluorescence analysis confirmed that all tested compounds effectively induced TR. Notably, IP14, IP15, IP17, and IP18 demonstrated p53 levels almost comparable to G418 and Ataluren, making them strong candidates for further development.

We next investigated whether the triazole-based compounds (IP14, IP15, IP17, and IP18) could restore not only p53 protein expression but also its functional responsiveness to DNA damage. To this end, H1299 p53R213X cells were treated with each compound, under the same conditions as above, in combination with the DNA-damaging agent doxorubicin (0.2 μg/ml). HCT116 cells served as positive control, H1299 p53-/- and untreated H1299 p53R213X cells served as negative controls, respectively.

Following 24 h co-treatment, all four triazoles induced a marked increase in full-length p53 levels in H1299 p53R213X cells, comparable to levels observed with G418 and HCT116 cells (Fig. 4a, b). No p53 expression was detected in H1299 p53-/- and untreated H1299 p53R213X cells confirming the specificity of the response and the reliability of the model (Fig. 4a, b). In contrast to the immunofluorescence results, ataluren treatment did not lead to a significant restoration of p53 expression. This discrepancy is likely attributable to the use of a primary antibody targeting the N-terminal epitope of p53, whereas the R213X mutation resides in another region of the protein. Precisely for this reason, immunofluorescence analysis allows us to evaluate a combination of fluorescence from the truncated and full-length forms of the protein, both recognized by the antibody. In the case of Western blot analysis, however, it is possible to distinguish the truncated form from the full-length form based on molecular weight. This allows us to quantify only the recovery of the full-length form, leading to the difference between the two methods.

Additionally, differences in antibody sensitivity between immunodetection techniques may contribute to the observed variation.

Finally, to assess p53 functionality, we performed a Real Time RT-PCR analysis of the canonical p53 targets CDKN1A (p21) and GADD45A. As shown in Fig. 5c, p21 transcript levels were elevated in G418-treated cells, but not with the IPs. This increase is likely due to an high oxidative stress caused by the toxicity of the antibiotic, as seen in literature, which prevents us from using p21 as the sole assay to evaluate p53 functionality.

We therefore focused on GADD45A, which showed a marked and a significant increase in transcript levels upon treatment with G418 and IPs compared to untreated cells (Fig. 5d). Notably, DNA damage further enhanced GADD45A expression in IP and doxorubicin treated cells compared to doxorubicin alone in p53R213X mutants, reaching levels comparable to those observed with G418 co-treatment. In particular, except for IP18, a significant increase in transcripts can be observed in IPs considering the before and after damage induction that is not present with G418.

Discussion

Nonsense mutations, which introduce PTCs and yield truncated, non-functional proteins, underlie approximately 11% of genetic disorders and 12% of tumor suppressor gene alterations, including TP53. The TP53 gene encodes the p53 transcription factor, a pivotal regulator of genomic integrity; Loss-of-function mutations in TP53 are present in over 50% of human cancers, with approximately 10% specifically caused by nonsense mutations. These mutations not only drive tumorigenesis but are increasingly implicated in emerging p53-related diseases. In this context, TR therapy offers a targeted strategy to restore p53 expression and function.

In this study, we developed a novel pharmacophore model and identified four 1H-1,2,4-triazole derivatives (IP14, IP15, IP17 and IP18) as candidate TRIDs.

For pharmacophore model constructions we focused on PTC124 analogues and other non-aminoglycosidic small molecules as these compounds have demonstrated superior TR activity at lower doses with reduced toxicity compared to aminoglycoside antibiotics, which were excluded from the library. Moreover, non-aminoglycosidic small molecules appear to act specifically at PTCs, as demonstrated by the Cooperman group, whereas aminoglycoside antibiotics primarily affect general proofreading activity, also involving NTCs.

The four 1H-1,2,4-triazole derivatives were selected for their synthetic accessibility, enhances their potential for further therapeutic development, and to allow a systematic comparison of the triazole core with the 1,2,4-oxadiazole scaffold of PTC124, the reference compound and the only EMA-approved TRID, in order to evaluate the impact of core substitution on TR activity. Moreover these compounds exhibit favorable physicochemical properties, such as increased stability and predicted affinity for biological macromolecules.

Early-stage in vitro toxicological screening, a crucial step in candidate drug evaluation, revealed no detectable toxicity in two independent assays for any of the newly synthesized compounds. This absence of observable cytotoxicity allowed us to proceed with functional assays to evaluate their capacity to restore p53 expression and activity.

All four triazoles successfully reinstated basal p53 expression in H1299 p53-R213X cells, a validated in vitro model generated as described by Wiman et al. (2018)39, to levels comparable with those achieved using G418, the current reference compound for in vitro TR studies, while requiring a markedly lower concentration (12 µM vs 630 µM).

For this study, a single concentration was used to obtain preliminary data in this cell model; future experiments will include higher concentrations to evaluate potential dose-dependent effects on expression.

In contrast, under the same conditions, Ataluren failed to induce appreciable p53 expression, suggesting higher efficacy of the triazole derivatives under the tested conditions in this model, although differences in detection sensitivity and Ataluren’s known variability warrant cautious interpretation.

Upon induction of DNA damage with doxorubicin, treatment with these compounds enhanced the expression of full-length p53, confirming their capacity to restore stress-induced p53 responsiveness. Notably, IP17 and IP18 induced a more significant increase in p53 levels. Moreover, following genotoxic stress, a significant increase in nuclear p53 localization, visualized through immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. 4d) was observed only in IP treated cells, indicating both successful rescue of the full-length protein and recovery of its stress responsiveness activity.

In this context, to assess the transcriptional activity of the recovered p53 we evaluated the expression of the canonical p53 targets p21 and GADD45A by mean of qRT-PCR.

Notably, p21 expression was elevated not only in G418-treated cells, but also in untreated and non-transfected cells, likely due to p53-independent activation which can also be activated through oxidative or ER stress via transcription factors such as Sp140, AP-141, and ATF4/PERK42. In contrast, GADD45A, a more p53-specific target than p2143, showed a clear and significant increase after treatment with G418 and IP compounds, compared to untreated cells.

Moreover, DNA damage further enhanced GADD45A expression in IP- plus doxorubicin-treated p53R213X cells, reaching levels comparable to G418 co-treatment.

G418 is known to induce oxidative44 stress and promote p53 stabilization and target gene activation, such as CDKN1A, independently of both DNA damage stimuli and potentially even p53 itself, as evidenced by p21 activation in untreated and non-transfected cells. This was further confirmed by immunofluorescence, where elevated p53 signals were detected even before DNA damage–induced stress, complicating the interpretation of p21 induction as a marker of TR-mediated p53 recovery versus a general stress response.

IP-treated cells, however, maintained low basal p21 expression while retaining the ability to induce it upon DNA damage. Importantly, despite being tested at lower concentrations, IP compounds significantly upregulated GADD45A both before and after DNA damage, supporting their role in restoring p53-dependent transcription.

This conclusion is further substantiated by the absence of known tumor-associated missense mutations at TP53 codon 21339,45,46, suggesting that random amino acid incorporation via readthrough at this site is unlikely to impair p53 activity.

Although being limited to the use of H1299 p53R213X cells, in which the cDNA-based reporter construct is not fully subject to the classical NMD pathway and may alter p53 expression dynamics relative to physiological controls, the use of multiple complementary techniques consistently support p53 recovery and function following IP14, IP15, IP17, and IP18 treatment and further indicate that p21 expression induced by p53 recovery acts as a cofactor in GADD45A transcription. Moreover, the induction of p21 expression following p53 restoration is indirectly supported by the enhanced expression of GADD45A, as p21 acts as a cofactor in its transcriptional activation.

However, although recovery of nonsense-mutant p53 has been clearly demonstrated using an orthogonal approach, p53 transcript levels resulting from NMD escape in cDNA-based models (as in H1299 p53R213X) may not reflect those observed in cells carrying endogenous p53 mutations. This limitation highlights the need to test these molecules in endogenous mutant cell lines in future work to strengthen the basis for the clinical translatability of IP14, IP15, IP17, and IP18.

The predicted ADMET profiles of IP14, IP15, IP17, and IP18 further support their viability as drug candidates, highlighting favorable bioavailability, metabolic stability, and low predicted toxicity.

Analysis of the tested 1H-1,2,4-triazole derivatives, together with the 1,2,4-oxadiazole and chromenopyridine derivatives included in the libraries used to construct the pharmacophore model, indicates that hydrophobic and aromatic moieties flanking an aromatic core play a critical role in modulating readthrough activity, as exemplified by substitution at the C-5 position of the 1H-1,2,4-triazole derivatives. Compounds bearing phenyl or extended aromatic substituents at this position, such as Ataluren, IP14, IP15, IP17, and IP18, displayed higher activity than unsubstituted or aliphatic analogues, highlighting the likely contribution of π–π stacking and hydrophobic interactions. Moreover, the presence of a hydrogen-bond acceptor in combination with the aromatic core and its hydrophobic/aromatic substituents, as observed for the C-3 substituent of 1H-1,2,4-triazole derivatives, was consistently associated with activity, further supporting the importance of acceptor-mediated interactions in promoting activity.

More generally, across distinct scaffolds, a common structural theme emerges: a heteroatom-rich core, such as 1H-1,2,4-triazole, chromenopyridine, or 1,2,4-oxadiazole, capable of establishing strong directional interactions appears to be required.

Taken together, these findings suggest that bulky aromatic substituents combined with a heteroatom-rich core, as identified by the pharmacophore model and supported by in vitro validation, enhance TR-inducing activity.

However, although recent studies have begun to clarify the molecular basis of TR47, the absence of a precisely defined site or sites of action still limits the development of robust structure–activity relationships and reliable pharmacophore models. This highlights the need to synthesize new compounds to expand the chemical space of available scaffolds and substitution patterns for the development of novel TRIDs, refine ligand-based pharmacophore models, and ultimately identify the site, or sites, of action of this class of molecules.

This study provides preliminary but strong evidence supporting the potential of 1,2,4-triazole derivatives as TRIDs for the treatment of TP53 nonsense mutations and lays the groundwork for their future development in this field. Future work incorporating additional TP53 nonsense alleles, endogenous expression systems, and in vivo models will be essential to validate and extend the translational potential of the triazole derivatives reported here, turning the limitations of this work in opportunities for future development.

In conclusion, we have identified four promising firstly reported triazole derivatives capable of rescuing the expression and stress response of nonsense-mutated p53 in a validated reporter system. These compounds offer a potential strategy for addressing a common genetic cause of cancer, responsible for approximately one million new cancer cases globally each year48.

The integration of computational screening into our drug design process has increased the sustainability and efficiency of our development efforts. By reducing reliance on animal testing and speeding up early-stage evaluations, this approach enhances the likelihood of successful clinical translation.

Notably, this study provides the first evidence that 1,2,4-triazole-based compounds can function as low-toxicity TRIDs with therapeutic potential in targeting nonsense mutations. These findings are relevant not only to cancer therapy but also to emerging p53-associated conditions, including cardiovascular and neurodegenerative disorders, as well as to other diseases driven by nonsense mutations like Cystic Fbrosis49, Primary Immuno Deficiency Disease (PIRD)24 and Douchenne Muscular Dystrophy50, where chronic treatment would be incompatible with the basal oxidative stress induced by G418, an effect not observed with IP compounds.

Moreover, whereas TR-based therapy for chronic diseases associated with nonsense mutations, such as cystic fibrosis, would likely require life-long administration, the use of TRIDs in cancers driven by nonsense-mutated p53, either as monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapeutic agents, would likely be limited to shorter treatment courses, potentially reducing the risk of adverse effects. Notably, no such effects have been reported for non-aminoglycosidic TRIDs to date in either in vivo models26,51 or clinical trials52–57, even in prolonged treatment schedules typically associated with chronic indications.

Future studies will further validate the restoration of p53 functionality using additional reporter systems harboring constitutive nonsense-mutated p53, as well as in more complex in vivo models. These investigations will also help to elucidate the mechanism of action of these intriguing molecules. With their favorable ADMET profiles and ability in restoring p53 functionality, these compounds hold promise as candidates for future preclinical development and potential clinical translation, offering a compelling treatment strategy for cancers driven by nonsense mutations.

Materials and methods

Ligand-based Pharmacophore model design

The pharmacophore model was developed through a ligand-based approach exploiting available experimental data on our previously identified compounds and compounds present in literature with proven TR-inducing activity28–35,58,59, reported in Table S1 of Supplementary data, using LigandScout 4.4 software.

The operation of conformer generation and alignment on the library’s structure was first performed before the pharmacophore model generation. Subsequently, we proceed with the identification of a common core of shared features. These shared features served as the basis for merging additional features, thereby enriching the pharmacophore model. During the generation process, certain features were excluded to maintain the optimal features of the pharmacophoric model. The structural and geometric parameters of Model 1 are reported in Table S2.

The obtained pharmacophore model has undergone a validation process to assess its capability to discern between active and inactive molecules and ensure the trustfulness and accuracy of the pharmacophore analysis.

The validation was conducted via screening of libraries formed by 61 known active molecules and 2651 decoys generated using the online enhanced Databases of Useful Decoys (DUDe)60 and a Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve was produced.

After validation, the pharmacophore model was used to screen new “in house” libraries of molecule candidates for synthesis to find new hits compounds61.

Cell culture conditions

To perform assays for evaluating newly synthesized compounds’ biological activity, HCT116 colon cancer cells constitutively expressing a p53 wild-type, H1299 lung adenocarcinoma p53-/- cells, and H1299 lung adenocarcinoma p53R213X cells stably transfected, vector bearing a premature stop codon UGA in the TP53 cDNA were used (a kindly gift of Prof. K. Wiman, Karolinska Institute, Stockolm).

Cells were cultured in 1X DMEM medium with 4.5 g/L glucose, L-glutamine, and sodium pyruvate (Corning, Mediatech, Manassas, USA), to which 10% FBS (yourSial, Rome, Italy) was added. Cells were maintained in an incubator under optimal growth conditions at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Cytotoxic assay

Trypan Blue exclusion assay

200.000 HCT 116 cells were seeded in 60 mm dishes and then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The cells were then treated with the newly synthesized compounds dissolved in DMSO at a concentration of 12 μM for 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h. Cells treated with diluent (0.001% DMSO) were included as controls.

After treatments, treated and untreated cells were harvested by trypsinization, incubated in 0.04% Trypan Blue (Gibco™, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA) for 4 min, and were counted under a hemocytometer for visual counting. The total number of cells that retained the dye was noted.

MTT assay

5,000 HCT 116 cells per well were seeded in a 96-well plate in 100 μL of medium and then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The cells were then treated with the newly synthesized compounds dissolved in DMSO at a concentration of 12 μM for 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h. Cells treated with diluent (0.001% DMSO) were included as controls.

After treatments, 10 μL of MTT solution in PBS (5 mg/ml) was added to the cells and incubated for 4 h in the dark. Then, the medium was removed, and cells were washed with PBS before 100 μL of fresh DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystal, and a spectrophotometric measure was realized.

The absorbance of each well was measured at 560 nm and 570 nm by means of an ELISA plate reader.

Immunofluorescence microscopy

Cells (200 000 per well) were seeded in 12-well plates and treated the following day with either newly synthesized triazole compounds (12 µM; non-toxic for Ataluren derivatives as reported by Pibiri et al., 2020), Ataluren (12 µM), or G418 (630 µM), alone or in combination with doxorubicin (0.2 µg/mL).

After 24 h, cell fixation was performed on coverslips with frozen methanol for 1 min at room temperature, followed by membrane permeabilization with 200 µL of Triton X-100 0.01% (Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany) for 10 min. Finally, blocking was carried out to eliminate the presence of non-specific signal using 200 µL of albumin from chicken egg white 0.1% (Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany) for 1 h at room temperature. Then, it was performed the incubation with 20 µL of primary antibody in-mouse α-p53 DO-1 1:500, (Santa Cruz, Dallas, USA) diluted in albumin 0.1%, O.N. at 4 °C. After 24 h, it was performed the incubation with 20 µL of secondary antibody α-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 1:500, (Abcam, Waltham, USA) diluted in albumin 0.1%, at 37 °C for 1 h. To visualize the nuclei, 10 µL of SlowFade ® Diamond Antifade Mountant with DAPI (InvitrogenTM, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA) was used. Cells were visualized by mean of a fluorescence microscope (Axiovision, Zeiss) equipped with a 63 × magnification oil immersion objective and collected image were analyzed using Anovision 4.8 and ImageJ software (Rasband, W.S., ImageJ, U. S. National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA, https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/, 1997–2018).

Protein extraction and quantification

Cells (200,000 per well) were seeded in 60-mm dishes and treated the following day with either newly synthesized triazole compounds (12 µM), or with the positive controls G418 (630 µM) and Ataluren (12 µM), alone or in combination with doxorubicin (0.2 µg/mL).

After 24 h of treatment, proteins were extracted by adding 500 µL of Pierce™ RIPA Buffer (Thermo Scientific™, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 1% Halt™ Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (100X, Thermo Scientific™). Cells were detached using a cell scraper (Thermo Scientific™) and transferred to 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes. Lysates were incubated on ice at 4 °C for 1 h and subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant containing soluble proteins.

Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford colorimetric assay, with a standard curve generated using chicken egg white albumin (Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany) at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. Absorbance was measured at 595 nm, and protein concentrations were calculated by interpolation from the standard curve. Samples were stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Western blotting

A total of 30 µg of protein from each sample was mixed with Milli-Q water, 4X (Bolt™, Invitrogen™, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA), and 10X Sample Reducing Agent (Bolt™, Invitrogen™, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA) to a final volume of 15 µL. Samples were denatured at 70 °C for 10 min using a thermoblock. Subsequently, the samples were loaded onto 10% Bis–Tris Plus gels (Bolt™, Invitrogen™, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA) and subjected to electrophoresis in 1X MES SDS Running Buffer (Bolt™, Invitrogene, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA) alongside the Precision Plus Protein™ Kaleidoscope™ Prestained Protein Standards (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Electrophoretic separation was performed at 150 V and 100 mA for approximately 1 h.

Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Thermo Fisher Scientific) by overnight electroblotting at 4 °C. Membranes were probed with mouse anti-p53 (DO-1, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and rabbit anti-vinculin (N3C1, GeneTex, Irvine, CA, USA) primary antibodies to detect p53 and vinculin levels, respectively. Protein bands were visualized using the SuperSignal™ West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (Thermo Scientific™, Waltham, MA, USA). Image acquisition was performed using the ChemiDoc XRS system (Bio-Rad), and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (Rasband, W.S., ImageJ, U. S. National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA, https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/, 1997–2018). Uncropped Western blot membranes are provided in the Supplementary Data.

Real-time RT-PCR

RNA extraction from cell cultures

Cells (500 000 per well9 were treated with IP molecules (12 µM) or G418 (630 μM), alone or in combination with doxorubicin at 0.2 µg/mL for DNA damage induction. After 24 h, cells were detached with 0.05% trypsin (Corning, Mediatech, Manassas, USA), centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min to obtain the cellular pellet and RNA extraction was performed using the RNeasy® Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany).

The RNA samples were quantified using the NanoDrop™ 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific™, Waltham, USA) and RNA integrity was assessed by gel electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel (UltraPure™ Agarose, Invitrogen™, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA) in TAE 1X buffer. Afterward, samples were stored at − 80 °C for further analysis.

Reverse transcription and real-time RT-PCR

Reverse transcription was performed using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), with 1 µg of total RNA in a final reaction volume of 10 µL. Synthesized cDNA was stored at − 20 °C for subsequent analyses. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was carried out using PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Two separate reaction mixtures were prepared: one for the target genes GADD45A and CDKN1A (p21), and another for the reference gene GAPDH. cDNA was added to the respective reaction mixes, and each sample was analyzed in technical triplicates. Amplification and quantification were performed on a 7300 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems), and data were analyzed using the 7300 System Software. Primer sequences used for qRT-PCR are listed in Table S5 of Supplementary data.

In-silico ADMET properties prediction

To evaluate the ADMET properties online tools SwissADME (Swiss Institute of Bioinformatics, Switzerland)36, ADMETlab 3.037 and ProTox 3.038 were deployed.

The estimated pharmacokinetic parameter with SwissADME includes lipophilicity, water-soluble, gastrointestinal (GI) absorption, blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeation, P-glycoprotein (P-gp) substrate prediction, major CYP isoform substrate prediction, CYP isoform inhibition profiles, plasma clearance, half-life and Liepinski law violation.

The endpoint estimated with ProTox 3.0 includes: LD50, Toxicity Class, Organ toxicity, and Toxicity Endpoint.

Statistics

All data are expressed as mean values ± standard error of the mean (SD). Statistical analysis was performed by Student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA when appropriate by GraphPad Prism software (Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA) version 10.1.1 for MacOS. A probability value (p) of less than 0.05 was regarded as significant and indicated in relevant graphs as one symbol (*) for p < 0.05, two symbols (**) for p < 0.01, three symbols (***) for p < 0.001, and four symbols (****) for p < 0.0001.
