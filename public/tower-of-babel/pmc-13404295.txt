Clinical ethics training needs among pediatricians and pediatric residents in Ethiopia: conference-based cross-sectional survey
Authors: Betelehem Kassa, Addishiwot Melesse, Eyob Kebede Etissa, Bitseat W Haile, Atnafu Mekonnen Tekleab, Stephanie Kukora
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPediatricians encounter ethically challenging cases in clinical practice throughout the world. In Ethiopia, ethically distressing pediatric cases are heightened by limited resources, lack of ethical frameworks to guide clinical care and withdrawal of supportive therapies, absence of child protection laws, and dearth of trained ethics consultants or institutional ethics committees providing clinical ethics consultation. Ethics training remains limited in undergraduate medical programs and absent in the resident curriculum at most institutions.MethodsA survey of Pediatric Residents and Pediatricians attending the Ethiopian Pediatric Society’s 24th Annual Conference on March 2023 was conducted using a self-administered, clinical ethics needs assessment. Data were checked, cleaned, and entered into SPSS for analysis. The magnitude and the association between the different variables in relation to the outcome was measured and a 95% confidence interval (CI) and P values below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsA total of 200 participants completed the survey (response rate 92% of conference attendees). Most of the study participants (85.8%) felt their clinical ethics trainings were inadequate to address the ethical challenges they faced frequently. Furthermore, 58% reported no established/formal method to address ethical concern in their respective Hospitals. Most common ethical dilemmas faced by participants included refusal of treatment by patient/family, disagreement between the healthcare team and patient/family, withholding/withdrawing life-prolonging measures (70%, 59.5%, 51.5%). Most participants (89.5%) wanted additional training. The common areas of interest for further training were withholding/withdrawing life-prolonging measures, refusal of treatment by patient/family and legal issues (45%, 46.5%, 40% respectively).ConclusionEthiopian pediatricians and pediatric residents perceive that their training on clinical ethics is inadequate to address the ethical challenges they face in their daily practice. Creation of culturally relevant, pediatric-specific ethics educational programs for Ethiopian pediatricians is much needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-026-01487-3.Keywords: Clinical ethics education, Pediatric ethics, Ethics training needs, Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs)

Background

Pediatricians routinely encounter ethically complex clinical situations in clinical practice. They must balance medical knowledge, professional obligations, and moral values to act in the best interests of children and their families [1]. Pediatric practice poses unique ethical challenges, including evolving decision-making capacity mirroring development and assent as well as complexities around surrogate decision-making, family dynamics, and limits to parental authority in decision-making [2]. End-of-life (EOL) discussions, decisions to limit invasive or anticipated non-beneficial procedures and therapies, and disagreements around values, acceptable quality of life, and what is in a child’s best interest also create ethical tensions [3]. Clinicians must be prepared to navigate medical, developmental, familial, cultural, and social factors when making clinical judgments.

The ethical landscape in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) is shaped by different contextual factors, such as resource limitations, inequitable access to care, and sociocultural influences on clinical decision-making [4]. Frameworks to guide ethical decision-making developed in high-income countries (HICs) cannot easily be applied as they may be culturally inappropriate or exclude or de-emphasize resource distribution dilemmas and justice concerns compared to autonomy-based conflicts which dominate ethical discussions when adequate resources exist. Limited existing evidence from Ethiopia suggests that physicians encounter ethically challenging situations related to resource allocation, financial barriers to care, disclosure issues, and decision-making for patients lacking capacity [5]. Such context-specific ethical challenges underscore the need for ethics education tailored to local clinical realities. Additionally, in many LMIC settings, including Ethiopia, clinicians must address these ethically difficult situations informally at the bedside, often without ethics infrastructure like an institutional ethics committee or access to trained clinical ethicists [6].

Despite recognizing of the importance of ethics education, structured clinical ethics training remains inconsistently integrated into postgraduate medical education globally. Reports from the US identify a lack of ethics training for pediatric residents [7] and that existing programs do not meet the actual ethical challenges of pediatric residents [8]. Even Pediatricians who report having formal ethics training, describe a deficiency of knowledge on ethics, supporting the need for more targeted educational methods [9]. In many LMIC contexts, ethics training remains fragmented or focused primarily on research ethics rather than clinical decision-making. Training needs assessments conducted in African settings highlight persistent gaps in ethics knowledge and emphasize the importance of sustained, locally relevant educational programs [10]. Educational interventions in LMIC residency programs demonstrate that contextually adapted ethics curricula can improve trainees’ confidence and ability to recognize ethical issues, particularly when interactive and case-based teaching approaches are used [11]. Evidence also suggests that clinicians who receive formal ethics education demonstrate improved ethical knowledge, attitudes, and clinical practices compared with those without training [12].

Although limited ethics training is included in undergraduate medical programs In Ethiopia, more targeted, pediatric specific ethics education is often absent from resident curricula in Pediatrics and Child Health. As a result, pediatricians and trainees rely on experiential learning rather than structured ethical frameworks when navigating complex clinical scenarios. Limited data exist describing clinical ethics training needs among pediatricians and pediatric residents in Ethiopia and other LMICs. For this reason, we sought to identify perceived gaps in ethics education, understand the specific educational needs of pediatric clinicians, and inform the development of culturally grounded and context-sensitive ethics training programs to support pediatric care by conducting a conference-based cross-sectional survey of Ethiopian Pediatricians.

Methods

Survey instrument

A structured questionnaire, written in English, was used to collect information. As there are many native languages and dialects in Ethiopia, English is used by physicians for medical education, formal discussions of patient care, and at academic conferences. Survey items were developed based on a targeted review of existing literature on clinical ethics challenges and ethics education among physicians and trainees, including studies conducted in both high-income and low- and middle-income settings [1, 5–8, 13–15]. The initial draft was reviewed and refined by a group of experts in pediatric clinical care and bioethics, including pediatricians practicing in Ethiopia and a U.S.-based ethicist with experience in global health. Feedback focused on content relevance, clarity, and cultural appropriateness.

Survey items included sociodemographic characteristics, and queries about the frequency and severity of clinical ethical dilemmas encountered in practice, ethics education, frequent scenarios causing ethical stress and perceived needs around ethics support. Formal definitions of terms such as ‘ethical difficulty,’ ‘ethical stress,’ and ‘ethical dilemma’ were not provided within the survey instrument. These terms were used based on commonly understood clinical usage; however, variation in interpretation among respondents is possible. The questionnaire was pre-tested on a sample of five pediatric residents at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital using a cognitive interview process [13]. These individuals were not included in the study’s findings. This feedback was used to modify questions on the standard questionnaire that need to be tweaked to better fit the Ethiopian community’s cultural and social standards. Given the focused nature of the survey and the relatively homogeneous target population, this sample size was deemed sufficient to identify major issues with clarity, comprehension, and contextual relevance. The investigators checked the completeness of the questionnaires before data entry, analysis, and interpretation. The survey instrument is provided in the supplementary materials.

Study population and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional survey study at the Ethiopian Pediatric Society’s 24th annual conference in 2023 in Addis Abeba, Ethiopia. The Ethiopian Pediatric Society (EPS) is a non-profit professional organization that represents pediatric healthcare workers, residents, educators, and others involved in childcare. It was founded in 1995 and currently has 698 registered members. All pediatricians and pediatric residents who were members of EPS and present at the conference were eligible. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained prior to the survey. Since no other similar study has been conducted, the required sample sizes of eligible participants for the study were determined using a single population proportion formula with the following assumptions: 80% power, 95% confidence interval, a margin of error of 5%, and P = 50% (Calculations provided in Supplement 2). The sample size was calculated to be 184 individuals. A 10% non-response rate was added to get a total minimum sample size of 202.

Data collection

A physical copy of the questionnaire was distributed in person during the conference period over two days. Members of the research team were present to distribute questionnaires and provide brief verbal instructions. Participants were provided time (12 min) to complete the survey independently and returned it to the research team upon completion. Participants were incentivized with American candy. Surveys were collected and reviewed for completion before being entered manually into EpiData Entry V4.6.0.x. Partially completed surveys were included in the analysis if key outcome variables were available. Missing responses were handled using pairwise deletion (2 questionnaires). Data cleaning included checking for completeness, consistency, and data entry errors prior to analysis.

Data analysis

The data was entered, coded, and cleaned using EpiData statistical software, and analysis was performed using SPSS version 25. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and survey responses. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were summarized using means or medians, as appropriate.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital, and St. Paul’s Millennium Medical College, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation and were provided the opportunity to decline or withdraw from the study without repercussions. Surveys were anonymous with de-identified responses; identification numbers were used throughout the data collection and analysis process to ensure participants’ confidentiality was maintained.

Results

Participant characteristics

Of the 698 registered members of the Ethiopian Pediatric Society, 218 attended the 24th Annual Meeting in 2023. Among attendees, 200 completed the survey, yielding a response rate of 92%.

Most respondents (81%) were 40 years of age or younger, and a majority had fewer than four years of experience practicing as pediatric physicians. Nearly half (47%) had completed or were currently pursuing subspecialty training. The majority of participants (78%) were employed in government hospitals, with 52% working in referral or teaching institutions. Slightly more than half of respondents (52%) were male. Over half (54%) reported working between 40 and 59 h per week. A substantial majority (84%) indicated that faith or religion plays a significant role in their lives. The study participants level of need on ethical education

Among respondents working in hospital settings, 116 (58%) reported the absence of a formalized process for managing ethical issues. Furthermore, 147 participants (74%) indicated that their institutions did not conduct routine team-based audits or debriefings following ethically challenging cases. Although collegial discussion of ethical issues was common (81%), dissatisfaction with team decisions remained high, with 147 participants (74%) reporting dissatisfaction with the outcomes of ethical decision-making in most cases.

Types and frequency of ethical dilemmas

More than half of participants (52%) reported experiencing ethical dilemmas related to withholding or withdrawing life-prolonging treatment, and 45% indicated a desire for additional training in this area. disagreements among healthcare team members were also frequently reported: 47% described such dilemmas as difficult to resolve, 11% identified them as the most frequently encountered ethical issue, 20% reported that they were addressed during training, and 30% expressed a need for additional education on managing these conflicts. Similarly, ethical challenges involving truth-telling were reported by a substantial proportion of participants. Specifically, 35% described these dilemmas as difficult to resolve, 15% identified them as frequently encountered, 14% indicated that they were addressed during training, and 30% expressed a desire for further training in this area.

Discussion

Our survey adds to the growing body of literature around clinical ethical distress and ethics education needs of clinicians practicing in LMICS, and highlights the specific ethical challenges faced by pediatricians and pediatric residents in Ethiopia. Our findings demonstrate that many clinicians experience ethical stress, and that there is an unmet need for ethics education personalized to the Ethiopian healthcare context.

The vast majority of participants in our study reported facing ethical difficulties in their clinical practice at least rarely, but also note structural deficiencies in ethics support systems that are often available in HICs. Many participants reported a lack of formalized mechanisms to address ethical concerns, absence of routine ethical debriefings, and limited institutional infrastructure to support ethical decision-making. Satisfaction with ethical decisions were low, suggesting that informal peer consultation alone is insufficient. These findings are consistent with studies from other resource-limited settings, where clinicians often rely on workplace experience and senior colleagues rather than structured ethics frameworks or formal training [14, 15].

To combat the frequent ethical distress in the context of limited support, most respondents expressed a need for additional ethics education, with 74% indicating a desire for more in-depth training. Although 67% of participants had received ethics education during undergraduate training, only a small proportion felt adequately prepared to address real-world ethical challenges. Many participants reported limited or absent ethics training during residency, suggesting that there had been little attention paid in their ethics education to the specific challenges of pediatric care. Majority of participants (90%) expressing a desire for further structured training during residency suggests that existing educational experiences may be insufficient in preparing clinicians to navigate real-world ethical challenges. These findings highlight a clear gap in exposure to ethics education.

The high prevalence of ethical challenges identified in this study is consistent with previous research. Studies among pediatric trainees, even in HICs where there is adequate ethics support and there are minimal additional challenges posed by resource constraints, have demonstrated that ethical dilemmas occur frequently and that clinicians often feel inadequately prepared despite receiving formal ethics education [6–8, 16].

Importantly, the ethical stress described by participants in this study can have negative consequences in clinical practice. A growing body of literature demonstrates that unresolved ethical distress may progress into moral injury. Unresolved experiences accumulate and intensify over and contributes to emotional exhaustion and professional dissatisfaction [17]. Studies in pediatric and neonatal settings further show that chronic exposure to ethically challenging or emotionally distressing clinical situations increases risk of compassion fatigue, burnout, and secondary traumatic stress, all of which can negatively affect clinician well-being and patient care [18, 19]. Importantly, burnout has been associated with leaving clinical practice, highlighting the potential workforce consequences of unaddressed ethical stress [20]. These risks are particularly concerning in LMICs where healthcare workforce retention is already fragile, and personnel shortages are among the serious resource constraints impacting care provision. Strengthening ethics education and institutional ethics support may serve as a critical strategy for clinician retention and health system sustainability.

Interestingly, the ethical dilemmas identified by Ethiopian Pediatricians differ from findings in HIC settings. Refusal of treatment by patients or families emerged as the most frequently encountered dilemma, differing from findings in European studies where disagreement between healthcare teams and families is more commonly reported [16]. For example, a pediatrician in Ethiopia caring for a critically ill child with a treatable condition may face a situation where the family declines life-saving treatment due to inability to afford care. This difference likely reflects socioeconomic factors in Ethiopia, where financial barriers to care, individual patient responsibility to bear healthcare costs, and resource constraints play a significant role in clinical decision-making. A national survey conducted among Ethiopian physicians from a range of specialties reported similar findings, with resource distribution challenges and inability to pay for treatment representing the ethical dilemma most frequently endorsed by respondents [14]. Refusal of treatment may occur in chronic or complex conditions requiring prolonged hospitalization or repeated interventions, where costs such as lost income, transportation, medication coast, and caregiving responsibilities further influence decision-making.

It is also important to note that majority of the prior literature on resource-related ethical dilemmas in Ethiopia has focused on physicians who primarily care for adult patients [14]. Refusal of treatment in pediatric practice carries additional ethical complexity. In HICs, legal frameworks often allow clinicians or the state to intervene when a minor is at risk of serious harm, thereby shifting the ethical analysis to protection of the child’s best interests [16]. In contrast, in settings where such legal mechanisms are less clearly defined and enforced, clinicians may have limited recourse when families decline treatment due to financial constraints or sociocultural factors. Unlike adult patients, children depend on surrogate decision-makers whose choices may be shaped by external pressures. This dynamic place pediatric clinicians in an ethically difficult position, amplifying moral distress within LMIC contexts.

Respondents in our study also indicated that dilemmas related to withholding or withdrawing life-prolonging treatment were common and difficult to resolve. While these findings align with HIC studies demonstrating that end-of-life decision-making is among the most challenging ethical areas for pediatric clinicians [16], additional barriers in Ethiopia may add complexity to these already challenging situations. The lack of laws guiding withdrawal, lack of palliative care specialists, challenges in discussing serious news, resource limitations in private spaces, lack of support staff like social work, medications for end-of-life care, cultural challenges are some of the challenges frequently faced by clinicians in Ethiopia and other LMICs [4, 21–23]. These findings must be interpreted within the broader context of pediatric healthcare delivery in Ethiopia. Ethiopia has a predominantly young population, with approximately 45.4% under the age of 18. Despite this, pediatric healthcare resources remain limited. Access to specialized services such as neonatal and pediatric intensive care is constrained, with even referral centers having only 6 to 8 PICU beds per center. National healthcare expenditure also remains low; total health spending was approximately US$200 million for the 2024–2025 period, and per capita health expenditure was only US$27.07 in 2022, far below international recommendations [24]. Out-of-pocket costs continue to represent a substantial barrier to care for many families. These structural constraints shape the ethical challenges encountered by pediatric clinicians, often forcing difficult decisions related to resource allocation, treatment limitation, and financial accessibility of care.

Comprehensive capacity building through global partnerships in not only ethics, but also sensitive communication and end-of-life care provision for pediatric and neonatal patients, is deeply needed. This ideally would include education and skill-building for individual clinicians, development of institutional policies and support mechanisms, and collaboration and advocacy by the government, professional societies, and public health organizations at a national level.

The findings support the need for structured, culturally relevant ethics education integrated into pediatric residency programs. Evidence suggests that case-based teaching and longitudinal ethics curricula are more effective than isolated lectures to prepare clinicians for real-world ethical challenges [25]. Given the high burden of ethically complex cases and limited access to clinical ethicists, inserting ethics training within existing clinical activities such as case conferences, mortality meetings, or interdisciplinary discussions may be a sustainable strategy. Additionally, strengthening institutional ethics capacity through training for clinical ethicists, ethics consultants, and multidisciplinary ethics committees may be an opportunity for global partnerships between HICs and LMICS to further support clinicians [6].

Though this study represents one of the first national assessments of clinical ethics training needs among pediatric clinicians in Ethiopia and provides valuable insight into both individual and institutional gaps, it has several limitations. The use of convenience sampling at a national conference may introduce selection bias as our sample may not fully represent all pediatricians in the country. Due to conflict and regional security concerns at the time of the survey, pediatricians from some regions were unable to attend the conference were not represented. Likewise, there was a high proportion of respondents who were trainees, and who worked in government hospitals that were teaching and referral centers; this may reflect which physicians are supported to attend a national pediatrics conference with both time off and travel expenses. This may also limit generalizability to many of the practicing pediatricians in the country. Additionally, self-reported measures may be subject to recall or social desirability bias. Furthermore, key constructs such as ‘ethical stress,’ ‘ethical difficulty,’ and ‘ethical dilemma’ were not explicitly defined in the survey instrument, which may have resulted in variability in how respondents interpreted these terms.

Conclusions

Ethiopian pediatricians report that their current clinical ethics training and institutional ethics support are inadequate to address the ethical challenges they face in their daily practice. Ethical dilemmas and moral distress are common, and there is a clear need for structured, culturally grounded ethics education integrated into pediatric residency programs. Additional research should focus on implementing and evaluating context-sensitive models for ethics education and institutional support.
