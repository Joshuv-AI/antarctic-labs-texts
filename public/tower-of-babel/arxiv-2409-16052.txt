Denoising graph superresolution toward improved collider event reconstruction
Nilotpal Kakati, Etienne Dreyer, Eilam Gross

Abstract
In preparation for Higgs factories and energy-frontier facilities, future colliders are moving toward high-granularity calorimeters to improve reconstruction quality. However, the cost and construction complexity of such detectors is substantial, making software-based approaches like superresolution an attractive complementary tool. This study explores integrating superresolution techniques into an LHC-like reconstruction pipeline to effectively enhance calorimeter granularity and suppress noise. We find that this software preprocessing step significantly improves reconstruction quality at fixed detector granularity, narrowing the performance gap to a finer calorimeter without closing it. To demonstrate its impact, we propose a novel transformer-based particle-flow model that offers improved particle reconstruction quality and interpretability. Our results serve as a proof of concept, demonstrating the potential of superresolution to enhance reconstruction in collider environments.



I
Introduction

The energy and precision frontiers of high-energy physics are driven by technological advances both in generating particle beams and in recording and reconstructing particle collisions. Detectors at current and future collider facilities are designed to extract precise information about outgoing particles using extensive arrays of sensors, including calorimeter layers. Calorimeters measure energy by instigating a shower of secondary particles, distributing and absorbing the incident particle’s energy as it propagates through successive layers of dense material. To help determine particle direction and type, as well as separate nearby particles, calorimeters are segmented into a gridlike array of cells.

Although calorimeter performance is generally characterized by energy resolution, spatial resolution (inversely related to granularity) assumes a crucial role in dense collision environments. At the Large Hadron Collider (LHC)
[
1
]
proton-proton collisions yield dense environments rich in hadronic jets and numerous simultaneous collisions (pileup), with a nearly tenfold increase expected in future high-luminosity runs
[
2
]
. To reconstruct jet properties accurately, particle-flow (PFlow) algorithms
[
3
,
4
]
leverage tracking information in tandem with calorimeter and other measurements. However, low-granularity calorimeters have limited imaging capabilities, constraining the performance of pattern reconstruction in PFlow algorithms such as assigning energy deposits to tracks, identifying neutral hadrons, and employing software compensation techniques
[
5
]
. These challenges have positioned
particle-flow calorimetry

[
6
]
as a leading design paradigm for future detectors, enabling the 3% jet energy resolution required at future electron-positron Higgs factories to distinguish hadronic
W
and
Z
boson decays
[
7
]
.

In practical applications, calorimeter granularity is constrained by cost as well as mechanical and electrical engineering challenges, leading to less than optimal granularity. Superresolution (SR) techniques, which have been extensively studied in the field of image processing
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
, offer the potential to mitigate the limitations of the spatial resolution intrinsic to the calorimeter design by imposing physical priors learned from simulation. By reconstructing high-resolution (HR) data from low-resolution (LR) inputs, these techniques could, in essence, recover substructure features obscured by coarse segmentation, thereby maximizing the performance of a given detector design.

In this study, we explore the integration of superresolution techniques within the core pipeline of particle reconstruction in collider experiments. The initial stage of any collider experiment involves reconstructing low-level particles or particlelike objects, referred to as PFlow objects. By applying superresolution to calorimeter data, we aim to enhance the accuracy of PFlow objects and, thereby, that of higher-level objects used in physics analyses, such as jets, leptons, and photons. We anticipate our approach to be especially useful for reconstructing boosted topologies such as top jets, boosted Higgs decays, and hadronically decaying tau leptons.

I.1
Related work

Calorimeter SR was first explored in
[
15
]
, which demonstrated a proof of concept using a neural network-based method with a simplified detector and physics setup. Since then, SR has found various applications in particle physics, such as enhancing jet constituent images with generative adversarial networks for improved jet property estimation
[
16
]
, employing normalizing flows for upsampling calorimeter showers for fast simulation
[
17
]
, and producing high-resolution photon shower images
[
18
]
. In the broader context, a large number of generative models have been developed for fast calorimeter shower simulation (see
[
19
]
for a review). Recent work
[
20
]
also highlights the potential of SR for enhancing event reconstruction in neutrino telescopes.

I.2
Key contributions

Our approach offers two enhancements to calorimeter data to benefit the downstream reconstruction task. The first is the suppression of electronic noise that distorts recorded energy deposits, which we perform by training our model to denoise the energy in each cell. Second, our approach improves spatial resolution by predicting a high-granularity graph of calorimeter cells, allowing the reconstruction of finer details such as nearby particles. Representing the calorimeter data as a graph is a natural choice for handling irregular geometry, nontrivial spatial correlations, and the sparsity in the recorded signal. Superresolution on graph data, where the number of nodes gets upscaled, is not an entirely new concept
[
21
,
22
]
but is uncommon in the literature. To assess the improvement in reconstruction, we introduce a novel transformer-based PFlow model, based on predicting hypergraphs, as introduced in
[
23
]
. This model benefits from modernized architecture and provides improved interpretability, allowing the impact of superresolution to be analyzed transparently.

We summarize our contributions as follows:

(i)

demonstrating superresolution with a full particle reconstruction chain,

(ii)

denoising energy deposits,

(iii)

graph-based architecture, and

(iv)

novel transformer-based particle-flow algorithm.

III
Methods

III.1
Continuous normalizing flows

Continuous normalizing flow
[
29
]
is a type of generative model that transforms a simple base probability distribution
p_{0}(x_{0})
into a more complex target distribution
p_{1}(x_{1})
through a continuous sequence of invertible transformations. This transformation is governed by the following neural ordinary differential equation:

\frac{dx}{dt}=v_{\theta}\left(x,t\right),

(1)

where
v_{\theta}
is the vector field parametrized by a neural network and
t\in\left[0,1\right]
. Flow matching, as proposed in
[
30
]
, is a technique for training continuous normalizing flows that aims to match the learned flow
v_{\theta}\left(t,x\right)
induced by the differential equations of the model with a target flow
u_{t}\left(x\right)
. The flow-matching objective can be defined as follows:

L_{FM}\left(\theta\right)=E_{t,p_{t}(x)}\lVert v_{\theta}\left(t,x\right)-u_{t}\left(x\right)\rVert^{2}.

(2)

The objective, however, is intractable because the target flow
u_{t}
, that generates the desired probability paths between
p_{0}
and
p_{1}
, is typically not available in closed form. As a solution,
[
30
]
also proposed a conditional flow-matching objective where
u_{t}
and
p_{t}
can be constructed in a sample-conditional manner:

L_{CFM}\left(\theta\right)=E_{t,q(z),p_{t}(x|z)}\lVert v_{\theta}\left(t,x\right)-u_{t}\left(x|z\right)\rVert^{2},

(3)

where
t\in\left[0,1\right]
,
z\sim q\left(z\right)
, and
x\sim p_{t}\left(x|z\right)
.
L_{CFM}
is fully tractable and the authors showed that optimizing it is equivalent to optimizing the
L_{FM}
objective. The probability path
p_{t}
and the target flow
u_{t}
can be defined in various ways
[
30
,
31
]
. For our studies, we use the formulation from the original paper
[
30
]
:

\displaystyle p_{t}\left(x|z\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\left(x|tx_{1},\left(t\sigma-t+1\right)^{2}\right),

(4)

\displaystyle u_{t}\left(x|z\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{1}-\left(1-\sigma\right)x}{1-\left(1-\sigma\right)t}.

This defines a straight path between the standard normal and a Gaussian distribution of width
\sigma
around
x_{1}
. We chose
\sigma
to be
10^{-4}
following the original paper. We used the
torchdiffeq

[
32
]
library with the Dormand-Prince (dopri5) solver for generation.

Superresolution model

The core concept of our superresolution model is inspired by the work
[
14
]
. To allow for irregular cell geometry and to cope with the sparsity of the recorded energy deposits, we represent the data as graphs. Each low-resolution graph is expanded into a high-resolution graph, where each low-resolution cell is divided into
f_{\eta}\times f_{\phi}
high-resolution cells. For each high-resolution cell, the target is its truth energy value, while the corresponding low-resolution energy serves as the conditional input, as illustrated in Fig.
2
. Although nearest-neighbor edges among nodes in the high-resolution graph would, in general, be motivated for larger graphs, we model them as fully connected for simplicity.

Figure 3

The superresolution architecture.
\odot
indicates concatenation.

Our model employs a transformer-based architecture to predict the vector field
v_{t}
, as illustrated in Fig.
3
. The process begins by upsampling the low-resolution graph through repetition, which serves as the conditional input. Subsequently, a copy of the high-resolution graph, initialized with random values
x_{0}
, is created. These two high-resolution graphs are then stacked, providing each node with both the upsampled information and the randomly initialized values. This combined input is fed into a transformer network, generating a neighborhood-aware representation that aids in understanding the energy deposition profile of the incident particles. Following this, a multilayer perceptron (MLP) predicts the vector field
v_{t}
for each node. To advance the graph, we integrate
v_{t}
over a small time interval
\Delta t
, yielding
x_{0+\Delta t}
. This updated graph
x_{0+\Delta t}
is then used as input, along with the conditional information, for the subsequent transformer iteration, and the process of predicting
v_{t}
is repeated. Finally, at
t=1
, the output
x_{1}
represents the denoised, superresolved graph.

III.3
Ensemble sampling

Generation with continuous normalizing flow is inherently a stochastic process. While this randomness is often beneficial in applications like image generation, where variation is desired, it poses a challenge in the superresolution of calorimeter data, where there is a specific deterministic target. Ideally, the network’s output should fluctuate around the true target if functioning correctly. To address this, we employ an ensemble approach. Unlike typical ensemble methods that involve training multiple copies of the model, our approach simply involves passing the same conditional input through the same model multiple times to produce the ensemble output, which is then averaged to obtain the final prediction. This technique helps reduce the impact of fluctuations and brings the output closer to the true target. However, it is important to acknowledge that due to the stochastic noise inherently added by the calorimeter, recovering the exact true energy is not possible; we can only approximate it.

III.4
Particle-flow model

Machine learning-based particle-flow models for LHC-like environments have been extensively explored
[
34

35

23

. Among these,
HGPflow

[
23
,
42
]
distinguishes itself by explicitly learning the energy assignment matrix between inputs and outputs rather than treating the reconstruction as a black-box regression. This approach offers a high degree of interpretability, as it allows for the direct inspection of how recorded energy in the detector is assigned to reconstructed particles. To better understand the impact of superresolution, we aimed for a similarly interpretable model. Here, we present a novel particle-flow architecture that builds on the principles of HGPflow but replaces the
iterative learning
approach with a
supervised attention
mechanism.

PFlow algorithms usually combine track and calorimeter inputs. However, since we are primarily interested in studying the impact of calorimeter SR, we do not include tracks in the input. To indicate this, we refer to our model as PFlow
–
, since the architecture itself can be used in the same capacity as HGPflow.

Figure

The transformer’s output is processed by two branches. One branch predicts the particle cardinality using an MLP, which treats this prediction as a classification task rather than regression. While predicting cardinality as a global metric is not the most robust approach, and many existing models
[
23
,
38
]
handle this as a padding problem (predicting a flag for each particle to indicate if it’s real or padded), we opted for a simpler classification approach given our lower cardinality and simpler environment.

The second branch focuses on kinematic prediction. It achieves this by learning the fractional energy distribution of each cell over the particles as shown in Eq. (
5
), where
E_{ai}
represents the energy deposited by particle
a
in cell
i
. This distribution, referred to as the incidence matrix in HGPflow
[
23
]
, was originally defined for topological clusters rather than individual cells. By using cells directly, we retain more detailed information, as topological clusters aggregate the features of multiple cells, potentially losing fine-grained details. Working with cells increases the complexity, since the cardinality can be an order of magnitude or more above that of clusters. In exchange it eliminates the dependency on topoclustering, which is not guaranteed to be an optimal algorithm. We implement this approach using an attentionlike framework, where particles and cells are processed through two MLPs to obtain the key (
K
) and query (
Q
), respectively. The resulting attention weights are computed as usual [Eq. (
6
)] and supervised to match the target incidence matrix. Given that we operate on sets, permutation invariance of particles is essential, and we achieve this using Hungarian matching
[
43
]
:

\displaystyle\text{target incidence},\quad I_{ai}

\displaystyle=\frac{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{energy deposited by particle }a\\[2.0pt]
\text{in cell }i\end{array}}{\begin{array}[]{c}\text{total energy deposited}\\[2.0pt]
\text{in cell }i\end{array}}

(5)

\displaystyle=\frac{E_{ai}}{\sum_{a}E_{ai}},

\displaystyle\text{predicted incidence}

\displaystyle=\text{attention weights}

(6)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Softmax}\left(\frac{QK^{T}}{\sqrt{d_{K}}}\right).

Once the energy-based incidence matrix is determined, the
proxy
kinematics of the reconstructed particles can be calculated from the associated cells using the approach outlined in
[
23
]
:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\hat{E}_{a}=\sum_{i}\left(I_{ai}\cdot E_{i}\right);\qquad\hat{p}_{\mathrm{T}_{a}}=\frac{\hat{E}_{a}}{\cosh\hat{\eta}_{a}}\,(\text{zero mass}),

(7)

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}_{a}=\frac{1}{\hat{E}_{a}}\sum_{i}\left(I_{ai}\cdot E_{i}\cdot\eta_{i}\right);\qquad\hat{\phi}_{a}=\frac{1}{\hat{E}_{a}}\sum_{i}\left(I_{ai}\cdot E_{i}\cdot\phi_{i}\right).

In HGPflow
[
23
]
, these proxy properties are further refined using MLPs, a step we omit in our default configuration. Keeping the kinematics an explicit function of the incidence matrix and the cell coordinates ensures that any difference in performance between the two resolutions can be attributed to the input cells rather than to a learned correction. This choice makes the comparison transparent, but it also ties the angular resolution to the granularity of the input, since the predicted position can only be expressed as an energy weighted combination of cell centers. To quantify the effect of this restriction, we additionally train a variant in which the proxy kinematics are refined by a correction network, following
[
23
]
. The correction is implemented as a residual on top of Eq. (
7
). It takes the full per-particle representation as input, and is initialized to zero, so that training begins from the uncorrected estimator. We apply it to both the low- and the high-resolution models and report the results.

We trained two identical models using the same set of hyperparameters: one with the low-resolution measured cells and the other with the high-resolution predicted cells. The particle-flow models were trained on the superresolution predictions for the 250 000 training events, and all results are reported on a separate sample of 10 000 events that was not used to train either the superresolution model or the particle-flow models. For the incidence loss, we followed a procedure similar to that in
[
23
]
, pairing truth and predicted particles using the Hungarian algorithm with the Kullback-Leibler divergence between their incidence values as the metric. Cardinality loss was handled using cross-entropy, and the sum of the incidence and cardinality losses was used for optimization. The same procedure was used for the variants with the correction network, with the addition of per-particle kinematic terms for the angular coordinates and the energy, without which the correction network receives no gradient. Their weights were chosen so that each term contributes comparably at the start of training, and these models were initialized from the corresponding uncorrected model and trained for a further 60 epochs. When a particle interacts with calorimeter cells, it often deposits a significant portion of its energy in a single cell, making that cell “hotter” than the others. Consequently, the
\eta

This results in the predicted particles not spreading evenly across the continuous space but instead forming diffused clumps at the midpoints of the highest resolution cells. In our case these are the cells in the ECAL1 layer. The effect is shown directly in Appendix
A

\eta

\phi

V
Discussion

The results from the studies above indicate that superresolution is a viable and promising approach for improving reconstruction. In the following paragraphs, we address the question of how these improvements should be understood, discuss the path toward deployment in real experiments, and present areas of future work.

V.1
Superresolution or hallucination

A central question in this context is understanding the origin of the “extra” information that enhances low-resolution inputs into high-resolution outputs. The key insight lies in the patterns learned from the training data, particularly the nature of electromagnetic (EM) showers. Although stochastic on a case-by-case basis, the showers sample from a statistical prior
p(\mathrm{HR})
that the network can learn from the high-resolution training examples. The model generates high-resolution features from the conditional distribution
p(\mathrm{HR}|\mathrm{LR})
, where the mapping is inherently one to many due to the stochastic nature of EM showers. While the model can approximate the manifold of consistent solutions arbitrarily well, the data processing inequality imposes a fundamental ceiling on its ability to recover the specific “truth” instance lost during measurement. Extra features thus stem from the information stored in model parameters, yielding samples that are distinct from the truth but physically consistent with its underlying prior. The same ceiling implies that superresolution cannot improve upon an optimal reconstruction from the low-resolution cells, and we do not claim that it does. What it provides instead is a representation: the subcell information already present in the low-resolution cells is expressed in the standard high-granularity form, where algorithms developed for fine-granularity detectors can use it directly. The relevant question for a reconstruction chain is what a given downstream model achieves in practice, and the comparison in Fig.
10
shows that the improvement persists when both models are free to correct for the granularity of their input.

V.2
Model dependence

Training on EM showers from Standard Model particles will not equip the SR model to handle showers drawn from an anomalous prior. Examples include hypothetical particles with long lifetimes, exotic couplings, fractional electric charge, or magnetic monopoles. Such scenarios present a challenge to standard reconstruction techniques as well as SR which are tuned to Standard Model signatures. These limitations are a reminder that SR cannot be considered a replacement for actual high-granularity calorimeters.

V.3
Data-simulation alignment

A related corollary is that training on simulation can lead to degraded or unexpected performance on real data. This alignment problem affects machine learning models in general and has also been addressed in collider physics applications
[
46
,
47
]
. To mitigate the effect, future applications of this technique must include rigorous validation strategies to quantify this bias. While direct comparisons are difficult with experimental data, it is possible to develop small, high-granularity prototype modules to validate the network’s outputs. In addition, techniques such as data augmentation can be employed to help reduce the model’s dependence on features specific to the simulation
[
48
]
.

V.4
Superresolution as an auxiliary task

Moreover, SR should be considered not just as an independent task but as an auxiliary to the broader reconstruction processes. By guiding the network to focus on relevant features, superresolution can complement traditional reconstruction algorithms, which explains why LR PFlow performance lags behind HR PFlow. In this paper, we explored SR as a preprocessing task, but it could also be embedded as a component of the PFlow training objective. The integration of SR techniques in this way has the potential to inform future detector designs and enhance the development of reconstruction algorithms, ultimately leading to more accurate and reliable data interpretation in collider experiments.

V.5
Toward deployment

To demonstrate a proof of concept, we have restricted our study to a simplified physics and detector setup. Several developments are needed to apply the SR technique to real experiments. First, the model needs to be trained on both hadronic and EM showers. Incorporating hadronic showers will introduce greater complexity due to the increased fluctuations inherent in these processes. Progressing toward full, realistic collision events will entail a significant rise in particle multiplicity due to jets and pileup. Including tracks as an input to the SR model may aid its performance in highly dense environments. To manage the model’s memory demands, limiting graph connectivity and possibly clustering in angular space will be advantageous. Another important effect we have ignored presently is that of a magnetic field which alters the incident angle of particles and, in some detectors, their daughter particles.

V.6
Ideas for future work

While we have focused on superresolution in the angular space, it would be interesting to try this in the longitudinal direction, where the number of calorimeter layers is upscaled. Uncertainty quantification is another idea that could improve the interpretability of the SR outputs and flag instances of poor generation. Uncertainty quantification for generative models is an active area of research, with recent proposals targeting Bayesian inference for diffusion and flow models
[
49
]
. Finally, while we have focused on generic particle reconstruction, future work could explore the potential of SR to enhance unfolding in specific topologies
[
50
]
.

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we demonstrated, for the first time, that superresolution techniques can be effectively incorporated into a simplified LHC-like particle-flow reconstruction chain. We introduced a novel graph-based model for superresolution that leverages the powerful flow-matching framework alongside a transparent and interpretable particle-flow model. However, the key takeaway is not the specific models employed; rather, it is that superresolution can indeed enhance reconstruction. Our approach faithfully replicates high-resolution cells event by event, leading to significantly improved modeling of cell substructure variables. Moreover, utilizing these high-resolution cells for particle flow substantially enhances both particle cardinality and angular resolution. This improvement persists when both particle-flow models are equipped with a correction network for the proxy kinematics, showing that it does not follow from the restricted form of the default kinematic estimator. We believe these findings motivate further exploration of SR in reconstruction pipelines. Detector designs are evaluated through the reconstruction performance they deliver in simulation, so a software step that improves that performance at fixed granularity bears directly on the performance-to-cost trade-off, without standing in for the measurement a finer detector would make.
