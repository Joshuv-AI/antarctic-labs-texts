Connecting reflective asymmetries in multivariate spatial and spatio-temporal covariances
Drew Yarger

Abstract
In the analysis of multivariate spatial and univariate spatio-temporal data, it is commonly recognized that asymmetric dependence may exist, which can be addressed using an asymmetric (matrix or space-time, respectively) covariance function within a Gaussian process framework. This paper introduces a new paradigm for constructing asymmetric space-time covariances, which we refer to as “reflective asymmetric,” by leveraging recently-introduced models for multivariate spatial data. We first provide new results for reflective asymmetric multivariate spatial models that extends their applicability. We then propose their asymmetric space-time extension, which come from a substantially different perspective than Lagrangian asymmetric space-time covariances. There are fewer parameters in the new models, one controls both the spatial and temporal marginal covariances, and the standard separable model is a special case. In simulation studies and analysis of the frequently-studied Irish wind data, these new models also improve model fit and prediction performance, and they can be easier to estimate. These features indicate broad applicability for improved analysis in environmental and other space-time data.



Keywords
: asymmetric dependence, Gaussian processes, multivariate covariances, space-time data, spatial statistics

1
Introduction

Accurately representing spatial dependence structures, which may be quantified through a covariance function, has broad applicability in a variety of environmental applications.
Two particular extended settings are multivariate spatial data
(
27
)
, where multiple spatially-indexed variables are modeled using a matrix-valued covariance function, and spatio-temporal data, where a variable indexed by both space and time is modeled using a space-time covariance function
(
11
;
55
)
.
In these situations, complex dependencies may arise that do not appear in the univariate spatial setting.
In particular, there may be asymmetric dependence between variables
(
45
)
or in space-time
(
28
)
.
Such asymmetries are physically realistic.
For example, the common model for modelling asymmetries in space-time data is the Lagrangian model
(
55
;
56
)
, which can be interpreted as a process evolving with directionality through space over time, like advection through the atmosphere or the ocean following the direction of currents.

In this work, we introduce a new perspective we refer to as “reflective” asymmetries.
These asymmetries are enabled by asymmetric cross-covariances in the purely spatial multivariate setting recently introduced in
65
.
These cross-covariances (describing the covariance between two spatial variables) result from multiplication in the spectral domain of
-\mathbbm{i}\textrm{sign}(\langle{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle)
to the spectral density
f({\boldsymbol{x}})
of a spatial covariance, where
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the spatial frequency (
d\in\{1,2,3,\dots\}
),
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a unit-vector directional parameter,
\mathbbm{i}
is the imaginary unit,
\textrm{sign}(z)
is the sign function, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the Euclidean inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The resulting term in the cross-covariance is an odd function, reflected across the origin, which motivates the “reflective” terminology.
We first introduce new spatial cross-covariances of this type, which previously had established closed forms only for
d=1
but are extended here to any dimension
d
for squared-exponential and Cauchy covariances.
The main focus of this work, however, is applying such constructions to the univariate space-time setting.

The new space-time models are defined by introducing a term that multiplies
\textrm{sign}(\langle{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle)\textrm{sign}(\eta)
onto a spectral density
f({\boldsymbol{x}},\eta)
of a symmetric space-time covariance, where
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
is the temporal frequency.
In many cases, the new asymmetric parts of the space-time covariance are constructed from asymmetric spatial cross-covariances, connecting the two settings.
This construction results in a two-term decomposition of the space-time covariance function into a symmetric space-time covariance function and a term that introduces asymmetry.
These models include some asymmetric versions of Gneiting-type nonseparable space-time covariances
(
31
)
, which are generally symmetric but nonseparable.

These models have some conceptual advantages over the Lagrangian model.
The nontrivial symmetric and separable space-time model is a special case of the reflective asymmetric model, which is not the case for the Lagrangian model.
Thus, one may evaluate the presence of asymmetric dependence through a likelihood ratio test.
Furthermore, one maintains control of both the marginal spatial and temporal covariance functions, while independent control of the temporal covariance function is not available for the Lagrangian model.
Control of the marginal covariances (and their spectral density) has implications for the asymptotic behavior of parameter estimation and prediction
(
22
, Section 5.2,)
.
The reflective asymmetric model also has fewer parameters than the full Lagrangian model, providing model parsimony, especially for larger spatial dimension
d
.

We also propose approaches for unconditional simulation, nonstationary and anisotropic construction, and computational implementation for large space-time data through Vecchia’s approximation
(
35
)
.
In multivariate spatial and univariate space-time simulation studies, we find that using asymmetric models can improve model fit when the true covariance is asymmetric.
We establish that the new models are substantially different than the Lagrangian model, where neither can perform as well when the other is the true model.

These models are also evaluated in an analysis of the Irish wind data of
37
, which has been frequently used as a benchmark dataset
(
28
;
48
)
.
We consider five variations of the new asymmetric models with different marginal covariances.
In general, the asymmetric models outperform the symmetric models in terms of model selection criteria.
Depending on the covariance function used, the reflective asymmetric covariances can provide improved model fit and faster likelihood estimation than the Lagrangian model.

These developments are presented in the following.
In Section
2
, we introduce relevant background in more detail.
In Section
3
, we review and introduce new spatial cross-covariance functions with reflective asymmetries.
In Section
4
, the new univariate space-time covariances are introduced.
In Section
5
, we discuss efficient simulation and computation.
Section
6
presents the simulation studies.
Section
7
presents a comprehensive analysis of the Irish wind data.
We conclude in Section
8
.

2
Background and literature

The basic univariate spatial setting considers a random field
\{Y({\boldsymbol{s}}),{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
for
d\in\{1,2,3,\dots\}
.
Throughout much of the exposition, we will assume second-order stationarity, where the mean and covariance do not depend on
{\boldsymbol{s}}
:
\mathbb{E}\{Y({\boldsymbol{s}})\}=\mu
and

\textrm{Cov}\left\{Y({\boldsymbol{s}}),Y({\boldsymbol{s}}+{\boldsymbol{h}})\right\}=\mathbb{E}\left\{(Y({\boldsymbol{s}})-\mu)(Y({\boldsymbol{s}}+{\boldsymbol{h}})-\mu)\right\}=C({\boldsymbol{h}}).

Here
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a lag; in the case
d=1
, scalar operations (for example,
\lVert{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert^{2}=h^{2}
) may be used.
Our primary interest is the covariance function
C({\boldsymbol{h}})
, which requires
C({\boldsymbol{0}})\geq 0
and symmetry
C({\boldsymbol{h}})=C(-{\boldsymbol{h}})
, including further conditions for validity.
We will assume that
\mu=0
and similarly for a multivariate mean below.
Throughout, covariance functions depend on parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, but we suppress this notation for simplicity.

We will next provide background on multivariate and space-time models.

2.1
Multivariate spatial models

Let

The matrix-valued stationary covariance

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{C}}}({\boldsymbol{h}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\rm\textrm{exp}}\left(\mathbbm{i}\langle{\boldsymbol{h}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle\right){{\boldsymbol{f}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})d{\boldsymbol{x}}=\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\textrm{exp}\left(\mathbbm{i}\langle{\boldsymbol{h}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle\right)f_{jk}({\boldsymbol{x}})d{\boldsymbol{x}}\right]_{j,k=1}^{p},

{\boldsymbol{x}}

See
29

Many of the cross-covariances proposed have been proportional to a covariance function
C^{*}({\boldsymbol{h}};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})
.
For example, if
C_{jj}({\boldsymbol{h}})=C^{*}({\boldsymbol{h}};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})

C_{kk}({\boldsymbol{h}})=C^{*}({\boldsymbol{h}};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k})

C_{jk}({\boldsymbol{h}})=\rho_{jk}C^{*}({\boldsymbol{h}};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{jk})

At times, ensuring the validity of such multivariate models is quite nontrivial

29
;
5
;
20
)
.
In general, cross-covariances, unlike spatial covariances, may be asymmetric, where
C_{jk}({\boldsymbol{h}})\neq C_{jk}(-{\boldsymbol{h}})

Definition 1

(Multivariate symmetry)
.

A stationary multivariate covariance
\boldsymbol{C}({\boldsymbol{h}})
is symmetric if
{{\boldsymbol{C}}}({\boldsymbol{h}})={{\boldsymbol{C}}}(-{\boldsymbol{h}})
for all
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Constructions based on
C_{jk}({\boldsymbol{h}})=\rho_{jk}C^{*}({\boldsymbol{h}};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{jk})

is proportional to a covariance function.
If the spectral density exists, symmetry holds if and only if
{{\boldsymbol{f}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})
is real-valued for all
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(
65
, Section 2.1,)
.
Most asymmetric cross-covariances
C_{jk}({\boldsymbol{h}})
are based on shifts, where
C_{jk}({\boldsymbol{h}})=\rho_{jk}C^{*}({\boldsymbol{h}}-{\boldsymbol{v}}_{jk};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{jk})
for a covariance
C^{*}({\boldsymbol{h}};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{jk})
and a shift vector
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{jk}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(
45
;
62
;
50
)
.
This shift corresponds to a multiplication of
\textrm{exp}\{-\mathbbm{i}\langle{\boldsymbol{v}}_{jk},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle\}
on the entry
f_{jk}({\boldsymbol{x}})
in the spectral domain.
Recently,
65
introduced asymmetric multivariate covariances using another approach with the spectral density, which is the main topic of Section
3
.

2.2
Univariate spatio-temporal models

We now review some key definitions and covariance models in spatio-temporal statistics.
We assume here that a covariance function
C({\boldsymbol{h}},u)
of a space-time process
\{Y({\boldsymbol{s}},t)\in\mathbb{R};{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},t\in\mathbb{R}\}
is finite and covariance stationary, in that the covariance
C({\boldsymbol{h}},u)=\textrm{Cov}\left\{Y({\boldsymbol{s}},t),Y({\boldsymbol{s}}+{\boldsymbol{h}},t+u)\right\}<\infty
does not depend on
{\boldsymbol{s}}
or
t
.
We focus on a few properties of space-time covariances, where more information can be found in
28
;
55
;
11
.

Theorem 2

(Simplified Bochner, space-time covariances)
.

Define a spectral density
f({\boldsymbol{x}},\eta)
that is integrable and satisfies
f(-{\boldsymbol{x}},-\eta)=f({\boldsymbol{x}},\eta)

{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\eta\in\mathbb{R}

Then the following is a valid space-time covariance on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}

C({\boldsymbol{h}},u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\textrm{exp}\left\{\mathbbm{i}(\langle{\boldsymbol{h}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle+u\eta)\right\}f({\boldsymbol{x}},\eta)d{\boldsymbol{x}}d\eta.

We propose asymmetric covariance models through the spectral density
f({\boldsymbol{x}},\eta)
.
One simplified construction of space-time models is through separability.

Definition 2

(Separability)
.

A space-time stationary covariance
C({\boldsymbol{h}},u)
is called separable if a decomposition
C({\boldsymbol{h}},u)=C_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}({\boldsymbol{h}})C_{t}(u)
exists for all
{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
u\in\mathbb{R}
.

In general, separability is often considered to be restrictive
(
11
;
14
)
.
If a covariance is not separable, then we say it is nonseparable.
A common separability measure is
R({\boldsymbol{h}},u)=C({\boldsymbol{h}},u)C({\boldsymbol{0}},0)-C({\boldsymbol{h}},0)C({\boldsymbol{0}},u)
, where
R({\boldsymbol{h}},u)=0
for all
{\boldsymbol{h}}
and
u
corresponds to separability.
Positive nonseparability occurs if
R({\boldsymbol{h}},u)>0
for some
{\boldsymbol{h}}
and
u
and negative nonseparability if
R({\boldsymbol{h}},u)<0

(
14
)
.
We refer to
C({\boldsymbol{h}},0)
and

as the marginal spatial and temporal covariance functions, respectively.

We now introduce a celebrated nonseparable and symmetric class of space-time covariances called the Gneiting class
(
31
)
, which built upon results in

We principally use the form of the wider “extended” class discussed in

With the additional parameter

\displaystyle C({\boldsymbol{h}},u)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma}{\left(\gamma(u)+1\right)^{\tau}}\varphi\left\{\frac{\lVert{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert^{2}}{\left(\gamma(u)+1\right)^{b}}\right\},

(1)

where
\gamma(u)

Gneiting covariances only introduce positive nonseparability
(
14
)

We next discuss symmetry and asymmetry in space-time covariances, the principal characteristic motivating this paper.

Definition 3

(Full symmetry)
.

A space-time covariance

{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

Definition 4

(Axial symmetry)

A space-time covariance is axially symmetric in space with respect to the

Previously-established and new asymmetric cross-covariances

We next consider an asymmetric version.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose that one can represent

{\boldsymbol{h}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

Then the corresponding asymmetric part based on the Fourier multiplier

C_{jk}^{\Im}({\boldsymbol{h}})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\textrm{exp}\left(-v\lVert{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert^{2}\right)\textrm{erfi}\left(\sqrt{v}\langle{\boldsymbol{h}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle\right)g(v)dv

This gives three ways to find a

in Proposition

Proposition 3

\displaystyle C_{jk}^{\Im}({\boldsymbol{h}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(a^{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert^{2}+1)^{\alpha}}\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\frac{a\langle{\boldsymbol{h}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle}{(a^{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert^{2}+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}+\alpha;\frac{3}{2};\frac{a^{2}\langle{\boldsymbol{h}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle^{2}}{a^{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert^{2}+1}\right),

3.4
Other covariance functions

In
64
, fast Fourier transforms were used to compute
C_{jk}^{\Im}({\boldsymbol{h}})
for the Confluent Hypergeometric covariance function, which has control over both the origin and tail behavior of the covariance.
However, closed forms of
C_{jk}^{\Im}({\boldsymbol{h}})
were not established.
While the mixing density
g(v)
is Beta-prime
(
47
, Theorem 1 of)
, the integral corresponding to Proposition
2
is unclear.

Compact covariance functions, where
C_{jk}^{\Re}({\boldsymbol{h}})=0
for all
\lVert{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert>c
for some
c>0
, have also been proposed in spatial or temporal settings
(
8
;
22
, e.g.)
.
By introducing zero entries in the covariance matrix, matrix calculations can be improved with these covariance functions.
A compactly supported
C^{\Re}_{jk}({\boldsymbol{h}})
does not usually translate to a compactly supported
C^{\Im}_{jk}({\boldsymbol{h}})
.
For example, consider the triangular function
C_{jk}^{\Re}(h)=(1-|h|)\mathbb{I}(0\leq|h|<1)
, which is a valid covariance function for (only)
d=1

(
12
)
.
It has Hilbert transform (9.6) of
42
:

\displaystyle C_{jk}^{\Im}(h)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\log\left|\frac{1+h}{1-h}\right|+h\log\left|\frac{h^{2}-1}{h^{2}}\right|\right),

which satisfies
C_{jk}^{\Im}(h)=0
only when
h=0
.

3.5
Multivariate nonseparability and flexibility

Above, we assumed throughout that
f_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}})=f_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}})
.
In Appendix
A.1
, we present the results when the squared-exponential (
a_{j}\neq a_{k}
), Cauchy (
a_{j}\neq a_{k}
,
\alpha_{j}\neq\alpha_{k}
), and Matérn (
a_{j}\neq a_{k}
,
\nu_{j}\neq\nu_{k}
) covariances have different parameters. As presented above, we need only check if
{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}
, corresponding to (

However, consider that
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}
may vary across cross-covariances, denoted
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{jk}=\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{kj}
for

.
In this case for
p\geq 3
, one needs the positive-definiteness for all relevant
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
of
\left[\Re(\sigma_{jk})-\mathbbm{i}\textrm{sign}(\langle{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{jk}\rangle)\Im(\sigma_{jk})\right]_{j,k=1}^{p}
.

4
Reflective asymmetric univariate space-time covariances

We next discuss the univariate space-time setting; the asymmetric functions in the previous section will be our building blocks for asymmetry in space-time covariances.
We will use
a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}
(
a_{t}
) as an inverse spatial (temporal) range parameter where necessary to distinguish, as well as marginal covariances
C_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}({\boldsymbol{h}})
and
C_{t}(u)
.

We propose this construction for asymmetric models in the spectral domain:

\displaystyle f({\boldsymbol{x}},\eta)

\displaystyle=\sigma f^{*}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert,|\eta|)\left\{1+\xi\textrm{sign}(\langle{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle)\textrm{sign}(\eta)\right\},

(4)

where
f^{*}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert,|\eta|)
is the symmetric spectral density of a space-time covariance,
\sigma>0
is a variance parameter,
\xi\in(-1,1)
is an asymmetry parameter with
\xi=0
corresponding to symmetry, and
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}
is again a fixed unit vector of length
d
.
We demonstrate the applicability of Bochner’s Theorem as presented in Theorem
2
.

(a)

The density is symmetric with respect to both arguments at the same time:
f(-{\boldsymbol{x}},-\eta)=\sigma f^{*}(\lVert-{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert,\lvert-\eta\rvert)\left\{1+\xi(-1)(-1)\textrm{sign}(\langle{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle)\textrm{sign}(\eta)\right\}=f({\boldsymbol{x}},\eta)
.

(b)

The positivity and integrability of
f({\boldsymbol{x}},\eta)
is ensured when
f^{*}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert,|\eta|)
is positive and integrable.
As
0<1-|\xi|\leq 1+\xi\textrm{sign}(\langle{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle)\textrm{sign}(\eta)\leq 1+|\xi|<2
for
\xi\in(-1,1)
, one has
(1-|\xi|)\sigma f^{*}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert,|\eta|)\leq f({\boldsymbol{x}},\eta)\leq(1+|\xi|)\sigma f^{*}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert,|\eta|)
.

We outline specific forms of these models, at first when
f^{*}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert,|\eta|)
is separable.

4.1
Separable-type models

We first consider building space-time models of the form (
4
) based on separable covariance models, where
f^{*}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert,|\eta|)=f_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert)f_{t}(|\eta|)
, and the integrals separate:

\displaystyle C({\boldsymbol{h}},u)

\displaystyle=\sigma\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\textrm{exp}\left(\mathbbm{i}\langle{\boldsymbol{h}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle\right)f_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert)d{\boldsymbol{x}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\textrm{exp}\left(\mathbbm{i}u\eta\right)f_{t}(|\eta|)d\eta

\displaystyle~~~+\sigma\xi\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\textrm{exp}\left(\mathbbm{i}\langle{\boldsymbol{h}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle\right)\textrm{sign}(\langle{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle)f_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert)d{\boldsymbol{x}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\textrm{exp}\left(\mathbbm{i}u\eta\right)\textrm{sign}(\eta)f_{t}(|\eta|)d\eta

\displaystyle=:\sigma\left\{C_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{\Re}({\boldsymbol{h}})C_{t}^{\Re}(u)+\xi C_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{\Im}({\boldsymbol{h}})C_{t}^{\Im}(u)\right\}.

(5)

This covariance is a sum of two separable parts, one symmetric and the other asymmetric, and is no longer separable when
\xi\neq 0
.
In this form of (
5
), the key observation is that the spatial and temporal parts correspond to cross-covariances in Section
3
, as
\textrm{sign}(\langle{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle)\textrm{sign}(\eta)=-\left\{-\mathbbm{i}\textrm{sign}(\langle{\boldsymbol{x}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle)\right\}\left\{-\mathbbm{i}\textrm{sign}(\eta)\right\}
.
We present an example from the large variety of combinations of covariances that may be chosen.

Example 6
.

Suppose we take a squared-exponential spatial covariance and a Cauchy temporal covariance with
\alpha=1/2
:
C_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{\Re}({\boldsymbol{h}})=\textrm{exp}\left(-a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert^{2}\right)
and
C_{t}^{\Re}(u)=(a_{t}^{2}u^{2}+1)^{-1/2}
.
The corresponding asymmetric separable-type space-time covariance is

\displaystyle C({\boldsymbol{h}},u)

\displaystyle=\sigma\frac{\textrm{exp}\left(-a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert^{2}\right)}{\sqrt{a_{t}^{2}u^{2}+1}}\left\{1+\xi\textrm{erfi}\left(a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}\langle{\boldsymbol{h}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle\right)\frac{2}{\pi}\sinh^{-1}(a_{t}u)\right\}.

We next plot Example
6
with
d=1
as an illustrative example in Figure

.
Interestingly, the asymmetric part can introduce negative covariance for some spatial and temporal lags.
Negative correlation in space-time covariances is considered previously in
49
and

When we take (

If

Remark 3

(Marginal covariances)

The marginal covariances are retained by the introduction of asymmetry:
C({\boldsymbol{h}},0)=\sigma C_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{\Re}({\boldsymbol{h}})

5
Practical considerations

We next discuss three practical aspects of the new space-time models: unconditional simulation, nonstationary and anisotropic models, and computational details.

5.1
Efficient spectral simulation

We propose the following algorithm for simulating random fields with separable-type asymmetric covariance.
For
\ell=1,2,\dots,L

\displaystyle Y({\boldsymbol{s}},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}2\cos(\langle{\boldsymbol{s}},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell}\rangle+\phi_{\ell})\cos(t\eta_{\ell}+\psi_{\ell})\sqrt{\frac{f_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell}\rVert)}{g_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell}\rVert)}\frac{f_{t}(|\eta_{\ell}|)}{g_{t}(|\eta_{\ell}|)}}

\displaystyle~~+\frac{\sigma\xi}{\sqrt{L}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}2\sin(\langle{\boldsymbol{s}},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell}\rangle+\phi_{\ell})\sin(t\eta_{\ell}+\psi_{\ell})\sqrt{\frac{f_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell}\rVert)}{g_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell}\rVert)}\frac{f_{t}(|\eta_{\ell}|)}{g_{t}(|\eta_{\ell}|)}}\textrm{sign}(\langle{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle)\textrm{sign}(\eta_{\ell}).

For each
\ell
, arguments from
18
, including trigonometric identities, show the desired parts of the covariance can be represented in expectation.

See
52
,
19
, and
2
, for example.
In particular, one may specify a
d\times d
spatially-varying positive-definite matrix
{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}}}}
representing a particular Mahalanobis distance used at
{\boldsymbol{s}}
.

We start with the squared-exponential model by replacing the spectral density
f_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert)
with
f_{{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\left(2\sqrt{\pi}\right)^{-d}\left|{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1}}\right|^{1/4}\left|{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}}\right|^{1/4}\textrm{exp}\left(-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\top}{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}}{\boldsymbol{x}}/4\right),
where
{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}}=({{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1}}+{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}})/2
.
It straightforwardly follows from previous results in this paper that this gives an asymmetric covariance between locations
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}
of
C_{{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}}({\boldsymbol{h}},u)=\left|{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1}}\right|^{1/4}\left|{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}}\right|^{1/4}\left|{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}}\right|^{-1/2}C\left({{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}}^{-1/2}{\boldsymbol{h}},u\right),
where
C({\boldsymbol{h}},u)
is a separable-type model from Section
4.1
with squared-exponential spatial covariance.
The spatial asymmetric portion
C_{{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}}^{\Im}({\boldsymbol{h}})
is now reflected across the plane perpendicular to
{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2}}^{-1/2}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}
instead of perpendicular to
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}
, inducing a spatially-varying asymmetry direction.
In this formulation, the parameter
a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}
is absorbed into
{{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}
, and forms may be constructed for other classes
(
19
)
.
In Figure
6
, we plot stationary
({{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}}_{\boldsymbol{s}}={{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}})
asymmetric covariances with anisotropy.

There is also a contrast with the Lagrangian model in terms of the number of covariance evaluations. In the setting where data is observed on a grid of space (of size
n_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}
) and time (regularly-spaced of size
n_{t}
) with
n=n_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}n_{t}
total data points, the Lagrangian model requires computing (
2
) on the order of
n_{t}n_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{2}
times to construct the covariance matrix of the data.
For a separable-type model introduced here, the
n\times n
symmetric covariance matrix may instead be represented using the Kronecker product
\otimes
:

\sigma{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{\Re}\otimes{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}_{t}^{\Re}+\sigma\xi{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{\Im}\otimes{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}_{t}^{\Im}
, where the matrices
{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}
are
n_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}\times n_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}
for the subscript
{\boldsymbol{s}}
,
n_{t}\times n_{t}
for the subscript
t
, symmetric for the superscript
\Re
, and skew-symmetric for the superscript
\Im
(the Kronecker product of two skew-symmetric matrices is symmetric).
The order of the number of covariance evaluations is then
n_{t}+n_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{2}
, a substantial reduction when
n_{t}
and
n_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}
are both moderately large.

However, unlike the separable model, where the likelihood evaluation can be reduced to operations on matrices of size
n_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}\times n_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}
and
n_{t}\times n_{t}
, when
\xi\neq 0
the likelihood still requires decomposing the full
n\times n
matrix.
This can be prohibitive when the total number of data points
n
is large.
We propose to use Vecchia’s approximation to make approximate likelihood inference manageable in this setting
(
61
;
41
)
.
For this approximation, conditional independence is assumed between many data points based on a neighborhood structure.
Vecchia’s approximation for basic space-time models are implemented in, for example, the
GpGp
package in
R

(
35
)
, as well as for Lagrangian models in
48
.

39
recently evaluate the choice of neighborhood structure for (symmetric) space-time models.
We implement many of the newly introduced models within the framework of
GpGp
in C++, which enables large-scale data analysis later.
The Kronecker product matrix representation does not necessarily apply within Vecchia’s approximation, as an observation’s neighbors generally will not be gridded.

We discuss parameter estimation, improved model fit when there is asymmetry, and in the space-time setting that the new asymmetric models are substantially different from the Lagrangian space-time model.

8
Discussion

In this paper, we introduce a new approach that vastly increases the possible choices for asymmetric multivariate covariances and asymmetric space-time covariances.
The key features of the new spatio-temporal models are flexibility over marginal covariance functions, model parsimony where asymmetric separable-type models represent a minimal increase of complexity over a separable model, and improvements in application and computation.
Among other features, the model makes a likelihood ratio test for symmetry straightforward only involving the estimation of a symmetric and asymmetric model.
Throughout, we make contrasts with the popular Lagrangian covariances that indicate a substantially different framework from them.
We provide implementations both within and outside of the Vecchia approximation framework to make application to various space-time data feasible.
In simulation studies and data analysis, we demonstrate that the models provide improved performance at times with faster computation.
As in
65
, a limitation is the lack of closed form Matérn asymmetric cross-covariances when
d>1
.
Addressing this, as well as establishing Gneiting-type asymmetric models for
d>1
, would additionally expand applicability of these models.

A number of additional directions for methodological advancement are opened by this work.
For example, the extension to where the “temporal” dimension is Euclidean with dimension
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{*}}
and
d^{*}>1
is straightforward in this framework by using an appropriate choice of
\left\{C^{\Re}_{t}({\boldsymbol{u}}),C^{\Im}_{t}({\boldsymbol{u}})\right\}
. In addition, since the new models do not intersect with Lagrangian models, one could potentially combine these approaches for a very flexible asymmetric model.
The extension to multivariate space-time models includes the possibility for developing additional types of asymmetries in space-time cross-covariance functions
(
9
)
.

Future research can evaluate these models in a large-scale data applications where handling nonstationarity and anisotropy is necessary and scientifically relevant.
As the Irish wind data only included 11 locations, including nonstationary flexibility in space may not be sensible.
However, an extensive data analysis of high-resolution climate model output
(
16
)
or oceanographic data
(
44
)
could make full use of the nonstationary models.

Appendix A

A.1
Cross-covariances between covariances with different parameters

We present cross-covariance forms for when the spectral densities of the original densities are not proportional with
f_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}})\neq f_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}})
.
We begin with the squared-exponential.
Suppose that
C_{jj}({\boldsymbol{h}})=\textrm{exp}\left(-a_{j}^{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert^{2}\right)
and
C_{kk}(h)=\textrm{exp}\left(-a_{k}^{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert^{2}\right)
for parameters
a_{j}
and
a_{k}
.
Let
a_{jk}=a_{j}a_{k}/\sqrt{(a_{j}^{2}+a_{k}^{2})/2}
.
Then the corresponding components of the cross-covariance are:

\displaystyle C_{jk}^{\Re}({\boldsymbol{h}})

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{a_{jk}}{(a_{j}a_{k})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right\}^{d}\textrm{exp}\left(-a_{jk}^{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert^{2}\right);

\displaystyle C_{jk}^{\Im}({\boldsymbol{h}})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{a_{jk}}{(a_{j}a_{k})^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)^{d}\textrm{exp}\left(-a_{jk}^{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert^{2}\right)\textrm{erfi}\left(a_{jk}\langle{\boldsymbol{h}},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{jk}\rangle\right),

where
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{jk}=\tilde{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{kj}
.
In general, the expression
\Re(\sigma_{jk})(a_{jk}/\sqrt{a_{j}a_{k}})^{d}
now corresponds to the marginal covariance
\textrm{Cov}\left\{Y_{j}({\boldsymbol{s}}),Y_{k}({\boldsymbol{s}})\right\}
between processes
j
and
k
.

Now, consider the Cauchy covariance, where

\displaystyle C_{jj}({\boldsymbol{h}})

\displaystyle=\left(a_{j}^{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert^{2}+1\right)^{-\alpha_{j}};

\displaystyle C_{kk}({\boldsymbol{h}})

\displaystyle=\left(a_{k}^{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert^{2}+1\right)^{-\alpha_{k}}.

Suppose that
a_{jk}
is defined as above and
\alpha_{jk}=(\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{k})/2
. Then valid cross-covariance components are defined by

\displaystyle C_{jk}^{\Re}({\boldsymbol{h}})

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{jk}^{2\alpha_{jk}}}{a_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}a_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{jk})}{\sqrt{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})\Gamma(\alpha_{k})}}\frac{1}{\left(a_{jk}^{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert^{2}+1\right)^{\alpha_{jk}}},

and

\displaystyle C_{jk}^{\Im}({\boldsymbol{h}})

B.2
Irish wind parameters and prediction study

We next present the prediction study.
For a particular time
t_{pred}
, we use data from all
t<t_{pred}
to predict

at times
Y({\boldsymbol{s}},t_{pred})
,
Y({\boldsymbol{s}},t_{pred}+1)
,
Y({\boldsymbol{s}},t_{pred}+2)
,

.
We consider two settings.
The first uses all locations
\{Y({\boldsymbol{s}},t)\}_{t<t_{pred},{\boldsymbol{s}}}
as prediction data for
Y({\boldsymbol{s}}_{pred},t_{pred})
.
The second uses only data from other locations, using
\{Y({\boldsymbol{s}},t)\}_{t<t_{pred},{\boldsymbol{s}}\neq{\boldsymbol{s}}_{pred}}
for
Y({\boldsymbol{s}}_{pred},t_{pred})
.
We refer to this as the leave-one-out setting, which is fundamentally more challenging than the first setting.
We take
t_{pred}
to be each day from January 1st, 1971 to December 30th, 1978, where the mean function and covariance parameters were estimated from data before January 1st, 1971.
This gives
2{,}922
total
t_{pred}
for each a prediction is formed for the subsequent days.
Predictions for different
t_{pred}
may be computed in parallel.

To form predictions, we once again use Vecchia’s approximation through
35
.
We use the same
m=60
neighbors for each method to compute conditional expectations of
Y({\boldsymbol{s}}_{pred},t_{pred})
.
In addition, we use
35
’s conditional simulation with
m=8
to simulate
50
realizations from the conditional distribution of
Y({\boldsymbol{s}}_{pred},t_{pred})
, whose variance is used as a conditional variance.
All distributions are taken to be Gaussian.

To evaluate the models, we use the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) that takes into account the entire distributional prediction
(
30
)
, with smaller values representing better prediction.
We provide summaries of this score for the two settings, as well as for a different number of days in advance of prediction, plotted in Figure
B3
.
In general, asymmetric versions of the model have improved prediction than their symmetric versions, especially when predicting just one day in advance.
When all locations are used, the models with Cauchy spatial covariances perform the best.
There are fewer differences in the leave-one-out setting, but the models with both squared-exponential spatial and temporal covariances lag behind other models.
The asymmetric Lagrangian model performs well in the leave-one-out setting, but is worse than some reflective asymmetric models when using all locations for prediction.
In general, prediction performance drops off quickly from one day in advance to two or three days in advance.
As expected, the leave-one-out-setting has worse predictions than the all locations setting.

Lemma C1

Consider a version of the Gneiting class with a squared-exponential spatial covariance:

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma}{(\gamma(u)+1)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\textrm{exp}\left(-\frac{a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert^{2}}{\gamma(u)+1}\right).

(6)

is

\displaystyle f^{*}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert,|\eta|)

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}}{(2\sqrt{\pi}a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}})^{d}\sqrt{\pi}a_{t}\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert}\textrm{exp}\left(-\frac{\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert^{2}}{4a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{2}}-\frac{a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{2}\eta^{2}}{a_{t}^{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert^{2}}\right).

Proof of Lemma
C1
.

First consider the case
\gamma(u)=a_{t}^{2}u^{2}
. We are interested in the inverse formula

\displaystyle f^{*}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert,|\eta|)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\textrm{exp}\left(-\mathbbm{i}\langle{\boldsymbol{h}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle-\mathbbm{i}\eta u\right)C({\boldsymbol{h}},u)d{\boldsymbol{h}}du

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\textrm{exp}\left(-\mathbbm{i}\langle{\boldsymbol{h}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle-\mathbbm{i}\eta u\right)}{\left(a_{t}^{2}u^{2}+1\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\textrm{exp}\left(-\frac{a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert^{2}}{a_{t}^{2}u^{2}+1}\right)d{\boldsymbol{h}}du

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\textrm{exp}\left(-\mathbbm{i}\eta u\right)}{\left(a_{t}^{2}u^{2}+1\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\textrm{exp}\left(-\mathbbm{i}\langle{\boldsymbol{h}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle\right)\textrm{exp}\left(-\frac{a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{h}}\rVert^{2}}{a_{t}^{2}u^{2}+1}\right)d{\boldsymbol{h}}du.

From the inverse Fourier transform of the squared-exponential function, we have

\displaystyle f^{*}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert,|\eta|)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\textrm{exp}\left(-\mathbbm{i}\eta u\right)}{(a_{t}^{2}u^{2}+1)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\textrm{exp}\left\{-\frac{\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert^{2}}{4a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{2}}\left(1+a_{t}^{2}u^{2}\right)\right\}\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{-d}(a_{t}^{2}u^{2}+1)^{\frac{d}{2}}du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi(2a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}\sqrt{\pi})^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\textrm{exp}\left(-\mathbbm{i}\eta u\right)\textrm{exp}\left\{-\frac{\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert^{2}}{4a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{2}}\left(1+a_{t}^{2}u^{2}\right)\right\}du

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}}{\sqrt{\pi}(2a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}\sqrt{\pi})^{d}a_{t}\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert}\textrm{exp}\left(-\frac{\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert^{2}}{4a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{2}}-\frac{a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{2}\eta^{2}}{a_{t}^{2}\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert^{2}}\right).

For the case
\gamma(u)=a_{t}|u|
, the integration with respect to
{\boldsymbol{h}}
is similar, leaving

\displaystyle f^{*}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert,|\eta|)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi(2a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}\sqrt{\pi})^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\textrm{exp}\left(-\mathbbm{i}\eta u\right)\textrm{exp}\left(-\frac{\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert^{2}}{4a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{2}}\left(1+a_{t}|u|\right)\right)du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi(2a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}\sqrt{\pi})^{d}}\textrm{exp}\left(-\frac{\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert^{2}}{4a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{2}}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\textrm{exp}\left(-\mathbbm{i}\eta u\right)\textrm{exp}\left(-\frac{\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert^{2}}{4a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{2}}a_{t}|u|\right)du.

The integral corresponds to the spectral density of the exponential covariance function, resulting in:

\displaystyle f^{*}(\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert,|\eta|)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi(2a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}\sqrt{\pi})^{d}}\textrm{exp}\left(-\frac{\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert^{2}}{4a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{2}}\right)\frac{2\frac{\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert^{2}a_{t}}{4a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{2}}}{\frac{\lVert{\boldsymbol{x}}\rVert^{4}a_{t}^{2}}{16a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{4}}+\eta^{2}}.

The result then follows from simplifying the fraction. ∎
