Spinal hydatid disease with epidural extension and spinal cord compression: A radiology case report
Authors: Balamurugan Ulaganathan, Arul Sakthi Priya Pandian, Eswaramoorthy Sellamuthu, Usha Jayagurunathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinal hydatid disease is rare, destructive parasitic infection of the spine. The disease often presents with nonspecific symptoms. that overlaps with spinal tuberculosis, pyogenic infection, and metastatic malignancy. We report a case of 65-year-old man with thoracic spine involvement of Hydatid disease. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated destructive vertebral disease with a characteristic cystic morphology. The imaging pattern favoured hydatid cyst as the most likely diagnosis. Correlation with epidemiologic history and serology was used to confirm the diagnosis and guide management. Due to neurological deficit and epidural spinal canal involvement, the patient underwent surgical decompression and cyst excision, followed by antiparasitic therapy. This case highlights the importance of recognizing the typical magnetic resonance imaging appearance of spinal hydatid disease to enable timely multidisciplinary management and prevention of severe complications including accidental surgical rupture leading to anaphylaxis.Keywords: Hydatid cyst, Thoracic spine, Epidural extension

Introduction

Hydatid disease is a parasitic zoonosis caused by the larval form of Echinococcus granulosus. Hydatid disease remains highly endemic in certain regions of South America, the Mediterranean, Eastern Europe, the Middle East, East Africa, and Central Asia [1]. The parasite lifecycle involves 2 hosts [2]. The definitive host is usually the dog, in which the adult parasite lives in the proximal small intestine. Eggs are shed with the host’s feces into the environment, where the intermediate host—usually sheep—becomes infected through contact with contaminated ground. Humans are accidental intermediate hosts and become infected via the fecal–oral route. After ingestion of eggs, the embryo hatches, penetrates the intestinal mucosa, enters the bloodstream, and develops into the vesicular metacestode. This stage contains the infective protoscoleces. The cycle is completed when the definitive host feeds on infected viscera.

Hydatid disease most commonly involves the liver and lungs. Bony involvement is one of the rarest clinical manifestations, and the spine accounts for approximately 50% of skeletal cases [3]. Among spinal locations, the thoracic spine is the most common site, followed by the lumbar spine. Spinal involvement is extremely debilitating and difficult to manage. We underscore the rarity of this presentation and the role of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in diagnosing the disease and its complications.

Case presentation

A 65-year-old gentleman presented with progressive thoracic back pain for several weeks followed by weakness and numbness for 2 weeks. No bladder and bowel symptoms on initial presentation. The patient’s clinical history included residence in an Echinococcus-endemic region; prior exposure risks such as contact with dogs were sought as part of standard evaluation. On neurologic examination, the patient had bilateral lower-limb weakness with upper motor neuron features consistent with thoracic myelopathy. Muscle tone was increased in both lower limbs. Power in each lower limb was grade 3/5. Deep tendon reflexes were brisk bilaterally in both knees and ankle. Babinski sign was present bilaterally. Sensory examination showed impairment below T10 level with relative sparing of sacral sensation. Anal tone was maintained at presentation. Gait could not be assessed fully initially due to the degree of weakness. The pattern of brisk reflexes, increased tone, decrease power and bilateral Babinski sign with decreased sensation below T10 level supported myelopathy secondary to epidural thoracic cord compression rather than isolated radiculopathy. The clinical picture was evaluated with the assumption that symptoms may be attributed to thoracic spine pathology could represent infection or malignancy. Primary blood tests revealed no abnormalities, except for the increased eosinophil count, which was 0.9 × 103/μL (reference range: 0.1-0.4 × 103/μL). Erythrtocyte sedimentation rate and C-Reactive protein were within the normal limits. MRI of thoracic spine was performed showing multilocular cystic mass. Chest radiograph and ultrasound abdomen was performed, and there was no abnormality. The serological test Ig ELISA of echinococcosis was performed which was weakly positive (13 U/mL). This case report describes a rare presentation of primary extradural intraspinal, vertebral and paravertebral primary hydatid disease. The purpose of this case report is to share our experience in the diagnosis and treatment of hydatid cyst disease.

Imaging findings

MRI of the thoracic spine was performed on a 3.0 Tesla scanner. The protocol included sagittal and axial T2-weighted, T1-weighted pre, and postcontrast T1 fat-suppressed (T1FS) imaging. Intravenous gadolinium contrast of 10 mL was administered for the postcontrast T1FS sequences. MRI of thoracic spine was performed with and without contrast. There was a multiloculated cystic mass with multiple daughter cysts on axial T2 weighted images centered in the T9 vertebra left pedicle, transverse process, extension into left T8-9 neural foramen and paravertebral soft tissues (Fig. 1B). Lesion was seen extending into epidural portion of spinal canal with mass effect on thoracic spinal cord and displacement of spinal cord to the right side (Fig. 1A). No abnormal signal intensity within the cord on T2 weighted images. Lesion was also seen extending into left neural foramen of T8-9 and left paravertebral soft tissues (Fig. 2A and B). On axial T1-weighted noncontrast sequence, the lesion was hypointense with no blood-fluid level (Fig. 3A). On axial T1weighted postcontrast images there was thin peripheral rim of enhancement with no evidence of nodular soft tissue component (Fig. 3B). The imaging features, particularly the destructive vertebral lesion with multiloculated cystic morphology and internal daughter cyst architecture, favored vertebral hydatid cyst as the most likely diagnosis. The study was limited to the thoracic spine only, and no additional spinal lesions were identified within the imaged field. CT was not performed in this case because MRI already provided sufficient assessment for posterior-element destruction and epidural/foraminal extension.

The main imaging-based differential diagnoses includes spinal tuberculosis, pyogenic spondylodiscitis with epidural abscess, aneurysmal bone cyst and spinal metastasis. Spinal tuberculosis usually manifests with findings of destructive end plate vertebral changes and paraspinal abscess-like collections, and it may show variable enhancement patterns. Pyogenic infection typically presents with associated disc involvement and inflammatory enhancement, often with systemic infection markers. Metastatic disease may cause vertebral destruction but usually demonstrates solid enhancing components rather than a predominantly multiloculated cystic architecture with daughter cysts. Aneurysmal bone cyst will demonstrate blood-fluid level and destructive changes in the bone [4]. Other cystic lesions of bone are uncommon and typically do not reproduce the multiloculated “cyst-within-cyst” pattern seen in hydatid disease.

Treatment and follow-up

Due to the neurological deficit, the patient underwent decompressive laminectomy of T9 vertebra with excision of cystic mass. The cyst was adherent to the dura and was difficult to excise due to scarring. However, the surgeons were able to completely excise all the epidural and foraminal components. The postoperative course was uneventful with no wound drainage. Histopathologic examination demonstrated cyst wall comprised of laminated acellular layers consistent with the laminated membrane. Multiple viable protoscolices were identified within the cyst fluid confirmed the diagnosis of hydatid cyst. The surrounding tissue showed a granulomatous inflammatory reaction with fibrosis and macrophage infiltration. Two weeks after surgery, there was improvement in motor weakness of both lower limbs, muscle power increased to 4/5. Antiparasitic therapy with albendazole 400 mg twice a day was initiated for a duration of 3 months. MRI follow-up was recommended after 3 months to evaluate for the recurrence, however, imaging follow-up was not available.

Discussion

Hydatid disease caused by E granulosus can involve the vascularized areas of musculoskeletal system through hematogenous dissemination, with vertebral involvement representing an uncommon but important manifestation. The most common involvement of Hydatid cyst is liver which accounts for 65%-70% followed by lung accounts for 14%-25% of cases [5]. Involvement of spine is less than 1% of documented cases.

There are various theories regarding the hematogenous spread of parasite to the bone, like arterial dissemination of the oncospheres, or “venous routes” via Porto-vertebral shunts and the retrograde passage of the parasite from the inferior vena cava to retroperitoneal and epidural venous plexuses [6]. There is no peri cyst formation in the bones unlike the viscera and the parasite proliferates along the line of least resistance of the bony canals.

Clinically, spinal hydatid disease often remain asymptomatic for years or presents with non-specific back pain. Symptom usually manifest when the cyst extends into spinal canal and compression of the cord resulting in various combinations of sensorimotor deficits, sphincter disturbances, altered muscle tone, and changes in tendon reflexes.

MRI is the primary imaging modality for evaluating spinal hydatid cyst [7]. On MRI, hydatid cysts typically appear hyperintense on T2-weighted sequences and hypointense on T1-weighted sequences. Inactive cyst appears as intermediate signal intensity on T2-weighted images. The cyst wall is usually iso to hypointense and enhances following gadolinium administration. Notably, an intermediate T2 signal may suggest an inactive cyst. CT is helpful in characterizing calcified inactive hydatid cyst, however ineffective in evaluating the spinal canal extension. Based on the location, anatomical classification has been described into 5 types. Type I—intramedullary, Type II—intradural and extramedullary, Type III—extradural and intraspinal, Type IV—vertebral, and Type V—paravertebral. Our case is combination of Type III, IV, and V.

The mainstay of treatment for spinal hydatid disease is decompressive laminectomy, and goal is relieving spinal cord compression and excision of the parasitic lesion [8]. Cyst aspiration may be considered as a second option if complete excision is not possible. Surgical management is followed by albendazole-based antiparasitic therapy, administered for a minimum duration of 3 months. Despite these combined therapeutic efforts, recurrence remains relatively frequent and strict follow-up is critical in the management of these patients.

Conclusion

Hydatid cyst in spine is extremely rare and needs to be diagnosed early to avoid debilitating complications. In a patient from an endemic region presenting with a destructive thoracic vertebral lesion, a predominantly cystic multiloculated MRI pattern with internal daughter cyst architecture should strongly suggest hydatid disease. Early radiologic recognition facilitates multidisciplinary planning, reduces the risk of complications during intervention, and supports initiation of appropriate anti-parasitic therapy.
