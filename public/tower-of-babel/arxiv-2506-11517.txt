Decidable Reversible Equivalences for Finite Petri Nets
Roberto Gorrieri, Ivan Lanese

Abstract
In the setting of Petri nets, we prove that causal-net bisimilarity [ 22 , 29 , 30 ] , which is a refinement of history-preserving bisimilarity [ 53 , 19 , 13 ] , and the novel hereditary causal-net bisimilarity, which is a refinement of hereditary history-preserving bisimilarity [ 4 , 35 ] , do coincide. This means that causal-net bisimilarity is a reversible behavioral equivalence , as causal-net bisimilar markings not only are able to match each other’s forward transitions, but also backward transitions by undoing performed events. Causal-net bisimilarity can be equivalently formulated as structure-preserving bisimilarity [ 22 , 30 ] , that is decidable on finite bounded Petri nets [ 8 ] . Moreover, place bisimilarity [ 1 ] , that we prove to be finer than causal-net bisimilarity, is also reversible and it was proved decidable for finite Petri nets in [ 28 , 30 ] . These results offer two decidable reversible behavioral equivalences in the true concurrency spectrum, which are alternative to the coarser hereditary history-preserving bisimilarity [ 4 , 35 ] , that, unfortunately, is undecidable even for safe Petri nets [ 34 ] .

Introduction

Traditionally, a behavioral equivalence relates states (or markings, or configurations) that have equivalent future behavior.
For instance, in a Deterministic Finite Automaton (DFA)
[
32
]
, two states are equivalent if they recognize the same language
(i.e., if their forward computations,
leading to a final state, give rise to the same words). Similarly, in a labeled transition system (LTS)
[
37
,
25
]
,
two states are bisimulation equivalent
[
50
,
44
]
if they simulate each
other’s behavior in the direction of the arrows, leading to pairs of bisimilar states.

When considering systems that should be able to undo past events to, e.g.,
take care of missed dependencies in optimistic simulation
[
7
]
, redo actions in case of errors in industrial robots
[
40
]
, or find by going backwards which bug caused a visible misbehavior in reversible debugging
[
41
,
31
]
,
it is useful that the behavioral equivalence of interest considers also backward transitions, related to the past history.
We will call
reversible behavioral equivalence
a behavioral equivalence relation
such that related systems are able to match not only their forward behavior, but also their past behavior, so that,
by undoing some performed events, the reached states are
still equivalent.

In their seminal paper, De Nicola, Montanari and Vaandrager
[
14
]
proposed

back and forth
bisimulation,
as an enhancement of ordinary bisimulation on LTSs
[
44
,
25
]
, and proved
that these two definitions define the same equivalence
relation. Similarly, they also proved that, when considering LTSs labeled also with the invisible
action
\tau
, the back and forth
version of branching bisimilarity
[
20
,
25
]
turns out to coincide with branching bisimilarity itself.
We will call
self-reversible
a behavioral equivalence which is a ‘forward’ equivalence relation, i.e., a relation defined
on the forward behavior only, that, nonetheless, is reversible.
Hence, according to
[
14
]
, on LTSs both bisimulation equivalence
[
44
]

and branching bisimulation equivalence
[
20
]
are self-reversible behavioral relations.

On LTSs we have to be a bit careful about the actual meaning of the past behavior.
As observed in
[
14
]
, the past history is the
actual computation that starts from the initial state and reaches the current state.
Only these transitions are to be considered in the
back (and forth) bisimulation game, as these are the only ones that can be undone.
This view is correct under the assumption that the LTS is modeling a

sequential system
, so that it cannot perform concurrent events.

If we want to model a concurrent system, it is better to use some truly concurrent model of concurrency,
such as
event structures

[
55
]
,
configuration structures

[
21
]
or
Petri nets

[
51
,
26
,
30
]
.
If we consider configuration structures, the past history is not the actual computation path,
obtained by performing an event sequence
\sigma
, from the initial (empty)
configuration to the current configuration, but rather the whole set of configurations that can be reached from the
initial configuration by performing any permutation of
\sigma
that is compatible with the partial order that
\sigma
induces.
For instance, if
X_{0}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}X_{1}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}X_{2}

e_{1}
and

are concurrent events, then
we have also to check the configuration
X_{1}^{\prime}
such that
X_{0}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}X_{1}^{\prime}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}X_{2}
, because this is reachable from

by undoing the event

.
This notion of reversibility takes the name of
causal-consistent reversibility
and it has been first introduced in
[
10
]
in the
context of reversible CCS. Since then, it has been applied to a number of models and languages, including
the
\pi
-calculus
[

]
, the higher-order
\pi
-calculus
[

]
, Place-Transition Petri nets
[

]
,

[

, and the Erlang programming language
[

]
. See
[

]
for a recent survey.

On truly concurrent models of concurrency, a popular behavioral equivalence is
history-preserving
bisimilarity

[
53
,
19
,
13
]
(hp-bisimilarity, for short), that on Petri nets takes the form of

fully concurrrent bisimilarity

[

]
. A back and forth version of hp-bisimilarity was proposed
in
[
4
,
35
]
under the name of

hereditary history-preserving bisimilarity
(hhp-bisimilarity, for short), that enhances hp-bisimilarity by requiring
that the hp-bisimulation game between two systems
also holds when going backward in their history. Hence, hhp-bisimilarity is a reversible equivalence,
actually the coarsest reversible bisimulation-based, behavioral relation
fully respecting causality.
This is also in line with the results in
[
2
]
, which prove hhp-bisimilarity equivalent to a back and forth bisimilarity in the context of
reversible CCS.
However, hp-bisimilarity is not self-reversible because
hp-bisimilarity is coarser than hhp-bisimilarity.
Moreover, while hp-bisimilarity is decidable on BBP nets
[

,

]
, safe finite Petri nets
[
54
]
and
on bounded finite Petri nets
[

,
8
]
,
unfortunately hhp-bisimilarity has much poorer decidability results; in fact,

[

]
,
but
it is undecidable even on safe nets
[
34
]
, hence making its usability for practical problems very limited.

The main aim of this paper is to find
reversible
,
decidable
,
finer
approximations
of hereditary history-preserving bisimilarity over finite Petri nets.

About

decidable approximations, there is an interesting result by Fröschle and Hildebrandt
[

have proposed an infinite hierarchy of bisimilarity notions, all decidable on safe finite Petri nets, that refine hp-bisimilarity,
and are coarser than hhp-bisimilarity, such that hhp-bisimilarity is the intersection of all these bisimilarities in the hierarchy.
However, this result is not very useful in practice because coarser approximations of hhp-bisimilarity do not ensure reversibility.

The main contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows:

\bullet

The definition of
hereditary causal-net bisimulation
, by extending the original definition
with backward transitions, whose induced
equivalence is denoted by
\sim_{hcn}
.

\bullet

The proof that
\sim_{cn}
and
\sim_{hcn}
coincide; this means that causal-net bisimilarity is self-reversible.

\bullet

Analogously, we also define
hereditary fully concurrent bisimulation
,
by extending the original definition in
[
6
]

with backward transitions; the induced reversible behavioral relation, which is the porting on Petri nets
of hereditary history-preserving bisimilarity, is denoted by
\sim_{hfc}
.

\bullet

The observation that
\sim_{cn}
is finer than
\sim_{hfc}
.

\bullet

The proof that place bisimilarity
\sim_{p}
is finer than
causal-net bisimilarity
\sim_{cn}
.

\bullet

The observation that place bisimilarity
\sim_{p}
, being finer than
\sim_{cn}
,
is also self-reversible. In fact, we show that place bisimilar markings are able not only to match their future behavior,
but also to match their past behavior. Since place bisimilarity is decidable
on finite Petri nets, it can be used as an
alternative to causal-net bisimilarity (or, better, the equivalent structure-preserving bisimilarity
[
22
]
)
when the marked Petri net is unbounded.

\bullet

A small case study, modeling two unbounded producer–consumer systems, that illustrates the real applicability of the proposed reversible equivalences.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
recalls the basic definitions about Petri nets,
including place bisimulation and structure-preserving bisimulation.
Section
3
defines the causal semantics of Petri nets, including
causal-net bisimulation, fully concurrent bisimulation,
and the novel definitions of
hereditary causal-net bisimulation
and
hereditary fully concurrent bisimulation
;
this section also contains the proofs that causal-net bisimilarity and hereditary causal-net bisimilarity coincide and that
place bisimilarity implies causal-net bisimilarity.
Section
4
argues that also place bisimilarity is self-reversible.
Section
5
discusses a small case study about two producer–consumer systems, showing the real applicability of the approach.
Finally, Section
6
overviews related literature, formulates a conjecture about the coarsest self-reversible
causality-respecting bisimulation-based behavioral relation and hints at some possible
future research.

2.1
Place Bisimulation

We now present the place bisimulation idea, introduced originally in
[
1
]
.
This proposal is an
improvement of a behavioral relation proposed earlier by Olderog in
[
48

on safe
nets (called

), which was not transitive.
First, an auxiliary definition.

Definition 6

(Additive closure)

Given a P/T net
N=(S,A,T)
and a
place relation

R\subseteq S\times S
, we define a
marking relation

R^{\oplus}\,\subseteq\,{\mathcal{M}}(S)\times{\mathcal{M}}(S)
, called
the
additive closure
of
R
,
as the least relation induced by the following axiom and rule.

\begin{array}[]{lllllllllll}\begin{array}[]{c}\\
\hline\cr(\theta,\theta)\in R^{\oplus}\end{array}&&\;\begin{array}[]{c}(s_{1},s_{2})\in R\quad(m_{1},m_{2})\in R^{\oplus}\\
\hline\cr(s_{1}\oplus m_{1},s_{2}\oplus m_{2})\in R^{\oplus}\end{array}\\
\end{array}

\Box

Note that, by definition, two markings are related by
R^{\oplus}
only if they have the same size;
in fact, the axiom states that
the empty marking is related to itself, while the rule, assuming by induction
that
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
have the same size, ensures that
s_{1}\oplus m_{1}
and

s_{2}\oplus m_{2}
have the same size.

Proposition 1

For each relation
R\subseteq S\times S
, if
(m_{1},m_{2})\in R^{\oplus}
,
then
|m_{1}|=|m_{2}|
.

\Box

Note also that the membership
(m_{1},m_{2})\in R^{\oplus}
may be proved in several different
ways, depending on the chosen order of the elements
of the two markings and on the definition of
R
. For instance, if
R=\{(s_{1},s_{3}),

(s_{1},s_{4}),

(s_{2},s_{3}),(s_{2},s_{4})\}
,
then
(s_{1}\oplus s_{2},s_{3}\oplus s_{4})\in R^{\oplus}
can be proved by means
of the pairs
(s_{1},s_{3})
and
(s_{2},s_{4})
,
as well as by means of
(s_{1},s_{4}),(s_{2},s_{3})
.
An alternative way to define that two markings
m_{1}
and
m_{2}

are related by
R^{\oplus}
is to state that
m_{1}
can be represented as
s_{1}\oplus s_{2}\oplus\ldots\oplus s_{k}
,

m_{2}
can be represented as
s_{1}^{\prime}\oplus s_{2}^{\prime}\oplus\ldots\oplus s_{k}^{\prime}
and
(s_{i},s_{i}^{\prime})\in R
for
i=1,\ldots,k
.

Proposition 2

[
27
,
30
]

For each relation
R\subseteq S\times S
, the following hold:

1.

If
R
is an equivalence relation, then
R^{\oplus}
is an equivalence relation.

2.

If
R_{1}\subseteq R_{2}
, then
R_{1}^{\oplus}\subseteq R_{2}^{\oplus}
, i.e., the additive closure is monotone.

3.

If
(m_{1},m_{2})\in R^{\oplus}
and
(m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime})\in R^{\oplus}
,
then
(m_{1}\oplus m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}\oplus m_{2}^{\prime})\in R^{\oplus}
, i.e., the additive closure is additive.

4.

If
R
is an equivalence relation,
(m_{1}\oplus m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}\oplus m_{2}^{\prime})\in R^{\oplus}
and
(m_{1},m_{2})\in R^{\oplus}
,
then
(m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime})\in R^{\oplus}
, i.e., the additive closure is subtractive.

5.

If
(m_{1},m_{2})\in R^{\oplus}
and
m_{1}^{\prime}\subseteq m_{1}
, then there exists
m_{2}^{\prime}\subseteq m_{2}

such that
(m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime})\in R^{\oplus}
and
(m_{1}\ominus m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}\ominus m_{2}^{\prime})\in R^{\oplus}
.

\Box

We are now ready to introduce place bisimulation, which is a non-interleaving behavioral
relation defined over the net places.

Definition 7

(Place Bisimulation)

Let
N=(S,A,T)
be a P/T net.
A

is a relation

R\subseteq S\times S
such that if
(m_{1},m_{2})\in R^{\oplus}

then

\bullet

\forall t_{1}
such that
m_{1}[t_{1}\rangle m^{\prime}_{1}
,
\exists t_{2}
such that
m_{2}[t_{2}\rangle m^{\prime}_{2}

(\mbox{${}^{\bullet}t_{1}$},\mbox{${}^{\bullet}t_{2}$})\in R^{\oplus}
,
\ell(t_{1})=\ell(t_{2})
,
(\mbox{$t_{1}^{\bullet}$},\mbox{$t_{2}^{\bullet}$})\in R^{\oplus}
and, moreover,

(m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime})\in R^{\oplus}
; and symmetrically,

\bullet

\forall t_{2}
such that
m_{2}[t_{2}\rangle m^{\prime}_{2}
,
\exists t_{1}
such that
m_{1}[t_{1}\rangle m^{\prime}_{1}

with
(\mbox{${}^{\bullet}t_{1}$},\mbox{${}^{\bullet}t_{2}$})\in R^{\oplus}
,
\ell(t_{1})=\ell(t_{2})
,
(\mbox{$t_{1}^{\bullet}$},\mbox{$t_{2}^{\bullet}$})\in R^{\oplus}
and, moreover,

(m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime})\in R^{\oplus}
.

Two markings
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are
place bisimilar
, denoted by

m_{1}\sim_{p}m_{2}
, if there exists a place bisimulation
R
such that
(m_{1},m_{2})\in R^{\oplus}
.

\Box

Note that the two matching transitions

and
t_{2}
in the place bisimulation game must have essentially the same shape
because
(i)

(\mbox{${}^{\bullet}t_{1}$},\mbox{${}^{\bullet}t_{2}$})\in R^{\oplus}
implies that

|\mbox{${}^{\bullet}t_{1}$}|=|\mbox{${}^{\bullet}t_{2}$}|
, then
(ii)

\ell(t_{1})=\ell(t_{2})
and, finally,
(iii)

from
(\mbox{$t_{1}^{\bullet}$},\mbox{$t_{2}^{\bullet}$})\in R^{\oplus}

we derive that
|\mbox{$t_{1}^{\bullet}$}|=|\mbox{$t_{2}^{\bullet}$}|
.

[

For each P/T net

In order to introduce this behavioral relation

Definition 8

(Link and linking)

Let
N=(S,A,T)
be a P/T Petri net.
A

is a pair
(s_{1},s_{2})\in S\times S
.
A

where
dom(l)
is finite as
S
is finite.
With abuse of notation, we denote by
\theta
the empty linking, i.e., the empty multiset of links.
Given a set
L
of links, we denote by
\mathcal{M}(L)
the set of all the linkings over
L

Given a linking
l

m_{1}
and
m_{2}
can be defined as

m_{1}(s_{1})=\pi_{1}(l)(s_{1})=\sum_{s_{2}\in S}l(s_{1},s_{2})
and

m_{2}(s_{2})=\pi_{2}(l)(s_{2})=\sum_{s_{1}\in S}l(s_{1},s_{2})
.

For instance, if
l=\{(s_{1},s_{2}),(s_{1}^{\prime},s_{2}^{\prime})\}
, then
\pi_{1}(l)=s_{1}\oplus s_{1}^{\prime}

and
\pi_{2}(l)=s_{2}\oplus s_{2}^{\prime}
. Note that the notation
(m_{1},m_{2})\in R^{\oplus}
actually means that there exists a
linking
l\in\mathcal{M}(R)
such that

\pi_{1}(l)=m_{1}
and
\pi_{2}(l)=m_{2}
. As a matter of fact, a linking
l
can be seen as a triple

(m_{1},f,m_{2})
, where
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are
markings of the same size, and
f
is a bijection between them.

We will use
l
,
\overline{l}
,
h
,
\overline{h}
,
c
and
\overline{c}
, possibly indexed, to range over linkings.
As linkings are multisets, we can use the operations defined over multisets, such as union

\oplus
and difference
\ominus
.
For instance,
(l\oplus c)(s_{1},s_{2})=l(s_{1},s_{2})+c(s_{1},s_{2})
. Moreover, a linking
composed of a single link
(s_{1},s_{2})

is denoted simply by its element, as we did for multisets in general. So, for instance, the
linking
l=\{(s_{1},s_{2}),(s_{1}^{\prime},s_{2}^{\prime})\}

is often simply denoted by
l=(s_{1},s_{2})\oplus(s_{1}^{\prime},s_{2}^{\prime})
.

\Box

Definition 9

(Structure Preserving Bisimulation)

such that

\mbox{$t_{1}^{\bullet}$}=\pi_{1}(\overline{c})

\mbox{$t_{2}^{\bullet}$}=\pi_{2}(\overline{c})

2.

\forall t_{2}
such that
\mbox{${}^{\bullet}t_{2}$}=\pi_{2}(c)

t_{1}

such that
\ell(t_{1})=\ell(t_{2})
,
\mbox{${}^{\bullet}t_{1}$}=\pi_{1}(c)

\overline{c}
such that

\mbox{$t_{1}^{\bullet}$}=\pi_{1}(\overline{c})
,
\mbox{$t_{2}^{\bullet}$}=\pi_{2}(\overline{c})
and

m_{1}
and
m_{2}

and
m_{2}=\pi_{2}(l)

and

in the structure-preserving bisimulation

(i)

\mbox{${}^{\bullet}t_{1}$}=\pi_{1}(c)

\mbox{${}^{\bullet}t_{2}$}=\pi_{2}(c)

|\mbox{${}^{\bullet}t_{1}$}|=|\mbox{${}^{\bullet}t_{2}$}|

(ii)

\ell(t_{1})=\ell(t_{2})
and, finally,
(iii)
from
\mbox{$t_{1}^{\bullet}$}=\pi_{1}(\overline{c})
and
\mbox{$t_{2}^{\bullet}$}=\pi_{2}(\overline{c})

|\mbox{$t_{1}^{\bullet}$}|=|\mbox{$t_{2}^{\bullet}$}|

Causality-based Semantics

We outline some definitions, adapted from the literature
(cf., e.g.,
[
24
,
5
,
48
,
22
,
29
]
).

Definition 10

(Acyclic net)

A P/T net
N=(S,A,T)
is

acyclic
if its flow relation
F
is acyclic (i.e.,
\not\exists x
such that
xF^{+}x
,
where
F^{+}
is the transitive closure of
F
).

\Box

The causal semantics of a marked P/T net is defined by a class of particular acyclic safe nets,
where places are not branched (that is, they have at most one incoming and one outgoing edge, hence the nets represent a single run) and all arcs have weight 1.
This kind of net is called causal net (or occurrence net)
[
24
,
5
,
48
,
22
]
.
We use the name
C
(possibly indexed) to denote a causal net, the set
B
to denote its
places (called conditions), the set
E
to denote its transitions
(called events), and

L
to denote its labels.

Definition 11

(Causal net)

A causal net is a finite marked P/T net
C(\mathsf{m}_{0})=(B,L,E,\mathsf{m}_{0})
satisfying
the following conditions:

1.

C
is acyclic;

2.

\forall b\in B\;\;|\mbox{${}^{\bullet}b$}|\leq 1\,\wedge\,|\mbox{$b^{\bullet}$}|\leq 1
(i.e., the places are not branched);

3.

\forall b\in B\;\;\mathsf{m}_{0}(b)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $\;\mbox{${}^{\bullet}b$}=\emptyset$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise;}\end{cases}

4.

\forall e\in E\;\;\mbox{${}^{\bullet}e$}(b)\leq 1\,\wedge\,\mbox{$e^{\bullet}$}(b)\leq 1
for all
b\in B
(i.e., all the arcs have weight
1
).

We denote by
Min(C)
the set
\mathsf{m}_{0}
, and by
Max(C)
the set

\{b\in B\;\;\big|\;\;\mbox{$b^{\bullet}$}=\emptyset\}
.

\Box

Note that any reachable marking of a causal net is a set, i.e.,
this net is safe; in fact, the initial marking is a set and,
assuming by induction that a reachable marking
\mathsf{m}
is a set and enables
e
, i.e.,

\mathsf{m}[e\rangle\mathsf{m}^{\prime}
,
then also

\mathsf{m}^{\prime}=(\mathsf{m}\ominus\mbox{${}^{\bullet}e$})\oplus\mbox{$e^{\bullet}$}
is a set,
as the net is acyclic and because
of the condition on the shape of the post-set of
e
(weights can only be
1
).

As the initial marking of a causal net is fixed by its shape (according to item
3
of
Definition
11
), in the following, in order to make the
notation lighter, we often omit the indication of the initial marking,
so that the causal
net
C(\mathsf{m}_{0})
is denoted by
C
.

Definition 12

(Moves of a causal net)

Given two causal nets
C=(B,L,E,\mathsf{m}_{0})

and
C^{\prime}=(B^{\prime},L,E^{\prime},\mathsf{m}_{0})
, we say that
C

moves in one step to
C^{\prime}
through
e
, denoted by

C[e\rangle C^{\prime}
, if
\;\mbox{${}^{\bullet}e$}\subseteq Max(C)
,
E^{\prime}=E\cup\{e\}

and
B^{\prime}=B\cup\mbox{$e^{\bullet}$}
.

\Box

Note that a move
C[e\rangle C^{\prime}
extends the causal net
C
to
C^{\prime}
by adding the maximal event
e
.

Definition 13

(Folding and process)

A
folding
from a causal net
C=(B,L,E,\mathsf{m}_{0})
into a P/T net system

N(m_{0})=(S,A,T,m_{0})
is a function
\rho:B\cup E\to S\cup T
, which is type-preserving, i.e.,
such that
\rho(B)\subseteq S
and
\rho(E)\subseteq T
, satisfying the following:

\bullet

L=A
and
\mathsf{l}(e)=\ell(\rho(e))
for all
e\in E
(i.e., foldings preserve labels of events);

\bullet

\rho(\mathsf{m}_{0})=m_{0}
, i.e.,
m_{0}(s)=|\rho^{-1}(s)\cap\mathsf{m}_{0}|
(foldings preserve the initial marking);

\bullet

\forall e\in E,\rho(\mbox{${}^{\bullet}e$})=\mbox{${}^{\bullet}\rho(e)$}
, i.e.,
\rho(\mbox{${}^{\bullet}e$})(s)=|\rho^{-1}(s)\cap\mbox{${}^{\bullet}e$}|

for all
s\in S
(foldings and pre-set commute);

\bullet

\forall e\in E,\,\rho(\mbox{$e^{\bullet}$})=\mbox{$\rho(e)^{\bullet}$}
, i.e.,
\rho(\mbox{$e^{\bullet}$})(s)=|\rho^{-1}(s)\cap\mbox{$e^{\bullet}$}|

for all
s\in S
(foldings and post-set commute).

A pair
(C,\rho)
, where
C
is a causal net and
\rho
a folding from

C
to a net system
N(m_{0})
, is a
process
of
N(m_{0})
.

\Box

Definition 14

(Moves of a process)

Let
N(m_{0})=(S,A,T,m_{0})
be a P/T net system
and let
(C_{i},\rho_{i})
, for
i=1,2
, be two processes of
N(m_{0})
.
We say that
(C_{1},\rho_{1})

moves in one step to
(C_{2},\rho_{2})
through
e
, denoted by

(C_{1},\rho_{1}){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}(C_{2},\rho_{2})
, if
C_{1}[e\rangle C_{2}

and
\rho_{1}\subseteq\rho_{2}
.
If
\pi_{1}=(C_{1},\rho_{1})
and
\pi_{2}=(C_{2},\rho_{2})
, we denote
the move as
\pi_{1}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}\pi_{2}
.

\Box

Definition 15

(Partial orders of events)

From a causal net
C=(B,L,E,\mathsf{m}_{0})
, we can
extract the
partial order of its events

\mathsf{E}_{\mathsf{C}}=(E,\preceq)
,
where
e_{1}\preceq e_{2}
if there is a path in the net from
e_{1}
to
e_{2}
, i.e., if
e_{1}\mathsf{F}^{*}e_{2}
, where

\mathsf{F}^{*}
is the reflexive and transitive closure of

\mathsf{F}
, which is the flow relation for
C
.

Two partial orders
(E_{1},\preceq_{1})
and
(E_{2},\preceq_{2})
are isomorphic
if there is a label-preserving, order-preserving bijection
g:E_{1}\to E_{2}
, i.e., such that

\mathsf{l}_{1}(e)=\mathsf{l}_{2}(g(e))
and
e\preceq_{1}e^{\prime}
if and only if
g(e)\preceq_{2}g(e^{\prime})
.
We also say that a map
g
is an

event isomorphism
between the causal nets

C_{1}
and

C_{2}
if it is an isomorphism between their associated
partial orders
\mathsf{E}_{C_{1}}

and
\mathsf{E}_{C_{2}}
.

\Box

(Causal-net bisimulation)

Let

(\rho_{1},C,\rho_{2})

(C,\rho_{i})
is a process
of

for some

,

\forall t_{1},C^{\prime},\rho_{1}^{\prime}
s.t.
(C,\rho_{1}){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}(C^{\prime},\rho_{1}^{\prime})
,
where
\rho_{1}^{\prime}(e)=t_{1}
,

\exists t_{2},\rho_{2}^{\prime}
s.t.

(C,\rho_{2}){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}(C^{\prime},\rho_{2}^{\prime})
,
where
\rho_{2}^{\prime}(e)=t_{2}
, and

(\rho^{\prime}_{1},C^{\prime},\rho^{\prime}_{2})\in R

\forall t_{2},C^{\prime},\rho_{2}^{\prime}
s.t.

.

m_{1}
and
m_{2}
of

,
if there exists a causal-net bisimulation

,
where

contains no events (i.e.,

) and

.

\Box

(Causal-net bisimilarity and sp-bisimilarity coincide)

Let

.

\Box

Since

]
.

(Hereditary causal-net bisimulation)

R

(\rho_{1},C,\rho_{2})\in R
, then

i)

\forall t_{1},C^{\prime},\rho_{1}^{\prime}
s.t.
(C^{\prime},\rho_{1}^{\prime}){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}(C,\rho_{1})
,
where
\rho_{1}(e)=t_{1}
,

\exists t_{2},\rho_{2}^{\prime}
s.t.

(C^{\prime},\rho_{2}^{\prime}){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}(C,\rho_{2})
,

, and

\forall t_{2},C^{\prime},\rho_{2}^{\prime}

Theorem 3.2

(Causal-net bisimilarity is self-reversible)

Let
N=(S,A,T)
be a P/T net. We have that
m_{1}\sim_{cn}m_{2}
if and only if

m_{1}\sim_{hcn}m_{2}
.

Proof

By definition, a hereditary causal-net bisimulation is a causal-net bisimulation,
hence we only have to prove
the implication from left to right.

Assume
R
is a causal net bisimulation justifying that
m_{1}\sim_{cn}m_{2}
; this means that

R
contains a triple
(\rho^{0}_{1},C^{0},\rho^{0}_{2})
,
where
C^{0}
contains no events (i.e.,
E^{0}=\emptyset
) and

\rho^{0}_{i}(Min(C^{0}))

=\rho^{0}_{i}(Max(C^{0}))=m_{i}\;
for
i=1,2
.
We want to prove, by induction on the length
n\geq 1
of the computations originating from configuration
(C^{0},\rho^{0}_{1})
,
that the hcn-conditions are satisfied. The case when
(C^{0},\rho^{0}_{2})
moves first is symmetric,
and so omitted.

The base case is
n=1
. Hence, assume
(C^{0},\rho^{0}_{1}){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}(C^{1},\rho^{1}_{1})
.
As
(\rho^{0}_{1},C^{0},\rho^{0}_{2})\in R
, there exists
(C^{0},\rho^{0}_{2}){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}(C^{1},\rho^{1}_{2})

such that
(\rho^{1}_{1},C^{1},\rho^{1}_{2})\in R
. If we backtrack event
e_{1}
, we end up to
(C^{0},\rho^{0}_{1})

and
(C^{0},\rho^{0}_{2})
, respectively, such that
(\rho^{0}_{1},C^{0},\rho^{0}_{2})\in R
. So, this case is trivial.
Now assume that

(C^{0},\rho^{0}_{1}){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}(C^{1},\rho^{1}_{1}){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}\ldots{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{n-2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}(C^{n-2},\rho^{n-2}_{1}){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{n-1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}(C^{n-1},\rho^{n-1}_{1}){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{n}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}(C^{n},\rho^{n}_{1})
,

with
n\geq 2
. By induction, we know that there exists a computation

(C^{0},\rho^{0}_{2}){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}(C^{1},\rho^{1}_{2}){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}\ldots{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{n-2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}(C^{n-2},\rho^{n-2}_{2}){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{n-1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}(C^{n-1},\rho^{n-1}_{2})
,

such that
(\rho^{i}_{1},C^{i},\rho^{i}_{2})\in R

i=1,\ldots,n-1
and, moreover, that is reversible, i.e., it satisfies the
hcn-conditions by backtracking events.

As
(\rho^{n-1}_{1},C^{n-1},\rho^{n-1}_{2})\in R
, a transition

(C^{n-1},\rho^{n-1}_{2}){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{n}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}(C^{n},\rho^{n}_{2})
exists
such that
(\rho^{n}_{1},C^{n},\rho^{n}_{2})\in R
.
If
e_{n}
causally depends on
e_{n-1}
, then
we can only backtrack

, going back to the pairs
(C^{n-1},\rho^{n-1}_{1})
and
(C^{n-1},\rho^{n-1}_{2})

(\rho^{n-1}_{1},C^{0},\rho^{n-1}_{2})\in R
, so that this case is trivial, too.
On the contrary, if
e_{n}
and
e_{n-1}
are causally independent, then we could also backtrack

e_{n-1}
.
Therefore, we get:

(C^{n-2},\rho^{n-2}_{1}){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{n}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}(\underline{C}^{n-1},\underline{\rho}^{n-1}_{1}){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{n-1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}(C^{n},\rho^{n}_{1})
and

(C^{n-2},\rho^{n-2}_{2}){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{n}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}(\underline{C}^{n-1},\underline{\rho}^{n-1}_{2}){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{n-1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}(C^{n},\rho^{n}_{2})
.

Therefore, it remains to argue that
(\underline{\rho}^{n-1}_{1},\underline{C}^{n-1},\underline{\rho}^{n-1}_{2})\in R
.
However, note that, given
(\rho^{n}_{1},C^{n},\rho^{n}_{2})\in R
and
(\rho^{n-2}_{1},C^{n-2},\rho^{n-2}_{2})\in R
, this is due to the
fact that the foldings on these independent events are already fixed in the target configurations

(C^{n},\rho^{n}_{1})
and
(C^{n},\rho^{n}_{2})

so that they are also fixed accordingly in the intermediate configurations
(\underline{C}^{n-1},\underline{\rho}^{n-1}_{1})

and
(\underline{C}^{n-1},\underline{\rho}^{n-1}_{2})
.
Finally, induction can be invoked to conclude that also the computations
of length
n-1
ending with
(\underline{C}^{n-1},\underline{\rho}^{n-1}_{1})

and
(\underline{C}^{n-1},\underline{\rho}^{n-1}_{2})

\Box

Fully Concurrent Bisimulation

Causal-net bisimilarity observes not only the partial order of generated events, but also the size of the
current distributed state, as it observes the causal nets. A coarser behavioral equivalence, ignoring the size
of the distributed state and the shape of transitions, is
fully concurrent bisimulation (fc-bisimulation, for short)
[
6
]
, whose definition
was inspired by previous equivalence notions on other concurrency models:
history-preserving bisimulation, originally defined in
[
53
]
under the name of behavior-structure bisimulation, and
then elaborated on in
[
19
]
(who called it by this name) and also independently defined in
[
13
]

(who called it mixed ordering bisimulation). Its definition follows.

Definition 18

(Fully concurrent bisimulation)

Given a P/T net
N=(S,A,T)
, a
fully concurrent bisimulation
(fc-bisimulation, for short)
is a relation
R
, composed of
triples of the form
(\pi_{1},g,\pi_{2})
, where, for
i=1,2
,

\pi_{i}=(C_{i},\rho_{i})
is a process of
N(m_{0i})
for some
m_{0i}
and

g
is an event isomorphism between
\mathsf{E}_{C_{1}}
and
\mathsf{E}_{C_{2}}
, such that
if
(\pi_{1},g,\pi_{2})\in R
then

1.

\forall t_{1},\pi_{1}^{\prime}
such that
\pi_{1}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}\pi_{1}^{\prime}
with
\rho_{1}^{\prime}(e_{1})=t_{1}
,

\exists t_{2},\pi_{2}^{\prime},g^{\prime}
such that

(a)

\pi_{2}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle e_{2}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}\pi_{2}^{\prime}
with
\rho_{2}^{\prime}(e_{2})=t_{2}
;

(b)

g^{\prime}=g\cup\{(e_{1},e_{2})\}
, and finally,

(c)

(\pi_{1}^{\prime},g^{\prime},\pi_{2}^{\prime})\in R
;

2.

and symmetrically, if
\pi_{2}
moves forward first.

Two markings
m_{1},m_{2}
are fully concurrent bisimilar,
denoted
m_{1}\sim_{fc}m_{2}
, if a fully concurrent bisimulation R exists,
containing a triple
(\pi^{0}_{1},\emptyset,\pi^{0}_{2})
where

\pi^{0}_{i}=(C^{0}_{i},\rho^{0}_{i})
is such that

C^{0}_{i}
contains no events (i.e.,
E^{0}_{i}=\emptyset
) and

\rho^{0}_{i}(Min(C^{0}_{i}))=\rho^{0}_{i}(Max(C^{0}_{i}))

=m_{i}\;
for
i=1,2
.

\Box

(\pi_{1},g,\pi_{2})\in R

\forall t_{1},\pi_{1}^{\prime}

\pi_{2}
moves backward first.

Place Bisimilarity is Self-reversible

Since
\sim_{p}
is finer than
\sim_{cn}
, we expect that also place bisimilarity is self-reversible.
In fact, consider two place bisimilar markings
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
. For each transition
t_{1}
performed by
m_{1}
,
marking
m_{2}
can respond with the transition
t_{2}
that generates the same causal net, reaching a
pair of place bisimilar markings; and this can be repeated from these reached markings. Of course,
since the generated causal net is the same,
we are sure that also by undoing events, we end up into a pair of place bisimilar markings.

Theorem 4.1

Place bisimilarity is self-reversible.

Proof

Assume
m_{1}\sim_{p}m_{2}
, i.e., a place bisimulation
R
exists such that

(m_{1},m_{2})\in R^{\oplus}
. We will prove, by induction on the length of the computation from
m_{1}
, that there

The base case is

Now assume

\ell(t_{i})=\ell(t_{i}^{\prime})

(m_{1}^{i},m_{2}^{i})\in R^{\oplus}

(m_{1}^{n-1},m_{2}^{n-1})\in R^{\oplus}
and that

.
We have to distinguish two cases.

\bullet

Hence,

,
(\mbox{${}^{\bullet}t_{n}$},\underline{m}^{2}_{2})\in R^{\oplus}

and
(m,m^{\prime})\in R^{\oplus}
.

Since

, there exist a transition

,

, where

because

If we backtrack on

\underline{m}^{n-1}_{2}=\mbox{${}^{\bullet}t_{n-1}^{\prime}$}\oplus\mbox{$t_{n}^{\prime\bullet}$}\oplus m^{\prime}

\bullet

Conclusion and Future Research

We tackled the question, not previously considered in
the literature, of whether truly-concurrent bisimilarities for Petri
nets are self-reversible, meaning that they require to match
behaviors also going backwards. As main
results we have shown that causal-net bisimilarity is self-reversible,
and that place bisimilarity is reversible. The latter is of particular
interest since place bisimilarity is decidable for finite (but
possibly unbounded) Petri nets, what enables us to use it to prove the correctness
of non-trivial implementations of given specifications, as we did in Section
5
.

An obvious question is whether causal-net bisimilarity is the coarsest self-reversible behavioral relation fully respecting causality.
The answer to this question is negative. In fact, it is possible to prove, by a simple adaptation of the proof of Theorem
3.2
,
that the coarser i-causal-net bisimilarity
[
8
]
is also self-reversible. This equivalence, which
ensures that related markings generate
the same causal nets, lies in between causal-net bisimilarity and hhp-bisimilarity; in fact, the markings in
Figure
2
are i-causal-net bisimilar (but not causal-net bisimilar),
while the two nets in Figure

1
are hereditary history-preserving bisimilar (but not i-causal-net bisimilar).
I-causal-net bisimilarity is decidable on bounded Petri nets
[
8
]
; this proof is based on a rather intricate
equivalent bisimulation-based characterization over ordered indexed marking (markings whose tokens are indexed,
to give identity to multiple tokens on a place, and onto which a generation ordering is defined), and this explains why
we have chosen to work directly with
causal-net bisimilarity, which is characterized in a very simple manner by structure-preserving bisimilarity.
We conjecture that i-causal-net bisimilarity is the coarsest self-reversible causality-respecting bisimulation-based behavioral
relation definable on Petri nets.

The problem we tackled differs from the ones in
[
3
,
43
]
or
[
42
,
11
]
,where the focus is respectively to understand the impact of adding the reverse of a single transition to a Petri net, or to add reverses of every transition so
to be able
to go backward on the history.
On the contrary, we do not
add reverse transitions to
the Petri net, but rather we define a reversible behavioral equivalence that ensures that behaviorally equivalent
markings can match not only their forward behavior, but also their backward behavior by undoing events.
We conjecture that if two nets are equivalent according to a reversible behavioral equivalence, their extensions as defined in
[
42
]

are equivalent as well.

Another direction for future work is to apply our results to CCS.
Indeed, CCS and other process algebras have been equipped with Petri net semantics
(see, e.g.,
[
12
,
48
,
26
]
, just to mention a few). In
[
30
]
one of the authors shows that a dialect of CCS,
called
Pet
, is sufficiently expressive to represent all and only the finite P/T nets.
For instance, the specification producer–consumer system in Figure
7
is represented as
\mbox{$({\bf\nu}a)$}(P_{1}\mbox{$\,|\,$}C_{1})
, where

\begin{array}[]{rclrclrclrclrcl}P_{1}&\doteq&{\mbox{\it prod}}.(P_{1}\mbox{$\,|\,$}D_{1})&&D_{1}&\doteq&\overline{a}.\mbox{\bf 1}&&C_{1}&\doteq&\mbox{$\lessdot a\,del\gtrdot$}.C_{1}^{\prime}&&C_{1}^{\prime}&\doteq&{\mbox{\it cons}}.C_{1}\\
\end{array}

1
denotes successful termination, and
\lessdot a,del\gtrdot
denotes that the sequence
a,del
is to be performed atomically.
Hence, thanks to the results proved here, we conclude that
place bisimilar
Pet
process terms are able to match their forward and backward behavior in a causal-consistent
manner. Moreover, place bisimilarity is a congruence over
Pet
, so that the verification that an implementation is equivalent to its specification can be done compositionally.
