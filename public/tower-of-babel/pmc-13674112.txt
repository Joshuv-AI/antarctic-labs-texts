Comorbid conditions after allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation in young adults in Germany—A prospective, multicentre trial
Authors: Pauline Rocke, Lena Tandetzky, Daniel Wolff, Katharina Egger‐Heidrich, Daniela Heidenreich, Kerstin Schaefer‐Eckart, Eva Wagner‐Drouet, Jochen J. Frietsch, Andreas Hochhaus, Inken Hilgendorf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryYoung adults (YA) are particularly affected by comorbid conditions following allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (alloHSCT), as they have to live with them for many years. This is the first multicentre prospective study investigating sequelae after alloHSCT in YA in Germany. In total, 107 patients who survived relapse‐free until day 100 after alloHSCT were enrolled. Median patient age was 29.3 years. Chronic graft‐versus‐host disease (cGvHD) occurred in 40.2%. Comorbidities were captured at days 100, 180, 365 and 910 by applying the post‐transplant multimorbidity index (PTMI). Considering all time points, 12.5%–23.5% of patients had no comorbidity, while 39.2%–49.5% had 1–3, 26.3%–33.0% had 4–6 and 5.7%–8.4% had >6 PTMI‐defined comorbidities respectively. The most prevalent PTMI‐based comorbidities were iron overload, hypogonadism, arterial hypertension, infections, hepatic dysfunction and osteopenia/osteoporosis. However, the spectrum of PTMI‐recorded comorbidities and those concurrently self‐reported by patients differed substantially. Factors linked to a higher PTMI were total body irradiation and active cGvHD. Investigating single PTMI‐defined comorbidities, we found significant associations of osteopenia/osteoporosis with cGvHD, ongoing immunosuppression and hypogonadism, which was more common among female survivors. These results provide a detailed analysis of the occurrence of a vast spectrum of PTMI‐defined comorbidities in YA after alloHSCT.

INTRODUCTION

Adolescents and young adult (AYA) patients are those diagnosed between 15 and 39 years from cancer, 1 hence those aged 18–39 years are described as young adults (YA). YA represent a minority among the population of survivors of allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (alloHSCT). 2 They are particularly vulnerable to profound changes to their lives caused by diagnosis and treatment. Despite increasing survival rates for YA after alloHSCT, 2 recent studies still show a poorer outcome for AYA compared to children. 3 Contributing factors include differences in disease biology and treatment protocols, as well as poorer adherence to therapy and follow‐up care. 4 , 5 , 6 Furthermore, due to their young age, aggressive treatment protocols and high cure rates, YA are particularly affected by long‐term sequelae. 7 Recipients of alloHSCT experience a variety of late effects that can affect most organ systems: 66.4% report at least one chronic health condition and 18.3% report a severe or life‐threatening condition. 8 Especially chronic graft‐versus‐host disease (cGvHD) contributes significantly to the increased risk of comorbid conditions. 8 , 9 As those late effects are associated with a high morbidity and mortality, their prevention and treatment gain significance. While sequelae following alloHSCT in paediatric and elderly patients have been investigated in several studies, 8 , 10 , 11 there is a lack of publications inquiring long‐term outcomes in YA. 9 Recently, the Post‐transplant Multimorbidity Index (PTMI), a new measure to capture comorbidity in survivors, has been developed and validated. 12 The PTMI demonstrated strong measurement properties and, compared to the Charlson Comorbidity Index 13 and Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation‐Specific Comorbidity Index, 14 captured a wider range of sequelae. The score also correlated with the severity of cGvHD, the quality of life (QoL) and physical functioning. 12

This is the first multicentre prospective study in which the PTMI was used to record comorbidities in YA in a prospective setting in order to investigate sequelae after alloHSCT in Germany. The specific objectives of this trial were to quantify the burden of PTMI‐based comorbidity in YA after alloHSCT, to demonstrate the manifestation of late effects over the course of time and to identify risk factors associated with increased comorbidity as well as specific late effects.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Design and setting

This prospective, multicentre, non‐interventional trial among six transplant centres in Germany was approved by the local ethics committee of Jena University Hospital (Reg.‐Nr. 4670‐01/16). All patients transplanted consecutively that fitted the inclusion criteria were invited to participate. Eligibility criteria included aged 18–39 years at date of alloHSCT, relapse‐free survival until inclusion, point of inclusion to the trial at day (d) 100 (±21 days) after alloHSCT and provided signed informed consent. Patients that withdrew their consent, had a calculated life expectancy lower than 6 months, were unable to understand and fill out the forms or suffered from relapse of the underlying malignancy, were excluded. Between 2016 and 2022, 109 patients were enrolled. Two patients were excluded because of early relapse, leading to 107 patients in the final analysis.

Data collection

Patient characteristics were entered into the ‘Computer‐based Health‐Evaluation‐System (CHES) – GvHD‐Register’ (Innsbruck, Austria). Graft‐versus‐host disease (GvHD) status, ‐treatment, immunosuppression and comorbidities were documented at d100 (±21), 180 (±21), 365 (±21) and 910 (±60). Information regarding remission and survival were collected at each date.

Measures

The underlying definition of comorbidity and the calculation of the PTMI were applied as previously published. 12 In addition, at d365 and d910, patients completed several self‐report questionnaires including the occurrence of adverse medical conditions. This list of comorbidities included 28 items, not identical to the comorbidities contained in the PTMI (see Supplementary Information S1).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for demographic data and transplantation factors. To examine the determinants of the PTMI, Mann–Whitney U‐test was applied. Pearson's chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test were used to examine factors influencing single comorbidities. Effect size was calculated using Pearson's correlation (‘r’). The effect sizes were categorized as small (0.1–<0.3), medium (0.3–<0.5) or large (>0.5). To investigate whether the occurrence of comorbidities differed across the assessed time points, a distribution analysis was conducted using the two‐factor Friedman test for related samples, and the significance level was adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni correction. A p‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. SPSS Statistics 29 (IBM, Chicago) was used for all analyses.

RESULTS

Study population

In total, 107 patients with a median age of 29.3 (range 18.6–39.9) years suffering from acute myeloid (38.3%) or lymphatic (23.4%) leukaemia, followed by lymphoma (12.1%) and non‐malignant diseases (11.2%), participated. The majority of patients received peripheral blood stem cells (81.3%) from an unrelated donor (78.5%) after myeloablative conditioning (MAC, 64.5%) and/or total body irradiation (TBI, 55.1%). At time of alloHSCT, 64.5% of patients were in remission. At the prespecified time points, 79.4% (d100), 43.9% (d180), 32.6% (d365) and 11.8% (d910) received an immunosuppressive treatment respectively. In total, 47.7% of patients had developed aGvHD and 40.2% cGvHD, which was active at d100/180/365/910 in 15%/27.3%/21.5%/11.3% of patients and showed a mild presentation in 68.8%/55.6%/50.0%/50.0% respectively. Overall survival at d910 was 84.8%. The patient characteristics are displayed in Table 1.

Documentation status

A fully documented PTMI was available at d100/180/365/910 for 95/88/82/51 patients respectively. Reasons for exclusion or dropout from the study were relapse or death of 5/11/22 patients and a lost to follow‐up of 2/2/22 patients at d180/365/910 respectively. The self‐report questionnaire of comorbidities was completed by 89 patients at d365 and 29 patients at d910.

The PTMI

Burden of PTMI‐defined comorbidity

Considering all time points, 12.5%–23.5% of patients had no comorbidities, while 39.2%–49.5% had 1–3, 26.3%–33.0% had 4–6 and 5.7%–8.4% suffered from >6 PTMI‐defined comorbidities. Median PTMI remained stable at 3 sequelae per patient with a slight decrease at d365 (median 2, 5). At least one PTMI‐defined comorbidity was present at d100/180/365/910 in 84.2%/87.5%/85.4%/76.5% of patients respectively.

Frequency of comorbidities

The distribution of the comorbidities by organ systems differed across the individual time points. Whereas at d100 37.9% of patients suffered from at least one cardiovascular sequelae, only 17.6% were affected at d910. Gastrointestinal conditions were present in 52.6% of patients at d100 and declined to 17.6% at d910 (p < 0.001). Endocrine, pulmonary and neuropsychiatric sequelae showed a slight incline over the course of time. Conditions of the skeletal system rose significantly from 10.5% of patients affected at d100 to 31.4% at d910 (p < 0.001). Looking into the most prevalent single PTMI‐defined comorbidities, iron overload, defined as an elevated serum ferritin >1000 μg/mL, was the most common late effect in the first year, but decreased until day 910 (p < 0,001). The occurrence of osteopenia/osteoporosis rose significantly post‐alloHSCT (p = 0.002). Moreover, a considerable number of YA developed avascular necrosis, in some cases severe enough to require joint replacement, or osteoarthritis as a further orthopaedic sequela by d910.

The prevalence of hepatic dysfunction declined over time (p = 0.056). Considering cardiovascular risk factors, the prevalence of dyslipidaemia inclined steadily (p = 0.014), while arterial hypertension declined non‐significantly until d910 (p = 0.459). Regarding endocrinopathies, an increase of hypogonadism was notable (p = 0.277). In addition, hypothyroidism remained among the most common comorbidities at each time point. Even though smoking was rare, up to 7.4% of YA reported on nicotine abuse. At each point, about one quarter of the YA suffered from infections. Psychiatric disorders requiring treatment were reported at each time point and exceeded the 10% threshold at d365, mainly caused by anxiety and panic disorders. Determinants

Investigating the determinants of the PTMI, at d100, non‐MAC was associated with a higher score (p = 0.005, r = 0.29, median PTMI 2.0 vs. 4.0), while no significant correlation was found for other time points. TBI was linked to a higher PTMI‐score at d910 (p = 0.013, r = 0.34, median PTMI 3.5 vs. 1.0), but not on d100, 180 and 365. An active cGvHD came along with increased PTMI‐defined comorbidity only on d100 (p = 0.02, r = 0.24, median PTMI 4.0 vs. 2.5), while the severity of the cGvHD showed no influence on the PTMI. Compared with patients who had received only one alloHSCT so far, patients with more than one alloHSCT obtained a median PTMI on d910 that was twice as high (p = 0.008, r = 0.37, median PTMI 3.0 vs. 6.0). No significant association was found for the sex of the patient, the donor type (related/unrelated), aGvHD, the disease status at alloHSCT and an ongoing immunosuppressive treatment.

Subsequently, the determinants of selected single comorbidities were explored. The influencing factors selected were relations already established in the literature. We found significant associations of osteopenia/osteoporosis with cGvHD (d910, p = 0.022), ongoing immunosuppression (d910, p = 0.021) and hypogonadism (d100, p = 0.017), but not with the sex or TBI. Patients with cGvHD (d180, p = 0.021) or active immunosuppressive treatment (d365, p = 0.02) showed higher rates of avascular necrosis, while the sex or TBI showed no influence. Hypogonadism was more common among female patients (d100/180/365/910, p = 0.010/0.008/0.003/0.044) and among patients with cGvHD (d100, p = 0.001), but was not associated with TBI, MAC or immunosuppressive treatment. Iron overload was associated with liver dysfunction (d100/180/910, p = 0.023/0.016/0.002), while no correlation was found for cGvHD or immunosuppressive treatment. Patients with nicotine abuse were more likely to suffer from infections (d100, p = 0.035) and a pulmonary impairment correlated with infections as a coexisting sequela (d180, p = 0.007). No association was found for pulmonary impairment with TBI, MAC and immunosuppressive treatment. Patients who received non‐MAC exhibited higher rates of renal failure at d100 (p = 0.047) and d180 (p = 0.042). We found an association of hypertension with renal impairment at d365 (p = 0.026) and d910 (p = 0.017). No correlation was observed for TBI, cGvHD and immunosuppressive treatment. Neurological conditions were more common among patients who had received non‐MAC at d100 (p = 0.011) but did not correlate with TBI, cGvHD or immunosuppressive treatment. For psychiatric disorders, no significant difference between male and female patients could be identified. All correlations are displayed in Table S2.

Comparison with self‐reported comorbidities

Similar to the rates detected by the PTMI, 89.9% of patients self‐reported at least one sequela at d365. However, the spectrum of comorbidities differs considerably: While neurological conditions as detected by the PTMI were relatively rare (6.1%–9.8%), nearly half of patients at d365 (48.3%) and three quarter at d910 (72.4%) reported at least one neurological issue, with concentration difficulties and cognitive impairment being the most reported. The rates of dyspnoea and deterioration of vision, both reported by around one‐third of patients, exceeded the rates of pulmonary (8.5%–13.7%) and visual impairments (1.1%–5.9%) according to the PTMI noticeably. Additionally, 29.2%–41.4% reported ophthalmological problems due to dry eyes. Conversely, the PTMI captured more cases of osteoporosis, osteonecrosis, arterial hypertension, iron overload and kidney diseases. Hepatitis was self‐reported by 4 patients at d365 and 3 patients at d910, while no cases were documented within the PTMI. In total, 39.0% (d365) and 45.1% (d910) of patients were diagnosed with hypogonadism, but only 24.7% and 27.6% reported receiving hormone replacement therapy on the respective time points. DISCUSSION

YA represent a sensitive population among alloHSCT recipients, deserving particular attention but still being understudied in research. However, knowledge about late effects after alloHSCT is essential to establish an adequate follow‐up care for YA that considers their specific needs. Addressing this gap, we used a multicentre, prospective approach to examine sequalae after alloHSCT in YA in Germany. Compared with the validation trial of the PTMI, we observed a higher number of patients with 1–3 and 4–6 comorbidities despite the younger age and the lower proportion of patients with cGvHD, whereas the rate of multimorbid patients was slightly lower. 12 In comparison with other studies, the patients overall exhibited a higher burden of PTMI‐defined comorbidity. In a questionnaire‐based survey, 71% of patients reported at least one sequela, although hypogonadism as one of the most common chronic comorbidities was not included. 8 In said assessment, the number and severity of late effects progressively increased steadily. However, we could not demonstrate this effect, likely due to the comparatively short follow‐up. Other studies reported chronic conditions in 19%–79% of HSCT‐recipients. 9 , 11 , 15 In line with previous studies, cGvHD was identified as a primary predictor of increased PTMI‐defined comorbidity. TBI also displayed a risk factor, while the literature provides mixed evidence regarding this association. 8 , 9 , 15 , 16 The observed association between non‐MAC and increased sequelae can be explained by reversed causality: They were more likely to receive this type of conditioning because of pre‐existing comorbidities.

AlloHSCT is associated with premature cardiovascular disease and adverse events. 17 , 18 These findings could not be reproduced in our data, most likely caused by the relatively short follow‐up period and a health‐conscious young age group. Nevertheless, YA exhibited a considerable higher prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors compared with the general population, particularly arterial hypertension, 19 an established side effect of immunosuppressives and dyslipidaemia. 20 Consistent detecting of these factors as well as preventive and therapeutic interventions are therefore essential. Lifestyle‐related factors also contribute to the increased cardiovascular risk. While the elevated prevalence of obesity reported in other publications 21 , 22 was not observed in our data, a notable proportion of YA engaged in harmful health behaviour through nicotine abuse. Although this behaviour, consistent with another study, occurred less frequently than in the general population, 23 smoking requires particular attention and patient education, as it represents a modifiable risk factor both for cardiovascular disease and for secondary malignancies.

Iron overload was common and is most plausible linked to transfusions, 24 while the decrease observed at d910 is attributable to phlebotomy. Pulmonary conditions were less frequent among YA compared with older patients. 12 , 25 In line with earlier studies, 26 , 27 hypogonadism represented the most significant endocrine late effect among the YA patients, affecting two‐thirds of women and one‐third of men even 30 months after alloHSCT. This emphasizes the importance of hormone replacement after alloHSCT. Neurological sequelae are commonly caused by neurotoxic agents or cGvHD. 28 , 29 Consistent with previous publications, depression requiring treatment was rare in terms of psychiatric comorbidities, 30 , 31 whereas anxiety disorders were more common. Compared to elderly patients, AYA tend to worry more about their future, treatment factors and emotional role functioning. 32 Close attention should be paid to bone diseases, as their prevalence increases steadily following alloHSCT. Despite a presumably healthy bone metabolism prior to transplantation, YA patients showed high rates of bone diseases comparable to elderly patients. 33 , 34 Kidney injury was scarce among YA patients and consistent with other studies, arterial hypertension emerged as a risk factor. 35 , 36 Divergent definitions of renal impairment complicate statements regarding incidence. The documented infections were mostly mild and did not require inpatient hospital care. Although fewer patients were receiving immunosuppressive treatment over time, infection rates did not decrease, most likely attributable to the prolonged immune reconstitution after alloHSCT. 37

The risk of secondary neoplasms rises continuously with time after alloHSCT. During the follow‐up‐period, two YA developed a secondary neoplasm. Furthermore, five YA already had a solid tumour prior to alloHSCT, and two cases of post‐transplant lymphoproliferative disorder were registered. These findings align plausibly with previous studies 9 , 38 and underline the necessity of lifelong, regular participation in cancer screening.

Chronic GvHD is the most significant long‐term complication and is known to increase the risk of several other comorbidities. 12 , 39 Although its prevalence among the YA patients was similar as reported in other cohorts, 40 , 41 it appeared to follow a mostly favourable course within the relatively short follow‐up period.

Limitations of the study are the relatively small sample size particularly regarding the PTMI‐defined comorbidity analysis. However, this must be evaluated in the context of the limited total number of patients transplanted in young adulthood 2 who also had to meet the inclusion criteria of relapse‐free survival at d100 post‐alloHSCT. Moreover, no information was available regarding pre‐existing comorbidities. Therefore, comorbidities arising de novo between alloHSCT and the first assessment cannot be distinguished from pre‐existing conditions. Thus, conclusions regarding causal relations between comorbidities and the transplantation process can only be drawn to a limited extent.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that late effects after alloHSCT are widespread also among YA, in whom lower morbidity and fewer pre‐existing comorbidities would be expected compared with elderly patients. The PTMI appears to be a suitable measure for capturing comorbidities after alloHSCT. However, comparison with the questionnaire‐based sequelae self‐assessment in the same cohort showed that, beyond objectively measurable late effects diagnosed by instrumental and laboratory diagnostics, patients experience a substantial burden of symptoms that are less easily objectifiable, but can still impair QoL. On the other hand, some organ impairments are reported less frequently by patients than documented by medical staff, suggesting an information gap on the patient side. This may reflect that not all diagnostic findings were consistently discussed with patients.

Based on these findings, existing follow‐up‐concepts can now be optimized to better meet the specific needs of this age group. Due to a high loss‐to‐follow‐up rate by d910, this analysis primarily allows conclusions regarding the first year after alloHSCT. As extended follow‐up (5 and 10 years) is planned, this article presents the initial findings. Regardless, further trials covering longer follow‐up periods as well as studies specifically designed to evaluate targeted interventions and their impact on post‐transplant outcomes are desirable to obtain more detailed insights into the post‐transplant course.
