Novel proof of concept assay for rapid, simplified non-invasive fetal RhD screening
Authors: Jean Gekas, Suvi Parviainen, Lawrence Prensky, Audrey Bélanger, Marc-André Rodrigue, Marie-Line Dubois, Capucine Gekas, Ville Veikkolainen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The RhD antigen is a key component of the Rh blood group system and is highly immunogenic, posing risks for RhD-negative pregnant women. While anti-D prophylaxis prevents maternal alloimmunization, concerns regarding availability, overuse, and universal effectiveness persist. Recent advances in non-invasive fetal RHD genotyping have shown potential to reduce unnecessary interventions. This study presents a new non-invasive fetal RhD screening method using cell-free DNA (cfDNA) and dry chemistry quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) with fluorescence measurements of three RHD exons, aimed at enhancing the accuracy and efficiency of RHD genotyping and improving clinical decision-making. We evaluated this new method using cfDNA extracted from plasma samples of 28 RhD-negative women carrying singleton pregnancies. The 20µL cfDNA samples correctly identified all 18 RhD-positive pregnancies and 9/10 RhD-negative pregnancy samples. Our study provides proof of concept that the cfDNA and dry chemistry qPCR method for fetal RHD genotyping is feasible, as it achieved 100% sensitivity. Despite the small sample size, these findings support the use of RHD genotyping as a precise and effective alternative or complement to anti-D prophylaxis, offering improved risk management and care for pregnant women worldwide.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23344-w.

Introduction

By the year 2023, 56 antigens were classified as part of the Rh blood group system1. These antigens are expressed by two cell membrane proteins: RhD and RhCE, encoded by the RHD and RHCE genes, respectively2. The D antigen (RhD) is the most immunogenic and the main trigger for antibody production in RhD-negative individuals.

When an RhD-negative pregnant woman is exposed to RhD-positive red blood cells she may develop anti-RhD alloantibodies, also known as maternal alloimmunization. Maternal alloimmunization often causes the destruction of the fetal red blood cells in subsequent RhD-positive pregnancies, which can lead to hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (HDFN)3. Therefore, D antigen is considered the most clinically significant antigen in the Rh blood group system as it is linked to this potentially lethal blood disorder3. This alloimmunization can be prevented with antenatal and postnatal anti-D prophylaxis, where the mother receives polyclonal anti-RhD immunoglobulin injections.

Despite prophylaxis, the residual risk of maternal RhD immunization is approximately 1% (0.2–1.5%)4,5. As a blood derived product, accessibility for anti-RhD immunoglobulins is limited and contains a small infection risk related to blood-borne diseases, even if it is properly processed and tested6.

Amongst prenatal testing options to determine the fetal RhD status, cfDNA screening stands out as the only non-invasive, highly accurate option that can be performed early enough in pregnancy to guide effective clinical decisions. It avoids the risks associated with invasive procedures and the limitations of late testing, making it the most suitable choice for optimizing RhD prophylaxis programs7.

Some countries have adopted the strategy of targeted anti-D prophylaxis to prevent maternal alloimmunization against the RhD antigen8–13. In this approach, fetal RHD non-invasive genotyping is performed first, and prophylaxis is only given when an RhD-negative mother is carrying a RhD-positive fetus. With this approach, approximately 40% of unnecessary anti-D injections can be avoided. Currently, these countries use qPCR technology for non-invasive screening8–13. This technology is often chosen for its high precision, sensitivity, rapid results, and efficiency in identifying the fetus’ RhD status8–14.

These adoptions illustrate the growing confidence in fetal RHD non-invasive genotyping and its increased use, reflecting its efficacy, safety, and practical benefits in managing RhD incompatibility during pregnancy and childbirth. Despite this progress, in North America, particularly in Canada, no national protocol has been implemented. Currently, no commercial kit has been approved by Health Canada or the FDA for clinical use in this application. This may be because, as with all screening technologies, concerns regarding possible discordant results, along with increased rates of failed or indeterminate results, have been reported in the literature15–19.

To address these concerns, we studied the utility of a new Dry Chemistry qPCR method. Our study aimed to ensure the accuracy and reliability of a simple, fast and non-invasive fetal RhD screening, thereby enhancing prenatal care and informing clinical decision-making for anti-D prophylaxis.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and/or national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. The studies involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the ethics committee of Prenato Clinic. Prenato’s Ethics and Feasibility Review Board gave its approval on September 12, 2023 (approval number 001–2023), with Dr. Jean Gekas, a medical geneticist, identified as the Principal Investigator to conduct this study. Written informed consent for research use of samples was obtained from all participants.

Study design and sample collection

Following the installation of the Eonis™ Q96 instrument (Revvity Inc, Waltham, MA USA) in January 2024, we initiated a proof of concept study. The study focused on plasma samples from 28 RhD-negative pregnant women with singleton pregnancies, selected from a retrospective study of over 1,000 pregnant women who underwent non-invasive prenatal screening (NIPS) for major aneuploidies at the Prenato clinic in Quebec City. All women were between the ages of 22 and 38 and were carrying euploid fetuses. 8–10 mL of maternal plasma was collected between 10 weeks 0 days and 14 weeks 6 days of pregnancy in Cell-free DNA BCT tubes (Streck, Omaha, NE, USA) for this test. All women received normal NIPT results concordant with the fetal status. These surplus samples were stored with patient consent for research use.

Only samples with a known newborn blood group and negative maternal RhD status were included. Newborn’s blood group was determined through follow-up by the clinical team at the Prenato clinic. The presence of the RhD antigen had been determined from blood samples of the babies after birth and consisted of 18 RhD-positive and 10 RhD-negative pregnancies. The fetal RhD status was blinded to the entire team performing the non-invasive RHD genotype screening and were only provided by the clinical team after final interpretations were resulted.

Positive run control was comprised of synthetic plasmids containing target RhD exon sequences. Nuclease-free water was used as negative control. One positive and one negative control were included in each run.

Sample processing and cfDNA extraction

Plasma samples from 28 RhD-negative pregnant women were stored at -80 °C until use for cfDNA extraction. Approximately 5-35 ng of cfDNA was then extracted from 3 mL plasma samples using the automated Vanadis Extract® unit (Revvity Inc, Waltham, MA USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The cfDNA was eluted in approximately 80μL of elution buffer by the Vanadis system and was used immediately after extraction. No quantification of the cfDNA was performed.

RHD genotyping assay

The RhD screening technology utilizes the dry chemistry qPCR workflow together with Eonis™ Q96-well real-time PCR thermal cycler. A research use only commercial kit (Eonis™ RhD qPCR kit, Revvity Inc, Waltham, MA USA) identifies RhD status from cfDNA samples using pre-loaded PCR plates with dried reagent pellets. The assay amplifies and detects the four targets (RHD exons 5, 7, and 10, as well as RPP30 as an internal amplification control) in a single PCR reaction. Based on in-silico analysis, the primers and probes used in this assay can detect the RHD ψ variant, which would result in a negative signal for exon 5 but positive signals for exons 7 and 10.

The extracted cfDNA samples were added directly to the dry chemistry qPCR plate, sealed optically, vortexed, and briefly centrifuged. Each sample was run as two replicates with 20µL and as two replicates with 10µL (+ 10µL TE-buffer) of cfDNA sample. The qPCR was conducted using the Eonis Q96 real-time PCR thermal cycler under the following conditions: 95 °C for two minutes, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 seconds, 61 °C for 30 seconds with fluorescence measurement for FAM™ (RHD exon 10), HEX™ (RHD exon 5), ROX™ (RHD exon7) and Cy®5 (internal control gene RRP30).

The presence of each target DNA is determined based on the fluorescence emitted by the probes during amplification, which is read by the Eonis Q96 Real-Time PCR Thermal Cycler. Fluorescence intensity is measured throughout each cycle. The assay detects RHD exon sequences in RhD-negative mothers, indicating a RhD-positive fetus.

Data analysis

The study took place from December 2023 to February 2024, encompassing the retrieval of 28 samples from our biobank of RhD-negative maternal plasma samples through result compilation. Simultaneous analysis of RHD exons 5, 7, and 10 should enable the detection of multiple RHD variants, while RPP30 was measured as an internal technical control to verify that the extraction and qPCR worked properly for each sample. A 20µL sample volume protocol was compared to a 10µL sample volume protocol.

The presence of the RHD gene was retrospectively analyzed from the cfDNA samples using the EonisQ RUO Software (version 1.0.1.12, Revvity Inc, Waltham, MA USA). As the samples were run in duplicate (for both 20µL and 10µL sample volumes) and as each reaction contained targets for exons 5, 7 and 10, each sample resulted in a total of six RHD target results for 20µL sample volumes and six RHD target results for 10µL sample volumes. A sample was considered to be positive for RhD if at least four RHD target results were positive, and negative for RhD if at least four targets were negative.

The prenatal molecular analysis was then compared to the newborn’s blood group after delivery in order to determine the performance of the assay.

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that this methodology has distinct advantages, with minimal concerns (Tables 7 and 8). It was highly accurate for determining fetal RhD status using cfDNA samples, with 100% accuracy for RhD-positive samples. Additionally, all positive samples tested consistently positive for the three targeted RHD exons, highlighting the robustness and reliability of the test in detecting RhD-positive fetuses. The study showed that using 20µL volume provided superior results for RhD-negative pregnancy samples compared to using 10µL volume.

Among the 10 samples from pregnancies with RhD-negative newborns, one yielded a false positive result. Our discrepant result is particularly interesting, as it showed positivity for all six RHD targets, despite being from a RhD-negative newborn. A plausible explanation for this scenario is that the newborn’s RhD-negative antigen status was due to an RHD allelic variant with incomplete exon representation or structural alteration (such as a partial deletion of the RHD gene). These variants or structural alterations (e.g. weak-D and partial-D phenotypes) can produce RhD-negative results using serological methods20, but the remaining RHD genetic material may still be amplified in a qPCR assay, resulting in a positive RhD result. This highlights a scenario where the RHD gene remains detectable through genetic testing despite a negative serological test at birth. While this could lead to an unnecessary, albeit benign, intervention, such discordant results could be attributed to variations in the RHD gene targets. This observation underscores the importance of complementing genetic tests with appropriate clinical assessments, such as sequencing, based on your region’s guidelines.

Cycle threshold (Ct) values were examined for all runs (Supplemental Tables 1–2) but were not used for the interpretation of sample results. The rationale being that this test would only be used for RhD-negative women, and thus there should not be any maternal contribution to the Ct value. As a result, if there is amplification of the RhD targets, it should solely come from a RhD-positive fetus. An exception could occur if the mother harbours a variant which would make her clinically RhD-negative, but which would be amplified and thus result in a Ct value and indicate that the fetus appears to be RhD-positive. Although in theory this may be a situation where using a Ct cutoff could assist in diagnosis, clinically it would only result in the woman receiving a prophylaxis injection that could be unnecessary but would ultimately be harmless. Additionally, as initial cfDNA levels vary tremendously based on the measured fetal fraction of the sample21, Ct values would also vary based upon the initial cfDNA amount in the sample, thus making the implementation of a Ct cutoff difficult.

This assay also addresses concerns surrounding false negatives, false positives, and the rates of failed or inconclusive results reported in prior studies. Studies utilizing single-exon methodologies have shown inconclusive rates of approximately 3.2–5.1% and discrepant results in 0.16–1.8% of samples15,16,22. Even studies employing multi-exon methodologies at later gestational ages reported inconclusive rates of 1.4–5.8% and discrepant results between 0.01–1.3%7,12,14. Meta-analyses and reviews combining various methodologies have reported inconclusive rates ranging from 0–17% and false negative rates between 0–1.5%7,17–19,23. Despite our limited sample size, our study reported no false negatives or inconclusive results using 20µL cfDNA sample volume, suggesting this methodology could improve reliability with this sample volume.

International health authorities strongly advocate for RHD genotyping as a cost-effective and clinically beneficial approach. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence concluded that RHD genotyping is cost-effective24. Similarly, the Haute Autorité de Santé in France25,26 and the British Committee for Standards in Haematology in the UK27 recommend RHD genotyping for targeted prophylaxis and therapy. While the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists supports evaluating RhD status through cfDNA screening, stating: “Noninvasive fetal red blood cell antigen genotyping utilizing cfDNA isolated from maternal plasma has demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity for detection of fetal Rh(D) antigen status” and that using non-invasive prenatal screening to prioritize the use of Rho(D) immune globulin (RhIg) to “conserve RhIg supply is a reasonable consideration in the practice setting that is experiencing RhIg shortages.”28

The prevention of RhD alloimmunization through prophylaxis has reduced incidence in at-risk pregnancies from 13–16% to 0.14–0.2%29. However, approximately 40% of RhD-negative women carry RhD-negative fetuses and receive unnecessary treatment14,24,30. Reducing these unnecessary prophylactic treatments would lead to cost savings by avoiding unnecessary serotherapy, consultations, high-risk pregnancy monitoring, and travel expenses for women from remote areas, along with alleviating patient anxiety, preventing global RhIg shortages, and the adverse outcomes occasionally associated with RhIg6,24,28.

Non-invasive fetal RHD genotyping has demonstrated a high acceptance rate, with Finnish and Dutch data showing that 97% of women opted for genotyping over prophylaxis8,10. In a study of 270 RhD-negative pregnant women in the UK, 92.1% supported offering cfDNA screening to all RhD-negative women31. Moreover, since 2013 in the Netherlands and 2014 in Denmark, cord blood typing after birth to guide postnatal prophylaxis has been discontinued, indicating that prenatal genotyping results are now used to guide care9,11.

Regardless, clinicians should continue to monitor RhD-negative pregnant women for antibodies throughout pregnancy and assess the infant’s RhD status at birth to confirm results and guide further care, given the possibility of rare false negatives and other causes of alloimmunization. If a false negative from prenatal screening is identified after birth, there will still be an opportunity to administer a dose of prophylactic immunoglobulin therapy to minimize the risk of RhD sensitization.

Beyond the potential for sampling bias due to our smaller sample size, a limitation to our study is that our sample classification depends on the number of RHD targets and does not take into consideration that some RHD variants may lead to a positive signal from only one exon and would therefore lead to a negative result. However, even in this scenario, we wouldn’t know whether such an RHD variant would be immunogenic for the mother, and thus clinically relevant. Nonetheless, a possible solution could be to have the test call a result positive if one RHD exon is deleted within both replicates. Implementing this change wouldn’t alter the results of our study, but it could make the result interpretation safer, despite being more complex to interpret. As the samples in our study did not include any of these potentially complicating scenarios, laboratories may choose to design their interpretation of the results as they see fit, including the consideration of using ct-values for the interpretation of maternal variants and/or exploring better ways to identify the RHD ψ variant by looking for positive signals from exons 7 and 10, but not exon 5. Our suspicion is that laboratories will ultimately decide to use parameters where these variant pregnancy samples will be considered “high risk” and will result in administering the treatment, as this is the safer approach due to the benign effect of the treatment in low risk pregnancies.

Conclusions

Our study shows that early and accurate detection of fetal RhD status using cfDNA and dry chemistry qPCR on a 20µL volume offers key advantages over other methodologies, including enhanced risk management, reduced need for invasive testing, and optimized use of anti-D prophylaxis, making it a reliable and essential tool that improves the clinical management of RhD-negative pregnancies while empowering healthcare professionals and expectant mothers to make informed decisions based on precise, non-invasive results.
