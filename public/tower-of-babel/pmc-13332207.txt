Probiotic potential of Lactobacillus acidophilus TMPC 41127 isolated from traditional cheese in inhibiting Salmonella Typhi biofilms and virulence genes
Authors: Behrooz Alizadeh Behbahani, Parisa Ghasemi, Alireza Vasiee, Zeinab Mousavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study comprehensively investigated the probiotic and anti-biofilm potential of the Lactobacillus acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain, isolated from traditional Khiki cheese. Initially, its phenotypic characteristics, including resistance to harsh gastrointestinal conditions (low pH and bile salts) and its ability to colonize (surface hydrophobicity 58.9%, auto-aggregation 47.5%, and adhesion to Caco-2 cells 13.9%), were evaluated. The results showed that the cell-free supernatant (CFS) of this strain has strong antimicrobial effects against pathogens, particularly Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhi (Salmonella Typhi). CFS dose-dependently inhibited the formation of Salmonella Typhi biofilms by 79.52% (1/4×MIC) to 13.16% (4×MIC) and degraded mature biofilms, demonstrating anticancer activity against HeLa, HT-29, and MCF-7 cells (IC50: 63.6–79.7 mg/mL). These anti-pathogenic effects were confirmed by observing morphological changes in the pathogen’s cells and a reduction in the expression of biofilm and invasion-related genes (csgD, csgA, bcsA, invA, and invC). Additionally, the strain demonstrated other health-promoting properties, including cholesterol reduction (54.62%) and high antioxidant activity. The safety assessments (no hemolytic or DNase activity and no biogenic amine production) and its ability to inhibit the proliferation of cancer cells were also favorable. In conclusion, due to its outstanding probiotic and anti-biofilm properties, the L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain is introduced as a promising candidate for food and therapeutic applications.Keywords: Anti-biofilm, Probiotic properties, Cell-free supernatant, Salmonella Typhi, Antimicrobial activitySubject terms: Biotechnology, Microbiology

Introduction

In recent years, with increasing public health awareness and significant scientific advancements, the use of microorganisms and their metabolites in industry, food safety, and disease treatment has gained considerable attention1. Among these, bacteria, especially species of the genus Lactobacillus, have played a pioneering role in the development of probiotic products. According to the guidelines set by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and the World Health Organization (WHO), probiotics are defined as “live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host”2. Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) are a diverse group of Gram-positive bacteria that produce lactic acid through the fermentation of sugars. Originally recognized for their role in the production and preservation of fermented foods, they are now also known as a crucial part of the natural microbiota in humans and animals3. With high resistance to harsh conditions such as acidic pH and bile salts, LAB not only colonize various environments, but also offer numerous health benefits due to their probiotic properties4. These advantages include inhibiting the growth of pathogens through the production of antimicrobial compounds like lactic acid, hydrogen peroxide, and bacteriocins, as well as alleviating lactose intolerance and boosting the immune system5,6.

One of the prominent LAB species is Lactobacillus acidophilus. This bacterium has been extensively studied for its physiology and function. Its high resistance to acid and bile salts ensures its survival under harsh digestive conditions (Huang et al., 20211;. Extensive research has demonstrated a wide range of probiotic health benefits associated with L. acidophilus, including modulating the immune system and assisting in the prevention and treatment of diarrhea. The bacterium’s ability to interfere with host metabolism and gut microbiota is attributed to the production of active substances like extracellular polysaccharides (EPS) and bacteriocins1. In contrast, biofilms, as communities of sessile microorganisms, represent one of the most prominent survival mechanisms for bacteria in nature. These complex structures enable bacteria to resist a wide range of environmental stressors, which can make them up to 1,000 times more resistant than in their free-living (planktonic) form. This characteristic makes biofilms a primary cause of persistent chronic infections and a major factor in the development of multidrug or even total drug resistance7.

In this context, Salmonella is one of the most common and significant foodborne pathogens, posing a serious threat to global public health (Liu et al., 2024). Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhi (Salmonella Typhi), which causes enteric fever, is an obligate human pathogen. The pathogenicity of Salmonella Typhi is linked to its ability to form biofilms. Biofilm-dwelling cells demonstrate higher resistance and greater virulence compared to their free-living counterparts (Liu et al., 2024). Given the growing concerns over food safety and antimicrobial resistance, there is an urgent need for new strategies to control pathogenic bacteria, particularly those in biofilms. Traditional antibiotic-based approaches are often ineffective against the resistant microbes within these structures8. This has led to the exploration of alternative strategies such as bacteriotherapy using probiotics, especially LAB. LAB and their antimicrobial metabolites, found in their CFS, offer a promising alternative to antibiotics8,9. However, the anti-biofilm activity of these bacteria is often strain-specific, indicating that more research is needed to identify the most effective strains. Therefore, considering the serious health and economic challenges posed by microbial biofilms, this study aimed to comprehensively investigate the probiotic and anti-biofilm potential of the strain L. acidophilus TMPC 41127, isolated from Khiki cheese. We adopted a multi-faceted approach to examine its safety, and anti-biofilm, antimicrobial, and anti-infective effects10,11.

Materials and methods

Medium and Reagents

In this study, a variety of culture medium from the brand Quelab (made in Canada) were used, including De Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) Broth, De Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) Agar, Mueller-Hinton Agar, Blood Agar, Skim Milk Agar, Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNase) Agar, Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB), Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), and De Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe with L-cysteine (MRSC) Agar. Also, various chemicals and reagents were used from the brands Sigma and Merck (USA and Germany). These include; 2,2’-Azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS), Sodium persulfate, methanol, 2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), Gallic acid, Folin-Ciocalteu reagent, Aluminum chloride, Sodium carbonate, sodium hydroxide, o-Phthalaldehyde, 2,3,5-Triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC). All culture medium and chemicals were of analytical grade (98–99% purity)12,13.

LAB Isolation and identification

Isolation and molecular identification

To isolate strain, 5 g of a traditional cheese sample was first homogenized in 45 mL of 0.1% peptone water. Subsequently, serial dilutions (10− 1 to 10− 6) of this suspension were prepared and cultured on MRS agar pleat medium at 37 °C for 48 h under anaerobic conditions. For the preliminary identification of the isolated strain, its phenotypic characteristics were first determined by examining cellular morphology and colony arrangement using a light microscope. For molecular identification, genomic DNA was first extracted using the Genomic DNA isolation VI kit. Prior to extraction, cell culture had been carried out in MRS broth for 24 h at 37 °C under anaerobic conditions. Subsequently, the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified via Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) using the universal primers 27FYM (5′-AGAGTTTGATYMTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). The PCR thermal cycling conditions were set as follows: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 30 s intervals at 94 °C and 54 °C, and 2 min at 72 °C; and a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min. The amplified fragments (amplicons) were visualized and evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis (95 V, 45 min). Subsequently, the homology of the amplified gene sequences was investigated through nucleotide sequencing14. Sequencing analysis revealed that the isolated strain, designated Lactobacillus acidophilus A2, shares 99% similarity with Lactobacillus acidophilus strain TMPC 4112715,16.

Phenotypic and biochemical identification

For the preliminary identification of the isolated strain, its phenotypic characteristics were first determined by examining cellular morphology and colony arrangement using a light microscope. Subsequently, the strain’s biochemical capabilities and environmental tolerance were evaluated. These tests included assessments of motility, Gram staining, catalase activity, and carbohydrate fermentation, as well as its tolerance to various pH levels (2.5, 3.5, and 5), salt concentrations (2%, 4%, and 6%), and temperatures (15, 37, and 40 °C)17–19.

Carbohydrate fermentation assay

To precisely identify the strain, the API 50 CHL kit (bioMérieux, Marcy l’Étoile, France) was used to assess the fermentation of 49 carbohydrates. This process was performed in full compliance with the manufacturer’s instructions. First, a bacterial suspension with a turbidity equivalent to McFarland standard 2 was prepared and inoculated into the medium. After inoculation, it was incubated under anaerobic conditions (with the creation of a mineral oil layer on the wellhead) at 37 °C for 48 h. Carbohydrate fermentation was evaluated by visual inspection of the medium’s color change. Carbohydrate fermentation was evaluated by visually observing the color change of the medium from blue-green (alkaline) to yellow (acidic). Results were recorded at 24 and 48 h as positive (+), weak/delayed (±), or negative (-)20.

Enzyme activity assay (API ZYM)

To determine the extracellular enzymatic activities of the L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain, the API ZYM kit (bioMérieux, France) with 19 substrates was used. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, then resuspended in a sterile phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.0) to a final concentration of 1 × 109 colony forming unit (CFU)/mL. This suspension was inoculated into the microcupules of the API ZYM plate and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Following incubation, ZYM A and ZYM B reagents were sequentially added to each well. Color changes, indicating enzymatic activity, were observed and recorded after 5 min at room temperature. Results were recorded using a visual color intensity scale from 0 to 5. A score of 0 indicated a negative reaction with no color change, while 5 represented the highest color intensity. Scores of 3, 4, and 5 were considered a positive reaction (Sneha et al., 2024)21,22.

Probiotic and functional characteristics

Gastrointestinal tolerance

Bile salt tolerance

To investigate bile salt resistance, the target strain was first incubated in MRS broth at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, to separate the bacterial cells, the culture was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting cell pellet was washed with phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.0) and resuspended to achieve a standardized bacterial suspension equivalent to 0.5 McFarland turbidity. Then, 100 µL of the strain suspension were inoculated onto MRS agar plates containing 0.1%, 0.3%, and 0.5% (w/v) bile salt. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the formed colonies were counted and reported log Colony Forming Units per milliliter (log CFU/mL)23.

Acid tolerance

To investigate the acid resistance of the target bacterium, the strain was first incubated in MRS broth under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 24 h. For bacterial cell separation, the culture was centrifuged (Hermle, Germany) at 6000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. Then, the resulting bacterial pellet was suspended in phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) to achieve a suspension with an optical density (OD) of 0.6 at 600 nm. 100 µL of the prepared microbial suspension were inoculated into media with different pH levels (2.5, 3.5, and 5), adjusted using HCl, and incubated for 3 h at 37 °C. Finally, 100 µL from each sample were serially diluted and spread onto MRS agar plates. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C under anaerobic conditions, the formed colonies were counted and reported as log CFU/mL23.

NaCl tolerance

To evaluate the NaCl tolerance of the strain, MRS broth containing NaCl at concentrations of 2%, 4%, and 6% (w/v) was prepared. A fresh culture of the strain was added and incubated for 48 h at 37 °C. Bacterial growth was monitored by measuring the OD at 600 nm after 24 and 48 h of incubation. A negative control containing only the medium without bacterial was included (Khushboo et al., 2023)24–26.

Tolerance to simulated gastric juice

The tolerance of the L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain to simulated human gastrointestinal conditions was evaluated using the method of Barzegar et al.27,. Briefly, the activated strain, cultured in MRS broth, was first centrifuged at 9000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was then washed twice with phosphate buffer. Subsequently, 1 mL of the bacterial suspension (9log CFU/mL) was added to 9 mL of a simulated gastric fluid (containing 125 mM NaCl, 7 mM KCl, 45 mM NaHCO3​, and 3 g/L pepsin, pH 2.5). This mixture was incubated for 3 h at 37 °C. Following this, the bacterial suspension was centrifuged at 3800 rpm for 10 min. The resulting pellet was mixed with a simulated intestinal fluid (containing 0.1% w/v pancreatin and 0.15% w/v bile salt, pH 8.0) and incubated for 3 h at 37 °C. Finally, the resulting bacterial suspension from each step was incubated on MRS agar medium under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C for 24 h, and then the number of surviving bacteria was counted and reported as log CFU/mL28–30.

Antimicrobial activity

CFS preparation

First, 100 µL of the fresh L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain was inoculated into 10 mL of MRS broth and incubated anaerobically for 24 h at 37 °C. The inoculated culture was incubated for 48 h at 37 °C. To isolate the supernatant, the sample was then centrifuged for 5 min at 9000 rpm at 4 °C. The supernatant was subsequently passed through a 0.45 μm filter to completely remove the cells and obtain the CFS. The CFS was freeze-dried for 24 h and stored at 4 °C until the assays were performed.

Disc diffusion agar (DDA) and well diffusion agar (WDA) methods

The antimicrobial activity of the CFS was evaluated using both the DDA and WDA methods. For the activation of pathogenic bacteria, 100 µL of stock culture of bacterial strains was transferred to MHA medium and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C under aerobic conditions. In the DDA method, 100 µL of the pathogenic bacterial suspension (at 0.5 McFarland turbidity, equivalent to 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL) was spread on the surface of the MHA plates. Discs containing CFS at a concentration of 200 mg/mL were then placed on the agar surface. A blank disc (without active substance) served as a negative control, and an antibiotic disc was used as a positive control. For the WDA method, after culturing the pathogenic bacteria on the MHA surface, wells with a diameter of 6 mm were created. CFS was added to one well, while sterile distilled water was added to another as a control. Finally, the plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C under aerobic conditions. Following incubation, the diameters of the inhibition zones (IZ) around the discs and wells were measured and recorded in millimeters23.

Modified double layer method

To examine the antimicrobial activity, the strain under study was first activated in MRS broth. It was then spotted onto an MRS agar plate, which was incubated for 24 h at 37 °C under anaerobic co1nditions. The plate was subsequently overlaid with a thin layer of MHA. Following this, the pathogenic bacterial suspension was diluted to a final concentration of 1 × 106 CFU/mL. Then, 100 µL of this diluted suspension was uniformly spread onto the surface of the culture medium, and the plate was re-incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Finally, to evaluate the susceptibility of the pathogens to the studied strain, the clear zones surrounding the spots were identified and measured34.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

The MIC was determined using the method of Safa Larijani et al.35,, with slight modifications. Briefly, the pathogenic strain was first activated, and a suspension with a turbidity of 0.5 McFarland was prepared, which is equivalent to 1.5 × 108 bacteria. Then, serial dilutions of CFS (0.5–256 mg/mL) were prepared using MHB, and then 100 µL of each dilution was transferred to the wells of a 96-well plate, and 20 µL of the microbial suspension was subsequently added to each well. The inoculated plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C under anaerobic conditions. After this incubation period, 10 µL of a 0.5% TTC solution was added to each well, and the plates were re-incubated for an additional 30 min to visualize microbial growth through a color change. The positive control was culture medium with microbial suspension (without CFS), and the negative control was culture medium with CFS (without microbial suspension).

Inhibition of formation and degradation of mature biofilms

The activity of LAB in inhibiting biofilm formation and degradation of mature biofilms was measured using the method of Vasiee et al.36, with slight modifications. First, bacteria were cultured in TSB for 24 h. A suspension with a concentration of 108 CFU/mL was then prepared from this culture and inoculated into TSB medium containing various concentrations (1/4×MIC – 4×MIC) of CFS extracts. After transferring 100 µL of this suspension into the wells of a 96-well microtiter plate, the plate was incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. The well without the extract served as the positive control, while the well without bacteria was the negative control. Subsequently, the wells were emptied, washed with PBS (pH = 7.0), and stained with a 2% crystal violet solution. The crystal violet was then dissolved by adding 33% acetic acid, and the OD of the solution was measured at a wavelength of 570 nm. To evaluate the degradation of mature biofilms, the cell suspension was removed after biofilm formation. The remaining biofilms were then treated with 100 µL of CFS at various concentrations. Following incubation and a final wash, the OD of the treated samples was measured and compared to the control to calculate the percentage of biofilm inhibition.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

To investigate the effect of the CFS from L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 on the cell morphology of Salmonella Typhi, SEM was used23. Briefly, the CFS was mixed with the bacterial suspension in a 1:1 ratio (v/v) and incubated for 18 h at 37 °C. After centrifugation (4500 rpm for 5 min), the resulting pellet was washed with PBS. The sample was then fixed with a 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution for 2 h at 4 °C. Subsequently, samples were dehydrated using a series of increasing alcohol concentrations. After washing with a sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4), the samples were coated with gold and then observed by SEM to examine their surface morphology.

Total phenolic content (TPC)

The TPC in CFS samples was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. For this purpose, a solution of CFS was prepared at a concentration of 10 mg/mL. Subsequently, 1 mL of this solution was combined with 1 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. Following this, 2 mL of a sodium carbonate (7.5%) solution was added, and the mixture was vortexed for 5 s. The samples were then incubated for 30 min at 25 °C in the dark. Finally, the absorbance of the solution was measured at 765 nm. The TPC of the samples was calculated and expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalent per gram of lyophilized CFS (mgGAE/g)42.

Total flavonoid content (TFC)

To determine the TFC in CFS samples, we used a modified method based on Noshad et al.43,. Briefly, 1 mL of CFS was mixed with 1.25 mL of distilled water. Subsequently, 150 µL of 5% sodium nitrite was added to the mixture. After 5 min, 300 µL of 10% aluminum trichloride was incorporated. Finally, 1 mL of 1 M sodium hydroxide was added, and the absorbance of the solution was measured at 510 nm. Distilled water served as the blank for instrument calibration. The total flavonoid content was reported as milligrams of quercetin equivalent per gram of CFS (mg QE/g CFS)43.

Cholesterol assimilation

To evaluate the strains’ ability to lower cholesterol, a method similar to Castorena-Alba et al.44, was used. Each LAB strain, at a concentration of 109 CFU/mL, was inoculated at 1% (v/v) into 10 mL of MRS broth. The medium was enriched with 0.2% sodium thioglycollate, 0.2% sodium taurocholate, and cholesterol at a final concentration of approximately 100 µg/mL. An un-inoculated control was also included. The cultures were incubated anaerobically for 24 h at 37 °C. Following incubation, bacterial cells were removed by centrifugation at 10,500 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The remaining cholesterol in the supernatant was measured using the o-phthalaldehyde colorimetric method. In this procedure, 1 mL of the supernatant was mixed with KOH and absolute ethanol, vortexed, and heated. Then, 2 mL of distilled water and 3 mL of hexane were added, and the hexane layer containing cholesterol was separated and evaporated under nitrogen gas. Finally, o-phthalaldehyde reagent and concentrated sulfuric acid were added, and the absorbance was measured at 550 nm with a spectrophotometer after 10 min.

Safety assessment

Antibiotic resistance

The susceptibility of the tested strain to common therapeutic antibiotics, including imipenem, penicillin, erythromycin, tetracycline, gentamicin, nitrofurantoin, chloramphenicol, amikacin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, was evaluated. For this purpose, 100 µL of the activated probiotic strain (turbidity equivalent to a 0.5 McFarland standard) were spread onto MRS agar medium. Subsequently, antibiotic discs and a blank disc (without antibiotic) were placed on the culture surface. After 48 h of anaerobic incubation at 37 °C, the diameter of the inhibition zone around each antibiotic disc was recorded. The susceptibility results for the strain were reported as resistant, intermediately susceptible, and susceptible45.

Hemolytic assay

The hemolytic activity of the target strain was determined by culturing it on blood agar medium containing 5% (w/v) sheep blood. The plates were incubated for 48 h at 37 °C. After this period, the strain’s hemolytic activity was analyzed and classified based on the lysis of red blood cells around the colonies. Alpha hemolysis (α) was indicated by clear halos, beta hemolysis (β) by green halos, and gamma hemolysis (γ) by the absence of any halos. Ultimately, the strain was identified as safe and non-hemolytic only if it exhibited gamma hemolysis (Khushboo et al., 2023).

DNase activity

To assess the ability of isolates to produce the DNase enzyme, the selected strains were streaked onto DNase agar medium (Tryptone, Deoxyribonucleic acid, Sodium chloride, 0.01% toluidine blue, and Agar). The plates were then incubated for 48 h at 37 °C and examined for the formation of a clear zone or discoloration. The observation of a discernible, pinkish halo around the colonies indicated positive DNase enzyme activity46.

Biogenic amine synthesis

To assess the ability of the L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain to produce biogenic amines, it was first cultured in MRS broth supplemented with 1% of amino acid precursors (tyrosine, ornithine, lysine, and histidine) for 24 h at 37 °C. Next, a 10 µL sample of the final culture was inoculated onto MRS agar plates containing the same precursors and 0.06% bromocresol purple. After a subsequent 24 h incubation at 37 °C, a color change in the medium from yellow to purple was considered an indication of biogenic amine production. In this experiment, the L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain and Escherichia coli were used as positive and negative controls, respectively47.

Cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxicity of the CFS was evaluated using the MTT assay, following the protocol by Alizadeh Behbahani et al.48,. Initially, HT-29 (Iranian Biological Research Center (IBRC) No. C10097), HeLa (IBRC No. C11311), and MCF-7 (IBRC No. C10682) cells (1 × 105 cells per well) were seeded in the wells of a 96-well microtiter plate. Once the cells reached at least 70% confluence, the culture medium was replaced with 200 µL of fresh DMEM supplemented with fetal bovine serum. Various concentrations of CFS (from 0 to 200 mg/mL) were then added to the wells, and the samples were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2​ atmosphere. After the 24 h incubation, 30 µL of a 5 mg/mL MTT solution was added to each well, and incubation continued for another 3 h. The culture medium was then removed, and 200 µL of DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals. Finally, absorbance was measured at 570 nm using an ELISA reader. The IC50 value, representing the CFS concentration that reduced cell viability by 50%, was then determined.

Statistical analysis

To ensure reproducibility and statistical validity, each assay was performed in triplicate as independent biological experiments. The results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA; https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare the means at a significance level of p < 0.05, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc multiple comparison test.

Results and discussion

Phenotypic and biochemical identification

A strain of L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 was isolated from local cheese and characterized through morphological and biochemical analyses to determine its key phenotypic features (Table 2). Colonies of the strain grown on MRS agar were round, smooth, and white to off-white in color. Microscopic examination (Fig. 1) confirmed that the cells were Gram-positive and rod-shaped (bacilli), as well as non-spore forming and non-motile. The catalase test was negative, which is a key characteristic of LAB. The strain exhibited growth under anaerobic conditions and demonstrated a wide range of environmental tolerance, with favorable growth observed at various salt concentrations (2, 3 and 6%), a broad range of pH levels (2.5 to 7), and diverse temperatures (15, 37, and 40 °C). Furthermore, the strain was capable of fermenting common sugars, including glucose, lactose, sucrose, and fructose. These collective characteristics comprehensively confirm the phenotypic profile of the strain for preliminary identification.

Carbohydrate fermentation assay

The results from the carbohydrate fermentation assay (details in Table 3) indicated the ability of the L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain to metabolize a variety of carbohydrate sources. This strain was capable of fermenting and utilizing simple sugars (such as D-ribose, D-galactose, D-glucose, D-fructose, and D-mannose), sugar alcohols (mannitol and sorbitol), disaccharides (cellobiose, maltose, lactose, and sucrose), and glycosides (amygdalin, arbutin, esculin, and salicin). From an ecological standpoint, the expansive metabolic flexibility demonstrated by L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 in utilizing a broad spectrum of complex dietary carbohydrates and glycosides is of immense physiological significance. In the highly competitive environment of the distal colon, where simple saccharides are vastly depleted, the ability to selectively perform saccharolytic fermentation on complex fibers serves as a massive survival advantage. This robust catabolic capacity not only ensures the strain’s competitive exclusion of non-saccharolytic pathogenic taxa but also acts as the primary driver for the production of essential Short-Chain Fatty Acids (SCFAs) predominantly lactate and acetate. These organic acids subsequently participate in dynamic cross-feeding networks with butyrate-producing commensals, ultimately reinforcing the gut epithelial barrier integrity and exerting profound systemic anti-inflammatory effects49,50. Furthermore, this versatile biochemical profile positions TMPC 41127 for targeted symbiotic formulations alongside specific prebiotics. Goderska et al., (2007) reported that after 48 h of incubation, L. acidophilus and B. bifidumstrains were able to assimilate glucose, fructose, N-acetyl-D-glucosamine, maltose, lactose, sucrose, trehalose, and gentiobiose. Similarly, Dumitru et al.51, showed that L. paracasei CCM 1837 and L. plantarum ATCC 8014 could ferment sugars such as galactose, glucose, fructose, mannose, esculin, cellobiose, lactose, and turanose. It is worth noting that the ability to ferment certain carbohydrates is plasmid-encoded20; therefore, the loss or acquisition of plasmids can lead to metabolic inconsistencies and alter the fermentation pattern.

Probiotic and functional characteristics

Gastrointestinal tolerance

Bile salt tolerance

The gut is a complex digestive environment where bile salts, reaching up to 10 mM, can negatively impact lactobacilli viability. Despite their vital role in fat digestion, this makes bile tolerance a crucial criterion for selecting effective and stable probiotic strains52. The results obtained, as shown in Fig. 2, clearly demonstrate that both an increase in bile salt concentration and incubation time have a significant impact on the viability of the tested strain. At time zero, the number of viable cells was 8.49 ± 0.12, 8.40 ± 0.10, and 8.31 ± 0.16 Log CFU/mL at bile salt concentrations of 0.2%, 0.4%, and 0.6%, respectively (p < 0.05). However, a decrease in the strain’s viability was observed upon exposure to bile stress, with the inhibitory effect of bile significantly increasing at the highest concentration (0.6%), leading to a reduction in viability to 7.43 ± 0.12 Log CFU/mL after 3 h. These findings are consistent with other studies that have shown LAB can survive at high bile concentrations (27,52,53; Behrooz Alizadeh et al., 2024). Some probiotics deconjugate bile acids via the bile salt hydrolase (BSH) enzyme, which enhances their survival. This process helps lower serum cholesterol by preventing intestinal reabsorption. Their bile tolerance allows for better gut colonization, contributing to a healthier digestive system52. The robust bile tolerance exhibited by L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 points directly to its sophisticated dynamic membrane remodeling capabilities. Bile salts act as potent biological detergents that can violently disrupt bacterial lipid bilayers, inducing intracellular oxidative stress and severe DNA damage51. The ability of this strain to maintain a highly viable population at 0.6% bile concentration (triple the standard physiological level) suggests the rapid upregulation of multidrug-resistance efflux pumps and the active alteration of its membrane fatty acid profile. By increasing the ratio of saturated to unsaturated fatty acids, the bacterial cell effectively decreases membrane fluidity, thereby preventing the intracellular accumulation of toxic bile conjugates and ensuring structural integrity throughout the duodenal transit54.

Acid tolerance

Initial tolerance to the low acidic pH of the stomach is a critical criterion for the survival of probiotics. Accordingly, in-vitro studies must confirm the ability of microorganisms to resist a stomach pH of approximately 1.5 for an estimated duration of 90 min44. Based on the results from the present study (Fig. 2), the viability of the L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain significantly decreased after three h of incubation at various pH levels (2.5, 3.5, 5, and 7) (p < 0.05). As the pH was lowered from 7 to 5, the cell count dropped from the control group to 8.08 log CFU/mL, representing a reduction of 1.221 log units. This decline in viability intensified with further increases in acidity, with the cell count reaching 6.18 log CFU/mL at pH 3.5 and 4.04 log CFU/mL at pH 2.5, respectively. The survival of TMPC 41127 under the extremely harsh acidic condition of pH 2.5 requires highly coordinated intracellular pH homeostasis. In such extreme environments, passive proton influx rapidly acidifies the cytoplasm, potentially denaturing essential metabolic enzymes. To counteract this, acid-tolerant lactobacilli heavily rely on the multi-subunit F0F1-ATPase system, which actively extrudes internal protons at the expense of ATP to maintain a sustainable transmembrane ΔpH (Papadimitriou et al., 2016). Additionally, the induction of general stress proteins, such as molecular chaperones GroEL and DnaK, likely plays a vital role in preventing the irreversible aggregation of acid-denatured structural proteins. Consequently, the observed resilience of this strain is not a passive trait but a highly energy-dependent physiological adaptation that guarantees a sufficient therapeutic threshold (typically > 106CFU/g) reaches the lower intestinal tract metabolically active. Studies such as those by Barzegar et al.27, on strains isolated from traditional Iranian cheeses and Castorena-Alba et al., and44 on strains isolated from food products, have reported favorable resistance to low pH, showing only a minor decrease in viability. While some studies, like Marcia et al., (2023), emphasize the high sensitivity of L. acidophilus strains and a rapid decline in their viability when exposed to highly acidic environments (pH < 3.0), our investigated strain, TMPC 41127, demonstrates uniquely distinct resistance. This strain maintains a cell count of over 104 CFU/mL even after 3 h of exposure to pH 2.5. This remarkable stability suggests that TMPC 41127 possesses potent intrinsic defense mechanisms (potentially including efficient intracellular pH homeostasis) ensuring that an effective functional dose successfully traverses the harsh gastric barrier to reach the intestine.

Tolerance to simulated gastric juice

Evaluation of bacterial resistance to simulated gastric and intestinal juices is considered a primary method for assessing their survival and growth under the harsh conditions of the gastrointestinal tract (GIT). As shown in Fig. 4, the studied strain maintained its viability during exposure to simulated gastric and intestinal fluids. The initial count of the strain was 8.36 log CFU/mL, which decreased to 7.29 log CFU/mL after one hour of exposure to simulated gastric juice, representing a reduction of 1.07 logarithmic cycles. Despite this, the strain demonstrated acceptable resistance to simulated intestinal juice, and the count of viable cells at the end of the experiment (3 h) was 5.49 log CFU/mL, having decreased by only 0.47 logarithmic cycles. The successful transit of TMPC 41127 through the sequential and compounding stressors of simulated gastrointestinal fluids definitively bridges the gap between in vitro survival and in vivo clinical efficacy. The minimal logarithmic reduction observed during exposure to simulated intestinal juice (which contains potent proteolytic enzymes like pancreatin alongside bile) underscores the crucial defensive barrier provided by the strain’s surface biochemistry. It is highly probable that the robust secretion of exopolysaccharides (EPS) forms an impermeable, mucosal-like microcapsule around the bacterial cells, effectively sequestering digestive proteases and neutralizing hostile ionic gradients55. This intrinsic capacity for self-encapsulation ensures that a critically high dosage of live cells bypasses the pyloric barrier intact, ready to seamlessly initiate epithelial colonization and exert immunoregulatory postbiotic effects in the colonic luminal matrix. In line with the present findings, Vasiee et al., (2020)showed that LAB strains have varying resistance to challenging GIT environments. Their viability in acidic gastric juice decreased by 1 to 2.5 logarithmic cycles, whereas they showed acceptable resistance in simulated intestinal juice, with a population reduction of only 0.15 to 0.49 logarithmic cycles. Furthermore, research by Su et al.53,, Farid et al.32,, and Zhang et al., (2022) also reported significant survival for L. acidophilus strains upon exposure to simulated GIT conditions. Overall, Gastric and intestinal juices are vital for digestion, utilizing complementary acidic and alkaline environments to break down food. While the extreme acidity of gastric juice is essential for eliminating pathogens, it can also have lethal effects on beneficial bacteria. Compounds such as polysaccharides can provide a protective effect, contributing to the survival of probiotic bacteria (Vasiee et al., 2020).

Adhesion and aggregation properties

Surface hydrophobicity

Surface hydrophobicity is considered a criterion for assessing the physical interactions between microorganisms and biological surfaces. This property reflects the ability of probiotic cells to adhere to fatty surfaces, such as host cell membranes, which is essential for their persistence and for exerting beneficial effects within the body (Kardooni et al., 2024). In this regard, bacterial strains are classified into three categories based on their level of surface hydrophobicity: highly hydrophobic (> 50%), moderately hydrophobic (20 − 50%), and hydrophilic (< 20%) (Bentahar et al., 2024). Based on this classification, the strain under investigation demonstrated a high surface hydrophobicity of 58.9 ± 1.56% (Fig. 4), placing it in the highly hydrophobic category. From a physicochemical perspective, the exceptionally high surface hydrophobicity of TMPC 41127 (58.9%) is fundamentally orchestrated by the complex macromolecular topography of its cell envelope, notably characterized by an abundance of surface-layer proteins (SlpA) and lipoteichoic acids. This thermodynamically driven, non-specific affinity for non-polar biological surfaces is the initial and most critical catalyst for in vivo colonization. By effectively overcoming the electrostatic repulsion forces naturally present at the intestinal mucosa interface, the strain can robustly anchor itself to the highly glycosylated mucin layer. This strong physical adhesion serves a pivotal clinical purpose: it provides a steadfast structural scaffold for persistent biofilm architecture (shielding the live bacterial colony from the mechanical shear stress of peristalsis) and establishes an intimate micro-niche essential for sustained immunomodulatory cross-talk with the underlying host epithelia56,57. Probiotics with high hydrophobicity (> 40%) are prioritized due to their superior ability to adhere to host cells, which is a vital prerequisite for their beneficial effects. Our findings are consistent with previous studies that have reported high hydrophobicity levels for L. acidophilus strains (32; Nami et al., 2015). For instance, Escobar-Sánchez et al., (2022) reported hydrophobicity levels for L. acidophilus strains ranging from 51.82% to 91.5%, while (23 recorded a surface hydrophobicity of 50.49% for the Lb. acidophilus ABC240 strain.

Auto-aggregation and co-aggregation

Probiotic strains play a vital role in preventing the colonization of pathogens on the intestinal surface through auto-aggregation and co-aggregation. This function is based on the principle that bacteria often exist as a consortium, acting by adhering to surfaces, non-bacterial cells, or other bacteria (32; Nami et al., 2018). As shown in Fig. 4, the L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain under investigation demonstrated a significant auto-aggregation ability of 47.5 ± 1.24% and co-aggregation of 53.1 ± 1.20%. The high capacity for self-aggregation and co-aggregation of the TMPC 41127 strain indicates its advanced structural and thermodynamic readiness for continuous biofilm formation. Auto-aggregation is a critical prerequisite for the establishment of a robust, three-dimensional probiotic biofilm on the intestinal mucosa. This self-assembling architecture, primarily driven by cellular surface hydrophobicity and exopolysaccharide (EPS) secretion, significantly maximizes the strain’s resilience against harsh luminal stressors, such as bile toxicity and proteolytic enzymes58. Concurrently, the exceptionally high co-aggregation rate with pathogen transforms this trait into an active “seek-and-destroy” bio-mechanism. By directly binding to the outer membrane of pathogenic specie, TMPC 41127 not only competitively limits their access to mucosal nutrients but also ensures that strictly localized gradients of its defense metabolites (namely organic acids, hydrogen peroxide, and specific bacteriocins) are delivered directly to the pathogen’s surface at lethal concentrations, thereby preventing pathogen cross-feeding and systemic dissemination59. Numerous studies have investigated these characteristics in various strains of L. acidophilus. Our TMPC 41127 strain demonstrates a significant functional advantage compared to several recently isolated probiotic strains. For instance, TMPC 41127 performs notably better than the L. acidophilus B103 strain, which, according to Kardooni et al., (2024),achieved only 42.38% auto-aggregation and 34.48% co-aggregation, values that are considerably lower. Furthermore, Farid et al.32, reported moderate aggregation levels among L. acidophilus strains ranging from 21.72% to 30.73%. In contrast, Escobar-Sánchez et al., (2022) recorded an 87% auto-aggregation capacity and over 74% co-aggregation with the pathogen L. monocytogenes 1639 for the L. acidophilus NCFM strain. These findings indicate that the antagonistic properties of probiotic strains are closely linked to their ability to form co-aggregates with pathogens. These co-aggregates create a physical barrier, which prevents pathogens from adhering to the intestinal epithelium, while also providing a localized microenvironment for the production of antimicrobial compounds and the ultimate elimination of the pathogens (Escobar-Sánchez et al., 2022).

Adhesion to Caco-2 cells

Adhesion and colonization on the intestinal surface are recognized as essential factors for the beneficial effects of probiotic strains in the gastrointestinal tract (Nami et al., 2018). This process is facilitated by a set of bacterial surface molecules and cellular structures. These factors include extracellular protein adhesins, lipoteichoic acid, and other protein factors attached to the bacterial cell surface, as well as the passive trapping of cells by fimbrial matrix materials (Nami et al., 2015). In the present study, the adhesion potential of the strain to Caco-2 cells was evaluated at 13.9%, as shown in Fig. 4. From a physiological and clinical perspective, an adherence rate is phenomenally significant in the highly dynamic and shear-stressed environment of the human gastrointestinal tract. The successful attachment of L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 to the intestinal epithelium serves as the critical first step in establishing a “competitive exclusion” mechanism. By robustly binding to the specific receptor sites on the enterocytes (likely mediated by surface-layer proteins (SlpA) and mucin-binding proteins) this strain effectively blocks the attachment points for enteric enteropathogens through steric hindrance60. Beyond mere physical occupation, this strong adhesion facilitates intense and sustained molecular crosstalk between the bacterial cells and the Gut-Associated Lymphoid Tissue (GALT). This intimate host-microbe interaction is essential for fortifying the mucosal barrier integrity, upregulating tight junction proteins (such as Zonula Occludens-1), and triggering localized immunomodulatory responses61. Therefore, the substantial Caco-2 adhesion potential of TMPC 41127 not only guarantees its persistence but also underpins its prophylactic efficacy in maintaining intestinal homeostasis. While various L. acidophilusstrains typically exhibit a binding range between 11% at 14% based on the findings of Barzegar et al.27,, our TMPC 41127 strain positions itself at the upper end of this spectrum, demonstrating a prominent adhesion characteristic. This high attachment propensity is considered an essential prerequisite for effective gastrointestinal colonization and competitive exclusion of pathogenic agents. For instance, Kardooni et al., (2024) reported an adhesion capacity of 11.85% for the L. acidophilus B103 strain, which is notably lower than that of our investigated strain. Therefore, the substantial Caco-2 adhesion potential of TMPC 41127 not only guarantees its persistence but also underpins its prophylactic efficacy in maintaining intestinal homeostasis.

Antimicrobial activity

The antimicrobial activity of the CFS from the L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain was evaluated against various pathogenic bacteria (Fig. 5 (A, B and C). Results from the DDA method indicated a significant difference in the response of bacterial strains to the antimicrobial agent (CFS) (Fig. 5A; p < 0.05). Among Gram-positive strains, Listeria monocytogenes (IZ: 16.22 ± 0.19 mm; Fig. 6. A1) showed the highest sensitivity to the antimicrobial agent; while Bacillus cereus (IZ: 15.37 ± 0.24 mm) and Staphylococcus aureus (IZ: 14.81 ± 0.13 mm) exhibited lower sensitivity. In contrast, among Gram-negative bacteria, Salmonella Typhi, with an average IZ diameter of 13.28 ± 0.19 mm (Fig. 6. B1), demonstrated the least sensitivity and the highest relative resistance compared to Escherichia coli (IZ: 13.42 ± 0.17 mm) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (IZ: 13.7 ± 0.2 mm). These findings clearly indicate inherent differences in the defense mechanisms and molecular susceptibility of various strains to the CFS used. In the WDA method, the overall trend of strain sensitivity was similar to the DDA method, but with a greater intensity of effect (p < 0.05). In this method, L. monocytogenes (IZ: 18.64 ± 0.11 mm) showed the highest sensitivity (Fig. 6. A2). Conversely, Salmonella Typhi (IZ: 14.03 ± 0.22 mm; Fig. 6. B2) exhibited the lowest level of inhibition. The observed difference in the inhibitory potency of the strains in this method is likely due to the increased surface contact and longer interaction time between the antimicrobial agents and bacteria in this assay, which generally leads to enhanced inhibitory activity. While the results of the Modified double layer showed that the CFS of L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 had the greatest inhibitory effect on B. cereus (IZ:19.8 ± 0.66 mm) and the least effect on Salmonella Typhi (IZ:14.2 ± 0.41 mm) (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, MIC test results confirmed that Gram-positive bacteria were more sensitive than their Gram-negative counterparts, suggesting a higher efficacy of the antimicrobial compounds against this group of pathogens. The antimicrobial activity of the tested strain’s CFS is attributed to a combined and synergistic action of a wide range of antimicrobial metabolites, particularly organic acids such as lactic and acetic acid. These compounds reduce the environmental pH and interfere with metabolic functions, creating an unfavorable environment for the growth and survival of pathogens (Hussain et al., 2022). Bacteriocins are also effective against Gram-positive pathogens, while organic acids and hydrogen peroxide demonstrate stronger activity against Gram-negative bacteria, a distinction linked to their different cell wall structures23,34. The significant antagonistic activity observed against Gram-positive pathogens aligns with previous reports for the L. acidophilus CM1 strain (Khushboo, 2023) regarding activity against strains such as Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus cereus, and Enterococcus faecalis, as well as the findings of Soltani et al., (2022) on the strong inhibitory effect of L. acidophilus strains on Gram-positive bacteria. Evidence suggests that the mechanisms involved in inhibiting Gram-positive bacteria, including the reduction of environmental pH due to the production of organic acids and/or the presence of specific bacteriocins, also function effectively in this strain. Furthermore, these findings demonstrate the generalizability of the antimicrobial effects observed across different L. acidophilus strains.

Inhibition of formation and degradation of mature biofilms

Biofilm formation, as a pivotal virulence factor, enables pathogens to establish and colonize. These structures, particularly in pathogenic species, act as a reservoir for persistent infections and play a key role in the onset of chronic diseases and the intensification of antimicrobial resistance62. The ability of probiotics to reduce Salmonella Typhi biofilms is therefore of significant importance as a key characteristic for therapeutic applications following a Salmonella Typhi infection. Consequently, the anti-biofilm effect of CFS from an L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain against Salmonella Typhi was evaluated in this study. The results demonstrated (Fig. 7) that the CFS from the L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain can significantly inhibit the formation of Salmonella Typhi biofilms. All tested concentrations showed a notable reduction in the rate of biofilm formation. This effect was observed in a dose-dependent manner; at the lowest concentration (1/4×MIC), the rate of biofilm formation was reduced to 79.52%, indicating a substantial initial inhibition compared to the control group (100%). As the concentration of CFS increased, the inhibitory effect was intensified, reaching 13.16% at the highest concentration (4×MIC). These findings emphasize the high capability of the CFS from the L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain to prevent initial bacterial adherence and inhibit biofilm formation. Furthermore, the CFS was also able to significantly (p < 0.05) degrade mature Salmonella Typhi biofilms in a concentration-dependent manner. This degradative effect exhibited a pattern similar to the inhibition of the initial phase of biofilm formation by the same substance. These findings demonstrate the capability of the CFS to combat the biofilm life cycle, both in the prevention stage and in the elimination of developed structures. Probiotics exert anti-biofilm effects through multiple mechanisms: production of antimicrobial compounds, competition with pathogens, modulation of gene expression, and damage to cell membranes. These synergistic activities lead to biofilm degradation23. Previous research has extensively emphasized the efficacy of CFS as an effective agent against biofilms of foodborne pathogens. For instance, a study by Siddique et al.63, demonstrated that CFS derived from various strains of LAB exhibited a significant ability to inhibit the biofilm formation of SalmonellaTyphi, increasing this inhibition by up to 84% compared to the control group. This finding indicates the high potential of CFS in preventing the adhesion and growth of this pathogen on surfaces. Furthermore, research by Kadhom et al.64, also reported the capability of CFS obtained from a specific strain of L. plantarum in inhibiting Salmonella Typhi biofilm using the microplate assay method. This study also highlighted the effective role of compounds present in CFS in disrupting biofilm formation processes. Additionally, the research by Soltani et al., (2022) proved the efficacy of CFS from L. acidophilus and L. casei strains in inhibiting the biofilm of E. coli bacteria. These findings collectively highlight the significance and high potential of probiotic-based therapeutic approaches, particularly the use of CFS, as an effective and natural strategy for combating and inhibiting pathogen biofilms, offering a promising outlook for the development of antimicrobial food and therapeutic products.

Anti-infection

Bacterial pathogens adhere to host intestinal epithelial cells via adhesion molecules to cause infection. This key virulence factor enables colonization and toxin release. In contrast, probiotic strains are protective agents, reducing pathogen adhesion through multiple mechanisms (Kardooni et al., 2024). In this study, the anti-infective effects of a L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 probiotic strain against the pathogen Salmonella Typhi were investigated. The results from the anti-infection assays showed that this strain has a remarkable ability to reduce pathogen adhesion; the competition effect led to a 53.5%±1.42 reduction, the displacement effect resulted in a 49.44%±1.33 reduction, and the inhibition effect caused a 30.38%±1.43 reduction in pathogen attachment (Fig. 8). The Significant effectiveness of L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 in the competition assay underscores a highly effective “competitive exclusion” mechanism. This phenomenon is primarily driven by steric hindrance, where the prolific expression of mucin-binding proteins and surface-layer proteins (SlpA) by the probiotic rapidly masks the specific host cellular receptors, effectively blinding the pathogen to its target. Conversely, the significant displacement and inhibition activities suggest a dynamic biochemical antagonism. The localized secretion of organic acids, biosurfactants, and putative bacteriocins actively degrades the pathogen’s primary adhesins (such as Type 1 fimbriae) and alters the micro-interfacial tension. This multifaceted anti-adhesive strategy ensures that Salmonella Typhi is strictly physically outcompeted and biochemically neutralized, allowing for its rapid clearance by intestinal peristalsis before establishing a pathogenic foothold65. While previous studies have established the general anti-adhesive capacity of lactobacilli, comparing distinct functional profiles reveals the unique therapeutic potential of L. acidophilus TMPC 41127. For instance, the study by Kardooni et al., (2024), using L. acidophilus B103 against E. coli, recorded a high competitive rate (52.13%) but demonstrated a weaker performance (25.20%) in pathogen displacement compared to our strain. Similarly, Saboktakin-Rizi et al.14, reported that their investigated probiotic primarily countered Salmonella Typhi through competition (50.19%) and inhibition (48.87%), lacking active displacement capabilities. In contrast, the prominent finding of our study is the remarkable efficacy of the TMPC 41127 strain in pathogen displacement (49.44%), which significantly distinguishes it. This highlights that beyond merely preventing initial attachment (prophylactic effect), our strain possesses a superior ability to actively dislodge and clear already-established pathogen populations through the aforementioned robust biochemical antagonism. This active displacement capability provides a more comprehensive anti-infective profile, positioning our strain as a potentially stronger candidate for mitigating early-stage colonization.

Assessment of gene expression

In this study, the effect of CFS from an L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain on the expression of Salmonella Typhi virulence genes was evaluated. Our results showed (Fig. 9), that the CFS of this strain effectively reduced the expression of biofilm-related genes, including csgD, csgA, and bcsA, to 0.31, 0.36, and 0.44 of the control level, respectively. Furthermore, the expression of the invA and invC genes, which are responsible for host cell invasion, was also reduced to 0.48 and 0.47 of the control level, respectively. The targeted downregulation of csgA, csgD, and bcsA transcripts highlights a potent molecular interference deeply affecting the pathogen’s biofilm architecture. Since csgD acts as the master transcriptional regulator for curli fimbriae (csgA) and cellulose production (bcsA), its suppression dictates a systemic collapse of Salmonella Typhi extracellular polymeric matrix, rendering the pathogen hyper-susceptible to host immune responses. Furthermore, the critical repression of invA and invC (core structural components of the Salmonella Typhi Pathogenicity Island 1 (SPI-1) Type III Secretion System (T3SS) reveals a sophisticated Quorum Quenching (QQ) capability. It is highly probable that bioactive peptides and SCFAs within the CFS disrupt the autoinducer-mediated inter-cellular communication of the pathogen. By functionally “disarming” the T3SS apparatus, the probiotic categorically abolishes Salmonella Typhi capacity to inject effector proteins into host enterocytes, effectively halting intracellular invasion66. These findings indicate that the metabolites present in the CFS are capable of attenuating SalmonellaTyphi pathogenicity by suppressing genes related to biofilm formation and cellular invasion. These results are consistent with the findings of Kiousi et al.37,, who reported that the CFS of Lp. pentosus L33 and Lp. plantarum L125 caused a significant reduction in the expression of the csgA gene in S. enterica. However, significant differences were observed in the results; for example, in their study, the expression of the csgD and cpxR genes was upregulated, whereas in our research, csgD expression was downregulated. This discrepancy could be due to differences in the probiotic bacterial strains, pathogen, and experimental conditions.

SEM

SEM observations revealed that Salmonella Typhi cells prior to treatment (Fig. 10A) exhibited normal structure and morphology with a smooth surface, free from any apparent damage. In contrast, treatment with CFS from L. acidophilus strain TMPC 41127 induced significant morphological alterations in the bacterial outer structure (Fig. 10B). The treated cells displayed wrinkled and uneven surfaces with prominent depressions, indicating clear destruction of the outer membrane and a loss of cellular integrity. These structural damages can lead to the leakage of intracellular contents and, ultimately, the demise of the cell. The cell membrane, which is vital for maintaining cellular homeostasis, often serves as a primary target for antibacterial agents; by disrupting membrane integrity, these substances cause the leakage of intracellular contents and the death of pathogens (Geng et al., 2022). Furthermore, the lethal structural deformities observed via SEM provide visual confirmation of a profound “dual-hit” membrane-targeting mechanism. Gram-negative pathogens like Salmonella Typhi rely heavily on a formidable outer membrane fortified by lipopolysaccharides (LPS). However, the undissociated organic acids in the CFS (particularly lactic acid) function as potent permeabilizers. They readily transverse this lipid bilayer and, upon entering the neutral cytoplasm, dissociate to trigger a rapid intracellular acidification. This internal crisis systematically dissipates the transmembrane proton motive force (PMF). Concurrently, the integration of positively charged probiotic bacteriocins into the negatively charged pathogenic envelope induces severe pore formation. This abrupt loss of osmotic turgor pressure inevitably leads to the catastrophic expulsion of essential cytoplasmic constituents (including ATP and nucleic acids) culminating in the irreversible cellular wrinkling, extensive lysis, and definitive eradication of the pathogen67. While previous research has broadly established the antimicrobial activity of LAB CFS against various pathogens (Saidi et al., 2022;23), our ultrastructural findings offer a more comprehensive mechanistic insight. For instance, Geng et al., (2023) reported that a synbiotic formulation derived from the L. paracasei VL8 strain degraded the Salmonella Typhi cell membrane, leading to its fragmentation and cavitation. In contrast, our study indicates that the CFS of the TMPC 41127 strain alone is capable of causing physical disruption of the Salmonella Typhi cell membrane. In other studies, Saidi et al., (2022) documented the efficacy of CFS from Lactobacillus casei and Lactobacillus rhamnosus against Staphylococcus aureus.

Antioxidant activities and bioactive compounds

DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging assay

Antioxidants protect cellular structures from destructive damage caused by reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative stress68. Their mechanism of action is based on their ability to donate an electron or a proton to unstable radicals (such as DPPH and ABTS). This process leads to the reduction of free radicals into inactive and stable molecular forms, which results in the formation of stable complexes32. This mechanism forms the basis for antioxidant activity assays in laboratory studies. The CFS of L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 exhibited significant antioxidant activity. As observed in Table 5, this supernatant demonstrated a DPPH radical scavenging activity of 56.05 ± 1.05%. This level is considerably higher than the antioxidant activity reported by Aleman et al.69, for the CFS of L. acidophilusLA K, which was 41.76%. However, the research by Farid et al.32,, focusing on indigenous L. acidophilus WFA1 strains isolated from traditional dairy products, recorded a much higher DPPH activity of 80.66%. These substantial differences in antioxidant activity levels clearly indicate the remarkable variation among strains in their capability to produce bioactive secondary metabolites, including bioactive peptides and exopolysaccharides, across the broad spectrum of the L. acidophilus species. Furthermore, the inhibition of ABTS (68.45 ± 0.97%) and linoleic acid peroxidation (43.55 ± 1.27%) indicates that the L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain can neutralize oxidative stress in both hydrophilic and lipophilic cellular compartments. Similarly, numerous studies have evaluated the ability of the ABTS cation radical and linoleic acid peroxidation as metrics for assessing the reducing power of CFS of LAB, confirming significant antioxidant potential in these microorganisms23,70. In line with this, Farid et al.32, reported higher antioxidant activity in the L. acidophilusWFA1 strain, with an ABTS radical scavenging activity of 81.97%. Additionally, a study by Bharti et al.71, also demonstrated remarkable antioxidant activity for an L. acidophilus strain, with the intensity correlated to the concentration of the CFS. Lactobacilli, which are often used in dietary supplements, are known for their intrinsic antioxidant properties. By increasing endogenous antioxidant enzyme activity and modulating systemic oxidative stress, these probiotic strains protect against carcinogen-induced cellular damage72. Therefore, these findings indicate that the CFS of L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 exhibits high antioxidant activity. These antioxidant properties of the CFS make the probiotic strains important potential agents for combating oxidative stress and promoting cellular health.

TPC and TFC

TPC and TFC of the CFS from a L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain were determined using a spectrophotometer at wavelengths of 760 and 510 nm, respectively. The results of these assays are presented in Table 5. The TPC and TFC values were 113.53 ± 0.95 mg GAE/g and 19.54 ± 0.79 mg QE/g, respectively. The substantial accumulation of phenolic and flavonoid compounds in the CFS of L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 is a direct result of the bacterial strain’s intrinsic metabolic activity, specifically its enzymatic machinery. It is widely documented that LAB strains possess metabolic enzymes, such as esterases, decarboxylases, and reductases, which actively synthesize or biotransform precursor molecules into the phenolic and flavonoid compounds detected during fermentation73,74. The elevated TPC and TFC values observed in our study strongly suggest that strain TMPC 41127 is highly efficient in this bioconversion process. Furthermore, the correlation between high phenolic content and enhanced biological activities is rooted in their structural chemistry. The abundance of hydroxyl groups in flavonoids and phenolic acids serves as excellent hydrogen-donating antioxidants, effectively scavenging free radicals and mitigating oxidative stress55. The results of this study demonstrated a notable concordance with the values reported by Kaynakci et al., (2025), who documented TPC of 90.89 mg GAE/g and TFC of 21.34 mg QE/g for CFS derived from probiotic strains. Furthermore, Jalali et al.75, attributed the high phenolic content in the CFS of Lactobacillus rhamnosus and Limosilactobacillus reuterito bioactive compounds that effectively neutralize free radicals, indicating their antioxidant potential. Pristavu76, stated that phenolic acids, due to their dual function, act as potent antioxidants and agents with antimicrobial activity. Overall, the results of this study show that the CFS of the L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain has significant potential as a rich source of phenolic compounds for health promotion due to its antioxidant and antimicrobial properties.

Cholesterol assimilation

Cholesterol is a vital molecule that plays an important role in building healthy cells; however, excessive amounts can increase the risk of serious diseases such as heart disease, stroke, and pancreatitis. Therefore, aggressive management of high cholesterol levels, especially LDL cholesterol, is essential. In such cases, moderate-to-high-intensity statin therapy is usually prescribed (Momin et al., 2023). In line with this, numerous studies have shown that some probiotic strains have the ability to assimilate cholesterol from the culture medium through various mechanisms, which suggests their potential to help control plasma cholesterol upon consumption44. In this study, the cholesterol reduction potential of probiotic bacterial strains was evaluated under in vitro conditions, and the results showed (Table 5) that the L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain effectively led to a cholesterol reduction, with the rate reaching 54.62 ± 1.46%. prominent potential as a cardiovascular-protective biotherapeutic. Mechanistically, the in vitro removal of cholesterol by LAB is a multifactorial process. The primary biological driver is naturally the secretion of the bile salt hydrolase (BSH) enzyme. BSH catalyzes the deconjugation of bile acids, rendering them less soluble. These deconjugated bile acids then co-precipitate with unbound cholesterol in the matrix, effectively removing it from the gastrointestinal absorption cycle and facilitating its excretion in feces77,78. Furthermore, during the logarithmic growth phase, active L. acidophilus cells can directly assimilate exogenous cholesterol into their cellular lipid bilayer to modulate membrane fluidity, or physically bind the cholesterol molecules to the peptidoglycan network of their cell walls79. While the general cholesterol-lowering capacity of LAB has been widely documented36,56,80, our strain demonstrates a noticeably superior efficiency when compared to recently reported isolates. For instance, while Kardooni et al., (2024) investigated another L. acidophilus strain (B103), it only achieved a maximum reduction rate of 46.27%±0.66. Similarly, the findings of Song et al., (2015) indicate that the L. acidophilus KU41, L. acidophilus NS1, L. acidophilus M23, L. fermentum NS2, L. plantarum M13, and L. plantarum NS3 strains were able to effectively reduce cholesterol levels by more than 50% under in vitro conditions. Given the well-documented adverse effects of long-term statin therapy, such as hepatotoxicity and statin-associated muscle symptoms, the administration of highly efficient, BSH-active probiotic strains like TMPC 41127 offers a promising, safe, and natural adjunctive strategy for hypercholesterolemia management. Future in vivo studies elucidating the lipid-lowering pharmacokinetics and gut microbiome modulation by this specific strain will be pivotal in validating its efficacy as a therapeutic functional food ingredient or a Live Biotherapeutic Product (LBP).

Safety assessment

Antibiotic resistance

Antibiotic resistance in probiotic strains is a complex and significant issue, particularly concerning their efficacy and safety. Based on the results obtained (Table 6; Fig. 11), the L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain showed varying sensitivities to different antibiotics. It was sensitive to commonly used antibiotics such as chloramphenicol, nitrofurfurane, erythromycin, and tetracycline, indicating that these antibiotics effectively inhibit its growth. Conversely, it showed intermediate resistance to trimethoprim sulfamethoxazole, imipenem, and penicillin. A moderate resistance to some antibiotics in probiotics can be considered a physiological advantage, as it allows them to survive and exert their beneficial effects during antibiotic therapy52. However, this hypothesis requires further investigation and confirmation. Additionally, the results indicated that this strain is resistant to amikacin and gentamicin. These findings are crucial for evaluating the safety and selecting this strain as a potential probiotic, as they suggest that resistance to certain antibiotics can be an advantage in specific therapeutic environments.

Hemolytic assay

Hemolytic activity is a critical safety criterion for selecting probiotic strains of LAB to ensure their safe use81. In the current study, the hemolytic activity of the L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain was evaluated by culturing it on blood agar plates. The results showed no hemolysis halo was observed around the colonies, indicating a negative hemolysis result and classifying the strain as non-hemolytic (gamma-hemolytic). The absence of hemolytic activity (γ-hemolysis) in L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 is a fundamental prerequisite for its clinical application. Hemolysins are classified as potent virulence factors that facilitate systemic dissemination by lysing host red blood cells and degrading the epithelial barrier, thereby leading to opportunistic infections82. By demonstrating absolute γ-hemolytic behavior, strain TMPC 41127 strictly fulfills the safety guidelines established by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and thoroughly satisfies the criteria for Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS) status. This phenotypic safety trait ensures that even in immunocompromised hosts or individuals with increased intestinal permeability (leaky gut syndrome), the continuous administration of this strain will not trigger hemolysin-mediated pathogenicity or mucosal toxicity. This finding is consistent with recent reports; for example, Qi et al.2, found that the L. acidophilusLA85 strain did not form any hemolysis halo on Columbia blood agar medium, and its hemolytic activity was reported as negative. Similarly, Hussein et al.83, confirmed that the L. acidophilus strain lacked hemolytic activity and was therefore identified as a gamma-hemolytic strain.

DNase activity

The absence of any DNase activity in the Lacticaseibacillus acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain, together with its lack of hemolytic activity, plays a crucial role in confirming the non-pathogenic, tissue-compatible, and safe nature of this bacterium. In clinical microbiology, DNase is widely recognized as a critical exoenzyme utilized by invasive pathogens to degrade the host’s extracellular DNA, damage mucosal tissue integrity, and actively evade localized immune responses particularly by dismantling Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs)84. Thus, a DNase-positive profile poses a severe risk of exacerbating intestinal inflammation. DNase activity is also a critical safety criterion for LABs and must be precisely examined in their pre-use safety assessments. In this study, it was confirmed that the L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain lacked DNase activity. This finding is consistent with reports by Kardooni et al.85, for the L. acidophilus B103 strain and Khan et al., and86 for the L. acidophilus QAULAN51 strain, which have confirmed similar results. The results of extensive studies show that LABs are generally devoid of this activity, and their safety has been confirmed in this regard23,27. Overall, the DNase-negative phenotype in TMPC 41127 provides strong evidence of its structural stability, the absence of tissue-damaging mechanisms, and the strain’s ability to therapeutically colonize the gut without disrupting epithelial homeostasis.

Biogenic amine synthesis

Biogenic amines (BAs) are nitrogenous organic compounds produced by microorganisms, particularly pathogenic bacteria and LABs, during fermentation. Due to the safety concerns they create in food products, they are of special importance (23; Özogul et al., 2018). The results of this study confirmed that the L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain was unable to produce biogenic amines. This finding is consistent with previous reports by Alizadeh Behbahani et al.23,, who also confirmed the inability of the L. acidophilus ABC240 strain to produce BAs. The strict inability of L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 to synthesize biogenic amines represents a highly favorable biochemical attribute for both the food and pharmaceutical industries. Bas (such as histamine, tyramine, and putrescine) are generated through the enzymatic decarboxylation of free amino acids. The accumulation of these vasoactive and psychoactive nitrogenous compounds in fermented matrices can trigger severe toxicological responses upon ingestion, including hypertensive crises, intense migraines, and pseudo-allergic reactions (Alvarez-MA et al., 2014). Therefore, the absence of amino-acid decarboxylase genes or their phenotypic expression in TMPC 41127 not only entirely eliminates the risk of amine intoxication but also prevents the potential downstream formation of carcinogenic nitrosamines in the gastric environment. This biochemical purity highlights the strain’s exceptional suitability as a safe, risk-free starter culture for developing hypoallergenic functional foods.

Cytotoxicity assay

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is recognized as a major cause of global mortality. Since traditional treatments are sometimes ineffective, new therapeutic approaches, including the use of probiotics, have garnered increasing attention. These bacteria, by producing bioactive compounds such as anticarcinogenic, anti-inflammatory, and antimutagenic agents, can act as a complementary treatment method and help improve human health (Abedi et al., 2024). In line with this, the cell proliferation inhibitory capacity of the CFS of the L acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain was evaluated against the HT-29 (Iranian Biological Research Center (IBRC) No. C10097), HeLa (IBRC No. C11311), and MCF-7 (IBRC No. C10682) cell lines. The cell proliferation inhibitory capacity of this extract was determined by assessing the 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50). The results showed that the extract had a cytotoxic effect on all three cell lines, though its efficacy was statistically significant (p < 0.05) and dependent on the cell line type. The lowest IC50 value, approximately 63.6 ± 1.27 mg/mL, was observed for HeLa cells, indicating the extract’s highest cytotoxic potential against this cell line. In contrast, IC50 values for the HT-29 and MCF-7 cell lines were approximately 75.3 ± 1.43 and 79.7 ± 1.53 mg/mL, respectively, suggesting the relative resistance of these cells to the extract’s effects (Fig. 12). The differential cytotoxicity of the L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 CFS (exhibiting higher potency against HeLa cells compared to HT-29 and MCF-7) strongly indicates a cell-type-specific antiproliferative mechanism. Rather than exerting secondary indiscriminate toxicity, the bioactive metabolites within this specific CFS, such as short-chain fatty acids or strain-specific bacteriocins, likely exploit distinct receptor profiles or metabolic vulnerabilities inherent to different malignancies (Chuah et al., 2019). While Baghbani-Arani et al. (2020) reported high sensitivity of HT-29 to another L. acidophilus extract, the distinct IC50 hierarchy observed in our study underscores the highly strain-dependent nature of postbiotic efficacy. This selective cytotoxicity highlights the targeted biotherapeutic potential of TMPC 41127.

Data analysis using heatmap charts

The compound under investigation exhibited significant antimicrobial capabilities across various physiological and stress conditions. In our acid tolerance assays, we observed a clear trend of reduced viability in the presence of acidic environments. Specifically, there was a substantial decrease in survival rates as pH levels diminished, with the most striking impact noted at pH 2.5, where we recorded a drop in colony-forming units (CFUs) from 9.30 log CFU/mL in the control group to just 4.04 log CFU/mL (Fig. 13, a). This indicates that the compound is particularly sensitive to highly acidic conditions, though it may maintain some viability in less extreme acidic environments. In terms of bile salt tolerance, the compound showcased a degree of resilience, maintaining relative stability at concentrations up to 0.4% bile salts (Fig. 13, b). However, as we increased the concentration to 0.6%, there was a marked decline in survivability, highlighting a threshold beyond which the compound struggles against gastrointestinal stressors. This suggests that while the compound can partially endure the harsh conditions simulating the gastrointestinal tract, its efficacy diminishes under increased bile salt concentrations. The compound also demonstrated a strong concentration-dependent effect on biofilm inhibition. In testing, both initial and mature biofilms were significantly repressed when treated with the compound at the MIC and above (Fig, 13, c). Notably, at four times the MIC (4×MIC), mature biofilms showed a drastic reduction in viability to just 11.95% of the control, translating to approximately 88% inhibition. This is a critical finding, as it underscores the compound’s potential to disrupt bacterial persistence mechanisms, which are significant contributors to chronic infections. Additionally, our antibacterial activity assessments, carried out through diffusion assays, indicated a broader spectrum of efficacy against Gram-positive bacteria (Fig. 13, d, e). Particularly, we noted high susceptibility in Bacillus cereus, which registered an inhibition zone of 19.8 mm using the modified double layer method, followed closely by Listeria monocytogenes at 18.64 mm and Staphylococcus aureus at 17.85 mm. In contrast, the inhibition observed against Gram-negative bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Salmonella Typhi was comparatively less robust. The enhanced effectiveness observed with the modified double layer technique suggests that the compound possesses better penetration capabilities and stability within the agar medium, reinforcing its potential antimicrobial properties. In summary, these collective findings highlight the strong inhibitory effects of the compound, particularly against Gram-positive bacteria, alongside concentration-dependent suppression of biofilm formation. Such characteristics indicate promising prospects for the compound as a natural antimicrobial and antibiofilm agent, particularly within food preservation systems, where its application could significantly enhance food safety and extend shelf life. Further research is warranted to explore the specific mechanisms by which this compound acts and its potential integration into food processing practices.

Conclusion

The findings of this study clearly indicate that the indigenous Lactobacillus acidophilus TMPC 41127 strain, isolated from khiki cheese, is a promising and multifunctional probiotic. With high resistance to harsh gastrointestinal conditions and strong adhesive and colonization properties, this strain is well-suited for survival and function within the human gut. Furthermore, this strain possesses remarkable therapeutic capabilities. Its CFS not only exhibits broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity but also effectively degrades pathogenic Salmonella Typhi biofilms and reduces the expression of virulence-related genes. The strain also demonstrates other health-beneficial properties, such as cholesterol reduction and potent antioxidant activity. Given these findings and the confirmed safety of the strain, including its lack of hemolytic and DNase activities, L. acidophilus TMPC 41127 is an excellent candidate for developing new and natural food and pharmaceutical products. However, while these comprehensive in vitro evaluations provide a robust foundational framework, the translational relevance of this strain necessitates further in-depth validation. Therefore, prospective studies must prioritize in vivo experiments using appropriate animal models to rigorously evaluate the strain’s complex colonization dynamics, immunomodulatory effects, and systemic safety within a living host. Ultimately, well-designed human clinical trials will be essential to definitively confirm the physiological efficacy and safety of TMPC 41127 under real-world conditions. Transitioning through these advanced methodological steps will solidify the scientific and commercial value of this indigenous isolate as a next-generation probiotic.
