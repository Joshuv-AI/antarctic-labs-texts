Cleft Lip with or without palate in seven countries with two income levels: a cross-sectional study

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCleft lip with or without palate (CL/P) rank among the most prevalent congenital anomalies globally. This study aimed to report the phenotypic patterns of CL/P across seven Middle Eastern and Asian countries, reflecting two different economic levels.MethodologyA cross-sectional, multicountry-study was carried out in seven Middle Eastern and Asian countries (high income: Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Oman, UAE; lower-middle income: Egypt, Pakistan, Jordan) at major government and university cleft care referral centers. Cleft phenotypes were classified using the LASHAL system, and severity was categorized into seven grades, ranging from unilateral incomplete CL (mild) to bilateral complete CLP (severe).ResultsThere were 836 CL/P patients; out of them, 337(40.3%) were CL, 499(59.7%) were CLP and 160 (19.1%) diagnosed with associated anomalies. A subgroup analysis demonstrated a significant relationship between country economic level and CL/P severity (P < 0.001), with lower-middle-income countries showing higher rates of unilateral-incomplete-CL and high-income countries presenting more bilateral-complete-CLP. Associated anomalies occurred significantly more among unilateral complete CL cases (P = 0.036). Regression analysis found that low-to-moderate income countries significantly decreased the adjusted odds ratio (AOR) of CLP compared to CL phenotypes (AOR: 0.494, P = 0.032), and having mild compared to severe CL/P (AOR = 0.431, P = 0.029).ConclusionThis multicenter study revealed that the socioeconomic level influences both cleft type and severity. Collaborative research focusing on genetic, environmental, and healthcare access factors is recommended to better explain the socioeconomic variations observed and guide targeted prevention and policy strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08689-6.Keywords: Clefts, Prevalence, Demographic, Socioeconomic status, Middle East, Saudi Arabia, Egypt, Pakistan, Jordan, Income

Introduction

Non-syndromic orofacial clefts (NSOFCs), which include cleft lip with or without cleft palate (CL/P), rank among the most prevalent congenital anomalies worldwide. The global prevalence is estimated to be approximately 1 in 700–1,000 live births, with substantial geographic and ethnic variability [1, 2]. A recent meta-analysis reported a global average prevalence of 1.4 per 1,000 live births [3].

A systematic analysis from the Global Burden of Disease (2019) reported that Sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East and North Africa (MENA), and South Asia carry the highest burden of orofacial clefts [4]. In addition, a recent systematic review found that prevalence rates in Arab countries range from 0.3 to 2.6 per 1,000 live births, with a pooled mean of 1.1 per 1,000 [5]. These variations highlight the need for further research exploring the epidemiology and etiology of CL/P, especially in these regions [3, 5].

In Saudi Arabia, the largest country in the Middle East and the second-largest Arabic country by land area, reported NSOFC prevalence rates range from 1.21 to 2.4 per 1,000 live births [6, 7]. However, a recent national population-based prospective study reported a prevalence of 1.26 per 1,000 live births for NSOFC and 0.89 per 1,000 live births for CL/P [8]. In Egypt, the most populous country in the Arab world and the Middle East, the prevalence of CL/P was reported to be 0.4 per 1,000 live births [9]. Additionally, in Pakistan, the incidence of NSOFC was reported to be 1.91 per 1,000 births (one per 523 births), which is higher than the global average [10].

One common feature shared by many countries in Asia, the Middle East, and the Arab region is the relatively high prevalence of parental consanguinity [11]. Several studies have reported an association between parental consanguinity and an increased risk of NSOFC in these regions [12]. In addition, CL/P phenotypes have been reported to be significantly associated with parental consanguinity (P = 0.02) [13]. This makes the region particularly important for understanding the epidemiology and determinants of CL/P.

Moreover, several other risk factors have been associated with CL/P, including maternal exposures to medication, infection, smoking, passive smoking, and environmental stress [14, 15]. However, these factors have rarely been evaluated in relation to the severity of CL/P and are often assessed using subjective tools such as questionnaires.

Previous studies have also reported a relationship between smoking, passive smoking, and national economic levels [16]. Additionally, maternal healthcare access and the availability of prenatal supplementation, such as folic acid, are often influenced by a country’s economic status [17–19]. Furthermore, populations living in countries with lower economic levels may experience higher levels of stress, which has also been suggested as a potential risk factor for CL/P [20–22].

Collectively, these observations highlight regional and economic disparities in NSOFC prevalence, underscoring the need for comparative analyses across countries with differing income levels and population characteristics. In addition, variation in the distribution of CL/P phenotypes and severity across countries may reflect socioeconomic differences [2, 3].

Further, although prevalence has been documented in some countries, there remains insufficient multicenter regional evidence describing phenotypic severity patterns and their relationship with national economic levels [7, 23]. To date, no study has compared high-income and lower-middle-income countries in the Middle East and Asia so as to evaluate whether socioeconomic contexts influence cleft type and severity. Therefore, the aim of this multicountry cross-sectional study was to evaluate the phenotypic distribution and severity of non-syndromic CL/P across seven Middle Eastern and Asian countries representing two economic levels, and to assess the association between economic level and cleft phenotypes and severity.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional, multicenter descriptive study was conducted across seven countries in the Middle East and Asia, namely, Saudi Arabia, Egypt, Jordan, Pakistan, the United Arab Emirates (UAE), Kuwait, and Oman. Participating centers included major government and university hospitals recognized as regional cleft care referral centers. Data collection was from November 1, 2020, to November 1, 2021.

Study population

Eligible cases included children aged 0–18 years presenting with cleft lip (CL), or cleft lip with palate (CLP). A total of 1,100 patients diagnosed with NSOFCs were screened. Syndromic clefts, isolated CP and those associated with known genetic syndromes or chromosomal abnormalities were excluded based on clinical evaluations by pediatricians or geneticists at each participating hospital, in line with diagnostic criteria adapted from Sabbagh et al. [8].

Data collection

Data was collected using a standardized form modeled after the validated tool used in previous Saudi studies [13, 24–26]. Forms captured demographic information (child age, parental education), and family income stratified into low (In-debt), middle (just meet routine expenses or meet routine expenses and emergency) and high (able to save/invest money), through parental interviews conducted in previous studies [20, 27–29]. In all countries included, this information was requested from parents at the time of the child’s delivery. In Pakistan only, the information was gathered retrospectively at the time of the study [2, 19, 25]. Country level of economy was classified as high income level (Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Oman, UAE) and lower-middle income countries (Egypt, Pakistan and Jordan) according to the World Bank country classification [30].

In addition, cleft phenotype was categorized as CL, CP, or CLP, associated anomalies, and family history of clefts. The LASHAL classification system was used to record detailed cleft phenotype information when available [31]. To evaluate the severity of CL/P, we grouped sub-phenotypes into seven categories ranging from mild to severe, as follows: Grade 1 – unilateral incomplete CL; Grade 2 – unilateral complete CL; Grade 3 – bilateral CL; Grade 4 – unilateral CLP; Grade 6 – bilateral incomplete CLP; and Grade 7 – bilateral complete CLP.

All cleft cases and associated anomalies were diagnosed by trained clinical teams including pediatric dentists, maxillofacial surgeons, and pediatricians in the medical centers when the children were born and documented in their files.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using SPSS software (version 25.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic data and cleft distribution. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Associations between cleft phenotype and demographic or clinical variables including income, parental education, and associated anomalies were tested for statistical significance (P < 0.05). Subgroup analyses were performed to assess cleft phenotype distribution by country, family history, and associated anomalies. Generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) were used to determine AORs for CL/P and socioeconomic level adjusting for clustering by country and individual-level covariates (country income level and family history of NSOFC). Binary logistic GLMM was used for a dependent variable comparing CL vs. CLP and Ordinal GLMM was used for CL/P severity.

Results

After excluding isolated CP cases, this study recruited 836 CL/P patients from seven countries. Out of them, 337 (40.3%) were CL, and 499 (59.7%) were CLP. The highest proportion of cases were from high income level countries (618 (56.2%) mainly from Saudi Arabia (462 (55.3%)), with CL at 132 (39.2%), and CLP at 283 (56.7%). High-income families accounted for 205 (24.5%) and low-income families accounted for 318 (38.0%). Parental education was high with 358 (42.8%) for fathers and 368 (44.0%) for mothers, with no statistically significant differences between CL and CLP (P = 0.109 and P = 0.261, respectively).

Associated anomalies were diagnosed in 160 (19.1%) of the total sample. Out of them 64 (7.7%) were because of congenital heart disease. Family history of CL/P accounted for 265 (31.7%) of patients. There were no statistically significant differences between CL and CLP for the frequency of associated anomalies and family history (P = 0.929 and P = 0.301, respectively).

For CL/P severity, ranging from mild (incomplete unilateral CL) and accounting for 212 (25.4%) cases to extremely severe cases (complete bilateral CLP) accounting for 132 (15.8%). The mean severity score was 3.9 ± 2.16 (95% confidence interval for mean: 3.755 to 4.05), and median was 4.00 (Table 1).

Discussion

This study included 836 CL/P patients with NSOFC from seven countries in the Middle East and Asia including Saudi Arabia, Egypt, Jordan, Pakistan, UAE, Kuwait, and Oman. Among them, 337 (40.3%) were CL, and 499 (59.7%) were CLP, suggesting a higher proportion of CLP relative to CL in this cohort. When examining socioeconomic and parental educational factors, no statistically significant differences were found between CL and CLP groups in relation to family income or parental education. Similarly, there were no significant differences between the two groups regarding the frequency of associated anomalies or family history. When looking at the cleft severity patterns, there was a significant relationship between the country’s income level and cleft severity (P < 0.001). Patients from lower-middle-income countries (LMICs), had a significantly reduced likelihood of presenting with CLP vs. CL (AOR = 0.494, P = 0.032) and severe versus mild forms (AOR = 0.431, P = 0.029).

The distribution observed in our study is consistent with the global pattern reported in a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis examining over 17 million individuals [3]. Furthermore, our findings are slightly close to that of Philipp et al. (2023) in Lower Saxony, Germany. In both studies, CLP constitutes the largest proportion of cleft types. In our data, CLP accounted for about 60%, whereas Philipp et al. reported 39.1% CLP. Conversely, CL alone was much higher in our sample 40% compared to 14.4% reported by Philipp et al. Differences in the distributions reported may arise from variability in the classification used, cleft types included, and their inclusion of both syndromic or non-syndromic cases [32]. This is consistent with national trends reported by a recent scoping/systematic overview of Saudi data in 2023, identifying CLP as the most common phenotype [33].

Despite global differences in the prevalence of NSOFC, the overall rates remain significant. Previous research has highlighted several demographic factors associated with OFCs. It is important to note that examining those factors can offer a scientific foundation for future prevention and care plans. SES is considered a contributing factor to the occurrence of OFC and affects access to care [34]. However, in this study selected parameters were investigated including the country’s economic level, family income and parental education, and the highest proportion of cases were from high economic level countries.

It has been reported that the prevalence rate of CL/P in LMICs is approximately 1 in 730 live births, which is higher than what is reported worldwide [35, 36]. Mothers living in LMICs may often face barriers to prenatal care including limited access to folic acid supplementation, which is linked to the development of congenital abnormalities. Additionally, children born in LMICs are exposed to greater environmental and biological risk factors than those wealthier nations, making them vulnerable to developmental problems as well as the occurrence of CL/P [37–40]. Several global studies have documented a link between lower SES and increased risk of OFC [41–43].

In our study, the distribution of cleft phenotypes and sub phenotypes (severity) significantly varied according to the income level of the countries represented. In our data set, the LMICs showed lower odds of both broad phenotype and sub-phenotype compared to high-income countries. Furthermore, unilateral incomplete CL was the most frequent presentation, accounting for approximately 60.8% of cases in LMICs, while bilateral complete CLP was predominant (63.6%) in high-income countries. This is in partial agreement with Gendel et al., where they found CL to be more prevalent in a low SES population. On the contrary, CLP was increased in middle SES while the risk for CPO was more in upper SES [44].

Although low-income families in this study accounted for 318 cases (38.0%), which was more than the high-income families 205 cases (24.5%), the difference between groups showed no statistically significant association with CL/P phenotype or sub-phenotype in our model. This finding suggests that even while SES is often considered an important determinant of health disparities, other variables such as genetic background, maternal health, and environmental exposures must be considered. It is possible that the socioeconomic level interacts with these factors in complex ways, rather than acting as a direct risk determinant [14, 45].

In our study, parental education was high among 358 (42.8%) for fathers and 368 (44.0%) for mothers with no statistically significant differences between CL and CLP (P = 0.109 and P = 0.261, respectively). Our findings further agree with those reported by Ly et al., which indicated that paternal level of education is not associated with an increased risk of orofacial cleft occurrence [46]. In contrast, Auslander et al., found that parental education levels were lower in patients with NSCL ± P, with more mothers and fathers having at least secondary education [47]. A systematic review by Ichingolo et al., identified socio-economical and parental educational as modifiable risk factors for the occurrence NSOFCs that can be prevented through preconception educational programs [48].

Out of the total sample, 160 (19.1%) displayed at least one associated anomaly. The most frequent comorbidity noted was congenital heart defect, observed in 64 cases (7.7%) among the NSCL ± P patients. A recent meta-analysis of non-syndromic cases reported a congenital heart disease (CHD) prevalence of approximately 16%, which is slightly lower than our findings [49]. Furthermore, Philip et al., reported higher comorbidities of 30.2% in their sample, splitting into 23.8% of syndromic and 76.2% of non-syndromic cases. The difference in the findings could be attributed to the inclusion of syndromic patients and a broader assessment of comorbidities in their cohort. They further found it to be higher in CLP in non-syndromic cleft patients [32].

While this study did not examine specific genetic differences, the influence of family history was apparent as 31.7% of patient cases (265) reported a familial background of clefting, but with no statistically significant difference between CL and CLP for the frequency of associated anomalies and family history. Among individuals with a positive family history, bilateral complete CLP demonstrated the highest proportions (53 cases, 40.2%), whereas unilateral incomplete CL exhibited the lowest (56 cases, 26.4%), but did not reach statistical significance. Although not statistically significant, it gave an AOR = 1.073 (95% CI 0.792-1.453) for the broad phenotype and AOR of 1.282 (95% CI 0.945–1.738) for the sub‐phenotype. These findings suggest that there is a trend toward higher odds of the occurrence of CL/P and more severe sub‐phenotype among those with a family history, but the evidence does not meet statistical significance in the analysis.

Familial history and family-related factors, particularly consanguinity, may contribute not only to the occurrence, but also to the complexity and severity of CL/P, especially in countries classified as having a high income level in this study. Although family history did not reach statistical significance in the multivariable analysis, it was associated with higher adjusted odds of more severe cleft sub-phenotypes (AOR = 1.282; 95% CI: 0.945–1.738), suggesting a potential trend toward increased severity among affected families. This finding is biologically plausible, as consanguinity increases the likelihood of homozygosity for deleterious recessive variants, which may amplify genetic susceptibility and phenotypic expression of congenital anomalies, including NSOFCs [6, 50]. High-income countries in the Middle East, such as Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Oman, and the UAE, are known to have persistently high rates of consanguineous marriages, which may partly explain the greater proportion of severe cleft phenotypes observed, despite better access to healthcare services.

Supporting this interpretation, Acuña-González and colleagues reported a strong association between positive family history and the risk of NSCL ± P, with significantly increased odds among individuals with affected first-degree relatives [41]. Similarly, Silva et al. found that approximately one-third of patients with NSOFCs reported a positive family history, with higher frequencies observed among CLP and CL cases compared with isolated cleft palate, although differences between CLP and CL were not statistically significant. Taken together, these findings suggest that familial aggregation and shared genetic background, potentially intensified by consanguinity, may influence not only cleft occurrence, but also phenotypic severity. While the association observed in the present study did not reach statistical significance, the consistent direction of effect highlights the need for larger, genetically informed, multicenter studies to further elucidate the role of consanguinity and inherited susceptibility in determining cleft severity across different socioeconomic contexts [51].

Furthermore, our dataset highlights significant variability of associated anomaly across cleft subtypes (P = 0.036). The highest proportions of anomalies were seen among unilateral complete CL (23 cases, 29.5%). However, bilateral complete CL had the lowest rates of associated anomalies (4 cases, 14.3%). The existing literature suggests a non-uniform distribution of comorbidities among cleft phenotypes. Thus, our findings emphasize the importance of comprehensive clinical evaluation of children born with certain subtypes as they may carry a higher risk of associated anomalies, even in clinically non-syndromic cases. The findings of this study differ from those reported by Fell et al., who observed no difference in the prevalence of additional anomalies between the sidedness and bilateral cleft lip cases. This discrepancy could be attributed to their inclusion of syndromic patients in their study [52].

This study has several limitations, including the use of a convenience sample, which introduces potential selection bias. Also, the sample is purely from CL/P with no comparison non-cleft group. However, this was carried out to highlight and describe the different severity groups, which is the main goal of this study. Furthermore, there is a risk of reporting bias, as parent and child information was gathered using subjective measurement tools. However, one of the key strengths of this study is its multicenter. It is a cross-sectional design with comprehensive regional outreach across seven Middle Eastern and Asian countries reflecting two economic levels. This approach enabled the identification and recruitment of eligible cleft cases and their families over a wide geographic area, ensuring a representative and diverse sample. Moreover, this study assists regional populations, offering valuable insight into cleft characteristics in the region.

Conclusion

This multi-country study provides a comprehensive overview of the phenotypic patterns and severity of non-syndromic cleft lip with or without palate across seven Middle Eastern and Asian countries representing two economic levels. The findings demonstrate that a country’s income level is significantly associated with both cleft phenotype and severity, with high-income countries showing a higher proportion of severe cleft sub-phenotypes, particularly bilateral complete CLP. Although family income and parental education were not independently associated with cleft severity, notable trends were observed in relation to family history and cleft complexity, suggesting an underlying contribution of genetic and familial factors.

Importantly, the study indicates that even in higher-income settings with better healthcare access, severe cleft presentations remain prevalent, potentially reflecting the influence of consanguinity and inherited susceptibility in the region. The observed variability in associated anomalies across cleft subtypes further underscores the need for thorough clinical evaluation of all children born with orofacial clefts, regardless of apparent syndromic status. Collectively, these findings emphasize the value of regional, collaborative research in understanding socioeconomic and familial influences on NSOFC presentation and provide a baseline to inform future genetic studies, preventive strategies, and healthcare planning tailored to regional needs.
