Chromosomal deletions in banana somaclonal variants reveal negative regulators of immunity underlying Fusarium wilt resistance
Authors: Puyam Tondonba Singh, Bo-Han Hou, Yi-Heng Tsai, Yuh Tzean, Chih-Ping Chao, Po-Xing Zheng, Yao-Cheng Lin, Wei-Chiang Shen, Hsin-Hung Yeh, Ho-Ming Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceFusarium wilt tropical race 4 (TR4) is a major threat to global banana production, especially in sterile, triploid cultivars that cannot be conventionally bred. Understanding the genetic basis of resistance in these bananas has been a long-standing challenge and is crucial for developing sustainable solutions. By analyzing multiple somaclonal variants, we identified chromosomal deletions associated with TR4 resistance. These deletions reduce the copy number of calcium pump genes that suppress immune responses, thereby enhancing disease resistance. Our findings underscore the importance of structural variation and dosage of susceptibility genes in shaping robust disease resistance in triploid bananas. This work provides critical genomic insights for selecting or engineering TR4-resistant banana varieties to safeguard one of the world’s most important fruits.

Bananas (Musa spp.) are among the most important fruit crops worldwide, serving both as a staple food for millions and a vital source of income in tropical and subtropical regions. Fusarium wilt of banana, caused by the hemibiotrophic fungal pathogen Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense (Foc), poses a severe threat to global banana production and is considered one of the most devastating plant disease epidemics in agricultural history (1). Prior to the 1950s, the global banana export trade was dominated by the cultivar “Gros Michel” (AAA group) due to its superior taste, size, and transportability. However, Foc Race 1 (R1) largely wiped out Gros Michel plantations (2). The subsequent replacement with R1-resistant Cavendish banana cultivars (AAA group), which now dominate the global banana markets, temporarily rescued the banana industry. This solution, however, is under threat from Foc Tropical Race 4 (TR4), a virulent strain capable of infecting Cavendish bananas (2). First identified in Taiwan in 1967 (3), TR4 has since spread across Asia, the Middle East, Africa, and Latin America, posing a renewed and serious threat to banana production worldwide (4, 5).

TR4 is extremely difficult to manage due to its ability to persist in soil for decades through the formation of resilient chlamydospores. Conventional control strategies including chemical, biological, and cultural approaches, have shown limited effectiveness against this pathogen (6). Traditional breeding for TR4 resistance in Cavendish bananas is constrained by their sterility and triploid genomes (7, 8). As an alternative, mutation breeding and somaclonal variation have emerged as promising tools for identifying resistant lines (7, 9, 10). The Taiwan Banana Research Institute (TBRI) has actively screened for TR4-resistant somaclonal variants of the Giant Cavendish cultivar “Pei-Chiao” (PC) in fields heavily infested with TR4. This effort has led to the development of several somaclonal variants that retain commercial value while exhibiting varying degrees of TR4 resistance (SI Appendix, Fig. S1) (7). Among these, three Giant Cavendish tissue culture variants (GCTCVs) developed by TBRI, “GCTCV-218-1” (cultivar: “Formosana” [FM]), “GCTCV-215-1-1A” (cultivar: “Tai-Chiao No. 5” [TC5]), and “GCTCV-105-1-1” (cultivar: “Tai-Chiao No. 7” [TC7]), have gained popularity among Taiwanese banana growers. The resistance of GCTCVs to TR4 has also been validated under field conditions in the Philippines, China, Australia, and Mozambique (11–15). Notably, the levels of TR4 resistance in certain GCTCVs have been shown to be influenced by factors such as increased inoculum density, temperature fluctuations, poor soil conditions, and suboptimal management (7, 13). TC5 is particularly promising due to its favorable agricultural traits and fruit quality, which are comparable to PC (16). FM has also been adopted for commercial cultivation in the Philippines and Mozambique, owing to its stable resistance and desirable yield performance. Studying these somaclonal variants may provide valuable insights into the genetic bases and mechanisms of TR4 resistance.

Both host resistance (R) and susceptibility (S) genes play key roles in determining crop resistance to fungal pathogens (17–22). R gene-mediated resistance is often race-specific and can be rapidly overcome by evolving pathogens, as likely the Foc resistance in Cavendish. In contrast, loss-of-function mutations in S genes, while not always fully preventing infection, generally confer broader and more durable resistance (23). Structural variations, such as chromosomal duplications and deletions, can enhance disease resistance by increasing the dosage of R genes or reducing that of S genes (24). Notably, large structural variations are more likely to be retained in polyploid or clonally propagated crops such as bananas (25, 26).

In this study, we employed a multifaceted approach to elucidate the genetic and molecular bases underlying TR4 resistance in GCTCVs. We found that large and overlapping deletions on one copy of chromosome 5 were recurrently selected in these cultivars. Transcriptomic analysis showed that these deletions led to reduced expression of genes within the affected regions. Moreover, silencing two calcium pump genes located within the deletion regions, both of which act as negative regulators of the salicylic acid (SA)-mediated defense response, increased the TR4 resistance of susceptible bananas. Our findings suggest that reducing calcium pump gene expression or copy number may offer a promising strategy for breeding bananas with durable resistance to Fusarium wilt.

Results

GCTCVs Retain TR4 Resistance under Artificial Inoculation.

GCTCVs have demonstrated moderate to high levels of resistance to TR4 under field conditions (7, 11–15). To evaluate their resistance under controlled conditions, we used a dipping inoculation method on plantlets at 5 to 6 leaf stage from five GCTCVs and the susceptible parent cultivar PC in growth chambers (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Two weeks postinoculation, resistance was assessed based on external symptoms (leaf yellowing and wilting) and internal symptoms (rhizome discoloration) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). All five GCTCVs showed significantly lower disease indices in both leaves and rhizomes compared with PC (Fig. 1). Among them, “GCTCV-119” exhibited the highest level of resistance. Notably, TC5, previously characterized as moderately resistant under field conditions (16), demonstrated resistance comparable to other variants under laboratory conditions. These findings confirm the enhanced resistance of GCTCVs to TR4, consistent with field-based evaluations.

A Deletion on Chromosome 4 Reduces MaACA7 Expression in TC5.

Due to the sterility of Cavendish bananas, conventional genetic mapping approaches are not applicable for identifying causal mutations conferring TR4 resistance in GCTCVs. As an alternative, we analyzed genomic mutations that may underlie resistance, focusing on shared or functionally analogous genes. Our previous transcriptomic analysis revealed genomic regions with extensive loss of heterozygosity (LOH) on chromosome 4 in TC5 and on chromosome 5 in FM and TC7, suggesting large deletions on one copy of these chromosomes (27). In this study, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) of five GCTCVs and PC confirmed and precisely mapped a ~30 kb deletion on one copy of chromosome 4 in TC5 (Fig. 2A). To validate this deletion, we designed primers targeting conserved sequences flanking the deletion region. PCR amplification yielded the expected product only in TC5, confirming the deletion (Fig. 2B).

Annotation based on the Musa acuminata ssp. malaccensis “DH Pahang” v4 genome (29) revealed that the deleted region in TC5 chromosome 4 harbors seven genes (Fig. 2C). Among them, MaACA7, encoding a putative autoinhibited Ca2+-ATPase (ACA), showed the highest expression in our root transcriptomic dataset (Fig. 2D). MaACA7 was also the only gene in this region with significantly reduced expression in TC5 compared to PC, based on root transcriptomes of hydroponically grown plants from the same tanks (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). RT-qPCR further confirmed a ~30% reduction in MaACA7 expression in TC5 roots (Fig. 2E). Comparative transcriptomic analysis between TC5 and PC identified 72 upregulated and 44 downregulated genes in TC5 roots (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). The relatively small number of differentially expressed genes under nonstress conditions may explain the phenotypic similarity between TC5 and PC (16). Nevertheless, several defense-related GO terms were enriched among TC5-upregulated genes, including “response to bacterium,” “response to SA,” and “regulation of defense response” (Fig. 2F).

Loss-of-function mutations in S genes have been demonstrated to enhance fungal resistance in several crop species (17–19, 30, 31). Within the TC5 deletion region, MaACA7 emerges as the most promising S gene candidate (Fig. 2 C and D). MaACA7 shares homology with AtACA1, AtACA2, and AtACA7 in Arabidopsis thaliana (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), which encode endoplasmic reticulum (ER)–localized calcium efflux pumps known to negatively regulate SA-mediated immunity (32). Based on the functional roles of Arabidopsis ACA homologs and the transcriptional changes observed in TC5, we hypothesize that the reduced gene dosage and lower expression of MaACA7 may contribute to enhanced TR4 resistance in TC5.

Four GCTCVs Share Similar Deletions on Chromosome 5.

We confirmed and defined the deletions on one copy of chromosome 5 in FM and TC7 using WGS. We also examined whether similar deletions occur in “GCTCV-217” and GCTCV-119, which exhibit TR4 resistance superior or comparable to FM and TC7 under field conditions (7). To infer copy number changes across chromosomes, we employed two complementary approaches: WGS read coverage and variant allele ratio (VAR) (see SI Appendix, Fig. S6 for analysis pipeline). All reads were mapped to DH Pahang v4 genome (29). In the coverage analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S7), WGS data from “Dwarf Cavendish” (33) were used as a control, and normalized read coverage values of all GCTCVs and Dwarf Cavendish were calculated relative to PC. Compared to Dwarf Cavendish and TC5, both FM and TC7 displayed reduced read coverage in the region corresponding to the previously reported LOH regions on chromosome 5 (27) (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Similarly, GCTCV-217 and GCTCV-119 exhibited reduced coverage in this region, suggesting large deletions on one chromosome 5 copy.

We further validated these copy number changes through VAR analysis (see SI Appendix, Fig. S6 for analysis pipeline), which has been demonstrated to be useful for detecting chromosomal deletions or duplications in triploid bananas (33, 34). In triploid bananas such as Cavendish, heterozygous sites typically yield a VAR of 0.33 or 0.67, reflecting a 2:1 allele ratio. However, in regions with copy loss, these ratios shift to 0.5 or become homozygous (VAR = 0 or 1) (Fig. 3A). When plotting VAR values across a chromosome, a continuous shift in VAR indicates a large deletion on one chromosome copy (Fig. 3B).

On FM, TC7, GCTCV-217, and GCTCV-119 chromosome 5, many sites that are heterozygous in PC became homozygous or showed a shifted VAR of 0.5, indicating loss of one allele (Fig. 3C). Sanger sequencing of selected loci further validated these changes in heterozygosity (SI Appendix, Fig. S8), supporting the presence of a large deletion on one copy of chromosome 5 in all four somaclonal variants. Among them, FM exhibits the smallest deletion, shared by all four GCTCVs, while GCTCV-119 harbors the largest deletion, spanning nearly the entire chromosome 5. Although additional copy number variants were detected on other chromosomes (SI Appendix, Fig. S7), none were consistently shared across all four GCTCVs. These observations suggest that deletions on chromosome 5 may have been independently selected during the TBRI breeding program for TR4 resistance. As with the chromosome 4 deletion in TC5, we hypothesize that reduced dosage of genes within the chromosome 5 deletion region may influence TR4 susceptibility.

To identify candidate S genes, we precisely mapped the deletion boundaries in FM using a modified split-read approach (35), which revealed a ~6 Mb deletion (956,425 to 7,030,674 bp), corroborated by shifts in VAR and reduced WGS coverage (Fig. 3D and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Again, to validate this structural change, we designed primers flanking the deletion region and conserved across all three genome copies. A PCR product of the expected size was amplified only in FM, confirming the deletion (Fig. 3E). In contrast, deletion boundaries in TC7, GCTCV-217, and GCTCV-119 could not be resolved due to breakpoint proximity to telomeric regions (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Nonetheless, all four GCTCVs share a common deleted interval on chromosome 5.

To assess whether the deletions occurred on the same chromosomal copy, we compared VAR patterns across the four GCTCVs. This analysis grouped the variants into two clusters with complementary LOH profiles (VAR = 0 or 1) (Fig. 3F), indicating that the deletions likely occurred on different chromosome 5 copies. Using the recently released haplotype-resolved Cavendish genome assemblies by Li et al. (36) and Huang et al. (37), coverage analyses suggest that FM and TC7 likely lost BXJ2-5/Ban05, while GCTCV-217 and GCTCV-119 lost BXJ1-5/Ze05 (SI Appendix, Figs. S9 and S10). The haplotype-based deletion maps largely match those inferred from DH Pahang, and support the loss of entire BXJ1-5/Ze05 in GCTCV-119 (SI Appendix, Figs. S9 and S10). Although M. acuminata ssp. malaccensis contributes minimally to Cavendish chromosome 5 (38), the deletion regions identified on FM chromosome 5, based on both DH Pahang and Cavendish sequences, were highly similar in size, gene content, and gene order, with 74 to 78% of genes having collinear orthologs (SI Appendix, Fig. S11).

Overall, despite differences in the haplotype copy affected, all four lines share a common deleted interval on chromosome 5. We therefore propose that dosage reduction, rather than haplotype identity, is the primary factor underlying resistance.

TR4 Triggers Stronger SA Response in Cultivars with a Chromosome 5 Deletion.

According to DH Pahang v4 genome annotations, the single-copy deletion regions in TC5 and FM span 7 and 836 genes, respectively (Figs. 2C and 4A). In TC5, MaACA7 is the only plausible S gene within the deleted region (Fig. 2 C and D). Intriguingly, another member of the MaACA gene family, MaACA8, resides within the chromosome 5 deletion region in FM (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Table S1). While MaACA7 is homologous to ER-localized AtACA1, AtACA2, and AtACA7 in Arabidopsis, MaACA8 shares homology with vacuole-localized AtACA4 (39) and AtACA11 (40) (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Notably, all of these Arabidopsis ACA genes are known negative regulators of SA-mediated plant immunity (32, 41).

Given the reduced expression of MaACA7 and the upregulation of SA-responsive genes in TC5 (Fig. 2 D–F), we investigated whether MaACA8 expression and SA signaling are similarly affected in cultivars with deletions on chromosome 5. RT-qPCR analysis confirmed that FM and TC7, both of which carry a single-copy deletion encompassing MaACA8, exhibit ~30% reduced MaACA8 expression (Fig. 4C), consistent with a gene dosage effect. Remarkably, our previous study showed that two SA marker genes, MaWRKY40 and MaDLO1, were significantly induced by TR4 inoculation in TC7 but not in the susceptible cultivar PC (43), suggesting that SA-mediated defense may be enhanced and crucial for TR4 resistance in TC7.

Extending beyond GCTCVs, VAR analysis of published transcriptome data revealed that the TR4-resistant cultivar “Nongke No. 1” (NK) shares a similar deletion region on chromosome 5 with FM (Fig. 4D) (42). Transcriptomic comparison between NK and the susceptible cultivar “Baxi” (BX) demonstrated a global downregulation of genes within the NK/FM deletion region, but not genome-wide (Fig. 4E and SI Appendix, Fig. S12), confirming a dosage effect on gene expression in the triploid banana genome. Upon TR4 inoculation, NK showed stronger induction of MaDLO1 and MaWRKY40 than BX (Fig. 4F), suggesting elevated SA pathway activation. Given that exogenous SA treatment improves TR4 resistance in susceptible Cavendish (44), we hypothesize that loss of one copy of MaACA genes may enhance SA-mediated immunity and contribute to TR4 resistance in somaclonal variants.

Silencing of MaACAs Increases TR4 Resistance.

To test whether reduced expression of MaACAs contributes to TR4 resistance, we employed a cucumber mosaic virus (CMV)-based virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) system (45) to knock down MaACA7 or MaACA8 in the susceptible Cavendish cultivar PC. Partial sequences of MaACA7 and MaACA8 were cloned into separate pJLCMV vectors (CMV::MaACA7 and CMV::MaACA8) (SI Appendix, Fig. S13), and these constructs, along with the vector control (CMV::N5), were delivered via agroinfiltration into the rhizome–pseudostem junction of PC plantlets (Fig. 5A). Silencing efficiency reached 77% for MaACA7 and 70% for MaACA8 (SI Appendix, Table S2). RT-qPCR confirmed reduced transcript levels to 0.71-fold for MaACA7 and to 0.74-fold for MaACA8 relative to the vector control in infiltrated tissues (Fig. 5B), mirroring the ACA expression reduction observed in GCTCVs (Figs. 2E and 4C).

Notably, even in the absence of TR4, silencing of either gene significantly upregulated the expression of SA-responsive marker genes MaDLO1 and MaWRKY40 (Fig. 5 C and D), reinforcing their proposed roles as negative regulators of SA-mediated defense. Following TR4 inoculation, plants with silenced MaACA7 or MaACA8 exhibited markedly reduced rhizome discoloration compared to the vector control (Fig. 5 E and F). These results support the model that MaACA7 and MaACA8 function as negative regulators of SA-mediated defense and that their downregulation enhances TR4 resistance in banana.

Discussion

This study identifies a potential genetic basis for enhancing TR4 resistance in triploid banana somaclonal variants, highlighting a role for chromosomal deletions that reduce the dosage of negative regulators of SA-mediated immunity in fungal disease resistance. In particular, the widely adopted TR4-resistant GCTCVs exhibit deletions affecting the dosage of ACA-type calcium pump genes, which suppress SA-mediated responses. Based on our findings and the conserved role of ACA genes in Arabidopsis SA-mediated immunity (32, 41), we propose that deletions encompassing either MaACA7 or MaACA8 reduce their expression, leading to elevated expression of SA marker genes and enhanced resistance to TR4. Supporting the critical role of calcium signaling in Fusarium wilt resistance, polymorphisms of a calcium-binding protein gene were also reported in “Grand Nain” resistant somaclones selected in Brazil (46). However, we cannot exclude the contribution of additional candidate genes whose loss or dosage reduction may prime or potentiate immune responses. Nonetheless, the overlap in deletion regions among independently derived GCTCVs and the convergence of functional evidence on ACA-mediated suppression of SA-dependent responses strongly suggest that copy number change in these negative regulators provides a selective advantage under TR4 pressure.

The feasibility of maintaining such large deletions is likely influenced by the reproductive biology of banana. In sexually reproducing diploids, large deletions are often deleterious and purged during meiosis. In contrast, polyploid or clonally propagated plants like Cavendish bananas can tolerate and retain large chromosomal deletions. This genomic flexibility has been exploited in other polyploid crops, such as common wheat (AABBDD), where deletion stocks have enabled trait mapping across chromosome arms (47). Our results indicate that similar deletions may be prevalent in banana tissue culture–derived populations and could be harnessed for trait dissection or breeding.

Recent advances in transgene-free genome editing in Cavendish bananas (48) open the possibility of precisely modifying ACA gene dosage to enhance TR4 resistance, while minimizing unintended effects on other loci for preserving agronomic traits. However, the broader application of genome editing across banana cultivars is still constrained by variable regeneration and transformation efficiencies. Therefore, alternative approaches to induce and select for beneficial mutations remain relevant. Gamma irradiation and tissue culture, for example, can generate large indels or chromosomal aberrations (27, 49), and when combined with marker-assisted selection, could facilitate the identification and propagation of aca-based resistant lines in susceptible non-Cavendish cultivars.

Our finding reinforces the strategy of engineering S genes to achieve broad-spectrum and durable resistance, a particularly relevant approach for the banana industry in combating Fusarium wilt. The rapid evolution of Foc poses a major challenge, as evidenced by the susceptibility of Cavendish bananas to TR4 despite their resistance to R1. R-gene–based approaches, such as transgenic expression of RGA2, have shown promise in enhancing TR4 resistance (50, 51), but their effectiveness can be undermined by pathogen adaptation, as documented for stem rust resistance in wheat (52). S gene-based strategies, in contrast, offer broader and more durable resistance. Broad-spectrum resistance to fungal pathogens is frequently associated with loss-of-function mutations in S genes (17–19). For instance, mutations in TaHRC and MLO confer resistance to Fusarium head blight in wheat and powdery mildew in barley, respectively. Importantly, mlo-based resistance has been successfully introduced into diverse crop species via genome editing and nontransgenic approaches such as TILLING (30, 31, 53), and the resistance level in wheat appears to increase with the number of MLO copies disrupted (30, 31). We view R-gene–based and S-gene–based approaches as complementary, and both merit further exploration to diversify and strengthen banana resistance to Fusarium wilt.

Intriguingly, TaHRC encodes a calcium-binding protein (54), while MLO family members function as calcium channels (55). Like ACA, both TaHRC and MLO act as negative regulators of plant immunity (54, 56). Besides, growth defects in mlo and aca mutants, such as leaf chlorosis and necrosis, can be alleviated by nutrient supplementation (57, 58). Given these parallels, it is reasonable to postulate that aca-based resistance may not only confer TR4 resistance but also broader resistance to Fusarium fungi in bananas and potentially other crops. Moreover, given the enhanced resistance of Arabidopsis, rice, and Nicotiana benthamiana to bacterial and viral pathogens following ACA knockdown or knockout (32, 41, 59, 60), the application of aca-based resistance may extend beyond fungi to a wide range of biotrophic pathogens.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials.

Tissue culture–propagated banana plantlets of PC and GCTCVs used in this study were obtained from TBRI in Pingtung, Taiwan. Seedlings from tissue culture jars were transferred to pots containing a soil mixture of six parts peat, one part perlite, and one part vermiculite. For gene expression comparisons among PC, FM, TC5, and TC7, plantlets were grown hydroponically in a modified Hoagland solution (61). PC and GCTCVs were cocultivated in the same tank under identical environmental conditions to ensure a fair comparison. The hydroponic solution was replaced weekly, and root tissues were harvested after 4 wk. All plants were grown in a growth chamber at 28 °C under a 16-h light/8-h dark cycle, with an illumination of 80 to 100 μmol m−2 s−1; hydroponically grown plants were maintained under 170 μmol m−2 s−2.

Whole-Genome Library Preparation and Sequencing.

All banana genomic sequences analyzed in this study were generated in-house, except for the Dwarf Cavendish genome sequence (Dataset S1) (33). Genomic DNA of each cultivar was extracted from the leaves of a single plantlet using a modified cetyl trimethylammonium bromide method (62), and further purified with the Zymo Genomic DNA Clean & Concentrator Kit and OneStep PCR Inhibitor Removal Kit (Zymo Research).

For library preparation, the genomic DNA was fragmented using the Covaris E220 sonicator (Covaris). The paired-end sequencing libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit II for Illumina (E7370S, New England Biolabs). Sequencing was performed by Welgene Biotech using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to produce paired-end reads of 150 bp. Raw reads were preprocessed using BBDuk and Clumpify from the BBTools suite (63). The clean reads were aligned to the DH Pahang v4 genome (29) or Cavendish genomes (36, 37) using BWA (v2.1.1) (64).

Relative Copy Number Estimation Based on WGS Coverage.

Normalized WGS coverage was used to estimate copy number changes. Coverage was calculated from BAM files using the bamCoverage tool in deepTools (v3.4.3) (65), with normalization set to reads per genomic content. A custom R script was used to import the coverage data and divide it into fixed-size bins. Coverage values in each bin were standardized using Z-scores, and bins with Z-scores between −1.64 and +1.64 were used to compute the baseline coverage. This baseline was then used to normalize coverage across all bins in each sample. Bins with extreme values were excluded, and normalized coverage values greater than two were capped at 2. Finally, the normalized coverage of each sample was divided by that of the PC sample to reveal relative copy number changes. To match the expected copy number, values were scaled by a factor of three for the DH Pahang genome. A workflow is presented in SI Appendix, Fig. S6.

Whole-Genome VAR Analysis.

For VAR analysis, variants were called using GATK HaplotypeCaller (v4.1.4.1) (66, 67). To investigate LOH in chromosome 5 of GCTCVs, we first selected biallelic sites from the control sample PC using WGS data. Biallelic sites with a sum of allele depth (AD) (Ref_AD+Alt_AD) >10 were retained, and corresponding VAR values were calculated in all samples and plotted using custom R scripts. A workflow is presented in SI Appendix, Fig. S6.

Collinearity Analysis.

The longest protein isoform for each gene within the FM deletion region of the two Cavendish genomes was compared to the subset of the DH Pahang proteome corresponding to that region using BLASTP (v2.17.0) (28) with an e-value cutoff of 1e−5. The resulting BLASTP hits were used as input to MCScanX (v1.0.0) (68) to identify collinear gene pairs under default parameters.

TR4 Inoculation and Symptom Assessment.

For TR4 inoculation, an isolate of VCG 01213/16 obtained from the TBRI was used. To assess external and internal disease symptoms, TR4 inoculation was performed using a modified root-dipping method (69) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Briefly, the roots of plantlets at the 5 to 6 leaf stage were washed with tap water to remove most of the soil from the root surface. For each plant, roots were immersed in 100 mL of TR4 spore suspension (1 × 105 spores/mL) for 2 h at room temperature, in accordance with the concentration recommended by the MusaNet evaluation guidelines (70). Mock-inoculated control plants were treated in the same way, except that sterile distilled water was used. After inoculation, plants were transplanted into soil-filled plastic pots using the previously described method, and each pot was irrigated with either TR4 spore suspension or sterile water before being returned to the growth chamber. Disease assessment and indexing were performed in accordance with MusaNet guidelines (70). Each treatment included 5 to 7 biological replicates (plants) per batch, and the experiment was independently repeated at least three times.

RNA-seq and Data analysis.

Total RNA was extracted using the PureLink Plant RNA Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for RNA-seq library preparation. Libraries were constructed with the KAPA mRNA HyperPrep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. RNA-seq datasets for Baxi and Nongke No.1, generated by Li et al. (42), were used for variant calling and gene expression quantification, while the PC and GCTCVs datasets from Hou et al. (27) were used only for variant calling (Dataset S1). All variant calling datasets were processed and analyzed following a modified protocol described by Hou et al. (27) as illustrated in SI Appendix, Fig. S6. In brief, biallelic sites in PC were identified, and the sites with a sum of AD >10 across all samples were retained. VAR was calculated at these sites for each sample and averaged across biological replicates for plotting using custom R scripts.

For gene expression quantification, adapter sequences and low-quality bases (Phred quality score < 20) were removed using Trimmomatic (v0.39) (71). Reads shorter than 100 nt were discarded. The filtered reads were then mapped to the DH Pahang v4 genome with STAR (v2.7.0f) (72). The unique mapped reads were used to calculate transcripts per million using RNA-seq by expectation-maximization (v1.3.0) (73), and differentially expressed genes were identified with the DESeq2 R package (v1.24.0) (74), retaining genes with >5 raw counts in at least half of the analyzed samples and applying a Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) adjusted P < 0.1 (Dataset S2).

For GO enrichment analysis, the GO annotation table of DH Pahang v4 genome was obtained from the Banana Genome Hub (https://banana-genome-hub.southgreen.fr/) (75). GO enrichment was analyzed with the R packages AnnotationForge (v1.26.0) (76) and clusterProfiler (v3.12.0) (77). GO terms with a BH-adjusted P < 0.05 were deemed significantly enriched.

Phylogenetic Tree Construction and Protein Domain Analysis.

Phylogenetic analysis was conducted using the ACA/ECA proteins, which contain cation transporter ATPase N-terminal and C-terminal domains from DH Pahang v4 genome (SI Appendix, Table S1), along with homologous sequences from Oryza sativa and A. thaliana (78). Sequences from Amborella trichopoda containing the corresponding domains were used as an outgroup in the analyses. ACA protein sequences from A. thaliana and O. sativa were retrieved from TAIR (http://www.arabidopsis.org) and the Rice Genome Annotation Project (http://rice.plantbiology.msu.edu), respectively.

Protein sequences were aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm (79) implemented in MEGA XI software (80). Phylogenetic model selection was performed using ModelTest-NG, guided by the Bayesian Information Criterion (81). The Whelan and Goldman model, with +I and Gamma Distributed Rate Variation, was selected as the best-fit model. The resulting multiple alignment was used to construct a maximum-likelihood tree using MEGA XI with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Protein domains were identified using the NCBI Conserved Domain Search tool (82), with the latest Pfam database version.

Construction of VIGS Vectors.

To clone cDNA fragments of MaACAs, total RNA was extracted from root tissue using PureLink Plant RNA Reagent. cDNA was synthesized using the TOOLS Quant II Fast RT Kit (Biotools). Gene-specific fragments of MaACA7 and MaACA8 were amplified from the cDNA, while a short fragment of the 35S promoter (N5) was amplified as a negative control. All fragments were inserted into the AfeI-digested pJLCMV-R2E (45) to construct pJLCMV-N5, pJLCMV-MaACA7, and pJLCMV-MaACA8 (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). Primer sequences are listed in SI Appendix, Table S3.

Functional Assay of ACAs Using VIGS.

All VIGS clones were separately introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 by electroporation. Agrobacterium cultures were grown in yeast extract broth medium with 50 µg/mL of kanamycin, as described previously (83). Cell mixtures containing pJLCMV-RNA1, pJLCMV-RNA3, and pJLCMV-GOI (RNA2 containing the gene of interest) were prepared at a 1:1:1 ratio and injected into the rhizome–pseudostem junction of PC plants with 4 to 5 leaves. Injections were performed using a 0.5-mL sterile syringe, following the previously described protocol (45). Rhizome–pseudostem junction samples were collected 7 d postinjection to determine the silencing efficiency and efficacy. At 7 d postinjection of Agrobacterium carrying VIGS constructs, VIGS plants were inoculated with TR4 using the dipping method (69). At 14 d post–TR4 inoculation, we photographed the plants and evaluated their internal symptoms.

RNA Isolation and Quantitative RT-PCR.

Total RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis were performed as aforementioned. To quantify transcript abundance, quantitative PCR was carried out using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq II Kit (Takara Bio) on an ABI Prism 7500 sequence detection system (Applied Biosystems). Plant samples from at least three independent batches were used for RNA preparations, each containing 4 to 5 biological replicates. MaACT3 was used as an internal control for normalization. The 2−ΔΔCt method was used to quantify gene expression levels. All primer sequences used in this study are listed in SI Appendix, Table S3.
