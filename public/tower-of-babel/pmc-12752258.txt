The neutrophil to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio is associated with disease severity in hypertriglyceridemic acute pancreatitis
Authors: Xiaogang Hu, Jia Xu, Juntao Yang, Menggang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe neutrophil to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHR) predicts disease severity in biliary pancreatitis, yet its prognostic role in hypertriglyceridemic acute pancreatitis (HTG-AP) remains unclear.MethodsThis study enrolled 421 eligible patients diagnosed with HTG-AP. Baseline comparisons were made using the Mann-Whitney U Test and Chi-Square Test. Logistic regression analysis was employed to identify risk factors for HTG-AP severity. The predictive value of NHR was assessed using the Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) and the Net Reclassification Index (NRI). Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to evaluate nonlinear associations. Subgroup analyses further explored the correlation between NHR and HTG-AP severity.ResultsPatients with moderate/severe HTG-AP exhibited significantly higher NHR levels than those with mild disease (p < 0.001). Elevated NHR correlated with prolonged hospitalization (p = 0.016), higher rates of acute peripancreatic fluid collection (APFC) (p < 0.001) and acute necrotic collection (ANC) (p = 0.008). NHR was an independent risk factor for moderate/severe HTG-AP (p < 0.001) and improved the predictive accuracy of the risk assessment model. NHR led to only a small improvement in AUC within the model, which indicated some limitations in predicting HTG-AP severity. RCS analysis revealed a linear relationship between NHR and HTG-AP severity (p for non-linearity > 0.05).ConclusionNHR is a simple, early biomarker for predicting HTG-AP severity, offering potential utility in guiding timely clinical interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04466-x.Keywords: Hypertriglyceridemic acute pancreatitis, Acute pancreatitis, Neutrophil to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio, Disease severity

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP), a prevalent gastrointestinal disease requiring hospitalization, arises from various etiologies, such as gallstones, alcohol abuse, and hypertriglyceridemia (HTG). Notably, the incidence of hypertriglyceridemic acute pancreatitis (HTG-AP) is rising worldwide [1, 2]. Importantly, HTG-AP presents with greater severity compared to other etiologies, underscoring the urgency to elucidate its pathophysiology and identify prognostic biomarkers [3]. AP severity is classified into mild (MAP), moderately severe (MSAP), and severe (SAP) categories based on the Revised Atlanta Classification [4]. While MAP typically follows a self-limiting course, MSAP and SAP are linked to poorer outcomes, with SAP mortality rates exceeding 20% despite advances in critical care [5]. Early prediction of disease progression is pivotal for optimizing therapeutic strategies and improving clinical results.

Emerging biomarkers integrating inflammatory and metabolic pathways have gained attention for risk prediction of AP. Among these, the neutrophil-to-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio (NHR) has demonstrated prognostic value in cardiovascular diseases and metabolic disorders, reflecting its dual role in inflammation and lipid dysregulation [6–9]. Yang et al. demonstrated an independent association between NHR and the severity of acute biliary pancreatitis (ABP) [10]. However, the relationship between NHR and the severity of HTG-AP remains unexplored, despite the distinct lipid-driven pathophysiology of HTG-AP. Therefore, we hypothesized that NHR, which encapsulates both the inflammatory and lipid components central to HTG-AP pathophysiology, could serve as a novel biomarker for predicting severity in this specific patient population. This study aims to investigate whether NHR serves as a biomarker for predicting HTG-AP severity, potentially filling knowledge gaps in early clinical decision-making.

Patients and methods

Patients

This study enrolled patients diagnosed with HTG-AP at Chongqing Liangjiang New Area People’s Hospital from January 2021 to June 2024. Inclusion criteria were: (1) diagnosis of AP based on the Revised Atlanta classification system [4]; (2) serum triglyceride (TG) levels ≥ 11.30 mmol/L, or TG levels between 5.65 and 11.30 mmol/L with chylous serum; (3) exclusion of alternative AP etiologies (e.g., biliary, alcoholic, post Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography‌); (4) age ≧ 18 years. Exclusion criteria were: (1) admission >24 h post-abdominal pain onset; (2) hospitalization stays < 48 h; (3) incomplete clinical data; (4) history of chronic/recurrent pancreatitis; (5) malignancies; (6) pregnancy; (7) severe non-pancreatitis-related comorbidities or chronic inflammatory diseases, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or heart failure; (8) Lipid-lowering therapy within 6 months prior to admission. By comprehensively analyzing the patients’ medical records and applying the Revised Atlanta classification, we determined the severity of HTG-AP patients as MAP, MSAP, and SAP.

All patients began fasting immediately after admission and received fluid resuscitation therapy. A combination of insulin and the molecular weight heparin calcium was administered to reduce triglycerides, and gabexate mesilate was used to inhibit protease activity. The treatment strategy for all patients was standardized. The effectiveness of fluid resuscitation was assessed by monitoring changes in urine output and heart rate, and these changes reflected improvements in circulatory volume and renal perfusion. Patients with hemodynamic instability or multiple organ dysfunction syndrome were transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for treatment.

Data collection

Demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were extracted from electronic medical records. Blood samples collected within 24 h of admission were analyzed for: complete blood count, lipid profile, C-reactive protein (CRP), pancreatic enzymes (amylase, lipase), hepatic and renal function parameters. Disease severity was categorized as MAP, MSAP, or SAP per the Revised Atlanta Classification [4]. Following the methodology of Yang et al., MSAP and SAP were combined as “Non-MAP” for analysis [10]. The NHR was calculated as: NHR = absolute neutrophil count (×109/L)/HDL-C (mmol/L).

Data preprocessing and assessment

The data in this study were mixed-type data with only 3.33% missing values, and we used the ‘missForest’ package to perform imputation of the missing values. The optimal NHR threshold for severity prediction was determined via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, maximizing the Youden index (J = Sensitivity + Specificity − 1). Univariable, multivariable, stepwise logistic and Lasso regression analyses were employed to identify potential risk factors for Non-MAP. Continuous variables were dichotomized using clinically validated cutoffs (Supplementary Table 1) before regression analyses. Variables with a p-value of < 0.05 in the univariable analysis were included in the multivariable model.

The area under the ROC curve (AUC), Brier score, Delong test, net reclassification improvement (NRI), and comprehensive discrimination improvement (IDI) were used to evaluate the performance of the model. Furthermore, we constructed the calibration curve for the model and plotted the curves for sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value across the full range of thresholds. Non-linear associations between NHR and HTG-AP severity were examined using Restricted Cubic Splines (RCS) with three knots. Subgroup analyses tested interactions between NHR and demographic/clinical variables (age, gender, diabetes, etc.).

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were reported as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and compared with the Mann-Whitney U Test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (%) and analyzed using Chi-Square or Fisher’s Exact Test. The primary R packages employed in this analysis include missForest, moonBook, reportROC, gtsummary, stepReg, glmnet, ggvenn, rcssci, jstable, rms, predictABEL, and runway. All analyses and visualizations were performed using R software (version 4.4.1, Vienna, Austria), with a two-tailed (P < 0.05) deemed statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 421 HTG-AP patients were included (Fig. 1), with 254 classified as mild (MAP) and 167 as moderate/severe (Non-MAP). The median age was 38 years (range: 22–66 years), with no significant age difference between groups. Patients with Non-MAP had longer hospital stays (median [IQR]: 10 [8–13] vs. 6 [5–7] days; P < 0.001) and higher ICU admission rates (27% vs. 0%; P < 0.001) compared to those with MAP. A total of two patients died, both in the Non-MAP group. Non-MAP patients exhibited elevated NHR levels (16.1 [10.4–23.9] vs. 12.2 [8.3–17.0]; P < 0.001), as well as elevated white blood cell counts (WBC), neutrophil counts, monocyte counts, C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels, total cholesterol (TC) levels, TG levels, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels, amylase levels, and lipase levels, alongside reduced lymphocyte counts, albumin levels, and serum calcium levels (all P < 0.05, Table 1). However, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels were not significantly different between MAP and Non-MAP groups (P = 0.086), which directly supported the value of using NHR instead of the individual components.

Patients with high NHR exhibit more severe conditions

ROC Curve analysis determined an optimal NHR cutoff value of 16.66, dividing patients into two groups based on this threshold (Fig. 2). High NHR (≥ 16.66) was associated with a higher proportion of patients with Non-MAP (55.0% vs. 31.3%; P < 0.001), a higher rate acute peripancreatic fluid collection (APFC) (47.0% vs. 29.0%; P < 0.001) and acute necrotic collection (ANC) (14.1% vs. 5.9%; P = 0.008). Patients with high NHR also showed prolonged hospitalization (median [IQR]: 8 [6,–10] vs. 7 [5,–9] days; P = 0.016), elevated WBC counts(15.9 [13.6–18.6] vs. 11.8 [9.2–13.9] ×109/L; P < 0.001) and neutrophil counts, alongside reduced lymphocytes counts and HDL-C levels (Table 2). Although there was no significant difference in mortality between the high NHR group and the low NHR group, there were only two deaths in both groups. This small number of deaths was insufficient to reflect the relationship between NHR and mortality.

High NHR is an independent risk factor for HTG-AP severity

Univariable analysis identified eight possible predictors of Non-MAP (WBC counts, neutrophils counts, lymphocyte counts, CRP levels, serum calcium levels, albumin levels, TG levels, and NHR levels; Supplementary Table 2). Subsequent multivariable and stepwise regression analyses demonstrated that NHR levels, CRP levels, TG levels, lymphocyte counts, and hypocalcemia independently predicted disease severity (Table 3). NHR improves predictive model performance

The final set of variables for model construction was selected by combining all factors identified as significant (P < 0.05) in the univariable, multivariable, stepwise regression analyses, and Lasso regression analyses(Fig. 4). This resulted in five factors: lymphocytes counts, CRP levels, serum calcium levels, TG, levels and NHR levels. Two predictive models were constructed. Model 1 (without NHR) included CRP levels, TG levels, calcium levels, lymphocyte counts (AUC: 0.696; 95% CI: 0.644–0.749). Model 2 (with NHR) showed significantly improved discrimination (AUC: 0.736; 95% CI: 0.685–0.786). NRI (value: 0.408, P < 0.001) and IDI (value: 0.48, P < 0.001) confirmed the incremental value of NHR (Fig. 5A). DeLong’s Test results revealed a ΔAUC (difference in AUC) of 0.040 (95% CI: 0.011–0.068; P = 0.006), suggesting that the addition of NHR enhanced the model’s performance. Visualized data demonstrated that Model 2 had higher sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive results (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) across most threshold ranges (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, we computed the Brier scores for both models and found that both scores were below 0.25, with Model 2 scoring 0.196, lower than that of Model 1 (0.208). Linear association between NHR and disease severity

To further explore the association between NHR and disease severity, Restricted Cubic Spline (RCS) curves were generated (Fig. 6). Both unadjusted (Fig. 6A) and adjusted (Fig. 6B, C and D) RCS curves demonstrated a linear association between NHR levels and disease severity (P for non-linearity > 0.05), indicating that higher NHR levels were associated with increased the severity of HTG-AP.

Subgroup analysis based on demographic variables

Subgroup analyses stratified by demographic factors (age, gender, diabetes, etc.) consistently demonstrated the association between high NHR levels and disease severity (all P < 0.05), and no significant interaction between NHR levels and the subgroup variables was observed (P for interaction > 0.05, Fig. 7).

Discussion

Current prognostic tools for acute pancreatitis (AP), such as the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) and the Bedside Index for Severity in Acute Pancreatitis (BISAP), rely on complex parameters that are often unavailable during early hospitalization [11]. Consequently, many researchers are focusing on developing simpler and more efficient prediction methods. NHR offers a simple, rapid biomarker measurable at admission. Yang et al. demonstrated its predictive value in ABP [10]. Our study extends this evidence to HTG-AP, a subtype with a distinct lipid-driven pathophysiology. We found that elevated NHR levels independently were associatied with moderate/severe HTG-AP and enhanced the accuracy of severity prediction models. This association remained robust across demographic subgroups and provided secondary evidence underscoring the generalizability of NHR.

Neutrophil-driven inflammation is pivotal to AP progression [12]. While neutrophil infiltration is essential for pathogen clearance in infectious conditions, this infiltration can exacerbate tissue damage in non-infectious diseases. During the early stages of AP, when infection is absent, excessive neutrophil activation can worsen disease severity, whereas neutrophil depletion strategies has been shown to have protective effects [13]. Neutrophil counts have been found to predict organ failure in biliary pancreatitis [14]. However, neutrophil counts alone exhibit limited prognostic value [14], necessitating integration with other parameters.

HDL-C, beyond its cardiovascular implications, is recognized for its anti-inflammatory properties [15]. Studies have shown that low HDL-C levels are associated with increased severity of AP [16, 17]. In AP patients, decreased serum HDL-C reflects impaired anti-inflammatory function, leading to an accumulation of free fatty acids (FFAs), thereby exacerbating pancreatic cell damage and disease severity [17]. Notably, while Yang et al. reported higher HDL-C levels in mild ABP [10], we observed no significant differences in HDL-C levels across HTG-AP severities, which was consistent with Lin et al.’s findings [18]. This discrepancy may arise from differences in lipid metabolism between patients with ABP and those with HTG-AP. Patients with hypertriglyceridemia have a higher lipid burden and are more likely to experience abnormalities in high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels and function [19].

Due to the technical challenges of directly assessing HDL particle functionality, HDL-C is widely used as a surrogate marker in clinical practice. The bidirectional interaction between HDL levels and neutrophil counts—where HDL modulates neutrophil activity and activated neutrophils alter HDL composition—has spurred interest in NHR as a novel biomarker [20]. Initially validated in acute myocardial infarction, the prognostic utility of NHR has expanded to other inflammatory conditions. Yang et al. first demonstrated the predictive value of NHR in biliary pancreatitis (ABP), linking elevated NHR levels to lipid metabolism dysregulation and disease severity [10]. Building on this, our study confirms a similar association in HTG-AP, with high NHR patients exhibiting prolonged hospitalization and higher complication rates, highlighting the clinical relevance of NHR for HTG-AP risk stratification.

Although NHR is a novel inflammation scoring index, the relationship between elevated NHR levels and various diseases has attracted increasing attention. There are significant differences in the degree of NHR elevation among different diseases, with the magnitude of NHR elevation being significantly lower in chronic diseases compared with acute diseases. For example, Xu et al. found that the cutoff value of NHR for predicting deep venous thrombosis in patients with spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage was 6.55 [21]. Hu’s study suggested that an NHR level greater than 3.65 could increase all-cause mortality in individuals aged 60 years and above [22]. In Yang’s study, patients with acute biliary pancreatitis and an NHR level greater than 9.54 had a higher risk of developing critical illness. In this study, the cutoff value of NHR was 16.66, which is greater than its value in other diseases. This may be because HTG-AP is an acute disease with a more severe systemic inflammatory response.

Our study has several limitations. First, the single-center retrospective design and relatively smallt sample size may restrict the generalizability of findings and reduce statistical power for subgroup analyses (e.g., SAP; only 26 SAP patients were included in this study). Second, dichotomizing patients into mild and non-mild (moderate to severe) categories precluded specific evaluation the association between NHR levels and SAP. Third, many confounding factors such as body mass index (BMI) and eating habits have been largely missing, leading to insufficient comprehensiveness of the indicators included in the analysis, which may affect the accuracy of the research results. Finally, the mechanistic interplay underlying the association between NHR levels and HTG-AP severity—including neutrophil extracellular trap formation or free fatty acid metabolism—remains unexplored and warrants experimental validation.

Conclusion

NHR levels were independently associated with moderate/severe HTG-AP and enhanced risk stratification models. By integrating neutrophilic inflammation and lipid dysregulation, NHR provides actionable insights for clinicians to prioritize high-risk patients and tailor aggressive therapeutic strategies, potentially reducing complications and healthcare burdens.
