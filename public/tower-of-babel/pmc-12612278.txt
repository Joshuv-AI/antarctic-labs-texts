Effect of MgO on the catalytic activity and stability of nickel-based catalyst in methane dry reforming reaction
Authors: Omid Sattari, Behzad Nematollahi, Mehran Rezaei, Seyed Mehdi Alavi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study investigates the efficacy of various MgO loadings on enhancing the catalytic resistance to carbon formation over nickel-based catalysts during the dry reforming of methane (DRM). One of the primary challenges in this reaction is carbon deposition, primarily derived from CO decomposition through the Boudouard reaction. This process leads to carbon formation, which creates a spatial barrier between the reactants and the active sites, resulting in a significant decline in catalytic performance. Given the acidic nature of CO2, MgO, an alkaline earth metal, can serve as an alkali component to improve catalyst performance. By increasing the basicity of the catalyst, MgO enhances CO2 adsorption on the catalyst surface, which competitively suppresses the progression of the Boudouard reaction. In this study, a range of MgO loadings (5%, 25%, 45%, 65%, and 85%) was evaluated in conjunction with a fixed NiO content (10%). The findings from TPO and SEM analyses indicate that increasing MgO content substantially reduces carbon deposition on the catalyst surface. Among the tested formulations, the 10%NiO-45%MgO-Al2O3 catalyst, with a methane conversion of 66.57% at 700 °C demonstrated exceptional catalytic activity for CH4 conversions. Additionally, it exhibited remarkable long-term stability under reaction conditions.Keywords: Dry reforming reaction, Catalyst, Carbon deposition, Synthesis gas, MgOSubject terms: Heterogeneous catalysis, Chemical engineering

Introduction

Human activities, which are accompanied by the release of CO2, have gradually caused various problems, including rising sea levels and ocean acidification1. All the measures that have been taken to reduce and control the emission of carbon dioxide have so far been unsuccessful in preventing or removing the excess CO2 that exceeded 40 metric gigatons per year2. Exploitation and use of CO2 is often one of the methods used to increase profits or reduce greenhouse gas emissions3. One of the strategies to convert CH4 and CO2 greenhouse gases into valuable products is to use methane dry reforming reaction (DRM)4. This reaction is extremely endothermic and usually takes place in the temperature range of 600–800°C5. In the dry methane reforming reaction, CH4 and CO2 gases are converted into synthesis gas, which is a mixture of H2 and CO (Eq. (4))6,7 that the ratio of hydrogen to carbon monoxide in the product of this reaction is equal to 1.

It has been demonstrated that dry reforming of methane (DRM) is an effective method for CO2 reduction, simultaneously producing valuable CO as a product. However, based on the stoichiometric coefficient of this reaction, only 1 mol of CO2 is reduced for every mole of CH4 consumed8. In addition of reducing environmental pollution, sustainable energy production is another global challenge9. Due to this issue, H2 gas has emerged as a clean and valuable fuel in the world arena10. H2 gas is generally produced through the processes of dry reforming of methane, steam reforming of methane and autothermal reforming (partial oxidation) in the temperature range of 700–1000°C11.

Both steam reforming of methane and autothermal reforming have certain drawbacks. In the case of steam reforming, for every 1 ton of H2 produced, 10 tons of CO2 are emitted. In the case of autothermal reforming, the equipment required for oxygen production is highly expensive12. As a result, methane dry reforming process is the most suitable method for converting CO2 into synthesis gas.

Among these, catalyst-based procedures are designed to enhance energy efficiency, as discussed in the literature13, and the catalyst plays a vital and central role in all types of chemical reactions, both on a laboratory scale and industrial scale. In fact, it can be stated that over 90% of chemical processes that result in the production of products such as fuels, polymers, fibers, pharmaceuticals, and others involve at least one catalytic reaction step14. In this field, catalysts that combine metals with suitable supports, particularly in gas-solid reactions, have been extensively evaluated and researched. These catalysts typically exhibit a well-dispersed active phase on the support, which increases the BET surface area15. Due to the desire for cost reduction and enhanced safety, the development of catalysts that exhibit suitable activity at low temperatures and pressures is of great interest16. Furthermore, catalysts with higher thermal stability offer improved resistance to sintering, thereby extending their lifetime and increasing productivity under operational conditions17. Catalysts based on precious metals such as rhodium, ruthenium, palladium, and platinum demonstrate outstanding catalytic efficiency and resistance to coke deposition during the dry reforming process18. However, despite their effectiveness in reducing the temperature required for methane dry reforming, the high cost of noble metal catalysts limits their widespread application in industrial settings. In contrast, transition metal catalysts, including nickel, iron, and cobalt, have gained increasing recognition in the methane dry reforming industry, with nickel-based catalysts receiving particular focus due to their satisfactory catalytic performance and cost-effectiveness relative to noble metals19. Generally, catalysts featuring finely dispersed metallic nickel have proven to be more attractive options, often surpassing the performance of noble metal catalysts20. Several studies have indicated that the presence of finely dispersed nickel particles, combined with stronger metal-support interactions, can significantly contribute to improved catalytic performance21. However, these catalysts are vulnerable to deactivation from carbon deposition reactions, such as the Boudouard reaction and methane pyrolysis (Eqs. (2) and (3))22, which lead to their inactivation23. The Boudouard reaction (Eq. (2)) is exothermic, and its thermodynamic shifts toward carbon deposition at lower temperatures, typically below 700 °C. At higher temperatures, the reaction is thermodynamically suppressed. On the other hand, methane pyrolysis (Eq. (3)) is endothermic and becomes more favorable at elevated temperatures, especially above 700°C24. In addition to carbon deposition, processes such as Ni oxidation and sintering also contribute to catalyst deactivation25.

Research indicates that parameters such as species size, support deficiencies, localized temperature increases, and species composition are key factors influencing catalyst deactivation2. In this context, numerous studies have focused on surface modification, which has been shown to enhance resistance and stability against deactivation mechanisms26. For instance, the size of active metal particles plays a critical role, significantly affecting catalytic activity27. Resistance to sintering and carbon deposition also requires thorough investigation to extend the catalyst’s lifetime28.

To enhance catalytic efficiency, several approaches can be employed, including catalyst design, the use of combined synthesis methods, and appropriate catalyst supports29. One essential aspect of catalyst synthesis is preventing interaction between the catalyst support and the active phase, as this can significantly impact catalytic activity. Consequently, controlling this interaction through specific procedures is vital30. Alumina is one of the commonly used supports in the dry reforming of methane. In recent years, different structural forms of alumina have been developed to achieve nickel-based catalysts with enhanced activity and stability. In addition to alumina, silicon oxide is also one of the commonly used supports in the dry reforming of methane process31. Even in comparison with silicon oxide support, alumina exhibits a higher specific surface area and larger pore volume32. Given the points discussed above and the extensive use of alumina for effectively dispersing nickel, alumina was employed as the catalyst support in this study. A recent approach for catalyst synthesis is the mechanochemical method, which utilizes the energy released during grinding. This method, which does not require solvents, provides a suitable and effective alternative to conventional solvent-based procedures33.

promoters also play a crucial role in improving catalytic performance and are generally categorized into textural and chemical types. Textural promoters primarily improve the synthesis process and catalyst particle dispersion, while chemical promoters, commonly used in industry, include alkali and alkaline earth metals34. The influence of alkali promoters was first explored in 184535. Alkaline earth metals like La and Ce, and alkaline metals such as K, Mg, and Ca, are used as promoters to enhance stability, prevent carbon deposition, increase CO2 absorption, and improve the basicity of the catalyst support36.

Given that the presence of MgO alongside NiO can effectively reduce graphite carbon deposition and improve catalytic activity37, this study investigates, for the first time, the addition of varying amounts of MgO into the NiO/Al2O3 catalyst structure as a promoter. The mechanochemical synthesis method was applied in the dry reforming reaction, and its effects on catalytic activity, carbon deposition, and thermal resistance of the prepared catalysts were thoroughly examined.

Experimental

Catalyst preparation

In this study, the catalysts were synthesized using a solvent-free mechanochemical method. This method involves thoroughly mixing and grinding the metal nitrate precursors with the active phase in a mortar for approximately 30 min. The grinding process generates sufficient mechanical energy to release crystalline water from the hydrated precursors, forming a uniform paste-like mixture without the addition of external solvents. To initiate precipitation, ammonium carbonate (for each sample, the amount of ammonium carbonate was calculated based on the stoichiometric coefficients of its reaction with each nitrate-based precursor, including a 20 wt% excess.) was added during grinding. The acidic nature of the metal nitrates, combined with the crystalline water released during the process, creates a mildly aqueous environment. The introduction of ammonium carbonate increases the pH, promoting the in-situ precipitation of metal hydroxides and carbonates, which later transform into oxides during calcination. A representative synthesis for the 10%NiO-5%MgO-Al₂O₃ catalyst is as follows: nickel nitrate hexahydrate, magnesium nitrate hexahydrate, and aluminum nitrate nonahydrate were weighed in stoichiometric amounts and mixed with the calculated amount of ammonium carbonate. The mixture was ground until a homogeneous paste was formed. The resulting paste was dried at 120 °C for 24 h to remove moisture, and subsequently calcined in air at 500 °C for 3 h, with a heating rate of 3 °C/min. This method shares similarities with co-precipitation but is distinguished by the absence of any external solvent, offering a more sustainable and efficient synthesis route. All catalysts in this study were prepared following the same procedure with varied metal loadings. The schematic representation of the synthesis process is also provided in Scheme 1.

Characterization

N₂ adsorption and desorption analyses were conducted to determine the BET (Brunauer-Emmett-Teller) surface area and to obtain the pore size distribution. These analyses were performed using a Belsorp Mini II automatic analyzer.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was carried out using a PANalytical X’Pert Pro diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiation, operating in the 2θ range of 10–80 degrees38. The average crystallite size of the catalyst (D) was calculated using the Scherrer equation (Eq. (4)).

This equation consists of three parameters which are λ: indicating the size of the crystal, θ: Bragg angle; and β: the width at half maximum intensity39.

Temperature-Programmed Reduction (TPR) analysis was conducted with a temperature ramp of 3 °C/min under a gas flow consisting of a mixture of hydrogen and argon (H₂:Ar = 10:90). Prior to the TPR evaluation, the catalysts were pretreated with Ar gas at 200 °C for 1 h. During the reduction process, the amount of H₂ consumption was measured using a thermal conductivity detector (TCD). To investigate the morphology and structure of the catalysts, a scanning electron microscope (SEM) was utilized, using a VEGA TESCAN system operated at 30 kV. For the Temperature-Programmed Oxidation (TPO) analysis, 0.5 g of the used catalyst samples was exposed to a gas flow of 30 mL/min, consisting of a helium and oxygen mixture (He: O₂ = 95:5). The furnace temperature was increased at a rate of 10 °C/min up to a final temperature of 700 °C.

Catalytic test

To evaluate the catalytic activity of the samples prepared in the catalytic reforming process of methane, initially, the prepared powders are compressed into discs using a powder press machine under a pressure of 20 atm. Subsequently, the particles are crushed to a specific size (30–50 mesh) and sieved. After loading 200 mg of catalyst into the reactor, the sample is reduced at a temperature of 700 °C with a heating rate of 10 °C/min for 4 h under a hydrogen gas flow rate of 20 ml/min. A thermal program is used to measure the activity and stability of the catalysts. According to this program, the conversion of methane and carbon dioxide takes place in the temperature range of 500–700 °C with temperature intervals of 50 °C. After the reduction of the catalyst, a feed (mixture CH4:CO2 = 1) is introduced into the reactor at a flow rate of 30 ml/min at 500 °C. During the reaction, the produced product stream is passed through a condenser and analyzed using an online gas chromatograph (GC, Thermofinigan) with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and a Carboxen 1010 column for further examination. Additionally, the stability of the catalysts is examined at 700 °C for approximately 1000 min. The schematic of the equipment used in the reactor testing is also presented in Scheme 2.

Results and discussion

Structural properties of the catalysts

The XRD patterns of the 10% NiO-Al₂O₃ catalysts with varying MgO loading levels are presented in Fig. 1. As evident from the graphs, increasing the MgO loading intensifies the peak corresponding to the MgAl₂O₄ phase. In the samples with 45%, 65%, and 85% MgO, the the MgAl₂O₄ (JCPDS card No. 01–074-1132) is observed at 2θ = 62.6°. In all the samples, the NiO (JCPDS card No. 01–078-0643) phase is observed at 2θ = 37.2°. The Al₂O₃ (JCPDS card No. 00–008-0013) phase is also observed in the range of 2θ = 62°−66° in the 5%, 25%, and 45% samples. In the 10%NiO-45%MgO-Al2O3 sample, due to the increased amount of MgO, in addition to the Al2O3 phase, MgAl2O4 peak also appeared. With further increases in MgO content in the 65% and 85% samples, the Al2O3 phase disappeared, and the intensity of the MgAl2O4 peak increased. In this sample, the NiAl₂O₄ (JCPDS card No. 01–078-0552) phase, which initially appeared at 44.7°, was also observed at 43.2°. As the MgO content increased in the 65% and 85% samples, the NiAl₂O₄ peak at 44.7° disappeared and at 43.2°, the intensity of this peak increased.

XRD patterns of 10% NiO-Al2O3 catalyst with and with different MgO loadings. Al2O3:*, MgAl2O4:♦, NiAl2O4:♥, NiO:●.

In general show that increasing MgO loading enhances the intensity of the peak related to the NiAl₂O₄ phase, which has been observed in the range of 2θ = 43°−45°, indicating stronger interactions between NiO and Al₂O₃. This stronger interaction, as reported in40, can reduce the catalyst’s reducibility.

The structural characteristics of the samples are presented in Table 1. As observed, the addition of MgO beyond 5% (in the 25% and 45% MgO samples) results in a decrease in both specific surface area and pore volume. This reduction is attributed to the filling of the catalyst base pores with MgO41. For the samples with 65% and 85% MgO, the increasing MgO loading further decreases the specific surface area, reaching its lowest value at 85% MgO. However, after the 45% MgO sample, the trend of pore volume demonstrates a reversal, showing an increase with higher MgO loadings. The pore width trend follows a similar pattern to pore volume, reaching a minimum of approximately 2 nm at the 45% MgO sample. Moreover, with the increase in MgO loading, the crystallite size of nickel has also increased, which accounts for the observed decrease in specific surface area.

The enhanced intensity of the NiAl₂O₄ peaks upon MgO addition can be attributed to the structural and chemical interactions of MgO with the Ni/Al₂O₃ system. The XRD patterns clearly indicate the formation of the MgAl₂O₄ phase with increasing intensity as MgO loading rises, suggesting that MgO reacts with Al₂O₃ to modify the support structure. This modification enhances the reactivity of Al₂O₃ toward NiO, thereby facilitating the solid-state reaction that forms NiAl₂O₄. The TPR profiles further support this finding, revealing a shift in the NiAl2O4 phase reduction peaks to higher temperatures (e.g., from approximately 830 °C in the 5% MgO sample to around 990 °C in the 85% MgO sample). This indicates stronger NiO–support interactions.

Table 1 provides additional evidence, showing a significant reduction in BET surface area (from 325.6 m²/g at 5% MgO to 34.42 m²/g at 85% MgO) and pore volume (from 0.374 cm³/g to 0.183 cm³/g), alongside an increase in pore width (from 4.54 nm to 21.32 nm) and NiO crystallite size (from 3.3 nm to 13.07 nm) with rising MgO. These alterations lead to a restructuring of the porous network, reducing the BET surface area and pore volume while increasing pore width. This restructuring facilitates the reactivity of NiO with Al2O3 to form NiAl2O4.

The N₂ adsorption/desorption isotherms for the prepared samples are shown in Fig. 2. From the graphs, it is evident that the isotherms of the catalysts correspond to type IV, typically associated with mesoporous materials42. For all samples, the hysteresis loops are classified as type H4 according to the IUPAC classification. H4-type hysteresis loops typically appear in materials with a combination of microporous and mesoporous structures43.

N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms of the catalyst with various MgO loadings.

Figure 3 illustrates the pore size distribution of these catalysts. As shown, increasing the MgO loading from 45% to 85% causes a noticeable shift in the pore size distribution toward larger values.

Pore size distribution of the catalyst with various MgO loadings.

The high-intensity peak observed for the 5% MgO sample in Fig. 3 reflects a well-defined mesoporous structure, due to minimal pore blocking at this low MgO loading. At 5% MgO, the incorporation of MgO into the Al2O3 support appears to preserve the original mesopore distribution, resulting in a sharper and more intense peak compared to higher loadings. For instance, at intermediate (e.g., 45%) and higher (e.g., 85%) MgO loadings, the peak intensity decreases, and the distribution broadens, which can be attributed to pore filling or partial blockage by excess MgO. This preservation of mesoporosity at 5% MgO is consistent with the relatively high BET surface area (325.6 m²/g) reported in Table 1, suggesting that the pore structure remains accessible and contributes to the distinct peak.

Temperature-programmed reduction (TPR)

The temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) analysis of the catalysts, shown in Fig. 4, reveals three principal peaks for all samples except the one with 25% MgO loading44. These peaks provide valuable insights into the interaction between NiO species and the support, which is essential for understanding the reducibility of the catalysts and their performance in dry methane reforming (DRM) reactions. First peak (400–500 °C): This peak corresponds to NiO species with weak interaction with the support. The largest peak area is observed in the 45% MgO catalyst, indicating a higher concentration of such species.

TPR analysis of catalysts with different MgO loadings.

The prominence of this peak in the 45% MgO sample suggests an optimal balance of MgO loading that maximizes the presence of these reducible NiO species. In contrast, the 25% MgO sample shows a moderate peak intensity, indicating a moderate presence of weakly interacting NiO, while the 5% MgO sample exhibits a significantly lower intensity, possibly due to a shift toward stronger interactions or early NiAl₂O₄ formation. The presence of these species suggests a higher potential for easy reduction, which is often linked to better catalytic activity in DRM reactions. This is because weaker interactions allow for easier reduction, and nickel sites become readily available for methane cracking and reforming. The intensity and position of this peak can vary with MgO loading, reflecting differences in NiO dispersion and support interaction strength across all samples. For example, the lower intensity in the 25% MgO sample compared to 45% MgO highlights a potential transition in NiO behavior, while the 85% MgO sample’s reduced peak suggests a more complex interaction, which is related to the formation of NiAl₂O₄ species as MgO loading increases.

Second peak (700–800 °C): This peak corresponds to NiO species with a stronger interaction with the support. For the 5%, 45%, 65%, and 85% MgO loadings, this peak shifts to lower temperatures, indicating higher reducibility of the nickel oxide. However, the 25% MgO sample does not exhibit this peak, which suggests that NiO either has weak interactions with the support or predominantly forms NiAl₂O₄ spinel species, which have a different reducibility behavior.

Third peak (above 800 °C): This peak corresponds to the reduction of NiAl₂O₄ spinel species45. For MgO loadings above 5%, the peaks related to NiAl₂O₄ shift toward higher temperatures, indicating decreased reducibility of the catalysts. This is significant because the presence of spinel phases can reduce catalytic activity, as nickel sites are less available for the methane reforming reaction. The 5% MgO sample shows the largest peak area for the NiAl₂O₄ phase, suggesting a significant portion of NiO is locked in the spinel structure. This can reduce the availability of nickel for the dry reforming reaction. Therefore, understanding the reducibility of NiO and the formation of NiAl₂O₄ is crucial for optimizing catalyst design, as these factors directly influence the activity and stability of the catalysts during the reaction. Finally, the TPR results provide important insights into the nature of nickel species and their interaction with the support. The reducibility of NiO and the formation of NiAl₂O₄ phases are key factors in determining the catalytic performance of the catalysts in DRM reactions. Catalysts that have higher amounts of reducible NiO species and less NiAl₂O₄ formation are expected to exhibit better performance in dry methane reforming.

Catalytic performance

The catalytic activity of the prepared samples was investigated, and the results presented in Fig. 5a and b indicate that the catalytic conversion does not necessarily increase with higher MgO loading in the catalysts. While increasing the MgO loading up to 25% had minimal effect, further incorporation of MgO up to 45% enhanced the methane conversion rate, achieving the highest performance for the 10% NiO-45%MgO-Al₂O₃ catalyst among all samples. However, increasing the MgO content beyond 45% to 85% resulted in a decline in methane conversion. This decrease can be attributed to a significant reduction in the specific surface area caused by excessive pore filling with MgO and a decrease in catalyst reducibility, as confirmed by the TPR results for this sample. Due to the endothermic nature of the reforming reaction, the conversion rate increases with rising temperature41. In all cases, the conversion rate of CO₂ is higher than that of CH₄, which can be attributed to the occurrence of the reverse water-gas shift reaction38. The H₂/CO ratio, as shown in Fig. 5c, is less than 1 for all samples due to the influence of the reverse water-gas shift reaction. The stability of the catalysts was evaluated at 700 °C over a period of approximately 16 h, with the results presented in Fig. 6. All samples, except for the 10%NiO-85%MgO-Al₂O₃ catalyst, exhibit considerable stability. In the 10%NiO-85%MgO-Al₂O₃ sample, a significant decline in both CH₄ and CO₂ conversions over time can be attributed to surface poisoning by carbon deposits formed as by-products of side reactions. This undesirable coke accumulation blocks the pores, reducing the accessible surface area for reactants and consequently causing a near-linear decrease in catalytic performance. Based on the catalytic performance and stability test results, the 10%NiO-45%MgO-Al₂O₃ catalyst emerges as the most suitable sample.

Coke formation

The temperature-programmed oxidation (TPO) profiles of the spent samples with varying MgO loadings are shown in Fig. 7. No peaks are observed in the range of 100–200 °C for any of the samples, indicating the absence of weakly bonded carbon species. In the range of 300–500 °C, a peak is observed only in the 10%NiO-65%MgO-Al₂O₃ catalyst, which corresponds to amorphous carbon that exhibits a stronger interaction with the catalyst surface45. The presence of this peak suggests that moderate MgO loading promotes some level of carbon formation, likely due to incomplete suppression of the CO decomposition reaction. In the range of 600–700 °C, a distinct peak appears in all samples, associated with filamentous carbon. This type of carbon is typically formed through the decomposition of methane or CO and grows in a fibrous structure that can interfere with catalyst performance. The TPO results reveal that increasing MgO loading enhances the basicity of the catalyst, which reduces the progression of the CO decomposition reaction, the primary pathway for carbon formation. This effect leads to a significant reduction in carbon deposition, particularly in samples with higher MgO content46. These findings demonstrate that MgO loading plays a crucial role in mitigating carbon deposition and improving the overall stability of the catalysts during the reaction.

TPO analysis of the spent catalysts with different loadings of MgO.

SEM images of the spent catalysts with varying MgO loadings, shown in Fig. 8, illustrate that the increased incorporation of MgO into the catalyst structure enhances the basicity characteristics of the catalyst. This enhancement significantly reduces the formation of filamentous carbon on the catalyst surface. The observed morphological trend in carbon deposition aligns well with the findings from the TPO analysis, confirming that higher MgO loading effectively mitigates carbon formation during the reaction.

Conclusion

In summary, this study investigated a series of alumina-based catalysts with varying MgO loadings (5, 25, 45, 65, and 85%), with a constant 10% NiO loading, synthesized via a simple and scalable method, for their performance in the dry reforming of methane (DRM). This study demonstrates that MgO plays a crucial role in reducing carbon deposition during the dry reforming of methane over Ni-based catalysts. The optimization of MgO loading significantly improves catalyst stability and reduces coke formation, thereby enhancing the catalyst’s longevity and performance. BET analysis indicated that increasing the MgO loading led to a decrease in the specific surface area, with a minimum in pore volume and pore size observed for the 45% MgO sample. TPR results showed that higher MgO loadings caused a shift in the reduction peaks of NiAl2O4 species to higher temperatures, suggesting a decrease in catalyst reducibility. The reactor tests demonstrated that increasing MgO loading up to 25% had negligible effect on catalytic performance, while loadings of 45% and 65% enhanced CH4 conversion. However, further increasing the MgO loading to 85% resulted in a decrease in CH4 conversion. Stability tests conducted over 16 h at 700 °C under reaction conditions showed similar trends to the activity tests. TPO and SEM analyses revealed that as MgO loading increased, the basicity of the catalysts improved, limiting the CO decomposition reaction, recognized as the main pathway for carbon deposition. Based on these findings, the 10%NiO-45%MgO-Al2O3 catalyst, with a methane conversion of 66.57% at 700 °C, was identified as the most effective catalyst due to its superior CH4 conversions, excellent long-term stability against coke formation, enhanced basicity, improved reducibility of NiO leading to higher accessible reduced nickel, and high surface area, which facilitated better Ni dispersion and increased active site density.
