Cocaine facilitates Ca2+ fluctuations in prefrontal cortex astrocytes via norepinephrine transmission in awake mice
Authors: Suhua Sun, Xi Wu, Min Gao, Jie Li, Liyuan Sun, Lun Wang, Guoqing Chen, Qinglong Wang, Yingfei Xiong, Yang Lu, Bo Zhou, Bing Liu, Quanfeng Zhang, Changhe Wang, Feipeng Zhu, Zhuan Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Astrocytic Ca2+ activity is crucial for maintaining normal brain function. However, the pathophysiological role of astrocytes in the context of addictive drugs remains largely unknown. Taking advantage of two-photon Ca2+ imaging in awake mice, we show that a physiologically relevant level of cocaine induces synchronized and hyperactive Ca2+ signals in frontal cortical astrocytes. Mechanistically, this process occurs primarily through the volume release of norepinephrine from locus coeruleus innervation, followed by the activation of Gq-coupled α1 adrenergic receptors in the prefrontal cortex and the subsequent IP3R2-dependent store Ca2+ release, thus causing hyperactive Ca2+ fluctuations in astrocytes. Importantly, interrupting the hyperactive astrocytic Ca2+ signals either by blocking store Ca2+ release or by conditionally knocking out the astrocytic α1AR rescued FrA neuronal activity and aggravated cocaine-induced locomotor sensitization. These findings revealed that, in addition to neurons, astrocytes can be super-activated by cocaine through the crosstalk between norepinephrine nerves and glia, providing a new perspective for the understanding and clinical treatment of cocaine addiction.

Introduction

Astrocytes are the most abundant glial cells in the brain, at least equaling or exceeding neurons in number [1]. They are highly branched and interact with many cell types, including neurons, other glial cells, and blood vessels [2]. Elaborate intracellular Ca2+ signaling is a major characteristic of astrocytes, allowing them to communicate with adjacent cells. The subsequent release of gliotransmitters following the elevated Ca2+ concentration plays critical roles in modulating synaptic transmission, synaptic plasticity, and brain blood flow [3–6]. However, circuit mechanisms mediating astrocytic Ca2+ fluctuations and how gliotransmission contributes to the modulation of emotion and behaviors remain largely unknown [7, 8].

Based on the subcellular area, astrocytic Ca2+ signals can be divided into global Ca2+ fluctuations occurring in the soma and dynamic Ca2+ changes spatially restricted to the fine processes, termed Ca2+ microdomains. Different from the soma, astrocyte processes generate Ca2+ fluctuations randomly [9], and the events occur much more frequently than in the soma [10–12]. The astrocytic Ca2+ signals can be attributed by either extracellular Ca2+ influx or inositol triphosphate type 2 receptor (IP3R2)-mediated store Ca2+ release [13]. However, whether and how these two types of Ca2+ sources contribute differentially to gliotransmission in different sub-cellular regions remains unclear. The physiological importance of astrocytic Ca2+ fluctuations in the soma and processes remains poorly understood.

Astrocytic Ca2+ fluctuations have been shown to occur in response to a wide array of neurotransmitters including norepinephrine (NE) and dopamine (DA). The Ca2+ signals of astrocytes are increased by electrical stimulation of the locus coeruleus (LC) in awake mice and this response is occluded by NE receptor inhibitors [14, 15], suggesting a role of LCNE neurons in the regulation of astrocytes. LCNE neurons provide abundant projections to the prefrontal cortex (PFC), where they release NE diffusely from axonal varicosities [16] and regulate brain functions including arousal, attention, and memory. Importantly, LCNE neurons are one of the primary targets of cocaine and are closely associated with cocaine stimulant and relapse behaviors [17–19]. We have developed a micro carbon fiber-based electrochemical recording electrode ProCFE and recorded the first NE quantum release events from mammalian adrenal chromaffin cells and LC slices in situ, with which we strikingly found that, in addition to blocking the reuptake of NE, cocaine also plays critical roles in facilitating the quantal NE secretion via NE transporter (NET)-dependent protein kinase C activation in LCNE neurons [19–22]. However, whether and how the increased NE release contributes to cocaine-related compulsive behaviors remains largely unknown.

Cocaine addiction is a chronic disease associated with adaptive changes in neural activity in discrete brain structures [23]. Previous research on cocaine addiction has mainly focused on the adaptive changes that occur in neurons, but little is known about the activity changes of astrocytes and their contribution to cocaine addiction. A recent study showed that astrocytes mediate synaptogenesis in the nucleus accumbens in response to cocaine experience and embed critical cue-associated memory traces that promote cocaine relapse behaviors [24]. This provides an insight that astrocytes are of critical importance to cocaine-associated behaviors. However, it remains an open question as to how astrocytes are regulated by cocaine, and the molecular and circuit mechanisms for astrocytic Ca2+ signals in response to cocaine are also unclear.

To address this question, we used two-photon Ca2+ imaging to monitor astrocyte activity during cocaine administration in anterior dorsal lateral part of the PFC as this brain region is highly associated with cocaine-induced sensitization [25]. Since astrocyte Ca2+ signals are largely disrupted by anesthesia [26, 27], we assessed Ca2+ fluctuations with genetically-encoded Ca2+ indicators in awake mice and strikingly found that frontal association cortex (FrA) astrocytes display Ca2+ hyperactivity in response to cocaine. We further provide compelling evidence that this modulation is critically dependent on the LCNE-astrocytic α1-adrenoceptor transmission in the FrA, which mediates IP3R2-dependent intracellular store Ca2+ release in the soma and Ca2+ influx into the processes of astrocytes. Importantly, this Ca2+ hyperactivity is critical for mediating cocaine-induced locomotor sensitization through the modulation of FrA neuronal activity. Therefore, our findings not only identify distinct Ca2+ sources for the region-specific (soma vs process) excitation of astrocytes, but also establish critical roles of cortical astrocytes in cocaine addiction and define LCNE-astrocytic α1-adrenoceptor transmission as a central mechanism antagonizing cocaine-induced locomotor sensitization.

Methods

Animals

Ip3r2+/- mice were from Prof. Ju Chen [28] and maintained as a heterozygous line. Knockout and WT littermates were used for two-photon imaging when they reached postnatal days 60–90. DBH-Cre transgenic mice were from the Jackson Laboratory (JAX stock, #033951). To generate FrA astrocyte-restricted Adra1α conditional knockout mice, homozygous Adra1α-floxed null mice were obtained from Cyagen (C57BL/6J-Adra1α em1Cflox/Cya, S-CKO-01078). Eight-week-old mice were stereotactically injected with GFAP promoter-driven Cre recombinase-expressing (AAV2/5-GFAP-Cre-mCherry-WPRE-hGH polyA) or mCherry control (AAV2/5-GFAP-mCherry-WPRE-hGH polyA) virus to achieve astrocyte-specific gene deletion. All procedures complied with the standards of the Peking University Animal Use and Care Committee and the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care, including breeding and experimental manipulations. Animals were housed under a 12 h light/dark cycle at 22-26 °C (2–5 mice per individually ventilated cage) with free access to food and water. All chemicals were from Sigma unless otherwise stated.

Virus and in vivo microinjection

The AAV2/5 gfaABC1D-cyto. GCaMP6f virus was from UPenn Vector Core (AAV2/5-52925, Addgene, USA). The chemogenetic virus AAV2/9 hSyn-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry-WPRE (5.24 × 1012 vg/mL) and AAV2/9 hSyn-DIO-hM3D (Gq)-mCherry-WPRE (5.0 × 1012 vg/mL) were from BrainVTA (Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). The optogenetic virus ChR2-eYFP loxp (2.68 × 1012 vg/ ml) was from BrainVTA. The GFAP-Cre virus (AAV2/5 GFAP-Cre-mCherry-WPRE-hGH polyA, 5 × 10¹² vg/mL), the control virus (AAV2/5 GFAP -mCherry-WPRE-hGH polyA, 5 × 10¹² vg/mL), and genetically encoded NE sensor (pAAV-GfaABC1D-GRABNE, 5 × 10¹² vg/mL) were obtained from BrainVTA. The retrograde tracing virus (AAV2/2 Retro-CAG-FLEX-Flpo-WPRE-pA, 2.08 × 1013 vg/mL), the TeNT-containing virus (AAV2/9-hEF1a-fDIO-mCherry-P2A-TetTox-WPRE-pA, 1.34 × 1013 vg/mL), and the control virus (AAV2/9-hEF1a-fDIO-mCherry-WPRE-PA, 1.87 × 1013 vg/mL) were obtained from Taitool Bioscience (Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) preparation and in vivo injections were described previously. A total of 0.5 µl virus was slowly injected (Micro-4, Nanoliter 2000, WPI, USA) over 20 min into the FrA (depth 150–300 μm) through a fine glass microelectrode with a tip diameter of ~15 µm, which was made on an electrode puller (Sutter, P97). After 3 weeks of expression, mice were used for brain slice Ca2+ imaging or two-photon Ca2+ imaging.

Craniotomy for two-photon imaging in awake mice

Cranial surgery and the two-photon imaging procedure were described previously [29–31] with minor modifications. Briefly, the mouse was anesthetized with Avertin (0.25 g/kg, i.p., Sigma-Aldrich) and kept on a warming plate maintained at 37.5 °C. After sterilizing the skin with 75% alcohol, and the local application of xylocaine, skin and muscle were removed, and a custom-made chamber was glued to the skull with cyanoacrylate reinforced with dental cement. Animals were allowed to recover and received stability training in the next 7 days with increasing durations from 30 min to 2 h. Craniotomies were made under anesthesia with isoflurane on the day of recording. A small square of cranial bone (~ 2.5 × 2.5 mm) was removed with a high-speed drill (0.5 mm tip diameter) centered above the prefrontal association cortex (AP, + 2.8 mm; ML, ± 0.75 mm). The dura was carefully removed, and a small glass coverslip (2.5 × 2.5 mm) was glued onto the open cranial window. For local drug application experiments, the coverslip was smaller than the window, leaving a 0.5 × 0.5 mm gap to allow pipette access to imaging regions.

Ca2+ indicator loading and local drug application

In addition to the genetic Ca2+ indicator GCaMP6f, we also recorded Ca2+ imaging with the glia-specific Ca2+ indicator Rhod-2 AM (R1244, Thermo Fisher, USA). Rhod-2 AM (500 µM) was loaded by topical application to the pial surface for 8–10 min [32]. Specific loading of astrocytes has previously been demonstrated using histochemical and double-loading techniques [32, 33]. Rhod-2 AM specifically labeled astrocytic somata.

For local drug application, a glass pipette (TW150-4, WPI, USA) with 4–5 MΩ resistance was filled with aCSF or drugs, and 20 µM Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) was mixed with them to aid visualization of drug delivery [34]. When the pipette was close to the target region, drugs were slowly delivered at low pressure and monitored by a digital manometer gauge (AZ-8215, Shenzhen Youfu Tools Co., Ltd.).

Two-photon Ca2+ imaging in vivo

Two-photon imaging was applied on a commercial two-photon laser-scanning microscope (FV1200MPE, Olympus) at 920 nm. Ca2+ fluctuations were recorded in a 100–300 µm region below the pial surface in the FrA at 8 Hz (0.129 s/frame) under a 25 × objective (1.05 NA, Olympus). A Ca2+ fluctuation was defined as an event in which the change of fluorescence intensity (dF/F0) was >3 standard deviations above the baseline [30, 35]. Ca2+ fluctuations in astrocytes were identified and analyzed as described previously [15]. Briefly, two distinct types of subcellular Ca2+ fluctuations were defined in astrocytes: (1) Somatic fluctuations, which are localized to the cell body and the initial segments of processes extending from it. For these signals, regions of interest (ROIs) were set along with the outline of the somata; and (2) Process fluctuations, which are confined to highly restricted areas within the astrocyte processes. For these, ROIs were set in the specific areas where Ca2+ fluctuations occurred. ROIs were labeled freehand with ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), and signals were analyzed using custom-made software based on MatLab (MathWorks, USA).

Two-photon Ca2+ imaging of FrA neurons

To visualize the activity of FrA neurons, we used the Ca2+ indicator GCaMP6s delivered via viral injection. Specifically, AAV2/9-hSyn-GCaMP6s-WPRE-pA (5 × 10¹² vg/mL, BrainVTA, Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) was injected in the FrA. Three weeks post-virus injection, a chronic glass cranial window was surgically created above the FrA, and a custom-made chamber was glued onto the skull using dental cement to facilitate subsequent head-fixed imaging. One week later, Ca2+ imaging was performed on the two-photon microscope. Prior to imaging, mice were trained for 3 days to acclimate to head fixation as described above.

Preparation of brain slices and confocal Ca2+ imaging

Prefrontal cortical astrocytes were labeled by microinjection of AAV2/5-GfaABC1D-cyto. GCaMP6f virus, and 3 weeks post-virus injection, brain slices were prepared as described previously [36–38]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with urethane and then transcardially perfused with 10 ml ice-cold cutting aCSF containing (in mM): 110 C5H14ClNO, 2.5 KCl, 0.5 CaCl2, 7 MgCl2, 1.3 NaH2PO4, 25 NaHCO3, 10 glucose (saturated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2). The brain was rapidly removed and cut into saggital slices (200 μm thick) on a vibratome (Leica VT 2000S, Nussloch, Germany) at 0.1 mm/s. The slices were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in recording aCSF (in mM): 125 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 2 CaCl2, 1.3 MgCl2, 1.3 NaH2PO4, 25 NaHCO3, 10 glucose (saturated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2), and then kept at room temperature for recording.

Ca2+ images were acquired using an upright confocal microscope (LSM 700, Carl Zeiss) with a 20 × water-immersion objective (NA 1.05). Images were sampled at 1.5 Hz. For all images, astrocytes were selected from the prefrontal region ~30 μm below the slice surface. All drugs were applied using a puffer system (MPS-1, Inbio Life Science Instrument Co., Ltd, China) at 0.5 ml/min. Slices were kept in fresh aCSF using a peristaltic pump at 2–3 ml/min during recording. In the case of the cocaine and NE experiments, movies were acquired for 3 min for baseline activity, and then cocaine or NE was applied for 3 min for drug effect calculation. In the Ca2+-free experiments shown in Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S15, the conditions were the same as described above except that the standard CaCl2-containing aCSF was replaced with 1 mM EGTA-containing Ca2+-free aCSF. According to the reports on plasma concentrations of cocaine (0.4–13 µM) from drug abusers [39, 40], we chose 10 µM as the cocaine concentration used for brain slices and 5 µM for primary cultured astrocytes.

Astrocyte culture and confocal Ca2+ imaging

Primary cultures of mouse prefrontal astrocytes were prepared as described previously [20, 41] with slight modifications. Briefly, neonatal or 1-day-old Ip3r2-/- mice and littermate WT mice were anesthetized by hypothermia in ice and the brains were rapidly removed into ice-cold dissection solution containing (in mM): 137 NaCl, 5.5 KCl, 0.2 Na2HPO4, 0.2 KH2PO4, 10 HEPES, 18 glucose and 22 sucrose (pH 7.3). The isolated PFC was digested for 10 min in 0.25% trypsin and the reaction was terminated by Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Gaithersburg, MD, USA). Single cells were plated in 12.5 cm2 culture flasks (Corning, New York, NY, USA) and maintained in DMEM with 10% FBS under 5% CO2 at 37 °C, and then the medium was refreshed every 48 h. After 7–9 days in culture, the astrocytes were purified by shaking, re-plated on coverslips pre-coated with poly-L-lysine (Sigma, USA), and used for Ca2+ imaging 24 h later.

For Ca2+ imaging of cultured astrocytes, cells were bathed in standard extracellular solution (SE, in mM): 150 NaCl, 5 KCl, 2.5 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, 10 HEPES, and 10 glucose, (pH 7.4) with 5 µM Fluo-8 AM (AAT Bioquest, US) at 37 °C for 30 min, and washed three times with SE. The Ca2+ fluorescence images were acquired under a confocal microscope (LSM 710, Carl Zeiss) with a 20 × objective; the excitation wavelength was 488 nm and the emission peak was at 517 nm. ZEN 2008 software (Carl Zeiss) was used to collect imaging data at 1 Hz.

Combined optogenetics and chemogenetics with two-photon imaging

For optogenetic manipulation of LC-NE neurons, we first injected ChR2-eYFP loxp AAV virus at a dilution of 2.68 × 1012 vg/ml into the LC (AP, –5.45 mm; ML, ±1.05 mm; DV, –3.75 mm) of DBH Cre/+ mice. Then, we fixed an optical fiber (NA 0.48, 5.0 mm long and 200 µm in diameter, Newdoon Genetics Co. Ltd, China) at an angle of 30° to the horizontal (AP, –7.56; ML, ±1.05 mm; DV, –3.75 mm) to the skull with cyanoacrylate reinforced with dental cement. For optical stimulation, the implanted optical fiber was connected to a fractal laser (473 nm, VA-I-N-473, Beijing Bangshou Co., Ltd, China), controlled by a HEKA EPC900 patch clamp amplifier through an FC/PC adaptor. The stimulation parameters were: 5-ms pulses at 20 Hz and power <20 mW. During two-photon imaging, the excitation wavelength was 920 nm and fluorescence was acquired through two independent channels, one for receiving eYFP fluorescence, and the other for Ca2+ fluctuations. Astrocytic Ca2+ images were acquired at 1.109 s/frame, which was sufficient to capture evoked responses while limiting laser-induced photodamage.

For chemogenetic manipulation of LCNE neurons, DBH-Cre mice received bilateral injections of hM4D (Gi), hM3D (Gq), or mcherry (control) virus into the LC. For in vivo Ca2+ imaging, CNO (A8787, ApexBio, USA) dissolved in saline was injected (2 mg/kg i.p.) to modulate LCNE neuronal activity. Fifteen minutes after CNO injection, cocaine (15 mg/kg in saline, i.p.) was injected and Ca2+ imaging was recorded after another 15 min to assess the effects of cocaine and the manipulation of LCNE neurons.

Startle stimulation

Startle was induced by a brief and gentle air puff ( ~ 3 s) to the face of the mouse under the two-photon microscope. The puff was delivered by pressing a 10-ml syringe attached to a 0.5-mm diameter PVC tube with its opening positioned ~1 cm away from the nostril (Supplementary Fig. S2B). This experiment was slightly modified from a previous work [15].

Microdialysis-based HPLC analysis in freely-moving mice

Microdialysis surgery was performed as described previously with slight modifications [29, 42]. Adult mice were anesthetized with urethane (1.5 g/kg, i.p., Sigma-Aldrich) and mounted in a stereotaxic frame (Narishige Scientific Instrument Lab., Japan) with a mouse adapter. A guide cannula (CMA Microdialysis, Sweden) was implanted into the prefrontal cortex (AP, +2.5 mm; ML, ±0.75 mm; DV, –1.5 mm). After 24 h for recovery, a dialysis probe (CMA-7, 2 mm, Sweden) was inserted into the guide cannula (each probe had been tested through the recovery of NE on the day before use). Microdialysis experiments were performed on freely-moving mice in a circular cage (Fig. 2C). The microdialysis probe was connected to a CMA/402 syringe pump and was perfused at 1 μl/min with aCSF (in mM: 125 NaCl, 25 KCl, 2.0 CaCl2, 1.3 MgCl2, 1.3 NaH2PO3, 1.3 NaHCO3, 25 glucose). After 2 h for equilibration, a dialysate sample was collected every 20 min into a collection tube with 2 μl glacial acetic acid (3 M) to avoid monoamine oxidization. Three samples were collected before drug treatment as the baseline, then the next four samples were collected immediately after cocaine injection (15 mg/kg, i.p.). All samples were collected at 4 °C and immediately analyzed with HPLC.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was applied as described previously [29, 38] with slight modifications. Briefly, anesthetized mice were perfused with 10 ml PBS followed by 10 ml 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Then the brain was removed and post-fixed in 4% PFA overnight. After dehydration in 30% sucrose for 48 h at 4 °C, a series of coronal sections (25 µm thick) across the FrA or LC were cut on a Leica cryostat. The sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton TM X-100 in PBS containing 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA, A3858, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 30 min at room temperature. After blocking with 2% BSA, the samples were incubated with the primary antibodies (GFAP, ab4674, 1:500, Abcam; TH, AB152, 1:500, Merck Millipore) at 4 °C overnight. After three washes with PBS, the sections were incubated for 3 h with the secondary antibodies. Nuclei were visualized by DAPI staining and samples were mounted on slides with 50% glycerol. Fluorescence images were captured on a Zeiss 710 inverted confocal microscope with a 40 × oil objective. All images were captured at the same gain and offset settings, and post-collection processing was applied uniformly to all images using ImageJ.

Cocaine sensitization behavioral test

Behavioral tests were carried out with male Ip3r2-/-, Arda1α flox/flox, and their control mice aged 10–13 weeks. To initiate cocaine sensitization, mice were habituated to saline treatment (10 ml/kg i.p.) for 3 consecutive days, and then they received injections of either saline or cocaine (15 mg/kg, i.p.) for the following five days [18]. Mice were placed in open field chambers for a 15 min habituation period before injection. After drug injection, mice were immediately evaluated for horizontal locomotor activity for another 30 min in the open field chamber.

Statistical analysis

In each experiment, we recorded almost the same number of cells or slices from each animal, and each assay was applied with at least three independent biological repeats. Our sample sizes in electrophysiological, pharmacological, and histological experiments were similar to those reported in the literature [15, 29]. Outliers were identified and removed using the Z-score method with a threshold of 3 standard deviations from the mean. All mice were randomly assigned to either the treatment group or the control group by a third-party technician who was blind to the study design, using a computer-generated random number list (GraphPad Software). During data collection and quantification, experimenters were blinded to group assignment. All experiments were performed with controls side-by-side with random order. Statistical analyses were applied using GraphPad Prism 8.2.1 (GraphPad Software, Inc.). The normality of all datasets was analyzed with Prism. Group differences were assessed using two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc comparisons where appropriate. Single-variable differences were assessed with paired or unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, the Mann-Whitney test, or one-way ANOVA as indicated. For all results, data are presented as the mean ± SEM or median and quartiles. The significant difference level was set at p < 0.05, and the differences for post-hoc comparisons were set at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001. The indicated “n” represents the number of cells or animals. Statistical details can be found in the results, figures, and figure legends. All graphs were made by Prism 8.2.1, Ca2+ traces were made by Igor 6.3, and the figures were assembled in Adobe Illustrator 2020.

Results

Cocaine administration induces Ca2+ hyperactivity of astrocytes in awake mice

Repeated exposure to cocaine disturbs neurotransmission in the PFC [43, 44]. To determine whether cocaine affects glial function in this region, we used two-photon imaging to record spontaneous Ca2+ signals of astrocytes in cortical layers 2/3 of awake mice (Fig. 1A). The GfaABC1D-driven GCaMP6f-expressing AAV2/5 virus was stereotactically injected into the FrA. The morphology of astrocytes typically with a smaller soma (area 68.2 ± 2.6 µm2, diameter <10 µm) and multiple fine processes (covering 1321 ± 109 µm2) were identified by the labeling with GCaMP6f (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. S1A–C). In the basal state, the fine processes exhibited random spontaneous Ca2+ fluctuations (0.764 ± 0.039 spikes/min), while the soma rarely showed spontaneous Ca2+ fluctuations (0.128 ± 0.031 spikes/min; Fig. 1C, D, and Supplementary Fig. S1D; see also [15, 45]). Remarkably, the astrocyte Ca2+ fluctuations became hyperactive 15 min after acute cocaine treatment (15 mg/kg, i.p., Fig. 1C). Not only was the frequency of Ca2+ fluctuations increased by 3.5-fold in the soma and 0.8-fold in the processes (Fig. 1D), but also the half-height duration of the individual Ca2+ events was increased by 1.8-fold in the soma and 0.7-fold in the processes (Fig. 1E). Moreover, the amplitude of Ca2+ fluctuations was increased by 2.6-fold in the soma, but remained unchanged in the processes (Fig. 1F). More astrocytes displayed Ca2+ fluctuations in response to cocaine administration than saline (saline 21.08% vs cocaine 88.9%, Fig. 1G). In addition, cocaine increased the area of Ca2+ fluctuations within the processes (Fig. 1H, I). Therefore, the Ca2+ fluctuations in cortical astrocytes appeared to be random and occurred in small process microdomains at high frequency and with fast kinetics under normal conditions, but became larger, spread more widely along the processes, and exhibited long-lasting (~ 60 min) burst waves upon cocaine administration. Cocaine induces astrocytic Ca2+ fluctuations through NE transmission

Cocaine acts as a psychostimulant by increasing the monoamine levels in the brain through blocking monoamine transporters [19, 46]. Previous studies have revealed that the monoamines NE and DA can trigger global Ca2+ elevation in astrocytes [15, 47, 48], see also our data in Supplementary Fig. S2. Thus, we proposed that monoamines may be responsible for the occurrence of cGCa. First, we perfused cocaine onto primary cultured astrocytes and found that it failed to evoke Ca2+ signals (Fig. 2A, B), excluding a direct action of cocaine on astrocytic Ca2+ signals. Interestingly, NE but not DA evoked a robust Ca2+ signal in primary cultured astrocytes (Fig. 2A, B). In line with this observation, the cocaine-induced astrocytic Ca2+ signals were attenuated in cocaine-insensitive NE transporter transgenic mice (NET-CI mice [19], Supplementary Fig. S3), while remaining intact in cocaine-insensitive DA transporter transgenic mice (DAT-CI mice [49], Supplementary Fig. S4A, B), suggesting that NE is a potential mediator of cGCa. Given that DA is a major neurotransmitter involved in the effects of cocaine in the brain, we sought to assess its role in cGCa by lesioning dopaminergic neurons in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) via local injection of 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OH-Dopa) in the medial forebrain bundle. Tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) staining confirmed the depletion of DA nerve fibers in the FrA (Supplementary Fig. S4C-E). To further determine whether NE is functionally involved in cGCa, we assessed the NE levels in the PFC before, during, and after cocaine administration in freely-moving mice with microdialysis-based high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis, and strikingly found that cocaine increased the NE level up to ~250% of the baseline in the PFC (Fig. 2C, D). Meanwhile, with ProCFE electrochemical recording, we confirmed that cocaine potentiates quantal NE release from the somata of LC neurons (Supplementary Fig. S5). To directly assess cocaine’s effects on extracellular NE release and astrocytic Ca2+ signaling, we conducted simultaneous recordings of extracellular NE and astrocytic Ca2+ using a genetically-encoded NE sensor (pAAV-GfaABC1D-GRABNE) and an astrocyte-specific Ca2+ indicator (Rhod-2, AM) in the FrA. We found that cocaine-induced increases in extracellular NE occurred within seconds, almost in synchrony with the elevation of astrocytic Ca2+ (Supplementary Fig. S6). These findings suggest that enhanced LC–FrA NE transmission may underlie cocaine-induced astrocytic Ca2+ signaling.

We then examined the effect of NE on astrocytic Ca2+ fluctuations by local delivery of NE (100 μM) into the FrA of awake mice (Fig. 2E). NE indeed activated astrocyte Ca2+ fluctuations in a similar manner as cocaine (Fig. 2F, G). In contrast, the delivery of artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) control solution failed to do so in the same mouse (Fig. 2F and G). Consistent with this, the lesioning of NE nerves via i.p. injection of DSP-4 (50 mg/kg) greatly attenuated cGCa (Supplementary Fig. S7). To precisely disrupt NE terminals in the FrA, we employed a viral strategy incorporating neurotoxins to selectively silence LC-NE neurons that innervate the FrA (Fig. 2H–J). After silencing these LC-NE neurons, cocaine failed to induce astrocytic Ca2+ fluctuations in the FrA, while it continued to elicit such responses in control mice (Fig. 2K–M). Collectively, these in vivo data demonstrated that NE release in the FrA is necessary for cGCa.

In line with astrocytic Ca2+ recordings in awake mice, cocaine and NE, but not DA, increased the frequency of Ca2+ fluctuations in both the somatic region (Fig. 2N–Q and Supplementary Fig. S4I–K) and processes (Supplementary Fig. S8) of astrocytes in FrA brain slices. Importantly, the ability of NE or cocaine to induce astrocytic Ca2+ fluctuations is independent of neuronal activity in the FrA. This is supported by the blockade of neuronal activity using tetrodotoxin (TTX) did not impair the effects of NE on astrocytic Ca2+ fluctuations (Supplementary Fig. S9). These findings from cultured cells, FrA brain slices, and awake animals consistently suggest that cocaine elicits astrocyte Ca2+ fluctuations in the FrA primarily through NE transmission.

To further verify the role of LCNE neurons in cGCa, we also manipulated LCNE neuronal activity via chemogenetic stimulation during two-photon Ca2+ imaging in awake mice (Fig. 3E–G). Clozapine N-oxide (CNO) alone showed no effect on the basal Ca2+ fluctuations in both control and hM4Di-expressing mice (Fig. 3H, I and Supplementary Fig. S11), while CNO partially mimicked the effect of cocaine on astrocytic Ca2+ fluctuations in hM3Dq-expressing mice (Fig. 3H, I and Supplementary Fig. S11B, C), confirming that activation of LCNE neurons is sufficient to elicit astrocytic Ca2+ fluctuations. In contrast, the frequency of cGCa in the soma (Fig. 3H, I) and processes (Supplementary Fig. S11B, C) were greatly decreased by the chemogenetic inhibition of LCNE neurons. Together, these results demonstrate that LCNE–FrA transmission mediates the cocaine-evoked Ca2+ fluctuations in astrocytes.

Somatic cocaine-induced glial Ca2+ signals are mainly mediated by α1-adrenoceptors (α1-ARs)

Given that cGCa was dependent on LC-derived NE transmission, the evoked Ca2+ signals may be a consequence of adrenoreceptor activation. α1-ARs are abundantly expressed on cortical astrocytes [51, 52] and are responsible for physiological stimulus-evoked astrocytic Ca2+ signals [15, 47, 48]. We then examined the role of α1-ARs in cGCa in awake mice and found that the somatic cGCa was dramatically reduced by systemic administration of prazosin (1 mg/kg, i.p.), a specific α1-AR antagonist (Fig. 4A–C). In contrast, cGCa in the processes was not affected by prazosin (Supplementary Fig. S12A–C). Consistent with this, the somatic cGCa was also attenuated by the local perfusion of prazosin (100 μM) into the FrA (Fig. 4D–F). Similarly, prazosin inhibited the effects of cocaine and NE on the somatic Ca2+ fluctuations in FrA slices as well, leaving Ca2+ fluctuations in the processes unchanged (Fig. 4G and Supplementary Fig. S12D, E). To confirm the functional involvement of α1-ARs, we injected the GFAP-Cre virus into the FrA of α1-AR floxed null mice (C57BL/6J-Adra1αem1Cflox) to generate FrA astrocyte-restricted α1-AR cKO mice (Fig. 4H–J). We found that cocaine-induced Ca2+ fluctuations were restricted to the processes, but not the soma, of astrocytes in these α1-AR cKO mice. In contrast, both the processes and soma of astrocytes exhibited intact cGCa in control animals (Fig. 4K–M). Since nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) have been reported to mediate astrocytic Ca2+ response during fear memory persistence [53], we then performed two-photon astrocytic Ca2+ recordings in mice pretreated with either muscarinic acetylcholine receptor (mAChR) antagonist atropine or the nonselective nAChR antagonists mecamylamine. Cocaine-induced somatic Ca2+ signals depend on the IP3R2 Ca2+ store and process cGCa mainly depends on Ca2+ influx

IP3R2, which is enriched in astrocytes [51], is the primary gating receptor mediating store Ca2+ release and intracellular Ca2+ signaling [15]. IP3R2-mediated intracellular Ca2+ fluctuations would be reactive upon activation of Gq-coupled receptors, such as α1-ARs [54]. We then assessed the issue of whether cGCa depends on this Ca2+ store by using IP3R2 knockout (Ip3r2-/-) mice [55]. Strikingly, somatic cGCa was almost abolished in Ip3r2-/- mice, compared with that in wild-type (WT) mice (Fig. 5A, B). In contrast, cGCa in the processes still occurred in Ip3r2-/- mice, but at a lower frequency than in the WT (Fig. 5A, B). Moreover, the increase in the area of cocaine-elicited Ca2+ fluctuations within processes was intact in Ip3r2-/- mice, although with a smaller baseline than the WT (Fig. 5A, B, and Supplementary Fig. S14A). In addition, deletion of Ip3r2 eliminated the Ca2+ response to NE in primary cultured astrocytes (Supplementary Fig. S14B, C). Next, we examined cGCa in FrA brain slices to further explore its Ca2+ sources. Consistently, the somatic cGCa was abolished in Ip3r2-/- mice, whereas cGCa in the processes remained (Supplementary Fig. S15). In contrast, cGCa in the processes but not soma was greatly suppressed in Ca2+-free aCSF (Fig. 5C, D, and Supplementary Fig. S15A, B), suggesting that extracellular Ca2+ is involved in the cGCa in processes. Thus, somatic cGCa is dependent on the IP3R2-gated intracellular Ca2+ store, while the process of cGCa is mainly through Ca2+ influx.

To further confirm the impact of cGCa on FrA neuronal activity and cocaine sensitization, we repeated the same experimental protocol using α1-AR cKO mice, in which the α1-AR was specifically depleted from FrA astrocytes. Consistent with our previous findings, the reduction in neuronal activity induced by cocaine was partially attenuated in α1-ARs cKO mice (Fig. 6G–K). Moreover, these mice showed significantly greater hyper-sensitization to cocaine compared to control animals (Fig. 6L). Collectively, these results demonstrate that somatic cGCa may play a compensatory, self-protective role during the development of cocaine sensitization through local transmission to FrA cortical neurons.

Discussion

The abuse of cocaine is of worldwide concern, and extensive studies have concentrated on the cocaine-induced adaptation of neurons [56]. However, as the most abundant cell type in the brain, little is known about the roles of astrocytes during cocaine administration. Here, we used cutting-edge approaches to assess the changes that occur within FrA astrocytes in response to acute cocaine administration in awake mice (in vivo) and brain slices (in situ). Cocaine elicits a robust activation of astrocytes with increased Ca2+ fluctuations through the elevated transmission of LCNE neurons in the FrA, and the astrocytic Ca2+ activities play critical endogenous-protective roles in cocaine-associated sensitization behaviors.

The intracellular Ca2+ signal is one of the most important physiological indices representing the activity of astrocytes. Glial cells are susceptible, and their morphology and functions are easily affected by changes in the surrounding microenvironment [57], mechanical injury [58, 59], and anesthetics [26, 27]. Therefore, to maintain the physiological state of astrocyte activity, we were able to monitor astrocyte Ca2+ fluctuations with two-photon imaging in the awake mouse. Taking advantage of in vivo methods, we found that cocaine induced hyperactivity of astrocyte Ca2+ fluctuations through NE transmission originating from the LC (Fig. 7). This major finding is supported by the following evidence: first, systemic and local administration of cocaine immediately increased the Ca2+ fluctuations in prefrontal astrocytes in awake mice as revealed by real-time imaging (Fig. 1 and Fig. 2E–G); Second, NE but not cocaine itself triggered Ca2+ fluctuations in primary cultured prefrontal astrocytes (Fig. 2A, B); Third, lesioning of the LC with DSP-4 or selectively silencing FrA-projecting LC-NE neurons, completely abolished the effects of cocaine on astrocyte Ca2+ signaling (Fig. 2H–M and Supplementary Fig. S7); and fourth, either optogenetic or chemogenetic activation of LCNE terminals in the FrA mimicked cocaine’s effect in eliciting Ca2+ fluctuations in astrocytes, while chemogenetic inhibition of LCNE transmission in the FrA completely abolished cGCa signals (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S10, S11). Thus, these findings support the conclusion that cocaine induces hyperactivity of astrocytes by facilitating NE transmission from LC terminals in the FrA in vivo.Fig. 7Model of cocaine-induced Ca2+ fluctuations in PFC astrocytes in awake mice.I Cocaine increases extracellular NE level in the PFC by facilitating quantal NE release and blocking NE reuptake via the NE transporter (NET) at the terminals of LC-NE neurons; II-III Increased NE activates α1-ARs in the astrocyte soma, and thus elicits Ca2+ release from the IP3R2-mediated store; IV While in the processes of astrocytes, in addition to the α1AR- IP3R2 pathway, Ca2+ fluctuations are mainly dependent on extracellular Ca2+ influx.

It has been well established by us and others that cocaine increases the NE levels (via blocking NE reuptake or facilitating quantal NE release) in multiple brain regions including the PFC [60–62]. Meanwhile, NE plays a profound role in the modulation of astrocyte activity [63–65]. Our present study mechanistically links these two lines of results that cocaine increases the astrocyte activity via intermediary NE transmission, as cocaine had no direct effect on astrocytic Ca2+ fluctuations in the absence of NE neurons (Fig. 2A, B). We noted that the combination of cocaine and CNO induced more robust astrocytic Ca2+ fluctuations compared to chemogenetic activation of LC-NE neurons alone (Fig. 3I and Supplementary Fig. S11B, C). This difference is probably attributed to the dual actions of cocaine. Chemogenetic activation of LC-NE neurons primarily mimics cocaine’s effect on the potentiation of NE release in the FrA, thereby triggering astrocytic Ca2+ fluctuations. However, in addition to boosting NE release, cocaine also blocks the reuptake of extracellular NE. This dual mechanism results in higher levels of extracellular NE and a prolonged duration of NE action, which in turn leads to more pronounced and sustained astrocytic Ca2+ signaling.

Like other groups [15], we use the term process Ca2+ signals to describe Ca2+ fluctuations that are restricted to small regions of astrocytic thin processes with high-resolution imaging techniques. Meanwhile, we defined the Ca2+ fluctuations that occurred in the soma and the main process as somatic Ca2+ signals. Although NE transmission is necessary for Ca2+ fluctuations in both the soma and processes of astrocytes, they have different downstream signaling pathways in mediating Ca2+ fluctuations. Strikingly, antagonism of α1-ARs with prazosin significantly blocked somatic cGCa, but hardly affected the response of processes to cocaine (Fig. 4). These findings suggest that other adrenal receptors, such as β receptors, may participate in the process cGCa [66–68], which deserves systemic investigation in future.

The difference between cGCa in the soma and processes was also reflected in the Ca2+ sources. The somatic cGCa was almost entirely dependent on Ca2+ release from the IP3R2-gated intracellular Ca2+ store, as shown by evidence from Ip3r2-null mice that lacked somatic cGCa in vivo and in brain slices (Fig. 5). In contrast, the process cGCa still existed in Ip3r2-/- mice, but was abolished in brain slices by removing extracellular Ca2+ (Fig. 5C, D). Taken together, we conclude that somatic cGCa is dependent on NE-α1-ARs transmission and the consequent IP3R2-gated store Ca2+ release, whereas process cGCa is dependent on NE transmission-mediated extracellular Ca2+ influx in addition to the Ca2+ store. This subset of Ca2+ signals in processes that are independent of both IP3R2 and α1-ARs has also been reported in previous studies [13, 69–71]. Further experiments are needed to identify the sources of the Ca2+ fluctuations in process cGCa, which include both intracellular store Ca2+ release and extracellular Ca2+ entry.

In our observations, 70-80% of the imaged astrocytes responded to cocaine or NE; however, the proportion of astrocytes that respond under physiological conditions remains unclear. According to a recent RNA atlas [72], about 20% of astrocytes (gfap-positive cells) in the FrA express Adra1α. This discrepancy between Adra1α expression levels and in vivo Ca2+ imaging results may be attributed to several factors, including the detection sensitivity of mRNA assays, regulation at the translational or post-translational level, and experimental variables (e.g., differences between nuclear regions and the local imaged area). Furthermore, the astrocytic Ca2+ responses to NE and cocaine may involve an alternative pathway in addition to Adra1α, such as other subtypes of adrenergic receptors, which could contribute to the higher functional response observed.

Astrocytes play a crucial role in modulating fundamental neuronal functions by releasing several chemical transmitters, such as GABA, ATP, and D-serine [6, 73–75]. Therefore, the long-lasting elevation of astrocyte Ca2+ induced by cocaine may enhance gliotransmission, thereby strengthening gliotransmitter-mediated modulation of neurons. By utilizing Ip3r2-/- mouse and α1AR cKO mice, we have demonstrated that prefrontal cortex astrocytes participate in the inhibition of neuronal activity induced by cocaine and regulate drug sensitization behavior. Specifically, preventing cocaine-induced astrocyte activation through deletion of IP3R2 or conditional knockout of astrocytic α1AR resulted in disinhibition of FrA neurons and hypersensitization of mice to cocaine (Fig. 6). Our results suggest that astrocytic Ca2+ activity in the FrA acts as a critical endogenous brake on cocaine-induced sensitization, likely through interactions with local neurons. Consistent with this notion, astrocytes have been shown to modulate DA neuron activity and cocaine reward by tonically releasing GABA in the ventral tegmental area [76]. Similarly, recent work has reported that astrocytes respond to DA with Ca2+ elevation and regulate excitatory synaptic transmission in the nucleus accumbens by releasing ATP/adenosine [77]. Thus, astrocytes have the capacity to regulate neuronal functions involved in cocaine-related behaviors through various neurotransmitters and function as intermediary neuromodulators bridging NE volume transmission and compulsive behaviors. Future research should explore how NE-derived astrocyte Ca2+ signaling affects neurons, synapses, and cocaine-mediated behavioral responses.

In contrast to extensive studies illustrating the modulation of neuronal functions by astrocytes, with two-photon imaging in awake mice, the current study provides direct in vivo evidence that cocaine increases Ca2+ fluctuations in astrocytes through NE nerves in the FrA, and this plays a critical role in cocaine-induced locomotor sensitization. This neuron–glia microcircuit is of great importance to understanding the interaction between neurons and glia. Considering the broad projection of noradrenergic LCNE neurons in the brain and the well-established LCNE functions in attention, emotion, arousal, and vigilance, we speculate that astrocytes in other brain regions receiving LCNE projection may have a similar response to cocaine and other physiological or pathological stimuli. Thus, this work not only defines a novel neural-glia transmission between LCNE neurons and astrocytes in the FrA as well as their critical roles in locomotor sensitization but also sheds new light on the functional link between NE-glia transmission and the related neurological disorders, such as emotional disorders, attention deficits, and drug addiction.
