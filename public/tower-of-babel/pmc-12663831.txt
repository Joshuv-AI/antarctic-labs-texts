Demographic and Clinical Determinants of Unicondylar Vs Total Knee Arthroplasty Selection
Authors: Nikita Golovachev, Kassem Ghayyad, Ramakanth Yakkanti, Obinna Adigweme
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKnee osteoarthritis is the most common degenerative joint disease in the elderly. Depending on affected compartments, treatment options include unicondylar knee arthroplasty (UKA) or total knee arthroplasty (TKA). Studies show higher osteoarthritis incidence and disparities in arthroplasty use and outcomes among minority groups. This study aimed to compare demographic and clinical differences in UKA and TKA procedures.MethodsPatients who underwent primary UKA or TKA at 2 different institutions between 2014 and 2024 were identified. Variables compared between UKA and TKA groups included patient sex, age, body mass index (BMI), ethnicity, smoking status, comorbidities, assistance in activities of daily living (ADL), number of falls, and the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class.ResultsOf the 58,794 patients identified, 2421 received a UKA, and 56,373 received a TKA. The UKA group had a higher proportion of men and a lower average BMI. UKA patients had a higher percentage of White patients and a lower percentage of African American patients. Fewer UKA patients required assistance in ADL, and fewer had experienced falls in the past year. TKA patients had higher percentages of diabetes, heart disease, and hypertension. A higher percentage of UKA patients were ASA class II, and a lower percentage were ASA class III.ConclusionsPatients who underwent primary UKA vs TKA showed significant differences in sex, BMI, race, ADL, comorbidities, falls in the past year, and ASA class. The results suggest that UKA patients are generally younger, have lower BMI, fewer comorbidities, and better functional status compared to TKA patients.

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (OA) is the most prevalent degenerative joint disease among the elderly, affecting 33.6% of the adult population in the United States, with the prevalence expected to rise due to an aging population and increasing rates of obesity [1]. This condition leads to significant pain, disability, and a reduction in quality of life, making it a major public health concern [2]. In cases of advanced tibiofemoral OA that do not respond to conservative treatments, surgical interventions such as unicondylar knee arthroplasty (UKA) or total knee arthroplasty (TKA) may be considered, depending on the degree of involvement of the knee compartments. Compared with TKA, UKA can provide faster early recovery, less postoperative pain, and lower perioperative complication rates in appropriately selected patients with isolated medial compartment osteoarthritis [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. In addition, more patients report UKA feeling closer to a native knee than after TKA, an effect that correlates strongly with higher overall satisfaction [7].

Studies have shown both a higher prevalence of knee OA among minority populations and notable racial and ethnic differences in the rates of knee arthroplasty utilization and its outcomes [8,9]. Minority patients, particularly African Americans, were found to be less likely to receive UKA for the treatment of isolated knee OA [9]. This disparity raises concerns about equitable access to advanced surgical options and the potential for different clinical outcomes based on race and ethnicity.

Another important consideration in knee arthroplasty is the selection of UKA vs TKA, which may depend on several patient-specific factors. UKA was traditionally recommended for elderly patients over 75 years old [10]. However, with the growing demand for minimally invasive surgery and advancements in the procedure, its indications have expanded significantly. Research now suggests that younger, healthier patients with lower body mass index (BMI) and fewer comorbidities are more likely to undergo UKA, while TKA remains more common among patients with more extensive joint damage and a higher comorbidity burden [11].

Furthermore, studies suggest that TKA may provide a more predictable mid-term outcome for morbidly obese patients, as UKA has been associated with higher rates of clinical failure and component revision in this population [12]. This discrepancy in outcomes may also be attributed to various surgical factors, including the learning curve associated with UKA. The UKA procedure is technically demanding and requires significant experience to reduce failure rates, with studies indicating that outcomes improve markedly after a surgeon has performed approximately 25 cases [13]. Surgeon preference and experience thus play critical roles in decision-making, with TKA often favored due to the familiarity and predictability of outcomes.

Despite the growing body of literature, the extent to which demographic factors influence the choice of surgical treatment remains unclear. This study aims to investigate whether factors such as age, sex, BMI, comorbidities, lifestyle factors, and functional status, play a significant role in the selection of UKA vs TKA for isolated tibiofemoral OA

Material and methods

Study population

We retrospectively identified all patients who underwent a primary UKA or TKA from 2 separate institutions from January 2014 to March 2024. UKA patients were identified using the Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code 27446, while TKA patients were identified using CPT code 27447. The study was Institutional Review Board approved. Before data collection, the database was deidentified to ensure patient confidentiality and compliance with ethical standards. Patients were included if they underwent primary UKA or TKA during the specified period. Patients who received revision surgeries were excluded from the study.

At the participating institutions, the choice between UKA and TKA was guided primarily by clinical assessments performed by orthopaedic surgeons based on standard clinical indications such as compartmental involvement (isolated vs multi-compartmental osteoarthritis), joint deformity, and ligament integrity. Because the study relies on an administrative dataset that captures CPT codes but not detailed imaging or examination findings, we inferred isolated compartment disease, acceptable alignment, intact ligaments, and adequate preoperative range of motion from the surgeon’s choice of a UKA rather than a TKA.

Data collection

The data collection process encompassed several variables. Demographic information was gathered, including sex, age, BMI, and ethnicity. Lifestyle factors such as smoking status and alcohol use were also recorded. The medical history of participants was gathered, noting comorbidities like diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease, among others. Additionally, functional status was assessed by examining assistance needed in activities of daily living (ADL), the number of falls within the past year, and the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class. All variables were extracted from structured fields in the eClinicalWorks electronic medical record system, which are populated at standard clinic visits through patient-completed questionnaires. The variables had to be recorded before the primary UKA or TKA. If the variable was not present, patients were excluded from the subgroup analysis. These omissions were missing at random, as they arose from documentation gaps.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population. These statistics included the mean and standard deviation for continuous variables such as age and BMI, and percentages for categorical variables such as sex, ethnicity, smoking status, comorbidities, ADL assistance, falls, and ASA class. Comparative analyses between the UKA and TKA groups were conducted using t-tests for continuous variables to compare the means between 2 independent groups, and chi-square tests for categorical variables to assess the distribution differences between groups. Due to the large sample size, a more stringent P value threshold of <0.01 was set for statistical significance to reduce the likelihood of false positive results (type I error).

Results

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the UKA and TKA patient cohorts are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. Due to variations in the recorded variables across patient charts, the sample size differed for each category in our analysis. The largest subgroup, analyzing sex differences, included 58,876 patients, while the smallest subgroup, analyzing ADL, comprised 14,635 patients. Patients undergoing UKA were significantly younger, with a mean age of 63.1 years compared to 67.2 years for TKA patients (P < .0001) (Table 1). The UKA group had a higher percentage of men (50.3% vs 40.8%; P < .0001) and a lower mean BMI (29.2 vs 31.2 kg/m2; P < .0001). Racial distribution showed that a higher proportion of UKA patients were White (84.5% vs 81.1%; P < .0001), whereas TKA patients had a higher proportion of African Americans (10.3% vs 6.9%; P < .0001). Smoking status did not differ significantly between UKA and TKA patients, with similar proportions of current smokers (11.2% vs 12.2%; P = .35), former smokers (28.2% vs 29.2%, P = .45), and nonsmokers (60.6% vs 58.6%; P = .18) (Table 1). However, alcohol use was more prevalent in the UKA group (69.0% vs 58.3%; P < .0001).

Regarding functional status, a smaller percentage of UKA patients required assistance with ADL compared to TKA patients (7.61% vs 10.8%; P < .0001) (Table 2). In terms of comorbidities, UKA patients had a lower prevalence of diabetes (8.7% vs 15.6%; P < .0001), heart disease (10.2% vs 14.0%, P < .0001), and hypertension (43.8% vs 56.4%; P < .0001), but chronic steroid use was not significant among UKA patients (1.6% vs 2.6%; P = .02). The incidence of falls in the past year was lower in the UKA group, with 69.9% reporting no falls compared to 62.6% in the TKA group (P < .0001). Conversely, TKA patients had higher rates of one fall (26.4% vs 22.4%; P = .0008) and 2 falls (11.1% vs 2.8%; P < .0001).

Lastly, the distribution of ASA class indicated that a higher percentage of UKA patients were classified as ASA class II (64.8% vs 49.4%; P < .0001), while TKA patients had a higher proportion of ASA class III (48.5% vs 34.3%; P < .0001). There were no significant differences in the proportions of ASA class I and IV between the groups.

Discussion

This study investigated the demographic and clinical characteristics of patients receiving UKA vs TKA for isolated tibiofemoral OA, revealing significant differences between the 2 groups. While previous studies have documented demographic disparities between patients undergoing UKA and TKA [9,14], our study builds on this prior work by examining a wider range of clinical characteristics—such as ASA class, incidence of falls, and functional status—providing deeper insight into the clinical factors influencing procedure selection.

In properly selected patients, UKA has demonstrated significant advantages compared to TKA. Studies have shown that patients with primarily medial compartment OA who underwent UKA had less postoperative pain, an earlier return to work, and higher Knee Society Scores compared to a matched group who underwent TKA [15]. However, some studies resulted in lower long-term survival rates of UKA; this difference is most pronounced in cohorts with a younger mean age, suggesting that careful consideration of longevity is warranted when selecting UKA for younger, active patients [16].

In our study, patients undergoing UKA were younger, a higher percentage male, had lower mean BMI, and fewer comorbidities compared to TKA patients. Previous studies have explored the demographic and clinical characteristics associated with UKA and TKA. Kamaraju et al. found that patients undergoing UKA were more likely to be men, younger, and have fewer comorbidities than those undergoing TKA, which is consistent with our results [9]. Additionally, they observed similar racial disparities, with a higher proportion of White patients undergoing UKA compared to African Americans [9]. Hansen et al. reported that UKA patients generally had lower BMI and fewer comorbid conditions, paralleling our findings of a healthier UKA patient population [11]. Although these earlier analyses highlighted demographic differences (eg, age, sex, BMI) between UKA and TKA patients, they commonly limited their scope to narrower datasets or single-institution cohorts, reducing generalizability.

ASA class, which evaluates a patient's overall health and operative risk, is a reliable predictor of postoperative mortality and functional outcomes following hip and knee arthroplasty [17]. Additionally, it can help identify patients at risk for early failure of total hip arthroplasty, potentially necessitating revision surgery [17]. Furthermore, Schaeffer et al. found that patients with an ASA score of 3 or higher had a significantly higher likelihood of being readmitted within 30 days after surgery compared to those with lower ASA scores, concluding that ASA class is a useful predictor of readmission risk and emphasizing the need for targeted interventions for higher-risk patients [18]. Our findings that UKA patients are more likely to fall within ASA class II, while TKA patients are more commonly classified as ASA class III, provide valuable insights into the decision-making process for these procedures. This correlation likely reflects surgeon preference to select healthier, lower-risk patients for UKA, given its technical complexity, narrower clinical indications, and favorable outcomes when performed on ideal candidates. Conversely, TKA tends to be favored in patients with higher ASA classifications due to its broader suitability across diverse clinical presentations, increased predictability, and potentially reduced risk of early failure in higher-risk patients.

Functional status (ie, assistance with ADLs) differed significantly between the 2 groups, with a smaller percentage of UKA patients requiring assistance with daily living. This matters clinically, because poorer baseline ADL independence has been shown to predict worse long-term functional trajectories postoperatively. UKA patients—who typically begin more independent—demonstrate faster recovery on ADL-oriented performance tests such as the Timed-Up-and-Go and 2-minute walk [19,20].

TKA patients were also found to have a higher incidence of falls. Although specific data on falls are limited, patients who undergo UKA tend to have shorter hospital stays and fewer mobility restrictions, which may indirectly lower the risk of falls [21]. Additionally, a history of preoperative falls has been identified as a predictor of multiple postoperative falls in knee arthroplasty patients [22]. Although the preservation of cruciate ligaments and native proprioception after UKA could theoretically mitigate this risk, robust comparative data are lacking as fall risk is influenced by baseline comorbidities, disease severity, and contralateral-knee status [23]. Therefore, fall history should be weighed alongside conventional selection criteria rather than used as an independent indication for UKA.

The racial differences observed in our study, with a higher proportion of White patients receiving UKA and African American patients receiving TKA, could potentially reflect broader socioeconomic factors and disparities in access to specialized orthopaedic care. African American patients often present with more advanced diseases, thus skewing procedure selection toward TKA [24]. The reasons for delayed presentation are multifactorial, including distrust of the healthcare system, inadequacies in culturally competent healthcare, and decreased access. Additionally, less access to fellowship-trained or high-volume arthroplasty surgeons in underserved communities may further influence procedure selection as UKA is considered a more specialized and technically demanding procedure. Furthermore, African-American patients often present with more comorbidities; thus, surgeons may opt for TKA due to the perceived increased reliability of the procedure [25].

The expanded scope of this study emphasizes the need for a more nuanced approach in the decision-making process for selecting between UKA and TKA. Our findings underscore the importance of considering a patient’s overall health status, as indicated by factors such as ASA class, comorbidities, and functional capacity. Incorporating these clinical variables into the decision-making process allows for a more personalized approach, ensuring that the chosen procedure aligns more closely with the patient’s needs and postoperative risks. Physicians should consider integrating these broader clinical considerations into practice, potentially developing more comprehensive assessment protocols that account for demographic and clinical factors and optimizing the surgical treatment of isolated tibiofemoral OA. Lastly, improving factors that lead to racial disparities in knee arthroplasty may narrow the differences in UKA utilization.

The study's limitations include its retrospective design, which may introduce selection bias. Additionally, the selection criteria for UKA and TKA were physician-dependent and specific to the 2 medical institutions involved. Furthermore, it is possible that patient preference introduced further inherent selection bias. Unmeasured confounders, such as radiographic osteoarthritis grade, limb alignment, and socioeconomic status were not captured and may have influenced the reported associations. All variables being extracted from fields in the eClinicalWorks electronic record also raise the possibility of miscoding or incomplete entry. Finally, it is important to acknowledge that different surgeons and institutions, especially across various geographical regions or healthcare systems, may have distinct clinical guidelines in procedure selection.

Advantages of the study include its large sample size collected from 2 separate institutions over a 10-year period, which enhances the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the study’s dataset integrates a variety of demographic, clinical, and functional status variables—including ASA classification, history of falls, and ADL dependence—which provides unique insight into factors influencing surgical decision-making between UKA and TKA. These strengths collectively contribute to a greater understanding of patient selection criteria than prior studies and may help guide clinical practice.

Conclusions

Our study highlights significant demographic and clinical differences between patients undergoing UKA and TKA for isolated tibiofemoral OA. Younger age, male sex, lower BMI, and fewer comorbidities were associated with the selection of UKA, while TKA was more commonly chosen by older patients with higher BMI and more comorbidities. Racial disparities were also evident, with African Americans being less likely to receive UKA. These findings support previous research while expanding on functional and lifestyle factors influencing surgical selection. Importantly, it delves deeper into lifestyle and functional status factors that may influence the decision to choose UKA or TKA, providing a more nuanced understanding of the variables affecting the choice of procedure. Future studies should include prospective designs and outcome-based comparisons to further refine surgical decision-making in UKA vs TKA.
