Technical Report: Exploring Automatic Model-Checking of the Ethereum specification
Igor Konnov, Jure Kukovec, Thomas Pani, Roberto Saltini, Thanh Hai Tran

Abstract
We investigate automated model-checking of the Ethereum specification, focusing on the Accountable Safety property of the 3SF consensus protocol. We select 3SF due to its relevance and the unique challenges it poses for formal verification. Our primary tools are TLA + \textsc{TLA}^{+} for specification and the Apalache model checker for verification. Our formalization builds on the executable Python specification of 3SF. To begin, we manually translate this specification into TLA + \textsc{TLA}^{+} , revealing significant combinatorial complexity in the definition of Accountable Safety. To address this, we introduce several layers of manual abstraction: (1) replacing recursion with folds, (2) abstracting graphs with integers, and (3) decomposing chain configurations. To cross-validate, we develop encodings in SMT (CVC5) and Alloy. Despite the inherent complexity, our results demonstrate that exhaustive verification of Accountable Safety is feasible for small instances — supporting up to 7 checkpoints and 24 validator votes. Moreover, no violations of Accountable Safety are observed, even in slightly larger configurations. Beyond these findings, our study highlights the importance of manual abstraction and domain expertise in enhancing model-checking efficiency and showcases the flexibility of TLA + \textsc{TLA}^{+} for managing intricate specifications.



The specifications, scripts, and experimental results can be accessed at:

https://github.com/freespek/ssf-mc

Key Outcomes

During the course of this project, we have developed a series of specifications
in
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
, Alloy, and SMT, each representing a different level of abstraction
of the 3SF protocol. Before we delve into the technical details, we summarize
the key outcomes of the project:

Exhaustive checking of
AccountableSafety
.

Our primary
objective was to verify the
AccountableSafety
property of the 3SF
protocol. Model-checking this property proved to be computationally challenging
due to the unexpectedly high combinatorial complexity of the protocol.
Nonetheless, we performed systematic experiments across various specifications
in
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
, Alloy, and SMT (CVC5), representing both a direct translation and
different levels of abstraction of the protocol. The largest instances we
exhaustively verified to satisfy
AccountableSafety
include up to 7
checkpoints and 24 validator votes (see Table
5
in
Section
8.3
). This comprehensive verification gives us
absolute confidence that the modeled protocol satisfies

AccountableSafety
for systems up to this size.

No falsification of
AccountableSafety
.

In addition to the
instances where we conducted exhaustive model-checking, we ran experiments on
larger instances, which exceeded generous time limits and resulted in timeouts.
Even in these cases, no counterexamples to
AccountableSafety
were
found. Furthermore, in instances where we deliberately introduced bugs into the
specifications (akin to mutation testing), Apalache, Alloy and CVC5 quickly
generated counterexamples. This increases our confidence that the protocol
remains accountably safe, even for system sizes substantially larger than
those we were able to exhaustively verify.

Value of producing examples.

Even though checking accountable
safety proved to be challenging, our specifications are not limited to proving
only accountable safety. They are also quite useful for producing examples. For
instance, both Apalache and Alloy are able to quickly produce examples of
configurations that contain justified and finalized checkpoints. We highlight
this unique value of specifications that are supported by model checkers:

•

Executable specifications in Python require the user to provide program
inputs. These inputs can be also generated randomly, though in the case of
3SF, this would be challenging: We expect the probability of hitting
“interesting” values, such as producing finalizing checkpoints, to be
quite low.

•

Specifications in the languages supported by proof systems usually do
not support model finding. The TLAPS Proof System is probably a unique
exception here, as
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
provides a common playground for the prover and the
model checkers
[
14
]
.

Advantages of human expertise over automated translation.

Applying
translation rules to derive checkable specifications from existing artifacts can
serve as a valuable starting point. However, such translations often introduce
inefficiencies because they cannot fully capture the nuances of the specific
context. This can result in suboptimal performance. Therefore, while
translations provide a baseline, manually crafting specifications from the
outset is usually more effective. When relying on translated specifications, it is
essential to apply manual optimizations to ensure both accuracy and efficiency.

Value of
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
.

\textsc{TLA}^{+}
is a powerful language for specifying and
verifying distributed systems. Although our most promising experimental results
were derived from the Alloy specification, the insights gained through
iterative abstraction in
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
were indispensable.
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
enabled us to
start with an almost direct translation of the Python code and progressively
refine it into higher levels of abstraction. This iterative process provided a
deeper understanding of the protocol and laid the groundwork for the more
efficient Alloy specification. The connection between the Python specification
and the Alloy specification is definitely less obvious than the tower of
abstractions that we have built in
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
.

Introduction

2.1
Quick Intro to 3SF

At the time of writing this report, Ethereum is using Gasper
[
7
]
as the underlying consensus protocol.
In Gasper, time is divided into slots, which represent intervals during which a new block is proposed to extend the blockchain and undergoes voting.
Finalizing a block – ensuring that it is permanently added to the blockchain and cannot be reversed – typically requires 64 to 95 slots.
This delay in finality makes the network more vulnerable to potential
block reorganizations when the network conditions change,
e.g., during periods of asynchronous network conditions.
In particular, this finalization delay heightens the network’s exposure to
Maximal Extractable Value (MEV) exploits,
which could undermine the network’s integrity.
Additionally, the extended finalization period forces users to weigh the
trade-off between economic security and transaction speed.

To address these issues and speed up finality, D’Amato et al.
[
9
]
have recently introduced the
3-slot-finality
(3SF) protocols
for Ethereum that achieve finality within three slots after a proposal, hence realizing 3-slot finality.
This feature is particularly beneficial in practical scenarios where periods of synchrony and robust honest participation often
last much longer than the time needed for finalization in the 3SF protocol.
Finally, the 3SF protocol enhances the practicality of large-scale blockchain networks by enabling the dynamically-available component,
which handles honest participants who may go offline and come back online
[
20
]
,
to recover from extended asynchrony, provided at least two-thirds of validators remain honest and online for sufficient time.

To that end, the 3SF protocols combine a partially synchronous finality gadget with two dynamically available consensus protocols –
synchronous protocols that ensure safety and liveness even with fluctuating validator participation levels.
This design is based on the
ebb-and-flow
approach introduced in
[
18
]
.
An ebb-and-flow protocol comprises two sub-protocols, each with its own confirmation rule, and each outputting a chain, with one serving
as a prefix of the other.
The first confirmation rule defines what is known as the
available chain
, which provides liveness under dynamic participation
(and synchrony).
The second confirmation rule defines the
finalized chain
, and provides safety even under network partitions, but loses liveness
either under asynchrony or in case of fluctuation in the participation level.

3
Basic 3SF concepts

In this section, we summarize the basic concepts of the 3SF protocol that this project depends on.
We refer the reader to the 3SF paper
[
9
]
for a more comprehensive explanation.

Validators.

Participants of the protocol are referred to as
validators
with
n
being their total number.
Every validator is identified by a unique cryptographic identity and the public keys are common knowledge.
Each validator has a
stake
but for the purpose of this project each validator’s stake is set to 1.

Slots.

Time is divided into
slots
.
In ideal conditions a new block (see below) is expected to be proposed at the beginning of each slot.

Blocks and Chains.

A
block
is a pair of elements, denoted as
B=(b,p)
. Here,
b
represents the
block body
– essentially, the main content of the block which contains a batch of transactions grouped together.
Each block body contains a reference pointing to its
parent
block.
The second element of the pair,
p\geq 0
, indicates the
slot
where the block
B
is proposed.
By definition, if
B_{p}
is the parent of
B
, then
B_{p}.p<B.p
.
The
genesis block
is the only block that does not have a parent. Its slot is
p=0
.
Given the definition above, each different block
B
implicitly identifies a different finite
chain
of blocks starting from block
B
, down to the genesis block, by recursively moving from a block to its parent.
Hence, there is no real distinction between a block and the chain that it identifies.
So, by chain
\mathsf{ch}
, we mean the chain identified by the block
\mathsf{ch}
.
We write
\mathsf{ch}_{1}\preceq\mathsf{ch}_{2}
to mean that
\mathsf{ch}_{1}
is a non-strict prefix of
\mathsf{ch}_{2}
.
We say that
\mathsf{ch}_{1}

conflicts
with
\mathsf{ch}_{2}
if and only if neither
\mathsf{ch}_{1}\preceq\mathsf{ch}_{2}
nor
\mathsf{ch}_{2}\preceq\mathsf{ch}_{1}
holds.

Checkpoints.

In the protocol described in
[
9
]
, a
checkpoint
is a tuple
(\mathsf{ch},c)
, where
\mathsf{ch}
is a chain and
c
is a slot signifying where
\mathsf{ch}
is proposed for justification (this concept is introduced and explained below).
However, for efficiency reasons, in the specification targeted by this project, a
valid
checkpoint
\mathcal{C}
is a triple
(H,c,p)
where
H
is the hash of a chain
\mathsf{ch}
,
c
is the slot at which chain
\mathsf{ch}
is proposed for justification, as per the definition above, and
p=\mathsf{ch}.p
.
The total pre-order among checkpoints is defined:

\mathcal{C}\leq\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
if and only if either
\mathcal{C}.c<\mathcal{C}^{\prime}.c
or, in the case where
\mathcal{C}.c=\mathcal{C}^{\prime}.c
, then
\mathcal{C}.p\leq\mathcal{C}^{\prime}.p
.
Also,
\mathcal{C}<\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
means
\mathcal{C}\leq\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\land\mathcal{C}\neq\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.

FFG Votes.

Validators cast two main types of votes:
ffg-vote
s and
vote
s.
Each
vote
includes an
ffg-vote
.
The specification targeted by this project only deals with
ffg-vote
s as the extra information included in
vote
s has no impact on AccountableSafety.
An
ffg-vote
is represented as a tuple
[\textsc{ffg-vote},\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2},v_{i}]
, where
v_{i}
is the validator sending the
ffg-vote
2
2

2

Digital signatures are employed to ensure that
v_{i}
is the actual sender and it is assumed that such digital signatures are unforgeable.
, while
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
are checkpoints.
These checkpoints are respectively referred to as the
source
(
\mathcal{C}_{1}
) and the
target
(
\mathcal{C}_{2}
) of the
ffg-vote
.
Such an
ffg-vote
is
valid

if and only if both checkpoints are valid,

\mathcal{C}_{1}.c<\mathcal{C}_{2}.c
and
\mathcal{C}_{1}.\mathsf{ch}\preceq\mathcal{C}_{2}.\mathsf{ch}
.

ffg-vote
s effectively act as
links
connecting the source and target checkpoints. Sometimes the whole
ffg-vote
is simply denoted as
\mathcal{C}_{1}\to\mathcal{C}_{2}
.

Justification.

A set of
ffg-vote
s is a
supermajority set
if it contains valid
ffg-vote
s from at least
\frac{2}{3}n
distinct validators.
A checkpoint
\mathcal{C}
is considered
justified
if it either corresponds to the genesis block, i.e.,
\mathcal{C}=(B_{\text{genesis}},0)
, or if there exists a supermajority set of links
\{\mathcal{C}_{i}\to\mathcal{C}_{j}\}
satisfying the following conditions. First, for each link
\mathcal{C}_{i}\to\mathcal{C}_{j}
in the set,
\mathcal{C}_{i}\to\mathcal{C}_{j}
is valid and
\mathcal{C}_{i}.\mathsf{ch}\preceq\mathcal{C}.\mathsf{ch}\preceq\mathcal{C}_{j}.\mathsf{ch}
. Moreover, all source checkpoints
\mathcal{C}_{i}
in these links need to be already justified, and the checkpoint slot of
\mathcal{C}_{j}
needs to be the same as that of
\mathcal{C}
(
\mathcal{C}_{j}.c=\mathcal{C}.c
), for every
j
. It is important to note that the source and target chain may vary across different votes.

Finality.

A checkpoint
\mathcal{C}
is
finalized
if it is justified and there exists a supermajority link with source
\mathcal{C}
and potentially different targets
\mathcal{C}_{j}
where
\mathcal{C}_{j}.c=\mathcal{C}.c+1
. A chain
\mathsf{ch}
is finalized if there exists a finalized checkpoint
\mathcal{C}
with
\mathsf{ch}=\mathcal{C}.\mathsf{ch}
. The checkpoint
\mathcal{C}=(B_{\text{genesis}},0)
is finalized by definition.

Slashing.

A validator
v_{i}
is subject to slashing for sending two
distinct

ffg-vote
s
\mathcal{C}_{1}\to\mathcal{C}_{2}
and
\mathcal{C}_{3}\to\mathcal{C}_{4}
if either of the following conditions holds:
\mathbf{E_{1}}
(Double voting) if
\mathcal{C}_{2}.c=\mathcal{C}_{4}.c
, implying that a validator must not cast distinct
ffg-vote
s for the same checkpoint slot; or
\mathbf{E_{2}}
(Surround voting) if
\mathcal{C}_{3}<\mathcal{C}_{1}

and
\mathcal{C}_{2}.c<\mathcal{C}_{4}.c
, indicating that a validator must not vote using a lower checkpoint as source and must avoid voting within the span of its other votes.

Definition 1

(AccountableSafety)
.

AccountableSafety holds if and only if, upon two conflicting chains being finalized,
by having access to all messages sent, it is possible to slash at least
\frac{n}{3}
of the validators.

3.1
Complexity of (model-checking) the protocol

The 3SF protocol is intricate, with a high degree of combinatorial complexity,
making it challenging for automatic model checking. During our specification
work, we have observed multiple layers of complexity in the protocol:

•

The Python specification considers all possible graphs over all proposed
blocks. From graph theory
[
8
]
, we know that the number
of labelled rooted forests on
n
vertices is
{(n+1)}^{n-1}
. (Observe that
this number grows faster than the factorial
n!
.) This is the number of
possible block graphs that the model checker has to consider for
n
blocks.

•

The protocol introduces a directed graph of checkpoints (pairs
(b,n)

of a block
b
and an integer
n
)
on top
of the block graph.
Validator-signed votes form a third labeled directed graph over pairs of
checkpoints. In addition, all of these edges have to satisfy arithmetic
constraints.

•

Justified and finalized checkpoints introduce an inductive structure
that the model checker has to reason about. Essentially, the solvers have to
reason about chains of checkpoints on top of chains of blocks.

•

Finally, the protocol introduces set cardinalities, both for determining a
quorum of validators and as a threshold for
AccountableSafety
.
Cardinalities are known to be a source of inefficiency in automated
reasoning.

The Python specification consists of class / type definitions and a number of
functions that define the behavior of the 3SF protocol. We obtain
\textit{Spec}_{1}
by
performing a manual but mechanical translation of the Python specification to

\textsc{TLA}^{+}

and the program logic elements that it defines. We then translate these
functions to
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
by following the principle of least surprise: we aimed to
preserve the syntax and semantics of the Python code as closely as possible –
at this stage, without regard to model-checking efficiency. We also translate
the data structures and types used in the Python code to their
\textsc{TLA}^{+}

counterparts. To this end, we have produced detailed translation rules, which we
present in Appendix

.

4.1
Shape of the Python Specification

A few key points to note about the Python code are as follows:

Data structures and types.

Type definitions come in the form of
Python classes annotated with the
@dataclass
decorator, which
automatically generates constructors, comparator methods, and other boilerplate
code. Composite types are defined using the
pyrsistent
library, which
provides immutable data structures such as sets and maps.

Functions.

The function definitions are pure functions that
take some input and return some output – side-effects are rare and limited to
local state. Some functions are
(mutually) recursive, as can be seen in Figure
2
.

Similarities between Python and
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
.

Despite being a programming
language and specification language respectively, Python specifications written
in a functional manner (using immutable data types and pure functions) and

\textsc{TLA}^{+}
overlap somewhat in what they can express. For instance, both allow us
to express sets, structures / records, and comprehensions over these data
structures. For example, the Python code defines a record

which we translate to a record in
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
.

4.3
Translating Complex Operators

Some further constructs of the Python language are particularly
relevant for the translation, and we describe how we handle them in the next
paragraphs. We do, however, not give explicit rules for the translation of

all
Python language primitives to
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
in general, since attempting to
establish those for the full language would vastly exceed the scope of this
project.

Assignments and local variables.

There are certain idiosyncrasies to
do with the fact that Python is imperative and executable, and
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
is not.
For instance, Python allows for arbitrary variable assignment and reassignment,
as well as the introduction of local variables. There are two constructs
available in
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
which can be used to express variable assignment:

•

a state-variable update
a^{\prime}=e

•

a LET-IN local operator definition
\mathrm{LET}\;v\;\mathrel{\smash{{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}}}}\;e\;\mathrm{IN}\;f

In principle, it is up to the translator to evaluate which of the two better
captures the semantics of the Python code. However, as we have noted, the Python
code is mostly functional over immutable data structures. In this project, we
have observed that we can establish a clear separation, which always translates
local/auxiliary variables to LET-IN definitions, and keeps the state-variable
updates for the state of the system.

Runtime exceptions.

Python code may throw at any time.
In this project in particular, the Python code is written in a defensive
manner, with many runtime checks in the form of
Requires
assertions
that perform as preconditions.
In general, this behavior is impossible to replicate without very convoluted

\textsc{TLA}^{+}
code, so we either have to omit those assertions, or return an
unspecified value of the correct type if the requirement is not met.
We note, however, that in the present Python specification, functions are only
called if their preconditions are met (for example, the function

get

parent(block, node

state)
contains a precondition

Requires(has

parent(block, node

state))
, but is only ever called on
code paths where this precondition holds). Thus, we can safely omit these
assertions in the translation.

Control flow.

Given the functional nature of the Python
specification, and given that both languages support if-statements, those
translate directly (although since

equivalent, we have to chain two IF-ELSEs in
\textsc{TLA}^{+}

As we have mentioned, the Python code contains a number

\textsc{TLA}^{+}

at the language level, which must be explicitly annotated with the keyword

However, Apalache does not support recursive

5.1
Translating Recursive
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
Operators

The primary goal of
\textit{Spec}_{2}
is to maintain the semantic structure of

\textit{Spec}_{1}
while eliminating recursion. In
\textit{Spec}_{1}
, (mutually) recursive
operators model key aspects of protocol behavior, such as the block tree and
block justification. Apalache does not natively support recursive
operators
3
3

3

https://apalache-mc.org/docs/apalache/principles/recursive.html
,
thus it cannot be used immediately to model-check
\textit{Spec}_{1}
. While the
explicit-state
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
model checker TLC supports recursive operators, it does
not scale to model-checking of this problem.

To resolve this, we reformulate
\textit{Spec}_{1}
into
\textit{Spec}_{2}
, by substituting
(mutually) recursive constructs with bounded

fold
operations
4
4

4

In functional programming,
fold
(sometimes
reduce
) is a
higher-order function that accepts a combining operation and an iterable data
structure, and applies the operation to each element of the data structure
to compute a single return value.
, which enable the same iterative
computations to be performed in a non-recursive manner. We provide a set of
translation rules to convert recursive operators to bounded
fold

operations, see Appendix
A.2
.

Let’s reconsider the example in Figure

Figure
6
, we present its equivalent that is using
folds. Following the rules in Appendix
A.2
, we have
constructed an accumulator

that is passed to the fold
operator. Its body represents a single iteration of the recursive definition: it
first checks if its second parameter, indicating if an ancestor-descendant match
has already been found, is
TRUE
– in this case, it simply propagates
the result
Pair(last

block, TRUE)
to the next iteration (this is
equivalent to the base case of the recursive definition). If the match has not
been found, it checks if the current block is the genesis block or an orphaned
block, and returns
FALSE
if it is. Otherwise, it retrieves the parent
block of the current block, checks if it is the ancestor block, and passes

Pair(parent, parent = ancestor)
to the next iteration. We pass this
accumulator to the fold operator, together with the initial value and the
structure to fold over (in this case, a generic sequence of length

MAX

the chain).

6
\textit{Spec}_{3}
: Adding a State Machine

In the course of writing
\textit{Spec}_{2}
, we realized
that the executable Python specification is essentially sequential. In other
words, even though the 3SF algorithm is distributed, its Python
specification as well as
\textit{Spec}_{1}
and
\textit{Spec}_{2}
are encoding
all possible protocol states in a single specification state.

6.1
State Machines in
\textsc{TLA}^{+}

Since
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
is designed for reasoning about state machines, Apalache is tuned
towards incremental model checking of the executions. For instance, if a state
machine is composed of
n
kinds of state-transitions (called actions), that
is,
\mathit{Next}=A_{1}\vee\dots\vee A_{N}
, the model checker tries to find
a violation to a state invariant
Inv
by assuming that a single
symbolic transition
A_{i}
took place. If there is no violation, that instance
of the invariant
Inv
can be discarded. By doing so, the model
checker reduces the number of constraints for the SMT solver to process. The
same applies to checking an inductive invariant. When there is no such
decomposition of
\mathit{Next}
, the model checker produces harder problems
for SMT.

Symbolic simulation.

In addition to model checking, Apalache offers
support for symbolic simulation. In this mode, the model checker
non-deterministically selects
one
symbolic transition at each step and
applies it to the current state. While symbolic simulation is not exhaustive
like model checking, it allows for more efficient exploration of deep system
states. Importantly, any counterexample discovered through symbolic simulation
is just as valid as one found via exhaustive model checking.

6.2
Introducing a State Machine

Having the observations above in mind, we introduce
\textit{Spec}_{3}
in which we
specify a state machine that incrementally builds possible protocol states by
constructing the following data structures:

•

The set of proposed blocks, and the graph containing these blocks,
called
blocks
and
graph
, respectively.

•

The ancestor-descendant relation, called

block

graph

closure
.

•

The announced FFG votes and the validators’ votes on them, called

ffg

votes
and
votes
, respectively.

•

The set of justified checkpoints that is computed as the greatest
fixpoint, called
justified

checkpoints
.

The most essential part of the specification is shown in
Figure

\textit{Spec}_{4}
: Two Chains in
\textsc{TLA}^{+}

We obtain this specification by preserving the vote and checkpoint behavior from
\textit{Spec}_{3}
, but we restrict the block-graph to exactly two chains, which are allowed to fork, i.e., they always share a common prefix.
To this end, we restrict block bodies of the two chains as follows:

1.

Block bodies on the first chain have non-negative sequential integer numbers starting from 0 (genesis), e.g.
0,1,2,3,4
.

2.

Block bodies on the second chain are the same as the ones on the first chain
up to the fork point
, after which they change sign, but otherwise maintain their sequence in absolute value terms, e.g.
0,1,2,-3,-4
.

The aim of this encoding is to decrease complexity for the SMT solver, since it simplifies block graph ancestry and closure reasoning significantly (e.g. we can check whether one block is an ancestor of another by simply comparing block body integer values), while preserving the behavior which arises from voting on checkpoint justification.

9.1
Inductive Invariant

This specification defines an inductive invariant
IndInv
. Recall, an inductive invariant satisfies the following two conditions, assuming we have an initial-state predicate
Init
, and transition predicate
Next
:

1.

It is implied by the initial state:
\mathit{Init}\Rightarrow\mathit{IndInv}

2.

It is preserved by the transition predicate:
\mathit{IndInv}\land\mathit{Next}\Rightarrow\mathit{IndInv}

Because of this characterization, inductive invariants lend themselves especially nicely to bounded symbolic model checking; with Apalache, one can prove (or disprove) an inductive invariant by running two queries of depth at most 1, corresponding to the above properties.
If no violation is found, we are assured that
IndInv
holds in all possible reachable states.
Since our goal is ultimately to prove or disprove
AccountableSafety
, we can additionally prove
\mathit{IndInv}\Rightarrow\mathit{AccountableSafety}
.

The challenge, typically, is that inductive invariants are more difficult to write, compared to state invariants.
They are usually composed of several lemmas, i.e. properties that we are less interested in on their own, but which are crucial in establishing property (2.).

As we explain below, the inductive invariant introduced in
\textit{Spec}_{4}
mainly consists of two sets of lemmas, one for characterizing the chain-fork scenarios, and a second one for characterizing justified checkpoints, shown in Figure
14
and Figure
15
respectively.

Figure 14
:
Chain and forking lemmas in
IndInv

Figure 15
:
Justified checkpoint lemmas in
IndInv

Since we represent a fork by means of a sign-change on block numbers, we have to specify that their absolute values are contiguous, that the chains coincide in the absence of a fork, as well as on the pre-fork prefix, and that both chains are monotone w.r.t. block numbers after the fork point.
Additionally, we require validity predicates for the vote set and checkpoints, as well as a precise characterization of the set of justified checkpoints. The latter merits further discussion.

Justified checkpoints.

To accurately describe the set of all justified checkpoints, we require two constraints:

1.

Consistency: Every checkpoint in the set is justified, and

2.

Completeness: Every justified checkpoint belongs to the set.

It is worth noting that both of these properties are required for an inductive invariant; if we do not specify consistency, the solver can trivially infer that the set contains all possible checkpoints, and that all checkpoints are finalized, which leads to bogus counterexamples to
AccountableSafety
.
On the other hand, if we don’t specify completeness, the solver can trivially infer that the set of justified checkpoints is empty (or contains exactly the genesis checkpoint). This leads us to be unable to detect real violations of
AccountableSafety
, since we can never observe conflicting finalized checkpoints, even when the votes cast necessitate their existence.

This is critical, because including both constraints places a heavy burden on the solver. No matter how we define the justification predicate, it will inevitably appear in both positive and negative form, forcing the solver to contend with both quantifier alternations and double universal quantification, both of which are known to be hard.
Fundamentally, this demonstrates the intrinsic complexity of the problem itself, regardless of the particular characterization of justified sets in
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
.

Summary & Future Work

We have presented a series of specifications modeling the 3SF protocol from
various perspectives. Initially, we developed a direct translation of the
protocol’s Python specification into
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
, but this approach proved
unsatisfactory due to the reliance on recursion. To address this, we modified
the specification to use folds in place of recursion, theoretically enabling
model-checking with Apalache. However, this approach also proved impractical
due to the high computational complexity involved. Subsequently, we applied a
series of abstractions to improve the model-checking efficiency.

In addition to the
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
specifications, we also introduced an SMT encoding
and an Alloy specification. The SMT encoding proved to be fairly performant,
while the Alloy specification demonstrated exceptional performance in
combination with the Kissat SAT solver.

Key Outcomes of the Project

.

11.2
Potential Extensions of this Project

1.

Prove some of the other properties guaranteed by 3SF.
This project focused on verifying AccountableSafety, arguably the most critical property that the 3SF protocol must satisfy.
However, it is also arguably the most challenging to verify through model checking, as it is the only property involving all honest participants that must hold true under fully asynchronous network conditions.
In contrast, proving that honest nodes never commit slashable offenses (a non-distributed system property dependent only on the behavior of a single node) or properties reliant on network synchrony, such as reorg-resilience and dynamic-availability, is expected to be simpler, albeit this would require extending the
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
encoding to include the behavior of honest nodes, which was unnecessary for verifying AccountableSafety.

2.

Generating inputs to the Python specification.
As we have noted,
the power of our
\textsc{TLA}^{+}
specifications is the ability to generate examples
with Apalache. This would help the authors of the Python specification to
produce tests for their specifications.

3.

Specifications of a refined protocol.
The current version of the
Python specification is very abstract. On one hand, it is usually
beneficial to specify a high-level abstraction. On the other hand, as we
found, the current level of abstraction is quite close to the general
inductive definitions of justified and finalized checkpoints. We expect a
refined protocol specification to be more amenable to model checking.

4.

Transferring the Alloy encoding to Apalache.
As we have found in
this project, Alloy offers richer options for fine tuning in terms of the
search scope. In combination with steady advances in SAT solving, adapting
the Alloy encoding to Apalache would improve model checking performance,
ultimately leading to a faster feedback loop and faster specification
development.

this section is quite long and technical, we have decided to add it in the
appendix.

\forall x\colon a\ .\ \forall y{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}V(x)\ .\ \gamma(y)<\gamma(x)

Recall the following definitions:

\mathrm{R}(x)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}e&;P(x)\\
G(x,F(V(x),\mathrm{R}))&;\text{otherwise}\\
\end{array}\right.,

\mathrm{R}_{m}(f)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}[x{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{f}\mapsto e]&;\forall x{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{f}\ .\ P(x)\\
\mathrm{G}_{m}(f,\mathrm{R}_{m}(\mathrm{next}(f))&;\text{otherwise}\\
\end{array}\right.

and

\mathrm{G}_{m}(f,m)[x]\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}m[x]&;x{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{m}\\
e&;x{\makebox[7.7778pt]{$\notin$}}D_{m}\land P(x)\\
G(x,F(f[x],m))&;\text{otherwise}\\
\end{array}\right.

with
D_{\mathrm{G}_{m}(f,m)}=D_{f}\cup D_{m}
.

B.2
Proofs

Lemma B.1
.

Let
f
be any function of type
a\to Set(a)
. Then

D_{f}\subseteq D_{\mathrm{R}_{m}(f)}

Proof.

Let
g\coloneqq\mathrm{R}_{m}(f)
. To prove the first part, we have two options to
consider, based on the definition of
\mathrm{R}
. If
\forall x{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{f}\ .\ P(x)
,
then
D_{f}=D_{g}
. Otherwise,
g=\mathrm{G}_{m}(f,\mathrm{R}_{m}(\mathrm{next}(f)))
. By definition,

D_{\mathrm{G}_{m}(f,m)}=D_{f}\cup D_{m}
, so it follows that
D_{f}\subseteq D_{\mathrm{G}_{m}(f,m)}
, for any
m
.
∎

Corollary B.2
.

If
D_{f}=\emptyset
, then
D_{g}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

If
D_{f}
is empty, then it is vacuously true that
\forall x{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{f}\ .\ P(x)
, and so
D_{f}=D_{g}
by definition.
∎

We define an auxiliary function
\alpha
that assigns every function of the type
a\to Set(a)
a value in
\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{-\infty\}
, defined as:

\alpha(f)\coloneqq\sup\left\{\gamma(v)\mid v{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{f}\right\}

Lemma B.3
.

Let
f
be a function, s.t. for any
x{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{f}
it is the case that
f[x]=V(x)
. Then

\alpha(f)\geq 0\Rightarrow\alpha(\mathrm{next}(f))<\alpha(f)

Proof.

Assume
\alpha(f)\geq 0
. This is trivially equivalent to saying
D_{f}\neq\emptyset
. We see that
\mathrm{next}
is defined as:

\mathrm{next}(f)[y]\coloneqq V(y)

D_{\mathrm{next}(f)}
is defined as:

D_{\mathrm{next}(f)}\coloneqq\bigcup_{v{\makebox[6.13892pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{f}}f[v]

It follows that:

\displaystyle\alpha(\mathrm{next}(f))

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\gamma(v)\mid v{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{\mathrm{next}(f)}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\gamma(v)\mid v{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}\bigcup_{w{\makebox[6.13892pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{f}}f[w]\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\gamma(v)\mid v{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}\bigcup_{w{\makebox[6.13892pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{f}}V(w)\right\}

By the precondition, we know that:

\forall y{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}V(x)\ .\ \gamma(y)<\gamma(x)

If we take an arbitrary
v{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}\bigcup_{w{\makebox[6.13892pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{f}}V(w)
, there exists a
w{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{f}
, s.t.
v{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}V(w)
. The precondition then implies, that
\gamma(v)<\gamma(w)
. Additionally, since
w{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{f}
, by the definition of
\alpha(f)
,
it follows that
\gamma(w)\leq\alpha(f)
. Since all sets in question are
finite,
D_{\mathrm{next}(f)}
is either empty, in which case
\alpha(\mathrm{next}(f))=-\infty

and the lemma trivially holds, or the supremum is actually a maximum, and
strict inequality is maintained when we infer:

\alpha(\mathrm{next}(f))=\sup\left\{\gamma(v)\mid v{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}\bigcup_{w{\makebox[6.13892pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{f}}V(w)\right\}<\alpha(f)

∎

As a trivial corollary to this lemma, we observe that
\alpha(\mathrm{next}(f))=\alpha(f)
iff
\alpha(f)=-\infty
.

Theorem
A.1
.

Let
f
be a function, s.t. for any
x{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}\textsc{domain }f
it is the case that
f[x]=V(x)
. Then, for

g\coloneqq\mathrm{R}_{m}(f)
:

\forall x{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}\textsc{domain }g\colon g[x]=\mathrm{R}(x)

Proof.

We prove this by using induction over
\alpha(f)
.

Base case
\alpha(f)=-\infty
: This can only be true if
D_{f}
is empty.
However, it is then vacuously true that:

\forall x{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{f}\ .\ P(x)

It follows from the corollary to Lemma
B.1
that
D_{g}
is empty too. In
that case, any universally quantified statement over
D_{g}
vacuously holds.

General case
: Let
\alpha(f)=N\geq 0
and assume the theorem holds for
any
f^{\prime}
, for which
\alpha(f^{\prime})<N
. We observe that
f^{\prime}\coloneqq\mathrm{next}(f)

satisfies all of the requirements. By definition, we have:

\mathrm{next}(f)[y]\coloneqq V(y)

The above holds true for every element in its domain, so the precondition of
the theorem is met. Additionally, by Lemma
B.3
, we know that

\alpha(f^{\prime})<N
, so we can use the induction assumption to conclude that:

\forall x{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{\mathrm{R}_{m}(f^{\prime})}\ .\ \mathrm{R}_{m}(f^{\prime})[x]=\mathrm{R}(x)

If it is the case that
\forall x{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{f}\ .\ P(x)
, then
\mathrm{R}_{m}(f)[x]=e
for
all domain elements, since
D_{f}=D_{\mathrm{R}_{m}(f)}
, by the same reasoning we used
in the case where
D_{f}
was empty. Similarly, for each such
x
,
\mathrm{R}(x)=e

by the definition of
\mathrm{R}
, since
P(x)
holds.

Next, we select an arbitrary
x{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{\mathrm{R}_{m}(f)}
. We look at the case split in
\mathrm{G}_{m}
:

1.

If
x{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{\mathrm{R}_{m}(f^{\prime})}
, by the induction hypothesis, we know that
\mathrm{R}_{m}(f^{\prime})[x]=\mathrm{R}(x)
.

2.

If
x{\makebox[7.7778pt]{$\notin$}}D_{\mathrm{R}_{m}(f^{\prime})}
, but
P(x)
holds, we know
\mathrm{R}(x)=e
. Here,
\mathrm{G}_{m}(f,\mathrm{R}_{m}(f^{\prime}))
trivially evaluates to
e
as well.

3.

Otherwise, it remains to be shown that the following holds true:

G(x,F(f[x],\mathrm{R}_{m}(f^{\prime})))=\mathrm{R}(x)

Since we know
P(x)
does not hold for this
x
, it follows that:

\mathrm{R}(x)=G(x,F(V(x),Op))

Hence, it suffices to see that:

F(f[x],\mathrm{R}_{m}(f^{\prime}))=F(V(x),Op)

By the property of
f
,
f[x]=V(x)
. As
x{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{f}
,
f[x]\subseteq D_{f^{\prime}}

by definition and
D_{f^{\prime}}\subseteq D_{\mathrm{R}_{m}(f^{\prime})}
by Lemma
B.1
, so for
every element
v{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}V(x)
it is the case that
\mathrm{R}(v)=\mathrm{R}_{m}(f^{\prime})[v]
. Since

F
is either a map or a filter, the equality above follows.

∎

Corollary
A.2
.

For any
x\colon a

\mathrm{R}(x)=\mathrm{R}_{m}([v{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}\{x\}\mapsto V(x)])[x]

Proof.

f\coloneqq[v{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}\{x\}\mapsto V(x)]

obviously satisfies the precondition of Theorem
A.1
:

\forall y{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{f}\ .\ f[y]=V(y)

Since
x{\makebox[7.7778pt][c]{$\in$}}D_{f}\subseteq D_{\mathrm{R}_{m}(f)}
by Lemma
B.1
, we can use Theorem
A.1
, to conclude that
\mathrm{R}_{m}(f)[x]=\mathrm{R}(x)
.
∎
