Circulation of avian influenza viruses in migratory birds in Europe and in Africa: a systematic review
Authors: Baptiste Defaye, Maeva Lamarque, Jerome Moreau, Thibaut Gelle, Christine Imbert, Denis Malvy, Laurence Delhaes, Aurélien Mercier, Sébastien Hantz, on the behalf of the EMERG Consortium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAvian influenza virus (AIV) is an increasing driver of the emergence and re-emergence of outbreaks in both animal and human populations worldwide. Its circulation is influenced by migratory birds, human activities, and climate change. Europe and Africa encompass several major migratory flyways that are undergoing substantial environmental and ecological changes. In this systematic review, conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines, we analysed published studies reporting AIV detection in migratory birds across Europe and Africa. A total of 72 publications (2005–2025) were included, comprising 53 from Europe and 19 from Africa. Overall, 10,960 migratory birds representing 166 species, 32 families, and 17 orders were reported across the three principal flyways connecting Europe and Africa: the Atlantic, Mediterranean, and Bosphorus flyways. Publication trends aligned with major HxNy outbreak over the past decade. Both low- and high-pathogenic avian influenza viruses (LPAIV and HPAIV) were detected, with a predominance of LPAIV (86% vs. 14% HPAIV). LPAIV were reported consistently throughout the study period, whereas HPAIV detections were largely confined to periods following the 2005 H5N1 outbreak and the outbreak waves after 2020. H5N1 accounted for 11% of AIV detections and was reported in 17 European countries and 4 African countries with a dominance of the clade 2.3.4.4b since 2020. Overall, this review underscores the contribution of diverse avian orders to AIV circulation – particularly in the emergence and re-emergence of H5N1 – and highlights the central role of the Mediterranean flyway in the interconnection of the 3 flyways in AIV surveillance between Europe and Africa.

Introduction

In recent years, there has been an increase in emerging and re-emerging diseases, particularly zoonotic diseases, which account for more than 60% of emerging diseases; approximately 70% of these originate from wildlife [1,2]. This trend has been observed in the Southern Hemisphere through the re-emergence of filoviruses, bunyaviruses and arenaviruses. In the Northern Hemisphere, recent increase in respiratory viruses and arboviruses diseases were described, and worldwide the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic [3]. The emergence of zoonotic pathogens is primarily driven by anthropogenic activities and climate change. These factors can alter the genetic diversity of both domestic and wild animal populations, potentially reducing their resistance to infectious agents [4–6]. They also influence animal movements and migration patterns, particularly those of migratory birds, thereby facilitating the spread and emergence of pathogens. In this context, understanding the ecology and dynamics of migratory birds is essential for assessing their potential role in the spread of zoonotic pathogens.

An estimated 2.1 billion birds migrate through Europe and Africa each year, following three main migratory flyways: the Atlantic, Mediterranean, and Bosphorus flyways. Depending on the migratory behaviour of each species, birds may undertake short-, medium-, or long-distance migrations. During migration, they periodically stop at major stopover sites, where birds from different migratory flyways may congregate, potentially facilitating pathogen transmission among migratory bird species [7–15] (Figure 1(A)). The migratory behaviour of birds may facilitate the emergence and circulation of zoonotic pathogens on a large geographical scale [16]. In addition, migratory flyways can be affected by climate change and direct anthropogenic activities. Alterations in migratory bird flyways may alter the configuration of migratory flyways, the location of stopover sites, and the availability of food resources. Such changes can modify migration routes and migratory behaviour. For example, some bird species have been observed switching from short-distance migration to a sedentary lifestyle, and vice versa [17]. Ultimately, these behavioural changes may influence the circulation of zoonotic pathogens associated with migratory birds. Among these pathogens, avian influenza viruses (AIVs) represent one of the most serious threats to animal and public health.

These viruses, responsible for avian influenza (“bird flu”), have caused severe outbreaks and pandemics in migratory birds and poultry and, more recently, have spread to marine and terrestrial mammals, including the recent spill-over into dairy cattle in the United States [18–21]. AIVs are considered global pathogens because of their worldwide distribution with no geographic boundaries; notably, H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b has even been detected in Antarctica [22]. AIVs are enveloped, single-stranded, negative-sense RNA viruses belonging to the Orthomyxoviridae family and are characterized by a high capacity for genetic evolution [23]. Their genome consists of eight RNA segments encoding ten viral proteins involved in viral replication and structure. Segments 4 and 6 encode the two surface glycoproteins used to classify AIV subtypes: haemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA), respectively [24]. Because of their segmented genome, AIVs readily undergo genetic reassortment when two or more influenza viruses infect the same cell. Reassortment between avian and mammalian influenza viruses may lead to the emergence of novel viruses with enhanced pathogenicity and host adaptation [25]. These newly emerged viruses can subsequently be disseminated by migratory birds, resulting in new outbreaks. Given this evolutionary potential and global spread, distinguishing between the different pathogenic profiles of AIVs is essential for understanding their epidemiological impact.

Consequently, AIVs are classified into two categories: low-pathogenicity avian influenza viruses (LPAIVs) and high-pathogenicity avian influenza viruses (HPAIVs) [26]. All AIV subtypes can occur as LPAIVs, which generally cause little or no pathogenicity in wild birds and poultry. LPAIVs are responsible for most influenza infections in poultry through direct transmission from wild birds. Some LPAIV subtypes have also been detected in non-avian hosts, such as the H1N1 subtype in swine [27]. In contrast, only H5 and H7 viruses can evolve from low- to high-pathogenic forms, and this transition occurs exclusively in poultry [26,28]. This transition occurs when the HA protein, which determines viral tropism and infectivity through its proteolytic cleavage, acquires a multibasic cleavage site instead of the monobasic cleavage site characteristic of LPAIVs [29]. This modification enables HA to be cleaved by ubiquitously expressed furin-like proteases throughout the host rather than by trypsin-like proteases, which are largely restricted to the respiratory and intestinal tracts. As a result, HPAIVs acquire the ability to replicate systemically, whereas LPAIV replication remains confined to these tissues [30]. The resulting HPAIVs can cause severe outbreaks, with mortality rates reaching 100% in infected wild birds and poultry [31]. Since 2005, HPAIVs have been responsible for numerous outbreaks, particularly those caused by the H5N1 subtype, affecting both wild birds and poultry [32]. More recently, H5N1 has crossed the species barrier, causing outbreaks in marine mammals with high mortality rates (56%) and infecting dairy cattle in the United States. In cattle, the H5N1 virus causes a localized infection of the mammary gland that is more similar to infections caused by low-pathogenic avian influenza (LPAIV) than to the systemic infection generally associated with highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAIV) viruses in avian hosts. Nevertheless, these viruses have acquired the capacity to spread to other mammals through the consumption of raw milk from infected cattle [21,33–35].

Therefore, wild migratory birds play a key role in the circulation of AIVs in a world increasingly shaped by anthropogenic pressures, including globalization, environmental degradation, and climate change. To better understand these dynamics, we conducted a systematic review. In this review, we aimed to provide the first comprehensive synthesis of published reports on AIVs in migratory birds across Europe and Africa (Figure 1(B)) in order to determine: The current situation of AIV in migratory birds, their evolution in the recent years, and the main families of migratory birds reported positives to the AIV; andHow HPAIV/LPAIV are distributed along three interconnected flyways, particularly the H5N1 subtype.

Methods

We conducted a systematic review of pathogens in migratory birds across Europe and Africa, focusing on AIV. The review followed PRISMA guidelines for the selection of the articles and creating exclusion/inclusion, with explicit, reproducible methods for study selection: (1) studies are related to migratory birds, (2) sampling sites are situated in African or European countries, (3) studies reports infection by pathogens, (4) study designs should be prospective, cross-sectional, retrospective, cohort, randomized controlled trials, case-control or case-series, (5) studies are published in English [36]. Articles published in English from January 2005 to December 2025 and indexed in PubMed®, African Journals Online, Scopus, and Web of Science were screened. Search terms combined migratory birds (“migratory birds” OR “wild bird” OR “waterfowl”) with pathogens (“pathogens” OR “microorganism” OR “zoonosis” OR “virus” OR “bacteria” OR “parasite” OR “fungus”) and infection terms (“infection” OR “detection” OR “disease”) within Europe or Africa. Grey literature, reviews, articles related to domestic’s birds, case reports, abstracts, and organization reports were excluded. Duplicates and inaccessible articles were removed, and titles and abstracts were screened according to predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria (Figure 2). Heterogeneity level which is an index calculated to assess the feasibility of meta-analysis was performed. Results

Of the 1,229 identified articles, 208 duplicates were removed, leaving 1,021 articles for abstract screening. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria to these abstracts, 298 articles underwent full-text review, of which 72 were excluded. Of the remaining 226 articles, 154 related to pathogens other than IAV were excluded from further analyses, resulting in the inclusion of 72 AIV-related articles (Figure 2; Supplementary Table S1). The planned meta-analysis was not conducted because of the high heterogeneity in sampling methods and sample sizes.

The included studies were published between 2005 and 2025. Most were conducted in Europe, with 53 publications covering 25 countries, compared with 19 publications from 17 African countries (Figure 3(A)). The highest numbers of studies reporting bird sampling were conducted in Sweden (9), the Netherlands (6), Italy (6), and Norway (6) in Europe, and in Egypt (7) and South Africa (5) in Africa (Figure 3(A)). Several studies were conducted across multiple countries. Among the 72 publications included in this review, a variety of sampling strategies were used, including the capture of birds with Japanese mist nets. Overall, 79% of the studies collected cloacal, tracheal, and/or oropharyngeal swabs, making these the most frequently collected sample types. Faecal samples and tissue samples were each reported in 20% of the studies, whereas blood samples were collected in only 8.3% of the publications. A total of 233,683 birds were screened for AIV, with substantial variation in sample sizes among countries in Europe (226,669 migratory birds sampled) and Africa (7,014 migratory birds sampled). Across countries, sample sizes ranged from 125,318 sampled migratory birds in the Netherlands to 14 in Libya (Figure 3(B)). Overall, 4.7% (10,785/233,683) of sampled birds tested positive for AIV, corresponding to 164 positive species from 32 families and 17 orders. Anseriformes accounted for the majority of sampled birds (>80%) and AIV-positive detections (84%), followed by Charadriiformes (13.6% of sampled birds; 9% of positive detections), Accipitriformes (2.8%; 2.7%), and Gruiformes (1.1%; 0.5%). The remaining 13 orders (Falconiformes, Pelecaniformes, Suliformes, Passeriformes, Podicipediformes, Phoenicopteriformes, Galliformes, Ciconiiformes, Strigiformes, Psittaciformes, Sphenisciformes, Cuculiformes, and Coraciiformes) each represented less than 1% of sampled birds (<200 individuals) and less than 0.5% of AIV-positive detections (<50 individuals).

Across publications, AIV-positive proportions ranged from 0.3% (sample size = 640) to 100% (sample size = 9) in Europe and from 0.6% (sample size = 467) to 100% (sample size = 2) in Africa. The annual contribution of AIV-positive migratory birds was calculated as the proportion of positive birds reported each year relative to the total number of AIV-positive birds identified during the study period (2005–2025; n = 10,785). Temporal trends revealed an initial peak following the 2005 H5N1 outbreak, a second peak in 2009 during the H1N1 pandemic, and additional increases associated with HPAIV H5N6 and H5N8 outbreaks (Figure 4). The 2016 H5N8 outbreak accounted for more than 5% of all AIV-positive detections (Europe only), whereas the 2018–2019 HPAIV H5Nx and LPAIV H9N2 outbreaks accounted for 9–14% of positive detections, predominantly in Europe. A decline in the proportion of AIV-positive birds was observed in 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic, followed by an increase during the 2021–2025 HPAIV H5N1 and H5N8 outbreaks (2.1–3.1%), mainly driven by European detections. The only exception was 2022, when Europe and Africa each accounted for 1.1% of AIV-positive detections (2.2% overall). In 2025, Europe slightly exceeded Africa in terms of the contribution of AIV-positive birds (2.14/3.61 compared with 1.47/2.14). The Atlantic flyway

The Atlantic flyway encompassed 13 countries (four in Africa and nine in Europe) and included 47 of the 72 publications, representing 161,286 birds (69.1%) with an overall AIV positivity of 3.6%. Anseriformes predominated (82% of birds; 3.6% positive), followed by Charadriiformes (16%; 15% positive) and Accipitriformes (0.4%; 1% positive). The remaining nine orders each represented <0.3% of the birds sampled and accounted for <1% of AIV-positive birds. Most samples originated from European countries (98% of the migratory birds sampled), with the Netherlands contributing 86% of the birds sampled and 54% of all AIV-positive cases (Supplementary Table S2).

Along the Atlantic flyway, 62 complete AIV subtypes were detected among 5,644 positive birds out of 161,286 sampled (Supplementary Figure S1), covering all nine neuraminidase and 14 of the 16 haemagglutinin subtypes. Serotype frequencies ranged from 0.02% to 9.4%, although >66% of positive samples were unidentified or imprecisely typed. H5Ny (16.8%) and H13Ny (5%) were the most frequently detected haemagglutinin subtypes, whereas HxN1 (11.3%) and HxN8 (4.2%) predominated among neuraminidase subtypes. H5N1 was the most frequently detected complete subtype (9.4%) (Supplementary Table S2).

The Mediterranean flyway

The Mediterranean flyway included data from 15 countries (five in Africa and 11 in Europe) across 35 of the 72 publications. A total of 66,197 birds were sampled (28.3% of all birds), with an overall AIV positivity of 6.8%. Anseriformes predominated (76% of birds; 88% of positives), followed by Accipitriformes (9%; 4.5%) and Charadriiformes (7.5%; 3.6%). Gruiformes (3.1%; 0.5%) and Falconiformes (1.8%; 0.6%) were the only other orders representing >1% of the sampled birds. The remaining 10 orders each represented only minor proportions of both the birds sampled and the AIV-positive birds (Supplementary Table S2). Most AIV-positive birds were sampled in Europe, with Denmark, Germany, Italy, and Sweden accounting for 80.1% of the birds sampled and 85.2% of the AIV-positive birds.

Among the 4,626 AIV-positive birds sampled along the Mediterranean flyway, 60 complete subtypes were identified, covering all nine neuraminidase and 15 haemagglutinin subtypes, although 73.3% of positive samples were unidentified or incompletely typed (Supplementary Figure S1). H5Ny was the most prevalent haemagglutinin subtype (>27%), whereas N1 subtypes predominated among neuraminidase subtypes (>17%). Among the fully typed subtypes, H5N1 was the most frequently detected (17.2%) (Supplementary Table S2).

The Bosphorus flyway

The Bosphorus flyway included data from 12 countries (eight in Africa and four in Europe) across 24 of the 72 publications, representing 6,139 birds (2.6% of the total), with an overall AIV positivity of 9.6%. Anseriformes predominated (85.6% of the birds sampled; 66.4% of positives), followed by Ciconiiformes (3.8%; 17%), Gruiformes (3.3%; 3%), and Galliformes (2.2%; 4.7%). The remaining 10 orders each accounted for <1% of both the birds sampled and the AIV-positive birds. Unlike the Atlantic and Mediterranean flyways, most birds sampled along the Bosphorus flyway originated from Africa (93.5% of the birds sampled; 82.7% of the AIV-positive birds) (Supplementary Table S2).

Among the 590 AIV-positive birds sampled along the Bosphorus flyway, 26 complete subtypes were identified, including 11 haemagglutinin and eight neuraminidase subtypes, whereas 43.6% of positive samples were unidentified or incompletely typed (Supplementary Figure S2). The most frequently detected haemagglutinin subtypes were H5Ny (19.6%) and H9Ny (19.1%), whereas N1 (11.7%) and N8 (8.7%) were the most common neuraminidase subtypes. Among the fully typed subtypes, H5N1 (8.6%) and H5N8 (6.1%) were the most frequently detected (Supplementary Table S2).

Trends of HPAIV and LPAIV circulation

Subtypes were classified according to their pathogenicity as either HPAIV or LPAIV. Genetically classified HPAIV, including H5 and H7 variants, represented 16% of the reported subtypes in migratory birds, whereas LPAIV, including some H5 and H7 variants as well as the remaining subtypes, accounted for 84%. In Europe, >90% of AIV were classified as LPAIV and ∼10% as HPAIV, whereas the distribution was broadly similar in Africa (84% LPAIV and 16% HPAIV) (Figure 5(B)). Among the 42 countries located along the Atlantic, Mediterranean, and Bosphorus flyways, 22 reported both HPAIV and LPAIV, 19 reported only LPAIV, whereas Hungary exclusively reported HPAIV (Figure 5(A)). The Atlantic flyway

Along the Atlantic flyway, 11% of the 5,443 AIV-positive birds with identified subtypes were infected with HPAIV (627 positive birds), whereas 89% were infected with LPAIV (4,942 positive birds) (Figure 5(B)). LPAIV was reported in all countries, representing 70–100% of AIV-positive birds, whereas HPAIV was mainly reported in the Netherlands (7.3% of 3,650 positive birds), Norway (10% of 830), Belgium (30% of 221), and Spain (3.7% of 135). Among the 55 LPAIV-positive species, 41.8% were shared with the Mediterranean flyway, 38.2% were specific to the Atlantic flyway, 1.8% were shared exclusively with the Bosphorus flyway, and 18.2% were common to all three flyways. These included Anseriformes (e.g. Anas acuta, A. clypeata, A. penelope, A. platyrhynchos, A. querquedula, Aythya ferina, and A. fuligula) and Gruiformes (Fulica atra and Gallinula chloropus) (Figure 6(A)). Publications reporting LPAIV peaked after the 2005 H5N1 outbreaks (2007–2010, 3.5–4.9%), during the H5N6/H5N8 outbreaks (2013: 3.4%; 2016: 5%), and during the H5Ny/H9N2 outbreaks (2018–2019, 9.2–12.6%) (Figure 7(A)). The Mediterranean flyway

Along the Mediterranean flyway, HPAIV accounted for 20.3% (1,231 positive birds) and LPAIV for 79.7% (3,395 positive birds) of the 4,474 AIV-positive birds, corresponding to the highest proportion of HPAIV among the three flyways (Figure 5(B)). Except for Hungary, LPAIV were reported in all countries, ranging from Czechia (7% of 15 AIV positives) to Tunisia and Slovakia (respectively 100% of 2 and 41 AIV positives). A total of 75 LPAIV-positive species were identified, representing the highest diversity among the three flyways. Of these, 37 were specific to the Mediterranean flyway, 23 were shared with the Atlantic flyway, five were shared exclusively with the Bosphorus flyway, and ten were common to all three flyways, including Anseriformes (Anas acuta, A. clypeata, A. penelope, A. platyrhynchos, A. querquedula, Aythya ferina, and A. fuligula) and Gruiformes (Fulica atra and Gallinula chloropus) (Figure 6(A)). Publications reporting LPAIV peaked following the major outbreaks of post-2005 H5N1 (2007, 6.6–15.6%), H1N1 (2009, 8.5–20.1%), H5Ny (2013, 7.5–17%), and the recent H5N1 outbreaks (41.7%) (Figure 7(A)).

HPAIV were reported in 14 of the 17 countries along the Mediterranean flyway, ranging from 3.4% in Italy (904 positive birds) to 100% in Hungary (61 positive birds). High proportions were also observed in Czechia (93% of 15 positive birds), Austria (89.8% of 246), Slovenia (80.6% of 72), and Germany (62% of 327), although these estimates were sometimes based on small numbers of positive birds, whereas Sweden showed a lower proportion (12.9% of 1,894 positive birds). Among the 46 HPAIV-positive species, 43.5% were specific to the Mediterranean flyway, 39.1% were shared with the Atlantic flyway, 6.5% were shared exclusively with the Bosphorus flyway, and 10.9% were common to all three flyways, including Anseriformes (Anas platyrhynchos, Cygnus cygnus, and C. olor), Accipitriformes (Buteo buteo), and Gruiformes (Fulica atra) (Figure 6(B)). Publications reporting HPAIV peaked during the H1N1 outbreak (2009, 4.5%) and the recent H5N1 outbreaks (2024, 1%) (Figure 7(B)).

The Bosphorus flyway

Along the Bosphorus flyway, HPAIV (69 positive birds) accounted for 10.3% and LPAIV (521 positive birds) for 89.7% of the 590 AIV-positive birds (Figure 5(B)). LPAIV were reported in all countries, with proportions ranging from 15% in Greece to 100% in eight countries (>10 positive migratory birds). Among the 41 LPAIV-positive species, 24 were specific to the Bosphorus flyway, five were shared with the Mediterranean flyway, one with the Atlantic flyway, and ten were shared between the Atlantic and Mediterranean flyways. These included Anseriformes (Anas acuta, A. clypeata, A. penelope, A. platyrhynchos, A. querquedula, Aythya ferina, and A. fuligula) and Gruiformes (Fulica atra and Gallinula chloropus) (Figure 6(A)). LPAIV detection peaked during 2009–2025 (1.4%) (Figure 7(A)).

HPAIV were reported in five of the 13 countries along the Bosphorus flyway, with the highest proportions observed in Greece (75% of 4 positive birds), South Africa (17.3% of 152), Kenya (10% of 10), Bulgaria (8.3% of 12), and Egypt (6.2% of 259). Among the 31 HPAIV-positive species, 23 were specific to the Bosphorus flyway, and three were shared exclusively with the Mediterranean flyway (Figure 6(B)). Five species were shared between the Atlantic and Mediterranean flyways, including Anseriformes (Anas platyrhynchos, Cygnus cygnus, and C. olor), Accipitriformes (Buteo buteo), and Gruiformes (Fulica atra). Peaks in HPAIV publications occurred in 2009 (0.3%) and 2022 (0.2%), with concurrent peaks across flyways following the 2005 outbreak and during recent H5Ny epizootics (2020–2025) (Figure 7(B)).

Discussion

This review synthesized 72 studies on avian influenza virus (AIV) circulation in migratory birds across Europe and Africa between 2005 and 2025, encompassing 233,683 sampled migratory birds and 10,785 AIV-positive migratory birds. To our knowledge, this is the first review to synthesize the circulation of avian influenza viruses (AIV) in migratory birds between Europe and Africa. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the geographic scope was restricted to Europe and Africa, and the classification into the three major flyways was constrained by the lack of data from Middle Eastern countries along the Bosphorus flyway. Second, to maximize temporal and geographic coverage, we conducted a systematic review rather than a full meta-analysis. Although meta-analysis could provide additional quantitative insights, the substantial heterogeneity in sampling methods, sample sizes, and temporal coverage limited its feasibility. The major disparity in sample sizes observed among countries, ranging from 125,421 to 14 sampled migratory birds, makes meta-analytic approaches and statistical comparisons inappropriate. We therefore prioritized the inclusion of studies with small sample sizes to maximize the breadth of geographic and temporal coverage rather than applying strict meta-analytic criteria.

The findings highlight a strong research focus on Europe regarding avian influenza in migratory birds, with 53 of the 72 publications originating from European countries. Research efforts were particularly concentrated in Sweden, whereas the largest number of sampled migratory birds was collected in the Netherlands (125,421), contributing to a total of 226,669 sampled birds in Europe. In contrast, fewer studies were conducted in Africa (19 publications), with Egypt and South Africa being the most investigated countries. Only 7,014 migratory birds were sampled in Africa, mainly from Egypt (2,089 birds). The limited availability of published data from sub-Saharan African settings was notable. Although this disparity cannot be fully explained, several factors may contribute to the limited prioritization of specific avian influenza virus surveillance in migratory birds. These may include political, economic, and health-related constraints, as well as the presence of other high-priority pathogens, such as Ebola virus and malaria. Consequently, these factors may reduce the resources allocated to avian influenza surveillance in migratory birds.

Limited data are available for several African countries heavily affected by AIV in both wildlife and poultry populations [40]. This is particularly relevant for West and South African countries recently impacted by HPAIV (H5N1 and H7N9), as well as for both sub-Saharan and North African countries affected by LPAIV, resulting in substantial economic losses [40]. Given the established role of migratory birds in the dissemination of HPAI H5 viruses in Africa, as well as their involvement in the intercontinental circulation of pathogens such as West Nile virus lineages 1 and 2 [41,42], expanding surveillance efforts in underrepresented African countries is crucial. Improved surveillance coverage would enhance our understanding of AIV epidemiology across a continent that hosts approximately five billion migratory birds annually and has a rapidly expanding poultry farming sector [43,44].

In our review, publication frequency appeared to be largely event-driven, with peaks occurring after major outbreaks. For example, following the HPAIV H5N1 outbreak in 2005 [45], reports of AIV-positive migratory birds increased in Europe, with publication peaks observed in 2009. More recently, the emergence of HPAIV H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b resulted in numerous reports between 2018 and 2024, although research activity declined during the COVID-19 pandemic [37,38]. LPAIV outbreaks also influenced research output; the H1N1 “swine flu” outbreak likely contributed to increased reporting in Europe (2010–2012) and Africa (2013) [37,46] while the emergence of LPAIV H9N2 in 2019 renewed scientific interest in subsequent years [38]. These patterns suggest that research on AIV in migratory birds has been conducted primarily in response to specific events (incidence or outbreaks) rather than as part of continuous surveillance programmes. This reactive approach may contribute to delayed responses to avian influenza outbreaks associated with migratory birds. However, the establishment of surveillance networks and research initiatives specifically focused on avian influenza in migratory birds – such as the EMERG project in south-western France and the Sentinel Wild Birds project [47,48] – could improve early detection, prediction, and management of future outbreaks.

A pronounced disparity in sampling among bird orders was evident. Anseriformes and Charadriiformes – representing less than 15% of the global migratory bird population – accounted for more than 90% of sampled individuals and AIV-positive detections [49], consistent with their well-established role as primary AIV reservoirs [50,51]. These orders typically undertake medium- to long-distance migrations and frequently interact with domestic poultry, thereby facilitating the circulation of diverse AIV subtypes and the emergence of novel reassortants [52,53]. In contrast, the contribution of other bird orders may be underestimated. Accipitriformes and Gruiformes were identified as carriers of HPAIV across the three flyways. In Accipitriformes, virus detection likely reflects scavenging behaviour, suggesting a potential role as spill-over indicators along migratory routes [54]. Similarly, Gruiformes represent important sentinel hosts due to their susceptibility to both LPAIV and HPAIV and their shared migratory routes with Anseriformes and Charadriiformes. Passeriformes are generally considered less affected by influenza A viruses and represented only a small proportion of sampled individuals and AIV-positive detections. However, they account for more than 60% of migratory bird species worldwide and along Europe-Africa migratory routes [49,55–57]. Consequently, they may still represent an important yet underexplored component of AIV surveillance in these regions. The marked underrepresentation of this order in available studies highlights the need for targeted field investigations focusing on under-sampled bird groups in both Africa and Europe. Such efforts would likely improve understanding of their contribution to AIV circulation and their potential implications for animal and human health along migratory flyways.

Avian influenza in three migratory flyways: specific patterns and interconnections

Data were categorized according to the three major flyways most commonly described in the literature [14,57]. Most data originated from the Atlantic (161,286 migratory birds sampled; 5,644 AIV-positive birds) and Mediterranean (66,197; 4,626) flyways, whereas fewer studies and sampled birds were available for the Bosphorus flyway (6,139; 590). Across the three flyways, the composition of AIV-positive species showed broadly similar patterns, with Anseriformes and Charadriiformes predominating. Flyway-specific characteristics were also observed, including the notable representation of Accipitriformes in the Mediterranean flyway and Ciconiiformes in the Bosphorus flyway. These findings suggest that surveillance across the three flyways can reveal distinct epidemiological patterns associated with species composition.

Nevertheless, the three flyways constitute an interconnected system, with 14 migratory species shared among them. This connectivity is particularly relevant, as H5Ny and H9Ny subtypes, among the 62 subtypes reported in our review, predominated across all flyways, driving recent outbreaks and demonstrating their potential for cross-species transmission[20,33,38,39]. The high diversity of AIV subtypes across the flyways may also promote viral reassortment, particularly in wetlands used as shared stopover sites. Notably, H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b was detected in all three major flyways, whereas clade 2.2.1 was identified in both the Mediterranean and Bosphorus flyways, and clade 2.3.2 was restricted to the Bosphorus flyway. These overlapping distributions suggest an increased potential for reassortment between these clades and the 62 LPAIV and HPAIV subtypes currently circulating in migratory birds along the Europe-Africa flyways. Both LPAIV and HPAIV were reported in comparable proportions in Europe and Africa (LPAIV: ∼85–90%; HPAIV: ∼10–15%), supporting the hypothesis of epidemiological connectivity along migratory routes. The proportion of HPAIV was higher in the Mediterranean flyway (22%) than in the Atlantic and Bosphorus flyways (9% each), possibly reflecting its central geographic position at the interface between Europe and Africa. The Mediterranean flyway also harboured the highest diversity of AIV-positive species, highlighting its importance for HPAIV surveillance. Across the three flyways, 14 shared migratory species tested positive for AIV, including 11 Anseriformes species, two Gruiformes species, and one Accipitriformes species. The widespread occurrence of Anseriformes is consistent with their recognized role as the primary reservoirs of AIV [58]. Gruiformes likely follow the migration patterns of aquatic birds [49], occupying wetlands located at flyway intersections that facilitate viral exchange [59]. Although aquatic birds are more commonly associated with LPAIV, increasing evidence indicates that they also contribute to the long-distance dissemination of HPAIV through migration and live bird trade [60,61]. Accipitriformes, while not considered natural reservoirs, may represent valuable surveillance targets because AIV has been detected in species such as Buteo buteo, whose migratory and scavenging behaviour may facilitate viral transport [54,62]. Alongside Anas platyrhynchos, Cygnus cygnus, Cygnus olor, and Fulica atra, these species should be prioritized for HPAIV surveillance along the Mediterranean flyway and across Europe-Africa migratory flyways. Collectively, these findings underscore the capacity of migratory birds to transport both LPAIV and HPAIV, thereby representing a potential risk for poultry while reinforcing their role as sentinel species in ecosystems increasingly influenced by climate change and human activities, particularly the 14 migratory species shared among the three flyways [52,63–65].

H5n1 dynamics over time: insights from migratory birds along Europe–Africa flyways

The main finding of this review is the sustained importance of H5N1, which accounted for more than 10% of all AIV-positive cases across 21 countries. H5N1 was detected in a diverse range of migratory bird orders, with Anseriformes accounting for 81.1% of all positive cases [66,67]. This predominance is likely explained by the fact that Anseriformes comprise the majority of natural avian influenza hosts among migratory birds and have therefore been the primary focus of surveillance and research compared with other migratory bird orders. However, less frequently studied orders also contributed to H5N1 detections, including Accipitriformes (Atlantic and Bosphorus flyways), Ciconiiformes (Mediterranean flyway), and Suliformes (Atlantic flyway), suggesting that broader taxonomic surveillance could improve our understanding of H5N1 circulation. Reported H5N1 positivity ranged from 0.05% to 100%, largely reflecting differences in sample size. Nevertheless, studies including large numbers of birds consistently reported H5N1 positivity below 1%, suggesting a baseline prevalence of approximately 1% across the three flyways, with no apparent dilution effect despite considerable host diversity. Most H5N1 detections occurred following major outbreaks (2005, 2018, and 2020–2024), whereas relatively few detections were reported during inter-epidemic periods. Before 2020, clade 2.2.1 spread from Asia to Europe and Africa during 2005–2006 and continued to be detected in migratory birds in 2010 and 2019, suggesting the potential for long-term circulation [68,69]. Clade 2.3.2 also expanded westward between 2009 and 2010 but was reported only sporadically [70]. Since 2020, clade 2.3.4.4b has become the dominant H5N1 lineage across migratory flyways worldwide and has been associated with numerous outbreaks in birds as well as infections in multiple mammalian species, including seals, cattle, mink, wild bears, and domestic cats, demonstrating repeated cross-species transmission events [20,33,71–73]. Although routine H5N1 surveillance is now widely implemented, detections outside epidemic periods remain limited, potentially reflecting either low-level viral circulation or under-detection. Continuous surveillance across Europe and Africa, encompassing the three major migratory flyways and with particular emphasis on underrepresented African regions, is therefore essential to assess inter-epidemic persistence and support the early detection of future outbreaks.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first review to synthesize the circulation of AIV in migratory birds between Europe and Africa. The available evidence highlights strong epidemiological connectivity between the two continents, indicating that AIV surveillance should be considered at a transcontinental scale across the Atlantic, Mediterranean, and Bosphorus flyways, with the Mediterranean flyway acting as a key epidemiological interface. Currently, avian influenza surveillance primarily focuses on waterfowl, clinical cases, and poultry farms as sentinel sites [74]. Expanding surveillance to include a broader diversity of migratory bird orders, including apparently healthy individuals, across the three major migratory flyways would improve our understanding of the role of bird migration in AIV dissemination and enhance the ability to anticipate future outbreaks. Such an approach could also provide a framework for monitoring the emergence and spread of other pathogens, particularly viruses, associated with migratory birds. Despite differences in species composition among the three flyways, several migratory bird species consistently contributed to AIV circulation, particularly for HPAIV-associated subtypes such as H5N1. Our findings reinforce the central role of migratory birds in the dissemination of both LPAIV and HPAIV along Afro-European migratory routes. However, surveillance remains largely reactive and outbreak-driven. Establishing continuous monitoring programmes targeting migratory birds, such as the EMERG project in Nouvelle-Aquitaine, south-western France [47], would strengthen early detection and improve outbreak forecasting. Expanding surveillance to encompass a greater diversity of bird species and improving geographic coverage – particularly in underrepresented regions of Africa – are becoming increasingly important as climate change and anthropogenic pressures reshape migratory systems and may influence viral circulation. Strengthening AIV surveillance in migratory birds could also support the future monitoring of other emerging pathogens associated with migratory systems, such as West Nile virus and Usutu virus, across Afro-European flyways.
