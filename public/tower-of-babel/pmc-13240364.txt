Risk Factors and Adverse Perinatal Outcomes in Severe Preeclampsia Complicated by HELLP Syndrome
Authors: Chengfang Sun, Jiaying Chen, Hao Gu, Ying Gu, Minhui Jiang, Hongqin Wu, Xue Sang, Hualong Zhu, Yaling Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study investigated the risk factors and adverse perinatal outcomes of severe preeclampsia complicated with HELLP (Hemolysis, Elevated Liver Enzymes, and Low Platelet Count syndrome). A total of 170 patients with severe preeclampsia and 30 healthy pregnant women admitted to Wuxi Maternal and Child Health Hospital from January 2020 to October 2023 were enrolled. Of the 170 patients, 30 were complicated by HELLP syndrome and 140 were not; 30 healthy women served as controls. Clinical data, laboratory results, and pregnancy outcomes were compared. Risk factors were analyzed by logistic regression, and predictive efficacy was evaluated by ROC curves. Maternal and neonatal outcomes were assessed, and correlations with risk factors were analyzed. Patients with severe preeclampsia had higher age, BMI, and blood pressure but lower gestational weeks than controls (p < 0.05). In the HELLP group, D‐dimer, bilirubin, liver enzymes, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) were significantly elevated, while platelets, albumin, and globulin were reduced compared with the non‐HELLP group (p < 0.05). Multivariate analysis identified D‐dimer and LDH as independent risk factors. ROC analysis showed AUCs of 0.929 for D‐dimer, 0.937 for LDH, and 0.956 for their combination, with cutoffs of 3.26 mg/L and 261.60 U/L, yielding 94.4% sensitivity and 92.6% specificity. Adverse outcomes, including stillbirth, preterm labor, neonatal asphyxia, and MICU transfer, were more frequent in HELLP patients (p < 0.05). Elevated D‐dimer and LDH correlated with fetal growth restriction and neonatal asphyxia. In conclusion, D‐dimer and LDH are independent predictors of HELLP syndrome and adverse perinatal outcomes.Keywords: HELLP syndrome, perinatal outcomes, risk factors, severe preeclampsia

Introduction

HELLP syndrome is a severe complication of severe preeclampsia, characterized by hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet counts. It can pose life‐threatening risks to both mothers and their newborns [1]. The incidence of HELLP syndrome ranges from 0.5% to 0.9% in healthy pregnancies, but rises to 2%–12% among patients with preeclampsia [2]. HELLP syndrome often leads to adverse outcomes such as placental abruption, disseminated intravascular coagulation, stillbirth, preterm birth, and neonatal respiratory distress syndrome [3]. Although HELLP syndrome is increasingly recognized, its rapid progression makes it difficult to predict clinically, resulting in a relatively high rate of missed or incorrect diagnoses. Therefore, we retrospectively analyzed 170 severe preeclampsia patients to identify risk factors and adverse outcomes of HELLP syndrome. Comparison of clinical data between HELLP and non‑HELLP patients aims to improve early recognition and intervention, thus enhancing maternal and perinatal outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Study Subjects

The study included 170 patients with severe preeclampsia and 30 healthy pregnant women (control group) who were admitted to Wuxi Maternal and Child Health Hospital between January 2020 and October 2023. Among the severe preeclampsia patients, 140 did not develop HELLP syndrome (non‐HELLP group), while 30 were complicated by HELLP syndrome (HELLP group). Inclusion Criteria: (1) Severe Preeclampsia: Diagnosis was based on the “Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Hypertensive Disorders in Pregnancy (2020)” issued by the Chinese Medical Association [4]. (2) HELLP Syndrome: Diagnosis was made according to the Tennessee criteria [5]. (3) Severe Preeclampsia Complicated by HELLP Syndrome: Patients who were diagnosed with both severe preeclampsia and HELLP syndrome simultaneously, or those initially diagnosed with severe preeclampsia and subsequently confirmed to have HELLP syndrome.

The specific diagnostic criteria for these two situations are as follows:

Disease Diagnostic Criteria Severe Preeclampsia: (1) Blood Pressure: ≥ 160/110 mmHg (interval of more than 4 h). (2) Proteinuria: ≥ 5 g/24 h or urine protein qualitative ≥ 3+. (3) Thrombocytopenia: < 100 × 109/L. (4) Liver Function Impairment: AST or ALT > 70 U/L. (5) Persistent Upper Abdominal or Right Upper Abdominal Pain. (6) Brain or Visual Disorders. (7) Pulmonary Edema. (8) Renal Function Impairment: Creatinine > 1.1 mg/dL or doubling from baseline. (9) Fetal Growth Restriction.

HELLP Syndrome Hemolysis: (1) Abnormal peripheral blood smear, total bilirubin ≥ 1.2 mg/dL, or LDH ≥ 600 U/L. (2) Elevated Liver Enzymes: AST ≥ 70 U/L or ALT ≥ 40 U/L. (3) Thrombocytopenia: < 100 × 109/L. (4) Diagnosis requires all three to be met (Complete HELLP)

Exclusion Criteria: Patients with chronic hypertension, gestational diabetes, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, or pregnancy complicated by hepatic or renal dysfunction, as well as other significant organic diseases, were excluded. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Jiangnan University, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Observational Parameters

The study compared the three groups of mothers in terms of general clinical characteristics, laboratory test results, and pregnancy outcomes. 1 General Clinical Data: Age, gestational age, gravidity, parity, body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP). 2 Laboratory Indicators: Platelet count (PLT), hemoglobin (HGB), plasma D‐dimer, serum creatinine (SCr), Globulin (GLB), Albumin (ALB), total bilirubin (TBil), Direct Bilirubin (DBil), Indirect Bilirubin(IBil), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase (HBDH), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). 3 Maternal and Neonatal Outcomes: Neonatal birth weight, fetal growth restriction, preterm birth, stillbirth, neonatal asphyxia, postpartum hemorrhage, and maternal transfer to the Maternal Intensive Care Unit (MICU).

Blood pressure values are the highest recorded during hospitalization before delivery or antihypertensive therapy. Additional characteristics included twin/multiple pregnancy and adverse pregnancy history (previous preeclampsia, recurrent miscarriage, where available).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov‐Smirnov test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (X̄ ± S), while non‐normally distributed data are presented as median with interquartile range (P25, P75). Given the known clinical heterogeneity of HELLP syndrome and the observed variability in laboratory parameters, all variables were assessed for normality prior to analysis. For variables with significant deviation from normal distribution (including ALT, AST, and LDH in the HELLP group), non‐parametric tests (Kruskal–Wallis H test) were applied for between‐group comparisons, and results were verified using appropriate robust statistical methods. Between‐group differences were assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed data or the Kruskal–Wallis H test for non‐normally distributed data. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage (n, %), and comparisons between groups were performed using the chi‐square (χ 2) test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Multivariate analysis was conducted using logistic regression to identify risk factors for severe preeclampsia complicated by HELLP syndrome, adjusting for potential confounding variables. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated, and combined predictive indicators were calculated using binary logistic regression. The predictive performance of individual risk factors and combined indicators for severe preeclampsia complicated by HELLP syndrome was evaluated, with the optimal cutoff value determined by the maximum Youden index. Correlation was assessed by Spearman's rank correlation. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Tables report exact p values (< 0.001 as shown, others to three decimals).

Among patients with severe preeclampsia, those complicated by HELLP syndrome had a significantly shorter gestational age compared with those without HELLP syndrome (p < 0.05). (Table 1.)

Logistic Regression Analysis of Risk Factors for Severe Preeclampsia Complicated by HELLP Syndrome

Factors that showed statistically significant differences in the univariate analysis were included as independent variables in a multivariate logistic regression, adjusting for potential confounders. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed, adjusting for all variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis, including gestational age, platelet count, D‐dimer, BUN, GLB, ALB, TBil, DBil, IBil, ALT, AST, LDH, and HBDH. However, due to multicollinearity among certain variables (e.g., DBil with TBil/IBil, HBDH with LDH) and the limited sample size of the HELLP group (n = 30), the final model retained only independent predictors that remained significant after stepwise selection and variance inflation factor diagnostics. The presence of HELLP syndrome in patients with severe preeclampsia was used as the dependent variable, coded as “1” for occurrence and “0” for non‐occurrence. The results indicated that plasma D‐dimer (OR = 2.462, p = 0.011, 95% CI: 1.229–4.934) and LDH (OR = 1.024, p = 0.002, 95%CI: 1.009–1.040) were independent risk factors for severe preeclampsia complicated by HELLP syndrome. The final binary logistic regression model was: ln(P/1‐P) = ‐11.002 + 0.901 × D‐dimer + 0.024×LDH (Table 3).

ROC Curve Analysis of D‐dimer and LDH for Predicting HELLP Syndrome Complication

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to evaluate the predictive performance of D‐dimer, LDH, and their combined indicators for the risk of severe preeclampsia complicated by HELLP syndrome. The results showed that D‐dimer had an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.929 (95% CI: 0.8727∼0.9844), with an optimal cutoff value of 3.895 mg/L. LDH had an AUC of 0.937 (95% CI: 0.8768∼0.9975), with an optimal cutoff value of 254.55 U/L. The combined D‐dimer and LDH had an AUC of 0.956 (95% CI: 0.9002∼1.000). When D‐dimer and LDH were set at 3.26 mg/L and 261.60 U/L, respectively, the combined prediction for HELLP syndrome complication yielded a sensitivity of 94.4% and a specificity of 92.6%, indicating good predictive performance (Table 4 and Figure 1).

Analysis of Adverse Perinatal Outcomes among the three Groups of Pregnant Women and Newborns

In the severe preeclampsia group, the rate of maternal transfer to the MICU was higher than in the healthy control group, and the risks of fetal growth restriction and preterm birth were also significantly increased (p < 0.05). Among patients with severe preeclampsia, those complicated by HELLP syndrome had newborns with significantly lower birth weights compared with the non‐HELLP group, and higher incidences of stillbirth, preterm birth, and neonatal asphyxia. All differences were statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Table 5). Among the 30 patients with HELLP syndrome, Spearman correlation analysis showed that elevated LDH levels were significantly associated with the occurrence of neonatal asphyxia (r = 0.500, p = 0.011) and very low birth weight infants (r = 0.638, p < 0.001). Of note, within the HELLP group, lower plasma D‑dimer levels were correlated with an increased rate of maternal transfer to the MICU (r = −0.401, p = 0.038). This negative correlation may be explained by early therapeutic interventions—such as plasma transfusion or expedited delivery—that can rapidly lower D‐dimer levels in the most critically ill patients prior to transfer, rather than suggesting a protective effect of low D‐dimer. However, given the small sample size and the post “hoc nature of this analysis, this finding should be considered hypothesis‑generating only.”

Discussion

Relationship Between Severe Preeclampsia and HELLP Syndrome

Severe preeclampsia is one of the most serious complications in obstetrics, posing significant threats to both maternal and fetal health. It also increases risks of heart failure, eclampsia, and HELLP syndrome [6]. According to Petca et al. [7], approximately 70%∼80% of HELLP syndrome cases occur in conjunction with preeclampsia, with maternal mortality ranging from 0% to 24% and perinatal mortality reaching as high as 37%. HELLP syndrome progresses rapidly and is often accompanied by serious complications such as placental abruption, pulmonary edema, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and subcapsular liver hematoma [8]. This study found that in the severe preeclampsia group complicated with HELLP syndrome, neonatal birth weight was significantly lower than in the non‐HELLP group. In addition, the risks of preterm birth, neonatal asphyxia, and stillbirth were higher, and the maternal transfer rate to the intensive care unit was also markedly increased. However, the pathogenesis of HELLP syndrome remains unclear, and its clinical manifestations lack specificity. Typical symptoms include general malaise, right upper abdominal pain, and sudden weight gain, while a minority of pregnant women may present with nausea, vomiting, and other gastrointestinal complaints, which may lead to misdiagnosis across disciplines. Thus, diagnosis depends on laboratory findings: thrombocytopenia, elevated liver enzymes, and hemolysis.

High‐Risk Factors for HELLP Syndrome in Severe Preeclampsia

Because the clinical manifestations of HELLP syndrome are nonspecific, its diagnosis still relies primarily on laboratory findings, which increases the risk of misdiagnosis or missed diagnosis. This study found that shorter gestational age, lower platelet counts, and elevated levels of D‐dimer, total bilirubin, indirect bilirubin, ALT, AST, and LDH were all associated with an increased risk of severe preeclampsia complicated by HELLP syndrome. Huang et al. [9] similarly reported that higher TBil and liver enzyme levels are associated with an increased likelihood of developing HELLP syndrome, highlighting the need for heightened clinical vigilance.

A decreased platelet count is one of the essential diagnostic criteria for HELLP syndrome [10]. Platelets are biologically active cytoplasmic fragments derived from the fragmentation of mature megakaryocytes in the bone marrow. They play a critical role in hemostasis, wound healing, thrombosis, and immune responses, including organ transplant rejection, participating in both physiological and pathological processes. Kappler S et al. [11] found that thrombocytopenia in patients with HELLP syndrome may result from an excessive maternal immune response to the placenta, leading to endothelial cell damage, exposure of subendothelial collagen, and platelet activation. This process triggers the release of endogenous adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and thromboxane A2, ultimately causing irreversible platelet aggregation and progressive platelet depletion. Therefore, platelet counts can serve as a predictor of HELLP syndrome severity and may also be used as an indicator of therapeutic response.

D‐dimer is a specific degradation product of cross‑linked fibrin following plasmin‐mediated fibrinolysis and serves as a key marker of fibrinolytic activity. Elevated D‑dimer indicates activation of coagulation and fibrinolysis. We found significantly higher D‑dimer in HELLP patients than in non‑HELLP and healthy controls. Guo et al. [12] reported that patients with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy exhibit an imbalance between anticoagulant factors and the fibrinolytic system. In HELLP syndrome, endothelial damage further activates coagulation factors, promoting thrombosis and stimulating the fibrinolytic system, which leads to elevated plasma D‐dimer levels and poses a serious threat to maternal and fetal health. Zhang et al. [13] demonstrated that changes in D‐dimer levels are closely associated with the severity of hypertensive disorders in pregnancy, suggesting that it can serve as a sensitive clinical indicator for assessing disease progression. Therefore, an elevated D‐dimer level can indicate hypercoagulability and fibrinolytic activity, serving as an early warning for the occurrence of HELLP syndrome with good sensitivity. On the other hand, an increased lactate dehydrogenase level suggests cellular tissue damage and target organ dysfunction, reflecting the severity of the condition. However, isolated elevation of lactate dehydrogenase is also commonly observed in acute myocardial infarction, liver diseases, and hematological disorders. Nevertheless, the combined elevation of both markers may reflect pregnancy‐related hypercoagulability as well as oxidative stress‐induced target organ damage, potentially complementing each other in terms of sensitivity and specificity. The findings of this study suggest that this combined diagnostic approach has potential advantages, highlighting its significant value in diagnosing HELLP syndrome.

This study found that patients with severe preeclampsia complicated by HELLP syndrome had significantly higher ALT and AST levels compared with the non‐HELLP group, while ALB levels were lower. ALT and AST are the most commonly used clinical biomarkers for hepatocellular injury. In patients with HELLP syndrome, endothelial damage leads to focal hepatocellular necrosis and hemorrhage around the portal areas and/or within the liver parenchyma. In severe cases, subcapsular or intraparenchymal hepatic bleeding may occur, ultimately resulting in elevated liver enzyme levels [14]. In addition, albumin, a plasma protein synthesized by the liver, is significantly reduced in HELLP syndrome patients due to impaired hepatic protein synthesis, compared with those without HELLP syndrome. Chen et al. [15] reported that serum albumin levels below 25 g/L are associated with disease severity and adverse perinatal outcomes.

LDH and bilirubin are important laboratory markers for the diagnosis of HELLP syndrome. This study found that patients with severe preeclampsia complicated by HELLP syndrome had significantly higher levels of LDH, TBil, and IBil compared with the non‐HELLP group. In HELLP syndrome, red blood cells undergo deformation and destruction as they pass through damaged endothelium and fibrin mesh deposits, ultimately leading to hemolysis [16]. Under normal conditions, bilirubin is primarily produced from the breakdown of aging red blood cells and is metabolized by the liver for excretion through the bile. In patients with HELLP syndrome, massive hemolysis occurs over a short period, overwhelming the liver's metabolic capacity and leading to elevated bilirubin levels, predominantly in the form of indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin [17]. LDH is an intracellular enzyme that catalyzes the interconversion of pyruvate and lactate, playing a key role in anaerobic glycolysis and gluconeogenesis. LDH is abundant in red blood cells and hepatocytes; therefore, in HELLP syndrome, hemolysis or hepatocellular necrosis leads to a marked increase in serum LDH levels [18]. Notably, LDH elevation in HELLP syndrome often occurs earlier than bilirubin elevation, making it a more sensitive predictor of disease onset [5]. Regarding LDH elevation, the underlying mechanism is multifactorial and directly linked to the pathological hallmarks of HELLP syndrome: hemolysis, hepatocellular necrosis, and ischemic tissue damage. First, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia—a defining feature of HELLP syndrome—occurs as red blood cells undergo mechanical fragmentation when passing through damaged endothelium and fibrin‐deposited microvessels, releasing intracellular LDH into the circulation [16]. Second, hepatocellular injury resulting from sinusoidal obstruction, periportal necrosis, or intraparenchymal hemorrhage leads to the release of LDH from damaged hepatocytes [14]. Third, systemic hypoperfusion and tissue ischemia in severe preeclampsia may contribute to LDH release from other organs, including the placenta and kidneys [18]. Notably, LDH elevation often precedes a significant rise in bilirubin, as hemolysis and tissue damage occur before the liver's metabolic capacity for bilirubin conjugation is overwhelmed, underscoring its utility as an early biomarker [5].'

However, it is important to note that the predictive factors identified for HELLP syndrome have varied across different studies. While our study identified D‐dimer and LDH as independent risk factors, other investigations have reported different or additional predictors. For instance, Erkılınç et al. [19] found that in addition to LDH > 1290 U/L, bilirubin > 2.0 mg/dL, and platelet count < 50 × 109/L were independent risk factors for adverse maternal outcomes in HELLP syndrome. Moharana et al. [20] emphasized the predictive value of LDH and uric acid for adverse fetomaternal outcomes, with LDH showing a stronger association with neonatal complications. Huang et al. [9] reported that elevated ALT, AST, and TBil, along with decreased PLT, were characteristic laboratory findings in HELLP syndrome, but did not identify D‐dimer as an independent predictor.

The variations in identified predictive factors across studies can be attributed to several factors. First, differences in study populations and inclusion criteria may influence results. Our study specifically focused on patients with severe preeclampsia, whereas some studies included patients with milder forms of hypertensive disorders or mixed populations. Second, the timing of laboratory measurements relative to disease onset and delivery may affect the levels of biomarkers, particularly dynamic markers like D‐dimer that can change rapidly with disease progression or intervention. Third, variations in diagnostic criteria for HELLP syndrome (Tennessee criteria versus Mississippi criteria) may lead to different patient classifications and subsequently influence which factors emerge as significant predictors. Fourth, differences in statistical approaches, including the selection of variables entered into multivariate models and adjustment for confounders, can yield different results. Finally, laboratory reference ranges and cutoff values may vary across institutions and populations, affecting the comparability of findings.

However, Yang et al. [2] pointed out that the clinical diagnostic criteria for HELLP syndrome remain controversial. Laboratory reference ranges are broad; for example, LDH ranges from 180 to 600 U/L, and platelet counts from 27 to 279 × 109/L, making the condition prone to misdiagnosis or missed diagnosis. This study found that when D‐dimer and LDH were set at 3.26 mg/L and 261.60 U/L, respectively, their combined prediction for HELLP syndrome complication yielded a sensitivity of 94.4% and a specificity of 92.6%. Therefore, elevated plasma D‐dimer levels can be used alongside other laboratory markers such as LDH and platelet counts to help predict the onset and progression of HELLP syndrome in patients with severe preeclampsia.

Analysis of Adverse Perinatal Outcomes in Severe Preeclampsia Complicated by HELLP Syndrome

HELLP patients had higher risks of stillbirth, preterm birth, neonatal asphyxia, low birth weight, and MICU transfer than non‑HELLP patients. Elevated D‑dimer and LDH correlated with fetal growth restriction and neonatal asphyxia. He et al. [21] reported that the average gestational age at delivery for HELLP syndrome patients was 28–33 weeks, with a preterm birth rate as high as 70%. Therefore, although timely termination of pregnancy is essential to ensure maternal and fetal safety, it also increases the risk of neonatal prematurity, leading to adverse outcomes such as immature fetal development and low birth weight. Wei and Zhang et al. [13, 22] demonstrated that elevated prenatal plasma D‐dimer levels significantly increase the risks of postpartum hemorrhage, placental abruption, fetal distress, and preterm birth, suggesting its potential as a predictor of adverse pregnancy outcomes. Erkılınç S et al. [19] found that bilirubin > 2.0 mg/dL, LDH > 1290 U/L, and platelet count < 50 × 109/L are independent risk factors for adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes in HELLP syndrome. Notably, when bilirubin exceeds 2.0 mg/dL, the risk of disseminated intravascular coagulation in pregnant women increases by 4.1‐fold. Moharana et al. [20] similarly reported that elevated LDH levels not only increase the risk of complications in pregnant women, such as placental abruption and disseminated intravascular coagulation, but also significantly raise the likelihood of adverse neonatal outcomes, including low birth weight, stillbirth, and admission to the neonatal intensive care unit.

Clinical Implications of Cutoff Selection

The choice of optimal cutoff values for predicting HELLP syndrome in clinical practice should be guided by the specific clinical context and the relative consequences of false‐positive versus false‐negative results [23]. In screening settings where high sensitivity is paramount to avoid missed diagnoses, a D‐dimer cutoff of 3.26 mg/L (sensitivity 97.7%) may be preferred as an initial rule‐out test. Conversely, when high specificity is needed to avoid unnecessary interventions—such as early delivery or transfer to a tertiary care center—an LDH cutoff of 261.60 U/L (specificity 92.9%) could be utilized for confirmation [24]. For general risk assessment where a balance between sensitivity and specificity is desired, the combined prediction model at the Youden index‐derived cutoff (sensitivity 94.4%, specificity 92.6%) provides excellent overall performance. This context‐dependent approach aligns with recommendations from major guidelines [25, 26] and with previous studies demonstrating that combining biomarkers improves overall discriminatory ability even when individual markers appear to have higher sensitivity or specificity at certain cutoffs [27, 28]. Clinicians may adjust these thresholds based on local practice patterns and the clinical consequences of misclassification.

In practical implementation, a two‐step strategy could be adopted: initial screening with D‐dimer (using a high‐sensitivity cutoff), followed by application of the combined model (using the balanced cutoff) for patients with elevated D‐dimer to refine risk stratification. This approach leverages the high sensitivity of D‐dimer for initial screening while utilizing the combined model's superior overall discrimination for definitive risk assessment, consistent with the risk stratification framework endorsed in obstetric guidelines [25, 26].

Study Limitations

Nevertheless, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the sample size of the HELLP syndrome group was relatively small (n = 30), which may limit the statistical power to detect smaller effect sizes and reduce the generalizability of our conclusions. Although the observed differences between groups were statistically significant and clinically meaningful, these findings should be interpreted with caution and validated in larger, multi‐center prospective cohorts. Second, as a retrospective single‐center study, selection bias and unmeasured confounding variables cannot be entirely ruled out. Third, longitudinal data on dynamic changes in laboratory parameters across different gestational stages were not available, which could provide deeper insights into the temporal progression of HELLP syndrome. Future studies with larger sample sizes and comprehensive longitudinal follow‐up are warranted to confirm our findings and improve early prediction and management of HELLP syndrome.

The negative correlation observed between D‐dimer levels and MICU transfer within the HELLP group should be interpreted with caution. This finding likely reflects the confounding effect of time‐dependent interventions and the dynamic nature of coagulation parameters in critically ill obstetric patients. Future studies with serial D‐dimer measurements before and after interventions would better elucidate the relationship between D‐dimer kinetics and clinical outcomes.

Finally, although this study did not include more clinical characteristics such as smoking history, medication history, and specific symptoms of severe preeclampsia, since this study adopted a retrospective design, these data were not consistently recorded in the medical records. The absence of these variables may imply the presence of potential confounding factors, which might affect the observed association. However, the existing data on twin/multiple pregnancies and adverse pregnancy histories did not show significant differences between the two groups, suggesting that these factors are unlikely to significantly bias our main findings. Future prospective studies should systematically collect comprehensive clinical data, including symptom descriptions, medication status, and lifestyle factors, in order to conduct more powerful multivariate adjustments and more accurately describe the clinical phenotypes of patients at high risk of HELLP syndrome.

Conclusion

In summary, HELLP syndrome is a rapidly progressing and potentially life‐threatening obstetric emergency. This study identified plasma D‐dimer and LDH as independent risk factors for HELLP syndrome in patients with severe preeclampsia, with combined ROC analysis demonstrating favorable predictive performance (AUC 0.956, sensitivity 94.4%, specificity 92.6%). Despite the inherent clinical heterogeneity of HELLP syndrome reflected in the wide variability of laboratory parameters, the consistent statistical significance across multiple analytical approaches supports the robustness of our findings. However, the relatively small sample size and single‐center design may limit the generalizability of the results. Future multicenter prospective studies with larger cohorts and comprehensive longitudinal monitoring of laboratory parameters across different gestational stages are warranted to validate these findings and establish optimal clinical thresholds for early prediction and intervention.
