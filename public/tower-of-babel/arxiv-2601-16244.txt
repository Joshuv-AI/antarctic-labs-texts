LiDMaS: Architecture-Level Modeling of Fault-Tolerant Magic-State Injection in GKP Photonic Qubits
Dennis Delali Kwesi Wayo

Abstract
Fault-tolerant quantum computation in photonic architectures relies critically on the efficient preparation of high-fidelity logical magic states under realistic constraints on finite squeezing and photon loss. In this work, we investigate logical T T -gate magic-state preparation in GKP-encoded photonic qubits using a repeat-until-success (RUS) injection protocol combined with outer surface-code protection. We adopt an architecture-level modeling framework based on a custom lightweight density-matrix simulator implemented using standard numerical linear algebra, in which finite squeezing is mapped to effective logical dephasing, depolarizing noise is included at the logical level, and photon loss is treated as a heralded erasure process. This approach deliberately avoids explicit continuous-variable wavefunction simulation, hardware-specific photonic models, and quantum software frameworks such as PennyLane, enabling transparent and computationally efficient exploration of architectural trade-offs. We systematically explore squeezing values from 8 8 to 16 16 dB, baseline loss probabilities in the range 0.01 0.01 – 0.03 0.03 , and surface-code distances d = 1 , 3 , 5 , d=1,3,5, and 7 7 . Across this parameter space, we evaluate RUS success probability, average injection overhead, and logical magic-state fidelity. We find that the RUS protocol achieves success probabilities exceeding 0.94 0.94 across all studied regimes, with an average overhead of approximately 1.15 1.15 – 1.20 1.20 rounds per successful injection. After outer-code protection, logical magic-state fidelities reach F log ≈ 0.77 F_{\mathrm{log}}\approx 0.77 – 0.80 0.80 , exhibiting weak sensitivity to moderate photon loss but a strong monotonic dependence on squeezing. Sensitivity analysis confirms that finite squeezing is the dominant continuous error source, while loss primarily affects heralded failure rates rather than logical quality. Phase-boundary diagrams further identify the minimum squeezing required to simultaneously satisfy success-probability thresholds ( ≥ 0.95 \geq 0.95 ) and logical-fidelity targets ( ≥ 0.79 \geq 0.79 ) as a function of code distance. These results provide quantitative design guidelines for scalable, fault-tolerant photonic quantum architectures.



I
Introduction

Photonic quantum computing has emerged as a leading platform for scalable fault-tolerant quantum information processing due to its intrinsic advantages, including low decoherence, room-temperature operation, and the natural compatibility of photons with long-distance communication
19
;
20
. However, achieving universal, fault-tolerant quantum computation in photonic systems requires overcoming fundamental challenges associated with non-Clifford gate implementation, photon loss, and the finite quality of experimentally realizable quantum states
15
;
2
. Among the most promising approaches to addressing these challenges is the use of the Gottesman–Kitaev–Preskill (GKP) encoding, which enables the embedding of qubit degrees of freedom into continuous-variable (CV) photonic modes and allows Gaussian operations to correct small displacement errors
9
;
14
.

Early theoretical work by Menicucci
et al.

14
established rigorous fault-tolerance thresholds for measurement-based quantum computation using GKP-encoded cluster states under finite squeezing. That analysis demonstrated that, in principle, universal fault-tolerant quantum computation is achievable with sufficiently high squeezing and idealized Gaussian operations. Subsequent studies have refined this picture by incorporating more realistic noise models and decoding strategies, including the use of analog measurement information to improve error correction performance at moderate squeezing levels
8
;
16
. Collectively, these works have established GKP encoding as a central ingredient in photonic fault-tolerance roadmaps
2
.

Despite this progress, a critical gap remains between decoder-level analyses of GKP error correction and the system-level requirements of universal fault-tolerant computation. In particular, non-Clifford gate synthesis, most notably the implementation of the logical
T
gate, is indispensable for universality and typically relies on magic-state preparation
3
;
12
. In photonic architectures, magic-state generation is subject to the same physical constraints as logical qubit storage and Clifford operations, including finite squeezing, residual logical noise, and photon loss
15
;
19
. Yet, most existing analyses of GKP-based fault tolerance focus either on asymptotic thresholds or on error suppression under stabilizer operations, without explicitly modeling magic-state injection protocols, repeat-until-success (RUS) dynamics, or the interplay between heralded loss and logical fidelity
4
.

Magic-state preparation has been studied extensively in qubit-based architectures, particularly through distillation protocols and measurement-based constructions
5
;
3
;
13
. These works provide deep insights into overhead scaling and resource optimality, but they generally assume abstract noise models and do not account for photonic-specific effects such as squeezing-dependent dephasing or loss-induced erasure. In photonic systems, photon loss is both ubiquitous and, crucially, often heralded, allowing failed operations to be detected and discarded rather than corrupting logical states
18
. This distinguishing feature suggests that the dominant constraints on logical gate quality may differ substantially from those in matter-based qubit platforms, motivating a dedicated analysis tailored to photonic architectures.

At the architectural level, surface-code-based protection has been proposed as a powerful outer layer to suppress residual logical errors in photonic systems. Notably, Nickerson, Li, and Benjamin
15
demonstrated that heralded photon loss can be efficiently tolerated by erasure-aware decoding, significantly relaxing loss requirements for large-scale quantum computation. However, their analysis focused primarily on Clifford operations and logical error rates, leaving open the question of how non-Clifford gate synthesis, and in particular magic-state fidelity, behaves under realistic loss and noise conditions
7
. Understanding this behavior is essential for assessing the viability of photonic fault-tolerant architectures beyond threshold arguments.

Recent architectural reviews and roadmaps, such as those by Rudolph
19
and subsequent system-level analyses
2
, have emphasized squeezing as a key hardware bottleneck for photonic quantum computing, while acknowledging uncertainty in how squeezing, loss, and code distance jointly constrain system performance. While these studies provide valuable qualitative guidance, quantitative design rules linking experimentally tunable parameters to operational metrics such as success probability, overhead, and logical fidelity remain scarce
21
. In particular, it is unclear to what extent photon loss versus finite squeezing limits the quality of successfully prepared logical magic states, and how outer-code protection reshapes these trade-offs.

In this work, we address these open questions by presenting a systematic, architecture-level study of logical
T
-gate magic-state preparation in GKP-encoded photonic qubits. Rather than performing explicit continuous-variable wavefunction simulations or decoder-level modeling, we adopt a deliberately simplified yet physically informed framework in which logical qubits are represented by
2\times 2
density matrices and noise processes are modeled as effective logical channels. Finite GKP squeezing is mapped to an effective Pauli-
Z
dephasing channel, residual imperfections are captured by logical depolarizing noise, and photon loss is treated as a heralded erasure process that aborts failed attempts without degrading successful outcomes. This abstraction allows us to efficiently explore a wide parameter space while retaining direct correspondence with experimentally relevant quantities.

We focus on a repeat-until-success (RUS) magic-state injection protocol combined with an outer surface-code abstraction, enabling us to quantify key operational metrics, including success probability within a finite round cap, average injection overhead, and logical magic-state fidelity after outer-code protection. By sweeping squeezing levels, baseline loss probabilities, and surface-code distances, we construct sensitivity maps and phase-boundary diagrams that reveal how hardware quality and error correction jointly determine performance. Importantly, this approach allows us to disentangle the roles of squeezing and loss: while loss primarily affects heralded failure rates and overhead, finite squeezing emerges as the dominant factor limiting logical fidelity.

The main contributions of this work are threefold. First, we provide a unified architecture-level model that explicitly incorporates finite squeezing, depolarizing noise, and heralded photon loss into logical magic-state preparation, without relying on CV simulation frameworks or quantum software libraries such as PennyLane. Second, we demonstrate through sensitivity analysis that logical magic-state fidelity is remarkably robust to moderate loss once heralded erasure is accounted for, but remains strongly dependent on squeezing across all studied code distances. Third, we introduce quantitative phase-boundary diagrams that identify the minimum squeezing required to simultaneously achieve high success probability and high logical fidelity as a function of loss and code distance, offering practical design guidance for photonic quantum computing platforms.

By bridging the gap between decoder-centric analyses and system-level gate synthesis, this study clarifies the dominant constraints on fault-tolerant photonic quantum computation and provides actionable insights for experimental and architectural development. The results underscore the central importance of squeezing improvements for enhancing logical gate quality, while highlighting the comparatively benign role of photon loss in heralded, RUS-based photonic architectures.

II
Methods

II.1
Architecture-Level Modeling Framework

The results presented in this work are obtained using a custom, lightweight density-matrix simulation framework designed to capture the dominant logical error mechanisms relevant to fault-tolerant photonic quantum architectures
2
;
6
. Rather than performing explicit continuous-variable wavefunction simulations of Gottesman–Kitaev–Preskill (GKP) states or relying on quantum software frameworks such as PennyLane
1
, we adopt an architecture-level approach in which encoded qubits are represented by
2\times 2
density matrices and logical operations are modeled using standard matrix algebra. This deliberate abstraction enables efficient numerical exploration of design trade-offs while preserving the essential structure of logical noise propagation and error correction
16
.

Finite squeezing of approximate GKP states is incorporated through an effective logical dephasing channel, parameterized by a squeezing-dependent error probability calibrated to reproduce the expected monotonic improvement with increasing squeezing
8
;
11
. Additional logical depolarizing noise is included to model residual non-Pauli errors arising from imperfect Clifford operations and finite stabilizer-cycle fidelity
10
. Photon loss, the dominant hardware impairment in photonic platforms, is treated as a heralded erasure process that terminates the current magic-state injection attempt without contributing unheralded logical errors
15
. This modeling choice reflects the strong loss-detectability inherent to photonic architectures and allows the separation of failure probability from logical-state quality.

At the logical level, non-Clifford gate implementation is realized using a repeat-until-success (RUS)
T
-gate magic-state injection protocol
17
. The output of the injection stage is subsequently protected by an outer quantum error-correcting code, modeled here using a surface-code-inspired logical error scaling law
7
. This layered abstraction, logical GKP encoding, RUS-based magic-state preparation, and outer-code suppression of residual Pauli errors, provides a transparent and computationally efficient framework for evaluating fault-tolerant performance across wide ranges of squeezing, loss, and code distance
2
.

Logical noise model

Each logical qubit is described by a density matrix

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{depol}}\circ\mathcal{D}_{Z}(\rho),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(1)

where
p_{\mathrm{E}}
is the erasure probability

II.2.1
Finite-squeezing-induced dephasing

Finite-energy GKP states exhibit residual phase uncertainty that manifests as effective logical dephasing after GKP error correction. We model this as a Pauli-
Z

2

\mathcal{D}_{Z}(\rho)=(1-p_{Z})\rho+p_{Z}Z\rho Z,

(2)

where
p_{Z}
is an effective dephasing probability.

To relate this to the experimental squeezing parameter
s
(expressed in dB), we introduce a monotonic proxy mapping in Equation
3

p_{Z}(s)=\min!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0.5,,\alpha_{s}e^{-\beta s}}}\right),

(3)

where
\alpha_{s}
and
\beta
are phenomenological constants. This form captures the exponential suppression of GKP logical errors with increasing squeezing and is consistent with analytic and numerical studies of finite-energy GKP codes.

II.2.2
Depolarizing noise

Residual logical noise not directly associated with squeezing is modeled as a depolarizing channel (Equation
4
),

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{depol}}(\rho)=(1-p_{\mathrm{dep}})\rho+\frac{p_{\mathrm{dep}}}{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(X\rho X+Y\rho Y+Z\rho Z}}\right).

(4)

where
p_{\mathrm{dep}}
is fixed independently for data qubits, ancilla qubits, and post-injection outputs.

This term captures logical errors arising from imperfect Clifford gates, syndrome extraction, and measurement errors not explicitly resolved in the model.

II.2.3
Photon loss and erasure

Photon loss in photonic architectures is modeled as a heralded erasure process. When an erasure event occurs, the current magic-state injection attempt is aborted and flagged as failed. Importantly, erasures do not propagate logical Pauli errors into successful outcomes.

The baseline erasure probability
p_{\mathrm{base}}
is coupled to finite squeezing via Equation
5

p_{\mathrm{E}}=p_{\mathrm{base}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\alpha_{\mathrm{LS}}p_{Z}}}\right),

(5)

where
\alpha_{\mathrm{LS}}
quantifies loss–squeezing coupling. This reflects the increased sensitivity of low-squeezing GKP states to photon loss.

The injection circuit operates on a data qubit

Conditioned on the measurement outcome

II.4
Outer-code abstraction: surface-code protection

Successful injection produces a logical state that is further protected by an outer surface code of distance
d

p_{L}(p_{\mathrm{phys}},d)=A\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{p_{\mathrm{phys}}}{p_{\mathrm{th}}}}}\right)^{(d+1)/2},\qquad p_{\mathrm{phys}}<p_{\mathrm{th}},

The effective physical error rate entering the outer code is modeled as Equation

p_{\mathrm{phys}}=w_{Z}p_{Z}+w_{P}p_{\mathrm{dep}},

II.4.1
Fidelity renormalization

Logical failures are conservatively assumed to produce a maximally mixed output with fidelity
1/2

F_{\mathrm{logical}}=(1-p_{L})F_{\mathrm{inj}}+p_{L}\times\frac{1}{2}.

(11)

This approximation intentionally underestimates performance, ensuring that reported fidelities remain conservative.

II.5
Sensitivity analysis

To quantify robustness, we compute finite-difference gradients of the logical fidelity with respect to loss and squeezing (Equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F_{\mathrm{logical}}}{\partial p_{\mathrm{base}}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{F(L+\Delta L)-F(L-\Delta L)}{2\Delta L},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F_{\mathrm{logical}}}{\partial s}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{F(s+\Delta s)-F(s-\Delta s)}{2\Delta s}.

(12b)

These gradients are evaluated over the discrete parameter grid used in simulations and visualized as sensitivity heatmaps.

P_{\mathrm{succ}}\geq P^{\ast},\qquad F_{\mathrm{logical}}\geq F^{\ast},

II.7
Numerical implementation

All simulations were performed using Monte Carlo sampling of density-matrix evolution under the effective noise channels described above. Each data point corresponds to
5\times 10^{3}
independent RUS trials, ensuring statistical convergence of success probabilities and fidelities. Random seeds were fixed for reproducibility.

These methods enable a transparent and computationally efficient evaluation of fault-tolerant logical magic-state preparation in photonic GKP architectures, while retaining direct correspondence with experimentally tunable parameters.

Sensitivity analysis

Finite-difference gradients of the logical fidelity were evaluated over the discrete architecture-level parameter grid explored in the simulations.

IV
Discussions

A major line of work on fault-tolerant photonic quantum computation has focused on the Gottesman–Kitaev–Preskill (GKP) encoding under finite squeezing, most notably in the seminal analysis by Menicucci et al.
14
, which established threshold theorems for continuous-variable cluster-state computation using idealized Gaussian operations. That study rigorously analyzed error propagation in measurement-based quantum computing and demonstrated that fault tolerance is achievable in principle with sufficiently high squeezing. However, the analysis primarily emphasized asymptotic thresholds and did not explicitly consider magic-state injection protocols, repeat-until-success dynamics, or the impact of heralded photon loss on gate-level overhead. In contrast, our work directly models logical
T
-gate magic-state preparation, explicitly incorporating finite squeezing, depolarizing noise, and erasure into a unified architecture-level framework. Rather than focusing on global thresholds, we quantify operational metrics, success probability, injection overhead, and logical fidelity, relevant to near-term photonic architectures.

Recent studies by Fukui et al.
8
and related follow-up work introduced high-performance GKP error-correction schemes based on analog information, demonstrating that exploiting continuous measurement outcomes can substantially improve logical error suppression at moderate squeezing levels. These works provide important insights into decoder-level performance and show that finite squeezing noise can be mitigated more efficiently than previously thought. However, they largely abstract away from higher-level logical workflows such as magic-state distillation or injection, and they do not address how residual errors interact with outer stabilizer codes under realistic loss conditions. Our approach complements these results by treating GKP protection as an effective logical noise map and explicitly studying how residual errors propagate through a non-Clifford injection protocol, thereby bridging the gap between decoder-centric analyses and system-level fault-tolerant gate synthesis.

Magic-state preparation in photonic systems has also been explored in detail by Campbell, Browne
5
, and collaborators, particularly in the context of measurement-based implementations and resource-efficient non-Clifford gate synthesis. These studies emphasize the theoretical optimality of magic-state distillation protocols and their overhead scaling in qubit-based architectures. While these results are foundational, they typically assume abstract error models and do not explicitly incorporate photonic constraints such as finite squeezing or photon-loss-induced erasure. In contrast, our work focuses on injection rather than distillation and shows that, in photonic architectures, high logical fidelity can be achieved with minimal overhead through RUS-style injection combined with outer code protection. This highlights an alternative pathway in which distillation-heavy pipelines may be deferred or partially replaced by high-quality injected states in sufficiently well-engineered photonic platforms.

Surface-code-protected photonic architectures have been proposed by Nickerson, Li, and Benjamin
15
, who analyzed fault-tolerant quantum computation in modular and networked systems subject to photon loss. Their work demonstrated that heralded loss can be tolerated efficiently using erasure-aware decoding, significantly relaxing hardware requirements. However, their analysis focused primarily on Clifford operations and logical error rates, without explicitly examining non-Clifford gate synthesis or magic-state fidelity. Our results align with their conclusions regarding loss tolerance but extend them by showing that, once heralded loss is accounted for, logical magic-state fidelity becomes largely insensitive to moderate loss levels. Instead, finite squeezing emerges as the dominant factor limiting logical quality, a distinction that is made explicit through our sensitivity and phase-boundary analyses.

Finally, recent photonic fault-tolerance roadmaps, such as those developed by Rudolph
19
and subsequent architectural reviews, have emphasized the central role of squeezing as a hardware bottleneck while acknowledging uncertainty in how squeezing, loss, and code distance jointly constrain performance. These works provide broad qualitative guidance but stop short of quantitative design rules. Our phase-boundary diagrams directly address this gap by identifying the minimum squeezing required to simultaneously achieve high RUS success probability and high logical fidelity across different loss regimes and code distances. By mapping out these boundaries explicitly, our study transforms qualitative architectural intuition into quantitative constraints, offering practical guidance for experimental photonic quantum computing platforms.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we have presented an architecture-level study of fault-tolerant logical magic-state preparation in GKP-encoded photonic quantum computing architectures. By combining a repeat-until-success (RUS) logical
T
-gate injection protocol with an abstracted outer surface-code layer, we systematically quantified the interplay between finite squeezing, photon-loss-induced erasure, and code distance. Our approach enables the efficient exploration of a broad design space that would be prohibitively expensive to access using full continuous-variable wavefunction simulations or decoder-level surface-code modeling.

Across all regimes studied, we find that logical magic-state injection remains robust against moderate photon loss, with the average RUS overhead remaining close to unity even in relatively lossy settings. In contrast, finite squeezing emerges as the dominant continuous error source affecting logical fidelity. Once protected by an outer code, logical magic-state fidelity rapidly saturates with increasing code distance, exhibiting only weak dependence on loss. Sensitivity analysis confirms this separation of roles, demonstrating that squeezing primarily governs logical quality, while loss predominantly impacts heralded success probabilities rather than post-selected fidelity.

Phase-boundary analysis further reveals clear threshold-like behavior, allowing us to identify the minimum squeezing requirements necessary to simultaneously achieve high success probability and high logical fidelity as a function of loss and code distance. These boundaries provide concrete quantitative guidance for the co-design of photonic hardware and fault-tolerant logical architectures.

All simulations in this work were performed using a custom density-matrix-based simulator implemented directly in Python and NumPy. No external quantum simulation frameworks, such as PennyLane, were employed. This design choice was deliberate: the objective of this study is to isolate architectural and logical trade-offs using analytically motivated noise abstractions, rather than to model device-level continuous-variable dynamics. The resulting framework is lightweight, transparent, and readily extensible, making it well suited for rapid architectural exploration.

Results clarify the relative importance of squeezing, loss, and code distance in photonic fault-tolerant quantum computing and provide a scalable modeling framework that complements more detailed device-level simulations. We anticipate that this approach will serve as a useful foundation for future studies bridging logical architecture design and experimental photonic platforms.

No external quantum simulation frameworks or hardware backends were employed; instead, a custom density-matrix-based simulator was developed to model logical quantum operations, noise channels, and fault-tolerant protocols at the architectural level. This lightweight approach enabled efficient exploration of large parameter spaces relevant to photonic fault-tolerant quantum computing.
