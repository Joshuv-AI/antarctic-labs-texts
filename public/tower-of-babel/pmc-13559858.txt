Phenolic Glycosides From Ficus erecta and Their Anti‐Inflammatory and Neuroprotective Activities
Authors: Wenming Wu, Ruiyue Hu, Gang Li, Qinxue Luo, An Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFive undescribed phenolic glycosides, ficuserectosides A‐E (1–5), and three previously reported congeners (6–8) were obtained from the whole plants of Ficus erecta. Their structures were determined by comprehensive analysis of 1D and 2D NMR and HRESIMS data. The identities and configurations of the monosaccharide units were established after hydrolysis and subsequent chiral derivatization followed by HPLC analysis. Notably, ficuserectoside E (5) was established as a Z‐configured prenylated coumarin glucoside, enabling revision of a previously misassigned related structure. All of the isolates were evaluated in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)‐stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages and in a 6‐hydroxydopamine (6‐OHDA)‐induced PC12 cell injury model. None of the compounds significantly inhibited nitric oxide (NO) production at 25 or 50 µM. However, compounds 5 and 8 dose‐dependently (12.5–50 µM) improved PC12 cells survival, as evidenced by reduced intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and increased cellular glutathione (GSH) levels. The present findings not only broaden the structural spectrum of Ficus erecta phenolic glycosides but also highlight selected glycosylated phenolics as promising leads for antioxidant‐based neuroprotective candidates.

Introduction

Phenolic glycosides constitute an important and structurally diverse class of plant secondary metabolites, typically characterized by one or more sugar units linked to phenolic aglycones through O‐, C‐, or occasionally ester glycosidic bonds [1]. Conjugation with sugars may alter the aqueous solubility, chemical stability, membrane transport, metabolism, and biological behavior of the corresponding phenolic scaffolds. Consequently, phenolic glycosides have attracted sustained attention in natural products chemistry and drug discovery. Numerous bioactivities have been documented for this compound class, including antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, antimicrobial, cytotoxic, hepatoprotective, antidiabetic, and neuroprotective activities [2]. Representative compounds have shown radical‐scavenging and antioxidant effects [3, 4], modulation of inflammatory mediators such as nitric oxide (NO) and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) [5, 6], and activity against pathogenic microorganisms [7, 8]. Moreover, some derivatives display neuroprotective [9], hepatoprotective [10], and antidiabetic effects [11, 12], highlighting their potential as lead compounds for drug discovery.

Ficus erecta Thunb. (Moraceae) is a deciduous shrub native to East Asia. The plant has been consumed as an edible wild vegetable, with its roots and leaves used as food materials in China. Additionally, all parts of F. erecta have long been employed in traditional remedies for addressing inflammatory and degenerative diseases [13, 14]. Previous phytochemical studies of this species have primarily identified flavonoids, coumarins, lignans, and phenolic acids. Some of these constituents have demonstrated antioxidant, anti‐inflammatory, or antineurodegenerative properties [15, 16, 17, 18]. In contrast, the glycosylated metabolites of F. erecta have received comparatively little attention, and no systematic study on this class of metabolites has been reported.

In order to elucidate the structural diversity of glycosidic constituents from F. erecta and to discover bioactive candidates, we investigated the glycosidic fraction of the whole plants of F. erecta. Chemical separation afforded five previously undescribed phenolic glycosides (1–5), together with three known analogues (6–8). Their structures, including the identities and stereochemical features of the sugar moieties, were established using spectroscopic and chemical methods. During this investigation, analysis of compound 5 also prompted revision of a structural assignment previously proposed for a related prenylated coumarin glycoside. Compounds 1–8 were subsequently evaluated for anti‐inflammatory and neuroprotective activities. This study describes the isolation, structural characterization, structural reassignment, and biological evaluation of these metabolites (Figure 1).

Experimental Section

General Experimental Procedures

General experimental procedures, including optical rotation, UV, HRESIMS, NMR, column chromatography, TLC, MPLC, and semi‐preparative HPLC conditions, were consistent with those described in our previous work [15, 16, 19] and are detailed in the Text S1.

Plant Material

The plant material of F. erecta used in this study was identical to that described in our previous study [15, 16]. Detailed information regarding collection, identification, and specimen deposition is available in the Text S2.

Extraction and Isolation

The dried and pulverized plant material (50.5 kg) was subjected to five rounds of maceration with 95% EtOH (50 L each) at room temperature, with each extraction lasting three days. The combined filtrates were concentrated under reduced pressure to give 7.5 kg of crude extract. This material was suspended in H2O (20 L) and successively partitioned with petroleum ether (PE), EtOAc, and n‐BuOH.

The EtOAc‐soluble fraction (1.1 kg) was chromatographed over silica gel column chromatography (Φ 20 × 50 cm, 5 kg) using stepwise mixtures of EtOAc and MeOH (100:0 to 0:100, v/v). Fractions showing similar TLC profiles were pooled to obtain 12 combined fractions (E1‐E12). Fraction E10 (38 g) was subjected to MCI gel chromatography and eluted with a MeOH‐H2O gradient (40:60 to 100:0, v/v), providing eight subfractions (E10.1‐E10.8) after removal of pigmented materials. Subfraction E10.3 (4.6 g) was separated by C18 MPLC using a MeOH‐H2O gradient (40:60 to 100:0, v/v) to produce five fractions (E10.3.1‐E10.3.5). Semi‐preparative HPLC of E10.3.3 (0.9 g), performed with MeOH‐H2O (27:73, v/v), afforded compounds 6 (3.7 mg, t R 11.9 min), 1 (4.2 mg, t R 14.5 min), and 2 (3.4 mg, t R 16.7 min). Similarly, Using MeOH‐H2O (20:80, v/v), purification of E10.3.4 (1.8 g) yielded compounds 3 (3.2 mg, t R 11.9 min), 7 (2.4 mg, t R 14.5 min), and 4 (3.6 mg, t R 16.7 min). Fraction E10.4 (5.8 g) was further resolved by C18 MPLC with a MeOH‐H2O gradient to give six subfractions (E10.4.1‐E10.4.6). Subsequent semi‐preparative HPLC purification of E10.4.2 (1.1 g) and E10.4.3 (1.5 g) with MeOH‐H2O (20:80, v/v) furnished compounds 5 (2.8 mg, t R 14.8 min) and 8 (3.4 mg, t R 15.6 min), respectively.

Cytotoxicity Assay

RAW264.7 cells (RRID: CVCL_0493) and PC12 cells (RRID: CVCL_F659) were obtained from Shanghai iCell Bioscience Inc. Cell viability was determined using an MTT assay in accordance with our previously reported procedure [15], which is detailed in the Text S3.

Nitrite Assay

The extent of lipopolysaccharide‌ (LPS)‐induced NO production in RAW264.7 macrophages was estimated from the nitrite concentration in the culture medium. Nitrite was quantified by the Griess reaction according to the method reported previously [15], with full details provided in the Text S4.

Neuroprotective Activity Assay

The protective activities of compounds 1–8 were examined in a PC12 cell model of 6‐hydroxydopamine (6‐OHDA)‐induced oxidative injury according to a previously described method [20]. The detailed procedure is available in the Text S5.

Measurement of ROS and GSH

PC12 cells were seeded in 24‐well plates at 1 × 105 cells/well and cultured for 24 h to permit cell attachment. The cells were then treated with the selected compounds at 12.5, 25, or 50 µM for 3 h, followed by exposure to 6‐OHDA (100 µM) for an additional 24 h. After treatment, the cells were harvested, and intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and glutathione (GSH) levels were quantified using commercial assay kits according to the manufacturers' instructions.

Acid Hydrolysis and Sugar Component Analysis

Compounds 1–5 (approximately 1.0 mg each) were separately hydrolyzed with 6 M trifluoroacetic acid (1.0 mL) at 90°C for 2 h. After cooling, each hydrolysate was extracted three times with CHCl3 to separate the aglycone‐derived components. The remaining aqueous phases, which contained the released monosaccharides, were concentrated to dryness under reduced pressure. The resulting hydrolysates and authentic monosaccharide standards were derivatized under identical conditions. Each sample was dissolved in pyridine and treated with L‐cysteine methyl ester hydrochloride at 60°C for 1 h. O‐tolyl isothiocyanate was subsequently introduced, and the reaction was continued at the same temperature for another 1 h. After cooling, the resulting mixtures were filtered and analyzed by HPLC. HPLC separation was performed on an Agilent EC‐C18 column using MeOH–water (27:73, v/v) as the isocratic mobile phase at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The derivatives were monitored by UV detection at 230 nm. The retention times of the derivatives were compared with authentic standard (Figure S41). Sugar assignments were made by comparing the retention times of the derivatives obtained from compounds 1–5 with those of the corresponding derivatives prepared from authentic monosaccharide standards under identical conditions.

Results and Discussion

Structural Elucidation of the Compounds 1–5

Compound 1 was isolated as a yellow gum. Its molecular formula was assigned as C16H22O8 from the HRESIMS signal at m/z 365.1194 [M + Na]+ (calcd for C16H22O8Na, 365.1212), corresponding to six degrees of unsaturation. Its 1H NMR spectrum (Table 1) contained three aromatic resonances at δ H 7.62 (1H, dd, J = 8.3, 2.0 Hz, H‐5), 7.60 (1H, d, J = 2.0 Hz, H‐3), and 7.21 (1H, d, J = 8.3 Hz, H‐6), defining an ABX spin system characteristic of a 1,2,4‐trisubstituted benzene ring. Additional resonances were observed for an ethoxy group δ H 4.35 (2H, q, J = 7.1 Hz, H‐1''), five oxygenated methine group at δ H 5.49 (1H, d, J = 1.8 Hz, H‐1'), 4.07 (1H, dd, J = 3.4, 1.8 Hz, H‐2'), 3.87–3.90 (1H, m, H‐3'), 3.68 (1H, m, H‐5'), and 3.47 (1H, t, J = 9.5 Hz, H‐4'), one methoxy group at δ H 3.88 (3H, s, 2‐OCH3), two methyl groups at δ H 1.38 (3H, t, J = 7.1 Hz, 2''‐CH3) and 1.22 (3H, d, J = 6.2 Hz, 6'‐CH3). The 13C NMR and DEPT spectra (Figures S2 and S3) accounted for all 16 carbon atoms, including two methyls, one methoxy, one oxygenated methylene, eight methines (five bearing oxygen), and four quaternary carbons (one ester carbon). The 1H‐1H COSY correlations (H‐5/H‐6, H2‐1''/H3‐2'') and HMBC correlations (Figure 2) (H‐6/C‐2, C‐4; H‐3, H‐5, and H‐1''/C‐7; H‐3, H‐5/C‐1; 2‐OCH3/C‐2) established an ethyl vanillate moiety. The characteristic oxygenated methine group [δ H 5.49 (1H, d, J = 1.8 Hz, H‐1')] and a sec‐methyl group [δ H 1.22 (3H, d, J = 6.2 Hz, 6'‐CH3)] indicated the presence of rhamnose in 1 [12]. Its proton sequence was established from the corresponding 1H‐1H COSY (Figure 2) correlations. An HMBC cross‐peak (Figure 2) from H‐1' to C‐1 located the glycosidic linkage at C‐1 of the aglycone. The small coupling constant of H‐1' (J = 1.8 Hz) supported an α‐rhamnopyranosyl linkage. After acid hydrolysis and chiral derivatization, the liberated sugar afforded an HPLC retention time identical to that of derivatized authentic L‐rhamnose, establishing its L configuration. Compound 1 was therefore assigned as ethyl‐1‐O‐α‐L‐rhamnopyranosylvanillate and named ficuserectoside A.

Compound 2, isolated as a yellow gum, gave a molecular formula of C17H24O9 on the basis of the HRESIMS ion at m/z 395.1304 [M + Na]+ (calcd for C17H24O9Na, 395.1318). This composition required six degrees of unsaturation. Its 1H and 13C NMR data (Tables 1 and 2) closely resembled those of 1, indicating that the two compounds shared the same rhamnosylated benzoate framework. The principal difference was the presence of an additional methoxy group in 2. Its position at C‐6 was established by the HMBC correlation from 6‐OCH3 to C‐6, identifying the aromatic portion as a syringate‐derived unit. Following acid hydrolysis, chiral derivatization, and HPLC analysis, the sugar derivative from 2 coeluted with that prepared from authentic L‐rhamnose. The small anomeric proton coupling constant of H‐1' (J = 1.8 Hz) (Table 1), together with the NMR characteristics of the sugar residue, established an α‐L‐rhamnopyranosyl unit. These findings defined 2 as ethyl‐1‐O‐α‐L‐rhamnopyranosylsyringate, and the compound was given the trivial name ficuserectoside C.

Compound 3 was obtained as a yellow gum. HRESIMS analysis showed a sodium‐adduct ion at m/z 393.1517 [M+Na]+, consistent with the molecular formula C18H26O8 (calcd for C18H26O8Na, 393.1525). Comparison of its NMR data (Tables 1 and 2) with those of 1 revealed an identical rhamnosylated vanillate core. However, the ethoxy resonances observed for 1 were replaced in 3 by signals attributable to a butoxy group. Its attachment to the ester carbonyl was demonstrated by the long‐range HMBC correlation from H2‐1″ to C‐7. Consequently, compound 3 was identified as butyl‐1‐O‐α‐L‐rhamnopyranosylvanillate, and the compound was given the trivial name ficuserectoside C.

Compound 4, obtained as a yellow gum, possessed the molecular formula C19H28O9, as determined from its positive‐ion HRESIMS peak at m/z 423.1624 [M+Na]+ (calcd for C19H28O9Na, 423.1631). Its 1H and 13C NMR spectra (Tables 1 and 2) were closely comparable to those of 3, suggesting that both compounds contained a butyl ester and an α‐L‐rhamnopyranosyl residue. Relative to 3, compound 4 exhibited one additional methoxy resonance. The HMBC cross‐peak from 6‐OCH3 to C‐6 placed this substituent at C‐6. Thus, the structure of 4 was established as butyl‐1‐O‐α‐L‐rhamnopyranosyl syringate and designated ficuserectoside D.

Compound 5, a yellow gum, had the molecular formula C20H24O9 from its negative HRESIMS ion (m/z 407.1342 [M‐H]−, calcd for C20H23O9, 407.1342). The 1H NMR spectrum (Table 1) displayed a pair of mutually coupled olefinic protons at δ H 7.84 (1H, d, J = 9.4 Hz, H‐4) and 6.15 (1H, d, J = 9.4 Hz, H‐3), together with two singlet aromatic protons at δ H 7.34 (1H, s, H‐5) and 6.70 (1H, s, H‐8), suggesting a 6,7‐disubstituted coumarin nucleus. In addition, signals attributable to a prenyl‐derived side chain were observed, including an olefinic proton at δ H 5.61 (1H, t, J = 7.6 Hz, H‐2'), an oxygenated methylene at δ H 4.49 (1H, d, J = 11.6 Hz, H‐4'), and 4.36 (1H, d, J = 11.6 Hz, H‐4'), and a vinylic methyl group at δ H 1.86 (1H, s, 5'‐CH3). The 1H‐1H COSY correlation of H‐3/H‐4, along with the key HMBC correlations (Figure 2) of H‐4/C‐2, C‐5, C‐8, H‐5/C‐7, C‐9, and H‐8/C‐6, C‐10, established the coumarin skeleton of 5. The prenyl‐derived side chain was further defined by 1H‐1H COSY correlations of H‐1′/H‐2′/H‐3′/H‐4′, and by HMBC correlations of H3‐5′/C‐2′, C‐4′. The attachment of this side chain to C‐6 of the coumarin core was confirmed by the HMBC correlations of H2‐1′/C‐6, C‐7, as well as H‐5/C‐1′. The geometry of the Δ 2′ double bond was assigned as Z by the diagnostic NOESY correlation (Figure S38) between H3‐5′ and H‐2′. The NMR data of 5 revealed a glucopyranosyl unit, as indicated by an anomeric proton at δ H 4.28 (1H, d, J = 7.7 Hz, H‐1'') and six oxygenated carbon resonances at [δ C 102.49 (C‐1''), 75.08 (C‐2''), 71.62 (C‐3''), 78.14 (C‐4''), 77.90 (C‐5''), and 62.68 (C‐6'')]. The HMBC correlations of H‐1″/C‐4′ and H2‐4′/C‐1″ established the glycosidic linkage at C‐4′ of the prenyl side chain. The β‐configuration for the sugar unit was deduced from the large coupling constant (J = 7.7 Hz) of H‐1″, which falls within the typical range for β‐glucopyranosides [21]. Acid hydrolysis followed by chiral derivatization and HPLC comparison with an authentic standard identified the released monosaccharide as D‐glucose. Taken together, these data established 5 as (Z)‐6‐(4‐β‐D‐glucopyranosyloxy‐3‐methylbut‐2‐en‐1‐yl)‐7‐hydroxycoumarin, for which the trivial name ficuserectoside E is proposed.

Notably, compound 5 was initially found to possess spectroscopic features closely resembling those of compound 6 reported in reference [21], for which the same structure had been previously proposed. To avoid ambiguity arising from solvent‐dependent chemical‐shift variations, the NMR spectra of 5 were acquired in CD3CN, the same solvent used in reference. Under identical solvent conditions, the C‐5' resonance of 5 appeared at δ C 21.8 (Figure S40), which differs markedly from that of the reported compound 6 in reference [21] at δ C 14.2. Moreover, the diagnostic NOESY correlation between H‐5′ and H‐2′ observed for 5, which provided diagnostic support for its Z geometry was not present in the spectroscopic data reported for the known analogue. Taken together, these observations indicate that the side‐chain double bond of the previously described compound should be reassigned as E.

The known phenolic glycosides 6–8 were identified as methyl 4‐[(6‐deoxy‐α‐L‐mannopyranosyl)oxy]‐3‐methoxybenzoate (6) [22], methyl syringate α‐L‐rhamnoside (7) [23], 6'‐O‐sinapinoyl esculin (8) [24], respectively (Figure 1), by comparing the acquired spectroscopic data with the corresponding values reported in the literature.

Anti‐Inflammatory and Neuroprotective Effects of 1–8

Before evaluating their biological effects, compounds 1–8 were examined for cytotoxicity toward RAW264.7 and PC12 cells. Treatment with each compound at 50 µM caused no appreciable loss of viability in either cell line (Table 3). Concentrations at or below this noncytotoxic level were therefore selected for subsequent experiments. The effects of compounds 1–8 on LPS‐stimulated NO production were investigated in RAW264.7 macrophages, with nitrite accumulation in the culture medium serving as an indirect measure of NO release. Neither 25 nor 50 µM treatment resulted in a statistically significant reduction in nitrite levels relative to the LPS model group (Figure 3). Thus, no detectable inhibition of LPS‐induced NO production was observed for these compounds under the conditions tested. The same compounds were subsequently examined in a PC12 cell model of 6‐OHDA‐induced oxidative injury. Among them, compounds 5 and 8 produced significant improvements in cell viability over the tested range of 12.5–50 µM, with greater effects observed at higher concentrations (Figure 4). At 50 µM, treatment with 5 and 8 increased cell viability by 14.32% and 14.77%, respectively, relative to cells exposed to 6‐OHDA alone. Intracellular ROS and GSH were then measured to explore whether the protective responses elicited by 5 and 8 were accompanied by changes in cellular redox status. In 6‐OHDA‐challenged PC12 cells, both compounds lowered ROS accumulation while elevating GSH levels across the concentrations examined (Figure 5). These changes suggest that the cytoprotective effects of 5 and 8 may be associated, at least partly, with reduced oxidative stress and maintenance of endogenous antioxidant capacity.

Conclusion

Phytochemical investigation of the whole plant of F. erecta afforded five previously undescribed phenolic glycosides, ficuserectosides A‐E (1–5), along with three known analogues (6–8). Notably, compound 5 was identified as a Z‐configured prenylated coumarin glucoside, leading to the revision of a previously misassigned related structure. Biological evaluation revealed that none of the isolated compounds significantly reduced LPS‐induced NO production in RAW264.7 cells at 25 or 50 µM. In the 6‐OHDA‐challenged PC12 cell model, however, compounds 5 and 8 protected PC12 cells against 6‐OHDA‐induced oxidative injury in a dose‐dependent manner, accompanied by reduced ROS accumulation and increased cellular GSH levels. These observations expand current knowledge of the glycosidic constituents of F. erecta and suggest that compounds 5 and 8 may serve as antioxidant‐based neuroprotective candidates.
