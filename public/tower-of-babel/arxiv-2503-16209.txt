High-dimensional sparse recovery from function samples – Decoders, guarantees and instance optimality
Moritz Moeller, Sebastian Neumayer, Kateryna Pozharska, Tizian Sommerfeld, Tino Ullrich

Abstract
We investigate the reconstruction of multivariate functions from samples using sparse recovery techniques. For Square Root Lasso, Orthogonal Matching Pursuit, and Compressive Sampling Matching Pursuit, we demonstrate both theoretically and empirically that they allow us to recover functions from a small number of random samples. In contrast to Basis Pursuit Denoising, the deployed decoders only require a search space V J V_{J} spanned by dictionary elements indexed by J J and a sparsity parameter n n to guarantee an L 2 L_{2} -approximation error decaying no worse than a best n n -term approximation error and the truncation error with respect to the search space V J V_{J} and the uniform norm. We show that this happens simultaneously for all admissible functions if the number of samples scales as n ​ log 2 ​ n ​ log ⁡ | J | n\log^{2}n\log|J| , coming from known bounds for the RIP for matrices built upon bounded orthonormal systems. As a consequence, we obtain bounds for sampling widths in function classes. In addition, we establish lower bounds on the required sample complexity, which show that the log-factor in | J | |J| is indeed necessary to obtain such instance-optimal error guarantees. Finally, we conduct several numerical experiments to show that our theoretical bounds are reasonable and compare the discussed decoders in practice.

Introduction

Many real-world applications aim to reveal correlations and dependencies in data.
Mathematically, we model the quantity of interest by a function
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
to which we have access only through point evaluations at sampling points
\mathbf{X}=\{x^{1},\ldots,x^{m}\}\subset\Omega
.
Thus, we have to approximate the unknown function
f
from its values
f(\mathbf{X})=(f(x^{1}),\ldots,f(x^{m}))^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
at the points
\mathbf{X}
, a process often referred to as decoding.
In practice, these samples
f(\mathbf{X})
are commonly perturbed by noise.
Let
\mathcal{B}=\{b_{j}\in C(\Omega)\colon\;j\in I\}
be a countable
L_{2}(\mu)
-orthonormal basis of continuous functions.
We aim to approximate
f
by (finitely many) atoms
b_{j}
indexed by
j\in J\subset I
.
To recover the associated coefficients
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{\lvert J\rvert}
, we may solve the (potentially underdetermined) linear system

(1.1)

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{y},\qquad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{A}=m^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bigl(b_{j}\bigl(x^{k}\bigr)\bigr)_{k=1,\ldots,m,\,j\in J}\,,\quad\mathbf{y}=m^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bigl(f\bigl(x^{k}\bigr)\bigr)_{k=1,\ldots,m}.

Here, we implicitly assume that the index set
J
is equipped with an enumeration.
Below, we refer to a (non-linear) solution operator
T_{\mathbf{X}}
for (
1.1
) with
f(\mathbf{X})\mapsto\sum_{j\in J}c_{j}b_{j}
as decoder.

The recovery turns out to be particularly easy if
f
can be well-represented by only a few atoms from the orthonormal basis
\mathcal{B}
.
In this case, we can deploy the non-linear decoders Square Root Lasso (
rLasso
)
[
8
]
, as well as Orthogonal Matching Pursuit (
OMP
)
[
34
]
and the related Compressive Sampling Matching Pursuit (
CoSaMP
)
[
41
]
.
The greedy decoders
OMP
and
CoSaMP
utilize an iterative atom selection process to exploit the intrinsic sparsity of the target function
f
.
Both algorithms require only the search spaces
V_{J}=\mathrm{span}(b_{j}\colon\;j\in J)
and a sparsity parameter
n
as
“parameters”
to produce an output vector with
\mathcal{O}(n)
nonzero entries.
Following a different strategy,
rLasso
recovers (approximate) coefficients
\mathbf{c}
by solving the convex optimization program

(1.2)

\argmin_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{\lvert J\rvert}}\bigl\{\lVert\mathbf{c}\rVert_{\ell_{1}}+\lambda\lVert\mathbf{Ac-\mathbf{y}}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\bigr\},

which is noise-blind: the regularization parameter
\lambda>0
is a function of
n
, independent of potential noise.

A natural question is whether we can control the approximation error
\lVert f-T_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{V}
with respect to a norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{V}
.
In general, this quantity depends on the chosen search spaces
V_{J}
and the sampling points
\mathbf{X}
.
For the proposed non-linear decoders, the role of
V_{J}
turns out to be not as crucial as for (weighted) least-squares decoders
[
14
,
31
,
18
]
, for which one has to know the
optimal
subspace
V_{J}
for the considered approximation, see also
Remark

5.2
.
Throughout, we consider independent and identically distributed (iid) points
\mathbf{X}
sampled from the orthogonality measure
\mu
.

Contribution

First, we investigate the conditions, in particular the required sample size
m=\lvert\mathbf{X}\rvert
, for the deployed decoders
T_{\mathbf{X}}
to achieve an approximation error comparable to the truncation error
\lVert f-P_{J}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}}
, where
P_{J}f
denotes the
L_{2}(\mu)
-orthogonal projection
P_{J}f=\sum_{j\in J}\langle f,b_{j}\rangle b_{j}
.
Our sparse recovery result in
Theorem

2.9
for

and

involves the best
n
-term approximation error

(1.3)

\sigma_{n}(f)_{V}\coloneqq\sigma_{n}(f;\mathcal{B})_{V}\coloneqq\inf\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subset I\\
\lvert J\rvert\leq n\end{subarray}}\,\inf\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}c_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\\
j\in J\end{subarray}}\,\Bigl\lVert f-\sum\limits_{j\in J}c_{j}b_{j}\Bigr\rVert_{V}

with respect to the Wiener space
V=\mathcal{A}_{1}
. The norm of this space is the sum over absolute values of the Fourier coefficients, defined in (
2.15
). This establishes for any

the universal bound

(1.4)

\lVert f-T_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{L_{q}(\mu)}\leq C_{\mathcal{B}}n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}}\bigl(n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(f;\mathcal{B})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}+\lVert f-P_{J}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}}\bigr),

which holds with high probability for all
f\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
provided that the number of samples
m
scales as

(1.5)

m\gtrsim n\log^{2}n\log\lvert J\rvert.

The sample complexity bound (
1.5
) relies on recent improved bounds for RIP matrices based on bounded orthonormal systems, see
[
24
]
,
[
2
]
.
Note that the dependence of the bound (

-term approximation error of

relates to

, a term introduced by Cohen, Dahmen and DeVore in
[
13
]
.
We extend
Theorem

2.9
to

Theorem

5.1
, and moreover we discuss the case of unbounded systems in
Section

2.3
.
Building upon techniques from combinatorics

,
21
]
, we also establish a lower bound on the number of required sampling points for
any
decoder, see
Theorem

3.6
:
If (
1.4
) is to hold for all

, then we must have

m\gtrsim n\log(\lvert J\rvert/4n).

Consequently, the

-factor in (

Finally, we present variants of our results for trigonometric approximation in
Section

4
, highlighting their implications for sampling complexity in function spaces.

To illustrate our theoretical observations, we conduct large-scale recovery experiments, for which we focus on the widely used Fourier and Chebyshev systems as
\mathcal{B}
.
While Legendre polynomials are theoretically admissible by the results of
Section

2.3
, their efficient numerical implementation turned out infeasible.
To control the size of the search space

, we rely on hyperbolic crosses
[
15
]
.
For
OMP
, we solve the least-squares problem via an iterative update of the Cholesky decomposition
[
47
]
, while we use the iterative least-squares solver LSQR for

.
To solve the

optimization program (
1.2

[
3
]
.
Although the theoretical approximation bounds coincide, our numerical experiments show that

and

are preferable when the number of coefficients required for a given approximation accuracy is large.
This is unsurprising, since each

On the other hand, our experiments show that both

and

are superior to

for highly sparse solutions, i.e., if we have to perform only few iterations.
Another interesting observation is that we frequently outperform the theoretically guaranteed asymptotic rates, indicating favorable pre-asymptotic behavior:
the decoders exhibit an error decay rate consistent with the numerically computed best

n
-term approximation, thereby aligning with the theoretical bound in (

).
This represents a substantial advancement over previous results on least-squares recovery reported in
[
5
]
and
[
28
]
.

2.1
Analysis of
rLasso
and

To derive recovery guarantees for
rLasso
and

, we require two robust recovery results from the literature, which involve the robust null space property and the restricted isometry property of a matrix as defined below.

Definition 2.3

(NSP and RIP)
.

Given a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times N}
with
m<N
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we say

•

that
\mathbf{A}
satisfies the
\ell_{q}
-robust null space property (NSP) of order

for a norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert
on

if there exist
\varrho\in(0,1)
and

such that

(2.7)

\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S}\rVert_{\ell_{q}}\leq\varrho n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-1}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S^{\mathrm{C}}}\rVert_{\ell_{1}}+\tau\lVert{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{v}\rVert

for all
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
and all
S\subset[N]
with
\lvert S\rvert\leq n
.
In this case, we write
\mathbf{A}\in\operatorname{NSP}(n,q,\lVert\cdot\rVert,\varrho,\tau)
.

•

that
\mathbf{A}
satisfies the restricted isometry property (RIP) of order
n
with RIP constant
\delta_{n}=\delta_{n}(\mathbf{A})
if we have

(2.8)

(1-\delta_{n})\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\leq\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\leq(1+\delta_{n})\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}^{2}

for all
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
with
\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\ell_{0}}\leq n
.

Remark 2.4
.

The definition of RIP implies that, if a matrix
\mathbf{A}
satisfies RIP of order
n

\delta_{n}(\mathbf{A})
, then we know that
1-\delta_{n}(\mathbf{A})\leq\lVert\mathbf{A}_{j}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\leq 1+\delta_{n}(\mathbf{A})
for all
j\in[N]
.
To see this, apply the definition to the standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
.
We now define the matrix
\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{diag}(\lVert\mathbf{A}_{j}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}^{-1})_{j\in[N]}
and consider
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}
, which is
\mathbf{A}
with

-normalized columns.
For an
s
-sparse vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
we can thus estimate

(2.9)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lVert\mathbf{B}\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}^{2}&=\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\leq(1+\delta_{n}(\mathbf{A}))\lVert\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\leq(1+\delta_{n}(\mathbf{A}))\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}(\mathbf{D})\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\leq\frac{1+\delta_{n}(\mathbf{A})}{1-\delta_{n}(\mathbf{A})}\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}^{2},\\[5.69054pt]
\lVert\mathbf{B}\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}^{2}&=\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\geq(1-\delta_{n}(\mathbf{A}))\lVert\mathbf{D}\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\geq(1-\delta_{n}(\mathbf{A}))\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}(\mathbf{D})\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}^{2}\geq\frac{1-\delta_{n}(\mathbf{A})}{1+\delta_{n}(\mathbf{A})}\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}^{2}.\end{split}

Elementary calculations now show that the RIP constant of

\delta_{n}(\mathbf{B})\leq 2\delta_{n}(\mathbf{A})(1-\delta_{n}(\mathbf{A}))^{-1}
.

).
For

∎

Proposition 2.7

(see

)
.

Let

Then there exists a constant

defined through the

\lVert\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{p}^{24n}(\mathbf{y})\rVert_{\ell_{q}}\leq\beta\bigl(n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-1}\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{v})_{\ell_{1}}+n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\bigr)

for all
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{\lvert J\rvert}
and

.

Note that the assumption that the matrix

must have

-normalized columns is missing in

.
However, the proof of this theorem in the book requires

, which needs this assumption, as stated later in the errata to this book, see
[

Proof.

For this proof, we abbreviate
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{p}^{24n}(\mathbf{y})
and define

to be the vector with the
2n

\mathbf{v}
in absolute value (it does not matter which ones if they are not unique), and all other entries set to zero.
Moreover, let
\overline{\mathbf{v}}_{2n}=\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{v}_{2n}
and
\mathbf{e}
be the error term defined by
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{e}
.
At this point, we use
[

, Theorem 3.7]
, which says

(2.12)

\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\leq 49\inf\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\\
\lvert\supp(\mathbf{z})\rvert\leq 2n\end{subarray}}\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{\ell_{2}},

and thus enables us to say
\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\leq 49\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_{2n}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}=49\lVert\mathbf{A}\overline{\mathbf{v}}_{2n}+\mathbf{e}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}

We now estimate

(2.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}&=\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{e}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}=\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_{2n}+\mathbf{A}\overline{\mathbf{v}}_{2n}+\mathbf{e}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}=\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{v}_{2n}-\mathbf{p})+\mathbf{A}\overline{\mathbf{v}}_{2n}+\mathbf{e}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\\
&\geq\lVert\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{v}_{2n}-\mathbf{p})\rVert_{\ell_{2}}-\lVert\mathbf{A}\overline{\mathbf{v}}_{2n}+\mathbf{e}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\geq\sqrt{1-\delta_{26n}}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{2n}-\mathbf{p}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}-\lVert\mathbf{A}\overline{\mathbf{v}}_{2n}+\mathbf{e}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\end{split}

The combination of (

) and the estimate after (

\lVert\mathbf{v}_{2n}-\mathbf{p}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\leq\frac{50}{\sqrt{1-\delta_{26n}}}\lVert\mathbf{A}\overline{\mathbf{v}}_{2n}+\mathbf{e}\rVert_{\ell_{2}},

which, applied to

2.2
Recovery of functions

In the following, we consider Wiener-type spaces, which are closely related to Barron spaces and have recently been studied, e.g., in
[

,
40

]

They have good embeddings into some Sobolev and Besov spaces with dominating mixed smoothness, see

spaces, see Remark

.
Now, we establish the main results of this section, which involve the Wiener-type spaces
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
with

given by

(2.15)

\mathcal{A}_{\theta}\coloneqq\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\mathcal{B})\coloneqq\bigl\{f\in L_{1}(\mu)\colon\;\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{A}_{\theta}}\coloneqq\lVert[f]\rVert_{\ell_{\theta}}<\infty\bigr\}

with respect to a basis
\mathcal{B}\subset L_{2}(\mu)
.
To unify the notation, we henceforth use the generic operator
H_{\mathbf{X}}
, which can be either the
rLasso
decoder

with
\lambda
as in

2.6
, or the

from (

).
The corresponding vector
\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{y})
or

Below, we combine
Theorem

2.5
,
Proposition

2.6
and
Proposition

2.7
with the general RIP result
Theorem

A.2
for the matrix
\mathbf{A}
in (
2.1
) induced by a bounded orthonormal system (BOS).

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\mathcal{B}\subset L_{2}(\mu)
be a BOS and
B\coloneqq\sup_{j\in I}\lVert b_{j}\rVert_{L_{\infty}}
.
For given
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
J\subset I
finite, we define the (random) matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times\lvert J\rvert}
as in (
2.1
) with
\mathbf{X}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mu
.
Moreover, for given

and
\mathbf{y}
,
\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{y})
denotes the reconstruction vector of

with
\lambda(n)
according to

2.6
, or

after at most

There exist universal constants
\alpha,\beta>0
such that for all

,
\gamma\in(0,1)
and

(2.16)

m\geq\alpha B^{2}n\bigl({\log^{2}}\bigl(B^{2}n\bigr)\log\lvert J\rvert+\log\bigl(B^{2}n\bigr)\log\bigl(\gamma^{-1}\log\bigl(B^{2}n\bigr)\bigr)\bigr),

we have with probability at least

\lVert\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{y})\rVert_{\ell_{q}}\leq\beta\bigl(n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-1}\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{v})_{\ell_{1}}+n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\bigr)

for all
q\in[1,2]
, all
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{\lvert J\rvert}
and all
{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
.

Proof.

First, we discuss the

case.
By
Theorem

A.2
, there exist
\alpha>0
such that, for

,
m
,

and

such that (

) holds, the matrix

has the RIP of order

with

with probability at least

.
For the instances where the RIP holds,

2.5
implies that
\mathbf{A}\in\operatorname{NSP}(n,2,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\ell_{2}},\varrho,\tau)
, where

formally depend on

.
However, since

we obtain universal constants

from (
A.5
) in its proof.
Then,
Proposition

2.6
implies that there exists
\beta>0
depending on

such that (

) holds.
For

, we again use

A.2
to see that there is an

such that
\mathbf{A}
has the RIP with

with probability at least

.
Now, the recovery bound (

) holds by
Proposition

2.7
.
∎

Next, we translate this result on the level of coefficients into a result for functions
f\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
.

Theorem 2.9
.

There exist universal constants
\alpha,C_{B}>0
such that for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
J\subset I
finite with
n<\lvert J\rvert
the following holds.
If we have at least as many sample points
\mathbf{X}=\{x^{1},\ldots,x^{m}\}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mu
as required in (
2.16
), then with probability at least
1-\gamma
we have

(2.18)

\displaystyle\lVert f-H_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{L_{q}}\leq C_{B}n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}}\big(n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(f;\mathcal{B})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}+\lVert f-P_{J}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}}\big)

for any
2\leq q\leq\infty
and
f\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
.

Proof.

First, we consider
q=\infty
.
Applying (
2.17
) from
Proposition

2.8
with
\mathbf{v}=P_{J}f
and
\mathbf{y}=m^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}f(\mathbf{X})
yields that

(2.19)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lVert P_{J}f-H_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{L_{\infty}}&\leq\sum_{j\in J}\lVert b_{j}\rVert_{L_{\infty}}\lvert[P_{J}f]_{j}-[H_{\mathbf{X}}(f)]_{j}\rvert\leq B\lVert[P_{J}f]-[H_{\mathbf{X}}(f)]\rVert_{\ell_{1}}\\
&\leq B\beta\big(\sigma_{n}([P_{J}f])_{\ell_{1}}+n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}m^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lVert P_{J}f(\mathbf{X})-f(\mathbf{X})\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\big)\\
&\leq B\beta\big(\sigma_{n}(P_{J}f)_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}+n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lVert P_{J}f-f\rVert_{L_{\infty}}\big)\end{split}

holds with the stated probability. Using (
2.19
) and
\sigma_{n}([P_{J}f])_{\ell_{1}}\leq\sigma_{n}([f])_{\ell_{1}}=\sigma_{n}(f)_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}
, we further obtain

(2.20)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lVert f-H_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{L_{\infty}}&\leq\lVert f-P_{J}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}}+\lVert P_{J}f-H_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{L_{\infty}}\\
&\leq B\beta\sigma_{n}([P_{J}f])_{\ell_{1}}+(B\beta n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+1)\lVert f-P_{J}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}}\\
&\leq C_{\infty}n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bigl(n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(f)_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}+\lVert f-P_{J}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}}\bigr).\end{split}

This concludes the case
q=\infty
.

For
q=2
, we note that
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L_{2}}\leq\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L_{\infty}}
.
Thus, the Parseval identity and (
2.17
) from
Proposition

2.8
imply

(2.21)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lVert f-H_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{L_{2}}&\leq\lVert P_{J}f-H_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{L_{2}}+\lVert f-P_{J}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}}\\
&=\lVert[P_{J}f]-[H_{\mathbf{X}}(f)]\rVert_{\ell_{2}}+\lVert f-P_{J}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}}\\
&\leq\beta n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}([P_{J}f])_{\ell_{1}}+(\beta+1)\lVert f-P_{J}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}}\\
&\leq C_{2}\big(n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(f)_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}+\lVert f-P_{J}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}}\big).\end{split}

For
2<q<\infty
, Hölder’s inequality implies

(2.22)

\displaystyle\lVert f-H_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{L_{q}}\leq\lVert f-H_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{L_{2}}^{1-\theta}\lVert f-H_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{L_{\infty}}^{\theta},

where
\theta
has to be chosen such that
\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}=\nicefrac{{(1-\theta)}}{{2}}+\nicefrac{{\theta}}{{\infty}}
, which yields
\theta=1-\nicefrac{{2}}{{q}}
.
Now, the desired estimate holds with probability at least
1-\gamma
.
∎

Remark 2.10

(Function classes)
.

1.

If
f=P_{J}f
, the second term in (
2.18
) vanishes.
Thus, the decoder satisfies
\lVert f-H_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{L_{q}}\leq C_{B}n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}}\sigma_{n}(f;\mathcal{B})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}
with high probability if
m\gtrsim B^{2}n\log^{2}(B^{2}n)\log\lvert J\rvert
, which is a simplification of the sampling complexity bound (
2.16
) by estimating
\log\bigl(\gamma^{-1}\log\bigl(B^{2}n\bigr)\bigr)
in terms of
\log(B^{2}n)
at the cost of a constant depending on
\gamma
.

2.

For the general case, the truncation error does not vanish.
However, when recovering all functions from the unit ball of a given and fixed function class
\mathbf{F}
, we directly obtain from
Theorem

2.9
that

(2.23)

\sup\limits_{\|f\|_{\mathbf{F}}\leq 1}\|f-H_{\mathbf{X}}f\|_{L_{q}}\leq C_{B}n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}}\big(n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{F};\mathcal{B})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}+\|I-P_{J}\|_{\mathbf{F}\to L_{\infty}}\big).

A similar estimate with
q=2
has been established in
[
29
]
for Basis Pursuit Denoising.
According to (
2.23
), it suffices to have a mild control of the worst-case truncation error, say

(2.24)

\|I-P_{J}\|_{\mathbf{F}\to L_{\infty}}=\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathbf{F}}\leq 1}\lVert f-P_{J}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}}=\mathcal{O}(|J|^{-\kappa})

for some
\kappa>0
.
Since
m
depends only logarithmically on
|J|
, we may control the second summand in (
2.18
) by the order of the first one at the price of using now
m\gtrsim n\log(n)^{3}
many samples. This requires an at most polynomial decay of
\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{F};\mathcal{B})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}
.
Inserting the bounds from
[
40
]
shows that our decoders outperform any linear decoder such as (weighted) least-squares for weighted Wiener and certain Sobolev-Besov spaces with mixed smoothness, see for instance
[
40
, Thm. 6.1 and Cor. 6.8]
.

2.3
General orthonormal bases

We now consider an orthonormal basis
\mathcal{B}
with atoms
b_{j}
that are not uniformly bounded in
L_{\infty}
.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
\mathcal{B}\subset L_{2}(\mu)
be orthonormal and assume that
\tilde{\psi}(x)\coloneqq\sup_{j\in I}\lvert b_{j}(x)\rvert\in L_{2}(\mu)
.
Then, we can define

(2.25)

\psi\coloneqq\frac{\tilde{\psi}}{\lVert\tilde{\psi}\rVert_{L_{2}(\mu)}}\quad\text{and}\quad\varphi^{2}\coloneqq\frac{1+\psi^{2}}{2}

and a probability measure
\tilde{\mu}
on
\Omega
with
\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu}=\varphi^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu
.
By setting
\tilde{b}_{j}\coloneqq\frac{b_{j}}{\varphi}
, we get an orthonormal basis
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}=\{\tilde{b}_{j}\colon\;j\in I\}
for
L_{2}(\varphi^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu)
.
Moreover,
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
is uniformly bounded, such that we can define

(2.26)

\displaystyle\tilde{B}\coloneqq\sup\limits_{j\in I}\lVert\tilde{b}_{j}\rVert_{L_{\infty}(\mu)}\leq\sqrt{2}\lVert\tilde{\psi}\rVert_{L_{2}(\mu)}.

The assumption in
Definition

2.11
is not trivial and may fail for certain bases, e.g, wavelets.
On the other hand, it holds for Jacobi polynomials, see
[
45
]
, and the special case of Legendre polynomials is discussed in
Remark

2.16
. The method described in the definition was first described in
[
6
, Equation 6.7]
and is a variation of the preconditioning trick which assures that
\varphi^{2}
is bounded from below by
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
. The preconditioning trick itself was first described in
[
45
]
for Legendre polynomials and in
[
23
]
for the general case. For further details, an overview is given in
[
2
, Sec. 5.5]
.

In principle, we can apply
Theorem

2.9
for the new basis
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
.
However, this would lead to estimates with the modified measure
\tilde{\mu}
.
If we want to formulate a result similar to
Theorem

2.9
based on the original measure
\mu
, we have to modify the two decoders
rLasso
and
OMP
.
For this, we again fix a finite index set
J
, and define the matrices

(2.27)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\coloneqq m^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\tilde{b}_{j}(x^{l}))_{1\leq l\leq m,j\in J}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times\lvert J\rvert}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\coloneqq\bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{j}\cdot\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{j}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}^{-1}\bigr)_{j\in J}

and the vector

(2.28)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\coloneqq m^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\frac{f}{\varphi}(\mathbf{X})=m^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\biggl(\frac{f(x^{l})}{\varphi(x^{l})}\biggr)_{l=1}^{m}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}.

Definition 2.12

(Modified
rLasso
)
.

For
\lambda>0
, we define
\tilde{R}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\lambda}\colon C(\Omega)\to V_{J}
by

(2.29)

\tilde{R}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\lambda}(f)=\sum_{j\in J}\tilde{r}_{j}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})b_{j},

where
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})=[\tilde{R}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\lambda}(f)]\in\mathbb{C}^{\lvert J\rvert}
is a (fixed) solution of the
rLasso
minimization problem

(2.30)

\min_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{\lvert J\rvert}}\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{\ell_{1}}+\lambda\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{z}-\tilde{\mathbf{y}}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}

with the matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
from (
2.27
).
Note that we implicitly assume that there is a deterministic selection rule in the case of multiple solutions.

Definition 2.13

(Modified
OMP
)
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
\tilde{P}_{\mathbf{X}}^{n}\colon C(\Omega)\to V_{J}
by

(2.31)

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{\mathbf{X}}^{n}(f)=\sum_{j\in J}\tilde{p}_{j}^{n}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})b_{j},

where
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{n}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})=[\tilde{P}_{\mathbf{X}}^{n}(f)]\in\mathbb{C}^{\lvert J\rvert}
is recursively defined through

(2.32)

\displaystyle S^{k+1}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})

\displaystyle=S^{k}\cup\operatorname{argmax}\bigl\{\lvert(\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\ast}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}-\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{k}))_{j}\rvert\colon\;1\leq j\leq\lvert J\rvert\bigr\},

(2.33)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{k+1}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{argmin}\big\{\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{y}}-\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{p}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\colon\;\supp(\mathbf{p})\subset S^{k+1}\big\}

with
S^{0}(\mathbf{y})\coloneqq\emptyset
and
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{0}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})\coloneqq\mathbf{0}
. If the
\argmax
is not a singleton, we select the smallest index
j
.

Remark 2.14
.

The decoders use the modified basis
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
only to obtain the coefficients
\tilde{\mathbf{r}}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})
and
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{n}(\tilde{\mathbf{y}})
. The reconstruction in (
2.29
) and (
2.31
) is still based on the original basis
\mathcal{B}
.

Analogously to our previous notation, we now use the generic operator
\tilde{H}_{\mathbf{X}}
to denote either the modified
rLasso

\tilde{R}_{\mathbf{X}}^{\lambda}
with
\lambda
as in
Proposition

2.6
, or the modified
OMP
output
\tilde{P}_{\mathbf{X}}^{24n}
.

Theorem 2.15
.

There exists a universal constant
\alpha>0
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
J\subset I
finite with
n<\lvert J\rvert
the following holds.
If we draw

(2.34)

m\geq\alpha\tilde{B}^{2}n\bigl({\log^{2}}(\tilde{B}^{2}n)\log\lvert J\rvert+\log\bigl(\tilde{B}^{2}n\bigr)\log\bigl(\gamma^{-1}\log(\tilde{B}^{2}n)\bigr)\bigr)

sample points
\mathbf{X}=\{x^{1},\ldots,x^{m}\}\overset{iid}{\sim}\varphi^{2}\,d\mu
, then with probability at least
1-\gamma
we have, with
\beta
from
Proposition

2.8
,

(2.35)

\displaystyle\lVert f-\tilde{H}_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{L_{2}(\mu)}\leq(\sqrt{2}\beta+1)\big(n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(f;\mathcal{B})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}+\lVert f-P_{J}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}(\mu)}\big)

for any
f\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
.

Proof.

We fix
f\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
and note that
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L_{2}(\mu)}\leq\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L_{\infty}(\mu)}
.
We then apply in turn the Parseval identity and
Proposition

2.8
to obtain

(2.36)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lVert f-\tilde{H}_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{L_{2}(\mu)}&\leq\lVert P_{J}f-\tilde{H}_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{L_{2}(\mu)}+\lVert f-P_{J}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}(\mu)}\\
&=\lVert[P_{J}f]-[\tilde{H}_{\mathbf{X}}(f)]\rVert_{\ell_{2}}+\lVert f-P_{J}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}(\mu)}\\
&\leq\beta n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(P_{J}f,\mathcal{B})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}+\beta\lVert\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varphi}}\cdot(f-P_{J}f)\rVert_{L_{\infty}(\mu)}+\lVert f-P_{J}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}(\mu)}\\
&\leq\beta n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(P_{J}f,\mathcal{B})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}+(\sqrt{2}\beta+1)\lVert f-P_{J}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}(\mu)}\\
&\leq(\sqrt{2}\beta+1)\bigl(n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(f,\mathcal{B})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}+\lVert f-P_{J}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}(\mu)}\bigr).\end{split}

This is due to the fact that
\varphi\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
and concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.16
.

The Legendre polynomials
L_{k}\colon[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, with

(2.37)

x\mapsto\frac{\sqrt{2k+1}}{2^{k}k!}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}^{k}}{\mathrm{d}x^{k}}\bigl(x^{2}-1\bigr)^{k}

are an orthonormal basis for the normalized Lebesgue measure
\mathrm{d}\mu=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\mathrm{d}x
.
This basis is not uniformly bounded.
However, due to
[
4
]
, one can show that the atoms
L_{k}
are bounded by the
L_{2}(\mu)
-function

(2.38)

\tilde{\psi}_{L}(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\nicefrac{{2}}{{\sqrt{\pi}}}\,(1-x^{2})^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}&x\in(-1,1)\\
\infty&x\in\{-1,1\}\end{cases}.

The version stated here can be found in
[
2
, Eq. 5.49]
.
Since
\lVert\tilde{\psi}_{L}\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}=2
, we can apply
Theorem

2.15
to obtain error bounds for
rLasso
or
OMP
with Legendre polynomials.

3
Minimal number of samples – instance optimality

In this section, we will derive lower bounds on the number of required samples for any decoder with a certain error decay.
Our results are based on the notion of instance optimality for vectors, which we briefly introduce below.
The proofs are along the lines of
[
21
, Sec. 11]
and included in
Appendix
B
for convenience.

3.1
Instance optimality notions for vectors

The concept of instance optimality was first introduced by Cohen, Dahmen and DeVore in
[
13
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
p,q\in[1,\infty]
. We call a pair
(\mathbf{A},\Delta)
of a measurement matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times N}
and a reconstruction map
\Delta\colon\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{N}

(q,p)
–
n
–instance-optimal with constant
C_{N}>0
if it satisfies for all
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{z}-\Delta(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z})\rVert_{\ell_{q}}\leq C_{N}n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{z})_{\ell_{p}},

denoted by
(\mathbf{A},\Delta)\in\operatorname{IO}(q,p,n,C_{N})
. If
p=q
, then we say that
(\mathbf{A},\Delta)
is
p
–
n
–instance-optimal with constant
C_{N}
and write
(\mathbf{A},\Delta)\in\operatorname{IO}(p,n,C_{N})
.

The following statement is a slight generalization of
[
21
, Thm. 11.4]
.
It can be obtained by tracking the involved constants and generalizing from the
\ell_{1}
- to the
\ell_{p}
-case.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
q\geq p\geq 1
and
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times N}
.
If there exists
\Delta\colon\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{N}
such that
(\mathbf{A},\Delta)\in\operatorname{IO}(q,p,n,C_{N})
, then we have

(3.2)

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{\ell_{q}}\leq C_{N}n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}\sigma_{2n}(\mathbf{z})_{\ell_{p}}

for all
\mathbf{z}\in\ker(\mathbf{A})
. Conversely, if (
3.2
) holds for all
\mathbf{z}\in\ker(\mathbf{A})
, then there exists
\Delta\colon\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{N}
such that
(\mathbf{A},\Delta)\in\operatorname{IO}(q,p,n,2C_{N})
.

In the definition of instance optimality, we can actually reduce the
q
as desired.

Lemma 3.3

(
[
21
, Thm. 11.8]
)
.

Let
q\geq q^{\prime}\geq p\geq 1
and
(\mathbf{A},\Delta)\in\operatorname{IO}(q,p,n,C_{N})
.
Then there exists
\Delta^{\prime}\colon\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{N}
such that
(\mathbf{A},\Delta^{\prime})\in\operatorname{IO}(q^{\prime},p,n,6C_{N}+2)
.

The following statement is a slight modification of
[
21
, Thm. 11.6]
, which is used to derive the main result of this section thereafter.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times N}
and
\Delta\colon\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{N}
.
If
(\mathbf{A},\Delta)\in\operatorname{IO}(p,n,C_{N})
for some
p\geq 1
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then

(3.3)

\displaystyle m\geq\frac{n\log(N/4n)}{4\log(2C_{N}+3)}.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times N}
and
\Delta\colon\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{N}
. Suppose that
(\mathbf{A},\Delta)\in\operatorname{IO}(q,p,n,C_{N})
for
q\geq p\geq 1
. Then

(3.4)

\displaystyle m\geq\frac{n\log(N/4n)}{4\log(12C_{N}+7)}.

Proof.

Lemma

3.3
for
q^{\prime}=p
ensures the existence of a reconstruction map
\Delta^{\prime}\colon\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{N}
such that
(\mathbf{A},\Delta^{\prime})\in\operatorname{IO}(p,n,6C_{N}+2)
.
Now,
Proposition

3.4
yields the desired result.
∎

3.2
Minimal number of samples for sparse recovery

With the tools from
Section

3.1
at hand, we can establish a lower bound on the number
m
of sampling points in
\mathbf{X}
if a reconstruction operator shall achieve a certain recovery accuracy.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{B}\subset L_{2}(\mu)
be an ONB and
T_{\mathbf{X}}\colon\mathcal{A}_{1}\to L_{2}
a recovery operator of the form
T_{\mathbf{X}}(f)=T\circ f(\mathbf{X})
with
T\colon\mathbb{C}^{m}\to L_{2}
, and let
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{V}
be some norm.
If

(3.5)

\lVert f-T_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}\leq C\big(\sigma_{n}(f)_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}+\lVert f-P_{J}(f)\rVert_{V}\big)

or

(3.6)

\lVert f-T_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{L_{2}}\leq C\big(n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(f)_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}+\lVert f-P_{J}(f)\rVert_{V}\big)

holds for all
f\in\mathcal{A}_{1}
, a constant
C>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then there exists
C^{\prime}>0
such that

(3.7)

m\geq C^{\prime}n\log(\lvert J\rvert/4n).

Proof.

We want to employ
Proposition

3.4
and
Theorem

3.5
, for which we consider the measurement matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times\lvert J\rvert}
with
A_{l,j}=b_{j}(x^{l})
.
Further, we define the recovery operator
(\Delta(\mathbf{c}))_{j}=[T(\mathbf{c})]_{j}
for
j\in J
and
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
.
Now, for any
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{\lvert J\rvert}
we define

(3.8)

\displaystyle f_{\mathbf{z}}=\sum\limits_{j\in J}z_{j}b_{j}\in V_{J}.

By construction, we have
\mathbf{Az}=f_{\mathbf{z}}(\mathbf{X})
and
\lVert f_{\mathbf{z}}-P_{J}(f_{\mathbf{z}})\rVert_{V}=0
.
We will show that we need sufficiently many samples even to recover these functions.
In the following, we distinguish the two cases.
If
T_{\mathbf{X}}
satisfies (
3.5
), we apply the definition of the space
\mathcal{A}_{1}
in (
2.15
) to obtain

(3.9)

\lVert\mathbf{z}-\Delta(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z})\rVert_{\ell_{1}}=\lVert f_{\mathbf{z}}-P_{J}\circ T_{\mathbf{X}}(f_{\mathbf{z}})\rVert_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}\leq\lVert f_{\mathbf{z}}-T_{\mathbf{X}}(f_{\mathbf{z}})\rVert_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}\leq C\sigma_{n}(f_{\mathbf{z}})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}=C\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{z})_{\ell_{1}},

and see that
(\mathbf{A},\Delta)\in\operatorname{IO}(1,n,C)
.
Hence, applying
Proposition

3.4
yields (
3.7
).
If instead
(
3.6
)
holds, we apply the Parseval theorem to get

(3.10)

\lVert\mathbf{z}-\Delta(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z})\rVert_{\ell_{2}}=\lVert f_{\mathbf{z}}-P_{J}\circ T_{\mathbf{X}}(f_{\mathbf{z}})\rVert_{L_{2}}\leq\lVert f_{\mathbf{z}}-T_{\mathbf{X}}(f_{\mathbf{z}})\rVert_{L_{2}}\leq Cn^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(f_{\mathbf{z}})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}=Cn^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{z})_{\ell_{1}},

that is
(\mathbf{A},\Delta)\in\operatorname{IO}(2,1,n,C)
.
Thus, applying
Theorem

3.5
concludes the proof.
∎

4
The multivariate trigonometric systems as a special case

In what follows, we specify our results for the multivariate trigonometric system

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{T}^{d}=\{\exp(2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot)\colon\;\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}

defined on the torus
\Omega=[0,1]^{d}
.
By
Q_{M}\coloneqq[-M,M]^{d}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}
we denote the
d
-dimensional discrete
M
-cube, and by
\mathcal{T}(Q_{M})=\spann_{\mathbb{C}}(\exp(2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot)\colon\;\mathbf{k}\in Q_{M})
the associated trigonometric polynomials.
For
D\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider the equidistant grid

(4.2)

\displaystyle G\coloneqq G(D,d)\coloneqq\Big\{\frac{\mathbf{n}}{2D}\colon\;\mathbf{n}\in\{0,\ldots,2D\}^{d}\Big\}\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}.

If we randomly draw
m
iid points
\mathbf{X}=\{\mathbf{x}^{l}\colon\;l\in[m]\}
from the discrete uniform distribution
\mu_{G}=\lvert G\rvert^{-1}\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in G}\delta_{\mathbf{x}}
, we can consider the associated random Fourier matrix

(4.3)

\mathbf{A}\coloneqq\big(m^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\exp(2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}^{l})\big)_{l\in[m],\mathbf{k}\in Q_{D}}.

The following theorem has been proven in
[
38
]
for
rLasso
and can be proven analogously for
OMP
. It relies on the RIP bound by Haviv and Regev in
[
24
]
.
Note that we have the
L_{\infty}
-error as well as the best approximation on the right-hand side.
Its proof relies on a specifically designed multivariate de la Vallée Poussin type operator, see
[
26
]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

There exist universal constants
\alpha,C,\gamma>0
such that for all
M,n\in\mathbb{N}
and
D\coloneqq(2d+1)M
the following holds.
If we draw

(4.4)

\displaystyle m\geq\alpha dn\log^{2}(n+1)\log(D+1)

samples
\mathbf{X}=\{\mathbf{x}^{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{m}\}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mu_{G}
, then with probability at least
1-(2D+1)^{-d\gamma\log(n+1)}
the decoder
H_{\mathbf{X}}
, which is either
rLasso
from
Definition

2.1
or
OMP
from
Definition

2.2
on the search space
V_{Q_{D}}
, satisfies

(4.5)

\displaystyle\lVert f-H_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{L_{q}}\leq Cn^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}}\bigl(\sigma_{n}(f;\mathcal{T}^{d})_{L_{\infty}}+\inf_{g\in\mathcal{T}(Q_{M})}\lVert f-g\rVert_{L_{\infty}}\bigr),

for any
f\in C(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and
2\leq q\leq\infty
.

Note that the size of the search space enters only logarithmically here. Let us compare this with the general result
Theorem

2.9
.
Since
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{T}^{d}
is bounded by one in
L_{\infty}
, the number of required samples (
2.16
) and (
4.4
) behave similarly.
Compared to the recovery estimate (
2.18
), the estimate (
4.5
) involves
\sigma_{n}(f;\mathcal{T}^{d})_{L_{\infty}}
instead of
n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(f;\mathcal{T}^{d})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}
.
This has the striking advantage that one may directly insert classical known bounds on
\sigma_{n}(f;\mathcal{T}^{d})_{L_{\infty}}
, see
[
7
,
40
,
48
]
and
[
15
, Sec. 7]
for an overview.

4.1
Minimal number of samples for sparse recovery on the
d
-torus

With the next theorem, we may get rid of
\sigma_{2n}(f;\mathcal{T}^{d})_{L_{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}
and use the results from
Theorem

3.6
.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
39
, Thm. 4.5]
)
.

For a trigonometric polynomial
f\in\mathcal{T}(Q_{M})
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
we have

(4.6)

\displaystyle\sigma_{4n}\bigl(f,\mathcal{T}^{d}\bigr)_{L_{\infty}}\leq C\sqrt{d}n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\log(N)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}

with
N=(2M+1)^{d}
and a constant
C<460
.

The following result shows that
m=\mathcal{O}(n)
samples do not suffice to obtain (
4.5
) for all
f
.

Theorem 4.3
.

For every recovery map
T_{\mathbf{X}}\colon C(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
of the form
T_{\mathbf{X}}(f)=T\circ f(\mathbf{X})
with
T\colon\mathbb{C}^{m}\to L_{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
that satisfies the condition

(4.7)

\lVert f-T_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{L_{2}}\leq C\bigl(\sigma_{2n}(f;\mathcal{T}^{d})_{L_{\infty}}+\inf_{g\in\mathcal{T}(Q_{M})}\lVert f-g\rVert_{V}\bigr)

for every
f\in C(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the number of required samples is

(4.8)

\displaystyle m\geq n\,\frac{\log(N/4n)}{4\log(C^{\prime}d\log(N))},

where
C^{\prime}
is an absolute constant and
N=(2M+1)^{d}
.

Proof.

We follow the proof of
Theorem

3.6
up to (
3.10
).
Using the assumption (
4.7
) then yields

(4.9)

\lVert\mathbf{z}-\Delta(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z})\rVert_{\ell_{2}}=\lVert f_{\mathbf{z}}-P_{Q_{M}}\circ T_{\mathbf{X}}(f_{\mathbf{z}})\rVert_{L_{2}}\leq C\sigma_{2n}(f_{\mathbf{z}})_{L_{\infty}}\,,

where
P_{Q_{M}}
denotes the projection onto
\mathcal{T}(Q_{M})
.
Next, we choose an
n
-sparse trigonometric polynomial
s
such that
\lVert f_{\mathbf{z}}-s\rVert_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}=\sigma_{n}(f_{\mathbf{z}})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}
, which is possible since
f
is a trigonometric polynomial, i.e. we only consider finitely many indices. We obviously see that
\sigma_{2n}(f_{\mathbf{z}})_{L_{\infty}}\leq\sigma_{n}(f_{\mathbf{z}}-s)_{L_{\infty}}
.
Since
f_{\mathbf{z}}-s\in\mathcal{T}(Q_{M})
we get, after employing
Theorem

4.2
,

(4.10)

\sigma_{2n}(f_{\mathbf{z}})_{L_{\infty}}\leq\sigma_{n}(f_{\mathbf{z}}-s)_{L_{\infty}}\leq\tilde{C}\sqrt{d}n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\log(N)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lVert f_{\mathbf{z}}-s\rVert_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}

where
\tilde{C}\geq 1
.
Now, since
\lVert f_{\mathbf{z}}-s\rVert_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}=\sigma_{n}(f_{\mathbf{z}})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}\leq\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{z})_{\ell_{1}}
, this implies that
(\mathbf{A},\Delta)\in\operatorname{IO}(2,1,n,C\cdot\tilde{C}\sqrt{d}\log(N)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})
, such that the claim follows by applying
Theorem

3.5
.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

The result in
Theorem

4.1
has an interesting counterpart for optimal approximation from linear subspaces using samples. In
[
30
, Thm. 17]
, which represents a local version of
[
35
]
, it is shown that for any
n
-dimensional subspace
V_{n}\subset C(\Omega)
we can find
m=4n
points
\mathbf{X}=\{x^{1},\ldots,x^{m}\}
such that the classical least-squares decoder

(4.11)

A(\mathbf{X},V_{n})f\coloneqq\argmin\limits_{g\in V_{n}}\Big(\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}|f(x_{i})-g(x_{i})|^{2}\Big)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

satisfies

(4.12)

\|f-A(\mathbf{X},V_{n})f\|_{L_{2}}\leq 84\inf\limits_{g\in V_{n}}\|f-g\|_{{L_{\infty}}}

for all
f\in C(\Omega)
.
This indicates a scaling of the number
m
of samples as
m=\mathcal{O}(n)
.
However, these are adapted to
V_{n}
.
On the other hand, (
4.7
) shows that a statement of the form

(4.13)

\|f-T_{\mathbf{X}}f\|_{L_{2}}\leq C\Big(\inf\limits_{|J|\leq n}\inf\limits_{c_{k}\in\mathbb{C},k\in J}\Big\|f-\sum\limits_{k\in J}c_{k}b_{k}\Big\|_{L_{\infty}}+\inf\limits_{g\in\mathcal{T}(Q_{M})}\|f-g\|_{L_{\infty}}\Big)

for all
f\in C(\mathbb{T}^{d})
requires

(4.14)

m\geq cn\frac{d\log(M/4n)}{\log(d\log(M))}

many samples no matter which point set
\mathbf{X}
and decoder
T_{\mathbf{X}}
we use.
The second summand in (
4.13
) cannot be dropped since a corresponding statement cannot hold true with finitely many samples.
This shows that a scaling of
m=\mathcal{O}(n)
is not possible if we switch from linear subspaces to
n
-term sums.

5.1
Implementation details

5.1.1
Scalable matrix-vector multiplications

Since the size
\lvert J\rvert
of the search space commonly scales with the dimension
d
(grid-based discretizations even scale exponentially), we require a memory-efficient and scalable implementation of the matrix-vector products with the sampling matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times\lvert J\rvert}
introduced in (
2.1
), and its adjoint.
In certain instances, such as for the Fourier system with dimension
d\leq 4
, see
[
9
,
44
]
, fast algorithms can beat the naive computational complexity
\mathcal{O}(m\lvert J\rvert)
.
Instead of restricting ourselves to such special cases, we use a parallel and matrix-free implementation for generic
\mathbf{A}
based on
PyKeops

[
12
]
.
To this end, we require (preferably simple) closed-form formulas for the atoms
b_{j}
, this is also the main reason why we did not implement the Legendre system.
Compared to a classical matrix-based implementation, we have to recompute the entries of
\mathbf{A}
on the fly so as to gain memory efficiency.
Our implementation also enables straightforward GPU acceleration using
PyTorch
.
Since we typically have
m\ll\lvert J\rvert
, the performance gap of our approach compared to fast methods is often relatively small.
One downside is that the single precision limit of GPUs is reached quite fast.
Thus, we do not include computations where the relative error gets below
1\text{\times}{10}^{-4}
.

5.1.2
Primal-dual method for
rLasso

The
rLasso
decoder from
Definition

2.1
involves a non-smooth convex optimization problem whose efficient numerical solution has been investigated in several works, see
[
3
,
8
,
37
]
.
Following Adcock et al. (
[
3
]
), we deploy the primal-dual hybrid gradient method (PDHGM)
[
11
]
with restarts to minimize the
rLasso
objective

(5.1)

\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{z})\coloneqq\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}-{\mathbf{y}}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}+\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{\ell_{1}}.

To this end, we perform the decomposition
\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{z})=F(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z})+G(\mathbf{z})
with
F(\mathbf{x})=\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}
and
G(\mathbf{z})=\frac{1}{\lambda}\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{\ell_{1}}
.
The resulting PDHGM iterations are given in
Algorithm

1
.
This algorithm relies on the proximal operators of
G
and the convex conjugate
F^{\ast}
, which are the softshrinkage

(5.2)

\mathrm{prox}_{\tau G}(x)=S_{\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\lambda}}}(x)=\begin{cases}\mathrm{sign}(x)(|x|-\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\lambda}})&\text{if }|x|>\nicefrac{{\tau}}{{\lambda}},\\
0&\text{else},\end{cases}

(which is applied entry-wise) and the rescaling
\mathrm{prox}_{\sigma F^{\ast}}(\mathbf{q})=(\mathbf{q}-\sigma\mathbf{y})/\max(1,\lVert\mathbf{q}-\sigma\mathbf{y}\rVert_{\ell_{2}})
.
The averaged iterates
\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{(K)}
and
\mathbf{q}_{\mathrm{avg}}^{(K)}
converge if the step sizes satisfy
\tau\sigma\leq 1/\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{2}^{2}
, see
[
11
, Thm. 1]
.
If unknown, the spectral norm
\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{2}
can be estimated with power iterations.

Next, we discuss the optimal choice of the step sizes
\tau
and
\sigma
.
Let
\mathbf{Z}\subset\mathbb{C}^{\lvert J\rvert}
denote the set of minimizers of (
5.1
).
For the initial iterate
\mathbf{z}^{(0)}
, we denote the (a priori unknown) distance to
\mathbf{Z}
by
\delta=\mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{z}^{(0)},\mathbf{Z})
.
Given a target optimality gap
\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{z}^{(K)})-\mathcal{J}(\mathbf{Z})\leq\varepsilon
, we initialize
\mathbf{q}^{(0)}=\mathbf{0}
and choose
\tau=\delta/\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{2}
,
\sigma=1/(\delta\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{2})
.
Then, performing at most
K(\varepsilon)=\frac{2\delta}{\varepsilon}\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{2}
iterations leads to the desired accuracy, see
[
3
, Prop. 4.12]
.
Even if we would know
\delta
, the asymptotically expected number of iterations is
K(\varepsilon)\sim\varepsilon^{-1}
.
This makes obtaining highly accurate solutions costly and an improved approach is necessary.

Given
\alpha
and

, we can deploy the restart scheme in
Algorithm

2
.
For
\eta\lesssim\varepsilon
, this leads to an expected number of iterations
K(\varepsilon)\sim\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{2}\log(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})\alpha^{-1}
for
\beta=1
, and
K(\varepsilon)\sim\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{2}\varepsilon^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\beta}}-1}\alpha^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\beta}}}
for
\beta>1
, see
[
3
, Tab. 1]
.
Hence, the approximate sharpness condition (
5.3
) enables improved convergence rates until the desired accuracy
\varepsilon
is in the order of
\eta
.
If the sharpness constants
\alpha
,
\beta
in (

) are unknown, we can deploy a line search, see
[
3
, Alg. 2]
, which leads to an additional logarithmic factor in the expected number of iterations for
\beta>1
.
To accelerate our implementation, we terminate the PDHGM in
Line
9
of
Algorithm

2
as soon as the (estimated) optimality gap is less than
\varepsilon
.
This is justified by the fact that
K(\varepsilon)
was chosen accordingly.
Since the cost of each iteration in
Algorithm

2
is dominated by the application of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{A}^{\top}
, we get the overall complexity
\mathcal{O}(m\lvert J\rvert\varepsilon^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{\beta}}-1}\lVert\mathbf{A}\rVert_{2}\alpha^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{\beta}}}
).
In our experiments, we set
\beta=2
for the Fourier system and
\beta=3
for the Chebyshev system.
The parameter
\alpha
is set to
\alpha=\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}
for the Fourier system and
\alpha=0.3\|\mathbf{A}\|_{2}
for the Chebyshev system.
Moreover, we use
11
restarts.

5.1.3
Orthogonal Matching Pursuit

The Orthogonal Matching Pursuit (
OMP
) from
Definition

2.2
is a greedy algorithm, see
[
34
,
49
]
, of which a brief pseudocode is given in
Algorithm

3
.
For every update, we add one entry to the set
S^{(k)}
of nonzero coefficients, thereby successively increasing the support of the approximation
\mathbf{z}^{(k)}
.
In
Line
7
of the
OMP
algorithm, we have to compute the best least-squares fit with the current support.
To solve the (linear) optimality condition
\mathbf{C}_{k}^{*}\mathbf{C}_{k}\mathbf{z}^{(k)}=\mathbf{C}_{k}^{*}\mathbf{y}
, where
\mathbf{C}_{k}\coloneqq\mathbf{B}_{\,:,S^{(k)}}
(and
\mathbf{B}
is the matrix
\mathbf{A}
with
\ell_{2}
-normalized columns), we can iteratively update the Cholesky decomposition of
\mathbf{C}_{k}^{*}\mathbf{C}_{k}
as detailed in
[
47
]
.
This approach leads to an improved complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\lvert J\rvert m+mk+k^{2})
per iteration compared to the naive implementation with complexity
\mathcal{O}(\lvert J\rvert m+mk^{2}+k^{3})
.
Since we expect
\mathbf{C}_{k}
to be well-conditioned, solving the normal equation is numerically stable.
In principle, we can also solve the least-squares problem with an iterative method.
However, we found this to be efficient only for sufficiently large systems, namely if the iteration count
K
is large.

Since every step of
Algorithm

3
adds precisely one support entry, the
OMP
reconstruction
\mathbf{z}^{(K)}
is always
K
-sparse.
While this is a useful property when proving theoretical results, this means that the number of iterations
K
is strongly related to the support of the reconstruction.
Thus, to ensure a fair comparison, we aim to choose
K
in the same order as the sparsity of the
rLasso
reconstruction, which often results in long runtimes for our experiments. Therefore for particularly large experiments we sometimes capped the number of iterations which leads to an early plateau for the error provided by OMP.

5.1.4
Compressive Sampling Matching Pursuit

To improve the runtime, we turn to a modification of
OMP
called Compressive Sampling Matching Pursuit (
CoSaMP
), see
[
41
]
, a brief pseudocode of which can be found in
Algorithm

4
.
This algorithm also aims to find the most efficient basis elements in every step, but, unlike
OMP
in
Algorithm

3
, it exchanges up to
n
basis elements per iteration.
To this end, it first adds up to
2n
indices in each iteration.
Then, it computes the best least-squares fit with this support set and only keeps the
n
indices with the highest magnitude.
To solve the least-squares problem, we use the iterative LSQR method, and initialize it with the relevant part of the previous solution.
Hence, we expect that the solver converges in less than 20 iterations after a few
outer iterations of
CoSaMP
, in particular if
\mathbf{A}
is well-conditioned.
Moreover,
K=\mathcal{O}(\log(\|\mathbf{c}\|_{2}/\eta))
outer iterations suffice to ensure
\|\mathbf{z}^{(K)}-\mathbf{c}\|_{2}\leq\max\{\eta,n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{c})_{\ell_{1}}\}
, see
[
41
, Thm. A]
.
Taking
[
21
, Thm. 6.28]
into account, there is a strong indication that an equivalent to
Theorem

2.9
holds.
For this, we denote with
C_{K,\mathbf{X}}(f)
the output of
Algorithm

4
after
K
iterations.

Theorem 5.1
.

\alpha,C_{B}>0
such that for

and

5.2
Function recovery

We perform recovery experiments for several functions from the literature.
Even for moderate dimensions
d
, we already suffer from the problem that a grid-based search space
J
grows exponentially with the dimension
d
.
Hence, we restrict ourselves to smaller search spaces such as a hyperbolic cross
[
15
]
.
Clearly, other restrictions are also possible.
Alternatively, to avoid the search space
V_{J}
, one can also optimize over the atoms directly, see
[
25
]
for details.
All plots in this section drop the
m\asymp n\log^{3}(n)
relation from
Proposition

.

5.2.1
The multivariate Fourier system

First, we discuss approximation for the Fourier system, which was treated as special case in
Section

4
.
Given
m
uniformly distributed sampling points
\mathbf{X}\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
, the values
\mathbf{y}=f(\mathbf{X})
at these positions, and a search space

, we aim to reconstruct the corresponding Fourier coefficients of

on
J
with both
rLasso
and

. We use the hyperbolic cross
J_{s}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\colon\;\Pi(\mathbf{k})\leq s\}
with the radius

(5.5)

\Pi(\mathbf{k})\coloneqq\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}(1+\lvert k_{i}\rvert),

as search space, which has asymptotical cardinality

, see
[
32
]
.
For small

, see
[
32
, Proof of Thm. 4.9]
.
For

, we specify the regularization parameter

for each experiment individually.
Regarding

, we choose the number of steps as

.
Taking the minimum is justified as follows.
Since each step of
Algorithm

3
adds one support point, it does not make sense to perform more than

Moreover, we do not want to end up with an underdetermined least squares problem.
Thus, we perform at most
m
steps.

Example 1, isotropic decay of coefficients.

Following
[
28

, we consider the 1-dimensional function
g\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

(5.6)

g(x)=\frac{15}{4\sqrt{3}}\cdot 5^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}}\max\bigl\{0.2-(x\bmod 1-0.5)^{2},0\bigr\}.

Its Fourier coefficients are
\hat{g}_{0}=3^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\cdot 5^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}}
and

(5.7)

\hat{g}_{k}=\frac{5^{\nicefrac{{5}}{{4}}}\sqrt{3}}{8}\,(-1)^{k}\,\frac{\sqrt{5}\,\sin\left(2k\pi/\sqrt{5}\right)-2k\pi\cos\left(2k\pi/\sqrt{5}\right)}{\pi^{3}k^{3}},\qquad k\neq 0.

To get an example in dimension
d
, we consider the tensor product
f\colon\mathbb{T}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
f(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}g(x_{i})
.
The corresponding Fourier coefficients are
\hat{f}_{\mathbf{k}}=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\hat{g}_{k_{i}}
for any
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
With an elementary calculation, one can show that
\lVert f\rVert_{L_{2}}=1
, which in particular means that the coefficients satisfy
\|\hat{f}\|_{\ell_{2}}=1
.

Below, we investigate the theoretical quantities appearing in
Theorem

2.9
.
For the Fourier coefficients
\hat{f}_{k}
, we know that

(5.8)

\lvert\hat{f}_{\mathbf{k}}\rvert\lesssim\Pi(\mathbf{k})^{-2}\eqqcolon a_{\mathbf{k}}\,,\quad\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

Thus, using
[
15
, Formula (2.3.2)]
, we directly get

(5.9)

\lVert f-P_{J_{s}}f\rVert_{L_{2}}\lesssim\biggl(\sum_{\mathbf{k}\colon\Pi(\mathbf{k})>s}\Pi(\mathbf{k})^{-4}\biggr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\asymp s^{-\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}(\log s)^{\nicefrac{{(d\!\!-\!\!1)}}{{2}}}\asymp\lvert J_{s}\rvert^{-\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}(\log\lvert J_{s}\rvert)^{2(d-1)}.

Similarly, we obtain for the
L_{\infty}
-truncation error that

(5.10)

\lVert f-P_{J_{s}}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}}\lesssim\sum\limits_{\mathbf{k}\colon\Pi(\mathbf{k})>s}\Pi(\mathbf{k})^{-2}\asymp s^{-1}(\log s)^{d-1}\asymp\lvert J_{s}\rvert^{-1}(\log\lvert J_{s}\rvert)^{2(d-1)}.

Further, we can obtain estimates for best
n
-term approximation of
f
based on (
5.8
).
To this end, we non-increasingly rearrange the multi-indexed sequence
(a_{\mathbf{k}})_{\mathbf{k}}
as

(5.11)

a_{n}^{\ast}\asymp n^{-2}(\log n)^{2(d-1)},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

see
[
32
, Sects. 2 and 3]
. Clearly, we have for any
q>0
that
\sigma_{n}(f;\mathcal{B})_{\mathcal{A}_{q}}\lesssim(\sum_{k>n}\lvert a_{k}^{\ast}\rvert^{q})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}}
.
Using (
5.11
), we thus get

(5.12)

\sigma_{n}(f;\mathcal{B})_{L_{2}}\lesssim\biggl(\sum\limits_{k>n}\lvert a_{k}^{\ast}\rvert^{2}\biggr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\asymp n^{-\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}(\log n)^{2(d-1)}

and

(5.13)

n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(f;\mathcal{B})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sum\limits_{k>n}\lvert a_{k}^{\ast}\rvert\asymp n^{-\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}(\log n)^{2(d-1)}.

Hence, by choosing

for some

, we can ensure that the bound in (
5.10
) has the same order as (
5.13
).
Then,
Theorem

2.9

(5.14)

\lVert f-H_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{L_{2}}\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(f;\mathcal{B})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}+\lVert f-P_{J_{s}}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}}\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}(\log n)^{2(d-1)},

where

Both use

For

To compute the errors, we use the fact that

(5.15)

\lVert f-H_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in J_{s}}\lvert[f]_{\mathbf{k}}-[H_{\mathbf{X}}(f)]_{\mathbf{k}}\rvert^{2}+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\notin J_{s}}\lvert[f]_{\mathbf{k}}\rvert^{2},

namely that we do not require any approximation of the norm.

Remark 5.2

(Least squares versus
rLasso
and
OMP
)
.

It is well-known that
f
can be recovered using a (weighted) least-squares decoder with respect to a hyperbolic cross
J_{s}
provided that the number of samples
m
is chosen such that
m=\mathcal{O}(\lvert J_{s}\rvert\log(\lvert J_{s}\rvert))
, see
[
14
,
28
,
31
]
.
The error is then given by
\|f-P_{J_{s}}f\|_{L_{2}}
in expectation for this individual
f
, see
[
28
, Sect. 6]
, which, of course, is optimal with respect to the fixed basis
\mathcal{B}
.
In addition, the number of samples scales better than our bound (
2.16
) and can be even reduced to
O(|J_{s}|)
using algorithmic subsampling
[
6
]
.
Thus, one might wonder why we care about non-linear decoders.
The answer is that the optimal search space for
L_{2}
-approximation is, of course, barely known in advance (at least in practice) and might differ significantly from
J_{s}
.
Our non-linear decoders do not require this knowledge and instead rely on the sparsity level
n
and a control of the truncation error
\|f-P_{J}f\|_{L_{\infty}}
, see
Remark

2.10
.

Sometimes, we can also infer reasonable search spaces from regularity assumptions.
The bounds (
5.9
) and (
5.10
) can be deduced from the fact that
f
has mixed Besov regularity, i.e.,
f\in\mathbf{B}^{2}_{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, see
[
15
, Chapt. 3]
.
Then, the hyperbolic cross
J_{s}
is the optimal search space, see
[
15
, Thm. 4.2.6]
.
Moreover, results in
[
18
,
30
]
show that we may recover
f
using only
m=\mathcal{O}(\lvert J_{s}\rvert)
many samples from a deterministically subsampled random point cloud with a weighted least squares decoder.
The
L_{2}
-error of this decoder is of order
\mathcal{O}(\lvert J\rvert^{-\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}(\log\lvert J\rvert)^{2(d-1)})
, which is asymptotically optimal with respect to this class and slightly outperforms
rLasso
and
OMP
in terms of the sample complexity.

Example 2, anisotropic decay of coefficients.

We proceed to an example where the isotropic hyperbolic cross
J_{s}
does not represent the optimal search space anymore.
Inspired by
[
27
, Sec. 5.3]
, we consider the function
f\colon\mathbb{T}^{7}\to\mathbb{R}
with

(5.16)

f(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2+2C_{2}^{3}C_{4}^{4}}}\biggl(\prod_{i\in\{1,3,7\}}N_{2}(x_{i})+\prod_{i\in\{2,4,5,6\}}N_{4}(x_{i})\biggr),

where

(5.17)

N_{\ell}(x)=C_{\ell}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{sinc}(\tfrac{\pi}{\ell}k)^{\ell}\cos(\pi k)\exp(2\pi ikx)

is the (normalized) B-spline of order
\ell
with
\|N_{\ell}\|_{L_{2}}=1
.
With the above normalization, we obtain
\|f\|_{L_{2}}=1
.
Compared to the original function in
[
27
]
, we removed the highest-order term involving
N_{6}
(which decays very fast) and reduced the dimension to
d=7
.

From the univariate series expansion (
5.17
), we can readily infer the Fourier coefficients of
f
. We observe an anisotropic decay, namely for
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{7}
, we have that

(5.18)

|\hat{f}_{\mathbf{k}}|\lesssim\prod\limits_{i=1}^{7}(1+|k_{i}|)^{-r_{i}}\eqcolon a_{\mathbf{k}},\qquad r_{i}=\begin{cases}2&i\in\{1,3,7\},\\
4&i\in\{2,4,5,6\}.\end{cases}

Using the hyperbolic cross
J_{s}
, we obtain truncation errors analogously to (
5.9
) and (
5.10
).
The striking advantage over Example 1 shows up when estimating the best
n
-term approximation error
n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(f;\mathcal{B})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}
.
In contrast to (
5.11
), the non-increasing rearrangement decays way faster.
In fact, using
[
33
, Thm. 3.4]
with
\mathbf{q}=(1,...,1)
shows that

(5.19)

a_{n}^{\ast}\lesssim n^{-2}\log^{4}n,\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

This in turn implies that

(5.20)

n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(f;\mathcal{B})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sum\limits_{k>n}|a_{k}^{\ast}|\lesssim n^{-3/2}\log^{4}n.

Remark 5.3

(Least squares)
.

To get a similar decay with the least squares decoder, one has to incorporate knowledge about the optimal linear subspaces for approximation in
L_{2}
, which is determined by an anisotropic hyperbolic cross index set, see
[
33
, Sect. 2.3]
, given by

(5.21)

J_{\mathbf{r},s}\coloneqq\Big\{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{7}\colon\;\prod\limits_{i=1}^{7}(1+|k_{i}|)^{r_{i}}\leq s\Big\}.

Note that the usual isotropic hyperbolic cross index set
J_{s}
would lead to a non-optimal bound.
Neither
rLasso
nor
OMP
uses such a priori information.

5.2.2
The tensorized Chebyshev system

Now, we consider functions in
L_{2}([-1,1]^{d},{\omega}_{d})
, where
\omega_{d}
is the
d
-dimensional normalized Chebyshev measure

(5.22)

\omega_{d}(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\pi^{-1}(1-x_{j}^{2})^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

An orthonormal basis of
L_{2}([-1,1],{\omega})
is given by the Chebyshev polynomials

(5.23)

T_{k}(x)=\sqrt{2}^{\min(1,k)}\cos(k\arccos x),\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

These can be equivalently characterized as eigenfunctions of the Sturm-Liouville problem

(5.24)

\left(\sqrt{1-x^{2}}T^{\prime}_{k}(x)\right)^{\prime}+\frac{k^{2}}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}T_{k}(x)=0.

Further, an orthonormal basis of
L_{2}([-1,1]^{d},{\omega}_{d})
is given by the tensor product functions

(5.25)

T_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}T_{k_{j}}(x_{j})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(\sqrt{2}^{\min(1,k_{j})}\cos(k_{j}\arccos x_{j})\right),\quad k\in\mathbb{N_{0}}^{d}.

A detailed survey on the Chebyshev polynomials can be found in the monograph
[
36
]
.
Note that due to the density
\omega_{d}
, we also need to sample
\mathbf{X}\sim\omega_{d}
.
As search space, we again use the (restricted) hyperbolic cross
J_{s}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\colon\;\Pi(\mathbf{k})\leq s\}
.
For
rLasso
, we use
\lambda=m^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
.
With the same reasoning as for the Fourier basis, we set
K=\min(m,|J|,20000)
for
OMP
.
For
CoSaMP
we set
n=\min(m/4,|J|,20000)
in order to ensure that the least squares problem remains sufficiently well-posed.

Example 3, a non-periodic function.

Following the authors of
[
5
]
, we approximate the function
f\colon[-1,1]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
with
f(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}g(x_{j})
, where

(5.26)

g(x)=\sqrt{\frac{1024\pi}{367\pi-256}}\begin{cases}-\frac{x^{2}}{4}-\frac{x}{2}+\frac{1}{2}&-1\leq x\leq 0\\
\frac{x^{2}}{8}-\frac{x}{2}+\frac{1}{2}&0\leq x\leq 1\\
\end{cases}.

Using the substitution
x=\sin(\theta)
, we obtain (with a lengthy but elementary derivation) that
\|f\|^{2}_{L_{2}([-1,1]^{d},\omega_{d})}=1
.
With another lengthy calculation
[
5
]
, we obtain its Chebyshev expansion

(5.27)

g=\sqrt{\frac{1024\pi}{367\pi-256}}\biggl(\frac{15}{32}T_{0}-\frac{\pi-1}{2\pi\sqrt{2}}T_{1}-\frac{1}{32\sqrt{2}}T_{2}-\frac{3}{2\pi\sqrt{2}}\sum\limits_{k=3}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(k\pi/2)}{k(k^{2}-4)}T_{k}\biggr).

Similarly to the Fourier system, we obtain estimates for the best
n
-term approximation of
f
with respect to the tensorized Chebyshev system via rearrangement. We first observe that

(5.28)

|[f]_{\mathbf{k}}|\lesssim\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}(1+k_{i})^{-3}\eqqcolon a_{\mathbf{k}},\quad\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}.

Using again
[
32
]
, the non-increasing rearrangement of
([f]_{\mathbf{k}})_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}
satisfies

(5.29)

a_{n}^{\ast}\asymp n^{-3}(\log n)^{3(d-1)},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

This implies

(5.30)

\sigma_{n}(f;\mathcal{B})_{L_{2}(\omega_{d})}\lesssim\biggl(\sum\limits_{k>n}\lvert a_{k}^{\ast}\rvert^{2}\biggr)^{1/2}\asymp n^{-5/2}(\log n)^{3(d-1)}

and similarly

(5.31)

n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(f;\mathcal{B})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}\lesssim n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sum\limits_{k>n}\lvert a_{k}^{\ast}\rvert\asymp n^{-5/2}(\log n)^{3(d-1)}.

5.30
).
This can be realized by choosing

with
\kappa>1
.

2.9

(5.32)

\lVert f-H_{\mathbf{X}}(f)\rVert_{L_{2}(\omega_{d})}\leq n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sigma_{n}(f;\mathcal{B})_{\mathcal{A}_{1}}+\lVert f-P_{J}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}}\lesssim n^{-5/2}(\log n)^{3(d-1)},

where

This is done with

The observed standard deviation is always less than 1%.
The relative approximation results (we normalize
f
according to (

and the size of

We also visualize the convergence behavior for

and
J_{s}

Again, all three decoders have roughly the same asymptotic decay.
This decay is determined by the fact that

\mathbf{B}^{2}_{1,\infty}([-1,1]^{d})

5.3
General discussion

For all examples, the errors decay with increasing number of samples
m
and expanding search space
J
.
However, there are certain exceptions to that rule.
First, for
m<\lvert J\rvert
fixed, we observe that the error often increases with
\lvert J\rvert
. Neither is this surprising since the problem becomes more under-determined, nor is it a contradiction to our theoretical observations since
\lvert J\rvert
appears in the required oversampling bound (
2.16
). Therefore, the number of samples required to achieve the same error increases.
Second, the errors stagnate as soon as they are in the order of the truncation error, which is, of course, exactly what we expect due to our bounds in
Theorem

2.9
, where the term
\lVert f-P_{J}f\rVert_{L_{\infty}}
is an upper bound to the truncation error. This effect seems to be more prevalent for
OMP
, while
CoSaMP
and the best
n
-term approximation perform much better with the same number of non-zero entries in the approximant.

One might also wonder why the errors do not converge to the truncation error.
Here, it should be noted that the samples are not drawn from the truncated function itself.
Consequently, a certain level of mismatch is to be expected.
Third, we observe that the truncation errors do not decay anymore as soon as we reach the numerical precision limit.
In particular, due to the GPU computing approach, we generally only have the single precision limit.

Appendix A
RIP and NSP with
rLasso
recovery guarantees

For convenience, we recite the key theorems from the literature adapted to our notation and requirements.
This also includes some simplifications leading to weaker statements.

Proof of
Theorem

2.5
.

Let
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
.
We consider the index set
J_{n}(\mathbf{v})
of the
n
largest entries of
\mathbf{v}
in absolute value, and partition
[N]
into
S_{k}\coloneqq J_{(k+1)n}(\mathbf{v})\setminus J_{kn}(\mathbf{v})
for
0\leq k<\lceil\nicefrac{{N}}{{n}}\rceil
.
It suffices to show the
\ell_{2}
-robust NSP for
S_{0}
since (
2.7
) holds for all
S\subset[N]
with
\lvert S\rvert\leq n
if it holds for
S_{0}
, namely the
n
largest entries.
Since
S_{0}\cap S_{k}=\emptyset
for all
k>0
, the
n
-sparse vectors
\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}}
and
\mathbf{v}_{S_{k}}
have disjoint support,
and
[
21
, Prop. 6.3]
gives us

(A.1)

\displaystyle\lvert\langle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}},\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_{S_{k}}\rangle\rvert\leq\delta_{2n}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{k}}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}.

Moreover, since
\lvert v_{i}\rvert\leq\lvert v_{j}\rvert
for all
i\in S_{k}
and
j\in S_{k-1}
, we have
\lvert v_{i}\rvert\leq n^{-1}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{k-1}}\rVert_{\ell_{1}}
for all
k\geq 1
and
i\in S_{k}
.
Summing over all
i\in S_{k}
, we see that

(A.2)

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{k}}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}=\Bigl(\sum_{i\in S_{k}}\lvert v_{i}\rvert^{2}\Bigr)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\leq n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\big(n^{-1}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{k-1}}\rVert_{\ell_{1}}\big)=n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{k-1}}\rVert_{\ell_{1}}.

With (
A.1
) and (
A.2
) at hand, we estimate as follows

(A.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}^{2}&\leq\frac{1}{1-\delta_{2n}}\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}^{2}=\frac{1}{1-\delta_{2n}}\Bigl\langle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}},\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}-\sum\limits_{k\geq 1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_{S_{k}}\Bigr\rangle\\
&\leq\frac{1}{1-\delta_{2n}}\Big(\lvert\langle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}},\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\rangle\rvert+\sum\limits_{k\geq 1}\lvert\langle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}},\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_{S_{k}}\rangle\rvert\Big)\\
&\leq\frac{1}{1-\delta_{2n}}\Big(\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}+\delta_{2n}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\sum_{k\geq 1}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{k}}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\Big)\\
&\leq\frac{1}{1-\delta_{2n}}\Big(\sqrt{1+\delta_{2n}}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}+\delta_{2n}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sum\limits_{k\geq 1}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{k-1}}\rVert_{\ell_{1}}\Big).\end{split}

Division by
\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}
yields

(A.4)

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{1+\delta_{2n}}}{1-\delta_{2n}}\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}+\frac{\delta_{2n}}{1-\delta_{2n}}n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\big(\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}}\rVert_{\ell_{1}}+\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}^{\mathrm{C}}}\rVert_{\ell_{1}}\big).

Due to the fact that
\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}}\rVert_{\ell_{1}}\leq n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}
, we can rearrange this to

(A.5)

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\leq\biggl(1-\frac{\delta_{2n}}{1-\delta_{2n}}\biggr)^{-1}\biggl(\frac{\delta_{2n}}{1-\delta_{2n}}n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{S_{0}^{\mathrm{C}}}\rVert_{\ell_{1}}+\frac{\sqrt{1+\delta_{2n}}}{1-\delta_{2n}}\lVert\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\biggr).

Comparing (
2.7
) and (
A.5
), the assertion follows by setting
\varrho
and
\tau
accordingly.
∎

The following theorem gives bounds for the
rLasso
minimization problem (
2.3
). Such bounds were first shown by Petersen and Jung in
[
43
, Thm. 3.1]
for real matrices. The version below is adapted to complex matrices.

Theorem A.1

(
[
2
, Thm. 6.29]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times N}
with
\mathbf{A}\in\operatorname{NSP}(n,2,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\ell_{2}},\varrho,\tau)
. If

(A.6)

\lambda\geq\frac{3+\varrho}{1+\varrho}\tau n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}},

then every minimizer

(A.7)

\mathbf{r(\mathbf{y})}\in\argmin_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}}\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{\ell_{1}}+\lambda\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}

obeys, for all
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
,

(A.8)

\lVert\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{y})\rVert_{\ell_{p}}\leq C\bigl(n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}-1}\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{v})_{\ell_{1}}+\bigl(\lambda n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}-1}+n^{1-\nicefrac{{p}}{{2}}}\bigr)\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\rVert_{\ell_{2}}\bigr).

for
p\in\{1,2\}
and a constant
C>0
depending on
\varrho
and
\tau
.

There is a similar result to
Theorem

A.2
below in
[
10
, Thm. 2.3]
.
However, we remain skeptical whether it shows the correct dependency on the parameter
\varepsilon>0
(which, however, is only of minor importance to us).

Theorem A.2

(
[
2
, Thm. 6.15]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}=\{b_{j}\colon\;j\in I\}\subset L_{2}(\mu)
be a BOS and
B=\sup_{j\in I}\lVert b_{j}\rVert_{L_{\infty}}
.
Then there is a constant
\alpha>0
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
J\subset I
finite,
\varepsilon>0
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
and

(A.9)

m\geq\alpha B^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}nw_{n}\bigl(\varepsilon^{-4}w_{n}\log(2\lvert J\rvert)+\varepsilon^{-1}\log\bigl(2(\gamma\varepsilon)^{-1}w_{n}\bigr)\bigr),\qquad\text{where }w_{n}=\log(2B^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}n),

the matrix
\mathbf{A}
defined in
(
2.1
)
satisfies the RIP with constant
\delta_{n}<\varepsilon
with probability at least
1-\gamma
.

Appendix B
Proofs for
Section

3.1

For our proofs, we require two results from the literature, which are given below.

Lemma B.1

(
[
15
, Lem. 7.4.1]
)
.

For any
q\geq p\geq 1
and any
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
we have

(B.1)

\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{z})_{\ell_{q}}\leq n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{\ell_{p}}.

Corollary B.2
.

Let
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
and
q\geq p\geq 1
. Then
\sigma_{3n}(\mathbf{z})_{\ell_{q}}\leq n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}\sigma_{2n}(\mathbf{z})_{\ell_{p}}
.

Proof.

Let
S
be the index set of the
2n
largest absolute entries of
\mathbf{z}
. By
Lemma

B.1
, we get

(B.2)

\sigma_{3n}(\mathbf{z})_{\ell_{q}}=\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{S^{\mathrm{C}}})_{\ell_{q}}\leq n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}\lVert\mathbf{z}_{S^{\mathrm{C}}}\rVert_{\ell_{p}}=n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}\sigma_{2n}(\mathbf{z})_{\ell_{p}}.\qed

Lemma B.3

(
[
20
, Lem. 2.3]
)
.

Let
n,N\in\mathbb{N}
with
n<N
. Then there is a family of subsets
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{P}(\{1,\ldots,N\})
satisfying

•

\lvert S\rvert=n
for all
S\in\mathcal{U}
,

•

\lvert S\cap T\rvert<\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}
for all
S,T\in\mathcal{U}
with
S\neq T
,

•

\lvert\mathcal{U}\rvert\geq(\nicefrac{{N}}{{4n}})^{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}}
.

Below, we provide the proofs for
Section

3
.

Proof of
Proposition

3.2
.

We first assume that
(\mathbf{A},\Delta)\in\operatorname{IO}(q,p,n,C_{N})
. Let
\mathbf{z}\in\ker(\mathbf{A})
and
S
be the index set of the
n
largest absolute entries of
\mathbf{z}
. Then the instance optimality implies that
-\mathbf{z}_{S}=\Delta(-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}_{S})
, and, since
\mathbf{z}\in\ker(\mathbf{A})
, we further get
-\mathbf{z}_{S}=\Delta(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}_{S^{\mathrm{C}}})
. Using again
(\mathbf{A},\Delta)\in\operatorname{IO}(q,p,n,C_{N})
, we obtain the claim (
3.2
) from

(B.3)

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{z}_{S^{\mathrm{C}}}+\mathbf{z}_{S}\rVert_{\ell_{q}}=\lVert\mathbf{z}_{S^{\mathrm{C}}}-\Delta(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}_{S^{\mathrm{C}}})\rVert_{\ell_{q}}\leq C_{N}n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{z}_{S^{\mathrm{C}}})_{\ell_{p}}=C_{N}n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}\sigma_{2n}(\mathbf{z})_{\ell_{p}}.

We now assume that (
3.2
) holds and define a reconstruction map
\Delta\colon\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{N}
via

(B.4)

\displaystyle\Delta(\mathbf{y})\in\argmin\bigl\{\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{v})_{\ell_{p}}\colon\;\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\text{ with }\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{y}\bigr\}.

For any
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
, applying (
3.2
) to
\mathbf{z}-\Delta(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z})\in\ker(\mathbf{A})
gives

(B.5)

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{z}-\Delta(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z})\rVert_{\ell_{q}}\leq C_{N}n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}\sigma_{2n}(\mathbf{z}-\Delta(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}))_{\ell_{p}}.

Now,
\sigma_{2n}(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v})_{\ell_{p}}\leq\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{u})_{\ell_{p}}+\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{v})_{\ell_{p}}
and the definition of
\Delta
in (
B.4
) yield

(B.6)

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{z}-\Delta(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z})\rVert_{\ell_{q}}\leq C_{N}n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}\bigl(\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{z})_{\ell_{p}}+\sigma_{n}(\Delta(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}))_{\ell_{p}}\bigr)\leq 2C_{N}n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}\sigma_{n}(\mathbf{z})_{\ell_{p}},

namely that
(\mathbf{A},\Delta)\in\operatorname{IO}(q,p,n,2C_{N})
.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

3.3
.

Let
\mathbf{z}\in\ker(\mathbf{A})
be arbitrary.
We denote the set of the
3n
largest absolute entries of
\mathbf{z}
by
S
.
From
Proposition

3.2
we get

(B.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lVert\mathbf{z}_{S}\rVert_{\ell_{q^{\prime}}}&\leq(3n)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q^{\prime}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}}\lVert\mathbf{z}_{S}\rVert_{\ell_{q}}\leq(3n)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q^{\prime}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}}\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{\ell_{q}}\leq(3n)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q^{\prime}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}}C_{N}n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}\sigma_{2n}(\mathbf{z})_{\ell_{p}}\\
&=3^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q^{\prime}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}}C_{N}n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q^{\prime}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}\sigma_{2n}(\mathbf{z})_{\ell_{p}}\leq 3C_{N}n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q^{\prime}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}\sigma_{2n}(\mathbf{z})_{\ell_{p}}.\end{split}

Moreover,
Corollary

B.2
yields

(B.8)

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{z}_{S^{\mathrm{C}}}\rVert_{\ell_{q^{\prime}}}=\sigma_{3n}(\mathbf{z})_{\ell_{q^{\prime}}}\leq n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q^{\prime}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}\sigma_{2n}(\mathbf{z})_{\ell_{p}}.

Combining (
B.7
) and (
B.8
), we infer that

(B.9)

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{\ell_{q^{\prime}}}\leq\lVert\mathbf{z}_{S}\rVert_{\ell_{q^{\prime}}}+\lVert\mathbf{z}_{S^{\mathrm{C}}}\rVert_{\ell_{q^{\prime}}}\leq(3C_{N}+1)n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q^{\prime}}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}\sigma_{2n}(\mathbf{z})_{\ell_{p}}.

Using again
Proposition

3.2
, we thus get
(\mathbf{A},\Delta)\in\operatorname{IO}(q^{\prime},p,n,6C_{N}+2)
.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

3.4
.

We consider the family
\mathcal{U}
from
Lemma

B.3
.
For all
S\in\mathcal{U}
, we define
\mathbf{z}^{S}
via
z^{S}_{k}\coloneqq n^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{S}(k)
, for which we have
\lVert\mathbf{z}^{S}\rVert_{\ell_{p}}=1
.
Next, we show that the sets
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}^{S}+\varphi\mathbb{B}_{p})
with
\varphi=(2C_{N}+2)^{-1}
are disjoint, where
\mathbb{B}_{p}
denotes the
\ell_{p}
-unit ball in
\mathbb{C}^{N}
. To this end, let
S,T\in\mathcal{U}
with
S\neq T
, and assume that there exist
\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\varphi\mathbb{B}_{p}
with
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}^{S}+\mathbf{z})=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}^{T}+\mathbf{z}^{\prime})
.
From
\lvert S\cap T\rvert<\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}
we infer that

(B.10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}1&<\bigl\lVert\mathbf{z}^{S}-\mathbf{z}^{T}\bigr\rVert_{\ell_{p}}=\bigl\lVert\mathbf{z}^{S}+\mathbf{z}-\Delta\bigl(\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}^{S}+\mathbf{z})\bigr)+\Delta\bigl(\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}^{T}+\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\bigr)-\mathbf{z}^{\prime}-\mathbf{z}^{T}-\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\bigr\rVert_{\ell_{p}}\\
&\leq\big\lVert\mathbf{z}^{S}+\mathbf{z}-\Delta\bigl(\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}^{S}+\mathbf{z})\bigr)\big\rVert_{\ell_{p}}+\big\lVert\mathbf{z}^{T}+\mathbf{z}^{\prime}-\Delta\bigl(A(\mathbf{z}^{T}+\mathbf{z}^{\prime})\bigr)\big\rVert_{\ell_{p}}+\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{\ell_{p}}+\lVert\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\rVert_{\ell_{p}}.\end{split}

Since
(\mathbf{A},\Delta)\in\operatorname{IO}(p,n,C_{N})
and
|S|=|T|=n
, we further have

(B.11)

\displaystyle\begin{split}1&<C_{N}\sigma_{n}\big(\mathbf{z}^{S}+\mathbf{z}\big)_{\ell_{p}}+C_{N}\sigma_{n}\big(\mathbf{z}^{T}+\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\big)_{\ell_{p}}+\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{\ell_{p}}+\lVert\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\rVert_{\ell_{p}}\\
&\leq C_{N}\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{\ell_{p}}+C_{N}\lVert\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\rVert_{\ell_{p}}+\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{\ell_{p}}+\lVert\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\rVert_{\ell_{p}}\leq(2C_{N}+2)\varphi=1.\end{split}

This is a contradiction, and thus the sets
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}^{S}+\varphi\mathbb{B}_{p})
are disjoint.
Since
\lVert\mathbf{z}^{S}+\mathbf{z}\rVert_{\ell_{p}}\leq 1+\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert_{\ell_{p}}
, we know that
\mathbf{z}^{S}+\varphi\mathbb{B}_{p}\subset\left(1+\varphi\right)\mathbb{B}_{p}
for all
S\in\mathcal{U}
, and consequently also

(B.12)

\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}^{S}+\varphi\mathbb{B}_{p})\subset\left(1+\varphi\right)\mathbf{A}(\mathbb{B}_{p}).

We continue with a volumetric argument. First, we define
r\coloneqq\operatorname*{rank}(\mathbf{A})
and recall that the complex
r
-dimensional balls have the same volume as the
2r
-dimensional real balls. Since the sets
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}^{S}+\varphi\mathbb{B}_{p})
are disjoint, we have by (
B.12
)

(B.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lvert\mathcal{U}\rvert\varphi^{2r}\operatorname*{vol}(\mathbf{A}(\mathbb{B}_{p}))&=\lvert\mathcal{U}\rvert\operatorname*{vol}(\mathbf{A}(\varphi\mathbb{B}_{p}))=\sum\limits_{S\in\mathcal{U}}\operatorname*{vol}\bigl(\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{z}^{S}+\varphi\mathbb{B}_{p})\bigr)\\
&\leq\operatorname*{vol}\left(\left(1+\varphi\right)\mathbf{A}(\mathbb{B}_{p})\right)=\left(1+\varphi\right)^{2r}\operatorname*{vol}(\mathbf{A}(\mathbb{B}_{p})).\end{split}

Thus, we see that
\lvert\mathcal{U}\rvert\varphi^{2r}\leq\left(\varphi+1\right)^{2r}
.
This further implies that
\lvert\mathcal{U}\rvert\leq(2C_{N}+3)^{2r}
.
Since
r\leq m
and
\lvert\mathcal{U}\rvert\geq(\nicefrac{{N}}{{4n}})^{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}}
, we then obtain
(\nicefrac{{N}}{{4n}})^{\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}}\leq(2C_{N}+3)^{2m}
.
Finally, applying the logarithm on both sides leads to

(B.14)

\displaystyle m\geq\frac{n\log(\nicefrac{{N}}{{4n}})}{4\log(2C_{N}+3)}.
