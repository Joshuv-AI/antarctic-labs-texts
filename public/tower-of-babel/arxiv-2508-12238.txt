On balancing and Lucas-balancing numbers expressible as product of two k k -Fibonacci numbers
Bibhu Prasad Tripathy, Bijan Kumar Patel

Abstract
A positive integer n n is called a balancing number if there exists a positive integer r r such that 1 + 2 + ⋯ + ( n − 1 ) = ( n + 1 ) + ( n + 2 ) + ⋯ + ( n + r ) 1+2+\cdots+(n-1)=(n+1)+(n+2)+\cdots+(n+r) . The corresponding value r r is known as the balancer of n n . If n n is a balancing number, then 8 ​ n 2 + 1 8n^{2}+1 is a perfect square, and its positive square root is called a Lucas-balancing number. For any integer k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , let { F n ( k ) } n ≥ − ( k − 2 ) \{F_{n}^{(k)}\}_{n\geq-(k-2)} denote k k -generalized Fibonacci sequence which starts with 0 , … , 1 0,\dots,1 ( k k terms) where each next term is the sum of the k k preceding terms. In this paper, we investigate all balancing and Lucas-balancing numbers that can be expressed as the product of two k k -generalized Fibonacci numbers.

1
Introduction

A positive integer
n
is said to be a balancing number if

\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}i=\sum_{j=n+1}^{n+r}j

holds for some positive integer
r
. Then
r
is referred to as the balancer corresponding to the balancing number
n
(see
[
3
]
). The sequence of balancing numbers, denoted by
\{B_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
, is defined by the recurrence relation

B_{0}=0,~B_{1}=1,~B_{n+1}=6B_{n}-B_{n-1},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all}\hskip 10.00002ptn\geq 1.

Associated with each balancing number
n
, the positive value of
\sqrt{8n^{2}+1}
is known as the Lucas-balancing number, denoted by
C_{n}
(see
[
14
,
15
]
). The sequence
\{C_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
, called the Lucas-balancing sequence, satisfies the same recurrence relation as
\{B_{n}\}
, but with different initial values

C_{0}=1,~C_{1}=3,~C_{n+1}=6C_{n}-C_{n-1},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all}\hskip 10.00002ptn\geq 1.

The balancing and Lucas-balancing sequences correspond to entries A001109 and A001541, respectively, in the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences (OEIS). Both sequences satisfy the characteristic equation
x^{2}-6x+1=0
, whose roots are
\gamma=3+2\sqrt{2}
and
\delta=3-2\sqrt{2}
. The Binet formulas for
B_{n}
and
C_{n}
are given by

B_{n}=\frac{\gamma^{n}-\delta^{n}}{4\sqrt{2}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptC_{n}=\frac{\gamma^{n}+\delta^{n}}{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all}\hskip 10.00002ptn\geq 1.

(1.1)

By induction, it can be shown that the inequalities

\gamma^{n-1}\leq B_{n}\leq\gamma^{n}\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\gamma^{n}\leq 2C_{n}\leq\gamma^{n+1}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{hold for all}\hskip 10.00002ptn\geq 1.

(1.2)

The classical Fibonacci sequence
\{F_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is the binary recurrence sequence defined by

F_{n+2}=F_{n+1}+F_{n}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all}\hskip 10.00002ptn\geq 0,

with initials
F_{0}=0
and
F_{1}=1
. Let
k\geq 2
be an integer. We consider a generalization of the Fibonacci sequence
\{F_{n}^{(k)}\}_{n\geq-(k-2)}
defined as

F_{n}^{(k)}=F_{n-1}^{(k)}+F_{n-2}^{(k)}+\dots+F_{n-k}^{(k)}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}F_{n-i}^{(k)}~~\text{for all}~n\geq 2,

(1.3)

with initials
F_{-(k-2)}^{(k)}=F_{-(k-3)}^{(k)}=\dots=F_{0}^{(k)}=0
and
F_{1}^{(k)}=1
. This sequence
F_{n}^{(k)}
is called the
k
-generalized Fibonacci sequence or the
k
-Fibonacci sequence. This generalization is a family of sequences, with each new choice of
k
producing a unique sequence. For example, if
k=2
, we get
F_{n}^{(2)}=F_{n}
, the classical Fibonacci sequence. For
k=3
, we have
F_{n}^{(3)}=T_{n}
, the
n
th Tribonacci number. They are followed by the Tetranacci numbers for
k=4
, and so on.

Diophantine equations have a long history involving the intersection of
k
-Fibonacci and other linear recurrence sequences. In recent years, the study of finding intersections of two linear recurrence sequences has been extended to the broader problem of determining all terms in a given sequence that can be expressed as products of terms from another sequence. For instance, Ddamulira et al.
[
7
]
investigated Fibonacci numbers that are products of two Pell numbers, as well as Pell numbers that are products of two Fibonacci numbers. In a related study, Adédji et al.
[
1
]
examined Padovan and Perrin numbers that are products of two generalized Lucas numbers. Liptai
[
11
]
has shown that
1
is the only balancing number appearing in the Fibonacci sequence. Rihane
[
16
]
generalized this result and proved that
1,6930
are the only balancing numbers and
1,3
are the only Lucas-balancing numbers, that occur as terms of a
k
-generalized Fibonacci sequence. Consequently, Erduvan and Keskin
[
10
]
determined all Fibonacci numbers that are products of two balancing numbers and, conversely, all balancing numbers that are products of two Fibonacci numbers. Recently, Rihane
[
17
]
extended the work of Erduvan and Keskin
[
10
]
by determining all
k
-Fibonacci numbers that are the product of two balancing or Lucas-balancing numbers.

Motivated by the above works, the present study extends the work of Erduvan and Keskin
[
10
]
and aims to determine the balancing and Lucas-balancing numbers that are products of two
k
-Fibonacci numbers. More precisely, we establish the following results.

Theorem 1.1
.

All the solutions of the Diophantine equation

B_{l}=F_{n}^{(k)}F_{m}^{(k)},

(1.4)

in positive integers
n,m,l
and
k
with
k\geq 3
and
1\leq m\leq n
are

B_{1}=F_{1}^{(k)}F_{1}^{(k)}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all}\hskip 10.00002ptk\geq 3,\hskip 10.00002ptB_{1}=F_{1}^{(k)}F_{2}^{(k)}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all}\hskip 10.00002ptk\geq 3,\hskip 10.00002ptB_{1}=F_{2}^{(k)}F_{2}^{(k)}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all}\hskip 10.00002ptk\geq 3,

B_{6}=F_{1}^{(5)}F_{15}^{(5)}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptB_{6}=F_{2}^{(5)}F_{15}^{(5)}.

Theorem 1.2
.

All the solutions of the Diophantine equation

C_{l}=F_{n}^{(k)}F_{m}^{(k)},

(1.5)

in positive integers
n,m,l
and
k
with
k\geq 2
and
1\leq m\leq n
are
C_{1}=F_{1}^{(2)}F_{4}^{(2)}\ \text{and}\ C_{1}=F_{2}^{(2)}F_{4}^{(2)}
.

The proofs of our theorems rely primarily on linear forms in logarithms of algebraic numbers, as developed by Matveev
[
12
]
, and on the reduction algorithm of Dujella and Pethö
[
9
]
. We begin by presenting a few preliminary results, which are discussed in detail in the subsequent section.

2
Preliminary Results

This section is devoted to gathering several definitions, notations, properties, and results that will be used in the rest of this study.

2.1
Linear forms in logarithms

Let
\gamma
be an algebraic number of degree
d
with a minimal primitive polynomial

f(Y):=b_{0}Y^{d}+b_{1}Y^{d-1}+\cdots+b_{d}=b_{0}\prod_{j=1}^{d}(Y-\gamma^{(j)})\in\mathbb{Z}[Y],

where the
b_{j}
’s are relatively prime integers,
b_{0}>0
, and the
\gamma^{(j)}
’s are conjugates of
\gamma
. Then the
logarithmic height
of
\gamma
is given by

h(\gamma)=\frac{1}{d}\left(\log b_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\log\left(\max\{|\gamma^{(j)}|,1\}\right)\right).

With the above notation, Matveev (see
[
12
]
or
[
6
, Theorem 9.4]
) proved the following result.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{s}
be positive real algebraic numbers in a real algebraic number field
\mathbb{L}
of degree
d_{\mathbb{L}}
. Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}
be non-zero integers such that

\Lambda:=\eta_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots\eta_{s}^{a_{s}}-1\neq 0.

Then

-\log|\Lambda|\leq 1.4\cdot 30^{s+3}\cdot s^{4.5}\cdot d_{\mathbb{L}}^{2}(1+\log d_{\mathbb{L}})(1+\log D)\cdot B_{1}\cdots B_{s},

where

D\geq\max\{|a_{1}|,\ldots,|a_{s}|\},

and

B_{j}\geq\max\{d_{\mathbb{L}}h(\eta_{j}),|\log\eta_{j}|,0.16\},~\text{for all}~j=1,\ldots,s.

2.2
The reduction method

Our next tool is a version of the reduction method of Baker and Davenport (see
[
2
]
). Here, we use a slight variant of the version given by Dujella and Pethö (see
[
9
]
). For a real number
x
, we write
||x||
for the distance from
x
to the nearest integer.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
M
be a positive integer,
p/q
be a convergent of the continued fraction of the irrational
\tau
such that
q>6M
, and
A,B,\mu
be some real numbers with
A>0
and
B>1
. Furthermore, let

\epsilon:=||\mu q||-M\cdot||\tau q||.

If
\epsilon>0
, then there is no solution to the inequality

0<|u\tau-v+\mu|<AB^{-w}

in positive integers
u
,
v
and
w
with

u\leq M\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptw\geq\frac{\log(Aq/\epsilon)}{\log B}.

The above lemma cannot be applied when
\mu
is a linear combination of 1 and
\tau
, since then
\epsilon<0
. In this case, we use the following nice property of continued fractions (see Theorem 8.2.4 and top of page 263 in
[
13
]
)

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\tau
be an irrational number,
\frac{p_{0}}{q_{0}},\frac{p_{1}}{q_{1}},\frac{p_{2}}{q_{2}},\dots
be all the convergents of the continued fraction of
\tau
, and
M
be a positive integer. Let
N
be a non-negative integer such that
q_{N}>M
. Then putting
a(M):=\max\{a_{i}:i=0,1,2,\dots,N\}
, the inequality

\Bigm|x\tau-y\Bigm|>\frac{1}{(a(M)+2)x},

holds for all
x<M
.

2.3
Properties of
k
-Fibonacci sequence

We recall some of the facts and properties of the
k
-generalized Fibonacci sequence which will be used after. Note that the characteristic polynomial of the
k
-generalized Fibonacci sequence is

\Psi_{k}(x)=x^{k}-x^{k-1}-\dots-x-1.

\Psi_{k}(x)
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}[x]
and has just one root outside the unit circle. It is real and positive, so it satisfies
\varphi(k)>1
. The other roots are strictly inside the unit circle. Throughout this paper,
\varphi:=\varphi(k)
denotes that single root, which is located between
2(1-2^{-k})
and
2
(see
[
20
]
). To simplify notation, we will omit the dependence on
k
of
\varphi
. Dresden and Du
[
8
]
gave a simplified Binet-like formula for
F_{n}^{(k)}
:

F_{n}^{(k)}=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{k}(\varphi_{i})\varphi_{i}^{n-1}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\varphi_{i}-1}{2+(k+1)(\varphi_{i}-2)}\varphi_{i}^{n-1},

(2.6)

where
\varphi:=\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},\dots,\varphi_{k}
are the roots of the characteristic polynomial
\Psi_{k}(x)
. It was also shown in
[
8
]
that the contribution of the roots lying inside the unit circle to the formula (
2.6
) is very small, which is given by the approximation

\left|F_{n}^{(k)}-f_{k}(\varphi)\varphi^{n-1}\right|<\frac{1}{2}

(2.7)

holds for all
n\geq 1
and
k\geq 2
. So, for
n\geq 1
and
k\geq 2
, we have

F_{n}^{(k)}=f_{k}(\varphi)\varphi^{n-1}+e_{k}(n),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where}\hskip 10.00002pt|e_{k}(n)|\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(2.8)

Furthermore, it was shown by Bravo and Luca in
[
4
]
that the inequality

\varphi^{n-2}\leq F_{n}^{(k)}\leq\varphi^{n-1}\text{ holds for all }n\geq 1~{\rm and}~k\geq 2.

(2.9)

One can observe that the first
k+1
non-zero terms in
F_{n}^{(k)}
are powers of
2
, namely

F_{1}^{(k)}=1,F_{2}^{(k)}=1,F_{3}^{(k)}=2,F_{4}^{(k)}=4,\dots,F_{k+1}^{(k)}=2^{k-1},

while the next term in the above sequence is
F_{k+2}^{(k)}=2^{k}-1
. Thus, we have that

F_{n}^{(k)}=2^{n-2}~\text{holds for all}~2\leq n\leq k+1.

The following result was proved by Bravo and Luca
[
4
]
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
k\geq 2
,
\varphi
be the dominant root of
\{F_{n}^{(k)}\}_{n\geq-(k-2)}
, and consider the function defined in (
2.6
). Then

\frac{1}{2}<f_{k}(\varphi)<\frac{3}{4}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\left|f_{k}(\varphi^{(i)})\right|<1,\hskip 10.00002pt2\leq i\leq k.

In addition they proved that the logarithmic height of
f
satisfies

h(f_{k}(\varphi))<\log(k+1)+\log 4\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all}\hskip 10.00002ptk\geq 2.

(2.10)

Bravo et al.
[
5
]
proved that for all
n\geq k+2
, we have

F_{n}^{(k)}=2^{n-2}(1+\xi),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where}\hskip 10.00002pt|\xi|<\frac{1}{2^{k/2}}.

(2.11)

2.4
Other useful lemmas

We conclude this section by recalling two lemmas that we will need in this work.

Lemma 2.5
.

(
[
19
]
, Lemma 2.2)

Let
a,x\in\mathbb{R}
. If
0<a<1
and

|\log(1+x)|<\frac{-\log(1-a)}{a}\cdot|x|

.

\left|l\left(\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\varphi}\right)-(n-1)+\frac{\log\left(\frac{f_{k}^{-1}(\varphi)}{2F_{m}^{(k)}}\right)}{\log\varphi}\right|<\frac{12.26}{\varphi^{n-1}}.

(4.52)

To apply Lemma
2.2
to (
4.52
), this time for
1\leq m\leq 554
, we set

\tau=\frac{\log\gamma}{\log\varphi},\hskip 10.00002pt\mu=1+\frac{\log\left(\frac{f_{k}^{-1}(\varphi)}{2F_{m}^{(k)}}\right)}{\log\varphi},\hskip 10.00002ptA:=12.26\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptB:=\varphi.

Again, for
(k,m)\in[2,500]\times[1,554]
, we find a good
approximation of
\tau
and a convergent
p_{l}/q_{l}
of the continued fraction of
\tau
such that
\epsilon=\epsilon(k):=\|\mu q\|-M_{k}\|\tau q\|>0
, where
M_{k}:=\lfloor 3.28\times 10^{32}k^{8}\log^{5}k\rfloor
which is an upper bound of a from Lemma
4.1
. After doing this, we use Lemma
2.2

on inequality (
4.52
). Again, a program in Mathematica revealed that the maximum value of
\log(Aq/\epsilon)/\log B
over all
(k,m)\in[2,500]\times[1,554]
is
567.728
, which according to Lemma
2.2
, is an upper bound of
n-1
.

Hence, we deduce that the possible solutions
(l,k,n,m)
of (
1.5
) for which

k\in[2,500]
satisfy
k+2\leq m\leq n\leq 568
. Therefore, we use inequality (
4.36
) to obtain
l\leq 454
.
∎

4.4
The case when
k>500

This subsection aims to establish that there are no solutions for the case where
k>500
and
n\geq k+2
.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
(l,k,m,n)
is a solution of the Diophantine equation (
1.5
) with
k>500
and
n\geq k+2
, then
k
and
n
are bounded as

k<5.82\times 10^{14}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptn<1.97\times 10^{158}.

Proof.

For
k>500
, it is easy to see that

n<3.28\times 10^{32}k^{8}\log^{5}k<2^{k/2}.

Inserting (
1.1
) and (
2.11
) in (
1.5
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{l}}{2}-2^{m+n-4}=2^{m+n-4}\left(2\xi+\xi^{2}\right)-\frac{\delta^{l}}{2}.

As
n\geq m\geq k+2
, this and the fact
\left(2\xi+\xi^{2}\right)<3/2^{k/2}
yield

|\Lambda_{6}|=\left|\gamma^{l}2^{-(m+n-3)}-1\right|<\frac{3}{2^{k/2}}+\frac{1}{2^{2k}}<\frac{4}{2^{k/2}}.

(4.53)

But
\Lambda_{6}
is not zero. Indeed, if
\Lambda_{6}
were zero, we would then get that
\gamma^{l}=2^{m+n-3}\in\mathbb{Q}
, which is impossible. Therefore, we can apply Theorem
2.1
with

(\eta_{1},a_{1}):=(\gamma,l),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt(\eta_{2},a_{2}):=(2,-(m+n-3)).

Since
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}
are elements of the field
\mathbb{L}:=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})
, then
d_{\mathbb{L}}=[\mathbb{L}:\mathbb{Q}]=2
. The fact that
m\leq n
and the inequality (
4.36
) implies that we can choose
D:=2n
because
m+n-3\leq 2n
. On the other hand, since
h(\eta_{1})=\frac{\log\gamma}{2}
and
h(\eta_{2})=\log 2
. Thus, we can take

B_{1}:=\log\gamma
and
B_{2}:=2\log 2.
Thus, considering inequality (
4.53
) and using Theorem
2.1
, we get

k<8.52\times 10^{10}\log n,

(4.54)

where we have used the fact that
1+\log 2n<2.3\log n
for
n\geq 4
. In contrast, Lemma
4.1
leads to

\log n<\log(3.28\times 10^{32}k^{8}\log^{5}k)<74.87+8\log k+5\log(\log k)<114\log k

for
k\geq 2
. Thus, from (
4.53
), we get

k<9.72\times 10^{12}\log k.

Further using Lemma
2.6
and Lemma
4.1
the above inequality leads to

k<5.82\times 10^{14}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptn<1.97\times 10^{158}.

This finishes the proof Lemma
4.3
.
∎

The above lemma provides very large bounds for
n
and
k
. Our next goal is to minimize this upper bound to a suitable range, for which we shall prove the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

The equation (
1.5
) has no solutions for
k\geq 500
and
n\geq k+2
.

Proof.

Now, we lower the bound on
n
and
k
. We define

\Gamma_{6}:=l\log\gamma-(m+n-3)\log 2.

Since
k>500
, then from (
4.53
) we have
|\Lambda_{6}|<0.01
. Therefore by Lemma
2.5
, we have

|\Gamma_{6}|=|\log(\Lambda_{6}+1)|<\frac{-\log(1-0.01)}{0.01}\cdot\frac{4}{2^{k/2}}<\frac{4.1}{2^{k/2}}.

(4.55)

Thus, it follows that

\left|(m+n-3)\log 2-l\log\gamma\right|<\frac{4.1}{2^{k/2}}.

Dividing the above inequality by
\log\gamma
, we get

\left|(m+n-3)\left(\frac{\log 2}{\log\gamma}\right)-l\right|<\frac{2.33}{2^{k/2}}.

(4.56)

To obtain a lower bound for the left-hand side of (
4.56
), we will apply Lemma
2.3
with

\tau:=\frac{\log 2}{\log\gamma}\notin\mathbb{Q},\hskip 10.00002ptx:=m+n-3,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pty:=l.

The fact
m+n-3<2n
and
n:=1.97\times 10^{158}
by Lemma
4.3
imply that we can take
M:=3.94\times 10^{158}
. Let

[a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\dots]:=[0,2,1,1,5,3,2,1,22,1,5,38,1,1,1,8,1,3,7,1,5,2,\ 5,2,2,200,1,4,4,6\dots]

be the continued fraction expansion of
\tau
. A quick search using
Mathematica
reveals that

q_{301}<3.94\times 10^{158}<q_{302}.

Furthermore,
a_{M}:=\max\{a_{i}:i=0,1,\dots,302\}=4008
. So by Lemma
2.3
, we have

\left|(m+n-3)\left(\frac{\log 2}{\log\gamma}\right)-l\right|>\frac{1}{4010(m+n-3)}.

(4.57)

Comparing estimates (
4.57
), (
4.56
), and by Lemma
4.3
, we get

\frac{k}{2}<\frac{\log\left(2.33\times 4008\times 3.94\times 10^{158}\right)}{\log 2}<541,

which implies that
k\leq 1082
. With this new upper bound of
k
and by Lemma
4.1
, we have

n<1.1\times 10^{61}.

We now proceed as we did before but with
M:=2.2\times 10^{61}
and we obtain
k<430
, which contradicts our assumption
k>500
. This completes the proof of Lemma
4.4
.
∎

4.5
The final step

According to Lemma
4.2
and
4.4
, if
(l,k,n,m)
is a solution of the Diophantine equation (
1.5
) then

2\leq k\leq 500,\hskip 10.00002ptk+2\leq n\leq 568,\hskip 10.00002pt1\leq m\leq n\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt1\leq l\leq 454.

Using
Mathematica
, we checked that all the solutions of the Diophantine equation (
1.5
) are those listed in the statement of Theorem
1.2
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
