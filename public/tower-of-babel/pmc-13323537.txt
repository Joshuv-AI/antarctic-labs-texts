UBE2M deficiency in alveolar macrophages promotes emphysema through HIF-2α/MMP12 axis
Authors: Zhouyang Li, Dongyu Guo, Yong Wang, Haipin Chen, Na Li, Lingling Dong, Xuchen Xu, Yongchao Zhao, Yi Sun, Songmin Ying, Wen Li, Jiesen Zhou, Zhihua Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a leading cause of global mortality; however, its pathogenesis remains incompletely understood, limiting therapeutic options. Protein neddylation, a key post-translational modification, has been implicated in chronic inflammatory diseases, but its role in COPD remains largely unexplored. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the role of protein neddylation in the pathogenesis of COPD and to explore its potential as a novel therapeutic target.MethodsWe assessed the neddylation pathway in macrophages from patients with COPD and from mice exposed to cigarette smoke (CS). Myeloid-specific ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2 M (Ube2m) knockout (UBE2M-conditional knockout [cKO]) mice were generated to investigate the causal role of UBE2M in emphysema. Cell-type specificity was confirmed using club cell-specific knockout (CC10-TetOn-Flox [CTF]-UBE2M) and myeloid-specific ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2 F (Ube2f) knockout (UBE2F-cKO) mice. Transcriptomic profiling, molecular biology techniques, and pharmacological inhibition were employed to delineate the underlying mechanisms.ResultsAlveolar macrophages from COPD patients and CS-exposed mice exhibited significant downregulation of UBE2M. UBE2M-cKO was sufficient to induce spontaneous emphysema, characterized by increased lung volume, alveolar destruction, and impaired lung function, and it exacerbated CS-induced lung injury. This phenotype was specific to myeloid UBE2M-cKO, as neither CTF-UBE2M nor UBE2F-cKO recapitulated the disease. Transcriptomic profiling of UBE2M-cKO macrophages identified matrix metalloproteinase 12 (Mmp12) as a key mediator of the emphysema phenotype, given its macrophage-specific expression and potent elastin-degrading function. Mechanistically, UBE2M deficiency led to the stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor-2α (HIF-2α, encoded by Epas1), a known neddylation substrate, resulting in transcriptional upregulation of Mmp12. The HIF-2α inhibitor PT2385 attenuated Mmp12 upregulation, and double knockout of Ube2m and Epas1 (UBE2M-EPAS1-DKO) significantly ameliorated emphysema development.ConclusionOur study identifies UBE2M as a critical protector against emphysema by promoting HIF-2α degradation, thereby suppressing MMP12 expression. The UBE2M–HIF-2α–MMP12 axis represents a novel and specific pathogenic pathway in COPD, offering promising therapeutic targets.Keywords: Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Neddylation, Matrix metalloproteinase 12 (MMP12), Ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2 M (UBE2M), Hypoxia-inducible factor-2α (HIF-2α)

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive lung disorder driven by persistent inflammation, airway remodeling, and alveolar destruction, ultimately leading to irreversible airflow limitation.1, 2, 3, 4 It poses a substantial global health threat, being the third leading cause of mortality worldwide; in China, the prevalence of spirometry-defined COPD among adults aged over 40 is 13.7%.5,6 However, the precise pathogenesis of COPD remains poorly understood due to its marked heterogeneity.7 Consequently, existing treatments are largely confined to symptom management and delaying progression, underscoring the urgent need for mechanistic insights.

Neddylation is a ubiquitin-like posttranslational modification in which the ubiquitin-like molecule neural precursor cell expressed, developmentally downregulated 8 (NEDD8) is conjugated to target proteins, thereby regulating their activity and function.8 This three-step enzymatic cascade, analogous to ubiquitination, is mediated by an E1 enzyme (NEDD8-activating enzyme E1 subunit 1, NAE1), two E2 enzymes (ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme E2 M and E2 F [UBE2M and UBE2F]), and multiple E3 ligases.9 While UBE2F primarily neddylates canonical substrates like cullin proteins,10 UBE2M exhibits a broader substrate spectrum that includes non-cullin targets like p73 and cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS), indicating its distinct and critical roles.11,12 Protein neddylation has been implicated in the pathogenesis of various tumors,13 as well as chronic inflammatory diseases including atherosclerosis and chronic liver disease.14 However, the function of the neddylation-dependent cullin-RING ligase (CRL) machinery in the context of COPD remains poorly understood.

Matrix metalloproteinase-12 (MMP12), also known as macrophage elastase, plays a well-established role in COPD pathogenesis by degrading extracellular matrix components such as elastin, thereby contributing to alveolar destruction and emphysema progression.15,16 Although hypoxia-inducible factor-2 alpha (HIF-2α) can drive MMP12 expression under conditions such as hypoxia,17, 18, 19 the key regulatory mechanisms controlling its pathological overexpression in COPD macrophages remain a critical knowledge gap. Specifically, it is unclear whether this HIF-2α–MMP12 regulatory axis is functional and central to macrophage-driven COPD pathogenesis.

In this study, we aimed to explore the potential role and mechanisms of neddylation in regulating emphysema development. To this end, we examined neddylation pathway components in macrophages from COPD patients and cigarette smoke (CS)-exposed mice, and generated myeloid-specific knockout mice to investigate its functional significance. Our results demonstrate that UBE2M deficiency in alveolar macrophages promotes emphysema development through HIF-2α-mediated MMP12 hyperproduction.

Methods

Animals

Wild-type C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the Shanghai Laboratory Animal Center (Shanghai, China). Ube2mflox/flox mice and Ube2f flox/flox (C57BL/6 background) were gifted by Prof. Yi Sun (Zhejiang University School of Medicine), and endothelial PAS domain-containing protein 1 (Epas1)flox/flox mice (C57BL/6 background) were purchased from the GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Lysozyme M (LysM)Cre mice (C57BL/6 background) were purchased from the Cyagen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Secretoglobin family 1A member 1-tetracycline operator (Scgb1a1-TetO)Cre mice (C57BL/6 background) were gifted by Prof. Yuehai Ke (Zhejiang University School of Medicine).

Mice with myeloid-specific Ube2m or Ube2f conditional knockout (cKO) were generated by crossing Ube2mflox/flox or Ube2fflox/flox mice with mice expressing Cre recombinase under the control of the lysozyme promoter (LysMCre). Mice with myeloid-specific Ube2m and Epas1 double knockout were generated by crossing Ube2mflox/flox-LysMCre mice with Epas1flox/flox mice. Club cell-specific Ube2m inducible knockout (Scgb1a1-TetOCre-Ube2mflox/flox) mice were generated by crossbreeding Scgb1a1-TetOCre and Ube2mflox/flox mice. In order to induce the expression of Cre, 6-week-old mice were fed with doxycycline in drinking water (2 mg/mL) for 20 days before any experiment. Age- and sex-matched LysMCre or Scgb1a1-TetOCre-negative littermates were used as the corresponding control group.

All mice were maintained in the animal facility of The Laboratory Animal Centre of Zhejiang University. Research on mouse samples was approved by the ZJU-Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Review (No. ZJU20240610).

Human study subjects

We enrolled healthy controls and stable COPD patients undergoing bronchoscopy for diagnostic evaluation (e.g., pulmonary nodule assessment). Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected from the contralateral (unaffected) lung of these patients. The diagnosis of COPD was made according to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) guidelines. All COPD patients were clinically stable, with no exacerbation or change in medication within the preceding 4 weeks. Exclusion criteria were as follows: any chronic cardiopulmonary disease other than COPD and an inability to give written informed consent or cooperate with the study investigators. Clinical characteristics are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Study protocols involving human samples, including informed consent, demographic data publication, and corresponding source data, were approved by the Institutional Review Board, Second Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. IRB Protocol 2021P00476). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Antibodies and reagents

Selective neddylation-activating enzyme inhibitor MLN4924 (S7109) and selective HIF-2α inhibitor PT2385 (S8352) were purchased from Selleck (Houston, TX, United States). The following antibodies were used: CD14 (D1U2Y) rabbit monoclonal antibody (mAb) (93882, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, United States), monoclonal mouse anti-UBE2M (sc-390064, Santa Cruz, Dallas, TX, United States), phycoerythrin (PE)/cyanine7 anti-mouse F4/80 antibody (123113, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, United States), allophycocyanin (APC) anti-mouse CD170 (Siglec-F) antibody (155507, BioLegend), fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) anti-mouse CD11c antibody (117305, BioLegend), Brilliant Violet 421™ donkey anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) (406410, BioLegend), monoclonal rabbit anti-UBE2M antibody (ab109507, Abcam, Cambridge, Cambridgeshire, United Kingdom), NEDD8 (19E3) rabbit mAb (2754, Cell Signaling Technology), monoclonal mouse anti-UBE2F (sc-398668, Santa Cruz), polyclonal rabbit anti-MMP12 antibody (ab137443, Abcam), polyclonal rabbit anti-EPAS1 antibody (GTX30114, GeneTex, Irvine, CA, United States), monoclonal mouse anti-beta-actin (ACTB) (sc-58673, Santa Cruz), goat anti-mouse IgG horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (A0350, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), goat anti-rabbit IgG HRP (A0352, Beyotime), and 4′,6-diamidino-2′-phenylindole (DAPI) (MA0127, Meilunbio, Shanghai, China).

In vivo cigarette smoke exposures

Mice were exposed to cigarette smoke (2.5 h/day, 100 cigarettes, 5 days/week) for either 8 weeks (for protein analysis, Fig. 1) or 12 weeks (for emphysema evaluations, Fig. 2 and thereafter) in a stainless‑steel whole‑body exposure chamber (TE‑10; Teague Enterprises, Woodland, CA, USA). The cigarettes used were Marlboro Filter (manufactured by Longyan Tobacco Industrial Co., Ltd. [Longyan, Fujian, China] under license from Philip Morris Products S.A. [Lausanne, Switzerland]) with a tar content of 10 mg/cigarette and nicotine content of 0.8 mg/cigarette. Total particulate matter concentrations in the exposure chamber were between 150 and 180 mg·m−3. Serum cotinine levels measured immediately after cigarette smoke exposure were around 20 ng·mL−1. Control groups were exposed to filtered room air.

Mouse BALF collection

Mice were sacrificed using an overdose of 2% pentobarbital sodium and a tracheotomy was performed. Each mouse was lavaged three times with 0.4 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) injected into the lungs and withdrawn. Total cell counts were measured using a cell counter (CounterStar, Ruiyu Biotech, Shanghai, China). The remaining BALF was centrifuged (400 × g for 10 min at 4°C). The supernatant was retained for further analysis, while the cell pellet was resuspended in PBS gently and cytospun onto glass slides. Then cells on glass slides were stained with Wright-Giemsa (BA-4017, Baso, Zhuhai, Guangdong, China), and differential counts were assessed by counting 200 total cells.

Tissue preparations for single cell suspension and flow cytometry

Lung tissues were digested in a solution containing 1 mg/mL collagenase A (17100-017, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, United States) and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (10104159001, Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, United States) at 37°C for 1 h with rotation. The digested tissue was filtered through a 40 μm filter, and red blood cells (RBCs) were lysed using RBC lysis buffer (420301, Biolegend) for 3 min. Single-cell suspensions were incubated with anti-mouse purified CD16/32 antibodies for 15 min at 4°C for Fc receptor blocking. Then single-cell suspensions were stained with fixable viability dye and fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against surface markers at 4°C for 30 min in the dark. Alveolar macrophages were identified as live F4/80+ Siglec-F+ CD11c+ cells; interstitial macrophages were identified as live F4/80+ Siglec-F−. For intracellular detection of NEDD8 and UBE2M, cells were fixed and permeabilized with intracellular fixation buffer (88-8824-00, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, United States) for 60 min at room temperature. Subsequently, cells were incubated with primary antibodies against NEDD8 and UBE2M for 30 min at room temperature, followed by incubation with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 30 min at 4°C in the dark. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using Cytoflex (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, United States), and all the results were analyzed using Flowjo X software (Treestar, Ashland, OR, United States).

Isolation and in vitro culture of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) and peritoneal macrophage (pMAC)

BMDMs were obtained and cultured from the bone marrow of 6- to 8-week-old mice. Briefly, mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation and soaked in 75% ethanol. The femurs and tibias were harvested and the bone marrow cells from all bones were flushed out. After centrifuging for 5 min at 400 × g, erythrocytes were eliminated using RBC lysis buffer. The remaining cells were seeded in plates and were incubated in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (BC-M-005, Biochannel, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) containing 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (DCF-201-0500, DCELL Biologics, Shanghai, China), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (C0222, Beyotime), and 10 ng/mL recombinant mouse macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) (CB34, Novoprotein, Suzhou, Jiangsu, China) for 7 days to form proliferative nonactivated cells. On day 3 and day 5 of culture, the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM.

Primary mouse peritoneal macrophages were obtained from the peritoneal exudates of 6- to 8-week-old mice that had been administered 1 mL of fluid thioglycolate medium via three intraperitoneal injections. The peritoneal exudate cells were washed twice with PBS and were cultured in DMEM for 3–4 h at 37°C and 5% CO2. The nonadherent cells were removed by washing with warm PBS.

Western blotting

The lysates of BALF and BMDM cells were prepared with radio-immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (P0013B, Beyotime) containing protease (04-693-116-001, Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Baden-Württemberg, Germany) and phosphatase inhibitors (04-906-837-001, Roche Diagnostics GmbH). The supernatants of cell lysates were run on gels and incubated with relevant antibodies using standard methods. ACTB was used as a loading control. Densitometric analysis of western blot bands was performed using ImageJ software (version 1.54f, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, United States).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

For immunoprecipitation analysis, cells were lysed in Co-IP lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013F) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, 04-693-116-001). The lysates were incubated with the indicated primary antibodies at 4°C overnight, followed by incubation with protein A/G agarose resin (6403ES05, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) for an additional 4 h at 4°C. Immunocomplexes were then washed four times with the same lysis buffer and subjected to western blotting analysis.

In vivo neddylation assays

Plasmids encoding His-NEDD8 (P56787, pCMV-NEDD8 [human]-6 × His-Neo), Flag-HIF-2α (P48938, pCMV-EPAS1 [human]-3 × FLAG-Neo), and corresponding control vectors (P6785, pCDNA3.1-MCS-6 × His and P39495, pCMV-T7-MCS-3 × FLAG-WPRE-Neo) were purchased from MiaoLingBio, Inc (Wuhan, Hubei, China). All constructs were confirmed by DNA sequencing (BGI, Shenzhen, Guangdong, China). UBE2M small interfering RNA (sc-76786) was purchased from Santa Cruz. For co-transfection experiments, HEK293T cells were seeded and then co-transfected with His-NEDD8 plasmid together with either Flag-HIF-2α plasmid or UBE2M small interfering RNA (siRNA) (or both, as indicated) for 48 h. Plasmid transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States), while co-transfection with siRNA was carried out using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (13778150, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following this, the cells were collected and disrupted in a denaturing lysis buffer. After sonication, the lysates were subjected to centrifugation at 13,000 revolutions per minute (12,000 × g, r = 6.35 cm) for 10 min using a Micro 21 centrifuge (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The supernatant was then incubated with nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) agarose beads for 4 h at ambient temperature. The beads were sequentially washed for 5 minutes each with the denaturing lysis buffer (His-Tag Protein Purification Kit with NTA-Ni Magnetic Agarose Beads, P2247S, Beyotime). The neddylated proteins were subsequently eluted from the beads using an elution buffer for 30 min at room temperature, before being analyzed by western blotting.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

ChIP was performed using the Pierce™ Magnetic ChIP Kit (#26157, Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde and quenched with glycine. Chromatin was extracted and digested with micrococcal nuclease (MNase) to achieve fragment sizes of 200–500 bp, followed by sonication to ensure complete nuclear lysis. An aliquot of sheared chromatin was saved as “input”. Immunoprecipitation was carried out overnight at 4°C using 5 µL of anti-HIF-2α antibody per reaction. Protein A/G magnetic beads supplied with the kit were then added to capture antibody–chromatin complexes. After serial washes, crosslinking was reversed by overnight incubation at 65°C, and DNA was purified using the spin columns provided in the kit.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

RNA from cultured cells, BALF cells and lung homogenates was isolated with Trizol (15596026, Invitrogen). Reverse transcription was performed with reverse transcription reagents (DRR037A, Takara Biotechnology, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan). RT-PCR was performed using the StepOnePlus PCR system and gene expression assays (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, United States). A ΔΔCt method was used to quantify messenger RNA (mRNA) levels. Gene expression was quantified using Applied Biosystems and Microsoft Excel software (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, United States). Each sample was assayed in triplicate. PCR primers used are shown in Supplementary Table 2.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Fluorescence images were visualized using a high-resolution laser confocal microscope (IX83-FV3000-OSR, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and processed with FV31S-SW software (Olympus). Cells were fixed and stained with primary antibody at 37°C for 2 h. Secondary antibody was used to visualize the bound primary antibody. Cell micrographs were obtained using a fluorescence microscope. The relative fluorescence intensity was measured with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, United States), and the mean relative fluorescence intensities were normalized to the levels of control.

Enzyme–linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The concentrations of MMP12 (KOA0622, Rockland, Gilbertsville, PA, United States), neutrophil elastase (NE) (DY9167-05, R&D, Minneapolis, MN, United States), and mouse cotinine (EA100902, OriGene, Rockville, MD, United States) in serum were determined with ELISA kits following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Measurement of lung function

Mice were sedated with intraperitoneal pentobarbital sodium (0.1 g/kg, with 1% solution), and the dose could be adjusted as necessary. The mice were intubated with a 2-inch-long, 14-gauge angiocatheter which was then connected to a rodent-specific forced maneuver system (Pulmonary Function Testing, Buxco Research Systems, Wilmington, NC, United States). A series of preprogrammed forced ventilation maneuvers were used to measure forced vital capacity (FVC) and forced expiratory volume in 20 seconds (FEV20) within 10 min. Each measurement was performed a minimum of three times to ensure reasonable repeatability.

Morphological analysis of lung sections

Paraformaldehyde-fixed and paraffin-embedded lung sections were prepared and serial longitudinal sections (3-μm-thick) stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) were used for morphological analysis. Ten randomly selected (100×) fields per slide were photographed using an Olympus BX53 inverted microscope (Olympus, Melville, NY, United States). The images were analyzed using the modified Image J software (National Institutes of Health). From each field, 10–12 areas of interest, free of airways and blood vessels, were picked for measurement of the number of intersections of virtual lines of known length, with alveolar septa. An increase in the average distance between intercepts (mean linear intercept, MLI) indicates enlarged airspaces.

Bioinformatic analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data

To investigate the transcriptomic landscape of lung macrophages in the context of COPD, we retrieved the publicly available scRNA-seq dataset GSE173896 from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). This dataset comprises single-cell transcriptomes derived from the lung tissues of COPD patients and healthy controls. Data processing and downstream analyses were implemented using R (version 4.3.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and the Seurat package (version 4.4.0, Satija Lab, New York, NY, United States). The macrophage population was extracted from the public dataset based on the original cell type annotations provided in the metadata. The identity of these cells was further verified by the expression of canonical markers, including apolipoprotein C1 (APOC1), macrophage receptor with collagenous structure (MARCO), lysyl-tRNA synthetase interacting protein 1 (LGMN), and mannose receptor C-type 1 (MRC1). To further investigate the specific roles of macrophages in COPD, we quantified the expression of EPAS1, UBE2M, and MMP12 using log-normalized relative abundance for inter-group comparisons.

RNA sequencing (RNA–seq)

Total RNA samples extracted from BMDMs of Ube2mflox/flox-LysMCre mice and control littermates were processed and analyzed by BGI. RNAseq data had been uploaded in China National Center for Bioinformation under BioProject ID: PRJCA049686.

Survival rate

For survival assay, mice were fed under normal condition. The survival percentages and body weights in each group were monitored weekly started at the age of 12 weeks. Death was seen as being equal to body weight loss of >20%.

Statistical analysis

For experiments involving three or more groups, one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess overall differences. Post hoc comparisons for pre‑selected pairs of groups were performed using Šídák’s multiple comparisons test to control the family‑wise error rate. For experiments comparing only two groups, an unpaired Student’s t‑test was applied. All data are expressed as mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). Statistical analyses and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, United States). A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Decreased UBE2M expression in alveolar macrophages from COPD patients and CS-exposed mice

To elucidate the possible role of neddylation in COPD pathogenesis, we first collected BALF from COPD patients and healthy controls (Supplementary Table 1). Cell pellets from the BALF were subjected to immunofluorescence staining using CD14 as a macrophage marker and co-staining for UBE2M. We observed that UBE2M expression in macrophages from COPD patients was significantly lower than that in the control group (Fig. 1A and B). Next, we isolated BALF cells, which are predominantly alveolar macrophages (AMs), from CS-exposed mice and performed western blotting to analyze the neddylation signaling pathway. Although the overall decrease in NEDD8-conjugated cullins was less pronounced than the reduction in UBE2M mean fluorescence intensity detected by flow cytometry, and NAE1 levels remained largely unchanged, UBE2M expression was significantly decreased in the smoke-exposed group, consistent with the findings in COPD patients (Fig. 1C and D). To validate these observations, we established a classic CS-exposed mouse model of COPD. Single-cell suspensions from mouse lung tissue were obtained, and flow cytometry was used to detect NEDD8 and UBE2M expression in AMs and interstitial macrophages (IMs). The results showed that the decrease in neddylation levels in the CS-exposed model was primarily localized to AMs rather than IMs (Fig. 1E and F).

Myeloid-specific UBE2M deletion triggers spontaneous emphysema and exacerbates CS-induced lung injury

To further investigate the cell-specific role of UBE2M in the lungs, we generated Ube2mflox/flox mice and crossbred them with LysMCre mice, resulting in myeloid-specific knockout of the Ube2m gene (hereafter referred to as UBE2M-cKO, Fig. 2A).20 Notably, we observed an increase in the total BALF cell count in the knockout group, primarily due to an elevated number of macrophages (Fig. 2B). This macrophage-dominant inflammatory response under basal conditions was consistent with the classic CS exposure model of COPD.21 We next examined other COPD-like phenotypes in these mice. The body weight of UBE2M-cKO mice was significantly lower than that of the wild-type (WT) group, whereas their lung volume was relatively larger, both features consistent with typical COPD phenotypes (Fig. 2C).22 Quantitative analysis of the MLI further confirmed that UBE2M-cKO mice exhibited a spontaneous emphysema phenotype (Fig. 2D). Enlarged alveolar septa were associated with a marked decline in forced expiratory volume in 20 seconds (FEV20), forced vital capacity (FVC), and the FEV20/FVC ratio (Fig. 2E), which are critical markers of airway obstruction. Interestingly, UBE2M-cKO mice even showed a tendency toward early death compared with their WT littermates (Fig. 2F), a finding that may be related to the COPD-associated aging process.

Next, we established a 12-week CS exposure-induced COPD model in UBE2M-cKO mice. As expected, UBE2M-cKO mice exhibited more pronounced alveolar damage, quantitatively reflected by a greater increase in MLI (Fig. 2G). Additionally, the cumulative weight gain of knockout mice lagged behind that of the control group over time (Fig. 2H), consistent with the emaciation commonly observed in COPD patients.

Transcriptomic profiling identifies MMP12 as a key target in UBE2M-cKO macrophages

To examine the potential mechanisms underlying the spontaneous emphysema observed in UBE2M-cKO mice, we performed RNA-seq on BMDMs from UBE2M-cKO mice, and this revealed a dysregulated gene network comprising 185 upregulated and 117 downregulated genes (Fig. 3A). Among these, Mmp12 emerged as a key candidate mediating the emphysema phenotype, given its macrophage-specific expression and potent elastin-degrading function.23 Consistent with this, heatmap analysis confirmed that Mmp12 was significantly upregulated in the UBE2M-cKO group (Fig. 3B). To further validate the RNA-seq findings, we isolated AMs, peritoneal macrophages (pMAC), and BMDMs from WT and UBE2M-cKO mice. RT-qPCR, ELISA, and immunofluorescence analyses corroborated the RNA-seq results at both the transcriptional (Fig. 3C upper panel) and translational levels (Fig. 3C lower panel and 3D), with MMP12 showing the most pronounced upregulation in AMs.

UBE2M deficiency stabilizes HIF-2α to upregulate MMP12 and promote emphysema

Neddylation is generally considered a posttranslational modification. However, UBE2M-cKO mice exhibited increased MMP12 expression at both the transcriptional and translational levels, suggesting that MMP12 may not be a direct substrate of neddylation. We therefore hypothesized that loss of UBE2M may lead to the accumulation of a critical neddylation substrate that acts upstream of MMP12, thereby enhancing its transcription. Previous studies have identified EPAS1, also known as hypoxia-inducible factor-2 alpha (HIF-2α), as a key upstream transcriptional regulator of MMP1217,18 and as a proven substrate of the neddylation-cullin 2 complex.24,25 To further test this hypothesis, we performed transcriptional profiling of Mmp genes in lung tissues, AMs and cultured BMDMs from UBE2M-cKO mice. In addition to the increase in Mmp12, we found that other HIF-2α-regulated MMPs, including Mmp3, Mmp9, Mmp13, and Ptgs2, were also upregulated to varying degrees. In contrast, MMPs unrelated to HIF-2α, such as Mmp2, Mmp14, Mmp15, and Nos2, showed no significant changes with only a few exceptions (Fig. 4A). These findings suggest that loss of UBE2M may impair HIF-2α degradation, leading to downstream transcriptional activation.

To validate our hypothesis, we cultured BMDMs in a hypoxic incubator (1% O2). We observed that hypoxia, similar to MLN4924 (a known NAE1 inhibitor)-mediated inhibition of neddylation,26 induced an increase in MMP12 levels, suggesting that hypoxia is indeed an important regulator of MMP12 (Fig. 4B). Next, we found significant accumulation of HIF-2α protein in BMDMs from mice with MLN4924-mediated neddylation inhibition (Fig. 4C), confirming that HIF-2α is a direct substrate of neddylation.25 Notably, transcripts of key molecules in the HIF signaling pathway, such as Hif1α, Epas1, Arnt, Egln1, and Fih, were unaffected by UBE2M deficiency, further supporting a posttranslational mechanism (Fig. 4D). To investigate whether UBE2M regulates HIF-2α occupancy at the Mmp12 promoter, we performed ChIP-qPCR using an anti-HIF-2α antibody. The percentage of input enrichment at the Mmp12 promoter was significantly increased in UBE2M-cKO cells compared with WT cells (Fig. 4E).

Finally, we applied the HIF-2α-specific inhibitor PT2385,27,28 which blocks subsequent transcription by preventing the dimerization of HIF-2α with aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator (ARNT)/hypoxia-inducible factor-1β (HIF1β). PT2385 treatment dose-dependently attenuated the upregulation of Mmp12 transcription caused by impaired neddylation (Fig. 4F). Moreover, co-IP assays revealed that Flag-HIF-2α co-precipitated endogenous UBE2M, and this interaction was abolished by UBE2M knockdown (Fig. 4G, right panel). Furthermore, UBE2M depletion reduced the NEDD8 signals pulled down by anti-His or anti-NEDD8 antibodies (Fig. 4G, left panels), indicating that UBE2M is required for HIF-2α neddylation in cells. In vivo, myeloid-specific Ube2m single knockout exacerbated cigarette smoke-induced emphysema compared with WT mice, indicating that loss of UBE2M hyperactivates downstream pathogenic signaling. Strikingly, additional deletion of Epas1 on the Ube2m-deficient background (UBE2M–EPAS1–DKO) completely rescued this aggravated phenotype, with emphysema severity markedly reduced to near air-control levels (Fig. 4H). To further validate the clinical relevance of the UBE2M–HIF-2α–MMP12 axis, we analyzed a publicly available single-cell RNA-seq dataset of human COPD lungs (GSE173896). In alveolar macrophages from COPD patients, UBE2M expression was significantly decreased, while EPAS1 and MMP12 were upregulated compared to healthy non-smokers (Fig. 4I).

Airway epithelial UBE2M and myeloid UBE2F are dispensable for spontaneous emphysema development

Finally, we sought to confirm the pivotal role of macrophage UBE2M in preventing emphysema. Although epithelial cells constitute a major fraction of the lung cellular composition, we focused on a specific epithelial subset, club cells, to conditionally delete Ube2m. Club cell-specific Ube2m knockout mice (Scgb1a1-TetOCre-Ube2mflox/flox, hereafter referred to as CTF-UBE2M) were generated by crossbreeding Scgb1a1-TetOCre and Ube2mflox/flox, followed by doxycycline induction to drive Cre expression and Ube2m deletion. Deletion efficiency of UBE2M was confirmed (Fig. 5A). Under basal conditions, no significant difference in lung MLI was observed between CTF-UBE2M mice and their control littermates (Fig. 5B).

Additionally, as mentioned earlier, the neddylation process involves two classical E2 conjugating enzymes,9 UBE2M and UBE2F. To clarify whether emphysema progression is UBE2M-dependent, we generated myeloid-specific Ube2f knockout mice (Ube2fflox/flox-LysMCre, hereafter referred to as UBE2F-cKO) (Fig. 5C). We then measured lung MLI and MMP12 levels in the BALF of UBE2F-cKO mice. In contrast to UBE2M-cKO mice, UBE2F-cKO mice did not exhibit enlarged alveolar septa (Fig. 5D) or elevated MMP12 expression (Fig. 5E).

Beyond the well-established role of MMP12 in elastin degradation and emphysema pathogenesis, NE represents another major elastase capable of disrupting the extracellular matrix.29,30 To investigate whether NE contributed to the emphysematous phenotype in UBE2M-cKO mice, we measured its levels in BALF. NE levels were comparable between the groups (Fig. 5F), indicating that the observed increase in MLI was primarily driven by elevated MMP12, not NE.

Discussion

In this study, we uncovered a previously unrecognized role for the neddylation pathway, specifically mediated by the E2-conjugating enzyme UBE2M in macrophages, in the pathogenesis of COPD and emphysema. Our findings delineate a mechanistic axis wherein CS-induced downregulation of UBE2M leads to stabilization of HIF-2α, subsequent transcriptional upregulation of MMP12, and ultimately the development of emphysematous lung destruction (Fig. 6).

Our work represents an early introduction of the ubiquitin-like protein neddylation into the field of non-malignant respiratory research. While the neddylation pathway has been extensively investigated and targeted therapeutically in oncology,31, 32, 33 its role in chronic inflammatory lung diseases has remained largely unknown. Previous research has predominantly focused on other signaling cascades, such as mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR),34,35 nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB),36 and various kinase-driven pathways,37, 38, 39, 40 to explain the persistent inflammation and tissue destruction in COPD. Our work fundamentally expands this molecular landscape by positioning neddylation, a master regulator of protein stability and function, as a central pathogenic switch. This newly identified pathway offers a novel lens through which to view COPD heterogeneity. Variability in patient presentation and disease progression may partly stem from polymorphisms or differential expression within the neddylation machinery (e.g., UBE2M, NAE1) or its downstream effectors (e.g., EPAS1). Thus, our study opens a new subfield, inviting future investigations into whether neddylation dysregulation underpins other obstructive diseases and how it may intersect with established pathogenic mechanisms such as the protease–antiprotease imbalance.

COPD has traditionally been viewed as an adult-onset disease driven predominantly by environmental toxins, most notably CS.41 However, the study of rare inherited forms of emphysema has reshaped our understanding of its pathogenesis, revealing critical vulnerabilities in lung homeostasis.42 The most compelling evidence for a single pathway driving emphysema comes from genetic disorders.43 Seminal work on alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency established the protease–antiprotease imbalance hypothesis.44,45 Our findings extend this paradigm by demonstrating that a primary macrophage defect, deficiency in UBE2M, is sufficient to trigger spontaneous emphysema, independent of epithelial injury. This places macrophage UBE2M as a non-redundant guardian of alveolar integrity. Furthermore, we connect this finding to a conserved transcriptional module, showing that the HIF-2α–MMP12 axis, previously implicated in conditions like osteoarthritis,17,18 is critically controlled by UBE2M in COPD macrophages. This consistency across disease models underscores the fundamental biological relevance of this pathway in tissue remodeling.

By identifying UBE2M-mediated neddylation as a critical vulnerability node, our work integrates the pathophysiology of common COPD with the mechanistic clarity of genetic models. We propose that the loss of this pathway, whether through inheritance or acquisition, defines a distinct pathogenic subset of COPD driven by dysregulated macrophage proteostasis. This reframing not only deepens our understanding of disease origins, but also illuminates a novel and druggable pathway for therapeutic intervention.

However, our study has certain limitations that warrant consideration. While we established the necessity of myeloid UBE2M in spontaneous and CS‑induced emphysema, we did not examine the impact of epithelial‑specific Ube2m deletion (beyond club cells) or myeloid Ube2f deletion in the CS‑induced COPD model. The club cell‑specific Ube2m knockout did not result in spontaneous emphysema under basal conditions, but this does not exclude a potential role for UBE2M in other epithelial lineages (e.g., alveolar type II cells or ciliated cells). Similarly, the absence of spontaneous emphysema in UBE2F‑cKO mice suggests that the observed phenotype is UBE2M‑specific, at least under baseline conditions. Nevertheless, their contributions under chronic smoke exposure remain to be directly tested.

Regarding the human data, our current sample size is limited, and the COPD patients enrolled were not exclusively those with a pure emphysema phenotype. Therefore, future studies with larger, well‑defined cohorts, specifically focusing on patients with emphysema‑predominant COPD, are necessary to robustly analyze the correlation between UBE2M expression in alveolar macrophages and key clinical parameters such as forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1)% predicted and smoking pack‑years. To provide additional support for our findings within the constraints of the current revision period, we preliminarily analyzed several publicly available single‑cell RNA‑seq datasets of human COPD lung tissues. Some (e.g., GSE173896) confirmed that UBE2M expression is consistently decreased in alveolar macrophages from COPD patients compared to healthy controls, aligning with our experimental conclusions. Nonetheless, large‑scale prospective studies are needed to validate UBE2M as a clinically relevant biomarker.

Concerning the mechanistic experiments, we acknowledge that AMs are the most physiologically relevant cell type for transcriptomic profiling. However, the yield of primary AMs from mouse bronchoalveolar lavage is extremely limited, and these cells exhibit reduced viability and activation-induced stress upon isolation, which could confound RNA-seq results. We therefore used BMDMs for the initial transcriptomic screen and subsequently validated the key findings in primary AMs, peritoneal macrophages, and BMDMs, all of which showed consistent phenotypic alterations. This multi-cell-type validation supports the robustness of our conclusions, but we recognize that future studies employing AM-specific RNA-seq under optimized low-stress conditions would further strengthen the analysis.

Finally, regarding the cell‑type specificity of the phenotype, our Ube2m‑LysMCre conditional knockout mice specifically delete Ube2m in the myeloid lineage, and the observed spontaneous emphysema directly demonstrates that macrophage UBE2M deficiency is sufficient to drive the pathological changes. To exclude potential contributions from other myeloid cells, we measured NE levels in BALF and found no difference between UBE2M‑cKO and control mice, indicating that neutrophil activation is unlikely to account for the emphysema phenotype. Nevertheless, we agree that definitive proof of causality would be further strengthened by macrophage‑specific rescue experiments or bone marrow adoptive transfer studies. We have added this as an important direction for future investigation.

Overall, our findings open several compelling avenues for future investigation. Most pressingly, the precise mechanism by which CS exposure triggers UBE2M downregulation in AMs remains unclear and warrants exploration of potential epigenetic regulation or oxidative stress responses. Furthermore, our characterization of HIF-2α as a key substrate likely represents only the initial component of a broader regulatory network; systematic profiling of the global neddylome in COPD macrophages promises to reveal additional pathogenic substrates of UBE2M. Most importantly, the compelling translational potential of this axis is underscored by the efficacy of HIF-2α inhibition in our models, suggesting that repurposing existing antagonists could offer a viable therapeutic strategy for a patient subset characterized by low UBE2M expression or high HIF-2α activity.

In summary, by mapping a previously unrecognized neddylation-dependent pathway in COPD pathogenesis, our work provides a new conceptual framework for understanding disease heterogeneity. It charts a course for future mechanistic discovery and positions the UBE2M–HIF-2α–MMP12 axis as a promising target for the development of personalized therapeutic interventions.
