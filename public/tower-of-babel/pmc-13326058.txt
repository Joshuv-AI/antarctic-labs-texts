Urdu translation and cross-cultural adaptation of fall risk awareness questionnaire for older adults
Authors: Rikza Naseer, Anong Tantisuwat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile numerous assessment tools exist, their availability in local languages ensures their applicability and requires cross-cultural adaptation. This study aimed to cross-culturally adapt and evaluate psychometric properties of the Urdu version of the Fall Risk Awareness Questionnaire (FRAQ) among older adults, assessing its reliability and preliminary validity in this population.MethodsTwo hundred older adults aged between 60 and 85 participated in the study. The FRAQ was translated into Urdu by two native language experts, followed by consensus-building and back-translation by two other bilingual translators to ensure linguistic and cultural equivalence. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, and construct validity was examined through correlation analysis with the Urdu version of the Falls Efficacy Scale-International (FES-I). The content validity index (item-level and scale-level CVI) was used to measure content validity.ResultsUrdu FRAQ was found to have moderate to good internal consistency according to Cronbach’s alpha (α = 0.747). A weak but statistically significant relationship exists between FRAQ scores and FES-I scores (Spearman rho, = 0. 144, p = 0.042), thus providing evidence of construct validity. The questionnaire presented satisfactory content validity with lower CVI values on the item level and acceptable on the scale level. Exploratory Factor analysis supported a predominantly unidimensional structure. All items demonstrated acceptable loadings.ConclusionThe study concluded that the Urdu version of the fall risk awareness questionnaire is satisfactorily reliable, culturally appropriate, and demonstrated preliminary validity for assessing awareness of fall risks in Urdu-speaking older adults.Keywords: Adaptation, Cross-cultural, Falls, Older adults, Risk Awareness

Introduction

Falls are a major cause of injuries and disability, with significant psychological and economic consequences, and hospitalization of older adults across the globe [1, 2]. Therefore, it is imperative to introduce educational and behavioral programs to improve mobility [3]. With the demographic trend towards ageing, especially in low-income and-middle income nations, the number of falls has increased, which highlights the need to implement culturally sensitive fall-prevention strategies [4].

Pakistan is experiencing a demographic shift where the percentage of older adults is increasing, and accompanying it is the corresponding increase in the morbidity of falls [5]. High fall incidence and a high fear of falling prevalence among Pakistani older adults have been documented in a community-based study done previously [6, 7]. However, the preventive efforts may be limited due to the lack of fall-risk factor awareness and obstacles to health-education spread, such as language and cultural differences [8]. Instruments that are administered in English might lack the ability to measure the experiences or knowledge of the Urdu-speaking population, and limit the ability of clinicians to detect gaps in fall-risk awareness and provide appropriate educational activities [9, 10].

The Fall Risk Awareness Questionnaire (FRAQ) was first developed by Sadowski et al., designed to assess the knowledge of older adults regarding the risk factors associated with falls [11]. Unlike other scales that assess concern or apprehension about falls, such as the Falls Efficacy Scale-International (FES-I), the FRAQ explicitly aims at the awareness and understanding of the factors that increase the likelihood of a fall by the participant [12]. The constructs are related, but they are theoretically still different: people can be afraid of falling without having the relevant knowledge about fall prevention methods, and vice versa; therefore, a better understanding of strategies does not always imply anxiety. On this basis, the FRAQ is a special resource in the form of teaching and screening aid that can enlighten specific preventative programs [13].

The FRAQ has been translated and tested in a variety of language settings, such as Brazilian Portuguese [13], and Malay [14] with acceptable reliability and validity. These adaptations highlight the possibility and suitability of using the FRAQ with different populations. However, an approved Urdu translation does not exist currently. Considering that Urdu is the national language of Pakistan, with both extensive use in the South Asian diaspora across the globe, the lack of a culturally sensitive FRAQ will impede clinical assessment, health-education programs, and studies related to fall-risk awareness among Urdu-speaking older adults in the South Asian region.

The need to cross-culturally adapt assessment tools leads to systematic translation, the assessment of cultural correspondence, and psychometric testing to ensure that the items can maintain the conceptual meaning and their measurement qualities in their target population [15]. The Urdu translation of the FRAQ would enable clinicians, public health practitioners, and researchers to more effectively investigate knowledge gaps, provide preventive counseling, and assess the effectiveness of fall-prevention interventions in Urdu-speaking populations.

Based on this, the current research aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and assess the psychometric quality of the Urdu version of the Fall Risk Awareness Questionnaire for use with community-dwelling older adults in Pakistan. In particular, the research investigated content validity, internal consistency, test-retest reliability, and initial evidence of construct validity. An effective and culturally competent Urdu FRAQ may serve as a valuable move towards better fall-prevention assessment and education of this group of people.

Method

The current methodological study involved translation, cross-cultural adaptation, and psychometric evaluation of the Urdu version of the FRAQ in Urdu-speaking older adults. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Chulalongkorn University (COA: 129/67) and the Bajwa Hospital Ethics Committee (Ref. No. BH/PTD/049). Permission for the use and translation of the FRAQ was obtained from the original authors at the beginning of the study and before submission for publication to ensure transparency and methodological clarity. All participants provided written informed consent before data were collected.

The translation and adaptation process adhered to internationally maintained principles of cross-cultural adaptation of patient-reported outcomes following Beaton et al.’s and COSMIN recommendations [16, 17]. The protocol included five consecutive steps: forward translation, synthesis, back-translation, expert review, and pre-testing.

Forward translation

Forward translations were done by two independent bilingual translators, whose primary language was Urdu. One of the translators was a physiotherapist who knew the terminology of falls, and the other was a professional translator, experienced in medical content writing. The research group then reconciled the two versions by coming up with a single correct version.

Back‑translation

The reconciled Urdu version was later back-translated into English by two other independent translators with postgraduate degrees in English linguistics, who did not know the original instrument. The differences were discussed in consensus meetings to make sure that the conceptual rather than the literal equivalence was achieved.

Expert panel review

The pre-final version was evaluated by a professional panel of eight members (a geriatrician, two physiotherapists, a nurse, a social worker, a bilingual researcher, and two language specialists) on the relevance, clarity, cultural appropriateness, and conceptual equivalence using a four-point Likert scale (1 = not relevant, 4 = highly relevant). All experts rated all items, and recommended wording changes were made, respectively, while S-CVI was calculated by the average method.

Pre-testing

To determine the level of understanding and cultural correctness, the pre-final Urdu FRAQ was administered to 30 older adults. Firstly, the data were collected from participants in face-to-face interaction in Bajwa Hospital OPD, and later on, each member was interviewed through a telephone call after 48 h. The respondents were asked to mention anything unclear or confusing. A few minor refinements in wording were made without changes in the intended meaning of the items.

Content validity

Content validity on the item-level (I -CVI) was determined as the proportion of specialists who gave ratings of 3 or 4 [18]. The average method (S-CVI / Avg.) was used to estimate the scale-level CVI [19], and it entailed the mean of all I-CVI scores. An item with an I-CVI below 0.78 was debated but kept as regarded conceptually important to achieve comparability with the original FRAQ.

Participants and sampling

The participants were recruited using a purposive sampling strategy based on predefined inclusion criteria. The participants were older adults, aged 60 to 85 years, attending regular outpatient care at Bajwa Hospital (February to May 2024), able to read and write in Urdu, and residing in the community independently. After the initial assessment, the researcher collected participants’ telephone numbers and asked them to complete the questionnaire again in 48 h, guided via a scheduled phone call. The questionnaire (FRAQ) was administered by an interviewer-guided protocol using face-to-face sessions and through the use of telephone, following up with the subjects after face-to-face sessions, thus ensuring that all participants understand regardless of their literacy levels. At first, in-person interviews at the outpatient department of Bajwa Hospital were used to obtain data. Then, to test-retest reliability, participants were again contacted after a period of 48 h through pre-arranged telephone interviews to fill in the questionnaire; this way reduced participant burden and withdrawal. The exclusion criteria included the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score less than 23, prior major chronic neurological or cardiovascular diseases, epilepsy, cancer history, or other aspects that could independently affect the level of awareness of falls. The cognitive status was assessed by the medical record, as the participants were already assessed by a physician using MMSE. The target sample was estimated at 220 participants, considering the rule of thumb, a minimum of ten participants per questionnaire item. Sample size calculation based on psychometric recommendations [20, 21]. The target sample size (n = 220) was determined using the rule of 10 participants per item (22 items), as recommended for exploratory factor analysis in psychometric validation studies. Eventually, 200 individuals responded to the instrument and were incorporated in the final analyses. 20 participants were not available for the 48-hour telephone assessment and therefore were not added in study.

Instruments

Fall Risk Awareness Questionnaire (FRAQ)

The FRAQ has 22 items, which are used to measure knowledge of risk factors related to falls. High scores represent increased awareness [12].

Falls Efficacy Scale -International (FES)-I, Urdu version

Construct comparison was performed by the use of the validated Urdu FES-I, which determines concern about falling during daily activities [22]. The Urdu adaptation of FES-I scores a very high internal consistency (Cronbach α > 0.90) and indicates greater fear of falling with a range of 1 to 4, with total score ranging from 16 to 64.

Reliability testing

Test‑retest reliability

In order to determine the reliability, 30 pilot subjects were asked to fill in the FRAQ on two occasions, separated by a 48-hour gap. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) as a two-way mixed-effects model of absolute agreement of a single measurement was used to quantify test-retest reliability. The alpha values were considered to be acceptable (0.70 or above), good (0.80 or above), and excellent (0.90 or above) according to the traditional psychometric rules. Correlation coefficients’ magnitudes have been interpreted based on accepted conventions [23]. The duration of 48 h was chosen to reduce the clinical change and attrition among the participants as close as possible match the recommendations described in the test-retest reliability literature [24, 25].

Internal consistency

The Cronbach alpha was computed at the total scale and at the items to assess inter-item correlation.

Construct validity

Construct validity was first tested using Spearman correlations between FRAQ and FES-I scores (data were not normally distributed). Testing hypotheses assumed a weak positive relationship since FRAQ is a measure of awareness, and FES-I is a measure of fear. The magnitude of the correlations was interpreted as conventional guidelines [26].

Exploratory factor analysis

An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed to explore the underlying structure of the Fall Risk Awareness Questionnaire in the Urdu version. Before extracting the factors, the suitability of the data to undergo factor analytic tests was tested through the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin sampling adequacy test and Bartlett-test of sphericity. Since the observed variables were not normal, principal axis factoring was used. Eigenvalues of more than one identified the factors to retain and were confirmed by looking at the scree plot. In line with the conceptual premise of the FRAQ as the instrument of knowledge-based awareness, a unidimensional solution was first examined. Items with a factor loading of 0.40 or above were accepted to be included in the factor.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed with SPSS version 27 with p < 0.05. Descriptive statistics provided a summary of the demographic/questionnaire data (mean ± SD or frequencies). Normality was checked with the Shapiro-Wilk test. The interpretation of ICC values was based on known thresholds [27]. Indices of content validity and reliability coefficients were presented using 95% confidence intervals. The Urdu adaptation Scale scores a very high internal consistency (Cronbach 0.90), and measures worry about falling in daily life activities with a scale of 16 items with a range of 1 to 4, with higher scores pointing to a higher fear of falling.

Results

Out of the total 220 older adults, 200 of them completed all the study procedures and were incorporated into the final analysis. The mean age of the participants was 72.8 ± 4.8 years, and 53.0% were women. The majority of the respondents were married (59.0%), and others were widowed or divorced. In terms of education, 22.5% were not educated, 39.0% had primary education, 26.5% had secondary education, and 12.0% received higher education. 19% stated that they had fallen on at least one occasion over the last few years, and 10.5% stated that they had fallen within the last year. Internal consistency

The Urdu FRAQ had an acceptable internal consistency. The Cronbach’s alpha of the total scale was 0.75, and that of the standardized item was 0.76. These results indicated acceptable inter-item relatedness to affect various characteristics of fall-risk awareness.

Content validity

After contemplating the expert panel ratings, item-level (I-CVI) and scale-level (S-CVI/Avg.) indices were used to assess the content validity of the Urdu FRAQ, wherein the range of both was measured to be 0.43-1.00. A complete agreement was found among some items (e.g., Q3, Q4, Q7, Q8, Q16, Q17, Q20, Q22) as they exhibited good relevance and clarity with I-CVI = 1.00, while a few items (Q6, Q9, Q12, Q13, Q14, Q19, and Q21) demonstrated lower I-CVI values (0.43–0.57), reflecting lower expert agreement regarding relevance and clarity. These items were retained due to conceptual importance and consistency with the original FRAQ. The total scale-level CVI (S-CVI/Avg.) was found to be 0.82, which represented overall content validity based on expert panel recommendations. Whereas lower I-CVI items were retained after expert panel discussions, as they contained conceptually significant fall risk domains that were in line with the original FRAQ, and also because they preserve cross-cultural comparability with the original version. While having some underlying limitations, these findings indicate an acceptable scale-level content validity, necessitating careful explanation of certain items and prospective betterment in future studies.

Construct validity

Spearman correlations were done since FRAQ and FES-I scores were not normally distributed. FRAQ and FES-I scores showed a statistically significant, weak positive association. This result indicates that the stronger knowledge of fall risk factors was associated with a minimal increase in fear of falling, yet the strength of the correlation implies that the two instruments tend to measure different constructs. Factor analysis

Exploratory factor analysis was conducted to determine the latent structure of Urdu FRAQ. A Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin test of sampling adequacy and a statistically significant Bartlett’s test for sphericity supported the suitability of the data for factor analysis. Principal axis factoring indicated multiple factors with an eigenvalue greater than 1. The first factor accounted for 26.55% of the total variance. Most items exhibited satisfactory factor loadings on the dominant factor, which entails overall fall-risk awareness. Because of the conceptual framing of the FRAQ as a knowledge-based awareness instrument, the scale was kept as a single-factor measure. (Tables 5 and 6).

Discussion

This study entails the foremost systematic psychometric evaluation and cross-cultural adaptation of the Fall Risk Awareness Questionnaire (FRAQ) in Urdu, intending to bridge a significant gap in analyzing fall risk knowledge among older adults in Pakistan, while highlighting that fall-risk awareness is context-oriented and needs to be addressed within specific cultural frameworks.

This research study evolves from existing literature by highlighting the importance of fall risk awareness evaluation, which is based on cultural contexts, particularly in nations where language issues, low literacy rate, and sociocultural elements may hamper the understanding and implementation of existing tools [13, 14]. Notably, the Urdu FRAQ not only replicates existing versions, but it also underpins local culture-specific contexts that critically shape the comprehension of fall risk and preventive approaches, among older adults in Pakistan. Whereas the current research indicates that, with some acceptable psychometric properties, FRAQ can be adapted for use in Urdu-speaking older adults.

Having a significantly high test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.99), the Urdu FRAQ exhibited acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.75), whereas ICC seems to be unusually high, which can be explained by a very short interval (48-hours) and low clinical variability, and is not an indication of a perfect underlying reliability. While comparing it with other adaptations, internal consistency aligns with the Malay version at 0.748 [14], but it is lower than the Brazilian version (α = 0.95) [13], indicating underlying sociocultural impacts on understanding and inter-item correlations.

Cultural variations in comprehending fall risk, literacy levels, and the array of item difficulty in knowledge-oriented instruments may reflect variability across adaptations [28]. For example, the participants included in the Brazilian study have relatively higher levels of education, and perhaps they were more familiar with health-related concepts, which could have boosted inter-item correlations. Contrary to the above, the less literate people or those with variable cultural insights of fall risk may understand items differently, decreasing internal consistency accordingly [29]. Hence, instead of expecting similar performance across various populations, these contradicting findings emphasize the significance of adapting psychometric outcomes in line with indigenous sociocultural contexts [30].

As awareness questionnaires cover multiple areas, moderate inter-item correlations are expected; thus, measured internal consistency aligns with the FRAQ’s conceptual framework. Other than internal consistency, Scale-level content validity S-CVI was measured to be 0.82, which approached recommended thresholds, whereas the values for item-level CVI ranged between 0.43 and 1.00, highlighting the critical variable expert consensus across items. Moreover, cultural orientation and contextual unfamiliarity with some fall risk factors were reflected by lower values of I-CVI at 0.43–0.57.

Although these items were retained due to conceptual significance and cross-cultural comparison, their analysis and explanation may be done quite carefully. In a nutshell, these results indicate that cross-cultural adaptation necessitates keeping a balance between statistical and conceptual relevance, as the same items may command different conceptual or empirical importance across various populations [31]. Items with lower item-content validity index (I-CVI) scores were reflective of cultural unfamiliarity with specific fall-risk concepts and were also due to linguistic peculiarities that arose during the Urdu translation. These inconsistencies underscore the importance of contextual interpretation and indicate that further qualitative refinement may help to increase clarity without loss of conceptual fidelity.

In addition to content validity, construct validity revealed a weak but statistically significant positive correlation. Theoretical differences in the constructs assessed can explain this: FRAQ measures cognitive awareness and knowledge of fall-related risk factors, while FES-I measures emotional concern or fear of falling in daily activities [32]. Fear does not necessarily accompany awareness, and awareness does not inevitably accompany fear.

This weak association is not a limitation; rather, it emphasizes that knowledge and emotion represent complementary dimensions of fall-related cognition and behavior [33]. Contrastingly, variable associations reported in other adaptations suggest that cultural context influences how fall-risk knowledge translates into behavior, indicating that awareness alone may be insufficient to drive change.

In order to further extend the contextualization of construct validity, it is necessary to analyze the dimensionality of the instrument, which has not been analyzed in prior FRAQ adaptations. The primary focus of the previous versions, for instance, the Malay and Brazilian adaptions was on reliability and content validity, wherein the exploratory and confirmatory factors were not addressed properly. This indicates that FRAQ is more of a knowledge-based awareness instrument instead of a multidimensional latent-construct scale [13].

Whereas knowledge-based questionnaires containing several risk areas mostly produce unstable or hard-to-analyze factor structures, while factor analysis can yield an understanding of the fundamentals of dimensionality [34]. In this study, empirical factor analysis affirmed a more unidimensional structure for the Urdu FRAQ [35]. This interpretation was further established by a scree plot, which was used to determine the factor structure of Urdu FRAQ, wherein a clear inflexion after the first component indicated that a single dominant factor accounted for most of the variance, and Urdu FRAQ works as a unidimensional scale for measuring fall risk awareness. This tool is aimed at gaining general fall-risk awareness instead of distinct components, hence making a single dominant factor fit in well both conceptually and methodologically [11].

Therefore, this structure strengthens the construct validity of Urdu FRAQ and emphasizes its utility as a one-score screening instrument in both community and clinical settings. Altogether, these results position the Urdu FRAQ within the wider context of cross-cultural psychometric adaptation, highlighting how social, educational, cultural, and contextual factors influence both conceptual interpretations and performance measurements. These findings carry significant insights into practice and further research.

The Urdu FRAQ offers a concise, culturally oriented tool to evaluate fall-risk awareness among Urdu-speaking older adults, where language barriers keep them from effective risk communication. By encompassing the knowledge gaps, the clinicians can customize patient education and emphasize preventive measures while doing routine checkups, initiating community programs, or providing geriatric consultations.

Moreover, FRAQ can be used as an outcome measure for behavioral and educational modifications, while it can also aid cross-cultural research by enabling systematic study in linguistically least represented populations. Henceforth, further research may analyze known-groups validity, predictive validity, and responsiveness with larger samples to explore dimensionality and measurement properties.

Limitations

There are considerable limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, the very short interval of 48 h kept for test-retest might have exaggerated the reliability measurements at ICC = 0.99 caused by minimum clinical variability and memory effects [29]. Some other items also indicated low item-level content validity, as demonstrated through expert consensus, but these items were retained to achieve conceptual comprehensiveness and the comparison with the original version of FRAQ, although these items require more qualitative assessment and improvement. Some small inconsistencies and replications in the tabular data were rectified to ascertain conformity with the raw data. Conduct validity was assessed only by a single correlational comparison, as no other instrument can exactly measure awareness of fall-risk; similar to the FRAQ, FES-I was considered to be the most appropriate comparator. Whereas the participants’ sample was taken from one clinical setting only, which clearly indicates the limited generalizability, therefore, further studies should incorporate larger representative populations. Advanced psychometric assessments, longitudinal designs, and adding discriminant validity and responsiveness to reinforce the Urdu FRAQ’s validation.

Study strengths

The main strength of the current investigation is that it has a systematic procedure of cross-cultural adaptation, followed by an established methodological technique and using bilingual translators and a panel of multidisciplinary experts to ensure conceptual, linguistic, and cultural synonymity. The research provides a comprehensive psychometric evaluation that also includes evaluations of content validity, internal consistency, test-retest reliability, and structural validity through exploratory factor analysis. Thus, it provides a solid initial evaluation of the Urdu FRAQ. The fact that the sample population was community-dwelling older adults, which has a relatively large size, would contribute to the increased statistical credibility of the findings and support the relevance of the instrument to both the clinical context and the research context. Furthermore, construct validity is interpreted in a transparent manner (based on theoretical frameworks), which enhances the validity of results.

Conclusion

The Urdu version of the FRAQ has an acceptable level of reliability, content validity, and initial construct validity, suggesting that it can be used as a culturally specific instrument to assess the fall-risk awareness of Urdu-speaking older adults, while the results from the exploratory factor analysis present a unidimensional structure which is consistent with the questionnaire’s conceptual design. The current findings provide a solid foundation for clinical practice, as well as the scope of future studies of fall prevention, even though further confirmation is required by future studies in this population.
