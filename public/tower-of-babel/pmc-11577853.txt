Impact of tumor type and size on macroscopic tissue core retrieval in endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine needle biopsy for pancreatic malignancies
Authors: Jian-Han Lai, Ching-Chung Lin, Kung-Chen Ho, Chen-Wang Chang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndoscopic ultrasonography (EUS) is pivotal for diagnosing and sampling pancreatic tumor tissues. This study aimed to assess how the histological type and size of tumors influence the adequacy of macroscopic tissue cores acquired using EUS-guided fine needle biopsy (FNB). We conducted a retrospective study involving 180 patients with pathologically confirmed pancreatic malignancies at our hospital, a medical center, between July 2020 and June 2023. Personal and clinical data, EUS findings, and pathological results were extracted from the patient records. The macroscopic tissue core acquisition rate was 86.1%. Patients with tumors larger than 3 cm had a higher sufficiency rate (93.3%) compared to those with tumors 3 cm or smaller (78.9%, p = 0.005). It was more difficult to obtain sufficient tissue cores from neuroendocrine tumors than from adenocarcinomas (67.7% vs. 89.9%, p = 0.001). Interestingly, obtaining a sufficient tissue core only affected the diagnostic rate of adenocarcinoma (93.3% vs. 60%, p < 0.001) but did not significantly influence the diagnostic rate of neuroendocrine tumors. This study highlights that small tumors (< 3 cm) and neuroendocrine tumors pose a challenge in obtaining sufficient tissue cores. Introduction

Endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS) plays a critical role in diagnosing and obtaining tissue samples from pancreatic tumors. Its ability to provide detailed imaging and aid in tissue sampling has rendered it indispensable for the management of pancreatic malignancies [1]. Pancreatic cancer, particularly pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, remains one of the most lethal malignancies, with a five-year survival rate of less than 10% [2]. Early and accurate diagnosis is crucial for improving patient outcomes, and EUS-guided fine needle biopsy (FNB) has become the standard practice for diagnosing primary and metastatic tumors [3, 4].

Recent advancements have focused on improving the diagnostic yield of EUS-guided tissue sampling, with various strategies being explored to enhance the cytopathological diagnosis rates [5, 6]. One such strategy is the integration of rapid on-site cytological evaluation (ROSE), which enables real-time assessment of tissue adequacy during the procedure, thereby improving the diagnostic yield of EUS tissue sampling [7]. However, collaboration with cytopathologists may not be feasible in some hospitals. Macroscopic on-site evaluation (MOSE) has emerged as a promising alternative to ROSE, demonstrating high accuracy in pathological diagnoses. And the diagnostic accuracy was defined as the percentage of sampled lesions that matched the final cytopathological diagnosis [8]. Integrating MOSE into the EUS-guided biopsy process enables clinicians to optimize tissue sampling and increases the likelihood of obtaining diagnostic specimens. Moreover, the adoption of fine needle biopsy techniques allows for the retrieval of longer tissue cores (> 4 mm), further enhancing diagnostic accuracy [9, 10]. However, obtaining a sufficient tissue core can be difficult in daily practice, resulting in only obtaining short tissue fragments or loose tissue granules. Therefore, we designed this study to determine the factors that might make the tissue core difficult and whether this difficulty influences pathological diagnosis.

Given these advancements, our study aimed to investigate the effect of tumor histological type and size on macroscopic tissue cores obtained during EUS-guided biopsies. By analyzing these factors, we aimed to elucidate potential correlations between tumor characteristics and tissue sampling adequacy. Understanding these relationships is critical for improving diagnostic strategies and patient management of pancreatic malignancies.

Methods

Study design and eligibility

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB number: 24MMHIS176e) of Mackay Memorial Hospital, Taiwan. The Ethics Committee waived the requirement for informed consent. This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a medical center in Taiwan and focused on patients with pancreatic malignancies who underwent EUS-FNB between July 2020 and June 2023. The inclusion criterion was patients with pathologically confirmed pancreatic malignancies. Patients diagnosed with benign tumors or those with uncertain final diagnoses were excluded (supplementary material 1). The final diagnosis was based on both pathological and imaging findings. Patients with benign tumors underwent imaging follow-up for more than 6 months to prevent missed diagnoses. In this study, benign tumors, defined as non-cancerous, were inflammatory masses [11].

Pathological diagnosis

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas were identified based on their characteristic histological features, including dense desmoplastic stroma containing angulated glands, small nests of malignant epithelial cells, and/or individual tumor cells [12]. Neuroendocrine tumor cells are arranged in solid nests or in trabecular and/or gland-like structures. The trabecular structures are irregularly anastomosed ribbon-like structures. The tumor nests are surrounded by thin vascular stroma. These features were confirmed through immunohistochemical analysis, specifically looking for chromogranin A and synaptophysin expression [13].

Defining success

Successful FNB pathological diagnoses were defined as positive findings of malignancy, such as ductal adenocarcinoma or neuroendocrine tumor, whereas unsuccessful diagnoses were defined as false negative results. Patients with unsuccessful FNB diagnosis underwent further surgical or transabdominal echo-guided biopsy for histological confirmation. A sufficient macroscopic tissue core was defined as tissue acquired that was longer than 4 mm, whereas an insufficient tissue core was defined as tissue obtained that was shorter than 4 mm or only loose tissue granules (Fig. 1) [9, 10].

Data collection

Personal and clinical data, including age, sex, tumor location, tumor size, pre-FNB imaging reports, and pathological results, were extracted from the patient records. Patients were initially categorized into two groups based on tumor type: adenocarcinoma and neuroendocrine tumors. This study focused on the rate of obtaining a sufficient tissue core and the factors influencing this rate. Furthermore, we investigated whether this rate influences the pathological diagnosis.

EUS procedure

All EUS-FNB procedures were performed with the patients positioned on their left side and receiving conscious sedation with a combination of midazolam and fentanyl. Two experienced endoscopists performed the procedures using a GF-UCT260 curvilinear echoendoscope (Olympus Optical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). A 22-gauge FNB needle (TopGain®, Medi-Globe, Achenmühle, Germany; Acquire TM, Boston Scientific, Natick, MA, USA) was utilized [14]. Both endoscopists were experienced and had achieved a learning curve. FNB involves a fanning method using either a stylet slow-pull or a low-negative suction technique [15]. The decision, made pass by pass, on which techniques to choose is based on tumor characteristics and the retrieval of tissue at each step. We often perform approximately 15 to-and-fro motions for each pass [16] and aim to perform more than two passes whenever possible [17]. The biopsy specimens were expelled into a smear for macroscopic evaluation by an endoscopist after each pass. The decision regarding the number of FNB passes required for each case was made individually, considering the patient's condition and the volume of tissue obtained. After the macroscopic evaluation, the endoscopists preserved the acquired tissues in formalin for pathological analysis. Rapid on-site cytological evaluations were not available at our hospital.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Student’s t-tests were used to compare continuous variables between the two groups, whereas Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were applied for categorical variables to measure associations. Logistic regression analysis was performed to identify the variables associated with insufficient macroscopic tissue core acquisition. Variables included in the regression model were selected based on their clinical relevance and statistical significance in the univariate analyses. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to quantify the strength of the associations.

All statistical analyses were conducted using the SPSS software (version 26.0; SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA), ensuring rigorous data management and analysis protocols. The significance level was set at a two-sided p-value of 0.05. Results with p-values less than this threshold were considered statistically significant.

Results

A retrospective review of 192 patients with pathologically confirmed pancreatic malignancies was conducted. Among them, 12 were excluded from the analysis because they were diagnosed with either benign (n = 9) or uncertain tumors (n = 3). In total, 180 patients were included in this study. The clinical characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. The pre-FNB imaging diagnosis is mentioned in Table of Supplementary Material 2. A total of 162 patients had a CT study, and 43 patients had an MRI study. Patients with tumors < 3 cm had a lower CT diagnostic sensitivity compared to those with tumors ≥ 3 cm (64.9% vs. 97.7%). Moreover, 21 patients who had an uncertain or false-negative CT diagnosis underwent a subsequent MRI study, and 18 of them had positive findings from the MRI study. Overall, the sensitivity of CT was 82.7%.

Characteristics of all 180 patients

Among them, there were 88 men and 92 women, with a mean age of 65 ± 12.7 years. The tumor size was 3.2 ± 1.4 cm. The histological types were pancreatic adenocarcinoma in 149 cases (82.8%) and neuroendocrine tumors in 31 cases (17.2%). The median number of needle passes was three. Macroscopic tissue cores were obtained from 155 patients (86.1%), and the success rate of the FNB pathological diagnosis was 91.1%. There were no significant differences in tumor characteristics, histological results, number of needle passes, or operators between patients with successful and unsuccessful FNB pathological diagnoses (Supplementary Material 3). One patient experienced a small amount of post-procedure melena without a drop in hemoglobin greater than 2 g/dL, and he improved with conservative treatment. None of the patients experienced procedure-related pancreatitis, perforation, or infection. The two types of FNB needles showed no difference in the macroscopic tissue core obtained or the pathological diagnostic rate (Supplementary Material 4).

The patients were categorized into two groups: those with adenocarcinoma (n = 149) and those with neuroendocrine tumors (n = 31), as detailed in Supplementary Material 5. There were significant differences between the two groups in terms of sex, age, tumor size, and tumor location. The percentage of patients at TNM stage 1 at the time of diagnosis was 19.5% for adenocarcinoma and 61.3% for neuroendocrine tumors (not shown in Table). Interestingly, although the macroscopic tissue core obtaining rate differed significantly between the two malignancies (89.9% vs. 67.7%, p = 0.001), the success rate of FNB pathological diagnosis showed no significant difference.

When classifying based on the sufficiency of macroscopic tissue cores obtained, tumor size appeared to be an influential factor (Table 2). We divided the patients into two groups based on tumor size (> 3 cm) for further analysis based on the findings of a previous study [18]. We found that we were relatively more able to obtain a sufficient macroscopic tissue core (n = 84, 93.3%) in patients with tumors > 3 cm. Conversely, the rate of sufficient tissue cores obtained in patients with tumors ≤ 3 cm was only 78.9% (n = 71). This difference significantly affected the pathological diagnostic rate of FNB across all patients, with rates of 93.5% and 76%, respectively (p = 0.004). There were no significant differences in tumor location or operator between the two groups.

To clarify the influence of tumor size, we performed another subgroup analysis (Table 4). Among patients with tumors > 3 cm, there was no significant difference in the macroscopic tissue core obtaining rate and FNB pathological diagnosis rate between the two types of cancer. Discussion

MOSE is commonly used by endoscopists during EUS-FNB to ensure adequacy of the obtained tissue cores. Acquiring longer tissue cores, rather than just tiny fragments or loose tissue granules, enhances diagnostic accuracy. However, this concept causes endoscopists to be troubled if they cannot obtain tissue cores and instead receive only loose tissue granules. Consequently, they might perform additional puncture passes in an attempt to obtain tissue cores, which could potentially offer no benefit to the diagnostic rate but rather prolong procedure times and increase the risk of bleeding. Therefore, understanding the conditions that lead to difficulties in obtaining tissue cores and their impact on pathological diagnosis can help avoid unnecessary puncture procedures.

In this study, we focused solely on FNB rather than fine needle aspiration (FNA) because previous research has demonstrated that FNB has superior accuracy and fewer passes compared to FNA [19, 20]. Moreover, FNB alone is non-inferior to FNA with rapid on-site evaluation [21]. Consequently, we routinely use FNB for diagnosing pancreatic cancer in our hospital, especially because there is no on-site cytologist available. Regarding the FNB needle type, there was no significant difference in diagnostic yield between different needles [22–25]. Similar results were observed in the present study. Regarding needle gauge choice, 19G needles may present difficulties in deployment, especially with transduodenal routes [26]. The 25G needle was found to be inferior to the 22G needle in terms of specimen quality [14]. Moreover, a meta-analysis praised the 22G needle for its high diagnostic accuracy rate, technical success rate, and low adverse event rate [27]. Hence, we use a 22G needle for pancreatic solid tumors in our clinical practice.

In this study, both the overall rate of obtaining macroscopic tissue cores and the success rate of FNB pathological diagnoses were consistent with the findings of previous studies [22, 28]. No significant factors were found to influence the diagnostic rate, which is consistent with previous studies on the fine needle aspiration learning curve [29]. It enhanced the credibility of the data collected in this study.

The age of the patients varied between those with adenocarcinoma and those with neuroendocrine tumors, reflecting the differing epidemiology of these malignancies. This observation is consistent with a previous study that demonstrated that patients with neuroendocrine tumors had a significantly younger median age at diagnosis (61 vs. 69 years) [30]. Regarding tumor location, the study revealed that the percentages of tumors located in the head and body/tail portions of adenocarcinoma and neuroendocrine tumors were 56.2% and 26% and 33.4% and 43.4%, respectively [30]. Hence, pancreatic adenocarcinoma mostly originates from the head portion, distinguishing it from neuroendocrine tumors, which is consistent with the findings of our study. In our study, the TNM staging distribution showed that stage 1 at the time of diagnosis of adenocarcinoma and neuroendocrine tumors was 19.5% and 61.3%, respectively. This distribution is similar to the results of Yadav’s study, which also found that neuroendocrine tumors were diagnosed earlier than adenocarcinomas [30]. In our patient cohort, most adenocarcinomas were diagnosed when patients experienced discomfort, whereas many neuroendocrine tumors were found incidentally during health examinations. This difference may explain the variation in tumor size and stage at diagnosis between the two types of tumors.

The overall rate of obtaining macroscopic tissue cores in this study was unrelated to different operators or tumor locations, suggesting these factors did not significantly affect tissue core sufficiency. However, tumors less than 3 cm had a notably lower acquisition rate, decreasing from 93.3% to 78.9% (p = 0.005). Thus, small tumor size has emerged as a significant factor affecting the sufficiency of tissue core acquisition. Takahashi et al. found that the pathological diagnostic rate decreased from 97.2% to 64.3% for small lesions [18]. Bellocchi also suggested that FNB, not FNA, should be used for sampling small lesions to increase the diagnostic accuracy from 79.1% to 89.8% [31]. Sampling small lesions is challenging for endoscopists. We hypothesized that the difficulty in obtaining sufficient tissue cores from smaller tumors may be related to the extent of fibrosis within the tumor. The presence of fibrosis makes the tissue more compact and easier to retrieve as a core. Conversely, in the absence of fibrosis and loose cell junctions, only short tissue fragments or loose granules were observed. This suggests that tumor-related fibrosis plays a crucial role in tissue core sufficiency. In pancreatic cancer, particularly pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, a critical relationship exists between tumor size and fibrosis. Larger tumors often exhibit more severe fibrosis driven by interactions within the tumor microenvironment [32]. As tumors increase in size, they produce higher levels of cytokines and growth factors that activate cancer-associated fibroblasts, leading to a desmoplastic reaction characterized by increased tissue stiffness and fibrosis [33, 34]. The extent of fibrosis tends to increase with tumor size due to the elevated production of signaling molecules. Additionally, as tumors grow, internal hypoxia becomes more prevalent due to inadequate blood supply. Hypoxia-inducible factors promote the expression of fibrotic factors and excessive extracellular matrix deposition, enhancing the fibrotic response [33]. In summary, there appears to be a positive relationship between tumor size and degree of fibrosis. Further investigation is warranted to validate this relationship, perhaps through methods such as Masson's Trichrome staining [35].

Macroscopic on-site evaluation is a valuable method for ensuring favorable FNB pathological results. In our study, obtaining a sufficient macroscopic tissue core was associated with a higher diagnostic rate (93.5%) compared to patients with insufficient tissue cores (76%), particularly in those with pancreatic adenocarcinoma. This finding is consistent with previous studies [36]. Therefore, macroscopic on-site evaluation should be routinely utilized in FNB practice. However, we observed that small neuroendocrine tumors had the lowest rate of obtaining sufficient macroscopic tissue cores at 64%, suggesting that acquiring macroscopic tissue cores from small neuroendocrine tumors is more challenging than from adenocarcinomas. In a large study with 996 patients, neuroendocrine tumors and small tumors were also factors affecting the accuracy of FNA. Crino suggested that FNB should be used instead of FNA for evaluating small neuroendocrine tumors [37]. These findings indicate that small neuroendocrine tumors are a significant problem in endoscopic sampling. We propose that the differing histologies of these two types of cancers may be responsible. Pancreatic adenocarcinoma originates from ductal epithelial cells and often presents as a firm mass with fibrosis, characterized by desmoplastic stroma with abundant fibrous tissue [12]. Conversely, neuroendocrine tumors arise from neuroendocrine cells and have a wide range of features. Cytology showed loosely cohesive tumor cell clusters with numerous isolated cells and a more vascular appearance with delicate fibrovascular stroma [13]. Histological variances, particularly in extracellular matrix fibrosis, between pancreatic adenocarcinoma and neuroendocrine tumors might contribute to differences in tissue core-obtaining rates. Despite these challenges, the diagnostic rate was not significantly affected. Thus, the criteria for sufficient macroscopic tissue cores, typically defined as > 4 mm, may not be suitable for small neuroendocrine tumors due to their distinct histological characteristics. But this hypothesis needs further study to be proven, such as through case-matching studies.

Regarding adverse events, post-FNB bleeding is defined by the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) criteria as a hemoglobin drop greater than 2 g/dL and/or evidence of hematemesis, melena, or hematochezia [38]. Our patient with bleeding did not meet these criteria due to the absence of a significant drop in hemoglobin levels. A meta-analysis indicated that fewer needle passes (< 3) could reduce the risk of post-procedural bleeding [39]. In our study, the median number of needle passes was 3, and there were no severe adverse events noted, indicating a safe approach for pancreatic tissue sampling, consistent with findings from previous studies. Moreover, non-beneficial additional puncture passes to obtain "sufficient" tissue cores should be avoided if the endoscopist knows that certain types of tumors are difficult to obtain a long core from or if additional passes are unnecessary for diagnosis.

Additionally, in our study, pre-FNB imaging examination showed that CT had an acceptable sensitivity for diagnosing pancreatic tumors [40]. However, if the CT results were inconsistent with clinical suspicion, MRI played an important role in further evaluation, especially in patients with tumors smaller than 3 cm.

One limitation of this study is its retrospective design and the relatively small number of patients, particularly those with neuroendocrine tumors. The underlying selection bias also needs to be carefully considered, especially in patients with adenocarcinoma and neuroendocrine tumors, as these patients in our study present with varying tumor sizes. Therefore, accounting for selection bias in these subgroups is essential to ensure accurate data interpretation and to draw reliable conclusions about the impact of FNB sampling across these two types of tumors. We did not measure the length of each tissue core individually [41]; instead, we ensured that the tissue length exceeded 4 mm. Additionally, contrast-enhanced imaging and elastography techniques were not incorporated into the analysis because of limitations in the equipment available at our hospital, and there was no evidence suggesting that these techniques would improve diagnostic accuracy [42, 43].

Conclusions

This study underscores the importance of tumor size in obtaining sufficient macroscopic tissue cores during EUS-guided fine needle biopsy. Smaller tumor size, particularly in neuroendocrine tumors, presents a significant challenge for tissue core acquisition. However, this does not decrease the pathological diagnostic rate in neuroendocrine tumors. Hence, obtaining a sufficient tissue core is important for adenocarcinoma, but not as critical for neuroendocrine tumors.
