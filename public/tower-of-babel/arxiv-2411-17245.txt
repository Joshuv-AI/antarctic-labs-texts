Smoothed Analysis of the k k -Swap Neighborhood for Makespan Scheduling
Lars Rohwedder, Ashkan Safari, Tjark Vredeveld

Abstract
Local search is a widely used technique for tackling challenging optimization problems, offering simplicity and strong empirical performance across various problem domains. In this paper, we address the problem of scheduling a set of jobs on identical parallel machines with the objective of makespan minimization , by considering a local search neighborhood, called k-swap . A k k -swap neighbor is obtained by interchanging the machine allocations of at most k k jobs scheduled on two machines. While local search algorithms often perform well in practice, they can exhibit poor worst-case performance. In our previous study, we showed that for k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 , there exists an instance where the number of iterations required to converge to a local optimum is exponential in the number of jobs. Motivated by this discrepancy between theoretical worst-case bound and practical performance, we apply smoothed analysis to the k k -swap local search. Smoothed analysis has emerged as a powerful framework for analyzing the behavior of algorithms, aiming to bridge the gap between poor worst-case and good empirical performance. In this paper, we show that the smoothed number of iterations required to find a local optimum with respect to the k k -swap neighborhood is bounded by O ⁡ ( m 2 ⋅ n 2 ​ k + 2 ⋅ log ⁡ m ⋅ ϕ ) O(m^{2}\cdot n^{2k+2}\cdot\log m\cdot\phi) , where n n and m m are the numbers of jobs and machines, respectively, and ϕ ≥ 1 \phi\geq 1 is the perturbation parameter. The bound on the smoothed number of iterations demonstrates that the proposed lower bound reflects a pessimistic scenario which is rare in practice.

1
Introduction

Local search algorithms are among the most widely used heuristics to tackle computationally difficult optimization problems
[
1
,
30
]
, due to their simplicity and strong empirical performance.
A local search procedure begins with an initial solution and iteratively moves from a feasible solution to a neighboring one until predefined stopping criteria are met.
The selection of an appropriate neighborhood function significantly impacts the performance of the local search. These functions determine the range of solutions that the local search procedure can explore in a single iteration.
A classic example of local search is the
2-opt
neighborhood for the traveling salesman problem
[
1
,
13
,
17
]
, which often yields good solutions in practice despite the weak worst-case performance.
In some forms of local search, it is possible to move to a worse feasible solution within the neighborhood, which can help explore a broader range of solutions. However, in this paper, we focus on a basic form of local search, known as
iterative improvement
, which always searches for a better solution within the neighborhood of the current one.
The iterative improvement stops when no further improvement can be achieved in the neighborhood, indicating that the current solution has reached a
local optimum
.

In this paper, we consider one of the most fundamental scheduling problems, in which we are given a set of
n
jobs with positive processing times
p_{j}(j=1,2,...,n)
.
Each of these jobs needs to be processed on one of
m
identical machines without preemption, so as to minimize the
makespan
: we want the last job to be completed as early as possible.
This problem is denoted by
P{\parallel}C_{max}

[
22
]
, and is strongly NP-hard
[
25
]
.
In consequence, many studies in the literature have suggested approximation algorithms to address this problem.
Among them, some of the first approximation algorithms
[
20
,
21
]
and polynomial time approximation schemes (PTAS)
[
23
]
have been developed in the context of this problem.
As PTAS’s are often considered impractical due to their tendency to yield excessively high running time bounds (albeit polynomial) even for moderate values of
\varepsilon
(see
[
29
]
), some studies have approached this problem by local search
[
10
,
16
,
31
,
34
]
.
One of the simplest local search neighborhoods is the so-called
jump
or
move
neighborhood.
To obtain a jump neighbor, the machine allocation of one of the jobs changes. Finn and Horowitz
[
16
]
proposed a simple improvement heuristic, called
0/1-Interchange
which is able to find a local optimum with respect to the jump neighborhood.
Brucker et al.
[
10
]
have provided an upper bound of
O(n^{2})
for this heuristic, and the tightness of this bound was shown by Hurkens and Vredeveld
[
24
]
.
Another well-known folklore neighborhood is the
swap
neighborhood, which is defined as the one consisting of all possible jumps and swaps
[
31
,
34
]
, where in a swap operator the machine allocations of two jobs, scheduled on two different machines, interchange (see Fig.
1
).
In our previous work
[
31
]
, we provided an upper bound of
O(n^{4})
for the number of swaps when we have only two machines in our schedule.
A natural generalization of the jump and swap neighborhoods is known as the
k
-swap
, which has been studied in this work
[
31
]
. A
k
-swap neighbor is obtained by selecting at most
k
jobs from two machines and interchanging the machine allocations of the selected jobs (see Fig.
).
Numerous alternative neighborhoods such as
push

[
34
]
,
multi-exchange

[
2
,
19
]
and
split

[
11
]
have been suggested in the literature to approach this problem.

Figure 1:
Jump (left diagram) and swap (right diagram) operators.

Furthermore, we provided a running time analysis of the
k
-swap neighborhood for the problem
P||C_{max}
in our previous work
[
31
]
. In this analysis, we showed that there exists an initial schedule as well as a sequence of 3-swaps such that the local optimum with respect to the
k
-swap neighborhood, for
k\geq 3
, is reached after
2^{\Omega(n)}
iterations.
Moreover, our computational results on different classes of instances showed that, on average, the number of iterations required to converge to a local optimum is not excessively large.
This raises the question of whether the provided lower bound reflects a pessimistic scenario which might be rare in practice.

To address such discrepancies between worst-case and practical performance, the notion of
smoothed analysis
was introduced by Spielman and Teng
[
36
]
.
Smoothed analysis is a hybrid of worst-case and average-case analysis, designed to provide a more realistic measure of an algorithm’s performance.
In the
classical
model of the smoothed analysis (also known as the
two-step
model), an adversary selects an arbitrary instance and then, the numbers or coefficients of this instance are (slightly) randomly perturbed by adding Gaussian-distributed random variables of mean 0 and standard deviation
\sigma>0
to them.
The smoothed performance is then defined as the worst expected performance among all adversarial choices; the expected value is taken over the random perturbation of the adversarial instance.
In this setting, the smaller
\sigma
, the more powerful the adversary, i.e., the analysis is closer to the worst-case analysis.
In a more general model of the smoothed analysis, known as the
one-step
model, introduced by Beier and Vöcking
[
6
]
, an adversary specifies probability density functions upper-bounded by
\phi\geq 1
for all the numbers that need to be perturbed.
Then, all the numbers are drawn independently according to their respective density function.
In this setting, the smaller
\phi
, the less powerful the adversary, i.e., the analysis is closer to the average-case analysis.
The one-step model of the smoothed analysis contains the two-step model as a special case.
Smoothed analysis was first applied to the simplex method
[
36
]
, and was successfully able to explain why this method is performing well in practice. Subsequently, it was applied to different problems and algorithms, including linear programming
[
8
,
32
,
35
]
, searching and sorting
[
4
,
14
,
18
,
27
]
, online and approximation algorithms
[
5
,
7
,
33
]
, game theory
[
9
,
12
]
, and local search
[
3
,
15
,
26
,
28
]
.

Motivated by the exponential lower bound established in our previous work
[
31
]
, we perform smoothed analysis on the number of
k
-swaps for the problem
P||C_{max}
, and show that it is upper bounded by
O(m^{2}\cdot n^{2k+2}\cdot\log m\cdot\phi)
, where
\phi\geq 1
is the perturbation parameter.
While the lower bound provided in our previous study
[
31
]
is exponential in the number of jobs, our result in this paper demonstrates a polynomial upper bound for the smoothed number of
k
-swaps in obtaining a local optimum when
k
is constant.

2
Preliminaries and Notation

Recall that in the problem under consideration, there are
n
jobs that need to be processed without interruption, on
m
identical machines.
A job can only be processed by one machine and each machine can process at most one job at a time. The time it takes to process job
j
is denoted by
p_{j}
.
The goal is to schedule the jobs such that the makespan,
C_{\max}=\max_{j}C_{j}
, is minimized, where
C_{j}
denotes the time at which job
j
is completed.
As the order in which the jobs, assigned to the same machine, are processed, does not influence the makespan, we represent a schedule by
(M_{1},M_{2},...,M_{m})
, where
M_{i}
denotes the set of jobs scheduled on machine
i
, for
i\in\{1,2,...,m\}
.
Let
L_{i}=\sum_{j\in M_{i}}p_{j}
denote the load of machine
i
, and
p(S)=\sum_{j\in S}p_{j}
denote the summation of the processing times of a set of jobs
S
.
It is easy to see as there is no advantage in keeping a machine idle while it still needs to process at least a job, the makespan of a schedule is equal to the maximum load
L_{\max}
, i.e.,
C_{\max}=L_{\max}
; we will use
C_{\max}
and
L_{\max}
interchangeably.
A
critical machine

i
is a machine with
L_{i}=L_{\max}
, and a
non-critical machine

i^{\prime}
is a machine with
L_{i^{\prime}}<L_{\max}
.
Additionally, we define an
\ell
min-load machine
as the machine with the
\ell
th minimum load among all the machines. We use the notation
L_{\ell\min}
to represent the load of this machine.
Moreover, we denote by
L_{\max}(t),L_{i}(t)
,
L_{\ell\min}(t)
and
M_{i}(t)
the values of the corresponding variables at the start of iteration
t
.

The
k
-swap neighborhood is defined as the one consisting of swapping all combinations of at most
k
jobs between any pair of machines. To be more precise, to obtain a
k
-swap neighbor, a set of jobs
A
and a set of jobs
B
are selected from machines
i
and
i^{\prime}
, respectively, such that
|A|\geq 1
,
|B|\geq 0
and
|A|+|B|\leq k
, and then the machine allocations of these jobs are exchanged (see Fig.
).
In order to improve the schedule,
i
needs to be a critical machine, and
0<p(A)-p(B)<L_{i}-L_{i^{\prime}}
.
We say we are in a
k-swap optimal solution
if, by applying the
k
-swap operator, we cannot decrease the makespan or the number of critical machines without increasing the makespan.

3
Smoothed Analysis

In this section, we provide a smoothed analysis of the number of iterations required to converge to a
k
-swap optimal solution.
To this end, we employ the one-step smoothing model
[
6
]
.
In this model, an adversary specifies probability density functions upper-bounded by
\phi\geq 1
for all the numbers that need to be perturbed. Then, all the numbers are drawn independently according to their respective density function.
Note that the perturbation parameter
\phi
controls the adversary’s power; the larger the value of
\phi
, the more powerful the adversary is.
Finally, we measure the maximum expected performance, where the maximum is taken over all the choices of the adversary, and the expectation is taken over drawing the numbers according to their density functions.
In our analysis, we perturb the processing times of the jobs. An adversary specifies density functions
f_{1},\dotsc,f_{n}
with
f_{i}:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,\phi]
. An instance of the problem is obtained by drawing
p_{1},\dotsc,p_{n}
independently according to the densities
f_{1},\dotsc,f_{n}
, respectively.

Figure 2:
(a) Type-1 of improving
k
-swap. After swapping
A
with
B
,
i^{\prime}
moves to
\gamma_{l}
. (b) Type-2 of improving
k
-swap. After swapping
A
with
B
,
i^{\prime}
stays in
\gamma_{s}
. Note that in both of the cases, machine
i
does not necessarily move to
\gamma_{s}
after the swap.

Let
\delta_{min}=\min|p(A)-p(B)|
, where the minimum is taken over all the sets of jobs
A
and
B
with
A\cap B=\varnothing
and
1\leq|A|+|B|\leq k
.
To perform the analysis, we split the set of machines in our schedule at the start of iteration
t
into two sets
\gamma_{l}(t)=\{i:L_{i}(t)>L_{max}(t)-\delta_{min}\}
and
\gamma_{s}(t)=\{i:L_{i}(t)\leq L_{max}(t)-\delta_{min}\}
.
We refer to these two sets as
\gamma_{l}
and
\gamma_{s}
if
t
is clear from the context.
In order to improve the schedule in iteration
t
, we need to swap a set of jobs
A
from a critical machine
i
with a set of jobs
B
from a non-critical machine
i^{\prime}
such that
|A|+|B|\leq k
, and
0<p(A)-p(B)<L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t)
.
Since all the critical machines are in the set
\gamma_{l}(t)
and loads of all the machines in
\gamma_{l}(t)
are greater than
L_{max}(t)-\delta_{min}
, we always have
i\in\gamma_{l}(t)
and
i^{\prime}\in\gamma_{s}(t)
in an
improving
iteration
t
(an iteration in which we find an improving
k
-swap neighbor).

In our analysis, we consider two types of improving iterations.
In an improving
k
-swap of
type-1
, machine
i^{\prime}
moves to
\gamma_{l}
after swapping
A
with
B
in iteration
t
(see Figure
2
(a)); formally, we get
i^{\prime}\in\gamma_{l}(t+1)
.
In an improving
k
-swap of
type-2
, machine
i^{\prime}
stays in
\gamma_{s}
after swapping
A
with
B
in iteration
t
(see Figure
2
(b)); formally, we get
i^{\prime}\in\gamma_{s}(t+1)
.
We analyze this type based on a fixed value of
\delta_{min}
, by employing a potential function and counting the number of times that this function decreases.
We will provide an upper bound of
O(m\cdot n^{k})
for the number of consecutive iterations of type-1, and an upper bound of
O(\dfrac{m\cdot n}{\delta_{\min}})
for the total number of iterations of type-2.
Therefore, the total number of iterations in obtaining a
k
-swap optimal solution is upper-bounded by
O(\dfrac{m^{2}\cdot n^{k+1}}{\delta_{min}})
.
In the worst case,
\delta_{min}
can be (close to) 0, resulting in a very large bound; however, in the smoothed setting, the probability that this happens is very small, and we can show a bound of
O(m^{2}\cdot n^{2k+2}\cdot\log m\cdot\phi)
.

First, we show that in a sequence of consecutive
k
-swaps of type-1, the sorted vector of machine loads in the set
\gamma_{s}
is pointwise non-decreasing.

Lemma 3.1
.

For all the
\ell
min-load machines in the set
\gamma_{s}
, we have
L_{\ell\min}(t+1)\geq L_{\ell\min}(t)
when a
k
-swap operation of type-1 occurs in iteration
t
.

Proof.

Let
i\in\gamma_{l}(t)
be a critical machine and
i^{\prime}\in\gamma_{s}(t)
, and let
A\subseteq M_{i}(t)
and
B\subseteq M_{i^{\prime}}(t)
with
0<p(A)-p(B)<L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t)
be two sets of jobs such that swapping them constitute an improving
k
-swap of type-1; i.e., we get
i^{\prime}\in\gamma_{l}(t+1)
.
After doing the swap, machine
i
either stays in
\gamma_{l}
or moves to
\gamma_{s}
. As we have
i^{\prime}\in\gamma_{l}(t+1)
, and the values of
L_{\max}-\delta_{\min}
are non-increasing,
|\gamma_{s}|
does not increase.
If after the swap, we have
i\in\gamma_{l}(t+1)
, the load of none of the machines in
\gamma_{s}
decreases.
If after the swap, we have
i\in\gamma_{s}(t+1)
, since
p(A)-p(B)<L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t)
, we get
L_{i}(t+1)>L_{i^{\prime}}(t)
, and therefore, the lemma holds.
∎

Next, we show that in a consecutive sequence of
k
-swaps of type-1 when a set
A
of jobs on a critical machine is swapped with a set
B
on an
\ell
min-load machine, then these two sets cannot be swapped again on a critical and an
\ell
min-load machine in the same sequence of consecutive
k
-swaps of type-1.

Lemma 3.2
.

Consider an improving
k
-swap of type-1 by swapping a set
A
on a critical machine
i
with a set
B
on an
\ell
min-load machine
i^{\prime}
in iteration
t
. Then, we are not able to swap
A
with
B
on a critical machine and an
\ell
min-load machine as long as we have not performed the
k
-swap operation of type-2.

Proof.

By swapping the jobs in
A
with the jobs in
B
, and according to Lemma
3.1
, we get:

\displaystyle L_{i^{\prime}}(t+1)-L_{\ell\min}(t+1)

\displaystyle\phantom{}=L_{i^{\prime}}(t)+p(A)-p(B)-L_{\ell\min}(t+1)

\displaystyle\leq L_{i^{\prime}}(t)+p(A)-p(B)-L_{\ell\min}(t)

\displaystyle=L_{i^{\prime}}(t)+p(A)-p(B)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t)=p(A)-p(B).

In iteration
t+1
, the jobs of set
A
are in machine
i^{\prime}
and machine
i^{\prime}
is in
\gamma_{l}(t+1)
.
The jobs of set
A
can be swapped with other jobs when machine
i^{\prime}
becomes a critical machine. If
i^{\prime}
becomes a critical machine in iteration
t^{\prime}
(
t^{\prime}\geq t+1
), we get
L_{\max}(t^{\prime})=L_{i^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})=L_{i^{\prime}}(t+1)
, because
i^{\prime}\in\gamma_{\ell}(t+1)
, and therefore there cannot be an improving
k
-swap with jobs on machine
i^{\prime}
, as long as
i^{\prime}
is not a critical machine.
As we have,
L_{\ell\min}(t^{\prime})\geq L_{\ell\min}(t+1)
, we get

\displaystyle L_{\max}(t^{\prime})-L_{\ell\min}(t^{\prime})\leq L_{i^{\prime}}(t+1)-L_{\ell\min}(t+1)\leq p(A)-p(B).

According to Lemma
3.1
, in a consecutive sequence of improving
k
-swaps of type-1, the values of
L_{\max}-L_{\ell\min}
are non-increasing, and we cannot swap
A
with
B
on a critical machine and an
\ell
min-load machine in the remainder of the sequence of consecutive
k
-swaps of type-1.
∎

According to Lemma
3.2
, by performing a
k
-swap operation of type-1 over the sets
A\subseteq M_{i}(t)
and
B\subseteq M_{i^{\prime}}(t)
, such that
i^{\prime}
is an
\ell
min-load machine at the start of iteration
t
, we are not able to swap the jobs in
A
with the jobs in
B
on a critical machine and an
\ell
min-load machine as long as we have not performed the
k
-swap operation of the type-2.
As we have at most
O(m\cdot n^{k})
different combinations of
A
,
B
, and
\ell
(we have
O(n^{k}\cdot 2^{k})
different combinations of the sets
A
and
B
), the number of consecutive
k
-swaps of type-1 is upper-bounded by
O(m\cdot n^{k})
.

Corollary 3.3
.

In at most
O(m\cdot n^{k})
consecutive iterations, the
k
-swaps of the type-1 occur.

To analyze the number of
k
-swaps of type-2, we make use of the potential function defined by
\Phi=\sum\limits_{i,i^{\prime}}|L_{i}-L_{i^{\prime}}|
.
Assume w.l.o.g. that
L_{1}\geq L_{2}\geq\dotsc\geq L_{m}
. Then,
\Phi=2\sum_{1\leq i<i^{\prime}\leq m}(L_{i}-L_{i^{\prime}})=2\sum_{1\leq i<i^{\prime}\leq m}(m-2i+1)L_{i}
.
As we know that the total sum of machine loads is upper-bounded by
n
, we get
\Phi\leq 2m\cdot n
.

Observation 3.4
.

We have
\Phi(1)\leq 2m\cdot n
.

Next, we provide a lower bound for the amount that
\Phi
decreases in each iteration of this case. To do so, first, we provide the following lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
0<c<a-b
. We have
|a-c|+|b+c|\leq|a|+|b|
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda=\frac{c}{a-b}
. Since
0<c<a-b
, we have
0<\lambda<1
. As the absolute value is a convex function, we have

|a-c|+|b+c|=|(1-\lambda)a+\lambda b|+|(1-\lambda)b+\lambda a|\leq(1-\lambda)|a|+\lambda|b|+(1-\lambda)|b|+\lambda|a|=|a|+|b|.\qed

Furthermore, since a
k
-swap of type-2 between a critical machine
i
and a machine
i^{\prime}
in iteration
t
results in a load for
i^{\prime}
that is at least
\delta_{min}
less than the makespan, i.e.,
L_{i^{\prime}}(t+1)\leq L_{\max}(t+1)-\delta_{\min}
, we have the following observation.

Observation 3.6
.

Consider an improving
k
-swap of type-2 by swapping a set
A
on a critical machine
i
with a set
B
on a machine
i^{\prime}
. Then, we have
(L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t))-(p(A)-p(B))\geq\delta_{min}
.

Subsequently, we show that by performing a
k
-swap operation of type-2, the value of
\Phi
decreases by at least
4\delta_{\min}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Consider an improving
k
-swap of type-2 in iteration
t
.
We have
\Phi(t)-\Phi(t+1)\geq 4\delta_{\min}
.

Proof.

Assume the
k
-swap of type-2 is obtained by swapping a set
A
on a critical machine
i
with a set
B
on a machine
i^{\prime}
in iteration
t
.
Let
\Delta_{\Phi}=\Phi(t)-\Phi(t+1)
. We have

\displaystyle\Delta_{\Phi}

\displaystyle=2|L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t)|-2|L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t)-2(p(A)-p(B))|

\displaystyle\quad+2\sum_{i^{\prime\prime}\neq i,i^{\prime}}|L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime\prime}}(t)|-2\sum_{i^{\prime\prime}\neq i,i^{\prime}}|L_{i}(t)-p(A)+p(B)-L_{i^{\prime\prime}}(t)|

\displaystyle\quad+2\sum_{i^{\prime\prime}\neq i,i^{\prime}}|L_{i^{\prime}}(t)-L_{i^{\prime\prime}}(t)|-2\sum_{i^{\prime\prime}\neq i,i^{\prime}}|L_{i^{\prime}}(t)+p(A)-p(B)-L_{i^{\prime\prime}}(t)|.

As swapping
A
with
B
in iteration
t
yields an improving
k
-swap, we know that
0<p(A)-p(B)<L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t)
.
Hence, by Lemma
3.5
, we have
|L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime\prime}}(t)|+|L_{i^{\prime}}(t)-L_{i^{\prime\prime}}(t)|\geq|L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime\prime}}(t)-(p(A)-p(B))|+|L_{i^{\prime}}(t)-L_{i^{\prime\prime}}(t)+p(A)-p(B)|
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\Delta_{\Phi}\geq 2|L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t)|-2|L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t)-2(p(A)-p(B))|.

If
p(A)-p(B)\leq\frac{L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t)}{2}
, then
|L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t)-2(p(A)-p(B))|=L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t)-2(p(A)-p(B))
and we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{\Phi}\geq 2(L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t))-2(L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t)-2(p(A)-p(B)))=4(p(A)-p(B))\geq 4\delta_{\min}.

On the other hand, if
p(A)-p(B)>\frac{L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t)}{2}
, then
|L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t)-2(p(A)-p(B))|=2(p(A)-p(B))-(L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t))
. By Observation
3.6
, we have

\Delta_{\Phi}\geq 2(L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t))+2(L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t)-2(p(A)-p(B)))=4(L_{i}(t)-L_{i^{\prime}}(t))-4(p(A)-p(B))\geq 4\delta_{\min}.\qed

Using Observation
3.4
and Lemma
3.7
, we can bound the number of improving
k
-swaps of type-2 as in Corollary
3.8
.

Corollary 3.8
.

In at most
O(\dfrac{m\cdot n}{\delta_{\min}})
iterations, the
k
-swaps of type-2 occur.

As the consecutive number of improving
k
-swaps of type-1 is upper-bounded by
O(m\cdot n^{k})
(Corollary
3.3
), we can bound the total number of iterations as in Corollary
3.9
.

Corollary 3.9
.

The total number of iterations in obtaining a
k
-swap optimal solution is upper bounded by
O(\dfrac{m^{2}\cdot n^{k+1}}{\delta_{\min}})
.

To bound the expected number of iterations in the smoothed analysis, we bound the probability that
\delta_{\min}
is small.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,k]
. Then,
\mathbb{P}(\delta_{min}\leq\alpha)\leq 2^{k+1}\cdot n^{k}\cdot\alpha\cdot\phi
.

Proof.

Let
A
and
B
be two disjoint subsets of jobs such that
1\leq|A|+|B|\leq k
. By the principle of deferred decisions, we have that
\mathbb{P}(|p(A)-p(B)|\leq\alpha)=2\cdot\alpha\cdot\phi
.
By taking a union bound over the
(2n)^{k}
possibilities for the sets
A
and
B
, we get the desired result.
∎

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
f_{1},\dotsc,f_{n}:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,\phi]
be arbitrary density functions bounded by
\phi\geq 1
, and let
p_{1},\dotsc,p_{n}
be the processing times of the jobs drawn independently according to
f_{1},\dotsc,f_{n}
, respectively. Let
T
denote the random variable of the number of iterations to find a
k
-swap optimal solution. Then

\mathbb{E}(T)=O(m^{2}\cdot n^{2k+2}\cdot\log m\cdot\phi).

Proof.

Observe that, by Corollary
3.9
, when
T\geq t
, for a given
t
, we need to have that
\delta_{min}\leq\dfrac{m^{2}\cdot n^{k+1}}{t}
. Hence, we can bound the expected value of
T
by the number of different schedules. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(T)=\sum_{t=1}^{m^{n}}\mathbb{P}(T\geq t)=\sum_{t=1}^{m^{n}}\mathbb{P}(\delta_{min}\leq\dfrac{m^{2}\cdot n^{k+1}}{t})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{m^{n}}\dfrac{2^{k+1}\cdot m^{2}\cdot n^{2k+1}\cdot\phi}{t}

\displaystyle=2^{k+1}\cdot\phi\cdot m^{2}\cdot n^{2k+1}\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{m^{n}}\dfrac{1}{t}

\displaystyle=O(m^{2}\cdot n^{2k+2}\cdot\log m\cdot\phi),

Where the inequality is according to Lemma
3.10
.
∎
