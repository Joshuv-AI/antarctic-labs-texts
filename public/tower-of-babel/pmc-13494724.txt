Public Comments and Federal Rulemaking for Menthol Cigarettes and Flavored Cigars
Authors: Andrea C. Villanti, Marisa Tomaino, Amanda Johnson, Jeannie Garmon, Aila Altunbilek, Olivia A. Wackowski, Ollie Ganz, Charity A. Ntansah, Jon-Patrick Allem, Kevin R. J. Schroth, Craig S. Fryer, Kymberle Landrum Sterling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat are the sources and content of public comments related to the US Food and Drug Administration’s proposed rulemaking on menthol cigarettes (MCs) and flavored cigars (FCs)?FindingsIn this qualitative study of 246 808 public comments, form letters dominated comments (97% for MCs; 93% for FCs), and content tied to tobacco companies was identified in 44% of MC comments and 12% of FC comments. Of the 2% of comments that were not form letters, most opposed these bans (96% for MCs; 99% for FCs).MeaningThese findings suggest that US government officials used public comment volume to justify delaying public health policymaking without addressing tobacco company influence over the number and content of the comments.

Introduction

Menthol cigarettes (MCs) and flavored cigars (FCs) are associated with increased smoking initiation and progression to regular use.1,2 MCs are also associated with higher nicotine dependence and decreased adult cessation.3,4,5 In May 2022, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) proposed long-awaited rules for product standards banning menthol in cigarettes1 and characterizing flavors in cigars,6,7 following evidence from systematic reviews,3,5,8,9 evaluations of Ontario’s MC ban,10,11,12 and simulation studies,13,14,15,16,17 supporting the likely positive public health impact of these policies, particularly for health equity.18 Subsequent empirical studies,19,20,21,22 evidence reviews,23,24 and government reports25 further strengthened support for FDA regulation of MCs and FCs for the protection of public health.

Consistent with the federal rulemaking process,26 members of the public were given the opportunity to provide comments on proposed rules prior to a final rule being issued, with the goals of providing the public with notice of the proposed rule and an opportunity to comment. This procedure promotes transparency and allows the public to participate in the rulemaking process by alerting the government of any concerns before a rule is finalized.26,27 For the FDA’s MC and FC proposed rules, public comment periods were open from May 4, 2022, through July 5, 2022 (62 days), and then extended to August 2, 2022 (90 days), for both dockets; this followed an earlier public comment period on regulation of flavors in tobacco products that had occurred in 2018. During the 2022 comment period, tobacco companies launched advocacy efforts, including direct emails to consumers in their databases, encouraging them to submit comments opposing the proposed rules and often providing suggested language to use.28 During this time, there were also numerous news articles, press releases, and opinion editorials about the proposed MC and FC rules.29 In 2022, the FDA received over 175 000 comments on the proposed MC rule and over 71 000 comments on the FC rule. In October 2023, the FDA sent final rules to the White House Office of Management and Budget for review and in April 2024, 2 years following the publication of the proposed rule, the US Health and Human Services Secretary noted that a proposed ban on MCs would take more time given the “historic attention” and “immense amount of feedback” yielded from the public comment period.30

In January 2025, FDA’s final rules for MCs and FCs were withdrawn.31,32 Given the stated influence of feedback from the comment period on delaying the rules from moving forward, the goal of the current study was to characterize the comments submitted to these dockets, including the sources and types of public submissions and the themes present in unique statements submitted to each docket. Findings from this study may provide a critical understanding of participation in the public comment process for health policies, as well as inform future educational or communication efforts about proposed MC and FC policies.

Methods

For this qualitative study, all comments from the MC (FDA-2021-N-1349) and FC (FDA-2021-N-1309) dockets were downloaded in October 2023. The Figure shows a flowchart of all docket submissions. Metadata associated with each comment included posted date; self-reported submitter category (eg, individual consumer or private industry), country, and state or province; and whether the comment had an attachment. Country was recoded as US or non-US and state was recoded into the 4 census regions (Northeast, Midwest, South, and West) or other (non-US). This study was approved by the Rutgers Institutional Review Board (Pro2023001258); since these analyses utilize publicly available data, informed consent was not obtained. Reporting follows the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) reporting guideline.33

Data Cleaning

An iterative data cleaning process was developed using R statistical software version 4.4.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing)34 to identify unique comments for thematic analysis in the MC docket comments and then applied to the FC docket comments (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). First, missing data fields (eg, category, country, and state) were flagged for analysis, the comments were retained, and blank comments were removed. Duplicate comments that shared exactly the same text originating from different submitters were identified and flagged for later removal, but a single copy was retained for text similarity calculation to enable the detection of similar but not identical comments at this step. Two researchers (M.T. and A.J.) reviewed a random sample of 3000 comments to develop a list of flags of common content signaling use of form letters (ie, prewritten letters with the same or similar information). This included commenters noting that they were using a form letter and the original source of the template letter, review of similar comments and duplicated phrases, and using WordStat version 9 (Provalis Research), a content analysis and text mining software, to automatically extract the most common phrases. This process identified 17 form letter templates. Details on the phrases used to identify each letter are provided in the eMethods, eAppendix 1, and eTables 1 and 2 in Supplement 1. Comments containing phrases from these form letters were separated for further analysis.

In the remaining sample, we measured the similarity of each comment against all other comments to further screen out those deemed nonunique; at this step, one of each duplicate comment was included so that other comments similar to these duplicates would be identified. Remaining comment text was standardized using R by removing all extra spaces, punctuation, and numbers and converted to lower case to assist with flagging key phrases from the form letters and calculating text similarity. The text2vec package version 0.6.4 in R35 was used to create a document-term matrix with term frequency–inverse document frequency transformation. We removed common words like an and the using the English tm text mining package in R.36 Following the approach investigated by Lee et al37 among others38,39 for identifying substantially related but not identical text, comments with a similarity of 0.5 or greater to any other comment were removed from the dataset. After removing similar comments, any comment that had a duplicate in the original set was also dropped. The final dataset produced for each docket retained unique comments and their metadata for qualitative analysis. No non-English comments were included in our analysis, and international comments were included in the analysis of submitter characteristics and form letters, but after unique comment identification, international comments were excluded from content analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative content analysis of the comments in each dataset was conducted in R from October 2023 to December 2024, including calculation of descriptive statistics for submitter characteristics and whether each comment was a form letter. There were only 8 comments removed due to missing data in the comment field (6 comments in the MC docket and 2 comments in the FC docket). Characteristics of all comments in each docket were assessed, including the count and proportion of comments identified as unique or form letters and the metadata associated with each comment.

After characteristics of all comments were assessed, analyses focused on comments determined to be unique in each docket. For the unique comments, we developed a common codebook of themes of arguments supporting and opposing the MC and FC bans to be used across multiple studies in our project.28,29 The initial codebook was informed by hand-coding of a random sample of 200 comments by 2 independent coders on the team (A.J. and A.A.). The independent coders met to review discrepancies and reach a consensus on which themes should be applied to comments with conflicting codes. During these discussions, the codebook definitions were refined and updated to reflect agreed-upon interpretations (see eAppendix 2 in Supplement 1). Interrater reliability was high (κ = 0.68; percentage agreement = 98.3%).

We then used WordStat to extract the most common phrases seen in all the comments. Parameters set for filtering contained phrases with no more than 5 words. Phrases were put into 2 distinct categories: opposing ban arguments and supporting ban arguments. WordStat automatically produced themes based on the frequency in which these phrases appeared. The themes that WordStat produced were comparable to the themes we identified during hand coding. No new codes were added to the codebook on the basis of the WordStat analysis; however, phrases not identified under a specific coding theme were added to the codebook as examples for that theme. This codebook was then input into the program QDA Miner version 6 (Provalis Research) for automated deductive content analysis of the unique comments in each docket and the coded data were brought back into WordStat for further analysis. Analytic options in WordStat allowed for crosstabs of theme by submitter category in each docket, with submitter category grouped as missing, individual consumer, private industry, federal government and other; χ2 tests identified differences in themes by submitter category, with a 2-sided P < .05 indicating statistical significance.

Results

The MC docket contained 175 285 comments, and the FC docket contained 71 523 comments, for a total of 246 808 comments (Table 1). Most comments submitted to each docket were identified as form letters (169 440 MC comments [97%]; 66 640 FC comments [93%]); after form letters were excluded, there were 4212 distinct MC comments and 1854 FC comments assessed for text similarity (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). Nearly 80% of these distinct comments were confirmed as unique and retained in analyses (3342 MC comments; 1432 FC comments), reflecting only 2% of the initial overall comments submitted to each docket (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). Comments were not submitted uniformly over the full time period during which the dockets were open; eFigures 1 and 2 in Supplement 1 depict specific dates when tens of thousands of comments were submitted to each docket.

Characteristics of Submitters

Among all comments submitted to both dockets (eTable 1 in Supplement 1), most were missing information on submitter category, state, or country (173 532 MC comments [99%]; 70 093 FC comments [98%]); missing data on any of these characteristics was also prevalent in the subset of unique comments (2273 MC comments [68%]; 888 FC comments [62%]). Among the unique comments to each docket, the most common category of submission when present was individual consumer (1457 MC comments [44%]; 706 FC comments [49%]), followed by private industry (340 MC comments [10%]; 209 FC comments [15%]), and the federal government (200 MC comments [6%]; 80 FC comments [6%]). Approximately half of unique submissions were from the US (1727 MC comments [52%]; 700 FC comments [48.9%]), and the state identified was most commonly from the South (771 MC comments [23%]; 326 FC comments [23%]), followed by the Midwest (357 MC comments [11%]; 129 FC comments [9%]), Northeast (23 MC comments [7%]; 106 FC comments [7%]), and West (307 MC comments [9%]; 162 FC comments [11%]) (Table 1).

Characteristics of Form Letters

Form letters were composed of content that identified their tobacco industry or advocacy sources: 2 letters in both dockets (Marlboro; Voices for Consumer Choice), 1 in the MC docket (Own It, Voice It), and 1 in the FC docket (Swedish Match); unidentified form letters opposing the rules (6 in the MC docket, 5 in the FC docket); 1 form letter supporting the rule in the MC docket; and tobacco company employee form letters (2 in the MC docket; 5 in the FC docket) (eTable 2 in Supplement 1). Examples of the 17 form letters are provided in eAppendix 2 in Supplement 1.

The majority of MC comments (169 440 comments [97%]) were identified as form letters that opposed the ban, of which 77 445 (46%) included content from tobacco companies, including RAI Services’ Own It Voice It (54 835 comments [32%]) and Altria’s Marlboro (20 370 comments [12%]) and Voices for Consumer Choice (2240 comments [1%]). An additional 595 comments (<1%) were form letters submitted by employees of ITG Brands and Universal Leaf (Table 2). The remaining 88 202 comments (52%) stemmed from 6 form letters that had no specific source but also opposed the MC ban. Only 1 form letter supported the policy (2%).

Similarly, 66 640 submissions (93%) to the FC docket were identified as form letters, although most of these (58 643 comments [88%]) did not have an identifiable source; 4 of 5 form letters in this docket opposing the FC ban were the same as those in the MC docket, and 1 letter (letter 2A) represented 77% (51 146 comments) of all form letter submissions (eTable 2 in Supplement 1). Characteristics of Unique Comments

The unique MC comments contained approximately 96% comments (3208 comments) opposing the MC ban and 4% (134 comments) supporting the ban; the unique FC comments contained 99% (1418 comments) opposing the FC ban and 1% (14 comments) supporting the ban. All themes coded in each docket and their distribution by category are reported in Table 3, Table 4, and eTables 3 and 4 in Supplement 1. Top themes in the unique comments coded in the MC and FC dockets represented opposition arguments, including loss of revenue and sales (1155 MC comments [35%]; 720 FC comments [49%]), illicit market concern (835 MC comments [25%]; 555 FC comments [38%]), more effective routes (607 MC comments [18%]; 420 FC comments [28%]), oppose menthol ban (530 MC comments [16%]; 340 FC comments [23%]), and prohibition or bans do not work (431 MC comments [13%]; 290 FC comments [20%]) (eTables 3 and 4 in Supplement 1). Individual consumers represented the category with the greatest proportion of responses under these themes. A small proportion of comments supporting the ban fell into support menthol ban (134 MC comments [4%]; 14 FC comments [1%]); arguments in this theme noted that, “This rule will reduce poor health outcomes,” and the ban would “Support the health outcomes of African American and low-income people who have been harmed by the past inaction of the federal government to guard against the predatory practices of the tobacco industry.” In both dockets, a small proportion of comments addressed scientific evidence supporting the bans, including targeted marketing of MCs in Black or African American communities (159 MC comments [5%]; 39 FC comments [3%]), menthol bans in other countries (29 MC comments [1%] for MC; 0 FC comments [0%]), and noting that menthol and flavor facilitate tobacco use (17 MC comments [1%]; 10 FC comments [1%]).

Discussion

In this qualitative study using a comprehensive quantitative content analysis of comments submitted to FDA dockets for proposed rules on MCs and FCs, most comments in both dockets (169 440 MC comments [97%]; 66 640 FC comments [93%]) were identified as form letters, and nearly all form letters opposed these policies. For most comments, the identity of the submitter could not be ascertained because information was missing. Consequently, it is unclear whether these comments reflect genuine responses from citizens. Regardless, few comments reflected individualized concerns. Viewing form letters in a favorable light, members of the public agreed with form letters drafted by corporate lobbyists. Viewing form letters in a less favorable light, tobacco companies and their advocacy consultants arrogated a procedure designed to promote public participation to advocate for their commercial interests. Ongoing use of advocacy consultants and software platforms by tobacco companies and associations (eg, Juul, ITG Brands, and Cigar Rights Association of America) to oppose specific tobacco control policies suggest a robust strategy to flood public comment opportunities rather than grassroots community action.40 Findings from this study reinforce prior studies of tobacco company influence on national tobacco policy efforts in the United Kingdom41,42,43 and New Zealand,44 as well as adoption of tobacco industry tactics to oppose alcohol policies.45,46,47

Consistent with our prior work documenting the websites by which tobacco companies encourage consumers to submit comments,28 tobacco company content comprised 46% of form letters submitted to the MC docket and 12% of form letters in the FC docket. As a result, the “immense amount of feedback submitted via public comment” largely reflected form letters and tobacco company arguments against FDA’s proposed policies,29 with few comments addressing the evidence base supporting FDA’s proposed rules or their public health goals. Interestingly, the main opposing themes present in these comments differed from those identified in content analyses of national news coverage of FDA’s proposed rules related to MCs and FCs.29 Most national news stories (75%) highlighted the potential health benefits of FDA’s proposed rules.29 In contrast, only 3% to 5% of unique comments in the dockets addressed tobacco and health harms. Additionally, concerns about enforcement against individuals and increased criminalization of Black or African American individuals were conveyed as prominent opposing arguments in the news articles,29 while top opposing arguments in the FDA comments were economic, relating to loss of revenue and sales and potential for an illicit market. Differences in themes across these data sources may reflect activation of tobacco consumers and retailers to submit comments given the presence of known industry arguments against these policies.48,49 They also reflect a difference in the goal of each medium—that is, to inform the public on a policy issue via news articles vs to advocate for a specific policy outcome based on more individualistic concerns in the public comments.

Limitations

Although this study represents a systematic evaluation of a large volume of public comments submitted to federal dockets, we note several limitations. First, our characterization of comments as form letters relied on matching distinct phrases from known letters and including them as separate flags in our dataset (ie, Marlboro and Voices for Consumer Choice). Overall, 17 distinct letters or formats were identified and separately flagged, which is likely an underestimate of common content or form letters in the docket. Second, any comment that was flagged in the process of identifying form letters was excluded from our analysis of text similarity, as we aimed to analyze only unique, non–industry-influenced comments in our qualitative analysis. Third, we did not analyze attachments submitted with comments, which may have included novel themes. A key limitation was missing data on submitters. We recommend that the federal docket submission system be updated to capture referral site or source and require data on each submitter to identify specific advocacy campaigns responsible for comments, as well as being able to identify nongenuine responses, including bots.

Conclusions

In this study of nearly 250 000 public comments submitted to inform FDA rulemaking, most comments were form letters that reflected tobacco company talking points. Comment periods represent one of the few ways in which US citizens can directly engage in federal policymaking. However, our study suggests that comment opportunities for federal tobacco policies were appropriated by tobacco companies. The combination of findings from this study and our prior review of consumer advocacy websites28 support that systems developed to feed comments into the docket controlled the narrative to policymakers rather than creating a civically engaged constituency by providing them with education about these policies. The sheer volume of comments was used as an excuse to delay public health policymaking,30 without acknowledging the prevalence of comments that could be directly attributed to tobacco company advocacy efforts, or that only 2% of the comments were unique. Delays, inaction, and the ultimate withdrawal of these 2 proposed policies will have meaningful impacts on tobacco-related death and disease in US individuals,13 while the market shares of MCs50 and FCs51 continue to grow.
