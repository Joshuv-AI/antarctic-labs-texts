A PBL-, CBL-, and seminar-based blended teaching model for chairside instruction of undergraduate dental students: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yinxue Huang, Xintong Yin, Rui She, Linqing Tian, Ming Cai, QunChao Han, Lijun Huo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis research aimed to evaluate the effect of a blended method combined with PBL, CBL and seminar methods in Operative Dentistry and Endodontics chairside teaching course on the level of theoretical scores, practical scores, teaching satisfaction, and student competence among dental undergraduate students.MethodsA 12-week randomized controlled trial of chairside teaching was conducted from September 2022 to September 2024, involving 58 fourth-year undergraduate dental students from Kunming Medical University, who were randomized to either an experimental group (PBL, CBL and seminar) or a control group (LBL). Objective assessments were carried out using the Mini-CEX and DOPS scales, whereas subjective outcomes (including evaluations of teaching model, internship effectiveness, and learning satisfaction) were measured via a validated questionnaire.ResultsAfter 12 weeks of clinical chairside teaching, compared with the control group, the experimental group obtained higher scores in interviewing skills (6.90 ± 0.94 vs. 6.21 ± 1.15, p < 0.05), humanistic qualities (7.14 ± 1.13 vs. 6.14 ± 1.25, p < 0.05), counseling skills (7.24 ± 1.15 vs. 6.38 ± 1.18, p < 0.05), preoperative preparation (7.24 ± 0.99 vs. 6.14 ± 0.83, p < 0.05), correct and standardized procedural steps (7.10 ± 1.08 vs. 6.34 ± 0.81, p < 0.05), and postoperative management (7.07 ± 1.09 vs. 6.24 ± 0.83, p < 0.05). There was no significant difference in clinical judgement and accurate and skilled technique (p > 0.05). For questionnaire response, the experimental group achieved significantly higher scores than the control group in teaching mode evaluation (38.28 ± 2.67 vs. 35.34 ± 6.13, p < 0.05), self-evaluation of internship effect (25.55 ± 4.13 vs. 23.14 ± 4.17, p < 0.05), and internship satisfaction evaluation (29.34 ± 1.12 vs. 27.62 ± 3.99, p < 0.05).ConclusionThe blended teaching model in Operative Dentistry and Endodontics chairside teaching enables efficient use of limited resources to support skill development. It also effectively enhances standardized clinical operations, teaching quality, and students’ subjective evaluations. Although no significant improvement was found in clinical judgment or technical proficiency, these areas offer potential for future focus. However, due to factors such as sample size, caution should be exercised when generalizing these findings to other educational contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08214-3.Keywords: Blended teaching model, Chairside teaching, Operative dentistry and endodontics, PBL, CBL, Seminar

Introduction

Dental education is systematically structured in two sequential phases: preclinical training and clinical education [1]. In China’s dental education system, students are required to complete all foundational and specialized dental courses within 18 months prior to engaging in clinical practice. This curriculum structure often leads to a heavy theoretical workload, with limited time dedicated to practical skills training. As a result, interns frequently encounter difficulties in applying theoretical knowledge to real-world clinical scenarios. Operative Dentistry and Endodontics education is essential not only for the training of future endodontists but also for general dentists as well [2]. Therefore, a key priority and challenge in reforming internship training in Operative Dentistry and Endodontics lies in effectively stimulating students’ motivation to learn and maximizing the efficiency of internship programs within the constraints of limited teaching resources.

Problem-based learning (PBL) and case-based learning (CBL) are student-centred, inquiry-based pedagogical approaches which are commonly used in medical and dental education [3]. Seminar learning is a teaching method in which students work together in small groups to discuss specific topics or questions under the guidance of teachers [4]. PBL and CBL share certain similarities, yet they also exhibit distinct characteristics [5, 6]. Specifically, PBL compensates for the limitations of CBL in fostering students’ innovative thinking, whereas CBL demonstrates a closer alignment with real-world clinical practice [6]. In recent years, the literature has introduced a novel teaching approach that integrates seminar with CBL. This method innovatively combines the effective communication fostered by seminar learning with the clinical reasoning emphasized in case-based learning [7, 8]. Therefore, integrating these three approaches effectively within the teaching process can be considered a promising and pedagogically sound strategy.

However, it remains to be observed whether the combined use of PBL, CBL, and seminar in chairside teaching can better assist students in improving clinical practical skills. Therefore, this study proposes a novel blended teaching model for chairside teaching of dental undergraduates: Based on the PBL teaching method, guided by specific cases from the CBL methodology, and supplemented by interdisciplinary seminar discussions. The objective is to help fourth-year undergraduates cultivate proper clinical diagnostic and treatment thinking and improve their hands-on clinical abilities, thereby achieving the Chairside teaching goal of progressing from isolated knowledge points to comprehensive understanding and application.

Materials and methods

Participants

The sample size was calculated based on prior data on overall performance from previous practice exams, as well as estimates derived from a randomized trial [2, 9]. The minimum effective sample size was calculated as 52 using G*Power 3.1 software based on the previous study effect size (d = 0.8, α = 0.05, power = 0.8) [2, 9, 10]. To ensure sufficient sample size, a total of 58 fourth-year dental students from the School of Stomatology at Kunming Medical University, who had not yet engaged in any clinical dental practice, were recruited for the study. Data collection occurred from September 2022 to September 2024.

These students were randomly assigned via simple randomization to the experimental group (n = 29) and control group (n = 29) to undergo 12 weeks of clinical chairside teaching in Operative Dentistry and Endodontics. The random assignment scheme was created from a table of random numbers by the assessors. Students were unaware of their group assignments before the study began.

Teaching methods

The clinical chairside teaching process was divided into three stages: the initial phase (Weeks 1–2), the mid-phase (Weeks 3–4), and the later phase (Weeks 5–12). The teaching focus for both groups was consistent in different stages. Both the experimental group and the control group were clinically taught by the same teacher. Additionally, all students are required to independently complete at least one premolar root canal treatment. Each student must also collect relatively complex or interdisciplinary clinical cases based on their individual clinical experience, support these cases with relevant literature review and data collection, refine and finalize the case summaries, and participate in a standardized presentation and demonstration session.

The control group primarily adopted lecture-based learning (LBL) as the primary teaching method [11]. At the commencement of the internship, review sessions are conducted focusing on common diseases. Additionally, students are organized to undergo relevant skill training on simulated head models in line with the requirements of the teaching syllabus. During the middle of the internship, under the guidance of their instructors, students receive clinical patients. Meanwhile, they participate in small-group lecture training on relevant competencies such as clinical reception, doctor-patient communication, comprehensive examination, result interpretation, and medical record documentation. At the end of the internship, students primarily continue to receive real clinical patients while participating in lecture-style training on difficult cases and treatment approaches [12] (Fig. 1).

Overview of the study design

The experimental group adopted a blended teaching approach. Considering the distinctive features of each internship phase, a hybrid instructional approach incorporating PBL, CBL, and seminar is systematically implemented. During the initial stage of the internship, pre-prepared PBL cases (such as caries, pulpitis, periapical periodontitis, etc.) are selected. The case materials are assigned one day in advance. Students formulate questions based on the theoretical knowledge they have acquired and consult relevant materials or conduct literature reviews to support their inquiries. On the next day, they have a centralized discussion, and finally clarify the diagnosis of the disease and the corresponding treatment methods. In this “scenario”, the teacher plays the role of a patient, and the students act as doctors. Students receive the “standardized patient”, clarify the diagnosis results and put forward treatment plans. And they carry out “treatment” for the “patient” on the simulated head model to train the corresponding clinical operation skills.​ In the middle stage of the internship, real and typical clinical cases are mainly used as teaching materials. Students conduct inquiries, clinical examinations, formulate treatment plans and complete some simple operations for patients in the department of endodontics. According to the patient’s condition on the day, the supervising teacher expands the content, increases the complexity and confusion of the disease, puts forward questions. A seminar discussion is held on the next day, and the teacher makes a summary.​ During the final phase of the internship, emphasis is placed on real clinical cases encountered in practice. Students are encouraged to conduct comprehensive examinations of all oral conditions beyond the patient’s chief complaint, formulate systematic treatment plans, and cultivate a general practice-oriented clinical mindset (Fig. 1).

Assessment of teaching effectiveness

Assessment scale

The Mini-CEX (Mini-Clinical Evaluation Exercise) and DOPS (Directly Observed Procedural Skills) scoring scales, which are internationally recognized, were modified to evaluate the teaching effectiveness in conjunction with oral clinical teaching [13]. The Mini-CEX is a standard evaluation system for basic clinical operation skills and educational functions [14], it consists of seven parts, interviewing skills, clinical examination skills, humanistic qualities/professionalism, clinical judgement, counseling skills, organization/efficiency, and overall clinical competence. DOPS is primarily used for assessing clinical operational procedures [15]. Here, the trainee is evaluated regarding his or her demonstrated understanding of indications, relevant anatomy, technique of procedure, obtaining informed consent, demonstrating appropriate preparation pre-procedure, technical ability, aseptic technique, seeking help where appropriate, post-procedure management, communication skills, consideration of patient/professionalism, and overall ability to perform the procedure [16].The modified rating scales for Mini-CEX and DOPS adopt a 9-point system divided into 3 levels: 1–3 points indicate failure to meet requirements; 4–6 points indicate meeting requirements; and 7–9 points indicate excellence.

Assessment methods

Both groups underwent Mini-CEX and DOPS assessments before and after the chairside teaching. Each assessment session lasted 30 min, with scores assigned by the same two non-teaching instructors in accordance with the scale content. The final score for each assessment was calculated as the mean of the scores awarded by the two assessors. To promote scoring consistency, the two assessors underwent a joint training session to calibrate their use of the Mini-CEX and DOPS scales prior to the study.

Faculty inclusion criteria

The teaching and assessment team consists of physicians who have served as attending doctors for at least five years, hold valid practicing physician qualification certificates, and have prior experience in laboratory instruction. Meanwhile, all the instructors serve as examiners for the practical/operational component of the dental licensing examination in Yunnan Province. The assessors were blinded to the group allocation of the students.

Questionnaire survey

A previously validated questionnaire adapted from Chen et al., consisting of 20 questions with a total score of 100 points, was used without modifications [12]. This included teaching mode evaluation (40 points), self-evaluation of internship effect (30 points), and internship satisfaction evaluation (30 points). Each item used a five-point Likert scale coded from 1 “strongly disagree” to 5 “strongly agree”. Students completed the survey anonymously within one week after chairside teaching, which serves as a subjective evaluation indicator of teaching effectiveness (Fig. 2).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 24.0 software. Means and standard deviations are used to present the distribution of continuous variables. The distribution of categorical variable was analyzed by chi-square. The Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to assess whether the data followed a normal distribution. The independent sample t-test was used to analyze the discrepancy between two sets of variables. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all statistical analyses.

Discussion

As a foundational subject, Operative Dentistry and Endodontics requires high levels of diagnostic ability, doctor-patient communication skills, and hands-on capabilities from doctors [2, 17]. Various challenges, including the limited duration of clinical internships, the uneven distribution of encountered cases, the operational difficulty of cases, and the restricted opportunities for hands-on practice, have led to less-than-satisfactory outcomes of clinical internships [1, 18].

Both PBL, CBL and seminar learning have been recognized as effective approaches in medical education [4, 19, 20]. In this study, compared with LBL, the blended teaching model significantly improved the score of standardization of clinical operations, teaching quality and students’ subjective evaluations of their learning experience. The findings were in line with previous studies [21, 22]. Through independently treating real patients, reviewing clinical cases, formulating diagnoses and treatment plans, participating in group consultations and presentations, and engaging in in-depth discussions with peers and instructors, who provide clarifications on challenging or controversial issues as needed, students can develop a clearer understanding and deeper insights into the material they are learning [8].

In this study, compared with the LBL model, the blended teaching model significantly enhanced the scores of communication skills, humanistic care, organization/efficiency, and overall clinical competence. The improvement in communication skills and humanistic care demonstrated may be due to the following reasons. First, simulation-based learning offers a platform to rehearse communication practices in prior to clinical work, allowing students to acclimate to clinical interaction scenarios in advance. The situational approach enhances students’ learning motivation, improves their communication skills, and expands their knowledge base [23, 24]. Second, during clinical practice, direct engagement with real patients—whether in taking medical histories or discussing diagnoses and treatments—hones their expressive abilities and empathy, while concurrently reinforcing their awareness of humanistic care. Third, case-centered seminars facilitate reflective discussions in post-clinical sessions, where instructor guidance and peer exchanges help students identify areas for improvement and implement refinements [8, 19]. Furthermore, the integration of medical ethics education throughout the entire process standardizes communication and care behaviors at a conceptual level. This synergy across multiple stages collectively drives enhancements in communication skills, humanistic care, and overall performance. This indicates that the blended teaching model is conducive to fostering students’ communication skills, empathy awareness, and humanistic care.

In this study, the absence of statistically significant differences in scores between the experimental group and the control group regarding clinical judgment, as well as the accurate and skilled technique and seek assistance when appropriate. This finding is similar to the results of Qin et al., who observed no statistically significant difference in case analysis examination between classes taught with PBL and those taught using conventional methods [25]. Which can be attributed to the following: Both groups maintained consistent teaching priorities across all training stages, with identical foundational training. The improvement of clinical judgment and operational techniques depends on development through prolonged, repetitive practice and experience. In short-term studies, this improvement may be less influenced by pedagogical styles than are communication or organizational skills. Although the blended teaching in the experimental group added interactions and case discussions, it did not surpass the control group in terms of the intensity and standards of core theoretical or operational training. Additionally, the scoring of these aspects by Mini-CEX and DOPS is based on standardized criteria, and students from both groups met the basic requirements after 12 weeks of teaching, with no breakthrough gaps arising from differences in teaching modes.

As a new teaching method, the blended teaching model still faces many difficulties. For students already balancing clinical internships with graduate entrance exam preparation or job hunting, this method may prove more time-consuming and cumbersome. Although the blended teaching model may increase the burden of learning, the results of this study that students in the blended teaching model group were more satisfied than those in the LBL group were. This is consistent with the findings of others, who demonstrated that students in the seminar-case learning (SCL) group exhibited higher levels of satisfaction compared to those in the LBL group [8, 26, 27]. However, students in the SCL group also experienced a greater sense of burden than their counterparts in the LBL group [8]. Furthermore, inadequate literature search skills or clinical knowledge among some students may result in insufficient preparation. The blended teaching model demands that educators possess not only profound medical expertise but also robust instructional skills and rich experience. They are required to dedicate extra time to curating representative PBL and CBL cases, motivating student engagement, managing seminar interactions, facilitating in-depth discussions, resolving controversial topics effectively, and ensuring comprehensive coverage of all learning objectives.

There were several limitations in this study. First, this study analyzed results from only one teaching institution, the study population is highly homogeneous—consisting exclusively of undergraduates from a specific grade. These results may have been different in other institution. Second, the sample size of students was relatively small. Consequently, caution should be exercised when generalizing these findings to other educational contexts, such as multi-center settings or student groups at different academic levels. Further randomized controlled trial with large sample size was needed to confirm the efficiency of the PBL-CBL- Seminar method. Third, this study did not conduct a formal test of inter-rater reliability. While a series of measures were implemented to ensure scoring consistency, the absence of quantitative reliability metrics may compromise the objective interpretation of assessment results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, within the context of clinical chairside teaching in Operative Dentistry and Endodontics, the blended teaching model can effectively enhance teaching outcomes. These outcomes encompass standardization of clinical operations, teaching quality and students’ subjective evaluations of their learning experience. However, the model did not yield significant gains in students’ clinical judgment or their ability to execute techniques with precision and proficiency. The model efficiently leverages limited teaching resources, facilitating students’ systematic acquisition of clinical technical competencies in Operative Dentistry and Endodontics. However, the blended model may require additional time investment and has the potential to increase the learning burden, given the necessity for active exploration and independent learning. As a viable chairside teaching strategy, medical educators may consider adopting this blended pedagogical approach.
