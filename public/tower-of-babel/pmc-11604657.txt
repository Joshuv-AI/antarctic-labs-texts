A comparative analysis of anthropometric indices for diagnosing obstructive sleep apnea among American Adults
Authors: Wenjia Liu, Shangyi Song, Hongguo Zhang, Chunping An
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The occurrence of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) syndrome is related to not only the overall obesity, but also the local distribution of fat. Therefore, we introduced anthropometric indicators to comprehensively assess the risks associated with obesity, including body mass index (BMI), which is used to assess the overall obesity, together with waist circumference (WC), body roundness index (BRI), visceral adiposity index (VAI) and lipid accumulation product (LAP), all of which reflect fat distribution. This cross-sectional study analyzed data from the 2005–2008 and 2015–2018 NHANES cycles, comprising 8966 participants. Logistic regression models were used to examine the associations between anthropometric indices and OSA, adjusting for relevant covariates. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed, and values of the area under the curve (AUC) were calculated to assess diagnostic performance. AUC comparisons between men and women were conducted using the DeLong’s test. After adjusting for covariates, WC and BMI emerged as the strongest diagnostic indicators of OSA risk. A ROC analysis revealed that WC and BMI had higher diagnostic performance performance among women (AUC: WC: 0.651, BMI: 0.653) than men (AUC: WC: 0.616, BMI: 0.619). Other indices demonstrated weaker diagnostic abilities. Both WC and BMI demonstrated a strong diagnostic ability for OSA across both sexes, with slightly better performance observed among women. These findings suggest that WC and BMI are practical and effective tools for the early detection, risk stratification and management of OSA in clinical practices.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSA) is an increasingly-serious public health problem that has a significant impact on personal health and global health systems, which is characterized by recurrent upper respiratory tract obstruction during sleep and is closely related to many adverse health outcomes, such as cardiovascular diseases, metabolic disorders and a decreased quality of life. The increased prevalence of OSA highlights the need for accurate diagnosis and early intervention strategies1–3. Obesity is one of the main risk factors for OSA. Traditional obesity indicators such as body mass index (BMI) have been widely used to assess the risk of OSA. However, BMI has limitations, as it cannot accurately reflect the distribution of body fat, a key factor in the pathogenesis of OSA4–6. Therefore, researchers have proposed other anthropometric indicators, such as waist circumference (WC), body roundness index (BRI), visceral adiposity index (VAI), lipid accumulation product (LAP) and weight-adjusted waist index (WWI), so as to assess obesity and its associated health risks more comprehensively7.

Each of these indicators provides distinct insights into fat distribution and metabolic health. WC is a direct indicator of abdominal fat accumulation and is closely related to a variety of health outcomes, including cardiovascular diseases and metabolic syndrome8. WWI is a new index that considers the ratio of body weight to waist circumference, aiming to better reflect the influence of visceral fat9. BRI is an index that estimates body fat distribution by considering waist circumference and height10. VAI is used to evaluate visceral fat by combining levels of WC, BMI, triglyceride and high-density lipoprotein11. LAP reflects fat accumulation through the levels of WC and triglyceride12. This study compares various anthropometric indicators to explore whether alternative indices can provide an improved diagnostic accuracy for OSA, addressing existing research gaps and offering a broader perspective of potential diagnostic factors for OSA. Importantly, the analyses will also examine potential gender-specific differences in the performance of these diagnostic indices in diagnosing OSA risk, as the distribution and metabolic implications of body fat can vary between males and females13.

This study used data from the National Health and Nutrition Survey (NHANES) to compare the diagnostic effects of different anthropometric measures, including BMI, BRI, VAI, LAP, WC and WWI, on OSA among American adults. By evaluating the relationships between these indicators and OSA risk, this study aims to identify the most accurate diagnostic indicators, strengthen the early detection and management of OSA, and ultimately improve the health of patients, thereby reducing the burden on the health care system14,15.

Methods

Study design and participants

The NHANES is an ongoing national study that provides extensive data on the dietary habits and general health of Americans. Prior to data collection, a written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and all study protocols were approved by the Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics. Further information about the NHANES can be found from NHANES.

This cross-sectional study utilized data from the NHANES (2005–2008 and 2015–2018), including participants aged 20 years and above with complete data for calculating their BMI, WC, BRI, LAP, VAI and WWI (n = 22,202). Participants with missing data on any of these indices (n = 13,235) or with a prior diagnosis of OSA syndromes (n = 1) were excluded, resulting in a final sample size of 8966 subjects for analyses. A detailed flowchart for participant inclusion and exclusion is available in Fig. S1 of Supplementary Materials.

In accordance with earlier studies, OSA is diagnosed when a person answers ‘yes’ to at least one of the following 3 questions of the NHANES22: (1) feeling excessively sleepy during the day despite getting at least 7 h of sleep per night, reported as 16–30 times; (2) experiencing episodes of gasping, snorting or stopping their breath on 3 or more occasions per week; (3) snoring on 3 or more occasions every week.

Potential covariates

The self-reported sociodemographic characteristics included age, sex (male/female), race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic-origin white individuals, Mexican American, non-Hispanic-origin black individuals, other Hispanics, or other races/multiple races), education level (lower than high school, completed high school, or higher than high school) and marital status (married/living with partner, never married, widowed, divorced or separated). Physical activities were assessed by asking participants to report the types of physical activities they engaged in regularly, which were categorized into vigorous (e.g., running, playing basketball) and moderate physical activities (e.g., brisk walking, swimming, cycling). Alcohol consumption and smoking status were also treated as categorical variables.

Alcohol consumption was classified into 2 categories, namely “Yes” and “No”, based on respondents’ answers to the question: "Have you consumed at least 12 alcoholic drinks in the past year?" The participants were divided into 3 smoking categories: never smokers (smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes), former smokers (smoked at least 100 cigarettes but not currently smoking), and current smokers (smoked at least 100 cigarettes and currently smoking daily or some days). The definition of metabolic syndrome (MetS) is in accordance with the criteria of updated National Cholesterol Education Program/Adult Treatment Panel III for Americans23. Additionally, total cholesterol concentrations were quantified using the enzymatic method, which utilized a single-reagent endpoint reaction highly specific for cholesterol, ensuring accurate and reliable measurements of the cholesterol level of the samples.

To address missing values in the dataset, the mean value of total cholesterol (TC) and the ratio of family income to poverty (PIR) were imputed. The confounding variables and their detailed definitions are collated in Table S1 for reference.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses for this study were performed using the R statistical computing environment (version 4.4.0). All analyses incorporated the complex survey design of NHANES, including the application of sample weights, stratification and clustering, so as to ensure that the findings are representative of the American population. Baseline characteristics were summarized using weighted means and a confidence interval (CI) of 95% for continuous variables, while categorical variables were expressed as weighted frequencies and percentages.

Continuous variables were analyzed using weighted t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests based on the distribution of data, which were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test for normality. Categorical variables were compared using weighted chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Statistical significance was set as P < 0.05.

The associations between the 6 anthropometric indices and OSA were investigated via the multivariate logistic regression model among 4 different models. The crude model was not adjusted for covariates, whereas Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex and race/ethnicity. Model 2 was adjusted for the variables of Model 1, in addition to education level, marital status, physical activities, smoking status and alcohol status. Model 3 was adjusted for the variables of Model 2, in addition to MetS and TC. A further assessment of the heterogeneity between 6 anthropometric indices and OSA was conducted through subgroup analyses, which included the following variables: age, sex, race/ethnicity, smoking status, alcohol consumption, marital status, physical activities and MetS.

Moreover, we conducted a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to illustrate the connections between the indices and the probability of developing OSA, with the reference value set as the median. This ROC analysis was performed separately for males and females to examine potential gender-specific differences in the diagnostic performance of the anthropometric indices.

Furthermore, the capacity of the indices to identify OSA was evaluated via a ROC analysis and area under the curve (AUC) values. The AUC values were compared via the Bonferroni correction and DeLong’s test24,25.

Associations between anthropometric indices and obstructive sleep apnea

In the RCS, anthropometric indices (WC, BMI, BRI, LAP, VAI and WWI) were positively associated with OSA (Fig. 1). Table 2 illustrates the associations between the anthropometric indices and the risk of OSA across different models. The results show a significant positive correlation between higher values of these indices and an increased risk of OSA. WC: Each increase of 10 cm in WC was associated with a 32% greater risk of OSA (OR = 1.32, 95% CI 1.26–1.39, P < 0.0001). Participants in the highest quartile (≥ 109.4 cm) had an OR of 3.36 (95% CI 2.66–4.24, P < 0.0001). BMI: A unit increase in BMI was correlated with an increase of 7% in the risk of OSA (OR = 1.07, 95% CI 1.06–1.08, P < 0.0001). The participants in the highest quartile (≥ 32.65 kg/m2) had an OR of 3.54 (95% CI 2.79–4.48, P < 0.0001). BRI: Each unit increase in BRI was linked to a 21% greater risk of OSA (OR = 1.21, 95% CI 1.17–1.25, P < 0.0001). The highest quartile (≥ 6.77) had an OR of 3.57 (95% CI 2.98–4.29, P < 0.0001). LAP: A unit increase in LAP was associated with a slightly-increasing risk of OSA (OR = 1.01, 95% CI 1.00–1.01, P < 0.0001). Subgroup analysis

The subgroup analysis in Table 3 shows that there is a significant interaction between demographic and lifestyle factors and anthropometric indicators (LAP, BRI, VAI, WC, BMI, WWI) in terms of OSA risk (interaction P < 0.05). A consistent positive correlation was observed in all subgroups, especially in the 40–60-year age group, non-Hispanic-origin white individuals, inactive participants and participants with higher education. The analyses of non-smokers and non-drinkers also showed slightly stronger correlations, which were further expanded by the presence of MetS. Overall, these results suggest that different demographic and lifestyle factors may regulate the relationships between these anthropometric indicators and OSA risk. Comparison of the ability of anthropometric indicators to diagnose OSA

Figure S2 shows the results of the ROC curve. The ROC analysis revealed that the AUC value of WC was the highest (0.637), followed by BMI (0.633). The AUC value of BRI, LAP, VAI and WWI was 0.622, 0.611, 0.572 and 0.568 respectively, indicating that WC and BMI had the best performance in diagnosing OSA. Table S3 demonstrates that WC (AUC: 0.637) and BMI (AUC: 0.633) had the highest diagnostic ability for OSA among the 6 anthropometric indices, both of which showed a relatively strong sensitivity and specificity compared to other measures, reinforcing their values as primary indicators for diagnosing OSA.

Additionally, we used Bonferroni correction (Supplementary Table S4) and DeLong’s test (Supplementary Table S5). The results of the Bonferroni correction revealed statistically-significant correlations of BMI with BRI, LAP, VAI and WWI (P < 0.05). Similarly, the DeLong’s test showed that the AUC differences of BMI with BRI, LAP, VAI and WWI were statistically significant (P < 0.05), while the difference between BMI and WC was not (p = 0.2393).

For males, the ROC curves of WC and BMI are closer together, with AUC values of 0.616 and 0.619 respectively. Discussion

This study used NHANES data to assess the relationships between multiple diagnostic indicators and the risk of OSA. The results revealed that WC and BMI were the strongest diagnostic indicators of OSA, which was consistent with our research hypotheses. Even after adjusting for all covariates, these associations were still statistically significant (Table 2). In addition, a group analysis also revealed consistent positive correlations, especially in the 40–60 age group, non-Hispanic-origin white individuals, inactive participants and highly-educated participants. Non-smokers and non-drinkers also showed a strong correlation, which was further expanded by the presence of MetS (Table 3). To further evaluate the potential of these indicators in diagnosing OSA, we performed a ROC curve analysis (Fig. S2) and compared the diagnostic power of 6 anthropometric indicators. The results showed that WC and BMI had the highest AUC values in diagnosing OSA, which were 0.637 and 0.633 (Table S3) respectively, indicating that they had a high accuracy in distinguishing OSA and non-OSA individuals.

These results were further verified through the Bonferroni correction and DeLong’s test. Although the AUC of WC (0.637) in Table S3 was marginally higher than that of BMI (0.633), the DeLong’s test (Table S5) indicated that this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.2393). Therefore, while WC may offer a slight advantage in diagnostic power, both WC and BMI remain robust diagnostic indicators of OSA, with no clear statistical superiority of one over the other. Although the AUC value of BRI, LAP, VAI and WWI is lower than that of WC and BMI, they are still associated with OSA risk and may serve as auxiliary indicators, particularly in specific populations.

Additionally, when stratifying by gender (Table 4), WC and BMI consistently showed strong diagnostic performance across both males and females, with slightly-enhanced diagnostic power in females. This reinforces the conclusion that WC and BMI are essential tools for the clinical risk assessment and management of OSA.

A number of studies have explored the relationship between obesity and OSA. Peppard et al. found a significant association between obesity and the incidence of OSA26. Jordan et al. noted out that obesity significantly increased the risk of OSA by increasing the deposition of fat around the upper respiratory tract while affecting the respiratory regulatory mechanism27. In addition, Gami et al. reported that systemic inflammation and metabolic disorders caused by obesity played important roles in the pathogenesis of OSA28. Vgontzas et al. further noted that obesity, especially the excessive accumulation of visceral fat, directly compressed the airway and increased the risk of OSA29. Mokhlesi and Tjaden emphasized that systemic inflammation and insulin resistance caused by obesity could affect respiratory regulation and sleep structure30. Ryan and McNicholas discussed the role of intermittent hypoxia and inflammatory molecular pathways in OSA31. These reviews further support the findings of this study and highlight the importance of obesity management in the prevention and treatment of OSA32.

The relationship between OSA and obesity is achieved mainly through a variety of physiological and metabolic pathways. Among obese individuals, an excessive accumulation of visceral fat, in particular, can lead to increased fat around the respiratory tract, directly compressing the airway and increasing the risk of OSA33. In addition, obesity can cause systemic inflammation and insulin resistance, which affect respiratory regulation and sleep structure34,35. In particular, the accumulation of visceral fat results in the release of many inflammatory factors, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), which affect the respiratory regulation function of the central nervous system and further increase the risk of OSA36. As a widely-used obesity index, BMI can reflect the total fat mass but cannot accurately distinguish fat distribution. WC is more likely to reflect the accumulation of abdominal fat, especially visceral fat. The strong correlation between increased abdominal fat and OSA may be related to the direct effect of visceral fat on respiratory structure and function37,38. An increase in visceral fat not only mechanically compresses the respiratory tract but also affects respiratory regulation and central nervous system function by releasing a variety of metabolic and inflammatory factors39. Therefore, compared with BMI, WC may be more suitable as the most direct and accurate diagnostic indicators for diagnosing OSA.

When stratifying the analysis by gender, the results revealed some interesting differences in the diagnostic performance of WC and BMI. While both anthropometric indices consistently demonstrated strong diagnostic capabilities for OSA across genders, their diagnostic power appeared to be slightly enhanced among females compared to males. These observed gender differences could be attributed to several factors. Firstly, the distribution and accumulation of body fat, particularly visceral adiposity, tended to differ between men and women. Studies have shown that women generally have a higher proportion of subcutaneous fat, while men tend to accumulate more visceral fat, which is more strongly associated with OSA risk40. The greater visceral fat deposition in men may lead to more pronounced mechanical compression of their upper airway, potentially reducing the diagnostic power of anthropometric measures like WC and BMI. Secondly, hormonal factors and sex-specific differences in the regulation of respiratory control and sleep architecture may also contribute to the gender-based variations in OSA risk as well as its diagnostic indicators41,42. For instance, estrogen has been found to have a protective effect on respiratory function, which can enhance the diagnostic accuracy of obesity indices among women. The slightly-stronger diagnostic ability of WC and BMI among females highlights the importance of considering gender-specific factors when assessing OSA risk in clinical practices. Clinicians should be aware of these nuances and potentially adjust their risk assessment strategies accordingly to improve the accuracy of OSA screening and management.

This study has several advantages. First of all, the study samples were from the NHANES, providing a large and reliable dataset. Secondly, we adjusted multiple confounding variables and performed group analyses to ensure that the results were applicable to a wider population. Thirdly, this study explored the relationship between multiple diagnostic indicators and OSA risk for the first time, used new anthropometric indicators to enhance the understanding of the relationship between obesity and OSA, and identified the potential of WC in diagnosing OSA risk. However, our study has several limitations. First of all, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to establish a causal relationship between the diagnostic indicators and OSA. Secondly, despite adjusting for relevant variables, fully eliminating the influence of confounding factors remains challenging. Additionally, the relatively low AUC values may be due to the broad and diverse population included in the analyses. The heterogeneity in demographic and health characteristics across the study population could have diluted the diagnostic power of the anthropometric indicators. Future studies focused on more specific subpopulations or stratifying analyses based on key demographic factors may improve the diagnostic accuracy and yield higher AUC values.

Conclusion

The results of this study revealed that multiple diagnostic indicators (including LAP, BRI, VAI, WC, BMI and WWI) were positively correlated with the risk of OSA among American adults. Among them, WC and BMI have the greatest diagnostic value for OSA risk. We corrected for multiple confounding variables and performed subgroup analyses to ensure that the results were applicable to a wider population. Compared with newly-developed indicators such as BRI, VAI, LAP and WWI, traditional obesity indicators such as WC and BMI have a greater sensitivity and specificity in assessing OSA risk, of which WC is simpler and more accurate than BMI. Notably, the diagnostic capabilities of these indices were found to be slightly better among females than males, indicating that gender may play a role in the effectiveness of these obesity indicators in diagnosing OSA risk.
