Navigating turbulent waters: resilience and recovery of a tertiary care hospital in Sri Lanka during economic crisis through situational leadership
Authors: Ranga Sabhapathige, Dilrukshi Deerasinghe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSri Lanka has been going through its worst economic crisis ever since April 2022. The economic crisis had a significant impact on a tertiary care hospital in Sri Lanka (THS), and the main challenges faced included a severe shortage of medical supplies, a lack of funding, staff transportation issues due to a nationwide fuel shortage, a shortage of reagents, human resource issues due to the outmigration of hospital staff and a lack of a business continuity plan in place. This perspective article aimed to describe how the economic crisis affected a THS in Sri Lanka and how the hospital’s administration overcame it by employing a leadership style similar to situational leadership. THS implemented situational leadership style, directing, coaching, supporting and delegating to effectively address the above challenges. The directing style strategies such as withholding renovation projects, purchasing drugs locally, hiring consultants, providing extra duty payments and prioritising resources were employed to guide the team through immediate challenges. The establishment of a crisis management committee served as a coaching approach. Enhancing communication among workers and implementing emotional support initiatives were key aspects of our supportive leadership, creating a positive work environment. We emphasised delegation, empowerment and teamwork, encouraging team members to take ownership of their roles and collaborate effectively. It is recommended that a business continuity plan to manage hospitals during an economic crisis be included. Medical supplies should be buffered in a larger hospital like THS for a minimum of 6 months. The government ought to enact new legislation and require applicants to sign bonds in order to retain healthcare professionals in the nation.

Introduction

Sri Lanka’s health system has been recognised as a low-cost model, high impact, on a global scale, and citizens of Sri Lanka enjoy free access to healthcare through a well-defined preventative and curative sector. Curative services are provided by a network of tertiary and secondary care facilities, divisional hospitals (outpatient and inpatient treatment) and primary medical care units (outpatient care).1 Approximately 95% of inpatient treatment and 50% of outpatient care are provided by the public sector. Through health units, the medical officer of health and his team provide preventive care to the entire island2. Since April 2022, Sri Lanka has been experiencing the most severe economic crisis in its history. All foreign reserves have been depleted as a result of debt payments, and there is no foreign reserve to buy fuel, necessities or medications. There were fuel queues all around the country, and individuals had to wait for several days to fill up their automobiles. Power outages were widespread across the country, lasting up to 12–16 hours at times.3

Finally, the central bank of Sri Lanka announced that the country was bankrupt and that it was not possible to pay all types of loans. Hospitals encountered many challenges as a result of such problems, and managing the hospitals in Sri Lanka was extremely difficult.4

THS is the third-largest hospital in Sri Lanka and the largest in the Southern Province, with 2066 hospital beds. The hospital is staffed with 4326 healthcare workers, including 121 consultants, 720 medical officers and 1970 nursing officers. The economic crisis had a significant impact on THS as well as all other major hospitals in Sri Lanka. Since THS is the final referral centre for the whole southern province, part of Sabaragamuwa province and part of the Uva province of Sri Lanka, it is very important to continue its services. Thus, it is more beneficial to understand how THS’s medical leaders oversaw the hospital during this financial downturn.

According to Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard’s book Management of Organizational Behavior (1969), situational leadership theory has two dimensions: supportive and directive.5 Directive behaviour entails clearly instructing individuals what to do, how to do it, where to do it and when to do it and then lightly supervising their actions. Supportive behaviour is listening to others, offering encouragement and support for their efforts and then encouraging their participation in problem-solving and decision-making.6 Hersey and Blanchard also divided all leadership styles into four behaviour patterns based on combinations of high or low task behaviour and relationship behaviour, which they called delegating, supporting, directing and coaching. According to Northouse7, ‘situational leadership’ refers to how leaders can become effective in a wide range of organisational situations and activities.7

The most proficient leaders are those who can modify their approach based on the circumstances and observe indicators such as the type of work, the composition of the team and additional elements that could aid in completing the mission. Situational leaders adapt their methods of leadership to suit the needs of their teams and employees.5

Objectives

This perspective article aimed to describe how the economic crisis affected the tertiary care hospital in Sri Lanka and how the hospital’s administration overcame it by employing a leadership style similar to situational leadership.

The impact of the economic crisis on a tertiary care hospital in Sri Lanka

This economic crisis had a major impact on the nation’s health system, and THS encountered significant issues as well. Sri Lanka imports more than 85% of medicines, including surgical consumables, medical equipment and parts of medical equipment that meet their requirements. Once a country announced bankruptcy, local banks were not allowed to open letters of credit (LCs) by the foreign banks since they lost faith in Sri Lankan banks. As a result of this, there was a huge shortage of medical supplies in THS initially and even not available to buy at private sector pharmacies, putting the lives of the patients in danger.8 Additionally, the hospitals had extremely limited funding. Thus, it was impossible to pay suppliers or necessary payments. Due to unpaid invoices and challenges accessing LCs, there was a lack of reagents for haematology and biochemistry analysers.

Furthermore, the human resource crisis also worsened the situation. An estimated 311 269 people left Sri Lanka in 2022, creating the highest number of migrants the country has ever experienced. Paramedics, doctors, nurses and IT specialists were among the many qualified workers who left the country. Due to the current economic crisis, about 1000 medical officers, 300 consultants and 2000 nursing officers have migrated to developing nations by 2023.9 THS was likewise negatively impacted by the brain drain. Twenty-two (22) of THS’s 100 consultants were gone, leaving some units vacant, particularly in subspecialties. Furthermore, in accordance with the International Monetary Fund’s directives, the retirement age for government employees has been reduced, and doctors have been obliged to retire earlier as a result, causing THS to lose some consultants. Also, due to the migration and retirement, there was a shortage of medical officers at THS, which led to another difficulty.

Furthermore, a severe shortage of healthcare assistants (HCA) was caused by a lack of new recruitments to the Ministry of Health, migration and lowering the retirement age of healthcare workers. Since there was no fuel in the country, public transport was disrupted, staff were unable to attend duties, and it was even very difficult to find fuel for ambulances.10

Application of situational leadership model in managing THS amid economic crisis

THS management deployed situational leadership styles, such as directing, coaching, supporting and delegating to overcome the aforementioned challenges.

Directing

All capital development initiatives, including THS renovations, were put on hold to save funds for necessary medical supplies. To obtain the credit period to pay for them, hospital management had to negotiate this with the suppliers.

THS got approval from the ministry to locally purchase items that were not available in the central medical supplies division. Due to the shortage of new medical equipment, doctors were advised to use old and already-used equipment after ethylene oxide or plasma sterilisation. Further, patients who could afford them were asked to purchase the medicines and other surgical consumables from outside, which are not available in THS.

Situational leaders in the top level of management at THS prioritised resource utilisation, ensuring that limited resources were allocated strategically to meet the most critical needs first. We identified efficiencies, eliminated waste and explored solutions to maximise resource utilisation.

The hospital prioritised the number of medical and surgical formulary items and reduced them by 30% by considering available alternatives. At the institutional level, various mechanisms were implemented to minimise the use of medical supplies by imposing strict controls on medication use. To sustain emergency and vital services, hospital management has had regular meetings with anaesthetists and surgeons. Routine procedures were postponed to save crucial drugs and surgical supplies for emergencies.

Hospital administration divided the hospital’s workforce into two groups, with only one group working at any given time and others being given formal leave. Healthcare workers were allowed to work extended hours, such as 24–48 hours, as we did during the COVID-19 pandemic, in order to face the transportation difficulties of health professionals. Special buses were arranged along predetermined routes to transport health personnel to hospitals in order to maintain hospital services.

THS hired retired medical consultants on a contract basis and arranged for consultant coverage from adjacent hospitals and the Faculty of Medicine at the University of Ruhuna to address the shortage of consultants. Furthermore, we cooperated with the Ministry of Health (the central appointing authority), searching for consultants who had completed both foreign and local training and wanted to be hired by THS as soon as possible after reporting to the Ministry from the PGIM.

Medical officers are authorised to claim full extra duty payments while also reducing the number of doctors in some units. Hospital administration reviewed the allocation of medical officers and relocated them to areas where there were fewer medical officers. Annual transfers of medical officers were temporarily halted to avoid future reductions in medical officers and doctors who finished internships and were promptly employed in needy areas.

THS management arranged more overtime payments for HCAs, started a laboratory cart system to collect specimens and deliver reports to wards, increased the staff pool at the overseer office to use them for multiple tasks in necessary places and allowed caregivers to accompany patients on possible occasions.

Leadership is critical in managing this type of crisis situation, and the management team at THS adopted a leadership style more similar to situational leadership. The hospital director provided the necessary direction, guidance and instructions to manage this crisis situation. All top-level and middle-level managers were invited to voice their opinions, and significant recommendations were put into practice. During the COVID-19 epidemic, military personnel assisted in the construction of isolation rooms and other infrastructure, as well as the provision of hospital services. Even during the economic downturn, hospital administrators obtained the necessary assistance from tri-forces to get gasoline and other services. The hospital’s medical director and deputy directors received healthcare management and leadership training at the Postgraduate Institute of Medicine at the University of Colombo and previously worked for the NHS in the UK as a part of their training. Leadership, management abilities and experience gained during their training were critical in handling the issue.

Coaching

Hospital management established a crisis management committee to examine the crisis’s impact on the hospital and determine the extent of shortages, financial limits, human resource depletion and service disruptions. This crisis management committee consists of all relevant stakeholders, including the director, deputy directors, administrative officer, accountant, chief nursing officer, senior physician, senior medical officer of the outpatient department, chief pharmacist, chief medical laboratory technologist and head overseer, to allow for collaborative decision-making, and it meets once a week to discuss the status of medical supplies, financial restrictions and human resources. Members of the crisis management committee were selected based on the importance of their managerial role in managing the crisis scenario.

Supporting

THS top management supported the pharmaceutical department by coordinating with the Ministry of Health donation management centre, ‘Sri Lanka Needs You’, to seek medical donations from various sources, including development partners, donor countries, international organisations, NGOs, local philanthropists and especially from the Sri Lankan diaspora. THS received lots of donations through this route.11 Also, THS management communicated with members of various professional colleges to use their international links to obtain donations of medicines to the hospital. THS has appointed a deputy director to communicate, organise and handle donations. She contacted the chief pharmacist regularly to update the list of out-of-stock medicinal items, which she then shared with donors, professional colleges and other hospitals to request assistance. Sri Lankans living in other countries came forward at a critical time, and medications were delivered to save the lives of innocent people.

Effective communication was essential during a crisis. Hospital management ensured clear and transparent communication with all stakeholders, including employees, patients, suppliers and government authorities via conducting physical meetings, internal memos, WhatsApp groups and the hospital public addressing system. We provided regular briefings on the situation, action plans and expectations during crisis management committee meetings. WhatsApp groups and other social media platforms were developed to facilitate communication, locate extra goods and distribute them to those in need.

During the economic crisis, both employees and their families were depressed owing to a variety of factors such as fuel and gas shortages, frequent power outages and transportation difficulties. Hospital management displayed empathy and compassion for the emotional needs of the staff. We offered emotional support, encouragement and recognition to help build morale and resilience during difficult circumstances by organising musical programmes, meditation and religious programmes, and mindfulness programmes.

During 1 day of the week, with the government’s assistance, staff were given fuel at designated fuel stations without waiting in long queues, and ambulances and hospital vehicles were given priority at petrol stations. We also organised a programme to deliver gas to employees with the assistance of the Litro Gas Company.

Delegating

As situational leaders, THS management delegated authority and decision-making responsibilities to our teams in laboratories, drug stores, radiology departments, operating rooms and intensive care units according to their level of maturity. We trusted our employees to make informed decisions based on the circumstances at hand, which fosters a sense of ownership and accountability.

Medical laboratory personnel were then encouraged to perform blood cultures, urine full reports and complete blood counts manually. Using the chemicals on hand, the hospital created reagents for biochemistry analysers.

Doctors were instructed to request only the most necessary investigations and steps to avoid repetition. Investigation reports were supplied to doctors’ mobile phones via the existing laboratory information system, reducing the use of paper, which was already scarce at the hospital. Radiographers were encouraged to upload X-rays, CT scans and MRI scans to Picture archiving and communication system (PACS) electronically because X-ray, CT and MRI printing films were not widely available in the country, and physicians were advised to view the images on tablets and smartphones.

The negative impact of employed strategies

THS policy of allowing employees to work extended shifts caused them to become accustomed to the situation, and once the crisis was resolved, it was very challenging to change. Long work hours can also cause staff fatigue and increase the chance of patient safety problems. However, throughout that time, no reports of patient safety issues connected to extended working hours were made. Although we postponed standard operations to save resources for emergencies, this may lengthen the wait time for routine surgeries, and patients may experience difficulties with the disease in the future. We tried several strategies for getting medications and other medical supplies, but the supply was insufficient to meet THS’s needs. As a result, patients are obliged to purchase certain drugs and other consumables from outside sources, causing them financial hardship.

What went wrong? How could we have done it better?

Although THS had a disaster readiness and response strategy in place to deal with common calamities in the area such as floods, tsunamis, landslides and other man-made disasters, it lacked a business continuity plan for dealing with this type of economic crisis. As a result, we had to devise our own reaction strategy based on ministry orders and local situations, leveraging our capabilities.

THS had only enough medications and other medical supplies to last 2–3 weeks. As a result, we initially were unable to cope with the medical supply deficit. We could have done it better if we had a 6-month buffer stock.

The bonds, agreements and laws governing the recruitment of personnel to the Ministry of Health in Sri Lanka are overly linear, and healthcare workers can readily leave their jobs without paying the government significant compensation. As a result, during an economic downturn, healthcare workers could readily leave their professions with no consequences, facilitating brain drain.

THS management had implemented certain expensive new technologies, such as extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, and purchased a few superfluous advanced equipment without conducting a full medical technology assessment since there was no proper framework for medical technology assessment in the country, some of which were idle in the hospital.12 Mega-construction projects, such as the new paediatric complex, surgical complex, dental complex and oncology complex, have begun without first assessing necessity and cost-effectiveness. Therefore, the financial status of the hospital was not good even at the start of the economic crisis, reducing its resilience.

Further, Sri Lankan public hospitals totally depend on government funds for medical supplies, remunerations and all other recurrent expenses, and they do not have any income generation pathways. Therefore, THS was also badly affected during this economic downfall since its sole funder was the government, and it was bankrupt. If THS had paid wards, paid investigation facilities at one wing and provided free care at the other wing (a two-tier system) within one hospital, we could have managed the crisis better.

Conclusions

THS, the third largest tertiary care hospital of Sri Lanka, could manage this worst economic crisis in Sri Lanka’s history by adapting a situational leadership style. The management of THS directed the unit’s heads and behaved in a more flexible way, allowing them to make decisions according to the ward and unit requirements and delegating more power and independence to unit heads.

Therefore, by examining THS’s experiences during times of economic instability, hospitals around the world can get important insights into what approaches were successful and what obstacles were encountered, enabling them to create business continuity plans that are more effective.

Recommendations

As situational leadership adopted by managers at THS was successful during this economic crisis, it is recommended that this leadership style be used during a similar crisis. Based on the experience we gathered from managing hospitals during this economic crisis, it was recommended to include a business continuity plan to manage hospitals during an economic crisis, especially for low- and middle-income countries. A bigger hospital like THS should have a buffer stock of medical supplies, at least for a 6-month period. The government should introduce new laws and bonds to sign at recruitment to keep healthcare workers within the country. A unit should be established within the hospital to conduct medical technology assessments to prevent unnecessary expenses. Further, necessity, feasibility and cost-effectiveness should be assessed before starting new construction projects. It was recommended to start paying for wards and other paid services at public hospitals while maintaining free services for the needy.
