Eupentacta fraudatrix: the first chromosome-level holothurian genome with extraordinarily high heterozygosity
Authors: Alexey V. Boyko, Igor Yu. Dolmatov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Eupentacta fraudatrix is the only species of sea cucumbers for which transdifferentiation has been described. This type of cell type switching occurs during gut regeneration after evisceration, which makes this species a scientifically valuable model for studying regeneration mechanisms. Moreover, chromosome-level genomes for the family Sclerodactylidae have not been available until now. In this study, we employed the MGI short-read, Oxford Nanopore, and Hi-C technologies to assemble and annotate two chromosome-level, high-quality haplotypes of E. fraudatrix. The estimated heterozygosity was 5.1%, which is much higher than the known values for sea cucumber genomes. Both haplotypes are nearly equivalent, containing 23 chromosomes with a total length of approximately 1.6 gigabase pairs, and 99% of assembled bases anchored to chromosome-level scaffolds. The annotation predicted 26,352 protein-coding genes for one haplotype and 25,238 genes for the other, with BUSCO assessment revealing 98.1 and 97.9% complete metazoa_odb12 core genes. The chromosome-level assembly and annotation of E. fraudatrix genome will provide a genomic basis for further phylogenetic, comparative, and molecular biological studies of echinoderm regeneration.

Background & Summary

Echinoderms are a group of animals related to vertebrates. Together with Hemichordata and Chordata, they form the clade Deuterostomia1. Many representatives of the phylum Echinodermata have strong regenerative abilities2,3. Holothurians, or sea cucumbers, are particularly diverse in their regenerative morphogenesis. Many species of these animals are capable of asexual reproduction by transverse fission4. Furthermore, holothurians exhibit a phenomenon known as autotomy5. They can discard the anterior or posterior parts of the body, and also their internal organs through a process referred to as evisceration. After fission or autotomy, animals can regenerate the lost organs within a time span of as little as 3–4 weeks6–8. Currently, there is evidence that almost all types of holothurian organs are capable of regeneration, including the nervous, muscular, and reproductive systems2,9. However, stem cells have not been found in holothurians, as in echinoderms in general10,11. The exceptions are the hematopoietic cells, which form the coelomocyte population, and primary stem cells12.

Regeneration in holothurians is most often based on the dedifferentiation of cells in the remaining tissues. In some cases, the formation of lost organs occurs from cells of other types, i.e., through transdifferentiation. Examples include muscles, the gut, and probably the reproductive system9,13–16. The most significant transformation occurs during gut regeneration. In this case, cells of mesodermal origin (the mesothelium or coelomic epithelium) are transformed into enterocytes, which are normally derivatives of the endoderm15. Studying the mechanisms of such a radical restructuring of the cellular genome is important for understanding the functioning of the genome of multicellular animals.

In holothurians, anterior evisceration and associated transdifferentiation are found in the “youngest” order, Dendrochirotida17, and may have evolved within the class Holothuroidea. On the other hand, transdifferentiation during gut regeneration has also been observed in the most ancient living echinoderms, crinoids18,19. Thus, comparing the genomes and gene regulation mechanisms of different species of Holothuroidea and other echinoderms is of great interest for studying the origin and evolution of regenerative mechanisms in deuterostomes and also such a rare and unique phenomenon as transdifferentiation. But, as noted in the latest study on the holothurian genome20, several holothurian orders are relatively poorly represented in genetic databases. The order Dendrochirotida is currently represented by two species from the family Cucumariidae (Cercodemas anceps and Cucumaria frondosa) and by Sclerodactyla briareus from the family Sclerodactylidae. However, only the genome of C. anceps has been sequenced to the chromosome level.

Sea cucumbers are producers of biologically active agents, in particular saponins, which exhibit anticancer and immunomodulatory activity21,22. Marine invertebrates, including holothurians, also possess many unique enzymes that may be useful in medicine or molecular biology. The first steps in studying them have already been taken, for example, several enzymes potentially involved in extracellular matrix remodelling during regeneration are isolated and studied23,24. Studying the biosynthesis of such substances in holothurians is a promising direction for biotechnological research.

The holothurian Eupentacta fraudatrix (D’yakonov & Baranova in D’yakonov, Baranova & Savelyeva, 1958) is a common representative of the Sea of Japan (East Sea) fauna (Fig. 1a). This species belongs to the family Sclerodactylidae from the order Dendrochirotida. A unique feature of this species, as in some other species of the order Dendrochirotida, is the ability to perform anterior evisceration11,25. With this method of autotomy, animals lose the entire digestive tract. Regeneration of the anterior part of the gut in E. fraudatrix is carried out by coelomic epithelial cells through their transdifferentiation15. To date, this is the only sea cucumber species for which transdifferentiation has been described15. RNA sequencing has also identified a number of transcription factors that may be directly involved in this process26. In this study, we describe the generation of a haplotype-resolved and chromosome-level genome for E. fraudatrix, providing the foundation for further phylogenetic, comparative, and molecular biological studies of this holothurian and its unique regenerative ability. We used Oxford Nanopore and Hi-C reads to assemble a chromosome-level genome of two haplotypes. As a result, we revealed that each haplotype is approximately 1,600 Mbp (megabase pairs) in size, with the entire draft diploid genome being assembled into a total of 5,607 contigs with an N50 of 1.47 Mbp. Using the Hi-C method in combination with Markov clustering of chromosome contacts via HapHiC, we combined these contigs into a diploid set of 46 chromosomes. The haploid chromosome number (1n = 23) is consistent with that of most other published sea cucumber genomes, with a distinctive feature being its high level of heterozygosity (5.1%) relative to other sea cucumbers20,27–29.

Methods

Animals, sample collection and sequencing

Adult mature individuals of the holothurian Eupentacta fraudatrix (D’yakonov & Baranova in D’yakonov, Baranova & Savelyeva, 1958) were collected in Peter the Great Bay, Sea of Japan (East Sea), and kept in 3 m3 tanks with running aerated seawater at 16 °C for one week. Body-wall tissue from one male individual was sampled for DNA extraction, then fresh-frozen and stored at −70 °C. Total RNA of body wall, coelomic fluid, tentacle, respiratory tree, male and female gonads, aquapharyngeal complex (intact and regenerative anlage after 15 days post-evisceration), egg, blastula, early and middle gastrula, and pentactula was isolated using the ExtractRNA protocol (BC032, Evrogen, Moscow, Russia), based on the Chomczynski and Sacchi method30. After the extraction, DNA and RNA were checked using a SH-NanoPura microspectrophotometer (SHST, Hangzhou, China), and samples with A260/A230 ratios greater than 2.0 were used for library preparation. RNA integrity was verified using a Qsep-100 (BiOptic, New Taipei, Taiwan); and samples with RQN greater than 9.0 were processed for sequencing.

High-molecular-weight DNA was extracted using a modified CTAB-based method31 with precipitation in 40% isopropanol on glass beads, because other methods, including phenol-based or commercial kits for animal DNA extraction (for example NEB Monarch HMW DNA Extraction kit for Tissue), leave substantial amounts of polysaccharides that clog pores during long-read sequencing. Monarch HMW DNA Extraction Kit (T3060, NEB, Ipswich, USA) beads were used to facilitate DNA dissolution and maintain its integrity. DNA integrity was checked using electrophoresis in 0.6% agarose gel with a 50 Kbp DNA ladder (M30, SibEnzyme, Novosibirsk, Russia). For library preparation, a DNA extract was considered suitable if the fragments had not undergone any changes or had shown only a slight shift down to the 10 Kbp (kilobase pairs) region (after storage). A short-insert library (400 bp) was constructed from 1 µg DNA using NEBNext dsDNA Fragmentase (M0348, NEB, NEB, Ipswich, USA) and a MGIEasy Universal DNA Library Prep kit (1000006985, MGI, Shenzhen, China) and was sequenced on DNBSEQ-G50 (MGI, Shenzhen, China) in the PE150 mode. After filtration and trimming of raw reads with Trimmomatic v0.3932 (parameters: ILLUMINACLIP:AllAdapters.fa:2:20:10:2:keepBothReads LEADING:20 TRAILING:20 SLIDINGWINDOW:3:25 AVGQUAL:25 MINLEN:30) 113.4 Gbp (gigabase pairs) of data (35.6× per nucleotide of the resulting diploid assembly) was retained (Table 1). The genomic library for long reads was prepared using the Native Barcoding kit (SQK-NBD114, ONT, Oxford, UK) with semi-selective DNA precipitation for long fragment enrichment33 and sequenced on four MinION flowcells (FLO-MIN114, ONT, Oxford, UK). Raw signal was basecalled with Dorado v0.7.3 in the duplex mode using the SUP model v4.1.0 for 260 bps signal and v.5.0.0 for 400 bps. Subsequently, the reads were trimmed with Dorado and filtered by a quality score of 7 using Chopper v0.5.034. After filtration, 71.6 Gbp (22.5× per nucleotide of resulted diploid assembly) was produced, with N50 of 13.7 Kbp and a median quality of 19.3 (Table 1). For Hi-C library preparation, we used cells from coelomic fluid and protocol of Lafontaine36 with some modifications for easier and faster operations. MboI (R0147, NEB, Ipswich, USA) and biotin-14-dATP (19524016, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) were used for chromatin digestion and biotinylation of DNA ends. Chromosome conformation-captured DNA (3 µg) was prepared for sequencing on DNBSEQ-G50 in the PE160 mode with a MGIEasy Universal DNA Library Prep Kit and NEBNext dsDNA Fragmentase. As result, 108.4 million (31.8 Gbp) Hi-C reads were produced after filtration in Trimmomatic with following parameters: LEADING:10 TRAILING:10 SLIDINGWINDOW:5:15 AVGQUAL:15 MINLEN:30.

For comprehensive transcriptome uncovering, we pooled all extracted total RNA and made a library using a MGIEasy RNA Directional Library Prep kit (1000006386, MGI, Shenzhen, China). Poly-A RNA enrichment was performed with VAHTS mRNA Capture Beads (N403, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Sequencing on DNBSEQ-G50 in the PE110 mode resulted in the generation a total of 452.3 million (96.6 Gbp) filtered reads (Table 1). We also included RNA-seq data from previous experiments on body wall and gut regeneration in the analysis16,26 (PRJNA509334). The reads were filtered with Trimmomatic as described above for short genome reads.

Genome features assessment and assembly

Before long-read sequencing and assembly, we investigated genome size and heterozygosity rate by the k-mer frequency analysis. For this, we counted 21-mers in filtered short reads using KMC v3.1.037 with the parameters recommended in the GenomeScope38 manual. GenomeScope v2.0.1 predicted a size of 1,392 Mbp and an impressive heterozygosity rate (5.1%) with 62.9% of repetitive sequences (Fig. 2a). The homozygous peak is very flattened and not conspicuous, but its center is in the region of 42, which corresponds to double the value of the center of the heterozygous peak (k-mer depth 21). Given that a total of 68.5 billion k-mers were obtained and taken into account the formula for genome size estimation39, the size was calculated to be approximately 1,630 Mbp. This difference was probably explained by the high level of heterozygosity, which prevents GenomeScope from automatically interpreting the histogram and calculating the haploid genome size. Furthermore, such high heterozygosity rate was not reported previously in studies on sea cucumber genomes. A similar but slightly lower (4–5%) level of heterozygosity has only been previously reported for the sea urchins Strongylocentrotus purpuratus and Echinometra sp. The assembly of long reads with Flye v2.9.541 (parameters: -- min-overlap 6000 --meta --no-alt-contigs--extra-params minimizer_window = 10,repeat_graph_ovlp_divergence = 0.005, assemble_ovlp_divergence = 0.005 --nano-corr) produced a total of 5,607 contigs, with a genome size of 3,184 Mbp and a N50 length of 1.47 Mbp. Accuracy and completeness of the assembly was assessed by the k-mer analysis in Merqury v1.442 and Meryl v1.4.142. The results showed a 95.55% completeness and a quality value of 39.1 (0.00012 error rate). The assembly was also evaluated using BUSCO v.6.0.043, which showed that 96.9% of the core genes were complete, and 93.3% were duplicated. This result indicated an almost completely diploid assembly obtained and was consistent regardless of the Flye settings (see Table 4 in the Technical Validation section). This also demonstrated that GenomeScope, at medium coverage, underestimates genome size to a greater extent in species with higher heterozygosity. Approaches for removing haplotypic duplications, such as purge_dups v1.2.544, generally yielded good results, with only 1% of genes lost in the process (see Table 4 in Technical Validation section). However, 10% of the genes remained duplicated. It is for this reason that we used the Flye parameters to maximize the haplotype separation, and a combination of Hi-C and HapHiC to facilitate construction of a haplotype-resolved assembly. This approach also made it possible to eliminate gene loss while reducing gene duplication arising from incomplete removal of haplotypic duplications.

Before scaffolding contigs, we used the NCBI FCS toolkit v0.5.545 to split one contig by an internal sequencing adapter and remove three contaminating contigs with a total length of 38,109 nt. We also removed one contig corresponding to the mitochondrial genome, identified with minimap2 v2.28-r120946 by the mtDNA sequence of C. anceps (NCBI, ON950725.1). To obtain haplotype-resolved chromosome-scale assembly, we applied Markov clustering of the contact graph implemented in HapHiC v1.0.747 (parameters: --correct_nrounds 5 –Nx 90) which uses Hi-C reads to assemble allele-aware scaffolds in a reference-free manner. After obtaining 23 chromosome pairs, we carried out manual orientation and order improvements using Juicebox v1.11.0848 (Fig. 2b). The number of pairs was consistent with the karyotype of this species (Fig. 1b) and most of Holothuroidea chromosome-level assemblies, except for Stichopus chloronotus49 (22 pairs). A total of 4,191 contigs out of 5,604 were anchored to chromosome-level scaffolds, representing 98.98% of the total assembly size. Completeness evaluated by BUSCO showed that one haplotype had 95.8% complete core genes with 1.5% duplicated, while the other had 95.8% complete core genes with 1% duplicated. Thus, the quality and completeness of the genome assembly is similar to previously published echinoderm genomes.

Repeat and non-coding RNA content

Repeat regions were predicted using RepeatModeler v2.0.650 (ab initio prediction; parameters: -LTRStruct) and RepeatMasker v4.1.9 (homology-based prediction; parameters: -no_is -gff -xsmall -engine rmblast). The de novo repeats were merged with repeats of echinoderms and all ancestors and descendants from the Dfam v3.9.051 and RepBase v2018102652 databases. The resulted repeat library was used for repetitive sequence annotation and masking by RepeatMasker. About 64% of the E. fraudatrix genome consisted of repetitive sequences, dominated by unclassified repeats (37.4 and 40.5% for the two haplotypes) and retroelements (18.7 and 16.7%). Most of the de novo repeats (3359 of 4847) were unclassified due to the small number of curated echinoderm repeats in the Dfam database. Most echinoderm species have 1–4 curated repeats, with the exception of the sea urchin (287).

Infernal v1.1.553 software and Rfam v15.054 database, together with tRNAscan-SE v2.0.1255, were applied to predict non-coding RNA. Infernal, with parameters recommended by Rfam documentation (--cut_ga --rfam --oskip --nohmmonly) and an E-value cutoff of 1 × 10–5, identified 21 (20 for the second haplotype) miRNAs, 591 (275) snRNAs, 17,328 (9410) rRNAs, and 13,477 (13,204) tRNAs (Table 2). Compared to the C. anceps genome20, the only published Dendrochirotida genome, the number of miRNAs was greatly underestimated (69-fold), but this depended on the parameters of Infernal. Thus, when disabling GA gathering cutoffs (without parameter --cut_ga), the number of miRNAs found increases to 2269. Also, the number of snRNAs and rRNAs, specifically the number of 5S rRNAs, is unbalanced between haplotypes (see Table 2). Protein-coding gene prediction and functional annotation

Before gene prediction, we assembled a comprehensive transcript set. First, all RNA short reads were normalized with ORNA v2.056 (parameters: -kmer 21 -sorting 1 -base 2) and assembled using SPAdes v3.15.557 (parameters: --rna --ss rf). Proteins with a minimum length of 50 amino acid residues (aa) were predicted on both strands using TransDecoder v5.5.0. The proteins were verified, as described in the TransDecoder manual, against echinoderm proteins using BLASTp v2.13.058 (see below) and HMMER v3.359 (parameters: --noali --cut_ga) with the Pfam database v36.060. Second, 355,578 redundant predicted proteins were filtered using CD-HIT v4.8.161 (parameters: -n 8 -g 1 -sc 1 -c 0.95 -T 0 -d 0 -G 0 -aS 0.8 -aL 0.0 -A 100 -M 0 -AS 100), and the remaining proteins were aligned using BLASTp to echinoderm proteins. Then proteins were selected with length of at least 60 aa and either a strong hit to a known protein (coverage of hit > 90% and percentage of positive-scoring matches > 70%) or hits to proteins from at least three different species (coverage of hit > 70% and percentage of positive-scoring matches > 30%). Finally, the proteins were mapped to the genome using miniprot v0.14-r26562 (parameters: -I --gff-only -- outc = 0.5), and bedtools v2.30.063 (parameters: intersect -wao) was applied to remove 127,327 proteins with more than 90% overlap with repeat regions predicted by RepeatMasker. As a result of this three-step procedure, a total of 21,712 proteins composed the final custom transcriptome-based protein set.

A similar procedure, with a few differences, was applied to 577,508 proteins from 17 annotated echinoderm reference genomes available in NCBI (06–26–2025) or previously published with annotations (Chiridota heheva64, Holothuria scabra29, and Stichopus monotuberculatus65). All proteins with “LOW QUALITY” tag were removed, and the threshold for overlap with repeat regions was reduced to 60% due to evolutionary distance. The final set of echinoderm proteins consisting of a total of 438,075 sequences was used for transcriptome filtering described above and merged with those from the transcriptome for subsequent gene prediction.

Protein-coding genes were predicted by EGAPx v0.4.1, BRAKER v3.0.866, and Funannotate v25.7.19, all using a combination of ab initio, homology-based, and transcript-based prediction. Funannotate predicted few-fold more protein-coding genes than EGAPx and BRAKER (2.1-fold and 2.3-fold, respectively), which showed similar results with a slight advantage for EGAPx. Evaluation of predicted protein completeness using BUSCO also favored EGAPx, which showed far fewer fragmented proteins (1.2%) when compared to Funannotate (12.1%). Due to these estimates and the high compatibility of the EGAPx annotation with NCBI database requirements, we chose to use the annotation produced by EGAPx. A total of 26,352 (25,238 for the second haplotype) protein-coding genes were predicted in this study (Table 3), which is consistent with previously published holothuroid genomes (20,754–33,857). To select an approach to protein-coding gene prediction, we used EGAPx, BRAKER, and Funannotate. EGAPx was run with three protein sets: the default protein set; the default and echinoderm proteins (described in the Methods section); and a set with additional transcriptome-predicted proteins (described in the Methods section). BRAKER and Funannotate were run with the custom protein set described above and a genome masked by RepeatMasker (see Methods). Funannotate (parameters: --max-intron 100,000) was run in two ways: with and without the SNAP v2006-07-2883, GlimmerHMM v3.0.484 and GeneMark-ES v4.7285 predictors. As shown in Table 5, turning off these predictors in Funannotate led to a substantial decrease in the number of false-positive genes. Additionally, Funannotate clearly produced more fragmented gene models compared to the other approaches. EGAPx without custom protein sets predicted significantly fewer genes than expected using data from other large sea cucumber genomes.
