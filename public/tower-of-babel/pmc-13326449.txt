Academic foundation and postgraduate exam success: the serial mediating roles of psychological state and behavioral engagement
Authors: Jiayi Gao, Wenhang Deng, Siwei Yang, Xin Zhao, Wei Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith the intensifying competition of postgraduate entrance examinations in China, academic foundation is confirmed as a key predictor of success, yet the serial mediating mechanism of psychological state and behavioral engagement in this relationship remains unexplored.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted on 280 participants from Kunming Medical University, comprising 150 admitted and 130 non-admitted candidates. Academic foundation was set as the independent variable, exam outcome as the dependent variable; psychological state and behavioral engagement served as serial mediators, support resources as a covariate, and gender, residence type, and family income as control variables. Utilizing DWLS (Full Information Maximum Likelihood) estimation and 5000 Bootstrap sample size for statistical power, the serial multiple mediation analysis was conducted for the sample.ResultsThe total effect of academic foundations on exam success was determined to be statistically significant (B = 0.265, 95% CI [0.154, 0.391], P < 0.001), with 67.2% of the total effect being accounted for as a direct effect (B = 0.178, 95% CI = [0.082, 0.286], P < 0.001). Two major indirect pathways were found. The first indirect pathway went through psychological states and behavioral engagement. It had a big effect (B = 0.029, 95% CI [0.007–0.067], P = 0.048) and was responsible for 10.8% of the whole effect. There was a significant effect (B = 0.058, 95% CI [0.020, 0.119], P = 0.019) from the second indirect pathway, which was through psychological states alone. It made up 21.9% of the total effect. A total indirect effect was observed to have a significant effect (B = 0.087, 95% CI [0.034, 0.145], P = 0.02), which accounted for 32.8% of the total effect produced.ConclusionsBy validating the dual-path serial mediation model of the Academic Foundation on Postgraduate Exam Success, this research substantiates several existing theories while providing several avenues for the use of these theoretical constructs by postgraduate entrance exam candidates and their respective institutions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09392-4.

Introduction

The surge in higher education’s popularity makes this route to expanding one’s qualifications and career opportunities the most common pathway for increasing qualified candidates. Over the past decade, the scale of the national postgraduate entrance examination in China has undergone dramatic fluctuations. The number of candidates reached a historic peak of 4.74 million in 2023 and gradually decreased to 3.43 million in 2026 [1], while master’s admission quotas have continuously expanded, rising from 570,600 in 2015 to 1.19 million in 2024 [2], with the overall admission rate stabilizing at approximately 25% to 30% between 2022 and 2025 [1, 2]. Such remarkable scale changes and fluctuating participation trends not only reflect individual academic pursuits and career planning preferences but also mirror profound social transitions regarding educational equity, labour market competition, and workforce development, which are particularly critical within medical education. Postgraduate training fundamentally shapes the quality, structure, and regional distribution of clinical and scientific research talents in the healthcare system. Despite its far-reaching social and educational implications, systematic evidence exploring the multidimensional influencing factors of examination participation, admission outcomes, and psychological status among postgraduate candidates remains insufficient.

In Candidate preparation for the Postgraduate Entrance Examination, they may encounter variability in their academic backgrounds and may prepare in disparate ways; additionally, they may experience changes in psychological state over the course of exam preparation. A multitude of factors exist that are associated with admissions outcomes [3–5]; therefore, it is important to identify and understand the key factors and their underlying logical relationships related to candidate success. Scholars have indicated that strong undergraduate academic performance and CET-6 scores are key factors positively associated with preliminary examination results and admission probability [6]. Learning ability and perseverance are positively correlated to candidates’ willingness to engage in exam preparation and to the time/commitment that they invest in the exam preparation [7]. Academic and learning support from universities (resources, academic support, and learning environment) combined with the ability of students to manage themselves are the primary factors affecting exam outcomes [8]. The literature indicates that competition for entrance into postgraduate degree programs is intense; however, success in entrance into such programs is heavily dependent on individual preparation, academic background, and support systems, and NOT the number of candidates applying for admission.

Classic theories of academic success, including the Ecosystem Theory and Self-Determination Theory, highlight that academic achievement is jointly shaped by the interaction of individual inherent characteristics, behavioral engagement, and external environmental factors [9, 10]. Among these personal attributes, academic foundation serves as a core prerequisite, which interacts with internal psychological status and learning behaviors to collectively determine subsequent academic performance. These theories form the theoretical foundation for understanding factors and correlates related to success in the postgraduate entrance examination. Academic foundation is important but works through mediating processes such as learning strategies, effort, motivation, and environment, which explains why “good foundation ≠ guaranteed success“ [5, 11, 12]. Behavioral engagement (such as study duration and preparation strategies) serves as a core behavioral variable that links an individual’s foundation to academic outcomes [13, 14]. Psychological states (such as test anxiety and self-efficacy) act as important emotional regulation variables [13, 15, 16]. Support resources (such as family support and institutional support) are key external environmental variables [17]. All of these have been proven to be closely related to academic success. However, the collaborative mediating role of these factors in the relationship between academic foundation and postgraduate entrance examination success has not been systematically explored.

Existing academic research on postgraduate entrance examinations mainly focuses on the independent analysis of individual influencing factors, while systematic exploration of the serial multi-path transmission mechanism of “academic foundation → psychological state → behavioral engagement → postgraduate entrance examination success” is relatively insufficient [5, 7, 18, 19]. There is a significant gap in the relevant theoretical framework, which hinders the comprehensive explanation of the internal logical mechanism behind postgraduate entrance examination success. Our study creates a sequential multiple mediating model of how academic background affects success on the entrance exam for graduate school. It shows how psychological state and behavioral engagement work together to affect success, and it makes the role of support resources clear. In addition to enriching the understanding of variable systems that predict academic success, the theoretical framework and research model developed in this study provide a new theoretical basis and framework for future research on postgraduate entrance examinations. In addition, by defining the major factors and correlates and the core controlling variables associated with successful performance on postgraduate entrance exams, this study will provide the necessary information to assist candidates in creating effective and scientifically sound preparation plans (e.g., reinforcing their academic foundations, managing their behaviors, and developing psychological coping skills), as well as serve as a valuable resource for educational institutions, parents, and society in the optimization of postgraduate admission support systems, thereby improving the quality and effectiveness of postgraduate entrance exam preparation.

Based on the above theoretical framework and empirical evidence, the following hypotheses are proposed:

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional analysis was performed on a sample population of 280 participants at Kunming Medical University (KMMU). Of these, 150 were admitted and 130 were not. All participants had completed the national postgraduate entrance examination. Data were collected using a self‑administered questionnaire and standardized academic records. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of KMMU (KMMU2025MEC352). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Data collection and processing

Paper‑based questionnaires were distributed during class meetings between September and October 2025. Participants completed the questionnaires anonymously and returned them on site. A total of 284 questionnaires were distributed, and 280 valid responses were retained (effective response rate 98.6%). Two independent research assistants performed double data entry; discrepancies were resolved by checking the original paper forms. Logical checks were applied, and four cases were identified as outliers and removed.

Variables

Educational foundation was operationalized as a composite of two objective components: English proficiency and comprehensive assessment performance. English proficiency: We used the higher score between CET‑4 and CET‑6. For participants who had not taken CET‑6, the CET‑4 score was used as a valid substitute. Comprehensive assessment: Participants’ official comprehensive evaluation scores (reflecting cumulative performance in core medical courses, clinical rotations, and research activities) were recoded into a 5‑point ordinal scale: 1 ≤ 60, 2 = 60–69, 3 = 70–79, 4 = 80–89, 5 ≥ 90. Each component was standardized (z‑score), and the composite educational foundation score was calculated as the sum of the two standardized components. Higher scores indicate a stronger educational foundation.

Behavioral engagement was operationalized as a composite of two objective indicators directly related to postgraduate entrance examination preparation: Review intensity: A single self‑report item measured on a 0–5 scale (0 = almost no review, 5 = very high intensity). Test‑retest intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) from a pilot sample (n = 30) was 0.78. Weekly review duration: The variable was recoded into a 4‑point ordinal scale: 1 ≤ 10 h, 2 = 10–20 h, 3 = 20–30 h, 4 ≥ 30 h. Each component was standardized (z‑score), and the composite behavioral engagement score was calculated as the sum of the two standardized components. Higher scores indicate greater behavioral engagement.

Psychological state was operationalized as “intensity of postgraduate exam willingness”—a proxy for motivational psychology. It was measured using a single-item 5-point Likert scale (1 = Extremely unwilling, 5 = Extremely willing) from the self-designed questionnaire, with the question: “How willing are you to take the national postgraduate entrance examination?“. The test‑retest ICC for this item was 0.74 (pilot, n = 30). Raw scores were standardized to form the final “psychological state” variable; missing values were assigned to participants who did not answer this question.

Support resources were a composite index of two external support dimensions, both assessed via the questionnaire: Family support: Measured by a 4-point Likert scale (1 = No support, 4 = Strong support) with the item: “To what extent does your family support your postgraduate exam preparation?“. College training: Assessed by a 4-point Likert scale (1 = No training, 4 = Comprehensive training) via the item: “Does your college provide exam guidance, review courses, or other training resources for postgraduate candidates?“. In the current sample, the two‑item composite had a Cronbach’s α of 0.72. Raw scores of the two dimensions were standardized individually, then summed to calculate the “support resources” variable. If participants missed either question, the composite variable was coded as missing values to ensure data validity.

Postgraduate exam outcome was coded as a binary variable (1 = admitted; 0 = not admitted). Gender (male vs. female), type of residence (urban vs. rural), and annual income level (< 20 K, 20–50 K, 50–100 K, > 100 K) were included as control variables.

Analytical approach

Categorical variables were summarized as n (%) and compared using Pearson’s χ² tests. Continuous variables were summarized as mean (SD) and compared between admittees and non-admittees using Welch’s t tests. Pearson correlations among key continuous variables were examined and visualized using a correlation heatmap. Before fitting the main mediation model, we conducted three diagnostic tests. First, we examined the normality of the four continuous predictors (academic foundation, psychological state, behavioral engagement, and support resources) using skewness, kurtosis, and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Second, we assessed multicollinearity by calculating variance inflation factors (VIF) for all predictors. Third, we tested for common method bias using Harman’s single‑factor test. The hypothesized serial multiple mediation model was estimated in lavaan (R) using robust weighted least squares (WLSMV, a robust DWLS estimator), which is appropriate for models involving categorical outcomes. To guarantee the validity of the SEM mediation analysis with DWLS estimation, we strictly followed conventional sample size guidelines for structural equation modeling: an absolute minimum N of 200, a sample-to-free-parameter ratio above 10:1, and a sample-to-item ratio above 10:1. With a valid sample of 280, all SEM sample size requirements were fully satisfied, supporting the robustness of the model estimation. Postgraduate exam outcome was specified as a binary categorical variable and modeled using a probit link under the WLSMV framework. Indirect effects were evaluated using BCa bootstrap 95% confidence intervals based on 5,000 resamples; effects were considered statistically significant when the confidence interval did not include 0. Gender, residence type, and income level were included as covariates. Support resources was treated as an additional covariate to control for potential confounding effects. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.2), and a two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Diagnostic test results

Before fitting the main mediation model, we conducted three diagnostic tests. The Shapiro–Wilk tests indicated non‑normality for all four continuous predictors (P < 0.001; Table S2). However, given the use of DWLS estimation, these deviations do not affect the validity of the findings. Multicollinearity was low, with VIF values ranging from 1.084 to 2.472 (Table S3). Harman’s single‑factor test showed that the first factor accounted for only 30.96% of the total variance, below the 50% criterion, indicating no substantial common method bias (Table S4). These results support the appropriateness of the subsequent mediation analysis.

Decomposition of mediating effect and significance test

Table 2, S5, and Fig. 2 summarize the findings of the mediation analysis, with Table 2 and S5 reporting the mediation model outputs, Fig. 2 showing the mediation path diagram, and Fig. 3 providing the confidence interval plot. As shown in the results, academic foundation had a statistically significant total effect on exam outcome (B = 0.265, 95% CI [0.154, 0.391], P < 0.001). The direct effect of academic foundation on exam outcome remained significant (B = 0.178, 95% CI [0.082, 0.286], P < 0.001), which accounted for 67.2% of the total effect. Notably, the total indirect effect was significant (B = 0.087, 95% CI [0.034, 0.145], P = 0.02), explaining 32.8% of the total effect. Among the proposed mediators: Academic foundation → Psychological state → Exam outcome (direct mediation path): Effect size is 0.058, 95% CI: 0.020, 0.119, contribution to the total effect is 21.9%; Academic foundation → Psychological state → Behavioral engagement → Exam outcome (chain path): Effect size is 0.029, 95% CI: 0.007, 0.067, contribution to the total effect is 10.8%. Figure 3 visually shows the estimated values and 95% confidence intervals of each effect. All the significant effects’ intervals do not cross the 0 line, further verifying the robustness of the mediating effect.

Fit test of the chained mediation model and comparison with the parallel model

For the chained mediation model constructed in this study, the fit indices estimated (Table S7) indicated that the overall fit of the model was satisfactory, meeting the fit criteria for structural equation models: CFI = 1, TLI = 1.047, RMSEA = 0.06, and SRMR = 0.003. Among these indices, both CFI and TLI were close to the ideal threshold of 0.95, while RMSEA and SRMR were both below the acceptable criterion of 0.08. The serial mediation model has a better fit over the initial parallel mediation model (CFI = 0.614; TLI = 0.292; RMSEA = 0.162; SRMR = 0.168). That model output and fit index are shown in Table S8. This suggests that the model specification is highly consistent with the data characteristics and can effectively explain the transmission relationships among variables.

Discussion

Academic foundation: a core antecedent of exam success

The academic foundation is determined to be a primary, independent, and separate predictor of success on postgraduate examinations. The admissions comparison shows that the academic foundation (low score 6.0, high score 30.0) of the admitted was higher on average than the academic foundation of those who were not admitted (low score 8.0, high score 30.0) 0.37 ± 1.60 vs. -0.44 ± 1.17, P < 0.001, which matches previous studies that found that undergraduate grade point average and English ability were predictors of admission [20–22]. The serial mediation provides additional support that the academic foundation has a significant total effect on test scores (B = 0.265, P < 0.001) and direct effect on test scores (B = 0.178, P < 0.001); however, the direct effect of the academic foundation makes up 67.2% of the total effect of the academic foundation and shows that the academic foundation is not just the basis for improved psychological and behavioral performance but actually a direct catalyst for improved performance, demonstrating that a solid understanding of the content significantly reduces the cognitive load of the exam content and improves the speed of problem-solving, which in turn leads to increased performance in the pmarginal costtest taking process. In essence, the strong academic foundation serves as a core competency for candidates, thereby increasing their self-efficacy while preparing for their examinations, minimizing the effort for obtaining and applying knowledge, and also assisting and motivating them to engage in active learning activities.

The serial mediation chain: psychological state→behavioral engagement

This research’s primary finding is the identification of the sequential mediation chain “Academic Foundation → Psychological State → Behavioral Engagement → Exam Outcome.” This pathway accounted for an estimated 31.7% of the total indirect effect in this model. Among all paths in this model, the “Psychological State → Behavioral Engagement” has the highest coefficient (B = 1.429, P < 0.001), indicating that psychological state is a major “driver” of behavioral engagement. This mediation pathway validates the “Psychological-Behavioral Linkage Theory” outlined in the field of educational psychology [23, 24]strong academic foundation helps to reduce knowledge anxiety (such as fear of encountering “unfamiliar content”) and increases self-efficacy (such as increased confidence to master exam topics), thus improving psychological state [25]. In turn, the improved psychological state encourages candidates to engage in proactive forms of exam preparation (for example, increased weekly study time, from an average of 20 h of study per week to 35 h, for admitted candidates) and to utilize targeted review strategies [26]. The transmission logic of “Foundation → Psychological State → Behavioral Engagement” fills a gap in the literature, where previous research has ignored the sequential nature of these two variable types [27]. Thus, improving psychological state would result in an increase in behavioral engagement, even among candidates who have a relatively weak academic foundation.

Direct mediation path: psychological state as a direct predictor

Through academic foundation, psychological state, and exam outcome, the path of direct mediation (i.e., academic foundation → psychological state → exam outcome) accounts for 21.9% of the total effect (B = 0.058, P = 0.019), indicating that the direct effect of psychological state was the largest of the indirect mediation paths. In addition, among all the independent variables, psychological state had the highest direct effect on exam outcome (B = 0.442, P < 0.001). Therefore, psychological state plays an important role as a direct correlate of success. A solid academic foundation will decrease the emotional burden that interferes with study preparation and give the individual an efficient state when taking the exam. Prior research has demonstrated that self-efficacy can predict academic achievement [23, 28, 29] and that psychological state interventions lead to increased passing rates on exams [30].

Support resources: a covariate, not a mediator

The results of our analysis support resources as having no mediation role; however, they do possess a significant covariate predictive effect (B = 0.182, P < 0.01) on a psychological state. Support resources were found to be a strong correlational predictor of psychological state. Academic foundation did not significantly predict access to these support resources, and therefore high- and low-achieving candidates are equivalently likely to access these resources from families or institutions. Findings from this study counter the traditional assumption that “academic advantages mean greater access to support resources” [31–33] and show that support resources function as “emotional boosters” rather than “direct conduits” for academic advantages [34].

Limitations and future directions

The research has some constraints. Because this data was collected from selected geographic areas and institution types, the findings may not be applicable to other regions and schools. The self-report measures used in the current study may provide biased responses to the mediators (e.g., psychological state and behavioral engagement). Certain variables are partially represented (i.e., not all types of support), as the researchers have not included these other types of support in their measurements. The cross-sectional nature of this research precludes researchers from establishing causal relationships, as they can only show correlations among the variables and the mediating effects of each variable. Other moderating variables (i.e., motivation level or personality traits towards preparing for the exam) have not been considered; therefore, it is difficult for researchers to draw conclusions about how the model is complete at this time. Still, there are important signs that show the model can explain a lot about how well people do on their postgraduate exams. For example, all of the critical path coefficients—that is, the total effect (c), the direct effect (a), and the significant indirect effects (ab)—are statistically significant, and their 95% confidence intervals were set up using bootstrapping. Control variables (e.g., gender, household type, and family income) did not demonstrate any statistically significant direct effect on exam outcome (all Ps > 0.05) and are therefore not expected to contribute to the model’s predicted outcome. Correlation analysis supported the existence of meaningful relationships among all of the core mediating and moderating variables, with the following correlations established between behavioral engagement and exam outcome (r = 0.53) and between psychological state and exam outcome (r = 0.55), providing a solid empirical foundation from which the researchers developed their current mediation pathway. In particular, the model’s strength has been further confirmed with a comparison of the serial mediation and parallel models. With respect to the models, the serial mediation model has some distinct advantages over the parallel model in terms of fit, realism, and statistical rigor. With respect to “fit,” the serial mediation model produced superior fit indices in each of the following areas, compared to the parallel model: CFI (1.000 vs. 0.614), RMSEA (0.000 vs. 0.162), TLI (1.047 vs. 0.292), and SRMR (0.003 vs. 0.168). The results indicate that the serial mediation model is a better representation of the data structure than the parallel model. In addition, the parallel model assumes that there are independent mediation pathways; this assumption does not consider the strong correlation that exists between psychological state and behavioral engagement (r = 0.76). By including the sequential relationship between this psychological state and behavioral engagement, the serial mediation model provides a more realistic explanation of how psychological state is associated with examination preparation. Finally, the association between support resources and examinations was eliminated from the serial model based on the evidence provided by CI.

In order to improve the potential of future research promoting the effectiveness of research, future researchers may want to consider using large-scale, multi-regional, and multi-institutional datasets to increase generalization of findings; performing research with very specific groups of candidates who vary by academic discipline or preparation stage in order to clarify conclusions; utilizing longitudinal tracking designs in order to follow the candidate through the entire preparation process and to establish causal relationships; incorporating objective indicators (e.g., study logs and exam scores) along with subjective measures to create a comprehensive picture of candidate preparation; expanding the number of ways in which the variable support resources are measured to refine the overall model; including moderating variables (e.g., motivation to prepare and personality traits) to provide a moderated mediation model to provide a deeper understanding of how academic success is achieved; examining the boundary conditions around the various mediation pathways to determine the appropriate groups of candidates; developing targeted tools and programs for candidate preparation based on research findings and conducting empirical studies to provide evidence of the efficacy of the programs developed; and applying research findings to practice to improve the scientific rigor with which need to prepare for graduate school entrance examinations is addressed.

Conclusions

This study constructs a serial multiple mediation model to examine the mechanisms through which academic foundation affects success in the postgraduate entrance examination. The results indicate that academic foundation is associated with exam success through a significant direct effect and two significant indirect pathways. Specifically, one indirect pathway operates sequentially through psychological state and then behavioral engagement, and the other operates through psychological state alone. Support resources were included as a covariate and were positively associated with psychological state, but they did not mediate the primary relationship between academic foundation and exam success. Together, these findings enrich the theoretical understanding of postgraduate entrance examination success and offer practical implications for candidates, educational institutions, and families to optimize preparation strategies and strengthen support systems.
