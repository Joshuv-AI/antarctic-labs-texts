Torqued and anti-torqued vector fields on hyperbolic spaces
Muhittin Evren Aydin 1, Adela Mihai 2, Cihan Ozgur 3

Abstract
. In this paper, we study the existence of torqued and anti-torqued vector fields on the hyperbolic ambient space ℍ n \mathbb{H}^{n} . Although there are examples of proper torqued vector fields on open subsets on ℍ n \mathbb{H}^{n} , we prove that there is no a proper torqued vector field globally defined on ℍ n \mathbb{H}^{n} . Similarly, we have another non-existence result for anti-torqued vector fields as long as their conformal scalar is a non-constant function. When the conformal scalar is constant some examples of anti-torqued vector fields are provided.

1.
Introduction

Let
(M,\langle,\rangle)
be a Riemannian manifold,
\omega
a
1
-form and
f
a smooth function on
M
. Let also
\mathcal{V}
be a nowhere zero smooth vector field on
M
. In 1944 Yano,
[
25
]
introduced a wide class of smooth vector fields known as
torse-forming
vector fields. A torse-forming vector field
\mathcal{V}
is defined by the property that

(1)

\nabla^{0}_{X}\mathcal{V}=fX+\omega(X)\mathcal{V},\quad\forall X\in\mathfrak{X}(M),

where
\nabla^{0}
is the Levi-Civita connection on
(M,\langle,\rangle)
. We respectively call the
1
-form
\omega
and the function
f
the
generating form
and
conformal scalar
(or
potential function
) of
\mathcal{V}
, respectively (see
[
2
,
21
]
). Denote by
\mathcal{W}
the dual to
\omega
on
M
, i.e.
\langle\mathcal{W},X\rangle=\omega(X)
, for every
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
. We call
\mathcal{W}

generative
of
\mathcal{V}
.

An immediate class among such vector fields appear when
\omega
is particularly assumed to be a zero map on
M
, which we call
concircular
vector fields
[
23
,
24
]
. This class has remarkable applications in Physics
[
7
,
13
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
. Similarly, we have the class of
recurrent
vector fields when
f
is identically zero
[
8
]
.

Another class of torse-forming vector fields is the
proper
ones, i.e., the generating form and conformal scalar are nowhere zero. More clearly, this condition means that there is no an open subset of
M
such that
\omega
and
f
are identically zero. There are non-trivial examples of such vector fields in various ambient spaces. For every rotational hypersurface in the Euclidean
n
-dimensional space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the axis passing the origin, the tangential part of the position vector field
\Phi
is a proper torse-forming vector field
[
5
]
. Another non-trivial example is on the hypersphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
; if
(x_{1},...,x_{n})
are the canonical coordinates of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
\mathcal{V}=e^{-x_{1}}(\partial/\partial x_{1}-x_{1}\Phi)
is a proper torse-forming vector field on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}

[
14
]
.

In this paper, we are interested in two particular types of proper torse-forming vector fields, called torqued and anti-torqued vector fields. In principle, these arise by emposing additional conditions on the generative
\mathcal{W}
. If
\mathcal{V}
is a proper torse-forming vector field then these two types are defined based on whether
\mathcal{W}
is perpendicular or parallel to
\mathcal{V}
.

More explicitly, Chen
[
4
]
defined a
torqued
vector field as a torse-forming vector field
\mathcal{V}
with
\langle\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}\rangle=0
on
M
. It was proved that a twisted product of an open interval and a Riemannian (or Lorentzian) manifold admits a torqued vector field (see
[
4
]
). Such vector fields were used to characterize Ricci solitons
[
6
]
. On Einstein manifolds, every torqued vector field is proportional to a concircular vector field
\mathcal{T}
by a smooth function
\lambda
with
\mathcal{T}\lambda=0

[
6
]
. In addition, Deshmukh et al.
[
11
]
proved that there is no a torqued vector field globally defined on the two standard simply-connected models of Riemannian space forms, the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n}
and the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In contrast, an example of such a vector field can be established on an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with nowhere vanishing conformal scalar. In
[
26
]
, Yoldas et al. showed that a characteristic vector field of a Kenmotsu manifold cannot be torqued. Submanifols of Kenmotsu manifolds were also characterized by using the torqued vector fields.

The second type of proper torse-forming vector fields that we focus on is the
anti-torqued
vector fields introduced by Deshmukh et al.
[
10
]
(see also
[
3
]
). These are proper torse-forming vector fields with
\mathcal{W}=-f\mathcal{V}
on
M
, which is contrary to the case of torqued vector fields with
\mathcal{W}\perp\mathcal{V}
on
M
. Denote by
\nu
the dual to
\mathcal{V}
. Then, by (
1
), an anti-torqued vector field
V
fulfills

(2)

\nabla^{0}_{X}\mathcal{V}=f(X-\nu(X)V),\quad\forall X\in\mathfrak{X}(M).

Because an anti-torqued vector field
\mathcal{V}
is nowhere zero on
M
, so is its dual
\nu
, then it is always proper. In contrast, there are non-proper torqued vector fields, e.g. concircular vector fields. In addition, there are anti-torqued vector fields
\mathcal{V}
where
\mathcal{V}=\nabla^{0}g
, for some smooth function
g
on
M
, which is not valid for the torqued vector fields. Such vector fields are also known as
concurrent-recurrent
vector fields when
f
is particularly assumed to be a nonzero constant
[
22
]
. The necessary and sufficient condition for a Riemannian manifold to carry an anti-torqued vector field is that it is locally a warped product
I\times_{\lambda}F
, where
I
is an open interval and
F
a Riemannian manifold, as one can be seen in
[
1
, Theorem 3.1]
and
[
22
, Theorem 3]
.

Assuming the existence of a vector field nowhere zero on Riemannian manifolds governs their geometry and topology (see
[
1
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
14
]
). Motivated by this, first we study the problem of determining whether a torqued vector field is globally defined on the third standard model
\mathbb{H}^{n}
of Riemannian space forms. The same problem is also considered for the anti-torqued case.

This paper is organized as follows. After giving the basic concepts in Section
2
relating to Riemannian (sub)manifolds, we provide non-trivial examples of torqued and anti-torqued vector fields in Section
3
. Moreover, we have a non-existence result for torqued vector fields on Riemannian manifolds as long as they are of constant length (Proposition
3.2
). In contrast, there exist other torse-forming vector fields of constant length on Riemannian manifolds (Propositions
3.5
and
3.6
). In Section 4, we prove that a proper torqued vector field cannot be globally defined on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
(Theorem
4.1
). Similarly, when the conformal scalar is a non-constant function, there is no an anti-torqued vector field globally defined on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
(Theorem
4.2
).

2.
Preliminaries

Let
(M,\langle,\rangle)
be a Riemannian manifold of dimension
n
and
\nabla^{0}
the Levi-Civita connection of
M
. The Riemannian curvature tensor is defined by

R(X,Y)Z=\nabla^{0}_{X}\nabla^{0}_{Y}Z-\nabla^{0}_{Y}\nabla^{0}_{X}Z-\nabla^{0}_{\left[X,Y\right]}Z,\quad X,Y,Z\in\mathfrak{X}(M),

where
[,]
is the bracket operation. Let
\Gamma
be a plane section of
T_{p}M
with a given basis
\{x,y\}
. The sectional curvature
K(\Gamma)
of
\Gamma
is defined by

K(x,y)=\frac{\langle R(x,y)y,z\rangle}{\langle x,x\rangle\langle y,y\rangle-\langle x,y\rangle^{2}}.

A Riemannian manifold
\mathbb{M}^{n}(c)
is called a space form if
K
is a constant
c
for every plane section at every point. The standard simply-connected models of a Riemannian space form
\mathbb{M}(c)
are the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}=\mathbb{M}^{n}(0)
, the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n}=\mathbb{M}^{n}(1)
and the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{n}=\mathbb{M}^{n}(-1)
.

There are several models for the hyperbolic ambient space, two of them are utilized in this paper. Let
(x_{1},...,x_{n})
be the canonical coordinates of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The first one is the upper half space model
\mathbb{H}^{n}=\{(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x_{n}>0\},
which is endowed with the metric

\langle,\rangle=\frac{1}{x_{n}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}dx_{i}^{2}.

Let
\mathbb{R}_{1}^{n+1}
be the Lorentz-Minkowski space endowed with the metric

\langle,\rangle=-dx_{1}^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{n+1}dx_{i}^{2}.

Then, the second model that we concentrate on is the hyperboloid model

\mathbb{H}^{n}=\{P=(x_{1},...,x_{n+1})\in\mathbb{R}_{1}^{n+1}:\langle P,P\rangle=-1\}.

Let
N
be a submanifold into a Riemannian manifold
M
. The Gauss formula is given by

\nabla^{0}_{X}Y=\nabla_{X}Y+h(X,Y),\quad X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(N),

where
\nabla
is the induced connection on
N
and
h
is the second fundamental form.

3.
Some examples and Results of Torse-Forming Vector Fields

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a torqued vector field on a Riemannian manifold
M
, i.e.

\nabla^{0}_{X}\mathcal{V}=fX+\omega(X)\mathcal{V},\quad\omega(\mathcal{V})=0,

for every
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
. Assume that
M
is a twisted product
I\times_{\lambda}F
, where
I
is an open interval and F a Riemannian manifold, and
\mu
is a smooth function on
F
. From
[
6
]
, it is known that

\mathcal{V}=\lambda\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial s},\quad s\in I,

is always a torqued vector field tangent to
I
, where
s
is the arc-length parameter of
I
.

If the ambient space
M
is a sphere or Euclidean space, then such vector fields cannot be defined on
M
globally
[
10
,
11
]
. However, there are examples of torqued vector fields on open subsets of Euclidean space (see
[
11
, Example 1]
) as well as of hyperbolic ambient spaces.

Example 3.1
.

We consider the hyperboloid model of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. If
\Phi:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{1}
is the standard isometric immersion, then by the Gauss formula we write

\nabla^{0}_{X}Y=\nabla_{X}Y+\langle X,Y\rangle\Phi,\quad X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{H}^{n}),

where
\nabla^{0}
stands for the Levi-Civita connection of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{1}
and
\nabla
the induced one on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Set
\mathcal{T}=P_{0}+\langle P_{0},\Phi\rangle\Phi
, where
P_{0}\neq 0
is a constant vector field in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{1}
. From
[
15
]
, it follows that

\nabla_{X}\mathcal{T}=\langle P_{0},\Phi\rangle X,\quad X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).

Next, we consider the function

f:\widetilde{M}\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},\quad(x_{1},...,x_{n+1})\mapsto f(x_{1},...,x_{n+1})=\frac{x_{1}}{x_{n+1}},

where

\widetilde{M}=\{(x_{1},...,x_{n+1})\in\mathbb{H}^{n}:x_{1}x_{n+1}\neq 0,x_{1}\neq x_{n+1}\}.

We set

P_{0}=\sum_{i=2}^{n}a_{i}\partial_{i},\quad a_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{V}=e^{f}\mathcal{T}.

It is clear that
\mathcal{V}(f)=0
and so

\nabla_{X}\mathcal{V}=f\langle P_{0},\Phi\rangle X+\omega(X)\mathcal{V},\quad X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{H}^{n}),

where
\omega(X)=X(f)
. Because
\omega(\mathcal{V})=0
,
\mathcal{V}
is a torqued vector field on
\widetilde{M}
.

On any Riemannian manifold, we have a non-existence result for a non-constant torqued vector field when it is of constant length.

Proposition 3.2
.

A proper torqued vector field that is non-constant on a Riemannian manifold is never of constant length.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a torqued vector field on a Riemannian manifold
M
, i.e.

\nabla^{0}_{X}\mathcal{V}=fX+\omega(X)\mathcal{V},\quad\omega(\mathcal{V})=0,

for every
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
. By contradiction, assume that

0=X(|\mathcal{V}|)=f|\mathcal{V}|^{-1}\langle X,\mathcal{V}\rangle+|\mathcal{V}|\langle X,\mathcal{W}\rangle,\quad\forall X\in\mathfrak{X}(M).

Writing
X=|\mathcal{V}|
and
X=|\mathcal{W}|
, we see that
f
and
\omega
are identically zero, which is not possible.
∎

Let
\mathcal{V}
be an anti-torqued vector field on a Riemannian manifold
M
. If
M
is a Euclidean space, then there are examples of
\mathcal{V}
globally defined on
M
but not on a sphere (see
[
10
]
). Next we give an example of
\mathcal{V}
when
M
is the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{n}
.

Example 3.3
.

In the upper-half space model of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, an orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},...,e_{n}\}
can be chosen as

e_{j}=x_{n}\partial_{j},\quad e_{n}=-x_{n}\partial_{n},\quad j=1,...,n-1.

The Levi-Civita connection gives, for
i=1,...,n
and
j=1,...,n-1,

\nabla_{e_{j}}e_{n}=e_{j},\quad\nabla_{e_{n}}e_{n}=0,\quad\nabla_{e_{i}}e_{j}=0\text{ }(i\neq j),\quad\nabla_{e_{j}}e_{j}=-e_{n}.

It is direct to see that
e_{n}
is an anti-torqued vector field, namely

\nabla_{X}e_{n}=X-\langle X,e_{n}\rangle e_{n},\quad\forall X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{H}^{n}),

where the conformal scalar is identically
1
.

Similar to Example
3.1
, on open subsets of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, we also may have other examples of anti-torqued vector fields.

Example 3.4
.

As in Example
3.1
, we consider the hyperboloid model of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. We introduce

f:\widetilde{M}\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},\quad(x_{1},...,x_{n+1})\mapsto f(x_{1},...,x_{n+1})=\frac{1}{x_{n+1}},

where

\widetilde{M}=\{(x_{1},...,x_{n+1})\in\mathbb{H}^{n}:x_{n+1}\neq 0\}.

We set
P_{0}=\partial_{n+1}:=\frac{\partial}{\partial_{x_{n+1}}}
,

\mathcal{T}=P_{0}+\langle P_{0},\Phi\rangle\Phi=\partial_{n+1}+x_{n+1}\Phi\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{V}=f\mathcal{T},

where
\Phi
is the position vector field. We will show that
\mathcal{V}
is an anti-torqued vector field. Since we know that

\nabla_{X}\mathcal{T}=\langle P_{0},\Phi\rangle X,\quad X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{H}^{n}),

the following expression occurs

\nabla_{X}\mathcal{V}=\frac{1}{x_{n+1}}\langle P_{0},\Phi\rangle X+X\left(\frac{1}{x_{n+1}}\right)\mathcal{T}

or equivalently

(3)

\nabla_{X}\mathcal{V}=X+X\left(\frac{1}{x_{n+1}}\right)\mathcal{T}.

A direct calculation gives

X\left(\frac{1}{x_{n+1}}\right)=-\frac{1}{x_{n+1}^{2}}\langle P_{0},X\rangle.

Because
\langle P_{0},X\rangle=\langle P_{0}+\langle P_{0},\Phi\rangle\Phi,X\rangle
, writing in Equation (
3
),

\nabla_{X}\mathcal{V}=X-\left\langle\frac{1}{x_{n+1}}\mathcal{T},X\right\rangle\left(\frac{1}{x_{n+1}}\mathcal{T}\right),\quad X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{H}^{n})

or equivalently

\nabla_{X}\mathcal{V}=X-\left\langle\mathcal{V},X\right\rangle\mathcal{V}\quad X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{H}^{n}),

implying that
\mathcal{V}
is an anti-torqued vector field on
\widetilde{M}\subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
with constant conformal scalar
1
.

In contrast to Proposition
3.2
, an anti-torqued vector field is always unit. Recall that a smooth vector field
\mathcal{V}
on a Riemannian manifold is called a
unit geodesic
vector field if
\nabla_{\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{V}=0
. We have the following.

Proposition 3.5
.

An anti-torqued vector field on a Riemannian manifold is always a unit geodesic vector field.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be an anti-torqued vector field on a Riemannian manifold
M
and
\mathcal{T}\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
a unit vector field with
\mathcal{V}=|\mathcal{V}|\mathcal{T}
. Using (
2
), we get

X(|\mathcal{V}|)=f|\mathcal{V}|^{-1}(1-|\mathcal{V}|^{2})\langle X,\mathcal{V}\rangle

and

fX-\langle X,\mathcal{V}\rangle\mathcal{V}=\nabla^{0}_{X}\mathcal{V}=f|\mathcal{V}|^{-1}(1-|\mathcal{V}|^{2})\langle X,\mathcal{V}\rangle+\nabla^{0}_{X}\mathcal{T}

for every
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
. Writing
\mathcal{V}=|\mathcal{V}|\mathcal{T}
,

fX-|\mathcal{V}|^{2}\langle X,\mathcal{T}\rangle\mathcal{T}=\nabla^{0}_{X}\mathcal{V}=f(1-|\mathcal{V}|^{2})\langle X,\mathcal{T}\rangle\mathcal{T}+\nabla^{0}_{X}\mathcal{T}

or equivalently

(4)

-fX+\left(|\mathcal{V}|^{2}+f(1-|\mathcal{V}|^{2})\right)\langle X,\mathcal{T}\rangle\mathcal{T}+\nabla^{0}_{X}\mathcal{T}=0

for every
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
. We have two cases:

1.
\mathcal{T}
is constant. Then, (
4
) becomes

fX=\left(|\mathcal{V}|^{2}+f(1-|\mathcal{V}|^{2})\right)\langle X,\mathcal{T}\rangle\mathcal{T},\quad\forall X\in\mathfrak{X}(M).

Assuming
X\perp\mathcal{T}
, we arrive at the contradiction
f=0
.

2.
\mathcal{T}
is non-constant. Writing
X=\mathcal{T}
in (
4
), we have

f(1-|\mathcal{V}|^{2})\mathcal{T}+\nabla^{0}_{\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{T}=0

implying that
|\mathcal{V}|=1
and
\nabla^{0}_{\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{T}=0
. This completes the proof.
∎

Notice that Proposition
3.5
is not valid for any proper torqued vector field
\mathcal{V}
. Otherwise, it follows from (
1
) that

0=\nabla^{0}_{\mathcal{V}}\mathcal{V}=f\mathcal{V}+\omega(\mathcal{V})\mathcal{V},

or equivalently
f\mathcal{V}=0
, which is not possible. On the contrary, every proper torqued vector field is always a non-trivial geodesic vector field whose potential function is the conformal scalar
f
of
\mathcal{V}
(see
[
12
]
).

A final conclusion regarding the torse-forming vector fields of constant length is the following.

Proposition 3.6
.

If a proper torse-forming vector field
\mathcal{V}
on a Riemannian manifold is of constant length, then it is parallel to the generative. Particularly, every unit torse-forming vector field is an anti-torqued vector field.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a torse-forming vector field of constant length
|\mathcal{V}|>0
given by (
1
) and
\mu
its dual. Then it follows

0=\langle\nabla^{0}_{X}\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}\rangle=f\langle X,\mathcal{V}\rangle+|\mathcal{V}|^{2}\omega(X)

or equivalently
\omega=-|\mathcal{V}|^{-2}f\mu.
This means that
\mathcal{V}
is parallel to the generative. If
|\mathcal{V}|=1
, then it is an anti-torqued vector field.
∎

4.
Non-Existence Results of (Anti-)Torqued Vector Fields on
\mathbb{H}^{n}

From
[
11
]
, we know that there are no proper torqued vector fields globally defined on either a sphere or on a Euclidean ambient space. In this section, we establish a similar non-existence result for those vector fields on the other standard complete simply-connected model of Riemannian space forms, more precisely on hyperbolic spaces.

Theorem 4.1
.

There is not a proper torqued vector field globally defined on
\mathbb{H}^{n}.

Proof.

By contradiction, suppose that
\mathcal{V}
is a torqued vector field globally defined on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
satisfying (
1
). Denote by
\mathcal{W}
the generative of
\mathcal{V}
which is dual of the generating form
\omega
. Then,
\omega(\mathcal{V})=0
, i.e.
\mathcal{V}\perp\mathcal{W}
. The curvature tensor of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is

(5)

R(X,Y)\mathcal{V}=\langle X,\mathcal{V}\rangle Y-\langle Y,\mathcal{V}\rangle X,\quad\forall X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).

Hence,

\displaystyle R(X,Y)V

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}(fY+\omega(Y)\mathcal{V})-\nabla_{Y}(fX+\omega(X)\mathcal{V})-

\displaystyle-f[X,Y]-\omega([X,Y])\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{-Y(f)+f\omega(Y)\}X+\{X(f)-f\omega(X)\}Y

\displaystyle+d\omega(X,Y)\mathcal{V}.

By (
5
) and the above equation, we have

(6)

\displaystyle\langle X,\mathcal{V}\rangle Y-\langle Y,\mathcal{V}\rangle X=

\displaystyle\{-Y(f)+f\omega(Y)\}X+\{X(f)-f\omega(X)\}Y+d\omega(X,Y)\mathcal{V}.

Since this identity holds for every
X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathfrak{\mathbb{H}}^{n}),
so does a linearly independent triple
\{X,Y,\mathcal{V}\}
. Then, it must be
d\omega=0
. By (
6
), we conclude

\langle X,V\rangle=X(f)-f\omega(X),\quad X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{H}^{n}),

which implies

\langle X,\mathcal{V}-\nabla f+f\mathcal{W}\rangle=0

or equivalently

\nabla f=\mathcal{V}+f\mathcal{W}.

If
\nabla f(p)=0,
for some point
p\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{H}^{n}),
then
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{W}
would be linearly dependent at
p,
which is not possible due to
\mathcal{V}\perp\mathcal{W}
. Hence we are able to suppose that
\nabla f(p)\neq 0,
for every point
p\in\mathbb{H}^{n}.
Then,
f:\mathbb{H}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a submersion and every level set
\Sigma_{x}=f^{-1}\{f(p)\}
is
a compact submanifold of dimension
n-1
(see
[
16
]
). Introduce a vector field
\mathcal{T}\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{H}^{n})

\mathcal{T}(p)=\frac{\nabla f(p)}{\langle\nabla f(p),\nabla f(p)\rangle},\quad p\in\mathbb{H}^{n}.

It is direct to observe that
\mathcal{T}(f)=1
and so the local
one-parameter group of local transformations
\{\varphi_{t}\}
of
\mathcal{T}
satisfies

(7)

\mathcal{T}(\varphi_{t}(p))=f(p)+t,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

Using escape Lemma (see
[
16
]
) and expression (
7
), we understand
that
\mathcal{T}
is a complete vector field and
\{\varphi_{t}\}
is one-parameter group of transformations of
\mathbb{H}^{n}.
We next define a smooth function

g:\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma_{x}\to\mathbb{H}^{n},\quad g(t,q)=\varphi_{t}(q).

Notice that

\varphi_{t_{1}}\circ\varphi_{t_{2}}=\varphi_{t_{1}+t_{2}},\quad t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

Then, for every
q\in\mathbb{H}^{n},
we can find a parameter
t\in\mathbb{R}
and a point
\varphi_{t}(q)=q^{\prime}\in\Sigma_{x}
with
q=\varphi_{-t}(q^{\prime}),
yielding that
g(-t,q^{\prime})=q.
Also, if
g(t_{1},q_{1})=g(t_{2},q_{2}),
then

(8)

\varphi_{t_{1}}(q_{1})=\varphi_{t_{2}}(q_{2})

and expression (
7
) gives

f(q_{1})+t_{1}=f(q_{2})+t_{2}.

Due to
q_{1},q_{2}\in\Sigma_{x}
and
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, we see
that
f(q_{1})=f(q_{2})
and
t_{1}=t_{2},
namely from (
8
) it follows
q_{1}=q_{2}.
Hence
g
is an one-to-one and onto mapping with its inverse

g^{-1}(q)=(-t,q^{\prime})=(-t,\varphi_{t}(q)).

Consequently, we observe that
\mathbb{R}\times\Sigma_{x}
is
diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, implying
\Sigma_{x}
is diffeomorphic
to
\mathbb{H}^{n-1}
. This is not possible because
\Sigma_{x}
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
.

Although there is no an anti-torqued vector fields globally defined on a sphere (see

10
]
), there are examples in a Euclidean and hyperbolic ambient spaces, as mentioned in Section
3
. Moreover, in
3
-dimensional setting, if the metric of a Riemannian manifold admitting an anti-torqued vector field with constant conformal scalar is Ricci soliton (or gradient Ricci almost soliton), then it is of constant negative curvature
[
22
]
. However, as long as the conformal scalar is a non-constant function, we have a non-existence result for anti-torqued vector fields globally defined on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

There is not an anti-torqued vector field globally defined on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
with non-constant conformal scalar.

Proof.

The proof is by contradiction. Suppose that
\mathcal{V}
is an anti-torqued vector field on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Then,

\displaystyle R(X,Y)V

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\{Y(f)+f^{2}\nu(Y)\}X+\{X(f)+f^{2}\nu(X)\}Y

\displaystyle+\{-X(f)\nu(Y)+Y(f)\nu(X)+d\nu(X,Y)\}\mathcal{V}.

By (
5
) and the above equation, we have

\displaystyle\langle X,\mathcal{V}\rangle Y-\langle Y,\mathcal{V}\rangle X

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\{Y(f)+f^{2}\nu(Y)\}X+\{X(f)+f^{2}\nu(X)\}Y

\displaystyle+\{-X(f)\nu(Y)+Y(f)\nu(X)+d\nu(X,Y)\}\mathcal{V}.

Assume that
\{X,Y,\mathcal{V}\}
is a linearly independent set. Then, using
\nu(X)=\langle X,\mathcal{V}\rangle,

\displaystyle X(f)+\left(f^{2}-1\right)\nu(X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle Y(f)+\left(f^{2}-1\right)\nu(Y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle-X(f)\nu(Y)+Y(f)\nu(X)+d\nu(X,Y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

for every
X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{H}^{n}).
Obviously, we
have
d\nu=0
and

\nabla f=(1-f^{2})\mathcal{V}.

By the hypothesis,
f
is non-constant and so we may assume
\nabla f(p)\neq 0
for every
p\in\mathbb{H}^{n}
such that
f(p)\neq\pm 1
. The proof can be completed by following the same way as in that of Theorem.
4.1
.
∎
