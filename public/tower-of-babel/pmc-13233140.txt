Functioning gonadotroph macroadenoma associated with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1
Authors: Carlos Eduardo de Melo Oliveira, Delmar Muniz Lourenço Junior, Teresa Neuma Albuquerque Gomes Nogueira, José Ítalo Soares Mota, Raquel Montenegro, Ana Rosa Pinto Quidute
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report the case of a 35-year-old woman with clinically and genetically confirmed multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1) who presented with abnormal uterine bleeding, hypermenorrhea, menstrual irregularities, and visual field defects. Hormonal evaluation revealed elevated estradiol levels, non-suppressed follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), suppressed luteinizing hormone (LH), and mild hyperprolactinemia. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a large pituitary macroadenoma. The patient underwent transsphenoidal surgery, and histopathological and immunohistochemical (IHQ) analyses confirmed a functioning gonadotroph adenoma. Postoperatively, clinical and gynecologic symptoms improved; however, residual tumor tissue showed progression on follow-up imaging. This case highlights a rare but clinically significant manifestation of MEN1-associated pituitary disease. Functioning gonadotroph adenomas may present with ovarian hyperstimulation rather than typical hypogonadism or mass-related symptoms. Recognition of this distinct biochemical and clinical profile is essential for timely diagnosis and management. Functioning gonadotroph adenomas, although rare in MEN1, should be considered in patients presenting with ovarian hyperstimulation and atypical gynecologic symptoms. Surgical resection is the mainstay of treatment, but long-term surveillance is required due to the risk of residual disease progression.

Introduction

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1) is a rare (prevalence: 3-20 per 100 000), highly penetrant autosomal dominant disorder caused by a germline pathogenic variant in the tumor suppressor gene MEN1. It is characterized by the development of multiple endocrine and non-endocrine tumors. The most frequent endocrine manifestations involve the parathyroid glands, the enteropancreatic system (duodenopancreatic neuroendocrine tumors), and the anterior pituitary gland. In addition, affected individuals may develop neuroendocrine tumors of the thymus, stomach, or lungs, as well as adrenocortical tumors, meningiomas, and cutaneous lesions such as facial angiofibromas, collagenomas, and lipomas [1-3].

The diagnosis of MEN1 is established when at least one of the following criteria is fulfilled: (i) a clinical criterion, defined by the presence of two or more MEN1-associated endocrine tumors involving the parathyroid glands, duodenum/pancreas, or pituitary gland; (ii) a familial criterion, defined by the presence of at least one of these tumors in an individual with a first-degree relative diagnosed with MEN1; or (iii) a genetic criterion, defined by the identification of a germline MEN1 mutation, even in asymptomatic individuals [1, 3]. To date, no consistent genotype–phenotype correlation has been established, and marked intrafamilial variability in clinical manifestations is well recognized [2].

Pituitary tumors occur in approximately 40% of patients with MEN1, although prevalence varies across cohorts [4-9]. Most MEN1-associated pituitary adenomas are hormonally active (52-85%), with prolactinomas being the most common subtype (43-79%). These are followed by clinically nonfunctioning adenomas, growth hormone–secreting tumors, corticotroph adenomas, and thyrotroph adenomas [2]. In general, the management and treatment of pituitary adenomas in MEN1 patients are similar to those applied to sporadic pituitary tumors [2].

Functioning gonadotropin-secreting pituitary adenomas associated with MEN1 are exceedingly rare, with only three such cases reported in the literature to date [10-12] (Table 1). Notably, none of these reports simultaneously provided comprehensive clinical, biochemical, imaging, genetic, and family history data.

Here, we report the case of a 35-year-old woman with clinically and genetically confirmed MEN1 who presented with symptoms of gonadotropin hypersecretion and ovarian hyperstimulation and was diagnosed with a functioning gonadotroph macroadenoma.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old woman was referred to the neuroendocrinology clinic with complaints of visual field impairment associated with abnormal uterine bleeding, hypermenorrhea, and menstrual irregularities. She had been clinically diagnosed with MEN1 for 10 years and had a positive family history (Fig. 1). Her past medical history included primary hyperparathyroidism, which was treated with total parathyroidectomy and forearm autotransplantation, as well as enteropancreatic tumors—namely a gastrinoma and probably nonfunctioning pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors—diagnosed in 2018 based on imaging studies (Fig. 2) and markedly elevated serum gastrin levels (>1000 pg/mL) (SI: >476 pmol/L) (reference range, <100 pg/mL [SI: 0.0-47.6 pmol/L]). These tumors were surgically treated with pancreaticoduodenectomy (Whipple procedure). In addition, she underwent surgical resection of an anterior mediastinal mass measuring 3.4 × 4.2 × 3.7 cm, which was histopathologically diagnosed as a thymolipoma (Fig. 3). Genetic testing identified a pathogenic germline splice-site variant in the MEN1 gene (c.654+1G>T; HGMD: CS982266; dbSNP: rs794728622), which has been previously reported in the literature [13-15].

In accordance with MEN1 follow-up guidelines [2], magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the sella turcica was performed, revealing a pituitary macroadenoma measuring approximately 4.0 × 2.6 × 2.6 cm (Fig. 5). At that time, the patient also reported visual disturbances. The presence of clinical signs such as hypermenorrhea and recurrent uterine bleeding prompted further hormonal evaluation and pelvic ultrasonography, which demonstrated elevated estradiol and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) levels, along with ovarian hyperstimulation (Table 2). Prolactin levels were slightly elevated. Serum insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), free thyroxine (free T4), and results of the dexamethasone suppression test were within normal limits. Taken together, these findings strongly supported the diagnosis of a functioning gonadotroph adenoma.

Treatment

Following the diagnosis of a pituitary macroadenoma, medical therapy with cabergoline was initiated, and the patient was jointly followed by the endocrinology and neurosurgery teams. In this context, with the hypothesis of a functioning gonadotropinoma already in place, she was referred for transsphenoidal surgery, which was uneventful. Histopathological examination revealed a pituitary adenoma, and immunohistochemical (IHQ) analysis showed positivity for synaptophysin and FSH (Fig. 6), thereby confirming the diagnosis of a gonadotropin-secreting tumor.

Outcome and follow-up

After surgery, the patient continued to be followed by the endocrinology and neurosurgery teams. She experienced improvement in hypermenorrhea and visual field defects, and her menstrual cycles became regular approximately six months after tumor resection. As shown in Fig. 5, postoperative MRI demonstrated residual tumor tissue that increased in size over subsequent months. The patient is currently under close surveillance with periodic MRI scans, and additional therapeutic options are being considered.

Discussion

We report an unusual case of a woman previously diagnosed with MEN1 who presented with an exceptionally rare combination of clinical findings, namely symptoms of ovarian hyperstimulation caused by a functioning gonadotropin-secreting pituitary macroadenoma.

Gonadotroph tumors (GTs) are a subtype of pituitary adenomas and account for approximately one-third of clinically nonfunctioning pituitary tumors in some immunohistochemical studies [16]. In surgical series, they may represent up to 70-75% of clinically nonfunctioning adenomas [17]. Owing to their typically non-secretory nature, these tumors are often diagnosed incidentally or as a result of local mass effects, such as visual field defects, as observed in our patient. In the general population, when GTs produce clinical manifestations beyond mass-related symptoms, they more commonly result in anterior pituitary insufficiency rather than gonadal hyperstimulation. Manifestations of gonadal hyperstimulation—whether testicular (macro-orchidism) or ovarian (stimulation resembling that seen during ovulation induction)—are distinctly uncommon, making the presentation observed in this case particularly rare [16, 17].

When hormonal secretion is sufficient to produce symptoms in women of reproductive age, clinical manifestations may include oligomenorrhea or amenorrhea, infertility, and recurrent or multiple ovarian cysts. These cystic changes may mimic ovarian neoplasms and, in some cases, lead to unnecessary surgical interventions [18]. In men, reported manifestations include sexual dysfunction, gynecomastia, increased testicular volume, and, rarely, precocious puberty during adolescence. In both sexes, features of secondary hypogonadism—such as decreased libido and infertility—may be present, particularly in the context of global pituitary dysfunction. In rare instances, the clinical course may progress to overt ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome in women or marked testicular enlargement in men [18].

Although the association between gonadotroph adenomas and MEN1 has been previously reported, the clinical presentation in our case is notably distinct from those described in earlier reports (Table 1). Most published cases, including those by Benito et al (2005) [11] and Sztal-Mazer et al (2008) [10], involved patients presenting with nonspecific manifestations such as amenorrhea, galactorrhea, or visual disturbances—symptoms commonly associated with pituitary macroadenomas [10, 11]. In contrast, our patient exhibited a distinctive presentation characterized by clinically overt ovarian stimulation, manifested by abnormal uterine bleeding, hypermenorrhea, and menstrual irregularities. This constellation of findings underscores the importance of considering a functioning gonadotropin adenoma in the differential diagnosis of MEN1 patients who present with atypical gonadal symptoms.

In MEN1, gonadotropin-secreting adenomas account for fewer than 2% of reported pituitary tumors, making them substantially less frequent than prolactinomas (≈65%), nonfunctioning adenomas (≈40%), somatotroph adenomas (6-8%; associated with gigantism or acromegaly), and corticotroph adenomas (1-4%). This distribution further underscores the rarity of the case reported herein. In addition, cosecreting adenomas have been described in up to 10% of patients, with some tumors exhibiting atypical plurihormonal secretion [19]. This possibility was considered during the patient's hormonal evaluation but was ruled out based on the biochemical test results.

Only a minority of gonadotroph tumors (GTs) are functioning, as in the present case, and secrete biologically active gonadotropins, leading to elevated circulating levels of intact FSH and/or LH. Even among functioning tumors, hormone concentrations are often insufficient to produce clinical symptoms, as GTs are typically poorly differentiated and secrete hormones inefficiently [20]. Hyperestrogenism represents the predominant biochemical abnormality, with estrogen levels ranging from mildly elevated—as observed in our patient—to markedly increased. Serum LH levels are usually suppressed, including in cases with positive LH immunoreactivity; however, less commonly, LH concentrations may fall within the reference range. It has been proposed that preserved pulsatile LH secretion is sufficient to maintain the synthesis of thecal androstenedione, thereby providing substrate for FSH-driven aromatase activity in granulosa cells, ultimately resulting in estrogen excess [18]. The suppression of LH levels may reflect either impaired normal gonadotroph function due to tumor mass effects or negative feedback from elevated estrogen levels.

Accordingly, the most commonly reported hormonal profile includes elevated or normal FSH levels, normal or increased α-subunit concentrations, suppressed LH, and elevated estradiol and prolactin levels—a pattern also observed in our patient (Table 2) [18, 20]. Notably, prolactin levels in this case were not sufficiently elevated to suggest a prolactinoma. During the diagnostic workup, the mild hyperprolactinemia was attributed to a possible pituitary stalk effect or another physiological mechanism involving hyperestrogenemia, such as estrogen acts on the tuberoinfundibular dopaminergic system, reducing dopamine synthesis and release, and can directly activate the prolactin gene promoter in the pituitary via estrogen response elements (ERE) was observed in animal models and pituitary GH3 cells [21, 22]. True co-secretion of FSH and prolactin by a single adenoma is rare and requires confirmation by IHQ analysis of surgical specimens. In our case, IHQ analysis demonstrated positivity exclusively for FSH, with no evidence of prolactin expression, supporting the diagnosis of a gonadotroph adenoma rather than a true plurihormonal tumor.

In general, surgical resection remains the primary treatment for both nonfunctioning and functioning gonadotroph tumors, particularly in functioning cases, given the increased risk of ovarian torsion associated with ovarian hyperstimulation. Adjuvant therapies, including radiotherapy and dopamine agonists such as cabergoline, may be indicated in the presence of high-risk tumor features or residual disease [23-25]. Although some case series of gonadotroph tumors report tumor recurrence or progression of residual lesions in approximately 6.6% of patients following surgery [20], the impact of germline mutations on the clinical course and long-term outcomes of these tumors remains poorly defined.
