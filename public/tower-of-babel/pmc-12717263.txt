Proteomic Dataset of Sparganum proliferum and Spirometra mansoni to Understand Asexual Proliferation in Hosts
Authors: Akito Fujihira, Kensei Kinoshita, Atsushi Hikima, Asuka Kounosu, Yasunobu Maeda, Kuniyo Sakamoto, Yoko Kondo, Taisei Kikuchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cestodes of the genus Spirometra are known for their complex life cycles, which involve aquatic and terrestrial hosts, with humans serving as the incidental hosts. This study presents a proteomic dataset of Sparganum proliferum and Spirometra mansoni, two medically important Spirometra group species that exhibit distinct plerocercoid developmental patterns. Using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), we analysed secreted proteins from the proliferative and non-proliferative plerocercoids to gain insight into the molecular basis of asexual proliferation, host-parasite interactions, and potential therapeutic targets. The dataset, which includes raw mass spectrometry files, peptide spectral matches, and protein functional annotations, is publicly available in jPOSTrepo (PXD063547 and PXD063548). This resource provides valuable data for helminth researchers investigating cestode biology and host-manipulation strategies.

Background & Summary

Cestodes of the genus Spirometra have complex life cycles involving multiple hosts1,2. Typically, aquatic copepods serve as the first intermediate hosts, amphibians or reptiles as the second intermediate hosts, and carnivorous mammals, especially felids and canids, act as definitive hosts where adult worms mature and reproduce. Humans can become incidental hosts through ingestion of infected intermediate hosts or contaminated water. Among these, Sparganum proliferum is unique in its ability to undergo asexual proliferation within vertebrate hosts, leading to an aggressive form of sparganosis3. Unlike Spirometra mansoni, whose plerocercoids remain confined to their developmental state in the intermediate hosts, S. proliferum can spread throughout the host’s body by continuous budding and migration, causing severe pathological consequences. This proliferative capacity is a rare phenomenon among cestodes, and raises significant questions regarding the molecular mechanisms that enable such a strategy4.

The ability of S. proliferum to propagate within the host is likely driven by key molecular pathways that regulate cell proliferation, immune evasion, and host tissue remodelling4. In contrast, S. mansoni follows a more conventional developmental pattern, with plerocercoids persisting in the tissues without extensive replication. By performing a comparative proteomic analysis of the secreted proteins (secretome) of these two species, we aimed to identify the proteins and pathways that distinguish between proliferative and non-proliferative phenotypes. A growing body of evidence suggests that helminth parasites, including cestodes, actively manipulate their host environment through secreted proteins. These, also referred to as excretory-secretory (ES) products, are the primary interface between parasites and hosts, and therefore have been key molecules in understanding the molecular interactions between parasites and hosts. They play a role in mediating host immune modulation, tissue adhesion, and nutrient acquisition. Prior work in cestodes has characterised ES/secreted repertoires in Hymenolepis diminuta5, Mesocestoides corti6, and Echinococcus spp.7, providing comparative context for conserved and lineage-specific secreted molecules. Our dataset offers a side-by-side view of secretomes from a budding/proliferative (S. proliferum) versus a non-budding (S. mansoni) plerocercoid, thereby highlighting species-biased secreted proteins that may underpin divergent host-parasite interaction strategies.

Collection of secreted proteins

Proteins secreted by S. proliferum and S. mansoni were collected from plerocercoid larvae, representing their parasitic stages in the human body. These larvae were cultured in high-glucose DMEM at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for four days. Three independent experiments yielded an average of 13.0 µg of protein (12.4 µg, 10.3 µg, 16.2 µg) from S. proliferum and 12.6 µg (13.6 µg, 14.2 µg, 10.1 µg) from S. mansoni. Identification of secreted proteins

To characterise the secreted proteins, a label-free quantitative proteomic analysis was performed8,9. In S. proliferum, 7101 peptide spectral matches were identified, corresponding to 1115 proteins10. In S. mansoni, 3806 peptide spectral matches were observed, mapping to 704 proteins10. The identified proteins in S. proliferum ranged in length from 66 to 9091 amino acids (aa), with an average length of 592.7 aa, whereas those in S. mansoni ranged from 64 to 6910 aa, with an average of 545.6 aa.

Among the identified proteins, 797 S. proliferum proteins (71.5%) possess known protein (Pfam) domains, while 627 proteins (56.2%) have Gene Ontology (GO) terms assigned. In S. mansoni, 482 proteins (68.5%) contain Pfam domains, and 381 proteins (54.1%) have GO terms assigned. Signal peptide analysis indicates that 259 proteins (23.2%) in S. proliferum and 196 proteins (27.8%) in S. mansoni exhibit predicted secretion signals, suggesting that most of the detected proteins are released through non-classical secretion pathways.

The ten most abundant secreted proteins from S. proliferum and S. mansoni are listed in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. In S. proliferum, the most abundant protein showed no significant similarity to known sequences, indicating a potentially novel, species-specific factor. Several highly abundant proteins contained fibronectin type III (fn3) domains, characteristic of immunoglobulin-like proteins that may mediate host interaction or immune signaling. Multiple proteins also carried the Taeniidae_ag domain, typical of tapeworm-specific antigen B-like proteins, potentially involved in immune modulation. Two serine-type endopeptidases with Trypsin domains were identified, suggesting roles in proteolytic activity within the host environment. In S. mansoni, the secretome was likewise dominated by tapeworm-specific antigen B-like proteins. A large retrotransposon-related protein containing multiple conserved domains was among the most abundant. Other prominent proteins included a cystatin domain–containing protease inhibitor, a centrin-like calcium-binding protein with EF-hand motifs, and several proteins of unknown function. Overall, both species secrete a combination of conserved antigen-like and immunoglobulin-like proteins, underscoring common strategies for host interaction and immune modulation. Enriched GO terms against the full predicted proteomes on S. proliferum (18,919 proteins) and S. mansoni (22,162 proteins) are summarised in Figs. 2 and 3. In the Biological Process category, S. proliferum enriched GO terms are from three main biological categories: (1) protein folding and maturation, including chaperone-mediated processes; (2) metabolic pathways involving pyridine-containing compounds such as NAD(P), reflecting redox and energy-related functions; and (3) immune response pathways centered on neutrophil activation and degranulation, as well as cytoskeleton remodeling and exocytosis necessary for effective immune defense (Fig. 2). The presence of these terms in tapeworm species, which lack a developed immune system, is interesting. GO annotations in this study were assigned by homology/orthology transfer. Therefore, enrichment of immune-related terms indicates parasite molecules that interact with host immunity, and does not imply endogenous immune pathways in the tapeworm. In S. mansoni, enrichment was observed for immune cell activation and secretion, particularly involving myeloid and granulocyte pathways. It broadens the functional landscape with extracellular matrix organisation and muscle differentiation, indicating a possible role in tissue remodelling or stress responses (Fig. 3). In the Cellular Component category, both species showed significant enrichment for extracellular region, vesicle lumen, and secretory granule terms, consistent with the secretory nature of the analysed proteins (Figs. 2 and 3). In the Molecular Function category, S. proliferum was enriched for enzymatic functions, particularly in protein degradation and oxidation-reduction. There’s also a strong presence of binding functions, including carbohydrate interaction and cytoskeletal/protein folding binding, indicating roles in metabolic processing, redox regulation, and protein quality control (Fig. 2). In S. mansoni, enriched GO in Molecular Function category indicate active roles in proteolysis, oxidative stress response, and immune regulation. Enrichment in serine-type peptidase and oxidoreductase activities suggests involvement in protein turnover, inflammation, and redox balance. A total of 702 Pfam domains were identified in S. proliferum, while 398 were detected in S. mansoni, with 350 domains being shared between the two species10. Among the shared domains, 254 exhibited similar abundance levels between species (defined as a fold change within ± 2) with an overall correlation of 0.60 (Pearson’s r) (Fig. 4). These include domains such as 14-3-3, astacin, CUB, and enolase, which have been implicated in parasitic adaptation in other parasites12,13. Domains that were enriched more than two-fold in S. proliferum included peptidase M1, fibronectin type III (fn3), trypsin, coesterase and CAP domains. In contrast, S. mansoni showed a higher abundance of the Taeniidae antigen, collagen, EF-hand and cystatin domains. Methods

Ethical statement

The animal experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the committees of the University of Tokyo and Tottori University. All experiments were conducted in compliance with the ethical guidelines of each university and the national regulations for the humane treatment of research animals. Experimental infections with S. proliferum and S. mansoni were performed according to ARRIVE guidelines. All efforts were made to ensure animal welfare throughout the study period.

Parasites and animals

The Sparganum proliferum strain Venezuela14 used in this study was maintained at the Parasite Systems Biology Lab, University of Tokyo. S. mansoni larvae were provided from Parasitology Lab in Tottori University obtained from experimentally infected hamsters.

Preparation of secretome

Live S. proliferum and S. mansoni plerocercoids were isolated from the infected animals and washed five times in sterile PBS to remove debris. Most S. proliferum plerocercoids displayed budding/branching outgrowths whereas no budding was observed in S. mansoni, consistent with the proliferative/non-proliferative morphotypes described previously4. Approximately 100 larvae were preincubated in 65 ml of PBS containing antibiotics [1 × Anti-Anti (Life Technologies)] for 1 h at 37 °C. The larvae were then transferred to 65 ml of high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; 4.5 g/l of D-glucose and L-glutamine; Life Technologies) supplemented with antibiotics [1 × Anti-Anti (Life Technologies)] and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The cultures were monitored to ensure viability and sterility, and the medium was collected after 36 h. The incubated solutions were concentrated using a 10-kDa molecular weight cutoff microconcentrator (Amicon Ultra-15; Millipore).

Concentrated E/S products were analysed for protein content using a Quant-iT™ Protein Assay Kit and Qubit fluorometer (Invitrogen, Tokyo, Japan). Approximately 15 μg of protein per sample was subjected to 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) using NuPAGE Novex gels (Invitrogen). Following electrophoresis, the gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 (Bio-Rad).

Mass spectrometric analysis

Protein samples were digested with trypsin at 37 °C for 20 h with continuous mixing and then subsequently treated with formic acid and ethyl acetate as described in12. Tryptic-digested peptides were analysed using a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) coupled with an Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano system (Thermo Scientific). Chromatographic separation was performed using an EASY-Spray column (25 cm × 75 µm ID, PepMap C18, 2 µm, P/N: ES902) maintained at 45 °C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in distilled water (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B) at a flow rate of 0.3 µL/min. Peptides were separated over a 70-minute gradient. Mass spectrometry analysis was conducted in the positive ion mode using an EasySpray nano-ESI probe. The spray voltage was set to 1.7 kV, the capillary temperature was maintained at 250 °C, and the S-lens RF level was 50. Data were acquired using a data-dependent acquisition (DDA) method with a full MS/dd-MS² (Top10) strategy. Full MS scans were acquired at a resolution of 70,000 over an m/z range of 380–1500 with an AGC target of 3e6 and a maximum injection time of 60 ms. The top 10 most intense ions were selected for fragmentation using an isolation window of 1.6 m/z, normalized collision energy (NCE) of 27.0, and resolution of 17,500. The AGC target for the dd-MS² scans was set to 1e5 with a maximum injection time of 55 ms. Dynamic exclusion was applied for 20 s, and the charge states of unassigned, 1+, 6–8+, and >8+ ions were excluded from fragmentation.

Protein identification and functional annotation

Proteomic identification was conducted using Proteome Discoverer 2.5 (Thermo Scientific). Raw files obtained from the Q-Exactive mass spectrometer were processed for peptide and protein identification using a database search. The search was performed using Sequest HT (Thermo Scientific) against the protein database of S. proliferum (v4.0)15 and S. mansoni (v2.0)4. Common contaminants were included in the database to ensure quality control. For the database search, trypsin was used as the digestion enzyme, allowing up to two missed cleavages. The precursor mass tolerance was set at 10 ppm and the fragment mass tolerance was set at 0.02 Da. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine (+57.021 Da) was included as a fixed modification, whereas oxidation of methionine (+15.995 Da) and protein N-terminal acetylation (+42.011 Da) were considered as variable modifications. Protein identification was validated using Percolator, with a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of less than 1% applied at both the peptide and protein levels. Only peptides with a minimum length of six amino acids were included in the analysis. Protein grouping was applied to report the most confident protein entries, and peptide confidence levels were categorised as high or medium, ensuring a stringent FDR control. Each experimental sample was analysed in triplicate. Protein quantification was performed in Proteome Discoverer (Thermo Scientific) using the Minora Feature Detector and Precursor Quantifier nodes. Peptide intensities were defined as precursor-ion chromatographic areas (arbitrary units). Protein-level abundance was computed as the mean of the three most abundant peptide groups per protein (“Top3”). Intensities were log₂-transformed and between-run normalised using the Total Peptide Amount method. Unless stated otherwise, no missing-value imputation was applied; coefficients of variation (CV) were calculated as 100 × SD/mean across biological replicates (n = 3). MA plotting: one-sided detections (present in only one species) were assigned fixed display values (M = ± 10); the A-axis was computed with a small offset (k = 1) added to abundances solely to stabilise the log transform.

Functional annotation was performed using eggNOG-mapper (v2.1.6)16 to assign protein descriptions and GO terms via orthology to eggNOG groups. GO enrichment analysis was performed using Fisher’s exact test with multiple testing corrections using topGO (v2.54.0)17 and visualised using ggplot2 (v3.4.4) implemented in R18. Additional protein domain searches were conducted with Pfam (v.31.0)19 using the HMMscan (v3.4)20 against Pfam-A v31.0 using gathering thresholds (–cut_ga), with clan overlap resolution (pfam_scan.pl -clan_overlap) and a minimum model coverage ≥0.5. Signal peptides were predicted with SignalP v4.0 g21 (Eukarya, TM-aware) using default criteria (D-score ≥0.45). Proteins with a SignalP call and a cleavage site were recorded as SP-positive (SignalP-noTM and SignalP-TM). No additional TM screen was applied.

Quality control and data processing

Before analysing the experimental samples, a quality control (QC) assessment was performed using the Pierce HeLa Protein Digest Standard. The QC sample was prepared at a concentration of 100 ng/µL, and a 2 µL injection was analysed under identical LC-MS conditions with a 70-minute gradient. The QC criteria required the identification of at least 10,000 peptides from a 200 ng HeLa digest injection.

Data Records

The dataset consisted of raw LC-MS/MS spectral files, peptide spectral matches, and functionally annotated protein lists. The raw data are available on jPOST under accessions JPST0037848 and JPST0037869 (ProteomeXchange accessions PXD063547 and PXD063548 respectively). Metadata accompanying the dataset provides essential details on sample origins, experimental conditions, data processing parameters, and validation metrics. The analysed protein lists (spreadsheet format) are deposited at Zenodo10, with descriptive README files to facilitate reuse. This repository structure enables straightforward reanalysis and integration into comparative studies of helminth secretomes.

Technical Validation

To ensure reproducibility and data reliability, multiple biological replicates were included for both the S. proliferum and S. mansoni samples. Peptide identification was performed in technical triplicate with a stringent false discovery rate of less than 1%, and quality control was maintained using internal standards and negative controls. The correlation of protein abundance among biological replicates ranged from 0.89 to 0.93 (Spearman’s rho) for S. proliferum, and from 0.72 to 0.85 for S. mansoni. The technical replicates of LC-MS/MS showed a correlation of 0.95–0.99 (Spearman’s rho), demonstrating high reproducibility in protein identification. Comparative analyses of biological replicates demonstrated consistency in protein identification and relative abundance, confirming the robustness of the dataset. Additionally, key proteins identified in previous cestode secretome studies were detected, supporting the biological relevance of these results.

Usage Notes

The dataset is accessible through [repository link] and can be utilised for various research applications, including comparative secretome analyses, host-parasite interaction studies, and drug target discovery. Researchers are encouraged to use bioinformatics tools such as [software] for data visualisation and further analysis. This dataset provides a foundation for future investigations into the molecular mechanisms underlying asexual proliferation in parasitic helminths, and may contribute to the identification of novel therapeutic targets.
