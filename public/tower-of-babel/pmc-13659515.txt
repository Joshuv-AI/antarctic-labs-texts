An artificial intelligence-augmented electrocardiogram model can detect undiagnosed chronic liver disease, but can this be applied in primary care?
Authors: Brad A Keith, Samuel O Schumann III, Andrew D Schreiner
Published: 2026

The burdens of chronic liver disease (CLD) and its complications of cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and liver-related death continue to grow throughout the world (1). Despite an immense morbidity and mortality, CLD is woefully underdiagnosed and often goes undetected until the symptoms and signs of late-stage liver disease appear (2,3). Developing and deploying interventions to improve CLD detection in primary care, and connecting affected patients to emerging pharmacotherapies and specialty care, is imperative.

Simonetto et al.’s pragmatic, cluster randomized clinical trial evaluated the impact of an electrocardiogram (ECG) machine learning (ECG-ML) model on detecting CLD with advanced fibrosis in primary care (4). The ECG-ML model significantly increased new diagnoses of CLD with advanced fibrosis [odds ratio (OR) 2.09; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.22–3.55] and CLD with any fibrosis (OR 3.17; 95% CI: 1.86–5.40) compared to usual care (4). This work is critically important for advancing CLD care because it leverages machine learning (ML) to improve the diagnosis of clinically significant CLD using ECG data that can be reliably and routinely obtained in any primary care setting. Taking advantage of clinical data that is either pre-existing or being obtained in alignment with ongoing chronic disease management and preventive care is remarkably novel. The electronic health record (EHR) contains vast amounts of vital sign, laboratory, radiographic, and ECG information. But, CLD, and specifically metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), goes underdiagnosed despite the availability of clinical signals within the EHR (5). This study demonstrates how an ML model can re-purpose clinical data to detect CLD with advanced fibrosis that might otherwise have gone undetected.

However, there are barriers to implementing this remarkable ECG-ML technology in primary care. First, the use of screening ECG in primary care is highly variable and has not been extensively studied. A study from 2001 demonstrated that ECGs were ordered in 4.4% of primary care visits for patients without symptoms, and performance was highly variable between physician practices (6). In 2018, the United States Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) issued a recommendation against screening for cardiovascular disease (CVD) with ECG for patients at low-risk for CVD and found insufficient evidence for ECG screening in patients at medium or high-risk for CVD (7). These recommendations have likely resulted in fewer screening and routine ECGs being performed in primary care clinics, which could limit the deployment of the ECG-ML tool for advanced CLD detection.

Outside of the USPSTF recommendations on routine CVD screening, ECG remains an integral part of primary care delivery for the evaluation and management of acute conditions including chest pain and tachycardia (8). If the ECG-ML CLD detection tool were applied to ECGs obtained for these indications, the clinician may find it challenging to navigate from the work-up of a cardiac condition to the pursuit the CLD diagnosis. In their study, Simonetto et al. described clinician adherence to the ECG-ML guidance as “moderate”, with 292 (21.1%) patients receiving an order for a sequential liver fibrosis assessment out of the 1,385 patients that were ECG-ML positive in the intervention group. In post-test surveys, clinicians cited clinical judgment and patient preference as reasons for not pursuing CLD diagnosis after the ECG-ML signal, which may represent this conflict of care priorities for patients receiving ECGs in primary care (4). The upcoming qualitative study on this work will be enlightening.

Limited primary care clinician comfort with CLD non-invasive liver disease assessment (NILDA) tests may also play a significant role in the underdiagnosis of CLD with advanced fibrosis in primary care and mitigate uptake of the ECG-ML recommendations in Simonetto et al.’s study (4). Most extensively studied in MASLD, primary care clinician knowledge and use of fibrosis risk assessment tools like the fibrosis-4 index (FIB-4) and vibration-controlled elastography are limited (9-11). FIB-4 is now the first step for advanced fibrosis risk assessment in MASLD and diabetes care algorithms, but a survey of primary care clinicians revealed that only 25% knew the inputs for FIB-4 score calculation, and only 15% had used FIB-4 in practice (9,12,13). Efforts to align primary care clinicians, hepatologists, endocrinologists, nutritionists, and health systems to improve MASLD and CLD care are underway and feature enhanced CLD education and increased access to diagnostic tools and technology (14).

Lastly, time pressures threaten the adoption of new technologies for pursuing new diagnoses of CLD in primary care. The mean time required for a primary care clinician to provide guideline recommended acute, chronic disease, and preventive care to an average primary care patient panel is estimated to be 26.7 hours per day (15). With this already immense workload, implementing new tools to improve CLD diagnosis in primary care will need primary care input, alignment with ongoing care strategies, and minimal interruption of primary care workflow. For the most part, the ECG-ML model in Simonetto et al.’s work addresses these issues. By making use of ECG data to prompt additional CLD diagnostic testing that is tailored to the setting (Fibrotest was chosen due to availability at all sites, while NILDA imaging was not available to the rural clinics), this study details an innovative application of ML embedded within the primary care context to improve advanced CLD diagnosis. While there is more work to be done, the ECG-ML model is a demonstration of significant progress towards addressing the underdiagnosis of CLD.
