Adverse childhood experiences and risk of living with HIV among lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer/questioning (LGBTQ+) people: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jingli Yang, Lei Liu, Junren Wang, Yueyao Xu, Peipei Zhao, Yumeng Wang, Peipei Du, Joseph D. Tucker, Stefan Baral, Elvin H. Geng, Xiaodong Wang, Huan Song, Dan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAdverse childhood experiences and HIV represent interconnected public health issues. However, studies characterizing the prevalence and associations remain limited among sexual and gender minorities (i.e., lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer/questioning individuals, collectively referred to as LGBTQ+), especially in China. This study aimed to examine adverse childhood experiences patterns, association with risk of living with HIV, and potential mediating factors among LGBTQ+ individuals in China.MethodsAn online survey was provided to LGBTQ+ people and the eligibility criteria were aged 16 or older and self-identified as LGBTQ+. Participants were enrolled in Chengdu, China, between August 2023 and March 2025. Adverse childhood experiences were assessed using a validated 10-item scale. Living with HIV was based on self-report. Cluster analysis based on the k-modes algorithm was used to identify distinct patterns of adverse childhood experiences. Unconditional logistic regression models were employed to explore the associations between adverse childhood experiences and living with HIV. Mediation analyses were conducted to quantify the mediating effects of modifiable psychosocial factors on the observed associations. Sex assigned at birth, age, ethnicity, number of siblings, and gender identity were adjusted in the above analyses.FindingsA total of 10,305 LGBTQ+ individuals were enrolled and 8384 met eligibility criteria. The median age of the participants was 26 years (interquartile range, 23–31 years). Overall, 63.5% (5325/8384) of participants reported at least one adverse childhood experience. Among specific adverse childhood experiences, physical abuse was most prevalent (34.4%, 2884/8384), closely followed by emotional abuse (34.1%, 2858/8384). Exposure to any adverse childhood experiences was significantly associated with higher risks of living with HIV (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.14–1.66). Subgroup analyses revealed that exposure to any ACE was significantly associated with living with HIV risk among male (1.34, 1.11–1.61), cisgender gay man (1.38, 1.08–1.76) and queer/questioning + individuals (1.57, 1.06–2.33). Mediation analysis revealed that substance use and mental health problems were significant mediators of the association between specific ACEs and living with HIV.InterpretationAdverse childhood experiences were associated with higher burden of living with HIV among LGBTQ+ people in China. These findings highlight the importance of integrating trauma-informed, identity-tailored sexual-health services into LGBTQ+ -competent care pathways.FundingNational Natural Science Foundation of China; Natural Science Foundation of Sichuan; West China Hospital, Sichuan University; Jiangsu Provincial Professorship Career Development Grant; Nanjing Medical University Career Development Grant.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed up to October 3, 2025, with no language restrictions, using the search terms: (“HIV”[Title/Abstract] OR “sexually transmitted diseases”[Title/Abstract] OR “sexually transmitted infection”[Title/Abstract] OR “STI”[Title/Abstract] OR “STD”[Title/Abstract]) AND (“adverse childhood experiences”[Title/Abstract] OR “childhood adversity”[Title/Abstract] OR “early life adversity”[Title/Abstract] OR “childhood maltreatment”[Title/Abstract]) AND (“Sexual and Gender Minorities”[Title/Abstract] OR “Sexual Minorities”[Title/Abstract] OR “LGBTQ”[Title/Abstract] OR “lesbian”[Title/Abstract] OR “gay”[Title/Abstract] OR “bisexual”[Title/Abstract] OR “transgender”[Title/Abstract] OR “queer”[Title/Abstract] OR “questioning”[Title/Abstract]). A total of 20 records were identified. After excluding eight unrelated studies, the remaining 12 included four studies on the prevalence of ACEs among people living with HIV, seven on the association between ACEs exposure and HIV-related risk behaviors, and one on physical abuse and HIV infection among young men who have sex with men. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have comprehensively assessed ACEs exposure and their associations with HIV across the full spectrum of LGBTQ+ identities.

Added value of this study

Using data from TREASURE cohort—China’s largest ongoing prospective cohort focused on LGBTQ+ adversity and health—comprising 8384 LGBTQ+ individuals including closeted groups, we found that ACEs exposure was common among LGBTQ+ people. Notably, 80% of ACEs occurred in clusters, revealing four distinct patterns. ACEs exposure was significantly associated with risk of living with HIV, especially in individuals assigned male at birth and those identifying as cisgender gay man and queer/questioning + individuals. Furthermore, substance use and mental health problems may mediate this observed association, addressing a critical evidence gap in low- and middle-income countries.

Implications of all the available evidence

These findings underscore the complex interplay between early-life adversities and subsequent HIV among LGBTQ+ individuals. Taken together, the results highlight the imperative for trauma-informed sexual health programs that integrate mental health support with substance use reduction to mitigate the long-term sexual health consequences of ACEs in LGBTQ+ populations.

Introduction

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) represent severe and chronic stressors affecting children during development, including emotional, physical, and sexual abuse, along with exposure to dysfunctional family environments, such as household violence, substance abuse, or parental incarceration.1,2 A meta-analysis of 206 studies involving 546,458 adult participants from 22 countries found that six out of ten adults reported experiencing at least one ACE.3 Sexual and gender minorities (i.e., lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, and/or queer/questioning individuals, collectively referred to as LGBTQ+) populations are disproportionally more affected by ACEs because of the unique vulnerabilities associated with their gender identities and sexual orientations.3 But research focused on ACEs among LGBTQ+ remained limited, particularly in China.3 This knowledge gap is particularly significant given the unique cultural, societal, and legal challenges faced by the LGBTQ+ community in China, including the lack of legal recognition and widespread stigma, which may shape distinct ACEs experiences.4 Further, given the considerable diversity within LGBTQ+ communities, an inclusive LGBTQ+ sample is essential to assess variations in ACEs prevalence and its impacts across different sexual and gender minority subgroups.

Research demonstrates that ACEs exposure has cumulative, lifelong effects across multiple health domains.5,6 These included behavioral risks (e.g., substance use and smoking),5 psychological conditions (e.g., depression),7 physical diseases (e.g., cardiovascular disease),8 and increased susceptibility to sexually transmitted infections (STIs) including human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infections and other STIs.6 The mechanisms connecting ACEs exposure to somatic disease susceptibility remain incompletely understood, but emerging evidence suggests that ACEs may contribute to immune system dysregulation.9,10 This aligns with observed elevated risks of infectious diseases (e.g., HIV and COVID-19),11,12 and inflammatory (e.g., hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis mediated endocrines disorders)2 among ACEs-exposed individuals. However, few studies have examined the association between ACEs and HIV among LGBTQ+ people. We selected HIV as an outcome measurement for assessing somatic diseases because these infections constitute a global public-health issue that disproportionately affect young adults and LGBTQ+ communities.13, 14, 15, 16 According to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) 2021 STI guidelines, gay men face a lifetime HIV risk of roughly 17%—orders of magnitude higher than the 0.4% risk for heterosexual women and 0.2% for heterosexual men.17 Despite this disproportionate burden, limited research has examined ACEs-HIV associations specifically within diverse LGBTQ+ subgroups in the Chinese context. This study addresses this critical gap by providing unique evidence on a severely understudied population and establishing essential groundwork for future investigations employing biomarker-confirmed HIV.

These well-documented consequences of ACEs align with established risk factors for STIs. A growing body of evidence has consistently identified behavioral factors (e.g., substance use and alcohol consumption), low socioeconomic status (e.g., low income), and psychological conditions (e.g., depression) as significant contributors to HIV risk.18, 19, 20 Given that ACEs are known to increase the prevalence of these factors, they may represent key intermediate pathways through which early-life adversity exerts its long-term effects on sexual health. Therefore, this study selected these modifiable psychosocial factors as potential mediators to investigate whether they explain, in part, the observed association between ACEs and HIV risk among LGBTQ+ populations. Clarifying these associations will advance our understanding of the psychosocial determinants of ACEs-related HIV acquisition and inform targeted, earlier prevention strategies across the lifespan.

This study aimed to investigate the prevalence and patterns of ACEs across LGBTQ+ subgroups and their association with people living with HIV risk. We also assessed modifiable factors mediating this association to inform targets for future interventions.

Methods

Data sources and study design

This study was based on TREASURE cohort, an ongoing Transformative Research on Equity And Social Determinants to Uplift Resilience and Empower LGBTQ+ Health in China (https://biomedbdc.wchscu.cn/JoylabErasePM/hx-portals/#/queue/1829042032129302530/index). From August 27, 2023, this longitudinal cohort study initiated its baseline survey among LGBTQ+ people in Chengdu, Sichuan Province. The study was a joint effort by the West China Hospital of Sichuan University, the School of Public Health of Nanjing Medical University, and a community-based organization (CBO) providing HIV testing services to LGBTQ+ people in Chengdu and nearby areas. The CBO operates four HIV testing clinics, reaching over 20,000 LGBTQ+ individuals annually. Individuals were eligible if they were aged 16 or older, self-identified as LGBTQ+ (see definitions of LGBTQ+ in Supplementary Methods), and were current residents of Chengdu or their primary LGBTQ+ social activities occurred outside the city.

We employed convenience sampling methods leveraging the established professional networks of our community organization. Specific sampling methods included clinic-based on-site recruitment, targeted outreach at community events, personal referrals from social network, and online advertising via Chengdu Tongle’s official WeChat account. After obtaining informed consent, participants accessed the online questionnaire via a WeChat-based distribution link, which directed them to the survey hosted on the data collection platform. Each WeChat account can only be registered once to avoid duplicate enrollment. This comprehensive survey encompassed 14 distinct scales, addressing a wide range of domains, including social determinants, psychological and physical assessments, traumatic experiences, and other pertinent factors. To ensure the robustness and reliability of the collected data, we used several quality controls measures, including pre-survey pilot test, attention checks, internal logical consistency checks, and a minimum response time constraint (Supplementary Fig. S1). We sent each participant a personalized psychological assessment report and offered a monetary incentive of 16 RMB (i.e., 2.3 USD). This study followed the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement.

Ethics approval

All participants completed informed consent forms before enrollment. This study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, in accordance with federal regulations governing research involving human subjects (approval number 2023-509).

Definitions of living with HIV

Diagnoses of people living with HIV was based on self-report. We relied on self-reported diagnoses rather than local health records because local residents who accessed HIV testing or treatment services outside of the municipality would not be captured by local health records. To validate self-reported data, we cross-referenced questionnaire responses with laboratory-confirmed results from provincial CDC and Voluntary Counseling and Testing clinical databases using phone number and sex assigned at birth, and only participants with exact matches on both variables were included in the validation dataset. The self-reported HIV status demonstrated high concordance with health record results, with a true-positive rate of 84.1%, a true-negative rate of 99.6%, and an overall accuracy of 83.7% (Supplementary Table S1).

Assessment of adverse childhood experiences

ACEs exposure was assessed using the 10-item scale from the CDC-Kaiser study, which has been validated for use in adult Chinese populations.21 This instrument dichotomizes (yes/no) exposure before age 18 to ten specific adversities, including emotional, physical and sexual abuse; emotional and physical neglect; and household challenges such as violence, substance abuse, mental illness, parental separation/divorce, and incarceration of a household member (Supplementary Table S2). A total ACEs score was calculated for each participant by summing all 'yes' responses, resulting in a range from 0 to 10. A higher score indicated greater exposure to ACEs. The presence of any ACEs was defined as endorsing at least one type, and we further categorized ACEs into four levels (0, 1–2, 3–4, ≥5) based on the quartile distribution of the sample to create balanced categories with sufficient sample sizes.22

Covariates and potential mediators

Covariates and potential mediators were identified from the literature18, 19, 20 and incorporated into a directed acyclic graph that was used to guide the modeling strategy (Supplementary Fig. S2). Covariates including the sex assigned at birth (male, female, or other [i.e., Intersex]), age (continuous), ethnicity (Han Chinese or other ethnicities [i.e., Tibetan, Yi, Qiang, Miao, Tujia, Hui, and other]), number of siblings (0, 1, or ≥2), and gender identity (cisgender, transgender, bigender, or others [i.e., Queer, Agender, Pangender, and other]) were collected through structured questions administered during the baseline survey.

Potential mediators included behavioral factors (e.g., smoking status, alcohol consumption, and substance use), socioeconomic status (e.g., education level and monthly income level), and psychological factors (e.g., mental health problems, depressive symptoms, and probable post-traumatic stress disorder [PTSD]) based on prior knowledge supported by published literature.23, 24, 25, 26 These mediators were assessed using structured questions or validated scales administered during the baseline survey. Specifically, participants were classified as smokers if they identified as either current or former smokers. Alcohol consumption was defined as self-reported drinking at least once a month. Substance use was defined as the use of illicit drugs within the past 12 months, including narcotics, hallucinogenics, stimulants, or substances used to enhance sexual performance (i.e., aphrodisiacs). Mental health problems were defined as a self-reported history of emotional or psychological distress that necessitated seeking professional help or informal support (e.g., from friends or family members). Depressive symptoms were defined as a score ≥10 on the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) scale,27 and the Chinese version of this scale has been shown to have good reliability and validity.28 Probable PTSD were defined as a score ≥33 on the PTSD Checklist for the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5), Fifth Edition (PCL-5),29 which has shown a classification accuracy of 95.7% when compared with clinician-diagnosed PTSD in Chinese populations.30

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive statistics to summarize the distribution of baseline characteristics, both overall and by number of ACEs co-occurrences. We reported frequencies and percentages of ACEs stratified by five LGBTQ+ subgroups, sex assigned at birth, gender identity, and sexual orientation. To visualize the co-occurrence of multiple ACEs, we used UpSet plots.31 Given the high prevalence of co-occurring ACEs suggested by these visualisations, we used cluster analysis (k-modes algorithm) to identify distinct patterns of ACEs exposures among participants.32 The optimal number of clusters was determined using the elbow method, selecting the point where within-cluster heterogeneity reduction levels off.

We used unconditional logistic regression to assess associations between ACEs exposure and the risk of living with HIV, estimating odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We conducted these association analyses in the overall population and stratified by both LGBTQ+ subgroups and sex assigned at birth. Additionally, we tested the robustness of our findings to the categorization of ACEs by repeating the main analyses in the overall population using the conventional five-level categorization (0, 1, 2, 3, ≥4) as the exposure in the sensitivity analyses.

To elucidate potential mediators underlying the associations between ACEs and living with HIV, we conducted mediation analyses using simple mediation models, with each factor related to behavior factors, socioeconomic status, and psychological factors examined separately. This approach avoids potential multicollinearity among mediators and allows for the estimation of each factor's unique indirect effect. Nonparametric bootstraps were used for confidence intervals, and the number of Monte Carlo draws was 1000. To examine whether distinct types of ACEs operate through different mediating factors, we conducted mediation analyses using individual ACE items that showed a statistically significant association with the risk of living with HIV. All models were adjusted for sex assigned at birth, age, ethnicity, number of siblings, and gender identity. Cases with missing data for key independent and dependent variables were deemed ineligible and excluded from this analysis. Furthermore, to ensure adequate statistical power for the primary analyses, we calculated the sample size using WebPower.33

A two-sided P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data analyses were performed using R software, version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The R packages used in this study mainly include ‘gtsummary’, ‘ggplot2’, ‘pheatmap’, ‘UpSetR’, ‘factoextra’, ‘radarchart’, ‘forestploter’, ‘rms’, and ‘mediation’.

The funders had no role in the study design, data collection, data analysis, interpretation, writing of the manuscript.

Results

In this study, we included all participants recruited into the TREASURE cohort as of March 24, 2025 (N = 10,305). A total of 1391 participants were excluded during the participant recruitment phase for the following reasons: 494 did not respond to the online questionnaire, 23 were under 16 years, 533 identified as cisgender heterosexual, and 393 resided outside Chengdu with their primary LGBTQ+ social activities occurring elsewhere. Additionally, 530 participants were excluded during data quality checks due to incomplete data (n = 272) or failure to pass attention checks or provide invalid data (n = 258). Finally, 8384 participants were eligible for analysis (Supplementary Fig. S3).

The mean age of the 8384 participants was 26 years (interquartile range or IQR: 23–31). Sex assigned at birth were female in 1627 (19.4%), male in 6727 (80.2%), and another in 30 (0.4%). Gender identity was cisgender in 5947 (70.9%), transgender in 1238 (14.8%), bigender in 855 (10.2%), and another in 344 (4.1%). Overall, 7205 (85.9%) sexual orientation reported homosexual orientation, 52 (0.6%) heterosexual, 910 (10.9%) bisexual, and 217 (2.6%) another. Associations of ACEs and living with HIV

Association analyses revealed that exposure to any ACE was significantly associated with increased risk of living with HIV, regardless of adjustment for confounding factors (adjusted OR [aOR] = 1.38, 95% CI = 1.14–1.66; Table 2 and Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). Subgroup analyses revealed that, when stratified by sex assigned at birth, exposure to any ACE was significantly associated with living with HIV risk among male (1.34, 1.11–1.61); when stratified by LGBTQ+ subgroup, exposure to any ACE was significantly associated with living with HIV risk among cisgender gay man (1.38, 1.08–1.76) and queer/questioning + individuals (1.57, 1.06–2.33; Table 2). The number of people living with HIV among female participants (n = 11), particularly cisgender lesbian women (n = 9), was too small to calculate the aOR. Sensitivity analyses using the conventional five-level ACEs categorization yielded largely consistent results (Supplementary Table S5).

Mediating effects of modifiable factors

Mediation analysis revealed that substance use and mental health problems were significant mediators of the association between specific ACEs and living with HIV (Fig. 4). Specifically, substance use mediated 48.2% of the association for physical abuse, 47.1% for sexual abuse, 30.5% for household violence, and 23.3% for parental separation or divorce. Discussion

These data suggest that ACEs are highly prevalent among LGBTQ+ people in Chengdu, China. Transgender people were the most affected subgroup (74.8%). Notably, 80% of ACEs co-occurred in clusters, revealing four distinct patterns. ACEs exposure were associated with the risk of living with HIV, especially in individuals assigned male at birth and those identifying as cisgender gay man and queer/questioning + individuals. Furthermore, mediation analyses indicated that mental health problems and substance use partially mediated the associations between ACEs and living with HIV, highlighting potential targets for future interventions.

Our findings reveal a substantial burden of ACEs among LGBTQ+ individuals in Chengdu, China, with 64% of participants reporting at least one ACE and 80% reporting co-occurring exposures. This prevalence is substantially higher than the 41% reported among heterosexual adults.34 Physical and emotional abuse were particularly prevalent (about 34% in our study).35,36 Transgender people were disproportionately more affected than other subgroups. These disparities likely reflect the pervasive effects of stigma, discrimination, and rigid gender norms in society, which increase vulnerability to childhood adversity—particularly among transgender youth whose expression may challenge normative expectations.36, 37, 38, 39 Our analysis also identified four distinct patterns of ACEs exposure, indicating that ACEs are often clustered and intersected with other social identities.40 These results underscore the urgent need for targeted interventions that address the unique vulnerabilities and compounded trauma faced by LGBTQ+ community, through the creation of supportive environments and evidence-based resources aimed at mitigating long-term health consequences.

We found that ACEs exposure was associated with an increased risk of living with HIV, highlighting the pervasive impact of early-life adversity on vulnerability to HIV. This aligns with the minority stress framework, which posits that chronic exposure to stigma and discrimination can contribute to health disparities, including susceptibility to HIV.41 Subgroup analyses revealed that the association between ACEs and living with HIV was observed across specific sub-populations, including males, cisgender gay man and queer/questioning + individuals, underscoring that the impact of ACEs extends beyond conventional high-risk groups and suggesting that interventions targeting early-life trauma may have broad-reaching benefits. The very low proportion of female participants living with HIV (11/1627, 0.68%), especially cisgender lesbian women (9/1231, 0.73%), precluded reliable analysis of the association between ACEs and living with HIV in this subgroup. This finding is consistent with the well-documented rarity of female-to-female HIV transmission.42 Therefore, the HIV-related findings should not be generalised to cisgender lesbian women. Notably, exposure to household violence conferred a risk comparable to physical abuse. This may be explained, in part, by the frequent co-occurrence of violence against mothers and children, which has been shown to intensify harm and compound developmental risk.43,44

Mental health problems and substance use partially mediated the association between ACEs and living with HIV risk. This pathway aligns with the psychological mediation framework, which posits that minority stress resulting from stigma and discrimination contributes to elevated psychopathology among LGBTQ+ community.45 Empirical evidence suggests that psychological distress is associated with sexual decision-making processes that may increase living with HIV risk, such as a reduced likelihood of condom use.46,47 Furthermore, substance use exhibited the stronger mediating effect. Substance use is more strongly and directly linked to sexual risk-taking, prolonged sexual encounters, and increased partner exchange compared to alcohol use.48 These findings underscore that targeted, trauma-informed substance use interventions focusing on substances prevalent in local LGBTQ+ communities may be particularly effective in disrupting the pathway from ACEs to sexual health disparities. Notably, socioeconomic status did not emerge as a significant mediator in the pathway from ACEs to living with HIV risk. This may be attributable to limited socioeconomic heterogeneity in our sample—specifically, a predominance of highly educated participants with constrained income variability—which likely reduced statistical power to detect mediating effects of socioeconomic factors. Additionally, as our findings suggest, psychological and behavioral factors may serve as more proximal mediators linking ACEs to living with HIV, potentially overshadowing the indirect effects of socioeconomic pathways. Future research with more socioeconomically diverse samples is warranted to further elucidate the potential mediating role of socioeconomic status.

Taken together, these findings provide empirical support for a syndemic pathway in which co-occurring epidemics of ACEs, mental health burden, and behavioral factors synergistically increase vulnerability to HIV. Therefore, trauma-informed sexual health programs tailored to LGBTQ+ populations in China may help mitigate the long-term sexual health consequences of ACEs. These programs should address both the psychological effects of early-life adversity and the behavioral pathways that increase sexual health risk.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of our study was comprehensive assessment of ACEs and their associations with living with HIV across the full spectrum of LGBTQ+ identities, based on a substantial sample from the LGBTQ+ people. This sample (N = 8384) substantially exceeds the minimum requirement of 4819 (Supplementary Table S6), providing sufficient statistical power and enabling detailed subgroup analyses by sex and LGBTQ+ status. This deliberate inclusion of multiple LGBTQ+ sub-groups enabled direct, intersectional comparisons of ACE–HIV associations that have rarely been possible in previous samples. Additionally, we explored the potential modifiable factors on the association between ACEs and living with HIV risk among LGBTQ+ population, revealing actionable targets for culturally tailored prevention strategies within LGBTQ+ communities.

However, there were several limitations. First, the baseline survey was a cross-sectional design that limits our understanding of causality. The temporal sequence of our findings—wherein ACEs precede the later development of living with HIV in adulthood—endorses the plausibility of a temporal association. Second, living with HIV were based on self-report data, which may have resulted in underestimation of true prevalence. This analysis revealed high concordance between self-reported and clinically confirmed HIV status, suggesting that participants in this sample reported their HIV status with a relatively high degree of accuracy. Third, the use of a convenience sampling method may introduce selection bias. Participants were recruited from a single city, potentially resulting in a cohort that is not fully representative of the broader LGBTQ+ community in China. Consequently, the generalisability of our findings may be limited.

Conclusions

In conclusion, ACEs were highly prevalent and associated with higher risks of living with HIV among LGBTQ+ people in Chengdu, China. Our findings underscore the imperative for trauma-informed sexual health programs that integrate mental health support with substance use reduction to mitigate the long-term sexual health consequences of ACEs in LGBTQ+ populations.
