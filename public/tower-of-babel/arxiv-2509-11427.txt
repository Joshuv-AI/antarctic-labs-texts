Abstract
. The lattice Boltzmann method has become a widely adopted approach in computational fluid dynamics, offering unique advantages in mesoscopic kinetic modeling, intrinsic parallelism, and simple treatment of boundary conditions. However, its conventional reliance on Cartesian grids fundamentally limits geometric fidelity in flows involving curved boundaries, introducing stair-step artifacts that propagate as spurious forces and boundary-layer inaccuracies. To address these challenges, we propose the isogeometric lattice Boltzmann method, which seamlessly integrates Isogeometric Analysis with LBM, leveraging the geometric precision of non-uniform rational B-Splines to construct body-fitted computational grids. Unlike conventional Cartesian-based LBM, the proposed approach eliminates stair-step boundary artifacts by providing sub-element geometric accuracy while maintaining the efficiency of LBM. Furthermore, the higher-order continuity of NURBS improves gradient resolution, reducing numerical diffusion in high-Reynold’s-number flows. The parametric grid adaptation of IGA enables h h -, p p -, and k k -refinement strategies, allowing for localized resolution enhancement in boundary layers and regions with high solution gradients. Additionally, the diffeomorphic mapping properties of IGA ensure intrinsic conservation, preserving advection invariants and suppressing numerical oscillations, leading to enhanced stability. Benchmark simulations on flows with curved and complex geometries demonstrate that IGA-LBM delivers significantly more accurate boundary-layer predictions and pressure/force estimates than standard Cartesian LBM, while preserving its computational efficiency and scalability. By combining geometric exactness with the algorithmic simplicity of LBM, IGA-LBM offers a practical route to high-fidelity simulations in engineering and scientific applications.



1.
Introduction

The lattice Boltzmann method (LBM) has emerged as a powerful mesoscopic computational framework for simulating fluid flows, offering inherent advantages in handling complex boundary conditions, multiphase interactions, and highly transient phenomena
(
Chen and Doolen 1998
)
. Rooted in the kinetic theory of gases, LBM discretizes the Boltzmann transport equation to model fluid dynamics through the evolution of particle distribution functions on a discrete lattice. This approach bypasses the direct solution of the Navier-Stokes equations, enabling efficient parallelization and adaptability to irregular geometries
(
Succi 2001
;
Krüger et al. 2017
)
. Over the past three decades, LBM has found widespread applications into diverse fields, including porous media flows
(
Guo and Zhao 2002
)
, microfluidics
(
Zhang 2011
)
, and biomedical engineering
(
Liu 2012
)
.

Despite its computational merits, traditional Cartesian-grid-based LBM faces significant challenges in accurately resolving flows over curved or intricate geometries. The stair-step approximation of boundaries introduces geometric errors that propagate as spurious forces and degrade boundary layer predictions, as shown in Fig.
1
. Although interpolation-based boundary treatments
(
Bouzidi et al. 2001
)
, cut-cell methods
(
Fei and Luo 2017
)
, and immersed boundary techniques
(
Feng and Michaelides 2004
)
have been proposed to mitigate these issues, they often compromise numerical stability, introduce interpolation artifacts, or incur prohibitive computational costs. These limitations motivate a geometrically faithful yet computationally efficient framework that preserves the simplicity of LBM and removes geometric discretization errors.

Figure 1
.
Staircase representation of a curved airfoil on a Cartesian lattice.
Sampling the smooth boundary on a uniform grid produces jagged, cell-aligned facets that
distort wall normals and the effective body shape, leading to spurious forces and near-wall
velocity/pressure oscillations. This artifact motivates the present IGA-LBM formulation,
which uses CAD-exact NURBS geometry to eliminate stair-step errors via body-fitted parameterization.

Isogeometric Analysis (IGA), pioneered by Hughes et al.
(
Hughes et al. 2005
;
Cottrell et al. 2009
)
, bridges the gap between Computer-Aided Design (CAD) and numerical simulation by employing B-splines and Non-Uniform Rational B-Splines (NURBS) for both geometric representation and solution approximation. Unlike conventional low-order finite element or finite volume discretizations, IGA inherits the exact geometry from CAD systems and provides higher-order continuity, enabling superior accuracy and stability in fluid-structure interaction
(
Bazilevs et al. 2008
)
, aerodynamics
(
Hsu et al. 2011
;
Bazilevs et al. 2023
)
, and boundary-layer flow simulations
(
Zhu et al. 2020
)
. This paradigm shift motivates the integration of IGA with LBM, a synergy we term isogeometric lattice Boltzmann method (IGA-LBM), to combine the geometric fidelity of spline-based parameterizations with the computational efficiency of LBM.

The primary advantages of the proposed IGA-LBM framework are fourfold:

•

Geometric exactness through NURBS parameterization: By inheriting CAD-compatible NURBS basis functions, the method eliminates staircase approximations of curved surfaces, achieving sub-element geometric precision critical for boundary layer and aerodynamic simulations;

•

Enhanced gradient resolution: The intrinsic
C^{k}
-continuity (
k\geq 1
) of B-spline spaces suppresses numerical diffusion artifacts, particularly beneficial for high-Reynold’s number flows and vorticity-dominated regions where traditional LBM suffers from excessive dissipation;

•

Natural refinement ability: Unified
h
-(element splitting),
p
-(order elevation), and
k
-(continuity preservation) refinement strategies enable resolution enhancement near critical features (e.g., boundary layers, shock discontinuities) while maintaining spline space coherence—a capability absent in conventional Cartesian LBM.

•

Intrinsic conservation properties: The diffeomorphic nature of isogeometric mappings inherently preserves discrete advection invariants, ensuring exact geometric mass conservation and reducing spurious oscillations.

By leveraging these features, IGA-LBM achieves high-fidelity simulations of flows over complex geometries while maintaining the explicit, parallelizable advantange of LBM.

Related work and challenges

Over the past few years, significant efforts have been made to extend LBM to complex geometries while maintaining its computational efficiency. A major challenge in traditional Cartesian-grid-based LBM is the poor representation of curved and irregular boundaries, which introduces stair-step approximations, leading to spurious forces and numerical errors in boundary-layer flows
(
Chekhlov et al. 2023
)
. Several techniques have been proposed to address these limitations. One common approach is the use of curvilinear coordinate transformations, where the governing equations of LBM are reformulated in a transformed coordinate system
(
He and Doolen 1997
;
Mei and Shyy 1998
;
Hejranfar and Ezzatneshan 2014
;
Velasco et al. 2019
)
. While this method improves boundary conformity, it can lead to grid distortion and anisotropy, which negatively impact numerical stability. Another widely used method is the immersed boundary approach
(
Feng and Michaelides 2004
;
Niu et al. 2006
)
, which embeds complex geometries into a Cartesian background grid. Although immersed boundary methods allow flexible handling of moving boundaries, they introduce interpolation errors and artificial forcing terms, which degrade accuracy in high-fidelity simulations
(
Wu and Shu 2009
;
Wu and Shu 2010
)
.

2.
Lattice Boltzmann method

The lattice Boltzmann method (LBM) is a mesoscopic numerical approach derived from the Boltzmann transport equation, offering unique advantages in simulating fluid dynamics with complex boundaries and multiphase interactions
(
Krüger et al. 2017
)
. Unlike traditional Navier-Stokes solvers, LBM operates at the particle distribution level, enabling efficient parallel computation.

Governing equations

The evolution of the particle distribution function
f_{\alpha}(\vec{x},t)
is governed by the Boltzmann equation:

(1)

\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}+\vec{e}_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla f_{\alpha}=\Omega_{\alpha}(f),

where
\vec{x}
and
t
denote spatial and temporal coordinates,
\vec{e}_{\alpha}
is the discrete velocity vector in the
\alpha
-th direction, and
\Omega_{\alpha}(f)
represents the collision operator. This operator drives the system towards thermodynamic equilibrium through particle interactions.

BGK approximation

While there are many different collision operators
\Omega_{\alpha}(f)
available, a common simplification of the collision operator is the Bhatnagar-Gross-Krook (BGK) approximation, which assumes a linear relaxation towards the equilibrium distribution
f^{\mathrm{eq}}
:

(2)

\Omega_{\alpha}(f)=-\frac{f_{\alpha}-f_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{eq}}}{\tau}\Delta t,

where
\tau
is the relaxation time, and
f_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{eq}}
is the equilibrium distribution. In this work, a Maxwell distribution up to second-order terms is used as equilibrium distribution function, i.e., the equilibrium distribution function
f_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{eq}}
is typically expressed as:

(3)

f_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\vec{x},t)=\omega_{\alpha}\rho(\vec{x},t)\left(1+\frac{\vec{e}_{\alpha}\cdot\vec{u}}{c_{s}^{2}}+\frac{(\vec{e}_{\alpha}\cdot\vec{u})^{2}}{2c_{s}^{4}}-\frac{\vec{u}\cdot\vec{u}}{2c_{s}^{2}}\right),

where
\rho(\vec{x},t)
is the macroscopic density,
\vec{u}
is the macroscopic velocity,
\vec{e}_{\alpha}
are the lattice velocity vectors,
\omega_{\alpha}
are the lattice weights, and
c_{s}
is the speed of sound, which is calculated as
c_{s}=c/\sqrt{3}
.

Discretization

In the classical LBM framework, the spatial domain is discretized into a regular lattice, and the velocity space is reduced to a finite set of discrete directions
\vec{e}_{\alpha}
. The discretized Boltzmann equation governs the evolution of particle distribution function:

(4)

f_{\alpha}\left(\vec{x}+\vec{e}_{\alpha}\Delta t,t+\Delta t\right)=f_{\alpha}\left(\vec{x},t\right)-\frac{\Delta t}{\tau}\left(f_{\alpha}\left(\vec{x},t\right)-f_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\vec{x},t)\right),

where
\Delta t
is the time step, and
\tau
is the relaxation time for viscous effects.

For example, the widely adopted D2Q9 model (Fig.
2
) defines nine discrete velocities in 2D space:

(5)

\vec{e}_{\alpha}=\begin{cases}(0,0)c,&\alpha=0\\
(\pm 1,0)c,(0,\pm 1)c,&\alpha=1,2,3,4\\
(\pm 1,\pm 1)c,&\alpha=5,6,7,8\end{cases}\quad(c=1),

where
c=\delta x/\delta t
is the lattice speed with
\delta x
and
\delta t
as lattice spacing and time step. The associated weights
w_{\alpha}
(
w_{0}=4/9
,
w_{1,2,3,4}=1/9
,
w_{5,6,7,8}=1/36
) ensure rotational invariance and recover Navier-Stokes hydrodynamics via Chapman-Enskog expansion.

.
D2Q9 lattice: discrete velocity set
\{\mathbf{e}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=0}^{8}
.
Cardinal directions (
\alpha=1\!:\!4
) have magnitude
c
; diagonals (
\alpha=5\!:\!8
) have magnitude
\sqrt{2}\,c
.
Weights are
w_{0}=4/9
,
w_{1,2,3,4}=1/9
, and
w_{5,6,7,8}=1/36
.

Collision-Streaming algorithm

The standard LBM algorithm iterates between two phases:

•

Collision. The collision step updates the particle distribution function at each lattice node, driving it towards equilibrium:

(6)

f_{\alpha}^{*}(\vec{x},t)=f_{\alpha}(\vec{x},t)-\frac{\Delta t}{\tau}\left(f_{\alpha}(\vec{x},t)-f_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\vec{x},t)\right),

where
f_{\alpha}^{*}(\vec{x},t)
is the distribution function after collisions, and
f_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\vec{x},t)
is the equilibrium distribution function found from (
3
).

•

Streaming. The streaming step propagates the post-collision distribution functions to neighboring lattice nodes along the discrete velocity directions
\vec{e}_{\alpha}
:

(7)

f_{\alpha}(\vec{x}+\vec{e}_{\alpha}\Delta t,t+\Delta t)=f_{\alpha}^{*}(\vec{x},t).

Macroscopic quantities

The macroscopic fluid properties, such as density
\rho
, velocity
\mathbf{u}
, and pressure
p
are recovered through moments of the particle distribution function:

(8)

\rho=\sum_{\alpha}f_{\alpha},\quad\vec{u}=\frac{1}{\rho}\sum_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}\vec{e}_{\alpha},\quad p=\rho c_{s}^{2}.

The kinematic shear viscosity
\nu
is linked to the relaxation time
\tau
via Chapman-Enskog analysis:

(9)

\nu=c_{s}^{2}\left(\tau-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\right).

To enforce a target viscosity
\nu
, therefore, the relaxation time is set as

(10)

\tau=\frac{\nu}{c_{s}^{2}}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}.

3.
Isogeometric analysis

Isogeometric Analysis (IGA) bridges the gap between computational geometry and numerical analysis by employing identical basis functions for both geometric modeling and physical field approximation. This section establishes the NURBS-based mathematical foundation essential for the subsequent integration with the lattice Boltzmann method (LBM) in Section
4
.

3.1.
NURBS-based geometric representation

Within the IGA framework, the Cartesian grid in the parametric domain
\hat{\Omega}
is transformed into a body-fitted curvilinear grid in the physical domain
\Omega
through the parameterization, i.e., the geometric mapping from
\hat{\Omega}
to
\Omega
, based on B spline and/or NURBS.

Basis function construction

The mathematical foundation of B-splines lies in the Cox-de Boor recursion formula, which generates piecewise polynomial basis functions from a knot vector. Given a knot vector:

(11)

\Xi=[\xi_{1},\xi_{2},...,\xi_{n+p+1}],\quad\xi_{i}\leq\xi_{i+1},

where
p
is the polynomial degree and
n
is the number of basis functions, the univariate B-spline basis functions
N_{i,p}(\xi)
are recursively defined as
(
Piegl and Tiller 2012
)
:

(12)

\displaystyle N_{i,p}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{\xi-\xi_{i}}{\xi_{i+p}-\xi_{i}}N_{i,p-1}(\xi)+\frac{\xi_{i+p+1}-\xi}{\xi_{i+p+1}-\xi_{i+1}}N_{i+1,p-1}(\xi)

starting with

N_{i,0}(\xi)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\text{if}\ \xi\in[\xi_{i},\xi_{i+1}),\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Here, ratios of the form
0/0
are conventionally defined as zero.

B-spline basis functions possess several advantageous properties that make them highly suitable for geometric modeling, such as

•

Local support
:
N_{i,p}(\xi)>0
only within
[\xi_{i},\xi_{i+p+1})
;

•

Partition of unity
:
\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i,p}(\xi)=1
;

•

Derivative continuity
: A basis function being
C^{p-m}
-continuous at a knot of multiplicity
m
.

Non-Uniform Rational B-splines (NURBS)

While B-splines provide a flexible and efficient basis for geometric modeling and numerical analysis, they have a fundamental limitation: they cannot exactly represent conic sections such as circles, ellipses, and hyperbolas. To overcome this limitation, NURBS extend B-splines by introducing additional weight parameters that allow for the exact representation of these curves. NURBS have become the standard for geometric representation in CAD and are widely adopted in IGA due to their enhanced flexibility and accuracy.

To overcome B-spline limitations in conic section representation, NURBS introduce weight parameters
\omega_{i}
:

(13)

R^{p}_{i}(\xi)=\frac{\omega_{i}N_{i,p}(\xi)}{W(\xi)},\quad W(\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{cp}}}\omega_{i}N_{i,p}(\xi),

where
N_{i,p}(\xi)
are the standard B-spline basis functions of degree
p
,
\omega_{i}
are the associated positive weights, and
W(\xi)
is the weight function ensuring partition of unity. The introduction of weights allows NURBS basis functions to form a rational function space, providing greater modeling capabilities compared to polynomial B-splines.

A NURBS curve in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is then defined as the weighted sum of control points:

(14)

\vec{C}(\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{cp}}}R^{p}_{i}(\xi)\vec{B}_{i},

where
\vec{B}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the control points defining the geometry.

Tensor product construction

NURBS surfaces and volumes are constructed using tensor-product basis functions, extending the one-dimensional formulation to higher dimensions. A NURBS surface is defined as:

(15)

\vec{S}(\xi,\eta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}R^{p,q}_{i,j}(\xi,\eta)\vec{B}_{i,j},

while a NURBS volume follows the extension:

(16)

\vec{V}(\xi,\eta,\zeta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{l}R^{p,q,r}_{i,j,k}(\xi,\eta,\zeta)\vec{B}_{i,j,k},

where
R^{p,q}_{i,j}(\xi,\eta)
and
R^{p,q,r}_{i,j,k}(\xi,\eta,\zeta)
denote the corresponding NURBS basis functions for 2D and 3D spaces, respectively. For notational simplicity, we adopt a single-index notation when no ambiguity arises. Under this convention, the parameterization of the domain can be consistently expressed as:

(17)

\vec{x}(\vec{\xi})=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{cp}}}\vec{P}_{i}\hat{R}_{i}(\vec{\xi}),

where
\hat{R}_{i}(\vec{\xi})
represents the NURBS basis functions, and
\vec{P}_{i}
are the corresponding control points, and
n_{\mathrm{cp}}
denotes control point count. This formulation provides a unified representation for NURBS curves, surfaces, and volumes.

NURBS inherit many desirable properties from B-splines, including:

•

Local support:
Each NURBS basis function is nonzero only over a finite number of knot spans, ensuring computational efficiency;

•

Partition of unity:
The basis functions satisfy the condition
\sum_{i}R^{p}_{i}(\xi)=1
, preserving affine invariance and guaranteeing that the parameterized geometry remains unaffected by uniform transformations such as translation, rotation, and scaling;

•

Higher-order continuity:
NURBS basis functions maintain
C^{p-m}
continuity across elements, providing smooth representations that enhance numerical stability and accuracy;

•

Exact representation of conic sections:
By appropriately choosing weights
\omega_{i}
, NURBS can exactly represent circles, ellipses, and hyperbolas, which is a fundamental limitation of standard B-splines.

Parametric-Physical Relationship

The accurate transformation between parametric and physical coordinates forms the cornerstone of IGA. As the physical domain
\Omega
is parameterized through NURBS mappings (
17
), all differential operators in the governing equations must be consistently transformed between these two spaces. This transformation is governed by the Jacobian matrix
\bm{\mathcal{J}}
, which encapsulates the local deformation characteristics of the NURBS parameterization.

For a 2D mapping
\vec{x}(\xi,\eta)=(x(\xi,\eta),y(\xi,\eta))^{\mathrm{T}}
, the Jacobian matrix is defined as:

(18)

\bm{\mathcal{J}}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial\xi}&\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial\eta}\\[10.0pt]
\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial\xi}&\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial\eta}\end{bmatrix},

where each component quantifies the sensitivity of physical coordinates to parametric variations.

The inverse relationship for differential operators follows from the chain rule:

(19)

\nabla_{\vec{x}}=\bm{\mathcal{J}}^{-\mathrm{T}}\nabla_{\bm{\xi}}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial\xi}{\partial x}&\dfrac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}\\
\dfrac{\partial\xi}{\partial y}&\dfrac{\partial\eta}{\partial y}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\\
\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\end{bmatrix},

where
\bm{\mathcal{J}}^{-\mathrm{T}}
denotes the inverse transpose of the Jacobian matrix.

The Jacobian determinant
|\bm{\mathcal{J}}|
is used to account for the transformation of area elements, and the inverse Jacobian
\bm{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}
is used to transform derivatives between the physical and parametric domains:

(20)

\bm{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial x}&\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}\\
\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial y}&\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial y}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{|\bm{\mathcal{J}}|}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial y}{\partial\eta}&-\frac{\partial x}{\partial\eta}\\
-\frac{\partial y}{\partial\xi}&\frac{\partial x}{\partial\xi}\end{bmatrix}.

Then for any scalar function
f
, the following transformation relation

(21)

\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\end{bmatrix}f=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial x}&\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}\\
\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial y}&\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial y}\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}\end{bmatrix}f

holds.

Derivative computation

The Jacobian components are computed directly from the NURBS parameterization. Taking the
\xi
-derivative of (
17
) yields:

(22)

\frac{\partial\vec{x}}{\partial\xi}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{cp}}}\omega_{i}\vec{B}_{i}\frac{dN_{i,p}}{d\xi}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{cp}}}\omega_{i}N_{i,p}}-\vec{x}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{cp}}}\omega_{i}\frac{dN_{i,p}}{d\xi}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{cp}}}\omega_{i}N_{i,p}},

where the first term arises from the explicit differentiation of the NURBS numerator, and the second term accounts for the spatial variation of the denominator. Similar expressions hold for
\partial\vec{x}/\partial\eta
.

This computation demonstrates two key advantages of NURBS in IGA:

•

Exact geometry awareness
: Derivatives inherit the smoothness of NURBS basis functions, avoiding approximation errors from discrete differentiation.

•

Adaptive resolution
: The local support property of NURBS ensures that derivative calculations involve only neighboring control points.

3.2.
Analysis framework

The seamless integration of Isogeometric Analysis (IGA) with the lattice Boltzmann method (LBM) hinges on three foundational pillars: (1) exact geometric representation via NURBS, (2) isoparametric solution approximation, and (3) consistent transformation of the Boltzmann equation. This framework overcomes the geometric limitations of conventional LBM while preserving its computational efficiency.

Geometric representation with NURBS

At the core of IGA-LBM lies the NURBS-based parameterization of complex fluid domains. Unlike traditional LBM that relies on Cartesian grids, the physical domain
\Omega
is mapped from a parametric space
\hat{\Omega}
through the parameterization (
17
). This mapping preserves CAD geometry exactly (Fig.
), critical for simulating flows around curved boundaries.

Isoparametric Solution Approximation

The distribution functions
f_{\alpha}(\vec{x},t)
are approximated using the same NURBS basis functions as the geometry, enforcing solution-geometry compatibility:

(23)

f_{\alpha}(\vec{x},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{cp}}}f_{\alpha,i}(t)R_{i}(\vec{x}),

where
R_{i}(\vec{x})=\hat{R}_{i}(\vec{\xi}^{-1}(\vec{x}))
are physical-domain NURBS basis functions. This isoparametric approach ensures:

•

Geometric consistency
: The solution space
\mathrm{span}\{R_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n_{\mathrm{cp}}}
contains the geometry mapping
\vec{x}(\vec{\xi})
;

•

Adaptive synchronization
:
h
-/
p
-/
k
-Refinement improves flow simulation accuracy;

•

Boundary alignment
: Control points naturally coincide with curved boundaries, simplifying boundary condition implementations, e.g., bounce-back boundary condition.

Transformed Boltzmann equation

The LBM governing equations are reformulated in the parametric space to accommodate the NURBS-based parameterization (
17
). Using the gradient operator transformation in (
19
), the discrete Boltzmann equation then becomes:

(24)

f_{\alpha}(\vec{\xi}+\Delta\vec{\xi}_{\alpha},t+\Delta t)=f_{\alpha}(\vec{\xi},t)-\frac{\Delta t}{\tau}\left(f_{\alpha}-f_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right),

where the advection displacement
\Delta\vec{\xi}_{\alpha}
is computed via:

(25)

\Delta\vec{\xi}_{\alpha}=\bm{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\vec{e}_{\alpha}\Delta t.

4.
IGA-LBM: isogeometric Lattice Boltzmann method

In this section, we present the integration of NURBS-based Isogeometric Analysis (IGA) with the lattice Boltzmann method (LBM), referred to as IGA-LBM, for solving fluid dynamics problems. By leveraging the smooth and high-continuity properties of B-splines and NURBS, this approach enables body-fitted parameterization while preserving the efficiency of LBM. The key components of the IGA-LBM framework are discussed below.

Notation and assumptions.

We consider low-Mach, isothermal, single-relaxation-time (SRT) BGK models on a 2D physical domain
\Omega_{p}

parameterized by a NURBS mapping
\mathbf{x}(\bm{\xi}):\Omega_{c}\to\Omega_{p}
, with Jacobian
\mathcal{J}
.
Bold symbols denote vectors, and
(\mathbf{e}_{\alpha},\omega_{\alpha})
the discrete velocity set and quadrature weights.
Tildes indicate contravariant components in parametric space, e.g.
\tilde{e}_{\alpha\xi}
.

4.1.
NURBS-adapted Boltzmann formulation

To apply the lattice Boltzmann method (LBM) on a transformed body-fitted parameterization, the governing equations must first be mapped from the physical domain
\Omega
to the computational domain
\hat{\Omega}
. This mapping is achieved through a NURBS-based parameterization, as defined in (
17
), which establishes a one-to-one correspondence between the parametric coordinates and the physical space
\Omega
.

In the following, we present the proposed IGA-LBM framework in the 2D case for clarity. However, the methodology can be readily extended to the 3D case without loss of generality. The transformation from the physical domain
\Omega
to the parametric domain
\hat{\Omega}
is defined by the inverse mapping of the adopted NURBS-based parameterization (
17
):

(26)

\xi=\xi(x,y),\quad\eta=\eta(x,y).

To express spatial derivatives in the parametric domain, we apply the chain rule of differentiation, which relates derivatives in the physical domain to derivatives in the parametric domain as:

(27)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial x}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}+\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial y}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial y}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}+\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial y}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta}.

The relationship between the isoparametric curves in the physical domain and the structured grid in the parametric space is governed by the inverse of the Jacobian matrix:

(28)

\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{x}&\xi_{y}\\
\eta_{x}&\eta_{y}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{|\bm{\mathcal{J}}|}\begin{bmatrix}y_{\eta}&-x_{\eta}\\
-y_{\xi}&x_{\xi}\end{bmatrix},

where the Jacobian determinant
\bm{\mathcal{J}}
of the transformation is given by

(29)

\left|\bm{\mathcal{J}}\right|=x_{\xi}y_{\eta}-x_{\eta}y_{\xi}.

The lattice Boltzmann equation (
1
) in the physical domain
\Omega
is transformed to the parameteric domain
\hat{\Omega}
as follows:

(30)

f_{\alpha}(\vec{x}(\xi)+\bm{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\vec{e}_{\alpha}\Delta t,t+\Delta t)-f_{\alpha}(\vec{x}(\xi),t)=-\frac{1}{\tau}\left(f_{\alpha}(\vec{x}(\xi),t)-f_{\alpha}^{eq}(\vec{x}(\xi),t)\right),

where
\bm{\mathcal{J}}
is the Jacobian matrix of the NURBS-based parameterization
\vec{x}(\vec{\xi})
.

Applying the chain rule of differentiation from (
27
), the convection term in the physical domain can be rewritten in the parametric domain as

(31)

\displaystyle\vec{e}_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla_{\vec{x}}f_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=e_{\alpha x}\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial x}+e_{\alpha y}\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial y}

\displaystyle=e_{\alpha x}\left(\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial\xi}\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial\eta}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}\right)+e_{\alpha y}\left(\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial\xi}\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial\eta}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial y}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(e_{\alpha x}\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial x}+e_{\alpha y}\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial y}\right)\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial\xi}+\left(e_{\alpha x}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial x}+e_{\alpha y}\frac{\partial\eta}{\partial y}\right)\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial\eta}

\displaystyle=\tilde{e}_{\alpha\xi}\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial\xi}+\tilde{e}_{\alpha\eta}\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial\eta}

\displaystyle=\vec{\tilde{e}}_{\alpha}\nabla_{\vec{\xi}}f_{\alpha},

where the transformed lattice velocities
\vec{\tilde{e}}_{\alpha}
are given by

(32)

\vec{\tilde{e}}_{\alpha}=(\tilde{e}_{\alpha\xi},\tilde{e}_{\alpha\eta})=(e_{\alpha x}\xi_{x}+e_{\alpha y}\xi_{y},e_{\alpha x}\eta_{x}+e_{\alpha y}\eta_{y}).

Thus, the single-relaxation-time (SRT) Lattice Boltzmann equation in the parametric domain is expressed as

(33)

\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}+\tilde{e}_{\alpha\xi}\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial\xi}+\tilde{e}_{\alpha\eta}\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial\eta}=-\frac{1}{\tau}\left(f_{\alpha}-f_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right).

4.2.
Transformed streaming and collision operators

Isoparametric representation

In the proposed IGA-LBM framework, the distribution functions are approximated using NURBS basis functions, ensuring a smooth and high-fidelity representation. The distribution function is expressed as

(34)

f_{\alpha}^{h}(\vec{x},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{cp}}}f_{\alpha,i}(t)R_{i}(\vec{\xi}),

where
f_{\alpha,i}
(
i=1,2,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{cp}}
) are the control coefficients representing the discrete distribution function at the control points,
R_{i}(\vec{\xi})
are the NURBS basis functions, and
n_{\mathrm{cp}}
is the total number of control points in the computational domain. This representation ensures that the numerical scheme inherits the geometric continuity and high-order accuracy of NURBS, which is a key advantage over traditional finite difference or finite volume methods.

Operator Discretization

Since the distribution function is expressed using NURBS basis functions, spatial derivatives can be exactly computed via basis function differentiation, eliminating numerical dissipation common in finite difference approximations. The derivatives of the distribution function are given by

(35)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial\xi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{cp}}}f_{\alpha,i}(t)\frac{\partial R_{i}(\vec{\xi})}{\partial\xi},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial\eta}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{cp}}}f_{\alpha,i}(t)\frac{\partial R_{i}(\vec{\xi})}{\partial\eta}.

Since the computational domain is mapped to the physical domain using a NURBS-based coordinate transformation, the discrete velocity vectors in the physical space can be transformed accordingly. This is achieved by multiplying the lattice velocities by the inverse of the Jacobian matrix
\bm{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}
, ensuring consistency between the particle streaming direction and the body-fitted NURBS-based parameterization.

Differential operators and precomputation

Let
\hat{\Phi}
be the collocation matrix of basis functions at
\{\bm{\xi}_{i}\}
and
\hat{\Phi}_{\xi},\hat{\Phi}_{\eta}
the matrices of first derivatives. The spectral differentiation operators read

(36)

D_{\xi}:=\hat{\Phi}_{\xi}\hat{\Phi}^{-1},\qquad D_{\eta}:=\hat{\Phi}_{\eta}\hat{\Phi}^{-1}.

Naively forming
\hat{\Phi}^{-1}
at each time step is expensive; therefore, we precompute

(37)

X_{\xi}:=\hat{\Phi}_{\xi}\hat{\Phi}^{-1},\qquad X_{\eta}:=\hat{\Phi}_{\eta}\hat{\Phi}^{-1},

store them once, and reuse throughout the simulation. Note that
X_{\xi},X_{\eta}
depend only on the basis and the
collocation points, but not on the mapping
\mathbf{x}(\bm{\xi})
.

Collision

The collision step follows the standard BGK (Bhatnagar-Gross-Krook) approximation, which relaxes the distribution function towards its equilibrium state. In the IGA framework, the collision update is performed at the control coefficients:

(38)

f_{\alpha,i}^{*}(t)=f_{\alpha,i}(t)-\frac{\Delta t}{\tau}\left(f_{\alpha,i}(\vec{x},t)-f_{\alpha,i}^{\text{eq}}(\vec{x},t)\right),\quad i=1,2,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{cp}},

where
f_{\alpha,i}^{*}(\vec{x},t)
is the post-collision distribution function at the control points,
f_{\alpha,i}(t)
is the pre-collision distribution function,
\tau
is the relaxation time, and
f_{\alpha,i}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\vec{x},t)
is the control coefficients of the equilibrium distribution function found by

(39)

f_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\vec{x},t)=\omega_{\alpha}\rho\left(1+\frac{\vec{e}_{\alpha}\cdot\vec{u}}{c_{s}^{2}}+\frac{(\vec{e}_{\alpha}\cdot\vec{u})^{2}}{2c_{s}^{4}}-\frac{\vec{u}\cdot\vec{u}}{2c_{s}^{2}}\right),

where
\omega_{\alpha}
are the lattice weights,
\rho
is the macroscopic density,
\vec{u}
is the macroscopic velocity, and
c_{s}
is the lattice speed of sound.

Streaming

The streaming step propagates the post-collision distribution function to neighboring control points along the transformed lattice velocity directions:

(40)

f_{\alpha}(\vec{x}+\bm{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\vec{e}_{\alpha}\Delta t,t+\Delta t)=f_{\alpha}^{*}(\vec{x},t),

where
\bm{\mathcal{J}}
is the Jacobian matrix of the NURBS-based parameterization that transforms the computational space to the physical space.

Macroscopic quantities

The macroscopic fluid properties are reconstructed from the NURBS-based distribution functions by taking moments of the transformed discrete velocity set

(41)

\rho=\sum_{\alpha}f_{\alpha},\quad\vec{u}=\frac{1}{\rho}\sum_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}\vec{e}_{\alpha},\quad p=\rho c_{s}^{2}.

Therefore, the macroscopic quantities are also expressed in a NURBS form, inheriting the smoothness and high-order continuity of the NURBS basis functions.

4.3.
Boundary conditions treatment

Accurate boundary conditions are crucial for ensuring the stability and correctness of the IGA-LBM framework. The most commonly used boundary conditions in fluid simulations include periodic boundaries, no-slip walls, and far-field conditions, each of which is handled within the proposed NURBS-based framework.

Periodic boundary conditions

Periodic boundary conditions are used when the flow domain exhibits repeating structures. This ensures that distribution functions at one boundary are mapped directly to the corresponding points on the opposite boundary:

(42)

f_{\alpha}(\vec{x}_{\mathrm{left}},t)=f_{\alpha}(\vec{x}_{\mathrm{right}},t),\quad f_{\alpha}(\vec{x}_{\mathrm{bottom}},t)=f_{\alpha}(\vec{x}_{\mathrm{top}},t).

When implemented in a NURBS-based parametric space, smooth transitions across periodic boundaries must be maintained to prevent numerical discontinuities.

No-Slip walls (Bounce-Back scheme)

For solid boundaries, a no-slip condition is enforced using a bounce-back scheme at control points:

(43)

f_{\bar{\alpha}}(\vec{x}_{b},t+\Delta t)=f_{\alpha}^{*}(\vec{x}_{b},t)-2w_{\alpha}\rho_{b}\frac{\vec{e}_{\alpha}\cdot\vec{u}_{\mathrm{wall}}}{c_{s}^{2}},

where
\vec{u}_{\mathrm{wall}}
represents the velocity of the solid wall, which is interpolated at the control points. This formulation ensures that velocity values on the boundary satisfy the no-slip condition while preserving consistency with the NURBS representation of the domain geometry.

Far-Field and pressure boundary conditions

For external flows, far-field and pressure boundaries are commonly used to impose physically realistic inflow and outflow conditions. A characteristic-based extrapolation approach is employed to enforce inflow velocity and pressure:

(44)

f_{\alpha}(\vec{x}_{b},t)=f_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\rho_{\mathrm{target}},\vec{u}_{\mathrm{inflow}})+\left[f_{\alpha}(\vec{x}_{i},t)-f_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{eq}}(\vec{x}_{i},t)\right],

where
\rho_{\mathrm{target}}
and
\vec{u}_{\mathrm{inflow}}
define the prescribed far-field conditions, and
\vec{x}_{i}
is the nearest interior control point.

At outflow boundaries, a Neumann-type condition is imposed, ensuring that macroscopic quantities exhibit minimal numerical reflections:

(45)

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial n}=0,\quad\frac{\partial\vec{u}}{\partial n}=0.

4.4.
Time integration

The evolution of the distribution functions follows the standard LBM streaming-collision procedure but is adapted to the transformed computational space. The streaming step transports the distribution functions along the transformed discrete velocity set, while the collision step relaxes them toward equilibrium.

Semi-discrete formulation and RK4 time stepping

Using (
31
) and the precomputed operators (
37
), the convection term is computed in flux form at collocation points:

(46)

C_{\alpha}=-\Big[\,D_{\xi}\big(\tilde{e}_{\alpha\xi}\odot f_{\alpha}\big)+D_{\eta}\big(\tilde{e}_{\alpha\eta}\odot f_{\alpha}\big)\,\Big],

and the BGK collision term is

(47)

\Omega_{\alpha}=-\frac{1}{\tau}\big(f_{\alpha}-f_{\alpha}^{\rm eq}\big).

Hence the semi-discrete system reads

(48)

\frac{\mathrm{d}f_{\alpha}}{\mathrm{d}t}=R_{\alpha}(f):=C_{\alpha}+\Omega_{\alpha}.

Time integration is performed by the explicit classical RK4 scheme

\displaystyle k^{(1)}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=R_{\alpha}(f^{n}),\quad k^{(2)}_{\alpha}=R_{\alpha}\!\Big(f^{n}+\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}k^{(1)}_{\alpha}\Big),\quad k^{(3)}_{\alpha}=R_{\alpha}\!\Big(f^{n}+\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}k^{(2)}_{\alpha}\Big),

(49)

\displaystyle k^{(4)}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=R_{\alpha}\!\Big(f^{n}+\Delta t\,k^{(3)}_{\alpha}\Big),\qquad f^{n+1}_{\alpha}=f^{n}_{\alpha}+\tfrac{\Delta t}{6}\big(k^{(1)}_{\alpha}+2k^{(2)}_{\alpha}+2k^{(3)}_{\alpha}+k^{(4)}_{\alpha}\big).

CFL-like constraint.

Unlike classical streaming-based LBM, here
\Delta t
must satisfy a CFL-like condition

(50)

\Delta t\;\leq\;\mathrm{CFL}\;\min_{\mathbf{x}}\left\{\frac{1}{\displaystyle\max_{\alpha}\!\left(\frac{|\tilde{e}_{\alpha\xi}(\mathbf{x})|}{h_{\xi}(\mathbf{x})}+\frac{|\tilde{e}_{\alpha\eta}(\mathbf{x})|}{h_{\eta}(\mathbf{x})}\right)}\right\},\quad 0<\mathrm{CFL}\lesssim 1,

where
h_{\xi}(\mathbf{x})=|\partial_{\xi}\mathbf{x}|\,\Delta\xi
and

h_{\eta}(\mathbf{x})=|\partial_{\eta}\mathbf{x}|\,\Delta\eta
denote local grid spacings.

For clarity, the transformed convection terms in (
31
) are defined as:

(51)

\mathcal{N}_{\alpha}=\tilde{e}_{\alpha\xi}\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial\xi}+\tilde{e}_{\alpha\eta}\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial\eta},

which represents the advection of distribution functions in the parameterized space.

Applying the isogeometric discretization, the semi-discrete form of the system leads to an ordinary differential equation:

(52)

\frac{\partial f_{\alpha}}{\partial t}=-\mathcal{N}(f).

This system is integrated in time using a four-stage, fourth-order explicit Runge-Kutta scheme:

(53)

\displaystyle f_{\alpha}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=f_{\alpha}^{n}-\frac{\Delta t}{4}\mathcal{N}(f_{\alpha}^{n})

\displaystyle f_{\alpha}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=f_{\alpha}^{n}-\frac{\Delta t}{3}\mathcal{N}(f_{\alpha}^{(1)})

\displaystyle f_{\alpha}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=f_{\alpha}^{n}-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{N}(f_{\alpha}^{(2)})

\displaystyle f_{\alpha}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=f_{\alpha}^{n}-\Delta t\mathcal{N}(f_{\alpha}^{(3)})

where
\mathcal{N}(f_{\alpha})
represents the discretized advection operator, and
f_{\alpha}^{n}
and
f_{\alpha}^{n+1}
denote the distribution functions at time steps
n
and
n+1
, respectively.

Adaptive Time-Stepping

By default, the time step
\Delta t
is set as a constant in this paper. However, for enhanced numerical stability and accuracy, it can be adaptively updated at each iteration according to the local grid deformation and the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) condition:

(54)

\Delta t=\mathrm{CFL}\cdot\min_{\mathrm{cp}}\left(\frac{h_{\mathrm{min}}}{\|\bm{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\vec{e}_{\alpha}\|_{2}}\right),

where
h_{\mathrm{min}}
is the minimum grid spacing in the parametric domain, and
\bm{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\vec{e}_{\alpha}
represents the transformed lattice velocities. This adaptive scheme ensures numerical stability, particularly for highly deformed grids. The CFL number is a user-defined parameter, typically chosen close to unity being typically optimal.

The Taylor–Green vortex is a widely used benchmark problem in fluid dynamics, defined by an initial condition for the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations. It serves as a valuable test case for validating numerical methods due to its smooth velocity and pressure fields, which evolve over time through viscous dissipation. Governed by periodic boundary conditions, this problem isolates numerical errors without the influence of physical boundary effects, making it well-suited for assessing numerical dissipation, stability, and conservation properties. In this study, we employ the Taylor–Green vortex to rigorously validate the numerical accuracy, convergence behavior, and conservation properties of the proposed IGA-LBM framework.

6.
Conclusions

In this work, we have developed an isogeometric lattice Boltzmann method (IGA-LBM) that seamlessly integrates the geometric flexibility of isogeometric analysis (IGA) with the computational efficiency of the lattice Boltzmann method (LBM). By employing analysis-suitable NURBS parameterizations, the proposed framework allows for accurate representation of complex, body-fitted geometries while preserving the standard LBM formulation. The use of higher-order, smooth basis functions ensures that both geometry and flow variables share the same discretization, thereby reducing interpolation errors and improving numerical stability.

The numerical experiments presented in this study confirm that the IGA-LBM achieves high accuracy and robust convergence on curved and irregular domains, outperforming conventional grid-based LBM approaches in terms of boundary conformity and solution smoothness. Furthermore, the transformation of the governing equations into the parametric space establishes a unified workflow between geometric modeling and flow simulation, facilitating efficient pre-processing and consistent geometry handling.

The advantages of the proposed method make it particularly well-suited for applications such as microfluidics, aerodynamic simulations involving complex surfaces, and flow analysis in bioengineering or energy systems where accurate geometry representation is critical.

Future work will focus on extending the present framework to fully multi-patch three-dimensional simulations, and exploring adaptive refinement strategies to further enhance computational efficiency. Additionally, coupling the IGA-LBM with multi-physics solvers (e.g., heat transfer or reactive flows) may broaden its applicability to a wider range of engineering and scientific problems.
