WHO exclusive breastfeeding recommendation and cognitive development in Germany
Authors: Debora Suppiger, Giancarlo Natalucci, Tilman Reinelt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated whether adherence to the World Health Organization (WHO) recommendation of six months exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) is associated with better cognitive development throughout early childhood in Germany. Furthermore, it was assessed whether this association differs between low-risk infants and “small vulnerable newborns” (SVN; infants born preterm and/or with low birth weight), and whether an optimal EBF duration for cognitive outcomes exists. Data were drawn from the German National Educational Panel Study (NEPS), including up to 2511 children assessed repeatedly from infancy to 7 years. Standardized cognitive outcomes included abstract thinking (3 years), and phonological working memory (3, 6, and 7 years). EBF duration was categorized into no EBF, short EBF (i.e., EBF for less than 6 months), and 6-month EBF. No cognitive benefits were found at any age for 6-month EBF compared to shorter EBF durations or no EBF. For SVN, no consistent benefits of longer EBF were observed. Maternal education predicted cognitive outcomes consistently (range η2 = 0.01–0.04). Generalized additive models indicated no optimal EBF duration across domains. In conclusion, WHO 6-month EBF was not linked to better cognitive performance in both low-risk infants and SVN in this collective.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47687-0.Keywords: Exclusive breastfeeding, Cognition, Maternal education, Small vulnerable newbornsSubject terms: Health care, Medical research

Introduction

WHO recommendation of six months EBF

The general consensus that human milk is the ideal food for infants, stems from its association with a variety of health benefits1, better performance in intelligence tests2, and overall better developmental outcomes3. According to a systematic review summarizing results from 20 studies across low- and high-income countries especially highlighted the importance of exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) in the early months1. Following these findings, in 2001, the WHO gave a public recommendation for six months exclusive breastfeeding (EBF)4, which was restated in 2012.

EBF and cognitive development

Recent research on associations of EBF and child cognitive development, however, has raised questions about whether six months is actually the optimal duration of EBF. While six months of EBF has been associated with higher IQ5,6, shorter periods of EBF, such as two7,8, three5,8,9 or four10 months, already showed benefits. A recent narrative review even concluded that EBF, irrespective of a defined duration, showed beneficial effects on neurodevelopment and cognitive outcomes11. Likewise, while some studies suggest a dose-dependent benefit of EBF duration on cognitive development5,8, other have reported no effect10. One potential reason for the heterogeneity in results might be explained by accounting for bias introduced by socio-economic status, as socio-economic status is associated with both cognitive development12 and breastfeeding behavior13. For instance, one study found no effect of EBF after propensity score matching based on socio-demographic variables14, while another study reported halved effect sizes after adjustment for socio-economic status8.

EBF in SVN

In addition to socio-economic status, infant characteristics may represent another source for heterogeneity in the associations of EBF duration and child cognitive development, such as infants at risk, like “small and vulnerable newborns” (SVN), who in particular could be affected by EBF15,16. Infants born preterm (before 37 weeks), small for gestational age (SGA) or with low birth weight (LBW; under 2500 g) are subsumed under the umbrella term “small vulnerable newborns” (SVN). Due to their size, maturity, or medical conditions, these infants are more susceptible to infections, complications, and mortality, and are prone to adverse developmental outcomes17. Children born SVN have an increased risk for adverse cognitive outcomes, performing worse in most cognitive tests compared to their term-born and normal weight peers12,18. Human milk is particularly beneficial for SVN, due to its protective effects against infections, necrotizing enterocolitis, retinopathy of prematurity, and mortality19–22. In addition, human milk feeding has been shown to promote better neurodevelopment23 and cognition19,24,25 in children born SVN. This beneficial effect of human milk in SVN might be dose-dependent, as longer duration and higher proportions in human milk feeding have been associated with better cognitive outcomes19,24,25.

However, most studies in SVN reported on the effects of “any” breastfeeding duration and not EBF duration, potentially due to lower EBF rates in SVN compared to term-born infants.

Although, like for term-born infants, six months of EBF are advised by the WHO for preterm and low-birth-weight infants26, EBF rates at discharge are lower than in term-born infants27, which may be due to the challenges of breastfeeding initiation. Mothers of SVN may have preexisting and pregnancy-related medical morbidities, which may lead to delayed lactogenesis or reduced milk production28. Furthermore, stress associated with the urgent birth and the stay in the neonatal unit, lack of support from staff and delayed initiation of pumping can negatively influence breastfeeding initiation and duration29,30. Given these challenges, any breastfeeding may be a more realistic goal for mothers of SVN than EBF.

The present study

The current study investigated the WHO EBF recommendation with regard to child cognitive development. The primary objective was to determine whether adherence to the WHO EBF recommendation leads to better cognitive performance across childhood and cognitive domains in the general population and in SVN in particular. We focused on the following research questions:

Methods

Procedure

This study is a secondary analysis that uses data from the newborn cohort31 of the National Educational Panel Study (NEPS)32,33 in Germany, which intends to observe educational processes from infancy onwards34. The NEPS study is conducted under the supervision of the German Federal Commissioner for Data Protection and Freedom of Information (BfDI) and in coordination with the German Standing Conference of the Ministers of Education and Cultural Affairs (KMK). All data collection procedures, instruments and documents were checked by the data protection unit of the Leibniz Institute for Educational Trajectories (LIfBi). The necessary steps are taken to protect participants’ confidentiality according to national and international regulations of data security. Participation in the NEPS study is voluntary and based on the informed and written consent of the parents. Children provide age-appropriate (verbal) assent and can refuse to participate in any task or withdraw at any time. To ensure a representative sample, the NEPS study selected 84 municipalities across Germany and contacted participants via local birth registry addresses. The first data were collected in 2012, beginning with wave 1 at infant age 6–8 months and the currently last data update was at wave 10 (9 years) in 2021 (see Fig. 1 for overview). At each wave, trained staff visited the families at home, interviewed a parent, and conducted at least one cognitive or educational task with the child34. In this study, only measures that used standardized and validated tasks to assess cognitive abilities were included (wave 4, wave 7 and wave 8). For full details on the study procedures of the newborn cohort see the online repository31.

An overview of the measurement waves in the NEPS study and sample size for each cognitive outcome.

Measures

EBF

Information on EBF duration was acquired retrospectively. At wave 2 (17–18 months) mothers were asked for how many weeks they exclusively breastfed (EBF, i.e., no additional infant formula or solids). There was no assessment of whether infants received human milk in addition to other fluids or solids (i.e., mixed/ any breastfeeding).

Abstract thinking and reasoning

The subtest “categories” from the Snijders-Oomen Nonverbal Intelligence Test (SON-R)35, a standardized test for the assessment of non-verbal intelligence, was administered at 3 years. The subtest is an indicator for basic non-verbal cognitive skills (i.e., abstract thinking and reasoning)36, where the child has to sort pictures according to characteristics (e.g., fruit). The test consists of one practice item and 15 test items which are divided into two parts. In part one (items 1 to 7), the child is asked to sort four to six images according to characteristics. In part two (items 8 to 15), the child is presented with three images that share a common feature. The child must then select two images from a set of five or more that match the same characteristic as the original three. The child receives feedback after each item. The test ends after three incorrect answers.36 The NEPS uses a Weighted Likelihood Estimator (WLE)37,38 to assess individual test performance relative to the overall group, adjusting for item-specific informational value. Scores above zero indicate above-average ability, while scores below zero reflect below-average ability.

Phonological working memory (WM)

Phonological WM was assessed using the digit span task from the Kaufman Assessment Battery for Children39(K-ABC) at ages 3, 6 and 7 years. At ages 3 and 6, only forward spans of up to 6 and 8 numbers were tested. At age 7, only backward spans up to 8 numbers were tested. The final score was the maximum length of the span that was answered correctly.

Confounders

Confounders included, the infant variables sex, age at test, and gestational age at birth. Furthermore, maternal education, which was measured on a scale with increasing educational levels from 0 (no formal schooling) to 9 (university degree), and family income was recorded. Lastly, mothers indicated their mental well-being in the first three months after birth on a 4-point Lickert scale (1 = “very bad” to 4 = “very good”), as well as their alcohol and nicotine consumption during pregnancy or breastfeeding (yes/no). Since there was less than 1% participation rate of fathers, only maternal variables were considered.

SVN

Being born SVN was defined as infants born before 37 weeks of gestation and/or with a birthweight lower than 2500 g.

Statistical analysis

Fisher’s exact test and Wilcoxon rank test were used to assess differences between the low-risk infants and SVN in demographic variables, EBF and cognitive outcomes.

To assess effects of EBF duration on cognitive development, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted with a set of confounders that correlated with both breastfeeding and cognitive outcomes. EBF duration was categorized into: (1) never exclusively breastfed; (2) exclusively breastfed for less than 6 months; (3) exclusively breastfed for six months or more, complying with WHO guidelines1,4,26. To ensure that EBF duration only contained plausible values, outliers were defined as being above the 1.5 inter-quartile range (i.e., 52.2 weeks) and assigned missing. Missing data in predictors or confounders were imputed using multiple imputation via the R mice package40, generating five datasets. All continuous variables (child age, gestational age at birth, family income and maternal education) in the regression analyses were mean centered. For interaction analysis between EBF duration and SVN, gestational age at birth was not included as a covariate, due to overlap with the SVN variable. Regression coefficients were estimated separately and pooled using Rubin’s rule. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to investigate whether (1) results from imputed and unimputed datasets differed (see supplement Section A and B); and (2) whether cognitive performance differed in infants who received EBF for longer than the recommended six months (see supplement Section C).

To assess whether results differed for SVN, the interaction of EBF duration and SVN status was added to the regression models.

To estimate the optimal EBF duration for maximizing cognitive outcomes, we employed generalized additive models (GAMs)41 within a multiple imputation framework. We modeled the cognitive score as a smooth function of EBF duration, allowing for non-linear effects and adjusted for confounders. This data-driven approach allows to identify peaks in the association.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 3481 families were included in the NEPS newborn cohort, of which 2511 participated in the cognitive outcome measures. About half (49.06%) were female and n = 200 (7.96%) were categorized as SVN. The number of valid scores for each outcome measure can be seen in Fig. 1.

About 67% of the families took part in three or more assessments, and 28% in all four. Sample characteristics did not differ between individual samples of the assessments (see supplement Section E Table 21). Attrition to the first three outcome measures (3 years and 6 years) was unrelated to EBF duration. However, those families participating at 7 years had longer EBF duration compared to those that were missing or dropout (t(949.5) = 2.99, p = 0.003). Those participating in any phonological working memory assessment (3, 6 and 7 years) had higher maternal education, than those who were missing or dropout (see supplement Section E Table 23). In line with this, those participating in more assessments, were more likely to have a higher education (x2(3, N = 2511) = 57.96, p < 0.001), and consumed alcohol during pregnancy/ breastfeeding (x2(3, N = 2511) = 13.43, p = 0.004), but were less likely to have smoked during pregnancy/breastfeeding (x2(3, N = 2511) = 21.44, p < 0.001). For more details on how characteristics change with attrition rate see supplement (Section E Fig. 1).

The sample characteristics are described in Table 1. About 81% of low-risk children were EBF. Of these, around 44% were exclusively breastfed for less than 6 months, while about 37% were exclusively breastfed for 6 months or more. On average, the duration of EBF was approximately 19 weeks. The remaining 19% of children were never exclusively fed human milk. Compared to low-risk children, children born SVN were less likely to be EBF (any EBF ~ 70%, EBF less than 6 months ~ 38%, EBF longer than 6 months ~ 32%, no EBF ~ 30%) and were EBF for shorter durations (~ 16 weeks). For abstract thinking and reasoning, the association with EBF duration became negative after about 5 weeks of EBF (see Fig. 2a) however, the association did not reach significance (Effective Degrees of Freedom (EDF) = 1.60, F(1.90, 2288.40) = 2.99, p = 0.094).

At 3 years (Fig. 2b), the association between EBF duration and WM was linear, but not significant (EDF = 1.04, F(1.07, 996.96) = 4.04, p = 0.053). At 6 years (Fig. 2c), the association resembled a weak negative quadratic term, with a maximum between 17 and 22 weeks, which, however, was not significant (EDF = 2.05, F(2.55, 1937.95) = 1.53, p = 0.270). At 7 years (Fig. 2d), the association was linear, but not significant (EDF = 1.07, F(1.13, 1820.93) = 0.21, p = 0.716).

Discussion

The WHO promotes universal recommendations as a global public health strategy to optimize child survival, development, and maternal health. While these recommendations are standardized across populations, they acknowledge the need for contextual adaptation. Global evidence supports associations between EBF and improved health outcomes, particularly in settings with limited resources or high child morbidity. However, the link between EBF and cognitive development remains less clearly established.

This study reports on the association between the adherence to the WHO six-month EBF recommendation and cognitive outcomes across childhood in a newborn cohort in Germany. We further investigated whether SVN were benefitting more from six months EBF and whether there is an optimal EBF duration to support cognitive development.

WHO EBF recommendation and cognitive outcomes

Across all cognitive domains, there was no difference in performance of infants receiving EBF for six months compared to those with shorter or no EBF periods. This is in line with a study in Japan, which has found no significant cognitive differences between partial breastfeeding and EBF42. A possible explanation could be that positive effects of EBF and breastfeeding are usually reported on total scores of generalized cognition tests such as intelligence scales7,9,43 or developmental scales8,10. However, these tests span multiple domains (e.g., attention, processing speed, memory) and modalities (e.g., verbal and spatial), and sometimes even include other measures, such as verbal2,42 or motor development44. Thus, it is difficult to determine whether EBF affects all cognitive domains the same or whether effects might even be driven by other task components (e.g., language). For example, studies reported no effects of EBF on individual cognitive tests that measure memory span6,45–47, spatial visualization and abstract reasoning48, and executive functions49,50, while another study reported that EBF only affected language-specific outcomes42. These findings are in line with neuroimaging studies, where human milk intake is associated with regional increases in grey and white matter volume51–54, rather than affecting all brain regions uniformly55. Human milk supplies several bioactive compounds, including long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (e.g., DHA and ARA), human milk oligosaccharides, and neurotrophic growth factors (such as brain derived neurotropic factor), that may contribute to early brain development by supporting critical neurodevelopmental processes, particularly myelination and synaptogenesis56. To effectively understand which cognitive domains may be supported by EBF, studies with extensive neuropsychological testing are needed, that measure effects across cognitive domains.

Another aspect to take into consideration is the importance of socio-economic variables. In this sample, cognitive performance was mainly predicted by maternal education, which is a known predictor of childhood cognition12,57–59. Several studies have reported that after controlling for the socio-economic situation effects of human milk on cognitive outcomes either become smaller or disappear2,8,14,48,60. The cognitive domains tested in this study may be more susceptible to the influence of socio-economic variables, as maternal education was one of the strongest predictors in all outcomes.

WHO EBF recommendation and SVN

There were no differences in cognitive outcomes between the low-risk children and children born SVN. Although the SVN sample included some very preterm infants, the majority were late preterm born children (i.e., born at 34 to 36 weeks’ gestation). As impairments in cognitive functioning tend to decrease with increasing gestational age61, this may explain why no differences were observed. Similar to the low-risk children, one of the strongest and most consistent predictors for cognitive outcomes in preterm infants is parental education12. Maternal education was relatively high with 43–50% with tertiary education, which again may explain the favorable outcome in SVN.

We also found no indication, that SVN benefit more from longer EBF durations. At three years, SVN with shorter EBF durations did show lower scores in phonological WM, compared to the recommended six months of EBF, however there was no difference between children with six months EBF and no EBF children. As the effect size was very small (less than 1% of variance explained) and no other assessment showed this trend, this result should be interpreted with caution. Many parents of SVN face challenges in providing EBF and often receive recommendations to supplement with infant formula for faster weight gain for their SVN offspring, which might be reflected by the lower EBF rate in this sample. While the evidence from previous studies clearly supports the benefits of EBF in SVN, particularly in reducing infections and mortality, our results indicate that non-exclusive breastfeeding may be sufficient to support certain cognitive abilities.

Ideal EBF duration

While some studies propose a uniform, linear improvement in cognitive performance with longer durations of EBF1, our findings do not support this. Furthermore, there was no indication of an optimal EBF duration across cognitive domains or for outcomes at different child ages. While this might be restricted to the context of a high-income country with a rather highly educated sample62, these results suggest that the relationship between EBF and child cognition may be more nuanced than previously assumed.

Limitations/outlook

Whilst the focus of this study was to investigate effects of EBF, a limitation of this study is that the NEPS newborn cohort did not acquire information about partial breastfeeding or overall breastfeeding duration. It is known that longer overall breastfeeding duration affects child cognition2. Infants never receiving EBF may have had similar or longer overall breastfeeding durations than infants receiving EBF for any period, which could not be controlled for. Furthermore, EBF duration was assessed via self-report and retrospectively, which could have led to recall bias. Studies investigating recall bias in maternal report of EBF duration have found that EBF duration is overestimated, especially with longer recall periods63–65. Other methods such as 24-hour recall, 7-day recall or prospective assessment of EBF have been shown to be more accurate in assessing EBF, with prospective assessment being the most accurate66. Despite the tendency of overestimation of EBF duration, retrospective recall was found to be a valid method in a recent study with over 3000 participants, where recall accuracy after 12 months was at 77.9% (sensitivity = 98%, specificity = 64.4–81.8%)67. However, to ensure accuracy in EBF prevalence and duration, future studies should implement more prospective assessments.

In addition, cognition was assessed with individual tasks, rather than full neuropsychological test-batteries such as an intelligence scale. In the case of working memory, the NEPS study only included a task for phonological working memory, leaving out other components of working memory (i.e., visuo-spatial memory and central executive). Using full test-batteries would allow for analysis distinguishing between overall and test-specific effects of EBF.

As many other studies, the NEPS cohort has an elevated level of highly educated mothers62. Additionally, attrition rate across all four outcome measures was only 28%. Notably, families who continued to participate in more outcome measures had characteristics that were generally favorable for healthy child development and were associated with the outcomes measured, such as higher education and lower smoking rates. Since higher maternal education is generally associated with longer EBF duration68 and with higher study participation69, there may be a selection bias resulting in the over-representation of favorable family characteristics, which could limit the generalizability of the results to middle- and low-socio-economic settings. Despite this, in our data, EBF duration itself was not associated with the number of outcomes completed, indicating that it did not directly introduce selection bias.

Lastly, even though the sample for the low-risk children was large, our SVN sample remained rather small and consisted of mostly late preterm children. Also, we could not identify children born SGA. To identify children born SGA precise gestational age at birth (i.e., in weeks and days) and an exact birth weight are needed. As this precision of data was not available in the NEPS study (e.g., gestational age only in weeks), SGA infants could not be identified. The SVN sample thus was not representative of all risk groups and different grades of severity. As some studies suggest that effects of EBF may differ between preterm and LBW infants25,49, future studies should try to generate a SVN sample that is balanced for type of risk groups and severity.

Conclusion

While the WHO’s recommendation of six months of EBF is associated with improved infant health and reduced mortality, the present findings suggest that, in a high-income country with a highly educated population, adherence to this guideline does not appear to be associated with enhanced cognitive performance.
