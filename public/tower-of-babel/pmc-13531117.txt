Factors Affecting Postoperative Delirium in Patients With Colorectal Cancer
Authors: TOSHIO SHIRAISHI, TETSURO TOMINAGA, KOKI WAKATA, MASATO NISHIMUTA, MASAKI UTSUGI, HIDETOSHI FUKUOKA, MITSUTOSHI ISHII, KEISUKE NODA, SHINTARO HASHIMOTO, KAIDO OISHI, KOYA TAGUCHI, SHOSABURO OYAMA, AYANO INAO, TAKASHI NONAKA, KEITARO MATSUMOTO
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimAs the population ages, the number of patients with colorectal cancer (CRC) is increasing. It is also said that older adults are at higher risk of delirium. The aim of this study was to investigate risk factors for delirium after CRC surgery, and its impact on perioperative outcomes.Patients and MethodsIn this multicenter, retrospective, observational study, 6434 consecutive patients with CRC who underwent colorectal surgery were divided into two groups: those with delirium (n=50), and those without delirium (n=6384). Clinical features were compared between the groups.ResultsPatients in the delirium group were older (over 80 years: 62.0% vs. 23.2%, p<0.001), more commonly underwent open surgery (30.0% vs. 8.8%, p<0.001), had a shorter operative time (192 vs. 221 min, p=0.048) and lower rates of pathological T4 stage (12.0% vs. 29.3%, p=0.007). Multivariate analysis revealed age ≥80 years (odds ratio=4.904, 95% confidence interval=2.747-8.756; p<0.001) and open surgery (odds ratio=2.171, 95% confidence interval=1.087-4.336; p=0.028) as independent predictors of delirium.ConclusionElderly age and open surgery are risk factors for delirium after CRC surgery and are associated with longer hospital stays. In elderly patients, selecting the least invasive surgical procedure possible is crucial for preventing postoperative delirium.

Introduction

The incidence of colorectal cancer (CRC) is on the rise, ranking as the third most common cancer globally and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths (1). With aging of the population, the incidence rate of CRC is expected to continue increasing (2).

The first-line treatment for CRC is surgery. While curative resection offers the prospect of long-term survival, a certain proportion of patients experience postoperative complications that can adversely affect both short- and long-term prognosis (3). Delirium is one of the most commonly experienced complications. Delirium is a transient organic neuropsychiatric syndrome characterized by sudden cognitive impairment, altered levels of consciousness, and disturbances in sleep and arousal (4). Since previous reports have indicated that delirium is more likely to occur in the elderly (5), it is possible that its incidence rate will increase in the future (6).

However, to date, reports examining delirium in patients with CRC are limited, and most studies were conducted at a single center, with a scarcity of large-scale, multicenter studies (5, 7).

In this study, we used a multicenter database to investigate risk factors for delirium occurring after CRC surgery, and its impact on perioperative outcomes.

Patients and Methods

Consecutive patients with CRC who underwent colorectal surgery at seven participating hospitals in Japan between April 2016 and December 2024 were retrospectively reviewed. Patients who had synchronous colon cancer, emergency surgery, only stoma construction, recurrent tumors, and incomplete data were excluded. Finally, 6,434 patients were eligible for this study. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the clinical research review boards of all participating hospitals (Approval Number: 16062715-5), and informed consent was obtained prior to inclusion in the study, through an opt-out process on the website of each participating institution. The study was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

For the purpose of this study, delirium was defined as a condition in which the patient exhibited postoperative abnormalities in consciousness or cognitive function that required intervention by the hospital’s liaison team or the administration of medication. The study participants were divided into two groups: those with delirium (n=50), and those without delirium (n=6,384).

Clinicopathological and surgical features were compared between the two groups. The following data were collected: sex, age, body mass index, American Society of Anesthesiologists performance status, comorbidities, tumor location, neoadjuvant treatment, surgical approach, colonic stent insertion, clinical tumor/node/metastasis (TNM) status (3), operative time, blood loss, pathological T/N status, lymphovascular invasion, postoperative complications excluding delirium, 30-day mortality, and duration of hospital stay. Postoperative complications were defined as Clavien–Dindo grade ³3 complications that occurred within 30 days of primary surgery.

Statistical analysis was performed using Bell Curve for Excel software, version 4.02 (Social Survey Research Information Co., Tokyo, Japan). Data are presented as either the number of patients and percentage, or the median and range. Differences in continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Differences in categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test or the chi-squared test. Multivariate analysis using logistic regression analysis was used to identify risk factors for life-threatening complications. Variables with a value of p<0.05 in univariate analyses were included in the multivariate analysis. All values of p<0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Table I shows a comparison of clinical characteristics between the delirium and no delirium groups. Patients were older (over 80 years; 62.0% vs. 23.2%, p<0.001) and open surgery was more common (30.0% vs. 8.8%, p<0.001) in the group with delirium. Other factors, such as sex, body mass index, American Society of Anesthesiologists performance status, comorbidities, tumor location, neoadjuvant treatment, colonic stent insertion, and clinical TNM status were similar between the groups.

Table III shows the results of univariate and multivariate analyses for predictors of postoperative delirium. Univariate analysis revealed that age ≥80 years, open surgery, and longer operative time correlated with postoperative delirium. Multivariate analysis revealed age ≥80 years (odds ratio=4.904, 95% confidence interval=2.747-8.756; p<0.001) and open surgery (odds ratio=2.171, 95% confidence interval=1.087-4.336; p=0.028) as independent predictors of postoperative delirium.

Discussion

This multicenter study examined risk factors for postoperative delirium in patients with CRC, and their impact on perioperative outcomes. Multivariate analysis revealed advanced age and open surgery as independent predictors of postoperative delirium. Additionally, delirium significantly prolonged the duration of hospital stay, but did not affect 30-day mortality.

In this study, advanced age (≥80 years) was identified as one of the independent risk factors for delirium. Older adults are known to have lower skeletal muscle mass compared to younger adults (8). Mosk et al. reported that reduced skeletal muscle mass influenced the incidence of postoperative delirium in patients with CRC, suggesting that older adults may be more susceptible to delirium than younger adults (9). Additionally, the report of Mosk et al. indicated that poor nutritional status and impaired physiological function are associated with delirium in older adults (9). This is because deteriorating nutritional status leads to malnutrition and metabolic abnormalities, which can trigger delirium (10, 11). In fact, it has been reported that patients with poor nutritional status are approximately 2.8 times more likely to develop delirium than those with good nutritional status (12). Therefore, for elderly patients with poor nutritional status, preoperative nutritional monitoring, management, and counseling are crucial for preventing delirium (6).

Open surgery was another significant risk factor for delirium identified in our study. One possible explanation for this is postoperative pain. A previous report indicated that open surgery is associated with significantly greater postoperative pain, and requires greater amounts of analgesics compared to laparoscopic surgery (13). Cui et al. compared patients who developed delirium following CRC surgery with those who did not; in their study, multivariate analysis revealed that the peak level of postoperative pain was an independent risk factor for delirium (14). This was attributed to pain triggering neuroinflammatory responses, such as increased neuronal excitability and the release of inflammatory mediators (15). Neuroinflammatory responses cause delirium by impairing neural transmission and brain function. Furthermore, postoperative pain itself is a source of physical stress and may induce changes in the patient’s cognition and emotions (such as emotional instability, anxiety, and fear) (15). Another possible explanation is the difference in recovery of physical function following open surgery (16, 17). In a randomized controlled trial examining the outcomes of open and laparoscopic surgery for CRC in Japan, open surgery was associated with slower recovery of bowel motility and a tendency toward longer hospital stays compared to laparoscopic surgery (16). Lee et al. reported that reduced physical activity is also a risk factor for delirium and that improving physical activity can also lead to improvement of delirium (7). Furthermore, a few reports have suggested that an elevated level of C-reactive protein, a marker of acute systemic inflammation following surgery, is associated with the onset of delirium (18, 19). In this regard, laparoscopic surgery is considered useful for preventing delirium because it is minimally invasive, suppresses the rise in inflammatory markers, and helps prevent infectious complications (16).

Given these findings, we believe that performing minimally invasive surgery whenever possible, particularly in elderly patients with CRC, and promoting postoperative pain relief and recovery of physical function are key factors for preventing postoperative delirium.

Reportedly, the onset of delirium prolongs hospital stay and contributes to increased rates of dementia and mortality, particularly among the elderly (6, 20-22). Jin et al. reported that postoperative delirium prolonged the duration of hospitalization by 2-3 days, and increased the 30-day mortality rate to 7-10% (21). In our study, the group delirium had a significantly longer hospital stay (15 vs. 12 days), supporting the findings of previous reports. However, regarding 30-day mortality, no such deaths were observed in the group with delirium, and there were no significant differences between the two groups. This may be due to the response of the liaison team following the onset of delirium and improvements in recent pharmacological treatments for delirium.

Study limitations. Firstly, the number of patients with delirium was relatively small due to the retrospective nature of the study. Secondly, previous reports have indicated that the incidence of postoperative delirium ranges from 8.2% to 54% (23). In our study, the patient population consisted of those who had received liaison or treatment interventions for delirium; consequently, the proportion of patients with delirium was lower than in previous studies, and the number of such patients was also smaller.

Conclusion

Even when our study limitations are taken into account, advanced age and open surgery were found to be risk factors for postoperative delirium following CRC surgery, and were associated with longer hospital stays. For elderly patients, selecting the least invasive surgical procedure possible is crucial for preventing postoperative delirium.
