BACKGROUND

Adolescence is a critical period for the development of body image and self‐evaluation. During this period, individuals are particularly sensitive to peer comparisons and external evaluations, and visible physical differences or delayed pubertal development may have substantial psychological effects. Turner syndrome (TS) is a sex chromosome disorder resulting from the complete or partial absence of one X chromosome and affects approximately 1 in 2500 females. 1 The major physical characteristics of TS include short stature, webbed neck, cubitus valgus, and low‐set ears. 1 Furthermore, following the onset of puberty, ovarian insufficiency often causes delayed secondary sexual development, making physical differences from age‐matched peers more pronounced. 2 Thus, these physical and pubertal differences may become particularly salient during this developmental period.

Females with TS have been reported to experience psychosocial difficulties and mental health problems more frequently than the general population, although the nature and severity of these difficulties vary considerably among individuals. 3 , 4 Consistent with these findings, current clinical practice guidelines emphasize that the care of girls and women with TS should include both longitudinal medical follow‐up and comprehensive psychosocial support. 4

Several reports have described anorexia nervosa (AN) in individuals with TS, although the relationship between TS and AN remains unclear. 5 AN predominantly affects adolescent and young adult females and is characterized by an intense fear of gaining weight and a distorted perception of body weight or shape. 6 , 7 Its development is believed to involve multiple psychosocial factors in addition to biological predispositions. 6 , 7 , 8 From a developmental standpoint, the physical characteristics and delayed pubertal maturation associated with TS may form part of the psychosocial context in which body‐related concerns emerge during adolescence.

In this report, we describe a case of severe AN in an adolescent girl with TS and discuss potential developmental and psychosocial factors surrounding symptom onset, including implications for treatment and support based on the clinical course.

CASE PRESENTATION

This case involved a 16‐year‐old girl who was diagnosed with TS by genetic testing in early childhood and had received regular follow‐up care in a pediatric department since diagnosis. Although no delay in language development was observed, delayed motor development had been observed, and she received occupational therapy as part of early developmental support until preschool age. During elementary school, her academic performance was adequate, and she attended regular classes with no apparent difficulties in peer relationships.

After entering junior high school, she reported becoming increasingly conscious of physical differences from her female peers, including short stature, webbed neck, and delayed development of secondary sexual characteristics. Around the same time, Kaufmann therapy was initiated to address ovarian insufficiency and promote the development of secondary sexual characteristics. However, she described feeling confused and distressed by the rapid bodily changes that followed the initiation of treatment, and she did not receive ongoing psychosocial support during this period.

After entering high school, she developed an intense fear of gaining weight following a friend's comment that her “neck looked thick.” Following this comment, she initiated severe dietary restriction. Over the following 6 months, her body weight decreased by approximately 10 kg. Because of progressive weight loss, she was referred to our psychiatric department by the pediatrician who had been providing her regular follow‐up care. At the initial assessment in our outpatient clinic, her body weight was 28.5 kg, with a body mass index (BMI) of 13.6 kg/m2. Given the severity of her underweight and the need for intensive nutritional and medical management, inpatient treatment was considered necessary, and she was admitted directly from the outpatient clinic. Despite her severe underweight, she stated, “I still think I am overweight” and repeatedly expressed concern that increasing her food intake would result in weight gain. Pediatric and endocrine evaluations did not identify an endocrine abnormality or other organic condition, including a complication of TS, that could explain her weight loss. Given her severe dietary restriction, intense fear of weight gain, and persistent perception of being overweight despite severe underweight, she was diagnosed with AN according to the diagnostic criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision.

To better understand her neurocognitive profile, we reviewed the results of a previously administered Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children–Fourth Edition. Her full‐scale intelligence quotient was 84. Her verbal comprehension index (VCI = 108) was relatively higher than her perceptual reasoning index (PRI = 84), working memory index (WMI = 82), and processing speed index (PSI = 71).

During hospitalization, care was provided by a multidisciplinary team comprising psychiatrists, pediatricians, nurses, and a registered dietitian. The psychiatrists coordinated the overall treatment plan for AN and provided psychoeducation and cognitive‐based interventions. Pediatricians were consulted regarding her physical management and provided guidance based on her general medical condition and underlying TS. The dietitian developed the nutritional rehabilitation plan and provided dietary counseling to the patient and her family. Nurses provided day‐to‐day inpatient care and supported the implementation of the nutritional and psychosocial treatment plan.

Under this multidisciplinary treatment framework, oral intake was initiated at 600 kcal/day with careful monitoring for refeeding syndrome. Nutritional intake was gradually increased by approximately 200 kcal per week alongside dietary guidance. Frequent blood tests were conducted, and electrolyte abnormalities were corrected as needed while continuously monitoring her physical condition.

In parallel with medical treatment, psychoeducation was provided to both the patient and her family. This intervention aimed to enhance their understanding of AN and to help the patient recognize that her physical characteristics and pubertal development were related to TS and its treatment rather than to personal shortcomings. During these sessions, she discussed how her short stature, webbed neck, delayed pubertal development, and the bodily changes following the initiation of Kaufmann therapy had contributed to her feeling different from her peers. Moreover, a thought‐recording technique was used to help organize her negative automatic thoughts related to body weight and shape. For example, thoughts such as “I am afraid of gaining weight” and “I still think I am overweight” were discussed with the patient to clarify how these cognitions provoked anxiety and led to dietary restriction. During psychoeducation and dietary guidance, particular attention was paid to her cognitive profile, which was characterized by relatively strong verbal abilities; therefore, verbal explanations were added to complement diagrams and charts. No psychotropic medication was administered during hospitalization, and treatment consisted of nutritional rehabilitation and psychosocial interventions. Over the course of these interventions, she gradually became able to view her physical and pubertal differences in a less self‐critical manner, although concerns about body weight and shape persisted.

With continued intensive nutritional therapy, her body weight gradually increased, and her daily caloric intake eventually reached 2400 kcal/day. Her body weight improved to 33.6 kg (BMI, 16.0 kg/m2), and she was discharged on Day 91 of hospitalization. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

This report describes a case of severe AN in an adolescent girl with TS. Her core clinical manifestations, including severe dietary restriction, an intense fear of weight gain, and a persistent perception of being overweight despite severe underweight, were consistent with a typical presentation of adolescent AN. Pediatric and endocrine evaluations did not identify an endocrine abnormality or other organic condition, including a complication of TS, that could explain her weight loss. Therefore, this case does not demonstrate a pathophysiological mechanism for AN specific to TS. Rather, the clinical relevance of TS in this case was observed in the developmental and psychosocial context surrounding the onset and treatment of AN. The patient became increasingly conscious of physical and pubertal differences from her peers and described confusion and distress regarding bodily changes after initiation of Kaufmann therapy. Dietary restriction subsequently began after a peer commented that her neck looked thick. This temporal sequence suggests that these experiences may have been relevant to her body‐related concerns, although their specific contribution to the development of AN cannot be determined from a single case.

TS is characterized not only by physical features such as short stature and webbed neck but also by delayed secondary sexual development due to ovarian insufficiency. Consequently, physical and pubertal differences from age‐matched female peers may become more noticeable during adolescence. 1 , 2 Adolescence is also a developmental period during which body image and self‐evaluation are shaped, and sensitivity to peer comparison and external evaluation may increase. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 In this context, clinicians caring for adolescents with TS may need to consider how individuals experience these physical and developmental differences, particularly when body‐related concerns or disordered eating emerge.

Management of TS requires ongoing medical follow‐up for associated physical comorbidities, including cardiovascular disease, renal and thyroid dysfunction, and hearing impairment. 4 During adolescence, individuals with TS may also undergo treatments aimed at promoting growth and pubertal development, including growth hormone therapy and estrogen replacement therapy. 13 , 14 In the present case, the patient described confusion and distress regarding bodily changes after initiation of Kaufmann therapy. Body weight and shape can become particularly salient in self‐evaluation among individuals with eating disorders. 15 , 16 The patient also stated that she felt pleased that her efforts were reflected in her weight loss. This statement suggests that the ability to influence her body weight may have provided her with some sense of control. In the context of her distress regarding bodily changes, dietary restriction may therefore have acquired a compensatory meaning by providing a sense of control, although this interpretation remains speculative. Previous reports have also described cases of TS in which AN developed after initiation of estrogen replacement therapy. 5 , 17 However, neither these reports nor the temporal sequence observed in the present case establishes a causal relationship between hormone therapy and AN. Psychosocial support for individuals with TS is recommended alongside medical management of associated physical complications. 4 , 18 , 19 Females with TS have been reported to experience psychosocial difficulties and mental health problems more frequently than the general population, 3 , 4 highlighting the importance of appropriate psychosocial assessment and support. International clinical guidelines recommend developmentally tailored psychoeducation for patients and their families, including information about TS‐related physical characteristics, expected pubertal changes, and treatment courses such as growth hormone therapy and estrogen replacement therapy. 4 , 18 , 19 These recommendations may be particularly relevant during adolescence, when concerns about appearance, pubertal development, and peer comparison may become more salient.

Cognitive assessment can also inform treatment planning for adolescents with AN, irrespective of the presence of TS. Individuals with TS are generally reported to have relatively preserved overall intellectual and verbal abilities, although difficulties in visuospatial processing, executive function, and social cognition may be present. 20 , 21 In the present patient, verbal comprehension was relatively strong (VCI, 108), whereas perceptual reasoning (PRI, 84), working memory (WMI, 82), and particularly processing speed (PSI, 71) were relatively lower. In particular, the low processing speed score should be interpreted cautiously, as it may have been influenced by the patient's physical condition at the time of testing. The clinically relevant implication was therefore not to assume a uniform cognitive profile associated with TS, but to tailor communication and psychoeducation to the patient's individual pattern of cognitive strengths and weaknesses.

TS did not necessitate a separate nutritional rehabilitation protocol in this case, and the core treatment was consistent with standard management of severe AN. The clinical relevance of TS was reflected primarily in the individualized content and delivery of psychosocial support, rather than in modification of the core AN treatment.

Psychoeducation was a central component of treatment. It aimed not only to enhance the patient's understanding of AN but also to place her TS‐related physical characteristics and pubertal development in an understandable developmental context. These physical characteristics were explained as innate traits rather than as consequences of personal failure or insufficient effort, with the aim of reducing negative self‐evaluation. In addition, a thought‐recording technique was used to identify negative automatic thoughts related to body weight and shape, 22 , 23 including “I am afraid of gaining weight” and “I still think I am overweight,” and to clarify their relationships with anxiety and restrictive eating behaviors. Together with the patient, we examined the evidence supporting these thoughts and explored alternative interpretations. During psychoeducation and dietary guidance, verbal explanations were used to complement diagrams and charts because her verbal comprehension was relatively strong compared with her perceptual reasoning, working memory, and processing speed indices. This adaptation was based on her individual cognitive profile rather than on the diagnosis of TS alone. During the course of these multifaceted interventions, stable dietary intake was achieved. This favorable course may have reflected the alignment between her principal treatment targets and the interventions provided: nutritional rehabilitation addressed severe malnutrition, while individualized psychoeducation and cognitive interventions supported her understanding of AN and addressed maladaptive weight‐ and shape‐related cognitions. Delivering these psychosocial interventions in a manner tailored to her cognitive profile may also have supported treatment engagement and weight restoration.

Psychotropic medication was not initiated because no clear comorbid psychiatric condition or target symptom requiring pharmacotherapy was identified. Her clinical improvement and maintenance of body weight for 18 months after discharge without psychotropic medication suggest the potential value of nutritional rehabilitation, psychoeducation, and cognitive interventions tailored to her clinical presentation and cognitive profile. However, this clinical course does not establish the effectiveness of any individual intervention, and pharmacotherapy remains an option when clinically indicated.

Taken together, this case highlights the importance of assessing individual experiences of physical and pubertal development, body‐related concerns, and peer interactions when an adolescent with TS develops AN. These factors may be clinically relevant when planning individualized psychosocial support, although their contribution to the onset of AN remains uncertain.

Several limitations should be noted. First, as this report describes a single case, the findings cannot be generalized, and no firm conclusions can be drawn regarding the mechanisms underlying the co‐occurrence of TS and AN or the effectiveness of specific interventions. Second, although the WISC‐IV provided a quantitative assessment of the patient's cognitive profile, standardized measures of body image, self‐evaluation, distress associated with pubertal development or hormone therapy, and other psychosocial factors were not administered. Therefore, interpretation of her subjective experiences was based primarily on the clinical course and her reported statements. Third, although her body weight was maintained for 18 months after discharge, longer term outcomes and relapse risk remain uncertain. Finally, the potential influence of cultural background and family environment could not be fully evaluated. Therefore, the clinical implications derived from this case should be considered exploratory. Further accumulation of carefully characterized cases is required to clarify whether and how TS‐related developmental experiences may be clinically relevant when AN occurs.

In conclusion, this case illustrates that experiences related to physical and pubertal development may form part of the individual psychosocial context surrounding the onset and treatment of adolescent AN in individuals with TS. Clinicians treating adolescents with TS and AN should assess patients' subjective experiences of bodily development, body‐related concerns, and peer interactions and incorporate these findings into standard AN treatment and individualized psychosocial support.
