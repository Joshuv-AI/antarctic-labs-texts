Microglia degrade Alzheimer’s amyloid-beta deposits extracellularly via digestive exophagy
Authors: Rudy G. Jacquet, Fernando González Ibáñez, Katherine Picard, Lucy Funes, Mohammadparsa Khakpour, Gunnar K. Gouras, Marie-Ève Tremblay, Frederick R. Maxfield, Santiago Solé-Domènech
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYHow microglia digest Alzheimer’s fibrillar amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques that are too large to be phagocytosed is not well understood. Here, we show that primary microglial cells create acidic extracellular compartments, lysosomal synapses, on model plaques and digest them with exocytosed lysosomal enzymes. This mechanism, called digestive exophagy, is confirmed by electron microscopy in 5xFAD mouse brains, which shows that a lysosomal enzyme, acid phosphatase, is secreted toward the plaques in structures resembling lysosomal synapses. Signaling studies demonstrate that the PI3K-AKT pathway modulates the formation of lysosomal synapses, as inhibition of PI3K1β or AKT1/2 reduces both lysosome exocytosis and actin polymerization, both required for the formation of the compartments. Finally, we show that small fibrils of Aβ previously internalized and trafficked to lysosomes are exocytosed toward large Aβ aggregates by microglia. Thus, the release of lysosomal contents during digestive exophagy may also contribute to the spread and growth of fibrillar Aβ.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative condition generally associated with the accumulation of large aggregates of amyloid-beta (Aβ) in the brain.1 Microglia, the immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS), maintain brain homeostasis by phagocytosing apoptotic cells,2 responding to injury,3 and refining synaptic structure and function.4 How microglia engage and interact with pathological Aβ aggregates has gathered significant attention, mainly driven by evidence brought by genome-wide association studies.5 In the AD brain, microglia surround and engage Aβ plaques in an attempt to contain or digest6–9 their diffuse outer layers, which have been described as sources of neurotoxic oligomeric Aβ species.10–12 Oligomeric Aβ triggers and accelerates Aβ deposition13 and has been reported to induce mitochondrial,14,15 blood-brain barrier, and neurovascular16 dysfunction. During normal aging and in AD pathology, the microglial population transitions from a homeostatic state to disease-associated states, and their neuroprotective barrier-like capacity declines.7 Consistently, in adult AD mouse models, depletion of microglia or reduction in chemotaxis toward Aβ via TREM-2 or TAM (Axl and Mer) receptor ablation led to an increase in diffuse plaque morphologies, neuronal dystrophy, and dendritic spine loss.9,10,17,18 In contrast, reduction of microglial density in young 5xFAD mice (without Aβ pathology at the time of treatment) led to impaired Aβ plaque formation,8 suggesting an additional role played by these cells in initiating Aβ deposition and/or spread.

Human clinical trials have shown that anti-Aβ monoclonal antibodies, which presumably work through microglia,19 can reduce plaque buildup in patients with AD.20,21 Microglia can internalize low-molecular-weight, soluble forms of Aβ by fluidphase pinocytosis,22,23 and small fibrils of Aβ (about 100 nm in length) are taken up by receptor-mediated endocytosis.24 Aβ plaques, however, are complex multi-component aggregates embedded in the brain’s extracellular matrix and are often much larger than microglia,25 which would dramatically impede microglial phagocytosis. How microglia trim and/or degrade these aggregates that cannot be phagocytosed is unknown.

Previously, we described a mechanism used by macrophages to digest objects that are too large to be phagocytosed, such as large lipoprotein aggregates similar to those seen in atherosclerotic plaques26–28 or dead adipocytes.29 To digest these objects, macrophages create tightly sealed regions on the target, into which protons and lysosomal contents are secreted. These compartments, which are called lysosomal synapses, are able to degrade extracellular aggregates and dead cells. This process has been called digestive exophagy, and it is very similar to the well-known degradative mechanisms used by osteoclasts during bone resorption.30

In this study, we examined whether microglia use digestive exophagy to degrade large Aβ deposits. We found that primary murine microglial cells contacting large synthetic Aβ1–42 aggregates form lysosomal synapses, creating contact areas or compartments into which lysosomal contents are released by exocytosis. The pH inside the compartments was found to be acidic, which would facilitate degradation by lysosomal enzymes. We demonstrated that microglial cells use digestive exophagy to degrade surface-attached Aβ1–42 aggregates that could not be phagocytosed. We also found that the PI3K-AKT pathway, which regulates digestive exophagy of aggregated low-density lipoprotein (agLDL) by macrophages,31 also modulates digestive exophagy of Aβ1–42 aggregates by microglia. Moreover, using electron microscopy (EM), we provide evidence of microglial digestive exophagy in the brain of 5xFAD mice. Specifically, regions between microglia and Aβ deposits showed the presence of acid phosphatase activity (a lysosomal enzyme), indicating secretion of lysosomal contents into contact areas. Finally, we found that fibrillar Aβ1–42 that was previously internalized by microglial cells is released toward extracellular Aβ1–42 aggregates upon contact with the cells, suggesting that microglia could also spread or grow Aβ during digestive exophagy. Overall, our findings provide strong indications for a mechanism, known as digestive exophagy by which microglia partially digest large Aβ aggregates extracellularly.

RESULTS

Microglial cells capture portions of Aβ deposits in partially sealed compartments into which lysosomal enzymes are secreted

To determine whether microglia form lysosomal synapses upon contact with Aβ deposits, we imaged large synthetic aggregates of Aβ1–42 co-incubated with primary microglial cells. Aβ components of senile plaques have been shown to adopt β-sheet structures,25,32 and we verified that our synthetic aggregates possessed equivalent β-sheet features using EM and circular dichroism33 (Figure S1). After 90 min of co-incubation at 37°C, microglial plasma membranes were stained with cholera toxin B,34 and the preparations were imaged using high-resolution Airyscan confocal microscopy.35 We found that microglial cells partially engulfed portions of the Aβ1–42 aggregates (Figure 1A). 3D reconstruction of the compartments revealed that these structures were partially sealed, with portions of the aggregates surrounded by microglial plasma membrane (Figures 1B and 1C, white arrowheads). Identical preparations stained with phalloidin showed increased actin polymerization at microglia-Aβ aggregate contact regions (Figure 1D, white arrowheads). 3D reconstruction revealed membrane structures, positive for F-actin, partially engulfing the aggregates, suggesting a required involvement of actin polymerization for the formation of the lysosomal synapses (Figures 1E and 1F).

In previous studies of macrophage digestion of agLDL, we found that lysosomal contents were secreted into lysosomal synapses.27,29 To test whether microglia release lysosomal contents into the compartments contacting Aβ1–42 aggregates, we loaded microglial lysosomes by endocytosis of biotin-fluorescein-dextran36 and then co-incubated the cells with large extracellular Aβ1–42 aggregates labeled with streptavidin and Alexa 647. A flowchart for the experiment is depicted in Figure 1G. After incubation, we blocked all remaining unbound streptavidin sites with excess unlabeled biotin and BSA and fixed and permeabilized the cells using Triton X-100, which released any remaining lysosomal dextran. Thus, only fluorescein signal corresponding to secreted dextran remained attached to the aggregates. Confocal microscopy revealed biotin-fluorescein-dextran deposition onto the streptavidin-Aβ aggregates, specifically in areas in contact with microglial cells (Figure 1H). The labeled areas were adjacent to cell membranes, showing that lysosomal dextran was secreted into the extracellular compartment encapsulating the aggregates. When microglial cells were co-incubated with both streptavidin-Aβ aggregates and 6-μm-diameter streptavidin-coated fluorescent purple beads, the exocytosed lysosomal fluorescein-dextran was almost exclusively secreted toward the aggregates (Figures 1I and 1J; statistical analyses in Table S2).

Overall, these results indicate that primary microglial cells form partially sealed surface-connected compartments—lysosomal synapses—in contact with large Aβ aggregates, into which lysosomal contents are exocytosed.

Lysosomal synapses are acidic

To determine whether lysosomal synapses become acidified, Aβ1–42 aggregates labeled with pH-sensitive probes and Alexa 633 (pH-independent) were incubated with microglial cells, and pH was measured at cell-aggregate contact sites using quantitative live ratiometric imaging. To measure pH we used acidic pH indicator dye (ApHID), a pH-sensitive dye with a pKa near 5 and increasing brightness with acidity,37 or fluorescein. In regions devoid of cell-aggregate contacts, ApHID fluorescence was dim, corresponding to its lower fluorescence at pH 7.4. However, in areas where the cells formed compartments in contact with the aggregates, ApHID fluorescence increased (Figure 2A, compartments indicated by arrowheads). Ratiometric pH imaging of Aβ1–42 aggregates labeled with fluorescein and Alexa 633 confirmed this finding. Fluorescein has weak fluorescence at acidic pH, and the fluorescein/Alexa 633 ratio, measured on Aβ aggregates incubated with buffers, increases with alkalinity (Figure 2B). The resulting ratio vs. pH curve can be fit to a sigmoid (Figure 2C and Table S3). The pKa of fluorescein, when bound to Aβ, was slightly higher than that of the free probe (pKa 7.17 vs. 6.4338; Table S3), which is likely explained by the effect of Aβ net-negative charge. When imaged live, aggregates that had not been contacted by the cells showed bright fluorescein signal, indicating pH near 7.4. However, at cell-aggregate contacts, fluorescein intensity decreased dramatically, indicating acidic pH (Figures 2D–2G, acidified compartments indicated by arrowheads). To quantify the acidity of lysosomal synapses, we measured fluorescein/Alexa 633 ratios for individual compartments and interpolated these to pH values using a ratio-to-pH calibration as in Figure 2C. We found the lysosomal synapse pH to be in the range of pH 5.5 to 6.5. Microglial cells treated with bafilomycin A1 were no longer able to acidify the compartments, indicating a role played by the V-ATPase proton pump in their acidification (Figure 2H; statistical analyses in Table S4).

These results demonstrate that primary microglial cells establish lysosomal synapses at cell-aggregate contact sites into which lysosomal contents are secreted. Lysosomal synapses are acidic in nature, as verified by live ratiometric pH imaging using two different pH-sensitive dyes, and their acidification is dependent on V-ATPase proton pumps. These observations are consistent with digestive exophagy.

Microglial cells degrade surface-attached Aβ1–42 aggregates by digestive exophagy

We next studied how the formation of lysosomal synapses contributes to the degradation of Aβ1–42 aggregates. To investigate this, we linked biotinylated fluorescein-Aβ1–42 aggregates on a streptavidin-coated coverslip (see STAR Methods) and measured degradation by primary microglial cells in real time. The multivalent streptavidin-biotin linkages prevented microglial cells from phagocytosing the aggregates, but the cells could use digestive exophagy for their degradation.

Microglial cells were seeded on top of the surface-attached aggregates for 1 h to allow engagement with the amyloid. To measure degradation, we monitored fluorescein intensity of the aggregates over time by live confocal microscopy in a sterile chamber at 37°C with 5% CO2. After imaging of the 1-h time point, the samples were immediately returned to the incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 and imaged again every 24 h for three successive days, using the same laser intensity and detector gain, for the same areas demarcated by a grid. A final imaging session was completed at 7 days of incubation. We controlled for the possibility of daily changes in laser power for each imaging session by imaging fluorescent beads after each recording and normalizing the fluorescence accordingly.

To quantify undigested fluorescein-Aβ1–42 aggregates, we applied a threshold to the fluorescein channel that allowed for the selection of aggregates. The same threshold was applied to all images acquired for a given experiment for all time points. Intracellular fluorescein signal was subtracted from the images by a mask generated using the transmitted light image. We measured fluorescein signal per field and normalized it to Nile red bead fluorescence for each corresponding time point. Our data show that microglial cells engage and degrade surface-attached aggregates of various sizes over 72 h (Figures 3A–3D), but near-complete degradation is not achieved until 7 days (Figures 3E and 3K). Overall, we found that 64% and 78% of all aggregates were completely degraded by microglial cells after 72 h and 7 days of incubation, respectively, with about one-third of the degradation occurring in the first 24 h (Figure 3K; statistical analyses in Table S5). Larger aggregates with diameters ≥10 μm (29 aggregates quantified; smaller aggregates were filtered out by a size threshold using digital image analysis) were partially degraded by the cells over time (Figures 3F–3J), with the greatest degradation (43%) also occurring in the first 24 h (Figure 3L; statistical analyses in Table S6). In the absence of microglial cells, surface-attached aggregates incubated over a 7-day period (Figures 3M and 3N) showed no significant change in fluorescein intensity (Figure 3O; statistical analyses in Table S7). For images corresponding to intermediate 1-h, 24-h, 48-h,72-h, and 7-day time points in absence of microglia, see Figure S2.

These results indicate that primary microglial cells can degrade Aβ1–42 aggregates that have been strongly attached to glass surfaces using biotin-streptavidin. The aggregates, some larger than the cells themselves, were partially or fully degraded without initial cellular internalization, which is consistent with extracellular degradation by digestive exophagy.

Inhibition of the PI3K1β and AKT1/2 pathway reduces lysosomal exocytosis and actin polymerization during digestive exophagy

We examined the signaling mechanisms involved in the formation of lysosomal synapses by primary microglial cells. We targeted PI3K1β, AKT1, and AKT2, which were previously shown to positively regulate digestive exophagy in macrophages contacting large LDL aggregates.31 We treated the cells with TGX-221, which inhibits the class IA P110β catalytic subunit of PI3K,39 and AKT1/2i, which inhibits AKT1 and AKT2.40

We first measured the effect of PI3K or AKT inhibition on lysosome exocytosis. Microglial lysosomes were labeled with biotin-fluorescein-dextran as previously described. The cells were pretreated with TGX-221, AKT1/2i, or control DMSO for 1 h and incubated with streptavidin-Alexa 647-Aβ1–42 aggregates for 90 min in the presence of inhibitors or DMSO. Next, streptavidin not bound to secreted dextran-biotin was blocked by adding excess unlabeled biotin, and the cells were fixed and permeabilized with Triton X-100 with excess unlabeled biotin and BSA. We found that deposition of biotin-fluorescein-dextran onto the aggregates was significantly reduced when microglial cells were treated with inhibitors, relative to control (Figures 4A–4C and 4G; statistical analyses in Table S8). We also measured the effect of inhibition of PI3K and AKT on actin polymerization. Microglial cells were pretreated with TGX-221, AKT1/2i, or DMSO for 1 , followed by incubation with Alexa 647-Aβ1–42 aggregates for 1 h. The cells were then fixed and stained with Alexa 488-phalloidin. We found a reduction in actin polymerization at microglia-aggregate contact sites for microglial cells treated with inhibitors, relative to control (Figures 4D–4F and 4H; statistical analyses in Table S9).

Altogether, these results show that PI3K1β and AKT1/2 inhibition reduce lysosome exocytosis and actin polymerization during microglial digestive exophagy of large Aβ1–42 aggregates.

Microglia form compartments filled with acid phosphatase in close proximity to Aβ deposits in the brains of 5xFAD mice

Lysosomal synapses formed during digestive exophagy receive hydrolytic enzymes due to lysosomal exocytosis.41 Acid phosphatase is a ubiquitous lysosomal enzyme that hydrolyzes organic phosphates at acidic pH.42 Due to its abundance, it is commonly used to identify lysosomes and related organelles using EM.43 To visualize lysosomal synapses in brain tissue, we prepared brain sections from 5xFAD mice, a model that recapitulates brain Aβ deposition and associated neuropathology,44,45 and stained them for acid phosphatase. Stained sections were processed for scanning EM visualization with methods previously used by us46 and others.47

Before fixation by intracardiac perfusion, mice were injected in vivo with Methoxy-X04, which fluorescently labels brain Aβ deposits and facilitates the identification of plaques when selecting sections for processing without being visible in EM.48 Imaging of unstained brain sections from a 13-month-old 5xFAD male revealed microglia in close proximity to large Aβ aggregates, forming pockets of extracellular space directly adjacent to fibrillar material (Figures 5A and 5B, red arrowheads). The fibrillar Aβ plaques associated with microglial cell bodies were identified by their heterogeneous fibrils.46,48 When sections of the same animal were stained for acid phosphatase, similar compartments appeared decorated with intense electron-dense material. This corresponds to the lead phosphate precipitate formed by hydrolysis of β-glycerophosphate in the presence of a soluble lead salt, which is indicative of acid phosphatase activity and supports the evidence of lysosomal synapses at the microglia-Aβ deposit interface (Figures 5C and 5D, red arrowheads). Stained brain sections for 15-month-old (Figures 5E and 5F) and 8-month-old (Figures 5G–5I) 5xFAD females also showed extracellular space pockets positive for acid phosphatase activity adjacent to large Aβ aggregates (red arrowheads).

The presence of lead ions at concentrations similar to those used for Gomori-type phosphatase localization might cause staining of some organelles and membranes, as has been reported for parts of the vacuolar system, including the Golgi complex.49,50 To account for these, control staining for acid phosphatase was performed in parallel on tissue sections treated with the same reagents and buffers used in the staining for acid phosphatase, but either replacing β-glycerophosphate with water or by incubating the tissues with Tris maleate buffer alone. Sections from an 8-month-old 5xFAD male stained for acid phosphatase in the absence of β-glycerophosphate revealed similar pockets of extracellular space, adjacent to Aβ aggregates, but with absent or much weaker staining (Figures 5J and 5K [red arrowheads]; Figure S3). By contrast, when matched sections for the same animal were stained in the presence of β-glycerophosphate, many similar compartments showed increased electron-dense material indicative of acid phosphatase activity (Figures 5L and 5M, red arrowheads). Additional examples of acid phosphatase control staining in the absence of β-glycerophosphate for the 15-month-old female and the 8-month-old male and female are shown in Figure S3. In general, in the absence of β-glycerophosphate, we found some examples of stained extracellular space pockets at microglial cell-Aβ deposit interfaces but with much weaker signal relative to staining with substrate condition. Sections incubated with Tris maleate buffer alone (no lead nitrate) were devoid of staining (Figure S4).

To confirm the occurrence of lysosomal synapses of microglial origin, we only included in our analysis images in which the microglial cell bodies were visible and in direct contact with Aβ plaques. This revealed acid phosphatase signal clearly associated with the microglial cell body juxtaposing Aβ plaques. The pattern of the staining was first examined near the tissue-resin border, at the surface of the sections where penetration of the reagents and their detection are the strongest.

Overall, these results, independently replicated in four animals (two males and two females), demonstrate acid phosphatase staining within ultrastructural compartments located at the interface of microglia and Aβ aggregates. These structures are highly consistent with the features of the hydrolytic lysosomal synapses.

Microglial cells secrete previously endocytosed Aβ fibrils toward large Aβ aggregates during digestive exophagy

Microglia in the brain of patients with AD show an increase in phagocytic markers, in some cases only when they associate with Aβ plaques (reviewed in Hopperton et al.51). Primary murine microglial cells were similarly shown to accumulate fibrillar Aβ material in lysosomes (reviewed in Sole-Domenech et al.52). During digestive exophagy, lysosomal synapses receive not only lysosomal enzymes but all lysosomal contents released during lysosomal exocytosis. Previously endocytosed fibrillar Aβ material, which is very difficult to digest by microglia in normal conditions,22,53,54 should also get released into the lysosomal synapse. This could promote Aβ plaque growth and provide a possible mechanism by which microglia contribute to plaque spread, which has been suggested previously.8,55

To test this hypothesis, microglial cells with lysosomes previously loaded with Aβ1–42 nanofibrils were incubated with Aβ1–42 aggregates and imaged by confocal microscopy. The cells were treated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA), a protein kinase C (PKC) activator.56 PMA treatment was found to increase microglial lysosomal exocytosis of dextrans toward extracellular Aβ1–42 aggregates (Figures 6A–6C; statistical analyses in Table S10). Aβ1–42 nanofibrils were prepared by sonication of larger Aβ1–42 aggregates53 labeled with fluorescein and verified by negative-stain EM (Figures 6D and 6E). Following sonication, only nanofibrils and small circular structures were seen. Some larger structures were occasionally observed, but the vast majority of fibrils were less than 200 nm in length, which would allow for their endocytosis. Microglial cells were incubated with nanofibrils overnight and chased for at least 2 h to ensure lysosomal localization. The cells were then co-incubated with large extracellular Alexa 647-Aβ1–42 aggregates for 45–180 min at 37°C. Microglial lysosomes appeared loaded with abundant fibrillar fluorescein nanofibrils. Upon contact with large aggregates, the cells formed structures at cell-aggregate contact sites that morphologically resembled lysosomal synapses. These compartments became heavily decorated with fluorescein nanofibrils secreted from the lysosomes (Figure 6F and orthogonal views, arrowheads). Microglial cells treated with PMA formed somewhat larger compartments and released large amounts of nanofibrils, which on some occasions decorated a considerable fraction of the extracellular aggregate (Figure 6G and orthogonal views, arrowheads).

Overall, these results indicate that previously endocytosed lysosomal contents, including fibrillar Aβ1–42, can be released into lysosomal synapses during digestive exophagy, which may contribute to plaque spread and/or growth.

DISCUSSION

In the current study, we investigated whether microglia use digestive exophagy to digest large Aβ aggregates. Using high-resolution Airyscan confocal microscopy, we found that primary murine microglial cells incubated with synthetic Aβ1–42 aggregates formed surface-connected compartments that closely resembled those established by macrophages contacting agLDL. At cell-aggregate contact sites, microglial cells recruited and polymerized actin. Lysosomal contents were exocytosed into the compartments, and live confocal ratiometric pH imaging revealed that these surface-connected structures were acidic in nature. Furthermore, both actin polymerization and lysosomal exocytosis were reduced following PI3K and AKT inhibition in microglia. We previously observed the same cellular features and signaling mechanisms during macrophage digestive exophagy of agLDL26,57 and dead adipocytes.29 In macrophages, digestive exophagy is modulated by Toll-like receptors, Myd88, PI3K, and AKT,31,57 and in vivo ablation of NADPH oxidase 2 or Toll-like receptor 2 leads to failure to clear dead adipocytes, resulting in systemic pathology.58 During bone resorption mediated by osteoclasts—another myeloid cell type—these cells establish sealed compartments, lacunae, in contact with the bone. These compartments are strikingly similar to lysosomal synapses in that they are also acidic in nature59 and receive lysosomal enzymes that allow for bone degradation and resorption.30 We believe that cells of the myeloid lineage use digestive exophagy as a general mechanism to degrade objects that are too large to be phagocytosed.

To demonstrate degradation of Aβ1–42 aggregates under conditions that impede endocytosis or phagocytosis, we attached biotinylated Aβ1–42 aggregates to streptavidin-coated coverslips and measured their degradation by microglial cells over a period of 7 days. In the absence of microglial cells, the aggregates remained immobilized and unchanged in size for a week. However, when microglial cells were added, we observed progressive clearance of the surface-immobilized aggregates. This demonstrates that the degradation occurred extracellularly, which is the main feature of digestive exophagy, and this is consistent with earlier studies showing microglial trimming and compaction of Aβ1–42 deposits in vivo.18

To demonstrate digestive exophagy of Aβ plaques by microglia in situ, we prepared brain sections of 5xFAD model mice and stained them for acid phosphatase, an abundantly expressed lysosomal enzyme. We chose acid phosphatase staining over immunogold methods against other lysosomal enzymes such as LAMP1 or TPP1 to minimize nonspecific staining. Given that brain phagocytes have been shown to be a source of high acid phosphatase activity,60,61 we expected acid phosphatase signal to have a predominantly microglial origin. Moreover, despite the massive accumulation of axonal lysosomes in dystrophic neurites, the majority of these organelles reside within swollen neuronal axons, which are deficient in lysosomal proteases62 as well as being easily identifiable by EM. We imaged the stained preparations using scanning EM, and we only included in our analysis images in which the microglial cell bodies were visible and in clear contact with Aβ plaques, revealing acid phosphatase signal clearly associated with the microglial cell body. At microglia-Aβ interfaces, microglia formed pockets of extracellular space adjacent to or in close proximity to Aβ deposits, and several of these compartments exhibited considerable acid phosphatase activity, which is consistent and supportive of digestive exophagy. These structures appear morphologically very similar to the lysosomal synapses observed in cell culture. We replicated these results independently in four animals. This is consistent with structures previously described as amyloid stars63 or deep membrane invaginations formed by microglia toward Aβ deposits,64 which were also observed by EM in human and mouse brain tissue, respectively.

In addition to plaque degradation, it has been suggested that microglia also play a role in promoting Aβ deposition.9,65 In the absence of pro-inflammatory stimuli,54,66 microglial cells degrade fibrillar Aβ material only very slowly, over the course of several days,22 which we showed here too. Digestive exophagy is therefore most likely not sufficient for rapid, efficient degradation of large Aβ aggregates. Previously, we observed that microglial cells can release undigested soluble forms of Aβ after endocytosis.22 In the current study, we preloaded microglial cells with aggregated Aβ1–42 nanofibrils, which are resistant to proteolysis in normal conditions. We found that lysosomal exocytosis triggered by interaction with large Aβ aggregates led to secretion of fibrillar material. The secreted intralysosomal fibrillar Aβ was deposited on the surface of the aggregates in structures resembling lysosomal synapses. This would provide a mechanism by which microglia contribute to amyloid growth. Digestive exophagy could thus constitute a double-edged sword: extracellular degradation would help trim portions of large aggregates, but secretion of indigestible fibrillar Aβ inside lysosomal synapses could contribute to plaque growth.

A recent paper by the Meyer-Luehmann lab demonstrated that microglia act as carriers of Aβ pathology in previously unaffected brain tissue.55 Moreover, microglia have been found near tau inclusions in various neurodegenerative diseases and have been shown to internalize and digest tau seeds in an inefficient manner, which may contribute to the spread of tau pathology.67,68 As a consequence of the enhanced lysosomal exocytosis upon contact with Aβ plaques, the microglia, which are highly mobile cells under some circumstances, could also contribute to the spread of neuropathic fibrils such as tau or α-synuclein.69,70 It has been shown that some Aβ deposits might have an intraneuronal origin, driven by failure in lysosomal function, autolysosomal acidification, and overall autophagy.71,72 Based on our hypothesis, microglial contribution to Aβ spread via digestive exophagy or other mechanisms involving lysosomal71–73 or synaptic74,75 dysfunction would be independent of the origin (intraneuronal or extracellular) of these aggregates.

In summary, this study provides evidence that microglia can use digestive exophagy to digest large Aβ plaques—an important process for which there has not been an adequate cell biological mechanism. During this process, microglia form lysosomal synapses to encapsulate a portion of plaques and degrade them with lysosomal enzymes secreted by lysosome exocytosis. It is interesting to speculate that enhanced digestive exophagy may be a mechanism by which anti-Aβ monoclonal antibodies can promote clearance of Aβ plaques in humans.19,76,77

Limitations of the study

The main component of brain Alzheimer’s aggregates is Aβ1–42, which is responsible for plaque β-sheet structure and aggregation.1 Microglial response to Aβ is specific and mediated by a plethora of phagocytic membrane receptors, such as SRAs,24 TAMs (via Gas6 ligand),9 TREM2,9,78,79 PIEZO1 mechanoreceptors,80 and a variety of Fc receptors such as CDs and Toll-like receptors (reviewed by Doens and Fernandez81). Our model plaques are primarily composed of aggregated Aβ1–42. Despite lacking damaged cellular components, cytokines, and other pro-inflammatory components, microglial cells still responded to our model Aβ aggregates and were able to form lysosomal synapses and slowly digest them. Microglial response could very well have been stronger had the cells been exposed to native, multi-component brain Alzheimer’s Aβ plaques instead. Nevertheless, there is a need for new Aβ aggregate models that mimic the native properties of brain Aβ plaques. We are currently exploring the preparation of multi-component Aβ aggregates and aim to conduct some of the experiments using organotypic brain slices from Alzheimer’s pathology mouse models.

It will be important in the future to characterize digestive exophagy in the living CNS using animal models, but we needed initial cell-culture studies to provide guidance on what to look for in the much more complex in vivo studies. Primary murine microglial cell-culture models lack essential CNS components and acquire an ameboid phagocytic profile, resembling microglia in injured tissue (reviewed by Pesti et al.82). However, plaque-associated brain microglia also display ameboid and phagocytic morphologies, which may not be too distant from our in vitro model. With that in mind, we have provided initial EM observations in an AD pathology mouse model brain that were consistent with our cellular work. Limitations associated with the EM imaging we performed also pertain to the small samples imaged and limited penetration of the staining in the samples, thus preventing quantitative ultrastructural analyses of lysosomal synapses. The imaging was also performed in two dimensions, using high-throughput chip mapping scanning EM, which did not allow resolution of the 3D geometry of the lysosomal synapses. In future studies, we will continue to characterize at the nanoscale the process of digestive exophagy using 3D scanning EM. Furthermore, to extend our findings, we are currently designing intravital experiments aiming to visualize lysosomal synapses in the living mouse brain.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Primary murine microglial cell extraction and culture

Murine microglia were extracted from wildtype C57BL/6J neonate mouse brains (P1-P3). Pups were decapitated and entire brains were dissected and placed in CMF-PBS buffer, composed of PBS, 2 g/L of glucose, and 0.04 g/L of sodium bicarbonate (Sigma-Aldrich, pn# S-5761), kept cold at 4°C. Brain meninges were removed under a stereotaxic microscope using blunt forceps, and cortices were isolated and cut in smaller pieces with a scalpel, followed by incubation in CMF-PBS containing 10 mg/mL of trypsin (Worthington biochemical, pn# LS003707), 1 mg/mL DNAase I (Worthington biochemical, pn# LS002139) and 1.6 mg/mL MgSO4 for 5 min at 37°C. Next, the trypsinized cortices were mixed with complete DMEM growth medium (Corning VWR, pn# 45000–316) supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS (ThermoFisher, pn# 10437028), 1% (v/v) (2 mM) L-glutamine (ThermoFisher, pn# 25030081), 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin (ThermoFisher, pn# 15140163) and containing 0.5 mg/mL DNAase. The mix was triturated with a glass pipette several times to obtain a homogeneous mixture, centrifuged at 400 xg for 5 min and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in complete DMEM, triturated with a 10 mL serological pipette, and then transferred to a vented T75 tissue culture-treated flask (Corning, pn# 353136) and incubated in a humidified cell incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 for two days. On the second day of incubation, the growth medium was removed from the flask and filtered through a 500 mL bottle filter top (0.45 μm filter size, VWR, pn# 73520–992). The flask was washed three times in fresh DMEM to remove debris, and the filtered growth medium was returned to the flask. The mixed glial culture was maintained in the cell incubator at 37°C for 14 days in order to achieve maturation. We add 5–10 mL of fresh complete DMEM media to the flask after the first 7 days of maturation. To harvest microglia, the flasks were sealed with paraffin in order to maintain internal CO2 concentration, placed on a rotator and gently shaken for 2h. The flasks were maintained warm with the help of a heat blower adjusted to 37°C, placed next to the rotor during shaking. Following shaking, the medium containing lifted microglia was transferred to a 15 mL Falcon tube, and the flask was rinsed vigorously with 10 mL of fresh DMEM to release any remaining loosely attached microglia and transferred to a second Falcon tube. The medium was then centrifuged at 400 xg for 5 min in order to pellet the cells. Microglia pellets were resuspended in complete DMEM and seeded either on 96-well plates with glass-like polymer bottom (for lysosomal exocytosis toward aggregates and beads, Cellvis pn# P96–1.5P) or on 35-mm coverslip-bottom dishes with 4-mm glass imaging chambers coated with poly-D-lysine (for all other experiments) at 20,000 cells/chamber. The seeded wells or dishes were allowed to settle overnight prior to experimentation. Unless indicated otherwise, microglial cells were maintained in complete DMEM growth medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% L-glut and 1% penicillin/streptomycin inside a humidified cell incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2 throughout all experiments and incubations.

Mouse models

Preparation of Aβ1–42 aggregates labeled with fluorophores. Characterization of Aβ1–42 aggregates by circular dichroism

To prepare Aβ1–42 aggregates for circular dichroism, unlabeled soluble Aβ1–42 peptide was solubilized in a minimal volume of 50 mM sodium tetraborate and mixed with soluble Alexa 555-Aβ1–42 and biotin-Aβ1–42 peptides at a 1:0.06 and 1:0.18 M ratio, respectively, for a total Aβ1–42 concentration of 200 μM. Because PBS contains chlorides that interfere with circular dichroism by absorbing at far UV (250 nm and below), phosphate buffer was added in excess to the reaction mixture to complete the desired volume. The mixture was incubated for 30 min at 37°C under constant rotation. After incubation, a small aliquot was diluted 1:10 in phosphate buffer and measured using a Chriascan V-100 circular dichroism spectrometer (Applied Photophysics).

Lysosome exocytosis assay

Primary murine microglia seeded in 96-well plates were incubated overnight with 1 mg/mL fluorescein-biotin-dextran (10 kDa, ThermoFisher, pn# D7178) in complete DMEM growth medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% L-glutamine and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. On the next day, the cells were washed 2X and chased in complete DMEM medium for at least 2h to ensure dextran delivery to lysosomes. After the chase, microglial cells were washed 2X in complete DMEM medium to remove any residual extracellular dextran followed by incubation with streptavidin-Alexa 647-Aβ1–42 aggregates for 90 min at 37°C. To study the specificity of lysosome exocytosis toward Aβ1–42 aggregates, the cells were also co-incubated with ~6-μm-diameter streptavidin-coated fluorescent purple beads (Spherotech, pn# SVFP-6062–5) for 90 min. After the incubations, the cells were washed 2X and incubated with 1 mM biotin (Sigma-Aldrich, pn# B4501) and 50 mg/mL BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, pn# A2153) in complete growth medium at 37°C for 10 min in order to block unoccupied streptavidin and reduce Aβ1–42 autofluorescence (with BSA), respectively. After blocking, the cells were fixed in 1% PFA in PBS for 15 min and washed 3X in PBS. Next, the cells were permeabilized with a solution containing 1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, pn# X-100), 1 mM biotin and 50 mg/mL BSA in PBS for 10 min. This step removed intracellular fluorescein-dextran without affecting fluorescein-dextran deposited on the aggregates. Finally, microglial nuclei were stained with a 2 μg/mL Hoechst 33342 (VWR, pn# 15547) solution in PBS for 10 min followed by 3X rinsing in PBS and the preparations were imaged by confocal microscopy. For this study, a total of 9 wells containing aggregates and beads were imaged (107 fields acquired) in 4 independent experiments.

To test the effects of PI3K1β and AKT1/2 inhibition on lysosomal exocytosis, microglial cells were seeded on 35-mm dishes and microglial lysosomes were loaded with fluorescein-biotin-dextran as described earlier, followed by incubation either with 2 μM TGX-221 (Selleckchem, pn# S1169) or 5 μM AKT1/2i kinase inhibitors (Sigma-Aldrich, pn# A6730) or an equivalent volume of DMSO in complete DMEM medium for 1h. Following incubation, the cells were washed 2X in complete DMEM medium and incubated with streptavidin-Aβ1–42 aggregates for 90 min in the presence of inhibitors or DMSO as described above. After incubation, unoccupied streptavidin sites and Aβ1–42 aggregates were blocked and the cells were fixed and permeabilized as described earlier, followed by confocal microscopy imaging. For this study, 8–9 dishes were imaged (43–45 fields acquired) for each condition, corresponding to TGX-221 and AKT1/2i inhibitors or DMSO, in three independent experiments.

To test the effects of phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, Sigma-Aldrich, pn# 8139) on lysosomal exocytosis of dextrans, microglial cells were seeded on 35-mm dishes and lysosomes were loaded with fluorescein-biotin-dextran as described. Following chase, the cells were treated with 1.6 μM PMA or an equivalent volume of DMSO for 1h in complete DMEM medium, followed by co-incubation with streptavidin-coated Aβ1–42 aggregates for 90 min in the presence of PMA or DMSO. Unoccupied streptavidin sites and Aβ1–42 aggregates were blocked, and the cells were fixed and permeabilized as described, followed by confocal microscopy imaging. For this study, 9 dishes were imaged (41–42 fields acquired) for each condition, in three independent experiments.

Actin polymerization assay

Microglial cells seeded on 35-mm dishes were incubated in serum-free DMEM medium (supplemented with 1% L-glutamine and 1% penicillin/streptomycin) for 2h in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2, followed by incubation with Alexa 647-Aβ1–42 aggregates for 1h in serum-free DMEM medium. After incubation, the cells were fixed in 2% PFA for 20 min, washed 3X in PBS and stained with a 1:100 solution of phalloidin-Alexa 488 (ThermoFisher, pn# A12379) containing 0.5% (v/v) saponin (Sigma-Aldrich, pn# S-7900) in PBS, for 45 min at room temperature. After staining, the cells were rinsed 3X in PBS and cell nuclei were stained with a solution containing 2 μg/mL Hoechst in PBS for 10 min. The cells were then rinsed 3X in PBS and imaged by confocal microscopy.

To investigate the effect of PI3K1β and AKT1/2 inhibition on actin polymerization, microglial cells seeded on 35-mm dishes were incubated in serum-free DMEM medium for 1h at 37°C, followed by incubation either with 2 μM TGX-221 or 5 μM AKT1/2i inhibitors or an equivalent volume of DMSO, also in serum-free DMEM medium for 1h at 37°C. The cells were then washed 2X with serum-free medium and incubated with A647-Aβ1–42 aggregates in serum-free medium for 1h in the presence of inhibitors or DMSO as described. Following incubation, the preparations were fixed and stained with Alexa 488-phalloidin and Hoechst as described and imaged by confocal microscopy. For this study, a total of 8 dishes were imaged (45–46 fields acquired) for each condition, corresponding to TGX-221 and AKT1/2i inhibitors or DMSO, in three independent experiments.

Ratiometric pH imaging of Aβ1–42 aggregates labeled with fluorescein and Alexa 633 in buffers

Buffers adjusted to pH 4.5, 5.0, 5.5, 6.0, 6.5, 7.0, 7.5 and 8.0 (see Materials section) were placed in the imaging chambers of 35-mm coverslip dishes (80 μL per chamber). Next, 10 μL Aβ1–42 aggregates labeled with fluorescein and Alexa 633 were deposited in the buffer. The dishes were incubated for 1h at 37°C and imaged by confocal microscopy at 37°C thereafter. One dish with aggregates was imaged for each pH buffer, and 2–4 fields were acquired per dish. The experiment was repeated twice. Data generated in this experiment were used to prepare fluorescein/Alexa 633 ratio to pH calibration curves, which were used to determine the pH of lysosomal synapses (see digital image analysis section).

Ratiometric pH imaging of microglial lysosomal synapses

Microglial cells seeded on 35-mm dishes were incubated either with 1 μM bafilomycin A or an equivalent volume of DMSO in complete DMEM medium, inside a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 1h. After incubation, the medium was carefully aspirated, and the cells were incubated with 70 μL of complete DMEM medium, carefully deposited on top of the imaging chamber, containing approx. 10 μM of Aβ1–42 aggregates labeled with either ApHID or fluorescein (pH-sensitive) and Alexa 633 (pH-independent). The medium also contained either 1 μM bafilomycin A or an equivalent volume of DMSO. The dishes were incubated with the aggregates at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 45 min. After incubation, the dishes were filled with 2 mL of warm medium-2 supplemented with 0.2% glucose (w/v) and containing either 1 μM bafilomycin A or DMSO, transferred to a confocal imaging chamber and allowed to equilibrate at 37°C for at least 10 min, followed by imaging by confocal microscopy. The experiment was repeated twice. For each experiment, one dish was imaged per condition, and 10 fields were acquired for each dish. A total of 174 and 122 lysosomal synapses were quantified for the DMSO-treated and bafilomycin A-treated conditions, respectively. At the end of each imaging session, Aβ1–42 aggregates labeled with fluorescein and Alexa 633 were incubated with pH 7.4 PBS buffer at 37°C for 1h and imaged by confocal microscopy using the same settings used during cell imaging. One dish was imaged for each experiment, and 2–4 fields were acquired per dish. These data were used along with the calibration curves described above to determine the pH values of lysosomal synapses.

Degradation of surface-immobilized Aβ1–42 aggregates by microglial cells

35-mm dishes with attached streptavidin-coated coverslips (Nanocs, pn# CS-SV-5) were prepared by punching 7-mm holes in the dishes and attaching the coverslips to the bottom surface using a mixture of paraffin and petroleum jelly to form a watertight seal.84 A grid (2x2) was imprinted at the bottom of the coverslips to assist in localizing regions of interest with cells and aggregates during the course of the experiment. Immediately prior to use, the coverslips were reconstituted by incubation with PBS in a humidified cell incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 30 min. Next, fluorescein-biotin-Aβ1–42 aggregates were added on top of the streptavidin-coated coverslips and incubated for 1h at 37°C to allow biotin-streptavidin interaction and immobilization. The dishes were thoroughly washed 3X with PBS, followed by the addition of another volume of Aβ1–42 aggregates incubated on top of the coverslips for another hour at 37°C. This step was repeated three to four times to maximize the number of aggregates attached to the dishes. Next, the dishes were incubated overnight at room temperature with a fresh 1 mM biotin solution in PBS under constant rotation to remove non-covalently attached aggregates. On the next day, the dishes were washed 3X with PBS. 60 μL of a microglial cell suspension in DMEM without phenol red (Gibco, pn# 31053028, supplemented with 10% FBS, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 1% L-glutamine and 1% penicillin-streptomycin) were then added on top of the coverslips (20,000 cells per chamber), and the dishes were allowed to settle for 1h in a humidified cell incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. Next, the dishes were filled with 2 mL of warm complete DMEM without phenol red and imaged by confocal microscopy. Imaging was subsequently repeated at 24h, 48h, 72h and 7-day time points. After each imaging session, the dishes were returned to the incubator. Regions of interest on the imprinted grids selected during the 1h time point imaging session were saved and imaged again during all subsequent imaging sessions. To account for variations in laser intensity across imaging sessions, streptavidin-coated Nile red fluorescent beads (Spherotech, pn# SVFP-6056–5) were imaged at the end of each imaging session using the same microscope settings. The mean fluorescence intensity of the beads recorded for each imaging session was used to normalize fluorescein signal. The experiment was repeated three times. For each experiment, 2 dishes were imaged, for a total of six dishes (91 fields acquired). 5 dishes (75 fields) were included in the quantification of the 7-day time point after eliminating data for one dish (16 fields) due to a dramatic decrease in cell density and the unhealthy status of the remaining cells in that dish. 29 large aggregates with a diameter of ≥10 μm selected from 91 fields were quantified separately (25 aggregates were quantified for the 7-day time point). As control for aggregate stability over time in absence of microglia, dishes with Aβ1–42 aggregates only (no microglia) were maintained inside the incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 and imaged as described, for 2 experiments (3 dishes imaged, 47 fields acquired in total).

Aβ1–42 nanofibril exocytosis by microglial cells

Microglial cells seeded on 35-mm dishes were incubated overnight with approx. 10 μM of fluorescein-Aβ1–42 nanofibrils in complete DMEM medium (60 μL of working solution were deposited on top of the imaging chamber), inside a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. On the next day, the cells were chased for at least 2h in serum-free DMEM medium to ensure nanofibril delivery to lysosomes. Next, the cells were incubated with 1.6 μM PMA or an equivalent volume of DMSO in 60 μL of serum-free DMEM medium added on top of the dish imaging chamber, for at least 1h at 37°C, followed by addition of 12–20 μL of Aβ1–42-Alexa 647 aggregates on top of the incubation medium. The cells were incubated with the aggregates for 45–180 min at 37°C. After incubation with aggregates, the dishes were filled with 2 mL of warm medium-2 supplemented with 0.2% (w/v) glucose, also containing either 1.6 μM PMA or DMSO. The cells were allowed to equilibrate in medium-2 at 37°C for at least 20 min and imaged live at 37°C by confocal microscopy thereafter. 8 or 9 dishes of DMSO-treated and PMA-treated microglial cells were imaged in total, respectively, in 4 independent experiments.

Mouse perfusion and tissue processing

Animals included in the study were two 8-month-old 5xFAD mice (one male and one female) and two 5xFAD; cx3cr1tm2.1(cre/ERT2)Jung; Gt(ROSA)26Sortm14(CAG-tdTomato)Hze mice (a 13-month old male and a 15-month-old female) (see Animal section). The 13-month-old 5xFAD male had been injected with tamoxifen intraperitoneally (IP) at 75 mg/kg for four consecutive days, one month prior to intracardiac perfusion, in order to induce tdTomato expression in microglia via cx3cr1creER -driven Cre recombinase (for another study). TdTomato fluorescence is not visible under EM and does not interfere with EM imaging. Besides inducing Cre recombinase and the subsequent tdTomato expression, tamoxifen treatment has no effect on adult mouse microglia.85 Perfusion and tissue processing protocols were adapted from previous publications.46,48 The day before perfusion, all animals were injected IP with Methoxy-X04 solution at 10 mg/kg of body weight. On perfusion day, animals were briefly exposed to isoflurane (3% in 1L/min oxygen flow) and then anesthetized with a ketamine/xylazine cocktail at a dose of 75 mg/kg ketamine and 7.5 mg/kg xylazine, injected IP. Depth of anesthesia was confirmed by verifying an absence of response to ear, toe and tail pinch. Once anesthetized, mice were secured in a supine position by taping the forepaws and hindpaws to a Styrofoam work surface. The heart was carefully exposed by cutting through the skin with surgical scissors along the thoracic midline beginning caudal to the ribcage and proceeding rostral to the clavicle. Once exposed, the heart was held with blunt forceps, and a 25 G needle was inserted into the left ventricle for perfusion. The right atrium was immediately cut with pointed scissors, followed by intracardial infusion. 15–20 mL of a 0.1 M sodium cacodylate solution adjusted to pH 7.4 were injected first, followed by 250–350 mL of 2% PFA-2% glutaraldehyde fixative in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate also adjusted to pH 7.4, at 25 mL/min using a peristaltic pump. The solutions were kept in ice during perfusion. Once perfused, mice were decapitated and the fixed brain was extracted and submerged into a glass vial containing fixative solution for at least 2h at 4°C. Following post-fixation, the brains were sectioned using a vibratome (Leica VT1000S, Leica Microsystems). 40 μm-thick sections (bregma 0.02 to −1.34 mm) were continuously sectioned, collected and submerged into 24-well culture plates (Sarstedt, pn# 83.3922) filled with 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer86 followed by storage at 4°C prior to acid phosphatase staining. For long term storage, the sections were submerged in cryoprotectant solution composed of 30% (v/v) ethylene glycol (ThermoFisher, pn# 433811000), 30% (v/v) glycerol (Millipore-Sigma, pn# G7893) and 40% (v/v) of a solution of 0.25 M sodium cacodylate (final concentration 0.1 M) previously adjusted to pH 7.4. Sections in cryoprotectant were stored at −20°C.

Acid phosphatase staining of mouse brain sections

Brain sections (bregma 0.02 to −1.34 mm) from 5xFAD mouse brains (one 13-month-old male, one 15-month-old female, one 8-month-old male and one 8-month-old female) were selected for acid phosphatase staining (see Mouse perfusion section). The staining protocol used in this study is based on the Gomori method.87 All glassware used in this experiment was previously cleaned by acid wash to eliminate any phosphate residue. Immediately prior to staining, selected sections were briefly washed in 50 mM TRIS maleate buffer and thereafter incubated in Gomori’s lead medium adjusted to pH 4.8–5.0 (see Materials section), for 60–90 min at room temperature with gentle shaking. During incubation with Gomori lead medium, acid phosphatase enzyme present in the tissue will hydrolyze β-glycerophosphate (substrate) and, in presence of a soluble lead salt (in this case, lead nitrate), form lead phosphate, which will be visible by EM and indicative of acid phosphatase activity. As a control, some tissues were incubated in parallel with Gomori lead medium lacking β-glycerophosphate (which typically leads to weaker or no staining) or 50 mM TRIS maleate buffer alone. After staining, the sections were washed three times in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate adjusted to pH 7.4 and transferred to 24-well culture plates filled with cryoprotectant as described and stored at −20°C. Stained sections stored in cryoprotectant were shipped refrigerated with ice blocks to the University of Victoria (British Columbia, Canada) for analysis by scanning EM.

Mouse brain tissue processing for scanning electron microscopy

Brain sections of 3 conditions, namely 1) acid phosphatase staining in presence of β-glycerophosphate; 2) acid phosphatase staining in absence of β-glycerophosphate, and 3) staining protocol in 50 mM TRIS maleate buffer only (no lead nitrate, no β-glycerophosphate) were thereafter processed for scanning EM. The sections were first washed in pH 7.4 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer and post-fixed in 3.5% acrolein (Oakwood Chemical, pn# 103755) in sodium cacodylate buffer. The sections were then washed in sodium cacodylate buffer and incubated for 1 h in a solution containing equal volumes of 4% osmium tetroxide (Electron Microscopy Sciences, pn# 19190) and 3% potassium ferrocyanide (Sigma-Aldrich, pn# P9387) solutions in sodium cacodylate buffer. After washing in sodium cacodylate buffer, the sections were incubated for 20 min in a filtered 1% thiocarbohydrazide solution (Sigma-Aldrich, pn# 223220) in purified distilled water, followed by 30 min incubation in 2% aqueous osmium tetroxide. Sections were then dehydrated in ascending concentrations of ethanol for 5 min each (2 3 35%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 3 3 100%) and washed 3 times for 5 min with propylene oxide (Sigma-Aldrich, pn# 110205–18L-C). Dehydrated sections were then flat-embedded in Durcupan ACM resin (Millipore-Sigma, pn# 44611–44614) for 24 h. On the following day, the resin-infiltrated tissues were placed between two ACLAR embedding sheets (Electron Microscopy Sciences, pn# 50425–25) and the resin was polymerized at 55°C for 72h. The dorsal subiculum was excised, glued on a resin block, and cut into ~75 nm ultrathin sections using Leica Ultracut UC7 and ARTOS 3D ultramicrotomes (Leica Biosystems). The ultrathin sections were collected on a dust-free silicon nitride chip and glued on a specimen mount.

Electron microscopy

Electron microscopy of Aβ1–42 aggregates. Aβ1–42 aggregates were prepared for EM by mixing unlabeled Aβ1–42 peptide with Alexa 555-Aβ1–42 and biotin-Aβ1–42 peptides at 1:0.06 and 1:0.18 M ratio, for a total Aβ1–42 concentration of 200 μM. The mixture was incubated at 37°C with constant rotation overnight. On the next day, the mixture was centrifuged and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in PBS and sonicated in a water bath sonicator at 4°C (EpiSonic 2000 Sonication System, EpigenTek, 5 min sonication at a 40% amplitude followed by 5 min cooling in ice, repeated twice). 10–20 μL of sonicated solution were deposited on top of a coated copper grid (Electron Microscopy Sciences, pn# FCF400-Cu) and allowed to sit on the grid for 1 min, followed by 5X rinsing and 1 min incubation with uranyl acetate. Grids were imaged using a JEM 1400 transmission electron microscope (JEOL) with an acceleration voltage of 120 kV. Images were acquired using a Veletta CCD camera.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Digital Image Analysis

Digital image analysis and quantification was done using FIJI (ImageJ)83 version 1.54f for Windows (https://fiji.sc/) and MetaMorph version 6.7.1 for Windows (Molecular Devices, San Jose, California USA, www.moleculardevices.com).

Lysosomal exocytosis assay. Fluorescein-dextran deposited on the surface of Alexa 647-Aβ aggregates was quantified. Stacks of images for each field acquired contained multiple planes (1 μm separation between planes), and up to four channels were acquired: Hoechst (cell nuclei, blue), fluorescein (dextran, green), purple dye (streptavidin-coated beads) and Alexa 647 (aggregates, red). First, a threshold was applied to the Alexa 647 channel, and a binary mask was generated for each plane in a stack. Next, the mask was transferred and applied to each matched plane on the fluorescein channel. The “Logical AND” function was then applied to identify co-localized signal between the masked Alexa 647 and fluorescein channels, which corresponded to fluorescein-biotin-dextran deposited on Alexa 647-Aβ aggregates. The resulting segmented fluorescein signal was quantified as integrated intensity for each plane. Fluorescein signal corresponding to the 5 planes containing the majority of lysosomal synapses was then added and normalized to the total number of cells for that particular field, which was quantified using MetaMorph’s “Count Nuclei” function applied to the sum projection of the Hoechst (blue) channel. To quantify dextran deposition in mixtures containing aggregates and fluorescent purple streptavidin-coated beads, a binary mask was generated for the beads (purple dye channel) or the aggregates (Alexa 647 channel), transferred to the fluorescein channel, and fluorescein signal that co-localized with the beads or the aggregates was quantified separately as described above. Total fluorescein intensity co-localized with beads was normalized to the total number of beads in the field, whereas total fluorescein intensity co-localized with the aggregates was normalized to the total number of cells in the field.

Degradation of surface-immobilized Aβ1–42 aggregates by microglial cells. Fluorescein signal from aggregates was quantified for each time point imaged. One large tile scan image was acquired for each time point and dish imaged (see confocal microscopy section). Each tile scan was composed of 16 individual stacks of images, stitched together by the native Leica LAS X confocal imaging software. The native confocal tile was split into 16 individual stacks of images and saved in TIFF format using “Bio-Formats Import Options” on FIJI (ImageJ). Background fluorescence was corrected by subtracting the total signal 5th percentile from each plane in a stack (each composed of 25–40 planes with 1 μm separation between frames), and sum projections integrating all planes were generated for each corrected stack using MetaMorph. Intracellular fluorescein signal was filtered out by a mask generated using the transmitted light channel. To generate the mask, individual microglial cells were identified using the ‘Holes’ function on MetaMorph. Signal corresponding to cells was selected with a threshold and an inverted 8-bit mask was generated. The mask was optimized using the ‘Close’ and ‘Dilate’ functions on MetaMorph and thereafter applied to the sum projection of the fluorescein channel. The resulting filtered extracellular fluorescein signal was thresholded to select fluorescein-Aβ-aggregates. The same threshold value was applied to all stacks within a given experiment. Segmented fluorescein integrated intensity was quantified for each field and normalized to Nile red bead fluorescence measured for each experiment. To measure bead fluorescence, beads deposited on a glass slide were imaged at the end of each imaging session. Sum projections of each image stack acquired per field were generated, and mean bead fluorescence intensity was measured per field using the ‘Count Nuclei’ function on MetaMorph.

Ratiometric pH imaging of Aβ1–42 aggregates and lysosomal synapses. Fluorescein and Alexa 633 signal on Aβ1–42 aggregates was quantified and used to calculate fluorescein/Alexa 633 ratios for each segmented lysosomal synapse. Stacks of images with varying number of planes, separated by 2 μm step size, were acquired for each field imaged. The stacks were corrected for background intensity by subtracting the 10th percentile intensity value for each plane in the stack. The vast majority of synapses were contained within one to three planes adjacent to the cell membrane-aggregate contact. Hence, two planes for each stack where lysosomal synapse fluorescence was maximal were selected, and the sum projection of these two planes was generated for each channel. Individual lysosomal synapses were identified in each sum projection, and regions of interest were drawn for each compartment, based on morphological criteria. Fluorescence signal from each synapse was selected using an intensity threshold applied to the Alexa 633 channel. A mask was then generated and transferred to the fluorescein channel. Integrated intensity was measured for the masked Alexa 633 and fluorescein channels, and fluorescein/Alexa 633 intensity ratios were calculated for each lysosomal synapse and then interpolated to pH values using a ratio-to-pH calibration curve. To prepare the calibration, Aβ1–42 aggregates also labeled with fluorescein and Alexa 633 were incubated with pH 7.4 PBS buffer and imaged by confocal microscopy at the end of each experiment. The fluorescein/Alexa 633 ratios corresponding to pH 7.4 were calculated as described above and used to generate ratio values for pH 4.5–8.0, using a complete titration generated previously for the same aggregates, incubated in buffers with pH adjusted between 4.5 and 8.0, and imaged using confocal microscopy on a separate session (two independent repeats). The resulting ratio-to-pH calibrations were fit to 4-component sigmoidal curves. This approach is possible because the pH-dependence of fluorophores remains constant if experimental conditions (such as temperature) are maintained. We have used this approach to measure lysosomal pH using various pH-sensitive probes.37 To generate color-coded, ratiometric pH images, background-corrected stacks were thresholded using an intensity threshold applied to the Alexa 633 channel. A mask was then generated, which was applied to both the Alexa 633 and fluorescein channels, so that only fluorescence corresponding to Aβ1–42 aggregates was left. Next, a Gaussian filter (7x7 pixels) was applied to all images. A 24-bit ratio image was generated with the filtered fluorescein and Alexa 633 channels using the “ratiometric image” module in MetaMorph. Fluorescein/Alexa 633 ratios measured from Aβ1–42 aggregates at pH 4.5 and 8 were assigned as minimum and maximum ratios, respectively. Ratiometric images were overlaid on top of the transmitted light image in order to display cells and generate orthogonal views. In the ratio images, blue-purple hue indicates buffer pH whereas tones toward green and red correspond to acidic pH.

Stacks of images acquired with the high resolution Airyscan module were processed using linear deconvolution of each of the 32 individual elements recorded with the GaAsP detector array in order to further enhance their lateral and axial resolution, followed by pixel reassignment of each element back to the center position. The Wiener filter associated with deconvolution was adjusted to the default value of 6.0 and optimized for 3D image rendering. Processed stacks were reconstructed into 3D projections using Imaris image analysis software version 10 for Windows (Oxford Instruments, Abingdon, UK). 3D textures were rendered for Alexa 555 (cell membranes) and Alexa 633 (Aβ1–42 aggregates) based on a threshold assignment for each channel.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.3.1 for Windows (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts, USA, www.graphpad.com). Unless stated otherwise, data quantification and statistical analyses are reported for 3 independent experiments. To assess statistical differences in experiments comparing two independent data groups, the unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used (Figures 2 and 6). If the variances of the two groups were significantly different, the two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test with Welch’s correction was used (Figure 1). To compare Aβ aggregate degradation by microglia at different time points in matched fields, fluorescein intensities measured for each field and time point were normalized to their respective initial value at time 1h, and fluorescein intensities measured at 1h were assigned a value of ‘1’. Mean or median fluorescein intensities calculated for each time point were compared with the 1h time point value of ‘1’ using the one-sample Student’s t-test (when the data followed a normal distribution as determined by the Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests) or the one-sample Wilcoxon signed rank test, respectively (Figure 3). Statistical differences in experiments comparing three independent data groups were assessed using the unpaired one-way ANOVA followed by Šídáks multiple comparison test (Figure 4). Confidence interval was set to 95%. Results plotted using geometrical shapes correspond to mean values and bars indicate ±SD or ±SEM. Unless stated otherwise, each data group is populated with measurements corresponding to wells or dishes (large geometrical shapes) and/or individual fields (gray triangles). P-values shown as p > 0.05 (ns), p % 0.05 (*), p % 0.01 (**), p % 0.001 (***) and p % 0.0001 (****).
