Exploring the experiences, views, and needs of family members of emergency response workers: a systematic review and meta-synthesis
Authors: Hana Matoshi, Jo Billings, Maya Janse Van Rensburg, Sahra Tekin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Emergency responders, including firefighters, police officers, and paramedics, face workplace challenges that may harm their well-being. They typically rely on family members for support, which may have damaging effects on the family system. While the experiences of emergency responders are well established, their families’ subsequent struggles are less well explored.Objective: This review explores the experiences, views, and needs of family members, through determining the mental health and well-being issues that they face, and establishing ways in which family members could be supported.Method: We searched five databases (MEDLINE, Embase, PsychINFO, Scopus, and ProQuest) for qualitative papers exploring experiences of family members of emergency responders. Seventeen studies met the inclusion criteria, and data were meta-synthesized using thematic analysis.Results: Six themes and two subthemes were identified from the meta-synthesis. ‘Experiences of emotional strain’ reflected worry over safety and coping with volatile moods. ‘Increased responsibilities’ involved safeguarding emergency responders’ well-being and managing household duties, at the expense of family members’ own needs. Families felt ‘devoted yet invisible’, with sacrifices overlooked by both emergency responders and society. ‘Impacts on the family dynamic’ included relational detachment and frustration. ‘Support for emergency responder families’ from fellow emergency responder families was valued, but organizational support was limited. ‘Media portrayal and societal perceptions’ varied, as police families faced hostility, while firefighter and paramedic families garnered respect, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic.Conclusions: This review highlights that family members play a central role in maintaining the well-being of emergency responders, yet simultaneously face hardships associated with their family member’s work and feel undervalued despite their support. This lack of recognition extends to limited professional support. There is a critical need for targeted mental health interventions to support family members, alongside family support measures from emergency responder organizations to assist with the practical difficulties faced by families.

Introduction

Emergency responders, including police officers, firefighters, and paramedics, play a crucial role in maintaining public safety, and are tasked with being the first on site to a range of emergencies, where they are expected to provide both physical and emotional support to those impacted (Lowery & Cassidy, 2022). In their line of work, emergency responders face various workplace challenges, including working in poor conditions (Elsässer et al., 2024), having irregular working hours, often with excessive workloads due to high staff turnover, and low pay (Bevan et al., 2022). High rates of sleep disturbances have been reported (Huang et al., 2022) and linked to elevated suicide risk in emergency responders (Vigil et al., 2021). Emergency responders are at an increased risk of a variety of mental health disorders, such as depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and anxiety, as well as substance abuse (Jones, 2017) owing to working in highly stressful situations and constant exposure to heightened emotions. This is evidenced in a cross-sectional study conducted in the UK, with 10,401 serving police officers, which found an 8% prevalence of PTSD and a 12.6% prevalence of complex PTSD (Brewin et al., 2022). Findings from the US National Wellness Survey for Public Safety Personnel also revealed that 40% of professional firefighters face clinically substantial levels of anxiety and depression (Gibbs et al., 2025). Likewise, a meta-analysis of 1638 articles showed that similar rates are mirrored in paramedics, with prevalence rates of 17.9% for PTSD, 56.4% for anxiety, and 34.7% for depression (Alzahrani et al., 2025). Overall, emergency responders experience some of the highest rates of occupational trauma, compared to all other occupational groups (Lewis-Schroeder et al., 2018).

Although research demonstrates that the mental health of emergency responders is a concern, uptake of professional support remains relatively low owing to a lack of awareness of available support and to stigma (Wright et al., 2022). Instead, emergency responders typically prefer informal support from trusted others, such as family members, to cope with workplace stressors (Tjin et al., 2022). Social support is a key protective factor against mental health issues (Brewin et al., 2000; Ozer et al., 2003) and enhances resilience to stress and trauma psychopathology (Ozbay et al., 2007). As a result, a pattern emerges whereby family members manifest as sounding boards in an attempt to relieve stress for the emergency responder (Casas & Benuto, 2022).

Evidently, family members hold a key responsibility for the well-being of emergency responders and become a solid foundation of support for them. Yet, this has a cascading effect on family members’ mental health, according to the ‘work-spillover’ theory, which underlines that stressors, moods, and emotions experienced at work are easily transferred to the family dynamic (Mennino et al., 2005). Considering the stressful and emotionally demanding nature of emergency responder work, family members are placed at increased risk of being affected (Sharp et al., 2022). Being a support system for individuals facing trauma can lead to a trajectory of secondary traumatic stress (STS), mirroring the symptoms of PTSD without the initial traumatic event being personally experienced (Vukčević Marković & Živanović, 2022). Likewise, experiences of compassion fatigue, characterized as burnout over providing constant care and attention for others, is also common (Cocker & Joss, 2016). These outcomes are consistent with the findings of a systematic review by Sharp et al. (2022), which included 43 papers from five countries and found that spouses and children of emergency responders were at high risk of developing mental health issues from strained relationships and overwhelming concerns over the safety of their emergency responder. Similar findings have been presented in research involving other family members of high-risk workers. A qualitative study, which was conducted with 14 family members of frontline healthcare workers (HCWs) who worked during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic in the UK, found that families reported experiencing increased emotional burden owing to anxiety over their loved one’s line of work (Tekin et al., 2022).

The challenges faced by emergency responders have made them a long-standing focus of research, with their experiences having been explored extensively. More recently, there has been a growing interest in understanding the experiences of family members. Two existing systematic reviews focused on family members of emergency responders. The first is the review by Sharp et al. (2022), which includes qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-method papers with an aim of explicitly investigating the well-being and mental health of family members of emergency responders. The second is a qualitative review by May, Van Hooff, Doherty, and Iannos (2023), which seeks to understand the wider experiences and perceptions of family members. A limitation of this review is its inclusion criteria, stipulating that the emergency responders must have diagnosed PTSD, which inadvertently limited the breadth of papers. However, given the evolving nature of the field and factoring in a need to comprehensively understand all nuances of family members’ experiences, there is a need for further research. Furthermore, despite there being overlaps between emergency responders and military/veteran populations in relation to occupational trauma, military families were not the primary focus of this review because of the distinct nature of deployment-related experiences and combat exposure. The present review specifically aimed to explore the experiences of families of civilian emergency responders. However, studies including mixed samples of veterans and emergency responders were retained where findings relating to emergency responder families could meaningfully contribute to the synthesis. Therefore, the aims of this systematic review were to explore the experiences, views, and needs of family members of emergency responders, what type of mental health and well-being issues are faced, and the ways in which family members could be supported through these struggles. This systematic review builds upon existing knowledge by synthesizing qualitative studies to better understand the psychological, emotional, and practical impacts that family members of emergency responders go through.

Method

This systematic review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidance (Supplementary Material 1) and was preregistered in PROSPERO on 28 May 2024 (CRD42024550714).

Search strategy

The literature search for this review was carried out on 18 June 2025 using the following electronic databases; MEDLINE ALL (Ovid), PsychINFO (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), Scopus, and ProQuest. Search terms were initially created using the PICOS framework and then developed with the expertise of a University College London (UCL) librarian. For a detailed breakdown of search terms, see Supplementary Material 2. Google Scholar was also used to obtain grey literature. This enabled the collection of unpublished research, such as doctoral dissertations.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criterion for this review was research involving any individual who was a family member of an emergency responder. Our classification of the family unit was defined as ‘any combination of two or more persons who are bound together over time by ties of marriage, mutual consent, birth and/or adoption or placement’ (Mirabelli, 2018, p. 3). This definition was purposefully chosen to reflect the broad and diverse dynamics of modern-day societal family units. Included studies had to be qualitative in methodology to maintain methodological consistency, allowing for an in-depth interpretive synthesis of lived experiences. Although mixed-method studies may contain qualitative components, these are often less conceptually rich or insufficiently detailed for meta-synthesis approaches focused on meaning and experience. In addition, we aimed to maintain methodological consistency across included studies and to ensure that the synthesis was based exclusively on data generated through qualitative research design. For these reasons, mixed-method studies were excluded. To allow for greater returns of studies, we did not place time or geographical restrictions.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies using quantitative or mixed-method methodologies; and (2) studies not in the English language. Studies focusing solely on the experiences of emergency responders, rather than including perspectives from family members, were also excluded. Papers were excluded if the occupation was not considered to be an emergency responder role.

Study selection

Search results from databases were exported into EndNote (Clarivate, 2024) for deduplication. Remaining studies were then exported into Covidence (Covidence Systematic Review Software, 2024) for further deduplication and title, abstract, and full-text screening.

Titles and abstracts of all papers were then independently screened by the first author of the study (HM), with a subset of 20% being screened by a second reviewer (MVR), with high interrater reliability between reviewers (κ = 0.93). Conflicts were discussed and resolved between the first and second reviewers (HM and MVR). Following a discussion of the eligibility criteria between the first and second reviewers, HM completed full-text screening, with MVR screening a subset of 20%, with no discrepancies.

Data extraction and quality appraisal

Data from 17 studies were extracted and structured into predefined tables. These included the lead author's name, title of study, date of publication, country, sample size, relationship to emergency responder, occupational setting of emergency responder, sample population, data collection method, method of analysis, and aims and results of the study, explaining themes and main findings.

To assess the quality and risk of bias of included studies, the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklist was used (CASP, 2018). CASP uses a checklist of 10 questions, which assess issues relating to the validity and appropriateness of decisions made in the study. It considers 10 domains relating to: (1) the aims of the study; (2) methodology; (3) research design; (4) recruitment strategy; (5) method of data collection; (6) the relationship between the researcher and the participants; (7) ethical issues; (8) data analysis; (9) clarity of findings; and, finally, (10) how valuable the research findings are. Questions were answered with ‘Yes’, ‘No’, or ‘Can’t tell’. The CASP tool does not yield an overall numerical score for the quality of studies; instead, it encourages reviewers to review studies through a critical lens. The questions were answered by two independent reviewers (HM) and (MVR) with the involvement of a third reviewer (ST) to resolve any disagreements.

Synthesis of results

To synthesize the outcomes of the included qualitative studies, we used the guidance presented by Lachal et al. (2017), explaining the steps in meta-synthesis for qualitative studies in psychiatry. To begin, data were extracted from the results sections of included studies in which any reference to experiences of family members was made. If articles included accounts from both emergency responders and family members, then data strictly from family members were used. These data were then exported into NVivo version 12 (Lumivero, 2024) to be thematically analysed using Braun and Clarke’s (2023) guidance. In line with this guidance, we first actively read the findings of the included studies to increase our familiarity and to ensure better understanding and organization of the data. The next step involved coding, where we labelled findings within the data to highlight what they referred to. In the third step, we categorized and grouped codes that related to one another, leading to the final step, which was the creation of themes that captured the overarching experience between families of emergency responders. Themes were reviewed to ensure that they did not overlap, and themes, subthemes, and codes were cross-checked between members of the research team.

Reflexivity

Having varied backgrounds within a research team can strengthen the quality of a qualitative synthesis by bringing differing perspectives to data interpretation, as well as ensuring rigour in the research process. There was a mix of early-career and senior researchers in this all-female team. HM and MVR were both Master’s students in Clinical Mental Health Sciences at UCL at the time of conducting this study, and had been taught to conduct systematic reviews and qualitative research in their studies. ST is a mixed-method post-doctoral researcher with experience in conducting and supervising systematic reviews and qualitative research. JB is a Clinical Professor of Psychological Trauma and Workplace Mental Health with over 25 years of experience conducting qualitative research and systematic reviews with high-risk occupational group workers. The variety of experience allowed for both fresh insight and methodological expertise in this review.

Results

Study selection

Searches on the five databases returned 9693 studies, with 8256 remaining after deduplication. Of these, 57 papers were included in full-text screening, with 40 of these being excluded owing to not being a qualitative study (n = 20), being a systematic review study (n = 2), not being in the English language (n = 2), exploring a specific traumatic event rather than emergency responder roles (n = 5), and not focusing on family members (n = 11). This resulted in a final 17 papers being included in the meta-synthesis. See Figure 1 for the PRISMA flow diagram. Study characteristics

Data from included studies were collected from eight countries, comprising the USA (n = 7), Australia (n = 3), Canada (n = 2), the UK (n = 1), Ireland (n = 1), South Africa (n = 1), Sweden (n = 1), and China (n = 1). All studies were published between 2005 and 2024. The method of data collection primarily consisted of interviews (n = 16) and focus groups (n = 2), with one study also using an open-ended questionnaire (n = 1). Methods of analysis varied; these included phenomenological analysis (n = 7), grounded theory analysis (n = 4), tentative analysis (n = 1), transcendental phenomenological inquiry (n = 1), inductive descriptive design (n = 1), and thematic analysis (n = 3). Included studies focused on a variety of emergency responder roles, comprising police officers (n = 8), fire services (n = 10), emergency medical services (n = 3), and paramedics (n = 4).

In terms of family members, one study focused solely on the experiences of children, and seven focused only on spouses/partners of emergency responders. Other studies included a combination of family members, such as experiences of siblings, spouses, and children. See Table 1 for the detailed characteristics of the included studies. Theme 1: Experiences of emotional strain

Seven studies underlined experiences of emotional strain in family members (Carrico, 2012; Helfers et al., 2021; Hill et al., 2020; Lantz et al., 2024; May, Van Hooff, Doherty, & Carter, 2023; Porter and Henriksen, 2016; Regehr et al., 2005; Roth & Moore, 2009), which primarily came from worry and rumination over safety, owing to the uncertainty of emergency responder work. For example, in Porter and Henriksen’s (2016) study conducted on six spouses, the wife of an emergency responder reported that: Being a spouse is stressful, scary. Anyone can be killed at any time, but especially when that’s what they do on a daily basis … that’s where it gets really scary, when you just sit and think about it. You try to always put those thoughts out of your mind, because that would make anyone go insane if that’s all they thought about. (Porter and Henriksen, 2016, p. 47)This constant concern over the emergency responder seemingly had a negative toll on the psychosocial state of family members. This pattern of worry and apprehension was also seen to manifest in children (Helfers et al., 2021; May, Van Hooff, Doherty, & Carter, 2023). In May, Van Hooff, Doherty, and Carter’s (2023) study conducted on 17 family members, looking at the experiences of parental PTSD in Australian service families, the parent of a 9-year-old child reported: [Child] had so much worry about [emergency responder]. I remember [child] was so scared that [emergency responder] was going to die all the time. (May, Van Hooff, Doherty, & Carter, 2023, p. 3824)Experiences of emotional strain were further reinforced when families had to deal with the explosive and unpredictable moods that emergency responders brought home as a result of workplace stressors, as presented in six papers (Carrico, 2012; Lawn et al., 2024; May, Van Hooff, Doherty, & Carter, 2023; Porter and Henriksen, 2016; Regehr et al., 2005; Waddell et al., 2020). This resulted in families experiencing a sense of tension and turmoil, as reported by Lawn et al. (2024), which led the wife of an emergency responder to consider leaving the relationship to protect her own well-being: I felt like I was walking on eggshells all the time. He was irrational about everything, and it went on and on. I was saying ‘I’m going to leave’ get away from this. (Lawn et al., 2024, p. 98)Being exposed to the volatile moods exhibited by the emergency responder also placed family members at an increased risk of adopting these behaviours, which is congruent with experiences of secondary trauma. An example of this is presented in May, Van Hooff, Doherty, and Carter’s (2023) paper, which included 17 family members of police, navy, and army officers: [The child] picks up on it naturally and if [emergency responder] was off or upset or angry or anything like that, [the child] would often mirror that same kind of emotions. (May, Van Hooff, Doherty, and Carter, 2023, p. 3823)Emotional strain also arose when family members absorbed emergency responders’ stress. In Regehr’s (2005) study of 14 paramedic spouses, one noted: As far as I’m concerned, I’m a sponge. Talk to me. I’ll soak it up. (Regehr, 2005, p. 107)

Theme 2: Increased responsibilities

Family members of emergency responders faced ‘increased responsibilities’ in relation to two aspects, related to: (1) ensuring the well-being of the emergency responder; and (2) responsibility for household duties.

Subtheme 2.1: Responsibility for the well-being of the emergency responder

In nine studies (Bonneau, 2010; Carrico, 2012; Lantz et al., 2024; Lawn et al., 2024; Porter and Henriksen, 2016; Regehr, 2005; Regehr et al., 2005; Traynor et al., 2024; Waddell et al., 2020), spouses experienced an overwhelming sense of responsibility and took on a primary role to safeguard the emergency responder’s mental state. This involved monitoring their moods and behaviour while maintaining a safe, comfortable home, as illustrated by an emergency responder’s wife: It doesn't matter what's going on with your day. You could be having a difficult day and then suddenly, you're expected to just make this whole safe place at home and, you look after them the whole time. (Traynor et al., 2024, p. 4)However, as a consequence of prioritizing the emotional stability of emergency responders, spouses felt as though their own needs and emotions were disregarded. This dedication to responsibility over the emergency responder even expanded to listening to unwanted or challenging descriptions of work experiences, to ensure that the emergency responders had an emotional outlet for their trauma, despite the risk of experiencing vicarious trauma, as seen in this extract: Even if I don’t necessarily want to hear what he’s saying. For him it’s a cleansing that he needs to get out. (Lawn et al., 2024, p. 98)

It doesn't matter what's going on with your day. You could be having a difficult day and then suddenly, you're expected to just make this whole safe place at home and, you look after them the whole time. (Traynor et al., 2024, p. 4)

Even if I don’t necessarily want to hear what he’s saying. For him it’s a cleansing that he needs to get out. (Lawn et al., 2024, p. 98)

Subtheme 2.2: Responsibility for the household

Six studies reported that there was an unequal distribution of housework in emergency responder families (Carrico, 2012; Lantz et al., 2024; Regehr et al., 2005; Roth & Moore, 2009; Traynor et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024). Non-responder spouses often had to maintain the household by cooking, cleaning, and child-rearing. This burden led to frustrations over imbalanced household responsibilities. Because he is dealing with so much, it feels like I've got to take on everything else. When he comes from work, he can go to bed, but when I've been up with the kids all night, I still have to go to work and sometimes I want to shout at him you're not the only one with a tough job around here. (Traynor et al., 2024, p. 4)Non-responder spouses often became sole parents, assuming the role of both mother and father as a result of the emergency responder’s absence or fatigue. In a study of police families, one wife described her husband’s lack of connection with their child: Our daughter grew up with me; he will never have the kind of deep attachment for our child as I do. He did not even take care of her when she was a little kid! (Xu et al., 2024, p. 190)

Theme 3: Devoted yet still invisible

Prioritization of the emergency responder was a common sentiment expressed in four papers (May, Van Hooff, Doherty, & Carter, 2023; Regehr et al., 2005; Traynor et al., 2024; Waddell et al., 2020). Family members felt as though they devoted themselves to the role of the emergency responder yet simultaneously felt unseen, and that their issues were less important in comparison. For instance, in a study conducted on 14 wives of firefighters, one wife explained that: Firefighters are heroes and do such important work. But as a wife, I do feel guilty if I’m having a bad day, if I get mad at him and blow up. He’s been up all night and [I should] lay off him. You feel guilty to ask for anything because you know his work is important. (Regehr et al., 2005, p. 428)Likewise, family members felt that the sacrifices they made to support and facilitate the emergency responders’ careers were overlooked by both the emergency responder and society, making them feel unappreciated. This was seen in the paper presented by Hill et al. (2020), where the wife of a firefighter argued that the fire-service organization failed to recognize their contribution: The wife is as much a part as the husband, and we’re making a lot of the sacrifices. (Hill et al., 2020, p. 399)These sacrifices meant that family members were unable to engage in activities in which they had an interest, as seen in this extract from the wife of a firefighter: I cannot go do something and leave the kids with him … so, I cannot have regular activities. (Hill et al., 2020, p. 398)

Theme 4: Impacts on the family dynamic

Fifteen papers reported on how the demanding nature of emergency responder work created a significant shift in the family dynamic, pertaining to both relational bonds between family members and practical impacts on family life (Bochantin, 2016; Bonneau, 2010; Carrico, 2012; Helfers et al., 2021; Hill et al., 2020; Lantz et al., 2024; May, Van Hooff, Doherty, & Carter, 2023; Porter and Henriksen, 2016; Regehr, 2005; Regehr et al., 2005; Roth & Moore, 2009; Traynor et al., 2024; Waddell et al., 2020; Wheater & Erasmus, 2017; Xu et al., 2024). For example, family members reported that emergency responders became emotionally detached and withdrawn, which negatively impacted family relationships. In Regehr’s (2005) study, which was conducted with 14 spouses of paramedics, a female spouse highlighted this: He’s not just withdrawing from me; he’s withdrawing from our children as well. It’s affecting us. (Regehr, 2005, p. 105)Likewise, in a study conducted in China by Xu et al. (2024), with wives and children of police officers, there were clear signs of a breakdown in communication between family members as a result of emotional detachment by emergency responders. Consequently, we see how occupational stress impacted the emotional climate of the household and diminished overall closeness, as one wife recounted: He often shows indifference to the family. Theme 5: Media portrayal and society’s perceptions

Media portrayal and society’s perceptions were an underlying theme in three papers (Helfers et al., 2021; Regehr et al., 2005; Traynor et al., 2024), which revealed that different emergency responder roles evoked different reactions from the public, impacting the treatment family received by members. For instance, in recent years, policing has gained a more controversial reputation in the eyes of the media and public (Succar et al., 2024).

In the study conducted by Helfers et al. (2021) on 19 children of police officers, a key sentiment was that police officers were viewed negatively. Therefore, children of police officers often faced harassment and judgement by their peers. For example, the child of a police officer reported that: I get teased and told that I’m a ‘goody goody’. I’ve been called a racist because my dad is a cop. (Helfers et al., 2021, p. 246)Some children highlighted that the narrative against the police was further reinforced by the media’s portrayal, which had a ripple effect on the image of the organization, as reflected in this extract: There is a lot of negative stuff out there. You know, f**k the police, the police are bad. (Helfers et al., 2021, p. 247)In contrast, there was a stark disparity in attitudes towards other emergency responders, such as paramedics and firefighters, which appeared to hold a greater level of respect and appreciation from the public. This notion was reflected in the account of a paramedic’s wife, who experienced a boost in social capital from neighbours, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic, which created a sense of pride and raised esteem for her: Everyone has huge respect and admiration for them. Theme 6: Support for emergency responder families

Seven papers revealed an insight into the fact that support and guidance were found with other emergency responder families (Bonneau, 2010; Hill et al., 2020; Landers et al., 2020; Porter and Henriksen, 2016; Regehr et al., 2005; Roth & Moore, 2009; Waddell et al., 2020). In some papers, it was reported that emergency responder families were able to bond with family members of other emergency responders, and they found relief in the shared experiences. For example, in Porter and Henriksen’s (2016) study on six spouses, the wife of an emergency responder explained that: All of the spouses know what it’s like; we can go out together and they just really understand. We all understand the weird shifts, weird workdays, and it’s nice to have a support group, people that you identify with. It helps on the days when it’s hard. (Porter and Henriksen, 2016, p. 49)However, when it came to receiving structured and professional support from emergency responder organizations, family members reported that they felt neglected. For example, in Regehr’s (2005) study, the wife of a paramedic revealed: He [paramedic] got excellent support, but we [family members] got zero … nobody called me. (Regehr, 2005, p. 109)This negligence by emergency responder organizations to provide support to family members to protect their mental well-being resulted in palpable frustrations, as seen in nine papers (Carrico, 2012; Hill et al., 2020; Lantz et al., 2024; Lawn et al., 2024; May, Van Hooff, Doherty, & Carter, 2023; Regehr, 2005; Regehr et al., 2005; Traynor et al., 2024; Waddell et al., 2020), especially since family members were primarily responsible for maintaining the mental stability of emergency responders, as seen in this quote: When are they [the organization] going to acknowledge the people who do most of the groundwork and actually keep them [emergency responder] together? (Lawn et al., 2024, p. 99)Four papers (Lawn et al., 2024; May, Van Hooff, Doherty, & Carter, 2023; Regehr, 2005; Roth & Moore, 2009) noted a lack of awareness regarding the mental health and well-being challenges faced by their emergency responder loved one. It’s something that dad needs to help himself with. […] And I know when Dad’s in his moods. I know how to kind of filter myself. (May, Van Hooff, Doherty, & Carter, 2023, p. 3826)

Discussion

In this systematic review, we synthesized findings from 17 qualitative studies, with the aim of exploring the experiences, views, and needs of family members of emergency responders. While previous reviews have highlighted mental health and well-being challenges (May, Van Hooff, Doherty, & Iannos 2023; Sharp et al., 2022), the present review identifies how emergency responder work shapes family life through emotional strain, increased responsibilities, feeling undervalued, and having to manage public perceptions, all while receiving limited support from emergency responder organizations.

A prominent finding of this review was that psychological strain was reinforced by excessive worrying and rumination over the safety of a loved one, consequently taking a toll on the family member’s own mental well-being. This finding is consistent with the systematic review presented by Sharp et al. (2022), which identified concerns over physical safety and trauma exposure as recurring stressors for family members. Similar patterns have been observed in other families of trauma-exposed occupational groups. For example, in their qualitative study conducted on family members and friends of frontline HCWs during the COVID-19 pandemic (including family members of ambulance staff), Tekin et al. (2022) reported that family members experienced increased anxiety over worrying about their HCWs loved ones’ health through high-risk periods. Emotional strain among family members was further reinforced by work stress spillover, incited by the negative emotional states of the emergency responders. Similarly, in their narrative review exploring resilience among family members of public safety personnel, Cox et al. (2022) also established that families were often subjected to anger and irritability from workers following long or demanding shifts. These findings are in line with previous research, emphasizing the psychological and emotional struggles faced by family members.

Family members also faced increased responsibilities because of the emergency responders’ work commitments. A key experience recounted by spouses was an obligation to provide unwavering emotional support for the emergency responder, such as being an emotional outlet for them. This emotional labour endured by family members demonstrates a profound sense of self-sacrifice. Similar caregiving burdens have been observed among military families, suggesting that emotional self-sacrifice may be a common experience among families supporting trauma-exposed occupations. For example, qualitative research by Oudi et al. (2023) on the wives of veterans with PTSD recognized that wives frequently sacrificed their own well-being to maintain family cohesion; however, it must be taken into consideration that their research was conducted in Iran, a collectivist country that places high importance on family cohesion (Shamsikhani et al., 2021), and thus the findings may have been shaped by this. A detriment to assuming this supportive role is that family members may face the risk of secondary traumatization owing to exposure to hearing about traumatic events. This pattern is not unique to emergency responder families, as similar risks have been reported among healthcare families. A cross-sectional study conducted with 320 household members of HCWs in the UK found that one-third of household members reported severe STS, with spouses/partners being at a significantly higher risk than other household members (Tekin et al., 2025). Although HCWs were outside the inclusion criteria of the current review, these findings provide a useful comparison by suggesting that intimate partners may be particularly vulnerable when occupational stress and trauma are repeatedly brought into the home. This highlights how secondary traumatization may arise not only from direct exposure to traumatic events, but also through the relational and caregiving roles adopted by family members.

The concept of increased responsibilities extended to unequal involvement in household duties. This was ultimately expressed as an additional burden, amid emotional labour, which led to exhaustion and potential burnout among spouses. Research demonstrates that disproportionate household responsibilities can result in a significant strain on personal well-being and relationship satisfaction (Ciciolla & Luthar, 2019); therefore, emergency responder families are more likely to experience conflict and a diminished sense of household unity.

This review emphasized the devotion that family members demonstrated towards prioritizing both the role and well-being of the emergency responder. Family members typically embraced the emergency responder lifestyle and formed their identities and routines around this way of life (Porter and Henriksen, 2016). Yet, as a result of this prioritization, family members reported feeling undervalued and neglected their own needs. A narrative review by Tjin et al. (2022) of trusted others’ experiences of providing social support to emergency responders, which included 24 articles, also reflected this finding, whereby trusted others felt the need to conceal their own emotional requirements to safeguard the emergency responder’s well-being. Withholding these emotions can eventually increase tension in the family and can negatively impact healthy communication in the household (Cox et al., 2022). However, differences emerged according to family role. Spouses appeared to be most affected by the practical and relational consequences of emergency responder work, through increased household responsibilities, caregiving, and emotional support. In contrast, children were affected more through emotional pathways such as fears over safety, confusion over their emergency responder parents’ moods, and disappointment over their lack of availability in their lives. This suggests that emergency responder work affects family members in differing ways, meaning that support for families should not adopt a single family-wide experience, but rather be tailored to the specific developmental stage of each family member and the role that they play in the family system.

A notable consequence of working as an emergency responder is that cumulative exposure to trauma affects the behavioural conduct of workers, leading to their becoming emotionally detached and withdrawn (Benincasa et al., 2022). This can have a damaging effect on the family system, leaving family members feeling neglected, which parallels previous research exploring the impact of PTSD on veteran families. For instance, in their study on 10 veterans with PTSD, Ray and Vanstone (2009) reported that emotional numbing and detachment by veterans affected unity between the family and negatively impacted the quality of parent–child relationships. However, a limitation of this paper is its lack of generalizability, owing to the unique experiences of veterans deployed abroad, which is a stark contrast to emergency responders’ homeland experiences.

Family dynamics were further affected when emergency responders experienced an inability to switch off from their work persona. This appeared to differ by occupational group. In particular, this finding was most evident in police families, where occupational habits such as suspicion, control, and hypervigilance were described as spilling over into the home environment, whereas firefighter and paramedic families did not report these issues. Police families revealed that officers exhibited suspicious and overprotective behaviour towards family members, creating an uncomfortable atmosphere in the household (Xu et al., 2024). This form of authoritarian spillover has been found to be associated with significant familial strain (Stinson & Liederbach, 2013) and may increase the risk of domestic abuse (Johnson et al., 2005). Although domestic abuse was not reported in the included studies, family members were affected by the constant sense of control and hypervigilance exerted by emergency responders. Other research also indicates that increased trauma is associated with increased relationship violence in police officers (Meffert et al., 2014). Given these associations, there is a need for more research exploring the occurrence and context of domestic violence in police families to better understand its prevalence.

A recurring theme throughout the included papers related to the impacts of irregular shift patterns. Family members mentioned that important family events were often missed because of the emergency responder’s absence. Research on the effects of shift work has found a strong association between irregular work patterns and higher rates of work–family conflict (Zhao et al., 2021). The effects of shift work are further accentuated in a literature review of 21 studies by Li et al. (2014), which established that children of parents with non-standard work schedules were at risk of negative developmental outcomes related to mental health and behavioural conduct problems. This is reflected in the present review, as children experienced a sense of anger and resentment towards their emergency responder parent when they were not present for important events.

This review further underlined the varying societal attitudes towards different emergency responder professions and highlights how these nuances in perception impact families. As illustrated in the data and wider literature, policing stands out as an occupation that garners negative attention (Gau et al., 2022). This perception is widely dependent on the media’s representation of police misconduct, brutality, excessive force, and institutional racism, which has correspondingly shaped public attitudes (Succar et al., 2024). As a result, families of police officers are more inclined to face harassment and shame in relation to their emergency responder’s profession (Helfers & Nhan, 2022). In contrast, other emergency responders, such as firefighters and paramedics, seem to experience a more favourable public perception, which results in better experiences for their family members. For instance, families of paramedics reported a heightened sense of pride specifically during the COVID-19 pandemic, a period where media discourse continuously promoted heroic narratives of frontline HCWs (Billings et al., 2021; Tekin et al., 2022). Firefighter families also experienced an increased level of respect and admiration owing to this reputation (Regehr et al., 2005). These findings display novel insights, as previous reviews have not explored the differing societal attitudes towards emergency responders and their effects on family members. It is important for future research to further explore this relationship.

Finally, references to support or lack thereof were consistent in the data. Family members reported that a prevalent coping mechanism was finding support in other emergency responder families owing to their shared experiences. This method of ‘collective coping’ is a form of social support that has been established to decrease the risk of negative psychological outcomes (Calhoun et al., 2022) and is common in trauma-affected groups (Wlodarczyk et al., 2016), including veteran families (Tam-Seto et al., 2018). Nevertheless, there were organizational shortcomings of emergency responder departments when it came to both acknowledging the efforts of emergency responder families and offering professional support. Correspondingly, most papers reflected a desire among family members to feel involved in and recognized by emergency responder organizations as integral parts of the support system. Alongside this, psychoeducation was seen as a crucial component for families, both to increase understanding of the psychological impacts of emergency responder work and to provide them with tools to recognize signs of distress in themselves and their loved ones. Therefore, a need for professional mental health support tailored to family members’ unique experiences was often requested (Lawn et al., 2024).

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this review is its transparency and robust methodology, achieved by employing PROSPERO and PRISMA guidance throughout. Methods for selecting, appraising, and synthesizing studies were strictly adhered to, increasing both the credibility and replicability of this review. Furthermore, qualitative syntheses have proven to be valuable methods to apply when aiming to examine the experiences, meanings, and perspectives of participants (Lachal et al., 2017). Since one aim of this review was to capture the unique experiences and views of family members of emergency responders, employing a meta-synthesis ensured that the depth and richness of participants’ experiences were not lost. Moreover, Paez (2017) emphasizes the importance of using grey literature to achieve a balanced display of evidence and, more importantly, to avoid publication bias, which is why ProQuest was used, resulting in the inclusion of two doctoral dissertations in this review. Finally, two independent reviewers (HM and MVR) were involved throughout the screening, appraisal, and synthesis, to increase the quality and trustworthiness of the review.

Limitations must also be considered. A significant drawback relates to our exclusion of papers not written in the English language, which introduces an English-language bias (Jackson & Kuriyama, 2019). As a result of this bias, all but two studies came from high-income Westernized countries. This lack of geographic diversity limits our understanding of the experiences of families of emergency responders from low- and middle-income countries. This is a particular limitation as emergency responder teams in low-income countries may face distinctive challenges, such as underdeveloped infrastructure (Friesen et al., 2024) and increased crime rates (De Courson & Nettle, 2021), which would affect the experiences of emergency responders and their families. Another limitation related to the sample, which was predominantly comprised of spouses. Although this brought forth interesting insights, it would be beneficial for future research to further explore the impacts of emergency responder work on children, as parental job roles have significant impacts on child development (Perry-Jenkins et al., 2020). Likewise, relationships primarily consisted of heterosexual partners with the non-responder spouse being female; therefore, our knowledge of the dynamics between same-sex partnerships and emergency responders with male spouses is limited owing to the lack of studies conducted in these populations.

Implications

The findings from this systematic review bring to light the unique experiences of family members of emergency responders. Consequently, through an exploration of these experiences, several key implications for wider contextual change have been identified.

This review highlighted that emotional strain over the hardships of emergency responder work is highly common for family members, yet support is limited, with current interventions mainly focusing on the emergency responders (Hesketh & Tehrani, 2024) or on ways to further integrate families into supporting the emergency responder through psychological first-aid training (O’Toole et al., 2022). This emphasizes a critical need for the development of mental health interventions targeted specifically at family members, helping with the management of stress and supporting resilience to overcome the impacts of emergency responders’ jobs. Future support efforts should prioritize psychoeducation for families, not only to build understanding of how emergency responder work can affect well-being, but also to provide practical skills for managing emotional demands and identifying early signs of psychological distress. Such measures could help to shift the focus from reactive support to proactive resilience-building, ensuring that families are better equipped to sustain their own mental health alongside supporting their emergency responder loved one.

The findings of this study further accentuated the inadequacies of emergency responder organizations. Families reported an increased burden of managing household responsibilities and child rearing, which suggests that there is a lack of practical support on offer. Organizations that implement workplace family-support measures, such as offering flexitime, acknowledging personal situations, providing financial support programmes, and, overall, valuing the roles played by families, have been found to successfully reduce work–family conflict and simultaneously increase job satisfaction (Matias et al., 2017). Therefore, by implementing these measures within emergency responder organizations, both families and emergency responders could be empowered.

Future research

Media portrayals had a significant impact on shaping society’s perception on the popularity of emergency responder roles. In this regard, Liao states that ‘media exposure and usage have a significant impact on shaping views, attitudes, and behaviours’ (Liao, 2023, p. 4), which is consistent with the findings of this review. Future research should explore the extent to which media representations influence public understanding of emergency responder work and examine whether these portrayals contribute to unrealistic expectations or misconceptions among emergency responder families.

Future research should also consider whose voices remain underrepresented in the current evidence base. Much of the existing literature reflects the experiences of adult spouses, meaning that the impact of emergency responder work on the family is still largely seen through a partner-focused lens. Greater attention should be paid to how children make sense of a parent’s emergency responder role, including how exposure to parental stress, absence, emotional withdrawal, or heightened vigilance may shape their emotional well-being and family relationships over time. Similarly, future studies should seek to capture a wider range of family structures, including male spouses and same-sex partners, to better understand whether existing findings reflect shared family experiences or are shaped by gendered and heteronormative assumptions in the literature. This would allow for a more inclusive and developmentally sensitive understanding of how emergency responders’ work affects their families.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-synthesis has highlighted the significant challenges faced by family members of emergency responders. By synthesizing qualitative data, it was recognized that families are faced with several hardships, such as experiencing substantial emotional strain, increased responsibilities, forms of harassment, and lack of recognition and support. While the struggles of emergency responders are well established and supported, their families often face a silent burden, which can impact their mental health and relationships. Providing tailored psychological support and family-friendly workplace measures may help to improve the well-being of families and contribute to the overall mental health and performance of emergency responders themselves. Finally, this review has contributed to the literature by presenting a new perspective on the impact of societal and media portrayals of emergency responders among family members, a topic that has been relatively unexplored in the literature.
