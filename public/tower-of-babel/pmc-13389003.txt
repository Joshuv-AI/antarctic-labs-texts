Diverse configurations of binary asteroids explained by multi-generation satellites
Authors: Wen-Yue Dai, Bin Cheng, Yukun Huang, Yifei Jiao, Wen-Han Zhou, Kun-Yi Ren, Harrison Agrusa, Seth Jacobson, Eiichiro Kokubo, Sébastien Charnoz, Yang Yu, Hexi Baoyin, Junfeng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Binary asteroid systems are ubiquitous in the Solar System. Many of them originate from rotational breakup, where an asteroid’s spin-up triggers mass shedding and subsequent satellite formation from a transient debris disk. While prolate satellites on compact orbits are expected in this scenario, recent space missions revealed remarkable diversity of binary configurations, such as the contact-binary satellite Selam on a wide orbit around (152830) Dinkinesh. Here we show that multiple episodes of mass shedding and the resulting multi-generation satellites provide a unified framework for these diverse configurations. We find that a pre-existing satellite can strongly influence subsequent satellite formation pathways through disk-satellite and inter-satellite interactions. This mechanism explains the dynamical histories of the Dinkinesh system and several triple systems. Our analysis indicates that about 44% of known binaries have configurations indicative of multi-satellite histories, suggesting that a significantly greater diversity of binary asteroid configurations remains to be revealed.

Introduction

Near-Earth asteroids (NEAs) and small main-belt asteroids (MBAs) with diameters between 200 m and 20 km are thought to be rubble piles1,2, and most of binary asteroid systems among them (“small” binaries) are believed to be born from rotational mass shedding of the primary body3–5. Previous studies have suggested that a prolate satellite in a compact orbit close to the fluid Roche limit (FRL) (approximately 2.5 Rp, here Rp is the primary radii) and a top-shaped primary asteroid are expected in such small binary asteroid systems6–10. However, recent space missions have revealed unique and unexpected characteristics. Dimorphos, the satellite of the (65803) Didymos system (Fig. 1a, b), is oblate, with a smooth surface11. More surprisingly, the (152830) Dinkinesh system features a satellite, Selam, composed of two lobes in contact and on an unusually wide orbit (Fig. 1c–e)12. In addition, several asteroids in this size range have been found to have more than one satellite13 (Fig. 1f). Existing models in the literature14–17 often reconstruct this diversity through a single mass shedding event, and have proposed various satellite formation pathways corresponding to different final configurations. However, recent simulations8,18,19 suggest that the picture of satellite formation can be quite different from these models, and the morphological properties of Selam have still not been convincingly reproduced. Furthermore, for asteroids with a diameter from sub-kilometer to about 10 km, their typical collisional lifetime is from about 102 Myr to about 104 Myr20, which is much longer than their YORP spin-up timescale of several Myr. Thus, a small binary may experience a series of rotational mass shedding events during its lifetime5, for which the mass loss observed in the 311P binary system serves as an example21,22.

In this work, we show that multiple mass shedding events and a multi-satellite history provide a unified framework to explain the configuration diversity of binary asteroids. We investigate how a pre-existing satellite can affect the formation of subsequent satellites, leading to various final configurations. This mechanism accounts for the dynamical histories of the Dinkinesh system and other multiple asteroid systems, demonstrating that the observed diversity arises naturally from multi-generation satellite histories.

Results

To explore the satellite formation with the presence of a pre-existing one, we implemented a disk-satellite-primary model (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1). In our model, the matter shed from the primary forms a debris disk whose mass is Md. A pre-existing satellite with a mass of Ms is set to be in a circular orbit with a semi-major axis of as at the initial instant. According to Lucy's observation, the two lobes that build Selam have roughly the same diameter of about 220 m12. Assuming that they have the same bulk density as Dinkinesh, this corresponds to a lobe mass of approximately 0.02 Mp, where Mp is the mass of the primary. Direct N-body simulations of rotational mass shedding have shown that producing a satellite with a mass comparable to one of the two lobes of Selam typically requires an initial debris disk with a mass of order Md/Mp = 0.048. Thus, we set Md/Mp = 0.04 in our simulations, which is also compatible with previous theoretical assess23. Additionally, since the observed satellite serves as a lower bound of the post-shedding disk, it also supports an initial disk mass of several percent of the primary’s mass. Previous research18 suggests that if there is no pre-existing satellite, such a massive disk tends to create a single satellite shortly, which is quite different from Saturnian moons formed in low-mass rings24,25. The accretion regime

During post-mass-shedding evolution, disk particles are transferred beyond the FRL of the primary or fall back onto the primary; thus, the disk eventually dissipates. In the absence of pre-existing satellites, though some particles coming outside the FRL eventually escape, many of them rapidly aggregate and grow into the satellite(s)26,27. However, our simulations indicate that if a pre-existing satellite orbits near the FRL, it will continue to accrete the mass shed from the primary (Fig. 3a) and no newly born satellite will form, which is also consistent with previous studies5,28,29. We refer to this scenario as the accretion regime. A qualitative explanation is that a satellite has its feeding zone, in which the particles tend to eventually accrete onto the satellite30,31, and a close satellite can increase the particles’ relative velocity to depress the aggregate growth32. Assuming a particle is on a low eccentricity orbit with a semi-major axis of a, this feeding zone is estimated as ∣a − as∣ ≤ Δcr, where Δcr is the critical orbital separation for the feeding zone (see “Methods” section). If the feeding zone overlaps the FRL of the primary, particles exiting the Roche limit would enter the feeding zone; thus, the perturbation from the pre-existing satellite prevents the formation of second-generation satellites, and these debris tends to be captured by the satellite. Consequently, accretion onto the pre-existing satellite dominates when as − Δcr ≤ aFRL. Figure 2 illustrates this regime in the as–Ms plane, with the Didymos system serving as an example of this evolutionary pathway11,29. For a binary system in the accretion regime, mass transfer from the primary to the satellite is efficient, allowing the satellite to grow with each subsequent mass shedding event. Therefore, a binary system with a satellite currently close to the Roche limit can be constructed incrementally through a series of mass shedding events over its history.

The isolation regime

Due to orbit migration driven by tidal dissipation and the thermal forces (the BYORP effect or the binary Yarkovsky effect)33–35, the satellite may not remain in the orbit where it originally formed. The detailed migration history of the satellite could be very complicated36,37, and for simplicity, we applied the timescale of migration due to thermal forces as a representative timescale for our population-level estimate. As inward migration can finally lead to tidal disruption of the satellite, only outward migration is considered in this work. Considering that the time interval between two distinct mass shedding events usually spans several Myr, the pre-existing satellite may migrate outwards by several primary radii, becoming too distant to perturb the disk after the subsequent rotational mass shedding event7. For example, our simulations show that for a pre-existing satellite of Ms/Mp = 0.01 and as/Rp = 9, a new satellite is expected to form near the Roche limit within tens of days, transforming the original binary system into a triple system (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 3).Fig. 4Overview of all simulations with their corresponding evolution types.The red region indicates the corresponding parameter range for the accretion regime. The stability of such a system depends on the orbital separation between the two satellites, which must be large enough to avoid their close approaches. Otherwise, this triple system will be destabilized by the gravitational interaction between the satellites. Thus, the two satellites should remain dynamically isolated in order to survive. We refer to this scenario as the isolation regime. The newly born triple system should have an inner satellite near the Roche limit, while the outer satellite is well separated from the inner one, and the orbital eccentricity of both satellites should be small. The interaction regime

If the satellite is neither far nor close enough to the Roche limit when the mass shedding occurs, the system enters the interaction regime. In this scenario, large aggregates are expected to form near the Roche limit rapidly and interact with the satellite, leading to a wide variety of outcomes. For a system in the interaction regime, the satellite can perturb the orbits of the newly formed aggregates, increasing their eccentricity. This increases the relative velocity among these aggregates and prevents them from growing further. Since the orbits of these newly born aggregates are very close to the Roche limit and their spin is usually far from the locking state, the tidal interaction with the primary can also contribute to the eccentricity excitation.

Our simulations show that the excited eccentricity may eventually cause them to approach too close to the primary, followed by disruption by the tidal effect of the primary (Figs. 3c, 5b). We find that in this case, a part of the aggregate can be thoroughly disrupted into individual particles by the tidal forces, which then will be re-integrated into the disk, i.e., recycled back into the disk. As the aggregates are disrupted, the disk particles are “reloaded,” and new aggregates are created in the following evolution. Likewise, the new aggregates can be recycled back to the disk again, creating a loop of disruption and re-aggregation. Meanwhile, some fragments of the disrupted aggregate will enter highly excited orbits or even escape from the system, leading to mass loss from the system. Our simulations show that this recycling process tends to occur when the original satellite orbit is relatively close to the Roche limit (but not residing in the accretion regime; Fig. 4). Moreover, the period of this recycling cycle is about a few days according to our simulations (Fig. 3c), which is roughly consistent with the time interval between two close mass loss events observed in the 311P system 21. This suggests that the observed multitails near the 311P system21 may be due to the recycling process.

We also find that large aggregates may collide with the satellite in the interaction regime due to instability, and this can lead to the formation of a contact-binary satellite, just like Selam. Our simulations show that this is more likely to occur when the original satellite is further away from the Roche limit than in the recycling case. Aggregate-satellite scattering is also observed in our simulation, in which the aggregate (or satellite) enters an excited orbit. This eventually leads to a triple system with the outer satellite in an excited orbit (Fig. 3b). In addition, in some cases, the aggregate (or the satellite) may finally escape from the system after scattering. If the original satellite itself escapes, this results in a binary system in which the original satellite has been replaced by the new one. Notably, system evolution in the interaction regime often involves a combination of several of the above evolution types. For example, Figs. 3b, 5a show that the aggregate tidal disruption follows the satellite scattering, and, furthermore, the satellite in a highly excited orbit can lead the subsequent disk evolution to a very chaotic way. Dynamical atlas of binary asteroid evolution

Our results suggest that, for different initial satellite masses and orbits, the subsequent evolution of the primary-debris-satellite system can enter vastly different dynamic regimes (Fig. 6). For a satellite with a larger mass, both the maximum semi-major axis for the accretion regime and the interaction regime are wider; that is, a heavier satellite can perturb the disk more effectively. If the pre-existing satellite is close to the FRL when a mass shedding event occurs, the following evolution is in the accretion regime, where the satellite grows with each subsequent mass shedding event. In addition, the accretion of the shed material onto the satellite during these events can reshape its surface features, meaning that its surface age is actually relevant to the most recent mass shedding rather than its original formation. As a realistic example, the pre-DART-impact configuration of the Didymos system resides in the accretion regime. According to the DART mission data, the surface crater counts suggest that Dimorphos has a surface age of <0.3 Myr, which is fairly younger than the age of Didymos (about 12.5 Myr)11,38. This may indicate that the surface features of the satellite, Dimorphos, have been reshaped by a series of mass shedding events in the past, making it more challenging to determine the dynamical history of Dimorphos.

Things can be very different when the migration of a satellite is taken into account. Taking the Dinkinesh system as an example, the timescale of migration from the FRL to about tens of Rp is comparable to the time interval between two nearby mass shedding events, typically on the order of several Myr (see “Methods” section). Consequently, the original satellite may no longer be close to the FRL when a subsequent mass shedding occurs, causing the post-mass-shedding evolution to enter the interaction regime or even further into the isolation regime. Thus, satellite orbit migration will lead to a greater variety of post-mass-shedding evolutionary pathways and a richer diversity of the final configurations.

The current orbit of the satellite of the Dinkinesh system, Selam, is far from the FRL, suggesting its orbit migration in the past. In addition, its two-lobe shape is consistent with the origin of a slow-contact merger between similar-sized moonlets. If Selam was born in a single mass-shedding event, its progenitor disk would need to have been very massive (Md > 0.05 Mp), which typically tends to create only a single satellite18. Thus, Selam is supposed to be the result of an inter-aggregate collision near the Roche limit shortly after the mass shedding (roughly a few days). However, in this case, the tidal forces from the primary are strong enough to reshape the final satellite (Fig. 7b), and the following accretion of the disk material can also erase the neck connecting the two lobes. Triple systems with a small primary might also was formed by multiple mass shedding. The MBA triple system (3749) Balam has two satellites (Ms is roughly 0.1 Mp)41, and the orbit of the outer satellite is highly excited. Assuming that the inner satellite is pre-existing, its current mass and orbit correspond to the interaction regime (Fig. 1f). This indicates that the orbit of the outer satellite is likely the result of tidal disruption or satellite scattering after a mass shedding event. In addition, its configuration is not Hill-stable, indicating that it is a transient system on its way to the next satellite's close approach. Furthermore, if the pre-existing satellite migrates outward further, the following evolution will be in the isolation regime. This can result in a triple system whose two satellites are well dynamically isolated, and the inner satellite is close to the FRL. An example of this configuration is the NEA triple system 2001 SN263, which has an outer satellite on an orbit of about 10 Rp and an inner satellite near the Roche limit, and the eccentricities of the satellites are both relatively low13. This configuration suggests that the 2001 SN263 system was probably created from the post-mass-shedding evolution in the isolation regime.

The general framework proposed in this work may also work for not only small binaries, but also large asteroids (with a diameter of several hundreds kilometer) with satellites, such as (216) Kleopatra, for which exogenetic impacts serve as the trigger of mass shedding events and continuous moon formation is possible42,43. However, since their highly irregular shapes can significantly influence the evolution of the impact ejecta and the satellites, the final outcomes could be different from the prediction of the minimal model in this work.

Our analysis shows that the duet of primary asteroid mass shedding and satellite migration plays a significant role in the configuration diversity of small binary asteroids. Once a satellite has formed, it is expected to remain in the accretion regime, growing in subsequent mass shedding events. The outward migration of the pre-existing satellite can lead the system into the interaction or isolation regime, where the secondary satellite formation and inter-satellite interactions, such as scattering or collision, can take place. Specifically, the contact-binary satellite like Selam is likely to be the result of such a collision. Thus, configurations differing from a prolate satellite on a compact orbit near the FRL are more likely to be found in the parameter ranges corresponding to the interaction and isolation regimes. Methods

Gravitational N-body simulation

We performed gravitational N-body simulations with an original code DEMBody, which stands for the discrete element body, and incorporates classical granular dynamics and N-body self-gravity calculation10,44,45. In our code, the contact forces between two contacting particles are calculated with Hertz–Mindlin contact theory, according to their mutual overlap. A physically based rotational resistance model incorporating rolling and twisting was implemented into our code, in which a parameter β represents a statistical measure of actual particle non-sphericity46.

In our simulations, the radius of the particles ranges from 3 to 10 m, and the particle size distribution follows a power law distribution with an index of α = − 3, which is similar to the boulder distribution on the surface of Dimorphos8,11. The density of all particles was set to 3.5 g cm−3, which matches the grain density of the meteoritic analogs for Didymos8,47. For the particle contact model, we set the inter-particle friction coefficient, the resistance coefficient, and the non-sphericity parameter to μ = 0.8, ε = 0.5, and β = 0.8, respectively. This corresponds to a macroscopic internal friction angle of θ = 31∘45, which is close to that of Dimorphos48.

The pre-existing satellite in our model is modeled as a spherical rubble pile consisting of particles. To reduce the computational cost, in this rubble pile, the inter-particle cohesion is strong enough (c = 103 Pa), which means that the preexisting satellite is nearly a rigid body. Formation of contact-binary satellite

In our primary-debris-satellite simulation, the particles are too large to capture the details of outcomes of inter-aggregation and aggregation-satellite collision. We thus performed detailed simulations to explore the outcomes of collisions between self-gravitating rubble piles with the presence of external tidal forces, and special attention was paid to the formation of a contact-binary satellite. For simplicity, the rubble piles in our collision simulations are set to be a sphere. A two-step procedure is designed to construct the spherical rubble pile with a given radius. First, a granular assembly is created by simulating the gravitational collapse of a randomly distributed particle cloud53. In the initial particle cloud, the particle size distribution follows a power law with an index of −3, and the particle radii range from 2–5 m, which is smaller than that in our primary-debris-satellite simulation. The extra particles are then removed to generate a rubble-pile sphere.

To match the Selam formation scenario, two spherical particle aggregates with an equal mass of m = 0.02 Mp are considered in collision simulations. At the initial instant of the simulations, the two spheres are set to be nearly in contact, their relative velocity and collision angle being v and θ, respectively. Timescale of YORP-induced rotational breakup

The Yarkovsky-O’Keefe-Radzievskii-Paddack (YORP) effect is a thermal torque that modifies the spin rates and obliquities of asteroids due to thermal emission from their irregular surfaces54. This torque is considered a primary driver of rotational disruption in asteroids, contributing to the formation of binary systems5. The YORP effect has been shown to be extremely sensitive to the fine structure of the surface55, such as boulders and craters56–60. Therefore, the YORP torque on a particular asteroid changes frequently due to resurfacing events (e.g., impacts and mass-shedding). Satellite migration due to thermal forces

Binary asteroids experience modifications to their mutual orbits due to thermal forces, specifically the binary YORP (BYORP)34 and the binary Yarkovsky effects35,65. These effects arise from the recoil force produced by thermal emission.

The BYORP effect is caused by the asymmetric shape of the binary system. Because the net force summed over the entire surface does not cancel out, a residual force emerges, leading to changes in the mutual orbit. Meanwhile, the binary Yarkovsky effect arises from asymmetric heating conditions over the orbit due to mutual shadowing, particularly for spinning asteroids with nonzero thermal inertia. These two effects complement each other: BYORP only acts on synchronous asteroids whose spin and mutual orbital motion have the same period, whereas the binary Yarkovsky effect influences asynchronous asteroids. The BYORP effect can either expand or shrink the orbit, depending on the shape of the asteroids, while the binary Yarkovsky effect generally drives the system toward synchronization.
