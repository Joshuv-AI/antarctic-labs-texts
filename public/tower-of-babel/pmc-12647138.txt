Effect of strurt shape mechanical and absorption performance of periodic lattices
Authors: Sepideh Bayat, Ahmadreza Farjood, Zahra-Sadat Seyedraoufi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the mechanical behavior and energy absorption characteristics of Modified Periodic Lattice Structures (MPLS), a class of mechanical metamaterials fabricated through Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM) using PLA+. Five lattice samples with varying strut-hole diameters (MPLS-0, MPLS-5, MPLS-10, MPLS-15, and MPLS-20) were designed and subjected to uniaxial compression tests in accordance with ISO 13,314 standards. The impact of strut perforation on key mechanical metrics—including strength, energy absorption density (EAD), specific energy absorption (SEA), and efficiency of energy absorption (EAE)—was evaluated through both experimental testing and finite element (FE) simulations using Abaqus. The results revealed a clear trade-off between perforation size and mechanical integrity: MPLS-0 (solid struts) achieved the highest yield strength (13.61 MPa), EAD (13.26 J/cm³), and SEA (2.41 J/g), whereas MPLS-20 (largest perforations) dropped to 6.90 MPa, 6.04 J/cm³, and 1.15 J/g, respectively. Intermediate designs provided balanced outcomes: MPLS-5 and MPLS-10 preserved 83–75% of the solid strut strength while offering up to ~ 10% weight savings. FE simulations confirmed these findings and highlighted localized stress concentrations and torsional effects in hollow-strut lattices, especially in MPLS-15 and MPLS-20. Interestingly, higher-porosity lattices exhibited prolonged deformation before densification, suggesting potential advantages for energy dissipation and composite reinforcement despite reduced strength. Overall, moderate perforation (MPLS-5 and MPLS-10) offered an effective compromise between mass reduction and mechanical performance, emphasizing the role of controlled geometry in designing lightweight, impact-resistant components for aerospace, automotive, and protective systems.

Introduction

Mechanical metamaterials are among the most advanced engineering materials, known for their exceptional mechanical properties. Their unique characteristics are driving their adoption across a wide range of industries, including aerospace1, bio-medical2, impact absorbers3, reinforcements4, and vehicle industry5 to even arts6. These innovative engineering materials are renowned for their superior strength-to-weight ratios and exceptional specific energy absorption capabilities, outperforming conventional bulk materials7. Moreover, they offer significant advantages in preserving mechanical properties even when their geometries are optimized for weight reduction8. Furthermore, these engineering materials are distinguished by the strong influence of geometry on their mechanical properties, as they are composed of systematically patterned unit cells, BCC9–11, FCC12, SC13, Voronoi3,14,15, TPMS16–18, Graded19–22, or even combination of these lattices23–25.

Many scientists have investigated different attributes of these mechanical metamaterials for further scientific achievements. From their pore per inches26, strut diameter27, strut length28, hybrid unit-cells5. Others were fixated on the materials for different claims from using Ni3, Cu15, combined metallic layers7,14, IN71829, TPU17, PLA30,31, and other famous materials6,18,28,32. Moreover, many production methods were used to produce these materials including additive manufacturing5,10,33,34, electroforming14,17,26, or even investment casting16,35,36. Building on these principles, the primary focus of this study was on the effect of strut diameter, with additive manufacturing—using both metallic and non-metallic materials—employed as the main fabrication method5,37.

Triply Periodic Minimal Surface (TPMS) lattice structures are among the most well-known lattice architectures, widely recognized for their unique and superior mechanical properties38. A major challenge with these lattice structures lies in their fracture behavior, which usually features a combination of high strength and failure dominated by shear throughout the entire structure39. Numerous researchers have explored these lattice structures, providing valuable insights into this innovative class of mechanical metamaterials through the investigation of various unit-cell designs, including gyroid and primitive configurations5,24,40. Rohani Nejad et al.17, developed a modified version of these lattices using 3D printing and electroforming with metallic materials, reporting that removing the curves from the TPMS primitive structure led to improved mechanical properties. Their findings showed that the strength of the modified lattices was enhanced compared to the conventional versions41.

In the current study, the primary focus is to investigate the impact of varying hole diameters on the topological optimization of these lattice structures, particularly highlighting the negative effects that arise from increasing perforations. While adding holes may contribute to weight reduction, this research demonstrates that several critical mechanical parameters such as strength, energy absorption capacity, and overall structural integrity are adversely affected as hole size increases. The findings emphasize that, unless minimizing weight is an overriding priority, introducing larger holes in the lattice struts should be avoided to maintain optimal mechanical performance. This investigation provides new insights into the delicate balance between weight savings and mechanical robustness in the design of additive-manufactured mechanical metamaterials, contributing valuable knowledge to the optimization strategies for these advanced structures.

Experimental method

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the influence of incorporating hollow struts on the mechanical performance of mechanical metamaterials. Mechanical metamaterials, known for their architected structures and tunable properties, can have their behavior significantly altered through modifications at the unit-cell level. In this study, special attention is given to the effect of hollow struts as a design variable that could potentially enhance or tailor mechanical responses such as stiffness, strength, and energy absorption. Figure 1 provides a graphical overview of the research workflow, highlighting each stage of the investigation. The 3D modeling of the metamaterial structures was carried out using SolidWorks, where different lattice geometries incorporating hollow struts were designed. These digital models were then fabricated using Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM), a widely used additive manufacturing technique suitable for producing complex geometries with acceptable precision and repeatability. To assess the mechanical behavior of the fabricated samples, uniaxial compression tests were performed. These tests allowed for the observation and measurement of how the lattice structures deform under load, providing insight into their stiffness, failure modes, and overall mechanical response. The experimental data obtained from these tests served as a foundation for comparison and validation. Furthermore, Finite Element Method (FEM) simulations were employed to complement the experimental findings. The FEM models were developed to replicate the real test conditions as accurately as possible, enabling the prediction of stress distributions, deformation patterns, and potential points of failure within the structures. The numerical results were used not only to validate the experimental observations but also to extend the analysis beyond the limitations of physical testing. This combined experimental-numerical approach provides a deeper understanding of the mechanical behavior of metamaterials with hollow struts and lays the groundwork for future design optimizations.

CAD modeling of lattice structures

Triply Periodic Minimal Surface (TPMS) mechanical metamaterials are known for their distinctive micro and macro-scale architectures, which result in exceptional and tunable mechanical properties. These structures exhibit a continuous surface geometry that contributes to their lightweight nature and high strength-to-weight ratio, making them suitable for a variety of advanced engineering applications. Among different TPMS structures, the Primitive type has been widely studied for its mechanical advantages, particularly in energy absorption and load distribution36. Building on previous research, an enhanced version of the TPMS Primitive lattice was selected for this study in order to investigate the effect of hollow struts on its mechanical strength and minimize the occurrence of shear-related fractures, a common failure mode in such geometries. The selected TPMS designs were developed using SolidWorks CAD software, with the goal of optimizing their structural performance while maintaining manufacturability through additive manufacturing techniques. Figure 2 illustrates the CAD modeling process and provides the detailed dimensions of the lattice geometries used in this research. The modeling workflow was organized into three main stages. The first stage involved the creation of a single unit cell. This unit cell was designed according to the dimensional guidelines shown in Fig. 2, featuring three intersecting rods (or pipe types) connected in such a way that they form a central junction at the midpoint of each rod. In the second stage, the unit cell was patterned along the X and Y axes to form a two-dimensional plane, establishing the basic in-plane lattice arrangement. Finally, in the third stage, this 2D plane was extruded along the Z axis to construct the full three-dimensional lattice structure used in the experiments. To explore the influence of strut internal hole on mechanical performance, the finalized TPMS lattice structures were fabricated using a PLA hyper series filament with varying strut hole diameters: 0 mm, 5.0 mm, 10.0 mm, 15.0 mm, and 20.0 mm. These variants will be referred to throughout this study as MPLS-0, MPLS-5, MPLS-10, MPLS-15, and MPLS-20, respectively. This classification provides a consistent naming convention for comparing the mechanical behavior of the different lattice configurations under experimental and numerical analysis.

Experimental examinations

The compression tests on the fabricated specimens were conducted in accordance with the ISO 13,314 standard42. which provides guidelines for evaluating the mechanical behavior of porous and cellular metals under uniaxial compression. This standard ensures consistency in testing methodology and allows for reliable comparison of results across similar studies. For the numerical analysis, the Finite Element Method (FEM) was employed to simulate the experimental conditions. The simulations were carried out using Abaqus software, a widely used and robust platform for advanced mechanical modeling. The finite element simulations were conducted to replicate the uniaxial compression tests, allowing for a detailed examination of stress distribution, deformation patterns, and failure mechanisms within the lattice structures. Beyond validating the experimental results, this computational approach offered deeper insights into structural behavior that could not be fully captured through physical testing alone.

Results and discussion

The MPLS lattice structures were investigated through uniaxial compression testing to evaluate their overall mechanical performance. A comprehensive analysis was conducted, examining several key attributes including mechanical behavior, energy absorption density (EAD), and specific energy absorption (SEA). These parameters were assessed through both experimental testing and finite element simulations, providing a detailed understanding of the structural response and energy absorption capabilities of the lattices under compressive loading.

Compressive behavior

In this section, the compressive response of the MPLS samples is examined based on the results of the experimental tests. The outcomes are presented in the form of force–displacement curves, stress–strain plots, normalized stress–strain plots, strength-to-weight ratio graphs, and compression sequences of the samples. Figure 5 illustrates the force–displacement curves obtained from the uniaxial compression tests, which provide valuable insights into the mechanical behavior of the lattice structures. A consistent trend was observed: the overall strength of the lattices increased as the diameter of the hollow sections within the struts decreased. This improvement can be attributed to the presence of thicker solid regions in the struts at smaller hole diameters, which enhances their load-bearing capacity. Among the tested configurations, the rod-like struts without hollow sections demonstrated the highest strength, as they were able to tolerate the greatest applied forces before failure. This trend indicates that reducing the hollow portion within the struts significantly improves the structural integrity and mechanical performance of the lattices under compressive loading.

Comparing the lattices, Fig. 7(a) illustrates the yield strength values of the MPLS lattice structures as a function of strut hole diameter. The data reveal a clear inverse relationship: as the hole diameter increases, the yield strength systematically decreases. The solid strut configuration, MPLS-0, exhibits the highest yield strength at 13.61 MPa, indicating superior load-bearing capacity due to its uninterrupted solid cross-section. In contrast, MPLS-20, with the largest internal cavity, demonstrates the lowest yield strength at 6.90 MPa, reflecting a significant reduction in structural integrity caused by material removal. This decline in yield strength can be attributed to the weakening effect of increased void volume within the struts. As the hole diameter increases from 0 to 20 mm, the load-carrying cross-sectional area of the struts is reduced, concentrating stress and promoting earlier onset of plastic deformation. Notably, the most dramatic reductions in strength occur between MPLS-0 to MPLS-10, suggesting that even modest increases in hole diameter can significantly compromise mechanical performance. This highlights a critical design trade-off between weight reduction and structural robustness. Figure 7(b) presents the strength-to-weight ratio, a key metric for evaluating structural efficiency, particularly in applications where minimizing mass is essential. Similar to the yield strength results, the strength-to-weight ratio declines as the hole diameter increases. MPLS-0, the heaviest and strongest sample, has the highest strength-to-weight ratio at 2.47 MPa, while MPLS-20 drops to 1.68 MPa. Interestingly, although MPLS-0 has the highest absolute weight, its superior mechanical performance compensates, yielding the most efficient ratio.

The gradual reduction in strength-to-weight ratio across the series reflects diminishing returns in mechanical efficiency as material is removed. While the initial introduction of hollow sections may offer substantial weight savings, these benefits are offset by a more rapid loss in structural performance. For example, the relatively small decrease from 2.47 MPa (MPLS-0) to 2.2 MPa (MPLS-5) suggests an acceptable trade-off, but the continued decline to 1.68 MPa in MPLS-20 reflects a point at which further material reduction compromises overall efficiency. Together, the results from Figs. 7(a) and 7(b) underscore the importance of carefully balancing mechanical strength and weight in the design of lattice structures. While introducing hollow features into the struts can reduce weight, excessive removal of material severely undermines mechanical reliability and efficiency. Therefore, from a practical design perspective, configurations such as MPLS-5 or MPLS-10 may represent optimal middle grounds offering meaningful weight savings without incurring critical losses in strength or mechanical performance. These findings are particularly relevant for applications in aerospace, automotive, and lightweight structural components, where both strength and mass efficiency are essential.

Figure 8 presents a comparative analysis of the different cross-sectional areas of the best-performing (MPLS-0) and the lowest-performing (MPLS-20) lattice samples in terms of their yield strength. The main objective was to highlight and demonstrate the inherent complexity of these structures, particularly in relation to the direction in which external forces are applied. Lattice structures, by their very nature, often exhibit highly anisotropic behavior, meaning that their mechanical response can vary significantly depending on the orientation of the applied load. In many cases, the same lattice geometry may display excellent stiffness or energy absorption in one direction, while being relatively weak or compliant in another. This directional sensitivity has been a recurring observation in numerous previous studies, where the performance of such architectures was shown to be heavily dependent on loading orientation. Understanding and characterizing this directional dependence is therefore essential, not only for predicting the mechanical performance of lattice structures, but also for tailoring their design toward specific applications where forces may act in multiple or unexpected directions. In this investigation, the stress values were recalculated using varied cross-sectional area assumptions: 625 mm², 528 mm², 471 mm², 396 mm², and 602 mm². This approach was used to assess the sensitivity of the calculated yield strength to the definition of the load-bearing area, providing further insight into how geometry influences performance interpretation. By adjusting the cross-sectional area in the stress calculation, it becomes evident that the perceived mechanical performance of the samples can change notably, especially in hollow structures. Smaller cross-sectional areas result in higher stress values for the same force, reflecting a more localized concentration of load. For the MPLS-0 sample (solid struts), the highest area value of 625 mm² was used, aligning with its fully dense geometry. As expected, this sample consistently demonstrated the highest yield strength, regardless of area adjustment, confirming its superior structural integrity. Conversely, for the MPLS-20 sample, which contains the largest hollow core, a significantly smaller effective cross-sectional area such as 396 mm² was considered. When recalculated using this area, the yield strength appears relatively higher than when using the nominal external area. However, even with this adjusted value, MPLS-20 remains the weakest sample, reinforcing that geometric hollowing significantly impairs mechanical resistance. This analysis demonstrates the importance of carefully selecting the effective cross-sectional area for stress calculations in latticed and metamaterial structures. Particularly in designs with internal voids, the choice of area definition (e.g., external vs. net cross-sectional area) can influence the reported strength values and, subsequently, the design decisions. In the context of performance comparison, normalizing or clearly defining area assumptions is critical for fair and accurate assessment of mechanical behavior. Ultimately, the recalculated data shown in Fig. 8 supports the earlier findings: MPLS-0 remains the most robust, while MPLS-20 is the least, regardless of how stress is defined. However, this figure also emphasizes the nuanced role of geometry in interpreting strength, especially when transitioning from traditional solid designs to architected metamaterials with engineered porosity.

FEA

The finite element analysis (FEA) was conducted using Abaqus software, with the geometry and boundary conditions illustrated in Fig. 9. The simulation employed C3D10M elements and included two reference points to define the boundary conditions. A dynamic explicit solver was used for the analysis, incorporating rigid upper and lower platens, and a mass scaling factor of 160,000 was applied to both the full MPLS lattice and its unit cell to accelerate computation. Moreover, the lattice structures were examined through a simple plastic model without any fracture attributes, its worth mentioning that the mechanical properties were extracted through a standard tensile test from the bulk material which repeated for 3 times. As shown in Fig. 9, BC1 corresponds to the upper platen, which moves downward along the negative Z-axis, simulating the compression. BC2 represents the lower platen, which is fully constrained (ENCASTRE condition). Reference Point 2 (RP2) was assigned to the bottom platen to define it as a rigid body, while Reference Point 1 (RP1) was used to record the reaction force generated during loading, which is essential for deriving the force-displacement and stress-strain data. This setup accurately replicates the experimental compression test conditions and ensures consistency between the simulated and physical behaviors of the MPLS lattices.

A comparison between MPLS-0 (solid struts) and MPLS-20 (largest strut perforations) highlights distinct differences in stress distribution and structural response. The MPLS-0 lattice exhibits a relatively uniform stress field and higher load-bearing capacity, reflecting efficient force transfer through the solid struts. Conversely, MPLS-20 shows pronounced stress concentrations around the thinned strut regions, which promote premature yielding and result in reduced mechanical performance. The unit cell results reinforce these trends, showing how local geometry modifications significantly affect global behavior. These comparative simulations not only validate the experimental findings but also emphasize the importance of geometric optimization in the design of mechanical metamaterials for improved performance.

Meanwhile, it is evident that the stress concentration observed in the MPLS-20 structure leads to torsional deformation at the edges of the struts. This behavior may appear as a mechanical drawback at first glance; however, it presents a potentially valuable characteristic when considering applications such as locking reinforcement in composite structures. Although it is true that hollow struts generally exhibit reduced mechanical strength compared to their solid counterparts, the observed torsional response can introduce a more gradual and porous deformation phase before the onset of the densification region. This extended energy-absorbing behavior is particularly significant in reinforcing composites, where controlled deformation and energy dissipation are critical. Therefore, despite their lower intrinsic strength, hollow-strut lattices like MPLS-20 may offer unique mechanical advantages in specific engineering applications requiring both structural flexibility and effective energy absorption.

The Energy Absorption Density (EAD) values for the MPLS samples exhibit a clear descending trend as the diameter of the strut holes increases. MPLS-0, which features a solid strut without any perforation, achieves the highest EAD value of 13.26 MJ/m³, indicating its superior ability to absorb mechanical energy during compression. However, when maximum energy absorption is the primary design goal, solid or minimally perforated struts—as in MPLS-0 and MPLS-5 clearly outperform their more porous counterparts.

The Specific Energy Absorption (SEA) values, measured in joules per gram (J/g), provide key insights into the energy efficiency of each MPLS lattice configuration under compressive loading. Among the tested samples, MPLS-0 recorded the highest SEA value of 2.41 J/g, indicating the strongest capability to absorb mechanical energy relative to its mass. As small holes were introduced into the struts (MPLS-5 and MPLS-10), SEA values slightly decreased to 2.29 J/g and 2.25 J/g, respectively. These reductions, though modest, highlight the trade-off between material removal and energy absorption efficiency. While these slightly perforated structures still maintain a favorable strength-to-weight ratio, the initiation of hollow sections begins to reduce their effectiveness in high-impact applications where maximizing energy absorption per unit mass is critical. The trend becomes more pronounced with MPLS-15 (1.91 J/g) and MPLS-20 (1.15 J/g), which exhibit a significant decline in SEA performance. As the hole diameter increases, the structural rigidity of the struts decreases, leading to premature local failures and lower energy dissipation capability. In particular, MPLS-20, with its substantial perforation, shows the least efficiency in energy absorption, suggesting that it may no longer be suitable for load-bearing or impact-mitigation roles without additional reinforcement. These findings clearly indicate that while strut perforation can be beneficial for reducing weight or enhancing porosity, it must be carefully balanced to avoid compromising energy absorption performance. Optimizing strut geometry with minimal but strategic material reduction is key for maintaining structural integrity and functional performance.

Conclusion

This study explored the mechanical performance and energy absorption capabilities of a novel class of mechanical metamaterials MPLS (Modified Periodic Lattice Structures) produced using FDM additive manufacturing. A series of lattice structures with varying strut-hole diameters (MPLS-0 to MPLS-20) were fabricated using PLA and tested under uniaxial compression. Both experimental tests and finite element simulations were conducted to assess how changes in strut geometry affect key mechanical metrics, such as strength, energy absorption density, and specific energy absorption. The aim was to evaluate how structural optimization through geometry modification can influence the material’s ability to carry load and dissipate energy effectively, particularly for potential use in lightweight and impact-resistant applications.

The compressive tests, carried out according to ISO 13,314 standards, revealed that the introduction of strut holes significantly influenced the strength and energy absorption capabilities of the lattices. The results indicated that solid struts (MPLS-0) provided the highest mechanical performance, with a gradual decline observed as the hole diameter increased. This behavior was reflected in both force-displacement and stress-strain responses, with clear yield point reductions from 13.61 MPa for MPLS-0 to 6.90 MPa for MPLS-20.Moreover, strength-to-weight ratios demonstrated a similar trend, with MPLS-0 again outperforming other variants. This highlights the trade-off between weight reduction and mechanical integrity when introducing strut perforations. While lattice structures with partial hollow sections (MPLS-5 and MPLS-10) maintained acceptable mechanical properties, excessive perforation in MPLS-15 and MPLS-20 compromised the structural load-bearing capacity. The balance between mass savings and mechanical performance is therefore critical, especially when these materials are considered for applications requiring both strength and lightweight characteristics, such as aerospace or protective gear.The finite element simulations using Abaqus software confirmed and supported the experimental data. Stress concentrations were observed around the edges of the struts, particularly in MPLS-20, which exhibited torsional distortions. This phenomenon, while detrimental in terms of pure mechanical resistance, may offer advantageous locking mechanisms in composite applications. The ability of certain structures to deform more before densification opens new avenues for using such lattices as energy-dissipating or load-distributing elements in sandwich structures and composites.
