xInv: Explainable Optimization of Inverse Problems
Sean Memery, Kevin Denamganai, Anna Kapron-King, Kartic Subr

Abstract
Inverse problems are central to a wide range of fields, including healthcare, climate science, and agriculture. They involve the estimation of inputs, typically via iterative optimization, to some known forward model so that it produces a desired outcome. Despite considerable development in the explainability and interpretability of forward models, the iterative optimization of inverse problems remains largely cryptic to domain experts. We propose a methodology to produce explanations, from traces produced by an optimizer, that are interpretable by humans at the abstraction of the domain. The central idea in our approach is to instrument a differentiable simulator so that it emits natural language events during its forward and backward passes. In a post-process, we use a Language Model to create an explanation from the list of events. We demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach with an illustrative optimization problem and an example involving the training of a neural network.

Introduction

The problem of estimating inputs (or parameters) that elicit a specified response from a simulation model has enjoyed much attention from mathematicians and computer scientists. These
inverse problems
are typically solved via iterative updates of hypothesized input (or parameter) values. At each iteration, the outcome of the resulting
forward simulation
is used to update the values and the procedure is repeated until convergence. The overall process remains largely opaque and does not lend itself to explanation regardless of the underlying optimization procedure used for the updates. The generation of explanations involves a tedious analysis procedure involving the domain expert, an expert in optimization and a detailed trace from the iterative optimizer. We propose a simple methodology to enable human-centric solution of inverse problems using traditional optimizers.

Explainable optimization can be achieved using a variety of methods. In some (rare) cases, such as rule-based methods
freytag1987rule
or symbolic regression
Angelis2023ArtificialII
, the optimization scheme is designed to inherently lend itself to explanation. Sometimes, even if the optimizer does not permit this, it is possible to construct surrogates using methods such as model distillation
craven1995extracting
;
liu2018improvinginterpretabilitydeepneural
or response surface
Box1951OnTE
;
myers1995response
. Failing these, post hoc explanation techniques are employed including analysis of feature importance
lundberg2017unifiedapproachinterpretingmodel
, sensitivity
saltelli2004sensitivity
and visualization
ggplot2
. Our method aligns best with post hoc methods, with the added benefits of considering a human-in-the-loop due to its interactivity. Modern Language Models (LM) are the obvious choice for explainability, especially when their knowledge of the application domain can be leveraged. However, they are infamously unreliable as optimizers or even at interpreting simulation traces containing numerical values
cherian2024llmphycomplexphysicalreasoning
.

The key idea we introduce in this paper is to instrument the optimization process to generate a secondary trace in natural language, both during its forward and backward (differentiable) paths (see Figure
1
).
This involves modifying the optimizer to emit three signals: events, rewards and updates. This step will, in principle, be done by the optimization expert. Then, we define transformation functions that
map these signals to natural language descriptions. These transformations will ideally be defined by domain experts. Finally, we implement a
post hoc
scheme that exploits LMs to derive explanations from the natural language trace. We demonstrate that this simple idea provides LMs with valuable contextual information enabling interactive queries and explanation.

We validate the efficacy of our method in two contexts. First, we present qualitative and quantitative results from an illustrative problem of optimizing a railroad, using multiple standard optimizers, in a differentiable physics simulation. Then we demonstrate the versatility of our approach by applying it to explain decisions by the optimizer while training a small LM.

2.1
Illustrative inverse problem involving a simulator and iterative optimization

We illustrate our method using an optimization problem involving the design of a trajectory
\tau_{\theta}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with parameters
\theta
specifying the curve. Consider
\tau_{\theta}
to also be parameterized by
u\in[0,1]
, so that
\tau_{\theta}(u=0)
and
\tau_{\theta}(u=1)
are the start and end points of the curve respectively.
We seek to minimize

\displaystyle\argmin_{\theta}\left[\;\int\limits_{0}^{1}{t(u)}\;\mathrm{d}{u}+\int\limits_{0}^{1}{c(u)}\;\mathrm{d}{u}\;\right]

(1)

where the integrals correspond to the time taken for an object (say a train) to travel from
\tau_{\theta}(u=0)
to
\tau_{\theta}(u=1)
and the construction cost (say of the railroad) respectively. The functions
t(u)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
c(u)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
are the time and cost associated with an infinitesimal length along the trajectory at
u
. The resulting optimization is non-trivial when the trajectory is constrained to negotiate obstacles (buildings, trees, ponds, etc.), since they affect dynamics (penalty on acceleration) as well as construction costs.

We consider three forces acting on the train–driving force, frictional force and force due to air resistance–which lead to three components of the acceleration
a_{d}(u)
,
a_{\mu}(u)
and
a_{\eta}(u)
respectively. Obstacles impose their individual penalties on acceleration
a_{o}(u)
leading to a net acceleration of
a=a_{d}+a_{\mu}+a_{\eta}+a_{o}
where
a_{d}
is proportional to the radius of curvature
\kappa(u)
,
a_{\mu}
and
a_{\eta}
depend on the velocity
v(u)
and
a_{o}
depends on the obstacle intersecting
\tau_{\theta}(u)
.

We represent
\tau_{\theta}(u)
using a Bézier curve of order
n
where
\theta
represents the control points
\mathbf{p}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},\;i=0,1,\cdots,n
. The trajectory
\tau_{\theta}(u)
and its derivative
\dot{\tau}_{\theta}(u)
(w.r.t. u) are given by

\displaystyle\tau_{\theta}(u)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n}C_{n,i}\;(1-u)^{n-i}\;u^{i}\;\mathbf{p}_{i}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\dot{\tau}_{\theta}(u)=n\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}C_{n-1,i}\;(1-u)^{n-1-i}\;u^{i}\;(\mathbf{p}_{i+1}-\mathbf{p}_{i})

respectively while the curvature is
\kappa(u)=||\dot{\tau}_{\theta}(u)\times\ddot{\tau}_{\theta}(u)||/||\dot{\tau}_{\theta}(u)||^{3}
.
We discretize the integrals in Equation
2
and approximate them as a left Riemann sum using
N
intervals.
The simulator proceeds in
N
steps where at the
m^{th}
step, the length of curved section
s_{m}
is obtained in closed form and the net acceleration for the
m^{th}
interval
a_{m}
is calculated based on the current dynamics state and obstacle constraints.
Using these, the time to travel the
m^{th}
interval is obtained from elementary equations of motion as
t_{m}=\left(-v_{m}+\sqrt{v_{m}^{2}+2a_{m}s_{m}}\right)/a_{m}
.
The cost
c_{m}
for the
m^{th}
interval is calculated as
c_{m}=\sigma_{g}(\mathbf{x}_{m})+\sigma_{o}(\mathbf{x}_{m})
where
\mathbf{x}_{m}=\tau_{\theta}(m/N)
,
\sigma_{g}
is a global cost associated with the terrain and
\sigma_{o}
is a local cost associated with nearby obstacles.
We use a Perlin noise model for the global cost.
Finally, the velocity
v_{m+1}
is updated via (explicit) time integration of the calculated acceleration and the calculation is repeated for the next step (
m+1
).
The resulting optimization is

\displaystyle\argmin_{\theta}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{N}\left[\;\frac{-v_{m}+\sqrt{v_{m}^{2}+2a_{m}s_{m}}}{a_{m}}\;+\;\left(\sigma_{g}(\mathbf{x}_{m})+\sigma_{o}(\mathbf{x}_{m})\right)s_{m}\right]

(2)

where all variables with the subscript
m
depend on the control points, and hence on parameters
\theta
.
At each iteration in the optimization, the simulation in Algorithm
1
is run for
m
ranging from
0
to
N-1
and the loss in the above optimization is calculated.
The gradients (w.r.t.
\theta
) of the loss are calculated via autodifferentiation of
t_{m}
and
\sigma_{o}
.

Input:

m
,
v_{m}
,
\theta=\{\mathbf{p}_{i}\}_{i=1,\cdots,n}
;

//
step number, velocity, control points

Output:

t_{m}
,
c_{m}
,
v_{m+1}
;

//
time and cost for
m^{th}
interval, velocity for next step

\mathbf{x}_{m}\leftarrow\tau_{\theta}(m/N)
;

a_{d}\leftarrow a\exp(-0.15\kappa)
;

//
driving acceleration

a_{\mu}\leftarrow-\mu_{\mathrm{fric}}\mathrm{sign}(v_{m})
;

//
deceleration due to frictional forces

a_{\eta}\leftarrow-\mu_{\mathrm{air}}v_{m}|v_{m}|
;

//
deceleration due to air resistance

a_{o}\leftarrow\mathrm{ObstacleConstraints}(\mathbf{x}_{m})
;

//
obstacle-induced deceleration

a_{m}\leftarrow a_{d}+a_{m}u+a_{e}ta+a_{o}
;

s_{m}\leftarrow\mathrm{ObjectConstraints}(m)
;

//
length of curve in
m^{th}
interval

t_{m}\leftarrow\left(-v_{m}+\sqrt{v_{m}^{2}+2a_{m}s_{m}}\right)/a_{m}
;

//
time to traverse
m^{th}
interval

c_{m}\leftarrow\sigma_{g}(\mathbf{x}_{m})+\sigma_{o}(\mathbf{x}_{m})
;

//
construction cost for
m^{th}
interval

v_{m+1}\leftarrow\text{clip}(v_{m}+a_{m}t_{m},v_{\min},v_{\max})
;

//
update velocity

return

(t_{m},c_{m},v_{m+1})
;

2.2
Generating an optimization trace in natural language

Our methodology consists of three phases: Emission of signals within the optimizer, functions to generate a trace in natural language, and post hoc generation of explanations.
At each step of the optimization process
k
, we instrument the optimizer to emit events, rewards, and parameter updates:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{k}

\displaystyle=\{e_{j}^{i}\}_{j=1}^{|E_{k}|}

a set of events: observable behavior of the system

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{k}

\displaystyle=[r_{k}^{1},r_{k}^{2},\ldots]

a set of rewards: quantitative performance metrics

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{k}

\displaystyle=\{\Delta\theta_{j}^{k}\}_{j=1}^{p}

a set of updates: changes to optimization parameters

(5)

Then, we deploy transformation functions, (
\Phi_{E}
,
\Phi_{R}
, and
\Phi_{U}
) defined by the domain expert, to convert the signals emitted by the optimizer into natural language representations.
In our illustrative example,
\Phi_{E}
transforms a restricted set of event types: grid position (world coordinates translated to be within a
100\times 100
grid), speed, acceleration, curvature, object influence entry, and object influence exit.
Each value is transformed to a simple natural language description that characterizes how the value changed since the last event step.
For instance, acceleration changes are described as “Change in acceleration: small (positive), at grid position (10, 20)” where the magnitude (small, moderate, large, etc.) is scaled relative to the average change observed throughout the simulation, and the direction is indicated in parentheses.

\Phi_{R}
transforms reward information by capturing differences in time and cost since the last optimization step, i.e the difference between
R_{k}
and
R_{k-1}
. These differences are scaled by the average change across the optimization process and labeled with qualitative descriptors, such as “Change in time: high (positive)” or “Change in cost: moderate (negative).”

\Phi_{U}
transforms the change in control points since the last optimization step. For each control point, it characterizes the change in world coordinates, scaled by the average change across the entire optimization process. The natural language representation includes both magnitude and direction information, such as “Control point 1: Magnitude: very small (ESE),” where ESE indicates the east-southeast direction of movement. Importantly,
\Phi_{U}
also incorporates contextual information about nearby obstacles, comparing previous and new positions: “Previous position: small sandbox is close by, small valley is close by. New position: small sandbox is close by, small valley is close by.”

2.3
Generating post hoc explanations from natural language traces

We then utilize a language model to generate descriptions of the optimization by conditioning on the natural language simulation trace. The description generation is a two step process:

1.

Step-Level Descriptions
. For each step
k
, we generate a description utilising: preceding events
\Phi_{E}(\mathcal{E}_{k-1})
, current events
\Phi_{E}(\mathcal{E}_{k})
, and parameter updates
\Phi_{U}(\mathcal{U}_{k})
.

2.

Global Description Synthesis
combines all step-level descriptions along with reward change information into a coherent narrative. Importantly, reward changes
\Phi_{R}(\mathcal{R}_{k}-\mathcal{R}_{k-1})
are incorporated directly into this global synthesis.

Importantly, event and update sample rates are utilized to control the granularity of the descriptions. An event sample rate of
10
logs events every
10
simulation steps, while an update sample rate of
5
logs updates every
5
simulation steps. This is useful for controlling the conciseness of descriptions.

Experimental Results

3.1
Optimization Validation

We performed an extensive number of optimizations, across instances, using Adam, SGD, rmsprop, and sign_sgd. We tested with linear, quadratic, and cubic objective functions. We evaluate them on
200
random seeds, with
250
optimization steps and the following parameters: learning rate 5e-3, number of obstacles
20
, number of control points
16
, and learning rate scheduler ‘cosine’. The results are presented in Figure
2
, as the relative savings for both time and cost, aggregated across random seeds to normalize for variability across instances (different obstacles and positions).
As shown, both metrics are improved by the optimizers. Notably, the cost metric exhibits a larger absolute improvement compared to time, reflecting the optimization’s effectiveness on this aspect of the objective. However, we observe substantial standard deviation across instances highlighting the suitability of our chosen optimization problem for robust and generalized evaluation. Further results are presented in the supplemental material.

3.2
Conversational Optimization Interaction

We highlight the capabilities of our methodology by integrating our trace directly into an LM agent (GPT-4.1 for this section).
This allows a natural interaction between the user and the domain expert, who can request certain optimizations and explanation in natural language. We exploit several key capabilities of LMs. First, the user can request a specific optimization to be run, having the LM initiating it and returning a detailed description of the process.
Second, users can request specific state changes, and the LM calls upon tools to directly modify the state, e.g. adding or removing obstacles, moving control points, changing obstacle properties, etc.
Finally, the LM requests to observe the output of
\Phi_{E}
and
\Phi_{U}
for a specific optimization directly. Our methodology enables examples, such as the following, for a user interacting with a domain-aware AI assistant.

3.3
Question-answering Evaluation

We evaluate our explanations by testing if they help an LM identify which obstacle removal would most improve performance. Using
50
random seeds with
12
obstacles and
12
control points each, we asked the LM to predict obstacle removals that would maximize improvement in either time or cost metrics, for both original and optimized paths. Ground truth answers were determined by simulating each individual obstacle removal for original path questions, and by re-running optimizations for each removed obstacle for optimized path questions.

The LM receives obstacle positions and radii, control point positions, and initial path events from
\Phi_{E}
, along with one of three explanation types: the complete optimization explanation (
Full description
), descriptions for each optimization step (
Step Level descriptions
), or the natural language updates from
\Phi_{U}
for each step (
Update description
). Results from the question-answering experiment are shown in Figure
3
.
All LMs evaluated outperform random chance (1/num_obstacles = 0.085), indicating that the natural language descriptions produced by our method provide useful, actionable information.
Performance is consistent across question types and description formats, with smaller Llama models
(
llama3modelcard
;
llama4modelcard
)
matching or even surpassing GPT-4.1.
Notably, GPT-4.1-nano performs worse than expected, likely due to the greater context length required for step level and update descriptions, leading to less understanding in smaller models.
Importantly, the full description achieves comparable accuracy to the longer, less summarized alternatives, demonstrating the efficiency and effectiveness of our approach for supporting downstream reasoning tasks.

3.4
Evaluation of robustness of explanations

We address the evaluation of the question
“How informative are explanations?”

by determining whether the parameters (optimized control points) can be discriminated from their noisy versions (distractors) when conditioning on the explanations of the whole optimization process and the control points’ initial positions.
We report success rates in matching descriptions across different levels of noise using an LM.
Our prompts, detailed in the supplementary material, provide the grid positions (within a
100\times 100
grid–hereafter referred to as grid coordinate space) of the initial control points rather than the exact or floating-point numerical positions, in order to alleviate LMs’ susceptibility to numerical understanding and processing abilities (NUPA -
yang2025number
).

We generate explanations via three types of descriptions as in Section
3.3
: Full, Step Level, and Update.
We generate distractors by adding Gaussian noise to the target control points’ final positions with increasing standard deviations
\sigma\in\{0.02,0.06,0.1,0.15,0.2\}
, within the floating-point coordinate space. We expect that the larger the value of
\sigma
the easier the discrimination task will be.
To keep a relatively small context length, we experiment with one distractor for each task. The LMs are prompted with
2
candidates (in an unknown, random order), one is a distractor while the other is the target that must be identified. As a control (
Numerical
), we use a method where the LM knows grid positions directly (without using the explanations). This represents a ‘best case’ scenario and could be considered an upper bound.

We experiment with a dataset of
10
randomly sampled instances (obstacles, cost Perlin noise, initial control points) and hyperparameters detailed in the supplementary material.
We aggregate results for each method over this dataset with
2
seeds each for distractor generation and
2
seeds to condition the LM test. This yields
40
data points per method.

The results are plotted in Figure
4
, showing that, even at the lowest noise level (
\sigma=0.02
), most explanation types perform above chance (0.50), demonstrating that LMs can extract meaningful information from our explanations of optimization processes.
The quick convergence (as soon as
\sigma\geq 0.06
) of all methods to near-perfect performance as distractor noise levels increase validates that our explanations are informative to some extent.
On the other hand, the performance differences at lower noise levels highlight the importance of the explanation design when subtle discrimination is required.
We leave it to subsequent work to consider more sophisticated explanation designs.
We refer readers to the supplementary material for results using different LMs.

3.5
Evaluation of explanations via survey

To evaluate our generated explanations, we conducted a user study with
50
participants on Prolific. A detailed explanation of the optimization task and survey questions (see supplemental material) was provided and each participant rated how well a text description (generated by our method) matched a visualization, using a
1
–
5
scale. Each visualization showed five static images from intermediate stages of an optimization, illustrating the change in control points at each step. Participants rated five descriptions, presented in random order.

For each description, two sets of images were presented. The optimization matching the description (referred to as the target) and a different set where obstacle positions were shuffled (referred to as the distractor). These sets were presented in random order, and participants were asked to rate how well the description matched each variant. The time taken for participants to complete each rating was measured to assess decision confidence and difficulty. Each participant was paid at least the UK minimum hourly wage, with an average survey time of
12
minutes.

The results for the user study are shown in Figure
5
. The total count of ratings for each variant of the optimization (left) shows a statistically significant preference for the target variant over the distractor variant. The median rating for each variant is plotted grouped by the time taken for the response. The time is quantized into bins: Low’ refers to the lowest third of the survey time, ‘Medium’ refers to the middle third of the survey time, and ‘High’ refers to the highest third of the survey time. Error bars visualize the interquartile range of the ratings and time taken. The plot shows a clear trend of higher ratings for the target variant by respondents who took longer to complete the survey. The inverse is observed for the distractor variant.

3.6
Application to a different optimization problem: Explainable LM training

To demonstrate the generality of our methodology, we apply it to the training of a small LM on a physics question-answering dataset
1
1

1

Both the model and code are adapted from
https://github.com/karpathy/nanoGPT
. This experiment aims to illustrate that our framework is not limited to physics optimization, but can also be extended to complex neural network training processes. Specifically, we wish to explore creating explanations about a system where the relationship between parameters and model behavior is black-box and unclear to the domain expert.

During each validation step
k
of training, we capture three key components: (i)
Events
(
E_{k}
), consisting of 10 generated text samples; (ii)
Rewards
(
R_{k}
), represented by the training and validation losses; and (iii)
Updates
(
U_{k}
), measured as the change in average parameter values per layer.
\Phi_{E}
preserves the raw text samples as human-interpretable events,
\Phi_{R}
normalizes loss differences and maps them to qualitative descriptors, and
\Phi_{U}
combines the layer names with the relative magnitudes of change between steps and bins them into qualitative categories. We present qualitative examples of this process in the results section, for a more in depth analysis see supplemental material.

We demonstrate our framework’s application to LM training, presenting both a global explanation of the training process and a stepwise explanation for a specific validation step. These natural language outputs are generated directly by our method, see supplemental material for further examples.

resulted in a
notable improvement in the model’s generated answer
. Prior to this step, the model’s output was:
"The force is... qwerty zxv mass..."
Following the update, the generated text became:
"The force on an object is equal to its mass times acceleration, according to Newton’s second law."
This qualitative leap was directly attributable to
improved contextual integration and normalization
, as highlighted by our framework’s attribution maps. The loss decreased only marginally, but the
semantic and syntactic coherence of the output increased substantially
, demonstrating how parameter changes in specific submodules can drive interpretable, fine-grained improvements in model behavior.

Thus, our methodology enables interpretable explanations that relate specific parameter updates to observable changes in model outputs. The global description summarizes the overall trajectory of learning, while the stepwise example demonstrates the framework’s ability to attribute qualitative improvements to targeted changes in model components, even when the loss shows minor differences.

4
Discussion and conclusion

LMs comprehend and interact with optimization processes
:
Section
3.1
demonstrates how LMs successfully interact with optimization systems through our generated explanations. These explanations serve as an effective interface between the LM and system, enabling sophisticated domain-specific interactions. This approach is highly modular and extensible, limited only by LM capabilities and user-defined events, updates, and rewards.

Method flexibility across domains
:
We demonstrate our method in both physics optimization and language model training, representing white-box and black-box systems respectively. This flexibility addresses the challenge of explaining optimization processes where parameter-behavior relationships may be clear but the rationale for parametric changes remains opaque. Our framework accommodates both system types through user-configurable emission and transformation functions.

Explanations enable domain-specific decision making
:
Section
3.3
shows that for tasks like obstacle identification, LMs can successfully leverage explanations to identify high-impact elements. Performance varies by task type—spatial reasoning tasks show promising results while coordinate estimation proves more challenging without explicit numerical information. Thus, explanation utility depends on both task nature and information representation. With human evaluation, in Section
3.5
, we see a clear preference for the target over the distractor, with a median rating of
4/5
for the target and
2/5
for the distractor when users are within the top 33% of survey time. We believe this shows the usefulness of our explanations for users to identify the corresponding optimization, when taking the time to fully interpret the explanations.

4.1
Limitations

We observe diminishing returns with scale in optimization complexity (e.g. number of obstacles and control points) partly due to difficulties in evaluating explanations. Our method depends on user-defined events, rewards, and updates, which might be challenging in some domains. Finally, although explainability can largely be considered as positive and impactful, there are some negative societal impacts that have been theorized
xaipitfallsdarkpatterns
.

4.2
Conclusion and Future Work

We demonstrated an effective methodology for enabling natural language explanations of optimization processes for inverse problems with simulators. We meld numerical optimization and human interpretability, and demonstrate that explanations effectively convey optimization behaviors across domains, with improvements in downstream tasks.
Future work could: (1) handle scaling to more complex optimization scenarios through improved explanation strategies; (2) develop interactive interfaces enabling users to interact through natural language with more complex optimization tasks; and (3) integrate with other XAI techniques in domains like robotics and autonomous systems where understanding optimization decisions is critical for safety and trust.

Conversational Interaction

Conversation Questions

1.

Running and refining optimizations

•

Run an optimization where you balance speed and cost.

•

Based on the feedback, modify the optimization to improve the speed and cost.

2.

Comparing and explaining optimization strategies

•

Run two optimizations: one prioritizing speed and one prioritizing cost.

•

Analyze and explain why the speed-prioritized optimization is faster and the cost-prioritized optimization is cheaper. Reference specific events and trajectory properties.

3.

Analyzing obstacle impact

•

Analyze the current state and identify which single obstacle would be most beneficial to remove to improve performance.

•

Create modified states where you remove each obstacle one at a time, run optimizations on each modified state, and identify which obstacle removal had the biggest impact on performance.

4.

Matching control points to optimized results

•

Run an optimization and then create a new state with control points manually set to match the optimized result.

•

Run an optimization on the new state with the manually set control points and compare the results with the original optimization.

5.

Exploring multiple randomized states

•

Create 5 different states with randomized control points and run optimizations on each.

•

Create a final state and move the control points to be in a position that you believe will yield the best results, based on the feedback from the optimizations, and run a final optimization on it.

6.

Path finding near specific obstacles

•

Identify all the obstacles in the current state. Then, pick an obstacle that’s not directly on the obvious path between start and end. Create many different states with randomized interior control points, run optimizations on each, until you find one where the optimized path passes near that obstacle.

•

Analyze why the path goes near that obstacle by observing the events and optimisation description and provide your reasoning in detail.

Conversation 2

user:

Run two optimizations: one prioritizing speed and one prioritizing cost.

assistant:

Both requested optimizations are complete:

- **Speed-prioritized optimization**:
- Minimizes time (speed); accepts higher construction cost if it provides significant speed advantages.
- The optimized track aggressively reroutes around barriers like barrels and flowerbeds for smoother, faster navigation, even if it must traverse more expensive construction zones.
- Result: Time = 0.648s, Cost = 0.653.

- **Cost-prioritized optimization**:
- Minimizes path construction cost; willing to accept slower travel if it lowers overall cost.
- The optimizer detours around the most expensive obstacles (like the shed), selectively choosing to traverse less costly obstacles even if this means sharper or more convoluted routing and slightly longer travel times.
- Result: Cost = 0.612, Time = 0.712s.

- **Key differences:**
- The speed-focused path reroutes more boldly, seeking high-velocity segments and tolerating cost increases where time savings are significant.
- The cost-focused path takes strategic detours, increases path length if needed, and smooths obstacle interactions to keep expenses minimal, accepting a minor time penalty.

If you’d like to see more on the specific path shapes, obstacle avoidance strategies, or details on how control points moved, just let me know!

user:

Analyze and explain why the speed-prioritized optimization is faster and the cost-prioritized optimization is cheaper. Reference specific events and trajectory properties.

assistant:

The speed-prioritized optimization results in a faster route because it deliberately allows the track to enter higher-cost zones and maintains smooth, high-speed arcs, leading to consistently large speed gains and limited braking throughout the journey. You can see this from the many "large" and "very large" positive speed changes in early and mid segments, matched by moderate curvature—reflecting a path that’s laid out to maximize kinematic efficiency rather than minimize expense.

Meanwhile, the cost-prioritized optimization is cheaper because it takes every opportunity to steer the route through lower-cost portions of the grid, often at the expense of higher (sometimes "very large") curvature and extra acceleration or deceleration. It avoids the large positive cost jumps characteristic of the speed-prioritized run and frequently logs "large (negative)" cost changes along its route—meaning it’s actively seeking cheaper construction ground, even if it means more turns or slower acceleration.

Specific segments illustrate these strategies:
- The speed-prioritized path has smooth, expansive trajectories (like from (5,5) up through (32,42)), with little regard for cost spikes—a trade for time savings.
- The cost-prioritized path, however, makes sharper deviations when expensive areas loom, resulting in a still-efficient but slightly slower path and an overall lower cost.

In short: The faster path spends more to avoid slow-downs and penalties, while the cheaper path accepts slower speeds and trickier curves to save on construction expenses.
