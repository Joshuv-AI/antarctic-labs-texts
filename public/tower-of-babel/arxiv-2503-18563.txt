Power-fractional distributions and branching processes
Gerold Alsmeyer 1, Viet Hung Hoang 2

Abstract
In branching process theory, linear-fractional distributions are commonly used to model individual reproduction, especially when the goal is to obtain more explicit formulas than those derived under general model assumptions. In this article, we explore a generalization of these distributions, first introduced by Sagitov and Lindo, which offers similar advantages. We refer to these as power-fractional distributions, primarily because, as we demonstrate, they exhibit power-law behavior. Along with a discussion of their additional properties, we present several results related to the Galton-Watson branching process in both constant and randomly varying environments, illustrating these advantages. The use of power-fractional distributions in continuous time, particularly within the framework of Markov branching processes, is also briefly addressed.

2
Power-law behavior

This section presents some basic properties of a power-fractional law
(p_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}=\mathsf{PF}(\theta,a,b)
for
\theta\in(0,1]
and
a,b>0
with
a+b\geqslant 1
. Our main result will demonstrate that, for
\theta<1
, this law is essentially a power law, meaning that
p_{n}
behaves like
n^{-(2+\theta)}
as
n\to\infty
. The precise statement of this result can be found in Theorem
2.2
below. The following proposition by Sagitov and Lindo
(
14
, Prop. and Cor. 14.1)
is not only essential for its proof, but also of interest in its own right. In particular, it shows that the function
f
defined by (
1.2
) for
\gamma=1
and
\theta\in(0,1]
is indeed the pgf of a proper probability distribution
(p_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Moreover, it reveals the monotonicity of the
p_{n}
and provides a formula for them, see (

). This formula plays a crucial role in the proof of Theorem

.

Proposition 2.1

Fixing
\theta\in(0,1]
and
a,b>0
with
a+b\geqslant 1
, we define nonnegative numbers
c_{n,i}
for
n\geqslant 2
and

as follows:

\displaystyle c_{2,1}=1+\theta\quad\text{and}\quad c_{n,0}=c_{n,n}=0\quad\text{for all }n\geqslant 2

(2.1)

and, recursively,

\displaystyle c_{n,i}=(n-2-i\theta)c_{n-1,i}+(1+i\theta)c_{n-1,i-1}

(2.2)

for

and

. Then
f(s)=\sum_{n\geqslant 0}p_{n}s^{n}
, where

\displaystyle p_{0}\,=\,1-\frac{1}{(a+b)^{1/\theta}},\quad p_{1}\,=\,\frac{a}{(a+b)^{1+1/\theta}}

(2.3)

and

\displaystyle p_{n}\,=\,\frac{p_{1}}{n!}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\frac{a}{a+b}\right)^{i}c_{n,i}

(2.4)

for
n\geqslant 2
. Moreover,
p_{n}\geqslant p_{n+1}
for all

.

Note that, since
f(1)=1
(as a consequence of (

)) and all

are nonnegative, the proposition does indeed establish that

is the pgf of a probability distribution. Note also that the

depend on the parameter
\theta
, but not on the parameters

.

Proof

Putting
\rho(s)=(a+b(1-s)^{\theta})^{-1/\theta}
, one can readily check that

f(s)\ =\ 1-\rho(s)(1-s).

Since
\rho^{\prime}(s)=b\rho(s)^{\theta+1}(1-s)^{1-\theta}
, it follows that

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(s)\,

\displaystyle=\,\rho(s)-b\rho(s)^{\theta+1}(1-s)^{\theta}\,=\,a\rho(s)^{\theta+1},

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(s)\,

\displaystyle=\,ab(\theta+1)\rho(s)^{2\theta+1}(1-s)^{\theta-1}.

(2.6)

We claim and prove by induction that, for all
n\geqslant 2
,

By (

), the claim holds true for

. Assuming it holds for an arbitrary fixed

(inductive hypothesis), we differentiate to obtain:

\displaystyle f^{(n+1)}(s)\

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}ab^{i}c_{n,i}b\big((i+1)\theta+1\big)\rho(s)^{(i+2)\theta+1}(1-s)^{(i+1)\theta-n}

\displaystyle\quad+\ \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}ab^{i}c_{n,i}(n-i\theta-1)\rho(s)^{(i+1)\theta+1}(1-s)^{i\theta-n}

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{i=2}^{n}ab^{i-1}c_{n,i-1}b\big(i\theta+1\big)\rho(s)^{(i+1)\theta+1}(1-s)^{i\theta-n}

Theorem 2.2

Let
(p_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}=\mathsf{PF}(\theta,a,b)
for
\theta\in(0,1)
and
a,b>0
such that
a+b\geqslant 1
. Then

p_{n}\ \asymp\ n^{-(2+\theta)}\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

(2.8)

Furthermore, if
a(a+b)^{-1}\leqslant\theta(1+2\theta)^{-1}
, then the sequence
(n(n-1)p_{n})_{n\geqslant 2}
is nonincreasing and

p_{n}\ \simeq\ cn^{-(2+\theta)}\quad\text{as }n\to\infty,

(2.9)

where
c=a^{-(\theta+1)/\theta}b(\theta+1)/\Gamma(1-\theta)
.

Proof

Note that
f^{\prime\prime}(s)=\sum_{n\geqslant 2}n(n-1)p_{n}s^{n}
and recall from Remark (e) in the Introduction that, in the given situation,
f^{\prime\prime}(s)
satisfies

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(s)\ =\ ab(\theta+1)(1-s)^{\theta-1}\psi(s)^{2\theta+1}

with
\psi(s)=\big[a+b(1-s)^{\theta}]^{-1/\theta}
. Since
\psi(s)\simeq a^{-1/\theta}
as
s\uparrow 1
, we see that

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(s)\ \simeq\ a^{-(\theta+1)/\theta}b(\theta+1)(1-s)^{\theta-1}\quad\text{as }s\uparrow 1.

Therefore, if the
n(n-1)p_{n}
are nonincreasing, then

n(n-1)p_{n}\ \simeq\ \frac{a^{-(\theta+1)/\theta}b(\theta+1)}{\Gamma(1-\theta)}\,n^{-\theta}\quad\text{as }n\to\infty

follows by a well-known Tauberian result by Hardy, Littlewood and Karamata (see e.g.
(
5
, Cor. 1.7.3 on p. 40)
). This establishes (
2.9
) with the given constant
c
. The required monotonicity of

will be verified next under the assumption
\rho:=a(a+b)^{-1}\leqslant\theta(1+2\theta)^{-1}
.

It follows with the help of (
2.4
) that

\displaystyle r_{n}\ :=\ \frac{n(n+1)p_{n+1}}{n(n-1)p_{n}}\ =\ \frac{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho^{i}c_{n+1,i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\rho^{i}c_{n,i}}

for any
n\geqslant 2
. Use (
2.2
) to see that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho^{i}c_{n+1,i}\ &=\ \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{n-1-i\theta}{n-1}\,\rho^{i}c_{n,i}\,+\,\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{1+i\theta}{n-1}\,\rho^{i}c_{n,i-1}\\
&=\ \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\frac{n-1-i\theta}{n-1}+\frac{1+(i+1)\theta}{n-1}\rho\right)\rho^{i}c_{n,i}\\
&=\ \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(1+\frac{\rho(1+\theta)}{n-1}-\frac{(1-\rho)i\theta}{n-1}\right)\rho^{i}c_{n,i}\\
&\leqslant\ \left(1-\frac{\theta-\rho(1+2\theta)}{n-1}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\rho^{i}c_{n,i},\end{split}

(2.10)

and therefore

\displaystyle r_{n}\ \leqslant\ 1-\frac{\theta-\rho(1+2\theta)}{n-1}

for
n\geqslant 2
, with strict inequality for
n\geqslant 3
.
This shows the asserted monotonicity of the sequence
(n(n-1)p_{n})_{n\geqslant 2}
when
\rho\leqslant\theta(1+2\theta)^{-1}
.

Now suppose
\rho>\theta
for the proof of (
2.8
), which may also be stated as

0\ <\ \liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{p_{n}}{n^{2+\theta}}\ \leqslant\ \limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{p_{n}}{n^{2+\theta}}\ <\ \infty.

Let
N
be a random variable with law
\mathsf{PF}(\theta,1,\xi)
, and let
X,X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
be iid random variables independent of
N
and with common law
\mathsf{PF}(\theta,a,b)
. Then the law of
Y:=\sum_{k=1}^{N}X_{k}
is also power-fractional, specifically

Y\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\ \mathsf{PF}(\theta,a,b+\xi).

We fix
\xi>0
such that
a/(a+b+\xi)=\theta
, thus
b+\xi=a(1-\theta)/\theta
. Then (
2.9
) applies to give

\mathbb{P}(Y=n)\ \simeq\ \frac{a^{-1/\theta}(1-\theta)(\theta+1)}{\theta\Gamma(1-\theta)}\,n^{-(2+\theta)}\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

Using
\mathbb{P}(N=1)=(1+\xi)^{-1-1/\theta}
by (
2.3
) and

\mathbb{P}(Y=n)\ \geqslant\ \mathbb{P}(N=1,X_{1}=n)\ =\ \mathbb{P}(N=1)\,p_{n}\ =\ \frac{p_{n}}{(1+\xi)^{1+1/\theta}},

we conclude

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{p_{n}}{n^{2+\theta}}\ \leqslant\ \frac{a^{-1/\theta}(1-\theta)(\theta+1)(1+\xi)^{1+1/\theta}}{\theta\Gamma(1-\theta)}\ <\ \infty.

Towards a positive lower bound for
\liminf_{n\to\infty}n^{-(2+\theta)}p_{n}
, we observe that, if
(u_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}=\mathsf{PF}(\theta,a,\zeta)
with
\zeta>b
is chosen such that
a/(a+\zeta)=\theta
, then, by (
2.4
),

\displaystyle p_{n}\ =\ \frac{p_{1}}{n!}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\rho^{i}c_{n,i}\ \geqslant\ \frac{p_{1}}{n!}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\theta^{i}c_{n,i}\ =\ \frac{p_{1}}{u_{1}}u_{n}

for all
n\geqslant 2
, and
p_{1}/u_{1}=(\rho/\theta)^{1+1/\theta}
by (
2.3
). Consequently,

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{p_{n}}{n^{2+\theta}}\

\displaystyle\geqslant\ \bigg(\frac{\rho}{\theta}\bigg)^{1+1/\theta}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{u_{n}}{n^{2+\theta}}

\displaystyle=\ \bigg(\frac{\rho}{\theta}\bigg)^{1+1/\theta}\frac{a^{-(\theta+1)/\theta}\zeta(\theta+1)}{\Gamma(1-\theta)}\ >\ 0,

and this completes the proof of the proposition.∎

Remark 2.3

Using (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), one can derive estimates for the
c_{n,i}
that, with the help of (
2.10
), show that for
\rho<\theta
, the sequence
(n(n-1)p_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
remains
ultimately
nonincreasing, and thus (
2.9
) in Theorem
2.2
continues to hold. However, to keep the proof concise, we have omitted the details of the argument.

3
Varying environment

The composition of pgf’s of power-fractional laws remains stable even when the parameters
a
and
b
vary, as long as
\theta
is kept fixed. Specifically, if
N,X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
are independent random variables such that the law of
N
is
\mathsf{PF}(\theta,a_{1},b_{1})
(with pgf
f_{1}
) and the common law of the
X_{k}
is
\mathsf{PF}(\theta,a_{2},b_{2})
(with pgf
f_{2}
), then the random sum
Y:=\sum_{k=1}^{N}X_{k}
has pgf

f_{1}\circ f_{2}(s)\ =\ 1\,-\,\left[\frac{a_{1}a_{2}}{(1-s)^{\theta}}+a_{1}b_{2}+b_{1}\right]^{-1/\theta},\quad s\in[0,1).

(3.1)

and thus again a law of power-fractional type, namely

\displaystyle Y\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\ \mathsf{PF}(\theta,a_{1}a_{2},a_{1}b_{2}+b_{1}).

(3.2)

This follows from (
1.3
), but can also be deduced with the help of the functional equation (
1.8
). By fixing
\theta
and thus
H=H_{\theta}
, we obtain

\displaystyle H(f_{1}\circ f_{2}(s))\

\displaystyle=\ a_{1}H(f_{2}(s))+H(f_{1}(0))

\displaystyle=\ a_{1}a_{2}H(s)+a_{1}H(f_{2}(0))+H(f_{1}(0)).

Here is a mnemonic – though somewhat unconventional in notation – for expressing (
3.1
) and (
3.2
):

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\mathsf{PF}(\theta,a_{1},b_{1})}\mathsf{PF}(\theta,a_{2},b_{2})_{k}\ =\ \mathsf{PF}(\theta,a_{1}a_{2},a_{1}b_{2}+b_{1}),

(3.3)

where the independence assumptions in the original formulation are implicitly assumed.
Generalizing to any finite number of compositions, we arrive at the following result:

Lemma 3.1

Fixing
\theta\in(0,1]
, let
f_{k}
for
k=1,\ldots,n
be the pgf of
\mathsf{PF}(\theta,a_{k},b_{k})
for arbitrary
(a_{k},b_{k})
satisfying (A1). Then
f_{1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{n}
is the pgf of the power-fractional law with parameters
\theta,\prod_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}
, and
\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{k}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}a_{i}
, abbreviated hereafter as

f_{1}\circ\ldots\circ f_{n}\ \sim\ \mathsf{PF}\left(\theta,\prod_{k=1}^{n}a_{k},\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{k}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}a_{i}\right).

(3.4)

For linear-fractional distributions
(\theta=1)
, Eq. (
3.4
) has already been stated in
(
1
, Eq. (1.7))
and then used to derive results for linear-fractional
\mathsf{GWP}
’s in iid random environment. The latter means to interpret
(a_{n},b_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
as the outcome of a sequence
\text{\rm e}=(\text{\rm e}_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
of iid random vectors
\text{\rm e}_{n}=(A_{n},B_{n})
(the random environment). In the following, the approach described in
[
1
]
is extended to the power-fractional case. More specifically, we will study the behavior of a Galton-Watson process in random environment (
\mathsf{GWPRE}
) with iid power-fractional offspring laws
\mathsf{PF}(\theta,A_{n},B_{n})
for
\theta
fixed. As (
3.4
) shows further, the sequence describing the parameter evolution, namely

(\Pi_{n},R_{n})\ :=\ \left(\prod_{k=1}^{n}A_{k},\sum_{k=1}^{n}B_{k}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}A_{i}\right),\quad n\geqslant 0

does not depend on the choice of
\theta
, apart from the constraints imposed by (A1) on the range of the
\text{\rm e}_{n}=(A_{n},B_{n})
. It is therefore exactly the same as in
[
1
]
and in fact the sequence of backward iterations of the random affine linear functions

g_{n}(x)\,=\,g(\text{\rm e}_{n},x)\,:=\,A_{n}x+B_{n},\quad n\geqslant 1.

(3.5)

which have been extensively studied in the literature, see e.g.
[
11
,
19
,
8
,
2
]
and especially the recent monographs
[
7
,
9
]
. A review of its most important properties for the present work will be given in the next section after some further background and notation needed throughout.

As usual, let
(Z_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
denote the power-fractional
\mathsf{GWPRE}
to be considered hereafter, and let
(A,B)
a generic copy of the environmental variables
(A_{n},B_{n})
which, according to (A1), must satisfy

\mathbb{P}(A>0,\,B>0,\,A+B\geqslant 1)\,=\,1.

(3.6)

Define
\text{\rm e}_{1:n}:=(\text{\rm e}_{1},\ldots,\text{\rm e}_{n})
and
\text{\rm e}_{\scriptscriptstyle\geqslant n}:=(\text{\rm e}_{n},\text{\rm e}_{n+1},\ldots)
, so that
\text{\rm e}=\text{\rm e}_{\scriptscriptstyle\geqslant 1}
.
Given
\text{\rm e}_{n}
, let
f_{n}=f(\text{\rm e}_{n},\cdot)
denote the
quenched pgf
of the power-fractional distribution
\mathsf{PF}(\theta,A_{n},B_{n})=(P_{n,k})_{k\geqslant 0}
. From (
1.2
), we have

\frac{1}{(1-f_{n}(s))^{\theta}}\ =\ \frac{A_{n}}{(1-s)^{\theta}}\,+\,B_{n}\quad\text{a.s.}

Let
(P_{k})_{k\geqslant 0}=\mathsf{PF}(\theta,A,B)
be a generic copy of the
(P_{n,k})_{k\geqslant 0}
.

Now, conditioned
\text{\rm e}_{1:n}
, the individuals of generation
n-1
produce offspring according to the power-fractional distribution
\mathsf{PF}(\theta,\gamma,A_{n},B_{n})
, which has the pgf
f_{n}
. Therefore

f_{1:n}\ :=\ f_{1}\circ\ldots\circ f_{n}

equals the random pgf of (the quenched law of)
Z_{n}
given both
\text{\rm e}_{1:n}
and also the full environment
e
. Equivalently, by (
3.4
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\in\cdot|\text{\rm e}_{1:n})\ =\ \mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\in\cdot|\text{\rm e})\ =\ \mathsf{PF}(\theta,\Pi_{n},R_{n})

(3.7)

and

\displaystyle f_{1:n}(s)=\ 1\ -\ \left[\frac{\Pi_{n}}{(1-s)^{\theta}}\,+\,R_{n}\right]^{-1/\theta}

(3.8)

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where

\Pi_{n}:=\prod_{k=1}^{n}A_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad R_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Pi_{k-1}B_{k}.

Since, with
g_{n}
defined in (
3.5
) and
\psi(s):=(1-s)^{-\theta}
,

\psi\circ f_{1:n}(s)\ =\ \frac{1}{(1-f_{1:n}(s))^{\theta}}\ =\ \frac{\Pi_{n}}{(1-s)^{\theta}}\,+\,R_{n}\ =\ g_{1:n}\circ\psi(s)

(3.9)

for
s\in[0,1)
, we see that, up to conjugation,
(f_{1:n})_{n\geqslant 0}
equals the sequence of backward iterations of the iid random affine linear maps
g_{1},g_{2},\ldots
already mentioned. The corresponding forward iterations
g_{n:1}(x):=g_{n}\circ\ldots\circ g_{1}(x)
form a Markov chain on
[0,\infty)
with initial state
x
and also a so-called
iterated function system (IFS)
. Based on these observations and similar to
[
1
]
for the linear-fractional case, we are able to derive properties of
\mathsf{GWPRE}
’s with power-fractional offspring laws by drawing on results about iterations of the
g_{n}
. For instance, when defining

q_{n}(\text{\rm e}_{1:n})\,:=\,\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}=0|\text{\rm e}_{1:n})\,=\,f_{1:n}(0),

we infer as an immediate consequence of (
3.9
) that

q_{n}(\text{\rm e}_{1:n})\ =\ 1\,-\,\big(\Pi_{n}+R_{n}\big)^{-1/\theta}\quad\text{a.s.}

(3.10)

for all
n\geqslant 1
. Let

q(\text{\rm e})\ :=\ \lim_{n\to\infty}q_{n}(\text{\rm e}_{1:n})

denote its a.s. limit, which is the quenched extinction probability of
(Z_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
given
e
.

Reversing the environment
. As in
[
1
]
, it will be preferable for the presentation of some of our results to consider the given
\mathsf{GWPRE}
up to time
n
under the time-reversed environment
\text{\rm e}_{n:1}=(\text{\rm e}_{n},\ldots,\text{\rm e}_{1})
rather than
\text{\rm e}_{1:n}
which means to use the random offspring laws
\mathsf{PF}(\theta,A_{k},B_{k})
in reverse order. This does not change the (annealed) law of
Z_{0:n}:=(Z_{0},\ldots,Z_{n})
, but the quenched laws are naturally different. On the other hand, they share the same distribution as random measures, see (
3.11
) below, and this will sometimes be used hereafter to formulate assertions about quenched asymptotic behavior in a more tangible form than they would appear without time-reversal. To be more specific, we define

\displaystyle\text{\rm P}\,:=\,\mathbb{P}(\cdot|\text{\rm e}),\quad\text{\rm P}^{(1:n)}\,:=\,\mathbb{P}(\cdot|\text{\rm e}_{1:n})\quad\text{and}\quad\text{\rm P}^{(n:1)}\,:=\,\mathbb{P}(\cdot|\text{\rm e}_{n:1}),

with corresponding expectations
\text{\rm E},\,\text{\rm E}^{(1:n)}
and
\text{\rm E}^{(n:1)}
, and note that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{\rm P}(Z_{0:n}\in\cdot)\ &=\ \text{\rm P}^{(1:n)}(Z_{0:n}\in\cdot)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\ \text{\rm P}^{(n:1)}(Z_{0:n}\in\cdot)\end{split}

(3.11)

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\text{\rm P}^{(n:1)}(Z_{n}\in\cdot)
has pgf
f_{n:1}:=f_{n}\circ\ldots\circ f_{1}
, giving

\displaystyle\text{\rm P}^{(n:1)}(Z_{n}\in\cdot)\ =\ \mathsf{PF}\left(\theta,\Pi_{n},\Pi_{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Pi_{k}^{-1}B_{k}\right)

(3.12)

upon using (
3.4
) for
f_{n:1}
. We thus see that, when opposed to
\text{\rm P}^{(1:n)}(Z_{n}\in\cdot)
, the random parameter
R_{n}
is exchanged by
\Pi_{n}R_{n}^{(-1)}
, where

R_{n}^{(-1)}\,:=\,\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Pi_{k}^{-1}B_{k}

(3.13)

and
(\Pi_{n},R_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(\Pi_{n},R_{n}^{(-1)})
holds for all
n
by (
3.11
).

Classification
. For a
\mathsf{GWPRE}
, the distinction between subcritical, critical and supercritical type is based on the behavior of the logarithm of the quenched mean
\log\text{\rm E}Z_{n}
as
n\to\infty
. Provided this quantity is a.s. finite for all
n
, it constitutes an ordinary random walk with generic increment
\log f^{\prime}(1)
which in the present situation equals
-\theta^{-1}\log A
.
In fact, if
Z_{0}=1
, then

\theta\log\text{\rm E}Z_{n}\ =\ -\log\Pi_{n}\ =\ -\sum_{k=1}^{n}\log A_{k}\ =:\ S_{n}\quad\text{a.s.}

for all
n\geqslant 0
. Depending on the fluctuation-type of the walk
(S_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
(the factor
\theta^{-1}
does not matter), namely

\bullet

positive divergence, i.e.,
S_{n}\to\infty\text{ a.s.}
,

\bullet

negative divergence, i.e.,
S_{n}\to-\infty\text{ a.s.}
,

\bullet

oscillation, i.e.
\textstyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}S_{n}=+\infty\text{ and }\liminf_{n\to\infty}S_{n}=-\infty\text{ a.s.}
,

\bullet

degeneracy, i.e.,
S_{n}=0\text{ a.s~for all }n\geqslant 0
,

the process
(Z_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
is called subcritical, supercritical, critical or strongly critical, respectively
(
10
, Def. 2.3)
. If
\mathbb{E}\log A
exists, this means that

\displaystyle(Z_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}\text{ is }\begin{cases}\hfill\text{subcritical}&\text{if }\mathbb{E}\log A>0,\\
\hfill\text{critical}&\text{if }\mathbb{E}\log A=0\text{ and }\mathbb{P}(A\neq 1)>0,\\
\text{strongly critical}&\text{if }A=1\text{ a.s.},\\
\hfill\text{supercritical}&\text{if }\mathbb{E}\log A<0.\end{cases}

Based on the very explicit knowledge of the
f_{1:n}
in the given situation, a precise description of when each of these cases occurs is possible and was given in some detail in
(
1
, Sect. 2)
. In the following section, we therefore limit ourselves to a summary of the most important facts, in particular with regard to the asymptotic behavior of the variables
\Pi_{n},R_{n}
and
R_{n}^{(-1)}
, which play a role in the development of the random parameters of the deleted laws of
Z_{n}
.

4
Prerequisites about iterations of random affine linear functions

Recall that
g_{n}(x)=A_{n}x+B_{n}
and Eq. (
3.9
) for the backward iterations
g_{1:n}
of the IFS and Markov chain
(g_{n:1}(x))_{n\geqslant 0}
generated by the
g_{n}
. The latter shows that

g_{1:n}(x)\ =\ \psi\circ f_{1:n}\circ\psi^{-1}(x)\ =\ \Pi_{n}x\,+\,R_{n},\quad n\geqslant 1,

for any
n\geqslant 1
, which does not depend on
\theta
. It also explains the appearance of the
(\Pi_{n},R_{n})
mentioned above in connection with the quenched laws of the given power-fractional
\mathsf{GWPRE}
. Goldie and Maller
(
8
, Thm. 2.1)
have provided necessary and sufficient conditions for the stability (positive recurrence) of
(g_{n:1}(x))_{n\geqslant 0}
that goes along with the almost sure convergence of the
g_{1:n}(x)
as
n\to\infty
. Before summarizing below their result for the case of positive
A,B
satisfying an additional nondegeneracy condition, we further introduce a second sequence of random affine linear maps that is of interest here and in dual relation to the
g_{n}
. Indeed, defining

\displaystyle g_{n}^{(-1)}(x)\,:=\,A_{n}^{-1}x+A_{n}^{-1}B_{n}\quad\text{for }n\in\mathbb{N},

the associated backward iterations take the form

\displaystyle g_{1:n}^{(-1)}\,=\,\Pi_{n}^{-1}x\,+\,R_{n}^{(-1)},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

with
R_{n}^{(-1)}
defined by (
3.13
), and the duality relation

\displaystyle\frac{R_{n}}{\Pi_{n}}\ =\ \frac{g_{1:n}(0)}{\Pi_{n}}\ =\ g_{n:1}^{(-1)}(0)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\ g_{1:n}^{(-1)}(0)\ =\ R_{n}^{(-1)}

holds for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proposition 4.1

Suppose that
A,B
are a.s. positive and

\mathbb{P}(Ax+B=x)\,<\,1\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}.

(4.1)

Define
J^{\pm}(x):=\mathbb{E}(x\wedge\log^{\pm}A)
, and let

R_{\infty}\,:=\,\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\Pi_{k-1}B_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad R_{\infty}^{(-1)}\,:=\,\sum_{k\geqslant 1}\Pi_{k}^{-1}B_{k},

denote the increasing limits of
R_{n}
and
R_{n}^{(-1)}
, respectively, called perpetuities. Then the following assertions hold:

(a)

The condition

\Pi_{n}\,\to\,0\text{ a.s.}\quad\text{and}\quad I_{-}\,:=\,\int_{[1,\infty)}\frac{\log x}{J^{-}(\log x)}\,\mathbb{P}(B\in dx)\,<\,\infty

(4.2)

is necessary and sufficient for
R_{\infty}<\infty
, the almost sure convergence of
g_{1:n}(x)
to
R_{\infty}
, and the convergence in law of the forward iterations
g_{n:1}(x)
to the same limit.

(b)

The condition

\Pi_{n}\,\to\,\infty\text{ a.s.}\quad\text{and}\quad I_{+}\,:=\,\int_{[1,\infty)}\frac{\log x}{J^{+}(\log x)}\,\mathbb{P}\bigg(\frac{B}{A}\in dx\bigg)\,<\,\infty

(4.3)

is necessary and sufficient for
R_{\infty}^{(-1)}<\infty
, the almost sure convergence of
g_{1:n}^{(-1)}(x)
to
R_{\infty}^{(-1)}
, and the convergence in law of the forward iterations
g_{n:1}(x)
to the same limit.

In the case when (
4.1
) fails, that is,
Ax+B=x
a.s. for some
x
, the violation of (
4.2
), respectively (
4.3
) entails
R_{\infty}=\infty
a.s., respectively
R_{\infty}^{(-1)}=\infty
a.s.

We note that we always have
R_{\infty}\geqslant 1
a.s. as a consequence of the basic constraint (
3.6
) on
(A,B)
(see
(
1
, Eq. (16))
). It is also easily verified that
R_{\infty}
and
R_{\infty}^{(-1)}
cannot be a.s. finite at the same time. Therefore, the trichotomy

(C1)

R_{\infty}<\infty=R_{\infty}^{(-1)}
a.s.

(C2)

R_{\infty}^{(-1)}<\infty=R_{\infty}
a.s.

(C3)

R_{\infty}=R_{\infty}^{(-1)}=\infty
a.s.

holds. A complete characterization of the three cases in terms of
(A,B)
is possible with the help of the previous proposition and has been provided in
[
1
]
. We refrain from repeating it here and only note that, as an outcome of this characterization, the
\mathsf{GWPRE}

(Z_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
is

supercritical if (C1) holds;

subcritical if (C2) holds;

critical if (C3) holds and
\mathbb{P}(A\neq 1)>0
;

strongly critical if (C3) holds and
A=1
a.s..

5
Basic results for power-fractional
\mathsf{GWP}
’s in a constant environment

Before proceeding with the random environment case, we devote the present section to a survey of the most basic results for power-fractional
\mathsf{GWP}
’s in a constant environment, which in view of our standing assumption means that the offspring law is
\mathsf{PF}(\theta,a,b)
for some
\theta\in(0,1]
and
a,b>0
such that
a+b\geqslant 1
.

1. Supercritical case.
Recall from the discussion in Section
1
that this case occurs iff
a\in(0,1)
in which case the mean offspring equals
\mathsf{m}=a^{-1/\theta}
(see also Table
3
). The two basic results, collected in the subsequent theorem, are about the extinction probability
q
and the limiting behavior of the normalization of the process which is well-known to be a nonnegative martingale.

Theorem 5.1

Let
(Z_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be a supercritical power-fractional
\mathsf{GWP}
with one ancestor, offspring distribution
\mathsf{PF}(\theta,a,b)
and normalization
W_{n}=\mathsf{m}^{-n}Z_{n}
for
n\geqslant 0
. Denote by
f
the offspring pgf Then the following assertions hold:

(a)

The extinction probability
q
is given by

q\ =\ 1\,-\,\bigg(\frac{1-a}{b}\bigg)^{1/\theta}.

(5.1)

(b)

As
n\to\infty
, the normalization
W_{n}
converges a.s. and in
L^{1}
to a random variable
W_{\infty}
with Laplace transform

\varphi(u)\ =\ \mathbb{E}e^{-uW_{\infty}}\ =\ 1\,-\,\left[\frac{1}{u^{\theta}}+\frac{b}{1-a}\right]^{-1/\theta},\quad u\geqslant 0,

(5.2)

which in turn satisfies the functional equation
\varphi(u)=f\circ\varphi(a^{1/\theta}u)
for all
u\geqslant 0
, known as (the multiplicative form of) Abel’s equation.

Proof

(a) Recalling that
b_{n}=b\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a^{k}=b(1-a)^{-1}(1-a^{n})
, (
5.1
) follows from

\displaystyle q\ =\ \lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}=0)\ =\ \lim_{n\to\infty}f^{n}(0)\ =\ \lim_{n\to\infty}1\,-\,[a^{n}+b_{n}]^{-1/\theta}.

(b) Since
\mathbb{E}e^{-uW_{n}}=\mathbb{E}e^{-uZ_{n}/\mathsf{m}^{n}}=f^{n}(e^{-u/\mathsf{m}^{n}})
and
\mathsf{m}^{n}=a^{-n/\theta}
, we obtain by another use of (
5.1
) that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-\mathbb{E}e^{-uW_{n}})^{\theta}}\

\displaystyle=\ \frac{1}{(1-f^{n}(e^{-u/\mathsf{m}^{n}}))^{\theta}}\ =\ \frac{a^{n}}{(1-e^{-u/\mathsf{m}^{n}})^{\theta}}\,+\,b_{n}

\displaystyle=\ \left[\frac{a^{n/\theta}}{1-e^{-ua^{n/\theta}}}\right]^{\theta}\,+\,b_{n}.

Now the last expression is easily seen to converge to
u^{-\theta}+\frac{b}{1-a}
as
n\to\infty
, and this proves (
5.2
). Moreover,

\mathbb{P}(W_{\infty}=0)\,=\,\lim_{u\to\infty}\varphi(u)\,=\,1\,-\,\bigg(\frac{1-a}{b}\bigg)^{1/\theta}\,=\,q

so that
W_{n}
must also converge in
L^{1}
to
W_{\infty}
by the Kesten-Stigum theorem.
The asserted functional equation is a well-known fact that can be found, e.g., in
(
4
, Eq. (5) on p. 10)
.∎

In the linear-fractional case
\theta=1
, we obtain
q=\frac{a+b-1}{b}
and then

\varphi(u)\ =\ q\,+\,(1-q)\,\frac{1-q}{1-q+u}.

(5.3)

This shows that the law of the normalized limit
W_{\infty}
equals a mixture of the Dirac measure at 0 and the exponential law with parameter
1-q
, here called
continuous linear-fractional law
and abbreviated as
\mathsf{CLF}
. Its precise definition will be given below within a wider class of distributions.

For general
\theta\in(0,1]
and with
q
given by (
5.1
),
\varphi(u)
can be rewritten as

\varphi(u)\ =\ q\,+\,(1-q)\left[1-\left(1-\frac{(1-q)^{\theta}}{u^{\theta}+(1-q)^{\theta}}\right)^{1/\theta}\right]

(5.4)

Thus the law of
W_{\infty}
is also a mixture of the Dirac measure at 0 with a law that has Laplace transform appearing in square brackets in (
5.4
). Both the latter law and the mixture are referred to as the
continuous power-fractional law
and abbreviated as
\mathsf{CPF}
. To refine the parametrization, we return to Eq. (
5.2
) and rewrite it as:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-\varphi(u))^{\theta}}\ =\ \frac{1}{u^{\theta}}\,+\,\frac{b}{1-a},

thus in a form very similar to Eq. (
1.3
) for power-fractional laws. This suggests to define
\mathsf{CPF}(\theta,\alpha,\beta)
for
\theta\in(0,1],\,\alpha>0
and
\beta\geqslant 1
to be the law with Laplace transform
\varphi(u)=1-\big[\alpha u^{-\theta}+\beta\big]^{-1/\theta}
, giving

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-\varphi(u))^{\theta}}\ =\ \frac{\alpha}{u^{\theta}}\,+\,\beta.

(5.5)

and

\displaystyle\varphi(u)\ =\ (1-\beta^{-1/\theta})\,+\,\beta^{-1/\theta}\left[1-\left(1-\frac{\alpha/\beta}{u^{\theta}+\alpha/\beta}\right)^{1/\theta}\right].

(5.6)

If we now let
\mathsf{CPF}_{+}(\theta,\alpha):=\mathsf{CPF}(\theta,\alpha,1)
be the continuous power-fractional law on the
positive
halfline with Laplace transform

\displaystyle\varphi_{\theta,\alpha}(u)\,:=\,1-\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{u^{\theta}+\alpha}\right)^{1/\theta}\ =\ 1-\left(\frac{u^{\theta}}{u^{\theta}+\alpha}\right)^{1/\theta},

(5.7)

then (
5.6
) means that

\displaystyle\mathsf{CPF}(\theta,\alpha,\beta)\,:=\,(1-\beta^{-1/\theta})\delta_{0}\,+\,\beta^{-1/\theta}\,\mathsf{CPF}_{+}(\theta,\alpha/\beta).

(5.8)

The continuous linear-fractional laws occur if
\theta=1
, and we therefore define

\mathsf{CLF}(\alpha,\beta)\,:=\,\mathsf{CPF}(1,\alpha,\beta)\ =\ (1-\beta)\delta_{0}\,+\,\beta\,\textit{Exp}(\alpha).

(5.9)

Finally, (
5.4
) can now be restated as

W_{\infty}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\,\mathsf{CPF}(\theta,1,(1-q)^{-\theta}).

(5.10)

We finish our discussion about the law of
W_{\infty}
with two lemmata. The first one provides an extension of the well-known fact that a geometric sum of iid exponentials is again an exponential random variable and, more generally, a linear-fractional sum of continuous linear-fractionals is again of the latter type.

Lemma 5.2

Given
\theta\in(0,1]
and positive
\alpha,\beta,a,b
with
\beta\geqslant 1
and
b\geqslant 1-a
, let
N,Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots
be independent random variables such that

\displaystyle N\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\,\mathsf{PF}(\theta,1,a,b)\quad\text{and}\quad Y_{n}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\,\mathsf{CPF}(\theta,\alpha,\beta)\quad\text{for each }n.

Then

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}Y_{k}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\ \mathsf{CPF}(\theta,a\alpha,a\beta+b)

(5.11)

Proof

With
f
and
\varphi
denoting the pgf of
N
and the Laplace transform of the
Y_{n}
, respectively, the random sum in (
5.11
) has Laplace transform
f\circ\varphi
. A combination of the functional equations (
1.3
) and (
5.6
) now yields the assertion.∎

The second lemma reveals a connection between continuous power-fractional, Sibuya and Mittag-Leffler distributions of order
\theta
. The latter have the Laplace transform
\psi_{\theta}(u)=(1+u^{\theta})^{-1}
for some
\theta\in(0,1]
and are abbreviated as
\mathsf{MiLe}(\theta)
here. When
\theta=1
, this reduces to the standard exponential distribution.

Lemma 5.3

Let
\theta\in(0,1]
and
S,X,Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots
be independent random variables such that
S\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathsf{Sib}\hskip 0.6pt(\theta)
,
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathsf{MiLe}(\theta)
, and the
Y_{n}
have common law
\mathsf{CPF}_{+}(\theta,1)
. Then

X\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\ \sum_{n=1}^{S}Y_{n},

(5.12)

that is, the Mittag-Leffler distribution can be represented as a Sibuya sum of continuous power-fractional variables on
(0,\infty)
.

Proof

This follows again by a look at Laplace transforms. The simple details can be omitted.∎

2. Subcritical case.
This case arises when
a>1
, and the following theorem presents the well-known results regarding the behavior of the survival probability and the asymptotic law of the process conditioned on survival, often referred to as the Yaglom law.

Theorem 5.4

Let
(Z_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be a subcritical power-fractional
\mathsf{GWP}
with one ancestor, offspring distribution
\mathsf{PF}(\theta,a,b)
and offspring pgf
f
. Then the following assertions hold:

(a)

The survival probability
\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}>0)
satisfies

\lim_{n\to\infty}a^{n/\theta}\,\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}>0)\ =\ \left(\frac{a-1}{a+b-1}\right)^{1/\theta}.

(5.13)

(b)

The quasi-stationary limit law of the process when conditioned upon survival is again power-fractional, namely

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\in\cdot|Z_{n}>0)\ =\ \mathsf{PF}\left(\theta,\frac{a-1}{a+b-1},\frac{b}{a+b-1}\right)

(5.14)

in the sense of total variation convergence.

Proof

(a) With
b_{n}
as in (
1.1
) and
Z_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathsf{PF}(\theta,a^{n},b_{n})
, we obtain

\displaystyle a^{n/\theta}\,\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}>0)\

\displaystyle=\ a^{n/\theta}\,(1-f^{n}(0))\ =\ \left(\frac{a^{n}}{a^{n}+b_{n}}\right)^{1/\theta}

\displaystyle=\ \left(1+b\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a^{k-n}\right)^{-1/\theta}\ =\ \left(1+\frac{b(1-a^{-n})}{a-1}\right)^{-1/\theta}

and thereby (
5.15
) upon letting
n\to\infty
.

(b) Here we invoke Lemma
9.2
to infer

\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\in\cdot|Z_{n}>0)\ =\ \mathsf{PF}\left(\theta,\frac{a^{n}}{a^{n}+b_{n}},\frac{b_{n}}{a^{n}+b_{n}}\right),

and we have just shown for (a) that
a^{n}/(a^{n}+b_{n})\to(a-1)/(a+b-1)
. The asserted convergence is now immediate.∎

Note that the above formulae for the survival probability
\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}>0)
and the conditional law
\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\in\cdot|Z_{n}>0)
hold regardless of the value of
a
.

2. Critical case.
Finally turning to the critical case when
a=1
, we recall from Table
3
that the offspring variance is infinite unless the law is linear-fractional and thus
\theta=1
. By drawing on the proof of Theorem
5.4
, but with
a=1
and thus
b_{n}=bn
, we obtain:

Theorem 5.5

Let
(Z_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be a critical power-fractional
\mathsf{GWP}
with one ancestor, offspring distribution
\mathsf{PF}(\theta,1,b)
and offspring pgf
f
. Then the following assertions hold:

(a)

The survival probability
\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}>0)
satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{1/\theta}\,\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}>0)\ =\ b^{-1/\theta}.

(5.15)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-1/\theta}\,\mathbb{E}(Z_{n}|Z_{n}>0)\ =\ b^{1/\theta}.

(5.16)

(b)

The conditional law of
(bn)^{-1/\theta}Z_{n}
given
Z_{n}>0
converges weakly as
n\to\infty
, namely

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{Z_{n}}{(bn)^{1/\theta}}\in\cdot\bigg|Z_{n}>0\right)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\longrightarrow}}\ \mathsf{CPF}_{+}(\theta,1).

(5.17)

In the linear-fractional case
\theta=1
, Eq. (
5.17
) reduces to the familiar Kolmogorov-Yaglom exponential limit theorem after pointing out that
\mathsf{CPF}_{+}(1,1)
equals the standard exponential distribution and
2b=\mathbb{V}{\rm ar\,}Z_{1}
.

Proof

(a) By drawing on the formulae pointed out in the proof of Theorem
5.4
, but now with
a=1
and
b_{n}=bn
, we obtain

n^{1/\theta}\,\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}>0)\ =\ \frac{n^{1/\theta}}{\mathbb{E}(Z_{n}|Z_{n}>0)}\ =\ \left(\frac{n}{1+bn}\right)^{1/\theta}

and then obviously the asserted limits in (
5.15
) and (
5.16
).

(b) Put
Y_{n}=(bn)^{-1/\theta}Z_{n}
and
h_{n}(s)=\mathbb{E}(s^{Z_{n}}|Z_{n}>0)
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
Since
\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}\in\cdot|Z_{n}>0)=\mathsf{PF}(\theta,(1+bn)^{-1},1-(1+bn)^{-1})
, we infer

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{(1-\mathbb{E}(e^{-uY_{n}}|Z_{n}>0))^{\theta}}\ &=\ \frac{1}{(1-h_{n}(e^{-u/(bn)^{1/\theta}})^{\theta}}\\
&=\ \frac{(1+bn)^{-1}}{(1-e^{-u/(bn)^{1/\theta}})^{\theta}}\,+\,\frac{bn}{1+bn}\end{split}

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(e^{-uY_{n}}|Z_{n}>0)\ =\ 1\,-\,\left[\frac{(1+bn)^{-1}}{(1-e^{-u/(bn)^{1/\theta}})^{\theta}}\,+\,\frac{bn}{1+bn}\right]^{-1/\theta}

for any
u\geqslant 0
. But the last expression converges to
1-[u^{-\theta}+1]^{-1/\theta}
which, by (
5.7
), equals the Laplace transform of
\mathsf{CPF}_{+}(\theta,1)
.∎

In the following, let
(Z_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}

Theorem 6.1

Let
(Z_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}

(a)

The quenched extinction probability

(a) Going back to Athreya and Karlin

By ergodicity of

q(\text{\rm e})\ =\ \lim_{n\to\infty}q_{n}(\text{\rm e}_{1:n})\ =\ 1\,-\,\lim_{n\to\infty}(\Pi_{n}+R_{n})^{-\theta}\ =\ 1\,-\,R_{\infty}^{-\theta}\quad\text{a.s.}

under the given assumptions, we see that (

6.3

(b) Since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-\text{\rm E}e^{-uW_{n}})^{\theta}}\

7
Decomposition of supercritical processes

Every supercritical
\mathsf{GWP}

\mathcal{Z}=(Z_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
with one ancestor and extinction probability
0<q<1
can be decomposed into two nontrivial parts
\mathcal{Z}_{1}=(Z_{1,n})_{n\geqslant 0}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{2}=(Z_{2,n})_{n\geqslant 0}
by dividing each generation
n\geqslant 1
into their individuals with finite progeny and those having an infinite line of descent, respectively, see
(
4
, p. 47ff)
. When conditioning upon extinction of
\mathcal{Z}
, the process
\mathcal{Z}_{1}
then forms a subcritical
\mathsf{GWP}
with offspring pgf
g(s)=q^{-1}f(qs)
, whereas
\mathcal{Z}_{2}
constitutes a supercritical
\mathsf{GWP}
with offspring pgf

h(s)\,=\,\frac{f(q+(1-q)s)-q}{1-q},

when conditioning upon explosion of
\mathcal{Z}
. With
P
denoting the transition kernel of the Markov chain
\mathcal{Z}
, the law of
\mathcal{Z}_{1}
, known as the Harris-Sevastyanov transform, is actually nothing but the Doob
h
-transform of
\mathcal{Z}
under the positive
P
-harmonic function
\mathbb{N}_{0}\ni i\mapsto q^{i}
, see
(
12
, Thm. 3.1)
. It is also stated there that, if
f
is linear-fractional, then
g
is linear-fractional, see
(
12
, Prop. 3.1)
, and the same holds true for
h
as pointed out in
(
1
, Sect. 4)
. The extension of these findings to power-fractional
\mathsf{GWP}
’s in fixed environment, which naturally includes the linear-fractional ones
(\theta=1)
, is provided by the next result which has the interesting feature that the offspring law of the subcritical part
\mathcal{Z}_{1}
is again power-fractional, but with
\gamma>1
.

Theorem 7.1

Let
\mathcal{Z}=(Z_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be a supercritical
\mathsf{GWP}
with offspring law
\mathsf{PF}(\theta,a,b)
for
a\in(0,1)
and
b>1-a
, thus
0<q<1
by (
5.1
). Then
the offspring laws of
\mathcal{Z}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{2}
, the latter when conditioned under
Z_{n}\to\infty
, are

\mathsf{PF}(\theta,q^{-1},a,bq^{\theta})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsf{PF}_{+}(\theta,a),

respectively.

The extra notation for the power-fractional laws
\mathsf{PF}_{+}(\theta,a)
on the
positive
integers has been given here because they form the extension of their linear-fractional counterpart, i.e., the geometric laws
\mathsf{Geom}_{+}(a)
.

Proof

Put
\gamma=q^{-1}
. Using the definitions of
g
and
h
in terms of
f
from above, the assertions follow from

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\gamma-g(s))^{\theta}}\

\displaystyle=\ \frac{q^{\theta}}{(1-f(qs))^{\theta}}\ =\ \frac{aq^{\theta}}{(1-qs)^{\theta}}\,+\,bq^{\theta}\ =\ \frac{a}{(\gamma-s)^{\theta}}\,+\,bq^{\theta}

and

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-h(s))^{\theta}}\

\displaystyle=\ \frac{(1-q)^{\theta}}{(1-f(q+(1-q)s))^{\theta}}\ =\ \frac{a}{(1-s)^{\theta}}\,+\,b(1-q)^{\theta}

\displaystyle=\ \frac{a}{(1-s)^{\theta}}\,+\,1-a,

where
(1-q)^{\theta}=b^{-1}(1-a)
has been utilized for the last equality.∎

In the random environment case, the situation becomes more complex. First, the extinction probabilities
q(\text{\rm e}_{\scriptscriptstyle\geqslant n}),\,n\geqslant 1,
figuring in the pgf’s of the quenched offspring laws of both
\mathcal{Z}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{2}
now depend on the entire future and vary with shifts in the environment. As a result, the environment of these processes changes from
e
to
(\text{\rm e}_{\scriptscriptstyle\geqslant n})_{n\geqslant 1}
and is therefore no longer iid but only stationary and ergodic. This was previously noted in
[
1
]
for the linear-fractional case. As a second complication, the quenched offspring laws of the subcritical part
\mathcal{Z}_{1}
are generally no longer of power-fractional type as described in (A1) or (A2). Instead, they fall under an extended interpretation, as will be discussed in Remark
7.3
.

Theorem 7.2

Let
\mathcal{Z}=(Z_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
be supercritical with
0<q(\text{\rm e})<1
a.s. Put also
q_{n}=q(\text{\rm e}_{\scriptscriptstyle\geqslant n})
and
R_{n,\infty}=\Pi_{n}^{-1}(R_{\infty}-R_{n})
for
n\geqslant 1
. Then the following assertions hold for
\mathcal{Z}_{1}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{2}
as introduced before:

(a)

8
Power-fractional laws in continuous-time branching

In this section, we briefly discuss the question of whether power-fractional branching processes in continuous time can be defined, but limit ourselves to the class of Markov branching processes. This has already been done in greater detail in
[
15
]
, including the case of varying environment.

The
linear birth-death process

(Y(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
is not only a simple example of a Markov branching process, but also the one whose one-dimensional marginals are all linear-fractional. Specifically, assume that individuals, acting independently, give birth to a new individual at rate
\lambda>0
and die at rate
\mu\geqslant 0
. This means that each individual has an exponential lifetime with parameter
\lambda+\mu
, and at the end of its life either gives birth to two children, which occurs with probability
\lambda/(\lambda+\mu)
, or dies with probability
\mu/(\lambda+\mu)
. Thus, the process has offspring distribution
\frac{\mu}{\lambda+\mu}\delta_{0}+\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu}\delta_{2}
, with associated pgf

g(s)\,:=\,\frac{\mu}{\lambda+\mu}\,+\,\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu}s^{2},\quad s\in[0,1].

Put
\rho:=\lambda-\mu
,

\displaystyle\alpha(t)\ =\ \begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\mu e^{\rho t}-\mu}{\lambda e^{\rho t}-\mu}&\text{if }\lambda\neq\mu\\[8.53581pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\lambda t}{\lambda t+1}&\text{if }\lambda=\mu\end{cases},\quad\text{and}\quad\beta(t)\ =\ \frac{\lambda}{\mu}\alpha(t)

for
t\geqslant 0
. Let

G(s,t)\,:=\,\mathbb{E}(s^{Y(t)}|Y(0)=1)\quad\text{for }(s,t)\in[0,1]\times[0,\infty)

denote the pgf of
Y(t)
given
Y(0)=1
. Then the branching property implies

\displaystyle G(s,t+u)\ =\ G(G(s,t),u)\quad\text{for all }(s,t,u)\in[0,1]\times[0,\infty).

(8.1)

Moreover, with the boundary condition
G(s,0)=s
, the bivariate function
G(s,t)
forms the unique solution to the forward and backward equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G(s,t)\ =\ (\lambda+\mu)(g(s)-s)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial s}G(s,t)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G(s,t)\ =\ g(G(s,t))-G(s,t)

respectively, in particular

(\lambda+\mu)(g(s)-s)\,=\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G(s,0+)\,:=\,\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{G(s,t)-s}{t},\quad s\in[0,1].

(8.2)

These equations can be solved explicitly, yielding

\displaystyle G(s,t)\ =\ \alpha(t)\ +\ (1-\alpha(t))\frac{(1-\beta(t))s}{1-\beta(t)s},

(8.3)

where, setting
\rho:=\lambda-\mu
,

\displaystyle\alpha(t)\ =\ \begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\mu e^{\rho t}-\mu}{\lambda e^{\rho t}-\mu}&\text{if }\lambda\neq\mu\\[8.53581pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\lambda t}{\lambda t+1}&\text{if }\lambda=\mu\end{cases},\quad\text{and}\quad\beta(t)\ =\ \begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\lambda e^{\rho t}-\lambda}{\lambda e^{\rho t}-\mu}&\text{if }\lambda\neq\mu\\[8.53581pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\lambda t}{\lambda t+1}&\text{if }\lambda=\mu\end{cases},

see e.g.
[
18
]
. The uniqueness of the solution ensures that the linear birth-death process
(Y(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
is the unique Markov branching process with pgf’s
G(\cdot,t)
for
t\geqslant 0
, see
(
4
, Thm. III.2.1)
. From (
8.3
), it follows that all
Y(t)
are linear-fractional, specifically

\displaystyle\begin{split}Y(t)\ &\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\ \alpha(t)\delta_{0}\,+\,(1-\alpha(t))\,\mathsf{Geom}_{+}(1-\beta(t))\\
&=\ \begin{cases}\mathsf{LF}\bigg(e^{-\rho t},\frac{\lambda}{\rho}(1-e^{-\rho t})\bigg)&\text{if }\lambda\neq\mu,\\
\mathsf{LF}(1,\lambda t)&\text{if }\lambda=\mu.\end{cases}\end{split}

(8.4)

In the pure birth case
\mu=0<\lambda
, the well-known
Yule process
is obtained, where

Y(t)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\ \mathsf{LF}(e^{-\lambda t},1-e^{-\lambda t})\ =\ \mathsf{Geom}_{+}(e^{-\lambda t}),\quad t\geqslant 0.

We will now demonstrate that Markov branching processes with power-fractional marginals
\mathsf{PF}(\theta,a(t),b(t))
for some
\theta
less than
1
– specifically, their pgf’s – can be derived from
G(s,t)
(which is representing the case
\theta=1
) by utilizing the connection between linear-fractional and power-fractional laws through conjugation with a Sibuya law, as described in (f) of the Introduction (see (
1.13
)).

To this end, let
h
be the pgf of the Sibuya distribution
\mathsf{Sib}\hskip 0.6pt(\theta)
for some fixed
\theta\in(0,1)
, and let
(Y(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
be the linear birth-death process defined above with individual birth and death rates
\mu
and
\lambda
, respectively, and with pgf’s
G(\cdot,t)
given by (
8.3
). For each
t\geqslant 0
, define the pgf
F(\cdot,t)
as the conjugation of
G(\cdot,t)
with
h
, that is

F(s,t)\,;=\,h^{-1}(G(h(s),t)\quad\text{for }(s,t)\in[0,1]\times[0,\infty)

(8.5)

where
h^{-1}
denotes the inverse of
h
on
[0,1]
and can easily be computed as

h^{-1}(s)\ =\ 1-(1-s)^{1/\theta},\quad s\in[0,1].

It is straightforward to verify that (
8.1
) carries over to
F(s,t)
, thus

\displaystyle F(s,t+u)\ =\ F(F(s,t),u)\quad\text{for all }(s,t,u)\in[0,1]\times[0,\infty).

Furthermore, with the initial condition
F(s,0)=s
and for some
\nu>0
and some pgf
f
, the function
F(s,t)
must be the unique solution to the forward and backward equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}F(s,t)\ =\ \nu(f(s)-s)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial s}F(s,t)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}F(s,t)\ =\ f(F(s,t))-F(s,t)

respectively. The function
f
is derived as follows using (
8.2
), the relation (
8.5
), and the constraint that
f^{\prime}(0)
, which represents the probability for an individual to have exactly one child, must vanish:

\displaystyle\nu(f(s)-s)\ =\ \frac{\partial}{\partial t}F(s,0+)\ =\ (h^{-1})^{\prime}(G(h(s),0))\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G(h(s),0+)

which simplifies to

\displaystyle f(s)-s\ =\ \frac{(\lambda+\mu)(h^{-1})^{\prime}(h(s))(g(h(s))-h(s))}{\nu}\ =\ \frac{(\lambda+\mu)(g(h(s))-h(s))}{\nu h^{\prime}(s)}.

With explicit expressions for
g
and
h
, this yields

\displaystyle f(s)\ =\ 1\,-\,\frac{\theta\nu+\rho}{\theta\nu}(1-s)\,+\,\frac{\lambda}{\theta\nu}(1-s)^{1+\theta}.

Finally,
f^{\prime}(0)=(\theta\nu)^{-1}(\theta\nu+\rho-\lambda(1+\theta))=0
provides

\nu\ =\ \lambda+\mu\theta^{-1},

(8.6)

in particular
\nu\geqslant\lambda
. Thus, with
\mathsf{m}:=(\theta\nu+\rho)/\theta\nu=\lambda(1+\theta)/\theta\nu
, we arrive at

f(s)\ =\ 1\,-\,\mathsf{m}(1-s)\,+\,\frac{\mathsf{m}}{1+\theta}(1-s)^{1+\theta},

(8.7)

which is the same form of
f
as stated in
(
15
, Eq. (3))
. We note that
\mathsf{m}=f^{\prime}(1)
, so it represents the mean of the distribution associated with
f
.

After these considerations, we can state the following result:

Theorem 8.1

Given any
\theta\in(0,1)
and
\nu>0
, let
\lambda>0
and
\mu\geqslant 0
be such that (
8.6
) holds. Let
(Y(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
be a linear birth-death process with individual birth rate
\lambda
, death rate
\mu
and pgf’s
G(\cdot,t)
,
t\geqslant 0
. Then there exists a Markov branching process
(Z(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
with pgf’s
F(\cdot,t)
for
t\geqslant 0
, which has the following properties:

(i)

Individuals in the population described by the process have exponential lifetimes with parameter
\nu
and offspring law with pgf
f
defined in (
8.7
).

(ii)

F(s,t)
is the conjugation of
G(s,t)
stated in (
8.5
) with the pgf of the Sibuya law
\mathsf{Sib}\hskip 0.6pt(\theta)
.

(iii)

For each
t>0
, the law of
Z(t)
is power-fractional, specifically

Z(t)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\ \mathsf{PF}\big(\theta,e^{-\rho t},\lambda\rho^{-1}(1-e^{-\rho t}\big)

(8.8)

Proof

For (i) and (ii), it suffices to refer to the discussion preceding the theorem. For (iii), we refer to

[
15
]
, where it is shown that

\displaystyle F(s,t)\ =\ 1\,-\,\bigg(e^{-(\mathsf{m}-1)\nu\theta t}(1-s)^{-\theta}\,+\,\frac{\mathsf{m}\theta\nu}{1+\theta}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(\mathsf{m}-1)\nu u}\,du\bigg)^{-1/\theta}

for all
(s,t)\in[0,1]\times[0,\infty)
. Consequently, the law of each
Z(t)
,
t>0
, is power-fractional, namely

Z(t)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\ \mathsf{PF}\bigg(\theta,e^{-(\mathsf{m}-1)\theta\nu t},\frac{\mathsf{m}\nu\theta}{\theta+1}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(\mathsf{m}-1)\theta\nu u}\,du\bigg).

Finally, this is the same as (
8.8
) because, by (
8.6
) and the definition of
\mathsf{m}
, we have
\mathsf{m}\nu\theta=\lambda(\theta+1)
and
\mathsf{m}-1=\rho/\theta\nu
.∎

Remark 8.2

If
(Y(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
is chosen as a Yule process, meaning the individual death rate
\mu=0
, it is easily verified that
f(0)=0
. This can be understood intuitively because, in this case, the Markov branching process
(Z(t))_{t\geqslant 0}
(in terms of its pgf’s
F(\cdot,t)
) is the conjugation of a pure birth process with a distribution that assigns no probability mass at zero. As an immediate consequence
F(0,t)=0
, which in turn entails that the individual offspring distribution also has no mass at 0. Since
\mu=0
implies
\rho=\lambda
, we see that (
8.8
) simplifies to

Z(t)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\ \mathsf{PF}(\theta,e^{-\lambda t},1-e^{-\lambda t})\ =\ \mathsf{PF}_{+}(\theta,e^{-\lambda t})

(8.9)

for each
t>0
. We have thus obtained a power-fractional analog of the Yule process.

Remark 8.3

Let
(p_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
denote the probability distribution associated with
f
. Then it follows directly from (
8.7
) that

\frac{1-f(s)}{\mathsf{m}(1-s)}\ =\ 1\ -\ \frac{1}{1+\theta}(1-s)^{\theta}\ =\ \frac{1}{\mathsf{m}}\sum_{n\geqslant 0}\Bigg(\sum_{k>n}p_{k}\Bigg)s^{n}.

This shows that the normalized
tail measure
associated with
(p_{n})_{n\geqslant 0}
, given by
(\mathsf{m}^{-1}\sum_{k>n}p_{k})_{n\geqslant 0}
, is the generalized Sibuya distribution
\mathsf{GSib}\hskip 0.6pt(\theta,(1+\theta)^{-1})
. Since the probability mass function of this law is known (see (
1.12
)), the

’s can be derived from it, resulting in

p_{n}\ =\ \begin{cases}1-\mathsf{m}\theta(1+\theta)^{-1}&\text{if }n=0,\\
0&\text{if }n=1,\\
\mathsf{m}\theta/2&\text{if }n=2,\\
\mathsf{m}\theta(1-\theta)\cdots(n-2-\theta)/n!&\text{if }n\geqslant 3.\end{cases}

Again, this is already stated in
[

.

9
Auxiliary lemmata about power-fractional laws

Lemma 9.1

Let
f
be the pgf of
\mathsf{PF}(\theta,\gamma,a,b)
for
\theta\in[-1,1]\backslash\{0\}
,
\gamma>1
, and
a\in(0,1)
(see Table
1
). Then
f_{\gamma}(s):=f(\gamma s)/\gamma
is the pgf of the proper supercritical power-fractional distribution
\mathsf{PF}(\theta,1,a,b\gamma^{\theta})
, which has a unique fixed point at
1/\gamma
in the interval
(0,1)
.

Proof

It follows from Eq. (
1.3
) upon multiplication with
\gamma^{\theta}
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-\gamma^{-1}f(\gamma s))^{\theta}}\ =\ \frac{a}{(1-s)^{\theta}}\,+\,b\gamma^{\theta}

and this obviously implies the assertions.∎

Lemma 9.2

Let
X
be a random variable with law
\mathsf{PF}(\theta,a,b)
for
\theta\in(0,1]
. Then the conditional law of
X
given
X>0
is again power-fractional, namely

\mathbb{P}(X\in\cdot|X>0)\ =\ \mathsf{PF}\left(\theta,\frac{a}{a+b},\frac{b}{a+b}\right).

Proof

Let
f
denote the pgf of
X
. Then the pgf of the conditional law in question is given by

\displaystyle h(s)\ =\ \frac{f(s)-f(0)}{1-f(0)}\ =\ 1\,-\,\frac{1-f(s)}{1-f(0)},

hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-h(s))^{\theta}}\ =\ \left(\frac{1-f(0)}{1-f(s)}\right)^{\theta}\ =\ \frac{1}{a+b}\left[\frac{a}{(1-s)^{\theta}}+b\right],

which provides the asserted result.∎

It is a well-known fact that when
thinning
a Poisson-distributed set of points by independently tossing a
p
-coin for each point, the number of remaining points follows again a Poisson distribution. More generally, this also holds for the number of points with a given label when each point is
tagged
independently by assigning a label
i
with probability
p_{i}
, where
i
belongs to a finite or countably infinite set of labels
\mathcal{L}
and
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{L}}p_{i}=1
. The following lemma shows that this property extends to power-fractional distributions.

Lemma 9.3
(Thinning/Tagging)

Let
X,I_{1},I_{2},\ldots
be independent random variables, where
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathsf{PF}(\theta,a,b)
for
\theta\in(0,1]
, and the
I_{n}
take values in a finite or countably infinite set of labels
\mathcal{L}
, with common law
(\gamma_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{L}}
. Then the law of the random sum
Z(i):=\sum_{k=1}^{X}\mathbf{1}_{\{I_{k}=i\}}
is also power-fractional, namely
\mathsf{PF}(\theta,a\gamma_{i}^{-\theta},b)
. for each
i\in\mathcal{L}
.

Proof

Since the law of
\mathbf{1}_{\{I_{k}=i\}}
is Bernoulli with parameter
\gamma_{i}
and has the pgf
g_{i}(s)=\gamma_{i}s+1-\gamma_{i}
, we infer that
Z(i)
has the pgf
f\circ g_{i}
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-f(g_{i}(s)))^{\theta}}\ =\ \frac{a}{(1-g_{i}(s))^{\theta}}\,+\,b\ =\ \frac{a\gamma_{i}^{-\theta}}{(1-s)^{\theta}}\,+\,b.

Hence,
Z(i)
has the asserted power-fractional law.∎

The lemma gives rise to some additional observations that finally result in the definition of
m
-dimensional power-fractional distributions
for
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.

(a)

One can combine any subset
B
of labels from the set
\mathcal{L}
into a single label, yielding

Z(B)\,:=\,\sum_{i\in B}Z(i)\,=\,\sum_{k=1}^{X}\mathbf{1}_{B}(I_{k}),

and it can be readily verified that

Z(B)\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\ \mathsf{PF}\bigg(\theta,\frac{a}{\gamma(B)^{\theta}},b\bigg),

(9.1)

where
\gamma(B):=\sum_{i\in B}\gamma_{i}
.

(b)

One can interpret
Z
as a random point measure, or equivalently, as a point process on the label set
\mathcal{L}
, by writing
Z=\sum_{k=1}^{X}\delta_{I_{k}}
. As stated in (
9.1
),
Z(B)
has a power-fractional distribution for every
B\subset\mathcal{L}
. However, unlike the Poisson case, the random variables
Z(B_{1}),\ldots,Z(B_{n})
for pairwise disjoint subsets
B_{i}
are generally not independent. In particular, the
Z(i)
for
i\in\mathcal{L}
are typically not independent.

(c)

Finally, we note that the conditional law of
(Z(i))_{i\in\mathcal{L}}
given
Z(\mathcal{L})=z\in\mathbb{N}
is multinomial with parameters
z
and
\gamma_{i},\,i\in\mathcal{L}
, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}(Z(i)=z_{i},\,i\in\mathcal{L}|Z(\mathcal{L})=z)\ =\ \frac{z!}{\prod_{i\in\mathcal{L}}z_{i}!}\prod_{i\in\mathcal{L}}\gamma_{i}^{z_{i}}

for each
(z_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{L}}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\mathcal{L}}
such that
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{L}}z_{i}=z
.

After these observations, the unique law of the random vector
(Z(i))_{i\in\mathcal{L}}
can be referred to as the
m
-dimensional power-fractional distribution with parameters
\theta,a,b
and
(\gamma_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{L}}
, abbreviated as
\mathsf{PF}_{m}(\theta,a,b,(\gamma_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{L}})
, where
m=|\mathcal{L}|
. It is of particular interest in connection with multitype power-fractional
\mathsf{GWP}
’s that will be discussed in a future paper.
