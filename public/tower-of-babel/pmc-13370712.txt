The impact of pregestational stress on neurodevelopment: Implications for autism spectrum disorder and attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder
Authors: Samaneh Ahmadian‐Moghadam, Ehsan Saboory, Shiva Roshan‐Milani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmerging evidence indicates that stress experienced by parents before conception can influence offspring neurodevelopment through enduring neuroendocrine and epigenetic mechanisms. This review addresses the central question of how pregestational stress (PGS) might alter developmental trajectories and increase vulnerability to autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). We examined human and animal studies investigating the effects of PGS on germline epigenetic regulation, GABAergic signalling maturation and hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis programming. We synthesized key findings using a narrative review approach, integrating mechanistic data, developmental neurobiology and evidence for transgenerational inheritance, and we summarized the converging results in a conceptual figure and comprehensive table. The findings indicate that PGS can disrupt germline DNA methylation patterns, alter early GABA system development and shift hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis responsivity, thereby influencing fetal brain maturation and increasing the risk for ASD‐ and ADHD‐related phenotypes. Additional evidence highlights that PGS might exert effects comparable to prenatal stress exposure, suggesting an overlooked but physiologically meaningful window of vulnerability. The conclusions emphasize that PGS represents a novel and under‐recognized factor contributing to neurodevelopmental risk and that its mechanistic pathways offer new insight into how early‐life vulnerability might begin even prior to pregnancy. The take‐home message is that parental stress reduction and preconception health optimization might serve as promising strategies to mitigate intergenerational neurodevelopmental risk. The wider context underscores the broader physiological significance of PGS and raises important questions regarding the reversibility of epigenetic alterations, the timing and type of stressors most critical, and potential interventions to enhance resilience across generations. Keywords: attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder, autism, epigenetics, GABAergic signalling, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, interneuron, neurodevelopment, noradrenaline, pregestational stress

Introduction

Neurodevelopment is profoundly sensitive to environmental and biological influences, particularly during prenatal and early postnatal life. Although much research has focused on prenatal stress, the concept of pregestational stress (PGS) has gained increasing attention. Although maternal and paternal stress exposures involve distinct biological pathways, the present review adopts an integrative perspective, focusing on PGS as a shared window during which parental experiences can converge on common epigenetic and neurodevelopmental mechanisms relevant to autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) risk. PGS refers to exposure to stressors prior to conception, affecting either the mother or the father, or both (Mahmoodkhani, Saboory, Roshan‐Milani, Azizi, Karimipour, Rasmi et al., 2018). Such stress can influence germline epigenetics and alter developmental trajectories, increasing susceptibility to neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), including ASD and ADHD (Dory et al., 2025). PGS refers to exposure to psychological, physiological or environmental stressors prior to pregnancy. These stressors can affect either parent and exert their effects via distinct biological pathways (Tan et al., 2023). In males, preconception stress might influence the epigenetic programming of sperm (Marcho et al., 2020), whereas in females it might affect oocyte quality, maternal physiology and the intrauterine environment even before pregnancy begins (Denicol & Siqueira, 2023; Zhang et al., 2025). The concept is grounded in the developmental origins of health and disease hypothesis, which posits that environmental factors during critical periods of development, including those before conception, can permanently alter developmental trajectories and predispose individuals to various health conditions, including mental health disorders (Arima & Fukuoka, 2020; Lacagnina, 2020).

One of the primary mechanisms by which PGS affects offspring is through epigenetic modifications in the germline. These include changes in DNA methylation, histone modification and non‐coding RNA expression, which do not alter the DNA sequence itself but regulate gene expression (Ben Maamar et al., 2021). In males, exposure to stress has been shown to induce epigenetic changes in sperm that persist and influence early embryonic development. A landmark study by Rodgers et al. (2013) demonstrated that male mice exposed to chronic variable stress prior to mating produced offspring with dysregulated hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis responses, despite the absence of direct exposure to stress during pregnancy (Rodgers et al., 2013). The researchers identified alterations in sperm microRNA (miRNA) content as a potential vector for this transgenerational transmission. These findings have since been corroborated by additional studies showing that stress‐induced epigenetic changes in sperm are associated with increased risks of anxiety, depressive‐like behaviours and altered brain morphology in offspring (Bale, 2015). In females, PGS can affect the oocyte epigenome and the maternal environment, including hormonal regulation, immune function and uterine receptivity. For instance, preconception stress has been shown to lead to reduced implantation rates, altered placental development and impaired fetal growth (Bozec et al., 2024; Jahan‐Mihan, Leftwich et al., 2024). These changes might prime the intrauterine environment to be less favourable for optimal neurodevelopment, thereby increasing susceptibility to disorders such as ASD, ADHD and epilepsy.

ASD and ADHD are two of the most prevalent NDDs, affecting approximately 1 in 36 and 1 in 10 children, respectively, in the USA (Al Ghamdi & AlMusailhi, 2024; Martinez et al., 2024). Both disorders are multifactorial in origin, arising from a combination of genetic predisposition and environmental influences (Karimi et al., 2017). Emerging evidence suggests that PGS might be one of these influential environmental risk factors (Magdalena et al., 2020). ASD is characterized by deficits in social communication and the presence of restricted, repetitive behaviours (Tsai et al., 2020). A growing number of animal and human studies indicate that parental stress prior to conception might increase the likelihood of offspring developing autistic‐like traits. For example, Yeshurun et al. (2017) found that male mice subjected to early‐life stress produced offspring with reduced social interaction and increased stereotypic behaviours, reminiscent of core ASD features (Yeshurun et al., 2017). These behavioural phenotypes were linked to altered gene expression in the prefrontal cortex and amygdala of offspring, which are brain regions crucial for social behaviour.

In human studies, paternal adverse childhood experiences have been associated with increased risk for ASD in their children, independent of maternal exposures (Gallo et al., 2022; Panisch et al., 2020). There is an urgent need for additional research on paternal adverse childhood experiences to better inform tailored prevention and intervention strategies for fathers. An adapted version of the heuristic model of fathering is suggested to guide future studies (Deneault et al., 2025). The heuristic model of fathering, originally proposed by Deneault et al. (2025), is a conceptual framework that integrates multiple levels of influence on fathering behaviours, including individual characteristics of the father (e.g., mental health, stress, substance use), child characteristics (e.g., temperament, developmental stage), family context (e.g., partner relationship, parenting practices) and broader socio‐environmental factors (e.g., culture, socioeconomic conditions). The model emphasizes that fathering is a dynamic and bidirectional process, in which fathers both influence and are influenced by their children and the surrounding context over time (Cabrera et al., 2014). In its adapted form (Deneault et al., 2025), the model is extended to highlight developmental timing, intergenerational mechanisms and biological pathways, making it particularly useful for guiding research on how paternal exposures (such as stress, substance use or lifestyle factors) might shape offspring neurodevelopmental and behavioural outcomes. This adapted framework provides a useful heuristic for organizing future studies examining paternal contributions to neurodevelopmental risk and resilience.

ADHD, characterized by inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity, has also been linked to PGS (Mahone & Denckla, 2017; Purper‐Ouakil et al., 2011). Several animal studies have demonstrated that both maternal and paternal preconception exposures, including psychological stressors and substance use, can influence offspring neurodevelopment through epigenetic mechanisms. For example, Kim et al. (2014) reported that male mice exposed to chronic ethanol consumption prior to mating produced offspring with altered stress responsivity and behavioural outcomes (Kim et al., 2014). Importantly, ethanol exposure in this context should be interpreted as a neuroendocrine perturbation rather than a direct model of psychological stress, although chronic ethanol intake is known to activate stress‐related pathways, including the HPA axis (Marty et al., 2020). Preconception paternal mental disorders can influence offspring neurodevelopment, affective functioning and behaviour, largely through non‐genetic mechanisms, particularly epigenetic modifications (Tan et al., 2023). In the following sections, we will review evidence from both animal and human studies relevant to this topic, aiming to compile and elucidate the exact or putative mechanisms underlying PGS‐induced aberrant neurodevelopment and its potential link to ASD and ADHD.

Evidence from animal models

Evidence from multiple animal studies suggests that PGS might induce alterations in neurodevelopmental processes. Azizi et al. (2019) demonstrated that preconception predator stress in rats significantly affected offspring anxiety‐like behaviour and corticosterone levels (Azizi et al., 2019). The study revealed sex‐specific vulnerabilities, with male offspring being more sensitive to maternal stress and females more responsive to paternal stress. Combined parental stress did not produce additive effects, suggesting complex epigenetic interactions. Importantly, maternal effects discussed here refer specifically to preconception (pregestational) influences on oocyte quality, endocrine status and metabolic signalling, which are conceptually and biologically distinct from direct prenatal (in utero) stress exposures. Mahmoodkhani, Saboory, Roshan‐Milani, Azizi, Karimipour, Sayyadi, et al. (2018) found that pregestational predator stress in both parents significantly decreased fertility rates and increased seizure susceptibility in rat offspring. Pups from stressed parents exhibited earlier seizure onset, longer seizure durations, reduced body weight at birth and early postnatal stages, and impaired sperm chromatin integrity in stressed fathers (Mahmoodkhani, Saboory, Roshan‐Milani, Azizi, Karimipour, Rasmi, et al., 2018). Saboory et al. (2020) reviewed the role of noradrenaline (NA) in neurodevelopment and showed that altered noradrenergic signalling attributable to stress affects neurogenesis, circuit formation and synaptic plasticity, potentially contributing to disorders such as ADHD and ASD (Saboory et al., 2020). Saboory et al. (2019) further revealed that prenatal stress increases seizure susceptibility in offspring, emphasizing the role of sex‐dependent epigenetic and neuroendocrine mechanisms, particularly involving GABAergic systems (Saboory et al., 2019). Rodgers et al. (2013) reported that paternal stress before mating alters sperm miRNA content, which in turn programs offspring HPA axis function and anxiety‐like behaviour (Rodgers et al., 2013). Bale (2015) further suggested that sperm miRNAs and extracellular vesicles mediate these stress‐related effects transgenerationally (Bale, 2015). Mashoodh et al. (2012) demonstrated that paternal stress alters gene expression in offspring brains through both direct paternal influences and indirect, postnatal mechanisms mediated by maternal care. Specifically, paternal stress exposure was associated with changes in offspring neural gene expression and behaviour that were modulated by variations in maternal caregiving, indicating that altered maternal care can act as a critical postnatal pathway, through which paternal stress‐related signals are expressed in offspring (Mashoodh et al., 2012). In a subsequent study, Mashoodh et al. (2023) further showed that chronic variable stress in male BALB/c mice produces sex‐specific neurobehavioural outcomes in offspring, concluding that paternal stress can influence offspring development both directly and indirectly, via stress‐induced changes in maternal behaviour, with important implications for sex‐dependent vulnerability (Mashoodh et al., 2023).

Epigenetic mechanisms

Epigenetic mechanisms provide a powerful biological framework through which PGS can influence neurodevelopment in the next generation. Stress experienced prior to conception has been shown to induce lasting and heritable changes in germ cells, both sperm and oocytes, through processes such as DNA methylation, histone modification and the regulation of non‐coding RNAs, including miRNAs (Duffy et al., 2021; Marcho et al., 2020). Unlike the relatively stable DNA sequence, epigenetic markers are dynamic and environmentally responsive, enabling modulation of gene expression and cellular phenotypes in response to external stressors.

Epigenetic regulation represents a highly complex and multilayered system that extends beyond DNA methylation to include histone post‐translational modifications, chromatin remodelling and diverse classes of non‐coding RNAs (e.g., miRNAs, PIWI‐interacting RNAs and long non‐coding RNAs), which interact to shape transcriptional programs during gametogenesis and early embryonic development (Dvoran et al., 2022). Importantly, these epigenetic layers do not act in isolation but form an interconnected regulatory network in both parents that influences chromatin accessibility, genomic stability and developmental gene expression trajectories critical for neurodevelopment. Within this framework, increasing attention has been directed towards the role of paternal stress in shaping offspring outcomes via epigenetic modifications in sperm. Early work in this field focused primarily on maternal influences, whereas a substantial and rapidly expanding body of evidence now demonstrates that paternal experiences prior to conception can also program offspring neurodevelopment through germline‐mediated mechanisms. In particular, paternal stress has been shown to alter the epigenetic cargo of sperm, with non‐coding RNAs emerging as key mediators of intergenerational and transgenerational inheritance (Table 1). Seminal studies have demonstrated that chronic stress induces specific changes in sperm miRNA profiles and that these altered miRNAs can transmit stress‐related phenotypes causally to the next generation. For instance, microinjection of sperm‐derived miRNAs from stressed males into naive zygotes has been shown to recapitulate alterations in offspring stress responsivity and HPA axis function (Rodgers et al., 2015). These findings provide direct experimental evidence supporting RNA‐mediated inheritance mechanisms. Subsequent studies have extended these observations by demonstrating that paternal stress also affects other classes of non‐coding RNAs, including long non‐coding RNAs, which can influence embryonic development and later‐life behavioural outcomes (Hoffmann et al., 2024).

Beyond RNA‐mediated mechanisms, emerging data indicate that paternal stress can also modify sperm chromatin architecture. Changes in chromatin accessibility and epigenetic landscape have been reported following activation of stress hormone signalling pathways, suggesting that higher‐order genome organization contributes to the transmission of environmental information across generations (Fischer et al., 2023). In parallel, specific miRNAs have been identified as functional regulators of offspring phenotypes. For example, reduced transmission of miR‐34/449 to preimplantation embryos has been implicated in the inheritance of behavioural alterations following paternal social instability stress (Champroux et al., 2024), and dysregulation of miR‐124 has been linked to impaired social memory in offspring (Chen et al., 2024).

In addition to germ cell‐intrinsic mechanisms, systemic signalling pathways appear to play an important role in mediating the effects of paternal stress. Circulating factors, including glucocorticoids and extracellular vesicles, can influence germ cells and modify their epigenetic content. Experimental studies have shown that elevated corticosterone levels alter sperm RNA profiles and subsequently affect offspring behaviour (Hoffmann et al., 2023). Moreover, recent evidence suggests that astrocyte‐derived exosomes can regulate sperm miRNA content, revealing a new axis of communication between the CNS and the germline (Champroux et al., 2025). Likewise, circulating blood factors have been implicated in transmitting stress‐related signals to sperm, further highlighting the systemic nature of epigenetic inheritance (van Steenwyk et al., 2020). However, it is important to note that the intergenerational transmission of paternal stress effects is not universal. Some studies have reported limited or absent effects depending on the nature, duration and timing of stress exposure, indicating that these processes are highly context dependent (Fennell et al., 2020). This variability underscores the need for careful interpretation and suggests that only specific types of stress might produce robust epigenetic inheritance. Importantly, epigenetic programming remains a dynamic and potentially reversible process. Environmental interventions, such as enrichment, have been shown to mitigate or prevent the transmission of stress‐induced phenotypes across generations (Gapp, Bohacek et al., 2016), supporting the concept of epigenetic plasticity. Furthermore, transcriptomic analyses demonstrate that complex stress protocols can broadly reshape sperm RNA profiles, which are associated with alterations in offspring anxiety‐like behaviour, brain function and metabolic regulation (Huang et al., 2025; Tian et al., 2021).

Collectively, these findings support a comprehensive model, in which paternal stress reshapes sperm epigenetic information through multiple interacting mechanisms, including non‐coding RNAs, chromatin remodelling and systemic signalling pathways, thereby influencing early embryonic development and long‐term neurobehavioural and metabolic outcomes in offspring.

During reproduction, the distinct epigenetic signatures of sperm and oocytes act as barriers to gamete fusion (Clark & Rager, 2020). To overcome this and restore totipotency, epigenetic reprogramming occurs in two key stages: after fertilization in the zygote and during gametogenesis in primordial germ cells, involving global erasure and re‐establishment of epigenetic marks (Tan et al., 2023). Traditionally, sperm were thought to contribute only the haploid genome. However, advances in sequencing technologies have revealed that sperm also transmit epigenetic information, including DNA methylation, histone modifications and retention and various non‐coding RNAs (for more details, see Fig. 2; Du et al., 2022; Tan et al., 2023). These molecular alterations do not change the DNA sequence but profoundly affect how genes are expressed during development. In paternal stress models, particularly in rodents, modifications in sperm miRNA content have been strongly associated with disrupted neurodevelopmental outcomes in offspring, including altered stress reactivity and emotional behaviour (Rodgers et al., 2013). Likewise, maternal stress before conception can affect the biochemical composition of oocytes and disrupt the hormonal and immunological balance of the reproductive tract, thereby shaping the intrauterine environment from the earliest stages of embryogenesis (Chan et al., 2018). These molecular alterations do not change the DNA sequence but profoundly affect how genes are expressed during development.

In paternal stress models, particularly in rodents, modifications in sperm miRNA content have been strongly associated with disrupted neurodevelopmental outcomes in offspring, including altered stress reactivity and emotional behaviour (Rodgers et al., 2013). Likewise, maternal stress before conception can affect the biochemical composition of oocytes and disrupt the hormonal and immunological balance of the reproductive tract, thereby shaping the intrauterine environment from the earliest stages of embryogenesis (Chan et al., 2018). Studies by Gapp et al. (2014) and Franklin et al. (2010) provide pivotal evidence supporting the role of PGS in epigenetically mediated neurodevelopmental alterations. Gapp et al. (2014), using a mouse model of unpredictable maternal separation combined with maternal stress, demonstrated that early‐life trauma in male mice (serving as a proxy for preconception stress) was associated with specific alterations in DNA methylation patterns and small non‐coding RNA profiles in sperm. These molecular changes were not only present in the sperm but were also mirrored in the brains of the offspring, particularly in areas related to emotional regulation and stress processing. Franklin et al. (2010) expanded on this by showing that chronic stress exposure in male mice prior to mating induced robust behavioural abnormalities in their progeny, including increased anxiety and depressive‐like behaviour. Importantly, these phenotypes were associated with changes in the expression of genes involved in synaptic plasticity and neurotransmission in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, highlighting the enduring impact of preconception stress on brain function (Harker et al., 2018).

GABAergic signalling in neurodevelopment

One of the central neurobiological systems affected by PGS through epigenetic programming is the GABAergic system (Camille Melón & Maguire, 2016; Zhu et al., 2018). GABA is the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the CNS, playing a crucial role in modulating neural excitability, shaping neurodevelopmental trajectories (Bolneo et al., 2022) and maintaining the balance between excitation and inhibition (E/I) within neural circuits (Caballero et al., 2021). Disruption of GABAergic signalling has consistently been implicated in the pathophysiology of NDDs, such as ASD (Zhao, Mao et al., 2021) and ADHD (Puts et al., 2020).

Evidence suggests that epigenetic alterations caused by stress in the paternal germline can influence the transcription of genes involved in GABA synthesis, such as glutamate decarboxylase 2 (GAD1) and 2 (GAD2), receptor formation (e.g., GABRA1) and synaptic organization, as demonstrated by DNA extracted from blood cells (Domschke et al., 2013). Such changes might reduce GABAergic tone during critical periods of brain development, leading to impaired cortical maturation, altered synaptic pruning and dysregulated HPA axis activity (Bolneo et al., 2022), all of which are common features of ASD and ADHD.

Tang et al. (2021) highlighted the central role of GABAergic signalling in early brain development, noting that although GABA is the main inhibitory neurotransmitter in the mature brain, it is initially excitatory in the immature brain owing to elevated intracellular chloride concentrations (Tang et al., 2021). The timely ‘GABA switch’ from excitatory to inhibitory action is essential for synaptogenesis, circuit refinement and neural plasticity. Disruptions in this switch are strongly associated with ASD and ADHD, often through mechanisms involving altered interneuron development, impaired synapse formation and dysregulated chloride transport via sodium–potassium–chloride cotransporter 1 (NKCC1) and potassium–chloride transporter 2 (KCC2) (McArdle et al., 2023; Peerboom & Wierenga, 2021).

Stress hormones, such as corticosterone, have been shown to modulate GABA receptor expression and transporter function (Maguire, 2018; Mody & Maguire, 2011), providing a mechanistic link between stress exposure and functional GABAergic deficits. Scimemi (2014) emphasized the role of GABA transporter plasticity, particularly GAT1, in regulating synaptic GABA dynamics in stress conditions. Lim et al. (2018) and Marín (2012) reviewed the development and function of specific interneuron subtypes, including parvalbumin (PV), somatostatin (SST) and vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) interneurons, highlighting how their disruption contributes to E/I imbalance observed in NDDs (Lim et al., 2018; Marín, 2012). Consistent with this, studies have demonstrated that prenatal stress can delay the GABA switch, prolonging the excitatory action of GABA and resulting in heightened neural excitability and social deficits (Ben‐Ari, 2002; Tyzio et al., 2014). Such findings support the hypothesis that PGS‐induced epigenetic dysregulation of GABAergic pathways represents a key mechanism linking parental stress to aberrant neurodevelopment in offspring.

Integration: stress, epigenetics and GABAergic dysfunction

The integration of stress‐induced epigenetic modifications and GABAergic dysfunction presents a biologically plausible and increasingly supported pathway linking PGS to altered neurodevelopment and increased risk for disorders such as ASD, ADHD and epilepsy. As mentioned earlier, stress experienced by either parent before conception can result in persistent molecular changes in germ cells through mechanisms including DNA methylation, histone modifications and the dysregulation of small non‐coding RNAs, such as miRNAs (Chan et al., 2018; Denicol & Siqueira, 2023). These alterations influence gene expression in the developing embryo, with lasting consequences for brain development and function. A central aspect of these consequences involves the disruption of GABAergic signalling. In early development, GABA has excitatory effects owing to high intracellular chloride concentrations, and its timely shift to inhibitory function is crucial for healthy brain maturation. Epigenetic alterations in genes that regulate GABA synthesis (e.g., GAD1 and GAD2), receptor subunits (e.g., GABAA1 and GABAB3) or chloride transporters (e.g., KCC2 and NKCC1) can delay or impair this critical transition, resulting in persistent neural circuit imbalances. PGS can prime the neuroendocrine and neural systems of the developing offspring towards heightened excitability and stress reactivity. Evidence from Mahmoodkhani, Saboory, Roshan‐Milani, Azizi, Karimipour, Rasmi et al., 2018 and Saboory et al. (2019) highlights seizure‐prone phenotypes in the progeny of animals subjected to stress prior to mating. Their findings demonstrate increased neuronal excitability and seizure susceptibility in offspring, which are hallmark indicators of disrupted E/I balance, probably attributable to altered GABAergic function. These outcomes were also associated with delayed maturation of GABAergic interneurons and altered expression of GABA receptor subunits in the hippocampus (Milenkovic et al., 2018) and cortex (Le Magueresse & Monyer, 2013), which are regions critically involved in learning, memory (Fuchsberger & Paulsen, 2022) and emotional regulation (Pronier et al., 2023). Moreover, structural and molecular evidence indicates that stressed fathers exhibit compromised spermatogenesis and reduced chromatin integrity, suggesting a germline‐mediated mechanism of transmission (Kaltsas et al., 2025). Stress‐induced chromatin condensation defects and histone‐to‐protamine replacement errors during spermatogenesis might result in the transmission of aberrant epigenetic marks to the zygote (Kaltsas et al., 2025). These aberrations can dysregulate key neurodevelopmental genes, including those governing inhibitory neurotransmission and neurogenesis. In this way, the offspring inherit not only the genome but also an epigenetically modified regulatory landscape that predisposes them to neural hyperexcitability and increased vulnerability to disorders such as epilepsy and ASD (Tang et al., 2021). Furthermore, the altered neuroendocrine profile seen in offspring of stressed parents, such as elevated corticosterone levels and hypersensitivity of the HPA axis, might exacerbate GABAergic dysfunction, because glucocorticoids are known to interfere with GABA receptor expression and synaptic plasticity (Di, Maxson et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2016). This multilayered interaction between inherited epigenetic dysregulation, altered neurotransmission and hormonal imbalance forms a coherent pathway through which PGS might shape offspring brain function. Collectively, these findings underscore the critical role of preconceptional health and environmental conditions in shaping neural development. The integration of epigenetic research with neurophysiological outcomes reveals that stress prior to conception is not only a transient parental experience but also a potential risk factor for long‐term neurodevelopmental vulnerability in the next generation (Saboory et al., 2019). Human evidence and translational implications of PGS in NDDs

Although direct experimental evidence in humans regarding the effects of PGS on offspring neurodevelopment is still emerging, several observational and translational studies offer compelling insights into the potential mechanisms and outcomes. Accumulating human data, particularly from longitudinal cohort studies, suggest that parental mental health status prior to conception plays a significant role in shaping the neurodevelopmental trajectory of offspring (Kępińska et al., 2024). These effects appear to be mediated by complex interactions among genetic predispositions, epigenetic programming, hormonal environments and neuroimmune mechanisms (Yuan et al., 2023). Parental PGS and mental conditions, including chronic anxiety, depression and exposure to trauma, have consistently been associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes and increased risk for NDDs such as ASD, ADHD and cognitive delay (Siracusano et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2022). For example, studies from the Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children (ALSPAC) have shown that maternal mental health even before pregnancy predicts child behavioural and emotional outcomes, independent of perinatal exposures (Chen et al., 2024). Furthermore, early‐life trauma or high allostatic load in women (they carry a heavier biological ‘stress burden’, which can harm their long‐term health) has been associated with altered placental gene expression and increased fetal exposure to glucocorticoids during pregnancy (Monk et al., 2016; Piotrowski et al., 2020; Zijlmans et al., 2017). This fetal overexposure to stress hormones can lead to lasting neuroendocrine changes, including dysregulation of the HPA axis and impaired maturation of inhibitory circuits in the brain (Miranda & Sousa, 2018).

Genetic studies also support the interaction between inherited vulnerability and environmental stressors in the development of NDDs. In a large‐scale sequencing study, Satterstrom et al. (2020) identified >100 risk genes for ASD, many of which are highly expressed in developing cortical interneurons, which are key components of the GABAergic system (Satterstrom et al., 2020). This finding supports the hypothesis that disruptions in GABAergic signalling, especially during critical periods of brain development, are a shared mechanism underlying ASD and related disorders. These risk genes are often involved in synaptic regulation (Bonsi et al., 2022), chromatin remodelling (Kim & Kaang, 2017) and transcriptional control and might be particularly sensitive to environmental modulation through epigenetic mechanisms (Li et al., 2021).

Other maternal health conditions present before conception, such as diabetes, obesity and metabolic syndrome, have also been linked to adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes. Chronic metabolic disorders, such as diabetes, although not psychological stressors themselves, are characterized by persistent physiological stress states, including glucocorticoid dysregulation, inflammation and oxidative stress, which are known to influence epigenetic programming relevant to neurodevelopment (Reagan, 2012). Ornoy et al. (2021) reviewed evidence indicating that maternal diabetes before and during early pregnancy is associated with increased risks of CNS malformations, developmental delay and ASD (Ornoy, Becker et al., 2021). These associations might reflect both metabolic and inflammatory dysregulation that disrupts the intrauterine environment and interferes with normal brain development (Lubrano et al., 2024; Parenti & Slupsky, 2024). On a molecular level, specific gene variants in GABA receptor subunits have been associated with NDDs. For instance, polymorphisms in the GABRB2 gene, which encodes the β2 subunit of the GABAA receptor, have been linked to increased risk for ASD, schizophrenia and epilepsy. GABRB2 expression is subject to specific epigenetic regulations in the form of partial maternal genomic imprinting (Barki & Xue, 2022). Studies have shown that these variants might influence GABAergic synaptic function and contribute to imbalances in cortical excitability. Neuroimaging and electrophysiological studies further corroborate this by demonstrating reduced GABA receptor availability and altered cortical inhibition in individuals with ASD and ADHD (Gaetz et al., 2014; Puts et al., 2017). Magnetic resonance spectroscopy studies have found decreased GABA concentrations in multiple brain regions, including the prefrontal cortex and auditory cortex, in children and adults with ASD, indicating a persistent E/I imbalance (Horder et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2021). Electrophysiological studies using EEG and magnetoencephalography have also shown atypical γ‐band oscillations and impaired neural synchronization in ASD and ADHD (Rojas & Wilson, 2014; Ye et al., 2014), which are believed to reflect disrupted inhibitory interneuron activity.

The convergence of animal and emerging human data underscores the importance of parental life experiences, particularly chronic or early‐life stress, as modulators of offspring brain development through epigenetic inheritance, while simultaneously highlighting the translational value of animal models that recapitulate both the behavioural phenotypes and neurophysiological signatures of human NDDs. For example, rodent models of paternal stress, maternal immune activation (Ronovsky, Berger et al., 2016) and genetic mutations in GABA‐related genes exhibit behaviours analogous to human symptoms such as reduced social interaction, hyperactivity and increased seizure susceptibility (Seney et al., 2013). They also show molecular and cellular alterations in GABAergic pathways and stress‐related gene expression. Importantly, translational studies have begun to identify potential therapeutic targets and intervention strategies. Environmental enrichment (Aghighi Bidgoli et al., 2020), exercise (Nowacka‐Chmielewska et al., 2022) and pharmacological modulation of GABAergic activity (Thompson, 2024) in animal models have been shown to reverse, in part, stress‐induced deficits, offering promising directions for prevention and treatment. Moreover, preconception health interventions, including mental health care, stress reduction and metabolic optimization, might reduce the risk of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes in offspring (Genuis & Genuis, 2016; Kedzior et al., 2024). In conclusion, although direct human evidence linking PGS to offspring neurodevelopment is still being established, a growing body of epidemiological, genetic and neuroimaging studies, supported by translational animal research, underscores the crucial importance of parental health before conception.

This evidence converges on a model in which stress‐induced epigenetic programming and GABAergic dysfunction play central roles in shaping neurodevelopmental vulnerability, offering new opportunities for early identification and targeted prevention strategies. As shown in Table 2, PGS alters sperm epigenetic profiles and disrupts key pathways, such as the HPA axis and GABAergic signalling, leading to increased risk for NDDs, such as ASD and ADHD, in offspring. Evidence from both animal and human studies supports PGS as a transgenerational risk factor, emphasizing the need to clarify its mechanisms and clinical relevance. Despite these associations, it remains difficult to establish causality in human studies owing to potential confounding by shared genetics, socioeconomic factors and unmeasured environmental exposures. Most available data are correlational, and unravelling genetic from epigenetic or psychosocial contributions remains a major methodological challenge. Longitudinal cohort studies often rely on self‐reported parental mental health measures, which might not fully capture chronicity or timing of symptoms, particularly during the preconception period.

Parental ageing and PGS as convergent risk factors for ASD and ADHD

Advanced parental age represents a biologically relevant pregestational factor that might interact with PGS to shape offspring neurodevelopmental vulnerability. Epidemiological studies consistently show that advanced paternal age is associated with increased risk of ASD in offspring, with similar trends observed for maternal age when analysed in large population samples (Reichenberg et al., 2006). Meta‐analyses also indicate significant associations between greater parental age and offspring ASD risk (Dehesh et al., 2024). Although the relationship between parental age and ADHD is more complex, systematic reviews support an association between age extremes and increased ADHD risk (Min et al., 2021). The underlying biological mechanisms are likely to involve both genetic and epigenetic processes, including the age‐related accumulation of de novo mutations and epigenetic alterations in germ cells, which might influence early embryonic development and neural gene regulation (for details, see Fig. 4; de Kluiver et al., 2017). Emerging evidence further suggests that accelerated epigenetic ageing in parents might influence cognitive outcomes and ASD‐related traits in offspring (Song et al., 2022). These convergent lines of evidence support the notion that parental ageing might act as a modifier of stress‐induced epigenetic reprogramming and contribute to neurodevelopmental vulnerabilities in ASD and ADHD.

Future directions

Despite a growing body of evidence supporting the influence of PGS on offspring neurodevelopment, findings across studies are not fully consistent, underscoring the need for cautious interpretation. Although many animal and human studies report significant associations, others have observed null or context‐dependent effects. For example, short‐term preconception corticosterone exposure in male mice did not induce anxiety‐related phenotypes in offspring, highlighting the importance of stress duration, intensity and timing in intergenerational inheritance models (Fennell et al., 2020). Likewise, in human cohorts, moderate levels of preconception maternal stress have not been associated consistently with differences in offspring birth weight (Harris et al., 2021), and some studies report no significant effects of periconception or prenatal perceived stress on cord blood DNA methylation (Polinski et al., 2022), suggesting limitations of current biomarkers and the need for replication in larger, well‐characterized samples.

Future research should prioritize longitudinal human cohort studies with precise characterization of stress exposure timing and severity to delineate better the conditions in which PGS impacts offspring neurodevelopment. In parallel, comprehensive profiling of sperm and oocyte epigenetic modifications in stressed parents will be essential to clarify molecular mechanisms of intergenerational transmission. Additionally, assessment of early alterations in GABAergic gene expression and inhibitory circuit development in neonates might help to identify critical developmental windows and convergent neurobiological pathways affected by PGS. Finally, carefully designed clinical studies evaluating GABA‐modulating interventions, where appropriate, might offer insight into potential strategies for mitigating neurodevelopmental risk in vulnerable populations, although such approaches require rigorous validation.

Summary

PGS represents a critical yet historically underappreciated window of vulnerability in neurodevelopmental programming. Traditional research has focused primarily on prenatal and postnatal exposures, whereas accumulating evidence from animal models and emerging human studies indicates that parental experiences before conception can exert lasting influences on offspring brain development. These intergenerational effects are mediated largely through epigenetic reprogramming of germ cells, including sperm and oocytes, leading to altered embryonic gene expression without changes to the DNA sequence (Dvoran et al., 2022; Lee & Surani, 2024). Key mechanisms include DNA methylation, histone modifications and non‐coding RNAs, such as miRNAs (Bure et al., 2022), which collectively regulate neuronal differentiation, synaptogenesis and stress‐regulatory circuits (Gapp et al., 2014). Among neurodevelopmental pathways, the GABAergic system appears particularly vulnerable to PGS‐related perturbations. GABA plays a central role in early brain maturation by regulating the E/I balance, synaptic pruning and circuit formation during embryonic and early postnatal periods (Ben‐Ari, 2014; Topchiy et al., 2024). Disruption of GABAergic signalling is a hallmark of NDDs, including ASD and ADHD (Bagheri et al., 2020). Consistent with this, preclinical studies demonstrate that PGS‐induced germline epigenetic alterations impair offspring GABAergic function and give rise to anxiety‐like behaviour, social deficits, seizure susceptibility and cognitive inflexibility, which are phenotypes relevant to human NDDs (Crombie et al., 2021; Mahmoodkhani, Saboory, Roshan‐Milani, Azizi, Karimipour, Rasmi et al., 2018; Roshan‐Milani et al., 2021; Shang et al., 2021). Human neuroimaging studies further support this link, revealing reduced cortical GABA levels and altered inhibitory control in ASD and ADHD (Marotta et al., 2020). Although direct causal evidence in humans remains limited, epidemiological data increasingly associate parental stress, trauma, psychiatric illness and metabolic disorders prior to conception with elevated neurodevelopmental risk in offspring (Xu et al., 2014; Yehuda & Lehrner, 2018).

Conclusions and conceptual model

Building on this body of evidence, we propose a multilevel conceptual model in which PGS influences offspring neurodevelopment through the interaction of parental experiential factors, germline epigenetic programming and developmentally sensitive neural systems. Taken together, these findings support an integrated model, in which maternal and paternal pregestational stressors, despite distinct biological origins, converge on common neurodevelopmental targets, justifying their discussion within a unified conceptual framework. In this framework, chronic or early‐life stress experienced by either parent alters germ cell epigenetic signatures, including DNA methylation patterns, histone marks and small RNA cargo, during gametogenesis and post‐testicular sperm maturation. These modifications are transmitted to the embryo at fertilization, where they bias early gene‐expression trajectories that shape neural proliferation, migration and circuit assembly. Crucially, this model emphasizes that PGS effects are probabilistic rather than deterministic, interacting with postconception factors, such as maternal physiology, parental caregiving behaviours and broader socio‐environmental contexts. Within this cascade, disruption of E/I balance, particularly via altered GABAergic development, represents a convergent neurobiological pathway linking diverse parental stressors to neurodevelopmental disorder‐relevant phenotypes. The model therefore integrates biological, behavioural and contextual levels of influence, aligning with contemporary developmental and intergenerational frameworks. From a translational perspective, these findings support a paradigm shift that extends neurodevelopmental risk assessment and prevention into the preconception period. Incorporation of mental health screening, stress‐reduction strategies and metabolic health optimization for both prospective parents might offer new opportunities to enhance intergenerational resilience. At the same time, the emerging science of PGS raises important ethical and social considerations, particularly for populations disproportionately exposed to chronic stress owing to structural and socioeconomic inequities.

In conclusion, PGS represents a biologically plausible and clinically relevant contributor to neurodevelopmental risk. Continued mechanistic studies, especially those integrating germline epigenetics with circuit‐level neurodevelopment, are essential to refine this model and to inform evidence‐based interventions aimed at improving neurodevelopmental outcomes across generations.
