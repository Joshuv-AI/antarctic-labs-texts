Federated learning based fire detection method using local MobileNet
Authors: Sridhar Panneerselvam, Senthil Kumar Thangavel, Vidya Sagar Ponnam, Sudhakar Sengan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFire is a dangerous disaster that causes human, ecological, and financial ramifications. Forest fires have increased significantly in recent years due to natural and artificial climatic factors. Therefore, accurate and early prediction of fires is essential. While significant advancements have been made in traditional and Deep Learning (DL) methods for fire detection, challenges remain in accurately pinpointing and recognizing fire regions, especially in diverse and large environments, to prevent damage effectively. To address these challenges, this paper introduces a novel Federated Learning (FL)-based method called Indoor-Outdoor FireNet (IOFireNet) for detecting and localizing fire regions. The proposed method incorporates a Bilateral Filter (BF) to effectively preprocess fire images to reduce noise artifacts and enhance detection clarity. It employs Super Pixel-based Adaptive Clustering (SPAC) to precisely segment fire and non-fire regions. A global IOFireNet model is developed to aggregate parameters from local models, improving detection accuracy across varied environments, while MobileNet is used for efficient data processing, enabling predictions on fire spread, severity, and affected areas to support early warnings. The proposed FL-based IOFireNet attains an accuracy rate of 98.65% for fire detection and 97.14% of mean IoU for segmentation. The proposed SPAC model reaches a mean IoU of 4.06%, which is 2.45% better than the graph cut algorithm and CRF model. The proposed model achieves an accuracy of 0.23%, 4.20%, 3.29%, and 10.02%, better than VGG-19, ResNet-50, Inception, and Dense Net, respectively.Keywords: Fire detection, Federated Learning, Bilateral Filter, Superpixel Based Adaptive Clustering, IOFireNetSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Natural hazards, Energy science and technology

Introduction

In human history, fire has proven to be a valuable tool. However, fire accidents are a common disaster and a significant cause of fatal injuries and property damage. Fire often produces toxic gases, pollutes the environment, and harms the general economy and human lives1. A recent National Fire Protection Association study found that fire departments respond to over 350,000 home structure fires nationwide, resulting in almost $7 billion in direct losses2. Therefore, accurate and early fire prediction is essential. The existing approaches, like smoke and heat detection, cause false alarms, delays in detection, and sometimes missed detection problems, which are unsuitable for large spaces3. These problems make it even more challenging to receive prior detection warnings.

Fire accidents have more severe repercussions. Early detection of fire accidents enables us to minimize loss of life and property damage4. A manual inspection was initially used to detect fires, but its human expenditures and resources are high5,6. Therefore, the manual review was soon replaced by sensor and infrared technology and remote sensing satellite imagery for fire monitoring7. Although these sensors can be effective, they also have drawbacks. Vision-based Fire detection technology that identifies the fire area through an image processing mechanism has recently significantly minimized damage8. Several computer vision-based fire detection techniques have been suggested by researchers, but there are still several challenges to be solved, such as fluctuating illumination, low-resolution frames, flickering, and color changes in objects that mimic fire9. Therefore, the algorithm used in the detection part is the most essential factor determining an image fire detector’s performance10.

In the past decades, researchers have proposed various image-processing approaches for this purpose with Deep Learning (DL) and Machine Learning (ML) technology11.

Despite advancements in Fire Detection (FD) technologies, several limitations persist. Traditional methods, such as smoke and heat detection, often produce false alarms, delayed responses, and sometimes miss fires in large or open spaces due to the inherent limitations of these sensors. Additionally, while vision-based fire detection technologies have improved damage mitigation, they are challenged by fluctuating illumination, low-resolution imagery, and changes in object appearance that can mimic fire, leading to inaccuracies12. The reliance on manual inspection or fixed-sensor approaches is resource-intensive and lacks scalability, limiting early warning capabilities and effectiveness.

This study’s proposed method relies on transfer learning for detecting fires. Transfer learning is an effective technique for classification, prediction, and recognition13. Transfer learning is being developed and used in Fire Detection (FD) to improve FD.

The paper addresses several critical scientific and technological challenges in the domain of FD. One of the primary challenges is the accurate identification and localization of fire regions in diverse environments, including indoor and outdoor settings. Existing methods struggle with this due to fluctuating illumination, visual noise, and low-resolution imagery, which can cause False Positives (FP) and inaccurate segmentation14. Another challenge is the need for scalability and adaptability in fire detection systems. Traditional approaches, often reliant on fixed sensors or manual inspection, lack flexibility and efficiency when deployed across large areas or in varied geographical settings. Moreover, there is a technological gap in existing methods, as they typically focus only on detection rather than providing additional predictive insights, such as the spread and intensity of fires, which are crucial for proactive management15.

Existing FD techniques, including traditional smoke and heat sensors and advanced vision-based systems, face several limitations that hinder their effectiveness. Traditional methods are prone to false alarms, delayed detections, and missed fire events, particularly in expansive or open environments where sensor coverage is inadequate16. Vision-based FD methods using image processing have shown promise; however, they are susceptible to varying lighting conditions, low-resolution frames, and visual noise, such as objects that mimic fire (e.g., sunlight reflections or red-colored objects). These methods struggle with accurately segmenting and identifying fire regions, especially in diverse environments with fluctuating illumination17.

Furthermore, many of these techniques lack scalability and adaptability, as they frequently rely on fixed sensors or manual inspection, which are resource-intensive and inefficient for large-scale deployment. To address these issues, the proposed Indoor-Outdoor FireNet (IOFireNet) method leverages a Federated Learning (FL) model that integrates data from multiple sources, ensuring scalability and adaptability across varied environments (indoor, outdoor, and forested areas). Additionally, using a Bilateral Filter (BF) and Super Pixel-based Adaptive Clustering (SPAC) enhances image clarity and segmentation precision, overcoming challenges related to visual noise and fluctuating lighting18. The proactive capability to predict fire spread, intensity, and area further distinguishes the IOFireNet model, providing early warning systems that are accurate and efficient.

Related works

Researchers have recently proposed many DL and ML techniques for FD using temporal and color models. An overview of a few current methods for FD is presented in this section.

S. Saponara et al.19 presented the smart cities’ forest fire classification and detection using DL. The model uses the pre-trained convolutional basis of MobileNetV2 to develop a DL-based dubbed FireNet. The suggested technique achieves an accuracy of 98.42%, a 1.58% error rate, 97.42% precision, and 99.47% recall in classifying the fire and no-fire images.

Z. Xue et al.20 developed a Support Vector Machine (SVM) and a rough set theory-based FD. Forecasting accuracy is improved by optimizing the parameters (kernel) of the Rough Set and Support Vectors Machine (RS-SVM) and alleviating the deficiencies in overfitting. The SVM affects the overall classification performance, and optimizing those parameters can improve a classifier’s efficiency. The RS-SVM analyzed the smog feature in fire images with different kernel functions and achieved a 94.3% True Positive (TP) value for the polynomial kernel function.

U. Dampage et al.21 applied Attention U-Net and Squeeze Net to FD in the forest region. A Channel Shuffle operation modifies the traditional squeeze net by replacing the convolution layer for Feature Selection (FS) and applying this modified network as an encoder in Attention U-Net to classify the fire from forest images. This model attains an accuracy of 93% and a 0.89 execution time per image.

M. Mukhiddinov et al.22 developed an embedded system with a smart Internet of Things (IoT) device for smoke and FD using surveillance video indoors and outdoors. Neural network-based YOLOv2 is designed with smart IoT for FD. This model was tested on 200 images and achieved 93% FD accuracy, 94% sensitivity, and 80% specificity, which work better on surveillance video than R-CNN and Fast R-CNN.

A.B. Abdusalomov et al.23 suggested a model built on YOLOv5 for small-target FD detection on forest fires. Images should function as sensors in practical apps to find forest fires. Consequently, the YOLOv5-based Spatial Pyramid Pooling-Fast-Plus (SPPFP) module is utilized to attain global data on minor forest fires.

S.T. Seydi et al.24 suggested a system and methodology for early-stage forest FD using a radiocommunication sensor network. Furthermore, the conditions for sensor node architecture and node placement in harsh forest areas are appropriately evaluated to reduce damage and negative effects produced by wild animals and bad weather. The overall system requires an accuracy of 90%.

F. Saeed et al.25 proposed a smart glass and Deep Learning (DL)--based vision-based FD and notification system for Blind and Visually Impaired (BVI) persons. The method allows for detecting fires before they spread from inside structures. The suggested design enables image brightness, and a novel CNN employs a convolutional block attention module and an improved YOLOv4 model. The improved YOLOv4 model gives the 73.6% and 71.5% accuracy, respectively.

Radhakrishna Achanta et al.26 presented using a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)-based DL model to enhance forest FD. The Detectron2 model’s potential for woodland FD has still not been investigated. Following a different DL object detection method, an FD was initially used to spot forest fires. The next step is to use small images and eliminate low-quality pixel images to identify fire more accurately in different phases and situations.

Jie Chen et al.27 developed a DL-based Fire-Net to predict burning biomass and active fires; the model is trained on Landsat-8 images. For an exact representation, combine the thermal and optical modalities from the images. The system also used residual and separable convolution blocks, which enabled extracting more precise features from large datasets. The overall accuracy for the experiment was 97.35%.

Joshua Zhexue Huang et al.28 developed an adequate DL for FD. The proposed model comprises three primary Deep Neural Networks (DNN): an Adaboost-LBP, an Adaboost-Hybrid MLP neural network, and a CNN. An Adaboost-MLP model is employed to forecast the fire. After making a prediction, a proposed model and CNN use images of surveillance cameras to FD29.

Majid, Saima, et al.1 propose a framework called Cross-to-Merge Training (C2MT), which enhances model robustness by being insensitive to prior information during the sample selection process. C2MT utilizes a co-teaching strategy with cross-divided training data, where two networks are trained simultaneously and merged periodically using federated averages. This method also introduces the Median Balance Strategy (MBS) to evenly split training data into labeled and unlabeled subsets based on the projected loss distribution. This innovative balancing technique minimizes the impact of noise, improving the model’s generalization capabilities across different noise rates and types30.

Another significant contribution by31 is the BPT-PLR, designed to address two key issues in NLL: class imbalance and the optimization conflict between supervised and unsupervised learning. The BPT-PLR corporates a Two-Dimensional Gaussian mixture model (2D-GMM) for balanced partitioning, ensuring class balance in the training subsets. Additionally, it uses a Pseudo-Label Relaxed Contrastive Loss (PL-PLR) to reduce conflicts during optimization, leading to better performance in semi-supervised learning scenarios. This approach has shown optimal or near-optimal results on benchmark datasets such as CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, Animal-10 N, and Clothing1M, demonstrating its effectiveness in handling noisy labels.

Zhang, Qian, et al.32 also developed an improved joint optimization framework for noise correction called CMKL. This method combines Mix-up entropy and Kullback-Leibler entropy as a new loss function to fine-tune label annotations, providing better correction results. CMKL does not rely on prior information, such as a clean validation set, making it practical for real-world scenarios where ground-truth data is unavailable. Experiments on datasets like CIFAR-10 and Clothing1M show the superior performance of CMKL compared to other state-of-the-art methods.

Addressing the challenge of noisy labels in DNN training33, suggested the ECMB. Unlike traditional methods that require prior information like a noise transition matrix, ECMB is a fully end-to-end noise correction framework that applies an improved Mixup entropy and a new balance term for label updating. This technique ensures a balanced distribution of classes while correcting noisy labels, resulting in higher accuracy. The framework has been validated on several datasets, including CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and Clothing1M, where it outperforms existing approaches.

Zhang, Qian, et al.34 proposed a Fire Segmentation-Detection Framework (FSDF) to address the limitations in traditional and DL-FD by combining HSV and CLBP for flame Feature Extraction (FE) with YOLOv8 for segmentation and VQ-VAE for unsupervised detection. Evaluations using real-world fire datasets show FSDF’s superiority over baseline methods, with notable improvements in precision (19.5%), recall (1.2%), and F-score (11.7%). Field tests with a robot deployment validate its practical application, underscoring FSDF’s adaptability and effectiveness in diverse fire scenarios35.

Proposed methodology

This paper proposes the use of FL-based IOFireNet to predict and localize fires. The fire images were preprocessed using the BF to eliminate the noise artifacts. After preprocessing, the images are segmented using SPAC to segment the fire and non-fire regions. A global IOFireNet is designed for nearby locations, and a local MobileNet delivers the fire accident data. The proposed algorithm will predict the direction and spread of fire, its severity (or intensity), and its area to supplement early warning51. The global model is generated by aggregating the parameters of the local model; then, it is applied to local fire data to create a detection model. The schematic representation of the proposed model is shown in Fig. 1.

The schematic representation of the proposed model.

The process begins with preprocessing using a BF, a non-linear, edge-preserving noise reduction technique. This step is crucial for eliminating noise artifacts while maintaining the clarity of important image features, particularly edges, which are vital for accurately detecting fire regions. Following preprocessing, the segmentation of fire regions is performed using the SPAC. SPAC is precisely selected due to its ability to align atomic pixel regions with natural object boundaries effectively. This technique considers color and integrates contour, texture, and spatial features, making it more robust and adaptive than clustering methods, such as K-means or graph-cut algorithms. By employing SPAC, we enhance segmentation precision, ensuring that fire regions are accurately delineated even under varying environmental conditions and illumination.

After segmentation, the IOFireNet is applied to detect and localize fire regions. The decision to use IOFireNet, which integrates an FL, is based on its ability to aggregate and adapt local model parameters, thus improving detection accuracy across varied indoor and outdoor environments. Furthermore, combining global and local models in IOFireNet ensures that localized data (collected via MobileNet) can be processed efficiently, supporting the prediction of fire spread, severity, and area52. This approach optimizes performance, reducing computation time while increasing FD accuracy.

Bilateral filter

The BF is a non-linear, leveling, edge-preserving noise reduction filter. Herein, the fire images were pre-processed using the BF to eliminate the noise artifacts53. The Gaussian smoothing process assumes a weighted mean of neighboring pixel values. The weights are inversely correlated with the distance from the neighborhood’s centre. In addition to these longitudinal weights, the BF adds a tonal weight, causing pixels with values close to the center pixel to be weighted more strongly than pixels with values farther apart. IOFireNet

IOFireNet layers are responsible for transforming pixels into features that describe the input image’s content and transmitting these features. Thus, in this instance, IOFireNet is used as a feature generator for another neural network57. IOFireNet is a set of CNN that can be used for categorization and other usual tasks. They are small, low-latency, and low-power models. Due to their size, these are considered good DL for mobile device use. Input images for the IOFireNet design should be 224 × 224 with features scaled between [-1,1]. The loaded input image is reduced, and the center value of the [0, 255] pixel range is used to feature scale the image tensor values. The output is then divided by the offset value to scale between [− 1,1] after each pixel in the image has its offset value removed. For on-device and embedded applications, it offers the advantages of minimum computational power, time, space, and increased accuracy.

Depth-aware Separable Convolution serves as IOFireNet’s foundation. Using fewer factors than networks with the same depth makes the neural network lighter. In the conventional convolution procedure, a point-first convolution follows a depth-first convolution, and the two collections are referred to as a depth-wise separable convolution. Three convolutional layers form its function. A 1 × 1 pointwise convolution layer follows only after the depth-wise convolution layer to filter the data. Pointwise convolution, a projection layer, will turn a tensor with many channels into one with fewer channels. Before advancing to depth-wise convolution, the first layer performs a 1 × 1 convolution to determine the number of channels in the data. The architecture diagram of IOFireNet is depicted in Fig. 3.

Conclusion and future work

This paper proposes a novel Federated Learning (FL)-based Indoor-Outdoor Fire Net (IOFireNet) method to detect and localize the fire region. The fire images were pre-processed using the BF to eliminate the noise artifacts. After preprocessing, the images are segmented using Super Pixel-based Adaptive Clustering (SPAC) to segment the fire and non-fire regions. A global IOFireNet is designed for nearby locations, and a local MobileNet delivers the fire accident data. The proposed algorithm will be capable of predicting the direction and spread of fire, its severity (or intensity), and its area to supplement early warning. The global model is generated by aggregating the parameters of the local model; then, it is applied to local fire data to generate a Fire Detection (FD) model. The proposed FL-based IOFireNet method achieves an overall accuracy of 98.65% for FD and 97.14% of the mean IoU for segmentation. The FL-based IOFireNet method shows better Accuracy, Precision, F1-score, and Sensitivity simulation results than existing techniques. The limitation of the proposed IOFireNet model is the lack of FD in terrain conditions. This limitation will be considered as future work for extending our proposed system.

The proposed model, IOFireNet, is an improvement compared to known architectures, including ResNet, VGG16, Inception, and Xception. Unlike these models, which are now less advanced, IOFireNet is designed to work efficiently on any system’s available computational resources, memory, and processing time, thus ideal for systems with limited resources. Moreover, IOFireNet provides better accuracy than these traditional models, which confirms the proposed model’s reliability in solving complex problems with low operating expenses. These advantages make IOFireNet suitable for application areas where defining the workflow and performance accuracy are critical.
