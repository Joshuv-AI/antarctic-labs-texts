Reproductive status effects on intraocular pressure in Mares
Authors: Sara Binesh, Hamid Ghasemzadeh-nava, Afshin Raoofi, Seyed Mehdi Rajaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The reproductive status affects the intraocular pressure (IOP) in mares.•Progesterone is likely the primary factor contributing to the IOP fluctuations, with higher serum progesterone levels associated with higher IOP and vice versa.•There is species-specific differences in how progesterone regulates IOP.Keywords: IOP, Mares, Progesterone, Reproductive status, Tonovet

Introduction

In the normal eye, intraocular pressure (IOP) is generated by the continuous flow of aqueous humor (AH) against resistance. This dynamic process is necessary to maintain the appropriate shape and optical properties of the globe. AH, a clear, colorless liquid, fills the anterior and posterior chambers as well as the pupil. As AH is formed by the ciliary body processes, it enters the posterior chamber and flows through the pupil into the anterior chamber, where it leaves the eye through the corneoscleral trabecular and uveoscleral outflow pathways. This continuous flow of AH supplies the avascular cornea and lens with nutrients and also removes their waste products (Gellat, 2021). The rate of AH formation equals the outflow, so the IOP is maintained relatively constant (Maggs et al., 2018). Considering that the orbital globe can be seen as a spherical rigid container, the pressure within the eye is influenced by both external forces and fluctuations in the volume of its internal constituents. Notably, the forces acting within a normal eye are primarily dictated by the dynamics of the aqueous humor. Although the vitreous body—a clear gelatinous mass situated between the retina and the lens—fills a significant portion of the eye's posterior segment, it possesses a relatively fixed volume and is not typically involved in the regulation of IOP (Machiele et al., 2024).

The production and drainage of AH are influenced not only by the anatomy of the anterior segment but also by a large number of endogenous compounds, including neurotransmitters, hormones, prostaglandins, proteins, lipids, and proteoglycans (Maggs et al., 2018). It is worth pointing out that there are various anatomic and physiologic factors that can lead to normal fluctuations in intraocular pressure (IOP), which may not indicate any underlying pathology (Ofri et al., 2002). Understanding the difference between physiological and pathological variations in IOP is crucial for accurately diagnosing and monitoring ocular diseases, such as glaucoma (in which IOP is typically raised) and uveitis (in which IOP is typically reduced).

Reproductive status is one of the physiological factors that may cause non-pathological changes in IOP. While many studies have been conducted in humans to explore the relationship between reproductive status and IOP, only a few have been done in animals such as lionesses and domestic cats (Ofri et al., 1999, 2002). The results of these studies suggest that reproductive status significantly affects IOP in these animals. Research on lionesses indicates that the luteal group demonstrates significantly higher IOP than the non-luteal. It is believed that the higher levels of progesterone during the luteal phase lead to increased resistance to aqueous humor outflow, subsequently resulting in elevated IOP (Ofri et al., 1999). Similar effects have been observed in domestic cats (Ofri et al., 2002).

This information is valuable for clinicians interpreting ophthalmic examination results, particularly when measuring IOP through tonometry, which is a routine diagnostic procedure in horses. It is important to emphasize that, at present, there is no established relationship between the reproductive status of horses and their IOP. However, should a correlation between the IOP of mares and their serum progesterone (SP) levels be established, this finding could enhance clinicians' ability to diagnose and differentiate between pathological and physiological ocular hypertension in equine species. Such insights may provide a valuable tool in the clinical assessment of ocular health in these animals. Consequently, our research team undertook a study to examine the potential association between IOP and SP levels in mares.

Material and methods

Animals

The study recruited seven mares with clinically healthy eyes. These mares were research horses and belonged to the College of Veterinary University.

Data collection

Measurements periods

IOP and SP levels were measured at different stages of the estrus cycle in mares: estrous (characterized by low levels of progesterone), diestrus (characterized by high levels of progesterone) during the breeding season, and anestrus (characterized by low levels of progesterone, follicles <20 mm in size, and no CL in ovaries) during the non-breeding season.

In other terms, the study considered four key time points for the mares - T1 (2 days before ovulation, known as estrus phase), T2 (the 2nd day of the estrous cycle, known as early diestrus phase), T3 (the 11th day of the estrous cycle, known as late diestrus phase), and T4 (during the anestrous season).

In breeding season, correct recognition of estrus (heat) in non-pregnant mares was done through daily teasing by bringing her close to a stallion to assess her receptiveness to the teaser stallion. As a mare in estrus, she would exhibit certain signs including opening and closing (“winking”) her vulva, tucking her hips (“posturing”), urinating, raising her tail, and interest in the stallion.

Predicting ovulation in mares can be challenging partly due to the variability associated with the estrus length. Hence, to make a fairly accurate prediction of when the mare will ovulate, daily transrectal ultrasonography was performed in each mare to track changes in the size and appearance of follicles since the follicle size is the most common and simplest criterion for estimating the time of ovulation (Samper et al., 2007). The uterine edema was also assayed. Reviewing the mare's previous breeding records and understanding the timing of her last ovulation also helped us estimate the follicular diameter at which the mare typically ovulates, as well as assess the stage of her reproductive cycle. Maximal follicle size was often reached 1 to 2 days prior to ovulation. Changes in follicle shape and tone (losing its spherical shape), increase in the thickness of the follicle wall, follicle wall echogenicity (mean echogenicity scores of the granulosa layer and prominence of the anechoic layer progressively increased and reached maximal values 24 h before ovulation), endometrial echotexture (from relatively homogenous during diestrus to relatively heterogeneous during estrus), and sensitivity of the mare to ovarian palpation were additional diagnostic aids that helped us to estimate ovulation to within hours more accurately. When two consecutive 24-hour ultrasounds showed that the size of the follicle has stopped increasing, this information also helped predict when ovulation will occur, as the rate of growth of the preovulatory follicle slows 24 h before ovulation.

Ovulations were recognized by palpation per rectum and ultrasonography together as a rapid decrease in follicular size, the absence of a previous large follicle, and/or the presence of a collapsed structure (Noakes et al., 2019).

IOP measurements and blood samples were obtained around about two days before ovulation (during estrus), about two days after ovulation (early diestrus), and about eleven days after ovulation (mid-diestrus). In order to minimize the potential impacts of diurnal variation, all samples were meticulously collected between 9:00 AM and 11:00 AM. During the midwinter months, reproductive hibernation or anestrous was detected due to the absence of positive signs of estrus during teasing, and by performing transrectal ultrasonography to assess the size of follicles and/or the absence of corpus luteum (CL) in both ovaries (Noakes et al., 2019). To determine whether the mares were in shallow anestrus or deep anestrus, weekly ultrasonography was conducted over the four weeks leading up to sampling to determine whether the mares were in shallow anestrus or deep anestrus. This process evaluated the absence of a reproductive cycle by specifically checking for the lack of a CL and the absence of follicles exceeding 20 mm. Since the study aimed to collect samples from mares in deep anestrus, any mare that had a CL or follicles greater than 20 mm was disqualified from the experiment, as this indicated that she was cyclic or in shallow anestrus. Deep anestrus refers to a period during which a mare does not exhibit any signs of estrus (heat) and is not cycling. It is characterized by a lack of ovarian activity and occurs during winter. During deep anestrus, the mare’s reproductive hormones are low. Mares in anestrus season may attain follicular sizes greater than 10 mm, but usually <20 (Squires et al., 2011).

Measurements and samples were obtained in anestrus. Ten days after the initial sampling, a follow-up transrectal ultrasonography was conducted to verify the absence of CL in both ovaries and assess the follicle diameters, ensuring that the mares had been in deep anestrus during the initial sampling period.

Steps of performing the experiment

After transferring each mare to the horse crushes in the examination room, a certain amount of time was taken to ensure that the mare was in a calm and relaxed state. Examination crush was utilized for easy and consistent access to the mare while maintaining a consistent neutral head position (above the heart level and without lateral deviation) in order to minimize artificial elevations of the IOP.

Tonometry

Given that the research horses were already accustomed to the environments and protocols employed in equine research, along with their prior exposure to testing conditions—including handling techniques and methods for measuring intraocular IOP (tonometry)—an acclimation period was deemed unnecessary for this group. Their extensive prior experiences indicate that these horses are likely to undergo a process of physiological adaptation, and they were used to it, which can lead to more stable baseline measurements and a diminished likelihood of the initial IOP elevation that is typically observed in naive subjects.

In order to prevent any inaccuracies in measuring IOP, tonometry was conducted before blood sample collection and transrectal ultrasound. To ensure consistency, a trained clinician used a rebound tonometer (Tono-Vet® tonometer, iCare), which was set to the equine calibration mode during all measurements, to take three consecutive readings of each mare's eyes. The iCare TonoVet is designed to be used without applying topical anesthetics. The measurements are painless, to the extent that patients usually do not even notice them, and it does not cause even a corneal reflex; hence, neither topical ocular anesthetics, sedatives, nor auriculopalpebral nerve block was necessary for IOP assessment using this tonometer. The same clinician did all the readings. The testing order of each eye was determined randomly to enhance the reliability and validity of the study’s findings.

To ensure accurate IOP measurements, the clinician maintained a neutral head position of the mares while using her non-dominant hand to gently keep the mares’ eyes open to avoid applying any external pressure on the eyes, thereby minimizing any artificial elevations of the IOP readings.

Transrectal ultrasonography

A rectal palpation and reproductive tract ultrasonography were conducted after tonometry to ascertain the presence of follicles or CL, determining the stage of the estrous cycle. The procedure used a hand-carried diagnostic ultrasound system (EMP V9) with a rectal linear probe.

The presence, number, and size of follicles on the ovaries helped to determine whether a mare was in estrus (heat) or anestrus (inactive). Additionally, the position and tone of the cervix indicated if the mare was in estrus (when the cervix was relaxed and open) or diestrus (when the cervix was tight and closed). The presence of a CL on the ovary suggested that the mare had ovulated recently, which was associated with the diestrus phase. During the anestrus season, anovulatory mares identified by the presence of follicles with a diameter of <20 mm, along with an absence of a CL over a 4-week period.

Blood sample collection and progesterone analysis

The last step of the procedure involved collecting blood samples. Five milliliters of blood were drawn from the jugular vein in the neck region. Next, the blood was transferred into the serum separator tube (SST) and subjected to centrifugation for 5 min at 3500 rpm to isolate the blood serum. Finally, the immunofluorescence analyzer was used to determine the concentration of progesterone hormone in the serum.

Statistical analysis

Repeated measure of ANOVA was used to compare the mean of obtained data. Pearson correlation was performed to check for any correlation between IOP and SP in both groups.

Discussion

The current study serves as a investigation, providing initial insights into the potential linkage between SP levels and IOP in mares. The study highlights significant time-dependent variations in SP levels and IOP measurements in mares, with a notable correlation between hormonal changes and intraocular pressure. SP levels and IOP measurements (both OD and OS) underwent changes across different time points in mares, with higher SP levels associated with higher IOP, and vice versa. Analysis of the results of this study suggests a statistically significant association between SP levels and IOP measurements in both right and left eyes, remarkably stronger in the case of left eye (correlation coefficient OS≈ 0.609, correlation coefficient OD≈ 0.467). These findings suggest that progesterone may be the principal steroid hormone associated with ocular hypertensive effects in mares. While these findings indicate a possible physiological linkage, further research with larger sample sizes is essential to validate these preliminary findings and elucidate the physiologic factors involved in IOP regulation and the mechanisms responsible for differences in IOP during the reproductive cycle of mares in greater detail.

While there might be several potential biological and methodological explanations for the stronger association between SP and IOP in the left eye compared to the right, it is essential to recognize that these findings should be interpreted with caution due to limitations in sample size. There are various potential biological and methodological explanations that might contribute to a stronger association between SP and IOP in the left eye as opposed to the right. These could include possible asymmetries in ocular anatomy and vascular supply, differences in progesterone receptor distribution or sensitivity between the two eyes, and external factors such as the circumstances under which measurements were taken—like the mares' stress levels or their handling during the measurement process. However, in this study, we did attempt to control for a range of external factors, which may suggest that these external influences were less likely to be responsible for the observed differences in associations. The mares involved were research horses accustomed to the environments and protocols typically used in equine research. Also, regarding handling the mares during measurement, we utilized an examination crush, which provided easy access while helping maintain a consistent head position (above heart level and without lateral deviation). Additionally, the order in which each eye was tested was determined randomly, which could enhance the reliability and validity of the study's findings. The main reason why the association between SP and IOP is stronger in the left eye than in the right eye might be the small sample size recruited for the study. To validate these observations and better understand the underlying mechanisms, further research with larger sample sizes is needed.

According to a study on rabbits (Green et al., 1989), regarding the possible physiological mechanism of progesterone increasing IOP, it was suggested that progesterone causes an increase in the synthesis of chondroitin sulfate in the iridocorneal angle, leading to an accumulation of excess glycosaminoglycan in the iridocorneal angle. This buildup increases resistance to aqueous outflow, ultimately resulting in ocular hypertension and an increase in IOP. Research has shown that the administration of exogenous topical progesterone in rabbits causes an elevation in IOP (Knepper et al., 1985). Administration of progesterone in rabbits leads to an increase in the synthesis of chondroitin sulfate, a glycosaminoglycan found in the iridocorneal angle of most species (Knepper et al., 1985). It is suggested that the excess amounts of chondroitin sulfate which accumulate in the iridocorneal angle increase the resistance to aqueous outflow, thus leading to elevation of IOP (Knepper et al., 1985). Furthermore, this hypertensive effect of progesterone in rabbits has been blocked by mifepristone, a progesterone antagonist administered topically (Green et al., 1989) or subconjunctivally (Tsukahara et al., 1986). Administration of mifepristone without prior progesterone treatment causes ocular hypotension in rabbits, thus implying that endogenous progesterone also increases IOP in this species (Green et al., 1989; Tsukahara et al., 1986).

The same potential ocular hypertensive effect observed in mares and rabbits is also suspected in lions and cats. In lions, research has shown that lionesses with luteal tissue have a significantly higher IOP than those without one in their ovaries. It is suggested that elevated progesterone levels associated with the luteal phase in lionesses cause increased resistance to aqueous humor outflow, leading to IOP elevation. Luteal females have been found to have a 10-fold increase in progesterone concentrations, which is associated with a notable increase in IOP (Ofri et al., 2002). Similarly, pregnant cats with high progesterone concentrations were found to have significantly higher IOP than those with low concentrations, suggesting that progesterone may play a role in IOP regulation in this species. However, hormonal regulation of IOP is probably complex and involves multiple factors beyond progesterone concentration. This is evidenced by the lack of significant differences in IOP between pregnant cats and non-pregnant females in estrus, despite a significant difference in progesterone levels (Ofri et al., 2002). Similarly, there were no significant differences in IOP between males and all pregnant cats, despite a notable difference in progesterone concentrations (Ofri et al., 2002). Additionally, even though progesterone levels were highly similar, cats in estrus had significantly higher IOP compared to females not in estrus (Ofri et al., 2002).

Extensive research has been conducted on the link between reproductive status and IOP in humans. Investigators have explored changes in IOP during the menstrual cycle (Feldman et al., 1978; Gharagozloo & Brubaker, 1991), pregnancy (Green et al., 1988; Qureshi et al., 2000; Ziai et al., 1994), and menopause (Qureshi, 1996). Generally, IOP in women fluctuates during the menstrual cycle (Feldman et al., 1978; Gharagozloo & Brubaker, 1991), decreases during pregnancy (Green et al., 1988; Ziai et al., 1994), (Qureshi et al., 2000), and increases during menopause (Qureshi, 1996). These variations in IOP raise intriguing questions regarding the physiological mechanisms that regulate IOP. Hormonal fluctuations are believed to be the primary cause of IOP changes in women. Several investigators attribute these changes in IOP to progesterone, hypothesizing that excess progesterone during pregnancy blocks the ocular hypertensive effect of endogenous corticosteroids and increases aqueous outflow, resulting in a reduction in IOP (Bertolucci et al., 2009; Kass & Sears, 1977; Ziai et al., 1994). Other investigators have failed to find a correlation between changes in IOP and hormone concentrations during the menstrual cycle (Feldman et al., 1978; Gharagozloo & Brubaker, 1991). The role of other reproductive hormones, including testosterone and estrogen, in IOP regulation is more controversial, and conflicting results are frequently reported (Kass & Sears, 1977). Research indicated that estrogen does not have an effect on IOP during the menstrual cycle in women (Feldman et al., 1978) or during the luteal phase in lionesses (Ofri et al., 2002). This may be attributed to fact that unlike progesterone, estrogen has negligible mineralocorticoid activity (Ofri et al., 2002).

The differential relationship between SP levels and IOP in humans versus animals seems to be attributed to species-specific physiological responses, as progesterone in humans increases aqueous outflow whereas in other species it increases resistance to aqueous outflow. There may also be genetic differences in the expression of progesterone receptors or downstream signaling pathways related to IOP regulation between species resulting in opposing effects of progesterone on IOP.

Conclusions

IOP in mares appears to be influenced by their reproductive status, suggesting a link between SP levels and IOP, with progesterone as a likely contributor to ocular hypertension. Subsequent research with a larger sample size will be essential to validate these findings. Additionally, there seem to be species-specific differences in how progesterone regulates IOP. While this hormone may induce ocular hypertension in rabbits, lions, and mares, it seems to lead to ocular hypotension in humans.
