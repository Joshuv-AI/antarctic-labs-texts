Epidemiology of pediatric ocular injuries in Poland: cross-sectional study
Authors: Ewa Toruńska, Piotr Engelgardt, Maria Szwajkowska, Maciej Krzyżanowski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOcular trauma in children results in long-term visual impairments and affects the child’s physical and psychosocial development. The variability in age, gender, and socio-environmental factors significantly determines their epidemiology. Consequently, this study aimed to characterize ocular injuries in children in Poland.MethodsThis retrospective, observational study included patients up to 18 years old, admitted to the Emergency Department of the Regional Specialized Children’s Hospital in Olsztyn, Poland, between June 2015 and December 2023, with diagnosed eye or orbital injury. Cases were categorized based on injury mechanism, tool utilized, and perpetrator. The decrease in visual acuity was divided according to ICD-10 – WHO.ResultsAmong 590 patients, 66.61% were males, mostly aged 5 to 9 years (32.37%). An active mechanism was responsible for most cases (77.97%), primarily involving the use of objects other than blunt, with a significant proportion of injuries being self-inflicted. Animal-inflicted injuries were most often observed among females. Males most frequently necessitated surgical procedures through active mechanisms. A decrease in visual acuity was observed in 121 cases (20.51%), with blindness diagnosed in 11 patients, mostly males, resulting from an active mechanism. Six of them did not fully recover their vision. Nineteen patients (3.22%) sustained open globe injuries, and two cases (0.34%) of brain injuries were documented.ConclusionsEye injuries constitute a diverse category of trauma, often constrained only by the child’s creativity and age. These injuries predominantly occur in young males through active mechanisms. While the majority do not result in permanent vision impairment, they frequently cause a temporary reduction in visual acuity.

Introduction

Despite the decrease in the frequency of eye injuries in children observed in studies, the number of cases recorded in 2014 in the United States was 2221 per million pediatric patients [1–3]. Moreover, eye injuries are the main cause of monocular blindness and long-term vision disorders, which occur in up to about 12–14% of children affected by injury, which greatly impacts the child’s further physical and psychosocial development [2, 4, 5]. In turn, Australian data indicate that the number of children affected by eye injuries is, on average, 2.5 per 100.000 patients [6]. On the other hand, in Suriname, out of 85.329 school-age children, eye injuries occurred in 538 cases [7]. Available studies show that the situation in Poland is slightly different. It is estimated that per 1 million inhabitants, only a few children suffer eye injuries every day, and about 2 of them report to the emergency room [8]. According to other sources, eye injuries in Poland constitute 3.5% of all hospitalizations in pediatric ophthalmology departments [9]. According to global studies, injuries occur most often in boys aged 5–9 years [10–12], although some publications indicate a more frequent occurrence of injuries in younger children up to 4 years old [2, 5]. On the other hand, the results that overlap in the literature indicate different mechanisms of injuries that characterize a given age group [3, 13]. The very diversity of the anatomical structure of the skull, depending on the child’s age, affects the differences in injuries in different age groups. Reduced mineralization of the facial skeleton, the presence of fat pads, and lower pneumatization of the sinuses allow for the absorption of the energy of the injury and result in a smaller number of facial fractures than in the adult population [14]. Moreover, the different ratio of the size of the neurocranium to the viscerocranium means that orbital fractures are observed more often in children younger than 5 years old than in older children [15]. Among the youngest children, injuries caused by falls, hitting with toys, or chemical burns predominate, which is related to carelessness and poorly developed motor skills. In teenagers, injuries most often occur while playing sports or as a result of peer violence [3, 12]. Although the gender and age of children themselves make them an extremely heterogeneous group in terms of eye injuries, social and environmental conditions play an important role in epidemiology. Most injuries occur in the spring and summer months [16, 17] while different injury mechanisms are noted in different parts of the world, which may result from both geographical and cultural conditions [13, 18].

In a study describing the evolution of eye protection measures, attention was drawn to the growing number of new technologies implemented along with the progress of evolution and medicine [19]. New laws and safety regulations coming into force allow for a reduction in the number of injuries in some areas of life [4]. Although, according to the literature, up to 90% of eye injuries can be prevented [4, 13], many of them are related to the natural development of a child who, along with acquiring new skills, becomes curious about the world, but is still unaware of the risks and potential consequences of their actions [18, 20]. Hence, these injuries seem to be inevitable.

To improve the development of further strategies for early recognition and effective treatment of these injuries, it is advisable to determine epidemiological trends, frequency of occurrence, causes, and effects of such injuries, taking into account geographical conditions.

Therefore, this paper aims to comprehensively characterize eye injuries in children in the years 2015–2023 in the Polish population, which is the region of Central Europe, due to the lack of such studies in the literature.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection

A study was conducted, including a retrospective review of medical records from the Department of Head and Neck Surgery in the field of Ophthalmology of the Regional Specialized Children’s Hospital in Olsztyn, Poland. The analysis included patients up to 18 years of age, admitted to the Emergency Department (ED) between June 2015 and December 2023, with diagnosed eye or orbital injury (S05.0-S05.9; S02.0-S02.9; T26.0-T26.9 according to ICD-10). The analysis began with the introduction of electronic medical records but was completed in 2023 to avoid the analysis of cases whose treatment was still ongoing after the data analysis was completed. All data were collected in an Excel spreadsheet. From the information available in electronic medical records, data on the following patient characteristics were extracted: year and month of admission, age, gender, mechanism of injury, consultations performed, necessity and duration of hospitalization, right/left eye, symptoms, necessity of surgical treatment, decreased visual acuity, type of eye injury, presence of a foreign body, damage to the posterior segment of the eyeball, skull fracture, brain injury. The calendar year was divided into periods, including warm months: April-September, and cold months: October-March. Patients were grouped into five categories depending on age: < 1 year; 1–4 years; 5–9 years; 10–14 years; 15–18 years. These groups were established based on other publications on eye injuries in children [1, 2]. In the comparison of the number of cases by season/month, data from 2015 were excluded because electronic medical records were introduced in June 2015. Visual acuity in the youngest children (preverbal age) was assessed by the ability to fixate on a toy and follow it. The preferential gaze test (PLT Lea) was impossible for most of these children. In the emergency room, the assessment of visual fixation was considered sufficient. In older children (anxious or preverbal), the LEA blocks were used – a 3-meter test. In children who were capable of verbal communication, the visual acuity of the subjects was assessed using Snellen boards, which contained pictures, numbers, and letters. In Poland, the results are reported using the decimal system. The decrease in visual acuity was divided according to ICD-10 – WHO into categories: 0 (mild or no visual impairment; ≥0.3), 1 (moderate visual impairment; ≥0.1), 2 (severe visual impairment; ≥0.05), 3 (blindness; ≥0.02), 4 (blindness; light perception), 5 (blindness; no light perception). Based on information obtained from the interview, a detailed division of the mechanisms of injury was made into five main groups: active, passive, caused by animals, burns, and traffic accidents. The above groups were further divided according to the type of tool or type of burn. Injuries were also divided depending on the perpetrator: into self-inflicted injury, caused by another person, or caused by animals. In the next step, eye injuries were divided into open and closed globe injuries according to The Birmingham Eye Trauma Terminology (BETT), including contusion and superficial damage [21]. In addition, the type of consultations and the number of hospitalizations were analyzed. The symptoms presented by the patients were distinguished as follows: bruising, skin wound, decreased vision, visual field defect, and diplopia. Information on surgical suturing of the wound concerns both skin and conjunctival wounds. The above divisions were also grouped according to the patient’s gender. The skin and conjunctiva were sutured using 6 − 0 absorbable sutures (Novosyn, Novosyn Quick). Children admitted to the ward were sutured under general anesthesia, while those treated in the emergency department received local anesthesia. Open globe injuries involving the sclera and cornea were treated in the operating room under general anesthesia after admission. Corneal wounds were sutured with 10 − 0 non-absorbable sutures, while scleral wounds were closed with 8 − 0 non-absorbable sutures. In cases of corneal injury, a dressing contact lens is placed after surgery. Corneal sutures are typically removed four months later under general anesthesia in the operating room. The conservative treatment plan involved the use of topical and systemic antibiotics, corticosteroids, and pupil-dilating agents. Tropicamide 1% was typically used, though 1% atropine was administered as needed. Intraocular pressure-lowering drugs were administered as needed. Bed rest and topical antibiotics were used for closed eye injuries. In cases where the cornea was not damaged, treatment involved using topical corticosteroids and tropicamide. If necessary, we used intraocular pressure-lowering agents such as intravenous mannitol. Tranexamic acid (Exacyl) was administered orally for major hyphemas (hemorrhages in the anterior chamber).

Data analysis

Data analysis, calculations, and graphs were performed in an Excel spreadsheet and the statistical package R 4.4.1. Descriptive statistics used mean, median, standard deviation (SD), and interquartile range (IQR). p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Adjusted Standardized Residuals (ASR) of the contingency tables were analyzed, and the value (ASR >|1.96|) was considered significant.

Ethics

The authors declare that the study complies with the ethical guidelines and applicable local law. The study was designed as a retrospective. Therefore, consent to participate was not required. This study was approved by the local Bioethics Committee of the University of Warmia and Mazury in Olsztyn – Decision No. 39/2024 – on December 5, 2024. The present study was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki.

Mechanism of eye injury in relation to gender and age group

Of the five major categories of injury mechanisms, the majority of cases were due to an active mechanism, mainly as a result of the use of blunt objects. Slightly fewer, 21.52% of active injuries were the result of a direct blow, in more than half of the cases with a finger. In 10 cases, there was an assault, while in 19 patients, an eye injury was described as a result of an upper limb impact. There were 32 (6.96%) injuries in the active mechanism, that occurred during sports activities. In 21 cases, while playing football, in 6 cases, while riding a bike, in 4 cases, during martial arts training, and in one case, while playing rugby. In 78 (16.96%) cases, the active injury was the result of a foreign body, while the most common, 50 cases, were merely the suspected presence of a foreign body, as determined by an interview with the patient and caregiver. Injuries in the passive mechanism most often occur as a result of a fall onto a blunt object. The only mechanism of injury in which a higher representation of girls was observed was injury caused by animals, most often by a cat, although injuries caused by dogs, a horse, a rooster, and a tick were also observed. Of all 24 (4.07%) burn cases, 17 were chemical burns, most often caused by glues (5 cases) and liquid laundry packets (4 cases). Of the seven thermal burns, four were caused by cigarette embers.

Discussion

Geographic and cultural diversity means that in many places, doctors encounter different mechanisms of eye injuries, which often require an individual approach to the patient. In numerous publications discussing this topic, we can find different results, often conditioned by environmental factors [3, 6, 7, 17, 18, 22, 23]. This means that the conclusions drawn from these papers, which often form the basis of specific therapeutic approaches, are not applicable to all patient groups.

Although Polish data indicate that the majority of admissions to pediatric ophthalmological emergency departments are for non-accidental reasons, injuries still constitute a significant percentage of all hospitalizations encountered by clinicians [24]. The vast majority of publications report that injuries occur most often in males [3, 6, 11, 17, 25], which was also confirmed in this study. Although we observed that the largest is the 5–9 age group, which is consistent with the results of other studies [11, 26], several publications indicated that eye injuries happen the most often in patients under 4–5 years of age [2, 3, 5]. Analysis of the results over 9 years showed that the fewest reports were recorded in 2020, which was most likely related to the COVID-19 pandemic. As a result of school closures and the implementation of restrictions, children’s activity decreased, which could have resulted in a smaller number of injuries. Hanson et al. observed an interesting phenomenon, in the first months of the pandemic, the number of all admissions to pediatric emergency departments decreased, while trauma-related admissions increased by 38% [27]. However, in our study, no such relationship was observed. Nevertheless, we observed the rising number of ocular injuries in 2022, which might result from the reduction of restrictions and lifting a state of the of the epidemic in Poland due to SARS-CoV-2 virus infections. Moreover, similarly to other authors, we observed the highest number of injuries reported in the spring and summer months, which is related to the holiday break, greater activity of children, and less parental control [3, 5, 17, 28].

According to previous Polish publications, the most common causes of eye injury are foreign bodies, blunt impacts, and superficial corneal irritation [8]. In our study, we obtained slightly different results. In all age categories, active injuries predominated. In boys up to 1 year of age, these injuries were mainly caused by a blunt object. In children up to 15 years of age, we observed mainly injuries using objects other than blunt. Interestingly, injuries caused by foreign bodies were most common in the oldest age group – they constituted 36% of all active injuries in girls and 38.98% in boys. In many of the available publications, the authors mention sticking a branch as a common mechanism of eye injury, accounting for up to 16% of injuries in developing countries [7, 23]. Our analysis also allowed us to conclude that eye injuries caused by a specific object were most frequently (20.26%) caused by branches, followed by toys.

What is important, various epidemiological studies highlight the mechanism of injury, which often depends on the geographic region and socio-cultural conditions. In developing countries, injuries caused by sharp objects such as knives are more common [29]. Additionally, other types of instruments responsible for most ocular injuries, like stones or catapult injuries, are not seen or are rarely observed in other regions [30]. In our study, such injuries were a minority compared to those caused by blunt objects. Furthermore, self-inflicted injuries, often caused by sharp objects including knives, wood, and glass, predispose patients to serious clinical manifestations like iris prolapse, traumatic cataract, limbus laceration, or endophthalmitis [31], which was not observed in our study.

A relatively common mechanism of injury in our analysis is an accidental finger struck. Among our patients, 58 such cases were observed. Slightly fewer, 19 patients were hit with the upper limb, and 10 were assaulted. The same mechanism of injury was noted in Suriname, where a finger struck the eye was one of the most common mechanisms [7]. Similarly, in Japan, injuries were most often caused by a direct impact to the eye (34%) and, in most cases, by a part of the body (20%). These authors also noted a significant number of injuries related to sports - they accounted for as much as 60% of injuries in children aged 12–15 [18]. In our study, however, only 5.42% of injuries related to physical activity were observed - mainly in children aged 10–14. Many researchers draw attention to the possibility of preventing these injuries by using protective measures, which is now almost standard, especially in professional sports. However, during regular physical activity, at school, or in the yard, children often do not use any protection, which increases the risk of injury [2]. The results we obtained above are also significantly lower than the results observed in Australia (13%) [6]. Interestingly, an American study observed an increasing number of sports injuries over the years 2006–2014, which on average constituted 14.2%. On the other hand, these authors noted a decrease in injuries caused by burns, weapons, or traffic accidents, which generally allowed them to conclude that the total number of hospitalizations of children due to eye injuries has been reduced by 26.1% in recent years [2]. A decrease in the total number of eye injuries in children was also observed by Patel et al. Interestingly, in their publication, most injuries were related to playing sports (28%), followed by those caused by chemicals (16%). These authors also emphasized that injuries caused by detergents were most common in young children aged 1–5 [3], and it was also highlighted by other authors [32]. According to research, the number of children hospitalized after contact with laundry capsules is higher than with other detergents [33]. Young children aged 9–36 months are the major risk group. The plastic form, shape, and multi-colored packaging may encourage children to reach for the capsule. Toddlers, unaware of the danger, consume the contents or inadvertently put them in their eyes. Interestingly, according to the latest research, the color of the capsule and its size do not correlate with a higher number of chemical poisonings [34].

In our study, we also observed a significant number of eye burns, which constituted 4.07%, and these injuries mainly affected children aged 1–4 years. This youngest age group is also most susceptible to eye injuries due to falls from their own height, which was also noted by other authors [5, 13, 35]. Moreover, younger children are also more likely to suffer injuries during contact with animals [2, 36]. Matsa et al. noted that of all injuries related to activities and household chores, caring for animals led to eye injuries in 16.9% of cases. These authors also pointed out that young children usually do not have developed cognitive abilities to interact appropriately with animals [2]. In addition to the fact that we actually observed this mechanism of injury among the youngest children aged 1–9 years, injuries caused by animals were the only mechanism in which more cases, with 64%, were reported in females. According to the literature, different animals are responsible for different injury patterns. Although we observed the predominance of cat-inflicted injuries, other animals were responsible for similar injuries. It is contrary to the literature, perhaps because of the different species of animals found in different countries [37].

Although in our study only 9 (1.53%) cases of injury occurred during a traffic accident, some authors showed a higher percentage of such injuries, reaching 8.5% [23]. The small number of such injuries in our case may be due to the general improvement in safety. The introduction of seat belts and airbags in cars has reduced the number of fatal accidents. While airbags reduce the number of serious eye injuries, they more often lead to minor injuries [2, 13]. Usually, these injuries include corneal abrasion, hyphema, and vitreous hemorrhage, but more serious injuries also occur, such as corneal and scleral rupture, lens dislocation, or retinal detachment [38]. The literature also describes alkaline eye injuries caused by an aerosol mixture containing sodium hydroxide and metal oxides released from an airbag [39].

In 60.34% of cases, the injuries were self-inflicted. These conclusions differ from the results of other studies. For example, Bućan et al. noted that in 2/3 of all eye injuries, the perpetrator was another person [17]. However, it is difficult to determine whether, in young children, injuries caused by peers were intentional or not. In our study, 19 injuries were caused by upper limb impact, and 10 cases involved assault. Gise et al. pointed out that most injuries causing damage to the visual pathway were unintentional, although, in their study, 26.60% were the result of the assault, which, interestingly, most often occurred in the group of the youngest children up to 3 years of age [35].

In our study, hospitalization in pediatric departments was necessary in 55 cases (9.32%). Twenty-three (3.9%) patients required surgery, most often boys, who sustained an active injury. In 11 cases (47.83%), surgery was performed after an open globe injury, while in 9 (39.13%) after a closed globe injury. The remaining three cases involved maxillofacial surgery without any injury to the eyeball. Of the 23 surgical procedures performed, eight involved maxillofacial surgery, including one case that involved neurosurgical elements (reconstruction of the skull vault and base). Two maxillofacial procedures were combined with eyelid surgery, and in one case, lacrimal duct drainage was also performed. The following procedures were performed in individual cases: transpupillary laser photocoagulation due to retinal tear; repair of a ruptured eyeball with removal of a subconjunctival hematoma; drainage of an ocular abscess; reposition of the prolapsed iris and choroid following a scleral laceration; and removal of a foreign body. In eight cases, repair of the conjunctiva, cornea, or sclera was performed. In two further instances, following the initial management of corneal wounds, patients were referred to other facilities for additional treatment: one for retinal detachment and the other for post-traumatic cataract. The authors were unable to access the medical records about the further treatment of the patients at other facilities. Canadian studies indicated that surgery after eye injuries in children was necessary in 7.4% of cases, mainly in boys, but without a clear trend regarding the mechanism of injury [11]. Another study found that surgery was necessary in all cases of open eye injuries, but only in about 30% of cases of closed globe injuries [6] .

The data we collected show that 100 patients had a decrease in visual acuity ≥ 0.3, which is category 0 according to ICD-10. In addition, blindness occurred in 11 cases, and in 4 of them, no visual acuity was noted after treatment, even in follow-up examination performed several years after the injury. Those cases included injuries in the mechanism of assault, glass injuries and being hit by a branch. Of these cases, 3 resulted in open globe injuries. Shah et al. described that among patients with closed globe injuries, the majority (24%) of cases resulted in a decrease in visual acuity < 1/60 (category 4 according to ICD-10) [40]. Liu et al. concluded that open globe injuries have a much worse prognosis and caused a decrease in visual acuity of 6/60 or more in 41% of cases [6]. Similar results were obtained several years earlier by Minderhoud et al., in whose study the final visual acuity < 6/60 was noted in 58% of open globe injuries [7]. Bućan et al. reported identical results. In their study, 96% of patients with closed globe injury achieved a final visual acuity test result of 0.3 or more. In contrast, 49% of children with open globe injuries achieved a score lower than 0.3 [17]. It is worth mentioning that somewhat similar relationships were observed in our study. Visual acuity ≥ 0.3 was achieved by 84% of patients with closed globe injury and 54.55% with open globe injury, while blindness after open globe injury occurred in 4 cases (36.36%). Although our results are similar to the literature data, worse visual acuity results were noted more often in patients with open globe injury, even after treatment, but these results were not statistically significant. Therefore, to stratify the visual acuity results, a study with a larger group should be conducted in the future.

In 31 (5.25%) patients, additionally, skull fractures occurred, including 9 cases of isolated orbital fractures. Interestingly, the mechanism of orbital fractures in children differs from that of adults. Trapdoor fractures occur most frequently in children. The high elasticity of bones, characteristic of children, means that a linear fracture occurs as a result of trauma, and the bone fragment displaced outward immediately returns to its original position. This may result in entrapment of the soft tissues of the orbit [41, 42]. Unfortunately, our medical records did not contain sufficient details to determine whether the fracture was of the trapdoor or blowout type.

It is worth emphasizing that ocular injuries harm mental health and quality of life. According to Karaman et al., children suffering from eye injuries might be at risk for mental disorders, are commonly diagnosed with anxiety disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, and depression. Furthermore, a child’s disability can also have a profound emotional effect on their parents [43]. Due to the retrospective nature of our study and the limitations of medical records, it was not possible to verify this aspect of eye injuries in our case.

To reduce the risk of the above clinical consequences, the medical community should focus on raising parental awareness about ocular trauma. This includes highlighting the most common mechanisms of injuries and educating parents on how to respond promptly when an injury occurs. As we described, the mechanisms of injury vary across age groups and geographical regions. Therefore, effective prevention of eye trauma requires a multidimensional approach that adapts to children’s developmental stages and diverse environments [13].

Although, as the literature shows, many injuries can be prevented by using personal protective equipment or following general safety rules [13], some of them are completely accidental or the result of the natural development of the child. Here, we can cite injuries in the mechanism of falling from own height, which most often occurs in the group of the youngest children who cannot maintain balance [5]. According to our results, eye injuries after a fall occurred in 11 patients (2.16%). Similar conclusions can be drawn from the analysis of cases of self-inflicted finger struck to the eye, which most often occur by accident when the patient does not have time to close the eyelid. Our research shows that this was a relatively common mechanism of injury, observed in a total of 58 cases (9.83%), mainly in the age groups 1–4 and 5–9 years. It should, therefore, be borne in mind that eye injuries in children can often occur in mundane circumstances that the parent will not be able to predict or prevent and will require urgent diagnostics and treatment.

This study has several limitations. The data were collected retrospectively, making it impossible to obtain information useful for comparing the results with literature data. In addition, the interview collected from the patient and caregiver in the ED is often characterized by a laconic description, serving only to obtain key information for establishing a diagnosis and initiating treatment. As a result, in many cases, there was a lack of information about the place of the event, a detailed description of the mechanism of injury, or the possible intentional action of the perpetrator. The documentation lacked specific details regarding the eye injuries, including the exact anatomical structures affected. This absence of information prevented meaningful comparison with data reported in the literature. Additionally, retrospective design might have contributed to inconsistent documentation of injury circumstances. It is also possible that errors in data entry resulted in the exclusion of some patients from the study. In addition, the number of ED admissions declined during the COVID-19 pandemic, making it impossible to objectively analyze the variability of the number of injuries over time, and thus to compare the results with other studies.

Conclusion

Eye injuries are a diverse group of injuries, the mechanism of which is often limited only by the child’s creativity. These injuries most often occur in young boys during active play. Boys consistently underperform on visual acuity tests. The older the age group, the worse the visual acuity observed after the injury. They rarely result in permanent vision loss, but they frequently cause a temporary decrease in visual acuity. Children with open eye injuries are more likely to experience permanent blindness compared to children with closed eye injuries. A child’s age is a key factor in determining the mechanism of eye injury, but geographic region and social and cultural conditions also play a significant role. The above observations provide a more comprehensive understanding of epidemiological trends in childhood eye injuries. However, the extensiveness of the discussed topic results in the need for further analysis of individual injury mechanisms to predict the long-term consequences of such injuries as accurately as possible and prevent them at an early stage of diagnosis and treatment.
