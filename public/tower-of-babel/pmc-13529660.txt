A data-driven machine learning model for effective diabetes diagnosis
Authors: T Shobha, S Pradeep, Seema Patil, J Subramanya, G B Sarthaka Mitra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes is a prevalent long-lasting disease marked by high blood glucose due to inadequate insulin secretion and insulin resistance may result in serious life-threatening complications. Diabetes global prevalence has raised by fourfold over the past thirty years, and is the ninth foremost disease leads to death across the globe. Meanwhile, developments in machine learning presents new opportunities for prediction and classification of disease. However, despite of numerous existing models, a need for classifying types of diabetes still remains. The objective of this study is to develop an integrated, data-driven machine learning model for predicting the occurrence and classification of diabetes in an effort to improve on these limitations and investigate the ability to differentiate between types of diabetes through machine learning approach. To evaluate the proposed framework for classification of diabetes and its subtypes, publicly accessible diabetes-related datasets were employed for model development and evaluation. Binary classification is used for detecting occurrence of diabetes, while multiclass classification was employed for subtype classification, namely Prediabetes(PD), Type 1 Diabetes(T1D), Type 2 Diabetes(T2D), and Pancreatogenic (Type 3c-T3cD) Diabetes. K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes, Random Forest, and XGBoost machine learning algorithms were implemented. The XGBoost demonstrated the highest performance among all models with an accuracy of 0.97. Its feature importance scores validated predictive accuracy and to identify key factors that distinguish types of diabetes. The proposed model is intended to serve as a decision-support system for screening and classification tool using routine clinical data to facilitate early diagnosis and treatment planning.Keywords: Diabetes, Machine learning models, Diabetes prediction, Prediabetes, Pancreatogenic diabetes, PrevalenceSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Endocrinology, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus, universally known as diabetes, denote to a collection of endocrine diseases. This chronic condition is instigated by impairments in insulin secretion, results in the elevated blood sugar levels1. Diabetes can be elicited by multiple factors, for instance pancreatitis, pancreatic cancer, genetic defects, and/or surgery. An individual’s likelihood of developing diabetes is also influenced by a variety of lifestyle factors, including food preferences, routine exercises, stress levels, and others2. In 2021, an estimated 529 million people lived with diabetes globally, with a global age-standardized prevalence of 6.1%3. Sun et al. reported similar findings4. Currently, around 537 million people aged 20 to 79 are affected, and this is projected to reach 643 million by 2045. Kumar et al. (2021) found that the incidence of diabetes in India will rise from 9.6% in 2021 to 10.9% in 20455. Insulin resistance or deficiency reduces the efficiency of food-to-energy conversion, leading to excessive hunger (polyphagia). The kidneys use more water to filter excess blood glucose, resulting in frequent urination (polyuria) and excessive thirst (polydipsia). The World Health Organisation classifies diabetes into six categories, with Type 1 and Type 2 being the most common. Prediabetes is an early stage, while diabetes from pancreatic disease is termed pancreatogenic or Type 3c diabetes.

Type 1 Diabetes is a consequence of autoimmune destruction, leading to the diminished secretion of insulin by pancreatic β-cells. This type likely to occur at a younger age compared to other types of diabetes owing to its genetic predisposition. People with this type of diabetes are at a higher risk of developing other autoimmune disorders. They require external doses of insulin for survival6. Type 2 Diabetes is characterized by insulin resistance and a progressive lack of insulin secretion. Initially, the pancreas compensates by secreting more insulin, but unable to keep up overtime, instigating the elevated blood sugar level. It is influenced by a variety of lifestyle factors, including food preferences, routine exercises, stress levels and obesity. Diabetes impacts one among the eleven persons globally with 90% of them are being attributed to Type 2 Diabetes. It may result in serious life-threatening complications like retinopathy, cardiovascular diseases, neuropathy, nephropathy and so on. Prediabetes is the initial stage before Type 2 Diabetes with elevated blood sugar levels but below threshold diabetes level having symptoms like polyuria and polyphagia. Schlesinger et al. showed that Prediabetes markedly increased the risk for cardiovascular, kidney, liver failure, as well as the risk of cancer, and dementia7. Pancreatogenic diabetes is the most common after Type 2 Diabetes. Patients with acute pancreatitis are at a 34.5% risk of developing diabetes8. Shivaprasad et al. concluded that higher chances of mortality and morbidity in case of Pancreatogenic Diabetes compared to Type 2 diabetes9.

The recent rise in artificial intelligence has a huge transformation in healthcare with machine learning models processing the health records to diagnose and predict patient outcomes more efficiently. Data-driven decisions enables an early and accuracy of diagnosis with greater confidence. Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems are capable of handling large datasets including electronic health records (EHRs), medical imaging, and genomics enabling the early detection of diseases often at stages when treatments are most effective. However, the healthcare poses its own challenges to AI. Availability of credible datasets are limited especially in economically challenged areas. Due to this, model may be biased owing to the prevalence of data from socio-ethnic groups with higher healthcare facilities. There are collective ethical concerns about the unequal impact on understated communities10. Data privacy is another concern, and the abstraction provided by many machine learning models are often questioned on their internal working and reliability. Research on combating biases in medical AI is required to make patient data more representative and enhance transparency. Detection and alleviating bias especially in clinical decision-making are crucial to avoid inconsistencies and warrant all patients benefit equally11.

Numerous efforts have been made to effectively incorporate Machine Learning approaches for detection of diabetes, and quite a few diverse methods have been employed for the same12. KNN predicts the class of a data by observing at the classes of its adjacent neighbors in the feature space. It is a modest, non-parametric technique that depend solely on the structure of data. Though it offers a direct interpretation, its performance is greatly reliant on the selection of the distance metric and the number of adjacent data points. Conversely, Logistic Regression evaluates the probability of a binary outcome by relating a sigmoid (logistic) function. It learns the relationship between the input features and the log-odds of the target variable, resulting in a linear classifier. However, it assumes a linear relationship between features and the target, which can limit its performance on complex datasets. Decision trees split the data into regions based on feature values, aiming to maximize the purity of each region. They are intuitive and easy to interpret, but tend to overfit the training data, especially when grown too deep. To counter this, a Random Forest classifier can be used, which combines multiple decision trees, each trained on a random subset of the data and features. The final prediction is made by averaging or taking a majority vote from all trees. This ensemble approach makes Random Forest robust and reliable for classification tasks. Abdulhadi and Al-Mousa obtained an accuracy of 82% using the Random Forest Classifier13. Ghosh et al. reported an accuracy of 99.35% using a Random Forest Classifier14. Thotad et al. also concluded that Random Forest outperformed other models in detecting the Indian demographic dataset15.

It has been shown that ensemble techniques perform better than logistic regression because logistic regression assumes linear relationships and is much more sensitive to outliers. An exception is the study by Khaleel and Al-Bakry, where Logistic Regression showed an accuracy of 94%, above that of Naive Bayes 79% and KNN 69%16. Rajendra and Latif concluded that the ensemble techniques Max Voting and Stacking perform better than Logistic Regression, with the latter providing evidence that Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (GBDT) and logistic regression models perform somewhat equally up to a training data size of . GBDT shows a significant increase beyond this point, while the Logistic Regression model’s metrics saturate17. Seto et al. provided evidence that Gradient Boosted Decision Trees and logistic regression models perform equally up to a training data size of 10000. GBDT shows a significant increase after this number, while the logistic regression model’s metrics become saturated18. Salem et al. utilized the basic K-Nearest Neighbors and two of its variants, which allow partial membership in multiple classes, Fuzzy KNN and Three-Fold Nested K-Nearest Neighbors(TF KNN). TF KNN turned out to be the best performer with 94.13% specific accuracy19. While instance-based algorithms like KNN and its fuzzy variants excel in modelling local data relationships, they tend to struggle with high-dimensional datasets and are computationally expensive during inference.

Researchers have begun using ensemble methods alongside boosting techniques to address these research limitations. The methods provide an optimal combination that delivers strong predictive results while needing fewer computational resources. The XGBoost framework, along with its Gradient Boosting framework, has achieved widespread popularity among users. The system can create models for complicated nonlinear relationships while using regularisation methods to control overfitting. Maulana et al. conducted a study comparing XGBoost with six machine learning models and found that XGBoost outperformed all other models in predicting diabetes. XGBoost achieved the highest scores in accuracy, precision, and sensitivity, which were used to determine its F1-score20.

The research community has devoted extensive effort to studying binary diabetes classification, but has not investigated diabetes subtypes. Researchers faced difficulties in developing models to distinguish between Type 1 Diabetes, Type 2 Diabetes, Prediabetes, and Pancreatogenic Diabetes because public datasets lack subtype annotations. Current models produce binary results because researchers lack access to subtype data that would enable them to develop accurate subtype identification systems. The approach does not identify the minor distinctions between subtypes that doctors recognise as separate clinical conditions, hindering their ability to deliver personalised care and implement early treatment methods. Uddin et al. note the need for a balanced dataset to reduce false-negative detections21.

For diabetes prediction, numerous machine learning models have been employed. KNN is more ideal for non-linear data, with a reasonable accuracy for binary classification. Naïve Bayes is a probabilistic approach, but it is not capable to handle the feature dependence. XGBoost has been rated as top gradient-boosted algorithm with the best accuracy and solid performance on imbalanced datasets. Random Forest algorithm is also effective one which offers interpretability of feature importance.

Though, most models emphasis on binary classification (Healthy and diabetes) and fail to discriminate between types, such as Type 1, Type 2, Prediabetes, and Pancreatogenic (Type 3c) Diabetes. This limitation underlines the necessity of more comprehensive approaches, as proposed in this work. The novelty of the present study lies in the development of a two-stage machine learning framework that combines binary diabetes prediction with multiclass subtype classification. Unlike conventional machine learning studies that primarily focus on binary classification or limited subtype differentiation, this work extends classification to clinically relevant diabetes subtypes, including Prediabetes, Type 1 Diabetes, Type 2 Diabetes, and the relatively underexplored Type 3c (Pancreatogenic) diabetes. Additionally, the study provides a comparative evaluation of multiple machine learning algorithms to identify the most effective model for subtype discrimination.

Methodology

Dataset

The datasets utilised in the current study were obtained from publicly accessible sources. The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases Pima Indians Diabetes Database, which comprises diagnostic parameters for forecasting the inception of diabetes, was used for binary classification focused on the detection of diabetes22. A publicly accessible Kaggle repository published by Batra was employed for multiclass classification intended to identify diabetes subtypes23. The dataset offers a comprehensive overview of numerous diabetes subtypes, including Type 1 Diabetes, Prediabetes, Neonatal Diabetes Mellitus (NDM), and Steroid-Induced Diabetes, along with an extensive range of metabolic, genetic, medical, and lifestyle attributes relevant to diabetes detection and classification. In this study, a targeted subset of physiologically relevant features was selected based on clinical significance and exploratory data analysis. A curated subset corresponding to specific clinical outcomes,viz. Prediabetes, Type 1 Diabetes, Type 2 Diabetes, and Type 3c Diabetes were selected from the primary dataset in accordance with the study objectives. In this work, we have performed multiclass classification to categorize four diabetes subtypes: Prediabetes, Type 1 Diabetes, Type 2 Diabetes, and Type 3c (Pancreatogenic) Diabetes. Prediabetes denotes to an intermediate metabolic state categorized by raised blood glucose levels which are higher than normal but below than the threshold for diabetes diagnosis24. Type 1 Diabetes is an autoimmune disorder triggered by the destruction of pancreatic β-cells, leads to inadequate production of insulin25. Type 2 Diabetes is categorized by insulin resistance and progressive dysfunction of β-cells and resistance to insulin, resulting in diminished regulation of glucose26. Type 3c (Pancreatogenic) Diabetes is a secondary type of diabetes instigated by pancreatic ailments such as pancreatic trauma, chronic pancreatitis, or pancreatic surgery, which disrupt both exocrine and endocrine pancreatic functions27.

The curated subset comprises 21,539 samples across 8 physiological and clinical features. The target variable represents four distinct diabetes subtypes-Prediabetes, Type 1 Diabetes, Type 2 Diabetes, and Type 3c Diabetes, which are distributed in a well-balanced manner across the cohort. Age plays a critical role in subtype differentiation. Type 1 diabetes is commonly diagnosed in young people due to autoimmune destruction of β-cells. Type 2 diabetes correlates with age-related insulin resistance and β-cells decline. Type 3c Diabetes often follows chronic pancreatic diseases in older adults. Thus, it reflects both pathophysiology and the onset period. It helps to model and distinguish between autoimmune, metabolic, and secondary causes. BMI (Body Mass Index) is a measure of body fat based on height and weight. A high BMI directly links to obesity-induced insulin resistance, which is a hallmark of Type 2 diabetes. Excess body fat disrupts insulin signaling and increases systemic inflammation. Obesity also elevates free fatty acid levels, which affect glucose uptake in the muscles and liver. A higher BMI correlates with a greater risk of developing diabetes and the progression of the disease. Waist circumference measures visceral fat, which is more metabolically active than subcutaneous fat. Visceral fat is strongly linked to insulin resistance and chronic inflammation. Unlike BMI, it specifically captures central obesity, which is a better predictor of Type 2 diabetes. Excess abdominal fat increases hepatic glucose production and impairs insulin action. Studies show that waist circumference is independently associated with diabetes risk. Cholesterol levels, particularly HDL and LDL, are often disrupted in people with diabetes. Type 2 diabetes commonly presents with dyslipidemia, which includes high LDL, low HDL, and elevated triglycerides.

This imbalance contributes towards insulin resistance and vascular dysfunction. Cholesterol disorders are also part of the metabolic syndrome, which signals a higher cardiovascular risk in all diabetes types. The most significant diagnostic indicator for diabetes is blood glucose levels. Diabetes is confirmed if the fasting glucose level is above 126 mg/dL or the postprandial glucose level is above 200 mg/dL. This condition develops due to a deficiency or ineffectiveness of the hormone insulin. These processes caused cellular damage, triggering inflammation and resulting in glucose toxicity in various tissues. Prediabetes, Type 1, and Type 2 diabetes differ in glucose patterns. The model depends on glucose levels to detect and segregate the subtypes based on this parameter. Insulin levels show not only the quality of insulin produced by the pancreas but also the body’s response. Low insulin levels suggest Type 1 diabetes mellitus, caused by the destruction of β-cells. However, when insulin levels are high, along with high blood sugar, insulin resistance develops, which characterises Type 2 diabetes and prediabetes. Insulin levels help distinguish diabetes driven by deficiency from that driven by resistance. They also help to identify failure of compensation in the later stages of Type 2 diabetes. Indicators of pancreatic health reflect structural or functional issues with the pancreas.

Insulin secretion gets disrupted by medical conditions such as chronic pancreatitis or pancreatic tumours. Damage to pancreatic tissue leads to the development of Pancreatogenic diabetes, which differs from Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes. The markers can include pancreatic enzyme levels along with surgical history and imaging results. These features are uncommon in standard datasets, yet they serve an essential role in detecting Type 3 diabetes. Their inclusion helps the model capture secondary diabetes stemming from exocrine dysfunction. The “Pancreatic Health” feature in the dataset ranges from 20 to 99 and does not correspond to a standard clinical measurement used in medical practice. Since the original dataset documentation does not specify the acquisition process, this attribute is interpreted as a derived health index representing pancreatic functional status. Therefore, in this study, the feature is treated as a secondary predictor variable obtained directly from the dataset rather than an independently measured clinical parameter.

Data preprocessing

The datasets used in this study required minimal preprocessing. Since the employed machine learning models—Decision Tree, Random Forest, and XGBoost–are tree-based algorithms that are largely insensitive to feature scales, no feature scaling, normalization, or standardization was performed. Categorical attributes, where present, were encoded using label encoding. Furthermore, the training datasets contained no missing values; therefore, no imputation techniques were applied during model development. During inference, any user-provided inputs left blank are represented as missing values (NaN) and handled directly by the XGBoost model via its native missing-value processing mechanism. Label encoding was used for categorical attributes when present. Additionally, no imputation techniques were used during model development because there were no missing values in the training datasets.

Data splitting

To ensure reproducibility of the experimental results, a fixed random seed of 42 was used throughout the data-splitting process. The dataset was partitioned into training and testing subsets using a hold-out validation strategy. Specifically, 60% of the data was used for model training, while the remaining 40% was reserved for testing (test_size = 0.4) with random_state = 42, ensuring that the same train–test split can be reproduced in subsequent experiments. To ensure consistency between training and evaluation cycles, the machine learning models also used the same random seed wherever appropriate.

The training dataset was subjected to the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) in order to correct class imbalance. By interpolating between existing minority class examples, SMOTE creates synthetic samples for minority classes, boosting class representation without merely repeating observations. To avoid information leaking into the test set, the oversampling procedure was limited to the training data. The Decision Tree, Random Forest and XGBoost classifiers were then trained for both binary and multiclass classification tasks using the balanced training dataset.

Cross-validation settings

To assess model generalization, 5-fold stratified cross-validation was performed on the SMOTE-balanced training dataset using classification accuracy as the evaluation metric. The stratified sampling strategy preserves the class distribution across folds, with each iteration using 4 folds for training and 1 for validation. The final cross-validation score was computed as the mean accuracy across all five folds.

Machine learning models

Five predictive models were built and evaluated using supervised learning algorithms: Random Forest, K-Nearest Neighbours (KNN), Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes Classifier, and XGBoost. The selection of models aimed to represent a variety of learning approaches, ranging from basic probabilistic methods to complex ensemble methods. Both models were trained and tested in two phases: the first, a binary classification task to detect diabetes; and the second, a multi-classification task to distinguish subtypes. We use a sequential two-stage classification strategy. In the first stage, a binary classifier trained on the Pima Indians Diabetes Dataset is used to determine whether a subject is diabetic or non-diabetic. If the binary classifier predicts a non-diabetic outcome, the prediction process terminates, and the subject is classified as having no diabetes. If the binary classifier predicts the presence of diabetes, the subject’s clinical features are passed to a multiclass second classifier. This classifier then categorises the diabetic subject into one of four clinically relevant classes: Prediabetes, Type 1 Diabetes, Type 2 Diabetes, or Type 3c Diabetes(This second model is trained on the curated subset of data from Kaggle published by Batra). The binary model acts as an initial layer, while the multiclass model performs subtype identification only for cases predicted as diabetic. Among all, XGBoost consistently outperformed the other models. It demonstrated increased accuracy and effectively controlled class imbalance; it is the most promising candidate for ultimately deployment and interpretation.

Ensemble learning approach

We evaluate several tree-based learning methods: Decision Tree, RandomForest and XGBoost, on their ability to classify examples. Random forest uses the bagging( bootstrap aggregation) strategy:many decisions trees are trained independently on different bootstrap samples of the training data, and their predictions are averaged to reduce variance and increase model robustness. XGBoost uses a boosting strategy that incrementally builds decision trees, where each successive learner tries to minimize the errors of the previous ensemble, thereby capturing more complex and non-linear patterns. We train our models independently on the balanced training dataset, and compare their outputs- class predictions, probabilities, performance metrics, and ROC curves-by examining the entire ensemble.

Hyperparameter values and model configuration

Hyperparameter optimization was performed using GridSearchCV to identify the best-performing parameter combinations for each classification model. After the optimal hyperparameters were determined, the models were retrained using only these selected values.

For the XGBoost classifier, the optimal parameters were:

n_estimators=96, max_depth=6, min_child_weight=100, subsample=0.8, colsample_bytree=0.8, learning_rate=0.1,

scale_pos_weight=1, eval_metric=’mlogloss’, and random_state=42.

The Random Forest classifier was trained with

n_estimators=96, max_depth=6, min_samples_split=100, min_samples_leaf=80, bootstrap=True, max_features=’sqrt’,

criterion=’entropy’, class_weight=’balanced_subsample’, and random_state=42.

For the Decision Tree classifier, the optimal configuration consisted of max_depth=6 and random_state=42.

These tuned hyperparameters were subsequently used for all model training and evaluation experiments.

Results and discussion

Here, we report our machine learning results obtained by testing several popular supervised learning algorithms on the same dataset for detecting diabetes. We analyze their performance with respect to different metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, sensitivity, specificity and ROC-AUC28,29. In order to examine possible overfitting, we compared the training and validation performance, plotted learning curves, and carried out repeated 5-fold stratified cross-validation. Results are given as average ± standard deviation across the folds. We performed hyperparameter tuning using nested cross-validation to avoid an optimistic bias. We added regularization terms and complexity constraints to mitigate overfitting. Bootstrap analysis was employed to estimate confidence intervals, while feature importance was assessed for stability across folds. Finally, we compare these results and conclude which classifier works best for this particular problem. We could improve the Decision Tree’s (DT) classification performance by pruning it using cost complexity pruning(CCP). We estimated its optimal ccp_alpha parameter using another 5-fold stratified cross-validation process, finding a reasonable compromise between the model’s generality and simplicity. Our final pruned tree obtained roughly 93% test accuracy, suggesting a decent fit without overfitting the training data.30. The classification report indicated almost equal precision and recall for all classes, with the F1-score being a fair representation of the overall balancing act.

From Figs. 2 and 3, it can be concluded that decision trees exhibit a higher misclassification rate for type 2(2) and type 3c(3) diabetes than other models31,32. This shows that the tree model had more difficulty distinguishing these two clinically similar classes. While the classification of prediabetes(0) and type 1(1) is still accurate, type 2 is often incorrectly labelled as type 3c. The rigid splits of decision trees likely do not capture subtle feature distinctions. This suggests limited generalisability across overlapping diabetes subtypes.

From Figs. 6 and 7, it is clear that XGBoost achieves the most consistent and reliable classification across all four diabetes subtypes, showing no errors in identifying prediabetes and type 1 diabetes, and substantially lower confusion between type 2 and type 3c compared to decision trees36. This high performance is due to XGBoost’s gradient-boosting framework, which combines weak learners into a more powerful ensemble. In this manner, it can better represent complex, nonlinear interactions of features. XGBoost is optimised with second-order gradients, uses regularisation to avoid overfitting, and supports missing data internally, unlike an ordinary decision tree. This enhances the model’s convergence and generalisation. These advantages are significant in medical datasets, where feature dependencies and minor differences among classes are common. As a finding, XGBoost performs better at separating the classes in high-dimensional spaces of clinical features, particularly when dealing with compromising conditions such as type 2 diabetes and type 3c. Logistic Regression is highly effective for Prediabetes(0) and Type 1(1), achieving almost perfect accuracy on both the training and test sets. It is, however, commonly misunderstood between Type 2(2) and Type 3c(3). The training samples were 898, and the testing samples were 240 Type 2, not classified as Type 3c. 189 testing samples were misclassified as Type 2. This causes the accuracy to reduce to 89.98 percent and 89.48 percent (training and testing, respectively), due to testing, worse than DB. The intersecting characteristics between Type 2 and Type 3c were very damaging to performance. The model is too simple for this distinction. Hence, this model was not selected. Confusion matrices further revealed that the decision tree was moderately successful at correctly classifying samples across all classes, with minor errors in adjacent or overlapping conditions; e.g., class 2 versus class 3. Insight into the most influential attributes in the model’s decision-making process was obtained through the feature importance plot. In all these, Blood Glucose Level, Insulin, and BMI were identified as strongly contributing to the model’s decisions, in collaboration with medical knowledge of diabetes biomarkers32.

On the other hand, the NaiveBayes classifier with the best var_smoothing parameter optimised using GridSearchCV displayed slightly worse results than the decision tree. The best model yielded a test accuracy of about 92%, with test precision higher than test recall. While the precision was relatively high, the recall was lower, indicating that although the model was confident in its positive predictions, it was less so when aggregating all relevant class instances37. Given that Naive Bayes assumes independence between features, the slightly diminished performance is expected, especially in the feature space where BMI and insulin, for instance, are somewhat correlated.

Nonetheless, the Naive Bayes confusion matrix showed that the classifier achieved reasonably good separation across the four target classes, though misclassifications were more pronounced than with the Decision Tree. In particular, class overlaps and false positives in adjacent classes highlighted the limitations of the model’s strong independence assumption.

Prediabetic (class 0)

Prediabetic class possessed blood glucose levels as the most dominant predictor followed by age, BMI and waist circumference. This pattern indicates early dysregulation of glucose homeostasis in association with central obesity and age-related metabolic decline. The minimal contribution of cholesterol and insulin levels indicates early metabolic modifications and compensatory hyperinsulinemia prior to explicit β-cell dysfunction. These results are consistent with clinical indications that insulin resistance connected with obesity and ageing is the major cause for prediabetes24,38. This supports the clinical aid for the early screening and preventive measures employing anthropometric and glycaemic markers.

Type 1 diabetes (class 1)

Age was the most significant feature for Type 1 Diabetes, with blood glucose, insulin levels, and lipid-associated parameters making relatively fewer contributions. This is the reflection of the autoimmune aetiology of Type 1 Diabetes, which is usually categorised by early onset of age and minimal insulin resistance linked with obesity25. The absence of autoimmune biomarkers in the dataset, along with the impact of insulin therapy after diagnosis, may explain the reduced association between insulin levels and outcomes. Notably, the model’s focus on demographic features rather than metabolic characteristics aligns with clinical practice and minimises the risk of misdiagnosing lean individuals with hyperglycaemia as Type 2 Diabetes24.

Type 2 diabetes (class 2)

In Type 2 Diabetes, blood glucose levels are the most important, followed by cholesterol levels, BMI, pancreatic health, and age. This characteristic outline usually represents the metabolic syndrome phenotype. In this condition, obesity, high cholesterol, and declining pancreatic β-cell function occur together with ongoing Type 2 Diabetes39,40. The effects of lipid levels highlight the risks associated with T2D and cardiovascular disease. These findings show that the model effectively captures the complex metabolic traits of T2D and its relevance in assessing metabolic risk.

Type 3c diabetes (class 3)

Blood glucose and insulin levels were the most significant features for Type 3c Diabetes, followed by age, BMI, cholesterol levels and pancreatic health. This pattern manifests in pancreatogenic diabetes, a condition that develops due to the lack of insulin and poor pancreatic health. Such results may lead to diseases such as chronic pancreatitis41. Since Type 3c Diabetes is often mistaken for Type 2 Diabetes, the need for a better understanding of the pancreas’s role becomes even more evident42. The model can be a useful tool for doctors to screen patients who may undergo further tests for pancreatic assessment. Subtypes can be differentiated based on pancreatic-related features.

Comparative insights and clinical implications

Blood glucose levels are a major determinant of classification in all classes of diabetes and therefore have universal diagnostic values24. However, the distinct influences of body, lipid, and pancreatic measurements suggest that diabetes subtypes have distinct underlying mechanisms, thereby highlighting the proposed framework’s clarity and clinical relevance. In conclusion, the model demonstrates pathophysiological consistency with diabetes and the ability to distinguish along the subtype continuum, while maintaining high agreement between model-derived feature importance and recognised clinical expertise. These findings suggest the potential for the model to be used for screening, identifying diabetes subtypes, and creating personalised treatment plans for diabetes patients (Table 2).

Clinical interpretation of XGBoost performance

The outperformance of XGBoost is due to its ability to capture complex nonlinear relationships and interactions among clinical features. Important diabetes-related parameters like insulin levels, age, BMI, pancreatic function markers, etc., are non-linearly associated with disease progression and subtyping. Unlike traditional linear models, XGBoost uses an ensemble of decision trees to efficiently learn these complex patterns and subtle differences among diabetes subtypes. For example, in the multiclass classification scenario, there is considerable overlap between clinically similar categories( like Prediabetes & Type 2 Diabetes) and sometimes even between Type 1 and Type 3c Diabetes. The boosting nature of XGBoost improves class discrimination by building decision trees iteratively, which focus on difficult to classify samples. In addition, XGBoost applies regularization and optimization procedures to boost its generalization performance while being robust over heterogeneous clinical datasets with different data quality. Also, boosting may help recognize rare diabetes subtypes more accurately by giving higher importance to challenging samples during training.

Limitations, ethical considerations and clinical applicability

A key limitation of the proposed two-stage framework is that the binary diabetes classification and multiclass subtype classification models were trained on two different datasets with potentially differing population characteristics, feature distributions, and collection methodologies. This was necessary due to the lack of a unified dataset containing both large-scale binary labels and clinically relevant subtype annotations. Therefore, the framework should be considered a modular proof-of-concept rather than a fully deployable clinical system.

Additionally, the multiclass subtype dataset was obtained from a publicly available source, which may limit clinical standardization and generalizability. In particular, the Pancreatic Health feature was a derived variable with unclear clinical derivation, limiting its interpretability. This was further reflected in the LIME analysis, where pancreatic health showed minimal contribution to Type 3c diabetes classification, despite pancreatic dysfunction being central to this subtype. Instead, subtype predictions relied predominantly on glucose-driven patterns, suggesting limited discriminative value of pancreas-specific biomarkers within the current dataset.

Furthermore, external validation using independent clinical datasets was not performed due to limited availability of subtype-specific data. Future work will focus on multicentre clinical validation and incorporation of clinically validated subtype-specific biomarkers, particularly pancreatic markers, to improve robustness and clinical applicability.

Clinically, the proposed framework has the potential to serve as a decision-support tool for early diabetes screening and subtype identification, thereby assisting clinicians in selecting appropriate diagnostic investigations and personalized treatment planning. However, it is intended as a decision-support tool and should complement, not replace, clinical judgment. Ethical deployment requires adherence to data privacy, fairness, and transparency principles. The use of explainable AI techniques, such as SHAP or LIME, improves model interpretability and supports trustworthy clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated machine learning based approaches for multi class diabetes classification using a real-world clinical dataset. We demonstrate that ensemble-based methods yield high predictive accuracy while simpler models such as pruned decisions trees offer advantages in terms of interpretability and transparency important for clinical use. We reiterate that ML models can aid early risk detection and assist practitioners with decision making but should be regarded as supportive tools rather than replacements for expert clinical judgement. Inclusion of explainable AI can also help enhance model transparency by revealing feature contributions and boosting end user confidence. Future work will focus on improving model robustness via advanced calibration, optimized hyper parameter search and addition of other clinically relevant features. Validation over larger and more diverse cohorts will also be explored to enable deployment of AI driven diabetes screening systems in practice.
