Exploring the toxic mechanism of excessive intake of naringin on drug-induced liver injury using network toxicology and experiment validation strategy
Authors: Yu Ding, Tong Yu, Jinrong He, Caixia Peng, Xiuling Wang, Jin Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExcessive intake of citrus fruits may affect liver metabolism and trigger various adverse reactions. Naringin, a natural flavonoid compound, is mainly extracted from the citrus fruits. Previous studies have reported that naringin poses the protective effects of hepatotoxicity. However, it is currently unclear whether high-dose intake of naringin causes liver toxicity. This study aims to rigorously evaluate the toxicity of excessive intake of naringin for drug-induced liver injury (DILI) utilizing network toxicology and molecular docking methodologies.Methodswe conducted a systematic investigation into the toxicity and underlying molecular mechanisms associated with high-dose intake of naringin-induced DILI by utilizing various bioinformatics approaches including drug toxicity analysis, PPI protein network analysis, GO and KEGG enrichment analysis, molecular docking simulation and in vitro experiment validation.ResultsOur findings indicated that naringin posed a significant risk in inducing DILI. The results observed that when the concentration of naringin reached 400µM, it inhibited the viability of HepaRG cells, boosted AST and ALT activity, and increased the levels of inflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-1β. Based on PPI network analysis, we identified six hub genes, including CASP3, PPARG, PTGS2, PARP1, HSP90AA1, and ESR1. KEGG enrichment analysis had screened out several crucial pathways. Molecular docking results showed that naringin might have strong binding ability to six proteins. We verified four key proteins for naringin induced DILI via the cross analysis between DILI datasets GSE54255 and GSE74000 and the naringin related targets. Finally, the mRNA expression level of the four genes was detected and found that when the concentration of naringin reached 400 µM, the expression level of HSP90AA1, CASP3, and PTGS2 significantly was upregulated while the PARP1 expression was decreased in HepaRG cells.ConclusionThe study found that high dose naringin could induce DILI in vitro experiment, and its potential mechanisms were explored through network toxicology and molecular docking strategies. This research provides a theoretical framework for understanding the underlying molecular mechanisms of high-dose intake of naringin-induced DILI.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40360-025-01062-3.Keywords: Drug induced liver injury, High-dose intake of naringin, Network toxicology, Molecular docking

Introduction

Drug-induced liver injury (DILI) represents a significant adverse drug reaction, potentially culminating in acute liver failure or mortality in severe instances. There is a notable surge in liver damage attributable to herbs and dietary supplements (HDS) in Western nations [1, 2].

Naringin, a natural flavonoid compound, is extracted from the immature or nearly mature dried fruit peels and pulp of citrus plants within the Rutaceae family. The compound was first identified by De Vry in 1857 in the flowers of grapefruit and is frequently utilized as a seasoning, flavoring agent, and additive in various food applications [3, 4]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that naringin exhibits a range of biological activities [5–7], including antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-osteoporotic, and neuroprotective and protective effect of hepatotoxicity [8–10]. Although naringin has been shown to have anti-hepatotoxic protective effects at low doses and short durations in animal models, recent studies have indicated that these natural plant supplements possess potential hepatotoxic properties and may contribute to drug-induced liver injury, particularly when consumed in high doses or over extended periods [11]. According to the literature, excessive consumption of grapefruit can inhibit the activity of the metabolic enzyme CYP3A4, thereby affecting drug metabolism and potentially leading to liver detoxification impairment and damage. Additionally, it can induce adverse reactions such as poisoning, dizziness, nausea, palpitations, tachycardia, fatigue, and hypotension [12]. Additionally, naringin has also been found to linked to the potential liver detoxification impairment and damage. It was reported that naringin can inhibit human CYP3A4 activity, which may lead to liver injury [13, 14]. The adverse reactions associated with the excessive consumption of citrus fruits, also pose the possibility that naringin, a principal component of citrus fruits, may induce liver toxicity when taken in excessive amounts. However, the potential toxicity of high-dose intake of naringin induced DILI has not been fully explored, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying high-dose intake of naringin’s contribution to drug-induced liver injury (DILI) remain unclear.

Traditional methods for assessing drug toxicity are plagued by challenges, including being time-consuming, costly, and raising ethical concerns regarding animal use. Network toxicology is a scientific approach that combines computational modeling and bioinformatics to study the toxic effects of chemicals on living organisms [15]. Network toxicology leverages big data and computational tools to rapidly predict the toxicity risks associated with pharmaceutical compounds. It systematically examines the effects of drugs on biological molecular networks, enabling the prediction of toxicity pathways and identification of core targets. This approach significantly enhances the efficiency and depth of drug safety evaluations. Molecular docking, a computational approach, is utilized to predict the binding interactions between chemical compounds and target proteins, thereby estimating the potential toxicity of drugs in relation to diseases [16]. In contrast to prior research focused on the hepatoprotective properties of naringin, this study systematically assesses the potential hepatotoxicity of naringin in the context of DILI through the application of network toxicology and molecular docking methodologies. Furthermore, we identified and validated the risk associated with naringin dosage factors on liver toxicity.

In this study, we developed a toxicity pathway network for various compounds using network toxicology, allowing for a systematic and comprehensive elucidation of the toxic targets and mechanisms associated with naringin. Furthermore, we used molecular docking methods to model the interactions between compounds and target proteins, forecasting possible binding sites. Finally, we validated the effect of hub genes in naringin induced DILI by in vitro experiments. These methodologies enabled us to identify the toxic targets and mechanisms underlying high-dose intake of naringin-induced drug-induced liver injury (DILI), thereby providing a theoretical basis for subsequent research endeavors.

Methods

Analysis of naringin toxicity

The structural details of naringin were retrieved from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and later analyzed with ProTox3.0 (https://tox.charite.de/protox3/) and ADMETlab 2.0 (https://admetmesh.scbdd.com/service/evaluation/cal). Utilizing network search algorithms, preliminary predictions regarding the toxicity of naringin were conducted.

Prediction of naringin-related targets

The structure of naringin was imported into three databases: SwissTarget Prediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/), SEA (https://sea.bkslab.org/), and SuperPred (https://prediction.charite.de/), with the species parameter limited to Homo sapiens. Possible targets of naringin were discovered, and the results from the three databases were consolidated. Duplicate entries were removed, and the target names were standardized using the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/).

Screening for DILI-related targets

The GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) and OMIM databases (https://omim.org/) were used to find targets associated with drug-induced liver injury. By entering the keyword “drug-induced liver injury,” relevant targets were retrieved. Subsequently, common potential targets between naringin and DILI were identified as potential targets for naringin-induced DILI, and Venn diagrams were generated to illustrate these findings.

Analysis of protein-protein interaction (PPI) network

We utilized the STRING 12.0 database (https://string-db.org/) to input the cross-referenced genes of naringin and drug-induced liver injury (DILI), restricting the species to “Homo sapiens,” to obtain protein-protein interactions. The data obtained were subsequently brought into Cytoscape 3.10.1 for network analysis and node value computation, culminating in the construction of a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network graph. Subsequently, the cytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape was employed to identify core targets of naringin-induced DILI using the Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC) method. The MCC algorithm is one of the core methods used to analyze the importance of nodes in PPI networks. Its principle is based on the global structural characteristics of the maximum cluster (complete subgraph), mainly by analyzing “the local dense connectivity”and“global influence”of the targets in the PPI network to screen key nodes. This study used CytoHubba to calculate the MCC values of all nodes and selected top 10 ranked as key targets. The six leading targets were identified for molecular docking analysis.

Analysis of GO and KEGG

For the analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment, we utilized the Metascape database [17] (https://metascape.org/) to investigate the key proteins associated with naringin-induced DILI. Initially, GO analysis, encompassing biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) assessments, was conducted to explore the primary biological functions of the core targets. Subsequently, A KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted to find signaling pathways linked to potential targets involved in naringin-induced DILI, using an FDR threshold below 0.05 to identify toxicity pathways associated with the intersecting targets. Furthermore, KEGG enrichment analysis was conducted to further investigate the principal signaling pathways related to the key targets of naringin-induced drug-induced liver injury (DILI), utilizing the Metascape database. The outcomes of the Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG analyses were visualized and presented using the Microbiotics website [18] (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/).

Molecular docking of naringin and hub targets

To investigate the interaction between naringin and the core target of DILI, molecular docking was performed using Quick Vina 2. A lower docking score indicates stronger binding affinity between naringin and the core target. Initially, the three-dimensional structures of the core targets were retrieved from the PubChem compound database and saved in SDF format. Subsequently, the SDF format was converted to PDB format using ChemBioDraw 3D. The core proteins and naringin were then preprocessed using AutoDock Tools 1.5.7. DoGSiteScorer (https://proteins.plus/) was employed to predict the binding sites of receptors for molecular docking. Finally, Quick Vina was utilized for molecular docking. Discovery Studio 2025 was used to visualize and analyze the results, and a heatmap was created to show the binding energy between naringin and the core targets.

External validation of core targets

To verify the expression of candidate target genes, the DILI datasets GSE54255 and GSE74000 from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) were employed. Using GEO2R, differentially expressed genes were selected with criteria of |log2 (fold change) | >1 and p-value < 0.05, and shown in a volcano plot and the heatmap. The Venn diagram was used to identify the shared targets between Naringin’s potential targets and the two DILI datasets, GSE54255 and GSE74000.

Cell culture and treatment of naringin

The hepatocellular carcinoma cell line HepaRG was originated from Servicebio (Wuhan, China). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modifed Eagle’s medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientiffc, USA) and maintained in a 5% CO2 incubator. Naringin was dissolved in DMSO to make a 100 mM solution as a stock solution. Then, it was diluted in DMEM medium to prepare the working solution for cell treatment.

CCK-8 assay

HepaRG cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 103 cells per well. After cell adhesion, they were treated with different concentrations of naringin and cultured for 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h respectively. Then, 10 µl of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, the cells were incubated for 2 h. The OD values were measured at 450 nm using an EnSpire Multimode plate reader (PerkinElmer, USA), and cell viability was calculated based on the OD values.

Assay of liver function

Cellular liver injury was detected using human aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) detection kits (Jiancheng Bioengineering, Nanjing, China). HepaRG cells were seeded in 12-well plates, and after adhering, they were treated with different concentrations of naringin for 24 h. The supernatants were collected and analyzed for the activity of ALT and AST according to the detection kit instructions.

QPCR

HepaRG cells were treated with different concentrations of naringin for 24 h and lysed using TRIZOL lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientiffc, USA), then total RNA was extracted. Total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Quantitative PCR (QPCR) was performed on an ABI StepOne Plus QPCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientiffc, USA) using UltraSYBR mixture (CWBio, Jiangsu, China). Relative gene expression values were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The primers were purchased from Shanghai Sangon Biotechnology and the information of the primer sequences used was showed in supplemental Table e1.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8.0 was utilized to analyze the experimental results. Each experiment was conducted three times. A one-way ANOVA was employed to assess the statistical significance among multiple groups. The experimental data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD), with P < 0.05 indicating a significant difference.

Results

Toxicity assessment of naringin

The toxicity profile of naringin was evaluated utilizing ProTox II and ADMETlab 2.0, with the results subsequently integrated and summarized (Fig. 1; Table 1). The findings from the toxicity analysis suggest a significant association between naringin and drug-induced liver injury (DILI).

Naringin at high concentration induced liver injure by reducing cell viability and increasing the levels of liver function enzymes and inflammatory factor in HepaRG cells

To verify that naringin can induce drug-induced liver injury, firstly, we treated HepaRG cells, a drug-induced liver injury cell model, with naringin at different concentrations (0, 100, 200, 400, 600, 800, 1000 µM). Using the CCK-8 assay to detect HepaRG cell toxicity, the results showed that naringin could increase HepaRG cell toxicity in a time- and dose-dependent manner when its concentration reached 400 µM (Fig. 2A). Secondly, we collected the supernatants of HepaRG cells treated with naringin for 24 h and detected the activities levels of ALT and AST. The results showed that high-dose naringin significantly upregulated the activities levels of ALT and AST (Fig. 2B and C). Finally, HepaRG cells were treated with different concentrations of naringin and qPCR was used to detect the expression level of inflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-1β. qPCR results showed that high-dose naringin increased the expression level of TNF-α and IL-1β in HepaRG cells (Fig. 2D and E). All these results indicated that high-dose naringin could induce liver injury.

Identification of targets for naringin-induced DILI

Naringin targets were retrieved from the SwissTarget Prediction, SEA, and SuperPred databases. Following the removal of duplicate entries, a total of 166 unique naringin targets were identified. Concurrently, DILI-related targets were sourced from the GeneCards and OMIM databases, resulting in 2,697 unique DILI targets after deduplication. By intersecting the naringin and DILI target datasets, 85 overlapping targets were identified as potential mediators of Naringin-induced DILI (Fig. 3).

Venn diagram of the targets of naringin and DILI

Analysis of the PPI network and core genes in naringin-induced DILI

Initially, we constructed a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network using the common targets associated with naringin-induced drug-induced liver injury (DILI), comprising 85 nodes and 568 edges, in the STRING 12.0 database. Using Cytoscape 3.10.1, the topological characteristics of network nodes, including proximity, centrality, and radiance, were examined. The results were visualized in the form of a PPI network diagram (Fig. 4). Subsequently, ten hub genes were identified through the application of the Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC) analysis method within the CytoHubba plugin of Cytoscape 3.10.1. A PPI network of these core targets was then constructed using the same software to further investigate their interactions (Fig. 5). The leading six targets were determined through MCC analysis, were Caspase-3 (CASP3), Heat Shock Protein HSP 90-alpha (HSP90AA1), Recombinant Peroxisome Proliferator Activated Receptor Gamma (PPARG), Poly (ADP-ribose) Polymerase 1 (PARP1), Prostaglandin-Endoperoxide Synthase 2 (PTGS2) and Estrogen Receptor 1 (ESR1). These proteins exhibited the largest nodes, more intense red coloration, and were centrally located within the PPI network, signifying their pivotal role as core proteins in naringin-induced DILI. These proteins play multiple roles in naringin induced DILI, including regulating cell proliferation, migration, apoptosis, and mediating lipid metabolism. By analyzing the interactions of core targets, the molecular mechanism between naringin and DILI was further explored.

Enrichment analysis of GO and KEGG for potential targets

The Gene Ontology (GO) functional analysis of 85 potential targets associated with naringin-induced drug-induced liver injury (DILI) was performed utilizing the Metascape database. This analysis encompassed a total of 4,968 GO entries, including 3,895 biological processes (BP), 330 cellular components (CC), and 743 molecular functions (MF). These entries were evaluated and ranked according to their false discovery rate (FDR) values, with the top 10 entries in each category-BP, CC, and MF presented in Fig. 6. Within the biological processes, naringin significantly influenced the G protein-coupled adenosine receptor signaling pathway, the response to staurosporine, and the transport of xenobiotics across the blood-brain barrier. The cellular component targets were predominantly associated with the endoplasmic reticulum lumen and the brush border membrane. At the molecular level, naringin’s function was primarily linked to G protein-coupled adenosine receptor activity and cysteine-type endopeptidase activity involved in the execution phase of apoptosis.

Pathway enrichment analysis of core targets

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying naringin-induced drug-induced liver injury (DILI), a KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed on the 10 core targets associated with naringin-induced DILI using the Metascape database. This analysis identified a total of 128 enriched signaling pathways, from which the top 20 were chosen for in-depth visualization and analysis. The enrichment outcomes were depicted using a Sankey plot and a bubble plot, highlighting the main targets for each pathway (Fig. 8). Notably, the important pathways tied to the critical genes of naringin-induced DILI were found to be closely linked to pathways implicated in various diseases, including lipid and atherosclerosis, human cytomegalovirus infection, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, Alzheimer’s disease, alcoholic liver disease, and hepatitis B. Additionally, these pathways encompassed multiple molecular signaling pathways, such as the IL-17 and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways, as well as pathways involved in cancer. The correlation between DILI and these diverse diseases was substantiated by the KEGG enrichment analysis of the core targets. Although further investigation is warranted, these findings suggest that naringin may exert effects across various tissues and organs.

Molecular docking of naringin and key proteins

To gain a deeper understanding of how naringin interacts with six key target proteins -CASP3, HSP90AA1, PPARG, PTGS2, PARP1, and ESR1—molecular docking analysis was conducted utilizing Quick Vina software. The results of the molecular docking were subsequently visualized and analyzed. The findings revealed that the binding energies between naringin and each of the six main proteins were all lower than − 7.3 kcal/mol, indicating a strong affinity between naringin and these core targets. This suggested that these interactions might play a significant role in the molecular mechanism of naringin-induced drug-induced liver injury (DILI) (Fig. 9). However, the interaction between naringin and these proteins needs to be further verified through in vitro experiments.

Effect of high dose naringin on target proteins

To further validation the effects of high dose naringin on HepaRG cells, the different concentrations of naringin were applied to HepaRG cells, the mRNA levels of the four key proteins were measured via qPCR experiments. qPCR results revealed that compared to the untreated group, high dose naringin treatment led to a significant increase the expression level of CASP3, HSP90AA1 and PTGS2 and the decrease the expression level of PARP1 in naringin group compared with the untreated group. Additionally, the mRNA expression levels of key targets exhibited a dose-dependent relationship (Fig. 11). Discussion

DILI refers to liver damage caused by a variety of substances, including chemical drugs, biological agents, traditional Chinese medicine, natural medicine, health supplements, dietary supplements, and metabolic products. It is a prevalent cause of acute liver injury in Western countries [19]. Clinically, DILI is typically classified into dose-dependent and idiosyncratic liver injury. The former mechanism is attributed to the direct hepatotoxic effects of drugs, with the severity of liver injury correlating with the drug dosage [20]. Here, we reported that high dose naringin could induce DILI, and explored its potential mechanisms through network toxicology and molecular docking strategies.

This study initially employed the ProTox-II and ADMETlab 2.0 databases to conduct a toxicity analysis of naringin. The results indicated that naringin possesses the potential to cause drug-induced liver injury (DILI). Then, we conducted in vitro experiments using hepaRG cells to validate the toxic effects of naringin on DILI. The experimental results showed that at low concentrations of naringin, there was no significant effect on HepaRG cell viability, liver function, or the expression of inflammatory factors. However, when the naringin concentration increased to 400 µM or higher, it significantly reduced HepaRG cell viability, increased ALT and AST levels, and elevated the levels of inflammatory factors. These results indicated that naringin as a potential inducer of drug-induced liver injury (DILI) at elevated concentrations.

This finding appears to contradict the well-documented safety profile of naringin in Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats [21] and its hepatoprotective effects observed in other animal models [10], as reported in the study. A comprehensive analysis suggests that this discrepancy may arise from several critical factors. Firstly, there is a significant disparity between dosage and exposure levels. The in vitro toxicity concentration observed in this study (≥ 400 µM) substantially exceeds the systemic exposure levels achievable with the reported safe doses in rats, suggesting the presence of effective buffering or threshold mechanisms in vivo. Secondly, interspecies metabolic differences play a crucial role. HepaRG cells retain the metabolic characteristics of the human liver, whereas the metabolic enzyme profile in rats differs markedly. The observed in vitro toxicity may be contingent upon a human-specific metabolic activation pathway, which may be inactive or produce different metabolites in rats. Thirdly, the inherent limitations of in vitro models must be acknowledged. The simplified single-cell culture system lacks the complete liver micro-environment and adaptive protective mechanisms present in vivo. Previous studies indicates that naringin may activate endogenous protective pathways, conferring hepatoprotective effects. However, this beneficial effect observed in vivo, particularly at lower doses, may be obscured by naringin’s direct cytotoxicity at elevated concentrations in vitro. Consequently, the in vitro findings of this study do not undermine the safety profile of naringin in a rat model but rather highlight critical risk considerations. Specifically, naringin demonstrated direct hepatotoxicity in human liver cell systems at exceedingly high concentrations. This underscores the necessity for further research to explore the species-specific metabolic mechanisms and the molecular basis of its toxicity and protective effects. In advancing the potential application of naringin, it is imperative to adhere to the extensive safety margins established in animal studies for dosage formulation, ensuring human exposure remains significantly below the in vitro toxicity threshold. Additionally, it is crucial to enhance monitoring of liver function, particularly in individuals with metabolic abnormalities, both prior to and during clinical trials.

Through network toxicology, the potential molecular mechanisms of naringin mediating DILI have been elucidated. We obtained 10 core targets of naringin and DILI, including CASP3, HSP90AA1, PPARG, PTGS2, PARP1, ESR1. GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of naringin’s core target in DILI revealed the involvement of several signaling pathways including PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, apoptosis, pathways in cancer, chemical carcinogenesis receptor activation. Molecular docking demonstrated the good binding between naringin and these core proteins. Furthermore, databases GSE54255 and GSE74000 were utilized to search for the common differentially expressed genes of DILI and naringin. Among these DEGs, CASP3, HSP90AA1, PTGS2 and PARP1 coincide with previously screened core targets, confirming their essential role in naringin-induced DILI. The results revealed the potential molecular mechanism of naringin induced DILI.

CASP3, as one of the most critical enzymes in the apoptosis pathway, is closely related to the occurrence of cancer, aging, and cardiovascular disease [22]. There are research reports indicating that inhibiting CASP3 activity can decrease PARP cleavage and cell apoptosis, ultimately mitigating liver injury [23]. One study discovered that PI3K/AKT pathway can facilitate the up-regulation of BCL2 protein and inhibit CASP3 activity, thus ameliorating liver injury [24]. Our KEGG enrichment analysis also found that PI3K/AKT and apoptosis signaling pathways are involved in naringin induced DILI. Therefore, we speculate that naringin may induce DILI by regulating CASP3 activity. HSP90AA1 is closely related to the occurrence of cancer and inflammatory reactions. Studies have found that down-regulating HSP90AA1 expression can protect against liver damage caused by DILI [25]. A large number of studies had found that the PTGS2 (COX2)- adrenergic pathway was closely related to liver diseases, including liver fibrosis, liver cancer, cirrhosis. The COX2 - adrenergic pathway can resist liver fibrosis by inhibiting the contraction, migration, and proliferation of hepatic stellate cells [26, 27]. PARP1 is a DNA repair enzyme that plays an important role in DNA damage repair and cell apoptosis. PARP1 is a substrate of caspase, which is a core member of cell apoptosis and can be cleaved by caspase3 and caspase7, leading to loss of activity and cell apoptosis [28, 29]. Our research results also found that apoptosis related proteins caspase3 and PARP1, and apoptosis related pathways, are involved in the process of naringin induced DILI. Therefore, we speculated that the apoptosis mechanism was the main mechanism of naringin’s toxic effect on DILI. To further explore the role of the key targets in naringin-induced DILI, we treated HepaRG cells with naringin and detected the mRNA expression levels of core targets using qPCR. The results revealed that when the dose of naringin reached 400µM, it significantly up-regulated the expression of HSP90AA1, CASP3 and PTGS2, while the expression of PARP1 was decreased in hepaRG cells. These findings suggested that CASP3, HSP90AA1, PTGS2 and PARP1 and apoptosis related pathways might play a crucial role in high dose naringin-induced DILI. However, further in-depth molecular mechanism studies require additional in vivo and in vitro experiments for validation.

Network toxicology has demonstrated efficacy in predicting novel toxicities and elucidating associated molecular mechanisms by integrating diverse resources, processing large-scale datasets, and examining multiple influencing factors. This study represents the first application of network toxicology and molecular docking methodologies to predict the toxicological mechanisms of naringin in drug-induced liver injury (DILI). However, the current research is constrained by its reliance on computational methods, necessitating a large number of clinical cohorts and epidemiological studies to support the conclusions of this study. Further in vivo experiments are needed to confirm the liver injury effect at an equivalent concentration of 400 µM at the overall animal level, and elucidate its mechanism from physiological, biochemical, histological, and molecular levels.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that high dose naringin could induce DILI through in vitro experiments and explored the toxicological targets and mechanisms of naringin in drug-induced liver damage through network toxicology and molecular docking techniques. This research not only clarifies the possible molecular mechanisms behind high dose naringin-induced DILI but also sets up a swift and efficient framework for investigating toxic substances by combining network toxicology and molecular docking approaches.
