Virtual reality simulator-based assessment of non-technical skills in eye surgery: managing complex cataract surgery
Authors: Lauriana Solecki, Rémi Yaïci, Léonard Sidhoum, Nabil Chakfé, Anne Lejay, Nicole Neumann, Emilia Koestel, Salome Kuntz, David Gaucher, Arnaud Sauer, Léa Dormegny, Tristan Bourcier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-technical skills (NTS) are cognitive, social, and personal abilities that, when combined with technical skills (TS), contribute to surgical competence through safe and efficient task performance. The objective of this study was to develop and implement a simulation-based training model that integrates both NTS and TS in complex cataract surgery.MethodsA comparative study was performed. Ophthalmology residents with experience in cataract surgery ranging from novice to beginner level were enrolled. During the first step, the participants were engaged in a “training simulation” on the Eyesi® Surgical simulator (CAT-D module: Advanced courses: white cataract). During the second step, an “evaluative simulation” was conducted using the Eyesi® simulator on the same complex case. After each simulation, participants were rated using HUFOES score (HUman Factors in intraoperative Ophthalmic Emergencies Scoring System) for NTS and Eyesi® performance score for TS. The primary outcome was the comparison of NTS (HUFOES score) between training and evaluative simulations. A p-value under 0.05 was considered significant.ResultsA total of 14 ophthalmology residents were included with a median age of 27 (interquartile range [IQR] 25–28). They had undergone a median of 30 cataract surgeries prior to the study (IQR 10–60). The mean global HUFOES score significantly improved between the simulations (from 42.0 ± 6.7 after the training simulation to 75.2 ± 6.3 after the evaluative simulation; p < 0.001). A positive correlation was identified between the different items of the HUFOES score following each simulation (p < 0.001). In multivariate analysis, the HUFOES score after the evaluative simulation was significantly associated with the HUFOES score after the training simulation (β = 0.49; p = 0.019) and with participant age (β = +1.89 per year; p = 0.008).ConclusionsA surgical simulation-based training program with debriefing led to a significant improvement in NTS for residents with a mean experience in cataract surgery ranging from novice to beginner level. NTS training could be implemented in a simulated environment and incorporated into the ophthalmology residents’ training curriculum for cataract surgery learning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09029-6.

Background

Surgical competence requires more than manual dexterity and technical proficiency. In the operating room (OR), it depends on the clinical context, as well as on the surgeon’s knowledge, experience, and specific skills [1]. According to the literature, surgical competence encompasses dexterity, intellectual and cognitive abilities, and appropriate behaviors and attitudes [2–4]. These non-technical skills (NTS) refer to the behavioral aspects of OR performance that are not directly related to medical expertise, equipment handling, or drug use (i.e. the technical skills - TS). NTS can be defined as the set of cognitive, social, and personal resource skills that, together with TS, contribute to safe and efficient task performance [4, 5]. In surgery, these include situational awareness, decision-making capability, leadership, teamwork, and communication [4, 6, 7]. NTS represent approximately 70–75% of the skills of global surgical competence [2, 8].

The occurrence of adverse events or intraoperative complications - nearly 50% of which occur in the OR [9] - highlights the importance of NTS, which complement TS and play an essential role in the safe and efficient execution of surgical procedures. Deficiencies in NTS have been shown to increase the risk of complications and surgical errors [10]. Indeed, poor communication and teamwork had a major impact on intraoperative incidents [11]. Moreover, errors in clinical reasoning and judgment represent the second most frequent cause of preventable patient harm after technical errors [12]. Although nearly half of these adverse events are considered avoidable [12], most surgical curricula continue to prioritize the development of TS, often at the exclusion of NTS [13]. It is therefore imperative that future generations of surgeons receive structured training in NTS alongside TS.

In particular, ophthalmic surgery, as a microsurgery, demands a distinct set of competencies compared with conventional surgery. A higher risk of safety events caused by human error is also observed, largely due to a lack of training in NTS, especially in high-volume surgical environments [14]. Failures in NTS have been identified as significant contributors to surgical errors such as wrong intraocular lens implantation or administration of local anesthesia to the wrong eye [15, 16]. Complex analysis and decision-making are often performed under constraints of time, uncertainty, and stress, increasing the potential for error [17]. Nearly 60% of serious cataract surgery complications could be avoided through enhanced human factors management and NTS training [18].

Cataract, defined as lens opacification, is the leading cause of reversible blindness worldwide, and therefore cataract surgery by phacoemulsification has become the most frequently performed surgical procedure globally, creating a major challenge in training sufficient competent surgeons. Simulation-based medical education offers a risk-free environment through wet labs using animal eyes and dry labs using synthetic models, but both approaches are limited by cost, restricted access, ethical concerns, and their incomplete replication of human ocular tissues and complex clinical scenarios. Over the past decade, virtual reality ophthalmic surgical simulators have revolutionized cataract surgery training by accurately reproducing surgical steps. Given these considerations, cataract surgery provides an ideal model for NTS training. Virtual reality simulation tool (i.e. Eyesi® Surgical Simulator) offers a promising approach to address this educational need [13, 19]. Eyesi® Surgical Simulator allows trainees to practice surgical procedures in a controlled and reproducible environment without compromising patient safety [20, 21].

The objective of this study was to develop and implement a simulation-based training model that integrates both NTS and TS in complex cataract surgery using the Eyesi® Surgical Simulator.

Methods

Design of the study

A comparative nonrandomized study was performed at the GEPROMED surgical training center (Strasbourg, France) in collaboration with the department of Ophthalmology of the University Hospital of Strasbourg. The study was conducted from January 1st to April 30th, 2025.

Ethical approval

Informed consent was obtained from participants prior to their involvement in the study. The protocol in question was subject to approval by the ethics committee of the French Society of Ophthalmology in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. (IRB00008855 Société Française d’Ophtalmologie IRB#1)

Population of the study

Ophthalmology residents from different French University Hospitals who had performed less than 80 cataract surgeries in a supervised autonomy setting were enrolled. This number was chosen according to the level of development and surgical training in cataract surgery ranking from novice to beginner (defined as 0 to 80 cataract surgeries) [22, 23].

Exclusion criteria were withdrawal of consent and any reason requiring discontinuation of the simulation.

Data collection protocol

A specific training protocol for NTS in cataract surgery was integrated into the curriculum of volunteer ophthalmology residents in the form of a one-day simulation-based training model. The following data were collected via questionnaire: sex, age, dominant hand, and number of cataract surgeries performed under autonomous supervision.

The design of the protocol was guided by the Best Evidence Medical Education (BEME) recommendations and the AMEE guide n°82 : “Simulation in healthcare education: A best evidence practical guide” [24]. The educational objective of this surgical simulation-based training was the cultivation of cataract surgery NTS in residents at the novice to beginner proficiency level.

First, the participants were engaged in a “training simulation” on the Eyesi® Surgical simulator (Haag-Streit Simulation, Mannheim, Germany), which was divided into the following segments: pre-briefing, briefing, role-playing, and debriefing. The protocol for each simulation stage was meticulously structured within a single day. (1) The pre-briefing was conducted to familiarize participants with the simulation equipment (Eyesi® simulator). (2) The briefing outlined the educational objectives, evaluation criteria, and instructions for the simulation scenario. (3) During the role-playing segment, the simulation scenario was presented: a complex case of white cataract surgery that was deemed to be “at risk of complication” (CAT-D module: Advanced courses). The simulation scenario was set in a realistic OR environment, with roles allocated to the main surgeon (participant) and the OR nurse (senior player). A simulated white cataract surgery scenario was conducted under the operating microscope (comprising the capsulorhexis, hydro-maneuver, and phaco-emulsification stages), employing the Eyesi® simulator. (4) After the role-playing, a structured debriefing was conducted by a senior in medical education (LS). The purpose of the debriefing was to guide the residents in developing NTS. The debriefing sessions were designed to create a supportive environment where mistakes could be addressed and transformed into valuable learning opportunities. The debriefing began with the reactions of the participants, followed by an in-depth analysis of the role-playing to ensure a comprehensive understanding of the situation and to encourage the reflective learning. Following the discussion, the key takeaways and the synthesis of the take-home messages were presented to conclude the session [24].

During the second phase of the study, an “evaluative simulation” was conducted using the Eyesi® simulator on the same complex case of white cataract surgery, which followed a similar sequence: pre-briefing, briefing, and role-playing.

Each simulation was conducted sequentially, with the resident and the senior engaging in the activity without any time constraints. During both simulations, NTS and TS were evaluated for each participant. The NTS were assessed by a validated questionnaire : HUFOES score (HUman Factors in intraoperative Ophthalmic Emergencies Scoring System) by LS [25, 26]. HUFOES score (0–90) can assess five dimensions of NTS: teamwork and communication, leadership, decision making, situational awareness and professionalism. (Appendix 1)

The TS were assessed by Eyesi® performance score (0-100), a global score was calculated using the mean of capsulorhexis score, hydro-maneuver score, and phaco-emulsification score [20].

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the comparison of NTS (HUFOES score) between training and evaluative simulations.

The secondary outcome was the correlation between NTS (HUFOES score) and TS (Eyesi® performance score).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were applied to analyze numerical and nominal data. Correlations were made between scores and participants characteristics or confounders. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used for continuous, normally distributed variables, with normality checked via the Shapiro-Wilk test and Q-Q plot. Spearman’s correlation was applied to ordinal or non-normally distributed variables. The Chi-square test evaluated categorical relationships, with Phi correlation for dichotomous variables. Student’s t-test was used for continuous, normally distributed variables, and Wilcoxon Mann-Whitney for non-normally distributed variables. Multiple linear regressions examined the relationships between risk factors and HUFOES Eyesi® performance scores. A p-value under 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Characteristic of the participants

A total of 14 ophthalmology residents were included in this study. All followed the entire simulation-based NTS training protocol for complex cataract surgery.

Of the 14 participants, 8 were male (57.1%) and 6 female (42.9%), with a median age of 27 (interquartile range [IQR] 25–28). Most of the subjects were right-handed (13 subjects), while 1 subject was left-handed. The participants had undergone a median of 30 cataract surgeries prior to the study (IQR 10–60). HUFOES score

The mean global HUFOES score significantly improved between training and evaluative simulations (from 42.0 ± 6.7 after the training simulation to 75.2 ± 6.3 after the evaluative simulation; p < 0.001). The internal coherence showed a global Cronbach Alpha coefficient of 0.79. This significant difference was also noticed in the sub scores of HUFOES score (Teamwork and communication 3.0 ± 1.5 vs. 4.4 ± 1.2; p < 0.001, Leadership 2.5 ± 2.7 vs. 4.1 ± 1.6; p < 0.001, Decision making 2.3 ± 3.3 vs. 4.3 ± 2.3; p < 0.001, Situational Awareness 2.2 ± 3.1 vs. 4.0 ± 2.6 ; p < 0.001, Professionalism 2.0 ± 1.7 vs. 4.4 ± 1.2 ; p < 0.001). Correlations

A positive correlation was identified between the 18 items of the HUFOES score following each simulation (training simulation then evaluative simulation) (p < 0.001). A negative significant correlation was found between the HUFOES score after the training simulation and its improvement after the evaluative simulation (rho − 0.60; p = 0.024) and between the Eyesi® performance hydro-maneuver score after the training simulation and its improvement after the evaluative simulation (rho − 0.82; p < 0.001). No significant correlation was revealed between the age of the participant and the improvement of the HUFOES score (rho 0.5; p = 0.071). However, no significant correlation was observed between HUFOES scores and EyeSi® performance scores, neither after the “training simulation” nor after the “evaluative simulation”.

Multivariate analysis

Regarding NTS, a multiple linear regression model (R² = 0.67) was constructed to identify factors associated with the HUFOES score after the evaluative simulation. The independent variables included the HUFOES score after the training simulation, age, and the number of cataract surgeries performed prior to the study. In this model, the HUFOES score after the evaluative simulation was significantly associated with the HUFOES score after the training simulation (β = 0.49; p = 0.019) and with participant age (β = +1.89 per year; p = 0.008).

Regarding TS, a multiple linear regression model (R² = 0.64) was constructed to explain the EyeSi® performance score after the evaluative simulation. The independent variables included the EyeSi® performance score after the training simulation, age, HUFOES score improvement, and the number of cataract surgeries performed prior to the study. In this model, the EyeSi® performance score after the evaluative simulation was significantly associated with the EyeSi® performance score after the training simulation (β = 0.84; p = 0.044).

Discussion

The present study found a positive impact of a surgical simulation-based training program on the development of NTS in 14 ophthalmologists with a mean experience in cataract surgery ranging from novice to beginner level. All five dimensions of the HUFOES score (teamwork and communication, leadership, decision-making, situational awareness, and professionalism) improved after the evaluative training consisting of a white complex cataract surgery scenario of simulation.

Although NTS are reported to represent up to 75% of surgical competence [2, 8], they are rarely discussed in the scientific surgical literature and classically not incorporated into faculty training or residents’ curricula. Moreover, NTS represent a significant opportunity to enhance patient safety and the efficiency of interventions [8, 27]. Nevertheless, there has been an increasing interest in NTS training in surgery over the past few years. The concept of NTS in surgery was largely described and published by Yule et al. They developed the following taxonomy of NTS in surgery: (1) Interpersonal/social skills: Communication, leadership, teamwork, briefing/planning/preparation, resource management, seeking advice and feedback, and coping with pressure/stress/fatigue. (2) Cognitive skills: Situation awareness, mental readiness, assessing risks, anticipating problems, decision-making, adaptive strategies/flexibility, workload distribution [4, 5, 7]. According to this taxonomy, NTS assessment scales was built, but a variability still exist between the different surgical specialties. For example, the Non-Technical Skills for Surgeons (NOTSS) system [6, 28] was useful for evaluating individual surgeons, while the Oxford Non-Technical Skills and Surgical (NOTECHS) scale was primarily used to assess surgical teams as a whole [29, 30]. In ophthalmology, HUman Factors in intraoperative Ophthalmic Emergencies Scoring System (HUFOES) was validated in cataract surgery [25, 26]. Considering the variability of development process, analysis tools, and purpose among surgical NTS scoring systems, the HUFOES score was the most suitable for the objectives and scope of our study.

We decided to use the approach of a simulation environment in the present study. Indeed, Ounounou et al. reported on a variety of training tools for the NTS, including high-fidelity operating room simulation, full immersion/distributed simulation, didactic teaching, and crew resource management [19]. In terms of training objectives and resident’s proficiency, the use of a high-fidelity virtual reality simulator, specifically the EyeSi® simulator, within the context of an operating room simulation appears to be a rational approach [31]. Indeed, each stage of a training simulation is essential: pre-briefing, briefing, role-playing, and debriefing [24]. While the simulation role-playing itself can be a valuable learning tool, providing feedback can enhance the learning experience for participants. Feedback to the resident is primarily facilitated through structured debriefing. This process ensures that learning objectives are achieved and that any emerging learning objectives are discussed [32]. Moreover, Savoldelli et al. found that simulations with only a situational scenario of role-playing, without debriefing, did not improve anesthesia learners’ NTS [33]. The process of debriefing facilitates the assessment of the knowledge, skills, and attitudes exhibited by a participant, thereby providing a comprehensive analysis of the actions observed during the role-playing of the simulation. This form of feedback assists in identifying the underlying causes of any discrepancies between observed and expected actions. It is crucial to acknowledge that positive reinforcement of correct performance, accompanied by examples of successful outcomes, is just as essential as identifying and addressing unwanted actions or results. Debriefing is a process that helps to identify the reasons behind the actions observed during the simulation exercise, leading participants to a more effective self-assessment, self-reflection and self-correction [24]. Training simulation facilitates experiential learning through surgical role-playing and reflective learning through debriefing. It is plausible that this debriefing stage contributed to the enhancement of NTS observed in our study. Studies have shown that simulation by role-playing [34, 35] and debriefing [36] are effective tools for developing NTS in surgery, regardless of the complexity of the task or the resident’s level.

Furthermore, competency-based learning theory suggests a continuum between TS and NTS [37]. The majority of studies have also demonstrated strong correlations between surgeons’ NTS and TS [27, 35, 38]. In the present study, no clear link was established with HUFOES score and EyeSi® performance score. This outcome may be attributable to the limited number of participants. Improving NTS does not automatically imply an immediate technical gain as measured by the EyeSi® performance score. This finding is consistent with the existing literature on the subject, as the EyeSi® simulator was developed and validated for the training of TS. In contrast, NTS requires the definition of clear tools [13]. However, the present study identified two learning curves : the improvement of TS is more modest (and with greater variability depending on the stage of the surgery) compared to NTS during the simulation training. A classical phenomenon referred to as the “catch-up effect” has been observed in the acquisition of TS and NTS. This effect suggests that residents who initially demonstrate lower levels of skill proficiency have the potential to exhibit greater improvement in comparison to those who initially demonstrate higher levels of proficiency [39, 40]. The age of the resident may also be a contributing factor to the enhanced improvement of NTS, as it is only found in the multiple linear regression analysis. This could be due to the absence of documentation of initial aptitudes, such as emotional intelligence or medical experience, at the baseline. These confusing factors have been demonstrated to influence the NTS [41, 42].

The present study has some limitations. The small number of participants was attributable to the time-consuming nature of the study protocol, which may have influenced the results to some extent. Nevertheless, this training program is aligned with the educational reality. However, it should be noted that the evaluation may be subject to bias due to the use of a single evaluator. Another potential limitation is the absence of a control group for comparison. This pilot study was conducted to developpe the NTS in ophthalmology and to evaluate the feasibility of incorporating such a study into an educational training program for the NTS in a single day. Further studies are needed to determine the long-term anchoring of the knowledge and to validate the findings with other complex cases and cases involving complications from cataract surgery. Indeed, this study utilized only a white complex cataract surgery simulation scenario, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other surgical scenarios or simulation contexts.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrated that a one-day surgical simulation-based training with debriefing significantly improved NTS in residents with novice to beginner level in cataract surgery. This result suggests that NTS training is feasible in a simulated environment and could be implemented in ophthalmology residents’ training curriculum for cataract surgery learning. However, further studies are necessary to identify the factors influencing NTS training and to design and evaluate the potential for embedding a genuine surgical training program with active teaching tools that enable the development of NTS in conjunction with TS adapted to the level of the residents in the long-term training.
