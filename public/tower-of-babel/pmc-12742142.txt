Are adverse socioeconomic conditions in childhood associated with intrinsic capacity in older age? A cross-sectional study from the Lausanne cohort Lc65+
Authors: Ekaterina Plys, Sarah Fustinoni, Mauricio Avendano, Giulia Belloni, Juan Manuel Blanco, Laurence Seematter-Bagnoud, Yves Henchoz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to assess the associations between childhood adverse socioeconomic conditions and intrinsic capacity in older adults using an integrative approach to ageing.DesignA cross-sectional study.SettingWe used data from the Lausanne Cohort 65+, a population-based longitudinal study that has been conducted in Lausanne, Switzerland, since 2004.ParticipantsLausanne citizens aged 67–71 years old.OutcomesIntrinsic capacity was assessed in 2015 using validated self-reported measures and performance tests. Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling was used in a formative approach to calculate the global score of intrinsic capacity and the scores of its domains. Adverse socioeconomic conditions in childhood were retrospectively assessed in 2014 using self-reported measures of financial strain, dietary restrictions and child labour.ResultsThe study sample included 1328 individuals. Multivariable regressions revealed that the global score of intrinsic capacity was lower in individuals who reported financial strain (B=−0.15, p=0.029) and dietary restrictions (B=−0.314, p=0.004) in childhood. Introduction

In 2015, the WHO proposed a new approach to healthy ageing as ‘the process of developing and maintaining the functional ability that enables well-being in older age’.1 Functional ability is determined by the environment in which people live and their intrinsic capacity that encompasses all physical and mental capacities.2 3 Intrinsic capacity comprehensively describes the individual’s biological and psychological health and covers five domains: locomotor capacity, sensory capacity, vitality, cognition and psychological capacity.1 Taking functional ability and intrinsic capacity into account plays a crucial role in promoting healthy ageing. This approach suggests that effort should be focused on understanding the factors responsible for preserving an individual’s abilities and capacities, rather than concentrating on the health losses that occur with age.2

Intrinsic capacity reaches its highest level in early adulthood, then declines.3 Nevertheless, intrinsic capacity shows considerable heterogeneity across individuals over the life course, especially in older adults. Early life conditions—such as socioeconomic status, nutrition, access to education, exposure to adversity and family environment—greatly contribute to an individual’s development and health1 and might be a factor for such heterogeneity. Indeed, previous research has demonstrated a negative relationship between childhood adversities and health outcomes in later life. Thus, lower socioeconomic position in childhood was associated with lower walking speed, longer chair rise time, difficulties in standing balance tasks and weaker hand grip strength in older age.4 A disadvantaged socioeconomic environment, poverty, undernutrition and violence in early life were also associated with cognitive impairment and depression in older people, with multiple adverse conditions having a greater negative effect.5,8

However, to the best of our knowledge, only a few studies have investigated variability in intrinsic capacity in relation to childhood life conditions. For example, a Chinese study has demonstrated that individuals who grew up in favourable conditions present a higher intrinsic capacity at the age of 45 years and over, particularly those whose parents are literate, who received mentoring support and adopt health protective behaviours. Poor childhood health, low neighbourhood quality and lacking friendships are factors with the most negative impact on intrinsic capacity.9

The literature has shown that adverse socioeconomic conditions in childhood can affect multiple health outcomes in later life. Importantly, previous research has predominantly focused on distinct health indicators rather than adopting a more integrative approach to ageing. To address this gap, our study aimed to investigate the relationship between adverse socioeconomic conditions experienced in early life and intrinsic capacity in older people. We hypothesised that early-life exposure to financial strain, dietary restrictions and child labour would be associated with lower intrinsic capacity in older age. Furthermore, we expected that these exposures would also be associated with lower scores in each intrinsic capacity domain.

Methods

Participants

In this cross-sectional study, we used data from the Lausanne cohort 65+ (Lc65+), an ongoing population-based longitudinal study with a cohort-sequential design. The Lc65+ study was launched in 2004 to investigate health and ageing in community-dwelling people aged 65 years and over. It includes three random samples of people who live in Lausanne, Switzerland and were born in 1934–1938, 1939–1943 and 1944–1948 (recruited in 2004, 2009 and 2014, respectively). The data collection comprised yearly self-administered questionnaires and in-person assessments carried out every 3 years by medical assistants. The response rate among eligible individuals (ie, a conditional response rate) remained between 91% and 95% over the years.10

This study included the sample of people born in 1944–1948, which allowed us to reduce the effect of drastically changing life conditions experienced by individuals born before and during World War II. We analysed data from the self-completed postal questionnaires in 2014 and 2015, as well as the in-person interview and physical examination in 2015.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or interpretation of the results of this study.

Measures

Adverse socioeconomic conditions in childhood (2014 questionnaire)

Financial strain was retrospectively assessed using two questions about the economic conditions that participants experienced from birth to 10 years of age and from 11 to 16 years of age. As both variables were strongly correlated (Spearman’s ρ=0.73, p<0.05), we combined them to create a three-level variable: 0 indicated the absence of financial strain, 1 stood for financial strain experienced during ages 0–10 or 11–16 years, and 2 referred to financial strain experienced throughout the age range from birth to 16 years. When a person described their situation as ‘difficult or very difficult’ or ‘rather difficult’, we considered that they had experienced financial strain. Reversely, when they reported having experienced a ‘rather comfortable’ or ‘comfortable or very comfortable’ situation, we took it that they did not experience any financial strain.

To assess dietary restrictions and child labour, we used two separate additional items: ‘Did you experience any dietary restrictions before the age of 16 years?’ and ‘Did you have to work in childhood more than twice a week (eg, fieldwork, deliveries)?’ Response options were ‘Yes’ (coded 1) or ‘No’ (coded 0).

Intrinsic capacity measures (2015 questionnaire and in-person assessment)

To operationalise the five intrinsic capacity domains, we selected measures based on the theoretical concept of intrinsic capacity proposed by WHO2 and their association with one of the intrinsic capacity domains in previous studies.11,15 We included both performance tests and self-reported measures to capture participants’ subjective perception of their difficulties and resources. For a detailed description of the measures, see online supplemental materials.

Locomotor capacity was measured using walking speed, timed five chair rises test16 17 and standing balance,17 as well as a self-reported 10-item questionnaire measuring mobility18 based on the capacity to perform various tasks in daily life, such as rising from a chair, climbing several flights of stairs or pulling or pushing large objects.

Sensory capacity was measured through the whispered voice test to assess hearing19 and the Landolt C chart for visual acuity.

Vitality was measured using low grip strength,20 the body mass index, the Mini Nutritional Assessment21 and a self-assessed measure of vitality that included two questions about physical fatigue and insomnia.

Cognitive capacity was assessed using four validated cognitive tests and one self-reported measure, which included the Mini–Mental State Examination (MMSE),22 the semantic Verbal Fluency (VF) Test,23 the Clock Drawing Test (Clock test)24 and the Trail Making Test (TMT). Finally, self-reported memory impairment, difficulty in concentrating and difficulty in making decisions were used to evaluate the participants’ subjective perception of their cognition.

Psychological capacity was measured by a two-question screen for depression by Whooley et al25 and single items assessing anxiety, serenity and optimism. These items come from the 36-Item Short Form Health Survey26 and the PRIME-MD.27

Scores on walking speed, timed five chair rises, self-assessed mobility, low grip strength, TMT, self-reported cognition, depressive symptoms, anxiety, serenity and optimism were reversed-coded to ensure that higher scores indicated a better performance or higher levels of psychological well-being.

Statistical analyses

Intrinsic capacity model

To calculate the global score of intrinsic capacity and the scores of its five domains, we employed the formative approach—a methodological framework that assumes that changes in the latent construct are explained (determined) by changes in measured variables.28 The formative approach implies that the selection of variables for each latent construct should be based on a theory and should include all the important variables for measuring the latent construct.29 30 As suggested by Koivunen and Schaap31 and Chhetri et al,32 this approach closely aligns with the conceptualisation of intrinsic capacity as defined by the WHO, ie, as the composite of all mental and physical capacities of the individual.1 2 33

Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM) was applied to assess the model, and to calculate the global score of intrinsic capacity and the scores of its five domains. The PLS-SEM is strongly recommended when the model contains formatively measured constructs and when the scores of the latent variables are used in further analyses.29

Our intrinsic capacity model included two parts: a higher-order construct measurement model that describes the relationships between the intrinsic capacity and its five domains, and a lower-order component measurement model that describes the relationships between the five domains and their measured variables (figure 1). A repeated-indicator approach was applied to identify the higher-order construct of intrinsic capacity (structural model) and calculate its global score.34

When assessing the model, we used the criteria suggested by Mehmetoglu and Venturini.35 Content validity was analysed in the study conceptualisation. We carefully examined the relevance of each measured variable to the intrinsic capacity construct based on the literature.211,13 Next, we examined the magnitude and statistical significance of the loading of each measured variable to assess their contribution to the model. A loading of ≥0.5 suggests that the measured variable makes a sufficient contribution and should be retained.30 Variables showing a nonsignificant magnitude of loading (<0.5) were excluded from the model after considering their theoretical importance for the intrinsic capacity construct. Although the contribution of nonsignificant measured variables is low, and their removal from the model has minimal impact on parameter estimates, they ‘should never be discarded simply on the basis of statistical outcomes’.36 Before removing a measured variable, its importance for content validity should be carefully assessed. Indeed, excluding a variable may result in overlooking part of the construct’s content. Finally, to assess multicollinearity, we applied the critical value of the variance inflation factor (VIF) equal to 2.5.35

To assess the quality of the higher-order construct measurement model (structural model), we inspected the magnitude and statistical significance of the path coefficients and conducted a collinearity test for the entire model.34 35 The magnitudes of path coefficients in the structural model were interpreted using the cut-offs suggested by Mehmetoglu and Venturini,35 where 0.05 corresponded to small effect, 0.1 to moderate effect and 0.25 to large effect.

The relationships between intrinsic capacity and socioeconomic conditions in childhood

The relationships between the global score of intrinsic capacity, the intrinsic capacity domains and adverse socioeconomic conditions in childhood were studied using separate ordinary least squares linear multivariable regression models. Each model was conducted for a specific exposure factor and was adjusted for age, sex and country of birth (Switzerland or abroad). Interactions with participants’ sex were tested.

To evaluate the impact of missing data, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) under the assumption that covariables were missing at random. Missing values, including the global intrinsic capacity score, were imputed iteratively, generating 40 complete datasets. Multiple regressions were then re-estimated on each imputed dataset, and the results were pooled using Rubin’s combination rules.37

Sample characteristics (n=1328)

Our final sample included 1328 individuals aged between 67 and 71 years (M=68.9, SD=1.4). Women represented 56.2% of the sample. Over half of the participants (68.6%) reported experiencing financial strain during childhood, with 11.2% mentioning dietary restrictions and 18.6% reporting working during childhood.

Moreover, we excluded from the main analyses 114 participants due to missing values on intrinsic capacity, sociodemographic variables or variables describing adverse socioeconomic conditions in childhood, and 209 participants as they were identified as outliers in one or more continuous characteristics.

The examination of missing value patterns revealed that excluded individuals (those having missing values or identified as outliers) reported poorer mobility, vitality, serenity and optimism, higher depressive symptoms and anxiety, as well as more frequent child labour. Intrinsic capacity model

Regarding the quality of the lower-order component measurement model, the loadings of hearing and VF were ≤0.5 and statistically nonsignificant. Recognising the importance of both hearing and vision as essential senses and their endorsement as indicators of sensory capacity,2 we decided to keep hearing in the model. By contrast, we opted to exclude VF, considering that the MMSE already provides an assessment of language function and memory. The PLS-SEM model was rerun after excluding VF, and the resulting loadings are presented in online supplemental table 3. No collinearity problem was detected (all VIFs<2.5).

All path coefficients in the higher-order construct measurement model (structural model) were statistically significant and ranged from 0.23 to 0.35 (table 2), indicating medium to large effect sizes. As expected, the signs of the coefficients were positive. The highest VIF value (1.61) indicated the absence of any collinearity problem.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to examine the relationship between intrinsic capacity as well as its five domains and adverse socioeconomic conditions in childhood in a representative sample of older adults living in Lausanne, Switzerland.

Our results showed that the global score of intrinsic capacity and locomotor and sensory capacities was significantly lower in older people who experienced financial strain from birth to the age of 16 years, compared with those who grew up in a more economically comfortable situation’. Interestingly, the latter group did not show significant differences compared with individuals who experienced financial strain only from birth to the age of 10 years or only between 11 and 16 years of age. This aligns with the cumulative approach, which suggests a dose-response relationship between the intensity and duration of adverse life conditions and individuals’ health.38 These theoretical assumptions found support in several studies. For instance, Zhao et al39 demonstrated that the global score of intrinsic capacity was poorer in older people who experienced multiple adverse socioeconomic determinants of health. Additionally, Dorji et al40 reported a significant link between the number of adverse events in childhood and the risk for lung disease, visual impairment, insomnia, blood pressure and memory decline at the age of 60 years and older. Therefore, the accumulation of adverse events appears to be a significant factor predicting poor health outcomes in later life.

The current study made a significant contribution in that it further showed that dietary restrictions in childhood negatively influenced the global score of intrinsic capacity. According to previous studies, older people who experienced famine in childhood presented lower walking speed, poorer cognitive functioning and higher scores of depressive symptoms.41 Moreover, undernutrition was associated with poor mental development and lower educational attainment in childhood, and with overweight, insulin resistance, hypertension and cardiovascular diseases in adulthood.42 43

Interestingly, child labour was negatively associated with cognitive capacity later in life. This may be related to having insufficient time and energy for studying. Indeed, prior research showed that academic achievements of working children of both sexes were lower and their performance in school was negatively associated with time spent at work.44 45 Conversely, we did not reveal any association between child labour and the global score of intrinsic capacity. According to past literature, child labour is a multifaceted phenomenon and encompasses both hazardous and non-hazardous activities. Hazardous child labour involves manipulating dangerous machinery, lifting heavy objects or exposure to heat, noise or water and is associated with adverse health outcomes.46,48 By contrast, non-hazardous child labour is associated with better child health compared with non-working children in disadvantaged communities, as it provided additional resources to families.46 47 In our study, the examples provided in the measurement item (eg, fieldwork, deliveries) likely guided participants to interpret 'work’ as activities outside the home. Moreover, we did not observe any interaction with sex, suggesting that the item was interpreted relatively consistently across genders. Nonetheless, future studies would benefit from more nuanced measures that distinguish between types of childhood work.

Interestingly, although a few previous studies operationalised the intrinsic capacity construct,1349,51 comparing their results to those of the present study proves challenging due to substantial variations in measured variables and methodology. First, although the concept of intrinsic capacity was described in a number of papers published by the WHO, there is no consensus on the measures that capture it best.31 52 Second, researchers employed two conceptually different approaches, reflective and formative, to operationalise the intrinsic capacity construct. On the one hand, the currently predominant reflective approach has an important limitation: by requiring that the measured variables be highly correlated, it potentially leads to the exclusion of theoretically meaningful measures due to their insufficient correlation with other variables in the domain. By way of illustration, this criterion was applied in the study conducted by Stolz,53 which resulted in the exclusion of the variable hearing impairment. On the other hand, the formative approach does not impose such constraints, and several authors consider it more suitable for investigating the intrinsic capacity construct.31 51 Such variations in approaches and results emphasise the need for concerted efforts to harmonise the measurement of intrinsic capacity, and to consolidate the work on identifying appropriate statistical methods.

We rigorously selected measures of intrinsic capacity based on recommendations of the WHO and previous studies. We used validated tools and performance tests to ensure the accuracy of our measures. Not only did we recognise the importance of the participants’ perception of their health and well-being, but we also included self-reported measures of mobility, vitality and cognition. We analysed the data in a formative approach deemed more suitable for studying the intrinsic capacity construct. The homogeneity of the participants’ sample, born at the end of World War II, facilitated the analysis of individuals who grew up in similar conditions. However, the narrow age range between 67 and 71 years restrained the possibilities to explore variations in intrinsic capacity by age. The excluded participants demonstrated differences in age, mobility, vitality, cognition and psychological well-being, which could lead to an overestimation of the global score of intrinsic capacity and its domains. However, since excluded individuals did not differ from study participants in terms of dietary restrictions and childhood financial strain, it is unlikely that their exclusion might impact the relationship between intrinsic capacity and adverse socioeconomic conditions in childhood. Finally, this study did not explore the effects of potential moderators or mediators such as the educational level or income in adulthood on the relationship between adverse socioeconomic conditions in childhood and intrinsic capacity.

Conclusions

In this investigation of intrinsic capacity in a formative approach, we found that early-life exposure to financial strain, dietary restrictions and child labour were negatively associated with ageing processes. These findings emphasise the role of childhood in individual development, highlighting the importance of financial support and access to nutrition for families. Additionally, our results reveal the need to harmonise measures and statistical methods used to investigate intrinsic capacity and to ensure the comparability of results.
