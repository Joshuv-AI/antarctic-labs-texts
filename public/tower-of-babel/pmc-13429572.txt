Effect of Combined Aerobic and Resistance Training on Exercise Capacity, Muscle Strength, Quality of Life, and Mortality in Patients with Coronary Heart Disease: A Systematic Review with Meta-analysis
Authors: Caroline Oliveira Gois, Alana Lalucha de Andrade Guimarães, Lino Sérgio Rocha Conceição, Mansueto Gomes-Neto, Vitor Oliveira Carvalho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCART (combined aerobic and resistance training) has shown important results in exercise-based cardiovascular rehabilitation. However, there are little data concerning the effects of CART on the exercise capacity, muscle strength, health-related quality of life (HRQoL), and mortality of coronary heart disease (CHD) patients. Therefore, the aim of this study was to analyze the published randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that investigated the effects of CART on exercise capacity [peak oxygen consumption (peak VO2) and 6-min walking distance (6MWD)], muscle strength [1-repetition maximum (1-RM), peak torque and handgrip strength], HRQoL, and mortality in patients with CHD.MethodsWe search for references in MEDLINE/PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and EMBASE databases to find RCTs that evaluated the effects of CART for CHD. Mean difference (MD), standardized mean difference (SMD), risk ratio (RR), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.ResultsCART improved relative peak VO2 of 2.20 ml/kg/min (P < 0.0001), peak torque of 16.69 N.m (P < 0.0001), handgrip strength of 4.68 kgf (P < 0.0001), and HRQoL of 1.00 (P < 0.00001) and reduced mortality (RR = 0.40; P = 0.002) compared to non-exercising usual care. CART improved (relative and absolute) peak VO2 by 0.28 (P < 0.002), 6MWD by 21.98 m (P = 0.004), 1-RM by 0.61 kg (P < 0.0001), peak torque by 4.03 N.m (P = 0.002), and HRQoL by 0.50 (P < 0.0004) compared to aerobic training. CART improved relative peak VO2 by 1.91 ml/kg/min (P < 0.00001) compared to resistance training.ConclusionCART was efficient in improving peak VO2, peak torque, handgrip strength, HRQoL, and lower mortality events compared to non-exercising usual care in patients with CHD. CART appears superior to aerobic training in improving peak VO2, 6MWD, 1-RM, peak torque, and HRQoL in patients with CHD. Furthermore, CART was more effective than resistance training for peak VO2 in patients with CHD. CART should be considered as a method in the rehabilitation of patients with CHD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40798-026-01080-3.

Background

oronary heart disease (CHD) is the most common type of heart disease and is responsible for a high mortality rate [1]. Low exercise tolerance and low health-related quality of life (HRQoL) represent a concern for people living with CHD [2,3]. There is consistent evidence about the positive effects of exercise training in CHD, such as increase in cardiorespiratory fitness, muscle strength, and HRQoL and improvement in mortality rates [1, 4–6]. Moreover, cardiorespiratory fitness has been considered a vital sign by the American Heart Association [7].

Aerobic exercise training is the most studied type of intervention in cardiovascular rehabilitation, which is endorsed by current guidelines [8,9]. Furthermore, it is known that aerobic exercise training improves peak oxygen consumption (peak VO2), peripheral vascular function, quality of life and some cardiovascular risk factors in CHD patients [10,11]. Despite that, it is reasonable to include resistance exercise training as part of cardiac rehabilitation programs for CHD [12]. Resistance exercise training is known to be effective in reducing CHD risk factors by reducing resting blood pressure, increasing insulin sensitivity and glycemic control, and improving lipid profile and body composition [13,14]. In addition, the resistance exercise training improves heart rate variability, muscle strength, and endurance in CHD patients [15].

Combined aerobic and resistance training (CART) has shown important results in exercise-based cardiovascular rehabilitation [16,17]. A meta-analysis involving patients with heart failure showed that, compared to aerobic training, CART resulted in greater improvement in muscle strength and HRQoL [18]. However, the authors did not find difference in peak VO2. A meta-analysis involving patients with CHD showed that CART (21 studies) was more efficient in improving peak VO2 and muscle strength when compared with control groups (no exercise) [19]. However, the authors included non-randomized trials and did not compare the combined training with aerobic exercise training alone [19]. In addition, since the last meta-analysis, new randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have been published. Additionally, this study expands on previous publication to potentiate the outcomes in patients living with CHD, in terms of accuracy, magnitude, and certainty of evidence.

The aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to analyze the published RCTs that investigated the effects of CART on exercise capacity [peak VO2 and 6-min walking distance (6MWD)], muscle strength [1-repetition maximum (1-RM), peak torque, and handgrip strength], HRQoL, and mortality in patients with CHD.

Methods

This study was designed and performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [20] statement and supplemented by guidance from the Cochrane collaboration Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [21]. The systematic review protocol has been registered with the PROSPERO International prospective register of systematic reviews database (CRD42024539104).

Eligibility Criteria

To establish the eligibility criteria, we considered the following PICOS elements (Participants, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study type). (1) Population: patients with CHD (any sex and any age with history of CHD with angina pectoris or myocardial infarction diagnosed by American Heart Association standard criteria [22] angiographically documented, and/or percutaneous coronary intervention); (2) Intervention: CART; (3) Comparison: control group (any type of exercise, e.g., aerobic training, resistance exercise) or no intervention (non-exercising usual care); (4) Outcome: exercise capacity (peak VO2, in ml/kg/min or in l/min in), (6MWD in meters), muscle strength (1-RM in kg, peak torque in N.m, and handgrip strength in kgf); HRQoL (total score or physical function score by Medical Outcomes Study Short Form-36, or global by EuroQol visual analogue scale, or physical domain by World Health Organization Quality of Life Brief Version or MacNew Heart Disease Health-Related Quality of Life instrument) and mortality (cardiac death and all-cause death, in years) (%); (5) Study type: RCTs.

Search Strategy

We searched for references in MEDLINE / PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and EMBASE up to May 2026. A gray literature search included Google Scholar and OpenThesis. The first 100 results of the Google Scholar search were analyzed. The search screened studies published in full-text versions or summarized versions eligible in relation to the inclusion criteria, without language restrictions. A search strategy was built using the Boolean operators “And”/“Or” in combination with the terms and its synonyms: “CHD,” “Exercise,” and “Resistance Training” (Supplementary Material 1). The reference lists of all eligible studies and reviews were also scanned to identify additional studies inclusion. We checked clinicaltrials.gov for ongoing studies. Authors of the studies were contacted by e-mail for confirmation of any data or additional information.

Quality of the Studies

The quality of the studies included in this systematic review was assessed by two researchers using the PEDro scale. Eleven domains were assessed for evaluation: (1) eligibility criteria and source of participants; (2) random allocation; (3) concealed allocation; (4) baseline comparability; (5) blinded participants; (6) blinded therapists; (7) blind assessors; (8) adequate follow-up; (9) intention-to-treat analysis; (10) between-group comparisons; and (11) point estimates and variability. Item 1 does not contribute to the total score, as it assesses the external validity of the study. These characteristics make the PEDro scale a useful tool for assessing the quality of rehabilitation trials [23–25]. Any disagreements in the rating of the studies were resolved by a third reviewer.

Data Collection

Two independent investigators (COG and ALAG) searched for relevant studies according to title and abstract using the Rayyan platform [26]. If at least one of the investigators considered one reference eligible, the full text was obtained for a complete assessment. The full texts were assessed according to the eligibility criteria by two independent investigators (COG and ALAG). Disagreements were resolved by a third investigator (MGN). Then, the following information was extracted: characteristics of study participants, intervention protocol (type, intensity, frequency, duration, and follow-up period), and outcomes data. Values of mean and standard deviation (SD) for peak VO2 in ml/kg/min or in l/min, 6MWD (meters), 1-RM (kg), peak torque (N.m), handgrip strength (kgf), HRQoL (total score or physical function score by Medical Outcomes Study Short Form-36, or global by EuroQol visual analogue scale, or physical domain by World Health Organization Quality of Life Brief Version or MacNew Heart Disease Health-Related Quality of Life instrument), and mortality (cardiac death and all-cause death in years) (%) were extracted pre- and post-intervention. In cases of missing data, the authors were contacted by email. We considered a deadline of 14 days for response.

Data Analysis and Quality of the Studies in Meta-analysis

The meta-analysis was performed using the post-intervention means and the SD for continuous data, and dichotomous data were performed using relative risk of the analyzed outcome, for each intervention group of interest. The mean difference (MD) or standardized mean difference (SMD) and risk ratio (RR) were pooled using the random-effects analysis model and variance inverse method. CART versus aerobic training, CART versus resistance exercise, CART versus non-exercising usual care were compared.

A forest plot was used to graphically present the effects sizes and 95% confidence intervals. A 2-tailed P value of < 0.05 was used to determine significance. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using by Cochran’s Q test and quantified by the I2 index [27]. Funnel plots were used to evaluate the effect of studies with smaller sample sizes for meta-analysis with 10 or more included studies [21]. All analyses were conducted using Review Manager (Version 5.4.1) [28].

The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was used for assessing quality of evidence for the outcomes in meta-analysis. Quantitative data from Cochrane’s review manager were imported into GRADEpro GDT 2015 to create a “Summary of findings table” (SoF) [29,30]. The assessment involved five items: risk of bias, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness, and publication bias. The quality of evidence was downgraded by one level for risk of bias when more than a quarter of the studies included in meta-analysis were considered at high risk of bias (studies without allocation concealment, random allocation, and/or sample size calculation). Risk of bias for the outcomes in meta-analysis was assessed according to the Cochrane guidelines for RCTs (Supplementary Material 2) [29,30]. Results were considered imprecise if the pooled sample size was < 300 for dichotomous or < 400 for continuous outcomes, and inconsistent if the heterogeneity between trials was substantial (i.e., I2 index between trials was greater than 40%) [27].

Results

Study Selection

The initial search resulted in 2126 potentially relevant titles and abstracts. After screening duplicates, we identified 1884 potentially eligible studies. Twenty-seven additional studies were identified by other sources. Ninety-two studies were considered for full-text analysis, 31 [31–60] were excluded (Supplementary Material 2) and 61 [16, 40, 61–119] RCTs were included in this systematic review. Study Characteristics

Sixty-one RCTs were included in this review. Five reports were excluded because of overlapping participants' data [40, 43–46]. The number of randomized participants in this systematic review ranged from 10 [84] to 507 [88], totaling 5388 participants. All studies included participants with CHD. However, the study by Karjalainen et al. [54] presented one of the control groups with type 2 diabetes and was excluded from this study. The age of participants ranged from 45.3 [79] to 78.5 years [67]. The prevalence of female participants was 23.4% among the 5407 included participants. The general characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1.

Among the intervention protocols of the 61 [16, 40, 61–119] included studies, 30 [16, 40, 62–64, 67, 72, 75,76, 78, 81–83, 85, 87, 89, 91–96, 99–102, 105, 107, 111,112] compared the CART versus aerobic training, 2 [62, 93] studies compared the CART versus resistance exercise, 28 [61,62, 65,66, 68–70, 73, 77, 80, 84, 86, 88, 90, 93, 97,98, 103,104, 106, 108–110, 112–115, 119] studies compared the CART versus non-exercising usual care, 13 [16, 64, 71, 74, 79, 95, 100–102, 107, 109, 111, 118] studies compared the CART versus different protocols of CART, two [116,117] studies compared CART compared to neuromuscular training, CART, one [61] study compared the CART versus water-based circuit training, and one [69] study compared the CART versus traditional Chinese tai chi exercises. Most studies reported the prescription components of physical training intervention protocols. Fifty-five [16, 40, 61–72, 74–87, 89–99, 101–104, 107–111, 113–119] studies reported training frequency, which varies from 1 to 7 days per week (average of 3 days per week among most studies). Follow-up periods ranged from 14 days to 2 years (average of 16 weeks of training among included studies). Only 41 [16, 40, 61–64, 66, 68–76, 78, 80, 82, 86,87, 89, 91, 93–98, 101–104, 106–110, 114, 116–119] studies reported supervision of the intervention protocol. Quality of the Studies

All included studies presented, in general, 59 included studies presented acceptable (20 studies) to good (42 studies) methodological quality [16, 40, 61–104, 106–112, 114–119]. Only two [105, 113] studies had low methodological quality. Furthermore, 41 [16, 40, 61–66, 71, 73,74, 77,78, 80–89, 91–94, 96, 99,100, 102, 104–109, 111–114] included studies did not report concealed allocation, and 45 [16, 40, 61,62, 64–66, 71, 73,74, 77,78, 80–89, 91–94, 96, 99,100, 102, 104–109, 111–119] studies did not report blind assessors. Twenty-one [65, 69, 75–77, 81, 83, 86, 89, 92, 94, 100, 102–106, 111–114] studies did not report intention-to-treat analysis. None [16, 40, 61–114] of the studies blinded the therapists and participants (Table 3). It is important to note that in 5 [92, 100, 105,106, 112] studies quality criteria were not reported. Discussion

The main results of our systematic review indicated that CART was efficient for the following outcomes: relative peak VO2, HRQoL, peak torque, handgrip strength and mortality (cardiac death) in patients with CHD, when compared to non-exercising usual care. Furthermore, CART was more efficient in improving overall (relative and absolute) peak VO2, HRQoL, 1-RM, peak torque and 6MWD in patients with CHD when compared to aerobic training. The CART was efficient in improving relative peak VO2 when compared to resistance training. However, results of our systematic review indicated that CART was not efficient in improving for peak torque, when compared to non-exercising usual care, and mortality events (all-cause death and cardiac death) when compared to resistance training in patients with CHD.

Common complications of CHD are symptoms and functional limitations, progression of atherosclerosis, non-fatal ischemic events and cardiac death [120]. Previous data show that patients with CHD frequently have impaired exercise capacity (peak VO2 and 6MWD) and muscle strength. It is already well established that low levels of exercise capacity are associated with impaired HRQoL, a high risk of cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality [121].

Peak VO2 is considered the gold standard method to assess exercise capacity and is related to HRQoL in participants with cardiovascular disorders [121]. In our meta-analysis we found that CART improved relative peak VO2 by 2.20 ml/kg/min and 1.9 ml/kg/min in comparison to non-exercising usual care and resistance training group, respectively. Additionally, our subgroup analysis shows that CART resulted in improvements in relative peak VO2 by 1.07 ml/kg/min in comparison to aerobic training group. However, no difference was found for subgroup analysis of absolute peak VO2. However, we suggest not generalizing the results for the subgroup analysis for absolute peak VO2, as the accuracy and validity of the results may be compromised due to the small sample size and limited number of studies included in the subanalysis [63, 72]. Therefore, our meta-analysis concluded that CART seems to be efficient in improving exercise capacity in patients with CHD.

The results of our meta-analysis were relevant and may have a strong prognostic effect in clinical practice, given that a previous meta-analysis showed than better capacity exercise was associated with lower risk of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular disease among patients with CHD [122]. Furthermore, each 1 ml/kg/min in peak VO2 increase was associated with an 11% and 15% reduction in all-cause and cardiovascular disease mortality, respectively [123].

Additionally, our meta-analysis showed that CART resulted in improvements in 6MWD of 21.98 m in comparison to aerobic training group. This meta-analysis of 6MWD showed low heterogeneity (I2 0%). A change of 14.0–30.5 m in 6MWD may be clinically important across multiple patient groups [124]. In a previous study, the six-minute walk test showed prognostic predictive ability for all-cause mortality in patients with cardiovascular disease [125]. Data from the Heart and Soul Study [126] showed that 6MWD predicted cardiovascular events in patients with stable CAD. Patients with the lowest 6MWD (87–419 m) had a fourfold higher rate of cardiovascular events than those with the highest 6MWD (544–837 m). A 104 m decrease in 6MWD was associated with a 55% higher rate of cardiovascular events [126]. Therefore, results of our meta-analysis on the 6MWD show how CART programs can benefit patients with CHD.

A previous published meta-analysis [127] examined the effects of CART on muscle strength and exercise capacity. The authors reported that CART was more effective than aerobic training in increases in lower body strength and upper body strength and improved exercise capacity in patients with CHD. In addition to being effective, the CART proved to be safe for patients with CHD [127]. In agreement, our meta-analysis showed that CART resulted in increased 1-RM compared to the aerobic training group. Furthermore, the subgroup analysis for 1-RM, according to the type of exercise for lower limbs in the CART group, showed an increase in 1-RM in leg press and leg extension exercises. Muscle strength has been linked to cardiovascular mortality risk [128]. In CHD patients, each 10% increase of maximal quadriceps isometric strength did associate with reductions of 23% and 34% in risks of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [129].

Peak torque of knee extension and knee flexion has been shown to be impaired in patients with CHD [130]. Besides, declines of peripheral muscle strength are associated with impairment of exercise capacity between patients with stable angina [131]. Knee extensor muscular endurance and its response after training were considering the strongest muscular predictors in explaining peak VO2 in CHD patients [132]. However, it is known that combining aerobic and resistance training is beneficial for the development and maintenance of neuromuscular and cardiovascular functions [133]. Finally, our results are in agreement with previous studies [134,135], that investigated the effects of CART on muscle strength in heart failure patients. In addition, our meta-analysis showed that CART improved for peak torque by 15.85 N.m and by 6.40 N.m for knee extensor in comparison to non-exercising usual care group and aerobic training group, respectively.

Handgrip strength is a predictor of overall survival and of cardiovascular events in patients with CHD [136,137]. A 5 kg increase in handgrip strength showed a lower risk of overall mortality [136]. However, the meta-analysis for handgrip strength showed substantial heterogeneity (I2 59%). Our meta-analysis showed that the CART group improved by 4.61 kgf in handgrip strength compared to the no-exercise usual care group.

In our analysis, CART did result in significant improvements in terms of HRQoL in patients with CHD, in comparison to non-exercising usual care group and aerobic training group. In previous published systematic review, the authors reported data for HRQoL for patients with CHD could not be pooled quantitatively, but all the studies that measured these outcomes reported improvements with combined training [138]. The exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation can increase HRQoL of patients with CHD [1]. In addition, the HRQoL is well known to be related to exercise capacity [139].

Our systematic review is one of the few studies that have shown the effect of CART exercise training, compared with usual care without exercise, on a lower risk of mortality events (all-cause death and cardiac death) in patients with CHD. Our meta-analysis also showed high evidence and downgraded by bias for studies that evaluated all-cause death and cardiac death events. These results corroborate previous studies [140], which show the effect of CART in patients with CHD. Furthermore, CHD remains the leading cause of death worldwide [141]. Therefore, we consider that CART can be a training option in cardiovascular rehabilitation programs.

Our systematic review did not find significant difference for peak torque and handgrip in patients with CHD after CART in comparison to aerobic training group and resistance training group. We believe that this negative result may be associated with exercise training protocols. The RCTs examined in these analyses presented different training variables, such as time, frequency, and duration protocol among studies. However, due to the small sample size and different training protocol, no conclusions can be made on this matter. Therefore, future studies about the effects of CART in peak torque and handgrip in patients with CHD are needed to assess possible clinical benefits in these outcomes.

This systematic review with meta-analysis has several strengths. Since it demonstrates the effects of CART on peak VO2, 1-RM, peak torque, HRQoL, and mortality in patients with CHD. This is the first systematic review with meta-analysis to show the effect of CART on the 6MWD and handgrip strength in patients with CHD. However, more methodologically high-quality RCTs are needed to investigate the effect of CART on 6MWD, 1-RM, peak torque, and handgrip strength compared to aerobic and resistance training, respectively, in individuals with CHD. Additionally, we encourage future studies to assess HRQoL in CART in patients with CHD. Furthermore, our systematic review suggests that future RCTs should describe the key components of aerobic and resistance training prescription (e.g., intensity, frequency, duration, volume, and progression) as this may improve understanding of the effects of CART in patients with CHD. The high heterogeneity of intervention protocols, small sample size, and limited number of included studies may have limited the results in the overall and subgroup analyses for peak VO2, 6MWD, 1-RM, peak torque, HRQoL, and mortality in this systematic review with meta-analysis.

The results of this systematic review are limited due to the low methodological quality and small sample size of the included studies. Although GRADE was applied, many included studies had low methodological quality as it reduces confidence in the observed effects. However, the criteria for methodological quality and the use of specific tools for the analysis were used to minimize the biases involved in this systematic review. Nevertheless, caution is important in interpreting the results due to the high heterogeneity among the intervention protocols and the high risk of bias among the included studies.

Conclusion

Considering the available data, our systematic review showed that CART was efficient in improving peak VO2, peak torque, handgrip strength, HRQOL, and reducing mortality events (cardiac death) compared to non-exercising usual care in patients with CHD. CART appears to be more effective than aerobic training in improving peak VO2, 6MWD, 1-RM, peak torque, and HRQoL in patients with CHD. Furthermore, CART was more effective than resistance training for peak VO2 in patients with CHD. Thus, CART appears to be a useful strategy to improve exercise capacity, muscle strength and HRQoL, as well as reducing mortality events in patients with CHD and can be seen as an option to be included in rehabilitation programs. However, more well-controlled trials are needed to understand the potential benefits of CART in this context.
