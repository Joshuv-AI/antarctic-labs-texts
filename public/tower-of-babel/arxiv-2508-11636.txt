Rigidity of Graphs and Frameworks: A Matroid Theoretic Approach
James Cruickshank, Bill Jackson, Tibor Jordán, Shin-ichi Tanigawa

Abstract
A d d -dimensional (bar-and-joint) framework ( G , p ) (G,p) consists of a graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) and a realisation p : V → ℝ d p:V\to{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d} . It is rigid if every continuous motion of the vertices which preserves the lengths of the edges is induced by an isometry of ℝ d {{\mathbb{R}}}^{d} . The study of rigid frameworks has increased rapidly since the 1970’s stimulated by numerous applications in areas such as civil and mechanical engineering, CAD, molecular conformation, sensor network localisation and low rank matrix completion. We will describe some of the main results in combinatorial rigidity theory and their applications to other areas of combinatorics, putting an emphasis on links to matroid theory.



1
Introduction

The study of the rigidity of frameworks can be traced back to a claim made by Euler in 1776 that “A closed spatial figure allows not changes, as long as it is not ripped apart.” Giving a formal proof for Euler’s claim turned out to be a challenging problem. A celebrated result of Cauchy in 1813 implies that every triangulated convex spherical surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
is rigid.
Gluck
[
40
]
showed that every generic triangulated spherical surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
is rigid.
Rather surprisingly, Connelly
[
17
,
18
]
disproved Euler’s original claim by constructing a flexible triangulated spherical surface.

We can use the language of rigidity theory to give a more precise description of these results.
A
d
-dimensional
(bar-joint) framework
is defined as a pair
(G,p)
consisting of a graph
G
and a
realisation

p:V(G)\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
We will also say that
(G,p)
is a
realisation
of
G
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Two realisations
(G,p)
and
(G,q)

of
G

in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

are
congruent

if
(G,p)
can be obtained from
(G,q)
by an isometry of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, i.e., a combination of translations, rotations
and reflections. The framework
(G,p)
is
globally rigid
if every framework which has the
same edge lengths as
(G,p)
is congruent to
(G,p)
. It is
rigid
if every continuous motion of
the vertices of
(G,p)
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
which preserves the lengths of its edges results in a framework which
is congruent to
(G,p)
, and otherwise it is
flexible
.
These concepts are illustrated in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
The 2-dimensional frameworks
(G,p_{0})
and
(G,p_{1})
are not rigid since
(G,p_{1})
can be obtained from
(G,p_{0})
by a
continuous motion of its vertices in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
which preserves all edge lengths, but
changes the distance between
v_{1}
and
v_{3}
.
The 2-dimensional framework
(G+e,p_{0})

is rigid. It is not globally rigid, however, since we can obtain a non-congruent framework with the same edge lengths by reflecting
v_{4}
in the line through
v_{1}v_{3}
.

It is straightforward to show that a realisation of a graph on the real line is rigid if and only if the graph is connected: necessity is obvious and sufficiency follows by an easy induction on the number of vertices. In contrast, Abbot
[
1
]
showed that it is NP-hard to determine whether a given

d
-dimensional framework is rigid when
d\geq 2
, and Saxe
[
93
]
showed the same holds for global rigidity when
d\geq 1
.
These problems become more tractable, however, if we restrict attention
to
generic frameworks
i.e. frameworks
(G,p)
in which the multiset of coordinates of all
points
p_{v}
,
v\in V
, is algebraically independent over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Gluck
[
40
]
showed that the rigidity of a generic framework
depends only on its underlying graph.
This allows us to define
a graph
G
as being
rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
if some (or equivalently every) generic realisation of
G

in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is rigid.
Analogous, but much deeper, results of Connelly
[
19
]
and Gortler, Healy and Thurston
[
42
]

imply that the global rigidity of a generic framework depends only on its underlying graph so we can define a graph

G
as being
globally rigid
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
if some (or equivalently every) generic realisation of
G
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is globally rigid.
The problem of finding a combinatorial characterisation of rigid or globally rigid graphs in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has been solved when
d=1,2
but is a major open problem in discrete geometry for
d\geq 3
.

Combinatorial rigidity theory has expanded rapidly since the 1970’s. It has branched out to include several other types of frameworks and has found applications in many diverse areas. The aim of this survey is to introduce the reader to some foundational results, proof techniques, and open problems in rigidity and to describe some striking applications to other areas of combinatorics. We refer the reader wishing to discover more to the notes
[
57
,
112
]
, text books
[
45
,
23
]
and survey articles on rigidity in
[
41
]
.
We will assume a knowledge of basic concepts in matroid theory and refer the reader to
[
90
]
as a source text for these.

2
Terminology and preliminary results

2.1
Graphs and matroids

We will use the term
multigraph
for graphs which may contain loops or multiple edges and will reserve the term
graph
for graphs without loops and multiple edges. Given a finite set
V
, we use
K_{V}
to denote the complete graph with vertex set
V
and
\binom{V}{2}
to denote its edge set. We say that a multigraph
G=(V,E)
is
(s,t)
-sparse
for two integers
s,t
if
|F|\leq s|V(F)|-t
for all
\emptyset\neq F\subseteq E
and
(s,t)
-tight
if it also satisfies
|E|=s|V|-t
. Given
X\subseteq V
we use
i_{G}(X)
to denote the number of edges in
G[X]
, the subgraph of
G
induced by
X
. Thus
G
is
(s,t)
-sparse if and only if
i(X)\leq s|X|-t
for all
X\subseteq V
which induce at least one edge in
G
. We will use the notation
G/uv
to denote the graph obtained from
G
by contracting an edge
e=uv
.

The following result of Edmonds
[
34
]
implies that the edge sets of the
(s,t)
-sparse subgraphs of
K_{V}
are the independent sets of a matroid on
\binom{V}{2}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
E
be a finite set and
f:2^{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
be a non-decreasing, submodular function. Put

{\cal I}_{f}:=\{F\subseteq E:|I|\leq f(I)\text{ for any $I\subseteq F$ with $I\neq\emptyset$}\}.

Then
{\mathcal{M}}_{f}:=(E,{\cal I}_{f})
is a matroid with rank function
\hat{f}:2^{E}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
given by

\hat{f}(F):=\min\left\{|F_{0}|+\sum_{i=1}^{k}f(F_{i}):\text{$F_{0}\subseteq F$ and $\{F_{1},\dots,F_{k}\}$ is a partition of $F{\setminus}F_{0}$}\right\}.

We refer to the matroid
{\mathcal{M}}_{f}
given by Edmonds’ theorem as the
matroid induced by
f
. For any finite set
V
and integers
s,t
, the
(s,t)
-sparsity matroid of
K_{V}
is the matroid on
\binom{V}{2}
induced by the function
f:F\mapsto s|V(F)|-t
for all
F\subseteq\binom{V}{2}
.

Nash-Williams
[
84
]
showed that the
(k,k)
-sparsity matroid of
K_{V}
is the matroid union of
k
copies of the cycle matroid of
K_{V}
for any integer
k\geq 1
. This implies, in particular, that it can be realised as the row matroid of a
|\binom{V}{2}|\times k|V|
-matrix
A
defined as follows. We first choose a generic map
q:\binom{V}{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{k}

and a reference ordering of the vertices in
V
. We then define the
row of
A
indexed by the edge
e=uv
with
u<v

to be

2.2
Infinitesimal rigidity and rigidity matroids

A basic step in deciding whether a given
d
-dimensional realisation of a graph
G=(V,E)
is rigid is to first linearise the problem. We consider the
measurement map

f_{G}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d|V|}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{|E|}
which maps each
p\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d|V|}
onto the vector of squared edge lengths of the framework
(G,p)
. The
rigidity matrix

R(G,p)
of the framework is the Jacobian matrix of
f_{G}
evaluated at
p
.
It is the
|E|\times d|V(G)|
matrix in which each row is indexed by an edge, sets of
d
consecutive columns are indexed by the vertices, and the row indexed by the edge
e=uv
has the form:

\hbox{}\vbox{\kern 0.86108pt\hbox{$\kern 0.0pt\kern 2.5pt\kern-5.0pt\left[\kern 0.0pt\kern-2.5pt\kern-5.55557pt\vbox{\kern-0.86108pt\vbox{\vbox{\halign{\kern\arraycolsep\hfil\@arstrut$\kbcolstyle#$\hfil\kern\arraycolsep&
\kern\arraycolsep\hfil$\@kbrowstyle#$\ifkbalignright\relax\else\hfil\fi\kern\arraycolsep&&
\kern\arraycolsep\hfil$\@kbrowstyle#$\ifkbalignright\relax\else\hfil\fi\kern\arraycolsep\cr 5.0pt\hfil\hbox{\vrule height=0.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt}$\scriptstyle$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle u$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle v$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle$\hfil\kern 5.0pt\\
5.0pt\hfil\hbox{\vrule height=0.0pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.0pt}$\scriptstyle e=uv$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle 0\dots 0$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle p_{u}-p_{v}$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle 0\dots 0$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle p_{v}-p_{u}$\hfil\kern 5.0pt&5.0pt\hfil$\scriptstyle 0\dots 0$\hfil\kern 5.0pt\crcr}}}}\right]$}}.

The space of
infinitesimal motions
of
(G,p)
is given by the right kernel of
R(G,p)
. It consists of the vectors
\dot{p}:V\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with the property that
\dot{p}_{u}-\dot{p}_{v}
is orthogonal to
p_{u}-p_{v}
for all
uv\in E
. Maxwell pointed out in his fundamental work on statics
[
80
]
that the space of infinitesimal motions will have a subspace of
trivial motions
of dimension
\binom{d+1}{2}
, generated by the infinitesimal translations and rotations of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, whenever
p(V)
affinely spans
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. He deduced that

\rank R(G,p)\leq d|V|-\binom{d+1}{2}

(1)

when
|V|\geq d+1
, and that
(G,p)
will be rigid whenever equality holds (since this will imply that every infinitesimal motion of
(G,p)
is an infinitesimal isometry of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
). We say that
(G,p)
is
infinitesimally rigid
if
|V|\geq d+1
and equality holds in (
1
), or
|V|\leq d
and
\rank R(G,p)=\binom{|V|}{2}
. The 2-dimensional framework on the right of Figure
2
is an example of a rigid framework which is not infinitesimally rigid (since it has a non-trivial infinitesimal motion which fixes each vertex of degree three and gives each vertex of degree two an arbitrary ‘infinitesimal velocity’ in the direction perpendicular to the line through its neighbours).

Asimow and Roth
[
4
]
used elementary differential geometry to show that infinitesimal rigidity is equivalent to rigidity whenever
p
is a regular point of the measurement map. Since the entries in
R(G,p)
are polynomial functions of the coordinates of
p
with integer coefficients, this gives

Theorem 2.2
.

A generic framework is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
if and only if it is infinitesimally rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Equivalently, a graph is (generically) rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
if and only if it has an infinitesimally rigid realisation in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. These observations have important algorithmic consequences: they tell us that the problem of deciding whether a graph is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
belongs to NP and, in combination with the Schwarz-Zippel Lemma
[
94
,
114
]
, give rise to a randomised polynomial algorithm for solving this problem. Unfortunately, this tells us nothing about the structure of graphs which are rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. We will use techniques from graph theory, matroid theory and geometry to obtain structural information on such graphs.

The
rigidity matroid

{\mathcal{R}}(G,p)
of a framework
(G,p)
is the row matroid of the rigidity matrix
R(G,p)
. Thus,
{\mathcal{R}}(G,p)
is a matroid on
E
in which a set of edges
F\subseteq E
is independent if the rows of
R(G,p)
indexed by
F
are linearly independent. Since the rows of
R(G,p)
are nonzero scalar multiples of the rows of an oriented edge/vertex incidence matrix of
G
when
p
is an injection from
V
to
{\mathbb{R}}
, the rigidity matroid of any such 1-dimensional realisation of
G
is just the cycle matroid of
G
.

The
d
-dimensional rigidity matroid

{\mathcal{R}}_{d}(G)
of a graph
G
is defined to be the rigidity matroid
{\mathcal{R}}(G,p)
of any generic realisation of
G
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Thus,
G
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
if and only if the rank of
{\mathcal{R}}_{d}(G)
is

d|V|-\binom{d+1}{2}
when
|V|\geq d+1
. Since every independent set in a matroid can be extended to a base, an algorithm to decide whether any given set of edges of
G
is independent in
{\mathcal{R}}_{d}(G)
would allow to determine whether
G
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

We will simplify terminology by describing subgraphs
H
of
G
using properties of their edge set in
{\mathcal{R}}_{d}(G)
. For example we will say
H
is
{\mathcal{R}}_{d}
-independent to mean
E(H)
is independent in
{\mathcal{R}}_{d}(G)
and will use
r_{d}(H)
to denote the rank of
E(H)
in
{\mathcal{R}}_{d}(G)
. We can apply (
1
) to the induced subgraphs of
G
to obtain the following graph theoretic necessary condition for
{\mathcal{R}}_{d}
-independence.

Lemma 2.3

(Maxwell’s independence criterion)
.

Suppose
G
is an
{\mathcal{R}}_{d}
-independent graph. Then
i_{G}(X)\leq d|X|-\binom{d+1}{2}
for all
X\subseteq V
with
|X|\geq d+1
.

Maxwell’s criterion is also sufficient to imply
{\mathcal{R}}_{d}
-independence when
d=1
since
{\mathcal{R}}_{1}(G)
is the cycle matroid of
G
and Maxwell’s criterion is equivalent to saying that
G
is a forest. We will see in the following sections that it remains sufficient when
d=2
(and hence the 2-dimensional rigidity matroid is equal to the
(2,3)
-sparsity matroid), but is not sufficient to imply
{\mathcal{R}}_{d}
-independence when
d\geq 3
.

2.3
Global rigidity and stress matrices

The space of
equilibrium stresses
of a
d
-dimensional framework
(G,p)
is given by the left kernel of
R(G,p)
. It consists of the vectors
\omega:E\to{\mathbb{R}}
with the property that, for all
v\in V
,
\sum_{uv\in E}\omega_{uv}(p_{u}-p_{v})={\bf 0}
, see Figure
2
. Given an equilibrium stress
\omega
of
(G,p)
we can define the corresponding stress matrix
\Omega
as the
|V|\times|V|
symmetric matrix in which the entry in row
u
and column
v
is
-\omega_{uv}
if
u\neq v
and
uv\in E
,
0
if
u\neq v
and
uv\notin E
, and the diagonal entries are chosen so that the row and column sums of
\Omega
are each equal to zero. Connelly showed in his fundamental work on global rigidity
[
22
,
19
]
that the rank of
\Omega
is at most
|V|-d-1
and that
(G,p)
is globally rigid in the case when equality holds and
p
is generic. Gortler, Healy, and Thurston
[
42
]
subsequently verified a conjecture of Connelly by showing that the converse of the second part of the previous statement also holds (when
|V|\geq d+2
). This gives us

Theorem 2.4
.

A generic
d
-dimensional framework
(G,p)
is globally rigid if and only if
G
is a complete graph on at most
d+1
vertices or
(G,p)
has a stress matrix of rank
|V|-d-1
.

Theorem
2.4
plays an analogous role for generic global rigidity as Theorem
2.2
does for generic rigidity. In particular it implies that the global rigidity of a generic
d
-dimensional framework
(G,p)
depends only on the graph
G
, and that
G
is (generically) globally rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
if and only if it has a realisation in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
which is infinitesimally rigid and has a stress matrix of rank
|V|-d-1
. This, in turn, gives rise to a randomised polynomial algorithm for deciding whether
G
is (generically) globally rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, see
[
42
,
25
]
. An example of a graph
G=(V,E)
which is not generically globally rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
but still has a realisation with a stress matrix of rank
|V|-3
is given on the right of Figure
2
.

Figure 2
:
Two 2-dimensional stressed frameworks with an associated stress matrix of maximum possible rank. The framework on the right is obtained from the one on the left by subdivisions. This operation preserves the maximum rank property of a stressed framework. The framework on the right is not infinitesimally rigid and its underlying graph does not have enough edges to be generically (globally) rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

Hendrickson
[
47
]
gave the following graph theoretic necessary conditions for a graph to be generically globally rigid. We say that a graph
G
is
redundantly rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
if
G-e
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
for all edges
e
of
G
.

Theorem 2.5
.

Suppose
G
is a graph which is globally rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Then either
G
is a complete graph on at most
d+1
vertices or
G
is
(d+1)
-connected and redundantly rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Necessity of the connectivity condition follows since, if
G
has a vertex-cut of size less than
d+1
, then, for any generic realisation, we can reflect one side of the cut in a hyperplane containing the vertices of the cut. This operation will preserve the length of all the edges but will change the distance between any two vertices on opposite sides of the cut. Hendrickson
verifies necessity of redundant rigidity by showing that, if
G
has at least
d+2
vertices and has an edge
e=uv
for which
G-e
is not rigid, then, for any generic realisation
(G,p)
,
we can continuously deform
(G-e,p)
in such a way that we preserve all its edge lengths and eventually arrive at a non-congruent framework
(G-e,p^{\prime})
which satisfies
\|p(u)-p(v)\|=\|p^{\prime}(u)-p^{\prime}(v)\|
.

We will see that Hendrickson’s necessary conditions for global rigidity are also sufficient when
d=1,2
but not for
d\geq 3
. Another necessary condition for global rigidity in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
has recently been obtained by Garamvölgyi, Gortler and Jordán
[
37
]
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
G
be a globally rigid graph in
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{d}
on at least
d+2
vertices.
Then
{\mathcal{R}}_{d}(G)
is a connected matroid.

This necessary condition is implied by Hendrickson’s conditions when
d=1,2
but not for
d\geq 3
. The combination of all three conditions is still not sufficient to imply global rigidity in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
when
d\geq 3
.

The following sufficient condition for global rigidity is given in
[
98
]
. It can be viewed as a partial converse to Theorem
2.5
.

Theorem 2.7
.

If
G=(V,E)
is a graph and
G-v
is rigid
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
for all
v\in V
, then
G
is globally rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

2.4
Inductive constructions

Proofs that a given family of graphs are rigid, or globally rigid, in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
are often inductive in nature and proceed by using a suitably chosen graph operation to reduce to a smaller graph in the family, applying induction to deduce that the smaller graph is rigid, and then using the property that the inverse of our chosen operation preserves rigidity. We will give examples of such ‘rigidity preserving operations’
in this subsection. The first two operations were introduced by Henneberg in
[
48
]
.

2.4.1
0-extensions

Given a graph
H
, the
d
-dimensional
0
-extension
operation creates a new graph
G
by adding a vertex
v
of degree at most
d
to
H
. It is well-known that the
d
-dimensional 0-extension operation preserves

{\mathcal{R}}_{d}
-independence.
This follows from the following more general statement for the rigidity matrix of an arbitrary framework. It can be easily verified using the fact that the only non-zero entries in the columns of this matrix labelled by
v
occur in the
d_{G}(v)
rows labelled by the edges incident to
v
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Suppose
(G,p)
is a framework in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
v
is a vertex in
G
.
If

\{p_{v}-p_{u}:u\in N_{G}(v)\}
is linearly independent in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

then
\rank R(G,p)=\rank R(G-v,p|_{V-v})+d_{G}(v)
.

2.4.2
1-extensions

Given a graph
H
, the
d
-dimensional
1
-extension
operation creates a new graph
G
from
H
by deleting an edge
xy
and then adding a vertex
v
and
d+1
new edges from
v
to
x
,
y
and
d-1
other vertices of
H
, see Figure
3
.

Figure 3:
The 2-dimensional
1
-extension operation.

Lemma 2.9
.

The
d
-dimensional 1-extension operation preserves both rigidity and global rigidity in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Sketch proof.

Rigidity can be verified by using a ‘special position’ argument. Suppose
H
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
G
is a 1-extension of
H
and
H=G-v+xy
.
Choose a generic framework
(H,p)
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and extend it to a framework
(G,p^{\prime})
by placing
v
on the line through
p_{x},p_{y}
. Then
(H,p)
is infinitesimally rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and we can use Lemma
2.8
to deduce that
(G+xy,p^{\prime})
is also infinitesimally rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. On the other hand, the fact that the points
p^{\prime}_{v},p^{\prime}_{x},p^{\prime}_{y}
are collinear implies that the rows of
R(G+xy,p^{\prime})
indexed by
xy,vx,vy
are minimally linearly dependent and hence
R(G+xy,p^{\prime})
and
R(G,p^{\prime})
have the same rank. This gives us an infinitesimally rigid realisation of
G
and implies that it is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Connelly
[
19
]
applied a similar special position argument to show that the 1-extension operation preserves the property of having a ‘maximum rank stress matrix’ and hence preserves global rigidity in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
by Theorem
2.4
. We refer the reader to
[
19
]
for more details.
∎

2.4.3
Vertex splitting

Suppose
z
is a vertex of a graph
H
.
The
(
d
-dimensional) vertex splitting operation
constructs a new graph
G
from
H
by deleting
z
and then adding two new vertices
u
and
v
with
N_{G}(u)\cup N_{G}(v)=N_{H}(z)\cup\{u,v\}
and
|N_{G}(u)\cap N_{G}(v)|\geq d-1
, see Figure
4
. (We can view vertex splitting as an inverse operation to contracting the edge
uv
.)
Whiteley
[
113
]
showed that
vertex splitting preserves generic rigidity in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Figure 4:
The 3-dimensional vertex splitting operation.

Lemma 2.10
.

Suppose
G
is a graph,
uv\in E
and
|N_{G}(u)\cap N_{G}(v)|\geq d-1
.
If
G/uv
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
then
G
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Sketch proof.

Whiteley’s proof uses yet another special position argument. He chooses a generic framework
(G/uv,p)
and extends it to a framework
(G,p^{\prime})
by putting
p^{\prime}_{u}=p^{\prime}_{v}=p_{z}
, where
z
is the vertex created by contracting
uv
. He then applies a limiting argument to the rigidity matrix to deduce that it has full rank for all realisations sufficiently close to this special position. We refer the reader to
[
113
]
for more details.
∎

Whiteley conjectures that an analogous result holds for global rigidity in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
as long as the vertex splitting is
non-trivial
i.e. the degrees of
u
and
v
in
G
are at least
d+1
. Connelly shows in his online notes
[
21
]
that vertex splitting preserves global rigidity whenever
G
has an infinitesimally rigid realisation in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
in which
u
and
v
are coincident. A more precise statement and proof of his result is given in
[
29
]
. Jordán and Tanigawa verify another special case of Whiteley’s conjecture in
[
62
]
by showing that a graph is globally rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
whenever it can be constructed from a globally rigid graph of maximum degree at most
d+2
by a sequence of non-trivial vertex splits.

2.4.4
Gluing and substitution

Our next two results describe situations in which we can construct a graph which is (globally) rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
by ‘gluing together’ two smaller graphs or replacing one subgraph by another subgraph.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
G
be a graph and
G_{1},G_{2}
be subgraphs of
G
with
V(G_{1})\cap V(G_{2})=X
,
G=G_{1}\cup G_{2}
and
V(G_{1}){\setminus}V(G_{2})\neq\emptyset\neq V(G_{2}){\setminus}V(G_{1})
.

(a) Suppose
G_{1},G_{2}
are rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Then
G
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
if and only if
|X|\geq d
.

(b) Suppose
G_{1},G_{2}
are globally rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Then
G
is globally rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
if and only if
|X|\geq d+1
.

Sketch proof.

Necessity is obvious since, for any generic realisation
(G,p)
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we can rotate a component of
G-X
about a
(d-2)
-dimensional affine subspace containing
p(X)
if
|X|<d
, and reflect it in a
(d-1)
-dimensional affine subspace containing
p(X)
if
|X|=d
. We refer the reader to
[
112
]
for proofs of sufficiency.
∎

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
G_{i}=(V_{i},E_{i})
be a graph for
i=1,2,3
, with
V_{1}\cap V_{2}=X=V_{1}\cap V_{3}
.

(a) If
G_{1}\cup G_{2}
and
G_{3}
are both rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
|X|\geq d
, then
G_{1}\cup G_{3}
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

(b) If
G_{1}\cup G_{2}
and
G_{3}
are both globally rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
|X|\geq d+1
, then
G_{1}\cup G_{3}
is globally rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Sketch proof.

(a) Choose generic realisations
p
of
G_{1}\cup G_{2}
and
q
of
G_{1}\cup G_{3}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
p|_{G_{1}}=q|_{G_{1}}
. Let
\dot{q}
be an infinitesimal motion of
(G_{1}\cup G_{3},q)
. Since
G_{3}
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
we may assume (by composing
\dot{q}
with a suitable trivial infinitesimal motion) that
\dot{q}|_{G_{3}}=0
. Then
\dot{q}
corresponds to an infinitesimal motion
\dot{p}
of
(G_{1}\cup G_{2},p)
with
\dot{p}|_{G_{1}}=\dot{q}|_{G_{1}}
and
\dot{p}|_{G_{2}}=0
. Since
G_{1}\cup G_{2}
is rigid and

this gives
\dot{p}=0
. Hence
\dot{q}=0
and
G_{1}\cup G_{3}
is rigid.

Part (b) can be proved similarly.
∎

2.5
Linked pairs of vertices

Let
(G,p)
be a
d
-dimensional framework. We say a pair of vertices
\{u,v\}
of
G
are
linked
in
(G,p)
if every continuous motion of its vertices in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
which preserves the lengths of its edges, preserves the distance between
u
and
v
. Thus
(G,p)
is rigid if and only if all its pairs of vertices are linked. In the case when
p
is generic,
\{u,v\}
is linked in
(G,p)
if and only
r_{d}(G+uv)=r_{d}(G)
; this follows from the fact that every infinitesimal motion of a generic framework can be extended to a finite motion. Hence ’
\{u,v\}
-linkedness’ is a generic property and we can define a pair of vertices
\{u,v\}
of
G
to be
linked (in
G
) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
if they are linked in every (or equivalently some) generic realisation of
G
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Figure 5
:
Two pairs of generic realisations of a graph
G
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. The vertex pair
\{u,v\}
is globally linked in the two frameworks on the left since these are the only two non-congruent realisations of
G
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with these edge lengths and the distance between
u
and
v
is the same in both of them. On the other hand,
\{u,v\}
is not globally linked in the two frameworks on the right since they have the same edge lengths and the the distance between
u
and
v
is different. Thus
\{u,v\}
is weakly globally linked, but not globally linked in
G
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

Similarly, we say that
\{u,v\}
is
globally linked
in
(G,p)
if, for every framework
(G,q)
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
which has the same edge lengths as
(G,p)
, we have
\|q(u)-q(v)|=\|p(u)-p(v)\|
. It is not so straightforward to define the generic version of this concept since
\{u,v\}
can be globally linked in one generic realisation of
G
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and not in another, see Figure
5
.
Hence we say
\{u,v\}
is
globally linked (in
G
) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

if
\{u,v\}
is globally inked in
(G,p)
for
all
generic
p:V\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and

\{u,v\}
is
weakly globally linked (in
G
) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

if
\{u,v\}
is globally inked in
(G,p)
for
some
generic
p:V\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. It follows immediately that
G
is globally rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
if and only every pair of vertices of
G
is globally linked in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Somewhat surprisingly, Jordán and Villányi
[
63
]
have recently shown that global rigidity can also characterised by weak global linkedness.

Theorem 2.13
.

A graph
G
is globally rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
if and only if every pair of vertices of
G
is weakly globally linked in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

3
Rigidity and global rigidity in dimensions at most two

3.1
Rigidity

We have already seen that Maxwell’s independence criterion characterises rigidity on the line. Pollaczek-Geiringer
[
91
]
showed that it also characterises generic rigidity in the plane in 1935 but her result went largely unnoticed until it was rediscovered by Laman
[
73
]
in 1970.
Pollaczek-Geiringer’s original paper was brought to the attention of the current day rigidity community by Brigitte Servatius in 2010.

Theorem 3.1
.

A graph with at least two vertices is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
if and only if it has a spanning subgraph
G=(V,E)
which satisfies
|E|=2|V|-3
and
i_{G}(X)\leq 2|X|-3
for all
X\subseteq V
with
|X|\geq 2
.

Necessity follows from Lemma
2.3
. To prove sufficiency it will suffice to show that every
(2,3)
-tight graph is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
We will sketch two proofs of this statement to illustrate proof techniques which are commonly used to verify rigidity.

First proof of Theorem
3.1

Let
G=(V,E)
be a
(2,3)
-tight graph. We show that
G
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
using the inductive strategy outlined in Section
2.4
.
Since
G
is
(2,3)
-tight,
G
has a vertex
v
of degree two or three. If
d_{G}(v)=2
then
G-v
is
(2,3)
-tight, so is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
by induction, and we can now apply Lemma
2.8
to deduce that
G
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Hence we may assume that
N_{G}(v)=\{x,y,z\}
. It will suffice to show that
G-v+uw
is
(2,3)
-tight for some
u,w\in\{x,y,z\}
since we can then deduce that
G
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
by applying induction and Lemma
2.9
to
G-v+uw
.

Suppose for a contradiction that
G-v+uw
is not
(2,3)
-tight for all
u,w\in\{x,y,z\}
. Let
M
be the
(2,3)
-sparsity matroid of
K_{V}
. We have
r_{M}(G)=|E|
and
r_{M}(G-v)=|E|-3
since
G
is
M
-independent. The assumption that
G-v+uw
is
M
-dependent for all
u,w\in\{x,y,z\}
now gives
r_{M}(G-v+xy+yz+xz)=r_{M}(G-v)
. Since
E(K_{4})
is a circuit in
M
, this implies that
r_{M}(G+xy+yz+xz)\leq r_{M}(G-v)+2=r_{M}(G)-1
, and contradicts the fact that
r_{M}
is non-decreasing. ∎

Our second proof of Theorem
3.1
is a version of a proof by Lovász and Yemini
[
78
]
which was given by Whiteley in
[
111
]
.

Second proof of Theorem
3.1

Let
G=(V,E)
be a
(2,3)
-tight graph. We will show that
G
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
by constructing an infinitesimally rigid realisation of
G
. Choose distinct vertices
xy\in E
and let
G^{xy}=(V,E^{xy})
be the multigraph obtained by adding a new copy of
xy
to
G
. The hypothesis that
G
is
(2,3)
-tight implies that
G^{xy}
is
(2,2)
-tight. Choose a generic map
q:E^{xy}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and a reference ordering for the vertices in
V
. Let
A^{xy}
be the
|E^{xy}|\times 2|V|
-matrix in which the entry in the row indexed by the edge
e=uw
with
u<w
and columns indexed by the vertex
v
is
q(e)
if
v=u
,
-q(e)
if
v=w
and
\bf 0
if
v\neq u,w
. Then
\rank A^{xy}=|E^{xy}|
by the discussion in the last paragraph of Section
2.1
. Thus, if
A
is the matrix obtained by deleting row
xy
from
A^{xy}
, then we have
\rank A^{xy}=\rank A+1
. This implies that there exists a vector
\alpha^{xy}\in\ker A
with
\alpha^{xy}_{x}\neq\alpha^{xy}_{y}
. By taking a suitable linear combination of the vectors
\alpha^{xy}
, we can construct a vector
\alpha\in\ker A
with the property that
\alpha_{x}\neq\alpha_{y}
for all
xy\in E
. We can now define our desired framework
(G,p)
by putting
p_{v}=\alpha_{v}^{\perp}
for each
v\in V
, where
(a,b)^{\perp}:=(-b,a)
. Then each row of
R(G,p)
will be a non-zero scalar multiple of the corresponding row of
A
. Hence
\rank R(G,p)=\rank A=2|V|-3
and
(G,p)
is infinitesimally rigid. ∎

Once we know that the 2-dimensional rigidity matroid is equal to the
(2,3)
-sparsity matroid, Theorem
2.1
immediately gives us an expression for the rank function of this matroid. Lovász and Yemini
[
78
]
showed that this expression can be simplified to

r_{2}(G)=\min_{\mathcal{X}}\left\{\sum_{X\in{\mathcal{X}}}(2|X|-3)\right\}

(2)

where the minimum is taken over all families
{\mathcal{X}}
of subsets of
V
, each of cardinality at least two, which cover
E
and are
1
-thin
i.e. they satisfy
|X_{i}\cap X_{j}|\leq 1
for all distinct
X_{i},X_{j}\in{\mathcal{X}}
. In addition they show that the right hand side of (
2
) achieves its minimum value when we take
{\mathcal{X}}
to be the vertex sets of the
2
-dimensional rigid components of
G
i.e. the maximal subgraphs of
G
which are rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

3.2
Global rigidity

It is not difficult to see that a graph
G=(V,E)
is globally rigid on the line if and only if
G
is a complete graph on at most two vertices or is
2
-connected. Necessity follows from Lemma
2.11
.
(Note that the redundant rigidity condition of Theorem
2.5
follows from the connectivity condition in this 1-dimensional case.) We can prove sufficiency by induction on
|E|
. We may assume that
|V|\geq 4
and that
G
is minimally 2-connected i.e.
G-e
is not 2-connected for all
e\in E
. Choose an ear decomposition of
G
. Since
G
is minimally 2-connected, the last ear of this ear decomposition is an induced path of length at least two. Let
v
be an internal vertex of this last ear and
x,y
be the neighbours of
v
in
G
. Then
G-v+xy
is 2-connected and hence is globally rigid on the line by induction. We can now apply Lemma
2.9
to deduce that
G
is globally rigid on the line.

A similar inductive proof technique was used in
[
50
]
to show that Hendrickson’s necessary conditions for global rigidity are also sufficient when
d=2
, based on the generalisation of ear decompositions from graphs to matroids given by Coullard and Hellerstein
[
26
]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

A graph
G
is globally rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
if and only if
G
is a complete graph on at most
3
vertices or
G
is
3
-connected and redundantly rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

The key idea in the proof of sufficiency in
[
50
]
is to
show that the 2-dimensional rigidity matroid of a
3
-connected, redundantly rigid graph
G=(V,E)
is connected and then take an ear decomposition of
{\mathcal{R}}_{2}(G)
.
We can assume inductively that
G-e
is not both
3
-connected and redundantly rigid, and also, by a previous result of Berg and Jordán
[
8
]
, that
G
is not an
{\mathcal{R}}_{2}
-circuit. We next show that the subgraph of
G
induced by the last ear in the ear decomposition of
{\mathcal{R}}_{2}(G)
contains a vertex
v
of degree 3 such that
{\mathcal{R}}_{2}(G-v+xy
) is connected (and hence
G-v+xy
is redundantly rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
) for some
x,y\in N_{G}(v)
. The argument is rather technical and an additional technical argument is needed to show we can find such a vertex
v
such that
G-v+xy
is 3-connected. Once we have such a vertex we can apply induction to
G-v+xy
and then use Lemma
2.9
to deduce that
G
is globally rigid.

A simplified version of this proof appeared in
[
98
]
. A different, and even simpler, proof based on Theorem
2.13
has recently been given by Jordán and Villányi in
[
63
]
. In fact, they go further and obtain a complete characterisation of when two vertices in a graph are weakly globally linked in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. The related problem of characterising when two vertices in a graph are globally linked in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is one of the few remaining open problems for generic rigidity in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. A conjectured characterisation, together with a proof of sufficiency and other partial results, can be found in
[
53
,
54
]
.

4
Rigidity and global rigidity in higher dimensions

The problem of characterising graphs which are generically rigid or globally rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is open for all
d\geq 3
. We will investigate how the rank formula for the 2-dimensional rigidity matroid given in (
2
) may be extended to higher dimensions. There are many results which show that special families of graphs are rigid in higher dimensions. We refer the reader to the comprehensive survey articles on rigidity in
[
41
]
for information on these results.

4.1
Rigidity in 3-space

We first note that Maxwell’s necessary condition, Lemma
2.3
, is not sufficient to imply
{\mathcal{R}}_{3}
-independence. The so called
double banana graph
of Figure
6
satisfies Maxwell’s condition for
{\mathcal{R}}_{3}
-independence. Since it has
18
edges, it would be rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
if it were
{\mathcal{R}}_{3}
-independent and this would contradict Lemma
2.11
(a).

Figure 6:
The double banana graph.

To motivate a conjectured characterisation of rigidity in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, we return to the expression for the rank function of the 2-dimensional rigidity matroid of a graph
G=(V,E)
given in (
2
) and the result of
[
78
]
that the right hand side of (
2
) achieves its minimum value when we take the 1-thin cover
{\mathcal{X}}
to be the vertex sets of the 2-dimensional rigid components of
G
.
It is easy to see that
r_{2}(G)
is at most
\sum_{X\in{\mathcal{X}}}(2|X|-3)
for any cover
{\mathcal{X}}
of
E
with vertex sets of size at least two: this follows from the
fact that any base of
{\mathcal{R}}_{2}(G)
can contain at most
2|X|-3
edges in
G[X]
for each
X\in{\mathcal{X}}
.

A similar reasoning tells us that

r_{3}(G)\leq\min\left\{|F|+\sum_{X\in{\mathcal{X}}}(3|X|-6)\right\}

(3)

where the minimum is taken over all
F\subseteq E
and all covers
{\mathcal{X}}
of
E{\setminus}F
with sets of size at least three.

Equality does not hold in (
3
) when we take
G=(V,E)
to be the double banana graph in Figure
6
since, for each
X\subseteq V
with
|X|\geq 3
, we have
i_{G}(X)\leq 3|X|-6
and hence the right hand side of (
3
) is at least
|E|=18
. We can, however, obtain a tight upper bound on
r_{3}(G)
if we take account of ‘hinges’ in the cover. Consider the graph
G+uv
and let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be the two copies of
K_{5}
in
G+uv
. Let
B
be a base of
{\mathcal{R}}_{2}(G+uv)
which contains
uv
and let
B_{i}=B\cap E(G_{i})
for
i=1,2
. Then
|B_{i}|\leq 3|V(G_{i})|-6=9
for each
i=1,2
. Since
uv\in B_{1}\cap B_{2}
, this gives
r_{3}(G)=|B|\leq|B_{1}|+|B_{2}|-1=17
. (We can also show that
r_{2}(G)\geq 17
by choosing an arbitrary edge of
G
and showing that
G-e
can be constructed recursively from a path of length two by applying the 3-dimensional
0
- and
1
-extension operations. Or equivalently, that
G-e
can be reduced to a path of length two by their inverse operations.)

We can generalise the argument in the preceding paragraph to obtain a better upper bound on
r_{3}(G)
for an arbitrary graph
G=(V,E)
. Given a cover
{\mathcal{X}}
of
E
, we say that
{\mathcal{X}}
is
t
-thin
if any two distinct sets in
{\mathcal{X}}
intersect in at most
t
vertices, and define the
set of hinges of
{\mathcal{X}}
to be

H({\mathcal{X}})=\left\{\{x,y\}\in\binom{V}{2}:\{x,y\}\subseteq X_{1}\cap X_{2}\mbox{ for two distinct sets }X_{1},X_{2}\in{\mathcal{X}}\right\}.

For each hinge
h\in H({\mathcal{X}}\}
, the
degree of
h
in
{\mathcal{X}}
,
d_{\mathcal{X}}(h)
, is the number of sets in
{\mathcal{X}}
which contain
h
.
The
{\mathcal{R}}_{3}
-closure of
G
,
\cl_{3}(G)
, is the graph obtained from
G
by adding an edge between all non-adjacent pairs of vertices which are linked in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
(equivalently, adding all edges
uv
which satisfy
r_{3}(G+uv)=r_{3}(G)
to
G
). We will refer to the maximal cliques in
\cl_{3}(G)
as the
rigid clusters
of
G
. Dress et al
[
31
]
conjectured that the cover of
E
given by the rigid clusters of
G
determines
r_{3}(G)
:

Conjecture 4.1

(Dress Conjecture)
.

For any graph
G

r_{3}(G)=|F|+\sum_{X\in{\mathcal{X}}}(3|X|-6)-\sum_{h\in H({\mathcal{X}})}(d_{\mathcal{X}}(h)-1)

(4)

where
F
is the set of edges of
G
which belong to rigid clusters of cardinality two and
{\mathcal{X}}
is the set of rigid clusters
of cardinality at least three.

This conjecture is of strong theoretical interest but it would not immediately allow us to calculate
r_{3}(G)
since it does not tell us how to determine
\cl_{3}(G)
. Dress et al offered a second conjecture in
[
31
]
which would imply that the problem of determining whether
G
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
belongs to NP
\cap
coNP. They conjectured that
r_{3}(G)
is equal to
\min\{|F|+{\rm val}({\mathcal{X}})\}
where the minimum
is taken over all
F\subseteq E
and all
2
-thin covers
{\mathcal{X}}
of
E{\setminus}F
with vertex sets of cardinality at least three, and

{\rm val}({\mathcal{X}}):=\sum_{X\in{\mathcal{X}}}(3|X|-6)-\sum_{h\in H({\mathcal{X}})}(d_{\mathcal{X}}(h)-1)\}.

Unfortunately this conjecture turned out to be spectacularly false: it was shown in
[
52
]
that
\min\{|F|+{\rm val}({\mathcal{X}})\}
is negative for all graphs with 56 vertices.

We can try to repair the second Dress conjecture by restricting the pairs
(F,{\mathcal{X}})
over which the minimum is taken to more closely resemble the cover of
E

given by the rigid clusters of
G
. Lemma
2.11
implies that the set
{\mathcal{X}}
of rigid clusters of
G
is 2-thin. In addition, Theorem
4.3
below gives some evidence that
{\mathcal{X}}
is
{\mathcal{R}}_{3}
-degenerate
i.e.
{\mathcal{X}}

can be ordered as
(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{t})
in such a way that, for all
2\leq i\leq t
, the set of hinges of the subfamily
\{X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{i}\}
which are contained in
X_{i}
is
{\mathcal{R}}_{3}
-independent (when viewed as a set of edges of
K_{V}
).

It had already been shown in
[
51
, Lemma 5 and Conjecture 3.2]
that, for any graph
G=(V,E)
,

5.1

Tools from rigidity theory, along with matroid and graph theoretic methods,

It follows from a classical theorem of Nash-Williams

It follows from the above mentioned result of Nash-Williams that

[

2
-connected orientations of Eulerian graphs. Thoughout this section, we will say that a graph

-rigid but

-rigid for all edges

Every

5.2
Rigidity and simplicial complexes

An
abstract simplicial complex

\Delta
is a finite family of sets with the property that
\tau\subseteq\sigma\in\Delta
implies that
\tau\in\Delta
. An element
\sigma\in\Delta
is called a
face of dimension
|\sigma|-1
, or a
(|\sigma|-1)
-face
for short, and we define
\dim(\Delta)=\max\{\dim\sigma:\sigma\in\Delta\}
. A
facet
of
\Delta
is a maximal face and we say that
\Delta
is
pure
if all its facets have the same dimension. We write
{\mathcal{F}}_{j}(\Delta)
for the set of
j
-faces of
\Delta
and put
f_{j}(\Delta)=|{\mathcal{F}}_{j}(\Delta)|
. The
f
-vector of
\Delta
is
f(\Delta)=(f_{0},f_{1},\ldots)
.
The
graph of
\Delta
is
G(\Delta)=({\mathcal{F}}_{0}(\Delta),{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(\Delta))
and so elements of
{\mathcal{F}}_{0}(\Delta)
, respectively
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(\Delta)
are called
vertices
, respectively
edges
, of
\Delta
. Abusing notation, we will write
v
instead of
\{v\}
for the vertex whose single element is
v
and, similarly
uv
instead of
\{u,v\}
. The
link
of a face
\sigma\in\Delta
is defined by
\lk_{\Delta}(\sigma)=\{\tau\setminus\sigma:\tau\in\Delta,\tau\supseteq\sigma\}
.

5.2.1
Simplicial circuits and Fogelsanger’s Theorem

Given a finite set
X
and positive integer
k
, the
k
-simplicial matroid

{\mathcal{S}}_{k}(X)
is the row matroid of the
\binom{|X|}{k+1}\times\binom{|X|}{k}
matrix
I
, defined over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
, in which the rows are indexed by
\binom{X}{k+1}
, the columns are indexed by
\binom{X}{k}
and the
(\sigma,\tau)
-entry is
1
if
\tau\subset\sigma
and
0
otherwise.
Clearly
{\mathcal{S}}_{1}([n])
is the graphic matroid on
K_{n}
. See
[
108
, Chapter 10]
for more information on simplicial matroids.
2
2

2

In Welsh’s notation the
(k+1)
-simplicial matroid is what we call
{\mathcal{S}}_{k}(X)
. Our notation is more in line with the topological convention that the dimension of a face
\sigma
is
|\sigma|-1
.

A
simplicial
k
-circuit
is a pure
k
-dimensional simplicial complex
\Delta
such that
{\mathcal{F}}_{k}(\Delta)
is a circuit in
{\mathcal{S}}_{k}({\mathcal{F}}_{0}(\Delta))
. For example, a cycle graph is a simplicial 1-circuit. More explicitly we have the following characterisation.

Lemma 5.8
.

An abstract simplicial complex
\Delta
is a simplicial
k
-circuit if and only if
{\mathcal{F}}_{k}(\Delta)\neq\emptyset
, every
(k-1)
-face of
\Delta
is contained in an even number of
k
-faces, and no proper subcomplex of
\Delta
has these properties.

In particular, for
d\geq 2
, the boundary complex of simplicial convex polytope is a simplicial
(d-1)
-circuit.
A more general example is the following. A
k
-pseudomanifold
is a pure
k
-dimensional simplicial complex
\Pi
such that

1.

every
(k-1)
-face of
\Pi
belongs to exactly two
k
-faces of
\Pi
, and

2.

\Pi
is strongly connected i.e. if
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{k}(\Pi)
then there is a sequence of
k
-faces
\sigma=\tau_{1},\cdots,\tau_{s}=\sigma^{\prime}
such that
|\tau_{i}\cap\tau_{i+1}|=k
for
i=1,\cdots,s-1
.

In particular, every triangulation of a
k
-dimensional manifold is a
k
-pseudomanifold.
3
3

3

Recall that a triangulation of a manifold is a simplicial complex which has a homeomorphism from its geometric realisation to the given manifold.

It follows easily from Lemma
5.8
that a
k
-pseudomanifold is a simplicial
k
-circuit for all
k\geq 1
, although the converse is not necessarily true when
k\geq 2
.

Theorem 5.9

(Fogelsanger’s Rigidity Theorem)
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial
k
-circuit for some
k\geq 2
. Then
G(\Delta)
is rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{k+1}
.

The proof is an inductive argument based on edge contraction/vertex splitting. Given two vertices
u,v
of a simplicial complex
\Delta
, we define
\Delta/uv
to be the complex obtained from
\Delta
by deleting every face that contains
\{u,v\}
and then replacing every face
\sigma
that contains
v
by
\sigma\setminus\{v\}\cup\{u\}
. An immediate difficulty for the induction is that edge contraction does not necessarily preserve the property of being a simplicial circuit. Fogelsanger discovered a key decomposition argument to circumvent this problem.

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial
k
-circuit for some
k\geq 2
,
uv\in{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(\Delta)
and suppose that
\Delta/uv
is not a simplicial
k
-circuit. Then there exist simplicial
k
-circuits
\Delta_{1},\cdots,\Delta_{m}
such that

(F1)

G(\Delta)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}G(\Delta_{i})
,

(F2)

uv\in\Delta_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq m
, and, either
\Delta_{i}
is the boundary complex of a
(k+1)
-simplex or
\Delta_{i}/uv
is a simplicial
k
-circuit,

(F3)

for
2\leq j\leq m
,
{\mathcal{F}}_{k}(\Delta_{j})\cap\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{j-1}{\mathcal{F}}_{k}(\Delta_{i})\right)\neq\emptyset
.

In fact Fogelsanger proved more general versions of Theorems
5.9
and Lemma
5.10
for
minimal homology cycles
over an arbitrary field. These coincide with our simplicial circuits when the field is
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
. See
[
35
]
for details.

Next we outline the proof of Theorem
5.9
,

Sketch proof.

Pick
uv\in{\mathcal{F}}_{1}(\Delta)
.
If
\Delta/uv
is again a simplicial
k
-circuit then by induction on the number of vertices
G(\Delta)/uv=G(\Delta/uv)
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{k+1}
. Now, since
\Delta
is a simplicial
k
-circuit,
\{u,v\}
is contained in at least two
k
-faces. This implies that
u
and
v
have at least
k
common neighbours in
G(\Delta)
and so, by Lemma
2.10
,
G
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{k+1}
.

Thus we can assume that
\Delta/uv
is not a simplicial
k
-circuit. Let
\Delta_{1},\cdots,\Delta_{m}
be the simplicial
k
-circuits whose existence is asserted by Lemma
5.10
. By (F2) and using the induction argument of the previous paragraph, we see that
G(\Delta_{i})
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{k+1}
for each
i
. Now (F3) implies that, for
2\leq j\leq m
,
\bigcup_{i=1}^{j-1}G(\Delta_{i})
and
G(\Delta_{j})
have at least
k+1
common vertices so an induction argument using Lemma
2.11
and (F1) proves that
G(\Delta)
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{k+1}
.
∎

The following result on the global rigidity of simplicial
k
-circuits was recently obtained in
[
27
]
.

Theorem 5.11
.

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial
k
-circuit for
k\geq 2
.

1.

If
k\geq 3
then
G(\Delta)
is globally rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{k+1}
if and only if
G(\Delta)
is
(k+2)
-connected or is a copy of

.

2.

If

then
G(\Delta)
is globally rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
if and only if

is
4
-connected and not planar, or,

is a copy of

.

The proof of Theorem
5.11
is again an induction based on vertex splitting and on Fogelsanger’s decomposition. However there are significant extra complications that arise from controlling the
(k+2)
-connectedness in the inductive arguments, and also from the fact that, as mentioned in Section

, the natural analogue of Lemma

for global rigidity is still a conjecture. For more details see
[

]
.

Theorems
5.9
and
5.11
have some important consequences for the theory of
f
-vectors of simplicial
k
-polytopes and more generally of
k
-pseudomanifolds. It is natural to wonder what is the smallest possible number of
j
-faces in a simplicial
k
-polytope with a given number of vertices.
For
0\leq j\leq k\leq n-2
define

\phi_{j}(k,n)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\binom{k+1}{j}n-j\binom{k+2}{j+1}&\text{ if }j<k\\
kn-(k-1)(k+2)&\text{ if }j=k.\end{array}\right.

The number
\phi_{j}(k,n)
is the numbers of
j
-faces in a
stacked
k
-spheres
. This family of
k
-polytopes can be defined recursively as follows. The smallest
stacked
k
-sphere
is the boundary complex of the
(k+1)
-dimensional simplex.
For
n\geq k+3
a stacked
k
-sphere with
n
vertices is a simplicial complex obtained by deleting one of the facets of a stacked
k
-sphere with
n-1
vertices and adding a new vertex whose link is the boundary complex of the deleted facet. An easy induction argument proves that if
\Sigma
is a stacked sphere with
n
vertices then
f_{j}(\Sigma)=\phi_{j}(k,n)
. The following theorem is due to Barnette
[
6
,
5
]
.

Theorem 5.12

(The Lower Bound Theorem)
.

Let
k\geq 2
be an integer and
\Delta
be the boundary complex of a simplicial
k+1
dimensional polytope with
n
vertices. Then, for
0\leq j\leq k
,

f_{j}(\Delta)\geq\phi_{j}(k,n).

(10)

Moreover, if
k\geq 3
and equality holds in (
10
) for some
1\leq j\leq k
then
\Delta
is a stacked
k
-sphere. If
k=2
then equality holds if and only if
\Delta
is a triangulation of the 2-sphere.

Sketch proof.

We outline a proof of the inequality using rigidity that is due to Kalai
[
67
]
. The use of global rigidity to characterise the case of equality is taken from
[
27
]
.

It is well known that the link of a vertex of
\Delta
is
the boundary complex of a simplicial polytope of dimension
k
. Based on this fact, McMullen, Perles and Walkup
[
81
]
gave an inductive argument to show that we can reduce the proof of
the theorem to the case
j=1
. In this case (
10
) becomes

f_{1}(\Delta)\geq(k+1)n-\binom{k+2}{2}.

(11)

Let
p:{\mathcal{F}}_{0}(\Delta)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{k+1}
be the embedding of the vertices that arises naturally from the given polytope. Note that sufficiently small perturbations of a simplicial polytope do not change its boundary complex, so we assume that
p
is generic from now on.
From Theorem
5.9
the framework
(G(\Delta),p)
is
infinitesimally rigid. Now (
11
) follows immediately from the fact that
equality holds in (
1
) for infinitesimally rigid frameworks.

To deal with with the case of equality when
k\geq 3
, we observe that if equality holds in (
11
) then the framework
of the polytope is minimally rigid. If
f_{0}(\Delta)\geq k+2
then Theorem
2.5
implies that
(G(\Delta),p)
is not globally rigid. Since
p
is generic Theorem
5.11
implies that

G(\Delta)

is not
(k+2)
-connected. Now it can be be shown that in this case
\Delta
is obtained by gluing two smaller complexes together along a common facet, whose set of vertices is a
(k+1)
-vertex separator for
G(\Delta)
, and removing the common facet. It also follows that each of these two smaller complexes is the boundary complex of a polytope and that equality in (
11
) holds in both cases, so by induction each of these complexes is a stacked sphere and therefore
\Delta
is also a stacked sphere.
The case of equality for
k=2
follows by a similar argument.
∎

Some historical comments.

Rigidity of frameworks associated to polytopes is a very classical topic, dating at least to Cauchy’s Rigidity Theorem for 3-dimensional convex polyhedra

]
.
Dehn proved an infinitesimal rigidity version of Cauchy’s Theorem

[

[

.
Whiteley extended Alexandrov’s theorem by showing that, for all

2

[
110
]
.
Kalai
[

]
proved Theorem
5.9
for triangulations of
manifolds of dimension at least

and used it to extend the Lower Bound Theorem to triangulated manifolds. Tay subsequently extended the Lower Bound Theorem to the class of normal pseudomanifolds
[
99
]
.
Following Kalai’s paper
[
67
]
,
rigidity properties and lower bound theorems have been investigated for several other classes of complexes including balanced complexes
[
71
,
89
]
, cubical complexes
[
69
]
and symmetric complexes
[

,
28
]
.
As far as we are aware the following is still open.

.

be an integer and

5.2.2
Connections with commutative algebra

In this section we will outline some connections between rigidity theory and combinatorial commutative algebra. We will assume that the reader is familiar with the basic concepts and terminology of commutative algebra. Since we are focused on the connection with rigidity, all of the rings that we discuss in this section will be
{\mathbb{R}}
-algebras. However many of the algebraic concepts and results are also valid over other fields.
Let
{\mathbb{R}}[X]={\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}]
be the polynomial ring in
n
commuting variables.
For
\tau\subset[n]
we will write
x_{\tau}
for the
squarefree monomial

\Pi_{i\in\tau}x_{i}
. The mapping
\tau\mapsto x_{\tau}
is a bijection between nonempty subsets of
[n]
and the set of squarefree monomials in
{\mathbb{R}}[X]
.

To a simplicial complex
\Delta
with
{\mathcal{F}}_{0}(\Delta)=[n]
we associate an ideal of
{\mathbb{R}}[X]
defined by

I_{\Delta}=(x_{\tau}:\tau\subset[n],\tau\not\in\Delta).

In words,
I_{\Delta}
is the ideal of
{\mathbb{R}}[X]
generated by squarefree monomials corresponding to non-faces of
\Delta
. The Stanley-Reisner ring of
\Delta
is defined by

{\mathbb{R}}[\Delta]=\frac{{\mathbb{R}}[X]}{I_{\Delta}}.

It is a commutative graded algebra whose
j
-homogeneous component
{\mathbb{R}}[\Delta]_{j}
is the vector space with a basis consisting of all monomials (including non-squarefree monomials) of total degree
j
whose support is in
\Delta
. The Stanley-Reisner ring is one of the central objects in combinatorial commutative algebra, see
[
96
,
82
,
49
]
for more comprehensive treatments.

How does this connect with rigidity theory? Suppose that
p:[n]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
For
1\leq j\leq d
let
\theta_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p(i)_{j}x_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}[\Delta]_{1}
and write
\theta
for the sequence
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d}
. Let
\ell=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}
, Let
B=B(\Delta,\theta)=\frac{{\mathbb{R}}[\Delta]}{(\theta)}
and let
C=C(\Delta,\theta)=\frac{{\mathbb{R}}[\Delta]}{(\theta,\ell)}
. Then
B
and
C
are graded
{\mathbb{R}}
-algebras. Let
B_{j}
, respectively
C_{j}
be the
j
-homogeneous component of
B
, respectively
C
.
The connection with classical rigidity theory is given by the following result of Lee
[
75
]
. Recall the definition of a stress matrix from Section
2.3
.

Theorem 5.14
.

There is a natural isomorphism between
C_{2}
and the vector space of all stress matrices of
R(G(\Delta),p)
.

Sketch proof.

The basis
\{x_{i}x_{j}+(I_{\Delta})_{2}:ij\in{\mathcal{F}}_{2}(\Delta)\}
induces an inner product on
{\mathbb{R}}[\Delta]_{2}
.
It can be shown that

(\theta,\ell)_{2}^{\perp}=\left\{\left(\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\Omega_{ij}x_{i}x_{j}\right)+(I_{\Delta})_{2}:\Omega\text{ is a stress matrix of }(G(\Delta),p)\right\}.

∎

In fact there are similar interpretations of
C_{j}
for
2\leq j\leq\dim\Delta+1
in terms of the skeletal rigidity matrices associated to the pair
(\Delta,p)
, see
[
75
]
for further details. In the case

, we have
B_{1}
, respectively
C_{1}
, is naturally isomorphic to the space of linear, respectively affine, dependencies among the points
p(1),\ldots,p(n)
and so, it has dimension

, respectively

, when

is in general position.

Up to now our discussion has only required elementary concepts of commutative algebra. The remainder of this section will make reference to some more advanced notions from commutative algebra, see
[

,

]
for more detail on these algebraic aspects.
For our purposes it suffices to mention the following facts.

Suppose that
p:{\mathcal{F}}_{0}(\Delta)\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is generic with
\dim\Delta=d-1
.
It is not too difficult to show that
B_{j}=0
for
j\geq d+1
. In particular, this means that in this case

is an
artinian

of
{\mathbb{R}}[\Delta]
.

The Cohen-Macaulay property is extensively studied in commutative algebra.
Macaulay has shown
{\mathbb{R}}[\Delta]
is a Cohen-Macaulay ring if and only if

\dim B_{i}=h_{i}(\Delta)=\sum_{j=0}^{i}(-1)^{i-j}\binom{d-j}{i-j}f_{j-1}(\Delta).

(12)

See
[
97
]
for an account of this. Also we note that it follows from a theorem of Reisner that the Stanley-Reisner ring of a simplicial sphere is Cohen-Macaulay, see
[

, Corollary 5.3.9]
.

Using (

) and Theorem
5.14
we obtain the following.

Lemma 5.15
.

Suppose that
{\mathbb{R}}[\Delta]
is Cohen-Macaulay and
\dim\Delta=d-1\geq 1
. Then
G(\Delta)
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
if and only if multiplication by
\ell
is an injective map
B_{1}\to B_{2}
.

An element

of a graded Artinian algebra
\mathcal{A}
is said to have the

if

and the multiplication map

from
\mathcal{A}_{i}
to
\mathcal{A}_{i+1}
has maximal rank for all

, i.e. this map is either injective or surjective. The weak Lefschetz property is of great interest in commutative algebra and algebraic geometry.
Lemma
5.15
indicates that there is a connection between rigidity theory and the weak Lefschetz property for an artinian reduction of the Stanley-Reisner ring. This connection has been developed much further, see
[
100
,
101
,
102
,
103
]
. In particular we mention Adiprasito’s recent proof of the

-vectors of rational homology spheres. This breakthrough result proves, among other things, that artinian reductions of the corresponding Stanley-Reisner rings have the weak Lefschetz property. The proof makes crucial use of the coning property of skeletal rigidity
[
2
]
.

Finally in this section we briefly discuss, without giving details, the theory of algebraic shifting. For a simplicial complex
\Delta

, Kalai

]
constructs a complex

with the following properties.

(1)

for all

.

(2)

If

and

i\not\in\Delta^{s}
then
\sigma\setminus\{j\}\cup\{i\}\in\Delta^{s}
.

(3)

For
d\geq 1
,
\rank{\mathcal{R}}_{d}(G(\Delta^{s}))=\rank{\mathcal{R}}_{d}(G(\Delta))
.

Roughly speaking the algebraic shifting operator
\Delta\to\Delta^{s}
corresponds at the level of Stanley-Reisner ideals to the generic initial ideal construction - there are some technicalities to deal with the fact that generic initial ideals of squarefree monomial ideals need not be squarefree.
Algebraic shifting has many other interesting algebraic and combinatorial properties. For example it is not difficult to deduce from (2) and (3) above that a graph
G
, considered as a 1-dimensional simplicial complex, with vertex set
[n]
for some
n\geq d+1
is rigid in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
if and only if
\{d,n\}\in G^{s}
. We note that this does not yield a more efficient algorithm for determining rigidity since computing the generic initial ideal of a squarefree monomial ideal is computationally as difficult as computing the rank of the generic rigidity matrix.

The basic construction of algebraic shifting was introduced in
[
66
]
in the context of the hyperconnectivity matroid of a graph, which is a relative of the rigidity matroid. Indeed Kalai shows that analogous shifting operators can be constructed for any suitably symmetric matroid, and uses this to study growth functions of symmetric matroids, see
[
68
]
. In his thesis Nevo developed the theory further and gave some applications to
f
-vectors of graded posets
[
87
]
. We also note some recent work on volume rigidity and algebraic shifting
[
13
]
.

5.3
Abstract rigidity and matroid maximality problems

We will describe Jack Graver’s ingenious concept of abstract rigidity matroids, which now plays a central role in combinatorial rigidity and can be used to connect rigidity theory to other topics in combinatorics such as weakly saturated sequences of graphs, tropical ideals and the problem of deciding if a given family of matroids contains a unique maximal element.

5.3.1
Abstract rigidity

A key observation of Graver
[
44
]
for the introduction of abstract rigidity is that the gluing property, one of the characteristic properties of graph rigidity given in Lemma
2.11
(a), can be described purely in terms of the closure operator of the rigidity matroid.

Let
M
be a matroid on
the edge set of the complete graph
K_{n}
with
n\geq d+1
,
and closure operator
{\rm cl}_{M}
. Then

M
is an
abstract
d
-rigidity matroid
if
M

satisfies the following two axioms (which correspond to the ‘only-if’ and ‘if’ directions of Lemma
2.11
(a), respectively):

(G1)

If
E_{1},E_{2}\subseteq E(K_{n})
with
V(E_{1})=V_{1}
,

V(E_{2})=V_{2}
and
|V_{1}\cap V_{2}|\leq d-1
, then

{\rm cl}_{M}(E_{1}\cup E_{2})\subseteq\binom{V_{1}}{2}\cup\binom{V_{2}}{2}
;

(G2)

If
E_{1},E_{2}\subseteq E(K_{n})
with
\cl_{M}(E_{1})=\binom{V_{1}}{2}
,

\cl_{M}(E_{2})=\binom{V_{2}}{2}
and
|V_{1}\cap V_{2}|\geq d
, then

{\rm cl}_{M}(E_{1}\cup E_{2})=\binom{V_{1}\cup V_{2}}{2}
.

It is known that Lemma
2.11
holds for any framework
(G,p)
such that
p
is in general position in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Hence the rigidity matroid of any
d
-dimensional framework
(K_{n},p)
in general position is an example of an abstract
d
-rigidity matroid, and this matroid may be distinct from
the generic
d
-dimensional rigidity matroid of
K_{n}
.

Although the axioms of abstract
d
-rigidity appear to be a rather specialised concept in rigidity theory, they actually define a very natural class of matroids on
E(K_{n})
if we consider some equivalent characterizations of abstract rigidity.
To describe these, we first list some basic properties of a matroid
M
on
E(K_{n})
, which all hold when
M={\mathcal{R}}_{d}(K_{n})
and
n\geq d+1
.

(R1)

The rank of
M
is
dn-{d+1\choose 2}
.

(R2)

Every copy of
K_{d+2}
is a circuit in
M
.

(R3)

Every copy of
K_{1,n-d}
is a cocircuit in
M
.

(R4)

If
G\subseteq K_{n}
,
v
is a vertex of
G
of degree
d
and
G-v
is
M
-independent then
G
is
M
-independent.

Theorem 5.16

(Graver-Servatius-Servatius
[
45
]
and Nguyen
[
88
]
)
.

Let
n,d
be positive integers with
n\geq d+1
and
M
be a matroid on
E(K_{n})
.
Then, the following statements are equivalent.

•

M
is an abstract
d
-rigidity matroid.

•

M
satisfies (R1) and (R2).

•

M
satisfies (R1) and (R3).

•

M
satisfies (R1) and (R4).

•

M
satisfies (R2) and (R3).

•

M
satisfies (R2) and (R4).

Let us consider the case when
d=1
as an example.
The graphic matroid of
K_{n}
is the generic
1
-dimensional rigidity matroid, and hence it is an abstract
1
-rigidity matroid.
Indeed, the graphic matroid has rank
n-1
, implying (R1),
and every copy of
K_{3}
is a circuit, implying (R2).
Conversely, suppose
M
is an abstract 1-rigidity matroid of
K_{n}
.
Then, by (R4), any forest is
M
-independent, and by (R1), any cycle is
M
-dependent.
Hence,
M
is the graphic matroid of
K_{n}
.
Thus, the graphic matroid of
K_{n}
is the unique abstract 1-rigidity matroid of
K_{n}
.
We will see that
the class of abstract
d
-rigidity matroids is much rich when
d\geq 2
.

Whiteley
[
112
]
observed that the generic
C^{d-2}_{d-1}
-cofactor matroid of
K_{n}
introduced in Section
4.1
is another important example of an abstract
d
-rigidity matroid.

Proposition 5.17
.

The generic
C^{d-2}_{d-1}
-cofactor matroid of
K_{n}
is an abstract
d
-rigidity matroid.

Several basic properties of generic rigidity matroids are valid for any abstract rigidity matroid.
For example, the following lemma shows the Maxwell’s condition (Lemma
2.3
) holds at the level of abstract rigidity, see
[
15
, Lemma 4.1]
.

Lemma 5.18
.

Let
M
be a matroid on the edge set of
K_{n}
.
Suppose that
M
satisfies (R2).
Then, for any
M
-independent subgraph
G=(V,E)
of
K_{n}
,

i_{G}(X)\leq d|X|-{d+1\choose 2}
for all
X\subseteq V
with
|X|\geq d+1
.

5.3.2
The Graver-Whiteley Maximality Conjecture

Graver’s main motivation for defining abstract
d
-rigidity was to gain a better understanding of the
d
-dimensional rigidity problem by characterising the generic
d
-dimensional rigidity matroid as an extremal instance in the family of all abstract
d
-rigidity matroids.

For a family
{\cal M}
of matroids on the same ground set,
the
weak order

\preceq
is defined as the partial order on
{\mathcal{M}}
such that, for any
M_{1},M_{2}\in{\cal M}
,
M_{1}\preceq M_{2}
if every independent set in
M_{1}
is independent in
M_{2}
.

Graver
[
44
]
conjectured that the generic
d
-dimensional rigidity matroid of
K_{n}
is the unique maximal matroid in the family of abstract
d
-rigidity matroids of
K_{n}
under the weak order, and verified his conjecture when
d=2
.

Theorem 5.19
.

The generic
2
-dimensional rigidity matroid of
K_{n}
is the unique maximal matroid in the family of all abstract
2
-rigidity matroids of
K_{n}
for sll
n\geq 3
.

Indeed, Theorem
5.19
is a direct consequence of Geiringer’s theorem
since every independent set in any abstract
2
-rigidity matroid satisfies Maxwell’s condition by Lemma
5.18

and such a set is independent in the generic
2
-dimensional-rigidity matroid by Theorem
3.1
.

Graver’s conjecture turned out to be false for
d\geq 4
.
The first counterexample was given by N. J. Thurston (cf.
[
45
]
) for
d=4
.
Whiteley subsequently used the complete bipartite graph
K_{d+2,d+2}
to show that the generic
C^{d-2}_{d-1}
-cofactor matroid provides a counterexample for all
d\geq 4
.

Lemma 5.20
.

For all
d\geq 4
,

E(K_{d+2,d+2})
is a circuit in the generic
d
-dimensional rigidity matroid
and is independent in the generic
C^{d-2}_{d-1}
-cofactor matroid.

The rigidity part of Lemma
5.20
follows from the Bolker-Roth
[
10
]
characterization of the rigidity of complete bipartite graphs.
The cofactor part follows from the fact that the X-replacement operation preserves independence in the generic
C^{d-2}_{d-1}
-cofactor matroid
[
112
]
, cf. the discussion in the first paragraph of Section
4.2
.

Lemma
5.20
motivates the following modified conjecture.

Conjecture 5.21

(Graver-Whiteley Maximality Conjecture)
.

The generic
C^{d-2}_{d-1}
-cofactor matroid of
K_{n}
is the unique maximal matroid in the family of abstract
d
-rigidity matroids of
K_{n}
for all
n\geq d+1\geq 2
.

If true, this conjecture would give useful structural information about the generic
C^{d-2}_{d-1}
-cofactor matroid. Indeed, it is shown in
[
16
]
that the partial result that
there exists a unique maximal abstract
d
-rigidity matroid
M
of
K_{n}
would imply that the following covering property holds for
M
: for each cyclic flat
F
of
M
, every
e\in F
is contained in a copy of
K_{d+2}
in
F
.

Clinch, Jackson, and Tanigawa
[
15
]
verified Conjecture
5.21
for
d=3
.

Theorem 5.22
.

The generic
C^{1}_{2}
-cofactor matroid of
K_{n}
is the unique maximal matroid in the family of abstract
3
-rigidity matroids of
K_{n}
for all
n\geq 4
.

They then used the above mentioned covering property from
[
16
]
to prove Theorem
4.5
.
Despite these positive results, Graver’s original conjecture still remains open when
d=3
.

Conjecture 5.23

(Graver’s maximality conjecture for
d=3
)
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. Then the generic
3
-rigidity matroid of
K_{n}
is the unique maximal matroid in the family of abstract
3
-rigidity matroids of
K_{n}
.

The truth of Conjecture
5.23
would give a combinatorial characterisation of generic rigidity in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
by
Theorems
4.3
and
5.22
.

As pointed out by Crespo and Santos
[
92
]
, when
d\geq 4
we do not even know if
{\mathcal{R}}_{d}(K_{n})\prec C^{d-2}_{d-1}(K_{n})
or the two matroids are incomparable in the weak order.

5.3.3
Duality, symmetric powers, and Mason’s problem

We will describe a connection between
abstract rigidity and the symmetric powers of a matroid introduced by Lovász
[
77
]
and Mason
[
79
]
.

Let
K_{V}^{\circ}
be the complete graph with vertex set
V
and with a loop at each vertex and
K_{1,t}^{\circ}
be the star with
t=1
vertices and a loop at its central vertex.
We denote the loop at a vertex
v
by the multiset
\{v,v\}
.

Given a matroid
M
on a finite set
V
of rank
t
,
a matroid
N
on
E(K_{V}^{\circ})
is said to be a
second symmetric power
of
M
if

(S1)

the rank of
N
is
{t+1\choose 2}
, and

(S2)

the rank in
N
of each copy of
K_{1,t}
and
K^{\circ}_{1,t}
in
K_{V}^{\circ}
is equal to the rank of the neighbours of its ‘central vertex’
in
M
. More precisely,

r_{M}(X)=r_{N}(vX)
for all
X\subseteq V
and all
v\in V
, where

vX=\{\{v,x\}:x\in X\}
.

A given matroid
M
may have several or no second symmetric powers, see, for example,
[
74
]
for more details.
In the special case when
M
is linear, however, we can always construct a canonical example of a symmetric second power by using
the symmetric power of the ambient space of its linear representation.
Specifically, suppose
M
has a linear representation over a field
\mathbb{F}
by associating a vector
p_{v}\in\mathbb{F}^{t}
to each
v\in V
.
Then we can construct a linear matroid on
E(K_{n}^{\circ})
by associating the symmetric power
p_{u}p_{v}
with each
uv\in E(K_{n}^{\circ})
.
4
4

4

We will be concerned with the case when
\mathbb{F}={\mathbb{R}}
. In this case, or any other case when
\mathbb{F}
has characteristic zero, we can take the symmetric power to be the symmetric matrix
p_{u}p_{v}^{T}+p_{v}p_{u}^{T}
.
The resulting matroid
N
will satisfy (S1) by the fact that the second symmetric power of
\mathbb{F}^{t}
has dimension
{t+1\choose 2}
and
N
will also satisfy (S2) by bilinearity.

For example, the uniform matroid
U_{n}^{t}
of rank
t
with
n
elements can be linearly represented by
assigning a generic vector
p_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{t}
to each
v\in V
, when
|V|=n
.
Thus we can obtain a second symmetric power of
U_{n}^{t}
by assigning
p_{u}p_{v}
to each
uv\in E(K_{n}^{\circ})
. We will refer to this matroid as the
generic second power of
U_{n,t}
and refer to
its restriction to
E(K_{n})
(by removing the loops
\{v,v\}
of
K_{n}^{\circ})
as
the
generic symmetric
t
-tensor matroid of
K_{n}
.

A recent result of Brakensiek et al.
[
11
]
tells us that the generic symmetric
t
-tensor matroid of
K_{n}

is the dual matroid of the
d
-dimensional generic rigidity matroid of
K_{n}
when we take
d=n-1-t
.

Theorem 5.24
.

Let
n,d,t
be positive integers with
d+t=n-1
.
Then the generic symmetric
t
-tensor matroid of
K_{n}
is the dual of the generic
d
-dimensional rigidity matroid.

Motived by Theorem
5.24
, we can also consider the dual of the other matroids in the family of abstract
d
-rigidity matroids.
We will see that this gives rise to other second symmetric powers of
U_{n}^{t}
restricted to
E(K_{n})
.
Specifically, consider axiom (S2) in the case when
M=U_{n}^{t}
and we take the restriction of
N
to to
E(K_{n})
.
Then (S2) can be simplified to:

(S2’)

Every copy of
K_{1,t+1}
is a circuit in
N
.

We can now define the family of
abstract symmetric
t
-tensor matroids
to be the family of matroids on
E(K_{n})

satisfying (S1) and (S2’).

For
d+t=n-1
, a matroid
N
satisfies (S1) if and only if its dual
N^{*}
satisfies (R1)
whereas
N
satisfies (S2’) if and only if
N^{*}
satisfies (R3).
We can combine this observation with Theorem
5.16
to obtain the following duality relation from
[
56
]
.

Lemma 5.25
.

Let
n,d,t
be positive integers with
d+t=n-1
. Then
a matroid
N
on
E(K_{n})
is an abstract symmetric
t
-tensor matroid if and only if its dual
N^{*}

is an abstract
d
-rigidity matroid.

Lemma
5.25
enables us to make dual formulations of the conjectures in Section
5.3.2
in terms of symmetric
t
-tensor matroids. The dual formulation of Graver’s maximality conjecture can be verified using techniques from rigidity theory when
t
is small.

Theorem 5.26
.

Let
n,t
be positive integers with
2\leq t\leq n-1
and
t\leq 5
.
Then the generic symmetric
t
-tensor matroid of
K_{n}
is the unique maximal element in the family of abstract symmetric
t
-tensor matroids of
K_{n}
.
Thus, Graver’s maximality conjecture for generic rigidity is true whenever
d\geq n-6
.

Theorem
5.26
is proved in
[
56
]
by providing a
sparsity condition which characterises independence in
the generic symmetric
t
-tensor matroid when
t\leq 5
.
It is also possible to derive Theorem
5.26
from the characterisation of all
{\mathcal{R}}_{d}
-circuits on
n
vertices when
n\leq d+6
given in
[
43
]
.

Mason
[
79
]
asked if there is always a unique maximal element in the family of second symmetric powers of a matroid.
Las Vergnas
[
74
]
showed that this property does not hold for the closely related family of
quasi second symmetric powers
, but to the best of our knowledge Mason’s original question is still open.
We formulate Mason’s question for the special case of uniform matroids as the following conjecture.

Conjecture 5.27
.

Let
n,t
be positive integers. Then the family of second symmetric powers of
U_{n}^{t}
has
a unique maximal element.

In view of Theorem
5.26
, it is tempting to conjecture that the generic second symmetric power of
U_{n}^{t}
will be the unique maximal element in Conjecture
5.27
, but this is false when
t\geq 7
.
We will use rigidity theory to show this but we first need to fill a small gap between second symmetric powers of
U_{n,t}
, which are defined on
E(K_{n}^{\circ})
, and abstract
d
-rigidity matroids, which are defined on
E(K_{n})
.

Suppose
N
is a second symmetric power of
U_{n}^{t}
.
Then, its restriction to
E(K_{n})
always gives an abstract symmetric
t
-tensor matroid
by definition.
We can use matroid duality to give an inverse construction.
Suppose
N
is an abstract symmetric
t
-tensor matroid on
E(K_{n})
and let
N^{*}
be its dual. Then
N^{*}
is an abstract
d
-rigidity matroid with
d=n-t-1
by Lemma
5.25
.
We can extend
N^{*}
to a matroid
\hat{N}^{*}
on
E(K_{n}^{\circ})
by appending each loop edge of
K_{n}^{\circ}
as a loop element in the matroidal sense.
Let
B_{n}
be the bicircular matroid on
E(K_{n}^{\circ})

and let
\hat{N}^{*}\vee B_{n}
be the matroid union of
\hat{N}^{*}
and
B_{n}
.
Then
\hat{N}^{*}\vee B_{n}
has rank
(d+1)n-{d+1\choose 2}
and
every copy of
K_{1,n-d+1}
and
K_{1,n-d}^{\circ}
is a cocircuit.
Therefore, its dual
(\hat{N}^{*}\vee B_{n})^{*}
satisfies (S1) and (S2) for
U_{n}^{t}
,
and hence is a second symmetric power of
U_{n}^{t}
.

We can now apply the above construction taking
N
to be the dual of the generic
C_{d-1}^{d-2}
-cofactor matroid of
K_{n}
. Then

M=(\hat{N}^{*}\vee B_{n})^{*}
is a second symmetric power of
U_{n}^{t}
.
In addition, we have
M\not\preceq M^{\prime}
when,
M^{\prime}
is the generic second symmetric power of
U_{n}^{t}
,
t\geq 7
and
n\geq 2t-2
. This follows from Theorem
5.24
and Lemma
5.20
by taking the dual of the restrictions of
M
and
M^{\prime}
to
E(K_{n})
. We can now deduce:

Theorem 5.28
.

The generic second symmetric power of
U_{n}^{t}
is not the the unique maximal element in the family of all second symmetric powers of
U_{n}^{t}
whenever
t\geq 7
and
n\geq 2t-2
.

5.3.4
Birigidity

As a bipartite analogue of rigidity matroids, Kalai, Nevo, and Novik
[

introduced birigidity matroids.

The special case when

In the subsequent discussion, we will assume that all bipartite graphs

and

Suppose
n_{i},d_{i}
are integers with

for
i=1,2
and
M
is a matroid on

is an

abstract
(d_{1},d_{2})
-birigidity matroid
if its closure operator
\cl_{M}
satisfies the following two axioms.

(BG1)

Suppose
E_{i}\subseteq V_{1,i}\times V_{2,i}\subseteq E(K_{n_{1},n_{2}})
for
i=1,2
,
|V_{1,1}\cap V_{1,2}|<d_{2}
and
|V_{2,1}\cap V_{2,2}|<d_{1}
.
Then
{\rm cl}_{M}(E_{1}\cup E_{2})\subseteq(V_{1,1}\times V_{2,1})\cup(V_{1,2}\times V_{2,2})
.

(BG2)

Soppse
E_{i}\subseteq V_{1,i}\times V_{2,i}\subseteq E(K_{n_{1},n_{2}})
with
{\rm cl}_{M}(E_{i})=V_{1,i}\times V_{2,i}
for

,

Conjecture 5.30

(Birigidity maximality conjecture)
.

Let
n_{1},n_{2},d_{1},d_{2}

-birigidity matroids of

The case when
