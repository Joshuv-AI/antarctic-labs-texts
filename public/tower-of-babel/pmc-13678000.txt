Factors influencing Thai parents’ COVID-19 vaccine acceptance for their children: Vaccine attitudes, beliefs, and children’s past influenza vaccination
Authors: Wantana Maneesriwongul, Suhong Deesamer, Nipaporn Butsing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Only 51.3% of Thai parents accepted COVID-19 vaccination for their children.•Trust in health professionals was independently associated with COVID-19 vaccine acceptance.•Belief in COVID-19 vaccine safety was a key determinant of vaccine acceptance.•Previous influenza vaccination was not independently associated with COVID-19 vaccine acceptance.•Findings highlight the importance of trusted health professionals in addressing parental vaccine concerns.Keywords: Vaccine hesitancy, Vaccine attitudes, COVID-19, Vaccine acceptance, Influenza vaccination

Introduction

On 11 March 2020, COVID-19 was declared a pandemic by the World Health Organization (2020). Several COVID-19 vaccines were rapidly developed and authorized for emergency used (Kaur and Gupta, 2020). Worldwide immunization was recognized as a key public health strategy to moderate the impacts of the pandemic (World Health Organization, 2021). COVID-19 immunizations reduced severe illness, hospitalizations, and mortality rates (Mohammed et al., 2022).

Despite scientific evidence supporting the safety and effectiveness of COVID-19 vaccines (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2020), vaccine acceptance has varied considerably across populations, such as general population (Saffari et al., 2026), people with chronic illness (Leelacharas et al., 2024), and older people (Kittipimpanon et al., 2022). Although of the COVID-19 vaccines were officially authorized for young children, the vaccines raised parents’ concerns about vaccine safety and unknown long-term health effects (Kitro et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2021). This has made parents uncertain about whether to vaccinate their children (World Health Organization, 2021). Parental involvement in decisions about COVID-19 vaccination is dynamic and is shaped by emotional, social, and informational factors (Marfo et al., 2026). Negative attitudes about vaccines, in general, have been linked to vaccine hesitancy (Pan et al., 2021; Fisher et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022; Dodd et al., 2021). Conversely, positive attitudes/beliefs could link to increase vaccination intention and reduce vaccine hesitancy (Fisher et al., 2020; Kadoya et al., 2021). In addition, the odds of parental intentions for children to get the COVID-19 vaccine significantly increase in parents with children who have received an influenza vaccine (Hill and Lavage, 2022; Goldman et al., 2020), suggesting that prior vaccination behavior is an important factor associated with COVID-19 vaccine acceptance.

The benefits of COVID-19 vaccination in children has been consistently reported. During the Omicron predominant period, the cumulative hospitalization rates among unvaccinated children were 2.1 times as high as were those reported among vaccinated children (Shi et al., 2022). Thailand reported that about 20% of COVID-19 cases from April to November 2021 were children (Department of Disease Control, 2021). The national COVID-19 vaccination program was implemented in phases, beginning with adolescents aged 12–18 years in October 2021 (World Health Organization, 2021) followed by children 5-11 years in January 2022 (World Health Organization, 2022). However, vaccine rollouts for children aged 6 months to 5 years in Thailand was not started until August 2022 (The Economic Times, 2022), leaving young children unprotected during the pandemic. During the fifth wave of Omicron in Thailand, the COVID-19 transmission rate was still higher among children aged 0-9 years, particularly in young children who were still unvaccinated (Center of COVID-19 Information, 2022). The infection rate and death rate of COVID-19 among children under five who were unvaccinated was 1.5 times and 3 times higher compared to older children who were vaccinated. During the first three-quarters of 2022 in Thailand, there were 443 intubated cases and 69 deaths among children under 5 years (Department of Disease Control, 2021; Center of COVID-19 Information, 2022).

In Thailand, multi-generational, high-density family living is a socio-cultural norm, thereby increasing the susceptibility of older adults and other vulnerable family members to getting COVID-19 as some older adults and those with chronic diseases still have vaccine hesitancy (Leelacharas et al., 2024; Kittipimpanon et al., 2022). The rates of child COVID-19 infection from household exposures were highest among children 0–4 years old (37.16%), followed by those 5–9 years (30.18%) (ThaiPost, 2022). Thus, more than two-thirds of the household COVID-19 exposures in Thailand occurred among children < 10 years old, placing young children at risk of acquiring COVID-19 from others via household transmission. Similarly, the highest rates of influenza transmission were reported among children 0–4 years old, followed by those aged 5–9 years (Department of Disease Control, 2021).

Similar to influenza vaccination, COVID-19 vaccination is an effective prevention strategy for children and other vulnerable populations. It provides protection to the community (herd immunity) (Breznik et al., 2025). However, the National Immunization Program in Thailand prioritizes annual influenza vaccination for children aged 6 months to <2 years. For children aged 3 years and older without high-risk conditions, annual influenza vaccination is recommended as a supplemental vaccine and may be administered based on parental preference and physician recommendation (Pediatric Infectious Disease Society of Thailand, 2022; Kahn et al., 2025).

A study exploring the relationships between children’s past influenza vaccination and parents' COVID-19 vaccine acceptance for their children is needed in Thailand. Therefore, this study aimed to 1) identify parents’ vaccine attitudes and beliefs, children’s past influenza vaccination and parents’ COVID-19 vaccine acceptance for their children; and 2) determine factors influencing parental COVID-19 vaccine acceptance for their children aged 6 months to <10 years.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection

This study was an online cross-sectional study which obtained survey responses from a larger online survey collected between January 13 and May 25, 2022. A sample of 973 participants was subset drawn from the large study based on the following inclusion criteria: 1) parents of children aged 6 months to 9 years of age (6 months up to 9 years, 11 months, and 29 days), 2) ability to read and understand Thai, and 3) agreement to participate. This sample size was adequate for exploring parents’ COVID-19 vaccine acceptance for their children and examining influencing factors of acceptance. The sample size estimation for logistic regression based on the concept of event per variable (EPV) was calculated with the formula of n = 100 + xi, where x is integer and i represents the number of independent variables (Bujang et al., 2018). The recommended rule of thumb of EPV was 50 (Bujang et al., 2018). Therefore, the minimum sample size for multivariate logistic regression involving 17 independent variables was 950.

Data collection was conducted using convenience sampling. Recruitment messages and e-posters containing a web link and a quick response code (QR code) to the Google Forms survey were circulated to reach out potential participants in all regions of Thailand through social media platforms. These included Facebook and LINE application (https:/line.me) of individuals, colleagues, friends, and social network groups. Individuals who received the message could re-distribute the recruitment message to other parents in their network. There was no incentive for participating in this study.

Measurement tools

The survey contained measures for one dependent and 17 independent variables:

Dependent variable

Parents’ COVID-19 vaccine acceptance for their children

Developed for a more extensive study, two single-item questions were used to ask the parents about COVID-19 vaccine acceptance for their children (Maneesriwongul et al., 2023a). Initially, parents were asked if their children had been vaccinated against COVID-19 vaccine (Yes-No). If the response was “no”, the survey asked about their willingness to allow their children to be vaccinated, “Will you have your child vaccinated against COVID-19?”. The three response options were 1) “Will definitely have,” 2) “Not sure,” and 3) “Will not have.” The parents who answered that their children received the COVID-19 vaccine or answered “Will definitely have” were defined as accepting the COVID-19 vaccine for their children; the second option was defined as hesitancy to accept the COVID-19 vaccine, while the third option was defined as refusing the COVID-19 vaccine for their children.

Independent variables

Parents’ and children’s characteristics

Background characteristics included parents’ age, sex, marital status, educational level, perceived income sufficiency, and occupations. In addition to giving their child’s age, parents indicated whether their child had an underlying disease and had been vaccinated against influenza.

Trust in health professionals

The item related to trust was measured by asking participants, “Advice about the COVID-19 vaccines for children from healthcare personnel is reliable”. Response options were a Likert scale of agreement ranging from 1 = “Strongly disagree” to 7 = “Strongly agree.” Scores of ≥ 4 were categorized as trusting in health professionals.

Attitudes about vaccination

A question measured the participants’ general attitudes about vaccination safety for their children. It was modified from Biasio et al. (2021). “You are not favorable to have your child/children vaccinated because COVID-19 vaccines are unsafety” (Agree-Disagree).

Attitudes about natural immunity

A question measured the participants’ general attitudes about vaccinations for their children. It was modified from Biasio et al. (2021). “There is no need for my child/children to be vaccinated because natural immunity exists” (Agree-Disagree).

History of children’s influenza vaccination

A question measured children’s past influenza vaccination. “Have your child/children been vaccinated against flu?” (Yes-No).

Beliefs in COVID-19 vaccine efficacy

A question, also modified from Biasio et al. (2021) was used to measure participants’ beliefs about COVID-19 vaccine efficacy “Do you think COVID-19 vaccines are efficacious in preventing COVID-19 in children?” (Yes-No).

Beliefs in COVID-19 vaccine safety

A question, also modified from Biasio et al. (2021) was used to measure participants’ beliefs about COVID-19 vaccine safety “Do you think the COVID-19 vaccines for children [are] safe?” (Yes-No).

Opinions about child vaccine coverage

One question measured participants’ opinions about vaccine coverage was modified from Biasio et al. (2021) “Do you think children under 12 years should receive COVID-19 too?” (Yes-No).

Preference to choose the COVID-19 vaccine

A question was modified from Biasio et al. (2021) asking about preference to choose the vaccine “If you could, would you choose which vaccine for your child to take?” (Yes-No).

Willingness to pay for the COVID-19 vaccine

A question was modified from Biasio et al. (2021) asking about willingness to pay for the COVID-19 vaccine. “Would you pay a fee for your child to be vaccinated?” (Yes-No).

Statistical analysis

Frequencies, percentages, means, medians, and standard deviations were used to describe the participants’ socio-demographics. Multinomial logistic regression models compared the acceptance and hesitant groups against the refusal group because the dependent variable had three categories. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 31 (ver. 31.0, IBM Corp, 2025, Armonk, NY, USA). Variables in the univariate models with a p-value <0.25 were candidates for inclusion in the multivariate models using a backward selection method (Hosmer and Lemeshow, 2000). An alpha level of <0.05 was set to determine factors that contributed significantly to parental acceptance or hesitancy rather than refusal of the COVID-19 vaccine for their children. The Chi-Square Goodness-of-fit test of the final model was not statistically significant (p-value=0.182), indicating that the model was a good fit for the data.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The Committee on Human Rights Related to Research Involving Human Subjects, Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University approved the study (COA.MURA2021/1005 and COA.MURA2023/371). It was conducted by following the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary and anonymous; no personal or electronic identifiers were collected. The potential participants gave informed consent by clicking “accept” on the online consent form before proceeding to the online survey questionnaire.

Results

Socio-demographic and background characteristics of the participants

Of the 973 participants, most were female, had earned a bachelor’s degree or higher, were non-healthcare personnel, and perceived they had a sufficient income (Table 1). Over one-fourth of the participants were from Central Thailand (27.9%), and nearly one-fourth came from Southern Thailand (24.6%). Although almost all participants reported they had been vaccinated against COVID-19 (98.6%), only about half would accept the COVID-19 vaccine for their children.

Correlates of parents’ acceptance of vaccinating their children against COVID-19

A higher rate of parents’ acceptance for their children to receive COVID-19 vaccination was detected among the parents whose children had past influenza vaccination, with higher proportions of parents who had positive beliefs in COVID-19 vaccine efficacy and safety, preference to choose and willingness to pay, and trust in health professionals (see Fig. 1, Fig. 2, respectively).

Factors influencing participants’ acceptance of COVID-19 vaccine for their children

The univariate analysis of multinomial logistic regression showed in Model 1 that participants’ age and the age of their children were significantly associated with COVID-19 acceptance (Table 3). None of the other socio-demographic variables were significant. Five factors had the highest magnitudes of association (odds ratios) with participants’ acceptance of COVID-19 vaccine: 1) Their belief in COVID-19 safety for children, 2) an opinion that children < 12 years old should be vaccinated, 3) belief about COVID-19 vaccine efficacy, 4) Preference to choose. Other factors had lower strengths of association with the acceptance of COVID-19 vaccines, such as willingness to pay for the vaccines, trust in health professionals, and a history of influenza vaccination. Odds ratios for significant factors associated with vaccine hesitancy (Model 2) were lower than their corresponding factors associated with vaccine acceptance (Model 1).

The two items in Model 1 regrading participants’ attitudes about vaccination were negatively associated with COVID-19 acceptance for their children. Conversely, participants were more prone to refuse vaccination if they held negative attitudes about vaccination, with odds ratios of 4.54 and 4.17 (given by the reciprocals of 0.22 and 0.24, respectively). Candidate factors with p-values < 0.25 in both univariate models were included in multivariate multinomial logistic regression.

Results of multivariate multinomial logistic regression showed three factors in Model 1 had the highest magnitudes of association with participants’ acceptance of COVID-19 for their children: 1) Belief in the safety of COVID-19 vaccines for children, 2) an opinion that children < 12 should be vaccinated, and 3) trust in the advice from health professionals (Table 4). Other factors in Model 1 that were significantly related but with lower magnitudes were the willingness to pay a fee for a child’s vaccine, ages of children and age of parents. In Model 1, the participants’ negative attitudes about vaccine safety were negatively associated with COVID-19 acceptance for their children. Conversely, participants were more prone to refuse vaccination if they held negative attitudes about vaccination, with an odds ratio of 1.97 (given by the reciprocal of 0.51). Similar to the univariate multinomial regression models, the odds ratios for significant factors associated with vaccine hesitancy (Model 2) in multivariate multinomial regression were lower than their corresponding factors associated with vaccine acceptance (Model 1), with one exception. The participants’ willingness to pay a fee for their child’s vaccine had a stronger association with Model 2’s hesitancy to be vaccinated than with the acceptance of vaccination in Model 1. The overall model explained 48.3% of the variance in parents’ COVID-19 vaccine acceptance for their children (Nagelkerke R² = 0.483). The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test showed that the model was a good fit to the data, with a Pearson Chi-Square Goodness-of-Fit = 1793.42, p-value = 0.182. Overall, the model correctly predicted 71.6% of cases.

Discussion

Nearly all parents reported receiving COVID-19 vaccination for themselves, but only half of the parents (51.3%) reported that they would accept COVID-19 vaccination for their children. This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted in Thailand, which reported parental intention to vaccinate their children ranging from 45.1% to 58.8% (Maneesriwongul et al., 2023a; Maneesriwongul et al., 2023b). A scoping review of worldwide studies between 2020 and 2022 about acceptance of COVID-19 vaccination for adolescents/children reported a median acceptance rate of 53.70% with wide range of acceptance rates between 4.9% and 91% (Liu et al., 2022). This review also reported a higher acceptance rate after the adolescent vaccine was authorized for use than before its approval. Later, another systematic review and meta-analysis studies reported pooled estimated parental acceptance rates to vaccinate their children with COVID-19 vaccine of 57% (Alimoradi et al., 2023) and 61% (Chen et al., 2022). The acceptance rates of COVID-19 vaccination for adolescents/children may be varied widely depending on the age of the children, time of data collection, vaccine rollout period, and countries/regions.

The findings that the ages of children and parents significantly influenced parents’ acceptance of vaccinating their children against COVID-19 were congruent with other studies on parental acceptance/hesitancy and uptake of COVID-19 vaccination (Maneesriwongul et al., 2023a) In addition, we found that nearly three-fourths of the parents indicated their children had been vaccinated against influenza (73.3%). In contrast, other studies in Thailand among children who had regular follow-up visits by pediatricians (Kittikraisak et al., 2015) and by family doctors (Kongwongsa, 2020) have reported much lower influenza vaccination rates (31%–36.4%). The National Immunization Program in Thailand prioritizes annual influenza vaccination for children aged 6 months to 2 years as children younger than 2 years of age are at particularly high risk of influenza-associated hospitalization (Sitlaothaworn et al., 2020).

For children older than 2 years without high-risk conditions, annual influenza vaccination is recommended as a supplemental (non-EPI) vaccine and may be administered based on parental preference and physician recommendation (Pediatric Infectious Disease Society of Thailand, 2022). There are several possible reasons for our study's higher rate of children’s influenza vaccination. Most parents were well educated and reported a sufficient income. About one-third were healthcare personnel. Thus, they could seek influenza vaccine information and afford to get their children vaccinated, although children aged 3 years or older were not eligible for Thailand’s free influenza vaccination at that time. Other reasons may be related to the data collection period in early 2022 during the Omicron-predominant period, the incidence of COVID-19 cases among children < 10 years old was reported to be increasing (Center of COVID-19 Information, 2022). In mean time, COVID-19 vaccine rollouts in Thailand for children aged 5-11 years was just started in January 2022 and for those young aged 6 months to 5 years was not started until August 2022 (National Vaccine Institute Ministry of Public Health, 2022). The increase in the incidence of COVID-19 among children may increase parental intention on children’s influenza vaccination (Hou et al., 2021) and raise the public awareness of being protected against influenza (Kong et al., 2022). Concerns about vaccine safety is the main reason for rejecting influenza vaccine for children (Ahmed et al., 2024; Kunč et al., 2026). A previous study revealed that 45% of parents who had children between the ages of 6 months and 11 years did not plan to vaccinate their children against the flu, citing concerns about side effects and vaccine effectiveness (Shmueli, 2023). If the parents were well informed, they may have also sought influenza vaccination for their children (Hou et al., 2021).

In our study, although the univariate multinomial regression models showed that children’s past influenza vaccination was significantly associated with parents' COVID-19 vaccine acceptance for their children, it was not included as a significant predictor in the final multivariate multinomial logistic regression models. This differs from previous studies which reported that children who received the influenza vaccine had higher odds of parents’ intentions to vaccinate their children against COVID-19 (Hill and Lavage, 2022; Goldman et al., 2020; Galanis et al., 2022).

There are several possible reasons for the discrepancy between our findings and those of previous studies. One possible explanation is that, differences in national immunization programs, vaccine recommendations, and healthcare access across countries may influence both influenza vaccine uptake and parental attitudes toward COVID-19 vaccination, contributing to inconsistent findings across studies. In Low-and -Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) such Thailand (Pediatric Infectious Disease Society of Thailand, 2022) and India (Mitra and Kumar, 2026) national influenza vaccination program covered children aged 6 months to 2 years, the influenza vaccination program in UK (National Health Service (NHS), 2025) and USA (Grohskopf et al., 2025) included children aged up to 12 years and 17years, respectively. Although previous influenza vaccination is generally considered a marker of positive vaccine attitudes, parental decisions regarding COVID-19 vaccination may be influenced more strongly by COVID-19-specific concerns, including vaccine safety, perceived effectiveness, and the novelty of the vaccines (Galanis et al., 2022). In addition, misinformation and conspiracy beliefs surrounding COVID-19 vaccines may have undermined parental confidence in vaccination (Gökalp et al., 2025). Consequently, children's previous influenza vaccination may not independently predict parents' intentions to vaccinate their children against COVID-19 after these factors are taken into account (Ruggiero et al., 2021).

Despite a non-significant finding in this study, we identify higher rate of parents’ acceptance for their children to receive COVID-19 vaccination among the parents whose children had past influenza vaccination (Fig. 1). We found that among children with past influenza vaccination, their parents were more likely to accept rather than refuse COVID-19 vaccination for them. Similar to other studies, the proportions of parents’ acceptance for their children to receive COVID-19 vaccination were also higher among the parents who had positive beliefs in COVID-19 vaccine safety (Krakowczyk et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2021; Lau et al., 2022), vaccine preference, and willingness to pay (Lau et al., 2022; Hayat et al., 2023; AlKetbi et al., 2022) and trust in health professionals (Kitro et al., 2022; AlKetbi et al., 2022; Rehman et al., 2023). A pediatrician is a key person the parents regard as a trusted health professional who should provide information about the COVID-19 vaccine for their child (Napoli et al., 2022; Khoodoruth et al., 2023). The role of attitudes about vaccination and beliefs about COVID-19 vaccination is significantly associated with parental vaccine acceptance for their children (Choi et al., 2022). Negative attitudes must be dispelled to increase parents’ COVID-19 vaccine acceptance (Dodd et al., 2021) while enhancing positive attitudes about vaccines. Understanding factors influencing parents' vaccine acceptance for their children and the key role of attitudes about vaccination and beliefs about COVID-19 vaccination could be beneficial for health professionals to tailor COVID-19 vaccination interventions more specifically for parents of child aged 6 months to 9 years.

Public health campaigns for COVID-19 and annual influenza vaccines with adequate information about the usefulness/benefits of both vaccines that outweigh the risks through health professionals will play key roles in scaling up parental vaccine acceptance for their children. The systematic reviews (Jwa et al., 2025) suggest that strategies for interventions to improve parental vaccine acceptance should be tailored according to parental trust levels, the child's age, and the local epidemiological context. For parents with high levels of trust, providing information about risk of disease and the benefits of vaccination, expected side effects, reminder/recall systems, and convenient access to vaccination may be sufficient. For parents with moderate vaccine hesitancy, healthcare providers should explore parental concerns and values through open communication and involve trusted health professionals in the decision-making process. Communicating firsthand experiences of vaccine-preventable diseases has been suggested as a potential approach to reducing vaccine hesitancy (Marotta and McNally, 2021). Dispelling negative attitudes of not favorable and no need for vaccination and reassuring about COVID-19 vaccination safety and efficacy could increase parents’ vaccine acceptance for their children. Parents with positive attitudes and trust in advice/information from healthcare personnel are associated with higher odds of intention to vaccinate their children against COVID-19 (De Freitas et al., 2021; Szilagyi et al., 2021). Sufficiency and credibility of the information about the vaccine were related to parental decision-making in favor of the COVID-19 vaccine for their children (Lee et al., 2022).

The COVID-19 pandemic, coupled with the clinical similarities between COVID-19 and influenza in this populations, has highlighted the need for integrated vaccination strategies, thereby linking the management of immunization against both infections. TheAmerican Academy of Pediatrics (2022), CDC (2022), and WHO (2022) currently recommend that children six months or older who are eligible for COVID-19 vaccination should receive the COVID-19 vaccine and annual influenza vaccine at the same visit. However, barriers due to insufficient vaccine allocations, parental concerns about side effects and other operational barriers should be removed (Saffari et al., 2026; Oranat et al., 2025). The results of our study will benefit designing future vaccination campaigns for both the COVID-19 vaccine and the annual influenza vaccine. Strengthening parents’ vaccine acceptance for their children will be an essential strategy to increase COVID-19 vaccination in children (Fisher et al., 2020; Kadoya et al., 2021; Fisher et al., 2022).

Limitations

Data were collected between January 13 and May 25, 2022, before COVID-19 vaccines for children < 5 years of age were authorized in Thailand. Although the online survey was circulated nationwide and reached parents with diverse occupations, one-third of the participants were healthcare personnel who might be more informed about health issues than Thais in other fields. The sample was predominantly more female respondents as parents, and they were better educated with higher socioeconomic status than the average Thai. They were also more likely to have internet access and digital competency. The use of convenience sampling could limit the generalizability of the study findings. Last, a cross-sectional design used this study limits the establishment of causal relationships between variables, and makes longitudinal data not possible.

Implications for practice and research

The critical role of children’s past influenza vaccination is related to parents' COVID-19 vaccine acceptance for their children. Future policies will be critically needed to ensure more effective COVID-19 and influenza vaccination coverage rates after the COVID-19 pandemic. Policymakers should understand the relationships between parents' attitudes about vaccination, their beliefs about COVID-19 vaccine efficacy and safety, and vaccine acceptance for their children. Because trust in vaccine information from health professionals significantly influences parents’ vaccine acceptance for their children, effective communication strategies about the COVID-19 vaccine and vaccination of young children and accurate information about research and development of the vaccines by health authorities are needed. This should be a focus of future vaccination campaign development after the COVID-19 pandemic to dispel parents’ negative attitudes about vaccination and reinforce vaccine uptake of both COVID-19 and influenza vaccination among young children. The effective communication strategies should be tailored according to parental trust levels, the child's age, and the local epidemiological context. For parents with high levels of trust, providing information on vaccine benefits, expected side effects, reminder/recall systems, and convenient access to vaccination may be sufficient. For parents with moderate vaccine hesitancy, healthcare providers should explore parental concerns and values through open communication and involve trusted health professionals in the decision-making process (Jwa et al., 2025).

Conclusions

The significant influencing factors of parents’ COVID-19 vaccine acceptance are a parent’s age, trust in health professionals, beliefs about COVID-19 vaccine safety, opinions that young children should also be vaccinated, negative attitudes that are not favorable about child vaccination, willingness to pay for the vaccines, and the child’s age. A child’s past influenza vaccination is not independently associated with parents’ COVID-19 vaccine acceptance for their children. This points to the critical role of vaccine attitudes, beliefs about the COVID-19 vaccine, and children’s past influenza vaccination, and parents’ COVID-19 vaccine acceptance for their children.
