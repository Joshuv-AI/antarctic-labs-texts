Novel synthesized covalent organic framework and determination of its adsorption capacity for coumarin dyes
Authors: Emra Özden, Aybek Yiğit, Tuğba Bayraktutan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Using a solvothermal Schiff-base condensation technique, a novel nitrogen-rich covalent organic framework (COFDFFT−PDKA) was created and its ability to adsorb coumarin-based dyes from aqueous solutions was assessed. FT-IR, XRD, SEM, and TEM investigations verified the successful development of the crystalline and porous framework. Fluorescence spectroscopy was used to conduct adsorption experiments of Coumarin 6, Coumarin 7, and Coumarin 30 under various pH, temperature, contact time, and metal-ion conditions. The Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin isotherm models were used to examine the adsorption equilibrium data. The findings showed that while Coumarin 6 exhibited heterogeneous surface interactions consistent with the Freundlich model, adsorption of Coumarin 7 and Coumarin 30 followed the Langmuir model, indicating dominant monolayer adsorption. The maximum adsorption capacities of COFDFFT-PDKA for C6, C7, and C30 were 51.33, 826.45, and 362.32 mg/g, respectively. According to kinetic investigations, a pseudo-second-order model best described the adsorption process, suggesting a mechanism regulated by chemisorption. In the three dyes, the ΔG and ΔH values were negative, while the ΔS values were positive. This demonstrated that adsorption operates spontaneously and is exothermic in nature. COFDFFT-PDKA has the potential to be an effective and selective adsorbent for wastewater treatment, as evidenced by its strong adsorption of Coumarin 7.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13065-026-01836-8.

Introduction

Covalent Organic Frameworks (COFs) are materials composed of organic molecules interconnected by covalent connections, resulting in regular, porous structures. These structures are distinguished by their extensive surface area and evident uniformity [1]. Moreover, these materials exhibit chemical and thermal stability, making them suitable for secure and effective use in industrial and laboratory settings. These characteristics make COFs optimal materials for adsorbing diverse contaminants from aquatic environments. Pure COFs and COF-based composites are being emphasized as a novel alternative for water treatment, particularly for the adsorption and removal of numerous contaminants, including heavy metals, medicines, dyes, and organic pollutants [2–4].

Over 700,000 metric tons of synthetic dyes are produced annually, with more than 100,000 types commercially available. Approximately 15% of this total dye output is released through textile wastewater, while the food, chemical, and textile sectors collectively contribute to about 97% of localized dye effluents. A significant portion of residual dyes generated during dyeing and finishing operations is discharged into the environment, often with minimal or no treatment. This poses serious ecological concerns, particularly when these toxic, mutagenic, and carcinogenic substances enter natural water bodies. Moreover, exposure to such dyes can harm human health, potentially leading to dysfunction in the kidneys, liver, reproductive system, and central nervous system [5–7]. One family of synthetic dyes in the literature and industry is coumarin derivatives. Coumarin is a member of lactone-type compounds featuring a six-membered heterocyclic ring, with a carbonyl group positioned at the α-location relative to the ring’s oxygen atom (Fig. 1A) [8, 9]. This specific placement of the carbonyl group plays a key role in determining the electronic structure of the excited state and the fluorescence properties of coumarin derivatives. It was reported that among coumarin derivatives, those bearing electron-donating substituents like a dimethylamino group at the 7 position are recognized as some of the most fluorescent members of the coumarin family [10] (Fig. 1B). Coumarin derivatives have outstanding fluorescence, photophysical, and chemical characteristics, making them useful in a variety of applications. They are useful fluorescent probes for biological imaging because of their high solubility, which enables real-time visualization of cellular activities. Coumarins are used in laser technologies and OLEDs to enable efficient light emission across a wide range of wavelengths, thereby advancing communications and consumer electronics. Furthermore, coumarin compounds are widely used in cosmetics due to their vibrant colors and pleasant fragrance, demonstrating their broad utility in both scientific and industrial applications [11, 12].

Even though synthetic dyes, including coumarin, have a very useful impact on our lives, they are dangerous to the environment and aquatic life. These dyes should be removed after the application has ended. The literature thoroughly addresses dye removal procedures from water for the previously mentioned reasons [13–16]. In recent years, the discovery of compounds with strong adsorption capabilities, such as COF, has led to the development of dye-removal processes using these materials, which have attracted increased attention in the literature [9, 17–23]. The literature includes studies on dye removal using fluorescence spectroscopy, although they are limited in number [24–28]. Many dyes have been removed from aqueous environments using techniques that employ nanoparticles, carbon-based materials, or diverse hybrid materials as adsorbents. In these studies, adsorption capabilities, indicative of dye removal efficacy, were computed as follows: Rhodamine B (RhB) 56.14 mg/g [26], Methylene Blue (MB) 27.57 mg/g [26], Methyl Orange (MO) 167 mg/g [25], Toxic brilliand green (95.567 mg/g), and Naphthol Green B (NB) 79 mg/g [24]. Considering these factors, our adsorption capabilities for fluorescence-based dye removal are exceptionally good, especially for C7 and C30.

We selected three important fluorescent coumarin dyes and evaluated their removal from water. The removal process was studied with a novel COF material, which was synthesized for the first time in this study. The COF formed by the reaction between the pyridine ring of 2,6-pyridinedicarboxaldehyde and the amino groups of 3,8-diamino-6-phenylphenanthridine contains multiple adjacent imino (C = N) and pyridine nitrogen atoms. This results in a high nitrogen density within the COF, enhancing the formation of electron-rich active sites that are particularly effective for applications such as dye, metal ion, or gas adsorption. Unlike many other COFs, which primarily consist of carbon and oxygen, this structure incorporates multiple nitrogen-linked centers, contributing to its unique chemical properties. Consequently, the removal process was studied using this novel COF material, synthesized for the first time in this work. Given the hydrophilic nature and presence of heteroatoms in the target molecules, the goal was to develop a nitrogen-rich COF to promote stronger noncovalent interactions. Thus, this specific COF structure was designed and created to achieve improved removal performance [29–33]. Then, the removal process of three coumarin dyes under different conditions, such as pH, ion interference, and temperature, was studied. The Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin adsorption models are characterized by distinct assumptions and considerations regarding the factors influencing adsorption, helping clarify the mechanism, surface characteristics, and sorbent affinities. In addition, we have used adsorption isotherms to determine the COF material’s adsorption capacity for three coumarin dyes. Consequently, we expect this research to provide a novel contribution to the discipline by examining COF adsorption by sensitive fluorescence spectroscopy.

Methods

Synthesis

The traditional solvothermal method was used to synthesize COFDFFT−PDKA. In brief, 2,6-pyridinedicarboxaldehyde (32.43 mg, 0.24 mmol) and 3,8-diamino-6-phenylphenanthridine (56.23 mg, 0.16 mmol) were placed in a round-bottomed glass flask, respectively. After adding the 1,4-dioxane/mesitylene solution (4:1, v/v; 5 mL) to the glass bottle, the mixture was processed in an ultrasonic water bath at 200 W for 3 min to ensure a homogeneous distribution. Following ultrasonication, 1.2 mL of ultrapure water and 1.8 mL of acetic acid were added to the mixture. The solution was stirred on a magnetic stirrer (10 min/25°C/600 rpm) and then briefly frozen in a refrigerator. After warming to room temperature, the flask was heated on a magnetic stirrer (72 h/120°C/600 rpm). The solid obtained at the end of the heating process was isolated by filtration, then washed with THF (3 × 10 mL) and methanol (3 × 10 mL), respectively, and dried in a low-temperature oven to obtain COFDFFT−PDKA. Material characterization

FT-IR provides descriptive information about the chemical bonds within a molecule or compound. A PerkinElmer Spectrum Two (KBr) series FT-IR (Fourier transform infrared spectrophotometer) was used in this characterization step. Another characterization step, XRD (X-ray diffraction), can determine the presence and quantity of mineral species in the sample and also identify phases. FE-SEM (Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopes) is an analysis technique that provides information about surface morphology by scanning a sample’s surface with an electron beam. The ZEISS GeminiSEM 500 type FE-SEM instrument was utilized in this instance. For the simultaneous determination of the crystalline and micro/nano structures of both organic and inorganic compounds, TEM (Transmission Electron Microscope) is a commonly used analysis method. Additionally, the TEM instrument allows for the assessment of particle size and structural morphology. We used a Hitachi HT7800-type TEM in this step.

Results and discussion

FT-IR studies

FT-IR spectra of COFDFFT–PDKA show that the desired covalent organic skeleton structure has been created (Fig. 3). (–OH) or (–NH) groups in the structure are responsible for the wide band seen at about 3352 cm⁻¹. The broad structure of this vibrational band can be attributed to heteroatomic functional groups within the COF skeleton and suggests the presence of hydrogen bonding. Aromatic or aliphatic (C–H) stretching vibrations are linked to the vibrational band at 2938 cm⁻¹, indicating the organic skeleton’s carbon-based structure. The prominent peak at 1710 cm⁻¹ that is easily seen can be linked to stretching vibrations of the (C = O) bond. This vibrational band shows that the structural integrity of the COF structure is preserved and that the carbonyl groups from the COFDFFT–PDKA units are conserved. The stretching vibrations of aromatic rings (C = C) are also responsible for the 1613 cm⁻¹ band, which confirms COF’s aromatic nature. It is possible to attribute the bands seen in the 1477 and 1367 cm⁻¹ regions to bending vibrations of (C–N) or (C–C) bonds as well as aromatic ring vibrations. This suggests the creation of a regular conjugated structure and the existence of heteroatom-containing bonds in the COF skeleton. Furthermore, vibrations of (C–O), (C–N), or aromatic ether-like bonds typically correlate to the 1231, 1148, and 1083 cm⁻¹ bands. Covalent bonding between the building blocks is successfully supported by these vibrational bands. Out-of-plane (C–H) bending vibrations of aromatic rings have been linked to the lower wave numbers seen in the spectra, namely the peaks at approximately 979, 882, 812, and 701 cm⁻¹. Because of the COF structure’s regular, distinct aromatic network, these bands can be inferred. Additionally, the bands at 592 and 442 cm⁻¹ can be linked to the bond and skeleton vibrations of heavy atoms [42–46].

The synthesized COFDFFT−PDKA’s diffraction pattern shows a highly ordered, crystalline structure, as evidenced by prominent, sharp diffraction peaks at low angles in the diffractogram (Fig. 4). The diffractogram’s prominent peak (2θ = 5.71°) corresponds to the (100) plane and indicates the periodic in-plane lattice arrangement of the COF structure. A prominent feature of two-dimensional COFs is the hexagonal or rectangular periodic pore structure, which is indicated by this diffraction peak. Its uniform pore distribution and long-range order are indicated by its high intensity and low angle. However, plane (210) is used to index the second separate peak, which was found at (2θ = 8.48°). This diffraction peak confirms the precision of the crystal lattice arrangement and indicates higher-order planar reflections in the structure. Moreover, the presence of peaks (100) and (210) together suggests that the structure has a clear crystal symmetry, whereas the lack of distinct peaks at high angles (0–90°) implies that the structure is primarily composed of ordered two-dimensional layers and that the interlayer π–π stacking provides weak or limited reflection (Fig. 3B) [42–44, 48, 49].

Assessment of adsorption isotherms and adsorption performance

The Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin adsorption models provide essential insights for analyzing and enhancing adsorption behavior. All three methods effectively assess adsorption capacity and enhance material design by analyzing the interactions between adsorbent and adsorbate. Each model accommodates distinct adsorption conditions, thereby offering versatility for a range of applications. These models serve as effective tools for predicting and optimizing adsorption processes and for formulating efficient treatment strategies [58]. The adsorption capacities of COFDFFT−PDKA for the coumarin series of dyes were evaluated using the Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin isotherm models to assess adsorption kinetics.

Figure 8a and d, and g illustrate the Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin adsorption isotherms for C6 dye. Figure 8b and e, and h present the adsorption isotherms for C7 dye, while Fig. 8c and f, and i depict the adsorption isotherms for C30 dye, respectively. Table 1 summarizes the adsorption parameters derived from these isotherms. Table 1 shows that the Freundlich isotherm provided the best fit for C6 dye removal, whereas the Langmuir isotherm provided the best fit for C7 and C30. While these fit values do not yield conclusive insights into the adsorption mechanism, using the adsorption isotherm with the optimal fit is a more precise approach [59]. The Langmuir isotherm model posits that all adsorption sites are uniform and exhibit equal affinity for all adsorbate molecules. This model suggests the development of a uniform monolayer of the adsorbate. The Freundlich isotherm model suggests multilayer formation, positing that the adsorbate sites are heterogeneous and exhibit varying affinities for the adsorbate molecules. The Temkin Isotherm Model posits that the energy distribution varies linearly during adsorption and accounts for interactions, including thermal effects [60].

In Table 1, the Langmuir model indicates that compound C6 has a maximum capacity of 51.33 mg/g and a moderate adsorption strength (b = 1.83, R2 = 0.87), whereas compound C7 demonstrates a capacity of 826.45 mg/g and a strong adsorption strength (b = 6.68, R2 = 0.99), suggesting an excellent fit of the model to the data. C30 exhibited a capacity of 362.32 mg/g and a moderate adsorption strength (b = 3.26, R2 = 0.97), signifying a strong fit of the model to the data. According to the Temkin model, compound C6 exhibits elevated adsorption energy (bT = 504.02 kJ/mol) and diminished capacity (AT = 1.21 L/g) (R2 = 0.84), but compound C7 demonstrates inadequate model fit (R2 = 0.62) with reduced energy (bT = 208.56 kJ/mol) and increased capacity (AT = 2.76 L/g). C30 exhibits moderate energy (bT = 263.84 kJ/mol) and substantial capacity (AT = 2.50 L/g), with an R2 value of 0.65, suggesting a suboptimal model fit. The data indicate that compound C7 aligns most closely with the Langmuir model due to its higher adsorption capacity and adsorption strength, whereas compound C6 shows inferior adsorption characteristics. Compound C30 demonstrates moderate efficacy. Despite C6, C7, and C30 possessing an identical coumarin core, variations in their heterocyclic terminal groups (N-methylation, sulfur substitution, and the presence of a –NH group) alter molecular planarity and interaction dynamics with the COFDFFT−PDKA surface, resulting in heterogeneous adsorption for C6 and more uniform monolayer adsorption for C7 and C30.

The adsorption mechanism is mostly governed by π–π stacking interactions between aromatic coumarin rings and the conjugated structure of COF [61]. The nitrogen-rich architecture of COF offers electron-dense areas that augment interactions with coumarin derivatives, reinforce π–π interactions, and facilitate additional stabilization via hydrogen bonds and weak electrostatic interactions involving heteroatoms (N, O, and S). Consequently, whereas π–π stacking serves as the principal driving force, hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions might operate as secondary forces in stabilizing the adsorbed dye molecules [62].

Assessment of adsorption kinetics

Pseudo-first order [69] and pseudo-second order kinetic models [70] enhance the comprehension of the adsorption mechanism of coumarin dye on COFDFFT−PDKA. The pseudo-first-order and second-order kinetic model well characterizes the initial phases of adsorption and the rate-limiting mechanisms involved. These models are based on a linear correlation between the adsorption rate and the number of available adsorption sites, incorporating the equilibrium rate constants (k1 and k2) and the adsorption capacity (qe). It can be used to predict adsorption kinetics, analyze the effect of contact time on adsorption, and evaluate various adsorbents. These models are an effective instrument for comprehending the adsorption behavior of adsorbents in dye adsorption systems [71].

Impact of metal ions on adsorption capacity and removal efficiency

qe estimates the adsorbent’s capacity, indicating the quantity of material retained, while removal efficiency (R%) evaluates the overall effectiveness of the adsorption process, signifying its ability to remove the material. In order to understand the degree to which metal interactions affect the adsorption process, these properties were compared and are depicted in Fig. 10, with the computed values (qe and R%) listed in Table S1.

This study examined the adsorption process of 12 distinct metal ions under consistent conditions. The ions included were Pb²⁺, Mg²⁺, Cu²⁺, Zn²⁺, Hg²⁺, Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺, Al³⁺, Ba²⁺, Ca²⁺, Sn²⁺, and Mn²⁺. Table S1 and Fig. 10 demonstrate that compound C6 exhibited slight increases in adsorption capacities relative to the control condition in the presence of various metal ions. The %R value increased across all metals, with significant enhancements for Fe³⁺, Al³⁺, Ba²⁺, and Ca²⁺ ions, reaching 72.90%, 73.20%, 72.96%, and 73.11%, respectively. The ions might enhance the availability of active sites on the DFFT-PDKA surface or facilitate a synergistic interaction with C6 [75].

The interactions between metal ions and C6, C7, and C30 compounds show that each compound exhibits enhanced interactions with specific metal ions, and that the roles of these ions in the adsorption process vary.

The C7 showed the highest removal efficiency for Fe³⁺ (96.85%), indicating a significant interaction between C7 and Fe³⁺. Additionally, high %R values of 88.68% and 88.17% were recorded in the presence of Fe²⁺ and Hg²⁺, respectively. The %R value decreased significantly in the presence of ions such as Al³⁺, Ca²⁺, Ba²⁺, and Zn²⁺, dropping to 74–75%. This indicates that these ions compete with the dye for adsorption on the adsorbent surface [76]. The presence of metal ions had the greatest impact on the C30 dye. The removal efficiency was 86.29% under the “blank” condition, but decreased to 63.45% with Fe³⁺, 68.69% with Mg²⁺, and 65.69% with Zn²⁺. This indicates that C30 adsorption is significantly influenced by competition with metal ions. Metal ions substantially modify the interaction between COFDFFT−PDKA and coumarin dyes. The C7 dye had superior performance in the presence of Fe ions, whereas the C30 dye displayed a more sensitive and suppressible adsorption profile. The findings indicate that COFDFFT−PDKA may exhibit varying efficacy in selective adsorption under different environmental conditions and in the presence of metal ions. This competitive behavior suggests that the adsorption process involves certain surface interactions, including π–π stacking and hydrogen bonding, which may be partially compromised when metal ions occupy or engage with these active sites. Several systems, including nanocomposites and biohybrid materials designed for decontamination, have demonstrated similar mechanisms for competitive adsorption and surface interaction in literature [77, 78]. These findings corroborate the predicted process by which dye molecules and competing ions interact with the same adsorption sites on the material’s surface.

The pH regulates the adsorption process by altering the charge characteristics of the adsorbent surface and the protonation or deprotonation state of the dye molecules. Therefore, dye speciation and surface contacts might alter with varying pH conditions, leading to corresponding changes in adsorption efficiency. The pKa values for C6 and C7 are approximately 5.0, whereas for C30, it is approximately 3.0 [79, 80]. This indicates that the protonation state of coumarin dyes varies with pH levels. Under low pH conditions, the generation of protonated species might improve their interaction with the COF surface. It was concluded that pH significantly influences adsorption efficiency, with acidic and neutral conditions being more conducive to adsorption. The results suggest that COFDFFT−PDKA may serve as an effective adsorbent, particularly for removing coumarin derivatives such as C7 and C30. Additionally, the results demonstrate that each chemical exhibits distinct adsorption capacities and efficiencies based on its structure across varying pH levels, and that its molecular interactions may vary with pH [81].

The research demonstrates potential for dye removal methods, owing to their adsorption capabilities and relative stability. However, laboratory-scale findings from studies performed in controlled settings are limited to particular pH and temperature limits and cannot be easily generalized for commercial application. Comprehensive studies across a broad pH and temperature range, along with pilot-scale experiments on actual textile wastewater, will establish a more definitive framework for application.

Conclusion

A novel covalent organic framework (COFDFFT−PDKA) was synthesized, and its adsorption efficacy for coumarin dyes in aqueous solutions was carefully examined. The material had significant adsorption capacity, especially for C7 and C30. The adsorption behavior was affected by pH, temperature, and the presence of concurrent metal ions. Isotherm study revealed Langmuir and Freundlich adsorption based on dye structure, but kinetic studies adhered to a pseudo-second-order model. Thermodynamic studies validated that the adsorption process is advantageous and spontaneous, indicating that COFDFFT−PDKA is a suitable adsorbent for the elimination of coumarin dyes from water.
