Homology character of the parabolic coset poset
Theo Douvropoulos, Matthieu Josuat-Vergès

Abstract
Motivated by the analogy with the Coxeter complex on one side, and parking functions on the other side, we study the poset of parabolic cosets in a finite Coxeter group. We show that this poset is Cohen-Macaulay, and get an explicit formula for the character of its (unique) nonzero homology group in terms of the Möbius function of the intersection lattice. This homology character becomes a positive element of the parabolic Burnside ring (in its natural basis) after tensoring with the sign character. The coefficients of this character essentially encode the colored h h -vector of the positive chamber complex (following Bastidas, Hohlweg, and Saliola, this complex is defined by taking Weyl chambers that lie on the positive side of a generic hyperplane). Roughly speaking, tensoring by the sign character on one side corresponds to the transformation going from the f f -vector to the h h -vector on the other side.



1
Introduction

Let
G
be a group and
\mathcal{H}
be a collection of subgroups of
G
. The
coset poset
associated to
G
and
\mathcal{H}
is defined as the set of cosets
gH
(with
g\in G,\;H\in\mathcal{H}
), ordered by inclusion. It was introduced by Abels and Holz
[
1
]
, who relate its topological properties with algebraic properties of
\mathcal{H}
. Brown
[
7
]
considers the coset poset in the case of a finite group
G
(and
\mathcal{H}
the collection of all proper subgroups), in connection with the probabilistic zeta function of
G
: the evaluation at
-1
of this zeta function is the inverse of the Möbius invariant of the coset poset.

In this work, we consider the case of a finite Coxeter group
W
of rank
n
(and its geometric realization as a reflection group in a real vector space
V\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}
).

•

By considering the collection of standard parabolic subgroups, the associated coset poset is the
Coxeter complex
. It also identifies with the face poset of the
W
-permutohedron. This link with the permutohedron shows that the proper part of the poset has the topology of a
(n-1)
-dimensional sphere. Moreover, the character of
W
acting on its
(n-1)
st homology group is the sign character (since each reflection reverses the orientation of the permutohedron).

•

By considering noncrossing parabolic subgroups, the associated coset poset is the
poset of parking functions
. This poset has been introduced by Edelman
[
14
]
in type A, further studied in
[
13
,
12
]
. In the latter, we proved that it is Cohen-Macaulay and gave the character of
W
acting on its
(n-1)
st homology group as a parking character tensored with the sign character.

Motivated by these two examples, we go one step further and investigate the coset poset
\mathcal{P}
associated to the collection of all parabolic subgroups. We show that
\mathcal{P}
is Cohen-Macaulay (Theorem
3.5
), by following the same method as with the parking functions poset
[
12
]
. Consequently, the interesting homology group to study is
\tilde{H}_{n-1}(\bar{\mathcal{P}})
, where
\bar{\mathcal{P}}:=\mathcal{P}-\{W\}
is the proper part of
\mathcal{P}
(see Section
2
for details). Denote by
\boldsymbol{\xi}
the character of
W
acting on this homology group. It is an element of the
parabolic Burnside ring
, which has a natural basis
(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{X})_{X\in\Theta}
that will be used throughout (see Orlik and Solomon
[
19
]
, and the review in
[
11
, Section 3.1]
). The combinatorial nature of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is unraveled as follows:

•

In Theorem
4.1
, we give a simple explicit formula for
\boldsymbol{\xi}
in terms of the Möbius function of the intersection lattice, and the basis
(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{X})_{X\in\Theta}
. It is a straightforward application of the Whitney homology technique devised by Sundaram
[
23
]
.

•

Let
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
denote the sign character of
W
. In Proposition
6.3
, we show that
\boldsymbol{\xi}\otimes\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is positive in the
(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{X})_{X\in\Theta}
basis, and show that the coefficients counts certain facets in the
positive chamber complex

\mathcal{C}^{>0}
.

The complex
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
is defined by taking all Weyl chambers that entirely lie in the positive side
H^{+}
of some generic hyperplane
H
, following Bastidas, Hohlweg, and Saliola
[
4
]
. Some basic properties of this construction will be given in Section
5
. It follows from
[
16
]
that the number of chambers in
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
is
\prod_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}
where
e_{i}
are the exponents of
W
(assuming it is irreducible). By building on this, we will see that
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is essentially an “almost-colored”
f
-vector of
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
, while
\boldsymbol{\xi}\otimes\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is the corresponding “almost-colored”
h
-vector. The precise meaning of this will be given in Proposition
6.3
. In the case where
H^{\perp}
meets the fundamental chamber
F
, this
h
-vector has a nice interpretation in terms of descents (see Theorem
6.4
), similar to the one obtained by Stembridge
[
24
]
in his study of Coxeter cones.

The case of the symmetric group is detailed in Section
7
. Parabolic cosets correspond to combinatorial objects known as
uniform block permutations
, see Sequence
A023998
in the OEIS
[
18
]
and references therein. To our knowledge, the poset structure on uniform block permutations hasn’t been considered before and most of the interest on these objects come from a monoid structure, see
[
15
]
. We give generating functions for the characters
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and for their dimensions, in terms of a Bessel function.

2
The parabolic coset poset

We keep
W
,
n
, and
V\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n}
as in the introduction. Let
\mathcal{L}
be the
intersection lattice
of
W
. We recall that its minimal element is
V
, its rank 1 elements are the
reflecting hyperplanes
(such a hyperplane is the fixed point subspace
\Fix(t)
of a reflection
t\in W
), and any other element is an intersection of reflecting hyperplanes. Its maximal element is the
0
-dimensional subspace
\{0\}
. It is a geometric lattice. Moreover, it is isomorphic to the lattice of parabolic subgroups of
W
(where the order is inclusion) via the map

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\owns X\mapsto W_{X}:=\{w\in W\;:\;X\subset\Fix(w)\}.

(1)

Definition 2.1
.

The
parabolic coset poset
is:

\mathcal{P}:=\biguplus_{X\in\mathcal{L}}W/W_{X}=\big\{wW_{X}\;:\;w\in W,\;X\in\mathcal{L}\big\}.

The partial order is inclusion. The action of
W
is left multiplication.

Clearly,
\mathcal{P}
has a unique maximal element, namely
W
itself. The minimal elements of
\mathcal{P}
are the singletons
\{w\}
for
w
in
W
(cosets modulo the trivial subgroup).

Proposition 2.2
.

For each
w\in W
, the interval
[\{w\},W]
in
\mathcal{P}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{L}
via the map

wW_{X}\mapsto X.

Proof.

Via the action of
w^{-1}
, the interval
[\{w\},W]
is isomorphic to
[\{e\},W]
where
e
is the neutral element of
W
. This interval contains precisely the parabolic subgroups of
W
. It is isomorphic to
\mathcal{L}
via the inverse of the map in (
1
).
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

For each
wW_{X}\in\mathcal{P}
, the order ideal

\{w^{\prime}W_{X^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{P}\;:\;w^{\prime}W_{X^{\prime}}\subset wW_{X}\}

is isomorphic to the parabolic coset poset of
W_{X}
.

Proof.

Via the action of
W
, we can assume
w=e
. First note that
w^{\prime}W_{X^{\prime}}\subset W_{X}
implies
w^{\prime}\in W_{X}
and
W_{X^{\prime}}\subset W_{X}
. So
w^{\prime}W_{X^{\prime}}
is a parabolic coset of
W_{X}
. The result easily follows (the isomorphism mentioned in the proposition is just the identity).
∎

Let
\bar{\mathcal{P}}:=\mathcal{P}-\{W\}
. It is called the
proper part
of
\mathcal{P}
, and is the interesting poset from the topological point of view: indeed, the topology associated to
\mathcal{P}
is trivial since it has a maximum (see
[
25
, Lecture 1]
). We also introduce
\hat{\mathcal{P}}:=\mathcal{P}\cup\{\varnothing\}
. This is again a poset ordered by inclusion, obtained from
\mathcal{P}
by adding the minimal element
\varnothing
. Its topological relevance stems from Philip Hall’s Theorem: the reduced Euler characteristic of the order complex of
\bar{\mathcal{P}}
equals
\mu(\hat{\mathcal{P}})
, the total Möbius invariant of
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
(see
[
25
, Lecture 1]
).

Proposition 2.4
.

The poset
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
is a lattice.

Proof.

The intersection of two parabolic cosets is either empty, or another parabolic coset. This shows that meets exist in
\mathcal{P}
. The existence of joins follows.
∎

3
Topology of the parabolic coset poset

We refer to Wachs
[
25
]
and Kozlov
[
17
]
for poset topology, in particular for the notion of shellability and EL-shellability. We refrain from writing a detailed introduction to this subject, because our topological result is a direct application of known methods and closely follows our previous work
[
12
]
(in the case of parking functions).

As a geometric lattice,
\mathcal{L}
is a shellable poset. We review the construction of an EL-labeling. Let
\mathcal{L}_{1}
be the set of rank 1 elements in
\mathcal{L}
,
i.e.
, the reflecting hyperplanes of
W
in
V
. Let
\prec
be an arbitrary total order on
\mathcal{L}_{1}
. To each cover relation
X\lessdot Y
in
\mathcal{L}
, we associate the label

\displaystyle\lambda(X,Y):=\min_{\prec}\big\{H\in\mathcal{L}_{1}\;:\;X\cap H=Y\big\}.

(2)

Each saturated chain
X_{1}\lessdot\dots\lessdot X_{k}
thus gives a
label sequence

(\lambda(X_{1},X_{2}),\dots,\lambda(X_{k-1},X_{k}))
. This turns out to be an EL-labeling, which means that for each relation
X\leq Y
: i) there exist a unique saturated chain
X\lessdot\dots\lessdot Y
having a
\prec
-increasing label sequence, ii) this increasing label sequence is minimal for the lexicographic order, among saturated chains for
X
to
Y
. The existence of the EL-labeling imply that
\mathcal{L}
is shellable. More precisely, a shelling is obtained by sorting maximal chains of
\mathcal{L}
via the lexicographic order of associated label sequences. We refer to Wachs
[
25
, Section 3.2.3]
for details and background about this.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
A\subset\mathcal{L}_{1}
with
A\neq\varnothing
. The subposet

\mathcal{L}^{(A)}:=\{V\}\cup\{X\in\mathcal{L}\;:\;\exists H\in A,\;H\leq X\}

of
\mathcal{L}
is shellable.

Proof.

Let
\prec
be a total order on
\mathcal{L}_{1}
such that
A
is a prefix. Let
\lambda
be the associated EL-labelling of
\mathcal{L}
defined in (
2
). Note that for each
H\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
, we have
V\lessdot H
and
\lambda(V,H)=H
.

It is straightforward to check that the restriction of
\lambda
is again a EL-labelling of
\mathcal{L}^{(A)}
. Alternatively, the maximal chains of
\mathcal{L}^{(A)}
form a prefix in the lexicographic order on maximal chains of
\mathcal{L}
, and it follows that this prefix is a shelling order for
\mathcal{L}^{(A)}
.
∎

Let
P
be a ranked poset and let
r
denote its rank (
i.e.
, every maximal chain in
P
has cardinality
r+1
). We call
P

spherical
if its order complex is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of
r
-dimensional spheres. A classical tool to prove this property is shellability. In particular, if
P
is bounded (
i.e.
, it has a minimal element
\hat{0}
and a maximal element
\hat{1}
) and has an EL-labeling, then
\bar{P}:=P-\{\hat{0},\hat{1}\}
is spherical. For example,
\bar{\mathcal{L}}
is spherical. The EL-labeling of
\mathcal{L}
naturally extends to an EL-labeling of
\mathcal{P}
, but it is not clear how to get an EL-labeling of
\hat{\mathcal{P}}=\mathcal{P}\cup\{\varnothing\}
. So, we use an alternative inductive method and the main tool is the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
P
be a ranked poset of rank
r
. Suppose that
P=P_{1}\cup P_{2}
where

•

P_{1}
and
P_{2}
are spherical posets of rank
r
,

•

P_{1}\cap P_{2}
is a spherical poset of rank
r-1
,

•

every chain in
P
is included in
P_{1}
or
P_{2}
(this condition means that the order complex of
P
is geometrically the union of those of
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
).

Then
P
is spherical as well.

Proof.

This is the same as
[
12
, Lemma 5.1]
. The idea is to use the Mayer-Vietoris long exact sequence to compute the homology of
P
in terms of those of
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
, and
P_{1}\cap P_{2}
. The third condition above is the assumption that permits to use this technique. We obtain that this homology is a free abelian group in degree
r
(via the first two conditions above, and the fact that an extension of two free abelian groups is a free abelian group), and
0
in other degrees. This suffices to characterize a wedge of
r
-dimensional spheres. See
[
12
]
for details.
∎

Let us introduce some notations needed for the proof of the next proposition. The Coxeter length of
w\in W
is denoted by
\ell(x)
. The (right) inversion set of
w\in W
is:

\Inv(w):=\big\{\Fix(t)\;:\;t\in T\text{ such that }\ell(wt)<\ell(w)\big\}.

It is convenient to use reflecting hyperplanes rather than reflections here, in order to apply Lemma
3.1
with
A=\Inv(w)
. Also, recall that the right weak order on
W
is defined by
w_{1}\leq w_{2}
iff
\Inv(w_{1})\subset\Inv(w_{2})
.

Proposition 3.3
.

The poset
\bar{\mathcal{P}}
is spherical (of rank
n-1
).

Proof.

We follow the scheme of proof in
[
12
, Section 5]
. For any nonempty subset
A\subset W
, define:

\bar{\mathcal{P}}[A]:=\Big\{wW_{X}\;:\;X\in\mathcal{L}\backslash\{0\},\;w\in A\Big\}\subset\bar{\mathcal{P}}.

This is the
order filter
of
\bar{\mathcal{P}}
generated by the elements
\{w\}
with
w\in A
. Now, let
w_{1},w_{2},\dots
be a linear extension of the right weak order (an indexing of the elements of
W
, such that
w_{i}\leq w_{j}
in the right weak order implies
i\leq j
). Let
A_{i}:=\{w_{j}\;:\;1\leq j\leq i\}
. The idea is to show that
\bar{\mathcal{P}}[A_{i}]
is spherical by induction on
i
, using Lemma
3.2
. The maximal value of
i
is
|W|
, and clearly we have
A_{|W|}=W
and
\bar{\mathcal{P}}[W]=\bar{\mathcal{P}}
. Also note that
\bar{\mathcal{P}}[A_{1}]
has a minimal element (namely
w_{1}
), so that this poset is topologically trivial.

The induction is as follows. Assume that
\bar{\mathcal{P}}[A_{i}]
is spherical of rank
n-1
. The poset
\bar{\mathcal{P}}[\{w_{i+1}\}]
is too, because it is topologically trivial. Each chain in
\bar{\mathcal{P}}[A_{i+1}]=\bar{\mathcal{P}}[A_{i}]\cup\bar{\mathcal{P}}[\{w_{i+1}\}]
is included in either
\bar{\mathcal{P}}[A_{i}]
or
\bar{\mathcal{P}}[\{w_{i+1}\}]
(because the minimal element of the chain is either in
\bar{\mathcal{P}}[A_{i}]
or
\bar{\mathcal{P}}[\{w_{i+1}\}]
). The last condition to check in order to apply Lemma
3.2
is that
\bar{\mathcal{P}}[A_{i}]\cap\bar{\mathcal{P}}[\{w_{i+1}\}]
is spherical of rank
n-2
. Lemma
3.4
below permits to identify this poset with the proper part of
\mathcal{L}^{(\Inv(w_{i+1}))}
. This poset is shellable by Lemma
3.1
(
\Inv(w_{i+1})\neq\varnothing
because
w_{1}=e
is the unique element with empty inversion set), so that its proper part is spherical.

This completes the induction showing that
\mathcal{P}[A_{i}]
is spherical for all
i
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

In the notations of the above proof, there is an isomorphism:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}[A_{i}]\cap\mathcal{P}[\{w_{i+1}\}]\to\mathcal{L}^{(\Inv(w_{i+1}))},

(3)

explicitly given by the map
w_{i+1}W_{X}\mapsto X
.

Proof.

Since this map is the restriction of the isomorphism
\mathcal{P}[\{w_{i+1}\}]\to\mathcal{L}
(as in Proposition
2.2
), it remains to check:

\displaystyle w_{i+1}W_{X}\in\mathcal{P}[A_{i}]\Longleftrightarrow X\in\mathcal{L}^{(\Inv(w_{i+1}))}.

(4)

To do this, let
u
be the minimal length representative of
w_{i+1}W_{X}
. We have
u\leq w_{i+1}
in the right weak order, moreover
u<w_{i+1}
is equivalent to
u\in A_{i}
(since, by construction,
A_{i}
contains all elements smaller than
w_{i+1}
in the right weak order). Let us check that both sides of (
4
) are equivalent to
u<w_{i+1}
:

•

The left-hand side is equivalent to the existence of
v\in A_{i}
such that
v\in w_{i+1}W_{X}
, which is equivalent to
u<w_{i+1}
by unicity of the minimal length representative.

•

The right-hand side is equivalent to the existence of
t\in T
such that
\Fix(t)\in\Inv(w_{i+1})
and
X\subset\Fix(t)
. This is equivalent to the existence of
t\in W_{X}\cap T
, with
\ell(w_{i+1}t)<\ell(w_{i+1})
,
i.e.
,
w_{i+1}
not being the minimal length representative in
W/W_{X}
.

∎

For any poset
P
, let
\hat{P}
denote the smallest bounded poset containing
P
, (obtained by adding a maximal/minimal element if it doesn’t already have one. (This notation agrees with
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
introduced earlier.) A ranked poset
P
is
Cohen-Macaulay
if every open interval in
\hat{P}
is spherical. For example, a shellable poset is Cohen-Macaulay. See again
[
17
,
25
]
for details.

Theorem 3.5
.

The poset
\mathcal{P}
is Cohen-Macaulay.

Proof.

Let
(x,y)
be an open interval in
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
.

First, consider the case where
x,y\in\mathcal{P}
. By Proposition
2.2
,
(x,y)
is isomorphic to an open interval in
\mathcal{L}
. This open interval is spherical, by shellability of
\mathcal{L}
.

Consider the case where
x
is the minimal element (
x=\varnothing
, in the convention of the previous section). The open interval
(x,y)
in
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
is thus the principal order ideal generated by
y
in
\mathcal{P}
. By Proposition
2.3
, an order ideal
\{w^{\prime}W_{X^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{P}\;:\;w^{\prime}W_{X^{\prime}}\subsetneq wW_{X}\}
identifies to the poset
\overline{P}
attached to the parabolic subgroup
W_{X}
. By induction on the rank of
W
, we can assume that it is spherical.

The last case to check is that
\overline{\mathcal{P}}
is spherical. This is Proposition
3.3
.

We thus have proved that every open interval in
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
is spherical, so
\mathcal{P}
is Cohen-Macaulay by definition.
∎

4
Homology character

Let us introduce some characters that were studied by Orlik and Solomon
[
19
]
. We also refer to the review in
[
11
, Section 3.1]
. For
X\in\mathcal{L}
, let

\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{X}:=\Ind_{W_{X}}^{W}(\mathbf{1}),

(5)

i.e.
, the trivial character of
W_{X}
induced on
W
. This is the character of the representation
\mathbb{C}^{W/W_{X}}
(with the natural left action of
W
on
W/W_{X}
). For example,
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{V}
is the character of the regular representation, and
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\{0\}}
is the trivial character. The
parabolic Burnside ring
of
W
is the subring of the character ring of
W
linearly generated by
(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{X})_{X\in\mathcal{L}}
. Let
\Theta
be a set of representatives for the orbits of
W
acting on
\mathcal{L}
. Then
(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{X})_{X\in\Theta}
is a linear basis of the parabolic Burnside ring.

Recall from the introduction that the character of
W
acting on
\tilde{H}_{n-1}(\bar{P})
is denoted by
\boldsymbol{\xi}
. Since
\mathcal{P}
is Cohen-Macaulay,
\boldsymbol{\xi}
can be computed via
Whitney homology
. This technique was introduced by Sundaram
[
23
]
, see also Wachs
[
25
, Section 4.4]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mu
denote the Möbius function of
\mathcal{L}
, and
\mu(X)=\mu(X,\{0\})
for
X\in\mathcal{L}
. The character of
W
acting on
\tilde{H}_{n-1}(\bar{\mathcal{P}})
is:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}=(-1)^{n}\sum_{X\in\mathcal{L}}\mu(X)\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{X}.

(6)

Alternatively, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}=(-1)^{n}\sum_{X\in\Theta}\mu(X)\cdot[W:N(W_{X})]\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{X},

(7)

where
N(W_{X})
is the normalizer of
W_{X}
in
W
.

Proof.

This is a direct application of the methods of Sundaram
[
23
]
. We use the formulation in
[
25
, Theorem 4.4.1]
, with the only
caveat
that we need to consider the dual poset
\mathcal{P}^{*}
to match the exact formulation of this reference (the dual poset is defined via
x\leq y
in
\mathcal{P}
iff
x\geq y
in
\mathcal{P}^{*}
and it satisfies
\mu_{\mathcal{P}}(x,y)=\mu_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}(y,x)
).

First note that the parabolic subgroups
W_{X}
are orbit representatives for the action of
W
on
\mathcal{P}
. For any such
W_{X}
, the open interval
(W_{X},W)
in
\mathcal{P}
identifies to the interval
(X,\{0\})
in
\mathcal{L}
via the inverse of the map in (
1
). Since
\mathcal{L}
is shellable (and consequently Cohen-Macaulay), the homology of the interval
(X,\{0\})
is given by:

\tilde{H}_{i}\big((X,\{0\})\big)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z}^{|\mu(X)|}&\text{ if }i=\dim(X)-2,\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note also that
W_{X}
acts trivially on
(X,\{0\})
(for
Y
in this interval and
w\in W_{X}
, we have
Y\subset X\subset\Fix(w)
). By definition,
W_{X}
thus contributes to the
k
th
Whitney homology group

WH_{k}(\mathcal{P})
(where
k=\dim(X)
) by a term
|\mu(X)|\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{X}=(-1)^{\dim(X)}\mu(X)\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{X}
, so that

\displaystyle WH_{k}(\mathcal{P})=\sum_{X\in\mathcal{L},\;\dim(X)=k}(-1)^{k}\mu(X)\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{X}.

(8)

The theorem by Sundaram mentioned at the beginning of this proof, in presence of the Cohen-Macaulay property (Theorem
3.5
), says that we have the following identity in the representation ring of
W
:

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{n-1}(\bar{\mathcal{P}})=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{n+k}WH_{k}(\mathcal{P}).

(9)

Combining (
8
) and (
9
) gives (
6
).

The alternative formula in (
7
) gives the coefficients of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
in the basis
(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{X})_{X\in\Theta}
. To get this, note that
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{X}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{Y}
if
X=w(Y)
for some
w\in W
. It remains only to show that the orbit of
X
for the action of
W
has cardinality
[W:N(W_{X})]
. This comes from the orbit-stabilizer theorem, because the stabilizer of
X
is
N(W_{X})
(we have
w(X)=X
iff
wW_{X}w^{-1}=W_{X}
).
∎

Remark 4.2

(Rank-selected subcomplexes)
.

Our proof of Theorem
4.1

Similarly, we define rank-selected subposets of the intersection lattice

Again,

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{|R|-1}(\mathcal{P}_{R})=(-1)^{|R|}\sum_{X\in\mathcal{L}_{R}\cup\{0\}}\mu_{R}(X)\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{X}.

(10)

We announced in the introduction that

.

The multiplicity of

Remark 4.4
.

Assume that

is irreducible, and let

By the previous proposition, the multiplicity of

5
The positive chamber complex

We mentioned above that the Coxeter complex
\mathcal{C}
can be seen as a coset poset (with respect to standard parabolic subgroups). But we take here a geometric point of view in terms of chambers. Let
F\subset V
be the fundamental chamber, so that the other chambers are the images
w(F)
for
w\in W
. The closure
\overline{F}
is a full-dimensional simplicial cone in
V
, and its faces are also simplicial cones. Let
\mathcal{C}
denote the
Coxeter complex
. By definition, every face
f\in\mathcal{C}
can be written
w(f^{\prime})
where
w\in W
and
f^{\prime}
is a face of
\overline{F}
. We refer to
[
6
]
. The geometric realization of
\mathcal{C}
as an abstract simplicial complex is a triangulation of the
(n-1)
-dimensional sphere, which is obtained explicitly by intersecting the simplicial cones in
\mathcal{C}
with the unit sphere of
V
. This triangulation of the
(n-1)
-dimensional sphere will be used to work out concrete examples of
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
in rank 3.

Remark 5.1
.

Below, the dimension
\dim(f)
of a face
f\in\mathcal{C}
means
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(\Span(f))
. We use the same convention for the subcomplex
\mathcal{C}^{>0}\subset\mathcal{C}
. If we were to consider
\mathcal{C}
as an abstract simplicial complex, the dimension of
f\in\mathcal{C}
as a simplex would be
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(\Span(f))-1
.

Remark 5.2
.

The
1
-dimensional cones in
\mathcal{C}
, (
i.e.
, its vertices as an abstract simplicial complex) are called
rays
. In the crystallographic case, each ray is the positive span of a weight (in the sense of Lie theory), and rays of
\overline{F}
correspond to fundamental weights.

Let
\rho\in F
be a generic vector. Here,
generic
means that
\rho
isn’t orthogonal to any ray of
\mathcal{C}
. Let
H:=\rho^{\perp}
, and

\displaystyle H^{-}:=\big\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;:\;\langle\alpha|\rho\rangle\leq 0\big\},\hskip 20.00003ptH^{+}:=\big\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;:\;\langle\alpha|\rho\rangle\geq 0\big\}.

(11)

Definition 5.3
.

The
positive chamber complex
is defined by

\mathcal{C}^{>0}:=\{f\in\mathcal{C}\;:\;f\subset H^{+}\}.

It is a subcomplex of the Coxeter complex.

Let us give examples in rank 3, where the complex is
2
-dimensional. We can see
\mathcal{C}
as a triangulation of the
2
-dimensional unit sphere in a
3
-dimensional real vector space, then use stereographic projection on the plane. Each hyperplane thus becomes a circle or a straight line. The examples are in Figures
1
,
2
,
4
,
5
.
The generic hyperplane is the red circle. The gray chamber is the fundamental chamber, and the rest of
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
is in light gray. These examples in rank 3 lead to the following (negative) observations.

•

The combinatorial structure of
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
depends on the chosen generic vector
\rho
. For example, the two examples in type
A_{3}
(respectively,
B_{3}
) are non-isomorphic complexes.

•

The complex
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
might not be convex in
\mathcal{C}
. In all examples but the first one, there is a pair of chambers in
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
and a minimal gallery between them which doesn’t stay in
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
.

We can already see why
\rho
is required not to be orthogonal to any ray. Indeed, this condition ensures that each chamber
C
of
\mathcal{C}
is such that:

•

either
C
is in the interior of
H^{+}
or
H^{-}
,

•

or
H
meets (the interior of)
C
.

Consequently, a perturbation of
\rho
(equivalently, of
H
) does not change the fact that a given chamber is in
H^{+}
or not. This means that
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
is invariant under perturbation of
\rho
. It is natural to introduce the hyperplane arrangement

\mathcal{D}:=\Big\{f^{\perp}\;:\;f\text{ ray of }\mathcal{C}\Big\}

in
V
, called the
dual arrangement
of
W
. See Figure
3
in type
A_{3}
, and Figure
6
in type
B_{3}
. The regions of the dual arrangement are open sets where
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
is constant (as a function of
\rho
).

Let us examine what happens when
\rho
crosses an hyperplane of
\mathcal{D}
. Denote by
\rho_{1}
the choice taken in Figure
1
,
\rho_{2}
the choice taken in Figure
2
, and
\rho^{\prime}
the point where a shortest path from
\rho_{1}
to
\rho_{2}
crosses an hyperplane of
\mathcal{D}
. Their locations in
F
is approximately as follows (repeating the picture in Figure
3
):

\rho_{1}
\rho_{2}
\rho^{\prime}

Let
f
be the unique ray such that
\rho^{\prime}\in f^{\perp}
,
f\in\mathcal{C}^{>0}(\rho_{1})
, and
-f\in\mathcal{C}^{>0}(\rho_{2})
. We have the following observations.

•

The facets in
\mathcal{C}^{>0}(\rho_{1})
incident to
f
are exactly those not in
\mathcal{C}^{>0}(\rho_{2})
.

•

Similarly, the facets in
\mathcal{C}^{>0}(\rho_{2})
incident to
-f
are exactly those not in
\mathcal{C}^{>0}(\rho_{1})

•

There is a bijection between the facets described in the previous two items (explicitly, compose the antipodal map
\alpha\mapsto-\alpha
defined on all chambers with the antipodal maps defined on chambers adjacent to
f
).

•

We have
\mathcal{C}^{>0}(\rho^{\prime})=\mathcal{C}^{>0}(\rho_{1})\cup\mathcal{C}^{>0}(\rho_{2})
, in particular
\mathcal{C}^{>0}(\rho^{\prime})
contains strictly more facets than
\mathcal{C}^{>0}(\rho_{1})
or
\mathcal{C}^{>0}(\rho_{2})
.

As a consequence, the number of facets of
\mathcal{C}^{>0}(\rho)
is minimal if and only if
\rho
is generic.

After these observations, we will (following
[
4
]
) use the result below to obtain the number of facets of
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
in the generic case.

Proposition 5.4

(Greene and Zaslawky,
[
16
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an hyperplane arrangement in a real vector space, and
L
the associated intersection lattice. Let
H
be a generic hyperplane in the same vector space, and
H^{+}
one of the half-spaces defined by
H
. Then, the Möbius invariant
\mu(L)
is, in absolute value, the number of regions of
\mathcal{A}
in
H^{+}
.

So, the number of facets in
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
is
(-1)^{n}\mu(\mathcal{L})
(in particular, it doesn’t depend on
\rho
as soon as
\rho
is generic). These facets are thus natural candidates to look for a combinatorial interpretation of the coefficients
c_{X}
defined at the end of Section
4
. The previous proposition will also be used in Section
6
to take into account all faces of
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
, not just facets.

Proposition 5.5
.

If
\rho\in F
, the set

\displaystyle\big\{w\in W\;:\;w(\overline{F})\in\mathcal{C}^{>0}\big\}

(12)

is an order ideal in
W
for the right weak order.

Proof.

By definition, a cover relation in the right weak order is
ws\lessdot w
for
w\in W
and
s\in S
such that

\ell(ws)<\ell(w)
(where
\ell
is the Coxeter length). Assuming
w(\overline{F})\in\mathcal{C}^{>0}
, we have to show
ws(\overline{F})\in\mathcal{C}^{>0}
.

Let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
be vectors that span the
n
rays of
w(\overline{F})
, indexed so that the positive span of
\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n}
is the common panel of
w(\overline{F})
and
ws(\overline{F})
. The
n
rays of
ws(\overline{F})
are
\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n}
and
t(\alpha_{1})
, where
t
is the unique reflection in
W
that fixes
\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n}
,
i.e.
,
t=wsw^{-1}
. Eventually, let
\beta\in V
such that
t(\beta)=-\beta
and
\langle\rho|\beta\rangle>0
.

The condition
ws\lessdot w
means that
\overline{F}
and
ws(\overline{F})
are on the same side of the hyperplane
\Fix(t)
. So,
\langle t(\alpha_{1})|\beta\rangle
has the same sign as
\langle\rho|\beta\rangle
,
i.e.
,
\langle t(\alpha_{1})|\beta\rangle>0
. We thus have
\langle\alpha_{1}|\beta\rangle<0
.

We have:

t(\alpha_{1})=\alpha_{1}-2\frac{\langle\alpha_{1}|\beta\rangle}{\langle\beta|\beta\rangle}\beta.

So,

\langle t(\alpha_{1})|\rho\rangle=\langle\alpha_{1}|\rho\rangle-2\frac{\langle\alpha_{1}|\beta\rangle}{\langle\beta|\beta\rangle}\langle\beta|\rho\rangle.

The first term is positive because
\alpha_{1}
spans a ray of
w(\overline{F})
which is in
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
. We have seen that
\langle\alpha_{1}|\beta\rangle<0
and
\langle\beta|\rho\rangle>0
. We get
\langle t(\alpha_{1})|\rho\rangle>0
from the previous equation. The
n
rays of
ws(\overline{F})
are thus in
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
. It follows that
ws(\overline{F})\in\mathcal{C}^{>0}
as required.
∎

Theorem 5.6
.

The complex
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
is shellable.

Proof.

Via the action of
W
, we can assume
\rho\in F
without changing the combinatorial structure of
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
. So we can use Proposition
5.5
.

Let
w_{1},w_{2},\dots
be a linear extension of the right weak order on
W
. By
[
5
, Theorem 2.1]
, the sequence of facets
w_{1}(\overline{F}),w_{2}(\overline{F}),\dots
is a shelling of
\mathcal{C}
.

The linear extension can be chosen so that the elements
w
satisfying
w(\overline{F})\in\mathcal{C}^{>0}
form a prefix, since these elements form an order ideal (by Proposition
5.5
). By definition, a prefix of a shelling order is a shelling order of the corresponding subcomplex. So
w_{1}(\overline{F}),w_{2}(\overline{F}),\dots
(where we keep only the facets of
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
) is a shelling of
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
.
∎

Theorem 5.7
.

The geometric realization of
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
is homeomorphic to a
(n-1)
-dimensional ball.

Proof.

As a consequence of shellability, any pair of chambers in
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
can be joined by a gallery that stays in
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
. Moreover, each
(n-2)
-dimensional face of
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
belongs to either 1 or 2 chambers of
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
(because this holds in
\mathcal{C}
). These conditions imply that
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
is a pseudomanifold with boundary.

By Danaraj and Klee
[
10
]
, a shellable pseudo-manifold with boundary is PL-homeomorphic to a closed ball. The result follows. (Alternatively, we could see the stereographic projection of the geometric realization
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
as a star-convex with respect to a point in the fundamental chamber.)
∎

6
The
f
- and
h
-vectors

The
f
-vector of an
(n-1)
-dimensional simplicial complex is
(f_{i})_{-1\leq i\leq n-1}
, where
f_{i}
is the number of
i
-dimensional faces. Its
h
-vector
(h_{i})_{0\leq i\leq n}
is then defined via the relation:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}f_{i-1}z^{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}h_{i}z^{i}(1+z)^{n-i}.

(13)

This
h
-vector is known to encode important information about the simplicial complex. A classical example states that the
h
-vector of the Coxeter complex
\mathcal{C}
are the coefficients of the
W
-Eulerian polynomial
\sum_{w\in W}z^{\operatorname{des}(w)}
.

Let
S
be the set of simple reflections of
W
. Define a “coloring” map from the vertices of
\mathcal{C}
to
S
by
wW_{(s)}\mapsto s
(where
W_{(s)}
is the subgroup generated by
S\backslash\{s\}
). This shows that
\mathcal{C}
is a
balanced
complex, which means that the
n
vertices of any facet are colored by the
n
elements of
S
. See
[
5
]
. The
h
-vector of a balanced complex can be refined via the notion of
flag
h
-vector
. In the present context, the positive chamber complex
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
is again a balanced complex. But the natural refinement of the
f
- and
h
- vectors is obtained using
\Theta
rather than subsets of
S
. Indeed, each subset
I\subset S
naturally gives an element of
X\in\Theta
by the condition that
W_{X}
is conjugate to the standard parabolic subgroup
W_{I}
(generated by
I
). The corresponding notion of
f
-vector and
h
-vector are what we called “almost-colored” above, and they can be seen as the coefficients of some element in the parabolic Burnside ring.

The next theorem says that
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is the (suitably signed) almost-colored
f
-vector of
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
.

Theorem 6.1
.

We have:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}=(-1)^{n}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{C}^{>0}}(-1)^{\dim(f)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\Span(f)}.

(14)

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}
denote the Coxeter arrangement
\{\Fix(t)\;:\;t\in T\}
in
V
. Let
X\in\mathcal{L}
. We count the number of
f\in\mathcal{C}^{>0}
with
\Span(f)=X
. To do this, consider the
restricted arrangement
:

\mathcal{A}^{X}:=\big\{X\cap Y\;:\;Y\in\mathcal{A},\;\dim(X\cap Y)=\dim(X)-1\big\}.

This is a hyperplane arrangement in
X
. Its intersection lattice is the interval
[X,\{0\}]
in
\mathcal{L}
, so its total Möbius invariant is
\mu(X)
(where
\mu
is the Möbius function of
\mathcal{L}
as before). Moreover, the regions of
\mathcal{A}^{X}
are the faces
f\in\mathcal{C}
with
\Span(f)=X
. By Proposition
5.4
(using
H^{+}\cap X
as a generic half-space in
X
), we obtain:

\#\big\{f\in\mathcal{C}^{>0}\;:\;\Span(f)=X\big\}=|\mu(X)|=(-1)^{\dim(X)}\mu(X).

It follows that the right-hand side of (
14
) is equal to the right-hand side of (
6
).
∎

The next step is to identify
\boldsymbol{\xi}\otimes\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
with the corresponding almost-colored
h
-vector. Let us first introduce some tools for dealing with tensoring by the sign character.

If
I\subset S
, let
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{I}:=\Ind_{W_{I}}^{W}(\mathbf{1})
. This notation is compatible with the definition of
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{X}
in (
5
), because the lattice of subsets of
I
naturally embeds in
\mathcal{L}
via

I\mapsto\bigcap_{s\in I}\Fix(s).

(15)

(In terms of parabolic subgroups, this is the inclusion of standard parabolic subgroups in all parabolic subgroups.)
The notation in terms of subsets of
S
is convenient to state
Solomon’s formula

[
20
]
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{I}\otimes\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=\sum_{J\subset I}(-1)^{\#J}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{J}.

(16)

However, in the present context it is better to forget about subsets of
S
and have a formulation in terms of
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{L}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
f,g\in\mathcal{C}
, such that
f\subset g
and
\dim(g)=n
(
g
is a chamber). We have:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\Span(f)}\otimes\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C},\\
f\subset f^{\prime}\subset g\end{subarray}}(-1)^{n-\dim(f^{\prime})}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\Span(f^{\prime})}.

(17)

Proof.

Using the action of
W
, we can assume that
g
is the fundamental chamber. The subsets of
S
identify with the faces of
g
through the map

I\mapsto g\cap\bigg(\bigcap_{s\in I}\Fix(s)\bigg),

and this map reverses the order relations. Equation (
17
) becomes (
16
) through this identification.
∎

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
f_{1},f_{2},\dots
be a shelling of
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
.
The type
\tau(f_{i})
of a facet of
f_{i}\in\mathcal{C}^{>0}
is defined as follows: let
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
be the panels of
f_{i}
that are not panel of
f_{j}
for some
j<i
, then

\tau(f_{i}):=\Span\Big(\bigcap_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\Big)\in\mathcal{L}.

We have:

\boldsymbol{\xi}\otimes\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{C}^{>0},\;\dim(f)=n}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\tau(f)}.

(18)

Proof.

Since
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\otimes\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=\mathbf{1}
, Equation (
18
) is equivalent to:

\boldsymbol{\xi}=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{C}^{>0},\;\dim(f)=n}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\tau(f)}\otimes\boldsymbol{\epsilon}.

(19)

Let
\mathcal{D}^{(j)}
be the subcomplex of
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
having
f_{1},\dots,f_{j}
as facets. Being a shelling means that the intersection of
\mathcal{D}^{(j-1)}
with
f_{j}
is purely
(n-1)
-dimensional (it is a nonempty union of panels of
f_{j}
). We will show that for
j\geq 1
, we have:

(-1)^{n}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{D}^{(j)},\;f\notin\mathcal{D}^{(j-1)}}(-1)^{\dim(f)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\Span(f)}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\tau(f_{j})}\otimes\boldsymbol{\epsilon}

(20)

(if
j=1
, the condition
f\notin\mathcal{D}^{(j-1)}
is omitted). By summing over
j
, we get

(-1)^{n}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{C}^{>0}}(-1)^{\dim(f)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\Span(f)}=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{C}^{>0},\;\dim(f)=n}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\tau(f)}\otimes\boldsymbol{\epsilon}.

(21)

This is equivalent to (
19
), by Theorem
6.1
. It remains only to prove (
20
).

For a fixed
j
, let
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
be the panels of
f_{j}
that aren’t a panel of
f_{i}
for
i<j
(
i.e.
,
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\notin\mathcal{D}^{(j-1)}
), and let
p_{k+1},\dots,p_{n}
be the other panels of
f_{j}
. From the definition of a shelling, a face
f\in f_{j}
is in
\mathcal{D}^{(j-1)}
iff it is included in some panel among
p_{k+1},\dots,p_{n}
. Since the faces of
f_{j}
form a boolean lattice, it follows that the faces
f\in\mathcal{D}^{(j)}
such that
f\notin\mathcal{D}^{(j-1)}
are the faces of
f_{j}
that can be written as an intersection
\cap_{i\in I}p_{i}
where
I\subset\{1,\dots,k\}
. These faces form the interval between
\cap_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}
and
f_{j}
in the face lattice of
f_{j}
, so that

\sum_{f\in\mathcal{D}^{(j)},\;f\notin\mathcal{D}^{(j-1)}}(-1)^{\dim(f)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\Span(f)}=\sum_{\cap_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\subset f\subset f_{j}}(-1)^{\dim(f)}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\Span(f)}.

(22)

By Lemma
6.2
, the latter expression is
(-1)^{n}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\tau(f_{j})}\otimes\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
. This proves (
20
).
∎

We eventually arrive at the result announced in the introduction. It is much more satisfying than the previous proposition, because it gives a formula for
\boldsymbol{\xi}\otimes\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
that doesn’t involve a shelling order.

The
ascents
and
descents
of
w\in W
are two complementary subsets of
S
defined, using the Coxeter length
\ell
, by:

\des(w):=\{s\in S\;:\;\ell(ws)<\ell(w)\},\hskip 20.00003pt\asc(w):=\{s\in S\;:\;\ell(ws)>\ell(w)\}.

Recall that for
I\subset S
, we defined
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{I}:=\Ind_{W_{I}}^{W}(\mathbf{1})
(see the paragraph before Lemma
6.2
).

Theorem 6.4
.

If
\rho\in F
(and
H
,
H^{+}
,
H^{-}
are defined accordingly via (
11
)), we have:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}\otimes\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in W\\
w(F)\subset H^{+}\end{subarray}}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\asc(w)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in W\\
w(F)\subset H^{-}\end{subarray}}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\des(w)}.

(23)

Proof.

We only prove the first equality. The other is deduced by taking
-\rho
and
-F
in place of
\rho
and
F
. (In the case where the long element
w_{\circ}\in W
is central and thus equal to
-I
, we have
w_{\circ}(H)=H
. The two sums are then equal, because we can pair elements on each side via the map
w\mapsto ww_{\circ}
.)

It remains to prove the first equality. Let
f_{1},f_{2},\dots
be the shelling of
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
given by Theorem
5.6
. The idea is to use Proposition
6.3
with this shelling.

Let
f_{j}
be a facet of
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
. It corresponds to
w\in W
such that
f_{j}=w(\overline{F})\subset H^{+}
. Let
p
be a panel of
f_{j}
. There is
s\in S
such that the two chambers adjacent to
p
are
w(F)
and
ws(F)
. Note that

\Span(p)=\Fix(wsw^{-1}).

By definition,
s
is a descent of
w
iff
\ell(ws)<\ell(w)
, iff
ws(\overline{F})
precedes
w(\overline{F})
in the shelling order, iff
p
is a panel of
f_{i}
with
i<j
. Let
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
be the panels of
f_{j}
that are not panel of
f_{i}
for some
i<j
. We thus have

\tau(f_{j})=\Span\bigg(\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}p_{k}\bigg)=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\Span(p_{k})=\bigcap_{s\in\asc(w)}\Fix(wsw^{-1})=w\bigg(\bigcap_{s\in\asc(w)}\Fix(s)\bigg).

Recall that we identified subsets of
S
to some elements in
\mathcal{L}
via (
15
). It follows
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\tau(f_{j})}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\asc(w)}
. We thus obtain the result by reformulation of Proposition
6.3
.
∎

Remark 6.5

(Equivariant
h
-vectors for
\bar{\mathcal{P}}
)
.

For a Cohen-Macaulay poset like
\bar{\mathcal{P}}
, the
h
-vector of its order complex
\Delta(\bar{\mathcal{P}})
records information about the top homology dimensions of its type-selected subposets. If the poset comes with a group action (a
W
-action in our case), this can be generalized to a statement about representations
[
22
]
:

h_{i}=\sum_{R\subset[n]\atop|R|=i}\dim\big(\tilde{H}_{|R|-1}(\mathcal{P}_{R})\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbf{h_{i}}:=_{W}\bigoplus_{R\subset[n]\atop|R|=i}\tilde{H}_{|R|-1}(\mathcal{P}_{R}),

where the
\mathbf{h_{i}}
, defined as direct sums of
W
-modules
\tilde{H}_{|R|-1}(\mathcal{P}_{R})
, form what is known as the
equivariant
h
-vector
of
\Delta(\bar{\mathcal{P}})
. The right hand sides of the two equations above are usually denoted
h_{R}
and
\mathbf{h_{R}}
and form the flag (and equivariant flag)
h
-vector of
\Delta(\bar{\mathcal{P}})
(which is balanced since
\bar{\mathcal{P}}
is ranked). The entries
h_{n}=h_{[n]}
and
\mathbf{h_{n}=h_{[n]}}
are respectively the dimension and the homology character of the group action on the top homology of
\Delta(\bar{\mathcal{P}})
.

After Remark
4.2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{h_{R}}=(-1)^{|R|}\sum_{X\in\mathcal{L}_{R}\cup\{0\}}\mu_{R}(X)\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{X}.

(24)

The entries
h_{i}
of the
h
-vector sum up to the number of maximal chains of the poset, which in our case equals
|W|\cdot\dfrac{|W|\cdot n!}{2^{n}}
by
[
9
, Prop. 4.3]
. However, no direct combinatorial interpretation is known for them. Moreover, a direct generalization of Theorem
6.4
does not hold. We calculate the equivariant
h
-vector for
A_{3}
in Remark
7.9
and observe that it is not (alone or tensored by sign) a positive sum in the
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
-basis.

7
The case of the symmetric group

Let
\Pi_{n}
denote the lattice of set partitions of
[n]
. The order is reverse refinement (the finest/coarsest set partition is the bottom/top element). It is well-known that the intersection lattice for
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is isomorphic to
\Pi_{n}
. The explicit construction is as follows. The geometric representation is
V=\{(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;:\;\sum v_{i}=0\}
where
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
acts by permuting the
n
coordinates:

\sigma\cdot(v_{1},\dots,v_{n}):=(v_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},\dots,v_{\sigma^{-1}(n)}).

To
\pi\in\Pi_{n}
, we associate the subspace of
V
defined by the equations
v_{i}=v_{j}
for each pair
i,j
of integers that are in the same block of
\pi
. This is an isomorphism between
\Pi_{n}
and the intersection lattice of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

Definition 7.1
.

A
uniform block permutation
(of size
n
) is a triple
(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\lambda)
where

•

\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in\Pi_{n}
,

•

\lambda
is a bijection from (the blocks of)
\pi_{1}
to (those of)
\pi_{2}
,

•

for each block
b\in\pi_{1}
,
\#b=\#\lambda(b)
.

The order relation is given by
(\pi^{\prime}_{1},\pi^{\prime}_{2},\lambda^{\prime})\geq(\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\lambda)
if: i)
\pi^{\prime}_{1}\geq\pi_{1}
and
\pi^{\prime}_{2}\geq\pi_{2}
in
\Pi_{n}
, ii)
\lambda^{\prime}
is the natural quotient map obtained from
\lambda
.

For example, a uniform block permutation of size
9
is given by the pair of set partitions

(178|25|349|6,237|14|569|8),

where
\lambda
is implicitly given by matching blocks with the same index:
178\mapsto 237
,
25\mapsto 14
,
etc
. A smaller element is

(18|7|25|34|9|6,37|2|14|69|5|8).

Minimal elements naturally correspond to permutations of
\{1,\dots,n\}
by

\mathfrak{S}_{n}\owns\sigma\mapsto(1|2|3|\dots|n,\sigma_{1}|\sigma_{2}|\sigma_{3}|\dots|\sigma_{n}).

The poset of uniform block permutations coincides with the type
A
parabolic coset poset, and the correspondence is as follows. The parabolic subgroups of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
are indexed by
\Pi_{n}
and are the products

\mathfrak{S}_{\pi}:=\prod_{\beta\in\pi}\mathfrak{S}_{\beta},\;(\pi\in\Pi_{n}),

together with their natural action on
\{1,\dots,n\}
(
\mathfrak{S}_{\beta}
permutes the elements of
\beta
). For each
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
\pi\in\Pi_{n}
, the parabolic coset
\sigma\mathfrak{S}_{\pi}
is associated to the uniform block permutation
(\pi,\sigma(\pi),\bar{\sigma})
where
\bar{\sigma}
is the quotient map induced by
\sigma
.

Recall that characters of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
are in correspondence with degree
n
symmetric functions, via the Frobenius characteristic
\Fr
. It is such
that

\Fr(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\pi})=H_{\lambda},\hskip 20.00003pt\Fr(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\pi}\otimes\boldsymbol{\epsilon})=E_{\lambda},

where
H_{\lambda}
and
E_{\lambda}
are respectively the homogeneous and elementary symmetric functions, and
\lambda
is the (integer) partition of
n
obtained by sorting block sizes of
\pi
. We also use the involution
\omega
defined by:

\omega(E_{\lambda})=H_{\lambda},\hskip 20.00003pt\omega(H_{\lambda})=E_{\lambda},

so that
\Fr(\boldsymbol{\chi}\otimes\boldsymbol{\epsilon})=\omega(\Fr(\boldsymbol{\chi}))
for any character
\boldsymbol{\chi}
of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

It is convenient to write
H_{\pi}:=H_{\lambda}
and
E_{\pi}:=E_{\lambda}
(with
\pi
and
\lambda
as above). More generally, we use the following:

Notation 7.2
.

For any sequence
(X_{i})_{i\geq 1}
indexed by positive integers, and a set partition
\pi\in\Pi_{n}
, we denote

X_{\pi}:=\prod_{\beta\in\pi}X_{\#\beta}.

Lemma 7.3

(The moment-cumulant formula)
.

Consider two formal power series

M(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}M_{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!},\hskip 20.00003ptK(x)=\sum_{n\geq 1}K_{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!},

with
M_{0}=1
. The following conditions are equivalent:

•

K(x)=\log(M(x))
,

•

\forall n\geq 1,\;M_{n}=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{n}}K_{\pi}
,

•

\forall n\geq 1,\;K_{n}=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{n}}\mu(\pi)K_{\pi}
.

(Moments and cumulants refer to quantities traditionally considered in probability theory.)

Let
\xi_{n}
be the symmetric function
\Fr(\boldsymbol{\xi})
for the group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

Proposition 7.4
.

We have

\sum_{n\geq 1}\xi_{n}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=-\log\bigg(\sum_{n\geq 0}H_{n}\frac{(-z)^{n}}{n!}\bigg).

(25)

Proof.

By the general formula for
\boldsymbol{\xi}
in Theorem
4.1
, we have:

(-1)^{n-1}\xi_{n}=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{n}}\mu(\pi)H_{\pi}

for all
n\geq 1
. By Lemma
7.3
, this is equivalent to the generating function in (
25
).
∎

Note also that the Möbius function of
\Pi_{n}
is given by

\displaystyle\mu(\pi):=\mu(\pi,[n])=(-1)^{\ell(\pi)}(\ell(\pi)-1)!.

(26)

An explicit formula for
\xi_{n}
follows.

Let us now describe one possible choice of
\rho
having particularly nice properties:
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
is convex in
\mathcal{C}
, and its facets have a simple combinatorial description. Let
(e_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
be the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The simple roots for
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
are the vectors
\alpha_{i}=e_{i}-e_{i+1}
(
1\leq i\leq n-1
). Define

\omega_{i}:=\frac{1}{n}\Big(\underbrace{n-i,\dots,n-i}_{\text{$i$ terms}},\underbrace{-i,\dots,-i}_{\text{$n-i$ terms}}\Big)\in V,

for
1\leq i\leq n-1
. These are the type
A
fundamental weights. In particular, we have

\overline{F}=\Big\{(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in V\;:\;v_{1}\geq\dots\geq v_{n}\Big\}=\Span_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\Big\{\omega_{i}\;:\;1\leq i\leq n\Big\}.

Proposition 7.5
.

Let
\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}r_{i}\omega_{i}\in F
,
where
r_{i}
are positive coefficients, and
H
,
H^{+}
be defined in terms of
\rho
as in (
11
). If
r_{1}
is sufficiently large compared to
r_{2},\dots,r_{n}
, we have
\sigma(F)\subset H^{+}
if and only if
\sigma(1)=1
(for any
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
).

Proof.

Since
F
is the positive span of
(\omega_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}
, we have:

\sigma(F)\subset H^{+}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\forall i,\sigma(\omega_{i})\subset H^{+}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\forall i,\langle\sigma(\omega_{i})\,|\,\rho\rangle\geq 0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\forall i,\langle\omega_{i}\,|\,\sigma^{-1}(\rho)\rangle\geq 0.

Explicit computations give:

\langle\omega_{1}\;|\;\sigma^{-1}(\omega_{1})\rangle=\begin{cases}1-\frac{1}{n}&\text{ if }\sigma(1)=1,\\
-\frac{1}{n},&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Since these two values are nonzero,
r_{1}
being sufficiently large ensures that
\langle\omega_{1}\;|\;\sigma^{-1}(\rho)\rangle
has the same sign as
\langle\omega_{1}\;|\;\sigma^{-1}(\omega_{1})\rangle
,
i.e.
, it is positive iff
\sigma(1)=1
.

We have thus
\sigma(1)=1
as a necessary condition for
\sigma(F)\subset H^{+}
. Since there are
(n-1)!
permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that
\sigma(1)=1
, this condition is also sufficient. (By (
26
), the Möbius number of
\Pi_{n}
is
(-1)^{n}(n-1)!
.)
∎

Remark 7.6
.

In the situation of the previous proposition,
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
is convex in
\mathcal{C}
. Indeed, the condition
\sigma(1)=1
is characterized by avoiding the inversions
(1i)
for
2\leq i\leq n
. It would be interesting to identify other situations where convexity holds, but none has been identified so far.

Although we don’t have any Coxeter theoretic result about the dimension of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
, the specific case of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is worth investigating. Let
D_{n}:=\langle\xi_{n}|H_{1^{n}}\rangle
be the dimension of
\xi_{n}
. The first values (beginning at
n=1
) are 1, 1, 4, 33, 456,
etc
. This sequence is known as
[
18
, A002190]
, and the following result confirm that
(D_{n})_{n\geq 1}
match this OEIS entry.

Proposition 7.7
.

The dimension
D_{n}
is the number of pairs
(\sigma,\tau)\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{2}
such that
\tau(1)=1
and
\des(\sigma)\subset\des(\tau)
.
Moreover, we have:

\sum_{n\geq 1}D_{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!^{2}}=-\log(J_{0}(2\sqrt{x})),

(27)

where
J_{0}
is the Bessel function with parameter
0
, explicitly:

J_{0}(2\sqrt{x})=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{(-x)^{n}}{n!^{2}}.

Proof.

We use Theorem
6.4
, with
\rho
as in Proposition
7.5
. This gives:

D_{n}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n},\;\tau(F)\subset H^{+}}\dim(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\asc(\tau)})=\sum_{\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n},\;\tau(1)=1}\dim(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\asc(\tau)}).

So,
D_{n}
is the number of pairs
(\sigma,\tau)
where
\tau(1)=1
, and
\sigma
is a minimal length representative in
W/W_{\asc(\tau)}
. The latter condition is equivalent to
\asc(\sigma)\supset\asc(\tau)
, or equivalently
\des(\sigma)\subset\des(\tau)
. This proves the first part of the proposition.

Let us now get the generating function (
27
). Recall that we have
\xi_{n}=(-1)^{n-1}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{n}}\mu(\pi)E_{\pi}
. Note that the dimension of
E_{\pi}
is the multinomial coefficient:

\langle E_{\pi}|H_{1^{n}}\rangle=\binom{n}{\pi}:=\frac{n!}{\prod_{\beta\in\pi}(\#\beta)!},

(In general,
\dim\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{X}=\frac{|W|}{|W_{X}|}
.) It follows:

D_{n}=\langle\xi_{n}\,|H_{1^{n}}\rangle=(-1)^{n-1}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{n}}\mu(\pi)\langle E_{\pi}\,|\,H_{1^{n}}\rangle=(-1)^{n-1}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{n}}\mu(\pi)\binom{n}{\pi}.

We get:

\sum_{n\geq 1}D_{n}\frac{x^{n}}{n!^{2}}=\sum_{n\geq 0}(-1)^{n-1}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{n}}\mu(\pi)\binom{n}{\pi}\frac{x^{n}}{n!^{2}}.=-\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{n}}\frac{\mu(\pi)}{\prod_{\beta\in\pi}(\#\beta)!}\frac{(-x)^{n}}{n!}.

By Lemma
7.3
, we obtain (
27
).
∎

8
Final remarks

Let us end the article with a few open questions.

First, note that there is an extra symmetry on the poset of parabolic subgroups. Since parabolic subgroups are closed under conjugation, the group
W^{2}
acts on
\mathcal{P}
by

(w_{1},w_{2})\cdot wW_{X}:=w_{1}wW_{X}w_{2}^{-1}=w_{1}ww_{2}^{-1}(w_{2}W_{X}w_{2}^{-1}).

As a
W^{2}
-set,
\mathcal{P}
can be seen to be equivalent to

\biguplus_{X\in\Theta}W^{2}/\delta(N(W_{X}))

where
\delta:W\to W^{2}
is the diagonal embedding. Note that this isn’t an element of the parabolic Burnside ring of
W^{2}
, as
\delta(N(W_{X})
might not be a parabolic subgroup. It would be interesting to say more about the character of
W^{2}
acting on
\mathcal{P}
and its homology.

It would be interesting to understand when
\mathcal{C}^{>0}
is convex in
\mathcal{C}
. The only known example so far is the one described in Proposition
7.5
. For example, if there is no other instances of convexity, there could be a somewhat conceptual explanation other than checking all cases of the finite type classification.

Eventually, it could be interesting to consider the parabolic coset poset of complex reflection groups, or other Coxeter groups beyond finite type.
