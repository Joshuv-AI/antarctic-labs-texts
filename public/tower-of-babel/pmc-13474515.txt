Response to: “Matters arising: effect modification by chronic kidney disease on renal outcomes in septic shock patients undergoing high-MAP targeting: a secondary analysis of the OPTPRESS trial”
Authors: Hiraaki Okuzawa, Akira Endo
Published: 2026

Main text

We sincerely thank Malik and Jayaswal for their thoughtful correspondence [1] regarding our recent secondary analysis of the OPTPRESS trial [2]. We appreciate their careful appraisal of our work and agree that post hoc interaction analyses require cautious interpretation. We welcome the opportunity to clarify the objectives of our study and the interpretation of our findings.

A central point is that our primary analysis was not the subgroup comparison itself, but the formal test for interaction performed in the overall randomized cohort. Our objective was to evaluate whether baseline chronic kidney disease (CKD) modified the association between the randomized mean arterial pressure (MAP) target strategy and renal outcomes. The subgroup analyses were performed solely to aid interpretation of the observed interaction and were explicitly described as secondary, exploratory, and hypothesis-generating. Accordingly, the subgroup-specific estimates should be interpreted in the context of the overall interaction rather than as independent tests of treatment efficacy within each subgroup [3].

We agree that the CKD subgroup was relatively small, resulting in imprecise estimates with wide confidence intervals. We acknowledge that, because CKD status was ascertained using information routinely available in clinical practice, rather than standardized diagnostic criteria or disease staging, some degree of misclassification may have occurred. In addition to the limited clinical history available, the presence of acute kidney injury at ICU admission likely further complicated the accurate ascertainment of preexisting CKD. These limitations were explicitly acknowledged in our manuscript and were among the principal reasons we consistently interpreted our findings as exploratory rather than confirmatory. If anything, the direction of bias introduced by misclassification would generally be expected to attenuate differences between CKD strata rather than exaggerate them. We agree that our analysis does not satisfy several credibility criteria proposed by Sun and colleagues [4]. However, this does not preclude exploratory interaction analyses; rather, it underscores the need for cautious interpretation and independent confirmation [5].

The correspondents also note that the interaction estimate was attenuated in the multiple imputation analysis and was not statistically significant in the additional sensitivity analyses. We agree with this observation. However, the purpose of these analyses was to evaluate the robustness of the overall pattern rather than to reproduce statistical significance under every analytical specification. Importantly, the interaction estimate remained directionally consistent across the complete-case, multiple imputation, IPTW, and two-part analyses, although precision decreased and statistical significance was not consistently maintained. For this reason, we intentionally described these findings as directionally consistent while explicitly acknowledging their imprecision and the need for cautious interpretation.

We also appreciate the discussion regarding the relationship between MAP targets and vasopressor exposure. We agree that the present analysis cannot distinguish whether the observed association was attributable to MAP elevation itself, increased vasopressor exposure, or both. However, this does not alter the interpretation of the randomized intervention, which compared MAP-target strategies rather than the isolated physiological effect of MAP itself. Our analysis therefore estimates the effect of the randomized MAP-target strategy, rather than the isolated physiological effect of MAP itself. Because vasopressor exposure lies on the causal pathway between randomized treatment allocation and renal outcome, adjustment for post-randomization vasopressor dose would not estimate the total effect of the randomized intervention and could introduce post-randomization bias [6]. Accordingly, we intentionally avoided definitive mechanistic claims and stated only that increased vasopressor exposure may have contributed to the observed findings as a biologically plausible explanation.

Finally, we respectfully disagree that our conclusions overstate the findings. The primary finding of our study was the statistically significant interaction between randomized MAP-target strategy and CKD status in the overall cohort. Our intention was not to advocate CKD-specific MAP targets, but rather to report an interaction signal that may inform future prospective studies of individualized hemodynamic management according to baseline renal function. It was precisely because we recognized these limitations that we intentionally used cautious language throughout the manuscript.

We thank Malik and Jayaswal again for their thoughtful comments and the opportunity to further clarify the interpretation of our findings. We hope this exchange contributes to future studies of individualized hemodynamic management in septic shock.
