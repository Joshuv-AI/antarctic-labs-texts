Impact of e-cigarette advertising, promotion, and sponsorship on cognition and behavior: a systematic review of public responses
Authors: Atchara Prajongjeep, Thanavutd Chutiphongdech, Kornkan Phuengnam, Thitiporn Sukaew, Theeraphon Lopradit, Yutthana Yeabkai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExposure to advertising, promotion, and sponsorship (APS) for e-cigarettes has expanded rapidly across retail, online, and social media platforms. Concerns remain that APS may shape harm perceptions, increase susceptibility, and encourage initiation and sustained use, particularly among youth and young adults. Yet evidence remains fragmented across regulatory contexts and outcomes, with limited synthesis of cognitive and behavioural effects. This study systematically reviews associations between APS exposure and e-cigarette–related cognition and behaviour.MethodsElectronic databases, including PubMed, Medline, Embase, PsycINFO, and Web of Science, were searched (last accessed May 14, 2025) using a comprehensive strategy that combined e-cigarette and APS-related terms. Eligible studies empirically assessed APS exposure and reported at least one relevant outcome. Screening and data extraction were conducted independently by two reviewers. Risk of bias assessments were performed using different Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools. Results were synthesized narratively.ResultsA total of 79 studies were included, comprising cross-sectional surveys (n = 39), longitudinal studies (n = 24), randomized controlled trials (RCTs) (n = 10), and quasi-experimental studies (n = 6). Most were from high-income countries. Across settings and populations, APS exposure was commonly associated with increased susceptibility and intention to use, and e-cigarette use, particularly among youth and young adults exposed through social media and retail environments. Evidence for the association between APS exposure and ever and current use was generally positive but varied by channel, with traditional media (TV, print, radio) showing weaker or nonsignificant effects. Longitudinal studies supported temporal associations between APS exposure and initiation, while experimental studies demonstrated that ad framing (e.g., social themes, promotions) can directly influence perceptions and openness to use.ConclusionsExposure to e-cigarette APS, most notably across digital, social media, and retail environments, was consistently associated with heightened susceptibility, stronger intentions to use, and increased uptake, with these patterns becoming more pronounced in recent years. While much of the existing evidence is cross-sectional and therefore cannot establish causal pathways, the overall body of findings underscores the importance of reinforcing regulatory attention and ongoing monitoring of marketing activities in digital and retail spaces, particularly to safeguard youth and other vulnerable groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13929-6.

Introduction

Although often perceived as less harmful than traditional cigarettes [1], e-cigarettes pose significant health risks [2, 3] that warrant increasing public health attention. In many countries with differing levels of tobacco-control regulation, such as the United States, Canada, Italy, Latvia, and Peru, smoking has declined among adolescents, whereas e-cigarette use among youth has risen substantially by 2019 [4, 5]. This is particularly concerning, as adolescence is a critical period for physical and psychological development and a common time for initiating tobacco use [6]. Beyond the potential progression from vaping to cigarette smoking, youth e-cigarette use frequently marks the onset of nicotine exposure and dependence, which can alter brain development and establish long-term addiction pathways [7]. Although evidence suggests that e-cigarettes may help to reduce cigarette smoking among cigarette users [8, 9], concerns persist about their potential to lead users, especially youth, toward cigarette smoking [10], raising important questions about their broader impact on public health.

In many countries, such as the Philippines, Romania, Ukraine, and Uruguay, e-cigarettes are marketed in ways that undermine public health goals, contributing to a sharp rise in youth vaping, at times surpassing adult usage rates [11]. This trend is especially concerning, given evidence that exposure to e-cigarette advertising may potentially play a significant role in youth uptake of these products [12]. Emerging evidence also indicates that e-cigarettes are promoted using many of the same advertising, promotion, and sponsorship (APS) strategies historically successfully employed by the tobacco industry [13]. Social media platforms become a significant avenue for e-cigarette promotion, utilizing tactics such as discount offers, highlighting flavors and product attributes, and involving influencers and celebrities in marketing campaigns [14].

To address this, Article 13 of the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC), which calls for a comprehensive ban on all forms of tobacco advertising, promotion, and sponsorship (TAPS), has increasingly been interpreted to include e-cigarettes [15]. Accordingly, many countries (e.g., Australia, Canada, Japan, France, and Brazil) have introduced restrictions on traditional and digital marketing channels such as television, radio, online media, and social networking platforms, as well as bans on point-of-sale (POS) displays, celebrity or influencer endorsements, and event sponsorships. However, implementation remains uneven. A global review of 130 countries found that only 79 had enacted e-cigarette regulations, and among them, just 58 had policies addressing any aspect of APS, typically focusing on specific media platforms and geographic restrictions [16]. These inconsistencies reflect broader enforcement challenges, including variations in national legal frameworks, limited monitoring of cross-border and digital marketing, and resource constraints, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), which hinder comprehensive implementation [17, 18].

Evidence also suggests that enforcement gaps continue to allow widespread public exposure to e-cigarette marketing. Social media remains a dominant promotional channel, featuring price discounts, flavored product offerings, and endorsements from influencers and celebrities [14]. People in countries such as Australia, China, and the UK remain exposed to e-cigarette advertising through various media channels [19]. In the United States, although e-cigarette marketing is regulated by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), noncompliant advertising remains prevalent online [20]. Similarly, studies from Poland and the Philippines show that product displays and promotional materials often persist at POS despite advertising and promotion bans [21], reflecting ongoing compliance challenges. Sponsorship activities, including brand involvement in music festivals and social events, have similarly been used to increase brand recognition and cultural appeal among young audiences [22]. Moreover, in countries where e-cigarettes are banned, such as India, Thailand, and Brazil, these products are still accessible via online platforms and grey markets [23–25], increasing global youth exposure to these emerging nicotine products. The effectiveness of existing regulations in reducing public exposure and preventing uptake, particularly among youth, therefore, remains uncertain.

The evidence required to enhance FCTC implementation on APS remains insufficient and fragmented. Although some systematic reviews have examined the effects of exposure to e-cigarette marketing on e-cigarette use [12, 26], significant gaps persist in understanding the underlying mechanisms and how these operate across varying regulatory and cultural contexts. Notably, much of the existing evidence is concentrated in high-income countries (HICs), particularly the United States, United Kingdom, and Canada, leaving limited insight into LMICs where regulatory capacity and enforcement differ substantially. Furthermore, few studies compare how APS exposure through various channels (e.g., digital, retail, and sponsorship) operates under contrasting policy environments. This review therefore systematically examines the association between e-cigarette APS exposure and public cognitions and behaviours, integrating evidence across multiple study designs and regulatory settings.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were developed using the Population–Concept–Context (PCC) framework, following Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidance [27]. This framework was applied to clearly define the review scope, structure the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and guide the extraction and synthesis of evidence. Population (P): Studies were eligible if they involved members of the public or consumers of any age. Research focused on retailers, businesses, or industry representatives was excluded. Concept (C): Eligible studies quantitatively examined APS exposure (direct or reported contact with e-cigarette APS), receptivity (attitudinal engagement or openness to marketing messages), or awareness (recognition of e-cigarettes or their availability), across any medium. Outcomes of interest included perceptions, susceptibility (the absence of a firm commitment not to use an e-cigarette), initiation, and use. Studies addressing tobacco APS without reporting e-cigarette-specific outcomes were excluded. In this review, APS refers to all direct or indirect marketing practices designed to promote the sale, use, or image of e-cigarette products, consistent with WHO FCTC Article [15]. Advertising encompassed paid or publicly distributed content via traditional (e.g., television, radio, print, outdoor) and digital media (e.g., websites, social media, influencer content); Promotion included discounts, giveaways, and point-of-sale (POS) displays; and Sponsorship referred to financial or in-kind support for events, activities, or individuals that enhanced brand visibility. Context (C): Studies conducted in community, retail, digital, or other public settings across any country or jurisdiction and regulatory environments were eligible if they examined public exposure to or interaction with e-cigarette APS, whether in real-world settings or experimental contexts simulating such exposure. Only quantitative study designs (e.g., cross-sectional, longitudinal, experimental, or quasi-experimental) were included, while secondary analyses were accepted if outcomes were adequately reported. Quantitative content analyses were excluded, as they describe message characteristics rather than public exposure, perception, or behavioral outcomes. Policy reviews without empirical results, and non-empirical works such as reviews, abstracts, proceedings, theses, editorials, and commentaries were excluded.

Literature search and data selection

Search terms were developed from topic keywords, subject headings, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) [28] terms, and prior studies [12, 26]. Both keywords and subject headings were used to account for incomplete indexing in the rapidly evolving e-cigarette field. Terms were grouped into two domains: (1) e-cigarettes (e.g., “electronic nicotine delivery systems,” “electronic cigarette*,” vaping, ENDS, JUUL, “pod mod*”) and (2) marketing and communication (e.g., social media, advertising, marketing, Facebook, sponsor*, promote*). Boolean operators, truncation, wildcards, database-specific subject headings, and proximity searches were applied as appropriate. Strategies (Supplementary material 1) were tailored to each database and refined via a pilot search. Comprehensive searches were then conducted in Medline (Ovid), EMBASE (Ovid), Web of Science, APA PsycINFO (Ovid), and PubMed. The search was limited to peer-reviewed studies published in English, with the final database search completed on 14 May 2025. All retrieved references were imported into EndNote, and duplicate records were automatically removed using EndNote’s default duplicate-detection function based on matching titles, authors, and publication years. After duplicate removal, the final set of citations was exported to Rayyan [29] for collaborative screening by two reviewers. The PRISMA flow diagram, showing how studies were identified, screened, assessed, and included [30], guided study selection, with two reviewers independently screening titles, abstracts, and full texts against predefined criteria (Supplementary material 2); disagreements were resolved by discussion, and reasons for full-text exclusions were recorded.

Data extraction and quality appraisal

Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers using a piloted form adapted from the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis [31] to ensure accuracy and completeness. The form captured study characteristics (e.g., authors, year, country, setting, design, participants), methods (e.g., sampling, recruitment, data collection, analysis), outcomes (e.g., exposure to e-cigarette APS, and outcomes of interest), and limitations. Both numerical and textual data were extracted, including descriptive and inferential statistics (e.g., percentages, means, odds ratios, confidence intervals) and outcome-specific details such as susceptibility, perceived harm comparisons, and group differences.

Quality assessment was performed by one reviewer using the relevant JBI Critical Appraisal tools [32] according to study designs. The tools address key sources of bias, including selection (systematic differences between participants included in the study and those not included, such as when samples are drawn from convenience or self-selected groups), information (errors in measuring or classifying key variables, such as exposures, outcomes, or covariates), and confounding. For analytical cross-sectional studies, judgement focused on appropriateness of sampling, representativeness of participants, reliability and validity of measures, and control of confounding. For longitudinal studies, judgement focused on selection of comparable exposed and unexposed groups, measurement of exposures and outcomes in the same way for both groups, adequacy and completeness of follow-up, and adjustment for confounding. For quasi-experimental studies, judgement focused on clarity of intervention and comparator, similarity of groups, and adequacy of follow-up. For RCTs, judgement focused on randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, and completeness of follow-up. Ratings were downgraded when these criteria were not met and rated as low, moderate and a high risk of bias. Studies with a high risk of bias were not excluded from the review, but their limitations were considered in the interpretation of the findings and in the strength of the conclusions drawn.

Data synthesis

Due to substantial heterogeneity across studies, including variation in study designs, participant age groups, exposure definitions, and outcome measurements, a meta-analysis was not conducted. All quantitative findings were then synthesized narratively, organized into a summary table (Supplementary 3), and cross-checked against the sources to ensure accuracy. Although no pooled estimates were calculated, forest plots were generated by extracting adjudged odds ratios (AORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from included studies. The plots were produced in Python (v3.11) using Matplotlib (v3.8) and pandas (v2.2). Different symbols were used to indicate study design (cross-sectional, longitudinal, randomized controlled trial), and colors indicated statistical significance (blue for significant, black for nonsignificant). To highlight context, the synthesis separated traditional APS channels (e.g., print, billboards, magazines, TV, radio, retail) from modern platforms (e.g., social media, internet, websites). These visualizations complemented the narrative synthesis by illustrating the distribution and direction of effects, allowing rapid identification of consistent patterns, contextual variations, and outliers across studies. Where available, multivariable-adjusted estimates were prioritized. In addition to AORs, other reported effect estimates, such as β coefficients, relative risks (RRs), and correlation coefficients (r), were narratively synthesized to capture the strength and direction of associations between APS exposure and cognitive or behavioral outcomes. Where available, multivariable-adjusted estimates were prioritized. In interpreting the results, consideration was given to the risk of bias as well as differences in the measurement of exposures (e.g., APS exposure channels such as retail, social media, online, and traditional media) and outcomes (e.g., perceived harm, intentions, susceptibility, initiation, current use, and ever use).

Results

Results of the search

The search yielded 14,352 records (PubMed, n = 5,124; Embase, n = 4,498; Medline, n = 2,106; WoS, n = 1,634; PsycINFO, n = 990). After duplicate removal, 9,140 records were screened by title and abstract, with 6 assessed in full. Of these, 586 were excluded for reasons including wrong population, outcome, study design, publication type, unavailable full text, and inaccessible full text. Ultimately, 79 studies met the inclusion criteria. The selection process is shown in Fig. 1, following the PRISMA flowchart.

Characteristics of studies

The majority of studies were conducted in the United States (n = 66). Several studies were conducted as multi-country investigations, particularly across the European countries (n = 5) [33–37], some of which also included Asian countries. Single-country studies from Europe were fewer, comprising the UK (n = 1) [38], the Netherlands (n = 2) [39], and Germany (n = 1) [40]. From Asia and Oceania, studies were conducted in China (n = 2) [41, 42], Hong Kong (n = 1) [43], Vietnam (n = 1) [44], Indonesia (n = 1) [45], Australia (n = 1) [46], and New Zealand (n = 1) [47].

The systematic review incorporated a diverse range of study designs, reflecting the breadth of evidence on e-cigarette APS (Supplementary material 3). The majority were analytical cross-sectional studies (n = 39), often survey-based, examining associations between advertising exposure and self-reported outcomes such as perceptions, attitudes, and use. A substantial subset of prospective cohort/longitudinal studies (n = 24; studies [39, 40, 48–68], followed participants over time, enabling stronger inferences about temporal relationships between marketing exposure and subsequent uptake or behavioral change. These included both school-based and multi-country panels, often using the Population Assessment of Tobacco and Health Study (PATH) or International Tobacco Control (ITC) Policy Evaluation Project (ITC cohorts). RCTs (n = 10; studies [69–76]) provided the highest internal validity, experimentally manipulating advertising features such as flavor, warning labels, or sponsorship disclosure. These trials, conducted mostly online or in laboratory settings, tested short-term cognitive and behavioral outcomes (e.g., appeal, intentions, cravings). Lastly, evidence from quasi-experimental studies (n = 6; studies [77–81]) used natural or policy experiments, econometric analyses, or lab-based contrasts to test effects of advertising exposure.

Characteristics of participants

Across the evidence base, participants ranged from adolescents in middle and high school to adults across the life course, with the majority of studies focusing on youth and young adults. Among experimental studies, RCTs and laboratory exposure experiments primarily recruited adolescents and young adults. College student samples (n = 296) [82] and (n = 393) [72], online young adult panels (n = 187) [73] (n = 900) [74], and school-aged youth (n = 564–5,020) [69, 70, 83] were especially common. Quasi-experimental studies primarily involved adults who are either past-year quitters or current smokers (n = 8,291) [78], smokers (n = 519) [81] and young adults (n = 26 and 1,051) [79, 80].

Longitudinal studies concentrated on youth and young adults, frequently within school-based samples in the United States (e.g., Texas Adolescent Tobacco and Marketing Surveillance cohort: TATAMS) [54, 55, 57, 66] and Germany [40]. The U.S. PATH cohort provided particularly rich data, following youth (12–17 years) [51, 60, 61, 64, 65] and young adults (18–24 years) [50, 59], as well as adult smokers and former smokers [50]. These studies linked marketing exposure with changes in susceptibility, harm perceptions, and use behaviors over time.

Cross-sectional surveys included the largest range of populations. Nationally representative youth samples such as the National Youth Tobacco Survey (NYTS) (n ~ 17,000–22,000) [84–91] and state-based surveys (e.g., Florida Youth Tobacco Survey (n = 149,407) [92]) focused on susceptibility, curiosity, and early experimentation. Adult samples came from large international surveys (e.g., Eurobarometer (n = 27,801) [36], ITC cross-sections (n = 6,011) [93], and China (n = 12,470) [41]. Several studies also targeted special populations: Appalachian youth in the U.S [94]. , hospitalized adult smokers [95], pregnant women in Kentucky [96], LGBTQ + subgroups [97], and dual users or ex-smokers [76].

Critical appraisal of included studies

Across all 79 studies, most were judged to have a moderate risk of bias (88%), with relatively few achieving low (4%) or high (8%) ratings (Fig. 2 and more details in Supplementary Material 2). Almost all cross-sectional studies were rated moderate, reflecting limitations inherent to the design, such as reliance on self-reported exposure and outcome measures, limited confounder control, and the inability to establish temporality. Longitudinal studies showed variability: while many were moderate, some were rated low risk [60, 61, 65] due to robust handling of attrition, repeated measurements, and advanced modeling of confounders, whereas high-risk ratings [57, 62, 63, 66] were applied when attrition was high, exposures were poorly measured, or temporality could not be clearly established. RCTs were predominantly moderate, while one [70] showed a high risk. Although they provided stronger causal inference for short-term outcomes, common limitations included lack of allocation concealment, absence of blinding (of participants, researchers, or assessors), and short-term or self-reported outcomes. Experimental studies were few and generally moderate, and one was high risk [81], reflecting issues such as the absence of true control groups or reliance on ecological data, though one [78] was rated low risk due to strong econometric modeling and multiple time points. Overall, moderate-quality evidence predominated (around two-thirds of studies), suggesting a reasonably reliable but methodologically constrained evidence base. The predominance of cross-sectional data limits causal inference, while higher-quality longitudinal and experimental studies provide more confidence in observed associations.

Synthesis of the results

The evidence based on APS exposure and e-cigarette outcomes is heterogeneous, spanning cross-sectional surveys, longitudinal studies, and experimental/RCT designs. Most studies examined APS exposure among youth and young adults. Risk of bias summary of each study is presented in Supplementary material 3. Cross-sectional studies dominate, offering broad associations but limited causal inference. Longitudinal studies, with follow-ups ranging from 6 months to 2.5 years, provide stronger evidence by tracking non-current users or never users over time, though effect sizes vary by exposure type and attrition. Experimental and RCTs show short-term causal effects of ad exposure on harm perceptions, curiosity, and intention, but external validity is limited.

APS and e-cigarette perceptions

Harm perception

Across settings, exposure to e-cigarette advertising and promotion among recent cross-sectional studies was associated with lower perceived harm or higher perceived benefits of e-cigarettes, most often compared with cigarettes [60, 66, 70, 87, 89, 98] (Fig. 3). The magnitude of these associations was generally modest and varied across advertising channels (e.g., retail, online, social media), with AORs ranging from approximately 1.16 to 1.83. Studies reporting different patterns of effects highlight the role of message framing: exposure to fruit- and sweet-flavored messages was associated with lower perceived harm, whereas exposure to tobacco-flavored messages corresponded with higher perceived harm [38].

In earlier years, however, evidence pointed to more pronounced and consistent effects of traditional media channels. For example, in an adjusted model from the 2014 PATH study among U.S. youth aged 12.17 years [60], exposure to radio (β = 0.028, p < .001), billboard (β = 0.028, p < .001), and television (β = 0.041, p < .001) advertising was positively associated with higher perceived harm, while exposure to social media (β = −0.016, p = .064) and newspaper advertisements(β = 0.007, p = .378) showed no significant associations. Findings from longitudinal studies also provide some support for an association between exposure and reduced perceived harm, including a 2014–2015 study in which exposure to TV advertisements predicted lower perceived harm at follow-up (AOR = 1.49) and general advertising exposure was associated with lower harm perceptions (AOR = 1.20) [66]. Across the small number of randomized controlled trials, several mechanisms linking. APS exposure to e-cigarette–related outcomes has been identified. One trial found that televised e-cigarette advertisements edited with industry- or ingredient-themed warning statements paradoxically increased favorable beliefs about e-cigarette risks compared with an unedited version (p < .05) [55]. Another RCT involving U.S. youth aged 13–17 who had never used e-cigarettes showed that televised e-cigarette advertising shaped their perceptions in positive ways and increased intentions to try the product [70]. Another RCT among U.S. young never e-cigarette users, Televised e-cigarette advertising influences how youth perceive e-cigarettes and increases their intentions to try them [69]. Among young adult non-smokers, exposure to e-cigarette advertisements, particularly those with social themes, produced more positive implicit attitudes and greater openness to trying e-cigarettes, even when explicit beliefs remained unchanged [73]. A further RCT among U.S. smokers and ex-smokers demonstrated that higher levels of advertising exposure strengthened perceived benefits of e-cigarette use, with participants more likely to believe that vaping improves concentration (t = 2.04, p = .042), boosts energy (t = 2.10, p = .037), and enhances pleasure (t = 3.40, p = .001) [76]. Together, these trials suggest that exposure to APS can shape both cognitive and affective responses to e-cigarettes across diverse populations.

Several studies also demonstrate mediated pathways from advertising exposure to reduced harm perceptions and subsequently to susceptibility or use. A cross-sectional survey of 2,078 college students in New York City indicates that exposure to online advertisements was associated with lower harm perception (AOR = 1.29), which mediated higher odds of ever use (indirect AOR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.02–1.18) and susceptibility (indirect AOR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.04) [99]. A survey found that marketing receptivity (liking or engaging with promotions) was associated with increased low-harm perceptions (β = 0.70), which in turn predicted recent use (β = 0.33; indirect effect = 0.45) [26]. A PATH longitudinal study reinforces the pattern, showing that exposure to e-cigarette advertising at Wave 3 (2-15-16) reduced perceived harm at Wave 4 (2016-17) (bp = − 0.055, p < .01), which in turn increased the likelihood of e-cigarette use at Wave 5 (2018–19) (bp = − 0.042, p < .001), producing a small but indirect effect on later use (bp = 0.002, 95% CI: 0.000–0.005; Sobel p = .021), although the direct long-term effect on use was non-significant (p > .05) [64]. Experimental work [72] also highlights this mechanism: it showed that ad exposure increased implicit attitudes that e-cigarettes are safer, with social-themed advertisements lowering explicit harm perceptions.

Addictiveness perceptions

Across cross-sectional studies, variation in advertising channels appeared to correspond with differences in perceived addictiveness. A 2014 survey among U.S. Midwestern university students [98] reported that exposure to internet and TV advertisements was associated with lowered perceptions of addictiveness. Similarly, the 2014 NYTS [89] revealed that internet exposure was associated with lower perceived addictiveness (AOR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.11–1.31). More recent work among adults [100] showed that digital advertising (β = − 0.32), retail advertisements (β = − 0.30), and radio/podcast content (β = − 0.44) were associated with lower addictiveness perceptions, whereas traditional media (β = 0.38) and social media content (β = 0.40) were associated with heightened perceived addictiveness. Conversely, an RCT among U.S. young adults found that participants exposed to televised e-cigarette advertisements containing industry- or ingredient-themed warning statements perceived e-cigarettes as less addictive compared with those who viewed the unedited advertisement without a warning message (p < .05) [74].

APS and susceptibility and intention to use e-cigarettes

All studies of susceptibility and intention outcomes restricted analyses to never or non-current e-cigarette users, except for Dai et al. [41], which analyzed the full sample. Most studies examined susceptibility and intention among youth and young adults. Susceptibility was commonly measured using the Pierce susceptibility scale or its adaptations [43, 51, 57, 66, 86, 101–103]. By contrast, intention was assessed with items asking about the likelihood of trying or using e-cigarettes within a defined timeframe, such as the next 12 months [41, 44, 69, 89, 91, 100, 104].

Across surveys reporting AORs (Fig. 4), exposure to various e-cigarette advertisements and promotions was generally associated with increased susceptibility and intention (AOR range 1.22–2.29) [41, 51, 89, 100–102, 105]. Other cross-sectional studies using alternative statistics reported similar patterns [43, 86, 91]. Effects were often stronger among digital and social media channels. New platforms also appear relevant: for example, recalling Netflix shows with vaping depictions was associated with higher susceptibility in a dose–response pattern (AOR ~ 1.65) [101]. Among U.S. adolescents, repeated NYTS between 2014 and 2020 found that exposure to e-cigarette advertising on the Internet among youth who had never used e-cigarettes was associated with increased susceptibility to vaping (AOR ≈ 1.5 for medium exposure; AOR ≈ 1.6 for high exposure, whereas print (newspapers/magazines) and TV channels showed sporadic or weak associations [86]. Moreover, one longitudinal study among Texas youth also highlighted the pattern showing that exposure to the Internet (AOR = 1.72) and retail advertisements (AOR = 1.77) was prospectively associated with susceptibility to vaping [66].

Multiple studies documented graded relationships: more frequent exposures or exposures through more channels were associated with higher susceptibility, intention, and pro-vaping cognitions (AOR range 1.1-1,9) [86, 89, 103]. Recognition of online e-cigarette marketing was linked to more than a twofold increase in susceptibility (AOR = 2.29) and showed a dose–response effect, with each additional recognized marketing source increasing the odds of susceptibility by 13% (AOR = 1.13) [102]. Similarly, recall of e-cigarette imagery in Netflix scripted content increased the likelihood of being susceptible (AOR = 1.65), also demonstrating a cumulative dose–response pattern where each additional program recalled raised susceptibility odds (AOR = 1.15) [101]. Finally, experimental evidence reinforced these findings. An RCT demonstrated that social-themed print advertisements lowered harm perceptions and increased openness to try within minutes of exposure [73]. Experimental studies using televised e-cigarette advertisements also showed increases in favorable attitudes and intentions among adolescents [69, 70].

Regarding sponsorship activities, exposure to celebrity-endorsed e-cigarette advertising on Instagram significantly increased positive advertisementattitudes, electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM; online sharing of opinions, recommendations, or discussions about a product) intentions, and intentions to use e-cigarettes, effects driven by strong celebrity–product congruence (β = 0.44, p < .001), celebrity–consumer risk-orientation match (β = 0.36, p < .001), and strengthened further by parasocial identification (β = 0.27, p < .01) [71].

Initiation of e-cigarette use has been investigated across multiple longitudinal studies, all of which followed participants who were free of the outcome at baseline. Importantly, exposures and outcomes were measured over different timeframes, ranging from 6 months to 2.5 years, and outcome measures varied between current use and ever use. Most studies examining e-cigarette initiation as an outcome were conducted in the U.S., except for a few from the Netherlands [39] and Germany [40].

The findings examining AOR are mixed (Fig. 5). The strongest association was observed in a U.S. household-based youth longitudinal study (ages 13–20 years), where exposure to multiple media channels (TV, retail, and online/social media) predicted current use at follow-up with AORs around 2.2–3.1, though with limited precision [52]. Consistent associations were also found in the U.S. for social media/ online (AORs ~ 1.2–2.3) [52, 55, 61] and retail advertising (AORs ~ 1.2–2.2) [48, 55, 61, 66] among adolescents. A school-based study from Germany also highlighted the pattern (AOR = 1.67) [51]. Looking more closely at online channels, Facebook advertising, but not Twitter, YouTube, or Pinterest/ GooglePlus, was associated with increased trial among youth [48]. Similarly, among U.S. young adult smokers, exposure to retail and social/online advertisements was linked to a greater likelihood of subsequent use (AOR ≈ 1.5–2), particularly for those not using e-cigarettes at baseline [50]. In a Hawaii young adult longitudinal study, trajectories of increasing ad exposure predicted increased e-cigarette use (β = 0.63, p < .05) [58]. Social identity appeared to moderate these pathways: young adults identifying with “Popular-Social” peer crowds showed greater initiation risk via APS exposure (indirect effect = 0.03, p = .04) [59].

By contrast, several U.S. studies reported non-significant associations, particularly for traditional media channels such as magazines, billboards, and radio [48, 53, 55, 61, 66]. A Dutch longitudinal study of smokers also found that although noticing e-cigarette advertisements (e.g., on television, on the radio, and in newspapers or magazines) increased over time, noticing e-cigarette advertisements did not independently predict initiation or use once covariates were controlled [39]. Similarly, another longitudinal study in the US [53] showed that frequent advertisements/promotion exposure for a vape shop was not consistently linked to initiation after 6 months, and among baseline current users, it was even associated with slightly fewer days of use at follow-up (IRR = 0.88). Overall, longitudinal evidence suggests that APS exposure, particularly via social media and retail channels, elevates the risk of e-cigarette initiation, while the effects of traditional media appear weaker or null. However, heterogeneity in measurement timeframes, channels assessed, and populations studied partly explains the inconsistency across findings.

Ever e-cigarette use

Fig. 6Forest plots of e-cigarette APS exposure and ever use of e-cigarettes by channel (Traditional APS channels: adjudged odds ratio (AOR) of 18 outcomes with 6 studies, and Modern APS channels and others: 7 outcomes within 6 studies)

Forest plots of e-cigarette APS exposure and ever use of e-cigarettes by channel (Traditional APS channels: adjudged odds ratio (AOR) of 18 outcomes with 6 studies, and Modern APS channels and others: 7 outcomes within 6 studies)

The evidence on ever-e-cigarette use highlights both definitional variation and heterogeneity in effect sizes. Most studies defined ever use as lifetime experimentation (at least once), although some also included past-30-day users, which may inflate estimates toward more regular use. Across studies, AORs show a mixed pattern (Fig. 6). The strongest association was reported in a small Sydney sample, where social media exposure was associated with increased odds nearly six-fold [46]; however, the limited sample size reduced generalizability. Consistently, social media and online marketing exposures were linked to a higher risk of ever use among young adults in Vietnam (AOR = 3.38) [44] and the U.S. (AOR = 1.77) [102]. The 2014 NYTS reported more moderate but precise associations for internet and retail advertising (AOR ≈ 1.5–1.7) [103]. A multi-country study [37] reinforced this evidence by demonstrating a dose–response effect: each additional type of advertising exposure (across 17 channels) was associated with increased odds of ever use by ~ 5%. Longitudinal evidence further supports a causal interpretation, albeit with smaller effect sizes [60].

In contrast, exposure to traditional media such as posters/billboards, TV/radios, newspapers, and movie/ music video advertisements was found to be nonsignificant associated with ever use among Vietnamese young adults [44]. The longitudinal study also highlighted this pattern; exposure to traditional media channels (print, TV, billboards) generally yielded weaker or nonsignificant associations, with smaller studies producing wider confidence intervals [60].

Current e-cigarette use

Fig. 7Forest plots of e-cigarette APS exposure and current use of e-cigarettes by channel (Traditional APS channels: adjudged odds ratio (AOR) of 30 outcomes within 7 studies, and Modern APS channels and others: 10 outcomes within 5 studies)

Forest plots of e-cigarette APS exposure and current use of e-cigarettes by channel (Traditional APS channels: adjudged odds ratio (AOR) of 30 outcomes within 7 studies, and Modern APS channels and others: 10 outcomes within 5 studies)

Current e-cigarette use, defined as use in the past 30 days, shows consistent associations with advertising and promotion exposure. Cross-sectional surveys among youth and young adults [42, 89, 90, 100] report significant effects, with AORs typically ranging from 1.3 to 1.6 for exposure to online and retail advertisements (Fig. 7). The strongest estimate was observed in the Australian study [46], where social media exposure was associated with an increased likelihood of current use nearly sixfold, though this study had a small sample size. POS marketing and promotional tactics (e.g., coupons, free samples) also show strong effects, with the 2016 PAHT-wave 3 [106] reporting elevated odds of current use among sexual minority females, although ad exposure did not moderate disparities by sexual minority status. Retailer environment factors contribute as well: a 2014 NYTS analysis [107] found that greater retailer density near schools (Adjusted prevalence ratio (APR) = 1.06, 95% CI 1.02–1.10) and higher advertising volume (APR = 1.03, 95% CI 1.00–1.07) were associated with increased past-month use.

Longitudinal studies [60, 61] confirm that advertising exposure was prospectively linked to current use over time, albeit with smaller effect sizes. In Sun et al. (2023) [61], exposure to social media/websites (AOR = 1.49) and convenience store advertisements (AOR = 1.27) significantly predicted later past-month use, whereas traditional media (TV, radio, print, billboards) showed no significant associations after adjustment.

Overall, advertisement exposure is likely linked to current use and ever use, particularly via digital/social media and retail promotion, though the longitudinal evidence suggests effects are modest, highlighting that advertising may play a stronger role in initiation. Although ever use and current use were mostly measured in cross-sectional studies, most outcomes were found to be significantly associated with exposure to APS.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesizes evidence on the influence of e-cigarette APS on cognitive and behaviour-related outcomes. Although most included studies were cross-sectional, some consistent patterns nonetheless point to a temporal relationship between APS exposure and both ever and current e-cigarette use. Across the evidence base, digital and retail marketing channels, particularly social media, online advertising, and POS environments, were more strongly associated with uptake, whereas traditional channels such as television, print, and radio generally showed weaker or nonsignificant associations. This distinction was also reflected over time: earlier studies relying on traditional media exposure rarely predicted later initiation, whereas more recent exposures through modern, digitally oriented channels were more frequently linked with subsequent use. Findings on susceptibility, harm perceptions, and perceived addictiveness were mixed; however, favourable perceptions of e-cigarettes appear to play a mediating role in facilitating progression toward experimentation and use. Experimental studies further highlight mechanisms, demonstrating that social and promotional themes reduce harm perceptions and increase openness to trying e-cigarettes. Our findings align with prior reviews of tobacco [108] and alcohol marketing [109], which demonstrate that commercial advertising and marketing are associated with reduced perceived harm and increased uptake.

Our findings suggest that APS exposure is more influential in prompting initiation by increasing susceptibility and experimentation than in sustaining ongoing use, where other factors, such as peer influence, may play larger roles [110, 111]. This distinction aligns with marketing theory [112], which highlights how advertising lowers barriers to trial and generates curiosity, particularly among youth and young adults. As Kotler and Keller (2016) [113] emphasize, advertising reduces perceived risk and shapes attitudes, making experimentation more likely. This reflects classic models such as AIDA (Attention–Interest–Desire–Action) and the Hierarchy of Effects [114], where advertising captures attention, stimulates interest, and facilitates first use. Consistent with these models, our results show APS is most strongly associated with susceptibility and intention, often through reduced harm perceptions, a key mediator of experimentation. Nevertheless, consistent with prior reviews [115, 116], digital and retail marketing continue to reinforce use, with social media amplifying both commercial and peer content and retail promotions normalizing use at the POS. This suggests APS restrictions may be most effective in preventing initiation, but additional controls on digital and retail marketing are needed to limit reinforcement of ongoing use. Future research should clarify how APS differentially shapes initiation versus maintenance to better guide regulation.

Despite the consistent direction of findings, there was notable variability in effect sizes across studies. This heterogeneity reflects differences in exposure measurement (e.g., recall vs. receptivity vs. objective ad counts), outcome definitions (e.g., susceptibility scales vs. single-item intention measures), country regulatory frameworks, age groups/study populations, and platforms of APS. Some large surveys showed only modest associations (AORs ~ 1.3–1.6), while smaller experimental or country-specific studies reported stronger effects. The limited precision of some estimates, particularly in small sample studies, cautions against over-interpretation. Furthermore, cross-sectional designs dominate the evidence base, leaving the role of unmeasured confounding and reverse causality as an important caveat. While the overall trend suggests that APS exposure may lower harm perceptions and promote use, many estimates lacked statistical significance, highlighting variability across studies and limiting the strength of conclusions about a consistent effect.

Strengths of this review include a comprehensive search strategy across multiple databases, use of explicit eligibility criteria, and consideration of multiple outcome domains from perceptions to behavioral use. By integrating findings from cross-sectional, longitudinal, and experimental designs, we provide an integrated synthesis that demonstrates both immediate and longer-term effects of APS. However, limitations must be acknowledged. First, heterogeneity due to measurements, types of e-cigarette APS, and age groups, for example, was high, precluding meta-analysis and necessitating narrative synthesis. Second, exposure measurement varied widely, often relying on self-report recall, which may be subject to bias. Third, most evidence comes from HICs, particularly the United States. This limits the generalizability of findings to global contexts, as marketing dynamics, enforcement capacity, and cultural receptivity to APS differ widely across regions. For example, in some LMICs where enforcement is weak and digital surveillance limited, online marketing and cross-border advertising may have far greater reach [117]. These disparities underscore the global equity dimension of e-cigarette marketing exposure, where populations in settings with fewer regulatory protections may face higher risks of unregulated and youth-targeted promotions [118]. Finally, relatively few studies examined sponsorship and POS promotions in detail, despite their central role in maintaining product visibility and shaping public awareness of e-cigarette and tobacco products. These marketing practices enhance visibility in retail and community settings, reinforcing perceptions of accessibility and social acceptability [119]. Future research is encouraged to investigate how sponsorship activities (e.g., events, music festivals, influencer collaborations) and POS marketing strategies (e.g., retail displays, price incentives) affect consumer perceptions and behaviors under varying regulatory environments.

Taken together, the evidence indicates that APS e-cigarette exposure is consistently associated with changes in perceptions and use. Digital and retail POS channels exert the greatest influence, underscoring the need for stronger regulatory oversight. These findings have important policy implications, suggesting that stricter regulation of e-cigarette marketing across social media, digital platforms, and retail environments may be warranted to protect vulnerable populations. This is aligned with a recent systematic review and meta-analysis [120], suggesting that bans on tobacco APS are associated with lower odds of current smoking and reduced initiation. Likewise, a 2024 cross-sectional study using data from 48 countries reported that implementation of WHO-recommended bans on internet and POS advertising was linked to lower rates of youth e-cigarette use, particularly in lower-income settings [121]. Evidence also points to promising regulatory approaches, including stricter age-verification systems, limits on influencer promotions, and enforcement of POS display bans [18, 122], though some studies note unintended effects such as displacement of advertising to unregulated media [123, 124]. Continuous monitoring of emerging promotional channels, such as influencer marketing and streaming platforms, is therefore essential. Public health policies should consider the possible potential harm-reduction role of e-cigarettes for adult smokers, with the urgent need to protect youth and never-smokers from the risks of initiation. Future research may benefit from adopting longitudinal and experimental designs, applying standardized measures of APS exposure (e.g., items adapted from the GYTS [125]), and expanding to LIMCs where regulatory capacity, enforcement, and marketing dynamics differ substantially, to inform equitable global prevention strategies. Moreover, future studies could consider examining mediators such as social identity and sociodemographic differences to inform targeted prevention strategies.

Conclusions

This review shows that exposure to e-cigarette APS, especially through digital, social media, and retail channels, is consistently associated with heightened susceptibility, stronger intentions to use, and increased uptake, with these patterns becoming more evident in recent years. Although most available evidence is cross-sectional and limits causal inference, longitudinal findings suggest that APS exposure may contribute to initiation, though effect sizes remain variable. Given uncertainties surrounding the overall public health impact of e-cigarettes, including risks of dual use and unclear smoking cessation benefits, widespread APS exposure may cultivate overly favorable perceptions of health risk among never-smokers. A further limitation is the concentration of studies in HICs, with very limited evidence from LMICs, where regulatory structures, marketing environments, and patterns of youth digital engagement may differ substantially. These findings highlight the value of reinforcing regulatory attention and continuous monitoring of marketing in digital and retail environments, while calling for more studies from LMICs, standardised exposure measures and robust longitudinal or experimental research, including in underrepresented regions, to strengthen causal understanding and inform equitable policy responses.
