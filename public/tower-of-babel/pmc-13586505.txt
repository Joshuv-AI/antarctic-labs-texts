Endovascular salvage of a dislodged renal bridging stent using balloon-induced foreshortening following thoracoabdominal multibranch endograft
Authors: Mark W. Mewissen, Safoora Choudry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 69-year-old man underwent thoracoabdominal multibranch endograft repair. Ten days later, computed tomography angiography demonstrated type III endoleak caused by the complete dislodgement of the left renal bridging stent. During brachial reintervention, controlled balloon dilation foreshortened the displaced stent, restoring alignment with the renal artery and enabling successful reconstruction. Six-month computed tomography angiography confirmed a patent reconstructed stent without residual endoleak. This case demonstrates the successful use of balloon-induced stent foreshortening for the endovascular salvage of a completely dislodged renal bridging stent.

Branched and fenestrated endovascular aortic repair interventions require stable bridging stents to maintain visceral and renal perfusion. Bridging stent dislodgement is an uncommon but severe complication that may result in type III endoleak and organ ischemia, particularly when standard recatheterization is not feasible. This case describes the use of balloon-induced stent foreshortening to restore renal artery access and facilitate endovascular reconstruction.

Case report

A 69-year-old man who underwent multiple prior endovascular aneurysm repair interventions, including infrarenal aortic and iliac interventions, was found to have a >6-cm partially thrombosed juxtarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm. Successful endovascular repair was performed using a thoracoabdominal multibranch endograft (TAMBE) with the incorporation of the celiac, superior mesenteric, and bilateral renal arteries. Preoperative computed tomography angiography (CTA) demonstrated a left renal artery measuring just over 5 mm in diameter with at least 30 mm of a relatively straight proximal landing zone before assuming a superior course. The celiac-to-left renal artery distance measured 73.4 mm (Fig 1), approximating the anticipated bridging distance. Based on the native renal artery diameter, a 6 × 79-mm GORE VIABAHN VBX Balloon Expandable Endoprosthesis bridging stent (W. L. Gore & Associates) was selected, with a telescoped 7 × 59-mm VBX stent for proximal fixation within the TAMBE renal portal. Approximately 25 mm of the distal stent extended into the left renal artery. Completion angiography revealed satisfactory alignment and perfusion without excessive angulation, tension, or endoleak (Fig 2). Reintervention

Left brachial access was obtained with the placement of a 7F sheath into the descending thoracic aorta. Selective initial angiography confirmed the complete separation of the bridging stent from the left renal artery, with the stent displaced into the aneurysm sac (Fig 4, A). Technical challenge: The dislodged stent was positioned distal to the left renal artery origin, preventing stable catheter engagement. Although the left renal artery could be catheterized with a Simmons-1 catheter, guidewire exchange repeatedly resulted in the loss of access because the catheter prolapsed into the aneurysm sac. Bailout strategy

An Armada 14 PTA Catheter 14 × 40-mm angioplasty balloon (Abbott) was advanced into the distal portion of the dislodged 6 × 79-mm VBX bridging stent and inflated once under fluoroscopic guidance. Balloon inflation was performed manually until full balloon expansion was achieved at approximately 4 atm (Fig 4, B). Deliberate overdilation of the distal stent segment induced controlled longitudinal foreshortening, restoring the alignment of the dislodged stent with the left renal artery and permitting successful guidewire recatheterization (Fig 4, C). The renal artery was then reconstructed using overlapping 7-mm VBX bridging stents deployed from distal to proximal, extending the distal landing zone farther into the main renal artery as an additional measure intended to reduce the risk of recurrent stent dislodgement. Completion angiography demonstrated excellent renal perfusion and complete exclusion of type III endoleak (Fig 4, D). The patient tolerated the procedure well and remained hemodynamically stable postoperatively. At the 6-month follow-up, CTA demonstrated patency of all the TAMBE branches, preserved left renal artery perfusion, and no evidence of residual endoleak (Fig 5). Discussion

Renal bridging stent dislodgement is an uncommon but severe complication following branched endovascular aortic repair. This mechanical failure generally leads to a Type IIIa endoleak while critically threatening blood flow to the kidney. In the TAMBE pivotal trial, target vessel instability was a recognized cause for reintervention, emphasizing the need for effective bailout strategies when bridging stent integrity is compromised.1 The management of a completely dislodged “floating” stent presents a unique technical challenge. Distal migration into the aneurysm sac often creates a sharp angulation or a “dead space” that makes standard re-cannulation through a brachial approach nearly impossible. Whereas Mezzetto et al2 previously described a maneuver to realign a displaced bridging stent using the external “thrust” from a balloon inflated in an adjacent visceral branch, our technique utilized the intrinsic mechanical properties of the stent itself.

By performing controlled overdilation of the dislodged VBX stent,3 we induced intentional stent foreshortening. This shortening of the longitudinal profile created the necessary clearance to realign the stent with the renal artery origin, enabling successful wire access and subsequent realignment.

The precise mechanism responsible for delayed left renal bridging stent dislodgement remains uncertain. The index procedure achieved approximately 25 mm of intrarenal stent coverage, exceeding the manufacturer's recommended minimum seal length, and completion angiography showed no excessive angulation, tension, or endoleak. Because the proximal bridging stent remained securely anchored within the TAMBE renal portal, failure occurred at the distal target vessel interface. Although speculative, the combination of a relatively long bridging stent construct with the abrupt increase in intrathoracic and intra-abdominal pressure associated with a violent coughing episode may have contributed to delayed stent dislodgement. Understanding the mechanical behavior of balloon-expandable covered stents facilitated successful endovascular salvage. Six-month CTA confirmed a patent reconstructed left renal bridging stent with preserved renal perfusion and no residual endoleak. This case highlights several technical considerations for renal bridging stent planning. Careful assessment of the target renal artery diameter and the available distal landing zone is essential to optimize stent selection and fixation. Whenever anatomically feasible, at least 2 cm of stable intrarenal purchase should be obtained. In retrospect, we would extend the distal landing zone farther into the straight segment of the renal artery. During reconstruction, two overlapping 7-mm VBX bridging stents were deployed, extending the distal landing zone farther into the renal artery and creating a uniform 7-mm bridging construct. Although it is uncertain whether the stent diameter contributed to the initial dislodgement, maintaining a uniform 7-mm construct was intended to provide greater radial support. This bailout technique should be reserved for highly selected cases. Deliberate overdilation of a balloon-expandable covered stent carries risks, including stent fracture, disruption of the expanded polytetrafluoroethylene graft material, loss of structural integrity, and injury to the adjacent vascular structures if the balloon size and position are not carefully selected. In the present case, the displaced bridging stent was sufficiently separated from the renal artery origin to permit controlled balloon-induced foreshortening without compromising the target vessel.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates the successful use of a controlled balloon-induced foreshortening technique to facilitate the endovascular salvage of a completely dislodged left renal bridging stent. This report represents a single-case experience, and additional clinical experience is needed to better define the safety, reproducibility, and broader applicability of this bailout strategy. At 6 months, CTA demonstrated a patent reconstructed left renal bridging stent with preserved renal perfusion and no evidence of residual endoleak.
