Gastrointestinal Parasites of Domestic Ducks (Anas platyrhynchos domesticus) in Kathmandu, Nepal
Authors: Priyanka Mandal, Roshan Babu Adhikari, Tirth Raj Ghimire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionDomestic ducks are important for meat, egg and livelihood production in Nepal; however, gastrointestinal parasites (GIPs) remain a major health constraint affecting productivity and posing zoonotic risks.AimsThis cross‐sectional study aimed to determine the prevalence and diversity of GIPs in ducks raised on smallholder farms in Kathmandu, Nepal.MethodsFresh faecal samples (N = 130) were collected randomly using non‐invasive methods, and copromicroscopy was performed after direct wet‐mount and acid‐fast staining techniques.ResultsA total of 51 samples (39.2%) tested positive for GIPs. The parasites identified included: Ascaridia galli, Cryptosporidium sp., Eimeria sp., Entamoeba coli, Entamoeba sp., Giardia sp., Strongyle, Taeniid and Tyzzeria sp. A chi‐square test (χ2) revealed that the prevalence of protozoa (33.8%) was significantly higher than that of helminths (14.6%) (p < 0.05). Older ducks showed a higher prevalence than young and adult ducks. Additionally, mixed infections were more common than single infections.ConclusionThe findings show that single‐celled protozoa, including the zoonotic Cryptosporidium sp., were the primary contributors to higher parasitic infections in ducks than helminths. These findings provide baseline information on duck parasitism and its potential risks to humans. More epidemiological and long‐term studies are needed to better understand risk factors, transmission dynamics and the effectiveness of potential parasite control strategies.

Introduction

Ducks (Anas platyrhynchos domesticus; Taxonomic Serial Number: 175062 (ITIS 2025) are multifaceted waterfowl with significant cultural, food and ornamental values (El Sabry and Almasri 2023; Glinskaia 2021; Shrestha 2018). Globally, duck farming alone contributes to 10%–30% of total egg demand in East and Southeast Asia (Ismoyowati 2019) and 4.1% of global poultry meat production (El Sabry and Almasri 2023). In Nepal, the poultry industry, along with duck farming, contributes 2.4% of the total agricultural gross domestic product (GDP), which comes from the livestock and poultry industry (MoALD 2023). However, unlike other poultry industries, duck production is still in its early stages, and many farmers rely on traditional, low‐input rearing practices. This conventional approach, while beneficial in some aspects, also poses challenges in terms of disease control and productivity. Moreover, the lack of farmers' knowledge of different aspects of duck management, changing production systems, indiscriminate crossbreeding, limited access to veterinary services and the lack of targeted research have further restricted the profitability and sustainability of duck farming in Nepal (Basnet et al. 2021). It is therefore worth exploring how traditional practices can be adjusted or improved to mitigate the risks of gastrointestinal parasites (GIPs), perhaps through rotational grazing or integrated pest management, which could enhance overall productivity while still respecting cultural practices.

Among the various constraints to duck farming in Nepal, diseases caused by GIPs can be critical. It is because, like other poultry, ducks are also vulnerable to a diverse range of GIPs, including single‐celled protozoa, such as Eimeria sp., Giardia sp. and Cryptosporidium sp., and multicellular helminths, such as Ascaridia galli, Heterakis gallinarum, Raillietina spp., Hymenolepis spp., Echinostoma spp. and Notocotylus spp. (Islam et al. 1988; Canaris and Lehker 2021). Because these parasites are pathogenic, they can limit growth rates, feed efficiency, reproductive performance, survivability, productivity and overall well‐being of the ducks (Shrestha et al. 2020; Sonaiya and Swan 2003; Van et al. 2020). Moreover, parasite‐infected ducks become immunodeficient and may become vulnerable to secondary infections (Chaves Hernández 2014; Gerhold 2023). On the other hand, mortality among ducks due to heavy GIP burdens has been repeatedly reported across different geographies (Jansson et al. 2021; Punnoose et al. 2021), indicating the global health impacts of GIPs in these birds. Furthermore, ducks serve as definitive, intermediate or paratenic hosts for a few zoonotically significant parasites, including Cryptosporidium, Giardia, Toxoplasma, Echinostoma, Capillaria and others (Dao et al. 2016; Saijuntha et al. 2013; Xiong et al. 2009; Li et al. 2024). This creates transmission risks, particularly in areas with poor hygiene, close human‐animal contact and where undercooked duck products are consumed.

While the study of GIPs in ducks is important, research on this topic in Nepal is limited. To date, only a single prevalence study with a limited sample size (N = 120) at the local level was conducted 6 years ago (Shrestha et al. 2020). Although the study identified few protozoa and helminths, such as Ascaridia galli, Heterakis sp., Capillaria sp., Trichuris sp., Raillietina sp. and Strongyloides sp., it lacked substantial information on risk factors and the causes of parasite diversity. Furthermore, no methodological attempts have been made to identify pathogenic and zoonotically significant Cryptosporidium sp. In addition, the influence of a few potential risk factors associated with GIPs in ducks remains largely unexplored, suggesting a critical gap in the study of GIPs in smallholder ducks in Nepal. In this context, a few risk factors, including duck‐rearing practices (stocking density, methods and related management conditions), hosts’ age and season, might be critical. Therefore, the current study was designed to fulfil this research gap by assessing the prevalence, diversity and associated risk factors of GIPs among ducks in smallholder farms in Kathmandu, Nepal. These findings provide baseline data on the prevalence of GIPs in ducks in Nepal and highlight the need for further epidemiological and intervention studies to evaluate management, treatment, and control strategies to reduce parasite burden and improve duck health.

Materials and Methods

Study Area

The study was conducted on 12 small‐scale duck farms located along the banks of the Monohara River, which flows through the eastern outskirts of the Kathmandu Valley, in Jadibuti, Kathmandu (27.683°N, 85.367°E) (Figure 1). The study site is a peri‐urban transition zone situated between Kathmandu's urban cores and the surrounding rural landscapes, with a year‐round humid subtropical climate. Notably, the Manohara River basin creates low‐lying, often marshy or seasonally flooded areas that are ideal for small‐scale, low‐input poultry and duck farming. Therefore, local farmers have adopted semi‐intensive or scavenging duck farming, with access to open water, manure‐rich soils and other animal wastes (Figure 2A, B). These factors increase the risk of contamination and the spread of zoonotic or environmental parasites, as described in a previous study near Karmanasa Khola in Lalitpur, Nepal (Adhikari, Ghimire et al. 2025). Therefore, the selected study area provided an appropriate setting to investigate GIPs in ducks and their potential association with local farming practices.

Sample Collection, Preservation and Transportation

The field survey was conducted from January to March 2025 across 12 duck farms, each with flock sizes ranging from 5 to 21. To calculate the sample size, the Sample Size Calculator (https://www.calculator.net/sample‐size‐calculator.html) for random sampling was used, applying values with a 95% Confidence Level, 5% Margin of Error, and a Population Proportion of 82% based on a previous prevalence study of GIPs of ducks in Nepal (Shrestha et al. 2020) in the total population size of about 295 ducks, which gave an estimation of 129 faecal samples. Thus, a total of 130 faecal samples were collected randomly, with 1 sample per duck.

In the current study, only freshly voided faecal samples were collected during the early morning (7:00–9:00 am) throughout the sampling period. To facilitate sampling, the duck population on each farm was divided into three age‐based groups: young (≤ 3 months), adult (> 3 to 12 months) and old (> 12 months), based on owners' reported ages. Each duck age group was separated into different compartments and left to defecate on a clean white piece of plastic laid on the floor. Immediately after defecation, the faecal samples were collected using a sterile metal spatula and transferred into sterile 20 mL plastic vials. To prevent duplicate sampling, each sampled duck was temporarily identified by tying a small red ribbon to one wing. About 5 mL of 2.5% weight/volume (w/v) potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7) solution was added to each vial for faecal preservation. Additionally, a structured questionnaire on farmers’ awareness of parasitism, medication history, adopted rearing practices and stocking density was administered to the duck owners. The collected samples were kept in a cool box and transported daily to the Parasitology laboratory at Tri‐Chandra Multiple Campus, Kathmandu. The samples were stored at 4°C in a refrigerator before microscopic examination and for further processing.

Laboratory Investigation: Sample Processing and Microscopic Examination

Two copromicroscopic techniques, direct wet mount (DWM) and acid‐fast staining (AFS), were employed for detecting GIPs in the current study. Since the DWM technique is a rapid and economical method for detecting protozoan cysts, helminth eggs (ova) and larvae, we used it for all samples. For DWM, a thin faecal smear was prepared by mixing a single drop of homogenized faecal sample with 2.5% K2Cr2O7 and two drops of Gram's iodine on a glass slide. The smear was covered with a coverslip and examined under a compound microscope (40×) (Adhikari et al. 2024; Adhikari and Ghimire 2026; Yadav et al. 2024). We followed a systematic zig‐zag scanning protocol (left‐to‐right, top‐to‐bottom) to ensure complete and uniform coverage of the coverslip and minimize the risk of missing positive cases (Adhikari et al. 2026).

The AFS technique was used only on Cryptosporidium‐like oocyst‐positive samples. For this technique, approximately 2 g of a homogenized faecal sample preserved in preservation medium was mixed with 10 mL of 10% formalin and 3 mL of ethyl acetate in a conical centrifuge tube, then centrifuged at 1200 revolutions per minute (rpm) for 5 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and the sediment was processed for thin smear preparations (Adhikari et al. 2023; Adhikari et al. 2021). After the smear dried, it was fixed in absolute methanol and flooded with carbol fuchsin for about 15 min. The smear was then destained with acid alcohol and counterstained with malachite green for a minute (Adhikari, Adhikari Dhakal, Ale et al. 2025; Adhikari, Adhikari Dhakal and Ghimire 2023). Thereafter, the smear was rinsed with cool water and left to dry at room temperature. The dried smear was then examined under 100× magnification with immersion oil for red oocysts of Cryptosporidium sp. (Adhikari et al. 2020; Adhikari, Adhikari Dhakal and Ghimire 2025).

Parasite Identification

The microscopic images of parasitic stages, such as protozoan cysts and oocysts, as well as helminth eggs and larvae (Figure 3), were captured using a Xiaomi Redmi Note 8 smartphone with a 48 MP wide‐angle main camera. The camera was carefully aligned with the microscope eyepiece to photograph the parasitic stages seen under the microscope (40×). Images were taken at the correct magnification, ensuring the camera lens and eyepiece remained properly aligned. Thereafter, parasitic bodies were identified based on morphological characters including size, shape, colour, internal structure and other diagnostic features, using established taxonomic keys and descriptions reported in the literature (Oliveira et al. 2025; Soulsby 2012; Zajac and Conboy 2012). Moreover, Entamoeba spp. were differentiated based on cyst diameter and the number of nuclei. Cysts with a smaller diameter were considered Entamoeba sp., while cysts with a larger diameter and eight nuclei were considered E. coli (Tanyuksel and Petri 2003). Oocysts with a large granular mass and eight free sporozoites were classified as Tyzzeria (Oliveira et al. 2025). Since morphometric analysis alone does not always enable precise identification, particularly for morphologically similar parasites, some organisms were identified only to genus. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Data Analysis

All generated data in the study were entered into Microsoft Word 2016 and MS Excel 2010. The prevalence of individual parasites was calculated by dividing the number of positive cases by the total sampled population, then multiplying by 100. Prevalence%=Numberofpositivecases/Totalsamplingpopulation×100

We used GraphPad Prism (version 5.00, 1992–2007; GraphPad Software, Inc.) to analyse the statistical data generated during the study. A chi‐square (χ2) test was used to assess significant differences in the prevalence rates of protozoa and helminths, as well as among variables during risk factor analysis. Fisher's exact test (two‐tailed) was used to compare two variables. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant at a 95% confidence interval. The relative risk (RR) and odds ratio (OR) were calculated, and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were evaluated, particularly when analysing the risk factors for gastrointestinal parasitosis in duck populations.

Results

Prevalence and Diversity of GIPs

The microscopic examination of the faecal samples from 130 domestic ducks (N = 130) revealed that 51 samples (39.2%) were positive for the presence of gastrointestinal parasites. Co‐infections involving two parasite taxa were more prevalent than single infections and infections involving three parasite taxa; however, these differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Figure 4). The total prevalence of protozoa was (33.8%, 44 of 130) higher compared to that of helminths (14.6%, 19 of 130), and the difference was statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Figure 5). Similarly, the prevalence of different protozoan species was significant (p < 0.05), but that of different helminth species was not significant (p > 0.05). In terms of prevalence, the protozoan parasites were ranked as follows: Cryptosporidium sp., Entamoeba sp., Eimeria sp., Entamoeba coli, Blastocystis sp. and Tyzzeria sp. (Figure 5). The helminths were ranked by prevalence as Ascaridia galli, strongyles and taeniids (Figure 5).

Risk Factors of GIPs

We evaluated the likely risk factors for GIP infection in farm‐raised ducks. A significant association was observed between age and infection prevalence (χ2 = 8.11, df = 2, p < 0.05). Infection was most prevalent in older ducks (> 12 months; 55.6%; 5/9), followed by young ducks (≤ 3 months; 50.9%; 28/55), while adult ducks (> 3–12 months) showed the lowest prevalence (27.3%; 18/66). Infection prevalence was higher in free‐ranging ducks (45.2%; 38/84) than in intensively reared ducks (28.3%; 13/46). Intensive rearing was associated with a lower risk (RR = 0.625; 95% CI: 0.373–1.050) and lower odds of infection (OR = 0.477; 95% CI: 0.250–1.033), although the association was not significant (p > 0.05). Ducks from mixed flocks showed a higher prevalence of infection (45.8%; 33/72) than those from duck‐only flocks (31.0%; 18/58). The estimated risk (RR = 0.677; 95% CI: 0.428–1.070) and odds (OR = 0.532; 95% CI: 0.259–1.100) were lower in duck‐only flocks, but the difference was not significant (p > 0.05). Infection was more common in high‐density flocks (60.0%; 30/50) than in low‐density flocks (26.3%; 21/80) (p < 0.05). Low stocking density was associated with reduced risk (RR = 0.438; 95% CI: 0.284–0.674) and lower odds of infection (OR = 0.237; 95% CI: 0.112–0.504). The prevalence of infection was lower among ducks managed by aware farmers (18.9%; 10/53) than by unaware farmers (53.2%; 41/77) (p < 0.05). Farmer awareness was associated with a reduced risk (RR = 0.354; 95% CI: 0.195–0.643) and lower odds of infection (OR = 0.204; 95% CI: 0.090–0.464). Ducks with a history of medication had a lower prevalence of infection (13.2%; 5/38) than those without prior medication (50.0%; 46/92). Medication history was significantly associated with reduced risk (RR = 0.263; 95% CI: 0.113–0.611) and lower odds of infection (OR = 0.152; 95% CI: 0.054–0.423; p < 0.05) (Table 1).

Discussion

The current study offers an in‐depth look at the prevalence and diversity of GIPs affecting domestic ducks on small‐scale farms in Central Nepal. Based on copromicroscopic examination, the overall prevalence of GIPs was 39.2%. This is comparable to the 39% prevalence reported in indigenous domestic ducks from the Chittagong/Sylhet region of Bangladesh (Hoque et al. 2014). However, it is considerably lower than the prevalence reported in free‐range domestic ducks from Chandragiri, Nepal (81.67%) (Shrestha et al. 2020) as well as those reported in Bangladesh (78%) (Begum et al. 2019) and India (42%–75.7%) (Jayentakumar Singh and Mohilal 2017; Phijam et al. 2025). This variation in the prevalence rate of GIPs across different countries in these studies may be attributed to several factors, which include biological characteristics such as age, sex and other host factors; environmental landscapes such as seasons and climates; and methodological differences such as diagnostic methods, sampling sizes, sampling designs, as well as management practices like free‐ranging or captive conditions of the hosts. Furthermore, the prevalence of single infection was lower than co‐infection involving two parasite taxa and infections involving three parasite taxa; however, the differences in data were not significant (p > 0.05), contrasting with the findings from studies in Nepal (Shrestha et al. 2020) and Iran (Larki et al. 2018). In general, polyparasitism is linked to greater virulence and has a significant synergistic effect on host health (Donohue et al. 2019; Bell et al. 2006). Such an association due to concomitant protozoa and metazoans has been reported in previous studies in fowls, such as chickens (Mousa et al. 2024) and pigeons (Adhikari et al. 2022). This implies that the current duck population, with a high frequency of mixed parasitic infections (28.5%), could experience worse outcomes, such as malabsorption, growth retardation and higher mortality, than those with single infections, indicating a need for histopathologic studies of the heavily infected ducks in the study area.

When comparing protozoa prevalence rates, a significant difference was observed (p < 0.05). However, no significant difference was observed in the prevalence rates of the different helminths (p > 0.05). The prevalence of protozoa (33.8%) was significantly higher than that of helminths (14.6%) (p < 0.05). In addition, diversity was higher in protozoa compared to helminths. Similar results were obtained from Egypt (Hasanien et al. 2025) and Iran (Shemshadi et al. 2017), although opposite results were recorded from the previous studies of Nepal (Shrestha et al. 2020) and Indonesia (Anggrahini et al. 2025). In Egypt, Cryptosporidium, Eimeria anatis, Tyzzeria spp., Eimeria mullardi and Wenyonella anatis were recorded in native breed and Muscovy ducks (Cairina moschata) (Hasanien et al. 2025). In Northern Iran, Trichomonas gallinae, Cryptosporidium bailey and Eimeria were detected in domestic ducks (Anas platyrhynchas) (Shemshadi et al. 2017). The higher frequency of protozoan parasites observed in the current study might be associated with the predominance of samples from free‐ranging duck populations (74.5%) and their rearing under traditional management systems, which could increase exposure to contaminated water sources. However, this interpretation is speculative, as environmental exposure and management practices were not directly assessed in the present study, suggesting further study must assess the combined role of environmental factors and conventional husbandry practices in shaping parasite prevalence and diversity.

The higher frequency in the occurrence of Cryptosporidium sp. (32.3%) among the protozoa is of particular concern because thick‐walled cryptosporidial oocysts are highly environmentally robust, demonstrating prolonged survival in water sources and moist environments (King et al. 2005). Notably, ducks raised in traditional free‐ranging, semi‐intensive systems often forage and drink from ponds, rivers and other open water bodies, which may easily become contaminated with Cryptosporidium oocysts. Such environmental exposure in the current duck population likely explains why Cryptosporidium was the most frequently detected parasite. Therefore, it is more likely that high stock density facilitates faecal contamination and moisture content in pens, favouring the greater survival of parasitic eggs/cysts/oocysts and larvae over extended periods (Waindok et al. 2022). In this scenario, ducks foraging, drinking or feeding in such pens may ingest these infective stages and acquire infection. Furthermore, low farmer awareness regarding farm management and preventive practices, including hygiene maintenance and the provision of nutritious feed inadequately, allows parasites to circulate unchecked (Sayeed et al. 2024; Chavarro‐Tulcán et al. 2021). Additionally, the adoption of irregular or no medication practices by farmers allows infecting parasites to complete their life cycle and persist within the flock, facilitating continuous shedding of eggs/cysts/oocysts and reinfection. Interestingly, we observed that older ducks exhibited a higher prevalence of parasites than younger ducks. These age‐related differences may be associated with changes in immune function, including immunosenescence (Chen et al. 2024), and prolonged cumulative infection over time, which facilitates the establishment and persistence of parasites. However, as such immunological parameters were not assessed, these require further explanation in these hosts.

The current finding was important for assessing the zoonotic potential of the parasites present in ducks. For example, similar to this study, zoonotic Cryptosporidium species such as C. parvum, C. meleagridis, C. baileyi, and Cryptosporidium duck genotype have already been detected in ducks (Wang et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2010). Notably, another zoonotic GIP, Blastocystis sp., has been reported in the current study. While its pathogenic impact in ducks remains unclear, it may contribute to subclinical gastrointestinal disturbances (Ilchyshyn and Monti 2019). Moreover, Blastocystosis may be a matter of concern, since molecular evidence of Blastocystis sp. ST7, a zoonotic strain, had been confirmed in the wild duck population from Portugal (Gomes‐Gonçalves et al. 2024), suggesting a greater risk of occupational exposure. Therefore, appropriate caution is advised for farmers, veterinarians and handlers who come into contact with duck faeces or contaminated water sources.

Finally, we identified a few GIPs critical for ducks but not evident in humans. Entamoeba spp. are considered low‐pathogenic parasites in birds; however, amoebiasis can still cause mild enteritis and reduced nutrient absorption, which can become problematic during concomitant infection (Yegani and Korver 2008). Although the diversity and host specificity of avian Entamoeba remain incompletely understood, two species, E. gallinarum of the coli group and E. anatis of the histolytica group, have been described in ducks (Martínez‐Díaz et al. 2000; Levine 1985). Because current ducks have access to nearby rivers and other water bodies that may be contaminated with human and animal faeces, this exposure may facilitate the ingestion and transient carriage of cysts of Entamoeba spp. via contaminated water, feed or other environmental sources within the free‐ranging production system. Nevertheless, molecular characterization would be required to confirm the taxonomic identity of the detected Entamoeba species in these ducks.

Similarly, the presence of Eimeria sp. can lead to coccidiosis, the primary cause of economic losses in poultry, including ducks (Dalloul and Lillehoj 2006; Pascalon‐Pekelniczky et al. 1994; Tadesse and Feyissa 2016). Tyzzeria sp. was detected in two (1.5%) of the faecal samples from young ducks. The rate was much lower than reported from Iraq, ranging from 11.25% to 25% (Abdulla 2010). Its presence warrants caution because it is highly pathogenic, especially for the ducklings (Larki et al. 2018; Berto et al. 2022), potentially causing diarrheal disease, poor weight gain and mortality. Additionally, the detection of Ascaridia galli, taeniid and Strongyle species, though at lower prevalence, suggests that these helminths can still adversely affect duck health and productivity.

The current study has a few limitations. First, we applied only DWM and AFS techniques to detect GIPs, but we did not apply concentration methods, such as sedimentation and flotation. It might have caused a lower detected rate of parasites than the actual diversity and prevalence. Second, we used only morphological confirmation of the parasite. It is not reliable and scientific to confirm Entamoeba sp., Tyzzeria sp., Eimeria sp. and Cryptosporidium sp. without molecular confirmation, and eggs of Taeniid and Strongyle without larval culture and molecular assays. Third, none of the ducks were examined for clinical signs, body conditions or mortality, so the parasite impact could not be evaluated. Fourth, quantitative faecal egg counting was not performed, so we could not evaluate the parasitic load in terms of GIP eggs or oocysts. Fifth, we did not conduct our study with respect to the season that could generate large amounts of data across broad ecological landscapes. Finally, using RR and OR could result in biased analyses, although we have included these results. However, despite these limitations, the current research is qualitatively controlled, and we strictly and clearly followed our objectives and methods, making it rational and scientific.

Conclusion

This study revealed a 39.2% prevalence and the presence of nine different taxa of GIPs among domestic ducks reared on smallholder farms in Kathmandu, Nepal. The predominance of protozoa, particularly the Cryptosporidium sp. implicated in zoonosis over helminths, highlights that protozoan infections constitute an important component of the GIPs burden in domestic ducks and may have implications for animal health and potentially, public health. Additionally, factors such as older age, high stocking density, lack of regular deworming and lack of farmer's knowledge regarding duck parasitism may be associated with higher prevalence and diversity of parasites. Based on our findings, further epidemiological and molecular investigations are needed to better understand the parasite acquisition and transmission dynamics, as well as parasite identification and evaluation of their zoonotic significance. The current findings provide baseline information that may inform future GIP surveillance and the development of evidence‐based sustainable farm management and parasite control strategies that include increasing farmers’ awareness and training for enhancing domestic duck production systems in the country.

Ethics Statement

Oral consent was obtained from the farm owners, who voluntarily agreed to provide information about ducks and farming practices before faecal sampling. They provided information on ducks' characteristics (age, medication history, rearing practices and other factors). Only the duck owners who wanted to provide information and permitted faecal collection were included in the study. No ducks were harmed during the sampling process. The study used faecal samples collected from naturally infected ducks, and no experimental infections were performed on any ducks during this work.
