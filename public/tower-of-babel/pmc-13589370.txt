Urban-rural disparities in injury mortality in China, 2002–2021
Authors: Youyou Wu, Ruobing Zhang, David C Schwebel, Li Li, Guoqing Hu
Published: 2026

Background

Injuries are a leading cause of death and disability worldwide, contributing significantly to the global disease burden. In China, injuries were responsible for over 540,000 deaths in 2021, comprising 5.6% of the national fatalities [1]. In response, the Chinese government prioritized injury prevention through strategic initiatives such as Healthy China 2030 [2] and the National Action Plan for Disease Prevention and Control (2024–2025), both of which aim to mitigate the overall injury burden [3].

Urban-rural disparities are a common challenge for injury prevention globally. Prior research examined urban-rural gaps at the national level for China in selected years—including 2006 [4], 2010–2016 [5], and 2010–2021 [6]—and at the provincial level in Hubei (2006–2008) [7] and Sichuan (2009–2017) [8]. These reports consistently reveal a higher injury burden in rural areas. However, these studies are limited by short temporal scopes [4, 5, 6, 7, 8], single-year snapshots [4], and restriction to specific provinces [7, 8], a particular injury cause [6], or certain age groups [6, 8]. A comprehensive and up-to-date national assessment of urban-rural disparities in injury mortality remains unreported.

This study aimed to examine long-term trends in urban-rural disparities in injury mortality in China using the latest official data from 2002 to 2021, with stratified analyses by age group, sex, and major causes of injury to provide a detailed characterization of these disparities.

Methods

Injury mortality data were obtained from the Chinese Health Statistics Yearbook (CHSY), which provides annual cause-specific injury mortality rates stratified by sex (male vs. female), age group (in 5-year intervals), and place (urban vs. rural area). The most recent available yearbook was updated through 2024, covering mortality data from 2002 to 2021 [1]. Urban areas were defined as municipal districts within municipalities directly under the Central Government and prefecture-level cities, while rural areas were defined as counties and county-level cities, in accordance with the administrative classification used in the CHSY. Injury-related deaths were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) and included the following categories: transport-related injury, poisoning, falls, drowning, suicide, homicide, fire/burn injuries, and mechanical suffocation.

Because CHSY only provides mortality rates in five-year age groups but without corresponding death counts or population denominators, we estimated the population denominators for each five-year age group by linear interpolation using data from the 2000, 2010, and 2020 Chinese Population Censuses, following a previously published approach [6]. These estimated population denominators were then used to calculate age-specific and age-standardized mortality rates. Age-standardization employed the 2020 national census population as the standard population. The five-year age groups were combined into five categories (0–4, 5–24, 25–44, 45–64, and ≥ 65 years) representing broad life stages with distinct injury profiles, while ensuring sufficient and stable mortality estimates within each stratum. Urban-rural disparities were quantified using rural-to-urban mortality ratios (rural rate divided by urban rate).

Line charts were plotted to demonstrate urban-rural injury mortality rates by sex, age group, and cause of injuries (transport-related injury, falls, suicide, drowning, poisoning, and other injury causes) during 2002–2021. Stacked bar charts were graphed to present contributions of major injury causes to the overall, sex- and age-specific urban-rural injury mortality differences.

Changes in injury mortality ratios were assessed using Joinpoint regression, by which annual percent change (APC) and average annual percent change (AAPC) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to quantify changes. Subgroup analyses stratified by age group, sex, and cause of injury were performed. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

All analyses were performed using Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.4.0, National Cancer Institute, Calverton, USA) and R (version 4.2.2).

Overall urban-rural differences in injury mortality

From 2002 to 2021, age-standardized injury mortality rates in China were consistently higher in rural areas than in urban areas (Fig. 1), with rural-to-urban mortality ratios ranging from 1.07 to 2.37 (Fig. 3A). The magnitude of these disparities did not change significantly over the study period (AAPC = 0.73%, 95% CI: -0.62%, 2.10%) (Table 1).

Sex- and age-specific urban-rural differences

Both sexes exhibited higher injury mortality rates in rural areas relative to urban areas throughout 2002–2021 (Fig. 1B and C). The temporal trends in rural-to-urban injury mortality ratios were similar between males and females, and neither demonstrated a significant change over time (males: AAPC = 0.88%, 95% CI: -0.54%, 2.32%; females: AAPC = 0.76%, 95% CI: -0.59%, 2.12%) (Table 1).

From 2002 to 2021, rural injury mortality rates were generally higher than urban rates across all five age groups studied, with the exception of 2005, when rural rates were lower than urban rates among aged ≥65 years (Fig. 1D to H). With 2005 excluded, the rural-to-urban mortality ratios showed broadly similar patterns across the five age groups (Fig. 3). No significant changes in these ratios were observed for any age group over the study period (Table 1 and Fig. 3).

Cause-specific urban-rural differences

Rural areas experienced higher cause-specific injury mortality than urban areas in most study years, but exceptions were observed for transport-related injuries in 2005 and for poisoning in 2002 and 2006 (Fig. 2). Rural-to-urban mortality ratios were generally greater for suicide and drowning than for other injury causes (Fig. 3). Notably, significant increases in rural-to-urban mortality ratios were observed for falls (AAPC = 1.21%, 95% CI: 0.27%, 2.16%) and poisoning (AAPC = 3.23%, 95% CI: 1.61%, 4.87%) (Table 1).

During 2002–2021, transport-related injuries, suicide, and falls were the primary contributors to the urban-rural disparity in injury mortality for both sexes (Fig. 4A and B, and 4C). The relative contributions of transport-related injuries and suicide decreased over time, while that of falls increased. Cause-specific contributions varied markedly by age group. Drowning was the leading contributor of the urban-rural disparity among children aged 0–4 years in 17 of the 20 years studied (34%–66%) and among youth aged 5–24 years in 10 of the 20 years. Transport-related injuries predominantly contributed to the urban-rural disparity among adults aged 25–44 years and 45–64 years in 18 (38%–66%) and 17 of the 20 years (32%–61%), respectively. Among adults aged ≥ 65 years, suicide accounted for the largest proportion of the disparity in 13 of the 20 years (29%–67%).

Discussion

Using the latest national data, this study performed a comprehensive assessment of urban-rural disparities in injury mortality across China from 2002 to 2021. Three key findings were generated. First, despite overall declines in age-standardized injury mortality rates in both urban and rural areas, the magnitude of the urban-rural difference did not change significantly over the two decades. Second, trends stratified by sex and age group were broadly similar, with no significant changes observed in the rural-to-urban mortality ratios for any subgroup. Third, cause-specific rural-to-urban mortality ratios were most pronounced for suicide and drowning, and the urban-rural gap widened significantly for falls and poisoning over time. The urban-rural disparity was mainly the result of transport-related injuries, suicide, and falls, though the leading contributor varied distinctly across five age groups.

Consistent with previous studies in China [8, 9], overall injury mortality declined in both urban and rural areas during the study period. This decline coincided with improvements in injury prevention policies and enhanced health services in China over the past two decades, factors that may have contributed to the observed trends [10]. Studies from other countries also report persistent rural disadvantages in injury mortality, although the magnitude of these disparities varies [11, 12]. In the United States, the urban-rural mortality disparity increased from the mid-1980s through 2016 [11]. In New Zealand, injury-related mortality was markedly higher in rural areas than in urban areas during 2014–2018 [12].

The sustained disparity between rural and urban areas across different settings may be partially explained by structural disadvantages commonly faced in rural areas, including greater exposure to environmental hazards, weaker law enforcement, lower infrastructure investment, and constrained emergency medical capacity. Many of these disadvantages may be associated with economic deprivation, and previous research reports higher injury mortality in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas, including in high-poverty United States counties during 1999–2012 [13]. These persistent structural differences may help interpret the continued urban-rural mortality gap, despite significant declines in injury mortality in both urban and rural China [7, 10, 12, 13].

The absence of significant changes in rural-to-– urban rate ratios across sex and age strata may reflect broadly similar improvements in injury prevention and care in both urban and rural areas, as parallel progress would reduce overall mortality without a corresponding change in the relative disparity. This finding contrasts with a previous study, however, that reported a notable narrowing of urban-rural disparities across a shorter time period (2010–2016) [5]. The discrepancy may be attributable to differences in the study periods as well as use of different data sources (Chinese Health Statistics Yearbook vs. Disease Surveillance Point system).

From a cause-specific perspective, overall urban-rural disparities were most pronounced for suicide and drowning between 2002 and 2021, while the urban-rural disparity in fall mortality widened significantly over the study time period. When examined by age group, the disparity was mainly observed for transport-related injuries, suicide, and falls, although the leading causes differed substantially across the five age groups. These patterns indicate that urban-rural inequalities likely reflect multiple structural and contextual factors that may differ across age groups.

In the early 2000s, suicide-related disparities were notably large, consistent with reports of historically elevated rural suicide rates in prior research from China [7, 14]. This pattern may be associated with limited access to mental health services, social isolation, and greater availability of lethal means, such as pesticides, in rural areas. The decline in suicide-related disparities during the late 2000s and the 2010s may be consistent with regulatory improvements, including pesticide control, and broad social and health system changes in rural China [14]. International evidence suggests that the direction and magnitude of urban-rural disparities in suicide mortality differ across countries. Consistent with our findings, a study from South Korea reported a narrowing urban-rural gap during 1992–2012, though the disparity widened among older adults [15]. In contrast, the urban–rural disparities in suicide mortality grew in the United States between 2005 and 2015 [16].

In more recent years, transport-related injuries have emerged as the dominant contributor to the urban-rural disparity among the working-age population. Rapid motorization and improved rural mobility, coupled with less developed road infrastructure, weak enforcement of traffic safety regulations, and limited emergency response and medical services in rural areas, may have increased vulnerability to transport-related injury mortality [17]. A study from the United States reported higher rural motor vehicle traffic mortality, with the age-adjusted rate in rural areas nearly twice that in urban areas in 2017 (19.7 vs. 10.2 per 100,000) [18]. Meanwhile, the widening urban-rural gap in fall mortality may be associated with the combined effects of accelerated population aging and the increasing burden of chronic conditions in both urban and rural areas [6, 19], together with comparatively poor fall injury prevention capacities in rural settings (e.g., weak safety awareness, living and community settings with more fall hazards, less developed fall prevention services, and limited access to emergency and medical services) [20].

We observed unusual mortality patterns in 2005 across multiple age groups and injury categories. The available CHSY data did not allow us to explore explanatory factors for these anomalies. A previous study documented limitations in China’s mortality registration system, however, including variation in surveillance coverage, registration completeness, and the accuracy of cause-of-death ascertainment [21, 22]. Underreporting, misclassification of injury causes, and changes in surveillance sites or reporting practices may have contributed to the observed anomaly. Therefore, findings, especially from 2005, should be interpreted with caution.

Policy implications

Our findings carry important implications. First, the persistence of large urban-rural disparities underscores the urgent need for enhanced and targeted injury prevention efforts in rural areas of China. Interventions should prioritize the leading injury causes among high-risk populations—such as drowning prevention in rural children and youths, transport-related injury prevention for working-age adults, and suicide and fall prevention among older rural residents. Evidence-based strategies should be scaled up in both rural and urban settings to reduce the preventable burden of injuries. Second, the anomalous mortality data observed in 2005 highlight the need to strengthen the evaluation, quality control, and accuracy of vital registration data in China. If and where data quality issues are identified, government agencies should adjust historically affected statistics and release revised estimates to ensure that evidence-based decisions are grounded in the most accurate information available.

Limitations

This study is subject to several limitations. First, our findings are inherently influenced by the quality of data in China’s national vital registration system, including variations in reporting completeness, cause-of-death classification, and changes in mortality surveillance practices over time, factors that may have affected the observed temporal trends. Second, this descriptive ecological study was based on aggregated national mortality data. Therefore, the observed population-level patterns should not be interpreted as individual-level results. In addition, the lack of individual- and community-level data on socioeconomic status, health care services, and environmental exposures prevents us from examining specific causal factors underlying observed urban-rural disparities. Future research incorporating these factors would be valuable to elucidate the pathways underlying urban-rural disparities and inform targeted injury prevention policy, research, and practice. Last, the Chinese Health Statistics Yearbook provides mortality data only at the national urban-rural aggregate level, and representative provincial-level data are unavailable. Therefore, geographic variation across Chinese provinces was not examined.

Conclusions

From 2002 to 2021, urban-rural disparities in injury mortality in China persisted without significant reductions, despite overall declines in age-standardized rates in both urban and rural areas. Transport-related injuries, suicide, and falls accounted for the largest proportions of the excess injury mortality burden in rural areas, with the leading contributor varying by sex and age group. These findings underscore the critical need for enhanced and targeted injury prevention efforts in rural China.
