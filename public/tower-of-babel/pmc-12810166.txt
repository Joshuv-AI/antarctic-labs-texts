Effect of Laser Irradiation on Fluoride Uptake in Primary Teeth Treated with 38% Silver Diamine Fluoride or 5% Sodium Fluoride Varnish: An In Vitro Study
Authors: Vaishali Bhati, Vinay Kumar Srivastava
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeFluoride is essential for protecting the teeth from dental caries. The combined use of lasers with fluoride is being explored to increase the fluoride uptake of the tooth after topical fluoride application. The present research aimed to assess the efficacy of diode laser irradiation in enhancing fluoride uptake in primary teeth enamel following topical fluoride application.MethodsA total of 14 primary teeth (70 enamel slabs) were selected for the study. From each primary tooth, five enamel slabs were made and were put randomly into five different groups, that is, group I: control (no treatment), group II: NaF varnish (sodium fluoride), group III: SDF (silver diamine fluoride), group IV: NaF varnish with laser irradiation, and group V: SDF with laser irradiation. NaF varnish (5%) and SDF (38%) were topically applied in groups II and III, respectively, without laser irradiation, while in groups IV and V diode laser was used following NaF varnish (5%) and SDF (38%) application, respectively. Fluoride content was measured in all groups using a scanning electron microscope and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM–EDX) analysis. Results were analyzed using the t-test and analysis of variance (ANOVA) test, followed by Bonferroni corrections.ResultsIncreased fluoride content was found in groups II, III, IV, and V in comparison to group I. Higher fluoride content was noted in NaF varnish with laser irradiation and SDF with laser irradiation groups in contrast to NaF varnish and SDF groups, respectively; however, the difference between NaF varnish and NaF varnish with laser irradiation groups as well as between SDF and SDF with laser irradiation groups was statistically insignificant (p < 0.05).ConclusionThe SDF with laser application group was found to be the most effective in increasing the fluoride content of the enamel, and the sequence of effectiveness is as follows: SDF with laser application > SDF ≥ NaF with laser application > NaF > control. Therefore, more in vitro and in vivo studies need to be conducted to evaluate the combined use of lasers and topical fluoride.How to cite this articleBhati V, Srivastava VK. Introduction

Fluoride has been identified as an essential factor in declining the severity and prevalence of dental caries.1 Its uptake into the tooth structure can either be through a systemic route or by topical fluoride application. Fluoride protects the tooth by increasing its acid resistance, remineralizing the incipient lesions, and interfering with microorganisms by inhibiting bacterial metabolism and enzymatic processes.2,3 Interaction between tooth surface and fluoride depends on various factors such as concentration and pH of the topical fluoride agent.4

Distinct mechanisms exist for fluoride to react with apatite: ion exchange of fluoride for hydroxyl (F for OH) and fluorapatite crystal development from supersaturated solutions, or apatite dissolution accompanied by the creation of calcium fluoride. Calcium fluoride formation is the main, or the only, reaction product on enamel during brief fluoride exposure with concentrated agents, although other reactions may occur over long-term exposure to low levels of fluoride in the solutions.5

After fluoride application, calcium fluoride precipitates are formed over the tooth and to a depth of 40 μm under the enamel surface, forming a fluoride ion reservoir.6 Whenever the intraoral pH decreases, these calcium fluoride crystals dissolve to release fluoride ions, replacing the hydroxyl ion of the hydroxyapatite crystals and forming stronger fluorohydroxyapatite crystals, thereby preventing degradation of tooth structure under acid attack.6 Saxegaard and Rolla found that reducing the pH of a fluoride solution or increasing the concentration and/or exposure duration could considerably increase the amount of calcium fluoride.7

Commonly used professionally applied fluoride treatments are 5% NaF (sodium fluoride) varnish (2.26% or 22,600 ppm fluoride), SDF (silver diamine fluoride) (38% Ag [NH3]2F or 44,800 ppm of fluoride), and acidulated phosphate fluoride (1.23% or 12,300 ppm fluoride).8,9

Recently, lasers have been utilized as a supplement to conventional caries prevention therapies. Different structural and physicochemical alterations that provide cariostatic properties can be brought about by different kinds of lasers when irradiated at the right wavelength. A few of these modifications include the reduction of carbonate from the enamel hydroxyapatite, modifying the morphology of the enamel surface to a melted and resolidified state to decrease permeability, increasing fluoride deposition on enamel surfaces, and causing the formation of fluorapatite, which is more resistant to acid demineralization than apatite containing carbonate or hydroxyl ions.10

Many studies have been performed in the past to evaluate the combined use of lasers and fluoride on caries-preventive effects in permanent teeth, but there is not enough literature regarding the evaluation of the impact of fluoride on primary teeth.11–14 It is important to evaluate the combined use of fluoride and lasers on fluoride uptake in primary teeth enamel, as the pattern of caries development is different in permanent and primary teeth. The primary teeth with more porosity and less mineral density are more prone to dental caries than permanent teeth.10

As only a few studies exist in the literature assessing the impact of different lasers on the fluoride content of enamel, our study aimed to assess and compare the fluoride content of primary teeth enamel after diode laser application following SDF or fluoride varnish application, using SEM–EDX at various depths from the enamel surface. The null hypothesis tested that there is no significant difference in fluoride content at various depths from the enamel surface between primary teeth treated with topical fluoride and diode laser or those treated with topical fluoride alone.

Materials and Methods

Tooth Selection and Slab Preparation

This in vitro study was performed at the Unit of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, which was approved from the Institutional Ethical Committee under reference number Dean/2022/EC./3480. The sample size was calculated using the software G*Power 3.1.9.2. Based on the study done by Mei et al., with the power of the study maintained at 95% and the effect size of 1.47, the sample size was calculated as 14 per group.12

In the study, a total of 14 noncarious primary teeth were included, while teeth with any caries, developmental defect, traumatic injury, or hypomineralization were excluded. Following extraction, defect-free primary teeth were thoroughly cleansed under running water to eliminate any blood contamination, and then left in a 10% formalin solution for a week to ensure disinfection.

Before sectioning, each tooth was examined with magnifying loupes for any cracks, hypoplastic defects, or white spot lesions, and was washed with running tap water followed by final cleaning with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Roots of the tooth, if present, were resected from the crown using a diamond disc (DFS Disc Rapido Cut, Germany) attached to a slow-speed handpiece (Marathon M4 Micromotor–Hand Piece, Equitech Engineers Private Limited) under constant water spray as a coolant. From each tooth, five enamel slabs of dimensions 2 × 2 × 2 mm3 were made and randomly allocated into five groups.

Resected enamel slabs were finished by rubbing them in a circular motion for 45–50 strokes against carbide paper of increasing grit size and finally finished with 2,500 sizes. Slabs were cleaned with distilled water following each round of finishing.

After finishing and polishing, all the samples were thoroughly cleaned in the ultrasonic bath for 5 minutes using distilled water for the removal of debris and smear layer from the slab surfaces. All the enamel slabs were stored at room temperature inside distilled water until the study began and dried with a 3-in-1 syringe before applying topical fluoride.

Topical Fluoride Application

Topical fluoride agents used were 5% NaF varnish (Profluorid by VOCO company) and 38% SDF (E-SDF by Kids-e-Dental), which were applied by a single operator. The five groups were:

Group I: Control (no treatment was done).

Group II: NaF varnish (0.05 mL of 5% NaF varnish was applied on the slabs using a microbrush with horizontal strokes and left for drying for 4 minutes as per the manufacturer's instructions).

Group III: SDF (0.05 mL of 38% SDF was applied on the slabs using a microbrush with horizontal strokes and left for drying for 3 minutes as per the manufacturer's instructions).

Group IV: NaF varnish with laser irradiation (0.05 mL of 5% NaF varnish was applied on the slabs using a microbrush with horizontal strokes, immediately followed by the diode laser irradiation).

Group V: SDF with laser irradiation (0.05 mL of 38% SDF was applied on the slabs using a microbrush with horizontal strokes, immediately followed by the diode laser irradiation).

Laser Treatment

The slabs in groups IV and V were irradiated with a laser (Doctor Smile Simpler) after topical fluoride application. Laser parameters were: 980 nm wavelength, 3 W output power, 30 seconds irradiation time, continuous wave (CW) mode, and were administered in sweeping motion. The slabs were irradiated with the laser in noncontact mode. The hand piece was moved uniformly 4 mm away from the slab surface all the time (Table 1). All the safety measures were taken care of during laser irradiation, followed by the analysis of slabs using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) and an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDX) (ZEISS Smart EDX, Germany).

SEM–EDX Analysis

The elemental assessment of fluoride was done by measuring fluoride content (in weight%) at five different levels. The fluoride levels were measured using EDX analysis at the enamel surface, 5, 10, 15, and 20 μm below the surface in every group by placing the resected surface of the tooth slab facing the electron beam of SEM and the normal enamel surface sideways. The SEM images were obtained at magnifications of 500×, 2000×, and 5000×. The entire flow chart of the study design is represented in Figures 1 and 2.

Statistical Analysis

Data was analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 28. For multiple comparisons, the ANOVA test was applied, followed by Bonferroni corrections. For comparing two groups, a t-test was used. A p-value of <0.05 was defined as a level of statistical significance.

Discussion

The uptake of fluoride is limited to the superficial layers of the tooth following topical fluoride application15; therefore, attempts are made to increase the depth of fluoride uptake with systemic and topical fluoride applications to improve the long-term effectiveness of the treatment. Studies on the combined use of topical fluoride and laser in permanent teeth have shown increased fluoride uptake in dental hard tissues; following the laser procedure, fluoride not only gets deposited over the tooth surface but also gets incorporated into the deeper crystalline structure.16

The interaction of a laser with different tissues depends on multiple laser parameters (wavelength, energy output, exposure time, and mode of laser irradiation emission), and the optical properties of the lased tissues (hydration condition, etc.)11 (Fig. 6). Laser considerably improves the enamel's acid resistance by breaking down the organic matrix and losing carbonates by surface melting and recrystallization, thus changing the enamel's permeability and crystallinity. Fluoride gets integrated into the enamel through the thermal action of laser therapy11 and is retained in surface modifications (such as microfractures and increased surface roughness) caused by the laser.17

Flow chart showing laser interaction with enamel following application

Lasing the tooth surface results in a rise of the tooth temperature, leading to an accelerated reaction rate between the fluoride and tooth structure, resulting in increased fluoride uptake and the formation of a greater amount of fluorohydroxyapatite crystals, replacing hydroxy and carbonates and thus generating a more stable molecular orientation.18

Application of laser irradiation following topical fluoride application improved the mechanical and tribological characteristics of the CaF2-like deposits, increased fluoride uptake, and resulted in a considerable reduction in the diameter and quantity of CaF2-like crystals, suggesting that the CaF2-like crystals are fused with the enamel.19 Similar findings were observed in our study, where globular structures were observed on the surface of enamel in groups without laser irradiation; however, after laser irradiation, a smoother surface was seen, which might occur from the melting of globular structures into the enamel, thereby leading to a smoother and stronger surface.

Presently, there are no standard parameters for utilizing lasers in caries prevention therapy. Diode lasers are one type of laser that has gained popularity in clinical practice because of their numerous benefits, such as their small size, affordability, and ease of use inside the oral cavity. In the past, various authors have used multiple diode laser settings: wavelengths (809, 940, or 980 nm), average powers (0.5–10 W), irradiation time (3 seconds–1 minute), and mode of laser irradiation (contact or noncontact: 3–5 mm away from the tooth surface).11,12,20,21 In our study, a CW laser source operating at 980 nm wavelength with 3 W of output power and 30 seconds of irradiation period in sweeping motion was used.22 The enamel slabs were exposed to laser radiation in non-contact mode. The hand piece was consistently moved across the slab surface, always keeping the laser 4–5 mm away from the slab. The goal was to replicate an in vivo setting because maintaining the irradiation distance precisely is not always possible.20

In our study, no significant difference in fluoride uptake was seen following laser irradiation between NaF varnish and NaF varnish with laser application groups or SDF and SDF with laser application groups (Tables 2 and 3). Previous research has proved significant uptake of fluoride after laser irradiation following topical fluoride application in permanent teeth.12 However, no such finding has been documented in primary teeth, as this may be associated with the greater porosity of primary tooth enamel and the difference in mineral content of primary tooth enamel in comparison to permanent teeth.23 In compliance with our study, Moghadam et al., Bahrololoomi and Lotfian, and Santaella et al., found that fluoride varnish (5% NaF), diode lasers, and both together reduce the loss of enamel microhardness value and prevent demineralization of enamel in primary teeth; however, the combined application of fluoride varnish along with diode lasers did not report better uptake than fluoride alone.10,22,24

There was no significant difference between the NaF varnish with laser application and the SDF with laser application groups in the present study (Table 4), which may depict the limit of laser irradiation in increasing fluoride uptake. No such research has been performed on primary teeth comparing these two fluoride agents with diode laser irradiation and evaluating their fluoride uptake.

The difference between the NaF varnish and SDF groups was insignificant (Table 4). It could be possibly as a result of the more pronounced effect of SDF on dentin in comparison to enamel, as dentin has a higher concentration of protein, carbonate, and phosphates that react with silver in SDF, thereby leading to its better absorption in dentin as compared to enamel.25 These results resembled a study in which the effects of SDF solution and fluoridated varnish on the demineralization resistance of enamel of primary teeth were evaluated, and no discernible difference between the effectiveness of fluoride varnish and SDF solution in preventing the demineralization of primary anterior teeth was found.25

Although the use of laser following fluoride varnish application may add to the cost, in the long run, the beneficial effect of more fluoride intake and fewer carious lesions will prevent the expensive dental treatment. The limitation of this study is a small sample size, due to which it has limited generalizability. Only one kind of laser was used after topical fluoride application in this study, so other studies evaluating and comparing different parameters of different types of lasers should be carried out. As the present study is an in vitro study and there are numerous other factors that could influence fluoride uptake in clinical settings, further large-scale clinical trials should be carried out to corroborate the findings of the present study.

Conclusion

Silver diamine fluoride with laser application group was found most effective in increasing the fluoride content of the enamel, and the sequence of effectiveness is as follows: SDF with laser application > SDF ≥ NaF with laser application > NaF > control. Therefore, lasers during topical fluoride application may be used in the primary or mixed dentition stage for better uptake of fluoride into the tooth structure, thereby reducing the chances of dental caries and its consequences.
