A Multifunctional Ionograsper Enabling Ion‐Redistribution Proximity Sensing and Structural‐Reconfiguration‐Driven Bidirectional Actuation
Authors: Yong Min Kim, Jin Han Kwon, Hyeon Woo Yang, Sungryong Kim, Gyeong Rok Lee, Hong Chul Moon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAutonomous soft actuators are essential for untethered intelligent robotic systems. However, achieving energy‐efficient operation and long‐term shape retention in solid‐state platforms remains a significant challenge, as most conventional systems rely on continuous power input or complex fluidic architectures. Here, we report an environmentally adaptive ionic actuator inspired by the Venus flytrap, capable of self‐sensing and bidirectional deformation without sustained external energy. By incorporating azobenzene moieties into an ionogel network, we trigger a unique nanopore‐mediated Janus actuation. A single, low‐power UV pulse (35.3 mW cm−2) induces rapid photoisomerization and localized dehydration, forming surface nanopores that facilitate asymmetric moisture uptake. This structural reconfiguration drives directional bending that is passively maintained for over 10 min, which is an exceptionally long retention time compared to conventional soft actuators. Furthermore, electrification‐induced voltage signals enable zero‐bias proximity sensing. By integrating these synergistic characteristics, we demonstrate a biomimetic ionograsper that combines ion‐redistribution‐induced sensing with energy‐efficient light‐triggered actuation for sophisticated object manipulation. Overall, this work provides a robust strategy for developing multifunctional and power‐efficient soft robots capable of intelligent environmental interaction.Keywords: bidirectional actuation, biomimetic ionograsper, ionic soft robotics, low‐power operation, noncontact sensing

Introduction

Robotic systems have enabled high‐precision operations in diverse fields, including factory automation, surgical assistance, and warehouse logistics [1, 2, 3]. Most conventional robots are based on rigid architectures that perform well in structured environments but lack the adaptability and safety required in dynamic, unpredictable, or delicate contexts. To overcome these limitations, soft robotics has emerged as a promising alternative. Typically composed of elastomers, hydrogels, and ionogels, soft robots offer a high degree of freedom in motion and functionality [4, 5]. Their compliant and deformable structures support smooth interaction with the human body, adaptability to irregular environments, and safe manipulation of delicate objects. Taking advantage of these attributes, soft robotics has expanded its applicability to areas that are challenging for traditional rigid systems, including biomedical devices and human‐machine interfaces [6, 7, 8, 9, 10].

Depending on their structural design and material characteristics, soft robots primarily utilize two actuation principles. The first involves stimuli‐responsive materials (e.g., liquid crystalline polymers [11, 12, 13], lower critical solution temperature (LCST) polymers [14, 15], shape‐memory polymers [16, 17], and azobenzene compounds [18, 19]) that undergo reversible shape changes in response to external stimuli such as heat, electricity, light, or humidity [20, 21]. The second strategy exploits pneumatic or hydraulic actuation, where motion is generated by pressurizing embedded fluidic networks within soft elastomeric matrices [22, 23]. While these approaches have enabled sophisticated demonstrations of programmable motion and adaptive deformation, they typically require complex structures, continuous power input, and external control modules for both activation and feedback processing [24, 25]. This dependency on sustained energy supply and peripheral hardware not only limits scalability and integration but also poses significant challenges for long‐term autonomy. Therefore, developing simpler and more energy‐efficient systems is essential.

In response, several efforts have been made to develop self‐regulating soft robotic systems that can autonomously adapt to their environment without continuous user intervention. Recent studies, inspired by biological systems that maintain homeostasis through intrinsic feedback loops, have demonstrated soft robotic components capable of closed‐loop, feedback‐driven actuation [26, 27, 28, 29]. For instance, a plant‐inspired gripper utilizing the gravity‐driven flow of liquid metal and exhibiting adaptive deformation has been proposed [28]. When tilted, the gravity‐induced movement of liquid metal through embedded microchannels altered the electrical resistance, generating localized Joule heating that triggers a phase transition in the liquid crystal elastomer. Consequently, the gripper automatically returned to its original shape through an internal feedback mechanism without complex external control.

More recently, a phototactic soft robot based on a photothermal hydrogel was reported to navigate autonomously under light irradiation [29]. In such systems, photothermal nanomaterials (e.g., gold nanoparticles) embedded in the hydrogel converted light into localized heat, creating spatial temperature gradients. These gradients induced asymmetric swelling or contraction of the polymer network, leading to directional deformation and motion toward the light source. The interplay between light absorption, thermal response, and shape transformation provided an intrinsic physical feedback mechanism, enabling untethered actuation independent of electronic control. Nonetheless, many current systems remain heavily dependent on liquid‐phase dynamics, where fluidity facilitates rapid and reversible responses through enhanced molecular mobility [30]. Such reliance typically requires encapsulated or confined architectures, which inherently compromise mechanical robustness while limiting stability in dry or load‐bearing environments. Moreover, maintaining the actuated state often demands continuous energy consumption. These limitations motivate the development of solid‐state soft actuators that operate stably under ambient conditions without relying on fluid mobility.

To realize energy‐efficient actuation without continuous energy input, we took inspiration from the Venus flytrap, a carnivorous plant that captures prey through rapid snap‐buckling and maintains its closed configuration for days (Figure 1a). In contrast to conventional actuators that exhibit only transient responses and require continuous energy consumption, this biological strategy enables sustained deformation from a single stimulus event. Mimicking this natural behavior, we developed an ionic actuator that integrates light‐triggered deformation, passive shape retention, and self‐powered sensing within a single platform. The actuator is based on a polymer network functionalized with azobenzene moieties, where UV illumination induces photoisomerization‐driven structural reconfiguration to trigger actuation. Notably, the device maintains a stable bent configuration for over 10 min after a single low‐power light stimulus (Figure 1b). In addition, the ionic actuator possesses intrinsic proximity‐sensing capabilities, analogous to the sensory hairs of the Venus flytrap. When an object approaches, mobile ions within the network are redistributed via electrification, forming an electric double layer (EDL) and generating measurable electrical signals (Figure 1c). By exploiting this synergistic sensing‐to‐actuation architecture, we demonstrated a low‐power ionograsper, a soft robotic system capable of detecting nearby objects and performing light‐triggered grasping (Figure 1d). These results establish our ionic actuator as a multifunctional platform for untethered operation without reliance on fluid mobility, external circuitry, or sustained energy input.

Results

Design of Bioinspired and Self‐Regulated Robotics Based on Ionic Actuator

The ionic actuator was straightforwardly fabricated by mounting the active material (i.e., azo gel) on a deformable PDMS substrate. The azo gel was composed of a hygroscopic polymer host, poly([(3‐acrylamidopropyl)trimethylammonium][Cl]) (denoted as PAAP), and a photoresponsive azo crosslinker, [4‐amino‐1,1′‐azobenzene‐3,4′‐disulfonic acid][Na] (denoted as 2‐azo) (Figure 1e). The resulting network behaved as an elastic solid due to spontaneous ionic crosslinking between the ammonium groups of PAAP and the sulfonate groups of 2‐azo (Figures S1–S4). After the azo gel precursor solution was coated onto the deformable PDMS substrate and completely dried to form the active layer, the resulting hygroscopic film was allowed to equilibrate under ambient conditions (40% ± 5% RH and 25°C ± 2°C) before use (Figure S5). Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) revealed that the glass transition temperature (T g) increased dependently on the 2‐azo concentration, indicating the formation of a more densely crosslinked and robust polymer network (Figure S6). Despite the increased network stiffness, which typically hinders ion transport, the ionic conductivity of the hydrated gels paradoxically increased with increasing crosslinker content (Figure S7). This enhancement can be rationalized primarily by an increased charge carrier density. Based on the Nernst‐Einstein relation (σ = nqµ), the increase in ionic conductivity can be rationalized by the dominant contribution of the increased charge carrier density (n), which outweighs the potential reduction in ion mobility (µ) induced by the denser network. The spontaneous ionic complexation between PAAP and 2‐azo displaces their original counterions, releasing highly mobile Na+, and Cl− ions into the matrix. Consequently, the surplus of free counterions dominates the overall ion transport behavior, leading to enhanced ionic conductivity. We note that this high conductivity plays a crucial role in enabling the ionic actuator to detect object proximity through the triboelectrification effect.

Beyond its role as a structural crosslinker, 2‐azo served as the photoresponsive unit that drives mechanical actuation of the gel. Upon UV irradiation, the azobenzene moiety underwent reversible trans‐to‐cis photoisomerization (Figure S8), which perturbs the ionically crosslinked polymer network and induces macroscopic deformation. Notably, the ionic actuator exhibited a distinctive bidirectional bending behavior, an active primary curvature under UV exposure followed by a spontaneous secondary deformation after the light was removed (Figure 1f). To quantitatively evaluate the photo‐actuation performance, we compared the operating time and average power consumption per cycle (Figure 1g). The azo gels exhibited prolonged deformation for over ∼10 min under a low UV light intensity of only 35 mW cm−2. This exceptional retention behavior arises from dehydration‐induced structural locking of the ionically crosslinked network, enabling long‐lasting deformation under minimal energy input. This result highlights an effective balance between low energy consumption and persistent actuation compared to conventional soft robotic systems.

Unique Janus Curvation and Unveiling the Mechanism

Azobenzene generally adopts a thermodynamically stable planar trans‐conformation, which favors ordered molecular packing through intermolecular interactions such as π‐π stacking [31, 32]. These features suggest that the initially dispersed 2‐azo linkers reorganized into locally ordered aggregates, generating compact phase‐separated domains within the polymer network [33, 34]. Upon UV irradiation, this packed structure underwent distinct morphological changes initiated by the trans‐to‐cis photoisomerization of the 2‐azo crosslinkers (Figure 2a). Because the cis isomer possesses a bent and less symmetric geometry, this photoisomerization disrupts the original packing order and redistributes internal stress within the network. While such perturbations typically induce a unidirectional bending response in previous azobenzene‐containing systems [33, 34, 35, 36], our system unexpectedly exhibited a bidirectional Janus curvature. The bending angle was defined relative to the initial planar state (0°), with negative and positive values denoting concave bending toward the UV source and convex bending away from the UV source, respectively. Under UV irradiation, the gel underwent concave deformation (negative bending angle), followed by convex bending after light removal (positive bending angle), and subsequently exhibited gradual relaxation toward its initial planar state over time (Figure 2b and Movie S1). This behavior implies that the actuation is not governed by photoisomerization alone, but arises from coupled dynamic processes involving structural reorganization, dehydration, rehydration, and network relaxation within the ionically crosslinked gel. To elucidate this bidirectional actuation mechanism, we categorized the process into three distinct regimes: (1) UV‐induced azobenzene photoisomerization and the associated network rearrangement, (2) moisture‐driven asymmetric swelling and convex curvature, and (3) polymer relaxation and structural recovery. These were further subdivided into four mechanistic sub‐stages, (i) through (iv), for detailed analysis (Figure 2c).

In sub‐stage (i) (regime 1), the trans‐azo linkers underwent rapid trans‐to‐cis photoisomerization within UV exposure, triggering local rearrangements within the polymer network. This process was reflected in the rheological response, where the storage modulus (G′) initially decreased under UV irradiation (Figure S9), indicating transient softening and enhanced local chain mobility. Once the UV light was removed before substantial dehydration occurred, G′ returned to its initial value, confirming the reversibility of this early‐stage viscoelastic modulation. Upon prolonged UV irradiation (>50 s, sub‐stage (ii)), the system entered a distinct second regime associated with surface dehydration, which induced concave deformation. Owing to the strong UV absorption of the azobenzene moieties, the surface temperature of the azo gel rose rapidly to ∼69°C (Figure S10), causing substantial water evaporation from the exposed surface layer. As dehydration proceeded, G′ increased markedly (Figure S9), indicating suppression of polymer‐chain mobility and kinetic fixation of the UV‐reorganized network. Consistent with this UV‐induced mechanical fixation, uniaxial tensile tests showed that UV irradiation increased the Young's modulus of the azo gel from 3.1 to 11.1 kPa, while decreasing the elongation at break from 88% to 10% (Figure S11). This reversible stiffening provides greater resistance to externally imposed deformation and is consistent with the observed shape retention and stable gripping behavior. During this process, the shrinkage of hydrated domains due to rapid dehydration created nanoscale pores within the surface layer (Figure 2d and Movie S2). This UV‐induced nanoporous transformation also enhanced surface wettability as evidenced by a decrease in contact angle from 44.5° to 32.6° (Figure S12), which in turn promoted faster water reabsorption during the subsequent stages (Figure 2e). Consistent with this interpretation, fluorescence‐based water‐uptake visualization showed that a fluorescently labeled water droplet rapidly infiltrated the UV‐treated film, whereas the untreated film retained the fluorescent water layer on its surface for a substantially longer period (Figure S13). These results support that the UV‐induced porous surface promotes rapid surface‐initiated hydration. To further examine whether this enhanced hydration promotes ionic transport, comparative hydrovoltaic measurements were conducted (Figure S14a). Upon water contact, free ions near the UV‐treated porous surface are rapidly hydrated, followed by water propagation through the polymer network. Owing to the difference in ionic mobility between Na+ and Cl–, with Cl– generally exhibiting higher diffusivity [37], hydration‐driven ion transport is expected to generate a transient ionic concentration gradient and charge separation along the film thickness. This transient ionic imbalance produces an ionic diffusion potential, which is detected as a measurable open‐circuit voltage (Voc ) between the two electrodes. The UV‐treated film exhibited a faster and stronger hydrovoltaic voltage response than the untreated film (Figure S14b), consistent with hydration‐mediated ionic redistribution enabled by the UV‐induced porous structure. As water becomes more uniformly distributed throughout the film, the transient ionic concentration gradient and associated diffusion potential gradually relax toward equilibrium, resulting in a decrease in the measured voltage.

In sub‐stage (iii) (regime 2), the nanoporous outer layer started to rehydrate, while the inner region remained relatively dehydrated. This asymmetric hydration gradient across the thickness of the film induced the convex curvature. To the best of our knowledge, this nanopore‐mediated Janus actuation has not been previously reported. Importantly, the actuator did not immediately relax but maintained a significantly curved state for an extended period, showing a self‐sustained post‐irradiation holding behavior rather than an instantaneous elastic rebound. As the surface‐initiated rehydration gradually propagated toward the interior of the polymer network, the system gradually returned to its original configuration (sub‐stage (iv), regime 3). This recovery was accompanied by a recovery of the segmental relaxation time (Figure 2f), corresponding to restoration of the original network dynamics. In the fully dried state, polymer‐chain mobility is severely restricted, preventing the structural rearrangement required to translate molecular‐level photoisomerization into macroscopic deformation. Real‐time analysis of curvature, weight, and viscoelasticity elucidated this stage‐dependent actuation cycle (Figure 2g). The sample weight remained nearly constant in sub‐stage (i) (photoisomerization only), decreased in sub‐stage (ii) due to dehydration, and then gradually recovered during sub‐stages (iii) and (iv) via moisture reabsorption. Correspondingly, the bending angle first became negative during UV irradiation, switched to positive during asymmetric rehydration, and finally returned toward baseline during recovery. Notably, during UV irradiation, the decrease in G′ indicates transient softening and enhanced chain mobility associated with photoisomerization‐driven network rearrangement. Upon prolonged exposure, the subsequent increase in G′ suggests reduced segmental mobility due to dehydration‐induced stiffening, which kinetically stabilizes the deformed state. The gradual recovery of both G′ and relaxation dynamics upon rehydration correlates with the delayed shape recovery, confirming that the actuation is governed by time‐dependent viscoelastic relaxation.

Consistent with this rehydration‐mediated recovery mechanism, the equilibrium water uptake was strongly dependent on relative humidity, with higher RH increasing both the water‐uptake rate and the equilibrium water content (Figure S15). Consequently, the rate of post‐irradiation shape recovery could be temporally regulated by the ambient humidity. In particular, low‐RH conditions slowed water uptake and prolonged the post‐irradiation convex‐bent state (>1300s), thereby extending the shape‐retention period relevant to gripping. These results further demonstrate that environmental moisture availability directly governs the kinetics of rehydration and, consequently, the duration of the metastable actuated state.

To distinguish the respective contributions of photoisomerization and dehydration and to validate the proposed actuation mechanism, a series of mechanistic control experiments was conducted. First, thermal drying alone did not reproduce the UV‐induced nanoporous morphology and the curvature (Figure S16), indicating that dehydration by itself is insufficient to induce the structural transformation. We then examined four control samples, bare PAAP, neat azo linker, an azo‐free ionic gel, and a glycerol‐containing gel (Figure S17), to further isolate the roles of azobenzene photoisomerization and reversible water loss. The azo‐free gel was designed to isolate the effect of ionic crosslinking in the absence of the photoresponsive azobenzene unit, whereas the glycerol‐containing gel was used to suppress evaporation‐driven water loss during UV exposure, thereby excluding the contribution of reversible dehydration and rehydration to the actuation cycle. Under UV irradiation, bare PAAP, the neat azo linker, and the azo‐free ionic gel exhibited negligible deformation or only unidirectional bending toward the UV source, without the delayed curvature reversal observed after UV removal in the azo gel. Notably, the glycerol‐containing gel exhibited initial UV‐induced bending but did not show delayed reverse bending after UV removal, further demonstrating that photoisomerization can initiate network rearrangement and the initial deformation, but cannot alone account for the full bidirectional actuation. Overall, these findings highlight the synergistic interplay among azobenzene photochemistry, dehydration‐rehydration dynamics, nanoporous restructuring, and polymer‐network mechanics in governing the self‐regulated bidirectional motion of the solid‐state actuator.

Engineering in Molecular Chemistry for Optimizing Actuation Performance

To systematically establish the design principle of the polymer host, we synthesized a series of cationic polymers with different side‐chain lengths: poly([(3‐acryloyloxypropyl)trimethylammonium][Cl]) (PAOP), poly([(3‐acryloyloxybutyl)trimethyl‐ammonium][Cl]) (PAOB), poly([(3‐acryloyloxyhexyl)trimethylammonium][Cl]) (PAOH), and poly([(3‐acryloyloxyoctyl)trimethylammonium][Cl]) (PAOO), containing 3, 4, 6, and 8 carbon atoms in the side chain, respectively (Figure 3a, S18–S20, and Note S2). To decouple the effects of side‐chain length on UV‐induced network perturbation and subsequent relaxation dynamics from variations in T gs, viscoelastic properties were measured at 120°C (Figure 3b). Under these conditions, all samples exhibited a qualitatively similar response, G′ decreased upon UV irradiation and recovered after the light was turned off. This reversible behavior indicates that the observed modulus change mainly originates from photoisomerization‐induced perturbation of the ionically associated polymer network. Polymers with shorter side chains (i.e., PAOP) exhibited both a larger decrease in G′ under UV and a significantly slower recovery after UV removal. For instance, PAOP showed a recovery time of 130 ± 25.1 s, whereas PAOO recovered rapidly within 18 ± 6.8 s. Similarly, the G′ 0/G′ UV ratio was significantly higher for PAOP (3.23 ± 0.20; n = 6), indicating pronounced network perturbation, while PAOO showed a near‐unity ratio (1.22 ± 0.17; n = 6), suggesting minimal viscoelastic disruption. These results indicate that shorter side chains enhance UV‐induced network perturbation and slow relaxation, leading to a more persistent nonequilibrium state, whereas longer side chains facilitate rapid recovery due to increased segmental mobility.

We next investigated how the density of ionic anchoring sites in the azobenzene linker influences actuation performance by comparing three linkers containing one, two, and three sulfonate groups, 4‐[4‐(Dimethylamino)phenylazo]benzenesulfonic acid sodium salt (1‐azo, 2‐azo, and 3‐Hydroxy‐4‐[(4‐sulfonatonaphthalen‐1‐yl)diazenyl]naphthalene‐2,7‐disulfonate trisodium salt (3‐azo), respectively (Figure 3d). The 1‐azo contains only a single ionic binding site and therefore cannot form sufficient multivalent ionic associations with the cationic polymer. The limited ionic interaction reduces the thermodynamic stabilization of the mixed state, leading to poor miscibility and macrophase separation (Figure 3e). In contrast, both 2‐azo and 3‐azo enabled a more uniform ionic network because of their multivalent anchoring capabilities. However, the 3‐azo generated an overly constrained network; its high anchoring density restricted the hydration of the gel relative to the 2‐azo‐based system. Consistently, the tan δ overshoot appeared at a lower temperature in the 3‐azo system, indicating that dehydration‐induced interchain interactions arise at an earlier stage (Figure 3f). This suggests that the network undergoes dehydration before sufficient azobenzene photoisomerization‐driven rearrangement can be established. As a result, the system becomes kinetically constrained prior to forming a reconfigurable network state, limiting its ability to generate a morphology that can support subsequent rehydration‐driven deformation. Consequently, the network lacks the dynamic segmental mobility and structural adaptability required for hydration‐driven Janus actuation, preventing the efficient translation of molecular photoisomerization into macroscopic bending (Figures 3g and S21). These results indicate that insufficient anchoring units lead to unstable network formation, whereas excessive anchoring suppresses the dynamic reconfiguration necessary for actuation.

Lastly, we evaluated the effects of azobenzene content and UV intensity on actuation efficiency and operational stability (Figure 3h). When the concentration of azobenzene linkers exceeded a critical threshold (∼30 mol%), phase separation occurred, indicating that excessive crosslinking disrupted network homogeneity. Within the stable compositional range, increasing the azobenzene content enhanced both concave and convex curvature, consistent with more extensive photoisomerization‐induced network reconfiguration (Figure 3i). UV intensity also played a crucial role. Higher UV intensity promoted more extensive photoisomerization, resulting in larger deformation and increased bending curvature (Figure 3j). Under optimized conditions (∼200 mW·cm−2), the system exhibited stable actuation over repeated cycles without noticeable loss of amplitude (Figure S22). Furthermore, the actuator consistently showed the same curvature behavior regardless of the direction of UV irradiation, indicating that the actuation mechanism is governed by intrinsic material responses rather than the light incident direction (Figure S23).

Electrification‐Induced Self‐sensing and Demonstration of Bio‐Inspired Ionograsper

Intriguingly, the ionic actuator enables zero‐power self‐sensing by harnessing free ions that are naturally produced through electrostatic coupling between the anionic azo linker and the cationic polymer. Although these free ions are not intentionally introduced, their unrestricted mobility facilitates an autonomous response to environmental changes, such as the proximity or movement of external objects. As illustrated in Figure 4a, the contact electrification mechanism is governed by ion redistribution. For example, when an object approaches the ionograsper (Figure 4ai), internal mobile ions redistribute to balance the external field (Figure 4aii), forming an EDL. This ionic polarization induces electron flow within the electrode to compensate, generating an electrochemical potential (Figure 4aiii). Upon the withdrawal of the object, ion reorientation restores equilibrium, resulting in a measurable voltage (Figure 4aiv).

To interpret the non‐contact sensing response, we adopted a distance‐dependent effective‐capacitance framework adapted from a previously reported soft artificial electroreceptor model [40]. In this framework, the electrostatic interaction between the charged object and ionic actuator is described using a distance‐dependent effective parameter that reflects the coupled contributions of object proximity and ionic relaxation. The detailed derivation and assumptions of this phenomenological framework are provided in Note S3. Moreover, finite element method (FEM) simulations validated a progressive increase in local charge density on the ionic actuator in response to object proximity (Figure 4b). The simulations further highlighted the crucial role of capacitance variations, as shorter distances facilitated a more intimate electrostatic interaction between the charged object and the actuator surface, promoting effective contact electrification and enhanced voltage generation. This relationship was further corroborated by Voc measurements at different object‐to‐sensor distances, which showed a systematic increase in signal amplitude as the charged object approached the ionic actuator (Figure 4c). These results confirm that the sensing response remains clearly distance‐dependent even within the short‐distance range. Notably, despite variations in oscillation frequency, the Voc signal remained consistent, further supporting that the sensing response is determined primarily by object proximity (Figure 4d).

To further assess the practical applicability of this electrostatic sensing mechanism, we examined the response of the azo gel sensor to various commonly encountered materials, including polyester, silicone, acrylic, polystyrene, and rubber (Figure S24). These materials readily acquire surface charges through routine contact electrification, and all generated measurable Voc signals upon approach to the ionic actuator, although the signal magnitude varied depending on the material. This variation is attributed to differences in triboelectric polarity [41] and the resulting surface charge density under the specific experimental conditions.

In addition to the electrostatic properties of the approaching object, the sensing response is also influenced by the mobile‐ion availability within the gel. Accordingly, the 2‐azo content was systematically varied to modulate the amount of mobile charge within the gel. Increasing the 2‐azo concentration from 0 to 20 mol% increased the measured induced charge from approximately 0.20 ± 0.05 nC to 4.5 ± 1.0 nC, accompanied by an increase in Voc from 0.39 to 5.6 V (Figure S25). The azo‐free sample exhibited a relatively weak sensing response, whereas the incorporation of 1 mol% 2‐azo increased the induced charge to ∼1 nC and produced a readily measurable Voc of approximately 2 V. Further increases in 2‐azo content increased both the induced charge and Voc , demonstrating that mobile‐ion availability within the gel strongly affects the sensing output.

In addition to its sensing function, the excellent solution processability of the azo gel enables large‐area, on‐demand fabrication of custom architectures (Figure S26). To demonstrate practical viability, a fully functional ionograsper was fabricated using a solution‐casting method (Figure S27). This ionograsper autonomously detected external objects by monitoring Voc fluctuations, allowing for the energy‐efficient execution of a task sequence: recognition, grasping, and releasing (Figure 4e,f). For instance, as an object approached the ionograsper (approach phase), the intimate electrostatic interaction triggered a local redistribution of mobile ions to maintain charge neutrality against the external field. This ion migration, which resulted in the formation of a capacitive EDL, induced an electron flow within the electrode to compensate for the potential shift, generating a negative Voc . During the recognition phase (static contact), the EDL stabilized, and the Voc reached a steady‐state plateau. Upon object withdrawal (escape phase), ionic reorientation to restore equilibrium triggered further charge redistribution, yielding a positive Voc signal (Figure 4e). Following detection, a brief UV pulse initiated the grasping motion; by drawing the ionograsper into closer proximity, this movement enhanced the contact‐induced EDL capacity, which consequently amplified the negative Voc (Figure 4f).

Inspired by the morphologically adaptive Venus flytrap, we designed a biomimetic soft ionograsper capable of ultra‐low power task performance (Figure 4g and Movie S3). Upon prey recognition, the ionograsper executed grasping in response to a single UV stimulus and maintained its closed configuration for an extended period without additional energy input, requiring only ∼35.3 mW cm−2 for actuation. The object was eventually released through passive relaxation driven by slow polymer network rearrangement. This behavior is driven by UV‐induced azobenzene isomerization and subsequent structural locking of the polymer network, which affords a self‐sustained post‐stimulus holding state. This self‐regulating, energy‐minimal actuation offers a compelling strategy for adaptive, untethered soft robotics, particularly for applications where a continuous power supply is limited.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a solid‐state ionic actuator in which sensing, actuation, and state retention are intrinsically integrated within a single material platform. A single low‐power UV stimulus is sufficient to induce deformation that persists for over 10 min without additional input, addressing a central limitation of conventional soft actuators that require continuous power or fluidic architectures. The key finding is that the observed bidirectional actuation does not arise from azobenzene photoisomerization alone, but from a coupled sequence of network rearrangement, dehydration‐induced structural fixation, and asymmetric rehydration. This mechanism establishes a transient, non‐equilibrium state that enables delayed, self‐sustained deformation and gradual recovery, effectively decoupling actuation from continuous energy supply. In parallel, mobile ions within the ionically crosslinked network generate voltage signals through electrification‐driven redistribution, allowing self‐powered proximity sensing that is directly linked to mechanical response. By combining these processes, the ionograsper exemplifies a material‐level feedback system capable of autonomous detection and subsequent grasping and release with minimal external intervention. This work highlights how the interplay between polymer network design, ionic transport, and hydration dynamics can be leveraged to encode functionality typically handled by complex system architectures. While conventional systems typically require continuous energy input and complex encapsulated architectures, our system operates with only a brief UV stimulus without such structural complexity, and the coupling of internal physicochemical gradients to mechanical output provides a broadly applicable design strategy. Overall, this study provides a materials‐design foundation for energy‐efficient soft robotic systems that operate with a brief light stimulus and exhibit self‐sustained shape retention. The coupling of internal physicochemical gradients with mechanical output offers a broadly applicable strategy for developing multifunctional ionotronic devices and future closed‐loop soft robotic systems.

Methods

Materials

The (3‐Acryloamidopropyl)trimethylammonium chloride solution ([AAHA][Cl]) (448281), 2,2′‐azobis(2‐methylpropionamidine) dihydrochloride (V‐50, 440914), 4‐[4‐(Dimethylamino)phenylazo]benzenesulfonic acid sodium salt (1‐azo, 114510), 4‐Amino‐1,1′‐azobenzene‐3,4′‐disulfonic acid sodium salt (2‐azo, A9536), 3‐Chloro‐1‐propanol (C46403), ‐Chloro‐1‐butanol (278823), 6‐Chloro‐1‐hexanol (C45008), 8‐Chloro‐1‐octanol (415693), Trimethylamine (243205), potassium carbonate (209619), acetonitrile (34851), acryloyl chloride (549797), 3‐Hydroxy‐4‐[(4‐sulfonatonaphthalen‐1‐yl)diazenyl]naphthalene‐2,7‐disulfonate trisodium salt (3‐azo, A1016), glycerol (G7893), and 1,3‐Propanedisulfonic acid disodium salt (P8579) were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich. Deionized (DI) water (HPLC) was purchased from J.T.Baker (New Jersey, USA).

Synthesis and Characterization of Modeled System

The Azo gel used in this work consists of high Mw poly([(3‐acryloamidopropyl)trimethylammonium][chloride]) (PAAP) as the polymer network (Figure S1) and 4‐amino‐1,1′‐azobenzene‐3,4′‐disulfonic acid sodium salt (2‐azo) as the azo‐crosslinker. The properties of polymer‐based hydrogels are influenced by several factors, such as molecular size, pore size, crosslinking density, and chemical composition. In this study, we specifically designed the azo‐actuator to verify the photothermal activation effect of the azo gel. To exclude the role of the azo‐linker, we maintained a consistent molecular weight for homopolymers (Figure S1c) and ensured uniform chain network density using a simple one‐pot synthesis process (Figure S1a,b).

Characterizations

The 1H, 13C NMR spectra were recorded on an Avance III HD500 instrument using D2O (99.9%, Sigma–Aldrich) as a solvent. The number‐average molecular weight (M n) and polydispersity (Đ) of the used polymer were characterized by size exclusion chromatography (SEC, LC‐4500, JASCO) with a refractive index detector (RI‐4030, JASCO) calibrated with standard poly(ethylene oxide) (calibration kit, Scientific Polymer Products, Inc.). The Fourier‐transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was evaluated using the attenuated total reflection (ATR) technique (Spectrum 100, PerkinElmer). The changes in ionic conductivity were evaluated using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS, IM6, Zahner) at a sinusoidal AC voltage with an amplitude of 50 mV over the frequency range of 10−1–106 Hz. The T g values of the prepared ionoconductors were measured by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, DSC 4000, PerkinElmer) under N2 atmosphere. The sample (typical weight ∼6.0 mg) was first heated to 150°C and held at this temperature for 20 min to eliminate its thermal history, followed by quenching at −30°C. The accumulated charge was measured using an electrometer (6514, Keithley). Fluorescence microscopy images were acquired using an Eclipse Ti‐U microscope (Nikon) with a 10× objective lens. The DSC thermograms were recorded during a second heating stage at a rate of 10°C min‒1. The viscoelastic characterization of azo gel was characterized by an oscillatory rheometer (MCR‐92, Anton Paar) with 8 mm parallel plates. The sample gap was fixed at ∼1 mm, and the strain was set to a small strain amplitude (1%) for frequency‐dependent measurements. A UV–vis spectrometer (V‐730, JASCO) was used to demonstrate the high transparency of the ionoconductors, with a scan range and rate of 200∼600 and 100 nm min‒1, respectively. The surface and cross‐sectional optical images of azo gel were analyzed using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, Zeiss, Oberkochen). Data were acquired at 10 kV. The photothermal images of azo gel were captured using a thermal‐imaging camera (MobIR Air, ZIYOUHU). The theoretically generated voltage analysis was computed by the FEM simulation using COMSOL Multiphysics.

Synthesis of Copolymer

A pre‐polymerization mixture containing [AAHA][Cl] monomer (52.82 g, 255.54 mmol) and V‐50 initiator (1.00 mg, 0.0037 mmol) was placed in a one‐neck flask. After purging with Ar gas at room temperature for 1 h, free radical polymerization was conducted at 70°C for 3 h in an oil bath, followed by quenching with liquid nitrogen. The resulting solution was precipitated by pouring it into excess acetone, and the collected copolymer was dried under vacuum at 60°C. Other polymers were synthesized using similar procedures.

Preparation of Patterned PDMS Substrate

Transparent and stretchable PDMS substrates were prepared by mixing elastomeric PDMS and curing agent at a 10:1 mass ratio. After degassing, the mixture was poured into a circular mold to form a substrate. The mold was cured at 60°C for over an hour, resulting in a PDMS substrate with a thickness of ∼200 µm. The cured substrate was cut from the mold and engraved with a pre‐designed pattern using a CO2 laser cutter (xTool, P2). The surface was then chemically modified by oxygen plasma treatment (FEMTO SCIENCE, CUTE) before use.

Fabrication of Azo Gel

The azo gel was prepared by mixing PAAP homopolymer with a predetermined amount of 2‐azo linker using deionized water as a co‐solvent. The mixture was stirred for 12 h at 80°C. The homogenized solution was poured onto a plasma‐treated rectangular PDMS substrate (thickness: 200 µm, width: 10 mm, length: 30 mm). After fully drying the solution under vacuum, transparent azo gel films (∼30 µm) were obtained. To verify the sensing capability of the azo gel, a Cu tape‐attached PDMS substrate was used, and changes in Voc were monitored at various distances from a charged subject using a source meter (2420, Keithley, USA).
