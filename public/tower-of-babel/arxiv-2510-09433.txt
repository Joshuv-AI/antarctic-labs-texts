Clustering Deposit and Withdrawal Activity in Tornado Cash: A Cross-Chain Analysis
Raffaele Cristodaro, Benjamin Kraner, Claudio J. Tessone

Abstract
Tornado Cash is a decentralised mixer that uses cryptographic techniques to sever the on-chain trail between depositors and withdrawers. In practice, however, its anonymity can be undermined by user behaviour and operational quirks. We conduct the first cross-chain empirical study of Tornado Cash activity on Ethereum, BNB Smart Chain, and Polygon, introducing three clustering heuristics—(i) address-reuse, (ii) transactional-linkage, and (iii) a novel first-in–first-out (FIFO) temporal-matching rule. Together, these heuristics reconnect deposits to withdrawals and deanonymise a substantial share of recipients. Our analysis shows that 5.1 – 12.6% of withdrawals can already be traced to their originating deposits through address reuse and transactional linkage heuristics. Adding our novel First-In–First-Out (FIFO) temporal-matching heuristic lifts the linkage rate by a further 15 – 22 percentage points. Statistical tests confirm that these FIFO matches are highly unlikely to occur by chance. Comparable leakage across Ethereum, BNB Smart Chain, and Polygon indicates chain-agnostic user misbehaviour, rather than chain-specific protocol flaws. These results expose how quickly cryptographic guarantees can unravel in everyday use, underscoring the need for both disciplined user behaviour and privacy-aware protocol design. In total, our heuristics link over $2.3 billion in Tornado Cash withdrawals to identifiable deposits, exposing significant cracks in practical anonymity.



I
Introduction

Blockchain technology has changed how individuals exchange value, introducing systems that are transparent, decentralised, and trustless. However, the inherent transparency of public blockchains also introduces significant privacy concerns. Every transaction is recorded permanently and openly, allowing observers to trace and link activities back to specific addresses and, potentially, real-world identities.

To address these concerns, privacy-enhancing protocols such as Tornado Cash have been developed. Tornado Cash operates as a non-custodial mixer that uses smart contracts to break the on-chain link between deposit and withdrawal addresses. By pooling funds of equal denominations, it allows users to withdraw assets to new addresses without directly exposing the origin of the funds, namely the deposit transaction.

Despite its technical sophistication, Tornado Cash and other mixers more broadly remain vulnerable to various de-anonymization techniques. Researchers and analysts have demonstrated that patterns in transaction timing, amounts, and operational behaviour can partially re-link supposedly anonymized deposits and withdrawals.

II-A

How Tornado Cash Works

Tornado Cash, is a decentralized, non-custodial privacy solution implemented as a set of autonomous smart contracts deployed on public blockchains. It enables users to break the on-chain link between deposit and withdrawal addresses through a cryptographic mixing process.

At its core, Tornado Cash allows users to deposit fixed amounts of native coins (such as 0.1, 1, 10, or 100 ETH) or ERC-20 tokens (such as 100 or 1’000 USDC) into a shared liquidity pool. Before making a deposit, a user generates a random secret off-chain and computes a cryptographic commitment based on it using a Pedersen hash function

The smart contract records the commitment by inserting it as a leaf into a Merkle tree that maintains a history of all deposits. Each deposit is associated with a unique commitment to prevent accidental reuse and potential linkage. Once the commitment is inserted, the user’s secret remains known only to them.

To withdraw funds, the user must prove ownership of a valid commitment without revealing which one it is. This is done by generating a zero-knowledge proof (zk-SNARK) off-chain and submitting it to the smart contract. The proof demonstrates that the user knows a valid commitment included in the Merkle tree without exposing any private information. Upon successful verification, the contract allows the user to withdraw the funds to a different address, breaking the observable connection between deposit and withdrawal.

To further enhance user privacy, Tornado Cash supports the use of relayers third-party actors who submit withdrawal transactions on behalf of users. Without a relayer, a user withdrawing funds would need to pay gas fees directly from their own address, preventing to withdrawal funds from an address never used before (not present on-chain).

By using a relayer, the user can generate a zero-knowledge proof and send it privately (off-chain) to a trusted relayer. The relayer then submits the withdrawal transaction, pays the necessary gas fees, and receives a fee set by the user that is part of the withdrawal parameters. Importantly, the relayer does not learn anything about the user’s original deposit beyond what is publicly available on-chain. Moreover, by using a relayer, the user avoids exposing gas fee payment patterns that could otherwise be analyzed to link deposit and withdrawal addresses when fees are paid directly.

The use of relayers breaks any direct link between the withdrawal address and the original depositor’s addresses, adding a critical layer of operational security. Nonetheless, users must carefully select relayers they trust, as malicious or poorly managed relayers could censor transactions or set a different address as receiver.

The fixed denomination structure is critical to maintaining anonymity, as it prevents observers from linking deposits and withdrawals based on transaction amounts.

The protocol’s security and anonymity guarantees rely heavily on user behavior though. Best practices such as delaying withdrawals, avoiding address reuse, and maintaining operational security are essential to avoid introducing patterns that could weaken anonymity.

Throughout this paper, we focus specifically on Tornado Cash’s operation on Ethereum, Polygon, and BNB Smart Chain, where the core mechanisms remain consistent despite differences in network parameters.

IV
Methodology

Our analysis focuses on clustering addresses and linking Tornado Cash deposits and withdrawals using three heuristics. The approach was applied independently to data from Ethereum, Polygon, and Binance Smart Chain.

IV-A

Dataset Preparation

We collected all deposit and withdrawal transactions involving Tornado Cash smart contracts from their deployment until August 2024. Additionally, we extracted all transactions involving native coins (ETH, MATIC, BNB) and the ERC-20 tokens USDT and WETH for addresses that interacted with Tornado Cash. USDT and WETH were selected because they are present across all examined blockchains and are widely used.

Each transaction was annotated with:

•

Blockchain and pool denomination

•

Transaction type (deposit or withdrawal)

•

Block number and timestamp

IV-B

Heuristic 1: Address Reuse

The first heuristic is based on the reuse of deposit addresses. If an address that was used to deposit into Tornado Cash is later observed making a withdrawal, it strongly suggests a linkage between deposit and withdrawal activities.

This heuristic identifies careless users who failed to use a new or different address for their withdrawal.

IV-C

Heuristic 2: Transactional Linkage Analysis

The second heuristic investigates direct transactions involving native coins, USDT, or WETH between addresses that performed deposits and addresses that performed withdrawals. If any such transaction was detected, a linkage between the deposit and withdrawal addresses was inferred.

To assess whether these linkages could have occurred randomly, we performed a null hypothesis test.

•

For each chain, we selected a random sample of addresses equal to the number of Tornado Cash interacting addresses (excluding addresses found through Heuristic 2).

•

For each random sample, we computed the number of transactions between the sampled addresses.

•

This process was repeated 150 times to establish a distribution of random connection counts.

In this study, null hypothesis analysis was used to test whether the number of transactions observed between addresses that made Tornado Cash deposits and withdrawals could be explained by random chance. By repeatedly selecting random sets of addresses and measuring the number of transactions between them, we established a baseline for random behaviour. Comparing this baseline to the actual observed linkages allowed us to assess whether the connections found were statistically significant rather than coincidental.

Null Hypothesis Formalization

Formally, the null hypothesis
H_{0}
states that the number of observed transaction links between deposit and withdrawal addresses identified through Heuristic 2 is consistent with the number of links expected under random chance.

Let:

= number of transactions observed between deposit and withdrawal addresses based on Heuristic 2

•

X_{\text{rand}}
= random variable representing the number of transactions between randomly selected addresses

Then the null hypothesis can be expressed as:

H_{0}:\quad X_{\text{H2}}\sim X_{\text{rand}}

where
\sim
denotes that
X_{\text{H2}}
follows the same statistical distribution as
X_{\text{rand}}
.

If the observed
X_{\text{H2}}
significantly exceeds the distribution of
X_{\text{rand}}
, the null hypothesis can be rejected, indicating that the links found through Heuristic 2 are unlikely to have occurred by random chance. The minimum, maximum, median, and mean number of random connections were recorded. These results allowed us to assess whether the observed number of links in the real Tornado Cash data significantly exceeded what would be expected by random chance.

IV-D

Heuristic 3: FIFO Temporal Matching

The third heuristic applies a First-In-First-Out (FIFO) matching strategy. After excluding all addresses identified through Heuristics 1 and 2, we attempted to link deposit addresses to withdrawal addresses based on block proximity.

Specifically, we match each deposit with the earliest withdrawal that occurred in the immediately following blocks.

This heuristic is based on the assumption that users may prefer fast withdrawals after deposits to minimize their exposure time on-chain. Although less confident than Heuristics 1 and 2, FIFO matching provides additional linkages that are otherwise difficult to detect.

IV-E

Heuristic Combination

The results of the three heuristics were combined to build clusters of addresses:

•

The results of Heuristics 1 and 2 were prioritised due to their higher confidence.

•

Heuristic 3 results were incorporated cautiously, recognising the weaker confidence of the FIFO temporal assumptions.

IV-F

Cross-Chain Considerations

Tornado Cash operates, among others, across Ethereum, Polygon, and BNB Smart Chain. In this study, the same heuristics were applied uniformly across all three networks without modifications. Differences in block times, gas fee structures, or token contract standards were not relevant to the heuristics developed, as the analysis was based solely on address reuse, the existence of transactions between addresses, and block number ordering. As a result, no chain-specific adjustments were necessary and the cross-chain analysis was conducted under consistent methodological assumptions.

V-B

Probability Density Function (PDF)

To analyze the distribution of temporal delays between deposits and withdrawals, we compute the probability density function (PDF) of the observed values, such as block differences and queue sizes. Unlike raw frequency counts, a probability density provides a normalized measure of likelihood over a continuous variable. Formally, the PDF describes the relative likelihood of observing a particular value within a small interval, scaled such that the area under the entire distribution integrates to one. This normalization enables meaningful comparisons across pools and blockchains, even when the number of observations differs. In practice, we discretize the observed values into logarithmically spaced bins and normalize the histogram by the total number of observations and the bin width, yielding a density estimate. The resulting distribution highlights the statistical properties of the underlying process—for instance, whether withdrawal delays are concentrated or exhibit heavy-tailed behavior.

The results of our analysis reveal several important insights into the practical effectiveness of Tornado Cash as a privacy tool across different blockchain networks. Despite its cryptographic soundness, Tornado Cash’s anonymity guarantees are compromised in practice by user behaviour.

First, address reuse (Heuristic 1) remains a significant vulnerability. A significant number of users-especially on Ethereum-were observed withdrawing to addresses that previously initiated deposits. These operational oversights directly weaken the anonymity set and demonstrate a widespread misunderstanding or oversight of best practices in using privacy tools. Notably, the higher match rates observed for Ethereum may be due to the network’s maturity, broader user base, and increased historical usage relative to BSC and Polygon.

Second, transactional linkages (Heuristic 2) between depositors and withdrawers indicate a recurring pattern of identifiable fund transfers, further weakening privacy. Our null hypothesis testing confirms (table
V
) that these observed interactions exceed what would be expected under random conditions, pointing to intentional or negligent actions that make tracing possible. Interestingly, Polygon showed a relatively higher number of matches using Heuristic 2, possibly due to its lower transaction costs encouraging faster fund recycling.

VII
Conclusion

This study provides a cross-chain analysis of Tornado Cash, highlighting how real-world usage patterns can erode the privacy assurances of decentralized mixers. By implementing three on-chain heuristics: address reuse, transactional linkage, and FIFO-based temporal matching, we identify significant linkability between deposits and withdrawals across Ethereum, BSC, and Polygon.

The results demonstrate that effective anonymity remains elusive without careful operational hygiene. While Tornado Cash’s cryptographic design is robust, its guarantees are weakened by common user behaviours, making partial traceability feasible through blockchain analysis alone.

Variations in match rates by denomination and chain further suggest that factors such as network cost and adoption scale can materially influence the risk of de-anonymisation.

Future research may explore advanced clustering methods, such as machine learning models incorporating transaction graph embeddings, as well as the integration of off-chain metadata where appropriate. Importantly, developers of privacy tools should prioritize user education, enforce operational best practices through interface design, and consider enhancements such as withdrawal delay enforcement or dynamic anonymity set estimation.

Ultimately, our work contributes to the ongoing dialogue on privacy in decentralized systems. It highlights the importance of holistic approaches that combine cryptographic rigour, system design, and behavioural awareness to protect user confidentiality in an increasingly monitored blockchain landscape.
