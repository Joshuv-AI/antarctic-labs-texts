Radiation exposure to deep brain stimulation leads during multiple cranial radiation courses: illustrative case
Authors: Adel Azghadi, Kyle O’Carroll, Haley K. Perlow, Michael D. Staudt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDDeep brain stimulation (DBS) is an established treatment for Parkinson’s disease (PD). Concurrently, brain tumors are increasing in incidence, creating complex clinical scenarios when both conditions coexist. The safety of delivering radiation therapy to patients with active DBS devices remains poorly documented, with limited guidelines available.OBSERVATIONSThis case report describes a 69-year-old male PD patient with bilateral DBS leads who developed metastatic melanoma with brain metastases. He underwent multiple radiation treatments, including stereotactic radiosurgery and hippocampal avoidance whole-brain radiation therapy, receiving a cumulative dose exceeding 47 Gy to the DBS device. Throughout treatment, the DBS device remained fully functional with no impedance changes or symptom deterioration. A review of similar cases indicates that radiation doses to DBS components, including leads and implantable pulse generators, are generally well tolerated, although no standardized safety protocols currently exist.LESSONSThis case reinforces the feasibility and safety of multiple radiation treatments in patients with implanted DBS devices when carefully planned and monitored. Cumulative high radiation doses may not impair DBS functionality, but attention to neutron contamination and dose distribution remains essential. Multidisciplinary collaboration and individualized planning are crucial to optimize tumor control while preserving device integrity in this emerging patient population. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25600

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is a widely used surgical treatment for managing motor symptoms of Parkinson’s disease (PD), including tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia, particularly in patients for whom medication has become less effective over time.1,2 Globally, more than 250,000 DBS devices have been implanted to date.3 In parallel, the global incidence of brain and CNS tumors continues to rise, with approximately 322,000 new cases reported in 2022. This number is projected to reach 474,000 by 2045 if current trends continue, driven by scientific advancements and demographic shifts.4

The coexistence of DBS systems and brain tumors presents a rare and complex clinical scenario that requires coordinated care among the neuro-oncology, neurosurgery, and radiation oncology teams. However, there is limited published evidence on the safety and feasibility of delivering radiation therapy to patients with active DBS devices. This case report adds to the growing body of literature exploring the use of radiotherapy in such patients. In addition, we review the current literature on the subject. Given the lack of clear safety guidelines, documenting clinical experiences and outcomes in these cases is essential to inform future practice.

Illustrative Case

This is the case of a 69-year-old male with a past medical history significant for PD, who underwent implantation of bilateral DBS leads (Abbott) targeting the subthalamic nuclei (STN) in August 2024. The bilateral leads (St. Jude Medical Infinity 6172) and implantable pulse generator (IPG; Liberta RC) were placed in a single-stage procedure. He also had a prior diagnosis of scalp melanoma in 2022. Following an initial biopsy that indicated subcutaneous extension of the melanoma, he underwent wide local excision and lymph node biopsy in November 2022. Postsurgical pathology showed no nodal involvement.

In November 2024, he presented to the emergency department with headaches, blurred peripheral vision, and a sensation of pressure behind his eyes. CT imaging and MRI revealed a 3-cm left occipital hematoma, raising suspicion for underlying metastatic melanoma (Fig. 1A). Further systemic imaging identified lesions in the lungs and liver. In December 2024, systemic therapy was initiated with nivolumab and relatlimab. For treatment of the brain lesion, he initially underwent stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) to the left occipital tumor, with a dose of 30 Gy delivered in 5 fractions. He presented 1 month later with an intratumoral hemorrhage (Fig. 1B), causing significant mass effect and a tentorial subdural hematoma, which required a left occipital craniotomy for resection. A few months later, he again presented with altered mental status and was found to have multiple new metastatic brain lesions. Based on a multidisciplinary tumor board discussion, additional SRS was recommended.

The treatment plan in February 2025 included delivering 24 Gy in 3 fractionated doses of 8 Gy each to multiple metastatic tumors located in the right frontal cortex (×2), left frontal cortex (×1), left parietal cortex (×2), and left temporal cortex (×1). The largest lesion, located in the right frontal cortex and measuring 17 mm × 9 mm in anteroposterior and lateral dimensions, was situated directly adjacent to the DBS lead and required special attention (Fig. 1C and D).

Follow-up MRI in May 2025 revealed three new brain metastases, although the patient remained neurologically asymptomatic. Treatment with hippocampal avoidance whole-brain radiation therapy (HA-WBRT), delivering 30 Gy in 10 fractions, was initiated.

The patient placed his DBS device in MRI mode before each MRI study or surgical procedure. However, the device remained active during chemotherapy and radiation treatments. Abbott device representatives met with him after each study and procedure, and interrogation of the IPG revealed no faults or changes in impedance values. During SRS, interrogations occurred after the full treatment course and not between fractions. His PD symptoms remained well controlled throughout treatment for his intracranial metastatic disease. Discussion

This case report demonstrates the safety of using both SRS and HA-WBRT in patients with metastatic melanoma brain tumors who have implanted DBS systems. It describes one of the highest cumulative doses delivered to a DBS device through multiple treatments, including both SRS and HA-WBRT, using 6FFF and 6X energies with a linear accelerator-based external beam approach. Throughout the radiosurgery sessions and on completion of the treatment course, the DBS devices remained fully functional, with no significant changes in impedance.

Observations

There are currently no standardized guidelines for the use of radiation therapy in patients with implanted DBS hardware. Consequently, the existing literature is limited to case reports describing patients who have safely undergone radiotherapy targeting various anatomical regions, including the brain, head and neck, chest wall, and pelvis (Table 2). Radiation therapy in patients with implanted DBS systems carries a recognized risk to the IPG. This risk is generally lower in the context of cranial irradiation, as the IPG is typically located at a safe distance from the radiation field. Nonetheless, while the potential hazards to the IPG have been acknowledged, the safety considerations and risk of DBS system malfunction (both cranial leads and IPG) in the setting of multimodal cranial radiation therapy have not been previously demonstrated. Furthermore, the short-term follow-up in this case (1 year since the first SRS treatment) does not rule out long-term effects of radiation exposure to neurostimulation devices. The limited evidence in the literature underscores the need for further investigation to better define these risks and to guide clinical decision-making in this patient population. Three cases have reported on radiation exposure to the DBS leads directly. Guy et al. described a case involving a PD patient who developed two frontal lobe metastases from lung cancer.5 The patient received stereotactic radiation therapy totaling 21 Gy in 3 fractions (7 Gy over 1 week). The estimated radiation dose to the DBS electrode was 1 Gy, and to the IPG was < 0.01 Gy. Following radiation therapy, the patient showed neurological improvement with no evidence of DBS system dysfunction.5 Schinwelski et al. described a patient with STN-DBS who developed two brain metastases from prostate cancer and received 20 Gy in 5 fractions, with a maximum estimated dose of 21 Gy to the DBS electrodes.6 Kotecha et al. reported on a patient with DBS for tremor management who underwent WBRT for multiple brain metastases from lung cancer. The patient experienced no adverse effects or DBS system failures.7

Three additional cases primarily involved radiation delivered to the IPG in the chest. Tran et al. described a patient who received locoregional radiation to the left chest wall and regional lymph nodes.8 The IPG was located directly over the treatment site; although relocation was considered, it was ultimately not pursued. The original IPG location was treated with 42.56 Gy to the chest wall and 40 Gy in 16 fractions to the supraclavicular region. Due to the position of the IPG, target coverage in this area was compromised, and the mean dose delivered to the IPG was 27.3 Gy. Following treatment, device impedances remained unchanged.8 Mushonga et al. reported on a PD patient who underwent radiation treatment to the left chest wall and regional lymph nodes, with the IPG located in the right chest wall.9 The device remained active during the course of radiation therapy, which delivered 40.05 Gy in 15 fractions to the target area, while the IPG itself received a dose of 1.56 Gy. No posttreatment malfunctions were reported; however, the device was reprogrammed during treatment.9 A second patient with STN-DBS reported by Schinwelski et al. received 20 Gy in 5 fractions to the left chest wall for recurrent breast cancer, with a maximum estimated dose of 1.7 Gy to the IPG; the DBS function was maintained at the 6-month follow-up.6

Other cases have described DBS patients who received radiation therapy to the head and neck region. Mazdai et al. reported on a patient with a laryngeal tumor who underwent treatment with an STN DBS system in place.10 The patient received radiation to the neck resection cavity, with a total dose of 66 Gy delivered in 33 fractions and an estimated 7.5 Gy reaching the DBS device. At the 3-year follow-up, no malfunction of the DBS system was observed.10 Borkenhagen et al. detailed the case of a PD patient who required definitive external beam radiation therapy for a parotid gland malignancy located near the DBS hardware.11 Using intensity-modulated radiation therapy, they delivered 66 Gy in 33 fractions while minimizing exposure to the DBS components. No DBS system malfunctions occurred during or after treatment.11 Page et al. reported a case involving radiation treatment in a PD patient 3 years after DBS implantation.12 Radiation therapy was indicated following metastasis of a sarcoma to the parotid glands. The patient underwent radiotherapy with the DBS device turned off prior to each fraction. A total dose of 60 Gy in 30 fractions was delivered to the parotid region, with an estimated 2.93 Gy reaching the IPG. Four years after treatment, the patient remained cancer free, with no reported issues related to the IPG or leads.12

Lessons

Special physics considerations for treating patients with implanted electronic devices are guided by the American Association of Physicists in Medicine (AAPM) Task Group 203 (TG-203). While TG-203 does not specifically address DBS systems, it provides recommendations for managing cardiac implanted electronic devices (CIEDs). These include considerations related to energy selection, dose rate, cumulative dose effects on at-risk devices, and other relevant factors.13

While CIED and DBS devices differ in design and function, the AAPM recommendations are widely applied by medical physicists across the United States. To reduce the risk of single-event upsets (SEUs), radiation energies of 10 MV or lower are typically selected to minimize neutron production and exposure to the device. SEUs occur when high linear energy transfer radiation, such as neutrons, interacts with electronic components, causing unintended changes in logic states (e.g., flipping from 0 to 1), which can lead to various device malfunctions, including incorrect pulsing or delayed signaling.

Additionally, higher dose rates have been associated with an increased risk of signal interference in implanted CIEDs, although the clinical significance of these effects is generally low. One study evaluating dose rate effects on CIEDs found that no pacemaker failures occurred at dose rates less than 0.2 Gy/min; however, effects were observed at higher dose rates.14

While monitoring remains essential—given that the existing literature does not directly address DBS systems—the benefit of reduced treatment time, which improves patient comfort, is generally considered to outweigh the relatively low risk of electronic interference related to high dose rates.

The final, and perhaps most important, consideration is the effect of cumulative radiation dose on electronic devices. Repeated exposure over time, particularly across multiple treatment courses, may impact device functionality. Within the device’s semiconductors, radiation therapy can lead to the accumulation of electron holes in insulating materials, potentially resulting in altered performance or permanent damage. Although the IPG was located at a considerable distance from the treatment field in all reviewed cases, concerns remained regarding radiation exposure to cranially mounted pulse generators and to the sensing and stimulating components of the intracranial electrodes. While the team was relatively unconcerned with the effect of dose to leads of our devices, they were contoured for pure evaluation purposes in our treatment planning system (TPS) (Fig. 2). It should be noted that the IPG was located far away from the treatment site and as such was not able to be adequately evaluated due to failures of TPS at large distances beyond field edges.15 The potential for electron backscatter around the relatively high-density, high-Z leads should also be considered at escalated dose as many TPSs cannot accurately model this effect.16 Despite this, in our treatment the perturbation in dose around the relatively small DBS device in comparison with the whole-brain treatment field rendered this consideration to be negligible. Consideration should be taken to ensure additional dose to the pulse generator in the chest is at low enough levels using diodes/thermoluminescent dosimeters during treatment overlying the site, as they are able to accurately measure dose in vivo at distance as well as in the treatment field. Additionally, concerns with neutron contamination of the beam should be considered depending of the proximity of the main pulse generator to the radiation field as well as the chosen energy.

Despite the difference between CIEDs and DBS devices, it might also be useful to note the thresholds provided by AAPM’s TG-203. TG-203 grouped patients into three different classes of increasing risk depending on dose delivered to the device, the likelihood of neutron contamination in the beam, and the patient’s dependence on the cardiac device. The low-risk patients were expected to be device independent, with no in-beam neutron contamination with the device receiving less than 2 Gy. Medium-risk patients were those whose device received 0–5 Gy and who were device dependent but still had no expected neutron contamination. Finally, high-risk patient devices received larger than 5 Gy and the patients were device dependent or there was significant risk of neutron contamination in the beam. These risk groups corresponded to increasing levels of patient supervision, ranging from device examination only before and after treatment in the lowest group, to weekly device interrogation once the device receives greater than 5 Gy in the high-risk group. Additionally, there was a suggestion for increased cardiac support ranging from cardiologist consulting (low risk) to cardiologist availability at treatment (high risk) throughout treatment, among other considerations. While these dose levels and risk categories may not be able to be directly correlated for DBS use, they can be used as a guideline for treatment.

Additionally, various device manufacturers (Abbott, Boston Scientific, Medronic, and LivaNova) have recommendations considering device irradiation. Similarly to the discussion above, the risk factors discussed mainly concern proximity of device to the beam, type and energy of the radiation beam, dose rate of the beam, and the presence or absence of device shielding.17 Many of the systems claim that while radiation may damage the circuitry, no specific radiation testing has been carried out, and due to differences in device- and patient-specific treatment variability, no safe dosage can be specified.17,–,19 While a recommendation was made to provide lead shielding, it should be noted that this is not an effective method to provide neutron shielding.13

The majority of dose delivered to the device was during the WBRT treatment of the patient (Fig. 3). During each radiation treatment, no special attempt was made to avoid dose to the regions of the brain with the device leads, and the device was not turned off during treatment. As such, the SRS plans as well as the HA-WBRT treatment were created using generic institutional guidelines, that is, prioritizing tumor coverage and sparing of healthy tissue as opposed to consideration of DBS device components. Each delivered course of radiation resulted in a maximal dose to 0.03 cm3 (D.03cc) in various regions of the device, depending on the proximity of the target of interest to the components of the DBS device. For the cumulative evaluation of the dose, the device received an excess of 47 Gy in the hottest 0.03cc of the DBS device (47.51 Gy). Dose cumulation for the treatments were created using a direct summation of dose, and contours of the DBS device were created on a per-scan basis to be representative of the actual positioning of the device on the day of the treatment delivery. Because these regions of maximal dose vary in their exact voxel positioning, the cumulative maximum will not be a direct summation of the per-plan DBS maximum dose. Instead, this maximum dose represents the region on the device where the dose overlap of each of the plans was highest for a particular voxel location. On a per-treatment basis, the largest dose delivered to the DBS device (which for every treatment was in the electrodes) was as follows: HA-WBRT delivered a D.03cc of 33.24 Gy, the initial left occipital treatment delivered D.03cc of 7.82 Gy, and the multisite SRS delivered a D.03cc of 16.1 Gy. Respective mean doses to the total device were as follows: 25.19 Gy for summed dose, 20.83 Gy for the HA-WBRT, 1.71 Gy for the left occipital SRS, and 3.5 Gy for the multimetastasis SRS. As the use of DBS expands beyond movement disorders to include a broader range of neurological conditions, the population of patients with implanted DBS systems continues to rise. Simultaneously, advancements in neuro-oncology have led to earlier diagnoses and improved survival rates in brain tumor patients, effectively transforming many cases from acute to chronic conditions. As a result, an increasing number of patients with both brain tumors and DBS implants may require radiotherapy as part of their treatment.

This case report demonstrates the feasibility of delivering multiple rounds of radiation therapy to patients with implanted DBS devices. In this instance, the patient received both SRS using 6FFF energy and HA-WBRT using 6X energy to the brain, resulting in a cumulative dose exceeding 47 Gy to the device, which remained fully functional. This finding contributes to the growing body of literature supporting the safe delivery of radiation therapy in patients with DBS systems. Nevertheless, despite these encouraging outcomes, heightened caution is warranted particularly regarding neutron contamination, which must be thoroughly assessed in any radiation treatment involving electronic implants. In the absence of well-established safety guidelines, such cases require meticulous planning and close multidisciplinary collaboration between neurosurgeons, radiation oncologists, and medical physicists.
