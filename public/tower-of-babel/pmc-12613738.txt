Multidisciplinary treatment for primary cardiac angiosarcoma: a case report and overview of the literature
Authors: Zhenzhong Wang, Shanwen Pang, Muhammad Khalid, Linbin Hua, Junqiang Qiu, Yuxin Li, Yingjie Ke, Junfei Zhao, Songxi Xie, Huanlei Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary cardiac angiosarcoma is rare and highly invasive to myocardial tissue and prone to metastasis. Surgery is an effective treatment, but it is difficult and risky. Postoperative therapy could improve survival rate, but specific regimens are controversial.Case presentationWe herein demonstrated the management experience of a patient with primary cardiac angiosarcoma at 19 weeks of pregnancy, which can be used as a reference for other patients. She underwent surgical R1 resection during pregnancy, but the fetus aborted spontaneously 4 h after surgery. Under the care of our multidisciplinary team, she also received radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy. After that, she was evaluated as stable disease at 14th months after surgery. She developed multiple bone metastases 16th months after surgery and finally died in the 18th month after surgery.ConclusionSurgical resection of cardiac angiosarcoma should remove as much tumor tissue as possible. Chemotherapy and targeted therapy are beneficial in shrinking tumors and control disease progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05256-9.Keywords: Angiosarcoma, Cardiac surgery, Pregnancy, Chemotherapy, Radiotherapy, Targeted therapy

Background

The incidence of primary cardiac tumors is between 0.02–0.45% [1]. Most are benign tumors, with papillary fibroelastoma and myxomas being the most common [2]. The incidence of malignant tumors is approximately 0.8%, with the most common pathological type being angiosarcoma (40.4%), which most frequently occurs in the right atrium (RA) [3]. Primary cardiac angiosarcoma is a high-grade aggressive malignancy originating from the endothelial lining of blood vessel. Treatment primarily involves surgery, with radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy serving as adjuvant measures. The median overall survival (OS) is 12–14 months [4, 5]. We performed a median sternotomy right atrial angiosarcoma resection in a 28-year-old pregnant woman, and the fetus died four hours postoperatively. She received radiotherapy, chemotherapy and targeted therapy and eventually died in the 18th month after surgery.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old woman, gravida two para one, at 19 weeks of gestation presented with shortness of breath, palpitations and edema for 3 days. The NYHA class was class Ⅱ. Before visiting our center, she received pericardiocentesis to drain the bloody fluid because of large pericardial effusion. Pertinent laboratory findings and the fetal ultrasound were normal. Preoperative echocardiography demonstrated a mass of 81 × 48 mm in the right atrium (Supplement). The mass was lobulated and unclearly demarcated from myocardial tissue, and mild tricuspid regurgitation (color flow jet area was 1.6 cm2) was observed. There were no significant abnormalities in atrial and ventricular size or left ventricular ejection fraction. Even though we had fully informed the patient about the fetal loss risks associated with radiation, surgery, and combined postoperative treatment, she still showed a strong willingness to continue the pregnancy. The PET-CT scan showed a mass with rich blood supply in the right atrium, approximately 74 × 66 × 84 mm in size, which was considered to be a primary sarcoma with pericardial and mediastinal lymph node metastasis in the right lower paratracheal lymph node (group 4R) (Fig. 1A). The sarcoma was in a regional stage.

The patient’s condition was rare and complex, so we established a multidisciplinary team (MDT) including cardiac surgeons, medical oncologists, radiation therapists, and obstetricians. No antitumor therapy was needed before surgery. A median sternotomy incision was necessary for the maximum resection of the tumor (Fig. 2A). During the operation, we found pericardial adhesions and selected the pericardial tissue near the tumor for pathological examination. To protect the fetus, we chose the mode of pulsatile flow perfusion, normothermic (> 35℃), and high-flow rate (> 2.5 l/min/m2) perfusion for cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). We found that the upper boundary of the tumor was close to the sinus node and the lower boundary was close to the tricuspid annulus. The tumor involved the right atrial appendage and the anterior and inferior walls of the right atrium. We made an incision along the edge of the tumor in the RA, then removed the tumor tissue visible to the naked eye and most of the right atrium(Fig. 2B). The tumor invaded a wide area of the heart, leaving only a portion of the right atrium posterior wall and other critical areas(Fig. 2C), so an appropriately sized pericardial mesh was selected to reconstruct the right atrial structure. The marginal myocardial tissue and tumor were sent for examination (Fig. 2D and E). After releasing the aortic clamp, the trans-esophageal echocardiography found that the tricuspid annulus was enlarged, resulting in severe regurgitation, so the tricuspid valvuloplasty ring (Edwards 26#) was used for tricuspid valvuloplasty. Finally, the temporary pacing leads were placed on the surface of the heart, and pericardial fenestration was performed. The CPB time and aortic cross-clamping time were 187 min and 53 min, respectively.

No malignant cells were seen in the pericardial tissue, but they were seen in the myocardial margins, so we attained macroscopic complete resection with microscopic involvement (R1) (Fig. 3). The immunohistochemical biomarkers were CD34(+), Ki67 (about 8% positive in hot spots), and ERG (+). After surgery, the mass was sent to next-generation sequencing (NGS). The proportion of malignant tumor cells in the sample, DNA quality assessment, and sequencing quality assessment are all qualified. The test indicated the deletion mutations in KDR and MED12 and microsatellite stability (MSS), with no recommended immunotherapy drugs. Postoperative transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed no obvious mass, with a right atrial diameter of 47 mm and a left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%.

The pathological diagnosis of the tumor was angiosarcoma, and malignant cells were seen in the myocardial margins (R1) (Fig. 3)

The fetus aborted spontaneously 4 h after surgery. The patient was transferred to obstetrics 5 days after cardiac surgery for curettage and discharged half a month later. MDT closely followed the patient and developed a specific treatment regimen, so the patient received radiotherapy and loboplatin chemotherapy one and a half months after surgery. Concurrent lobaplatin (59 mg monthly) and volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT) were used to deliver 66 Gy in 33 fractions. Overall treatment time was 60 days. The patient was given oral warfarin for three months after surgery, and the INR was controlled between 1.8 and 2.5. During the follow-up period, TTE was examined monthly. At the 5th month post-surgery, TTE detected a right-atrial hypoechoic mass measuring 23 × 17 mm. In the same time window, contrast-enhanced cardiac CT measured the lesion at 37 × 28 mm without enhancement, consistent with thrombus (Fig. 1B). The patient did not have other signs of embolism, such as stroke or pulmonary embolism. MDT recommended the patient take warfarin 1.5 mg every day for anticoagulation, and then the thrombus had shrunk to 17 × 15 mm in the 6th month post-surgery. In the 8th month post-surgery, TTE and PET-CT showed soft tissue shadows of 43 × 13 mm and 23 × 11 mm in the left and right atrium, respectively (Fig. 1A, C). The shadows were diagnosed as angiosarcoma recurrence, and it metastasized to two nodules in the anterior mediastinum, the left third rib, and the left humerus (Fig. 1D, E). As a result, patients were treated with a combination of chemotherapy and targeted therapy every 21 days. Chemotherapy was paclitaxel 250 mg and cisplatin 110 mg, and targeted therapy was anlotinib (12 mg oral/day, days 1–14). After 5 circles of treatment, contrast-enhanced cardiac CT demonstrated the masses were reduced and the size was 27 × 17 mm and 15 × 12 mm in left and right atrium, respectively (Fig. 1F). The efficacy of RECIST was evaluated as stable disease (SD). Because creatinine was elevated by 91.5 umol/L (normal range, 41–81 umol/L), cisplatin was not used in the 7th circle. The results of the CT and TTE after 7 circles were consistent with the results after 5 circles. The patient received a total of 7 cycles of chemotherapy, while anlotinib was scheduled to continue. However, the patient underwent VMAT 30 Gy ten times from 16th months postoperatively for distant spinal and pelvic metastases. The patient developed spleen and liver metastases and received chemotherapy once with cyclophosphamide 600 mg and vinorelbine 40 mg. Unfortunately, the patient eventually died of cardiac arrest 18 months after surgery. The clinical data of the perioperative period and follow-up are shown in Table 1.

Discussion and conclusions

Cardiac malignancy during pregnancy is rare, and only a few cases were reported in the past [6–8]. All of these patients underwent cardiac surgery after cesarean section, and some patients did not receive post-operative treatment. In our case, the patient refused to have a miscarriage, so we performed R1 resection for her cardiac angiosarcoma during pregnancy, followed by radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy under the MDT. The OS is 18 months which is longer than the median OS reported in previous studies [4, 5].

The standard treatment for cardiac angiosarcoma is surgery. Surgery should be aimed at achieving a single en bloc specimen and macroscopically complete resection, minimising R1 margins [9]. Because of the positive tumor cells at the resection margin and the metastasis of regional lymph nodes, the MDT decided to perform chemo-radiotherapy on the patient. Progression-free survival (PFS) of chemo-radiotherapy was significantly longer than either surgery or chemotherapy [5]. Postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy can not only improve the local control rate but also decrease the recurrence rate after resectioning of the primary tumors [10]. In the post-operative setting, doses up to 66 Gy are suggested for the soft tissue and visceral sarcomas [9]. We selected the highest dose of 66 Gy for radiotherapy at first. However, the tumor recurred and bone metastases developed six and a half months after chem-oradiotherapy. The shorter PFS in this case is related to many causes. On the one hand, the patient had regional lymph node metastases prior to treatment and the surgical margin was R1. On the other hand, it may be that the chemotherapy drugs are not strong enough. There is no specific chemotherapy recommended for cardiac angiosarcoma, and anthracyclines are a widely accepted first-line treatment in patients with soft tissue sarcoma other than the heart. However, in patients with angiosarcoma, taxanes have similar efficacy compared to anthracyclines, but without the cardiotoxicity of anthracyclines [11]. Therefore, we used paclitaxel for the second chemotherapy. After multiple bone metastases occured, the patient underwent second radiotherapy, but the dose was only 30 Gy. This was mainly because the patient had significant cardiac dysfunction. The final results showed that the benefit of small doses of radiotherapy was not obvious.

Anlotinib is a novel oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) targeting c-kit with notable anti-angiogenesis activity. The efficacy of anlotinib in bone and soft tissue sarcoma has been confirmed in several clinical studies [12], and there have also been case reports of its use in cardiac angiosarcoma to achieve partial remission [13]. After the use of combination therapy, the overall survival of this patient exceeded 14 months, which is longer than the median survival reported previously [14]. Postoperative therapy is associated with better median survival following resection of cardiac sarcoma [15]. Angiogenesis-related genes KDR mutation is common in primary cardiac sarcoma, and recurrent MED12 alterations have also been reported recently, which is consistent with this case [16]. The patient with new distant metastases developed after 2 months of monotherapy with anlotinib, suggesting that anlotinib maybe has little significant effect on the control of metastasis of angiosarcoma.

Our MDT has extensive experience in cardiac surgery during pregnancy [17, 18]. We believe that the management of cardiac tumors during pregnancy should comprehensively consider factors such as gestational weeks, tumor type (benign or malignant), tumor characteristics (size, activity, impact on hemodynamics), intention to preserve the pregnancy, and embolism. The type of tumor is a key factor in determining the prognosis of pregnant women. The early symptoms of malignant tumors are often not obvious. But in the late stage, it has involved a large amount of myocardial tissue or metastasized to other organs, resulting in poor cardiac function, high surgical difficulty, and a mortality rate. Moreover, the fetus cannot tolerate postoperative antitumor treatment, so the abortion should be performed as soon as possible after obtaining the patient’s consent.

Medications and CPB strategies are strongly associated with fetal outcomes. The patient received low-dose diuretics and potassium supplementation before surgery. We suggest that diuretics can adversely affect amniotic fluid volume, so low-dose diuretics are safer for the fetus. Cardiac surgery with CPB is associated with at least a 20% risk of fetal loss [19]. A short CPB time, normothermia, the maintenance of high flow rates (5 l/min) and perfusion pressure(70-75mmHg) are beneficial for placental perfusion [20]. The effect of pulsatile perfusion on placental perfusion is unclear. Based on the results of multiple randomized trials, guidelines suggest that pulsatile perfusion is more consistent with the body’s inherent hemodynamics than non-pulsatile perfusion and has better renal and pulmonary outcomes [21]. Low molecular weight heparin anticoagulation (LWMH, body weight×3 mg for initial dose, target ACT >480 s) was used in cardiopulmonary bypass. LWMH does not enter the placenta. The use of phenylephrine during CPB to increase blood pressure ensures placental perfusion, which is the first choice vasopressors [22]. After opening the aorta, we found that the tricuspid annulus enlarged, causing severe regurgitation. This was unavoidable when removing tumors in the immediate vicinity of the tricuspid valve. So we performed tricuspid annulus reconstruction and valvuloplasty surgery, which resulted in a prolonged CPB time. There is a contradiction between prolonged CPB time and fetal death [20, 23]. We believe this may be due to the small and heterogeneous sample data. Theoretically, hemodilution and the release of vasoactive substances during CPB will reduce placental perfusion. Moreover, the obstruction of venous drainage due to cannulation of the inferior vena cava, uterine artery spasm, and particulate and gaseous embolism can affect placental circulation and result in fetal hypoxia [24]. Therefore, we believe that the prolonged CPB time is a possible main cause of the fetal loss in this case.

Cardiac angiosarcoma is a rare disease that is often detected at a late stage. Resection of the tumor is risky but is an effective method and should be used to remove the tumor as much as possible while preserving the structures of the heart. Postoperative combination therapy is effective in patients with positive margins or concomitant metastases. The principle of management of cardiac malignancies during pregnancy is to maximize maternal survival, so a multidisciplinary team is indispensable.
