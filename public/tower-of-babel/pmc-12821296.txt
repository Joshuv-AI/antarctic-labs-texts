A distinct radiologic perspective on parotid pleomorphic adenoma: sialo-CBCT case report
Authors: Fatma Çağlayan, Muhammed Akif Sümbüllü, Abdülkadir Şahin, Kübra Törenek Ağirman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPleomorphic adenoma (PA) is the most prevalent benign salivary gland tumor, manifesting predominantly in the parotid gland. The imaging techniques most frequently employed for diagnosis include ultrasonography (USG), computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Sialography is an interventional imaging technique that utilizes a positive contrast medium to illustrate the ductal anatomy of the parotid and submandibular glands. While there are studies in the literature demonstrating the sialography combined with cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) in the diagnosis of non-tumoral pathologies of salivary glands, there is a paucity of literature evaluating tumors with sialo-CBCT. The objective of this case report was to present the diagnostic and treatment processes of a pleomorphic adenoma of the parotid gland diagnosed by sialo-CBCT and to review the literature. To the best of our knowledge, no other case of PA has been reported in the literature that has been radiologically visualized and characterized with sialo-CBCT.Case presentationA 61-year-old female patient exhibited swelling in the left cheek and was initially diagnosed with parotid PA on USG examination. Subsequently, a sialo-CBCT was performed to visualize the lesion and parenchymal structure. Post-contrast CBCT images revealed a focal lesion area in the left parotid superficial lobe, partially surrounded by contrast-enhanced parenchymal tissue with regular borders. Subsequently, surgical excision of the lesion was performed, and the presence of PA was confirmed as the definitive diagnosis.ConclusionAlthough sialo-CBCT is not the primary diagnostic tool for diagnosing PA, it allows simultaneous assessment of salivary gland tumors, parenchymal architecture, and the ductal system without superimposition, offering precise and comprehensive three-dimensional visualization.

Background

Pleomorphic adenoma (PA) is the most prevalent salivary gland tumor, accounting for two-thirds of all cases [1–5]. Parotid tumors typically present as small, nodular formations developing within a pseudo capsule and may exhibit clinical symptoms such as painless swelling and asymmetry, with growth patterns influenced by the tumor’s size [2, 3]. The dimensions of these tumors range from 2 to 6 centimeters. The excision is frequently utilized in the treatment of pleomorphic adenomas, with superficial or total parotidectomy being performed, contingent upon the tumor’s dimensions [4]. The absence of a regular capsule has been demonstrated to elevate the probability of recurrence, with the risk varying contingent upon the surgical method employed [4, 5].

The imaging techniques most frequently utilized in the evaluation of salivary gland neoplasms are ultrasonography (USG), computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [2, 6]. Ultrasonography (USG) is an ideal diagnostic tool for the initial diagnosis of superficial lesions of the parotid, submandibular, and sublingual salivary glands [7, 8]. USG provides high-resolution images with high-frequency linear probes and is an inexpensive, rapid, and simultaneous technique that provides information about the characteristics of lesions without the risk of ionizing radiation. It also allows for the evaluation of cervical lymph nodes. However, in cases where deep tissue invasion is suspected or malignancy is confirmed on cytology, an MRI or CT is necessary to assess the full extent of the tumor, local invasion, and perineural spread [7]. USG can also be used in combination with investigations such as fine needle aspiration biopsy (FNAB) or core biopsy for further investigation [2, 7]. FNAB is a useful, minimally invasive, cost-effective, and widely used technique for the preoperative diagnosis of salivary gland lesions. The diagnostic accuracy of fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) in the diagnosis of PA has been reported to be 89.5%-96.2% [9].

PA is observed on USG as limited, lobulated, hypoechogenous areas with posterior acoustic enhancement that are easily distinguished from normal echogenic parenchyma [2, 5]. The borders, shape, size, internal structure, and blood supply of the lesion can be evaluated by USG. PA shows no or minimal blood supply on Doppler USG [5]. Irregularly circumscribed lesions and heterogeneous appearance raise suspicion in terms of malignancy risk.

PA can be observed in various ways on CT, depending on its histologic subtype. Specifically, fibrous and cellular subtypes exhibit soft-tissue density, lipometaplasia subtypes exhibit fat density, ossification subtypes exhibit a mixture of amorphous bone attenuation and soft tissue components, cystic degeneration subtypes exhibit low-density areas, and infarction subtypes exhibit various low- to high-density areas [6]. CT’s merits include its short imaging time and high spatial resolution, while its drawbacks include limited soft tissue visualization and ionizing radiation exposure CT is more commonly used in the examination of minor salivary gland lesions, while MRI is more commonly used in the diagnosis of major salivary gland lesions [10]. MRI provides excellent soft tissue differentiation, but it has disadvantages such as being expensive and long scan time.

Sialography is an interventional imaging technique that utilizes a positive contrast medium to radiographically illustrate the ductal anatomy of the parotid and submandibular glands. Its contraindications include acute infection and allergy. Notably, sialography is the sole imaging modality that examines the intricate anatomy of the salivary gland parenchyma and ductal system [11]. Salivary gland tumors are visualized on sialography as areas without contrast enhancement causing duct displacement [12]. The capabilities of sialography are constrained by the limitations of the imaging modalities with which it is associated. Sialography is commonly employed in conjunction with plain radiography, CT, Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT), MRI, and fluoroscopy [13]. Historically, sialography has been most frequently used with two-dimensional radiography techniques, such as plain radiographs. Nevertheless, the diagnostic capability of the two-dimensional images created may be restricted. Sialography has been integrated with medical CT; however, the anisotropic voxel resolution may not adequately depict the intricate anatomical structures of the ductal systems within the gland [14].

The utilization of CBCT as an imaging modality for sialography has also been documented in the literature [13, 15]. The integration of CBCT with sialography for the visualization of the intra- and extraglandular ductal system constitutes a judicious approach. The integration of 3-dimensional and multiplanar reconstruction in sialo-CBCT (i.e., the transformation of data obtained in a single plane to another plane or planes) facilitates the accurate mapping of the salivary ducts [16]. Sialo-CBCT enables the execution of 3-dimensional reconstruction in various windowing modalities (e.g., skeletal-bone, MIP), allowing for the acquisition of images in any plane at the user-defined angle and thickness.

The use of CBCT systems is advantageous due to their low cost, relatively low radiation dose and short acquisition time. In addition, CBCT images have isotropic voxels that can be as small as 0.125 mm and subjective image quality is high, even compared to helical CT, for the highest resolution modalities [17]. CBCT sialography may also be preferred in cases where MRI is contraindicated, such as in cases of pacemaker-cardiac pacemakers, heart valves made of magnetic materials, and heart valves with metal implants and infusion pumps. Furthermore, CBCT sialography may be advantageous when access to MR devices is limited.

It is documented that sialo-CBCT is a distinctive technique that demonstrates ductal mapping and parenchymal damage in salivary glands [18]. However, there is a paucity of literature on the use of sialo-CBCT in the diagnosis of salivary gland tumors. Although there is a case report demonstrating the role of sialo-CBCT in the diagnosis of space-occupying lesions of the salivary glands, the exact diagnosis of the areas observed in this report is unclear [19]. The aim of this report is to present the diagnostic and treatment processes of a case of parotid gland pleomorphic adenoma has been visualized and characterized with sialo-CBCT for the first time and to review the relevant literature.

Case report

Clinical examination

A 61-year-old female patient was referred to the Ataturk University Faculty of Dentistry, Department of Oral Diagnosis and Maxillofacial Radiology clinic in 2024 May, with a painless swelling of the left cheek in front of and below the ear, of which she had been aware for a considerable period of time. A detailed review of the patient’s medical history revealed no other diseases except for the use of anticoagulants for the treatment of coronary artery disease. A thorough clinical examination was conducted, which revealed the presence of a palpable, moderately firm, non-fluctuant, non-mobile mass in the affected area. The patient was found to be completely edentulous, and no skeletal dentoalveolar pathology was observed on the panoramic radiograph taken as part of the routine dental examination (Fig. 1). The decision was taken to proceed with a USG scan.

Ultrasonography procedure

An USG was performed transcutaneously with a Toshiba Applio 300 (Toshiba Corp.) using a 12 MHz linear probe. The USG revealed a relatively heterogeneous, regularly circumscribed lesion in the superficial lobe of the parotid gland with a hypoechoic appearance compared to the surrounding parenchymal tissue, causing expansion on the outer surface of the gland and mild posterior acoustic enhancement (Fig. 2). A Doppler USG showed minimal blood supply to the lesion. The lesion was diagnosed with a presumptive diagnosis of PA. Subsequently, a decision was made to perform Sialo-CBCT to examine the ductal system, obstruction, and localization of the tumor together with the ductal system in three dimensions.

Sialo-CBCT procedure

Prior to undergoing the procedure, the patient was assured that there was no acute infection and no history of allergies. The Terumo Radifocus transradial set (4Fr/RT-R40A07PQ, Terumo Europe, Leuven, Belgium) was utilized for CBCT sialography. The patient was attired in a sterile disposable gown, and antiseptic 7.5% povidone iodine (Betadine) solution was applied to the perioral area. Subsequent to the expansion of the stenosis using air spray to open the canal mouth, the canal was entered slowly and in a controlled manner with the guidewire. Once adequate progress was achieved (2.5–3 cm), the cannula (dilator) was utilized to dilate the canal with unidirectional forward rotation movements at an average distance of 2–2.5 cm. In instances where the patient exhibited sensations of tension or discomfort, or where there was observed resistance to the injection process, an average of 4 milliliters of contrast medium solution (OpaxolR, 300 mg/ml iohexol) was injected into the primary cannula (dilator), under the assumption that sufficient saturation had been attained. During the scan, the cannula was fixed between the patient’s upper and lower incisors to prevent the dilator cannula from coming out of the canal, leakage of the contrast solution into the oral cavity, and volume loss of the gland. The CBCT scan was then performed using a Newtom VGI-EVO (QR, Verona, Italy). To mitigate the potential risks associated with radiation exposure, the FOV was strategically focused on the gland, with a dimension of 150 × 120 mm. The device autonomously adjusted the tube voltage, tube current, and exposure time according to the guide image, ensuring optimal dose modulation. The patient was discharged without any significant complications, with the exception of mild tenderness, which was attributed to the completion of the evacuation process as a result of panoramic radiography taken after a while to monitor the complete removal of the contrast material.

Contrast-enhanced CBCT images revealed a non-enhancing focal lesion area in the left parotid superficial lobe, partially surrounded by contrast-enhanced parenchymal tissue with regular borders (Figs. 3 and 4). The continuity of the superficial and deep lobes, as well as the ductal structures, were monitored using panoramic, sagittal sections with multiplanar images, created from axial sections with a thickness of 0.5 mm. The lesion was observed to be located intra-glandularly in the superficial part of the parotid gland (Fig. 5). The lesion was found to be localized within the superficial lobe, laterally situated to the major bifurcation of the primary duct, and associated with the outer periphery of the gland. As the lesion was superficial, it did not cause displacement or narrowing in the primary and secondary ducts. The lesion exhibited isodensity relative to the surrounding soft tissues.

FNAB procedure

A decision was made to perform a FNAB to confirm the preliminary diagnosis before surgical excision. The location of the extraoral access was determined by ultrasound (US) examination, and the buccal area and extraoral probes (12 MHz) were stained with antiseptic povidone iodine solution. The appropriate access site for FNAB was determined by US in color Doppler mode. A 27-gauge dental needle and a 20 cc syringe were inserted from the edge of the probe towards the lesion center, and the movement of the needle was monitored simultaneously with USG. When the needle reached the lesion center, cytological samples were collected by making minimal reciprocating movement of the needle and pulling the plunger of the syringe (Fig. 2). The FNAC was reported as probable PA. The patient, whose cytologic preliminary examination result was also PA, was referred to the Ear, Nose and Throat service for surgical treatment of the lesion.

Surgery procedure

Preoperative MRI was obtained to evaluate nerve involvement (Fig. 6). MRI sections revealed a solitary lesion measuring 7 mm in diameter, with homogeneous contrast enhancement, located superficially in the superior portion of the left parotid gland. After the necessary preliminary preparations, the lesion was excised under general anesthesia (Fig. 7). The operative material, consisting of five pieces, was sent for histopathologic examination after surgical excision. Histopathologic examination revealed a regularly circumscribed lesion area with a diameter of 0.9 mm in adipose tissue and a well-defined, benign tumor with epithelial and myxoid/chondroid stromal components. No additional pathology was observed in the other materials except one reactive lymph node. The histopathologic examination of the lesion confirmed the diagnosis of PA.

Discussion

The majority of salivary gland tumors, approximately 64–80%, occur in the parotid gland and 68–85% of parotid tumors are benign. PA is the most prevalent benign salivary gland tumor and occurs mostly in the parotid gland superficial lobe [3, 4, 20, 21]. Despite its benign nature, it has a propensity to recur and transform into malignancies [2]. The presence of the facial nerve in the parotid gland can pose significant challenges during preoperative diagnosis and surgical excision.

The prevalence of PA typically occurs between the third and sixth decades of life, with a higher incidence observed in women [1, 4, 22]. In this particular instance, the lesion was identified in the superficial lobe of the parotid gland of a 61-year-old female patient, aligning with the existing literature on the subject. The lesion manifests as a painless, slowly growing swelling [4]. While the lesion progresses slowly, if left untreated, it may develop into malignancy and metastasize [23]. The optimal treatment of PA is surgical excision with wide negative margin. Depending on the condition of the case, superficial or total parotidectomy may be considered, provided that the facial nerve is preserved [4]. In this case, the risk of recurrence decreases. However, in many cases, due to the proximity of the tumor to the facial nerve or its branches, enucleation or capsular dissection may be necessary [4]. In this case, total parotidectomy is not considered during the surgical operation due to the superficial location of the lesion and the close proximity of the lesion to the facial nerve branches. No postoperative complications indicating nerve damage were observed in the patient.

The dimensions of PAs range from 1 to 10 centimeters. In the absence of intervention, these lesions have the potential to attain substantial proportions, often resulting in a weight measurement of several kilograms [21]. The abrupt escalation in the size of the lesion, which ordinarily exhibits a gradual growth pattern, is indicative of an intra-lesional hemorrhage [11]. The literature has documented cases of giant PAs [21]. Pareek et al. reported a 25-centimeter PA in their review of giant PAs [21]. In this case, the size of the lesion was not particularly large, but what sets this report apart is the use of sialo-CBCT for the radiologic examination, a method that has not been previously documented in the literature.

The histological subtypes of PA manifest radiographically as soft tissue, fat, calcific foci, low-density cystic areas, or heterogeneous areas of low to high density on CT scans [6]. However, the Hounsfield scale of CBCT is not valid [17], the PA tumor area had a homogeneous appearance and isodensity with surrounding soft tissue on CBCT sections in this case. Typical PAs on MRI manifest low-intermediate signal intensity on T1-weighted images and markedly high signal intensity on T2-weighted images, reflecting the myxochondroid stroma within hypo-intense borders, indicative of the fibrous capsule [6, 11]. However, intra-tumoral signal intensity also exhibits variability according to cellular density, the ratio of epithelial and stromal components, and the nature of stromal components. It also demonstrates high signal intensity on T2-weighted images in the presence of hemorrhage or cystic degeneration within the lesion [11]. In this case, the lesion manifested as a consistently circumscribed, homogeneous, hypo-intense lesion on T1 and T2-weighted images of the preoperative MRI also. This finding underscores the potential for the oversight of small lesions on MRI and underscores the significance of clinical examination and interventional procedures. Furthermore, sialo-CBCT, an interventional technique, facilitated the precise observation of the tumor’s location within the parenchymal structure and its relationship with the ductal pattern.

Of course, MRI is the most useful technique for soft tissue imaging. It provides information about the content and characteristics of the lesions, as well as nerve involvement. However, as seen in this case, sialo-CBCT also allows for morphometric analysis of the lesion and clear examination of its relationship with the ductal system. Ductal involvement, stenosis, obstruction, and ductal displacement can be clearly observed. This advantage will also contribute to minimizing damage to the main ducts during surgical procedures. Indeed, in this case, the lesion was found to be located adjacent to the major bifurcation of the primary duct.

FNAB is a technique that is relatively straightforward to perform, generally does not require anesthesia, does not compromise patient comfort, has a low complication rate, and permits sampling from multiple foci simultaneously. However, it is not without drawbacks, including the potential for false negative results due to inadequate material collection and the inability to ascertain the subtype of malignant lesions. However, when FNAB is performed under USG guidance, it offers several advantages. This approach allows for precise localization in small and deep lesions, ensures an accurate approach with visualization of adjacent vascular structures, avoids necrotic and cystic areas, and places the needle precisely into the solid component of the lesion. This results in a more controlled and safe application. According to the PA diagnostic algorithm, FNAB is preferred before excision. Tommola et al. demonstrated that the diagnostic accuracy of FNAC in the diagnosis of PA was 96.5% [9]. Fikova et al. reported that USG-guided FNAB is a simple, accurate, minimally invasive technique in the diagnosis of PA and found the sensitivity [24]. In this case, following FNAB, the excisional biopsy result was confirmed as PA, thus confirming the accuracy of the FNAC procedure.

Sialography was first performed in 1902 [17] and is regarded as the gold standard for delineating the delicate ductal structures of the major salivary glands, non-calcified sialoliths, and ductal strictures [25]. However, conventional sialography is not preferred nowadays due to the development of imaging techniques. Three-dimensional sialography has emerged as a preferred alternative to two-dimensional sialography [26]. The effectiveness of sialography is contingent upon the imaging technique employed in its execution. In recent years, sialography has been utilized in conjunction with CBCT, offering a comprehensive approach to duct imaging. Sialo-CBCT facilitates three-dimensional, detailed visualization of the branches of the major salivary gland ducts, offering distinct advantages due to its isotropic voxel resolution [13, 26].

A substantial body of research has been published on the superior sensitivity and specificity of CBCT in contrast-enhanced examinations of the salivary gland when compared with conventional radiography, USG, sialoendoscopy, and conventional sialography [27–29] and favorable results when compared with 3D imaging modalities such as medical CT and MRI [29]. A comparison of CBCT imaging with USG, MRI, and 2D techniques revealed that despite radiation exposure, USG and 2D examinations exhibited low sensitivity in diagnostic information [30]. This suggests that CBCT is more advantageous because MRI requires laborious technical effort and relatively low accessibility of the device. In the context of sialography in conjunction with conventional radiography, it has been documented that small sialoliths may be overlooked due to superposition effects, tortuous canals may not be adequately mapped, and the radiation exposure may equal that of CBCT when a second or even third technique is employed for diagnostic purposes [30]. Moreover, the literature suggests that CBCT is a preferable imaging modality due to its high diagnostic performance compared to radiation dose [31].

Contrary to the assertions of certain authors who regard sialography as an invasive procedure [29]. Kalk et al. [32] have stated that when performed with the requisite degree of skill and care, sialography can be considered a minimally invasive and painless procedure. In their study, they observed that of the 24 patients who underwent parotid sialography, 19 reported no adverse effects, and only 5 patients experienced mild discomfort [32]. In the present case, no complications were observed during or after the sialography procedure. The limitations of CBCT sialography include the invasiveness of the procedure, the need for skill in cannulation of the canal, the potential development of claustrophobia in patients depending on the type of device (supine devices) or the movement of patients during imaging, and the high radiation dose used compared to conventional sialography [33].

The most significant disadvantages of CT compared to CBCT are the higher equipment and imaging costs and the high radiation dose. The disadvantages of CBCT include the small scanning area, the invalidity of the Hounsfield unit, and the insufficient soft tissue resolution. Moreover, due to its working principle and beam structure, the presence of image artefacts caused by metal restorations is more prevalent. CBCT is subject to inherent limitations when utilized in conjunction with contrast media for the purpose of examining soft tissue. The following are considered to be significant disadvantages of contrast-enhanced CBCT applications: beam hardening, low contrast resolution, the invalidity of Hounsfield units, and the blooming effect caused by contrast agents. Conversely, the merits of contrast-enhanced CBCT encompass reduced radiation dosage, enhanced bone detail resolution, expedited acquisition time, facilitated visualization of the ductal system, cost-effectiveness, compact device configuration, and support in surgical planning. This will be of particular benefit to surgeons, as it will allow them to demonstrate bone invasion and canal involvement. These advantages play a significant role in the preference for CBCT, particularly in dental and maxillofacial areas. However, it is imperative to acknowledge the limitations inherent in soft tissue evaluation.

The utilization of sialography in conjunction with CT for the diagnosis of parotid tumors has been previously recommended [34]. Kandula et al. reported that CT-sialography is a useful and valuable technique for precise mapping of surgical landmarks and ductal abnormalities prior to parotidectomy or sialendoscopic procedure [35]. Hansson and Johansen similarly asserted that parotid CT sialography is very advantageous in showing tumor size and its relationship with surrounding structures [36]. On CT-sialography, tumors form a “ball-in-hand” image without contrast filling as a result of ductal displacement caused by tumor compression within the gland [35]. Similarly, in this case where Sialo-CBCT was performed, the tumor appeared as an area without contrast filling. In addition to its capacity to reveal the tumor, Sialo-CBCT also offers the advantage of clearly demonstrating the parotid ductal pattern and parenchymal structure. Despite the existence of studies in the literature demonstrating the application of sialo-CBCT in the diagnosis of non-tumoral pathologies of salivary glands [16, 26, 37] there is no other case evaluating PA with sialo-CBCT.

The most significant advantages of Sialo-CBCT include the demonstration of the relationship between the duct system and the tumor, the assessment of compression or invasion within the ducts, the evaluation of bone invasion if present, and the facilitation of surgical planning for the tumor. This approach enables the mitigation of damage, particularly to the primary ducts, during surgical interventions. Furthermore, it is a valuable diagnostic tool for identifying duct obstructions. However, it should be noted that MRI and/or contrast-enhanced CT should be preferred if there are needs such as distinguishing between benign and malignant tumors, visualizing the internal structure of the tissue, and assessing vascular structures.

Conclusion

The present case report offers a valuable opportunity to distinguish PA on sialo-CBCT as a non-contrasting structure within the normal parenchyma of the parotid gland. Sialo-CBCT is a distinctive technique that allows for simultaneous evaluation of both salivary gland tumors and ductal structure without superposition, facilitating accurate three-dimensional mapping through its 3-dimensional and multiplanar reconstruction capabilities. Although sialo-CBCT is not the primary diagnostic tool for diagnosing PA, it is a highly useful technique for supporting the relationship of the tumor with the ductal system, differential diagnosis of obstruction, and surgical planning.

In conclusion, sialo-CBCT offers significant contributions in the evaluation of salivary gland tumors, particularly in visualizing the ductal system, distinguishing the tumor, assessing the tumor’s relationship with the ductal system—especially in terms of displacement, narrowing, and obstruction within the ducts—and detecting bone invasion. However, due to the low soft tissue contrast, methods such as CT and MRI should be preferred for tumor characterization and determining its spread. It is an irrefutable fact that this case report will contribute to the extant body of knowledge.
