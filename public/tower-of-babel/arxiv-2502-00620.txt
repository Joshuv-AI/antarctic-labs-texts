Representations Shape Weak-to-Strong Generalization: Theoretical Insights and Empirical Predictions
Yihao Xue, Jiping Li, Baharan Mirzasoleiman

Abstract
Weak-to-Strong Generalization (W2SG), where a weak model supervises a stronger one, serves as an important analogy for understanding how humans might guide superhuman intelligence in the future. Promising empirical results revealed that a strong model can surpass its weak supervisor. While recent work has offered theoretical insights into this phenomenon, a clear understanding of the interactions between weak and strong models that drive W2SG remains elusive. We investigate W2SG through a theoretical lens and show that it can be characterized using kernels derived from the principal components of weak and strong models’ internal representations. These kernels can be used to define a space that, at a high level, captures what the weak model is unable to learn but is learnable by the strong model. The projection of labels onto this space quantifies how much the strong model falls short of its full potential due to weak supervision. This characterization also provides insights into how certain errors in weak supervision can be corrected by the strong model, regardless of overfitting. Our theory has significant practical implications, providing a representation-based metric that predicts W2SG performance trends without requiring labels, as shown in experiments on molecular predictions with transformers and 5 NLP tasks involving 52 LLMs.

Introduction

As AI systems become increasingly capable of performing complex tasks beyond human comprehension, humans will inevitably serve as “weak supervisors” in aligning advanced AI. To investigate this fundamental problem,
Burns et al. (2023)
propose an analogy that can be empirically explored today: can a weak model effectively supervise a stronger one? This framework, known as Weak-to-Strong Generalization (W2SG), involves leveraging a weak model, finetuned on a specific task, to supervise the finetuning of a stronger model.
In this analogy, the finetuning task represents concepts tied to human values or skills, the finetuned weak model represents humans—limited in capability but aligned with human values, and the strong model represents superhuman intelligence–powerful but initially unaligned. Promising results from
(
Burns et al., 2023
)
show that the strong model can significantly outperform its weak supervisor. For instance, a GPT-4 model supervised by a fine-tuned GPT-2-level model achieves nearly 20% better performance than the weak supervisor on NLP tasks.

, determining the contribution of the weak model’s errors to

.

At first glance, this phenomenon seems counterintuitive. After all, the strong model is explicitly trained to fit the weak supervision. Yet, it goes beyond mere imitation and generalizes better. It is important to understand which intrinsic properties of the weak and strong models enable W2SG.

Efforts have been made toward a theoretical understanding of W2SG.
Charikar et al. (2024)
demonstrates that the disagreement between finetuned weak and strong models correlates with performance gains in W2SG. However, their analysis assumes high-quality representations in the strong model and does not address the role of the weak model’s representations. The analysis of
(
Lang et al., 2024
;
Shin et al., 2024
)
assumes a generalized version of an adversarially robust strong model, where W2SG arises solely from underfitting weak supervision. This framework excludes important scenarios such as benign overfitting, where W2SG occurs despite overfitting.
Wu & Sahai (2024)
particularly studied benign overfitting and examined the impact of number of weakly labeled data points. However, we still lack an overarching explanation that captures the interaction between weak and strong models in enabling W2SG, as well as how it determines which weak supervision errors are corrected in general scenarios. The challenge lies in characterizing the abstract concepts including the knowledge embedded in the weak and strong models, their utilization, and their respective roles in W2SG. Striving for results that are general enough to capture a spectrum of behaviors without overly strict assumptions further adds to the complexity.

To address this, we adopt a representation-based perspective, analyzing finetuning as a process of learning a function on fixed representations to uncover how the internal structures of weak and strong models influence W2SG. Under a very general assumption about the representations, we demonstrate (illustrated in Fig.
1
) that the key quantifiable property governing W2SG is the overlap between two spaces: one representing what the weak model’s
principal representations
(capturing key knowledge gained during pretraining) do not cover, and the other representing what the strong model’s
principal representations
do cover. Errors in weak supervision that fall within this overlap hinder the strong model from reaching its full potential, leading to a prediction gap between the strong model finetuned with weak supervision and that finetuned with ground truth labels.
A smaller overlap implies that fewer of the weak model’s mistakes are replicated, resulting in better W2SG performance.

We then demonstrate an important use case of our main result: explaining
benign overfitting
, where the W2S model overfits the weak model’s mistakes on finetuning data yet paradoxically generalizes better on the test set. Using our theoretical framework, we establish a general condition for benign overfitting and apply it to a toy example to concretely illustrate the role of representations in error replication: errors that do not align with the kernel defined by the strong model’s principal representations are not replicated by the W2S model, regardless of the extent of overfitting.

Our theory offers a metric that predicts trends in W2SG performance in practice
without having the finetuning task labels
. This metric, which measures the overlap between the two highlighted regions in Fig.
1
, shows a strong correlation with W2SG performance across various settings. The extensive experiments across 8 datasets, involving 150 small transformers and 52 LLMs,
not only validate our theoretical insights but also suggest their potential applications in managing W2SG, providing a deeper understanding of LLM behavior through their internal representation structures.

2
Related Work

There have been many recent works that theoretically explore W2SG.
Somerstep et al. (2024)
adopt a transfer learning perspective, focusing on improving W2SG through in-context learning rather than explaining how W2SG emerges.
Lang et al. (2024)
;
Shin et al. (2024)
analyze W2SG by considering a generalized version of adversarially robust models, showing that certain errors in weak supervision can be corrected by leveraging the good neighborhood structure in the data. However, their argument attributes error correction solely to underfitting—i.e., avoiding fitting mislabeled finetuning data. This overlooks an important scenario recently discussed in
(
Wu & Sahai, 2024
)
, known as benign overfitting, where the strong model overfits mislabeled finetuning data but still achieves accurate test-time predictions. Benign overfitting is particularly relevant in practice, as large neural networks often have the capacity to overfit while still generalizing effectively
(
Zhang et al., 2021
)
. Closer to our setting,
Charikar et al. (2024)
formalized W2SG using a representation-based perspective. Their work demonstrates that performance gain in W2SG correlates with the disagreement between the finetuned weak and strong models, assuming high-quality representations for the strong model. While insightful, it does not characterize the role of the weak model’s representations, leaving the exact conditions for effective W2SG unclear.

Compared to
(
Lang et al., 2024
)
, we analyze W2SG in a more realistic setting where error correction can result from either underfitting or overfitting, allowing for a full spectrum of behaviors. While benign overfitting is not our primary focus, we discuss it as a special case in Sec.
4
due to its importance and offer new insights. Compared to
(
Charikar et al., 2024
)
, we explicitly links W2SG performance to the interaction between the weak and strong models’ representations, providing a more comprehensive view of how the intrinsic properties of the two models jointly determine W2SG.

3
W2SG from a Representation Perspective

We first formalize finetuning from a representation-based perspective, then introduce the properties of the representations considered, and finally present our main theory.

3.1
A representation-based perspective

The knowledge a model acquires through pretraining enables it to interpret inputs, extract relevant information, and organize it into meaningful intermediate states. This can be formalized as a “representation function”,
h
, which transforms data into structured representations. Finetuning leverages this knowledge to produce the desired output, which we formalize as learning a new function
f
on the fixed
h
. The entire model is thus represented as the composition
f\!\circ\!h
. For simplicity, we consider the outputs of
h
as vectors, and focus on the case where
f
is a linear functions. This is practically relevant because: (1) Training a linear task head on fixed representations is common with large foundation models, e.g., using embedding LLMs
(
Muennighoff et al., 2022
)
, linear probing on intermediate activations
(
Zou et al., 2023
;
Nanda et al., 2023
;
Marks & Tegmark, 2023
)
. (2) fine-tuning of LLMs largely operates in the NTK regime
(
Jacot et al., 2018
)
, where training dynamics are captured by a linear model on representations derived from model gradients
(
Malladi et al., 2023
)
. (3) Our experiments in Sec.
5
show that insights from analyzing linear functions generalize to the complex non-linear setting of finetuning entire LLMs from pretrained weights.

3.2
Preliminaries

Notations.
We sometimes abbreviate a matrix
{\bm{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{l\times m}
as
[A_{i,j}]_{1\leq i\leq l,1\leq j\leq m}
when each element
A_{i,j}
can be expressed as a generic term in terms of its indices.
\lambda_{\text{min, $\neq 0$}}({\bm{A}})
denotes the smallest nonzero eigenvalue of matrix
{\bm{A}}
.

Data.
Let
{\mathcal{D}}
denote the distribution of the finetuning task’s data, defined over the input-label pairs
({\bm{x}},y)\in{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}
, where
{\mathcal{Y}}={\mathbb{R}}
. In W2SG, we have two splits of data sampled from
{\mathcal{D}}
. The first subset,
\tilde{{\mathcal{D}}}=\{(\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{i},\tilde{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{\tilde{n}}
, consists of
\tilde{n}
i.i.d. samples and is used for finetuning the weak model. The second subset,
\hat{{\mathcal{D}}}=\{(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i},\hat{y}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}
with
\hat{n}
i.i.d. samples is used for finetuning the strong model. Note that the weak model’s outputs will be used as labels in place of the actual
\hat{y}_{i}
’s. In our notation, quantities associated with the two splits are marked by the diacritical symbols,
\tilde{}
and
\hat{}
, respectively.

Models.
We denote the weak and strong models’ representation functions as
h_{\text{w}}
and
h_{\text{s}}
, respectively. The finetuned weak model is represented as
f_{\text{w}}\!\circ\!h_{\text{w}}
, with

f_{\text{w}}\!=\!\arg\min_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\text{w}}}(\frac{1}{\tilde{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{n}}(f(h_{\text{w}}(\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{i}))-\tilde{y}_{i})^{2}\!+\!\beta_{\text{w}}R(f)).

where
R(\cdot)
represents
\ell_{2}
regularization.

The
W2S model
, which refers to the strong model finetuned with weak supervision, is represented as
f_{\text{w2s}}\!\circ\!h_{\text{s}}
, with

f_{\text{w2s}}\!=\!\arg\min_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\text{s}}}(\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}(f(h_{\text{s}}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i}))\!-\!f_{\text{w}}(h_{\text{w}}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i})))^{2}\!+\!\beta_{\text{s}}R(f)).

Additionally, as a reference, we define the
strong ceiling model
as the strong model finetuned with the ground truth labels. It is represented as
f_{\text{sc}}\circ h_{\text{s}}
with

f_{\text{sc}}=\arg\min_{f\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\text{s}}}(\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}(f(h_{\text{s}}(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i}))-\hat{y}_{i})^{2}+R_{\text{s}}(f)).

Evaluation.
At test time, given any labeling function
g:{\mathcal{X}}\!\rightarrow\!{\mathcal{Y}}
, we define its test error as the loss on the population:
\text{Err}(g)=\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[(g({\bm{x}})\!-\!y)^{2}]
. We then introduce the shorthand notations: the weak model’s test error
\text{Err}_{\text{w}}=\text{Err}(f_{\text{w}}\circ h_{\text{w}})
, the W2S model’s test error
\text{Err}_{\text{w2s}}=\text{Err}(f_{\text{w2s}}\circ h_{\text{s}})
, and the strong ceiling model’s test error
\text{Err}_{\text{sc}}=\text{Err}(f_{\text{sc}}\circ h_{\text{s}})
.
\text{Err}_{\text{w2s}}
measures the performance achieved through W2SG, while
\text{Err}_{\text{sc}}
serves as the upper limit.

We also introduce
PredGap
, the squared difference between the predictions of the W2S and strong ceiling models:

\textbf{PredGap}=\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}},y)\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[(f_{\text{w2s}}(h_{\text{s}}({\bm{x}}))\!-\!f_{\text{sc}}(h_{\text{s}}({\bm{x}})))^{2}].

It captures how much the strong model falls short of its full potential due to weak supervision.
It is also indicative of
\text{Err}_{\text{w2s}}
, the direct measure of W2SG performance, through these connections: (1) If the strong ceiling model is nearly perfect, it follows that
\textbf{PredGap}{}\approx\text{Err}_{\text{w2s}}
as the strong ceiling’s predictions are almost identical to the ground truth. This is not unlikely, since the ultimate goal of W2SG is to operate in cases where the strong model is a superhuman-level AI
(
Burns et al., 2023
)
, plausibly capable of achieving perfect results if provided with ground truth labels. (2) With small regularization and well-conditioned representations,
\text{Err}_{\text{w2s}}\approx\textbf{PredGap}{}+\text{Err}_{\text{sc}}
(Thm.
B.3
), analogous to the Pythagorean theorem. Then,
PredGap
directly determines
\text{Err}_{\text{w2s}}
for fixed
\text{Err}_{\text{sc}}
. (3) For general cases, the upper bound
\sqrt{\text{Err}_{\text{w2s}}}\!\leq\sqrt{\textbf{PredGap}{}}\!+\!\sqrt{\text{Err}_{\text{sc}}}
follows from the triangle inequality. Furthermore, the result obtained from analyzing
PredGap
helps predict
\text{Err}_{\text{w2s}}
in our experiments (Sec.
5
). Thus, our main analysis focuses on
PredGap
.

3.3
Setting: representations with a well-concentrated principal part
and a manageable non-principal part

We first define two basic concepts, kernel and covariance, before introducing a general assumption on representations.

Definition 3.1

(Kernel Matrix)
.

Given
h:\!{\mathcal{X}}\!\rightarrow\!{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we define the kernel matrix on the finetuning dataset
\hat{{\mathcal{D}}}
as
\hat{{\bm{K}}}(h)\!=\![h(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i})^{\top}h(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{j})]_{1\leq i,j\leq\hat{n}}
, a
\hat{n}\times\hat{n}
matrix where each element represents the inner product between a pair of representations.
\tilde{{\bm{K}}}(h)
is defined on
\tilde{{\mathcal{D}}}
in the same manner.

Definition 3.2

(Population/Empirical Covariance Matrices)
.

Given
h:\!{\mathcal{X}}\!\rightarrow\!{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we define the population covariance over distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
as
{\bm{\Sigma}}(h)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}[h({\bm{x}})h({\bm{x}})^{\top}]
. The empirical version on
\hat{{\mathcal{D}}}
is defined as
\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}(h)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}h(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i})h(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i})^{\top}
.
\tilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}(h)
is defined on
\tilde{{\mathcal{D}}}
in the same manner.

Given a representation function and a reasonable sample size, certain components in the representations should
concentrate well
, meaning they adequately reflect the population distribution. These components are pivotal to the model’s generalization. In our analysis, we focus on cases where the remainder—the less-well-concentrated components—satisfies certain conditions, ensuring their impact remains theoretically tractable. The decomposition of representations into these two parts is formalized as follows.

Definition 3.3

(
(\delta,\hat{\gamma},\tilde{\gamma})
-decomposability)
.

Given
{\mathcal{D}}
,
\tilde{{\mathcal{D}}}
,
\hat{{\mathcal{D}}}
, and a representation function
h:\!{\mathcal{X}}\!\rightarrow\!{\mathcal{R}}
, we say that the representations of
h
are
(\delta,\hat{\gamma},\tilde{\gamma})
-decomposable w.r.t. a subspace
{\mathcal{V}}
(of
{\mathcal{R}}
)
, for some
\delta\!=\!O(1)
,
\hat{\gamma}\!=\!O(1)
, and
\tilde{\gamma}\!=\!O(1)
, if there exists a subset of eigenvectors of
{\bm{\Sigma}}(h)
corresponding to non-zero eigenvalues such that the following holds. Let
{\mathcal{V}}
denote the span of these eigenvectors, and let
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
denote its orthogonal complement. Let
{\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}
and
{\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}}
denote the orthogonal projections onto
{\mathcal{V}}
and
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
, respectively. Define
\rho=\lambda_{\text{min, $\neq 0$}}({\bm{\Sigma}}({\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}h))
and
\gamma=\min(\hat{\gamma},\tilde{\gamma})
. With high probability of
1-o(1)
:

(a)
Boundedness
. A basic condition that ensures reasonable magnitudes of representations and labels:
\lVert{\bm{\Sigma}}(h)\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\!=\!O(1)
,
\lVert\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}(h)\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\!=\!O(1)

\lVert\tilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}(h)\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\!=\!O(1)
,
\mathbb{E}[y^{2}]=O(1)
,
\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}\hat{y}_{i}^{2}\!=\!O(1)
and
\frac{1}{\tilde{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{n}}\tilde{y}_{i}^{2}\!=\!O(1)
.

(b)
Concentration on
{\mathcal{V}}
. Representations are well-concentrated in the subspace
{\mathcal{V}}
, both in terms of their covariance and their correlation with labels:
\lVert\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}({\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}h)-{\bm{\Sigma}}({\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}h)\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=o(\gamma^{2}+\delta^{2}+\rho^{2})
,
\lVert\tilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}({\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}h)-{\bm{\Sigma}}({\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}h)\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=o(\gamma^{2}+\delta^{2}+\rho^{2})
,
\|\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}{\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}h(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i})\hat{y}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}h({\bm{x}})y]\|=o(\gamma+\delta+\rho)
and
\|\frac{1}{\tilde{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{n}}{\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}h(\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{i})\tilde{y}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}h({\bm{x}})y]\|=o(\gamma+\delta+\rho)
.

(c)
Kernel-wise
\delta
-isotropy on
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
. The kernels constructed using only the components in
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
exhibit certain uniformity in all orientations, with the extent of uniformity controlled by
\delta
:
\lVert\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{K}}}({\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}}h)\!-\!\hat{\gamma}{\bm{I}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\!=\!o(\gamma^{2}+\delta^{2})
, and
\lVert\frac{1}{\tilde{n}}\tilde{{\bm{K}}}({\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}}h)\!-\!\tilde{\gamma}{\bm{I}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\!=\!o(\gamma^{2}+\delta^{2})
.

(d)
Small cross-sample inner-product on
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
.
\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}\tilde{n}}}[({\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}}h(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i}))^{\top}{\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}}h(\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{j})]_{1\leq i\leq\hat{n},1\leq j\leq\tilde{n}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\!=\!o(\gamma\!+\!\delta)
, which holds when representations on
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
are nearly orthogonal across samples or have small magnitudes.

(e)
Diminishing population covariance on
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
. The representations on
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
have small magnitude in the population:
\lVert{\bm{\Sigma}}({\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}}h)\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=o(\gamma+\delta)
.

Additional explanation for Kernel-wise
\delta
-isotropy on
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
.
To provide a clearer understanding of this condition, consider the following: If
\delta
is very small (e.g.,
\delta=0
), the kernel on
\hat{{\mathcal{D}}}
is nearly identical to
\hat{\gamma}{\bm{I}}
, meaning it does not exhibit any specific patterns that differentiate between data points. In contrast, with a larger
\delta
(e.g.,
\delta\gg\hat{\gamma}
), this requirement is much more relaxed—the kernel no longer needs to closely resemble
\hat{\gamma}{\bm{I}}
but instead must simply have its magnitude bounded by
o(\delta)
. Thus, it accommodates scenarios where the kernel is highly isotropic, very small in scale, or anywhere in between. This is key to our analysis, as it ensures the effect of the less well-concentrated part of the representations remains tractable.
We note that this condition is not only analytically convenient but also practically relevant in real-world scenarios. For example, high-dimensional sub-Gaussian noise satisfies this condition with a small
\delta
—a situation highly relevant to deep neural networks with large internal dimensions, where vectors tend to be approximately orthogonal in the high-dimensional limit. More concrete instances will be presented in Examples
3.4
and
3.5
, as well as in Theorem
3.6
, along with discussions of their significance and relevance.

Additional explanation for Diminishing population covariance on
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
.
We note that this condition does not imply negligible impact of representations on
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
. For example, when
\delta
is small, the model can in fact leverage the components in
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
to interpolate the training data, even when such interpolation cannot be achieved by the components in
{\mathcal{V}}
(see Example
4.2
).

We refer to
{\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}h({\bm{x}})
, the well-concentrated part of the representation, as the
principal representation
, and the remainder,
{\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}}h({\bm{x}})
, as the
non-principal representation
.

Examples of Def.
3.3
.
Def.
3.3
is highly general, covering various representation distributions and dimensionalities. One simple case is when all components are well-concentrated, i.e., the entire representation is principal. This occurs when the representations exhibit a certain low-rank structure, which is common in deep neural networks

)

Example 3.4

(Arbitrarily parameterized; bounded representations with low intrinsic dimension)
.

Given
h:{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, for any
({\bm{x}},y)
,
\|h({\bm{x}})\|^{2}\leq B
and

, where

. Additionally,
\lVert{\bm{\Sigma}}(h)\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\!=\!\Theta(1)

q
. Let

and assume
n^{1-c}=\omega\big(B\log(q)\big)
for some constant

. Then, the representations are
(n^{-0.1c},0,0)
-decomposable w.r.t.
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Remark
.

The conditions imply a low intrinsic dimension relative to the sample size:
q\log q\!=\!o(n^{1-c})
(App.
C.1
), but without restricting the actual dimension

, allowing both under-
(d\!<\!n)

Example 3.5

(Heavily overparameterized; sub-Gaussian with spiked covariance)
.

Given
h:\!{\mathcal{X}}\!\rightarrow\!{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and randomly drawn
{\bm{x}}
,

h({\bm{x}})
has independent zero-mean sub-Gaussian entries.
The first
k

Compared to Example
3.4

{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{s}}
.

3.4
Principal representations shape
PredGap

Intuition.
One implication of Def.
3.3
is that only what is learned through the principal representations will be reflected at test time. Thus, the weak model’s mistakes primarily stem from its inability to generate certain outputs using its principal representations. For the same reason, among these mistakes, only those expressible through the strong model’s principal representations will affect its test performance. Therefore, a key concept affecting W2SG performance is
“what the weak model is unable to learn but is learnable by the strong model using their respective principal representations”
, which we seek to quantify.

Formalization.
To formalize the above idea, we leverage
\hat{{\bm{K}}}({\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{w}}}h_{\text{w}})
and
\hat{{\bm{K}}}({\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{s}}}h_{\text{s}})
–kernels computed using only the weak and strong models’ principal representations, referred to as
principal kernels
. We define the following

\displaystyle\vskip-8.5359pt{\bm{P}}_{\text{w}}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{K}}}({\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{w}}}h_{\text{w}})\left(\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{K}}}({\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{w}}}h_{\text{w}})+(\beta_{\text{w}}+\tilde{\gamma}_{\text{w}}){\bm{I}}\right)^{-1},

\displaystyle{\bm{P}}_{\text{s}}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{K}}}({\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{s}}}h_{\text{s}})\left(\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{K}}}({\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}_{\text{s}}}h_{\text{s}})+(\beta_{\text{s}}+\hat{\gamma}_{\text{s}}){\bm{I}}\right)^{-1}.

{\bm{P}}_{\text{w}}
and
{\bm{P}}_{\text{s}}

represent scaled projections onto the spans of the principal kernels. Each captures the space of output patterns that its respective model can express through its principal representations (with regularization taken into account). Then, the earlier intuition can be characterized as follows.

Theorem 3.8

(Main result)
.

Under Assump.
3.7
, and assuming reasonable regularization:
\delta_{\text{w}}\!\leq\!\beta_{\text{w}}\!=\!O(1)
and
\delta_{\text{s}}\!\leq\!\beta_{\text{s}}\!=\!O(1)
, let
\hat{{\bm{y}}}=[\hat{y}_{1}~\hat{y}_{2}~\dots~\hat{y}_{\hat{n}}]^{\top}
. Then, w.h.p., we have

\textbf{PredGap}=\|{\bm{P}}_{\text{s}}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}_{\text{w}})\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}\|^{2}\pm o(1)

(1)

{\bm{P}}_{\text{s}}({\bm{I}}\!-\!{\bm{P}}_{\text{w}})
captures “what the weak model is unable to learn but is learnable by the strong model using their respective principal representations”.
Therefore, it determines the mistakes that will be learned by the strong model, as discussed in the intuition. A more powerful weak model has a
{\bm{P}}_{\text{w}}
that covers more space, shrinking
{\bm{P}}_{\text{s}}({\bm{I}}\!-\!{\bm{P}}_{\text{w}})
and potentially leading to a smaller
PredGap
.

Propagation of Errors.
The earlier intuition is reflected in the proof (App.
A.7
). Given the labeling
\hat{{\bm{y}}}
, its projection
({\bm{I}}\!-\!{\bm{P}}_{\text{w}})\hat{{\bm{y}}}
is orthogonal to the scaled weak model’s principal kernel and thus cannot be effectively learned, contributing to the weak model’s error (Lem.
A.12
). The projection of this error onto the scaled strong model’s principal kernel,
{\bm{P}}_{\text{s}}({\bm{I}}\!-\!{\bm{P}}_{\text{w}})\hat{{\bm{y}}}
, is learned by the strong model and contributes to
PredGap
(Lem.
A.13
).

4
A Case Study on Benign Overfitting

Our theory can be applied to study and provide new insights into
benign overfitting
, an intriguing special case of W2SG, where the W2S model appears to mimic the weak supervision during finetuning, yet generalizes better at test time.

4.1
A general condition

Benign overfitting has been studied in the general machine learning context to understand deep neural networks’ generalization
(
Bartlett et al., 2020
;
Wang et al., 2021
;
Frei et al., 2022
;
Mallinar et al., 2022
)
. Recently,
(
Wu & Sahai, 2024
)
theoretically characterized benign overfitting in W2SG for a specific data distribution. Here, we aim to derive broader insights from a representation perspective. We consider the scenario where the strong model’s representations are highly expressive, enabling near-perfect overfitting of arbitrary labelings on the finetuning data, mirroring the behavior of very large neural networks in practice
(
Zhang et al., 2021
)
. This occurs when
\delta_{\text{s}}=o(\hat{\gamma}_{\text{s}})
(Lem.
B.4
), yielding a highly isotropic non-principal kernel. Meanwhile, since generalization depends solely on the principal representations by Thm.
3.8
, a small
\|{\bm{P}}_{\text{s}}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}_{\text{w}})\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}\|^{2}
suffices for good W2SG performance, regardless of the extent of overfitting. In this way, we connect benign overfitting to the general relationship between the weak and strong models’ representations:

Theorem 4.1

(A general condition for benign overfitting
1
1

1

Thm
4.1
can be extended to cases where the strong ceiling is not perfect, but we omit this for brevity.
)
.

In addition to Assumption
3.7
, suppose that
(1)
\delta_{\text{s}}=o(\hat{\gamma}_{\text{s}})
and
\delta_{\text{s}}\leq\beta_{\text{s}}=o(\hat{\gamma}_{\text{s}})
, (2) w.h.p., the strong ceiling model achieves nearly perfect performance, i.e.,
\text{Err}_{\text{sc}}=o(1)
, (3) w.h.p.,
\|{\bm{P}}_{\text{s}}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}_{\text{w}})\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}\|^{2}=\text{Err}_{\text{w}}-\Delta
with
\Delta=\Theta(1)
. Then, w.h.p., the W2S model achieves an almost zero (
o(1)
) training error on
\hat{{\mathcal{D}}}
, but generalizes better than the weak model:
\text{Err}_{\text{w2s}}\leq\text{Err}_{\text{w}}-\Delta+o(1)
. See proof in App.
B.3.1
.

Remark
.

Compared to
(
Wu & Sahai, 2024
)
, which focuses on demonstrating that benign overfitting can occur under specific assumptions—such as a bi-level ensemble structure and labels depending 1-sparsely on representations—we extract more general insights into when and how benign overfitting arises. Specifically, we identify a single key quantity driving benign overfitting in W2SG:
\|{\bm{P}}_{\text{s}}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}_{\text{w}})\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}\|
. When this quantity is small, the strong model can avoid repeating the weak model’s mistakes—regardless of the extent of overfitting—thereby achieving error mitigation. This precise mechanism was not revealed in prior work.

4.2
Instantiation of Theorem
4.1
on a toy example

We present a concrete example of the scenario in Theorem
4.1
to demonstrate the realizability of the conditions.
While more complex examples could be constructed, we focus on a simple one to succinctly illustrate the core ideas.

Example 4.2
.

The label is a Gaussian:
y\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
. Given
({\bm{x}},y)
, the weak model’s representation is

h_{\text{w}}({\bm{x}})=[(\sqrt{\eta}~y+\sqrt{1-\eta}~\zeta)~~~{\bm{\xi}}_{\text{w}}^{\top}]^{\top}
, where
\eta\in(0,1)
is some constant,
\zeta\!\sim\!{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
and
{\bm{\xi}}_{\text{w}}\!\sim\!{\mathcal{N}}(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d-1}{\bm{I}})
are both independently drawn. The strong model’s representation is

h_{\text{s}}({\bm{x}})=[y~~~{\bm{\xi}}_{\text{s}}^{\top}]^{\top}
,
where
{\bm{\xi}}_{\text{s}}\!\sim\!{\mathcal{N}}(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d-1}{\bm{I}})
independently. The scalings satisfy
\tilde{n}=\Theta(\hat{n})=\omega(1)
,
d=\omega(\hat{n}^{2})
, and
\sigma^{2}=o(\hat{n})
but
\neq 0
. Additionally,
\beta_{\text{s}}=o(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\hat{n}})
and
\beta_{\text{w}}=o(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\hat{n}})
.

Here, the weak model’s first coordinate carries a signal about the label
y
, but corrupted by noise
\zeta
, with
\eta
controlling the signal strength (i.e., with SNR
\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}
). The strong model’s first coordinate carries a perfect signal about
y
. The remaining coordinates in both models are high-dimensional random noise. Both models’ representations are special cases of Example
3.5
and are therefore
(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\hat{n}},\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tilde{n}})
decomposable.

Corollary 4.3
.

Benign overfitting occurs in Example
4.2
. Specifically, w.h.p., (1) The weak model’s errors on both
\hat{{\mathcal{D}}}
and the population are
(1\!-\!\eta)\!\pm\!o(1)
. (2) The W2S model overfits the weak model’s outputs on
\hat{{\mathcal{D}}}
, achieving a training loss of
o(1)
. (3) However, compared to the weak model, the W2S model achieves a smaller test error:
\text{Err}_{\text{w2s}}\!=\!(1\!-\!\eta)^{2}\pm o(1)
.

For instance, if
\eta\!=\!0.6
, then
\text{Err}_{\text{w}}\approx 0.4
, while
\text{Err}_{\text{w2s}}\approx 0.16
, despite nearly perfect overfitting on
\hat{{\mathcal{D}}}
.

4.3
A closer look at error propagation

We provide a rough derivation of the W2S error (with details in App.
B.3.2
), illustrating which errors are replicated and which are corrected (overfitted but benignly) by the W2S model, and how representations determine this.

The principal representations for both models are simply at their first coordinates. Thus, the spans of their principal kernels are one-dimensional. Let
\hat{{\bm{\zeta}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\hat{n}}
denote the vector collecting the
\zeta
values on
\hat{{\mathcal{D}}}
, i.e.,
\hat{{\bm{\zeta}}}=[\hat{\zeta}_{1},\dots,\hat{\zeta}_{\hat{n}}]^{\top}
. Similarly, define
\hat{{\bm{y}}}=[\hat{y}_{1},\dots,\hat{y}_{\hat{n}}]^{\top}
. We can approximate the projection matrices as:

{\bm{P}}_{\text{w}}\approx\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{q}}}\hat{{\bm{q}}}^{\top}
and
{\bm{P}}_{\text{s}}\approx\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}^{\top}
, where
\hat{{\bm{q}}}=\sqrt{\eta}\hat{{\bm{y}}}+\sqrt{1-\eta}\hat{{\bm{\zeta}}}
.
Note that vectors
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}
and
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{{\bm{\zeta}}}
are almost orthogonal as the corresponding random variables are uncorrelated:
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}^{\top}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{{\bm{\zeta}}}=\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\sum_{i}\hat{y_{i}}\hat{\zeta}_{i}\approx\mathbb{E}[y\zeta]=0
. Let
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\text{w}}
be the vector whose
i
-th element is the weak model’s error on data point
(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i},\hat{y}_{i})
. By Lemma
A.12
, we can approximate
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\text{w}}
as:

{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\text{w}}\approx({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}_{\text{w}})\hat{{\bm{y}}}\approx(1-\eta)\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}-\sqrt{\eta(1-\eta)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{{\bm{\zeta}}}

The strong ceiling model’s error
\text{Err}_{\text{sc}}\approx 0
as its representations directly encode
y
in the first coordinate. Thus,
\text{Err}_{\text{w2s}}\approx\textbf{PredGap}{}
. By Thm
3.8
,
\textbf{PredGap}{}\approx{\bm{P}}_{\text{s}}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\text{w}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\text{Err}_{\text{w2s}}\approx\underbrace{\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}^{\top}(1-\eta)\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}}_{\textbf{\footnotesize replicated}}~~\underbrace{-\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}^{\top}\sqrt{\eta(1-\eta)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{{\bm{\zeta}}}}_{\textbf{\footnotesize avoided}\text{; $\approx 0$ since $\hat{{\bm{\zeta}}}\perp\hat{{\bm{y}}}$ almost}}\vskip-14.22636pt

The first term of the weak model’s error,
(1-\eta)\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}
, aligns with
{\bm{P}}_{\text{s}}
which spans the strong model’s principal kernel, and is therefore replicated by the W2S model. The second term,
-\sqrt{\eta(1-\eta)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{{\bm{\zeta}}}
, is orthogonal to
{\bm{P}}_{\text{s}}
and thus mitigated. Notably,
-\sqrt{\eta(1-\eta)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{{\bm{\zeta}}}
aligns with the strong model’s non-principal kernel, which is highly isotropic (
\gamma_{\text{s}}=\omega(\delta_{\text{s}})
), causing the corresponding errors to appear mimicked by the W2S model during finetuning. However, they do not manifest at test time. In other words, only errors within the span of the strong model’s principal kernel are overfitted harmfully, while overfitting elsewhere remains benign.

measured as classification error.
(2) The form of finetuning.
Thm.
3.8
assumes that finetuning involves training a function on fixed representations. However, in Exp. III, the entire LLM is finetuned. Despite the complex training dynamics in this scenario, a strong correlation between
\text{Err}_{\text{w2s}}
and
\lVert{\bm{P}}_{\text{s}}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}_{\text{w}})\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
is still observed when activation maps are heuristically used as representations. These results underscore the broad applicability of our conclusion.

Capturing W2SG beyond model size.
Smaller weak models can sometimes achieve better
\text{Err}_{\text{w2s}}
than larger ones. For example, in Exp. I, the leftmost yellow point (size 64) outperforms the rightmost teal point (size 128) in Fig.
2
, likely because these smaller models were pretrained for more epochs
(recall that we have 150 models span different combinations of sizes and pretraining epochs), resulting in better representations.
Similarly, in Exp. III, the middle column of Fig.
5
shows a poor correlation between
\text{Err}_{\text{w2s}}
and size for models with dimension
\leq 8000
. Testing another dimension-based metric—the dimension of approximated principal representations—also reveals weak correlation with
\text{Err}_{\text{w2s}}
(last column of Fig.
5
). This underscore the complexity of predicting W2SG performance, as larger models or higher representation dimensions do not guarantee better results. Factors such as the pretraining recipe, the quality and relevance of the pretraining data, etc., all contribute to the final outcome. However, even in these cases,
\lVert{\bm{P}}_{\text{s}}({\bm{I}}-{\bm{P}}_{\text{w}})\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
consistently captures the trend in
\text{Err}_{\text{w2s}}
(Fig.
2
and the first column of Fig.
5
), demonstrating its robustness as a metric that surpasses simple dimensional measures and provides meaningful insights for W2SG.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we show that W2SG can be characterized using kernels derived from the principal components of weak and strong models’ representations. The theory is applicable to a wide range of representation distributions, provides insights into how models’ internal structures influence error correction and the conditions for benign overfitting. Additionally, it offers a label-free metric for predicting W2SG performance, validated through experiments on diverse datasets and LLMs.

Impact Statement

We see positive societal impacts in our work as it advances the understanding of Weak-to-Strong Generalization, a crucial problem for aligning superhuman AI in the future. Our results could enhance transparency in AI systems’ behavior through analysis of their internal structures and contribute to the broader goal of improving AI safety and reliability.

\displaystyle\textbf{intdim}({\bm{\Sigma}})=\frac{\Tr({\bm{\Sigma}})}{\lVert{\bm{\Sigma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}\leq\frac{B}{\tau}=O(B),

(26)

where the last step holds because
\tau=\lVert{\bm{\Sigma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=\Theta(1)
. Given that
n^{1-c}=\omega(B\log(q))
, we then have
n^{1-c}=\omega(q\log(q))
, as mentioned in the remark.

Additionally, since
\textbf{intdim}({\bm{\Sigma}})\geq 1
, Equation
26
also implies

\displaystyle B\geq\tau\quad\text{and}\quad B=\Omega(1),

(27)

which we will use later.

Next, we introduce the following two lemmas, both of which rely on the matrix Bernstein inequality with intrinsic dimension, as stated in Theorem 7.3.1 of
(
Tropp et al., 2015
)
.

Lemma C.1
.

With a probability of at least
1-8q\exp(\frac{-0.5\hat{n}^{1-c}}{B\tau+(B+\tau)/3})=1-o(1)
, the following holds

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}-{\bm{\Sigma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\hat{n}^{-0.5c}.

The same conclusion applies to
\tilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}
as well.

Proof.

We prove the result for
\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}
; the result for
\tilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}
can be proved in the same way.
Define
{\bm{S}}_{i}=\frac{1}{\hat{n}}(\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}^{\top}-{\bm{\Sigma}})
. The random matrices
{\bm{S}}_{i}
are independent, identically distributed, and centered. Their norms are bounded as follows

\displaystyle\lVert{\bm{S}}_{i}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{1}{\hat{n}}(\lVert\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}^{\top}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}+\lVert{\bm{\Sigma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}})\leq\frac{B+\tau}{\hat{n}}\coloneqq L.

Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\bm{S}}_{i}^{2}=\frac{1}{\hat{n}^{2}}\mathbb{E}(\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}^{\top}-{\bm{\Sigma}})^{2}=\frac{1}{\hat{n}^{2}}\mathbb{E}(\|\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}\|^{2}\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}^{\top}-2{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2}+{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2})\preccurlyeq\frac{1}{\hat{n}^{2}}\mathbb{E}(B\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}^{\top}-{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2})\preccurlyeq\frac{B}{\hat{n}^{2}}{\bm{\Sigma}}

Define
{\bm{Z}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}{\bm{S}}_{i}
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}\preccurlyeq\mathbb{E}{\bm{Z}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}\mathbb{E}{\bm{S}}_{i}^{2}\preccurlyeq\frac{B}{\hat{n}}{\bm{\Sigma}}\coloneqq{\bm{V}}

{\bm{V}}
’s norm can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\lVert{\bm{V}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=\frac{B\lVert{\bm{\Sigma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}{\hat{n}}=\frac{B\tau}{\hat{n}}\coloneqq v

Define
d=\textbf{intdim}(\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{V}}&0\\
0&{\bm{V}}\end{bmatrix})
, which can be simplified as:

\displaystyle d=2\frac{\Tr(\frac{B}{\hat{n}}{\bm{\Sigma}})}{\lVert\frac{B}{\hat{n}}{\bm{\Sigma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}}=2\textbf{intdim}(\frac{B}{\hat{n}}{\bm{\Sigma}})=2\textbf{intdim}({\bm{\Sigma}})=2q.

Now we are ready to apply Theorem 7.3.1 of
(
Tropp et al., 2015
)
. It leads to the conclusion that, for any
t\geq\sqrt{v}+L/3
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\lVert{\bm{Z}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\geq t\}\leq

\displaystyle 4d\exp{(\frac{-t^{2}/2}{v+Lt/3})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 8q\exp(\frac{-t^{2}/2}{\frac{B\tau}{\hat{n}}+\frac{B+\tau}{\hat{n}}t/3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 8q\exp(\frac{-\hat{n}t^{2}/2}{B\tau+(B+\tau)t/3})

(28)

By assumption:

\displaystyle n^{1-c}=\omega(B\log q)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle n^{1-c}=\omega(((\tau+1/3)B+\tau/3)\log q)\quad\quad\text{because $\tau=O(1)$}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{n}^{1-c}}{(\tau+1/3)B+\tau/3}=\omega(\log q)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle 0.5\frac{\hat{n}^{1-c}}{(\tau+1/3)B+\tau/3}=\omega(\log q)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\exp\left(\frac{0.5\hat{n}^{1-c}}{(\tau+1/3)B+\tau/3}\right)=\omega(q)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle q\exp\left(\frac{-0.5\hat{n}^{1-c}}{(\tau+1/3)B+\tau/3}\right)=o(1)

(29)

Therefore, we set the value of
t
to
\hat{n}^{-0.5c}=o(1)
in Equation
28
. It is easy to verify that
\hat{n}^{-0.5c}\geq\sqrt{v}+L/3
. Substituting, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\lVert{\bm{Z}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\geq\hat{n}^{-0.5c}\}\leq

\displaystyle 4d\exp{(\frac{-t^{2}/2}{v+Lt/3})}\leq 8q\exp(\frac{-\hat{n}t^{2}/2}{B\tau+(B+\tau)t/3})=

\displaystyle 8q\exp(\frac{-0.5\hat{n}^{1-c}}{B\tau+(B+\tau)\hat{n}^{-0.5c}/3})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 8q\exp(\frac{-0.5\hat{n}^{1-c}}{B\tau+(B+\tau)/3})\quad\quad\text{because $\hat{n}^{-0.5c}\leq 1$}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle o(1)\quad\quad\text{by Equation \ref{eq: qexp}}.

Since
{\bm{Z}}=\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}-{\bm{\Sigma}}
, restating the above, we have that with a probability of at least
1-8q\exp(\frac{-0.5\hat{n}^{1-c}}{B\tau+(B+\tau)/3})
, the following holds

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}-{\bm{\Sigma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\hat{n}^{-0.5c}.

∎

Lemma C.2
.

With a probability of at least
1-(q+4)\exp(\frac{-0.5\hat{n}^{1-c}}{4BC+\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{BC}})=1-o(1)
, the following holds

\displaystyle\|\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}y_{i}-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{r}}y]\|\leq\hat{n}^{-0.5c}.

The same conclusion applies to
\frac{1}{\tilde{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{n}}\tilde{{\bm{r}}}_{i}y_{i}
as well.

Proof.

We prove the result for
\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}y_{i}
; the result for
\frac{1}{\tilde{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{n}}\tilde{{\bm{r}}}_{i}y_{i}
can be proved in the same way. Define
{\bm{S}}_{i}=\frac{1}{\hat{n}}(\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}y-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{r}}y])
. The random matrices (vectors)
{\bm{S}}_{i}
are independent, identically distributed, and centered. Their norms are bounded as follows

\displaystyle\|{\bm{S}}_{i}\|\leq\frac{1}{\hat{n}}(\|\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}y\|+\|\mathbb{E}[{\bm{r}}y]\|)\leq\frac{1}{\hat{n}}(\|\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}\||y|+\mathbb{E}[\|{\bm{r}}\||y|])\leq\frac{2}{\hat{n}}\sqrt{BC}\coloneqq L.

(30)

Define
{\bm{Z}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}{\bm{S}}_{i}
. We analyze the semidefinite upper bounds for the variances
\mathbb{E}{\bm{Z}}{\bm{Z}}^{\top}
and
\mathbb{E}{\bm{Z}}^{\top}{\bm{Z}}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\bm{Z}}{\bm{Z}}^{\top}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}\mathbb{E}{\bm{S}}_{i}{\bm{S}}_{i}^{\top}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hat{n}^{2}}(\mathbb{E}y_{i}^{2}\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{r}}y]\mathbb{E}[{\bm{r}}y]^{\top})

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hat{n}^{2}}\mathbb{E}y_{i}^{2}\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}^{\top}

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq

\displaystyle\frac{C}{\hat{n}^{2}}{\bm{\Sigma}}\coloneqq{\bm{V}}_{1}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\bm{Z}}^{\top}{\bm{Z}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}\mathbb{E}{\bm{S}}_{i}^{\top}{\bm{S}}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{n}\mathbb{E}\|{\bm{S}}_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{4}{\hat{n}}BC\coloneqq{\bm{V}}_{2}\quad\quad\text{by Equation \ref{eq: bound_S_L}}.

Define
v=\max(\lVert{\bm{V}}_{1}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}},\lVert{\bm{V}}_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}})
. It can be simplified as follows

\displaystyle v=

\displaystyle\max(\lVert\frac{C}{\hat{n}}{\bm{\Sigma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}},\frac{4}{\hat{n}}BC)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4}{\hat{n}}BC\quad\quad\text{because $B\geq\lVert{\bm{\Sigma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}$ as in Equation \ref{eq: B_tau_relation}}.

Define
d=\textbf{intdim}(\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{V}}_{1}&0\\
0&{\bm{V}}_{2}\end{bmatrix})
, which can be simplified as

\displaystyle d=

\displaystyle\textbf{intdim}(\begin{bmatrix}\frac{C}{\hat{n}}{\bm{\Sigma}}&0\\
0&\frac{4}{\hat{n}}BC\end{bmatrix})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Tr(\frac{C}{\hat{n}}{\bm{\Sigma}})+\frac{4}{\hat{n}}BC}{\max(\lVert\frac{C}{\hat{n}}{\bm{\Sigma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}},\frac{4}{\hat{n}}BC)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Tr(\frac{C}{\hat{n}}{\bm{\Sigma}})+\frac{4}{\hat{n}}BC}{\frac{4}{\hat{n}}BC}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Tr(\frac{C}{\hat{n}}{\bm{\Sigma}})}{\frac{4}{\hat{n}}BC}+1

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle q/4+1\quad\quad\text{because $B\geq\tau$ as in Equation \ref{eq: B_tau_relation} and $\frac{\Tr({\bm{\Sigma}})}{\tau}=q$ }.

Applying Theorem 7.3.1 of
(
Tropp et al., 2015
)
, we have that for any
t\geq\sqrt{v}+L/3
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\|{\bm{Z}}\|\geq t\}\leq

\displaystyle 4d\exp{(\frac{-t^{2}/2}{v+Lt/3})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(q+4)\exp(\frac{-t^{2}/2}{\frac{4}{\hat{n}}BC+\frac{2\sqrt{BC}}{\hat{n}}t/3}).

(31)

By assumption:

\displaystyle n^{1-c}=\omega(B\log q)

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle n^{1-c}=\omega(B\log(q+4))

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle n^{1-c}=\omega((4BC+\frac{2\sqrt{BC}}{3})\log(q+4))\quad\quad\text{because $C=\Theta(1)$, and $B=\Omega(1)$ as in Equation \ref{eq: B_tau_relation} }

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\frac{0.5n^{1-c}}{(4BC+\frac{2\sqrt{BC}}{3})}=\omega(\log(q+4))

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle(q+4)\exp\left(\frac{-0.5n^{1-c}}{4BC+\frac{2\sqrt{BC}}{3}}\right)=o(1).

Therefore, we set the value of
t
to
\hat{n}^{-0.5c}=o(1)
in Equation
31
. It is easy to verify that
\hat{n}^{-0.5c}\geq\sqrt{v}+L/3
. Substituting, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\lVert{\bm{Z}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\geq\hat{n}^{-0.5c}\}\leq

\displaystyle(q+4)\exp(\frac{-0.5n^{-c}}{\frac{4}{\hat{n}}BC+\frac{2\sqrt{BC}}{\hat{n}}\hat{n}^{-0.5c}/3})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(q+4)\exp(\frac{-0.5n^{-c}}{\frac{4}{\hat{n}}BC+\frac{2\sqrt{BC}}{\hat{n}}/3})\quad\quad\text{because $\hat{n}^{-0.5c}\leq 1$}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(q+4)\exp(\frac{-0.5n^{1-c}}{4BC+2\sqrt{BC}/3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle o(1).

∎

Now, we are ready to show that Example
3.4
satisfies Definition
3.3
. We let
{\mathcal{V}}
be the entire representation space. Then,
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
is the zero space
{\mathbf{0}}
. In this case, the conditions
Kernel-wise
\delta
-isotropy on
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
,
Small cross-sample inner-product on
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
, and
Diminishing population covariance on
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
trivially hold. Thus, we only need to prove that
Boundedness
and
Concentration on
{\mathcal{V}}
hold.

We let
\delta=n^{-0.1c}
and
\gamma=0
. First, note that
\delta^{2}=n^{-0.2c}\geq\hat{n}^{-0.2c}
. Then, by Lemma
C.1
, we obtain that
\lVert\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}-{\bm{\Sigma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\hat{n}^{-0.5c}=o(\hat{n}^{-0.2c})=o(\delta^{2})=o(\gamma^{2}+\delta^{2}+\rho)
with probability
1-o(1)
. Similarly, we can show that
\lVert\tilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}-{\bm{\Sigma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=o(\gamma^{2}+\delta^{2}+\rho)
with probability
1-o(1)
.

Next, since
\delta=n^{-0.1c}\geq\hat{n}^{-0.1c}
, applying Lemma
C.2
gives us
\left|\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{r}}}_{i}y_{i}-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{r}}y]\right|\leq\hat{n}^{-0.5c}=o(\hat{n}^{-0.1c})=o(\delta)=o(\gamma+\delta+\rho)
with probability
1-o(1)
. Similarly, the same conclusion can be shown for
\frac{1}{\tilde{n}}\tilde{{\bm{r}}}_{i}y_{i}
.

Note that there are only four events above, so the probability that all of them occur remains
1-o(1)
. To now, we have proved
Concentration on
{\mathcal{V}}
.

Finally, regarding
Boundedness
,
\lVert{\bm{\Sigma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=\Theta(1)
is directly given in the assumption. Keeping in mind that
{\mathcal{V}}
is the entire space, the conditions regarding covariance matrices are readily satisfied through the triangle inequality. For example:
\lVert\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\lVert\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}-{\bm{\Sigma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}+\lVert{\bm{\Sigma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=o(1)+\Theta(1)=O(1)
. The other two conditions are directly implied by the boundedness of each
y
.

Example

Muthukumar et al., 2021
;
Pezeshki et al., 2022

)
. Furthermore, Example
3.5
also subsumes the sparse coding model as a special case, which has its roots in computer vision
(

;
Foldiak, 2003
;
Olshausen & Field, 2004
;
Yang et al., 2009
;
Mairal et al., 2014
;
Papyan et al., 2017
)
, has been used to model language data
(
Arora et al., 2018
)
, and has been extensively employed in recent theoretical studies
(

;
Allen-Zhu & Li, 2020
;

;

;

)
.

In the following proof, we start with a simple case where the data are Gaussian. We then extend the result to sub-Gaussian data by replacing the technical lemmas for Gaussian data with appropriate alternatives.

C.2.1
Over-Parameterized Gaussian Data

Suppose that we have
\hat{{\bm{R}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times\hat{n}},\tilde{{\bm{R}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times\tilde{n}}
with
\hat{n}=\Theta(\tilde{n})
and
d=\omega(\hat{n}^{2})
drawn from a high-dimensional
{\bm{\Sigma}}
-Gaussian ensemble with zero mean, where

\displaystyle{\bm{\Sigma}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{I}}_{k}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d-k}{\bm{I}}_{d-k}\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{I}}_{k}={\bm{\Lambda}}^{\prime}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}}_{{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\prime}}+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d-k}{\bm{I}}_{d-k}={\bm{\Lambda}}^{\prime\prime}\end{bmatrix}}_{{\bm{\Sigma}}^{\prime\prime}},\quad\text{with }\sigma^{2}=O(\hat{n}),\hat{n}=\omega(k^{2}).

(32)

Here the two data splits have comparable sizes, and the model is heavily over-parameterized.
By splitting the matrix
\hat{{\bm{R}}}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{{\bm{F}}}\\
\hat{{\bm{A}}}\end{bmatrix}
, where
\hat{{\bm{F}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times\hat{n}}
corresponds to the
k
principal features (which form the space
{\mathcal{V}}
) and
\hat{{\bm{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d-k)\times\hat{n}}
corresponds to the rest (which form the space
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
), we can write the sample covariance matrix as

\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}=\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{R}}}\hat{{\bm{R}}}^{\top}=\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{{\bm{F}}}\hat{{\bm{F}}}^{\top}&\hat{{\bm{F}}}\hat{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}\\
\hat{{\bm{A}}}\hat{{\bm{F}}}^{\top}&\hat{{\bm{A}}}\hat{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}.

We note that
d-k=\omega(\hat{n}^{2})
, and the corresponding labels have bounded mean and variance. The same decomposition applies to
\tilde{{\bm{R}}}
. Note that here
{\bm{U}}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{I}}_{k}\\
\mathbf{0}_{(d-k)\times k}\end{bmatrix}
and
{\bm{U}}^{\prime\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{k\times(d-k)}\\
{\bm{I}}_{d-k}\end{bmatrix}
allow us to define the projection matrices
{\bm{U}}^{\prime}{\bm{U}}^{\prime\top}
and
{\bm{U}}^{\prime\prime}{\bm{U}}^{\prime\prime\top}
on
{\mathcal{V}}
and
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
respectively.

In this section, we show that our assumptions hold in the above setting with
\delta=0
and
\hat{\gamma}=\sigma^{2}/\hat{n},\tilde{\gamma}=\sigma^{2}/\tilde{n}
.
We only prove for
\hat{{\bm{R}}}
whenever the same proof can be easily applied to
\tilde{{\bm{R}}}
.

First, let us introduce the following Lemmas:

Lemma C.3

(Restatement of Example 6.2 in
(
Wainwright, 2019
)
)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
be a random matrix with i.i.d. entries drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
(that is a
{\bm{\Sigma}}
-Gaussian ensemble with
{\bm{\Sigma}}={\bm{I}}_{d}
). Then with probability at least
1-2e^{-n\delta^{2}/2}
for some
\delta>0
, the following inequality holds:

\lVert\frac{1}{n}{\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{T}-{\bm{I}}_{d}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 2\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}+\delta\right)+\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}+\delta\right)^{2}.

Lemma C.4
.

Consider two independently sampled Gaussian matrices where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times n}
has columns
{\bm{a}}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{1}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{d_{1}})
and
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}\times n}
has columns
{\bm{b}}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{2}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{d_{2}})
. Then for some
\frac{1}{d_{1}d_{2}}>\delta>0
and constant
C
, with probability at least
1-d_{1}d_{2}\delta
, we have

\frac{1}{n}\lVert{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}^{\top}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}{n}\sqrt{Cd_{1}d_{2}n\log(\frac{2}{\delta})}.

Proof.

Let
{\bm{Q}}={\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}^{T}
. Then each entry of
{\bm{Q}}
is an inner product
{\bm{Q}}_{ij}={\bm{a}}_{i}\cdot{\bm{b}}_{j}
, where
{\bm{a}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the
i
-th row of
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{b}}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the
j
-th row of
{\bm{B}}
. Since each entry of
{\bm{a}}_{i}
is
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{1}^{2})
and each entry of
{\bm{b}}_{j}
is
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{2}^{2})
, by Lemma 4 from
(
Shen et al., 2022
)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
(taking
\frac{1}{d_{1}d_{2}}>\delta>0
), for some constant
C_{ij}
,

{\bm{Q}}_{ij}^{2}=({\bm{a}}_{i}\cdot{\bm{b}}_{j})^{2}\leq C_{ij}\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}n\log(2/\delta^{\prime}).

We define
C=\max\left\{C_{ij}:1\leq i\leq d_{1},1\leq j\leq d_{2}\right\}
. Now we bound the operator norm with

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\lVert{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}^{\top}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{1}{n}\|{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}^{\top}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\|{\bm{Q}}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{\sum_{1\leq i\leq d_{1},1\leq j\leq d_{2}}{{\bm{Q}}_{ij}^{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{\sum_{1\leq i\leq d_{1},1\leq j\leq d_{2}}{C_{ij}\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}n\log(2/\delta)}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sqrt{Cd_{1}d_{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}n\log(2/\delta)}=\frac{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}{n}\sqrt{Cd_{1}d_{2}n\log(2/\delta)}

with probability at least
1-d_{1}d_{2}\delta
since the inequality has to hold for each entry.
∎

We now prove that the example satisfies the five aspects of the definition:

1.

Boundedness
:

First, we have
\lVert{\bm{\Sigma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=1=O(1)
from its definition, and

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}-{\bm{\Sigma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{F}}}\hat{{\bm{F}}}^{\top}-{\bm{I}}_{k}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{A}}}\hat{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d-k}{\bm{I}}_{d-k}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\text{op}}+\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\hat{{\bm{F}}}\hat{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}\\
\hat{{\bm{A}}}\hat{{\bm{F}}}^{\top}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\text{op}}.

(33)

By Lemma
C.3
, we take
\delta_{1}=\hat{n}^{-1/4}
and have that with probability at least
1-2e^{-\hat{n}\delta_{1}^{2}/2}=1-2e^{-\sqrt{\hat{n}}/2}=1-o(1)
,

\displaystyle\lVert\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{F}}}\hat{{\bm{F}}}^{\top}-{\bm{I}}_{k}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{k}{\hat{n}}}+\frac{2}{\hat{n}^{1/4}}+\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{\hat{n}}}+\frac{1}{\hat{n}^{1/4}}\right)^{2}=o(1)\quad\text{since $\hat{n}\gg k$}.

As
\hat{{\bm{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d-k)\times\hat{n}}
is sampled from
\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d-k}{\bm{I}}_{d-k}
,
\frac{\sqrt{d-k}}{\sigma}\hat{{\bm{A}}}
is sampled from
{\bm{I}}_{d-k}
. With this scaling, similarly, Lemma
C.3
implies that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\left(\frac{\sqrt{d-k}}{\sigma}\hat{{\bm{A}}}\right)\left(\frac{\sqrt{d-k}}{\sigma}\hat{{\bm{A}}}\right)^{\top}-{\bm{I}}_{d-k}\right\|_{\text{op}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{d-k}{\hat{n}\sigma^{2}}\hat{{\bm{A}}}\hat{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}-{\bm{I}}_{d-k}\right\|_{\text{op}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{d-k}{\hat{n}}}+\frac{2}{\hat{n}^{1/4}}+\left(\sqrt{\frac{d-k}{\hat{n}}}+\frac{1}{\hat{n}^{1/4}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\iff\left\|\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{A}}}\hat{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d-k}{\bm{I}}_{d-k}\right\|_{\text{op}}\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d-k}\left[2\sqrt{\frac{d-k}{\hat{n}}}+\frac{2}{\hat{n}^{1/4}}+\left(\sqrt{\frac{d-k}{\hat{n}}}+\frac{1}{\hat{n}^{1/4}}\right)^{2}\right]=O(1)\quad\text{as $\sigma^{2}=O(\hat{n})$}.

We have bounded the first term on the right side of Eq.
33
and have that
\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{{\bm{F}}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
and
\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{{\bm{A}}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
are
O(1)
. It follows that

\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\hat{{\bm{F}}}\hat{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}\\
\hat{{\bm{A}}}\hat{{\bm{F}}}^{\top}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\text{op}}=\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\lVert\hat{{\bm{F}}}\hat{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=O(1)\Longrightarrow\lVert\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}-{\bm{\Sigma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=O(1).

Hence,
\lVert\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=O(1)
directly follows from
\lVert{\bm{\Sigma}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=O(1)
.

Now we consider
\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\|\hat{{\bm{y}}}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\sum_{i=0}^{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{i}^{2}
, where
\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{i}
represents the
i
-th entry of the vector. Since the label has bounded population variance
O(1)
, the i.i.d assumption implies

\mathrm{Var}(\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\sum_{i=0}^{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{i}^{2})=\frac{1}{\hat{n}^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{\hat{n}}\mathrm{Var}(\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{i}^{2})=\frac{1}{\hat{n}^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{\hat{n}}O(1)=O(\frac{1}{\hat{n}}).

Then by Chebyshev’s inequality, for any
\epsilon>0
and some constant
C_{1}
, we let
z=\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\|\hat{{\bm{y}}}\|^{2}
for simplicity and then have

P\left(|z-\mathbb{E}[z]|>\epsilon\right)\leq\frac{\mathrm{Var}(z)}{\epsilon^{2}}\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\hat{n}\epsilon^{2}}.

We take
\epsilon=\hat{n}^{-1/4}
. Then with probability at least
1-\frac{C_{1}}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}=1-o(1)
,

|\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\|\hat{{\bm{y}}}\|^{2}-\mathrm{Var}({\bm{y}}_{i})|=o(1)\Longrightarrow\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\|\hat{{\bm{y}}}\|^{2}=O(1)\quad\text{since the variance of the label is bounded.}

2.

Concentration on
{\mathcal{V}}
:

With
{\bm{U}}^{\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{I}}_{k}\\
\mathbf{0}_{(d-k)\times k}\end{bmatrix}
preserving only the first
k
components, we have from above that with probability at least
1-o(1)
,

\lVert{\bm{U}}^{\prime\top}\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}{\bm{U}}^{\prime}-{\bm{\Lambda}}^{\prime}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=\lVert\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{F}}}\hat{{\bm{F}}}^{\top}-{\bm{I}}_{k}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=o(1).

Now we consider

\|\frac{1}{\hat{n}}{\bm{U}}^{\prime\top}\hat{{\bm{R}}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{U}}^{\prime\top}{\bm{r}}y]\|=\|\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{F}}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{f}}y]\|,

where
{\bm{f}}={\bm{U}}^{\prime}{\bm{r}}
. We define a new random variable
{\bm{z}}={\bm{f}}y
and its sample mean
\hat{{\bm{Z}}}=\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{F}}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. We first show that the variance of each entry of
\hat{{\bm{Z}}}
is of magnitude
\sim\frac{1}{\hat{n}}
:

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{{\bm{Z}}}_{i})=\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\hat{n}}\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{F}}}_{ij}\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{j}\right)=\frac{1}{\hat{n}^{2}}\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{F}}}_{ij}\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{j}\right)\quad\forall i=1,\cdots,k.

For each term in the summation,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\hat{{\bm{F}}}_{ij}\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{j})=\mathbb{E}[(\hat{{\bm{F}}}_{ij}\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{j})^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[\hat{{\bm{F}}}_{ij}\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{j}]^{2}=O(1)

since
\hat{{\bm{F}}}_{ij}
and
\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{j}
are both bounded. By the i.i.d assumption,

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{{\bm{Z}}}_{i})=\frac{1}{\hat{n}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{\hat{n}}O(1)=O\left(\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\right).

By Chebyshev’s inequality, for any
\epsilon>0
and some constant
C_{2}
,

P\left(\|\hat{{\bm{Z}}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}_{i}]\|>\epsilon\right)\leq\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\hat{{\bm{Z}}}_{i})}{\epsilon^{2}}\leq\frac{C_{2}}{\hat{n}\epsilon^{2}}

P\left(\|\hat{{\bm{Z}}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}_{i}]\|>\epsilon\quad\forall i=1,\cdots,k\right)\leq\frac{kC_{2}}{\hat{n}\epsilon^{2}}

Similarly, by choosing
\epsilon=\hat{n}^{-1/4}
, the probability of large deviation decays rapidly as:

P\left(\|\hat{{\bm{Z}}}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}_{i}]\|>\frac{1}{\hat{n}^{1/4}}\quad\forall i=1,\cdots,k\right)\leq\frac{kC_{2}}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}=o(1)\quad\text{since }\hat{n}=\omega(k^{2}).

This statement implies that with probability at least
1-o(1)
,

\|\hat{{\bm{Z}}}-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{z}}]\|=\|\frac{1}{\hat{n}}{\bm{U}}^{\prime\top}\hat{{\bm{R}}}\hat{{\bm{y}}}-\mathbb{E}[{\bm{U}}^{\prime\top}{\bm{r}}y]\|\leq\sqrt{\frac{k}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}}=o(1)=o(\gamma+\delta+\lambda_{\min}({\bm{\Lambda}}^{\prime}))

as we sum up the
k
terms. This shows that our setting satisfies the second part of the definition.

3.

Kernel-wise
\delta
-isotropy on
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
:

We define
{\bm{Z}}=\frac{\sqrt{d-k}}{\sigma}\hat{{\bm{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d-k)\times\hat{n}}
, which has standard normal entries. With the scaling, we plug in
{\bm{U}}^{\prime\prime}
,
\hat{\gamma}=\sigma^{2}/\hat{n}
and have

\displaystyle\lVert\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{R}}}^{\top}{\bm{U}}^{\prime\prime}{\bm{U}}^{\prime\prime\top}\hat{{\bm{R}}}-\hat{\gamma}{\bm{I}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=\lVert\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}\hat{{\bm{A}}}-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\hat{n}}{\bm{I}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\hat{n}}\lVert\frac{1}{d-k}{\bm{Z}}^{\top}{\bm{Z}}-{\bm{I}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}.

(34)

Now we apply Lemma
C.3
and have that with probability at least
1-2e^{-\hat{n}\delta_{2}^{2}/2}
for some
\delta_{2}>0
,

\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\hat{n}}\lVert\frac{1}{d-k}{\bm{Z}}^{\top}{\bm{Z}}-{\bm{I}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\hat{n}}\left[2\left(\sqrt{\frac{\hat{n}}{d-k}}+\delta_{2}\right)+\left(\sqrt{\frac{\hat{n}}{d-k}}+\delta_{2}\right)^{2}\right].

The rest follows similarly by taking
\delta_{2}=\hat{n}^{-1/4}
.

4.

Small cross-sample inner-product on
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
:

By
{\bm{U}}^{\prime\prime}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}_{k\times(d-k)}\\
{\bm{I}}_{d-k}\end{bmatrix}
and Lemma
C.4
with
\hat{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\hat{n}\times(d-k)}
and
\tilde{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{\tilde{n}\times(d-k)}
, each having
\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d-k})
entries, the target expression becomes

\displaystyle\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{{\bm{R}}}^{\top}{\bm{U}}^{\prime\prime}{\bm{U}}^{\prime\prime\top}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tilde{n}}}\tilde{{\bm{R}}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}\tilde{n}}}\lVert\hat{{\bm{A}}}^{\top}\tilde{{\bm{A}}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

(35)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}\tilde{n}}}\sqrt{C_{4}\hat{n}\tilde{n}\frac{\sigma^{4}}{(d-k)^{2}}(d-k)\log{(2/\delta_{3})}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{C_{4}\frac{\sigma^{4}}{d-k}\log{(2/\delta_{3})}}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\sqrt{C_{4}\log{(2/\delta_{3})}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{d-k}}

for some constant
C_{4}
and with probability at least
1-\hat{n}\tilde{n}\delta_{3}
for some
0<\delta_{3}<\frac{1}{\hat{n}\tilde{n}}
. We choose some
\delta_{3}=o(\frac{1}{\hat{n}\tilde{n}})
in this range and then have that with probability at least
1-o(1)
, the previous bound can be expressed as:

\sigma^{2}\sqrt{C_{4}\log{(2/\delta_{3})}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{d-k}}=\Theta\left(\sigma^{2}\sqrt{C_{4}\frac{\log(\hat{n}\tilde{n})}{d-k}}\right)=o(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\max\{\hat{n},\tilde{n}\}})=o(\gamma+\delta)

since
d-k=\omega(\hat{n})=\omega(\tilde{n})
.

5.

Diminishing population covariance on
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
:

By definition, it is trivial to see that

\lambda_{max}({\bm{\Lambda}}^{\prime\prime})=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d-k}=o(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\max\{\hat{n},\tilde{n}\}})=o(\gamma+\delta)

since
d-k=\omega(\hat{n})=\omega(\tilde{n})
.

C.2.2
Further Relaxation to Sub-Gaussian Data

Now, we consider the more general sub-Gaussian setting outlined in Example
3.5
. The population covariance is:

{\bm{\Sigma}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{I}}_{k}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d-k}{\bm{I}}_{d-k}\end{bmatrix},

where the top left block has a corresponding sub-Gaussian parameter of
\Theta(1)
and the rest has a parameter of
\Theta(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{d-k})
.

We adopt the following definitions from Chapter 2 of
(
Vershynin, 2018
)
for reference.

Definition C.5
.

A zero-mean random variable
X
is sub-Gaussian if there is a positive parameter
K_{g}
such that

\mathbb{E}[e^{X^{2}/K_{g}^{2}}]\leq 2.

Definition C.6
.

A zero-mean random variable
X
is sub-exponential if there is a positive parameter
K_{e}
such that

\mathbb{E}[e^{|X|/K_{e}}]\leq 2.

We can also define the following norms that give the sub-Gaussian or sub-exponential parameter:

\|X\|_{\psi_{2}}=\inf\{t>0:\mathbb{E}[e^{X^{2}/t^{2}}]\leq 2\}=K_{g}

\|X\|_{\psi_{1}}=\inf\{t>0:\mathbb{E}[e^{|X|/t}]\leq 2\}=K_{e}

Remark
.

There are many different characterizations for these two definitions, each with a different sub-Gaussian/sub-exponential parameter. A detailed summary can be found in Chapter 2 of
(
Vershynin, 2018
)
. Notably, these parameters differ from each other only by at most a constant factor.

Lemma C.7
.

(Extension of Lemma 4
(
Shen et al., 2022
)
to sub-Gaussian)
Consider high-dimensional independent sub-Gaussian vectors
{\bm{z}}_{1}
,
{\bm{z}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whose i.i.d. entries have variances
\sigma_{1}^{2}
,
\sigma_{2}^{2}
and sub-Gaussian parameters
\Theta(\sigma_{1})
,
\Theta(\sigma_{2})
respectively. Then for
\delta>0
such that
\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)}>\sqrt{cd}
for some constant
c
, there exists a constant
C
such that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

|{\bm{z}}_{1}\cdot{\bm{z}}_{2}|\leq C\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sqrt{d\log(2/\delta)}.

Proof.

We consider the product
{\bm{z}}_{1}\cdot{\bm{z}}_{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\bm{z}}_{1i}{\bm{z}}_{2i}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}
, where we define
a_{i}
for simplicity. It is a well-known result that the product of two sub-Gaussian random variables is sub-exponential. More precisely,

\|a_{i}\|_{\psi_{1}}\leq\|{\bm{z}}_{1i}\|_{\psi_{2}}\|{\bm{z}}_{2i}\|_{\psi_{2}}=C\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}.

By Bernstein’s inequality for sub-exponential functions (see Theorem 3.8.1
(
Vershynin, 2018
)
), this summation can be bounded as: for some constant
c>0
,

\displaystyle P\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}\right|\geq t\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left[-c\min\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\|a_{i}\|_{\psi_{1}}^{2}},\frac{t}{\max_{i}\|a_{i}\|_{\psi_{1}}}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left[-c\min\left\{\frac{t^{2}}{dC^{2}\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}},\frac{t}{C\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}\right\}\right]

Let
t=\frac{C}{\sqrt{c}}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sqrt{d\log(2/\delta)}
for some
\delta
that satisfies the condition
\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)}>\sqrt{cd}
(e.g.
\delta=1/d^{2}
). The probability statement becomes:

\displaystyle P\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}\right|\geq\frac{C}{\sqrt{c}}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sqrt{d\log(2/\delta)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left[-c\min\left\{\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{c},\sqrt{\frac{d\log(2/\delta)}{c}}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=2\exp\left[-\min\left\{\log(2/\delta),\sqrt{cd\log(2/\delta)}\right\}\right].

Since our choice of
\delta
ensures that the first quantity is smaller,

P\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}\right|\geq\frac{C}{\sqrt{c}}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sqrt{d\log(2/\delta)}\right)\leq\delta

In other words, letting
C^{\prime}=C/\sqrt{c}
, we have that with probability at least
1-\delta
,

|{\bm{z}}_{1}\cdot{\bm{z}}_{2}|\leq C^{\prime}\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sqrt{d\log(2/\delta)}.

∎

Now we are ready to show that our assumptions capture the setting in Section
C.2.1
but with sub-Gaussian data. That is, we now allow the data to have possibly even lighter tail than that of Gaussian. The proof can be easily replicated, as Chebyshev’s inequality still applies here and Lemmas
C.3
,
C.4
find the following “sub-Gaussian” alternatives, namely Lemmas
C.8
,
C.9
:

Lemma C.8
.

(Restatement of Theorem 6.5 in
(
Wainwright, 2019
)
)
Let
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
be a random sub-Gaussian matrix with parameter
K_{g}
and population covariance
{\bm{I}}_{d}
. Then for all
\delta\geq 0
, there are universal constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}
such that

\lVert\frac{1}{n}{\bm{X}}{\bm{X}}^{T}-{\bm{I}}_{d}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq K_{g}^{2}\left[C_{1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}+\frac{d}{n}\right)+\delta\right]

with probability at least
1-C_{2}e^{-C_{3}n\min\{\delta,\delta^{2}\}}
.

Lemma C.9
.

Consider two independently sampled row-wise sub-Gaussian matrices
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times n}
,
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}\times n}
that have i.i.d. entries with variances
\sigma_{1}^{2}
,
\sigma_{2}^{2}
respectively. Then for some
\frac{1}{d_{1}d_{2}}>\delta>0
and constant
C
, with probability at least
1-d_{1}d_{2}\delta
, we have

\frac{1}{n}\lVert{\bm{A}}{\bm{B}}^{\top}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\frac{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}{n}\sqrt{Cd_{1}d_{2}n\log(\frac{2}{\delta})}.

Proof.

The proof is the same as Lemma
C.4
except that we now use Lemma
C.7
to bound the squared value of each entry in the Frobenius norm.
∎

With these alternative extended results, the proof in Section
C.2.1
immediately generalizes to sub-Gaussian data. This extension potentially allows us to accommodate more realistic scenario and enhances the theoretical robustness of our assumptions. Sub-Gaussian distributions capture a wider class of data behaviors; for instance, the fact that bounded random variables are sub-Gaussian makes the theory more applicable to many real-world datasets, which naturally exhibit sub-Gaussian characteristics. In the following section, we show a general result that even more examples can be constructed.

C.3
Proof of Theorem
3.6

The intuition behind this theorem is that adding high-dimensional sub-Gaussian entries to the given representation preserves decomposbility while slightly modifying the parameters. Due to the orthogonality of
{\bm{M}}
and
{\bm{M}}^{\perp}
, we let
{\bm{U}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{M}}\quad{\bm{M}}^{\perp}\end{bmatrix}
and then
\alpha({\bm{x}})={\bm{U}}\begin{bmatrix}h({\bm{x}})\\
\xi({\bm{x}})\end{bmatrix}
; naturally, the column space of
{\bm{M}}
can be regarded as the subspace
{\mathcal{V}}
, and the column space of
{\bm{M}}^{\perp}
is
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
. Given that
h({\bm{x}})
’s representations are
(\delta,0,0)
-decomposable w.r.t.
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we now prove that the new representations are
(\delta,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\hat{n}},\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\tilde{n}})
-decomposable. Again we only present the proof for one data split whenever it can be replicated for the other.

For notation, we let
\gamma=\sigma^{2}/\max\{\hat{n},\tilde{n}\}
.

1.

Boundedness:

\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}\hat{y}_{i}^{2}=O(1)
follows from the previous proof using Chebyshev’s inequality. For the population covariance,

\displaystyle\lVert{\bm{\Sigma}}(\alpha)\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=\lVert\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}[\alpha({\bm{x}})\alpha({\bm{x}})^{\top}]\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}\begin{bmatrix}h({\bm{x}})h({\bm{x}})^{\top}&h({\bm{x}})\xi({\bm{x}})^{\top}\\
\xi({\bm{x}})h({\bm{x}})^{\top}&\xi({\bm{x}})\xi({\bm{x}})^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{op}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}\begin{bmatrix}h({\bm{x}})h({\bm{x}})^{\top}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\xi({\bm{x}})\xi({\bm{x}})^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{op}+\left\|\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}&h({\bm{x}})\xi({\bm{x}})^{\top}\\
\xi({\bm{x}})h({\bm{x}})^{\top}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{op}

(36)

We have that
\lVert\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}[h({\bm{x}})h({\bm{x}})^{\top}]\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=\lVert{\bm{\Sigma}}(h)\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=O(1)
by the
(\delta,0,0)
-decomposibility assumption on
h
’s representations. From the proof for sub-Gaussian data in Section
C.2.1
,
\lVert\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}[\xi({\bm{x}})\xi({\bm{x}})^{\top}]\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=\lVert{\bm{\Sigma}}(\xi)\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=O(1)
. These bound the first term on the RHS of Equation
36
.

By the definition of operator norm,

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}[h({\bm{x}})\xi({\bm{x}})^{\top}]\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=\sup_{\|{\bm{u}}\|=1}\sup_{\|{\bm{v}}\|=1}{\bm{u}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}[h({\bm{x}})\xi({\bm{x}})^{\top}]{\bm{v}}=\sup_{\|{\bm{u}}\|=1}\sup_{\|{\bm{v}}\|=1}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}[({\bm{u}}^{T}h({\bm{x}}))({\bm{v}}^{T}\xi({\bm{x}}))].

(37)

By Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we can bound this expectation as:

\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}[({\bm{u}}^{T}h({\bm{x}}))({\bm{v}}^{T}\xi({\bm{x}}))]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}[({\bm{u}}^{T}h({\bm{x}}))^{2}]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}[({\bm{v}}^{T}\xi({\bm{x}}))^{2}]},\text{ where}

\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}[({\bm{u}}^{T}h({\bm{x}}))^{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}[{\bm{u}}^{T}h({\bm{x}})h({\bm{x}})^{\top}{\bm{u}}]={\bm{u}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}[h({\bm{x}})h({\bm{x}})^{\top}]{\bm{u}}\leq\|{\bm{u}}\|^{2}\lVert{\bm{\Sigma}}(h)\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=O(1),

\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}[({\bm{v}}^{T}\xi({\bm{x}}))^{2}]=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}[{\bm{v}}^{T}\xi({\bm{x}})\xi({\bm{x}})^{\top}{\bm{v}}]={\bm{v}}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}[\xi({\bm{x}})\xi({\bm{x}})^{\top}]{\bm{v}}\leq\|{\bm{v}}\|^{2}\lVert{\bm{\Sigma}}(\xi)\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=O(1).

Combing these results, we have that Equation
37

=\lVert\mathbb{E}_{{\mathcal{D}}_{{\bm{x}}}}[h({\bm{x}})\xi({\bm{x}})^{\top}]\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=O(1)
, bounding the second term in Equation
36
. Hence,
\lVert{\bm{\Sigma}}(\alpha)\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=O(1)
.

Simiarly, we can prove for the empirical covariance:

\displaystyle\lVert\hat{{\bm{\Sigma}}}(\alpha)\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=\lVert\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}\alpha(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i})\alpha(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i})^{\top}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}h(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i})h(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i})^{\top}&\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}h(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i})\xi(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i})^{\top}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}\xi(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i})h(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i})^{\top}&\sum_{i=1}^{\hat{n}}\xi(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i})\xi(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i})^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\text{op}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{{\bm{H}}}\hat{{\bm{H}}}^{\top}&\hat{{\bm{H}}}\hat{\mathbf{\Xi}}^{\top}\\
\hat{\mathbf{\Xi}}\hat{{\bm{H}}}^{\top}&\hat{\mathbf{\Xi}}\hat{\mathbf{\Xi}}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{\text{op}},

where the
i
-th column of
\hat{\mathbf{\Xi}}
is
\xi(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i})
and the
i
-th column of
{\bm{H}}
is
h(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i})
.

The rest is straightforward: the assumption on
h
and the existing proof for sub-Gaussian data imply
\lVert\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{H}}}\hat{{\bm{H}}}^{\top}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=O(1)
and
\lVert\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{\mathbf{\Xi}}\hat{\mathbf{\Xi}}^{\top}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}=O(1)
. Hence,
\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{{\bm{H}}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
and
\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}}}\hat{\mathbf{\Xi}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
are
O(1)
, and we have
\lVert\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{H}}}\hat{\mathbf{\Xi}}^{\top}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}
is also O(1). These together bound the empirical covariance.

2.

Concentration on
{\mathcal{V}}
: Since
{\mathcal{V}}
corresponds to the representation space of
h({\bm{x}})
, this condition is automatically satisfied by the
(\delta,0,0)
-decomposibility assumption on
h
.

3.

Kernel-wise
\delta
-isotropy on
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
: In this setting, since
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
corresponds to the column space of
{\bm{M}}^{\perp}
(the high-dimensional sub-Gaussian part), we have

\lVert\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{K}}}({\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}}\alpha)\!-\!\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\hat{n}}{\bm{I}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\!=\lVert\frac{1}{\hat{n}}\hat{{\bm{K}}}(\xi)\!-\!\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\hat{n}}{\bm{I}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\!

By definition of the kernel matrix,
\hat{{\bm{K}}}(\xi)=[\xi(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i})^{\top}\xi(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{j})]_{1\leq i,j\leq\hat{n}}=\hat{\mathbf{\Xi}}^{\top}\hat{\mathbf{\Xi}}
with
\hat{\mathbf{\Xi}}
defined above. Then the equation is essentially in the same form of Equation
34
, so the previous proof applies here.

4.

Small cross-sample inner product on
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
: Similar to 3, we have

\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}\tilde{n}}}[({\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}}\alpha(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i}))^{\top}{\bm{\Pi}}_{{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}}\alpha(\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{j})]_{1\leq i\leq\hat{n},1\leq j\leq\tilde{n}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\!=\lVert\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}\tilde{n}}}[\xi(\hat{{\bm{x}}}_{i})^{\top}\xi(\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{j})]_{1\leq i\leq\hat{n},1\leq j\leq\tilde{n}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\!=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\hat{n}\tilde{n}}}\lVert\hat{\mathbf{\Xi}}^{T}\tilde{\mathbf{\Xi}}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}},

where
\tilde{\mathbf{\Xi}}
is defined in the same manner. Then the proof after Equation
35
for sub-Gaussian data applies.

5.

Diminishing population covariance on
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
: This refers covariance matrix of the sub-Gaussian part, and we simply have:

Cross-model hyperparameter transfer.
We note that, although each model could technically require different hyperparameters, in experiments we let all weak models share hyperparameters for simplicity and still achieve strong results, suggesting that our approach is not very sensitive to hyperparameters. Further, we present a new experiment demonstrating that hyperparameters selected using one group of models (i.e., as a validation set) generalize to other models. Appendix E
Discussion

Using activation maps as representations in Exp. III is a simple heuristic that yields promising results. However, more principled methods for defining and extracting representations from LLMs, such as those through NTK
(
Malladi et al., 2023
)
or representation engineering
(
Zou et al., 2023
)
, could be explored. Future research could leverage these approaches to improve results and uncover new applications. For instance,
(
Zou et al., 2023
)
introduces a method for extracting specific concept directions in representations, such as honesty and power-seeking. This could enable computing our metric based on topic-specific representations, allowing predictions of W2SG for general tasks within specific topical domains.
