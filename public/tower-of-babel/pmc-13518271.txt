Co‐ and Multi‐Pathologies in Parkinson's Disease: An International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society Scientific Issues Committee Review
Authors: Michele Matarazzo, Per Borghammer, Inas Elsayed, Jennifer G. Goldman, Yue Huang, Katja Lohmann, Per Svenningsson, Lorraine V. Kalia, Daniela Berg, Jeffrey H. Kordower, the MDS Scientific Issues Committee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParkinson's disease (PD) has been historically defined as a disease of striatal dopamine deficiency secondary to degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta, related to the presence of Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites. Since the discovery of pathogenic variants in the gene encoding α‐synuclein, as well as the finding that α‐synuclein is a major constituent of Lewy pathology, PD is considered as a prototypical synucleinopathy. However, neuropathological studies consistently show that most people with PD display copathologies, many of which are linked to specific clinical features and outcomes. In this review, we summarize the spectrum and frequency of these co‐ and multi‐pathologies in idiopathic and genetic PD and their impact on disease initiation and progression. Additionally, we also discuss how this multi‐pathological landscape may impact biomarker research and the implementation of emerging disease‐modifying therapies. © 2026 The Author(s). Movement Disorders published by Wiley Periodicals LLC on behalf of International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society.

Parkinson's disease (PD), a progressive neurodegenerative disorder, is characterized clinically by motor and non‐motor features and pathologically by the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra (SN) pars compacta and the presence of Lewy pathology in the form of Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites. 1 α‐Synuclein aggregates are a major component of the Lewy bodies and neurites, and they represent a defining pathological hallmark of synucleinopathies, including PD, dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), and multiple system atrophy (MSA). Differences exist among the synucleinopathies, however, regarding the location of α‐synuclein pathology and its conformational strains and fibril structure. 2 Advances in biomarker identification and detection now enable the in vivo identification of α‐synuclein‐based Lewy pathology in people with PD‐related clinical symptoms, who are at risk, or may have early‐stage disease. 3 , 4 , 5 However, why α‐synuclein aggregation occurs, what triggers this, whether α‐synuclein aggregation drives disease progression, and how the cascade of events unfolds remain incompletely understood.

The progression of α‐synuclein pathology throughout the nervous system has been described by several different models: the Braak staging hypothesizes a topographically predictable sequence of caudal to rostrally located Lewy pathology, beginning in the enteric plexus, spreading to the lower brainstem and subsequently cortical regions in later disease or beginning in the olfactory bulb and progressing caudally to the amygdala and cortical structures. 6 , 7 Notable criticisms of the Braak model, however, include heterogeneity in topographical progression (ie, involvement of more rostral structures before more caudal ones or amygdala‐centered pathology), lack of correlation between pathology stage and clinical severity (ie, lower brainstem pathology does not necessarily denote early PD), presence of incidental Lewy bodies in older individuals without PD diagnoses, and “body‐first vs. brain‐first” presentations. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 In contrast, some have argued against the gut‐onset of Lewy pathology as enteric nervous system pathology without central nervous system (CNS) pathology has not been seen in a large autopsy series. [Correction added 18 June 2026, after first online publication: “not” has been added to the prior sentence.]. 12 Therefore, the Unified Staging System of Lewy Body Disorders proposes that Lewy pathology primarily begins in the olfactory bulb, followed by a brainstem‐ or limbic‐predominantly stage. The α‐synuclein origin site and connectome model (SOC) combines aspects of both models into a pathobiological and clinical framework. 13 , 14 , 15 In most patients, α‐synuclein pathology begins in one location and then spreads through the nervous system connectome; however, the individual patient subtype may differ by where pathology is initiated as suggested by the recent brain‐first verses body‐first hypotheses. 14 , 15 , 16 Furthermore, in these staging models, along with cellular, animal, and other research, α‐synuclein is hypothesized to propagate through a prion‐like process, by which the misfolded pathological protein induces misfolding in nearby normal neuronal cells. 17 , 18 The concept of α‐synuclein aggregation and its spread via a prion‐like mechanism also has been debated as to whether this reflects a physiological, pathological, epiphenomenon, or other process. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22

A major limitation of current approaches to studying α‐synuclein is the paucity of autopsies in PD, particularly in its rarer genetic forms. Technical constraints and methodological discrepancies in brain‐tissue analyses further impede progress. 23 Recent advances are enabling the identification of different and smaller aggregates (ie, oligomers) and at lower levels, which may be critical in the process of neurodegeneration. The identification of these species, which will allow for the quantitative and topographical characterization of α‐synuclein with greater precision and is likely to provide further insights into the dynamics of misfolded protein aggregation. 24 Consequently, data obtained from the living patients are highly valuable, especially in the light of disease‐modifying molecular therapies that might specifically target α‐synuclein aggregates.

Phosphorylated α‐synuclein deposits have been detected in peripheral cutaneous nerves of idiopathic and certain genetic PD patients 25 , 26 and cutaneous synucleinopathy appears to mirror cerebral pathology. 26 This has increased interest in peripheral biomarkers and reinforced the broader effort to develop in vivo measures of α‐synuclein pathology. Molecular imaging of α‐synuclein has long been a major objective, particularly given the success of amyloid‐β (Aβ) and tau positron emission tomography (PET) imaging. Several candidate ligands have shown promise in vitro and in preclinical models. 27 However, in vivo imaging remains in its early stages. Initial studies using non‐selective tracers suggest the possibility of detecting both misfolded tau and α‐synuclein in PD patients. 28 More selective PET ligands are beginning to be evaluated in early human trials, with variable results, such as, higher binding has been reported in MSA, whereas findings in PD remain more limited and less consistent. 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 Recently, seed amplification assays (SAA), a technique initially developed for prion diseases, 33 exploit seed‐dependent amplification to detect misfolded α‐synuclein, primarily in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), 34 , 35 and increasingly in plasma and serum. 36 The latter seems to be most successful when neuron‐derived extracellular vesicles are enriched first. 37 , 38 Recent SAA applications have demonstrated their ability to detect the seeding activity of misfolded α‐synuclein in the brain, CSF, and skin samples from individuals with Lewy body diseases with sensitivity rates of 92% to 95% and specificity rates of 98% to 100%. 39 , 40 These findings suggest that CSF‐, blood‐, or cutaneous‐based α‐synuclein SAA analyses reveal prion‐like activity and may serve as accurate biomarkers for both genetic and idiopathic PD, with potential applications in early diagnosis, disease monitoring, and therapeutic response assessment in the future.

In recent years, it has become evident that PD is not a single‐protein entity disorder defined solely by α‐synuclein pathology. Instead, the majority of PD patients display mixed brain pathologies (Fig. 1), including the presence of Aβ plaques, tau tangles, and TAR DNA‐binding protein 43 (TDP‐43) aggregates, which have been typically associated with Alzheimer's disease (AD), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and frontotemporal dementia (FTD), and other proteinopathies, respectively. Recognition of these co‐pathologies has important implications for the understanding of the clinical heterogeneity of PD symptoms and progression, and for the therapeutic development of disease‐modifying and symptomatic agents. The presence and severity of co‐pathologies, especially those related to typical AD‐related neuropathological changes, often correlate with greater cognitive impairment, more rapid disease progression, and worse outcomes in PD. 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 Evidence of co‐existing tau and α‐synuclein pathologies demonstrate a complex interplay of protein misfolding, progression, and interactions on molecular levels and effects on clinical symptomatology such as cognitive decline in PD (for more detail see below). 45 , 46 Moreover, relationships between Aβ, tau, TDP‐43, cerebrovascular lesions, and other factors such as immune responses, genetics, and environment with α‐synuclein may influence diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic approaches to PD. 45 , 47 , 48

The growing recognition of pathological heterogeneity in PD underscores the need to move beyond the view of PD as a pure synucleinopathy and to consider co‐pathologies as the “rule” rather than the exception. By integrating these concepts, therapeutic strategies can more effectively address the multifactorial nature of PD. This review discusses the presence and impact of different co‐pathologies, namely tau, Aβ, TDP‐43, and cerebrovascular lesions, in idiopathic and genetic PD.

Tau Pathology

One of the most common PD co‐pathologies is the microtubule associated protein tau, encoded by the MAPT gene. 49 Tau has long been linked to a long list of neurodegenerative diseases, including AD, FTD, progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), and corticobasal degeneration (CBD), among others. 50 In these diseases, as in PD, tau undergoes extensive post‐translational modification, which results in hyperphosphorylation. Consequently, tau becomes insoluble, misfolded, and it aggregates. 50 Although not a canonical feature of PD, tau has been identified in Lewy bodies in patients' brains, 51 and studies have found pathological tau in more than half of PD patients and almost all PD patients over 60 years of age, signifying that tau pathology is a common feature of PD rather than a rarity. 49 In a group of patients with parkinsonism and mild motor deficits, loss of dopaminergic neurons and putamenal dopaminergic innervation, was seen equally across individuals with and without nigral α‐synuclein aggregates, while pathologically phosphorylated forms of tau were seen across the majority of individuals, again pointing to a significant role of tau pathology in PD pathogenesis. 52 Intriguingly, this tau pathology, which comes in the form of both 3R and 4R tau and rarely co‐localize with α‐synuclein in these cases, 52 was associated with decreased levels of tyrosine hydroxylase in dopaminergic neurons, suggesting a potential link between tau pathology and disease mechanisms in PD. 52 Neurofibrillary tau tangles within the SN have also been linked to gait disturbance in a study of older individuals. 53 Additionally, there is a large body of evidence to suggest that α‐synuclein and tau interact, and each may even influence the propensity of the other to aggregate. 54 , 55 , 56 , 57 Tau staining has long been identified within Lewy bodies 51 , 58 and individuals with pathological features of both AD and DLB have faster rates of disease progression than individuals with a single pathological signature. 59 Accordingly, PD patients with increased tau in CSF are more likely to develop dementia. 60 There is also evidence to suggest that tau pathology is capable of spreading in PD brains, with hyperphosphorylated tau being identified in grafted fetal neurons in PD patients receiving fetal tissue transplants. 61 , 62

Although pathological evidence for a role of tau in PD is mounting, there is also a genetic link. Genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) in sporadic PD patients identified a relationship between the MAPT haplotype and PD risk, with the H1 MAPT haplotype associated with greater risk of PD. 63 , 64 Individuals homozygous for the H1 haplotype have a higher burden of Lewy body pathology suggesting a potential mechanism for this association. 65 Although the exploration of the role of tau pathology in PD is just beginning, growing evidence suggests an important role for this protein in a significant portion of PD patients. The identification of widespread tau pathology in individuals with parkinsonism without α‐synuclein pathology supports the idea that tau may play a central role in disease pathogenesis, rather than a bystander getting pulled into preexisting inclusions. Furthermore, of all the co‐pathologies reviewed in this article, tau has the greatest association with decrease in cognitive function as measured by the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE). 66

Of note, although AD patients consistently display high regionally specific uptake of PET tau tracers, the absolute amount of detectable tau pathology in PD patients without cognitive impairment appears similar to that of age‐matched healthy controls in most studies. In contrast, increased tau tracer uptake has been observed in PD patients with cognitive impairment and in DLB, 67 , 68 although these findings are inconsistent across studies and can depend on the specific PET tracer used. 69 It is important to understand whether co‐pathologies are incidental or impactful clinically. Toward this end, several PET studies, as with postmortem neuropathology assessments, also report meaningful correlations between tau tracer uptake and declining cognitive scores in these PD populations. 28 , 70

Aβ Pathology

Aβ plaques are less frequently present in PD than neurofibrillary tangles. 71 Nevertheless, a recent study reported Aβ plaques in more than 40% of PD patients in their 60s, and 85% in their 90s, 66 which significantly exceeds the rates of amyloid positivity seen in age‐matched controls, (6% and 39%, respectively, according to a recent PET study). 72 A meta‐analysis of amyloid PET studies reported that the overall pooled prevalence of positive PET scans was 68% in DLB, 41% in PD with dementia (PDD), and 5% in PD with mild cognitive impairment. 73 In contrast to the tau PET studies discussed above, amyloid PET reveals that absolute quantities of cortical Aβ load are very high in many PDD and DLB patients, equaling that seen in AD patients. 74 Region‐specific deposition of Aβ pathology seems to be associated with cognitive decline, and individuals with PDD show more Aβ pathology in the temporal cortex, striatum, and frontal cortex than PD patients without dementia. 75 Moreover, the total burden of Aβ plaques is strongly associated with faster progression toward dementia in PD and a short latency between motor symptom onset and subsequent dementia. 76 , 77 , 78 , 79 , 80 Studies of CSF Aβ42 levels suggest that low levels are predictive of future cognitive impairment and dementia in PD. 81 Moreover, a more recent clinical study showed that both plasma‐tau181 and plasma‐Aβ42/40 ratios strongly predicted conversion to DLB rather than PD in a cohort of isolated rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder patients. 82 Of note, some postmortem studies have documented that the quantitative burden of tau predicts dementia more strongly than the Aβ plaque burden in PD, and that the combined pathology burden of α‐synuclein, tau, and Aβ together is a better predictor than any of the pathology types individually. 79 , 83 , 84

A true understanding of PD will require further exploration of the roles of tau and Aβ pathology, and future therapeutic strategies, particularly those aimed at mitigating α‐synuclein pathology, may require consideration of contributions of concomitant AD pathologies to be successful.

TDP‐43 Pathology

The TDP‐43 is a nuclear protein encoded by the TARDBP gene, involved in the regulation of RNA processing and metabolism. 85 TDP‐43 deposits are a common neuropathological feature of several neurodegenerative diseases, including ALS, FTD, limbic‐predominant age‐related TDP‐43 encephalopathy (LATE), and AD. 86 Although there has been significant focus on tau and Aβ co‐pathology in PD, the number of neuropathological studies investigating TDP‐43 in PD remains limited. TDP‐43 co‐pathology in Lewy body diseases was first reported by Nakashima‐Yasuda et al. 87 in up to 7% of PD patients and 19% of patients with PD‐related dementia, with aggregates predominantly located in the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex. Notably, the same study did not find TDP‐43 aggregates in patients with pure LBD. In contrast, subjects with both LBD and AD‐type pathology were the most affected, with 31% showing TDP‐43 aggregates, although the LBD group was small (n = 10). 87 Subsequent studies confirmed the presence of TDP‐43 aggregates in Lewy body diseases in 17% to 18% of patients, 88 , 89 with the highest likelihood of co‐pathology observed in cases with neocortical‐predominant α‐synuclein aggregation. A recent study reported a higher frequency of TDP‐43 aggregates in PD patients carrying the LRRK2 G2019S mutation, with over 70% of patients showing positive immunostaining. 90

The location of TDP‐43 pathology in Lewy body disorders has primarily been reported in the amygdala and entorhinal cortex. 87 , 88 , 89 However, because most studies focused specifically on these regions, rather than a more comprehensive approach, involvement of other cortical and subcortical areas cannot be ruled out. TDP‐43 pathology in the SN has been reported in a limited number of patients with parkinsonism, particularly in LRRK2 mutation carriers 90 and Perry syndrome, a form of parkinsonism associated with pathogenic variants in the DCTN1 gene, which is uniquely characterized by brainstem TDP‐43 inclusions. 91

Interestingly, TDP‐43 involvement in the SN has been linked to the development of clinical parkinsonism even in the absence of α‐synuclein pathology. 92 , 93 Further evidence for a complex relationship between TDP‐43 and parkinsonian disorders comes from its presence in Guam Parkinson‐dementia complex 94 and the identification of pathogenic variants in the TARDBP gene in patients clinically diagnosed with PD. 95 , 96 , 97 , 98 Additionally, a link between TDP‐43 and the Parkin (PRKN)/PTEN‐induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1) pathway of mitophagy has been demonstrated, suggesting a potential interaction between TDP‐43 and proteins altered in patients with young‐onset PD. 99

Despite growing evidence of TDP‐43 co‐pathology in PD, most studies indicate that the prevalence of these aggregates in Lewy body diseases is lower than that of tau and Aβ. 100 Furthermore, among common neurodegenerative diseases, PD appears to exhibit the lowest frequency of TDP‐43 positivity. 100

Therefore, significant uncertainty remains regarding the true prevalence and role of TDP‐43 aggregates in PD. The recent advances in the development of PET tracers targeting TDP‐43 pathology may enable in vivo characterization of its distribution and burden, offering an opportunity to clarify its contribution to PD and other neurodegenerative disorders. 101 Further research is needed to elucidate its interplay with other proteinopathies and its potential contribution to the pathogenesis and progression of parkinsonian disorders, particularly in relation to cognitive decline and limbic involvement.

Cerebral Vascular Co‐Pathology

Although PD and cerebrovascular diseases (CVD) are two distinct disease entities, their co‐occurrence is increasingly recognized, particularly in the elderly, where both conditions are more prevalent. CVD encompasses conditions such as stroke, small vessel disease (SVD), and other forms of vascular damage in the brain. 102 Research showed that over 70% of PD patients have white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) (vs. ~30% in age‐matched controls). 103 Autopsy studies confirm cerebrovascular lesions in 44% of PD patients, with SVD accounting for 70% of cases. 104 , 105 In patients with PD, WMHs, a marker of SVD, are correlated with greater motor impairment and cognitive decline, suggesting that cerebrovascular co‐pathologies contribute to the overall burden of neurodegeneration in PD. 106 , 107 The prevalence of cerebrovascular co‐pathology in PD is driven by risk factors, such as aging, hypertension, diabetes, sedentarism, and hyperlipidemia, some of which are shared with PD risk factors. 102 , 108 , 109 The coexistence of PD and CVD can complicate making the diagnosis and exacerbate motor and non‐motor symptom severity. Elucidating the presence and effects of cerebrovascular co‐pathology in PD is crucial for determining the biological interplay between these two factors and, from a clinical standpoint, for optimizing patient management.

In PD, α‐synuclein deposits in vascular endothelial cells can promote inflammation and oxidative stress, thereby worsening vascular damage. 110 In addition, some of the most common monogenic forms of PD are linked to pathogenic variants in genes with overlapping functions in the vascular system. For example, LRRK2 modulates immune responses, maintaining blood–brain barrier integrity, and inhibiting LRRK2 exhibited neuroprotective activities in a stroke model. 111 PINK1 and PRKN genes are critical for mitophagy regulation, and genetic variants result in abnormal mitophagy, which is known to be closely related to the occurrence, development, and pathological mechanism of cerebral ischemic changes. 112 Overall, PD patients exhibit endothelial dysfunction and increased blood viscosity, which heightens cerebral ischemic risk, leading to the cause of SVD. 113 , 114

Furthermore, SVD can accelerate PD progression by exacerbating neuronal dysfunction. Motor symptoms of PD are primarily driven by the striatal dopamine insufficiency secondary to degeneration of dopaminergic neurons originating in the SN. SN and cerebral vascular ischemic damage to these areas can promote neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, processes that are also implicated in the progression of PD. 115 Eventually, this process may worsen motor symptoms by further disrupting the already impaired dopaminergic signaling in PD patients.

Shared mechanisms like oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction create a “vicious cycle.” PD‐related oxidative stress damages vascular endothelium, whereas SVD‐induced ischemia increases α‐synuclein aggregation. 105 The glymphatic and autophagic dysfunction are the other shared mechanisms caused by chronic hypertension or poor metabolic conditions that lead to vascular damage and contribute to neurodegenerative processes that exacerbate PD symptoms. 106 , 107 PD patients exhibit reduced aquaporin‐4 (AQP4) expression in astrocytic endfeet and enlarged perivascular spaces, 116 impairing α‐synuclein clearance and promoting vascular damage. 117 , 118 Animal models showed that AQP4 deletion worsens α‐synuclein pathology 117 and AQP4 expression was decreased in the WMHs, 119 linking glymphatic failure to the co‐pathology in PD. Autophagic dysfunction further exacerbates impaired protein degradation in neurons and vascular cells that leads to accumulation of toxic α‐synuclein aggregates and vascular stenosis. 110 , 120 , 121 This overlap in pathological mechanisms of neurovascular unit dysfunction and α‐synuclein‐vascular interactions could result in a faster progression of PD symptoms.

The co‐pathology of SVD in PD presents a significant clinical challenge because of the overlapping risk factors, shared mechanisms of neurodegeneration, and the complexity of symptom presentation. Effective management requires a comprehensive approach that addresses both the neurodegenerative and vascular aspects of the diseases, including dopaminergic therapies, SVD prevention, and rehabilitation.

Pathology in Monogenic Forms of PD

Monogenic causes of PD, that is, pathogenic and coding risk variants in genes such as LRRK2, SNCA, PRKN, PINK1, and GBA1, are present in approximately 4% to 20% of PD patients, 122 , 123 especially in patients with early‐onset disease. 124 , 125 In addition, pathogenic variants in CHCHD2 and RAB32 are found in autosomal dominant early‐ and late‐onset PD. 126 , 127 Our knowledge about the neuropathology in genetic PD is still limited because of the small number of autopsies in monogenic PD patients, especially for the rarer forms. Although neuronal loss in the SN pars compacta is always seen, 128 it became clear that not all monogenic PD patients show α‐synuclein deposition, and that some patients presented rather with tau pathology or even TDP‐43‐positive inclusions (Table 1). Specifically, α‐synuclein deposition, which pathologically manifests as Lewy bodies, has not been shown as a consistent feature of PRKN‐linked PD. 129 Current autopsy studies in more than 20 patients, revealed Lewy bodies in only about a third of cases. 130 , 131 , 132 Furthermore, studies with SAA to detect aggregated α‐synuclein from living individuals, 133 using CSF, blood, or skin, reveal that only a fraction of PARK‐PRKN patients present with aggregations. 26 , 36 , 37 Additionally, in another recessively inherited form of PD, that is, PARK‐PINK1, not all of the four autopsy‐investigated patients had Lewy bodies 134 (Table 1) and negative SAA supports this observation. 135 There are no hints that disease duration correlates with the spread and distribution of Lewy bodies in PARK‐PINK1. 130 However, Prkn/Pink1 double knockout rats showed α‐synuclein aggregates in the striatum. 136 Notably, also in patients with dominant forms of PD, such as PARK‐LRRK2, Lewy bodies are not present in all autopsied patients or in mice with the LRRK2 gene knocked in. Furthermore, no Lewy bodies have been demonstrated in RAB32‐, ATP13A2‐, DCTN1‐, and SYNJ1‐linked PD, although data is limited, and conclusions are often based on single individual cases only (Table 1). In contrast, Lewy bodies are a consistent finding in PD linked to pathogenic variants in SNCA, which seems biologically intuitive. 137 In GBA1‐related PD, Lewy bodies are usually observed, but do not have more advanced neuropathology, that is, increased density of protein aggregates than idiopathic PD patients despite their more rapid progression and more often observed cognitive impairment. 138 An autopsy of an individual with CHCHD2 T61I mutation revealed widespread Lewy pathology with amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tau tangles throughout the brain. 139 Interestingly, recent findings suggest a role for α‐synuclein pathology beyond the presence of Lewy pathology in PARK‐LRRK2, because of technical advances that enable the identification of small oligomeric aggregates. 140 , 141

As in idiopathic PD, tau‐positive neurofibrillary tangles and neurites are frequently observed in many genetic forms of PD except for PARK‐GBA1 (Table 1). For instance, for PARK‐LRRK2, early observations have already suggested a broad pathological spectrum in the brains of patients with pathogenic LRRK2 variants, involving aggregation of tau proteins. 142 , 143 , 144 The pleiomorphic pathology in PARK‐LRRK2 has even been noticed within families. 145 Furthermore, although Aβ aggregations have not been described in genetic PD, TDP‐43 aggregates were reported in some patients with PD linked to variants in LRRK2 and GBA1 and are a common finding in patients with DCTN1 variants. 128 , 146 Notably, in the one autopsied patient with PD because of ATP13A2 variants, no pathological protein aggregates were found, but sparse iron deposits were observed in several brain areas. 147

The small number of autopsies of genetic PD cases limits genotype‐clinical‐pathological‐phenotype correlations, and it is challenging to account for variable disease durations, treatments (levodopa doses and/or deep brain stimulation), ethnicities, and times to autopsy. The observation of the inconsistent presence of Lewy bodies, even after many years of disease duration and within the same family, suggests that Lewy bodies may not be necessary nor sufficient for the clinical expression of PD in these monogenic forms of PD. 130 It has been suggested that catabolic processes, particularly related to lipid homeostasis, autophagy, and mitochondrial function could contribute to the formation of Lewy bodies. 148 Impairment of these processes because of pathogenic variants in PD‐linked genes, especially in mitochondrial based PARK‐PRKN and PARK‐PINK1, might be related to the lack of Lewy bodies in some patients. Interestingly, classical Lewy body pathology has also been observed in these PD forms, although confined to the brainstem, which further supports different disease mechanisms than in idiopathic, late‐onset PD. 149

Interface of Genetics and Pathology

Studying shared genetics of PD and co‐pathologies is essential for understanding the disease's mechanisms, progression, and potentially overlapping therapeutic targets. The coexistence of pathological changes other than α‐synuclein aggregation was found to complicate the clinical profile of PD, suggesting an interplay and pleiotropy of pathological mechanisms that may at least partially be driven by genetic factors. 150 , 151 It has been proven that overlapping neurological disorders share common genetic loci. Dissecting such genetic components and their role in the neuropathological features of PD is crucial to understanding its development and progression and improving diagnosis and personalized therapy. 151 , 152 , 153 , 154 , 155

Apolipoprotein E (APOE) gene, primarily associated with AD, was also found to be implicated in PD. 156 It is now known that the presence of certain APOE alleles may predict the risk of developing PD and its progression, particularly in patients with comorbid AD's pathology. The presence of APOE ε4 allele in early PD was found to be associated with higher levels of amyloidopathy and an increased risk of cognitive impairment, although these findings were not consistently replicated in non‐European cohorts. 157 , 158 , 159 Furthermore, preclinical studies suggested an implication of APOE in synucleinopathy independently of amyloid pathology, with APOE knockout mice exhibiting exacerbated α‐synuclein pathology. 160 In the context of APOE, α‐synuclein appears to induce metabolic changes and neuroinflammation culminating in neurodegeneration and synaptic loss. 161 It is proposed that α‐synuclein impairs astrocytes mitochondrial clearance of harmful fatty acids taken up by APOE positive particles, leaving neurons vulnerable to the damage caused by these toxic metabolites. 162 The role of immunomodulation as a common pathogenic mechanism between multiple neurodegenerative disease has been previously highlighted and warrants further investigation in the context of shared genetic mutations. 163 , 164

In addition to APOE, two other genes (GRN and C9orf72) were reported in patients exhibiting parkinsonian phenotype. 165 These genes, primarily linked to FTD, have been associated with parkinsonian features, and on occasions mimicking idiopathic PD. Despite the clinical similarities, neuropathological examinations of patients carrying pathogenic variants in GRN revealed TDP‐43 inclusions rather than α‐synuclein pathology, indicating that pathogenic GRN variants may contribute to PD through a distinct neurodegenerative process. 165 It is suggested that the neuroinflammation propagated by the pro‐inflammatory action of GRN‐encoded progranulin is a shared neurodegenerative mechanism that links GRN to a spectrum of neurodegenerative diseases. 166 A similar situation is observed with C9orf72, a gene best known for its association with FTD and ALS. The implication of a repeat expansion in C9orf72 in PD pathology remains controversial, with only limited evidence suggesting an association with PD and atypical parkinsonism. In the reported patients, C9orf72 mutations carriers with parkinsonian symptoms exhibited C9orf72‐related neuropathological features without coexisting Lewy body pathology, suggesting a distinct C9orf72‐mediated neurodegeneration mechanism in these individuals. 167 , 168

Exploring genetic contributions to the complex neuropathological scenario is crucial for elucidating the mechanisms of PD development and progression. Understanding the roles of genes such as GRN, C9orf72, APOE, and also MAPT in PD can provide valuable insights into disease risk, progression, and potential therapeutic targets. Continued research in this area is essential for advancing our knowledge of PD and improving clinical outcomes for affected individuals.

Non‐Neuronal Features of PD Pathology

Although α‐synuclein pathology in PD, as opposed to MSA, is predominantly neuronal, it would be misguided to overlook the significant role that non‐neuronal cells play in the pathophysiology of PD. It seems clear that PD has a strong chronic inflammatory component with both central and peripheral innate and adaptive immune responses that occur as the disease begins and progresses. 169 In vivo evidence for inflammation in PD includes microglial activation, increased expression of inflammatory genes in the periphery and in the CNS, infiltration of peripheral immune cells into the CNS, and altered composition and phenotype of peripheral immune cells, findings that are recapitulated in various animal models of PD. 170 Indeed, Tansey and coworkers, 170 examined the potential relevance of PD‐linked genetic variants to altered immune function and the extent to which environmental exposures recapitulate these phenotypes, which may lead to sporadic PD through similar mechanisms. The cell‐to‐cell propagation of α‐synuclein aggregates appears to be mediated, at least in part, by microglial activation in PD and other neurodegenerative diseases, 171 underscoring the contribution of non‐neuronal cells to disease progression. A question that has been difficult to assess in humans, is whether the neuroinflammation is a driver of pathology or a response to other pathogenic events since postmortem idiopathic PD patients virtually always display both. One unique human data set, however, is the temporal pattern of inflammation and α‐synuclein expression in fetal dopaminergic transplants in subjects with PD. In this rare human model, robust inflammatory responses as determine by CD45 and TMEM199 staining are seen in grafted cells as early as 18 months post‐transplantation with a similar degree of inflammation seen for up to 16 years post‐transplantation. 172 In contrast, only monomeric α‐synuclein is seen between 4 and 7 years post‐grafting, and aggregated α‐synuclein is not seen in grafted neurons until at least 10 years post‐grafting, suggesting that inflammation is a driver of pathology, and not a result of other pathogenic mechanisms.

Conclusion

Co‐ and multi‐pathologies appear to be the rule rather than the exception for patients with idiopathic and monogenic PD, as well as in many parkinsonian syndromes. Elucidating the contribution of each pathology to the complex clinical sequelae of PD is crucial for enabling a targeted understanding of the disease's course, for example, in terms of the progression rate and for identifying which additional features, including non‐motor ones, are likely to emerge. Furthermore, the important role of inflammatory and immune cells needs to be better understood, and each pathology needs to be considered in the context of therapeutic interventions. Clearly, however, PD is not purely neuronal, not purely α‐synuclein, and not a single disease entity making the use of the term Neuronal Synuclein Disease (NSD) as a substitute for Parkinson's disease3 scientifically inaccurate.
