Comparable outcomes of arthroscopic and mini-open tightrope fixation for acute acromioclavicular joint dislocations
Authors: İbrahim Ulusoy, İsmail Güzel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute high-grade acromioclavicular (AC) joint dislocations are commonly treated surgically, with multiple fixation options available. Arthroscopic TightRope (AT) and mini-open TightRope (MOT) techniques are widely used, but comparative evidence remains limited.MethodsIn this retrospective comparative study, 96 patients with Rockwood type III–V AC joint dislocations were treated between 2018 and 2023 using either AT (n = 52) or MOT (n = 44). Two patients lost to follow-up were excluded. Primary outcomes included Constant–Murley and UCLA scores, while secondary outcomes were visual analog scale (VAS) pain scores, coracoclavicular (CC) distance on radiographs, operative time, and complications. The sample size/power calculation was corrected (two-group comparison, α = 0.05, achieved power ~ 90% with d ≈ 0.67). A sensitivity analysis restricted to Rockwood IV–V was additionally performed to test robustness.ResultsAt one year, both groups showed significant improvements in Constant–Murley and UCLA scores (p < 0.001 within groups) with no between-group differences (p > 0.05).The mean difference in operative time between the groups was 14.3 min (95% CI 11.5–17.1, p < 0.001). CC distance difference between groups was 0.2 mm (95% CI − 0.3 to 0.7, p = 0.41), indicating similar radiological stability. Complication rates were low and comparable. The IV–V–only sensitivity analysis yielded results consistent with the overall cohort.ConclusionAT and MOT provide comparable one-year functional outcomes, pain relief, and radiological stability in acute AC dislocations. Differences were limited to perioperative parameters (longer operative time with arthroscopy). Choice of technique should be individualized based on patient factors and surgeon expertise.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03456-w.Keywords: Acromioclavicular joint, TightRope fixation, Arthroscopy, Mini-open, Shoulder surgery, Comparative study

Introduction

Acromioclavicular (AC) joint dislocations account for approximately 9–12% of shoulder girdle injuries and are frequently encountered in young and physically active individuals following direct trauma to the shoulder [1, 2]. The stability of the AC joint is provided by the acromioclavicular and coracoclavicular (CC) ligaments, the capsule, and surrounding musculature. Disruption of these structures leads to varying degrees of displacement, classified according to the Rockwood system into six types [3]. While types I–II are typically managed conservatively, surgical treatment is widely recommended for high-grade injuries (types IV–VI). Type III remains controversial; although nonoperative treatment is commonly sufficient, surgical fixation is often advocated in young, active patients or those with high functional demands [4, 5].

Several surgical methods have been described, including hook plate fixation, CC screw fixation, ligament reconstruction, and suspensory fixation systems [6, 7]. Open approaches allow stable fixation and direct visualization but are associated with hardware irritation, subacromial impingement, and the need for implant removal [8–10]. In contrast, arthroscopic-assisted techniques, particularly suspensory fixation with TightRope systems, have gained popularity due to less soft-tissue dissection, superior cosmetic results, and the ability to diagnose and treat concomitant intra-articular lesions [11–16]. However, arthroscopic procedures are technically demanding, require longer operative times, and are associated with complications such as tunnel widening or implant migration [8, 9, 17].

The current literature remains inconsistent. Koch et al. reported that both arthroscopic double TightRope and mini-open reconstruction yield comparable long-term functional outcomes, with arthroscopy offering better cosmetic results but longer operative duration; however, they noted superior return-to-sport rates with the mini-open technique [18]. Faggiani et al. similarly found no difference in long-term function, although arthroscopy was associated with earlier return to sport [19]. Yin et al. and Loriaut et al. demonstrated satisfactory outcomes with arthroscopic double-button fixation, but highlighted the risks of tunnel widening and reduction loss [20, 21]. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses confirm satisfactory outcomes for both methods, yet significant heterogeneity in technique, implants, and rehabilitation protocols complicates direct comparisons [1, 4, 13].

Given these ongoing controversies, particularly regarding the role of type III injuries and the balance between operative parameters and long-term outcomes, further comparative evidence is warranted. The aim of this study was therefore to compare the clinical, functional, and radiological outcomes of arthroscopic TightRope (AT) and mini-open TightRope (MOT) fixation in patients with acute Rockwood type III–V AC joint dislocations. We hypothesized that both techniques would provide comparable functional recovery and radiological stability at one year, but with differences in perioperative parameters such as operative time and blood loss.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This retrospective comparative cohort study was conducted at two tertiary referral centers between January 2018 and December 2023 in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Institutional review board approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Malatya Turgut Özal University (Approval No: 2023-78). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to inclusion. Consent for publication was not applicable as no identifying images or personal data were used.

Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Patient selection

Adults (≥ 18 years) presenting within 2 weeks of trauma with unilateral acute AC joint dislocation were screened. Acute was defined as injuries treated within 14 days of trauma, in line with previous literature [18, 19]. Inclusion criteria: Rockwood type III–V dislocations confirmed radiographically, absence of associated fractures, and minimum 12-month clinical and radiological follow-up. Exclusion criteria: chronic injuries (> 2 weeks), coracoid or clavicle fractures, prior ipsilateral shoulder surgery, rotator cuff tears requiring repair, neurovascular injury, systemic/neurological disorders affecting shoulder function. The previous wording “other reasons” was removed and replaced with the explicit exclusion criterion of incomplete follow-up (< 12 months).

The inclusion of Rockwood type III cases was based on their debated management in young, active patients, where surgical stabilization may offer superior functional recovery compared to nonoperative treatment [22]. To minimize bias, subgroup analysis for Rockwood type IV–V was also performed. Patients were allocated according to surgeon preference into two groups:

Arthroscopic TightRope (AT): n = 52.

Mini-open TightRope (MOT): n = 44.

Surgical Techniques

Arthroscopic TightRope (AT)

All surgical procedures were performed under general anesthesia. Patients were positioned in the beach-chair position on the operating table. Before the procedure commenced, complete reduction of the dislocation was confirmed by elevating the shoulder. Two standard arthroscopic portals were established during the procedure. Initially, the posterior glenohumeral portal was placed 2 cm inferior to the posterolateral edge of the acromion. The anterior portal was created using an outside-in technique through the rotator interval. Visualization was achieved using a 30° angled optical arthroscope. A diagnostic arthroscopy was performed to assess associated intra-articular lesions of the shoulder joint. The rotator interval was carefully opened, and the inferior surface of the coracoid process was exposed using a radiofrequency device and a burr. To prevent damage to adjacent neurovascular structures, bone surfaces were approached with caution. After exposure of the coracoid base via the rotator interval, a 1-cm mini-incision was made over the AC joint to assist accurate localization of the distal clavicle and allow passage of the implant.

A C-shaped drill guide was utilized to ensure accurate positioning beneath the coracoid process. To secure the clavicle in the correct position, a 2.4 mm drill was used to create a tunnel extending from the clavicle to the coracoid through a guidewire. A double-button implant system composed of metal buttons (TightRope; Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA) was placed beneath the coracoid and secured via the guidewire. Traction sutures were pulled to stabilize the implant at both the coracoid and clavicle. The distal clavicle was gently pulled downward using traction sutures to complete the reduction. The accuracy of the procedure was confirmed using intraoperative fluoroscopy. All incisions were meticulously closed, ensuring that knots were buried subcutaneously. The entire surgical procedure was completed arthroscopically without the need for open reduction [6, 13, 23, 24] (Fig. 2).

Mini-open TightRope (MOT)

The procedural steps were as follows: A 3–4 cm skin incision was made over the distal clavicle, centered at the coracoclavicular ligament attachment site. The deltotrapezial fascia was carefully incised and elevated, ensuring minimal disruption to the surrounding soft tissue. The coracoid process was identified under direct visualization, which helped prevent misplacement of the implant. A 2.4 mm guidewire was inserted percutaneously through the clavicle and center of the coracoid process, avoiding medial cortical penetration, which has been reported as a risk factor for coracoid fractures. A 4.0 mm cannulated drill was then used to create the tunnel for TightRope passage. The TightRope implant (TightRope; Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA), a double-button construct, was inserted, with one button positioned under the coracoid and the other resting on the superior clavicle.

The AC joint was manually reduced while gradually tensioning the TightRope system to ensure anatomical alignment. Fluoroscopy was used to confirm adequate reduction before securing the final fixation. The tensioning mechanism of the TightRope allowed for dynamic stabilization, mimicking the physiological function of the coracoclavicular ligaments. The deltotrapezial fascia was meticulously repaired to maintain soft tissue integrity [6, 7, 22] (Fig. 2).

Postoperative rehabilitation

Both groups followed an identical protocol: sling immobilization for 4 weeks; passive pendulum exercises from post-op day 1; active-assisted/active ROM from week 4; strengthening from week 8; return to non-contact sports at ~ 3 months and contact sports at ~ 6 months, conditional on clinical and radiographic recovery.

Clinical and radiological evaluation

Assessments were performed preoperatively and at 3, 6, and 12 months:

Primary outcome: Constant–Murley score (CMS), selected for its validity and wide clinical use in shoulder function assessment [25].

Secondary outcomes: UCLA, ASES, QuickDASH, and visual analog scale (VAS) pain scores (rest, night, activity), as well as patient satisfaction VAS. Contralateral shoulder ROM and scores were recorded as internal controls [26].

Radiological outcomes: Coracoclavicular (CC) distance on anteroposterior radiographs, relative CC ratio, and tunnel widening in the AT group. Horizontal stability parameters (AC distance and clavicular tilt) were assessed where available.

Other measures: Operative time, estimated blood loss, and return-to-sport/work.

Complications: Loss of reduction (> 5 mm), implant migration, fracture, infection, or thromboembolic events.

Two independent, blinded observers performed all radiographic measurements; inter-observer reliability was assessed (intraclass correlation coefficient).

Statistical analysis

Power analysis was recalculated using G*Power 3.1 [27]. For a two-tailed t-test with α = 0.05, β = 0.10, and effect size d = 0.67, the achieved power for the current sample (n1 = 52, n2 = 44) was approximately 0.90.A total of 128 participants (64 per group) would have been required for 80% power at d = 0.50.The Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) for the Constant–Murley Score has been reported as 8–10 points; the between-group difference in our study (0.5 points) therefore represents statistical detectability rather than clinical significance. This clarification has been added for transparency.

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared with χ² or Fisher’s exact test. Repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni correction was used for within- and between-group comparisons across follow-up intervals. Holm correction was applied for multiple secondary outcomes to minimize type I error. Results are presented with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and operative data

Patient demographics and Follow-up

A total of 96 patients were analyzed (52 in the AT group, 44 in the MOT group). The mean age was 39.8 ± 11.6 years (AT) and 40.9 ± 12.4 years (MOT, p = 0.68). The male-to-female ratio was similar (AT: 38/14, MOT: 32/12; p = 0.84). Rockwood type distribution was III: 59 (61.5%), IV: 27 (28.1%), V: 10 (10.4%), with no significant difference between groups (p = 0.91) (Table 1).

Mean operative time was significantly longer in AT compared with MOT (77.4 ± 9.2 vs. 63.1 ± 8.6 min, p < 0.001; mean difference 14.3 min, 95% CI 11.5–17.1). Pain and satisfaction

VAS pain scores at rest, night, and activity decreased significantly in both groups (p < 0.001 within groups). At 12 months, mean VAS was 1.2 ± 0.6 (AT) vs. 1.1 ± 0.7 (MOT, p = 0.48). Patient satisfaction VAS was high in both groups (AT 93.4 ± 5.0, MOT 92.7 ± 5.3; p = 0.48).

Radiological outcomes

Baseline CC distance was comparable (AT 19.6 ± 2.4 mm vs. MOT 19.5 ± 2.5 mm, p = 0.87). Postoperative reduction was maintained at 12 months (AT 8.9 ± 1.2 mm vs. MOT 8.7 ± 1.1 mm, p = 0.41; mean diff 0.2 mm, 95% CI − 0.3 to 0.7).

Loss of reduction (> 5 mm) occurred in 2/52 AT (3.8%) and 2/44 MOT (4.5%) (p = 0.92).

In the AT group, mean tunnel widening at 12 months was 0.7 ± 0.4 mm; this showed no correlation with functional outcomes (r = 0.11, p = 0.28). Shown in Table 4.

Discussion

This retrospective comparative study investigated the outcomes of arthroscopic TightRope (AT) and mini-open TightRope (MOT) fixation in patients with acute Rockwood type III–V acromioclavicular (AC) joint dislocations. Our results demonstrate that both approaches provided significant improvement in functional outcomes, pain relief, and radiological stability at one year, with no significant intergroup differences. Operative time was longer in AT, whereas blood loss was slightly lower. Complication rates were low and comparable.

Our findings are consistent with previous reports suggesting equivalent clinical outcomes between arthroscopic and mini-open suspensory fixation. Koch et al. compared arthroscopic double TightRope and mini-open fixation, reporting similar long-term functional outcomes, with arthroscopy offering superior cosmesis but longer operative duration; notably, they observed higher rates of return to original sports activity with the mini-open technique [18]. Ibrahim et al. [28] also demonstrated excellent postoperative function with arthroscopically treated AC dislocations, regardless of rehabilitation posture, highlighting the robustness of the arthroscopic technique. In our study, return-to-sport rates were similar between groups (~ 89%), possibly reflecting improvements in arthroscopic techniques and rehabilitation protocols over the last decade.

Faggiani et al. also reported no significant difference in functional scores between arthroscopic and mini-open fixation, although patients treated arthroscopically returned to sport earlier [19]. Loriaut et al. and Yin et al. demonstrated satisfactory functional and radiological results with arthroscopic double-button fixation, but highlighted complications such as tunnel widening [20, 21]. In our series, tunnel widening was observed in the AT group but was minimal (0.7 mm at 12 months) and not correlated with function, suggesting that with modern implants this issue may be of limited clinical relevance.

One of the main controversies concerns the inclusion of Rockwood type III injuries. While some authors recommend nonoperative treatment [13], surgical stabilization is increasingly performed in young, active individuals due to the risk of persistent pain or instability [6, 7]. In our cohort, type III represented 62% of patients, reflecting real-world practice. To address reviewer concerns, a sensitivity analysis restricted to type IV–V cases confirmed comparable results, supporting the robustness of our conclusions. Nevertheless, our findings should be interpreted with caution, and we acknowledge that type III injuries remain a gray zone in AC joint management. Additionally, subgroup analysis limited to Rockwood IV–V injuries confirmed that all key outcomes (Constant–Murley, UCLA, CC distance, and complication rates) remained statistically comparable between groups. This further supports that inclusion of type III cases did not bias the main findings.

Our study adds to the literature by reporting perioperative parameters. Operative time was significantly longer in AT (mean + 14 min), while blood loss was slightly lower. Although the difference in blood loss (approximately 3 mL) reached statistical significance, it is below the threshold of clinical relevance and unlikely to affect patient recovery. Nevertheless, this parameter remains useful for perioperative planning and cost-efficiency considerations in high-volume centers. These findings align with Koch et al., who also observed longer operative times in arthroscopy [18]. Although the difference in blood loss was statistically significant, the absolute values (< 25 mL in both groups) are unlikely to be clinically relevant. Still, such data are important for surgical planning and cost–efficiency discussions. The longer operative time observed in the AT group in our series mirrors prior reports [29, 30]. Arthroscopy allows for intra-articular inspection and treatment of concomitant lesions—such as rotator cuff tears or labral injuries—which is a recognized advantage [31–34]. However, this may prolong surgery, particularly for surgeons in the early stages of the arthroscopic learning curve. The mini-open approach, while lacking this diagnostic capability, often provides faster fixation and may be technically less demanding. The present study contributes novel insights by including a detailed comparison of perioperative outcomes such as operative time, intraoperative blood loss, and tunnel widening — variables rarely reported together in prior literature. Additionally, by analyzing return-to-sport and work data, this study provides a more comprehensive view of postoperative functional recovery beyond traditional shoulder scores.

Preservation of the CC distance in both groups in our study aligns with findings by Loriaut et al. [21] and Shin et al. [24], who demonstrated that arthroscopic suspensory fixation achieves durable radiological stability over at least one year of follow-up. Nevertheless, some comparative studies, such as Koch et al. [18] and Jensen et al. [12], suggest that open or mini-open techniques may offer slightly better maintenance of horizontal stability, potentially due to differences in soft tissue handling.

Both groups showed excellent improvement in Constant–Murley, UCLA, ASES, and QuickDASH scores, approaching contralateral shoulder function at one year. Although statistical comparability was observed, it is important to note that the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the Constant score has been reported at 8–10 points [24]. The between-group differences in our study were well below this threshold, suggesting clinical comparability rather than superiority of either technique. Similarly, reported MCID thresholds for UCLA (~ 3 points), ASES (6–12 points), and QuickDASH (10–15 points) were also greater than the observed intergroup differences in our cohort, further supporting that the lack of statistical difference reflects clinical equivalence. This aligns with the meta-analysis by Gowd et al., which concluded that functional outcomes are generally satisfactory regardless of technique [22].

The post-hoc analyses confirmed that functional recovery was progressive across all follow-up intervals in both groups, with significant improvements between preoperative, 3-month, 6-month, and 12-month assessments for Constant–Murley, UCLA, ASES, and QuickDASH scores. These results indicate that both arthroscopic and mini-open TightRope techniques provide steady functional gains during the first postoperative year, without any significant intergroup differences. Our findings are consistent with those of Salzmann et al. [35], who reported comparable functional outcomes between arthroscopic and mini-open techniques for high-grade AC dislocations, with significant improvements over time but no clear superiority of one method. Similarly, Shin et al. [23] found that postoperative Constant and ASES scores improved markedly at each interval, aligning with our post-hoc trends. However, contrary to reports suggesting higher complication rates in arthroscopic procedures due to tunnel widening or hardware migration [36, 37], we observed no significant differences in complication rates between groups.

In terms of radiographic outcomes, the stability of CC distance maintenance in both AT and MOT parallels the results of Beitzel et al. [6], who emphasized that both techniques achieve reliable reduction when performed with proper fixation principles. Some meta-analyses, such as those by Arirachakaran et al. [38], have suggested that arthroscopic approaches may offer advantages in cosmesis and less soft tissue trauma, but these factors were not directly assessed in our study. Overall, our results reinforce the growing body of evidence that both AT and MOT are effective options for acute high-grade AC joint dislocations. The choice of procedure should therefore depend more on surgeon expertise, available resources, and patient-specific considerations rather than expectations of superior clinical outcomes.

Return to work and sport are highly relevant outcomes in this patient population. In our series, return-to-sport rates were nearly 90% in both groups, with no significant difference. These findings differ from earlier reports suggesting superiority of mini-open fixation for athletic return [18], but may reflect improvements in rehabilitation and arthroscopic familiarity.

Both groups in our study demonstrated low complication rates, in line with published literature [39–42]. Loss of reduction occurred in 4 cases (4.2%), equally distributed between groups, with two requiring revision surgery. These rates are consistent with prior series [24]. Importantly, radiological reduction was maintained in the majority, and CC distance at one year was nearly identical in both groups. Tunnel widening in the AT group did not influence outcomes, similar to prior reports [21]. Our data, similar to the systematic review by Gowd et al. [24], Pan et al. [43] and Hess et al. [44] suggest that these rates are lower compared to historical hook plate fixation.

The comparable outcomes between AT and MOT indicate that both techniques are viable, effective, and safe options for acute high-grade AC joint dislocations. Surgical decision-making should therefore be individualized, considering factors such as surgeon expertise, patient cosmetic expectations, the likelihood of concomitant intra-articular pathology, and resource availability. This study provides additional value by reporting perioperative parameters (operative time, blood loss, tunnel widening) together with return-to-sport and return-to-work outcomes, which are rarely evaluated collectively in previous comparative studies.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and surgeon preference for technique introduce potential selection bias. Although demographic variables were balanced, unmeasured confounders cannot be excluded. Second, the follow-up period (mean 12.4 months) is relatively short and may not capture long-term complications such as post-traumatic arthritis, late reduction loss, or tunnel widening. A Kaplan–Meier survivorship analysis was not performed due to this limited duration, restricting our ability to assess long-term implant survival or recurrent instability. Third, although type III cases were included to reflect real-world practice, this may limit generalizability; we attempted to mitigate this through sensitivity analysis of Rockwood type IV–V cases. Fourth, while intra- and inter-observer reliability was assessed and found to be excellent, our study was not powered to detect rare complications. Moreover, pre-injury activity level and surgical-side dominance were not systematically recorded, which may represent minor confounding when interpreting return-to-sport and functional recovery outcomes. Finally, larger multicenter randomized trials with longer follow-up remain necessary to confirm the comparability of arthroscopic and mini-open TightRope fixation.

Conclusion

Both arthroscopic TightRope and mini-open TightRope fixation provided excellent short-term functional recovery, pain relief, and radiological stability in patients with acute Rockwood type III–V acromioclavicular joint dislocations. Differences were limited to perioperative parameters, with longer operative times observed in arthroscopy and slightly higher blood loss in the mini-open approach. Given the retrospective design and limited follow-up, our findings support that both techniques are viable options, and surgical choice should be tailored to patient characteristics and surgeon expertise. Larger prospective studies are warranted to confirm these results. The comparable outcomes observed reinforce that both techniques are viable, safe, and effective when applied in appropriately selected patients by experienced surgeons.
