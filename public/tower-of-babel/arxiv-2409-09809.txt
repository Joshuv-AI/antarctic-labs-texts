Kei Beauduin

Abstract
We present several formulas for both the discrete and fractional iterates of an invertible power series f f , using a new unifying approach based on umbral calculus. 1
Introduction

The ring of formal power series is closed under addition, multiplication and, as a corollary, under composition with a zero constant term series as well. This makes it meaningful to study repeated self-composition –
iteration
– of a compositionally invertible power series
f
, denoted
f^{s}
for
s\in\mathbb{N}:=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
. Investigations on this problem trace back to Condorcet (1777)
[
13
]
. This topic became even more intriguing once authors noticed the natural extension of iteration to complex values
s\in\mathbb{C}
, a generalization sometimes known as
fractional iteration
. Despite its long history, explicit formulas for fractional iterates remain relatively rare in the literature, owing to the substantial difficulty of tackling the issue directly. To overcome the challenges, Schröder (1870)
[
36
]
and Jabotinsky (1963)
[
24
]
used different techniques yielding distinct formulas for the fractional iterates of
f
when
f^{\prime}(0)=1
. Schröder relied on an intriguing identity of Cayley
[
10
]
, whereas Jabotinsky used a matrix representation of power series due to Bennett
[
9
]
, sometimes referred to as
Jabotinsky matrices
. Unbeknownst to Jabotinsky, Tambs Lyche
[
39
,
29
]
had arrived at the same results in 1927, with a more involved proof, but under fewer restrictions on
f^{\prime}(0)
.

In
Section

2
, we introduce a new technique from the theory of
umbral calculus
that unifies the two approaches. Specifically, in
Section

4.1
we employ a generalization of Jabotinsky matrices, that enables the derivation of both Jabotinsky’s and Schröder’s formulas. Then, utilizing
q
-calculus
, in
Sections

4.3
and
4.4
we extend these findings to situations where
q:=f^{\prime}(0)
is either equal to
1
or not a root of unity. This not only shortens Tambs Lyche’s original proof but also establishes the general form of Schröder’s formula. In addition, in
Section

3.2
we demystify the recent formula for discrete iterates proposed by Monkam (2020)
[
33
]
that we generalize to fractional iterates in
Section

4.5
. Finally, the corresponding new expressions for the
iterative logarithm
are computed in
Section

5
.

2.2
Bell polynomials

In this section and thereafter, we consider
f
to be an invertible (formal) power series. That is, it can be expressed as

f(t):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}a_{n},\quad a_{1}\neq 0,

(7)

where
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a sequence of numbers known as the
exponential coefficients
of

. The
ordinary coefficients
are given by
q_{n}:=a_{n}/n!
. For later use, we define

q:=f^{\prime}(0)=a_{1}=q_{1}.

(8)

The
exponential partial Bell polynomials

[
12
, Chap. 3.3]
are defined by the following equation:

\frac{f(t)^{k}}{k!}=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}B_{n,k}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-k+1}),

(9)

i.e., they are the exponential coefficient of

and given explicitly by

B_{n,k}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-k+1})=n!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\ell_{1}+\ldots+n\ell_{n}=n\\
\ell_{1}+\ldots+\ell_{n}=k\end{subarray}}\prod_{p=1}^{n-k+1}\frac{a_{p}^{\ell_{p}}}{(p!)^{\ell_{p}}\ell_{p}!}.

(10)

Similarly, the
ordinary partial Bell polynomials
are the ordinary coefficients of

, that is

f(t)^{k}=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}t^{n}\hat{B}_{n,k}(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n-k+1}),

(11)

The two kinds of Bell polynomials are isomorphically linked by the relationship

B_{n,k}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-k+1})=\frac{n!}{k!}\hat{B}_{n,k}(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n-k+1}).

(12)

Jabotinsky matrices

\textbf{B}(f)

[
23
,
19
,
24
,
12
]
are infinite lower triangular matrices consisting of partial ordinary Bell polynomials, i.e.,
\textbf{B}(f)=(\hat{B}_{n,k}(q_{1},\dots,q_{n-k+1}))_{n,k\in\mathbb{N}}
.

2.3
Fractional iteration

Invertible formal power series form a group under composition. Thus, for

, the iterate

is defined as the

(13)

holds for

Fractional iteration
extends the parameter from integers to complex numbers: a family of formal power series

is called a family of

of

if

and

13
holds for all

The existence of such families is justified for

by Theorem 5 in either of
[

,
18
]

]

{lemma}

If there exists a sequence of polynomials

such that

f^{s}(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{n}(s)t^{n},

(14)

for
s\in\mathbb{N}
, then the power series defined by
equation

14
with
s\in\mathbb{C}
satisfy
equation

13
for
r,s\in\mathbb{C}
.

{proof}

For
r,s\in\mathbb{N}
, equating coefficients in the equality (
13
) yields polynomial identities in the variables

and

. Since these identities hold for all
r,s\in\mathbb{N}
, they remain valid for all
r,s\in\mathbb{C}
. Under the extension defined by
equation

14
, the identities are equivalent to
equation

13
.

When
f^{\prime}(0)=q=1
, the hypotheses of
Section

2.3
are always satisfied, as follows from

3.2
and
28
below. A classical and illustrative example of fractional iterates in that case is given by the function

for which

.

When

is not a root of unity, the coefficients instead take the form

for suitable polynomials

]
. The values

are distinct, so the polynomial-identity argument used in the proof of

still applies. The function

under a branch of the complex logarithm

When

2.4
Umbral calculus

An invertible power series gives rise to a sequence of polynomials
(\phi_{n}(x))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
via the generating function
[
7
]

e^{xf(t)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\phi_{n}(x).

(16)

The sequence, referred to as a
basic sequence
, is characterized by the following properties
[
7
]
:

•

(\phi_{n}(x))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
polynomial sequence
, that is,
\phi_{n}(x)
is of degree
n
. We shall write these with braces:
\{\phi_{n}(x)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

•

\phi_{0}(x)=1
.

•

\phi_{n}(0)=0
for
n>0
.

•

\{\phi_{n}(x)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
Sheffer sequence
, i.e., there exists a
delta operator

Q
such that for all
n>0
,
Q\phi_{n}(x)=n\phi_{n-1}(x)
.

Q
is, in fact, unique and equal to
f^{-1}(D)
, where
D
is the differentiation operator. Furthermore, each polynomial sequence
\{\phi_{n}(x)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
can be uniquely associated with an invertible linear operator
\phi
, so that
\phi x^{n}=\phi_{n}(x)
. When
\{\phi_{n}(x)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a basic sequence,
\phi
is called an
umbral operator
. If
\psi
is the umbral operator associated to the power series
g
, then
\phi\psi
is the umbral operator associated to
f\circ g
. Consequently, the iterates
\phi^{s}
, for
s\in\mathbb{Z}
, are umbral operators for
f^{s}

[
7
]
.

It follows from
equation

16
that the generating function of the coefficients of an umbral operator can be expressed by

\frac{f(t)^{k}}{k!}=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi}.

(17)

Therefore, looking back at
equation

7
, we understand that
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{1}_{\phi}=a_{n}
and that we can express the coefficient with Bell polynomials

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi}=B_{n,k}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-k+1}).

(18)

Thanks to the additional properties established in
Section

2.1
, these coefficients extend the notion of Jabotinsky matrices to the case where
\phi
is not an umbral operator. The property

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi\psi}=\sum_{r=k}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{r}_{\psi}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r}{k}_{\phi}

is the coefficient analogue of the fundamental composition rule for Jabotinsky matrices
\textbf{B}(f\circ g)=\textbf{B}(g)\textbf{B}(f)

[
12
, Thm. A of Sec. 3.7]
. By contrast, there is no corresponding linearity property for Jabotinsky matrices, since the map
f\mapsto\textbf{B}(f)
is not linear. Operator coefficients remain meaningful for addition, even though
\phi+\psi
is generally not an umbral operator, and satisfy
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi+\psi}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi}+\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\psi}

Their diagonal terms are given by

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{n}_{\phi}=q^{n},

(19)

and from the preceding discussion, for
s\in\mathbb{Z}
, the following result is clear:

\frac{f^{s}(t)^{k}}{k!}=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{s}}.

(20)

Using
equation

16
, we define the fractional iterates
\phi^{s}
of

s\in\mathbb{C}
. Their coefficients are therefore given by
equation

\phi^{s}
as a notation, it can also be realized as a fractional power of the linear operator
\phi

[
8
]
.

The goal of the subsequent sections is to give different expressions for the coefficient
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{s}}
. Readers interested solely in fractional iterates may skip
Section

3
and proceed directly to
Section

30
]
, we know that for a power series
f
, satisfying
f^{\prime}(0)=1
, the set of values of
s
for which
f^{s}
possesses a positive
radius of convergence
is either
\mathbb{C}
or
\lambda\mathbb{Z}
, for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. See the survey
[
2
]
. In the former case, we say that
f
is
embeddable
. However, it is also known that most usual power series are not embeddable
[
6
]
and therefore that for most convergent power series, only their discrete iterations converge (the case
\lambda=1
). The function
f(t)=t/(1-t)
and its iterates
f^{s}(t)=t/(1-st)
are examples of embeddable power series, also known for being the only ones meromorphic over the entire complex plane under
f^{\prime}(0)=1

[
5
]
. See the surveys
[
35
,
2
]
for further discussions.

In this section, we present three formulas for the coefficients of the fractional iterates of an invertible power series. We begin by revisiting the classical formulas for the unitary case
f^{\prime}(0)=1
within our new framework. This simpler setting sharpens our understanding and prepares the way for a more general treatment, where tools from
q
-calculus are introduced and their relevance motivated.

4.1
Unitary case
f^{\prime}(0)=1

The earliest known formula for iterating power series was introduced by Schröder in 1870
[
36
]
. Building on an important observation of Cayley
[
10
]
, he obtained a closed-form expression for the coefficients of discrete iterates. This result was later recognized by Bennett
[
9
]
and subsequently by Jabotinsky
[
23
]
as extending naturally to fractional iterates (see also
[
12
, Sec. 3.7]
and
[
26
]
). In what follows, we further generalize this result to the
k
-th powers of the iterates.

{theorem}

[Schröder
[
36
, eqs. (38b), (40)]
]
If
q=1
and
s\in\mathbb{C}
, the coefficients of
f^{s}
(
20
) are given by

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{s}}=\sum_{p=0}^{n-k}\binom{s}{p}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{(\phi-1)^{p}},

(29)

with

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{(\phi-1)^{p}}=\sum_{k=r_{0}<\ldots<r_{p}=n}\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r_{i+1}}{r_i}_{\phi},

(30)

which vanishes for
p>n-k
.

Equation

29
was known to Jabotinsky
[
23
,
24
]
and to Écalle
[
14
,
15
,
17
]
, but
equation

30
was not. This makes Schröder’s original insight all the more remarkable.

{proof}

This expansion follows from the formal power-series expansion of exponentiation and from the linearity of the coefficients (
5
):

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{s}}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\binom{s}{p}(\phi-1)^{p}}=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\binom{s}{p}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{(\phi-1)^{p}}.

(31)

To justify the infinite linearity, we need to verify that the above infinite sum in
equation

31
is a finite number. We show that the summation of
equation

31
is in fact finite. Indeed, by
equation

6

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{(\phi-1)^{p}}=\sum_{k=r_{0}\leq\ldots\leq r_{p}=n}\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r_{i+1}}{r_i}_{\phi-1},

(32)

and by
equation

19
,
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{n}_{\phi-1}=0
, thus we can remove zeros from the sum in
equation

32
by tightening the restrictions of the sum from inequalities to strict ones:

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{(\phi-1)^{p}}=\sum_{k=r_{0}<\ldots<r_{p}=n}\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r_{i+1}}{r_i}_{\phi}.

(33)

Moreover, by the same argument used in the proof of
Section

3.2
,
equation

33
vanishes whenever
p>n-k
. Hence, the series in
equation

31
is indeed finite and terminates at
n-k
.

Years later, Jabotinsky extended the result to arbitrary
k
and provided a convenient alternative formulation
[
24
]
, expressing fractional iterates in terms of discrete iterates, which can be computed via
Section

3.1
.

{theorem}
[Jabotinsky
[
24
]
]
If
q=1
and
s\in\mathbb{C}
, the coefficients of
f^{s}
(
20
) are given by

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{s}}=\sum_{p=0}^{n-k}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{p}}\binom{s}{p}\binom{n-k-s}{n-k-p}.

Jabotinsky established the result by first proving it for integer values of
s
, and then extending it to arbitrary
s
using an argument based on the equality of polynomials on the integers (see also
[
26
]
). Our approach, by contrast, yields a direct proof.

{proof}

First, we use the binomial expansion and
equation

5
in the equation

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{(\phi-1)^{p}}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\sum_{\ell=0}^{p}\binom{p}{\ell}(-1)^{p-\ell}\phi^{\ell}}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{p}\binom{p}{\ell}(-1)^{p-\ell}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{\ell}},

which we substitute in
equation

29
. Exchanging sums and applying usual binomial identities
[
21
, Tab. 174]
yield the result.

More specifically, Jabotinsky derived the following formula:

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{s}}=\sum_{p=0}^{n-k}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{p}}\binom{s}{p}\binom{s-1-p}{n-k-p}(-1)^{n-k-p},

(34)

equivalent to the theorem through the identity

\binom{a}{b}(-1)^{b}=\binom{b-1-a}{b}.

(35)

An alternative expression of interest can be derived from Jabotinsky’s. Specifically, by substituting
n
with
n+k
and employing different combinatorial identities, it is possible to isolate most of the
s
-dependence in front:

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n+k}{k}_{\phi^{s}}=\binom{s}{n}\sum_{p=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n+k}{k}_{\phi^{p}}\binom{n}{p}\frac{s-n}{s-p}(-1)^{n-p},

(36)

valid for values of
s\notin\mathbb{N}
. This version has potential for the asymptotic study of the coefficient as
n
or
s
approaches infinity.

4.2
q
-calculus

The requirement that
f^{\prime}(0)=q=1
is crucial to prove
equation

33
, and it does not seem that the argument can be altered to cover the scenario where
q\neq 1
. Therefore, an entirely different analysis is necessary. We aim to motivate the application of the
q
-calculus
for this purpose, as the expression for the first few coefficients seems to point this way. When
s\in\mathbb{N}
, these can be computed via Taylor’s formula. By repeatedly applying the chain rule, we find for the first coefficient:

\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}f^{s}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|_{t=0}:=\left.\prod_{i=0}^{s-1}f^{\prime}(f^{i}(t))\right\rvert_{t=0}=q^{s}.

(37)

To derive the next term, we employ logarithmic differentiation on the product of
equation

37
to obtain

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}f^{s}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\right|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\left.\left\lparen\prod_{i=0}^{s-1}f^{\prime}(f^{i}(t))\right\rparen\left\lparen\sum_{i=0}^{s-1}\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(f^{i}(t))}{f^{\prime}(f^{i}(t))}\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}f^{\prime}(f^{j}(t))\right\rparen\right\rvert_{t=0}

(38)

\displaystyle=q^{s}\sum_{i=0}^{s-1}\frac{a_{2}}{q}q^{i}=[s]_{q}q^{s-1}a_{2},

(39)

where
[s]_{q}:=1+q+\ldots+q^{s-1}
is a
q
-integer
. This suggests that
q
-calculus is involved in the general formulation of the coefficients; this is also visible in
equation

15
since
[s]_{q}=\frac{q^{s}-1}{q-1}
for
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
q\neq 1
.

In the following, we will present basic elements of the theory that we will need in the next section to derive the formula. These elements will be directly taken from Kac’s monograph
[
25
]
on the subject, starting with the
q
-derivative
:

D_{q}f(t)=\frac{f(qt)-f(t)}{qt-t},

(40)

for
q\neq 1
. Observe that when
q\rightarrow 1
, the standard derivative is recovered. This limiting behavior is a typical aspect of
q
-calculus, that is, when allowing
q
to tend to
1
, we recover the “classical” calculus.

The previously defined
q
-integers satisfy a relationship with the
q
-derivative:

D_{q}t^{n}=[n]_{q}t^{n-1},

(41)

which follows from the geometric sum formula. After defining the
q
-factorial

[n]_{q}!:=[n]_{q}[n-1]_{q}\cdots[1]_{q}
, the above equation generalizes, for
p\leq n
, to

D_{q}^{p}t^{n}=\frac{[n]_{q}!}{[n-p]_{q}!}t^{n-p}.

(42)

Then, the
q
-binomial coefficients
are defined by

\binom{n}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}:=\frac{[n]_{q}!}{[p]_{q}![n-p]_{q}!},

(43)

and readily extended to complex upper argument as such:

\binom{s}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}:=\frac{[s]_{q}[s-1]_{q}\cdots[s-p+1]_{q}}{[p]_{q}!}=\frac{(q^{s}-1)(q^{s-1}-1)\cdots(q^{s-p+1}-1)}{(q^{p}-1)(q^{p-1}-1)\cdots(q-1)},

(44)

where
q
-complex
numbers are naturally defined by
[s]_{q}:=\frac{q^{s}-1}{q-1}
and
[s]_{1}:=s
.
Equation

44
makes clear why
q
is assumed different from a root of unity. These coefficients are useful to express the expansion of the
q
-analogue of a binomial, that is
Gauss’s formula
:

(a+b)_{q}^{n}:=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(a+bq^{i})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}\binom{n}{\ell}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{\binom{\ell}{2}}a^{n-\ell}b^{\ell}.

(45)

Finally, the
q
-analogue of Taylor’s formula for an arbitrary constant
c
is

f(t)=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}D_{q}^{p}f(c)\frac{(t-c)_{q}^{p}}{[p]_{q}!},

(46)

which will only be considered in the formal sense
[
25
, Sec. 8]
.

4.3
Extension of Schröder’s formula

Using the
q
-Taylor formula (
46
), we can now extend Schröder’s formula (
Section

4.1
) to the nonunitary case

{theorem}

Suppose that
q=f^{\prime}(0)
is not a root of unity. If
s\in\mathbb{C}
, the coefficients of
f^{s}
(
20
) are given by

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{s}}=\sum_{p=0}^{n-k}\binom{s}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{k(s-p)}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{(\phi-q^{k})_{q}^{p}},

(47)

with

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{(\phi-q^{k})_{q}^{p}}=\sum_{k=r_{0}\leq\ldots\leq r_{p}=n}\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\left\lparen\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r_{i+1}}{r_i}_{\phi}-q^{i+k}\delta_{r_{i+1}-r_{i}}\right\rparen,

(48)

which vanishes for
p>n-k
.

Note that, with
q\rightarrow 1
, the same argument used for
equation

33
can be applied to recover
equation

30
from
equation

48
.

{proof}

In proving Schröder’s formula, we relied on the binomial expansion of the exponential, which in that specific setting allowed the coefficient to be written as a finite sum. To obtain an analogous finite sum in the general case, the expansion must instead be performed using the
q
-Taylor formula (
46
):

\phi^{s}=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\binom{s}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}c^{s-p}(\phi-c)_{q}^{p}.

(49)

When
q=1
, it is natural to take
c=1
. In the general case, a choice
c=c(q)
that satisfies
c(1)=1
is thus necessary. The naive choice
c(q)=1
still produces a finite sum, but its upper limit becomes
n
rather than the sharper bound
n-k
obtained in
Section

4.1
. To recover the optimal upper bound
n-k
, the appropriate choice is
c(q)=q^{k}
. Therefore, by
equation

5

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{s}}=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\binom{s}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{k(s-p)}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{(\phi-q^{k})_{q}^{p}}.

(50)

Now, to show that the sum is finite (and ranging up to
n-k
), we first expand the coefficient in
equation

50
. By
equation

6

\displaystyle\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{(\phi-q^{k})_{q}^{p}}

\displaystyle=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}(\phi-q^{i+k})}=\sum_{k=r_{0}\leq\ldots\leq r_{p}=n}\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r_{i+1}}{r_i}_{\phi-q^{i+k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=r_{0}\leq\ldots\leq r_{p}=n}\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\left\lparen\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r_{i+1}}{r_i}_{\phi}-q^{i+k}\delta_{r_{i+1}-r_{i}}\right\rparen.

(51)

Now we show that the expression above vanishes whenever
p>n-k
. In this situation, the integers
R_{i}:=r_{i-k}
, for
i\in\left\llbracket k,n+1\right\rrbracket
, where the
r_{i}
are the summation indices, satisfy the inequalities

k=R_{k}\leq\ldots\leq R_{n+1}\leq n.

(52)

R
admits at least one fixed point
k
. Let
a\in\left\llbracket k,n\right\rrbracket
denote the largest fixed point. Then, by definition of
a
, the interval
\left\llbracket a+1,n+1\right\rrbracket
contains no fixed points of
R
.

Assume, for contradiction, that
R_{a+1}\geq a+1
. Since
a+1
is not a fixed point, we must have
R_{a+1}>a+1
. As
R
is nondecreasing, it follows that
R_{a+2}\geq R_{a+1}\geq a+2
. Iterating this argument yields
R_{n+1}\geq n+1
, contradicting
equation

52
. Hence,
a=R_{a}\leq R_{a+1}<a+1
which forces
R_{a}=R_{a+1}=a
. Translating this to
r
, set
\alpha=a-k\in\left\llbracket 0,n-k\right\rrbracket
. Then,
r_{\alpha}=r_{\alpha+1}=\alpha+k
. The
\alpha
-th factor in the product of
equation

51
equals

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r_{\alpha+1}}{r_\alpha}_{\phi}-q^{\alpha+k}\delta_{r_{\alpha+1}-r_{\alpha}}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{\alpha+ k}{\alpha+ k}-q^{\alpha+k}=0,

by
equation

19
. Therefore, the product itself is also
0
. This shows that the summands of
equation

50
vanish at the indices
p>n-k
.

4.4
Extension of Jabotinsky’s formula

The
q
-binomial
(\phi-q^{k})_{q}^{p}
appearing in
equation

47
may be used in either of the two forms given by
equation

45
. Retaining its product form yields the extension of Schröder’s formula obtained above. Expanding it as a sum of powers of
\phi
instead leads to the following extension of Jabotinsky’s formula (
Section

4.1
).

{theorem}

[Tambs Lyche
[
39
,
29
]
]
Suppose that
q=f^{\prime}(0)
is not a root of unity. If
s\in\mathbb{C}
, the coefficients of
f^{s}
(
20
) are given by

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{s}}=\sum_{p=0}^{n-k}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{p}}\binom{s}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}\binom{n-k-s}{n-k-p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{(n-p)(s-p)}.

(53)

{proof}

Similarly to the proof of
Section

4.1
, we expand the following using
equations

29
and
45
:

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{(\phi-q^{k})_{q}^{p}}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\sum_{\ell=0}^{p}\binom{p}{\ell}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{\binom{\ell}{2}}\phi^{p-\ell}(-q^{k})^{\ell}}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{p}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{p-\ell}}\binom{p}{\ell}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{\binom{\ell}{2}+k\ell}(-1)^{\ell}.

We substitute the above equation into the formula (
47
) and swap sums:

\displaystyle\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{s}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=0}^{n-k}\binom{s}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}\sum_{\ell=0}^{p}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{\ell}}\binom{p}{\ell}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{\binom{p-\ell}{2}+k(s-\ell)}(-1)^{p-\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-k}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{\ell}}q^{k(s-\ell)}\sum_{p=\ell}^{n-k}\binom{s}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}\binom{p}{\ell}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{\binom{p-\ell}{2}}(-1)^{p-\ell}.

We then use the combinatorial identity which follows from the definition of the
q
-binomial coefficient

\binom{s}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}\binom{p}{\ell}_{\mkern-7.0muq}=\binom{s}{\ell}_{\mkern-7.0muq}\binom{s-\ell}{p-\ell}_{\mkern-7.0muq},

and reindex the sum:

\displaystyle\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{s}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-k}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{\ell}}\binom{s}{\ell}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{k(s-\ell)}\sum_{p=\ell}^{n-k}\binom{s-\ell}{p-\ell}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{\binom{p-\ell}{2}}(-1)^{p-\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-k}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{\ell}}\binom{s}{\ell}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{k(s-\ell)}\sum_{p=0}^{n-k-\ell}\binom{s-\ell}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{\binom{p}{2}}(-1)^{p}.

The theorem is proved upon application of the identity
[
37
, Id. 3.9 with
q=1
and
m=i=0
]

\sum_{p=0}^{b}\binom{a}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{\binom{p}{2}}(-1)^{p}=\binom{b-a}{b}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{ba}.

(54)

The case
k=1
was identified by Lavoie and Tremblay
[
29
]
, who noted that it is a direct reformulation of a formula discovered much earlier by Tambs Lyche in 1927
[
39
]
. We can now extend this result to arbitrary natural values of
k
:

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{s}}=\sum_{p=0}^{n-k}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{p}}\binom{s}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}\binom{s-p-1}{n-k-p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{k(s-p)+\binom{n-k-p+1}{2}}(-1)^{n-k-p}.

(55)

Indeed, it follows from the identity

\binom{a}{b}_{\mkern-7.0muq}(-1)^{b}=\binom{b-a-1}{b}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{ba-\binom{b}{2}},

(56)

q
-analogue of
equation

35
.

Tambs Lyche proved this result for positive integers
s
although, like Jabotinsky, he argued that his formula was valid for all
s
, only to address the case
s=-1
, corresponding to inversion.

As we did in
equation

36
, we can factor out most of the dependence in
s
to arrive at the equivalent statement

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n+k}{k}_{\phi^{s}}=\binom{s}{n}_{\mkern-7.0muq}\sum_{p=0}^{n}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n+k}{k}_{\phi^{p}}\binom{n}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}\frac{[s]_{q}-[n]_{q}}{[s]_{q}-[p]_{q}}q^{k(s-p)+\binom{n-p}{2}}(-1)^{n-p},

(57)

which could be further simplified with the equality
\frac{[a]_{q}-[b]_{q}}{[c]_{q}-[d]_{q}}=\frac{q^{a}-q^{b}}{q^{c}-q^{d}}
.

4.5
Extension of Monkam’s formula

In this section, we generalize Monkam’s formula (
Section

3.2
) to fractional iteration. To this end, we introduce the
divided differences

[
32
, Chap. 1]
of a function
g
defined by the base case
g[x_{i}]:=g(x_{i})
and the recurrence relation

g[x_{i},\dots,x_{j}]:=\frac{g[x_{i+1},\dots,x_{j}]-g[x_{i},\dots,x_{j-1}]}{x_{j}-x_{i}}.

(58)

They are given precisely by the equation
[
32
, §1.31]

g[x_{0},\dots,x_{p}]=\sum_{i=0}^{p}\frac{g(x_{i})}{\prod_{j\neq i}(x_{i}-x_{j})}.

(59)

In
Section

3.2
, we used the complete homogeneous symmetric polynomials, which are related to divided differences by the following equation:

h_{s-p}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{p})=[x_{0},\dots,x_{p}]^{s},

(60)

where
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
[x_{0},\dots,x_{p}]^{s}
represents
g[x_{0},\dots,x_{p}]
when
g(x)=x^{s}
.
Equation

60
can be proved via
Sylvester’s identity

[
34
]

h_{s-p}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{p})=\sum_{i=0}^{p}\frac{x_{i}^{s}}{\prod_{j\neq i}(x_{i}-x_{j})}

(61)

and
equation

59
. When
s
is not an integer, the symmetric polynomial cannot be used, since it is defined only for integer indices. This motivates the use of divided differences instead, which naturally leads to the following generalization.

{theorem}

Suppose that
q=f^{\prime}(0)
is not a root of unity. If
s\in\mathbb{C}
, the coefficients of
f^{s}
(
20
) are given by

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{s}}=\sum_{p=0}^{n-k}\sum_{k=r_{0}<\ldots<r_{p}=n}[q^{r_{0}},\dots,q^{r_{p}}]^{s}\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{r_{i+1}}{r_i}_{\phi},

(62)

At
p=1
, we have
r_{0}=k
and
r_{1}=n
, therefore

[q^{r_{0}},q^{r_{p}}]^{s}=\frac{q^{ks}-q^{ns}}{q^{k}-q^{n}}=q^{k(s-1)}[s]_{q^{n-k}},

(63)

where
[s]_{q^{n-k}}
is a
q^{n-k}
-complex number. At
p=n-k
, the conditions of the summation force
r_{i}=k+i
for
i\in\left\llbracket 0,p\right\rrbracket
, thus we have only one term and the divided differences are given by

[q^{k},q^{k+1},\dots,q^{n}]^{s}=\binom{s}{n-k}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{k(s-n+k)}.

(64)

Equation

64
is also sufficient to show that letting
q\rightarrow 1
in
equation

62
leads to Schröder’s formula.

The linearity of the divided differences enables a simple proof for
Section

4.5
.

{proof}
[of
Section

4.5
]
By
Section

4.3
, Gauss’s formula (
45
) and Monkam’s formula (
24
),

\displaystyle\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{s}}={}

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}\binom{s}{j}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{k(s-j)}\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}\binom{j}{\ell}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{\binom{j-\ell}{2}+k(j-\ell)}(-1)^{j-\ell}

5
The iterative logarithm

The iterative logarithm is a natural object of study in iteration theory
[
17
,
24
]
. It is defined by the equation

\itlog(f):=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}f^{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right|_{s=0},

(71)

and is an analog of the natural logarithm, with composition playing the role of multiplication. For instance,
\itlog(f^{s})=s\itlog(f)
. Although this definition was introduced by Frege in 1874
[
20
,
22
]
, the iterative logarithm had already appeared, under a different form, in Collins’s 1836 work
[
11
]
.

The iterative logarithm also arises naturally in umbral calculus. Indeed, the logarithm of an umbral operator
\phi
is given explicitly by
\text{x}\itlog(f)(D)
, where
x
denotes multiplication by
x
and
D
differentiation with respect to
x

[
8
]
.

The convergence of the expansion of
\itlog(f)
was shown to be equivalent to the embeddability of
f

[
19
]
. The first terms of its (typically divergent) expansion were derived by Korkine
[
27
, p. 241]
, and the case
f^{\prime}(0)=q=1
was later fully determined by Jabotinsky
[
24
]
. We now present the expansion in wider generality, in forms similar to Schröder’s formula (
Section

4.3
) and Tambs Lyche’s formula (
Section

4.4
).

{theorem}

Suppose that
q=f^{\prime}(0)
is not a root of unity. The iterative logarithm of
f
is given by

\itlog(f)(t)=t\log q+\frac{\log q}{q-1}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\sum_{p=1}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{p-1}}{[p]_{q}}q^{-\binom{p+1}{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{1}_{(\phi-q)_{q}^{p}},

(72)

where the coefficient term is given by
equation

48
.

{theorem}

Under the same conditions, we have

\itlog(f)(t)=t\log q+\frac{\log q}{q-1}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\sum_{p=1}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{p-1}}{[p]_{q}}\binom{n-1}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{\binom{p+1}{2}-pn}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{1}_{\phi^{p}},

(73)

where the coefficient term is given by
Section

3.1
.

It is worth noting that the index of the first nonzero coefficient (the
order
) is
1
whenever
q\neq 1
, whereas it is at least
2
when
q=1
.

{proof}

[of
Sections

5

5
]
To differentiate the fractional iterates, we first need an expression for the derivative of the
q
-binomial coefficient. For this purpose, we apply logarithmic differentiation:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\log\binom{s}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{p-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\log[s-\ell]_{q}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{p-1}\frac{q^{s-\ell}}{[s-\ell]_{q}}\frac{\log q}{q-1},

and deduce that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\binom{s}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}=\binom{s}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}\frac{\log q}{q-1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{p-1}\frac{q^{s-\ell}}{[s-\ell]_{q}}=\frac{\log q}{q-1}\left\lparen\frac{[s-1]_{q}\cdots[s-p+1]_{q}}{[p]_{q}!}q^{s}+\binom{s}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}\sum_{\ell=1}^{p-1}\frac{q^{s-\ell}}{[s-\ell]_{q}}\right\rparen.

Evaluating the latter at
s=0
yields

\begin{gathered}\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\binom{s}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}\right|_{s=0}=\frac{\log q}{q-1}\frac{[-1]_{q}\cdots[-p+1]_{q}}{[p]_{q}!}\\
=\frac{\log q}{q-1}\frac{[1]_{q}\cdots[p-1]_{q}}{[p]_{q}!}\prod_{k=1}^{p-1}(-q^{-k})=\frac{\log q}{q-1}\frac{(-1)^{p-1}}{[p]_{q}}q^{-\binom{p}{2}}.\end{gathered}

By the Leibniz product rule

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\binom{s}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{s}=\left\lparen\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\binom{s}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}\right\rparen q^{s}+\binom{s}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{s}\log q.

(74)

At
s=0
, when

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{1}_{\phi^{s}}\right|_{s=0}

\displaystyle=(\log q)\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{1}_{1}+\sum_{p=1}^{n-1}\frac{\log q}{q-1}\frac{(-1)^{p-1}}{[p]_{q}}q^{-\binom{p}{2}}q^{-p}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{1}_{(\phi-q)_{q}^{p}}

\displaystyle=(\log q)\delta_{n-1}+\frac{\log q}{q-1}\sum_{p=1}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{p-1}}{[p]_{q}}q^{-\binom{p+1}{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{1}_{(\phi-q)_{q}^{p}},

hence the first formula holds. The second formula is derived in a manner analogous to the proof of

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{p-1}}{[p]_{q}}q^{-\binom{p+1}{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{1}_{(\phi-q)_{q}^{p}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p=1}^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{p-1}}{[p]_{q}}q^{-\binom{p+1}{2}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{p}\binom{p}{\ell}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{\binom{p-\ell}{2}}(-q)^{p-\ell}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{1}_{\phi^{\ell}}
