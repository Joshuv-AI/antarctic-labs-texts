Sustained remission with PD-1 and BTK inhibitors maintenance after chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy in CNS lymphoma
Authors: Lingzi Yu, Nana Ping, Rui Zou, Qian Zhu, Junhong Li, Xiao Zhang, Fan Xia, Jiajie He, Jiajie Tu, Danqing Kong, Depei Wu, Zhengming Jin, Changju Qu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy is gradually reshaping the treatment paradigm for patients with central nervous system lymphoma (CNSL). However, the duration of remission (DOR) has remained the major challenge in existing studies. This study aimed to provide real-world long-term follow-up data on CAR-T therapy in CNSL patients and primarily investigate the impact of maintenance therapy on prolonging DOR.MethodsThis study evaluated the efficacy and safety of CAR-T therapy in 22 patients with CNSL, including 5 with primary CNSL and 17 with secondary CNSL (2025004, retrospectively registered). Maintenance therapy with programmed cell death protein-1 inhibitor (PD-1i) and Bruton’s tyrosine kinase inhibitor (BTKi) was initiated in responding patients, while subgroup analysis comparing maintenance versus non-maintenance was restricted to those who achieved complete remission after CAR-T therapy.ResultsThe best overall remission rate was 90.9%, with the best complete remission rate of 68.2%. With a median follow-up of 20.7 months (range, 3.1–88.9 months), the estimated 2-year progression-free survival and overall survival rates were 59.1% and 71.6%, respectively. No patient suffered severe immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome, and only 3 patients experienced severe cytokine release syndrome. Patients who underwent PD-1i and BTKi maintenance achieved a 2-year DOR rate of 100%, which was significantly superior to those without maintenance therapy (Log-rank, P = 0.04).ConclusionOur findings suggested the promising efficacy and manageable toxicities of CAR-T therapy in patients with CNSL. This study preliminarily proposed the concept that maintenance therapy may improve the DOR after CAR-T therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-025-04236-4.Keywords: Central nervous system lymphoma, Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy, Bruton’s tyrosine kinase inhibitor, Programmed cell death protein-1 inhibitor, Duration of remission

Introduction

Central nervous system lymphoma (CNSL) is a highly aggressive subset of non-Hodgkin lymphoma with a dismal prognosis, including primary CNSL (PCNSL) and secondary CNSL (SCNSL) [1]. PCNSL is a distinct form of lymphoma confined to the brain, spinal cord, leptomeninges, or eyes, with no evidence of systemic involvement. The randomized International Extranodal Lymphoma Study Group trial IELSG32 demonstrated excellent outcomes of the MATRix (methotrexate, cytarabine, thiotepa, rituximab) regimen followed by consolidation therapy with either autologous stem cell transplantation or whole brain radiation in patients with PCNSL [2, 3]. However, real-world studies revealed that 25% of patients were refractory to the MATRix regimen. In addition, 65% experienced serious infectious complications during MATRix treatment, and more than half required dose reductions or treatment delays. SCNSL refers to systemic lymphoma involving the central nervous system, either at initial diagnosis or in the setting of disease relapse. Most patients with SCNSL develop CNSL involvement during ongoing treatment and undergo multiple lines of therapy, therefore having a worse prognosis than those with SCNSL at initial diagnosis. Moreover, due to the heterogeneous patient cohorts and limited clinical trials, effective therapies remain uncertain, and a standard of care has yet to be established.

Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy has revolutionized the treatment of patients with relapsed/refractory (R/R) B-cell lymphoma. Though most CNSL cases belong to B-cell lymphoma, patients with CNSL were initially excluded from most pivotal clinical trials due to concerns about increased risks of cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), challenges posed by the blood–brain barrier, the heterogeneous tumor microenvironment, and limited CAR-T expansion in the absence of peripheral lesions [4, 5]. Nevertheless, the TRANSCEND trial included 6 evaluable patients with CNSL involvement and showed reasonable efficacy and safety, providing preliminary insights into the feasibility of CAR-T therapy in this population [6]. Recently, Frigault et al. reported that CAR-T can expand and traffic to the central nervous system, reinforcing the feasibility of CAR-T therapy for CNSL patients [7]. Although previous studies have demonstrated encouraging efficacy and safety of CAR-T therapy in CNSL patients, there remains substantial potential for further improvements [8–11]. Notably, the short duration of remission (DOR) in the available data is the main challenge [12–14]. Therefore, it is imperative to explore optimal strategies to improve the efficacy, in particular DOR, of CAR-T therapy in CNSL patients.

Here, we reported our institutional experience of CNSL patients treated with CAR-T therapy at the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. We showed and analyzed the observed results in detail. Notably, our study raised the preliminary hypothesis that maintenance therapy may improve the long-term prognosis of CAR-T therapy in CNSL patients.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a real-world retrospective study evaluating the clinical efficacy and safety of CAR-T therapy in patients with CNSL at the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between September 2017 and March 2024. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (2025004) and was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The relevant data required for the analysis were collected from the electronic medical record. Next-generation exon sequencing was used for genetic mutation examination of lymphoma-related genes.

CAR-T therapy

The peripheral blood (PB) lymphocytes were acquired through density gradient separation from patients’ PB. T lymphocytes were further separated using degradable anti-CD3 magnetic microbeads and activated with 5 μg/mL CD3 and CD28 for 18–24 h. T cells were then transduced with recombinant lentiviral vectors containing the anti-CD19/CD20/22 single-chain variable fragment (scFv), the 4-1BB costimulatory moiety, and the CD3z T-cell activation domain by adding lentivirus directly to the medium. After 48 h, transduced cells were washed and expanded in X-vivo 15 medium (Lonza, GA) with 5 ng/mL interleukin-7 (Peprotech, NJ, USA) and interleukin-15 (Peprotech, NJ) cytokines under a 37 °C, 5% CO2 humidified condition and maintained at a concentration of 0.5 × 106 cells/mL for 12–20 days until their numbers met the preset value. Finally, the second-generation CAR-T targeting CD19, CD20, or CD22 were harvested, washed with 0.9% saline, and concentrated in cryopreservation followed by quality tests before infusion. Acceptance criteria for CAR-T cell infusion were as follows: (1) Trypan blue viability > 90%, (2) CAR transduction efficiency > 10%, (3) purity of CD3 + cell > 95%, CD19 + cell: negative, (4) killing ability in vitro > 10%, (5) cytokine release capacity > 10%, (6) Gram stain: negative, endotoxin < 3 EU/mL, (7) mycoplasma (polymerase chain reaction): negative, and (8) fungus: negative. All CAR-T products were provided by the UnicarTherapy Bio-medicine Technology Co. CAR-T expansions were analyzed as described previously [15–17].

Maintenance therapy

The maintenance therapy consisted of a programmed cell death protein-1 inhibitor (PD-1i) combined with a Bruton’s tyrosine kinase inhibitor (BTKi), given the synergistic effect and feasibility of this combination. The specific regimens were tislelizumab (200 mg intravenously every 3 weeks) with either zanubrutinib (160 mg orally twice daily), orelabrutinib (150 mg orally once daily), or ibrutinib (560 mg orally once daily). Before 2020, the concept of maintenance therapy following CAR-T therapy was not yet established. Thus, no patients were eligible for maintenance therapy before that time. From 2020 to 2024, the criteria for maintenance therapy included: (1) achievement of complete remission (CR) or partial remission (PR) at 1 month post-CAR-T infusion; (2) recovery from CAR-T-associated toxicities, specifically CRS or ICANS; (3) adequate hematologic function, defined as an absolute neutrophil count ≥ 1.0 × 109 /L and an absolute platelet count ≥ 80 × 109 /L, independent of growth factor or transfusion support within 7 days before maintenance initiation; (4) willingness and ability to adhere to the prolonged treatment regimen. For eligible patients, maintenance therapy was initiated as soon as CR or PR was achieved. The maintenance regimen was planned to continue for up to 1 year or until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity, whichever occurred first.

Endpoints and assessments

The primary endpoint was overall remission rate (ORR, calculated as the combined rates of CR and PR). Response assessments were scheduled at protocol-defined time points (1, 3, 6, 9, 12, 18, 24 months after CAR-T infusion) using positron emission tomography-computed tomography and/or brain magnetic resonance imaging. After 2 years, assessments were performed with contrast-enhanced computed tomography and/or brain magnetic resonance imaging every 6 months until the end of the fifth year post-CAR-T infusion, and annually thereafter. Additional scans were obtained at any time if clinical progression was suspected. Systemic disease response and CNSL response were assessed by the investigators according to Lugano criteria and International Primary CNS Lymphoma Collaborative Group guidelines, respectively [18, 19]. Secondary endpoints included progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), DOR, the incidence of adverse events (AEs), and CAR-T copies in PB. CRS and ICANS were documented by physicians based on consensus guidelines from the American Society of Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (ASTCT) [20]. Other AEs were evaluated according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0. PFS was defined as the time from CAR-T infusion to disease progression or death from any cause. Patients without an event were censored at the last disease assessment date. OS was defined as the time from CAR-T infusion to death from any cause. Surviving patients were censored at the date of last follow-up. Patients who were lost to follow-up were censored at the date they were last known to be alive. DOR was defined as the time from the first remission to relapse or death from any cause. Patients who remained alive without relapse were censored at the last disease assessment date.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.0). Continuous variables were reported as medians and ranges, and categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables with normal distribution were compared by Student’s t-test. Continuous variables with abnormal distribution were compared through Mann–Whitney test and visualized via Box-plot. The Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate survival probabilities, including PFS, OS, and DOR. The log-rank test was used to determine significant survival differences between groups of individuals. P values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patients and baseline characteristics

A total of 22 CNSL patients treated with CAR-T therapy between September 2017 and March 2024 were included in our study. These patients comprised 5 PCNSL and 17 SCNSL patients, with a median age of 53 years (range, 29–70y), and 68.2% of patients were male. The most common histological subtype was diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (77.3%), followed by Burkitt’s lymphoma (9.1%), follicular lymphoma (9.1%), and mantle cell lymphoma (4.5%). Sixteen patients had brain parenchymal involvement, while 10 patients had leptomeningeal infiltration, with 4 patients showing involvement at both sites. This cohort included 12 patients with primary refractory disease and 10 patients with relapsed disease following initial treatment. Among SCNSL patients, 2 patients had CNSL involvement at the initial diagnosis, while 15 patients with systemic disease developed CNSL involvement during treatment or at relapse. The median number of previous treatment lines was 2 (range, 1–3) in the SCNSL group and 3 (range, 1–4) in the PCNSL group, respectively. Of the 22 CNSL patients, 13 patients were in a state of progressive disease at CAR-T infusion. Regarding conditioning regimens, 7 patients received the FC lymphodepletion regimen, and 15 patients received the azacitidine (AZA)/decitabine (DAC)-FC lymphodepletion regimen. Seven patients underwent CAR-T therapy following autologous stem cell transplantation, and 7 patients received autologous stem cell infusion after CAR-T therapy to support hematologic recovery. Additionally, 9 patients received PD-1i and BTKi as maintenance therapy for 1 year post-CAR-T therapy. The median time from the first remission to the initiation of PD-1i and BTKi maintenance was only 0.4 months (range, 0.0–0.7 months). More baseline characteristics were shown in Table 1. Efficacy and survival

The best ORR was 90.9%, with the best CR rate (CRR) of 68.2% in the entire cohort. In the PCNSL cohort, 4 out of 5 patients (80.0%) achieved CR at 3 months after CAR-T infusion, including 1 patient who initially showed PR at 1 month but subsequently improved to CR by 3 months. In the SCNSL cohort, 16 patients (94.1%) responded within 3 months, with 11 patients (64.7%) achieving CR and 5 patients attaining PR, respectively. Moreover, the patients achieving CR were characterized by lower baseline ferritin levels compared to those failing to achieve CR (Mann–Whitney test, P = 0.02) (supplementary Fig. 1).

With a median follow-up of 20.7 months (range, 3.1–88.9 months) in the entire cohort, the median PFS and OS were unreached, with estimated 2-year PFS and OS rates of 59.1% and 71.6%, respectively (Fig. 1A–B). With a median follow-up of 17.8 months (range, 6.2–58.4 months) in the PCNSL group, both estimated 2-year PFS and OS rates were 80.0%, respectively. With a median follow-up of 26.4 months (range, 3.1–88.9 months) in the SCNSL group, the 2-year PFS and OS rates were 52.9% and 68.8%, respectively (Fig. 1C–D). Individual responses over time were shown in Fig. 2. To date, 8 patients, including 1 PCNSL patient and 7 SCNSL patients, experienced disease progression with a median time from CAR-T therapy to disease progression of 5.2 months (range, 2.7–8.3 months). This cohort of progressing patients included 2 primary non-responders (both of whom had stable disease at 1 month and progressed at 3.3 and 6.4 months post CAR-T therapy, respectively) and 6 patients who relapsed after an initial response. Of these relapsed patients, 5 patients experienced CNSL progression alone, 2 had progression in systemic lymphoma alone, and 1 had both CNSL and systemic progression simultaneously. Among patients who were eligible for salvage therapies, 1 patient received local radiation, 1 patient received allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT), 1 patient received intrathecal methotrexate, 1 patient received tislelizumab and orelabrutinib, and 1 patient received allo-HSCT combined with glofitamab. However, only the patient who received allo-HSCT combined with glofitamab was successfully salvaged and remained in ongoing CR. The median OS of the patients experiencing disease progression was only 7.5 months (range, 4.4–12.9 months) (Individual responses over time were shown in Fig. 3A).

Safety

All AEs occurring within 1 month after CAR-T infusion were recorded and reported for the 22 (100%) patients. CRS was observed in 12 out of 22 patients (54.5%), including 3 PCNSL patients and 9 SCNSL patients. Among these, 5 patients experienced a fever exceeding 38 °C and were diagnosed with grade 1 CRS, and 4 patients exhibited grade 2 CRS characterized by fever accompanied by hypotension or hypoxemia. Three patients (13.6%) presented with grade 3 CRS, requiring vasopressor support for hypotension or high-concentration supplemental oxygen for hypoxemia. Only 1 patient experienced grade 1 ICANS, manifesting as drowsiness but spontaneous awakening. In total, 3 patients were managed for immune-related AEs with dexamethasone, and both CRS and ICANS were controlled ultimately in all cases. The difference in peak CRP between patients with CRS and those without CRS showed statistical significance (t-test, P = 0.02). The peak IL-6 showed a trend toward elevated values in patients with CRS compared to those without (Mann–Whitney test, P = 0.08) (supplementary Fig. 2). Regarding other vital AEs, 21 patients developed severe neutropenia (grade 3/4), while none died from infection. Meanwhile, 17 patients experienced severe thrombocytopenia (grade 3/4), and 1 patient died from intracerebral hemorrhage due to prolonged thrombocytopenia at 3.1 months after CAR-T infusion. Moreover, all 9 patients completed the planned 1-year PD-1i and BTKi maintenance without any severe AEs.

CAR-T copies

In this study, 21 patients were tested for absolute CAR-T copies to evaluate in vivo expansion. The remaining patient declined the test of CAR-T copies for personal reasons. The median peak CAR-T count in vivo across all patients was 22,300 /mL (range, 823–2,340,000 /mL), with median values of 23,750 /mL in SCNSL patients and 18,800 /mL in PCNSL patients. Patients with SCNSL and PCNSL achieved peak levels with a median time of 0.3 (range, 0.2–0.8 months) and 0.3 (range, 0.2–0.4 months) months after CAR-T infusion, respectively.

Discussion

Herein, we presented the long-term promising outcomes of CAR-T therapy in 22 patients with CNSL, including 5 patients with PCNSL and 17 patients with SCNSL. Notably, for patients with CNSL, our study provided a more precise evaluation of the efficacy of CAR-T therapy in an extended follow-up period. Furthermore, it preliminarily explored the potential of maintenance therapy in improving DOR.

Our study supplemented the existing evidence regarding the feasibility of CAR-T therapy for CNSL patients and explored potential strategies to improve initial remission. In a meta-analysis by Cook et al., including 128 CNSL patients (SCNSL n = 98, PCNSL n = 30) across 15 studies, grade 3/4 CRS was reported in 11% of SCNSL and 13% of PCNSL patients, and grade 3/4 neurotoxicity occurred in 26% and 18%, respectively [21]. The meta-analysis compared its safety data with data from ZUMA-1 and JULIET trials, demonstrating the feasibility of CAR-T therapy in patients even with CNSL involvement [4, 5]. In our cohort, grade 3/4 CRS occurred in 13.6% (17.6% of SCNSL patients and none of PCNSL patients) of the patients, with no cases experiencing grade 3/4 ICANS. Our findings were consistent with those in the meta-analysis, further supporting the safety of CAR-T therapy in CNSL. For efficacy, the meta-analysis reported ORRs of 64% in PCNSL and 57% in SCNSL, with CRRs of 56% and 47%, respectively. In our study, we observed superior ORRs of 90.9% (SCNSL: 94.1%, PCNSL: 80.0%) and CRRs of 68.2% (SCNSL: 64.7%, PCNSL: 80.0%). The high remission rates may be related to the improved lymphodepletion regimen and the dual-target CAR-T product in our cohort. As the abnormal hypermethylation of tumor suppressor genes is common in CNSL, 15 patients received the AZA/DAC-FC lymphodepletion regimen, with 93.3% achieving remission [22–24]. AZA and DAC can restore the anti-tumor functions of T cells by reversing tumor suppressor gene hypermethylation, upregulate CD19 expression on lymphoma cells, and enhance the proliferation of CAR-T, thereby comprehensively improving the efficacy of CAR-T therapy [25–27]. As antigen escape is a well-established mechanism of resistance in CD19 CAR-T therapy, 17 patients received a CD19/CD22 CAR-T product, with 94.1% achieving remission [28, 29]. Frank et al. reported that patients who progressed after CD19 CAR-T therapy or with CD19-negative disease achieved an ORR of 65.5% (reported as 66% in the original article) and a CRR of 51.7% (reported as 52% in the original article) at a dose of 1 million CD22 CAR-T cells per kilogram of body weight. These outcomes demonstrated that CAR-T therapy targeting CD22 was active in patients with R/R large B-cell lymphoma (LBCL), establishing CD22 as a potential target for CAR-T therapy in LBCL [30, 31]. Taken together, our study not only reconfirmed the safety and efficacy of CAR-T therapy in CNSL patients but also highlighted potential approaches to optimize efficacy relative to previous reports, suggesting a meaningful step forward in CAR-T therapy for CNSL. Additionally, it is essential to note that though CAR-T therapy in CNSL has achieved promising results under close monitoring in specialized centers, CAR-T therapy for CNSL remains off-label or unavailable in most countries. Under this situation, clinical trial participation remains the recommended setting.

Moreover, our study preliminarily explored the possibility of PD-1i and BTKi maintenance in improving the DOR following CAR-T therapy. As recent multicenter studies mentioned, the limited DOR was still the central problem in CAR-T therapy for CNSL. Mercadal et al. reported R/R PCNSL patients receiving CAR-T therapy (23 patients available to assess) with 2-year PFS and OS rates of only 28.0% and 50.0%, respectively [13]. Epperla et al. described 61 R/R SCNSL patients treated with CAR-T therapy and reported that the 1-year PFS rate was only 16.0% with a median PFS of 3.3 months [10]. In our cohort, the estimated 2-year PFS and OS rates were 59.1% (SCNSL: 52.9%, PCNSL: 80.0%) and 71.6% (SCNSL: 68.8%, PCNSL: 80.0%), respectively. Of note, among CR patients, the estimated 2-year DOR rate was 100.0% with PD-1i and BTKi maintenance (n = 8) versus 57.1% without maintenance (n = 7) (Log-rank, P = 0.04). Although complex baseline characteristics limited direct cross-study comparisons, our data preliminarily showed a trend toward sustained remission in patients receiving this maintenance regimen. In our study, to minimize potential bias from differences in response depth, the subgroup analysis was restricted to patients who achieved CR. However, response assessments were based solely on radiographic criteria in our research and did not include more sensitive measures such as circulating tumor DNA. Thus, a comprehensive evaluation of residual disease burden and its balance between the maintenance and non-maintenance groups was not available. Furthermore, due to the small sample size, other baseline differences between the two groups cannot be reliably compared. Therefore, the benefit of PD-1i and BTKi maintenance in improving long-term outcomes of CAR-T therapy in CNSL patients remained hypothesis-generating. The exact benefit should be validated through prospective studies with larger cohorts.

In our study, although PD-1i and BTKi maintenance appeared to sustain remission after CAR-T therapy, it is also imperative to acknowledge that this maintenance regimen may not be universally effective. CAR-T exhaustion with loss of a functional immune response against antigen-bearing tumor cells is widely recognized as a significant cause of CAR-T therapy failure in lymphoma. Although the PD-1/PD-L1 axis is a clinically targetable mechanism associated with T-cell exhaustion, the clinical utility of PD-1i for durable remission following CAR-T therapy was disappointing. Roddie et al. reported results using CAR-T therapy in combination with pembrolizumab in R/R LBCL. Among all patients who received infusion, only 25.8% remained progression-free at 1 year with a median PFS of 3.3 months, indicating that pembrolizumab failed to prevent relapse in a substantial proportion of patients receiving CAR-T therapy [32]. Moreover, pembrolizumab as salvage therapy showed inferior results in patients with B-cell lymphoma who were either refractory to or relapsed after CAR-T therapy, with the best ORR of 25.0% and the best CRR of 8.3%, representing the significant heterogeneity in the efficacy of pembrolizumab [33]. These results collectively indicated that CAR-T exhaustion may be driven by multifactorial mechanisms and exhibit considerable interpersonal heterogeneity in its dominant factor. Therefore, targeting a single mechanism may be insufficient to improve CAR-T exhaustion. Our study adopted the combination of PD-1i and BTKi as maintenance therapy, initiated promptly following CAR-T-induced remission. For patients achieving CR, the 2-year DOR rate was 100.0% under a median follow-up of 29.2 months. Although our findings preliminarily suggested that simultaneously targeting multiple mechanisms may improve outcomes after CAR-T therapy, this result was limited by its small sample size. In truth, there are many other mechanisms of CAR-T exhaustion in a larger population, and our maintenance regimen cannot cover all mechanisms in all patients. Therefore, future studies can integrate multi-omics approaches in larger cohorts, such as single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics, to comprehensively characterize the immune escape landscape and understand discrete mechanisms of CAR-T exhaustion in a given patient, providing personalized maintenance strategies to prevent relapse effectively.

At last, the development of salvage therapies is equally urgent. Among the 5 patients receiving salvage therapy, only 1 patient receiving allo-HSCT combined with glofitamab achieved CR after salvage therapy, indicating that salvage therapies are still limited and costly till now. We need to recognize that even a maintenance regimen based on individualized immunological insights cannot guarantee translation into tangible clinical benefits for every patient. Therefore, the development of effective salvage regimens remains a complementary and imperative endeavor.

Conclusion

Several fascinating insights can be drawn from this study. First, this study reconfirmed the promising efficacy and manageable safety of CAR-T therapy for patients with CNSL. Second, our findings suggested the potential benefit and feasibility of combining PD-1i with BTKi as maintenance therapy to sustain remission after CAR-T infusion. While these results were encouraging, certain limitations of our study should be noted. These findings were derived from a small cohort and were based on retrospective analysis. Further prospective randomized controlled trials with expanded sample sizes will be essential to confirm these preliminary observations. Moreover, elucidating the mechanisms underlying post-CAR-T therapy relapse of CNSL patients will be beneficial to refine the maintenance regimen and guide the selection of personalized maintenance strategies ultimately.
