Seroprevalence and associated risk factors of Peste des petits ruminants in goats and sheep in two zones, Oromia Region, Ethiopia
Authors: Lencho Megersa Marami, Bedaso Mammo Edao, Tesfaye Rufael Chibssa, Hika Waktole Ayana, Kebede Debebe Sedessa, Kemal Emiyu, Sanne Charles Bodjo, Fufa Dawo Bari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeste des Petits Ruminants (PPR) is a severe, contagious, and transboundary viral disease of goats, sheep, camels, and wild small ruminants in more than 70 countries, including Ethiopia. Hence, a cross-sectional study was conducted from September 2023 to June 2024 to assess the seroprevalence of PPR and investigate risk factors of the disease in the West Arsi and West Hararghe zones. A total of 751 serum samples were collected from 398 goats and 353 sheep of 422 flocks found in six districts of the two zones that had not a history of PPR vaccination. A hemagglutinin-based blocking enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay was used to test the serum for antibodies against PPR. A structured questionnaire survey was used to determine potential risk factors of the disease. The overall PPR seroprevalence in the West Arsi and West Hararghe zones was 9.7% at the flock level (95% CI: 7.1–12.9) and 5.9% at the animal level (95% CI: 4.4–7.8). At the species level, the seroprevalence of PPR was found to be 5.3% in goats (95% CI: 3.5–8.0) and 6.5% in sheep (95% CI: 4.4–9.6). Multivariable logistic regression analysis indicated that a geographic zone was a significant predictor of PPR infection in goats and at the flock level. Flocks in the West Hararghe Zone were 4.5 times more likely to be seropositive than those in West Arsi (95% CI: 2.0–9.8, p < 0.001). Age was a significant predictor of PPR exposure in both goats and sheep, whereas altitude was specifically a risk factor in sheep. The present study findings indicate a generally low PPR seroprevalence in both goats and sheep in the study areas. However, the disease was present in 20 out of the 26 kebeles (wards) surveyed, showing ongoing circulation. Therefore, vaccination campaigns should be expanded to cover both zones, with emphasis on West Hararghe. A further study is recommended to identify the influence of agroecology on PPR persistence and transmission.Keywords: Ethiopia, Peste des petits ruminants, Risk factors, SeroprevalenceSubject terms: Diseases, Immunology, Microbiology, Zoology

Introduction

Ethiopia possesses one of the largest small ruminant populations globally, with an estimated 52.5 million goats and 42.9 million sheep, accounting for approximately 10% of Africa’s and 4% of the world’s small ruminant population1,2. In developing countries such as Ethiopia, the livelihoods of millions of poor households and farmers primarily rely on these animals for meat, milk, and monetary income3,4. Annually, more than 1.13 million goats and 1.1 million sheep are slaughtered for meat consumption in Ethiopia5. In addition, goat milk is widely believed to have medicinal value and is commonly consumed by patients, the elderly, and children6. Farmers favor goats and sheep production over other livestock such as cattle because they are small, prolific, and highly adaptable ruminants that offer rapid economic returns with low input costs4.

Despite their socioeconomic importance, small ruminant production is considerably hindered by infectious diseases, notably Peste des Petits Ruminants (PPR), which is among the most lethal diseases affecting these animals. PPR is a highly infectious, contagious, and transboundary viral disease affecting sheep and goats and other animals like camels and several wild small ruminant species3,7. The disease was first identified in Côte d’Ivoire in 19428 and it is now widespread in more than three continents, including Africa, Asia, the Middle East, and Europe, and is endemic in over 70 countries3,9. It is caused by a virus known as Morbillivirus caprinae, characterized by a single serotype and four genetic lineages10,11. The disease can cause severe morbidity up to 100% with mortality ranging from 50% to 100% in immunologically naïve sheep and goat populations12. Beyond its direct effects on animal health, PPR disrupts livestock trade, limits the introduction of improved breeds, and constrains the development of intensive small ruminant production systems13. Overall economic losses attributable to PPR globally range between USD 1.4 and USD 2.1 billion per year9, including an estimated vaccination cost of USD 38014,15.

In response to noted challenges, the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH) and the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) launched the Global PPR Eradication Strategy in 201514, with the aim to eradicate the disease by 2030. Ethiopia has joined the initiative and set a national target to eliminate the disease by 202716. However, updated epidemiological data remain scarce in high-risk mixed crop-livestock production systems, such as those in the West Arsi and West Hararghe zones, which border PPR-endemic areas like the Afar and Somali Regions17–19 and the Guji Zone in the Oromia Region. Generating updated information on disease seroprevalence and associated risk factors across these diverse ecological settings is critical for guiding vaccination, surveillance, and targeted control strategies, thereby improving small ruminant health and supporting rural households’ livelihoods and food security.

Materials and methods

Study area description

The investigation of PPR seroepidemiology and associated risk factors was conducted in two zones of the Oromia Regional State, the West Arsi and West Hararghe zones (Fig. 1).

Map of the PPR study area. The map was drawn using ArcGIS 10.8.223 and shapefile data from Ethiopia24. The top map displays the regions of Ethiopia, with the two study zones highlighted in red. The bottom left map shows the selected three districts of the West Arsi Zone, and the bottom right map shows the selected three districts of the West Hararghe Zone. In each district, 24 kebeles selected for the study were marked with blue dots.

West Arsi Zone has 12 districts with a total area of 11,776.72 km². The zone has a total projected human population of 3,134,951, of which 1,548,073 are males and 1,586,878 females. There are approximately 626,990 households in the zone, with an average of 5 people per household20,21. The West Hararghe Zone consists of 14 districts with a total projected population of 2,894,857, of which 1,477,231 are males and 1,417,626 are females. The zone contains about 578,971 households, with an average of 5 persons per household20,21.

In Ethiopia, goats and sheep are primarily raised in specialized pastoral, agro-pastoral, and mixed agricultural livestock systems, with most goats and sheep being indigenous breeds. Due to area and capital constraints, in the highland mixed crop-livestock systems, flock sizes are small due to limitations in land availability and production inputs. Goats and sheep mostly feed on communal natural grasslands, crop residues, fallow grazing lands, roadside grazing, crop stubble, non-traditional feeds (like home food leftovers, weeds, crop tillers, and fillers) and browse trees and shrubs22.

The Oromia Regional State has 9,752,387 sheep and 8,425,727 goats. Out of the total sheep population, 771,085 and 135,962 are in the West Arsi and West Hararghe zones, respectively. Of the goat population, there are 245,950 in West Arsi and 1,112,239 in West Hararghe1.

Study animals

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Apparently healthy goats and sheep that had no prior history of PPR vaccination were included in the study. These animals were indigenous breeds of sheep and goats raised in extensive and semi-intensive production systems under a stationary crop-livestock mixed agricultural system. Animals were excluded if they were ill, receiving treatment, or did not have owner consent. Moreover, small ruminants younger than 6 months were excluded from sampling to avoid false positives resulting from maternal antibodies25.The animals’ ages were estimated by tooth inspection26 and supported by further evidence from the owners.

Study design

The West Arsi and West Hararghe Zones of the Oromia Regional State were purposively selected based on altitude difference, rainfall pattern, and climatic conditions27,28. A cross-sectional study design was employed in both zones from September 2023 to June 2024.

Sample collection

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated using the standard formula: , where Z is the standard normal deviate at a 95% confidence level (z = 1.96), n= sample size, Pexp = expected prevalence, and d = absolute precision29. The sample size calculation was based on the 13% PPR seroprevalence previously reported from the Arsi Zone30, with an absolute precision of 5% and a 95% confidence level. Accordingly, the calculated sample size per zone and per animal species was 174 animals. To increase representativeness and account for potential sample loss, the calculated sample size increased by 8%, and finally a total of 751 small ruminants (398 goats and 353 sheep) were sampled. However, to ensure the study was representative of local small ruminants’ population, a slightly higher number of goats were considered.

Sampling technique

Information on the absence of PPR vaccination histories in the districts was obtained from the regional laboratories in the two zones: the Asella Regional Veterinary Laboratory for the West Arsi Zone and the Hirna Regional Veterinary Laboratory for the West Hararghe Zone. Based on the information, six districts from the two zones were selected: Adaba, Dodola, and Shashamane districts from the West Arsi Zone and Chiro, Doba, and Tulo districts from the West Hararghe Zone. From the six districts, a total of 26 kebeles were randomly selected: 14 kebeles from districts of the West Arsi Zone (five kebeles each from Adaba and Shashamane and four kebeles from Dodola) and 12 kebeles from three districts of the West Hararghe Zone (four kebeles from each of the three districts, namely, Chiro, Doba, and Tulo districts) (Fig. 1). Within the kebeles, 422 households were considered through a systematic random sampling technique. Two animals were randomly selected per household for blood sampling.

Blood sample collection

Blood samples were collected using plain vacutainer tubes and sterile vacutainer needles after disinfecting the jugular vein area with 70% alcohol. Approximately ten milliliters of blood samples for serum separation were aseptically collected from each animal. The collected blood samples were kept in a slant position for 2–3 h at room temperature to allow blood to clot. Serum was separated from the blood by centrifugation at 3000 revolutions per minute (RPM) for 10 min. The separated sera were transferred to labeled cryovials and transported in an icebox to the Animal Health Institute (AHI) laboratory, Sebeta, Ethiopia. The samples were then stored at -20 °C until serological testing was performed.

Questionnaire survey and variables description

A structured questionnaire was used during blood sample collection to capture potential risk factors. The individual animal biodata collected include sex, age group (young: < 2 years, adult: ≥ 2 years)31, and body condition score (poor: BSC 1–2, medium: BSC 3, good: BSC 4–5)32. Geographical information, such as zone, district, kebele, and geographical coordinates, was also recorded. Flock-level variables recorded included flock size (small: ≤ 5 animals; medium: 6–15 animals; large: ≥ 16 animals)33,34, animal origin (newly introduced or born), settlement type (rural or urban), and mixed flock (yes: if both sheep and goats are present in the flock and no: if only sheep or only goats are present). Altitude was grouped into midland (1500–2300 m above sea level (masl)) and highlands (> 2300 masl)35. Proximity to main roads was classified as near (< 2 km) or far (≥ 2 km). Market exposure was defined as “yes” if the flock was within 1 km of a livestock market or had direct market contact, and “no” otherwise. These variables were included as risk factors; the assumption was that animal movement associated with market activities may facilitate PPR virus transmission. The flock’s origin was categorized as new (all animals bought), mixed (some bought, some born), or born (all animals born in the flock)36. All information was collected through interviews with animal owners or herders supported by direct observation during sampling.

Laboratory analysis

Serological test

A PPR blocking enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (HPPR-bELISA; obtained from the African Union Pan African Veterinary Vaccine Centre (AU-PANVAC), Ethiopia) based on haemagglutinin protein was employed for the serology test. The assay plates were pre-coated with inactivated PPR virus antigen. The test is based on a monoclonal antibody specific to the hemagglutinin protein of the PPR virus and has been reported to have a specificity and sensitivity of 94.2% and 100%, respectively37. The serology test was performed in a viral serology laboratory of the Animal Health Institute (AHI), Sebeta, Oromia, Ethiopia.

The serological test was performed as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 75 µl of blocking buffer was added to all wells. A 25 µl volume of each control buffer (CB) and positive control (PC) was added to two wells designated for each suspension. A negative serum (NS) was dispensed into four designated wells marked for negative serum. Similarly, equal volumes of test serum samples were added to the wells marked for sample wells. The test plate was gently shaken, covered with plate sealer, and incubated at room temperature for one hour. After incubation, the test plate was emptied and washed three times with 300 µl of 1x washing buffer. An equal volume of blocking buffer and conjugate buffer was dispensed to two wells assigned for the control buffer and the remaining wells, respectively. The plate was again gently shaken, covered with a plate sealer, and incubated at room temperature for 45 min. Following the incubation the test plate was emptied and washed as described above, and then 50 µl of tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) was distributed to each well, covered, and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. The reaction was stopped by dispensing 50 µl of 1 M H₂SO₄ to each well, and the optical density (OD) was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using an ELISA reader. Validation and interpretation of the test results were performed according to the manufacturer guidelines. Accordingly, the average optical density of control buffer (ODcb) wells should be less than or equal to 0.1, and a median OD of the negative control wells should be greater than or equal to 0.7. The validation percent inhibition (PI) was obtained for both positive (PC) and negative controls (NC). The PI of all wells allocated to positive control should be greater than 70%, while at least three wells assigned to negative control should be less than 15% to be classified as negative controls. A serum sample was described as PPR seropositive if the PI of the well was higher than or equal to 35%, negative if it was less than or equal to 30%, and assessed as doubtful if it was between 30% and 35%.

Ethical clearance and consent to participate

This study involved routine blood sample collection from sheep and goats for disease prevalence assessment and did not include any experimental procedures. All animal‑related procedures were performed in accordance with institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of animals in research, including the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH, formerly OIE) standards. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Animal Research Ethical Review Committee (ARERC) of the College of Veterinary Medicine and Agriculture, Addis Ababa University, Ethiopia (reference number: VM/ERC/41/03/15/2023). Informed written consent was obtained from all animal owners prior to sample collection from their animals and administration of the husbandry‑related questionnaire, in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data analysis and management

All collected data were entered into Microsoft Excel 365 and analyzed using STATA version 1738. The seroprevalence of PPR at the animal level was calculated by dividing the number of seropositive animals by the total number of animals tested and multiplying the result by 10039. A flock was considered seropositive if at least one animal in the tested flock was positive for PPR antibodies. Flock-level seroprevalence was determined by dividing the number of seropositive flocks by the total number of flocks tested and then multiplying the result by 100. In this study, a “flock” refers to a group of sheep, goats, or both that are herded together and share shelter, water, and feed resources40.

Potential risk factors were selected based on biological plausibility and prior literature. The association of these factors with the disease was evaluated using the chi‑square test and univariable logistic regression, and they were considered predictors of the disease after conducting multivariable logistic regression. Before the variables were included in the multivariable logistic regression model, multicollinearity among explanatory variables was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) with the collin command, and VIF values below 10 were considered for further analysis. Cramer’s V statistic with a value ≤ 0.7 was considered acceptable to avoid redundant factors and find strong correlations41,42. Ultimately, the p-value of each variable was assessed, and those variables that had a p-value ≤ 0.25 in univariable logistic regression were subsequently included in the multivariable logistic regression model43.

A manual forward selection technique was used to construct a multivariable logistic regression model, and the model goodness of fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and the model’s predictive performance was assessed using the Receiver Operating Characteristics (ROC) curve. In the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, a model was deemed to be a good fit if its p-value was more than 0.0544 and its area under the curve (AUC) was greater than 0.7. Model selection was further guided using Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)45,46. Ultimately, variables with a p-value ≤ 0.05 were kept in the final model. A p-value equal to or less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used to indicate the strength and direction of variable association to PPR seropositivity.

Result

PPR seroprevalence in both animal species and its spatial distribution

PPR seroprevalence in both goats and sheep

The overall animal-level seroprevalence of PPR in both zones was 5.9% (44/751, 95% CI: 4.4–7.8), with goats having a prevalence of 5.3% (21/398, 95% CI: 3.5–8.0) and sheep having a prevalence of 6.5% (23/353, 95% CI: 4.4–9.6). However, the difference between sheep and goats was not statistically significant (p = 0.470, chi-square (χ²) = 0.52).

Of the 44 PPR seropositive goats and sheep, 72.7% were found in the West Hararghe Zone, representing the highest proportion, while 22.3% were in the West Arsi Zone. At the district level, the highest proportions of seropositive cases were observed in Chiro and Tulo districts, each accounting for 27.3%. At the kebele level, the highest seroprevalence (11.4%) was observed in Efebas Kebele, located in the Tulo district of the West Hararghe Zone (Fig. 2).

The sunburst diagram illustrates the proportion of PPR seroprevalence in two zones, six districts, and positive kebeles. The % next to each kebele’s name represents the proportion of the disease. Note: Four kebeles, where serological tests found no disease, were omitted from the map.

Spatial mapping of PPR seropositivity

Of the 26 kebeles surveyed, PPR was detected in 20 kebeles (76.9%), all 12 kebeles in the West Hararghe Zone and 8 of the 14 kebeles in the West Arsi Zone. Figure 3 illustrates the spatial distribution of the confirmed PPR seropositive cases using Global Positioning System (GPS) coordinates across the 20 affected kebeles. At the district level in the West Arsi Zone, PPR was found in two of five kebeles in the Adaba district, in all four kebeles in the Dodola district, and in two of five in the Shashamane district. In contrast, in the West Hararghe Zone, PPR seropositivity cases were detected in all the four kebeles found in the Chiro, Doba, and Tulo districts (Fig. 3).

Association between risk factors and PPR seropositivity

Risk factor analysis in goats

The univariable logistic regression analysis revealed that sex, age group, zone, altitude category, and district were significantly associated with PPR seropositivity in goats (p < 0.05). Female goats had 10.5 times greater crude odds of PPR seropositivity than male goats. Similarly, goats found in the West Hararghe Zone had a 6.8 times higher chance of having antibodies against the PPR virus than those in the West Arsi Zone (Table 4).

The final model (Table 4) identified a zone and age of animals as predictors of PPR seroprevalence in goats (p < 0.05). Except for a variable district, which was excluded due to collinearity, other variables were reduced from the model due to a higher p-value when compared to other variables. However, the variable sex was included after the model was evaluated and achieved the lower AIC (130.1) and a higher area under the ROC curve (AUC = 0.8565), showing improved model discrimination. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test confirmed that the final model adequately fit the data (p > 0.05; Table 4). The probability of PPR seropositivity was 10.5 times higher in goats living in the West Hararghe Zone compared to those in the West Arsi Zone (AOR = 10.5, 95% CI: 2.4–47.0, p = 0.002). Similarly, the odds of an adult goat having PPR seropositivity were 7.9 times higher than those of young goats (AOR = 7.9, 95% CI: 1.7–36.7, p = 0.008, Table 4).

Risk factor analysis in sheep

Among the potential risk factors evaluated, age group was the only independent variable that was significantly associated with PPRV seropositivity in sheep (p < 0.05). Specifically, the crude odds of PPR seroprevalence were 5.2 times higher in adult sheep than in young sheep. On the other hand, there was no significant association between PPR seroprevalence in sheep and the other risk factors considered (p > 0.05; Table 5).

Discussion

The major constraints to sheep and goat production and productivity in Ethiopia, including the study area, are a shortage of feed and water, a scarcity of grazing land, diseases, and inadequate veterinary service47. Disease is the foremost of these challenges. Most diseases threatening the health of sheep and goats are infectious in nature, encompassing PPR, contagious ecthyma (orf), and sheep and goat pox, all of which are viral in origin, as well as bacterial infections such as anthrax, contagious caprine pleuropneumonia (CCPP), and ovine pasteurellosis48–50.

Peste des petits ruminants is a contagious viral disease that mainly affects goats, sheep, and wild small ruminants in Africa, including Ethiopia; the Middle East; Asia; and49 Europe, causing significant economic losses and food insecurity51. In the present study, the seroprevalence of PPR in goats at the animal level was 5.3%, which is consistent with the reports of the disease in goats in the Horo Gudur Zone, Ethiopia (4.5%)52, and the neighboring country, Djibouti (6.8%)53. There was much lower seroprevalence (2.3%) of PPR in goats reported in other parts of Ethiopia, like the Kafa and Bench Maji zones, than in the current study54. However, several other studies conducted in various zones of Ethiopia reported higher prevalence ranging from 28.3% to 69.1% of the disease in goats36,55–57.

In this investigation, sheep also had a slightly higher seroprevalence of PPR (6.5%) as compared with goats. Comparable results were reported in other zones of Oromia, Ethiopia, where the prevalence reached 5% in the North Shewa Zone31 and 7% in the Horo Guduru Zone52. In contrast to the current findings, lower prevalences of 0.3% in sheep were reported in the Awash Fentale area, Afar, Ethiopia58, and 2.7% in Djibouti53. Conversely, higher seroprevalence of PPR (ranging from 14.9% to 61.9%) has been reported in sheep in other areas of Ethiopia56,57,59–61.

The current finding of low PPR seroprevalence in goats and sheep could be attributed to differences in disease epidemiology, climate, animal production systems, altitude, improved vaccination coverage around the study area, or cumulative effects rather than a lack of virus circulation. Livestock turnover due to market conditions and slaughter might have reduced the proportion of seropositive individuals at the time of sampling. In Ethiopia’s highlands, where mixed crop and livestock agriculture is common, including in the study area, sheep and goat production is generally low because of limited grazing land22. This might be reducing animal density and contact rates, thereby limiting disease transmission and antibody formation. Furthermore, the colder temperatures of the highlands may not be favorable for PPR virus propagation62,63.

In our study, flock-level seroprevalence in both goat and sheep flocks was relatively low (9.7%) in the West Hararghe and West Arsi zones. In contrast, higher seroprevalence of PPR at the flock level, ranging from 27.4% to 100%, was found in various zones in Ethiopia31,36,52. The observed disparities in PPR seroprevalence at the flock level in various places may relate to changes in agroecology and altitude range, disease outbreak incidence, animal movement, and the number of animals sampled per flock.

Based on a multivariable logistic regression model, age was one of the significant risk factors for PPR seropositivity in goats and sheep in the West Arsi and West Hararghe zones. Previous studies reported similar results, showing that adult animals were more likely to contract PPR infection than young animals18,31. Although it was an expected result, the higher seroprevalence of the disease in adult animals might be due to their greater chances of exposure to the virus because of age compared to young animals31. On the other hand, it is known that PPR is highly lethal in young animals, and affected ones may die and thus go undetected during surveillance12.

In this investigation, the zone was also found to be an independent predictor of PPR seropositivity at the species level in goats and at the flock level in both sheep and goats. Goats living in the West Hararghe Zone were 11 times more likely to be PPR-positive than those living in the West Arsi Zone, while flocks of goats and sheep in the West Hararghe Zone were five times more likely to be PPR seropositive. The higher prevalence of the disease in the West Hararghe Zone is possibly due to its relatively warmer temperature compared to those in West Arsi, which might favor greater disease transmission63. Another key risk factor in sheep was altitude, which was one of the predictors of PPR seropositivity. Sheep in midland regions exhibited three times higher odds of PPR seropositivity than those in highland areas. Several studies have reported that altitude influences PPR distribution in different areas, with the likelihood of PPR seroprevalence increasing as elevation decreases from highland to lowland36,64. The researchers reasoned that midland areas are typically warmer and wetter than highland areas and are therefore more conducive to the stability and transmission of the PPR virus.

Although female goats and sheep had a higher probability of PPR seropositivity than males, this difference was not statistically significant in the current investigation. On the other hand, research conducted in Ethiopia and Djibouti revealed that the sex of goats and sheep was associated with PPR seropositivity, with females being at a higher risk31,53.

The chi-square test in our study revealed no statistically significant association between the animal species (goats and sheep) and PPR seropositivity. Although some researchers have reported consistent findings with the present study56,57, others have observed significant differences in seroprevalence among animal species, with goats showing a higher likelihood of being PPR seropositive53,59. The non-significant difference in PPR seropositivity between sheep and goats in our study could be attributed to an equal probability of exposure resulting from similar husbandry and management practices for both species in the study area, notwithstanding their biological differences.

Although the present study was able to produce baseline PPR seroprevalence data in the area, it had some limitations. First, the use of HPPR-bELISA offers high specificity (100%) and has slightly lower sensitivity compared to competitive ELISA, which might lower the prevalence of the disease in the area. Second, the type of study design used captures only a cumulative exposure snapshot, without monitoring the exact seasonal peak of disease transmission in the area. These findings may not apply to nomadic or other pastoral systems where the disease spreads due to mobility, as these districts are sedentary. Overall, despite the aforementioned limitations, this study effectively identified and confirmed the presence of the disease in the study area.

Conclusion and recommendations

The PPR seroprevalence investigation in both goats and sheep found in the West Arsi and West Hararghe zones revealed low levels at both animal and flock levels. The low seroprevalence indicates that a minimal mass vaccination campaign is necessary to eradicate the disease from the area and achieve the global goal of a PPR-free world by 2030. Although the overall seroprevalence of the disease was low, there was a statistically significant difference between the West Arsi Zone and the West Hararghe Zone among goats and flocks. A higher PPR seroprevalence was recorded in the West Hararghe compared to the West Arsi Zone. This might be attributed to the actual climatic and environmental differences between the two zones that influenced disease transmission dynamics. Likewise, statistically significant differences were seen in different age groups (young and adult) in both goats and sheep. As the age of animals increased, the probability of being PPR seropositive also increased, most likely due to frequent exposure to the virus or disease. In conclusion, although the overall seroprevalence of the disease was low, there was serological evidence suggesting its distribution across six districts or in both zones. Therefore, vaccination efforts should be planned and implemented to eradicate the disease from both zones and contribute to the global plan. Additionally, agro-ecology based molecular surveillance is needed to explore and strengthen the current findings and elucidate transmission dynamics of the disease.
