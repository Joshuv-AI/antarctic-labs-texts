Sarcoidosis in a 57-year-old woman
Authors: Xinxing Jin, Juan Bai
Published: 2026

A 57-year-old woman presented to our dermatology clinic with a 20-day history of painful erythematous nodules on her lower limbs and polyarthralgia, predominantly affecting her knees and ankles. She was afebrile, and her symptoms were refractory to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. She had undergone cosmetic eyebrow tattooing 1 year earlier. Examination showed yellow-red plaques infiltrating these tattoos, accompanied by psoriasiform plaques along her hairline (Figure 1A) and tender lower-limb erythematous nodules (Appendix 1, Figure S1A, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260254/tab-related-content). Computed tomography of the patient’s chest showed bilateral hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy (Appendix 1, Figure S1C). Negative laboratory tests — including an interferon-γ release assay, treponemal serology, antinuclear antibodies, and rheumatoid factor — ruled out tuberculosis, syphilis, and common autoimmune diseases. Biopsy of a hairline plaque showed dermal noncaseating epithelioid granulomas with negative fungal and acid-fast bacilli stains. We diagnosed sarcoidosis. Given the patient’s specific granulomatous lesions and refractory systemic symptoms, we initiated prednisone (initially 30 mg daily) and added hydroxychloroquine (200 mg twice daily) as a steroid-sparing agent. After 3 months, her skin lesions had nearly resolved, and lymphadenopathy regressed remarkedly (Figure 1B and Appendix 1, Figures S1B and S1D). At 4 months’ follow-up, she remained in clinical remission on oral hydroxychloroquine 100 mg twice daily.

(A) Yellow-red plaques infiltrating tattoos, and psoriasiform plaques along the hairline, of a 57-year-old woman with sarcoidosis. (B) Marked resolution of both lesions after 3 months of oral prednisone and hydroxychloroquine.

Sarcoidosis is a multisystem granulomatous disorder of unknown cause. It shows a bimodal age distribution, with a first peak at age 25 to 35 years and a second in women aged 45 to 65 years.1 Cutaneous lesions occur in approximately 25% to 33% of patients and are often the presenting feature. Cutaneous sarcoidosis is classified as “specific” (noncaseating granulomas on biopsy; e.g., papules, plaques, scar infiltration) or “nonspecific” (reactive; e.g., erythema nodosum).2 This patient had 2 specific morphologies (tattoo nodules and psoriasiform plaques) and nonspecific lesions of erythema nodosum. Although this patient had no uveitis, we suggested routine ophthalmologic evaluation, as uveitis is a common extrathoracic manifestation.3 Treatment is guided by disease severity and organ involvement: topical corticosteroids for mild cutaneous disease, systemic corticosteroids for acute symptoms or organ-threatening disease, and steroid-sparing agents (e.g., methotrexate, hydroxychloroquine) for chronic or refractory cases.1
