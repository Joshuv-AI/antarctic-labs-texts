Clinical characteristics and treatment analysis of children with glucose transporter type 1 deficiency syndrome: a report of two cases
Authors: Menglu Cao, Bingbing Zhang, Xiao Xiao, Fangfang Cheng, Jianmei Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlucose transporter type 1 deficiency syndrome (GLUT1-DS) is a rare neurometabolic disorder caused by mutations in the SLC2A1 gene, leading to glucose transporter type 1 (GLUT1) dysfunction and cerebral energy failure. Clinically, it presents with intractable epilepsy, movement disorders, and developmental delay. Timely diagnosis remains challenging due to clinical heterogeneity.Case DescriptionWe report two pediatric cases that presented with early-onset drug-resistant epilepsy, ataxia, and developmental delay. Case 1 presented with refractory epilepsy that was poorly responsive to antiseizure medications. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed hypoglycorrhachia (CSF glucose: 2.17 mmol/L; CSF-to-blood glucose ratio: 0.388). Genetic testing identified a heterozygous SLC2A1 variant (c.1198C>A, p.Arg400Ser), confirming GLUT1-DS. A ketogenic diet (KD) was initiated, leading to a significant reduction in seizure frequency. Case 2 presented with paroxysmal movement disorders and drug-resistant seizures. CSF analysis showed a glucose level of 2.27 mmol/L with a CSF-to-blood ratio of 0.315. SLC2A1 sequencing identified a mutation (c.988C>T, p.Arg330Ter). Following KD initiation, both patients showed significant clinical improvement (complete seizure freedom in Case 2 and marked reduction in Case 1), although long-term efficacy requires further monitoring.ConclusionsA decreased CSF glucose level and a low CSF-to-blood glucose ratio are key diagnostic clues for GLUT1-DS. Early initiation of a KD yielded favorable short-term clinical outcomes in both cases, highlighting the importance of prompt metabolic and genetic screening.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Early-onset drug-resistant epilepsy and paroxysmal movement disorders in children are key manifestations of glucose transporter type 1 deficiency syndrome (GLUT1-DS). Prompt identification of hypoglycorrhachia and SLC2A1 mutations enables early initiation of a ketogenic diet (KD), leading to significant seizure reduction or complete seizure freedom.

• GLUT1-DS is a treatable autosomal dominant genetic disease. The decrease in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) glucose and the CSF-to-blood ratio are important screening clues. Early KD can significantly improve the prognosis as it bypasses the defective metabolism.

What is known and what is new?

• GLUT1-DS is a treatable neurometabolic disorder characterized by hypoglycorrhachia (decreased CSF glucose and low CSF-to-blood glucose ratio), with KD as the primary targeted therapy.

• We report two pediatric cases with distinct clinical presentations carrying novel heterozygous SLC2A1 variants (c.1198C>A and c.988C>T), expanding the genetic and phenotypic spectrum of GLUT1-DS and confirming rapid clinical response to KD.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Pediatric clinicians should maintain a high level of vigilance for GLUT1-DS in children presenting with unexplained refractory epilepsy or exercise-induced dyskinesia. Lumbar puncture for CSF glucose evaluation should be considered early, preferably in parallel with or before complex genetic testing to facilitate timely metabolic intervention and improve long-term outcomes.

Introduction

Background

In 1991, De Vivo et al. first described glucose transporter type 1 deficiency syndrome (GLUT1-DS), characterized by pharmacoresistant epilepsy, dyskinesia, developmental delay, and acquired microcephaly (1). In GLUT1-DS, ketone bodies serve as an alternative energy source to glucose in brain tissue, thereby improving clinical symptoms (2). Pathogenic variants in the SLC2A1 gene (encoding GLUT1) can cause paroxysmal exercise-induced dyskinesia (PED) and may induce hemolytic anemia via cation leakage (3). In 2012, the first three Chinese cases of GLUT1-DS were reported (4). The clinical manifestations of GLUT1-DS are highly diverse, including refractory epilepsy, psychomotor retardation, exercise-induced paroxysmal dystonia, chorea, fatigue, ataxia, microcephaly, migraine, tremor, and hemolytic anemia (3). Seizure types vary widely, encompassing generalized tonic-clonic seizures, absence seizures, atonic seizures, myoclonic seizures, tonic seizures, myoclonic jerks, focal seizures, as well as convulsive and non-convulsive status epilepticus. Patients with sporadic mutations often exhibit more severe clinical phenotypes than those with familial inheritance (5,6). The electroencephalogram (EEG) in patients with GLUT1-DS lacks characteristic features; however, Akman et al. (7) demonstrated that EEG abnormalities are diet-sensitive, offering a potential diagnostic clue. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is normal in most patients, although non-specific abnormalities such as cerebral atrophy and delayed myelination are occasionally observed. Rarely, reversible leukoencephalopathy has been reported on brain MRI in GLUT1-DS (8).

In patients with GLUT1-DS, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cell counts, as well as protein, chloride, and lactate levels, are typically normal (9,10). Hypoglycorrhachia in the setting of normoglycemia is the hallmark diagnostic feature, generally defined as a CSF glucose level below 3.3 mmol/L and a CSF-to-blood glucose ratio below 0.45. The 3-O-methyl-D-glucose (3-OMG) uptake assay in red blood cells can indirectly assess GLUT1 function across the blood-brain barrier (BBB). Ho et al. (11) reported that using 60% of the normal uptake rate as the diagnostic threshold yields a sensitivity of 86% and a specificity of 97%. However, as glucose uptake levels do not strictly correlate with CSF glucose concentrations, a normal 3-OMG uptake result does not exclude GLUT1-DS, and genetic analysis remains necessary. Quantitative and localized assessment of GLUT1 expression can evaluate the functional impact of coding region variants and determine whether non-coding region mutations affect protein structure or expression, thereby helping to clarify variant pathogenicity. Additionally, 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (18F-FDG PET) studies have demonstrated decreased glucose metabolism in the thalamus and cerebral cortex, accompanied by a relative increase in the basal ganglia (12).

Based on clinical manifestations, GLUT1-DS is generally categorized into three phenotypic types. Type 1 represents the classic phenotype (accounting for approximately 85% of cases), characterized by seizures, intellectual disability or developmental delay, and variable motor dysfunction. Type 2 and type 3 constitute the non-classic (atypical) forms, accounting for the remaining 15%. Specifically, type 2 features intellectual disability and movement disorders without seizures, whereas type 3 is predominantly marked by paroxysmal movement disorders, with or without epilepsy.

The ketogenic diet (KD) is currently established as the standard of care for GLUT1-DS. In the present report, KD achieved rapid and effective clinical control in both cases, which suggests promising short-term efficacy, although long-term outcomes require extended follow-up. Given that a substantial proportion of affected individuals remain undiagnosed due to the pronounced clinical heterogeneity of GLUT1-DS, enhancing clinical awareness of this disease is of vital importance for achieving early diagnosis and timely therapeutic intervention.

Objective

This report describes the clinical features, laboratory findings, treatment regimens, and outcomes of two pediatric patients with SLC2A1 mutations diagnosed at the Department of Neurology, Children’s Hospital of Soochow University in May 2019. These cases serve to highlight the importance of prompt diagnostic evaluation and early therapeutic intervention for GLUT1-DS. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-1-0159/rc).

Case 1

A 5-year-old boy presented with drug-resistant seizures, ataxia, and developmental delay. His prenatal, perinatal, and early neonatal history was unremarkable. The first seizure occurred at 1 year of age, characterized by eye deviation, jaw clenching, acute dyspraxia, language and motor delays, and ataxia. Long-term interictal EEG demonstrated bilateral occipital 8–9 Hz alpha discharges, with no recording of generalized epileptiform discharges or focal abnormalities during monitoring. At 5 years of age, brain MRI revealed a pineal cyst. CSF analysis showed a glucose level of 2.17 mmol/L and a CSF-to-blood glucose ratio of 0.388. Genetic testing identified a heterozygous variant in the SLC2A1 gene (NM_006516.2:c.1198C>A, p.Arg400Ser; Chr1:43393356). Sanger sequencing confirmed that both parents were wild-type (Figure 2). According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines, the variant was classified as “likely pathogenic” (evidence codes: PS2+PM2+PP3), establishing a definitive diagnosis of GLUT1-DS.

Genetic analysis of case 1.

At the 3-month follow-up on KD therapy, the patient exhibited a significant reduction in seizure frequency and subtle improvements in developmental delay. However, due to dietary compliance issues, several seizure relapses occurred, necessitating the initiation of adjunctive therapy with levetiracetam.

Case 2

A 2-year-old girl presented with generalized tonic-clonic seizures. Her gestational and birth history was unremarkable. Notably, the patient’s 14-year-old elder brother had a history of epilepsy but had not undergone genetic testing. The patient experienced her first generalized tonic-clonic seizure at 13 months of age, manifesting as eye deviation, jaw clenching, cyanosis, and limb hypertonia. Initial brain MRI demonstrated abnormal signal intensities adjacent to the posterior horns of the bilateral lateral ventricles, suggestive of hypoxic-ischemic changes; however, a follow-up brain MRI was unremarkable.

From 19 months of age onward, the patient experienced six episodes of focal impaired-awareness seizures and PED. These attacks, typically triggered by extreme excitement or screaming and resolving after sleep, were characterized by impaired consciousness, aphasia, and sudden dyspraxia. Overall, the clinical phenotype was consistent with early-onset epileptic encephalopathy, including severe epilepsy, psychomotor delay, ataxia, and PED. Neuropsychological evaluation confirmed mild cognitive impairment. Long-term video-EEG monitoring during sleep revealed bilateral frontal slow-wave (1–1.5 Hz) discharges. CSF testing demonstrated hypoglycorrhachia (CSF glucose: 2.27 mmol/L; CSF-to-blood glucose ratio: 0.315). Genetic testing revealed a novel heterozygous nonsense variant in the SLC2A1 gene (NM_006516.2:c.988C>T, p.Arg330Ter; Chr1:43394689). Sanger sequencing of the parents confirmed that they were wild-type (Figure 3). The variant was classified as “pathogenic” according to ACMG guidelines (evidence codes: PVS1+PS2+PM2). Following the initiation of a modified KD, the patient achieved complete seizure freedom within 3 months, though long-term follow-up is ongoing to assess efficacy.

Discussion

GLUT1 is a membrane-bound glycoprotein expressed on vascular endothelial cells at the BBB, where it transports glucose into the brain to meet metabolic demands (13-15). In patients with GLUT1-DS, impaired glucose transport leads to cerebral energy failure, resulting in diverse neurological manifestations, including refractory seizures, cognitive impairment, hunger- or fatigue-induced paroxysmal movement disorders, developmental delay, ataxia, and cerebral palsy-like symptoms. Both cases reported herein share common hallmark features of early-onset drug-resistant epilepsy, ataxia, and developmental delay. Case 1 presented with refractory epilepsy that failed to respond to conventional antiseizure medications, leading to the prompt initiation of a KD. Case 2 presented with paroxysmal dyskinesia and alternating hemiplegia-like events that were unresponsive to antiepileptic therapy and physical rehabilitation. Studies have demonstrated that KD can effectively control or completely abolish epileptic seizures and paroxysmal motor events while significantly improving cognitive and neurodevelopmental outcomes in GLUT1-DS (16). In our report, Case 1 achieved a marked reduction in seizure frequency and subtle developmental gains after 3 months of KD therapy, whereas Case 2 achieved complete seizure freedom within 3 months on a modified KD. While KD therapy demonstrated robust short-term efficacy in both pediatric cases, its long-term prognosis and neurodevelopmental impact necessitate extended follow-up.

Both patients presented with early-onset epilepsy accompanied by psychomotor delay. Electrophysiological findings differed between the two cases: Case 1 showed no specific epileptiform abnormalities on interictal EEG, whereas Case 2 exhibited bilateral frontal slow-wave (1–1.5 Hz) activity during sleep stages. Although both patients achieved rapid clinical response following KD initiation, continued long-term follow-up is necessary to evaluate sustained efficacy and cognitive prognosis, particularly considering adherence challenges as observed in Case 1.

SLC2A1 encodes the GLUT1 protein. In 1998, Seidner et al. (17) first identified that SLC2A1 maps to chromosome 1 (1p34.2). Most cases of GLUT1-DS result from heterozygous SLC2A1 mutations, which predominantly occur de novo (sporadic). Previous studies have revealed a distinct lack of genotype-phenotype correlation in GLUT1-DS, with marked phenotypic variability even among family members carrying identical pathogenic variants. In Case 2, the patient’s 14-year-old elder brother also had a history of epilepsy but did not undergo genetic testing. Given the clinical features of GLUT1-DS and its known phenotypic heterogeneity, several explanations for this discordant family history merit consideration. The brother could share the same SLC2A1 variant but express a milder phenotype due to incomplete penetrance. Another possibility is that genetic modifiers or environmental factors within the family alter the clinical expression. A third scenario is that the brother’s seizures have an independent origin, whether genetic or non-genetic, unrelated to SLC2A1.

Although the KD effectively controls epileptic seizures and non-epileptic paroxysmal events, its therapeutic impact on psychomotor development in pediatric patients remains a subject of ongoing discussion. Ramm-Pettersen et al. (18) followed up six pediatric patients and observed varying degrees of improvement in motor function, cognition, and language skills following KD implementation. However, when KD was initiated in older children, neurodevelopmental improvements were less pronounced. This age-dependent responsiveness suggests a critical therapeutic time window for KD, implying that the pathophysiology of GLUT1-DS extends beyond simple cerebral energy failure and warrants further mechanistic investigation.

Compared with conventional therapy for cerebral energy deficiency, treatment with odd-chain fatty acids offers distinct advantages by enhancing both cerebral anaplerosis and neurotransmitter synthesis. Mochel et al. (19) evaluated patients treated with oral triheptanoin (a synthetic C7 triglyceride) and reported effective seizure control along with improvement in paroxysmal movement disorders; nevertheless, potential side effects and long-term outcomes require extended evaluation. Additionally, preclinical studies using adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated gene therapy (AAV-hSLC2A1) in GLUT1-DS mouse models demonstrated successful expression of exogenous GLUT1 mRNA and protein in brain tissue, resulting in significant improvements in motor function and CSF-to-blood glucose ratios (20). These findings highlight gene therapy as a highly promising therapeutic avenue for GLUT1-DS in the future.

Conclusions

GLUT1-DS is a treatable neurometabolic disorder caused by SLC2A1 mutations. Insufficient cerebral glucose supply can lead to brain injury of variable severity. In clinical practice, decreased CSF glucose and a low CSF-to-blood glucose ratio are key diagnostic clues. Both of our pediatric patients responded well to the KD in the short term, though longer follow-up is needed to assess durability of seizure control and neurodevelopmental benefits.
