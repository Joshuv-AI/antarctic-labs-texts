Association between galectin-3 and echocardiographic changes in hospitalized patients with coronavirus disease 2019: A retrospective observational study
Authors: Nana Egiashvili, Nona Kakauridze, Davit Kartvelishvili, Nino Gvajaia, Levan Ratiani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo assess the association between serum galectin-3 levels and echocardiographic changes in hospitalized patients with coronavirus disease 2019 across different disease severities and stages, including the post- coronavirus disease 2019 period.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included 86 adults with reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction–confirmed coronavirus disease 2019 who were treated at the First University Clinic of Tbilisi State Medical University. Patients were divided into four groups: moderate disease within the first week of symptom onset (n = 21), severe disease during weeks 2–3 (n = 20), critical illness not requiring mechanical ventilation (n = 20), and post- coronavirus disease 2019 status 2 months after infection (n = 25). Serum galectin-3 was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, and transthoracic echocardiography was performed in all patients. Clinical, laboratory, and echocardiographic variables were compared across groups, and associations with galectin-3 were assessed using Spearman rank correlations. Group comparisons were performed using independent-samples t tests and one-way analysis of variance. Because normality and homogeneity of variance were not formally assessed and no adjustment for multiple testing was applied, the findings were interpreted as exploratory.ResultsGalectin-3 levels increased across the acute disease groups and were highest in critically ill nonventilated patients, whereas lower levels were observed in the post- coronavirus disease 2019 group. Nominally significant between-group differences were observed in ascending aortic diameter, left ventricular volumes and mass, left atrial diameter, pulmonary artery diameter, and selected right heart functional parameters. Galectin-3 showed nominally significant correlations with atrial dimensions, right ventricular fractional area change, and selected left ventricular structural parameters, although the pattern varied across groups.ConclusionsSerum galectin-3 was associated with selected echocardiographic abnormalities, with patterns varying according to coronavirus disease 2019 severity and disease stage. Combined assessment of galectin-3 and echocardiographic findings may help characterize cardiovascular involvement. However, because of the small subgroup sizes, unadjusted multiple comparisons, unassessed assumptions of parametric tests, and exclusion of mechanically ventilated patients, these findings are hypothesis-generating and should be interpreted with caution.

Introduction

Cardiovascular involvement has emerged as an important determinant of adverse outcomes in patients with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). Patients with pre-existing cardiovascular disease are at increased risk of complications and death; however, cardiac injury may also occur in individuals without known cardiovascular disease, indicating a direct effect of COVID-19 on the cardiovascular system.1–5

In recent years, considerable attention has been directed toward identifying biomarkers that may help assess disease severity, myocardial involvement, and postinfectious cardiovascular sequelae in patients with COVID-19. Galectin-3 (Gal-3), a β-galactoside-binding lectin involved in inflammation, immune regulation, oxidative stress, and fibrotic remodeling, has emerged as a potentially useful biomarker in this setting.2,6–8 Elevated Gal-3 levels have been associated with heart failure, coronary artery disease, ischemic stroke, cardiometabolic risk factors, and increased mortality, supporting its role as a marker of cardiovascular and systemic injury.8–10

Echocardiography is a practical and widely used tool for assessing cardiac structure and function in patients with COVID-19. Previous studies have shown that echocardiographic abnormalities in these patients may include left ventricular structural changes, systolic and diastolic dysfunction, right ventricular impairment, and elevated pulmonary pressures.4,6,11 Because Gal-3 reflects inflammatory and fibrotic activity, its combined evaluation with echocardiographic parameters may improve the characterization of cardiovascular involvement during both the acute and post-COVID phases.11,12

However, evidence regarding the association between Gal-3 levels and echocardiographic findings across different stages and severities of COVID-19 remains limited. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the association between serum Gal-3 levels and echocardiographic changes in hospitalized patients with COVID-19 across different disease severities and stages, including the post-COVID period.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study was conducted at the First University Clinic of Tbilisi State Medical University, Tbilisi, Georgia, from September 2022 to September 2023. A total of 86 hospitalized adult patients with laboratory-confirmed COVID-19 were included. Clinical, laboratory, and echocardiographic data were obtained from hospital records and analyzed retrospectively. Eligible patients were included according to predefined eligibility criteria. Reporting of the study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. 13

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were age ≥18 years and laboratory-confirmed COVID-19 verified by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction testing of nasopharyngeal or oropharyngeal swab samples.

The exclusion criteria were age younger than 18 years, a poor echogenic window, type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus (except steroid-induced hyperglycemia) requirement for mechanical ventilation, pregnancy, dialysis, and type 4 or type 5 myocardial infarction (procedure-related myocardial injury).

Patient classification

Patients were categorized according to disease severity and stage. Acute COVID-19 was defined as disease within 4 weeks of symptom onset, whereas patients with post-COVID status were evaluated 2 months after acute infection. Clinical severity during the acute phase was classified according to established COVID-19 severity criteria. 14

Moderate disease was defined as evidence of lower respiratory tract involvement without criteria for severe or critical illness. Severe disease was defined by oxygen saturation ≤93% at rest, respiratory rate >30 breaths/min, PaO2/FiO2 ≤300, or lung infiltrates involving >50% of the lung parenchyma. Critical illness was defined by respiratory failure, shock, or multiple organ dysfunction syndrome.

The 86 patients were stratified into four groups (Figure 1). Group I (moderate stage) included 21 patients who were evaluated within the first week of symptom onset, including 10 women (47.6%) and 11 men (52.4%). Group II (severe stage) included 20 patients who were evaluated during weeks 2–3 after symptom onset, including nine women (45.0%) and 11 men (55.0%). Group III (critical stage) included 20 patients with critical illness who did not require mechanical ventilation, including eight women (40.0%) and 12 men (60.0%). Group IV (post-COVID stage) included 25 patients who were evaluated 2 months after acute infection, including 14 women (56.0%) and 11 men (44.0%). Coronary heart disease among the study participants was identified according to established international guidelines and recommendations.

Distribution of study participants across coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) severity and post-COVID groups. Data are presented as the number (percentage) of patients in each group.

Echocardiographic assessment and Gal-3 measurement

All patients underwent standard transthoracic echocardiography in accordance with the recommendations of the American Society of Echocardiography. 15 Examinations were performed using a GE Healthcare Vivid S5 cardiovascular ultrasound system. The following parameters were assessed using M-mode, two-dimensional imaging, and color Doppler techniques: left ventricular myocardial mass, posterior wall thickness, interventricular septal thickness in diastole, left ventricular end-diastolic diameter, left atrial diameter, right ventricular diameter, right atrial diameter, pulmonary artery systolic pressure, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion, and right ventricular fractional area change.

Standard laboratory testing was also performed, including measurement of serum Gal-3 using a human enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (SUNLONG).

Heart failure was identified according to American Heart Association criteria, including echocardiographic findings, and functional status was classified according to the New York Heart Association functional classification.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. In the original analysis, continuous variables were compared using independent-samples t tests for pairwise comparisons and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for comparisons across the four groups. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test, as appropriate, normality and homogeneity of variance were not formally assessed. Consequently, the assumptions required for parametric testing could not be confirmed, particularly for variables with greater dispersion, and the corresponding p values were interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory. Associations between serum Gal-3 levels and echocardiographic parameters were assessed using Spearman rank correlation coefficients, which are less dependent on distributional assumptions than Pearson correlation coefficients.

Multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to explore independent associations, with Gal-3 level as the dependent variable. Clinical, laboratory, echocardiographic, and comorbidity variables considered relevant were entered into the model, and a backward elimination approach was used to reduce the number of predictors and limit overfitting given the sample size. The regression results were interpreted cautiously because of the modest sample size and the exploratory model-selection strategy. No formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied to the pairwise comparisons or group-specific correlation analyses. All reported p values are therefore unadjusted (nominal). A two-sided p value <0.05 was used as an exploratory threshold, and the findings were considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Nonparametric sensitivity analyses of the group comparisons were not performed, which is acknowledged as a methodological limitation.

In addition, normality and homogeneity of variance were not formally assessed; therefore, the parametric group comparisons should be interpreted as exploratory. The ascending aortic diameter was 3.48 ± 0.25 cm in Group I, 3.68 ± 0.35 cm in Group II, 3.67 ± 0.44 cm in Group III, and 3.44 ± 0.32 cm in Group IV. Nominally significant differences were observed between Groups I and II (p = 0.041), Groups II and IV (p = 0.021), and Groups III and IV (p = 0.049), whereas no nominally significant differences were found in the other pairwise comparisons (Table 3).

No nominally significant differences were observed in interventricular septal thickness or posterior wall thickness between the groups (Table 3).

The left ventricular end-diastolic diameter was 49.4 ± 3.7 mm in Group I, 50.7 ± 4.7 mm in Group II, 53.1 ± 8.1 mm in Group III, and 46.3 ± 9.8 mm in Group IV. A nominally significant difference was observed only between Groups III and IV (p = 0.017) (Table 3).

The left ventricular end-diastolic volume was 112.2 ± 13.0 mL in Group I, 122.0 ± 22.9 mL in Group II, 133.1 ± 37.9 mL in Group III, and 114.9 ± 20.4 mL in Group IV. Nominally significant differences were found between Groups I and III (p = 0.022) and between Groups III and IV (p = 0.045) (Table 3).

The left ventricular end-systolic volume was 53.8 ± 12.5 mL in Group I, 62.2 ± 18.7 mL in Group II, 73.3 ± 39.0 mL in Group III, and 54.9 ± 18.8 mL in Group IV. Nominally significant differences were observed between Groups I and III (p = 0.036) and between Groups III and IV (p = 0.044) (Table 3).

The left ventricular mass was 81.0 ± 24.4 g in Group I, 98.6 ± 36.4 g in Group II, 118.0 ± 63.2 g in Group III, and 82.2 ± 29.4 g in Group IV. Nominally significant differences were observed between Groups I and III (p = 0.017) and between Groups III and IV (p = 0.016) (Table 3).

The left atrial diameter was 3.9 ± 0.4 cm in Group I, 4.1 ± 0.5 cm in Group II, 4.4 ± 0.6 cm in Group III, and 3.8 ± 0.5 cm in Group IV. Nominally significant differences were found between Groups I and III (p = 0.005), Groups II and IV (p = 0.022), and Groups III and IV (p = 0.001), whereas the difference between Groups I and II was not nominally significant (p = 0.056) (Table 3).

The left ventricular ejection fraction was 52.5% ± 7.1% in Group I, 49.7 %± 6.1% in Group II, 47.5 %± 10.7% in Group III, and 53.2% ± 6.9% in Group IV. A nominally significant difference was observed only between Groups III and IV (p = 0.036) (Table 3).

Fractional shortening was 26.2% ± 3.5% in Group I, 25.0% ± 2.9% in Group II, 23.9% ± 5.4% in Group III, and 26.8% ± 3.4% in Group IV. A nominally significant difference was observed only between Groups III and IV (p = 0.033) (Table 3).

The cardiac index was 2.46 ± 0.62 L/min/m2 in Group I, 2.63 ± 0.49 L/min/m2 in Group II, 2.60 ± 0.66 L/min/m2 in Group III, and 2.84 ± 0.48 L/min/m2 in Group IV. A nominally significant difference was observed only between Groups I and IV (p = 0.024) (Table 3).

The stroke volume was 58.5 ± 7.4 mL in Group I, 59.8 ± 7.8 mL in Group II, 59.7 ± 9.3 mL in Group III, and 60.0 ± 5.9 mL in Group IV, with no nominally significant differences between the groups (Table 3).

The cardiac output was 4.53 ± 1.18 L/min in Group I, 5.11 ± 1.15 L/min in Group II, 5.10 ± 1.44 L/min in Group III, and 5.21 ± 1.02 L/min in Group IV. A nominally significant difference was observed only between Groups I and IV (p = 0.042) (Table 3).

No nominally significant differences in E/e′ were observed between Groups I and II or between Groups I and IV. However, nominally significant differences were found between Groups I and III (p = 0.031) and between Groups III and IV (p = 0.004) (Table 3).

Right heart parameters

The right atrial diameter was 3.6 ± 0.4 cm in Group I, 3.9 ± 0.7 cm in Group II, 6.5 ± 1.1 cm in Group III, and 3.5 ± 0.3 cm in Group IV. Nominally significant differences were observed between Groups I and II (p = 0.043) and between Groups II and IV (p = 0.004) (Table 3). These comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because the distributional and equal-variance assumptions underlying the parametric analysis were not formally assessed.

The right ventricular diameter was 3.4 ± 0.3 cm in Group I, 3.5 ± 0.4 cm in Group II, 3.5 ± 0.2 cm in Group III, and 3.4 ± 0.3 cm in Group IV, with no nominally significant differences between the groups (Table 3).

The right ventricular fractional area change was 46.6% ± 5.4% in Group I, 46.0% ± 4.6% in Group II, 44.2% ± 4.9% in Group III, and 48.3% ± 3.2% in Group IV. A nominally significant difference was observed between Groups III and IV (p = 0.002), whereas the difference between Groups II and IV approached the nominal significance threshold (p = 0.055) (Table 3).

The pulmonary artery diameter was 1.9 ± 0.1 cm in Group I, 2.0 ± 0.0 cm in Group II, 2.0 ± 0.1 cm in Group III, and 1.9 ± 0.1 cm in Group IV. Nominally significant differences were observed between Groups I and II (p = 0.028), Groups I and III (p = 0.039), and Groups II and IV (p = 0.048), whereas the difference between Groups III and IV approached the nominal significance threshold (p = 0.055) (Table 3).

The pulmonary artery systolic pressure was 35.8 ± 11.8 mmHg in Group I, 41.5 ± 13.0 mmHg in Group II, 40.5 ± 13.1 mmHg in Group III, and 33.4 ± 12.1 mmHg in Group IV. A nominally significant difference was observed only between Groups II and IV (p = 0.037), whereas the difference between Groups III and IV approached the nominal significance threshold (p = 0.066) (Table 3).

The tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion was 2.10 ± 0.22 cm in Group I, 2.04 ± 0.21 cm in Group II, 2.01 ± 0.18 cm in Group III, and 2.16 ± 0.16 cm in Group IV. Nominally significant differences were observed between Groups II and IV (p = 0.035) and between Groups III and IV (p = 0.005), with no other nominally significant between-group differences (Table 3).

Correlation of galectin-3 with echocardiographic parameters

Gal-3 demonstrated different correlation patterns across the study groups (Table 4). In Group I, nominally significant positive correlations were observed with left atrial diameter (r = 0.737, p < 0.001) and right atrial diameter (r = 0.722, p < 0.001). In Group II, Gal-3 showed a nominally significant negative correlation with right ventricular fractional area change (r = −0.614, p = 0.004), along with nominally significant positive correlations with left atrial diameter (r = 0.702, p = 0.001) and right atrial diameter (r = 0.531, p = 0.016). In Group IV, nominally significant positive correlations were found with left ventricular end-diastolic volume (r = 0.401, p = 0.047), left ventricular end-systolic volume (r = 0.442, p = 0.027), left ventricular mass (r = 0.474, p = 0.017), left atrial diameter (r = 0.421, p = 0.036), and right atrial diameter (r = 0.454, p = 0.023) (Table 4). No nominally significant correlations were identified in Group III.

Discussion

In this retrospective observational study of hospitalized patients with COVID-19 and patients evaluated during the post-COVID period, serum Gal-3 levels varied across disease stages and severities and were highest in critically ill patients who did not require mechanical ventilation. Several echocardiographic parameters showed nominal between-group differences, including ascending aortic diameter, left ventricular volumes, left ventricular mass, left atrial diameter, pulmonary artery diameter, and selected right heart indices. Group-specific analyses also identified nominal associations between Gal-3 and left- and right-sided echocardiographic parameters, although the pattern varied according to disease stage. These findings should not be interpreted as confirmatory because the subgroups were small, numerous tests were performed without adjustment for multiple comparisons, and the assumptions underlying the parametric comparisons were not formally assessed.

Our findings support the concept that Gal-3 may reflect both inflammatory activation and fibrotic remodeling in COVID-19. Previous studies have identified Gal-3 as a biomarker linked to inflammation, myocardial injury, heart failure, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes, and our results are broadly consistent with that literature.5,8,16–18 The highest Gal-3 concentration was observed in the nonventilated critical group, which is in keeping with the view that severe systemic illness is associated with greater inflammatory and tissue-remodeling responses. At the same time, the lower values observed in the post-COVID group suggest that Gal-3 elevation is most pronounced during the acute phase, although persistent associations with some echocardiographic parameters may indicate ongoing remodeling during recovery.

An important observation was the association between Gal-3 and atrial dimensions. Left atrial diameter showed nominal between-group differences and nominally significant positive correlations with Gal-3 in the moderate, severe, and post-COVID groups. Right atrial diameter showed a similar correlation pattern. Because atrial enlargement is associated with increased filling pressures, structural remodeling, arrhythmia risk, and heart failure, these findings raise the possibility that Gal-3 reflects cardiac structural changes extending beyond isolated ventricular systolic dysfunction.19,20 The persistence of these associations in the post-COVID group may be relevant to ongoing remodeling after acute infection, but confirmation in larger studies with appropriate distribution-based analyses is required.

We also observed changes in left ventricular structure and function across the groups. Left ventricular end-diastolic volume, left ventricular end-systolic volume, and left ventricular mass were nominally higher in the critical group than in the moderate and post-COVID groups, whereas left ventricular ejection fraction and fractional shortening were nominally lower in the critical group than in the post-COVID group. In the correlation analysis, Gal-3 was associated with left ventricular parameters in the moderate and post-COVID groups, including left ventricular volumes, left ventricular mass, and selected measures of systolic performance. These findings are consistent with previous reports suggesting that COVID-19 may be associated with subclinical myocardial dysfunction and that Gal-3 may reflect myocardial stress and remodeling.4,11,12 However, because left ventricular ejection fraction remained within a relatively preserved range in many patients, these observations may indicate subtle rather than overt systolic impairment.

Recent evidence further supports the possibility of persistent or subclinical myocardial remodeling after COVID-19, whereas also emphasizing heterogeneity across populations. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of 66 studies found alterations in left- and right-ventricular echocardiographic indices in postacute and long-COVID survivors, including less favorable left- and right-ventricular strain measurements, sometimes despite only small or nonsignificant differences in conventional left ventricular ejection fraction. 21 In a 2025 population-based longitudinal study, comparison of pre-COVID and post-COVID echocardiography showed small declines in both ejection fraction and global longitudinal strain after nonhospitalized infection, although cardiovascular magnetic resonance findings were largely normal. 22 In addition, a 2025 follow-up pilot study performed more than 2 years after hospitalization reported impairment in global longitudinal strain more frequently than reduced ejection fraction. 23 Together with other strain-based recovery studies, 24 these findings suggest that conventional echocardiographic measurements alone may not fully characterize subtle post-COVID myocardial dysfunction and that the magnitude and persistence of abnormalities may vary according to initial disease severity, follow-up timing, and imaging modality.

Right heart findings were also notable. Pulmonary artery diameter, pulmonary artery systolic pressure, and tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion showed nominal differences between some groups, and in the severe group, Gal-3 showed a nominal negative correlation with right ventricular fractional area change. These observations are consistent with previous studies showing that COVID-19 can affect the right heart through pulmonary inflammation, increased pulmonary vascular resistance, and right ventricular afterload.25–29 However, in our study, the correlations between Gal-3 and right heart parameters were not uniform across all groups. In particular, no nominally significant correlations were identified in the nonventilated critical group, despite the highest mean Gal-3 level being observed in that group. One possible explanation is that, in patients with critical illness, Gal-3 may reflect a broader systemic inflammatory response rather than more specific cardiac structural associations. This interpretation remains speculative and should be confirmed in larger studies.

An important consideration is that patients requiring mechanical ventilation were excluded. Mechanically ventilated patients with severe COVID-19 generally represent a more physiologically compromised population and may have greater myocardial injury, pulmonary vascular stress, and right ventricular dysfunction. Positive-pressure ventilation and positive end-expiratory pressure may also alter venous return, pulmonary vascular resistance, right ventricular loading conditions, and echocardiographic measurements.28,30 Although excluding these patients reduces the influence of ventilation-related hemodynamic effects, it also selects a less severely affected critical care population. Consequently, the cardiac abnormalities observed in this study may underestimate those present across the full spectrum of critical COVID-19, and the findings in Group III should not be generalized to mechanically ventilated patients.

Another relevant observation was the increase in ascending aortic diameter in the severe and critical groups compared with the post-COVID group. This finding may reflect vascular involvement in acute COVID-19, potentially related to endothelial dysfunction or inflammation, as suggested in previous reports. 31 Nevertheless, aortic size is influenced by multiple factors, including age, hypertension, and pre-existing vascular disease; therefore, this finding should be interpreted cautiously.

The multivariable linear regression analysis was exploratory, with Gal-3 level as the dependent variable. In the final model, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, lactate dehydrogenase, left atrial diameter, and the presence of heart failure remained nominally associated with Gal-3. These relationships are biologically plausible and may reflect links among Gal-3, cardiac structure, and systemic tissue injury. However, the sample size was modest relative to the number of candidate predictors, backward elimination was used, and the model assumptions were not comprehensively documented. The regression findings should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of independent causal or prognostic effects.

The clinical relevance of our findings lies in the potential complementary role of Gal-3 and echocardiography in assessing cardiovascular involvement in patients with COVID-19. Gal-3 alone is a nonspecific biomarker, but when interpreted together with echocardiographic parameters, particularly atrial size, ventricular volumes, and selected right heart indices, it may help identify patients with greater cardiac involvement during both the acute and recovery phases of the disease. At present, however, these data are insufficient to support routine prognostic use without further validation.

Recent biomarker-imaging studies also support an integrated cardiovascular assessment strategy. In a 2024 longitudinal cohort of patients hospitalized with moderate-to-severe COVID-19, higher high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T concentrations during convalescence were associated with echocardiographic or cardiovascular magnetic resonance abnormalities, whereas very low concentrations helped identify patients with a low likelihood of cardiac pathology. 32 These observations support interpreting Gal-3 as one component of a multimodal assessment that combines clinical findings, conventional and advanced cardiac imaging, and established cardiac biomarkers, rather than as a stand-alone diagnostic or prognostic marker.

This study has several limitations. First, the total sample size was small, and stratification into four groups yielded only 20–25 participants per group. This reduced statistical power, increased uncertainty, and may have produced unstable subgroup estimates. Second, numerous pairwise comparisons and group-specific correlation analyses were performed without formal adjustment for multiple testing. Consequently, the probability of type 1 error was increased, and some nominally significant findings, especially those with p values close to 0.05, may represent chance observations. Third, normality and homogeneity of variance were not formally assessed, and nonparametric sensitivity analyses were not performed. Therefore, the appropriateness of the t tests and ANOVA cannot be confirmed, particularly for variables with greater dispersion or findings close to the nominal significance threshold. Fourth, the retrospective observational design limits causal inference. Fifth, the grouping strategy combined disease severity with disease stage, including a separate post-COVID group, which introduced heterogeneity and complicated interpretation. Sixth, residual confounding is likely, particularly from age, hypertension, obesity, pre-existing cardiovascular disease, and other comorbid conditions. Seventh, the exploratory multivariable regression may have been affected by overfitting and data-driven variable selection. Eighth, advanced echocardiographic techniques, particularly speckle-tracking assessment of left and right ventricular global longitudinal strain, were not available. Because strain abnormalities may occur despite preserved left ventricular ejection fraction, subtle myocardial dysfunction or remodeling may not have been detected by the conventional measurements used in this study. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging was also not performed, limiting myocardial tissue characterization. Ninth, patients requiring mechanical ventilation were excluded. Because these patients often represent the most severely affected population with COVID-19 and may have substantial myocardial and right heart involvement, this exclusion may have introduced selection bias, reduced representation of the sickest patients, and led to underestimation of the full extent of cardiovascular abnormalities in critical illness. Therefore, the findings from Group III apply only to critically ill patients who did not require mechanical ventilation and should not be generalized to mechanically ventilated intensive care populations. Finally, the absence of sex-stratified echocardiographic analyses and the lack of longitudinal follow-up beyond 2 months further limit interpretation.

In conclusion, serum Gal-3 levels showed exploratory associations with selected echocardiographic abnormalities in patients with COVID-19, and the observed patterns varied according to disease severity and stage. Gal-3 may have a complementary role when interpreted together with conventional and advanced cardiac imaging and established cardiac biomarkers. However, the small subgroup sizes, multiple unadjusted tests, unassessed parametric-test assumptions, potential residual confounding, and exclusion of mechanically ventilated patients substantially limit inference. These findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and confirmed in larger prospective studies using prespecified analyses, distribution-appropriate statistical methods, strain imaging, and inclusion of the full spectrum of critical illness.

Conclusions

Serum Gal-3 levels showed exploratory associations with selected echocardiographic abnormalities in patients with COVID-19, with patterns that varied according to disease severity and stage. Gal-3, interpreted together with echocardiographic findings, may help characterize cardiovascular involvement during acute COVID-19 and the post-COVID period. However, because of the retrospective design, small subgroup sizes, multiple unadjusted comparisons, unassessed parametric-test assumptions, potential residual confounding, and exclusion of mechanically ventilated patients, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in larger prospective studies using distribution-appropriate analyses, advanced strain imaging, and inclusion of mechanically ventilated patients.
