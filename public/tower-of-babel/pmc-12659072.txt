Association between adherence to Dietary Approach to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet with sleep quality, sleep duration, inflammation, and oxidative stress among Iranian adults
Authors: Elaheh Foroughi, Zahra Moradmand, Alireza Rashki, Farnaz Shahdadian, Parisa Rouhani, Parvane Saneei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe aimed to evaluate the relationship between Dietary Approach to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet adherence and sleep quality, quantity, inflammation, and oxidative stress in Iranian adults.MethodsThis cross-sectional research was carried out in 2021 and 535 individuals contributed to the present analysis. Using a standardized 168-item food frequency questionnaire, we assessed the subjects’ typical dietary intakes and estimated DASH score. Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was employed to assess both quantity and quality of sleep. Serum inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers were measured through fasting blood sampling.ResultsHigher adherence to DASH (vs. lower adherence) was inversely associated with poor sleep quality and short sleeping [fully-adjusted model for poor sleep quality: ORT3 vs. T1 = 0.64; 95%CI: 0.44–0.94; for short sleeping: ORT3 vs. T1 = 0.66; 95%CI: 0.45–0.96]. These relations were observed particularly in women [fully-adjusted model for poor sleep quality: ORT3 vs. T1 = 0.14; 95%CI: 0.04–0.56; for short sleeping: ORT3 vs. T1 = 0.12; 95%CI: 0.03–0.55]. Moreover, moderate and higher adherences to DASH diet were respectively linked to a lower odds of two sleep quality domains: delay in falling asleep [fully-adjusted model: ORT2 vs. T1 = 0.39; 95%CI: 0.15–0.98], and sleep disorders [fully-adjusted model: ORT3 vs. T1 = 0.26; 95%CI: 0.08–0.84]. In the fully adjusted model, no link was found between adherence to the DASH diet and biomarkers of inflammation or oxidative stress (p ≥ 0.05).ConclusionAmong studied middle-aged Iranian population, adherence to DASH dietary pattern was associated with improved sleep quality and quantity, especially in women. None of the biomarkers acted as mediators in these relationships.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07375-5.Keywords: Dietary Approach to Stop Hypertension (DASH) Diet, Sleep quality, Sleep duration, Adults

Introduction

Sleep disorders rank third on the list of health issues affecting adults, especially elders [1]. Sleep problems can impair productivity, quality of life and health [2]. Adverse health outcomes like cardiovascular disease [3], type 2 diabetes [4], and metabolic dysfunction [5] are connected with insufficient or deficient sleep. It has also been linked to risk-taking behavior, accidents, and higher mortality rates. These days, approximately one-third of adults in developed countries have reported that they had sleep difficulties at least at one stage of their lives [6]. However, there is little information on how much sleep disorders affect quality of life in developing populations [7]. In Iran, sleep disorders are highly prevalent, particularly with increasing age of adults, posing a significant public health concern [1].

Sleep quantity and quality might be influenced by hormonal changes, metabolic dysfunction, environmental factors, weight status, and inflammatory processes [8]. Research has revealed that lifestyle factors like dietary intake have a significant impact on sleep quality [9]. There is growing interest in the possible relation between sleep disorders and dietary patterns such as Dietary Approach to Stop Hypertension (DASH). DASH diet appears to be helpful in preventing metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, stroke, and cardiovascular disease [10, 11]. Adherence to this pattern is recognized based on high intake of whole grains, vegetables, fruits, low-fat dairy, nuts, and beans, as well as restricted consumption of red or processed meat, sweets, and drinks with added sugar [12].

Several studies have examined the association between DASH diet and sleep quality, but most of them were conducted in Western countries [13], or just evaluated domains of sleep quality (such as sleep disturbances) in relation to dietary patterns [14]. A study conducted among 7,987 Chinese men and women found that a healthy diet, such as DASH diet, was linked to better sleep quality [15]. Due to differences in genetics, lifestyle, and physical activity, these results cannot be fully generalized to the Iranian population. In Iran, limited research has examined the association between DASH diet and sleep, and existing studies have focused on specific groups, such as adolescents or women with specific health conditions which are not representative of the entire Iranian adult population [16, 17]. For instance, an investigation on 350 Iranian breastfeeding mothers indicated that adherence to DASH diet was associated with shorter sleep latency, fewer sleep disorders score and higher sleep efficiency [18]. While in another cross-sectional study conducted among Iranian adolescent girls, no significant association was observed between DASH diet and insomnia [19]. Another cross-sectional study on 337 Iranian men and women employees indicated that a higher DASH score was associated with a lower probability of having short-sleep duration, but not poor sleep quality [20]. Studies showed that sex and hormonal factors could affect these associations. Sex hormones such as estrogen, progesterone, and testosterone play a crucial role in sleep patterns in both men and women, and their fluctuations are closely related to life stages such as andropause in men and menopause in women. In women, progesterone and estrogen undergo dramatic changes during menopause and are associated with increased sleep disturbances and insomnia [21, 22]. In men, testosterone gradually declines during andropause and is related to changes in sleep patterns, although the direct link is less clearly defined than in women [21, 23]. Taken together, the findings of previous studies were not homogeneous. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to evaluate the association between DASH diet adherence and sleep quality, quantity, inflammation, and oxidative stress in Iranian adults.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional research was carried out in 2021 at a large city (Isfahan) in the center of Iran on a somewhat representative group of adults. Overall, 600 adults (of two genders) aged 20 to 65 were picked from 20 schools in Isfahan, using a multistage cluster random sampling approach. Detailed protocol was published before [24]. All adults working at chosen schools, including teachers, workers, assistants, administrators, and crews, were included to create a representative sample of an adult population across different levels of socioeconomic status. Participants in this research project were not permitted to be included in the investigation if they have any of the following characteristics: (1) having a history of chronic disease; (2) being pregnant or breastfeeding women; and (3) adhering to a diet plan with a restricted- or an increased-calorie to lose or gain weight. Out of those who were invited, 543 were accepted to participate in our research (90.5%). Individuals who met the following conditions were additionally excluded: (1) reporting energy intake outside of the range of 800–4200 kcal/day (n = 3); (2) leaving more than 70 items blank on the questionnaire concerning food frequency (n = 4); and (3) declining to provide a blood sample (n = 1). As a result, 535 individuals contributed to the present analysis. The study was carried out based on the Helsinki Declaration, and each participant was informed and signed a written consent. The study has received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences.

Assessment of dietary intakes

Typical consumption of subjects was assessed by using a standardized valid Willett-format semi-quantitative 168-item food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) [25]. In a previous validation investigation on 132 middle-aged Iranian individuals, significant relationships were identified between food intakes measured by this FFQ and 24-hour recalls [25], resulting in reasonable correlations for overall calorie (r = 0.55), protein (r = 0.65), fat (r = 0.59), and fiber (r = 0.67). A qualified dietitian gave the study participants instructions on how to complete the FFQ and asked them to indicate how much and how often they had eaten every item of food over the previous year. Subsequently, the items were converted to grams per day using the weight of common portion sizes [26]. Then, to determine daily nutritional and energy consumption, we put all items into the Nutritionist IV software.

Estimation of DASH score

Adherence to DASH was estimated based on the following 8 food groups: higher consumptions of 1) fruits, 2) vegetables, 3) whole grains, 4) nuts and legumes, 5) low-fat dairy products, and lower intake of 6) sodium, 7) sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs), and 8) red and processed meat [27]. All of these food items were first adjusted for energy intake by the residual method. In case of each DASH component, participants were categorized into five groups based on energy-adjusted quintiles. A high score of 5 was assigned to the highest quintile of whole grains, fruits, vegetables, nuts and legumes, and low-fat dairy products. On the other hand, a high score of 5 was allocated to the first quintile of sodium, SSBs, and red and processed meats. Therefore, quintiles of DASH components were directly or indirectly scored as 1 to 5. By summing up these scores, a total DASH score (varied from 8 to 40) was estimated.

Assessment of outcomes

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), a standardized valid tool, was employed to assess the quantity and quality of sleep [28]. Reliability and validity of this instrument have been already proved by a validation study [28]. Furthermore, validation of the Persian version of this questionnaire was tested among Iranian adults [29]; a sensitivity of 93.6 and specificity of 72.2 were documented [29]. PSQI consists of 19 questions that are categorized into the following seven fields: person’s general descriptions of his/her sleep, delay in falling asleep, duration of beneficial sleep, sleep efficiency, sleep disorders, use of sleeping medicine, and daily functional disorders. Each category was scored based on a four-point grading system (0–3), 0 for a normal status and 3 for a problematic status. Results of these seven categories were combined to create a final score that ranged from 0 to 21; a greater total score denoted poorer sleeping quality. Individuals were grouped into 3 groups: 0–3, good quality; 4–5, intermediate quality; and ≥6, poor quality [28]. A total PSQI score of 6 or more was considered as poor sleep quality in the current investigation. The PSQI's fourth item was used to measure the length of sleep: “How many hours during the night do you sleep?” Fewer than six hours of sleep/night was regarded as having short sleep duration or being short sleeper. Because, based on a previous meta-analysis, most prior studies considered sleep duration less than 6 hours as short sleeping [30]. Furthermore, this meta-analysis demonstrated that sleeping less than 6 hours was associated with an increased risk of mortality, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and obesity. However, a sensitivity analysis was done by considering less than 7 hours of sleep as a cutoff for short sleepers to assess the robustness of the findings.

Assessment of other variables

Weight and height of participants were measured when they were standing in their bare foot and minimum of clothing. A bioelectrical body composition analyzer machine (Tanita MC-780 MA, Tokyo, Japan) was used to calculate body weight, muscle mass, fat mass, fat-free mass, and fat percentage. Height was measured with a wall-mounted tape meter, which was accurate to within 0.1 cm. Body mass index (BMI) was estimated. Data of further influencing variables, including age, sex, marital status, education, smoking habits, socioeconomic conditions, drinking tea and coffee after 8:00 PM, and usage of antidepressant medicine were also gathered through a self-reported questionnaire. Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) was assessed using the STOP-Bang questionnaire [31], a self-reported screening tool with eight “yes or no” questions involving snoring, fatigue, observed apnea, high blood pressure, BMI > 35 kg/m2, age > 50, neck circumference > 40 cm, and male sex [31, 32]. Individuals with an unfavorable score of 5 were considered as those with OSA. Furthermore, exercise levels of the individuals were evaluated using the validated International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [33]. A fast blood sample of participants was drawn and collected to evaluate inflammatory, oxidative stress, and antioxidant biomarkers. Commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits were applied to measure the concentrations of malondialdehyde (MDA) (Kiazist Co, Hamedan, Iran), superoxide dismutase (SOD) (Kiazist Co, Hamedan, Iran), glutathione peroxidase (GPX) (Kiazist Co, Hamedan, Iran), and C-reactive protein (CRP) (turbidimetry kit, latex enhanced turbidimetric method, Delta.DP).

Statistical analysis

Based on tertiles of DASH score, participants were categorized (score of 9–21: T1, score of 22–26: T2; score of 27–37: T3). Continuous variables were analyzed by analysis of variance (ANOVA) across tertiles of DASH and reported as mean±SD/SE. Categorical variables were evaluated by chi-square test among tertiles of adherence to DASH diet and are presented as percentages. Dietary intakes of individuals across tertiles of DASH score were assessed by analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) (by adjusting sex, age, and energy intake). To evaluate possible relations between DASH adherence and poor sleep quality (score ≥6 vs. < 6) and being short sleeper ( < 6 vs. ≥6 hours/night), we applied binary logistic regression and linear trends across tertiles. Also, a sensitivity analysis was performed by considering less than 7 hours of sleep as a cutoff for short sleepers to assess the robustness of the findings. In binary logistic regression first tertile of DASH diet was regarded as the reference tertile, and for linear trends DASH score was considered as an ordinal variable. Then, all odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were evaluated. Adjustments for age, sex, and energy intake were made for first model. In second model, we conducted further adjustments for marital status, education, smoking, physical activity, socio-economic status, obstructive sleep apnea, use of antidepressant medicine, and drinking tea and coffee after 8:00 PM. In last model, additional adjustment for BMI was done. Based on sex of participants, a stratified analysis was conducted. By the use of linear regression analysis, the potential links between inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers and DASH diet adherence were evaluated; age, sex, and BMI were possible confounders that considered. In addition, ANOVA was applied to estimate levels of oxidative stress and inflammatory biomarkers across different categories of sleep quality (good, moderate, poor sleep quality) and sleep duration ( < 6, 6–8, > 8 hours). Analyses were done by SPSS software version 26 (IBM, Chicago, IL), and we considered P-values lower than 0.05 as statistically significant. Mediation analyses were conducted to examine whether the association between adherence to DASH diet and poor sleep quality or short sleeping was mediated by CRP, MDA, SOD, and GPX. The “paramed” module in STATA (version 17) was employed in this regard to estimate the direct, indirect, and total effects. DASH, sleep quality, sleep duration, and the mediators were considered as continuous variables. The median of the first (19) and the third (29) tertiles of DASH were compared for the effect estimation. Confounding variables considered in the mediation analyses were age, sex, energy intake, marital status, education, smoking, physical activity, socio-economic status, obstructive sleep apnea, use of antidepressant medicine, and drinking tea and coffee after 8:00 PM. BMI was not included in the analysis as a confounder, because it was suspected to lie on the causal path from the exposure to the outcomes, as a mediator.

Results

Mean age and BMI of 535 studied participants were 42.58 (±11.14) years and 26.91 (±4.41) kg/m2. Participants were comprised of 54% males (289 men) and 46% females (246 women). General characteristics of included participants across energy-adjusted tertiles of DASH score are presented in Table 1. Participants in the third tertile of DASH were more likely to be older (p < 0.001), have lower weight (p = 0.03) and height (p = 0.01), be more physically active (p = 0.03), and less likely to consume tea or coffee after 8:00 PM (p = 0.02). Other differences across tertiles of DASH adherence were not statistically significant.

Among all participants, 136 and 153 individuals had respectively poor sleep quality (sleep quality score ≥6) and short sleeping ( < 6 hours of sleep/night). Prevalence of participants with poor sleep quality and short sleepers across tertiles of DASH score is shown in Fig. 1. In the first, second, and third tertiles of adherence to the DASH diet, 36.4%, 25.1%, and 25.2% of individuals had poor sleep quality (p = 0.03); but, prevalence of being short sleepers across tertiles of DASH score did not remarkably vary (p = 0.96).

Prevalence of having poor sleep quality and being a short sleeper across tertiles of DASH score

Multivariable-adjusted ORs for poor sleep quality and short sleeping across tertiles of adherence to DASH are provided in Table 3. In crude model, moderate and high adherence to DASH were related to lower odds of poor sleep quality (ORT2 vs. T1 = 0.59; 95% CI: 0.38–0.92; ORT3 vs. T1 = 0.59; 95% CI: 0.37–0.95). After adjustment of potential confounders, this association became stronger and participants in the middle and top tertile of DASH had lower odds of poor sleep quality than individuals in the bottom tertile (ORT2 vs. T1 = 0.47; 95% CI: 0.24–0.93; ORT3 vs T1 = 0.43; 95% CI: 0.20–0.92); significant protective associations were also found for each tertile increment in DASH adherence, in crude (OR = 0.76; 95% CI: 0.60–0.97) and multivariable-adjusted models (OR = 0.64; 95% CI: 0.44–0.94). In crude model, high adherence to DASH diet compared to low adherence reduced odds of being a short sleeper, but this association was not significant. However, after full adjustment, the third vs. first tertile of DASH score was associated with significantly lower odds of short sleeping (ORT3 vs. T1 = 0.43; 95% CI: 0.20–0.92) and each tertile increase in adherence to DASH diet could reduce odds of being a short sleeper by 34% (OR = 0.66; 95% CI: 0.45–0.96).

When we considered less than 7 hours of sleep as the cutoff for being short sleeper in a sensitivity analysis, among participants, 70.1% (n = 375) were classified as short sleepers, and there was no significant difference in frequency of short sleepers across tertiles of DASH score (p = 0.79). As shown in Supplementary Table 1, in this sensitivity analysis, no statistically significant association was found between DASH and having short sleep duration, in crude and fully adjusted models (p > 0.05).

Multivariable-adjusted ORs for having poor sleep quality and being a short sleeper across tertiles of DASH score, stratified by sex, are shown in Table 4. Among sex categories (male vs. female participants), significant associations were found between DASH score and poor sleep quality and being a short sleeper, only among female individuals. Such that a lower odds of poor quality of sleep was found in females in crude model (ORT3 vs T1 = 0.47; 95% CI: 0.24–0.93); after adjustments, this relation became remarkably stronger (ORT3 vs T1 = 0.14; 95% CI: 0.04–0.56). Discussion

Among the studied middle-aged Iranian adults, those who adhered to DASH diet had reduced likelihood of having poor sleep quality, and being a short sleeper. These associations were stronger in female individuals than males. DASH diet was inversely related to two main domains of sleep quality (delay in falling asleep and sleep disorders). Higher quality of sleep was related to lower level of MDA, as well. As higher adherence to DASH diet was relatively associated with decreased MDA levels, and lower MDA levels was found in those with better sleep quality. However, in mediation analyses, none of the biomarkers acted as mediators between DASH diet and better sleep quality.

Sleep disorders could decrease quality of life and social performance [2, 34], and increase cardiovascular disease [3], type 2 diabetes [4], and metabolic dysfunctions [5]. Findings of the present study emphasize that more adherence to DASH dietary pattern should be recommended to general population in order to reduce sleep disorders, particularly in women.

Prior investigations have reported controversial relationships between DASH diet and sleep disorders. A clinical trial study on 66 Iranian women with diabetes found that following DASH diet for 12 weeks could improve stress and sleep [35]. Similarly, a cross-sectional study that examined the association between adherence to DASH diet and maternal and infant sleep disorders involving 350 Iranian breastfeeding mothers reported that adherence to this diet was associated with a lower score for sleep disorders in mothers and their infants [18]. Another cross-sectional study among 3,939 Chinese subjects that examined the association between DASH diet and self-reported sleep-disordered breathing demonstrated that adherence to DASH was associated with a reduced chance of sleep-disordered breathing [36]. On the other hand, a cross-sectional study examined the association between DASH diet and sleep quality in 3,941 American adults over 30 years old found that a higher DASH diet quality score was inversely associated with poor sleep-related daytime dysfunction, but it was not significant for sleepiness and sleep disturbance in the general population [13]. Another cross-sectional study focusing on relationship between DASH dietary pattern and insomnia, conducted among 488 Iranian adolescent girls, stated that there was no significant association between adherence to DASH diet and insomnia [19]. Additionally, a cross-sectional study among 6,084 Iranian individuals that examined relationship between both DASH and Mediterranean diets on quality and quantity of sleep in individuals with psychological disorders stated that Mediterranean diet could improve sleep of people with severe depression, but this was not significant for DASH diet [37].

Several underlying mechanisms for relationships between DASH diet and sleep quality and duration have been proposed. DASH diet, rich in antioxidant-containing foods such as fruits and vegetables and omega-3 fatty acids, can reduce inflammatory markers, which are linked to sleep disorders [38]. By limiting added sugars and increasing dietary fiber from whole grains and vegetables, this diet helps regulate blood glucose levels and improve insulin sensitivity. Blood glucose fluctuations can disrupt circadian rhythms [39]. Food components such as cherries and nuts, which are emphasized in DASH diet, increase melatonin synthesis due to their tryptophan content, a precursor to serotonin and melatonin, facilitating better sleep regulation [40]. Following DASH diet supports weight management by providing high-fiber, low-calorie foods, which can reduce obstructive sleep apnea and significantly improve sleep quality [41]. High fiber intake from components of DASH diet has been additionally shown to improve gut microbiome composition, which is associated with sleep disorders [42]. Some components of this diet are rich in magnesium, which acts as a cofactor in regulating neurotransmitters, increases relaxation and reduces neural excitability in people, and improves sleep quality [43]. Increasing potassium intake from fruits and vegetables and reducing excess sodium intake from processed foods in this diet can improve electrolyte levels, and better balance in electrolyte levels can lead to improved sleep quality [44, 45].

In the current investigation, associations between DASH diet, sleep quality, and being a short sleeper were not significant in subgroup analyses among men, despite the relatively large male sample, which might indicate the presence of sex-specific mechanisms that should be explored. The prevalence of poor sleep quality was substantially lower among men compared to women (24.2 vs. 33.7%, p = 0.01), but no such difference was observed regarding the frequency of short sleepers (26.0 vs. 24.8%, p = 0.42). As indicated, a higher prevalence of poor sleep quality in females could lead to stronger associations. Additionally, female participants had a 0.4-point higher score of adherence to DASH diet (24.2 vs. 23.8). Also, estrogen and progesterone in women and testosterone in men could affect sleep patterns and quality; decreasing these sex hormones during menopause and andropause might be linked to more sleep disturbances [21–23]. Furthermore, other hormones (melatonin and cortisol) could regulate circadian rhythms, and their age-related fluctuations could affect sleep quality in males and females [46].

One of the strengths of this study was conducting it on both male and female participants. The sample selection was based on multistage cluster random sampling to provide a somewhat representative sample of the entire population. Participants were chosen from various socio-economic categories. However, this study included the following limitations: the study design was cross-sectional, and a causal relation was not established. Additionally, there might be a possibility of reverse causality; poor sleep quality, sleep deprivation, and short sleep duration might lead to poor dietary choices and lower adherence to DASH diet [47, 48]. Further prospective studies are required to establish the direction of the DASH-sleep relationship. Despite efforts to eliminate confounding factors, there might be additional confounding factors that could not be considered; this issue might have influenced the results. The next limitations were misclassification, recall, and other possible reporting biases, due to the use of an FFQ to assess dietary consumption, which was susceptible to these biases as it required individuals to remember their dietary intake over the last year. To reduce such biases in future investigations, FFQ should be supplemented with more objective dietary assessment methods, such as 24-hour dietary recalls. Moreover, although cluster random sampling was employed to select a sample that was, to some extent, representative of the target population, various factors, including lifestyle, dietary habits, cultural dietary variations, and genetic factors, might limit the generalizability of our results to other communities. To address generalizability, future investigations should include participants from diverse age groups and populations, to account for cultural and lifestyle differences. In addition, sample size was not calculated separately for women and men, so the study did not specifically account for potential differences between male and female participants. Subsequent studies should carefully consider this aspect and explore it alongside hormonal factors to a shed light on the underlying pathways. Due to financial constraints, the sample size was determined based on sleep disorder rather than oxidative stress or inflammatory biomarkers. Consequently, this might result in non-significant associations between these biomarkers and DASH diet score, as well as sleep quality and duration. Future studies should consider this point or include other oxidative stress biomarkers—including total antioxidant capacity (TAC), and antioxidant enzymes such as catalase—as well as a variety of inflammatory markers, such as interleukins (IL-6, IL-1β), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α).

In conclusion, the present study involving a middle-aged Iranian population demonstrated that adherence to DASH dietary pattern was associated with improved sleep quality and quantity, particularly in women. Although declined MDA levels might be expected to act as a mediator in these relationships, our analysis did not support a mediating role for this biomarker. More adherence to DASH diet was linked to lower prevalence of two sleep quality domains, delay in falling asleep and sleep disorders. Further prospective studies are necessary to validate these findings.
