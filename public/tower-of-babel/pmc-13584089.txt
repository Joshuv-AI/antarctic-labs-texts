Hydrogel-Based Monitoring in Perioperative Anesthesia: A Conceptual Review and Translational Perspective
Authors: Jinyue Chen, Yue Qi, Cong Wang, Dalong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPerioperative anesthesia monitoring increasingly extends beyond intermittent vital-sign measurements to continuous assessment of bioelectrical signals, tissue-device interfaces, local pressure, wound microenvironments, and selected biochemical markers. Hydrogels are attractive soft-interface materials because their water-rich networks can combine tissue-like mechanics, adhesion, ionic or electronic conductivity, microfluidic sampling, molecular recognition, and stimulus-responsive functions. This conceptual review evaluates hydrogel-based monitoring technologies from a perioperative perspective and maps evidence from wearable bioelectronics, microneedle and interstitial-fluid sensing, smart wound care, local drug delivery, and implantable interfaces onto preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative use cases. Importantly, the current evidence base is predominantly preclinical or derived from adjacent clinical contexts; among the studies summarized, we identified no hydrogel monitoring device that has yet been prospectively evaluated in a dedicated perioperative patient cohort. Accordingly, this review distinguishes demonstrated material and device performance from proposed perioperative applications. We organize the field by dominant material-function systems rather than treating nanostructure as a standalone class, and examine soft-tissue matching, conductivity, wet adhesion, dehydration resistance, shelf life, biosensing accuracy, and device integration. We also discuss translational requirements for perioperative glycemic monitoring and AI-assisted systems, including reference-method validation, independent datasets, explainability, and Software as a Medical Device considerations. Hydrogels are therefore best viewed not as replacements for established anesthesia monitors, but as enabling interfaces that may add continuous local and molecular information. Future progress depends on standardized performance testing, storage and sterilization validation, prospective clinical studies, and regulatory-ready system integration.

Introduction

The primary objective of perioperative anesthesia monitoring has progressively shifted from merely maintaining vital signs within normal ranges to early risk detection, continuous tracking of pathophysiological change, and integration of monitoring data into clinical decisions. Local interface complications are not rare: a 2024 meta-analysis of 22 studies involving 10,510 adult inpatients estimated the prevalence of medical adhesive-related skin injury at 16% and the incidence at 25%.1 Although these data are not specific to hydrogel monitors or to all perioperative settings, they illustrate a clinically relevant burden associated with repeated adhesion, moisture, fragile skin, and device fixation - problems for which soft, conformal interfaces may offer practical advantages. Research Progress on Hydrogels and Their Monitoring Effects

Hydrogels are porous, three-dimensional network structures composed of highly hydrated polymer chains that are cross-linked through chemical or physical means. Physical cross-linking processes primarily involve hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and ionic interactions. Chemical cross-linking mechanisms include Schiff base reactions, Michael additions, photopolymerization, click chemistry, and enzymatic cross-linking. The cross-linking method plays a critical role in determining the hydrogel’s stability and its final properties. Techniques such as electrospinning, patterning, freeze-drying, salt impregnation, and 3D bioprinting are employed to design hydrogels tailored for various applications.14,15 Compared to traditional rigid electrodes and hard probes, conductive hydrogel electronic skin more accurately replicates the mechanical environment of skin and mucous membranes, enhancing adhesion and comfort while minimizing motion artifacts. Furthermore, conductive dressings that integrate both monitoring and therapeutic functions indicate that perioperative monitoring does not have to be separated from localized intervention.16,17

Recent studies have shown that AI-assisted dressings can now extract trend data from complex wounds, while stretchable nano-hydrogels facilitate the collection of electrophysiological signals. This advancement allows for the continuous monitoring of changes related to local inflammation, pain, and surface electrical activity—key factors in anesthesiology—on a single flexible interface.18,19 In clinical applications, perioperative and postoperative local anesthetic delivery systems are among the most relevant areas for hydrogels. Additionally, composite hydrogel platforms designed for health monitoring have demonstrated that it is feasible to achieve a balance of softness, conductivity, and long-term stability at the material level.20,21 The use of natural polymer hydrogels in motion and biomedical monitoring highlights their exceptional suitability for recording continuous, subtle mechanical changes. Electrochemical wearable hydrogel devices further extend this capability to monitor body fluids and metabolic indicators, providing new opportunities for preoperative baseline monitoring and postoperative follow-up.22,23 More importantly, the anesthesia setting involves several typical “interface issues”, such as oral mucosal trauma from intubation, exposure of the ocular surface, pressure on catheter fixation points, and the tolerance of recovery-phase dressings. Research on biomaterials addressing these challenges indicates that a truly effective hydrogel platform must balance flexibility, low irritation, and long-term usability.24,25

A critical limitation of the current literature is the gap between device feasibility and perioperative clinical validation. Most hydrogel monitoring platforms summarized in this review were tested in laboratory models, animals, healthy volunteers, chronic-wound settings, or other non-perioperative contexts. We did not identify a hydrogel monitoring device prospectively evaluated in a dedicated perioperative patient cohort. Therefore, this article is intentionally framed as a conceptual review and translational perspective: it maps adjacent evidence to perioperative needs while explicitly separating demonstrated material or device functions from proposed clinical use.

Hydrogels as Foundational Materials and Platforms for Perioperative Monitoring Interfaces

Various hydrogel fabrication processes are employed to create sensing interfaces, including photolithography-based techniques, microchannel molding, evaporation, electrochemical deposition, laser ablation, roll-to-roll printing, and microfabrication.26,27 These techniques affect morphology, strain sensing, physiological-signal acquisition, adhesion, and integration with microfluidics or electrodes. Because nanoscale structuring - including nanofillers, nanoporous networks, nanofibers, and nanocomposites - can occur across multiple hydrogel chemistries, this review does not treat “nanostructured hydrogels” as a separate candidate class. Instead, the field is organized into six dominant material-function systems, while nanoscale design is treated as a cross-cutting strategy within those systems (Figure 1). Hydrogels Primarily Based on Natural Polymers for Monitoring

Natural polymer-based monitoring hydrogels are materials primarily composed of natural polymers such as cellulose, alginate, hyaluronic acid, gelatin/GelMA, chitosan, proteins, or polyphenols. The main advantages of these systems include their high biocompatibility, strong hydrophilicity, and tissue-like properties. Additionally, they often feature inherent degradability, modifiable sites, and a favorable environment for cell adhesion.22,28 In the perioperative setting, this nanoscale functional delivery system, created by such a natural scaffold, makes the material more suitable for interacting with delicate skin, wound margins, and interfaces that are subject to prolonged contact.

Cellulose-based systems are among the most representative categories of natural monitoring hydrogels. Research has demonstrated that cellulose-based materials offer stable film-forming properties, tunable mechanical characteristics, and good interfacial compatibility in applications such as pressure/strain sensing, wearable biosensing, and medical diagnostics. Additionally, the incorporation of conductive components enables these materials to combine flexibility with electrical performance.29,30 In the perioperative setting, cellulose hydrogels are particularly well-suited for monitoring positional pressure, limb movement, rehabilitation postures, and strain in catheter fixation areas, as they are readily available and easy to prepare in large quantities. GelMA and gelatin systems combine the biological activity of natural macromolecules with the convenience of photo-crosslinkable materials. GelMA microneedles and GelMA 3D hydrogels have been widely utilized for percutaneous fluid sampling, cell culture, and the maturation of myocardial models. These systems have proven to be effective not only as surface sensing layers but also as key media for minimally invasive sampling and in vivo model validation.31,32 In perioperative monitoring, GelMA’s potential primarily lies in minimally invasive interstitial fluid sampling, in-situ metabolite analysis, and device validation when used alongside tissue engineering models.

Interstitial fluid (ISF) contains molecular biomarkers derived from cells and subcutaneous capillaries and is increasingly used for minimally invasive biosensing. Qiao et al developed gelatin methacrylate (GelMA) and graphene oxide (GO) microneedles for enrichment and detection of microRNA biomarkers from ISF. The GO-GelMA microneedles combined mechanical strength, rapid sampling, and swelling capacity, collecting up to 21.34 uL of ISF within 30 minutes; catalytic hairpin assembly was then used for signal amplification.33 Although this work addressed psoriasis-related biomarkers rather than perioperative monitoring, it illustrates how hydrogel microneedles can combine sampling, enrichment, and molecular detection in a single interface.

Hyaluronic acid and alginate systems, in contrast, exhibit superior adaptability to moist environments and strong integration capabilities with biosensing. Hyaluronic acid hydrogels have been incorporated into patent portfolios for smart wound monitoring and biosensing, highlighting their strong translational potential for detecting glucose, pH, inflammatory proteins, and wound status. Additionally, real-time characterization of alginate’s in situ gelation behavior offers a materials science-based understanding of the advantages and limitations of natural ion-crosslinked networks during injection, spraying, and in situ formation at monitoring interfaces.3,34 These materials are ideal for use around surgical incisions, drainage sites, and in scenarios that integrate local drug delivery and monitoring.

Another notable feature of natural polymer systems is their enhanced ability to integrate both “monitoring” and “therapeutic environmental regulation” onto a single platform. Hydrogels loaded with polyphenols or natural products often exhibit antioxidant, antibacterial, angiogenic, and moist wound healing properties simultaneously. This makes them especially suitable for the local management of postoperative wounds, skin at high risk of pressure ulcers, and patients with impaired glucose metabolism.35,36 In the perioperative period, the advantages of natural polymer-based systems lie not in their extreme electrical conductivity, but in their low irritation, sustained adhesion, and compatibility with the local microenvironment. However, natural polymers have some limitations. Batch-to-batch variability, sensitivity to cross-linking rates, insufficient mechanical strength, and poor long-term resistance to dehydration often restrict their potential as high-precision electronic interfaces. In particular, applications that require high signal-to-noise ratio electrophysiological recording, long-term calibration-free continuous operation, or standardized mass production often necessitate the combination of natural polymers with synthetic polymers, conductive phases, or nanofillers to meet stringent clinical requirements.37,38

In terms of perioperative adaptability, natural polymer systems are ideally suited for three types of applications: first, creating long-term contact interfaces that are compatible with skin, wounds, and mucous membranes; second, delivering local drugs, antimicrobial agents, or bioactive nutrients to enable synergistic monitoring and intervention; and third, providing a testing substrate that closely mimics the physiological environment for organ-on-a-chip systems, myocardial models, and regenerative medicine validation platforms.32,39 In other words, systems dominated by natural polymers serve more as “clinically friendly monitoring platforms”. The key to their successful translation lies in addressing their mechanical and electrical limitations through compositing strategies, rather than merely focusing on the material’s inherent maximum performance.

Recent biobased and hybrid-hydrogel studies, although largely developed outside perioperative medicine, provide additional design lessons relevant to clinically deployable soft interfaces. Starch-alginate networks strengthened through glucono-delta-lactone demonstrate how biopolymer interactions can improve toughness and thermal resilience.40 Starch barrier coatings on chitosan hydrogels illustrate control of water transport and macromolecule release.41 Conductive hydrogel confinement has been used to amplify electrochemical aptasensing,42 while nanocellulose-chitosan composite networks and self-healing carboxymethyl-chitosan/oxidized-alginate systems demonstrate mechanical reinforcement, controlled release, dynamic bonding, pH responsiveness, and biodegradability.43,44 These reports should not be interpreted as direct perioperative evidence; rather, they identify transferable design strategies - polysaccharide blending, barrier coatings, confined conductive domains, nanoscale reinforcement, and reversible crosslinking - that may improve future perioperative sensing interfaces.

Hydrogels Primarily Based on Synthetic Polymers for Monitoring

Synthetic polymer-based monitoring hydrogels typically use PEG, PVA, PVP, polyacrylamide, polyurethane, or other engineered polymers as their matrix. The key advantages of these systems include structural controllability, consistent batch-to-batch quality, predictable degradation and swelling behaviors, and seamless integration with industrial manufacturing processes.45,46 For clinical devices, such systems enable standardized evaluation and streamline regulatory submissions.

Research on PEG-based systems has clearly shown that monitoring hydrogels must not only “function” but also indicate “how long they will function”. The degradation of PEG-based hydrogels is significantly influenced by factors such as terminal group chemistry, molecular weight, network structure, and environmental conditions. As a result, multiple factors impact the degradation rate, including the chemical properties of the main chain and terminal groups, the molecular weight of the macromonomer, and the network structure. Advanced non-invasive imaging technologies, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), ultrasound, and computed tomography (CT), are currently being developed to enable comprehensive monitoring of hydrogels using existing clinical techniques. This progress allows for a more accurate prediction of their functional lifespan through preliminary design, as well as in vitro and in vivo testing.45,47 This is especially critical for perioperative applications, as the lifespan requirements for short-term intraoperative electrodes, continuous three-day postoperative patches, and semi-implantable monitoring platforms vary significantly. Synthetic polymers such as PVA and PVP, however, offer greater engineering stability in microneedle and body fluid sampling applications. Expandable PVA/PVP microneedle patches have been successfully used for interstitial fluid extraction and glucose-related monitoring. Additionally, colloidal photonic crystals based on synthetic polymers, along with polymer-based signal amplification strategies, have advanced the visualization and highly sensitive detection of glucose, metabolites, and low-abundance analytes.48,49 This makes synthetic polymer systems ideal for preoperative metabolic screening, continuous perioperative glucose monitoring, and rapid point-of-care analysis.

Hu et al achieved a significant breakthrough by developing a polyvinyl alcohol/glycerol/polyaniline (PGA) hydrogel sensor, which shows exceptional sensitivity in detecting human movement, with a range factor of 2.14 and a response time of just 230 milliseconds. Its high sensitivity enables the detection of subtle movements, while its quick response time allows real-time tracking of fast actions. These features make the sensor particularly suitable for applications requiring precise motion capture, such as rehabilitation monitoring. The sensor’s success in tracking complex joint movements during human motion represents a major advancement in biomechanics. By providing detailed biomechanical data and the ability to capture fine-motion data in real time, these sensors contribute to the development of more effective injury prevention strategies. Building on this, Yu et al incorporated polyaniline nanoparticles (PANI-NPs) into poly(poly(ethylene glycol) methacrylate-co-acrylic acid) (P(PEG-co-AA)) to create a scaffold through dynamic electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding. PANI-NPs serve as conductive fillers, imparting electrical conductivity to the hydrogel, while enhanced interfacial interactions between the PANI-NPs and the P(PEG-co-AA) matrix provide the hydrogel with high tensile strain capacity (>1000%), low modulus, outstanding elasticity (η = 0.07, energy dissipation factor at 500% strain), and rapid self-healing properties. This remarkable elasticity enables the sensor to be seamlessly integrated into athletic apparel without restricting the wearer’s movement. In practical wearable sensor tests, the system successfully detected various external stimuli and human movements, including speaking, writing, joint movements, and even tiny water droplets, indicating potential applications for next-generation epidermal sensors. The seamless integration of sensors into clothing marks a paradigm shift in wearable technology, transitioning from bulky external devices to unobtrusive “smart” textiles. By embedding multiple sensors into clothing, researchers and clinicians can gain a comprehensive understanding of biomechanical conditions during complex movements.50,51

Frequent charging or battery replacement has been a significant barrier to the development of wearable technology. To address this, Jung et al innovatively combined sensing capabilities with energy harvesting and storage functions. They developed a hydrogel using a water/ethylene glycol binary solvent system, and the resulting supercapacitor demonstrated impressive specific capacitance values of 123.4 F g−1, 147.0 F g−1, and 156.2 F g−1 at −20°C, 25°C, and 80°C, respectively. This material functions as both a strain sensor and a supercapacitor, enabling it to detect various biological signals, including finger bending and swallowing. This dual functionality marks a breakthrough in wearable technology. By integrating sensing and energy storage capabilities into a single material, this approach significantly reduces the complexity and bulk of wearable devices. The application of this technology in self-powered wearable health monitors underscores its immense potential. These devices can generate and store energy from human movement, eliminating the need for external power sources or frequent battery replacements. This not only enhances user convenience but also extends the duration of continuous monitoring, which is vital for long-term health tracking and early disease detection.52

Fabrication compatibility is another key advantage of synthetic polymer systems. Reviews of deposition methods and studies on print-compatible rheological control suggest that synthetic polymers are better suited for achieving stable film quality and reproducibility through techniques such as dip-coating, spin-coating, spray-coating, extrusion printing, and micro- and nanofabrication. This is essential for the large-scale production of monitoring patches, transparent electrodes, microfluidic channels, and encapsulation layers.53,54 In this context, synthetic polymers serve as the ideal bridge for facilitating the integration of hydrogels into the industrial production chain for monitoring applications.

However, systems dominated by synthetic polymers also have clear limitations. Their bioregistration capabilities often depend on the addition of enzymes, aptamers, or nanophases. When relying solely on the polymer network, they typically lack the tissue and cell affinity found in natural macromolecules. Additionally, some systems may experience issues such as residual monomers, biodegradation products, and insufficient evaluation of long-term contact safety.45,46 Therefore, in perioperative settings, synthetic polymers are better suited to function as “precision scaffolds” and “manufacturing scaffolds”, to be used alongside natural, bioactive, or conductive components.

When comparing natural and synthetic polymers within the same selection framework, it becomes evident that they are not substitutes for one another, but rather complement each other in terms of “biocompatibility” and “engineering controllability”. Natural polymers are ideal for the layer in direct contact with tissue, while synthetic polymers are better suited for providing structural support, encapsulation, controlled mass transfer, and large-scale production. As a result, many future perioperative monitoring devices are likely to incorporate a natural/synthetic bilayer or interpenetrating network structure: the outer layer would handle wet adhesion and biocompatibility, while the inner layer would provide mechanical support, long-term stability, and integration with microfluidic systems or circuits.55,56

Hydrogels Primarily Based on Conductive Polymers for Monitoring

Conductive polymer-based monitoring hydrogels primarily utilize electroactive polymers such as PEDOT:PSS, polyaniline, and polypyrrole as key conductive phases. By interpenetrating or compositing with a hydrophilic network, these materials create continuous electronic transport pathways. These systems are currently the closest type of monitoring hydrogel to the definition of “soft electronic electrodes” and are particularly well-suited for applications such as ECG, EEG, EMG, and high-sensitivity strain detection.57,58 PEDOT:PSS is the most extensively studied material in this class, offering distinct advantages in terms of aqueous processability, electrical conductivity, and thin-film fabrication. By utilizing techniques such as secondary doping, multi-network anchoring, and interfacial engineering, it is possible to achieve low interfacial resistance and high signal stability while preserving flexibility.59–61 In perioperative medicine, the most practical application of the PEDOT:PSS-based system is replacing traditional gel electrodes for combined monitoring of ECG, EEG, and postoperative EMG/kinematics on the forehead and chest.

An electrocardiogram (ECG) measures the heart’s electrical activity and rhythm by detecting signals generated with each heartbeat, aiding in the identification and monitoring of various heart conditions. Traditional ECG monitoring typically relies on wet electrodes, which use conductive gel to enhance electrical conductivity between the skin and electrodes. However, these methods have limitations for long-term monitoring, such as skin irritation, signal attenuation over time, and discomfort. To address these challenges, 3D printing technology has enabled the development of dry electrodes, offering improved comfort while maintaining signal quality during extended cardiac monitoring. Microneedle array electrodes are a significant advancement in dry electrode technology; their design penetrates the epidermis, reducing the insulating effect of the stratum corneum and significantly lowering skin-electrode impedance. In an innovative approach, microcolumnar functional units were fabricated using a 3D-printed PLA mold with cylindrical holes (approximately 1.5 mm in diameter and 2 mm in depth). A PEDOT:PSS–WPU–D-sorbitol mixture was cast, cured at approximately 60°C, and the patterned film was peeled off. This method results in electrodes with well-defined microstructures, whose impedance levels are comparable to or lower than those of conventional wet electrodes. Experimental results confirm that, compared to conventional surface electrodes, these electrodes provide superior performance in capturing high-quality recordings of key ECG components.62

In another study, scientists developed a method to simultaneously measure electroencephalogram (EEG) and electrocardiogram (ECG) signals from zebrafish. Poly(lactic acid) (PLA) filament was chosen to print the electrodes on the printing bed, with an extrusion speed of 30 mm/s and the bed temperature set to 50°C. After the printing process, Ti/Au was deposited onto the printed electrodes to ensure the proper electrical properties for use as bioelectric sensors. This approach demonstrates the potential for creating highly miniaturized biosensors suitable for small-animal research, an area that has historically faced challenges due to size limitations. Given the roughly 2 mm × 2 mm cranial region of the zebrafish, this study is particularly significant because it requires extremely high precision in both electrode placement and signal detection.63 This miniaturized, flexible printing technology introduces innovative approaches to the fabrication of composite hydrogels.

Traditional electromyography (EMG) measurements are typically conducted in clinical settings using needle electrodes inserted directly into muscles or surface electrodes placed on the skin. The advent of wearable biosensors opens up the potential for non-invasive, continuous monitoring of muscle activity. However, ensuring stable contact between the skin and electrodes during movement presents a significant challenge, as poor contact can lead to motion artifacts and degrade signal quality. To overcome this, Wan et al developed a high-performance bioelectronic interface fabricated via 3D printing using a novel poly(vinyl alcohol-formaldehyde) (PVAF)-PEDOT:PSS composite ink. This ink allows for the precise printing of complex structures and produces a hydrogel interface with excellent conductivity, strong adhesion, and stable electrochemical performance (exceeding 100 S/m). With high adhesion (~31 kPa) and a mechanical modulus similar to that of skin, the interface prevents delamination and minimizes motion-induced artifacts. The electrode conforms comfortably to the skin’s dynamic movements, providing a significant improvement over rigid or less adhesive alternatives. In EMG recording tests, the hydrogel electrode outperformed commercial options (such as Ag/AgCl) in terms of signal stability, maintaining a high signal-to-noise ratio (>10 dB) even under varying stress and repetitive motion.64

Another advantage of conductive polymer-based systems is their ability to integrate monitoring with localized treatment. Conductive hydrogels can function not only as electronic skin and flexible electrodes but also perform additional roles such as electrical stimulation, promoting tissue repair, and monitoring conditions in chronic wounds.60,65 This is especially crucial for postoperative incisions, areas at high risk of pressure injuries, and the skin surrounding catheters, as these regions often require both continuous monitoring and localized microenvironmental intervention. From a device engineering standpoint, conductive polymer hydrogels have evolved beyond simple “patches” and are increasingly being integrated into microfluidic chips, implantable bioelectronics, and soft tissue interface systems. Their key advantage lies in their ability to collect electronic signals and perform ion-to-electron conversion, acting as a bridge that connects internal bodily information to external circuits.2,23 If perioperative monitoring is to expand from the body surface to the surfaces of specific organs or to minimally invasive deep monitoring, conductive polymer-based systems will be one of the most critical technological pillars. The main challenges with these materials include the trade-off between improving conductivity and increasing stiffness, reducing transparency, or requiring more complex chemical treatments. Additionally, the long-term stability of conductive polymers in environments characterized by high humidity, high salinity, and repeated deformation still requires systematic validation.66,67 Therefore, future breakthroughs in this field will not solely focus on achieving higher electrical conductivity, but will aim to strike a balance between electrical conductivity, toughness, fatigue resistance, low irritation, and manufacturability.

Furthermore, systems based on conductive polymers are essential for integrating monitoring with feedback-based regulation. Thanks to their strong ion-to-electron conversion capabilities, these materials are naturally suited for integration with micro-stimulation, localized electrochemical modulation, or tissue repair strategies. For instance, reviews on neurodegenerative diseases, tissue repair, and health monitoring indicate that conductive polymer/conductive hydrogel platforms have great potential for linking “recording”, “interpretation”, and “local intervention” into a closed-loop system.65,68 In the perioperative setting, this presents promising opportunities for the localized management of postoperative pain, stimulation of tissue perfusion, and monitoring of functional recovery.

Hydrogels Primarily Based on Carbon-Based and Two-Dimensional Nanomaterials for Monitoring

Carbon-based and two-dimensional nanomaterial-dominated monitoring hydrogels are composite systems that use materials such as graphene, carbon nanotubes, MXenes, MOF-derived two-dimensional sheets, quantum dots, and other nanomaterials as their core functional phases. Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) are cylindrical structures formed by rolled-up graphene sheets and are classified into single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs) and multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs). SWCNTs have diameters of approximately 1–2 nm and exhibit very high electrical conductivity, reaching up to 10−4 S/cm. In contrast, MWCNTs consist of multiple concentric layers with diameters ranging from 2 to 50 nm and have lower electrical conductivity, typically between 102 and 103 S/cm. To improve the dispersibility of CNTs in polar solvents, oxygen-containing functional groups, such as carboxyl and hydroxyl groups, are often introduced onto their surfaces. Graphene, a two-dimensional, single-layer arrangement of carbon atoms with sp2 orbital hybridization, forms a delocalized electronic network through out-of-plane π bonds, which accounts for its excellent electrical conductivity. These materials share key characteristics such as a high specific surface area, high electron mobility, and strong surface modifiability, enabling them to significantly enhance electrical, optical, electrochemical, and mechanical responses through sheet or nanotube networks.69–72

The advantages of graphene and carbon nanotube systems in flexible sensing primarily stem from their high sensitivity and multimodal coupling capabilities. Graphene-liquid crystal hybrid systems show potential for electrical-optical coupling, while supramolecular assemblies of single-walled carbon nanotubes provide innovative solutions for near-infrared fluorescence monitoring and molecular recognition interfaces.70,73 During the perioperative period, these materials are especially suited for the highly sensitive and continuous detection of subtle deformations, localized temperature changes, metabolites, or optical indicators. Among two-dimensional materials, the MXene/MOF system has advanced rapidly in recent years. Studies have shown that MXene-MOF composite hydrogels have become a significant platform for electrochemical biosensing and health monitoring. Their advantages include high conductivity, a wide range of surface functional groups, ease of constructing composite networks, and the ability to simultaneously perform electron transport and catalytic amplification functions.74,75 For perioperative applications, this makes it especially suitable for multimodal monitoring of pressure, strain, pH, electrochemical metabolites, and molecules linked to local inflammation. Carbon-based nano-composite hydrogels have also proven to be highly valuable in wound management. Hydrogels reinforced with carbon-based nano-components enhance electrical conductivity and mechanical properties while also providing photothermal, antimicrobial, or imaging capabilities, thereby enabling the integration of “monitoring and intervention”.75,76 This closely aligns with postoperative incision management, infection risk monitoring, and localized treatment. However, carbon-based and two-dimensional nanomaterial systems also face significant challenges in their clinical translation. Issues such as difficulty in uniformly dispersing the layers, interface detachment after prolonged use, and the impact of color and opacity on optical readout—along with concerns regarding the potential biosafety of nanomaterials and unclear metabolic pathways—may limit their adoption in large-area clinical patches and implantable applications.70,74 Therefore, these materials are better suited as high-performance functional components rather than standalone “all-in-one solutions”.

It is important to note that the true advantage of carbon-based and two-dimensional materials in the perioperative setting may not lie in performing all monitoring tasks independently, but in acting as “signal amplifiers” at critical junctures. For example, in high-risk postoperative incisions where low-concentration inflammatory mediators, subtle pressure differentials, or localized electrochemical changes need to be detected, materials such as MXene, carbon nanotubes, and quantum dots can greatly enhance detection sensitivity. Conversely, in scenarios requiring imaging or photothermal interventions, these materials can integrate both monitoring and treatment into a single composite platform.77,78

Hydrogels Primarily Based on Ion-Conductive Nanomaterials for Monitoring

Ion-conductive-dominated monitoring hydrogels consist of a core made up of salt ions, polyelectrolytes, ionic liquids, deep eutectic solvents, or ionic gel networks, and they facilitate signal transduction through ionic migration rather than solely relying on electronic migration. The key advantages of these systems include high stretchability, low modulus, a bio-tissue-like electrical environment, and the ability to maintain continuous responsiveness even under large deformations.78,79 Therefore, such systems are especially well-suited for perioperative monitoring tasks that involve significant mechanical coupling, such as respiration, joint movement, facial expressions, swallowing, and postural pressure.

In recent years, self-powered ionic hydrogels and gel-based triboelectric nanogenerators have greatly expanded the range of applications for this class of materials. Research on self-powered ionic hydrogel sensors, polymer gel triboelectric systems, and gel-based flexible sensing platforms has shown that these materials can function as both sensing and energy-harvesting media, reducing reliance on external power sources and minimizing the need for wiring.80,81 This is especially advantageous for bedside monitoring during the post-anesthesia recovery period and for continuous monitoring in general wards. Ionic conductive systems also offer inherent benefits in gas, humidity, and soft-tactile sensing. Research on wearable gas and humidity hydrogel sensors, as well as bionic ionic skin, suggests that these materials can sensitively detect respiration-related humidity fluctuations, changes in skin surface evaporation, and subtle mechanical contact by monitoring variations in ionic resistance or capacitance.82,83 Therefore, in perioperative respiratory rate monitoring, identification of airway moisture levels, and pressure warning systems for patient positioning, ion-conductive systems often provide greater flexibility than purely electronic systems. The main drawback of these materials is their high sensitivity to environmental factors. Changes in ion concentration, water loss through evaporation, freezing at low temperatures, and contamination by body fluids can all lead to baseline drift. If ionic liquids and deep eutectic solvents are used to improve freeze and desiccation resistance, their biocompatibility and long-term contact safety must be reassessed.84,85 Therefore, ion-conductive hydrogels are better suited for use as wearable mechanical and humidity monitoring modules, or for integration with electronic conductive modules to create hybrid signal systems.

To address the significant challenges of applying ionic hydrogels under low-temperature conditions and water evaporation, Chen et al designed a dual-crosslinked ionic hydrogel that uses Al3+ to crosslink the polymer network via dynamic metal-coordination bonds in a binary solvent system of water and ionic liquid (IL). This approach achieves excellent mechanical properties (~1 MPa, ~600%), transparency (>90%), high ionic conductivity (~12.40 mS cm−1), and strong adhesion, along with excellent freeze resistance and long-term dehydration resistance. These unique properties inspired the development of a dual-responsive sensor capable of simultaneously detecting human motion signals and a wide range of temperature variations (from −30 to 40°C), exhibiting impressive temperature coefficient of resistance (TCR) values (ranging from −0.035 to −0.44 °C−1). Even more promising, the excellent interfacial adhesion between polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) and the ionic hydrogel allows the triboelectric nanogenerator to be assembled in a single-electrode configuration, providing sustainable energy for wearable ionic electronic devices even at sub-zero temperatures. This advancement paves the way for medical monitoring in extreme environments.86

In terms of material trends, ion-conductive systems are evolving from basic “soft conductors” to “smart ionic skins”. On one hand, biomimetic structural design and ionic pathway engineering enable these materials to replicate the skin’s graded responses to light touch, sliding, and stretching. On the other hand, the integration of soft sensing with algorithms allows these systems to perform state recognition, rather than simply outputting raw resistance changes.82,87 This is especially important for identifying agitation during the post-anesthesia recovery period, assessing tolerance for postoperative activity, and triggering bedside nursing alerts.

Hydrogels Primarily Based on Biometric and Stimulus-Responsive Component-Driven Nanomaterials for Monitoring

While the five systems described above primarily focus on the question of “how to stably immobilize analytes and convert physical/chemical changes into signals”, monitoring hydrogels driven by biosensing and stimulus-responsive components address the challenge of “how to selectively identify a specific analyte of clinical interest”. These systems typically incorporate enzymes, antibodies, aptamers, nucleic acid loops, molecularly imprinted sites, glucose-responsive components, pH/redox-responsive groups, or cellular inorganic elements into the hydrogel matrix, allowing them to exhibit predictable responses to specific molecules or microenvironments.88,89

Enzyme immobilization and glucose-responsive systems are among the most well-established branches of this class of materials. Comparative studies on the roles of hydrogels, ionic liquids, and deep eutectic solvents in enzyme-based biosensing have shown that hydrogels are particularly effective in maintaining the enzyme microenvironment and reducing interfacial inactivation. Glucose-responsive systems, such as Con A, further highlight the potential for “sensing-release” coupling in these systems.85,90 For perioperative glucose metabolism management, this suggests that future hydrogel platforms will not only be capable of detecting glucose but may also integrate local drug delivery or closed-loop regulation. Molecularly imprinted and nucleic acid hydrogels represent two distinct approaches for highly selective recognition. Molecularly imprinted hydrogels and nanoparticles can recognize proteins, peptides, and small molecules without depending on natural receptors, providing greater stability and ease of storage. On the other hand, DNA/nucleic acid hydrogels, with their sequence programmability and amplification capabilities, offer unique advantages in multiplex recognition, logic responses, and signal amplification.89,91 Such systems are ideal for the precise detection of perioperative inflammatory factors, stress metabolites, drug-related molecules, and infection markers. Stimulus-responsive hydrogels have also opened up new possibilities for monitoring the local microenvironment. Research on ATP-fluorescent microneedles, stress hormone visualization microneedles, pH-magnetic particle tomography, and optical contact lens sensors collectively shows that hydrogels are no longer confined to measuring surface electrical resistance. Instead, they can leverage fluorescence, color changes, magnetic responses, and lens readings to enable more intuitive and even non-invasive point-of-care analysis.92,93 At the same time, antigens or antibodies can be incorporated into the hydrogel through chemical conjugation or polymerization, or they can bind to macromolecules to provide reversible cross-linking within the hydrogel matrix. Although this concept is still in its early stages, it lays the foundation for the future development of enzyme-responsive hydrogels.94 The most promising applications in the perioperative period include preoperative stress assessment, intraoperative tissue acidification monitoring, and postoperative monitoring of ocular surface and tear-related parameters.

Advances in microneedle and point-of-care diagnostic platforms have brought these materials closer to real-world clinical workflows. Microneedle sensors for point-of-care diagnostics, expandable microneedles, laboratory-on-a-tip systems, and portable fluorescence analysis methods offer a comprehensive workflow for “minimally invasive sampling—on-site reading—rapid diagnosis”.95,96 In the perioperative setting, this means that interstitial fluid data can be obtained without the need for repeated blood draws before surgery, and continuous monitoring can be conducted in the ward or at home after surgery. The challenges associated with such systems primarily lie in three areas: selectivity, stability, and calibrability. As the sensing elements become more complex, it becomes more difficult to control inter-batch variability, sensitivity to temperature and humidity, and long-term drift. Additionally, most highly selective systems require more sophisticated optical or electrochemical readout modules, which increases the overall complexity of the device system.97,98 Therefore, monitoring hydrogels dominated by biometric and stimulus-responsive components will be crucial for enabling continuous monitoring of high-value analytes. However, their clinical translation must progress alongside efforts in standardization, algorithm calibration, and the development of low-cost readout platforms.

Looking ahead, stimulus-response systems are transitioning from “passive sensing” to “active regulation”. Research on engineered living hydrogels, synthetic genetic circuits, and smart composite networks suggests that future hydrogels will not only read signals but may also autonomously adjust drug release, optical readings, or electrical stimulation based on local pH, glucose levels, inflammatory responses, or mechanical conditions.88,99 When this logic is applied to perioperative pain management, local infection control, and tissue protection, it has the potential to create a true closed-loop monitoring system.

Design Principles in Materials Science for Monitoring Hydrogels (Figure 2)

Soft Tissue Matching and Low-Disturbance Interfaces

Monitoring hydrogels are primarily interfacial materials. Their three-dimensional network with high water content gives them an elastic modulus and wettability similar to soft tissue, allowing them to conform to surfaces such as the forehead, chest wall, neck, ocular surface, wound margins, and even organ surfaces. This “soft matching” reduces noise amplification caused by respiratory motion, muscle tension, or skin folds, and minimizes indentations and irritation from prolonged adhesion.22,100 In the perioperative setting, this characteristic directly impacts the reliability of monitoring for forehead EEG, surface ECG, laryngeal vibrations, pressure at the catheter fixation site, and the microenvironment around the incision site.

Natural or biomimetic interfaces do not always imply passive adhesion. By adjusting factors such as crosslinking density, porosity, polyelectrolyte composition, and multi-network ratio, hydrogels can achieve a balance between softness, toughness, and dimensional stability. This is especially important in complex scenarios, such as fragile elderly skin, passive limb positioning after anesthesia, contact with oral and nasal masks, and high-humidity surfaces following intraoperative disinfection. In these cases, the synergistic combination of low modulus and high adhesion is often more clinically significant than simply focusing on high conductivity.101,102 Figure 2Materials-science design requirements for a clinically deployable perioperative hydrogel interface. Electronic Conductivity, Ionic Conductivity, and Mixed Conductivity

Monitoring hydrogels can be broadly classified into electronically conductive, ionically conductive, and hybrid conductive types based on their conduction mechanisms. Electronically conductive hydrogels typically use conductive polymers, carbon-based fillers, or metallic nanostructures to create continuous charge transport pathways, making them ideal for high-fidelity recording of weak bioelectric signals such as ECG, EEG, and EMG. In contrast, ionic conduction closely resembles the electrical transmission mechanisms of natural tissues, offering excellent flexibility and stretchability for strain, pressure, tactile, and humidity sensing. Hybrid conductive systems aim to balance biocompatibility, mechanical toughness, and signal stability.66,103

Perioperative monitoring requires not only “higher conductivity” but also “greater usability”. For instance, chest wall respiration monitoring, pharyngeal vibration recognition, and pressure-related position alerts demand materials that can tolerate large deformations. In contrast, EEG monitoring for anesthetic depth, responses to electrical stimulation, or assessments of neuromuscular recovery depend more on low interfacial impedance and high signal-to-noise ratios. Therefore, material selection should be tailored to the specific monitoring task, rather than focusing solely on a single conductivity metric.2,23

Resistant to Dehydration and Freezing; Self-Healing; Adheres to Wet Surfaces

Once hydrogels are introduced into clinical use, they encounter a highly challenging environment, exposed to disinfectants, sweat, exudates, low temperatures from air conditioning, interference from electrosurgical instruments, and prolonged stretching. To address the limitations of traditional hydrogels—such as water loss, brittleness at low temperatures, and electrical drift following repeated deformation—innovative solutions like binary solvent systems, self-healing networks, dynamic covalent bonds, and dual-network structures have been introduced. These advancements aim to enhance the performance and durability of hydrogels in such demanding conditions.84,104 These strategies are especially crucial for long-term use in operating rooms and recovery rooms, as monitoring quality can degrade quickly if the material shrinks, becomes brittle, or curls at the edges. Perioperative patients are particularly vulnerable to hypothermia, mainly due to low operating room temperatures and impaired thermoregulation following general anesthesia. This increases the risk of postoperative hypothermia, especially during recovery and in the ICU, highlighting the importance of cold resistance.

On the other hand, many high-value monitoring sites during the perioperative period are located at moist interfaces, such as around the mouth and nose, near mucous membranes, wound edges, catheter insertion sites, and around negative pressure drainage areas. Moisture-adhering hydrogels enhance adhesion stability in high-humidity environments through mechanisms such as catechol, Schiff base, topological entanglement, and interface dehydration, while also reducing secondary damage caused by re-adhesion.24,101 Therefore, wet adhesion is not a secondary function but a crucial prerequisite for determining whether the hydrogel can be effectively used in real-world perioperative settings.

Shelf Life, Packaging, and Storage Stability

Pre-use shelf life must be distinguished from in-use dehydration. A perioperative hydrogel patch may be stored for months but used for only hours or days, so packaging must preserve water content, ionic composition, mechanical properties, adhesion, and sensor calibration until the point of care. Stability programs should prespecify storage temperature and humidity, packaging barrier properties, and acceptance criteria for mass loss, swelling, modulus, conductivity, baseline drift, sensitivity, adhesion, sterility, and biocompatibility after aging. Real-time stability remains necessary for definitive shelf-life claims, while accelerated aging can support formulation and packaging selection. Low-water-vapor-transmission pouches, humectants, organohydrogel strategies, ionic liquids, glycerol, and secondary barrier films can reduce water loss, but each additive or package-contact material must be evaluated for its effect on signal performance and tissue safety.40,41,44,84

The Advantages of Hydrogels as Interfaces for Perioperative Monitoring in Anesthesia

Hydrogels are particularly well-suited for perioperative monitoring because they are not just “soft substrates”, but multifunctional interfaces that can simultaneously facilitate adhesion, sampling, detection, and feedback. For example, the microneedle-based stress hormone monitoring platform and the polyphenol-based monitoring hydrogel illustrate that once a material can perform local sampling and molecular responses, it can translate subtle biochemical changes into signals that are readable at the bedside.36,93 At the sensing level, the integration of enzyme-based biosensing systems, ionic liquids or deep eutectic solvents, and implantable bioelectronics enables hydrogels to go beyond simple electrical conduction. This allows them to perform multiple functions, including ion conduction, amplification of enzymatic reactions, and long-term tissue compatibility.85,105

Of particular significance to anesthesiology is the parallel development of local anesthetic hydrogels and microneedle platforms for interstitial fluid, which challenges the traditional separation between “monitoring devices” and “therapeutic delivery systems”. While the former focuses on maintaining local concentration and temporal control, the latter emphasizes minimally invasive, continuous sampling. Together, they create the foundation for a closed-loop management system.48,106 Inflammation-responsive hydrogels and smart wound platforms further highlight that the most clinically important factors in the perioperative period are often not isolated physiological parameters, but rather microenvironmental signals such as acidosis, reactive oxygen species, proteases, and bacterial load. When materials can respond to these changes either structurally or through sensory feedback, they transform from passive coverings into active sensing layers.107,108 Therefore, one of the key strengths of modern hydrogel platforms is their ability to apply the concept of “microenvironment recognition—risk characterization—on-demand release”, originally developed in chronic wound care and pain management, to the perioperative setting. Both chronic wound management and local anesthetic-responsive systems demonstrate that materials can act as a bridge between monitoring and treatment.109,110

However, device engineering is what ultimately determines whether these technologies can be successfully translated into clinical practice. Organic bioelectronics in microphysiological systems have shown that hydrogels can be integrated with electrodes, cell models, and microfluidics. Meanwhile, 3D-printed biosensors offer the ability to customize structure, thickness, and localized functionality—an essential capability for complex surfaces, incision edges, and mucosal interfaces.55,111 In the fields of wound monitoring and wearable electronics, hydrogel dressings have evolved from merely “maintaining moisture” to “simultaneously monitoring and treating”. Wearable electronic systems for wound care place even greater emphasis on the need for flexible wiring, thin structures, and continuous data readouts. These advancements can be directly applied to managing postoperative incisions, skin around catheters, and pressure points.112,113 At the same time, research on flexible optical biosensors and implantable regional anesthesia devices suggests that readout methods for perioperative hydrogel platforms do not need to be limited to electrical signals. Optical, color-changing, fluorescent, and even imaging-compatible readouts could provide safer and more intuitive monitoring options in specific scenarios.114,115

An Exploration of the Application of Monitoring Hydrogels Throughout the Perioperative Period of Anesthesia (Figure 3)

Preoperative Phase: Baseline Assessment and Minimally Invasive Screening

The preoperative phase generally refers to the period from the decision to proceed with surgery and the anesthesiological assessment, through to the patient’s entry into the operating room, just before the initiation of anesthesia induction. The preoperative assessment and interventions carried out by the anesthesiologist are critical in establishing the baseline for perioperative safety. The key task during this phase is to create a continuous baseline that accurately reflects the patient’s true condition. Flexible piezoelectric materials and responsive monitoring hydrogels indicate that subtle surface deformations, movement patterns, and dynamic changes in the local microenvironment can be recorded without increasing invasiveness. This approach provides a more stable foundation for anesthesia risk stratification compared to single outpatient measurements.116,117

On the other hand, perioperative pain management and local anesthesia strategies often require careful advance planning. Gels and gelation systems designed for local anesthetic delivery offer the material foundation for personalized preoperative planning, while hydrogel microfluidic chips integrated with conductive electrodes demonstrate the feasibility of combining the monitoring of bodily fluid parameters with local interface monitoring.118,119 If the monitoring time window is extended from minutes to hours or even days, data processing methods become crucial. Machine learning-based soft electronic systems can extract more stable trends from long-term raw signals. Additionally, a review of safety-oriented soft electronics highlights the fundamental importance of wearability, thermal safety, and skin compatibility for long-term monitoring.102,120

For recording baseline ECG, respiration, and sleep-like respiratory patterns prior to surgery, flexible optical waveguides and PEDOT:PSS hydrogels provide two key technical approaches. The former is ideal for environments with low electromagnetic interference, while the latter is better suited for scenarios that require low-impedance bioelectrical coupling. Both approaches share the same goal: to acquire repeatable, high-quality baseline signals under conditions as close to natural as possible.59,121 Preoperative assessment should also involve evaluating the skin and local tissue condition, as prolonged immobilization, positional pressure, and catheter contact during surgery often worsen preexisting risks. Research on conductive dressings and flexible strain sensors suggests that hydrogels can simultaneously detect mechanical stimuli and assess local tissue tolerance. This offers a potential means to predict the risk of pressure injuries and removal-related injuries before anesthesia is administered.122,123 Furthermore, 3D-printed hydrogel dressings and multifunctional chronic wound management systems show that personalized manufacturing can be applied not only to treatment but also to monitoring. By customizing interfaces prior to surgery based on factors such as surgical positioning, incision location, and the patient’s skin condition, the reliability of perioperative monitoring can be significantly enhanced.56,124

Hydrogel monitoring systems offer excellent skin adhesion, flexibility, biocompatibility, and the potential for multifunctional integration in preoperative anesthesia applications. They can enhance the quality of physiological signal acquisition and patient comfort, while providing a new technological pathway for continuous, precise, and personalized anesthesia assessment. However, their clinical application still faces challenges such as limited long-term stability, insufficient sterilizability, difficulties in balancing adhesion with skin safety, poor resistance to environmental interference, and constraints on standardization and large-scale translation. Intraoperative Phase: Monitoring of Vital Signs, the Surgical Site, and Surgical Instruments

The unique challenge of intraoperative monitoring is that the materials must perform reliably under complex conditions, including sterilization, stretching, temperature fluctuations, and sustained pressure. Research on flexible bioelectronics and bioactive materials suggests that an ideal interface must not only be flexible but also resistant to damage and interference, while maintaining consistent output in humid environments.131,132

For anesthesiologists, one of the most easily overlooked yet clinically significant factors during surgery is local pressure and surface deformation. The hydrogel-flexible electronics integrated platform and cellulose pressure/strain sensors show that risks at pressure points, around fixation straps, and near bony prominences can indeed be measured. In the past, the lack of suitable interface materials was the limiting factor in monitoring these risks.29,133 Compared to conventional patches, hydrogel interfaces with biomimetic microstructures provide superior performance in slip resistance, anti-displacement, and localized adhesion. Meanwhile, 3D-printed conductive hydrogels offer the ability to adjust thickness, texture, and sensitivity based on the monitoring location, which is especially crucial for complex patient positions, such as prone, lateral, and lithotomy positions.54,134 Intracellular signals are often weak and susceptible to complex noise, making the signal amplification capabilities of the materials themselves crucial. Polymer-mediated signal amplification mechanisms, along with the development of MXene/MOF composite hydrogels, have significantly improved the sensitivity of electrochemical and impedance measurements. These advancements provide a solid material foundation for the real-time detection of local pH, electrolytes, and metabolic molecules.74,135 From a molecular perspective, research on nucleic acid hydrogel biosensors and medical imaging highlights that perioperative monitoring does not have to be confined to traditional vital signs. If local inflammation, infection, bleeding, or drug release can be translated into visual or molecular readouts, monitoring can evolve from merely “seeing numbers” to “observing pathological processes”.136,137

Particularly in relation to tissue reactions at wound sites and around medical devices, conductive polymer wound systems and pH-sensitive hydrogel imaging show the potential for integrated monitoring, interpretation, and intervention. For high-risk incisions, deep drainage channels, and the edges of transplanted tissues, this type of “visualized microenvironment” could offer a more accurate assessment of actual risk than surface-level parameters alone.138,139 Furthermore, the development of microscale architectures, ranging from functional microneedles to bio-integrated wearable systems, demonstrates that intraoperative monitoring does not necessarily require large, external devices. Meanwhile, smart diabetic wound dressings highlight that continuous monitoring systems can fully integrate multi-parameter channels and local-release modules within a compact area.140,141 For local analgesia and surgical site management, carbon-based nanocomposite hydrogels and local anesthetic microsphere systems indicate that intraoperative material platforms can simultaneously offer local protection, signal detection, and drug delivery. This integrated approach holds significant potential for future applications in nerve blocks, incision infiltration, and superficial tissue protection.142,143

However, the true value of perioperative care lies not in “measuring as much as possible”, but in prioritizing the identification of indicators that are directly relevant to clinical decision-making. Both studies on wound biomarkers and reviews of wound monitoring materials highlight the importance of identifying, from the wide range of measurable indicators, those core variables that genuinely impact analgesic adjustments, the timing of dressing changes, and the early detection of complications.144,145 From a systems perspective, the combination of soft sensors powered by machine learning and bedside microneedle monitoring is approaching the prototype stage for “automatic intraoperative alerts for local abnormalities”. If threshold settings, alarm logic, and workflows can be integrated with anesthetic practices, the intraoperative hydrogel platform has the potential to transition from a research prototype to a practical, usable tool. Body Fluid Sampling and Microneedle Monitoring: From Minimally Invasive Testing to Metabolic Assessment

While flexible skin-worn platforms primarily focus on the continuous collection of “surface physiological signals”, microneedles and biofluid interfaces expand monitoring to the metabolic and molecular levels. Transparent wearable electronics and optical contact lens biosensors show that, in addition to the skin, the ocular surface and tears can also act as minimally invasive monitoring ports during the perioperative period.156,157 Before advancing to clinical trials, it is crucial to carefully consider manufacturing and deposition processes. Factors such as device consistency, scalability, adaptability to real-world samples, and intellectual property strategies will all directly influence whether the technology can successfully transition from the laboratory to the hospital.34,53

In respiratory management and PACU monitoring, factors such as humidity, sweat, and the skin’s surface microclimate are particularly important. Wearable sensors made from 2D-printed and 3D-printed materials, along with gas/humidity hydrogel sensors, have shown the capability to continuously monitor respiratory-related humidity fluctuations, the sweat environment, and the condition of the sensor-skin interface.69,83 On the other hand, multi-network hydrogels and integrated biomaterial systems highlight that what is truly needed in the perioperative setting is not a material with the strongest single function, but rather a comprehensive system that is mechanically stable, capable of transmitting signals, carrying drugs, and maintaining long-term functionality. Therefore, tensile strength, resistance to dehydration, and multi-network synergy are more critical than individual performance metrics.158,159 Research on peptide-loaded hydrogels for monitoring various bodily fluids, such as sweat and tears, suggests that future perioperative fluid monitoring platforms should not focus solely on glucose. Proteins, peptides, inflammatory factors, stress hormones, and even molecules related to local tissue repair may serve as early indicators of abnormalities, often appearing before those detected through traditional blood draws.159,160

Of course, the limitations of specific interfaces must be fully understood. Research on corneal abrasions reminds us that the safety of ocular surface materials cannot be judged solely by their “softness”. Meanwhile, predictive modeling for non-invasive glucose monitoring highlights the importance of establishing a clear correlation between local readings and clinical events before biofluid platforms can be introduced into anesthetic settings.47,161

Accuracy Requirements for Perioperative Glycemic Monitoring

For proposed perioperative glucose sensing, performance claims should be benchmarked against established accuracy frameworks rather than reported only as sensitivity or limit of detection. ISO 15197:2013 applies to capillary self-monitoring systems rather than interstitial hydrogel sensors, but it provides a familiar point-accuracy comparator: at least 95% of results should fall within ±15 mg/dL of the reference value when glucose is below 100 mg/dL and within ±15% when glucose is 100 mg/dL or higher.162 For continuous interstitial glucose monitoring, CLSI POCT05 is more directly relevant and addresses point accuracy, trend accuracy, threshold alerts, system stability and reliability, calibration, measurement traceability, shelf life, and labeling.163 Accordingly, a hydrogel-based perioperative glucose monitor should be evaluated against a traceable reference method across the clinically relevant range and should report lag behavior, rate-of-change accuracy, mean absolute relative difference or equivalent error metrics, and clinically meaningful error-grid analyses where appropriate. Validation should also test perioperative stressors such as vasopressor use, hypoperfusion, edema, temperature change, electrosurgical interference, and rapid glucose excursions. Until such validation is available, hydrogel glucose systems should be regarded as investigational adjuncts rather than replacements for accepted blood-glucose measurement.

Specific Perioperative Scenarios: Airway Management, Ocular Care, Analgesia, and Oxygen Supply and Demand

From a practical application standpoint, the first hydrogel applications are unlikely to be “all-purpose monitoring platforms”, but rather specialized interfaces designed to address specific challenges. Research on conductive hydrogel designs for electronic skin and wearable sensing strategies suggests that once the specific application—whether it’s airway stabilization, skin protection, or postoperative recovery monitoring—is clearly defined, the material structure and device logic can be significantly optimized.57,103 Perioperative materials must balance electrical conductivity, moisture retention, and the management of oxygen supply and demand. Research on high-performance conductive hydrogels and oxygen biosensing technology suggests that, under conditions of continuous adhesion or local occlusion, achieving a balance between electrical conductivity, water content, breathability, and local tissue metabolism is crucial for determining clinical applicability.60,164

Superficial tissue sites, such as postoperative incisions and donor sites, are the most natural application areas for hydrogels. Hydrogels designed for skin wounds and electrospun hydrogel fibers have already reached a high level of maturity in terms of moisture retention, exudate absorption, and local protection. This makes it relatively straightforward to enhance them into “dressings with monitoring capabilities”.165,166 Long-duration surgeries place higher demands on the toughness and fatigue resistance of materials. Research on dual-network hydrogels and composite conductive electronic skin has demonstrated that synergistic network interactions can balance flexibility with strength and toughness, making them better suited for highly dynamic areas such as the head and face, joints, and the thoracic and abdominal walls.167,168 Low-cost bedside monitoring is equally crucial. Research on using personal blood glucose meters to measure non-traditional biomarkers, along with glucose-responsive smart hydrogels, provides valuable insights for developing “compact, rapidly deployable” metabolic monitoring tools for the perioperative period. These tools are especially beneficial for managing blood glucose levels in high-risk patients.169,170 In terms of posture and pressure injury risk management, MXene pressure sensors provide a highly sensitive solution for monitoring surface forces. Regarding analgesic strategies, the liposome-bupivacaine-hydrogel system demonstrates that local analgesia can be designed to be more long-lasting, stable, and responsive to local conditions.75,171 For deep or semi-implanted applications, multifunctional implantable hydrogels and strain-responsive conductive polymer hydrogels show that monitoring is not limited to surface-mounted sensors. In the future, semi-implanted interfaces may offer more direct, localized information near nerves, drainage sites, superficial cavities, or residual spaces in surgical areas.58,172

It is important to note that local drug delivery systems must undergo rigorous in vivo validation. Studies evaluating the release of chloroprocaine using a piglet model, along with a systematic review of skin-like medical electronics, indicate that the true challenge of translation lies not in conceptual innovation, but in establishing a reproducible correlation between material behavior, drug exposure, and clinical outcomes.173,174

Monitoring of Postoperative Wounds, Infections, and Recovery Quality

The postoperative period represents the most promising window for hydrogels to demonstrate real clinical value, as this phase involves well-defined local targets and requires longer-term monitoring than traditional methods. Synthetic genetic circuits in living materials and bionic tactile sensors suggest that future postoperative monitoring will go beyond reading static values, potentially identifying more complex dynamic patterns.87,88

Nanofiber composites and PDA-embedded polymer networks are now capable of simultaneously processing physiological signals from the body surface, information from bodily fluids, and responses to various stimuli. This enables the integrated monitoring of parameters such as temperature, humidity, exudate, and minor displacement around surgical incisions.98,175 To effectively apply these prototypes in clinical settings, they must be integrated with standard assays and physiological models. Research on cellulose biosensors and organ-on-a-chip/organ-in-a-chip biosensors shows that a bridge is gradually being established between in vitro models, calibration systems, and bedside sample readouts.30,176

For long-term wear, self-healing and mechanical recovery capabilities are crucial. Research on self-healing hydrogels for flexible devices, along with reviews of hybrid hydrogels for pain management, highlight that a material’s ability to retain functionality after repeated stretching, compression, and minor damage is essential for the success of postoperative care and home follow-up.104,177 Self-powering and interference resistance are crucial factors in reducing the burden on patients. Both triboelectric platforms and binary solvent-assisted composite hydrogels used for health monitoring suggest that future postoperative monitoring should minimize the reliance on wires, external power sources, and frequent replacements, thus encouraging consistent use of the devices by patients.84,178 In the management of localized infections and wound healing, conductive polymer hydrogels and silver nanoparticle-based antimicrobial dressings represent two key approaches: first, using bioelectricity and a conductive environment to promote tissue repair, and second, integrating antimicrobial functionality into the monitoring interface. This combination allows for the integration of “risk detection” and “risk mitigation” within a single system.76,179 Furthermore, in-situ 4D printing and sensor-equipped lignin/dopamine composite hydrogels show that complex or irregular postoperative wounds do not necessarily require standardized flat dressings. Handheld or on-site molded hydrogel interfaces offer the potential for better-fitting coverage and more immediate functional deployment at the bedside.180,181

At the same time, hydrogel-molded microneedles and swellable microneedles show that postoperative follow-up can evolve from a single check during dressing changes to minimally invasive, intermittent, or continuous sampling. When combined with wireless transmission or portable readout devices, this capability is expected to bridge the monitoring gap between in-hospital care and outpatient recovery periods.95,182 Table Molecular Recognition, Artificial Intelligence, and Systems Integration

Artificial intelligence becomes clinically relevant only after the underlying hydrogel sensor has demonstrated stable and interpretable signal acquisition. For model development, reports should describe participant and data-source characteristics, total sample size and event counts, missing-data handling, preprocessing, class imbalance, train/tuning/evaluation splits, calibration, uncertainty, and subgroup performance. There is no universal minimum dataset size that can guarantee clinical validity; the required sample should instead be justified by the intended task, model complexity, outcome frequency, learning-curve behavior, and the need for an independent evaluation dataset. Repeated measurements from the same patient should not be allowed to leak across training and evaluation sets. Geographic, temporal, or multi-center external validation is particularly important because hydrogel signals may vary with device batch, placement site, skin condition, temperature, motion, and perioperative physiology. TRIPOD+AI and PROBAST+AI provide useful contemporary frameworks for transparent reporting and assessment of prediction-model quality and bias.189,190

Explainability should also be linked to the intended clinical decision rather than treated as a decorative model output. Clinicians need to understand which sensor channels or time windows drive an alert, how uncertainty is represented, when the model is outside its validated operating range, and what action the output is intended to support. Feature attribution or saliency maps alone are insufficient if they do not clarify failure modes, calibration, and clinically meaningful consequences. Human-factor testing should therefore evaluate whether anesthesia and nursing staff can correctly interpret algorithmic outputs under time pressure.

The regulatory status of an AI-enabled hydrogel system depends on intended use and system architecture. Under the International Medical Device Regulators Forum framework, software that performs a medical purpose independently of hardware may qualify as Software as a Medical Device (SaMD); software embedded within a hardware medical device may instead be regulated as part of that device. Clinical evaluation should establish a valid clinical association, analytical or technical validation, and clinical validation, with evidence commensurate with risk.191 For future perioperative hydrogel platforms, this implies controlled software versions, documented data provenance, cybersecurity and change-management plans, prospective clinical validation, and post-deployment performance monitoring rather than one-time algorithm development.

Future Perspectives and Translational Priorities

Although hydrogels show substantial potential for perioperative applications, translation should proceed through scenario-specific products rather than a universal platform. Priority indications should have a clear unmet interface need, a measurable comparator, and a realistic clinical workflow. Examples include low-impedance soft electrodes for prolonged ECG/EEG/EMG acquisition, pressure-warning interfaces for complex surgical positioning, minimally invasive metabolic patches for selected high-risk patients, and smart postoperative dressings that combine wound-status monitoring with local intervention.192,193 Across these indications, adhesion in wet environments, mucosal and skin safety, removal-related injury, sterilization, storage stability, calibration, and long-term mechanical integrity are at least as important as peak laboratory sensitivity.194–199 Specialty-specific requirements will also differ: cardiac surgery may prioritize hemostasis and tissue protection, whereas enhanced-recovery pathways may prioritize local analgesia, mobility, wound surveillance, and transdermal pain-management strategies.200–202

Although the composition of monitoring hydrogels is becoming increasingly diverse, several common challenges persist. First, the conflict between material complexity and manufacturability is becoming more pronounced. As hydrogels incorporate more fillers and functions, they become more susceptible to batch-to-batch variations, phase separation, mechanical brittleness, and signal drift. While 3D printing and hybrid manufacturing enhance customization capabilities, they also impose stricter requirements on rheological properties, gelation rates, and uniformity of filler dispersion. Second, many high-performance formulations remain validated only under laboratory conditions, lacking systematic evaluation under real-world scenarios such as dehydration, exposure to sweat, exudate, disinfectants, sterilization, and long-term adhesion. Relevant studies suggest that before monitoring hydrogels can be used clinically, challenges like dehydration resistance, fatigue, long-term adhesion safety, storage stability, and sensor calibration must be addressed. This implies that future material optimization should not only focus on “new ingredients” but also on how these ingredients maintain functionality under real-world conditions. Third, monitoring hydrogels are evolving towards an integrated “sensing–assessment–intervention” approach, requiring composition design to shift from simple readout to closed-loop responses. Research in wound care has already shown that future materials could automatically release antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, or tissue-repairing components upon detecting signs of acidosis, inflammation, or infection. For perioperative applications, such closed-loop materials are equally promising, as they can serve as proactive intervention points for postoperative incision care, early warning systems for pressure ulcers, or localized pain management.

Ultimately, it is validation that determines success or failure. Both the comprehensive review of conductive hydrogels and the guidelines for evaluating the degradation of PEG hydrogels emphasize that factors such as sterilization, batch consistency, dehydration and swelling, body fluid contamination, long-term aging, and in vitro-to-in vivo correlation studies must be systematically addressed before clinical use. Only by making the transition from “good performance” to “interpretable, reproducible, and regulatable compliance” can hydrogels become a standard component in perioperative anesthesia monitoring.45,66 In the future, research on hydrogels will concentrate on material optimization and the integration of new components. By incorporating materials with properties such as self-healing, hydrolysis resistance, and fatigue resistance, the stability and reliability of hydrogels in clinical applications will be significantly improved. Simultaneously, advances in nanotechnology, conductive polymers, and smart materials will expand the functionality of hydrogels, enabling the integration of sensing, drug delivery, and antimicrobial capabilities. Future developments will emphasize integrated, multifunctional monitoring systems. For instance, by combining microfluidic technology, flexible electronics, and artificial intelligence, hydrogel monitoring systems capable of real-time data analysis and feedback will be created. These systems will provide more precise monitoring and treatment throughout the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative phases, ultimately improving patient safety and comfort.

Conclusion

The current evidence supports hydrogels as enabling interfaces for perioperative monitoring, but not yet as clinically validated replacements for established anesthesia monitors. Their strongest rationale is the ability to add continuous local information - bioelectrical coupling, pressure and deformation, wound microenvironment, or minimally invasive biochemical sampling - where rigid or intermittent systems are limited. At present, however, the evidence is derived mainly from preclinical studies and adjacent clinical fields, and dedicated prospective perioperative patient studies remain a major unmet requirement.

A credible translational pathway therefore requires four linked steps: first, stable and biocompatible material/device engineering with defined shelf life and sterilization compatibility; second, analytical benchmarking against accepted reference methods and clinically meaningful accuracy criteria; third, prospective validation in representative perioperative populations with human-factor assessment; and fourth, regulatory-ready integration of software, connectivity, and any AI-based interpretation. With these requirements met, hydrogel platforms could extend perioperative monitoring from intermittent device-centered measurements toward multimodal, continuous, patient-centered sensing without overstating the maturity of the current evidence.
