On the ergodicity of anti-symmetric skew products with singularities and its applications
Przemysław Berk, Krzysztof Frączek, Frank Trujillo

Abstract
. We introduce a novel method for proving the ergodicity of skew products of interval exchange transformations (IETs) with piecewise smooth cocycles having singularities at the ends of exchanged intervals. This approach is inspired by Borel-Cantelli-type arguments in [ 6 ] . The key innovation of our method lies in its applicability to singularities beyond the logarithmic type, whereas previous techniques were restricted to logarithmic singularities. Our approach is particularly effective for proving the ergodicity of skew products for symmetric IETs and antisymmetric cocycles. Moreover, its most significant advantage is the ability to study the equidistribution of error terms in the spectral decomposition of Birkhoff integrals for locally Hamiltonian flows on compact surfaces, applicable not only when all saddles are perfect (harmonic) but also in the case of some non-perfect saddles.

1.
Introduction

Let
T=T_{\pi,\lambda}:[0,1)\to[0,1)
be an interval exchange transformation (IET) given by an irreducible permutation
\pi=(\pi_{0},\pi_{1})
(
\pi_{0},\pi_{1}:\mathcal{A}\to\{1,\ldots,d\}
are bijections describing the position of intervals, labeled by elements of
\mathcal{A}
, before and after the translation) and the vector
\lambda=(\lambda_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
collecting the lengths of exchanged intervals. Denote by
I_{\alpha}=[l_{\alpha},r_{\alpha})
,
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
the intervals exchanged by
T
. We mainly deal with symmetric IETs, i.e.
\pi_{0}(\alpha)+\pi_{1}(\alpha)=d+1
for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
. If
\mathcal{I}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is the reflection across
1/2
, i.e.
\mathcal{I}x=1-x
, then
\mathcal{I}\circ T=T^{-1}\circ\mathcal{I}
and this map acts on every interval
I_{\alpha}
as the reflection across the center of
I_{\alpha}
denoted by
m_{\alpha}
.

The main objective of the article is to develop novel methods to prove the ergodicity of skew products
T_{f}:[0,1)\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
of the form
T_{f}(x,r)=(Tx,r+f(x))
, where
f:[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
is a
C^{1}
-map on the interior of all exchanged intervals and having singularities at their ends. Suppose that

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{$\theta:[x_{0},+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ is an increasing $C^{1}$-map such that $\int_{x_{0}}^{+\infty}\frac{dx}{x\theta(x)}=+\infty$,}\\
\text{ and the map $\tau:(0,x_{0}^{-1}]\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ given by $\tau(s)=\frac{s^{2}}{\theta^{\prime}(1/s)}$ is increasing.}\quad\end{aligned}

Notice that we can always extend the functions
\theta
and
\tau
so that
x_{0}=1
while preserving the properties above.

In this work, we consider maps with singularities that behave like
s\mapsto\theta(1/s)
. More precisely, we deal with the space
\Upsilon_{\theta}(\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}I_{\alpha})
of functions
f:[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
which are
C^{1}
on the interior of all exchanged intervals
I_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
and

(1.2)

Z_{\theta}(f):=\max_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\Big\{\sup_{x\in(l_{\alpha},m_{\alpha}]}|f^{\prime}(x)\tau(x-l_{\alpha})|,\sup_{x\in[m_{\alpha},r_{\alpha})}|f^{\prime}(x)\tau(r_{\alpha}-x)|\Big\}<+\infty.

For any
f\in\Upsilon_{\theta}(\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}I_{\alpha})
let

(1.3)

z_{\theta}(f):=\max_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\Big\{\inf_{x\in(l_{\alpha},m_{\alpha}]}|f^{\prime}(x)\tau(x-l_{\alpha})|,\inf_{x\in[m_{\alpha},r_{\alpha})}|f^{\prime}(x)\tau(r_{\alpha}-x)|\Big\}.

Roughly speaking, the positivity of
z_{\theta}(f)
expresses non-triviality of at least one singularity of the type
s\mapsto\theta(1/s)
.

In this article, we focus on the ergodic properties of skew products
T_{f}
when the function
f
is additionally
anti-symmetric
, which means that
f\circ T^{-1}\circ\mathcal{I}=-f
, so is anti-symmetric with respect to the central reflection of any interval
I_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
. Moreover, we say that
f
is
piecewise monotonic
if
f
it is increasing when restricted to the interior of each interval
I_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
or is always decreasing.

The main basic result of the paper, based on ideas developed in
[
6
]
for rotations, is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\theta:[x_{0},+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, with
x_{0}>0
, be a slowly varying increasing
C^{1}
-map such that
\int_{x_{0}}^{+\infty}\frac{dx}{x\theta(x)}=+\infty
and the map

x\mapsto x^{2}\theta^{\prime}(x)
is increasing. Then for a.e. symmetric IET
T
if

(1)

f\in\Upsilon_{\theta}(\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}I_{\alpha})
with
z_{\theta}(f)>0
;

(2)

f
is anti-symmetric;

(3)

f
is piecewise monotonic,

then the skew product
T_{f}
is ergodic.

This theorem and its versions are directly applicable to studying of the behavior of error terms in the spectral decomposition of Birkhoff integrals for locally Hamiltonian flows on compact surfaces when all saddles are perfect. The aforementioned spectral decomposition has recently been studied in
[
12
]
(for non-degenerate saddles) and in
[
8
]
(more generally, when all saddles are perfect). We present all the details in Section
2
. One of the main results of
[
12
]
is to prove the equidistribution of the error term when almost every flow has no saddle loops, which is a consequence of the ergodicity of a certain skew extension of the flow. In turn, the ergodicity of the extension follows from the ergodicity of a certain skew product
T_{f}
for
f
having logarithmic singularities of symmetric type.

One of the goals of this article is to show that the error term is equidistributed also when the locally Hamiltonian flow has saddle loops.
In this case, the methods developed so far fail because the symmetry condition is broken. To overcome this problem, we use a version of Theorem
1.1
for
\theta=\log
to prove the equidistribution of the error term in the case of locally Hamiltonian flows (with perfect saddles) of hyper-elliptic type, see Theorem
2.4
, which constitutes another main result of the paper.

However, Theorem
1.1
also has applications beyond the use of the logarithmic function. In Appendix
A
, we construct a family of locally Hamiltonian flows with a new type of degenerate and imperfect saddles whose antisymmetric skew product extensions are ergodic. This is an entirely new class of ergodic skew products unknown even in the context of extensions of rotations.

2.
Applications to locally Hamiltonian flows on compact surfaces

Let
M
be a smooth, compact, connected, orientable surface of genus
g\geq 1
.
We focus on smooth flows
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}=(\psi_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
on
M
preserving a
smooth
area form
\omega
, i.e., such that for any (orientable)
local coordinates
(x,y)
, we have
\omega=V(x,y)dx\wedge dy
with
V
positive and smooth.
Then for (orientable) local coordinates
(x,y)
such that
\omega=V(x,y)dx\wedge dy
, the flow
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
is (locally) a solution to the Hamiltonian equation

\frac{dx}{dt}=\frac{\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}(x,y)}{V(x,y)},\qquad\frac{dy}{dt}=-\frac{\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}(x,y)}{V(x,y)},

for a smooth real-valued locally defined function
H
, or equivalently
\frac{dz}{dt}=-2\iota\frac{\frac{\partial H}{\partial\overline{z}}(z,\overline{z})}{V(z,\overline{z})}
in complex variables. The flows
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
are usually called
locally Hamiltonian flows
or
multivalued Hamiltonian flows
.

For any smooth observable
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
, we are interested in understanding the asymptotic behavior of the so-called
Birkhoff integrals

\int_{0}^{T}f(\psi_{t}x)dt\quad\text{as}\quad T\to+\infty.

We always assume that all fixed points of the flow
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
are isolated, so the set
\mathrm{Fix}(\psi_{\mathbb{R}})
of fixed points of
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
is finite and, if
g\geq 2
, then it is non-empty. As
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
is area-preserving, fixed points are either centers, simple saddles, or multi-saddles (saddles with
2k
prongs with
k\geq 2
). In this article, we will mainly consider
perfect
(also known as
harmonic
) saddles. A fixed point
\sigma\in\mathrm{Fix}(\psi_{\mathbb{R}})
is a (perfect) saddle of multiplicity
m=m_{\sigma}\geq 2
if there exists a chart
(x,y)
(called
a singular chart
) in a neighborhood
U_{\sigma}
of
\sigma
such that
d\mu=V(x,y)dx\wedge dy
and
H(x,y)=\Im(x+\iota y)^{m}
(
(0,0)
are coordinates of
\sigma
). Then the corresponding local Hamiltonian equation in
U_{\sigma}
is of the form

\frac{dz}{dt}=\frac{m\overline{z}^{m-1}}{V(z,\overline{z})}
. We denote the
set of perfect saddles
of
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
by
\mathrm{PSd}(\psi_{\mathbb{R}})
.

Note that perfect saddles are, in a sense, a generalization of non-degenerate saddles. Indeed, suppose that
(0,0)
is a non-degenerate saddle for the local Hamiltonian equation
\frac{dz}{dt}=-2\iota\frac{\frac{\partial H}{\partial\overline{z}}(z,\overline{z})}{V(z,\overline{z})}
, i.e. the Hessian of
H
at
(0,0)
is non-zero. Then, by Morse Lemma, after a smooth change of coordinates, we have
H(z,\bar{z})=\Im z^{2}
, so the saddle is perfect of multiplicity
2
.

However, in the current article, we also go beyond the case of perfect saddles. The techniques we propose are also proving effective for flows with certain imperfect saddles, see Appendix
A
, which seems to be a major breakthrough in understanding the behavior of Birkhoff integrals for locally Hamiltonian flows.

A
saddle connection
of
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
is an orbit of
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
running from a saddle to a saddle. A
saddle loop
is a saddle connection
joining the same saddle.
We will deal only with flows for which all their saddle connections are loops.
We denote by
\mathrm{SL}
the set of all saddle loops of the flow.
Recall that if every fixed point of
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
is isolated, then
M
splits into a finite number of
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
-invariant surfaces (with boundary) so that any such surface is either a
minimal component
(i.e., every orbit, except fixed points and saddle loops, is dense in the component) or is a periodic component (filled by periodic orbits, fixed points and saddle loops). The boundary of each component consists of saddle loops and fixed points. Since the dynamics of flows on periodic components is not interesting, we only focus on the study of minimal components.

2.1.
Historical overview

The first important step towards a full understanding of the asymptotic behavior of Birkhoff integrals was the phenomenon of deviation spectrum and its relation with Lyapunov exponents observed by Zorich
[
31
]
while studying deviations of Birkhoff sums for piecewise constant observables for almost all interval exchange translations.
Inspired by this, Kontsevich
[
15
]
and Zorich
[
16
]
formulated their famous conjecture: for almost every
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
with non-degenerate fixed points
there exist values
1=\nu_{1}>\nu_{2}>\ldots>\nu_{g}>\nu_{g+1}=0
so that
for every smooth map
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
there exists
1\leq i\leq g+1
such that

\limsup_{T\to+\infty}\frac{\log\left|\int_{0}^{T}f(\psi_{t}(x))\,dt\right|}{\log T}=\nu_{i},\quad\text{ for almost every }x\in M.

They related the values
\nu_{i}
, for
1\leq i\leq g
, with the positive Lyapunov exponents of the Kontsevich-Zorich cocycle.
Fundamental steps in the verification of the conjecture have been done in the seminal paper by Forni
[
7
]
, introducing Forni’s distributions
D_{i}(f)
, for
1\leq i\leq g
, and later developed by Bufetov
[
4
]
, constructing Bufetov’s cocycles
u_{i}(t,x),
for
1\leq i\leq g
, for a family of observables
f
. More precisely,
for almost every locally Hamiltonian flow
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
without saddle connections (
M
is the only one minimal component) and for every observable
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
from a weighted Sobolev space, we have

(2.1)

\int_{0}^{T}f(\psi_{t}(x))dt=\sum_{i=1}^{g}{D_{i}}(f)u_{i}(T,x)+err(f,T,x),

where

(2.2)

\displaystyle\limsup_{T\to+\infty}\frac{\log\big|u_{i}(T,x)\big|}{\log T}=\nu_{i},\ \quad\lim_{T\to+\infty}\frac{\log|err(f,T,x)|}{\log T}=0,\quad\text{ for a.e. }x\in M.

However, the final results on the deviation spectrum for Birkhoff integrals in full generality have only recently been obtained in
[
12
]
for non-degenerate saddles and in
[
8
]
for arbitrary perfect saddles. Both articles go beyond the setting considered so far, when the flow is minimal on
M
and the observables
f
vanishes at fixed points, and exploit and develop the techniques introduced by Marmi-Moussa-Yoccoz in
[
18
,
19
]
.
In particular, in
[
12
]
the authors proved (
2.1
) with (
2.2
) for a.e. non-degenerate locally Hamiltonian flow
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
restricted to any of its minimal components and any smooth observable
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
, fully solving the Kontsevich-Zorich conjecture and giving a somewhat deeper analysis of the asymptotics of the error term is provided.

The transition to the setting in which perfect saddles of any multiplicity appear gives rise to new invariant distributions and new terms in the deviational spectrum, which we now intend to describe.

2.2.
Deviation spectrum in full generality

Suppose
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
is a locally Hamiltonian flow on
M
with isolated fixed points such that all saddles are perfect and all saddle connections are loops. Let
M^{\prime}
be its minimal component. For any saddle
\sigma\in\mathrm{PSd}(\psi_{\mathbb{R}})
, any
0\leq l<2m_{\sigma}
and
0\leq k\leq m_{\sigma}-2
, let us consider after
[
9
]
the distribution
\mathfrak{C}^{k}_{\sigma,l}:C^{k}(M)\to\mathbb{C}
given by

(2.3)

\mathfrak{C}^{k}_{\sigma,l}(f):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i\leq k\end{subarray}}\theta_{\sigma}^{l(2i-k)}\binom{k}{i}\mathfrak{B}\big(\tfrac{(m_{\sigma}-1)-i}{m_{\sigma}},\tfrac{(m_{\sigma}-1)-k+i}{m_{\sigma}}\big)\frac{\partial^{k}(f\cdot V)}{\partial z^{i}\partial\overline{z}^{k-i}}(0,0),

where
\theta_{\sigma}
is the principal
2m_{\sigma}
-th root of unity and

\mathfrak{B}(x,y)=\frac{\pi e^{\iota\frac{\pi}{2}(y-x)}}{2^{x+y-2}}\frac{\Gamma(x+y-1)}{\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y)}
, for any
x,y>0
, where we adopt the convention
\Gamma(0)=1
. By
[
9
]
, the distributions
\mathfrak{C}^{k}_{\sigma,l}
are
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
-invariant. Moreover, by definition,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}^{k}_{\sigma,l+m_{\sigma}}=(-1)^{k}\mathfrak{C}^{k}_{\sigma,l},\text{ for }0\leq l<m_{\sigma},\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{0\leq l<m_{\sigma}}\theta_{\sigma}^{(k-2j)l}\mathfrak{C}^{k}_{\sigma,l}=0,\text{ if }k<j<m_{\sigma}.

A chain of adjacent saddle loops.

Recall that the boundary of the minimal component
M^{\prime}
consists of saddle loops of the flow
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
. Suppose that
\sigma\in M^{\prime}\cap\mathrm{PSd}(\psi_{\mathbb{R}})
is a saddle on the boundary of
M^{\prime}
, that is,
\sigma
emanates at least one saddle loop. The neighborhood
U_{\sigma}
(in local singular coordinates associated with
\sigma
) splits into
2m_{\sigma}
invariant angular sectors

U_{\sigma,l}:=\{z\in U_{\sigma}:\operatorname{Arg}z\in[l\pi/m_{\sigma},(l+1)\pi/m_{\sigma}]\},\quad\text{ for }0\leq l<2m_{\sigma}.

Denote

\mathcal{L}^{\sigma}:=\{0\leq l<2m_{\sigma}:U_{\sigma,l}\subseteq M^{\prime}\}

and let us consider an equivalence relation
\sim
on
\mathcal{L}^{\sigma}
as follows:

l\sim l^{\prime}
if the angular sectors
U_{\sigma,l}
and
U_{\sigma,l^{\prime}}
are connected through a chain of adjacent saddle loops emanating from the saddle
\sigma
. Two saddle loops are adjacent when they touch the same angular sector. For every equivalence class
\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{\sigma}_{\sim}:=\mathcal{L}^{\sigma}/\sim
(an example of such a class is shown in Figure
1
), let

\mathfrak{C}^{k}_{\sigma,\ell}(f):=\sum_{l\in\ell}\mathfrak{C}^{k}_{\sigma,l}(f).

The following result is a more subtle version of Theorem 1.1 from
[
8
]
, obtained by applying techniques from
[
8
]
together with Theorem 5.6 of
[
9
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

For almost every locally Hamiltonian flow
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
on a compact surface
M
, the following holds. Let
M^{\prime}
be a minimal component of
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
of genus
g\geq 1
and denote
m:=\max\{m_{\sigma}:\sigma\in\mathrm{Fix}(\psi_{\mathbb{R}})\cap M^{\prime}\}
. There exist Lyapunov exponents

1:=\nu_{1}>\nu_{2}>\cdots>\nu_{g}>0,

invariant distributions
{D_{i}}:C^{m}(M)\to\mathbb{R}
,
1\leq i\leq g
, smooth cocycles
u_{i}(T,x):\mathbb{R}\times M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
1\leq i\leq g
, and smooth cocycles
c^{k}_{\sigma,\ell}(T,x):\mathbb{R}\times M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, for all
\sigma\in\mathrm{Fix}(\psi_{\mathbb{R}})\cap M^{\prime}
,
0\leq k\leq m_{\sigma}-2
and
\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{\sigma}_{\sim}
, such that for every
f\in C^{m}(M)
,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}f(\psi_{t}(x))dt&=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Fix}(\psi_{\mathbb{R}})\cap M^{\prime}}\sum_{0\leq k<m_{\sigma}-2}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{\sigma}_{\sim}}\mathfrak{C}^{k}_{\sigma,\ell}(f)c^{k}_{\sigma,\ell}(T,x)\\
&\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{g}{D_{i}}(f)u_{i}(T,x)+err(f,T,x),\end{split}

with the following conditions on the growth of main terms satisfied for a.e.
x\in M^{\prime}
:

(2.5)

\displaystyle\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log{|c^{k}_{\sigma,\ell}(T,x)|}}{\log T}=\frac{(m_{\sigma}-2)-k}{m_{\sigma}}\quad\text{for }\sigma\in\mathrm{Fix}(\psi_{\mathbb{R}})\cap M^{\prime},\ 0\leq k<m_{\sigma}-2,\ \ell\in\mathcal{L}^{\sigma}_{\sim};

(2.6)

\displaystyle\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log{|u_{i}(T,x)|}}{\log T}=\nu_{i}\quad\text{for }\ 1\leq i\leq g;

(2.7)

\displaystyle\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log|err(f,T,x)|}{\log T}\leq 0.

An outline of the proof for this result can be found in Section
9
.

Suppose that
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
is minimal and has only non-degenerate saddles, i.e.,
m=2
. Then the first part of the deviation spectrum in (
2.4
) disappears,
\mathcal{L}^{\sigma}_{\sim}=\{0,1,2,3\}
and
\mathfrak{C}^{0}_{\sigma,l}(f)=2f(\sigma)
.
In this case,
[
12
]
provides a more in-depth analysis of the error term, which has subpolynomial growth. More precisely, if
f(\sigma)\neq 0
for some
\sigma\in\mathrm{Fix}(\psi_{\mathbb{R}})
then for a.e.
x\in M
the error term
err(f,T,x)
is equidistributed on
\mathbb{R}
as
T\to+\infty
, that is,

\lim_{T\to+\infty}\frac{|\{t\in[0,T]:err(f,T,x)\in I\}|}{|\{t\in[0,T]:err(f,T,x)\in J\}|}=\frac{|I|}{|J|},

for any pair of bounded intervals
I,J\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. On the other hand, if
f(\sigma)=0
for all
\sigma\in\mathrm{Fix}(\psi_{\mathbb{R}})
then the error term
err(f,T,x)
is uniformly bounded.

In fact, in the first scenario, the equidistribution of the error term follows from the equidistribution of a.e. Birkhoff integral
\int_{0}^{T}f(\psi_{t}(x))\,dt
whenever
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
is of class
C^{2}
,
D_{i}(f)=0
for
1\leq i\leq g
, and
f(\sigma)\neq 0
, for some fixed point
\sigma
. In turn, for such an observable
f
, the equidistribution of the Birkhoff integrals follows directly, applying Hopf’s ergodic theorem, from the ergodicity of the skew product flow
\psi^{f}_{\mathbb{R}}=(\psi^{f}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
acting on
M\times\mathbb{R}
by

\psi^{f}_{t}(x,r)=\left(\psi_{t}(x),r+\int_{0}^{t}f(\psi_{s}(x))\,ds\right).

Recall that the ergodicity of skew product flows as above can be deduced from the ergodicity of certain discrete skew products over IETs. Indeed, if
I\subseteq M
is a transversal curve identified with an interval, then the first return map of the flow
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
to
I
is an interval exchange transformation
T:I\to I
for the so-called
standard parameterizations
. Moreover, the first return map of the flow
\psi^{f}_{\mathbb{R}}
to
I\times\mathbb{R}
is the skew product map
T_{\varphi_{f}}:I\times\mathbb{R}\to I\times\mathbb{R}
given by

(2.8)

T_{\varphi_{f}}(x,r)=(T(x),r+\varphi_{f}(x)),\quad\text{ with }\varphi_{f}(x)=\int_{0}^{\tau(x)}f(\psi_{t}(x))\,dt,

where
\tau:I\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}\cup\{+\infty\}
is the first return time map of the flow
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
to
I
. Thus, the ergodicity of the flow
\psi^{f}_{\mathbb{R}}
is equivalent to the ergodicity of the skew product map
T_{\varphi_{f}}
.

Remark 2.2
.

Recall that a subset
A
of locally Hamiltonian flows has full Lebesgue measure (in the sense of Katok’s fundamental class, see
[
13
]
) if and only if a full measure set of IETs appears in the
base of special flows representations of flows in
A
. For a more detailed explanation of the space of locally Hamiltonian flows, the partition of the surface into invariant components, and the measure class, we refer the reader to
[
21
]
.

Let us mention that the proof of ergodicity in
[
12
]
relies on the fact that the map (cocycle)
\varphi_{f}:I\to\mathbb{R}
has logarithmic singularities of
symmetric type
at the ends of the intervals exchanged by
T
. A similar argument has been recently used in
[
3
]
to conclude the ergodicity of a certain symmetric class of cocycles
with symmetric logarithmic singularities. This symmetry condition is a consequence of the absence of saddle loops. Notice that if there are saddle loops, then we are limited to studying the flow on the minimal components, and the symmetry condition is naturally broken. We, however, expect that the symmetry condition for logarithmic singularities is not relevant to the ergodicity of the skew product and, consequently, to the equidistribution of the error term in the deviation spectrum.
Our working conjecture is as follows:

Conjecture 2.3
.

Let
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
be a locally Hamiltonian flow on a compact surface
M
for which Theorem
2.1
holds. Let
M^{\prime}
be one of its minimal components of genus
g\geq 1
and denote
m:=\max\{m_{\sigma}:\sigma\in\mathrm{Fix}(\psi_{\mathbb{R}})\cap M^{\prime}\}
. For every
f\in C^{m}(M)
such that

•

D_{i}(f)=0
for all
1\leq i\leq g
,

•

\mathfrak{C}^{k}_{\sigma,\ell}(f)=0
for all
\sigma\in\mathrm{Fix}(\psi_{\mathbb{R}})\cap M^{\prime}
,
0\leq k<m_{\sigma}-2
and
\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{\sigma}_{\sim}
, and

•

\mathfrak{C}^{m_{\sigma}-2}_{\sigma,\ell}(f)\neq 0
for some
\sigma\in\mathrm{Fix}(\psi_{\mathbb{R}})\cap M^{\prime}
and
\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{\sigma}_{\sim}
,

the skew product flow
\psi^{f}_{\mathbb{R}}
is ergodic.

Note that this yields the equidistribution of the error term in (
2.4
) whenever
\mathfrak{C}^{m_{\sigma}-2}_{\sigma,\ell}(f)\neq 0
for some
\sigma\in\mathrm{Fix}(\psi_{\mathbb{R}})\cap M^{\prime}
and
\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{\sigma}_{\sim}
.

2.3.
Main results and technical novelties

In the current article, we positively verify Conjecture
2.3
in two important special cases. First, see Theorem
9.1
, we consider the case when the flow
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
has no saddle loops, that is, the flow is minimal over the entire surface
M
. Then, in view of
[
8
, Theorem 5.6]
applied to
r=0
, the map
\varphi_{f}
has logarithmic singularities. By the absence of saddle connections,
\varphi_{f}
satisfies the symmetry condition. Then, we can use directly the ergodicity criterion developed in
[
12
, Theorem 8.1]
. In conclusion, this case does not require the development of new tools.

Second, we consider the case when the flow
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
has exactly
one saddle point, and we restrict ourselves to a minimal component
M^{\prime}
. We additionally assume that
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
on
M^{\prime}
is of hyper-elliptic type, that is, there is a transversal curve
I\subseteq M^{\prime}
such that the IET
T:I\to I
given by the first return map of
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
to
I
is symmetric. In this context, the main result is the following.

Theorem 2.4
.

For almost every locally Hamiltonian flow
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
on a compact surface
M
having exactly one perfect saddle, the following holds. Let
M^{\prime}
be a minimal component of
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
of genus
g\geq 1
and assume that
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
on
M^{\prime}
is of hyper-elliptic type. Then, for every
f\in C^{m_{\sigma}}(M)
such that

•

D_{i}(f)=0
for all
1\leq i\leq g
,

•

\mathfrak{C}^{k}_{\sigma,\ell}(f)=0
for all
0\leq k<m_{\sigma}-2
and
\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{\sigma}_{\sim}
, and

•

\mathfrak{C}^{m_{\sigma}-2}_{\sigma,\ell}(f)\neq 0
for some
\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{\sigma}_{\sim}
,

the skew product flow
\psi^{f}_{\mathbb{R}}
is ergodic.

In particular, for every
f\in C^{m_{\sigma}}(M)
such that
\mathfrak{C}^{m_{\sigma}-2}_{\sigma,\ell}(f)\neq 0
for some
\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{\sigma}_{\sim}
, we have

\int_{0}^{T}f(\psi_{t}(x))dt=\sum_{0\leq k<m_{\sigma}-2}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}^{\sigma}_{\sim}}\mathfrak{C}^{k}_{\sigma,\ell}(f)c^{k}_{\sigma,\ell}(T,x)+\sum_{i=1}^{g}{D_{i}}(f)u_{i}(T,x)+err(f,T,x)

with the cocycles
u_{i}
and
c^{k}_{\sigma,\ell}
that grow polynomially according to rules given by (
2.5
) and (
2.6
), and the error term
err(f,T,x)
that oscillates sub-polynomially and which is equidistributed on
\mathbb{R}
as
T\to+\infty
.

Arguments coming from
[
9
]
show that
\varphi_{f}
has logarithmic singularities, but due to the existence of saddle loops, the symmetry condition is broken, and the techniques developed in
[
12
]
do not work. In this case, we take advantage of the fact that the flow has only one saddle point and is hyperelliptic, so the corresponding interval transformation is symmetric. This, in turn, provides an opportunity to decompose
\varphi_{f}
into a symmetric and anti-symmetric part. In fact, only its anti-symmetric part is responsible for the ergodicity of the skew product map
T_{\varphi_{f}}
, and finally also for the ergodicity of the skew product flow
\psi^{f}_{\mathbb{R}}
.

The main novelty of this article is the development of completely new techniques for proving the ergodicity of skew products based on the anti-symmetry of cocycles having singularities. The developed tools are mainly inspired by ideas from
[
6
]
, in which Fayad and Lemańczyk prove the ergodicity of skew products of a.a. rotations with cocycles having one logarithmic singularity using Borel-Cantelli-type results, and by ideas from
[
2
]
, in which anti-symmetry was used to prove the ergodicity of skew products of interval exchange transformations with piecewise constant cocycles. We should emphasize that the power of the developed techniques is also revealed by their applicability to anti-symmetric skew products with non-logarithmic singularities. In Appendix
A
, we present a family of locally Hamiltonian flows with a new type of degenerate and imperfect saddles whose antisymmetric skew product extensions are ergodic. This is an entirely new class of ergodic skew products unknown even in the context of extensions of rotations.

3.
Interval exchange transformations and translation surfaces

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a
d
-element alphabet. An interval exchange transformation
T=(\pi,\lambda)
is a piecewise isometry of the interval
I=[0,|I|)
determined by a pair
\pi=(\pi_{0},\pi_{1})
(to which we refer to as a
permutation
) of bijections
\pi_{\varepsilon}:\mathcal{A}\to\{1,\ldots,d\}
, for
\varepsilon=0,1
, and a vector
\lambda=(\lambda_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{\mathcal{A}}
. For any
\lambda=(\lambda_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{\mathcal{A}}
, let

|\lambda|=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\lambda_{\alpha},\quad I=\left[0,|\lambda|\right),

and define

I_{\alpha}=[l_{\alpha},r_{\alpha}),\qquad\text{ where }\qquad l_{\alpha}=\sum_{\pi_{0}(\beta)<\pi_{0}(\alpha)}\lambda_{\beta},\;\;\;r_{\alpha}=\sum_{\pi_{0}(\beta)\leq\pi_{0}(\alpha)}\lambda_{\beta}.

We denote the midpoint of the exchanged interval
I_{\alpha}
by
m_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}(l_{\alpha}+r_{\alpha})
. With these notations,
|I_{\alpha}|=\lambda_{\alpha}
.

Denote by
\Omega_{\pi}
the matrix

[\Omega_{\alpha\,\beta}]_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}}
given by

(3.1)

\Omega_{\alpha\,\beta}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}+1&\text{ if
}\pi_{1}(\alpha)>\pi_{1}(\beta)\text{ and
}\pi_{0}(\alpha)<\pi_{0}(\beta),\\
-1&\text{ if }\pi_{1}(\alpha)<\pi_{1}(\beta)\text{ and
}\pi_{0}(\alpha)>\pi_{0}(\beta),\\
0&\text{ in all other cases.}\end{array}\right.

Then, the IET
T
associated with
(\pi,\lambda)
is given by
Tx=x+w_{\alpha}
, for any
x\in I_{\alpha}
, where
w=\Omega_{\pi}\lambda
. The vector
w
is called the
translation vector
of the IET.

In this work, we consider only
irreducible
permutations, that is, pairs of bijections
(\pi_{0},\pi_{1})
for which
\pi_{1}\circ\pi_{0}^{-1}\{1,\dots,k\}=\{1,\ldots,k\}
implies
k=d
. We denote by
S^{\mathcal{A}}
the set of all irreducible permutations.

As already mentioned, we will often deal in this article with
symmetric
permutations. More precisely, a permutation
\pi
on
\mathcal{A}
is called
symmetric
if
\pi_{1}(\alpha)=d+1-\pi_{0}(\alpha)
, for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, that is, any IET
T
with combinatorial data given by
\pi
exchanges intervals symmetrically.

Let
p:\{0,1,\ldots,d,d+1\}\to\{0,1,\ldots,d,d+1\}
be the
extended permutation
given by

p(j)=\left\{\begin{matrix}\pi_{1}\circ\pi^{-1}_{0}(j)&\text{ if }&1\leq j\leq d\\
j&\text{ if }&j=0,d+1.\end{matrix}\right.

Following Veech (see
[
26
]
), denote by
\sigma=\sigma_{\pi}
the
corresponding permutation on
\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
,

\sigma(j)=p^{-1}(p(j)+1)-1\text{ for }0\leq j\leq d.

Denote by
\Sigma(\pi)
the set
of orbits for the permutation
\sigma
. Let
\Sigma_{0}(\pi)
stand
for the subset of orbits that do not contain zero. The set
\Sigma(\pi)
corresponds to singular points of translation flows, which in turn to the saddle points of the locally Hamiltonian flows. The cardinality of
\Sigma(\pi)
relates to the matrix
\Omega_{\pi}
by the formula

\#\Sigma(\pi)=\dim(\textup{Ker}(\Omega_{\pi}))+1.

For every
\mathcal{O}\in\Sigma(\pi)
, we denote by

\mathcal{A}^{-}_{\mathcal{O}}=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{A},\ \pi_{0}(\alpha)\in\mathcal{O}\},\qquad\mathcal{A}^{+}_{\mathcal{O}}=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{A},\ \pi_{0}(\alpha)-1\in\mathcal{O}\}.

On the space of all interval exchange transformations, there exists a classical notion of induction on the space of interval exchange transformations called
Rauzy-Veech induction
(or
Rauzy-Veech algorithm
), see
[
20
,
26
]
. Namely, we define

\hat{R}:S^{\mathcal{A}}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}_{>0}\to S^{\mathcal{A}}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}_{>0},\ \hat{R}(\pi,\lambda):=(\pi^{(1)},\lambda^{(1)}),

where
\tilde{T}:=(\pi^{(1)},\lambda^{(1)})
is an IET obtained by inducing
T
on
[0,|\lambda|-\min\{\lambda_{\pi_{0}^{-1}(d)},\lambda_{\pi_{1}^{-1}(d)}\})
. Keane in
[
14
]
proved that for Lebesgue-a.e. parameter
\lambda
, the map
\hat{R}
is defined indefinitely. We denote
\hat{R}^{n}(\pi,\lambda)=(\pi^{(n)},\lambda^{(n)})
(also
(\pi,\lambda)=(\pi^{(0)},\lambda^{(0)})
). We also denote by
I^{(n)}_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
the intervals exchanged by
\hat{R}^{n}(\pi,\lambda)
and by
I^{(n)}
its domain. For
j=0,1
, if
|I_{\pi_{j}^{-1}(d)}^{(n)}|>|I_{\pi_{1-j}^{-1}(d)}^{(n)}|
, then the symbol
\pi_{j}^{-1}(d)
is called the
n
-th
winner
of the Rauzy-Veech algorithm, while
\pi_{1-j}^{-1}(d)
is the
loser
.

Finally, we say that
(\pi,\lambda)
is of
top
(forward) type if
\lambda_{\pi_{0}^{-1}(d)}>\lambda_{\pi_{1}^{-1}(d)}
and of
bottom
(forward) type if
\lambda_{\pi_{0}^{-1}(d)}<\lambda_{\pi_{1}^{-1}(d)}
and we describe a single action of
\hat{R}
as of
top
and
bottom
type, in respective cases. If
\lambda_{\pi_{0}^{-1}(d)}=\lambda_{\pi_{1}^{-1}(d)}
, then
\hat{R}
is not defined. It is easy to see that
\hat{R}
is continuous on the set
\{(\pi,\lambda)\in S^{\mathcal{A}}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}_{>0}\mid\lambda_{\pi_{0}^{-1}(d)}\neq\lambda_{\pi_{1}^{-1}(d)}\}
.

One of the crucial objects describing the action of
\hat{R}
is the so-called
Rauzy-Veech matrix
. More precisely, it is a
d\times d
matrix
B(1):=B(1)(\pi,\lambda)
such that

\lambda^{(1)}B(1)=\lambda.

Then for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\lambda^{(n)}B(n)=\lambda,

where

(3.2)

B(n):=B(1)(\pi^{(n-1)},\lambda^{(n-1)})\cdot\ldots\cdot B(1)(\pi^{(0)},\lambda^{(0)}).

In other words, the matrices
B(\cdot)
describe how the lengths of intervals change under the Rauzy-Veech induction. More precisely, for every
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}
, the coefficient
B_{\alpha\beta}(n)
gives the number of visits of interval
I^{(n)}_{\alpha}
in
I_{\beta}
before its first return to
I^{(n)}
.

Another related object is the Rokhlin tower decomposition. Namely, for every
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
we denote by
q^{(n)}_{\alpha}
the first return time of the interval
I^{(n)}_{\alpha}
to
I^{(n)}
via
T
. Then, the whole interval
I
can be decomposed into the disjoint union of towers of intervals:

I=\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\bigsqcup_{i=0}^{q^{(n)}_{\alpha}-1}T^{i}I^{(n)}_{\alpha}.

In view of the interpretation of entries of
B(\cdot)
as numbers of visits, we have that

q^{(n)}_{\alpha}=\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{A}}B_{\alpha\beta}(n).

As we are going to deal with the cocycles, it is useful to introduce the natural action of
\hat{R}
on the functions defined over the intervals. If
f:I\to\mathbb{R}
, then

S(n)(f):I^{(n)}\to\mathbb{R},\ \text{with}\ S(n)(f)(x):=S_{q^{(n)}_{\alpha}}(f)(x)\ \text{for every}\ x\in I^{(n)}_{\alpha},

where
S_{k}(\cdot)(\cdot)
denotes the
k
-th Birkhoff sum, see (
6.1
).

It is easy to see that
\hat{R}
is not reversible. Indeed, it is typically a 2-to-1 map, with one pre-image coming from the top type induction and the other from the bottom type. However, there is the more natural setting of
translation surfaces
, where the extended induction is actually reversible, which we now introduce.

Take
(\pi,\lambda)\in S^{\mathcal{A}}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}_{>0}
and consider a vector
\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}
, such that for every
1\leq j<d
we have

(3.3)

\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\mid\pi_{0}(\alpha)\leq j}\tau_{\alpha}>0\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\mid\pi_{1}(\alpha)\leq j}\tau_{\alpha}<0.

Then in
\mathbb{C}
we consider a polygon made by connecting the vertices given by
\sum_{i=1}^{j}(\lambda_{\pi_{0}^{-1}(i)}+i\tau_{\pi_{0}^{-1}(i)})
and
\sum_{i=1}^{j}(\lambda_{\pi_{1}^{-1}(i)}+i\tau_{\pi_{1}^{-1}(i)})
for
1\leq j\leq d
and the point 0. Due to (
3.3
), this polygon is made of two broken lines, one
upper
, whose all non-extreme vertices are above the real axis, on the other,
lower
, whose all non-extreme vertices are below the real axis. Note that both broken lines consist of
d
intervals and that every segment in the upper broken line has its equivalent in the lower line obtained by translation. By gluing all of such pairs, using said translation, we obtain the
translation surface

M=(\pi,\lambda,\tau)
. We denote by
\Sigma=\Sigma(\pi,\lambda,\tau)
the set of vertices of
(\pi,\lambda,\tau)
which form the singularities of
M
. There exists a natural atlas of charts on
M\setminus\Sigma
such that each transition map is a translation.

The translation structure on
(\pi,\lambda,\tau)
allows to define the global notion of direction derived from
\mathbb{C}
and, in particular, the directional flows on
(\pi,\lambda,\tau)
. We will consider two such flows:
the vertical translation flow

\phi=(\phi_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
which moves points upwards with unit speed and
the horizontal translation flow

\psi=(\psi_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
which moves the points rightwards with unit speed. Neither of these flows is defined in
\Sigma
. We often refer to the points in
\Sigma
as
singularities
of
\phi
and
\psi
. It is easy to see that the directional flows preserve two-dimensional Lebesgue measure. Moreover, by considering a horizontal interval
I\subseteq M
starting at
0
of length
|\lambda|
as a Poincaré section for
\phi
, we get that the first return map to
I
is an IET
T=(\pi,\lambda)
. We can use this map to get a different representation of the surface
(\pi,\lambda,\tau)
. Namely, for every
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
we consider a
zippered rectangle

D_{\alpha}:=\bigcup_{t\in[0,h_{\alpha})}\phi_{t}(I_{\alpha})
, where
h_{\alpha}
is the first (positive) return time of
I_{\alpha}
to
I
via
\phi
. We refer to
h_{\alpha}
as the
height of the zippered rectangle

D_{\alpha}
. With these notations, it is easy to show that the
heights vector

h=(h_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
satisfies
h=-\Omega_{\pi}\tau
. We refer to the vertical segment
E_{\alpha}:=\{\phi_{t}(l_{\alpha})\mid t\in[0,h_{\alpha})\}
as the
edge of the zippered rectangle

D_{\alpha}
.

The set
\Sigma(\pi)
defined earlier corresponds to the set
\Sigma(\pi,\lambda,\tau)
in the way that the permutation
\sigma
describes the gluing of sides in the polygonal construction around a fixed singularity. In particular, if
\phi
has only one singularity, then
\Sigma(\pi)
has only one element. Moreover, the genus of the corresponding surface is related to
\Sigma(\pi)
by the formula

(3.4)

g=\frac{d+1-\#\Sigma(\pi)}{2}.

As already mentioned, we can define an extension of
\hat{R}
on the space of translation surfaces so that it is invertible. More precisely, we define

R(\pi,\lambda,\tau):=(\pi^{(1)},\lambda^{(1)},\tau^{(1)}),\ \text{where}\ (\pi^{(1)},\lambda^{(1)})=\hat{R}(\pi,\lambda)\ \text{and}\ \tau^{(1)}:=\tau B(1)^{-1}.

One can check that
\tau^{(1)}
still satisfies (
3.3
), hence the surface
(\pi^{(1)},\lambda^{(1)},\tau^{(1)})
is well defined. We refer to
R
as the
2-dimensional Rauzy-Veech induction
. However, we simply write "Rauzy-Veech induction" if it does not cause any confusion. As before, we define
(\pi^{(n)},\lambda^{(n)},\tau^{(n)}):=R^{n}(\pi,\lambda,\tau)
. Moreover, one can show that the vector of heights in the zippered rectangle representation
h^{(n)}=(h^{(n)})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
is given by the formula
h^{(n)}=B(n)h
.

For
(\pi,\lambda,\tau)
there exists only one pre-image
R^{-1}(\pi,\lambda,\tau):=(\pi^{(-1)},\lambda^{(-1)},\tau^{(-1)})
for which
\tau^{(-1)}
satisfies (
3.3
). We say that
(\pi,\lambda,\tau)
is of
backward top
type if
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\tau_{\alpha}<0
and of
backward bottom
type if
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\tau_{\alpha}>0
. The backward induction
R^{-1}
is not defined if
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\tau_{\alpha}=0
. It is worth underlining that if
(\pi,\lambda,\tau)
is of backward top (bottom) type, then
(\pi^{(-1)},\lambda^{(-1)},\tau^{(-1)})
is of top (bottom) type.

The set
S^{\mathcal{A}}
can be divided into a disjoint union of sets, minimal and invariant under projected action of
\hat{R}
called
Rauzy graphs
.

One of the main reasons to consider Rauzy-Veech induction is that it defines a renormalization scheme on the space of translation surfaces as well as on the space of interval exchange transformations. To be more precise, we fix a Rauzy graph
\mathcal{G}\subseteq S^{\mathcal{A}}
and consider a unit simplex
\Lambda^{\mathcal{A}}:=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}_{>0}\mid|\lambda|=1\}
. Moreover we consider
\Theta_{\pi}:=\{\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}\mid\tau\ \text{satisfies \eqref{eq: deftau}}\}
. We define a
Rauzy-Veech renormalization

\mathcal{R}
on
\mathcal{M}:=\bigsqcup_{\pi\in\mathcal{G}}\{\pi\}\times\Lambda^{\mathcal{A}}\times\Theta_{\pi}
given by

\mathcal{R}(\pi,\lambda,\tau):=(\pi^{(1)},\frac{\lambda^{(1)}}{|\lambda^{(1)}|},|\lambda^{(1)}|\cdot\tau^{(1)}).

Let
\mathcal{M}^{1}:=\{(\pi,\lambda,\tau)\in\mathcal{M}\,|\,-\langle\lambda,\Omega_{\pi}\tau\rangle=1\}
recalling that
-\langle\lambda,\Omega_{\pi}\tau\rangle=\langle\lambda,h\rangle
is the area of the surface
(\pi,\lambda,\tau)
. It was proven by Masur
[
17
]
and Veech
[
26
]
, that there exists an infinite measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{M}^{1}
, which is equivalent to the product of counting and Lebesgue measures, with respect to which
\mathcal{R}
is measure-preserving, recurrent and ergodic. This already allows us to deduce a large number of results that hold for typical translation surface or interval exchange transformation. However, to talk about the hyperbolic properties of the cocycles, we want to work with finite measures. For this reason, Zorich in
[
30
]
introduced an acceleration of the Rauzy-Veech induction, obtained by inducing to an appropriately chosen set, such that the induced measure is finite. Namely, we consider a subset
\mathcal{X}
of triples
(\pi,\lambda,\tau)\in\mathcal{M}^{1}
such that
(\pi,\lambda,\tau)
is of forward top type and backward bottom type or it is of forward bottom type and backward top type. Then
\mu(\mathcal{X})<\infty
. The map obtained by inducing
\mathcal{R}
on
\mathcal{X}
is called
Zorich induction
, and we denote it by
\mathcal{Z}
. The cocycle obtained by inducing
B
, as defined in (
3.2
), on
\mathcal{X}
is called
Kontsevich-Zorich cocycle
(KZ-cocycle), and we denote it by
Z
. We also denote
m:=\mu|_{\mathcal{X}}
. For every
(\pi,\lambda,\tau)\in\mathcal{X}
, if
n_{k}:=n_{k}(\pi,\lambda,\tau)
is the
k
-th return time of
(\pi,\lambda,\tau)
to
\mathcal{X}
via
\mathcal{R}
, where
k\geq 1
, then
\mathcal{Z}(\pi,\lambda,\tau)=\mathcal{R}^{n_{k}}(\pi,\lambda,\tau)
and we denote

Z(k)=Z(k)(\pi,\lambda,\tau):=B(n_{k}-n_{k-1})(\pi^{(n_{k-1})},\lambda^{(n_{k-1})},\tau^{(n_{k-1})}),

as well as
Q(k):=Z(k)\cdot\ldots\cdot Z(1)
.
Moreover, since
\mu
is infinite and
m
is finite, the function of the first return time
n_{1}(\cdot)
is unbounded. Since
\mathcal{R}
is ergodic, then so is
\mathcal{Z}
.

One of the most important features of the Kontsevich-Zorich cocycle is its log-integrability, that is

(3.5)

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\log\|Z(1)\|\,dm<\infty.

Recall that the log-integrability is inherited by induced cocycles, i.e., accelerating the induction by considering returning to smaller subsets of
\mathcal{X}
does not destroy this property.

Later in the article, we will use many notions defined for the Rauzy-Veech induction and then used for the Kontsevich-Zorich induction. However, for simplicity of the notation, we will often write
(\cdot)^{(k)}
instead of
(\cdot)^{(n_{k})}
whenever it does not cause confusion.

For more information on interval exchange transformations and translation surfaces, we refer to
[
27
,
28
]
and
[
29
]
.

4.
Horizontal spacing

In this section, we prove a crucial property of interval exchange transformations, namely that levels of each Rokhlin tower obtained via the Rauzy-Veech algorithm are roughly evenly distributed inside an interval
I=[0,|I|)
. This is partially inspired by the proof of the
d+2
-gaps theorem obtained by Taha in
[
23
]
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
T=(\pi,\lambda)
and let
\tilde{\tau}\in\Theta_{\pi}
be rationally independent. There exists
\epsilon:=\epsilon(\tilde{\tau})>0
and
C:=C(\tilde{\tau},\epsilon)>0
such that for every
\tau\in\Theta_{\pi}
, if
\mathcal{R}^{n}(\pi,\lambda,\tau)\in\{\pi\}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}_{>0}\times B_{\Theta_{\pi}}(\tilde{\tau},\epsilon)
1
1

1

B_{\Theta_{\pi}}(\tilde{\tau},\epsilon)
is the ball in
\Theta_{\pi}
(on
\Theta_{\pi}
we consider standard supremum metric) of radius
\epsilon
centered at
\tilde{\tau}
.
, then for every
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
we have that any interval
{\mathcal{J}}\subseteq I\setminus\bigsqcup_{j=0}^{q_{\alpha}^{(n)}-1}T^{j}I_{\alpha}^{(n)}
satisfies

|{\mathcal{J}}|<C\cdot\max_{\beta\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{\alpha\}}|I_{\beta}^{(n)}|

and

\max\big\{\min_{0\leq i<q^{(n)}_{\alpha}}\operatorname{dist}(0,T^{i}(I^{(n)}_{\alpha})),\min_{0\leq i<q^{(n)}_{\alpha}}\operatorname{dist}(|I|,T^{i}(I^{(n)}_{\alpha}))\big\}<C\cdot\max_{\beta\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{\alpha\}}|I_{\beta}^{(n)}|.

A natural approach to studying the spacing between intervals is to consider them inside the corresponding surface and then study the orbit of the horizontal translation flow on the translation surface given by
(\pi,\lambda,\tau)
. Hence, we first prove the following reduction to the regime of translation surfaces.

Lemma 4.2
.

Fix
\pi\in S^{\mathcal{A}}
and let
\tilde{\tau}\in\Theta_{\pi}
be a rationally independent vector. There exists
\epsilon:=\epsilon(\tilde{\tau})>0
and
C:=C(\tilde{\tau},\epsilon)>0
such that for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}_{>0}
, any
\tau\in B_{\Theta_{\pi}}(\tilde{\tau},\epsilon)
and any
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
the following holds.
Consider the translation surface
(\pi,\lambda,\tau)
with associated zippered rectangles
\{D_{\beta}\}_{\beta\in\mathcal{A}}
of heights
(h_{\beta})_{\beta\in\mathcal{A}}
and vertical (resp. horizontal) flow
\phi_{t}
(resp.
\psi_{t}
). Then, for any vertical segment
J
of length
h_{\alpha}
starting at
I_{\alpha}
and for any point
x\in J
, the orbit segment given by the first return of
x
to
J
by the horizontal flow crosses at most
C
times the edges of
\{D_{\beta}\}_{\beta\neq\alpha}
.

Proof.

Take
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}_{>0}
and consider the surface
(\pi,\lambda,\tilde{\tau})
. Due to the rational independence of
\tilde{\tau}
, this surface has no horizontal saddle connections. Fix
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
and
x_{0}\in I_{\alpha}
. Let
J:=\bigcup_{t\in[0,h_{\alpha})}\phi_{t}(x_{0})
be a vertical segment inside the zippered rectangle
D_{\alpha}=\bigcup_{t\in[0,h_{\alpha})}\phi_{t}(I_{\alpha})
. Since
J
is a transversal interval for the horizontal flow
\psi
, we may consider a first return map
S:J\to J
via
\psi
. Note that the endpoints of
J
lie on the outgoing separatrix of the singularity at
0
, hence, due to
[
29
, Corollary 5.5]

and the fact that
(\pi,\lambda,\tilde{\tau})
has no horizontal connections,
S
is an IET of
d=\#\mathcal{A}
intervals.

The general strategy of the proof is the following. The lengths of the intervals exchanged by
S
vary continuously depending on
\tau
, without changing the combinatorial data of
S
, as long as the order of the first hitting points of the outcoming and incoming separatrices is kept. Moreover, as long as the perturbation of the parameter
\tau
is small enough, the number and order of zippered rectangles visited by the points from
J
via the horizontal flow do not change. Since there are only finitely many exchanged intervals, by taking

From now on, for simplicity we assume that

\tilde{\tau}_{\alpha}>0,\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\tau}_{\beta}<0,\quad\text{ for every }\beta\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{\alpha\},

|J|=h_{\alpha}=-(\Omega_{\pi}\tilde{\tau})_{\alpha}=-\sum_{\beta\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{\alpha\}}\tilde{\tau}_{\beta}.

are given by the first backward intersections of

On the other hand, for every

-axis and due to (

) (and by (

y_{k}:=\phi_{t_{k}}(x_{0}),\qquad\text{ where }t_{k}:=\sum_{\pi_{0}(\beta)\leq k}\tilde{\tau}_{\beta}.

.

5.
Diophantine conditions

In this section, we show that a certain collection of properties holds for typical IETs. These properties concern the parametric description of properly chosen return sets for the Zorich induction
\mathcal{Z}
. For an overview of the history and recent development of techniques related to different types of Diophantine properties for IETs and translation surfaces, we refer to the survey article
[
25
]
.

Fix a Rauzy graph
\mathcal{G}\subset S^{\mathcal{A}}
and consider an IET
(\pi,\lambda)
such that
\pi\in\mathcal{G}
is an irreducible permutation over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
of
d\geq 2
elements. Assume that there exists an increasing sequence
\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of natural numbers, a sequence
\{\{T^{i}(\Delta^{n})\mid 0\leq i<q_{n}\}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of Rokhlin towers, and a constant
C>1
, such that

(5.1)

\limsup_{n\to\infty}(q_{n})^{\frac{1}{n}}<\infty;

and, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(5.2)

\displaystyle q_{n+1}\geq 320C^{2}q_{n},

(5.3)

\displaystyle C\geq q_{n}|\Delta^{n}|\geq C^{-1}>0,

(5.4)

\displaystyle T^{i}(\Delta^{n})\text{ is an interval disjoint from the discontinuities of $T$, for any $0\leq i<q_{n}$,}

(5.5)

\displaystyle\Big(\bigcup_{0\leq i<q_{n}}T^{i}(\Delta^{n})\Big)^{c}\text{ consists of at most $q_{n}+1$ intervals (holes) of lengths less than $\tfrac{C}{q_{n}}$;}

(5.6)

T^{q_{n}}\mid_{\Delta^{n}}
is a translation by a number from
(\tfrac{1}{16}|\Delta^{n}|,\tfrac{1}{8}|\Delta^{n}|)
.

Proposition 5.1
.

For almost every
\lambda\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{A}}
there exists an increasing sequence
\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of natural numbers and a sequence
\{T^{i}(\Delta^{n})\mid 0\leq i<q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of Rokhlin towers satisfying (
5.2
)–(
5.6
). Moreover, these conditions are obtained via returning to an appropriate subset
\mathfrak{L}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
of positive measure.

Proof.

Fix
\pi\in S^{\mathcal{A}}
and let
\tau\in\Theta_{\pi}
be rationally independent. We will choose an appropriate on which to induce the Zorich induction
\mathcal{Z}
. We will define
\Delta_{n}
as a properly chosen subinterval of the base of the tower
\bigcup_{j=0}^{q^{(n)}_{\alpha}}T^{j}(I^{(n)}_{\alpha})
, for some
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{1}\subseteq\Lambda^{\mathcal{A}}
be a set of length parameters such that

(5.7)

\mathcal{D}_{1}:=\{\lambda\in\Lambda^{\mathcal{A}}\mid\lambda_{\pi_{0}^{-1}(d)}\geq\tfrac{7}{8}\ \text{and}\ \tfrac{1}{16}<w_{\pi_{0}^{-1}(d)}<\tfrac{1}{8}\},

where

is the translation vector associated with
(\pi,\lambda)
.

Note that since
\lambda_{\pi_{0}^{-1}(d)}\geq\tfrac{7}{8}
for
\lambda\in\mathcal{D}_{1}

(\pi,\lambda)
has top type for the RV induction.
Moreover, whenever
\mathcal{Z}^{n}(\pi,\lambda,\tau)\in\{\pi\}\times\mathcal{D}_{1}\times\Theta_{\pi}
, we have that
T^{q^{(n)}_{\pi_{0}^{-1}(d)}}
acts on

via translation by a number between
\tfrac{1}{16}|I_{\pi_{0}^{-1}(d)}^{(n)}|
and
\tfrac{1}{8}|I_{\pi_{0}^{-1}(d)}^{(n)}|
. Thus, the interval
I_{\pi_{0}^{-1}(d)}^{(n)}
satisfies (
5.6
).

Let now
\tilde{\tau}\in\Theta_{\pi}
be such that
(\pi,\lambda,\tilde{\tau})
is of bottom backward type and
\tilde{\tau}
is rationally independent. Let
\epsilon=\epsilon(\tilde{\tau})>0
and
C_{1}=C(\tilde{\tau},\epsilon)>0
be given by Proposition
4.1
. Denote

\mathcal{E}:=\mathbb{R}_{>0}B_{\Theta_{\pi}}(\tilde{\tau},\epsilon).

In view of Proposition
4.1
, whenever
\mathcal{Z}^{n}(\pi,\lambda,\tau)\in\{\pi\}\times\mathcal{D}_{1}\times\mathcal{E}
, the tower
\bigcup_{j=0}^{q^{(n)}_{\pi_{0}^{-1}(d)}-1}{I_{\pi_{0}^{-1}(d)}^{(n)}}
satisfies (
5.5
) with

. Moreover, since the cone
\mathcal{E}
is relatively compact (with respect to the Hilbert pseudometric) in
\Theta_{\pi}
and, by considering smaller
\epsilon
if necessary, we may assume that all
\tau\in\mathcal{E}
are of bottom backward type and

\sup_{\tau\in\mathcal{E}}\max_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{h_{\alpha}}{h_{\beta}}\ \leq\ C_{2},

for some

, where

\mathcal{Z}^{n}(\pi,\lambda,\tau)\in\{\pi\}\times\mathcal{D}_{1}\times\mathcal{E}

\max_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{A}}\frac{h^{(n)}_{\alpha}}{h^{(n)}_{\beta}}\ \leq\ C_{2}.

For every
\tau\in\Theta_{\pi}
let

and

.
As the zippered rectangle
D_{\alpha}^{(n)}
consists of
q_{\alpha}^{(n)}
rectangles of heights
h_{\beta}
for

, we have

m(\tau)\leq\frac{h^{(n)}_{\alpha}}{q^{(n)}_{\alpha}}\leq M(\tau)\quad\text{for any }\alpha\in\mathcal{A}.

It follows that if
\mathcal{Z}^{n}(\pi,\lambda,\tau)\in\{\pi\}\times\mathcal{D}_{1}\times\mathcal{E}

for all

and letting

Since Zorich induction

\mathcal{Z}
on
\mathcal{M}^{1}

\mathcal{D}_{3}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{2}

) is met.
Summarizing, by taking

\Delta^{n}:=I_{\pi_{0}^{-1}(d)}^{(k_{n})}\quad\text{and}\quad q_{n}:=q^{(k_{n})}_{\pi_{0}^{-1}(d)},

where

is the

-th visit of
(\pi,\lambda,\tau)
in
\mathfrak{L}:=\{\pi\}\times\mathcal{D}_{3}\times\mathcal{E}
, we obtain (

)-(

) are satisfied and the proof is complete.

The presented conditions on the returns of the IET via the Zorich induction

yield the geometric picture used to prove Proposition

To obtain the conditions UDC and SUDC in

chosen in

in Lemma 3.6 in

Proposition 5.3

Let

All the properties implied by the UDC and SUDC conditions that we use in this article come from

6.
Ergodicity Criterion and Borel-Cantelli Argument

In this section, we show that appropriate control of the Birkhoff sums for cocycles with singularities over large enough sets, but whose measure still decreases to 0 in a controlled manner, is enough to deduce the ergodicity of the corresponding skew products over IETs for which there exist Rokhlin towers as described in Section
5
.

This ergodicity criterion (see Theorem
6.3
) relies on the classical notion of
essential values
(see Definition
6.1
). Before stating it, we give here a brief overview of the tools needed to prove ergodicity. For further background material concerning skew products and infinite measure-preserving dynamical systems, we
refer the reader to
[
1
]
and
[
22
]
.

Let
T
be an ergodic measure-preserving automorphism on a probability measure space
(X,\mu)
.
For any measurable map (cocycle)
\varphi:X\to\mathbb{R}
denote by
T_{\varphi}
the skew product extension on
X\times\mathbb{R}
given by
T_{\varphi}(x,r)=(Tx,r+\varphi(x))
. Then for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
we have
T^{n}_{\varphi}(x,r)=(T^{n}x,r+S_{n}\varphi(x))
, where

(6.1)

S_{n}\varphi(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\sum_{0\leq j<n}\varphi(T^{j}x)&\text{if }n\geq 0,\\
-\sum_{n\leq j<0}\varphi(T^{j}x)&\text{if }n<0.\end{array}\right.

The skew product
T_{\varphi}
preserves the infinite product measure
\mu\times Leb
.

Two measurable cocycles
\varphi,\psi:X\to\mathbb{R}
are
called
cohomologous
if there exists a measurable function

g:X\to\mathbb{R}
(called a transfer function) such that

\varphi=\psi+g-g\circ T
. Then the corresponding skew products
T_{\varphi}
and

T_{\psi}
are measure-theoretically isomorphic via the maps

(x,y)\mapsto(x,y+g(x))
. A
cocycle
\varphi:X\to\mathbb{R}
is a
coboundary
if it is
cohomologous to the zero cocycle.

Definition 6.1
.

A real number
r\in\mathbb{R}
is said to be an
essential value
of
\varphi
, if for any
\epsilon>0

and any measurable set
\Omega\subseteq X
with
\mu(\Omega)>0
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

(6.2)

\displaystyle\mu(\Omega\cap T^{-n}\Omega\cap\{x\in X:S_{n}\varphi(x)\in(r-\epsilon,r+\epsilon)\})>0.

The set of essential values of
\varphi
is denoted by

{E}(\varphi)
.

Proposition 6.2

(See
[
22
]
)
.

The set
{E}(\varphi)
is a closed subgroup of
\mathbb{R}
.
The skew product
T_{\varphi}
is ergodic if and only if

E(\varphi)=\mathbb{R}
.

Recall that the singularities of the cocycles considered in this work can be described through an auxiliary increasing function
\theta:[1,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
(see (
1.2
)) for which certain growth properties hold (see (
1.1
)).

In the following, we use a similar auxiliary function to describe sets and properties of the (derivative of) Birkhoff sums of a piecewise smooth cocycle that guarantee the ergodicity of the associated skew product over IETs as in Section
5
.

From now on, for any interval
J=[a,b]
and any
0\leq\delta<1/2
we denote by
J(\delta)
its
\delta
-slimming
, that is,
J(\delta):=[a+\delta|J|,b-\delta|J|]
. We define the
\delta
-slimming of open and semi-open intervals analogously.

Theorem 6.3

(Ergodicity criterion)
.

Let
T:[0,1)\to[0,1)
be an IET and assume that there exist a sequence
\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and a sequence of Rokhlin towers
\{\{T^{i}(\Delta^{n})\mid 0\leq i<q_{n}\}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying (
5.1
)-(
5.6
). Let
\theta:[1,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be an increasing function satisfying

(6.3)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\theta(q_{n})}=\infty.

Suppose that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a family of intervals
\{\Delta_{i}^{n}\mid 0\leq i<q_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
such that

\Delta_{i}^{n}\subseteq T^{i}\Delta^{n}(\tfrac{1}{4})\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{D_{1}}{q_{n}\theta(q_{n})}\leq|\Delta_{i}^{n}|\leq\frac{D_{2}}{q_{n}\theta(q_{n})}\quad\text{for all }0\leq i<q_{n},

for some constants
0<D_{1}<D_{2}
.

Assume that
f:[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
is a piecewise
C^{1}
map (
C^{1}
on the interior of exchanged intervals) and there exist
E>1
and
v\in\mathbb{R}
such that, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
0\leq i<q_{n}
,

(6.4)

S_{q_{n}}(f)(\xi_{i}^{n})\in[v-D_{1}(4E)^{-1},v+D_{1}(4E)^{-1}],\ \text{where}\ \xi_{i}^{n}\text{ is the midpoint of }\Delta_{i}^{n},

S_{q_{n}}(f)
is strictly monotonic on
\Delta^{n}_{i}
and

(6.5)

E^{-1}q_{n}\theta(q_{n})\leq|S_{q_{n}}(f^{\prime})(x)|\leq Eq_{n}\theta(q_{n})\text{ for }x\in\Delta_{i}^{n}.

Then the skew product
T_{f}:[0,1)\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,1)\times\mathbb{R}
is ergodic.

The proof of this criterion relies on the following fact, which is in the spirit of the classical Borel-Cantelli Lemma and whose proof is inspired by a similar result obtained in
[
6
]
.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
T
,
\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
\{\Delta^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\theta
as in Theorem

. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
\{J^{n}_{i}\}_{0\leq i<q_{n}}
be a family of intervals and let
0<D_{1}<D_{2}
be constants such that

(6.6)

\displaystyle J^{n}_{i}\subseteq T^{i}\big(\Delta^{n}(\tfrac{1}{4})\big),

(6.7)

\displaystyle\frac{D_{1}}{q_{n}\theta(q_{n})}<|J^{n}_{i}|<\frac{D_{2}}{q_{n}\theta(q_{n})},

for all
0\leq i<q_{n}
and

Then for any interval
(r,s)\subseteq[0,1)

\frac{10C}{q_{k}}\leq(s-r)
and

\tilde{A}_{n}\subseteq A_{n}
, given by the union of a subcollection of intervals in
\{J^{n}_{i}\}_{0\leq i<q_{n}}
, such that

(6.8)

\displaystyle\big\{\tilde{A}_{n},\,T^{q_{n}}(\tilde{A}_{n})\,\big|\,n\geq k\big\}\text{ is a collection of pairwise disjoint subsets of $(r,s)$;}

(6.9)

\displaystyle Leb\,\Big(\bigcup_{n\geq k}\tilde{A}_{n}\Big)=\frac{1}{2}(s-r).

Proof.

The idea to prove this result is to show that for every
n>k
a large proportion of the set
A_{n}
“fills” the holes left by the sets
A_{k},\ldots,A_{n-1}
(more precisely, by sets
\tilde{A}_{j}
and

for

A_{j}
. Later in the proof, we provide the construction of
\tilde{A}_{n}

Note that, for every
n\geq k
, the set
A_{n}^{c}
is a union of
q_{n}+1
disjoint intervals. This follows from the fact that each interval
J^{n}_{i}
belongs to a different level of the tower
\bigcup_{0\leq i<q_{n}}T^{i}(\Delta^{n})
and is separated from its left and right endpoints. In particular, the set
\bigcap_{j=k}^{n-1}A_{j}^{c}
consists of at most
q_{n-1}+q_{n-2}+\ldots+q_{k}+1
disjoint intervals. We refer to each of these intervals as a
hole
. Passing to
\bigcap_{j=k}^{n-1}\hat{A}_{j}^{c}
, where

\hat{A}_{j}:=\tilde{A}_{j}\cup T^{q_{j}}\tilde{A}_{j},

we extend the holes such that their lengths are at least
\frac{10C}{q_{n}}
. Roughly speaking, all intervals from
A_{n}
that are contained in these holes are used in the construction of
\tilde{A}_{n}
.

Preliminary arguments.
Assume that
(a,b)\subseteq[0,1)
is a hole (interval) such that
b-a\geq\frac{10C}{q_{n}}
.
By (
5.3
) and (
5.5
), we have that the maximal distance between consecutive intervals forming
A_{n}
is at most
\frac{3C}{2q_{n}}
. In view of (
6.7
), the hole
(a,b)
intersects
l
intervals of the form
J^{n}_{i}
with
l\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying

(6.10)

b-a\leq l\frac{D_{2}}{q_{n}\theta(q_{n})}+(l+1)\frac{3C}{2q_{n}}.

Hence

10\leq l\frac{D_{2}}{C\theta(q_{n})}+(l+1)\leq l\frac{1}{32}+\frac{3}{2}(l+1),

so
l\geq 6
. In particular, we get that
2(l-2)\geq l+1
and thus by (
6.10
) we obtain

(6.11)

2(l-2)\geq\frac{b-a}{\frac{D_{2}}{q_{n}\theta(q_{n})}+\frac{3C}{2q_{n}}}.

Denote by
\mathcal{I}^{n}_{(a,b)}
the set of indices
0\leq i<q_{n}
for which
J^{n}_{i}\cap(a,b)\neq\emptyset
after throwing out the indices corresponding to the leftmost and rightmost intervals in
A_{n}
intersecting
(a,b)
. Then
\#\mathcal{I}^{n}_{(a,b)}=l-2\geq 4
and

(a,b)\setminus\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{n}_{(a,b)}}J^{n}_{i}

consists of
l-1
holes. In view of (
6.6
), the length of every such hole is at least
\frac{1}{2}|\Delta^{n}|
and hence, since by (
5.6
)
T^{q_{n}}
translates the interval
J^{n}_{i}
by at most
\frac{1}{8}|\Delta^{n}|
, we obtain

(6.12)

T^{q_{n}}J^{n}_{i}\subseteq(a,b),\qquad\text{for every }i\in\mathcal{I}^{n}_{(a,b)}.

Note that, by (
5.6
), we have that

(6.13)

\{J_{i}^{n},T^{q_{n}}J_{i}^{n}\mid 0\leq i<q_{n}\}\quad\text{is a collection of pairwise disjoint sets.}

Indeed, as
T^{q_{n}}\Delta^{n}(\tfrac{1}{4})\subseteq\Delta^{n}(\tfrac{1}{8})\subseteq\Delta^{n}
, for every
0\leq i<q_{n}
, we have
J_{i}^{n},\,T^{q_{n}}J_{i}^{n}\subseteq T^{i}\Delta^{n}
and for
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
the intervals
T^{i_{1}}\Delta^{n}
and
T^{i_{2}}\Delta^{n}
are two different levels of the Rokhlin tower.
It follows that
J^{n}_{i_{1}}
and
T^{q_{n}}J^{n}_{i_{1}}
are disjoint from
J^{n}_{i_{2}}
and
T^{q_{n}}J^{n}_{i_{2}}
and each pair of intervals is distant by at least
\frac{1}{4}|\Delta^{n}|
.
Moreover, by (
5.6
),
T^{q_{n}}J_{i}^{n}
is a translation of
J_{i}^{n}
by at least
\frac{1}{16}|\Delta^{n}|
. In view of (
6.7
) and (
5.3
),

|J^{n}_{i}|\leq\frac{D_{2}}{q_{n}\theta(q_{n})}\leq\frac{1}{32}|\Delta^{n}|
.
It follows that
J_{i}^{n}
and
T^{q_{n}}J_{i}^{n}
are also disjoint and they are distant by at least
\frac{1}{32}|\Delta^{n}|
.
In summary,

(6.14)

\displaystyle(a,b)\setminus\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{n}_{(a,b)}}(J^{n}_{i}\cup T^{q_{n}}J^{n}_{i})\text{ consists of $2(l-2)+1$ holes of length at least }\frac{1}{32}|\Delta^{n}|.

Moreover, by (
5.2
) and (
5.3
),

(6.15)

\frac{1}{32}|\Delta^{n}|\geq\frac{1}{32Cq_{n}}\geq\frac{10C}{q_{n+1}}.

On the other hand, by (
6.7
) and (
6.11
),

(6.16)

\displaystyle\begin{split}Leb\Big(\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{n}_{(a,b)}}J^{n}_{i}\Big)=Leb\Big(\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{n}_{(a,b)}}T^{q_{n}}J^{n}_{i}\Big)&\geq(l-2)\cdot\frac{D_{1}}{q_{n}\theta(q_{n})}\geq\frac{b-a}{\frac{2D_{2}}{q_{n}\theta(q_{n})}+\frac{3C}{q_{n}}}\cdot\frac{D_{1}}{q_{n}\theta(q_{n})}\\
&\geq\frac{D_{1}(b-a)}{(3C+2D_{2})\theta(q_{n})}=\frac{c}{\theta(q_{n})}(b-a),\end{split}

with
c:=D_{1}/(3C+2D_{2})
.

Inductive construction.

Fix any interval
(r,s)\subseteq[0,1)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\frac{10C}{q_{k}}\leq(s-r)
and
\theta(q_{k})\geq 32CD_{2}
.
We will define inductively a sequence
\{\tilde{A}_{n}\}_{n\geq k}
of subsets of
(r,s)
such that, for every
n\geq k
, the set
\tilde{A}_{n}\subseteq A_{n}
is the union of intervals
J_{i}^{n}
with
0\leq i<q_{n}
and

(6.17)

\displaystyle\text{all holes of }(r,s)\,\setminus\bigcup_{k\leq j\leq n}(\tilde{A}_{j}\cup T^{q_{j}}\tilde{A}_{j})\text{ have length at least }\frac{10C}{q_{n+1}},

(6.18)

\displaystyle Leb(\hat{A}_{n})\geq\frac{2c}{\theta(q_{n})}Leb\Big((r,s)\setminus\bigcup_{k\leq j<n}\hat{A}_{j}\Big),\qquad\text{ where }\hat{A}_{j}=\tilde{A}_{j}\cup T^{q_{j}}\tilde{A}_{j}.

Moreover, we define this sequence so that the collection
\{\tilde{A}_{n}
,
T^{q_{n}}\tilde{A}_{n}\mid n\geq k\}
consists of pairwise disjoint subsets of
(r,s)
.

First step.
Let us define the set
\tilde{A}_{k}
. As

, using the preliminary arguments at the beginning of the proof, we can define

as

\tilde{A}_{k}:=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{k}_{(r,s)}}J^{k}_{i}.

By (

6.15

6.18
) from (

Inductive step.

Suppose that the sets
\tilde{A}_{k},\ldots,\tilde{A}_{n}
are already defined, and let us define the set
\tilde{A}_{n+1}

\mathcal{H}_{n}

Final arguments.

such that

(6.19)

Leb(\Omega\cap(r,s))>\frac{9}{10}(s-r).

By Proposition
6.4
, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that for every
n\geq k
there exists a set
\tilde{A}_{n}
consisting of some intervals of the form
J_{i}^{n}
such that
\big\{\tilde{A}_{n},\,T^{q_{n}}(\tilde{A}_{n})\,\big|\,n\geq k\big\}
is a collection of pairwise disjoint subsets of
(r,s)
and

(6.20)

Leb\Big(\bigcup_{n\geq k}\tilde{A}_{n}\Big)=\frac{1}{2}(s-r).

We claim that there exists
n\geq k
and
0\leq i<q_{n}
such that

(6.21)

Leb\left(\left\{x\in J_{i}^{n}\mid x,T^{q_{n}}x\in\Omega\cap(r,s)\right\}\right)>0.

Before proving this, notice that this implies that
a
is an essential value. Indeed, since we chose the interval
J_{i}^{n}
so that
S_{q_{n}}f
restricted to the interior of
J_{i}^{n}
attains values in
(a-\epsilon,a+\epsilon)
, this would verify the condition required to check that
a
is an essential value.

Therefore, it suffices to show that (
6.21
) holds for some
n\geq k
and
0\leq i<q_{n}
. For the sake of contradiction, let us assume that (
6.21
) is never satisfied. Then for any
n\geq k
, any
0\leq i<q_{n}
, and for a.e.
x\in J_{i}^{n}
we have
x\notin\Omega\cap(r,s)
or
T^{q_{n}}x\notin\Omega\cap(r,s)
, so

\displaystyle Leb(J_{i}^{n})

\displaystyle\leq Leb(J^{n}_{i}\setminus(\Omega\cap(r,s)))+Leb(J^{n}_{i}\setminus T^{-q_{n}}(\Omega\cap(r,s)))

\displaystyle=Leb(J^{n}_{i}\setminus(\Omega\cap(r,s)))+Leb(T^{q_{n}}J^{n}_{i}\setminus(\Omega\cap(r,s)))

\displaystyle=Leb((J^{n}_{i}\cup T^{q_{n}}J^{n}_{i})\setminus(\Omega\cap(r,s))).

For any
n\geq k
, by summing up over all
0\leq i<q_{n}
with
J^{n}_{i}\subseteq\tilde{A}_{n}
, we get

Leb((\tilde{A}_{n}\cup T^{q_{n}}\tilde{A}_{n})\setminus(\Omega\cap(r,s)))\geq Leb(\tilde{A}_{n}).

Then, by summing up over
n\geq k
and using (
6.20
), we get

Leb\left((r,s)\setminus(\Omega\cap(r,s))\right)\geq Leb\Big(\bigcup_{n\geq k}(\tilde{A}_{n}\cup T^{q_{n}}\tilde{A}_{n})\setminus(\Omega\cap(r,s))\Big)\geq Leb\Big(\bigcup_{n\geq k}\tilde{A}_{n}\Big)=\frac{1}{2}(s-r).

Hence

Leb(\Omega\cap(r,s))\leq\frac{1}{2}(s-r),

which is a contradiction with (
6.19
).
∎

7.
Antisymmetric skew products over symmetric IETs

Let
T=(\pi,\lambda):[0,1)\to[0,1)
be a symmetric IET, i.e.
\pi_{0}(\alpha)+\pi_{1}(\alpha)=d+1
for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
. Denote by
\mathcal{I}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
the reflection across the midpoint
m_{I}:=\tfrac{1}{2}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{I}(x)=1-x
. Then
\mathcal{I}\circ T=T^{-1}\circ\mathcal{I}
and this map acts on every interval
I_{\alpha}
as the reflection across the midpoint
m_{\alpha}
of the exchanged interval
I_{\alpha}
, for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
. Note that if the permutation
\pi
is irreducible and
non-degenerated
, that is, there are no two adjacent intervals translated in parallel by
T
, and the length of exchanged intervals are rationally independent (in fact, it is enough to know that they are different) then the condition
\mathcal{I}\circ T=T^{-1}\circ\mathcal{I}
yields that
T
is symmetric.

Let
f:[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
be a piecewise continuous function with a finite number of discontinuities. We say that
f
is
piecewise monotonic
if it is increasing (resp. always decreasing) when restricted to the interior of each interval of continuity. The map
f
is said to be
anti-symmetric with respect to
T
if it is continuous on the interior of the intervals exchanged by
T
and satisfies
f\circ T^{-1}\circ\mathcal{I}=-f
, which means that the restriction of
f
to any of the intervals exchanged by
T
is anti-symmetric with respect to the central reflection on the exchanged interval. If there is no risk of confusion, we will sometimes omit the dependence on
T
in the previous definition and simply say that
f
is anti-symmetric.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
T=(\pi,\lambda):I\to I
be a symmetric IET and
f:I\to\mathbb{R}
be piecewise monotonic and anti-symmetric. Then

(7.1)

S_{n}f(x)=-S_{n}f(\mathcal{I}\circ T^{n}x),\quad\text{for any }x\in I\text{ and any }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\cup\{I\}
and
0<\epsilon<\tfrac{|I|}{2}
. Suppose that
\Delta_{\alpha}=[m_{\alpha}-\varepsilon,m_{\alpha}+\varepsilon]
and
q\in\mathbb{N}
are such that
\{T^{i}(\Delta_{\alpha})\mid 0\leq i<q\}
is a Rokhlin tower of intervals included in the exchanged intervals of
T
and

T^{q}m_{\alpha}=m_{\alpha}+\delta,\qquad\text{ with }0<|\delta|<\varepsilon/3.

Then, for every
-q<i<q
the map
S_{q}f
is continuous on
T^{i}[m_{\alpha}-2\varepsilon/3,m_{\alpha}+2\varepsilon/3]
and there exists
\xi_{i}\in T^{i}[m_{\alpha}-\varepsilon/2,m_{\alpha}+\varepsilon/2]
such that

S_{q}f(\xi_{i})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}f(m_{\alpha}-\delta/2)&\text{ if }&\alpha\in\mathcal{A},\\
0&\text{ if }&\alpha=I.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

By the anti-symmetricity of
f
, for any
x\in[0,1)
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} S_{n}f(x)&=\sum_{0\leq j<n}f(T^{j}x)=-\sum_{0\leq j<n}f(T^{-1}\circ\mathcal{I}\circ T^{j}x)\\
&=-\sum_{0\leq j<n}f(T^{n-j-1}\circ T^{-n}\circ\mathcal{I}x)=-S_{n}f(T^{-n}\circ\mathcal{I}x)=-S_{n}f(\mathcal{I}\circ T^{n}x),\end{aligned}

which gives (
7.1
).

Let
-q<i<q
. Notice that
T^{\pm q}(m_{\alpha})=m_{\alpha}\pm\delta
and, more generally,

T^{\pm q}(x)=x\pm\delta,\qquad\text{for any }x\in T^{i}[m_{\alpha}-2\varepsilon/3,m_{\alpha}+2\varepsilon/3].

Hence
T^{\pm q}[m_{\alpha}-2\varepsilon/3,m_{\alpha}+2\varepsilon/3]\subseteq\Delta_{\alpha}
, and the continuity of
S_{q}f
on
T^{i}[m_{\alpha}-2\varepsilon/3,m_{\alpha}+2\varepsilon/3]
follows directly from our assumptions.

Without loss of generality of reasoning, let us assume that
f
is piecewise increasing and that
0<\delta<\varepsilon/3
. In all other cases, the reasoning goes the same way. We now consider the cases
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
and
\alpha=I
separately.

Case 1:
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
.
By the anti-symmetricity of
f
, for any
x\in[0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle S_{q}f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq j<q}f(T^{j}x)=f(x)-f(T^{q}x)+\sum_{0<j\leq q}f(T^{j}x)

\displaystyle=f(x)-f(T^{q}x)-\sum_{0<j\leq q}f(T^{-1}\circ\mathcal{I}\circ T^{j}x)

\displaystyle=f(x)-f(T^{q}x)-\sum_{0<j\leq q}f(T^{-j-1}\circ\mathcal{I}x)

\displaystyle=f(x)-f(T^{q}x)-S_{q}f(T^{-q}\circ T^{-1}\circ\mathcal{I}x).

As
T^{q}(m_{\alpha}-\delta/2)=m_{\alpha}+\delta/2
and
T^{-1}\circ\mathcal{I}(m_{\alpha}-\delta/{2})=m_{\alpha}+\delta/{2}
, it follows that

\displaystyle S_{q}f(m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{\delta}{2})

\displaystyle=f(m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{\delta}{2})-f(T^{q}(m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{\delta}{2}))-S_{q}f(T^{-q}\circ T^{-1}\circ\mathcal{I}(m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{\delta}{2}))

\displaystyle=f(m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{\delta}{2})-f(m_{\alpha}+\tfrac{\delta}{2})-S_{q}f(T^{-q}(m_{\alpha}+\tfrac{\delta}{2}))

\displaystyle=2f(m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{\delta}{2})-S_{q}f(m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{\delta}{2}).

Therefore,
S_{q}f(m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{\delta}{2})=f(m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{\delta}{2})
.
Note that, if
0\leq i\leq q
, for any
x\in[m_{\alpha}-3\varepsilon/4,m_{\alpha}+3\varepsilon/4]
we have

\displaystyle S_{q}f(T^{i}x)=\sum_{i\leq j<q}f(T^{j}x)+\sum_{0\leq j<i}f(T^{q}T^{j}x)=\sum_{i\leq j<q}f(T^{j}x)+\sum_{0\leq j<i}f(T^{j}x+\delta).

As
f
is piecewise increasing, this yields

S_{q}f(x)\leq S_{q}f(T^{i}x)\leq S_{q}f(x+\delta).

It follows that

S_{q}f(T^{i}(m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{\delta}{2}))\geq S_{q}f(m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{\delta}{2})=f(m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{\delta}{2})

and

S_{q}f(T^{i}(m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{3\delta}{2}))\leq S_{q}f(m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{\delta}{2})=f(m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{\delta}{2}).

By continuity, there exists

\xi_{i}\in T^{i}[m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{3\delta}{2},m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{\delta}{2}]\ \text{ such that }\ S_{q}f(\xi_{i})=f(m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{\delta}{2}).

If
-q\leq i\leq 0
, then we take
\xi_{i}:=\xi_{q+i}
. Then

\xi_{i}=\xi_{q+i}\in T^{q+i}[m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{3\delta}{2},m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{\delta}{2}]=T^{i}[m_{\alpha}-\tfrac{\delta}{2},m_{\alpha}+\tfrac{\delta}{2}].

Finally, we have
\xi_{i}\in T^{i}[m_{\alpha}-\varepsilon/2,m_{\alpha}+\varepsilon/2]
in both cases.

Case 2:
\alpha=I
.
As
T^{-q}(m_{I}-\delta/2)=m_{I}+\delta/2
and
\mathcal{I}(m_{I}+\delta/2)=m_{I}-\delta/2
, by (
7.1
) it follows that

\displaystyle S_{q}f(m_{I}-\tfrac{\delta}{2})=-S_{q}f(m_{I}-\tfrac{\delta}{2}),\quad\text{so}\quad S_{q}f(m_{I}-\tfrac{\delta}{2})=0.

As
f
is piecewise increasing, if
0<i\leq q
, the arguments used in the first case show that

S_{q}f(x)\leq S_{q}f(T^{i}x)\leq S_{q}f(x+\delta),\qquad\text{ for any }x\in[m_{I}-2\varepsilon/3,m_{I}+2\varepsilon/3].

It follows that

S_{q}f(T^{i}(m_{I}-\tfrac{\delta}{2}))\geq S_{q}f(m_{I}-\tfrac{\delta}{2})=0\quad\text{ and }\quad S_{q}f(T^{i}(m_{I}-\tfrac{3\delta}{2}))\leq S_{q}f(m_{I}-\tfrac{\delta}{2})=0.

By continuity, there exists

\xi_{i}\in T^{i}[m_{I}-\tfrac{3\delta}{2},m_{I}-\tfrac{\delta}{2}]\ \text{ such that }\ S_{q}f(\xi_{i})=0.

If
-q\leq i\leq 0
, then we take again
\xi_{i}:=\xi_{q+i}
.
∎

For any integrable map
f:I\to\mathbb{R}
and any subinterval
J\subseteq I
let
m(f,J)
be the mean value of
f
on
J
, that is,
m(f,J):=\frac{1}{|J|}\int_{J}f(x)\,dx
. For any IET
T:I\to I
denote by
T_{J}:J\to J
the induced map and by
f_{J}:J\to\mathbb{R}
the induced cocycle, that is,
T_{J}(x)=T^{r_{J}(x)}(x)
and
f_{J}(x)=S_{r_{J}(x)}f(x)
, for any
x\in J
, where
r_{J}:J\to\mathbb{N}
denotes the first time return map.

Recall that for any interval
J=[a,b]
and
0\leq\delta<1/2
we let
J(\delta)=[a+\delta|J|,b-\delta|J|]
.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
T=(\pi,\lambda):I\to I
be a symmetric IET whose interval lengths are rationally independent, and let
f:I\to\mathbb{R}
be an anti-symmetric integrable map. Then for any
0\leq\delta<1/2
, we have
m(f,I_{\alpha}(\delta))=0
, for every
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
.

Let
J\subseteq I
be a symmetric interval, i.e.,
\mathcal{I}(J)=J
, such that the induced IET
T_{J}
exchanges the same number of intervals as
T
. Then
T_{J}
is a symmetric IET, and
f_{J}:J\to\mathbb{R}
is an antisymmetric cocycle with respect to
T_{J}
. In particular,
m(f_{J},J_{\alpha}(\delta))=0
for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
and any
0\leq\delta<1/2
.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\int_{I_{\alpha}(\delta)}f(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{l_{\alpha}+\delta|I_{\alpha}|}^{m_{\alpha}}f(x)\,dx+\int_{m_{\alpha}}^{r_{\alpha}-\delta|I_{\alpha}|}f(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{l_{\alpha}+\delta|I_{\alpha}|}^{m_{\alpha}}f(x)\,dx-\int_{m_{\alpha}}^{r_{\alpha}-\delta|I_{\alpha}|}f(\mathcal{I}\circ T^{-1}x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{l_{\alpha}+\delta|I_{\alpha}|}^{m_{\alpha}}f(x)\,dx-\int_{l_{\alpha}+\delta|I_{\alpha}|}^{m_{\alpha}}f(x)\,dx=0.

As
\mathcal{I}\circ T=T^{-1}\circ\mathcal{I}
, we have
\mathcal{I}\circ T^{n}=T^{-n}\circ\mathcal{I}
for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Hence, for any
x\in J
, we have

T^{r_{J}(x)}\circ\mathcal{I}\circ T_{J}x=\mathcal{I}x\in J
. It follows that
r_{J}(\mathcal{I}(T_{J}x))=r_{J}(x)
and
\mathcal{I}\circ T_{J}=T_{J}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{I}
. By assumption, the permutation of
T_{J}
belongs to the Rauzy class of
\pi
, so it is irreducible and non-degenerated.
Moreover, since
\lambda
is rationally independent, the lengths of the intervals exchanged by
T_{J}
are different. Therefore,
T_{J}
is also symmetric. Moreover, by (
7.1
),

f_{J}(\mathcal{I}(T_{J}x))=S_{r_{J}(\mathcal{I}(T_{J}x))}f(\mathcal{I}(T_{J}x))=S_{r_{J}(x)}f(\mathcal{I}(T^{r_{J}(x)}x))=-S_{r_{J}(x)}f(x)=-f_{J}(x).

By the first part of the lemma, this gives
m(f_{J},J_{\alpha}(\delta))=0
for every
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
.
∎

Suppose that

(7.2)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{$\theta:[1,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ is an increasing $C^{1}$-map such that $\int_{1}^{+\infty}\frac{dx}{x\theta(x)}=+\infty$,}\\
\text{ and the map $\tau:(0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ given by $\tau(s)=\frac{s^{2}}{\theta^{\prime}(1/s)}$ is increasing.}\quad\end{aligned}

Recall that
\Upsilon_{\theta}(\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}I_{\alpha})
is the space of functions
f:[0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
which are
C^{1}
on the interior of the intervals
I_{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, and

(7.3)

Z_{\theta}(f):=\max_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\left\{\sup_{x\in(l_{\alpha},m_{\alpha}]}|f^{\prime}(x)\tau(x-l_{\alpha})|,\sup_{x\in[m_{\alpha},r_{\alpha})}|f^{\prime}(x)\tau(r_{\alpha}-x)|\right\}<+\infty.

The following proposition shows how to verify the assumption (
6.5
) in Theorem
6.3
for functions in
\Upsilon_{\theta}(\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}I_{\alpha})
with
\theta
as in (
7.2
).

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
T:[0,1)\to[0,1)
be an IET and assume that there exist a sequence
\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and a sequence of Rokhlin towers
\{\{T^{i}(\Delta^{n})\mid 0\leq i<q_{n}\}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying (
5.3
)-(
5.6
). Let
\theta
and
\tau
as in (
7.2
).
Then, for any
f\in\Upsilon_{\theta}(\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}I_{\alpha})
, there exists
E>1
such that

(7.4)

\displaystyle|S_{q_{n}}(f^{\prime})(x)|\leq Eq_{n}\theta(Eq_{n})\qquad\text{ for any }x\in T^{i}\Delta^{n}(\tfrac{1}{4})\text{ and any }0\leq i<q_{n}.

If additionally
f
is piecewise monotonic and

(7.5)

z_{\theta}(f):=\max_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\left\{\inf_{x\in(l_{\alpha},m_{\alpha}]}|f^{\prime}(x)\tau(x-l_{\alpha})|,\inf_{x\in[m_{\alpha},r_{\alpha})}|f^{\prime}(x)\tau(r_{\alpha}-x)|\right\}>0,

then

(7.6)

\displaystyle|S_{q_{n}}(f^{\prime})(x)|\geq E^{-1}q_{n}\theta(E^{-1}q_{n})\qquad\text{ for any }x\in T^{i}\Delta^{n}(\tfrac{1}{4})\text{ and any }0\leq i<q_{n}.

Proof.

By (
5.3
) and (
5.5
), there exists
\bar{C}>1
such that for any
x\in T^{i}\Delta^{n}(\tfrac{1}{4})
with
0\leq i<q_{n}
, we have that
the distance between two consecutive elements of the set

\{T^{j}x:0\leq j<q_{n}\}\cup End(T),

where
End(T)
denotes the set of endpoints of the exchanged intervals of
T
, is always between
\bar{C}^{-1}/q_{n}
and
\bar{C}/q_{n}
. As
1/\tau
is decreasing, this observation together with (
7.2
) yield

|S_{q_{n}}f^{\prime}(x)|\leq\sum_{0\leq j<q_{n}}|f^{\prime}(T^{j}x)|\leq 2dZ_{\theta}(f)\sum_{1\leq j\leq q_{n}}\frac{1}{\tau(\frac{1}{\bar{C}q_{n}}j)}\leq 4dZ_{\theta}(f)\bar{C}q_{n}\int_{\frac{1}{2\bar{C}q_{n}}}^{1}\frac{ds}{\tau(s)}.

Moreover, by the definition of
\tau
,

\int_{\frac{1}{2\bar{C}q_{n}}}^{1}\frac{ds}{\tau(s)}=\int_{\frac{1}{2\bar{C}q_{n}}}^{1}\frac{\theta^{\prime}(1/s)ds}{s^{2}}=-\int_{\frac{1}{2\bar{C}q_{n}}}^{1}\frac{d}{ds}\theta(1/s)ds=\theta(2\bar{C}q_{n})-\theta(1)\leq\theta(2\bar{C}q_{n}),

which gives (
7.4
).

Suppose now that
f
is piecewise monotonic and that
z_{\theta}(f)
given by (
7.5
) is positive. Let us assume without loss of generality that
f
is increasing and that, for some
\alpha_{0}\in\mathcal{A}
,
z_{\theta}(f)=\inf_{x\in(l_{\alpha_{0}},m_{\alpha_{0}}]}f^{\prime}(x)\tau(x-l_{\alpha_{0}})>0
. In all other cases, the arguments go in the same way.

Then, for any
x\in T^{i}\Delta^{n}(\tfrac{1}{4})
with
0\leq i<q_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle|S_{q_{n}}f^{\prime}(x)|

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq j<q_{n}}f^{\prime}(T^{j}x)\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j<q_{n}\\
T^{j}x\in(l_{\alpha_{0}},m_{\alpha_{0}}]\end{subarray}}f^{\prime}(T^{j}x)\geq z_{\theta}(f)\sum_{1\leq j\leq\frac{\lambda_{\alpha_{0}}}{2\bar{C}}q_{n}}\frac{1}{\tau(\frac{\bar{C}}{q_{n}}j)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{z_{\theta}(f)}{\bar{C}}q_{n}\int_{\frac{\bar{C}}{q_{n}}}^{{\lambda_{\alpha_{0}}}}\frac{ds}{\tau(s)}=\frac{z_{\theta}(f)}{\bar{C}}q_{n}\left(\theta(\tfrac{q_{n}}{\bar{C}})-\theta(\tfrac{1}{\lambda_{\alpha_{0}}})\right),

which gives (
7.6
).
∎

.

Applications to skew product with logarithmic singularities

Given an IET

\mathrm{LG}(\sqcup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}I_{\alpha})

\varphi:I\to\mathbb{R}

Appendix A
Locally Hamiltonian flows with imperfect saddles

The main purpose of this section is to build a natural class of examples of ergodic locally Hamiltonian flows
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
for which
\varphi_{f}
have singularities other than logarithmic and power.
More precisely, we find a natural family of functions
\theta
satisfying (
1.1
) for which
\varphi_{f}\in\Upsilon_{\theta}(\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}I_{\alpha})
. Finally, for this type of flows
\psi_{\mathbb{R}}
which are of hyper-elliptic type (there exists an involution
\mathcal{I}
such that
\psi_{t}\circ\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}\circ\psi_{-t}
), we construct anti-symmetric observables
f
(i.e.
f\circ\mathcal{I}=-f
) for which the skew extension
\psi^{f}_{\mathbb{R}}
is ergodic, using Theorem
1.1
for
\varphi_{f}\in\Upsilon_{\theta}(\bigsqcup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}I_{\alpha})
.

To implement this idea into practice, in Sections
A.1
and
A.2
we consider some local Hamiltonians having degenerate saddle points that are not perfect. Such non-perfect saddles have pairs of tangent separatrices, unlike the perfect situation, see Figures
3
and
4
. In Section
A.1
, we perform an analysis of transition times near simple imperfect saddles, with only four separatrices, see Figure
3
. Using the results obtained in Section
A.2
, we examine imperfect multi-saddles. These, in turn, are used to construct hyper-elliptic locally Hamiltonian flows with saddle loops and their ergodic skew extensions in Section
A.3
.

A.1.
Degenerate simple saddles

Let
H:[-1,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{2}
-hamiltonian such that
H(x,y)=-H_{1}(x)+H_{2}(y)
and
H_{1},H_{2}:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
are unimodal functions with the minimum at zero, i.e.

H_{1}(0)=H_{2}(0)=0,\quad H^{\prime}_{1}(0)=H^{\prime}_{2}(0)=0,\text{ and }xH_{1}^{\prime}(x)>0,\quad xH_{2}^{\prime}(x)>0\text{ for }x\neq 0.

Most often, we will assume that
H_{1},H_{2}
are even, i.e.

(A.1)

H_{1}(-x)=H_{1}(x)\ \text{and}\ H_{2}(-x)=H_{2}(x).

Then, the corresponding Hamiltonian equation is of the form

x^{\prime}=H_{2}^{\prime}(y),\quad y^{\prime}=H_{1}^{\prime}(x).

We denote the corresponding local Hamiltonian flow on
[-1,1]^{2}
by
(\Psi_{t})
. It has four separatrixes, which together form the set of solutions to the equation
H_{1}(x)=H_{2}(y)
. The set of solutions is the union of two graphs of the maps
H_{2,+}^{-1}\circ H_{1}
and
H_{2,-}^{-1}\circ H_{1}
, where
H_{i,\pm}^{-1}
are two inverse branches of
H_{i}
for
i=1,2
. Both maps
H_{2,\pm}^{-1}\circ H_{1}
are continuous, and
C^{2}
except perhaps zero. We will deal also with level sets
L_{s}:=\{(x,y)\in[-1,1]^{2}:-H_{1}(x)+H_{2}(y)=s\}
for
s\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. Each of them is the union of two disjoint
C^{2}
-curves. Let
\gamma
be a compact smooth transversal curve. Let us consider its parametrization
\gamma:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
H(\gamma(s))=s
for
s\in[a,b]
. This parametrization is of class
C^{2}
and is standard in the sense that
\int_{\gamma([a,s])}\eta=s-a
, where
\eta=\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}dx+\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}dy
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma([a,s])}\eta=\int_{a}^{s}\Big(\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}(\gamma(t))\gamma^{\prime}_{1}(t)+\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}(\gamma(t))\gamma^{\prime}_{2}(t)\Big)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{s}\frac{d}{dt}H(\gamma(t))dt=H(\gamma(s))-H(\gamma(a))=s-a.

Figure 3.
The phase portrait of the local Hamiltonian on the rectangle
R
. The map
r_{\pi}
is the rotation by
\pi
. The symmetry of the picture is due to (
A.1
).

For any
0<x_{0}\leq 1
and
0<\varepsilon<s_{0}:=H_{1}(x_{0})
(we will constantly assume that
s_{0}=H_{1}(x_{0})\leq 1
), let
\gamma^{+},\gamma^{-}
are two curves (lines) given by

(A.2)

\gamma^{+}(s)=(x_{0},H_{2,+}^{-1}(H_{1}(x_{0})-s))\text{ and }\gamma^{-}(s)=(x_{0},H_{2,-}^{-1}(H_{1}(x_{0})-s))\text{ for }s\in[0,\varepsilon].

Then,

(A.3)

H(\gamma^{\pm}(s))=-H_{1}(x_{0})+H_{2}(H_{2,\pm}^{-1}(H_{1}(x_{0})-s))=-s,

so their reverse parametrizations are standard. Since
\gamma^{+}(s),\gamma^{-}(s)\in L_{-s}
for
s\in(0,\varepsilon]
, they lay on the same orbit of
(\Psi_{t})
. We denote the time it takes for the flow to get from
\gamma^{-}(s)
to
\gamma^{+}(s)
by
r(s)>0
. Since the orbit starts from the lower half-plane (from
\gamma^{-}(s)
), ends in the upper half-plane (at
\gamma^{+}(s)
), and goes up all the time, there is exactly one its intersection point with the horizontal axis, that is
(\xi(s),0)=\Psi_{t(s)}(\gamma^{-}(s))
with
0<t(s)<r(s)
. Moreover,

-H_{1}(\xi(s))=H(\xi(s),0)=H(\gamma^{-}(s))=-s,\text{ so }\xi(s)=H^{-1}_{1,+}(s).

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function. We are interested in studying the behavior of ergodic integrals

\varphi_{f}(s)=\int_{0}^{r(s)}f(\Psi_{t}(\gamma^{-}(s)))dt=\int_{0}^{t(s)}f(\Psi_{t}(\gamma^{-}(s)))dt+\int_{t(s)}^{r(s)}f(\Psi_{t}(\gamma^{-}(s)))dt

when
s
converges to zero. Let
(x(t),y(t))=\Psi_{t}(\gamma^{-}(s))
. Then
H_{1}(x(t))-H_{2}(y(t))=s
, so

y(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}H_{2,-}^{-1}(H_{1}(x(t))-s)&\text{for }t\in[0,t(s)]\\
H_{2,+}^{-1}(H_{1}(x(t))-s)&\text{for }t\in[t(s),r(s)].\end{array}\right.

Moreover,
x^{\prime}(t)=H^{\prime}_{2}(y(t))=H^{\prime}_{2}(H_{2,+}^{-1}(H_{1}(x(t))-s))
. Substituting
u=u(t)=H_{1}(x(t))
, for
t\in[0,t(s)]
, we get

du=H^{\prime}_{1}(x(t))x^{\prime}(t)dt=H^{\prime}_{1}(H^{-1}_{1,+}(u))H^{\prime}_{2}(H_{2,-}^{-1}(u-s))dt

with

u(0)=H_{1}(x(0))=H_{1}(\gamma^{-}_{1}(s))=H_{1}(x_{0})=s_{0}\text{ and }u(t(s))=H_{1}(x(t(s)))=H_{1}(\xi(s))=s,

and for
t\in[t(s),r(s)]
, we get

du=H^{\prime}_{1}(x(t))x^{\prime}(t)dt=H^{\prime}_{1}(H^{-1}_{1,+}(u))H^{\prime}_{2}(H_{2,+}^{-1}(u-s))dt

with

u(r(s))=H_{1}(x(r(s)))=H_{1}(\gamma^{+}_{1}(s))=H_{1}(x_{0})=s_{0}\text{ and }u(t(s))=s.

Therefore, integrating by substitution, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t(s)}f(\Psi_{t}(\gamma^{-}(s)))dt

\displaystyle=-\int_{s}^{s_{0}}\frac{f(H^{-1}_{1,+}(u),H_{2,-}^{-1}(u-s))}{H^{\prime}_{1}(H^{-1}_{1,+}(u))H^{\prime}_{2}(H_{2,-}^{-1}(u-s))}du

\displaystyle\int_{t(s)}^{r(s)}f(\Psi_{t}(\gamma^{-}(s)))dt

\displaystyle=\int_{s}^{s_{0}}\frac{f(H^{-1}_{1,+}(u),H_{2,+}^{-1}(u-s))}{H^{\prime}_{1}(H^{-1}_{1,+}(u))H^{\prime}_{2}(H_{2,+}^{-1}(u-s))}du.

Recall that
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are even. Then,
-H^{-1}_{i,-}=H^{-1}_{i,+}=:H^{-1}_{i}
and

(A.4)

\varphi_{f}(s)=\int_{s}^{s_{0}}\frac{f(H^{-1}_{1}(u),H_{2}^{-1}(u-s))+f(H^{-1}_{1}(u),-H_{2}^{-1}(u-s))}{H^{\prime}_{1}(H^{-1}_{1}(u))H^{\prime}_{2}(H_{2}^{-1}(u-s))}du.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be a
C^{1}
-map such that

(A.5)

g\text{ is increasing and concave with}\quad\lim_{x\to+\infty}g(x)=+\infty\text{ and }\int_{2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{xG(\log x)}\,dx=+\infty,

where
G
is the primitive of
g
with
G(0)=0
. Observe that
g(x)=\log(x)
satisfies (
A.5
).

Remark A.1
.

Note that

(A.6)

\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{g(x+a)}{g(x)}=1\text{ for any }a>1.

Indeed, by (
A.5
),
\frac{d}{dx}\log g(x)=\frac{g^{\prime}(x)}{g(x)}\to 0
as
x\to+\infty
. Therefore, for any
a>0
,

\log 1\leq\liminf_{x\to+\infty}\log\frac{g(x+a)}{g(x)}\leq\limsup_{x\to+\infty}\log\frac{g(x+a)}{g(x)}\leq\limsup_{x\to+\infty}a\frac{d}{dx}\log g(x)=0,

which gives (
A.6
).

Lemma A.2
.

Let
k\geq 1
. For any
C^{k}
-map
g:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
satisfying (
A.5
), there exists an even
C^{2}
-map
H:[-x_{0},x_{0}]\to[0,s_{0}]
with
0<x_{0}\leq 1
and
s_{0}:=H(x_{0})\leq 1
, such that

(H^{-1})^{\prime}(u)=\frac{g(-\log u)}{\sqrt{u}}\text{ for }u\in(0,s_{0}],\text{ and }H(0)=H^{\prime}(0)=H^{\prime\prime}(0)=0.

Moreover,
H
is of class
C^{k+1}
on
(0,x_{0}]
.

Proof.

Let us start by observing that, by the concavity of
g
, the function
\frac{g(-\log u)}{\sqrt{u}}=O(\frac{-\log u}{\sqrt{u}})
is integrable. Let us define the inverse
H^{-1}:[0,s_{0}]\to[0,x_{0}]
, which is given by

H^{-1}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{g(-\log u)du}{\sqrt{u}}

which is a continuous increasing function and is
C^{k+1}
on
(0,s_{0}]
and
H(0)=0
. More precisely, we choose
0<s_{0}\leq 1
such that the integral
x_{0}=H^{-1}(s_{0})
is at most
1
.
Then, its inverse can be extended to an even continuous function
H:[-x_{0},x_{0}]\to[0,s_{0}]
such that
H
is of class
C^{k+1}
on
(0,x_{0}]
,
H(0)=0
, and
(H)^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{H(x)}}{g(-\log H(x))}
for
x\in(0,x_{0}]
.
To show that
H
on
[-1,1]
is class
C^{2}
, it is enough to show that

\lim_{x\to 0}H^{\prime}(x)=\lim_{x\to 0}H^{\prime\prime}(x)=0.

As
H(0)=0
, by (
A.5
), we have

(H)^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{H(x)}}{g(-\log H(x))}\to 0\text{ as }x\to 0,

and

\displaystyle(H)^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{(H)^{\prime}(x)}{2\sqrt{H(x)}g(-\log H(x))}+\frac{(H)^{\prime}(x)g^{\prime}(-\log H(x))}{\sqrt{H(x)}g^{2}(-\log H(x))}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2g^{2}(-\log H(x))}+\frac{g^{\prime}(-\log H(x))}{g^{3}(-\log H(x))}\to 0\text{ as }x\to 0.

∎

We now provide a construction of the Hamiltonian in the simple saddle case, where one of the components
H_{i}
of the Hamiltonian is simply a square, while the other is a perturbation of a square so that finally, the ergodic integral obtained via approaching the saddle, is of the form for which the Theorem
9.1
holds in the simple case. Moreover, this construction will also be crucial in the general (non-simple saddle) case, where the picture seen on some of the pairs of neighboring sectors can be viewed as a pre-image of the simple saddle picture through the map
z\mapsto z^{m}
, where
2m
is the multiplicity of the saddle. The cases depend on the coordinate on which we see the perfect square. It is worth mentioning that in the final construction, we use Case 2. However, Case 1 (depicted in Figure
3
) corresponds to the picture seen in Case 2 rotated by
\tfrac{\pi}{2}
and allows for some simplification in the computation.

Case 1.

Suppose that
H_{2}(y)=y^{2}
,
H_{1}
is an even
C^{2}
-map such that
(H^{-1}_{1})^{\prime}(u)=\frac{g(-\log u)}{\sqrt{u}}
for
u\in(0,H_{1}(1)]
, and
f\equiv 1
.
Then, by (
A.4
), we have

\varphi_{1}(s)=\int_{s}^{s_{0}}\frac{g(-\log u)}{\sqrt{u}\sqrt{u-s}}du=\int_{1}^{\frac{s_{0}}{s}}\frac{g(-\log su)}{\sqrt{u}\sqrt{u-1}}du

and, by the Leibniz integration formula, we have

\varphi^{\prime}_{1}(s)=-\frac{s_{0}}{s}\frac{g(-\log s_{0})}{\sqrt{s_{0}}\sqrt{s_{0}-s}}-\int_{1}^{\frac{s_{0}}{s}}\frac{g^{\prime}(-\log su)}{s\sqrt{u}\sqrt{u-1}}du.

Moreover,

\displaystyle-s\varphi^{\prime}_{1}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{g(-\log s_{0})\sqrt{s_{0}}}{\sqrt{s_{0}-s}}+\int_{1}^{\frac{s_{0}}{s}}\frac{g^{\prime}(-\log su)}{\sqrt{u}\sqrt{u-1}}du

\displaystyle\geq g(-\log s_{0})+\int_{1}^{\frac{s_{0}}{s}}\frac{g^{\prime}(-\log su)}{u}du=g(-\log s),

and for any
n\geq 2
and any
s\in(0,s_{0}/n]
, we have

\displaystyle-s\varphi^{\prime}_{1}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{g(-\log s_{0})\sqrt{s_{0}}}{\sqrt{s_{0}-s}}+\int_{1}^{\frac{s_{0}}{s}}\frac{g^{\prime}(-\log su)}{\sqrt{u}\sqrt{u-1}}du

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{n}{n-1}}g(-\log s_{0})+\int_{1}^{n}\frac{g^{\prime}(-\log su)}{\sqrt{u}\sqrt{u-1}}du+\sqrt{\frac{n}{n-1}}\int_{n}^{\frac{s_{0}}{s}}\frac{g^{\prime}(-\log su)}{u}du

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{n}{n-1}}g(-\log s)+\int_{1}^{n}\frac{g^{\prime}(0)}{\sqrt{u}\sqrt{u-1}}du.

It follows that there exists
C>1
such that

(A.7)

\displaystyle\frac{g(-\log s)}{s}\leq|\varphi^{\prime}_{1}(s)|\leq C\frac{g(-\log s)}{s}\text{ for }s\in(0,s_{0}/2],

\displaystyle 1\leq\liminf_{s\to 0}\frac{-\varphi^{\prime}_{1}(s)}{\frac{g(-\log s)}{s}}\leq\limsup_{s\to 0}\frac{-\varphi^{\prime}_{1}(s)}{\frac{g(-\log s)}{s}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{n}{n-1}}.

By passing with
n
to infinity, we get

(A.8)

\lim_{s\to 0}{\frac{s}{g(-\log s)}{\varphi^{\prime}_{1}(s)}}=-1.

Remark A.3
.

Note that, in this case, the map
H^{-1}_{2}\circ H_{1}=\sqrt{H_{1}}:[-x_{0},x_{0}]\to\mathbb{R}
is of class
C^{1}
with the derivative at zero equal zero. Indeed, this follows from

(\sqrt{H_{1}})^{\prime}(x)=\frac{(H_{1})^{\prime}(x)}{2\sqrt{H_{1}(x)}}=\frac{1}{2g(-\log H_{1}(x))}\to 0\text{ as }x\to 0.

Therefore, the level set
L_{0}
is the union of the graphs of two
C^{1}
-maps
\pm\sqrt{H_{1}}
, and the phase portrait of the local Hamiltonian flow is as shown in Figure
3
.

Case 2.
Suppose that
H_{1}(x)=x^{2}
,
H_{2}
is an even
C^{2}
-map such that
(H^{-1}_{2})^{\prime}(u)=\frac{g(-\log u)}{\sqrt{u}}
, and
f\equiv 1
. Then,

\varphi_{1}(s)=\int_{s}^{s_{0}}\frac{g(-\log(u-s))}{\sqrt{u}\sqrt{u-s}}du=\int_{1}^{\frac{s_{0}}{s}}\frac{g(-\log s(u-1))}{\sqrt{u}\sqrt{u-1}}du,

and, for any
n\geq 2
and any
s\in(0,s_{0}/n]
, the Leibniz integration formula yields

\displaystyle-s\varphi^{\prime}_{1}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{g(-\log(s_{0}-s))\sqrt{s_{0}}}{\sqrt{s_{0}-s}}+\int_{1}^{\frac{s_{0}}{s}}\frac{g^{\prime}(-\log s(u-1))}{\sqrt{u}\sqrt{u-1}}du

\displaystyle=\frac{g(-\log(s_{0}-s))\sqrt{s_{0}}}{\sqrt{s_{0}-s}}+\int_{1}^{n}\frac{g^{\prime}(-\log s(u-1))}{\sqrt{u}\sqrt{u-1}}du+\int_{n-1}^{\frac{s_{0}}{s}-1}\frac{g^{\prime}(-\log su)}{\sqrt{u+1}\sqrt{u}}du.

Moreover, since
s\leq s_{0}/n
, we have

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{g(-\log(s_{0}-s))\sqrt{s_{0}}}{\sqrt{s_{0}-s}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{n}{n-1}}g(-\log(s_{0}(1-\tfrac{1}{n})))=:C_{n},

\displaystyle 0\leq\int_{1}^{n}\frac{g^{\prime}(-\log s(u-1))}{\sqrt{u}\sqrt{u-1}}du\leq\int_{1}^{n}\frac{g^{\prime}(0)}{\sqrt{u}\sqrt{u-1}}du=:D_{n}<+\infty.

It follows that for
s\in(0,s_{0}/2]
, we have

\displaystyle-s\varphi^{\prime}_{1}(s)

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}+D_{2}+\int_{1}^{\frac{s_{0}}{s}-1}\frac{g^{\prime}(-\log su)}{\sqrt{u+1}\sqrt{u}}du

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}+D_{2}+\int_{1}^{\frac{s_{0}}{s}}\frac{g^{\prime}(-\log su)}{u}du\leq C_{2}+D_{2}+g(-\log s),

and any
s\in(0,s_{0}/n]
, we have

\displaystyle-s\varphi^{\prime}_{1}(s)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{n-1}^{\frac{s_{0}}{s}-1}\frac{g^{\prime}(-\log su)}{\sqrt{u+1}\sqrt{u}}du\geq\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{n}}\int_{n-1}^{\frac{s_{0}}{s}(1-\frac{1}{n})}\frac{g^{\prime}(-\log su)}{u}du

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{n}}(g(-\log(n-1)s)-g(-\log(s_{0}(1-\tfrac{1}{n}))).

It follows that there are
0<c<C
such that

(A.9)

\displaystyle c\frac{g(-\log s)}{s}\leq|\varphi^{\prime}_{1}(s)|\leq C\frac{g(-\log s)}{s}\text{ for }s\in(0,s_{0}/4],

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{n}}\leq\liminf_{s\to 0}\frac{-\varphi^{\prime}_{1}(s)}{\frac{g(-\log(n-1)s)}{s}},\text{ and }\limsup_{s\to 0}\frac{-\varphi^{\prime}_{1}(s)}{\frac{g(-\log s)}{s}}\leq 1.

Using (
A.6
), by passing with
n
to infinity, we get

(A.10)

\lim_{s\to 0}{\frac{s}{g(-\log s)}{\varphi^{\prime}_{1}(s)}}=-1.

Let us consider two maps
\tau:(0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
given by
\tau(s)=\frac{s}{g(-\log s)}
, and
\theta:[1,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
given by

\theta(x)=\int_{1}^{x}\frac{du}{u^{2}\tau(1/u)}=\int_{1}^{x}\frac{g(\log u)}{u}du=\int_{0}^{\log x}g(u)du=:G(\log x).

Proposition A.4
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
be a
C^{1}
-map satisfying (
A.5
). Then,
\theta
and
\tau
satisfy (
7.2
), and
\theta
is slowly varying.

Suppose that
H:[-1,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{2}
-map such that
H(x,y)=H_{1}(x)+H_{2}(y)
with

•

H_{1}(x)=x^{2}
,
H_{2}
is an even
C^{2}
-map such that
(H^{-1}_{2})^{\prime}(u)=\frac{g(-\log u)}{\sqrt{u}}
, or

•

H_{2}(y)=y^{2}
,
H_{1}
is an even
C^{2}
-map such that
(H^{-1}_{1})^{\prime}(u)=\frac{g(-\log u)}{\sqrt{u}}
.

Then,
\varphi_{1}\in{C^{1}((0,s_{0}])}
and there are
0<c<C
such that

(A.11)

c\leq\tau(s)|\varphi_{1}^{\prime}(s)|\leq C\text{ for }s\in(0,s_{0}/4]\text{ with }\lim_{s\to 0}\tau(s)\varphi_{1}^{\prime}(s)=-1.

Proof.

By definition,
\theta
is increasing and

\tau^{\prime}(s)=\frac{1}{g(-\log s)}+\frac{g^{\prime}(-\log s)}{g^{2}(-\log s)}>0,

so
\tau
is also increasing. By (

), we also have
\int_{2}^{+\infty}\frac{dx}{x\theta(x)}=+\infty
, so
\theta
and

satisfy (
7.2
).

In view of (
A.6
), for any

, we have

\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{\theta(\alpha x)}{\theta(x)}=\alpha\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{\theta^{\prime}(\alpha x)}{\theta^{\prime}(x)}=\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{g(x+\log\alpha)}{g(x)}=1,

the map
\theta
is also slowly varying.

Finally, (
A.11
) (in particular,
\varphi_{1}\in\Upsilon_{\theta}((0,s_{0}])
) follows directly from (

), (
A.8
), (
A.9
), and (

).

A.2.
Imperfect multi-saddles

We are now ready to consider flows with saddles of higher multiplicities. A large part of what follows was inspired by techniques used in
[
9
]
, where a very thorough local analysis of flow with perfect saddles was performed.

Let us consider the standard Hamiltonian equation on the complex plane
z^{\prime}=-2i\frac{\partial H}{\partial\bar{z}}(z,\bar{z})
.
Suppose that
\widetilde{H}:[-1,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
C^{2}
-Hamiltonian which is
C^{\infty}
outside
(0,0)
. Later on, it will simply be the Hamiltonian considered in Section
A.1
. Let
m\geq 2
. We denote by
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{m}
the pre-image of the square
[-1,1]\times[-1,1]
by the map
\mathbb{C}\ni\omega\mapsto\omega^{m}\in\mathbb{C}
. Let
H:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}
be the new Hamiltonian given by
H(z,\bar{z})=\widetilde{H}(z^{m},\bar{z}^{m})
. The map
H
is of class
C^{2m}
. We analyze the dynamics of trajectories of the Hamiltonian flow derived from the equation
z^{\prime}=-2i\frac{\partial H}{\partial\bar{z}}(z,\bar{z})
by changing variables
\omega=z^{m}
and comparing to the flow associated with Hamiltonian
\widetilde{H}
. We study our new local flow separately on each angular sector of
\mathcal{D}
, which is the image of
[-1,1]^{2}
through any branch of the
m
-th root.

Let
G_{0}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be the principal branch of the
m
-th root
G_{0}(re^{\iota t})=r^{1/m}e^{\iota t/m}
if
t\in[-\pi,\pi
) and let
\zeta

be the principal
2m
-th
root of unity.
For any
0\leq l<2m
let
G_{l}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be given by
G_{l}=\zeta^{l}G_{0}
.

As
z^{\prime}=-2i\frac{\partial H}{\partial\bar{z}}(z,\bar{z})
, in new coordinates
\omega=z^{m}
, we have

(A.12)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \omega^{\prime}&=mz^{m-1}z^{\prime}=-2imz^{m-1}\frac{\partial H}{\partial\bar{z}}(z,\bar{z})=-2imz^{m-1}\frac{d}{d\bar{z}}\widetilde{H}(z^{m},\bar{z}^{m})\\
&=-2im^{2}|z|^{2(m-1)}\frac{\partial\widetilde{H}}{\partial\bar{\omega}}(z^{m},\bar{z}^{m})=-2im^{2}|\omega|^{2\frac{m-1}{m}}\frac{\partial\widetilde{H}}{\partial\bar{\omega}}(\omega,\bar{\omega}).\end{aligned}

Suppose that
\widetilde{H}(x,y)=-H_{1}(x)+H_{2}(y)
, where
H_{1},H_{2}
are even
C^{2}
-maps as in Section
A.1
. Choose

We will deal with the case where

(A.16)

\displaystyle\varphi_{f_{k,l}\circ\zeta^{j}}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\zeta^{j(k-l)}}{m^{2}}\Re\int_{s}^{s_{0}}\frac{\Big(\frac{G_{0}(H^{-1}_{1}(u)+iH_{2}^{-1}(u-s))}{G_{0}(H^{-1}_{1}(u)-iH_{2}^{-1}(u-s))}\Big)^{k-(m-1)}}{H^{\prime}_{1}(H^{-1}_{1}(u))H^{\prime}_{2}(H_{2}^{-1}(u-s))}du.

Suppose that

Lemma A.6
.

Suppose that
H_{2}(y)=y^{2}
and
H_{1}
is an even
C^{2}
-map such that
(H^{-1}_{1})^{\prime}(u)=\frac{g(-\log u)}{\sqrt{u}}>0
. Let
k,l\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that
k+l=2(m-1)
. Then
\varphi_{f_{k,l}\circ\zeta^{j}}:(0,s_{0}]\to\mathbb{C}
is a
C^{2}
-map such that

(A.17)

\varphi^{\prime}_{f_{k,l}\circ\zeta^{j}}(s)=O(\tau(s)^{-1})\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{s\to 0}\tau(s)\varphi^{\prime}_{f_{k,l}}(s)=-\frac{\zeta^{2j(k-(m-1))}}{m^{2}}.

Proof.

As
k+l=2(m-1)
, by (
A.16
), we have

\varphi_{f_{k,l}\circ\zeta^{j}}(s)-\zeta^{j(k-l)}\varphi_{f_{m-1,m-1}}(s)=\int_{s}^{s_{0}}\frac{2\psi\left(\frac{H_{2}^{-1}(u-s)}{H^{-1}_{1}(u)}\right)\frac{H_{2}^{-1}(u-s)}{H^{-1}_{1}(u)}}{H^{\prime}_{1}(H^{-1}_{1}(u))H^{\prime}_{2}(H_{2}^{-1}(u-s))}du,

where
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
is an analytic map given by

\psi(x)=\zeta^{j(k-l)}\Re\frac{1}{m^{2}x}\left(\frac{(G_{0}(1+ix))^{k-(m-1)}}{(G_{0}(1-ix))^{k-(m-1)}}-1\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\varphi_{f_{k,l}\circ\zeta^{j}}(s)-\zeta^{j(k-l)}\varphi_{f_{m-1,m-1}}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{s}^{s_{0}}\frac{\psi\left(\frac{\sqrt{u-s}}{H^{-1}_{1}(u)}\right)\cdot\frac{\sqrt{u-s}}{H^{-1}_{1}(u)}\cdot g(-\log u)}{\sqrt{u}\sqrt{u-s}}du

\displaystyle=\int_{s}^{s_{0}}\frac{\psi(\frac{\sqrt{u-s}}{H^{-1}_{1}(u)})g(-\log u)}{\sqrt{u}H^{-1}_{1}(u)}du

with

\varphi_{f_{m-1,m-1}}(s)=\int_{s}^{s_{0}}\frac{g(-\log u)}{m^{2}\sqrt{u}\sqrt{u-s}}du.

It follows that

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}_{f_{k,l}\circ\zeta^{j}}(s)

\displaystyle-\zeta^{j(k-l)}\varphi^{\prime}_{f_{m-1,m-1}}(s)=-\frac{\psi(0)g(-\log s)}{\sqrt{s}H^{-1}_{1}(s)}+\int_{s}^{s_{0}}\frac{\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{\sqrt{u-s}}{H^{-1}_{1}(u)}\right)g(-\log u)}{2\sqrt{u}\sqrt{u-s}(H^{-1}_{1}(u))^{2}}du.

By definition,

H^{-1}_{1}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}(H^{-1}_{1})^{\prime}(u)du=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{g(-\log u)}{\sqrt{u}}du\geq\int_{0}^{x}\frac{g(-\log x)}{\sqrt{u}}du=2\sqrt{x}g(-\log x),

so

(A.18)

\frac{\sqrt{u}}{H^{-1}_{1}(u)}\leq\frac{1}{2g(-\log u)}\leq\frac{1}{2g(-\log s_{0})}\text{ for }u\in(0,s_{0}].

Moreover, by concavity of
g
, we have

\displaystyle H^{-1}_{1}(x)-2\sqrt{x}g(-\log x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{g(-\log u)-g(-\log x)}{\sqrt{u}}du=\int_{0}^{x}\int_{u}^{x}\frac{g^{\prime}(-\log v)}{v\sqrt{u}}dvdu

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{x}\frac{g^{\prime}(0)}{v}\int_{0}^{v}\frac{du}{\sqrt{u}}dv=4g^{\prime}(0)\sqrt{x}.

It follows that there exists
C_{g}>0
such that

(A.19)

2\sqrt{x}g(-\log x)\leq H^{-1}_{1}(x)\leq C_{g}\sqrt{x}g(-\log x)\text{ for all }x\in[0,s_{0}].

As
\psi
is analytic, there is
C>0
such that
|\psi^{\prime}(x)|\leq C
for
|x|\leq\frac{1}{g(-\log s_{0})}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle|\varphi^{\prime}_{f_{k,l}\circ\zeta^{j}}(s)

\displaystyle-\zeta^{j(k-l)}\varphi^{\prime}_{f_{m-1,m-1}}(s)|\leq|\psi(0)|\frac{g(-\log s)}{\sqrt{s}H^{-1}_{1}(s)}+C\int_{s}^{s_{0}}\frac{g(-\log u)}{2\sqrt{u}\sqrt{u-s}(H^{-1}_{1}(u))^{2}}du.

Moreover, by (
A.18
), it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{s_{0}}\frac{g(-\log u)}{2\sqrt{u}\sqrt{u-s}(H^{-1}_{1}(u))^{2}}du\leq\int_{s}^{s_{0}}\frac{1}{4u\sqrt{u-s}H^{-1}_{1}(u)}du

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}H^{-1}_{1}(s)}\int_{1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{4u\sqrt{u-1}}du.

Therefore,

(A.20)

\varphi^{\prime}_{f_{k,l}\circ\zeta^{j}}(s)-\zeta^{j(k-l)}\varphi^{\prime}_{f_{m-1,m-1}}(s)=O\Big(\frac{g(-\log s)}{\sqrt{s}H^{-1}_{1}(s)}\big)=O(1/s).

As
\varphi_{f_{m-1,m-1}}(s)=\int_{s}^{s_{0}}\frac{g(-\log u)}{m^{2}\sqrt{u}\sqrt{u-s}}du
, in view of (
A.7
) and (
A.8
), we have

\varphi^{\prime}_{f_{m-1,m-1}}(s)=O\Big(\frac{g(-\log s)}{s}\Big)\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{s}{g(-\log s)}\varphi^{\prime}_{f_{m-1,m-1}}(s)=-\frac{1}{m^{2}}.

Together with (
A.20
), this gives (
A.17
).
∎

.
