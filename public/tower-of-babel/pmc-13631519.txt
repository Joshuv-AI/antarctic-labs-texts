Comment on “A pragmatic trial evaluating the impact of the anumana clinical decision support tool for guideline-directed management of heart failure (ACT-HF): Clinical trial design and methods”
Authors: Bingjun Che, Lijiang Li
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the study by Lopez-Jimenez et al. [1], examining a clinical decision support tool for heart failure therapy.

A medication-count primary endpoint that does not capture titration or persistence

The primary endpoint tallies the number of guideline-directed medical therapy drug classes prescribed at 90 days without accounting for whether each medication reaches a tolerated or target dose or whether it remains active at the 90-day assessment rather than having been started and subsequently discontinued for intolerance. The manuscript cites registry data showing that reduced mortality tracks with achieving target doses of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors and beta blockers, not simply with drug-class count [2]. Against this background, a positive result on the primary endpoint could represent clinically inert prescribing, brief trials of therapy that patients cannot tolerate, or genuine sustained quadruple therapy, and the design as described cannot distinguish between these scenarios. Capturing dose level and medication persistence at 90 days, rather than class count alone, would strengthen the clinical significance of the primary comparison.

Contamination risk from clinician-level randomization within shared care teams

Clinicians are clustered and randomized at the care-team level within each of the two health systems; however, care teams routinely share patients, corridors, and informal case discussions. A clinician randomized to usual care can plausibly learn, through a colleague's software-prompted conversation or a shared electronic note, that a mutual patient has a documented gap in guideline-directed therapy [3]. This raises a consequential question about whether the trial's between-arm contrast measures the isolated effect of the software or a diluted effect after informal information leakage between intervention and control clinicians working in the same wards and clinics [4]. Prespecified contamination monitoring, such as tracking whether control-arm clinicians independently addressed the same care gaps that the software would have flagged, was absent from the described design.

Unverified baseline comparability of intervention and control clinician panels

Because clinicians rather than patients are randomized, and only two health systems and up to 250 clinicians are recruited, more than 2148 eligible clinical encounters are not distributed under any stated allocation ratio or stratification scheme tied to baseline patient comorbidity burden or existing guideline-directed therapy gaps. Cluster-randomized trials of this scale are vulnerable to chance imbalances in panel case-mix, and a control arm that happens to manage a lower-acuity patient population could produce a spurious treatment effect entirely independent of software performance. The clinical implication is direct: a baseline comparability study of encounter-level severity and pre-existing therapy gaps, alongside a prespecified adjustment strategy, is needed before the 90-day prescribing difference can be confidently attributed to the intervention.

Conclusion

Taken together, ACT-HF addresses a genuine and consequential gap in heart failure care by testing an artificial intelligence-enabled alert system against usual care. Against this background, confidence in the eventual findings will depend on whether the completed trial reports medication dose and persistence rather than class count alone, documents any contamination between clinicians sharing patients, and demonstrates the baseline comparability of the intervention and control panels.
