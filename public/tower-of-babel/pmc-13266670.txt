E-Visit Usage Among a Population of Young Adults
Authors: Abigail Eaton, Stephanie R Prausnitz, H Nicole Tran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAsynchronous electronic visits (e-visits) and telehealth may provide comparable health outcomes to in-person care in line with the implementation of value-based care.MethodsStudy population was newly enrolled individuals (ages 18–26) in an integrated health plan in 2023 (N = 30,099). In this cross-sectional study, multivariate analyses focused on covariates associated with e-visit utilization. The association of emergent and discharge status with urinary tract infection (UTI) e-visit usage was examined in the subset of emergency department (ED) visits for female patients with UTI (n = 258).ResultsDuring the first 18 months of membership, the newly enrolled 18- to 26-year-olds completed 22,483 e-visits. Needing a translator, using Medicare/Medicaid, and residing in areas with higher neighborhood deprivation indices were associated with a reduced likelihood of using e-visits. Having a chronic problem or identification of a primary care practitioner within 3 months were associated with increased likelihood of using an e-visit. In the ED subset, an association was seen between Hispanic identity and decreased likelihood of e-visit usage. A total of 2537 (11 %) of all e-visits were for UTI, and 258 (3%) of all ED visits were for UTI. Of the 258 ED visits for UTI, 95% were discharged home.DiscussionMost UTI-related ED visits were deemed not emergent and resulted in discharge home, which suggests room for improvement in delivery of value-based care. This could be achieved by increasing access to e-visits for individuals living in deprived neighborhoods, needing a translator, having Medicare/Medicaid, and identifying as Hispanic.ConclusionPromotion of e-visit utilization among new young adult members could improve value-based health care utilization and maintain clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Asynchronous electronic visits (e-visits) and telehealth may provide comparable health outcomes to in-person care in line with the implementation of value-based care.

Telehealth has been utilized as an increasing modality for care fulfillment for uncomplicated urinary tract infections (UTIs), especially since the COVID-19 pandemic. 1 A comprehensive retrospective study of real-world telemedicine programs for uncomplicated and complicated cases suggested that large-scale telemedicine programs can effectively treat UTIs using self-reported symptoms. 2 Evidence suggests that e-visits also can provide equivalent outcomes to in-person care for certain health care conditions. 3 A study conducted at an integrated health plan found that e-visits were an effective clinical modality to care for patients with acute symptoms, offering comparable follow-up rates to more traditional scheduled telemedicine visit for UTI. The authors suggest additional outreach may be needed to ensure access and awareness of e-visits. 4 This evidence is consistent with the increased implementation of value-based care in care management programs and the need for patient-centered care. 5

Patient perception of e-visits among older adults suggests that they generally accept the concept of e-visits but prefer synchronous visits. 6 The younger generation’s preferences differ from older age groups; they place a high value on convenience. 7 Young adults, with their expectations of immediate care, may well be served by e-visits. Young adults of the 18- to 26-year-old population have been shown to be more likely to use the emergency department (ED) over office-based visits for health care. 8 Patient perception of telemedicine is generally positive, with the potential to decrease emergency room utilization. 9,10

The authors examined factors associated with e-visit use among young adults (ages 18–26) newly enrolled in an integrated health care plan. The authors then examined a subset with ED visits for UTI for differences in e-visit utilization for UTI, with particular interest in the visit acuity level. The authors’ aim was to determine groups for targeted e-visit promotion, as well as to determine any potential impact on ED utilization for UTI associated with the use of e-visits for UTI.

Methods

POPULATION DESCRIPTION

The study population comprised 18- to 26-year-old, newly enrolled individuals in 2023 at Kaiser Permanente Northern California, an integrated health care system with more than 4.6 million members who demographically represent the broader population of Northern California. All data were extracted from the Kaiser Permanente Northern California Electronic Health Records. Excluded were individuals who were not health plan members for at least 18 months postenrollment, allowing for a 3-month gap. Analyses were focused on covariates that may be associated with e-visit utilization in this population.

The authors also analyzed a subset of visits by female patients in this study population to the ED with diagnoses of UTI. The time frame for both ED visits and e-visits was within 18 months after joining the health plan. The study design was cross-sectional, where the presence of e-visit was defined as any e-visit, and UTI ED visit was defined as any UTI ED visit, both within the 18 months after joining an integrated health care system. This study was approved by the Kaiser Permanente Northern California Institutional Review Board.

ANALYTIC APPROACH

A multivariate analysis was conducted to examine factors associated with e-visit utilization among the study population. Need of interpreter (yes, no, unknown), insurance type (commercial, Medicare/Medicaid, missing), sex, race and ethnicity (Asian, Black, Hispanic, Pacific Islander, Indigenous, White, other, unknown), neighborhood deprivation (a geocoded measure based on census tract representing socioeconomic status where higher values indicated greater deprivation), 11 designation of a primary care practitioner (PCP) within 3 months of joining, and chronic problems indicated by a chronic condition in the clinical problem list database were included in the multivariate model. A modified Poisson distribution was used to determine incidence rates ratios using R software (v4.2.1). 12 A bivariate analysis was conducted on the ED subset to determine if an association was present between emergent acuity level in the ED and the use of e-visits for UTI. Emergent was defined as “emergent” or “resuscitative” and nonemergent as “urgent” or “nonurgent.”

Results

The study population consisted of 30,099 individuals. Table 1 shows the covariate distribution by e-visit usage. A greater proportion of those who had a PCP within 3 months used e-visits as compared with those who did not (68% vs 45%). Among individuals with chronic problems, a greater proportion used e-visits than did not (37% vs 16%). Lower proportions of individuals who used e-visits were seen in the groups representing more vulnerable populations such as those living in the most deprived neighborhood, where 23% of the group used e-visit as compared to 29% who did not. In the group of individuals needing an interpreter, 1% of the population used e-visits as compared to 5% who did not. The top 50% of e-visit topics covered were sexual health, cold/cough/sinus/sore throat/flu, urinary (bladder infection), and birth control.

Multivariate analyses found that young adults who had a PCP within 3 months of joining had a 48% increase in likelihood of using e-visits compared with young adults who did not establish a PCP within 3 months (incidence rate ratio [IRR] = 1.48; 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.40–1.56). Having a chronic problem also increased the likelihood of using e-visits (IRR = 1.53; 95% CI = 1.45–1.61). There were 258 visits for UTI to the ED during the time span of 18 months after an individual joined Kaiser Permanente. Table 3 shows the different demographic distributions among patients who had UTI ED visits. A greater proportion of UTI ED visits were associated with individuals who had a PCP within 3 months of joining the health plan (74%) as compared with those who did not (26%). However, among those with a PCP early, the proportion associated with e-visits usage was 78% compared to those visits not associated with any use of e-visits at 71%. The reverse was seen among those visits associated with individuals using Medicare/Medicaid. In those individuals, only 4% of ED UTI visits were found, and the proportion of the UTI e-visits was 2% as compared to 5% in those without UTI e-visits. This also held for visits among individuals who lived in the most deprived neighborhood quartile. This represented 31% of ED UTI visits, where 17% had UTI e-visits as compared with 38% who did not. The findings were opposite among visits associated with individuals who lived in the least deprived neighborhood quartile, where 19% of individuals with UTI ED were from this quartile. However, 39% used UTI e-visits and 8% did not. Thirty-five percent of the UTI ED visits were among Hispanic persons. However, only 18% used UTI e-visits, and 44% did not. This was flipped in the Asian population, where 18% of the UTI ED visits were among Asian patients, but a higher proportion used UTI e-visits (30%), as compared to 12% who did not. A similar pattern was seen in the White population, which encompassed 27% of the UTI ED visits. There, 39% used UTI e-visits as compared with 21% who did not. Forty-three percent of the UTI ED visits were among those who had a chronic problem; 49% of those were among patients with UTI e-visits as compared with 41% that were not.

Discussion

E-visits that allow care on first contact, known as “point of contact resolution,” may be particularly useful for acute cases, such as UTI symptoms, 13 especially among young adults who are known to expect efficient (and instant) service. 14 To better understand the urgency driving ED visits and how it may correlate with e-visit usage, the authors analyzed the characteristics of young female patients who presented to the ED for UTI, with a focus on acuity and discharge status.

Overall, Medicare/Medicaid members, those in most deprived neighborhoods, and those needing an interpreter were substantially less likely to use e-visits. This was a pattern also reflected in UTI-related ED visits. The most deprived neighborhood quartile accounted for the largest share of UTI ED visits but had the lowest e-visit utilization. Although UTI ED visits associated with those who needed an interpreter were a small proportion (7%), a similar pattern was seen with those who used e-visits (2%) vs those who did not (9%). Those residing in the most deprived quartile of neighborhoods and those requiring an interpreter comprised vulnerable populations for whom promoting the use of e-visits could substantially improve access.

In the UTI ED analyses, individuals identifying as Hispanic comprised 35% of the UTI ED visits, compared to 22% of the overall study population. Within the Hispanic group, only 18% used e-visits, and 44% did not, indicating that Hispanic patients may face barriers to accessing e-visit services. Providing targeted support to help these patients navigate and utilize e-visits could enhance their health care experience. More broadly, focusing on vulnerable groups within this study population, many of whom are new to integrated health care, could improve patient outcomes and strengthen the health care system.

Individuals with chronic conditions or who had a PCP within 3 months were more likely to use e-visits within 3 months of joining. Their early engagement may reflect greater adeptness at navigating health care, either due to the ongoing demands of managing a chronic condition or the support provided by having an established PCP.

Most notably, the acuity level for all ED visits for UTI with e-visit use was entirely skewed to nonemergent compared to 11% in the group without e-visit use who had an emergent acuity level. Similarly, all ED visits for UTI associated with individuals who used UTI e-visits were discharged to home, as compared with 8% of the population of UTI ED visits not using e-visits with a discharge status other than home. This could reflect the fact that patients using e-visits had better access or continuity of care in managing an acute situation.

Limitations of this study included the fact that the causal association as to what drives utilization of e-visits cannot be ascertained from a cross-sectional examination. Moreover, although an association was seen between UTI e-visits and ED visit acuity, the causal path for this cannot be mapped out in a cross-sectional analysis, suggesting that future research is needed. The neighborhood deprivation index, which draws from ZIP code and census tract data, was used to assess socioeconomic status as a composite of variables measuring wealth and income, education, occupation, and housing conditions. How each individual component contributed to e-visit usage was not determined in this study.

Conclusion

E-visits are shown in the literature 13 to be oriented toward value-based care by serving patients through point of contact resolution. The level of care in the ED for UTI for individuals who used UTI e-visits was never seen to be emergent, in contrast to a subset of those who did not utilize e-visits presenting with emergent conditions. This suggested augmented care from these asynchronous visits. Vulnerable groups included those residing in areas with higher neighborhood deprivation indices, those needing a translator, those with Medicare/Medicaid, and those of Hispanic ethnicity. These groups were less likely to use e-visits compared to those in other groups. Promotion of e-visit utilization among new young adult members could improve value-based health care utilization, maintain clinical outcomes, and help meet this demographic’s expectation of convenience.
