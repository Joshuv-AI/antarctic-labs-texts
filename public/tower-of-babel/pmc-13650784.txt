Dietary and Ranging Behavior of Semi‐Free Ranging Lemur catta and Varecia rubra at Myakka City Lemur Reserve, Florida, USA
Authors: Ethan Gulledge, Hannah L Lyles, Lydia E O Light, Taylor Fisher, Elizabeth Gandy, Bruce Holst, Timothy D Bransford, Erik R Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLemurs are severely threatened due to anthropogenic habitat loss and climate change. Therefore, understanding how lemurs adapt their diets to novel habitats is critically important for maintaining healthy wild populations and effectively managing captive populations. Here we present two case studies of the dietary and ranging behavior of semi‐free ranging ring‐tailed lemurs (Lemur catta; two groups) and red ruffed lemurs (Varecia rubra; two groups) at the Myakka City Lemur Reserve managed by the Lemur Conservation Foundation. We found that ring‐tailed lemurs spent 64.5% of their time resting and 13.4% foraging and feeding. The most important food types for ring‐tailed lemurs were mature leaves (55.9%) and detritus (12.6%); however, they consumed nine other food types, including living animals (0.7%). Ring‐tailed lemurs consumed 14 plant species, 9 of which have not previously been reported. Home ranges for the two ring‐tailed lemur groups were 0.18 ha and 0.20 ha, representing 9.7% and 11.4% of their forested enclosures. Ring‐tailed lemurs foraged primarily within one meter from the ground. Red ruffed lemurs spent 44.9% resting and 29.6% foraging and feeding. The most important food types for red ruffed lemurs were young leaves (32.9%), mature leaves (22.0%), flower buds (15.7%), and unripe fruit (11.9%) with only three other food types consumed. Red ruffed lemurs consumed 43 different species, all of which have not been previously reported. Home ranges for the two red ruffed lemur groups were 0.83 ha and 0.87 ha representing 40.8% and 55.9% of their forested enclosures. Red ruffed lemurs also foraged primarily within one meter from the ground. This is one of very few published studies on semi‐free ranging ring‐tailed lemurs outside Madagascar and the first dietary study on semi‐free ranging red ruffed lemurs outside Madagascar. We hope that these results can be used to inform management practices among captive lemur populations, other primates, and other taxa living in novel environments.Keywords: diet, foraging behavior, semi‐free ranging lemurs

Introduction

Madagascar is an island that is heavily impacted by anthropogenic climate change (Brown and Yoder 2015), and the island's vast diversity of species faces an uncertain future (Ralimanana et al. 2022). There are more than 100 recognized species of lemurs living on Madagascar, 98% of which are threatened with extinction (IUCN 2020; Mittermeier et al. 2023), rendering ex‐situ conservation actions increasingly important for the long‐term survival of these species. Through sponsoring cooperative breeding programs such as the AZA's Species Survival Plan (SSP), genetic variation is maintained within captive populations to prevent a decrease in reproductive success as well as other health‐related issues due to inbreeding within familial social units (Putnam et al. 2023). Understanding how lemur species adapt their diets to novel habitats offers insight into how these lemurs, and ecologically similar species, may respond to their rapidly changing environment in the wild as well as how to best care for them in captivity (Ratsimbazafy 2006).

Furthermore, ex‐situ conservation initiatives are critically important for a wide variety of taxa, and studies with lemurs can provide insight into other social mammals living in novel habitats such as zoos and other conservation‐centered facilities. For example, Reimes et al. 2021 noted a trend within European Association of Zoos and Aquaria (EAZA) facilities showing a disproportionate representation of lemur species compared to those represented in the wild and found that IUCN status did not correlate with presence in these captive settings. McGowan et al. (2017) suggested that oftentimes the decision to integrate at‐risk species into an SSP is made too late to have an effective impact. If these issues are noted regarding lemur species representation in association with conservation needs, one can assume it is likely that said issues are applicable to other species as well.

Ring‐tailed lemurs are Endangered omnivores found in a large variety of Southern Madagascar habitats, including dry forests, spiny bush, mangroves, gallery forests, rocky outcrops, and occasionally rainforest (Mittermeier et al. 2023). In gallery forests, their home ranges can vary from roughly 4–17 hectares, while in drier habitats, they can be up to roughly 32 hectares (Gould 2006). They are the most terrestrial lemur and their diet is primarily folivorous/frugivorous but have a broad diet in the wild, especially during the dry season, with leaves and fruit being the primary ingested items, and supplemented by spiders, caterpillars, cicadas, birds, and soil (LaFleur and Gould 2020; Sauther et al. 1999; Simmen et al. 2003; Soma 2006). More specifically, their diet in the wild consists of young leaves, mature leaves, ripe fruit with seeds, unripe fruit, flowers, floral buds, wood, and other plant matter. Their extremely diverse wild diet includes 241 known food species with considerable site variability (Canington 2021). And while many of the foods eaten are endemic to Madagascar, more common food species like Tamarindus indica and Ficus polita have been identified as key food resources due to their year‐round availability (LaFleur 2012; Simmen et al. 2006; Sussman 1972).

To our knowledge, few studies have published foraging data for semi‐free ranging lemurs outside their native habitats. One such study on Saint Catherine's Island in the US state of Georgia observed ring‐tailed lemurs eating 31 different species, most of which are not found in Madagascar (Dierenfeld and McCann 1999). A second study here reported ring‐tailed lemurs foraging on 16 species of locally available plants but failing to forage on several other species that were highly available (Keith‐Lucas et al. 1999). A study of ring‐tailed lemurs at the Duke Lemur Center reported the lemurs consuming a variety of plant parts from the local flora, particularly those with high sugar or fiber contents, and suggests this reflects dietary patterns found in the wild (Greene et al. 2024). Another study at the Duke Lemur Center found that ring‐tailed lemurs spent significantly more time foraging for natural foods than ingesting provisioned fruits and monkey chow (Ganzhorn 1986). Ganzhorn also notes that the ring‐tailed lemurs at Duke Lemur Center had similar dietary patterns as those in the wild, subsisting on a broad omnivorous diet that included more leaves, bark, and woody plants as well as less fruit than that of sympatric Eulemur fulvus. These studies demonstrate the ability of ring‐tailed lemurs to adjust their diets dramatically based on the food options available but also emphasize the importance of systematic studies at very local scales.

Ring‐tailed lemurs and red ruffed lemurs differ widely in their habitats and behaviors. The red ruffed lemur is a Critically Endangered lemur species (Borgerson et al. 2020) that lives in the tropical rainforests of the Masoala Peninsula of Madagascar, preferring the canopy of the forest to the ground. Home ranges vary from roughly 13–58 hectares in the wild and are highly dependent on group population size (Vasey 1997). The red ruffed lemur is the largest existing member of the Lemuridae family and is a highly frugivorous species, spending up to 90% of their feeding time on fruit with significantly less time spent feeding on flowers, nectar, and leaves (Vasey 2003). In one study, red ruffed lemurs were observed eating parts of 132 different plant species from 36 families of plants (Vasey 2003). Even though the red ruffed lemur is one of the most frugivorous lemur species, they are able to supplement their diet through increased leaf foraging during seasons in which fruit is less readily available and conserve energy during fruit‐scarce times by decreasing travel through their home range (Vasey et al. 2018). Their fission–fusion social structure and infant‐parking behavior allow for the species to produce offspring during the most fruit‐rich times of year leading to rich milk production and relatively fast maturation of offspring, reducing pressure on females during fruit‐scarce times (Vasey et al. 2018).

While there has been significant research on the dietary and ranging habits of red ruffed lemurs in wild habitats, much less is known about what, if any, differences are seen in captive populations. To our knowledge, there are no published studies on the diet of free‐ranging red ruffed lemurs in forests outside of Madagascar. Naturalistic captive environments allow ex‐situ populations to experience some of the challenges they would face in the wild and encourage similar feeding behaviors to what is seen with in‐situ populations (A. Britt 1998). Studies have investigated the nutritional composition of provisioned captive diets and impacts on red ruffed lemur health. The availability of an appropriate diet is important to ensure the health of ex‐situ populations and inappropriate diets can lead to increased incidence of disease. Obesity and iron storage disease have both been seen in captive ruffed lemur populations and inappropriate diets can lead to nutrient deficiencies, resulting in reproductive disorders and increased mortality (Carravaggi et al. 2018). Understanding what a free‐ranging population is choosing to consume within their given environment could allow for further understanding of their dietary needs, especially considering that the diets of wild populations are not necessarily representative of their optimal dietary requirements and nutrient composition of captive lemur diets may differ widely from their wild counterparts (Carravaggi et al. 2018; Donadeo et al. 2016; S. Britt et al. 2015).

Ranging and foraging behavior for this species in the wild are likely impacted by distribution of food sources and reproductive patterns in both species, while daily patterns of these behaviors are related to circadian rhythms of plants and predator avoidance strategies (Vasey 2004, 2006). It has been shown that in‐situ red ruffed lemurs populations exhibit daily diet patterns, largely consuming fruit earlier in the day and transitioning to more non‐fruit items later in the day, likely in an effort to consume protein‐rich foods that also have a high carbohydrate value (Vasey 2004).

Here we present two case studies examining the dietary and ranging habits of two lemur species, ring‐tailed lemurs (Lemur catta) and red ruffed lemurs (Varecia rubra), that are housed at the Lemur Conservation Foundation's Myakka City Lemur Reserve in Southwest Florida in an effort to compare their behaviors to those of in‐situ populations. For these descriptive case studies, we researched the foraging diets of semi‐free ranging ring‐tailed and red ruffed lemurs at the Lemur Conservation Foundation (LCF). We aimed to determine what plant species the lemurs were eating and what percentage of their time during the day was spent foraging. We also wanted to investigate their home range size and determine how much of their enclosures they were using. Given the general paucity of data among semi‐free ranging lemurs, we made the following predictions based primarily on the diet and behaviors of wild lemurs discussed above and facility staff's familiarity with the subjects' behavioral patterns:

Prediction A: For the ring‐tailed lemur case study, a majority of the foraged plant material would consist of leaf or leaf‐like foliage material.

In Florida, foliage is in abundance during the summer season, which led to our prediction that the ring‐tailed lemurs would ingest a significant amount of leaves as opposed to other materials. We also predicted that young leaves would be ingested at a greater frequency than mature leaves due to the increased availability during the growing season, as seen in other species of wild lemurs (Campera et al. 2021), as well as the higher nutritional quality of young leaves for primates broadly (Glander 1982; Chapman et al. 2003). Plant species were also recorded to compare with previous studies done in the Southeastern United States.

Prediction B: For the red ruffed lemur case study, ingested items would be largely composed of fruit and flowers.

With red ruffed lemurs being frugivores, we predicted that the early spring season would allow for the lemurs to forage large amounts of floral buds, flowers, and unripe fruit. As has been seen in the wild, we also believed that the lemurs would supplement their foraging diets with both young and mature leaves.

Prediction C: Lemurs of both species would forage with predictable spatial patterns.

With the lemurs being fed by the LCF staff in the locations where they sleep, one of our predictions was that the lemurs would remain close to these locations. Areas that we predicted might be foraging hotspots were near the feeding structure and near the fence gates. Due to the structure of the slash pine forest at this site and damage sustained from recent hurricanes, the remaining tree canopy is sparse and often discontinuous throughout the enclosure. Therefore, we predicted that the red ruffed lemurs would be limited to a smaller portion of the enclosure than available.

Prediction D: Both species will forage at heights based on the vertical placement of their food sources.

Ring‐tailed lemurs are primarily terrestrial. Therefore, we predicted that most of what they forage for would be on or near the ground. This includes anything within a one‐meter range above ground level.

Red ruffed lemurs are known as a canopy species in both wild and captive populations. While the canopy of the slash pine forest in Florida is significantly less dense than other forest types, the availability of raised walkways and structures and many mid‐canopy plant species in the LCF forests led us to predict that most of the red ruffed lemur foraging would occur above 0.5 meters.

Materials and Methods

Study Site

Both case studies presented here were located at the Lemur Conservation Foundation's Myakka City Lemur Reserve. The LCF was founded in 1996 with the goal of preservation and conservation of Madagascar's many endemic primates. The Florida site consists of 130 acres (52 hectares) and contains two enclosed forests with semi‐free ranging lemurs, with one of those forests split into two adjacent forest enclosures, Dome forest (1.556 ha) and Tower forest (2.033 ha), which were the focus of this study. Each forest is connected by 20‐meter‐long aerial walkways to the North building where indoor enclosures can be freely accessed. Both forests represent a slash pine (Pinus elliotti) flatwoods ecosystem, with wet flatwoods grading into mesic flatwoods with occasional hardwoods, that hosts a variety of Florida native plant species, which the lemurs forage for in addition to their provisioned foods (Marie Selby Botanical Gardens 2017). Both lemur species forage in the forest enclosures in addition to their daily provisioned diets which consist of primate chow and fresh fruits and vegetables. The lemurs also have access to several water containers for drinking in the North building enclosures as well as near the entrances to both forest enclosures. Neither of the two forests contained many fruiting trees during the study periods.

Study Subjects

Ring‐Tailed Lemurs

Ring‐tailed lemurs have been consistently described as female‐dominant with stable female hierarchies and dynamic but enduring male hierarchies (Sauther et al. 1999); therefore, we limited our focal subjects to these dominant individuals. The Dome ring‐tailed group included five individuals. Sobe (female, 11 years old) and Molson (male, 19 years old) were chosen as the focal animals for the Dome group due to their roles as the dominant female and male of the group. They are fed in the Dome structure within the forest. The Tower ring‐tailed group included three individuals, with Ansell (female, 19 years old) and Yuengling (male, 30 years old) chosen as the focal animals for the Tower group. They receive their provisioned food inside their enclosed space within the keeper's building.

Red Ruffed Lemurs

The Dome red ruffed group consisted of four individuals, all of which were observed. The individuals included Ravina (female, 16 years old), Tsambiky (female, 2 years old), Rivotra (male, 15 years old), and Tsikey (male, 24 years old). The Tower red ruffed lemur group consisted of five individuals, of which the four adults were chosen for observation. Those individuals were Zazabe (female, 7 years old), Mangoky (female, 3 years old), Onilahy (female, 3 years old), and Ranomamy (male, 5 years old). Both red ruffed lemur groups received their provisioned diets in the North building enclosure, which is outside of the semi‐free ranging forest enclosures. As red‐ruffed lemurs do not exhibit dominance hierarchies, we did not incorporate this in our sampling methods.

Data Collection

We followed a total of four social groups, two groups from each species, housed in two multi‐acre forested enclosures (“Dome” and “Tower” forests). Each forest housed one group from each species. For both species, we spent 12 weeks collecting data, alternating weeks with each social group (6 weeks of data for each social group). The schedule for focal individuals rotated each week with a different individual starting on Monday and alternating or rotating between the focal animals each day.

Ring‐Tailed Lemurs

Data were collected from May 23, 2022 to August 12, 2022. We made every effort to be in the enclosures 4 days a week between 8 a.m. and 12 p.m. to collect foraging and behavioral data, but this schedule was heavily reliant on the lemurs' scheduled management routines as well as the weather or other instances that made observations not possible. We originally attempted to collect data until 3 p.m., but the lemurs predictably reduced their activities after 12 p.m. when heat indices were higher (Gammon 2021), and they stayed inactive and located in shade or inside structures during this period. The Tower ring‐tailed lemur group was unavailable for a 2‐week period, so we observed the Dome group instead. This resulted in a total of 149 h of focal observations for the ring‐tailed lemurs.

Red Ruffed Lemurs

Data were collected from January 15th, 2024 to April 9th, 2024, 5 days per week. We shifted our data collection time to between 10 a.m. and 3 p.m. for this study period due to cooler temperatures and feedback from facility staff. This project concluded with 128 h of focal observation.

For all groups, we conducted behavioral observations using instantaneous focal animal sampling (Altmann 1974) with observations at 5‐min intervals, and we switched to continuous focal sampling as foraging bouts were initiated. As the study subjects were well habituated, observations of both the ring‐tailed lemurs and red ruffed lemurs occurred within less than a three‐meter distance at all times except for rare occurrences when the red ruffed lemurs climbed into the canopy; this occurred twice and the subjects were no more than four meters from the observer before returning to ground level within 5 min. We recorded behavioral data on smartphones using CyberTracker (Tacha et al. 1985) behavioral observation recording software. Two of us (E. G. and T. F.) recorded data concurrently during the ring‐tailed lemur case study while another (H. L.) recorded data separately during the red ruffed lemur case study. We ran two interobserver reliability analyses between E. G. and T. F. at the beginning and end of the ring‐tailed data collection, which both resulted in 90% agreement. While it was not possible to run interobserver reliability tests with HL as she was not present at the facility at the same time as E. G. and T. F., both E. G. and H. L. were trained in primatological field methods by LEOL during UNC Charlotte's Primate Field Methods course previously offered every 2 years at the Lemur Conservation Foundation's Myakka City Lemur Reserve.

Our ethogram for both species contained 35 common behaviors as well as 16 plant terminologies and was used to create a general activity budget. The recorded behaviors were divided into categories such as drinking, feeding, foraging, locomotion, mutual grooming, other, rest, scent marking, and self‐grooming. For foraging behavior, we recorded the ingested items as detritus, exudate, floral bud, flower, living animal, mature leaf, ripe fruit, roots, seeds, soil, stem, unripe fruit, wood, and young leaf. For each feeding bout, start and stop times were recorded to the nearest second, using the repeat timer feature on Timex Ironman digital watches. We defined detritus as any decaying plant matter, such as dead leaves, sticks, fallen branches, or dislodged tree bark. Once a month, botanists from Marie Selby Botanical Gardens (Selby Gardens) in Sarasota visited LCF to formally identify any plants that we had flagged to the species level wherever possible.

We also recorded the vertical placement of foraged items by estimating the height of the lemur's mouth from the ground while foraging, aside from when they were ingesting items on the ground. We recorded the vertical foraging position as ground level, 0–0.5 m high, 0.5–1 m high, and > 1 m high. We marked both flagging tape and utility flags with individual identification numbers using a permanent marker and we tied the flagging tape to the food source as soon as the focal animal had left the location. We made every effort to flag the area of the plant that was consumed, and we placed a utility flag in the ground at the base of plant items in which tying the tape was not practical. GPS points were taken at intervals of 20 min for the ring‐tailed lemurs and 30 min for the red ruffed lemurs using a Garmin GPSMAP 66SR or a Garmin Montana 680 (< 1.8 m, 95% accuracy) to map foraging locations within the forests. GPS locations were always taken within one meter of the subject or where the subject had been located. The same number marked on the flagging tape was used to mark the foraging location on the handheld GPS systems and later imported into ArcGIS Pro version 3.2 for analysis. We created home ranges per individual and group using the minimum convex hull polygon method.

Ethical Note

All research for this project was approved by the Lemur Conservation Foundation's Scientific Advisory Board and Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Study subjects were not trapped, darted, or physically contacted in any way, and all behavioral data were collected through observation only.

Results

Ring‐Tailed Lemur Case Study

Activity Budgets

Overall, ring‐tailed lemurs spent the majority of their time, an average of 64.45% resting, 8.07% foraging, 7.28% locomoting, 5.36% feeding, and 14.85% engaged in other activities (Figure 1). The Dome group spent 61.49% of their time resting, 9.15% foraging, 8.4% locomoting, 6.6% feeding, 4.79% mutual grooming, 2.87% self‐grooming, 3.51% scent marking, 1.28% drinking, and 1.91% of time spent on other activities. The Tower group spent 67.79% of their time resting, 6.85% foraging, 6.01% locomoting, 3.97% feeding, 5.89% mutual grooming, 6.97% self‐grooming, 0.84% scent marking, 0.36% drinking, and 1.32% of their time spent on other activities.

Dietary Items

Overall, mature leaves accounted for 55.94% of the total dietary items consumed by the ring‐tailed lemur groups. Following mature leaves, detritus accounted for 12.59%, young leaves 8.39%, unripe fruit 8.39%, wood 5.59%, soil 3.5%, seeds 2.1%, stems 1.4%; living animals, ripe fruit, and roots all accounted for 0.7% of dietary items. Some group differences in diet were observed. The Dome group consumed a notably larger percentage of mature leaves (62.79%) compared to the Tower group (45.61%). Only the Dome group consumed seeds (3.49%) and stems (2.33%). Only the Tower group consumed roots (1.75%), ripe fruit (1.75%), or living animals (1.75%). Soil was only consumed by Yuengling, the male from the Tower group, with 5 recorded events (8.77%). Yuengling was also the only individual to consume a living animal; during one observation, he caught and ate the upper half of a grasshopper (Table 1).

Plant Species Foraged

We observed a total of 14 identified species foraged by the ring‐tailed lemurs, with some variation between the 2 groups due to a difference in plant availability in their enclosures (Table 2). Four species accounted for 51.71% of foraging time for ring‐tailed lemurs. These species were Carolina redroot (Lachnanthes caroliniana; 17.72%), wax myrtle (Morella cerifera; 15.66%), laurel oak (Quercus laurifolia; 10.78%, not observed in the Tower group), and saw palmetto (Serenoa repens; 7.55%). The first three of these were mostly foraged for leaves, while unripe fruit was mostly ingested when the lemurs were foraging on saw palmetto. Soil is also included in the table because adult male Yuengling spent a total of 218 s licking/ingesting it throughout the project. The lemurs were observed eating nine species that have not previously been reported as dietary items: Chinese dwarf bamboo (Bambusa multiplex; 0.27%), Mexican primrose willow (Ludwigia octovalvis; 0.36%), American elm (Ulmus americana; 0.37%), Lilac tasselflower (Emilia sonchifolia; 0.81%), Dwarf huckleberry (Gaylussacia dumosa; 1.4%), Carolina willow (Salix caroliniana; 1.96%), Saw palmetto (S. repens; 7.55%), Laurel oak (10.78%), and Carolina redroot (17.72%).

Area Usage

The Dome ring‐tailed lemur group only used 0.178 hectares of space within their 1.556‐hectare enclosure and mostly stayed within close proximity to the dome itself. The Tower ring‐tailed lemur group used only 0.198 hectares of space out of their 2.033‐hectare enclosure. Overall, the Dome ring‐tailed lemur group used 11.4% of their enclosure, and the Tower ring‐tailed lemur group used roughly 9.7% of the enclosure (Figure 2).

Vertical Foraging

Contrary to our prediction, most foraged items (87.41%) were within a one‐meter range from the ground (Figure 3). Within that range, the lemurs mostly foraged between 0 and 0.5 meters above ground, with 80 recorded instances out of 143 recorded foraging events (55.95%). The second largest category for ingested items was at ground level with 36 records (25.17%). We recorded nine foraging events between 0.5 and 1 meter (6.29%) and 18 events exceeding one meter above the ground (12.59%).

Red Ruffed Lemur Case Study

Activity Budgets

Red ruffed lemurs spent 44.89% of their time resting, 27.40% foraging, 14.36% locomoting, 2.19% feeding, and 11.16% engaged in other activities (Figure 1). The Dome forest group spent 44.57% of their time resting, 27.86% foraging, 10.45% locomoting, 1.39% feeding, 0.97% mutually grooming, 6.96% self‐grooming, 1.67% scent marking, 1.67% drinking, and 4.46% on other activities. The Tower forest group spent 45.29% of their time resting, 26.82% foraging, 17.94% locomoting, 3.20% feeding, 0.18% mutually grooming, 3.91% self‐grooming, 0% scent marking, 1.24% drinking, and 1.42% on other activities.

Dietary Items

The most frequently ingested items for red ruffed lemurs were young leaves with 166 events (32.94%), mature leaves at 111 events (22.02%), and floral buds with 79 events (15.67%). Unripe fruit was the fourth most ingested item at 60 events (11.90%), however, ripe fruit only accounted for 3 foraging events (0.60%). Of note is that soil and detritus are the fifth and sixth most ingested items with 29 and 19 events, respectively and accounting for 9.52% of the red ruffed lemur foraging diet (Table 1). There were no significant differences in the consumption of dietary items between the Dome and Tower groups.

Plant Species Foraged

For red ruffed lemurs, we observed a total of 43 species foraged (Table 3). The four main plant species foraged were wax myrtle (M. cerifera; 23.18%), tenangle pipewort (Eriocaulon decangulare; 12.71%), highbush blueberry (Vaccinium corymbosum; 2.42%), and saw palmetto (S. repens; 8.48%), which made up 56.79% of the foraging time for red ruffed lemurs. Wax myrtle and tenangle pipewort were mainly foraged for leaves, while highbush blueberry was largely foraged for floral buds and unripe fruit, and saw palmetto for unripe fruit. While Carolina redroot (0.94%) was not available in the forested enclosures until the last 1.5 weeks of observation, this plant species accounted for 5.33% of the Tower red ruffed lemur group's diet. The red ruffed lemurs in this study were observed consuming plant species that are known to be toxic to domestic animals. Both groups foraged on poison ivy (Toxicodendron radicans; 0.42%) and the Dome group foraged on Brazilian pepper (Schinus terebinthifolius; 1.97%). We did not observe any negative effects from the consumption of these items.

Area Usage

The red ruffed lemurs in the Dome forest used 0.87 hectares within the 1.556‐hectare space. The red ruffed lemurs in the Tower forest used 0.83 hectares of the 2.033 available hectares. These measurements show that the Dome group used 55.9% of their available space while the Tower group used 40.8% of the enclosure (Figure 2).

Vertical Foraging

Red ruffed lemurs largely foraged items that were less than one meter above the ground (Figure 3). Out of the 345 total recorded foraging events, 188 occurred at ground level, making it the most common vertical placement for foraging (54.49%). The second most common placement of ingested items happened between 0 and 0.5 meters with 84 recorded events (24.35%). Another 45 events occurred at 0.5–1 meters (13.04%), and only 28 foraging events happened above one meter (8.12%).

Discussion

These two case studies aimed to increase our understanding of the diet and behavior of semi‐free ranging lemurs living in forested areas outside of their native Madagascar to better understand the extent of their ecological flexibility. We analyzed data from two groups for two separate species. Below, we discuss our findings by topic, rather than by species, to emphasize both species' responses to their non‐native habitats. While we acknowledge that these two case studies are not directly comparable due to seasonal differences in the study periods, we observed a considerable amount of foraging and exploratory behavior from all groups, despite all subjects being provided with prepared foods that met all their nutritional needs.

In general, we view foraging in captive settings as important enrichment activities that provide animals an opportunity to develop and strengthen naturalistic behaviors (A. Britt 1998) and encourage animal care specialists to emphasize non‐nutritional benefits when planning foraging enrichment. We also acknowledge that managed populations such as this one face different challenges than lemurs living wild in Madagascar and their behaviors should not be interpreted as directly comparable; our goal here is not to seek evidence of semi‐free ranging lemurs replicating behavioral patterns of their wild counterparts but instead to document ways in which this semi‐free ranging population utilizes the novel environment even when social and nutritional needs are managed by facility staff. We did observe both species foraging on native Florida plants and recorded the movement patterns for both species. This information can be used by facility staff to document food items and ranging patterns, as these data were not available previously, and allow for future comparisons of any observed dietary changes. Secondarily, these data allow the facility to monitor the enclosures for plants that may pose health risks to the lemurs and locate any invasive plant species of interest.

Activity, Diet, and Food Species

As expected, both species spent the majority of their time resting, similar to wild populations (Vasey 2005; Ellwanger and Gould 2011). Foraging was the second most common observed behavior in all groups. Interestingly, red ruffed lemurs spent much more time foraging than did the ring‐tailed lemurs. The time spent foraging by different species can likely be explained by distinct nutritional needs and dietary requirements. Alternatively, differences in average and maximum heat may explain these differences, as the two study periods occurred at different times of the year and primates are known to reduce activity during high temperatures (Gammon 2021; Thompson and Hermann 2024). Additionally, researchers have suggested that cathemerality in lemurs may be a behavioral adaptation to heat stress (Donati and Borgognini‐Tarli 2006); however, humidity was observed to have a stronger influence than temperature on summer cathemerality among the ring‐tailed lemurs at the Myakka City Lemur Reserve (Richardson 2022). We suggest one difference that may be at play within this study is the high amount of energy that red ruffed lemur females invest in reproduction. In the wild, studies have shown that females vary and increase their diets during gestation and lactation (Vasey 2003). Data collection for this species at LCF began near the start of their breeding season and ended a few weeks before their expected birth windows.

Ring‐tailed lemurs primarily ate leaves during our study, though they mostly ingested mature leaves rather than young ones, which partially refutes the original prediction that mainly young leaves would be selected. This, however, does lie in congruence with our initial prediction of leaves being the primary ingested material for this species. Their diet did vary with a mix of other plant parts or non‐plant items as well, detritus being a large portion of it. There may also be a correlation to Yuengling's elderly age, as he was the only ring‐tailed lemur observed ingesting soil, perhaps as a dietary supplement (Semel et al. 2019). It is also surprising that during this portion of our data collection, there was only one instance where ring‐tailed lemurs were observed eating a non‐provisioned ripe fruit, but that is likely due to lack of availability due to seasonal lack of abundance. Interestingly, while Keith‐Lucas et al. (1999) did not observe ring‐tailed lemurs foraging on saw palmetto despite it being abundant on St. Catherine's Island, we did observe ring‐tailed lemurs foraging on the unripe saw palmetto berries. In contrast, the ring‐tailed lemurs at St. Catherine's Island consumed fruit and needles from slash and loblolly pine (1999) whereas the lemurs in our study only consumed woody material from slash pine and did not have any loblolly pine available.

Contrary to our initial prediction, red ruffed lemurs largely consumed young and mature leaves rather than unripe fruit and floral buds. In the wild, leaves are generally consumed to supplement the species' frugivorous diet. This difference could be explained by the fact that the lemurs are provisioned by the LCF staff and their food is mainly fruits and vegetables. The large amount of leaf foraging that was observed may have also been influenced by the availability of plants that flower and the lack of fruiting trees in these forests (Marie Selby Botanical Gardens 2017). The two red ruffed lemur groups also ingested a surprising amount of soil and detritus. In the wild, this species is known to occasionally consume soil, but it is rare considering their preference for the forest canopy. This difference may be a result of the discontinuity of the canopy in the LCF forest, resulting in the lemurs being more terrestrial. Geophagy in lemurs may alleviate gastrointestinal distress by preventing absorption of dietary toxins, provide essential minerals, protect against parasites and pathogens, or represent nonadaptive behavioral traditions. In Milne‐Edwards' sifakas (Propithecus edwardsi), clay minerals in soils may mediate gut pH and/or prevent absorption of dietary toxins from plant secondary compounds (Semel et al. 2019), although further study is needed for ring‐tailed lemurs.

Both lemur species frequently foraged on wax myrtle leaves and saw palmetto fruit. Red ruffed lemurs also exploited additional resources, consuming wax myrtle flower buds and exudate, as well as saw palmetto stems and leaves, while ring‐tailed lemurs fed on wax myrtle flowers. While ring‐tailed lemurs showed a preference for Carolina redroot, the plant was not in season until the last few weeks of red ruffed lemur data collection, making it difficult to determine if results for red ruffed lemurs reflect preference or availability. However, the Tower red ruffed lemur group did consume Carolina redroot when it began to grow, and the species ended up comprising 0.94% of the overall species diet when calculated together with the Dome red ruffed lemur group. When calculated by group, Carolina redroot comprised 5.33% of the Tower red ruffed lemur diet for the entire study period and 7.23% of the diet for the period of time during which the plant was available (the final 1.5 weeks of data collection). Although flowers and fruit did not end up being the most foraged plant parts for red ruffed lemurs, highbush blueberry did account for 12.42% of their foraging, with the lemurs largely consuming the floral buds and unripe fruit of the plant. Seasonality and plant availability in the forests, combined with the lemurs' provisioned diets, likely played a significant part in the lemurs' selection of which plant species to consume.

Spatial and Vertical Patterns

Neither species ranged broadly throughout their forest enclosures, with the red ruffed lemur groups using less than half the available area and the ring‐tailed lemur groups using roughly 10% of the available area. While it is possible that the high temperatures reduced activity and ranging patterns during the ring‐tailed lemur study period, LCF staff consider this lack of exploration to be a consistent aspect of behavior at this facility. As a result, LCF has sponsored projects investigating the effectiveness of modified enrichment to encourage the lemurs to range more broadly and have found that the lemurs remain in the most often‐used areas despite additional enrichment (Atkinson 2024). Therefore, we consider our results to be a fairly accurate representation of the lemurs' behavior year‐round. However, spatial use between ring‐tailed lemurs and red ruffed lemurs did differ in meaningful ways during these case studies.

Initially, we expected that red ruffed lemurs would be limited in their area usage due to the distribution of trees of appropriate height, but the two red ruffed lemur groups used 48.4% of their available enclosure space, while ring‐tailed lemurs only used an average of 10.5% of their enclosures. The Dome ring‐tailed lemur group tended to stay around their provisioned feeding location in the Dome forest and rarely strayed from the area. It should be noted that because the Tower ring‐tailed lemur group is fed within the keeper's building outside of the forest, they spent much less of their time in the forest itself. This made it impossible to compare the two groups directly, as the Tower group is fed separately from the forest, though their forest range is also similar in size compared to the Dome group. Both red ruffed lemur groups received their provisioned diets in the North building enclosure which is outside of the semi‐free ranging forest enclosures. Once given access, the red ruffed lemur groups used significant amounts of their available space in the forest. The Tower red ruffed lemur group was observed walking the entire perimeter of the enclosure on two separate occasions, although neither the Dome nor Tower groups spent much time toward the far areas of their spaces. Similar to the ring‐tailed lemurs, red ruffed lemurs tended to stay more toward the tunnel entrances to the North building although they roamed further throughout the day. Home ranges of in‐situ populations of ring‐tailed lemurs are much smaller than those of red ruffed lemurs (ring‐tailed lemurs: 4–32 ha, Gould 2006; red ruffed lemurs: 13–58 ha, Vasey 1997), and we recorded the same pattern here (ring‐tailed lemurs: 0.178 ha and 0.198 ha; red ruffed lemurs: 0.83 ha and 0.87 ha). This difference could also have been influenced by the fact that the red ruffed lemur data collection coincided with their breeding season. Copulation and attempted copulations were recorded at locations further from the building entrances than most other behaviors. Nest building by the pregnant females also tended to occur in areas away from the main building. The fission‐fusion social structure of red ruffed lemurs also lends itself to the groups using more of their available forested enclosures. Solitary travel of individuals away from others in the group for extended periods of time was regularly observed, similar to the observations of Pereira et al. (1988).

The vertical placement of ingested items for ring‐tailed lemurs was mostly within a one‐meter range from ground level. Ring‐tailed lemurs spent much of their time on the ground but foraged for items mostly at 0.5 meters above the ground. Red ruffed lemurs largely foraged items below 0.5 meters, with the majority of those events happening at ground level. These results differ significantly from our prediction of the vertical placement of foraged food items for this species.

Conclusion

The primary goal of our project was to provide the LCF with foraging data for the two lemur species that have semi‐free ranging access in the forest enclosures on site. This data would serve to help LCF increase their care options for the lemurs by allowing them to use this information to maintain these foraged species within the forest, as well as provide information as to what the lemurs are eating, should they get sick. Another goal of our project was to understand how semi‐free ranging lemur diets differ from their in‐situ counterparts. The biggest takeaway from this project is that both ring‐tailed lemurs and red ruffed lemurs are versatile species that are able to adapt to eating plant species not found on Madagascar, which can assist in future studies at other semi‐free ranging or captive sites. In addition, this is the first study on the diet and ranging behavior of semi‐free‐ranging red ruffed lemurs in forests outside Madagascar, which further exemplifies the importance of and need for dietary studies such as this one.

Beyond lemurs, ex‐situ conservation projects with a wide variety of species may be interested in our findings here. Primates are an intelligent and diverse group of social mammals, many of which exhibit female dominance that contributes to overall group behavior, such as feeding and reproduction. Other social mammals living in captive settings may show equal potential for utilizing native flora if provided in their enclosures, especially given the lack of nutritional necessity in our case studies. Furthermore, providing captive populations with a more naturalistic environment enriches their surroundings, supporting their needs in addition to the needs of local wildlife, which also use their enclosures, arguably leading toward healthier animals and ecosystems.
