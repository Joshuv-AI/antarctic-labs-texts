Treating central sleep apnoea in heart failure: is positive airway pressure and adaptive servo-ventilation in particular the gold standard?

To the Editor:

We read with great interest the review article by Randerath et al. [1] recently published in the European Respiratory Review. We would like to congratulate the authors on this clearly structured review, which emphasises the urgent need for an increasingly differentiated view of central sleep apnoea (CSA) in the context of precision medicine.

In recent years research in the field of CSA has developed rapidly. In particular, in patients with heart failure due to reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) randomised controlled endpoint trials are now available. In 2005, Bradley et al. [2] presented the first large therapy study with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), which found no superiority regarding the primary endpoint of mortality in 128 patients on CPAP versus 130 patients without CPAP therapy.

This was attributed to insufficient suppression of apnoea and hypopnoea events [3] which is why, in the following years, much research was carried out in the field of adaptive servo-ventilation (ASV). ASV is highly effective in suppressing CSA. However, in the SERVE-HF study, which randomised 1325 patients, ASV therapy showed no difference in the combined primary endpoint and, alarmingly, even showed a significant increase in all-cause mortality in the ASV therapy group by 28% and cardiovascular mortality by 34% [4]. Besides ASV showing a significant decrease in central apnoeas and hypopnoeas, hypoxaemic burden was not addressed appropriately. The resulting contraindication for ASV therapy in patients with CSA and symptomatic HFrEF with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤45% is still valid in many countries today.

Another recently published study on ASV therapy, the ADVENT-HF trial, started in 2010 and was stopped prematurely in March 2021 (after 64.1% of the planned endpoint events) due to the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic [5]. In contrast to the SERVE-HF trial, it recruited HFrEF patients with both obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) and CSA. The majority of recruited HFrEF patients had OSA (n=533), and ultimately 198 patients had CSA, of whom 92 were assigned to ASV. This study found no difference in the combined primary endpoint, but also no significance in all-cause mortality.

Even if methodological weaknesses of the SERVE-HF study are mentioned in the current review by Randerath et al. [1] and the authors point to divergent ventilation algorithms and pressure settings in the studies, the main difference between these two major trials on ASV is certainly the number of patients with CSA recruited: with a total of 198 chronic heart failure (CHF) patients with CSA, the ADVENT-HF study comprised only 14.9% of the study size of the SERVE-HF trial. Thus, this study is not powered for a robust statement on mortality and therefore not suitable for a firm conclusion regarding patient safety. The discussed improvements in the secondary endpoints (e.g. improved sleep and improved Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS)) should also be interpreted with caution: formally secondary endpoints are, of course, not conclusive.

Based on these sobering study results, should we refrain from treating CSA in patients with HFrEF? We do not think so! Because, as Randerath et al. [1] note, various studies show an independent prognostic significance of CSA in CHF, especially when causing a significant hypoxaemic burden [6, 7]. In addition, many patients show diffuse symptoms with reduced quality of life [8]. However, in view of the neutral study results and the increased mortality for the group of CHF patients with an LVEF ≤45% in the SERVE-HF study, both ASV therapy and CPAP therapy should not be used under prognostic aspects, but rather for symptom improvement and preferably in patients with preserved or only slightly reduced LVEF (>45%).

In this context, we also see transvenous phrenic nerve stimulation (TPNS) as an important component of the treatment algorithm. Although there are no primary endpoint data on prognostic outcome for TPNS, a large randomised, controlled study with 151 patients (98 of whom had CHF) showed no safety signal, similar to the CANPAP and ADVENT-HF studies [9]. Regarding symptoms, a significant improvement in ESS as well as MLHFQ (Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire) could be achieved as an indicator of an improved quality of life both for the overall study population and for the CHF patients in particular [10].
