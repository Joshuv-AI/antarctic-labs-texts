Poncelet Triangles and Tetragons over Finite Fields
Milena Radnović, Ruzzel Ragas

Abstract




Abstract:
In the projective plane over a finite field of characteristic not equal to 2, we compute the probability that a randomly selected pair of distinct conics
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
, with
\mathscr{A}
smooth or singular and
\mathscr{B}
smooth, in a fixed pencil of conics will admit a triangle or a tetragon inscribed in
\mathscr{A}
and circumscribed about
\mathscr{B}
. We do this for all pencils, classified up to projective automorphism, with at least one smooth conic; effectively allowing the case where our conic pairs intersect non-transversally.

MSC:
60C05, 51N15, 51E15, 11T06, 11G20, 51N35

Keywords:
Poncelet’s theorem, Cayley’s condition,
n
-Poncelet pairs, Finite projective plane

1
Introduction

Quoted as one of the most beautiful results in projective geometry, Poncelet’s theorem has been discussed extensively, together with its vast connection to other areas of mathematics; see, for example
[
6
,
9
,
10
]
. As a brief recall, Poncelet’s theorem states that if we have an
n
-sided polygon that is inscribed in a conic
\mathscr{A}
and circumscribed about another conic
\mathscr{B}
, then we can construct another
n
-sided polygon that is inscribed and circumscribed about the same pair of conics starting from any point in
\mathscr{A}
as a vertex. This holds for a projective plane over any field of characteristic not equal to 2
[
1
,
13
]
. In this context, we call such polygon a Poncelet
n
-gon and say that the ordered pair
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
is an
n
-Poncelet pair.

In the setting of the projective plane over a finite field of characteristic not equal to 2, the probability of obtaining a
3
-Poncelet pair was discussed in
[
3
]
. In this setting, Cayley’s condition
[
11
]
and Dickson’s classification
[
5
]
have been utilized to compute bounds for the probability of obtaining a
3
-Poncelet pair among smooth pairs of conics, for each pencil in Dickson’s classification with transversally intersecting generators. Under the same setting,
[
19
]
gave exact probabilities of obtaining
3
-Poncelet pairs, and provided bounds for the probabilities of obtaining
4
-Poncelet pairs.

We develop on these previous works by considering a larger collection of conic pairs in the projective plane over a finite field of characteristic not equal to 2. In this paper, we allow the conic
\mathscr{A}
in which the Poncelet polygon is inscribed to be smooth or singular while retaining the smoothness condition for the conic
\mathscr{B}
about which the Poncelet polygon is circumscribed. With this, we consider every pencil in Dickson’s classification that will contain at least one smooth conic, effectively allowing the case where our conic pairs intersect non-transversally. In this setting, we focus on Poncelet triangles and tetragons, and we aim to compute the exact probability of obtaining a
3
-Poncelet pair or
4
-Poncelet pair for each pencil under consideration. Moreover, we discuss in detail the probabilities for
3
-Poncelet pairs in the instance where the characteristic of the finite field is 3.

In Section
2
, we discuss pencils of conics and Cayley’s condition. In Section
3
, we define the collection of conic pairs that we consider in this paper and describe precisely how we obtain a random pair of conics. Within this framework, we derive the probabilities of obtaining a
3
-Poncelet pair or
4
-Poncelet pair. We conclude with possible future directions in Section
4
. A more detailed discussion of Dickson’s classification is available in Appendix
A
.

1.1
Notation

We denote by
\mathbb{F}_{q}
a finite field with
q
elements and refer to
q
as its order. Such a field exists if and only if
q=p^{k}
where
k
is a positive integer and
p
is prime, which we refer to as the characteristic of our finite field, denoted by char
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. In this paper, we always work on the base plane
\boldsymbol{P}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
with char
\mathbb{F}_{q}\neq 2
.

Conics will be denoted by capital script letters
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
with their matrix representation denoted by
A
and
B
, respectively. Following the notations in
[
3
]
, we denote the algebraic closure of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
as
\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
and given a conic
\mathscr{A}
in
\boldsymbol{P}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, we define the conic
\overline{\mathscr{A}}
in
\boldsymbol{P}^{2}(\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}})
as the conic defined by the same equation as
\mathscr{A}
.

A pencil of conics generated by
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
is the collection of conics with matrix representation in
\{\eta A+B\,|\,\eta\in\boldsymbol{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\}
. We follow the approach in
[
3
]
by considering pencils of conics up to projective automorphisms using Dickson’s classification
[
5
]
, which we give in Table
A.1
in Appendix
A
, see also
[
12
]
. For any pencil
\mathscr{P}_{j}
from Table
A.1
, each
\eta\in\boldsymbol{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
correspond to the conic with matrix representation
C_{j}(\eta)=\eta A_{j}+B_{j}
where
A_{j}
and
B_{j}
are the matrix representations of the generators
\mathscr{A}_{j}
and
\mathscr{B}_{j}
, with the convention that
C_{j}(\infty)=A_{j}
. For a comprehensive discussion about projective geometry, see for example,
[
17
]
and
[
8
]
for that of finite fields.

In this paper, we consider all pencils with at least one smooth element, leaving us with 11 pencils to consider. See Table
A.2
in Appendix
A
for important properties of these pencils, such as the index
\eta\in\boldsymbol{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
corresponding to singular elements of the pencil and the coordinates of the base points.

2
Poncelet’s Theorem and Cayley’s Condition

Now, we state Poncelet’s theorem as used in our setting

Theorem 2.1

(Poncelet
[
1
,
13
,
16
]
)
.

Let
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
be a pair of conics in
\boldsymbol{P}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. If there exists an
n
-sided polygon that is inscribed in
\mathscr{A}
and circumscribed about
\mathscr{B}
, then every point in
\mathscr{A}
is a vertex of an
n
-sided polygon that is inscribed in
\mathscr{A}
and circumscribed about
\mathscr{B}
.

This tells us that the number of sides of our Poncelet polygon is only dependent on the pair
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
. With this, we can consider an ordered pair
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
of conics from
\mathscr{P}_{j}
and ask whether it is an
n
-Poncelet pair. This question can be answered using Cayley’s condition, which we state for the triangle and tetragon case in Theorem
2.2
.

Theorem 2.2

(Cayley
[
2
,
11
]
)
.

Consider the formal series expansion

\sqrt{\det(tA+B)}=H_{0}+H_{1}t+H_{2}t^{2}+H_{3}t^{3}+\cdots

where
A
and
B
are the matrix representations for conics
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
, respectively.

Then,

•

(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
is a
3
-Poncelet pair if and only if
H_{2}=0
.

•

(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
is a
4
-Poncelet pair if and only if
H_{3}=0
.

More precisely, our statement of Poncelet’s theorem and Cayley’s condition works for
(\overline{\mathscr{A}},\overline{\mathscr{B}})
in
\boldsymbol{P}^{2}(\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}})
. In this paper, since our condition only depends on the matrix representations
A
and
B
, we say that
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
is an
n
-Poncelet pair in
\boldsymbol{P}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
if the induced pair
(\overline{\mathscr{A}},\overline{\mathscr{B}})
in
\boldsymbol{P}^{2}(\overline{\mathbb{F}_{q}})
is an
n
-Poncelet pair. Geometrically, this is equivalent to having at least one of the vertices of our Poncelet
n
-gon in
\mathscr{A}
.

3
Computation of Probabilities

In this section, we define precisely the probability spaces under consideration and, from this, derive the probabilities of obtaining a
3
-Poncelet pair or a
4
-Poncelet pair.

For a fixed pencil
\mathscr{P}_{j}
, we assign the ordered pair
(r,s)\in\boldsymbol{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\times\boldsymbol{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
to the conic pair
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
with matrix representation
A=C_{j}(r)
and
B=C_{j}(s)
. With this,
H_{2}
and
H_{3}
in Theorem
2.2
will take the form of a polynomial in
r
and
s
as will be shown later in this section.

We exclude from our sample space the pairs that cause problems in constructing the Poncelet polygon. We refer to such pairs as irregular.

Definition 3.1
.

For a fixed pencil
\mathscr{P}_{j}
from Table
A.2
, we call
(r,s)\in\boldsymbol{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\times\boldsymbol{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
an irregular pair if it belongs to the the following cases:

Case 1:
\mathscr{A}
is a double line

•

for
j=6
,
(0,\eta)\,,\eta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}

•

for
j=8
,
(\infty,\eta)\,,\eta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}

•

for
j=17
,
(\infty,\eta)\,,\eta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}

Case 2:
\mathscr{A}
is a union of two lines, one of which is tangent to
\mathscr{B}

•

for
j\in\{4,6,15\}
,
(\infty,\eta)\,,\eta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}

•

for
j=5
,
(\infty,\eta)\,,\eta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}

•

for
j=17
,
(0,\eta)\,,\eta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}

Remark 1
.

For the case where
\mathscr{A}
is a double line, every Poncelet polygon inscribed in
\mathscr{A}
has all of its vertices equal to the chosen starting vertex. Indeed, each tangent line to
\mathscr{B}
passing through the starting vertex intersects
\mathscr{A}
only at that point, treated as an intersection of multiplicity 2.

Remark 2
.

Inscribing a polygon in a union of two lines will result in vertices that alternate between each line
[
7
]
. Hence, if
\mathscr{A}
is a union of two lines and one of which is tangent to
\mathscr{B}
, we eventually obtain a vertex lying in a line tangent to
\mathscr{B}
which coincides with one of the lines making up
\mathscr{A}
. In which case, our construction terminates, since the next vertex is no longer well-defined.

Now, we define our valid pairs, which are the pairs we consider in this study.

Definition 3.2
.

For a fixed pencil
\mathscr{P}_{j}
from Table
A.2
, we call
(r,s)\in\boldsymbol{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\times\boldsymbol{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
a valid pair if
(r,s)
is not an irregular pair and
\det C_{j}(s)\neq 0
. We denote by
\Phi_{j}
the set of valid pairs in
\mathscr{P}_{j}
.

To allow comparisons to the previous works of
[
3
,
19
]
, we also consider the subset of valid pairs
(r,s)
in pencil
\mathscr{P}_{j}
with the additional constraint that
\det C_{j}(r)\neq 0
and call them smooth pairs in pencil
\mathscr{P}_{j}
.

Definition 3.3
.

For a fixed pencil
\mathscr{P}_{j}
from Table
A.2
, we call
(r,s)\in\boldsymbol{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\times\boldsymbol{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
a smooth pair if
(r,s)
is a valid pair and
\det C_{j}(r)\neq 0
. We denote by
\Psi_{j}
the set of smooth pairs in
\mathscr{P}_{j}
.

For each
j\in\{3,4,5,6,8,14,15,16,17,18,19\}
, we compute probabilities in the sample space
\Phi_{j}
, equipped with the uniform measure which gives each valid pair in
\mathscr{P}_{j}
an equal chance of being selected. Additionally, we consider the case when the sample space is restricted to
\Psi_{j}
as in
[
3
,
19
]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

\mathscr{P}_{j}

1.

Let

Lemma 3.4

Suppose

Let

, and
Z_{\varphi}=\{s\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\,|\,\varphi(s)=0\}
. Then,

\left\lvert Z^{*}_{\varphi}\right\rvert=\frac{q-1-\left\lvert Z_{\varphi}\right\rvert}{2}

Proof.

Recall that the non-zero square elements in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
form a group under multiplication, and the product of a square and a non-square element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
will yield a non-square element. Since

is a non-zero square, then
\varphi(s)
is a non-zero square if and only if

is a non-zero square. Hence,
\left\lvert Z^{*}_{\varphi}\right\rvert=\left\lvert Z^{*}_{\frac{\varphi}{u_{2}}}\right\rvert
. Moreover, the zeros of
\varphi(s)
do not change when we divide it by a non-zero element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. So, we also have
\left\lvert Z_{\varphi}\right\rvert=\left\lvert Z_{\frac{\varphi}{u_{2}}}\right\rvert
. Thus, without loss of generality, we can assume that
u_{2}=1
; otherwise, we just consider
\frac{\varphi(s)}{u_{2}}
.

By completing the square, we obtain
\varphi(s)=(s-\beta)^{2}+\gamma
where
\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
but
\gamma\neq 0
by the assumption that
\varphi(s)
is not a square of a linear polynomial. Now, we are interested in
s
such that

\varphi(s)=(s-\beta)^{2}+\gamma=y^{2}

(3.2)

where

. Isolating

on one side of the equation gives us

y^{2}-(s-\beta)^{2}=\underbrace{(y-s+\beta)}_{\omega}\underbrace{(y+s-\beta)}_{\frac{\gamma}{\omega}}=\gamma

Since
\gamma
is non-zero, the first factor
\omega=(y-s+\beta)
is a non-zero element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and the second factor
(y+s-\beta)
can be expressed as
\dfrac{\gamma}{\omega}
. Thus, we get

and substituting this back to Equation
3.2
, we obtain
(s-\beta)^{2}+\gamma=(\omega+s-\beta)^{2}
where we can solve for

s=\beta+\frac{\gamma}{2\omega}-\frac{\omega}{2}

(3.3)

Here, each

corresponds to a value of
s
that will make
\varphi(s)
a square in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. In this case, for a fixed
s
where
\varphi(s)
is square, Equation
3.3
can be treated as quadratic polynomial in
\omega
with the form

\omega^{2}+(2s-2\beta)\omega-\gamma=0

and discriminant
4\varphi(s)
. Hence, each

\varphi(s)

\omega
but only one
\omega
corresponding to each

. With this, we have

\omega
corresponding to the elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}
and we remove one

values of

s
which makes
\varphi(s)
a non-zero square. Since we only get one

\left\lvert Z^{*}_{\varphi}\right\rvert=\frac{q-1-\left\lvert Z_{\varphi}\right\rvert}{2}

Lemma 3.7

Suppose char
\mathbb{F}_{q}\neq 2

Lemma 3.19
.

Let
P_{k}
be the
k
th vertex of a Poncelet
n
-gon inscribed in
\overline{\mathscr{A}}
and circumscribed about
\overline{\mathscr{B}}
with
P_{0}=P_{n}
and
P_{1}=P_{n+1}
. Then,
P_{k}\in\overline{\mathscr{A}}\cap\overline{\mathscr{B}}
if and only if
P_{k-1}=P_{k+1}
. Moreover,
P_{k}\in\overline{\mathscr{A}}\cap\overline{\mathscr{B}}
is an intersection of multiplicity greater than 1 if and only if
P_{k-1}=P_{k}=P_{k+1}
.

Proof.

Let
\tau_{k}
be the tangent line to
\mathscr{B}
passing through
P_{k}
and
P_{k+1}
.
P_{k}\in\overline{\mathscr{A}}
since it is a vertex of our Poncelet polygon. Notice that
P_{k}\in\overline{\mathscr{B}}
if and only if the polar line to
\mathscr{B}
with respect to
P_{k}
coincides with the tangent line to
\overline{\mathscr{B}}
at
P_{k}
. This is equivalent to having the
\tau_{k}=\tau_{k-1}
, which happens if and only if
P_{k-1}=P_{k+1}
.

Now, in the case where
P_{k-1}=P_{k}=P_{k+1}
,
P_{k}=P_{k+1}
would imply that
\tau_{k}
intersects
\mathscr{A}
at a point of multiplicity 2. Hence,
\tau_{k}
is a common tangent of
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
at
P_{k}
, which will only happen if
P_{k}\in\overline{\mathscr{A}}\cap\overline{\mathscr{B}}
is an intersection of multiplicity 2 or above. Similar argument holds for
P_{k-1}=P_{k}
.
∎
