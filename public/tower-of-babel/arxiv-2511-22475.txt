Adversarial Flow Models
Shanchuan Lin, Ceyuan Yang, Zhijie Lin, Hao Chen, Haoqi Fan

Abstract
We present adversarial flow models, a class of generative models that unifies adversarial models and flow models. Our method supports native one-step or multi-step generation and is trained using the adversarial objective. Unlike traditional GANs, where the generator learns an arbitrary transport plan between the noise and the data distributions, our generator learns a deterministic noise-to-data mapping, which is the same optimal transport as in flow-matching models. This significantly stabilizes adversarial training. Also, unlike consistency-based methods, our model directly learns one-step or few-step generation without needing to learn the intermediate timesteps of the probability flow for propagation. This saves model capacity, reduces training iterations, and avoids error accumulation. Under the same 1NFE setting on ImageNet-256px, our B/2 model approaches the performance of consistency-based XL/2 models, while our XL/2 model creates a new best FID of 2.38. We additionally show the possibility of end-to-end training of 56-layer and 112-layer models through depth repetition without any intermediate supervision, and achieve FIDs of 2.08 and 1.94 using a single forward pass, surpassing their 2NFE and 4NFE counterparts.

Introduction

Flow matching
(
Lipman et al. 2022
;
Song et al. 2020
)
is a class of generative models that achieves the current state of the art in multiple domains, including image generation
(
Esser et al. 2024
;
Seedream et al. 2025
;
Baldridge et al. 2024
)
and video generation
(
Wan et al. 2025
;
Kong et al. 2024
;
Polyak et al. 2024
;
Seawead et al. 2025
;
Gao et al. 2025
)
,
etc
. It formulates the generation problem as the transport of samples from a prior distribution to the data distribution. A probability flow is established by interpolating the data and the prior samples, and a neural network learns the gradient of the flow. At inference time, the sample is iteratively transported by querying the network for gradients, incurring a high computational cost.

Recent methods accelerate the generation process by formulating the network to directly predict farther positions on the flow instead of the instantaneous gradients. This can be achieved either by distilling from a pre-trained flow-matching model
(
Salimans and Ho 2022
;
Liu et al. 2022
)
, or by training from scratch with consistency objectives as a new class of generative models
(
Song et al. 2023
)
. However, despite the goal of single-step or few-step generation, consistency-based models must still be trained on all timesteps of the flow for consistency propagation. This wastes model capacity and incurs error accumulation. A more critical problem is that networks in fewer steps have less capacity to predict the exact transformations of the more-step targets, so using pointwise-matching or even moment-matching losses causes some extent of blurriness. For these reasons, many state-of-the-art few-step generation models still rely on distributional matching methods, especially adversarial training, for final refinement
(
Lin et al. 2025a
;
Chen et al. 2025
)
.

Adversarial training originates from generative adversarial networks (GANs)
(
Goodfellow et al. 2014
)
. It itself is a standalone class of generative models that supports single-step generation. However, adversarial training from scratch often faces stability issues. A handful of recent works exploring adversarial training from scratch adopt non-standard architectural designs
(
Huang et al. 2024
;
Kang et al. 2023
;
Zhu et al. 2025
;
Hyun et al. 2025
)
. Some further rely on the assistance of additional frozen feature networks
(
Kang et al. 2023
;
Hyun et al. 2025
)
. When we switch to a standard transformer architecture
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
, the training simply diverges.

As shown in
Fig.

1
, we discover that one of the key reasons GANs are hard to train is that the adversarial objective alone does not present a single optimization target. This is markedly different from other established objectives, such as flow-matching, which has a unique ground-truth probability flow predetermined by the interpolation function, and autoregressive modeling, which has ground-truth token probabilities predetermined by the training corpus. In GANs, the generator is tasked to transport samples from the prior to the data distribution, but the adversarial objective only enforces the matching of the data distributions without constraining the transport plan. Therefore, there exist infinite valid transport plans that the generator may pick, influenced by the weight initialization and the stochastic training process. This causes optimization difficulties as the generator keeps drifting during training.

In this paper, we propose adversarial flow models, a class of generative models that unifies the adversarial and flow families of models under the same framework. Our model is trained by the adversarial objective. Therefore, it naturally supports single-step training and generation without wasting capacities on learning the intermediate timesteps needed by consistency methods. Our model also belongs to the flow family of models. Therefore, it has the same deterministic transport mapping for training stability, and it naturally supports multi-step training and generation. Our method can be trained on standard transformer architectures without modifications, which opens the door for wider adoption.

On ImageNet-256px, our B/2 model approaches the performance of consistency-based XL/2 models due to the conservation of modeling capacity, while our XL/2 model creates a new best FID of 2.38 under the same 1NFE setting with guidance. Our method also enables fully end-to-end training of 56-layer and 112-layer 1NFE models through depth repetition, and achieves FIDs of 2.08 and 1.94, surpassing the 2NFE and 4NFE counterparts. Furthermore, in the same no-guidance setting, we show that the adversarial objective can even outperform flow matching due to better distribution matching properties.

Method

3.1
Adversarial Training Preliminaries

Our method is built on top of generative adversarial networks (GANs)
(
Goodfellow et al. 2014
)
, where a generator
G:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
aims to transport samples
z
from a prior distribution
\mathcal{Z}
,
\emph{e.g}.\hbox{}
Gaussian, to samples
x
from the data distribution
\mathcal{X}
, and a discriminator
D:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{1}
aims to differentiate real samples from the generated ones. The adversarial optimization involves a minimax game where
D
is trained to maximize differentiation while
G
is trained to minimize the differentiation by
D
. We adopt the relativistic objective
(
Jolicoeur-Martineau 2018
)
as it has better properties for the loss landscape
(
Sun et al. 2020
)
and achieves the current state of the art
(
Huang et al. 2024
;
Hyun et al. 2025
)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{D}_{\mathrm{adv}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{z,x}\left[f(D(x)-D(G(z)))\right],

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{adv}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{z,x}\left[f(D(G(z))-D(x))\right],

(2)

where
f(\cdot)=-\log(\mathrm{sigmoid}(\cdot))
.

Additionally, gradient penalties
R_{1}
and
R_{2}

Roth et al. 2017
are added on
D
. They prevent
G
from being pushed away from equilibrium
(
Mescheder et al. 2018
;
Huang et al. 2024
)
and impose a constraint on the Lipschitz constant of
D

(
Gulrajani et al. 2017
)
. Directly computing the gradient penalties requires expansive double backward and twice differentiation, so we use finite difference approximation
(
Lin et al. 2025a
)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{D}_{\mathrm{r1}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\|\triangledown_{x}D(x)\|^{2}_{2}\right]

(3)

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\left\|D(x)-D(\mathcal{N}(x,\epsilon^{2}\textbf{I}))\right\|^{2}_{2}\right],

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{D}_{\mathrm{r2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[\|\triangledown_{G(z)}D(G(z))\|^{2}_{2}\right]

(5)

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\left\|D(G(z))-D(\mathcal{N}(G(z),\epsilon^{2}\textbf{I}))\right\|^{2}_{2}\right],

(6)

where
\epsilon
is set to a small value 0.01. We only compute the penalties on 25% of the samples of a batch and observe no performance degradation.

To prevent the discriminator logits from drifting unboundedly in the relativistic setting, a logit-centering penalty is added, similar to prior work
(
Karras et al. 2017
)
:

\small\mathcal{L}^{D}_{\mathrm{cp}}=\mathbb{E}_{z,x}\left[\|D(x)+D(G(z))\|^{2}_{2}\right].

(7)

Therefore, the final GAN loss is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{D}_{\mathrm{GAN}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{D}_{\mathrm{adv}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{gp}}\mathcal{L}^{D}_{\mathrm{r1}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{gp}}\mathcal{L}^{D}_{\mathrm{r2}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{cp}}\mathcal{L}^{D}_{\mathrm{cp}},

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{GAN}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{adv}},

(9)

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{gp}}
is a searched hyperparameter controlling the gradient penalty scale of both
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, and
\lambda_{\mathrm{cp}}
is universally set to 0.01.

The expectation is obtained through Monte Carlo approximation over minibatches of data during training. The
G
and
D
are updated in alternation. For conditional generation, the condition
c
is given to both networks as
G(z,c)
and
D(x,c)
. These are abbreviated in writing.

3.2
Single-step Adversarial Flow Models

The GAN objective above only enforces
G(z)
to match the data distribution. However, there are infinite transport plans from
z
to
x
, and the model is free to learn any arbitrary one. Our proposed adversarial flow models learn a deterministic transport plan, which is the same transport plan as flow-matching models with the linear interpolation function. This allows for the existence of a single solution, prevents generator drifting, and stabilizes training.

In flow matching, the ground-truth probability flow exists prior to model training and is deterministically defined by the data distribution, the prior distribution, the interpolation function, and the loss objective. It is known that when linear interpolation and the squared distance loss are used, this combination establishes a transport plan that minimizes the
squared Wasserstein-2 (
\mathbf{W_{2}^{2}}
)
distance between the prior and data distributions
(
Lipman et al. 2022
)
.

Formally, let
\bm{\pi}(x,z)
be a valid transport plan, and
\mathbf{c}(x,z)
be a cost function, flow matching with linear interpolation and squared distance objective results in the transport plan
\bm{\pi}(x,z)
that minimizes the total transport cost where the cost function
\mathbf{c}(x,z)=\|x-z\|^{2}_{2}
by the definition of
\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}
:

\small\min_{\pi}\int_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Z}}\mathbf{c}(x,z)\,d\bm{\pi}(x,z).

(10)

Therefore, for adversarial flow models, we first restrict the prior distribution to have the same dimensionality
n
as the data distribution,
\emph{i.e}.\hbox{},x,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This is required by all flow-based models and does not reduce the generality of our method. Then we substitute
\pi(x,z)
with our generator
G(z)
:

\small\min_{G}\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\mathbf{c}(G(z),z)\,dG(z).

(11)

Our goal is to find a
G
that is simultaneously a valid transport plan and a plan that minimizes the total transport cost under our defined cost function. Since the adversarial objective already enforces the validity of the transport plan, we only need to add an additional optimal transport (OT) loss on
G
. The loss minimizes the expectation of
\mathbf{c}(G(z),z)
for all
z
:

\small\boxed{\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{ot}}=\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[\frac{1}{n}\|G(z)-z\|^{2}_{2}\right].}

(12)

The objectives in adversarial flow models (AF) become:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{D}_{\mathrm{AF}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{D}_{\mathrm{adv}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{gp}}\mathcal{L}^{D}_{\mathrm{r1}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{gp}}\mathcal{L}^{D}_{\mathrm{r2}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{cp}}\mathcal{L}^{D}_{\mathrm{cp}},

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{AF}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{adv}}+\boxed{\lambda_{\mathrm{ot}}\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{ot}}}.

(14)

\lambda_{\textrm{ot}}=0
is equivalent to GANs.
\lambda_{\textrm{ot}}
being too small fails to escape local minima.
\lambda_{\textrm{ot}}
being too large forces identity output.

In theory, even an infinitesimal
\lambda_{\mathrm{ot}}
will break the symmetry in the loss landscape and create a unique global minimum. But in practice, if the value is too small, the optimization may get stuck in local minima, and if the value is too large, it forces the model to output identity, hurting distribution matching, as illustrated in
Fig.

. Therefore, we adopt a schedule to decrease
\lambda_{\mathrm{ot}}
over time during training.

Unlike consistency-based methods, our model does not need to be trained on other timesteps of the probability flow and can be trained for native one-step generation. This saves model capacity, reduces training iterations, and avoids error propagation. Furthermore, for the one-step training case, this formulation completely removes the hyperparameter of choosing timestep sampling and weighting. Our one-step model also completely avoids teacher-forcing.

3.3
Multi-step Adversarial Flow Models

Adversarial flow models can be generalized to multi-step generation and allow the model to jump between arbitrary timesteps on the probability flow. We introduce an interpolation function of the same form as flow-matching models:

\small x_{t}=\textit{interp}(x,z,t):=A(t)x+B(t)z,

(15)

where
t\in[0,1]
. Although
A(t)
and
B(t)
can be any continuous monotonic functions satisfying
x_{0}=x,x_{1}=z
, we adopt linear interpolation, where
A(t)=1-t
and
B(t)=t
, to match the
\mathrm{W}^{2}_{2}
transport behavior.

We change the generator to accept additional source timestep
s
and target timestep
t
as
G(x_{s},s,t)
, and the discriminator to accept additional target timestep as
D(x_{t},t)
. During training,
x_{s},x_{t}
are obtained by interpolating randomly sampled
x,z
. The adversarial loss is extended as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{D}_{\mathrm{adv}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x_{s},x_{t},s,t}\left[f(D(x_{t},t)-D(G(x_{s},s,t),t))\right],

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{adv}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x_{s},x_{t},s,t}\left[f(D(G(x_{s},s,t),t)-D(x_{t},t))\right].

(17)

The
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
gradient penalties are substituted accordingly. We omit the approximation form for simplicity:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{D}_{\mathrm{r1}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x_{t},s,t}\left[w(s,t)\ \|\triangledown_{x_{t}}D(x_{t},t)\|^{2}_{2}\right],

(18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{D}_{\mathrm{r2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x_{s},s,t}\left[w(s,t)\ \|\triangledown_{G(x_{s},s,t)}D(G(x_{s},s,t),t)\|^{2}_{2}\right].

(19)

In flow-matching models with linear interpolation, the transport plan between any source and target timestep’s marginal distributions also minimizes the
\mathrm{W}_{2}^{2}
distance, so the optimal transport loss is generalized as:

\small\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{ot}}=\mathbb{E}_{x_{s},s,t}\left[\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{w(s,t)}\cdot\|G(x_{s},s,t)-x_{s}\|^{2}_{2}\right].

(20)

We empirically find that the following weighting function works well:

\small w(s,t)=\max\left(|s-t|,\delta\right),

(21)

where
\delta=0.001
is set for numerical stability.

During training, the timesteps can be sampled as continuous values:
s\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1),t\sim\mathcal{U}(0,s)
. In this case, the model is trained to support transporting between any arbitrary timesteps
t<s
on the probability flow for generation. When
s
and
t
are close, the model behaves like flow-matching models. When they are far, the model behaves like trajectory models. Since
G
directly learns the target distribution through
D
without needing consistency propagation, the model can alternatively be trained to only learn a discrete set of timesteps that is needed for a specific few-step inference setting, where single-step generation is just a special case. This saves model capacity and training iterations. Our framework unifies single-step and flow generation under the same adversarial training setting.

3.4
Discriminator Formulation

It is important
not
to condition the discriminator on the source sample. Specifically, formulating the discriminator as
D(x,\underline{z})
for single-step, or
D(x_{t},t,\underline{x_{s},s})
for multi-step, is incorrect. This is because at training,
x
and
z
are randomly sampled and paired. This formulation incorrectly tells
D
that
z
should be paired with all
x
, but
G
can only produce a single mapping, and it is impossible to achieve such a goal, so the training will oscillate or diverge.

When searching for hyperparameters, a complication we have encountered is gradient magnitude. Specifically, the
G
objective consists of both the adversarial and the optimal transport losses. However, the adversarial loss received from
D
may have varying magnitudes, influenced by the
D
architecture, its weight initialization, the gradient penalty strength, and the adversarial objective. This creates difficulties in finding
\lambda_{\mathrm{ot}}
across model sizes.

Formally, we break down the gradient of the generator loss with respect to the generator network parameter
\theta
following the chain rule:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{AF}}}{\partial\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{adv}}}{\partial\theta}+\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{ot}}\partial\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{ot}}}{\partial\theta}

(22)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial G(z)}{\partial\theta}\cdot\boxed{\underbrace{\frac{\partial D(G(z))}{\partial G(z)}}_{\text{discriminator}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{adv}}}{\partial D(G(z))}}_{\text{adversarial obj.}}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\partial G(z)}{\partial\theta}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{ot}}\partial\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{ot}}}{\partial G(z)}}_{\text{transport obj.}}.

The boxed terms are the gradient passed down from
D
that has varying magnitudes. In traditional GANs, only the adversarial loss is used, and adaptive optimizers rescale the magnitude per parameter so
G
is invariant to the absolute scale of the gradient. However, in our case, the magnitude matters because it determines the ratio between the adversarial and the optimal transport losses.

Therefore, we propose a gradient normalization technique. Specifically, we change the formulation to
D(\phi(G(z)))
, where
\phi
is an identity operator in the forward pass but rescales the gradient in the backward pass:

\small\phi^{\prime}=\frac{\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{adv}}}{\partial G(z)}}{\sqrt{n}\cdot\sqrt{\mathrm{EMA}(\|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{adv}}}{\partial G(z)}\|_{2}^{2},\beta_{2})}}.

(23)

The operator
\phi
tracks the exponential moving average (EMA) of the gradient norm, normalizes the gradient by the average norm, and rescales by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
where
n
is the data dimension. It can be seen as an extension of Adam
(
Kingma 2014
)
into the backward path, so we use the same
\beta_{2}
as in Adam for the EMA decay. After normalizing the adversarial gradient to a unit scale, we can find
\lambda_{\mathrm{ot}}
that works well across model sizes.

3.5
Better Distribution Matching

Adversarial flow models enable the complete disentanglement of distribution-matching and optimal-transport objectives. Although the optimal-transport objective matches flow matching, the distribution-matching objective does not.

Flow matching’s squared distance loss objective minimizes the Euclidean distance in the
\mathbb{R}^{n}
space instead of the semantic distance on the data manifold
(
Lin and Yang 2023
)
. As a result, in high-dimensional space, new data samples generated by the model through generalization appear element-wise blended instead of semantically morphed from the training data. This is why flow-matching models without guidance generate perceptually out-of-distribution samples. The effect of classifier-free guidance (CFG)
(
Ho and Salimans 2022
)
is not only low-temperature sampling, but also perceptual guidance
(
Lin and Yang 2023
)
. However, CFG entangles perceptual quality with strong conditional alignment. This limits flow-matching models from representing the full data distribution and can make samples unnatural and canonical
(
Lin et al. 2024a
;
Karras et al. 2024
)
. Consistency-based methods share the same pathology. Latest works have explored training on more representational latent spaces
(
Zheng et al. 2025a
;
Leng et al. 2025
)
and using other guidance types
(
Karras et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2022
;
Xu et al. 2025
)
to counter this congenital defect. (More in
Appendix

E
.)

Adversarial training is different because of the introduction of a learnable
D
. The distance between real and generated distributions is measured through the semantic network, which captures the perceptual and semantic distance
(
Zhang et al. 2018
)
instead of the Euclidean distance. Our experiments find that our model can outcompete flow matching in the guidance-free setting.

t^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)
before feeding to the classifier:

\small\boxed{\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{cg}}=\mathbb{E}_{z,c,z^{\prime},t^{\prime}}\left[-C(\textit{interp}(G(z,c),z^{\prime},t^{\prime}),t^{\prime},c)\right]}.

(26)

This maximizes the expectation of classification on all timesteps, giving us similar results to CFG as shown in
Fig.

3(d)
. The timestep
t^{\prime}
can be sampled from a custom range, which corresponds to performing CFG on selected timesteps.
Eq.

24
is a special case of
Eq.

26
where
t^{\prime}
is always 0. The hyperparameters,
i.e
.
the scale
\lambda_{\textrm{cg}}
and the range of
t^{\prime}
, can be optionally amortized into
G
to allow inference-time adjustment.

For clarity, our experiment trains
C
offline as an independent network. If adversarial flow is used for post-training an existing flow-matching model
v
, the gradient of an implicit classifier can be derived from
v
following CFG:

\small\triangledown_{x_{t}}C(x_{t},t,c)=v(x_{t},t)-v(x_{t},t,c),

(27)

\small\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\textrm{cfg}}=\mathbb{E}_{z,c,z^{\prime},t^{\prime}}\left[-\frac{1}{n}\sum G(z,c)\,\triangledown_{(\cdot)}C(\cdot,t^{\prime},c)\right],

(28)

where
(\cdot)
is short for
\textit{interp}(G(z,c),z^{\prime},t^{\prime})
.

3.7
Model Architecture

We parameterize the single-step or multi-step generator
G
using a neural network
g
. We find that it can work equally well by either formulating it directly:

\displaystyle G(z)

\displaystyle=g(z),

(29)

\displaystyle G(x_{s},s,t)

\displaystyle=g(x_{s},s,t),

(30)

or as residual:

\displaystyle G(z)

\displaystyle=z-g(z),

(31)

\displaystyle G(x_{s},s,t)

\displaystyle=x_{s}-(s-t)\ g(x_{s},s,t).

(32)

The latter is closely related to the velocity prediction formulation of existing flow-matching models and consistency-based models. To demonstrate the feasibility of both, we train our single-step models using the direct formulation and multi-step models using the residual formulation. We parameterize
D
directly using a neural network
d
.

Both the
g
and
d
networks use standard diffusion transformer architecture (DiT)
(
Peebles and Xie 2023
)
. For single-step models, the timestep projection is removed. For multi-step models with fixed discretizations, one timestep projection is used. For any-step models, two timestep projections are used in
g
. The condition is injected through modulation for both
g
and
d
following the original DiT. Our
d
is nearly identical to
g
, except for the addition of a learnable [CLS] token prepended to the input. The [CLS] token is used to produce the logit through a final LayerNorm
(
Ba et al. 2016
)
and a linear projection. Our architecture has minimal modifications to the original DiT.

Deep Model Architecture

(a)
Multi-step

(b)
Extra-deep single-step

Figure 4:
Deep architecture through transformer block repetition. Extra-deep models are trained end-to-end using single-step objective without any intermediate supervision.

Prior research
(
Lin et al. 2025a
)
indicates that the effective model depth is critical for capturing the non-linear transformations required for generating high-quality samples, and the lack thereof is the primary reason causing artifacts on single-step models. We experiment with end-to-end training of extra-deep single-step models. In theory, extra-deep single-step models can outperform their multi-step counterparts because the models can pass hidden states end-to-end without projecting to and reinterpreting from the data space, there is no manual definition of timestep discretizations, and the network is trained without teacher-forcing.

As illustrated in
Fig.

4
, our extra-deep models use transformer block repetition
(
Dehghani et al. 2018
)
. The hidden state after the initial pass is recycled. Timestep-like embedding is still given to the transformer blocks only to differentiate repetition iterations, but the whole network is trained end-to-end for single-forward generation without any intermediate supervision. This design allows us to exactly match the number of parameters and model behavior with the multi-step counterpart for comparison.

3.9
Techniques for Adversarial Training

We share additional training techniques that are, though imperfect, very effective. They address the remaining challenges of adversarial training, not introduced by our adversarial flow models.

On optimization, minimax optimization with gradient descent is prone to oscillation. The equilibrium is better achieved through the weight average than at the last iterate
(
Daskalakis et al. 2017
)
. We find optimistic optimizer
(
Daskalakis et al. 2017
)
and asymmetrical learning rates
(
Heusel et al. 2017
)
ineffective. Our approach is to keep an EMA of
G

(
Yazıcı et al. 2018
)
, which consistently outperforms the online model by a large margin. More importantly, once the performance of the EMA model plateaus toward the end of the training, we replace online
G
with the EMA weights and repeat automatically after every epoch onward. Learning rate is also decreased during this phase. We find this simple technique very effective for approaching peak performance.

On training dynamics, vanilla adversarial training needs
G
and
D
to learn at a synchronized pace. If
G
learns too slowly,
D
will separate the real and the generated distributions perfectly, and the gradient vanishes. To address the vanishing gradient problem, WGAN
(
Arjovsky et al. 2017
)
is proposed but requires a K-Lipschitz
D
. DiffusionGAN
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
projects
D
onto a flow in the same spirit as our approach with guidance, but this only guarantees support up to the signal-to-noise ratio that
G
can perfectly fool
D
under each’s capacity. Discriminator augmentation (DA)
(
Karras et al. 2020a
)
is another approach to increase the distribution overlap used by recent GANs
(
Huang et al. 2024
;
Hyun et al. 2025
)
. However, we find that the choice of augmentation may implicitly inject inductive biases. For example, prior work finds that affine transforms outperform other distortions on image data
(
Karras et al. 2020a
)
because it implicitly encourages
D
to recognize affine transforms as a more acceptable generalization for image generation. The last approach is to simply reload
D
from an earlier checkpoint to reset the pace. Our experiment finds that reloading
D
performs surprisingly well while DiffusionGAN has limited effectiveness. Therefore, we reload
D
when training stalls for the no-guidance setting to avoid introducing any additional biases, and additionally use DA for the guidance setting.

Experiments

4.1
Setups and Training Details

Experiment setups.

We train on class-conditional ImageNet
(
Russakovsky et al. 2015
)
generation to compare with prior works. We follow the protocols to resize the images to 256
\times
256 resolution and augment with horizontal flips. We use pre-trained variational autoencoder (VAE)
1
1

1

https://huggingface.co/stabilityai/sd-vae-ft-mse

(
Rombach et al. 2022
)
and train the models in
32\times 32\times 4
latent space. Evaluations use Fréchet Inception Distance on 50k class-balanced samples (FID-50k)
(
Heusel et al. 2017
)
against the entire train set.

Training details.

We use a learning rate of
1\times 10^{-4}
and a batch size of 256 consistent with prior works
(
Peebles and Xie 2023
;
Frans et al. 2024
;
Geng et al. 2025
)
. We use AdamW
(
Loshchilov and Hutter 2017
)
optimizer with
\beta_{1}=0,\beta_{2}=0.9

(
Lin et al. 2025a
)
. We set weight decay to 0.01 and EMA decay to 0.9999. We follow MeanFlow’s definition of model sizes: B/2, M/2, L/2, XL/2, where 2 denotes the patch size.
G
and
D
always use models of the same size. We use separate dataloaders for
G
and
D
. Epochs are measured as the number of images seen by
G
. Since different models reach peak FID at different epochs, we report the earliest epoch that reaches the best FID. Additional training details are in the appendix.

Classifier guidance.

We train the model without guidance till the best FID, and then continue training it with guidance. Our classifier is trained from scratch on ImageNet using the cross-entropy objective for 30 epochs. It uses the same B/2 transformer in the latent space. We do not amortize the scale of CG into models for a fair comparison with prior works.

Extra-deep models.

We only repeat depth on
G
while keeping
D
normal depth. The learning rate of
G
is reduced by the factor of repetition. Extra-deep models are trained end-to-end from scratch following the single-step objective.

4.3
Comparisons to the State of the Art

Single-step with guidance.

Footnote

5
compares single-step generation with guidance against state-of-the-art consistency-based and GAN models. Under the exact latent space and architectures, denoted by (•), our method achieves new best FIDs with large improvement margins across all model sizes, even when compared to concurrent works
(
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Hyun et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025b
)
. Notably, our B/2 model surpasses many XL/2 consistency-based models, likely because B/2 models are tight in capacity, and our method does not waste capacity on other timesteps. Our XL/2 model reaches a new best FID of 2.38.
Table

8
shows the effect of guidance, and our method with only CG is still the best. Note that StyleGAN-XL has a slightly better FID while being smaller, but it operates in the pixel space, while ours is restricted by the VAE and DiT patch size 2. We mainly compare our method against others with the same settings.

Few-step with guidance.

Footnote

5
shows that our model also achieves better FIDs in the few-step setting. Ours are trained on designated timesteps to conserve capacity. We find that any-step training performs worse due to the dilution of capacity and batch size. This is also observed in our 1D experiments (
Fig.

1
) where adversarial flow models need a larger batch size and converge more slowly for any-step training. This is often not a real limitation in practice, as achieving the best performance in a designated few-step setting is the priority.

No-guidance generation.

Footnote

5
shows that without guidance, even our 1NFE and 2NFE models outperform flow matching using 250NFE+ in the same latent space setting denoted by (•). We leave the exploration of combining adversarial flow models with representational methods to future work. However, we are concerned that current representational methods may have an unfair advantage. Since ImageNet is a small dataset where each class only contains about 1000 images, models cannot learn the correct generalizations from limited data on classes with large variations. However, DINO
(
Caron et al. 2021
;
Oquab et al. 2023
)
is trained on a much larger dataset, and using it may leak prior knowledge.

Extra-deep models.

Table

7
shows that our 56-layer and 112-layer models achieve better FIDs of 2.08 and 1.94, surpassing the 2 and 4-step counterparts. This confirms our hypothesis in
Section

3.8
. The results reveal an important insight: the quality of single-step generation may not be bounded by the training method, but by the depth of the generator. Depth scaling is a worthy direction for future research.

Computation efficiency.

Both consistency and adversarial methods require multiple forward passes per iteration, except adversarial methods commonly use different samples for independent calculations of the expectation of
G
and
D
losses. Counting epochs by
G
is a fair comparison of
G
’s update iterations against consistency methods. We provide a breakdown of per-update compute in
Section

C.2
. Our XL/2 1NFE model takes 1.88
\times
training compute compared to AlphaFlow but obtains 15% gain in best FID. The additional compute comes from losses and regularizations on
D
, which can be improved in future work.

5
Conclusion

Our work proposes a framework to unify adversarial and flow modeling. We show that learning a deterministic transport greatly stabilizes training. We propose techniques to normalize gradients and incorporate guidance. Our method achieves new best FIDs and demonstrates training on deep architectures. Despite limitations, this
offers exciting prospects for future research.

(

(

Appendix B
Experimental Details

Training.

Table

10
provides the details of our architecture and constant hyperparameters.
Table

11
lists the training schedule of our models. The hyperparameter patterns are what we find along the training process that produce the best FID. We suppose it can be put on an automatic schedule, but we leave it to future work. When EMA reload is used, it is automatically reloaded after every epoch onward. When
D
reload is used, we manually try a few checkpoints from different epochs and reload only when the training stalls again.

Precision.

We use TF32 precision to match most prior works. We notice that some prior works
(
Wang et al. 2025b
;
Hyun et al. 2025
)
train on BF16, but all adopt modified architectures with the addition of QK-normalization and other changes. We also find that QK-normalization is critical for the training stability in BF16
(
Esser et al. 2024
)
. However, we do not see a major throughput improvement on the 256px latent setting with a patch size of 2. Therefore, we stick to no architecture modification and TF32.

Guidance.

Flow-based guidance as discussed in
Section

3.6
is used for 1NFE models. For multi-step models, we find it is sufficient to only apply guidance on selected target timesteps as indicated in the table. We find that multi-step models need a slightly higher guidance scale to get the best FID and are adjusted as such. For the deep models, we keep the exact setting as any other 1NFE models. When training the classifier, we add affine transforms to data augmentation, and it yields better downstream performance.

Evaluation.

We perform class-balanced evaluation, where we generate 50 images per class for 1000 classes to constitute the total 50k evaluation samples. Recent works
(
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Zheng et al. 2025a
)
find that this approach reduces the stochasticity in the evaluation process and yields more accurate evaluations of the models. Note that class-balanced evaluation may yield a 0.1 FID advantage compared to class-imbalanced counterparts. However, many prior works do not explicitly state the details of their evaluation protocols. We hence report class-balanced FIDs if the work explicitly provides, and otherwise report the original metrics in the work. Our method yields a large-margin improvement on FID, especially surpassing the best consistency-based method AlphaFlow
(
Zhang et al. 2025
)
, which is evaluated in the same class-balanced setting.

The FID and other metrics are computed using the code provided by ADM
(
Dhariwal and Nichol 2021
)
. The FID is computed against the entire training set using the pre-computed statistics by ADM
(
Dhariwal and Nichol 2021
)
.

Discriminator augmentation.

Discriminator augmentation (DA) is used along with classifier guidance (CG). DA is directly performed in the latent space. Let
\rho
denote the augmentation operation,
\phi
is the gradient normalization operation, the exact composition for the final adversarial loss is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{D}_{\mathrm{adv}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{z,x,\rho}\left[f(D(\phi(\rho(x)))-D(\phi(\rho(G(z)))))\right],

(33)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{adv}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{z,x,\rho}\left[f(D(\phi(\rho(G(z))))-D(\phi(\rho(x))))\right].

(34)

Notice that the same random augmentation
\rho
must be applied to the real and generated samples as a pair in calculating the expectation of the relativistic loss. We only perform integer translation and cutout with a fixed probability in the latent space.

shows our implementation using Kornia
(
Riba et al. 2020
)
.

Appendix C
Limitations

C.1
Adversarial Training

The need to gradually reduce the scale of the optimal transport objective
\lambda_{\mathrm{ot}}
is theoretically justified. The scale cannot reduce too fast, or
G
cannot fully escape local minima. However, the scale also cannot reduce too slowly, or
D
outlearns
G
, and the gradient vanishes when
D
can perfectly separate the real and generated distributions. This is problematic because we would prefer an algorithm that guarantees the eventual convergence of
G
given a long enough
\lambda_{\mathrm{ot}}
decay duration, instead of being sensitive to the decay schedule.

WGAN.

WGAN
(
Arjovsky et al. 2017
)
proposes using a 1-Lipschitz
D
to represent the Wasserstein-1 (
\mathrm{W}_{1}
) distance between the real and generated distributions, which theoretically guarantees gradients for
G
. WGAN is a special case of the relativistic objective where
f(\cdot)=-(\cdot).
WGAN-GP
(
Gulrajani et al. 2017
)
proposes adding gradient penalties on linearly interpolated
x
and
G(z)
samples to limit the Lipschitz constant of
D
. This is theoretically correct for one-dimensional data, but in high-dimensional spaces, linear interpolation only occupies a thin slice of the full space, and thus cannot theoretically guarantee the requirement of WGAN. A stronger approach is to directly limit the Lipschitz constant through
D
’s architecture. Since in practice, only a
K
-Lipschitz
D
is really needed, which is equivalent to
\mathrm{W}_{1}
scaled by a factor of
K
, we experiment using spectral normalization
(
Miyato et al. 2018
)
and L2 attention
(
Kim et al. 2021
)
in the transformer architecture of
D
to set an upperbound on the Lipschitz constant of
D
, but we find that this setting is too limiting to the expressiveness of
D
and significantly hinders the learning of
G
. We leave further explorations to future work.

DiffusionGAN.

An alternative approach is to bridge the gap between the real and the generated distributions. Early approaches add instance noises
(
Mescheder et al. 2018
)
. DiffusionGAN
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
gives
D
the noise scale as a condition, essentially projecting
D
to a probability flow, similar to our approach with guidance. However, this only guarantees support up to the signal-to-noise ratio that
G
can perfectly fool
D
under each’s capacity constraint. Our preliminary experiments do not find that DiffusionGAN provides benefits. We leave further explorations to future work.

Discriminator Augmentation.

Non-leaky discriminator augmentation (DA) is an alternative method to increase the overlapping of the distributions
(
Karras et al. 2020a
)
. It offers more flexibility to augment samples in the spatial or frequency domains. However, augmentation filters are often modality-specific, limiting the generality. For example, affine transforms can be easily applied in the pixel space, but not in the latent space. Additionally, we suspect that the choice of augmentation filters also injects additional inductive biases that encourage the models to learn toward specific forms of generalization. This can actually be a very useful thing, especially with limited data, because the proper ways of generalization are impossible to deduce from the dataset alone. However, we choose not to use DA in the no-guidance setting. This avoids introducing any additional unfair advantages against flow-matching methods. We only use DA in the guidance setting to achieve the best performance. We only use integer translation and cutout due to the limitation in the latent space. We only use a fixed augmentation probability, though it could be dynamically adjusted
(
Karras et al. 2020a
)
for future works.

Weaker discriminator.

We try gradually increasing dropout in
D
, but find it not very effective. To our surprise, we find that reloading an older checkpoint of
D
is very effective in keeping improving the FID of
G
. However, we acknowledge that this is not a principled approach. Overall, we would like an algorithm to guarantee
G
’s eventual convergence given an arbitrarily strong and optimal
D
without introducing additional modality-specific augmentations. Although our work improves the stability and formulation of
G
, we believe the distribution-matching aspect of
D
is still worthy of further research.

Connections to score distillation.

Score distillation
(
Luo et al. 2023b
)
is another distribution matching objective that can be used to train one-step generators from a pre-trained flow-matching model. Current score distillation approaches yield one-step generators with arbitrary transport plans, the same problem as GANs. Our optimal transport objective can also be applied to regulating the one-step generator to learn a deterministic mapping when using score distillation. We leave further explorations to future work.

C.2
Computational Efficiency

As discussed in the main text, both consistency and our methods require multiple forward passes per update iteration. The difference is that consistency-based models use the same data samples across all the forward passes, while adversarial methods have traditionally used different data samples to independently calculate the expectation of the
G
and
D
losses. We count the epoch by
G
as a fair reflection of the number of optimizer update steps taken by
G
. Since our model only needs to be trained on selected timesteps, our
G
indeed reaches peak performance using fewer optimizer update steps, given the same learning rate and batch size compared to consistency models.

However, when we analyze the computation needed per
G
’s update step, current adversarial methods are still at a disadvantage due to the excessive computation and regularizations needed by
D
. This is a limitation of the adversarial formulation, not introduced by our adversarial flow.

Table

12
shows the breakdown of the computation per
G
update. Current adversarial approach consumes 3.625
\times

G
’s update compared to MeanFlow. Adjusting the reduced iterations needed, on XL/2 models, our method uses

compute compared to MeanFlow but obtains 15% FID improvement. For adversarial methods, it may be possible to reduce the batch size on
D
, apply clever result reuse, explore other methods to limit the Lipschitz constant other than using gradient penalties, and explore other objective functions than the relativistic one to save compute. But for our research, we take the most conservative approach and mostly follow the prior state-of-the-art GANs’ formulation
(
Huang et al. 2024
;
Hyun et al. 2025
)
. We leave the optimization to future work.

Appendix D
Guidance Details

Multi-step guidance.

The main text only describes our flow-based guidance approach for single-step generation. Since we find out that we do not need very strong guidance for ImageNet generation, our multi-step models simply apply guidance by setting
t^{\prime}=t
:

\small\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{cg}}=\mathbb{E}_{x_{s},s,t,c}\left[-C(G(x_{s},s,t,c),t,c)\right].

(35)

But for completeness, we discuss possible ways to still project it onto a flow. When given
x_{t}
, we may not always be able to derive
x_{t^{\prime}}
as drawn from the marginal distribution at timestep
t^{\prime}
if the interpolation function is not Markov. Linear interpolation is an example. We can, however, form a new probability flow between
x_{t}
and
x_{1}
by taking the marginal distribution at
x_{t}
as the source distribution. The classifier conditions on both the target
t
and projected
t^{\prime}
as
C(x_{t}^{\prime},t,t^{\prime},s)
:

\small\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\mathrm{cg}}=\mathbb{E}_{x_{s},s,t,c,z^{\prime},t^{\prime}}\left[-C(\mathrm{interp}(G(x_{s},s,t,c),z^{\prime},t^{\prime}),t,t^{\prime},c)\right].

(36)

Derivation of an implicit classifier.

We show the derivation of
Eq.

39
in the main text. CFG
(
Ho and Salimans 2022
)
shows that an implicit classifier can be derived following Bayes’ rule:

\displaystyle\log p(c|x_{t})

\displaystyle=\log\frac{p(x_{t}|c)p(c)}{p(x_{t})}

(37)

\displaystyle=\log p(x_{t}|c)+\log p(c)-\log p(x_{t})

\displaystyle\sim\log p(x_{t}|c)-\log p(x_{t})

Flow-matching models predicting velocity
v(x_{t},t)=x_{1}-x_{0}
is proportional to the negative score
-\triangledown_{x_{t}}\log p(x_{t}|c)
. Therefore, the gradient of an implicit classifier is derived:

\displaystyle\triangledown_{x_{t}}C(x_{t},t,c)

\displaystyle=\triangledown_{x_{t}}\log p(c|x_{t})

(38)

\displaystyle\sim\triangledown_{x_{t}}\log p(x_{t}|c)-\triangledown_{x_{t}}\log p(x_{t})

\displaystyle\sim(-v(x_{t},t,c))-(-v(x_{t},t))

\displaystyle=v(x_{t},t)-v(x_{t},t,c)

Given the gradient of an implicit classifier, we want to directly pass it down the generator
G
in the backpropagation process. This can be achieved using the constant multiple rule trick, where
f(x)=ax,f^{\prime}(x)=a
. So we multiply the gradient of the implicit classifier by the generator output:

\small\mathcal{L}^{G}_{\textrm{cfg}}=\mathbb{E}_{z,c,z^{\prime},t^{\prime}}\left[-\frac{1}{n}\sum G(z,c)\,\triangledown_{(\cdot)}C(\cdot,t^{\prime},c)\right],

(39)

where
(\cdot)
is short for the flow interpolation process
\textit{interp}(G(z,c),z^{\prime},t^{\prime})
before passing to the implicit classifier. Summation is taken to produce a scalar for loss backpropagation. Negative is used to invert loss minimization for classification maximization.

Implicit guidance in prior GAN works.

Many techniques used in prior GAN works are, in fact, classifier guidance. Our work explicitly labels them for clarity.

Multiple GAN works use cGAN discriminator architecture
(
Miyato and Koyama 2018
)
. This architecture resembles CLIP
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
, where the inner product between the visual embedding and the class embedding is computed at the end and maximized along with the adversarial objective. This is an implicit form of classifier guidance, as this architecture clearly does not apply to unconditional generation tasks.

GigaGAN proposes a matching loss
(
Kang et al. 2023
)
, which trains the
D
to evaluate class alignment in addition. When training
G
, this implicitly encourages
G
to generate samples that maximize classification. Hence, it is also an implicit form of classifier guidance.

Appendix E
On Distribution Matching

Why does flow matching generate bad samples?

Flow matching defines an interpolation function between samples
x
and
z
and learns the gradient (velocity) of the interpolated sample
x_{t}
as
v_{t}
:

\displaystyle x_{t}

\displaystyle=A(t)x+B(t)z=(1-t)x+tz,

(40)

\displaystyle v_{t}

\displaystyle=A^{\prime}(t)x+B^{\prime}(t)z=-x+z.

(41)

The model learns the expected velocity of all random pairings. It is trained to minimize the following loss objective with
d:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{1}
as the distance function.
d
can be any strictly convex function in principle, and flow matching adopts the squared L2 distance:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x,z,t}\left[d\left(v(x_{t},t),v_{t}\right)\right]

(42)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x,z,t}\left[\|v(x_{t},t)-v_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right].

If given enough capacity, the model will learn to memorize the exact mean of
v_{t}
for all
x_{t}
. Such a model overfits to the ground-truth probability flow and only generates the training samples at inference. But in practice, models have limited capacities, so they do not memorize the exact
v_{t}
but regress to a generalized approximation of it. This enables the generation of new data samples through generalization.

However, the way of generalization is influenced by the distance function
d
. With squared L2, generalized samples are encouraged to minimize the Euclidean distance to the training data, instead of the semantic distance on the actual data distribution manifold. This causes the generated samples to look out-of-distribution. For images, they appear pixel-blended instead of semantically morphed from the training data, as shown in
Fig.

9(b)
.

From the perspective of diffusion.

In flow matching,
d
can be any strictly convex function. The use of the squared L2 has a special interpretation from the diffusion model perspective. In DDPM
(
Ho et al. 2020
)
, the forward diffusion process is defined as a Markov Gaussian process:

\small q(x_{t}|x_{t-1}):=\mathcal{N}(\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}}x_{t-1},\beta_{t}\mathbf{I}).

(43)

The transition to
x_{t}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}q(x_{i}|x_{i-1})
has a closed form:

\small q(x_{t}|x_{0}):=\mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar{a}_{t}}x_{0},(1-\bar{a}_{t})\mathbf{I}),

(44)

where
a_{t}=1-\beta_{t}
, and
\bar{a}_{t}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}a_{s}
. The reverse process is also Gaussian, where the ground-truth posterior is given as:

\small q(x_{t-1}|x_{t},x_{0}):=\mathcal{N}(\tilde{\mu}(x_{0},x_{t},t),\tilde{\beta}(t)\mathbf{I}),

(45)

where:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}(x_{0},x_{t},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\bar{a}_{t-1}}\beta_{t}}{1-\bar{a}_{t}}x_{0}+\frac{\sqrt{a_{t}}(1-\bar{a}_{t-1})}{1-\bar{a}_{t}}x_{t},

(46)

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\bar{a}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{a}_{t}}\beta_{t}.

(47)

This can be modeled by a neural network:

\small p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}|x_{t}):=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\theta}(x_{t},t),\Sigma_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\mathbf{I}).

(48)

KL divergence is chosen as the loss objective:

\small\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DDPM}}=\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},t}\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(x_{t-1}|x_{t},x_{0})\|p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}|x_{t}))\right].

(49)

The KL divergence between two Gaussian distributions has a closed-form solution. The original DDPM only parameterizes
\mu_{\theta}
, which simplifies to the squared L2 objective if omitting the additional weighting factor:

\small\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DDPM}}\propto\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},t}\left[\|\mu_{\theta}(x_{t},t)-\tilde{\mu}(x_{0},x_{t},t)\|^{2}_{2}\right].

(50)

The DDPM derivation follows the same limitation as that of flow matching. Specifically, the model will overfit to the training data if it fits the expectation of the ground-truth posterior exactly, or it will generalize to new samples influenced by the chosen divergence objective. The divergence objective can be arbitrary, and KL is only chosen for convenience. KL divergence maximizes likelihood, but the generated samples are perceptually out of distribution.

Why does adversarial work?

If
d
can be an arbitrary function, then it should be learned instead of being manually designed. Adversarial training involves a learnable discriminator as the distance function
D:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{1}
. In the adversarial minimax game.
D
is trained to maximize the distance between real and generated samples, so
D
learns to put more weight on the most important and discriminative features.
G
is then updated according to the gradient of
D
, which is highly non-linear unlike the Euclidean distance objective. We believe having a learnable
\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{1}
distance function is critical for generating perceptually correct samples. This justifies our experimental results where the adversarial models significantly outperform flow matching in the no-guidance setting. Prior works also find adversarial post-training improves the realism
(
Lin et al. 2025a
;
Wang et al. 2025a
)
.

Why does CG work?

Classifier guidance (CG)
(
Dhariwal and Nichol 2021
)
introduces a learned classifier network
C:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{1}
whose gradient is added on
v(x_{t},t)
. We believe the presence of a learnable
\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{1}
network is critical. Its backward gradient
\mathbb{R}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is non-linear and guides the generation toward maximizing
p(c|x_{t})
as evaluated by the semantic network, which helps the samples to appear semantically and perceptually correct. Discriminator guidance
(
Kim et al. 2022
)
and reward models are other examples of using a learned
\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{1}
networks to help generation quality. On the other hand, only adjusting the sampling temperature alone without CG is not sufficient
(
Xu et al. 2025
)
, indicating that the effect of CG is not just temperature sampling.

Why does CFG work?

Recall in CG, the classifier network
\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{1}
predicts
p(c|x_{t})
and it is its backward gradient
\mathbb{R}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
that is added to the
\mathbb{R}^{n}
velocity prediction. Classifier-free guidance (CFG)
(
Ho and Salimans 2022
)
is derived from the Bayes’ rule where
\triangledown_{x_{t}}C(x_{t},t,c)=v(x_{t},t)-v(x_{t},t,c)
is the gradient of the implicit classifier, as previously discussed in
Eq.

38
. Therefore, despite not having an explicit
\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{1}
classifier, the conditional and unconditional
\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
flow models together provide the gradient of an implicit classifier
\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Why does representational latent space help?

If flow matching is performed in a latent space where the Euclidean interpolations still yield valid latents that can be subsequently decoded into a perceptual image by the decoder, then it can alleviate the limitation of flow matching. This offloads the responsibility to the decoder, which is often trained with adversarial and LPIPS
(
Zhang et al. 2018
)
perceptual objectives. We suspect that representational latent spaces have more semantic features where the Euclidean interpolations can be better decoded. This may be part of the reason, but we defer to future research.

Connections to evaluation metrics.

FID metric measures the distance between two distributions of images by comparing their representations in the feature space of a pretrained network,
i.e
.
Inception-V3
(
Szegedy et al. 2016
)
. Therefore, FID also measures the semantic distance instead of the Euclidean distance. Thus, adversarial, CG, and CFG approaches all help improve FID.
