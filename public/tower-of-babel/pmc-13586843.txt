Transcription and Three‐Dimensional Genome Organization: Cause, Consequence, or Coordination?
Authors: Dagyeong Yang, Elissa P Lei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTranscription and three‐dimensional (3D) genome organization are closely coupled, but their precise relationship remains unresolved. Evidence from perturbation, imaging, and modeling studies suggests that transcription is not strictly required for the establishment of large‐scale genome features such as compartments and topologically associating domains (TADs). Instead, transcription seems to exert more prominent effects at finer spatial scales, where it influences enhancer–promoter interactions, local chromatin loops, and microcompartments. RNA molecules, polymerase‐driven supercoiling, and R‐loop formation may further modulate chromatin organization at the gene level. At the same time, many architectural proteins and transcriptional regulators operate with functional overlap, complicating efforts to disentangle cause and consequence. Together, these observations suggest that genome architecture provides a relatively stable framework for regulated gene expression, while transcription and associated factors refine chromatin organization in a context‐dependent manner. Understanding how these processes are integrated will require systematic, multi‐scale perturbations across different biological and biochemical systems.Keywords: chromatin, cohesin, ctcf, drosophila, genome, genomic organization, insulator (genetics), transcription (biology)

Introduction

The three‐dimensional (3D) spatial organization of the interphase genome plays an important role in regulating essential cellular processes such as transcription and maintenance of genome integrity. Interphase chromosomes are organized into various structures of different scales [1, 2]. At the chromosomal level, genomes segregate into A and B compartments, which are preferential long‐range contacts between genomic regions sharing similar chromatin states and correspond to active and inactive chromatin [1]. At the sub‐megabase scale, chromosomes fold into highly self‐interacting regions often insulated by boundary elements, termed topologically associating domains (TADs) [2, 3]. Aside from and sometimes overriding domain‐scale organization, chromosomes also harbor specific long‐range chromatin loops between regulatory elements and architectural anchors [4, 5, 6, 7]. At finer scales, chromatin loops between distal enhancers and promoters are evident and can be regulated in a cell‐type or temporal manner [8, 9, 10].

Despite extensive studies, the exact relationships between transcription and various levels of 3D genome organization remain unsolved. Large‐scale B compartment interactions may be mainly driven by homotypic interactions of repressive chromatin proteins such as HP1 in Drosophila and mammals [11, 12]. Excluded regions could thus form actively transcribed A compartments, which might also be promoted by the propensity of RNA Polymerase II (Pol II) and some transcription factors to form condensates (reviewed in [13]). How transcription relates to TAD‐level and loop‐level organization has been a focus of the genome organization field in recent years, and this essay will focus mainly on these levels of organization.

In vertebrates, a large body of evidence suggests that TADs can be formed by cohesin‐dependent loop extrusion of DNA. The architectural insulator protein, CCCTC‐binding factor (CTCF), is highly enriched at TAD boundaries, and it blocks cohesin translocation along the DNA [4, 14, 15]. Multiple studies have shown that loss of cohesin leads to genome‑wide weakening or loss of TADs and loop‑domain structures in mammalian cells [5, 6, 16]. More recent acute cohesin‐depletion studies have reported that a subset of TAD boundaries overlapping active promoters persist, suggesting that promoter‑proximal features and/or other architectural factors can preserve some boundary signals even when cohesin‑dependent loops are disrupted [17]. In contrast to vertebrates, little evidence exists in Drosophila to support loop extrusion as a mechanism involved in the formation of TADs. Drosophila CTCF is not highly enriched at TAD boundaries and plays a less extensive role in TAD formation genome‐wide [18, 19]. Instead, TAD boundaries in Drosophila are enriched with the binding of various insulator complexes, and Drosophila and other organisms that lack CTCF harbor a large number of actively transcribed genes at TAD borders [20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. Therefore, formation of TAD boundaries in these species may rely predominantly on insulator complexes and/or active promoter‐associated features. This strong correlation between TAD boundaries and active promoters across evolution raises the question as to whether transcription itself contributes directly to TAD formation.

The mechanisms underlying genome organization are not identical across all species and involve various architectural proteins and transcription factors [19, 28]. Recent studies in vertebrates and Drosophila demonstrate that the relationship between transcription and 3D genome organization also varies across cell types and stages, reflecting both conserved and developmental context‐specific mechanisms of regulatory insulation and chromatin folding [8, 29, 30]. Despite rapid advances in imaging and sequencing technologies, the causal relationships between transcription and genome architecture remain unclear. Conflicting observations across organisms, developmental stages, and experimental perturbations challenge earlier models and suggest that multiple mechanisms may operate. This essay examines emerging paradigms with an emphasis on more recent studies regarding the reciprocal relationship between transcription and 3D genome organization, highlighting current proposed models and key open questions, and pointing to areas that require further investigation.

How Does Transcription Shape Genome Organization?

Is Transcription Required for TAD Formation?

Large‐scale perturbation studies provided initial evidence that the process of transcription itself can alter 3D genome organization. Transcription has been disrupted using a variety of experimental strategies, including pharmacological inhibitors and targeted depletion of RNA polymerases, with differing effects on genome architecture across organisms and experimental systems (summarized in Table 1). Global inhibition of transcription using triptolide or flavopiridol inhibitors weakened TAD border strength, increased long‐range inter‐TAD contacts, and reduced A‐A compartment interactions in Drosophila cell lines, suggesting that active transcription is needed to help stabilize both TAD borders and the A compartment [24, 31]. Similarly, transcriptional inhibition using Actinomycin D treatment of human K562 cells led to reduction of TAD border strength and altered local chromatin interactions, while RNase treatment alone had limited effects on TAD boundary strength but reduced B–B compartment interactions [32]. These findings suggest that it is the act of transcription rather than the presence of RNA that is important for full TAD border strength. In contrast, targeted degradation of RNA polymerase large subunits in mouse ES cells found that loss of Pol I, Pol II, or Pol III had minimal impact on large‐scale compartments and TADs but did alter local, smaller chromatin loop interactions [33]. Another targeted degradation study of Pol II in human HCT116 cells observed mild disruption to TAD borders and extrusion loops in unsynchronized cells but observed much stronger effects in cells following mitotic exit, a system that allows study of the establishment of interphase 3D genome organization [34]. Despite some contrasting results that could be due to experimental differences such as dosage used or types of inhibitors that block RNA polymerases at different stages of transcription, these results collectively suggest that transcription can contribute to the establishment and maintenance of TADs.

Studies in various organisms have explored the developmental timing of establishment of TADs relative to the onset of high levels of zygotic transcription. In Drosophila, initial Hi‐C analyses of staged embryos showed that domain‐like chromatin organization emerges around the onset of widespread transcription of the zygotic genome (zygotic genome activation (ZGA)), and that TAD boundaries strengthen over time. Treatment of embryos with transcriptional inhibitors α‐amanitin or triptolide reduced boundary strength [22]. In zebrafish, early work reported compartments and TAD‑like domains before ZGA that progressively strengthened during development [35], whereas more recent studies using higher‑resolution Hi‑C concluded that chromosome organization is largely featureless before ZGA and that loop‑extrusion‐dependent structures become prominent only upon and after genome activation [36]. In mouse embryos, compartments and TADs are largely established after ZGA, but their emergence does not strictly require transcription [37]. Additional work in Xenopus tropicalis embryos combining Hi‐C with transcriptional inhibition similarly concluded that TAD establishment does not depend on ZGA, but architectural features become more prominent during development [38]. Together, these studies indicate that transcription is not strictly required for the initial establishment of domain‐scale genome organization in the developing embryo, but high levels of zygotic transcription correlate with increased definition of TADs. One possibility is that architectural factors, which are only robustly expressed around ZGA, generate a foundation upon which TADs can develop, and transcription‐related processes can reinforce TAD boundaries over time.

Transcription‐Dependent Effects on Genome Organization Are More Evident at Finer Chromatin Scale

Although large‐scale genome features such as compartments and TADs remain largely stable when transcription is inhibited or RNA polymerases are depleted, recent studies indicate that transcription exerts stronger architectural effects at finer spatial scales. Technical innovations allowing ever‐increasing mapping resolution have allowed interrogation of these finer‐scale structures. An imaging‐based Hi‐M study in early Drosophila embryos showed that sub‐TAD‐level cis‐regulatory chromatin loops comprised of a variety of enhancer–promoter (E–P), enhancer–enhancer (E–E), and promoter–promoter (P–P) interactions can form prior to mature TADs and before robust gene activation [39]. Interestingly, cells of different fates harbored similar loops, suggesting that these structures are not sufficient to ensure gene activation and could also precede gene repression. These results are consistent with large‐scale 4C studies in Drosophila embryos showing that E‐P loops are pre‐established before gene activation and may be related to paused Pol II [40]. Furthermore, high‐resolution Micro‐C and Pico‐C mapping efforts in Drosophila embryos were also able to detect chromatin loops and TAD‐like structures being established before ZGA, and these structures subsequently strengthen during transcriptional activation [30, 41]. Injection of embryos with transcriptional inhibitors abrogated some early chromatin loops but left others intact. Taken together, these finer‐scale chromatin loops could represent an early level of genome organization possibly involving pioneer transcription factors and paused Pol II at early regulatory regions that provides a platform for later transcription‐dependent structures.

At even finer resolution, polymer modeling and high‐resolution contact maps in mouse ES and human lymphoblastoid cells have identified dynamic, active gene‐level interaction patterns termed microcompartments or active contact clusters that may be influenced by Pol II transcription [42, 43, 44, 45]. Notably, microcompartments persist despite triptolide treatment [44], suggesting that microcompartments may be formed upstream of transcription and mechanistically distinct from Pol II‐sensitive local chromatin loops observed upon polymerase depletion [33]. Reminiscent of gene loops proposed to help recycle Pol II and act as a transcriptional memory (reviewed in [46]), these microcompartments could also correspond to transcription factories or condensates. Furthermore, chromatin‐associated RNAs produced during transcription in human ES cells were shown to inhibit local chromatin loop formation [47]. Interestingly, nascent transcripts that form RNA‐DNA hybrid structures known as R‐loops have been shown to impede cohesin translocation in vitro and reduce local chromatin contacts [48]. In contrast, R‐loops have alternatively been proposed to nucleate transcriptional condensates and promote specific local interactions, providing an additional possible mechanism linking transcription and chromatin organization (reviewed in [49]). These studies suggest that RNA molecules themselves, including R‐loops, might either inhibit or facilitate local chromatin organization depending on genomic and regulatory context.

How Does Transcription Affect Loop Extrusion in Mammals?

Recent models propose that transcription directly interfaces with cohesin‐mediated loop extrusion, raising the question of how RNA polymerase activity influences cohesin positioning and loop formation (Figure 1). Cohesin and the cohesin loader Nipped‐B‐like protein (NIPBL) were originally observed to be enriched at promoters and enhancers at steady state in mouse ES cells, and both cohesin and NIPBL were found physically associated with the transcription coactivator Mediator and with sequence‐specific transcription factors [50, 51, 52]. Later studies in various cell types using alternative antibodies or tags obtained contrasting results regarding both NIPBL and cohesin steady‐state chromatin association profiles relative to promoters, enhancers, and other cis‐regulatory elements [53, 54, 55, 56]. Given that these studies were performed in both mouse ES cells and several differentiated human cell types, differences in experimental approaches and cell types studied may contribute to these discrepancies. Consequently, a key unresolved issue is whether cohesin is preferentially loaded at active promoters, enhancers, in between these elements, and/or redistributed by transcription after loading.

Cohesin is preferentially loaded near promoters and extrudes chromatin until it encounters convergent CTCF binding sites, forming loops that can juxtapose promoters (P) and enhancers (E). (b) Enhancer‐loading model. Cohesin loads at enhancers and extrudes chromatin toward nearby promoters, stabilizing enhancer–promoter contacts. (c) Pol II as a “moving barrier”. Elongating RNA polymerase II pushes or repositions cohesin complexes along active genes, acting as a moving barrier that redistributes extruding cohesin. (d) R‐loop formation and supercoiling. Progression of RNA polymerase II generates positive supercoiling ahead and negative supercoiling behind the transcription complex, promoting R‐loop formation at negatively supercoiled regions, which can in turn influence local chromatin folding and/or cohesin dynamics.

Several studies support the concept that cohesin often accumulates at promoters, consistent with a model where cohesin is loaded there and subsequently pushed by the transcriptional machinery during elongation (Figure 1a). However, steady‐state occupancy data cannot exclude an alternative scenario in which cohesin loads elsewhere and then accumulates at promoter‐proximal regions. Early work showed that double knockout of CTCF and the cohesin release factor Wings apart‐like protein homolog (WAPL) in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) led to accumulation of cohesin at islands at transcription termination sites of convergent genes, suggesting that elongating RNA polymerase can push cohesin along the DNA until collision with an oncoming cohesin molecule [55]. Importantly, treatment of double KO MEFs with the DRB transcription elongation inhibitor caused accumulation of cohesin at transcription start sites (TSSs) and reduction of cohesin at termination site islands, and both effects could be reversed by washout of the drug. This result is consistent with a model in which cohesin is loaded at the TSS and is pushed downstream by ongoing transcription. Consistent with transcription‐dependent remodeling of cohesin positioning, Heinz et al. showed that inducing high levels of transcription via influenza infection in human macrophages led to widespread, reversible loss and redistribution of cohesin binding upon transcriptional inhibition [57]. A recent study showed that acute depletion of Pol II following mitotic exit reduces chromatin‐bound levels of NIPBL and results in accumulation of cohesin, particularly at inactive TSSs and intergenic regions. Polymer simulations reproduced the observed genome folding defects when cohesin was assumed to load predominantly at Pol II‑occupied promoters [34]. Together, these studies support a model in which cohesin is frequently loaded near active regulatory regions such as promoters, and its subsequent redistribution reflects a balance among translocation, transcriptional pushing, and the presence of CTCF at boundary elements.

On the other hand, an accumulating body of evidence suggests that cohesin may also be loaded at enhancers (Figure 1b). Using calcium activation of human neutrophils as a transcriptional induction model, Zhu et al. showed that NIPBL was recruited to both promoters and enhancers but with different kinetics and specific requirements at enhancers for certain transcriptional and chromatin regulators [58]. Focusing on glucocorticoid receptor as a model, Rinaldi et al. showed that NIPBL is recruited to GR‐responsive enhancers upon dexamethasone induction to mediate long‐range 3D interactions [59]. Interestingly, repeating acute Pol II depletion in human DLD‐1 cells but assaying genome organization with Micro‐C, as opposed to Hi‐C as performed previously, was able to detect a modest loss of a subset E–P loops specifically anchored by Pol II, and computational modeling results suggested preferential loading of cohesin at both promoters and enhancers [60]. However, this experiment involved prolonged Pol II depletion and possibly induced secondary effects. In contrast, Banigan et al. combined CTCF and WAPL knockout in MEFs with computational modeling and concluded that elongating Pol II can interfere with or reposition extruding cohesin complexes but favored a model in which cohesin loads primarily at enhancers and not promoters [54]. Additional modeling and experimental work similarly indicated that facilitated cohesin loading at active enhancers can initiate characteristic “fountain” or “jet” patterns in contact maps and may seed extrusion toward distal promoters [36]. This “moving barrier” effect of Pol II reshapes contact patterns, ultimately positioning cohesin near actively transcribed regions (Figure 1c, reviewed in [13]). As proof of principle, a recombinant single‐molecule assay showed that elongating T7 RNA polymerase can act as a barrier to cohesin translocation along DNA [61]. Overall, these studies converge towards the idea that transcription affects where cohesin loads and how far it extrudes by constraining, redirecting, or locally antagonizing cohesin‐dependent loop formation (reviewed in [62]).

Torsional Stress and R‐Loops Affect Local Chromatin Organization

An understudied area, transcription might also influence loop extrusion and local chromatin folding through polymerase‐driven physical effects. As RNA polymerase progresses along DNA, it induces torsional stress on the surrounding chromatin, generating positive supercoiling ahead of the polymerase and negative supercoiling behind. Excess negative supercoiling can also promote the formation of R‐loops [49] (Figure 1d), which have been implicated in local chromatin organization and can impede cohesin translocation in vitro. Recent studies support a role of transcription‐mediated supercoiling in modulating local chromatin organization and in the establishment and maintenance of chromatin domains [63]. Type II topoisomerases, which are enriched at active regulatory regions and chromatin domain boundaries, resolve transcription‐associated changes in DNA topology to maintain DNA integrity during ongoing transcription [64, 65]. These polymerase‐driven changes in DNA topology may influence how extruding cohesin engages chromatin and reshapes local chromatin folding, concepts requiring further investigation.

Reconstitution of TAD‐Like Structures in the Absence of Transcription and Loop Extrusion

Recent in vitro reconstitution studies have shown that it is possible to recapitulate TAD‐like structures in the absence of transcription and loop extrusion. Oberbeckmann et al. reconstituted a library of 10 Kb regions of Saccharomyces cerevisiae genomic DNA with purified recombinant histone octamers and then added purified DNA‐binding transcription factors and ATP‐dependent chromatin remodelers in order to reposition nucleosomes [66]. This assembly formed chromatin domains, and boundaries were formed at regularly spaced, remodeler‑generated nucleosome arrays around nucleosome‑free transcription factor binding sites. Consistent with these results, deleting S. cerevisiae ISWI and CHD1 chromatin remodelers globally perturbed nucleosome positioning, which weakened short‑range chromatin contacts and domain boundaries [67]. In fact, binding of both human CTCF and Drosophila Su(Hw) architectural proteins corresponds to regions of regularly spaced nucleosomes [68, 69, 70], and new cryo‐EM work has revealed how dimerization of human CTCF promotes nucleosome oligomerization [71]. Disruption of CTCF dimerization led to weakened chromatin looping genome‐wide, suggesting a role for these structures in the formation of higher‐order structures. Altogether, these findings suggest that nucleosome‐free regions are important nucleators of functional boundary elements, and regularly spaced nucleosome arrays reinforced by architectural proteins may be central to formation of robust TAD boundaries.

In addition, reconstitution of Drosophila genomic sequences with chromatin assembly extracts from pre‐ZGA embryos that lack transcriptional activity allowed formation of the well known eve TAD and additional 3D structures resembling mini‐TADs [72]. In this study, in vitro formed boundaries did not correspond to well‐positioned nucleosomes, and formation of the eve TAD was shown to be dependent on the Su(Hw) insulator protein but not cohesin or ATP hydrolysis. Although unable to faithfully recapitulate all aspects of 3D genome organization observed in vivo, these in vitro reconstitution systems allow a starting point for systematic interrogation of when and where transcription may be required to promote different levels of 3D genome organization.

Genome Architecture as a Regulator of Transcription

Disruption of Mammalian CTCF Boundaries With Respect to E–P Communication

One of the earliest and most widely supported models proposed that genome architecture on the TAD level helps dictate transcriptional specificity by insulating regulatory interactions. In this view, insulator elements partition chromosomes into regulatory units that either restrict inappropriate or favor necessary interactions between enhancers and promoters. In addition to enhancer‐blocking or enhancer facilitation depending on their relative position to enhancers and promoters, insulator sequences can act as boundaries to the spread of repressive chromatin. This insulating activity is mediated by architectural proteins such as CTCF in vertebrates and various insulator complexes in Drosophila and likely involves their ability to promote DNA looping interactions through homo‐ and hetero‐oligomerization (reviewed in [73, 74, 75]).

Recent studies in developing mice have demonstrated that TAD boundary strength and its transcriptional consequences depend on local architectural composition, including the number, orientation, and clustering of CTCF motifs, which are thought to define cohesin‐mediated loop extrusion boundaries. Deletion of multiple individual TAD boundaries via CRISPR showed that disruption of boundary integrity can lead to fusion of TADs and rewiring of nearby enhancer–promoter contacts and associated changes in gene expression [76]. Although CTCF has been shown to block cohesin extrusion in an orientation‐dependent manner in vitro [48, 77] and convergently oriented CTCF sites are enriched at TAD boundaries [4], multiple juxtaposed CTCF sites are often observed in various orientations at different loci. Furthermore, many CTCF binding sites are located within TADs and do not correspond to TAD borders, and it remains unknown as to why CTCF binding at some sites can block cohesin while others do not. Studies targeting clustered CTCF binding sites at TAD borders demonstrated that combined deletions often produce stronger cross‐boundary interactions and more pronounced transcriptional effects than single‐site perturbations, although outcomes remain locus‐dependent [29, 78, 79]. At one cluster of FGF genes, even a single CTCF motif deletion at the same boundary was sufficient to weaken insulation, cause aberrant gene expression, and result in developmental defects in vivo [79]. These findings demonstrate that transcriptional consequences of boundary disruption depend on the local architectural composition at each locus, perhaps by altering how efficiently cohesin is positioned and retained at boundaries.

Disruption of TAD boundaries does not uniformly lead to changes in gene expression, and its impact depends on local regulatory wiring and enhancer distance. At the mouse Slc29a3/Unc5b locus harboring ubiquitously expressed, essential genes separated by a TAD border, deletion of CTCF‐bound boundary sites reduced local chromatin architecture and insulation. Remarkably, resultant effects on gene expression were moderate and tissue‐specific [80]. Similarly, during CD8+ T cell differentiation, changes in CTCF‐mediated chromatin interactions were associated with lineage‐specific gene expression patterns but did not completely inhibit transcription at these loci [81]. These observations indicate that disruption in chromatin architecture does not always produce proportional changes in gene expression, and regulatory consequences depend on the local genomic context. Predictably, the dependence of transcription on higher‐order genome organization does vary with enhancer distance. In human K562 and mouse ES cells, genes regulated by long‐range enhancers show higher sensitivity to cohesin or Mediator disruption than genes with short‐range E–P interactions [82, 83, 84]. This distance‐dependent sensitivity suggests that long‐range regulatory interactions, including those that span TAD interiors or boundaries, depend more strongly on cohesin‐mediated loop extrusion whereas short‐range E–P interactions may be less sensitive to changes in TAD structures. Along with the locus‐specific boundary studies above, these results may reflect a combination of boundary architecture and local regulatory features, including the density and activity of nearby enhancers and promoters, such that boundaries insulating highly active regulatory regions may show stronger transcriptional effects when disrupted. Live‑cell imaging and modeling at the Fbn2 TAD in mouse ES cells estimate that the fully looped CTCF–cohesin state is both rare and transient, with a median loop lifetime of approximately 10–30 min [85]. Related live‑cell studies of CTCF, cohesin, and TAD boundary pairing at additional loci in mouse ES and differentiated cells similarly suggest that domain‑level contacts frequently form and dissolve over tens‐of‐minutes timescales [86, 87]. Taken together, these observations are consistent with domain architecture providing a baseline, yet dynamic framework for potential regulation, while transcriptional activity, enhancer distance, and local regulatory features influence how strongly gene expression depends on this higher order organization.

Drosophila Tethering Elements and Meta‐Loops Can Overcome TAD‐Level Organization

In Drosophila, certain regulatory elements have been identified that can promote specific long‐range interactions when enhancers and promoters reside in different TADs (Figure 2). While developmental genes show strong enrichment for intra‐TAD E–P interactions, a minority of E–P interactions in Drosophila embryos can occur across TAD boundaries [88]. One recently identified class of regulatory elements that can promote long‐range E–P interactions are termed tethering elements (Figure 2a), which have been shown to facilitate efficient activation of certain developmental genes residing in Hox clusters [89]. Tethering elements can also mediate P–P interactions between pairs of distant (∼250 kb) co‐regulated paralogous genes in Drosophila embryos [90]. In these cases, activation of one gene influences the expression of another distant gene, suggesting that this tethering mechanism allows for coordinated regulation by shared enhancers. At a larger scale, meta‐domains in the Drosophila nervous system were found to be higher‐order chromatin arrangements in which multiple distant TADs interact in 3D space [91] (Figure 2b). These meta‐domains are enriched for genes expressed in neurons and require the activity of architectural proteins such as GAGA factor (GAF) or CTCF. Together, these observations indicate that genome architecture does not simply act as a barrier to regulatory interactions but can be reorganized by specific elements to promote long‐range E–P contacts for particular sets of co‐regulated genes. Questions remain as to under what conditions domain boundaries constrain regulatory interactions versus allow coordinated gene expression across TADs. Similar long‐range cross‑TAD contacts are also observed at certain mammalian loci such as Sox2 and Shh [92, 93]. Moreover, CpG‐rich promoters and orphan CpG islands in mouse ES cells have been proposed to act as tethering elements that promote communication between poised enhancers and distal target genes [94] (reviewed in [95]), although it remains to be determined to what extent these CpG‑island‐based mechanisms are mechanistically analogous to Drosophila tethering elements. Identification of additional regulatory proteins involved in these long‐range interactions should shed more light into these mechanisms.

(b) Meta‐domain and meta‐loop at megabase scale. Functional Coupling of Transcription and Genome Architecture

One reason transcription and genome architecture cannot be clearly separated conceptually is that several key proteins appear to perform both architectural and transcriptional roles. Multiple factors that were originally identified as transcriptional regulators also contribute to genome organization, while a number of architectural proteins have been found to affect transcription. This functional overlap complicates attempts to assign strictly or even primarily architectural or transcriptional roles to individual factors.

One classic example of functional overlap between transcriptional and architectural regulation is vertebrate CTCF. CTCF was originally identified as a transcription factor that regulates Myc gene expression [96, 97] before it was found to be responsible for chromatin insulator activity at the chicken beta‐globin locus [98]. Further work analyzing 3D genome organization first implicated CTCF [2] and later showed that CTCF is a key factor required for the formation of TADs in mammalian cells [2, 3]. Nevertheless, a large number of CTCF binding sites are not localized at TAD borders, and CTCF binding within TADs could reflect functions independent of its architectural roles as an insulator protein. Although a small number of gene expression changes were observed in cells acutely depleted of CTCF in mouse ES cells [3], more extensive gene expression and chromatin accessibility changes were observed in synchronized CTCF knockout mouse rod photoreceptor cells [99]. It remains to be determined whether CTCF may play a more direct role in transcriptional regulation in specific cell types, such as those derived from the nervous system (reviewed in [100, 101]).

Because housekeeping gene promoters are often located at TAD boundaries, there is substantial overlap of transcription factor and architectural protein binding at these regions. In Drosophila, the Motif1 promoter‐associated sequence was found to be enriched at TAD borders [102, 103], although depletion of the Motif1 binding protein (M1BP) transcription factor could not be assessed for changes in TAD organization due to disruption of cell cycle [23]. M1BP was later shown to physically interact with the well‐established architectural protein CP190 [20, 21, 104] at Motif1‐containing promoters near TAD borders, and depletion of either factor reduces chromatin accessibility and alters local compaction [103]. Moreover, the insulator protein Boundary element‐associated factor of 32 kD (BEAF‐32) [105] is highly enriched at housekeeping gene promoters and works cooperatively with M1BP and DNA replication‐related element factor (DREF) to support housekeeping gene expression [106, 107]. Intriguingly, CP190 itself was shown to also be required for Motif1‐dependent transcription, raising the possibility that its function in transcription may be central to its ability to promote the formation of a TAD boundary [103]. Consistent with the functional redundancy of Drosophila boundary factors, these proteins act in overlapping complexes at housekeeping promoters, such that loss of individual factors M1BP, CP190, or BEAF‐32 leads to modest weakening of local boundary function rather than global disruption of TAD organization (reviewed in [75]).

Functional overlap between transcriptional and architectural regulators is particularly evident at early stages of development. In Drosophila embryos, the pioneer transcription factor Zelda and the architectural protein GAF are necessary for chromatin opening during ZGA [108, 109, 110]. These proteins are found to be highly enriched in early regulatory regions and boundaries of newly formed topological domains [22, 108]. Consistent with an architectural role for Zelda, cis‐regulatory chromatin loops in early embryos form before TAD establishment and before robust transcriptional activation, and their formation depends on Zelda occupancy [39]. These observations suggest that pioneer factors establish accessible regulatory regions that contribute to early domain organization, whereas subsequent transcription may be needed to further shape and maintain these structures during development.

Conclusion and Prospects

The interplay between transcription and three‐dimensional genome organization has been investigated in various systems, but the exact relationships at various scales are not yet fully understood. Variability in chromatin structure and transcriptional regulation observed across loci, cell types, developmental contexts, and temporal windows can complicate interpretation of experimental results. Current observations suggest that transcription and genome architecture are not strictly hierarchical but instead influence each other in a scale‐ and context‐dependent manner. While large‐scale features such as compartments and TADs remain largely stable after transcription is perturbed, transcription exerts stronger effects at finer spatial scales through local chromatin looping, RNA‐mediated interactions, and polymerase‐driven mechanical forces. In addition, many regulatory factors contribute to both transcriptional control and chromatin architecture, further blurring the distinction between structural and regulatory mechanisms. An emerging idea is that these processes are coordinated through shared regulatory factors, and that transcriptional and architectural functions are often mechanistically inseparable.

Several key questions remain unresolved. Boundary disruption can produce substantial transcriptional changes at some loci, yet boundary function is not explained solely by CTCF binding or architectural protein occupancy. Current studies indicate that features such as local E–P wiring, transcriptional activity at boundary‐proximal genes, and the number, orientation, and clustering of CTCF motifs all contribute to how strongly a given boundary constrains regulatory interactions. However, how these features combine to determine boundary strength at individual loci remains unclear. Differences in genome organization across species further suggest that although genome organization itself is indispensable, the underlying mechanisms may not be fully conserved. In particular, whether loop extrusion during interphase occurs as a general mechanism in organisms aside from vertebrates remains to be determined.

Future studies combining acute perturbations with high‐resolution and single‐cell approaches will be critical for determining whether changes in chromatin structure precede or follow transcriptional events at specific loci. Disentangling their respective contributions will likely require experimental designs that perturb both transcription simultaneously with acute depletion of architectural proteins. Ideally, such perturbations would be applied before and after the establishment of domain‐scale genome organization, although suitable model systems that clearly capture a “pre‑TAD” state remain to be identified. In vitro reconstitution systems will allow the identification of minimal requirements to form boundaries at different loci as well as systematic study of the contribution of transcription to this process. Current evidence suggests that chromatin accessibility and some precursor of domain‑level organization are established before robust transcription acts to refine these structures, but definitive temporal ordering of causal events remains to be determined. Live‐cell 3D imaging of chromatin and nascent transcripts could help clarify the temporal events regarding how chromatin interactions change during gene activation. Comparative analyses across organisms and developmental stages will help determine how the relationship between transcription and genome organization is established and dynamically regulated during development.
