Concurrent RUNX1::MECOM and RUNX1::PRDM16 Fusions from a Novel Genomic Breakpoint: First Report of a Dual-Fusion Mechanism in Acute Myeloid Leukemia
Authors: Siyu Chen, Shengwang Wu, Jing Zhang, Xing Qiang, Siheng Liu, Wuchen Yang, Xiangui Peng, Xi Zhang, Jun Ren, Cheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile complex fusions involving three chromosomes are already uncommon in AML, the formation of two distinct fusion genes from an identical breakpoint is exceptionally rare. This paper reports the adult case of acute myeloid leukemia harboring dual fusion genes RUNX1::MECOM and RUNX1::PRDM16, both originating from the same RUNX1 breakpoint (chr21:36206707, hg19). The 62-year-old male patient presented with primary refractory acute myeloid leukemia (AML), failed to achieve remission after induction chemotherapy, and had an overall survival of only 3 months. The RUNX1::PRDM16 translocation was cryptic and resulted in PRDM16 overexpression, which is associated with poor prognosis. Integrated molecular profiling revealed concurrent mutations in FLT3-ITD, U2AF1, and PTPN11, collectively indicating a very high disease risk. This case provides the first evidence for the novel mechanism of dual fusion gene formation from a single genomic breakpoint.

Introduction

AML, the most common type of acute leukemia in adults, is characterized by abnormal proliferation and differentiation arrest of myeloid cells. Its pathogenesis involves a multi-step clonal evolution driven by genetic events such as translocations or mutations.1 However, AML exhibits high molecular heterogeneity and age-specificity, making it a group of diseases rather than a single entity. As a result, despite all patients currently receiving similar chemotherapeutic regimens, the intrinsic complexity of AML leads to suboptimal overall cure rates.2 Somatic mutations and chromosomal translocations involving RUNX1 are frequently observed in myelodysplastic syndromes and both myeloid and lymphoid leukemias. The first RUNX1 gene fusion was reported in 1991.3 To date, over 50 distinct chromosomal translocations involving the RUNX1 gene have been identified.4 The most common types include t(3;21), t(8;21), and the pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) associated t(12;21), which generate the RUNX1::MECOM, RUNX1::RUNX1T1, and ETV6::RUNX1 fusion genes, respectively.4 Complex translocations involving RUNX1 are relatively rare, with most cases related to t(8;21), accounting for approximately 6–10% of RUNX1::RUNX1T1 fusions.4 The t(1;3;21)(p36;q26;q22) complex translocation identified in this patient involves three chromosomes and, to our knowledge, represents the first documented case of this specific rearrangement in AML. This case provides a unique model for understanding how a single genomic breakpoint can simultaneously engage two different partner genes, and suggests that this region may represent a fragile site prone to complex rearrangements. Clinically, the patient was refractory to conventional chemotherapy and survived only three months, underscoring the aggressive nature of such multiple‑hit genetic profiles and the potential need for early allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in similar high‑risk patients.

Case Presentation

A 62-year-old male patient developed headache without obvious cause in July 2024 and admitted to the Hematology Department of our hospital on December 12, 2024. Bone marrow smear revealed hypercellularity with granulocytic series accounting for 69.5% and erythroid series accounting for 18.5% (Figure 1A). The granulocytic lineage was hyperplastic, showing a markedly increased proportion of blasts (55.5%). Later-stage granulocytes exhibited megaloblastic changes, poor specific granule dysplasia development, and nuclear abnormalities. Erythroid hyperplasia was noted predominantly involving intermediate and late normoblasts, with morphological abnormalities including megaloblastic changes, binucleation, budding nuclei, flower-like nuclei, and internuclear bridges. Lymphocytes accounted for 8.5% and appeared morphologically normal. Megakaryocytes were sparse, with only 7 observed per entire slide, including 2 platelet-producing forms. Platelets were seldom seen. Bone marrow biopsy showed diffuse infiltration by atypical lymphoid cells (blasts) with scant cytoplasm and finely granular chromatin (Figure 1B). Flow cytometric revealed that the CD45-dim population accounted for approximately 68.1% of total nucleated cells. Within this subpopulation, CD34 positive cells accounted for 45.87% of all nucleated cells, showing expression of CD34, CD117, CD13, CD33, CD38 (dim), HLA-DR, CD123, and CD7 while lacking expression of CD19, CD10, CD22, CD4, CD8, and CD3. In addition, cytogenetic analysis exhibited the karyotype 46, XY, t(3;21)(q26.2;q22)[20] (Figure 1C). FISH analysis demonstrated EVI1 gene rearrangement was examined in 96% of the cells (Figure 1D). Next-generation sequencing (NGS) of multi-gene panel analysis of this sample identified an internal tandem duplication somatic mutation in the FLT3 gene (FLT3-ITD) with a variant allele frequency of 0.714, which is indicative of a high mutation level. Additionally, the analysis revealed concomitant missense mutations in PTPN1 (c.1508G>A, p.Gly503Ala) and U2AF1 (c.101C>T, p.Ser34Phe) (Table 1). Total RNA was extracted from bone marrow aspirate, and RNA‑seq was performed on the DNBSEQ-T7sequencer (MGI Tech, Shenzhen, China) platform. Fusion genes were identified using STAR‑Fusion and Arriba pipelines, and validated by Sanger sequencing with primers spanning the breakpoints. Transcriptome sequencing identified that the RUNX1 gene (chr21:36206707, exon 6) fused with the MECOM gene (chr3:169099312, exon 2) and the PRDM16 gene (chr1:3102689, exon 2) respectively, resulting in the formation of two fusion genes, RUNX1::MECOM and RUNX1::PRDM16. Both fusion genes were confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Figure 1E–H and Table 2). The patient began induction therapy with the VAA (Venetoclax, 100 mg d1, 200 mg d2, 400 mg, d3-28; Azacitidine, 75 mg/m2, d1-7; Cytarabine, 10 mg/m2/12h, d1-7) regimens on December 21, 2024. After completion of one cycle of induction therapy, bone marrow smear examination still showed 69% myeloblasts and peripheral blood smear displayed 2% myeloblasts. Minimal residual disease (MRD) was also detected by flow cytometry at a level of 23.22%. Given the non-remission of disease, continued hospitalization for chemotherapy was further recommended. Nevertheless, the patient and family refused and chose to discharge. The patient ultimately died three months after diagnosis.

Discussion

In this case, both the RUNX1::MECOM and RUNX1::PRDM16 fusion genes were detected, sharing an identical RUNX1 breakpoint located within exon 6, which preserves the Runt homology domain (RHD). The RUNX1 (AML1) gene is situated on the long arm of chromosome 21, spans approximately 261 kb, and encodes the DNA-binding subunit of the core-binding factor, playing a crucial regulatory role in hematopoiesis and vascular development.6 The t(3;21)(q26;q22) translocation involving RUNX1 has been observed in therapy-related myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) and during the blast phase of chronic myeloid leukemia (BC-CML). This rearrangement leads to the fusion of the RUNX1 gene at 21q22 with either the MECOM (MDS1 and EVI1 complex locus) gene or the RPL22L1 (also known as EAP, ribosomal protein L22-like 1) gene at 3q26, generating multiple fusion transcripts.7,8 The RUNX1::MECOM fusion protein promotes leukemogenesis through several mechanisms: it ablates TGF-β–mediated growth inhibition and stimulates proliferation, acts as a dominant-negative regulator of normal transcriptional activation, thereby blocking myeloid differentiation and enhancing proliferation, counteracts the growth-inhibitory effects of transforming growth factor and suppresses JNK activity, protecting cells from stress-induced apoptosis.6,9,10 According to the European LeukemiaNet (ELN) 2022 risk stratification guidelines, AML with the RUNX1::MECOM fusion is classified as adverse risk.11

RUNX1::PRDM16 is an infrequent but recurrent fusion gene. To date, dozens of related fusion cases have been reported in patients with AML, CML, ALL, and anemia (Table 3). One study detected a 21.4% frequency (3/14 cases, two CMLs, one ALL) of the t(1;21)(p36;q22) translocation in patients with Ph-positive leukemia; among these, two patients also harbored RUNX1 mutations, resulting in biallelic alteration of RUNX1. Previous reports suggest that the RUNX1::PRDM16 fusion gene generated by t(1;21)(p36;q22) typically retains the RHD of RUNX1 and the breakpoint in the PRDM16 gene is generally located within intron 1 or exon 2, thereby preserving nearly the entire coding sequence of PRDM16. In the present case, the fusion breakpoint lies within exon 2 of the PRDM16 gene. PRDM16 (also named MDS/EVI1-like1 gene, MEL1) encodes a zinc finger protein that shares 63% sequence similarity with MDS1/EVI1. Moreover, the two genes exhibit a similar structure, both containing a PR domain (PRDI-BF1-RIZ1 homologous domain) with a high degree of homology in this region.12,13 Their DNA-binding consensus sequences also overlap, suggesting that PRDM16 may possess DNA-binding and transcriptional regulatory activities similar to those of EVI1.14 In AML or myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), both PRDM16 and MDS1/EVI1 can be transcriptionally activated by translocations involving the 3q21 region, leading to diseases with similar characteristics.12,14,15 This indicates that the two genes may contribute to leukemogenesis through shared molecular mechanisms. The RUNX1::PRDM16 fusion gene leads to upregulation of PRDM16 expression.20 Studies indicate that high expression of PRDM16 is significantly associated with shortened overall survival (OS) and event-free survival (EFS) inAML patients (3-year OS: 51% vs 81%, P < 0.001; 3-year EFS: 32% vs 64%, P < 0.001), and this adverse prognostic impact is independent of other karyotypic or genetic abnormalities, with the exception of the NPM1 mutations. The present case also harbors an FLT3-ITD mutation. According to the 2022 ELN risk stratification, patients with FLT3-ITD in the absence of other high-risk genetic features are classified as intermediate risk.11 However, since this patient also carries the adverse-risk genetic abnormality RUNX1::MECOM, the overall risk should still be classified as high risk. Moreover, existing research has shown that high expression of the PRDM16 gene can further refine risk stratification in FLT3-ITD–positive patients, the high-expression group had a 3-year OS of only 30%, significantly lower than the 70% observed in the low-expression group (P < 0.001).24,25 Patients with this fusion gene often derive little benefit from conventional chemotherapy; allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation may represent a potentially effective treatment option for this population.26 The negative prognostic impact of additional genetic abnormalities has also been demonstrated in other hematological malignancies; for example, comorbidity burden is significantly associated with poor prognosis in CML patients.27

The coexistence of two distinct RUNX1 fusions from an identical breakpoint raises several biological questions. First, the breakpoint region (chr21:36206707) may possess structural features, such as chromatin accessibility or DNA fragility, that permit concurrent rearrangements with multiple loci. Second, as PRDM16 is a structural and functional paralog of MECOM, both fusion proteins retain the RUNX1 RHD but replace its C‑terminal domain, potentially exerting cooperative or additive effects on transcriptional dysregulation and myeloid differentiation blockade. Third, simultaneous expression of two RHD‑containing fusions may competitively or synergistically interfere with wild‑type RUNX1, creating a “double‑hit” scenario that accelerates leukemogenesis. These hypotheses warrant functional validation in future studies. Due to the rarity of the RUNX1::PRDM16 fusion, specific risk stratification guidance for this genetic alteration is currently lacking. Nevertheless, available case reports indicate that patients carrying the RUNX1::PRDM16 fusion gene are associated with dismal outcomes and that the fusion may be involved in the transformation of CML from chronic phase to blast crisis.20,23 Current reports also suggest that AML patients with this fusion gene often derive little benefit from conventional chemotherapy, and allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation may represent a potentially effective treatment option.15,17,18

It was also noted that the patient harbored concurrent U2AF1 S34F and PTPN11 G503A mutations. According to current diagnostic criteria, U2AF1 mutations in the absence of TP53 mutation are categorized as AML with myelodysplasia-related gene mutations. Under the 2022 ELN risk stratification, such genetic abnormalities are classified as adverse risk, indicating a poor prognosis.11 In AML, the U2AF1 S34F mutation induces aberrant splicing of key translational regulators such as EIF4A2, triggering an integrated stress response. This systematically enhances the stress-adaptive capacity of leukemic cells and thereby drives chemotherapy resistance.28 The PTPN11 gene encodes the SHP2 protein, a protein tyrosine phosphatase that normally participates in cellular signal regulation. Mutations in PTPN11 lead to hyperactivation of SHP2, causing persistent activation of multiple signaling pathways including RAS/MAPK, PI3K/AKT, and PD-1/PD-L1. This results in dysregulated cell proliferation, differentiation, and immune evasion, and is closely associated with tumor progression, drug resistance, and adverse prognosis.29 Similar findings have been reported linking Akt phosphorylation to poor prognosis in AML.30 Studies have reported that PTPN11 mutations are linked to poor outcomes in AML patients without NPM1 mutations when treated with intensive induction chemotherapy.31 Thus, this patient presents four distinct prognostic genetic abnormalities: RUNX1::MECOM and U2AF1 S34F, which are classified as high-risk by ELN; RUNX1::PRDM16 fusion and PTPN11 mutation, although not formally categorized, are both associated with adverse outcomes. Together, these findings indicate an extremely adverse overall risk for this patient.

This AML patient had an OS of only 3 months and failed to achieve remission after one cycle of induction chemotherapy. The case exhibited high-frequency mutations in FLT3-ITD and U2AF1; due to the availability of only a single time-point sample, the precise clonal evolution dynamics along with a low-frequency mutation in PTPN11. We speculate that the FLT3-ITD and U2AF1 mutations may have cooperated with the RUNX1::MECOM and RUNX1::PRDM16 fusions in driving leukemogenesis, with the PTPN11 mutation emerging subsequently in a subclone. However, this hypothesis could not be delineated due to the lack of longitudinal samples, and functional studies would be required for validation. Among the 13 reported cases of RUNX1::PRDM16 fusion, conventional karyotyping yielded a positive detection rate of 38.5% (5/13), indicating that this translocation is often cryptic and falls below the sensitivity threshold of standard cytogenetics. Thus the identification should rely on NGS, ddPCR, qPCR or FISH. NGS has proven valuable in detecting cryptic fusions that are otherwise missed by conventional methods, and has enabled genotype‑driven therapeutic approaches across various tumor types.32

In summary, we report a case of AML in which a single RUNX1 breakpoint concurrently gave rise to dual fusion genes RUNX1::MECOM and RUNX1::PRDM16, suggesting a potential mechanism that enriches the molecular spectrum of RUNX1-related fusion leukemias. However, this hypothesis requires validation in future functional studies and larger patient cohorts. The patient presented with primary refractory disease and died within a short period. The molecular profile was exceptionally complex, integrating RUNX1::MECOM, RUNX1::PRDM16, U2AF1, FLT3-ITD, and PTPN11 mutations, collectively constituting a “genomic storm”. The case proved refractory to conventional chemotherapy and was associated with an extremely poor prognosis. Unfortunately, the patient did not have the opportunity to undergo hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Moving forward, more aggressive therapeutic strategies should be considered for patients harboring such multiple adverse risk and chemotherapy‑resistant genetic abnormalities.
