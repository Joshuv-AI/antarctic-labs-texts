Density of rational languages under shift invariant measures
Valérie Berthé, Herman Goulet-Ouellet, Dominique Perrin

Abstract
We study density of rational languages under shift invariant probability measures on spaces of two-sided infinite words, which generalizes the classical notion of density studied in formal languages and automata theory. The density for a language is defined as the limit in average (if it exists) of the probability that a word of a given length belongs to the language. We establish the existence of densities for all rational languages under all shift invariant measures. We also give explicit formulas under certain conditions, in particular when the language is aperiodic. Our approach combines tools and ideas from semigroup theory and ergodic theory.

The natural density for a language
L
on a finite alphabet
A
is defined as the limit
in average (if it exists) of the probability that a word of length
n
belongs to
L
,
where letters are drawn independently with equal probabilities. This notion can be traced
back to the work of Berstel
[
5
]
, who took inspiration from
Schützenberger
[
37
]
. It has been widely studied in the context of automata theory, logic and the theory of codes
[
7
,
36
,
26
,
19
,
20
,
10
,
39
,
22
,
23
]
, and also appears in ergodic theory, for instance in the work of Veech
[
40
,
41
]
. Densities have also been studied in the more general case where words are drawn with a Bernoulli measure, i.e. letters are drawn independently with possibly different probabilities
[
7
]
.

This paper is related with recent work on the density of group languages
[
9
]
using a more general notion of density motivated by symbolic dynamics. Let
\mu
be a probability measure on the space
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
of two-sided infinite words on the alphabet
A
. Then the density of a language
L
with respect to
\mu
is the limit

\delta_{\mu}(L)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mu(\{x\in A^{\mathbb{Z}}\mid x_{0}\dots x_{i-1}\in L\}),

(1)

if it exists. The classical definition discussed above corresponds to the case where
\mu
is a Bernoulli measure. Our main result states that the density of a rational language
L
under a shift invariant probability measure
\mu
always exists (
Theorem

20
). A shift invariant measure is a probability measure that behaves well with respect to left extensions of words (see Equation (
5
)). The proof closely combines dynamical and algebraic methods.

On the dynamical side, the proof relies mainly on the construction of a skew product between the shift space (made of two-sided infinite words) that supports the measure

\mu
and an
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}
-class of the finite monoid
M
defining the rational language
L
(the transition monoid). The skew product we use is closely related to the notion of wreath product used in semigroup and automata theory
[
16
, Chapter 1, Section 10]
. We construct a natural measure on the skew product, called the weighted counting measure, which leads to a closed formula for the density under the condition that this measure is ergodic (
Theorem

27
). This generalizes known results for the case of Bernoulli measures. We also consider in
Theorem

16
aperiodic languages (also called star-free) for which densities are proved to exist in a strong sense.

On the algebraic side, this construction relies crucially on the theory of Green’s relations and on the key notion of the
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
-class associated with a shift. Let the monoid
M
be the image of
A^{*}
by a morphism
\varphi
. The
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
-class of
M
associated with
X
is the set of generators of the least ideal of
M
which meets the image by
\varphi
of the language of the shift
X
.
Therefore, it can be called the minimal
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
-class of the monoid
M
with respect to the shift
X
.
The use of such a
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
-class is a useful tool in automata theory, and appears in several
different contexts. See
[
13
]
for a survey on the use of this idea,
originating in the proof by Schützenberger of the characterization of aperiodic languages
and
[
29
]
for an application close to the present paper.

Let us give a brief outline of the paper. In
Section

1
we present preliminaries on symbolic dynamics. In
Section

2
we show how to calculate the densities of ideals of
A^{*}
. In
Section

3
, we study the
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
-class associated with a shift space in a finite monoid and derive an explicit formula for the density of aperiodic languages (
Theorem

16
). We also discuss connections with the notion of degree of an automaton and decidability results concerning the
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
-class. In
Section

4
, we introduce the skew products and weighted counting measures, give the proof of our main result (
Theorem

20
), along with an explicit expression for the density when the weighted counting measure is ergodic (
Theorem

27
). In
Section

5
, we consider algebraic properties of the density (see
Corollary

28
). Lastly,
Section

6
contains a discussion on the case of sofic measures, motivated by
Corollary

28
.
Additionally, we provide in
Appendix

A
a brief introduction to semigroup theory, focusing on ideals and Green’s relations.

Symbolic dynamics

This section covers some necessary material from symbolic dynamics. We refer to
[
14
]
for a complete exposition on the topic, including proofs, and also to
[
17
,
34
]
as alternative sources. For more specialized texts on topological dynamics and ergodic theory, we refer to
[
31
,
42
]
.

1.1
Topological dynamical systems

We first recall some terminology from topological dynamics. A
topological dynamical system

is a pair
(X,T)
of a compact metric space
X
and a continuous transformation
T\colon X\to X
. A subset
Y\subseteq X
is called
stable
if
T(Y)\subseteq Y
. A nonempty topological dynamical system
(X,T)
is
minimal
if the only closed stable subsets of
X
are
X
and
\varnothing
. Equivalently, all
x\in X
have dense forward orbits
\{T^{n}(x)\mid n\geq 0\}
. As a weaker notion,
(X,T)
is
transitive
if there exists
x\in X
with dense forward orbit. Transitivity is equivalent to the condition that
U\cap T^{-n}V\neq\emptyset
for all pairs of open sets
U,V\subseteq X
.

Let
\mu
be a Borel probability measure on
X
. The
support
of
\mu
is the smallest closed set of measure 1. We say that
\mu
is
invariant
if
\mu(T^{-1}U)=\mu(U)
for every Borel set
U\subseteq X
. Note that the support of an invariant measure is a closed stable subset.

The measure
\mu
is called
ergodic
if it is invariant and every Borel set such that
T^{-1}(U)=U
has measure
0
or
1
. By Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem, ergodicity can be interpreted as a form of asymptotic independence. More precisely, an invariant measure
\mu
is ergodic if and only if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mu(U\cap T^{-i}V)=\mu(U)\mu(V)

(2)

for every pair
U,V
of Borel sets (see
[
31
]
). In particular, note that the support of an ergodic measure is a transitive system. As a stronger property, the measure
\mu
is
mixing
if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(U\cap T^{-n}V)=\mu(U)\mu(V)

(3)

for every pair
U,V
of Borel sets. Every mixing measure is ergodic but the converse is false.

Let
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}(X,T)
be the set of invariant probability measures on
(X,T)
and
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}(X,T)
be the subset of those measures which are ergodic. We can view
\mathcal{M}
as a subset of the dual
\mathcal{C}(X,\mathbb{R})^{*}
of the space of continuous maps
X\to\mathbb{R}
. If we equip
\mathcal{C}(X,\mathbb{R})^{*}
with the weak-
*
topology, then
\mathcal{M}
is a compact convex subset and
\mathcal{E}
is its set of extreme points. In particular if the system
(X,T)
has only one invariant measure, it must be ergodic; and we call
(X,T)

uniquely ergodic
. Standard results from functional analysis imply that any invariant measure can be decomposed in terms of ergodic measures. More precisely, for every invariant measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
, there exists a Borel probability measure
\tau
on
\mathcal{E}
such that

\mu=\int_{\mathcal{E}}\nu\,d\tau(\nu).

(4)

More details can be found in e.g.
[
32
, Chapter 12]
.

1.2
Shift spaces

We now turn to symbolic dynamics. Let
A
be a finite alphabet. We denote by
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
the set of two-sided infinite sequences over
A
equipped with the product topology, where
A
has the discrete topology. We denote by
S
the shift transformation on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
, defined by
y=S(x)
if
y_{n}=x_{n+1}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. A
shift space

X
on the alphabet
A
is, by definition, a closed subset of
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
which is stable under the shift transformation. Note that
(X,S)
is a topological dynamical system.

Let
A^{*}
be the free monoid on
A
,
\varepsilon
be the empty word, and
A^{+}=A^{*}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}
. We denote by
\mathcal{L}(X)
the set of finite words
w
which appear in the elements
x\in X
and by
\mathcal{L}_{n}(X)
the set of words of length
n
in
\mathcal{L}(X)
. For a shift space, transitivity and minimality can be characterized in terms of the language
\mathcal{L}(X)
. A shift space
X
is transitive if and only if for every
u,v\in\mathcal{L}(X)
, there is some
w\in\mathcal{L}(X)
such that
uwv\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. Moreover
X
is minimal if and only if for every
n\geq 0
, there is an
N\geq 0
such that every word of
\mathcal{L}_{n}(X)
appears in every word of
\mathcal{L}_{N}(X)
. Shift spaces which are transitive are also called
irreducible
.

For words
u,v
of length
m,n
respectively, we define the right and two-sided cylinders by

[v]_{X}=\{x\in X\mid x_{0}\dots x_{n-1}=v\},\qquad[u\cdot v]_{X}=\{x\in X\mid x_{-m}\dots x_{n-1}=uv\}.

The two-sided cylinders form a clopen basis for the topology of
X
. For
L,K\subseteq A^{*}
, we define

[L]_{X}=\bigcup_{w\in L}[w]_{X},\qquad[L\cdot K]_{X}=\bigcup_{u\in L,v\in K}[u\cdot v]_{X}.

Let
\mu
be a Borel probability measure on a shift space
X
. By a slight abuse of notation, we also denote by
\mu
the map which assigns to a word
w\in\mathcal{L}(X)
the number
\mu([w]_{X})
. With this notation, we have
\mu(\varepsilon)=1
and

\sum_{a\in A}\mu(wa)=\mu(w).

We extend this to subsets
L\subseteq\mathcal{L}(X)
by letting
\mu(L)=\sum_{w\in L}\mu(w)
.
When the cylinders
[u]_{X}
for
u\in L
are disjoint, we have
\mu(L)=\mu([L]_{X})
.
Moreover, if
\mu
is an invariant measure, then

\mu(w)=\mu([w\cdot\varepsilon]_{X})=\sum_{a\in A}\mu(aw).

(5)

When the cylinders
[u\cdot\varepsilon]_{X}
for
u\in L
are disjoint, we have
\mu(L)=\mu([L\cdot\varepsilon]_{X})
.

Observe that the support of an invariant measure on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
is a shift space
X
of measure 1, and when the measure is ergodic,
X
is irreducible.

A
Bernoulli measure
is the simplest case of an ergodic measure on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
. The values
\mu(w)
for
w\in A^{*}
are given by a morphism
\mu\colon A^{*}\to[0,1]
such that
\sum_{a\in A}\mu(a)=1
. It corresponds, in classical terms of probability theory, to a sequence
(\zeta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of independent identically distributed random variables, where
\zeta_{n}\colon x\mapsto x_{n}
. A Bernoulli measure is in fact mixing. Further mixing examples can be found among sofic measures, also called rational measures or hidden Markov measures
[
20
,
11
]
.
See
Section

6
for a discussion on this topic.

Another important source of examples is the following. A
substitution
is a monoid morphism
\sigma\colon A^{*}\to A^{*}
. The substitution
\sigma
is
primitive
if there is an
n\geq 0
such that every
b\in A
occurs in every
\sigma^{n}(a)
. The shift
X(\sigma)
, called a
substitution shift
, is the set of all
x\in A^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that all words in
\mathcal{L}(x)
appear in some
\sigma^{n}(a)
,
n\geq 0
,
a\in A
. If
\sigma
is primitive then
X(\sigma)
is minimal, and uniquely ergodic
[
27
]
.

Example 1
.

The Fibonacci substitution is the morphism
\sigma\colon\{a,b\}^{*}\to\{a,b\}^{*}
defined by
\sigma\colon a\mapsto ab,b\mapsto a
. It is primitive and the substitution shift
X=X(\sigma)
is called the
Fibonacci shift
. A description of its unique ergodic measure can be found e.g. in
[
14
, Example 3.8.19]
.

The Fibonacci shift also belongs to the family of
Sturmian shifts
, whose definition may be recalled in
[
25
]
. More precisely, the Fibonacci shift is Sturmian of slope
(3-\sqrt{5})/2
. The following is an example of an automatic sequence (see
[
1
]
).

Example 2
.

The primitive substitution
\sigma\colon a\mapsto ab,b\mapsto ba
is called the
Thue–Morse substitution
. The shift
X(\sigma)
is called the
Thue–Morse shift
. Its unique ergodic measure is described in
[
14
, Example 3.8.20]
.

1.3
Density of a language

Let
L
be a language on
A
and let
\mu
be an ergodic measure on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
with support
X
. We define the
density
of
L
relative to
\mu
as

\delta_{\mu}(L)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mu(L\cap A^{i}),

whenever the limit exists. In other words
\delta_{\mu}(L)
is the Cesàro limit of
(\mu(L\cap A^{n}))_{n=0}^{\infty}
. When the classical limit of
(\mu(L\cap A^{n}))_{n=0}^{\infty}
exists, we say that
L
has a density
in the strong sense
. It is known that when
\mu
is a Bernoulli measure, the density of any rational language exists, and if
\mu
has rational values on letters, all densities are rational numbers
[
5
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Let us note some basic properties of the density. If
L
has a density, then
0\leq\delta_{\mu}(L)\leq 1
, as
0\leq\mu(L\cap A^{i})\leq 1
for every
i\geq 0
. Moreover, since
\mu(w)=0
when
w\notin\mathcal{L}(X)
, we have
\delta_{\mu}(L)=\delta_{\mu}(L\cap\mathcal{L}(X))
. Thus if
L,K
satisfy
L\cap\mathcal{L}(X)=K\cap\mathcal{L}(X)
then
\delta_{\mu}(L)=\delta_{\mu}(K)
. The density is also finitely additive: if
L,K
have densities and
L\cap K=\varnothing
, then
L\cup K
has density
\delta_{\mu}(L\cup K)=\delta_{\mu}(L)+\delta_{\mu}(K)
. Additionally, we have
\delta_{\mu}(A^{*}\setminus L)=1-\delta_{\mu}(L)
, and if
L\cup L^{\prime}=A^{*}
then
L\cap L^{\prime}
has density
\delta_{\mu}(L\cap L^{\prime})=\delta_{\mu}(L)+\delta_{\mu}(L^{\prime})-1
. However, the density of an intersection
L\cap K
might not exist, even when the density of both
L
and
K
exist.

Example 3
.

On a fixed finite alphabet
A
, consider the two languages:

L=\{w\in A^{*}:|w|\equiv 1\mod 2\},\qquad K=\{w\in A^{+}:|w|\equiv\lfloor\log_{2}|w|\rfloor\mod 2\}.

It is clear that
\delta_{\mu}(L)=\delta_{\mu}(K)=1/2
(no matter the measure
\mu
). However, it can be shown that the sequence of sums
s_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu(L\cap K\cap A^{i})
has subsequences
(s_{2^{2n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(s_{2^{2n+1}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to
1/3
and
1/6
respectively, thus
\delta_{\mu}(L\cap K)
does not exist.

In the above example, the language
K
is not rational. When both languages are rational our main result (
Theorem

20
) implies that the density of the intersection also exists.

2
Density of ideals

In this section, we show how to calculate densities for languages that are ideals of
A^{*}
(and thus not necessarily rational). We start with
right ideals
, that is, languages
L
such that
LA^{*}=L
. The set
D=L\setminus LA^{+}
is the minimal generating set of
L
as a right ideal, which means that
L=DA^{*}
and
D
is contained in every other set with this property. Moreover the set
D
is a
prefix code
, meaning that no element of
D
is a proper prefix of another element of
D
. For example, for
a\in A
, the language
L=aA^{*}
is the set of words that have the letter
a
as a prefix. Then one has
D=\{a\}
.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
L
be a right ideal of
A^{*}
. Then

L
has a density in the strong sense and
\delta_{\mu}(L)=\mu(D)
where
D=L\setminus LA^{+}
.

Proof.

Let
X
be the support of
\mu
. Since
D
is a prefix code,

\mu(DA^{*}\cap A^{n})=\sum_{u\in D}\mu(uA^{*}\cap A^{n})=\sum_{u\in D,|u|\leq n}\mu(uA^{n-|u|})=\sum_{u\in D,|u|\leq n}\mu(u)

where the last equality uses the fact that
\mu(uA^{i})=\mu(u)
for all
i
. Clearly this tends to
\mu(D)
when
n\to\infty
.
∎

Next we establish a similar result for
left ideals
of
A^{*}
, that is, languages
L
such that
A^{*}L=L
. Similar to the right-sided case, the set
G=L\setminus A^{+}L
is the minimal generating set of
L
as a left ideal and also a
suffix code
, i.e., no element of
G
is proper suffix of another.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\mu
be an invariant probability measure of
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
L
be a left ideal of
A^{*}
. Then

L
has a density in the strong sense and
\delta_{\mu}(L)=\mu(G)
where
G=L\setminus A^{+}L
.

Proof.

Let
X
be the support of
\mu
. Since
G
is a suffix code,

\mu(A^{*}G\cap A^{n})=\sum_{u\in G}\mu(A^{*}u\cap A^{n})=\sum_{u\in G,|u|\leq n}\mu(A^{n-|u|}u)=\sum_{u\in G,|u|\leq n}\mu(u),

where the last equality uses the fact that since
\mu
is invariant,
\mu(A^{i}u)=\mu(u)
for all
i
. By invariance of
\mu
again, this tends to
\mu(G)
when
n\to\infty
.
∎

The density of a left ideal may not exist if the measure is not invariant, as shown next.

Example 6
.

Let
x\in A^{\mathbb{Z}}
and let
\mu
be the Dirac measure of
x
, that is, the probability measure
\mu
on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
\mu(U)=1
if
x\in U
and
0
otherwise. Then, for
a\in A
, the density of
A^{*}a
is the frequency of
a
in the sequence
x^{+}=x_{0}x_{1}\cdots
. If the frequency of
a
does not exist in
x^{+}
, the language
A^{*}a
does not have a density. For example, if
x^{+}=aba^{2}b^{2}\cdots a^{2^{n}}b^{2^{n}}\cdots
, then the frequency of
a
does not exist. Indeed, the frequency of
a
is
1/2
in
aba^{2}b^{2}\cdots a^{2^{n}}b^{2^{n}}
, while it is close to
2/3
in
aba^{2}b^{2}\cdots a^{2^{n}}b^{2^{n}}a^{2^{n+1}}
.

Next we consider the case of a
quasi-ideal
, that is, the intersection of a left
ideal and a right ideal. In this case we need the stronger assumption that
\mu
is ergodic.

Proposition 7
.

Let
\mu
be an ergodic measure on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
,
L
be a right ideal of
A^{*}
, and
K
be a left ideal of
A^{*}
. Then
L\cap K
has a density and
\delta_{\mu}(L\cap K)=\mu(D)\mu(G)
where
D=L\setminus LA^{+}
and
G=K\setminus A^{+}K
. Moreover, if
\mu
is mixing, then
L\cap K
has a density in the strong sense.

Proof.

Let
X
be the support of
\mu
. Fix
u\in D
and
v\in G
and let
m=\max(|u|,|v|)
. Then
\mu(uA^{*}\cap A^{*}v\cap A^{n})=0
if
n<m
and otherwise

\mu(uA^{*}\cap A^{*}v\cap A^{n})=\mu([u]_{X}\cap S^{|v|-n}[v]_{X})

and thus using Equation (
2
),

\displaystyle\delta_{\mu}(uA^{*}\cap A^{*}v)=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mu(uA^{*}\cap A^{*}v\cap A^{i})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=m}^{n-1}\mu([u]_{X}\cap S^{|v|-i}[v]_{X})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mu([u]_{X}\cap S^{-i}[v]_{X})=\mu([u]_{X})\mu([v]_{X})=\mu(u)\mu(v).

This shows that

\delta_{\mu}(DA^{*}\cap A^{*}G)=\sum_{u,v}\delta_{\mu}(uA^{*}\cap A^{*}v)=\sum_{u,v}\mu(u)\mu(v)=\mu(D)\mu(G).

If
\mu
is mixing, then with similar arguments

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(uA^{*}\cap A^{*}v\cap A^{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu([u]_{X}\cap S^{-n}[v]_{X})=\mu(u)\mu(v),

which shows that the density exists in the strong sense.
∎

Example 8
.

If
X=\{(ab)^{\infty},(ba)^{\infty}\}
, then the language
L=aA^{*}\cap A^{*}b
has density
1/2
with respect to the unique invariant measure
\mu
on
X
. However
\mu
is not mixing and the density does not exist in the strong sense as
\mu(aA^{*}\cap A^{*}b\cap A^{n})
alternates between 0 and 1.

Next we turn to
two-sided ideals
, that is, languages
L
such that
A^{*}LA^{*}=L
.

Proposition 9
.

Let
\mu
be an ergodic measure on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
with support
X
. Every two-sided ideal
L
that intersects
\mathcal{L}(X)
has density
1
in the strong sense.

Proof.

Let
D=L\setminus LA^{+}
and
G=L\setminus A^{+}L
. Then
L=DA^{*}=A^{*}G
and by the formulas for right and left ideals, the density exists in the strong sense and
\delta_{\mu}(L)=\mu(D)=\mu(G)
. But, using the formula for quasi-ideals, we also have
\delta_{\mu}(L)=\mu(D)\mu(G)=\delta_{\mu}(L)^{2}
, so
\delta_{\mu}(L)=0
or 1. The fact that
L\cap\mathcal{L}(X)\neq\varnothing
implies
\delta_{\mu}(L)>0
.
∎

Let
X
be a shift space. We derive from
Proposition

9
the following property for
X
-thin codes, where the languages
L
such that there exists
w\in\mathcal{L}(X)
satisfying
A^{*}wA^{*}\cap L=\varnothing
are called
X
-thin
by the terminology of
[
6
]
. A suffix code
C\subseteq\mathcal{L}(X)
is
X
-maximal
if it is not properly included in a suffix code
D\subseteq\mathcal{L}(X)
. A dual notion holds for prefix codes. For example, every
\mathcal{L}_{n}(X)
, for
n\geq 1
, is an
X
-maximal suffix code.

Proposition 10
.

Let
\mu
be an ergodic measure with support
X
and let
C\subseteq\mathcal{L}(X)
be an
X
-maximal prefix or suffix code. If
C
is
X
-thin, then
\mu(C)=1
.

Proof.

We assume that
C
is an
X
-maximal suffix code.
Let
w\in\mathcal{L}(X)
be such that
A^{*}wA^{*}\cap C=\varnothing
. We claim that

A^{*}wA^{*}\cap\mathcal{L}(X)\subseteq A^{*}C
.
Indeed, let
u,v
be such that
uwv\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. Since
C
is
X
-maximal, either
uwv
has a suffix in
C
or is a suffix of some
c\in C
. The second case being impossible, we conclude that
uwv\in A^{*}C
, which proves the claim. Therefore, we have
\delta_{\mu}(A^{*}wA^{*})\leq\delta_{\mu}(A^{*}C)
. Then by Propositions
9
and
5
, we have
\mu(C)=\delta_{\mu}(A^{*}C)\geq\delta_{\mu}(A^{*}wA^{*})=1
.
The proof for the dual statement for prefix codes works analogously.
∎

In particular, a finite prefix or prefix code is
X
-maximal if and only if
\mu(C)=1
for an ergodic measure with support
X
.

3.1
Definition and first properties

Let
X
be a shift space on
A
and let
\varphi\colon A^{*}\to M
be a morphism onto a finite monoid
M
. We first introduce two subsets of
M
naturally associated with
X
.

Definition 11
.

Let
K_{X}(M)
be the intersection of all two-sided ideals
I
of
M
such that
I\cap\varphi(\mathcal{L}(X))\neq\varnothing
, called the
X
-minimal ideal of
M
. Let
J_{X}(M)
be the set of elements of
M
which generate
K_{X}(M)
as a two-sided ideal, i.e.,

J_{X}(M)=\{m\in M\mid MmM=K_{X}(M)\}.

It follows from the definition that
J_{X}(M)
is a
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
-class. Consider the quasi-order defined on
M
by
m\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}n\iff MmM\subseteq MnM
. Note that
m\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}n
if and only if
m\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}n
and
n\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}m
. The next proposition establishes some basic properties of
J_{X}(M)
when
X
is irreducible.

Proposition 12
.

Let
X
be an irreducible shift space on
A
and let
\varphi\colon A^{*}\to M
be a morphism onto a finite monoid
M
. Then

1.

K_{X}(M)
is an ideal of
M
which meets
\varphi(\mathcal{L}(X))
;

2.

J_{X}(M)=\{m\in K_{X}(M)\mid MmM\cap\varphi(\mathcal{L}(X))\neq\varnothing\}
;

3.

J_{X}(M)\cap\varphi(\mathcal{L}(X))
is the non-empty set of
\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}
-minimal elements of
\varphi(\mathcal{L}(X))
;

4.

Either
J_{X}(M)
is the minimal ideal
K(M)
of
M
, or
J_{X}(M)\cup\{0\}
is the unique 0-minimal ideal in the quotient of
M
by the largest ideal of
M
which does not meet
\varphi(\mathcal{L}(X))
.

Proof.

1
. Let
I,J
be two ideals which meet
\varphi(\mathcal{L}(X))
. Let
u,v\in\mathcal{L}(X)
be such that
\varphi(u)\in I
and
\varphi(v)\in J
. Since
X
is irreducible, there is a word
w
such that
uwv\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. Then
\varphi(uwv)\in I\cap J
. This proves that
K_{X}(M)
is an ideal which meets
\varphi(\mathcal{L}(X))
.

2
. Let
m\in J_{X}(M)
and let
n\in K_{X}(M)\cap\varphi(\mathcal{L}(X))
. Then
n
is in
MmM
. This proves the inclusion from left to right. Conversely, let
m\in K_{X}(M)
be such that
MmM\cap\varphi(\mathcal{L}(X))\neq\varnothing
. Then, the ideal
MmM
generated by
m
is contained in
K_{X}(M)
and it meets
\varphi(\mathcal{L}(X))
. This implies
MmM=K_{X}(M)
and therefore
m
is in
J_{X}(M)
.

3
. Let
\varphi(u)
be
\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}
-minimal in
\varphi(\mathcal{L}(X))
. Fix
v\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. By irreducibility there is
w\in\mathcal{L}(X)
such that
uwv\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. It follows that
\varphi(uwv)\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}\varphi(u)
, which by minimality implies
\varphi(u)\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}\varphi(uwv)\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}}\varphi(v)
. This shows that
\varphi(u)
generates an ideal contained in every ideal which meets
\mathcal{L}(X)
, thus
\varphi(u)\in J_{X}(M)
.

4
. First assume that
K(M)\cap\varphi(\mathcal{L}(X))\neq\varnothing
. Since the elements of
K(M)
are
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
-minimal in all of
M
, it follows from part
3
that
J_{X}(M)=K(M)
.

Assume next that
K(M)\cap\varphi(\mathcal{L}(X))=\varnothing
. Let
I=\{s\in M\mid MsM\cap\varphi(\mathcal{L}(X))=\varnothing\}
. It is clear that
I
is the largest ideal of
M
which does not meet
\varphi(\mathcal{L}(X))
. It is non-empty since it contains the minimal ideal
K(M)
by assumption. Let us show that
M/I
is a prime monoid (see
[
7
, Section 1.12]
). Take
s,t\in M/I
which are
\neq 0
. Then there exist
x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2}
such that
x_{1}sx_{2}=\varphi(u)
and
y_{1}ty_{2}=\varphi(v)
, where
u,v\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. By irreducibility there is
w
such that
uwv\in\mathcal{L}(X)
, hence
x_{1}sx_{2}\varphi(w)y_{1}ty_{2}=\varphi(uvw)
, and in particular
sx_{2}\varphi(w)y_{1}t\neq 0
. This shows that
M/I
is prime, and thus it admits a unique 0-minimal ideal, by
[
7
, Proposition 1.12.9]
. Finally notice that any non-zero element
s
of the quotient

must satisfy

for some

, thus it is
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
-above some element of
J_{X}(M)
. This implies that
J_{X}(M)\cup\{0\}
is the 0-minimal ideal of
M/I
.
∎

Corollary 15
.

Let

3.2
On aperiodic languages

The next theorem concerns the density of aperiodic languages. Recall that a language is
aperiodic
if it can be recognized by an aperiodic monoid. By Schützenberger’s theorem
[
38
]
, aperiodic languages are precisely the
star-free
languages, as well as the languages defined by first-order formulas (see
[
30
, Theorem 4.1]
). The density of aperiodic languages was also studied, from a logic perspective, by Lynch
[
26
]
. Lynch’s definition of density involves sequences of Bernoulli measures, thus it is both more general than our definition (since we use only one measure) and much more particular, since we use more general measures than Bernoulli measures.

Theorem 16
.

An aperiodic language
L
has a density with respect to an ergodic measure
\mu
. More precisely, there exist a finite number of pairs
(D_{i},G_{i})_{1\leq i\leq k}
of a prefix code and a suffix code such that
L\cap\mathcal{L}(X)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}(D_{i}A^{*}\cap A^{*}G_{i})\cap\mathcal{L}(X)
and

\delta_{\mu}(L)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu(D_{i})\mu(G_{i}).

(6)

Moreover, if the measure is mixing, then the density exists in the strong sense.

Proof.

Let
\mu
be an ergodic measure and
\varphi\colon A^{*}\to M
be a morphism onto an aperiodic monoid
M
. Let
J=J_{X}(M)
, let
m\in M
and let
L=\varphi^{-1}(m)
. It is enough to prove that Equation (
6
)
holds for such
L
. If
m\notin J
, then
\delta_{\mu}(L)=0
in the strong sense by
Corollary

15
, so we may assume that
m\in J
. We claim that

L\cap\mathcal{L}(X)=LA^{*}\cap A^{*}L\cap\mathcal{L}(X).

Indeed, assume that
u\in(LA^{*}\cap A^{*}L)\cap\mathcal{L}(X)
. Then
\varphi(u)\in mM\subseteq K_{X}(M)M=K_{X}(M)
, and thus
\varphi(u)\in J
by the second part of
Proposition

12
. But then
\varphi(u)\in mM\cap Mm\cap J
and since
M
is aperiodic, we have
mM\cap Mm\cap J=\{m\}
. It follows that
\varphi(u)=m
and
u\in L
. This concludes the proof of the claim, as the other inclusion is trivial. Finally, we can use
Proposition

7
to deduce that
\delta_{\mu}(L)=\mu(D)\mu(G)
, where
D=L\setminus LA^{+}
and
G=L\setminus A^{+}L
, and that the density exists in the strong sense if
\mu
is mixing.
∎

3.3
Decidability and degree

Next we consider the question of whether membership in
J_{X}(M)
is decidable. We say that a shift space
X
is
rationally decidable
if the emptiness of
\mathcal{L}(X)\cap L
is decidable for every rational language

. Sofic shifts are obviously rationally decidable. The class of rationally decidable shift spaces also includes all substitution shifts by a result of
[

, Lemma 3]
(see also
[
4
, Lemma 3.15]
and
[
12
]
).

Proposition 19
.

Let
X
be a rationally decidable shift space. Then for every morphism
\varphi\colon A^{*}\to M
, there is an algorithm which decides which elements
m\in M
are in
J_{X}(M)
.

Proof.

We have
m\in K_{X}(M)
if and only if
m\in MnM
for every
n\in M
such that
\varphi^{-1}(n)\cap\mathcal{L}(X)\neq\varnothing
. Since
X
is rationally decidable, this is decidable for every
n\in M
. Therefore, we can decide whether
m\in K_{X}(M)
, and then whether
m\in J_{X}(M)
.
∎

\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}

be an automaton on

of the automaton, denoted

. Note that

states that

contains all elements of rank

. For instance the automaton

from

has

has

-degree 1. This result also implies that

is computable if and only if membership in

is decidable (cf.

19
for cases whether the latter is decidable).

,

be an irreducible shift, let

) is larger than or equal to

. When

4
Existence of the density

The goal of this section is to establish the existence of density of regular languages under all invariant measures. More precisely, we will prove the following, which is our main theorem.

Theorem 20
.

Let
\mu
be an invariant measure on
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Then every rational language
L
has a density with respect to
\mu
.

The main ingredient for the proof is the dynamical system defined as follows, called a skew product. Let
X
be an irreducible shift space and
\varphi\colon A^{*}\to M
a morphism onto a finite monoid. Fix
R
an
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}
-class of
J_{X}(M)
such that
R\cap\varphi(\mathcal{L}(X))\neq\varnothing
. Let
M
act on the right of
R\cup\{0\}
by
r\cdot m=rm
if
rm\in R
and
r\cdot m=0
otherwise.
The specific choice of an
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}
-class class
R
plays no role here, as long as
R
satisfies
R\cap\varphi(\mathcal{L}(X))\neq\varnothing
.

Definition 21
.

The skew product
R\cup\{0\}\rtimes X
is the system
((R\cup\{0\})\times X,T)
where

T(r,x)=(r\cdot\varphi(x_{0}),Sx).

Note that the projection
\pi_{X}\colon(R\cup\{0\})\times X\to X
satisfies
\pi_{X}\circ T=S\circ\pi_{X}
. It follows that if
\nu
is an invariant (respectively ergodic) measure on
(R\cup\{0\})\rtimes X
, then
\nu\circ\pi_{X}^{-1}
is an invariant (respectively ergodic) measure on
X
. When
M=G
is a group, then
R=G
and
G\rtimes X
forms a subsystem of
(G\cup\{0\})\rtimes X
, which in effect means we can get rid of 0. In particular we recover the type of skew products studied in the preprint
[
9
]
. Next, we introduce a natural probability measure on
(R\cup\{0\})\rtimes X
induced by a measure
\mu
on
X
. In the group case, we recover the product of
\mu
with the normalized counting measure on the group considered in
[
9
]
.

Definition 22
.

Fix an invariant probability measure
\mu
on
X
. The
weighted counting measure
on the skew product
(R\cup\{0\})\rtimes X
is the Borel measure
\nu
defined by
\nu(\{0\}\times X)=0
, and for every
u,v\in\mathcal{L}(X)
and
r\in R
,

\nu(\{r\}\times[u\cdot v]_{X})=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{s,s\varphi(u)=r}\mu(G_{s}uv),

where
d
is the cardinality of the
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
-classes of
J_{X}(M)
and
G_{s}
, for
s\in R
, is the suffix code such that
\varphi^{-1}(Ms)=A^{*}G_{s}
.

That
\nu
is a well-defined Borel measure on
(R\cup\{0\})\rtimes X
follows from Carathéodory’s extension theorem, since the above formula defines a pre-measure on the Boolean algebra of clopen sets of
(R\cup\{0\})\times X
. Note that if
r\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}s
, then
G_{r}=G_{s}
. For an
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
-class
H\subseteq R
, let
G_{H}
be the common value of
G_{r}
for
r\in H
. The following technical lemma will be useful. In its proof, we use the following notion: we call a monoid
M

stable
if for every
s,t\in M
, the implications

s\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}st\implies s\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}st
and

s\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}ts\implies s\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}ts

hold. Every finite monoid is stable
[
18
, Lemma 1.1, Chapter V]
.

Lemma 23
.

The union
G=\bigcup_{H\subseteq R}G_{H}
over all
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
-classes of
R
is a suffix code such that
\mu(G)=1
.

Proof.

The fact that
G
is a suffix code is a consequence of the fact that the monoid
M
is stable. Moreover,
G
is
X
-maximal since, for all
w\in\mathcal{L}(X)
, there exists
u
such that
\varphi(uw)\in J_{X}(M)
, by irreducibility and the fact that
R\cap\varphi(\mathcal{L}(X))\neq\varepsilon
. Note that a word
w\in\mathcal{L}(X)
such that
\varphi(w)\in J_{X}(M)
cannot be a proper factor of any element of
G
, since
G
is a suffix code. Thus there must exist
w\in\mathcal{L}(X)
such that
A^{*}wA^{*}\cap G=\varnothing
(simply fix
u\in G
and then take

). It follows that
\mu(G)=1
by

∎

Next we establish some properties of the weighted counting measure.

Proposition 24
.

The weighted counting measure is an invariant probability measure on the skew product
(R\cup\{0\})\rtimes X
which satisfies
\nu\circ\pi_{X}^{-1}=\mu
.

Proof.

Next we show that
\nu
is invariant under the map
T
. Note that it suffices to check invariance for sets of the form
B=\{r\}\times[u\cdot v]_{X}
. First, assume that
u\neq\varepsilon
. If we let
u=u^{\prime}a
,
a\in A
, then
T^{-1}(\{r\}\times[u\cdot v]_{X})
can be expressed as the disjoint union

T^{-1}(\{r\}\times[u\cdot v]_{X})=\bigcup_{s,s\varphi(a)=r}\{s\}\times[u^{\prime}\cdot av]_{X},

and from the definition of
\nu
we find

\displaystyle\nu(T^{-1}(\{r\}\times[u\cdot v]_{X}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{s,s\varphi(a)=r}\nu(\{s\}\times[u^{\prime}\cdot av]_{X})=\sum_{s,s\varphi(a)=r}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{s^{\prime},s^{\prime}\varphi(u^{\prime})=s}\mu(G_{s^{\prime}}u^{\prime}av)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{s,s\varphi(u)=r}\mu(G_{s}uv)=\nu(\{r\}\times[u\cdot v]_{X}).

Next let us treat the case where
u=\varepsilon
, or in other words where
B=\{r\}\times[v]_{X}
. Observe that

T^{-1}(\{r\}\times[v]_{X})=\bigcup_{s\varphi(a)=r}\{s\}\times[av]_{X},\qquad[G_{r}v\cdot\varepsilon]_{X}=\bigcup_{s\varphi(a)=r}[G_{s}av\cdot\varepsilon]_{X},

where the unions are taken over pairs
(s,a)\in R\times A
such that
s\varphi(a)=r
. Since
G=\bigcup_{s\in R}G_{s}
is a suffix code by
Lemma

23
, the second union in the above equation is disjoint (as is the first, for obvious reasons). Thus we have

\displaystyle\nu(T^{-1}(\{r\}\times[v]_{X}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\varphi(a)=r}\nu(\{s\}\times[av]_{X})=\sum_{s\varphi(a)=r}\frac{1}{d}\mu(G_{s}av)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\mu(G_{r}v)=\nu(\{r\}\times[v]_{X}).

Finally, let us show that
\nu(R\times U)=\mu(U)
for every Borel set
U
. Using
Lemma

23
together with the invariance of
\mu
, we have

\nu(R\times U)=\sum_{r\in R}\mu([G_{r}\cdot\varepsilon]_{X}\cap U)/d=\sum_{H\subseteq R}\mu([G_{H}\cdot\varepsilon]_{X}\cap U)=\mu([G\cdot\varepsilon]_{X}\cap U)=\mu(U),

where the last equality follows since
\mu([G\cdot\varepsilon]_{X})=\mu(G)=1
. Taking
U=X
, we find that
\nu(R\times X)=\mu(X)=1
, which shows that
\nu
is indeed a probability measure.
∎

The following lemma will also be needed for the proof of
Theorem

20
. Its proof uses standard arguments from ergodic theory. We include it for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 25
.

For every ergodic measure
\mu
on
X
, there exists an ergodic measure
\bar{\mu}
on
(R\cup\{0\})\rtimes X
such that
\bar{\mu}(\{0\}\times X)=0
and
\bar{\mu}\circ\pi_{X}^{-1}=\mu
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the set of all invariant probability measures on
(R\cup\{0\})\rtimes X
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
the subset of those measures
\zeta
such that
\zeta(\{0\}\times X)=0
and
\zeta\circ\pi_{X}^{-1}=\mu
. Note that
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
contains the weighted counting measure by
Proposition

24
and thus
\mathcal{M}_{\mu} \neq\varnothing
. Moreover,
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
is a closed convex subspace of
\mathcal{M}
, so it must contain an extreme point
\bar{\mu}
by the Krein–Milman theorem. Let us show that
\bar{\mu}
is also an extreme point of
\mathcal{M}
. Suppose that
\bar{\mu}=s\zeta^{\prime}+(1-s)\zeta^{\prime\prime}
for some
\zeta^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
and
0<s<1
. It is clear that both
\zeta^{\prime}
and
\zeta^{\prime\prime}
must give to
\{0\}\times X
zero measure, and the fact that
\mu
is an extreme point in the convex set of invariant measures on
X
implies that
\zeta^{\prime}\circ\pi_{X}^{-1}=\zeta^{\prime\prime}\circ\pi_{X}^{-1}=\mu
. Thus
\zeta^{\prime},\zeta^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
, contradicting the fact that
\bar{\mu}
is an extreme point of
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
. Since the extreme points of
\mathcal{M}
are precisely the ergodic measure on
(R\cup\{0\})\rtimes X
, we are done.
∎

for the case where

Proposition 26
.

Let

be an ergodic measure on

with support a shift space

. Let

-class

where

is any ergodic measure on

such that
\bar{\mu}(\{0\}\times X)=0

\bar{\mu}\circ\pi_{X}^{-1}=\mu

Proof.

If

\delta_{\mu}(L)=0

. We may now assume that

. Let

. For

We claim that for every

The left-to-right inclusion is obvious. For the converse, we take

such that

(r,x)\in U_{r,[C_{\leq i}]_{X}}\cap T^{-i}(U_{rm,X})
. This means that there exists
some

, such that
\varphi(x_{0}\cdots x_{j-1})=m
and furthermore

where
m^{\prime}=\varphi(x_{0}\cdots x_{i-1})
. Clearly

, hence

-classes of

and

\displaystyle\delta_{\mu}(L)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{n-1}\mu(L\cap A^{i})=\sum_{r,rm\in R}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{n-1}\bar{\mu}\bigl(U_{r,[C_{\leq i}]_{X}}\cap T^{-i}(U_{rm,X})\bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{r,rm\in R}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{n-1}\bar{\mu}\bigl(U_{r,[C]_{X}}\cap T^{-i}(U_{rm,X})\bigr)-\epsilon

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{r,rm\in R}\bar{\mu}(U_{r,[L]_{X}})\bar{\mu}(U_{rm,X})-\epsilon.

On the other hand, we have

\displaystyle=\sum_{r,rm\in R}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{n-1}\bar{\mu}\bigl(U_{r,[C_{\leq i}]_{X}}\cap T^{-i}(U_{rm,X})\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r,rm\in R}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{n-1}\bar{\mu}\bigl(U_{r,[C]_{X}}\cap T^{-i}(U_{rm,X})\bigr)\leq\sum_{r,rm\in R}\bar{\mu}(U_{r,[L]_{X}})\bar{\mu}(U_{rm,X}),

.

A formal series
f(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}f_{n}z^{n}
, with
f_{n}\geq 0
, is
\mathbb{R}_{+}
-rational if the map
a^{n}\mapsto f_{n}
is
\mathbb{R}_{+}
-rational. In this case, we have
f(z)=p(z)/q(z)
for two polynomials
p,q
with real coefficients. The following statement, originally due to Berstel, is well known (see
[
15
]
for example).

Proposition 33
.

Let
L
be a rational language. If
\mu
is a sofic measure, then
f_{L}(z)
is
\mathbb{R}_{+}
-rational. Let
\mathbb{K}
be a subfield of
\mathbb{R}
containing the values of
\mu
on
A^{*}
. Then
\mu(L)\in\mathbb{K}\cup\{\infty\}
and
\delta_{\mu}(L)\in\mathbb{K}
.

Proof.

Let
g\colon A^{*}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be defined by

g(w)=\begin{cases}\mu(w)&\mbox{if $w\in L$,}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Thus,
g(w)
is the product of the values of
\mu
and of the characteristic function
\chi_{L}
of
L
. The map
w\mapsto\mu(w)
is
\mathbb{R}_{+}
-rational by
Proposition

31
and
\chi_{L}
is
\mathbb{R}_{+}
-rational since
L
is rational. Therefore, by
[
15
, Theorem 5.2]
, the map
g
is
\mathbb{R}_{+}
-rational. Set
h_{n}=\sum_{w\in A^{n}}g(w)
. Then
h=\sum_{n\geq 0}h_{n}z^{n}
is
\mathbb{R}_{+}
-rational. Since
h_{n}=\mu(L\cap A^{n})
, this proves that
f_{L}(z)
is
\mathbb{R}_{+}
-rational.

By
[
15
, Theorem 7.2]
, there is an integer
p\geq 0
such that
r_{i}=\lim_{n\to\infty}h_{np+i}
exists for
0\leq i<p
. Moreover
r_{i}\in\mathbb{K}
whenever
h_{n}\in\mathbb{K}
. This implies that
\delta_{\mu}(L)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{0\leq i<p}r_{i}
exists and is in
\mathbb{K}
. Finally, we have
f_{L}(z)=p(z)/q(z)
for two polynomials
p,q\in\mathbb{R}[z]
. Since
h_{n}\in\mathbb{K}
, we may assume
p,q\in\mathbb{K}[z]
by
[
15
, Proposition 3.2]
. Thus, if
\mu(L)=\sum_{n\geq 0}h_{n}
is finite, the radius of convergence of
f_{L}(z)
is
>1
and the sum is equal to
p(1)/q(1)
, which is in
\mathbb{K}
.
∎

Example 34
.

Let
\mu
be the Bernoulli measure on
\{a,b\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
defined by
\mu(a)=p
,
\mu(b)=q
. Let
L=\{a,b\}^{*}ab
. Then

f_{L}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}pqz^{n+2}=\frac{pqz^{2}}{1-z}

and consequently
\delta_{\mu}(L)=pq
.

Appendix A
Semigroup theory

This appendix recalls some basic definitions and results concerning ideals in monoids. For a more detailed exposition, we refer to textbooks such as
[
7
,
24
,
18
,
21
,
15
,
16
]
. Most of the section focuses on Green’s equivalence relations. These relations are used to produce a partition of a given monoid in terms of the principal ideals generated by its elements.

A
right ideal
in a monoid
M
is a set
R
such that
RM\subseteq R
. A
left ideal
in a monoid
M
is a set
L
such that
ML\subseteq L
. A
two-sided ideal
is a set
I
such that
MIM\subseteq I
. For instance, for
m\in M
, the sets
mM
,
Mm
and
MmM
are respectively the smallest right, left and two-sided ideals containing
m
. The quotient of a monoid
M
by an ideal
I
is the monoid
M/I=(M\setminus I)\cup\{0\}
with the operation

mn=\begin{cases}mn&\text{if $mn\notin I$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

The Green relations of a monoid
M
are the equivalence relations defined by

•

m\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}n\iff mM=nM
,

•

m\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}n\iff Mm=Mn
,

•

m\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}n\iff MmM=MnM
.

In other words, two elements of
M
are
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}
-equivalent when they generate the same right ideal,
\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}
-equivalent when they generate the same left ideal, and
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
-equivalent when they generate the same two-sided ideal. One also denotes by
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
the equivalence
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}\cap\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}
and by
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
the equivalence
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}=\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}
. The equivalence classes of these relations are called the
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}
-classes,
\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}
-classes,
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
-classes,
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
-classes and
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
-classes respectively. Note that
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
is contained in
\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}
and
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}
, which are both contained in
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
, which is contained in
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
.

Consider the quasi-orders defined by
m\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}}n
if
mM\subseteq nM
, and
m\leq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}}n
if
Mm\subseteq Mn
. A useful property of Green’s relations in finite monoids is the following. We say that a monoid
M
is
stable
if for every
s,t\in M
such that
s\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}t
, the following implications hold:

s\geq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}}t\implies s\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}t,\qquad s\geq_{\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}}t\implies s\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}t.

It is well-known that every finite monoid is stable
[
18
, Lemma 1.1, Chapter V]
. In stable monoids,
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}=\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
, and thus when dealing with finite monoids we do not need to distinguish between
\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}
and
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
.

A
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
-class
D
is called
regular
if it contains an idempotent. Every
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
-class in
D
containing an idempotent is a group and the groups corresponding to different
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
-classes contained in
D
are all isomorphic. Moreover every submonoid of
M
which is a group is contained in a regular
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
-class, thus the regular
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
-classes are also known as the
maximal subgroups
.

When
M
is a monoid of partial mappings from a set
Q
to itself, the Green relations have natural interpretations. Let us adopt the convention that
M
acts on the right of
Q
(so the operation on
M
is reversed composition) and let us define the
kernel
and
image
of an element
m\in M
as

\ker(m)=\{(p,q)\in Q\times Q\mid pm=qm\},\qquad\operatorname{im}(m)=\{qm\mid q\in Q\}.

Moreover, define the rank of
m
as
\operatorname{rk}(m)=\operatorname{Card}(\operatorname{im}(m))
. Then it is not hard to see that the following implications hold:

m\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}n\implies\ker(m)=\ker(n),\quad m\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}n\implies\operatorname{im}(m)=\operatorname{im}(n),\quad m\mathrel{\mathscr{J}}n\implies\operatorname{rk}(m)=\operatorname{rk}(n).

If
M
is the monoid of all partial mappings on
Q
, then the reverse implications also hold.

The partition of a monoid using Green’s relations is typically depicted using a so-called
eggbox picture
. In an eggbox picture, the
\mathrel{\mathscr{D}}
-classes are represented by boxes where each row is an
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}
-class and each column an
\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}
-class. The cells (where rows and columns intersect) thus represent the
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
-classes. It is customary to indicate the regular
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
-classes using an asterisk.

Lemma 37

(Green’s lemma)
.

Let
r
and
s
be
\mathrel{\mathscr{L}}
-equivalent elements of a monoid
M
and let
u
and
v
be such that
ur=s
and
vs=r
. Then the mappings
x\mapsto ux
and
y\mapsto vy
define mutually inverse bijections between the
\mathrel{\mathscr{R}}
-classes of
r
and
s
. Moreover those bijections preserve
\mathrel{\mathscr{H}}
-classes.
