Algebraic Fusion in a (2+1)-dimensional Lattice Model with Generalized Symmetries
Chinmay Giridhar, Philipp Vojta, Zohar Nussinov, Gerardo Ortiz, Andriy H. Nevidomskyy

Abstract
Résumé The notion of quantum symmetry has recently been extended to include reduced-dimensional transformations and algebraic structures beyond groups. Such generalized symmetries lead to exotic phases of matter and excitations that defy Landau’s original paradigm. Here, we develop an algebraic framework for systematically deriving the fusion rules of topological defects in higher-dimensional lattice systems with non-invertible generalized symmetries, and focus on a (2+1)-dimensional quantum Ising plaquette model as a concrete illustration. We show that bond-algebraic automorphisms, when combined with the so-called half-gauging procedure, reveal the structure of the non-invertible duality symmetry operators, which can be explicitly represented as a sequential quantum circuit. The resulting duality defects are constrained by the model’s rigid higher symmetries (lower-dimensional subsystem symmetries), leading to restricted mobility. We establish the fusion algebra of these defects. Finally, in constructing the non-invertible duality transformation, we explicitly verify that it acts as partial isometry on the physical Hilbert space, thereby satisfying a recent generalization of Wigner’s theorem applicable to non-invertible symmetries.



Introduction.

In physics, symmetries are typically defined within the framework of group theory, where each symmetry operator has an inverse
Peres 2002
. However, it has long been recognized that continuum theories in two spacetime dimensions can exhibit symmetries that transcend this conventional group structure. Instead, symmetry operators or their associated defects may form more complex algebraic structures, such as a
fusion category
, allowing for elements with non-trivial kernels – that is,
non-invertible
symmetries. The canonical example is the two-dimensional Ising conformal field theory (CFT)
Fröhlich et al. 2004
, whose fusion rules encompass both the global
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry operators and more subtle
topological defect lines

Chang et al. 2019
extending along the time direction. In certain rational CFTs, these defect lines are known as Verlinde lines
Verlinde 1988
, and generalizations exist for broader classes of CFTs
Chang et al. 2019
. More recently, it has been shown that non-invertible symmetry operators (NSOs) can also be defined in certain quantum (1+1)-dimensional
lattice
models. This allows for precise, non-perturbative statements about the full theory, not just its long-wavelength behavior
Aasen et al. 2020
;
Lootens et al. 2023
;
Choi et al. 2023a
;
Lootens et al. 2024
;
Seiberg et al. 2024
;
Seiberg and Shao 2024
;
Chatterjee et al. 2024
;
Pace et al. 2024
;
Ebisu and Han 2025
;
Cao et al. 2025
. The transverse-field Ising chain model (TFIC)
Nishimori and Ortiz 2010
is the most studied example, exhibiting a quantum analogue of the classical Kramers–Wannier duality
Kramers and Wannier 1941
;
Nishimori and Ortiz 2010
. It is important to note that such dualities are generally maps between
different
models and only become genuine symmetries at special, fine-tuned self-dual points in parameter space.

The task of constructing explicit, lattice-specific expressions for the NSOs is highly nontrivial. It involves manipulating inherently non-local objects and carefully defining their associated movement and fusion actions in a way that faithfully reproduces the expected fusion algebra
Seiberg et al. 2024
. These NSOs can often be represented using the formalism of matrix product operators
Lootens et al. 2023
, alternatively described in terms of sequential quantum circuits
Chen et al. 2024
, thus providing useful computational and conceptual tools. A further subtlety lies in the fact that NSOs often require an enlargement of the Hilbert space of the original lattice model. For example, translation operators appearing in fusion expressions can add or remove lattice sites, thereby modifying the Hilbert space dimension
Seiberg et al. 2024
.
Moreover, any such NSO must conform to Wigner’s theorem
Peres 1995
, which stipulates that all physical symmetries must be implemented in a way that preserves transition probabilities between any two states.
These considerations highlight the deep challenges involved in defining the actions performed by non-invertible symmetries in a manner that is consistent both algebraically and physically.

In light of these challenges, this paper introduces a unified algebraic framework to construct and manipulate topological defects in quantum lattice theories. This leads to the explicit derivation of the relevant NSOs and their fusion properties by utilizing the
bond-algebraic
method – a powerful approach developed earlier by two of us
Nussinov and Ortiz 2009a
;
Cobanera et al. 2010
;
Cobanera et al. 2011
that enables the study of dualities in both classical and quantum systems. A central notion is that of homomorphisms – here taken to be linear automorphisms – of bond algebras, which admit implementation via unitary operators acting as isometries on the Hilbert space. In the current work, we demonstrate the power of this approach with a compelling example in a (2+1)D theory that harbors a non-invertible symmetry – especially relevant given that the vast majority of prior studies focused on either (1+1)D
Aasen et al. 2020
;
Choi et al. 2023a
;
Lootens et al. 2024
;
Seiberg et al. 2024
;
Seiberg and Shao 2024
;
Chatterjee et al. 2024
;
Pace et al. 2024
or (3+1)D lattice theories
Choi et al. 2022
;
Kaidi et al. 2022
;
Koide et al. 2022
;
Koide et al. 2023
;
Choi et al. 2023b
;
Gorantla et al. 2024
. We note that dualities in 2+1 D theories have been studied in
Ebisu and Han 2025
;
Cao et al. 2023
. The example that we consider exhibits paradigmatic rigid generalized symmetries
Nussinov and Ortiz 2009b
;
Nussinov and Ortiz 2009c
that are prevalent in compass type and many other theories (these symmetries are also commonly referred to as subsystem symmetries, particularly when they arise in the context of celebrated theories harboring dynamically constrained particles – fractons
Vijay et al. 2016

Gro 2024

From a mathematical perspective, the NSO is realized as the composition of an invertible unitary transformation and a non-invertible projection onto a specified global symmetry sector, in accordance with the constraints set by a
generalized symmetry
extension of Wigner’s theorem
Ortiz et al. 2025

Xu–Moore model: Symmetries and duality.

The Xu–Moore model (a.k.a. the Ising plaquette model)
Xu and Moore 2004
;
Xu and Moore 2005
is defined in terms of the Pauli
X
and
Z
operators on the vertices of a square lattice. The sites are labeled by coordinates
q=(q_{x},q_{y})
,
q_{x,y}\in\mathbb{Z}

\hat{x}\equiv(1,0)

\hat{y}\equiv(0,1)
where
1\leq q_{x/y}\leq L_{x/y}

H_{\text{XM}}[g]=-\sum_{q}\left(g^{-1}Z_{q}Z_{q+\hat{x}}Z_{q+\hat{y}}Z_{q+\hat{x}+\hat{y}}+gX_{q}\right).

(1)

The model has an extensive number of commuting (invertible) subsystem symmetries formed by the products of Pauli operators along individual rows/columns,

\hat{\eta}^{\text{row}}_{j}=\prod_{q:q_{y}=j}X_{q};\quad\hat{\eta}^{\text{col}}_{i}=\prod_{q:q_{x}=i}X_{q}.

(2)

On a lattice of
L_{x}\times L_{y}
sites, there are
L_{x}+L_{y}-1
independent subsystem symmetries
Nussinov and Ortiz 2023
.
It was realized early on
Xu and Moore 2004
;
Xu and Moore 2005
;
Nussinov and Fradkin 2005
that one can perform a duality transformation
D
that maps the two terms in Eq. (
1

DH(g)=H(g^{-1})D

Cobanera et al. 2011

We begin with a simple demonstration of this non-invertibility via a simple proof by contradiction. Towards that end, let us choose the bond algebra
\mathcal{A}
to be the von Neumann algebra generated by the local terms
(bonds) whose sum forms the Hamiltonian

\mathcal{A}:=\{Z_{q}Z_{q+\hat{x}}Z_{q+\hat{y}}Z_{q+\hat{x}+\hat{y}};X_{q}\}.

(3)

Now suppose that there exists an invertible duality transformation
\Phi
that maps
Z_{q}Z_{q+\hat{x}}Z_{q+\hat{y}}Z_{q+\hat{x}+\hat{y}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Phi}}{{\longrightarrow}}X_{q}
and, conversely, maps
X_{q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Phi}}{{\longrightarrow}}Z_{q}Z_{q-\hat{x}}Z_{q-\hat{y}}Z_{q-\hat{x}-\hat{y}}

Z
operators in any row or column equals to identity, and acting by the algebra automorphism
\Phi
yields, schematically,

\Phi(1)=\Phi\bigg(\prod_{p\in\begin{subarray}{c}\text{row,}\\
\text{col}\end{subarray}}[ZZZZ]_{p}\bigg)=\Phi\bigg(\prod_{q\in\begin{subarray}{c}\text{row,}\\
\text{col}\end{subarray}}X_{q}\bigg)\neq 1,

(4)

resulting, since any automorphism must map the identity into itself, in the said contradiction underlying this proof. Thus, such an invertible duality
\Phi
cannot exist within the above confines.
This non-invertibility may, however, be remedied. In the following, we show that a unitary automorphism can be constructed, at the cost of
extending
the Hilbert space of the problem
Ortiz et al. 2025
.

Enlarging the Hilbert space.

Adding auxiliary
(gauge-like) degrees of freedom, acting outside the physical Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}

necessary to achieve a bond-algebraic automorphism. This then guarantees

Before turning to the Xu–Moore model, we illustrate the core concept using the TFIC. To achieve a bond-algebraic duality for the TFIC, it suffices to enlarge its Hilbert space by a single site,

The constant of motion

Conclusions and outlook.

We demonstrated that bond-algebraic methods
Nussinov and Ortiz 2009a
;
Cobanera et al. 2010
;
Cobanera et al. 2011
, when combined with the generalized Wigner theorem
Ortiz et al. 2025
, offer a systematic and transparent way to construct NSOs directly on the lattice. Although these ideas can already be illustrated in the familiar setting of the TFIC, their full power becomes apparent in higher dimensions. We therefore focused on a (2+1)-dimensional system with rigid symmetries, using the Xu–Moore model as a concrete example.

Within this framework, we explicitly constructed the symmetry-defect operators and determine their fusion rules. An important lesson that emerges is that these fusion rules depend sensitively on the choice of boundary conditions (see also Ref.
Maity et al. 2025
), highlighting the subtle interplay between global structure and local algebraic relations. Our approach is entirely algebraic and self-contained: it does not assume the presence of an underlying conformal field theory, nor does it rely on prior knowledge of whether a fusion category exists.

These results are especially noteworthy because systems with rigid higher symmetries (“subsystem symmetries”)
do not admit a topological quantum field theory description of their vacua. Nevertheless, by analyzing the fusion of defect operators, one can still extract meaningful information about the universality class of the critical phase.

Finally, we showed that the non-invertible duality can be naturally expressed as a sequential quantum circuit. This perspective not only clarifies the structure of the duality but also points toward possible applications in quantum information and quantum computing. More broadly, our work provides a foundation for exploring non-invertible dualities in higher-dimensional lattice systems.

III
General Defect Configurations

To illustrate the procedure of gauging the subsystem symmetries and its connection to the self-duality, we first review how this self-duality can be realized by gauging all subsystem symmetries, then projecting into an equivalent, non-trivial sector which hosts the dual degrees of freedom. Subsequently, we discuss the concept of partial gauging to generate defect configurations corresponding to performing a “local” duality transformation within a sub-region of the lattice - which comes at the cost of a defect line on the boundaries between the duality-transformed and original regions of the lattice.

Unlike the case of the
1+1
D TFIC, these defect lines are not freely deformable as the Hilbert space dimension needed to host them depends on the perimeter of the defect line. However there still exists a sequence of unitary transformations that can be performed to map the
minimally gauged
theory to a duality transformed one. Being invertible maps, they cannot “shrink” one defect configuration into another with a smaller perimeter and we explicitly see this from the “tears” (which contain the extra degrees of freedom) in the outer defect line after each movement step of the inner line.
