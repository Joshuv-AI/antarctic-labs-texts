Selenium-modified Hericium erinaceus polysaccharides improves intestinal immunity and ND vaccine responses in chickens
Authors: Yi Lin, Zijie Zheng, Xianjie Lin, Guilan Fan, Chunyan Chen, Tao Qin, Jin He, Zhe Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the immunomodulatory effects of selenium-modified Hericium erinaceus polysaccharides on intestinal immunity and Newcastle disease (ND) vaccine responses in chickens. Selenium modification of HEP (SHEP) was confirmed via SEM, TEM, FT-IR, Monosaccharide composition, Molecular weight, stability, release assay and the selenium content of SHEP. In vitro, SHEP-M group significantly decreased ROS, CD86, IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α levels, and enhanced CD206 levels. In vivo, chickens immunized with Newcastle disease vaccine showed that 160 mg/kg SHEP significantly increased thymus, spleen, and bursa indices, as well as ND-HI antibody titers. SHEP enhanced CD4+/CD8+ T cell proportions in peripheral blood/intestine, and elevated IL-4, IFN-γ, TLR4, and CCR9 levels. SHEP also improved jejunal villi, increased villus height, crypt depth, and VH/CD ratio. 16S analysis revealed SHEP increased the abundances of Bacteroides, Parabacteroides and Alistipes, and optimized the F/B ratio and intestinal flora structure. Collectively, SHEP may enhance immunity and offer insights for the development of novel polysaccharide formulations.Keywords: Selenium-modified Hericium erinaceus polysaccharide, Immunomodulatory effects, Newcastle disease vaccine

Introduction

The Newcastle disease virus (NDV), a member of the superfamily Mononegavirales in the family Paramyxoviridae, remains one of the most perilous threats to the global poultry industry (Tan et al., 2018). Infection triggers severe respiratory and digestive complications, including elevated body temperature, dyspnea, greenish diarrhea, and the production of thin-shelled eggs, leading to substantial economic losses (Cai et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024). Currently, ND vaccines are widely administered in clinical practice to mitigate this viral threat. However, the application of conventional vaccines is often limited by drawbacks such as delayed early immune responses and the short-lived nature of the resulting humoral antibodies (Dimitrov et al., 2017; Slamanig et al., 2025). Therefore, there is an urgent need for novel immune boosters to enhance the protective efficacy of NDV vaccines. In recent years, traditional Chinese medicine has attracted considerable attention due to their immunomodulatory activities (Wan et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2023).

Hericium erinaceus is mainly distributed across Europe, Asia, and America, and is taxonomically classified under the class Basidiomycetes, subclass Holobasidiomycetidae, order Hericiales, and family Hericiaceae (Friedman, 2015). As a traditional edible and medicinal fungus in China, it also serves as an important medicinal resource in traditional Chinese medicine (Gravina et al., 2023). Polysaccharides are considered as one of the main active ingredients of H. erinaceus (HEP), including various biological activities such as anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and anti-tumor effects (Wu et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025). Notably, these polysaccharides function as prebiotic fibers, demonstrating a significant capacity to alleviate oxidative stress, protect the gastric mucosa, and regulate intestinal flora (Arunachalam et al., 2022; Jayachandran et al., 2017). These properties indicate their potential in maintaining intestinal homeostasis, which is intrinsically linked to local gut immunity.

Selenium (Se) is an essential trace element renowned for its strong anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer properties (Mojadadi et al., 2021). Recently, selenylation modification has emerged as an effective strategy to optimize the structure of polysaccharides, preventing their aggregation, improving stability, and amplifying their biological functionalities (Peng et al., 2024; Zhan et al., 2022). Previous research has shown that selenized polysaccharide can significantly enhance the immune function of chickens in both in vitro and in vivo models after Newcastle disease vaccination (Liu et al., 2023; Qiu et al., 2014). Specifically, our prior studies demonstrated that the selenylation of HEP (SHEP) promotes the maturation and activation of dendritic cells (DCs) by upregulating surface molecules (e.g., MHC-II and CD86) and activating critical downstream immune signaling pathways, such as MAPK and NF-κB (Luo et al., 2020; Qin et al., 2017). These findings suggest that SHEP acts as a potent immunomodulatory agent capable of enhancing vaccine efficacy.

In recent years, the interaction between the immune system and the intestinal microbiota has garnered extensive attention (Shi et al., 2017). The gut microbiota profoundly influences the early development of the immune system by regulating gene expression in intestinal cells, facilitating effector T cell differentiation, and triggering the production of cytokines (Sittipo et al., 2018). Emerging evidence suggests that selenium-modified polysaccharide can influence gut microbiota by increasing beneficial bacteria, reducing harmful bacterial populations, and making it a promising functional food for gut health (Álvarez-Mercado and Plaza-Diaz, 2022; Zhang et al., 2022). Based on this well-established gut-immune axis, we hypothesized that SHEP could significantly augment the efficacy of the ND vaccine by modulating both the intestinal microbiota and local gut immunity.

To test this hypothesis and building upon our previous successful synthesis of SHEP using the nitric acid-sodium selenite (HNO3—-Na2SeO3) method (Qin et al., 2017), the present study aimed to comprehensively evaluate its immunoregulatory capabilities. We synthesized and characterized of SHEP with immunoregulatory properties, and evaluated its regulatory effects on HD-11 cells polarization in vitro. More importantly, we investigated its capacity to improve the immune response to the NDV vaccine in chickens in vivo, with a particular focus on its modulatory functions within the intestinal immune system and gut microbiota (Fig. 1). These findings aim to highlight SHEP as a highly promising candidate for enhancing intestinal immunity and optimizing ND vaccine responses in poultry.

Materials and methods

Materials

HEP and SHEP were self-made in the laboratory. Newcastle disease virus vaccine and NDV HI-positive serum were purchased from Qingdao Yebio Biological Engineering Co., Ltd. Mouse Anti-Chicken CD3-APC Antibody, Mouse Anti-Chicken CD4-FITC Antibody and Mouse Anti-Chicken CD8-PE Antibody were acquired from Nanjing Zeweil Biological Technology Co., Ltd. d-Hanks' Solution, PBS buffer powder, DMSO, and CCK8 were purchased from Beijing Soleberg Biotechnology Co. Chicken-Specific Lymphoprep™ Density Gradient Medium was obtained from Tianjin Haoyang Biological Products Technologies Co., Ltd. SteadyPure RNA Extraction Kit” was purchased from Hunan Aikori Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

Preparation of SHEP

The SHEP was synthesized using the nitric acid‑sodium selenite (HNO3—Na2SeO3) method (Qin et al., 2016). Briefly, 500 mg of HEP was solubilized in 50 mL of 0.5 % HNO3. 300 mg of Na2SeO3 was included and agitated at 70 °C for 8 h. The pH was modified to 5-6. The solution was subjected to dialysis using a dialysis bag (dialysis bag: 1 kDa cut-off value) for 72 h (water was changed every 6 h). The liquid was collected, concentrate it using pressure distillation, and subsequently freeze-dry to acquire SHEP.

Characterization of SHEP

The characteristics of SHEP and HEP were systematically detected using Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) (Hydro 2000Mu, Malvern Instruments, UK) for particle size and zeta potential measurements, Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) (Phenom ProX, Phenom-World BV, Netherlands) and Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) (Tecnai12, Philips, Holland) for morphological analysis, and Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy (Thermo Scientific, USA) for structural determination at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹ in the range of 4000 to 400 cm-1. The total selenium content of SHEP was determined by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS).

Monosaccharide composition analysis

Briefly, SHEP was added to 2 M TFA and heated at 121 °C for 2 h. After drying under a nitrogen stream, the residue was washed with 99.99 % methanol. This methanol wash was repeated 2-3 times. The final residue was dissolved in sterile water and transferred to a chromatography vial, then analyzed using an ICS 5000+ HPLC system and Dionex CarboPac PA20 (150 × 3.0 mm, 10 μm) at 30 °C. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (H2O), solvent B (0.1 M NaOH), and solvent C (0.1 M NaOH + 0.2 M NaAc), delivered at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. The standard monosaccharides considered as references include fucose (Fuc), rhamnose (Rha), arabinose (Ara), galactose (Gal), glucose (Glc), xylose (Xyl), N-acetylgalactosamine (GalN), N-acetylglucosamine (GlcN), mannose (Man), ribose (Rib), Fructose (Fru), Galacturonic Acidand (Gal-UA), Glucuronic Acid (Glu-UA), Mannuronic Acid (Man-UA) and Guluronic Acid (Gul-UA).

Molecular weight assessment

The weight-average molecular weight (Mw), number-average molecular weight (Mn) and MWD (Mw/Mn) of SHEP were determined using high performance size-exclusion chromatograph (HPSEC) equipped with multi angle laser light scattering detector and refractive index detector (Agilent, G1362A, USA).

In vitro release assay of SHEP

The release of SHEP in vitro was assessed in PBS at pH 7.4 and pH 4.5, simulating the physiological environment of body fluid and gastric juice, respectively. 10 mg SHEP was added in 2 mL of each buffer solution, then placed on a constant temperature shaker (37 °C, 100 r/min). The suspension was collected at predetermined time points (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 48 and 72 h), followed by centrifugation to collect the supernatant (0.5 mL). The polysaccharide concentration was measured by the phenol-sulfuric acid method (Yue et al., 2022).

The stability of SHEP

The temperature stability of SHEP was evaluated based on the method of Cao et al. (Cao et al., 2025). SHEP dispersions were placed in a water bath at different temperatures (30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80 and 90 °C) for 1.5 h. To evaluate the effect of temperature on the stability of nanoparticles, the samples were analyzed for particle size and zeta potential after reaching room temperature (25±2 °C).

In vitro experiments

Cells culture

HD-11 cells were purchased from Shanghai Fuheng Biotechnology Co., LTD. The HD-11 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin solution at 37 °C in a 5 % CO₂ cell culture incubator.

ROS assay

The SHEP concentrations for HD-11 cells were determined as 3.13, 6.25, and 12.5 μg/mL, corresponding to the low, medium, and high dose groups, respectively (Yu et al., 2021). Briefly, HD-11 cells were cultured in 24-well plates with a density of 2 × 105 cells/mL for 24 h. Then cells were exposed to LPS for 8 h. After culturing with different concentrations of HEP and SHEP for 1 h, cells were collected. ROS+ cells were detected by BD Accuri® C6 flow cytometer.

CD86 and CD206

HD-11 cells were cultured as detailed in section 2.8.2 and seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well. To induce macrophage polarization, LPS and IL-4 were used for induction respectively. Cells were stained with anti-CD86 or anti-CD206 antibody, respectively, for 30 min in the dark at 4 °C. Finally, cells were resuspended in PBS and subsequently analyzed by BD Accuri® C6 flow cytometer.

Measurement of cytokines

The HD-11 cell samples were conducted according to the instructions provided by the manufacturer. Inflammatory factors (IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α) were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China).

Animal experiments

Grouping and treatment of experimental animals

One-day-old, male Hy-Line brown chickens were kept in observation for 14 d with no immunity. Subsequently, they were randomly divided into 8 groups of 25 chickens per group: control group, Vaccine group, HEP low dose group (HEP-L, 40 mg/kg), HEP medium dose group (HEP-M, 80 mg/kg), HEP high dose group (HEP-H, 160 mg/kg), SHEP low dose group (SHEP-L, 40 mg/kg), SHEP medium dose group (SHEP-M, 80 mg/kg), SHEP high dose group (SHEP-H, 160 mg/kg). One group was randomly appointed as the control, and no vaccine or other immune treatment was given. The remaining 7 groups were immunized by Newcastle disease virus (ND-Ⅳ LaSota) vaccine with eye drops and nose drops. The first immunization was administered on day 14, and a second immunization was given 14 days later. After each immunization, the vaccine was administered with eye drops and nose drops for three consecutive days starting on the same day. On the day of vaccination, all groups began intragastric administration of 1 mL of the respective drug concentrations. The control group received an equal volume of normal saline. The testing period lasted 49 d.

Determination of organ index

On 21, 28, 35, 42, 49 d, 5 chickens from each group were randomly selected, weighed, and euthanized. After the chickens were euthanized, the thymuses, spleens and bursa weight of the chickens taken immediately. The formula for calculating the immune organ index is as follows:

The immune organ index = weight of immune organ (g) /weight of body (g) × 100 %

Measurement of ND antibody titer

Serum was collected from 5 chickens randomly selected chickens in each group on 21, 28, 35, 42, 49 d. The hemagglutination inhibition titer was determined using a suspension of 1 % chicken red blood cells. Serum samples were serially diluted, and the maximum dilution at which complete inhibition of agglutination was observed was recorded as the antibody titer. The titer of serum ND-HI antibody was evaluated by a standard HI assay (Lestari et al., 2025).

Peripheral blood CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T cell ratio

On 21 and 35 d after immunization, T cells in peripheral blood were isolated from anticoagulated blood samples randomly collected from 5 chickens, and adjusted to a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL in complete RPMI-1640 medium. After centrifuging and resuspending, cell suspension was incubated with CD3-APC, CD4-FITC and CD8-PE for 20 min on ice in the dark. After centrifugation at 2500 rpm for 10 min, cells in each tube were collected and analyzed by cell flow cytometry (BD Accuri C6) (Cai et al., 2022).

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The spleen was homogenized and total RNA was extracted according to TRIzol reagent protocol (AG21102, AG, Changsha, China) on 21 and 35 d. RNA concentration and purity were measured using NanoDrop 2000. cDNA was synthesized from 500 ng RNA using Evo M-MLV Reverse Transcriptase Kit (AG11707, AG, Changsha, China). The mRNA expression was performed using the SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (AG11701, AG, Changsha, China) on the iCycler IQ5 (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Intestinal morphology analysis

The jejunum tissues of 3 randomly selected chickens in each group were collected on 49 d. Briefly, the samples were processed and followed by stained in Hematoxylin and Eosin (H & E) as described in 2.9.8.

16S rRNA gene sequencing analysis

Total DNA from cecum chyme microbiota was extracted using the E.Z.N.A. Fecal DNA Kit (D4015, Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA). Amplicons were pooled in equal proportions and purified using TIANgel Purification Kit (TIANGEN Biotech). Sequencing libraries were generated using TIANSeq Fast DNA Library Prep Kit (illumina) (TIANGEN Biotech). The library quality was assessed on the Qubit@ 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific) and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. At last, the libraries were sequenced on the Illumina platform. Species annotation was performed using QIIME2 software. For 16S, the annotation database was Silva Database. Data analysis, including species clustering at the phylum and genus levels, was conducted. Alpha diversity was applied in analyzing complexity of species diversity for a sample through Observed-species, Pielou, Simpson. LEfSe analysis was used for inter-group differential analysis.

Statistics analysis

SPSS software was used for multifactor comparative analysis, and all test data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. A significance level of P < 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

Result

Characterization of polysaccharides

As shown in Fig. 2A, the dispersions of HEP and SHEP exhibited clarity and transparency. The HEP and SHEP lyophilized samples were respectively white powder (Fig. 2B) and slightly brownish-yellow powder (Fig. 2C).

Characterizations and release properties of HEP and SHEP. (A) The appearances of HEP and SHEP dispersion; (B)The appearance of HEP; (C) The appearance of SHEP; (D) FT-IR spectra of HEP and SHEP; (E) Temperature stability; (F) The cumulative release rate at pH 5.4; (G) The cumulative release rate at pH 7.4; (H) The standard curve and SHEP of HPLC; (I-J) SEM images of the HEP and SHEP (Scale bar: 100 µm); (K-L) TEM images of the HEP and SHEP (Scale bar: 200 nm).

The particle sizes, PDI values and zeta potential values of HEP and SHEP were shown in Table 2. The average particle size of HEP was 893.93±80.51 nm, with a PDI of 0.20±0.07 and a negative charge zeta potential of −19.78±0.29 mV. SHEP had an average particle size of 533.89±18.87 nm, with a PDI of 0.25±0.04 and a negative charge zeta potential of −22.57±1.61 mV. The selenium content of SHEP was measured at 462.09 μg/g, confirming selenium was successfully enriched in the HEP. As shown in Fig. 2E, after heated at 30-90 °C, the average particle sizes and zeta potential of SHEP exhibited minor fluctuations, indicating that SHEP maintains its integrity under varying thermal conditions.

The FT-IR spectra of HEP and SHEP are presented in Fig. 2D. Broad peak in the region of 3400-3200 cm-1 represented the O—H stretching vibration, consistent with hydroxyl groups in polysaccharides. Peaks observed between 2920 and 2850 cm-1 were assigned to aliphatic C—H stretching vibrations. The absorption band at 1650-1600 cm-1 corresponded to C = O stretching vibrations, associated with acetyl or carboxyl groups in polysaccharides. Absorptions in the range of 1150-1100 cm-1 indicated C—O-C stretching and C—O-H bending vibrations, characteristic of polysaccharides ring ether linkages and hydroxyl deformations. A distinct peak appearing at 880-840 cm-1 signified Se-O-C or Se-O-Se stretching vibrations, confirming the selenization modification of the polysaccharides.

The release experiments of HEP and SHEP were detected in different pH environments (Fig. 2F-G). The cumulative release of HEP from SHEP in pH 7.4 release medium reached 72.78 % within 72 h, which was slightly higher than 75.42 % of pure HEP. However, in the release medium of pH 5.4, the difference was further increased, and the final release amount of SHEP was 74.47 %, which was much higher than that of pure HEP (11.16 %).

Fig. 2H showed the monosaccharide composition of SHEP. SHEP was composed of 2.04 % Gal and 97.96 % Glc. The Molecular weight (MW) of SHEP was 45.63 kDa. The micromorphology of the HEP and SHEP were qualitatively observed by SEM (Fig. 2I-J). HEP exhibited a near-spherical structure with a smooth surface. However, SHEP predominantly exhibited lamellar and bladed structures, with spherical structures attached to rough surfaces. The morphology of the samples under the TEM was shown in Fig. 2K-L. The TEM image of HEP displayed a spherical structure, which was consistent with the results of SEM.

In vitro immunological activity studies

The effects of HEP and SHEP in vitro immunological activity studies were shown in Fig. 3. As shown in Fig. 3A, compare with control group, HEP and SHEP groups reduced ROS levels of HD-11 cells (P < 0.05). Notably, the HEP-H and SHEP-M groups exhibited favorable effects. In addition, SHEP-M group significantly decreased ROS levels of HD-11 cells (P < 0.05).

(A) ROS levels of HEP and SHEP; (B) CD86 expression of HEP and SHEP; (C) CD206 expression of HEP and SHEP; (D-F) IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α levels of HEP and SHEP.

The levels of CD86 and CD206 were shown in Fig. 3B-C. Compared with the LPS group, the middle and high concentrations of HEP and SHEP groups significantly decreased the levels of CD86 expression (P < 0.05). Notably, SHEP-M group showed lower levels of CD86 expression compared with HEP-M group. Meanwhile, SHEP-M group showed higher levels of CD206 expression compared with the IL-4 group and control group.

The levels of cytokine were shown in Fig. 3D-F. Compared to the control and LPS groups, all the concentrations of HEP and SHEP decreased the levels of IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α (P < 0.05). Particularly, SHEP-M group showed the lowest levels of IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α than other groups except the control group.

Influence of SHEP on the gut microbiome

The impact of SHEP on gut microbiota composition was shown in Fig. 9. Compared with control group and vaccine group, SHEP group increased Simpson index (Fig. 9B) and Pielou index (Fig. 9C) in chickens, indicating an increase of microbiota diversity. At the phylum level, SHEP and HEP groups significantly increased Firmicutes abundance, increased Bacteroidota abundance, and optimized the F/B ratio (Fig. 9D). At the genus level, SHEP and HEP groups significantly reduced Lactobacillus abundance and increased Bacteroides and Parabacteroides abundances (Fig. 9E). Meanwhile, SHEP and HEP groups significantly increased the abundance of beneficial bacterial genera, including Alistipes, Parabacteroides, Barnesiella, promoting the gut microbiota to form a more stable community structure. These results suggest that selenium modification maintained the modulatory efficacy of HEP on gut microbiota, with both treatments enhancing intestinal flora structure in chickens.

Discussion

HEP are recognized as safe and promising immunopotentiators, whose immunomodulatory activities can be further enhanced by selenium modification. Meanwhile, recurrent global outbreaks of Newcastle disease (ND) in vaccinated flocks underscore the urgent need for innovative adjuvant formulations. Addressing this need, our findings demonstrate that selenium-modified HEP (SHEP) serves as a highly effective adjuvant for ND vaccines, significantly boosting systemic immune responses in chickens.

The superior adjuvanticity of SHEP is intrinsically linked to its successful structural modification. In this study, SHEP was synthesized using the nitric acid-sodium selenite method, and the successful selenylation was confirmed by FT-IR spectra, which displayed characteristic O-Se-C and Se=O absorption peaks (Chen et al., 2025; Xiang et al., 2022). The SEM images of HEP presented a near-spherical structure with a smooth surface, SHEP presented lamellar and bladed structures. Spherical Se structures are attached to the surfaces of HEP (Xu et al., 2023). This morphological shift is likely driven by intermolecular and van der Waals interactions between selenium and the polysaccharide backbone during the acidic hydrothermal treatment (Liu et al., 2025). Crucially, this structural integration imparts a pH-dependent release property to SHEP. Under acidic conditions, the cleavage of Se-O-C linkages progressively relaxes the polysaccharide network, facilitating the sustained co-release of selenium and carbohydrate moieties (Xiao et al., 2021). This mechanism not only improves the oral bioavailability of SHEP but also provides a rational physicochemical basis for its robust in vivo immunostimulatory performance.

Macrophage polarization serves as a critical role of immune response intensity, with ROS accumulation being intimately linked to this process (Herb and Schramm, 2021). Our results demonstrate that SHEP significantly reduced ROS levels and facilitated a phenotypic shift from M1 to M2 macrophages, characterized by reduced CD86 and elevated CD206 expression (P < 0.05). Meanwhile, SHEP significantly decreased the levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 (P < 0.05). These results showed that SHEP effectively dampens excessive inflammatory responses and alleviates oxidative stress damage (Cao et al., 2019).

Antibody-mediated immunity is crucial for protection against Newcastle disease (Mahmood et al., 2024). To evaluate the efficacy of the NDV vaccine, we quantified ND-HI titers in each group. The results showed that the SHEP-M group exhibited a favorable synergistic effect with NDV, while the SHEP-H group demonstrated the strongest enhancement. These findings indicate that SHEP can effectively enhance the efficacy of the ND vaccine (Bo et al., 2021).

The thymus, spleen, and bursa of Fabricius are crucial immune organs in chickens, and serve as the primary sites for the development and differentiation of immune cells (Wang et al., 2024). The previous study has found that the weight of immune organs is positively correlated with the degree of immune function activity. In our study, compared with the HEP-H group, SHEP-H group significantly increased the immune organ index (P < 0.05). Thus, SHEP can enhance the immune function of organisms to a certain extent (Gao et al., 2020).

The intestine, especially the jejunum, is the main site for digestion and nutrient absorption in the poultry intestine (Tu et al., 2024). Intestinal villi and crypts are two important components of the intestine (Wang et al., 2024). The ratio of VH/CD can indirectly reflect the overall condition of the intestinal villi and suggest changes in intestinal functions that affect the growth and development of the organism (Park et al., 2024). In our study, compare with control group and vaccine group, SHEP-H group significantly enhanced the villus height, crypt depth, and VH/CD ratio (P < 0.05). The results indicate that SHEP promotes intestinal mucosal development and improves absorptive function in chickens (Zhao et al., 2022).

The activation of helper T cells (CD4+ T) and cytotoxic T cells (CD8+ T) is crucial for cellular immune responses and immune regulation (Lin et al., 2021). Numerous studies have reported that CD4+ T cells can promote the proliferation and activation of CD8+ T cells. In this experiment, SHEP increased the proportion of both CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells in the intestinal and peripheral blood lymphocytes. This is consistent with the results reported in the literature (Cao et al., 2023). Th2 cells can assist in the activation of B cells and mainly secrete IL-4 (Lu et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2025). Based on the flow cytometry analysis of peripheral blood and intestinal lymphocytes after NDV vaccination, SHEP upregulated the CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, which led to an increase in cytokines. The levels of IL-2 and IFN-γ in spleen and jejunum were increased on the 21 and 35 d, which demonstrated the improvement of activity of CD8+ T cells (Roszczyk et al., 2022).

Toll-like receptors (TLRs) are pathogen recognition receptors (Cong and Zhang, 2018). It is involved in innate and adaptive immunity. In this study, the mRNA expression of the TLR4 had all increased to a certain extent. It is suggested that SHEP may activate the cellular immunity of the body by binding to TLRs. The upregulation of CCR9 expression is usually associated with the increase of T cells and IgA+plasma cells in the intestinal mucosa (Zhao et al., 2023). This study found that after adding SHEP, the relative expression levels of CCR9 gene mRNA in the jejunum of chickens were upregulated. This result is basically consistent with the previous research (Pathak and Lal, 2020).

The gut microbiota, as the core component of the intestinal microecology, plays a crucial role in carbohydrate metabolism, vitamin synthesis, and the maintenance of health by preserving internal homeostasis (Álvarez-Mercado and Plaza-Diaz, 2022). These microbes facilitate the breakdown of complex carbohydrates, which strengthen the intestinal barrier, maintain gut equilibrium, and support overall well-being. Our findings showed that SHEP and HEP groups significantly increased the abundances of Bacteroides, Parabacteroides and Alistipes, decreased Lactobacillus abundance, and optimized the F/B ratio and intestinal flora structure. The changes in microbial composition may reflect an optimization of the gut microbiota structure to better adapt to the host’s health requirements (Li et al., 2024). Overall, these findings suggest that SHEP modulates gut microbiota composition and regulate a gut microbial balance.

Conclusion

In this study, SHEP was successfully obtained by HNO3—Na2SeO3 method to immunize chickens. Briefly, compared with HEP, SHEP more effectively promoted a phenotypic shift from M1 to M2 macrophages, suppressing inflammation and oxidative stress-induced immune dysfunction. Meanwhile, SHEP showed stronger humoral and cellular immune responses in vitro. Moreover, SHEP improved intestinal barrier function and modulated gut microbiota composition, thereby enhancing intestinal mucosal immunity and ultimately boosting the overall immune response in chickens.
