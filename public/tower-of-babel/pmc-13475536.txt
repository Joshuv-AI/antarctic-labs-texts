Comparative Analysis of Metagenomic Next-Generation Sequencing and Conventional Culture for Pathogen Detection in 218 Patients with Pulmonary Infectious Diseases: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Hongmei Sheng, Jiayu Liu, Qihong Yu, Haiying Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) is a promising technique, but comparative studies of mNGS and culture across different pulmonary diseases are limited.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed data from 218 patients who underwent BALF mNGS testing between November 2021 and April 2025, and patients were categorized into pneumonia, bronchiectasis, NTM, tuberculosis, and other groups based on discharge diagnoses. We compared detection rates, pathogen spectra, co-infection rates, and special pathogen distributions between mNGS and culture. We also assessed concordance (Kappa) and complementary rates.ResultsThe overall positive detection rate of mNGS was significantly higher than that of culture (95.4% vs 67.4%, P<0.001). The overall concordance rate was 71.1%, with a Kappa of 0.42. mNGS additionally detected pathogens in 84 cases (38.5%), primarily viruses (38), Nocardia (8), NTM (15), fungi (45), and Legionella (4). Culture additionally detected 23 cases (10.6%). Co-infection was detected by mNGS in 118 cases (54.1%), far higher than culture (42 cases, 19.3%, P<0.001). The bronchiectasis group had significantly higher detection of Pseudomonas aeruginosa (54.5%) and Nocardia (18.2%).ConclusionmNGS provides a higher detection rate than culture in this cohort, particularly for special pathogens, and is complementary to culture. Pathogen profiles varied across disease types; however, the clinical benefit of mNGS-guided therapy remains to be evaluated in prospective studies.

Introduction

Pulmonary infectious diseases are common, and their causative pathogens are complex. Traditional culture methods have low sensitivity, are time-consuming, and cannot detect viruses, anaerobes, or fastidious organisms.1,2 Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) can detect all pathogens in a single assay,3,4 but comparative studies covering multiple pulmonary disease categories simultaneously remain limited.5

Mixed infections are associated with more severe disease and require broader antibiotic coverage.6 NTM lung disease is closely related to bronchiectasis.7 Special pathogens such as Nocardia, fungi, and viruses are easily missed by culture, and mNGS can compensate.8

This retrospective study analyzed BALF mNGS and culture results from 218 patients to describe detection rates, analyze pathogen profiles across diseases, evaluate the additional findings of mNGS for special pathogens, and generate hypotheses for disease-specific testing strategies. We hypothesized that mNGS would have a higher detection rate, particularly for special pathogens, and that pathogen profiles would differ across disease types.

Methods

Study Population

We consecutively identified 218 patients who underwent bronchoscopy and BALF mNGS testing at Tianjin Chest Hospital between November 2021 and April 2025, and we included all eligible patients with complete clinical data. No formal sample size calculation was performed, as this was a descriptive study based on all available cases meeting the inclusion criteria during the study period.

Patients were classified into the following groups according to discharge diagnoses:

Pneumonia group: Pneumonia, interstitial pneumonia, organizing pneumonia, cryptococcal/aspergillus/Legionella/Chlamydia/Mycoplasma/Pneumocystis Jirovecii/eosinophilic pneumonia, etc.

Bronchiectasis group: Bronchiectasis.

NTM group: NTM lung disease, M. avium complex, M. kansasii, M. abscessus, M. chelonae, M. gordonae, M. paragordonae, M. frederiksbergense, etc.

Tuberculosis group: Pulmonary tuberculosis.

Other group: COPD, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, lung cancer, pulmonary fibrosis, hemoptysis, pulmonary nodule, pleural effusion, lung abscess, asthma.

Inclusion criteria: age ≥18 years; complete clinical data; both mNGS and culture from BALF. Exclusion criteria: sputum/non-BALF samples; unclear diagnosis.

Sample Collection

Bronchoscopy was performed with instillation of 60–120 mL sterile saline, with BALF recovery ≥30%. Samples were aliquoted for mNGS and culture.

mNGS Workflow

DNA Extraction

Total DNA was extracted from 400 μL BALF using a QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen).

Library Preparation & Sequencing

DNA was fragmented, and libraries were prepared using a KAPA Hyper Prep Kit (KAPA Biosystems). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq 550 platform (PE75), generating ~20 million reads per sample.

Bioinformatics

Raw data were quality-controlled and human sequences were removed using Bowtie2. Remaining sequences were aligned to microbial databases using Bracken. Positivity required species-specific reads ≥3 (a threshold commonly used in published mNGS studies)4,8 and relative abundance higher than negative controls and background subtraction.

Culture methods

BALF samples underwent aerobic, anaerobic, fungal, and mycobacterial culture according to standard procedures.

Statistical Analysis

SPSS 26.0 was used. Categorical data were compared using the χ2-test or Fisher’s exact test. Concordance was assessed using Kappa (Kappa <0.4: poor; 0.4–0.75: moderate; >0.75: excellent). P<0.05 was significant. For between-group comparisons, odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated where applicable. Multivariable logistic regression was not performed due to the descriptive nature of the study and limited sample size in subgroups.

Discordant Cases

Among mNGS-positive/culture-negative cases (84), the detected pathogens were viruses (38), NTM (9), Nocardia (3), fungi (13), and anaerobes/fastidious bacteria (21).

Among culture-positive/mNGS-negative cases (23), the detected pathogens were P. aeruginosa (6), S. pneumoniae (5), H. influenzae (4), Candida (3), and others (5). Possible reasons include low read counts, RNA viruses (not detected), sampling errors, or colonization.

Discussion

Diagnostic Performance: Advantages and Complementarity

mNGS had a much higher detection rate than culture (95.4% vs 67.4%), consistent with previous reports.9–12 The two methods were moderately concordant (Kappa 0.42) but complementary. mNGS additionally detected pathogens in 38.5% of patients — mostly viruses, NTM, Nocardia, and fungi — while culture additionally detected common bacteria in 10.6%. However, the clinical significance of these additional detections (eg, whether they represent true infection, colonization, or contamination) requires careful interpretation in each case, as discussed below.

Special Pathogens: Clinical Value Beyond Culture

Viruses were detected by mNGS in 17.4% of patients (predominantly CMV, EBV, HHV-7). Their frequency was similar in bronchiectasis (22.7%) and NTM (13.3%) patients. Viral co-infections may promote secondary bacterial infections.13 Viral testing might be considered in selected patients, but its clinical impact remains uncertain.

Nocardia was detected in 8 cases (3.7%), three of which were culture-negative. Nocardia is difficult to culture.14 Its detection rate in bronchiectasis was 18.2%, much higher than in pneumonia (3.0%), suggesting a potential association that warrants further investigation. mNGS may enable early identification and appropriate treatment (sulfonamides).

NTM was detected in 15 patients (6.9%), nine of whom were culture-negative. NTM and bronchiectasis are closely linked.15 We found that 40% of NTM patients also had bronchiectasis, and 9.1% of bronchiectasis patients had NTM. The NTM group had a high co-infection rate (66.7%), often with P. aeruginosa and S. maltophilia. These findings suggest that NTM screening may be valuable in bronchiectasis, and co-infections should be evaluated in NTM patients.

Fungi were detected in 45 cases (20.6%), including difficult-to-culture pathogens such as Pneumocystis jirovecii and Cryptococcus neoformans.16 Fungal detection was distributed evenly across disease groups, indicating they are common and warrant attention, particularly in immunocompromised patients.17

Pathogen Profiles Across Disease Groups and Clinical Implications

Pathogen profiles differed significantly across the four disease groups.

In the bronchiectasis group, the profile was dominated by P. aeruginosa (54.5%) and Nocardia (18.2%), accompanied by a high co-infection rate (68.2%). The NTM group also had a high co-infection rate (66.7%), with P. aeruginosa and S. maltophilia as the most frequent co-pathogens. The pneumonia group exhibited a diverse profile, where bacterial, viral, and fungal co-infections were all common. The tuberculosis group was characterized by M. tuberculosis (81.3%).

These findings carry several hypotheses for clinical practice. In bronchiectasis, empirical therapy usually covers P. aeruginosa; early mNGS could be considered when response is poor or special pathogens are suspected. In NTM patients, co-infections may need evaluation before therapy. For smear-negative or culture-delayed tuberculosis, mNGS may offer rapid diagnostic information.

Co-Infection: The Hidden Burden

mNGS revealed co-infection in 54.1% of patients, far higher than culture (19.3%). This finding may partly explain some cases of empirical treatment failure, but does not directly demonstrate causality. Whether combination therapy guided by mNGS improves outcomes remains to be tested.

Infection vs Colonization

mNGS detects nucleic acids from both pathogens and colonizers. Low-abundance detections (eg, CMV with <20 reads) must be interpreted with clinical context and host immune status. For viruses, low-level CMV/EBV may represent reactivation; high counts or symptoms warrant treatment. Clinical judgment remains essential. This is particularly relevant for CMV, EBV, and Candida, which may represent colonization rather than true infection in many cases.

Limitations

This study is single-center and retrospective, which may introduce bias. We lacked a composite reference standard, so we report detection rates, not true diagnostic sensitivity. RNA viruses (eg, influenza, RSV) were not detected, potentially underestimating viral rates. The asthma group (n=2) was too small for meaningful analysis and was merged into the other group, and we have no prognostic data on how mNGS results affected outcomes. In addition, cost-effectiveness, turnaround time, and treatment changes were not assessed, so clinical utility cannot be determined from this study.

Future Directions

Multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate our findings, along with integration of host immune markers, longitudinal microbiome monitoring, outcome studies, cost-effectiveness analyses, and better detection of RNA viruses.

Conclusions

mNGS detected more pathogens than culture in this cohort, especially viruses, Nocardia, NTM, and fungi. The two methods are complementary. Pathogen profiles differed by disease type: bronchiectasis patients had high rates of P. aeruginosa and Nocardia; NTM patients frequently had bronchiectasis and co-infections; pneumonia patients showed diverse profiles. Co-infection was detected in 54.1% of patients. mNGS may be considered in selected patients, but prospective studies are needed to confirm its clinical benefit.
