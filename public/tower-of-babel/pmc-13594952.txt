Acute administration of methylphenidate modulates functional connectivity during irrelevant stimuli processing in Parkinson's disease
Authors: Ling Li, Tan Bo, Estela Martinez Lopez, Mirian Roson Gonzalez, Miguel Fernandez-del-Olmo, Tanya Gurevich, Lydia Vela Desojo, Noa Fogelson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMethylphenidate (MPD) may have therapeutic benefits in Parkinson's disease (PD) patients. However, the effects of MPD on attentional functional connectivity (FC) networks, in PD, have not been investigated to date. Our objective was to examine the effects of a single dose of MPD, on event-related FC, in PD. The study was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, single-dose, cross-over clinical trial.MethodsWe recorded electroencephalogram (EEG) in PD patients during the performance of an extended version of a visual oddball task. We evaluated FC during the processing of task-relevant (targets, predictive sequence) and task-irrelevant (randomized standards) stimuli, using the weighted phase-locking index and graph theory. Two recording sessions (20 mg MPD or placebo) were performed in each patient.ResultsMPD, in comparison with placebo, modulated FC during the processing of standards, within the theta band, in PD patients. MPD induced decreases in the FC and global efficiency within fronto-parietal networks, during the processing of task-irrelevant stimuli.ConclusionsIn PD patients, the administration of a single dose of MPD, compared with placebo, attenuated FC within fronto-parietal networks, during the processing of non-informative standards.SignificanceMPD may have cognitive therapeutic effects in PD, by modulating attention during the processing of task-irrelevant stimuli.Keywords: Methylphenidate, EEG, Functional connectivity, Parkinson's disease, Attention

Introduction

Cognitive deficits are common in patients with Parkinson's disease (PD) and include executive dysfunction and deficits in attention and working memory (WM) (Brown and Marsden, 1990; Cools, 2001; Fogelson et al., 2013; Fogelson et al., 2011; Gabrieli et al., 1996; Lewis et al., 2005; Li et al., 2018). Importantly, these cognitive impairments have a significant impact on the quality of life of PD patients as well as intensifying the burden of caregivers.

Methylphenidate (MPD) has been widely used as a treatment for the facilitation of cognitive performance, particularly in clinical populations who are compromised in catecholaminergic neurotransmission (Mehta et al., 2004), such as subjects with attention deficit hyperactive disorder (ADHD). The drug has also been shown to improve cognitive functions in patients with neurodegenerative disorders and vascular cognitive impairment (Azhar et al., 2022; Jenkins et al., 2019; Leijenaar et al., 2020; Prommer, 2012), and its use has been associated with decreased risk of parkinsonism (Cepeda et al., 2019). Thus, it is of importance to further investigate the underlying mechanisms of the potential therapeutic effect of MPD on attention and cognition in patients with PD.

A recent study has demonstrated the potential beneficial cognitive benefits of MPD in PD patients (Fogelson et al., 2025). The findings showed that a single dose of MPD, in patients with PD, induced an electrophysiological facilitation of predictive target detection, compared with placebo, as indicated by shortened P3b latencies. Other studies examining the therapeutic effects of MPD on gait, showed inconsistent results in PD patients (Delval et al., 2015; Devos et al., 2006; Espay et al., 2011; Moreau et al., 2012), while two open-label MPD studies observed enhanced levels of attention in PD (Auriel et al., 2006; Devos et al., 2006).

MPD has been shown to induce increases in striatal extracellular dopamine (Volkow et al., 2001), as well as having an inhibitory action on dopamine and norepinephrine reuptake, particularly in the prefrontal cortex (Berridge et al., 2006; Berridge and Arnsten, 2013; Gamo and Arnsten, 2011; Spencer et al., 2015). It has been proposed that the MPD-induced amplification of dopamine release may underlie the cognitive enhancements associated with MPD, such as improved attention and decreased distractibility (Volkow et al., 2001), and that these are associated with modulation of functional attentional networks in healthy adults (Müller et al., 2005; Peled-Avron et al., 2024; Tomasi et al., 2011). Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) studies showed that MPD modulates functional connectivity (FC), within frontal networks in healthy adults (Farr et al., 2014; Mueller et al., 2014; Rosenberg et al., 2016) and in subjects with ADHD (Mehta et al., 2000; Silberstein et al., 2017). In subjects with ADHD, MPD has also been shown to increase the suppression of the default mode network, thus improving its interaction with the prefrontal cortex (Peterson et al., 2009). To our knowledge there are no studies to date evaluating MPD FC modulations in PD patients. Earlier EEG studies in PD patients showed alterations of frontal attentional regulation, during the detection of targets, compared with healthy control subjects (Fogelson et al., 2011; Li et al., 2018). These alterations were associated with task-specific functional connectivity abnormalities within frontal networks in PD (Fogelson et al., 2013; Fogelson and Diaz-Brage, 2021; Li et al., 2018).

The present study reports functional connectivity analysis of electrophysiological data, collected previously in the same sample of PD patients (Fogelson et al., 2025), to further examine the mechanisms underlying the potential therapeutic effects of MPD on attention in non-demented PD patients. The earlier study utilized behavioural measures and event-related potentials to demonstrate modulatory effects of MPD during predictive target processing in PD patients (Fogelson et al., 2025). The objective of the current study was to examine the effects of acute administration of MPD, compared with placebo, on attention and WM in PD patients, by evaluating event-related FC. Thus, we evaluated the functional coupling across brain regions during the processing of task-relevant (targets, predictive sequence) and task-irrelevant (randomized standards) stimuli, using the phase locking index (Lachaux et al., 1999; Stam et al., 2007; Vinck et al., 2011) and graph theory (Bullmore and Sporns, 2012; Fogelson et al., 2013; Stam et al., 2007; Stam and Van Straaten, 2012). Our study was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, single-dose, cross-over study. We recorded EEG in PD patients after the administration of MPD and placebo. Recording sessions were performed while patients were in ON state. During the EEG recording patients performed a WM target detection task that evaluated the processing and utilization of predictive information within WM. This paradigm previously showed specific frontal cognitive deficits in PD related to an impairment in the translation of contextual information into a predictive cue, within WM (Fogelson et al., 2013; Fogelson et al., 2011; Li et al., 2018). We hypothesized that MPD will modulate functional connectivity within frontal-parietal networks, associated with attention, during the performance of a target detection task, and that these changes would be stimulus specific.

Methods

We used EEG data whose ERP results were reported in a previous study (Fogelson et al., 2014). In the present study we used the EEG data recorded in the subjects to evaluate event-related functional connectivity.

Participants

Patients were diagnosed with idiopathic Parkinson's disease, with the Hoehn and Yahr (Hoehn and Yahr, 1967) score ranging from 1 to 3. Inclusion criteria for the study included patients with PD who were 30–78 years old, up to 8 years of diagnosis of PD, having a good response to levodopa. Patients with the following criteria were excluded from the study: dementia, as indicated by a score of less than 26 on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) (Nasreddine et al., 2005), clinically significant depression, patients who have suffered from hallucinations, delusions or psychosis, severe motor fluctuations and prominent ON dyskinesia, unbalanced arterial hypertension of more than 140/80 during siting on the day of the study, uncontrolled hypertension, hepatic or renal failure, uncontrolled malignancy, or untreated glaucoma, and patients treated with monoamine oxidase inhibitors. Forty patients diagnosed with PD in the ‘Hospital Universitario Fundación of Alcorcon’, where the study was performed, fit the inclusion criteria, and were contacted to participate in the study. Fourteen patients refused to participate, and four patients were unable to perform at least one of the recording sessions due to high blood pressure measurements on the day of the experiment. One adverse event was reported by a patient who experienced dizziness after completing the recording session, where MPD was administered. In total 22 patients completed both recording sessions. All the subjects were right-handed and had normal or corrected-to-normal visual acuity. Subjects were asked to take their regular medication on the day of the experiment. The local ethics committee of ‘Hospital Universitario Fundación Alcorcon’ approved the study (ParKMetil/001). All the participants gave an informed consent before participating in the study. The study was registered as a clinical trial in the ‘European Union Drug Regulating Authorities Clinical Trials Database’ (2020-005073-28).

Experimental procedure

Each subject performed a total of two recording sessions, one after the administration of 20 mg of MPD and another after the administration of a placebo saccharine pill. Each session was performed on a different day, 7 to 14 days apart (Ben-Itzhak et al., 2008), using the same procedures. EEG recordings were performed 90 mins after the administration of either the MPD or placebo pill. The order of administration of MPD versus placebo was randomized and counterbalanced across the subjects. All the sessions were recorded in the morning hours. Recording sessions were performed while patients were in ON state, with no change to their daily administered medication across the sessions, and during the three-month period prior to the recordings.

During the EEG recording subjects performed a cognitive task that evaluates processing of predictive targets and sequences (Fogelson et al., 2011; Fogelson et al., 2009). Subjects were seated in front of a PC-computer screen. Stimuli were presented to either the left or right visual field 6 degrees from a central point of fixation. Subjects were asked to centrally fixate throughout the recording. Stimuli consisted of 15% targets (downward facing triangle) and 85% of equal amounts of three types of standards (triangles facing left, upwards and right, at 90-degree increments). In each block a total of 78 stimuli (12 targets, 22 of each standard type) were presented each for 150 ms and inter-stimulus interval (ISI) of 1 s. Recording blocks consisted of targets preceded by either randomized sequences of standards or by sequences including a three-standard predictive sequence (see Fig. 1). Each block consisted of 6 different randomized sequences of standards (3–8 standards long) preceding the target; and 6 sequences of standards (3–8 standards long), with a predictive sequence preceding the target in each. A single sequence of trials appeared on the right or left hemifield in a randomized order within each block. Blocks were counterbalanced so that there were equal amounts of stimuli presented to the right and left visual hemifield across the blocks. Within a single sequence of trials (predictive or random) all stimuli were presented in one hemifield. The predictive sequence always consisted of the three standards: triangles facing left, up and right, always in that order. The predictive sequence was always followed by the target stimulus (downward facing triangle). Subjects were asked to detect and press a button for target events and to pay attention to the predictive sequence when it appears. Presentation of the stimuli and response recordings were controlled using E-prime (Psychology Software Tools, Inc., Pittsburgh, USA). Each recording session consisted of 14 different blocks, displayed in randomized order, each approximately 1.6 min long.

Task timeline. Sequences of standards S1, S2 and S3 with a predicted sequence (top) and in randomized order (bottom) preceding the target (T). The predictive sequence is always S1 (n-3), followed by S2 (n-2), and then S3 (n-1). Stimuli are presented to the left or right visual field. Inter-trial intervals (1000 msec), including duration of stimulus presentation (150 ms) are displayed. Each block consisted of six different randomized sequences of standards (3–8 standards long) preceding the target; and six sequences of standards (3–8 standards long) with the predictive sequence preceding the target in each.

Before each experimental session subjects performed a brief training session. In the first practice block subjects were introduced to the stimuli (triangles) and were asked to detect targets. Once all the subjects were able to detect the targets accurately, they were shown the predictive sequence and were told that it would be 100% predictive of a target, but that targets would also appear randomly throughout the block. Subjects then performed another brief training session to ensure that they were confident in the detection of both the predictive sequence and the targets before the EEG recording session began.

EEG recordings

EEG was recorded from a 64 Ag-AgCl electrode array, with a 10–10 system layout using the ActiveTwo system (Biosemi, The Netherlands). Signals were amplified and digitized at 512 Hz. Post processing and ERP analysis of the data was performed using Brain Vision Analyzer (Brain Products GmbH, Germany). All channels were re-referenced to averaged linked earlobes.

EEG analysis

EEG signals were sorted and averaged relative to the stimulus onset, with epochs set from 200 to 700 ms relative to stimulus onset. EEG signals in each epoch in each subject were filtered with a two-way least-squares FIR bandpass filter (eegfilt.m from EEGLAB toolbox, Delorme and Makeig, 2004) between 4 and 8 Hz (theta band), 8 and 12 Hz (alpha band), and between 12 and 28 Hz (beta band). A current source density (CSD) toolbox (MATLAB) was used to estimate scalp current density with 4 as the spline interpolation constant for each trial in the three frequency bands (Perrin et al., 1989) and to minimize the contribution of volume conduction and remove spurious synchronization.

For each session (MPD, placebo) in each subject, epochs were segmented for the following six conditions: targets after predictive sequences (predicted, P), targets after non-predictive random sequences (random, R), random preceding standards (standards excluding those comprising the predicting sequence, S), and the three standards consisting of the predictive sequence: the last most-informative standard (n-1), the middle standard (n-2) and the first least-informative standard (n-3) of the predicting sequence. The segmented signal in each trial, for each condition, was linked to a long multi-channel signal for further analysis.

Computation of the weighted phase-locking index

The level of synchronization between the EEG signals was measured using phase synchrony. Phase synchronization is a widely used method for the estimation of FC within whole-brain networks (Lachaux et al., 1999; Stam et al., 2007; Vinck et al., 2011). The phase lag index (PLI) measures the asymmetry of the distribution of phase differences between a pair of signals (Lachaux et al., 1999; Stam et al., 2007) and is defined as the sum of the signs of the imaginary part of the complex cross spectral density of two signals (x and y) at time point (t). In the weighted PLI (wPLI) the contributions of the observed phase leads and lags are weighted by the magnitude of the imaginary components of the cross-spectrum (Vinck et al., 2011). Thus, wPLI is a method that is more likely to detect real changes in phase synchronization due to its robustness and reduced sensitivity to uncorrelated noise sources, such as volume conduction or active reference electrodes. We evaluated the wPLI between all the channel pairs in each subject, to compare FC between the MPD and placebo sessions. EEG signals were decomposed into instantaneous amplitude and phase, using the Hilbert transform. We then evaluated the phase angle differences between two signals, to estimate the strength of the instantaneous phase coupling between two EEG signals. The wPLI analysis generated a value of connectivity strength for each channel pair. After this calculation, a 64*64 matrix was obtained, for each condition, in each frequency band, in each subject, and each session. The wPLI takes on values between 0 and 1, describing a continuum between no coupling and perfect phase locking, respectively.

Computation of graph parameters

Graph analysis utilizes a mathematical model in which a graph consists of a collection of nodes connected through a collection of edges (Bullmore and Sporns, 2012; Stam and Reijneveld, 2007; Stam and Van Straaten, 2012). The clustering coefficient (C) evaluates the number of connections between neighbours, and is a measure of the local structure of the network and its resilience to random attacks (Bullmore and Sporns, 2012; Stam et al., 2007; Watts and Strogatz, 1998). Path length (L) evaluates the number of steps from one node to another and is an index of the network global properties and the overall integration of the network (Bullmore and Sporns, 2012; Stam et al., 2007; Stam and Van Straaten, 2012). We also evaluated the local (Elocal) and global (Eglobal) efficiency of the networks. The local nodal efficiency was calculated as the sum of the inverse shortest path lengths between the neighbours of a given node divided by the total number of potential connections. The network local efficiency was then evaluated by averaging the nodal local efficiency across all the nodes in the network. The global efficiency was calculated using the average of the inverse of the shortest paths, between all possible node pairs, within the network (Latora and Marchiori, 2001).

The wPLI was used to generate matrices of phase synchrony in each frequency band, and these matrices were converted into binary matrices with the thresholding based on the sparsity of networks, which was defined as the ratio of the number of existing edges divided by the maximum possible number of edges in a network. A wPLI matrix was converted to a binary graph where the nodes corresponded to EEG channels and undirected edges to the wPLIs that were above threshold. The thresholds were determined by calculating the network cost, which was defined as the percentage of the total number of edges retained in the network when fully connected. The thresholds ranged from 0.10 to 0.50% (with an interval of 0.05). We then evaluated the following FC measures: mean clustering coefficient C, mean shortest path length L, and the mean local (Elocal) and global (Eglobal) efficiencies (Bullmore and Sporns, 2012; Fogelson et al., 2013; Li et al., 2018; Stam et al., 2007; Watts and Strogatz, 1998).

Statistical analysis

Graph parameters comparisons, for C, L, Elocal, Eglobal, were performed using paired t-tests, across threshold values 0.10 to 0.50%. In addition, the area under the curve (AUC) was evaluated for each of the graph parameters, across the range of thresholds, for each subject, condition and frequency band. Comparisons of the AUC values were performed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the Greenhouse-Geisser correction, followed by post-hoc parametric paired t-tests, Bonferroni corrected for multiple comparisons unless otherwise stated. Mean values with the standard error of the mean (SEM), are used throughout the text.

Network-based statistic (NBS) was used to compare network differences between MPD and placebo sessions (Zalesky et al., 2010). We used this approach to localize specific connected components that were significantly different between the two sessions. The wPLI values were used to construct weighted networks and NBS analysis was performed on these weighted networks. We computed thresholded t-tests for each pair-wise association, identifying supra-threshold linked components. The null distribution of the connected component size was derived using a nonparametric permutation approach. Thus, 100,000 permutations were performed for each threshold value in each between-session comparison, with a p-value < .05, as a significant threshold. We used two one-tailed tests to determine the components that were significantly different between the two sessions (MPD > placebo and MPD < placebo).

Furthermore, to avoid false-positive results for the functional connectivity analysis across multiple comparisons, we defined a significant result as that corroborated by all the three methods described above (Fogelson et al., 2019; Li et al., 2018). Thus, a result was considered significant if there were significant differences between sessions for a specific frequency band and condition: (i) for the NBS analysis (ii) for the AUC values and (iii) across at least 7 out of the 9 threshold values.

Correlations between the AUC values of the graph parameters and the clinical measures of the PD patients were calculated using Pearson's Product Moment rank or Spearman's correlation coefficients, for parametric and non-parametric correlations, respectively.

Results

Twenty-two patients with PD (mean age ± standard error of mean = 68.5 ± 1.7 years, 10 females) were included in the study (Table 1). The mean duration since the PD diagnosis was 3.8 ± 0.8 years, prior to participating in the study. Patients had a mean score of 21.7 ± 1.6 on the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale III (UPDRS, (Fahn and Elton, 1987) and a mean Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) score of 1.9 ± 0.1. Seventeen patients were taking Parkinsonian medication, while five patients who were recently diagnosed were not undergoing treatment (see Table 1).

Behavioural results for the PD patients have been reported in detail in an earlier study (Fogelson et al., 2025), showing a faster reaction times for predicted compared to random targets, with comparable accuracy levels across the MPD and placebo sessions.

Graph parameters

We compared the clustering coefficient C, the path length L, the local efficiency (Elocal), and the global efficiency (Eglobal), between the MPD and placebo sessions in each condition (targets, predictive sequence, randomized standards), across three frequency bands (theta, alpha, beta). We found significant increases of C values and significant decreases in global efficiencies for MPD compared to placebo, for randomized standards (S), in the theta frequency band. NBS showed that compared to the MPD session, the placebo session showed stronger connections between frontal, temporal and parietal regions during the processing of randomized standards in the theta frequency range (see Fig. 2).

To compare theta C AUC values, across the sessions, an ANOVA was performed with condition (P, R, n-1, n-2, n-3, S) and session (MPD, placebo) as the repeated measures factors. There was a significant main effect for condition (F (3,52) = 16.70, p < .0001), and a significant condition x session interaction (F(4,85) = 5.114, p = .001). Post hoc tests showed significantly larger C AUC values in the MPD (mean AUC = 0.196 ± 0.007) compared with the placebo session (mean AUC = 0.178 ± 0.005), for condition S, t(21) = 4.046, p = .001, in the theta band. This was corroborated by significant increases in C values, across all nine threshold values (0.10 to 0.50%, p ≤ .024) in the MPD, compared with the placebo session, for condition S, in the theta frequency band (see Fig. 3A).

To compare theta Eglobal AUC values, across the sessions, an ANOVA was performed with condition (P, R, n-1, n-2, n-3, S) and session (MPD, placebo) as the repeated measures factors. There was a significant main effect for condition (F (3,69) = 11.92, p < .0001), a significant main effect for session (F (1,21) = 7.99, p = .01) and a significant condition x session interaction (F(4,75) = 4.78, p = .003). Post hoc tests showed significantly smaller Eglobal AUC values in the MPD (mean AUC = 0.247 ± 0.002) compared with the placebo session (mean AUC = 0.256 ± 0.002), for condition S, t(21) = 4.359, p < .0001, in the theta band. This was corroborated by significant decreases in global efficiencies, across eight threshold values (0.10 to 0.45%, p ≤ .003) in the MPD, compared with the placebo session, for condition S, in the theta frequency band (see Fig. 3B).

We found no significant differences between the two sessions, for the path length and local efficiency. There were also no significant differences between the sessions, as these were not corroborated for all three statistical methods (see section 2.6), across the four functional connectivity measures for conditions P, R, n-1, n-2, and n-3 or for the frequency bands alpha and beta.

Correlations

In order to determine the possible effects of clinical symptoms on the FC measures that were found to be significantly different between MPD and placebo in the PD patients, we calculated correlations between the AUC values for C and Eglobal, for S in the theta band, and between the UPDRS scores, illness duration daily LED dose and age, across the 22 patients. We found no significant correlations between these clinical and FC measures.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrated that in patients with PD, acute administration of MPD modulated FC during the processing of task-irrelevant stimuli. Specifically, MPD induced, increased cluster coefficients and decreased global efficiencies, within the theta band, during the processing of non-informative standards. Processing of task-irrelevant standard stimuli were associated with decreased FC within fronto-temporo-parietal networks after the administration of MPD, compared with placebo.

MPD induced modulation of FC

The current study showed modulatory effects of MPD on EEG FC measures in PD patients that were specific to task-irrelevant standards. Thus, a single dose of MPD, compared with placebo, induced increased cluster coefficients and decreased global efficiencies, within the theta band, during the processing of randomized standards, that did not consist of the predictive sequence. These MPD-induced alterations of the topological properties, suggest a shift towards a more structured network organization (Bullmore and Sporns, 2012; Stam et al., 2007; Stam and Van Straaten, 2012; Watts and Strogatz, 1998) during the processing of non-informative standards. We also observed weaker FC, after acute MPD compared with placebo, within fronto-temporo-parietal networks (theta band), during the processing of these standard stimuli. Thus, MPD may have attenuated the activity within attentional frontal networks during the processing of task-irrelevant stimuli, allowing for a more efficient allocation of attentional resources towards task-relevant stimuli, such as the targets in the task. These results are in line with our earlier findings of the same sample of PD patients, showing an MPD-induced electrophysiological facilitation, of predictive target processing speed, as indicated by the shortening of P3b latencies to task-relevant predicted targets (Fogelson et al., 2025). However, the findings did not demonstrate a behavioural facilitation, possibly due to the use of a relatively low, single dose of MPD, and of behavioural ceiling effects of the task (Fogelson et al., 2025). The lack of behavioural findings is in line with other studies studying the effects of MPD in PD patients (Camicioli et al., 2001; Devos et al., 2006), but in contrast with MPD effects on reaction time, during a WM task, in healthy adults (Tomasi et al., 2011). Other studies using 20 mg of MPD in healthy adults showed changes in the number of choices in a spatial attentional bias task (Peled-Avron et al., 2024), 2024), and a tendency for enhanced performance on a cognitively demanding task, with no changes in latency of response (Müller et al., 2005). Importantly, the studies in healthy adults showed significant MPD-induced modulations of functional connectivity within frontal networks (Müller et al., 2005; Peled-Avron et al., 2024; Tomasi et al., 2011). Thus, reaction times may not be a sufficiently sensitive measure to evaluate MPD-induced modulation of behaviour in PD.

Earlier studies in PD patients, showing deficits in the differential processing of predicted versus random target conditions (Fogelson, 2015; Fogelson et al., 2013; Fogelson et al., 2011; Li et al., 2018), suggest that PD patients may be limited in their ability to translate predictive information into a self-guided internal cue. The current study provides further insights into the mechanisms underlying these deficits, suggesting that, in PD patients, attentional resources may be distributed inefficiently, and allocated towards the processing and/or prolonged maintenance of irrelevant stimuli within WM. In the current study the task demands allocation of attention not only towards target conditions, but also towards standard stimuli, so that the predictive sequence can be detected (Fogelson et al., 2009). This may pose additional demands during the processing of randomized standard stimuli, as these may potentially be task-relevant in the case of the predictive sequence. Thus, our findings suggest that patients with PD may be compromised in their ability to update WM content, due to inefficient removal of irrelevant information, or due to inflexible reallocation of attention towards subsequent stimuli. These findings are in line with other studies in PD patients, showing inefficient resource allocation, as well as poor ability to focus attention and to resist interference, during the performance of tasks requiring mental flexibility (Dujardin et al., 2013; Weber et al., 2021). PD patients demonstrated WM impairments in the presence of task-irrelevant distractors, resulting in prolonged decision processing time (Camalier et al., 2017). Others have shown that the ability to inhibit irrelevant distracting contents in WM declines with age, and that the storing of this irrelevant information in WM, causes interference effects in PD (Di Rosa et al., 2017). These impairments may have direct implications in the daily life of the patients. This has been demonstrated in studies showing that PD patients explore complex visual environments less efficiently, as indicated by increased irrelevant fixations, and fewer task-relevant fixations during walking (Hunt et al., 2018), and by increased number of fixations towards task-irrelevant areas of the visual scene, during driving (Gotardi et al., 2022).

The fact that we observed stimulus-specific FC changes, between the MPD and placebo session, for randomized standards, suggests that MPD may facilitate the reallocation of attentional resources away from task-irrelevant stimuli. Thus, we propose that the underlying mechanism for the potential cognitive therapeutic effects of MPD in PD, may be related to modulatory effects of attentional networks associated with more efficient processing of task-relevant versus task-irrelevant stimuli.

Role of frontal networks

In the current study, modulatory effects of MPD were observed across fronto-temporo-parietal networks, inducing decreased stimulus-specific FC, compared with placebo, in the PD patients. These findings are in line with other MPD FC studies in both healthy adults and subjects with ADHD. Resting state imaging studies in healthy adults show that MPD decreases FC within frontal networks (Farr et al., 2014; Mueller et al., 2014; Rosenberg et al., 2016), and between the default mode network and other task-dependent functional networks (Daood et al., 2024; Sripada et al., 2013). fMRI studies in healthy adults showed MPD modulation of frontal attentional networks during the performance of cognitive tasks evaluating spatial attentional bias (Peled-Avron et al., 2024), choice motor reaction (Müller et al., 2005) and WM (Mehta et al., 2000; Tomasi et al., 2011). Specifically, MPD-induced WM improvements were associated with task-related reductions in blood flow to prefrontal and parietal cortices (Mehta et al., 2000). In subjects with ADHD, EEG and imaging studies, also demonstrated MPD modulatory effects on frontal functional networks (Silberstein et al., 2017), a normalising effect of MPD on resting- state fronto-parietal FC (An et al., 2013; Silk et al., 2017), and improved suppression of the default mode network and its interactions with the prefrontal cortex (Peterson et al., 2009). Our results suggest that MPD modulated the activity of attentional frontal networks in PD, and supports findings showing MPD induced alterations across brain regions that are involved in sustained attention (Mueller et al., 2014; Rosenberg et al., 2016). Importantly, a recent EEG study, demonstrated increased activation of top-down fronto-parietal control networks, in PD patients compared with control subjects, during the processing of task-irrelevant standards (Fogelson and Diaz-Brage, 2021), suggesting that PD patients allocated increased attentional resources to non-task related stimuli. Thus, it is of relevance that in the current study MPD attenuated stimulus-specific FC that was previously observed to be abnormally elevated in PD patients (Fogelson and Diaz-Brage, 2021). It has been proposed that, the MPD-induced, increase of dopamine enhances task-specific signalling and the salience of cognitive tasks, by decreasing background activity of neurons (Volkow et al., 2001). Thus, in PD patients, earlier findings of an MPD-induced facilitation of predictive versus random target evaluation (Fogelson et al., 2025), may be due the enhanced signal to noise ratio of targets versus irrelevant standards. The current study gives further evidence for the proposition that modulations of attention network strength may underlie the attention-related benefits of MPD in healthy adults (Rosenberg et al., 2016) and extend these to PD patients.

The differences in network properties were primarily observed in the theta frequency range. This is in line with earlier FC studies in PD patients showing alterations of network properties in this frequency band, compared with healthy controls (Fogelson et al., 2013; Li et al., 2018). The theta frequency band has been related to attention, processing of internal mental context (Sauseng et al., 2005; Von Stein and Sarnthein, 2000), and to the organization of multiple items in WM (Raghavachari et al., 2001). Our findings suggest that the modulation of theta synchronization across frontal functional networks may also have a significant role in the processing of irrelevant items within WM.

Limitations

Finally, we would like to point out the limitations of our experimental approach in the current study. First, the sample size and medication dose used in the current study were relatively small. Our findings need to be replicated in a larger sample, in a multicentre study, with administration of MPD for longer durations. Second, seventeen out of the twenty-two patients were taking parkinsonian medication, and this may have affected the results. However, this seems unlikely since we found electrophysiological changes that were stimulus-specific, and that were not significantly correlated with the levodopa equivalent daily dose that was administered. Third, with regards to the functional connectivity analysis, although we used current source density measures to minimize spurious synchronization, residual effects from EEG volume conduction should be considered. Fourth, the findings of the current study were limited to modulatory effects of the processing of randomized standards and showed no correlations with the clinical features of the patients. Finally, a control group of health age-matched subjects was not included in the study. Further studies are warranted to evaluate whether a course of MPD may have modulatory FC effects in PD, compared with healthy age-matched controls. Nonetheless, the current study is the first double-blind study in patients with PD to show significant MPD induced modulations of FC, during a cognitive task.

In conclusion, our results demonstrated that in patients with PD, the administration of a single dose of MPD, compared with placebo, attenuated FC during the processing of non-informative standards. Our findings suggest that in PD patients, MPD may modulate activity within fronto-parietal networks by attenuating the allocation of attention towards task-irrelevant stimuli. Thus, MPD may have therapeutic cognitive benefits in patients with PD.
