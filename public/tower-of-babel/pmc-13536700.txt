Insecticide resistance in larval populations of Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus from Panama
Authors: Lorenzo Cáceres Carrera, Dan Martínez, Luis Piedra, Antonio Bruno, Anakena M. Castillo, Rolando Torres-Cosme
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe control of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus is the most effective strategy for preventing the transmission of the arboviruses DENV, ZIKV, and CHIKV. However, insecticide resistance threatens the sustainability of vector control programs. Monitoring insecticide resistance enables the identification of potential associated factors, its intensity and distribution, and contributes to the development of more effective strategies for its management. The aim of this study was to evaluate insecticide resistance in larvae of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus from Panama.Materials and methodsStandardized WHO methodologies were used to generate dose–response curves and to estimate resistance ratios (RR₅₀ and RR₉₀) for the insecticides tested in larvae of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus. Differences in RR₅₀ values were analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s test.ResultsAedes aegypti populations from 24 de Diciembre, Puerto Armuelles, and San Isidro showed moderate resistance to temephos (RR₅₀ = 6.5–7.3). Additionally, populations from El Coco, Aguadulce, David, and Puerto Armuelles exhibited moderate resistance to deltamethrin (RR₅₀ = 7.3–8.5) and lambda-cyhalothrin (RR₅₀ = 5.8–7.8). In Ae. albopictus, the population from Canto del Llano showed moderate resistance to deltamethrin (RR₅₀ = 9.7) and temephos (RR₅₀ = 7.3). Significant differences in resistance levels between Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus were observed based on RR₅₀ values (F = 20.82, df = 1, p < 0.001).ConclusionThe variable resistance patterns observed for the insecticides evaluated may limit the effectiveness of operational interventions aimed at controlling Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus. Considering the results of this study, it can be indicated that the evaluated insecticides could still be used, provided that the PNCA implements and maintains a systematic resistance monitoring program to prevent its evolution and establishment or to eliminate selection pressure over time, allowing mosquito populations to recover susceptibility. Furthermore, it is essential to evaluate and implement alternative nonchemical strategies to preserve and extend the useful life of currently available insecticides for public health vector control in Panama.

Introduction

Dengue (DENV), chikungunya (CHIKV), and Zika (ZIKV) viruses continue to pose a major public health threat in many regions worldwide, including Panama. Approximately 5.6 billion people are at risk of infection with dengue and other arboviruses [1]. Dengue is the most prevalent arboviral disease globally, with an estimated 100–400 million cases reported annually [2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), 14.1 million dengue cases and 9,508 dengue-related deaths were reported worldwide in 2024 [3]. The Americas represent one of the most affected regions [3], where dengue is considered one of the most important re-emerging diseases [4]. In this region, a total of 13,063,434 dengue cases and 8,431 deaths were reported in 2024 [4]. In Panama, from the re-emergence of dengue in 1993 through 2025, a cumulative total of 189,501 cases and 183 deaths has been recorded. Since its emergence in 2014, chikungunya has accumulated 438 cases up to 2024, while from the first reported Zika cases in 2015 through 2024, a total of 1,371 cumulative cases have been documented [4, 5]. Dengue has continued to increase globally in 2025, placing unprecedented strain on healthcare systems worldwide. Since the beginning of the year, dengue cases have been reported across 94 countries, underscoring the growing geographic spread and public health burden of the disease [6].

Currently, Ae. aegypti is present in 167 countries [7] and is closely associated with humans, making it a highly important vector for human pathogens [8]. In the Americas, Ae. aegypti is the primary vector of dengue [9], including in Panama [10]. Both Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus are vectors of major infectious diseases, particularly those caused by DENV, CHIKV, ZIKV, and yellow fever virus [11]. Aedes albopictus has a wide distribution in the Americas, and several published studies have identified it as a potential arbovirus vector. However, despite its importance as a vector and reservoir of dengue virus, few studies have evaluated its vector competence in the region. More than 70% of publications reporting naturally infected Ae. albopictus with DENV and ZIKV originate from Brazil, Mexico, Colombia, and Costa Rica [12]. The co-circulation of these arboviruses, which produce similar clinical symptoms in humans, represents a significant challenge for public health systems [13].

The current situation of DENV, CHIKV, and ZIKV transmitted by Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus is complex due to the absence or limited availability of effective vaccines, as well as the lack of specific antiviral treatments targeting these diseases [14]. Consequently, vector control remains the cornerstone of prevention and control strategies [15]. However, it continues to be challenging, as decades of vector control programs have failed to achieve sustained and effective mosquito population control [16]. Historically, the main strategies used for managing arbovirus vector populations, particularly Ae. aegypti, have focused on larval control through source reduction and the application of insecticides targeting larvae and adults [17, 18]. The surveillance and control of Ae. aegypti continue to pose major challenges for public health services worldwide. In this context, the Integrated Vector Management (IVM) framework established by the WHO promotes sustainable, evidence-based, and intersectoral approaches to vector control. This strategy emphasizes the integration of entomological surveillance, environmental management, community engagement, and political commitment to reduce disease transmission [19]. During arbovirus outbreaks, the most common response is the use of chemical control measures [20, 21], including ultra-low volume (ULV) space spraying to eliminate infected female Ae. aegypti capable of transmitting viruses [22]. However, Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus have developed resistance to a wide range of insecticides [23, 24], posing a serious threat to the sustainability of prevention and control interventions [25]. Therefore, monitoring insecticide resistance in local mosquito populations is essential for the effective control of vector-borne diseases [26].

In Panama, the application of chemical insecticides has been the primary tool for controlling Ae. aegypti populations during the reinfestation of 1941 and its subsequent eradication in 1958, as well as during the reinfestations of 1969 and 1974, followed by eradication in 1976. This strategy was also used during the most recent reinfestation recorded in 1985 [27]. Starting in 1996, the National Aedes Control Program (Programa Nacional de Control de Aedes—PNCA) of the Ministry of Health (Ministerio de Salud—MINSA) replaced the organophosphate adulticides fenitrothion and malathion with the pyrethroids (PYs) deltamethrin, lambda-cyhalothrin, and cyfluthrin for routine applications against Aedes populations [10]. For larval control, temephos has historically been the most frequently used first-line larvicide. Subsequently, the biological control agent Vectobac® (Bacillus thuringiensis var. israelensis, Bti) and the insect growth regulator Flubex® (diflubenzuron) were incorporated, although both have been used infrequently [27, 28]. However, studies in Ae. aegypti have reported resistance to the organochlorine DDT (Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane) [29]; to organophosphates such as temephos [29, 30], fenitrothion and malathion [31], chlorpyrifos-methyl, and pirimiphos-methyl [29, 32]; and to PYs including deltamethrin, lambda-cyhalothrin, cypermethrin, permethrin, and cyfluthrin [29, 30]. Additionally, resistance to pirimiphos-methyl and chlorpyrifos-methyl has been reported in Ae. albopictus [30, 32]. In Ae. aegypti from 24 de Diciembre, the esterase Est-A4 associated with resistance to temephos, pirimiphos-methyl, and chlorpyrifos-methyl was detected [30]. More recent studies have identified altered activity of α-esterases (α-Est), β-esterases (β-Est), and glutathione S-transferases (GSTs), as well as incipient activity of mixed function oxidases (MFOs) in Ae. aegypti. In Ae. albopictus, altered activity of α-Est and β-Est has also been reported [29, 31]. Regarding knockdown resistance (kdr) mutations, Val1016Gly, Val1016Ile, Ile1011Met, and F1534C mutations have been detected in Ae. aegypti populations [29, 33, 34]. Despite the use of different classes of insecticides by the PNCA against Aedes populations, few studies to date have evaluated insecticide resistance in larval populations of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus across different regions of the country. Considering the geographic expansion and increasing intensity of dengue infections in Panama [4, 5], it is essential to generate updated data on susceptibility patterns of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus populations to ensure the efficacy of currently applied and alternative insecticides, strengthen national policies on the rational use of these compounds, and support the implementation of new control strategies. The objective of this study was to evaluate insecticide resistance in larval populations of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus.

Materials and methods

Study site selection

To determine the resistance status to different insecticides in Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus populations, a total of 15 urban and semi-urban communities were selected in coordination with the PNCA. Each community belonged to a different municipality and was located in dengue endemic regions of Panama. Site selection was based on epidemiological relevance, considering the number of reported dengue cases and the circulation of multiple serotypes, as well as entomological importance, characterized by high infestation indices and frequent insecticide applications. Sampling covered the central, eastern, and western regions of the country. Mosquito collection and colony maintenance

Collections of immature stages (larvae and pupae) of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus were carried out in different types of larval habitats with different ecological and local environmental characteristics, located in intra and peridomestic areas of urban and semi-urban environments. Sampling was carried out within an area of 100 m2, corresponding approximately to one city block. All collected biological material was placed in pre-labeled containers and transported to the Department of Medical Entomology at the Gorgas Memorial Institute of the Health Studies (Instituto Conmemorativo Gorgas de Estudios de la Salud—ICGES) for species identification using taxonomic keys for mosquito larvae and pupae [35]. Subsequently, colonies were established under laboratory conditions at a temperature of 28.5–30 °C, relative humidity of 70–80%, and a photoperiod of 12:12 h (light/dark) [28, 29]. Adults emerging from field collected immature stages were designated as the F₀ generation. The first laboratory generation obtained from eggs oviposited by F₀ adults was designated as the F₁ generation. Susceptibility bioassays were performed using F₁ larvae from the different field strains of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus.

Susceptibility bioassays

Standardized larval susceptibility bioassays recommended by the WHO were used to establish complete dose mortality curves [36–38]. The susceptibility of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus populations was evaluated against the PYs deltamethrin and lambda-cyhalothrin, the organophosphate temephos, as well as the alternative insecticides propoxur and fenitrothion. Late third instar or early fourth instar F1 larvae of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus were used in the bioassays. Groups of 25 larvae were exposed to solutions containing 99 ml of water plus 1 ml of the selected insecticide; control groups contained only 1 ml of acetone diluted in water. Seven discriminating concentrations of each insecticide were evaluated, prepared in analytical-grade acetone (w/v), within a range of 0.0015–0.09 mg/l, in order to obtain mortality rates between 10 and 95% and determine lethal concentrations LC₅₀ and LC₉₀, respectively. Each bioassay consisted of exposing larval samples in 100 ml of diagnostic concentrations of the selected insecticides for 24 h. A total of 100 larvae were tested per concentration, using 700 larvae per bioassay. Each bioassay was replicated at least three times. Mortality was recorded at 24 h, and Abbott’s formula was applied when control mortality ranged between 5 and 20%; bioassays were discarded when control mortality exceeded 20% [39]. The Ae. aegypti Rockefeller (Rock) and Ae. albopictus Fraga strains were used as susceptible reference strains (Fraga: susceptible Ae. albopictus strain maintained since 2012 at the insectary of the “Pedro Kourí” Institute of Tropical Medicine, Havana, Cuba). Moribund larvae were counted as dead [28, 29].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Probit analysis (Polo-PC statistical package, version 27) to determine lethal concentrations causing 50% (LC₅₀) and 90% (LC₉₀) mortality in field populations of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus. The resistance ratio (RR₅₀ and RR₉₀) was calculated by dividing the LC₅₀ and LC₉₀ values of field populations of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus by those of the reference strains Ae. aegypti Rock and Ae. albopictus Fraga, respectively. Populations were classified as susceptible or resistant according to the following criteria: RR₅₀ < 5 = susceptible; RR₅₀ between 5 and 10 = moderate resistance; RR₅₀ > 10 = resistant [40]. To identify statistically significant spatial differences in resistance and/or susceptibility among Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus populations, data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test.

Ethical considerations

This study did not require approval from the Institutional Bioethics Committee of ICGES, as no personal information was collected from residents of the households where Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus collections were conducted. Biological material was collected only after providing detailed information to household owners about the purpose and relevance of the study. Collections were carried out exclusively after obtaining verbal informed consent from the homeowners.

Discussion

Larval susceptibility bioassays

The PNCA has used larvicide agents for the control of Ae. aegypti larval populations over the past 40 years [10] and for Ae. albopictus over the past 23 years. Among the larvicides used, temephos has historically been the most frequently applied first-line agent in operational vector control programs, whereas diflubenzuron and Bti have been used much less frequently. To date, despite the continuous use of these larvicides, particularly temephos, few studies have evaluated the resistance status of Aedes larval populations to the insecticides deltamethrin and lambda-cyhalothrin, as well as to the alternative insecticides propoxur and fenitrothion, across different endemic regions of the country. This situation generates considerable uncertainty regarding the true evolutionary dynamics and patterns of resistance and/or susceptibility. The lack of updated information may contribute to the progressive increase in resistance intensity in areas where resistant mosquito populations exist. Furthermore, this information gap compromises the durability and efficacy of insecticidal compounds used in vector control strategies. Insecticides represent an essential component in the control of mosquito vectors, and maintaining their surveillance is of fundamental importance to prolong the useful lifespan of insecticide molecules.

According to the WHO, insecticides constitute an essential component of vector-borne disease control and represent the most widely used and effective strategy for reducing transmission and disease burden. Therefore, knowledge of the insecticide resistance status, its associated mechanisms, and the spatiotemporal changes occurring in Ae. aegypti populations is essential for designing effective control interventions [41]. Furthermore, the implementation of routine insecticide resistance monitoring programs is of critical importance for guiding evidence-based vector control strategies. In addition, evidence-based decision-making helps ensure that effective insecticides are selected and used based on scientific evidence and can inform policy development as well as operational decisions regarding insecticide use [42]. To date, the PNCA does not have an established program for surveillance and monitoring of the resistance to the applied insecticides; consequently, there is no comprehensive database on the resistance status to the different classes of insecticides used in the various dengue-endemic regions of the country. In Brazil, for example, the National Program of Dengue Control applies operational criteria to assess resistance to temephos. According to these criteria, Ae. aegypti populations with RR₉₅ > 3 are considered resistant. This threshold accounts for the time required by the Ministry of Health and municipalities to replenish insecticides used in control programs [43].

This study covered the main regions of epidemiological relevance for dengue at the national level, providing an updated assessment of the resistance and/or susceptibility status of larval populations of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus. In Panama, studies on resistance in larval populations of Aedes are scarce; therefore, this work represents the first study aimed at mapping the distribution of resistance and/or susceptibility to larvicides used for the control of these two Aedes species in the country. In our study, all evaluated Ae. aegypti strains were susceptible to the carbamate propoxur. In a previous study, eight Ae. aegypti populations were also reported as susceptible to propoxur and bendiocarb [29]. Studies conducted in Colombia [44] and Mexico [45] likewise reported susceptibility of Ae. aegypti strains to propoxur. In Malaysia, susceptibility to propoxur was observed in Ae. aegypti larvae; however, adults of this species in eight districts of Selangor State showed high levels of resistance to this insecticide [46]. It is important to highlight that propoxur has not been used by the PNCA for Aedes control; therefore, these findings suggest that it could be considered as an alternative insecticide in the event of resistance to currently used PYs. In this context, it is essential to evaluate and monitor the resistance and/or susceptibility status of Ae. aegypti populations to guide rational insecticide use and resistance management strategies.

Regarding the organophosphate insecticides evaluated, all strains were susceptible to fenitrothion. In an initial study involving two Ae. aegypti populations (Victoriano Lorenzo and Río Abajo), both were susceptible to temephos, fenitrothion, malathion, fenthion, pirimiphos-methyl, and chlorpyrifos [32]. In a subsequent study, eight Ae. aegypti strains were also susceptible to fenitrothion [28]. More recently, in a study involving 12 adult Ae. aegypti strains, seven showed possible resistance to fenitrothion [29]. In Brazil, a study evaluating temephos and fenitrothion in 10 Ae. aegypti populations found that only two (Marília and Presidente Prudente) were susceptible to both insecticides, while only one (Santos) exhibited established resistance to fenitrothion [47]. Resistance to fenitrothion has also been detected in Cuba [48, 49], Venezuela [50], and Colombia [25]. In a similar study in Africa, two larval populations of Ae. aegypti (Niamey and Yantala) were found to be susceptible to both temephos and fenitrothion [51]. Importantly, the PNCA has not used fenitrothion or malathion since 1996, when they were replaced by deltamethrin, cyfluthrin, and lambda-cyhalothrin as adulticidal agents.

With respect to resistance to temephos, the main chemical larvicide used, moderate resistance was detected in Ae. aegypti strains from 24 de Diciembre, San Isidro, and Puerto Armuelles, while the remaining strains were susceptible to this insecticide. In a previous study, Ae. aegypti from 24 de Diciembre showed high resistance to temephos (RR₅₀ = 23.3) and pirimiphos-methyl (RR₅₀ = 12.3) [30]. In a similar study, moderate resistance to temephos was detected in Ae. aegypti strains from Parque Lefevre (RR₅₀ = 7.2) and Pueblo Nuevo (RR₅₀ = 6.2) [31]. In evaluations conducted with Ae. aegypti strains from La Villa and Llano Bonito, susceptibility to the temephos formulation (Abate®) was observed [52]. Similarly, Ae. aegypti strains from Villa Cárdenas, Caminos de Omar, and Río Abajo were also susceptible to Abate® and Natular® DT (Saccharopolyspora spinosa) [53]. This variable pattern of susceptibility to temephos among Ae. aegypti populations highlights the need for routine monitoring to determine potential changes in susceptibility status in mosquito populations subjected to selective pressure from this larvicide agent.

Resistance to temephos has been reported in Thailand [54], Malaysia [55] and India [56]. In the Americas, a study conducted in Peru across 39 localities in three regions found that Ae. aegypti exhibited varying levels of resistance to temephos, even 3 years after its use had been discontinued [57]. In Colombia, Ae. aegypti populations from 14 municipalities were found to be susceptible to temephos and malathion, but resistant to fenitrothion [58]. In Mexico, high frequencies of resistance to temephos were reported in 23 Ae. aegypti populations [59]. In Venezuela, all Ae. aegypti strains were resistant to malathion but susceptible to pirimiphos-methyl, temephos, and propoxur [60]. In Brazil, resistance to temephos is widespread; it is no longer considered a first line larvicide for controlling Ae. aegypti and has been replaced by other non-neurotoxic products [61]. Based on previous studies, susceptibility to temephos may recover over time [62]. Other studies have described that resistance to temephos in field populations of Ae. aegypti may decrease [63–65] or increase over time, depending on the selection pressure [23]. In Cuba, studies on resistance reversion showed that susceptibility to temephos could be restored as α-EST activity levels declined. These findings suggested alternating the use of temephos with other WHO recommended larvicides, such as Bti or pyriproxyfen, to maintain its efficacy and achieve more effective control of Ae. aegypti [66].

Regarding the PYs insecticides evaluated, moderate resistance to deltamethrin and lambda-cyhalothrin was observed in Ae. aegypti strains from El Coco, Aguadulce, David, and Puerto Armuelles, while all other strains were susceptible to these insecticides. Despite the use of PYs such as deltamethrin, lambda-cyhalothrin, and cyfluthrin by the PNCA, there is a lack of studies assessing the evolutionary status and distribution of resistance in both larval and adult populations of Ae. aegypti. In previous studies using pyrethroid insecticides, two Ae. aegypti larval strains (Victoriano Lorenzo and Río Abajo) were susceptible to deltamethrin, lambda-cyhalothrin, cypermethrin, and cyfluthrin [32]. In another similar study, only the Ae. aegypti strain from Chitré showed moderate resistance to deltamethrin, while all other strains were susceptible to the evaluated insecticides [28]. Resistance of Ae. aegypti to PYs insecticides, particularly deltamethrin, has been reported in several countries in South, Central, and North America, as well as in some African countries and multiple countries across Asia and Oceania [67].

The results of our study show a heterogeneous pattern of resistance to the insecticides evaluated among the different strains of Ae. aegypti, similar to that reported in studies conducted in other countries. In general, mosquito populations subjected to intense selective pressure through frequent insecticide applications tend to develop resistance, although the development of resistance depends on several factors. Factors that may influence the development of resistance include biological, genetic, ecological, and operational factors [68]. It is important to highlight that biological and genetic factors are intrinsic to the species and, therefore, beyond our control; however, understanding their contribution is essential, as it helps assess the propensity for resistance in mosquito populations. In contrast, operational factors can be controlled and monitored through the implementation of resistance surveillance and management programs.

In our study, the variable susceptibility patterns observed among the different Ae. aegypti strains in response to the insecticides evaluated were similar to those reported in studies conducted across various countries and regions worldwide. In Singapore, larvae from seven Ae. aegypti populations were found to be highly resistant to permethrin but exhibited low levels of resistance to temephos [69]. In Malaysia, susceptibility in Ae. aegypti was heterogeneous across study sites, with resistance levels ranging from low to high for all PYs [55]. In Indonesia, the Ae. aegypti Garut strain showed moderate resistance to permethrin [70]. In Brazil, the Ae. aegypti Rio strain exhibited high resistance to deltamethrin (RR₅₀ = 32.6) and moderate resistance to temephos (RR₅₀ = 7.0) [71]. In another study, two out of three Ae. aegypti populations (Crato and Barbalha) showed resistance to cypermethrin, whereas Juazeiro do Norte displayed only altered susceptibility [72]. In Mexico, Ae. aegypti larvae showed high resistance to permethrin and low resistance to deltamethrin [73]. In Colombia, a study of 10 Ae. aegypti populations found resistance to permethrin in all populations analyzed [74]. In Costa Rica, high resistance to deltamethrin was detected in Ae. aegypti strains from Limón and Puntarenas, along with low resistance to cypermethrin [75].

This is the first nationwide study conducted with larval populations of Ae. albopictus. In the evaluations, only the Ae. albopictus population from Canto del Llano showed moderate resistance to deltamethrin and temephos, while all other populations were susceptible to the evaluated insecticides. In a previous study, moderate resistance to temephos was detected in Ae. albopictus populations from Parque Lefevre (RR₅₀ = 7.2) and Pueblo Nuevo (RR₅₀ = 6.2), whereas populations from Juan Díaz and Río Abajo were susceptible [31]. The moderate resistance to temephos and deltamethrin detected in Ae. albopictus from Canto del Llano may be associated with the use of agrochemicals and the possible presence of cross-resistance mechanisms among different compounds [76]. In contrast to Ae. aegypti, relatively few studies have examined insecticide resistance in Ae. albopictus worldwide [77, 78]. A study conducted in China with Ae. albopictus larvae revealed varying degrees of resistance to deltamethrin, permethrin, and beta-cypermethrin [79]. In another study in China involving eight Ae. albopictus strains, different levels of resistance to beta-cypermethrin, deltamethrin, and permethrin were observed [80]. In Latin America, studies on insecticide resistance in Ae. albopictus reveal a heterogeneous pattern influenced by geography, historical insecticide use, and local coexistence with Ae. aegypti [81]. In a recent study in Cuba, resistance to temephos and deltamethrin was detected in Ae. albopictus populations from Mulgoba and Plaza, and resistance to lambda-cyhalothrin was observed in Plaza [82]. In Mexico, Ae. albopictus showed resistance to malathion and, to a lesser extent, to chlorpyrifos, temephos, permethrin, and deltamethrin [45]. Current evidence suggests that resistance levels in Ae. albopictus remain relatively low compared to Ae. aegypti [83]. One possible explanation for this pattern is that Ae. albopictus populations have been less frequently exposed to household insecticide applications and indoor vector control interventions than Ae. aegypti. However, due to the expansion of Ae. albopictus into areas where insecticides are intensively used in agriculture, particularly in newly colonized larval habitats [84], it is highly likely that insecticide resistance will eventually increase and negatively affect the ability to control this vector in the future [85].

Overall, in this study, larval populations of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus exhibited variable susceptibility profiles to the insecticides evaluated, with resistance levels differing across study sites and among insecticides. It was observed that the resistance profiles or levels to the tested insecticides in Ae. albopictus were generally lower than those in Ae. aegypti. It is important to note that the dynamics of resistance acquisition in mosquito populations may be influenced by multiple factors, including the history of insecticide use, preexisting susceptibility to chemical compounds, the frequency and inheritance of resistance associated genes, and the co-selection of different resistance mechanisms [86]. Previous studies have suggested that this variability in susceptibility may be due to differences in the underlying mechanisms responsible for resistant phenotypes, which may not be shared between species. Aedes aegypti, which is highly adapted to urban environments, is more frequently exposed to insecticides and, consequently, is more likely to develop resistance compared to Ae. albopictus [87, 88]. Indeed, several studies have reported that resistance levels in Ae. albopictus remain relatively low compared to Ae. aegypti, possibly due to reduced exposure to insecticides [85, 89]. A review of multiple datasets on insecticide resistance in Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus from 2008 to 2015 revealed that resistance patterns to commonly used insecticides such as deltamethrin and temephos vary geographically between these species [67]. Another study demonstrated that the susceptibility and resistance status of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus to insecticides varies considerably worldwide [11]. In Malaysia, significant differences in pyrethroid resistance profiles between the two species have been documented, with consistently higher resistance prevalence in Ae. aegypti, whereas Ae. albopictus populations were mostly fully susceptible [55]. These findings underscore the importance of continuous resistance monitoring at regional scales to identify both geographic and temporal trends in insecticide resistance [90].

When the results obtained in the present study were compared with those previously reported for adult mosquitoes from the same strains [10], only the Ae. aegypti strains from El Coco, Aguadulce, David, and Puerto Armuelles exhibited a nearly similar resistance pattern to the PYs deltamethrin and lambda-cyhalothrin. Likewise, the Ae. albopictus strain from Canto del Llano showed a similar resistance pattern to deltamethrin. The resistance observed in these larval populations is likely associated with the application of these PYs for the control of adult mosquito populations. It should be noted that the use of PYs as larvicides is generally limited [69]. Pyrethroids have been widely used by vector control programs to control Ae. aegypti populations through ULV spraying conducted with either handheld or vehicle-mounted equipment. Studies on insecticide resistance in Ae. aegypti larvae have shown that resistance phenotypes selected in adult mosquitoes may also be expressed in both adult and immature stages. However, it is important to note that the resistance profile observed in larvae does not always correspond to that observed in adults, and additional adulticide bioassays are required to validate this observation [90]. It is also important to highlight the increasing reports of widespread resistance involving multiple mechanisms in Aedes populations worldwide [91]. Nevertheless, we emphasize studies suggesting that resistance may be associated with the nature of larval habitats in which immature stages develop [92]. Likewise, it has been proposed that resistance may be influenced by the characteristics of larval habitats and by rearing conditions, particularly the type of larval habitats site, which may affect resistance levels to pyrethroid insecticides [93].

The PNCA has used PYs insecticides against adult Ae. aegypti populations since 1996, primarily deltamethrin throughout the country [10]. As previously noted, surveillance of insecticide susceptibility by the PNCA is currently lacking, as there is no established resistance monitoring program. Only a limited number of localized studies are available, which do not provide a temporal framework to assess the evolutionary dynamics and distribution of resistance to the insecticides applied in the main dengue-endemic regions of the country. In the Americas, only a few countries including Cuba, Colombia, Mexico, and Brazil have extensively documented, through numerous studies, the evolutionary status and distribution of resistance to different classes of insecticides in Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus. More broadly, many countries in the region lack comprehensive national regulatory frameworks for the selection, management, appropriate use, surveillance, and resistance management of insecticides within vector control programs. Public health regulations governing insecticide use for vector-borne disease control are often inadequate and inefficient in many regions [94, 95]. If not properly regulated, the use of pesticides for vector control including domestic applications and those conducted by pest control companies [96, 97] may compromise the effectiveness of interventions and pose risks to human health and the environment. In Panama and across the region, there is a pressing need to implement national policies, legislation, and appropriate guidelines to ensure the safe and effective use of public health insecticides, as well as the surveillance and management of resistance within vector control programs. These efforts are essential to develop strategies that prevent or delay the emergence of resistance, reduce the need for frequent changes in insecticide use, prolong the useful life of insecticide compounds, lower vector control costs, and ultimately reduce the incidence of mosquito-borne diseases.

Limitations

One limitation of this study is that a comparative analysis of the evolution and distribution of insecticide resistance could not be conducted for most Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus strains due to the lack of previous reports. Furthermore, although temephos is recognized as the primary tool for controlling mosquito populations and responding to outbreaks, records of application sites and treatment frequencies were unavailable. Consequently, we were unable to fully characterize the history of previous larvicidal interventions in each locality. In addition, pyrethroid-based interventions, such pyrethroids ULV nebulization using portable and truck-mounted equipment, the use of PYs treated long-lasting insecticidal nets, and household aerosol insecticides, were not assessed during field data collection. This limitation restricts our ability to identify factors that may potentially influence the resistance patterns observed in the study populations. However, our findings provide a valuable basis for the PNCA to evaluate and optimize insecticide selection and application strategies for the control of larval populations of Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus.

Conclusions

The variable resistance patterns observed among the insecticides evaluated in this study may compromise the effectiveness of operational interventions targeting Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus populations. Nevertheless, these insecticides may continue to be used, provided that the PNCA implements and sustains a systematic insecticide resistance monitoring program to detect changes in susceptibility, prevent the development and establishment of resistance, and reduce selection pressure over time. Such measures may allow mosquito populations to regain susceptibility and support the long-term effectiveness of vector control interventions. Furthermore, alternative nonchemical approaches, including biological control agents such as BTi and S. spinosa, as well as insect growth regulators (diflubenzuron, methoprene and, pyriproxyfen), should be evaluated and incorporated into integrated vector management strategies. The adoption of these approaches may help preserve and extend the operational lifespan of the insecticides currently available for public health vector control in Panama.
