Therapeutic-scale CAR-NK microrobots with programmable swarm navigation for targeted solid tumor therapy
Authors: Zhiguang Wu, Yalin Wang, Hongyue Zhang, Xinjian Fan, Mingtong Li, Zesheng Li, Gongzi Zhang, Jialin Wang, Yumeng Lu, Hongda Wang, Yuxin Jia, Hongri Gu, He Tan, Jiawei Tian, Ximing Sun, Hui Xie, Lihai Zhang, Metin Sitti, Jie Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An urgent requirement in chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) immune cell therapy [e.g., CAR–natural killer (NK) cells] for solid tumors is to engineer the cells for precise tumor targeting. Meanwhile, medical microrobots can be navigated toward specific target sites using external magnetic fields. Here, by bridging these approaches, we turn CAR-NK cells into magnetic biohybrid microrobots (CAR-NK microrobots) that achieve controllable long-distance targeted navigation at therapeutic doses (∼106 cells) in both murine and nonhuman primate animal models in vivo. CAR-NK microrobots are developed through genetic engineering with CAR structures and loading with magnetic nanoparticles for field-based navigation. Different magnetic field inputs reconfigure their swarms between vortex and ribbon modes, allowing dense aggregation for targeted navigation and further broad dispersion for large-area coverage. Using programmable magnetic fields, in vivo CAR-NK microrobot–based therapy is demonstrated for metastatic leptomeningeal carcinomatosis in brain’s cerebrospinal fluid space. After intrathecal injection, the microrobots are magnetically guided long distance through the spinal canal toward the brain in vortex mode and subsequently spread over the target site in ribbon mode, as visualized by real-time angiographic imaging in a nonhuman primate model, providing a proof-of-feasibility demonstration in a nonhuman primate anatomical setting.

INTRODUCTION

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)–expressing immune cells, in which immune cells are genetically engineered to acquire redirected specificity for targeting malignant cells, have demonstrated remarkable success in curing leukemia and other cancers in diverse clinical trials (1–3). Among various types of cells, natural killer (NK) cells have been represented as a next-generation cell-based cancer treatment strategy owing to their mild side effects and excellent therapeutic efficacy compared with T cell–based immunotherapy (4–6). However, in solid tumors, their potency is blocked by local immunosuppression and the heterogeneous expression of the antigen that the CAR targets (7). Various strategies, such as the upgrading of CAR designs and engineered bacteria guidance, have been reported to improve the therapeutic response toward solid tumors. For example, CARs incorporating receptors such as NKG2D or HER2-scFv can recognize a broad range of ligands for wider target specificity, whereas interleukin-15 (IL-15) signaling can be harnessed to enhance NK cell–mediated cytotoxicity (8–10). Moreover, to shorten the pathway toward tumor in the brain, intrathecal delivery of CAR immune cells has been reported to treat metastatic medulloblastoma and ependymoma (11). Among metastatic brain tumors, leptomeningeal metastasis occurs in ∼5% of patients with cancer, particularly those with breast, lung, or ovarian cancer (12). The leptomeningeal tumor mainly adheres to the pia mater of the brain, which is unresectable, and residual tumor cells after resection eventually cause recurrence (13). It has been reported that the antitumor activity, dosing, and safety of HER2-CAR T cells against breast leptomeningeal tumors support the conclusion that intrathecal HER2-targeted therapy is a useful option for the treatment of leptomeningeal tumors (14). Although progress has been made in solid tumors, CAR immune cells that rely on passive diffusion in complex fluids still suffer from slow migration and limited persistence in vivo.

In contrast, the rise of micro- and nanorobotics holds considerable promise to revolutionize conventional medical practice, sparking burgeoning research interest across a wide range of applications from targeted drug delivery to minimally invasive surgery (15–28). These miniature robotic systems, which can navigate hard-to-reach tissues, from narrow vasculature to confined tissue niches, embody the convergence of robotics and medicine, thereby garnering substantial attention in precision medicine (29–38). Early in vivo studies on micro- and nanorobotics primarily focused on individual micro/nanorobots, validating their potential to navigate micro- and nanoscale environments through diverse propulsion mechanisms (39–54). Notably, recent advances in reconfigurable robotic swarms that can dynamically adapt their form and function through programmable interactions represent a transformative frontier in robotics, enabling unprecedented versatility in complex environments (55–63). To endow these micro- and nanorobots with biofunctions, recent progress has focused on developing cell-based microrobots, leveraging their biocompatibility and inherent biological properties such as chemotaxis to penetrate various biological barriers for versatile and robust biomedical applications. As a result, various cell types, including erythrocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, stem cells, and engineered T cells, have been used to develop biohybrid swimming microrobots, given their established utility in cell therapy (64–67). Therefore, we propose that the approach of engineering CAR-NK cells into microrobotic swarms can enable active long-range delivery along with precise cytotoxicity against tumors, offering an innovative technology for advanced CAR-based immunotherapy.

However, turning CAR-equipped immune cells into effective microrobotic medicines remains elusive as four practical hurdles have not been overcome. First, swarm size. To date, magnetic control has been demonstrated over only a few dozen immune cells at once (68), far below the 107 to 108 cell doses routinely administered in clinical settings (69). Second, magnetization. T and NK lymphocytes lack the phagocytic “gulp” of macrophages, so nanoparticles (NPs) must adhere to their cell membranes (70); this results in a weak magnetic moment, poses risks of blocking surface receptors, and generates insufficient thrust to navigate against physiological fluid flows or migrate effectively over long distances in vivo. Third, range of action. Engineered lymphocytes can recognize and eliminate targets upon contact, but they crawl at a rate of only ∼3 mm hour−1 and rely on passive diffusion. Consequently, deep-seated or highly dispersed solid tumors remain out of reach unless an external guidance system can bring the cells within notably distance (71). Fourth, anatomical scale. Nearly all in vivo studies have been conducted in mice, whose 1- to 2-mm spinal canals and slow, laminar cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flows bear little resemblance to the centimeter-wide, pulsatile human spinal canals an oversimplification evocative of the “EPR gap,” in which NP therapies succeed in rodents but fail in patients (72).

Herein, we design and validate an approach to engineer CAR-NK cells into magnetic swimming microrobots (CAR-NK microrobots) and achieve their magnetic long-distance swarming propulsion in CSF for targeting metastatic leptomeningeal carcinomatosis in vivo (Fig. 1). The CAR-NK microrobots are functionalized with magnetic NPs for multimodal swarm navigation and incorporate a specifically designed CAR structure to target HER2-positive metastatic cancer. Our approach leverages electroporation-based intracellular loading of magnetic NPs, which circumvents the limited phagocytic capacity of NK cells and achieves a fivefold enhancement in saturation magnetization (Ms) compared with surface adsorption or phagocytosis-based methods, enabling robust propulsion performance under an external magnetic field. Combined with a robotic arm–assisted rotating gradient magnetic field (RARGMF; as illustrated in fig. S2), this design drives CAR-NK microrobots to form macroscopic, reconfigurable swarms that transition from compact vortex formations, optimized for long-distance CSF migration (over 2 cm in mice and ∼15 cm in nonhuman primates), to expanded ribbonlike swarms that conform to the surfaces of leptomeningeal tumors. This reconfiguration achieves a substantial increase in lesion coverage compared to passive CAR-NK cell controls. In vivo imaging reveals the dynamic swarm behavior of CAR-NK microrobots: Vortex swarms facilitate navigated migration through CSF from the lumbar spine to brain metastases, followed by morphological transformation into ribbon swarms that blanket tumor surfaces, enabling high coverage of leptomeningeal carcinomatosis lesions. Nonhuman primate studies, integrated with clinical digital subtraction angiography (DSA) imaging, further support the feasibility of image-guided navigation at a larger anatomical scale, demonstrating migration through the spinal canal and adaptive dissemination in the brain. This represents a critical step toward clinical translation. This work bridges the gap between microrobotics and immunotherapy by treating CAR-NK cells as multimodal microrobotic units, each functioning as a biocompatible actuator-sensor module that collectively addresses complex in vivo navigation challenges via reconfigurable swarm intelligence. By addressing the dual challenges of propulsion efficiency and morphological adaptability, our platform establishes a universal strategy for targeted solid tumor therapy, overcoming the limitations of static immune cell delivery and pushing the boundaries of biohybrid, reconfigurable microrobotics for precision medicine.

RESULTS

Rational design and fabrication of CAR-NK microrobots

The fabrication of CAR-NK microrobots involves two critical steps: genetic engineering of tumor-targeting CARs and intracellular loading of magnetic NPs (Fig. 2A). The CAR structure was designed with modular components: NKG2D for innate immune activation, 4-1BB for costimulatory signaling, HER2-scFv for antigen recognition, enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) for fluorescence labeling, and human IL-15 for autocrine survival support-tailored for HER2-positive metastatic carcinomatosis while enabling broader applicability to HER2-expressing tumors. Genetic modification was achieved using the Sleeping Beauty (SB) transposon system, wherein the CAR plasmid was delivered via electroporation to ensure stable integration into NK cell genomes (detailed plasmid map in fig. S1). Moreover, to enable navigation under an external magnetic field, magnetic NPs needed to be loaded into the CAR-NK microrobots. Relying on the phagocytic capacity of native neutrophils and macrophages, the functionalization of these particles has been achieved through cocultivation and surface chemistry of various NPs with neutrophils and macrophages, respectively (64, 65, 68). However, the limited phagocytic capacity of NK cells may interfere with the loading efficiency of magnetic NPs into CAR-NK microrobots. To address this, three magnetic NP loading methods (using 10-nm Fe3O4 NPs as an example) were compared: cocultivation with magnetic NPs (denoted as cocultivation), biotin conjugation via surface chemistry (denoted as surface chemistry in Fig. 2B), and electroporation (denoted as electroporation in Fig. 2B).

As shown in Fig. 2B, both cocultivation and surface chemistry strategies yielded low saturation magnetization [Ms: 0.8 to 1.0 electromagnetic units (emu)/g] due to insufficient NP uptake or interference from the cell membrane. In contrast, electroporation enables intracellular delivery of magnetic NPs, achieving an Ms of 5.1 emu/g; subsequent magnetization further increased this value to 5.6 emu/g, an ∼5-fold enhancement compared to surface-based approaches (Fig. 2B). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging (Fig. 2C) reveals that the CAR-NK microrobots preserved the intact morphology of native CAR-NK cells, ranging in size from ∼3 to 5 μm. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging (Fig. 2D) further confirms the presence of intracellular magnetic NP clusters within vesicles, verifying the efficient intracellular loading of Fe3O4 magnetic NPs. Moreover, the fluorescence microscope image in Fig. 2E shows the green fluorescence signal from EGFP in CAR-NK microrobots, and Western blot analysis indicates the presence of HER2 in these microrobots (Fig. 2F). Both sets of data verify the successful CAR expression in CAR-NK microrobots. Assessment of cell viability via trypan blue staining indicates minimal differences in cell viability after electroporation (∼92.4%) and subsequent magnetization (∼91.4%) steps. These values are comparable to those of unmodified NK cells (∼93.0%) and CAR-NK cells (∼92.4%), with minimal cell damage during electroporation or magnetization, confirming the biocompatibility of the procedure (Fig. 2G).

Inheriting the mobility toward cancer associated with CXC chemokines from natural NK cells, CAR-NK microrobots exhibit directed chemotactic motility along the gradient of CXCL12, a typical CXC chemokine involved in both physiological and pathological processes. They travel a distance of ∼420 μm over 7 hours, and their sensing range toward tumor cells is estimated to be ∼170 μm (Fig. 2H and movie S1), indicating that CAR-NK microrobots have the ability to sense and move toward tumors in a targeted manner. In addition to tumor sensing, time-lapse microscopic images in Fig. 2I further demonstrate that CAR-NK microrobots migrate toward G422 tumor cells (a typical cell line of intracranial glioblastoma), extend pseudopodia upon arrival, and subsequently induce tumor cell apoptosis within 40 min (movie S2). Collectively, these results validate the successful fabrication of CAR-NK microrobots that retain the biological and structural characteristics of CAR-NK cells. The microrobots have a net magnetization that enables magnetic alignment in response to an external magnetic field, as well as chemotaxis toward and subsequent killing of tumor cells.

Mobility of individual CAR-NK microrobot upon different magnetic field inputs

The navigation of CAR-NK microrobots is magnetically powered with various magnetic field inputs, which enables efficient actuation of the microrobots in a controllable manner. Initially, the magnetic movement of CAR-NK microrobots fabricated via different methods is compared under a typical rotating magnetic field (RMF) with uniform intensity. Time-lapse images in Fig. 3A show that the displacements of CAR-NK microrobots prepared by various fabrication methods over 8 s under a 15-Hz RMF are 12.3 μm (cocultivation), 22.8 μm (surface chemistry), 70.1 μm (electroporation), and 82.0 μm (electroporation followed by magnetization). The velocities of CAR-NK microrobots with different fabrication methods are examined under RMFs with frequencies ranging from 1 to 20 Hz. The dependence of velocities shows that CAR-NK microrobots prepared by different strategies exhibited similar behavioral trends but distinct step-out frequencies and corresponding velocities. Notably, CAR-NK microrobots fabricated via electroporation followed by magnetization exhibit optimal propulsion performance compared with those prepared by other methods, achieving a velocity of 17.2 ± 2.7 μm/s (Fig. 3B) under an 11-Hz RMF. Furthermore, previously reported cell-based microrobots are mainly composed of macrophages or neutrophils. These adherent cells tend to attach to the substrate, generating anisotropic fluid flow under an RMF to drive propulsion (68). In contrast, NK cells or T cells are suspension cells that naturally remain dispersed in the fluid. When exposed to RMF, they typically produce isotropic flow, leading to inefficient magnetic propulsion. Specifically, their velocity per body length is lower compared with microrobots constructed with adherent cells.

To this end, different magnetic field inputs, including gradient magnetic field (GMF), RMF with uniform intensity, and rotating gradient magnetic field (RGMF), are used to actuate CAR-NK microrobots for comparative analysis. Microscopic images in Fig. 3C (captured from movie S3) illustrate the motion trajectories of a CAR-NK microrobot over 8 s under the magnetic inputs of GMF, RMF, and RGMF. Specifically, the CAR-NK microrobot travels ∼75.2 μm in 8 s under RGMF exposure, roughly four times the distance achieved under RMF and eight times that under GMF. Further quantification in Fig. 3D reveals that the velocity of CAR-NK microrobots under GMF is primarily below 5 μm/s. In contrast, their velocity under RGMF reaches 43.3 μm/s at 15 Hz (Fig. 3D). Regarding the proportion of actuated CAR-NK microrobots, both RGMF and RMF inputs drive nearly all microrobots (∼99.8% for both RGMF and RMF) to move (Fig. 3E), whereas GMF only propelled ∼65.6% of the CAR-NK microrobots.

The controllable movement of CAR-NK microrobots can be precisely navigated via vision-based real-time feedback. Microscopic images in Fig. 3F (left; taken from movie S4) show controllable trajectories, indicating that the CAR-NK microrobots not only achieved circular turning with minimal velocity changes but also followed a predetermined circular path. Given that the velocity under RGMF is higher than that under RMF, this suggests that the CAR-NK microrobots under RGMF perform rotational motion while being attracted to the substrate (Fig. 3F, right). In addition, CAR-NK microrobots exhibit durability for long-distance travel. Data in Fig. 3G show that cell viability remains at ∼99.2% after traveling a distance of 8 cm. These data clearly indicate that CAR-NK microrobots can be efficiently propelled, and the magnetic input of RGMF is identified as the optimal strategy to actuate suspended cell-based magnetic biohybrid microrobots such as CAR-NK microrobots.

Swarm formation and transformation of CAR-NK microrobots under magnetic manipulation

After successfully actuating the motion of individual CAR-NK microrobots, swarm control investigations are conducted to further enhance the navigation performance of high-density CAR-NK microrobots. To simplify the investigation of swarm behaviors, various magnetic field inputs applied to the CAR-NK microrobots are designed with uniform intensity. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 4A, typical vortex-like swarms that enable high-density aggregation for transportation are induced in the CAR-NK microrobots under a horizontal RMF (denoted as Hv input). As shown in the microscopic image in Fig. 4B (captured from movie S5), the Hv magnetic field input (with a frequency of 10 Hz) drives the well-separated CAR-NK microrobots to aggregate and form a vortex-shaped swarm with an area of 1.25 mm2 within 10 s. Statistical data demonstrate that vortex swarms of CAR-NK microrobots exceeding 40 μm in size are generated under Hv input with frequencies ranging from 5 to 25 Hz, and the swarm size reaches a maximum of 64.6 μm at a frequency of 15Hz (Fig. 4C). The simulated stream plots of the flow velocity profile illustrate the fluidic interactions occurring at the initial stage of vortex formation (Fig. 4D).

(I) Time-lapse images showing the fusion of the vortex swarm of CAR-NK microrobots into a large vortex swarm. (J) Time-lapse images showing the transformation of CAR-NK microrobots swarm between the vortex and the ribbon. Scale bars, 100 μm.

On the other hand, Fig. 4E schematically illustrates the emergence of ribbonlike swarms of CAR-NK microrobots under a conical RMF (denoted as Hr input), which facilitates large-area synchronized spreading. Considering that the permanent magnetic moment of CAR-NK microrobots is oriented along their magnetization direction, these microrobots are thus subjected to hydrodynamic and magnetic dipole forces upon the application of the Hr magnetic field. Specifically, the magnetic dipole forces first attract surrounding microrobots, then align the adjacent microrobots along their polarization axis, and lastly drive their assembly into ribbon-shaped swarms. Microscopic images in Fig. 4F (taken from movie S6) demonstrate the formation of a CAR-NK microrobot ribbon swarm within 10 s of Hr magnetic field exposure (frequency: 5 Hz), where each ribbon swarm comprises ∼16 to 21 CAR-NK microrobots. The dependence of swarm length on the frequency of the Hr magnetic field shows that the average length of the ribbon swarm increases from 55.7 μm at a frequency of 1 Hz and subsequently reaches a maximum of ∼145 μm at an Hr magnetic field frequency of 15 Hz (Fig. 4G). Numerical simulations of the flow profile indicate that the fluid approaching the surrounding microrobots generates an attractive force, whereas the fluid flowing away produces a repulsive force (Fig. 4H), collectively leading to the formation of ribbonlike CAR-NK microrobot swarms. The above results confirm the successful formation of vortex and ribbon swarms of CAR-NK microrobots under the corresponding magnetic field inputs.

Apart from single aggregates, CAR-NK microrobots swarm as a dynamic integrated system, which also facilitates vortex fusion through long-range attractive forces exerted on surrounding microrobots upon application of the Hv magnetic field. Time-lapse microscopic images in Fig. 4I (captured from movie S6) demonstrate the fusion process of CAR-NK microrobot vortex swarms over 19 s, including exchange, fusion, and stabilization. Once the CAR-NK microrobot vortex swarms approach each other, the closest regions of the two adjacent vortices initiate microrobot exchange within 0 to 6 s. The swarms then enter a fusion phase, forming an irregularly shaped vortex aggregate. By sequentially engulfing smaller vortices along a spiral trajectory, several giant vortex swarms with a diameter of ∼0.4 mm emerge within 20 s, encompassing nearly 800 CAR-NK microrobots. Furthermore, changes in the magnetic field can disrupt the original swarm state and generate an additional dynamic steady state for the swarm. Through programmable manipulation of the magnetic field input, reversible transformations can be achieved between different swarming patterns. Time-lapse microscopic images in Fig. 4J illustrate the process of programmatically reconfiguring microrobot swarms between vortex and ribbon morphologies. The swarm type switched within ∼0.5 s upon switching the magnetic field input from Hv to Hr. Together, the reconfigurable and multimodal swarms of CAR-NK microrobots confer substantial propulsion performance advantages compared with individual CAR-NK microrobots. To clarify the operational meaning of reversible swarm reconfiguration, we define reversibility here as bidirectional switching between vortex and ribbon patterns under the tested magnetic inputs. In the recorded experiments, switching from Hv to Hr induced the vortex-to-ribbon transition within ∼0.5 s, and switching back restored compact vortex aggregation under Hv input. Because the maximum number of repeated cycles and the accumulated magnetic actuation duration are expected to depend on cell density, confinement, viscosity, and field amplitude, these limits were not generalized beyond the tested conditions. Dedicated repeated-cycle assays quantifying swarm size retention, cell viability, chemotaxis, and cytotoxicity after prolonged actuation will be required before clinical translation.

Long-distance swarm movement of therapeutic-scale CAR-NK microrobot swarms under a robotic arm–assisted magnetic field

Following the achievement of diverse swarm movements under different magnetic field inputs and the transformation between distinct swarm types, the next requirement for in vivo precision therapy is to realize long-distance navigation of CAR-NK microrobot swarms at a therapeutic scale. Given that the clinical dose of CAR immunotherapy is ∼107 cells per patient (73), the centimeter-scale navigation of more than 10% of this dose (∼106 cells) toward target tumors is expected to enhance therapeutic efficacy in practical applications. To address this, an RARGMF is used to actuate the long-distance (∼8 cm) movement of therapeutic-scale CAR-NK microrobots (∼5 × 106 cells). A GMF mounted on a robotic arm (denoted as RAGMF) is also used as a control, and the movement behavior of microrobots under RAGMF is captured for comparison (Fig. 5A). An 8-cm-long model spinal canal is constructed to mimic the in vivo distance between the intrathecal injection site and the brain. Because the microscopic field of view cannot cover the movement of therapeutic-dose CAR-NK microrobots, their swarm movement is visualized using a macro camera.

(F) Arrival ratio of CAR-NK microrobots upon RAGMF and RARGMF input of Hv. (G) Velocity of the vortex swarm of CAR-NK microrobots upon the migration velocity of robotic arm. (H and I) Schematic (H) and experimental (I) exhibiting the large area spread of ribbon swarm of CAR-NK microrobots upon RARGMF input of Hr. Scale bar, 1 cm.

Time-lapse images in Fig. 5B (captured from movie S7) show that ∼5 × 106 CAR-NK microrobots exhibited minimal displacement during the first actuation with RAGMF. Subsequently, the displaced portion of the microrobots gradually dissipates along the direction of magnetic attraction exerted by RAGMF, resulting in only a small fraction of CAR-NK microrobots reaching the target site even after two rounds of RAGMF actuation. Gray value analysis of the corresponding images indicates that a small portion of CAR-NK microrobots move during the first RAGMF actuation, whereas negligible displacement is observed after the second actuation (Fig. 5C). In contrast, under RARGMF with Hv input (frequency: 10 Hz), a relatively large portion of CAR-NK microrobots first formed aggregates with a size of ∼0.8 mm within ∼2 s, and these aggregates then migrate synchronously with the robotic arm at a velocity of 0.1 mm/s (Fig. 5D). Subsequently, the RARGMF is maneuvered back to the initial position, and the magnetic actuation is repeated for the second time. Most CAR-NK microrobots remaining at the initial position re-form aggregates and move toward the target site, with almost all microrobots successfully arriving. Notably, the aggregates formed during the first RARGMF actuation migrate continuously and fuse with the formed aggregates when the RARGMF approaches the target site. Corresponding gray value analysis demonstrates that CAR-NK microrobots under Hv magnetic input traveled along a long directional trajectory through the channel of the model spinal canal (Fig. 5E). Further quantitative analysis reveals that ∼45.1% of the microrobots reach the destination after the first Hv-based RARGMF actuation, and this proportion increases to ∼83.0% following the second actuation (Fig. 5F). It is noteworthy that the relatively low arrival rate at 25 min is attributed to the gentle actuation force when the RARGMF is positioned between the two vortex swarms during the second actuation. In addition, the swarm movement of CAR-NK microrobots is consistent with the RARGMF manipulation when the actuation velocity was below 0.5 mm/s (Fig. 5G).

In addition to long-distance navigation, therapeutic-scale CAR-NK microrobots can also achieve large-area spreading upon Hr magnetic field actuation via RARGMF (Fig. 5H). As shown in Fig. 5I (captured from movie S8), ∼5 × 106 CAR-NK microrobots are placed on a 3.5-cm-diameter petri dish with a 2-mm-diameter glass slide. Under Hr magnetic field actuation using RARGMF, the microrobots spread along the glass slide, covering an area of ∼12.5 mm2 within 70 s. These results confirm the programmable magnetic navigation capability of therapeutic-scale CAR-NK microrobots.

Intrathecal navigation of CAR-NK microrobots to leptomeningeal carcinomatosis in vivo

Given that intrathecal administration is a conventional therapeutic approach for metastatic carcinomatosis, which minimizes drug-induced toxicity to other tissues, a navigation strategy is designed wherein CAR-NK microrobots are delivered via intrathecal injection to target metastatic leptomeningeal carcinomatosis, aiming to evaluate their targeting capability. The experimental procedures include (i) intrathecal injection of CAR-NK microrobots at the intervertebral space between the fifth and sixth lumbar vertebrae (L5 to L6), (ii) formation and migration of vortex swarms toward the brain under RARGMF actuation, and (iii) subsequent large-area spreading of CAR-NK microrobots within the leptomeningeal carcinomatosis lesions by switching to the Hr magnetic field input. To visualize the in vivo dynamics of CAR-NK microrobot swarms, 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide (DiR; a near-infrared fluorescent carbocyanine dye for in vivo fluorescence imaging) is used to label the CAR-NK microrobots, whereas d-luciferin potassium salt is administered to visualize metastatic leptomeningeal carcinomatosis via bioluminescence. An in vivo imaging system (IVIS) is used to monitor the distribution of brain tumor sites and CAR-NK microrobots in mice. For quantitative analysis of the dynamic distribution of CAR-NK microrobots, the spinal canal and brain are divided into the following anatomical segments: lumbar vertebrae (L1 to L5), thoracic vertebrae (T6 to T12), T1 to T5, cervical vertebrae (C5 to C8), C1 to C4, and the brain. Magnetic navigation using RARGMF is performed at 5, 10, 20, and 40 min postinjection.

As shown in Fig. 6A, the fluorescence signal of passive CAR-NK microrobots (without magnetic actuation) exhibits minimal diffusion over 40 min. By comparison, CAR-NK microrobots under Hv magnetic field actuation display a continuous fluorescence signal along the spinal canal, with only a slight decrease during migration over 40 min, and successfully reach the brain region. Further quantitative analysis (Fig. 6B) reveals that 69.5% of passive CAR-NK microrobots remain in the L1 to L5 lumbar segment 40 min after intrathecal administration, whereas only 4.2% reach the brain. In contrast, magnetically navigated CAR-NK microrobots achieve 7.3% brain accumulation within 10 min, and this proportion subsequently increases to 25.3% after 40 min of magnetic navigation. Moreover, following their arrival in the brain, the CAR-NK microrobots are further navigated to spread into metastatic leptomeningeal carcinomatosis lesions by switching to the Hr magnetic field input. To further verify their targeting capability toward metastatic leptomeningeal carcinomatosis, three-dimensional (3D)–reconstructed IVIS imaging is performed, and the overlay of the bioluminescence signal from tumors and the fluorescence signal from CAR-NK microrobots is analyzed to evaluate targeting efficacy. As shown in Fig. 6C, the yellow-red fluorescence signals (representing passive CAR-NK microrobots) are mainly localized at the intrathecal injection site in the spinal canal, showing minimal overlap with the bioluminescence signal of metastatic leptomeningeal carcinomatosis. In contrast, after reaching the brain under RARGMF (Hv input), CAR-NK microrobots are further spread into metastatic leptomeningeal carcinomatosis lesions by switching to Hr magnetic field actuation (Fig. 6D).

Those CAR-NK microrobots that reached the brain display a widespread distribution following further actuation with RARGMF (Hr input; right panels of Fig. 6, E and F). Moreover, signals from metastatic leptomeningeal carcinomatosis and CAR-NK microrobots at different depths are extracted and 3D reconstructed, as shown in Fig. 6G. The 3D overlay between magnetically navigated CAR-NK microrobots (Hv input) and metastatic leptomeningeal carcinomatosis confirms their targeting capability and indicates that the overlapping signals at the tumor-bearing brain region support spatial colocalization of CAR-NK microrobots with metastatic leptomeningeal carcinomatosis at the IVIS/ROI scale, consistent with the chemotactic penetration observed in Fig. 2 (G and I). Furthermore, their overlapping distribution is notably enhanced by switching to the Hr magnetic field input (Fig. 6H). Statistical analysis reveals that only 0.53% of passive CAR-NK microrobots overlap with the tumors, whereas the targeting efficiency of CAR-NK microrobots under Hv magnetic navigation is elevated to 15.6%, and this proportion further increases to 24.6% following the switch to Hr magnetic input. Together, magnetic intrathecal navigation of CAR-NK microrobots in vortex swarms enhances their accumulation in the brain, and subsequent switching to ribbon swarms via Hr magnetic input substantially improves their targeted penetration into tumors. Therefore, the strategy with RARGMF, which first uses Hv input toward brain and further change to Hr input, was used to intrathecally navigate the CAR-NK microrobots for further therapeutic investigation of leptomeningeal carcinomatosis therapy in vivo.

Therapeutic efficacy of CAR-NK microrobots against leptomeningeal carcinomatosis in vivo

After evaluating the targeted navigation toward leptomeningeal carcinomatosis, the in vivo therapeutic potential of CAR-NK microrobots is assessed using mice bearing metastatic meningeal carcinomatosis using nude mice (Fig. 7). Six experimental groups are established including saline as control, NK microrobots with nonactuation (noted as NK microrobot-NA), NK microrobots upon RARGMF (Hv input and then Hr input, which was noted as NK microrobot), CAR-NK cells for conventional CAR-NK cell therapy (noted as CAR-NK cell), CAR-NK microrobots with nonactuation (noted as CAR-NK microrobot-NA), and CAR-NK microrobots upon RARGMF (Hv input and then Hr input, which was noted as CAR-NK microrobot). To establish the leptomeningeal carcinomatosis model, mice were intracranially implanted with 3 × 105 SK-OV-3 cells to induce leptomeningeal carcinomatosis, and treatments are initiated at day 6 and day 8 post-tumor establishment (normalized brain bioluminescence signal ∼107). All groups receive systemic IL-2 and IL-15 cytokine support to ensure cell survival, with IL-15 supplementation standardized across groups to control for genetic variations in cytokine production. Bioluminescence imaging (luciferase) in Fig. 7A reveal that the tumors in saline group grew from day 5 to day 7, and all the mice died after day 11. Similarly, tumors in NK microrobot-NA group began to grow from day 5, whereas the mice began to die since day 9 and a subset survived until day 13, suggesting that NK microrobot therapy may not inhibit the growth of tumor but extend the survival period. The treatment of NK microrobot further extended the survival of the leptomeningeal carcinomatosis-bearing mice and all of them survived beyond day 13. By comparison, the death of the mice in CAR-NK cell group occurred on day 13, and the mice in CAR-NK microrobot-NA group survived on day 13, but also the growth of the tumors in CAR-NK cell group were confined. Among different groups, the tumors upon the treatment of CAR-NK microrobots were obviously inhibited, and all leptomeningeal carcinomatosis-bearing mice survived after day 13, indicating the notable therapeutic performance in inhibition of leptomeningeal carcinomatosis.

Comparative analysis in Fig. 7B showed that even magnetically actuated non-CAR NK microrobots exerted therapeutic effects, outperforming conventional CAR-NK cell treatment, highlighting the critical role of magnetic propulsion in enhancing tumor accumulation. The CAR-NK microrobot group achieves the most profound tumor inhibition, with absolute signal intensities remaining below 1 × 105 and decreasing to 28.4% of peak values. In contrast, the saline group shows an ∼9.8-fold increase in tumor signal from day 5 to day 9, underscoring the aggressiveness of the tumor model and the necessity of active targeting. Moreover, to study the localization of CAR-NK microrobots in leptomeningeal carcinomatosis in vivo, intrathecal navigation of Invitrogen PE-Cyanine5 (Cy5)–labeled CAR-NK microrobots were conducted. Histological analysis of the serial brain sections using immunofluorescence staining in fig. S3 exhibited minor fluorescence from the Cy5 channel in the control group (intrathecal administration of saline) located in leptomeningeal carcinomatosis, which was stained with HER2 antibody. The fluorescence signal from the mice in NK microrobot-NA was higher than that in the control group. NK microrobot, CAR-NK cell, and CAR-NK microrobot-NA achieved moderate fluorescence signal in tumor. Notably, the Cy5 fluorescence signal in leptomeningeal carcinomatosis in the CAR-NK microrobot group showed the most performance in tumor penetration. Survival curves in Fig. 7C demonstrate a median survival time of 7 days for saline-treated mice, which is substantially shorter than that of all cell-based treatment groups. The magnetically actuated CAR-NK microrobot group exhibits the longest survival, with no mice succumbing to tumor progression within the observation period (up to 40 days; approaching the median life span of naive mice).

It should be noted that the whole CAR-NK microrobots were developed in human-derived cells, which could be obtained through the expansion from NK cells from patients or obtained by differentiation of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) (fig. S4). Therefore, the HER2-positve metastatic leptomeningeal carcinomatosis model were constructed using immunodeficient mice, leading to the absence of immune effect upon the treatment of CAR-NK microrobots. Therefore, the immunotherapy effect of CAR-NK microrobots were additionally investigated with tumor cells in vitro. To this end, the tumor cells were cultivated at the bottom chamber in a 96-well plate, and then the CAR-NK microrobots were added at the upper chamber, followed by the magnetic actuation toward the tumor cell sheets. The cytotoxic function of CAR-NK microrobots toward tumor cells was initially studied. As shown in fig. S5, CAR-NK microrobots upon magnetic actuation for 24 hours still maintain considerable cytotoxic effect compared with that without magnetic actuation, suggesting that CAR-NK microrobots retained their cytotoxic function after prolonged magnetic navigation. Subsequently, the 3D multicellular tumor spheres (3D-MTSs) were constructed to further evaluate the deep penetration ability of the CAR-NK microrobots within the dense collagen fibers of tumor tissues. In the 3D-MTS model, it was obvious from the optical microscope images that the CAR-NK microrobots group showed optimal performance of infiltration into tumor tissues (fig. S6). At last, the cytokines [interferon-γ (IFN-γ), perforin, and granzyme B] in the tumor microenvironment were also tested. Compared with CAR-NK microrobots with nonactuation, the perforin, IFN-γ, and granzyme B were notably elevated in the tumor microenvironment in 24 hours (934.97 pg/ml versus 2880.47 pg/ml in IFN-γ, 366.23 pg/ml versus 896.16 pg/ml in perforin, and 318.39 pg/ml versus 924.43 pg/ml in granzyme B) (fig. S7). In addition, the therapeutic efficacy of CAR-NK microrobots against metastatic leptomeningeal carcinomatosis in the murine model was also verified using NOD/ShiLtJGpt-Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22/Gpt (NCG) mice (fig. S8), showing the similar results as that in Fig. 7.

Last, a 3-month-scale safety estimation studies were carried out by the histological analysis and biomarker tests. Negligible pathological abnormalities or inflamed cells in the brain, heart, liver, spleen, lung, or kidney in all groups were observed (fig. S9). Minor distinguish difference of ALT, AST, UREA, and CREA in all groups compared with saline group indicate the optimal safety of CAR-NK microrobots (fig. S10). The body weights of mice in all groups exhibit similar tendency in 3 months (fig. S11). These results confirm that magnetic actuation and swarm control substantially enhance the therapeutic efficacy of CAR-NK cells, overcoming the limitations of passive delivery and achieving durable tumor suppression in a clinically relevant metastatic cancer model.

Application verification of CAR-NK microrobots in a nonhuman primate model in vivo

To better meet clinical application requirements, CAR-NK microrobots are used and evaluated in nonhuman primate models. For this purpose, an experimental setup is integrated with DSA imaging, the RARGMF system, and an anesthesia device enabling precise control and monitoring of CAR-NK microrobots (Fig. 8, A and B). Intrathecal injection of CAR-NK microrobots directly into the spinal canal is selected as the administration route, which is critical for targeting central nervous system (CNS) lesions. It should be noted that the intrathecal injection site is L6 to L7 in a nonhuman primate model to access the CSF-containing spinal canal while preserving safety as its anatomical location avoids the terminal end of the spinal cord, and the surrounding cauda equina can be avoided, providing ample operating space. The difference of vertebral level between cynomolgus monkeys and humans results in the different injection site, which is L4 to L5 in human. The DSA imaging results indicate that CAR-NK microrobots likely undergo adaptive swarm deformation to traverse the limited space between the spinal canal wall and the conus medullaris (fig. S12). Notably, because of the limited observation field of DSA imaging, it is challenging to visualize the subsequent migration of the CAR-NK microrobots. RARGMF (Hv mode) actuation is continued toward the brain, followed by switching to RARGMF (Hr mode). DSA imaging of the brain region reveals a dynamic signal change within 50 s of Hr mode actuation, with the signal-enhanced area covering ∼2 mm2 (Fig. 8C), indicating the successful transformation of CAR-NK microrobots into ribbon swarms in the brain of nonhuman primates in vivo. Furthermore, no noticeable signs of nerve damage (e.g., tremors or balance impairments) are observed, verifying the biocompatibility of CAR-NK microrobots and supporting their potential for clinical translation.

DISCUSSION

Four key challenges must be overcome to translate CAR-equipped immune cells into effective microrobotic medicines in clinical practice: (i) therapeutic-scale swarm control in vivo, (ii) advanced techniques for magnetizing cells to confer a magnetic moment, (iii) long-range migration toward targets and large-area dispersion to cover metastatic leptomeningeal tumors, and (iv) the “mouse-to-human” size gap. To address these issues, this study introduces a transformative approach by engineering CAR-NK cells into magnetically actuated biohybrid microrobots, overcoming critical limitations of traditional immune cell therapy. Through comparative analysis of different fabrication methods, electroporation-based magnetic functionalization circumvents the nonphagocytic nature of NK cells, achieving a fivefold increase in saturation magnetization compared to surface-based approaches. Furthermore, unlike macrophages and neutrophils, T cells and NK cells are suspension cells, resulting in limited isotropic fluid generation for propulsion under uniform-intensity RMFs, a constraint not encountered in previously reported macrophage-based or neutrophil-based microrobots. To resolve this, the study introduces an RGMF, which drives the rotation of CAR-NK microrobots while anchoring them to the substrate to amplify isotropic fluid flow for enhanced propulsion. These breakthroughs enable robust magnetic propulsion, a prerequisite for large-scale swarm control. A robotic arm–assisted RGMF system further enhances mobility performance, facilitating centimeter-scale migration of a substantial cell population (104 to 106 cells) and dynamic swarm reconfiguration, from vortex swarms optimized for long-distance travel to ribbon swarms designed for improved lesion coverage, resulting in a notable enhancement in target engagement efficiency.

Unlike humans, the region corresponding to the seventh lumbar vertebra (L7) in nonhuman primates primarily contains the conus medullaris rather than the cauda equina; this area is surrounded by dense connective tissue and CSF. The absence of noticeable nerve damage not only confirms the safety of the CAR-NK microrobots but also indicates that the injection was administered into the spinal fluid (rather than the conus medullaris). Had the injection targeted the conus medullaris, obvious nerve damage symptoms such as urinary incontinence would have occurred. DSA images of CAR-NK microrobots in nonhuman primates reveal a band-like distribution within the spinal cord when a magnetic field of intensity Hv is applied, in contrast to the vortex-like pattern observed in an open (in vitro) environment. These results demonstrate that the movement of CAR-NK microrobots within the spine is primarily mediated by spinal fluid. These nonhuman primate experiments validate the anatomical translatability of the CAR-NK microrobot system, representing a critical step toward clinical application. Furthermore, the versatility of the CAR-NK microrobot platform, enabled by customizable CAR structures, extends beyond oncology to potential therapeutic use in diseases requiring precise cell delivery, such as neurodegenerative disorders or autoimmune diseases.

However, several challenges remain to be addressed. First, the long-term biocompatibility of magnetic NPs requires systematic evaluation, despite the retention of short-term cell viability observed in current studies. In addition, optimizing magnetic field gradients to accommodate the anatomical complexity specific to humans is essential for translating this technology to clinical settings, human spinal and cranial structures exhibit greater variability compared to nonhuman primates, demanding tailored magnetic actuation strategies. Future research directions should focus on integrating real-time imaging modalities (e.g., DSA or magnetic resonance imaging) for closed-loop control, enabling dynamic adjustment of magnetic fields based on the microrobots’ in vivo position and thereby improving targeting precision. Furthermore, combining the CAR-NK microrobot platform with gene-editing technologies (such as CRISPR-Cas9) may endow these cells with additional functionalities, for instance, enhanced resistance to tumor microenvironment immunosuppression or targeted secretion of therapeutic cytokines, thereby expanding the application horizons of biohybrid microrobotics. In essence, this study establishes a critical bridge between immunotherapy and robotic engineering, offering a robust framework for developing adaptive, targeted therapies in complex biological landscapes (e.g., the CNS or metastatic tumor niches). This work marks a pivotal advance in modular, reconfigurable biohybrid systems, laying the groundwork for next-generation cell-based therapeutics that combine the specificity of immune cells with the controllability of microrobots.

Recombinant Human Stromal-Cell Derived Factor-1α (SDF-1α, CXCL12) was purchased from PeproTech Inc. Serum-free rapid freezing solution was acquired from Biosharp Co. Ltd.

Expansion of NK cells

Fresh human blood was collected from a healthy donor (Z.W.), and NK cell selective amplification was performed using the Human NK Cell Robust Expansion Kit (Fujian StemEry Bio Tech Co. Ltd.). In accordance with the expansion kit’s standard procedure, recombinant human IL-2 was continuously supplemented during the amplification process. After 11 to 13 days of cultivation, the number of NK cells was approximately expanded by 105-fold. The amplified NK cells were then enriched using the ImunoSep Human NK Cell Enrichment Kit (Precision BioMedicals Co. Ltd.). Subsequently, the enriched NK cells were resuspended in serum-free rapid freezing solution, packaged, and stored in an ultralow temperature freezer for subsequent experiments. The usage of human blood sample was approved by the Experimental Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Harbin Institute of Technology (approval number: IACUC-2024315).

Synthesis of magnetic Fe3O4 NPs

First, 0.86 g of FeCl2 and 1.40 g of FeCl3 were dissolved in 40 ml of deionized water (H2O). The resulting solution was heated to 80°C, and 5 ml of ammonium hydroxide solution was added dropwise to the mixture. With continuous stirring for 30 min, the color of the solution gradually turned black (indicating the formation of magnetic NPs). The black suspension (containing magnetic NPs) was then collected using an external magnet and washed three times with degassed ultrapure water to remove residual reactants. After washing, the NPs were redispersed into 150 ml of degassed water. Next, 2 ml of a trisodium citrate solution (0.5 mg/ml) was added to this dispersion, and the mixture was heated to 95°C for an additional 90 min (to modify the NP surface with citrate). Last, the black dispersion was washed twice via magnetic separation to purify the citrate-modified magnetic NPs. The purified NP dispersion was then set aside for subsequent fabrication of CAR-NK microrobots.

Preparation of CAR-NK cells

For the genetic engineering of CAR-NK cells, an SB transposon/transposase-based technique was used, wherein the target genes (encoding the CAR and associated functional elements) were loaded into low-molecular-weight DNA plasmids (fig. S1). Luria-Bertani (LB) broth medium was used to culture the engineered E. coli strains harboring the corresponding SB plasmids. After 24 hours of cultivation, the target plasmids were extracted from the bacterial cultures using an EndoFree Maxi Plasmid Kit [Tiangen Biotech (Beijing) Co. Ltd.]. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were then determined using a Thermo Fisher Scientific NanoDrop microvolume spectrophotometer. Subsequently, the purified plasmids were introduced into the expanded NK cells via electroporation, using the Cell Electroporation System X-Porator H1 (Etta Biotech Co. Ltd., Suzhou), consistent with the instrumentation described in prior experimental sections.

Fabrication of CAR-NK microrobots

Three methods were used to load magnetic NPs into CAR-NK cells: For the cocultivation method, CAR-NK cells (∼107 cells) were incubated with magnetic Fe3O4 NPs (1 μg/ml) for 24 hours under standard cell culture conditions (37°C, 5% CO2) to enable passive uptake of NPs; for the streptavidin-biotin–mediated surface chemistry method, CAR-NK cells were first biotinylated by incubating with BiotinSE (biotinamidohexanoic acid N-hydroxysuccinimide ester) for 30 min at room temperature, after which excess BiotinSE was removed to terminate the reaction by replacing the solution with 10 ml of fresh cell culture medium [subsequently discarded and replaced with 5 ml of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for rinsing], then the PBS was replaced with 5 ml of EDTA solution and the biotinylated cells were incubated at 37°C for 15 min to facilitate detachment from the culture vessel, and last the detached biotinylated cells were mixed with streptavidin-conjugated Fe3O4 NPs, vortexed for 15 s at room temperature to promote streptavidin-biotin binding, and the resulting CAR-NK microrobots were collected and washed twice with PBS to remove unbound NPs; for the electroporation method, CAR-NK cells (2 × 107 cells) were resuspended in EBEL transfection buffer (Etta Biotech) alongside magnetic Fe3O4 NPs, CAR-anti-HER2 SB plasmids, and SB100X mRNA, the mixture was transferred to an electrode cuvette (Etta Biotech), and electroporation was performed using a program of four pulses at 200 V (100-ms pulse duration, 700-ms interval between pulses), followed by removal of redundant (unloaded) magnetic NPs via discontinuous density gradient centrifugation (using 43% Percoll and 100% Percoll solutions) to purify the CAR-NK microrobots.

Characterization of CAR-NK microrobots

SEM was conducted using a Zeiss Merlin Compact field-emission scanning electron microscope, whereas TEM was performed with a Hitachi H-7650 transmission electron microscope. Hysteresis loop data were analyzed using a Lakeshore 7404 vibration sample magnetometer from Lakeshore Cryotronics Inc. Cell viability assessment and cell counting were carried out using a C100-SE automatic cell counter (RWD Life Science Co. Ltd.). In addition, digital stereotaxic instruments paired with an R500 universal small animal anesthesia machine were also purchased from RWD Life Science Co. Ltd. The Cell Electroporation System X-Porator H1 comprising an electroporator, electrode cuvettes, and a docking station was acquired from Etta Biotech Co. Ltd. (Suzhou). An MA-1520 magnetizer (Shenzhen Jiuju Industrial Equipment Co. Ltd.) was used to magnetize the CAR-NK microrobots. Magnetic field strength was measured using a TLE493DW2B6MS2GOTOBO1 3D magnetic sensor and its corresponding software, both supplied by Infineon Technologies AG.

Magnetic actuation and RARGMF operation

For actuation of individual CAR-NK microrobot, GMF, RMF, and RGMF inputs were compared using the same optical tracking setup. RGMF actuation was tested over 1 to 20 Hz for movement of individual microrobot. For swarm actuation, Hv input was used to form compact vortex swarms for long-distance migration, whereas Hr input was used to form ribbonlike swarms for large-area spreading; the respective frequency ranges tested were 5 to 25 Hz for Hv-induced vortex swarms and 1 to 15 Hz for Hr-induced ribbon swarms. In long-distance therapeutic-scale assays, Hv-RARGMF was applied at 10 Hz and the robotic arm was translated at 0.1 mm/s unless otherwise noted.

Chemotactic motion

A μ-slide Chemotaxis 3D (Ibidi) was used to establish a chemokine gradient in accordance with the manufacturer’s standard operating procedure (SOP). For this setup, CXCL12 (stromal cell–derived factor-1α) and G422 tumor cells were used as chemokine sources. The chemotactic motion of the CAR-NK microrobots was observed and recorded using an inverted optical microscope (Olympus, model IX73).

Establishment of leptomeningeal carcinomatosis mice model in vivo

Nude mice were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Co. Ltd., NCG mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co. Ltd., and SK-OV-3-GFP-luc cells were obtained from Shanghai Aoyin Biotechnology Co. Ltd. To establish the leptomeningeal carcinomatosis model, 3 × 105 SK-OV-3-GFP-luc cells were intracranially injected into the pia mater region of the mice’s brains with the aid of a stereotaxic apparatus. The precise injection coordinates were set as follows: 2.5 mm posterior to the bregma, 2.1 mm left of the midline, and at a depth of 2.6 mm. Leptomeningeal carcinomatosis was confirmed to form in the mice’s brains more than 3 to 5 days after cell injection. For in vivo tumor luminescence imaging, d-luciferin potassium salt was dissolved in PBS to a final concentration of 15 mg/ml. A 0.2-ml aliquot of this luciferin solution was injected into each leptomeningeal carcinomatosis-bearing mouse. The IVIS Spectrum system (PerkinElmer Inc.) was then used to visualize both the luminescence (from SK-OV-3-GFP-luc cells) and fluorescence of the tumor-bearing mice. For 3D fluorescence imaging of CAR-NK microrobots, 3 × 106 DiR-labeled CAR-NK microrobots were injected into the CSF cavity via the lumbar spine, and 3D images were acquired and recorded using the IVIS Spectrum system.

Tumor immunotherapy in mice model in vivo

At 6 and 8 days after the intracranial injection of SK-OV-3 tumor cells, 3 × 106 cells of each type [NK microrobots without magnetic navigation (NK microrobot-NA group), NK microrobots with magnetic navigation (NK microrobot group), CAR-NK cells (CAR-NK group), CAR-NK microrobots without magnetic navigation (CAR-NK microrobot-NA group), and CAR-NK microrobots with magnetic navigation (CAR-NK microrobot group)] were sequentially injected into the CSF cavity via the lumbar spine. Following these cell treatments, the mice received subsequent cytokine administration: Recombinant human IL-2 (2 × 104 IU per mouse) was injected every 2 days, whereas recombinant human IL-15 (30 ng per mouse) was injected daily, with both administrations continuing from day 0 to day 10 post–cell treatment. All mouse model experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. In addition, the animal experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Harbin Institute of Technology (approval number: IACUC-2024114).

Nonhuman primate model experiments

Healthy cynomolgus monkeys were provided by and housed at Lingfu Biosciences Co. Ltd. During the experiment, the cynomolgus monkeys were anesthetized using isoflurane (induction concentration: 5%, maintenance concentration: 1.5 to 2.5%) to ensure painless and hemodynamically stable conditions throughout the procedure. First, the lumbar vertebrae (L3 to L7) were identified via manual palpation and anatomical landmarking. Under strict aseptic conditions, a suspension of magnetically functionalized CAR-NK microrobots was administered into the spinal canal via intrathecal injection. The injection volume and cell count both optimized through preexperimental safety and efficacy evaluations were carefully controlled to preserve the biological activity of the microrobots and ensure procedural safety. DSA imaging was performed using an Optima IGS 330 system (GE Healthcare). DSA imaging was used to provide precise anatomical localization of the injection site and spinal structures (74), whereas DSA enabled real-time visualization of the CAR-NK microrobots’ dynamic movement and distribution within the spinal canal.

An RARGMF system was used to apply controlled magnetic fields to the cynomolgus monkeys; this system enabled precise modulation of magnetic field direction, intensity, and rotation frequency. For DSA imaging, the magnetic field was turned off to clearly visualize the behavior of the CAR-NK microrobots without magnetic interference. DSA image sequences were recorded continuously to track the dynamic movement and spatial distribution of the CAR-NK microrobots within the spinal canal. Upon activation of RARGMF (Hv mode) was actuated after the intrathecal injection of CAR-NK microrobots, followed by the manipulation of the robotic arm along the spine of the cynomolgus monkeys. Before imaging the intracranial dynamics of CAR-NK microrobots upon RARGMF (Hr mode), additional CAR-NK microrobots were injected into the brain for better visualization. All nonhuman primate experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. In addition, the experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Lingfu Biosciences Co. Ltd. (approval number: LFSW-DWSY-20250321-01).
