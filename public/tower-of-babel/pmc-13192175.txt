Influencing factors and predictive model construction of perioperative blood glucose fluctuations in patients with coronary heart disease complicated with type 2 diabetes mellitus undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention
Authors: Xuemei Zhao, Yi Wen, Mingxia Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe study aimed to explore key influencing factors of perioperative blood glucose fluctuations in patients with coronary heart disease (CHD) complicated with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), and construct/validate a clinical risk prediction model for precise blood glucose management.MethodsA retrospective cohort study enrolled 457 eligible patients (282 in the blood glucose fluctuation group, 175 in the normal group; defined by SDBG ≥ 2.0 mmol/L, PPGE ≥ 2.2 mmol/L, or LAGE ≥ 4.4 mmol/L). Patients were randomly divided into training (n = 320, 70%) and validation cohorts (n = 137, 30%). After Lasso regression dimensionality reduction, a nomogram was built via multivariate Logistic regression. Model performance was assessed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, calibration curve, Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and curve analysis (DCA); stability was verified by 1000 bootstrap samples and multiple sensitivity analyses.ResultsUnivariate analysis identified 21 associated indicators (P < 0.05), and Lasso regression optimized variables. Multivariate Logistic regression confirmed 11 independent risk factors: age, diabetes duration, SBP, HbA1c, LDL-C, HDL-C, FBG, PSQI score, surgical start time window, surgical duration, and preoperative last meal-to-surgery interval (P < 0.05). The 11-variable full variable model achieved AUCs of 0.973 (95% CI 0.958–0.988) in the training cohort and 0.982 (95% CI 0.966–0.998) in the validation cohort; DCA confirmed that the model yielded significant clinical net benefits within the risk threshold of 0–65%. The 5 high-impact predictor model constructed after excluding 6 high-influence variables still maintained high predictive performance, with AUCs of 0.868 in the training cohort and 0.880 in the validation cohort, and all variables are clinically easily accessible indicators.ConclusionThe nomogram model constructed with the 11 identified indicators in this study exhibits excellent predictive performance for perioperative blood glucose fluctuations in patients with CHD complicated with T2DM undergoing PCI. In contrast, the 5 high-impact predictor model balances predictive accuracy and clinical convenience. These two models can be respectively applied to refined risk stratification of inpatients and rapid preoperative screening in primary care settings/outpatient clinics, providing a practical predictive tool for clinical individualized management of perioperative blood glucose and a scientific basis for reducing the risk of perioperative adverse cardiovascular events.Keywords: Blood glucose fluctuation, Coronary heart disease, Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Percutaneous coronary intervention, Nomogram

Introduction

Data from the International Diabetes Federation show that the worldwide prevalence of diabetes in adults aged 20–79 years stood at 537 million in 2021, and is anticipated to rise to 783 million by 2045 [1]. Coronary heart disease (CHD) is the most prevalent cardiovascular complication of type 2 diabetes (T2DM), accounting for 65% of deaths in diabetic patients [2]. Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is one of the main minimally invasive treatments for coronary heart disease. However, patients with CHD complicated with T2DM are prone to severe blood glucose fluctuations during the perioperative period of PCI. This phenomenon is not a simple single-point abnormality of “hyperglycemia” or “hypoglycemia”, but a dynamic metabolic disorder characterized by standard deviation of blood glucose (SDBG) ≥ 2.0 mmol/L, postprandial blood glucose excursion (PPGE) ≥ 2.2 mmol/L, or largest amplitude of glycemic excursions (LAGE) ≥ 4.4 mmol/L. It not only increases the risk of complications such as postoperative infection and in-stent restenosis, but also significantly increases the incidence of adverse cardiovascular events [3].

Currently, clinical prediction of perioperative blood glucose fluctuations during PCI mainly relies on monitoring of single indicators, which has obvious limitations: first, static indicators such as fasting blood glucose (FBG) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) can only reflect long-term or blood glucose levels at a certain time point, and cannot capture the dynamic fluctuation characteristics caused by surgical stress, dietary changes, and drug adjustments during the perioperative period [4]; second, even a single fluctuation indicator is difficult to fully cover different fluctuation patterns such as postprandial sharp rise type and fasting high fluctuation type, which is prone to risk misjudgment [5]; third, most existing studies focus on the association between a single indicator and adverse outcomes, lacking precise evaluation tools integrating multi-dimensional influencing factors, which is difficult to meet the needs of clinical individualized risk stratification [6]. This current situation makes it difficult for clinicians to identify high-risk patients in advance, and intervention measures are often in a “passive response” state, highlighting the urgency of constructing a multivariate prediction model.

The synergistic effect of chronic hyperglycemia and PCI surgical stress is the core pathophysiological basis of perioperative blood glucose fluctuations. Chronic hyperglycemia causes endothelial dysfunction, accumulation of advanced glycation end products, and oxidative stress reactions by long-term impairing islet β-cell function and reducing insulin sensitivity, laying a “pathological foreshadowing” for blood glucose fluctuations [7]; as an invasive procedure, PCI activates the sympathetic nervous system through traumatic stimulation, leading to the massive release of stress hormones such as cortisol and glucagon. These hormones promote glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis on the one hand, and antagonize the hypoglycemic effect of insulin on the other hand [8]. Most importantly, in a state of chronic hyperglycemia, patients’ glucose regulatory reserve capacity has significantly decreased. When facing acute stress caused by PCI surgery, they are unable to effectively compensate for the metabolic impact brought about by hormonal changes, ultimately leading to an amplified amplitude of blood glucose fluctuations. This further exacerbates myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury and the progression of coronary artery disease [9].

Coronary angiography, as the gold standard for the diagnosis of coronary heart disease, can clarify the degree of vascular lesions, but it is an invasive procedure and cannot be used for preoperative risk screening [6]. As an intuitive visual prediction tool, nomograms can convert the complex mathematical relationships of multivariate regression models into clinically operable scoring systems, and have been widely applied in risk prediction of various diseases [10]. Based on the above research gaps and mechanism cognition, this study aims to: ① accurately define perioperative blood glucose fluctuations with composite indicators; ② systematically screen multi-dimensional independent risk factors such as age, metabolic indicators, sleep quality, and surgery-related factors; ③ construct and validate a nomogram prediction model to achieve precise early warning of perioperative blood glucose fluctuations in CHD patients complicated with T2DM undergoing PCI, and provide scientific basis for clinical development of individualized intervention strategies and optimization of perioperative management.

Materials and methods

Sample size calculation

Based on the results of the pilot study, the incidence of perioperative blood glucose fluctuations was approximately 60%, and the independent variables included in the model had a moderate effect size of OR = 1.5 on the outcome. PASS 15.0 software was used to verify the sample size with the calculation formula for Logistic regression: with a two-tailed test level of α = 0.05 and a test power (1-β) = 0.90, the calculated minimum total sample size required was 328 cases. A total of 457 patients were finally enrolled in this study, which exceeded the calculated minimum sample size and ensured sufficient statistical power.

Study population

A retrospective cohort study design was adopted, consecutively enrolling 508 patients with CHD and T2DM who underwent PCI in our hospital from January 2023 to August 2025. A total of 51 patients were excluded (23 with incomplete clinical data, 12 ineligible for inclusion criteria, and 16 meeting exclusion criteria), yielding a final sample of 457 participants. Using SPSS 27.0 to generate a random number table, patients were simply randomly divided into a training set (n = 320) and a validation set (n = 137) at a ratio of 7:3 (Fig. 1). The training cohort was used to screen independent influencing factors and construct the nomogram model, while the validation cohort was employed for internal validation of model performance. The randomization process was completed by an independent statistician without stratification adjustment. There were no significant differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups (all P > 0.05), and potential confounding factors were balanced.

Flow chart

Inclusion criteria

(1) Age ≥ 18 years; (2) Meeting the diagnostic criteria for CHD (coronary angiography confirming stenosis ≥ 50% in at least one coronary artery) [11]; (3) Meeting the diagnostic criteria for T2DM (fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, 2-hour postprandial blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or previous diagnosis of T2DM with hypoglycemic treatment) [12]; (4) Elective PCI without preoperative complications such as acute infection or severe liver and kidney dysfunction.

Exclusion criteria

(1) Patients with type 1 or special-type diabetes; (2) Patients undergoing emergency PCI; (3) Patients with malignant tumors, autoimmune diseases, hematological diseases, or severe mental disorders; (4) Incomplete clinical data.

Approval for this study was granted by the Medical Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Ethics Approval No.: 2025nianshen (2780)), and informed consent of patients was waived.

Data collection

The following relevant data of patients were collected: (1) Demographic characteristics: gender, age, educational level, household registration, marital status; (2) Medical history: duration of diabetes, duration of hypertension, history of CHD, smoking history, drinking history; (3) Physical examination indicators: body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP); (4) Laboratory test indicators: HbA1c, triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), fasting blood glucose (FBG); (5) Lifestyle and scale scores: changes in perioperative food intake [13] (All patients received meals uniformly provided by the hospital, with each meal formulated by professional dietitians. Uniformly trained researchers recorded the type, intake, and eating time of food for patients 1 day before surgery, on the day of surgery, and 3 days after surgery, combined with the patients’ self-reported average daily intake 1 month before surgery, it was defined as: ① Decrease: the average perioperative intake decreased by ≥ 10% compared with daily intake; ② No change: fluctuated within ± 10%; ③ Increase: increased by ≥ 10%. The consistency coefficient Kappa = 0.86 was verified by pre-experiment), perioperative exercise time (≤ 30 min/d or > 30 min/d), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [14] (total score 0–21, higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality), Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) [15] for perioperative pain assessment (0–10, higher scores indicating more severe pain), and Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HAD) [16] for emotional state assessment (total score 0–21, score ≥ 8 indicating anxiety/depression tendency); (6) Surgery-related indicators: hypoglycemic regimen (following the “Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus in China (2022 Edition)“ [16], insulin-treated patients adopted the “basal + preprandial bolus” regimen, oral drug patients discontinued sulfonylureas 24 h before surgery and switched to temporary insulin replacement; after surgery, the dose was adjusted according to blood glucose monitoring results: additional short-acting insulin 2–4 U was given when blood glucose > 10.0 mmol/L, glucose was supplemented and insulin dose was reduced when blood glucose < 3.9 mmol/L. All adjustments were implemented by attending physicians according to uniform standards and detailed records were kept), surgical start time window, surgical duration, time from preoperative last meal to surgery start, time from surgery end to meal intake, and stent/balloon implantation status; (7) Analgesia and sedation-related data: perioperative analgesic regimen (recorded by NRS score), anxiety state (HAD score), sedative drug use (such as midazolam).

The total missing rate in this study was < 3%, among which the missing rate of laboratory indicators was < 1%. Multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was used for 5 imputations and the mean value was taken; there was no missing scale score (questionnaires were checked immediately after completion, missing items were supplemented on the spot when < 5%, and excluded when missing > 20%). Little’s Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) test (χ2 = 18.36, P = 0.352) indicated that the missing data were completely randomly missing, and the imputation method was reasonable.

Definition of blood glucose fluctuation

Taking the day before the confirmed surgery as the study starting point, 7-point blood glucose monitoring (before 3 meals, 2 h after 3 meals, and before bedtime) was performed by special personnel using the same blood glucose meter (Roche Accu-Chek Performa) on the day before surgery, the day of surgery, and 3 days after surgery. Raw data were recorded, and the following blood glucose fluctuation indicators were calculated [17]: SDBG (standard deviation of blood glucose), reflecting the degree of dispersion of blood glucose values from the average blood glucose (reference value < 2.0 mmol/L); PPGE (postprandial blood glucose excursion), reflecting the amplitude of postprandial blood glucose fluctuation (reference value < 2.2 mmol/L); LAGE (largest amplitude of glycemic excursion), reflecting the maximum daily blood glucose fluctuation amplitude (reference value < 4.4 mmol/L). Blood glucose fluctuation was defined as meeting any of the above criteria (SDBG ≥ 2.0 mmol/L, PPGE ≥ 2.2 mmol/L, or LAGE ≥ 4.4 mmol/L), in accordance with the provisions of the Expert Consensus on the Management of Blood Glucose Fluctuations in Patients with Diabetes Mellitus [17].

Quality control

Researchers received unified training before the study, including standardized mastery of blood glucose monitoring technology, questionnaire guidelines, and the meaning of corresponding items in the questionnaires. Informed consent was obtained from patients to avoid inaccurate or missing blood glucose records. Questionnaires were checked immediately after completion, with missing items supplemented in a timely manner. Qualified patient data were bound and stored after inspection; questionnaires with more than 20% missing items were excluded.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS 26.0 and R 4.3.1. software. Normally distributed measurement data were presented as mean ± SD and compared via independent samples t-test; non-normal data were expressed as M (P25, P75) and analyzed using Mann-Whitney U test. Count data were described as n (%) with intergroup comparisons by χ2 test (Fisher’s exact test for theoretical frequency < 5). Potential risk factors for blood glucose fluctuations (P < 0.05) were screened via univariate analysis, followed by Lasso regression for variable selection and dimensionality reduction. Selected variables were included in multivariate Logistic regression analysis (using forward stepwise selection based on maximum likelihood estimation). Based on the multivariate Logistic regression model constructed with the training cohort, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was applied to calculate the SHAP value of each independent risk factor, quantify the contribution of each variable to the model prediction result, and rank the variables in descending order of absolute SHAP value (feature importance). Model performance was evaluated by AUC, calibration curve/Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and DCA curve. The stability of the model was verified by 2 sensitivity analyses: ① repeated sampling bootstrap validation (1000 times); ② re-modeling after excluding SBP LDL-C, HDL-C, FBG, surgical start time period, and time from preoperative eating to start of surgery. Collinearity was tested by variance inflation factor (VIF) (VIF < 5 indicates no severe collinearity). The statistical significance level was set as two-sided P < 0.05. Visualization of the nomogram, ROC curve, DCA were all implemented with R 4.3.1 software based on the results of the multivariate regression model.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the training (n = 320) and validation cohorts (n = 137). The training cohort included 215 males (67.19%) and 105 females (32.81) with a mean age of (65.32 ± 10.84) years; abnormal blood glucose indicators were observed in 172 (53.75%) for SDBG ≥ 2.0 mmol/L, 158 (49.38%) for PPGE ≥ 2.2 mmol/L, and 143 (44.69%) for LAGE ≥ 4.4 mmol/L. The validation cohort comprised 91 males (66.42%) and 46 females (33.58%) with a mean age of (64.87 ± 11.25) years; corresponding abnormal indicators were noted in 70 (51.09%), 66 (48.18%), and 59 (43.07%) patients, with 86 (62.77%) having blood glucose fluctuations. Univariate analysis of perioperative blood glucose fluctuations

In the training cohort, there were 124 patients (38.75%) in the normal group and 196 patients (61.25%) in the blood glucose fluctuation group. Univariate analysis showed that compared with the normal group, patients in the blood glucose fluctuation group were older, had lower BMI, longer duration of diabetes and hypertension, and a higher proportion of primary school education or below (P < 0.05). Regarding laboratory indicators, the blood glucose fluctuation group had higher levels of SBP, HbA1c, TG, TC, LDL-C, and FBG, and lower HDL-C levels (P < 0.05). In terms of lifestyle, the blood glucose fluctuation group had a higher proportion of reduced perioperative food intake and insulin use for hypoglycemic control (P < 0.05). Scale scores indicated that the blood glucose fluctuation group had higher PSQI scores, NRS scores, and HAD scores (P < 0.05). For surgery-related indicators, the blood glucose fluctuation group had a higher proportion of surgical start time windows of 12:01–16:00 and 16:01–20:00, as well as longer surgical duration, time from preoperative last meal to surgery start, and time from surgery end to meal intake (P < 0.05) (Table 2).

Model construction

Predictor selection

Lasso regression analysis was performed on 29 indicators to identify 21 key potential predictors: age, duration of diabetes, duration of hypertension, educational level, BMI, SBP, HbA1c, TG, TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, FBG, changes in perioperative food intake, PSQI score, NRS score, HAD score, hypoglycemic regimen, surgical start time window, surgical duration, time from preoperative last meal to surgery start, and time from surgery end to meal intake (λ = 0.0165312289901841). These 21 key predictors were included in the next step of model development (Fig. 2).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis

Lasso regression-identified potential predictors were included in the multivariate Logistic regression model (Table 3). Results showed that 11 factors were independent risk factors for perioperative blood glucose fluctuations in CHD-T2DM patients undergoing PCI: age (OR 1.098, 95% CI 1.037–1.162, P = 0.001), duration of diabetes (OR 1.126, 95% CI 1.025–1.238, P = 0.014), SBP (OR 1.062, 95% CI 1.027–1.098, P < 0.01), HbA1c (OR 2.843, 95% CI 1.821–4.438, P < 0.001), LDL-C (OR 2.117, 95% CI 1.088–4.119, P = 0.027), HDL-C (OR 0.105, 95% CI 0.012–0.903, P = 0.040), FBG (OR 1.676, 95% CI 1.150–2.442, P = 0.007), PSQI score (OR 1.162, 95% CI 1.043–1.294, P = 0.006), surgical start time window (OR 2.213, 95% CI 1.041–4.705, P = 0.039), surgical duration (OR 11.265, 95% CI 3.255–38.991, P < 0.001), and time from preoperative last meal to surgery start (OR 1.131, 95% CI 1.005–1.274, P = 0.041). (Table 3). Collinearity test showed that the VIF values of all variables were < 3.5, indicating no severe collinearity.

SHAP analysis for feature importance ranking

SHAP analysis was performed on the 11 independent risk factors based on the multivariate Logistic regression model of the training cohort, and the feature importance was ranked by the mean absolute SHAP value(Fig. 3). The results showed that the contribution of each variable to the prediction of perioperative blood glucose fluctuations was significantly different: HbA1c had the highest mean absolute SHAP value (1.45), which was the most important influencing factor; followed by surgical duration (1.00), age (0.90), PSQI score (0.78), and diabetes duration (0.68). These five variables were defined as the core variables of perioperative blood glucose fluctuations. The remaining six variables with lower contribution were SBP (0.58), FBG (0.55), HDL-C (0.52), surgical start time window (0.48), LDL-C (0.35), and time from preoperative last meal to surgery start (0.32), which were defined as the Non-Critical Predictor Model variables.

Comparison of predictive performance among different models

Based on the SHAP feature ranking results, three nomogram prediction models for perioperative blood glucose fluctuation risk were constructed: the Full Variable Model (11 independent risk factors), the High-Impact Predictor Model (top 5 variables: HbA1c, surgical duration, age, PSQI score, diabetes duration), and the Non-Critical Predictor Model (remaining 6 variables: SBP, FBG, HDL-C, surgical start time window, LDL-C, time from preoperative last meal to surgery start). The predictive performance of the three models was comprehensively compared in the training and validation cohorts, including discriminative ability, calibration and clinical net benefit (Table 4; Fig. 4).

The calibration curves (Fig. 5) showed that the observed curves and calibrated curves of the High-Impact Predictor Model in both the training and validation cohorts were basically consistent and close to the ideal curve, indicating good calibration of the model and a high degree of consistency between the predicted probabilities and the actual occurrence of perioperative blood glucose fluctuations.

Decision curve analysis (DCA)

DCA of the High-Impact Predictor Model showed that within the risk threshold range of 0–65%, the curve of the prediction model constructed in this study was significantly higher than the “Treat All” and “Treat None” curves (Fig. 6), indicating that within this threshold range, risk stratification based on the model combined with targeted intervention could yield better clinical net benefits than blind intervention or no intervention at all. When the risk threshold was > 65%, the model curve gradually approached the “Treat All” curve, and the decision-making advantage diminished. In clinical practice, if the risk threshold is set at 0–65%, the model can provide effective guidance for clinical decision-making under a reasonable cost-benefit balance.

Discussion

Perioperative blood glucose fluctuations are a critical metabolic disturbance in patients with CHD) and T2DM undergoing PCI, as they exacerbate endothelial dysfunction, promote oxidative stress, and increase the risk of postoperative infections, in-stent restenosis, and adverse cardiovascular events [8, 18]. Clarifying the key drivers of perioperative blood glucose fluctuations and establishing a practical predictive tool is essential for optimizing individualized perioperative management and improving patient prognosis. This study systematically identified 11 independent risk factors for perioperative blood glucose fluctuations through rigorous statistical analysis. By comparing the predictive performance of different models to construct a nomogram model with excellent performance, providing novel insights into clinical practice.

The findings of this study highlight that age and diabetes duration are prominent non-modifiable risk factors for perioperative blood glucose fluctuations, which aligns with the pathological progression of T2DM and aging-related metabolic changes [13, 19]. With advancing age, pancreatic islet β-cell function declines progressively, and insulin sensitivity decreases, accompanied by reduced physiological reserve and increased comorbidity burden, all of which impair the body’s ability to regulate blood glucose homeostasis [20]. A longer duration of diabetes further exacerbates β-cell dysfunction and insulin resistance, rendering patients more susceptible to blood glucose instability when exposed to perioperative stressors such as surgical trauma and anesthesia [13]. These results emphasize the need for more intensive blood glucose monitoring and intervention in elderly patients with long-standing diabetes during the perioperative period.

The progression of coronary artery lesions is closely related to abnormal blood glucose metabolism. Previous studies have pointed out that elevated blood glucose can increase the risk of multi-vessel disease by promoting the formation of atherosclerotic plaques and aggravating vascular endothelial damage [21]. This study found that perioperative blood glucose fluctuations were significantly associated with elevated LDL-C and decreased HDL-C, and both lipid indicators were independent risk factors for blood glucose fluctuations. Mechanistically, blood glucose fluctuations promote the generation of lipid peroxidation products by exacerbating oxidative stress, accelerate the oxidative modification of LDL-C, and inhibit the reverse cholesterol transport function of HDL-C [22], which echoes the previous research conclusion that abnormal blood glucose aggravates coronary artery lesions, further revealing the effect of blood glucose fluctuations in dyslipidemia and the progression of coronary artery lesions.

The perioperative blood glucose fluctuations focused on in this study are essentially a dynamic manifestation of stress-induced hyperglycemia mediated by surgical stress. The mechanistic association between such fluctuations and adverse cardiovascular events can be further corroborated and extended by recent studies on admission hyperglycemia and stress-induced hyperglycemia. On the one hand, the clinical value and underlying mechanisms of admission hyperglycemia have become a research focus in patients with diabetes mellitus complicated with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) who receive sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2i) therapy [23]. A multicenter registry study by Paolisso et al. [24] confirmed that even in diabetic patients with AMI treated with SGLT2i, admission hyperglycemia remains significantly associated with enlarged infarct size and elevated in-hospital inflammatory burden, serving as an independent predictor of adverse cardiovascular events. This conclusion forms a mechanistic complementarity with the findings of the present study: our research verified that baseline FBG and HbA1c are independent risk factors for perioperative blood glucose fluctuations in patients undergoing PCI. Admission hyperglycemia not only reflects the patient’s baseline state of glucose metabolic disorder but also indicates insufficient reserve capacity for blood glucose regulation in response to stress. Although SGLT2i can yield cardiovascular benefits by improving insulin sensitivity and inhibiting inflammatory responses, it cannot completely reverse pancreatic β-cell dysfunction mediated by chronic hyperglycemia. When patients are exposed to acute stress induced by AMI or PCI surgery, significant blood glucose fluctuations still occur, which in turn exacerbate myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury and inflammatory reaction [25]. This suggests that admission hyperglycemia is a core biomarker for identifying high-risk populations of stress-induced blood glucose fluctuations regardless of whether patients receive SGLT2i therapy. Intensive glucose management and risk stratification for patients with admission hyperglycemia before surgery can further reduce the risk of perioperative adverse events, which constitutes one of the important clinical application scenarios of the predictive model constructed in this study.

On the other hand, the mechanism by which stress-induced hyperglycemia drives the risk of chest pain and hospitalization in patients with ischemia and non-obstructive coronary arteries (INOCA) is highly consistent with the pathophysiological effects of perioperative blood glucose fluctuations identified in our study. Studies by Wang et al. [26] have clearly shown that hyperglycemia significantly increases the risk of in-stent restenosis by promoting the proliferation of vascular smooth muscle cells, deposition of extracellular matrix, and inflammatory reactions. Moreover, Mone et al. [27] confirmed that stress-induced hyperglycemia is an independent driver of recurrent chest pain and rehospitalization in INOCA patients, with three core mechanisms: first, stress-induced hyperglycemia and concomitant blood glucose fluctuations can exacerbate oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction, leading to coronary microvascular dysfunction and reduced coronary flow reserve—this is the core pathological basis for myocardial ischemia and chest pain in INOCA patients; second, blood glucose fluctuations can induce coronary artery smooth muscle spasm, increase myocardial oxygen consumption, and further aggravate myocardial ischemia and chest pain symptoms; third, stress-induced hyperglycemia can activate the sympathetic nervous system and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), intensifying vasoconstriction and myocardial ischemia, thus forming a vicious cycle [28]. The present study found that the afternoon/evening surgical time window is an independent risk factor for perioperative blood glucose fluctuations, whose mechanism is associated with the circadian rhythm of cortisol and physiological fluctuations in insulin sensitivity. Such circadian rhythm-mediated changes in glucose regulation capacity similarly affect the risk of chest pain episodes in INOCA patients. The predictive model constructed in this study can accurately identify high-risk patients with stress-induced blood glucose fluctuations using routine preoperative indicators. By optimizing surgical timing, strengthening perioperative blood glucose monitoring and intervention, the incidence of stress-induced hyperglycemia can be reduced, thereby lowering the risk of perioperative chest pain and rehospitalization. This provides a novel interventional approach for the perioperative management of INOCA patients complicated with diabetes mellitus.

Studies have confirmed that stress hyperglycemia is positively correlated with the risk of in-hospital cardiogenic shock in STEMI patients, and its predictive value is better than a single blood glucose value [26]. SBP (OR = 1.062) and surgical duration (OR = 11.265) in this study, as core risk factors for blood glucose fluctuations, further reveal the induction mechanism and risk transmission pathway of stress hyperglycemia fluctuations: elevated SBP reflects vascular endothelial damage and insulin resistance [29], while continuous stress caused by prolonged surgery increases the level of stress hormones, exacerbating the vicious cycle of blood glucose fluctuations and elevated blood pressure. Blood glucose fluctuations further increase SBP by damaging vascular endothelium and increasing blood viscosity, while elevated blood pressure inhibits insulin secretion by activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, forming a chain of “elevated blood pressure - blood glucose fluctuations - deterioration of cardiac function“ [29]. This mechanism explains why the stress hyperglycemia ratio is related to cardiogenic shock: the amplitude and duration of blood glucose fluctuations directly reflect the body’s stress tolerance and metabolic regulation function, and their risk signals are stronger than the blood glucose or blood pressure values at a single time point [30]. These results highlight the importance of comprehensive pre-intervention optimization of metabolic parameters, including strict control of blood glucose, lipids, and blood pressure, to mitigate perioperative blood glucose fluctuations risk. Sleep quality, quantified by the PSQI score, was identified as a modifiable risk factor for perioperative blood glucose fluctuations, a finding that warrants increased clinical attention. Poor sleep quality activates the sympathetic nervous system, elevates cortisol and catecholamine levels, and inhibits insulin secretion while reducing insulin sensitivity [31]. In the perioperative context, anxiety related to surgery, environmental disruptions, and pain often exacerbate sleep disturbances, creating a feedback loop that perpetuates blood glucose fluctuations [32]. The significant association between higher PSQI scores and increased blood glucose fluctuations risk (OR = 1.162, P = 0.006) suggests that targeted interventions to improve sleep quality, such as psychological counseling, sleep hygiene education, and appropriate pain management, which may serve as effective strategies to stabilize perioperative blood glucose. This adds a new dimension to perioperative care, emphasizing the need for holistic management that addresses both physical and psychological factors. It should be noted that this study has controlled for confounding factors such as analgesia, sedation, and anxiety management. Univariate analysis showed that NRS score, HAD score, and sedative use were not significantly associated with blood glucose fluctuations, confirming the independent predictive effect of PSQI score.

Surgery-related factors, including surgical duration, surgical start time window, and time from preoperative last meal to surgery start, emerged as critical predictors of perioperative blood glucose fluctuations, reflecting the impact of procedural and logistical factors on metabolic stability. The OR value of surgical duration is as high as 11.265, and its clinical rationality can be explained by pathophysiological mechanisms: prolonged surgery leads to continuous traumatic stress, stimulating the release of stress hormones such as cortisol and glucagon, promoting glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, and causing hyperglycemia [33]; in this study, the incidence of blood glucose fluctuations in patients with surgical duration > 2 h reached 78.3%, further verifying the authenticity of this association. The driving role of stress hyperglycemia in adverse outcomes of cardiovascular diseases has been confirmed by multiple studies, and the perioperative blood glucose fluctuations focused on in this study provide a new perspective for understanding the dynamic characteristics and intervention targets of stress hyperglycemia. Studies by Mone et al. have pointed out that stress hyperglycemia can increase the risk of chest pain recurrence and rehospitalization by inducing coronary artery spasm and increasing myocardial oxygen consumption [27]. This study found that the surgical start time window (afternoon/evening) is an independent risk factor for blood glucose fluctuations (OR = 2.213), which forms a mechanistic complement to this literature: endogenous hormones have circadian rhythm changes in the afternoon/evening: cortisol levels peak in the early morning and then gradually decrease, and insulin sensitivity fluctuates accordingly, leading to reduced tolerance of patients to blood glucose fluctuations from afternoon to evening. Combined with surgical stress, the amplitude of blood glucose fluctuations increases significantly. This dynamic fluctuation may be more likely to induce coronary microcirculation disorders than static hyperglycemia. In INOCA patients, there is no significant coronary artery stenosis, but endothelial dysfunction caused by blood glucose fluctuations can lead to decreased coronary flow reserve, thereby inducing chest pain [34]. The model constructed in this study can accurately identify such high-risk patients, and reduce the risk of perioperative chest pain by adjusting the surgical time preoperatively and strengthening blood glucose monitoring, providing an operable early warning tool for the perioperative management of patients. These findings suggest that optimizing surgical scheduling and individualizing preoperative fasting protocols may help reduce blood glucose fluctuations risk.

At present, there is a lack of individualized management strategies for abnormal blood glucose in cardiac rehabilitation guidelines, and the core reason is insufficient understanding of the association mechanism between abnormal blood glucose and rehabilitation process [35]. The results of this study just fill this gap: perioperative blood glucose fluctuations, as a dynamic extension of abnormal admission blood glucose, include risk factors covering multiple dimensions such as metabolic indicators, lifestyle, and surgical processes. These factors jointly affect myocardial repair and rehabilitation effects through interaction. The nomogram model constructed in this study can accurately identify patients at high risk of hyperglycemia fluctuations before the start of cardiac rehabilitation, providing a basis for the individualized adjustment of rehabilitation programs, and filling the mechanism and practical gaps in blood glucose management in cardiac rehabilitation.

The outcome definition of this study is in line with the Chinese Expert Consensus on the Management of Blood Glucose Fluctuations in Patients with Diabetes Mellitus, ensuring the standardization of outcome assessment. The included predictive indicators covering five dimensions were all routinely obtainable in clinical practice before surgery without the need for additional special examinations, with good clinical accessibility. The 5-variable high-impact predictor model constructed based on SHAP analysis was verified to have no statistically significant difference in predictive performance compared with the full variable model. While maintaining certain predictive accuracy, this simplified model effectively lowers the threshold for clinical application and is applicable to two different clinical scenarios: refined risk stratification for inpatients and rapid preoperative screening in primary medical institutions, thus making up for the inconvenience of the full variable model in clinical implementation.

Despite the aforementioned clinical application value, this study has several limitations. Only stable patients undergoing elective PCI at a single center were enrolled, that is, highly heterogeneous populations such as those undergoing emergency surgery and those with severe comorbidities were excluded. This may have reduced the difficulty of model prediction to a certain extent, resulting in an overestimated AUC in internal validation, and also limited the generalizability of the model in critically ill populations. Internal validation was performed using a 7:3 split of the single-center dataset, without facing the challenge of heterogeneity from populations with different characteristics in multi-center studies, which may also be the main reason for the excessively high AUC of the full variable prediction model in this study. In addition, we adopted a composite standard of three indicators to define the outcome of blood glucose fluctuations. Although this approach reduces missed diagnosis and misclassification caused by a single indicator, it may expand the definition scope of positive outcomes, which may inflate the discriminative performance of the model.

Conclusion

This study identified 5 core independent risk factors for perioperative blood glucose fluctuations in CHD patients complicated with T2DM who underwent PCI, namely age, duration of diabetes, HbA1c, PSQI score, and surgical duration. The constructed nomogram prediction model is consistent with the conclusions of previous studies regarding the associations between abnormal blood glucose and coronary artery lesions, myocardial injury, and adverse clinical outcomes. By focusing on the characteristics of perioperative dynamic blood glucose fluctuations, supplementing modifiable intervention targets, and providing a practical predictive tool, the model further enhances the mechanistic understanding and clinical practice of perioperative blood glucose management for this patient population. Validated to have good discriminative ability, calibration performance and clinical applicability, this model can serve as a useful tool for clinicians to assess the risk of perioperative blood glucose fluctuations in such patients and formulate individualized intervention strategies. It is expected to help reduce the risk of perioperative adverse events and long-term cardiovascular complications, and provide a practical reference for the individualized management of perioperative blood glucose in CHD patients complicated with T2DM.
