Introduction

Dental caries is one of the most widespread chronic conditions in children, affecting both developed and developing nations. Because of structural weaknesses such as larger pulp chambers, thinner enamel, and less calcified dentin, it is more aggressive in primary teeth.1 Globally, it is estimated that over 530 million children suffer from untreated dental caries in their primary teeth,2 resulting in pain, infection, impaired chewing, poor nutrition, and absences from school.3 Beyond causing oral discomfort, dental caries in early childhood has been associated with poor academic performance, speech delays, and a lower quality of life for children and their families.

Traditional restorative procedures involving mechanical excavation with high-speed handpieces remain a cornerstone in caries treatment. While effective, these methods may inadvertently remove healthy tissue, induce microfractures in dentin and enamel,4,5 potentially compromising tooth strength and reducing pulp vitality. Furthermore, the accompanying heat, vibration, and noise may be distressing to young children. These factors can significantly raise pediatric patients' dental anxiety,4 frequently requiring sedation or local anesthesia.6,7 To alleviate these concerns, alternative minimally invasive techniques have been gaining popularity.

Recent years have witnessed a growing acceptance of minimally invasive dentistry (MID) in dental research and clinical practice. This philosophy places the highest focus on early diagnosis, prevention, and tissue preservation. MID principles advocate the selective removal of damaged tissue while preserving remineralizable dentin in order to maintain the structural and functional integrity of teeth.8 Chemomechanical caries removal (CMCR), one of the MID techniques, has demonstrated favorable results, especially in pediatric dentistry, where patient comfort and cooperation are crucial.

Chemomechanical caries removal combines chemical softening agents with gentle mechanical removal, selectively targeting denatured collagen in infected dentin and leaving behind intact or remineralizable tissue, thereby reducing trauma to healthy tissue.9 Several CMCR agents have been developed, beginning with GK101 in the 1970s and evolving through Caridex, Carisolv, and papain-based gels.10–14 Interest has particularly grown in natural enzyme-based gels because of their biocompatibility, antimicrobial properties, and enzymatic selectivity.

Carie-Care, introduced in India around 2010, is a papain-based gel. Papain, a cysteine protease extracted from papaya latex, is incorporated with essential oils such as clove oil and chloramine and is designed to soften carious dentin while offering analgesic and anti-inflammatory benefits.7,15 Papain breaks down the partially damaged collagen in weakened dentin while preserving healthy tissue. It can be used without specialized kits, requiring only manual scraping tools, and leaves behind a dentin surface conducive to bonding. Clinical trials have demonstrated Carie-Care's efficacy in eliminating caries from primary molars, and evidence suggests improved patient acceptance, especially in anxious children.16 Clinical trials indicate that, although Carie-Care takes longer than conventional methods, it enhances patient comfort and cooperation, yielding efficient caries removal and promising restoration outcomes in primary teeth.10,14,17

A comparable CMCR modality is provided by bromelain, a proteolytic enzyme complex extracted from pineapple stems. While bromelain's medical uses, such as debriding burn wounds, are well documented,10,18 its dental application is more recent. It possesses anti-inflammatory, anti-edematous, and fibrinolytic properties.19In vitro comparisons indicate that bromelain gel removes more demineralized dentin than papain-based gels, with comparable treatment times.10,12 According to these results, bromelain may offer greater selectivity without compromising efficiency.

Despite encouraging preliminary studies, there are currently few head-to-head in vivo comparisons between Carie-Care and bromelain gel. Most existing randomized trials evaluate papain-based products against mechanical excavation or other CMCR agents, but direct clinical comparisons between papain and bromelain formulations in primary teeth are scarce.

This study aims to bridge that gap through a randomized controlled clinical trial comparing the efficiency of Carie-Care and bromelain gel for CMCR in primary teeth, focusing on several key outcomes: the time required to reach caries-free dentin, the efficacy of caries removal measured according to clinical excavation criteria, child pain perception, and behavioral acceptance, assessing patient cooperation and the overall treatment experience.

According to the null hypothesis, there is no discernible difference in caries removal using CMCR agents, namely Carie-Care gel and bromelain gel, in terms of child behavior, pain evaluation, caries removal efficacy, or time required.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

In this clinical trial, two methods of caries removal were evaluated in a randomized controlled setting. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (RUHS-CDS/ECI2025/PG-SS/03) to conduct the clinical trial.

Patient Recruitment

Patients who visited the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, RUHS College of Dental Sciences, Jaipur (from May 2025 to July 2025), were assessed, and a total of 30 children who met the eligibility criteria were recruited for the study after obtaining informed consent from their parents or guardians.

Inclusion Criteria

Children in the age-group of 5–8 years of any gender.

Children in the age-group of 5–8 years of any gender.

Children with open carious lesions in primary teeth with dentin involvement only [International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS) score 5 or 6].

Children with open carious lesions in primary teeth with dentin involvement only [International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS) score 5 or 6].

Children with carious lesions wide enough to allow the use of a spoon excavator.

Children with carious lesions wide enough to allow the use of a spoon excavator.

Children with a Frankl Behavior Rating Scale score of 3 or 4.

Children with a Frankl Behavior Rating Scale score of 3 or 4.

Exclusion Criteria

Children with radiographic findings of pulp involvement.

Children with radiographic findings of pulp involvement.

Children with a history of allergies or other medical ailments.

Children with a history of allergies or other medical ailments.

Randomization and Grouping

The study participants were randomly allocated to one of the two groups according to the caries removal method, with 15 children in each group.

Group I: Carie-Care gel

Group II: Bromelain gel

The patients, their parents, the assistant, and the statistician were blinded to the study group allocation, whereas the operator could not be blinded because of the difference in the color of the gels used in the two groups.

Preparation of Bromelain Gel

Bromelain gel was self-prepared using the following ingredients16:

To prepare the bromelain gel, 0.8 gm of Carbopol was first homogeneously mixed with 8 mL of water. Subsequently, 32 mL of additional water was added to the mixture. Separately, 1 gm of bromelain powder was dissolved in 5 mL of water and then incorporated into the Carbopol solution. Following this, 2 mL of alpha-D-tocopherol was directly added. In a separate step, 0.2 gm of methyl paraben was dissolved in 5 mL of distilled water and then introduced into the existing solution. The final mixture was subjected to homogeneous stirring for 30 minutes to obtain freshly prepared bromelain gel, which was then stored in airtight containers.

Procedure

Using cotton rolls and saliva ejectors, the selected teeth were partially isolated in both groups (Figs 1A and D), and caries was removed using either of the two gels. For group I, caries was removed using Carie-Care gel (V-CARIE SOLVE, Vishal Dentocare Pvt. Ltd., India), which was administered into the carious cavity for 60 seconds (Fig. 1B). The assistant started the stopwatch after the application of the gel. Using a sterile spoon excavator (API*dental), the softened dentin was scraped away without applying pressure when the gel appeared cloudy. The gel was reapplied until the light hue of the cavity did not change and the cavity surface felt firm on excavation; this was considered the endpoint (Fig. 1C). The stopwatch was stopped, and the time was noted by the assistant. Moistened cotton pellets were used to remove any residual gel. The same procedure was followed in group II using freshly prepared bromelain gel (Fig. 1E) until firm dentin, which was resistant to hard excavation, was reached and the caries excavation endpoint was identified (Fig. 1F). Glass ionomer cement (Glasionomer Fx Ultra Bulk Fill, Shofu Inc., Japan) was used to restore each tooth in the study.

Assessment Procedures

The time taken for caries removal was recorded in seconds, from the start of caries removal after gel application up to the endpoint in each group, using a stopwatch operated by the assistant.

The efficacy of caries removal was assessed using a blunt explorer that moved smoothly across the dentinal wall, according to Hegde et al.'s20 scoring criteria (Fig. 2).

Hegde et al.'s scoring criteria for efficacy of caries removal20

Pain assessment: Objective pain perception was scored using the sound, eye, motor (SEM) scale by the assistant. Subjective pain perception was scored using the visual analog scale (VAS) at the end of the treatment by asking the child.

Child behavior was noted according to the Frankl behavior rating scale immediately at the end of the treatment by the assistant.

Statistical Analysis

The data for the present study were entered into Microsoft Excel 2007 and analyzed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) statistical software, version 23.0. The descriptive statistics included frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation. The level of significance for the present study was fixed at 5%. The ordinal and nominal variables were compared using the Chi-square test. Intergroup comparisons were performed using independent t-tests. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to investigate the distribution of the data, and Levene's test was used to assess the homogeneity of the variables.

Results

A total of 30 children aged 5–8 years were included in the study and divided equally into two groups: group I, treated with Carie-Care gel, and group II, treated with bromelain gel (Fig. 3). The mean time taken for caries removal in group I was 270.73 ± 26.21 seconds, whereas group II demonstrated a significantly shorter mean duration of 242.93 ± 15.97 seconds (p = 0.001), indicating that bromelain gel was more time-efficient (Table 1 and Fig. 4A). There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in caries removal efficacy, as determined by Hegde et al.'s criteria (p = 0.842), with most children in both groups achieving scores of 1, indicating partial removal of infected dentin (Table 2 and Fig. 4B). Pain perception, evaluated using the VAS, was low in both groups, with group I showing a mean score of 0.20 ± 0.56 and group II a mean score of 0.13 ± 0.35 (p = 0.700), suggesting no significant variation in subjective pain experience (Table 3 and Fig. 4C). Similarly, the SEM scale scores showed minimal discomfort in both groups (group I: 1.13 ± 0.35; group II: 1.06 ± 0.25), with no statistically significant difference (p = 0.558) (Table 3 and Fig. 4C). Behavior was assessed using the Frankl behavior rating scale, where 86.7% of children in both groups exhibited a rating of 4 (definitely positive behavior), while the remaining 13.3% showed a rating of 3 (positive behavior), with no differences between the groups (p = 1.000) (Table 4 and Fig. 4D). Overall, both Carie-Care and bromelain gel were well tolerated and effective; however, bromelain gel significantly reduced the procedure time.

Discussion

Chemomechanical caries removal has gained prominence as a minimally invasive technique, particularly suitable in pediatric dentistry, where cooperation, comfort, and tissue preservation are essential. This randomized controlled clinical trial compared the clinical performance and behavioral outcomes of two CMCR agents—Carie-Care, a papain-based gel, and a custom-formulated bromelain gel—in children aged 5–8 years with carious primary molars.

During the early mixed dentition phase, represented by the selected age range of 5–8 years, children's attitudes toward dentistry and compliance are significantly molded. During this stage, cooperation is still developing, and the introduction of minimally invasive, atraumatic methods can significantly improve the child's dental experience. In addition, this age group is most susceptible to caries in primary molars, which are essential for arch integrity, mastication, and guidance of permanent tooth eruption. The choice of primary molars was thus strategic, as their preservation with conservative techniques such as CMCR reduces the need for space maintainers and future orthodontic intervention.21

The study found that bromelain gel required significantly less time for caries excavation (mean: 242.93 ± 15.97 seconds) compared to Carie-Care (270.73 ± 26.21 seconds), with a p-value of 0.001. This outcome aligns with prior studies showing that bromelain, a cysteine protease from pineapple stem, possesses stronger proteolytic action than papain, allowing faster degradation of infected dentin.10 The reduced treatment time holds clinical relevance, as shorter procedures are closely linked to enhanced child cooperation and decreased operator fatigue during pediatric dental care.22,23

In terms of clinical efficacy, both gels performed comparably, with no statistically significant difference (p = 0.842) based on the Hegde et al. visual criteria. This supports earlier work by AlHumaid24 and Venkataraghavan et al.25, who found Carie-Care effective in selectively removing infected dentin while sparing sound dentin. This study was among the first to evaluate bromelain in pediatric clinical settings, and considering its similar efficacy, it may be an acceptable substitute for papain-based CMCR therapies.

Pain perception, an important consideration in pediatric care, was assessed via both subjective (VAS) and objective (SEM) indices. Both groups recorded low scores, with no significant difference (p > 0.05), reaffirming that CMCR agents, in general, are well tolerated and cause minimal discomfort.26 This result is crucial for lowering dental anxiety and encouraging positive behavior, especially in young children attending their first dental appointment.

These results were further corroborated by the Frankl behavior rating scale, which showed that 86.7% of children in both groups exhibited “definitely positive” behavior. This is most likely due to the atraumatic and anesthesia-free nature of CMCR procedures. Similar behavioral responses were reported by Waterhouse et al.27 and Morankar et al.28, establishing that young patients act more cooperatively when rotary instrumentation is eliminated.

Both enzymes are noteworthy for their additional therapeutic advantages and biocompatibility. Papain, the active ingredient in Carie-Care, is known for its anti-inflammatory and bactericidal properties, aided by additives such as clove oil and chloramines. Recent research indicates that bromelain may be used as an adjuvant in pulpal therapies and wound healing because of its antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory properties.17,29 These benefits extend the therapeutic role of CMCR agents beyond simple caries excavation.

However, a few limitations exist. The sample size was relatively small and derived from a single center, limiting generalizability. There was also no microbiological or histological assessment, which would have provided a more objective measure of caries removal.

Conclusion

Both Carie-Care and bromelain gels proved to be equally effective in terms of caries removal, patient comfort, and behavioral response in children aged 5–8 years. Bromelain gel demonstrated a significant advantage in time efficiency, potentially offering practical benefits in busy pediatric settings. These findings support the use of enzyme-based CMCR agents as viable, child-friendly alternatives to conventional methods.

To validate the sustained efficacy and patient satisfaction associated with these therapies, further research with longer follow-up periods and larger sample sizes is required.

Clinical Significance

The early mixed dentition phase is an ideal time to implement minimally invasive techniques that can shape a child's future cooperation and dental attitudes.

CMCR in primary molars preserves tooth structure and arch integrity, which are critical for normal growth and occlusal development.

Bromelain gel, while not yet commercially available, showed significant promise as a fast, safe, and effective CMCR agent, comparable to Carie-Care in efficacy and child comfort.

Both agents are safe, effective, and behaviorally acceptable, supporting their use in child-friendly, conservative caries management protocols and reinforcing the philosophy of atraumatic restorative treatment (ART) and minimally invasive dentistry (MID).
