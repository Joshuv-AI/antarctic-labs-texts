Human papillomavirus vaccination patterns among youth aged 16–26 in the context of publicly-funded eligibility changes: a retrospective cross-sectional study from Alberta, Canada
Authors: Laura Reifferscheid, Li Huang, Misha Miazga-Rodriguez, Amy Colquhoun, Colleen M Norris, Shannon E MacDonald
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHuman papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination programs in Canada primarily target school-aged adolescents (≤ 15 years) through publicly-funded, school-based delivery. In the province of Alberta, publicly-funded HPV vaccination for older youth (age ≤ 26) began with a risk-based program (i.e. availability limited to those at elevated risk for HPV infection and/or progression to disease) in 2018, expanding to an age-based program (i.e. available to all individuals within funded age groups) in July 2020. This study explores HPV vaccination patterns among 16–26-year-olds during this time period, in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study using population-wide administrative health data (February 2018–December 2023). Rates of HPV vaccination per 10,000 individuals were calculated by sex and age group, comparing the quarterly absolute and percent difference between the pre-pandemic risk-based period and the pandemic age-based period. We also calculated the quarterly proportion of doses delivered by sex (stratified by age group), delivery site, and individual residence (metro/urban/rural, age of arrival to province).ResultsHPV vaccine administration rates were higher among females than male throughout the study period (84–224 doses/10,000 compared to 40–135 doses/10,000). Rates dropped sharply after pandemic onset (March 2020), with the largest and most sustained drops among 16–17-year-olds (156 doses/10,000). Rates for 18–21 and 22–26-year-olds, who were newly eligible under the age-based program, returned to or exceeded pre-pandemic levels by 2022. Delivery site distribution shifted, with increased vaccination at post-secondary and STI health centres. Dose delivery remained consistently highest among metro residents, and individuals who entered Alberta after age 16.ConclusionsExpansion to an age-based HPV vaccination program may have mitigated pandemic-related declines in vaccination rates among older youth, though overall administration remained low. Results highlight persistent inequities by sex and geography, and underscore the importance of targeted promotion, improved service delivery, and expanded provider engagement to optimize HPV vaccine uptake outside school-based settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25283-7.

Background

The human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine is a safe and effective method of preventing multiple types of HPV infections that cause genital warts and cancers (i.e. anogenital and oropharyngeal). Since its licensure in 2006, HPV vaccination has primarily been promoted during early adolescence, in order to develop immunity prior to the age of sexual debut, and to leverage enhanced immune response among younger recipients [1, 2]. However, vaccinating older age groups can contribute to both individual [3, 4], and population-level protection [5]. In Canada, the National Advisory Committee on Immunization (NACI) has recommended HPV vaccination for females aged 9 through 26 years since 2007; beginning in 2012, that recommendation expanded to include males [6]. Publicly-funded HPV vaccination programs in Canada, which are under the purview of provincial/territorial governments, have focused primarily on vaccinating school-aged adolescents using a school-based delivery model [7]. More recently, programs have begun to expand publicly funded vaccine access to older age groups, through both risk- (i.e. availability limited to those at elevated risk for HPV infection and/or progression to disease) and age-based (i.e. available to all individuals within funded age groups) programs [8, 9].

Unlike younger adolescents who are typically vaccinated through school-based delivery, the burden often falls on adults to navigate vaccination opportunities. Accordingly, program-related barriers, such as restrictions to publicly funded vaccination, service access, and limited/unclear promotion are implicated in low vaccine uptake among older age groups, as evidenced by frequently reported barriers including lack of awareness about vaccine importance [10, 11], process for receipt [10], and concern over costs [11, 12]. Previous studies found that implementation of a risk-based publicly funded vaccine program resulted in substantial gains in HPV vaccine coverage for eligible males (men who have sex with men [MSM] up to age 27) in Canada [9, 13]. However, the impact of age-based programs is largely unknown.

Since February of 2018, the Canadian province of Alberta has offered publicly funded HPV vaccination to individuals until age 27, first through a risk-based program, then shifting to an age-based program in July of 2020. Given the timing of Alberta’s program switch, the impact of this policy change is complicated by the COVID-19 pandemic. Specifically, healthcare access limitations related to the COVID-19 pandemic may also have impacted both program delivery, and potentially overshadowed awareness of the program change. Thus, the focus of our study was to explore the dual impact of the COVID-19 pandemic and program change on patterns in HPV vaccine delivery among Albertans vaccinated outside the routine school-based immunization program.

Alberta context

The HPV vaccine was added to Alberta’s routine vaccination schedule in 2008, with offering initially limited to females in Grade 5 (age 10–11 years) (Fig. 1; [14]). Since that time, coverage for school-aged children has evolved to include males (beginning in the 2014/15 school year), and expanded to allow those offered the vaccine in early adolescence the opportunity to receive catch-up doses until the age of 18. A quadrivalent HPV vaccine (Gardasil)was offered until September 2016, when it was replaced by a nonavalent vaccine (Gardasil 9) [14]. Through these routine offerings, supplemented by time-limited catch-up plans, vaccination through publicly-funded school programs has been available to females born in 1995 or later, and males born in 2000 or later [8].

Timeline of HPV vaccine eligibility in Alberta

As with most other Canadian jurisdictions, HPV vaccine delivery in Alberta is managed mainly through school-based vaccination programs delivered by public health nurses [7]. School-based programs for HPV vaccine typically target adolescents in grade 5 or 6, though the vaccine is offered in grade 9 (age 14–15 years) for those who missed it in grade 5/6. Outside of school-based programs, publicly funded vaccines are provided by public health nurses in community-based public health clinics. Those who are eligible for vaccination but not covered by public funding can purchase the HPV vaccine through a pharmacy. Thus, older youth eligible for publicly-funded HPV vaccination (age 16–26 years) are routinely served outside of school-based programs.

The COVID-19 pandemic resulted in critical drops in HPV vaccine coverage for school-aged children [15]. Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, two-dose (complete) coverage by age 13 was approximately 69% for both males and females, with coverage dropping to below 45% by 2022 and subsequently recovering to >60% by 2023 [16].

Methods

Population and data sources

We conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study to evaluate HPV vaccine uptake among 16–26 year olds, who were residents of Alberta between February 2018 and December 2023. Alberta has approximately 4.7 million residents, of which 99% are registered with the universal health care insurance plan, Alberta Health Care Insurance Plan (AHCIP) [17]. Through this plan, all vaccines listed in the Government of Alberta routine vaccination schedule are available free of charge to eligible individuals. All publicly-funded vaccines delivered by public health nurses in the province are recorded in the provincial vaccination repository, Immunization and Adverse Reactions to Immunization (Imm/ARI). Non-publicly funded vaccines delivered by pharmacists are reported to the Pharmaceutical Information Network (PIN) (prior to 2021) or to Imm/ARI directly (after January 01, 2021) [18].

The dataset for this study was created by linking data from AHCIP, ImmARI, and PIN. Upon registration with AHCIP, an individual is assigned a unique lifetime identifier (ULI). This ULI is captured in all the healthcare databases each time a person accesses the healthcare system, allowing deterministic linkage of health records across these databases. Participant informed consent was waived by the University of Alberta Human Research Ethics Board (ethics ID: Pro00140216).

Study variables and analysis

HPV vaccination events during the study time period were identified by extracting immunization information from Imm/ARI and PIN, limited to vaccines that were received by individuals between the ages of 16 and 26 years. We included all HPV vaccine types, and both privately purchased and publicly funded. HPV vaccination events that occurred within 15 days of another HPV vaccination event were removed as potential duplicate entries (per Government of Alberta practice). We chose to evaluate patterns in HPV vaccination administration (i.e. number of doses) rather than coverage (i.e. the proportion of the eligible population who received the recommended vaccine), as we were unable to determine the denominator, or population eligible for publicly-funded vaccine [19], during the risk-based program.

To account for population changes during the observation period, we calculated the quarterly rate for vaccine administration by age group and sex. Age was divided into three groups: the 16–17 year age group, who would have been eligible for publicly-funded vaccination throughout the study time period and thus not impacted by the policy change, and the 18–21 year, and 22–26 year groups, whose eligibility was directly impacted by the policy change. Quarterly rates were defined as the number of vaccines administered to that group in a given quarter, relative to the age- or sex-appropriate number of individuals enrolled in AHCIP at quarter end, reported as doses per 10,000 of the population. Based on a natural break in the data, and to facilitate comparison with the growing literature evaluating the impact of the pandemic on vaccine delivery, we differentiated “baseline” (pre-pandemic) and “impact” (pandemic) time periods based on COVID-19 pandemic emergency declarations in March 2020 [20]. We determined expected vaccination rates for the impact period (i.e. quarter 2 of 2020 to end of study period) as the vaccination rates for analogous quarters in 2019. We did not include 2018 rates in determination of expected vaccination rates, as the risk-based program change was newly implemented in 2018. We then compared these expected rates to observed rates by calculating both quarterly rate differences, and percent changes, to facilitate comparison with existing literature.

We further stratified HPV administration events by factors we hypothesized would be impacted by the shift from the risk-based to age-based program, including age-group for each sex, type of delivery site, and individual residence factors. Delivery site types included in the analysis included public health centres focused on sexual health services or located on post-secondary school campuses. We were unable to separate doses delivered by pharmacies, as the number was too low; those doses are included in the “other” category, along with doses delivered through public health centres not included in the previous categories. Individual residence factors included: (1) location of residence by population density (metro [population >500,000], urban [population >25,000 but < 500,000], rural [population < 25,000] [21]), and; (2) timing of entry to the province, differentiating those who entered the province prior to age 16, when they would have been screened and offered vaccination through school-based programs. To evaluate shifts in the distribution of dose administration for each variable, we calculated proportions of total HPV administration events by variable category for each quarter of the observation period. Results

For the entire period of observation, the overall rate of HPV vaccine administration was higher in females than males, though dose administration rate patterns were similar in both groups (Fig. 2). Similarly, administration rates for both sexes dropped in the quarter after the pandemic emergency declaration, increasing to at or above base line levels by the first quarter of 2022 (Fig. 2, Additional File 1). HPV vaccine administration rates were highest in the youngest age group (16–17 yrs) during the risk-based, pre-pandemic time period. While rates were lower in the 18–21 year and 22–26 year age groups during this time period, these groups experienced smaller drops in administration rates after pandemic declaration (absolute decrease of 84.7 doses/10,000 individuals aged 18–21 yrs and 78.2 doses/10,000 individuals aged 22–26 yrs, compared to 156 doses/10,000 individuals age 16–17 yrs; Additional File 1). By the first quarter of 2022, vaccine delivery rates had reached (18–22 year age group) or exceeded (22–26 year age group) baseline rates.

During the risk-based program, total HPV vaccine administration counts ranged from 1766 to 2318 doses administered per quarter prior to pandemic emergency declaration (Fig. 3). Doses delivered during the age-based program ranged from a low of 1094 doses during the second quarter (April-June) of 2021, to a high of 2521 doses during the second quarter (April-June) of 2023. Among females, the 22–26 year age group received the majority of doses delivered throughout the study time period (ranging from 46.3% to 54.6% prior to the pandemic, and from 43.4% to 64.3% after the pandemic) (Fig. 3). For males, administration was divided approximately evenly between all three age groups during the pre-pandemic, risk-based period, shifting to the 22–26 year age group after pandemic declaration (ranging from 42.4% during the Q2 of 2020 to 60%+ during most quarters of 2021 and 2022) (Fig. 3).

There was a shift in site delivery distribution between the pre-pandemic, risk-based time period and the pandemic, age-based time period (Fig. 4). Delivery at post-secondary institutions increased from < 1.0% during the risk-based program to 14.0% during the age-based program. The proportion of doses delivered through sexually transmitted infection (STI) centres also increased, from 4.8% during the risk-based program to 5.4% during the age-based program. Throughout the study period, most HPV vaccine doses administered were delivered through other public health centres (95.0% and 80.6% during the risk- and age-based programs, respectively).

During the pre-pandemic period, 67.2% of all doses administered were provided to individuals who were residents of the province before they turned 16 years of age; this dropped to 60.5% during the pandemic (Fig. 5). Compared to the pre-pandemic period, the average proportion of doses delivered to metro and rural residents decreased (76.9% vs. 74.5% and 14.3% vs. 12.4%, respectively), while the proportion of doses delivered to urban residents increased (8.7% vs. 13.1%).

Discussion

We used population-wide vaccination data to explore patterns in HPV vaccine administration in the province of Alberta, among those vaccinated outside of routine school-based programs before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. We found some evidence that the increased availability of publicly-funded vaccine that occurred due to the shift from a risk- to an age-based program may have mitigated the impacts of pandemic-related service interruptions. Vaccine administration rates for the 18–21 and 22–26 year age groups, which would have been directly impacted by this policy change, experienced minimal drops in the immediate pandemic period (2020, 2021), followed by similar or higher rates during the remaining period (2022, 2023). In contrast, the 16–17 year age group, for whom publicly funded eligibility did not change, experienced the largest and most sustained declines in administration rates. In the absence of changes to public funding, one study from the US reported pandemic-related changes to adult vaccine administration rates (not specific to HPV) greater than we see here, with overall administration rates changing by as much as −70%, and a more prolonged decline (with deficits persisting in 2022) [22]. We also found that vaccine delivery after pandemic emergency declaration and implementation of the age-based program corresponded with a significant increase in the proportion of vaccines delivered through post-secondary public health centres. This potentially indicates greater focus on delivering to the 18 + year old age groups.

We found that HPV vaccination administration rates were lower for males than females throughout the study period. This was unexpected for two reasons: first, during the risk-based program, males would have had increased access to publicly funded vaccine, relative to females; second, males would have made up the largest pool of unvaccinated individuals during our study time period, as they were not included in school-based delivery until 2014, six years after school-based delivery was implemented for females. However, these results support the generally lower coverage observed among males in this age group: as of 2023, Canadian coverage for males aged 18–26 yrs is estimated to be approximately 46.9%, compared to 77.3% among females [23]. Numerous factors have been identified to contribute to relatively lower HPV vaccination in males. Healthcare providers may be more likely to encourage uptake for females, both because provider knowledge around the importance of male vaccination lags [24], and because synergies with routine cancer screening for females may act to prompt vaccination discussions [25]. While the risk-based program expanded access, free vaccination required disclosure of sexual orientation, which likely limited uptake. A Canadian study indicated that only 26–56% of men eligible for free vaccination reported disclosing their sexual behaviour to their provider [13]. It will be important to understand whether the males being vaccinated during the age-based program have the same risk-profile as those vaccinated during risk-based delivery.

We found that those who entered the province after the age of 16 yrs, before which routine school-based immunization programs were in place, made up a significant proportion of the individuals vaccinated during the risk-based program, increasing during the age-based program. This increase appears to correspond to an increase in immigration to the province, which reached record levels in 2023. Previous research has shown that newcomers in this age group are generally open to receiving the HPV vaccine [9, 26] though may face added challenges to being vaccinated, including limited knowledge about HPV and the vaccine [9, 26]. Healthcare providers may also be less likely to recommend and have discussions around vaccination with immigrants, leading to reduced awareness [26, 27]. While expanded eligibility is important to provide protection to those new to the province [28], this must be accompanied by appropriately targeted information campaigns.

Although present results indicate HPV vaccination outside of school programs is happening, rates are very low. In particular, we noted a disproportionately low proportion of vaccine doses were delivered to rural residents, which made up a minimum of 15% of Alberta’s population during the study time period [29], raising concern given that vaccination rates for school-aged children are lower among rural Albertans [30]. Lack of awareness about HPV vaccine eligibility is a commonly cited barrier to vaccination among young adults [31, 32]. Despite the growing influence of Internet and social media, traditional media remains important for informing public knowledge, attitudes and awareness of HPV vaccination [33, 34]. However, there appears to have been limited attention to Alberta’s program change in Canadian media, with articles published after the program change reporting only on the school grades that HPV vaccination is routinely provided in, erroneously identifying eligibility as “up to 17 years” [35], or available for “some adults” [36–38]. While cervical cancer prevention was promoted through an annual awareness campaign, published by a variety of newpapers across the province in Jan/Feb of 2022 and 2023 [e.g. 37], these releases focused on cervical cancer screening only, with no mention of immunization. These provide an opportunity to promote HPV vaccination as a cancer prevention strategy, which has been shown to be more influential than a focus on sexual health [39]. The paucity of media coverage for the program change may also contribute to limited knowledge of publicly-funded vaccine eligibility among healthcare professionals who do not provide the vaccine but may be well-positioned to advocate for it. This may be especially important in regions like Alberta, where vaccine delivery is separate from other healthcare.

Both STI centres and public health centres at post-secondary institutions played an increased role in vaccine delivery to this age group after the age-based program was implemented, indicating established processes for screening and providing vaccines to older age groups. It may be useful to examine these processes for improvement opportunities, to maximize delivery at these locations. For example, previous work in the STI clinic setting has shown that a combination of healthcare provider prompts, along with patient prompts and education was effective at increase HPV vaccine delivery [40].

Strengths and limitations

Our study benefited from population-wide vaccination data, allowing us to accurately identify the timing of vaccine administration and avoid the potential biases of self-report. However, because of the overlapping timing of the pandemic, program change, and pandemic-related catch-up efforts, our ability to determine statistically significant relationships between program change and change in vaccination activity was limited. In addition, we were limited to data available in administrative health databases, which do not include gender identity or sexual risk factors. As a result, we were unable to determine the impact of the risk-based delivery program on HPV vaccine coverage, or account for these individual-level factors important for HPV vaccine acceptance and uptake. While administration counts provide a general picture of program activity, individual-level analysis of vaccination status would be required to understand program impact on population-level protection. Future work should consider the impact of expanding publicly-funded eligibility on inequities in vaccine coverage.

Conclusions

This work provides insight on vaccine uptake outside of school-based programs, in a location with well-established school-based programming, and highlights opportunities for directing vaccination programming in this age group. Findings indicate that there is a need to strengthen program delivery and promotion, important considerations for locations that have already expanded age-base HPV vaccine eligibility, as well as those who are still considering.
