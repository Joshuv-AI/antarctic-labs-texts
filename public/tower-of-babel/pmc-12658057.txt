Rat models of lactose intolerance: sexually dimorphic comorbidities with neuropsychiatric disorders modulated by tryptophan hydroxylase and the dopaminergic system
Authors: Marcella C. Galvão, Nathalia A. Galvão, Vanessa S. A. da Silva, Tifany V. Rocha, Daniel N. L. Santos, Liebert B. Carvalho, Rodrigo A. F. da Silva, Thiago Berti Kirsten
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Some studies indicate a sexually dimorphic effect, along with a potential predisposition to neuropsychiatric disorders in patients with lactose intolerance. This study proposed rat models of lactose intolerance, evaluating the manifestation of behaviors related to neuropsychiatric disorders, their incidence in each sex, and the underlying neurobiological processes. Rats were exposed to two lactose-rich diets: a solution containing 14% lactose and a commercial milk formula. Clinical signs were monitored. Behavioral tests for anxiety (light-dark box), social preference, and depression (forced swim test) were conducted. Brain dopamine levels and genetic and epigenetic (DNA methylation) expressions of tryptophan hydroxylase (Tph)1 in the intestine were also examined. Both diets were well consumed and led to impaired stool consistency. Males exhibited anxiolytic-like behavior, increased striatal dopamine levels, and decreased cortical dopamine levels after consuming the lactose solution. In contrast, females showed anxiogenic-like behavior and decreased cortical dopamine levels following the commercial formula. Male socialization was impaired by both diets. Furthermore, both diets reduced Tph1 gene expression. In conclusion, this study introduced two rat models of lactose intolerance that revealed sexually dimorphic comorbidities with neuropsychiatric disorders, along with modulation of striatal and cortical dopaminergic systems and reduced genetic expression of the Tph1 enzyme in the intestine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-26166-y.

Introduction

During lactation, the digestive tract of a newborn produces lactase (beta-galactosidase), an enzyme which acts in the lactose degradation by hydrolysis, in order to break down the molecules into D-glucose and D-galactose for nutrient absorption1,2. Human newborns have enough lactase to digest about one liter of breast milk per day (d)3. After the lactation period, a decline in the enzymatic lactase production should occur, since under natural conditions adult mammals no longer ingest milk - lactase non-persistence (LNP)3. However, humans have continued to consume milk and its derivatives, replacing breast milk by those produced by other mammals, such as cows and goats4.

The beginning of human consumption of milk from other species occurred a long time ago, during the agricultural revolution. There is evidence from over 9 thousand years where organic residues of cattle milk were found preserved in archaeological pottery5. Ever since, the constant human consumption of milk from other species has favored the selection of genetic mutations of some populations in order to maintain the production of the lactase enzyme after childhood6. Hereditary lactase persistence is common in people of northern Europe and is attributed to the inheritance of an autosomal dominant mutation6. At least five human populations worldwide have already developed independent mutations that result in a dominant lactase persistence phenotype in adulthood1.

On the other hand, the maturational decline in lactase activity turns the individual intolerant to the consumption of milk and its derivatives. In conditions of primary or secondary lactase deficiency, lactose pass through the gastrointestinal tract undigested/partially digested and/or are digested by enzymes produced by the intestinal microbiota, producing short-chain fatty acids, hydrogen, carbon dioxide, and methane6. Lactose intolerance symptoms include diarrhea, abdominal pain and distension, and flatus7. Therefore, lactose intolerance is defined as symptoms and signs arising from lactose exposure in animals that have a reduction or absence of lactase8. It is estimated that 70% of the world’s population suffers from LNP, which is the primary physiological cause of lactose intolerance3.

The elderly and women are classically described as those most affected by lactose intolerance4,9–12. The studies to explain these phenomena are few and somehow contradictory, making further studies necessary to unravel their mechanisms.

Interestingly, there are reports of a greater predisposition to neuropsychiatric disorders, such as depression and anxiety in women with lactose intolerance. In an experimental study with healthy female volunteers who received oral doses of lactose, higher prevalence of lactose intolerance symptoms and depression were observed13. Moreover, there are indirect evidences of the association between gastrointestinal dysfunctions and anxiety. It is reported a high comorbidity among patients who have gastroenteritis and/or irritable bowel syndrome with anxiety disorders14,15. One explanation for these relationship is that lactose ingestion in individuals who do not have persistent lactase would lead to an inflammatory process and consequent impairment of the availability of L-tryptophan, affecting the activity of the serotonergic system (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT), which is known to be closely linked to depression and anxiety13,16. In fact, women produce less L-tryptophan than men17.

Incidentally, the available concentration of serotonin is 5% supplied by the brain and 95% produced in the peripheral organs of the gastrointestinal epithelium18. The peripheral serotonin concentration is regulated by the enzyme tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH) 1, expressed predominantly in the intestine19. Furthermore, enteric dopaminergic neurons receive direct serotonergic innervations20 and several neuropsychiatric disorders are also associated with dysfunctions in the dopaminergic system21.

There are few studies with experimental models in rodents and lactose intolerance. One example is the exposure of mice to high doses of lactose and a treatment with a recombinant Lactococcus lactis strain, which improved the symptoms of lactose intolerance22. A similar study was carried out on Wistar rats exposed to a lactose-rich diet and treated with shen-ling-bai-zhu-san, a traditional Chinese herbal compound23. In fact, rodents have high intestinal lactase activity before weaning, but this activity decreases significantly after weaning; a phenomenon similar to what occurs in humans24. Therefore, adult rats are naturally lactase deficient and even low concentrations of lactose can cause digestive harms25.

Thus, considering the high incidence of lactose intolerance and the limited knowledge regarding its mechanisms and experimental models, the objectives of this study were to propose and study two experimental models of lactose intolerance in adult male and female rats, evaluating the manifestation of behaviors related to neuropsychiatric disorders and the incidence in each sex. It was also an objective to understand the neurobiological processes involved.

Results

Experiment 1: lactose solutions and consumption and general clinical health

Supplementary Table S1 online describes and Supplementary Figure S1 online illustrates the results obtained in the seven tests performed in the Experiment 1, including information about solubilization, crystallization, and physical-chemical aspects of solutions, the solutions consumption by the rats, and general clinical health of the rats. Briefly, all the concentrations higher than 14% (50, 35 and 25%) of lactose solutions, regardless of the preparation protocol, crystallized and sedimented, as well as resulted in obstruction of the drinkers, impairing the rats’ consumption. On the other hand, the solution with 14% lactose did not result in crystallization and sedimentation. There were also no alterations in visual and olfactory aspects of the solutions, such as color, odor, viscosity, and evidence of colonization by microorganisms even after the 168-h evaluation. Moreover, the 14% lactose solution was well consumed by the rats, denoting good palatability, and its consumption resulted in whitish stool. No other impairment in the general clinical health of the rats was observed during the daily assessments. Thus, the 14% lactose solution and its preparation protocol (Test 7) were selected for the Experiment 2.

Experiment 2: clinical health monitoring

Figure 1A shows the body weight of male and female rats over 14 days of the lactose-rich diets. As expected, it was observed a homogeneous distribution of two populations: male (heavier) and female (lighter), regardless of the diets. The diet factor and the days of exposure factor were not affected, without interaction between these factors. The only difference observed was for males, with an increase in body weight over the days. However, there was no change between the diets for all the analyses over the days. Therefore, both lactose-rich diets (14% lactose solution and commercial formula) did not result in body weight gain or loss in the male and female rats.

However, diet intake was different between groups (Fig. 1B). All factors evaluated showed changes: diet, sex, and days of exposure, as well as there were interactions between diet vs. sex, diet vs. days, sex vs. days, and diet vs. sex vs. days. Males preferred the formula (d4 and 6). Result was heterogeneous for females: preference for the commercial formula on d2 and preference for the lactose solution on d4 and 14. Thus, both lactose-rich diets presented good palatability for male and female rats, an essential requisite for establishing the model.

Similar to the overall diet intake results, diet intake normalized to body weight differed between groups (Fig. 1C). All factors evaluated showed significant changes: diet, sex, and days of exposure. In addition, interactions were observed between diet and sex, diet and days, sex and days, and diet, sex, and days. From d4 onwards, females consumed more lactose-rich diets than males. Notably, the lactose solution was the most preferred intake among females.

The stool consistency score was different between groups (Fig. 1D). The lactose solution affected the stool consistency of males from d6, maintaining the impairment on d12 and 14. The formula affected the stool consistency of males from d2, maintaining the impairment on d4, 12, and 14. Talking about females, the lactose solution affected the stool consistency from d4, maintaining the impairment on d8, 12, and 14. The formula affected the stool consistency of females from d10, maintaining the impairment on d12 and 14. The greatest statistical difference in the stool consistency of females occurred also on d12, but with the lactose solution.

Analysis of the clinical health monitoring revealed that both lactose-rich diets (14% lactose solution and commercial formula) were well accepted and consumed by male and female rats throughout the 14 days of exposure. Furthermore, the diets impaired the stool consistency. Males consumed more of the commercial formula and females consumed more of the lactose solution. In both cases, the most affected day for the stool consistency was d12.

Experiment 2: light-dark test

Figures 2A-C show the results of the anxiety-like behavior evaluated in the light-dark test of male and female rats after 14 days on lactose-rich diets. The total time spent in the dark side of the apparatus was affected by both the diet and sex factors; interactions between these factors were also observed (Fig. 2B). Males exposed to the lactose solution spent less time in the dark side, whereas females exposed to the commercial formula spent more time in the dark side, compared to the values ​​of their respective controls. The rearing frequency was also affected by sex and an interaction between diet and sex was observed, although diet did not affect it (Fig. 2C). The other parameters evaluated in the light-dark test were not affected by diets and sex and there was no interaction between these factors: dark side entry latency (Fig. 2A) and number of attempts to enter in the light side (data not shown). Therefore, these results revealed a sexually dimorphic effect: the lactose solution induced anxiolytic-like behavior in males and the commercial formula induced anxiogenic-like behavior in females. Exploratory activity was not affected by the diet factor.

Experiment 2: social preference test

Figures 2D-F show the results of the social preference test on male and female rats after 14 days on lactose-rich diets. The total time spent on social interaction was affected by both the diet and sex factors; interactions between these factors were also observed (Fig. 2E). Males exposed to both lactose-rich diets interacted less with the stimulus rat, compared to the values ​​of their respective controls, being the effect more robust after the commercial formula exposure. The other parameters evaluated in the social preference test were not affected by diets and sex and there was no interaction between these factors: first social interaction latency (Fig. 2D), time spent in the empty chamber (Fig. 2F), number of entries into the empty chamber, time spent in the chamber with the stimulus rat, and number of entries into the chamber with the stimulus rat (data not shown). Therefore, the results of the social preference test revealed that both lactose-rich diets (14% lactose solution and commercial formula) reduced social interaction in male rats, with the most pronounced effect caused by the commercial formula; an effect not observed in females.

Experiment 2: forced swim test

Figures 2G-I show the results of the forced swim test on male and female rats after 14 days on lactose-rich diets. The diet factor was not affected in any of the parameters analyzed: first immobility latency (Fig. 2G), total immobility time (Fig. 2H), and total time climbing attempts (Fig. 2I). The sex factor was only affected for the total immobility time, whereas an interaction between the diet and sex factors was observed for the immobility time and climbing time. The only difference found between groups was in immobility time, which was greater in males exposed to the commercial formula compared to those exposed to the lactose solution. Therefore, the forced swim test revealed that none of the lactose-rich diets (14% lactose solution and commercial formula) affected parameters related to depressive-like behavior. An effect was observed only in the differences between genders, with males remaining more immobile than females.

Discussion

Experiment 1 of the present study was design to define the dose, preparation protocol, and administration route of the lactose solution to be administered to rats as an experimental model of lactose intolerance. Different concentrations of lactose were evaluated and prepared using various protocols that included factors such as heating temperature and agitation time. The solutions containing 50%, 35%, and 25% lactose crystallized, obstructing the drinkers and impairing the rats’ consumption. The solution containing 14% lactose showed no physicochemical changes throughout the evaluation period. Furthermore, the 14% lactose solution was well consumed by the rats, indicating good palatability, and its consumption resulted in whitish stools. Therefore, the 14% lactose solution and its preparation protocol were selected for Experiment 2.

Additionally, Experiment 1 served as an initial characterization of lactose sensitivity in the Wistar rats. The results showed that 48 h after the introduction of the 14% lactose solution, the rats exhibited whitish stools. Stool consistency, in fact, is considered a reliable parameter for studying lactose-induced diarrhea, as well as other gastrointestinal and inflammatory diseases23,26,27, which may be comparable to studies on human lactose intolerance28–30.

One of the objectives of the study was to develop two experimental models of lactose intolerance. Both models utilized lactose-rich diets (14% lactose solution and commercial formula), as lactose, when ingested in dairy products, is the primary factor responsible for inducing lactose intolerance symptoms. One model focused on studying the pure substance (lactose) to better understand the neurobiological mechanisms involved. The second model used a commercial formula to mimic the real consumption of commercial compounds by the population that becomes lactose intolerant. Considering the limited number of studies using rodent models for lactose intolerance, these are the first findings from two novel experimental models of the condition. The results include data on clinical health, behavior, brain, intestine, genetic, and epigenetic parameters. These models—based on lactose and formula exposure in rats—may serve as suitable tools for future mechanistic and naturalistic studies on lactose intolerance, respectively.

It is important to highlight that a 14% lactose concentration is considered a lactose-rich solution compared to the lactose concentrations found in human breast milk (6.9–7.2%)31,32, cow’s milk (4.6–4.8%)32,33, and rat’s milk (1.1–2.6%)32.

There are some studies in the literature that use lactose-rich solutions to induce typical signs of lactose intolerance in rodents22,23,25,34,35. However, all these studies used routes of lactose administration different from the one proposed in the present study. They used the oral administration route, either mixed/prepared in a lactose-rich feed or via forced oral administration (gavage). Among the cited studies, the one conducted in the same animal strain as the present study–feeding Wistar rats with a 35% lactose diet replacing starch for 14 days–reported complementally but distinct results, including watery/loose stools, reduced body weight, and inactive or grouping behaviors.

The present study is groundbreaking in proposing an experimental model of lactose intolerance in rodents based on exposure to lactose-rich solutions provided simultaneously with the rodents’ standard diet, allowing them to freely choose their diet, as humans do. This model aimed to replicate a natural scenario in which the subject consumes a lactose-rich diet simply because it is enjoyable. Moreover, it avoids forced feeding methods (gavage), considering that gavage stresses rodents and leads to behavioral impairments36, which were the focus of the study. Thus, the present study developed models for exposing rats to lactose-rich diets through solutions provided ad libitum. These solutions demonstrated good consumption and palatability, successfully replicating the human consumption conditions associated with lactose intolerance.

The commercial formula used in this study contains nutrients other than lactose, including carbohydrates, proteins, fats, fiber, vitamins, and other elements. Each of these nutrients could potentially affect the results. However, as previously noted, the purpose of using this formula was to mimic a natural intake of industrialized components. Moreover, its comparison with the control and lactose solution groups enables a reliable interpretation of the data.

The lactose concentrations in the lactose and formula solutions were different. Therefore, direct volumetric comparisons between the lactose and formula groups should be interpreted with caution. One of the objectives of this study was to propose and investigate two distinct experimental models of lactose intolerance in adult male and female rats, and this objective was successfully achieved.

There is a consensus that lactose intolerance is more prevalent in women9,10. However, these studies rely on methodologies involving volunteers completing questionnaires, often without an accurate medical diagnosis, and/or fail to consider that women have historically participated more as research volunteers and reported health issues more openly. On the other hand, more in-depth studies that analyze epidemiological data suggest otherwise. A retrospective study reviewing gender, age, and ethnicity as possible factors influencing prevalence analyzed various databases and included publications from prominent journals in the field, spanning the years 1974 to 201437. The study concludes that there is no evidence to support the claim that women and any specific ethnic group are more likely to develop lactose intolerance, apart from genetic differences related to LNP. A more recent study presents similar findings: 248 lactose-deficient volunteers showed no differences based on gender and race38. Thus, the present findings of similar impairments in the stool consistency in male and female rats (both exhibiting greater impairment on the 12th day of the experimental model) suggest that there are no differences between sexes regarding the main sign of lactose intolerance. Both males and females showed a similar prevalence of lactose intolerance manifestations.

Also considering the manifestation of significant impairments in stool consistency observed on the 12th day of the experimental model, it can be concluded that the protocol design, involving a 14-day exposure to lactose-rich diets, effectively revealed gastrointestinal impairments, thereby supporting the proposition of two experimental models of lactose intolerance.

Further studies on the lactose intolerance phenotype—such as fecal water content, stool frequency, fecal output mass, and gastrointestinal motility—should complement the present findings and help confirm the phenotype. Thus, the phenotype cannot be considered fully defined without additional quantitative measures. In addition, gastrointestinal discomfort should be assessed to avoid behavioral misinterpretations, although no significant impairments in the general clinical health of the rats were observed. Nevertheless, as previously mentioned, stool consistency is considered a reliable parameter for assessing lactose-induced gastrointestinal impairments, and that adult rats are naturally lactase-deficient—making them susceptible to digestive issues even at low lactose concentrations—the observed impairment in stool consistency was deemed sufficient to indicate lactose-induced gastrointestinal disturbances.

Regarding body weight results, it was demonstrated that the two experimental models of lactose intolerance (14% lactose solution and commercial formula) did not affect the weight of male and female rats. In other words, there was neither weight gain nor loss after 14 days of lactose-rich diets. There are interesting publications on body weight in patients with lactose intolerance. Mediterranean residents diagnosed with LNP exhibit a lower body mass index, smaller waist circumference, and reduced risk of obesity compared to individuals with lactase persistence39. Similarly, Portuguese young adults with lactase persistence tend to have higher obesity rates40. Therefore, significant weight changes are not typically observed in patients with lactose intolerance. On the contrary, multifactorial aspects are considered to explain why lactose-intolerant patients tend not to experience significant weight changes. This includes their tendency to consume little or no lactose-containing foods to avoid gastrointestinal disturbances, resulting in a reduced intake of fats and carbohydrates39. Hence, the absence of body weight changes in the experimental models aligns with the characteristics of lactose-intolerant patients.

As previously mentioned, patients with lactose intolerance, lactose maldigestion, and inflammatory bowel disease are more likely to develop anxiety, depression, and other psychosomatic illnesses. Although the mechanism remains unclear, studies suggest a shared etiology involving both psychological and gastrointestinal dysfunction41.

The present findings revealed that both lactose-rich diets (14% lactose solution and commercial formula) impacted behavioral aspects in rats. Regarding the findings on anxiety, it can be briefly defined as an unpleasant physiological state resulting from an exaggerated reaction to a stimulus42. Gastrointestinal dysfunctions may act as triggers for anxiety processes. For instance, when an inflammatory process occurs in abdominal organs, clinical symptoms manifest, and the brain integrates these interoceptive signals with emotional, cognitive, and memory-related inputs43. Connections between brain networks that mediate anxiety within the autonomic nervous system, such as the amygdale, and mechanisms modulating colonic sensitivity and intestinal motility have been demonstrated. Furthermore, both increased and decreased activation of endogenous pain excitatory and inhibitory pathways have been observed in nervous system sites associated with visceral afferent processing and emotional arousal44.

The mesolimbic, mesocortical, and nigrostriatal systems are intimately involved in the modulation and expression of anxiety45. Specifically, dopamine plays a critical role in modulating anxiety42,46. Both D1 and D2 dopamine receptors modulate anxiety responses. The infusion of D1 and D2 agonists and antagonists elicits both anxiogenic and anxiolytic effects, depending on the nature of the threat experienced by the animal46. For instance, the D2 agonist quinpirole, when injected into the ventral tegmental area, produces an anxiolytic effect in rats47,48. This anxiolytic effect of the dopamine agonist aligns with the present findings, where male rats exhibited increased striatal dopamine levels and an anxiolytic-like effect in the light-dark box test. Consequently, the lactose solution functioned as an anxiolytic in males through the activation of the striatal dopaminergic system.

Still talking about the heterogeneous modulatory framework of the dopaminergic system in anxiety, SCH 23,390, a D1 antagonist, when injected into different brain areas also produces an anxiolytic effect in rats47,49–52. A similar pattern is observed with the infusion of eticlopride, a D2 antagonist, which also induces anxiolytic effect in rats53. The anxiolytic effects of D1 and D2 dopaminergic antagonists observed in these studies are consistent with the present findings, where male rats exhibited decreased dopamine levels in the frontal cortex along with an anxiolytic-like effect in the light-dark box test. Thus, the lactose-rich diet modulated the cortical dopaminergic system, triggering an anxiolytic-like effect in males.

Last but not least, the heterogeneous modulatory framework of the dopaminergic system in anxiety demonstrates that the infusion of raclopride, a D2 antagonist, into the amygdala produces an anxiogenic effect in rats54. This anxiogenic effect induced by the dopaminergic antagonist aligns with the findings observed in female rats in the present study, where lactose-rich diets reduced cortical dopamine levels and resulted in an anxiogenic-like effect in the light-dark box test. Overall, findings suggest that exposure to lactose-rich diets modulated anxiety-related behaviors in a sexually dimorphic manner through the striatal and cortical dopaminergic systems.

The sexually dimorphic effects observed in the study of anxiety in rats—specifically, anxiolytic-like effects in males and anxiogenic-like effects in females—are intrinsic to the pathology of this disorder. Compelling evidence highlights extensive sex differences in pathological conditions, including anxiety and depressive disorders, with females being more than twice as likely to be affected55. Epidemiological differences between sexes in anxiety disorders and major depression are well-established. However, the circuits and mechanisms underlying these differences remain poorly studied, partly because preclinical research has historically excluded female rodents56.

One possible explanation for the sexually dimorphic effects involves L-tryptophan, which is produced in lower quantities by women17. L-tryptophan is converted into 5-HT through the enzyme TPH1 in the intestine19. Given the strong link between the serotonergic system and conditions like depression and anxiety, this study evaluated the genetic and epigenetic processes of the Tph1 gene. Additionally, variation in the Tph1 gene contributes to the regulation of serotonin and dopamine turnover rates in the central nervous system57 and is associated with neuropsychiatric disorders58,59. This phenomenon may help explain behavioral and dopaminergic brain modulation induced by lactose-rich diets.

Females were more affected by lactose, as both lactose-rich diets reduced Tph1 gene expression in the small intestine. In contrast, in males, Tph1 gene expression was reduced only following exposure to the commercial formula. Reduced Tph1 gene expression results in lower production of the TPH1 enzyme. Consequently, lactose ingestion in individuals lacking persistent lactase may trigger an inflammatory process, interfering with L-tryptophan metabolism and 5-HT availability13,16. These findings suggest that lactose intolerance operated through reduced TPH1 enzyme availability, which may explain its comorbidity with neuropsychiatric disorders and its sexually dimorphic effects.

In recent years, an increasing body of evidence has highlighted the role of DNA methylation in the development and progression of various complex diseases, such as cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and neurodegenerative disorders. At the molecular level, these conditions induce changes in gene expression, contributing to the decline in physiological function60. In the present study, the absence of an inversely proportional correlation between methylation of the Th1 gene promoter region and the negative modulation of its expression does not allow us to rule out the involvement of other epigenetic mechanisms in transcriptional control. These findings open new avenues for future research focusing on the role of non-coding RNAs and/or post-translational modifications of histones–mechanisms that, like DNA methylation, have also been implicated in the pathophysiology of various multifactorial diseases61.

Both lactose-rich diets reduced social interaction in male rats. Social interaction is a complex behavior vital for the survival of many species62. Various cortical and subcortical brain areas, such as the frontal cortex and striatum, are involved in a range of social behaviors62,63. Dopamine, produced in the ventral tegmental area, has dense projections to the ventral striatum and significant connections to the frontal cortex. Dopamine, known for its role in processing natural and conditioned rewards64, has been highlighted by several studies as central to encoding and modulating social behavior62. For instance, the dopamine transporter DAT1, which increases striatal dopamine levels, is linked to a stronger social approach tendency in a study where participants distinguished between images with happy and inviting expressions and those with aggressive ones65. An adverse social experience during adolescence leads to reduced dopamine levels in the prefrontal cortex, which are associated with behavioral impairments in juveniles66. Similarly, dopamine receptor agonists and antagonists modulate social interaction67. Infusing amphetamine (a dopamine releaser/reuptake inhibitor) into the nucleus accumbens of rats enhances social play behavior by activating both D1 and D2 receptors64. A comparable effect is observed with apomorphine (a dopamine agonist) infusion, but not with GBR-12,909 (a dopamine reuptake inhibitor) infusion. Additionally, blocking both D1 and D2 receptors decreases social play behavior even in animals strongly motivated to socialize following social isolation64. SCH23390 (a D1 receptor antagonist), administered at different doses to mice, inhibits socialization in a dose-dependent manner—a result not replicated following raclopride (a D2 receptor antagonist) administration—indicating distinct roles of dopaminergic receptors in agonistic behaviors68. The literature provides robust evidence for dopamine’s central role in socialization, supporting the present findings. Therefore, reduced cortical dopamine levels were linked to the diminished socialization observed following exposure to lactose-rich diets. In other words, lactose-rich diets reduced cortical dopamine levels, leading to decreased socialization.

To date, no study has been identified that associates lactose intolerance with dopamine and/or Tph1. This underscores the innovative role of the present study in explaining behavioral comorbidities stemming from lactose intolerance in relation to neurobiological phenomena.

Collecting brain samples after the forced swim test was not considered a study limitation, although stresses can alter monoamine levels. This is because the control groups were evaluated using the same protocol, so the results reflect the effects of the dietary variable. Moreover, including additional experimental groups without exposure to the forced swim test or extending the rats’ lifespan would not align with the 3Rs principles. The present approach prioritized minimizing potential discomfort or prolonged distress in the animals. Using extra animals without behavioral testing would violate the principle of Reduction, and extending the rats’ lifespan after the forced swim test would not conform to ethical best practices.

Future studies involving measurements of the TPH1 enzyme in the intestine and assessments of sexual hormones in both males and females may help elucidate the current findings on sexually dimorphic effects and behavioral modulations observed after exposure to lactose-rich diets. Additionally, studies focusing on the hippocampus could contribute to a better understanding of anxiety- and depressive-like behaviors. Measuring DOPAC and HVA levels could provide insights into how monoamine metabolism occurs. Furthermore, data on circulating mediators (e.g., serotonin and cytokines) could help bridge the findings between the gut and the brain.

In conclusion, this study proposed and investigated two experimental models of lactose intolerance in rats through exposure to lactose-rich diets: a 14% lactose solution and a commercial formula. Both diets were well-accepted and consumed by male and female rats, resulting in impaired stool consistency, with the peak effect observed on the 12th day of exposure. These models revealed sexually dimorphic comorbidities with neuropsychiatric disorders (anxiety and social behavior), along with modulation of striatal and cortical dopaminergic systems and reduced genetic expression of the Tph1 enzyme in the intestine.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study was carried out in strict accordance with the recommendations of the ARRIVE guidelines, the U.K. Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act, 1986 and associated guidelines, and the National Institutes of Health guide for the care and use of Laboratory animals (NIH Publications No. 8023, revised 1978). The protocol was approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of the Paulista University, Brazil (Permit No.: 3308170621 and 8426191121). All efforts were made to minimize suffering, reduce the number of animals used, and utilize alternatives to in vivo techniques when available. The experiments were also performed in accordance with good laboratory practice protocols and quality assurance methods.

Animals

Fifty-four Wistar rats (Rattus norvegicus) were used; 30 males (401.97 ± 8.81 g) and 24 females (230.52 ± 3.80 g), adults (approximately 12 weeks-old). Rats were obtained from University of São Paulo (São Paulo, Brazil) and a minimum period of seven days was applied for acclimatization and habituation in our facilities (Paulista University, São Paulo, Brazil). Standard conditions for rat’s housing, nutrition, handling, and care were conducted69. All the manipulation and evaluations performed in the rats were conducted between 2:00 p.m. and 5:00 p.m., to minimize the effects of circadian rhythms.

Experimental design

This study was divided in two experiments. Experiment 1 was designed to define the lactose concentrations of the diets, their formulation, route of administration, palatability, and effects on the general clinical health. Experiment 2 was designed to propose and study two experimental models of lactose intolerance in adult rats by providing lactose-rich diets. It was evaluated the manifestation of signs related to lactose intolerance, behaviors related to neuropsychiatric disorders, the incidence in each sex, and neurobiological pathways.

Experiment 1: lactose solutions

To prepare lactose solutions at different concentrations, lactose monohydrate (U.S.P. 40, CAS No. 10039-26-6, Labsynth Ltda, Diadema, Brazil) was used. Different lactose concentrations were tested: 50%, 35%, 25%, and 14% lactose, diluted in filtered water. All these concentrations were based in previous data: 50% lactose – mice fertility34; 35% lactose – rats and lactose intolerance signs23; 25% lactose – rats and metabolic parameters25; and 14% lactose – technical information of solubility (Labsynth Ltda, Diadema, Brazil).

The different lactose solutions at different concentrations were formulated using various protocols, modifying variables such as the temperature at which the solution was prepared, the way it was heated using different devices, its agitation, different containers for its preparation, and resting time. All details of these variables and methodological protocols for preparing the lactose solutions are described in Supplementary Table S2 online. The methodological variations were always intended to obtain a lactose-rich solution that would be palatable for rat consumption and that would not crystallize or deteriorate quickly.

After preparing each lactose solution, they were evaluated daily, for up to 168 h, regarding visual and olfactory aspects of the solutions, such as its color, odor, viscosity, and evidence of colonization by microorganisms.

Experiment 1: lactose consumption and general clinical health

Six male rats were exposed to drinkers with different lactose solutions and concentrations, while maintaining the supply of food and water ad libitum. The rats were monitored daily for general clinical health, including stool consistency and the consumption of the lactose solution (mL). A total of seven tests were performed, described in details in Supplementary Table S2 online.

Experiment 2: diets and groups

To induce lactose intolerance signs in male and female rats, two different types of lactose-rich diets were provided. By comparing the results of the two diets it would be possible to elect the most successful in mimicking lactose intolerance.

The first diet used a 14% lactose solution (monohydrate, U.S.P. 40, CAS No. 10039-26-6, Labsynth Ltda, Diadema, Brazil), based on the findings of no crystallization, good palatability, and no deterioration found in the Experiment 1. The 14% lactose concentration is considered high for any milk found among mammals, especially for rats, which has 1.1–2.6% lactose in milk32.

The second diet used a commercial powdered milk formula solution for infants from the lactation segment that was rich in lactose: Aptamil 2 Premium (Danone, Buenos Aires, Argentina). Nutritional information provided by the manufacturer details a 53% lactose concentration in the formula. The solution was prepared following the manufacturer’s instructions, with 210 mL of previously boiled filtered water and seven measuring spoons of the formula.

Both diets were stored at room temperature (23 ± 2 °C) for cooling and then stored in drinkers identical to those used for water consumption. These solutions were made available daily for 14 consecutive days23. The solutions were prepared and replenished daily. The lactose solution remained available to the rats for 24 h, based on the findings of the Experiment 1. The commercial formula was made available for 6 h per day, in order to prevent its deterioration (Danone, Buenos Aires, Argentina).

The rats were divided into six groups (n = 8 rats/group): (1) control male: male rats with standard chow and filtered water; (2) lactose male: male rats with 14% lactose solution for 14 consecutive days; (3) formula male: male rats with a milk commercial formula solution for 14 consecutive days; (4) control female: female rats with standard chow and filtered water; (5) lactose female: female rats with 14% lactose solution for 14 consecutive days; and (6) formula female: female rats with a milk commercial formula solution for 14 consecutive days. All experimental groups had free access to standard chow and filtered water throughout the 14 days.

Experiment 2: clinical health monitoring

Throughout the 14 days of supply of the lactose-rich diets (and respective controls), all six groups of rats were monitored for clinical health. Monitoring occurred every 48 h from the first day of diet until the last day of the experiment: days 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14. The parameters evaluated were: body weight (g), diet intake (solutions, mL), diet intake normalized to body weight (mL/kg/day), and stool consistency score. The stool consistency score was rated from 1 to 4 points representing normal texture and appearance (score 1), progressive loose texture and appearance (scores 2 and 3), and watery diarrhea (score 4)27. Visual health indicators such as general behavior and piloerection were also assessed every 48 h.

Experiment 2: light-dark test

On the day after the end of the diet protocols (day 15), the rats were evaluated in the light-dark test, to evaluate anxiety-like behavior. The arena and the protocol was the same as those applied previously70. Each rat was evaluated for 5 min. The parameters evaluated were: dark side entry latency (s), number of attempts to enter in the light side, total time (s) spent in the dark side, and total rearing frequency.

Light-dark test and the other behavioral tests were evaluated in a small test room with dim lighting, isolated from the experimenter, with acoustic isolation, and with a video camera mounted above/in front of the arena to collect the data, using the software EthoVision XT (Noldus InformationTechnology). Each apparatus was cleaned with a 5% alcohol/water solution before placement of each rat to obviate possible biasing effects from odor cues left by previous rat.

Experiment 2: social preference test

Immediately after the light-dark test, the social preference test was performed71. Briefly, this test used an arena divided into three chambers, one of them with visual and olfactory communication of a stimulus rat of the same sex and age as the experimental subject. All rats underwent a 5-min acclimatization session in the arena on the day before the test. The parameters evaluated in 5-min sessions were: first direct social interaction latency (s, ≤ 1 cm with the stimulus rat), total time (s) spent on social interaction (≤ 1 cm with the stimulus rat), time spent in the empty chamber, number of entries (with all paws) into the empty chamber, time spent (s) in the chamber with the stimulus rat, and number of entries (with all paws) into the chamber with the stimulus rat.

Experiment 2: forced swim test

Immediately after the social preference test, rats were subjected to the forced swim test to evaluate depressive-like behavior70. The following parameters were evaluated over a period of 6 min for each rat: first immobility latency (s), total immobility time (s), and total time (s) climbing attempts on the walls of the cylinder.

Experiment 2: dopamine

Immediately after the forced swim test, the rats were euthanized by decapitation for brain analyzes. Striatum and frontal cortex were evaluated because of their involvement in depression and anxiety69,72. Dopamine levels were measured using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (cat. no. MBS774549, MyBioSource, San Diego, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Experiment 2: tryptophan hydroxylase

Fifteen millimeters of the small intestine from the same rats whose brains were removed were used to determine gene expression and the DNA methylation status of the promoter region of the Tph1 gene with specific primers (Gene expression - F: 5′ AAT TTG CCA AAA CCG TGA AG 3′; R: 5′ AAG GCT TTC TGC ACT GGA AA 3′ and methylation of the promoter region - F: 5′ GTG CCG CGA GAC CTC T 3′; R: 5′ TAG CTG CCT TCC CCT GCG 3′) using the real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) technique. For extraction of genetic material, the lumen was washed with saline solution and processed following standard methodology73. The total RNA was extracted by the Trizol method (Life Sciences - Fisher Scientific Inc, Waltham, MA, USA)74 and genomic DNA by the phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol method (Invitrogen™ product code 10544645)75. For the gene expression study, cDNA was previously synthesized using the High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, qPCR reactions were performed in a QuantStudio® 3 Real-Time PCR (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) in 10 µL reactions, consisting of 5 µL of GoTaq® qPCR and RT-qPCR Systems Master Mix 2x (Promega, Catalog number selected), 0.4 µM of each primer (Exxtend Biotecnologia, Paulínia - SP, Brazil) and 50 ng of cDNA and nuclease-free H₂O. The methylation pattern of the gene promoter region was determined using gDNA previously treated with endonucleases purchased from New England Biolabs, T4-Bgt (M0357S), MspI (R0106T) and HpaII (R0171L)76. Primer sequences were designed in regulatory regions with CpG islands within DnaseI hypersensitive regions. The primers, reaction conditions, and annealing temperatures are described in Supplemental Table S3 online. The endogenous control used for the studied gene expression was the β-actin gene.

Statistical analysis

Homogeneity and normality were verified using Bartlett, F, and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA, factors: diet and sex) followed by Tukey multiple comparison test was used to compare the parametric data among the six groups. Three-way ANOVA (factors: diet, sex, and days of exposure) followed by Tukey multiple comparison test was used to compare the parametric data among the six groups throughout the days of diets exposure. For the stool consistency score, a non-parametric parameter, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used. In all cases, the results were considered significant if p < 0.05. The results are expressed as the mean ± SEM in box & whiskers (min to max, showing all values) graphs.
