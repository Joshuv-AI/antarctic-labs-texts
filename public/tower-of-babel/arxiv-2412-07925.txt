Hermite-type interpolation in terms of exponential polynomials Thanks: The research of the second author was supported by the K-134191 NKFIH Grant.
Ali Hasan Ali, Zsolt Páles

Abstract
. Motivated by classical results of approximation theory, we define an Hermite-type interpolation in terms of n n -dimensional subspaces of the space of n n times continuously differentiable functions. In the main result of this paper, we establish an error term in integral form for this interpolation in the case when the n n -dimensional subspace is the kernel of an n n th order linear differential operator with constant coefficients. Several corollaries are deduced illustrating the applicability of this result.

1.
Introduction

In what follows,
I
will always denote a nonempty open subinterval of the real line. The symbol
\mathscr{C}(I)
will stand for the linear space of continuous complex-valued functions defined on
I
. If additionally
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then
\mathscr{C}^{n}(I)
will denote the space of
n
-times continuously differentiable complex-valued functions defined on
I
.

To describe the classical result about Hermite interpolation, for
\ell,n\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the set

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\ell,n}(I):=\Big\{(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})\mid\,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{{a}}=({a}_{1},\dots,{a}_{\ell})\in I^{\ell}\mbox{ with }{a}_{1}<\dots<{a}_{\ell}\mbox{ and}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}\mbox{ with }n=n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell}\Big\}.

Given an interpolational system
(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})=\big(({a}_{1},\dots,{a}_{\ell}),(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})\big)\in\mathscr{H}_{\ell,n}(I)
, the number
n
will be called its
dimension
. The order
\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})
of
(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})
is defined to be number
\max(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})-1
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})=\big(({a}_{1},\dots,{a}_{\ell}),(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})\big)\in\mathscr{H}_{\ell,n}(I)
and set
N:=\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})
. Given a function
f\in\mathscr{C}^{N}(I)
, the problem of classical Hermite interpolation is to find a polynomial
P
of degree at most
n-1
such that

(1)

\displaystyle f^{(j)}({a}_{i})=P^{(j)}({a}_{i})\qquad(i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\},\,j\in\{0,\dots,n_{i}-1\})

holds.

Define, for
\alpha\in\{1,\dots,\ell\},\,\beta\in\{0,\dots,n_{i}-1\}
, the polynomial
H_{\alpha,\beta}
as follows:

(2)

\displaystyle H_{\alpha,\beta}(x):=\sum_{k=0}^{n_{\alpha}-1-\beta}\frac{\omega(x)}{\beta!k!(x-a_{\alpha})^{n_{\alpha}-\beta-k}}\bigg[\frac{(x-a_{\alpha})^{n_{\alpha}}}{\omega(x)}\bigg]^{(k)}_{x=a_{\alpha}},

where

\displaystyle\omega(x):=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}(x-a_{i})^{n_{i}}.

Then, for all
i,\alpha\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
and
j,\beta\in\{0,\dots,n_{i}-1\}
, one can show that

\displaystyle H_{\alpha,\beta}^{(j)}(a_{i})=\delta_{i,\alpha}\cdot\delta_{j,\beta}.

Therefore, the function
P
defined by the formula

\displaystyle P(x):=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\beta=0}^{n_{\alpha}-1}f^{(\beta)}(a_{\alpha})H_{\alpha,\beta}(x)

is the unique polynomial of degree less than or equal to
n-1
, which satisfies condition (
1
). For the error of the approximation, the following result is known.

Theorem
.

Let
\ell,n\in\mathbb{N}
and
(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})=\big(({a}_{1},\dots,{a}_{\ell}),(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})\big)\in\mathscr{H}_{\ell,n}(I)
.
Define the subintervals
I_{0},I_{1},\dots,I_{\ell}
of
I
as follows

\displaystyle I_{0}:=(-\infty,a_{1}]\cap I,\qquad I_{r}:=(a_{r},a_{r+1}]\quad(r\in\{1,\dots,\ell-1\}),\qquad I_{\ell}:=(a_{\ell},\infty)\cap I

and, for
t\in I
, let
r(t)
denote the unique index in
\{0,1,\dots,\ell\}
such that
t\in I_{r(t)}
. Then, for every function
f\in\mathscr{C}^{n}(I)
and for all
x\in I
,

(3)

\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\beta=0}^{n_{\alpha}-1}f^{(\beta)}(a_{\alpha})H_{\alpha,\beta}(x)+\int_{I}f^{(n)}(t)G(x,t)dt,

where, for
\alpha\in\{1,\dots,\ell\},\,\beta\in\{0,\dots,n_{i}-1\}
, the polynomial
H_{\alpha,\beta}
is defined by
(
2
)
and the function
G:I\times I\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

\displaystyle G(x,t):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r(t)}\sum_{j=0}^{n_{j}-1}\dfrac{(a_{i}-t)^{n-j-1}}{(n-j-1)!}H_{i,j}(x)&\mbox{if }t\leq x,\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle-\sum_{i=r(t)+1}^{\ell}\sum_{j=0}^{n_{j}-1}\dfrac{(a_{i}-t)^{n-j-1}}{(n-j-1)!}H_{i,j}(x)&\mbox{if }x<t.\end{cases}

The proof of this theorem can be found, for instance, in the book
[
1
]
. Several generalizations and applications of this result can be found in the literature, e.g., see the papers
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
.

It seems to be a natural problem to obtain similar interpolations in terms of linear combinations of a given finite set of functions, in particular, in terms of exponential polynomials, which span the kernel of a linear differential operator with constant coefficients.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we recall from
[
2
,
3
]
the description of the so-called characteristic element of the kernel of a linear differential operator and establish an addition formula for the elements of the kernel. Several particular cases of this formula are mentioned, e.g., the addition formula for trigonometric and hyperbolic functions and the binomial theorem. In Section 3, we define interpolational systems and describe a generalized Hermite-type interpolation in terms of an
n
-dimensional subspaces of the space of
n
times continuously differentiable functions. We also introduce the notion of the standard base of such a subspace with respect to the given interpolational system. The standard base is determined in several particular cases. The main result, which is presented in Section 4, establishes an error term in integral form for the Hermite-type interpolation introduced in the previous section in the case when the
n
-dimensional subspace is the kernel of an
n
th order linear differential operator with constant coefficients. Then, several corollaries are deduced illustrating the applicability of our main result.

2.
Auxiliary results on linear differential equations

For
c=(c_{0},\dots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{K}^{n+1}
with
c_{n}=1
, let
n
th-order linear differential operator
D_{c}\colon\mathscr{C}^{n}(I)\to\mathscr{C}(I)
be defined by the formula

(4)

\displaystyle D_{c}(f):=c_{n}f^{(n)}+\dots+c_{1}f^{\prime}+c_{0}f\qquad(f\in\mathscr{C}^{n}(I)).

Let
P_{c}
denote the
characteristic polynomial of
D_{c}
, which is given by

(5)

\displaystyle P_{c}(\lambda):=c_{n}\lambda^{n}+\dots+c_{1}\lambda+c_{0}\qquad(\lambda\in\mathbb{C}).

Let
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
be pairwise distinct roots
P_{c}
with multiplicities
m_{1},\dots,m_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
, respectively. From the theory of linear differential equations, it is well-known that null space of the differential operator
D_{c}
, denoted as
\ker(D_{c})
, is an
n
-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathscr{C}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
consisting of
exponential polynomials
, which is spanned by the following family of
exponential monomials
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\ni t\mapsto t^{j}\exp(\lambda_{i}t),\qquad(i\in\{1,\dots,k\},\,j\in\{0,\dots,m_{i}-1\}).

It is also elementary to see that
\ker(D_{c})
is closed with respect to differentiation and translation, i.e., if
\omega\in\ker(D_{c})
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
, we have that
\omega^{\prime},\tau_{a}\omega\in\ker(D_{c})
, where
(\tau_{a}\omega)(t):=\omega(t+a)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let
\omega_{c}\in\mathscr{C}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
denote the unique solution of the initial value problem

(6)

\displaystyle D_{c}(\omega_{c})=0,\qquad\omega_{c}^{(\ell)}(0)=\delta_{\ell,n-1}\quad(\ell\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}).

The function
\omega_{c}
will be called the
characteristic solution of the differential equation
D_{c}(\omega)=0
or the
characteristic element of the kernel of
D_{c}
. One can see that if
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, then
\omega_{c}
is real-valued. The following result, which offers an explicit formula for
\omega_{c}
, was obtained in
[
2
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
c=(c_{0},\dots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
with
c_{n}=1
, and let
\omega_{c}
be characteristic element of the kernel of
D_{c}
. Let
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
be pairwise distinct roots of the characteristic polynomial
P_{c}
with multiplicities
m_{1},\dots,m_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
, respectively. Define the polynomials
P_{1},\dots,P_{k}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
by

\displaystyle P_{i}(\lambda):=\prod_{\ell\in\{1,\dots,k\}\setminus\{i\}}(\lambda-\lambda_{\ell})^{m_{\ell}}\qquad(i\in\{1,\dots,k\}).

Then, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\omega_{c}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{m_{i}-1}\frac{(P_{i}^{-1})^{(m_{i}-1-j)}(\lambda_{i})}{(m_{i}-1-j)!j!}\cdot t^{j}\exp(\lambda_{i}t).

We shall also need the following result from the paper
[
2
]
which was not stated explicitly therein, however is formulated and proved in the proof of Theorem 3.1.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
c=(c_{0},\dots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
with
c_{n}=1
, and let
\omega_{c}
denote the characteristic solution of the differential operator
D_{c}
. Then, for all
j,\beta\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
,

\displaystyle\delta_{j,\beta}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-j-1}c_{i+j+1}\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta+i)}(0).

Using this lemma, we can obtain the following addition theorem for the elements of the kernel of the differential operator
D_{c}
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
c=(c_{0},\dots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
with
c_{n}=1
, and let
\omega_{c}
denote the characteristic solution of the differential operator
D_{c}
. Then, for all
\omega\in\ker(D_{c})
and for all
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
,

(7)

\displaystyle\omega(u+v)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1-j}c_{i+j+1}\omega_{c}^{(i)}(u)\omega^{(j)}(v).

In view of the formula (
7
), we can see that each member of the kernel of
D_{c}
satisfies a Levi–Civita-type functional equation.

Proof.

Let
v\in\mathbb{R}
be fixed. As functions of the variable
u
, both sides of the formula (
7
) are elements of the kernel of
D_{c}
, i.e., the functions
\varphi,\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
defined by

\displaystyle\varphi(u):=\omega(u+v),\qquad\psi(u):=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1-j}c_{i+j+1}\omega_{c}^{(i)}(u)\omega^{(j)}(v)

belong to
\ker(D_{c})
. On the other hand, for all
\beta\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
,

\displaystyle\varphi^{(\beta)}(0):=\omega^{(\beta)}(v)

and, according to Lemma
2.2
, we can obtain that

\displaystyle\psi^{(\beta)}(0)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\bigg(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1-j}c_{i+j+1}\omega_{c}^{(\beta+i)}(0)\bigg)\omega^{(j)}(v)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\delta_{j,\beta}\omega^{(j)}(v)=\omega^{(\beta)}(v).

Therefore, for all
\beta\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
, we have that

\varphi^{(\beta)}(0)=\omega^{(\beta)}(v)=\psi^{(\beta)}(0)
. By the uniqueness of the solutions of the linear differential equation
D_{c}(f)=0
with a given initial value condition at
u=0
, it follows that
\varphi
and
\psi
are identical functions. This proves that the equality (
7
) holds for all
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎

Example 2.4
.

Let
n=2
and
c:=(c_{0},c_{1},c_{2}):=(-1,0,1)
. Then the differential operator
D_{c}
is given as
D_{c}(f)=f^{\prime\prime}-f
. The kernel of
D_{c}
is spanned by the functions
\cosh
and
\sinh
and the characteristic solution
\omega_{c}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

(8)

\displaystyle\omega_{c}=\sinh.

According to Theorem
2.3
, for all
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
, we have that

\displaystyle\cosh(u+v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{1}\sum_{i=0}^{1-j}c_{i+j+1}\sinh^{(i)}(u)\cosh^{(j)}(v)

\displaystyle=c_{1}\sinh^{(0)}(u)\cosh^{(0)}(v)+c_{2}\sinh^{(1)}(u)\cosh^{(0)}(v)+c_{2}\sinh^{(0)}(u)\cosh^{(1)}(v).

\displaystyle=\cosh(u)\cosh(v)+\sinh(u)\sinh(v),

and

\displaystyle\sinh(u+v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{1}\sum_{i=0}^{1-j}c_{i+j+1}\sinh^{(i)}(u)\sinh^{(j)}(v)

\displaystyle=c_{1}\sinh^{(0)}(u)\sinh^{(0)}(v)+c_{2}\sinh^{(1)}(u)\sinh^{(0)}(v)+c_{2}\sinh^{(0)}(u)\sinh^{(1)}(v).

\displaystyle=\cosh(u)\sinh(v)+\sinh(u)\cosh(v).

These equalities are the standard addition formulae for hyperbolic functions.

Example 2.5
.

Let
n=2
and
c:=(c_{0},c_{1},c_{2}):=(1,0,1)
. Then the differential operator
D_{c}
is given as
D_{c}(f)=f^{\prime\prime}+f
. The kernel of
D_{c}
is spanned by the functions
\cos
and
\sin
and the characteristic solution
\omega_{c}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

(9)

\displaystyle\omega_{c}=\sin.

According to Theorem
2.3
, for all
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
, we have that

\displaystyle\cos(u+v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{1}\sum_{i=0}^{1-j}c_{i+j+1}\sin^{(i)}(u)\cos^{(j)}(v)

\displaystyle=c_{1}\sin^{(0)}(u)\cos^{(0)}(v)+c_{2}\sin^{(1)}(u)\cos^{(0)}(v)+c_{2}\sin^{(0)}(u)\cos^{(1)}(v).

\displaystyle=\cos(u)\cos(v)-\sin(u)\sin(v),

and

\displaystyle\sin(u+v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{1}\sum_{i=0}^{1-j}c_{i+j+1}\sin^{(i)}(u)\sin^{(j)}(v)

\displaystyle=c_{1}\sin^{(0)}(u)\sin^{(0)}(v)+c_{2}\sin^{(1)}(u)\sin^{(0)}(v)+c_{2}\sin^{(0)}(u)\sin^{(1)}(v).

\displaystyle=\cos(u)\sin(v)+\sin(u)\cos(v).

These equalities are the standard addition formulae for trigonometric functions.

Example 2.6
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
c:=(c_{0},\dots,c_{n-1},c_{n}):=(0,\dots,0,1)
. Then the differential operator
D_{c}
is given as
D_{c}(f)=f^{(n)}
. The kernel of
D_{c}
is spanned by the polynomials of degree less than or equal to
n-1
and the characteristic solution
\omega_{c}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

(10)

\displaystyle\omega_{c}(t)=\frac{t^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\qquad(t\in\mathbb{R}).

According to Theorem
2.3
, for all
k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
and
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle(u+v)^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\sum_{i=0}^{\min(k,n-1-j)}c_{i+j+1}\frac{k!u^{k-i}}{(k-i)!}\frac{v^{n-j-1}}{(n-j-1)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=n-1-k}^{n-1}\frac{k!u^{k-(n-1-j)}}{(k-(n-1-j))!}\frac{v^{n-j-1}}{(n-j-1)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\frac{k!u^{k-i}}{(k-i)!}\frac{v^{i}}{i!}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}u^{k-i}v^{i},

which shows that the binomial theorem is also a particular case of Theorem
2.3
.

3.
Hermite-type interpolation with exponential polynomials

Assume that
\Omega
is an
n
-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathscr{C}^{n}(I)
. We are going to construct Hermite-type interpolation for the elements of
\mathscr{C}^{n}(I)
in terms of the elements of
\Omega
. To describe such interpolations, for
\ell,n\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the set

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\ell,n}(I):=\Big\{(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})\mid\,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{{a}}=({a}_{1},\dots,{a}_{\ell})\in I^{\ell}\mbox{ with }{a}_{1}<\dots<{a}_{\ell}\mbox{ and}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}\mbox{ with }n=n_{1}+\dots+n_{\ell}\Big\}.

Given an interpolational system
(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})=\big(({a}_{1},\dots,{a}_{\ell}),(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})\big)\in\mathscr{H}_{\ell,n}(I)
, the number
n
will be called its
dimension
. The order
\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})
of
(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})
is defined to be number
\max(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})-1
.

Let
(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})=\big(({a}_{1},\dots,{a}_{\ell}),(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})\big)\in\mathscr{H}_{\ell,n}(I)
and set
N:=\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})
. Given an
n
-dimensional subspace
\Omega
of
\mathscr{C}^{N}(I)
and function
f\in\mathscr{C}^{N}(I)
, the problem is to find an element
\omega\in\Omega
such that

(11)

\displaystyle f^{(j)}({a}_{i})=\omega^{(j)}({a}_{i})\qquad(i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\},\,j\in\{0,\dots,n_{i}-1\})

hold. The following lemma offers a condition for the unique solvability of the above interpolation problem.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\ell,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})=\big(({a}_{1},\dots,{a}_{\ell}),(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})\big)\in\mathscr{H}_{\ell,n}(I)
and set
N:=\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})
. Let
\Omega
be an
n
-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathscr{C}^{N}(I)
and let
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}\in\Omega
be a linear base for
\Omega
. Assume that

(12)

\displaystyle W_{\boldsymbol{{a}}}^{\boldsymbol{n}}(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}):=\begin{vmatrix}\omega_{1}({a}_{1})&\dots&\omega_{1}^{(n_{1}-1)}({a}_{1})&\quad\dots\quad&\omega_{1}({a}_{\ell})&\dots&\omega_{1}^{(n_{\ell}-1)}({a}_{\ell})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\omega_{n}({a}_{1})&\dots&\omega_{n}^{(n_{1}-1)}({a}_{1})&\dots&\omega_{n}({a}_{\ell})&\dots&\omega_{n}^{(n_{\ell}-1)}({a}_{\ell})\\
\end{vmatrix}\neq 0.

holds. Then, for any
f\in\mathscr{C}^{N}(I)
, the system of linear equations

(13)

\displaystyle f^{(j)}({a}_{i})=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\mu_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}^{(j)}({a}_{i})\qquad(i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\},\,j\in\{0,\dots,n_{i}-1\}).

has a unique solution
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
and hence the function
\omega=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\mu_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}
satisfies the interpolation condition
(
11
)
. Furthermore, if
f\in\Omega
, then
f=\omega
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathscr{C}^{N}(I)
. Then, the base determinant of the system of linear equations
(
13
) is non-vanishing according to condition (
12
). This ensures the unique solvability of (
13
) with respect to
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
follows. The equalities in (
13
) also show that
\omega=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\mu_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}
satisfies the interpolation condition (
11
).

If
f
belongs to
\Omega
, then
f
is a linear combination of the functions
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}
. Therefore, there exist
\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
f=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\nu_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}
. Then, obviously,

\displaystyle f^{(j)}({a}_{i})=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\nu_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}^{(j)}({a}_{i})\qquad(i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\},\,j\in\{0,\dots,n_{i}-1\}).

Combining this system of equations with (
13
), we can see that

\displaystyle 0=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}(\nu_{\alpha}-\mu_{\alpha})\omega_{\alpha}^{(j)}({a}_{i})\qquad(i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\},\,j\in\{0,\dots,n_{i}-1\}).

Due to condition (
12
), it follows that
(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n})=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n})
, and hence
f=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n}\mu_{\alpha}\omega_{\alpha}
must be valid.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\ell,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})=\big(({a}_{1},\dots,{a}_{\ell}),(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})\big)\in\mathscr{H}_{\ell,n}(I)
and set
N:=\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})
. Let
\Omega
be an
n
-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathscr{C}^{N}(I)
and let
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}\in\Omega
be a linear base for
\Omega
. Assume that
(
12
)
holds.
Then, for any
\alpha\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
and
\beta\in\{0,\dots,n_{\alpha}-1\}
, there exists a unique element
\chi_{\alpha,\beta}\in\Omega
which satisfies the following conditions

(14)

\displaystyle\chi_{\alpha,\beta}^{(j)}({a}_{i})=\delta_{i,\alpha}\delta_{j,\beta}\qquad(i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\},\,j\in\{0,\dots,n_{i}-1\}).

Furthermore, the system
\{\chi_{\alpha,\beta}\colon\alpha\in\{1,\dots,\ell\},\beta\in\{0,\dots,n_{\alpha}-1\}\}
forms a base of
\Omega
and, for any
f\in\mathscr{C}^{N}(I)
,

(15)

\displaystyle\omega=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\beta=0}^{n_{\alpha}-1}f^{(\beta)}({a}_{\alpha})\chi_{\alpha,\beta}

is the unique element of
\Omega
which satisfies the interpolation condition
(
11
)
. In addition, if
f\in\Omega
, then
f=\omega
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
and
\beta\in\{0,\dots,n_{\alpha}-1\}
be fixed. To prove the existence of
\chi_{\alpha,\beta}\in\Omega
satisfying (
14
), we search for

\chi_{\alpha,\beta}
in the form
\chi_{\alpha,\beta}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu_{k}\omega_{k}
with some constants
\mu_{1},\dots\mu_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
. Then(
14
) reduces to the following linear system of equations
\mu_{1},\dots\mu_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
:

\displaystyle\delta_{i,\alpha}\delta_{j,\beta}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu_{k}\omega_{k}^{(j)}({a}_{i})\qquad(i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\},\,j\in\{0,\dots,n_{i}-1\}).

The base determinant of this system is nonvanishing according to our assumption (
12
), therefore this system of equations is uniquely solvable for
\mu_{1},\dots\mu_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
and this proves the existence of
\chi_{\alpha,\beta}\in\Omega
satisfying (
14
).

To point out that the system
\{\chi_{\alpha,\beta}\colon\alpha\in\{1,\dots,\ell\},\beta\in\{0,\dots,n_{\alpha}-1\}\}
forms a base of
\Omega
, it suffices to show its linear independence. Assume that, for some constants
c_{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathbb{C}
,
\alpha\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
,
\beta\in\{0,\dots,n_{\alpha}-1\}
, we have

(16)

\displaystyle 0=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\beta=0}^{n_{\alpha}-1}c_{\alpha,\beta}\chi_{\alpha,\beta}.

Then, for all
i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
and
j\in\{0,\dots,n_{i}-1\}
,

\displaystyle 0=\bigg(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\beta=0}^{n_{\alpha}-1}c_{\alpha,\beta}\chi_{\alpha,\beta}\bigg)^{(j)}({a}_{i})=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\beta=0}^{n_{\alpha}-1}c_{\alpha,\beta}\chi_{\alpha,\beta}^{(j)}({a}_{i})=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\beta=0}^{n_{\alpha}-1}c_{\alpha,\beta}\delta_{i,\alpha}\delta_{j,\beta}=c_{i,j}.

Hence (
16
) can only hold when
c_{\alpha,\beta}=0
for all
\alpha\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
,
\beta\in\{0,\dots,n_{\alpha}-1\}
. This shows that the system
\{\chi_{\alpha,\beta}\colon\alpha\in\{1,\dots,\ell\},\beta\in\{0,\dots,n_{\alpha}-1\}\}
is linearly independent, indeed.

Being a linear combination of elements of
\Omega
, it is obvious that the function (
15
) belongs to
\Omega
. Furthermore, for all
i\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
and
j\in\{0,\dots,n_{i}-1\}
, we also have that

\displaystyle\omega^{(j)}({a}_{i})=\bigg(\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\beta=0}^{n_{\alpha}-1}f^{(\beta)}({a}_{\alpha})\chi_{\alpha,\beta}\bigg)^{(j)}({a}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\beta=0}^{n_{\alpha}-1}f^{(\beta)}({a}_{\alpha})\chi_{\alpha,\beta}^{(j)}({a}_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\beta=0}^{n_{\alpha}-1}f^{(\beta)}({a}_{\alpha})\delta_{i,\alpha}\delta_{j,\beta}=f^{(j)}({a}_{i}).

Therefore,
\omega
is the unique element of
\Omega
which satisfies the interpolation condition (
11
).

If, in addition
f\in\Omega
, then, according to the last statement of Lemma
3.1
, for its interpolating function
\omega\in\Omega
, we have that
f=\omega
holds. This implies the last statement of this lemma.
∎

The system
\{\chi_{\alpha,\beta}\colon\alpha\in\{1,\dots,\ell\},\beta\in\{0,\dots,n_{\alpha}-1\}\}
constructed in the above lemma will be called the
standard base of
\Omega
related to the interpolational system
(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})\in\mathscr{H}_{\ell,n}(I)
.

In what follows, we consider the case when
(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
forms a fundamental system of solutions of the
n
th-order homogeneous linear differential equation
D_{c}(f)=0
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
c=(c_{0},\dots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
with
c_{n}=1
, and the differential operator
D_{c}:\mathscr{C}^{n}(I)\to\mathscr{C}(I)
is defined by (
4
). Let
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
denote the pairwise distinct roots of the characteristic polynomial
P_{c}
with multiplicities
m_{1},\dots,m_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
, respectively. Then, according to the theory of higher-order linear differential equation, a fundamental system of solutions
(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
of the differential equation
D_{c}(f)=0
can be given by

\displaystyle(\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n})(t):=\big(\exp(\lambda_{1}t),\dots,t^{m_{1}-1}\exp(\lambda_{1}t),\dots,\exp(\lambda_{k}t),\dots,t^{m_{k}-1}\exp(\lambda_{k}t)\big).

In the first two examples, we describe the settings related to standard Lagrange and Taylor interpolations, respectively.

Example 3.3
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\big(({a}_{1},\dots,{a}_{n}),(1,\dots,1)\big)\in\mathscr{H}_{n,n}(I)

(17)

\displaystyle\chi_{i,0}(t)=\prod_{j\in\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus\{i\}}\frac{t-{a}_{j}}{{a}_{i}-{a}_{j}}\qquad(t\in\mathbb{R}).

These polynomials of degree

and hence they belong to the null space of
D_{c}
. (They are usually called the basic Lagrange interpolational polynomials.) It is easy to see that

\displaystyle\chi_{i,0}({a}_{\alpha})=\delta_{i,\alpha}\qquad(i,\alpha\in\{1,\dots,n\}).

Consequently,
\{\chi_{1,0},\dots,\chi_{n,0}\}
is the standard base of the null space of

related to the interpolational system
\big(({a}_{1},\dots,{a}_{n}),(1,\dots,1)\big)
. The characteristic element
\omega_{c}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
of
\ker(D_{c})
now has the form (
10
).

.

Let

. (In fact, they are the basic Taylor interpolational polynomials at the point

Example 3.5
.

Let

.

.

4.
The main theorem and its corollaries

The following theorem contains the main result of this paper.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\ell,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})=\big(({a}_{1},\dots,{a}_{l}),(n_{1},\dots,n_{\ell})\big)\in\mathscr{H}_{\ell,n}(I)
, let
c=(c_{0},\dots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
with
c_{n}=1
and let the differential operator
D_{c}:\mathscr{C}^{n}(I)\to\mathscr{C}(I)
be defined by
(
4
)
. Assume that the system
\big\{\chi_{\alpha,\beta}\colon\alpha\in\{1,\dots,\ell\},\beta\in\{0,\dots,n_{\alpha}-1\}\big\}
is the standard base of the kernel of
D_{c}
related to the interpolational system
(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})
.
Then, for all
f\in\mathscr{C}^{n}(I)
and
x\in I
, we have

(29)

\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\beta=0}^{n_{\alpha}-1}\bigg(f^{(\beta)}({a}_{\alpha})+\int_{{a}_{\alpha}}^{x}(D_{c}f)(t)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta)}({a}_{\alpha}-t)dt\bigg)\chi_{\alpha,\beta}(x),

where
\omega_{c}
is the characteristic element of the kernel of
D_{c}
defined by
(
6
)
.

Proof.

For fixed
k\in\{0,\dots,n\}
,
\alpha\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
,
\beta\in\{0,\dots,n_{\alpha}-1\}
, and
x\in I
, we can obtain

\displaystyle\int_{{a}_{\alpha}}^{x}

\displaystyle f^{(k)}(t)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta)}({a}_{\alpha}-t)dt

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Big(f^{(k-i)}(x)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta+i-1)}({a}_{\alpha}-x)-f^{(k-i)}({a}_{\alpha})\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta+i-1)}(0)\Big)+\int_{{a}_{\alpha}}^{x}f(t)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(k+\beta)}({a}_{\alpha}-t)dt.

To check this formula, observe that the above equality is valid at
x={a}_{\alpha}
, and the derivatives of both sides with respect to
x\in I
are equal everywhere. Indeed, the derivative of the right hand side equals

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Big(f^{(k+1-i)}(x)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta+i-1)}({a}_{\alpha}-x)-f^{(k-i)}(x)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta+i)}({a}_{\alpha}-x)\Big)+f(x)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(k+\beta)}({a}_{\alpha}-x),

which simplifies to
f^{(k)}(x)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta)}({a}_{\alpha}-x)
, which is exactly the derivative of the left hand side. Using that
\omega_{c}^{(\beta)}
is in the kernel of the operator
D_{c}
, we can see that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}\int_{{a}_{\alpha}}^{x}f(t)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(k+\beta)}({a}_{\alpha}-t)dt=\int_{{a}_{\alpha}}^{x}f(t)\cdot(D_{c}\omega_{c}^{(\beta)})({a}_{\alpha}-t)dt=0,

Combining the above equalities, for
\alpha\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
,
\beta\in\{0,\dots,n_{\alpha}-1\}
, and
x\in I
, we get

\displaystyle f^{(\beta)}({a}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle+\int_{{a}_{\alpha}}^{x}(D_{c}f)(t)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta)}({a}_{\alpha}-t)dt

\displaystyle=f^{(\beta)}({a}_{\alpha})+\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}\int_{{a}_{\alpha}}^{x}f^{(k)}(t)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta)}({a}_{\alpha}-t)dt

\displaystyle=f^{(\beta)}({a}_{\alpha})+\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Big(f^{(k-i)}(x)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta+i-1)}({a}_{\alpha}-x)-f^{(k-i)}({a}_{\alpha})\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta+i-1)}(0)\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}f^{(k-i)}(x)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta+i-1)}({a}_{\alpha}-x).

To see the last equality, one should use Lemma
2.2
. Then, for
\alpha\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
,
\beta\in\{0,\dots,n_{\alpha}-1\}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}f^{(k-i)}({a}_{\alpha})\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta+i-1)}(0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}f^{(j)}({a}_{\alpha})\sum_{i=0}^{n-j-1}c_{i+j+1}\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta+i)}(0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}f^{(j)}({a}_{\alpha})\cdot\delta_{j,\beta}=f^{(\beta)}({a}_{\alpha}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\beta=0}^{n_{\alpha}-1}

\displaystyle\bigg(f^{(\beta)}({a}_{\alpha})+\int_{{a}_{\alpha}}^{x}(D_{c}f)(t)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta)}({a}_{\alpha}-t)dt\bigg)\chi_{\alpha,\beta}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\beta=0}^{n_{\alpha}-1}f^{(k-i)}(x)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta+i-1)}({a}_{\alpha}-x)\cdot\chi_{\alpha,\beta}(x).

For fixed
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
and
y\in I
,
consider the function
F_{k,i,y}:I\to\mathbb{C}
defined by

\displaystyle F_{k,i,y}(x):=f^{(k-i)}(y)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(i-1)}(x-y).

Then,
F_{k,i,y}
belongs to the kernel of
D_{c}
(since the derivatives and translates of
\omega_{c}
are included in it). On the other hand, the interpolation of
F_{k,i,y}
with respect to the interpolational system
(\boldsymbol{{a}},\boldsymbol{n})
is equal to

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\beta=0}^{n_{\alpha}-1}F_{k,i,y}^{(\beta)}({a}_{\alpha})\cdot\chi_{\alpha,\beta}(x)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\beta=0}^{n_{\alpha}-1}f^{(k-i)}(y)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta+i-1)}({a}_{\alpha}-y)\cdot\chi_{\alpha,\beta}(x).

According to Lemma
3.2
, this expression is equal to
F_{k,i,y}(x)
, i.e., for all
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
and
x,y\in I
, we have the following identity

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\beta=0}^{n_{\alpha}-1}f^{(k-i)}(y)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta+i-1)}({a}_{\alpha}-y)\cdot\chi_{\alpha,\beta}(x)=f^{(k-i)}(y)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(i-1)}(x-y).

Using this equality for

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\beta=0}^{n_{\alpha}-1}f^{(k-i)}(x)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(\beta+i-1)}({a}_{\alpha}-x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}f^{(k-i)}(x)\cdot\omega_{c}^{(i-1)}(0)=f(x).

This completes the proof of the equality (
29
).
∎

)
