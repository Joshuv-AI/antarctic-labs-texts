Slug-flow microchannel enables efficient and controllable preparation of sensitive protein nanoparticles
Authors: Zhangyi Gao, Yi Zhang, Anning Tan, Pengli Chen, Saier Liu, Min Qiu, Zhenlun Wang, You Ma, Guozhi Qian, Jasper H A Schuurmans, Minjing Shang, Xin Jin, Timothy Noёl, Yuanhai Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroreactors are valued for efficient mixing and precise control in nanoparticle synthesis. However, when encapsulating sensitive proteins and enzymes, conventional fluidic shear causes serious damage and activity loss. Critically, the interplay between mixing performance and shear effects within microreactors remains poorly understood, yet is pivotal for the successful preparation of protein-based nanoparticles. This study applied a gas-liquid slug-flow microchannel to synthesize protein nanostructures and enzyme nanocapsules, compared to the single-phase flow microchannel, microstructured continuous stirred-tank reactor (micro-CSTR), and batch reactor. Mixing, residence time distribution, and shear effects in these reactors were examined via experiments and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations, linking them to the properties of prepared protein nanoparticles. Results show that the slug-flow microchannel provides efficient mixing, narrow residence time distribution, and suitable shear. This combination offers significant advantages for the uniformity of particle size distribution, drug release, enzyme activity, and stability in both the thermodynamically driven self-assembly of nanoparticles and kinetically driven synthesis of nanocapsules. Specifically, for catalase nanocapsules, this strategy achieved a low PDI of 0.165 (vs. 0.3-0.5 in references) and a productivity of 4 g·day-1, equivalent to 100 lab-scale batch reactors. This demonstrates the strategy’s strong potential for industrial-scale production and biomedical application of precious protein nanoparticles. Subject terms: Synthesis and processing, Microfluidics

Introduction

With breakthrough advancements in modern biotechnology, protein nanoparticles are offering new revolutionary tools for fields such as drug delivery, vaccine development, and gene therapy1–4. As an important type of protein nanoparticles, niosomes (nio) are capable of encapsulating hydrophilic and hydrophobic compounds within self-assembled vesicular structures, making them a promising platform for drug delivery. Typically, the self-assembly of protein-based niosomes is achieved by rapid solvent exchange5, which often exhibits dynamic and non-equilibrium characteristics6. Inadequate assembly can easily lead to rupture of niosomes, reducing stability of the system, especially for sensitive biomaterials7. In-situ polymerization synthesis of enzyme nanocapsule as a form of protein nanoparticle, initiates free-radical polymerization directly at the enzyme surface, forming a cross-linked polymeric shell that encapsulates the enzyme core within a protective hydrogel network – effectively creating a nano-confined biocatalyst with the enzyme as the catalytic center shielded by flexible polymer matrix. Such nanocapsulation offers significant advantages, including enhanced enzymatic stability against environmental stressors8,9 and enhanced pharmacokinetic profiles10. However, it is difficult to achieve scalable production, fundamentally impeded by the limitations of conventional batch reactors11,12. This challenge primarily results from two aspects: (1) inappropriate shear effects on the fragile enzyme structure and inducing enzyme denaturation; (2) non-uniform mixing resulting in inconsistent modification or immobilization during the polymerization process13. Therefore, the development of efficient and controllable preparation strategies for protein nanoparticles with uniform size distribution, stability, and drug release properties is of crucial importance for advancing the fields of precision medicine and synthetic biology14.

In recent years, microreaction technology has demonstrated great potential in the biomedical field, leveraging its unique microscale effects and precise manipulation capabilities15–18. This technology enables nanoliter-scale precise distribution of reactants, rapid mixing, and ordered transport of multiphase fluids through micron-sized channel networks, significantly overcoming the spatiotemporal control limitations of conventional preparation methods19–21. Recently, Chen et al. prepared mRNA-loaded lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) using two representative microreactors, including a T-junction microreactor and a ring microreactor with comparable mixing performance22. Both these two microreactors were found to yield mRNA-loaded LNPs with comparable sizes at high monodispersity, mRNA encapsulation efficiencies, and proportions of empty LNPs. Notably, the T-junction microreactor produced a higher proportion of bleb LNPs, while the ring microreactor predominantly formed spherical and smaller LNPs, which exhibited more efficient transfection, enhanced cellular uptake, and faster liver accumulation through in vitro and in vivo studies and fluorescent labeling experiments.

Regarding the preparation of some protein nanoparticles with intrinsically ultra-fast processes (e.g., the required time on the order of milliseconds and even sub-milliseconds), basic microstructured devices, i.e., microchannels, are hard to provide sufficient mixing performance due to low values of Reynolds number, leading to the laminar flow regime for single-phase processing. In this case, the mixing is mainly driven by molecular diffusion, and the diffusive time strongly depends on the diffusion length. A reduction in the characteristic dimension of microchannels can improve the mixing efficiency23, but it eventually results in increased pressure drop or reduced productivity. Typically, in terms of mixing enhancement, different passive microreactors have been developed, such as interdigital microreactor, split-and-recombine microreactor, microreactor based on the collision of micro-segments (e.g., ring microreactors), while active microreactors are fabricated with integrated active components, including ultrasonic microreactor, electrodynamic microreactor, magnetic microreactor, and so on. Microstructured continuous stirred-tank reactor (micro-CSTR) based on magnetic stirring shows excellent mixing efficiency and can be applicable for the reaction systems involving the formation of solids with a high content without channel blockage24–26.

Specifically, the gas-liquid slug flow formed within microchannels, comprising regularly alternating discrete gas bubbles and segmented liquid slugs27,28. In contrast to single-phase flow microchannels, the specific flow architecture (i.e., internal circulation) and enhanced mixing performance inherent to gas-liquid slug-flow microchannels facilitate more uniform concentration distribution of substrates and narrower residence time distribution, which holds significant applications for measurement of fast reaction kinetics parameters, and preparation of quantum dots, polymer particles, and protein particles29–32. Even though microfluidic strategies are widely applied for controllable synthesis of diverse particles, shear effects that are highly associated with mixing efficiency in microreactors have not been distinctly investigated. More importantly, the activity of proteins, especially sensitive proteins, might be significantly damaged due to local high shear stress during the preparation process of protein particles/nanoparticles in microreactors.

In this work, we prepared nanoparticles from fragile proteins using various microreactors, including the gas-liquid slug-flow microchannel, single-phase flow microchannel, micro-CSTR, and batch reactor for comparison. The mixing performance, residence time distribution, and shear effects in these reactors were characterized by both experimental methods and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulation. Two types of sensitive protein nanoparticles, i.e., niosomes and catalase nanocapsules, were prepared by self-assembly and in-situ polymerization, respectively. As a key indicator, the uniformity of particle size distribution was measured for the prepared protein nanoparticles, demonstrating the superiority of the gas-liquid slug-flow microchannel. Other important properties of niosomes and catalase nanocapsules, such as drug release performance, enzyme activity, and stability, also observed the advantages of this slug-flow microfluidic strategy, showing great potential for the large-scale production and biomedical applications of sensitive protein nanoparticles.

Results and discussion

The segregation index (XS) experimentally determined by the Villermaux-Dushman method was used to evaluate mixing efficiency. The value of XS is 0 for a perfect mixing state, and 1 for a fully segregated state. The experimental results indicate that the mixing efficiency in the gas-liquid slug-flow microchannel, single-phase flow microchannel, and micro-CSTR increased with increasing flow velocity, as depicted in Fig. 1a. It can be seen that the mixing efficiency in the slug-flow microchannel was higher than that in the single-phase flow microchannel for the same liquid flow velocity, while the mixing efficiency in the micro-CSTR was the highest due to the strong miniaturized magnetic stirring. The mixing state of different flow rates could be directly observed in a microchannel (i.e., 1.0 mm I.D. PFA microchannel) using an aqueous solution with methyl orange as a tracer and pure water. Both solutions were contacted and mixed through a T-junction micromixer at the same flow velocity, and the flow state was photographed at 10 cm from the outlet of the micromixer. For the single-phase flow, the mixing in the microchannel mainly relied on molecular diffusion, and the mixing efficiency was low. For the gas-liquid slug-flow operation, the two miscible aqueous solutions and nitrogen were introduced into the microchannel through two T-junction micromixers. The mixing efficiency in the slug-flow microchannel was remarkably higher than the single-phase flow microchannel, due to the secondary flow disturbance from internal circulation in the liquid slugs. As shown in Fig. 1(b), at the same distance from the outlet of the T-junction micromixer, the miscible aqueous solutions in the slug-flow microchannel have completed the mixing process, while the diffusion in the single-phase flow microchannel was still incomplete.

a The segregation index (XS) at different flow rates in four reactors. b Comparison of flow status between the single-phase flow (i, QL = 1 mL·min−1) and gas-liquid slug flow (ii, QG = QL = 1 mL·min−1) at a specific zone of the microchannel (approximately a 10 cm distance from the outlet of the connected micromixer).

The residence time distribution in reactors is crucial for reaction processes and particle synthesis. In this work, the pulse method was used to evaluate the residence time distribution in different microreactors. After the flow state was stable, a six-way valve was switched to introduce 20 μL of 0.01 mol·L−1 methyl orange solution as a tracer into a microreactor. The concentration of the tracer was continuously monitored at the reactor outlet over time, and the outlet concentration response curve was obtained. We used a photoelectric detection device developed by our group to measure the absorbance of the solution at the reactor outlet33. The liquid phase residence time distribution density function (E(t)) was calculated (see Supplementary Section 2).

As is well known, the Bodenstein number (Bo) represents the ratio of the convective mass transfer rate to the axial diffusion rate. As the Bo increases, the hydrodynamic behavior of reactors increasingly approaches an ideal plug-flow pattern. This shift minimizes axial diffusion, meaning less back mixing and ensuring a narrower residence time distribution (RTD), which is critical for achieving a uniform particle size in protein self-assembly and encapsulation. As shown in Fig. 2, the slug-flow microchannel had a much narrower residence time distribution and much higher Bo number compared with the other reactors, indicating that it is extremely close to the plug flow performance.

The shear effects in a reactor can be characterized by the shear force over a specific zone of the reactor, which can be described by the item of μLuL (i.e., the product of the viscosity of the liquid and the local linear flow velocity). As shown in Fig. 3a, the shear force in the micro-CSTR is rather strong at a liquid flow rate of 3 mL·min−1 and a magnetic stirring rate of 1500 rpm, up to 1.6 mN·m−1 at the maximum, presenting a great challenge to maintain the activity of highly sensitive materials. Compared with the micro-CSTR, the shear effects in the slug-flow and single-phase flow microchannels are closer to each other at the same liquid flow rate (Fig. 3b and 3c). The shear effects of slug flow are slightly higher compared with the single-phase flow due to the internal circulation inside the liquid slugs. The shear effects in the slug-flow microchannel are in one order of magnitude lower compared with the micro-CSTR at the same liquid flow rate, creating a milder synthetic environment for sensitive materials. For the batch reactor, the shear effects vary dramatically over the reactor, with a very low one on the upper zone and a strong one near the stirrer (Fig. 3d). Such spatially insufficient mixing and shear differences contribute to the uncontrollable synthesis of sensitive materials such as protein nanoparticles. As shown in Fig. 3e, the average shear effects in the batch reactor are significantly lower than in the microreactors. While this accounts for the high activity retention in batch for cold-model experiments, it underscores the inherent trade-off between shear effects and mixing efficiency required for controllable preparation. All the above simulations were performed according to the actual operating conditions, which can be seen in more detail in the section about the evaluation of mixing performance and the preparation of protein nanoparticles.

Preparation of nanostructures from fragile proteins

In this section, we prepared protein-based niosomes and enzyme-based nanocapsules in various reactors, including the slug-flow microchannel, single-phase flow microchannel, micro-CSTR, and batch reactor with different shear and mixing characteristics (Methods, Procedure for preparation of niosomes), aiming to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of thermodynamic-driven self-assembly of nanoparticles and kinetically-driven in-situ polymerization for catalase encapsulation in flow.

Preparation of protein-based niosomes through self-assembly

In this study, Oridonin (ORI) was used as a model drug molecule, while recombinant humanized collagen (COL) was the carrier protein. These were co-assembled into niosomes together with non-ionic surfactants, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 4a. Cryo-TEM images show well-defined niosomes of approximately 40 nm in diameter (Fig. 4b), consistent with the intended structural design. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements confirmed a mean particle size of ~40 nm and narrow size distribution (polydispersity index PDI = 0.177), indicating uniform vesicle formation under slug-flow conditions (Fig. 4ci).

Compared with slug flow, single-phase flow was unable to promote rapid mixing on the molecular scale (Fig. 1). The uniform assembly was not achieved during solvent exchange in the mixing process, resulting in suboptimal particle morphology and prepared niosomes with increased average diameter and broader particle size distribution (Fig. 4c (ii)). The residence time distribution (RTD) in the slug-flow microchannel was much narrower compared with the other reactors, and it has the plug flow characteristics (Fig. 2). A broader RTD can lead to an increased polydispersity of niosomes as a result of various self-assembly times and also facilitate back mixing34,35. The enhanced back mixing may cause structural disruption of niosomes upon contact with newly added ethanol. As reported by Alvarado Galindo et al.36, the incorporation of ethanol into the lipid bilayer can significantly reduce the bending rigidity of the membrane, effectively softening the niosomes. This structural alteration is often accompanied by a reduction in bilayer thickness and an increase in membrane permeability, which renders the niosomes more susceptible to rupture and deformation under the shear effects encountered during the mixing process. For the micro-CSTR and batch reactor, however, quite wide residence time distribution (especially for the latter) as well as excessive shear effects disrupted the structure-sensitive niosomes membrane, leading to vesicle rupture and re-coalescence, thus affecting the average particle size and distribution (Fig. 4c (iii, iv)).

The optimized shear environment and efficient mixing inherent to the slug-flow microchannel critically enhanced the assembly and conferred exceptional structural stability to the sensitive niosomes. The resulting niosomes exhibited remarkable colloidal stability over time, exhibiting negligible changes in both size and size distribution (PDI) during 4-day storage at 25°C (Fig. 4d). In contrast, the samples prepared by alternative reactors showed significant size growth and PDI broadening, probably attributed to the vesicle rupture and coalescence (Fig. 4d).

Therefore, the slug-flow microchannel enabled the production of niosomes, achieving high drug encapsulation efficiencies exceeding 80% (Fig. 4e), a direct result of the excellent mixing and moderate, controlled shear effects. In vitro release experiments conducted at 37°C revealed that ORI-loaded niosomes released the encapsulated drug ORI at a markedly slow rate, with approximately 60% cumulative release over 24 hours (Supplementary Fig. S10), suggesting that the niosomes can successfully achieve controlled, sustained drug release.

Preparation and characterization of Catalase nanocapsules (nCAT)

Beyond the physical self-assembly processes involved in the niosome formation, the preparation of protein-based nanocapsules represents a more intricate level of control, transitioning from physical interactions to in-situ chemical modifications. While the previous section demonstrated that the slug-flow microchannel can effectively regulate niosomes during self-assembly, the preparation of nanocapsules further challenges the reactor’s ability to protect biological structures during chemical cross-linking. In this stage, the delicate protein structure is highly susceptible to mechanical denaturation, and even transient exposure to excessive shear can compromise the enzymatic activity. Chemical modification strategies are pivotal for enzyme functionalization and immobilization, particularly to meet the strict requirements of industrial biocatalysis, such as enhanced stability under harsh operational conditions and improved reusability37. To illustrate the slug-flow microchannel in enabling controlled chemical modification processes, we focus here on the in-situ polymerization synthesis of an enzyme nanocapsule, using Catalase (CAT) as a model enzyme (Fig. 5a). We successfully realized the controllable and high-throughput synthesis of nCAT by leveraging the stable shear-induced mixing environment in the slug-flow microchannel. The results show that nCAT presented a spherical to ellipsoidal shape, with diameters of ~13 nm (Fig. 5b). Moreover, compared with previous in-situ polymerization studies in batch reactors8, this slug-flow microfluidic strategy increased the production capacity of enzyme nanocapsules to 4 g·day−1, which is equivalent to the production capacity of 100 batch reactors (based on a benchmark of 5 mg enzyme per reactor with a 4-hour preparation cycle).

To further demonstrate the advantages of conducting surface radical polymerization within slug flow, this study proceeded to prepare nCAT using three other reactors (i.e., the single-phase flow microchannel, micro-CSTR, and batch reactor) under the same conditions. Additionally, cold-model experimental control groups were set up to compare the effects of shear conditions among different reactors on enzyme activity without polymerization (Methods, Catalase processing), and to illustrate the shell encapsulation effects. Cold-model experiments demonstrated the impact of shear effects to enzyme structure (Table S1), which was proved by circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy (Methods, Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy). Among them, gentle batch processing was the most effective in maintaining the native structure (66.9% α-helix, 13.4% turns) and enzymatic activity residue (92.4%). Exposure to shear force generally led to a decrease in α-helix content and an increase in β-sheet and random coil content, indicative of protein unfolding and loss of ordered structure. In contrast, the significant shear effects in the micro-CSTR brought in significant structural damage, retaining only 53.2% of its activity and resulting in approximately 74% loss of turn structure. Given that turns are crucial for active sites, this finding validates that the enzyme behaves with high sensitivity to the shear effects. The slug flow and single-phase flow processing conditions caused intermediate-level damage, leading to around 52% turn loss in turn structures. Enzyme encapsulation in the micro-CSTR exhibited low activity due to encapsulating pre-damaged enzyme resulting from the intense shear effects during the cold-model experiment stage. When comparing enzyme encapsulation experiments with the cold-model experiments, a further decline in activity was observed. The CAT prepared in the micro-CSTR showed the most activity loss (~50%) (Fig. 5c), indicating the combined impacts of shear effects and chemical modification. The detailed quantitative changes in secondary structure percentages for the four enzyme processing conditions are presented in Table S1. Moreover, the CAT prepared in the micro-CSTR behaved with the highest rate of disruption of the turn structures, relating to the maintenance of the active sites38.

Following the in-situ polymerization, a significant increase in particle diameter was observed across all reactors (the slug-flow microchannel, single-phase flow microchannel, and micro-CSTR: ~18 nm; the batch reactor: ~25 nm; Fig. 5b&e), confirming successful encapsulation. Moreover, the key indicator of structural preservation during chemical modification is the enzymatic activity retention. As anticipated, the encapsulation inherently reduced activity (Fig. 5c). For our study, the nCAT prepared in the batch reactor exhibited the most severe deactivation: despite starting with the highest pre-polymerization activity (90%, indicative of minimal initial shear damage under weak mixing), post-encapsulation activity plummeted to only 20%. This drastic loss was attributed to the inherently poor mass transfer efficiency under weak shear conditions in the batch reactor, leading to incomplete, uneven polymerization and significant enzyme-enzyme cross-linking/aggregation. Supporting this, the samples from the batch reactor and micro-CSTR displayed broad particle size distributions (high PDI, Fig. 5f), reflecting uncontrolled aggregation and non-uniformity of polymerization.

In contrast, the nCAT prepared in the slug-flow and single-phase flow microchannels showed excellent activity preservation (50% and 70% retention, respectively), and the enzyme activity was much higher than that of the native CAT at extreme high temperature, acid and alkaline environment (Fig. 5d), signifying their ability to maintain enzyme structural integrity while providing adequate mass transfer for the reaction. The activity difference between these two flow regimes (50% vs. 70%) warrants examination. While slug flow yielded nanocapsules with the narrowest PDI distribution (0.165, Fig. 5f) and minimal disparity between intensity- and number-mean diameters (Fig. 5e, S9), indicating exceptional uniformity and minimal aggregation, its activity retention was lower than single-phase flow. This suggests a dual role for the gas-liquid interface characteristics of slug flow with enhanced mass transfer and potential interfacial denaturation because of the disruption to the secondary structure of enzymes39.

This study clearly demonstrates that the slug-flow microfluidic approach facilitates the high-throughput preparation of catalase nanocapsules, which matches the output of 100 lab-scale batch reactors. The synthesized nanocapsules display a homogeneous particle size distribution and maintain sufficient enzymatic activity, suggesting a proper balance between controlled polymerization and enzyme stability.

Conclusion

This study establishes a slug-flow microfluidic strategy as a superior paradigm for the preparation of sensitive protein nanoparticles by masterfully reconciling the often-conflicting demands of efficient mixing and gentle processing. By systematically evaluating the hydrodynamic performance of four different reactors, we provided a mechanistic understanding of how reactor geometry and flow regimes dictate the structural and functional properties of the sensitive protein nanoparticles.

The superior control over nanoparticle uniformity in the gas-liquid slug-flow microchannel is primarily attributed to its near-ideal plug-flow characteristics (high Bodenstein numbers), which significantly suppress axial dispersion and back mixing. For the self-assembly of niosomes, the inhibition of back mixing is crucial to prevent the uncontrolled rupture and re-coalescence of vesicles. As ethanol intercalation is known to reduce the bilayer bending rigidity, which is termed membrane softening. These weakened structures are highly susceptible to morphological disruption or irregular re-aggregation upon contact with newly added ethanol. By providing a strictly controlled environment with negligible back mixing, the slug-flow microchannel mitigates detrimental solvent effects, ensuring a narrow polydispersity (PDI 0.177). This spatio-temporal consistency extends to the in-situ encapsulation of catalase nanocapsules (nCAT), where uniform reaction kinetics within each liquid slug leads to lower PDI (0.165) compared with the reported preparation results40,41 (PDI in the range of 0.3-0.5). Unlike the micro-CSTR, where localized shear effects often exceed the mechanical stability threshold of protein structure, the slug-flow microchannel avoids such excessive shear according to CFD simulation. This protection is essential for preventing the irreversible conformational changes and denaturation of sensitive protein nanoparticles during the encapsulation process.

Furthermore, the platform’s industrial relevance is demonstrated by its high-throughput capability, achieving productivity of 4 g·day−1 equivalent to production capacity of 100 lab-scale batch reactors. Moving forward, this work offers future directions, including but not limited to optimizing slug flow dynamics, integrating inline monitoring for adaptive control, and extending the platform to other sensitive biologics like mRNA vaccines. Hopefully, this strategy will bridge lab innovation to industrial-scale manufacturing of nanomedicines and biocatalysts.

Methods

Characterization of mixing efficiency and shear effects

The mixing performance in a reactor can be evaluated by the Villermaux-Dushman method42 based on a parallel competing reaction system (R1-R3), which is extremely sensitive to the degree of mixing and is often used to study the mixing efficiency of the reactor. The neutralization reaction (R1) is quasi-instantaneous, while the redox reaction (R2) is fast but slower than R1. And reaction (R3) is an instantaneous equilibrium reaction. Meanwhile, we applied the COMSOL Multiphysics 6.2 CFD module to solve the Navier-Stokes equations to simulate the shear effects in different reactors. For visualization studies, we used experimental methods similar to those shown in our previous study43. Images were captured at a specific zone of the microchannel (approximately a 10 cm distance from the outlet of the connected micromixer) within slug flow and single-phase flow by a high-speed camera. We used a homemade photoelectric detection device to determine the residence time distribution33. Methyl orange was used as a tracer, and the change in the absorbance of the outlet solution was measured to characterize the residence time distribution. In this study, we characterized the mixing efficiency and shear effects and residence time distribution in reactors by the above methods, and the detailed operation and characterization methods are described in the Supplementary Section 2.

Procedure for preparation of niosomes

Batch condition: 30 mg ORI and 15 mg APG were dissolved in 2 ml 75% ethanol and ultrasonicated at 200 W for 10 min, and this solution was injected into a 15 ml glass vial (as a batch reactor) containing 10 ml COL (3 mg·mL−1) PBS solution at the rate of 100 μL·min−1, which was stirred rate of 200 rpm for 20 min. Then the assembly of niosomes was accomplished. Preparation was accomplished in other reactors with the same ratio and component concentrations as mentioned in Supplementary Section 3.

Drug-loading and physicochemical stability evaluation of ORI@Col niosomes

To validate the efficiency of drug encapsulation in ORI@Col nio, the free drug in nanoparticles was eliminated by ultrafiltration centrifugation, which was then determined. The encapsulation rate was calculated using HPLC. The ORI@Col nio samples were incubated at 25°C for 4 days, and the Zetasizer NanoZS instrument (Malvern, UK) was used daily to assess the particle size and its distribution.

In Vitro drug release measurement

The dialysis bag was fitted with ORI@Col nio. Subsequently, such a bag was immersed in simulated body fluid at 37°C. At designated intervals (5 min, 10 min, 15 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 10 h, and 24 h), the bag was extracted, and the drug content was determined using an Agilent Technologies 1260 infinity HPLC system. The release percentage of the drug was calculated by the cumulative release (Ct×Vt+∑Ci×ViC×Vo×100%, where C is the initial drug concentration and Vo is the initial volume in the dialysis bag, Ct is the drug concentration and Vt is the extracted volume at the specified time point, ∑Ci×Vi means the sum weight of the extracted drug).

Catalase processing: Cold-model experiments and enzyme encapsulation experiments

Cold-model experiments (control groups)

The catalase (with a final concentration of 2 mg·mL−1 in phosphate buffer (PB) at pH of 7.0) was processed using different reactors, and then an environment mimicking a polymerization condition was created without adding monomers or initiators.

Enzyme encapsulation experiments

The in-situ polymerization was carried out on acrylate-modified catalase using different reactors to form a polymer shell.

Reactor conditions

Batch processing with gentle mixing; Slug-flow microchannel; Single-phase flow microchannel; Micro-CSTR.

Implementation detail

All processing and reactions were performed at 37°C. For the enzyme encapsulation in the batch reactor, the reaction time was 5 min, while the residence time was 30 seconds for the continuous-flow enzyme encapsulation in the slug-flow microchannel, single-phase flow microchannel, or micro-CSTR. Detailed protocols for protein modification (NAS chemistry), concentrations of reagents (catalase, DEAEMA/NVP monomers, TEGDA crosslinker, APS/TEMED initiators), specific reactor configurations (PFA microchannel dimensions, flow rates, residence time, etc.), and purification procedures are provided in Supplementary Section 4.

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy

Secondary structure analysis of pure catalase processed under various conditions of the cold-model experiments was carried out using a JASCO J-1500 spectropolarimeter. Far-UV spectra (190–250 nm) were recorded at 25°C. The quantitative estimation of secondary structural elements (α-helix, β-sheet, turns, random coil) was achieved using Reed’s Reference method. Experimental parameters, data processing steps (including conversion to mean residue ellipticity), and detailed analysis are provided in Supplementary Section 5.

Catalase activity assay

The enzymatic activity of all samples derived from the cold-model experiments, enzyme encapsulation experiments, and stability tests was quantified by spectrophotometrically monitoring the decomposition rate of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) at 240 nm. Activity was reported relative to an unmodified catalase standard. The detailed assay protocol, encompassing sample dilutions and reaction conditions, is described in Supplementary Section 5.
