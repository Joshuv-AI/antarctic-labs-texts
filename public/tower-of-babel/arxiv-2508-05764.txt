Stochastic Trace Optimization of Parameter Dependent Matrices Based on Statistical Learning Theory Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Arvind K. Saibaba, Ilse C.F. Ipsen

Abstract
We consider matrices 𝑨 ⁡ ( 𝛉 ) ∈ ℝ m × m \boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m} that depend, possibly nonlinearly, on a parameter 𝛉 \boldsymbol{\uptheta} from a compact parameter space Θ \Theta . We present a Monte Carlo estimator for minimizing trace ⁡ ( 𝐀 ⁡ ( 𝛉 ) ) \trace(\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})) over all 𝛉 ∈ Θ \boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta , and determine the sampling amount so that the backward error of the estimator is bounded with high probability. We derive two types of bounds, based on epsilon nets and on generic chaining. Both types predict a small sampling amount for matrices 𝑨 ⁡ ( 𝛉 ) \boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}) with small offdiagonal mass, and parameter spaces Θ \Theta of small “size.” Dependence on the matrix dimension m m is only weak or not explicit. The bounds based on epsilon nets are easier to evaluate and come with fully specified constants. In contrast, the bounds based on chaining depend on the Talagrand functionals which are difficult to evaluate, except in very special cases. Comparisons between the two types of bounds are difficult, although the literature suggests that chaining bounds can be superior.

Introduction

The goal is to optimize the trace of parameter dependent
matrices.
We consider symmetric matrices
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}

and regularization terms
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})

that depend, possibly nonlinearly, on parameters
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}
from a compact
parameter space
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{K}
. The objective
is to minimize, over all elements of
\Theta
,

\min_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}\{F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})+\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\}\qquad\text{where\quad}F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\equiv\trace(\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})).

(1)

Optimization problems of this form arise in optimal experimental design
[
1
]
, Gaussian processes
[
2
,
12
]
, biclustered matrix completion
[
8
]
, and
Bayesian inverse problems
[
9
]

discussed in Section
2
.

In the above applications, it is prohibitively
expense to compute the matrix
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
explicitly, while the
regularization term
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
does not contribute a significant computational cost. This means, the computation of the trace is a major bottleneck in large-scale implementations. A popular approximation of the trace relies on the computation
of an optimizer
\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}
by means of a Monte Carlo estimator

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}\{\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})+\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\}\qquad\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{\upomega}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\boldsymbol{\upomega}_{i}}.

Here

\boldsymbol{\upomega}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are independent random vectors satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\upomega}_{i}]=\boldsymbol{0}

and
1
1

1

Here

\boldsymbol{I}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
denotes the
m\times m
identity matrix.

\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\upomega}_{i}\boldsymbol{\upomega}_{i}^{\top}]=\boldsymbol{I}_{m}
, so that
\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
is an unbiased estimator of
F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
. This is an example of stochastic average approximation (SAA) optimization
[
19
]
. SAA is computationally attractive,
since any appropriate deterministic optimization routine can be applied to
\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
, and the random vectors
\boldsymbol{\upomega}_{i}
can be reused in different
iterations.

The statistical properties of SAA estimators
\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}
in the asymptotic regime as
N\rightarrow\infty
have been studied in great detail
[
19
]
. In practice, one can only draw a finite number of samples
N
, hence
it is important to understand the accuracy and the statistical properties of SAA estimators in the non-asymptotic setting. Our goal is to quantify the probabilistic backward error
F(\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}})+\mathcal{R}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}})-(F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})+\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*}))
, where
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*}
is a minimizer of (
1
).

There is a lot of existing work on stochastic methods for estimating the trace of a single, fixed matrix:
[
3
,
10
,
13
,
14
,
18
]
just to name a few. The methods make use of concentration inequalities to derive the minimal sampling amount required for a
desired relative error with high probability. However, they are only capable of estimating the accuracy of the objective function at a single point
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta
, and they do not give information about the accuracy of the SAA estimator, or the accuracy of the objective function in the optimization trajectory.
In contrast, we derive
non-asymptotic bounds on the backward error with
tools from high-dimensional probability and statistical learning theory.

1.1
Related work

To our knowledge, ours are the first non-asymptotic SAA estimators for trace optimization.

The stochastic trace estimators for parameter dependent
matrices in
[
17
]
assume that the parameter

\boldsymbol{\uptheta}
is a scalar and that the elements
of
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
depend continuously on
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}
.
Since the problem in
[
17
]
is not an optimization problem, the
same Gaussian random vectors are re-used for different
values of
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}
.
Because these stochastic trace estimators rely on quadratic forms with random vectors, they represent an instance of second-order chaos

[
6
,
16
,
20
]
.

Contributions

We present probabilistic bounds on the sampling amount
N
for
Hutchinson’s trace estimator with Rademacher vectors.

•

This is the one of the first investigations
into the potential of statistical
learning theory for numerical linear algebra
problems.

•

In contrast to existing asymptotic analyses of SAA estimators in
[
19
, Chapter 5]
and
[
2

,
our approach produces
quantitative and computable error bounds for a finite

•

In contrast to spectral density estimation methods
[
17
]

\boldsymbol{\uptheta}
.

•

We derive bounds from epsilon nets (Section

and express them in

sampling amount

is small,
if the maximal offdiagonal

\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
is small and if the

\Theta
is small.

1.3
Main results

•

For finite parameter spaces
\Theta
, the
sampling amount
N
does not depend on the matrix
dimension
m
, if the offdiagonal mass of the matrices
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
is small and the
cardinality of
\Theta
is small (Theorem
5
). There is no dependence
on the matrix dimension
m
.

•

For infinite parameter spaces
\Theta
,
the bound on
the sampling amount
N
is the same as the bound
for finite spaces, except that the cardinality
of
\Theta
is replaced by the covering number
of the epsilon net which features a weak dependence
on the matrix dimension
m
through the logarithm
(Theorem
7
).

•

The bounds from chaining
(Theorems
13
and
16
)
do not require the Lipschitz continuity of
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
but have the disadvantage of depending on

•

For spherical parameter spaces
\Theta
the bounds
on the sampling amount

also
depend on the dimension and the radius of
\Theta

however they feature unknown constants
(Corollaries
19
and
20
).

•

Although the literature suggests that chaining bounds can be superior, we found
comparisons of chaining and epsilon net bounds difficult
(Remarks
5.1
and
5.4
).
The epsilon nets bounds are easier to evaluate and come with fully specified constants. In contrast, the
chaining bounds depend on the Talagrand functionals which are difficult to evaluate, except in very special cases.

2
Application to Bayesian inverse problems

We illustrate how Bayesian inverse problems give rise to trace optimization problems of the form (
1
).
After reviewing the basics of Bayesian inverse problems,
we discuss specific applications in

Optimal Experimental Design
(Section
2.1
) and
Hyperparameter estimation
(Section
2.2
).

Given a parameter vector
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a
forward operator
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
the
data acquisition
or

forward problem
consists of
computing a vector
\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
so that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}=\boldsymbol{F}\mathbf{m}+\boldsymbol{\upepsilon},\qquad\text{where}\qquad\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{m}).

(2)

The measurement noise
\boldsymbol{\upepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is normally distributed with zero mean and covariance
\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{m}
.
On the other hand,
given a data vector
\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and a
forward operator
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
the
inverse problem
consists of recovering the parameter vector
\mathbf{m}
.
In many applications, (
2
) is underdetermined with
m\leq n
, and, thus, ill-posed.

The Bayesian approach to inverse problems puts a prior distribution
on
\mathbf{m}
, where the prior is a Gaussian distribution

\mathbf{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}_{\rm pr},\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\rm pr})
.
Application of Bayes rule gives the posterior distribution
\mathbf{m}|\boldsymbol{d}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}_{\rm post},\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\rm post})
, where

\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\rm post}\equiv(\sigma^{-2}\boldsymbol{F}^{\top}\boldsymbol{F}+\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\rm pr}^{-1})^{-1}\qquad\mathbf{m}_{\rm post}\equiv\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\rm post}(\sigma^{-2}\boldsymbol{F}^{\top}\boldsymbol{d}+\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\rm pr}^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{\rm pr}).

The following two Bayesian optimization problems can be cast in the form (
1
).

2.1
Optimal Experimental Design

Data are important in making inference about parameters, but
the data acquisition process can be laborious and expensive.
Optimal Experimental Design (OED)
seeks to optimize this process subject to budgetary constraints. In the OED formulation of
sensor placement
, one seeks an optimal placement of
k
sensors at
m>k
candidate locations.

Bayesian D-optimality places the sensors to maximize the expected information gain, which is defined as the expected Kullback-Leibler divergence from the prior to the posterior distribution. The D-optimality problem is of the form

\max_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}\in\Theta}\left\{\log\det(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{\Theta}\boldsymbol{G}^{\top})\right\},

where
\boldsymbol{G}\equiv\sigma^{-1}\mathbf{\Gamma}^{1/2}_{\rm pr}\boldsymbol{F}^{\top}
,
and

\boldsymbol{\Theta}\equiv\text{diag}\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{1}&\cdots&\theta_{m}\end{pmatrix}

is a diagonal matrix with diagonal elements from
the set

\Theta=\left\{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\{0,1\}^{m}\,|\>\sum_{j=1}^{m}\theta_{i}=k\right\}.

With the identity
\log\det(\mathbf{K})=\trace(\log(\mathbf{K}))
for nonsingular matrices
\mathbf{K}
, the D-optimal design problem can be cast as (
1
) with

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})=-\log(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{\Theta}\boldsymbol{G}^{\top}),\qquad\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})=0.

In the context of sensor placement,
\theta_{i}
is
1
or
0
, meaning a sensor should either be placed at
or else excluded from the
i
th candidate location,
1\leq i\leq m
.

2.2
Hyperparameter estimation

In many applications
[
9
]
, the prior distribution is expressed in terms of unknown hyperparameters
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
that must be estimated from the data.

The
hierarchical Bayesian
approach treats the hyperparameters as random variables and posits a hyperprior
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\sim\pi(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
, to model the parameter vector
as
\mathbf{m}|\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}_{\rm pr}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}),\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\rm pr}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))
.
Application of Bayes rule gives the joint posterior distribution

\pi_{\rm post}(\mathbf{m},\boldsymbol{\uptheta}|\boldsymbol{d})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\boldsymbol{d}-\boldsymbol{F}\mathbf{m}\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{m}_{\rm pr}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\|_{\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\rm pr}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})^{-1}}^{2}\right)\pi(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}),

where
\propto
denotes proportionality up to a constant. This
joint distribution is non-Gaussian, and the joint estimation of
\mathbf{m}
and
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}
is, in general, computationally expensive.

As an alternative, the
empirical Bayesian approach
determines the posterior distribution
\pi(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}|\boldsymbol{d})
by marginalizing over
\mathbf{m}
,

\pi(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}|\boldsymbol{d})\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det{\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})}}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{d}-\boldsymbol{F}\mathbf{m}_{\rm pr}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\|_{\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})^{-1}}^{2}\right)\pi(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}),

where
\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\equiv\boldsymbol{F}\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\rm pr}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\boldsymbol{F}^{\top}+\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{m}
, and computes the maximum a posteriori estimate by minimizing the negative log-likelihood,

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}}\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{d}-\boldsymbol{F}\mathbf{m}_{\rm pr}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\|_{\rm\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})^{-1}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\log\det\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-\ln\pi(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}).

(3)

As in Section
2.1
, the identity
\log\det(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))=\trace(\log(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})))

allows this problem to be cast as (
1
) with

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})=\log(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})),\qquad\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})=\|\boldsymbol{d}-\boldsymbol{F}\mathbf{m}_{\rm pr}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\|_{\rm\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})^{-1}}^{2}-2\ln\pi(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}).

Although the problem ranges over
\mathbb{R}^{K}
, we can restrict it to a compact set
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{K}
.
The detailed numerical computation of (
3
)
is discussed in
[
9
]
.

3.1
Concentration inequalities

We present two different concentration inequalities that
lead to two different trace bounds, and ultimately two different sampling amounts for trace optimization.

Hoeffding’s inequality below is the basis for the
trace bound in Lemma
3
.

Theorem 1
(Theorem 2.2.6 in

[
23
]
).

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
be independent scalar random variables with
a_{i}\leq X_{i}\leq b_{i}

for
0\leq a_{i}\leq b_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq N
. Let

\hat{X}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{X_{i}}
. Then for any
t>0

\displaystyle\myP\left[|\hat{X}-\E[\hat{X}]|\geq t\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{2N^{2}t^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}{(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}}\right).

The inequality below is the basis for the trace bound in Lemma
4
.

Theorem 2
(Theorem 2 in
[
10
]
).

Let
\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
be symmetric with
b_{jj}=0
,
1\leq j\leq m
, and
\boldsymbol{\upomega}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

be a Rademacher vector, then

\displaystyle\myP\left[|\boldsymbol{\upomega}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{\upomega}|\geq t\right]\leq 2\,\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{8(\|\boldsymbol{B}\|_{F}^{2}+t\|\boldsymbol{B}\|_{2})}\right).

3.2
Hutchinson’s trace estimator

We review properties and bounds for Hutchinson’s trace estimator with Rademacher vectors
[
13
,
14
]
.

A Rademacher vector
\boldsymbol{\upomega}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
has independent elements
\omega_{j}=\pm 1
, each chosen with probability
1/2
,
1\leq j\leq m
.

Given
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and a Rademacher vector
\boldsymbol{\upomega}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the quadratic form

\boldsymbol{\upomega}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\upomega}
is an unbiased
estimator
[
14
, Proposition 1]
,
that is,

\displaystyle\E[\boldsymbol{\upomega}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\upomega}]=\trace(\boldsymbol{A})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}}.

Extract the offdiagonal part of
\boldsymbol{A}
,

\displaystyle\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}\equiv\boldsymbol{A}-\diag(\boldsymbol{A}).

(4)

If
\boldsymbol{A}
is also symmetric, then the variance can be expressed as

\displaystyle\V[\boldsymbol{\upomega}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\upomega}]=2\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{F}^{2},

because the elements of
\boldsymbol{\upomega}
satisfy
\omega_{j}^{2}=1
.
Furthermore, since all elements of
\boldsymbol{\upomega}
are bounded by one in magnitude,
the random variable

\boldsymbol{\upomega}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\upomega}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\trace(\boldsymbol{A})-\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{M}\leq\boldsymbol{\upomega}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\upomega}\leq\trace(\boldsymbol{A})+\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{M},

(5)

where for any matrix
\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
we define the norm

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{B}\|_{M}\equiv\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\sum_{j=1}^{m}{|b_{ij}|}}=\|\myvec(\boldsymbol{B})\|_{1}.

(6)

For
N\geq 1
independent Rademacher vectors
\boldsymbol{\upomega}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the
Monte Carlo trace estimator

\displaystyle\hat{F}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{\upomega}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\upomega}_{i}}

(7)

is an unbiased estimator with

\displaystyle\E[\hat{F}]=\trace(\boldsymbol{A}),\qquad\V[\hat{F}]=\frac{1}{N}\V[\boldsymbol{\upomega}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\upomega}_{i}]=\frac{2}{N}\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{F}^{2},

(8)

since the vectors
\boldsymbol{\upomega}_{i}
are independent.

The deviation from expectation of Hutchinson’s estimator can be bounded for
general matrices
\boldsymbol{A}
by means of Hoeffding’s inequality.
Tail bounds of the form below are called subgaussian
[
23
, Section 2.5]
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
,
\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}
as defined in (
4
),
\hat{F}
as defined
in (
7
), and
t>0
. Then

\displaystyle\myP\left[|\hat{F}-\trace(\boldsymbol{A})|\geq t\right]\leq\exp\left(\frac{-Nt^{2}}{2\,\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{M}^{2}}\right),

where
\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{F}\leq\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{M}\leq m\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{F}
.

Proof.

Set
X_{i}\equiv\boldsymbol{\upomega}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\upomega}}_{i}
,

1\leq i\leq N
. From (
5
) follows that
a_{i}\leq X_{i}\leq b_{i}
with

\displaystyle a_{i}=\trace(\boldsymbol{A})-\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{M},\qquad b_{i}=\trace(\boldsymbol{A})+\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{M},\qquad 1\leq i\leq N.

Then
\sum_{i=1}^{N}{(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}=4N\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{M}^{2}.
Now apply Theorem
1
.

For the bounds on
\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{M}
, apply the vector norm relations to

\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{F}=\|\myvec(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{2}
and

\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{M}=\|\myvec(\boldsymbol{A})\|_{1}
, and use the fact that
\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}
has
at most
m(m-1)\leq m^{2}
non-zero elements.

Lemma
3
implies that the probability of

\hat{F}
being an accurate estimator increases
with decreasing
offdiagonal mass
\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{M}
of
\boldsymbol{A}
.

The bound below is an alternative to Lemma
3
. It
requires a symmetric matrix, although a real nonsymmetric matrix

\boldsymbol{B}
can be replaced by its symmetric part,

\mathbf{x}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}\mathbf{x}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{B}+\boldsymbol{B}^{\top})\mathbf{x}

[
10
, Section 1]
.
Tail bounds of the form below are referred to as
mixed tails since they represent a mixture of subgaussian and subexponential tails
[
23
, Section 2.8]
.

Lemma 4
(Corollary 1 in
[
10
]
).

Let
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
be symmetric,
\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}
as defined
in (
4
),
\hat{F}
as defined in (
7
) and
t>0
. Then

\displaystyle\myP\left[|\hat{F}-\trace(\boldsymbol{A})|\geq t\right]\leq 2\exp\left(\frac{-Nt^{2}}{8(\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{F}^{2}+t\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}\|_{2})}\right).

Like Lemma
3
, Lemma
4
implies that the probability of

\hat{F}
being an accurate estimator, for a fixed
t>0
, increases
with decreasing
offdiagonal mass of
\boldsymbol{A}
.

4.1
Assumptions

Let
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
be a set of symmetric matrices that depend on parameters
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}
from a
compact parameter space
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{K}
.

We use the following abbreviations that focus on the
parameters
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}
.
The trace and its desired minimizer
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*}
are
given by

\displaystyle F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\equiv\trace(\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})),\qquad F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})\equiv\min_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}{F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})}.

(9)

In practice, we can only compute an estimator and its minimizer
\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}
, based
on
N\geq 1
independent Rademacher vectors

\boldsymbol{\upomega}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
given by

\displaystyle\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\boldsymbol{\upomega}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\boldsymbol{\upomega}_{i}},\qquad\hat{F}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}})\equiv\min_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}{\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})}.

From (
8
) follows that

\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
is an unbiased estimator,

\displaystyle\E[\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})]=F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\qquad\text{for all}\ \boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta.

(10)

Remark 4.1
.

In a number of applications, the optimization problem (
1
) contains a regularization term
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
. Since the Monte Carlo estimator does not involve this additional term, we assume without loss of generality that
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})=0
. Extending the results to include
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
should be straightforward.

Strategy

In general, we do not know how the matrices

\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})

depend on the parameters
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}
, so bounding the
forward error

\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}-\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*}
is not feasible. Instead,
we bound the backward error
F(\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}})-F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})
of
the computed
\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}
.
Motivated by statistical learning theory

[
15
]
,

[
23
, Section 8.4]
, we interpret this
backward error as an ‘excess risk’.
This also allows us to ignore uniqueness issues of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}
and
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*}
in this general context.
Under specific assumptions
[
2
, Section 2.1]
, though, it is possible to show
local uniqueness.
Below we bound the excess risk in terms of the ‘overall loss function’
\max_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}{|F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})|}
.

Remark 4.2
.

Under the above assumptions

\displaystyle 0\leq F(\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}})-F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})\leq 2\max_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}{|F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})|}.

The lower bound follows from
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*}
being a minimizer for
F
.
The upper bound follows from
\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}
being a minimizer for
\hat{F}

[
15
, Section 2.1]
,

\displaystyle F(\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}})-F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq F(\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}})-\hat{F}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}})+\underbrace{\hat{F}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}})-\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})}_{\leq 0}+\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})-F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq|F(\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}})-\hat{F}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}})|+|\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})-F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})|\leq 2\max_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}{|\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})|}.

All that remains is to bound the overall loss
function
\max_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}{|F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})|}
. To this end, we need the following
ingredients. Analogous to (
4
),
define the offdiagonal part
of a matrix
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
by

\displaystyle\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\equiv\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-\diag(\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})).

(11)

The maximal norms of the offdiagonal part are

\displaystyle\alpha_{\xi}\equiv\max_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}{\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\|_{\xi}},\qquad\xi\in\{2,F,M\},

(12)

where
\|\cdot\|_{M}
is defined in (

).

4.2
Finite parameter space

For a parameter space
\Theta
with finite cardinality

, we determine two expressions for the sampling amount

so that the backward error

is bounded by

Theorem 5
.

Given the assumptions in Section
4.1
,
let
0<\epsilon<1
and
0<\delta<1
.
If

\displaystyle N\geq\frac{8}{\epsilon^{2}}\,\alpha_{M}^{2}\,\ln(|\Theta|/\delta),\quad\text{or}\quad N\geq\frac{16}{\epsilon^{2}}\,(2\alpha_{F}^{2}+\epsilon\alpha_{2})\ln(2\,|\Theta|/\delta)

then
0\leq F(\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}})-F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})<\epsilon
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

For the first bound, let
t>0
, and apply Lemma
3
and the union bound,

\displaystyle\myP[\max_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}|F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})|\geq t]\leq\sum_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}\exp\left(\frac{-Nt^{2}}{2\,\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\|_{M}^{2}}\right)\leq|\Theta|\exp\left(\frac{-Nt^{2}}{2\,\alpha_{M}^{2}}\right).

Set the upper bound equal to
\delta
and
t=\epsilon/2
.
Solving for
N
bounds the overall loss function by

\max_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}|F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})|\leq\epsilon/2

with

\displaystyle N\geq\frac{8}{\epsilon^{2}}\,\alpha_{M}^{2}\,\ln(|\Theta|/\delta).

At last, insert the above bound for the overall loss
into Remark
4.2
.

For the second bound, let
t>0
, and apply Lemma
4
and the union bound,

\displaystyle\myP[\max_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}|F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})|\geq t]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}2\,\exp\left(\frac{-Nt^{2}}{8(\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\|_{F}^{2}+t\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\|_{2})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\,|\Theta|\exp\left(\frac{-Nt^{2}}{8(\alpha_{F}^{2}+t\alpha_{2})}\right).

Now proceed as above:
Set the upper bound equal to
\delta
and
t=\epsilon/2
.
Solving for
N
bounds the overall loss function by

\max_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}|F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})|\leq\epsilon/2

with

\displaystyle N\geq\frac{16}{\epsilon^{2}}\,(2\alpha_{F}^{2}+\epsilon\alpha_{2})\ln(2\,|\Theta|/\delta).

At last, insert the above bound for the overall loss
into Remark
4.2
.

The sampling amount

is small if the maximal offdiagonal mass is small and

Lemma 9

Under the assumptions in Sections

1.

First bound: Lemma

Lemma 10

Under the assumptions in Sections

\displaystyle\myP\left[\max_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}{|\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})|}\geq 2t\right]\leq 2\,S\left(\frac{t}{2mL_{2}}\right)\exp\left(\frac{-Nt^{2}}{8(\alpha_{F}^{2}+t\alpha_{2})}\right).

Since

\displaystyle|F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{h})-F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0})|

\displaystyle=|\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{h})-\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}))\boldsymbol{e}_{i}}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}{|\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{h})-\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}))\boldsymbol{e}_{i}|}

Similarly, the fact that the elements of Rademacher vectors are

\displaystyle=2mL_{2}\eta+|\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0})-F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0})|

5
Bounds based on generic chaining

The literature

[
6
,
20
,
21
,
23
]
suggests that

generic chaining
can produce bounds
that are superior to those derived from epsilon nets. Indeed, for Gaussian processes, Talagrand’s majorizing measures theorem

[
23
, Theorem 8.6.1]

shows that the bounds from chaining are optimal up to constants.
However, we show that, in the context of trace optimization,
this superiority is not obvious. The bounds from
chaining do not require the Lipschitz continuity of
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
but have the disadvantage of depending on functionals that are difficult to evaluate.

In contrast to the
\eta
-net in Definition
6
, which is often called an
epsilon-net or simply a net, chaining methods rely on multiscale nets.
The idea is to set the context for chaining as follows: Represent the backward error
of the trace estimator for
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})

as an element of a stochastic process
on the parameter space
\Theta
,

X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}=F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\trace(\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))-\boldsymbol{\upomega}_{j}^{\top}\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\boldsymbol{\upomega}_{j}),\qquad\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta.

Since
\hat{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
is an unbiased estimator for
F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
, the error

X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}
has zero mean.
To avoid issues with measurability of the supremum
\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}|X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}|
, we
assume that the process

\{X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}\}
is separable
[
21
, Definition 5.22]

– an assumption that is almost always satisfied

[
21
, Remark 5.23]
.

We determine the sampling amount
N
required to bound
the backward error of the estimator
for stochastic processes on
a parameter space
\Theta
. We derive
bounds with subgaussian tails (Section
5.1
)
and mixed tails (Section
5.2
), as well as bounds
specialized to parameter spaces that are spheres (Section
5.3
).
We end with several auxiliary results (Section
5.4
).
Where possible, we track the constants explicitly,
but make no claims as to their optimality.

5.1

We derive a bound for the backward error

After defining multi-scale nets

We need the following (pseudo)-metrics

2

2

on the offdiagonal

Theorem 13

For

\myP\left[{F}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}})-F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})\geq\sqrt{\frac{8}{N}}\,u\left(2\gamma_{2}(\Theta,d_{M})+\alpha_{M}\right)\right]\leq 2\exp({-u^{2}/2}).

From

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}|X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}-X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}}|+|X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}}|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\,}{N}}\,u\,\left(2\gamma_{2}(\Theta,d_{M})+\alpha_{M}\right).

u=\sqrt{2\ln(2/\delta)}
. Then with probability at least
1-\delta
,

0\leq F(\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}})-F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{N}}\,2u\,\left(2\gamma_{2}(\Theta,d_{M})+\alpha_{M}\right)\leq\epsilon,

where the penultimate inequality follows
from the choice of
N
in (
20
).

We compare the bound on the sampling
amount from generic chaining to that from the

\eta
-net.

Remark 5.1
(Comparison of Theorems
7

and
13
).

A comparison of the two bounds for the minimal sampling amount
N
is difficult and inconclusive.

The bound in Theorem
7
is

\displaystyle N\geq\frac{8\alpha_{M}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\ln\left(\gamma/\delta\right),\qquad\text{where}\quad\gamma\equiv S\left(\frac{\epsilon}{4mL_{2}}\right)

(24)

and
S(\cdot)
denotes the covering number of the net.
The bound in Theorem
13
is

\displaystyle N\geq\frac{144}{\epsilon^{2}}\ln(2/\delta)\max\{\gamma_{2}(\Theta,d_{M})^{2},\alpha_{M}^{2}\},

(25)

where
\gamma_{2}(\Theta,d_{M})
is the Talagrand functional (
17
).

1.

Since
\hat{F}
is a Monte Carlo estimator,
both bounds grow with
-\ln(\delta)/\epsilon^{-2}
.
That is, the sampling amount increases as the desired
error
\epsilon
and the failure probability
\delta
become smaller.

2.

Both bounds depend on the “size” of
\Theta
,
either through the covering number or the Talagrand
functional. However, (
25
) with the Talagrand functional is much more difficult to evaluate.

3.

The chaining bound (
25
) has the advantage of not requiring Lipschitz continuity of
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
, while (
24
) does.

4.

The chaining bound (
25
)
might grow less with the offdiagonal
mass
\alpha_{M}
and the “size” of
\Theta

because it depends on the maximum
of
\alpha_{M}^{2}
and
\gamma_{2}(\Theta,d_{M})^{2}
, while
(
24
)
depends on the product
\alpha_{M}^{2}\ln(\gamma)
.

But then again, the chaining bound (
25
)
depends more
strongly on the “size” of
\Theta
than (
24
), which depends only
on the logarithm of the covering number.

Next is the foundation for the proof of
Theorem
13
.

An illustration of the chaining argument. We construct the chain
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}=\pi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\rightarrow\pi_{1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\rightarrow\pi_{2}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\rightarrow\pi_{3}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})=\boldsymbol{\uptheta}.
The shaded regions represent the sets
\mathcal{T}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{2}
, and
\mathcal{T}_{3}=\Theta
, with
\mathcal{T}_{0}=\{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}\}
.

Theorem 14
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}\in\Theta
and
u\geq 2
. With probability at least
1-\exp(-u^{2}/2)
,

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}|X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}-X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}}|\leq\sqrt{\frac{8}{N}}\,u\,\gamma_{2}(\Theta,d_{M}).

Proof.

The separability assumption allows us to limit
consideration to the case where
\Theta
is a finite set. An example for extensions to the infinite case in
a more general context is
given in
[
21
, Theorem 5.24]
.

The idea is to break down
\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}{|X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}-X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}}|}
into a sequence of
suprema over smaller subsets
\mathcal{T}_{i}
from an admissible sequence.
To this end,
the proof proceeds in several steps, along the lines
of
[
20
, pages 10-11]
.

1.

Concentration inequality.

Since the elements
X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}
have zero mean,
lemma

3
implies that they have subgaussian increments,

\displaystyle\myP[|X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}-X_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}|\geq t]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{Nt^{2}}{2\,d_{M}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})^{2}}\right).

Below we use the following version:
For
u>0
, integers
i\geq 1
, and change of variable

t=u\,\sqrt{2}\,2^{i/2}d_{M}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})/\sqrt{N}
the above
is equal to

\displaystyle\myP\left[|X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}-X_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}|\geq u\,\sqrt{2}\,2^{i/2}d_{M}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})/\sqrt{N}\right]\leq\exp\left(-u^{2}2^{i}\right).

(26)

2.

Constructing a chain from
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}
to another parameter.
Let
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta
, let
\{\mathcal{T}_{i}\}
with
\mathcal{T}_{0}=\{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}\}
be an admissible sequence, and let
\pi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})

be an element closest to
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}

from the subset
\mathcal{T}_{i}
, that is,

\displaystyle\pi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}d_{M}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{\prime}),\qquad i\geq 0.

Since the subsets
\mathcal{T}_{i}
are increasing
in size, the elements
\pi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
define a
chain from
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}

to
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}
,

\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}=\pi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\rightarrow\pi_{1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow\pi_{T}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})=\boldsymbol{\uptheta},

where
T
is finite because
\Theta
is finite. This is illustrated in Figure
2
.

We can decompose the distance between the processes

X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}
and
X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}}
as the telescoping sum of successive increments,

X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}-X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}}=\sum_{i=1}^{T}(X_{\pi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})}-X_{\pi_{i-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})}).

Furthermore, because
\pi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
is an element in
\mathcal{T}_{i}
closest to
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}
, the distance
between the parameters
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}
and
\pi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
is equal to the
distance between
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}
and the set
\mathcal{T}_{i}
,

\displaystyle d_{M}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\pi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))=d_{M}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\mathcal{T}_{i})\equiv\inf_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{T}_{i}}{d_{M}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})},\qquad 0\leq i\leq T.

(27)

3.

Bounding the distance between successive processes in the chain.

We define events that represent the
distance between successive processes,

E_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta},i}\equiv\left\{|X_{\pi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})}-X_{\pi_{i-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})}|\geq u\sqrt{2}\,2^{i/2}\frac{\sigma_{i}}{\sqrt{N}}\right\}\qquad\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta,\quad 1\leq i\leq T,

where
\sigma_{i}\equiv d_{M}(\pi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}),\pi_{i-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))
is the distance between the
corresponding parameters
in the chain. Then (
26
) implies

\displaystyle\myP[E_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta},i}]\leq\exp\left(-u^{2}2^{i}\right),\qquad\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta,\>1\leq i\leq T.

(28)

4.

Union bound over all possible process distances.

Since the elements in the chain are not unique, we need to sum over all possible events, that is, over all possible
parameter pairs.
The number of possible pairs

\{\pi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}),\pi_{i-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\}
is bounded by

\displaystyle|\mathcal{T}_{i}||\mathcal{T}_{i-1}|\leq|\mathcal{T}_{i}|^{2}\leq 2^{2^{i+1}},\qquad 1\leq i\leq T.

The probability of all events occurring is

\displaystyle\myP\left[\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq T}E_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta},i}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{T}|\mathcal{T}_{i}|^{2}\myP[E_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta},i}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2^{2^{i+1}}\exp\left(-u^{2}\,2^{i}\right)\leq\exp(-u^{2}/2),

(29)

where the second inequality follows from (
28
), and the third from Lemma
21
in Section
5.4
for
u\geq 2
.

The expression (
4
) is the probability
that
\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}|X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}-X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}}|
is not bounded.
Now we derive the
actual bound for this supremum by adding the
bounds in the individual
E_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta},i}
,

1\leq i\leq T
.

5.

Bounding
\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}|X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}-X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}}|
.

From (
4
) and the definition of

E_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta},i}

follows that with probability at least
1-\exp(-u^{2}/2)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}|X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}-X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}}|\leq

\displaystyle\>\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}\sum_{i=1}^{T}|X_{\pi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})}-X_{\pi_{i-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})}|\leq\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}u\sum_{i=1}^{T}\sqrt{2}\,2^{i/2}\frac{\sigma_{i}}{\sqrt{N}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}\sqrt{\frac{2}{N}}\,u\sum_{i=1}^{T}2^{i/2}(d_{M}(\pi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}),\boldsymbol{\uptheta})+d_{M}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\pi_{i-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}\sqrt{\frac{2}{N}}\,2u\sum_{i=0}^{T}2^{i/2}d_{M}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\mathcal{T}_{i})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}\sqrt{\frac{8}{N}}\,u\,\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2^{i/2}d_{M}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\mathcal{T}_{i}).

Here, the third inequality follows from the triangle inequality,

\sigma_{i}=d_{M}(\pi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}),\pi_{i-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))\leq d_{M}(\pi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}),\boldsymbol{\uptheta})+d_{M}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\pi_{i-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})),\qquad 1\leq i\leq T,

and the fourth inequality from (
27
). We have shown that with probability at least
1-\exp(-u^{2}/2)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}|X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}-X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}}|\leq\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}\sqrt{\frac{8}{N}}\,u\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}2^{i/2}d_{M}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\mathcal{T}_{i}).

Taking the infimum over all admissible sequences
\{\mathcal{T}_{i}\}_{i\geq 0}
in the
upper bound implies that with probability at least
1-\exp(-u^{2}/2)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}|X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}-X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}}|\leq\sqrt{\frac{8}{N}}\,u\,\gamma_{2}(\Theta,d_{M}).

5.2
Mixed tails

We derive a bound for the backward error
and minimal sampling amount in terms of a
mixed tail.
After defining another version of multi-scale nets
(Definition
15
), we state our
main result for the backward error (Theorem
16
),
and make a comparison with the
corresponding
\eta
-net
bound (Remark
5.1
),
followed by a supporting result
(Theorem
17
).

For the mixed tails,
we need a more stringent version of nets.
Recall that the
\eta
-nets in
Definition
12
contain, at every level
k
, a single set
\mathcal{T}_{k}
which is a proper subset of
\Theta
(except possibly at the last level). In contrast, the partitions defined below contain, at every
level
k
, a collection of sets that cover
all of
\Theta
. This is necessary because the
proof of Theorem
17
requires intersections of sets associated with different tails,
and some of them have to be non-empty. This would not be possible with a
single set at every level.

Definition 15
(Definition 1.2.3 in
[
20
]
).

Given a set
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
, a partition of

\Theta
is a set
\mathcal{A}
whose
elements are disjoint subsets of
\Theta
and whose union
equals
\Theta
. That is,

\mathcal{A}=\{A_{i}\}
where
A_{i}\subset\Theta
,
A_{i}\cap A_{j}=\emptyset

for
i\neq j
, and
\bigcup_{i}{A_{i}}=\Theta
.

A sequence of partitions

\{\mathcal{A}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
of
\Theta
is increasing if every element of
\mathcal{A}_{k+1}
is a subset of an element of
\mathcal{A}_{k}
.

An increasing sequence of partitions
\{\mathcal{A}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
of
\Theta
is
admissible if
|\mathcal{A}_{k}|\leq 2^{2^{k}}
for
k>0
and

|\mathcal{A}_{0}|=1
.
This is illustrated in Figure
3
.

Figure 3:
An illustration of the sequence of partitions
\{\mathcal{A}_{k}\}_{0\leq k\leq 2}
. The red circle denotes the point
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}
, and the blue squares denote the unique elements
A_{0}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}),A_{1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
, and
A_{2}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
.

The diameter of a set
\mathcal{T}\subset\Theta
associated with a (pseudo-)metric
d_{\xi}
is

\Delta_{\xi}(\mathcal{T})\equiv\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{T}}d_{\xi}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi}),\qquad\xi\in\{2,F,M\}.

(30)

Let
\{\mathcal{A}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be an admissible sequence of
partitions of
\Theta
, and let

A_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
be the unique element in
\mathcal{A}_{k}
containing
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}
.
For any integer
\alpha>0
, define the second Talagrand functional

\gamma_{\alpha}^{\prime}(\Theta,d_{\xi})\equiv\inf\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{k/\alpha}\Delta_{\xi}(A_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})),

(31)

where the infimum ranges over all admissible sequences of partitions
\{\mathcal{A}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
.

The Talagrand functionals (
17
) and
(
31
) are equivalent, in the sense that for any (pseudo-)metric
d
and integer
\beta>0

\displaystyle\gamma_{\beta}(\Theta,d)\leq\gamma^{\prime}_{\beta}(\Theta,d)\leq C_{\beta}\gamma_{\beta}(\Theta,d),

(32)

where
C_{\beta}
is a constant that depends only on
\beta
.

Remark 5.2
.

The functional
\gamma_{\alpha}^{\prime}(\Theta,d_{\xi})

is an upper bound on the diameter of
\Theta
. That is,

\displaystyle\gamma_{\alpha}^{\prime}(\Theta,d_{\xi})\geq\Delta_{\xi}(\Theta).

This follows from
A_{0}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})=\Theta
and the fact
that regardless of the partition

\{\mathcal{A}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{k/\alpha}\Delta_{\xi}(A_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))\geq\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}\Delta_{\xi}(A_{0}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))=\Delta_{\xi}(\Theta).

Below we present a mixed tail bound on the
backward error of the estimator,
as well as a bound on the minimal
sampling amount
N
required to bound the error by
\epsilon
.

Theorem 16
.

For
u\geq 4
,
with probability at least
1-4\exp(-u/2)

0\leq F(\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}})-F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})\leq\frac{32u}{N}\,\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(\Theta,d_{2})+\sqrt{\frac{128u}{N}}\,\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(\Theta,d_{F})+\frac{8u}{N}\,\alpha_{2}+\sqrt{\frac{16u}{N}}\,\alpha_{F}.

Alternatively, let
0<\delta\leq 4\exp(-2)
and
\epsilon>0
. If

\displaystyle N\geq 4096\epsilon^{-2}\ln(4/\delta)\max\left\{\epsilon\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(\Theta,d_{2}),\epsilon\alpha_{2},(\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(\Theta,d_{F}))^{2},\alpha_{F}^{2}\right\},

(33)

then
0\leq{F}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}})-F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})\leq\epsilon
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

lemma

4
implies for a fixed
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}\in\Theta
,

\myP[|X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}}|\geq t]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{Nt^{2}}{8(\alpha_{F}^{2}+t\alpha_{2})}\right).

Analogous to step 3 in the proof of the subsequent Theorem
17
, a change of variable
gives for
u>0

\myP\left[|X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}}|\geq\frac{4u}{N}\alpha_{2}+\sqrt{\frac{4u}{N}}\alpha_{F}\right]\leq 2\exp(-u/2).

From (
21
), the union bound and Theorem
17

follows that for
u\geq 4
with probability at least
1-4\exp(-u/2)
,

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}|X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}|\leq\frac{16u}{N}\,\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(d_{2})+\sqrt{\frac{32u}{N}}\,\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(d_{F})+\frac{4u}{N}\,\alpha_{2}+\sqrt{\frac{4u}{N}}\,\alpha_{F},

and

0\leq F(\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}})-F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})\leq\frac{32u}{N}\,\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(d_{2})+\sqrt{\frac{128u}{N}}\,\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(d_{F})+\frac{8u}{N}\,\alpha_{2}+\sqrt{\frac{16u}{N}}\,\alpha_{F}.

Set
\delta=4\exp(-u/2)
so that
u=2\ln(4/\delta)
for

0<\delta\leq 4\exp(-2)
. The expression for
N

in (
33
) is obtained by setting each of
the 4 summands above to
\epsilon/4
, solving for
N
, and taking the maximum.

A comparison of the sampling amounts from
Theorems
16

and
7
is similar to but as inconclusive
as the one in Remark
5.1
.

The following
represents the basis for the proof of Theorem
16
.

Theorem 17
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}\in\Theta
and
u\geq 4
.
With probability at least
1-2\exp(-u/2)

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}|X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}-X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}}|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{16u}{N}\,\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(d_{2})+\sqrt{\frac{32u}{N}}\,\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(d_{F}).

Proof.

The proof essentially follows that of
[
20
, Section 1.2]
, but it pays attention to the constants and relies on the mixed tail bound in
lemma

4
. As in the proof of Theorem
14
,
the separability assumption allows us to limit consideration
to the case where
\Theta
is a finite set.

1.

Constructing admissible sequences
of partitions.

Since

lemma

4
involves two different (pseudo-)metrics
d_{2}
and
d_{F}
, the chaining argument in
theorem

14
needs to be expanded to two different admissible sequences of partitions, one for each (pseudo-)metric, followed by the formation
of their intersection
[
20
, Proof of Theorem 1.2.7]
.

We construct these two admissible sequences
of partitions
\{\mathcal{B}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
and
\{\mathcal{C}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
with

\mathcal{B}_{0}=\Theta=\mathcal{C}_{0}
so that they
are close to their respective lower bounds
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(\Theta,d_{2})
and

\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(\Theta,d_{F})
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{k}\Delta_{2}(B_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))

\displaystyle\leq 2\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(\Theta,d_{2}),\qquad\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta,

(34)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{k/2}\Delta_{F}(C_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))

\displaystyle\leq 2\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(\Theta,d_{F}),\qquad\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta,

(35)

where, as in (
31
), the sets
B_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
and
C_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
are the unique elements in
\mathcal{B}_{k}
and
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, respectively, that contain
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}
.

Then we combine
\{\mathcal{B}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
and
\{\mathcal{C}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}

to construct a new increasing sequence of partitions
of
\Theta
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0}\equiv\Theta,\qquad\mathcal{A}_{k}=\{B\cap C|B\in\mathcal{B}_{k-1},C\in\mathcal{C}_{k-1}\},\qquad k>0.

Each set
\mathcal{A}_{k}
of intersections is non-empty because the elements of
\mathcal{B}_{k-1}
and

\mathcal{C}_{k-1}
cover all of
\Theta.

The sequence of partitions
\{\mathcal{A}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is admissible with cardinality
|\mathcal{A}_{0}|=1
and

\displaystyle|\mathcal{A}_{k}|\leq|\mathcal{B}_{k-1}||\,\mathcal{C}_{k-1}|\leq\left(2^{2^{k-1}}\right)^{2}=2^{2^{k}},\qquad k>0.

This is not the tightest bound on the cardinality,
but what is important is the upper bound of the form
2^{2^{k}}
.

2.

Defining the chain.

For the admissible sequence of partitions
\{\mathcal{A}\}_{k\geq 0}
we construct sets

T_{k}\subset\Theta
by selecting exactly one element from each set of
\mathcal{A}_{k}
for
k>0
,
starting with
T_{0}=\{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}\}
.
The sets have cardinality
|T_{0}|=1
and
|T_{k}|\leq 2^{2^{k}}
for
k>0.

Pick a parameter
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}
, and define
\pi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
as the element in the singleton set

T_{k}\cap A_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})=\{\pi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\}
for
k\geq 0
.
Define a chain from
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}
to
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}
,

\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}=\pi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\rightarrow\pi_{1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow\pi_{M}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})=\boldsymbol{\uptheta},

where
M
is finite because
\Theta
is finite.

As before, we decompose the distance between the processes

X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}
and
X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}}
as the telescoping sum of increments,

X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}-X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}}=\sum_{k=1}^{M}(X_{\pi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})}-X_{\pi_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})}).

We need the following bounds for the increments. As in (
31
), let
A_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
be the unique element of
\mathcal{A}_{k}
containing
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}

for
k\geq 0
.
The nestedness of the sets in the partition implies
that

\pi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}),\pi_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\in A_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})\subset B_{k-2}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
, hence

\displaystyle d_{2}(\pi_{1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}),\pi_{0}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))\leq\Delta_{2}(B_{0}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})),\qquad d_{2}(\pi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}),\pi_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))\leq\Delta_{2}(B_{k-2}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))

for
2\leq k\leq M
.
This together with (
34
) implies

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{M}2^{k}d_{2}(\pi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}),\pi_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))\leq\sum_{k\geq 0}2^{k}\Delta_{2}(B_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))\leq 2\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(\Theta,d_{2}).

(36)

Similarly, (
35
) implies

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{M}2^{k/2}d_{F}(\pi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}),\pi_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))\leq 2\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(\Theta,d_{F}).

(37)

3.

Re-expressing the concentration inequality.

Lemma
4
implies

\displaystyle\myP[|X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}-X_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}|\geq t]\leq

\displaystyle\>2\exp\left(\frac{-Nt^{2}}{8(d_{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})^{2}+td_{2}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi}))}\right).

Set
\zeta_{F}^{2}=8d_{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})^{2}/N
and
\zeta_{2}=8d_{2}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})/N
. In
u=t^{2}/(\zeta_{F}^{2}+t\zeta_{2})
solve for
t
by taking the positive root

t=\frac{u\zeta_{2}}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{u^{2}}{4}\zeta_{2}^{2}+u\zeta_{F}^{2}}\leq u\,\zeta_{2}+\sqrt{u}\,\zeta_{F}.

This gives for
u>0

\myP\left[|X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}-X_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}|\geq\frac{8}{N}d_{2}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})\,u+\sqrt{\frac{8}{N}}d_{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})\,\sqrt{u}\right]\leq 2\exp(-u).

4.

Applying the union bound.

Motivated by (
36
) and (
37
) we can define the event for
u>0
,

\begin{split}\Omega_{k,\boldsymbol{\uptheta},u}&\equiv\biggl\{|X_{\pi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})}-X_{\pi_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})}|\geq\>\frac{8u}{N}\,2^{k}\,d_{2}(\pi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}),\pi_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))\\
&\qquad+\sqrt{\frac{8u}{N}}\,2^{k/2}\,d_{F}(\pi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}),\pi_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))\biggr\},\quad 0\leq k\leq M.\end{split}

The previous step implies
\myP[\Omega_{k,\boldsymbol{\uptheta},u}]\leq 2\exp(-2^{k}\,u).
For every
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta
, and fixed
k
, there are
|T_{k}||T_{k-1}|\leq 2^{2^{k+1}}
different possible pairs
(\pi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}),\pi_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))
.
A simple union bound gives for
u\geq 4

\displaystyle\myP\left[\cup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}\cup_{0\leq k\leq M}\Omega_{k,\boldsymbol{\uptheta},u}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\>2\sum_{k=0}^{M}|T_{k}||T_{k-1}|\exp(-2^{k}\,u)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\>2\sum_{k=0}^{M}2^{2^{k+1}}\exp(-2^{k}\,u)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{2^{k+1}}\exp(-2^{k}\,u)\leq 2\exp(-u/2),

where the last inequality follows from

lemma

21
in Section
5.4
.

Combining this with steps 2 and 3 shows that with probability at least
1-2\exp(-u/2)
for
u\geq 4

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\Theta}|X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}}-X_{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{0}}|\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{M}\frac{8u}{N}\,2^{k}\,d_{2}(\pi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}),\pi_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))+\sqrt{\frac{8u}{N}}\,2^{k/2}\,d_{F}(\pi_{k}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}),\pi_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{8u}{N}\,2\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(d_{2})+\sqrt{\frac{8u}{N}}\,2\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(d_{F})=\frac{16u}{N}\,\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(d_{2})+\sqrt{\frac{32u}{N}}\,\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(d_{F}),

where the last inequality follows from (
36
) and (
37
).

5.3
Spherical parameter spaces

Working with the Talagrand functionals
\gamma_{\beta}
and
\gamma_{\beta}^{\prime}
can be difficult in practice.
It is possible, however, in certain instances
when the functionals can be expressed
in terms of explicitly known covering numbers,
as in the case of spherical parameter spaces
(Lemma
11
).

Below, we derive bounds on the backward error
and sampling amounts for parameter spaces that are
K
-dimensional spheres (Corollaries
19
and
20
), and make a comparison with the
corresponding
\eta
-net
bound (Remark
5.4
).

We extend the definition of
\eta
-net in
Definition
6
to general metrics and nets that are not necessarily subsets of
\mathcal{T}
.

Definition 18
(Definition 5.5 in
[
21
]
).

Let
\eta>0
, and
(\mathcal{T},d)
with
\mathcal{T}\subset\Theta
be a metric space.
A set
\mathcal{C}
is called an
\eta
-net of
(\mathcal{T},d)
if,
for every
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\mathcal{T}
, there exists a point
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
with
d(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{\prime})\leq\eta
. The smallest cardinality of an
\eta
-net for
(\mathcal{T},d)
is called the covering number
S(\mathcal{T},d,\eta)
.

The
\eta
-nets enjoy the following monotonicity property.

Remark 5.3
.

If for two (pseudo-)metrics
d
and
d^{\prime}
on a set

\mathcal{T}

there is a constant
C>0
so that

\displaystyle d(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})\leq Cd^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})\qquad\text{for all}\quad\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{T}

(38)

then

\displaystyle S(\mathcal{T},d,\eta)\leq S(\mathcal{T},d^{\prime},\eta/C).

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a
(\eta/C)
-net for

(\mathcal{T},d^{\prime})

with covering number
S(\mathcal{T},d^{\prime},\eta/C)
.
That is,
for all
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\mathcal{T}
there exists a

\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{C}
with

\displaystyle d^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})\leq\eta/C.

Then (
38
) and the above imply that
for all
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\mathcal{T}
there exists a

\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{C}
so that

\displaystyle d(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})\leq C\,d^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})\leq C(\eta/C)=\eta.

Thus,
\mathcal{C}
is an
\eta
-net for
(\mathcal{T},d)
, and the minimality of the covering number
implies

S(\mathcal{T},d,\eta)\leq|\mathcal{C}|=S(\mathcal{T},d^{\prime},\eta/C).

Example
5.1
in Section
5.4
presents
special cases of Remark
5.3
that are
required for the proofs of Corollaries
19

and
20
.

We convert Talagrand’s functionals into Dudley integral inequality
[
23
, Theorem 8.1.3]
which lets us
bound
\gamma_{\beta}(\Theta,d)
in terms of the covering numbers
S(\Theta,d,\eta)

of infinitely many
\eta
-nets,

\displaystyle\gamma_{\beta}(\Theta,d)\leq C_{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\ln S(\Theta,d,\eta)\right)^{1/\beta}d\eta,

(39)

where
C_{\beta}
is a constant that depends only on
\beta
. Note that the conversion to the Dudley integral form results in a loss of optimality
[
23
, Exercise 8.2]
, but this integral form is easier to work with, in practice.

We derive two bounds for the case where,
as in (
14
), the parameter space
\mathcal{H}

is a
K
-dimensional sphere that is centered at
\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{c}\in\mathcal{H}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\equiv\{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}:\|\boldsymbol{\uptheta}-\boldsymbol{\uptheta}_{c}\|_{2}\leq B\}\qquad\text{for some}\quad B>0.

Corollary 19
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
be Lipschitz continuous,

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\|_{M}\leq L_{M}\|\boldsymbol{\uptheta}-\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{2}\qquad\text{for all}\ \boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}.

If

N\geq C\,\frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\,\max\{\alpha_{M}^{2},K(BL_{M})^{2}\}

for an absolute constant
C>0
, then

0\leq\hat{F}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}})-F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})\leq\epsilon
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

In
theorem

13
, we only need to bound
\gamma_{2}(\Theta,d_{M})
.
From the Lipschitz continuity of
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
, (
42
) in Section
5.4
,
and Lemma
11
follows

S(\Theta,d_{M},\eta)\leq S(\Theta,d_{2}^{\prime},\eta/L_{M})\leq\max\left\{\left(\frac{3BL_{M}}{\eta}\right)^{K},1\right\}.

Convert this to the Dudley integral form (
39
)

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}(\Theta,d_{M})\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\ln S(\Theta,d_{M},\eta)}d\eta\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\max\left\{\sqrt{K\ln\left(\frac{3BL_{M}}{\eta}\right)},0\right\}d\eta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}\sqrt{K\ln\left(\frac{3BL_{M}}{\eta}\right)}d\eta,

where
a=3BL_{M}
. The maximum is zero for
\eta>a
because the logarithm is negative then.
Simplify the integral with
lemma

22
,

\gamma_{2}(\Theta,d_{M})\lesssim\int_{0}^{a}\sqrt{K\ln(a/\eta)}d\eta\leq a\sqrt{K}\Gamma(3/2)\lesssim\sqrt{K}BL_{M}.

With the expression in
theorem

13
,
the lower bound for
N
is now

\displaystyle N\geq C\,\frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\,\max\{K(BL_{M})^{2},\alpha_{M}^{2}\}\geq C\,\frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\,\max\{\gamma_{2}(\Theta,d_{M})^{2},\alpha_{M}^{2}\}.

Remark 5.4
(Comparison of Corollaries
19

and
8
).

A comparison of the two bounds for the sampling
amount
N
is difficult and inconclusive.

The bound in (
15
) in Corollary
8
is

\displaystyle N\geq\frac{8\alpha_{M}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(K\ln(12mL_{2}B/\epsilon)-\ln(\delta)\right)

(40)

while the one in Corollary
19
is

\displaystyle N\geq C\,\frac{\ln(2)-\ln(\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\,\max\{\alpha_{M}^{2},K(BL_{M})^{2}\}.

(41)

1.

Since
\hat{F}
is a Monte Carlo estimator,
both bounds grow with
-\ln(\delta)/\epsilon^{-2}
.
That is, the sampling amount increases as the desired
error
\epsilon
and the failure probability
\delta
become smaller.

2.

Although both bounds require
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
to be
Lipschitz continuous, the Lipschitz constants
L_{M}

and
L_{F}

belong to different norms for
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
.

3.

Clearly, (
40
) has the advantage of all
constants being explicitly specified, while the constant
C
in (
41
) is not known.

4.

Barring the unknown constant
C
, (
41
)
does not depend explicitly on the matrix dimension
m
,
while (
40
) depends on
m
weakly through
the logarithm.

5.

The chaining bound (
41
)
might grow less with the offdiagonal
mass
\alpha_{M}
and the parameter
space dimension
K
because it depends on the maximum
of

\alpha_{M}^{2}
and
K(BL_{M})^{2}
, while
(
40
)
depends on the product
\alpha_{M}^{2}K\ln(L_{2}B)
.

But then again, the chaining bound depends more
strongly on the radius
B
and the Lipschitz
constant than (
40
).

Below is the mixed tail version of Corollary
19
.

Corollary 20
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
be Lipschitz continuous,

\|\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\|_{F}\leq L_{F}\|\boldsymbol{\uptheta}-\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\|\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\|_{2}\leq L_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\uptheta}-\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{2}

for all
\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}
.
If

N\geq C\,\frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\,\max\{\alpha_{F}^{2},\epsilon\alpha_{2},K(BL_{F})^{2},\epsilon KBL_{2}\}

for an absolute constant
C>0
, then

0\leq\hat{F}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\uptheta}})-F(\boldsymbol{\uptheta}^{*})\leq\epsilon
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Corollary
19
, except we use Theorem
16
,
where we need to bound
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(\Theta,d_{2})
and
\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(\Theta,d_{F})
.

As for
\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(\Theta,d_{F})
, the Lipschitz continuity of
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
,
(
43
) in Section
5.4
,
and Lemma
11
imply

S(\Theta,d_{F},\eta)\leq S(\Theta,d_{2}^{\prime},\eta/L_{F})\leq\max\left\{\left(\frac{3BL_{F}}{\eta}\right)^{K},1\right\}.

With (
32
), (
39
) and the above, we bound as in the proof of Corollary

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}^{\prime}(\Theta,d_{F})

\displaystyle\lesssim\gamma_{2}(\Theta,d_{F})\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\ln S(\Theta,d_{F},\eta)}d\eta

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\max\left\{\sqrt{K\ln\left(\frac{3BL_{F}}{\eta}\right)},0\right\}\,d\eta\lesssim\sqrt{K}BL_{F}.

As for
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}(\Theta,d_{2})

\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})

) in Section

Again, with (

\displaystyle\lesssim\gamma_{1}(\Theta,d_{2})\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\ln S(\Theta,d_{2},\eta)}d\eta\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\max\left\{\sqrt{K\ln\left(\frac{6BL_{2}}{\eta}\right)},0\right\}\,d\eta

Auxiliary results

We present a bound for an infinite sum
(Lemma
21
), the simplification
of an integral (Lemma
22
),
a bound on the offdiagonal norm of a matrix
(Lemma
23
), and examples of covering
numbers for different types of Lipschitz
continuity (Example
5.1
).

The bound below is used in Theorem
17
for the case
p=1
.

Lemma 21
.

Let
p\geq 1
be an integer and
u\geq 4^{1/p}
. Then

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{2^{k+1}}\exp(-2^{k}u^{p})\leq\exp(-u^{p}/2).

Proof.

Pull the power of two into the exponential,

\displaystyle 2^{2^{k+1}}\exp(-2^{k}u^{p})

\displaystyle=\exp(2^{k}(2\ln 2-u^{p}))

\displaystyle=\exp\left(2^{k}\left(2\ln{2}-\frac{u^{p}}{2}\right)-2^{k}\frac{u^{p}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(2^{k}\left(2\ln{2}-\frac{u^{p}}{2}\right)\right)\exp\left(-2^{k}\frac{u^{p}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(2^{k}\left(2\ln{2}-\frac{u^{p}}{2}\right)\right)\exp\left(-\frac{u^{p}}{2}\right).

Since
2\ln 2<2
and, by assumption,

u^{p}\geq 4

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{2^{k+1}}\exp(-2^{k}u^{p})\leq

\displaystyle\exp(-u^{p}/2)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\exp(2^{k}(2\ln 2-u^{p}/2))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\exp(-u^{p}/2)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\exp(2^{k}(2\ln 2-2)).

The last inequality follows since
u^{p}/2\geq 2
and
2^{k}\geq 1
for
k\geq 0
. Finally, we can bound the sum as

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\exp(2^{k}(2\ln 2-2))\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\exp(2k(2\ln 2-2))<1,

by summing the geometric series.

The simplification below is used in
Corollary
20
.

Lemma 22
.

Let
a>0
,
x>0
, and
p>0
be an integer. Then

\int_{0}^{a}(\ln(a/x))^{1/p}dx=\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{p}+1\right)a,

where
\Gamma(x)\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{x-1}e^{-t}dt
is the Gamma function.

Proof.

Change the integration variable to
t=\ln(a/x)
so that
x=ae^{-t}
. Differentiating
gives
dx=-ae^{-t}dt
and

\int_{0}^{a}(\ln(a/x))^{1/p}dx=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{1/p}ae^{-t}\,dt=a\,\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{p}+1\right).

matrix is used in Example
5.1
.

Lemma 23
(Section 1 in
[
5
]
).

Let
\boldsymbol{B}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
and
\overline{\boldsymbol{B}}\equiv\boldsymbol{B}-\diag(\boldsymbol{B})
its offdiagonal part. Then

\|\overline{\boldsymbol{B}}\|_{2}\leq 2\|\boldsymbol{B}\|_{2}.

Below are special cases of Remark
5.3
,
where we bound the covering numbers
for three different types of Lipschitz continuity
required in the proofs of Corollaries
19
and
20
.

Example 5.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
be Lipschitz continuous with

\displaystyle d_{M}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})\leq L_{M}\|\boldsymbol{\uptheta}-\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{2}\qquad\text{for all}\quad\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\Theta.

Then

\displaystyle d_{M}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})=\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\|_{M}\leq\|\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\|_{M}\leq L_{M}\|\boldsymbol{\uptheta}-\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{2},

where the first inequality follows from

\|\overline{\boldsymbol{B}}\|_{M}=\sum_{i\neq j}|b_{ij}|\leq\sum_{i,j}|b_{ij}|=\|\boldsymbol{B}\|_{M}
.
Hence

\displaystyle d_{M}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})\leq L_{M}\,d_{2}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})\qquad\text{with}\quad d_{2}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})\equiv\|\boldsymbol{\uptheta}-\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{2},

and

\displaystyle S(\mathcal{T},d_{M},\eta)\leq S(\mathcal{T},d_{2}^{\prime},\eta/L_{M}).

(42)

Let
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
be Lipschitz continuous with

\displaystyle d_{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})\leq L_{F}\|\boldsymbol{\uptheta}-\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{2}\qquad\text{for all}\quad\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\Theta.

Then

\displaystyle d_{F}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})=\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\|_{F}\leq\|\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\|_{F}\leq L_{F}\|\boldsymbol{\uptheta}-\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{2},

and, as above,

\displaystyle S(\mathcal{T},d_{F},\eta)\leq S(\mathcal{T},d_{2}^{\prime},\eta/L_{F}).

(43)

Let
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})
be Lipschitz continuous with

\displaystyle d_{2}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})\leq L_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\uptheta}-\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{2}\qquad\text{for all}\quad\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\Theta.

Then

\displaystyle d_{2}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})=\|\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-\overline{\boldsymbol{A}}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\|_{2}\leq 2\,\|\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta})-\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\|_{2}\leq 2\,L_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\uptheta}-\boldsymbol{\phi}\|_{2},

where the first inequality follows from Lemma

.
Hence

\displaystyle d_{2}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi})\leq 2\,L_{2}\,d_{2}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{\uptheta},\boldsymbol{\phi}),

\displaystyle S(\mathcal{T},d_{2},\eta)\leq S(\mathcal{T},d_{2}^{\prime},\eta/(2\,L_{2})).

6
Conclusion and discussion

We have extended the randomized trace estimation of a fixed
matrix
to trace optimization of parameter dependent matrices,
and derived bounds for the required sampling amounts.
The bounds based on nets (Section
4
) are straightforward to verify and apply, and come with constants fully specified.
In contrast, the bounds based on chaining (Section
5
), although optimal in some contexts, are more difficult to apply and do not necessarily have a clear advantage over net-based arguments. Our results contribute theoretical justifications in the Monte Carlo trace estimators
for SAA optimization techniques in
[
8
,
9
]
.

A drawback of our chaining argument is the
dependence of the bounds (Theorem
17
) on Talagrand’s
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}
functional. This dependence
could have possibly been avoided by bounding instead
the suprema of chaos processes, which
are intimately connected to trace estimation.
Arguments
based on chaining with decoupling can produce bounds
that involve only the
\gamma_{2}
functional

[
11
,
16
]
.

One avenue for future work could be practical bounds for Talagrand functionals in special cases.
There are several options. The Dudley inequality bounds
Talagrand functionals in terms
of covering numbers
(Section
5.3
). The covering numbers, in turn, can be bounded if the matrix has low rank
[
7
]
, while a
more general technique relies on Sudakov minorization

[
23
, Section 7.4]
.
Talagrand functionals can also be bounded in optimization problems with sparsity promoting solutions
[
16
,
22
]
.

Finally, moving beyond nets,
it is worth exploring the potential of another approach
from statistical learning in trace optimization, namely
that of Rademacher
complexity
[
4
, Section 4.5]
.
