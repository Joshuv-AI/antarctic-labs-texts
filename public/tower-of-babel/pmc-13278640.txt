Healthcare Providers' Perspectives on Teledentistry Use in Supporting Oral Care of First Nations Children: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Natalie Arshat, Fabio Arriola‐Pacheco, Nashat Cassim, Maria Mylopoulos, Wendy Wardlaw, Glen Rodgers, Herenia P. Lawrence
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFacilitating pediatric oral healthcare access in remote First Nations communities in Canada is a challenge due to costs, accessibility, and provider availability. A diverse healthcare workforce confronts this task and could benefit from teledentistry utilization.AimUnderstand the perspectives of healthcare providers in remote First Nations communities on the potential benefits of teledentistry to improve access to pediatric oral healthcare.DesignA qualitative descriptive study design was used to elaborate on the experiences of community‐based healthcare providers. Semi‐structured, one‐on‐one interviews were conducted virtually. Thematic analysis was applied to interview transcripts, which were then organized and categorized into recurring themes.ResultsDental professionals, nurses, and a paramedic participated in the study. Themes that emerged included “facilitating interprofessional collaboration, documentation, and communication”; “mitigating burden for patients and the system”; “delivering education”; and “concerns with feasibility.” Knowledge mobilization feedback from community members was confirmatory of the results.ConclusionThe findings suggest that teledentistry can be beneficial in supporting healthcare providers serving remote First Nations communities, where access to and sustainability of oral healthcare are a challenge. The favored response to teledentistry reflects that this medium could aid in addressing the management of the considerable levels of oral disease among the pediatric population.

Introduction

According to the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP), Indigenous people around the world have the right to: access, without any discrimination, to all social and health services as well as: an equal right to the enjoyment of the highest attainable standard of physical and mental health [Article 24] [1]. Despite this recognized right, disparate health outcomes, including oral health, persist among First Nations children in Canada. Research has shown that First Nations children experience higher rates of early childhood caries and greater need for dental treatment under general anesthesia (GA), compared to their non‐Indigenous counterparts, especially in more remote and hard‐to‐access communities [2, 3, 4].

Though efforts to decrease the burden of oral health disease in First Nations children have been made at local and federal levels, challenges related to affordability, acceptability, and accessibility prevail. Affordability is hindered by both direct costs (e.g., supplies, lack of infrastructure, and available workforce) and indirect costs (e.g., travel and lodging expenses for providers) [5, 6, 7]. Acceptability is complex in terms of trust in oral healthcare providers, the latter's cultural competency (most of the workforce is from outside communities), and language‐barrier‐related factors [7, 8, 9]. In terms of accessibility, the remote location of some First Nations communities creates an array of intricate conditions. For instance, Phase III of the First Nations Regional Health Survey reported that only 37.8% of 0‐ to 2‐year‐olds and 28.5% of 3‐ to 5‐year‐olds had received dental care by oral healthcare providers stationed in their community in the year prior, as reported by their caregivers [4]. Therefore, the rest of the population within these age cohorts had oral healthcare delivered by locum providers visiting the community periodically or situated within or more than 90 km from their communities [4]. These complexities related to the accessibility of oral healthcare providers often result in non‐oral healthcare professionals within the communities provisionally managing oral health needs. This can include tasks such as triaging, screening, prescribing medications, and issuing referrals, a reality lived across rural and remote localities [10].

As exemplified, challenges for oral healthcare delivery for First Nations children are complex and require multidimensional, comprehensive strategies that facilitate, enhance, and broaden how pediatric care is implemented. Teledentistry, a type of telehealth, is one of the strategies that could enhance care provision in the region. Teledentistry is defined as “the use of technology and various devices (e.g., video, audio, and secure message) for remote oral healthcare delivery between patients and oral healthcare providers or between healthcare providers, aiming to facilitate access to care, reduce oral health inequalities, mitigate the economic impact of oral diseases and treatment, and foster interprofessional collaboration” [11]. While teledentistry is not new, its implications within oral healthcare were enhanced during the COVID‐19 pandemic [12]. Teledentistry, similar to other telehealth endeavors, can be delivered in multiple ways, such as asynchronous or “store‐forward,” which involves the transmission of previously recorded health information; synchronous or “real‐time,” which is a live two‐way mode of communication; and remote monitoring, in which patients are monitored by a provider or staff through a device or platform. Mobile health (mHealth) also exists within telehealth modalities, and it refers to public healthcare practices and education that is delivered through messages and applications [13, 14].

Teledentistry has been recognized as a valuable tool for detecting caries, diagnosing oral soft tissue lesions, and improving access to care, yet there is a reported lack of research on its application in pediatric dentistry and limited knowledge regarding its use within Indigenous communities [15]. Teledentistry and telehealth initiatives have been recognized for their potential to enhance the frequency and duration of patient–provider communication, irrespective of structural barriers (e.g., transportation) that may impede access to care. This, in turn, may contribute to the reduction of health inequities [16, 17, 18]. Nevertheless, for healthcare changes such as those offered by teledentistry to be adopted and implemented in the system, there has to be provider acceptance and value recognition in order to adequately implement them. This is especially relevant in those First Nations communities where pediatric oral healthcare delivery is shared among multiple healthcare providers. Considering the complexities that delivering pediatric care in First Nations communities represents, teledentistry could potentially serve as a valuable tool in the pursuit of the expansion of pediatric oral healthcare for said communities. Thus, the aim of this study was to gain an understanding of the perceptions of community‐based healthcare providers in remote First Nations communities on the potential for teledentistry to improve access to pediatric oral healthcare.

Materials and Methods

Study Location and Ethical Considerations

The study was conducted in partnership with the Weeneebayko Area Health Authority (WAHA), which is a First Nation organization that provides healthcare services to six communities along the western James Bay and Hudson Bay coasts in the northern part of the province of Ontario (ON), Canada [19]. This land has been home to Mushkegowuk Cree people for thousands of years, and through WAHA, Indigenous people in the area have rightfully gained sovereignty over their health management and delivery. This rightful attainment of health sovereignty is relevant in the wider context of Indigenous health transfer policy in Canada, as well as serving as part of the basis and rationale for this project [20]. Oral healthcare services in the region are mostly provided out of the Weeneebayko General Hospital in the central community of Moose Factory, and fly‐in locum dentists periodically travel to the neighboring communities. Pediatric patients who present behavioral challenges and/or have extensive oral healthcare needs are often referred for treatment to the general hospital. Referrals often depend on non‐oral healthcare providers, as they are typically the first point of contact for patients during emergencies when no oral healthcare provider is available in the community. Through multiple consultations and in the context of the COVID‐19 pandemic, the opportunity of utilizing teledentistry for pediatric care support was brought forth by the community partners, and thus, the project was co‐created.

Ethics approval for this project was obtained through the University of Toronto Research Ethics Board (RIS #43459) as well as the WAHA Ethics Committee. Individual, written consent was collected from participants via signed consent forms. Participants were offered monetary compensation for their time. The confidentiality of all involved participants was maintained, and any identifying factors were excluded from reports and presentations.

Study Design, Participant Characteristics, and Sampling

This study utilized a qualitative descriptive design with a community‐based participatory research (CBPR) approach [21]. Due to the nature of oral healthcare for children in the region (nonspecialized, infrequent, and inconsistent), sampling included a diversity of healthcare providers stationed in the region. Currently, pediatric patients in remote communities in this study are serviced by a variety of community healthcare professionals, all of whom are involved in the provision or arrangement of care. Purposive snowball sampling (mainly initiated through email and virtual contacts) was conducted to capture information‐rich cases that led to other potential participants [22]. This approach aligned well with the study's objective and context, given that pediatric patients with dental needs in the remote communities of interest are managed by a diverse range of community healthcare professionals, all of whom are involved in the provision or coordination of care and may stand to benefit from the implementation of teledentistry.

This study implemented the concept of information power to ground sample inclusion (i.e., size) as described by Malterud et al. [23]. Information power holds five different dimensions that impact a study's sample: aim, specificity, theory, dialog, and analysis. The study's sample size was evaluated using each of these aspects. First, the study's aim was narrowly focused on assessing the relevance of teledentistry within selected remote communities under the WAHA jurisdiction, allowing for concentrated and specific inquiry. Second, the sample specificity was dense, targeting healthcare workers who are consistently in contact with pediatric dental patients in need of treatment, thereby ensuring the collection of highly relevant perspectives regarding the role of teledentistry in their clinical practice. Third, this study's theoretical foundation was grounded on teledentistry's documented aspects across various settings; in this study, it was sought to ascertain teledentistry's specific relevance within the regions that WAHA serves. Fourth, the quality of the dialogue was maintained through reliable internet connectivity, the interviewer's ability to establish rapport, and the clarity of the interview questions. The interviewer's awareness of the challenges involved in treating young children (pediatric dentistry background), as well as the capabilities and limitations of teledentistry in this context, further enhanced the depth and relevance of the conversations. Finally, the analysis strategy employed sought to offer a comprehensive account of healthcare workers' perspectives in an in‐depth manner.

Data Collection and Analysis

Data were collected in the form of one‐on‐one audiovisual interviews conducted through an online video‐calling platform between June and November of 2023 by N.A. (female, DMD, pediatric dentistry resident at the time of data collection). N.A. had been academically trained in qualitative research methodology for healthcare research and was accompanied in expertise by M.M. Additionally, N.A. prepared for respectful Indigenous data gathering, took the First Nations Principles of Ownership, Control, Access, and Possession (OCAP) course, and was guided and supervised by H.P.L. and the WAHA partners [24]. A semi‐structured interview guide was utilized to guide data collection sessions. The interview guide included questions that inquired about how pediatric patients with oral healthcare needs were managed by healthcare providers, the challenges faced in providing care for these patients, and perceptions on the use of teledentistry (in any of its delivery manners) to facilitate care. The interview guide was pilot tested with pediatric healthcare providers at both the host university and the local community site to ensure topics were adequately expressed, understandable, and culturally appropriate. Interviews were recorded (as per agreed upon in the consent form), transcribed, and securely stored under a numerical code to maintain confidentiality. Field notes were taken after data collection sessions.

Data were analyzed applying reflexive, inductive, thematic analysis [25]. Data analysis was grounded in and driven by the data content in order to represent what was communicated by participants. To ensure trustworthiness during the analysis process, consensus coding was carried out by both N.A. and F.A.‐P. NVivo 14 software (Lumivero, Denver, USA) was utilized to organize and process the data.

Knowledge Mobilization

In line with the CBPR approach, emphasis was placed on the circulation of information gathered back to the community members and the project's key collaborators. An electronic poster presentation with the study results was initially disseminated to the key collaborators in May 2024. In addition, an in‐person knowledge mobilization visit to Moose Factory First Nation, ON, was conducted in July 2024, where the team was involved in round‐table discussions with additional members of the WAHA healthcare team. Furthermore, members of the community, specifically caregivers, were also engaged through conversations and infographics that raised awareness during the community activity: “Gathering of our People.” This gathering consisted of cultural demonstrations, information booths, feasts, and pow‐wows that celebrate Cree culture. These interactions provided a platform for assessing the project's viability and determining its potential to add value to the community's oral healthcare needs.

Reflexivity and Positionality

The authors recognize their positionality in this research, as all authors are non‐First Nations healthcare researchers and providers who are fortunate to be working with valuable community partners through WAHA. Decades of partnership (H.P.L.) and years of training (N.A. and F.A.‐P.) in Indigenous studies have led to the close appreciation and respect of people, culture, and society within these contexts. Furthermore, intersecting parts of the authors' identities are also acknowledged, such as being parents (N.A.) and working with pediatric patients (N.A., F.A.‐P., and N.C.). W.W. and G.R. are community‐based healthcare providers with multiple years of experience working with and in the community. The authors further want to highlight that the work they conduct seeks to build capacity and improve oral health outcomes for children and the population in the region.

Facilitating Interprofessional Collaboration, Documentation, and Communication With Patients

Due to the nature and particularities of how services are delivered in the region, a sense that teledentistry could facilitate communication arose, with various subthemes (Table 2). In the absence of a dentist, patients seeking urgent care turn to the nursing stations. While some cases fall within the nurses' scope, others are in a “gray zone,” thus often presenting nurses with challenges in management. One nurse expressed that they shoulder responsibilities typically reserved for dental professionals by saying, “I feel like I'm a dentist—why am I a dentist?” This sentiment underscored the difficulties in managing oral healthcare cases—a task not traditionally within the scope of nursing professionals. These challenges lead participants to express their needs for improved communication and collaboration with remote oral healthcare professionals to better manage patients' oral healthcare.

Throughout the interviews, the advantages of having access to remote oral healthcare consultation became evident, especially concerning cases that presented at the emergency department. A shared desire among nurses for collaborative feedback emerged as a common thread, aiming to boost confidence and certainty in patient care. Using a virtual means to consult with remote dentists also emerged as a valuable resource in situations where clinical decisions remained uncertain. As the discussions continued, nurses shared their current methods of seeking remote consultations from dentists, primarily relying on mobile phone cameras (asynchronous modality) to capture patient images. The intraoral camera was described by nurse participants as a noninvasive tool, similar in size to a dental handpiece, that allows easy capturing and secure storage of intraoral images. Nurses viewed this technology favorably as an effective and practical means of providing information to remote dentists for guidance on case management and assessing urgency by streamlining the communication process.

Another facet of communication that surfaced was the application of teledentistry to bolster standard clinical documentation, which typically includes clinical examination notes and radiographs. In these instances, photographic images can serve to visually appraise the extent of the oral condition. Furthermore, these images could be utilized by subsequent oral healthcare practitioners who might not have had the opportunity for a direct clinical examination. From a nursing perspective and in the realm of operational considerations, a parallel argument surfaced in favor of maintaining intraoral and extraoral photographic records. Frequently, patients who deviate from recommended care or have frequent “no‐shows” for scheduled appointments (as discerned in interviews) revisit the nursing station or emergency department for the same dental concerns or when these have been intensified. The act of capturing photographs is thought to serve the purpose of visually chronicling patient history or disease progression.

Shifting the focus to another modality within teledentistry, the use of live video conferencing (i.e., synchronous consultation) was another topic that was raised by nurses. This mode can serve as an adjunct, rather than a replacement for conventional clinical examinations, and enable immediate examination through specialized cameras and equipment.

Mitigating Burden for Patients and the System

Multiple methods of mitigating burdens for the patient and the system as part of teledentistry implementation were brought forth by participants (Table 3). Some respondents expressed that virtually communicating a patient's oral healthcare needs could be instrumental in reducing wait times for those patients who are referred for care outside the community. One dentist suggested that having visual proof to convey a patient's oral health status might expedite access to GA services, thereby reducing wait burden for both the patient and their family. Although establishing virtual consultations may present its own challenges, the possibility remains to decrease the necessity for an additional trip or the delay in treatment, which might heighten the likelihood of chronic caries‐related complications. Throughout the interviews, nurses mentioned scenarios where patients presented multiple times to the emergency department with deteriorating oral health conditions. The reasons behind these repeat visits are multifactorial and cannot be solely attributed to treatment wait lists. Yet despite this, participants expressed the belief that virtual communication of findings could play a crucial role in preventing disease progression through shortening wait times.

Another topic pertained to the reliance on antibiotic prescriptions within remote communities to address various dental issues, particularly infections. Support regarding antibiotic decision‐making, as well as the potential reduction of their unnecessary use, was a voiced concern among the participants.

The notion of teledentistry helping to create more personal connections was highlighted, since many times patients are scheduled for oral healthcare procedures under GA, and there is often limited prior communication with the performing dentist. These prior circumstances sometimes contribute to a sense of unfamiliarity for patients and their families during the hospital visit.

On a systemic level, discussions also pivoted toward the reduction of burdens on the healthcare system, particularly in terms of cost. Cost here specifically centers around the scheduling of GA appointments for children who may require dental treatment. It was postulated that the strategic application of teledentistry, particularly in triaging cases presenting at the emergency department, could contribute to the reduction of unnecessary referrals for GA. The suggestion involves leveraging remote oral health assessments, facilitated through images or video conferencing, to more precisely identify patient concerns.

Delivering Education

When the concept of utilizing teledentistry to deliver dental educational content surfaced, unanimous support emerged from all participants, particularly among nurses (Table 4). Despite possessing some foundational knowledge about the oral cavity and certain oral health conditions, the insights obtained from interviews indicated a favorable inclination toward receiving more structured educational content on fundamental oral health concepts. The potential benefits of providing education to nurses to enhance the triage process and foster better communication between remote consulting dentists and nurses were also emphasized.

Concerns With Feasibility

The introduction of technological shifts, like that brought by teledentistry, invited a spectrum of concerns about feasibility, which surfaced throughout the interviews (Table 5). Apprehensions regarding teledentistry's diagnostic reliability were voiced, particularly among dental providers. The participants suggested a distinctive role for teledentistry, not as a standalone diagnostic tool but rather as a useful communication tool.

Concerns about potential pushback, particularly from medical personnel, in response to the introduction of new technology, emerged from conversations with the participants. Nurses, while generally expressing positive attitudes toward teledentistry, also acknowledged the likelihood of resistance, especially from more seasoned colleagues. Nurse 1 captured this sentiment by stating, “I think when it first starts, it'll probably be slow to catch on.” Nevertheless, participants generally held a belief in the transformative potential of teledentistry, even in the face of initial hesitations or resistance.

The need for training to use teledentistry as an innovation was also brought forth. Despite the user‐friendly nature of the intraoral camera, concerns centered around patient management during image capture arose, with a dentist expressing: “You'd have to have training of personnel to be able to at least put a [dental bite] block in, and there are limits in terms of restraining the patient.” Despite reservations like the prior, most participants shared an overall positive outlook regarding the feasibility of training to use adjuncts like the intraoral camera (emphasizing the user‐friendly nature of the technology).

Furthermore, feasibility discussions of teledentistry delved into considerations of internet access, revealing diverse perceptions among participants. Two distinct components emerged from these conversations: the availability of adequate internet bandwidth and speed in remote regions and patient access to technology. Regarding access to modern communication devices such as cell phones, participants generally perceived that there is limited access by members of the community to such devices.

Knowledge Mobilization Feedback Results

As part of knowledge mobilization efforts, a poster summarizing the study's findings was distributed to participants via email. While no specific feedback was provided, there was confirmation that participants' ideas had been adequately reflected, thus permitting member‐checking. Round‐table conversations with other healthcare providers who had extensive experience and knowledge with oral healthcare delivery in the region generated valuable insights. The presentation of findings elicited positive and optimistic responses toward the implementation of teledentistry. The discussion highlighted the importance of teledentistry's use in triaging and determining case urgency. A longstanding member of the community with a background in dental operating room experience remarked, “I think something like this could help triage the patient into different extremities. Do they need the O.R. [operating room] ASAP? Do they just need nitrous? Is the cause not even dental‐related?” There was also an allusion that virtual consults help alleviate patients' stress and frustration of not receiving immediate care when a provider was not in town and/or immediately available. Much of the other feedback shared was confirmatory of our findings, with the gauged providers having a positive outlook on the application of teledentistry. During a community gathering, the caregivers who were briefed about the project and its use in the region positively reacted to the idea of having an additional means of accessing oral healthcare. Several caregivers described past challenges involving their children's emergent dental needs or GA cases and acknowledged teledentistry's potential to reduce the burden associated with limited‐service availability. The results of this study are now held by the partnering organization and can help inform further program planning for oral healthcare and other areas.

Discussion

The present study describes the perceptions of oral and non‐oral healthcare providers in remote First Nations communities regarding the potential of teledentistry to improve access to pediatric oral healthcare. The results of the study indicate that, though concerns with the feasibility of teledentistry in this context exist, there is a perceived notion that its use could facilitate interprofessional collaboration, mitigate burden for patients and the system, and serve as an educational adjunct for healthcare providers in the region. Although the applications of telehealth with Canadian Indigenous communities have been explored, these have mainly addressed mental health, maternal health, and diabetes‐related issues [26]. Within pediatric dentistry, the use of teledentistry has yielded favorable views, similar to the ones presented, with existing studies primarily examining parental perspectives and child acceptability [17, 27]. However, non‐oral healthcare providers' viewpoints and involvement with teledentistry for pediatric oral healthcare remain underexplored.

Providers' outlooks on teledentistry's potential to enhance interprofessional collaboration are highly relevant, especially in contexts where pediatric oral care delivery is complex and shared among healthcare professionals. The findings in this study are comparable with published literature from the psychiatric field, where nurses and psychiatrists agreed that using videoconferencing to resolve challenging clinical situations promoted teamwork, collaborative problem‐solving, and increased confidence in the care provided [28]. Yet, it is important to consider the remaining challenges that exist for the adequate integration of teledentistry into healthcare systems, such as patient privacy and security, adequate medico‐legal legislation, and workforce training [14, 16]. The latter point is particularly important in the context of pediatric Indigenous healthcare services [29]. Our study underscores the potential for nurses to serve as key collaborators in teledentistry efforts, a role further supported by existing research that emphasizes the critical contributions of nurses in interdisciplinary collaboration to improve disease management within primary care settings [30]. Explorations of nurses' involvement in pediatric dentistry suggest they can also play a key role in delivering preventative and non‐restorative care (e.g., silver diamine fluoride), which further accentuates their role as vital pediatric dentistry allies [10, 31].

Teledentistry's value in reducing barriers, as expressed by this study's participants, can be conceptualized from both an individual (patient and family) and a systemic level. Recognition and management of disease severity, treatment adherence, and opportunities for building provider familiarity were identified as factors that teledentistry could assist in addressing at an individual level. Teledentistry facilitates the transmission of photographs and patient details to healthcare decision‐makers, thus allowing for more efficient case prioritization by urgency. If this optimization alleviates the strain of prolonged wait times for treatment, it could also promote treatment adherence. Initial (before treatment) synchronous pediatric teledentistry engagements have demonstrated an increase in subsequent in‐person appointment adherence and treatment completion rates among children who resided in rural and remote areas in the United States [32, 33]. Likewise, these prior provider‐patient contacts can serve to mitigate uncertainties regarding appointments and help establish rapport before in‐person visits, as suggested by the participants in our study. This initial exposure to providers and procedures through teledentistry has the potential to reduce dental anxiety. The basis for this notion can be attributed to studied strategies from the behavior guidance field, such as positive pre‐visit imagery, desensitization, and tell‐show‐do, but implemented in virtual settings [34]. At a systemic level, cost reduction is commonly presented and perceived as a potential benefit to gain from teledentistry [11, 17, 27, 32, 33]. The present study's participants echoed this remark, yet published literature regarding the cost‐effectiveness of teledentistry suggests that this domain still needs further exploration, preferably through robust economic evaluations [15, 35]. Considerations such as costs to patients, infrastructure requirements, provider reimbursement models, and health outcomes require further investigation to determine which teledentistry models are most effective across different contexts.

The idea of education being provided via teledentistry presents itself as an attractive benefit of the resource. The use of telehealth holds great potential in cultivating and sharing knowledge among healthcare providers who are located in remote areas [36]. This was broadly supported among our study's participants, as it was implied that this initiative would help non‐oral healthcare providers expand their oral healthcare knowledge and decision‐making capacity. Educational strategies that could further advance instruction in teledentistry could include the delivery of structured training modules, certification opportunities, and the integration of the subject matter into continuing professional development. Nevertheless, this embrace was not shared among participants when it came to teledentistry's potential as an educational tool for patients. However, results from a recent systematic review and meta‐analysis suggested that educational interventions delivered through telecommunication or electronic means might be valuable strategies with clinical implications, such as reductions in plaque index, gingival index, and the incidence of white spot lesions when compared to control interventions, such as in‐office education or not receiving digital engagements [37]. Furthermore, in the context of diabetic eye care, it has been reported that First Nations and Métis women see mHealth as a viable option for receiving education, especially when the strategy integrates the use of traditional languages, utilizes supportive and encouraging tones, and is co‐designed with the target population [38]. The integration of Indigenous communities in telehealth initiatives is a resounding point in the literature, as it must be ensured that telehealth approaches are culturally safe and respect Indigenous values [26, 29]. The exploration of utilizing teledentistry and virtual mediums for educational purposes should be further explored within this study and similar contexts. Adequately designed strategies that deliver timely, preventive oral healthcare education to caregivers and children could potentially play a role in decreasing the burden of oral disease in the region in which this study was conducted.

The feasibility of teledentistry was an issue that was raised in this study and is one that resonates with other populations' concerns regarding pediatric teledentistry implementation and telehealth use in Indigenous communities [17, 26, 27, 29]. The capacity to perform an adequate diagnosis through teledentistry was one of the voiced concerns among the dental providers in this study. Nevertheless, Casas et al., in a systematic review and meta‐analysis that compared teledentistry versus in‐person examinations, reported that caries diagnoses with the two methods demonstrated moderate to high sensitivity and specificity values, indicating a substantial level of agreement [39]. Although direct clinical diagnosis will always be desirable, routine diagnostic procedures via teledentistry can be achieved with appropriate training and equipment, making it especially valuable in remote communities. Nevertheless, feasibility regarding equipment, infrastructure, and internet connectivity must not be overlooked, as these are persistent challenges in Indigenous and remote telehealth endeavors [26, 29, 36, 38]. Manners in which infrastructure‐related feasibility issues can be addressed should consider integrating mobile‐based systems (e.g., facilitating the use through smartphones or tablets) and asynchronous data capturing [15]. Moreover, telehealth initiatives require sustained investment from health systems to ensure scalability and long‐term sustainability, while also addressing issues related to digital equity [15]. These models could potentially be financed through singular or mixed funding schemes by government agencies, Indigenous Health Authorities, or nongovernmental organizations that could target broad telehealth endeavors. Finally, resistance to change is a prevalent health service domain and thus unsurprisingly emerged within the present data, as it has been reported in other teledentistry contexts [40]. The best possible efforts must be made to implement teledentistry in a manner that accounts for the work climate where it is being implemented, and it does not limit workforce autonomy and increase the existing workload for healthcare providers [40].

This study's unique perspective on teledentistry, provided by the context and participants, is, in the authors' opinion, one of its biggest strengths. Though First Nations communities are richly diverse and unique throughout Canada, the results from this study could be valuable in informing health authorities and leadership on the benefits of teledentistry, especially if sanitary crises like COVID‐19 were to arise again. Furthermore, general elements of this study, such as teledentistry's potential for workforce integration and education use, could be applied or help stimulate interest in the topic in other remote and Indigenous communities. The limitations of this study must also be acknowledged. Although the aim of qualitative research is not generalizability, the characteristics of the participants in this study may constrain the extent to which the findings could be applied to other contexts. Moreover, the authors recognize that the perspectives of First Nations patients (e.g., caregivers) in the region should also be collected to complement this study's findings. Including caregiver perceptions is extremely valuable in terms of adequate pediatric teledentistry implementation planning and design, as their families and communities are the ones that stand to benefit from this service. The latter point is an important avenue of future research and health advocacy.

In conclusion, the findings of this study underscore the importance of seeking an understanding of perceptions toward the implementation of teledentistry from the very people who could utilize it for the population's benefit. This study seeks to contribute to the growing body of literature on telehealth and First Nations children's health, providing insights that can inform future research, policy development, and practice in Indigenous and remote healthcare settings. By harnessing the potential of teledentistry, while respecting cultural contexts and addressing systemic challenges, we can work toward achieving more equitable and accessible oral healthcare for Indigenous communities across Canada and beyond.
