Effectiveness of ChatGPT-assisted and drawing-based learning in enhancing understanding of intraoral radiographic anatomy among dental students: a comparative study
Authors: Suresh Kandagal Veerabhadrappa, Jayanth Kumar Vadivel, Seema Yadav, Vipin Kailasmal Jain, Anand Marya, Siddharthan Selvaraj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate identification of intraoral radiographic landmarks is essential for diagnosis; however, complex jaw anatomy presents learning challenges for undergraduate dental students. The educational potential of AI-assisted tools and drawing-based learning for intraoral radiographic anatomy remains unexplored. This study evaluated the effectiveness of ChatGPT-5.0-assisted learning and drawing-based learning as supplementary tools to enhance students’ understanding of intraoral radiographic anatomy.Materials and methodsThis comparative study included third-year Bachelor of Dental Surgery students. After a standardized lecture, students were randomly assigned to three groups: ChatGPT, self-directed learning, and drawing (n = 26 each), and baseline knowledge was assessed. The ChatGPT group used structured prompts, the drawing-based learning group drew and labelled landmarks, and the self-directed learning group studied independently. A post-intervention assessment was conducted after 12 weeks. Intragroup changes were analysed using paired t-tests with Cohen’s d, and intergroup differences using one-way ANOVA with Eta-squared (η²).ResultsChatGPT and drawing groups showed significantly greater improvement than the Self-directed learning group. The drawing group demonstrated the most consistent gains, with significant improvement in 12 landmarks and moderate-to-large effect sizes (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.45–0.77). The ChatGPT group showed significant improvements in several landmarks, particularly in the maxillary and mandibular anterior regions. Intergroup analysis showed higher post-instructional scores for the drawing group in 20 of 30 landmarks, with moderate-to-large η² values (≈ 0.08–0.25).ConclusionChatGPT and drawing-based learning are effective supplementary strategies for learning intraoral radiographic landmarks, outperforming self-directed learning alone. Their integration into dental radiology education may enhance anatomical understanding and long-term learning outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08307-5.Keywords: Anatomical Landmarks, Artificial Intelligence, ChatGPT, Drawing, Dental education, Education, Intraoral radiography, Self-directed

Introduction

Intraoral radiographs provide essential information regarding the teeth, alveolar bone, periapical regions, and surrounding anatomical structures of the jaws. A sound understanding of intraoral radiographic anatomy (IRA) is therefore imperative for dental students to accurately distinguish normal anatomical features from abnormalities and pathological changes [1]. Nonetheless, the complex anatomy of the jawbones, close proximity of anatomical landmarks, frequent superimposition, and inherent anatomical variations pose significant challenges in landmark identification [2]. Furthermore, the appearance and location of these landmarks vary according to the type of radiograph and the radiographic technique employed [3], which further complicates identification, particularly for students who are still developing familiarity with normal IRA. These factors may ultimately lead to misinterpretation and diagnostic errors [4].

IRA together with intraoral radiographic techniques, constitutes a core component of the introductory radiology curriculum in dental education. Traditionally, these topics are taught through didactic lectures, textbook-based learning, clinical discussions, and self-directed learning (SDL) [5]. Although these conventional methods are foundational and widely adopted, existing literature suggests that they often fall short in ensuring adequate comprehension due to one-way communication, limited interaction, and low student engagement [6]. Consequently, they may not provide the perceptual depth required for students to fully appreciate subtle anatomical details [7].

To overcome these limitations, virtual reality technologies (VRTs) have been explored as adjunctive tools for teaching radiographic anatomy, as contemporary learners increasingly prefer interactive [8], technology-driven educational approaches [9]. While VRTs have demonstrated positive effects on student engagement, their high implementation costs limit feasibility in institutions with constrained financial resources [10].

More recently, the Chat Generative Pre-Trained Transformer (ChatGPT), an artificial intelligence model developed by OpenAI, has emerged as a potential tool in medical and dental education [11]. Its deep learning–based natural language processing capabilities enable information analysis, clinical reasoning support [12], and facilitation of complex concept comprehension [13]. In radiology, ChatGPT has shown promise in identifying anatomical landmarks, recognizing pathologies, supporting diagnostic reasoning, and interpreting imaging-related content [11, 14]. Although prior studies report its use in radiology reporting and text-based examinations, evidence supporting its effectiveness for enhancing dental students’ comprehension and long-term retention of intraoral radiographic anatomical landmarks remains limited. ChatGPT was selected for this study due to its accessibility, cost-effectiveness, interactive format, and suitability for learning IRA.

Drawing exercises have long been employed by medical and dental educators as an effective strategy to enhance understanding of human anatomy [15–17]. Previous studies have demonstrated that manual and digital tracing of anatomical landmarks in panoramic radiographs and lateral cephalograms improves comprehension of anatomical structures [18, 19]. However, similar tracing approaches are not feasible for intraoral radiographs due to their smaller size. Consequently, the application of drawing-based learning (DBL) for IRA remains unexplored in dental radiology.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of AI-supported learning using ChatGPT-5.0–assisted learning (ChatGPT-AL) and DBL in enhancing undergraduate dental students’ understanding and identification of IRA. Student performance in the ChatGPT-AL and DBL groups was compared with that of a self-directed learning (SDL) group among third-year Bachelor of Dental Surgery (BDS) students.

Materials and methods

Study design

This comparative study was conducted among third-year BDS students in the Department of Oral Diagnostic Sciences, Faculty of Dentistry, SEGi University. The study was conducted during the radiology module over a four-month period from September to December 2025.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Students who attended the introductory lecture on IRA and were willing to participate and complete all components of the study were included. Students with prior exposure to AI-assisted radiographic tools for learning anatomical landmarks or those with incomplete participation at any stage of the study were excluded.

At the beginning of the study, all participants attended a compulsory two-hour theoretical lecture delivered by a board-certified oral radiologist. The lecture covered fundamental concepts of IRA, including anatomical landmarks, their radiographic appearance, and clinical significance, using a standardized PowerPoint presentation by an oral radiologist. Following the lecture, students were randomly and equally assigned to three groups (n = 26 per group) using a computer-generated randomization sequence performed by the oral radiologist who delivered the introductory lecture (Fig. 1).

Baseline score evaluation

Immediately after the lecture and group allocation, a baseline assessment was conducted using a 30-item written test. The test, developed by an oral radiologist with over 10 years of experience who was not involved in the study, included intraoral radiographs of patients with specific anatomical landmarks. These landmarks were selected based on standard oral radiology textbooks and the predefined learning objectives of the oral radiology curriculum. Students were required to identify all 30 marked anatomical landmarks within 30 min. Responses were evaluated by a single experienced oral radiologist blinded to group allocation, using a standardized answer key in which one mark was awarded for each correct identification, and incorrect or missing responses received zero.

Study groups

Group A

ChatGPT- AL- Students used ChatGPT 5.0 to interactively study intraoral radiographic anatomical landmarks. Prior to the learning sessions, participants received training from an oral radiologist on the effective use of the tool, including formulating queries, using guided prompts, and interpreting responses.

Students were provided with standardized prompts and guiding questions for each anatomical landmark. These prompts covered the landmark name, anatomical location, radiographic appearance, clinical significance, and simple memory aids. In addition, follow-up questions were included to reinforce understanding and promote active learning. A checklist of anatomical landmarks was also provided, and the detailed prompts are described in Supplementary File 1.

Students were instructed to use ChatGPT 5.0 for 30 to 45 min per day for four-week period. To ensure adherence and consistency, they were required to submit compiled transcripts of their interactions through the institutional online learning platform at the end of the intervention period. The transcripts were reviewed for completeness by an oral radiologist who was not involved in group allocation or outcome assessment.

Group B

SDL- Students assigned to the SDL group independently studied IRA using conventional learning resources, including textbooks, lecture notes, and instructional videos.

Group C

DBL- Students were instructed to draw and label intraoral radiographic anatomical landmarks visible on standard intraoral radiographs of the maxillary and mandibular jaws. They were instructed to accurately draw and label these landmarks over four-week period. To support learning, reference images of standard intraoral radiographs along with correctly labelled drawings were provided.

Completed drawings were submitted via the institutional online learning platform at the end of the eight-week period (Figs. 2A–B, 3A–B and 4A–B). The submissions were evaluated for completeness, accuracy of anatomical representation, and correctness of labelling by an independent oral radiologist who was not involved in group allocation or outcome assessment.

Post-intervention score evaluation

Twelve weeks after submission of the drawings and ChatGPT 5.0 transcripts, a post-intervention assessment was conducted. All participants completed a 30-item written test similar in format to the baseline assessment, using intraoral radiographs marked with anatomical landmarks. Assessment of post-intervention tests was performed by a blinded oral radiologist with over 10 years of clinical and academic experience, who was not involved in the theoretical teaching or group allocation.

Ethical considerations and confidentiality

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Research Board (SEGIEC/SR/FOD/15/2024–2025). All participants were informed about the study objectives, methodology, and confidentiality measures, and written informed consent was obtained prior to commencement. No personally identifiable information was collected.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software (Version 26; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Intra-group comparisons between baseline and post-intervention scores were analysed using paired-samples t-tests. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated as the mean difference divided by the pooled standard deviation of the baseline and post-instructional scores.

The inter-group comparisons post-instructional scores were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Eta Squared (η²) was calculated to determine the practical significance of intergroup differences. All statistical analyses were two-tailed. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The SDL group demonstrated marginal improvement in 24 of the 30 landmarks however, a statistically significant change was observed only for the median palatine suture (p = 0.016). Few landmarks showed negative Cohen’s d values (Table 1).

The DBL group exhibited consistent improvement across all the anatomical landmarks, with statistically significant gains observed for 12 landmarks. These included the Y-line of Ennis (p = 0.006), zygomatic process (p = 0.004), hamular process (p = 0.005), pterygoid plate (p = 0.043), nasolacrimal duct (p = 0.001), genial tubercle (p = 0.011), lingual foramen (p = 0.029), mental fossa (p = 0.009), nutrient canals (p = 0.032), mental foramen (p = 0.018), and the submandibular fossa (p = 0.018) (Table 1). Effect size analysis demonstrated moderate to large Cohen’s d values (≈ 0.45–0.77) for these landmarks.

The DBL group achieved higher post-instructional scores in 20 out of 30 anatomical landmarks compared with the ChatGPT-AL and SDL groups. Higher scores were observed for three landmarks in the ChatGPT-AL group, while six landmarks demonstrated comparable post-instructional scores between the ChatGPT-AL and DBL groups.

A region-wise comparison of anatomical landmarks across the three groups was performed (Table 2). In the maxillary anterior region, statistically significant differences were observed for the shadow of the nose and the Y-line of Ennis. For both landmarks, post-instructional scores were higher in the DBL and ChatGPT-AL groups compared with the SDL group (Table 2).

In the maxillary posterior region, statistically significant differences were noticed for the radiopaque structures, including the zygomatic process (p = 0.012), coronoid process (p = 0.039), hamular process (p < 0.001), and pterygoid plate (p = 0.020). For these landmarks, both the ChatGPT-AL and DBL groups outperformed the SDL group, with the DBL group demonstrating the highest post-instructional scores. Among the radiolucent structures, a significant difference was observed for the nasolacrimal duct (p < 0.001), where the DBL group achieved the highest score, followed by the ChatGPT-AL and SDL group (Table 2).

In the mandibular anterior region, statistically significant differences were observed for the genial tubercle (p = 0.003), lingual foramen (p = 0.037), mental fossa (p = 0.005), and nutrient canals (p = 0.001). For these landmarks, the DBL group achieved the highest post-instructional scores while the SDL group demonstrated comparatively lower performance. In the mandibular posterior region, the DBL group demonstrated higher scores across all landmarks. However, a statistically significant difference was observed only for the submandibular fossa (p = 0.018), with a near-significant trend noted for the external oblique ridge (p = 0.051).

The effect sizes, as indicated by Eta-Squared values (η²) were of moderate to large magnitude (η² ≈ 0.08–0.25) and substantial in size. The highest effect sizes were for landmarks such as the Y-line of Ennis (η² = 0.203), Hamular process (η² = 0.206), and the nasolacrimal duct (η² = 0.250) (Table 2).

Discussion

Previous studies have evaluated the effectiveness of tracing anatomical landmarks on extraoral radiographs, such as panoramic radiographs and lateral cephalograms [18, 19]. However, in intraoral radiography, the presentation of anatomical landmarks differs considerably in terms of location, radiographic appearance, and their relationship with adjacent structures. Therefore, the present study systematically assessed the effectiveness of ChatGPT-AL and DBL as supplementary instructional tools for enhancing students’ comprehension of IRA.

Intragroup comparisons of scores in ChatGPT-AL group demonstrated improvements for complex landmarks in the maxillary posterior and mandibular regions, including the zygomatic process, coronoid process, hamular process, nasolacrimal duct, mental fossa, and mental foramen. These findings suggest that ChatGPT-AL may enhance conceptual understanding of anatomical structures that are often difficult to recognize [20].

Ocbe et al. [21] reported that ChatGPT-4.0 correctly identified 25 of 35 anatomical landmarks on panoramic radiographs, performing better for soft-tissue landmarks and ghost images than for hard-tissue structures, with frequent misidentification of closely positioned landmarks. Similarly, Kahalian et al. [13] observed that ChatGPT-4.0 showed improved identification of anatomical landmarks, cysts, and tumors after the provision of additional diagnostic clues, while limitations persisted in distinguishing adjacent structures and generating comprehensive differential diagnoses.

In contrast, the present study employed a structured, text-based conversational approach in which students used predefined prompts to understand the radiographic appearance and anatomical location of each landmark, rather than identifying pre-marked images. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on image-based interactions using earlier model versions [22, 23], this approach emphasized systematic text-based prompts to support conceptual learning. Notably, the diagnostic and interpretative performance of ChatGPT has been shown to vary across versions (3.0–4.5), with newer models demonstrating improved capabilities in radiology-related tasks [24].

The educational potential of large language models in anatomy education is increasingly recognized, with studies reporting high accuracy in answering head and neck anatomy questions [25]. Furthermore, personalized learning strategies, such as Medical Subject Headings–based guides and individualized feedback generated by advanced language models, have been shown to significantly improve radiographic diagnostic performance among dental students, supporting their role as effective learning tools [26].

Although the SDL group showed marginal improvement for few landmarks, a decline in scores for several maxillary anterior radiopaque landmarks was observed, suggesting that learning based primarily on memorization may diminish without reinforcement [27]. While SDL remains an important component of dental radiology education, these findings indicate that unstructured SDL alone may be inadequate for mastering complex intraoral radiographic landmarks. Furthermore, lack of guided instruction, feedback, and reinforcement may limit students’ ability to resolve ambiguities and recognize subtle radiographic features, particularly during the early stages of IRA learning [27].

The DBL group demonstrated the most consistent improvement across both jaws, with significant gains in several complex landmarks, including the Y-line of Ennis, zygomatic process, hamular process, pterygoid plate, nasolacrimal duct, genial tubercle, lingual foramen, mental fossa, nutrient canals, and submandibular fossa. This superior performance may be attributed to the active learning inherent in drawing tasks, which require students to visually reconstruct, spatially organize, and label anatomical structures [15, 16].

Intergroup comparisons of post-instructional scores showed that the ChatGPT-AL and DBL groups outperformed the SDL group, with the DBL group achieving the highest scores for complex landmarks. Moderate-to-large effect sizes were observed in these regions, particularly the maxillary posterior and mandibular anterior regions, which are often difficult to interpret due to structural superimposition and variability in radiographic appearance. These findings indicate that the improvements were not only statistically significant but also educationally meaningful. Furthermore, the magnitude of the effect sizes highlights the practical impact of the interventions, demonstrating that both DBL and ChatGPT-AL enhanced students’ ability to identify complex anatomical landmarks.

Drawing exercises have been widely adopted as an adjunctive learning strategy in dental education with favourable outcomes. In the present study, DBL resulted in improvements across all anatomical regions. This may be attributed to its engagement of active learning processes, including tactile and visual interaction, which enhance spatial recognition and motor involvement, thereby strengthening memory retention and promoting deeper learning [16, 28–30]. Previous studies support these findings. Integrating drawing into dental anatomy education has been shown to enhance anatomical understanding and improve clinical skills [16], clinical competence [28, 29], and manual dexterity among dental students [30–32].

A recent study on ChatGPT-AL showed improved student comprehension of bony landmarks and air spaces in panoramic radiographs compared with SDL [20]. However, performance was lower than with manual tracing, suggesting that combining traditional and ChatGPT-AL may optimize learning in dental radiology [20]. In the present study, the superior performance of the ChatGPT-AL group, particularly in the maxillary anterior and posterior regions, may be attributed to interactive explanations, conceptual clarification, and personalized feedback.

From a cognitive perspective, both approaches engage complementary learning mechanisms. ChatGPT-AL primarily supports verbal–conceptual processing through explanation, reasoning, and reinforcement, whereas DBL emphasizes visuospatial processing, requiring learners to actively construct knowledge through visual representation. The combination of these approaches may provide a more comprehensive learning experience by addressing both conceptual understanding and spatial cognition.

Region-wise analysis revealed that the most pronounced intergroup differences occurred in the mandibular anterior region, except for the mental ridge, followed by the maxillary posterior region. Fewer significant differences were observed in the maxillary anterior region. These findings likely reflect variations in anatomical complexity. Previous studies have shown that bony landmarks are generally easier to identify due to their distinct radiographic outlines and consistent anatomical positions, whereas non-bony structures are more challenging to recognize [19–21]. Consistent with these observations, higher baseline scores were noted for bony landmarks compared with non-bony landmarks in our study.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single institution with a relatively small sample of third-year students, which may limit generalizability and applicability to learners at other stages of training. Second, individual factors such as prior academic performance, learning preferences, and digital proficiency were not assessed. Third, the study did not evaluate the transfer of knowledge to clinical practice. Additionally, the follow-up period was limited to twelve weeks and may not reflect long-term retention. Finally, formal validation of the assessment tool, including content validation, pilot testing, and inter-rater reliability assessment, was not performed, and allocation concealment was not implemented.

Future research involving multicentre designs, larger and more diverse cohorts, and the inclusion of clinical performance outcomes is recommended to further validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

ChatGPT-AL and DBL were effective supplementary tools for enhancing students’ understanding of IRA compared to SDL, suggesting that interactive instructional approaches are more effective than conventional SDL. DBL produced the most consistent and widespread improvements by promoting active visualization and engagement. In contrast, ChatGPT-AL provides a cost-effective interactive tool for conceptual understanding of IRA. Incorporating DBL and ChatGPT-AL into undergraduate dental radiology curricula may enhance both conceptual understanding and long-term retention of IRA.
