Process Reward Modeling with Entropy-Driven Uncertainty
Lang Cao, Renhong Chen, Yingtian Zou, Chao Peng

Abstract
This paper presents the Entropy-Driven Unified Process Reward Model (EDU-PRM), a novel framework that approximates state-of-the-art performance in process supervision while drastically reducing training costs. EDU-PRM introduces an entropy-guided dynamic step partitioning mechanism, using logit distribution entropy to pinpoint high-uncertainty regions during token generation dynamically. This self-assessment capability enables precise step-level feedback without manual fine-grained annotation, addressing a critical challenge in process supervision. Experiments on the Qwen2.5-72B model with only 7,500 EDU-PRM-generated training queries demonstrate accuracy closely approximating the full Qwen2.5-72B-PRM (71.1% vs. 71.6%), achieving a 98% reduction in query cost compared to prior methods. This work establishes EDU-PRM as an efficient approach for scalable process reward model training.



1
Introduction

The rise of large language models (LLMs)
Zhao et al. 2025
;
Vaswani et al. 2023
has redefined the boundaries of machine reasoning, yet their ability to systematically solve complex problems from mathematical proofs to multi-step decision-making
Wang et al. 2024b
;
Zhang et al. 2025a
remains constrained by a critical bottleneck: how to evaluate and optimize intermediate reasoning steps. While methods like chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting
Wei et al. 2022
and step-aware verifiers
Li et al. 2022
have advanced LLMs’ ability to generate structured reasoning, they rely heavily on human annotations or post-hoc verification, often failing to provide scalable, real-time feedback during the reasoning process. As an alternative approach, Reward models (RMs)
Wang et al. 2024a
refine the outputs to increase reliability
Yang et al. 2024a
. Unlike Outcome RM
Yuan et al. 2024
;
Luo et al. 2024b
, which gives sparse feedback, Process reward models (PRMs)
Li and Li 2025
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
address the bottleneck by introducing dense, stepwise supervision that directly targets the core challenge of intermediate reasoning quality.

Traditional PRMs are constrained by two limitations: their reliance on fixed reasoning paths, which disregard uncertainty in complex solution spaces
Zhang et al. 2025b
;
Luo et al. 2024a
, and their costly step-segmentation workflows
Lightman et al. 2023
. The deterministic nature of conventional PRMs fails to identify critical decision points where reasoning trajectories diverge.
Currently, previous step segmentation initiatives have entailed substantial costs, either through labour-intensive manual annotation or post-annotation fine-tuning, that are poorly scaled with complex taxonomies
Wang et al. 2024d
. Even when augmented with Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) to explore combinatorial path spaces
Luo et al. 2024a
, these models still suffer from high sampling variance
Song et al. 2024
due to their inability to dynamically identify uncertainty-rich branching points.

Our investigation reveals that lexical tokens dismissed as irrelevant in prior work actually encode significant entropy signals marking critical junctures
Mirzadeh et al. 2024
.
This finding inspires us to propose the Entropy-Driven Uncertainty PRM (EDU-PRM), which leverages high-uncertainty tokens as natural branching points. By parallelizing top-1 and top-2 logit computations at these junctures
Wang and Zhou 2024
, EDU-PRM dynamically explores different possible reasoning paths, thus increasing the diversity.

Our contribution lies in the development of a novel data generation approach called EDU-PRM for PRM training that ensures both result diversity and generation quality. Specifically, the proposed EDU-PRM architecture achieves performance equivalent to the state-of-the-art Qwen2.5-72B-PRM
Zhang et al. 2025b
using only 7,500 queries—a significant query cost reduction compared to prior PRM methods. This efficiency stems from its entropy-guided dynamic step partitioning mechanism, which leverages logit distribution entropy as an uncertainty metric to dynamically identify regions of high predictive uncertainty during generation. By enabling the model to self-assess uncertainty at each token generation step, EDU-PRM addresses the critical challenge of process supervision requiring precise feedback at every reasoning step, while avoiding the impracticality of manual fine-grained annotation.

2
Related Works

The discriminative methods for LLM outputs have evolved from rule-based heuristics to sophisticated reward modeling frameworks. Early approaches relied on handcrafted rules to match keywords or structural patterns in generated outputs
Mu et al. 2024
, such as numerical answers in math problems. These methods were efficient but domain-specific and unable to handle open-ended tasks. With the rise of instruction-tuned LLMs, researchers shifted to LLM-as-judge
Zheng et al. 2023
paradigms, where models were prompted to self-evaluate outputs for logic or factual accuracy. While eliminating manual rules, this approach suffered from self-verification
Wang et al. 2023
biases and high computational costs.

A significant advancement came with ORMs
Wang et al. 2024c
;
Yuan et al. 2024
;
Luo et al. 2024b
, which used human-annotated labels to score final outputs directly. However, ORMs ignored intermediate reasoning steps, leading to misjudgments of outputs with flawed processes but correct results. Recent breakthroughs highlight PRMs
Lightman et al. 2023
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
, introducing fine-grained supervision by scoring substeps (e.g., reasoning chains) with soft labels (continuous quality scores from LLMs) or hard labels (binary correctness from experts). PRMs improve reliability in tasks like math reasoning and code generation by penalizing flawed processes.

Despite PRM’s dominance, challenges remain: label efficiency (costly expert annotations vs. biased LLM-generated soft labels), cross-task generalization (task-specific PRMs lack universality)
Wu et al. 2024
, and ethical alignment (ensuring process rewards reflect human values)
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025
.

3
Method

3.1
Data Generation

This section presents a novel methodology for constructing the Entropy-Driven Uncertainty Process Reward Model (EDU PRM), characterized by two distinctive methodological innovations. First, we propose a stepwise decomposition framework guided by entropy to systematically address solution space exploration. Secondly, our architecture employs an adaptive branching mechanism. During the branching process, we utilize the logits ranked first and second (top-1 and top-2 logits) as parallel computational pathways.
This dual-strategy approach synergistically combines probabilistic reasoning with combinatorial optimization principles, enabling both rigorous uncertainty management and efficient solution space navigation.

3.1.1
Segmented by Entropy

During the decoding phase of LLMs, the softmax function is applied to the logits at the current sequence position. The resulting predictive entropy is then leveraged to identify structural boundaries in the generated output. Specifically, when the entropy value exceeds a predefined threshold, the corresponding token is designated as a separation point to partition the context into step-by-step solutions.

Given an LLM output logits tensor
\mathbf{O}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times S\times V}
(where
B
= batch size,
S
= sequence length,
V
= vocabulary size), the entropy calculation proceeds as follows:

1.

Token Logit Extraction
For the most recent token position across all batches:

\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{O}_{:,S-1,:}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times V}

This operation isolates the model’s conditional predictions for the next token at the current decoding step
Kwon et al. 2023
;
Aminabadi et al. 2022
.

2.

Probability Distribution Normalization
Applying the softmax function along the vocabulary dimension
Shen et al. 2023
:

\mathbf{P}=\text{softmax}(\mathbf{L})\quad

\text{with}\quad P_{b,v}=\frac{\exp(L_{b,v})}{\sum_{v^{\prime}=1}^{V}\exp(L_{b,v^{\prime}})}

Here,
P_{b,v}
denotes the normalized probability of token
v
in batch
b
.

3.

Entropy Calculation
The Shannon entropy
H_{b}

Baez 2024
for each batch is computed as:

H_{b}=-\sum_{v=1}^{V}P_{b,v}\cdot\log\left(P_{b,v}+\epsilon\right)

A small smoothing constant (
\epsilon=10^{-10}
) is introduced to ensure numerical stability, preventing undefined gradients when
P_{b,v}=0
.

3.1.2
Decoding Strategy

At the separation point (termed the branch point in PRM), rather than conventional high-temperature sampling, we initiate two decoding branches by selecting the top-1 and top-2 logits. For subsequent decoding, we exclusively employ greedy sampling to generate content until the next branching event
Wang and Zhou 2024
.

Empirical analysis revealed that specific mathematical symbols often induce decoding artifacts when selected as branching nodes. To address this, we introduce a whitelist mechanism to explicitly exclude these symbols from node detection criteria. This ensures that even symbols with entropy values meeting the threshold condition are not recognized as branching nodes, preserving core branching functionality while maintaining generation quality.

Formally, let
S
denote the whitelist logit set. The revised separation condition is:

H>\tau\quad\text{and}\quad\{z_{1},z_{2},\cdots,z_{V}\}\not\subseteq S

3.1.3
Data reorganization and Tokenizer

After the aforementioned data generation process, we perform slicing using the MC-estimate
Katzgraber 2011
method to obtain response segments corresponding to each question, along with the probability of each segment being ultimately correct.

All label values were truncated to two decimal places. These normalized labels were subsequently encoded as consecutive special tokens within the model’s vocabulary, where each token corresponds to a unique value in this predefined numerical sequence through a mapping relationship.

3.2
Model Training

In contrast to conventional supervised fine-tuning, our methodology introduces two dedicated special tokens to represent positive/negative categories. During training, a masked loss strategy is employed: only prediction errors for these two special tokens are computed, while losses from all other sequence tokens are systematically ignored. This targeted optimization focuses the model’s learning on distinguishing class-specific representations through dedicated token embeddings.

The methodology comprises three steps:

1.

Label Normalization:

Raw integer labels
l_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
are normalized using constants
C_{1},C_{2}
:

\tilde{l}_{i}=\frac{l_{i}-C_{1}}{C_{2}},

scaling labels to a bounded range suitable for probabilistic supervision (where
C_{1},C_{2}
are the start of special token ID and 101 in our method).

2.

Target Distribution Construction:

Each normalized label
\tilde{l}_{i}
(quasi-probability range) generates a 2D target distribution:

\mathbf{y}_{i}=\left[\tilde{l}_{i},\ 1-\tilde{l}_{i}\right],

enforcing class complementarity (unit sum supervision target).

3.

Loss Computation:

Let
\mathbf{z}_{i}=[z_{i0},z_{i1}]
denote class logits. Cross-entropy loss between softmax probabilities
\mathbf{p}_{i}=\text{softmax}(\mathbf{z}_{i})
and target
\mathbf{y}_{i}
:

\mathcal{L}_{i}=-\sum_{k=0}^{1}y_{ik}\log p_{ik},\quad

\text{where }p_{ik}=\frac{e^{z_{ik}}}{\sum_{k^{\prime}}e^{z_{ik^{\prime}}}}\text{ (softmax probabilities)}.

Final loss (with distributed averaging) over
N
masked tokens:

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}_{i}.

4
Experiments

4.1
Data Generation

For this experiment, we utilized 7500 training samples from the MATH dataset
Hendrycks et al. 2021
, processing up to 120 samples per query. Applying entropy-based filtering with an optimal threshold of 1.0, we generated 723,000 query-response pairs for evaluation. Each instance comprised a problem-solution pair annotated with binary correctness labels (0: incorrect, 1: correct), serving as training data for the Outcome Reward Model (ORM).

The dataset was systematically partitioned: 682,000 process-oriented slices were reserved for Soft PRM training. Each procedural step’s quality was evaluated via Monte Carlo (MC) estimation, generating continuous reward signals normalized to the [0,1] interval. By integrating PRM and Outcome Reward Model (ORM) training datasets, we constructed a composite training set comprising 1.4 million instances.

4.2
Models

This study adopts a dual-phase model collaboration framework for dataset construction: The primary phase generates initial data using the Qwen2-7B-Instruct model
Yang et al. 2024b
, followed by cross-model consistency validation via the advanced Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct model
Qwen et al. 2025
to ensure semantic alignment with reference solutions. The workflow concludes with supervised fine-tuning of the Entropy-Driven Uncertainty (EDU) Process Reward Model (PRM) module on the Qwen2.5-72B architecture
Qwen et al. 2025
.
For comparative analysis, we employed two state-of-the-art baselines: Qwen2.5-72B-PRM
Zhang et al. 2025b
and Qwen2.5-72B-RM
Yang et al. 2024c
. Best-of-N (BoN) sampling results
Sun et al. 2024
were validated for correctness using GPT-4

;

, establishing a gold-standard verification pipeline.

4.3.2
BoN Evaluation

This study systematically evaluates the efficacy of reward modelling in refining language model outputs. Using the Qwen2-7B-Instruct architecture as the experimental platform, we quantitatively benchmark how Process Reward Models (PRMs) and Outcome Reward Models (ORMs) influence output quality via Best-of-N (BoN) decoding. Our analysis focuses on accuracy (Acc) metrics over the MATH test dataset, establishing a baseline through 0-shot Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning outputs.

This demonstrates the EDU-PRM’s efficiency in leveraging uncertainty-aware branching to approximate full-scale PRM performance with 62× fewer training instances.

5
Conclusion

This work introduces an entropy-guided decoding framework that dynamically balances generation quality and computational efficiency through adaptive branching strategies. By integrating entropy-thresholded node detection (augmented with a symbol whitelist to prevent decoding artifacts) and hierarchical path exploration—combining greedy decoding with top-k sampling at critical nodes—our method achieves performance on par with the full-scale Qwen2.5-72B-PRM baseline (trained on 500K queries) using only 7,500 training queries (1.5% of the baseline’s data scale).
