Using social media data to improve specific urban park activities: the case of park camping
Authors: Jiarui Liu, Shuhao Liu, Ruike Xiao, Lian Liu, Chi Zhang, Jia Ma, Xiong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmerging urban nature-based leisure practices in public parks require activity-oriented evidence that can identify experience gaps, prioritize improvement needs, and clarify feasibility boundaries under governance constraints; this study develops an interpretable workflow using urban park camping as a representative case. We collected 5449 Little Red Book posts, retained 4,840 valid posts after cleaning, and extracted 71 camping-related topics via LDA with TF-IDF weighting; keyword-level sentiment evaluation and ODI opportunity scoring were then applied to prioritize underserved topics, and semi-structured expert interviews provided cross-actor validation and feasibility clarification. Across all topic keywords, the mean sentiment score was 0.654, indicating generally positive evaluations, while negative evaluations concentrated on a limited set of high-salience elements within topics, enabling targeted diagnosis beyond theme-level summaries. Six high-opportunity topics were identified as priority areas for improvement, and integrating within-topic weight–sentiment configurations, opportunity scores, and expert evidence revealed three linked mechanisms explaining persistent gaps in park camping: misalignment between activity demand and feasible supply, scale differences between comfort-oriented environmental evaluation and cumulative ecological constraints, and conflict amplification through shortcomings in basic facilities and reservation management. Methodologically, the study operationalizes transparent prioritization by combining importance- and satisfaction-related signals and by locating dissatisfaction drivers at the keyword level; practically, it provides a structured basis for targeting peak-sensitive bottlenecks and communicating feasibility boundaries for boundary-sensitive demands in urban parks.Keywords: Urban park, Social media, Camping, Topic modeling, Sentiment analysisSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Urban parks are an essential component of urban public green infrastructure. They provide settings for everyday recreation and social interaction, and they play a foundational role in improving environmental quality and supporting psychological and physiological well-being1–5. As lifestyles diversify and living standards rise, public expectations for urban park recreation have increased, with growing emphasis on activity diversity, participation, autonomy, and professional service quality6. At the same time, urban parks commonly face heightened management pressures during peak periods, including crowding and competition for space, visible complaints about cleanliness and maintenance7, and disputes over how to priorities competing needs under constrained resources8,9. Activities that involve longer stays and stronger space occupation amplify these pressures10, because operational weaknesses accumulate over time into dissatisfaction and governance tension, and can further trigger disputes related to order and fairness11.

Against this social backdrop, urban nature-based leisure practices have proliferated and are increasingly becoming a key object of service provision in urban parks. Such practices typically involve diverse participation modes, longer stays, stronger space occupation, and more pronounced externalities, making structural tensions between experiential benefits and governance constraints more likely12,13. On the one hand, they extend the public-service functions and nature-experience value of urban parks. On the other hand, they are more likely to expose coupled contradictions among activity provision, environmental comfort, facility assurance, and order maintenance7,14. Accordingly, activity-oriented experience research on emerging urban nature-based leisure practices has clear governance relevance and a distinct academic focus: under high-density public-space conditions, how activity-specific experience dimensions take shape, and how experience gaps and perceived conflicts are triggered and persist over time.

Urban park camping is one of the most representative forms of these emerging practices15. Compared with short-duration park visits, camping involves longer stays and temporary occupation of lawn space, and it relies more heavily on on-site service conditions and orderly use. It combines relaxation and leisure, family-oriented social interaction, and comfort-oriented environmental experience within a single activity context16. Because the duration and space occupation are higher, experiential benefits, expressions of dissatisfaction, and governance constraints are more likely to co-occur within the same practice. This makes camping a clearer activity-scale entry point for understanding the formation of experience gaps and the emergence of perceived conflicts in high-density public spaces17.

Literature review

Advantages, biases, and the need for activity-specific interpretation in social-media evidence for urban park research

Platforms such as Weibo, Twitter, and Facebook provide large-scale and time-sensitive information on public opinions and behaviors18. Existing studies use user-generated texts, photographs, and comments to identify landscape and activity preferences19, to characterise park users and reveal differences across age, region, and gender20, and thereby to support planning and management decisions that account for user heterogeneity21. Research also indicates that park attributes and surrounding contexts influence experience and behavior, shaping perceptions of infrastructure completeness22, vegetation coverage23, socio-cultural background24, and the density of surrounding service facilities25. Geotagged data have also been used to infer spatiotemporal patterns of park use and seasonal differences26,27.

These advances enhance early sensing of experience signals, yet they also reveal recurrent challenges in activity-oriented inference. Social-media signals are shaped by platform user composition, self-selection in posting, and uneven spatial representation, and discourse visibility does not equate to governance priority. Recent studies based on online reviews and social-media assessments of urban parks show that negative evaluations often concentrate on a small number of specific operational problems and vary across seasons and events7,14, suggesting that dissatisfaction drivers need to be located at a finer level than broad themes. Systematic reviews further emphasise representativeness limitations and cross-platform differences28. These constraints imply that when social-media evidence is used for prioritisation, interpretive granularity must be improved and cross-actor validation should be introduced to reduce the risk that expression intensity is misread as problem severity.

Interpretability limits and within-topic structure in topic models and sentiment analysis for experience measurement

Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA), lexicon-based classification, machine learning, and large language model (LLM) approaches are widely used to extract themes and sentiment information from user-generated content29–31. In urban park research, these methods provide quantitative evidence for experience-related issues such as landscape aesthetics, facility quality, and environmental comfort, and they support evidence-informed optimization of park design and management32,33. Their advantage lies in scalability and comparability across time or across sites.

However, interpretability is often limited when results remain at the level of topic labels or mean topic sentiment. Topic-level summaries can obscure within-topic concentration of dissatisfaction. Negative experience is often driven by a small number of concrete elements, while other elements within the same topic may remain positively evaluated. Studies of urban park online reviews indicate that negative feedback often focuses on specific operational aspects such as maintenance, cleanliness, costs, and responsiveness7,14. Recent social-media-based management assessments further show that issues related to activities, facilities, environment, and services fluctuate with seasons and events, and fine-grained text signals can reveal management weaknesses that broad themes fail to capture14. This motivates a within-topic interpretation strategy: keyword weights can represent salient concerns, and keyword-level sentiment can locate where negative evaluations concentrate, improving traceability between evidence and mechanism interpretation and providing a more stable basis for prioritization.

Prioritization challenges and cross-actor feasibility validation in activity-oriented urban park experience research

Beyond general visitation studies, activity-scale research in urban parks has expanded. Walking, running, and cycling remain primary subjects and are often analyzed using social media and fitness-platform data34–37. Research shows that environmental features and path design influence activity intensity and spatial distribution34,35, and that activity types differ in spatial coverage and temporal concentration37. These findings explain traditional use behavior, yet emerging practices introduce additional governance complexity.

Long-stay practices such as camping increase duration, space occupation, and externalities, making trade-offs among experience, equity, and ecology more salient. Social-media analyses show clustering of camping sites in large cities and preference differentiation, including gender-based differences38. Multimodal studies also highlight collective practices such as outdoor classes and group gatherings39. Resource constraints prevent managers from responding to all expressed needs simultaneously, so activity-oriented research requires prioritization frameworks40. Importance–Performance Analysis has been used to identify priority sequences for multifunctional optimization in urban parks41, and opportunity-scoring logic provides an additional pathway for identifying targets that are important yet insufficiently satisfied. Meanwhile, activity-oriented issues often include boundary-sensitive topics whose feasibility is shaped by safety, ecology, and policy. Social media cannot reliably infer such feasibility conditions28. Cross-actor evidence is therefore needed to distinguish direct service deficits from tensions bounded by feasibility constraints, and to explain divergence between immediate user experience and longer-term management considerations.

Existing research demonstrates the value of social media for identifying experience signals in urban parks and shows that topic and sentiment methods can structure large-scale discourse. However, activity-specific urban park experience research repeatedly encounters three limitations. First, activity-specific experience dimensions are insufficiently specified. Activities are often treated as broad labels, and the concrete elements that drive dissatisfaction and experience gaps are not consistently identified. Second, prioritization lacks an operational and interpretable quantitative framework. Studies can present themes and complaints, yet they often lack a resource-constrained ranking logic. Third, UGC evidence is commonly interpreted from a single-actor perspective, and the absence of cross-actor validation leaves feasibility boundaries and institutional constraints unclear, which sustains credibility concerns in governance-oriented interpretation. These limitations make research more likely to remain at the level of trend description or action lists, and less able to produce testable mechanism explanations or support prioritized governance decisions. To respond to the social context of rising peak pressure and conflict perception, this study selects urban park camping as a representative urban nature-based leisure practice. It identifies activity experience dimensions from user discourse, derives a prioritized set of improvement opportunities by combining importance- and satisfaction-related information, and uses expert evidence for cross-actor validation and boundary clarification. This positioning provides the conceptual basis and evidential pathway for the subsequent research questions and methodological design.

Methods

The method proposed in this study for identifying park activity opportunities through social media mining is based on three theoretical foundations: topic modeling, sentiment analysis, and opportunity algorithm (Fig. 1).

Data source and preprocessing

We collected 5,449 posts marked with the hashtag #urban park camping on the Little Red Book website from May 29, 2021, to April 10, 2024. The social media data used in this study were obtained through the publicly accessible open API provided by the Little Red Book platform42. These posts covered 29 provincial-level administrative units across China (Fig. 2). Little Red Book had over 300 million users by 202343, and it is primarily positioned as a lifestyle platform and often referred to as “China’s Instagram”. Among the five major lifestyle categories on Little Red Book, camping and hiking were the hottest topics in 2022, with the number of posts increasing by 287% year-on-year, ranking first and accounting for 23.61% of the total posts44. Therefore, Little Red Book can provide many user shares and feedback on park camping45. Because the analytical workflow in this study is text-based, it requires sufficiently rich experience-oriented textual descriptions; compared with short-video-centric platforms such as TikTok, Little Red Book posts typically provide longer narratives that are directly suitable for topic modeling, keyword-level sentiment evaluation, and ODI opportunity scoring. Among the 5,449 posts, user gender information was available for 4,357 posts (approximately 80%), of which 3,351 (61.5%) were posted by female users. According to the 2022 Camping Quality Research Report by the travel platform Mafengwo, women were the primary decision-makers in camping-related consumption in China in 2021, accounting for 64% of the camping population46. This similarity indicates that although Little Red Book is known for its female-skewed user base, the gender structure in our dataset al.igns well with the actual demographics of the camping population, thus providing reasonable representativeness for our research focus. Additionally, the content shared by Little Red Book users is not limited to park camping activities but also includes product recommendations and various lifestyle aspects47, thus offering multi-faceted information on park camping activities and providing comprehensive and rich data for research. In this study, park camping activities refer to camping activities conducted in urban parks.

Geographic distribution of posts. Maps were drawn by authors, using ArcGIS 10.4.1 (Environmental Systems Research Institute, USA. https://www.esri.com/).

This study defined noise documents as duplicate comments, very short or non-informative posts, posts without keywords related to park camping, and posts about camping activities not conducted within urban parks48,49. Such content was manually identified and removed. Manual screening is useful for short social media posts because it captures contextual cues that fixed textual rules often miss50,51. Posts not conducted within urban parks were identified and excluded through a multi-coder, group-based manual screening procedure. Multiple trained coders were organised into parallel groups to review posts independently using the textual description and available contextual cues, including place names mentioned in the text, location tags if provided, and accompanying images when relevant. Posts were excluded when they clearly referred to non-urban-park settings such as forest parks, scenic areas, commercial campgrounds, or other non-urban-park venues. Borderline cases, including peri-urban forest parks or semi-urban recreational areas, were handled conservatively. When the setting could not be reliably classified as an urban park based on the available cues, the post was excluded to maintain conceptual consistency with the study scope. Disagreements among coders were resolved through group discussion and consensus after joint review. This multi-coder, cue-assisted manual screening supports consistent classification for borderline cases and reduces misclassification52,53. After cleaning, the number of valid posts was 4,840.

Unlike English words, which are separated by spaces, Chinese and similar languages require word segmentation for analysis54. Jieba is an excellent open-source tool for Chinese word segmentation known for its speed, efficiency, and high accuracy in handling large-scale text55,56. Using Jieba, we segmented the 4,840 posts and initially obtained 28,557 keywords. Meaningless keywords, conjunctions, pronouns, and proper nouns, such as place names, were removed. A synonym dictionary was established to ensure that synonymous terms were represented as the same word, preventing repetition and confusion in topic modeling. A custom word bank was also created for new internet or domain-specific terms, allowing for accurate recognition of these words during the word segmentation process. This process ultimately filtered 26,314 keywords for the LDA topic modeling analysis.

Analysis of the posting times revealed that spring and autumn, particularly April and October, are the peak seasons for park camping because the number of posts was significantly higher in these months than in other months; this is likely because the weather during these seasons is more suitable for camping. Regional climatic heterogeneity may moderate these seasonal patterns; for example, snowy and icy winters in Northeast China can constrain the feasible camping season and influence activity conditions and experience expressions. Additionally, the number of posts showed a gradual upward trend each year, with 3,385 posts in 2024, indicating an explosive growth trend; this suggests that the popularity of park camping activities has been rapidly increasing annually, which may be related to the growing interest in outdoor activities and improvements in camping-related facilities and services (Fig. 3).

Determining activity topics and their importance

Topic modeling is a powerful statistical method for revealing hidden thematic structures within a large text data collection57,58. Several approaches have been developed recently, including Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA), Non-negative Matrix Factorization (NMF), Top2Vec, and BERTopic, each with strengths and limitations. For example, NMF is computationally efficient and often combined with TF-IDF weighting to reduce the influence of frequent terms. Still, it lacks a probabilistic framework and may generate topics with limited interpretability. Top2Vec and BERTopic leverage semantic embeddings to capture subtle contextual relationships, yet they typically assign each document to a single topic and offer limited transparency for managerial applications59,60. In this study, LDA was ultimately selected because it can efficiently process and analyze large datasets61 to reveal latent topics that are not immediately apparent62,63. Moreover, LDA is highly interpretable, as the topics generated by the algorithm can be directly examined and understood64, thereby providing deeper insights into the content and structure of large corpora65. More importantly, LDA produces both document-topic and topic-keyword distributions66, which is crucial for this study since our objective was not only to identify broad themes in social media discourse but also to calculate the importance and satisfaction of each demand category, which were further incorporated into the ODI-based opportunity scoring framework. By contrast, while Top2Vec and BERTopic are semantically powerful, they cannot provide document–topic distributions and thus lack the potential for further quantitative analysis67. NMF, when used alone, also falls short in offering a probabilistic representation of topic mixtures60. Therefore, besides adopting LDA, this study incorporated TF-IDF weighting—commonly used in NMF—as input to enhance topic distinctiveness59. This hybrid approach retains the interpretability and mixture modeling strengths of LDA while integrating the discriminative capacity of TF-IDF, and previous studies have demonstrated that such a combination can effectively improve topic quality and robustness68, particularly for short and sparse social media texts by reducing the influence of ubiquitous terms and strengthening discriminative words across documents58.

The Gensim library in Python was used for topic modeling69. The advantages of the Gensim library are its efficiency, ease of use70, and ability to support large-scale text processing and parallel computing71. It provides a rich set of tools for text preprocessing and model evaluation, making LDA topic modeling more convenient and accurate72. LDA topic modeling requires an input document-term frequency matrix (Table 1), where each row represents a single document identified by a “Document ID”, each column represents a keyword, and each cell represents the frequency of the keyword in the document. To determine the keyword frequency, we used the TF-IDF (term frequency–inverse document frequency) method to convert documents into feature vectors and assign a weight to each word to reflect its importance in the document. These TF-IDF vectors were used as inputs for the LDA model73, thus replacing the simple term frequency input. During the modeling process, we cleaned the high- and low-frequency words. High-frequency words appeared in more than 40% of the texts, whereas low-frequency words were defined as those appearing in ≤ 20%. These steps help reduce noise, highlight important words, and improve the quality of the topic model, thereby making the topics generated by the LDA more accurate and meaningful. Finally, based on the commonly used default parameters in related LDA model studies and the performance of existing data74–76, we set the number of model iterations to 50, retained a maximum vocabulary size of 500 during computation, and set both the alpha and beta values to automatic learning. Using the above parameters and procedures, we generated a document-topic matrix (Table 1) and a topic-keyword matrix (Table 2).

The topics output by the LDA model reveal the main themes in user discussions on park camping activities. To evaluate the importance of each topic identified by the LDA model, we used the document-topic matrix (Table 1), where each value represents the probability that a given document belongs to a particular topic. We obtained the cumulative score (CSt) (Eq. 1) by summing the distribution probabilities of each topic across all documents. A higher cumulative score indicates that the topic appears more frequently in user discussions and is therefore more important.

Each topic is ultimately associated with a single cumulative score, which reflects its relative importance in the overall corpus (Fig. 4). However, since this importance score and the subsequent satisfaction score differ in scale, direct mathematical operations between them would be inappropriate. Therefore, we applied min–max normalization to rescale all cumulative scores to a uniform range of 0–10 (Eq. 2). This normalization ensures that both importance and satisfaction indicators can be jointly used in the opportunity scoring model, thus preventing any single metric from dominating the results due to differences in value range.

Computing satisfaction levels of activity topics

This step aimed to evaluate the satisfaction of each park camping topic identified using the LDA method through the topic-keyword matrix (Table 2). The topic-keyword matrix stores the keywords under each topic and their weights. To calculate the satisfaction with the topics, we used PaddleNLP to perform a sentiment analysis for each keyword. Paddle (PArallel Distributed Deep LEarning) is an open-source deep learning platform developed by Baidu with significant advantages in Chinese keyword sentiment analysis. These advantages include optimized pre-trained models, such as ERNIE, powerful Chinese word segmentation and processing tools, training on large-scale Chinese data, knowledge graph integration, an active developer community, and extensive Chinese documentation support. These features make Paddle an excellent choice for Chinese sentiment analysis tasks because they enable more accurate and in-depth sentiment analysis77,78.

The Paddle sentiment analysis model outputs a sentiment polarity (positive, negative, or neutral) and a sentiment score (between 0 and 1) of the document and keywords. To standardize the scores within a range of -1 to + 1, we directly used the score for positive sentiments (between 0 and 1), took the negative score for negative sentiments (between − 1 and 0), and set the score to 0 for neutral sentiments. Thus, a positive sentiment score closer to 1 indicated more positivity, a negative sentiment score closer to -1 indicated more negativity, and 0 indicated neutrality. According to the sentiment analysis, the same keyword may appear with different sentiments in different documents. For example, the keyword “picnic” in the text “The wind is too strong for a picnic at Hanjiang Park, blowing dust everywhere, making it unbearable. I suggest not going for a picnic at Hanjiang until the weather gets better” had a score of -0.941. However, in the text “Hello friends. During this Mid-Autumn Festival and National Day holiday, if you don’t plan to go to crowded and charged scenic spots or travel far to find camping grounds, then come to Baiyun Lake in Guangzhou with your family and friends! It’s full of greenery, with large grassy areas for picnics and camping. You can cycle around the beautiful lake, there’s children’s playground with sandpits and slides, and you can see the Ferris wheel and the big yellow duck across the lake, which is perfect for photos,” the keyword had a score of 0.977. Therefore, the average sentiment score of each keyword across all documents was used as the sentiment weight of the keyword.

Next, we multiplied the sentiment score of each keyword by its weight in the corresponding topic to create a sentiment matrix. By summing the weighted sentiment scores across all keywords within a topic, we obtained the overall sentiment score for each topic, representing the topic’s satisfaction among users (Eq. 3; Fig. 5). Finally, we rescaled all topic-level sentiment scores using min–max normalization to transform them into a range from 0 to 10. This process ensured comparability with the topic importance scores, which were also normalized to the same scale (Eq. 4).

Opportunity algorithm used to identify park activity improvement opportunities

In this step, we used an opportunity algorithm to analyze the potential of park activity topics. The opportunity algorithm is a key component of the Outcome-Driven Innovation (ODI) method developed by Ulwick79,80, and it is used to identify innovation opportunities. This algorithm prioritizes opportunities with the highest growth potential by evaluating user requirements’ importance and satisfaction levels81–83. Although this method was originally developed for product and service innovation, it applies particularly to public service environments like urban parks. Park camping activities are not static physical products but dynamic user experiences that rely heavily on aligning user expectations and service delivery84. The opportunity score for each park camping activity topic was determined by the opportunity algorithm using the normalized importance and satisfaction scores of each topic through Eq. 5. According to this algorithm, if the importance of a topic is lower than its satisfaction, then the opportunity score will equal its importance; otherwise, the score increases proportionally based on the gap between importance and satisfaction, ensuring that only areas that are “important but not yet satisfactory” will receive high opportunity scores79. This evaluation assessed the potential of each park activity topic and generated an opportunity landscape diagram (Fig. 6). This diagram visually displays the performance of different activity topics regarding importance and satisfaction by dividing them into three zones: served right, overserved, and underserved.

Served right zone: This zone indicates that the activity topics have balanced current satisfaction and importance.

Overserved zone: This zone indicates that satisfaction with the activity topics exceeds their importance, suggesting a potential overallocation of resources.

Based on the evaluation of the opportunity algorithm, the underserved zone should be prioritized for innovation and improvement. These topics have the highest potential for improvement because they are highly important to users but currently have low satisfaction79.

After identifying these high-potential park activity topics, a sentiment-weighted topic-keyword matrix was used to further analyze and confirm future park planning and management directions. This matrix displays the keywords for each topic and their sentiment-weighted values, thereby helping to identify keywords that significantly impact user satisfaction within specific topics. For each park activity topic with improvement opportunities, strategies can be developed to enhance the satisfaction of keywords with high sentiment weights or reduce negative factors causing user dissatisfaction. Thus, park managers can more precisely target areas for improvement, optimize resource allocation, enhance visitor experiences, and achieve continuous innovation and development in park activities.

Expert interviews for empirical validation

To empirically validate the user demand topics identified from social media and to serve as an external validation procedure ensuring their consistency and practical relevance, semi-structured expert interviews were conducted to facilitate their translation into practical recommendations85. The expert interviews were conducted in May 2025. Using purposive sampling combined with snowball recommendations, a total of seven experts were invited86,87, with their expertise covering primary knowledge domains relevant to urban park camping activities. The expert panel included one professional landscape designer, two park managers with recent experience operating camping projects, one practitioner from the outdoor camping industry, one landscape researcher and designer, one scholar specializing in public leisure studies, and one policy consultant. Interviews were conducted online and lasted approximately 30–45 min each. With participants’ authorization, all sessions were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim within 24 h. The interview guide focused on user demands identified through social media text analysis, emphasizing their practical importance, feasibility, and priority. It also covered local policy orientations and regulatory constraints relevant to park camping. Experts were encouraged to suggest potentially overlooked topics in the social media analysis. All interview transcripts were analyzed using NVivo software88, which employs automated coding based on word frequency and contextual clustering algorithms to identify common issues discussed by the experts. Themes derived from expert interviews were compared with those identified through social media mining to examine convergences and divergences in content. Background explanations provided by experts were documented for subsequent analysis and discussion89.

Results

Regional and gender-based differences in perceptions of urban park camping

Analysis of provincial-level average sentiment scores revealed a distinct “high-west–low-east” pattern (Fig. 7). This trend contrasts with China’s urbanization and economic development trajectories. Urban park camping has not developed uniformly across China but exhibits clear regional disparities corresponding to different stages of development. In western regions, where urban park camping is still emerging, users tend to be in a phase of novelty-driven exploration, resulting in more positive feedback. In contrast, in eastern provinces, where park camping activities are more mature, lower sentiment scores were observed despite substantial infrastructure adaptation and optimization; this reflects a “satisfaction gap” phenomenon driven by heightened user expectations. A contiguous low-sentiment zone emerged across Henan, Hunan, Hubei, and Guangxi. In these provinces, camping has become relatively widespread; however, service provision remains inadequate due to limited park facilities and lagging urban renewal processes. These areas are “functionally lagging transitional zones,” where participation has outpaced infrastructural support.

Mean sentiment scores were calculated for each gender category and compared with a one-way ANOVA (SciPy 1.11) (Table 3), followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc tests90. Users with unspecified gender scored significantly lower than female (p = 0.0005, mean diff = 0.049) and male users (p = 0.037, mean diff = 0.034). In contrast, no significant difference was found between female and male users themselves (p = 0.259) (Table 4). These results suggest that female and male users who disclose gender have comparable sentiment toward urban park camping. Therefore, gender appears to have a limited impact on overall sentiment, and distinguishing between female and male groups is unlikely to yield meaningful differences in perception.

Topics related to camping activities in urban parks

LDA topic modeling requires the number of topics as another primary input. The topic number (K) directly controls the granularity of the resulting topic structure: a smaller K tends to merge distinct activity scenarios into broader themes, whereas a larger K can fragment semantically close themes and produce redundant or overlapping topics91. In this study, we comprehensively used the perplexity92 and topic coherence93,94 methods to determine the optimal number of topics. This approach allows for a thorough evaluation of the model’s generative quality and the semantic relevance of the topics, avoiding the potentially misleading nature of a single metric and improving the accuracy of selecting the number of topics95. We examined the perplexity and coherence charts and selected several candidate topic numbers that performed well for both metrics. For each candidate K, we additionally checked topic interpretability by reviewing the coherence of top keywords and the degree of redundancy across topics, prioritising topic sets that could be meaningfully labelled and that maintained clear boundaries between activity-related themes. We combined domain knowledge and experience for each candidate topic number to select the best-performing number of topics after model computation, and the optimal number of topics was 71 (Fig. 8). In this sense, K = 71 represents a compromise point where model fit and semantic coherence were jointly acceptable, while the resulting topics remained sufficiently distinct to support downstream opportunity scoring and discussion.

The following six underserved topics exhibited high opportunity scores: “Relaxing Leisure Activities,” “Camping Environment,” “Public Facilities for Park Camping,” “Activity Reservation,” “Fishing Activities,” and “Night Barbecue.”

The “Relaxing Leisure Activities” keywords describe an outdoor activity theme centered on nature, relaxation, and family. The core keywords included “activities,” “campfire,” “children,” “relaxation,” and “experience,” which emphasized the enjoyment of life and stress relief through close contact with nature and participation in diverse activities. This topic was most important, indicating that park camping participants have higher desires and expectations for “Relaxing Leisure Activities.”

The “Camping Environment” keywords focused on comfortable outdoor spaces and environmental protection. Core keywords included “waterside,” “trash,” “take away,” and “attention,” which emphasized the importance of protecting the environment, leaving no trace, and taking away trash while engaging in activities such as fishing, walking, and playing in natural spots.

The “Public Facilities for Park Camping” keywords emphasized the need for basic and convenient facilities during park camping. The main keywords included “sand,” “parking,” “toilet,” and “campsite,” indicating that campers would like additional and better infrastructure and services to enhance the camping experience.

The keywords for “Activity Reservation” highlight the importance of booking and planning park camping activities in advance to ensure a smooth and optimal experience. Main keywords include “reservation,” “advance,” “appointment,” and “plan.” With the increase in park camping participants, many parks have implemented reservation systems, but still face challenges such as complex processes, limited slots, and technical issues.

“Fishing Activities” and “Night Barbecue” are two newly identified underserved camping activities, and their importance indicates that users have high demands for these specific activities. However, current park camping management or activity guidance has not adequately addressed these activities.

The average sentiment score for all topic keywords was 0.654, indicating that users were generally satisfied with park camping activities. However, the six high-opportunity topics are not characterised by uniformly negative sentiment; negative evaluations tend to cluster around a limited set of high-salience keywords. This pattern suggests that existing facilities and services can support generally positive experiences, while specific bottlenecks continue to shape perceived quality and generate stable sources of dissatisfaction. The following discussion therefore focuses on these within-topic weight–sentiment configurations to explain how experience gaps and perceived conflicts arise, before deriving managerial implications.

To assess the alignment between expert perspectives and user feedback, we conducted a cross-comparison between the expert topics coded by NVivo and the six priority topics identified from social media discourse. The results revealed substantial overlap between the two, suggesting that the highlighted issues are unlikely to be random products of data processing and instead correspond to recurring concerns in emerging urban nature-based leisure practices, including restorative experience, service quality, capacity management, and governance constraints. Specifically, the findings indicate strong convergence in three key areas: “Relaxing Leisure Activities,” “Public Facilities for Park Camping,” and “Activity Reservation.” Experts consistently highlighted deficiencies in activity organisation, infrastructure provision, and booking mechanisms, thus echoing the concerns raised by social media users.

However, noticeable divergence appears in the interpretation of “Camping Environment.” While users emphasised aesthetic and comfort-related aspects, such as grass quality, lighting, and atmosphere, experts focused on ecological sustainability, spatial capacity, and systemic resilience. Moreover, in response to high-demand topics like “Night Barbecue” and “Fishing Activities,” experts were predominantly cautious or negative, citing policy, safety, and environmental constraints. These discrepancies underscore a mismatch between user aspirations and institutional feasibility. While users prioritised immediate experience, experts emphasised governance, regulation, and long-term ecological balance. The following discussion focuses on three issues: misalignment between activity demand and supply that widens experience gaps; scale differences between environmental comfort and ecological constraints; and perceived conflicts amplified by shortcomings in basic facilities and reservation management.

Misalignment between activity demand and supply widens experience gaps

Activity-related experience gaps indicate a mismatch between what users seek and what can be provided in urban parks, with two distinguishable patterns. One pattern concerns insufficient provision of relaxing leisure activities. The other concerns gaps in more immersive nature-oriented activities, represented here by fishing and nighttime barbecues, where demand signals are strong but feasibility is shaped by safety, ecology, and policy boundaries, leading to expectation and participation gaps.

The quantitative results show where these patterns are most visible. Within “Relaxing Leisure Activities,” “schedule” has the highest keyword weight (0.033) and the lowest sentiment score (0.291). “navigation” (0.018, 0.344) and “arrival” (0.011, 0.381) are also relatively low. In contrast, benefit- and experience-related terms remain positive, including “children” (0.028, 0.823), “relaxation” (0.024, 0.890), and “experience” (0.024, 0.740). This configuration indicates that relaxing leisure is valued, while dissatisfaction concentrates on the organisation and availability of such activities. For immersive nature-oriented activities, “Fishing Activities” has the highest opportunity score among the six high-opportunity topics (16.0779), and “Night Barbecue” is also in the underserved zone (8.8631). At the keyword level, “fishing” (0.033, 0.845) is positive, yet co-occurs with negative waste- and order-related terms, including “takeaway” (0.015, -0.450), “trash” (0.015, -0.497), and “trash bin” (0.008, -0.346). For night barbecue, “barbecue grill” (0.044, 0.364) is comparatively low under high salience, and “overnight” (0.027, 0.177) and “toilet” (0.033, 0.226) are also low, indicating that restrictions and insufficient supporting conditions increase negative evaluations.

Expert interviews align with these two patterns. NVivo coding identifies “Activities,” “Management,” and “Facilities” as frequently discussed topics. Experts repeatedly described limited guided content and perceived monotony as key issues associated with relaxing leisure in park camping, especially under peak demand. They treated fishing and nighttime barbecues as activities with high risk and high supervision costs, citing policy, safety, and environmental constraints, and noted that social media can amplify isolated demands and raise expectations, increasing on-site governance pressure96.

Relaxing leisure activities capture campers’ most direct and basic needs in urban park camping, namely relaxation, leisure, and enjoyment38. Urban-park studies consistently associate such leisure with stress relief, mental restoration, and improved well-being97–101. In the camping setting, the experience depends on whether relaxing leisure is available as organised, easy-to-join content that can sustain a longer stay102. When provision is thin, repetitive, or weakly organised, users report positive feelings toward relaxation and family experience yet evaluate the overall experience more negatively because they cannot readily participate in meaningful leisure during their stay103,104. This mechanism is reflected in the contrast between positive sentiment for “relaxation,” “children,” and “experience” and the low sentiment attached to “schedule,” and it is reinforced by experts’ repeated emphasis on limited guided content and monotony during high-demand days8.

Fishing and nighttime barbecues capture a different gap. Social media content indicates that some users seek more immersive nature experiences within urban parks105. Dense urban settings impose feasibility limits106,107 because these activities raise safety, ecological, and governance challenges108,109. Environmental factors such as lighting and landscape design shape the feasibility of nighttime activities110, while barbecue-related activities remain especially difficult to manage safely108. Recreationist heterogeneity studies also show that groups such as anglers can exert distinct ecological pressures and require tailored regulation111 These constraints shape how demand is expressed and evaluated. Fishing is often framed as desired immersion and shows more positive affect, while night barbecue is more frequently linked to restriction, prohibition, and insufficient supporting conditions, contributing to more negative evaluations. The co-occurrence of fishing demand with negative waste-related terms indicates that aspiration can quickly shift into hygiene and order complaints when on-site capacity is strained, matching experts’ concerns about governance pressure and expectation escalation.

Accordingly, improvements should address insufficient provision of relaxing leisure activities and manage expectation gaps for boundary-sensitive activities within feasible safety and environmental conditions. The combination of high weight and low sentiment for “schedule” (0.033, 0.291) supports increasing the availability and clarity of relaxing leisure offerings through consistent information release and replicable activity templates that are easy to join during a camping stay. The low sentiment for “navigation” (0.018, 0.344) and “arrival” (0.011, 0.381) indicates higher coordination costs around joining and organising activities, supporting clearer participation instructions, meeting arrangements, and on-site facilitation that reduce uncertainty during peak demand. The relatively low sentiment for “barbecue grill” (0.044, 0.364) together with “overnight” (0.027, 0.177) supports clearer rule explanation and feasible substitutes that reduce disputes under night-time constraints. The positive sentiment for “fishing” (0.033, 0.845) together with negative sentiment around “takeaway,” “trash,” and “trash bin” supports coupling any permitted fishing provision with explicit safety guidance, waste handling, and routine maintenance capacity; where fishing remains restricted, concise explanations and substitutes can reduce expectation spillovers into hygiene complaints.

Scale differences between environmental comfort and ecological constraints

Scale Differences Between Environmental Comfort and Ecological Constraints. Activity-related experience gaps unfold within a longer-stay and shared-space setting, which also makes environmental comfort and ecological constraints more salient during park camping. Environmental quality is a central driver of urban park camping experience because it shapes immediate comfort and the long-term sustainability of green spaces112. Prior studies have shown that park environmental quality is associated with well-being, recreational satisfaction, and revisit intention97,113,114. This study identifies a stable scale difference in how “camping environment” is evaluated, which produces a form of dual anxiety. Campers assess the environment at the scale of a single visit and focus on visible comfort cues and perceived fairness under crowding, with dissatisfaction directed toward hygiene, waste, and on-site order. Experts assess the environment at the scale of repeated use and emphasize ecological protection and carrying-capacity control, highlighting cumulative risks to soil, lawns, water quality, and the night-time environment under unmanaged camping behavior115.

The within-topic results make this difference concrete. In “Camping Environment,” positive evaluations concentrate on comfort-related settings, including “waterside” (0.036, 0.594) and “playing with water” (0.020, 0.763). Strong negative evaluations concentrate on daily cleanliness and waste-handling problems, with “trash” (0.034, -0.497) and “takeaway” (0.031, -0.450) showing the lowest sentiment under high salience. “suggestion” (0.025, 0.483) indicates that users actively propose improvements while expressing limited confidence in current responses. Norm-oriented terms are present but remain low in sentiment, including “protection” (0.008, 0.221), “civilization” (0.007, 0.060), “no trace” (0.007, 0.061), and “care” (0.010, 0.017). This configuration indicates that environmental dissatisfaction is driven by visible disorder, while the language of environmental responsibility does not translate into a more positive experience116.

The empirical pattern and expert evidence point to a single mechanism operating at two scales. At the single-visit scale, comfort in high-value settings such as watersides is sensitive to small, visible failures in hygiene and order. Waste, leftover food, and crowding cues quickly dominate evaluation because they are immediate, shared by many visitors, and easy to attribute to “management” or “other users”117,118. At the repeated-use scale, the same behaviors generate cumulative pressures that are less visible in one visit but decisive for long-term function, including turf root damage, soil compaction, water pollution risks, and light and noise disturbance115,119. Experts described these impacts as “invisible ecological damage,” and they also described escalating operational difficulty during peaks because cleaning, supervision, and ecological repair compete for limited staff time and budget120. The low sentiment attached to “no trace” and related terms is consistent with this mechanism: when low-impact practices remain abstract, compliance is uneven, and the cost of maintaining comfort rises for both managers and visitors121. The result is a persistent gap between comfort-oriented expectations and constraint-oriented feasibility, which keeps environmental issues salient even when overall experience remains generally positive.

The evidence supports improvement directions that balance comfort and ecological constraints. The highly negative sentiment for “trash” (0.034, -0.497) and “takeaway” (0.031, -0.450) supports prioritizing waste-handling capacity in camping areas, including bin placement and adequacy, peak-period collection frequency, and simple on-site routines that reduce visible disorder. The low sentiment for “protection,” “civilization,” “no trace,” and “care” supports shifting communication from general slogans to specific, short behavioral prompts that match on-site conditions, supported by facilities that make compliance easy116. The positive sentiment for “waterside” (0.036, 0.594) and “playing with water” (0.020, 0.763), together with expert concerns about cumulative impacts115, supports tighter zoning and higher maintenance standards in water-adjacent camping areas, with clear boundaries and capacity controls to keep comfort stable while reducing repeated ecological stress122. Measures such as trampling management, tent-density guidance, and rotation of high-use lawns can be aligned with these boundary settings123. When comfort-oriented expectations and constraint-oriented feasibility diverge, baseline service conditions and access procedures can further shape whether dissatisfaction escalates into perceived conflict, which motivates the next section.

Shortcomings in basic facilities and reservation management amplify perceived conflicts

Perceived conflicts in urban park camping are amplified when basic facilities do not meet peak-period needs and when reservation management is experienced as difficult or unfair. Basic facilities shape on-site tolerability through hygiene, mobility, and safety conditions, while reservation management shapes access and procedural experience through allocation rules and system performance.

Within “Public Facilities for Park Camping,” “free” has the highest weight (0.056) and a positive sentiment score (0.687), indicating a strong preference for low-threshold access. “toilet” remains a salient source of dissatisfaction (0.028, 0.226), and “parking lot” is moderate (0.017, 0.548). Within “Activity Reservation,” “reservation,” “advance,” and “appointment” carry relatively high weights (0.051–0.040), while sentiment scores remain neutral to low (0.554–0.609). “only” (0.015, 0.115) and “finally” (0.020, 0.466) reflect disappointment after repeated failures. “work” (0.026, 0.492) and “personal” (0.026, 0.614) indicate tensions between booking schedules and daily routines.

Expert interviews align with these patterns. NVivo coding identifies “Activities,” “Management,” and “Facilities” as frequently discussed themes. Experts described toilets, parking, water supply, and delayed waste removal as baseline pain points that trigger complaints quickly, and they noted that holiday peaks produce longer queues and visibly poorer hygiene, with stronger impacts on women’s experience. For reservations, experts described a severe supply–demand imbalance that turns access into frequent competition. They also reported that technical instability, fragmented platforms, complicated interfaces, and unclear rules intensify frustration and undermine trust.

In the camping context, facility and reservation shortcomings amplify perceived conflicts because camping is duration-intensive and space-exclusive. In urban park camping activities, the shortage of public facilities is one of the primary factors limiting user satisfaction and the sustainability of such activities124. Campers stay longer, occupy fixed lawn space, and depend on nearby toilets, hand-washing points, waste handling, and parking to keep the experience acceptable125. Longer time spent in parks increases the chance that small service failures become repeated irritants114,126,127 and that physical conditions are interpreted through safety and comfort, with stronger sensitivity among groups such as females and parents10. Public toilets function as highly visible service nodes, and gender-related differences in demand make toilet configuration and day-to-day operation especially consequential during peak periods128. Reservation management intensifies this dynamic because camping access functions as a scarce “time-slot plus space” bundle. A successful booking secures limited space for an extended period, while repeated failures can invalidate trip planning. Under high demand, the booking process is evaluated through predictability and perceived fairness, and dissatisfaction can arise from the allocation procedure itself when rules are unclear or execution is unstable129,130. Restricted or scheduled access increases the salience of transparent information and clear rules in shaping fairness perceptions11. When resources are limited, basic amenities and operational services become equity-relevant public responsibilities, and mismatches between importance and satisfaction indicate where quality concerns and equity concerns concentrate131,132. Facility shortages therefore raise the on-site cost of staying, reservation shortcomings raise the off-site cost of obtaining access, and both pressures become more visible during peaks.

Accordingly, improvements should prioritise peak-time service capacity in camping zones and strengthen the transparency and reliability of reservation allocation. The low sentiment for “toilet” (0.028, 0.226), together with experts’ accounts of peak-period queues and hygiene decline, supports prioritising peak-responsive provision in camping zones, including portable or modular toilets, temporary hand-washing points, clearer parking guidance, and faster waste removal during peak hours114,124–126. Evidence on gender-related demand differences supports treating toilet provision as a capacity-and-equity issue rather than a static facility count128. “free” (0.056, 0.687) supports maintaining a low-threshold baseline, while limited service-oriented facilities can be used to sustain routine maintenance without raising entry barriers132,133. The neutral-to-low sentiment for “reservation”, “advance” and “appointment” (0.051–0.040; 0.554–0.609), combined with “only” and “finally,” supports improving rule clarity and system reliability through a unified entry, clear release schedules, real-time availability information, and dynamic quota release for no-shows, with a simplified interface for older users134,135. “work” and “personal” support adjusting release timing and providing multiple booking windows to better fit daily routines and reduce speed-based competition.

Overall, the results show a generally positive experience backdrop, while dissatisfaction concentrates around a limited set of high-salience elements. Across the six high-opportunity topics, three linked mechanisms explain why gaps persist in urban park camping. First, activity-related gaps arise when relaxing leisure provision is insufficient and when immersive nature-oriented demands encounter binding feasibility boundaries, so interest does not consistently translate into participation. Second, environmental concerns remain salient because comfort is evaluated through immediate, visible cues, whereas ecological constraints accumulate through repeated use and become apparent through maintenance pressure and degradation risks. Third, shortcomings in basic facilities and reservation management amplify perceived conflicts by increasing the cost of staying on site and the cost of obtaining access off site, especially during peak periods. Interpreting topics through within-topic weight–sentiment structure clarifies activity-specific experience dimensions and pinpoints dissatisfaction drivers, ODI-based prioritisation supports an explicit ranking logic under resource constraints, and expert evidence strengthens feasibility clarification by distinguishing direct service deficits from boundary-sensitive tensions. These interpretations are grounded in text-based evidence from a single social media platform and expert validation, and they are most applicable to urban park settings where camping involves longer stays and shared-space occupation under peak demand. Generalisation across platforms, regions, seasons, and local policy regimes should be made with caution, as these contextual factors may shift both participation conditions and the salience of specific dissatisfaction drivers.

Concluding remarks

This study examines a recurring challenge in activity-oriented experience research on emerging urban nature-based leisure practices in public parks, using urban park camping as a representative case. Based on social media text mining and expert validation, we identified six high-opportunity topics and showed that overall evaluations were generally positive (mean keyword sentiment = 0.654), while dissatisfaction clustered around a limited set of high-salience elements within topics. The findings indicate three linked mechanisms that explain why gaps persist under peak conditions: (1) experience gaps arise when activity demand is misaligned with feasible supply, including insufficient provision of relaxing leisure activities and boundary-sensitive demands such as fishing and nighttime barbecues; (2) environmental concerns remain salient because comfort is evaluated through immediate, visible cues, while ecological constraints accumulate through repeated use; and (3) perceived conflicts are amplified when basic facilities and reservation management are insufficient for longer-stay, space-occupying practices. Together, the results provide an interpretable basis for prioritising improvements while clarifying feasibility boundaries relevant to park governance.

Contributions

This study contributes to activity-oriented urban park research in three ways. First, it strengthens activity-specific inference by interpreting topics through within-topic weight–sentiment configurations, thereby locating dissatisfaction drivers at the element level rather than treating activities as broad labels. Second, it operationalises prioritisation by integrating importance-related and satisfaction-related information via ODI opportunity scoring, allowing improvement targets to be ranked explicitly under resource constraints. Third, it improves evidential credibility through expert interviews as cross-actor validation, which helps clarify feasibility boundaries and explains divergences between user experience signals and management constraints. The framework is potentially transferable to other activity contexts that involve longer stays and shared-space occupation, provided that platform characteristics and local governance regimes are considered.

Managerial implications

The results suggest that improvements should focus on a small number of high-salience bottlenecks that repeatedly attract negative evaluations, especially during peak periods. For activity provision, evidence of low sentiment on high-weight organisational elements supports strengthening the availability and joinability of relaxing leisure offerings through replicable activity formats and lightweight facilitation. For environmental management, strongly negative signals around waste and cleanliness, together with low sentiment on norm-related terms, support combining peak-responsive waste handling with targeted behavioural prompts and zoning that protect ecologically sensitive areas while maintaining perceived comfort. For conflict-prone conditions, low sentiment around toilets and reservation-related frustration signals supports prioritising peak-time service capacity and more transparent, reliable allocation procedures. For boundary-sensitive demands such as fishing and nighttime barbecues, feasibility constraints indicate the importance of clear rule communication and feasible substitutes to reduce expectation gaps and secondary complaints about hygiene and order.

Limitations

Despite the contributions of this study in identifying improvement opportunities for park activities, further research is required. First, the relative importance and satisfaction levels were quantified through direct normalization because of the difficulty in determining the absolute values corresponding to the 0–10 range. Thus, future research should employ more precise statistical methods to define standard importance and satisfaction levels. Additionally, overserved needs should be examined to better coordinate and allocate resources for specific park activity opportunities. Moreover, this study did not distinguish between user groups, usage scenarios, or specific park types. Future research could stratify data collection and analysis by variables such as age, occupation, or family structure, as well as distinguish between usage contexts, including weekdays versus holidays and individual versus group-based activities. It is also important to differentiate parks based on their spatial characteristics, including size, functional positioning, and location within the urban system. Regional heterogeneity should be further considered by analyzing parks across areas with varying degrees of urban development. Future research may further consider regional climatic conditions when interpreting seasonal feasibility and experience expressions of park camping. Furthermore, as this study relied solely on social media text data, future studies are encouraged to incorporate complementary methods such as on-site surveys or structured questionnaires to improve the validity and contextual depth of the findings. Future work may incorporate systematic cross-jurisdictional comparison of official policy documents to better contextualize feasibility boundaries under different local policy orientations. Future research may also adopt time-stratified or rolling-window analyses to examine whether topic salience and evaluation concentrations shift over time. Although our proposed method was applied to a specific park activity, it can also be extended to cover multiple types of park activities simultaneously, offering more targeted and comprehensive recommendations for the overall enhancement of park services.
