Circuit Complexity From Physical Constraints: Scaling Limitations of Attention
Benjamin Prada, Ankur Mali

Abstract
We argue that the standard circuit complexity measures derived from N ​ C , A ​ C , T ​ C NC,AC,TC provide limited practical information and are now insufficient to further differentiate model expressivity. To address these new limitations, we define a novel notion of local uniformity and a family of circuit complexity classes R ​ C ​ ( ⋅ ) RC(\cdot) that capture the fundamental constraints of scaling physical circuits. Through the lens of R ​ C ​ ( ⋅ ) RC(\cdot) , we show that attention mechanisms with ω ⁡ ( n 3 / 2 ) \omega(n^{3/2}) runtime cannot scale to accommodate the entropy of increasingly complex datasets. Our results simultaneously provide a methodology for defining meaningful bounds on transformer expressivity and naturally expose the restricted viability of attention.

2
Approach

Our work addresses the challenge of quantifying model expressivity by reframing it in terms of scalability. Because a given model must be scaled to support inputs of increasing size, any meaningful measure their expressivity must depend on the rate at which they can be scaled. In practice, this scaling rate is strongly constrained by the speed with which information can propagate: the number of components of a causal system is bounded in time
t
by
\mathcal{O}(t^{3})
, and the entropy rates of the inputs and outputs are each bounded as
\mathcal{O}(t^{2})
. These bounds remind us that, while polynomial growth is a useful abstraction for theory, knowing the
degree
of the polynomial is instrumental to understanding utility. By justifying
natural, precise
bounds on circuit proportions, we can directly apply complexity theory to a forward-looking design of efficient machine learning architectures.

2.1
Realizable circuits (RC)

We introduce a new family of complexity classes
RC(t(n))
that capture these scalability restrictions. See Definition
B.6
for a more detailed definition.

Definition 2.1

(
RC(\cdot)
)
.

A function
f\in RC(t(n))
if there exists a sequence of circuits
\{C_{n}\}_{n>0}
that each implement
f
for inputs of length
n
such that, given a description of
C_{n}
, a sufficient circuit
C_{n+1}
can be constructed in
\mathcal{O}(t(n))
time, and such that each
|C_{n}|=\mathcal{O}(t(n)^{3})
.

The parameter
t(n)
can be interpreted as the maximum desired time in which circuit implementations can be constructed for inputs of length
n
. For example,
RC(\sqrt{n})
is the set of functions whose implementations can scale quadratically in time. Be careful
not
to interpret
t(n)
as the scaling rate. The rate that the input length scales is given by
n(t)=t^{-1}(n)
.

Notice that this definition diverges from typical uniformity constraints in that the construction may be incremental. The criteria for
DTIME(t(n))
-uniformity necessitates that a mapping
n\mapsto C_{n}
be computable in
\mathcal{O}(t(n))
time, but our constraint is looser, requiring only that
C_{n}\mapsto C_{n+1}
be computable in
\mathcal{O}(t(n))
time. This condition–which we call local uniformity–reveals the scaling benefits of architectures that can be
extended
efficiently but not necessarily
rebuilt
efficiently.

The second condition bounds the number of circuit components
|C_{n}|
relative to the time
t(n)
. The
|C_{n}|=\mathcal{O}(t(n)^{3})
constraint is derived simply from the
\mathcal{O}(t^{3})
bound on the volume of a causal system. Notice that
|C_{n}|
and the scaling rate
t^{-1}(n)
are inversely related. As the scaling rate increases, the ratio of model size to input length decreases. For example,
RC(\sqrt{n})
equates to a fast scaling rate of
n=\Omega(t^{2})
but limits the size of the circuit significantly to
|C_{n}|=\mathcal{O}(n^{3/2})
.

4
Impact

In this work, we extend the theoretical model of circuit complexity to a practical setting directly applicable to the analysis and design of machine learning architectures. Through the construction of
RC(t(n))
, we begin to uncover the precise limits of model expressivity in terms of a balance between scalability, time complexity, parallelism, and depth. By adopting only the most conservative, information-theoretic assumptions for physicality, our results can be interpreted to hold agnostic of the choice of computation method. We strongly advocate for a wider theory of expressivity focused on pragmatism. We highlight that expressivity is inseparably tied to both the precise allocation of computation resources and the ability for the computation to scale; however, this effort is incomplete. Although we avoid the discussion of circuits with recurrent connections, such circuits greatly improve efficiency by the reuse of components. Directions for future research are considered in Appendix D.

A
Background

Our work operates in the domain of circuit complexity. To present a complete picture of our contributions, we begin by introducing the standard classes of circuit complexity and their known relationships with different transformer architectures.

A.1
Circuit complexity

Circuit complexity classifies the inherent difficulty of computing a function by the necessary size and scale of a Boolean circuit that implements it. As the length of the input increases, the circuit may grow at most by some asymptotic rate. The three primary class families are

NC:=\cup_{k>0}NC^{k},\qquad AC:=\cup_{k>0}AC^{k},\qquad TC:=\cup_{k>0}TC^{k}

related as

NC^{k}\subseteq AC^{k}\subseteq TC^{k}\subseteq NC^{k+1}.

Each class consists of the functions computable by circuits with a polynomial number of gates and depth
\mathcal{O}(log^{k}(n))
for inputs of size
n
. The distinction between the classes lies in their permitted sets of gates. Under
NC
the implementation circuit is allowed only Boolean gates with fan-in 2. The class
AC
extends
NC
to unbounded fan-in. Lastly,
TC
extends
AC
with the addition of threshold gates that output
1
iff the number of inputs with value
1
exceeds a programmed threshold.

A.2
Uniform circuits

By default, circuit complexity classes only enforce that a circuit of the required proportions
exists
for every input size
n
. The structure of these circuits is allowed to vary wildly as the input size changes, even to the degree that the construction of the circuit sequence is uncomputable.

This motivates the definition of circuit uniformity. A function
f
in circuit complexity class
C
is said to be in class
T
-uniform
C
if there exists a function
F
in class
T
such that,
\forall n,F(n)
is a
C
-circuit implementation of
f
for inputs of size
n
.
T
-uniformity guarantees that the circuit sequence not only exists but can be computed under class
T
.

B
Approach

The recent status of circuit complexity research indicates that the current system might be too broad to precisely reflect the expressive subtleties of transformer architectures. It follows that a more nuanced framework is needed to better characterize their computational capabilities. We formulate our research to address the need for a more pragmatic theory of circuit complexity. The scope of the present work is concentrated on the physical limitations of scaling circuits to support inputs of increasing length.

B.1
Local uniformity

First, we define a variant of uniformity that highlights an often overlooked reality of scaling: the growth of a circuit can be more efficient if the circuit can be
extended
rather than entirely
rebuilt
.
Local
uniformity embodies the intuitive idea that the development of the circuit family occurs through small, manageable updates performed incrementally. Consequently, it reforms the coarse notion of uniformity into a more detailed, structure-sensitive property.

Definition B.1

(Local uniformity)
.

Fix an effective encoding
\langle\cdot\rangle
of circuits as finite strings. A circuit family
\{C_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is
local
T
-uniform
if there exists a mapping
\langle C_{n}\rangle\mapsto\langle C_{n+1}\rangle
that lies in
T
.

Notice that local uniformity is a strictly looser condition than uniformity. The following lemmas show that uniformity implies local uniformity but local uniformity
does not
imply uniformity.

Lemma B.2
.

For all classes
T
, if a circuit family is
T
-uniform then it is also local
T
-uniform.

Proof.

Suppose that a circuit family
\{C_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is
T
-uniform; i.e., the mapping
\phi(n)\mapsto\langle C_{n}\rangle
lies in
T
. Let
\psi
be the mapping
\psi(\langle C_{n}\rangle)\mapsto n
. Since the encoding
\langle C_{n}\rangle
contains (or can be padded to contain) the value of
n
, the function
\psi
can also be computed in
T
. Then
\phi\circ\psi(n)
is a mapping
\langle C_{n}\rangle\mapsto\langle C_{n+1}\rangle
that lies in
T
.
∎

Lemma B.3
.

Assume
t(n)
is non-decreasing. If
f
is local
DTIME(t(n))
-uniform, then
f
is also
DTIME(n\cdot t(n))
-uniform. Moreover, the relationship is tight in general.

Proof.

Start from a hard-coded
\langle C_{1}\rangle
. For
k=1,2,\ldots,n-1
,
\langle C_{k+1}\rangle
can be computed sequentially in
O(t(k))
time by local uniformity. The total time to reach
\langle C_{n}\rangle
is
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\mathcal{(}t(k))
. Since
t
is non-decreasing,
\mathcal{O}(\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}t(k))=\mathcal{O}(n\cdot t(n))
.
∎

B.2
Physical constraints of computation

Although the concept of local uniformity
relaxes
the traditional uniformity requirements, our primary aim is to
impose
meaningful constraints on the circuit’s structure. In this section, we derive physically motivated bounds on circuit proportions based on principles from entropy and communication theory. It is important to emphasize that these justifications are conservative; they do not account for numerous additional effects that could further limit the computational capacity. Consequently, our conclusions are broadly applicable and independent of the specific computational methods employed.

Our initial constraint concerns the maximum number of gates permissible in the circuit. We recognize that the circuit must be confined within a causal region–specifically, a light cone. Without loss of generality, we model this region as an expanding sphere
B_{r}(\mathbf{0})
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with radius
r=\mathcal{O}(t)
. The volume of this region is then bounded by
\mathcal{O}(t^{3})
. Since the entire circuit must reside within this sphere at each step, the number of gates in each circuit
C_{n}
is also bounded by
\mathcal{O}(t^{3})
, assuming a minimum gate density. We now generalize this reasoning.

Proposition B.4
.

Consider a circuit
C
evolving within a
d
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose that the causal region containing the circuit at time
t
is a ball
B_{r}(\mathbf{0})
with radius
r=\mathcal{O}(t)
. If the circuit gates are distributed with a minimum density
\rho>0
, then

|C|=\mathcal{O}(t^{d}).

Our second and final constraint pertains to the circuit’s width. Building on the same
B_{r}(\mathbf{0})
perspective, consider that the amount of entropy entering the causal region is proportional to its surface area. Entropy change within this region can be modeled as a vector field in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where the magnitude of each vector is bounded by the speed of light
c
. The rate at which entropy flows into the region (i.e., the entropy influx) is proportional to the flux of this vector field across the boundary surface. Similarly, the entropy outflux corresponds to the flux leaving the surface. The total influx and outflux are both bounded by
\mathcal{O}(r^{d-1})=\mathcal{O}(t^{d-1})
, since the surface area of a
d
-dimensional sphere scales as
r^{d-1}
. Assuming that the entropy vector field is uniform, this extends to non-spherical regions.

Proposition B.5
.

Consider a circuit
C
evolving within a
d
-dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose that at time
t
, the causal region containing the circuit is a ball
B_{r}(\mathbf{0})
with radius
r=\mathcal{O}(t)
. Assuming a uniform entropy change vector field bounded by
c
, both the entropy influx and outflux rates are bounded by
\mathcal{O}(t^{d-1})
. Consequently, the effective width of the circuit–corresponding to the maximum entropy exchange across its boundary–is also bounded by
\mathcal{O}(t^{d-1})
.

B.3
Realizable circuits

Now we combine our definition of local uniformity with our derived physical circuit constraints to construct a new circuit complexity class that encompasses a realistic scaling environment. We call this the class of
realizable circuits
(
RC
), which we parameterize by
t(n)
: the time cost for extending the circuit.

Definition B.6

(
RC(\cdot)
)
.

A function family
f
belongs to
\mathbf{RC(t(n))}
if there exists a circuit family
\{C_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
computing
f
on length
n
such that:

1.

(
Local uniformity
)
\{C_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is local
DTIME(t(n))
-uniform.

2.

(
Size budget
) The circuit sizes obey
|C_{n}|=O(t(n)^{3})
for all
n
.

The first condition is simply local uniformity with respect to the desired time cost
t(n)
. Similarly, the second condition is just the application of Proposition
B.4
for time
t(n)
. We reiterate that the simplicity of
RC(t(n))
is a
feature
of the class due to its generality. The only assumptions needed to interpret
RC
as containing reasonable circuits are that gate density is bounded and that our local uniformity condition is not too strong. Even with the most conservative of assumptions, we still greatly contract the scope of circuit complexity: from polynomial size to at most cubic size.

RC(\cdot)
also has a rich hierachy. Consider the following properties of
RC(\cdot)
:

Lemma B.7

(Lower-bound transfer)
.

Let
s(n)
be a circuit size lower bound for the family (i.e., any circuit for length
n
inputs has size
\geq s(n)
).
If
f\in RC(t(n))
, then
t(n)=\Omega(s(n)^{1/3})
.

Proof.

By Def.
B.6
,
|C_{n}|=O(t(n)^{3})
.
Since any valid circuit must have
|C_{n}|\geq s(n)
, we get
s(n)\leq c\,t(n)^{3}
for a constant
c
.
Hence
t(n)\geq(s(n)/c)^{1/3}
.
∎

Theorem B.8

(Size-driven hierarchy)
.

Fix
\alpha>0
and suppose a family requires size
\Omega(n^{\alpha})
.
Then, for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, it does not belong to
RC(n^{\alpha/3-\varepsilon})
.

Proof.

Immediate from Lemma
B.7
with
s(n)=\Omega(n^{\alpha})
.
∎

This result shows that distinct polynomial size lower bounds yield a strict hierarchy of
RC(\cdot)
classes. Hence, we can be assured that containment of an architecture within
RC(t(n))
for a unique
t(n)
constitutes a true refinement of the architecture expressivity.

Until this point, we have not discussed the impact of Proposition
B.5
on the
RC(\cdot)
family. The
\mathcal{O}(t^{2})
width bound introduces an type of truncation to the hierarchy. Observe that:

t(n)=o(\sqrt{n})\implies n(t)=o(t^{2}).

Hence, any time cost lower than
\sqrt{n}
equates to an input size rate exceeding
t^{2}
. If we assume the entropy of the input remains proportional to the input size over time, then input scaling violates Proposition
B.5
. In effect, a class
RC(t(n))
can only be deemed physically admissible if we add the additional condition that
t(n)=\Omega(\sqrt{n})
. This insight will underpin the significance of our main transformer scaling result.

C
Results

Finally, we apply the preceding constructions and properties to present our main results.

C.1
Scaling-complexity tradeoff

We begin by tying the
RC(\cdot)
hierarchy directly to the deterministic polynomial-time hierarchy.

Theorem C.1

(Hierarchy connection)
.

RC(n^{k/3})\subseteq DTIME(n^{k}),\quad\forall k\geq 3/2
.

Proof.

Fix
k>0
and let
f
be an arbitrary function in
RC(n^{k/3})
. Thus, for every
n
there exists a circuit
C_{n}
such that
|C_{n}|=\mathcal{O}((n^{k/3})^{3})=\mathcal{O}(n^{k})
. This circuit, once constructed, can be simulated by a Turing machine in
DTIME(n^{k})
(assuming bounded fan-in). Recall by Lemma
B.3
that
f
, being local
DTIME(n^{k/3})
uniform, is also
DTIME(n^{k/3+1})
uniform. That is,
C_{n}
can be constructed in
DTIME(n^{k/3+1})
and so
DTIME(n^{k})
since
k\geq 3/2
. Together with the
DTIME(n^{k})
simulation obtained, we conclude
f\in DTIME(n^{k})
.
∎

Notice that the condition
k\geq 3/2
is always met within the refined setting
t(n)=\Omega(\sqrt{n})
. This further justifies
t(n)=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n})
as the minimal acceptable time cost, as it is the exact asymptotic rate at which the correspondence between
RC(\cdot)
and
DTIME(n^{k})
breaks down.

As a direct corollary:

Corollary C.2

(Ideal time complexity)
.

RC(\sqrt{n})\subseteq DTIME(n^{3/2})
.

Under the assumption that
n^{2}
is the fastest possible scaling rate (
\sqrt{n}
minimum time cost), this result implies that ideal scaling
requires
a
\mathcal{O}(n^{3/2})
time complexity.

Note, however, that the
\mathcal{O}(n^{3/2})
time complexity bound is still relative to deterministic Turing machines, which are
non-parallel
machines. When implemented by the corresponding
parallel
circuits
\{C_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, a
DTIME(n^{3/2})
function
f
may actually be executed in less time. But again, since the input and output entropies are each bounded as
\mathcal{O}(t^{2})
, the effective parallelism of the circuit is limited. A function may seem to be more efficient on a circuit with access to more than
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
parallel components, but additional components will become unsaturated over time as the input rate becomes the main bottleneck.

C.2
Transformer scaling limit

Finally, we establish our tightened transformer expressivity bounds as informed by
RC(\cdot)
.

Theorem C.3

(RC-augmented transformer expressivity)
.

\displaystyle SMAT,AHAT

\displaystyle\subseteq RC(n^{2/3})\cap\text{DLOGTIME-uniform }TC^{0}

\displaystyle SMAT,AHAT

\displaystyle\not\subseteq RC(\sqrt{n})\cap\text{DLOGTIME-uniform }TC^{0}

Proof.

Given that
SMAT,AHAT\subseteq\text{DLOGTIME-uniform }TC^{0}
, it suffices to prove that
SMAT,AHAT\subseteq RC(n^{2/3})
but
\not\subseteq RC(\sqrt{n})
.

1.

Because both classes are
DLOGTIME
-uniform, they are local
DLOGTIME
-uniform by Lemma
B.2
and thus local
DTIME(n^{2/3})
uniform. In addition, the circuit size for both is bounded as

|C_{n}|=\mathcal{O}(n(t)^{2})=\mathcal{O}((t(n)^{3/2})^{2})=\mathcal{O}(t(n)^{3}).

Together, we find
SMAT,AHAT\subseteq RC(n^{2/3})
.

2.

Assume by Keles (2023)
[
6
]
that
SMAT,AHAT
are not in
DTIME(n^{k})
for
k<2
. By applying the converse of Theorem
C.1
, we then obtain

SMAT,AHAT\not\subseteq RC(\sqrt{n}).

∎

D
Analysis

Our work has extended the capability of circuit complexity theory to model real-world architectures in two major ways.

Local uniformity relaxes the standard uniformity condition to spotlight the architectures that are inefficient to build but are otherwise efficient to extend. We hope that by clarifying this criteria for scalability, we can direct our focus toward a more modular design philosophy. If the different computational resources required by a model are kept separated, it is often easier to extend. For instance, consider the case of external memory. So long as a memory system is coupled to a machine loosely enough, it is possible to increase the amount of available memory with minimal to no effect on the remaining structure. We advocate for research into models that can learn and grow over time without the need for major restructuring.

The family of circuit complexity classes
RC(\cdot)
characterizes such models that can grow dynamically. We posit that
RC(\cdot)
(or some variation thereof) is necessary to ground circuit complexity, and therefore expressivity research, in the realities of the physical world. We also anticipate that these efforts will reveal better methods for analyzing tradeoffs inherent in computation, including: time/space, parallelism/depth, and accuracy/efficiency.

Future research in the area of
RC(\cdot)
should focus on further calcifying the framework and on incorporating certain realistic elements that we excluded that can further constrain computation. These elements include but are not limited to: the relationship between entropy and heat dissipation, energy requirements, and noise.

Lastly, we make note that our work stays within the standard Boolean circuit definition and does not consider the effect of recurrent connections. We strongly believe, however, that our understanding of
RC(\cdot)
and circuit complexity will be greatly enhanced by a better theory of recurrent networks. There remains a wealth of connections to be made in this space.
