A Variational Inequality Approach to Independent Learning in Static Mean-Field Games
Batuhan Yardim alibatuhan.yardim@inf.ethz.ch, Semih Cayci cayci@mathc.rwth-aachen.de, Niao He niao.he@inf.ethz.ch

Abstract
Competitive games involving thousands or even millions of players are prevalent in real-world contexts, such as transportation, communications, and computer networks. However, learning in these large-scale multi-agent environments presents a grand challenge, often referred to as the "curse of many agents". In this paper, we formalize and analyze the Static Mean-Field Game (SMFG) under both full and bandit feedback, offering a generic framework for modeling large population interactions while enabling independent learning. We first establish close connections between SMFG and variational inequality (VI), showing that SMFG can be framed as a VI problem in the infinite agent limit. Building on the VI perspective, we propose independent learning and exploration algorithms that efficiently converge to approximate Nash equilibria, when dealing with a finite number of agents. Theoretically, we provide explicit finite sample complexity guarantees for independent learning across various feedback models in repeated play scenarios, assuming (strongly-)monotone payoffs. Numerically, we validate our results through both simulations and real-world applications in city traffic and network access management.

Introduction

Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) has been an active area of research, with a vast range of successful applications in games such as Chess, Shogi
(
Silver et al., 2017
)
, Go
(
Silver et al., 2018
)
, Stratego
(
Perolat et al., 2022
)
, as well as real-world applications for instance in robotics
(
Matignon et al., 2007
)
.
However, the applications of MARL to games of a much larger scale involving thousands or millions of agents remain a theoretical and practical challenge
(
Wang et al., 2020
)
.

Despite this limitation, competitive games with many players are ubiquitous and typically high-stakes.
In many real-world games, extremely large-scale competitive multi-agency is the rule rather than the exception: for instance, when commuting every morning between cities using infrastructure shared with millions of other commuters,
when periodically accessing an internet resource by querying servers used simultaneously by many users,
or when sending information over common communication channels.
Crucially, in such settings, learning can only feasibly occur in a
decentralized manner
due to the massive scale of the problem.
Each agent can only utilize their local observations (often in the form of partial/bandit feedback) to maximize their selfish utilities.
Another common feature in such games is
statelessness
.
For instance, the capacity of an intercity highway or an internet server is only a function of its (and other highways’/servers’) immediate load and not a function of time
1
1

1

Unless the timescale is years/decades, in which long-term degradation effects will become significant.
.
In our setting, we are interested in
independent learning (IL) using repeated play
in such games, that is, algorithms where each agent learns from their own (noisy) interactions without observing others or a centralized coordinator.
IL, despite being theoretically challenging, is natural for such games as centralized control can be an unrealistic assumption for large populations.

1.1
Game Formulation

We formalize the static mean-field game (SMFG), the main mathematical object of interest in this work.
The SMFG problem with repeated plays consists of the following.

•

Finite set of players
\mathcal{N}=\{1,\ldots,N\}
with
|\mathcal{N}|=N\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
,

•

Set of finitely many actions
\mathcal{A}
, with
|\mathcal{A}|=K\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
,

•

A payoff function
\mathbf{F}:\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow[0,1]^{K}
, which maps the occupancy measure of the population over actions to a corresponding payoff in
[0,1]
for each action.

Here
\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
denotes the probability simplex over a finite set
\mathcal{A}
. For
\bm{\mu}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
,
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we denote the entry of
\mathbf{F}(\bm{\mu})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}
corresponding to action
a
as
\mathbf{F}(\bm{\mu},a)=\mathbf{F}(\bm{\mu})(a)\in\mathbb{R}
.

SMFG is played across multiple rounds where players are allowed to alter their strategies in between observations, where at each round
t\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
,

1.

Each player
i\in\mathcal{N}
picks an action
a_{t}^{i}\in\mathcal{A}
,

2.

The empirical occupancy measure over actions is set to be
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{e}_{a_{t}^{i}}

3.

Depending on the feedback model, each agent
i\in\mathcal{N}
observes noisy rewards:

Full feedback:
The (noisy) payoffs for
each
action
\mathbf{r}^{i}_{t}:=\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t})+\mathbf{n}_{t}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
or,

Bandit feedback:
The (noisy) payoff for their chosen action
r^{i}_{t}:=\mathbf{r}^{i}_{t}(a_{t}^{i})\in\mathbb{R}
.

4.

Each agent receives the payoff
r^{i}_{t}
.

Here
\mathbf{e}_{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}}
is the standard unit vector with coordinate
a
set to 1, and we assume for each action
a\in\mathcal{A}
the noise
\mathbf{n}_{t}^{i}(a)
are independent for all
i\in\mathcal{N},t\geq 0
and have zero mean and variance upper bounded by
\sigma^{2}
.
Intuitively, SMFG models games where the payoff obtained from choosing an action depends on how the population is statistically distributed over actions, without distinguishing between particular players.
Hence, each agent observes a noisy version of the payoff of their action (or the payoff of all actions with full feedback) under the current empirical occupancy over actions.

We motivate the relevance of the SMFG framework with three examples from real-world applications.

1.

Network resource management.

Assume there are
K
resources (e.g., servers or computational nodes) available on a computer network shared by a large number of (say,
N\gg K
) users.
These resources might have varying capabilities, hence the delay in accessing each will be a non-linear function of the overall load.
At each time step, each user tries to access a resource and experiences a delay that increases with the number of users trying to access the same node.
The Tor network application is a particularly good example of SMFGs: the network has 2-3 million users and functions by each user choosing an entry node to use in a decentralized fashion
(
Tor Metrics, 2024
)
.

2.

Repeated commuting with large populations.

Every morning,
N
commuters from city A to city B choose among
K
routes to drive to their target (typically
N\gg K
), observing only how long it takes them to commute.
The distribution of choices among the population affects how much time each person spends traveling.
The commute times in modern road infrastructure can be a very complex function of overall load
(
Hoogendoorn and Knoop, 2013
)
due to non-trivial feedback loops and adaptive systems such as load-dependent traffic lights.
On the other hand, city traffic is a prime example of finite resources (with potentially complex payoff functions) shared by thousands or millions of inhabitants, especially during heavy commute hours
(
Ambühl et al., 2017
)
.
The empirical study of macroscopic flow diagrams
(
Geroliminis and Daganzo, 2008
;
Ambühl et al., 2017
)
suggests that at least in aggregate, city traffic flow is explained by its momentary occupancy.
This empirical observation motivates modeling periodic commutes as a stateless game.

3.

Multi-player multi-armed bandits with soft collisions.

Multiplayer MAB have already been studied in the special case where collisions (i.e., multiple players choosing the same arm) result in zero returns.
In many real-world applications, arms used by multiple players yield diminished (but non-zero) utilities when occupied by multiple players.
For instance, in many radio communications applications (Bluetooth, Wi-Fi), common frequencies are automatically used via
time-sharing
, yielding delayed but successful communication when collisions occur.

1.2
Learning Objectives

We consider competitive games, that is, each agent aims to maximize their personal expected payoff without regard to social welfare.
We allow agents to play randomized actions (mixed strategies), where each agent
i
randomly chooses their actions at time
t
with respect to their mixed strategy
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
.
The primary solution concept for such a game is the Nash equilibrium (NE).

Definition 1

(Expected payoff, exploitability, Nash equilibrium)
.

For an
N
-tuple of mixed strategies
(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{N})\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}^{N}
, we define the expected payoff
V^{i}
of an agent
i\in\mathcal{N}
as

\displaystyle V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{N}):=\Exop\left[\mathbf{F}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{e}_{a^{j}}\right)(a^{i})\middle|a^{j}\sim\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j},\forall j=1,\ldots,N\right].

We define the
exploitability
of agent
i
for the tuple as
\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}):=\max_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{-i})-V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{N})
. An
N
-tuple
(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{N})\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}^{N}
is called a NE if for all
i
,
\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})=0
, namely,

V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{N})=\max_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{-i}),

and it is called a
\delta
-NE (
\delta>0
) if
for all
i
,
\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})\leq\delta
, namely,
V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{N})\geq\max_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{-i})-\delta
.

Intuitively, under a mixed strategy profile
(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{N})
that is a Nash equilibrium, no agent has the incentive to deviate from their mixed strategy as in expectation each agent plays optimally with respect to the rest.
A Nash equilibrium of the SMFG can be shown to always exist in this setting without any further assumptions
(
Nash, 1950
)
, as the SMFG is also an
N
-player normal form game when
\mathcal{A}
is finite.

Goal.

With the SMFG problem definition and the solution concept introduced, we state our objective: in both feedback models, we would like to find
sample efficient
algorithms
2
2

2

We rigorously formalize the notion of an algorithm in the different feedback models in the appendix (Section
C
).
that learn an
approximate NE
(in the sense of low exploitability)
independently
from repeated plays by
N
agents when
N
is
large
.

We will solve the SMFG in the regime when
N
is very large by leveraging its connection to the corresponding problem in the infinite-player limit, i.e.,
N\rightarrow\infty
.
For this purpose, we introduce the following MF-NE concept.

Definition 2

(MF-NE)
.

A mean-field Nash equilibrium (MF-NE)
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
associated with payoff operator
\mathbf{F}
is a probability distribution over actions such that
\sum_{a}\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}(a)\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*},a)=\max_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}\sum_{a}\boldsymbol{\pi}(a)\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*},a)
.
If it holds that
\sum_{a}\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}(a)\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*},a)\geq\max_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}\sum_{a}\boldsymbol{\pi}(a)\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*},a)-\delta
for some
\delta>0
, we call
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
a
\delta
-MF-NE.

Intuitively, the mean-field limit simplifies the SMFG problem by assuming each agent follows the same policy and replacing the notion of
N
independent agents with a single agent playing against a continuum of infinitely many identical agents.
Notably, finding an MF-NE is equivalent to solving the following variational inequality (VI) problem corresponding to the operator
\mathbf{F}
and domain
\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
:

\displaystyle\text{Find }\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}\text{ s.t. }\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\pi})\geq 0,\forall\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}.

(MF-VI)

The
\delta
-MF-NE is related to the strong gap function of (
MF-VI
), as
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
is a
\delta
-MF-NE if and only if
\min_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\pi})\geq-\delta
.
While the MF-VI is not a concrete game and MF-NE is not strictly speaking a Nash equilibrium of any game, they will be crucial tools in constructing independent learning (IL) algorithms for SMFG.

1.3
Summary of Contributions

Based on the VI perspective, we develop algorithms that efficiently converge to approximate NE solutions for the SMFG problem, providing finite-sample, finite-agent, and IL guarantees under different feedback models.
Our key technical contributions are as follows:

1.

VI formulation and approximation.
We formulate the infinite-agent mean-field game limit as a VI.
We make connections between MF-NE, VIs with regularization, and NE of the SMFG, providing explicit bounds on the bias introduced due to approximating the
N
-agent game with an infinite player game.
In particular, we show that a solution of the (
MF-VI
) is a
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{N}}})
-NE of the
N
-player game, thereby justifying the usefulness of the approximation (
MF-VI
) when
N
is large.

2.

Independent learning with
N
agents and full feedback.
Leveraging techniques from optimization and VIs, we analyze independent learning in the
N
-agent setting and prove finite sample bounds with regularized learning. Our work highlights how VI and operator theory can provide insights into IL with full or partial feedback.
In each feedback model, we propose a
\tau
-Tikhonov regularization scheme that stabilizes the divergence between agent policies without communication, which introduces a
\mathcal{O}(\tau)
bias.
When agents have full (though noisy) feedback of reward payoffs, we show that after
T
rounds of play, our algorithm produces policies with expected exploitability upper bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\tau^{-2}}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\tau^{-1}}{\sqrt{N}}+\tau)
with monotone payoffs.
For
\lambda
-strongly monotone payoffs, we improve this to
\mathcal{O}(\frac{\tau^{-\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}\lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\tau^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\sqrt{N}}+\tau)
.

3.

Learning guarantees with bandit feedback.
We further analyze the learning algorithm under a bandit feedback model and prove finite sample guarantees.
In this case, we propose a probabilistic exploration scheme that enables agents to obtain low-variance estimates of the payoff operator
\mathbf{F}
without centralized coordination.
In this scenario, we show that after
T
rounds of play in expectation the exploitability in the
unregularized
SMFG is bounded by

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{N^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}}}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{N}})
for monotone payoffs and by

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{N^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}}\lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\sqrt[3]{N}})
for
\lambda
-strongly monotone payoffs.

4.

Empirical validation.
We verify our theoretical results through synthetic and real-world experiments such as the beach bar problem, linear payoffs, city traffic management, and network access within the Tor network.
These experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the SMFG formulation and our IL algorithms for addressing games with large populations.

1.4
Related Work and Comparisons

This work is situated at the intersection of multiple areas of research, and we discuss each.

Mean-field games.

Mean-field games (MFG), originally proposed independently by
Lasry and Lions (2007)
and
Huang et al. (2006)
, have been an active research area in multi-agent RL literature.
MFG is a useful theoretical tool for analyzing a specific class of MARL problems consisting of a large number of players with symmetric (but competitive) interests by formulating the infinite-agent limit.
The MFG formalism has been used successfully to model large-scale problems such as
auctions
(
Pinasco et al., 2023
)
,
electricity markets
(
Gomes and Saúde, 2021
)
,
epidemics
(
Aurell et al., 2022
;
Huang and Zhu, 2022
)
,
and carbon markets
(
Carmona et al., 2022
)
.

In this work, we analyze a learning problem in a static (or stateless) finite-action MFG.
Closer to our setting, discrete-time dynamic MFGs have been analyzed under various assumptions, reward models, and solution concepts, such as Lasry-Lions games
(
Perrin et al., 2020
;
Pérolat et al., 2022
)
, stationary MFG
(
Anahtarci et al., 2022
;
Xie et al., 2021
;
Zaman et al., 2023
;
Yardim et al., 2023
)
, linear quadratic MFG
(
Guo et al., 2022
;
Fu et al., 2019
)
, and MFGs on graphs
(
Yang et al., 2018
;
Gu et al., 2024
)
.
However, finite-sample learning guarantees for mean-field games only exist under specific assumptions.
For Lasry-Lions games, convergence guarantees exist with an infinite agent oracle
(
Perrin et al., 2020
;
Zhang et al., 2024a
)
, while IL guarantees with finite agents in this setting do not exist to the best of our knowledge.
Recently, theoretical learning guarantees for centralized learning on monotone MFGs with graphon structure
(
Zhang et al., 2024a
)
and potential MFGs
(
Geist et al., 2022
)
have been shown.
IL in stationary MFGs has been studied either under a subjective equilibrium solution concept
(
Yongacoglu et al., 2022
)
or with strong regularization bias and poor sample complexity
(
Yardim et al., 2023
)
.

Variational inequalities (VI).

VIs and relevant algorithms have been an active area of research in classical and recent optimization literature; see e.g.,
Nemirovski (2004)
;
Facchinei and Pang (2003)
;
Nesterov (2007)
;
Lin and Jordan (2024)
;
Kotsalis et al. (2022)
, just to list a few.
VIs have been extensively analyzed in the case of (strongly) monotone operators.
Classical algorithms in monotone VIs include the projected gradient method
(
Facchinei and Pang, 2003
)
, the extragradient method
(
Korpelevich, 1976
)
, and proximal point algorithms
(
Rockafellar, 1976
)
.
Several works provide extensions to more general operator classes such as generalized monotone operators
(
Kotsalis et al., 2022
)
or pseudo-monotone operators
(
Karamardian, 1976
)
.
A recent survey for methods for solving VIs can be found in
Beznosikov et al. (2023)
.
In this work, we use VIs to formalize the infinite-player limit and use algorithmic tools from VI literature to analyze the more challenging IL setting.
Our algorithms are adaptations of the projected gradient method to the decentralized learning under partial feedback.
However, in the SMFG setting, it is not possible to have oracle access to the VI operator (or even unbiased samples of it), posing an algorithmic and theoretical design challenge.
Strictly speaking, the VI given by (
MF-VI
) exists only as an abstract approximation in our work and not as an oracle model.

Decentralized VI algorithms.

Another setting that is related but orthogonal to our problem is the distributed/decentralized VI problem studied for example by
Srivastava et al. (2011)
;
Mukherjee and Chakraborty (2020)
;
Kovalev et al. (2022)
.
In this setting, the VI operator is assumed to decompose into
M
components
\mathbf{F}:=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\mathbf{F}_{m}
each of which can be evaluated locally by
M
workers.
Workers are permitted to occasionally transmit information along a communication graph: the communication complexity of algorithms as well as the computational complexity is of interest.
Our setting fundamentally differs from the decentralized VI problem.
Firstly, the VI operator in our case exists only implicitly as an approximation of the finite-player game dynamics: strictly speaking, the problem becomes a VI only at the infinite-player limit.
Furthermore, the VI operator
\mathbf{F}
in our setting does not admit components that can be independently evaluated by any player due to the game interactions: our stochastic oracle is much more restrictive.
Finally, the independent players cannot communicate at all throughout the repeated plays, introducing the algorithmic challenge of fully decentralized learning.

Multi-player MAB (MMAB).

Our model is related to the MMAB problem.
The standard reward model for MMAB is collisions
(
Anandkumar et al., 2011
)
, where agents receive a reward of 0 if more than one pulls the same arm.
Results have focused on the so-called no-communications setting, proving regret guarantees for the cooperative setting without a coordinator
(
Avner and Mannor, 2014
;
Rosenski et al., 2016
;
Bubeck et al., 2021
)
.
Our SMFG model can capture much more general reward models than binary collisions.
Moreover, in our competitive setting, our primary metric is exploitability or proximity to NE rather than regret as typically employed in the collaborative setting of MMAB (similar to the NE for MMAB result established by
Lugosi and Mehrabian (2022)
).

Other work.

A setting that has a similar set of keywords is mean-field bandits
(
Gummadi et al., 2013
;
Wang and Jia, 2021
)
, as mentioned in the survey by
Laurière et al. (2024)
.
However, these models do not analyze a
competitive (Nash)
equilibrium, but rather a population steady-state reached by an infinite population under prescribed unknown agent behavior.
In these works, optimality or exploitability is not a concern, hence a direct comparison with our work is not possible (differences detailed in appendix, Section
B
).
On the other hand, our model is equivalent to decentralized learning on static MFGs
(
Laurière et al., 2024
)
with finitely many agents and partial feedback.
The problem of IL is fundamental in algorithmic game theory and has been separately investigated in zero-sum Markov games
(
Daskalakis et al., 2020
;
Sayin et al., 2021
;
Ozdaglar et al., 2021
)
and potential games
(
Ding et al., 2022
;
Heliou et al., 2017
;
Alatur et al., 2024
)
.
Another related literature is population games and evolutionary dynamics
(
Sandholm, 2015
;
Quijano et al., 2017
)
, where competitive populations are analyzed with differential equations.
While solution concepts overlap, we are interested in IL with repeated play under partial feedback, not the continuous-time dynamic system.

2
Assumptions on Payoffs and Examples

In general, finding a NE in normal form games is known to
be challenging and possibly computationally intractable
(
Papadimitriou, 1994
;
Daskalakis et al., 2009
)
. This difficulty persists even in the mean-field regime
(
Yardim et al., 2024
)
.
Therefore, we will analyze SMFG under certain structural assumptions on
\mathbf{F}
that are closely aligned with many real-world applications.

2.1
Assumption on Lipschitz continuity

Our first assumption is the Lipschitz continuity of the payoff.
In practice, action payoffs should not change drastically with small variations in population behavior: Assumption
2.1
formalizes this intuition.

{assumption}

[Lipschitz continuous payoffs]
The payoff map
\mathbf{F}:\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow[0,1]^{K}
is called Lipschitz continuous with parameter
L>0
if for any
\bm{\mu},\bm{\mu}^{\prime}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
,
\|\mathbf{F}(\bm{\mu})-\mathbf{F}(\bm{\mu}^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq L\|\bm{\mu}-\bm{\mu}^{\prime}\|_{2}
.

As a direct consequence of the Lipschitz continuous payoffs, we have the following technical lemmas, which will be used in the subsequent analysis.

Lemma 3
.

For any policy profile
(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{N})\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}^{N}
, it holds that

\displaystyle|V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{N})-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i,\top}\Exop\left[\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}})\middle|\begin{subarray}{c}a^{j}\sim\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j},\forall j\\
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{e}_{a^{j}}\end{subarray}\right]|\leq\frac{L\sqrt{2}}{N}.

In addition, we can also obtain the Lipschitz continuity of
V^{i}
and
\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}
, as stated below.

Lemma 4

(
V^{i},\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}
are Lipschitz)
.

For any
i\in\mathcal{N}
, the value functions
V^{i}:\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and exploitability functions
\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}:\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are Lipschitz continuous, that is, for any
(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{N})\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}^{N},\boldsymbol{\pi},\bar{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
,

\displaystyle|V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{-j})-V^{i}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\pi}},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{-j})|\leq

\displaystyle L_{j,i}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\|_{2},

\displaystyle|\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{-j})-\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\pi}},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{-j})|\leq

\displaystyle\bar{L}_{j,i}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\|_{2},

with the Lipschitz moduli given by

\displaystyle L_{i,j}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{K},\text{if }i=j,\\
\frac{2L\sqrt{2K}}{N},\text{if }i\neq j,\end{cases}\qquad\bar{L}_{i,j}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{K},\text{if }i=j,\\
\frac{4L\sqrt{2K}}{N},\text{if }i\neq j.\end{cases}

We defer the proofs to Sections
E.1
and
E.2
in the appendix.

2.2
Assumption on Monotonicity

The next fundamental assumption, monotonicity, is standard in variational inequality literature
(
Facchinei and Pang, 2003
)
and is similar in form to Lasry-Lions conditions in mean-field games literature (initially studied by
Lasry and Lions (2007)
, later analyzed with reinforcement learning in MFGs by
Perrin et al. (2020)
;
Pérolat et al. (2022)
and many others).

{assumption}
[Monotone/Strongly monotone payoff]

The vector map
\mathbf{F}:\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow[0,1]^{K}
is called monotone if for some
\lambda\geq 0
, for all
\forall\bm{\mu}_{1},\bm{\mu}_{2}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{F}(\bm{\mu}_{1})-\mathbf{F}(\bm{\mu}_{2})\right)^{\top}(\bm{\mu}_{1}-\bm{\mu}_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}(\bm{\mu}_{1}(a)-\bm{\mu}_{2}(a))(\mathbf{F}(\bm{\mu}_{1},a)-\mathbf{F}(\bm{\mu}_{2},a))

\displaystyle\leq-\lambda\|\bm{\mu}_{1}-\bm{\mu}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}.

Furthermore, if the above holds for some
\lambda>0
,
\mathbf{F}
is called
\lambda
-strongly monotone.

Monotone payoffs, as in the case of Lasry-Lions conditions, can be intuitively thought of as modeling problems where the payoff of an action on average decreases as the occupancy increases.
In comparison to
Lasry and Lions (2007)
;
Perrin et al. (2020)
, since the game is stateless in our case, the monotonicity condition degenerates to “decreasing payoffs with increasing
action occupancy
”.

While Assumption
2.2
is abstract, one large class of concrete monotone payoff functions are given by games where actions have payoffs non-increasing with their occupancy and there is no interaction between actions, demonstrated below by Example
5
.

Example 5

(Non-increasing payoffs)
.

Let
F_{a}:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
for
a\in\mathcal{A}
be Lipschitz continuous and non-increasing functions, define
\mathbf{F}(\bm{\mu}):=\sum_{a}F_{a}(\bm{\mu}(a))\mathbf{e}_{a}
.
Then
\mathbf{F}
is monotone and Lipschitz.
If
F_{a}
is also
strictly
decreasing and there exists
\lambda>0
such that
|F_{a}(x)-F_{a}(x^{\prime})|\geq\lambda|x-x^{\prime}|
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in[0,1],a\in\mathcal{A}
, then
\mathbf{F}
is
\lambda
-strongly monotone.

Applications where such an
F_{a}
exists are common and intuitive: non-increasing
F_{a}
models congestion effects on common resources.
Even theoretically, the existence of a non-increasing
F_{a}
in wireless communications can be motivated by well-known results on the capacity of multiple access channels in information theory
(
El Gamal and Cover, 1980
;
Skwirzynski, 1981
)
.
Likewise, the conceptual models of traffic flow (e.g., see the macroscopic flow diagram
(
Loder et al., 2019
)
) imply traffic
speed
is monotonically decreasing with occupancy in aggregate.
As a special case of Example
5
, the classical multi-player multi-armed bandits setting with collisions yields a monotone Lipschitz continuous payoff.
We state this explicitly in the following example.

Example 6

(Multi-player MAB with Collisions)
.

For the
N
-player game, for each action
a\in\mathcal{A}
, take the functions
F_{col}^{a}:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle F_{col}^{a}(x)=\begin{cases}\alpha^{a},\text{if }x\leq\frac{1}{N},\\
\alpha^{a}N(\frac{2}{N}-x),\text{if }\frac{1}{N}\leq x\leq\frac{2}{N},\\
0,\text{if }x\geq\frac{2}{N}.\end{cases}

where
\alpha^{a}\in[0,1]
is the expected payoff of the action
a
when no collision occurs.
Take the payoff map
\mathbf{F}(\bm{\mu}):=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}F_{col}^{a}(\mu_{a})\mathbf{e}_{a}
.
The payoff map
\mathbf{F}
is Lipschitz continuous and monotone and corresponds to the classical multi-player MAB with collisions.

Due to this fact, one interpretation of our SMFG setting is that we allow soft collisions for action payoffs, as actions may yield non-zero but diminished payoffs when chosen by multiple players.
In fact, a model with hard collisions would not be feasible (or realistic) in applications where
N\gg K
as any policy supported by all actions would lead to collisions with probability approaching
1
.
On the other hand, a general non-increasing function
F_{col}^{a}
as in Example
5
can model more realistic use cases where collisions happen almost surely due to a large number of players/users.

Before concluding the discussion of monotonicity of payoffs, we also compare our payoff assumptions with two other common game-theoretical settings -- potential games
3
3

3

Not to be confused with
\mathbf{F}
that admits a potential
\Phi
such that
\mathbf{F}=\nabla\Phi
, also called a gradient field.

(
Monderer and Shapley, 1996
)
and their subset, congestion games
(
Rosenthal, 1973

, for which algorithms with IL guarantees already exist
(
Leonardos et al., 2022

.
We show that SMFGs with monotone payoffs are not special cases of either of these settings. Specifically, we provide a counter-example of SMFG that does not admit a game potential.

Remark 7

(SMFG is not a potential game)
.

The SMFG is a
potential game
if there exists a map
P:\mathcal{A}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that for all
i\in\mathcal{N},\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})\in\mathcal{A}^{N},a\in\mathcal{A}
, it holds that

\displaystyle V^{i}(a^{i},\mathbf{a}^{-i})-V^{i}(a,\mathbf{a}^{-i})=P(a^{i},\mathbf{a}^{-i})-P(a,\mathbf{a}^{-i}),

(1)

where
V^{i}(\mathbf{a})
denotes the expected reward of player
i
when each player
j\in\mathcal{N}
(deterministically) plays action
a^{j}
.
Note that
V^{i}(\mathbf{a})=\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(\mathbf{a}))(a^{i})
where
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}(\mathbf{a})\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
is the empirical distribution of actions over actions induced by action profile
\mathbf{a}
.

We provide an example of an SMFG where no such
P
exists.
Let
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{D}
be a symmetric positive definite matrix,
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
be an arbitrary vector, and
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{M}^{D,D}
be a anti-symmetric matrix such that
\mathbf{X}=-\mathbf{X}^{\top}
.
Take the payoff operator

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(\bm{\mu})=(-\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{X})\bm{\mu}+\mathbf{b},

which can be trivially shown to be monotone.
In particular, take
N=3,K=3,\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\}
, and define the reward
\mathbf{F}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{1}\\
\mu_{2}\\
\mu_{3}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\\
\mu_{1}\\
-\mu_{3}\end{pmatrix},

which is monotone.
Assume that a potential
P
exists and satisfies Equation (
1
).
Then, it would follow that:

\displaystyle V^{1}(a_{2},a_{1},a_{1})-V^{1}(a_{3},a_{1},a_{1})

\displaystyle=P(a_{2},a_{1},a_{1})-P(a_{3},a_{1},a_{1}),

\displaystyle V^{2}(a_{3},a_{1},a_{1})-V^{2}(a_{3},a_{2},a_{1})

\displaystyle=P(a_{3},a_{1},a_{1})-P(a_{3},a_{2},a_{1}),

\displaystyle V^{1}(a_{3},a_{2},a_{1})-V^{1}(a_{2},a_{2},a_{1})

\displaystyle=P(a_{3},a_{2},a_{1})-P(a_{2},a_{2},a_{1}),

\displaystyle V^{2}(a_{2},a_{2},a_{1})-V^{2}(a_{2},a_{1},a_{1})

\displaystyle=P(a_{2},a_{2},a_{1})-P(a_{2},a_{1},a_{1}).

Hence, adding the inequalities above, we have that

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle V^{1}(a_{2},a_{1},a_{1})-V^{1}(a_{3},a_{1},a_{1})+V^{2}(a_{3},a_{1},a_{1})-V^{2}(a_{3},a_{2},a_{1})

\displaystyle+V^{1}(a_{3},a_{2},a_{1})-V^{1}(a_{2},a_{2},a_{1})+V^{2}(a_{2},a_{2},a_{1})-V^{2}(a_{2},a_{1},a_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}((\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}},0),a_{2})-\mathbf{F}((\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}},0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}),a_{3})+\mathbf{F}((\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}},0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}),a_{1})-\mathbf{F}((\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}),a_{2})

\displaystyle+\mathbf{F}((\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}),a_{3})-\mathbf{F}((\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}},\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}},0),a_{2})+\mathbf{F}((\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}},\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}},0),a_{2})-\mathbf{F}((\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}},\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}},0),a_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}-(-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}})+(-\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}})-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}+(-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}})-\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}-(-1)\neq 0,

leading to a contradiction.
As a result, no such potential
P
could exist, and this (monotone) SMFG cannot be a potential game.

2.3
Assumption on Stochastic Noise

Finally, our results require the standard assumption of independent noise with bounded variance.
We formalize this below in Assumption
2.3
.

{assumption}
[Independent, bounded variance noise]

We assume that the payoff noise is zero mean, has entrywise variance upper bounded by
\sigma^{2}
, and is independent.
That is, we assume the following hold:

1.

For all
t\geq 0,i\in\mathcal{N}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
, it holds that
\Exop[\mathbf{n}_{t}^{i}(a)]=0
and
\Exop[\mathbf{n}_{t}^{i}(a)^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}
,

2.

\left\{\mathbf{n}_{t}^{i}(a)\right\}_{i\in\mathcal{N},t\geq 0,a\in\mathcal{A}}
are independent random variables,

3.

For any
t\geq 0,i\in\mathcal{N}
, the random vector
\mathbf{n}_{t}^{i}
is independent from
\{a_{t^{\prime}}^{j}\}_{t^{\prime}\leq t,j\in\mathcal{N}}
(i.e., actions taken up to step
t
by all players).

The assumption of independent noise with bounded variance is common in bandits literature
(
Anandkumar et al., 2011
;
Avner and Mannor, 2014
;
Bubeck et al., 2021
)
.
We emphasize that the noise vectors
\mathbf{n}_{t}^{i}
have entry-wise bounded variance by
\sigma^{2}
in Assumption
2.3
.
In optimization or variational inequality settings, the assumption of independent sources of noise in stochastic oracles is also standard, although typically a bound on
\Exop[\|\mathbf{n}_{t}^{i}\|_{2}^{2}]
is assumed
(
Juditsky et al., 2011
)
.

3
The VI Approximation as
N\rightarrow\infty

Our first set of theoretical results presented in this chapter makes the connection between the NE and a solution of (
MF-VI
) explicit.
We will show that solutions of (
MF-VI
) form good approximations of the true NE in the
N
-player game if
N
is large.

Remark 8

(Existence and Uniqueness of MF-NE)
.

Let
\mathbf{F}:\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow[0,1]^{K}
be a continuous function.
Then
\mathbf{F}
has at least one MF-NE
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
, and the set of MF-NE is compact.
Furthermore, if
\mathbf{F}
is also
\lambda
-strongly monotone for some
\lambda>0
, then the MF-NE is unique.
This can be seen as follows.
The MF-NE corresponds to solutions of the VI:
\forall\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}},\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\pi})\geq 0
.
The domain set
\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
is compact and convex, and the assumption that
\mathbf{F}
is continuous yields the existence of a solution using Corollary 2.2.5 of
Facchinei and Pang (2003)
.
For uniqueness in the case of strong monotonicity, see Theorem 2.3.3 of
Facchinei and Pang (2003)
.

The following theorem shows that the solution of (
MF-VI
), when deployed by all players, is a
\mathcal{O}\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{N}}}\right)
solution of the
N
-player game.
Therefore, the MF-NE solution will be an arbitrarily good approximation of the true NE when
N\rightarrow\infty
, and the bias introduced by studying the
N
-player game can be explicitly quantified.

Theorem 9
.

Let
\mathbf{F}
be
L
-Lipschitz,
\delta\geq 0
arbitrary, and let
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
be a
\delta
-MF-NE.
Then, the strategy profile
(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}^{N}
is a
\mathcal{O}\left(\delta+\frac{L}{\sqrt{N}}\right)
-NE of the
N
-player SMFG.

Proof.

Firstly, define the independent random variables
a^{j}\sim\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
for all
j\in\mathcal{N}
for a
\delta
-MF-NE
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
.
Define the random variable
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}:=\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{e}_{a^{j}}
, which is the empirical distribution of players over actions in a single round of an SMFG.
The proof will proceed by formally proving that if
N
is large enough, then
\Exop[\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}})]\approx\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})
and
a^{j}
is approximately independent from
\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}})
.

It is straightforward that
\Exop\left[\widehat{\bm{\mu}}\right]=\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
.
Furthermore, by independence of the random vectors
\mathbf{e}_{a^{j}}
, we have

\displaystyle\Exop\left[\left\|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\right\|_{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\Exop\Big[\Big\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{e}_{a^{j}}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\Big\|_{2}^{2}\Big]}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Exop\left[\left\|\mathbf{e}_{a^{j}}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]}\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{N}}.

Hence, as
\mathbf{F}
is
L
-Lipschitz, we have that

\displaystyle\|\Exop[\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}})|a_{j}\sim\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}]-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})\|_{2}\leq\Exop[\|\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}})-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})\|_{2}]\leq\frac{2L}{\sqrt{N}}.

(2)

Now let
i\in\mathcal{N}
be arbitrary, and let
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
be any distribution over actions that satisfies
V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*,-i})=\max_{\boldsymbol{\pi}}V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*,-i})
.
We also define the quantities

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{1}=\Exop\left[\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}})\middle|a^{j}\sim\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*},\forall j\in\mathcal{N}\right],\qquad\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{2}=\Exop\left[\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}})\middle|a^{j}\sim\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\text{ for }\forall i\neq j,\quad a^{i}\sim\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}\right].

We will bound
V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*,-i})-V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*,-i})
.
Combining Lemma
3
and the inequality (
2
), we observe

\displaystyle|V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*,-i})-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime\top}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})|\leq

\displaystyle|V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*,-i})-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime\top}\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{2}|+\Big|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime\top}\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime\top}\mathbf{F}\Big(\frac{N-1}{N}\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}+\frac{1}{N}\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}\Big)\Big|

\displaystyle+\Big|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime\top}\mathbf{F}\Big(\frac{N-1}{N}\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}+\frac{1}{N}\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}\Big)-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime\top}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{L\sqrt{2}}{N}+\frac{2L}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{2L}{N},

since
\mathbf{F}
is
L
-Lipschitz.
Likewise, using Lemma
3
once again, we have

\displaystyle|V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*,-i})-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*,\top}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})|

\displaystyle\leq|V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*,-i})-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*,\top}\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{1}|+|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*,\top}\overline{\mathbf{F}}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*,\top}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L\sqrt{2}}{N}+\frac{2L}{\sqrt{N}}.

Finally, using the definition of a
\delta
-MF-NE, it holds that

\displaystyle V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*,-i})-V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*,-i})\leq

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})+|V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*,-i})-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*,\top}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})|

\displaystyle+|V^{i}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*,-i})-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime\top}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\delta+\frac{L(2\sqrt{2}+4)}{N}+\frac{4L}{\sqrt{N}}.

∎

Recall our goal in the context of the
N
-player SMFG is to find policies
\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
with low exploitability
\mathcal{E}_{\text{exp}}^{i}
for all
i
. Theorem
9
considers that agents adopt the same policy
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
from solving the VI corresponding to operator
\mathbf{F}
to obtain a low-exploitability approximation.
We will generalize this result to explicitly bound
\mathcal{E}_{\text{exp}}^{i}
when agent policies can also deviate and when agents can employ regularization.
In our algorithms, regularizing the MF-VI problem will play a crucial role in the IL setting, as it will prevent the policies of agents from diverging when there is no centralized controller synchronizing the policies of agents.
For this reason, our algorithms in the later sections will introduce extraneous regularization to (
MF-VI
) and instead solve the following
\tau
-Tikhonov regularized VI problem:

\displaystyle\text{Find }\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}\text{ s.t. }(\mathbf{F}-\tau\mathbf{I})(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\pi})\geq 0,\forall\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}.

(MF-RVI)

The following theorem quantifies the additional exploitability incurred in the
N
-player game due to (1) extraneous regularization, which is useful for algorithm design, and (2) deviations in agent policies from the MF-NE, potentially due to stochasticity in learning.
Theorem
10
will be a more useful result later in a learning setting since the learned policies
(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{N})
will only approximate the solution of (
MF-RVI
).

Theorem 10
.

Let
\mathbf{F}
be monotone,
L
-Lipschitz.
Let
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau}^{*}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
be the (unique) MF-NE of the regularized map
\mathbf{F}-\tau\mathbf{I}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{N}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
be such that
\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau}^{*}\|_{2}\leq\delta
for all
i
, then it holds that
\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})=\mathcal{O}(\tau+\delta+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{N}}})
for all
i\in\mathcal{N}
.

Proof.

By the Lipschitz continuity of exploitability (Lemma
4
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{N})+\sqrt{K}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau}^{*}\|_{2}+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{4L\sqrt{2K}}{N}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}-\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau}^{*}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{N})+\delta\sqrt{K}+4L\sqrt{2K}\delta.

(3)

Since
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau}^{*}
is the unique MF-NE of the operator
\mathbf{F}-\tau\mathbf{I}
, it holds by definition that

\displaystyle(\mathbf{F}-\tau\mathbf{I})(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau}

\displaystyle\geq(\mathbf{F}-\tau\mathbf{I})(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\pi}.

Organizing both sides, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\pi}+\tau\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau}-\boldsymbol{\pi})\geq\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\pi}-2\tau,

as
|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau}-\boldsymbol{\pi})|\leq\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau}-\boldsymbol{\pi}\|_{2}\leq 2
.
Then,
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}_{\tau}
is a
2\tau
-MF-NE for the operator
\mathbf{F}
, and by Theorem
9
,
\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\tau}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{N})\leq\mathcal{O}(\tau+\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{N}}})
.
Placing this in (
3
) proves the theorem.
∎

To summarize, this section presented key approximation results linking the solutions of (
MF-VI
) and (
MF-RVI
) to the
N
-player exploitability in the SMFG.

4
Convergence in the Full Feedback Case

We first present an IL algorithm for the full feedback setting, as a first step towards analyzing the more interesting bandit feedback setting.
In this setting, while there is no centralized controller, independent noisy reports of all action payoffs are available to each agent after each round.

Is it possible to simply solve MF-RVI in our IL setting?

Before we present our results, we note the following:
Past works in MFG have already proved approximation results of
N
-agent games by MFG albeit in different settings
(
Saldi et al., 2019
;
Yardim et al., 2024
)
, but these results do not consider when
learning itself
is carried out with
N
agents.
If
N
agents can not communicate, it is theoretically challenging to approximate the MF-RVI and to tackle bandit feedback.
Most importantly, the IL algorithms formalized in Section
C
can not query an operator oracle or maintain a common iterate throughout repeated plays.
Therefore, the approximation properties of (
MF-VI
) do not immediately imply the MF-NE can be learned using VI algorithms.
In this section and the next, we prove the more challenging result of convergence with IL, first under full feedback and later under partial (bandit) feedback.

Our analysis builds up on Tikhonov regularized projected ascent (TRPA).
The TRPA operator is defined as

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\eta,\tau}(\boldsymbol{\pi}):=\Pi_{\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}(\boldsymbol{\pi}+\eta(\mathbf{F}-\tau\mathbf{I})(\boldsymbol{\pi}))=\Pi_{\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}((1-\eta\tau)\boldsymbol{\pi}+\eta\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi})),

(TRPA)

for a learning rate
\eta>0
and regularization
\tau>0
.
Intuitively,
\Gamma^{\eta,\tau}
uses
\mathbf{F}
evaluated at
\boldsymbol{\pi}
to modify action probabilities in the direction of the greatest payoff, incorporating an
\ell_{2}
regularizer of
\tau
.
Furthermore, the unique MF-NE
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
of (
MF-RVI
) is also a fixed point of
\Gamma^{\eta,\tau}
.
The analysis of TRPA is standard and known to converge for monotone
\mathbf{F}

(
Facchinei and Pang, 2003
;
Nemirovski, 2004
)
, when (stochastic) oracle access to
\mathbf{F}
is assumed.
Naturally, the main complication in applying the method above will be the fact that in the IL setting, agents can not evaluate the operator
\mathbf{F}
arbitrarily, but rather can only observe (a noisy) estimate of
\mathbf{F}
as a function of the empirical population distribution and not of their policy
\boldsymbol{\pi}
.
In the full feedback setting, we analyze the following dynamics:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\pi}_{0}^{i}:=\operatorname{Unif}(\mathcal{A})=\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{1}_{K},\hskip 10.00002pt\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t+1}=\Pi_{\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}((1-\tau\eta_{t})\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}+\eta_{t}\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}),

(TRPA-Full)

for a time varying learning rate
\eta_{t}
, for each agent
i\in\mathcal{N}
.
The extraneous
\ell_{2}
-regularization incorporated in each agent running TRPA-Full is critical for the analysis and convergence in IL, as it allows explicit synchronization of policies of agents without communication.
We state the TRPA-Full algorithm in Algorithm
1
for reference.

We state the following standard result regarding the TRPA operator without proof, as it will be used later.

Lemma 11

(cf. Theorem 12.1.2 of
Facchinei and Pang (2003)
)
.

Assume
\mathbf{F}
is
\lambda\geq 0
-monotone and
L
-Lipschitz.
Then
\Gamma^{\eta,\tau}
is Lipschitz with constant
\sqrt{1-2(\lambda+\tau)\eta+\eta^{2}(L+\tau)^{2}}
with respect to the
\ell_{2}
-norm.

Algorithm 1

i\in\mathcal{N}
.

Number of actions
K
, regularization
\tau>0
, learning rate
\{\eta_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
, rounds
T>0
.

\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{0}\leftarrow\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{1}

for

Observe payoff
\mathbf{r}^{i}_{t}

Return
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{T}

For abuse of notation, let
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
be the unique solution of (
MF-RVI
) for the regularization
\tau>0
.
Also define the sigma algebra
\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\mathcal{F}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t^{\prime}}^{i}\}_{t^{\prime}=0,\ldots,t}^{i=1,\ldots,N})
.
We maintain the definitions of the core random variables of the SMFG dynamics introduced in Section
1.1
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{e}_{a_{t}^{i}},\quad\mathbf{r}^{i}_{t}:=\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t})+\mathbf{n}_{t}^{i}.

Under TRPA-Full dynamics, we also define the following random variables that assist our analysis.

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i},\quad e_{t}^{i}:=\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}-\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2},\quad u_{t}^{i}:=\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\right].

We call
\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t}
the mean policy,
e_{t}^{i}
the mean policy deviation, and
u_{t}^{i}
the expected
\ell_{2}
-deviation from the regularized MF-NE.
Our goal is to bound the sequence or error terms
u_{t}^{i}
; however, the process is complicated by the fact that in general the policy deviations of agents

are nonzero.
Our strategy is as follows:
(1) derive a recursion for
u_{t}^{i}
incorporating the terms
e_{t}^{i}
,
(2) bound the terms
e_{t}^{i}
, showing the deviation of the policies of the agents goes to zero in expectation, and
(3) solve the recursion to obtain the convergence rate.

The following lemma captures the first step and provides a recurrence for the evolution of
u_{t}^{i}
under TRPA-Full.

Lemma 12

(Error recurrence under full feedback)
.

Under TRPA-Full with learning rates
\eta_{t}
, it holds for
L
-Lipschitz and
\lambda
-strongly monotone
\mathbf{F}
that

\displaystyle\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t+1}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle 3\eta_{t}^{2}K(1+\sigma^{2})+2\eta_{t}^{2}(L+\tau)^{2}+\frac{4\eta_{t}L^{2}\lambda^{-1}}{N}

\displaystyle+2\eta_{t}L^{2}\lambda^{-1}\Exop\left[e_{t}^{i}\right]+\left(1-2\eta_{t}(\nicefrac{{\lambda}}{{2}}+\tau)\right)\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\right],

and for
L
-Lipschitz and monotone
\mathbf{F}
that

\displaystyle\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t+1}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle 3\eta_{t}^{2}K(1+\sigma^{2})+2\eta_{t}^{2}(L+\tau)^{2}+\frac{4\tau^{-1}\eta_{t}L^{2}\delta^{-1}}{N}

\displaystyle+\tau^{-1}\eta_{t}L^{2}\delta^{-1}\Exop\left[e_{t}^{i}\right]+\left(1-2\tau\eta_{t}(1-\delta)\right)\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\right],

for arbitrary
\delta\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

We analyze for any
i\in[N]
the error term
\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}
.
Denote
\alpha_{t}:=(1-\tau\eta_{t})
.
For the regularized solution
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
, we have the fixed point result

\displaystyle\Pi_{\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}((1-\tau\eta_{t})\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}+\eta_{t}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}))=\Pi_{\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}+\eta_{t}(\mathbf{F}-\tau\mathbf{I})(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}))=\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}.

The proof strategy is to decompose the
\ell_{2}
distance of player policies to
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
into 3 components using this property.
We can bound the quantity
\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t+1}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}
by using the non-expansiveness of
\Pi_{\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}
:

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t+1}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}=

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}(\alpha_{t}\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}+\eta_{t}\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i})-\Pi_{\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}(\alpha_{t}\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}+\eta_{t}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}))\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\alpha_{t}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}+\eta_{t}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i})-\alpha_{t}\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}-\eta_{t}\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})+\eta_{t}(\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}))\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{t}^{2}\|\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}+2\eta_{t}(\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})+\eta_{t}(\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i})-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})))^{\top}(\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}))

\displaystyle+\|\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})+\eta_{t}(\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i})-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}))\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\underbrace{\eta_{t}^{2}\|\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}+2\eta_{t}^{2}(\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i})-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}))^{\top}(\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}))}_{(a)}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{2\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}))}_{(b)}+\underbrace{\|\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})+\eta_{t}(\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i})-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}))\|_{2}^{2}}_{(c)}.

(4)

We analyze the three marked terms separately.
For term
(a)
, using the independence assumption of the noise vectors and Young’s inequality, in expectation we obtain

\displaystyle\Exop[(a)]\leq

\displaystyle\eta_{t}^{2}\Exop[\|\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}]+\eta_{t}^{2}\Exop[\|\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i})-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})\|_{2}^{2}]+\eta_{t}^{2}\Exop[\|\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\eta_{t}^{2}\Exop[\|\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}]+\eta_{t}^{2}\Exop[\|\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i})-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})\|_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\eta_{t}^{2}\Exop[\|\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\|\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t})-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}]+\eta_{t}^{2}K

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\eta_{t}^{2}\sigma^{2}K+3\eta_{t}^{2}K\leq 3\eta_{t}^{2}K(\sigma^{2}+1)

For the term
(c)
, we obtain

\displaystyle(c)=

\displaystyle\|\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})+\eta_{t}(\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i})-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}))\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})+\eta_{t}(\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i})-\tau\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})+\tau\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-2(\lambda+\tau)\eta_{t}+(L+\tau)^{2}\eta_{t}^{2}\right)\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-2(\lambda+\tau)\eta_{t}\right)\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+2(L+\tau)^{2}\eta_{t}^{2}

where the last inequality holds from the Lipschitz continuity result of Lemma
11
.

For the term
(b)
, first taking the strongly monotone problem
\lambda>0
, we have that

\displaystyle(b)=

\displaystyle 2\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t}))+2\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t})-\mathbf{F}(\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t}))

\displaystyle+2\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{F}(\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t})-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\left(\frac{\lambda}{4}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\|\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t})-\mathbf{F}(\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right)+2\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\left(\frac{\lambda}{4}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\|\mathbf{F}(\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t})-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+2\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\eta_{t}\lambda\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+2\eta_{t}\lambda^{-1}\|\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t})-\mathbf{F}(\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+2\eta_{t}\lambda^{-1}\|\mathbf{F}(\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t})-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+2\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t})),

which follows from applications of Young’s inequality.
For the last three terms we observe:

\displaystyle\Exop\left[2\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t}))|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle\Exop[\|\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t})-\mathbf{F}(\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq

\displaystyle L^{2}\Exop\left[\|\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t}-\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L^{2}\Exop\left[\frac{1}{N^{2}}\|\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\sum_{i}\mathbf{e}_{a_{t}^{i}}\|^{2}_{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{L^{2}}{N^{2}}\sum_{i}\Exop[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{e}_{a_{t}^{i}}\|^{2}_{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t}]\leq\frac{2L^{2}}{N},

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{F}(\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t})-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle L^{2}\|\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}\|_{2}^{2}=L^{2}e_{t}^{i}.

The second inequality above follows from the fact that
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t}
is the sum of
N
independent random variables and has expectation
\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t}
.
Hence, putting in the bounds for
(a),(b),(c)
and taking expectations, we obtain the inequality

\displaystyle\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t+1}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle 3\eta_{t}^{2}K(1+\sigma^{2})+\frac{4\eta_{t}L^{2}}{\lambda N}+\frac{2\eta_{t}L^{2}}{\lambda}\Exop\left[e_{t}^{i}\right]

\displaystyle+\left(1-2(\nicefrac{{\lambda}}{{2}}+\tau)\eta_{t}\right)\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+2(L+\tau)^{2}\eta_{t}^{2}.

Turning back to the monotone case, if
\lambda=0
, vary the upper bound on
(b)
as follows.
Take any arbitrary
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Then, once again applying Young’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle(b)=

\displaystyle 2\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t}))+2\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t})-\mathbf{F}(\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t}))

\displaystyle+2\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{F}(\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t})-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\left(\frac{\tau\delta}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\tau\delta}\|\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t})-\mathbf{F}(\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\right)+2\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}\left(\frac{\tau\delta}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\tau\delta}\|\mathbf{F}(\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t})-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+2\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\eta_{t}\tau\delta\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\eta_{t}}{\tau\delta}\|\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t})-\mathbf{F}(\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\eta_{t}}{\tau\delta}\|\mathbf{F}(\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t})-\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+2\eta_{t}\alpha_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t})).

Applying the same bounds for the terms
(a),(c)
as before yields the lemma.
∎

This above lemma has two key features: a dependence on expected mean policy deviation
\Exop\left[e_{t}^{i}\right]
, and a term that scales as
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}})
.
While the
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}})
term can be anticipated (and asymptotically ignored when
N
is large) due to the finite-agent mean-field bias (as shown previously in Section
3
), the term
\Exop\left[e_{t}^{i}\right]
must be controlled separately in the independent learning setting, where policies cannot be synchronized through explicit communication between agents.
The term
\Exop\left[e_{t}^{i}\right]
reflects the core difference of the SMFG model from typical VI stochastic oracles.
Unlike typical VI oracle models, in SMFG the operator
\mathbf{F}
cannot be evaluated at the current iterate
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}
of a player
i
but only approximately at the mean
\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t}
.
This is due to decentralized learning: players can only evaluate the current payoffs at the “mean-iterate” given by
\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t})\approx\mathbf{F}(\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t})
(up to some stochastic noise) that is almost surely different than their iterates
\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}\}_{i}
apart from the case with degenerate/zero noise.
Furthermore, Lemma
12
suggests that the algorithmic scheme must guarantee that
\Exop\left[e_{t}^{i}\right]
decays with the rate at least
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{t}})
to obtain a non-vacuous bound on exploitability.
Taking inspiration from algorithmic stability literature
(
Ahn et al., 2022
;
Zhang et al., 2024b
)
, we utilize a regularization scheme to ensure the iterates of players do not diverge.
The following lemma shows that by introducing explicit regularization
\tau>0
, the expected mean policy deviation can be controlled throughout training.

Lemma 13

(Policy variations bound)
.

Under TRPA-Full with learning rates
\eta_{t}:=\frac{\tau^{-1}}{t+2}
, we have
\Exop\left[e_{t}^{i}\right]\leq\frac{14\tau^{-2}K\sigma^{2}+14}{t+2}
.

Proof.

Note that for any
i,j\in\mathcal{N}
such that
i\neq j
, using the non-expansiveness of the projection operator it holds that

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}=

\displaystyle\|\Pi_{\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}((1-\tau\eta_{t})\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}+\eta_{t}\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i})-\Pi_{\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}((1-\tau\eta_{t})\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t}+\eta_{t}\mathbf{r}_{t}^{j})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|(1-\tau\eta_{t})\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}+\eta_{t}\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-(1-\tau\eta_{t})\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t}-\eta_{t}\mathbf{r}_{t}^{j}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|(1-\tau\eta_{t})(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t})+\eta_{t}(\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{r}_{t}^{j})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\tau\eta_{t})^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\eta_{t}^{2}\|\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{r}_{t}^{j}\|_{2}^{2}+2(1-\tau\eta_{t})\eta_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t})^{\top}(\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{r}_{t}^{j})

Taking the conditional expectation on both sides, we obtain

\displaystyle\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\leq

\displaystyle(1-\tau\eta_{t})^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\Exop\left[\eta_{t}^{2}\|\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{r}_{t}^{j}\|_{2}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle+2(1-\tau\eta_{t})\eta_{t}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t})^{\top}\Exop\left[\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}-\mathbf{r}_{t}^{j}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\tau\eta_{t})^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+\eta_{t}^{2}\Exop\left[\|\mathbf{n}^{i}_{t}-\mathbf{n}^{j}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\tau\eta_{t})^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+2\eta_{t}^{2}K\sigma^{2}

almost surely, since we have
\mathbf{r}_{t}^{i}:=\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{t})+\mathbf{n}^{i}_{t}
.
Then, taking the expectation on both sides,

\displaystyle\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle(1-\tau\eta_{t})^{2}\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+2\eta_{t}^{2}K\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{1}{t+2}\right)^{2}\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\left(\frac{\tau^{-1}}{t+2}\right)^{2}2K\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{2}{t+2}\right)\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{(t+2)^{2}}\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\frac{2\tau^{-2}K\sigma^{2}}{(t+2)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{2}{t+2}\right)\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\frac{2\tau^{-2}K\sigma^{2}+2}{(t+2)^{2}}

To bound the recurrence, we can use the recurrence lemma (Lemma
26
, noting
\gamma=2,a=2,u_{0}=0,c_{0}=0,c_{1}=2\tau^{-2}K\sigma^{2}+2
in its statement):

\displaystyle\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t+1}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle 5\frac{2\tau^{-2}K\sigma^{2}+2}{(t+2)^{2}}+3\frac{2\tau^{-2}K\sigma^{2}+2}{t+2}+\frac{2\tau^{-2}K\sigma^{2}+2}{(t+2)^{2}}\leq\frac{14\tau^{-2}K\sigma^{2}+14}{t+2}.

Then, the expected values of
e_{t}^{i}
can be bounded using:

\displaystyle e_{t}^{i}=

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}-\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}=\left\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}

by an application of Jensen’s inequality.
Then we have
\Exop\left[e_{t}^{i}\right]\leq\frac{14\tau^{-2}K\sigma^{2}+14}{t+2}
.
∎

With an explicit bound in expectation on the mean policy deviation
e_{t}^{i}
, we can now proceed to the main recurrence for the expected error terms
u_{t}^{i}
in order to prove our main convergence result.
We state our main convergence result for TRPA-Full dynamics in Theorem
14
by solving these two recurrences for the monotone and strongly monotone cases.

Theorem 14

(Convergence, full feedback)
.

Assume
\mathbf{F}
is Lipschitz, monotone.
Assume
N
agents run the TRPA-Full update rule for
T
time steps with learning rates
\eta_{t}:=\frac{\tau^{-1}}{t+2}
and arbitrary regularization
\tau>0
.
Then it holds for any
i\in[N]
that
\Exop\left[\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{T}\}_{j=1}^{N})\right]\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{\tau^{-2}}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\tau^{-1}}{\sqrt{N}}+\tau)
.
Furthermore, if
\mathbf{F}
is
\lambda
-strongly monotone, then
\Exop\left[\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{T}\}_{j=1}^{N})\right]\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{\tau^{-\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}\lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\tau^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\sqrt{N}}+\tau)
.

Proof.

Note that the exploitability in the main statement of the theorem can be related to
u_{t}^{i}
as follows using Lemma
4
:

\displaystyle\Exop[\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{t}\}_{j=1}^{N})]\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{N})+\sqrt{K}\Exop[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}]+\frac{4L\sqrt{2K}}{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\Exop[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{j}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{N})+\sqrt{K}\sqrt{u_{t}^{i}}+\frac{4L\sqrt{2K}}{N}\sum_{j\neq i}\sqrt{u_{t}^{j}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{N})+\frac{\max\{\sqrt{K},4L\sqrt{2K}\}}{N}\sum_{j}\sqrt{u_{t}^{j}}

Hence the bounds on
u_{t}^{j}
will yield the result of the theorem by linearity of expectation, along with an invocation of Theorem
10
.

Finally, we solve the recurrences for
\lambda=0
and
\lambda>0
using Lemma
12
.
For the case
\lambda>0
, if
\eta_{t}=\frac{\tau^{-1}}{t+2}
, Lemma
12
provides the bound

\displaystyle u_{t+1}^{i}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{3\tau^{-2}K(1+\sigma^{2})+2\tau^{-2}(L+\tau)^{2}}{(t+2)^{2}}+\frac{4\tau^{-1}L^{2}\lambda^{-1}}{N(t+2)}+\frac{2\tau^{-1}L^{2}\lambda^{-1}}{t+2}\Exop\left[e_{t}^{i}\right]

\displaystyle+\left(1-\frac{2\tau^{-1}(\nicefrac{{\lambda}}{{2}}+\tau)}{t+2}\right)u_{t}^{i}.

By placing
\Exop\left[e^{i}_{t}\right]\leq\frac{14\tau^{-2}K\sigma^{2}+14}{t+2}
due to Lemma
13
, we obtain

\displaystyle u_{t+1}^{i}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{3\tau^{-2}K(1+\sigma^{2})+2\tau^{-2}(L+\tau)^{2}+28\tau^{-3}K\sigma^{2}\lambda^{-1}L^{2}+28\tau^{-1}L^{2}\lambda^{-1}}{(t+2)^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{4\tau^{-1}L^{2}\lambda^{-1}}{N(t+2)}+\left(1-\frac{2}{t+2}\right)u_{t}^{i}.

Invoking a generic recurrence lemma (Lemma
26
in Appendix
D
) leads to the main statement of the theorem.

For the monotone case
\lambda=0
, we have the recursion:

\displaystyle u_{t+1}^{i}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{3\tau^{-2}K(1+\sigma^{2})+2\tau^{-2}(L+\tau)^{2}}{(t+2)^{2}}+\frac{4\tau^{-2}L^{2}\delta^{-1}}{N(t+2)}+\frac{\tau^{-2}L^{2}\delta^{-1}}{t+2}\Exop\left[e_{t}^{i}\right]

\displaystyle+\left(1-\frac{2(1-\delta)}{t+2}\right)u_{t}^{i}.

and once again placing the upper bound on expected policy deviation due to Lemma
13
,

\displaystyle u_{t+1}^{i}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{3\tau^{-2}K(1+\sigma^{2})+2\tau^{-2}(L+\tau)^{2}+28K\tau^{-4}L^{2}\delta^{-1}\sigma^{2}+28\tau^{-2}L^{2}\delta^{-1}}{(t+2)^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{2\tau^{-2}L^{2}\delta^{-1}}{N(t+2)}+\left(1-\frac{2(1-\delta)}{t+2}\right)u_{t}^{i}.

Another invocation of Lemma
26
concludes the proof, choosing
\delta=\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
.
∎

This convergence result is stated in terms of exploitability of the unregularized game, leading to an additional
\mathcal{O}(\tau)
term.
However, in many cases, the Nash equilibrium of the regularized game itself is of interest, in which case the upper bounds should read

\mathcal{O}(\frac{\tau^{-2}}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\tau^{-1}}{\sqrt{N}})

and

\mathcal{O}(\frac{\tau^{-\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}\lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\tau^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\sqrt{N}})

for the monotone and strongly monotone cases respectively.

In the choice of learning rate
\eta_{t}
above, no intrinsic problem parameter is assumed to be known.
Furthermore, due to (1) the regularization
\tau
and (2) a finite population, a non-vanishing exploitability of
\mathcal{O}(\tau+\nicefrac{{\tau^{-1}}}{{\sqrt{N}}})
will be induced in terms of the NE in the monotone case.
While Theorem
10
readily suggested a bias of order
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{N}}})
is fundamental, when learning is conducted with finitely many agents Theorem
14
shows this is amplified to
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{\tau^{-1}}}{{\sqrt{N}}})
.
Since for finite population SMFG, there will always be a non-vanishing exploitability in terms of NE due to the mean-field approximation, in practice
\tau
could be chosen to incorporate an acceptable bias level.
Alternatively, if the exact value of the number of players
N
is known by each agent, one could choose
\tau
optimally, to obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 15

(Optimal
\tau
, full feedback)
.

Assume the conditions of Theorem
14
.
For monotone
\mathbf{F}
, choosing regularization parameter
\tau=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt[4]{N}}}
yields

\Exop\left[\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{T}\}_{j=1}^{N})\right]\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{N}})
for any
i
.
For
\lambda
-strongly monotone
\mathbf{F}
, choosing
\tau=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt[3]{N}}}
yields
\Exop\left[\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}_{T}\}_{j=1}^{N})\right]\leq\mathcal{O}(\frac{\lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\sqrt{N}}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\sqrt[3]{N}})
.

Even though TRPA-Full solves the regularized (hence strongly monotone) problem, compared to the
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{T}})
rate in classical strongly monotone VI
(
Kotsalis et al., 2022
)
or strongly convex optimization
(
Rakhlin et al., 2011
)
,
our worse
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{T}}})
time dependence is due to independent learning.
Intuitively, additional time is required to ensure the policies of independent learners are sufficiently close when “collectively” evaluating
\mathbf{F}
.
The additional dependence of the time-vanishing term on
\sqrt{N}
is also a result of this fact.
Furthermore, when learning itself is performed by
N
agents, we note that the bias as a function of
N
decreases with
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt[4]{N}}})
(or
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt[3]{N}}})
for strongly monotone problems), and not with
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{N}}})
as Theorem
9
might suggest.
We leave the question of whether this gap can be improved and whether knowledge of
N
is required in Corollary
15
, as future work.

5
Convergence in the Bandit Feedback Case

We now move on to the more challenging and realistic bandit feedback case, where agents can only observe the payoffs of the actions they have chosen.
Once again, we analyze the IL setting (or in bandits terminology, the “no communications” setting) where agents can not interact or coordinate with each other.
One of the main challenges of bandit feedback with IL in our setting is that it is difficult for each agent to identify itself (i.e., assign itself a unique number between
1,\ldots,N
) so that exploration of action payoffs can be performed in turns.
For instance, in MMAB algorithms, this is typically achieved using variants of the so-called musical chairs algorithm
(
Lugosi and Mehrabian, 2022
)
, which is not available in our formulation.
Instead, we adopt a
probabilistic
exploration scheme where each agent probabilistically decides it is its turn to explore payoffs while the rest of the agents induce the required empirical population distribution on which
\mathbf{F}
should be evaluated.

Our algorithm, which we call TRPA-Bandit, is straightforward and relies on exploration occurring over epochs, where policies are updated once in between epochs using the estimate of action payoffs constructed during the exploration phase.
We use the subscript
h
to index epochs, which consist of
T_{h}
repeated plays indexed by
(h,t)
for
t=1,\ldots,T_{h}
.
While we formally presented TRPA-Bandit (Algorithm
2
), the procedure informally is as follows for each agent, fixing an exploration parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and an agent
i\in\mathcal{N}
:

1.

At each epoch
h
, for
T_{h}>0
time steps, repeat the following:

(a)

With probability
\varepsilon
, sample uniformly an action
a^{i}_{h,t}
, observe the payoff
r^{i}_{h,t}
, and keep the importance sampling estimate
\widehat{\mathbf{r}}^{i}_{h}\leftarrow Kr_{h,t}^{i}\mathbf{e}_{a^{i}_{h,t}}
.

(b)

Otherwise (with probability
1-\varepsilon
), sample action according to current policy
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{h}
.

2.

Update the policy using TRPA,
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{h+1}=\Pi_{\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}}((1-\tau\eta_{h})\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{h}+\eta_{h}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}^{i}_{h})
.
If the agent did not explore this epoch, use
\widehat{\mathbf{r}}^{i}_{h}=\mathbf{0}
.

Intuitively, the probabilistic sampling scheme allows some agents to build a low-variance estimate of
\mathbf{F}
, while others simply sample actions with their current policy in order to induce the empirical population distribution at which
\mathbf{F}
should be evaluated.

Similar to the full feedback setting, we introduce useful notation used throughout this chapter.
We define the sigma algebra
\mathcal{F}_{h}:=\mathcal{F}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{h^{\prime}}^{i}\}_{h^{\prime}=0,\ldots,h}^{i=1,\ldots,N})
.
Adapting the notation from Section
1.1
to the case with multiple epochs, we use

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{h,t}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{e}_{a_{h,t}^{i}},\qquad\mathbf{r}^{i}_{h,t}:=\mathbf{F}(\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{h,t})+\mathbf{n}_{h,t}^{i},

where the updated time indices
h,t
simply refer to the
t
-th round of play in epoch
h
, and
a_{h,t}^{i}
is the action played by player
i
at epoch
h
, round
t
.
Under the dynamics of Algorithm
2
, we define the following random variables to assist our proofs:

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{h}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{h}^{i},\qquad e_{t}^{i}:=\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{h}-\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{h}\|_{2}^{2},\qquad u_{h}^{i}:=\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{h}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\right],

which correspond to the mean policy at epoch
h
, the policy deviation of agent
i
from the mean at epoch
h
and the
\ell_{2}
distance from the MF-NE.
Note that since policies are updated only in between epochs, the above quantities are indexed by epochs
h
rather than rounds
h,t
.

Our analysis follows the ideas in the case of expert feedback, the main difference being randomization due to the exploration probabilities and the errors being analyzed per epoch rather than per round.
Similar to the full feedback setting, we will proceed in several steps expressed as intermediate lemmas:
(1) we bound the added bias and variance due to the importance sampling strategy,
(2) we obtain a non-linear recursion for the expectation of the terms
e_{t}^{i}
and possible sampling bias,
(3) we bound the expected differences of each agent’s action probabilities
e_{t}^{i}
, showing the deviation of the policies of the agents goes to zero in expectation,
(4) we solve the recursion to obtain the convergence rate.

The next result, Lemma
16
, provides an answer to the first step.
We show that despite the probabilistic exploration step, the estimates
\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{h}^{i}
in Algorithm
2
have low bias and variance.

Lemma 16

(Exploration bias)
.

Under the dynamics of TRPA-Bandit, it holds almost surely for each epoch
h\geq 0
that

\displaystyle\|\Exop[\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{h}^{i}|\mathcal{F}_{h}]-\mathbf{F}(\varepsilon\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{1}+(1-\varepsilon)\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{h})\|_{2}\leq K^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}}\exp\left\{-\varepsilon T_{h}\right\}+\frac{2L}{N}+\frac{2L}{\sqrt{N}}.

The full proof has been postponed to Appendix
E.4
.
In summary, the proof strategy is to decompose and analyze the bias due to the possibility of no exploration round happening (the term
K^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}}\exp\left\{-\varepsilon T_{h}\right\}
), the impact of the exploring agent on payoffs (the term
\frac{2L}{N}
), and bias due to the finitely many agents, similar to Theorem
9
(the term
\frac{2L}{\sqrt{N}}
).
The additional bias due to probabilistic exploration originates from the possibility that no exploration occurs in a given epoch: the probability of this event can be bounded by
\exp\left\{-\varepsilon T_{h}\right\}
.

Lemma
16
shows that even if the players do not have full feedback, they can obtain low-bias, low-variance estimates of
\mathbf{F}(\varepsilon\frac{1}{K}\mathbf{1}+(1-\varepsilon)\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{h})\approx\mathbf{F}(\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{h})
when
\varepsilon
is small.
It guarantees that even if the agents do not explore each epoch, in expectation the probabilistic exploration scheme yields a low bias if the epoch lengths
T_{h}
are logarithmically large: hence, full feedback can be simulated by paying a logarithmic cost.
Therefore, in our epoched exploration scheme, the bias in “querying” the payoff operator
\mathbf{F}
due to an exploring population can be controlled by tuning
\varepsilon
and
T_{h}
.

We next state the error recursion in Lemma
17
, which uses the result of Lemma
16
to construct the main recurrence for the bandit feedback case.

Lemma 17

(Main recurrence for TRPA-Bandit)
.

Under TRPA-Bandit dynamics, it holds for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and each epoch
h\geq 0
that

\displaystyle\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{h+1}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle 4\eta_{h}^{2}K^{3}(1+\sigma^{2})+8\eta_{h}^{2}(L+\tau)^{2}+8K^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}\eta_{h}\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}}\exp\{-\varepsilon T_{h}\}

\displaystyle+128\eta_{h}\lambda^{-1}L^{2}N^{-1}+16\eta_{h}\lambda^{-1}L^{2}\varepsilon^{2}+2\eta_{h}\lambda^{-1}L^{2}\Exop\left[e_{h}^{i}\right]

\displaystyle+\left(1-2\eta_{h}(\nicefrac{{\lambda}}{{2}}+\tau)\right)\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{h}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\right],

for strongly monotone
\lambda>0
payoffs, and

\displaystyle\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{h+1}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq

\displaystyle 4\eta_{h}^{2}K^{3}(\sigma^{2}+1)+8\eta_{h}^{2}(L+\tau)^{2}+8K^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}\eta_{h}\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}}\exp\{-\varepsilon T_{h}\}

\displaystyle+64\tau^{-1}\eta_{h}\delta^{-1}L^{2}N^{-1}+8\tau^{-1}\eta_{h}\delta^{-1}L^{2}\varepsilon^{2}+\tau^{-1}\eta_{h}\delta^{-1}L^{2}\Exop\left[e_{h}^{i}\right]

\displaystyle+\left(1-2\tau\eta_{h}(1-\delta)\right)\Exop\left[\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{h}^{i}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\right],

for monotone payoffs for arbitrary
\delta\in(0,1)
.

Once again, the full proof has been postponed to Appendix
E.5
.
The proof of Lemma
17
follows a similar path as in the recurrence in the full feedback case (Lemma
12
), with the exception that
\mathbf{r}_{h,t}^{i}
has been replaced by the importance sampling estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{r}}^{i}_{h}
.
In the decomposition due to Inequality (
4
), the analysis of term (a) remains the same, whereas the terms (b), (c) must be further analyzed using Lemma
16
to account for deviations between
\mathbf{r}_{h,t}^{i}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{r}}^{i}_{h}
, as well as the
\varepsilon
fraction of the population now exploring each round.

The recurrence in Lemma
17
is similar in form to the full feedback case (Lemma
12
), apart from the term
8K^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}\eta_{h}\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}}\exp\{-\varepsilon T_{h}\}
due to the exploration scheme.
However, keeping the exploration epoch lengths
T_{h}
logarithmically large can make this term small.
Furthermore, once again the recursion produces a dependence on expected mean policy deviations,
\Exop[e_{h}^{i}]
.
Hence, the expected policy deviation
\Exop[e_{h}^{i}]
at epoch
h
must be bounded once again at a rate
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{h}})
in order to obtain a non-vacuous upper bound on exploitability.
As in the full feedback case, we employ regularization to ensure
\Exop[e_{h}^{i}]
is small.
The next lemma presents our upper bound.

Lemma 18

(Policy deviation under TRPA-Bandit)
.

Under TRPA-Bandit dynamics, with learning rates
\eta_{h}:=\frac{\tau^{-1}}{h+2}
, arbitrary exploration rate
\varepsilon>0
and epoch lengths
T_{h}:=\lceil\varepsilon^{-1}\log(h+2)\rceil

it holds for any
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,N\},i\neq j
and each epoch
h\geq 0
that

\displaystyle\Exop[e_{h}^{i}]\leq\frac{24\tau^{-2}K^{3}(\sigma^{2}+2)+48\tau^{-2}+24}{h+1}+\frac{16\tau^{-2}L^{2}}{N^{2}}.

The proof of Lemma
18
follows similar ideas to Lemma
13
, while accounting for (a) the increased variance due to importance sampling, and (b) potential further deviation between agent policies due to the
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}})
impact of exploration on
\widehat{\bm{\mu}}_{h,t}
.
In particular, an additional source of policy deviation occurs when an agent does not explore in a given epoch, in which case the payoff estimator is uninformative (
\widehat{\mathbf{r}}_{h}^{i}=\mathbf{0}
) causing additional policy deviation.
The full proof has been postponed to Appendix
E.6
.

In the case of TRPA-Bandit, due to the additional variance of probabilistic exploration, the policy deviations between agents might be larger: compare the upper bounds of Lemma
18
and Lemma
13
.
In particular, the upper bound of Lemma
18
contains a non-vanishing term unlike Lemma
13
.
Nevertheless, they can still be controlled of order
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{(h+1)}}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{N^{2}}})
, where the additional
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{N^{2}}})
term compared to TRPA-Full vanishes very quickly when
N
is large.

With these intermediate lemmas established, we state and prove the main convergence result for TRPA-Bandit Theorem
19
, the main result of this work.
We provide asymptotic rates for brevity, although the proof of the theorem provides explicit bounds.

Theorem 19

(Convergence, bandit feedback)
.

Assume
\mathbf{F}
is Lipschitz, monotone.
Assume
N
agents run TRPA-Bandit (Algorithm
2
) for
T
time steps with regularization
\tau>0
and exploration parameter
\varepsilon>0
, and agents return policies
\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}\}_{i}
after executing Algorithm
2
.
Then, for any agent
i\in\mathcal{N}
that
\Exop\left[\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})\right]\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{\tau^{-2}\varepsilon^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\sqrt{T}}+\tau^{-1}\varepsilon+\tau+\frac{\tau^{-1}}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{\tau^{-\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}}{N})
.
If
\mathbf{F}
is
\lambda
-strongly monotone, then
\Exop\left[\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})\right]\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{\tau^{-\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}\lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\varepsilon^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\sqrt{T}}+\tau^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\varepsilon+\tau+\frac{\tau^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{\tau^{-1}\lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{N})
.

The proof follows from a straightforward combination of Lemmas
16
,
17
, and
18
as in the full feedback case.
The exact bounds and proof are in the appendix, Section
E.7
.

Once again, the values of
\tau
and exploration probability
\varepsilon
can be chosen to incorporate tolerable exploitability.
In the case where the number of participants
N
in the game is known, the following corollary indicates the asymptotically optimal choices for the hyperparameters.

Corollary 20

(Optimal
\varepsilon,\tau
, bandit feedback)
.

Assume the conditions of Theorem
19
for
N
agents running TRPA-Bandit.
For monotone
\mathbf{F}
, choosing
\tau=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt[4]{N}}}
and
\varepsilon=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{N}}}
yields

\Exop\left[\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})\right]\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{N^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}}}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{N}})
for any
i
.
For strongly monotone
\mathbf{F}
, choosing
\tau=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt[3]{N}}}
and
\varepsilon=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{N}}}
yields
\Exop\left[\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})\right]\leq\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\frac{N^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}}\lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\sqrt{T}}+\frac{\lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{\sqrt[3]{N}})
.

The dependence of
N
of the sample complexity in the bandit case is worse compared to the full feedback setting as expected: intuitively the agents must take turns to estimate the payoffs of each action in bandit feedback.
Furthermore, while our problem framework is different and a direct comparison is difficult in terms of bounds, we point out that classical MMAB results such as
(
Lugosi and Mehrabian, 2022
)
have a linear dependence on
N
, while in our case the dependence on
N
scales with
N^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{4}}}
.
We emphasize that the time-dependence is sublinear in terms of
N
, up to the non-vanishing finite population bias.
As in the full feedback case, the non-vanishing finite population bias in the bandit feedback case scales with
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt[4]{N}}})
or
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt[3]{N}}})
, rather than
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{N}}})
which would match Theorem
9
.
Note that the dependence of the bias on
N
varies in various mean-field game results
(
Saldi et al., 2019
)
, but asymptotically is known to converge to zero as
N\rightarrow\infty
, as our explicit finite-agent bound also demonstrates.

Finally, we note that as expected the algorithm for bandit feedback has a worse dependency on the number of actions.
This is as expected due to the fact that (i) the importance sampling estimator increases variance on payoff estimators by a factor of
K
, and (2) in other words, a factor of
\mathcal{O}(K)
is introduced in order to explore all actions.

Experimental Results

We validate the theoretical results of our work on numerical and two real-world experiments.
The details of the setup are presented in greater detail in the appendix (Section
F
) and the code is provided in supplementary materials.
Our experiments assume bandit feedback, although in the appendix we also include results under the full feedback model.
We provide an overview of our setup first.

Numerical problems.

Firstly, we formulate three numerical problems, which are based on examples suggested in Sections
2
.
We randomly generate monotone payoff operators that are linear (
Linear
) and a payoff function that admits a KL divergence potential (
kl
).
We also analyze a particular “beach bar process” (
bb
), a stateless version of the example presented in
(
Perrin et al., 2020
)
.
For numerical examples, we use
K=5
, and vary between various population sizes in
N=\{20,50,100,200,500,1000\}
to quantify the effect of finite
N
and compare with theoretical bias bounds.
We set the parameters
\varepsilon,\tau
using the theoretical values from Corollary
20
.

Traffic flow simulation.

Using the UTD19 dataset
(
Loder et al., 2019
)
, we evaluate our algorithms on traffic congestion data on three different routes for accessing the city center of Zurich (
utd
).
The UTD19 dataset features many closed loop sensors across various urban roads in Zurich, providing granular measurements of road occupancy and traffic flow.
We use these real-world stationary detector measurements to approximate traveling times as a function of route occupancy with a kernelized regression model on three routes.
We then evaluate our algorithms on estimated traveling times.
The real-world data from the UTD19 dataset suggests the travel duration (and therefore the payoffs) are in fact non-increasing in the occupancy of each segment, and hence can be reasonably modeled by a monotone payoff.
Further empirical evaluation of monotonicity is discussed in Appendix
F.5
.

Access to the Tor network.

We also run experiments on accessing the Tor network, which is a large decentralized network for secure communications and an active area of research in computer security
(
Jansen et al., 2014
;
McCoy et al., 2008
)
.
The Tor network consists of many decentralized servers and access to the network is achieved by communicating with one of many entry guard servers (a list advertised publicly
(
The Tor Network,
)
, some hosted by universities).
As the entry guards serve a wide public of users and each user is free to choose an entry point, the network is an ideal setting to test our algorithms.
We simulate 100 independent agents accessing Tor by choosing among
K=5
entry servers every minute and use the real-world ping delays for bandit feedback.
Our empirical evaluations show that monotonicity is also a reasonable assumption for the Tor network experiment due to the fact that response times tend to be non-decreasing in occupation: we provide an empirical argument in Appendix
F.6
.

Overall, our experiments in both models and real-world use cases support our theory.
Firstly, our experiments verify learning in the sense of decreasing maximum exploitability
\max_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\mathcal{E}^{i}_{\text{exp}}(\{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})
and mean distance to MF-NE given by
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}_{t}\|_{2}
during IL (Figure
1
-b,c).
As expected from Corollary
20
, the maximum exploitability oscillates but remains bounded (due to the effect of finite
N
).
Furthermore, the agents converge to policies closer to the MF-NE as
N
increases (Figure
1
-c), empirically demonstrating that the MF-NE is an accurate description of the limiting behaviour of SMFG as
N\rightarrow\infty
.
The scale of the excess exploitability in our experiments decreases rapidly in
N
, allowing our results to be significant even for hundreds or thousands of agents.

7
Discussion and Conclusion

In this work, we proposed a finite-agent, independent learning algorithm for learning SMFGs under bandit feedback, and established finite sample guarantees. Our results hinge on the variational inequality perspective of the mean-field game limit, demonstrating that efficient independent learning is achievable.

As the first study on decentralized learning on SMFGs, our results are based on the standard (strong) monotonicity assumption on VIs.
However, research on VIs has identified several more general yet still tractable conditions that might be more applicable to real-world SMFGs, such as the weak Minty condition
(
Diakonikolas et al., 2021
)
, the variationally stable condition
(
Mertikopoulos and Zhou, 2019
)
, pseudo-monotone operators
(
Karamardian, 1976
)
, and generalized monotonicity
(
Kotsalis et al., 2022
)
.
More abstractly, an intriguing open question is whether the existence of efficient algorithms for solving the (possibly non-monotone) MF-VI always implies the existence of decentralized algorithms for learning SMFG.
At present, several challenges remain when generalizing our results beyond monotonicity: firstly, our current method requires the expected policy deviation to converge to zero, restricting the algorithmic tools that can be deployed.
Moreover, our proof strategy relies on the convergence of iterates
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}
to the unique regularized solution
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
due to the contractivity of the regularized ascent operator
\Gamma^{\eta,\tau}
. Yet for more general VIs, obtaining such a contractive operator in the
\ell_{2}
-norm might be difficult.

From a game-theoretic and learning perspective, another relevant question is whether our VI approach to decentralized learning can be extended to Markovian MFGs.
The current theory does not immediately suggest our analysis could be extended to Markovian games.
Specifically, it is not clear if the Markovian Nash equilibrium would still be characterized compactly as a VI, or when the corresponding operator would be monotone.
The question of IL in stationary MFGs has been tackled by
Yardim et al. (2023)
but with poor sample complexity; future work could address this problem using more general VIs.
Finally, while our results focus on expected exploitability, they could potentially be generalized to guarantee convergence to low exploitability policies with high probability.

Appendix B
A Detailed Comparison to the Setting in
Gummadi et al. (2013)

Since specific keywords seem to correspond to the works on mean-field approximations with bandits, we provide a greater discussion of our setting and the results by
Gummadi et al. (2013)
.
In general, our settings and models are very different, hence almost none of the results between our work and Gummadi et al. are transferable to the other.
Our problem formulation, analysis, and results are fundamentally different from their setting due to the following points.

Stationary equilibrium vs Nash equilibrium.

The most critical difference between the two works is the solution concepts.
Our setting is competitive, as a natural extension, the solution concept is that of a Nash equilibrium where each agent has no incentive to change their policy.
On the other hand, the setting of Gummadi et al. need not be competitive or collaborative and this distinction is not significant for their framework, their goal is to characterize convergence of the population to a stationary distribution.
Their main results show that if a particular policy map
\sigma:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2n}\rightarrow\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
is prescribed on agents, the population distribution will converge to a steady state.
The equilibrium concept of
Gummadi et al. (2013)
is not
Nash
, rather stationarity.

Optimality considerations.

As a consequence of analyzing stationarity, the results by
(
Gummadi et al., 2013
)
do not analyze or aim to characterize optimality.
In their analysis, a fixed map
\sigma:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2n}\rightarrow\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
is assumed to be the policy/strategy of a continuum of (i.e., infinitely many) agents, which maps observed loss/win counts (from Bernoulli distributed arm rewards) to arm probabilities.
The stationary distribution in general obtained from
\sigma
in
Gummadi et al. (2013)
does not have optimality properties, for instance, a fixed agent will can have arbitrary large exploitability.
The main goal of
Gummadi et al. (2013)
is to prove the convergence of the population distribution to a steady state behaviour.

Algorithms.

As a consequence of the previous points, Gummadi et al. abstract away any algorithmic considerations to the fixed map
\sigma
and the particular algorithms employed by agents do not directly have significance in terms of their theoretical conclusions.
Since we analyze optimality in our setting, we require a specific algorithm to be employed (TRPA and Algorithm
2
).

Independent learning.

In our setting, the notion of learning and independent learning become significant since we are aiming to obtain an approximate NE.
Hence, our theoretical results bound the expected exploitability (Theorems
14
,
19
) in terms of number of samples.
In the work of
Gummadi et al. (2013)
, the main aim is convergence to a steady state rather than learning.

Population regeneration.

Finally, to be able to obtain a contractive mapping yielding a population stationary distribution/steady state,
Gummadi et al. (2013)
assume that the population regenerates at a constant rate
\beta
, implying agents are constantly being replaced by oblivious agents that have not observed the game dynamics.
This smooths the dynamics by introducing a forgetting mechanism to game participants.
Our results on the other hand are closer to the traditional bandits/independent learning setting.
For instance, this would correspond to non-vanishing exploitability scaling with
\mathcal{O}(\beta)
in our system as agents constantly “forget” what they learned.

Other model differences.

In our setting, we assume general noisy rewards while in
Gummadi et al. (2013)
, the rewards are Bernoulli random variables with success probability dependent on the population.

that maps past
t-1
observations from previous rounds to a mixed strategy on actions
\mathcal{A}
at time
t>0
for each agent
i
.

(Rational learning algorithm with full feedback)
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be an algorithm with full feedback as defined in Definition
21
.
We call
\mathbf{A}

\delta

i
, the induced mixed strategies
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{i}

In our proofs, we will need to repeatedly bound certain recurrences and sums.

Lemma 25

(Harmonic partial sum bound)
.

For any integers
s,\bar{s}
, constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
1\leq\bar{s}<s
,
p\neq-1
, and
a\geq 0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\log(s+a)-\log(\bar{s}+a)+\frac{1}{s+a}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=\bar{s}}^{s}\frac{1}{n+a}\leq\frac{1}{\bar{s}+a}+\log(s+a)-\log(\bar{s}+a),

\displaystyle\frac{(s+a)^{p+1}}{p+1}-\frac{(\bar{s}+a)^{p+1}}{(p+1)}+(\bar{s}+a)^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=\bar{s}}^{s}(n+a)^{p}\leq\frac{(s+a)^{p+1}}{p+1}-\frac{(\bar{s}+a)^{p+1}}{p+1}+(s+a)^{p},\text{ if }p\geq 0

\displaystyle\frac{(s+a)^{p+1}}{p+1}-\frac{(\bar{s}+a)^{p+1}}{p+1}+(s+a)^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=\bar{s}}^{s}(n+a)^{p}\leq\frac{(s+a)^{p+1}}{p+1}-\frac{(\bar{s}+a)^{p+1}}{p+1}+(\bar{s}+a)^{p},\text{ if }p\leq 0

Proof.

Let
f_{1}:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a non-decreasing positive function and
f_{2}:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a non-increasing positive function.
Then it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{x=\bar{s}}^{s}f_{1}(x)dx+f_{1}(\bar{s})\leq\sum_{n=\bar{s}}^{s}f_{1}(n)\leq\int_{x=\bar{s}}^{s}f_{1}(x)dx+f_{1}(s),

\displaystyle\int_{x=\bar{s}}^{s}f_{2}(x)dx+f_{2}(s)\leq\sum_{n=\bar{s}}^{s}f_{2}(n)\leq\int_{x=\bar{s}}^{s}f_{2}(x)dx+f_{2}(\bar{s}).

The result follows from a simple integral bound with
\int\frac{1}{x}dx=\log x
and
\int x^{p}dx=\frac{x^{p+1}}{p+1}
.

Lemma 26

(General error recurrence)
.

Let
c_{0}\geq 0,c_{1}\geq 0,\gamma>1,a\geq 0
be arbitrary constants such that
a\geq\gamma
.
Furthermore, let
\{u_{t}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
be a sequence of non-negative numbers such that for all
t\geq 0
, it holds that

\displaystyle u_{t+1}\leq\frac{c_{0}}{t+a}+\frac{c_{1}}{(t+a)^{2}}+\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{t+a}\right)u_{t}.

Then, for all values of
t\geq 0
, it holds that:

\displaystyle u_{t+1}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{u_{0}a^{\gamma}+c_{1}(a^{\gamma-2}+1)(1+a^{-1})^{\gamma}+c_{0}(1+a^{-1})^{\gamma}a^{\gamma-1}}{\left(t+a\right)^{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{c_{0}+c_{1}(1+a^{-1})^{\gamma}(\gamma-1)^{-1}}{t+a}+\frac{c_{1}}{(t+a)^{2}}+\gamma^{-1}(1+a^{-1})^{\gamma}c_{0}

Proof.

We note that inductively, we have

\displaystyle u_{t+1}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{c_{0}}{t+a}+\frac{c_{1}}{(t+a)^{2}}+u_{0}\prod_{s=0}^{t}\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{s+a}\right)+\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\left(\frac{c_{0}}{s+a}+\frac{c_{1}}{(s+a)^{2}}\right)\prod_{s^{\prime}=s+1}^{t}\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{s^{\prime}+a}\right).

Using the inequality
1+x\leq e^{x}
, we obtain

\displaystyle u_{t+1}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{c_{0}}{t+a}+\frac{c_{1}}{(t+a)^{2}}+u_{0}\prod_{s=0}^{t}\exp\left\{-\frac{\gamma}{s+a}\right\}+\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\left(\frac{c_{0}}{s+a}+\frac{c_{1}}{(s+a)^{2}}\right)\prod_{s^{\prime}=s+1}^{t}\exp\left\{-\frac{\gamma}{s^{\prime}+a}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{c_{0}}{t+a}+\frac{c_{1}}{(t+a)^{2}}+u_{0}\exp\left\{-\sum_{s=0}^{t}\frac{\gamma}{s+a}\right\}+\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\left(\frac{c_{0}}{s+a}+\frac{c_{1}}{(s+a)^{2}}\right)\exp\left\{-\sum_{s^{\prime}=s+1}^{t}\frac{\gamma}{s^{\prime}+a}\right\}.

Here, using Lemma
25
, since
a-1>0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{t}\frac{\gamma}{s+a}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{s=1}^{t+1}\frac{\gamma}{s+(a-1)}\geq\gamma\log(t+a)-\gamma\log a=\log\left(\frac{(t+1)^{\gamma}}{a^{\gamma}}\right)

\displaystyle\sum_{s^{\prime}=s+1}^{t}\frac{\gamma}{s^{\prime}+a}

\displaystyle\geq\gamma\log(t+a)-\gamma\log(s+a+1)\geq\log\left\{\frac{(t+a)^{\gamma}}{(s+a+1)^{\gamma}}\right\},

therefore
u_{t+1}
can be further upper bounded by

\displaystyle u_{t+1}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{c_{0}}{t+a}+\frac{c_{1}}{(t+a)^{2}}+u_{0}\frac{a^{\gamma}}{\left(t+a\right)^{\gamma}}+\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\left(\frac{c_{0}}{s+a}+\frac{c_{1}}{(s+a)^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{s+a+1}{t+a}\right)^{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{c_{0}}{t+a}+\frac{c_{1}}{(t+a)^{2}}+\frac{u_{0}a^{\gamma}}{\left(t+a\right)^{\gamma}}+(t+a)^{-\gamma}\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\left(\frac{c_{0}}{s+a}+\frac{c_{1}}{(s+a)^{2}}\right)\left(s+a+1\right)^{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{c_{0}}{t+a}+\frac{c_{1}}{(t+a)^{2}}+\frac{u_{0}a^{\gamma}}{\left(t+a\right)^{\gamma}}+(t+a)^{-\gamma}(1+a^{-1})^{\gamma}\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\left(c_{0}\left(s+a\right)^{\gamma-1}+c_{1}\left(s+a\right)^{\gamma-2}\right).

The last term can be bound with the corresponding integral (see Lemma
25
), yielding (since
\gamma-1>0
):

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}(s+a)^{\gamma-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(t+a)^{\gamma}}{\gamma}+a^{\gamma-1}.

For the term
\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}(s+1)^{\gamma-2}
, we analyzing the two cases
1<\gamma\leq 2
and
\gamma>2
using Lemma
25
we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}(s+a)^{\gamma-2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(t+a)^{\gamma-1}}{\gamma-1}+a^{\gamma-2}+1.

The two inequalities combined yield the stated bound,

\displaystyle u_{t+1}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{c_{0}}{t+a}+\frac{c_{1}}{(t+a)^{2}}+\frac{u_{0}a^{\gamma}}{\left(t+a\right)^{\gamma}}+(t+a)^{-\gamma}(1+a^{-1})^{\gamma}\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\left(c_{0}(s+a)^{\gamma-1}+c_{1}(s+a)^{\gamma-2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{c_{0}}{t+a}+\frac{c_{1}}{(t+a)^{2}}+\frac{u_{0}a^{\gamma}}{\left(t+a\right)^{\gamma}}+\gamma^{-1}(1+a^{-1})^{\gamma}c_{0}+\frac{c_{0}(1+a^{-1})^{\gamma}a^{\gamma-1}}{(t+a)^{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{c_{1}(1+a^{-1})^{\gamma}}{(t+a)(\gamma-1)}+\frac{c_{1}(a^{\gamma-2}+1)(1+a^{-1})^{\gamma}}{(t+a)^{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{u_{0}a^{\gamma}+c_{1}(a^{\gamma-2}+1)(1+a^{-1})^{\gamma}+c_{0}(1+a^{-1})^{\gamma}a^{\gamma-1}}{\left(t+a\right)^{\gamma}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{c_{0}+c_{1}(1+a^{-1})^{\gamma}(\gamma-1)^{-1}}{t+a}+\frac{c_{1}}{(t+a)^{2}}+\gamma^{-1}(1+a^{-1})^{\gamma}c_{0}.

∎

F.1
Problem Generation Details

We provide further details on how we generate/simulate the SMFG problems.

Linear payoffs.

We generate a payoff map

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{lin}(\bm{\mu}):=(\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{X})\bm{\mu}+\mathbf{b}

for some
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{K\times K}
and
\mathbf{X}
anti-symmetric matrix, which makes
\mathbf{F}_{lin}
monotone.
We randomly sample
\mathbf{S}
from a Wishart distribution (which has support contained in positive definite matrices), generate
\mathbf{X}
by computing
\frac{\mathbf{U}-\mathbf{U}^{\top}}{2}
for a random matrix
\mathbf{U}
with entries sampled uniformly from
[0,1]
and
\mathbf{b}
having entries uniformly sampled from
[0,1]
.

Payoffs with KL potential.

Next, we construct the following payoff operator
\mathbf{F}_{KL}
for some reference distribution
\bm{\mu}_{\text{ref}}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
:

\displaystyle\Phi_{KL}(\bm{\mu})

\displaystyle:=\operatorname{D}_{KL}(\gamma\bm{\mu}+(1-\gamma)\bm{\mu}_{\text{ref}}||\bm{\mu}_{\text{ref}})

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{KL}(\bm{\mu})

\displaystyle:=\nabla\Phi_{KL}(\bm{\mu})

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{KL}(\bm{\mu},a)

\displaystyle=\gamma\log\left(\frac{\gamma\bm{\mu}(a)+(1-\gamma)\bm{\mu}_{\text{ref}}(a)}{\bm{\mu}_{\text{ref}}(a)}\right)+\gamma

Note that as
\Phi_{KL}
is convex,
\mathbf{F}_{KL}
is monotone.
In our experiments, we use
\gamma=0.1
, and we generate
\bm{\mu}_{\text{ref}}
by sampling
K
uniform random variables in
[0,1]
and normalizing.

Beach bar process.

Following the example given in
Perrin et al. (2020)
, we use the action set
\mathcal{A}=\{1,\ldots,K\}
for potential locations at the beach and assume a bar is located at
x_{bar}:=\lfloor\frac{K}{2}\rfloor
.
Taking into the proximity to the bar and the occupancy measure over actions (i.e., the crowdedness of locations at the beach), the payoff map is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{bb}(\bm{\mu},a)=1-\frac{|a-x_{bar}|}{K}-\alpha\log(1+\bm{\mu}(a)).

The above payoff map is monotone.
We use
\alpha=1
for our experiments.

F.5
Experiments on Traffic Congestion

UTD19 and closed-loop sensors.

The UTD19 dataset contains measurements by closed-loop sensors which report the fraction of the time a particular section of the route remains occupied (i.e., a car is located in between sensors placed on the sides).
The data consists of measurements every 5 minutes, from various sensors across 41 European cities.
The dataset contains 2 weeks of data collected by sensors placed around Zurich.

Payoff models.

We fit kernelized ridge regression models to model the flow as a function of occupancy using the UTD19 dataset.
We use an RBF kernel and a regularization of
\alpha=1.0
for all models.
The dependence of traffic speed on route occupancy in two sample paths in the dataset can be seen in Figure
8
-(a, b).
The measurements motivate the monotonicity assumption as both the original data and the fitted regression model, increased occupancy leads to increased travel time, at least, when ignoring interactions.
We compute a proxy for the travel velocity using the flow and occupancy measurements on each route, and a scaling factor
c_{\text{dist}}
due to varying lengths of each route, leading to the estimated travel time

\displaystyle T_{travel}

\displaystyle=c_{\text{dist}}\frac{\text{flow}}{\text{occupancy}}.

We use
-T_{travel}
as the reward for each agent.

F.6
Experiments on Network Access

For the Tor network access experiment, we randomly chose 5 entry guard servers (the complete list available publicly
The Tor Network ()
) in various geographic locations, among the servers that have the longest recorded uptime.
To simulate access to each server, we ping each node 5 consecutive times and average the delays to compute the cost.
As expected, due to varying bandwidths/computational power, each server has different sensitivities to load in terms of delay.
We plot measured ping times vs the number of agents simultaneously accessing a particular node in Figure
8
-(c).
The ping times were measured to be stochastic but clearly sensitive to occupancy, motivating a monotone payoff operator.

We use parameters
\tau=0.01,\varepsilon=0.3
for the experiments in this section.
The arbitrary choice is due to the fact that in the presence of external agents in the game that do not use TRPA-Bandit (in this case, thousands of other users accessing the Tor network), the theoretically optimal parameters implied by Corollary
20
can not be used.
While more realistic simulations that are also closer to the theory could be run by keeping
N
larger and simulating a Tor access rather than simple pings, we refrain from this in order to minimize the footprint of our experiments on the Tor network.
