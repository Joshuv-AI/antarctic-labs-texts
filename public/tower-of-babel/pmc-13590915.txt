Sex differences in cartilage anatomy, imaging & treatment response
Authors: Felix Eckstein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo review findings on sex differences in cartilage anatomy, cartilage imaging, and treatment in osteoarthritis, based on an invited presentation held at the International Workshop of Osteoarthritis Imaging (IWOAI) 2025.DesignA non-systematic literature search (Pubmed) and consultations with experts. Additionally, comparative radiographic joint space width (JSW) and femorotibial cartilage thickness data were obtained from 2396 knees (from 2004 male and female Osteoarthritis Initiative participants), covering radiographic joint space narrowing (JSN) grades 0–3. Sex differences in JSW and cartilage morphology were related to those of other articular and periarticular tissues in qualitative form.ResultsMen displayed greater JSW and femorotibial cartilage thickness than women. For JSW, but not for cartilage thickness, sex differences disappeared with greater joint space narrowing (JSN) grade. The sex difference in cartilage thickness was maintained after adjusting for relevant covariates, e.g. body height. These morphological findings differ from those in cartilage transverse relaxation time (T2) and from other (peri‑) articular tissues. Preliminary findings suggest that men may respond more strongly to surgical and anabolic pharmacological cartilage therapy.ConclusionsBetween and within species, large differences exist in the heterogeneity of morphological traits. Sexual dimorphism can be male- or female biased in different species; within humans some musculoskeletal characteristics are greater in men and others in women. Men display “genuinely” greater JSW, cartilage thickness, and joint surface areas than women, the latter potentially contributing to a lower joint pressure and lower incidence in knee osteoarthritis than in women. Sex-differences in cartilage treatment response deserve deeper exploration.

Introduction

A review of sex differences in anatomical structures should begin with a principal consideration of inter-subject heterogeneity. Traits associated with male and female sex often appear as two clusters within the observed spectrum, explaining a portion of this heterogeneity, depending on the specific parameter examined. Contrary to common perception, however, the human species is remarkably homogeneous.

Dogs, in contrast, are a species that displays enormous variability, for instance in weight (>150-fold), with the heaviest breed being the English Mastiff (≈150 kg) and amongst the lightest the Chihuahua (<1 kg). This also applies to body shape of dogs: the average body mass index (BMI) ranges from ≈45 kg/m² in Greyhounds to >110 kg/m² in Bulldogs and Pugs. Dogs also exhibit variable lifetime risks of incident hip osteoarthritis (OA), from 2 to 4 % in Greyhounds to >70 % in breeds such as German Shepherds, Retrievers, and others. Interestingly, the risk of hip OA is not correlated with body weight or BMI, suggesting that genetic factors and joint conformation play major roles.

A recent publication in Science [1] reported that humans experienced an extreme evolutionary bottleneck during the Mid-Pleistocene (≈800,000–900,000 years ago). The effective breeding population of Homo sapiens dropped to only ≈1300 individuals, who survived for ≈117,000 years under the constant threat of extinction. This prolonged reduction led to a substantial loss of genetic diversity. The bottleneck may have been driven by climatic fluctuations, volcanic activity, and other ecological pressures, which limited resources and habitable regions. The persistence of the small population underscores the precariousness of human survival during this period and the relatively high degree of homogeneity in traits that may have resulted from it.

The term “sex” is used here in its biological sense, referring to differentiation between male and female individuals based on genetic traits. The term “gender,” that relates to sex-specific social identity is not relevant in this specific context. Sex differences vary widely between species. A dramatic male-biased example is the elephant seal: adult males weigh ≈4000 kg and measure over 5 m—nearly ten times the weight of females (400–900 kg). This difference arises from intense male–male competition during the breeding season, during which male seals fight for dominance and access to females. An extreme female-biased example, in contrast, is the “triplewart seadevil” (deep-sea anglerfish). The tiny males (1–3 cm) permanently attach to the much larger females (20–30 cm), becoming fully dependent on their blood supply and functioning more or less only as reproductive appendages.

When referring to sexual dimorphism, it is important to distinguish between “observed” and “genuine” differences. Men are, for example, generally heavier than women, but they are not more obese, as they are also taller. Hence, the BMI is similar between men and women, without a “genuine” sex difference to be observed. Identifying genuine sex differences requires normalization—straightforward in some cases, such as expressing muscle cross-sectional area (CSA) as a percentage of total thigh CSA, but more complex in others, such as comparing cartilage thickness (see below).

The objective of this mini-review is to explore “observed” and “genuine” sex-differences in human knee cartilage anatomy, cartilage imaging properties, and cartilage therapy, and to relate these to other articular and peri‑articular tissues.

Results

Radiographic JSW

Radiographic JSW is the space visible between opposing bone surfaces in anterior–posterior knee X-rays, which in real is filled – during load bearing - by the femorotibial cartilages and the menisci. The minimum medial JSW (mmJSW) is widely used as a one-dimensional measure for tracking knee OA progression and has, in the past, been recommended by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA- [2]) for evaluating responses to disease-modifying OA drugs (DMOADs). Occasionally, it is used as an entry criterion for DMOAD trials, ensuring measurable changes over time and avoiding a floor effect [3].

Medial fixed-location measurement (22.5 % medial to lateral distal femur) has been shown to depend less on meniscus extrusion than mmJSW [4], and to predict knee replacement outcomes better than mmJSW [5]. Both the observed mmJSW and the fixed-location JSW assume greater values in men than women (Fig. 1: A,B; Table 1), raising concern that applying a minimum threshold in patient selection may introduce a bias toward inclusion of more male participants in a trial. Cartilage thickness

The picture for cartilage thickness findings in the total and central medial femorotibial compartment, however, is slightly different (Fig. 1: C,D): Men display ≈17 % thicker cartilage than women with JSN 0–1, with sex-differences being similar in both the tibia and the weight-bearing femur (Fig. 1: E,F). These differences are somewhat smaller (≈14 %) with JSN 3, but they do not show an inverted relationship between men and women at all, as they do for JSW.

Adolescent boys (9–18 years) were found to display greater cartilage volume than girls [7], and larger cartilage volume accrual rates. Cartilage volume change, however, does not directly translate into cartilage thickness change, because volume is strongly determined by metaphyseal bone size (and its longitudinal changes during growth), the latter differing markedly between sexes. In children (7–16 years), boys exhibited significantly thicker cartilage than girls by ultrasound, cartilage thickness decreasing with age in both sexes [8]. In adolescent (age ≈16 years) and mature (≈46 years) athletes, men displayed ≈20 % thicker femorotibial cartilage than women.; the annual increase was ≈0.8 % in adolescent boys and ≈1.4 % in girls, without obvious sex differences [9]. Similar findings were obtained in patellar cartilage [10].

When matching knees without radiographic structural pathology (KLG 0) or symptoms in either knee (n = 63) for body height, age, BMI, and sex, femorotibial cartilage morphology remained greater in men. Sex differences were ≈23 % in cartilage volume, ≈11 % in cartilage thickness, and ≈10 % in joint surface areas (all p < 0.001; Table 2), suggesting a genuine male-biased sex difference. Thinner cartilage and primarily a smaller (normalized) joint surfaces area (in relation to body weight) may – amongst other factors - contribute to a greater prevalence of knee OA in women. Articular cartilage composition and deformation

MRI also permits assessment of cartilage relaxation properties and so, indirectly, cartilage matrix composition [14,15]. Cartilage transverse relaxation time (T2) has been found to be associated with collagen content, collagen orientation, hydration, mechanical properties, and early OA status [15], with higher values (longer T2) indicating matrix pathology.

Mosher et al [16] did not identify sex differences in cartilage T2 between young adults. No sex differences in T2 were noted in children and adolescents with an open physis, but in those with a closed (or closing) physis (Table 2), with T2 elevated in boys vs. girls [17]. A longitudinal decrease in deep (but not superficial) medial femorotibial T2 was seen in female and male athletic adolescents [18]. In adult athletes, no longitudinal change in T2 change was observed in either sex [18] . Sex-specific reference values of T2 across 16 femorotibial subregions were provided for superficial and deep T2 in the OAI healthy reference cohort [19]. Medially, superficial T2 tended to be longer in men than in women, while laterally, deep T2 tended to be longer in women than in men; yet, averaged across joint surface or compartment, no sex differences in T2 occurred in either layer. The strongest sex differences were found in the superficial anterior medial tibia, in the deep internal lateral tibia, and in the internal lateral femur (women > men).

In vivo patellar cartilage deformation was measured after 30 deep knee bends and was similar between older women and men (50–78 years; −2.6 % vs. −2.2 %). It was greater in the young (22–30 years; 4.5 % vs. 6.2 %), but sex differences did not reach significance in either age group [20]. A lack of sex differences in femorotibial cartilage deformation was also reported after running 5000 steps [21]. Sex differences in cartilage properties and other articular and peri‑articular tissues are summarized in Table 2.

Sex differences in structural pathology (MOAKS scores) of articular cartilage of the knee have been published in a large cohort study [22], also including other articular tissues. Sex differences were noted for meniscus pathology (female > male), but not for cartilage, using either looser or stricter criteria defining the pathology.

Articular tissue response to treatment

Surgical knee joint distraction (KJD) substantially increased cartilage thickness and radiographic JSW in knees with late-stage OA [23]. Male sex and more severe baseline radiographic OA status were observed to be associated with greater JSW increase over one year after distraction [24]. The 10-year joint survival after distraction was also higher in men than in women, and the increase in cartilage thickness and JSW at year one was positively related to joint survival when men and women were analyzed together [25]. Recent stratification results for the FORWARD trial showed that treatment effects of Sprifermin (highest dose) over placebo on cartilage thickness of the 2 year treatment period tended to be greater in men than in women (Fig. 2) [26]. Sex differences in cartilage biology have been recognized in the scientific community. However, the implications for clinical trial design remain unclear, with many studies arbitrarily assigning demographic characteristics [27]. Further, the sex of stem cell donors and recipients was proposed to contribute to observed sex differences in stem cell treatment efficacy [27].

Conclusions

Between and within species, there exist tremendous differences in the heterogeneity / homogeneity of genetic, physiological, morphological, and pathological traits. Humans are extermely homogeneous due to a historic bottleneck in evolution, whereas dogs are extremely heterogeneous, also regarding a life-time risk of hip OA. Amongst species, sexual dimorphism can be male- or female biased, and within humans, some musculoskeletal characteristics are greaten in men, and some in women.

Radiographic JSW and femorotibial articular cartilage thickness in men exceed those in women, and a statistically significant difference persists when matching women and men for age, BMI, and body height (“genuine” difference). These sex difference deviate from other articular and peri‑articular tissues. For JSW, the sex differences disappears (and is actually reversed) with higher JSN grades, whereas the sex difference in cartilage thickness with advanced radiographic disease persists at only slightly lower level.

The discordance in sex differences between JSW and cartilage thickness with advanced radiographic disease (high JSN) may be due to the role of the meniscus in knee OA and represents an interesting topic of future research. Thinner cartilage, but primarily smaller (normalized) joint surfaces areas, may contribute to a greater prevalence of knee OA in women. Further, men appeared to respond better structurally and symptomatically to anabolic cartilage treatment. However, these results are preliminary, so far specific to one compound and one surgical technique, and not statistically significant. Sex-differences in cartilage treatment response therefore deserve deeper exploration.
