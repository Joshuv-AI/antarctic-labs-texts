Fostamatinib (R788), a spleen tyrosine kinase inhibitor, sensitizes pancreatic cancer cells to oncolytic vesicular stomatitis virus
Authors: Cassandra Catacalos-Goad, Dakota W. Goad, Molly C. Holbrook, Quinton Krueger, Isha M. Wilson, M. Abdul Hajjar, Valery Z. Grdzelishvili
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is one of the most lethal malignancies with limited treatment options. Oncolytic virus (OV) therapy exploits replication-competent viruses, including vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV), to preferentially infect and kill malignant cells, while sparing non-malignant cells. We previously showed that human PDAC cell lines are highly heterogeneous in their permissiveness to VSV and other OVs. Although ruxolitinib and other JAK1/2 inhibitors can enhance VSV replication in resistant PDAC cell lines, they also increase the susceptibility of non-malignant cells to OVs. Here, we show that fostamatinib (R788), a spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK) inhibitor approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of chronic immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), specifically boosts VSV replication in PDAC cells without affecting non-malignant cells. Fostamatinib treatment suppressed both the secretion of interferons and inflammatory cytokines by VSV-infected PDAC cells and diminished their responsiveness to type I interferons. However, unlike ruxolitinib, fostamatinib promoted viral replication without severely impeding innate immune responses essential for adaptive anti-cancer immunity. Additionally, fostamatinib inhibited PDAC cell proliferation even in the absence of viral infection, while ruxolitinib did not. Our data suggest that fostamatinib may be repurposed as an effective drug that enhances OV therapy in PDAC by promoting OV replication and suppressing tumor growth.

Introduction

Oncolytic virus (OV) therapy is a promising anticancer approach that harnesses the natural or genetically modified abilities of replication-competent viruses to preferentially infect and lyse malignant cells while sparing normal (non-malignant) cells.1 OV exploits various vulnerabilities of cancer cells, particularly the reduced capacity to mount effective antiviral responses. Vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) is a nonsegmented negative-sense RNA virus (order Mononegavirales, family Rhabdoviridae) that has shown potent oncolytic activity in various cancer models due to its natural tumor selectivity and strong immunogenic potential, which could also be further improved via genetic modifications of VSV.2,3 VSV can directly lyse infected tumor cells, activate antitumor immune responses, and holds promise for use in combination with other therapies.2,3 Notably, VSV-based oncolytic virotherapies are currently undergoing evaluation in phase I and II clinical trials in human subjects (ClinicalTrials.gov trials NCT01628640, NCT03120624, NCT04046445, NCT03865212, NCT03017820, NCT03647163, NCT06508463, NCT05644509, and NCT03456908).

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the most lethal malignancies, characterized by late diagnosis, resistance to conventional therapies, and a 5-year survival rate of less than 10%.4 The development of effective therapeutic strategies is urgently needed to improve outcomes for PDAC patients. Alongside other OVs, VSV has potential as a future treatment for PDAC.5 Our previous studies demonstrated that VSV-based recombinant viruses are effective OVs against a majority of PDAC cell lines.6 However, some PDAC cell lines are resistant to VSV, mainly due to a constitutive or inducible expression of interferon (IFN)-stimulated antiviral genes (ISGs).7,8,9,10 Our previous studies,7,11,12,13,14 and other studies using VSV-based OVs in different cancer systems,15,16,17,18,19 have demonstrated that pharmacological inhibition of the JAK/STAT pathway using Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors (such as Food and Drug Administration [FDA]-approved JAK1/2 inhibitor, ruxolitinib) can overcome the antiviral barrier posed by this pathway, which results in enhanced VSV replication in cancer cells. However, while effective in sensitizing resistant PDAC cells to OVs, JAK inhibitors also suppress antiviral defenses in non-malignant cells, raising safety concerns about off-target viral replication and systemic toxicity.20,21,22

To address these limitations, there is a growing interest in identifying immune modulators that can selectively impair antiviral defenses in cancer cells without compromising the immune competence of normal tissues. Fostamatinib (R788), an FDA-approved spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK) inhibitor for the treatment of chronic immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), has recently emerged as a potential immunomodulatory agent in ovarian cancer.23 In this study, we discovered that, unlike ruxolitinib, fostamatinib specifically boosts VSV replication in PDAC cells without affecting non-malignant cells and without severely impeding innate immune responses essential for adaptive anti-cancer immunity. Additionally, fostamatinib inhibited PDAC cell proliferation even in the absence of viral infection, while ruxolitinib did not. Our data suggest that fostamatinib may be repurposed as an effective drug that enhances OV therapy in PDAC by promoting OV replication and suppressing tumor growth.

Results

Spleen tyrosine kinase inhibitor fostamatinib sensitizes PDAC cells to VSV

To identify FDA-approved drugs that can enhance replication of oncolytic VSV in resistant PDAC cells, we used VSV-ΔM51-GFP in a diverse panel of human cell lines with varying levels of innate antiviral signaling (Figure 1A), comprehensively characterized previously by our lab.6 VSV-ΔM51-GFP is a widely studied oncolytic VSV recombinant harboring a deletion of methionine 51 (ΔM51) in the matrix (M) protein. This mutation impairs the ability of the virus to inhibit host antiviral responses, rendering it sensitive to type I IFN activities in normal cells while enabling preferential replication in cancer cells that typically have impaired IFN signaling.24,25 Additionally, VSV-ΔM51-GFP encodes a GFP reporter transgene inserted between the VSV G and L genes (Figure 1A), allowing for monitoring of VSV infection and spread.26 The panel of tested PDAC cell lines included SUIT-2, HPAF-II, and Hs766T. These three human PDAC cell lines have previously been shown to exhibit various levels of resistance to VSV-ΔM51-GFP, largely due to inducible or/and constitutive expression of antiviral ISGs (Figure 1A). Our previous studies showed that SUIT-2 cells are moderately resistant to VSV-ΔM51-GFP due to their residually active type I IFN antiviral signaling and inducible expression of ISGs, while HPAF-II and Hs766T are highly resistant to VSV-ΔM51-GFP due to their upregulated type I IFN signaling and constitutive high-level expression of antiviral ISGs.6,7,8 These cell lines reflect the heterogeneous antiviral landscape of PDACs and provide a stringent model for identifying compounds that can overcome resistance to VSV in a wide range of PDACs. Our screening of a library of FDA-approved drugs (data not shown) identified fostamatinib (R788), an FDA-approved oral SYK inhibitor for the treatment of ITP, as a top candidate for enhancing PDAC-specific replication of VSV-ΔM51-GFP. Given the limited known role of SYK in antiviral responses, its identification was of particular interest. Although fostamatinib has been explored for its potential to mitigate the pathological effects associated with SARS-CoV-2 infection, its specific role in modulating innate antiviral signaling pathways has not been elucidated.27,28

Following two independent screenings of FDA-approved drugs, both identifying fostamatinib as a top candidate, we validated our screening findings using western blot analysis (Figure 1B). In addition to fostamatinib, we included several other FDA-approved agents from our screening, including those that did not produce a desirable positive effect on VSV replication (negative controls). In addition, we used ruxolitinib, a well-characterized specific JAK1/2 inhibitor, which we have previously shown to dramatically enhance VSV replication in different PDAC cell lines (positive control).7,11,12,13,14 Western blot analysis of VSV protein levels following infection demonstrated enhanced viral protein accumulation with fostamatinib treatment across all tested PDAC cell lines (SUIT-2, HPAF-II, and Hs766T) but not in the non-malignant AG01519 fibroblasts, suggesting tumor-selective activity (Figure 1B). As expected, ruxolitinib treatment resulted in elevated VSV protein levels across all three PDAC lines but also stimulated VSV accumulation in non-malignant AG01519 cells, thus demonstrating limited oncoselectivity. In contrast, phenazopyridine, promethazine, losmapimod, and everolimus had minimal impact on viral protein expression relative to VSV alone in PDAC cell lines or AG01519 cells (Figure 1B).

To examine if enhanced VSV-ΔM51-GFP replication resulted in improved oncolytic abilities of the virus in PDAC cells, we tested virus/drug combinations in SUIT-2 (moderately resistant to VSV) and HPAF-II (highly resistant to VSV) using a standard plaque assay (Figure 1C). Plaque assays were conducted to assess the ability of VSV to initiate virus infection and form virus-induced plaques (oncolysis). Cells were infected with a 3-fold serial dilution of our VSV-ΔM51-GFP stock and then treated with 5 μM of either fostamatinib, ruxolitinib, or DMSO only as a vehicle control (all conditions had the same concentration of DMSO) (Figure 1C). At 48 h postinfection (h p.i.), cells were fixed and stained with crystal violet. Consistent with western blot data and our previous studies, ruxolitinib enhanced the number and size of viral plaques in both PDAC cell lines. In fostamatinib-treated cells, this effect was even more evident. Strikingly, HPAF-II, highly resistant to VSV infection, exhibited clear evidence of viral cytopathic effect only upon drug treatment, and no plaques were detectable in DMSO-treated VSV-infected HPAF-II cells. Treatment of HPAF-II cells with ruxolitinib and fostamatinib led to an increase in plaques, resulting in a dramatic increase in viral titer (PFU/mL) by at least 250-fold and 2,400-fold, respectively. As compared to mock-treated SUIT-2 cells, ruxolitinib and fostamatinib led to an increase in viral titer by approximately a 3-fold increase and a 98-fold increase, respectively. These findings confirm that fostamatinib not only promotes viral replication but also enhances the oncolytic potency of VSV in otherwise resistant PDAC cell lines.

Previous studies using JAK inhibitors demonstrated that the timing of drug treatment (pre- vs. post-infection) can significantly affect their effects on virus replication, with most antiviral effects examined primarily through pre-treatment prior to infection.7,13,14,29 To determine the treatment schedule most effective for overcoming the intrinsic resistance of PDAC cells to VSV-ΔM51-GFP, SUIT-2 and HPAF-II cells were treated with ruxolitinib or fostamatinib (5, 0.5, or 0.05 μM) either 24 h before infection (“pre”), 1 h post-infection (“post”), or both before and after infection (“pre + post”), with treatment maintained for 120 h p.i. (Figure S1). In both SUIT-2 and HPAF-II cells, pre-treatment alone did not significantly enhance VSV replication. In contrast, post-infection treatment markedly increased viral replication in HPAF-II cells for both inhibitors. A modest additional increase was observed in HPAF-II, with fostamatinib administered both pre- and post-infection, though no significant difference was detected between post-only and pre + post ruxolitinib treatment. In SUIT-2 cells, ruxolitinib achieved the strongest effect when applied post-infection, whereas fostamatinib yielded only a marginal improvement when administered both pre and post, consistent with the lower basal antiviral activity of SUIT-2 relative to HPAF-II. Given that HPAF-II constitutively expresses interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), these findings suggest that drug responses differing between pre + post and post-only conditions may reflect suppression of ISG or interferon signaling. Together our data suggest that both ruxolitinib and fostamatinib reversibly inhibit their targets and enhance VSV replication but are not effective if removed prior to viral exposure. To minimize confounding impacts on cell growth prior to infection, all subsequent experiments employed post-infection drug addition only. The effects of these inhibitors on cell growth dynamics are further explored later in this study.

To further compare the effects of fostamatinib and ruxolitinib on oncoselectivity of VSV-ΔM51-GFP (Figure 1B), we examined viral replication and cytotoxicity in the PDAC cell line SUIT-2 and three non-malignant cell lines, HPDE, AG01519, and AG08498 (Figure 2). VSV-driven GFP expression was quantified using a fluorescent plate reader (Figure 2A) and visualized by fluorescence microscopy at 48 h p.i. (Figure 2B). Both fostamatinib and ruxolitinib significantly enhanced GFP fluorescence in SUIT-2 cells relative to VSV alone; however, only ruxolitinib treatment led to increased GFP signal in HPDE, AG01519, and AG08498 cells (Figures 2A and 2B). To determine whether these drug combinations augmented VSV-mediated oncolysis in non-malignant cells, we measured cell viability at 72 h p.i. Consistent with Figure 1C, both fostamatinib + virus and ruxolitinib + virus significantly reduced SUIT-2 viability compared with virus alone (Figure 2C). In contrast, while ruxolitinib + virus markedly decreased viability in all three non-malignant cell lines, fostamatinib + virus produced only a modest reduction in HPDE cells and minimal effects in AG01519 and AG08498 (Figure 2C). To assess productive viral replication, we quantified titers of de novo VSV-ΔM51-GFP particles in culture supernatants collected at 48 h p.i. (Figure 2D). VSV-ΔM51-GFP alone produced the highest titer in SUIT-2 cells (2.92 × 106 FFU/mL), followed by AG01519 (2.50 × 106 FFU/mL), AG08498 (2.00 × 105 FFU/mL), and HPDE (1.07 × 105 FFU/mL). Addition of ruxolitinib significantly increased viral titers across all cell lines, yielding approximately 10-fold increases in SUIT-2 (3.33 × 107 FFU/mL) and AG01519 (2.92 × 107 FFU/mL) and about 100-fold increases in HPDE (1.17 × 107 FFU/mL) and AG08498 (3.33 × 107 FFU/mL). These findings show that SYK inhibitor fostamatinib, like JAK1/2 inhibitor ruxolitinib, greatly boosts VSV-ΔM51-GFP replication in VSV-resistant PDAC cell lines. However, unlike ruxolitinib, fostamatinib does not overcome the resistance of non-malignant cells to VSV-ΔM51-GFP, highlighting the need for further investigation into its underlying mechanism of oncoselectivity.

Fostamatinib targets antiviral signaling pathways to enhance viral replication in a SYK-independent manner

To determine whether the observed pro-viral effects of fostamatinib were specific to VSV-ΔM51-GFP only, we evaluated the impact of drug treatment across a panel of viruses with distinct attenuation and immune evasion profiles (Figure 3A). The following viruses (Figure 3A) were compared to VSV-ΔM51-GFP in SUIT-2 (Figures 3B–3E) and HPAF-II cells (Figures 3F–3I), representing moderately and highly resistant PDAC phenotypes, respectively: VSV-rWT-GFP encoding wild-type M and GFP transgene between G and L, VSV-p1-GFP encoding wild-type M and GFP transgene in the first position, and SeV-GFP, a Sendai virus (SeV, family Paramyxoviridae) recombinant encoding GFP in the first position. The three tested VSV recombinants (VSV-ΔM51-GFP, VSV-rWT-GFP, and VSV-p1-GFP) differ in VSV-M sequence (wild-type M in VSV-rWT-GFP and VSV-p1-GFP but ΔM51 M in VSV-ΔM51-GFP) and the GFP insertion position (fourth position in VSV-ΔM51-GFP and VSV-rWT-GFP but first position in VSV-p1-GFP), the latter reducing expression of viral genes located downstream of GFP. Compared to VSV-rWT-GFP, VSV-p1-GFP is slightly attenuated due to the GFP position, while VSV-ΔM51-GFP is the most attenuated of the three tested viruses, due to ΔM51 mutation in VSV-M. SeV represents another nonsegmented negative-sense RNA virus with much slower replication kinetics and higher sensitivity to type I IFN signaling in PDAC cells, compared to VSV.6,14 We included SeV in this study to determine whether fostamatinib enhances VSV-ΔM51-GFP replication in PDAC cell lines via modification of a cellular environment (e.g., inhibition of antiviral signaling), rather than virus-specific mechanisms. We reasoned that modification of a cellular environment should stimulate not only VSV-ΔM51-GFP but also other viruses (such as SeV) highly sensitive to innate antiviral signaling.

To compare viruses, SUIT-2 (Figures 3B–3E) and HPAF-II (Figures 3F–3I) cells were infected at an MOI of 0.1 (throughout this study, the MOI was calculated based on the viral titer determined using BHK-21 cells) or mock-infected for 1 h, then treated with 5 μM of either ruxolitinib or fostamatinib or DMSO as a vehicle control. GFP expression was monitored over 144 h p.i. In SUIT-2 cells, which were previously shown to exhibit inducible ISG expression and are moderately resistant to VSV-rWT-GFP infection, VSV-ΔM51-GFP replication was significantly and similarly enhanced by both ruxolitinib and fostamatinib treatment compared to vehicle control (Figure 3B). In contrast, replication of VSV-rWT-GFP and VSV-p1-GFP in SUIT-2 cells was unaffected by either drug treatment, consistent with the ability of the wild-type M protein to suppress host antiviral responses even in the absence of the drug treatment (Figures 3C and 3D). These findings support our hypothesis that fostamatinib primarily enhances viral replication by attenuating inducible antiviral signaling, a mechanism ineffective against VSV with wild-type M protein capable of effective immune evasion. Importantly, fostamatinib significantly increased SeV-GFP replication in SUIT-2 cells compared to both ruxolitinib and DMSO control, indicating that its pro-viral effects are not limited to VSV-ΔM51 and may extend to other viruses sensitive to host IFN responses (Figure 3D).

In HPAF-II cells, which were previously shown to exhibit high constitutive ISG expression and are highly resistant to VSV-rWT-GFP infection, both fostamatinib and ruxolitinib enabled detectable viral replication across all tested viruses, while GFP expression in untreated virus-infected cells remained comparable to uninfected controls (Figures 3F–3I). The observed differences between SUIT-2 cells and HPAF-II cells could be due to the constitutive ISG expression in HPAF-II cells that makes these cells resistant even to viruses equipped with abilities to evade inducible antiviral responses effectively. HPAF-II cells also express very high levels of surface-associated mucin (MUC1), which likely acts as a barrier to VSV-rWT-GFP infection and contributes to the observed resistance.30

In general, these findings demonstrated that the pro-viral activity of fostamatinib is not limited to VSV-ΔM51 and can extend to other RNA viruses and suggest that fostamatinib stimulates viral replication via inhibition of antiviral signaling. To further investigate potential immunomodulatory effects of fostamatinib on virus-induced antiviral signaling, SUIT-2 and HPAF-II cells were incubated with VSV-ΔM51-GFP for 1 h and then treated with either 5 μM of ruxolitinib, 5 μM of fostamatinib, or DMSO vehicle control. Protein lysates were collected at 4, 24, 36, and 48 h p.i. and analyzed for viral protein abundance and key components of innate immune signaling (Figure 4). Interestingly, while both ruxolitinib and fostamatinib treatments led to a dramatic increased viral protein accumulation in both SUIT-2 and HPAF-II cells at 36 and 48 h p.i., there was no effect on viral accumulation at 24 h p.i. in SUIT-2 cells (and below detection level in HPAF-II). This result suggests that drug treatments do not affect the early stages of the virus replication cycle but rather impede the abilities of cells to respond to viral infection. In agreement with this hypothesis, in VSV-infected, mock-treated cells, phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT3 was robust at all time points, while SUIT-2 markedly suppressed pSTAT1 (Y701), and both cell lines treated with drugs resulted in decreased pSTAT3 activation (unfortunately, STAT2 and pSTAT2 accumulation was below the detection level under our experimental conditions in these experiments). Fostamatinib-treated HPAF-II resulted in a reduction of pSTAT1 in mock wells but not in VSV-infected cells. In agreement with the expected target of fostamatinib as an SYK inhibitor, we also observed reduced phosphorylation of MAPK (ERK1/2). Expression of important antiviral ISGs, OAS2 and MX1, was significantly upregulated in mock-treated VSV-infected cells but downregulated in drug-treated infected cells. In SUIT-2 cells, OAS2 levels were comparably suppressed by both drugs, while ruxolitinib was more effective in inhibiting MX1 expression. The production of IFNs and other cytokines in response to viral infection plays a large role in both autocrine and paracrine antiviral signaling. To test whether fostamatinib modulates antiviral responses through the production of cytokines, we sought to evaluate the secretion by infected PDAC cells of key signaling molecules involved in innate immune activation following viral infection. To test this, SUIT-2 cells were incubated with VSV-ΔM51-GFP at an MOI of 0.1. At 1 h p.i., we replaced the virus-containing medium with fresh medium containing either 5 μM fostamatinib, 5 μM ruxolitinib, or DMSO vehicle control. Twenty-four hours later, we collected media for cytokine production (Figure 5A) and cells for western blot analysis (Figure 5B). The media were examined for a broad panel of cytokines and chemokines that represent major arms of the innate immune response by using the Human Cytokine/Chemokine 96-Plex Discovery Assay (Eve Technologies). This panel includes 96 important cytokines and chemokines, all presented in Table S1. In Figure 5A, we included the data on 16 selected cytokines, including type I IFNs (IFN-α2, IFN-β, and IFN-ω) and type II IFN (IFN-γ), as well as tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), a potent pro-inflammatory cytokine, and several pro-inflammatory interleukins, such as IL-1α, IL-6, and IL-8. Also, to assess chemotactic signaling, Figure 5A includes several chemokines, including GRO-α (CXCL1), CXCL9, MIP-1β (CCL4), RANTES (CCL5), and MIP-3α (CCL20), all of which direct the migration of specific immune cell subsets to sites of infection or inflammation. Additionally, Figure 5A includes macrophage colony-stimulation factor (M-CSF) and fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF-2), growth factors involved in immune cell differentiation and tissue remodeling, respectively, as well as HMGB1, a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) released during cell stress and necrosis. Together, this panel allowed us to capture a comprehensive snapshot of the innate immune landscape of the cytokines secreted in response to infection and drug treatment. Under VSV-ΔM51-GFP infection alone, we observed a significant upregulation of multiple immune mediators. This included a marked increase in type I IFNs (IFN-α2, IFN-β, and IFN-ω), pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1α, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α), chemokines (GRO-α, CXCL9, MIP-1β, and RANTES), and other immune-modulatory factors such as M-CSF, FGFR-2, and HMGB1. These data indicate a robust antiviral and inflammatory response triggered in SUIT-2 cells by VSV-ΔM51-GFP infection. Apart from IFN-ω and M-CSF (similar strong downregulation by both drugs), treatment with ruxolitinib and fostamatinib resulted in distinct cytokine and chemokine profiles across the panel of innate immune signaling molecules. In virus-infected cells, ruxolitinib-treated cells consistently exhibited higher levels of nearly all measured cytokines and chemokines compared to fostamatinib-treated cells. This divergence suggests that, despite both drugs similarly enhancing viral replication (Figure 5B), fostamatinib modulates innate immune signaling mechanisms through dampening cytokine production. Most notably, VSV-induced secretion of IFN-α2, IFN-β, IL-6, TNF-α, CXCL9, and RANTES was specifically inhibited by fostamatinib (but not ruxolitinib), pointing to potential roles of fostamatinib inhibiting an upstream kinase of TBK-1 through nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) and/or IRF3/7 pathways. The second facet of innate immune signaling critical to anti-viral response is the response to type I IFNs, primarily through the JAK-STAT pathway. Type I IFNs (IFN-α and IFN-β) signal mainly through the JAK/STAT pathway. When IFN-α or IFN-β binds to the IFN-α/β receptor, it activates JAK1 and TYK2, which phosphorylate STAT1 and STAT2. These form a complex with IRF9 (ISGF3) that translocates to the nucleus and drives ISG transcription via IFN-sensitive response element (ISRE) binding. To investigate this, we compared the abilities of fostamatinib and ruxolitinib to inhibit JAK/STAT pathway activation in response to the exogenously added IFN-α (in the absence of viral infection) (Figures 6A and 6B). It should be noted that fostamatinib (R788) is a prodrug converted by cells to its active form, R406.31,32 Therefore, we compared short (2 h; Figure 6A) and extended (16 h; Figure 6B) exposure to fostamatinib, as well as to R406, the active metabolite, prior to IFN-α stimulation. The culture medium was not replaced during the IFN treatment; therefore, after 2 or 16 h of treatment, the drug compounds remained present during the subsequent 4-h IFN-α exposure, resulting in 6- or 20-h drug treatments, respectively. R406 treatment led to a more pronounced decrease in P-STAT1 levels compared to fostamatinib even at a shorter, 6-h drug treatment (Figure 6A), and prolonged exposure (20 h) further enhanced this effect (Figure 6B). These findings suggest that the kinetics of intracellular drug accumulation and/or metabolic activation of R877 into R406 may contribute to the proviral effects of fostamatinib. While our data demonstrate that fostamatinib and R406 can inhibit IFN-α-mediated JAK/STAT activation even in the absence of viral infection, in both (fostamatinib or R406) treatment conditions, P-STAT1 remained detectable, unlike in ruxolitinib-treated cells, where P-STAT1 was completely abrogated. Although P-STAT2 activation appears less consistent and conclusive compared to P-STAT1, notable trends are still evident. Specifically, all treatment conditions resulted in reduced P-STAT2 levels (Figures 6A and 6B), with the reduction being more pronounced following 20 h pre-treatment compared to 6 h pre-treatment before IFN stimulation (Figure 6B). In general, our data show that while fostamatinib can inhibit IFN-α-mediated JAK/STAT activation, it does so less effectively than ruxolitinib. However, the observed limited inhibition of JAK/STAT signaling can be sufficient to stimulate VSV replication, especially as fostamatinib-treated VSV-infected cells secrete less IFNs and other JAK/STAT-activating cytokines, compared to ruxolitinib-treated cells (Figure 5). Given that fostamatinib is a clinically approved SYK inhibitor, we hypothesized that SYK should contribute to the innate immune response, limiting viral replication, and that fostamatinib enhances viral replication by inhibiting this pathway. To test this, we evaluated a panel of alternative SYK inhibitors, including entospletinib (Ento), RO9021, PRT062607, and BAY 61-3606, in both SUIT-2 and HPAF-II cell lines (Figures 6C and 6D). SUIT-2 and HPAF-II cells were infected with VSV-ΔM51-GFP at an MOI of 0.1 or mock-infected. One h p.i., medium was replaced with fresh medium containing each respective SYK inhibitor or ruxolitinib (as a positive control) at 5 μM or DMSO only. VSV-directed GFP expression was measured throughout 144 h p.i. Remarkably, only ruxolitinib and fostamatinib enhanced VSV replication; the other SYK inhibitors had no detectable effect in either SUIT-2 or HPAF-II, suggesting that fostamatinib stimulates VSV replication via an SYK-independent mechanism. To independently address this, SUIT-2 cells were transfected either with siSYK (small interfering RNA [siRNA] targeting SYK), siSCR (siRNA scramble control), or (−) siRNA (no siRNA). Forty-eight hours later, cells were infected with VSV-ΔM51-GFP at an MOI of 0.1 or mock-infected (Figure 6E). One h p.i., medium was replaced with either 5 μM fostamatinib, 5 μM ruxolitinib, or DMSO only. Twenty-four h p.i., cell lysates were collected and analyzed using western blot. Interestingly, we observed that fostamatinib had the same stimulating effect on VSV-ΔM51-GFP replication in the presence (siSCR-treated cells) or absence (siSYK-treated cells) of SYK expression (Figure 6E), suggesting that fostamatinib stimulates viral replication via SYK-independent mechanisms. Also, in the absence of drug treatment, no increase in VSV protein accumulation or STAT1 or P-STAT1 phosphorylation was observed in siSYK-treated VSV-infected SUIT-2 cells compared to siSCR-treated VSV-infected SUIT-2 cells, suggesting that SYK does not play an important role in antiviral signaling or VSV replication in SUIT-2 cells (Figure 6E). Together, these results suggest that fostamatinib enhances viral replication through an SYK-independent mechanism, likely through a combination of TBK-1 signaling (Figure 5), inhibition of JAK/STAT signaling (Figures 6A and 6B), and possibly even other alternative pathways controlling innate immune signaling. This hypothesis is in agreement with a previous study that characterized a broad range of kinases functionally inhibited by R406 using in vitro functional kinase assays, demonstrating R406 can very effectively inhibit various kinases involved in the antiviral signaling, including upstream kinases of TBK-1, such as SRC, JAK2, and ULK3, and does it with a higher pIC50 (the negative logarithm of the IC50 value) than SYK, the marketed target of fostamatinib.33

Global transcriptome analysis of the effect of fostamatinib on antiviral innate immune signaling

To examine the impact of fostamatinib on antiviral signaling and related biological processes in PDAC cells, we performed global transcriptomic analysis using RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) on SUIT-2 cells treated with either R406 (active metabolite of fostamatinib), ruxolitinib, BAY 61-3606, or DMSO vehicle control only in the presence or absence of IFN-α stimulation. We used R406, rather than fostamatinib, to allow for a more direct assessment of inhibition. Ruxolitinib, a known stimulator of VSV replication via JAK1/2 inhibition, was included to assess gene expression changes associated with JAK1/2 inhibition, while BAY 61-3606, an SYK inhibitor that does not promote VSV replication, was used to isolate the effects of SYK inhibition from the specific antiviral activity of R406 (Figures 6C and 6D). We anticipated that R406 would influence not only pathways involved in antiviral signaling but also those regulated by SYK, despite SYK not being involved in fostamatinib-induced viral replication. Cells were pre-treated with 5 μM of each drug, or DMSO vehicle control, for 16 h, followed by 4 h of stimulation with 5,000 U/mL human IFN-α (total of 20 h total drug treatment) before total RNA was isolated and analyzed by poly-A enriched RNA-seq. This analysis provided a comprehensive overview of how each drug influences gene expression in cells with or without IFN-α stimulation.

We first analyzed gene expression in cells treated with ruxolitinib, R406, or BAY 61-3606 in the absence of IFN-α stimulation. A Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that ruxolitinib treatment alone did not significantly enrich any immune-related pathways, reflecting its limited effect on baseline immune signaling (Figure S2A). In fact, ruxolitinib-treated cells resulted in minimal enrichment in the top 40 pathways (Figure S2A), which is also reflected in the volcano plot showing a small number of up- or down-regulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Figure S2B). In contrast, R406 (Figure S3A) and BAY 61-3606 (Figure S4A) induced substantial enrichment in pathways associated with apoptosis and immune modulation, with all top 40 GO terms in R406-treated cells surpassing the –log10(adjusted p value) threshold of 2 and the top 8 exceeding 3.5. Also, in comparison to ruxolitinib-treated cells (Figure S2B), R406 (Figure S3B) and BAY 61-3606 (Figure S4B) treatments resulted in a substantially more up- and down-regulated gene expression seen in the volcano plots. To understand and compare the differentially expressed genes resulting from each drug treatment, we used a dot plot to compare each drug treatment to the DMSO vehicle control (Figure S5). Each dot plotted surpassed a log2 fold change threshold of +1 or −1. To see which of these DEGs are associated with immune functions (“immune genes”), we used a gene list from The NanoString nCounter Human Immunology V2 Panel that includes 579 genes covering the core pathways and processes of the immune response (Table S2). If any of these immune genes were differentially expressed in drug-treated cells, they were indicated as colored diamonds, (all other DEGs were shown as gray circles) (Figure S5). Ruxolitinib alone resulted in minimal effects in SUIT-2 cells in the absence of IFN-α stimulation, while R406- and BAY 16-3606-treated cells resulted in significantly more DEGs, with BAY 16-3606 treatment producing not only more immune-related DEGs but also the highest total number of DEGs than the other groups (Figure S5). Notably upregulated genes induced by BAY 16-3606 treatment included IFNA1, IRF8, IFI27, several TRIM family members, and other innate immune regulators such as TICAM1, PLCG1-AS1, and UBA52P5 (Table S3). Conversely, BAY 16-3606 downregulated key IFN signaling mediators such as IRF1, DDX58, RNASEL, TYK2, and JAK2. These results suggest BAY 61-3606 has a broad impact on immune gene expression even in the absence of IFN-α, resulting in 69 total DEGs from the NanoString nCounter Human Immunology V2 Panel, as opposed to ruxolitinib and R406, which resulted in 1 and 17, respectively (Table S3). Seventeen immune-related DEGs from R406-treated cells include several down-regulated (TNFRSF1B, BATF3, IL12A, B3GAT1, CMKLR1, and PDGFB) and up-regulated (CD55, IL1R1, IL18R1, CXCR4, IRAK2, TNFSF10, CFP, TNFSF15, IL23A, STAT5A, and ICOSLG) genes (Table S3). The only immune gene downregulated in ruxolitinib-treated cells was TRAF1 (Table S3).

Upon IFN-α stimulation, the expected upregulation of many canonical ISGs (e.g., IFI35, IFITM1/2/3, IRF7, ISG15, OAS1, MX1, STAT1, etc.) was observed in DMSO-only-treated SUIT-2 cells (Table S4). Overall, SUIT-2 cells exhibited IFN-α-inducible antiviral signaling, as demonstrated by GO analysis revealing enrichment of immune-related pathways (Figure S6A) and a volcano plot showing widespread upregulation of antiviral and immune-associated genes (Figure S6B). Also, a principal-component analysis (PCA) of immune gene expression demonstrated that IFN-α-stimulated DMSO only treated samples clustered distinctly from IFN-α-unstimulated DMSO-only-treated groups (DMSO [bright yellow] vs. DMSO + IFN-α [light yellow]) (Figure 7A). Ruxolitinib + IFN-α samples (gray) almost overlap with IFN-α-unstimulated ruxolitinib-treated (black) or DMSO-only-treated groups (bright yellow), reflecting almost complete suppression of antiviral signaling by ruxolitinib regardless of IFN-α stimulation, validating its robust abilities to inhibit JAK1/2 (Figure 7A). BAY 61-3606-treated samples (−IFN: dark blue; +IFN: light blue) formed a unique cluster, indicating a distinct transcriptional signature, while R406+IFN samples (light red) retained partial immune activation, which aligned with a similar transcriptional signature as DMSO + IFN-α, supporting a gradient of immune modulation across treatments (Figure 7A). In agreement with the PCA analysis, R406 only partially suppressed ISG induction, with a distinct subset of ISGs (e.g., CIITA, HLA-B/E, IFITM2, JAK2, and TRIM31) being fully downregulated, while others (e.g., IFI35, OAS1, MX1, STAT1/2, and ISG15) remained partially upregulated, compared to DMSO + IFN-α (Figure S7). A dot plot revealed a global analysis of immune-related genes (diamonds) and total genes (dots) differentially expressed after IFN-α stimulation, which further validates the moderate retention of IFN-α-stimulated DEGs with R406 treatment (Figure 7B). This indicates partial modulation of IFN responses by R406. GO analysis and volcano plot results further support that. Thus, compared to ruxolitinib-treated cells that exhibit minimal IFN-induced responses and fewer upregulated genes (Figure S8), R406 treatment leads to a moderate suppression of innate immune pathways in IFN-stimulated SUIT-2 cells (Figure S9). However, it must be noted that this may have been due to the 4-h long IFN treatment before collection, potentially limiting further effects on genes found in the PanCancer dataset.

Overall, these findings suggest that fostamatinib induces distinct suppression of immune-related pathways and modulates proliferation/stress signaling, distinct from both SYK-specific (BAY 61-3606) and JAK1/2-specific (ruxolitinib) inhibition. This dual regulatory capacity may underlie its ability to enhance viral replication despite partial immune gene suppression.

Fostamatinib disrupts cell proliferation, slowing PDAC cell growth

GO analysis, along with targeted evaluation of proliferation- and apoptosis-related genes in our RNA-seq dataset, revealed significant enrichment of pathways associated with cell death and epithelial cell proliferation in R406-treated (uninfected) SUIT-2 cells. These findings prompted us to further examine the effects of fostamatinib on cell viability and growth dynamics (Figures 8A–8E). Given the enhanced virus-mediated oncolysis observed in both ruxolitinib- and, more notably, fostamatinib-treated cells (compared to virus-only treatment) (Figure 1C), we sought to investigate why fostamatinib-treated cells exhibited greater cell death upon VSV infection, compared to DMSO- and ruxolitinib-treated cells. This was particularly intriguing because our data indicate that VSV replicates at similar levels in fostamatinib- and ruxolitinib-treated cells (Figures 1B and 3). Therefore, we first examined the impact of fostamatinib on cell growth in the context of VSV infection (Figure 8A). To test this, we performed a cell growth assay on ruxolitinib-, fostamatinib-, and BAY 61-6106-treated SUIT-2 and HPAF-II cells with or without VSV-ΔM51-GFP infection. SUIT-2 and HPAF-II cells were seeded for 30% confluence the following day. Cells were infected with VSV-ΔM51-GFP at an MOI of 0.1 or mock-treated. At 1 h p.i., the medium was replaced with 5 μM of either ruxolitinib, fostamatinib, or BAY 61-6106 or with DMSO vehicle control. At 96 h p.i., the medium was aspirated, and cells were fixed with crystal violet. Consistent with the data shown in Figure 1C, both ruxolitinib and fostamatinib enhanced VSV-mediated cell death in HPAF-II cells, with fostamatinib showing a greater effect compared to the DMSO vehicle control (Figure 8A). Interestingly, BAY 61-6106 also appeared to further increase cell death in VSV-infected HPAF-II cells. Also, fostamatinib-treated HPAF-II did not result in a similar confluence under all other treatment conditions, compared to the mock condition 96 h post-drug treatment. This was interesting to us because in Figure 1C, fostamatinib-treated cells were confluent in mock wells; however, it must be noted that cells intended for the plaque assay were ∼90% confluent before receiving treatment (Figure 1C) compared to 30% confluent cells in Figure 8A. In contrast to HPAF-II, under these experimental conditions (30% confluent SUIT-2 cells), VSV-driven oncolysis was successful in all treatment conditions in SUIT-2 cells, even when cells were treated with VSV-ΔM51-GFP only. Importantly, fostamatinib alone (without viral infection) reduced cell growth in SUIT-2 even more than observed in HPAF-II. This finding prompted us to further explore cell growth during treatment with fostamatinib. We next measured relative cytotoxicity in HPAF-II and SUIT-2 cells treated with drugs only (Figure 8B). To do this, we seeded SUIT-2 and HPAF-II cells for 30% confluence the following day. The next day, we treated cells with 5 μM of ruxolitinib, 5 μM fostamatinib, or DMSO vehicle control. Immediately following treatment and 24 h post-drug treatment, a portion of the cell medium was collected to measure cell cytotoxicity, and the remaining cell medium and cell culture were measured for cell viability to normalize cell cytotoxicity. As an important control, we measured cell viability at 0 and 24 h to ensure expected cell growth over the course of the experiment, as we expect cell viability to directly correlate to cell growth. Fostamatinib-treated cells exhibited significantly lower levels of cell viability compared to ruxolitinib-treated and DMSO-treated cells in both SUIT-2 and HPAF-II cell lines; however, these levels are still greater than that of DMSO-treated cells 0 h post-drug treatment (Figure 8B, left graphs). Although in both cell lines there were almost 50% fewer measured viable cells, cell cytotoxicity was not significantly changed between any of the groups, except for a slight increase in fostamatinib-treated HPAF-II (Figure 8B, middle graphs). To account for cell cytotoxicity per viable cell, we proceeded with the ratio of cell cytotoxicity normalized by cell viability (Figure 8B, right graphs). To determine whether these effects extended to longer term growth, we evaluated cell proliferation in the context of a clonogenic assay. SUIT-2 cells were seeded at 1,000 single cells per well on a 12-well plate (Figure 8C). The following day, 5 μM ruxolitinib, 5 μM fostamatinib, 5 μM BAY 61-3606, or DMSO vehicle control were added to the cells. Microscopy images were taken at 40× magnification at 0, 24, and 72 h post-drug treatment, at which point cells were fixed and stained with crystal violet (Figure 8C). Fostamatinib treatment significantly impaired SUIT-2 cell growth and colony formation, as evidenced by a marked reduction in colony-forming units. In contrast, BAY 61-6106 led to a moderate decrease in colony formation, while ruxolitinib had minimal effect on uninfected cells. Beyond the crystal violet staining, visual inspection revealed a pronounced reduction in cell density and proliferation in fostamatinib-treated cultures, with few to no colonies forming, unlike the more robust growth observed in all other treatment conditions.

We next characterized growth kinetics in SUIT-2 and HPAF-II cells over time under each drug treatment (Figure 8D). To do this, we seeded 1,000 cells per well of SUIT-2 and HPAF-II cells in 96-well plates. The following day, 5 μM of either ruxolitinib, fostamatinib, or BAY 61-3606 or DMSO vehicle control was added to cells. Immediately following treatment, and every 24 h, cell viability was measured. While the timing of entry into and exit from the logarithmic growth phase was similar across all conditions, we observed a notable divergence in the final cell density. Cells treated with DMSO vehicle control, ruxolitinib, or BAY 61-3606 reached a plateau near full confluence, whereas fostamatinib-treated cells arrested growth at substantially lower densities, as visualized by crystal violet staining once DMSO-treated cells reached full confluence, 144 h post-drug treatment for SUIT-2 and 96 h post-drug treatment for HPAF-II (Figure 8D, left panel). Consistent with the findings in Figure 7A, SUIT-2 cells exhibited a more pronounced reduction in cell confluency, compared to HPAF-II cells, following fostamatinib treatment (Figure 8D, left panel). This suggests that SUIT-2 cells are potentially more sensitive to the antiproliferative and/or cytotoxic effects of fostamatinib.

Finally, we examined the effect of these treatments on cell migration using a wound-healing assay (Figure 8E). SUIT-2 cells were seeded for 100% confluence the following day. Using a pipette tip, an even clearing of cells was made in each well, cells were extensively washed with PBS, and then treated with either 5 μM ruxolitinib, 5 μM fostamatinib, 5 μM BAY 61-3606, or DMSO vehicle control. Images were taken immediately after drug treatment and 17 h post-drug treatment at six locations per condition. Averages of normalized width differences were used to determine % of migration (Figure 8E, right panel). Strikingly, fostamatinib-treated SUIT-2 cells showed only ∼20% wound closure after 24 h, compared to nearly complete closure in cells treated with DMSO, ruxolitinib, or BAY 61-3606. These results indicate that fostamatinib significantly impairs the migratory capacity of SUIT-2 cells, suggesting a potential inhibitory effect on cytoskeletal dynamics or motility-related signaling pathways that are not observed with ruxolitinib or BAY 61-3606 treatment. These data collectively suggest that fostamatinib exerts broad suppressive effects on PDAC cell viability, proliferation, and migration, independent of its impact on viral susceptibility.

Discussion

OV therapy exploits impaired antiviral signaling in tumors to enable preferential viral replication.1 Although VSV-based OVs (such as VSV-ΔM51) are effective against many PDAC cell lines, other PDACs exhibit resistance due to constitutive or inducible expression of IFN-stimulated antiviral genes.6,7,8,9,10,11,12 This limits the efficacy of OV monotherapy in PDAC, highlighting the need to understand resistance mechanisms and develop strategies to overcome them.5 In this study, we demonstrate that SYK inhibitor fostamatinib (R788), an FDA-approved drug that is used to treat ITP, specifically boosts VSV replication in PDAC cells without affecting non-malignant cells.

Fostamatinib emerged as an unexpected candidate in our screening of FDA-approved drugs, alongside the more anticipated discovery of several JAK inhibitors, including a specific JAK1/2 inhibitor, ruxolitinib, extensively studied in our previous studies.3,8,14 This was of particular interest to us for two important reasons. First, fostamatinib targets a distinct arm of immune signaling not traditionally associated with antiviral defense. Unlike JAK inhibitors, which directly disrupt canonical IFN-associated antiviral signaling, SYK is primarily linked to signaling in hematopoietic, myeloid, and innate immune cells.34,35,36,37 Fostamatinib has never been shown before to enhance viral replication in cancer cells. However, a previous paper highlights a role for SYK in supporting viral persistence, demonstrating that Epstein-Barr virus exploits SYK/NF-κB signaling to promote immune evasion and survival of infected B cells, thereby facilitating viral replication and pathogenesis.38 Another study showed that SYK plays a pivotal role in viral infection with VSV, vaccinia virus, and herpes simplex virus by phosphorylating MYO1F.39 This phosphorylation event enables the recruitment of KAT2A, which then acetylates cGAS, a crucial step for its activation.39 As a result, cGAS is properly positioned and fully functional at the plasma membrane, leading to robust cGAMP synthesis and type I IFN production in response to virus-cell fusion events.39 Thus, fostamatinib’s ability to enhance viral replication in PDAC cells revealed a previously underappreciated role for SYK-dependent pathways in the resistance of cells to viral infection, warranting further investigation into its mechanistic contributions. Second, while both fostamatinib and ruxolitinib promoted VSV replication, viral protein accumulation, and infectious particle production across all tested PDAC cell lines, including the highly resistant cell lines HPAF-II and Hs766T,6,7,8 ruxolitinib also increased susceptibility of non-malignant cells to VSV, while fostamatinib boosted VSV replication in PDAC cells without affecting non-malignant cells.

Our studies revealed that fostamatinib stimulates VSV replication via at least two distinct mechanisms that inhibit cellular abilities to resist viral infection. The first mechanism is responsible for the fostamatinib-mediated reduction of type I and type II IFNs and pro-inflammatory cytokine production in response to VSV infection. With regard to inhibiting the production of IFNs, fostamatinib was much more effective than ruxolitinib. Moreover, in contrast to ruxolitinib-treated VSV-infected cells, fostamatinib-treated VSV-infected cells exhibited a unique cytokine signature with an almost complete suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, MIP-1β, and CXCL9, which were all dramatically more abundant in ruxolitinib-treated cells. Although the precise mechanism underlying the suppression of cytokine production from fostamatinib treatment remains to be fully elucidated, we observed that fostamatinib treatment in SUIT-2 cells led to a marked reduction in phosphorylated TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1). TBK1 plays a pivotal role in innate immune signaling by activating IRF3 and IRF7, which are critical for type I IFN induction, and by modulating the NF-κB pathway, which regulates pro-inflammatory cytokine expression.40,41 Therefore, the observed reduction in TBK1 phosphorylation suggests that fostamatinib may impair both IRF3/7- and NF-κB-dependent signaling cascades, leading to the broad suppression of antiviral and inflammatory responses during VSV infection.

Our data suggest an additional mechanism by which fostamatinib can enhance VSV replication, which is through inhibition of JAK/STAT pathway activation in response to IFN-α (Figure 5). This result was surprising, considering that fostamatinib is not a known JAK inhibitor. Interestingly, unlike ruxolitinib, fostamatinib does not completely suppress STAT1/2 phosphorylation following the exogenously added IFN (Figure 5) or viral infection (Figure 3). This result suggests that fostamatinib reduces JAK/STAT activation in PDAC cells just enough to fall below a critical threshold required for an effective antiviral defense. The observed incomplete inhibition of JAK/STAT activation in fostamatinib-treated cells can explain why fostamatinib can boost VSV replication in PDAC cells but not in non-malignant cells that have an intact and more robust antiviral signaling.

Interestingly, although fostamatinib and its active form R406 demonstrated only modest suppression of IFN-stimulated immune responses compared to ruxolitinib, both drugs similarly enhanced VSV replication, suggesting that inhibition of a specific subset of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) may be sufficient to permit viral propagation. To investigate this, we used a Venn diagram to identify genes commonly downregulated by both R406 and ruxolitinib, but not by BAY 61-3606, in IFN-stimulated SUIT-2 cells compared to IFN-stimulated DMSO vehicle control (Figure 7C). This analysis revealed 13 downregulated genes: TCF4, IRF1, IFIT2, CFH, SOCS3, FAP2, NOD1, IFIH1, PSMB8, STAT2, MX1, MYD88, and PML. Notably, several of these genes, including IFIT2, IFIH1, STAT2, MX1, and MYD88, encode key antiviral effectors and may collectively contribute to the resistance of SUIT-2 cells to VSV infection. In the future, we will investigate the individual roles of these ISGs in mediating antiviral responses and their potential contribution to the enhanced viral replication observed upon R406 treatment.

The discovered actions of fostamatinib, such as its inhibition of cytokine production in VSV-infected cells and its suppression of antiviral signaling in response to exogenously added IFN, may stem from off-target effects of fostamatinib and/or inhibition of currently unidentified SYK functions. Despite the known function of fostamatinib as an SYK inhibitor, our data strongly argue against SYK being the critical mediator of its proviral activity. In fact, our studies suggest that the observed enhancement of VSV through fostamatinib treatment is SYK-independent, as neither SYK knockdown (Figure 6) nor treatment with other selective SYK inhibitors replicated the effects observed with fostamatinib.

Together, our data suggest that the observed effects of fostamatinib on VSV replication and antiviral signaling in PDAC cells are due to an SYK-independent mechanism and likely involve multiple off-target activities of fostamatinib, including inhibition of JAK/STAT signaling, TBK-1 signaling, and possibly even other alternative components of innate immune signaling. This hypothesis is in agreement with a previous study that characterized a broad range of kinases functionally inhibited by R406 in vitro functional kinase assays, including upstream kinases of TBK-1, such as SRC, JAK2, and ULK3, with a higher pIC50 than SYK, the marketed target of fostamatinib.33 Another study also suggested TBK-1 inhibition by fostamatinib.42 Additionally, several kinases inhibited by R406 in the same study (such as SRC, TNK2, BMX, YES1, IRAK1, and JAK3) may also regulate the JAK/STAT pathway.33

Transcriptome analysis not only confirmed that fostamatinib only partially inhibits ISG induction but also showed enrichment in genes associated with proliferation, apoptosis, and migration. Specifically, genes involved in promoting cell death after R406 treatment include CXCR4, ESR2, FOXO1, NR4A1, NR4A2, and members of the TNFSF family (TNFSF10, TNFSF13B, and TNFSF15) (Figure S10). R406 treatment also increases the expression of genes linked to the suppression of cell growth, such as DEPTOR, GDF15, IFNA1, NOTCH3, PLAT, and TP53INP1 (Figure S10). Conversely, R406 downregulates several genes known to act as positive regulators of cell proliferation, including CCL5, CCND1, FOSL1, JUN, MYC, MYEOV, PDGFB, PIK3R2, and STC2, suggesting an overall inhibitory effect on proliferative signaling pathways (Figure S10). Interestingly, many viral sensitizers have been reported to impair the antiviral response of target cells by inducing a cell-cycle arrest at the G2/M phase.43 Our RNA-seq data revealed patterns consistent with this mechanism, suggesting that fostamatinib may partially sensitize PDAC cells to VSV infection by promoting a shift toward G2/M arrest, thereby diminishing their antiviral competence. Future studies will focus on elucidating the functional roles and regulatory mechanisms of these gene expression changes, with particular attention to how they contribute to the anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects of R406. To address the possible effect of fostamatinib on the proliferation abilities of PDAC cells, we conducted cell-based assays, which confirmed that fostamatinib does, in fact, dramatically stunt cell proliferation, growth, and migration. Our data agree with a previous study that investigated the potential of repurposing fostamatinib as an anti-cancer agent by evaluating its cytotoxic effects on tumor cells and its ability to modulate immune checkpoint pathways.35 These results suggest that fostamatinib may exert a dual mechanism of action, dampening antiviral signaling while simultaneously impairing PDAC cell viability, ultimately resulting in not only better viral replication but also enhanced oncolysis, which is a goal of any successful OV.

Our study highlights the potential of repurposing FDA-approved drugs to enhance oncolytic virus therapy for PDAC. The dual action of fostamatinib, facilitating OV replication and impairing tumor cell fitness, positions it as a promising candidate for combinatorial therapy. Unlike ruxolitinib, which broadly suppresses IFN signaling and carries the risk of dampening systemic immune responses, the observed partial and distinct inhibition in fostamatinib-treated PDAC cells may reduce the likelihood of widespread immunosuppression, offering a more tumor-selective therapeutic profile. This distinction is particularly appealing in the context of PDAC, which is characterized by an exceptionally immunosuppressive or “cold” tumor microenvironment (TME), marked by poor T cell infiltration, dense stromal barriers, and limited innate immune activation.44,45,46,47,48 In such a setting, preserving systemic immune competence while locally enhancing viral replication and tumor cell lysis is highly advantageous. The ability of fostamatinib to modulate antiviral signaling within tumor cells, without abolishing innate immune signaling pathways more broadly, has the potential to permit synergistic strategies that combine oncolytic virus therapy with immune checkpoint blockade or TME reprogramming. In essence, it may “warm up” the PDAC microenvironment by triggering immunogenic cell death and antigen release, while avoiding the immune-paralytic effects associated with more potent, systemic JAK-STAT inhibition. However, it must be noted that fostamatinib led to a reduction in HMGB1, and several additional pro-inflammatory cytokines, which may have unintended consequences for antitumor immunity by dampening the release of key damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and limiting the recruitment or activation of immune effector cells within the TME. Additional future studies are necessary to comprehensively address this point. Additionally, its distinct mechanism of action provides a valuable tool to dissect noncanonical pathways regulating antiviral responses in cancer.

While our current study focused on the mechanism of VSV-fostamatinib interaction, future in vivo validation using animal PDAC models will be essential to assess therapeutic efficacy and safety in an immunocompetent context. To fully assess the anticancer efficacy and safety of this treatment, testing in an immunocompetent in vivo system is required. However, our current in vitro system, which uses clinically relevant human PDACs, presents challenges, as SUIT2, HPAF-II, and other VSV-resistant human PDAC cell lines cannot be tested in immunocompetent mice. To address this, we are currently exploring several mouse PDAC cell lines to identify VSV-resistant cell lines, which could then be tested with VSV/fostamatinib combinations in an immunocompetent mouse model of PDAC. We hope that this combination approach could ultimately be utilized to treat PDAC tumors resistant to OV monotherapy in the future. Future studies may also explore the potential of combination therapy using both ruxolitinib and fostamatinib. Given that ruxolitinib may impact healthy cells more broadly due to its systemic inhibition of the JAK/STAT pathway, a strategy involving reduced concentrations of ruxolitinib could be employed to minimally “prime” this pathway in PDAC cells, enhancing susceptibility to viral oncolysis while minimizing off-target effects in non-malignant tissues. When combined with fostamatinib, which appears to act through a distinct mechanism and demonstrates more selective effects in PDAC cells, such an approach could potentiate therapeutic efficacy. This combinatorial strategy may prove particularly effective given the observed differences in cytokine expression profiles induced by each drug, suggesting a potential for synergistic immune modulation and enhanced tumor-specific viral replication.

In conclusion, our work provides compelling evidence that fostamatinib sensitizes PDAC cells to oncolytic virus therapy through a previously undiscovered mechanism. These findings not only challenge existing assumptions about antiviral competence in cancer but also broaden the landscape of therapeutic strategies that can be leveraged to overcome resistance to OV therapy.

Materials and methods

Virus and cell lines

The previously described recombinant virus VSV-ΔM51-GFP26 has the methionine at amino acid position 51 of the matrix protein (M) deleted and the GFP open reading frame (ORF) inserted at position 5 of the viral genome (between VSV G and L genes). VSV-rWT-GFP is similar to VSV-ΔM51-GFP (and contains the GFP ORF inserted at the same position) but has a wild-type M.49 VSV-p1-GFP also has a wild-type M but the GFP ORF inserted at position 1 of the viral genome.26 The recombinant Sendai virus SeV-GFP (SeV-GFP-Fmut) is described previously50 and has the GFP ORF at position 1 of the viral genome and a mutation in the cleavage site of the fusion (F) protein, allowing F activation and production of infectious particles in cells without acetylated trypsin added to the medium. Baby hamster kidney fibroblast cells BHK-21 (ATCC CCL-10) were used to generate VSV and determine titers. All viral titers were determined by adding 5-fold serial dilutions of a virus to BHK-21 cells in a 12-well plate. An agar overlay was used an hour after infection, and at 24 h p.i., both fluorescent focus units per mL (FFU/mL) and plaque-forming units per mL (PFU/mL) were calculated. To count PFUs, cells were fixed and stained with crystal violet. To count FFUs, GFP fluorescent foci were quantified using fluorescent microscopy. The human PDAC cells used in this study were SUIT-2,51 HPAF-II,52 and Hs766t.53 The human origin of all tested PDAC cells lines was confirmed as previously described.8 AG01519 (Coriell Institute) are normal untransformed human foreskin fibroblast cells. SUIT-2 and HS766t cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM [Corning, 10-013-CV]). HPAF-II, AG01519, and BHK-21 cells were maintained in minimum essential medium (Corning 10-010-CV). All cell growth media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS [Gibco]), 4 mM L-glutamine, 900 U/mL penicillin, 900 μg/mL streptomycin, and 1% nonessential amino acids. HPAF-II and BHK-21 cells were additionally supplemented with 17.5% glucose. All cells were kept in a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37°C. For all experiments, cells were kept in no more than 15 passages. All described experiments were approved by the University of North Carolina at Charlotte Institutional Biosafety Committee (IBC).

Virus plaque assay

Twelve-well plates were seeded at 90% confluence and were given 24 h to adhere. Cells were infected with VSV-ΔM51-GFP dilutions or mock-infected (control) for 1 h. One h p.i., the virus was aspirated, and cells were overlaid with 2% agarose in DMEM with 5% FBS; the overlay media was supplemented with either ruxolitinib, fostamatinib, or 0.05% DMSO before wells. After 72 h, formalin (20%) was added to fix cells for 4 h. After fixation, agarose was removed, and cells were stained with 2% crystal violet in 25% methanol. For the plaque assay figures, each row represents one 12-well plate that was cut and stitched to view the highest amount of virus to the least amount of virus from left to right.

Virus replication kinetics

For all experiments, the MOI was determined by assessing virus titers (FFU/mL and PFU/mL) through standard plaque assays on BHK-21 cells cultivated in 12-well plates. For virus replication kinetics experiments, cells were plated onto 96-well plates. Virus dilutions were prepared in DMEM supplemented with 0% FBS. After washing cells once with PBS, virus was added and allowed to absorb for 1 h at 37°C. Subsequently, the virus-containing medium was removed, and fresh DMEM containing 5% FBS was replenished, followed by further incubation at 37°C in 5% CO2 for the experiment’s duration. Virus-encoded GFP fluorescence was monitored at various time points over 120 h using a fluorescence multi-well plate reader, with GFP fluorescence readings taken at 485/530 nm (ns, not significant; ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001).

Western blot analysis

Cells were seeded into 12-well plates. After treatment, cells were lysed, and total protein was isolated using buffer in lysis buffer containing 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 10% glycerol, 2% SDS, 5% beta-mercaptoethanol, and 0.02% (wt/vol) bromophenol blue. Total protein was separated by electrophoresis on 10% or 15% SDS-PAGE gels and electroblotted onto polyvinyl difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked by using 5% nonfat powdered milk or BSA in TBS-T (0.5 M NaCl, 20 mM Tris [pH 7.5], 0.1% Tween 20) for 1 h at room temperature. Cell viability and cell proliferation assays

SUIT-2 and HPAF-II cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1,000 cells per well in 100 μL of complete DMEM. After 24 h, cells were either treated with 5 μM of ruxolitinib, fostamatinib, or BAY 61-3606 or mock-treated with 0.05% concentration of DMSO in DMEM containing 5% FBS. For cell viability, WST-8 (Dojindo, CK04) was added to each well for 4 h at 37°C in 5% CO2, then read at 450 nm using a multi-well plate reader. Monitoring continued until DMSO-treated control wells reached approximately 100% confluence. At that time point, all wells were fixed and stained with crystal violet to visualize and qualitatively assess cell proliferation. To fix cells, cold 100% methanol was added on top of cells and incubated at 4°C for 10 min. Next, crystal violet in 25% methanol was added on top of cells and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. After staining, the crystal violet solution was removed, and cells were washed using deionized water to remove the unbound dye. The stained cells were then allowed to dry completely at room temperature before images were taken (ns, not significant; ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001).

Cell cytotoxicity assay

SUIT-2 and HPAF-II cells were seeded in 12-well plates to reach a confluence of 85% the following day in 1 mL of complete DMEM. After 24 h, cells were treated with 5 μM of either ruxolitinib or fostamatinib or were mock-treated with 0.05% concentration of DMSO in DMEM containing 5% FBS. Immediately following drug treatment (0 h) and 24 h post-drug treatment, 100 μL of each condition was collected and mixed with the cytotoxicity reagent in a flat white 96-well plate at a 1:1 ratio to measure cell cytotoxicity using the CytoTox-Glo Cytotoxicity Assay (Promega, G9290). After a 20-min incubation at room temperature, the luminescence was measured using a luminometer. For cell viability, WST-8 (Dojindo, CK04) was added to each well for 4 h at 37°C in 5% CO2, then read at 450 nm using a multi-well plate reader (ns, not significant; ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001).

Colony-forming unit assay

SUIT-2 cells were seeded at 1,000 cells per well on a 12-well plate. The following day, cells were treated with 5 μM of either ruxolitinib, fostamatinib, or BAY 61-3606 or mock-treated with a 0.05% concentration of DMSO in DMEM containing 5% FBS. Bright field 40× microscopy images were taken at 0, 24, and 72 h post-drug treatment, and cells were fixed and stained with crystal violet at 72 h post-drug treatment. To fix cells, cold 100% methanol was added on top of cells and incubated at 4°C for 10 min. Next, crystal violet in 25% methanol was added on top of cells and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. After staining, the crystal violet solution was removed, and cells were washed using deionized water to remove the unbound dye. The stained cells were then allowed to dry completely at room temperature before images were taken.

Wound-healing assay

One hundred percent confluent monolayers of SUIT-2 cells grown in 24-well plates were subjected to a mechanical scratch using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip to create a linear wound. Detached cells were removed by washing the wells three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Cells were then incubated in DMEM containing 5% FBS with the indicated compounds or vehicle control for 17 h. Images of the wounds were captured immediately after scratching (0 h) and after 17 h of treatment using a light microscope. Wound closure was quantified by measuring the width of the scratch at six predefined regions per well at both time points. The extent of cell migration was calculated as the relative wound closure using the following formula:Relativemigration=(Width0h−Width17h)/Width0h.

Data represent the average of six measurements per condition. All values are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA followed by post hoc multiple comparisons. p values are indicated as follows: ns, not significant; ∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.

RNA isolation, RNA-seq analysis, and gene expression analysis

Three biological replicates were used for each treatment condition for RNA-seq. SUIT-2 was seeded into 12-well plates to reach ∼85% confluence in DMEM containing 10% FBS. Cells were given 24 h to adhere. Cells were then treated with 5 μM of either ruxolitinib, fostamatinib, or BAY 61-3606 or mock-treated with 0.05% (v/v) concentration of DMSO in DMEM containing 5% FBS. After 2 h, cells were treated with 5,000 U/mL IFN-α, and cellular RNA was collected 4 h later. Cellular RNA was isolated with TRIzol (Life Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The RNA samples were then sent to GeneWiz for RNA-seq analysis. Sequence reads were trimmed to remove adapter sequences and low-quality bases using Trimmomatic v.0.36. The trimmed reads were mapped to the Homo sapiens GRCh38 reference genome available on ENSEMBL using the STAR aligner v.2.5.2b. Unique gene hit counts were calculated by using featureCounts from the Subread package v.1.5.2. The hit counts were summarized and reported using the gene_ID feature in the GRCh38 annotation file. Only unique reads that fell within exon regions were counted. After the extraction of gene hit counts, the gene hit count table was used for downstream differential expression analysis (see “data and code availability Statement”). Using DESeq2, pairwise comparisons were performed. The Wald test was used to generate p values and log2 fold changes. Genes with an adjusted p value <0.05 and absolute log2 fold change >1 were identified as differentially expressed. Gene lists were generated from “The NanoString nCounter Human Immunology V2 Panel that includes 579 genes covering the core pathways and processes of the immune response” and “The NanoString nCounter PanCancer Pathways Panel that includes 770 genes from 13 cancer-associated canonical pathways”) for further analysis.

Statistical analysis
