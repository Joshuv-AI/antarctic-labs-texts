Abstract
Let k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 and consider the sequence { P n ( k ) } n ≥ 2 − k \{P_{n}^{(k)}\}_{n\geq 2-k} of k k -generalized Pell numbers, which begins with the first k k terms as 0 , … , 0 , 0 , 1 0,\ldots,0,0,1 , and satisfies the recurrence relation P n ( k ) = 2 ​ P n − 1 ( k ) + P n − 2 ( k ) + ⋯ + P n − k ( k ) P_{n}^{(k)}=2P_{n-1}^{(k)}+P_{n-2}^{(k)}+\cdots+P_{n-k}^{(k)} for all n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 . A
repdigit
in base 10 is a number composed entirely of repeated digits. Such a number can be represented as

N=\underbrace{\overline{d\ldots d}}_{\ell\text{ times}}=d\left(\frac{10^{\ell}-1}{9}\right),

where
0\leq d\leq 9
and
\ell\geq 1
. Investigations into the Diophantine nature of recurrence sequences, especially concerning their interaction with repdigits, have garnered considerable interest in recent years. Some works have examined whether certain sequence elements can be represented as sums or concatenations of special kinds of numbers.

Initial work by Luca and Banks
[
2
]
provided preliminary insights into such questions, though the scope was limited. In a more specific case,
[
6
]
studied
k
-generalized Pell numbers expressible as repdigits, showing that for this instance, we only have
P_{5}^{(3)}=33
and
P_{6}^{(4)}=88
. This was as a result of extending the work in
[
13
]
.

Recent studies have also focused on palindromic numbers formed from repdigits within linear recurrence sequences. A palindrome is an integer that reads identically forward and backward – for instance 33, 7007, 1220221, or 959. Research into palindromes occurring in recurrence sequences has intensified. Chalebgwa and Ddamulira
[
10
]
found that 151 and 616 are the only Padovan numbers that can be expressed as palindromic concatenations of two distinct repdigits. Likewise, it was shown in
[
11
]
that 595 is the sole such occurrence in the Narayana’s cows sequence. Additional related results can be found in
[
3
]
,
[
5
]
and
[
4
]
. In
[
4
]
, the authors showed that
F_{11}^{(5)}=464
is the only
k
-Fibonacci number that can be written as palindromic concatenations of two distinct repdigits.

Inspired by this growing body of work, we turn our attention to the
k
-Pell sequence. Specifically, we investigate solutions to the Diophantine equation

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(k)}=\overline{\underbrace{d_{1}\ldots d_{1}}_{\ell\text{ times}}\underbrace{d_{2}\ldots d_{2}}_{m\text{ times}}\underbrace{d_{1}\ldots d_{1}}_{\ell\text{ times}}},

(1.1)

where
d_{1},d_{2}\in\{0,1,\dots,9\}
with
d_{1}>0
,
d_{1}\neq d_{2}
, and
\ell,m\geq 1
. That is, we search for those
k
–Pell numbers that are palindromes formed by concatenating two distinct repdigits in a symmetric pattern. Since a number must contain at least three digits to meet this structure, we consider
n\geq 7
throughout the paper. We prove the following.

1.2
Main Results

Theorem 1.1
.

The only
k
-Pell numbers satisfying the Diophantine equation (
1.1
) are

\displaystyle P_{8}^{(3)}=545\qquad\text{and}\qquad P_{7}^{(5)}=232.

The approach used here to prove Theorem
1.1
is as follows.

(a)

We begin by transforming Eq. (
1.1
) into two separate linear forms in logarithms of algebraic numbers, ensuring both are non-zero and relatively small. Then, we apply Matveev’s theorem twice to derive lower bounds on these linear forms, which allows us to obtain polynomial bounds on
n
in terms of the parameter
k
.

(b)

We proceed by splitting two cases depending on
k
, i.e.,
k\leq 1400
and
k>1400
. For the case
k\leq 1400
, we utilize a reduction technique attributed to the LLL-algorithm, to further decrease the size of the bounds, making them more manageable computationally. When
k>1400
, we apply approximations from
[
6
]
, noting that the dominant root of
P_{n}^{(k)}
is very close to
\varphi^{2}
, where

\varphi:=\dfrac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2},

is the golden ratio. This allows us to replace the root with
\varphi
in our logarithmic estimates, thereby obtaining absolute upper bounds for all involved variables. These bounds are then reduced using the LLL-algorithm.

Some identities on
k
-Pell numbers

We begin with an identity from
[

]
, which relates

with the

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(k)}=F_{2n-1},

(2.1)

for all
1\leq n\leq k+1
, while the next term is
P_{k+2}^{(k)}=F_{2k+3}-1

\Psi_{k}(x)=x^{k}-2x^{k-1}-\cdots-x-1.

This polynomial is irreducible in
\mathbb{Q}[x]

\alpha:=\alpha(k)>1
. All the other roots of
\Psi_{k}(x)
are inside the unit circle, see
[
7
]
. The particular root
\alpha
can be found in the interval

\displaystyle\varphi^{2}(1-\varphi^{-k})<\alpha<\varphi^{2},\qquad\text{for}\qquad k\geq 2,

(2.2)

as noted in
[
7
]
. As in the classical case when
k=2
, it was shown in
[
7
]
that

\displaystyle\alpha^{n-2}\leq P_{n}^{(k)}\leq\alpha^{n-1},\qquad\text{holds for all}\qquad n\geq 1,\quad k\geq 2.

(2.3)

Next, we rewrite relation (
1.1
) as

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\overline{\underbrace{d_{1}\ldots d_{1}}_{\ell\text{ times}}\underbrace{d_{2}\ldots d_{2}}_{m\text{ times}}\underbrace{d_{1}\ldots d_{1}}_{\ell\text{ times}}}

\displaystyle=\overline{\underbrace{d_{1}\ldots d_{1}}_{\ell\text{ times}}}\cdot 10^{\ell+m}+\overline{\underbrace{d_{2}\ldots d_{2}}_{m\text{ times}}}\cdot 10^{\ell}+\overline{\underbrace{d_{1}\ldots d_{1}}_{\ell\text{ times}}}

\displaystyle=d_{1}\left(\frac{10^{\ell}-1}{9}\right)\cdot 10^{\ell+m}+d_{2}\left(\frac{10^{m}-1}{9}\right)\cdot 10^{\ell}+d_{1}\left(\frac{10^{\ell}-1}{9}\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{9}\left(d_{1}\cdot 10^{2\ell+m}-(d_{1}-d_{2})\cdot 10^{\ell+m}+(d_{1}-d_{2})\cdot 10^{\ell}-d_{1}\right),

(2.4)

where
d_{1},d_{2}\in\{0,1,2,\ldots,9\}
,
d_{1}>0
and
d_{1}\neq d_{2}
. Since we are working under the assumption that

, then equation (

) together with (

) imply that

\displaystyle\alpha^{n-1}

\displaystyle\geq P_{n}^{(k)}=\frac{1}{9}\left(d_{1}\cdot 10^{2\ell+m}-(d_{1}-d_{2})\cdot 10^{\ell+m}+(d_{1}-d_{2})\cdot 10^{\ell}-d_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{9}\left(1\cdot 10^{2\ell+m}-(9-0)\cdot 10^{\ell+m}+(1-9)\cdot 10^{\ell}-9\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{9}\cdot 10^{2\ell+m}\left(1-\frac{9}{10^{\ell}}-\frac{8}{10^{\ell+m}}-\frac{9}{10^{2\ell+m}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{9}\cdot 10^{2\ell+m}\left(1-\frac{9}{10^{1}}-\frac{8}{10^{2}}-\frac{9}{10^{3}}\right)

\displaystyle>10^{2\ell+m-3},

where we have used the fact that

,

and

are positive integers. Taking logarithms on both sides yields

\displaystyle 2\ell+m<n,

(2.5)

holds for all

\displaystyle\alpha^{n-2}

\displaystyle\leq P_{n}^{(k)}<10^{2\ell+m},

has

\displaystyle n<6(2\ell+m)+2.

(2.6)

Lastly, for

\displaystyle f_{k}(x):=\dfrac{x-1}{(k+1)x^{2}-3kx+k-1}=\dfrac{x-1}{k(x^{2}-3x+1)+x^{2}-1}.

In Lemma 1 of

\displaystyle 0.276<f_{k}(\alpha)<0.5\qquad\text{and}\qquad|f_{k}(\alpha_{i})|<1,

3
Methods

3.1
Linear forms in logarithms

We apply four instances of lower bounds due to Baker for nonzero linear forms in three logarithms of algebraic numbers. Various results of this kind exist in the literature, such as those by Baker and Wüstholz
[
1
]
, and Matveev
[
8
]
. Prior to stating these inequalities, we introduce the concept of the height of an algebraic number, which we define below.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\gamma
be an algebraic number of degree
d
with minimal primitive polynomial over the integers

a_{0}x^{d}+a_{1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+a_{d}=a_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\gamma^{(i)}),

where the leading coefficient
a_{0}
is positive. Then, the logarithmic height of
\gamma
is given by

h(\gamma):=\dfrac{1}{d}\Big(\log a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\max\{|\gamma^{(i)}|,1\}\Big).

In particular, if
\gamma
is a rational number represented as
\gamma:=p/q
with coprime integers
p
and
q\geq 1
, then
h(\gamma)=\log\max\{|p|,q\}
.
The following properties of the logarithmic height function
h(\cdot)
will be used in the rest of the paper without further reference:

\displaystyle h(\gamma_{1}\pm\gamma_{2})

\displaystyle\leq h(\gamma_{1})+h(\gamma_{2})+\log 2;

\displaystyle h(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{\pm 1})

\displaystyle\leq h(\gamma_{1})+h(\gamma_{2});

\displaystyle h(\gamma^{s})

\displaystyle=|s|h(\gamma)\quad{\text{\rm valid for}}\quad s\in\mathbb{Z}.

With these properties, it was proved in
[
6
, Lemma 3]
that

\displaystyle h\left(f_{k}(\alpha)\right)<4k\log\varphi+k\log(k+1),\qquad\text{for all}\qquad k\geq 2.

(3.1)

A linear form in logarithms is an expression

\Lambda:=b_{1}\log\gamma_{1}+\cdots+b_{t}\log\gamma_{t},

where for us
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{t}
are positive real algebraic numbers and
b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}
are nonzero integers. We assume,
\Lambda\neq 0
. We need lower bounds
for
|\Lambda|
. We write
{\mathbb{K}}:={\mathbb{Q}}(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{t})
and
D
for the degree of
{\mathbb{K}}
.
We start with the general form due to Matveev, see Theorem 9.4 in
[
8
]
.

Theorem 3.1

(Matveev, see Theorem 9.4 in
[
8
]
)
.

Put
\Gamma:=\gamma_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots\gamma_{t}^{b_{t}}-1=e^{\Lambda}-1
. Assume
\Gamma\neq 0
. Then

\log|\Gamma|>-1.4\cdot 30^{t+3}\cdot t^{4.5}\cdot D^{2}(1+\log D)(1+\log B)A_{1}\cdots A_{t},

where
B\geq\max\{|b_{1}|,\ldots,|b_{t}|\}
and
A_{i}\geq\max\{Dh(\gamma_{i}),|\log\gamma_{i}|,0.16\}
for
i=1,\ldots,t
.

3.2
Reduction methods

The bounds derived from Matveev’s theorem are often too large to be directly useful for explicit computations. To overcome this limitation, we employ a refinement technique involving the LLL-algorithm. Before explaining this approach, we provide some background.

Let
k
be a positive integer. A subset
\mathcal{L}
of the
k
-dimensional real vector space
\mathbb{R}^{k}
is defined as a
lattice
if there exists a basis
\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}b_{i}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}b_{i}\mid r_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

We say that
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
form a basis for
\mathcal{L}
, or that they span
\mathcal{L}
. We
call
k
the rank of
\mathcal{L}
. The determinant
\text{det}(\mathcal{L})
, of
\mathcal{L}
is defined by

\displaystyle\text{det}(\mathcal{L})=|\det(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k})|,

with the
b_{i}
’s being written as column vectors. This is a positive real number that does not depend on the choice of the basis (see
[
9
]
, Section 1.2).

Given linearly independent vectors
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we refer back to the Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization technique. This method allows us to inductively define vectors
b^{*}_{i}
(with
1\leq i\leq k
) and real coefficients
\mu_{i,j}
(for
1\leq j\leq i\leq k
). Specifically,

\displaystyle b^{*}_{i}

\displaystyle=b_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mu_{i,j}b^{*}_{j},~~~\mu_{i,j}=\dfrac{\langle b_{i},b^{*}_{j}\rangle}{\langle b^{*}_{j},b^{*}_{j}\rangle},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the ordinary inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Notice that
b^{*}_{i}
is the orthogonal projection of
b_{i}
on the orthogonal complement of the span of
b_{1},\ldots,b_{i-1}
, and that
\mathbb{R}b_{i}
is orthogonal to the span of
b^{*}_{1},\ldots,b^{*}_{i-1}
for
1\leq i\leq k
. It follows that
b^{*}_{1},b^{*}_{2},\ldots,b^{*}_{k}
is an orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Definition 3.2
.

The basis
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}
for the lattice
\mathcal{L}
is called reduced if

\displaystyle\|\mu_{i,j}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq j<i\leq n,~~\text{and}

\displaystyle\|b^{*}_{i}+\mu_{i,i-1}b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{4}\|b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1<i\leq n,

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the ordinary Euclidean length. The constant
{3}/{4}
above is arbitrarily chosen, and may be replaced by any fixed real number
y
in the interval
{1}/{4}<y<1

(see
[
15
]
, Section 1)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a
k-
dimensional lattice with reduced basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
and denote by
B
the matrix with columns
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
.
We define

l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\min_{x\in\mathcal{L}}||x-y||\quad;\quad y\not\in\mathcal{L}\\
\min_{0\neq x\in\mathcal{L}}||x||\quad~~;\quad y\in\mathcal{L}\end{array}\right.,

where
||\cdot||
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. It is well established that the LLL–algorithm can be used to efficiently compute a lower bound for
l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)
in polynomial time. Specifically, it provides a positive constant
c_{1}
such that
l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)\geq c_{1}
(see
[
16
]
, Section V.4).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
z=B^{-1}y
with
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})^{T}
. Furthermore,

(i)

if
y\not\in\mathcal{L}
, let
i_{0}
be the largest index such that
z_{i_{0}}\neq 0
and put
\lambda:=\{z_{i_{0}}\}
, where
\{\cdot\}
denotes the distance to the nearest integer.

(ii)

if
y\in\mathcal{L}
, put
\lambda:=1
.

Then, we have

c_{1}:=\max\limits_{1\leq j\leq k}\left\{\dfrac{||b_{1}||}{||b_{j}^{*}||}\right\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\delta:=\lambda\dfrac{||b_{1}||}{c_{1}}.

In our application, we are given real numbers
\eta_{0},\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k}
which are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
and two positive constants
c_{3}
and
c_{4}
such that

\displaystyle|\eta_{0}+x_{1}\eta_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}\eta_{k}|\leq c_{3}\exp(-c_{4}H),

(3.2)

where the integers
x_{i}
are bounded as
|x_{i}|\leq X_{i}
with
X_{i}
given upper bounds for
1\leq i\leq k
. We write
X_{0}:=\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq k}\{X_{i}\}
. The basic idea in such a situation, from
[
12
]
, is to approximate the linear form (
3.2
) by an approximation lattice. So, we consider the lattice
\mathcal{L}
generated by the columns of the matrix

\mathcal{A}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\ldots&0&0\\
0&1&\ldots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\ldots&1&0\\
\lfloor C\eta_{1}\rfloor&\lfloor C\eta_{2}\rfloor&\ldots&\lfloor C\eta_{k-1}\rfloor&\lfloor C\eta_{k}\rfloor\end{pmatrix},

where
C
is a large constant usually of the size of about
X_{0}^{k}
. Let us assume that we have an LLL–reduced basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
of
\mathcal{L}
and that we have a lower bound
l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)\geq c_{1}
with
y:=(0,0,\ldots,-\lfloor C\eta_{0}\rfloor)
. Note that
c_{1}
can be computed by using the results of Lemma
3.1
. Then, with these notations the following result is Lemma VI.1 in
[
16
]
.

Lemma 3.2

(Lemma VI.1 in
[
16
]
)
.

Let
S:=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}X_{i}^{2}
and
T:=\dfrac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}}{2}
. If
\delta^{2}\geq T^{2}+S
, then inequality (
3.2
) implies that we either have
x_{1}=x_{2}=\cdots=x_{k-1}=0
and
x_{k}=-\dfrac{\lfloor C\eta_{0}\rfloor}{\lfloor C\eta_{k}\rfloor}
, or

H\leq\dfrac{1}{c_{4}}\left(\log(Cc_{3})-\log\left(\sqrt{\delta^{2}-S}-T\right)\right).

4.1

In this case,

\displaystyle F_{2n-1}=\overline{\underbrace{d_{1}\ldots d_{1}}_{\ell\text{ times}}\underbrace{d_{2}\ldots d_{2}}_{m\text{ times}}\underbrace{d_{1}\ldots d_{1}}_{\ell\text{ times}}}.

This Diophantine equation was studied in
[

4.2
The case

We proceed by proving a series of results. We start with the following.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(\ell,m,n,k)

) with

,

\ell<4.3\cdot 10^{12}k^{5}(\log k)^{2}\log n.

Proof.

Let

and

1.1
) with
n\geq k+2
and

. We rewrite (

) using (

\displaystyle\left|\dfrac{1}{9}\left(d_{1}\cdot 10^{2\ell+m}-(d_{1}-d_{2})\cdot 10^{\ell+m}+(d_{1}-d_{2})\cdot 10^{\ell}-d_{1}\right)-f_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{n}\right|<\dfrac{1}{2}.

Therefore, for all
\ell
,

, we have

\displaystyle\left|f_{k}(\alpha)\alpha^{n}-\dfrac{1}{9}\left(d_{1}\cdot 10^{2\ell+m}\right)\right|

\displaystyle<\dfrac{1}{2}+\dfrac{1}{9}\left(|d_{1}-d_{2}|\cdot 10^{\ell+m}+|d_{1}-d_{2}|\cdot 10^{\ell}+d_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{2}+10^{\ell+m}+10^{\ell}+1<1.2\cdot 10^{\ell+m}.

Dividing through both sides of the above inequality by

\displaystyle\left|\Gamma_{1}\right|:=\left|\dfrac{9f_{k}(\alpha)}{d_{1}}\cdot 10^{-2\ell-m}\alpha^{n}-1\right|

\displaystyle<11\cdot 10^{-\ell}.

(4.2)

Notice that
\Gamma_{1}\neq 0

def k_pell_sequence(k, n_max):
"""Generates the k-Pell sequence up to n_max terms."""
P = [0] * (k - 1) + [1] # P_{2-k} to P_1
for _ in range(n_max - len(P) + 1):
term = 2 * P[-1] + sum(P[-2:-k-1:-1]) # 2*P_{n-1} + P_{n-2} + ... + P_{n-k}
P.append(term)
return P
def is_palindromic_concatenation(num):
s = str(num)
n = len(s)
for l in range(1, n // 2 + 1):
m = n - 2 * l
if m <= 0:
continue
part1 = s[:l]
part2 = s[l:l + m]
part3 = s[l + m:]
if (
part1 == part3
and len(set(part1)) == 1
and len(set(part2)) == 1
and part1 != part2
):
return True
return False
def find_all_palindromic_k_pells(k_min=2, k_max=1400, n_max=2288):
results = []
for k in range(k_min, k_max + 1):
pells = k_pell_sequence(k, n_max)
for i, val in enumerate(pells):
math_n = i + 2 - k # Convert index to actual P_n^{(k)}
if math_n < 2:
continue
if val > 0 and is_palindromic_concatenation(val):
results.append((k, math_n, val))
return results
