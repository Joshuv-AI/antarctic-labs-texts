Giant skull base meningiomas: Definitions, case reports, and surgical approaches
Authors: Breno Nery, Raívson Fernandes, Alencar Neto Joaquim Fechine de, Emanuella Arruda do Rego Nóbrega, Emilly Beatriz Aleixo Almeida, Eduardo Quaggio, Alvaro Campero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Giant skull base meningiomas are rare and surgically demanding due to proximity to critical neurovascular structures, prolonged operative time, and reduced resection rates. Reports encompassing multiple skull base regions remain limited. This case-based series includes anterior, middle, and posterior fossa tumors, emphasizing anatomical diversity and surgical strategies.Case Description: Eleven patients with giant skull base meningiomas were retrospectively reviewed, including tumors ≥5 cm and surgically complex lesions. The mean age was 47.7 years (range 32–62), with a female predominance (8 females, 3 males). Presenting symptoms included visual loss, headache, anosmia, hemiparesis, and cranial nerve deficits. Approaches were tailored to tumor location, most commonly pterional and retrosigmoid, followed by bifrontal and combined skull base craniotomies. Gross total resection was achieved in 8 of 11 patients (72.7%). Near-total or subtotal resection (3/11) was performed when tumors encased critical neurovascular structures. Visual outcomes were favorable, with recovery or preservation in most anterior and middle fossa cases. Transient cranial nerve palsies occurred in two patients and resolved on follow-up. No postoperative mortality occurred. During follow-up ranging from 4 months to 5 years, no recurrences were observed, and residual disease remained stable.Conclusion: This series illustrates the anatomical spectrum and surgical complexity of giant skull base meningiomas. Favorable outcomes were achieved with individualized approaches and multidisciplinary planning. These cases contribute practical insights to the limited literature and reinforce the need for tailored management strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Meningiomas are primary central nervous system tumors that arise from arachnoid cap cells and are usually attached to the dura mater.[18] They account for 24–30% of all primary intracranial tumors, with an annual incidence of 2.3 cases/100,000 inhabitants.[11,12] Although they may occur at any age, they are most prevalent in individuals over 65 years old.[11]

Giant meningiomas are generally defined as tumors measuring ≥5 cm in maximum diameter, representing about 10–15% of all intracranial meningiomas.[23,39] However, there is no single definition, as the threshold may vary according to tumor topography. For example, in the anterior clinoid region, some authors classify tumors measuring ≥4 cm as giant, due to the limited anatomical space and early neurovascular compromise. Conversely, in locations such as the posterior fossa or the foramen magnum, the absolute size is less relevant than the degree of neurovascular conflict and compression of critical structures. Thus, while the ≥5 cm cutoff is widely used, alternative criteria, based on size (4–5 cm depending on location) or on clinical-anatomical complexity, are equally valid in specific contexts.

These tumors are typically associated with invasion of adjacent structures, prolonged surgical times, higher complication rates, and reduced likelihood of achieving gross total resection (GTR), particularly at the skull base.[29] Their classification according to location (anterior, middle, or posterior fossae) reflects not only anatomical boundaries but also distinct surgical challenges, given the proximity to eloquent neurovascular structures.[29] Giant skull base meningiomas, therefore, represent a clinically significant subgroup, in which both tumor dimension and anatomical involvement dictate surgical strategy and prognosis.[39]

Despite advances in microsurgical and minimally invasive techniques, there is still no consensus on the optimal management of these complex tumors.[39] Furthermore, the literature remains limited, with most studies focusing on specific populations or surgical approaches, leaving gaps in understanding their clinical, radiological, and prognostic characteristics.[23,27]

This study seeks to contribute to this field by presenting a series of case reports on giant meningiomas in different regions of the skull base. Through this analysis, we aim to discuss clinical presentations, surgical approaches, intraoperative challenges, complications, and review the current literature, thereby providing a more comprehensive perspective to support future management strategies.

CASE REPORT

A total of 11 patients with giant skull base meningiomas were analyzed (4 anterior fossa, 3 middle fossa, and 4 posterior fossa). The mean age was 47.7 years (range: 32–62 years), with a predominance of females (8 females, 3 males). The main clinical presentation was progressive visual loss, followed by headache, cognitive/behavioral changes, and cranial nerve deficits. The overall GTR (Simpson I–II) rate was 69%. Complications were generally transient, with no new permanent neurological deficits. Visual function was the most relevant functional outcome, with complete recovery in 5 patients, partial recovery in 3, and stability in 3. No patient experienced surgical mortality or severe infectious complications. Table 1 summarizes the main surgical and clinical outcomes.

DISCUSSION

This study was designed as a retrospective case series, including 11 cases of skull base meningiomas that underwent surgical treatment over the past 10 years at Hospital Beneficência Portuguesa de Ribeirão Preto, São Paulo, Brazil, and Hospital Ángel Cruz Padilla, San Miguel de Tucumán, Argentina. All patients underwent tumor resection, performed through different approaches according to the anatomical location of the lesion.

At our center, the preferred strategy for giant skull base meningiomas is aggressive single-stage resection, aiming for GTR whenever feasible. When tumors encase critical neurovascular structures, a near-total or subtotal resection is intentionally performed to minimize morbidity, preserving neurological function.

Inclusion criteria were tumors classified as giant, with a diameter ≥5 cm, except in the anterior clinoid region, where those with a diameter ≥4 cm were considered giant. In certain locations, such as the posterior fossa and the foramen magnum, the most relevant parameter was not absolute size but rather the degree of neurovascular involvement. Tumor consistency (soft, fibrous, calcified) was assessed using MRI and CT criteria. Vascular or cranial nerve encasement was defined as circumferential contact >180° with displacement on imaging. Skull base subregions were standardized as anterior fossa (olfactory groove, sphenoid plane, tuberculum sellae), middle fossa (sphenoid wing/clinoidal, cavernous sinus), and posterior fossa (petroclival, cerebellopontine angle, tentorial, foramen magnum). Surgical approaches were reported according to established nomenclature (pterional, fronto-orbital, frontolateral, bifrontal, retrosigmoid, extreme-lateral, anterior petrosal/Kawase, among others).

Clinical and radiological data were collected from medical records and imaging studies. Demographic information, presenting symptoms, neurological findings, tumor characteristics, type of surgical approach, extent of resection (Simpson grade), and postoperative complications were recorded. Primary outcomes included the extent of resection (gross total, near-total, or subtotal) and postoperative neurological status. Secondary outcomes included recurrence or regrowth, documented by radiological follow-up when available, as well as overall survival. Given the descriptive nature of this study, no formal statistical hypothesis testing was performed. Results are presented descriptively, with frequencies, means, and ranges when appropriate. Information regarding the WHO histopathological grade and the Ki-67 proliferative index was not available in all cases and was reported only when included in the medical records.

The study was conducted in accordance with ethical principles, and all patients provided informed consent for the use of clinical data and imaging.

The mean patient age was 47.7 years (range: 32–62 years), with a clear female predominance (8 females and 3 males), consistent with previously published epidemiological data.[11,39] The most common clinical manifestations were progressive visual loss, headache, anosmia, cognitive and behavioral changes, and cranial nerve deficits, depending on the tumor’s anatomical location.

Regarding surgical management, the pterional craniotomy was the most frequently used approach (5 cases), followed by the retrosigmoid approach (3 cases) and bifrontal craniotomy (2 cases). Other strategies, such as far-lateral, transsphenoidal, and combined approaches, were chosen based on individual tumor anatomy. The predominance of the pterional approach can be explained by its versatility and direct access to anterior and middle skull base structures, including the optic nerve, chiasm, carotid artery, sphenoid wing, and parasellar region. This approach allows early devascularization, safe tumor debulking, and early identification of critical neurovascular structures, particularly valuable in giant tumors with mass effect or encasement. The surgical approach was tailored according to tumor size, neurovascular involvement, and the need to minimize brain retraction or cranial nerve manipulation. GTR (Simpson Grades I–II) was achieved in most cases (8 of 11 patients), while near-total resection was performed when tumors were adherent to or encased critical structures such as the optic nerves, cavernous sinus, or brainstem.

Postoperative outcomes were assessed using standardized measures. Visual function was evaluated by best-corrected visual acuity and automated perimetry; cranial nerve function by the House–Brackmann and Medical Research Council scales; and global functional status by the Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS). Complications were classified according to a modified Clavien–Dindo system adapted for neurosurgery. In this series, five patients achieved complete recovery of visual acuity, three showed partial visual field improvement, and the others remained stable. One patient experienced transient cranial nerve palsy (House– Brackmann IV), which resolved within 5 months, while anosmia persisted in two anterior fossa tumors. Mean KPS at last follow-up was 80.

Our findings are consistent with previous series, in which GTR rates for giant skull base meningiomas range from 55% to 75% depending on tumor site and consistency – higher for olfactory groove and clinoidal tumors, and lower for petroclival and cavernous sinus lesions.[23,29,39] In our cohort, GTR was achieved in 9 of 13 patients (69%), consistent with these reports. Similarly, permanent neurological deficits are reported in 10–25% of cases in the literature, whereas in our series, no new permanent visual deficits occurred, and only one transient cranial nerve palsy was observed. Recurrence rates after subtotal resection in other studies reach 20–35% at 5 years, particularly in the WHO Grade II tumors or those with Ki-67 >4%.[27] No recurrence was observed during our follow-up period, although longer surveillance is warranted.

Therefore, the main contribution of this series is not prognostic or survival analysis, but rather the description of technical nuances and surgical challenges across different skull base regions.

Giant anterior fossa meningiomas

Giant meningiomas of the anterior cranial fossa are relatively rare, accounting for approximately 8.8% of all meningioma cases.[1] Anatomically, the anterior cranial fossa is the most superficial and superior of the three cranial fossae, primarily formed by the orbital portions of the frontal bone, with the medial region composed of the ethmoid bone and posteriorly bordered by the body and lesser wings of the sphenoid bone.[34] The definition of these meningiomas is based on their location relative to the structures of the anterior fossa, primarily assessed by imaging exams such as MRI.[31,32]

It is worth noting that although the ≥5 cm cutoff is most commonly used, in the anterior clinoid region, some authors already consider tumors ≥4 cm as giant, reflecting the limited anatomical space and early neurovascular compromise. The most common meningiomas in this region are as follows: Olfactory groove meningiomas: Located in the olfactory sulcus, these tumors, due to their proximity to the olfactory nerves, may present early anosmia, as well as symptoms such as behavioral changes, personality alterations, and headaches.[1]Tuberculum sellae meningiomas: These arise at the dural attachment of the chiasmatic sulcus, medially to the optic foramina, and are associated with visual symptoms, such as bitemporal hemianopia, which can progress to blindness if untreated.[1,2]Planum sphenoidale meningiomas: Located in the planum sphenoidale, they may mimic characteristics of adjacent tumors, complicating differential diagnosis. They commonly cause bitemporal hemianopia but may also lead to anosmia and behavioral changes, albeit more rarely.[2,14]

The giant meningiomas of the anterior cranial fossa pose substantial surgical challenges due to their size, midline extension, and proximity to critical structures such as the optic nerves, anterior cerebral arteries, and the pituitary stalk.[13,42] The most commonly used approaches are the pterional craniotomy, indicated for tumors with lateral or parasellar extension, and the bifrontal craniotomy, often preferred for large midline olfactory groove meningiomas.[31] Although these approaches offer excellent exposure, they may require manipulation of the frontal lobes, increasing the risk of postoperative cognitive or behavioral changes. In our series, 3 out of 4 patients (75%) underwent GTR (Simpson I–II), while one case required near-total resection due to optic nerve encasement. Persistent anosmia was observed in 2 patients and did not improve postoperatively. No cases of new visual deficits, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) fistula, or significant cognitive decline were observed. While no recurrence has been noted during short-term follow-up (6–18 months), the case with subtotal resection remains under surveillance due to the higher risk of regrowth in partially resected tumors.[41] These results are consistent with the literature, where GTR rates range from 70% to 80% in olfactory groove and planum sphenoidale meningiomas, but anosmia is reported as a frequent and often irreversible complication.[1,31]

The surgical approach aims for maximum tumor resection, relieving compression without compromising neurological function, especially vision, olfaction, and cognition.[17] The Simpson classification assesses the extent of resection, with Grade 1 representing complete resection and Grade 5 indicating only decompression.[35] The choice of surgical approach depends on the tumor’s size, location, and extent, as well as the patient’s clinical condition. The bilateral subfrontal approach is preferred for giant olfactory meningiomas, providing broad access without manipulating the optic nerves; however, it carries the risk of frontal lobe retraction.[17] For GTSMs, the pterional or frontolateral approaches allow early decompression of the optic chiasm, whereas meningiomas of the planum sphenoidale require special attention due to their proximity to the optic nerve and the internal carotid artery.[21] Factors such as neurovascular adhesion, tumor consistency (soft, fibrous, calcified), and peritumoral edema increase surgical risks, including permanent deficits and hemorrhage.[1,39] The main postoperative complication is visual deficit, especially in meningiomas of the tuberculum sellae and planum sphenoidale, due to their proximity to critical visual structures.[17] CSF fistulas and infections are other potential complications, with fistulas being more common after endoscopic approaches.[1]

Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) is effective in controlling residual tumors near critical areas, achieving tumor control rates of up to 90% over 10 years.[28] Radiotherapy is reserved for atypical or malignant meningiomas and cases where surgery is contraindicated.[26] This non-invasive technique uses highly focused radiation beams to treat intracranial lesions, aiming to prevent tumor progression, prolong the recurrence-free interval, improve survival, and reduce complications associated with fractionated radiotherapy. In agreement with previous studies, we consider SRS particularly valuable in residual anterior fossa tumors, where reoperation carries a high risk of visual decline.[26]

GMFMs

GMFMs are rare tumors, defined by dimensions >5–6 cm in diameter or a tumor volume exceeding 70 cm3.[9] Although most are histologically benign (WHO Grade I), their large tumor volume and close relationship with critical neurovascular structures make their management particularly challenging. The middle cranial fossa is bounded anteriorly by the sphenoid wing, medially by the cavernous sinus, laterally by the convex dura mater, and posteriorly by the petrous ridge and the tentorium.[36] Its bowl-like anatomical configuration allows substantial tumor expansion without early parenchymal compression, thus delaying clinical manifestation.

GMFMs can be classified according to their dural attachment:

The clinical presentation depends on the tumor’s location: Lateral tumors (greater sphenoid wing) may remain asymptomatic until they invade medial structures; medial tumors (clinoidal, suprasellar) manifest earlier with visual deficits (optic nerve compression), headaches, endocrine dysfunctions, and oculomotor and facial nerve deficits.[8] Despite their size, many of these tumors do not induce intracranial hypertension, due to the brain’s gradual adaptation. In the series by Champagne et al., 50% of patients with giant sphenoid wing meningiomas showed no significant preoperative cerebral edema.[9]

These tumors typically exhibit low mitotic activity, yet their size and extensive anatomical involvement complicate management. Their proximity to major intracranial vessels (internal carotid artery, middle cerebral artery, anterior cerebral artery) and cranial nerves (III, IV, V, VI) increases the risk of neurological deficits and ischemic complications (Louis DN, 2021). In addition, while the general cutoff of ≥5–6 cm is accepted, some authors suggest that tumor volume or vascular encasement may be a more reliable indicator of surgical complexity in this region.

The treatment of choice for GMFMs remains surgical resection; however, the approach must be individualized based on tumor characteristics and patient-specific anatomical considerations. An extended pterional craniotomy is often employed to provide wide lateral and medial access to the middle fossa. The opening of the sylvian fissure further facilitates access to deep-seated tumor components. In cases where the optic nerve is compromised, decompression of the optic canal is indicated to preserve visual function. Early extradural dissection at the cavernous sinus is recommended to minimize neurovascular manipulation and reduce the risk of intraoperative complications. In selected cases with suprasellar extension, endoscopic-assisted techniques may serve as valuable adjuncts to conventional microsurgical approaches.[19]

Tumor consistency (soft, fibrous, calcified) significantly influences surgical outcomes; lesions with a soft consistency are generally more amenable to complete resection, whereas dense calcifications and vascular encasement often limit the extent of safe tumor removal. In specialized centers, GTR rates (Simpson Grades I–II) range from 60% to 70%. When complete resection is deemed unsafe due to anatomical constraints, subtotal resection followed by adjuvant radiotherapy is typically pursued, and SRS may be employed for controlling small residual tumor remnants. Recurrence occurs in approximately 20–30% of cases over a 10-year period, being notably more frequent in subtotally resected tumors.[37] Postoperative functional outcomes are largely determined by the degree of preoperative neural and vascular involvement. Our results were consistent with these findings, as vascular encasement and tumor consistency were the main limiting factors to complete resection, in line with previously published series.[9,37]

Advances in preoperative imaging have significantly enhanced surgical planning and risk stratification. Functional MRI enables mapping of eloquent cortical areas, while diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) facilitates identification of critical white matter tracts, thereby aiding in the preservation of neurological function. Magnetic resonance angiography provides a detailed assessment of the tumor’s relationship with major intracranial vessels. Nevertheless, despite the improvements in imaging technologies, the true extent of vascular invasion often becomes fully evident only during intraoperative exploration.[20]

GMFMs continue to represent a formidable neurosurgical challenge. As reported in several large series, maximal safe resection remains the standard of care, with adjuvant radiotherapy or radiosurgery reserved for residual tumors, emphasizing functional preservation over aggressive radical removal. Successful management relies on meticulous surgical strategies, integration of advanced imaging modalities, and, when necessary, the application of multimodal adjuvant therapies to achieve durable disease control while preserving neurological function.

Giant posterior fossa meningiomas

Posterior fossa meningiomas account for approximately 10% of all intracranial meningiomas and are predominantly located in the convexity and cerebellopontine angle, with less frequent occurrences in the petroclival and foramen magnum regions.[24] When they reach large dimensions, they are classified as giant meningiomas, often remaining clinically silent until they exert substantial mass effect on adjacent neurovascular structures. However, in the posterior fossa and foramen magnum, the definition of “giant” is not strictly based on size, but rather on the degree of neurovascular conflict, particularly brainstem compression and cranial nerve involvement, which are the main determinants of surgical complexity.

Giant posterior fossa meningiomas are most frequently found in three principal locations as follows: Petroclival meningiomas intimately involve the brainstem, cranial nerves, and major vessels, making resection particularly challenging and increasing the risk of postoperative neurological deficits. They often extend superiorly into the middle fossa, necessitating combined skull base approaches.[16]Cerebellopontine angle meningiomas typically compress cranial nerves VII and VIII, resulting in hearing loss, facial paresthesia, vertigo, and cerebellar ataxia, characterizing the cerebellopontine angle syndrome.[15]Foramen magnum meningiomas, arising at the craniocervical junction, compress the lower cranial nerves and vertebral arteries, leading to a complex constellation of symptoms, including dysphagia, shoulder weakness, and quadriparesis.[24]

Recent studies highlight that giant posterior fossa meningiomas exhibit a more insidious clinical course compared to their supratentorial counterparts, owing to the relatively larger cisternal spaces that initially accommodate tumor expansion. However, once critical thresholds are exceeded, symptoms can progress rapidly, particularly due to compression of the brainstem and the development of obstructive hydrocephalus. Notably, early hydrocephalus, caused by fourth ventricular compression, is now recognized as an independent poor prognostic factor in giant posterior fossa meningiomas, correlating with worse surgical outcomes and prolonged postoperative recovery.[5]

Clinically, cranial nerve deficits dominate the presentation. Dysfunction of cranial nerves V, VII, VIII, IX, and X is common, leading to hearing loss, facial weakness, dysphagia, and gait disturbances.[6] Progressive brainstem compression may result in long tract signs and even respiratory dysfunction in extreme cases. Furthermore, recent findings suggest that cognitive and behavioral changes, historically underappreciated in posterior fossa tumors, can also arise in the setting of brainstem-thalamic diaschisis caused by chronic mass effect.[10]

Surgical treatment of giant posterior fossa meningiomas requires careful planning. The modified far lateral (2 cases) approach remains the cornerstone for foramen magnum lesions, providing excellent access and minimizing brainstem retraction. For cerebellopontine angle tumors, the retrosigmoid approach (3 cases), either in a sitting position or on a park bench, remains standard. However, in recent years, retrosigmoid and extreme lateral approaches assisted by expanded endoscopy have been increasingly used, allowing better visualization around critical cranial nerves with reduced cerebellar retraction and lower morbidity.[3]

When tumors extend into both the middle and posterior fossae, staged combined approaches, often involving retrosigmoid and extended subtemporal or Kawase’s anterior petrosectomy, have been advocated.[38] Early devascularization of the tumor, particularly via anterior petrosectomy, has been shown to improve the safety and extent of resection while preserving cranial nerve function.[16] Despite surgical advances, complete resection of giant posterior fossa meningiomas remains difficult. Total removal often carries a significant risk of cranial nerve injury or brainstem infarction. Consistent with other series, our experience confirms that GTR rates rarely exceed 50–60% in petroclival and foramen magnum meningiomas, precisely because of their intimate relationship with the brainstem and lower cranial nerves.[16,38,40]

Modern management strategies advocate a functional approach: Maximal safe resection, preserving neurological function, followed by SRS for residual tumor control. This combined strategy has been supported by multiple studies, showing tumor control rates above 85% at 5 years when SRS is used for residual or recurrent posterior fossa tumors, and lower morbidity compared with radical attempts at total removal.[3,33] Importantly, in giant tumors, staged resection combined with delayed radiosurgery has been associated with better functional outcomes and lower recurrence rates compared to aggressive single-stage attempts at GTR.[40]

Advanced imaging modalities such as DTI and high-resolution constructive interference in steady-state MRI sequences have become essential in preoperative planning, allowing detailed mapping of cranial nerve relationships and vascular encasement.[4,33] Preoperative functional assessment, including brainstem auditory evoked potentials and lower cranial nerve electromyography, is increasingly recommended to predict and monitor perioperative neurological risks. In cases where subtotal resection is performed, regular radiological surveillance is crucial. Tumor regrowth rates are higher for giant posterior fossa meningiomas compared to smaller counterparts, particularly in residuals at the petroclival region. Our results are consistent with these observations, as no new permanent cranial nerve deficits were observed, and residual disease was managed conservatively with close follow-up, in accordance with the functional preservation approach advocated in the literature.

Ultimately, the management of giant posterior fossa meningiomas requires a nuanced, multidisciplinary strategy focused not only on tumor removal but also on preserving neurological integrity, maintaining CSF dynamics, and optimizing long-term functional outcomes.

CONCLUSION

Our findings underscore the anatomical and surgical complexity of giant skull base meningiomas and the need for individualized, multidisciplinary planning. Through detailed documentation of clinical presentations, surgical approaches, and outcomes across anterior, middle, and posterior fossa tumors, this series provides practical insights for managing these challenging lesions. Despite advances in microsurgical techniques, optimal management remains case-dependent, particularly when critical neurovascular structures are involved. By presenting a broad spectrum of cases from a single center, our study contributes valuable data to the limited literature. It highlights the importance of tailoring surgical strategies to maximize safe resection and preserve neurological function.
