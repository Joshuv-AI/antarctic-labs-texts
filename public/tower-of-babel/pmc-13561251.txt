Management of Refractory Lymphedema of Perineum and Lower Limbs
Authors: Chunchi Chuang, Honda Hsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Lymphedema is a chronic, progressive condition characterized by the accumulation of lymphatic fluid, followed by fat deposition and tissue fibrosis. It can also lead to local immune compromise and, in some cases, cutaneous malignancies. Here, we report a case of a patient with stage III combined perineal and lower limb lymphedema secondary to trauma. The patient was treated with lymphaticovenous anastomoses in the suprapubic region and both lower limbs, along with excision of excess skin and tissue in the perineal area. Optimal clinical improvement was achieved through this staged approach. This case emphasizes that although lymphaticovenous anastomosis effectively addresses extremity edema, radical excision remains essential for fibrotic genital elephantiasis. Comprehensive postoperative management is crucial for maintaining long-term results and minimizing recurrence.

Lymphedema is a chronic, progressive disorder characterized by lymphatic fluid accumulation, fat deposition, fibrosis, and local immune dysfunction, which may occasionally lead to cutaneous malignancies. Lymphaticovenous anastomosis (LVA) is widely accepted as a first-line surgical treatment due to its minimally invasive nature and efficacy, even in advanced disease stages.1,2 However, managing concurrent extremity and severe genital elephantiasis remains a significant clinical challenge. This report describes the successful use of a combined, staged LVA and excisional approach to treat a patient presenting with stage III lymphedema simultaneously involving both lower limbs and the perineal region.

CASE REPORT

A 55-year-old man sustained burn injuries to both lower limbs in adolescence. Three years later, he developed progressive leg swelling, diagnosed as lymphedema. Lipectomy was performed on both legs over 30 years ago, but symptoms persisted and eventually extended to the thighs. Despite compression therapy, the lymphedema worsened with recurring cellulitis episodes. Approximately 10 years ago, the patient developed swelling involving the penis and scrotum, progressing to severe elephantiasis that markedly restricted his mobility (Fig. 1).

Magnetic resonance imaging revealed substantial edema and skin thickening in the penis and scrotum, with fat accumulation in both thighs. Lymphoscintigraphy showed no visible lymphatic flow or enhancement of inguinal lymph nodes. The patient initially declined excisional surgery, so LVA was performed first.

First Surgery—LVA

Under general anesthesia and in the supine position, indocyanine green (ICG) dye was injected into the lower limbs and genital area; however, no linear lymphatic channels were visualized via fluorescence imaging by SPY-PHI (Stryker, MI). Using ultrasound (Prosound alpha 7, Hitachi, Japan), suitable perforators were located in the suprapubic region and both thighs. Superior-edge-of-the-knee incision points were identified to target high-flow lymphatic vessels in the distal medial thigh. The longitudinal and transverse markings in Figure 2 define these anatomical landmarks, whereas short oblique lines indicate the specific LVA sites.

Post-LVA condition, including suprapubic area and both thighs. The markings visible include the preoperative landmarks for superior edge of the knee incision points and the specific incision sites for LVA.

LVA was performed using a surgical microscope (OPMI Pentero 900, Zeiss, Germany). Three anastomoses were constructed in the suprapubic area, 4 in the right thigh, and 3 in the left thigh using 11-0 or 12-0 nylon sutures (Fig. 2). Following the procedure, compression therapy was applied to the lower limbs, achieved initially with elastic bandages and followed by lifelong medical-grade compression garments (class II/III) after suture removal. After 2 weeks, the scrotum and lower limb circumferences decreased significantly (Table 1), and the tissues became noticeably softer. Recurrence and Second Surgery—Excision

Due to occupational demands as a chief requiring long hours of standing and the anatomical difficulty of maintaining consistent scrotal compression, scrotal swelling recurred 6 months later (Fig. 3). The patient subsequently consented to excisional surgery. Upon readmission, his thighs and legs remained soft and responsive to compression therapy. Laboratory findings were unremarkable except for an elevated C-reactive protein level, and prophylactic antibiotics were initiated.

Six months after LVAs, recurrent lymphedema of perineal region was noted.

Under general anesthesia, a dorsal skin incision exposed the glans. A T-shaped incision was performed from the suprapubic area extending to the inferior penis while preserving partial ventral skin for coverage. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which demonstrates how after the excision procedure, we preserved bilateral spermatic cords and testes. The wounds were closed with a local flap, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/F283.) Dissection was meticulously carried down to the Buck fascia. Under ultrasound guidance, the bilateral spermatic cords and testes were identified and protected. A W-shaped incision was utilized at the ventral scrotum to create local triangular flaps for tension-free wound closure. A total of 7.2 kg of fibrotic tissue was resected, and the wounds were closed with local flaps (Supplemental Digital Content 2, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/F283). Postoperatively, the patient received antibiotic therapy and wound care. Minor skin necrosis required localized debridement under local anesthesia 2 weeks later. He was discharged after 30 days of hospitalization.

Follow-up

At the 6-month follow-up, the patient remained free of perineal swelling, and his legs continued to improve with elevation and compression garments. He noted increased nighttime urination and successfully resumed work, though scrotal compression remained a long-term compliance challenge.

DISCUSSION

LVA is a minimally invasive microsurgical technique connecting lymphatic vessels to venules to bypass obstructed pathways, demonstrating high efficacy in reducing limb volume and decreasing infection rates. However, in advanced stages, progressive lymphosclerosis often complicates outcomes. Mihara et al highlighted that the degree of fibrosis in the lymphatic vessels directly correlates with lymphedema severity.3 Nonetheless, viable channels may still coexist in fibrotic fields, allowing for effective anastomosis and symptomatic relief.

In advanced (stage III) lymphedema, the role of LVA is frequently debated. In this case, although the patient presented with severe fibrosis, we opted for a staged multimodal strategy.4 The initial LVA successfully addressed the fluid component of the limb edema and reduced cellulitis frequency while respecting the patient’s preference. Although LVA significantly reduced limb circumference (Table 1) and softened the tissues, it had a limited impact on the irreversible, fibrotic scrotal elephantiasis. This surgical sequence—physiological bypass followed by anatomical resection—provided a safer, less inflamed surgical field for the massive 7.2-kg resection. Crucially, this clinical trajectory demonstrates that although LVA effectively decompresses extremity fluid, sequential treatment delaying radical perineal excision leads to early gravity-induced recurrence. Thus, a synchronized physiological and anatomical approach should be prioritized for multi-regional disease.

Conversely, although LVA yields good results in early genital lymphedema, its efficacy diminishes in advanced stages with severe fibrosis and tissue distortion.5,6 The literature supports radical excision in such cases to restore function and quality of life.7–9 This case illustrates the clear benefit of a staged approach, allowing targeted treatment tailored to the anatomical severity of each region.4,10 High-resolution ultrasound and ICG imaging played a critical role in locating recipient venules within these heavily fibrotic tissues. However, due to the lack of linear ICG patency, definitively confirming whether multilevel LVAs bypassed distinct or identical lymphatics remained a limitation, highlighting the future necessity of ultra-high-frequency ultrasound for precise mapping.

Despite successful surgery, a long-term management strategy is essential to prevent recurrence. Compression garments are key for the limbs, but anatomical constraints make scrotal compression difficult, which likely contributed to the post-LVA recurrence in our patient. Adjuvant manual lymphatic drainage is highly beneficial for areas that cannot be easily compressed. Long-term follow-up using physical exams and ultrasound helps detect early recurrence. Furthermore, lifestyle adjustments are vital; our patient’s job required prolonged standing, which exacerbated gravity-induced fluid accumulation. Patient education regarding skin care, regular leg elevation, and weight control remains crucial for sustaining long-term outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

Although LVA effectively decompresses extremity fluid, advanced fibrotic perineal lymphedema mandates radical excisional surgery for optimal outcomes. A staged approach combining LVA and excision provides an optimal, tailored solution for complex, multiregion stage III lymphedema. Rigorous postoperative care, including compression, lifestyle adjustments, and regular follow-up, is essential to sustain long-term success.
