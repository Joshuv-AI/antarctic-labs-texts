18F-FAPI PET/CT imaging in pneumoconiosis: a pilot study on a novel tool for early diagnosis and guiding the treatment of pulmonary fibrosis
Authors: Chaofeng Liu, Zhongyuan Guo, Yiwei Shi, Zhifang Wu, Zhenyu Xiang, Tian Yao, Gang Liang, Zhixing Qin, Ruonan Wang, Li Li, Min Guo, Hailong Wang, Min Pang, Sijin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPneumoconiosis, characterized by dust-induced pulmonary fibrosis, lacks reliable methods to assess fibrotic activity. This study aimed to monitor fibroblast activation and identify activated pulmonary fibrosis for early diagnosis and anti-fibrotic therapy response by 18F-fibroblast activation protein inhibitors (FAPI) positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) in pneumoconiosis.MethodsA single-center prospective clinical study was conducted on 6 pneumoconiosis patients and 4 healthy control individuals. The uptake of 18F-FAPI in the pulmonary fibrosis areas of participants and its correlation with pulmonary diffusion function were analyzed. Sprague-Dawley rat experiments were performed on three groups including pneumoconiosis model, pirfenidone-treated, and normal control groups. 18F-FAPI and 18F-fluoro-D-glucose (FDG) PET/CT, histopathologic, and hematological analysis were assessed monthly from modeling until 6 months.ResultsIn our preliminary cohort, compared to the controls, the 18F-FAPI uptake in fibrotic areas of pneumoconiosis patients was higher, and a strong negative correlation with the diffusing function was observed (r = -0.929, P = 0.022). In the pneumoconiosis model, 18F-FAPI activity peaked one month earlier than relative collagen content (%) in Masson trichrome staining and the level of connective tissue growth factor in plasma, an indicator reflecting the fibroblast activation. The uptake of 18F-FAPI in the pirfenidone-treated group significantly decreased compared to the pneumoconiosis group. Additionally, in pneumoconiosis rats, 18F-FDG uptake peaked at Month 3, correlating with progressive inflammation (granuloma formation, elevated interleukin-6 [IL-6]/tumor necrosis factor-α [TNF-α]), while pirfenidone showed timepoint-specific metabolic reduction, no significant difference in the overall mean standardized uptake value (SUVmean) was observed.Conclusions18F-FAPI PET/CT imaging may hold promise for identifying active pulmonary fibrosis and monitoring its progression in pneumoconiosis, suggesting a potential clinical opportunity for targeted anti-fibrotic treatment. These preliminary findings warrant further investigation in larger studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13550-025-01364-x.

Introduction

Pneumoconiosis is a pulmonary interstitial fibrotic disease characterized by structural lung tissue damage and impaired lung function. This condition arises from prolonged inhalation of mineral dust particles with varying pathogenicity [1]. A significant clinical challenge is the lack of reliable, non-invasive methods to distinguish the activity of fibrotic lesions (i.e., ongoing fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix deposition) from stable scar tissue [2, 3]. While the pulmonary inflammation and fibrosis associated with pneumoconiosis can be commonly observed via High-Resolution Computed Tomography (HRCT) in clinical practices [4], it primarily provides structural information and cannot reliably assess fibrotic activity or therapy response [5]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a sensitive and non-invasive method to identify active fibrotic lesions, monitor their progression, and assess therapy responses in pneumoconiosis, functionalities beyond the capability of HRCT.

The search for a solution has converged on fibroblast activation protein (FAP), a key biomarker upregulated during the profibrotic activity of human interstitial lung disease (ILD), and single-cell and immunohistochemical analyses confirm that FAP expression closely correlates with active fibrotic lesions in human ILDs [3]. This biological insight has been leveraged for imaging, with FAP inhibitors (FAPIs) developed as radiopharmaceuticals for in vivo positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT), initially in malignant tumors [6], and now extensively in fibrotic diseases (ILD) [7]. Recent studies across various ILDs, including systemic sclerosis [7], connective tissue disease-associated ILD (CTD-ILD) [8], and post-acute sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC) [9], validate that FAPI PET/CT uptake correlates with disease progression and fibrotic activity. Importantly, [68Ga]Ga-FAPI PET/CT has demonstrated the ability to evaluate fibrotic activity by distinguishing among populations of resident fibroblasts, active fibroblasts, and mature scar tissue—based on HRCT patterns in ILD patients [9], underscoring its superior functional characterization beyond structure [10]. Exploratory studies in various fibrotic ILDs (fILD) and murine models further confirm that FAPI PET/CT can visualize FAP expression in fibrotic lesions [11], and it shows promise for risk stratification in myositis-associated ILD [12]. The technique [18F]FAPI-74 also offers a viable non-invasive assessment in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) [13], highlighting its broad potential.

However, despite this growing evidence base, the application of FAPI PET/CT specifically in pneumoconiosis remains unexplored. It remains unclear whether 18F-FAPI PET/CT imaging is feasible in non-invasively visualizing the activity and progression of pulmonary fibrosis lesions and, further, detecting the efficacy of anti-fibrotic treatment in this context. Therefore, in this study, we preliminarily observed 18F-FAPI uptake in pneumoconiosis patients and its correlation with pulmonary functional parameters. Further, an in-vivo and ex-vivo animal experiment involving pneumoconiosis model, pirfenidone-treated, and normal control groups were studied, comparing 18F-FAPI imaging with the commonly used 18F-fluoro-D-glucose (FDG) inflammation imaging. These findings may support the potential of 18F-FAPI PET/CT as a valuable tool for assessing active fibroblast activation and for monitoring anti-fibrotic therapy in pneumoconiosis-related pulmonary fibrosis, addressing a critical unmet need in occupational lung disease management.

Methods

Clinical study

Participants

This single-center prospective study enrolled six patients diagnosed with pneumoconiosis from Oct 1, 2022 to Sep 30, 2023. This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University (KYLL-2023-130). All participants signed the informed consent form.

The inclusion criteria were (1) aged 18–80 years and (2) a confirmed diagnosis of stage I pneumoconiosis based on diagnostic chest CT scans, in accordance with the diagnostic criteria outlined in Diagnosis of Occupational Pneumoconioses (GBZ 70–2015). Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with mild pulmonary fibrosis in HRCT; (2) patients with other lung diseases, including tuberculosis, malignancy, pulmonary hypertension, and sarcoidosis; (3) Patients who refused to participate; (4) patients who have previously undergone lung surgery; and (5) patients who could not give informed consent. All assessments were done by the senior chief physician in the Interstitial Lung Disease Subspecialty Group, based on the medical histories, clinical data, and auxiliary laboratory examinations. Healthy individuals without lung diseases were confirmed by chest CT as controls. All confirmatory chest CTs were conducted in one month before inclusion.

Pulmonary function test for participants

All enrolled participants underwent pulmonary function tests within 1 week before PET/CT imaging (Discovery VCT, General Electric Company). The parameters included the forced vital capacity (FVC) for the ventilation function and the carbon monoxide diffusion capacity (DLCO) for the diffusion function.

18F-FAPI PET/CT imaging and analysis

The participants performed 18F-FAPI PET/CT imaging without any preparation. Static PET/CT scans (2 min/bed, 5–7 beds) were performed 10 min, 60 min, and 180 min after the 18F-FAPI (244.2–358.9 MBq) injection intravenously.

Images were transferred to and analyzed on the Advanced Workstation (Version 47, GE Healthcare) after acquisition and reconstruction. PET/CT fusion images in the coronal, axial, and sagittal planes were reviewed. The outlining and measurement of the volume of interest (VOI) were done separately by two experienced senior nuclear medicine physicians, and the average values were taken. To ensure a comprehensive and reproducible measurement of tracer uptake across the entire lung parenchyma, the bilateral lungs were divided into 10 equal levels from the lung apex to the base. On each level, a VOI was manually outlined 0.5 cm from the outer surface to minimize partial volume effects from the chest wall. The mean standardized uptake value (SUVmean) from all 10 levels was automatically generated by the software and then averaged to calculate a single, representative whole-lung SUVmean for each participant, which was used for all subsequent group comparisons and correlations. The delineation of fibrotic and non-fibrotic areas on the accompanying CT images was performed by two experienced senior nuclear medicine physicians, with any discrepancies resolved through consensus or, when necessary, consultation with a board-certified thoracic radiologist. Fibrotic areas were defined based on established HRCT criteria for pulmonary fibrosis, including the presence of reticulation, architectural distortion, traction bronchiectasis, or honeycombing. Non-fibrotic areas were defined as regions of lung parenchyma appearing normal on CT, devoid of the aforementioned fibrotic features as well as significant emphysema, consolidation, or nodules. For each patient, two spherical volumes of interest (VOIs) of 1 cm3 were manually placed within visually confirmed fibrotic regions, and two additional VOIs were placed within confirmed non-fibrotic regions to obtain the respective SUVmean values [14]. Muscle tissue was used as background, and spheres with 1 cm3 were placed to obtain the SUVmean. The ratio of SUVmean of the lung and the muscle was used to determine the target-to-background ratio (TBRmean).

Animal experiments

In vivo pneumoconiosis modeling

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee and the Animal Experimentation Control and Supervision Committee of Shanxi Medical University (SYDL2019016). Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (aged 6–8 weeks, weighed 180–220 g) were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Center of Shanxi Medical University. All rats were housed in pathogen-free micro-isolator cages under a temperature of 18–24℃, relative humidity of 40–70%, and an ammonia concentration of below 20ppm with 12 h of light and 12 h of darkness per day at the Collaborative Innovation Center for Molecular Imaging. The rats were fed commercial chow (specific pathogen-free [SPF], Beijing Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) and purified water ad libitum. All rats experienced one week of acclimatization in the facility before the experiment started.

All rats were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6/group): the control, pneumoconiosis, and pirfenidone therapy groups. After weight measurement, each rat was anesthetized with isoflurane (3.0–5.0%, VETEASY, R510-22-10, RWD Life Science Co., Ltd). The pneumoconiosis model was established by a single intratracheal instillation of 0.5 mL of 50 mg/mL SiO2 suspension (1–5 μm silica particles, s5631, Sigma-Aldrich). Control rats received 0.5 mL saline via the same intratracheal procedure. A micro-CT scan (Inviscan Imaging Systems, France) was performed on 7 days after intratracheal administration to verify the pneumoconiosis model. The pirfenidone therapy group was administered with Pirfenidone (10 mg/kg, ETUARY, Beijing Continent Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd.) via the esophagus using a gavage needle (55 mm, 16G) with a 2.5 mm ball tip once a day for 6 months from Day 7 after modeling verification. In vivo PET/CT imaging was performed each month thereafter. Within each monthly imaging session, 18F-FAPI and 18F-FDG PET/CT scans were conducted 48–72 h apart to prevent any potential tracer interference. The 18F-FAPI scan was always performed first, followed by the 18F-FDG scan two or three days later. For 18F-FDG PET/CT imaging, rats were fasted overnight the day before the scan.

In vivo imaging of active fibrosis and inflammation by 18F-FAPI and 18F-FDG PET/CT

Synthesis and labeling of 18F-FAPI were performed as described previously [15]. Rats were injected with 6–8 MBq of tracer in a volume of 500–700 µL via the tail vein. After 60 min post-injection, rats were anesthetized with 1.5–3.0% isoflurane, and scanned using a micro PET/CT (Inviscan Small Animal In Vivo Imaging System, France) for 10 min. PET images were reconstructed via the 3D-ordered-subsets implementations-Monte Carlo algorithm. PMOD software (version 4.1) was used for image analysis. The bilateral lungs were manually outlined from apex to base on each trans-axial CT, 0.5 cm apart from the lung margins. The SUVmean of the whole lung was automatically generated to reflect the level of the tracer uptake. The SUVmean of the lung tissue/SUVmean of the muscle tissue was used to determine the target-to-background ratio (TBRmean). The volumetric analysis of active lesions was performed in addition to the SUVmean assessment. For both 18F-FAPI and 18F-FDG PET/CT images, a VOI was automatically generated around voxels with an uptake exceeding a threshold of 40% of the maximum SUV within the lung. The total volume (in cm3) of these VOIs was termed the Fibroblast Activation Volume (FAV) for 18F-FAPI and the Metabolic Tumor Volume (MTV) for 18F-FDG. The TL-FAPI and Total Lesion Glycolysis (TLG) were then calculated as TL-FAPI = FAV × SUVmean-FAPI and TLG = MTV × SUVmean-FDG.

Histology examination

The lung was harvested for histological analysis. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Masson trichrome staining were used to detect lung inflammation and fibrosis. FAP expression levels and the percentages of FAP + areas were examined using immunofluorescence (IF) staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC) in the lung tissue of pneumoconiosis rats.

HE and Masson trichrome staining

To detect tissue fibrosis, rat lung tissue specimens were collected for HE and Masson trichrome staining each month. Dripped 4% paraformaldehyde into the lung, fixed it overnight in the fixative at room temperature, and embedded it in paraffin blocks. Then, lung slices (5 μm) were cut, deparaffinized, and stained with HE or Masson trichrome staining according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Pulmonary pathological changes were assessed based on a standardized scoring system. According to the established scoring criteria [16], the severity of four key pathological features—edema, hemorrhage, inflammatory cell infiltration, and small airway injury—was evaluated under a light microscope. The scoring criteria was defined as follows: 0 points (no abnormalities or extremely mild abnormalities); 1 point (mild, localized changes); 2 points (moderate, localized changes); 3 points (moderate, widespread changes or severe localized changes); and 4 points (extensive, severe pathological changes). Masson’s stain images were analyzed using Image J software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD). Relative collagen content (%) was calculated as: (blue area/total observed area) × 100%.

IF staining

FAP expression in lung tissue from the control, pneumoconiosis, and pirfenidone therapy groups was quantified using the IF method. Sections were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Solarbio, A8010, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.) for 1 h, then incubated with primary antibody at a dilution of 1:50 (FAP Rabbit pAb, ABclonal, A6349, ABclonal Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) at room temperature for 1 h, followed by incubation with secondary antibody at a dilution of 1:200 (F2765, ThermoFisher) for 50 min. The slices were placed in a medium with 4’, 6-diamido-2-phenylindole (Powerful Biology, Wuhan Powerful Biology Technology Co., Ltd.). The fluorescence images were collected using a fluorescence microscope (Leica Dmi8). Image J software (National Institutes of Health) was used for image analysis. The mean fluorescence intensity of the FAP was calculated from immunofluorescence-stained slides as fluorescence area (Intden)/total observed area (Area) × 100%.

IHC

The spatial distribution of FAP was visualized and comparatively analyzed via IHC. Paraffin-embedded lung tissue Sect. (5 μm) were deparaffinized and rehydrated with graded ethanol, followed by antigen retrieval by microwave heating in citric acid at pH 6.0. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked by 25-min incubation in 3% H2O2. After incubating with 3% BSA for 30 min, the rabbit anti-rat monoclonal antibody against FAP (ABclonal, A6349, 1:50) was incubated overnight at 4 ℃ and then incubated with biotinylated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ab205718, Abcam, 1:200) at room temperature for 50 min. All sections were stained with peroxidase-conjugated avidin/biotin and 39-3-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate (K3468, DAKO), followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. Image analysis was performed using Image J software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD). FAP + area% was calculated from IHC slides as Intden/Area × 100%.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The inflammatory cytokines and fibrogenic cytokines of plasma in pneumoconiosis rats were measured by ELISA. Blood specimens were taken from rats at the Month 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. from the abdominal aorta and then centrifuged in a vacuum tube at 3500 rpm for 10 min to obtain the plasma. Concentrations of plasma interleukin 6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor ɑ (TNF-ɑ), and connective tissue growth factor (CTGF) were measured using enzyme immunoassay kits (JIUBANG, ZC-10049, ZC-10160, ZC-10165, Quanzhou Jiubang Biotechnology Co., Ltd.).

Statistical analysis

SPSS software (Version 26.0) was used for statistical analysis. Data were expressed as the mean value ± standard deviation. Comparisons were performed using independent sample t-tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA). Pearson correlation was used to evaluate the associations between SUVmean and DLCO%, and the associations 18F-FAPI uptake between fibrotic and nonfibrotic areas in pneumoconiosis patients. A two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

The baseline characteristics and pulmonary function of the participants were shown in Table 1. Compared to the healthy individuals, the FVC% (pneumoconiosis vs. controls = 91.7 ± 16.21% vs. 95.17 ± 1.57%, P = 0.731) and DLCO% (pneumoconiosis vs. controls = 70.58 ± 16.37% vs. 84.46 ± 3.66%, P = 0.203) of the pneumoconiosis patients were lower, indicating reduced diffusion function.

18F-FAPI PET/CT reveals distinct spatiotemporal uptake patterns in pneumoconiosis: pleural predilection, fibrotic specificity, and pulmonary function correlation

The PET/CT images of a representative pneumoconiosis patient and a healthy control individual were displayed in Fig. 2a, while Maximum Intensity Projection (MIP) images for all participants are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1. Healthy controls exhibited slight 18F-FAPI uptake in the whole lung, whereas pronounced uptake was observed under the pleura of pneumoconiosis patients. The SUVmean of 18F-FAPI in the whole lungs peaked at 10 min after injection in both groups and then decreased with time. Notably, the greatest uptake difference between the groups was observed at 60 min (P = 0.002), as shown in Fig. 2b. The SUVmean of 18F-FAPI in the fibrotic areas of pneumoconiosis patients peaked 10 min post-injection and then gradually declined, which was significantly higher in the fibrotic regions of pneumoconiosis compared to the nonfibrotic regions (P = 0.028, Fig. 2c). We further analyzed the TBRmean to normalize the lung uptake to the background muscle activity. Consistent with the SUVmean results, the TBRmean in the whole lungs of pneumoconiosis patients was significantly higher than in healthy controls at 60 min (1.04 ± 0.09 vs. 0.63 ± 0.15; P = 0.001, Fig. 2d). Similarly, the TBRmean in fibrotic areas was significantly higher than in non-fibrotic regions (1.31 ± 0.20 vs. 0.75 ± 0.15; P < 0.001, Fig. 2e). Correlation analysis suggested that in fibrotic lung regions, the SUVmean strongly correlated with DLCO% (r = −0.929, P = 0.022), whereas FVC% showed no association with uptake in any region. The scatter plot of individual data points (Fig. 2f) confirms this relationship and shows it is not driven by a single outlier.

18F-FAPI PET/CT tracks pneumoconiosis progression: temporal correlation with fibrosis and FAP expression, and therapeutic response to pirfenidone

The 18F-FAPI uptake in the pneumoconiosis group peaked at Month 4, and then decreased, which was higher than that in the pirfenidone therapy group (Fig. 3a). The TBRmean of the 18F-FAPI in the pneumoconiosis group peaked at Month 4 and was significantly higher than that of the control model (pneumoconiosis vs. Controls = 3.85 ± 0.58 vs. 2.13 ± 0.17) (P = 0.011) (Fig. 3c). In the pirfenidone therapy group, the TBRmean also peaked at Month 4, but was statistically lower than that in the pneumoconiosis group (pirfenidone therapy vs. pneumoconiosis = 2.93 ± 0.26 vs. 3.85 ± 0.58) (P = 0.014) (Fig. 3c). The FAV (pneumoconiosis vs. pirfenidone therapy = 5.75 ± 0.67 vs. 4.46 ± 0.69) (P = 0.048) (Fig. 3d) and TL-FAPI (pneumoconiosis vs. pirfenidone therapy = 23.18 ± 1.52 vs. 13.12 ± 1.84) (P = 0.003) (Fig. 3e) in the pneumoconiosis group peaked at Month 4, mirroring the TBRmean trend, and were significantly reduced in the pirfenidone-treated group. Masson trichrome staining revealed progressive collagen deposition and fibrosis in the pneumoconiosis group, with significantly higher relative collagen content at Months 5 and 6 compared to Month 1 (P < 0.001, Fig. 3b, f). In contrast, lung tissues from the control group showed minimal collagen deposition throughout the study. Quantitatively, pirfenidone treatment significantly attenuated these fibrotic changes, resulting in a collagen content that was significantly lower than the pneumoconiosis group at Months 5 and 6 (P = 0.007, P = 0.004) (Fig. 3b and f).

IF and IHC staining were performed to directly correlate the 18F-FAPI PET signal with its molecular target, FAP, across all experimental groups. In the control group, FAP expression remained at minimal levels throughout the study period (Fig. 4a-d). In contrast, the pneumoconiosis group exhibited a significant time-dependent increase in FAP expression, which peaked at Month 4, followed by a decline at Month 6. This trend was confirmed by both IF intensity (Month 1, 4, 6: P = 0.873, P = 0.001, P = 0.229, Fig. 4b) and IHC FAP + area% (Month 1, 4, 6: P = 0.163, P < 0.001, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4c). This temporal pattern closely paralleled the 18F-FAPI uptake dynamics shown in Fig. 3. Crucially, pirfenidone treatment notably attenuated FAP expression at Months 4 and 6 relative to the pneumoconiosis group, as quantified by both IF intensity (Month 4, 6: P = 0.026, P = 0.049, Fig. 4b) and IHC FAP + area% (Month 4, 6: P = 0.005, P = 0.027) (Fig. 4c), demonstrating a clear molecular-level response to the anti-fibrotic treatment. These initial findings collectively suggested that 18F-FAPI PET/CT effectively tracks disease progression in pneumoconiosis, with tracer uptake dynamics closely mirroring both histopathological fibrotic changes and molecular markers of fibroblast activation, while pirfenidone treatment significantly attenuates these pathological processes.

18F-FDG PET Reveals Inflammatory Dynamics in Pneumoconiosis

In the pneumoconiosis group, the 18F-FDG uptake of rat lungs peaked at Month 3, and decreased gradually. The 18F-FDG uptake in the pirfenidone therapy group was lower than that in the pneumoconiosis group at Months 3–6 (Fig. 5a). The SUVmean displayed no statistically significant difference between the pirfenidone therapy group and the pneumoconiosis group, despite the reduction of pirfenidone at Months 3, 4, 5, 6 (P = 0.781, P = 0.469, P = 0.781, P = 0.561, Fig. 5c). MTV and TLG, the parameters derived from 18F-FDG reached their maximum at Month 3 (vs. Month 1, P = 0.007 for MTV, Fig. 5d; vs. Month 1, P = 0.001 for TLG, Fig. 5e), correlating with the peak of inflammatory cytokine levels and granuloma formation observed histologically. The observed temporal dissociation between the peaks of FAPI-based and FDG-based volumetric parameters underscores their respective associations with fibrotic activity and inflammatory processes. HE staining provided a comparative analysis of pulmonary inflammation across the groups (Fig. 5b; Table 2). Lung sections from the control group displayed normal histoarchitecture with clear alveoli and minimal inflammatory cell infiltration. Conversely, the pneumoconiosis group exhibited a time-dependent escalation in inflammation, characterized by pronounced alveolar wall thickening, abundant lymphocyte and macrophage infiltration, and the eventual formation of multiple, well-defined granulomas in the later stages (Months 4–6) (Fig. 5b). The pirfenidone therapy group showed a noticeable, though incomplete, reduction in inflammatory pathology. While some inflammatory cell infiltration and mild alveolar thickening persisted, the extent and severity were visibly less than in the untreated pneumoconiosis group at Month 3, 4, 5 (P = 0.003, P = 0.023, P = 0.013), and the formation of large, consolidated granulomas was largely suppressed (Table 2). Furthermore, the dynamics of key inflammatory and fibrogenic cytokines mirrored the imaging and histological findings: plasma levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and CTGF showed significant increases at Month 2, 4, and 5, respectively, compared to Month 0 (P = 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 2a; P = 0.006, Supplementary Fig. 2b; P = 0.043, Supplementary Fig. 2c), underscoring the complex interplay between inflammation and fibrosis during disease progression.

Discussion

In this study, 18F-FAPI PET/CT was employed as a promising radioactive tracer for visualizing lung fibroblasts and meticulously monitoring the progression of pulmonary fibrosis in pneumoconiosis. Our initial data revealed a significant accumulation of 18F-FAPI within the regions of the fibrotic lung in pneumoconiosis patients, an occurrence inversely correlated with pulmonary diffusion capacity - a harbinger of adverse clinical prognosis. Extending these observations to a rat model of pneumoconiosis, the present study found that 18F-FAPI accumulated in lung tissue, reaching a peak at Month 4, after which its levels declined with the progression of pulmonary fibrosis. The strong concordance between the 18F-FAPI PET signal and the ex vivo FAP expression levels—not only in the pneumoconiosis group but also in the suppressed expression observed in the pirfenidone-treated group—preceded the fibrotic manifestations delineated by Masson trichrome staining. These preliminary findings collectively suggest that 18F-FAPI PET/CT imaging may hold promise as a frontline diagnostic modality capable of identifying patients in the active fibrotic phase of pneumoconiosis at an early stage, suggesting prognostic insights crucial for informing therapeutic strategies. This dual capability of early detection and prognostication underscores the need for further investigation of 18F-FAPI PET/CT as a transformative tool in the treatment decisions of pneumoconiosis.

Pulmonary fibrosis stands as a paramount clinical determinant of pneumoconiosis progression; its pathogenesis, shrouded in mystery, remains elusive. Within this intricate web, the activation of fibroblasts and the abnormal deposition of extracellular matrix weave a dual tapestry of destruction. This process not only fosters the formation of lung fibrotic scars but also relentlessly advances toward respiratory failure [17, 18]. FAP, a biomarker signifying activated fibroblasts, manifests its elevated expression in pulmonary fibrosis tissue as a harbinger of impending fibroblast foci development [19–21]. Detecting these processes and quantitatively analyzing the expression levels of FAP provide a scientific basis for the early prevention and treatment of pneumoconiosis and pulmonary fibrosis. Qin et al.. detected the dynamics in lung injury using a rat model of radiation-induced lung injury, specifically employing 18F-FAPI PET/CT imaging. Different from our research findings, 18F-FAPI uptake decreased to baseline levels in weeks 2–3 after irradiation, with a secondary increase in week 4 and stabilization during weeks 4–6 [22]. The potential explanation is that radiation-induced lung injury leads to radiation pneumonia in the early stages and progresses to radiation-induced lung fibrosis in later stages. Conversely, in pneumoconiosis, inflammation persists throughout the entire course, with pulmonary fibrosis manifesting only in the advanced stages, whose progression has been corroborated by 18F-FDG imaging. Yang et al.. compared lung tissues from silicosis patient post-transplantation with those of healthy organ donors. Their findings revealed that 12 out of the studied silicosis patients exhibited elevated levels of FAP protein compared to healthy donors, a result consistent with our study [3].

18F-FDG PET/CT imaging was employed to investigate the correlation between inflammation and fibrosis and to evaluate the effectiveness of anti-fibrotic therapy. Our findings indicate persistent active inflammation in fibrotic lesions from the onset through the progression of pneumoconiosis, accompanied by a suboptimal therapeutic response to pirfenidone. This implies that 18F-FDG has limited utility in assessing active fibrosis in pneumoconiosis [23]. The uptake of 18F-FDG reflects the recruitment of neutrophils, macrophages, and fibroblasts involved in inflammatory and fibrotic processes during the development of pneumoconiosis. Kubota et al.. reported that a significant component of 18F-FDG uptake by tumor tissues originates from active inflammatory cells surrounding the tumor, which are more likely to take up 18F-FDG than tumor cells themselves [24]. The interplay of multiple factors in the inflammatory course can continuously increase FDG uptake in active inflammatory disorders. PET/CT imaging can delineate the location, extent, and severity of inflammatory lesion involvement [25, 26]. However, it remains challenging to discern which pathological alterations predominate at any given stage, thereby complicating the selection of optimal timing and therapeutic regimen. A clinical investigation by Bondue et al.. revealed no significant changes in 18F-FDG uptake three months following pirfenidone treatment [27]. The findings of the current study align with previous research, underscoring the limitations of 18F-FDG in the early detection and therapeutic assessment of pneumoconiosis-induced pulmonary fibrosis. This highlights the superiority of 18F-FAPI for non-invasively evaluating active fibrotic lesions.

An important consideration in the interpretation of our findings is the specificity of 18F-FAPI for activated fibroblasts. While FAP is a well-established marker of activated fibroblasts in fibrotic niches [19, 20], it is also expressed by cancer-associated fibroblasts in the tumor stroma [6], and its expression can be induced in chronic inflammatory conditions [7]. In pneumoconiosis, the persistent inflammation and formation of granulomas are central to the disease pathogenesis. It is therefore plausible that the 18F-FAPI signal we observed could have contributions from both active fibrosis and the granulomatous inflammatory response. However, several lines of evidence from our study suggest that the signal predominantly reflects fibroblast activation and fibrotic activity. First, the spatiotemporal dynamics of 18F-FAPI uptake differed from those of the inflammatory tracer 18F-FDG; 18F-FAPI uptake peaked at Month 4 in our rat model, whereas 18F-FDG uptake, which reflects inflammatory cell metabolism, peaked earlier at Month 3. This temporal dissociation suggests that the processes being imaged are distinct. Second, the strong correlation between 18F-FAPI uptake and the expression of FAP at the protein levels, as confirmed by our IF and IHC staining, provides direct histopathological support for its association with fibroblast activation. Finally, the significant reduction in 18F-FAPI uptake in response to the anti-fibrotic agent pirfenidone, which has a primary mechanism of action targeting fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis, further supports that the signal is linked to a treatable fibrotic process. While we cannot entirely rule out a component of FAP expression from inflammatory stromal cells within granulomas, our collective data indicate that 18F-FAPI PET/CT is primarily tracking the pivotal process of fibroblast activation and the progression of pulmonary fibrosis in pneumoconiosis.

18F-FAPI PET/CT emerges as a valuable biomarker for real-time assessment of pulmonary fibrosis activity in pneumoconiosis, offering superior sensitivity for early detection of activated fibroblasts-the precursors of tissue fibrosis-compared to other tracers. This enables earlier diagnosis of active fibrosis, facilitating timely intervention and improved prognosis. The technique’s potential extends to various interstitial lung diseases, where it may guide optimal treatment timing and potentially modify disease progression by reducing structural damage and preserving lung function. However, this study has several limitations: (1) the animal treatment protocol initiated pirfenidone therapy one week after model induction. This design was chosen to ensure intervention during the active phase of fibroblast activation and early fibrosis, which is the primary target of both the anti-fibrotic drug and our 18F-FAPI imaging biomarker. While this is optimal for a proof-of-concept therapeutic monitoring study, it differs from the clinical reality where pneumoconiosis is diagnosed and treatment is considered years or decades after the cessation of dust exposure. Future studies should investigate the utility of 18F-FAPI PET/CT for monitoring treatment response in established, late-stage fibrosis models to better mimic the clinical scenario; (2) clinical cases were restricted to ILO Stage I pneumoconiosis, which strengthens the study’s focus on early detection but limits the generalizability of our findings to more advanced disease stages. Furthermore, the absence of a semi-quantitative HRCT fibrosis score is a limitation, and future studies should incorporate such scoring for a more nuanced correlation with imaging biomarkers; (3) the clinical cohort lacked treatment response evaluation and pathological correlation, which precludes a direct link between the imaging signal and histological changes or therapeutic efficacy. (4) Furthermore, the clinical cohort was relatively small, consisting of only six pneumoconiosis patients and four healthy controls. While the findings are encouraging and statistically significant in some analyses, the limited sample size constrains the statistical power and generalizability of our conclusions. Therefore, this work should be regarded as a pilot study, and future investigations with larger, multi-center cohorts are warranted to validate the diagnostic and prognostic utility of 18F-FAPI PET/CT in pneumoconiosis.

Beyond these study-specific limitations, the broader clinical translation of 18F-FAPI PET/CT also face several practical challenges. Firstly, regulatory approval from agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) will require large-scale, multi-center clinical trials to definitively establish its diagnostic efficacy and impact on patient management. Secondly, the cost-effectiveness of 18F-FAPI PET/CT compared to the current standard of care (e.g., serial HRCT) needs rigorous health-economic analysis. Key metrics such as the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) gained will be crucial for determining its value. While PET/CT is a relatively expensive modality, its ability to precisely identify active fibrosis could position it as a complementary tool to HRCT, used for resolving diagnostic uncertainty or guiding therapy initiation, thereby enabling more targeted and cost-effective use of anti-fibrotic therapies. Finally, the wider availability of 18F-FAPI radiopharmaceuticals is currently limited to major academic centers with an on-site cyclotron and specialized radiochemistry expertise. Broader adoption will depend on the development of reliable distribution networks or kit-based preparation methods that are simpler for regional radiopharmacies to handle. Future work should focus on addressing these translational barriers to realize the full clinical potential of this promising technique.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the preliminary findings of this pilot study suggest that 18F-FAPI PET/CT imaging holds potential as a valuable tool for assessing pulmonary fibrosis activity in pneumoconiosis. Our data warrant further investigation in larger, prospective cohorts to validate its clinical utility for guiding timely intervention and improving the management for monitoring anti-fibrotic therapy compared to 18F-FDG.
