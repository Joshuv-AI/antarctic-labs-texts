Simultaneous circulation of all four dengue virus serotypes during a single outbreak in Kassala, Eastern Sudan 2023
Authors: Abdualmoniem O Musa, Mohamed I M Awadap, Mahmoud T M Faki, Hasan A Ibrahim, Ali Adam, Emmanuel Edwar Siddig, Ayman Ahmed, Hamdan Z Hamdan, Elhadi A Ahmed, Tajeldin M Abdallah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDengue is one of the major health problems worldwide and Sudan is one of thecountries with high burden of the disease especially the eastern part which is endemic fordengue. This study aimed to identify the circulating dengue serotypes during the outbreak of thedisease in Kassala 2023.MethodsWe prospectively enrolled 548 febrile patients suspected of dengue infection who visitedKassala teaching hospital between September 2023 and February 2024.Diagnostic confirmation was achieved through rapid tests (NS1 antigen, IgM, and IgG), and reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was employed to identify various dengue serotypes.ResultsOf the 548 febrile patients, dengue was confirmed in 50.4% (276/548) using rapid diagnostic tests. Among these, the NS1 antigen was positive in 70.7% (195/276), while IgM was detected in 0.4% (1/276). Seropositivity for both NS1 antigen and IgM was observed in 27.5% (76/276), with IgM and IgG co-detected in 1.4% (4/276). Serotype differentiation via RT-PCR revealed that DENV-III was the most prevalent at 50% (133/266), followed by DENV-II at 39.8% (106/266). DENV-IV accounted for 3% (8/266), and a co-detection of DENV-II and DENV-III in same sample was observed in 4.9% (13/266) as well as serotypes I and III together constituted 2.4% (6/266). The patient demographic included predominantly males (56.8%, 151/276), laborers (45.7%, 126/276), individuals with secondary education (41.7%, 115/276), and urban residents (83.7%, 231/276). The mean age of patients was 32.1 ± 12.9 years. The most common symptoms included fever (100%), headache (95.7%), back pain (91.7%), joint pain (87.7%), sweating (84%), nausea (84.8%), chills (83%), and hemorrhagic manifestations (15.6%). Statistical analysis indicated significant associations between male gender (P = 0.014), headache (P = 0.040) and chills (P = 0.010) with dengue serotypes III.ConclusionThis study highlights the high prevalence of dengue infection among febrile patients at Kassala Teaching Hospital, with all four dengue virus serotypes being circulated duringthe outbreakof the disease 2023. Further large-scale studies, including genomic analyses of dengue, are essential to elucidate the relationships among dengue serotypes, genotypes, and clinical manifestations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12013-y.Keywords: Dengue, RT-PCR, Co-circulation, Serotypes, Sudan

Introduction

Dengue is one of the most prevalentmosquito-borne viral infections globally, withsignificant increases inthe casefatality rates [1].It is causedby on the four serotype of thedengue virus (DENV), which belongs to the genus Flavivirus and the familyFlaviviridae. Four distinct antigenically classified serotypes have been identified and designated as type DENV I, DENV II, DENV III, andDENV IV. Infection with one serotype confers lifelong immunity to that specific serotype; however, it provides only heterotypic immunity to the other three serotypes [2, 3]. The virus is transmitted to humans throughthe bite offemale Aedes mosquitoes, notably A. aegypti and A. albopictus [4].

Clinically, dengue can manifest as a spectrum of illness, ranging from mild symptoms to severe cases, including fatal hemorrhagic disease [2].Globally, dengue is endemic in 129 countries, affecting an estimated 390 million people each year, with approximately 70% of cases occurring in Asia [4, 5]. In2019, the largest dengue epidemic has occurred and involved all the World Health Organization (WHO) Regions including the USA where, around 3.1 million cases reported, of them more than 25,000 cases classified as having severe infection [6]. In Sudan, dengue was reported first in1986 during an outbreak of the disease in the Red Sea state, Eastern Sudan [7]. Since then, the disease is spreading rapidly to the western and central regions of Sudan. However, the eastern part of the country remains endemic with dengue [8, 9].Where, the incidence of dengue infection has increased considerably after rainfall during September and through November. Recently, repeated outbreaks of dengue infection have been reported from the Red Sea and Kassala states in eastern Sudan. Wherenumerous reports have documented active circulation of different serotypes of dengue including DENV II, DENV III, and DENV IV [10–12]. In eastern Sudan, most cases with dengue infection are asymptomatic or having mild infection, however, recent dengue epidemics have shown severe cases of infection particularly, when new DENV serotypes is circulating [12]. Moreover, information on the association of disease severity with dual infection by multiple serotypes remained limited in Sudan.

Therefore, this study aims to confirm the current outbreak and to identify the co-circulation of different DENV serotypes associated with this resurgence. Insights gained from this research will provide essential information for health planners and enhance the understanding of the molecular epidemiology of dengue, ultimately contributing to more effective control measures.

Methods

This is cross sectional hospital-based study, which was conducted as collaborative work between Kassalateaching hospitaland the Public Health Laboratory (PHL), Ministry of Health, Kassala State, Sudan, during September 2023 and through February 2024. All patients with suspicion of dengue infection during an outbreak of the disease who visiting the outpatient clinic of Kassala teaching hospital were enrolled in this study. A suspected DENV infected case was defined according to WHO guidelines as high-grade fever of 38.5 °C with or without hemorrhagic manifestations, vomiting, headache, and joint pain. A confirmed DENV case was defined as laboratory confirmed acute or recent DENV infection by positive DENV NS1Ag detection and/or positive reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR). Inclusion criteria: Patients ≥ 5 years with acute fever (≥ 38 °C, 2–7 days) and at least two dengue-like symptoms per WHO guidelines. Exclusion criteria: Confirmed alternative diagnoses or unsuitable blood samples [13]. Kassala teaching hospital is a tertiary care facility that provides health service for all patients referred to from rural hospitals, health centers and refugees’ camps. Structured questionnaire was used to gather information about socio-demographic characteristics clinical and laboratory results (Supplementary 1).

Specimen collection and transportation

Six ml of venous blood were drowned from each participant and collected in a sterile clot activator vacutainer and EDTA vacutainer. Then blood samples were sent to PHL with maintaining a proper cold chain. Then sera samples were separated from clotted blood and prepared for serologic diagnostic tests and PCR amplification, Thereafter, sera samples were stored at − 20 °C until further testing.

Dengue virus detection

Immunochromatographic assay

STANDARD Q Dengue Duo test is an immunochromatographic assay for the detection of NS1 antigen and Dengue virus-specific IgM and IgG antibodies in human serum were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions (SD Biosensor, INC) (https://www.sdbiosensor.com/). Briefly, For NS1 antigen detection, 100 µL of the serum was added to the sample well, and results were read after 15 min and disregarded after 30 min. For IgM and IgG detection, 10 µL of the serum was applied to the test device, followed by the addition of three drops (approximately 90 µL) of assay diluent. Results were interpreted according to the manufacturer’s instructions: a positive result showed both control (C) and test (T) lines; a negative result showed only the control line; and tests with no control line or only the test line were considered invalid.

Dengue virus serotyping

RNA extraction

Viral RNA was extracted from patient sera using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (QIAamp, Hamburg, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 140 µl of serum was mixed with 560 µl of AVL buffer containing carrier RNA, incubated at room temperature for 10 min, and combined with 560 µl of ethanol. The mixture was transferred to a QIAamp spin column and centrifuged, followed by sequential washes with WB1 and WB2 buffers. RNA was eluted with 60 µl of AVE buffer. Extracted RNA was stored at − 20 °C until use, with 10 µl used for RT-PCR.

One step RT-PCR assay for serotyping

RT-PCR was performed in 276 dengue Rapid Diagnostic Test (RDT) positive samples using Bosphore Dengue Virus Genotyping Kit v1 according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Anatolia TanıveBiyoteknoloji A.Ş). Briefly the assay is based on a Real-Time PCR method, utilizing two PCR master mixes containing highly specific and accurate Taq DNA Polymerase, reverse transcriptase, PCR buffers, dNTPs, forward and reverse primers, and dual-labeled probes specific for each serotype and internal control. PCR Master Mix 1 targets Dengue virus serotypes I and IV and includes probes labeled with FAM and HEX filters for the respective serotypes, with Cy5 for internal control. PCR Master Mix 2 targets Dengue virus serotypes II and III, using FAM and HEX filters for their detection, along with Cy5 for internal control. 15 µl of the PCR Master Mix were added into PCR tubes followed by 10 µl of RNA (sample/ positive or negative control). The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: reverse transcription at 50 °C for 30 min, initial denaturation and enzyme activation at 95 °C for 14.5 min, followed by 50 cycles of denaturation at 97 °C for 30 s, and annealing/data collection at 58 °C for 90 s. Fluorescent signal acquisition occurred during the annealing step.

Statistical analysis

Patients’ demographics and clinical data were entered and analyzed using the statistical package for social science software (SPSS, version 24). Shapiro-Wilk normality test was used to test the normal distribution of data among the studied population. Means and proportions of the socio-demographic and epidemiological characteristics were calculated for Dengue serotypes using chi-square test and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethical Research Committee, Ministry of Health, and Kassala State, Sudan (Ethical approval number: 28). This study conducted in accordance to the regulations and Helsinki declaration. The objectives of the study were clearly explained to allparticipants and informed written consent was obtained from them.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

A total of 548 febrile patients with features that are highly suggestive of dengue infection were enrolled in the study, the prevalence of dengue infection was 50.4% (276/548) using rapid diagnostic tests(NSI antigen and IgM, IgG). RT-PCR was positive in 48.5% (266/548) patients. The mean age was 31.91 ± 12.9. The majority were male 56.5% (156/276), urban residents 87.7% (242/276), laborers 46.7% (129/276) and of secondary level of education 42.8% (118/276).

Regarding the clinical manifestations, fever 100% (276/276), headache 95.3% (263/276), back pain 94.6% (261/276), joint pain 91.3% (252/276), nausea88% (243/276),sweating87.7%(242/276), chills 85.9% (237/276) andhemorrhagic manifestations in 15.6% (43/276) (Tables 1 and 2).

Seroprevalence of dengue virus as per rapid tests

Out of 548 participants, dengue virus antigens and antibodies were detected at varying frequencies using rapid diagnostic tests (Table 2). Dengue NS1 antigen was reactive in 35.6% (195/548) participants. Dengue IgM was reactive in only 0.2% (1/548) participants. Combined reactivity of NS1Ag and IgM was observed in 13.9% (76/548), while IgM and IgG seropositivity were found in0.7% (4/548). Overall, 50.4% (276/548) participants tested positive for at least one marker of dengue infection (Table 2).

Molecular characterization of dengue virus

Among the 276 reactive samples, dengue virus detected using RT-PCR in 96.4% (266/276) patients while a small proportion 3.6% (10/276) tested negative for stereotyping despite being reactive by RDT. Dengue virus stereotyping using RT-PCR identified Serotype III as the most prevalent, detected in50% (133/266) cases (Table 2). This was followed by Serotype II in 39.8% (106/266) cases. Other serotypes included Serotype IV 3% (8/266) and a combination of Serotypes II and III4.9% (13/266). Mixed Serotype I and Serotype III infections were identified in 2.3% (6/266) cases (Table 2).

Association of dengue virus serotypes with clinical and demographic variables

Several clinical symptoms were evaluated in relation to dengue virus serotypes. Headaches werereported by 264 (95.5%) participants, with a significant association observed between headache and serotype distribution (P = 0.040). Among these, participants with Serotype III had the highest prevalence of headache (51.6%), followed by Serotype II (37.8%). Joint pain was reported by 242 (91%) participants, though no significant association with serotype was found (P = 0.257).

Vomiting was present in 74 (27.8%) cases, predominantly associated with Serotype III (51.4%), but this was not statistically significant (P = 0.117). Back pain was reported in 253 (95.1%) participants, with a higher prevalence in those with Serotype III (51%) and Serotype II (39.1%); however, the association with serotype was not significant (P = 0.241). Skin rash was observed in 46 (17.7%) cases, primarily linked to Serotype III and Serotype II, but without significant correlation (P = 0.171) (Table 3).

Chills showed a statistically significant relationship with serotype distribution (P = 0.010). Among those experiencing chills, Serotype III accounted for the majority (53.3%), followed by Serotype II (37.6%). Bleeding manifestations were reported in 43 (16.2%) cases but were not significantly associated with specific serotypes (P = 0.211) (Table 3).

Laboratory data indicated a significant association between C-reactive protein (CRP) positivity and serotype distribution (P = 0.021). CRP positivity was observed in 253 (95.1%) participants, with the highest prevalence in those with Serotype III (50.2%), followed by Serotype II (40.7%). Platelet counts revealed that thrombocytopenia was present in 130 (48.9%) cases, with no significant association with serotypes (P = 0.834). Similarly, leukopenia was detected in 120 (45.1%) participants, but it did not show a significant correlation with serotype distribution (P = 0.921) (Table 3).

Discussion

Dengue represents a significant public health challengein Sudan, particularlyin the eastern region, where the virushas become endemic, with all four serotypes identified and actively circulating [12]. Numerous outbreaks of dengue infections have been reported in eastern Sudan, underscoring the urgent need for effective public health responses [12, 14–16].

This study offers crucial insights into the dengue outbreak in Kassala, eastern Sudan, in 2023.The current study reported the prevalence of dengue infection as high as 50.3% using NS1antigen and IgM, which is comparable to that shown in a study conducted in El-Gadarif state 47.6% [17]. However, it is lower than that previously documented by Abdallah et al. in the same study area, at71.7% [14]. And it is higher than the rate of 11.4% that was reported by Edosogiet al. in eastern Sudan 2024 [12]. A prevalence of 33.3% was noted in the study carried out in neighboring Ethiopia [18]. The difference in the results between these studies may be attributed to different diagnostic procedures as well as variation in samplesize and socio-demographic characteristics of the studied populations. Among socio-demographic characteristics male gender (P = 0.014) was the only factor significantly associated with dengue stereotyping. The male preponderance found in our cohort is not unusual in Kassala, eastern Sudan. Earlier report demonstrated a higher rate of dengue infection among males [14]. Likewise, a study conducted in eastern Sudan during the epidemic of dengue 2019, revealed Male to female ratio of 2:1 [12]. In Pakistan, Ali et al. reported dengue among male and femalesas 56% and 44% respectively [19]. In Kenya, Konongoiet al. reported dengue as 68% in male patients and as 32% in female patients [20]. The high rate of dengue infection among males may be explained by the increased outdoor activities of men which may raise the risk of exposure to the Aedes mosquito, also, it may be related to the health care-seeking behavior of the male gender. This study demonstrated that the majority of subjects studied were urban residents. This is consistent with the report by Duong et al.. who found dengue as 77.2% among urban residents of Hanoi, Vietnam, between 2006 and 2011 [21]. Also, in Vietnam Nguyen-Tien et al.observed that people living in central districts have a 3.2 times higher risk of developing dengue infection than those living in peri-urban districts [22]. In a systemic review and meta-analysisGuoet al. have observed that 50% of the global estimate of dengue outbreaks during 1990 and through 2015 was reported from urban areas while 28.6% was from rural areas [23]. The high prevalence of dengue among urban residents is likely due to the accessibility of suitable breeding sites for the Aedes mosquito in the urban area. In addition, massive population growth in the cities may increase the risk of dengue dispersion in urban regions.

Despite previous dengue outbreaks in eastern Sudan, the relatively low IgG positivity observed in this study can be attributed to two main factors. Firstly, most samples were collected within first week of symptom onset, a timeframe during which IgM and NS1 antigens are typically detectable, whereas IgG, particularly in primary infections, may not yet be present. Secondly, the ongoing conflict in Sudan has caused large-scale population displacement, with huge population moving from non-endemic areas to eastern Sudan. These newly arrived individuals, who were inexperienced to dengue, likely contributed to the higher proportion of primary infections observed, as indicated by the predominance of NS1 and IgM reactivity. This context helps to explain the lower expected IgG prevalence and underscores the impact of sample timing and population dynamics on serological findings.

Common clinical manifestations observed among our participants included fever, headache, joint pain, nausea, sweating and chills, consistent with prior studies. Notably, these symptoms were most frequently associated with DENV-III, followed by DENV-II [24]. In line with previous findings, thrombocytopenia (48.9%) and leucopenia (45.1%) were the predominant hematological abnormalities observed. Specifically, thrombocytopenia was identified in 51.5% of DENV-III and 38.5% of DENV-II infections, whereas leucopenia was seen in 50% of DENV-III cases and 40% of DENV-II cases, corroborating earlier research conducted in eastern Sudan [12, 14].

In the current study, the group-specific RT-PCR assay was used to detect DENV RNA which was observed in 266(96.4%) participants. However, ten samples were negative for RT-PCR. This is likely due to later collection of blood samples after the appearance of DENV-specific Ig M/ IgG antibodies in the blood circulation. To our knowledge, this is the first study demonstrating the all four dengue serotypes during the outbreak of dengue in Kassala 2023. This agrees with other reports confirming that all DENV serotypes were actively circulated in Kassala, eastern Sudan [12].

In the present study, the prevalence of DENV-III was higher than that of other serotypes followed by DENV-II. This is consistent with the study conducted by Mawahibet al., during the epidemic of dengue infection in Kassala, 2019, where genotype III of DENV-III was confirmed to be related to disease outbreak [10]. In another study undertaken by Abbas et al. in the Red Sea state, eastern Sudan DENV-III was found to be the cause of the dengue outbreak in Port Sudan in2020 [11]. A previous report from Pakistan confirmed that DENV-II and DENV-III were the major causes for disease outbreaksin 2011 and 2013 [18].In a study conducted in India Dinkar et al., reported that DENV-II and DENV-III were the most prevalent circulating dengue serotypes in Northern India .Additionally, the case fatality rate was reported as 23(65.7%) and 9(25.7%) in serotypes DENV-II and DENV-III, respectively [25]. By contrast, in Chittagong, Bangladesh, during the outbreak of dengue fever 2023, the DENV-II (67.9%) serotype was found to be more prevalent than the DENV-III (20.3%) [26]. This study confirmed co-infection with different DENV serotypes, which is in agreement with the study reported by Eldasogiet al., who noted co-circulation of all serotypes of dengue in Kassala and red states in eastern Sudan in 2019 [12]. These findings reflect the emergence of co-circulation of multiple serotypes of dengue in Kassala, eastern Sudan. Previous studies have documented co-infection with different serotypes and indicate that Co-circulation of multiple serotypes of dengue will influence the clinical and survival outcomes of the disease [27, 28]. The emergence of all dengue serotypes during this epidemic is really alarming and may reflect hyperendemicity of the dengue virus in Kassala, eastern Sudan. Co-infection with multiple serotypes may have been the factors implicated in the occurrence of dengue hemorrhagic manifestations among our patients. Around 16.2% of the dengue patients had Hemorrhagic manifestations, of whom 55.8% were associated with DENV-III and 30.2% with DENV-II. The co-circulation of multiple DENV serotypes, as illustrated in this study, may influence the epidemiology and clinical outcomes of dengue infection in eastern Sudan. Therefore, more studies on co-infection with multiple s of DENV serotypes and associated genotypes were of paramount importance so as to better understand the molecular epidemiology of DENV. In Sudan, during the war, huge numbers of people were internally displaced, the health system collapsed, and mosquito control measures were missed. These factors have an important influence on the breeding of mosquitoes in a large population density and increase the risk of dengue epidemic in the future. As the study revealed that all four dengue virus serotypes were present in Kassala, with DENV-III and DENV-II responsible for the 2023 outbreak, these findings highlight the need for improved public health intervention. Molecular surveillance can facilitate the early detection of emerging serotypes, whereas integrated vector control strategies, including environmental management and community engagement, are crucial for reducing Aedes mosquito populations. The presence of multiple serotypes also supports the potential future introduction of tetravalent dengue vaccines once deemed suitable for the region. In addition, serotype data can inform seroepidemiological studies and guide targeted awareness campaigns to prevent severe dengue and future outbreaks. One of the limitations of this study was that it was a hospital-based study. This does not reflect the true burden of the disease at the level of the community, as asymptomatic patients and those with mild dengue infection were not included in the study. Furthermore, integration of genomic analysis into future outbreak investigations is essential. Understanding the genetic variations and genotypes of DENV serotypes can provide insights into their virulence, transmission dynamics, and vaccine design. Thus, we propose a follow-up study focused on sequencing DENV genomes from Kassala to characterize genotypic variations across serotypes, assess associations between genotypes and clinical severity, compare circulating strains with global DENV phylogenies, support genotype-informed public health strategies, and vaccine suitability.

Conclusion

The findings of this study revealed that all four serotypes of dengue virus were circulated in Kassala eastern Sudan, whereas serotypes III and II were the predominant outbreak-causing serotypes in Kassala in 2023. Moreover, dual infection with more than one serotype was also noted in this study. These results emphasize the urgent need for further studies to correlate the severity of the disease, circulating serotypes, and associated genotypes. Furthermore, many efforts should be directed towards continuous dengue surveillance and improving diagnostic methods, which may help in early detection and rapid control of outbreaks in endemic areas.
