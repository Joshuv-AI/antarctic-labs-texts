In praise of prime numbers: satire as a tool for teaching clinical reasoning
Authors: Robert S. Holzman, Cynthia A. Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryScientific satire occupies an unusual place in medical publishing. This editorial discusses the role of medical satire by highlighting an article that poses as a retrospective observational trial using a large database, and compares a clinically relevant outcome between two arbitrary, physiologically meaningless groups. The differences between the groups are statistically significant, but physiologically nonsensical. The editorial explores epistemology as it applies to the practice of medicine. The greatest value of the satire may be medical education and its teaching of clinical reasoning. It reminds clinicians that cognitive heuristics, arbitrary categorisation, and large datasets can combine to produce statistically convincing, yet clinically meaningless, associations.

Scientific satire occupies an unusual place in medical publishing. When executed poorly, it risks being mistaken for frivolity; when executed well, it exposes assumptions that conventional scholarship often leaves unexamined. The Research Communication ‘Mechanical ventilation using prime numbers: an exploratory study of numerical preferences and oxygenation’, published in this issue of BJA Open, belongs firmly in the latter category.1 Although ostensibly an investigation into prime-number ventilator settings and oxygenation, it is actually a delightfully insightful commentary on heuristics, statistical reasoning, and the psychology of clinical decision making.

The manuscript describes a conventional retrospective observational study. It contains all the expected features of contemporary clinical research: a hypothesis, a large dataset, objective outcome measures, formal statistical testing, and appropriately cautious interpretation. Its structure faithfully reproduces the conventions of a respectable clinical investigation, using anonymised (real) data, three clinically relevant ventilatory variables, significance testing, and a clinically familiar endpoint. The result is an entirely credible scientific framework applied to a deliberately incredible hypothesis.

That contrast is precisely what gives the paper its educational value.

The authors hypothesised that there is a significant difference in the ratio of peripheral oxygen saturation to the fraction of inspired oxygen when ventilator settings (positive end-expiratory pressure, plateau pressure, and respiratory rate) are chosen using prime numbers compared with even numbers. The premise, of course, is physiologically implausible. There is no biological mechanism by which prime numbers should influence gas exchange. However, the manuscript elegantly demonstrates how easily statistically significant differences may emerge when large datasets are partitioned into arbitrary study groups, in this case, even-number compared with prime-number ventilator settings.

The study rationale begins with an observation that many practising anaesthesiologists will recognise. Ventilator settings frequently migrate towards even numbers. Respiratory rates of 13 become 14, PEEP of 7 becomes 8, often without any accompanying physiological justification. Whether this reflects cognitive convenience, convention, or subconscious preference for even numbers is uncertain. The manuscript simply asks whether these habits possess any basis in evidence. If even numbers are no more physiologically deserving than prime numbers, perhaps neither preference deserves unquestioning acceptance.

The title is neutral and avoids any implication of therapeutic optimisation. The results of the appropriate nonparametric statistical analyses are reported with medians and interquartile ranges. Sample sizes are adequate, and the limitations and cautions regarding causal inference are well articulated.

Importantly, the manuscript never seriously advocates for the clinical adoption of prime-number ventilation parameters. The authors emphasise that the observed (statistically significant) differences are minute and should be regarded as ‘a prompt for critical reflection rather than a basis for clinical guidance’.1 This explicit qualification ensures that the educational intent of the research report cannot reasonably be mistaken for a therapeutic recommendation. The manuscript's principal contribution lies not in contributing evidence for ventilation management but in its exploration of epistemology as it applies to the practice of medicine.

Clinical medicine is rich with conventions that acquire authority through repetition rather than evidence. The practice of anaesthesia is strongly numerical. Clinicians favour round numbers, memorable thresholds, and familiar increments because these facilitate communication, reduce cognitive effort, and promote consistency. These tendencies are neither irrational nor undesirable. Indeed, they often improve efficiency in demanding clinical environments. Difficulties arise only when convenient conventions become confused with physiological principles.

The manuscript gently and creatively exposes this distinction. By replacing one arbitrary numerical preference with another, equally arbitrary, alternative, it demonstrates how readily plausible narratives may be constructed around meaningless comparisons, redolent of the Texas sharpshooter fallacy.a The statistical analysis remains entirely legitimate; the hypothesis does not. It is precisely this juxtaposition that makes the satire intellectually effective.

The philosophical framing is similarly thought provoking. Although the manuscript invokes reductio ad absurdum,b it functions more as epistemic parody by exposing weaknesses in reasoning rather than formally deriving a contradiction. Moreover, the tongue-in-cheek invocation of the Sieve of Eratosthenesc illustrates how mathematical elegance can be conflated with biological significance when persuasive narratives replace mechanistic reasoning.

Perhaps the manuscript's greatest strength is its value as a teaching resource. It is well suited to journal clubs, evidence-based medicine curricula, research methodology courses, philosophy of science seminars, and teaching about cognitive bias and its role in clinical reasoning. Its importance lies in demonstrating how cognitive heuristics, arbitrary categorisation, and large datasets can combine to produce statistically convincing, yet clinically meaningless, associations.

Medical education traditionally devotes considerable attention to performing statistical analyses correctly. Far less emphasis is placed on recognising when statistically impeccable analyses address scientifically uninteresting or biologically implausible questions. This manuscript reminds us that sophisticated methodology cannot rescue an arbitrary hypothesis, nor should statistical significance be mistaken for biological plausibility. The low P-values also help remind us that statistical significance does not mean clinical significance, even when biological plausibility exists. Large datasets are remarkably powerful tools, but they remain fully capable of generating persuasive nonsense when divorced from sound mechanistic reasoning.

There is an important place for using satire as a literary device within medical education. Satire permits assumptions to be examined without accusation, exposing habits that often pass unnoticed precisely because they are familiar. Like all successful scientific satire, this manuscript succeeds because its target is not clinical practice itself, but the uncritical acceptance of patterns that merely seem rational.

The paper should not persuade readers to adopt prime-number ventilator settings. If it encourages them instead to pause before accepting the next statistically significant observational association at face value—and to reflect upon the cognitive shortcuts that shape everyday clinical decisions—it will have achieved something considerably more valuable.
