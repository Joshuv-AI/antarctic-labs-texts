Empagliflozin Versus Dapagliflozin and Urogenital Adverse Events in Type 2 Diabetes: A Target Trial Emulation
Authors: Christine Ljungberg, Kasper Bonnesen, Morten Schmidt, Christina Vandenbroucke‐Grauls, Michael Dalager‐Pedersen, Henrik Toft Sørensen, Mette Nørgaard, Reimar Wernich Thomsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsUrogenital adverse events are common with sodium‐glucose co‐transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and may influence treatment decisions. Variations in SGLT2i selectivity, potency and effects on renal glucose excretion could lead to differences in safety profiles. We compared the risks of urinary tract infection (UTI), genital tract infection (GTI) and phimosis among adults with Type 2 diabetes who initiated empagliflozin versus dapagliflozin.Materials and MethodsThis population‐based, active‐comparator, new‐user cohort study emulating a target trial included adult metformin users initiating empagliflozin or dapagliflozin during 2015–2022. We identified UTIs and GTIs through pharmacy antimicrobial prescriptions and inpatient or outpatient diagnoses. Phimosis was identified through hospital diagnoses and procedure codes. Baseline characteristics were balanced using inverse probability of treatment weighting, and cumulative risks and risk ratios were estimated using an intention‐to‐treat approach.ResultsThe study population included 53 083 empagliflozin and 33 349 dapagliflozin initiators, with median follow‐up ranging from 2.6 to 3.8 years across outcomes. The weighted 1‐year UTI risk was similar between groups (empagliflozin 10.3% vs. dapagliflozin 10.6%, RR 0.98 [95% CI, 0.95–1.01]). Risks of GTI (2.2% vs. 2.0%, RR 1.14 [95% CI, 1.04–1.25]), and phimosis (1.0% vs. 0.8%, RR 1.23 [95% CI, 1.04–1.52]) were slightly higher with empagliflozin, though absolute risk differences were small. Safety profiles were consistent throughout 5‐year follow‐up.ConclusionsInitiation of empagliflozin versus dapagliflozin was associated with comparable absolute risks of urogenital adverse events, and slightly higher relative risks of GTI and phimosis among empagliflozin initiators, supporting broadly similar safety profiles.

Introduction

Patients with Type 2 diabetes are at elevated risk of urinary tract infections (UTIs), genital tract infections (GTIs) and phimosis [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Urogenital adverse events are common with sodium‐glucose co‐transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and may influence treatment decisions. SGLT2i lower blood glucose by inhibiting renal glucose reabsorption, causing glucosuria that may promote bacterial and fungal growth [7, 8]. While the increased risk of GTIs with SGLT2i use is well‐established [9, 10, 11, 12], evidence for UTIs in women and men remains inconsistent: randomised trials show very modest UTI risk increases, and large real‐world comparisons with glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RAs) use suggest no increased risk [12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. In contrast, we have previously reported a nearly two‐fold increased risk of phimosis, that is, constriction of the prepuce (foreskin), among males with Type 2 diabetes who initiate SGLT2i versus GLP‐1RAs [17].

SGLT2i are generally regarded as a drug class with consistent metabolic, renal and cardiovascular benefits [18, 19] and current clinical guidelines do not recommend one specific agent over another [20], despite limited comparative safety evidence between individual agents. In Denmark, empagliflozin and dapagliflozin account for nearly all SGLT2i use [21]. Although their mechanisms of action are similar, differences in SGLT2 selectivity, potency and effects on renal glucose and sodium reabsorption may lead to variation in their safety profiles [22]. However, direct comparative evidence on urogenital safety for empagliflozin and dapagliflozin is limited. One large target‐trial emulation cohort study reported comparable risks of severe UTI and a slightly lower risk of genital infections with dapagliflozin versus empagliflozin (hazard ratio [HR] 0.92; 95% CI, 0.89–0.95) [23], while few small randomized and observational studies have yielded inconsistent findings [24, 25, 26]. Importantly, prior comparative studies have focused on hospitalized urogenital infections and have not been able to evaluate phimosis, which is frequently encountered in older men in clinical practice.

Understanding potential differences in SGLT2i urogenital safety may help inform optimal drug selection in clinical practice. We therefore conducted a large population‐based cohort study, emulating a target trial, to examine comparative risks of UTI, GTI and phimosis among adults with Type 2 diabetes initiating empagliflozin versus dapagliflozin.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Data Sources

This cohort study emulated a target trial (i.e., a hypothetical pragmatic trial designed to address the causal question of interest) comparing the risk of UTI, GTI and phimosis in empagliflozin versus dapagliflozin initiators with Type 2 diabetes. Table S1 outlines the key components of the target trial and its emulation [27, 28].

The trial emulation was conducted in Denmark, where the tax‐funded healthcare system requires registration of all healthcare encounters in nationwide databases. Individual‐level linkage across registries is possible using the unique personal identifier assigned to all residents at birth or immigration [29]. We used the following data sources: the Danish Civil Registration System, which provides information on residence, migration and vital status since 1968 [29]; the Danish National Patient Registry, which includes records of inpatient non‐psychiatric hospitalisations since 1977 and all outpatient clinic, inpatient and emergency department contacts since 1995 [30]; the Danish National Prescription Registry, which contains data on all reimbursed prescriptions dispensed at community pharmacies since 1995 [31]; and the Register of Laboratory Results for Research, which contains nationwide biochemical test results since 2015 [32].

Eligibility Criteria

We identified all individuals aged ≥ 18 years initiating dapagliflozin or empagliflozin in Denmark between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2022. The index date was defined as the date of the first reimbursed prescription for either drug. Individuals with any prior SGLT2i use were excluded. To ensure inclusion of patients who had Type 2 diabetes receiving usual care, cohort members were required to have redeemed at least one prescription for metformin with or without other glucose‐lowering therapies in the preceding year. In accordance with Danish diabetes guidelines in place during the study period, patients with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 were ineligible for SGLT2i treatment and therefore excluded. The eGFR value used was the most recent measurement prior to treatment initiation. Only individuals with at least 12 months of residence in Denmark before the index date were included to ensure complete baseline data.

Treatment Strategy

Eligible patients were assigned at baseline to one of two strategies, either initiating empagliflozin or initiating dapagliflozin at the maximal tolerated dose (i.e., 10 mg or 25 mg for empagliflozin and 5 mg or 10 mg for dapagliflozin). The treatment strategy was defined on the basis of the first SGLT2i prescription redeemed [33]. All other glucose‐lowering therapies were allowed as background treatment, reflecting routine clinical care. Given the lack of randomisation, we used stabilised inverse probability of treatment (IPT) weights.

Outcomes and Covariates

UTI, GTI and phimosis were assessed as separate outcomes and identified from the Danish National Patient Registry and the Danish National Prescription Registry. UTI was defined as the first occurrence of either a hospital inpatient or outpatient clinic diagnosis of nephritis, renal abscess, cystitis, urethritis or urethral abscess or as redemption of a prescription for a urinary tract‐specific antibiotic. According to Danish guidelines, all recommended first‐line agents (sulfamethizole, trimethoprim, pivmecillinam or nitrofurantoin) used to treat UTI are very rarely used to treat other infections [34, 35]. GTI was defined as the first occurrence of either an inpatient or outpatient hospital diagnosis of candidiasis of the vulva, vagina or other urogenital sites, balanitis, vaginitis or vulvitis or as redemption of a reimbursed prescription for the genital tract‐specific antifungal agent clotrimazole, the recommended first‐line treatment for balanitis and vulvovaginitis in Denmark [36]. Phimosis was defined as a hospital diagnosis identified in inpatient or outpatient records of male participants.

Data on covariates were obtained from nationwide registries and are listed in Tables 1 and S2. These included glucose‐lowering drug therapies, Type 2 diabetes complications, Type 2 diabetes duration, glycosylated haemoglobin (HbA1c) level, eGFR level, presence of any urogenital pathology, social and frailty markers, a range of other comorbidities and comedications.

Causal Contrast

We compared initiators of dapagliflozin with initiators of empagliflozin using an intention‐to‐treat approach, classifying individuals according to their initial treatment on their index date and following them for the entire study period, regardless of subsequent changes in therapy.

Statistical Analyses

To minimise unmeasured confounding between treatment groups in our active comparator design, we used IPT weighting. Propensity scores (PSs) were computed using logistic regression models predicting the probability of initiating dapagliflozin versus empagliflozin. Stabilised IPT weights then were calculated by dividing the overall proportion of dapagliflozin users by each PS for dapagliflozin and the overall proportion of empagliflozin users by one minus the PS for empagliflozin users. Covariate balance after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences, with values below 0.1 indicating adequate balance. We described baseline characteristics for both crude and IPT‐weighted populations. As phimosis occurs only in males, analyses were restricted to males with propensity scores and IPT weights re‐estimated within this subpopulation using the same covariate set.

The primary analyses followed an intention‐to‐treat approach, in which participants were considered exposed starting on their index date and continuing throughout follow‐up, without censoring at the time of drug discontinuation or a switch. Follow‐up continued until the occurrence of an outcome, death, emigration, a maximum of 5 years or study end (January 31, 2024), whichever came first.

IPT‐weighted cause‐specific cumulative risk curves for UTI, GTI and phimosis were estimated using the Aalen–Johansen method, accounting for death as a competing risk. From these curves, we derived 1‐, 3‐ and 5‐year cumulative risks, risk differences (RDs) and risk ratios (RRs) comparing dapagliflozin and empagliflozin users [37]. We used bootstrapping with 500 samples to obtain 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Complete case analysis was conducted.

We repeated all outcome analyses among dapagliflozin and empagliflozin initiators stratified by different baseline characteristics, including sex, age, HbA1c, eGFR and diabetes duration.

All analyses were performed using R, version 4.3.

Sensitivity Analyses

We first conducted an on‐treatment analysis, in which individuals were censored at the time of treatment discontinuation or initiation of a drug from the comparator class. Treatment duration was estimated by summing the number of days supplied for each redeemed prescription, calculated as the number of packages dispensed multiplied by the number of doses per package. To accommodate suboptimal adherence and dose adjustments among new users, we added a 180‐day grace period to the last treatment period, applied prospectively from the end of each prescription.

We then examined more severe cases of each outcome. For UTI and GTI, severity was defined as the condition being the primary reason for a hospital contact, while for phimosis, severity was defined by the presence of a relevant surgical procedure code.

Data and Resource Availability

Under Danish law, researchers are not permitted to share raw registry data with third parties. To protect patient privacy, the combined dataset used in this study is accessible only through the National Danish Health Data Authority. This study was registered at Aarhus University's research repository (No. 2016‐051‐000001/810; FSEID No. 00003734).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

We identified 119 406 empagliflozin and dapagliflozin users. After applying eligibility criteria, the study population included 53 083 empagliflozin initiators and 33 349 dapagliflozin initiators (Figure S1).

Already before IPT weighting, the two cohorts were similar with respect to clinical characteristics. The most notable differences were observed in relation to calendar year of SGLT2i initiation, probably reflecting differences in SGLT2i market entry, time of publication of their major outcome trials, and availability of other alternative glucose‐lowering drugs. Empagliflozin and dapagliflozin initiators had a comparable median age (63 versus 65 years), very similar proportions of male users (62.2% vs. 62.4%) and the same median diabetes duration (8 years). Additionally, they had similar prevalences of hospital‐registered obesity (15.9% versus 14.8%) and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (26.3% versus 26.2%). However, empagliflozin initiators were more likely to use insulin therapy (19.2% versus 14.4%) and had a lower prevalence of heart failure (6.8% versus 9.4%) and loop diuretic use (13.0% versus 16.0%) compared to dapagliflozin initiators. IPT weighting reduced all standardized mean differences to ≤ 0.01, except for initiation year (Figure S2). Baseline characteristics in the IPT‐weighted male population used for the phimosis analysis were similar to those of the overall weighted cohort (Table S3).

During the five‐year follow‐up period, 47% of empagliflozin initiators and 46% of dapagliflozin initiators discontinued treatment, while 1% of empagliflozin discontinuers and 4% of dapagliflozin users initiated the comparator study drug class (Figure S3).

Urinary Tract Infections

The median follow‐up was 3.1 years [IQR, 1.7–5.0 years] among empagliflozin initiators and 2.6 years [IQR, 1.5–4.7 years] among dapagliflozin initiators.

The 1‐year risk of UTI was nearly identical for empagliflozin and dapagliflozin initiators: 10.3% (95% CI, 10.1–10.6) for empagliflozin versus 10.6% (95% CI, 10.3–10.9) for dapagliflozin. The corresponding RD was −0.2% (95% CI, −0.6–0.1) and the RR was 0.98 (95% CI, 0.95–1.01). After up to 5 years of follow‐up, the risks of UTI remained similar at 27.2% (95% CI, 26.7% to 27.6%) for empagliflozin and 27.6% (95% CI, 27.0% to 28.2%) for dapagliflozin initiators (RD: −0.5% [95% CI, −1.2% to 0.2%], RR: 0.98 [95% CI, 0.96–1.01]) (Table 2 and Figure 1).

Genital Tract Infections

The median follow‐up in the GTI analyses was 3.7 years [IQR, 2.2–5.0 years] for empagliflozin and 3.1 years [IQR, 1.9–5.0] for dapagliflozin initiators.

Risks of subsequent GTI were slightly higher among empagliflozin initiators compared with dapagliflozin initiators, although absolute differences were very small. The 1‐year risk was 2.2% (95% CI, 2.1% to 2.4%) for empagliflozin and 2.0% (95% CI, 1.8% to 2.1%) for dapagliflozin initiators (RD: 0.3% [95% CI, 0.1% to 0.5%], RR 1.14 [95% CI, 1.04% to 1.25%]). After up to 5 years of follow‐up, the risk of GTI was 5.1% (95% CI, 4.9% to 5.3%) for empagliflozin and 4.7% (95% CI, 4.4% to 5.0%) for dapagliflozin initiators (RD: 0.4% [95% CI, 0.0% to 0.7%], RR: 1.07 [95% CI, 1.00–1.16]) (Table 2 and Figure 1). Consistently, in subgroup analyses the risk ratios for genital infections were slightly above one, indicating a slightly higher risk with empagliflozin, particularly among females, who, as expected, also had a higher baseline risk of genital infections (Figures 2, S4 and S5).

Phimosis

The median follow‐up was 3.8 years [IQR, 2.3–5.0 years] among male empagliflozin initiators and 3.1 years [IQR, 1.9–5.0 years] among male dapagliflozin initiators.

The risks of phimosis were slightly higher for empagliflozin initiators compared to dapagliflozin initiators; however, the absolute differences were small. The 1‐year risk was 1.0% (95% CI, 0.9% to 1.1%) for empagliflozin and 0.8% (95% CI, 0.7% to 0.9%) for dapagliflozin initiators, corresponding to an RD of 0.2% (95% CI, 0.0% to 0.4%) and an RR of 1.23 (95% CI, 1.04–1.52). The 5‐year risk of phimosis was 3.7% (95% CI, 3.5% to 4.0%) for empagliflozin and 3.5% (95% CI, 3.3% to 3.9%) for dapagliflozin initiators (RD 0.2% [95% CI, −0.2% to 0.6%], RR: 1.06 [95% CI, 0.94–1.18]) (Table 2 and Figure 1).

Across subgroups, the risk ratios for phimosis similarly favoured dapagliflozin. Analyses suggested numerically higher phimosis risk among empagliflozin initiators with eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2; however, these subgroup estimates were based on small numbers and should be interpreted cautiously (Figures 2, S4 and S5).

Sensitivity Analyses

The on‐treatment analyses yielded results consistent with the primary findings across all outcomes. For UTIs, the 1‐year RR was 0.98 (95% CI, 0.95–1.02) and the 5‐year RR was 0.98 (95% CI, 0.95–1.02) for empagliflozin versus dapagliflozin initiators. For genital infections, the 1‐year RR was 1.14 (95% CI, 1.04–1.26) and the 5‐year RR was 1.08 (95% CI, 0.99–1.19). For phimosis, the 1‐year RR was 1.24 (95% CI, 1.03–1.51) and the 5‐year RR was 1.17 (95% CI, 1.02–1.35) (Table S4 and Figure S6).

For severe UTI, risks were comparable between empagliflozin and dapagliflozin users, with a 1‐year RR of 1.06 (95% CI, 0.92–1.25) and a 5‐year RR of 1.06 (95% CI, 0.97–1.16). Severe genital infections were rare, with no clear differences between treatments (1‐year RR: 1.27 [95% CI, 0.64–3.12], 5‐year RR: 1.05 [95% CI, 0.69–1.67]). Severe phimosis showed slightly higher risks with empagliflozin, with a 1‐year RR of 1.28 (95% CI, 1.04–1.63) and a 5‐year RR of 1.06 (95% CI, 0.94–1.20) (Table S5 and Figure S7).

Discussion

In this population‐based cohort study emulating a target trial, we observed broadly comparable absolute risks of urogenital adverse events among empagliflozin and dapagliflozin initiators with Type 2 diabetes. UTI risks were nearly identical between the two drugs. Relative risks for GTI and phimosis were slightly higher with empagliflozin, although risk differences were small. The comparable safety profiles were consistent over 5 years of follow‐up and across subgroups.

Evidence directly comparing dapagliflozin and empagliflozin is limited and somewhat conflicting. Shin et al.'s large US comparative‐effectiveness study using a target‐trial emulation included 129 881 dapagliflozin and 295 043 empagliflozin initiators with a mean follow‐up of ~12 months. They observed a comparable risk of severe UTI (HR 1.06, [95% CI, 0.97–1.17]) and a modestly lower risk of genital infections with dapagliflozin (HR 0.92 [95% CI, 0.89–0.95]) [23]. Two small Pakistani RCTs (n = 305 and n = 255) suggested slightly lower UTI and genital infection risks with empagliflozin [24, 25], whereas a smaller US cohort study (n < 2000) suggested trends favoring dapagliflozin [26]. Overall, both the study by Shin et al. [23] and our study indicate that empagliflozin and dapagliflozin have broadly comparable urogenital risk profiles; however, our study extends prior evidence by incorporating much longer follow‐up and inclusion of routine‐care community‐based outcomes. Importantly, none of the prior studies assessed phimosis, and our findings indicate a slightly higher risk with empagliflozin use, providing new population‐level evidence on this outcome. Furthermore, we incorporated key laboratory values, including HbA1c and eGFR, in our IPT weighting, as these are known risk factors [6].

A potential explanation for the slightly higher risks observed with empagliflozin may relate to pharmacodynamic differences between the two agents, although such interpretations remain speculative given the small absolute risk differences seen in our observational study design. Genital infections are a recognized class effect of SGLT2i and are attributed to therapy‐induced glucosuria, which may promote local microbial growth [7, 9, 10, 11, 12] Empagliflozin has higher selectivity ratios for SGLT2 over SGLT1 than dapagliflozin, which could result in slightly more urinary glucose excretion [22, 38, 39, 40]. This could theoretically contribute to more favourable conditions for microbial proliferation. A similar mechanism may contribute to phimosis, as recurrent infections and associated inflammation in the preputial space are established contributors to acquired phimosis [4, 41]. However, further studies are warranted to confirm these findings and explore potential mechanisms underlying any differences between individual SGLT2i.

A strength of our study was the use of population‐based registries, which ensured complete follow‐up and allowed us to identify all empagliflozin and dapagliflozin initiators in Denmark. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered. First, despite the high quality of Danish registries [30, 31, 32], some misclassification of urogenital outcomes is possible. Our UTI definition may have missed episodes treated with antibiotics that have multiple indications, as these were not included in our definition. In Danish primary care, urine culture is generally advised before initiating antibiotics for suspected UTI in patients with Type 2 diabetes [42]. Therefore, prescription‐defined UTIs in our study are likely to reflect clinically confirmed community‐treated episodes. Still, GTIs treated exclusively with over‐the‐counter topical agents were not captured in the prescription registry, potentially leading to underestimation of absolute GTI rates. Similarly, our phimosis definition captures clinically recognized and hospital‐managed cases, potentially underestimating absolute risks. Nonetheless, the sources of misclassification are unlikely to differ between initiators of the two drugs and would therefore be expected to be nondifferential, leading to attenuated relative estimates. Second, diagnoses made by primary care physicians are not generally available in the Danish registries. We therefore relied on prescription data from primary care to identify community‐treated infections. This may have led us to miss milder episodes not treated pharmacologically while capturing clinically relevant cases. Third, our analytic strategies have some limitations. The intention‐to‐treat approach estimates the effect of treatment initiation and is less vulnerable to informative censoring, but treatment discontinuation and switching during follow‐up may have attenuated true associations. To address potential exposure misclassification, we additionally conducted on‐treatment analyses censoring individuals at discontinuation or upon switching to the comparator drug. Although this approach reduces exposure misclassification, it may introduce informative censoring because the reasons for stopping and switching treatment are uncertain. The relative risk estimates for GTI and phimosis were modestly attenuated during longer follow‐up in the intention‐to‐treat analyses, whereas corresponding on‐treatment estimates remained more stable. However, both approaches showed similar effect estimates, supporting the comparable safety profiles of empagliflozin and dapagliflozin. Fourth, empagliflozin and dapagliflozin initiators differed by calendar year, reflecting evolving prescribing patterns over the study period. Although we were unable to balance calendar time, other baseline characteristics were well balanced. In stratified analyses, RRs for GTI and phimosis were slightly higher in earlier calendar years and attenuated in later periods, while estimates for UTI remained stable. These findings indicate some temporal heterogeneity; nonetheless, the direction and magnitude of estimates remained broadly consistent indicating that calendar‐time imbalance is unlikely to be a major driver of observed associations. Finally, although we used an active‐comparator design and weighting to balance key clinical and laboratory characteristics, some uncontrolled confounding cannot be excluded. Detailed data on lifestyle factors, socioeconomic status, hygiene‐related behaviours and BMI were not available. However, such factors would need to be both associated with the specific SGLT2i agent and with the likelihood of urogenital outcomes to bias the relative estimates. In Danish clinical practice, empagliflozin and dapagliflozin are prescribed interchangeably for Type 2 diabetes [43], reducing the likelihood of systematic differences between the treatment groups. To specifically address BMI as a potential confounder, we assessed BMI levels among empagliflozin and dapagliflozin users in an external Danish clinical cohort of patients with Type 2 diabetes [44] and observed a very similar BMI level in the two groups (Table S7). This finding indicates that the two treatment groups are likely comparable, making major residual confounding unlikely.

In conclusion, empagliflozin and dapagliflozin initiators have broadly similar absolute risks of urogenital adverse events in patients with Type 2 diabetes. Although empagliflozin was associated with slightly higher relative risks of GTI and phimosis, the absolute differences between the two drugs were small, indicating largely comparable safety profiles. Observed differences should be interpreted cautiously, given the small absolute risk differences.
