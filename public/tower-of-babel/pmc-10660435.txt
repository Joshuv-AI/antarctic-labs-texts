Can online and app-based interventions be used by people with diabetes to reduce diabetes distress? A protocol for a scoping review
Authors: Christina Maar Andersen, Anne Sophie Mathiesen, François Pouwer, Julie Drotner Mouritsen, Kim Mathiasen, Mette Juel Rothmann
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDiabetes distress has been defined as "the negative emotional or affective experience resulting from the challenge of living with the demands of diabetes". Diabetes distress affects 20%–25% of individuals living with diabetes and can have negative effects on both diabetes regulation and quality of life. For people living with diabetes distress, innovative tools/interventions such as online or app-based interventions may potentially alleviate diabetes distress in a cost-effective way. The specific research questions of this scoping review are: (1) what are the effects of online or app-based interventions on diabetes distress for adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and (2) what are the characteristics of these interventions (eg, type of intervention, duration, frequency, mode of delivery, underlying theories and working mechanisms)?Methods and analysisA scoping review will be conducted, using the methodological framework of Arksey and O’Malley along with Levac et al. Eligible studies are: studies of adults ≥18 years old with type 1 or 2 diabetes using an online or app-based intervention and assessing diabetes distress as the primary or secondary outcome. Five databases (Medline, EMBASE, CINAHL, PsycINFO and Scopus) will be searched and is limited to articles written in English, Danish, Norwegian, Swedish or Dutch. Two reviewers will independently screen potentially eligible studies in Covidence, select studies, and together chart data, collate, summarise, and report the results. We will adhere to the Preferred reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).Ethics and disseminationThe scoping review has been exempt from full ethical review by the Regional Committees on Health Research Ethics for Southern Denmark (case number: S-20232000-88). The results of the review will be published in a peer-reviewed journal and presented at relevant conferences and workshops with relevant stakeholders.

Introduction

The term diabetes distress refers to "the negative emotional or affective experience resulting from the challenge of living with the demands of diabetes",1 including experiencing lack of support and understanding from others, difficulties communicating with healthcare professionals and distress from the burden of lifestyle changes.2 Diabetes distress occurs on a continuum from low to high and it can fluctuate over time and may peak during challenging periods for example after diagnosis and following complications.3 Diabetes distress is not a mental illness, but it can contribute to the development of psychiatric problems such as anxiety and depression and vice versa.4 It has recently been reported in a study among Canadian people with type 2 diabetes that particularly regimen-related and physician-related diabetes-specific distress are key contributors to elevated diabetes distress.5 Furthermore, the symptom of depression ‘feeling of failure’ was related to the items of the Diabetes Distress Scale-17 and may potentially explain the overlap between diabetes distress and depression.5 A high degree of diabetes distress does not only negatively affect the individual’s quality of life, but is also associated with suboptimal diabetes self-management including insufficient physical activity, unhealthy eating habits, lower medication intake, less frequent blood glucose measurements and elevated glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c).3 Optimal diabetes self-management and well-being are crucial for prognosis.6 However, international studies report that approximately 20%–25% of people with diabetes experience elevated diabetes distress.3 7 In people with type 2 diabetes, the 18-month incidence rate is as high as 45% indicating the magnitude of the problem. Further, according to the Diabetes Attitudes, Wishes, and Needs (DAWN) study, the prevalence increased from 2005 to 2013 suggesting that diabetes distress in people with diabetes is inadequately met by the healthcare system.8 Additionally, diabetes distress may be more severe and prolonged in subgroups of people with diabetes, for example, those with a low educational level, multiple comorbidities, living alone, smoking, unhealthy diet and with a sedentary lifestyle.9 Therefore, from an equity perspective, addressing diabetes distress is essential.

The American Diabetes Association proposed in a position statement that people with diabetes distress should only be referred to a mental health provider if self-care remains impaired after they have received tailored diabetes education.10 Therefore, given the high prevalence of diabetes distress and the limited resources in the healthcare systems,11–14 it is essential to develop innovative ways of supporting people with elevated diabetes distress, for example, with online or app-based interventions.

Lately, increasing evidence shows that therapist-supported online therapy is as effective as face-to-face therapy for many psychiatric and somatic disorders.15 During the last decade, more attention and effort have been applied to develop and test online interventions to improve psychological and self-management aspects of diabetes,16 17 but very few online interventions have been developed to directly target diabetes distress. A study by Bending et al. offered psychological treatment based on acceptance and commitment therapy for alleviating diabetes distress among 42 patients with type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The study found a significant reduction in diabetes distress from a score of 45.1 (13.8) preintervention to 23.9 (19.8) postintervention on the scale Problem Areas in Diabetes-20 with a Cohen’s d of −0.65.18 Other online or app interventions have been developed to target for example, depression, self-management or medication decision-making support in people with diabetes and even though these interventions were not developed to directly target diabetes distress, they report a decrease in diabetes distress.19–21 Online or app-based interventions have several advantages, including that the person with diabetes has the opportunity of receiving treatment whenever, and wherever it fits into their everyday life.22

The current review will focus on the effectiveness or efficacy of online and app-based interventions where diabetes distress has been assessed as an outcome. The specific research questions of this scoping review are: (1) What is the potential effect of online or app-based interventions on diabetes distress for adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes? (2) What are the characteristics of the interventions (eg, aim of intervention, duration, frequency, mode of delivery, underlying theories and working mechanisms)?

Methods

This review adheres to the methodological framework of Arksey and O’Malley23 further developed by Levac et al.24 The framework outlines six stages of a scoping review: (1) identifying the research question, (2) identifying the relevant literature, (3) selecting studies, (4) charting the data, (5) collating, summarising and reporting the data and (6) consulting relevant stakeholders. The review will follow the Preferred reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines for reporting scoping reviews.25

Patient and public involvement

People with diabetes will be involved in the consultation stage of this scoping review to validate the finding and inform future research in the area.

Stage 1: identifying the research question

The aim of this scoping review is to identify what is empirically known about the effects and characteristics of online and app-based interventions for lowering diabetes distress in adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes. It is recommended that a multidisciplinary research team combine a broad research question with a clearly articulated scope of inquiry.24 Accordingly, we adhere to the definition of online and app-based interventions developed by Barak et al. 26 "…a primarily self-guided intervention programme that is executed by means of a prescriptive online programme operated through a website and used by consumers seeking health-related and mental-health related assistance. The intervention programme itself attempts to create positive change and or improve/enhance knowledge, awareness and understanding via the provision of sound health-related material and use of interactive web-based components". To the above definition, we have added two amendments. Firstly, web-based interventions can be both guided and unguided. Secondly, the interventions are not necessarily operated through a website but can also be app-based.

It is recommended to thoroughly consider the rationale for conducting the scoping study to help clarify the purpose.24 Our rationale for conducting this review is elaborated in the introduction and is further specified below: it is intended that the findings from this review will inform further studies on online and app-based interventions for treating diabetes distress. Thus, it is anticipated that the findings of this review will guide the development (e.g., type of intervention, duration, frequency, mode of delivery, underlying theories and working mechanisms) of future interventions which could lead to improved treatment of diabetes distress among adults with diabetes. The research group behind this scoping review are developing an online intervention for people with type 1 diabetes and diabetes distress and will use the results to guide the development process. Regarding the development of the research question of this review, the broad population-concept-context mnemonic by Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) will be used as a suitable alternative to the population, intervention, comparator and outcome for systematic reviews.

Population

The target population consists of adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes (i.e., ≥18 years old). As we will include all online or app-based interventions where diabetes distress is assessed as a primary or secondary outcome, some of these interventions may not have been designed to decrease diabetes distress specifically, but instead to treat depression or for example, designed to improve diabetes self-management. However, we will not set a criterion concerning the level of diabetes distress at baseline, but discuss whether the mean baseline level of diabetes distress or the focus of the intervention could have impacted the study results.

Concept

Identifying what is known about online or app-based interventions and their characteristics to reduce diabetes distress for adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

Context

There are no geographical limitations regarding where the study has been conducted or published as diabetes distress is found among patients with diabetes worldwide.27 There will be no restriction of the setting (eg, hospital or primary practice) in which participants in the studies have been recruited from. Studies that focus on other forms of diabetes such as gestational diabetes will not be included.

To ensure a comprehensive search within health sciences, the following databases will be searched: Medline (Ovid), EMBASE (Ovid), CINAHL (Ebsco), PsycINFO (Ovid) and Scopus.

The search strategy will include title, abstract, keywords and related terms for the concept of diabetes, online or app-based intervention, and diabetes distress. The search will use block 1 (diabetes)+2 (online or app-based interventions)+3 (diabetes distress) (see table 1 for an example of the search strategy for Medline database). Papers from the searches will be imported in Covidence32 and duplets will be removed. Snowballing will be used on the included studies in the review searching for citations in the reference list (backward snowballing) but also identifying studies that cite the included studies (forward snowballing). The search will be performed on 31 March 2023.

Stage 3: study selection

Two authors will independently screen the titles and abstracts of identified literature for initial level 1 screening. Full text screening of selected literature from level 1 screening will be carried out to determine eligibility of the articles (level 2 screening). A third author will be available to resolve cases of disagreement.

Exclusion criteria include studies focused on other forms of eHealth, for example, telephone coaching and studies not measuring diabetes distress. Studies that include both participants at ≥18 years old and younger a decision will be made whether it is possible to pull separate data on those ≥18 years old. In cases where this is not possible the study will be excluded.

Stage 4: charting the data

A collective developed data extraction framework will be used to confirm study relevance and to extract study characteristics and results. The data extraction form will be continually reviewed and updated to ensure all necessary data will be collected. Two authors will independently extract data from the first 5–10 included studies using the data-charting form and determine whether their approach to data extraction is consistent with the research question and purpose.24 JBI’s critical appraisal tools will be used to assess the quality of the included studies. Different tools will be used for different study types (https://jbi.global/critical-appraisal-tools).

Stage 5: collating, summarising and reporting the results

The PRISMA-ScR checklist will be used to guide the reporting of the results of this review.25 To provide an overview of the breadth of the literature, the PRIMA flow chart from 2020 will be used to report the number of articles at each stage.34

The review will combine quantitative and qualitative syntheses to provide an overview of the findings. According to Levac et al.24 it is recommended to break this stage of the review into three distinct stages: analysis, reporting the result and consider the meaning of the findings.24 For the quantitative data, we will apply meta-analysis if appropriate to assess the potential effect of the intervention on decreasing diabetes distress. We will explore heterogeneity in subgroup analysis, for example, primary outcome (i.e., diabetes distress, depression or HbA1c) of the included studies. We will apply the random or the fixed effect, considering heterogeneity of the included studies.

For the qualitative data, we will use thematic analysis and the data will be grouped by primary outcome (i.e., diabetes distress, depression or HbA1c). These data will be coded and analysed manually to determine the key characteristics of the interventions (e.g., aim of intervention, duration, frequency, mode of delivery, underlying theories, working mechanisms).

Stage 6: consultation

Although Arksey and O’Malley propose the use of consultation by stakeholders as adding extra value to a scoping review, this stage is viewed as optional.23 However, Levac et al. stress the importance of this stage and argue that it should be considered a required component of scoping reviews.24 In this scoping review, stakeholders with knowledge about diabetes distress or eHealth will be presented the preliminary findings. The aim of this stage will be to use the stakeholders (end-users and experts) expert opinion to validating the findings of the scoping review and informing future research. The stakeholders will be contacted via email or phone and invited to share their feedback either my email, phone or video meeting.

Strengths and limitations

This review is expected to have several strengths. First, it is to our knowledge that the first scoping review to review and summarise research using online or app application for reducing diabetes distress among people with type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Second, it uses established scoping review methodology by Arksey and O’Malley and Levac et al. Furthermore, it addresses a common and burdensome problem, for which innovative solutions should be made available, as the number of psychologists is limited. Besides, the research team conducting the scoping review is a multidisciplinary team consisting of experts within the field of psychosocial research in diabetes and eHealth and persons with diabetes, using transparent and replicable processes to conduct the review.

The review will also have some limitations. While the search terms and included databases have been selected to capture all relevant studies, the rapid development of the technological area and technological terminology may pose a risk that not all relevant studies will be identified. Also, the review will be limited to published research literature and those papers published in English, Swedish, Norwegian, Danish or Dutch possibly missing out data on existing interventions not published on research platforms or in other languages.

Ethics and dissemination

The Regional Committees on Health Research Ethics for Southern Denmark assessed this protocol, but exempted it from full ethical review (case number: S-20232000-88).

To disseminate the results of the scoping review, the findings will be published in a peer-reviewed journal and presented at conferences and workshops with relevant stakeholders. The results of the review will also guide the development of an online intervention for people with diabetes distress by the research group behind the review.
