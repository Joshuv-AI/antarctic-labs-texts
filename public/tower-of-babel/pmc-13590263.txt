Translational Progress of Inorganic Nanotheranostic Platforms for Gynecologic Malignancies: A Narrative Review of Endometrial, Ovarian, and Cervical Cancer Focusing on Fertility Preservation, Biosafety, and Industrial Translation
Authors: Yile Jin, Jiangang Wang, Na Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndometrial, ovarian, and cervical cancers differ substantially in epidemiology, anatomy, and unmet clinical need, yet the same inorganic nanomaterial toolbox is often discussed without disease-specific translational context. This narrative review synthesizes preclinical and clinical literature on gold, magnetic, mesoporous silica, carbon-based, and metal-organic framework nanotheranostic platforms identified through PubMed and Web of Science searches from database inception through August 2026, using a structured comparison across endometrial, ovarian, and cervical cancer. Particular attention is given to biosafety, reproductive toxicity, patient-derived organoid (PDO) models, AI-assisted design, manufacturing, and regulatory translation. Clinically, carbon nanoparticle suspensions have direct evidence for sentinel lymph node mapping in endometrial cancer, whereas the strongest ovarian cancer precedents for locoregional and nanomedicine delivery remain non-inorganic, including hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy and pegylated liposomal doxorubicin. In cervical disease, organic hexaminolevulinate photodynamic therapy for CIN2, approved by China’s NMPA in March 2026, provides a relevant local drug-device comparator. We identified no inorganic nanomedicine currently approved specifically for a gynecologic malignancy; ferumoxytol is approved as an intravenous iron replacement product, and AuroLase gold nanoshells remain investigational, with no gynecologic trial identified. Route-matched delivery - intrauterine or hysteroscopic for endometrial cancer, intraperitoneal for ovarian cancer, and topical or intracervical for cervical disease - may therefore be more clinically relevant than increasing platform complexity alone. EC- and OC-derived organoids provide increasingly mature disease models that could support nanotherapeutic screening, but direct nanomedicine validation remains limited, while AI-assisted design is constrained by small heterogeneous datasets and limited external validation. Future progress will depend on reproductive-safety endpoints, appropriately validated PDO models, standardized manufacturing and characterization, and regulatory strategies that clearly distinguish established evidence from preclinical promise.

Introduction

The three major gynecologic malignancies—corpus uteri (predominantly endometrial) cancer, ovarian cancer, and cervical cancer—together account for a substantial share of cancer incidence and mortality in women. According to GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates, corpus uteri cancer accounted for approximately 420,000 new cases and 97,000 deaths, ovarian cancer for approximately 324,000 new cases and 207,000 deaths, and cervical cancer for more than 660,000 new cases and approximately 348,000 deaths worldwide.1 Beyond their shared gynecologic origin, these are three epidemiologically and biologically distinct diseases. Endometrial cancer (EC) has become the most frequently diagnosed gynecologic malignancy in many high-income countries, with incidence rising in association with obesity and increasingly affecting premenopausal women.2 Ovarian cancer (OC) remains the most lethal gynecologic malignancy: the majority of patients are diagnosed with advanced-stage disease, population screening has failed to reduce mortality, and although poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors and anti-angiogenic agents have improved outcomes, most patients still relapse and die of platinum-resistant disease.3,4 Cervical cancer (CC), caused by persistent high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, is in principle preventable through vaccination and screening; yet approximately 90% of cases occur in low- and middle-income countries lacking organized programs, and the disease remains the fourth most common cancer in women worldwide.5

The unmet clinical needs of the three cancers differ accordingly. For selected young patients with early-stage endometrioid EC who wish to preserve fertility, progestin-based conservative therapy—oral progestins or the levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system (LNG-IUS)—is a fertility-sparing option; a meta-analysis of 25 studies (821 women with stage IA grade 1–2 disease) found a pooled complete response rate of only 63.4% with a recurrence rate of 29.6%, and pooled live birth rates of 39.3%.6 For OC, the central unmet needs are earlier detection and effective control of peritoneal and platinum-resistant disease.3,4 For CC, excisional treatment of high-grade premalignant lesions (conization, large loop excision) is effective but damages cervical structure and increases the risks of cervical insufficiency, miscarriage, and preterm birth—a particular problem for young women in whom these lesions are most commonly diagnosed—while radical trachelectomy preserves fertility in selected low-risk early-stage cancers.5 These divergent needs define distinct translational requirements for nanotheranostic platforms.

Inorganic nanomaterials with tunable size, morphology, and optical or magnetic properties create opportunities to address these distinct clinical needs. Gold, magnetic, mesoporous silica, carbon-based, and metal-organic framework (MOF) platforms can serve as imaging contrast agents, biosensors, drug carriers, and photothermal or photodynamic transducers. Importantly, the relevant route of administration differs by disease anatomy: the endometrial cavity is accessible through the cervix, enabling intrauterine or hysteroscopic delivery; ovarian cancer commonly disseminates within the peritoneal cavity, favoring intraperitoneal strategies; and the cervix is directly accessible for topical or intracervical administration. Carbon nanoparticle suspensions have accumulated clinical evidence for sentinel lymph node mapping in endometrial cancer.7–9 In ovarian cancer, hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy improved recurrence-free and overall survival in a Phase III trial,10 and pegylated liposomal doxorubicin (PLD), an organic liposomal nanomedicine, has long been used for recurrent disease.3,4 In cervical disease, APL-1702 hexaminolevulinate photodynamic therapy for CIN2 provides a clinically relevant local light-based precedent despite its organic photosensitizer; the drug-device product received NMPA approval in China in March 2026.11–13 These examples are used here as translational anchors rather than as evidence that inorganic gynecologic nanotherapeutics have already reached routine care.

Most previously published reviews on inorganic nanomaterials discuss structural optimization and in vitro antitumor activity with limited attention to cancer-specific clinical translation. Pei et al14 comprehensively reviewed bioactive inorganic nanomaterials for cancer theranostics but did not address gynecologic tumor specificity or fertility preservation. Rai et al15 summarized bioengineered nanomaterials broadly without discussing reproductive toxicity, PDO evaluation, or industrial translation. Aza et al16 reviewed nanotheranostics for gynecologic cancers in resource-limited settings, and Ke et al7 cataloged nanoparticle applications in EC. More recent literature has examined cervical nanocarriers,17 polymeric nanoparticles across gynecologic cancers,18 field-level bibliometric trends,19 Nectin-4-centered theranostics,20 and personalized/precision nanomedicine in OC.21 These studies demonstrate rapid growth of the field but do not provide the same route-by-cancer comparison of EC, OC, and CC together with fertility preservation, reproductive safety, PDOs, AI, manufacturing, and regulatory distance. Literature Search Strategy

This is a narrative review; no protocol was registered and no quantitative synthesis was performed. PubMed and Web of Science were searched from database inception through August 2026 without language restriction. Search concepts combined gynecologic disease terms (“endometrial cancer”, “endometrial carcinoma”, “corpus uteri”, “ovarian cancer”, “cervical cancer”, and “cervical intraepithelial neoplasia”) with nanomaterial and theranostic terms (“inorganic nanoparticle”, “gold nanoparticle”, “iron oxide”, “superparamagnetic”, “mesoporous silica”, “quantum dot”, “carbon dot”, “carbon nanoparticle”, “metal-organic framework”, “photothermal”, “photodynamic”, and “theranostic”). Additional targeted searches addressed fertility preservation, reproductive toxicity, organoids, artificial intelligence, manufacturing/regulation, hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy, sentinel lymph node mapping, ferumoxytol, and AuroLase. ClinicalTrials.gov was additionally searched to identify and verify relevant registered clinical studies, NCT identifiers, and recruitment/status information for clinical-stage platforms discussed in the review. Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance to EC, OC, or CC, followed by full-text assessment when a study informed material performance, disease-specific delivery, biosafety, fertility preservation, organoid/AI methodology, or clinical/regulatory translation. Priority was given to original gynecologic preclinical or clinical studies, landmark trials, current regulatory or trial records, and recent reviews needed for contextual comparison. Purely organic platforms were included only as clearly labeled comparator precedents. Because this was a narrative synthesis, study selection was purposive rather than PRISMA-based and thematic weighting reflects author judgment; this limitation is acknowledged below.

Fundamentals and Structure-Activity Relationships of Inorganic Nanomaterials

Size and Morphology Effects: From EPR Effect to Active Targeting

Particle size and morphology influence circulation, tissue penetration, clearance, cellular uptake, and optical or magnetic behavior. Although the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect can support passive tumor accumulation, its magnitude varies markedly among tumors and patients, and systemic delivery often yields low intratumoral deposition.15,22 This limitation is especially relevant in gynecologic oncology because the three diseases offer different opportunities for local administration: intrauterine or hysteroscopic access in EC, intraperitoneal access in OC, and topical or intracervical access in CC. Consequently, particle dimensions should be optimized together with route, target tissue, and intended function rather than treated as a universal design variable.

Active targeting can supplement, but not eliminate, these delivery constraints. Nanorods, nanostars, and mesoporous structures alter surface area and cellular interactions,23 while ligand-directed or molecularly responsive platforms use antibodies, peptides, receptor-binding motifs, or disease-associated nucleic-acid signals to enrich or trigger activity in selected tumor models. Examples include ITGA7-directed siRNA delivery in obesity-associated EC models,24 folate-receptor targeting in OC,25,26 a miR-21-responsive DNA-origami nanocarrier tested in ovarian cancer cells,27 and locally deployed cervical platforms.28,29 These examples remain predominantly preclinical and should be interpreted as disease-specific design strategies rather than evidence of clinical targeting efficiency.

Surface Chemical Engineering: PEGylation, Ligand Density, and Charge Regulation

Surface chemical properties dominate the in vivo stability, biocompatibility, and targeting capacity of inorganic nanomaterials. PEGylation, the classical modification strategy, grafts hydrophilic polyethylene glycol chains onto nanoparticle surfaces to form a steric barrier that blocks nonspecific serum protein adsorption, prolongs circulation half-life, and reduces reticuloendothelial clearance.22 Excessive PEGylation, however, can hinder interaction with target cells and can trigger accelerated blood clearance upon repeated dosing, requiring careful tuning of chain length and grafting density.

Surface functionalization with targeting ligands can increase receptor-mediated uptake, while surface charge influences protein adsorption, circulation, and nonspecific cellular interaction.30 TMTP1-conjugated nanocarriers have enhanced tumor accumulation in cervical cancer models,28 and folate- or EGFR-directed approaches have been explored in OC and EC.24–26 However, reproductive-age safety datasets remain sparse: long-term ovarian reserve, uterine retention, implantation-related endpoints, and repeated-dose reproductive effects are rarely integrated into nanomaterial optimization.31,32 This evidence gap is particularly relevant when platforms are proposed for younger patients with EC or CC.

Tumor Microenvironment-Responsive Design: PH, GSH, Enzymes, and Hypoxia

The tumor microenvironment (TME) displays physiological signatures distinct from normal tissues—acidic pH, elevated glutathione (GSH), upregulated proteases, and hypoxia—which can serve as endogenous triggers for stimuli-responsive nanomaterials. Nanocarriers engineered to undergo protonation or bond cleavage under low pH can accelerate payload liberation within tumor interstitium and intracellular compartments.14 High intratumoral GSH can cleave disulfide-crosslinked nanovehicles: programmed cross-linked systems have enabled redox-responsive release in multidrug-resistant OC models,33 while reduction-sensitive polymer nanoparticles co-delivering artesunate and isosorbide 5-mononitrate illustrate a related strategy in OC.34 These findings support redox-responsive design in ovarian cancer, but extrapolation to other gynecologic tumors requires disease-specific validation. Tumor-associated proteases can likewise be used as release switches,35 and hypoxia-responsive designs have been discussed in EC-related and broader nanomedicine contexts.7,24 Exogenous physical triggers provide an additional proof of principle: ultrasound-responsive folate-modified liposomes—an organic comparator—permit externally timed gemcitabine release.36 This concept may inform inorganic carrier development, but direct transferability cannot be assumed. For the locoregional delivery routes discussed in Route-First Design: Intrauterine, Intraperitoneal, and Intracervical Delivery, formulation design should account for compartment-specific physiological conditions, including uterine cavity and endometrial tissue remodeling, peritoneal fluid dynamics, and cervical mucus barriers.

Synergistic Mechanisms of Multimodal Integration

Multifunctional platforms can combine imaging with therapy or integrate two therapeutic modalities. In preclinical OC models, photothermal heating can increase local drug release and penetration, and photoimmunotherapy can couple light-triggered tumor injury with immunomodulation.35,37 Such combinations are mechanistically attractive, but added components also increase characterization, manufacturing, and regulatory requirements. For gynecologic translation, therapeutic gain should therefore be weighed against platform complexity, reproducibility, route feasibility, and reproductive safety rather than assuming that additional functions necessarily improve clinical value.

Increasing platform complexity can create additional characterization, manufacturing, and regulatory barriers to translation. Each added functional module requires validation of its contribution, reproducibility, stability, and interactions with the complete formulation (see Clinical Translation Status of Inorganic Nanomaterials). The clinical precedents discussed in Intraoperative Lymphatic Figureping: A Three-Cancer Comparison, 4.5, and 7.1 suggest that relatively simple, locally deployed approaches may offer practical translational advantages, although this relationship should be evaluated case by case rather than treated as a universal rule.

Applications of Inorganic Nanomaterials in Diagnosis

Gold Nanoparticle-Based Imaging and Biosensing

Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) exhibit strong potential for imaging and biosensing owing to their localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR). Modulating AuNP size and morphology—nanorods, nanoshells, nanostars—shifts absorption peaks into the near-infrared (NIR) window, rendering them suitable for photoacoustic imaging (PAI) and surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS). In preclinical gynecologic oncology, photoacoustic strategies have been applied to circulating tumor cell detection in OC models,38 and NIR-responsive nanoparticles with dual-modal imaging guidance have been used for targeted combination therapy in OC xenografts.39 Analogous EC- and CC-specific PAI studies remain scarce; application to local lesion characterization in these anatomically accessible sites remains a research direction requiring direct disease-specific validation.

AuNP-based SERS biosensing is closer to clinical deployment in diagnostics. An active SERS substrate composed of gold nanoparticles and porous silicon photonic crystals, combined with multivariate statistical algorithms, achieved an overall screening accuracy of 97.77% in serum from patients with cervical and breast cancers.40 Dual light-scattering and fluorescence sensing of cancer antigen 125 (CA125) using gold nanoparticle probes has achieved picomolar-level detection,41 and AuNP/GaN Schottky-junction photoelectrochemical aptasensors have reached comparable sensitivity for the same biomarker.42 Beyond optical sensing, inorganic nanoparticulate contrast agents are also being developed for spectral CT, although these applications remain largely preclinical and are not specific to gynecologic oncology.43 For EC—a disease that still lacks an approved population screening test—nanoparticle-enabled analysis of endometrial aspirates or uterine lavage is conceptually attractive; for CC, blood-based nanoparticle sensing would require demonstration of added value over established HPV-based pathways before clinical adoption.16,40

Magnetic Nanoparticles for MRI: Promise and the Ferumoxytol Reality Check

Superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) are efficient T2-weighted MRI contrast agents that shorten transverse relaxation of surrounding protons and sharpen tumor-to-tissue contrast. In an ovarian cancer cell model, a folate-functionalized PLGA platform containing magnetic nanoparticles generated concentration-dependent T2 MRI contrast while co-delivering hTERT siRNA and wortmannin.25 Related image-guided ovarian nanoplatforms are not uniformly magnetic or inorganic; for example, antibody-functionalized protein nanoparticles have enabled deep-red fluorescence imaging together with photothermal/photodynamic therapy and are cited here only as a non-inorganic comparator.44

Clinical translation of iron oxide imaging agents requires careful distinction between approved and investigational uses. Ferumoxytol (Feraheme) is FDA-approved as intravenous iron replacement for iron deficiency anemia, not for gynecologic oncologic imaging.45 Since 2015 it has carried a boxed warning for potentially fatal hypersensitivity reactions. Ferumoxytol-enhanced MRI has also been investigated in several non-gynecologic cancers.45,46 Ultrasmall superparamagnetic iron oxide-enhanced MRI can differentiate metastatic from benign lymph nodes with high sensitivity in several cancers,46 and a related agent, ferumoxtran-10, has been studied for lymph node imaging in pelvic malignancies.7 We identified no dedicated trial of ferumoxytol-enhanced MRI for lymph node staging in any of the three gynecologic cancers. A disease-specific opportunity remains plausible: in EC, nodal status informs adjuvant treatment decisions and conventional imaging has limited sensitivity for small retroperitoneal metastases; in OC, nodal involvement contributes to staging and surgical assessment; and in early CC, nodal status can redirect patients from surgery to chemoradiation. USPIO-enhanced nodal MRI therefore represents an underexplored diagnostic research direction, but dedicated gynecologic validation is required.

Quantum Dot and Carbon-Based Fluorescence Imaging

Quantum dots (QDs), with broad excitation, narrow emission, and superior photostability, are ideal probes for multiplexed biomarker detection. Their clinical translation, however, is constrained by heavy-metal toxicity: cadmium-containing QDs raise neurotoxicity and organ accumulation concerns that are difficult to reconcile with regulatory expectations.15 Carbon dots (CDs) and graphene quantum dots (GQDs) offer lower-toxicity alternatives. Carbon-dot-embedded epitope-imprinted polymers have been fabricated for specific recognition of EGFR, displaying targeted fluorescence imaging capacity and distinguishing EGFR-high from EGFR-low cells in cervical cancer models;47 because a substantial fraction of EC tumors also overexpress EGFR,7 analogous imprinted nanoprobes could be adapted for endometrial lesion characterization. As a non-gynecologic proof-of-principle, GQD–AuNP fluorescence “on-off” nanoprobes have demonstrated ultralow detection limits for circulating tumor biomarkers.48 For all three cancers, ex vivo biosensing of biopsy, aspirate, or lavage specimens may be a more plausible near-term use than in vivo administration, because systemic reproductive safety of QD-based probes remains unresolved.31,32

Intraoperative Lymphatic Mapping: A Three-Cancer Comparison

Lymphatic mapping provides clinically relevant tracer evidence in gynecologic oncology, particularly in EC and CC, making comparison across the three cancers instructive.

In EC, one of the most clinically mature inorganic applications is carbon nanoparticle (CNP) suspensions used as lymphatic tracers. After local injection, CNPs preferentially enter lymphatic drainage and stain draining lymph nodes black.8,9 Zuo et al8 compared cervical (n = 65) versus fundal (n = 50) injection of 50-mg CNP suspension in laparoscopic staging of early endometrioid EC: overall SLN detection was 100% in the cervical group versus 92% in the fundal group, bilateral detection was 97% versus 68%, sensitivity for metastatic node detection was 100% versus 80%, and false-negative rates were 0% versus 20%, with no allergic reactions. Tao et al9 evaluated CNP-guided SLN biopsy in 54 EC patients, reporting a detection rate of 70.4% with sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of 100% each for nodal metastasis, and demonstrating that CNP labeling increased the yield of small (≤5 mm) lymph nodes during systematic dissection (mean 33.1 vs 24.8 nodes per patient).

In CC, SLN mapping rests on a broader multicenter evidence base with non-inorganic tracers. A meta-analysis of 21 studies (2234 women) found that indocyanine green (ICG) achieved a pooled side-specific detection rate of 85.5%—superior to blue dye (75.2%) and technetium-99m (74.7%)—with a pooled side-specific sensitivity of 88% for detecting nodal metastasis in early-stage disease.49 In OC, the sentinel concept is applied selectively, chiefly to apparent early-stage disease where sparing of lymphadenectomy matters most, using ICG or radiocolloid tracers.

Three comparative lessons follow. First, local injection into an accessible organ can reduce dependence on systemic biodistribution and may facilitate clinical translation, as illustrated by CNP mapping in EC and ICG mapping in CC. Second, clinical mapping provides measurable outcomes (CNP detection rates of 70–100% in EC8,9 and pooled side-specific ICG detection of 85.5% in CC49) that are more directly interpretable than heterogeneous preclinical accumulation metrics. Third, SLN mapping can reduce the extent of nodal surgery in appropriately selected patients, potentially lowering surgical morbidity; any fertility-related benefit is indirect and should not be conflated with preservation of reproductive function itself. Applications of Inorganic Nanomaterials in Drug Delivery

Mesoporous Silica Nanoparticle-Based Delivery Systems

Mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) provide a high internal surface area, tunable mesopores, and abundant surface silanol chemistry, making them versatile carriers for drug loading and controlled release.22,50 Surface modification can further provide pH-responsive or externally triggered release behavior.22,50 In drug-resistant OC models, nanoparticle systems designed to co-deliver cytotoxic agents and resistance-modulating nucleic acids have been investigated as a strategy to overcome multidrug resistance.26 Rather than assigning fixed pore-size ranges to specific payload classes, the translationally relevant point is that pore architecture, surface chemistry, and cargo properties must be optimized together for loading, release kinetics, and stability. Dysregulated ubiquitination pathways also contribute to drug resistance across gynecologic cancers, providing additional biological targets for future delivery systems.51

Cancer-specific advances in this class are worth separating. For OC, the challenge is delivering dose intensively into peritoneal implants while managing resistance.26 For EC, the distinctive advance targets the disease’s dominant epidemiology: Cuatin et al24 developed nanoparticle-based therapeutics explicitly for the obesity-induced EC microenvironment, including lipid-modified MSNs conjugated with ITGA7 antibodies for siRNA delivery. This line of work is significant because it treats EC biology—rather than a generic tumor model—as the design input, and because obesity-driven EC is the fastest-growing patient segment in many countries.2

Magnetic Nanoparticles for Magnetically Targeted Delivery and Hyperthermia: The HIPEC Context

SPIONs internalized by tumor cells support magnetically guided delivery and magnetic hyperthermia (MH), a minimally invasive modality capable of inducing apoptosis, ferroptosis, and immunogenic cell death under an alternating magnetic field.52 Advances in particle engineering—size and shape tailoring, metallic ion doping, multicore nanostructures, and surface coatings—have improved heating efficiency, colloidal stability, and tumor targeting.52 Separately, a reduction-sensitive polymeric nanoparticle carrying a Pt(IV) prodrug and an NIR-II photothermal agent improved chemo-photothermal treatment in an ovarian cancer model, illustrating heat-drug synergy without magnetic targeting.53 MH also synergizes with radiotherapy and immunotherapy, with emerging theranostic applications combining imaging and treatment.52

For OC, the translational context of locoregional heat is unusually strong. The multicenter phase III OVHIPEC-1 trial randomized 245 patients with stage III epithelial OC to interval cytoreductive surgery with or without cisplatin HIPEC: median recurrence-free survival was 14.2 versus 10.7 months (hazard ratio 0.66) and median overall survival was 45.7 versus 33.9 months (hazard ratio 0.67), with no increase in grade 3–4 adverse events.10 This trial established—in the most rigorous form available to the field—that locoregional thermal intensification of chemotherapy improves survival in OC. Magnetic hyperthermia and magnetically targeted delivery can be understood as nanoparticle-native successors to this proof of principle: they pursue the same biological rationale (heat-augmented local cytotoxicity) with a modality that is triggerable, imageable, and potentially repeatable We identified no gynecologic trial of these magnetic therapeutic nanoplatforms; clinical translation remains limited by insufficient tumor accumulation after systemic delivery, safety concerns, and a lack of standardized treatment protocols.52

For EC, magnetic platforms hold a different, anatomy-driven attraction: the uterus is a fixed, superficially accessible pelvic organ, so external magnetic field guidance to a uterine tumor is anatomically more plausible than to disseminated abdominal disease. Magnetic targeting after intrauterine or intracavitary instillation could confine both particles and drug to the endometrial compartment, minimizing gonadal exposure—a consideration that remains theoretical until reproductive-toxicity data are generated.31,32

Gold Nanocarriers for Nucleic Acid and Targeted Drug Delivery

Gold nanoparticles serve as promising non-viral vectors for nucleic acid delivery. Surface conjugation with polyethyleneimine or RGD peptides improves cellular uptake and endosomal escape, achieving potent gene silencing.54 Aptamer-functionalized AuNPs (eg, AS1411-conjugated constructs) display strengthened tumor tropism toward gynecologic carcinoma cells.54 The mechanistic rationale is cancer-specific: PIK3CA and PTEN pathway alterations are among the most common genomic events in endometrioid EC,2 TP53 and homologous recombination deficiency dominate HGSC of the ovary,3,4 and HPV oncoprotein expression defines CC5—each suggesting distinct siRNA payloads. Current in vivo validation, however, remains scarce; gynecologic evidence is largely confined to aptamer-directed gold carriers in cancer cell lines54 and to conceptual discussions in disease-specific reviews.7,29 Gold-based carriers can additionally co-load chemotherapeutics or imaging probes, laying the groundwork for integrated theranostic constructs.

Route-First Design: Intrauterine, Intraperitoneal, and Intracervical Delivery

Route of administration is a major design consideration for gynecologic nanotherapeutics because the three cancers occupy distinct anatomical compartments that interact with material properties and formulation design.

The intraperitoneal route (OC). OC commonly disseminates transcoelomically within the peritoneal cavity, creating a disease compartment that can be directly accessed for locoregional treatment. HIPEC has provided phase III evidence for the value of locoregional intensification in this compartment,10 and carrier-free multifunctional nanomedicines for intraperitoneal disseminated OC have been developed toward the same end.55 The known constraints—incomplete drug penetration into bulky implants, catheter complications, and renal toxicity—apply to nanoparticle successors as well, and peritoneal clearance kinetics of inorganic particles remain incompletely characterized.

The intrauterine route (EC). The clinical use of LNG-IUS-based therapy demonstrates the feasibility of sustained local endometrial drug delivery,6 providing a pharmacological precedent for future local nanoformulations. An intrauterine nano-depot combining progestin with a stimuli-responsive or photothermal inorganic component is a hypothesis-generating concept rather than a demonstrated fertility-preserving strategy.6,7,24 Its potential advantages would need to be tested against existing conservative therapy with respect to response, recurrence, systemic exposure, ovarian function, and pregnancy outcomes. Formulation development must also address uterine-cavity retention, cyclic endometrial remodeling, local fluid conditions, and material clearance.

The intracervical/topical route (CC). The cervix is directly visualizable and reachable, which helps explain why topical photodynamic formulations have advanced clinically in this setting (Light-Based Therapy at the Bedside: The Cervical PDT Precedent and What It Teaches Inorganic Platforms),12,56 and why lipid- and polymer-based composite systems for intracervical cisplatin delivery have been explored preclinically.57 Local administration may reduce systemic exposure, but fertility compatibility must be demonstrated through cervical, reproductive, and pregnancy-related safety endpoints rather than assumed from route alone.

Two cross-cutting points deserve emphasis. First, our search identified no inorganic nanomedicine approved specifically for a gynecologic cancer. PLD, an intravenous liposomal (organic) formulation, is a clinically established nanomedicine comparator in recurrent OC.3,4 Second, route and material simplicity may interact: among the examples reviewed here, the platforms that progressed furthest (CNP tracers and local PDT) use relatively simple compositions and clinically accessible routes, whereas we identified no gynecologic trial of intravenous multifunctional inorganic constructs.

Applications of Inorganic Nanomaterials in Photothermal and Photodynamic Therapy

Gold Nanomaterials for Photothermal Therapy

Gold nanorods and nanoshells absorb near-infrared light and convert it into localized heat, enabling photothermal ablation.50 Surface functionalization with folate or hyaluronic acid directs accumulation at lesions overexpressing the corresponding receptors.29 Combinations of PTT with chemotherapy or immunotherapy further enhance efficacy: mesoporous silica-coated gold nanorods loaded with doxorubicin exert dual functions under irradiation.50

The three cancers offer different PTT-enabling geometries. EC offers a favorable local-access geometry: the endometrial cavity is anatomically accessible through the cervix, creating the possibility of intrauterine or hysteroscopic light delivery without relying exclusively on transcutaneous penetration. In EC models, gold nanoparticle-based strategies targeting fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR)—a recognized driver in a subset of EC—combined with PTT have shown effective tumor cell ablation with improved stability and selectivity.7 CC also offers direct access for local illumination: the transformation zone is directly illuminable through a speculum, as the APL-1702 light-device strategy illustrates.12 OC is the least favorable for PTT optics—peritoneal implants are numerous, variably located, and often beyond NIR penetration—which is why intraperitoneal heat delivery in OC has historically relied on perfusion (HIPEC) rather than light.10 The feasibility of intracavitary photothermal regimens has been reinforced by injectable hydrogel systems that co-deliver photothermal agents with drugs and antibodies for synergistic local tumor ablation,58 an approach that may inform future uterine or pelvic local-delivery concepts, although direct gynecologic validation is lacking. It must nevertheless be acknowledged that all PTT evidence for all three cancers remains preclinical, and thermal protection of surrounding normal tissue—myometrium in EC, bladder and rectum in CC, bowel in OC—will require careful dosimetry.

Titanium Dioxide and Zinc Oxide Nanoparticles for Photodynamic Therapy

TiO2 and ZnO are classic inorganic photosensitizers: upon UV or visible excitation they generate electron-hole pairs that react with water and oxygen to produce hydroxyl radicals and superoxide anions, inducing oxidative tumor cell death.59 Their wide band gaps limit visible-light utilization, and metal ion doping or heterostructure construction has been used to shift absorption toward the therapeutic window.60 In cervical cancer cell models, TiO2-mediated PDT suppressed viability and triggered ICD with release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), activating dendritic cells and systemic antitumor immunity59—findings of particular relevance to a virus-associated tumor whose biology is already shaped by immune evasion. Distinguishing Type I electron-transfer pathways from the more oxygen-dependent Type II singlet-oxygen mechanism may be relevant in hypoxic tumors, where some Type I-oriented designs can retain activity under lower-oxygen conditions.61

Carbon-Based Nanomaterials for Synergistic PTT/PDT

Carbon-based nanomaterials combine strong NIR absorption with ROS generation, integrating PTT and PDT in one platform.59 CDs combine photoluminescence, biocompatibility, and photostability, with demonstrated PTT/PDT capacity in OC models including hypoxic tumors, and their surface functionalization supports targeted delivery and fluorescence imaging.62,63 CD-based delivery systems inhibit cancer cell growth through pH-responsive release.62,63 The low inherent toxicity of carbon-based materials relative to heavy-metal QDs makes them comparatively attractive for topical and intrauterine application in reproductive-age EC and CC patients, although “lower toxicity” is not “proven reproductive safety,” and dedicated retention and fertility studies are still required.31,32

Nanomaterial-Mediated Photoimmunotherapy

Nanomaterial-delivered PTT/PDT can remodel the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) by inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD): release of tumor antigens and DAMPs can activate dendritic cells and promote antitumor T-cell responses.37,64,65 Nanoplatforms can also be combined with immune-checkpoint blockade to amplify photoinduced immune responses, although the optimal delivery configuration remains preclinical.37,66 In OC-focused nanomedicine literature, immune-modulating nanoparticle strategies have been associated with enhanced antitumor immune activity.64 The cancer-specific rationales differ: microsatellite instability-high EC is an immunologically responsive subgroup,2 CC is an HPV-driven disease in which immune control is biologically relevant,5 and OC—despite limited activity of checkpoint monotherapy in unselected populations—remains a candidate for combinatorial immunologic approaches.66,67 Nanoenabled ICD plus checkpoint blockade is therefore a plausible research direction, but no gynecologic photoimmunotherapy trial was identified.

Light-Based Therapy at the Bedside: The Cervical PDT Precedent and What It Teaches Inorganic Platforms

Among the examples reviewed here, CC has randomized clinical evidence for local light-based therapy, albeit not with an inorganic photosensitizer. A prospective non-randomized controlled study in 60 reproductive-age patients with cervical HSIL found 5-aminolevulinic acid photodynamic therapy (5-ALA-PDT) non-inferior to loop electrosurgical excision at 12 months, with cure rates of 86.67% versus 83.33% and fewer reported adverse reactions (3.33% vs 30.00%).56 The international phase III APRICITY trial enrolled 402 women with cervical HSIL and compared APL-1702, a topical hexaminolevulinate ointment activated by a vaginal light device, with placebo. The 6-month response rate was 41.1% versus 21.7% (p < 0.001), HPV16/18 clearance was 31.4% versus 15.4%, and treatment-emergent adverse-event rates were similar between groups.11,12 In March 2026, APL-1702 (Cevira) received a Drug Registration Certificate from China’s NMPA for CIN2, moving this organic drug-device comparator from late-stage development to national regulatory approval.13 This provides a clinically relevant precedent while not constituting evidence that inorganic photosensitizers themselves are clinically established.

The photosensitizer in APL-1702 is an organic prodrug (hexaminolevulinate), not an inorganic nanoparticle. Its development nevertheless offers three relevant lessons for inorganic nanotheranostics. First, local light-based treatment of cervical disease can be evaluated in randomized, placebo-controlled trials with clinically meaningful lesion- and HPV-related endpoints.12 Second, a combined drug-device development pathway is feasible for a photosensitizer that requires a dedicated light source. Third, procedure-sparing local treatment can address a clinical need in reproductive-age patients without assuming that the same efficacy or safety will transfer to inorganic materials. Whether inorganic nanoscale photosensitizers can achieve comparable clinical benefit remains unanswered and requires material-specific preclinical safety, manufacturing control, and prospective clinical evaluation.

Multimodal Integrated Theranostics Based on Inorganic Nanomaterials

Gold–Magnetic Hybrids and MOF Platforms

Gold–magnetic hybrid nanoparticles, especially core-shell Au@Fe3O4 architectures, merge the PAI/SERS capacity of gold with the MRI functionality of iron oxide, delivering both high-resolution anatomy and ultrasensitive molecular detection. Upon functionalization with targeting moieties and chemotherapeutic cargos, such hybrids integrate imaging, delivery, and phototherapy into one construct.14 Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) have emerged as an equally versatile theranostic class, and their biomedical translation has been comprehensively reviewed by Lu et al:68 tunable porosity, diverse metal nodes, and stimuli-responsive decomposition support drug loading, imaging, and catalytic therapy. A Mn-ZIF-8-derived epigenetic MOF nanoagonist co-loading cisplatin and histone deacetylase inhibitors achieved chemo-metalloimmunotherapy against platinum-resistant OC with MRI guidance.69 For all three cancers, hybrid and MOF platforms remain at the stage of cell-line and xenograft proof-of-concept; their multi-component nature will magnify, not reduce, the manufacturing and regulatory burdens discussed in Clinical Translation Status of Inorganic Nanomaterials.

Mesoporous Silica–Gold Nanorod Hybrids

Mesoporous silica-coated gold nanorods can combine drug loading/release, photothermal conversion, and fluorescence-based treatment monitoring. In HeLa cells, AuNR@mSiO2 nanoparticles loaded with doxorubicin supported two-photon fluorescence imaging and chemo-photothermal therapy.50 These data are preclinical and cell-based rather than evidence from cervical cancer xenografts or patients. For reproductive-age EC and CC populations, future in vivo development would require material-specific biodistribution, clearance, and reproductive-safety assessment.

Carbon Dot–MOF Composites

CD@MOF composites integrate the fluorescence of carbon dots with the porosity and loading capacity of MOFs. General MOF literature supports their potential for combined sensing, delivery, and stimuli-responsive functions.14 CD–MOF composites have also been reviewed as fluorescent platforms for cancer-biomarker biosensing;70 however, direct gynecologic therapeutic evidence for CD@MOF composites remains limited. Their near-term relevance to gynecologic oncology is therefore better framed as a research opportunity in ex vivo biosensing and platform development rather than as established in vivo efficacy.

Biosafety Evaluation and Fertility Preservation

In vivo Biodistribution, Metabolism, and Storage Stability

Nanoparticle stability is an important determinant of in vivo performance and manufacturability. As a non-inorganic formulation comparator, RBC-derived nanoparticles doped with ICG retained hydrodynamic diameter, zeta potential, optical density, and NIR fluorescence after a freeze-thaw cycle and eight weeks of storage at −20 °C, supporting storage stability over the tested eight-week interval without cryoprotectants71—a practically relevant attribute for storage and distribution. Cell-type-specific uptake also shapes efficacy: in the study by Bromma et al, cancer cells and cancer-associated fibroblasts showed over 25-fold and over 10-fold higher AuNP uptake per unit cell volume, respectively, than normal fibroblasts, while both showed over 30% higher nanoparticle retention, without significant cytotoxicity.72 For intracavitary formulations—whether intrauterine, intraperitoneal, or intracervical—analogous questions (uptake by target versus bystander tissue, translocation across compartmental barriers, retention through physiologic turnover) remain incompletely characterized and should be incorporated into gynecologic nanodelivery evaluation. Functionalized inorganic nanomaterials are known to interact with, and traverse, biological barriers in ways that depend on size and surface chemistry73—a caution of direct relevance to peritoneal and uterine compartments.

Toxic Mechanisms and Safety Assessment

Nanomaterial toxicity depends on composition, formulation, dose, exposure duration, and biological context. AuNP biological effects are strongly formulation- and cell-type-dependent: green-synthesized AuNPs showed anticancer cytotoxicity against HeLa cells while sparing the normal osteoblast comparator used in that study,74 whereas linalool-loaded/CALNN-functionalized gold nanoparticles and peanut-shaped gold nanoparticles induced apoptosis or autophagy-related cytotoxicity in SKOV-3 ovarian cancer cells.75,76 These antitumor efficacy studies should not be interpreted as evidence of systemic or reproductive safety. Heavy-metal-containing quantum dots raise additional concerns related to ion leakage and persistence.15 Because long-term nonspecific tissue accumulation can itself limit clinical use, biodegradable or rapidly clearable inorganic designs are an important safety strategy.77 Comprehensive safety evaluation should address acute and chronic toxicity, immunotoxicity, biodistribution and clearance, and—central to this review—reproductive toxicity.31,32 Standardized long-term in vivo safety frameworks tailored to gynecologic applications remain limited.31,32

Anatomical and Microenvironmental Specifics of the Three Disease Sites

Each cancer’s anatomical setting imposes distinct formulation requirements. The uterine cavity (EC) is accessed naturally through the cervix, enabling minimally invasive intrauterine administration, but cyclic endometrial remodeling and local fluid conditions can alter nanoparticle retention and release. The peritoneal cavity (OC) offers a directly instillable compartment, while transcoelomic dissemination makes intraperitoneal delivery particularly relevant.3 As a non-inorganic comparator, preclinical work using cancer-cell-membrane-enveloped PLGA nanoparticles has enhanced Doxil-mediated treatment in cervical cancer and metastatic ovarian cancer models, including intraperitoneal administration in the ovarian model.78 The cervix (CC) is directly visualizable and amenable to topical treatment; phase III development and subsequent NMPA approval of APL-1702 for CIN2 illustrate the feasibility of a local cervical drug-device approach.11–13 Locoregional delivery strategies in EC and CC therefore converge on a shared principle—direct access to the disease site—while requiring different formulations and safety assessments.57

Fertility Preservation as a Design Requirement, Not an Afterthought

Fertility preservation is a central consideration for young patients across all three cancers, though with different clinical anchors. In EC, conventional chemotherapy and radiotherapy can impair ovarian and endometrial function; conservative management with progestin therapy or LNG-IUS remains important but imperfect, with pooled complete response of 63.4%, recurrence of 29.6%, and live birth of 39.3% in one meta-analysis of LNG-IUS-based regimens.6 In CC, excisional treatment of HSIL is associated with adverse obstetric consequences, and fertility-preserving surgery is available for selected early-stage cancers;5 the APL-1702 phase III trial and subsequent NMPA approval established a local non-excisional strategy for CIN2.12,13 In OC, fertility-sparing surgery is an option for carefully selected early-stage disease, whereas systemic treatment and recurrence risk continue to constrain reproductive outcomes.3,4

Nanomedicine may improve treatment selectivity in cancer-specific ways: for EC, tumor-targeted or intrauterinely administered platforms could increase local drug exposure while limiting systemic exposure;6,7,24 for CC, future inorganic photosensitizers could be developed within the local drug-device translational pathway illustrated by APL-1702, although their efficacy and safety would require independent validation;12 and for OC, intraperitoneal nanotherapeutic strategies may exploit the dominant peritoneal compartment while reducing unnecessary systemic exposure.10,55 Potential reproductive harm must also be considered: persistent inorganic particles in the endometrium, myometrium, ovary, or cervix could themselves compromise reproductive function. Reproductive-safety endpoints—including ovarian reserve, follicular counts, cyclicity, endometrial receptivity, cervical integrity, and pregnancy outcomes—should therefore be routinely incorporated when relevant.31,32 We propose that future preclinical studies of gynecologic nanotherapeutics explicitly report reproductive-toxicity assessment. A layered framework can begin with cytotoxicity, biodistribution, and clearance, followed by ovarian and uterine functional assessment in reproductive-age models and, where persistence or developmental exposure is plausible, appropriate developmental or multigenerational endpoints.

Patient-Derived Organoids Across the Three Cancers

Conventional 2D culture poorly predicts in vivo nanodrug behavior, and all three cancers have historically lacked representative preclinical models. Organoid technology has closed much of this gap—unevenly across the three diseases.

For EC, Turco et al79 established long-term expandable human endometrial organoids that retain hormone responsiveness, and Boretto et al80 developed patient-derived organoids (PDOs) from a broad spectrum of endometrial pathologies, including atypical hyperplasia, Lynch syndrome precursor lesions, and EC of different grades and stages. Crucially, EC-derived organoids captured cancer subtypes, replicated the mutational landscape of parent tumors, displayed patient-specific drug responses, and reproduced the original lesion upon transplantation in vivo.80

For OC, Kopper et al81 established 56 organoid lines from 32 patients representing all main subtypes, showed that the organoids recapitulated histological and genomic features of the parent tumors and captured intra- and interpatient heterogeneity, demonstrated subtype-specific responses to platinum chemotherapy including acquisition of chemoresistance in recurrent disease, and achieved xenograftable in vivo drug-sensitivity testing.81 For a disease whose platinum sensitivity defines its clinical management,3,4 this provides a potentially useful preclinical screening platform.

For CC, organoid literature is comparatively sparse, reflecting the field’s historical reliance on HPV-transformed cell lines rather than any biological barrier; cervical PDO models remain an open methodological front.

For nanomedicine specifically, PDO-based screening remains at an early stage. AI-assisted organoid analysis can standardize morphological, viability, and growth-kinetic readouts,82 and these tools could be adapted to quantify nanoparticle uptake, penetration, and response heterogeneity across patient-derived models. EC and OC organoid systems are sufficiently established to support such studies,79–81 but direct evidence that PDO-guided selection improves nanotherapeutic efficacy or predicts patient outcomes is not yet available. Cervical PDO evidence is less mature. Accordingly, PDOs should be presented as a promising translational bridge that requires prospective validation rather than as an already established personalized nanomedicine platform.

Clinical Translation Status of Inorganic Nanomaterials

Clinical-Stage and Approved Platforms: An Evidence-Calibrated Inventory

Despite extensive preclinical activity, our search identified no inorganic nanomedicine currently approved specifically for a gynecologic malignancy. Feraheme (ferumoxytol) is FDA-approved for iron deficiency anemia and carries a boxed warning for serious hypersensitivity reactions; no gynecologic imaging indication was identified.45,46 AuroLase uses silica-core/gold-shell nanoshells for laser-mediated focal ablation and remains investigational. NCT00848042 and NCT02680535 are completed, NCT01679470 was terminated, and the extension study NCT04240639 was last verified as active, not recruiting, in May 2023 and is currently listed with unknown status; none involved gynecologic tumors. Other inorganic clinical-stage platforms summarized in Table 2 likewise lack an established gynecologic indication. Conversely, PLD and HIPEC provide clinically established comparator precedents for nanoparticulate or locoregional treatment in OC, and NMPA approval of hexaminolevulinate PDT for CIN2 provides a local light-based drug-device precedent.10,12,13 These comparators clarify translational feasibility without being misclassified as inorganic gynecologic nanotherapeutics.

A cancer-specific summary therefore requires explicit evidence tiers. In EC, carbon nanoparticle lymphatic tracers have direct clinical evidence, whereas USPIO-enhanced nodal MRI and therapeutic inorganic platforms remain unproven in this disease. In OC, PLD and HIPEC demonstrate that nanoparticulate and locoregional strategies can be clinically viable, but intraperitoneal inorganic thermo-chemotherapy remains preclinical. In CC, topical PDT provides a clinical and procedural precedent, but inorganic photosensitizers have not reached approved use in the evidence reviewed here. AuroLase is relevant only as a non-gynecologic example of image-guided nanoparticle photothermal ablation and should not be interpreted as direct evidence for hysteroscopic treatment of EC.

Core Regulatory Considerations for FDA and NMPA Approval

Clinical translation of inorganic nanomaterials requires product definitions and control strategies that are substantially more complex than those for conventional small molecules. Safety packages must characterize pharmacokinetics, tissue distribution, clearance, acute and chronic toxicity, immunotoxicity, and, for fertility-oriented gynecologic indications, local reproductive-organ exposure. Product-quality programs should define critical attributes including particle-size distribution, morphology, surface chemistry and ligand density, zeta potential, aggregation state, drug loading or payload ratio, residual reagents and elemental impurities, sterility/endotoxin status, and stability during storage. Scale-up must demonstrate that these attributes remain within prespecified ranges across GMP batches and after sterilization or aseptic processing. Route-specific products - intrauterine, intraperitoneal, or intracervical - additionally require local tolerability, retention, device compatibility where relevant, and compartment-specific biodistribution. The phase III development and NMPA approval of the hexaminolevulinate PDT drug-device system for CIN2 illustrate a pathway for locally administered light-based combination products supported by controlled clinical evidence;12,13 however, inorganic photosensitizers would still require their own material-specific chemistry, manufacturing, biodistribution, and long-term safety packages.

Cost and Scalability Barriers for Industrialization

Industrial translation is constrained by reproducibility, scale-up, and quality control as well as cost. Nanomedicine manufacturing requires control of critical material attributes and process parameters because changes in mixing, mass transfer, nucleation, purification, or surface functionalization can alter particle-size distribution, morphology, surface properties, aggregation, and payload characteristics.83,84 Quality-by-design approaches can help connect formulation variables and manufacturing processes with predefined critical quality attributes during scale-up.83 Regulatory quality assessment also requires control of conventional and nano-specific attributes, including identity and composition, particle size and morphology, surface characteristics, stability, impurities, microbial quality, and other product-specific release criteria.84 Manufacturability should therefore be treated as a design input rather than a late-stage correction. Platforms with robust process windows, reproducible scale-up, adequate storage stability, and clinically practical administration are generally more plausible translational candidates than constructs whose performance depends on narrowly controlled laboratory conditions.73,83,84

AI-Assisted Nanomaterial Design: Capabilities and Critical Appraisal

Machine-learning methods can support nanomaterial research by relating physicochemical descriptors to experimental outcomes and by prioritizing candidate formulations, although the evidence base remains heterogeneous.85 Automated image analysis can also support more standardized organoid assessment.82 These applications may reduce empirical screening burden, but their value depends on dataset quality, transparent model development, and validation beyond the laboratory in which a model was trained.

Current AI applications in nanomedicine face recurring limitations, including small and heterogeneous datasets, inconsistent experimental protocols, class imbalance, overfitting, limited external or prospective validation, and incomplete interpretability.85 In silico models also cannot by themselves establish dynamic protein-corona behavior, immune clearance, organ-specific retention, reproductive toxicity, or patient-level clinical benefit. For gynecologic nanomedicine, AI should therefore be viewed as a hypothesis-generation and optimization tool rather than evidence of clinical translatability. Priorities include curated multicenter datasets, harmonized reporting of material descriptors and biological endpoints, transparent model reporting, and prospective validation against independent in vitro, organoid, animal, and ultimately clinical datasets.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the bibliographic literature search was limited to PubMed and Web of Science and did not systematically capture gray literature, conference proceedings, or all regional databases; ClinicalTrials.gov was used only for targeted verification of registered studies and trial status. Relevant local clinical experience may therefore be underrepresented. Second, this was a narrative rather than systematic review, so no quantitative meta-analysis or formal risk-of-bias assessment was performed and thematic weighting reflects author judgment. Third, a three-cancer framework necessarily compresses disease-specific detail. Fourth, most therapeutic evidence for inorganic platforms remains preclinical, and claims about fertility preservation, patient-specific PDO screening, and long-term reproductive safety are therefore inferential rather than clinically demonstrated. Fifth, several regulatory and 2025–2026 literature developments are recent and may change with follow-up. Finally, clinical-trial and approval status should be interpreted as time-dependent and checked against current regulatory or registry records when clinical decisions are made.

Conclusion

The translational landscape is best interpreted by separating established evidence, plausible but unproven approaches, and speculative concepts. In EC, carbon nanoparticle suspensions have direct clinical evidence for sentinel lymph node mapping,8,9 whereas USPIO-enhanced nodal MRI, intrauterine stimuli-responsive therapy, and hysteroscopic photothermal approaches remain unproven. In OC, PLD and HIPEC are clinically established comparator precedents but are not inorganic nanotherapeutics;3,4,10 intraperitoneal inorganic thermo-chemotherapy and PDO-guided nano-screening remain investigational. In CC, phase III and regulatory evidence for hexaminolevulinate PDT in CIN2 demonstrates the feasibility of local light-based treatment,11–13 but inorganic photosensitizers and photoimmunotherapy remain preclinical or conceptual. This evidence hierarchy is more informative than grouping all nanotechnology-related approaches together as if they were equally close to clinical use.

Inorganic nanomaterials demonstrate preclinical utility in imaging contrast, controlled release, photothermal and photodynamic conversion, and multimodal integration. The central translational challenge is not demonstrating additional functionality but converting reproducible material performance into clinically appropriate products. For gynecologic oncology, priorities should include route-matched administration, reproductive-safety and long-term biodistribution endpoints, validated organoid models where appropriate, restrained use of AI with external validation, and manufacturing strategies that control critical quality attributes at scale. Simpler platforms may often be easier to characterize and regulate, but this should be tested case by case rather than treated as a universal rule. Progress will require coordinated input from materials science, reproductive medicine, gynecologic oncology, pharmacology, manufacturing, and regulatory science.
