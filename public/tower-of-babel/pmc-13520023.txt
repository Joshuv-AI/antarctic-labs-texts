Structural Basis and Conformational Switching of Capture-SELEX Aptamers for Designing Equol Sensors
Authors: Tamaki Endoh, Hidenobu Yaku, Peter Podbevsek, Janez Plavec, Naoki Sugimoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Expanding healthy life expectancy requires technologies for continuous and noninvasive health monitoring. Sensors detecting health-related biomarkers in urine can be used for such applications. Aptamers are nucleic acids that interact with biomolecules through the formation of unique tertiary structures, some of which undergo target-induced conformational switching. The structural and dynamic properties of aptamers can be utilized for constructing sensors. In this study, DNA aptamers targeting equol, a gut microbiota-derived metabolite with various health benefits, were obtained by Capture-SELEX. Highly enriched 5 aptamers, Eq1 to Eq5, exhibited equilibrium association constants more than 106 M–1 toward equol. The aptamers were applied to the construction of fluorometric and colorimetric sensors. The fluorometric sensor employed fluorescence indicator displacement based on the static tertiary structure of the aptamer, while the colorimetric sensor utilized aggregation of gold nanoparticles (AuNP) based on dynamic conformational switching of the aptamer dissociating from a capture strand immobilized on AuNP. Both assays quantified equol in urine samples with LOD values of 2.73 μM and 846 nM, and linear detection ranges of 0–5 μM and 0–6 μM, for fluorometric and colorimetric assays, respectively. The sensitivities were sufficient to evaluate favorable equol production in urine. The promising proof-of-concept sensor demonstrated in this study offers a versatile platform for aptamer-based health-monitoring devices.

Introduction

Although advances in medical technology and public health have increased life expectancy worldwide, healthy life expectancy has not improved at the same pace, emphasizing the need for effective health management technologies. Recent studies in microbiology report that microbiomes in the gut intestine produce various metabolites involved in human health states, including disease progression. Therefore, monitoring gut microbiomes, including their specific metabolites, is attracting research attention to extend healthy life expectancy. Mass spectrometry is a powerful tool for metabolomic analysis of samples, such as blood. However, daily monitoring of specific metabolites requires a simpler noninvasive method. Because of the noninvasive nature of its collection and availability, urine is an ideal sample for biomolecular monitoring.

Nucleic acids that recognize specific molecules, such as proteins, peptides, and small molecules, are known as aptamers, which can serve as a sensor material. Aptamers have been developed through systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment (SELEX) and modifications of this method. In general, aptamers form target-binding pockets or surfaces through the formation of tertiary structures, which tend to be stabilized upon binding of the target molecules. The energy to stabilize the tertiary structure can be utilized for exhibiting a large conformational change in the aptamer, such as the switching of regions for base pairing. The switching of the aptamer structure is subsequently integrated as a working mechanism to functionalize aptamer-based sensors that generate detectable signals. , For example, we have constructed fluorometric biosensors for natural metabolites based on the conformational switching of aptamers. The sensors were applied for fluorescence imaging of intracellular metabolites. For in vitro detection, aptamer-based sensors, which enable colorimetric detection of target molecules, have been developed by conjugating aptamers with materials showing unique optical properties, such as gold nanoparticles (AuNPs). − Both fluorometric and colorimetric detection are simple processes to monitor target molecules in solution.

In this study, we demonstrate a strategy to construct a simple monitoring system for target molecules in urine by using a DNA aptamer obtained through Capture-SELEX, a modified version of SELEX. By employing Capture-SELEX, aptamers with guaranteed conformational switching and dissociation from the capture strand in response to the target molecule are obtained (Figure S1). Thus, the obtained aptamer can be easily transferred to the monitoring system as a sensor material to trigger the output signal through conformational switching (Figure A). Here, equol, a small metabolite produced by gut microbiota from the metabolism of daidzein, a compound found in soybeans, was selected as a target analyte in urine. Equol has the potential to exhibit beneficial effects in various diseases, including tumors, neuroinflammation, and myocardial infarction. , In addition, due to its activity similar to human estradiol, equol exhibits preventive effects against symptoms involving female hormones, such as menopause, postmenopausal osteoporosis, and skin aging. Despite the numerous health benefits of equol, only 30–50% of the population can produce equol, depending on the individual gut microbiome; thus, equol supplements have been commercially marketed. The health benefits of equol suggest the importance of individual periodic equol monitoring for one’s health care. After the selection of equol aptamers, the aptamers were used to construct a colorimetric equol sensor by conjugating with AuNPs, which immobilizes the capture strand used for Capture-SELEX. In addition, since the obtained aptamers formed tertiary structure units that interact with thioflavin T and enhance its fluorescence, fluorometric equol detection based on fluorescence indicator displacement (FID) was demonstrated. Since the detection is simply adding urine to pretreated samples, these aptamer-based systems have potential as periodic equol monitoring devices for individual health care.

Experimental Section

Oligonucleotides and Chemical Reagents

All natural and chemically modified DNA oligonucleotides used in this study were purchased from Fasmac Co., Ltd. (Kanagawa, Japan) or GeneDesign, Inc. (Osaka, Japan). For analyses of thermodynamic stabilities, binding affinities, and structural analyses, high-performance liquid chromatography–grade DNA oligonucleotides were used. Equol ((S)-equol) was purchased from Daicel Corp. (Osaka, Japan). Daidzein and liquiritigenin were purchased from Selleck Chemicals (Houston, TX, USA). Dihydrodaidzein was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). Thioflavin T (ThT) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Gold colloidal nanoparticles (AuNP) with an average diameter of 40 nm were purchased from BBI Solutions (Wiltshire, UK). A human urine sample (Male, age 54) was purchased from BioIVT (Hicksville, NY, USA). Sample collection was conducted by the vendor in accordance with ethical standards. For other chemical reagents, a grade appropriate for biochemical experiments was used.

Selection of Equol Aptamer by Capture-SELEX

A DNA library consisting of constant primer annealing sites at the 5′ and 3′ regions and a 36-nt randomized region in between (5′-GGAGGCTCTCGGGACGAC­(N36)­GTCGTCCCGATCACTTGAATGGTCT-3′) was mixed with a 5-fold equimolar amount of biotinylated capture strand (5′-GTCGTCCCGAGAGCCATA-biotin-3′) in a Capture-SELEX buffer containing 20 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 7.5), 1 M NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, and 5 mM KCl. The mixture was heated at 90 °C and gradually cooled to 25 °C at a rate of 5 °C min–1 to make an annealed DNA mixture. Then, Capture-SELEX was performed according to a previously reported improved process. At the first selection cycle, 500 pmol of the DNA library and 2,500 pmol of the biotinylated capture strand were used. From the second to 4th cycles, the entire amount of the DNA library recovered from the amplification step was used. From the 5th to 15th cycles, an aliquot of the recovered DNA library was used, reducing its amount by increasing the number of selection cycles (Table S1). The concentration of equol in the elution buffer was also reduced by increasing the number of selection cycles (Table S1). After 15 rounds of selection, the collected DNA library was amplified by PCR using NGS-forward and NGS-reverse primers (Table S2), and sequence abundances were analyzed using the IonS5 Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Detailed experimental processes for Capture-SELEX and subsequent sequencing are described in the Supporting Information.

Interaction Analysis

The interaction between equol and the selected DNA aptamers and their variants (Table S3) was analyzed by isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) using MicroCal iTC 200 (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK). Equol (50 μM) in Capture-SELEX buffer containing 0.3% ethanol or a buffer was placed in an ITC syringe. DNA oligonucleotides (5 μM) dissolved in the same buffer were incubated at 70 °C for 5 min, cooled to 25 °C at 1 °C min–1, and collected in an ITC cell. Equol (2 μL) was injected 19 times at 150 s time intervals. The thermogram was normalized to equol titration without DNA oligonucleotides, and fitted using MicroCal software following a single binding site model. When performing the interaction analysis using human urine samples, the DNA solution in the cell and the solution with chemical indicated in the syringe were prepared to contain 50 vol % urine and 0.6% DMSO instead of ethanol.

Ultraviolet (UV) Melting

The melting of DNA oligonucleotides (3 μM) was measured by absorbance signals at 295 or 260 nm in Capture-SELEX buffer containing 0.3% ethanol in the absence or presence of 20 μM equol with a heating rate of 1 °C min–1 using a Shimadzu-1800 UV/vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a temperature controller. Before measuring the melting curves, the samples were heated at 90 °C and gradually cooled at a rate of 1 °C min–1. The thermodynamic parameters were calculated from the melting curves following the two-state melting transition model of oligonucleotides. When the UV melting was analyzed under the condition in which sodium and potassium were replaced by lithium, a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES-LiOH (pH 7.5), 1,005 mM LiCl, 10 mM MgCl2, and 0.3% ethanol was used.

Interaction Assay with ThT

Interaction affinities between DNA oligonucleotides and ThT were calculated by titrating varied concentrations of ThT with DNA oligonucleotides (200 nM) that were pre-refolded from 70 to 25 °C at a rate of 1 °C min–1 in Capture-SELEX buffer containing 0.3% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Titration of ThT in 0.3% Tween 20 was performed using Multidrop Pico 1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The final concentration of Tween 20 was normalized to 0.01% in all samples. Fluorescence signals of ThT at 500 nm emission with 460 nm excitation were measured at 25 °C after 60 min incubation by using a fluorescence microplate reader (Infinite M200 PRO; Tecan, Mannedorf, Switzerland). Signals of ThT in the absence of DNA oligonucleotide were subtracted to evaluate the observed equilibrium association constant (K OBS (ThT)) according to eq , assuming 1:1 binding stoichiometry. 1 F=Finitial+(Ffinal−Finitial2×[DNA])×{([DNA]+[ThT]+1KOBS(ThT))−([DNA]+[ThT]+1KOBS(ThT))2−4×[DNA]×[ThT]} where F is the measured fluorescence intensity, F initial and F final are the initial and maximum fluorescence intensities, respectively, [DNA] is the concentration of DNA oligonucleotide, and [ThT] is the concentration of ThT.

Fluorescence Indicator Displacement (FID) Assay in Buffer

Indicated concentrations of refolded DNA oligonucleotide and ThT were mixed in a Capture-SELEX buffer containing 0.01% Tween 20. Stock solutions of chemicals, equol, daidzein, dihydrodaidzein, and liquiritigenin, in DMSO, were titrated using Multidrop Pico 1 at different concentrations. The final concentration of DMSO was adjusted to 0.3% in all samples. The fluorescence signals of ThT at 500 nm emission with 460 nm excitation were measured at 25 °C after 30 min incubation using Infinite M200 PRO. The equilibrium association constant (K A) between the DNA oligonucleotides and chemicals was calculated from the concentration-dependent decrease in ThT fluorescence according to eq , assuming competitive binding of ThT and the chemicals to the DNA oligonucleotide. 2 F=Finitial+(Ffinal−Finitial)×{[Lig][Lig]+(1+KOBS(ThT)×[ThT]KA)} where F is the measured fluorescence intensity, F initial and F final are the initial and maximum fluorescence intensities, respectively, [ThT] is the ThT concentration, K OBS(ThT) is the observed equilibrium association constant between ThT and each DNA oligonucleotide, which was calculated by ThT titration, and [Lig] is the concentration of chemicals.

FID Assay in Human Urine Samples

The FID assay with urine sample was performed by mixing DNA oligonucleotides, ThT, and human urine samples containing varied concentrations of equol. DNA oligonucleotides and ThT were premixed and refolded in a buffer containing 40 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 7.5), 2 M NaCl, 20 mM MgCl2, 10 mM KCl, and 0.02% Tween 20. Human urine sample was diluted twice with the pretreated mixture. Equol in DMSO was titrated using Multidrop Pico 1 at different concentrations. The concentration of DMSO in the samples was adjusted to 0.3%. The fluorescence signals of ThT were measured at 25 °C after 30 min incubation. The limit of detection (LOD) for equol was calculated from the linear correlation between the concentration of equol and ThT fluorescence according to eq . 3 LOD=3.3×σ/S where σ is the standard deviation of ThT fluorescence in the absence of equol and S is the slope of the linear correlation.

Preparation of Capture Strand-Immobilized AuNP

DNA-immobilized AuNP was prepared according to a previous report. Briefly, AuNP (1 mL), a thiol-modified capture strand (Table S2) (5 nmol), 0.5 M sodium phosphate (pH 8) (20 μL), and 1% Tween 20 (10 μL) were mixed and incubated at 50 °C for 20 min. NaCl was added to the solution to a concentration of 50 mM and incubated at 50 °C for 20 min following sonication for 10 min. The addition of NaCl (each 50 mM), sonication, and incubation at 50 °C were repeated until the NaCl concentration became 200 mM. The final incubation was performed at 50 °C for 24 h. DNA-immobilized AuNP was washed three times by centrifugation at 2500g with 0.01% Tween 20 and resuspended in H2O to 600 μL. The concentration of AuNP was determined from the absorbance at 530 nm. The amount of capture strand immobilized on AuNP was estimated from absorbance at 260 nm using the DNA solution cut and purified from AuNP.

Colorimetric Detection of Equol in Buffer

Equal volumes (5 μL) of AuNP (650 pM), which immobilizes approximately a thousand copies of the capture strand per particle, and indicated concentration of Eq5-ex (Table S3) were diluted 2.5-fold into a buffer containing 8 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 7.5), 400 mM NaCl, 4 mM MgCl2, and 2 mM KCl, and incubated at 90 °C for 5 min followed by cooling to 25 °C at a rate of 5 °C min–1. Stock solutions of chemicals in 3% DMSO (2.5 μL) were added to the mixture in a transparent 384-well plate and incubated at 25 °C for 30 min. A buffer (10 μL) containing 40 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 7.5), 2 M NaCl, 20 mM MgCl2, and 10 mM KCl was added to the mixture using the automatic dispenser of a multiwell plate reader, Varioskan Flash (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Changes in absorbance signals at 520 and 800 nm were monitored during incubation at 25 °C for 60 min.

Colorimetric Detection of Equol in Human Urine Samples

Eq5-ex and the capture strand were annealed on AuNPs according to the process described above. Sucrose (50 w/v %) and trehalose (25 w/v %) were added to the sample to 10 and 5 w/v %, respectively. The mixture was subsequently dried down and stored at 4 °C until use. A stock solution of equol in DMSO was added to human urine samples at different concentrations. The urine sample was diluted 1.6-fold using a buffer containing 40 mM HEPES-NaOH (pH 7.5), 2 M NaCl, 20 mM MgCl2, and 10 mM KCl. The concentration of DMSO in the samples was adjusted to 0.3%. The dried AuNP sample was dissolved in the mixture of urine and buffer (25 μL), and incubated at 25 °C for 60 min. The absorption signals at 520 and 800 nm of the AuNP-containing urine samples were measured using Varioskan Flash. Results and Discussion

Selection of Equol Aptamer by Capture-SELEX

Capture-SELEX, a modification of SELEX technology, , has been utilized to obtain aptamers for small molecules. This technique does not require the chemical immobilization of target molecules on a solid support. In addition, the collection of aptamer candidates is based on the dissociation from the capture strand in response to the target molecule (Figure S1) that ensures the obtained aptamers exhibit conformational switching. In this study, the capture strand and DNA library, which contains consecutive randomized 36 nucleotides, was designed according to a previous report (Figure B). The capture strand and the DNA library have a 15-nt complementary sequence. In the DNA library, the constant sequence region for primer annealing at 5′ and 3′ was designed to have a 10-nt complementary region to stabilize the aptamer structure and support dissociation of the aptamer from the capture strand when bound to the target.

In this study, we have performed Capture-SELEX in a buffer containing an unusually high salt concentration, 1 M NaCl, with the aim of extending the obtained aptamer as a sensor unit by conjugating with AuNPs, in which aggregation is induced by the high salt concentration. Furthermore, the use of a buffer with a high salt concentration provides an advantage to functionalize the obtained aptamers in urine, because the high salt concentration is expected to exert a “buffering effect” against the diverse physiological salt concentrations derived from urine, thereby supporting signal stability. During the selection of aptamers through Capture-SELEX, concentrations of the library oligonucleotide and biotinylated capture strands applied to the streptavidin-conjugated resin were reduced by increasing the number of selection cycles (Table S1). The concentration of equol to dissociate the library from the capture strand was also reduced (Table S1). Wash and elute fractions were collected before and after the addition of equol, respectively, and qPCR was used to compare the amounts of DNA in both fractions. Until the ninth cycle, there were no significant differences in the qPCR amplification profiles between the wash and the elute fractions. However, after the 10th cycle, a distinct increasing trend in the amount of DNA in the elute fraction compared to the wash fraction was observed. We interpreted this specific shift in the amplification profiles as clear evidence for the evolutionary enrichment of DNA sequences that dissociate from the capture strand in response to equol (Figure S2). Finally, we performed a 15-cycle selection by further reducing the equol concentration from 10 to 1 μM from the 12th to 15th cycle (Table S1) to dissociate the library. DNA sequences were analyzed using NGS. The top five sequences, Eq1 to Eq5, in which the abundance values were more than 5% of the total, are shown in Table . The abundance of the most enriched sequence was 26.2%, and more than 60% of the total was from the five sequences. The results indicate that Capture-SELEX enriched the sequences well. Predicted secondary structures, including the constant stem-forming region, are shown in Figure S3. Nucleotides at the 5′ and 3′ ends of the randomized region were forming one to three base pairs (Figure S3). These additional base pairs are expected to stabilize the aptamer structure and enhance the dissociation of the aptamer from the capture strand in the presence of equol.

Interaction between Selected Aptamers and Equol

The interactions between the obtained aptamers and equol were analyzed by ITC. For each sequence, we designed two kinds of derivatives (Table S3). The first derivative, which was named EqX-s1 (“X” indicates the number of the sequence), was designed to have a closing stem consisting of five base pairs based on the predicted secondary structures in Figure S3. The other, which was named EqX-s2, was designed to remove the stem region to evaluate the effect of the stem region on the interaction.

The ITC thermograms of the EqX-s1 oligonucleotides (Figure A) clearly demonstrated interactions with equol through a favorable enthalpic contribution (negative ΔH interaction value). The equilibrium association constants at 25 °C calculated from the thermograms (K A25(ITC)) were more than 106 M–1 (Table S4). Notably, the association constant observed for Eq1-s1 (5.20 × 106 M–1) was similar to that of a recently reported adenosine aptamer, which was obtained through an improved Capture-SELEX strategy as used in this study. The strategy of decreasing target concentration as the selection cycle progressed likely led to the selection of aptamers showing high affinities.

The difference of largest K A25(ITC) value for Eq1-s1 (5.20 × 106 M–1) and smallest K A25(ITC) value for Eq2-s1 (1.25 × 106 M–1) was 4.16-fold that corresponds to ΔΔG 25(ITC) value of 0.84 kcal mol–1 (Table S4). Since we repeated Capture-SELEX cycle until just the five sequences with high abundance accounted for over 60% of the total, it is in a sense that the resulting aptamers exhibit similar K A25(ITC) values. Despite this small difference, ΔH interaction values showed relatively large differences depending on the aptamers, suggesting different equol binding mechanisms between the aptamers. Since the absolute values of ΔH interaction for Eq4-s1 (20.1 kcal mol–1) and Eq5-s1 (22.7 kcal mol–1) were relatively large compared to the other three aptamers, these two aptamers are considered to interact with equol accompanied by conformational rearrangement of nucleobases. The average stoichiometry parameter (n) was around 0.86, with Eq1-s1 (0.845), Eq2-s1 (0.891), Eq3-s1 (0.873), and Eq5-s1 (0.840), suggesting 1:1 binding. In contrast, the different n value of Eq4-s1 (0.623) suggests a different binding stoichiometry. The interactions of EqX-s2 oligonucleotides were completely lost in Eq1-s2, Eq2-s2, Eq3-s2, and Eq4-s2, and the affinity was reduced approximately 8.5 times in Eq5-s2 (Figure S4 and Table S4). These results clearly indicated the importance of the stem region for equol binding, and the binding sites of equol are expected to locate at the space formed between the stem and loop-forming regions.

Stabilization of Aptamer Structure in Response to Equol Binding

To investigate the thermodynamic stabilities of the aptamers, the UV melting profiles of EqX-s1 oligonucleotides were recorded in the absence and presence of equol. Since four of the five sequences from Eq1 to Eq4 are guanine-rich and have more than four regions of at least two consecutive guanines, formation of a G-quadruplex (G4) was expected in these sequences (Table S3). Prediction of G4-forming potential by G4Hunter, a simple and widely tool hosted by independent servers, showed the possibilities of G4 formation in the sequences from Eq1-s1 to Eq4-s1, although the score values were close or slightly less comparing to threshold (Table S5). Score values calculated by another prediction software, QGRS Mapper, also showed possibility of G4 formation in these sequences (Table S5). In addition, prediction of secondary structures of EqX-s1 by using RNAfold with energy parameters for DNA and the “Incorporate G-quadruplex formation into the structure prediction algorithm” option showed formation of G-quadruplexes in Eq2-s1, Eq3-s1, and Eq4-s1 (Figure S5). These results indicated a high possibility of G4 formation in the selected aptamers except Eq5. Thus, the UV melting profiles were analyzed at 295 nm, at which absorbance changes with G4 melting. For Eq1-s1, Eq2-s1, Eq3-s1, and Eq4-s1, clear melting profiles were observed at 295 nm, both in the absence and presence of equol (Figure B). These results suggest the formation of G4 structures in these four sequences. The melting behaviors of these four sequences at 295 nm disappeared when sodium and potassium in the buffer were replaced by lithium (Figure S6). In addition, evaluation of interactions by ITC in the buffer containing lithium instead of sodium and potassium demonstrated loss of the equol binding ability in the aptamers except Eq5-s1 (Figure S7 and Table S6). These results clearly suggested the contribution of G4 structures from Eq1-s1 to Eq4-s1, because the lithium ion does not support the formation of G4 structures. We also measured circular dichroism (CD) spectra of Eq1-s1, Eq2-s1, Eq3-s1, and Eq4-s1 in the presence of potassium and sodium with 20 μM equol (Figure S8). Eq1-s1, Eq2-s1, and Eq3-s1 showed similar spectra with negative and positive peaks of ellipticity around 260 and 290 nm, respectively. These peak positions are characteristic of G4 structures with antiparallel topology. Eq4-s1 showed a different spectrum having a negative ellipticity peak around 240 nm and broad and weak positive ellipticity signal from 260 to 300 nm. Although the signal intensity at wavelengths longer than 250 nm is weak, the positive ellipticity peak around 210 nm is one of the characteristics of G4 structures. , By considering the clear UV melting at 295 nm and high predicted scores for G4 formation (Table S5), Eq4-s1 was expected to form a G4 structure, in which the topology is different from Eq1-s1, Eq2-s1, and Eq3-s1. Previous reports have suggested interactions between G4 structures and flavonoids such as quercetin and fisetin, which have extended aromatic rings. Although equol is a benzopyran derivative with a C2–C3 single bond and lacks an extended aromatic ring, it likely interacts with the G4 structure formed on the aptamer. In the case of Eq5-s1, even if the buffer contains sodium and potassium, there was almost no melting at 295 nm (Figure S6), suggesting a structure distinct from the G4 is involved in the equol interaction. Due to the absence of melting at 295 nm, the thermodynamic stability of Eq5-s1 was evaluated from the absorbance at 260 nm in the absence and presence of equol (Figure C).

Based on the experimental results showing similar thermodynamic stabilities (Table ) and CD spectra (Figure S8), and relatively small −ΔH interaction in the ITC thermogram (Table S4), Eq1-s1, Eq2-s1, and Eq3-s1 were categorized as G-quadruplex-forming aptamers with the same topology and distinguished from the other two aptamers. Thus, we have performed the following equol detection mainly focusing on Eq1-s1, Eq4-s1, and Eq5-s1.

Detection of Equol by Fluorescence Indicator Displacement

FID is a simple fluorometric assay to investigate nucleic acid–binding chemicals. Since previous experimental results suggested the formation of G4 structures in the aptamers except Eq5, FID was performed using ThT, which is one of the fluorescence indicators of G4 structures (Figure A). To check whether ThT interacts with the aptamers and exhibits fluorescence enhancement, varied concentrations of ThT were mixed with EqX-s1 oligonucleotides. The fluorescence enhancement was evaluated by subtracting the signal obtained from the ThT solution without oligonucleotide (Figures B and S9A), and the observed association constant between aptamers and ThT at 25 °C (K obs(ThT)) was calculated from the fluorescence signals by assuming a 1:1 binding stoichiometry (Table S7). The results indicated that all EqX-s1 oligonucleotides, including Eq5-s1, enhanced ThT fluorescence and exhibited K obs(ThT) values in the range of association constants observed between ThT and known G4 structures. Eq4-s1 showed the highest K obs(ThT) value (17.3 × 105 M–1), which is more than 30-fold higher than other aptamers. Previous reports show different binding preferences of G4-interacting chemicals, including ThT, depending on the G4 topology. Since Eq4-s1 was indicated to form a G4 structure, in which the topology is different from Eq1-s1, Eq2-s1, and Eq3-s1 (Figure S8), the topology is likely particularly favorable for ThT binding.

ThT is also known to interact with nucleic acid structures other than G4. In the case of Eq5-s1, although the sequence did not show melting at 295 nm in the absence of equol, a little melting, at which hyperchromicity is less than other aptamers, was observed in the presence of equol (Figure S10). The result suggests the potential to form a tertiary structure unit in Eq5-s1, because tertiary structure units, such as triplets consisting of Hoogsteen base pairs, tend to melt at 295 nm. ThT likely interacted with the tertiary structure formed on Eq5-s1 and enhanced its fluorescence. To check the possibility of tertiary structure formation on the Eq5 aptamer, we have examined the 1H NMR spectrum of Eq5-s3, in which the stem region of Eq5-s1 was extended by an additional nine base pairs. We observed only the expected signals between 12 and 14 ppm corresponding to Watson–Crick base pairs. However, upon addition of equimolar equol, new signals emerged between 9 and 12 ppm (Figure S11). New resonances indicate that the Eq5 aptamer may form a tertiary structural element upon interacting with equol. Guanine nucleobases in the loop region (Figure S2) are likely to contribute to this tertiary motif, potentially through Hoogsteen-type interactions or G–T wobble base pairs; this conformational rearrangement is expected to be a reason for the relatively large – ΔH interaction value of Eq5-s1 observed in the ITC experiment (Table S4). Similar equol binding abilities of Eq5-s1 irrespective of the type of monovalent cation (Tables S4 and S6) also suggested the formation of a tertiary structure unit distinct from G4.

Then, FID assays were performed in the presence of 1 or 10 μM ThT with Eq4-s1 or other EqX-s1, respectively. To investigate the specificity of the aptamers, equol and related chemicals, daidzein and dihydrodaidzein (Figure C), and an analogous molecule, liquiritigenin (Figure C), were titrated into the mixture of EqX-s1 and ThT. In the metabolic pathway of intestinal microbiota, daidzein is converted to equol through dihydrodaidzein. Liquiritigenin is a compound existing in the isoflavone synthesis pathway and can be a precursor of daidzein. For all EqX-s1 oligonucleotides, ThT fluorescence was reduced by increasing the concentration of titrated equol (Figures D and S9B). The results indicated competitive interaction between ThT and equol.

In the cases of Eq1-s1, Eq2-s1, and Eq3-s1, which were categorized as G4-forming aptamers with the same topology, daidzein, dihydrodaidzein, and liquiritigenin did not induce the decrease of ThT fluorescence with as much efficiency as equol. In contrast, for Eq4-s1, which is a G4-forming aptamer with a topology different from that of Eq1-s1, Eq2-s1, and Eq3-s1, modest ThT decreases were observed with daidzein and liquiritigenin, suggesting a different preference for compounds based on the G4 topologies. It is expected that the G4 structure formed on Eq4-s1 has the potential to widely interact with benzopyran derivatives. In contrast, since the antiparallel G4 topology formed on the other G4-forming aptamers theoretically has diagonal and/or lateral loops, which cross over the quartet plane, the nucleobases in the loops likely contributed to the high selectivity by forming direct interactions with equol. The selectivity of Eq5-s1 toward equol was intermediate between the two different categories of G4-forming aptamers, yet it still exhibited sufficiently high specificity, likely because it recognizes equol through a distinct tertiary structure unit. These results suggest that we were able to obtain highly selective aptamers, whereas we did not perform counter-selection using potentially competitive molecules; this is likely because we selected the aptamers based on the strand dissociation driven by the aptamer stabilization. The rate of decrease in the fluorescence signal was larger in the cases of Eq4-s1 and Eq5-s1 than the category of Eq1-s1, Eq2-s1, and Eq3-s1 (Figures D and S9B). Using the K obs(ThT) values for each aptamer evaluated from the ThT titration, the equilibrium association constants at 25 °C (K A25(FID)) between EqX-s1 oligonucleotides and the analyzed chemicals were calculated based on the theoretical competitive binding model (Table ). In the case of equol, all the K A25(FID) values were similar and within a 2-fold difference compared to K A25(ITC) values (Table S4), suggesting the accuracy of the quantitative treatment.

Fluorometric Detection of Equol in Human Urine

Equol in human urine was detected by FID assay using ThT. Before the assay, the presence of equol below the detection limit in the urine sample was confirmed using an equol test kit (EQUOTEST, Techno Medica Co., Ltd., Yokohama, Japan), which outputs positive or negative result. When a urine sample was spotted onto the kit, the test line showed a red mark, indicating a negative result for equol. In contrast, when equol was added to the urine sample at a concentration of 10 μM, the test line signal disappeared, indicating a positive result for equol (Figure S12). Eq1-s1 and Eq5-s1 were selected as sensor aptamers in urine because Eq1-s1 showed the highest q eq1-s1 showed the highest K A25(FID) value for equol (Table ), and Eq5-s1 showed a relatively high K A25(FID) value and a large signal change in buffer (Figure D). Urine samples mixed with varied concentrations of equol were diluted with a buffer containing the aptamer and ThT. The final concentrations of aptamer and ThT were 1 μM and 10 μM, respectively. The fluorescence signals of ThT were measured at 25 °C after 30 min incubation. The FID results showed that Eq1-s1 was not suitable for detecting equol in urine, because the fluorescence signals of ThT were almost at a similar level in the presence of equol (Figure E). It was considered that the affinity between equol and Eq1-s1 was reduced in urine. To check the interaction between the aptamer and equol, ITC was performed in the presence of 50 vol % urine (Figure S13 and Table S8). The results indicated that Eq1-s1 lost its ability to interact with equol in urine-containing solution. The topology of the G4 structure is known to be sensitive to cations such as sodium and potassium. It is considered that cations derived from urine likely affected the G4 topology of Eq1-s1 and reduced its affinity for equol in solutions containing urine. Other chemicals in urine, such as urea, also have the potential to affect binding affinities. In contrast to Eq1-s1, Eq5-s1 kept its affinity toward equol in the presence of urine (Figure S13 and Table S8) and showed an equol concentration-dependent decrease in ThT fluorescence in the FID assay (Figure F). Interaction selectivity of Eq5-s1 with equol was also demonstrated with no clear ITC signals with other chemicals, daidzein, dihydrodaidzein, and liquiritigenin (Figure S13 and Table S8). Since Eq5-s1 was indicated to form a different tertiary structure unit compared to that of G4 (Figure S11), the binding affinity was expected not to be sensitive to the chemicals derived from urine. Although the sensitivity toward equol seemed a little less than the FID assay in buffer, the LOD for equol with Eq5-s1 was 2.73 μM, which is sufficient to detect a favorable state of equol production in urine (Figure F).

Colorimetric Detection of Equol Using Aptamer-Conjugated AuNP

AuNPs show different spectroscopic properties depending on their size, shape, and state of aggregation; thus, they can be a platform for constructing colorimetric biosensors. Modification of DNAs on AuNPs is one of the strategies to functionalize AuNPs as biosensors. , The ease of AuNP aggregation varies depending on whether the DNA strand immobilized on the AuNP forms duplexes with the complementary strand or presents as a single strand. By considering the aggregation property of DNA-immobilized AuNPs and the conformational switching property of the aptamer obtained through Capture-SELEX, the aptamers can be easily incorporated into the AuNP-based colorimetric sensor (Figure A). Dissociation of the aptamer from the capture strand on the AuNP in response to equol induces AuNP aggregation that results in a color change of the solution.

To construct the colorimetric sensor, the capture strand used for Capture-SELEX was immobilized on AuNPs. The results of the FID assay and ITC indicated that Eq5-s1 is suitable for detecting equol in urine samples, whereas Eq1-s1 is not suitable due to loss of its equol binding ability (Figures E,F and S13). Hence, we designed Eq5-ex, which contains a constant region complementary to the capture strand (Table S3). Changes in AuNP aggregation in the absence and presence of equol were first evaluated in a Capture-SELEX buffer under different concentrations of Eq5-ex to be hybridized to the capture strand on AuNPs (Figure S14). Assuming that approximately 1,000 copies of the capture strand are immobilized on one AuNP, the concentration of the capture strand in the mixture of AuNPs and Eq5-ex is approximately 250 nM. When Eq5-ex was hybridized to the capture strand under more than equimolar conditions, AuNPs did not show aggregation even in the presence of equol. On the other hand, when a smaller amount of Eq5-ex was hybridized to the capture strand, AuNP aggregation occurred in the presence of equol, and the relative absorbance signal (800 nm/520 nm) increased. These results suggest that Eq5-ex is not efficiently dissociated from the capture strand on AuNPs, which is required for inducing AuNP aggregation. Thus, the condition under which a part of the capture strands on the AuNP hybridizes with the aptamer strand was expected to be optimal for the AuNP to efficiently aggregate in response to equol. In addition, the signal changes showed different time courses to be saturated depending on the concentration of Eq5-ex. The results suggested that the rate-determining step of the AuNP aggregation is the dissociation process of Eq5-ex from the capture strand in response to equol. The lower dissociation efficiency from the capture strand also suggests a potential reason for the lower enrichment and abundance of Eq5 after Capture-SELEX compared to other aptamers (Table ).

On the basis of the condition under which Eq5-ex was hybridized to AuNPs at an 80 nM concentration, equol-dependent AuNP aggregation was confirmed in the Capture-SELEX buffer (Figure B). The relative absorbance signal increased with increasing equol concentration, and an LOD of 115 nM was obtained. The relatively low LOD value can be attributed to a small deviation in the absorbance signals in the absence of equol and the high absorbance coefficient of the AuNP. To confirm whether the colorimetric sensor exhibits selectivity comparable to that of the fluorometric sensor, AuNP aggregation was tested in the presence of daidzein, dihydrodaidzein, and liquiritigenin. The presence of 500 nM daidzein, dihydrodaidzein, and liquiritigenin did not cause AuNP aggregation, and the relative absorbance signals were almost the same as those of the control sample without additional chemicals (Figure C).

Colorimetric Detection of Equol in Human Urine

Finally, colorimetric equol detection was demonstrated in urine samples (Figure ). To facilitate the straightforward detection of equol in human urine samples, Eq5-ex was hybridized to the capture strand on AuNPs and dried in the presence of sucrose and trehalose, ensuring high storage stability and portability (Figure S15). Then, urine samples containing varied concentrations of equol were added to the AuNPs and incubated at 25 °C for 60 min. The color of the AuNPs changed with increasing equol concentration in the urine sample (Figure ). Linear correlation was observed in the range of 0 to 6 μM equol concentration. The LOD value calculated from the linear correlation was 846 nM, which is sufficient to detect a favorable state of equol production in urine, although the relative absorbance value of the blank sample was slightly higher in urine compared to the buffer condition. The increase in the relative absorbance value suggests a minor degree of nonspecific aggregation caused by the urine matrix or the disaccharides, sucrose and trehalose, added for long-term storage. For further optimization, adjusting the dilution factor of the urine sample and the concentration of the sugars would be an effective approach to suppress this nonspecific aggregation while maintaining the equol concentration within the detectable range.

The concentration-dependent colorimetric change was demonstrated by a simple process; the urine sample was mixed with the buffer and added to the pretreated AuNPs, which had been kept at 4 °C for at least one month. Considering its application as a sensor for individual health monitoring, the simple detection process and the capacity for long-term storage while maintaining its performance are advantageous. As discussed above, the rate-determining step of this sensor was considered the dissociation process of the aptamer from the capture strand. Therefore, optimizing the sequence complementarity between the aptamer and the capture strands is expected to shorten the time to detect equol. Since the colorimetric change depending on equol concentration can be detected using mobile cameras, this equol detection system is expected to enable more precise testing compared to test kits that qualitatively produce negative and positive results.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed equol-specific DNA aptamers through Capture-SELEX. As a proof of concept for the construction of a sensor based on the conformational switching of the aptamers, we demonstrated that a colorimetric sensor detects equol in a commercial human urine sample. Since the aptamers formed tertiary structure units that potentially interact with ThT and enhanced its fluorescence, we also demonstrated fluorometric equol detection based on the displacement of ThT in response to equol. The fluorometric and colorimetric detection systems offer complementary advantages. The fluorometric detection system based on a label-free FID assay allows high-throughput handling of multiple samples, making it suitable for health checks and contract analysis services. In contrast, the colorimetric detection system enables visual recognition of signal changes, facilitating its application as a health-monitoring device for individual use.

To summarize the utility of our proposed sensing platforms, a comprehensive comparison of the analytical performances between the fluorometric and colorimetric sensors is presented in Table S9. Both sensors exhibited sufficient linear detection ranges and LODs for evaluating favorable equol production in human urine. Furthermore, we evaluated the apparent recovery rates of the spiked points as back-calculated accuracy. The apparent recoveries were highly quantitative, yielding 98–104% for the fluorometric assay and 93–101% for the colorimetric assay within their quantifiable ranges. These results provide preliminary evidence that both sensing systems can accurately quantify equol. Although further validation using multiple real human urine samples from different individuals would be required to evaluate the potential interference from diverse urine matrices, the dual detection modalities potentially provide a versatile platform for equol sensing that can be tailored to both professional and consumer healthcare settings. Furthermore, the workflow demonstrated in this studyfrom aptamer selection through Capture-SELEX to sensor constructioncan be adapted for various target molecules. The process described here serves as a useful construction principle for health-monitoring devices, which are crucial for extending healthy life expectancy.
