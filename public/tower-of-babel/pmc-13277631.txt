Impact of lactic acid bacterial strain and storage temperature on the quality and functionality of fermented mulberry juice
Authors: Yuanxia Wang, Sixian Tan, Tianhang Wang, Dejian Huang, Pingkang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study systematically analyzes the differences in quality parameters and volatile flavor compounds of mulberry juice fermented with Lactobacillus plantarum (LPJ) and Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus (LBJ) during storage at room temperature (25 °C) and 4 °C. Lactic acid fermentation reduced sugar content, pH, and protein levels. After fermentation, the total phenol, total flavonoid, and total anthocyanin contents, along with antioxidant activity, were enhanced. However, the levels of active compounds declined during storage, with a slower decline observed at 4 °C. The LPJ group exhibited superior antioxidant activity. A total of 144 flavor compounds were identified, with the variety and abundance of volatile compounds increasing after fermentation compared to unfermented mulberry juice. After 21 days of storage, the number of volatile compounds decreased to 120, with a notable reduction in ester and aldehyde contents. Crude polysaccharides from LPJ-fermented mulberry juice showed significant ethanol dehydrogenase and hypoglycemic activities, supporting the medicinal potential of mulberries.

Introduction

Plants of the genus Morus Linn. (Moraceae), originating from China and Korea, are distributed across temperate, subtropical, and tropical regions (Luo et al., 2024), with Morus rubra, Morus nigra, and Morus alba being economically and medicinally important species (Ali Redha et al., 2023; Li et al., 2019). Mature mulberry fruits are rich in vitamins B and C, organic acids, minerals (zinc, iron), and bioactive compounds such as anthocyanins, polyphenols, flavonoids, stilbenes, and polysaccharides (Bian et al., 2025; Kumar et al., 2024). These constituents exert antioxidative, anti-inflammatory (Wen et al., 2019), antidiabetic, anticancer (Wang et al., 2019), and neuroprotective effects (Luo et al., 2025). Given their rich nutritional value and functional components, mulberries must be harvested at full maturity and processed or consumed promptly to preserve optimal flavor and nutritional quality (Herman et al., 2022). However, the fruit is highly perishable, which restricts its marketability. Consequently, mulberries are commonly processed into dried mulberries, mulberry wine, mulberry paste, and mulberry beverages to enhance product value and prolong shelf life (Boasiako et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2025). Lactic acid fermentation, recognized as a safe and efficient processing technology, is widely used in fruit and vegetable juices and is known to significantly improve flavor and overall product quality (Cai et al., 2025). During lactic acid fermentation, various organic acids and aroma-related metabolites are produced, facilitating the degradation of macromolecules and positively influencing the sensory attributes of fruit-based products (Duan et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024).

Research on lactic acid fermentation of mulberry juice has grown substantially in recent years. Liu et al. (2025) revealed that lactic acid fermentation remodels the juice metabolome and enhances flavor characteristics. Wang et al. (2022) demonstrated that fruit maturity significantly influences the physicochemical properties, nutritional composition, volatile profile, and sensory attributes of fermented mulberry juice. Beyond flavor and basic quality traits, increasing attention has been directed toward the modulation of bioactive components during fermentation, particularly mulberry polysaccharides, which represent a critical class of macromolecules contributing significantly to the functional properties of mulberry products. These polysaccharides have been reported to exhibit antioxidant, hypoglycemic, and hepatoprotective effects, including activity against acute alcoholic liver injury (Chen et al., 2016; Hu et al., 2021). Alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH), a key metabolic enzyme, plays a significant role in alcohol metabolism and antioxidant processes, as demonstrated by Chen et al. (2025); thus, alterations in its activity can indirectly reflect the functional characteristics of fermented products. Despite their importance, the influence of lactic acid bacteria fermentation on the functional performance of mulberry polysaccharides remains poorly characterized. Although the effects of lactic acid fermentation on mulberry juice quality and flavor have been extensively studied, most existing research remains focused on single bacterial strains or short-term fermentation outcomes. Moreover, while some studies have explored metabolic differences among lactic acid bacteria during fermentation, comprehensive evaluations of post-fermentation product performance, including flavor stability and retention of bioactive compounds during refrigerated storage are still scarce.

The present study investigates the quality characteristics of mulberry juice fermented by lactic acid bacteria. While lactic acid fermentation effectively combines the nutritional value of fruit substrates with beneficial microbial metabolites and enjoys broad consumer acceptance, it frequently results in products with limited flavor complexity, thereby constraining sensory appeal and product diversification (Hu et al., 2021). To address this challenge, Lactobacillus plantarum (L. plantarum) and Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus (L. bulgaricus) were compared in terms of their effects on the physicochemical properties, protein content, total phenolics, total flavonoids, anthocyanins, antioxidant activity, flavor profiles, and crude polysaccharide characteristics of fermented mulberry juice. This comparative approach not only evaluates immediate fermentation outcomes but also tracks changes during subsequent refrigerated storage, offering a more holistic assessment of product quality and functionality. This study aims to enhance the utilization value of mulberries and expand the diversity of mulberry-derived products. Additionally, the research provides preliminary insights into the functional activities of fermented mulberry polysaccharides, laying a foundation for future structure function studies. The findings will offer theoretical support and technical guidance for the large-scale development of mulberry-based fermented products, contributing to the green, healthy, and sustainable advancement of the related industry.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Mulberries were sourced from the Sichuan International Agricultural Products Trading Center (Sichuan, China) and Shandong Yantai Dongshun Food Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). Food-grade L. bulgaricus was acquired from Tianyi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). L. plantarum was isolated from Sichuan traditional paocai and preserved as a laboratory strain (Sichuan, China). α-Glucosidase and α-amylase were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All analytical-grade reagents, including, gallic acid (CAS: 149–91-7, 97.7%), rutin (CAS: 153–18-4, 98%), potassium persulfate (CAS: 7727-21-1, GR), and acarbose (CAS: 56180–94-0, GR) were supplied by Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Absolute ethanol and food-grade pectinase were obtained from Hefei Bomei Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hefei, China). A handheld digital refractometer (model FAL-102) was purchased from Shenzhen Yuanhengtong Technology Co., Ltd., a digital pH meter (model PHS-320) and a precision constant temperature incubator (model BPX-82), both purchased from Chengdu Century Ark Technology Co., Ltd., a high-speed centrifuge (model 5804R) was sourced from Eppendorf AG (Hamburg, Germany), a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (model UV2400) supplied by Shanghai Sunny Hengping Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd.

Sample preparation and fermentation

Bacterial strains were activated in MRS broth at 37 °C for 24 h, washed twice with sterile physiological saline, and adjusted to an OD₆₀₀ of 0.8–1.0 prior to inoculation. Frozen mulberries stored at −80 °C were thawed at 4 °C for 12 h, homogenized using a high-speed blender, and filtered through three layers of sterile gauze. The filtrate was pasteurized at 65 °C for 30 min and then cooled to room temperature. For L. plantarum–fermented mulberry juice (LPJ), 200 mL of pasteurized mulberry juice was inoculated with L. plantarum at 3% (v/v) and fermented at 37 °C. For L. bulgaricus–fermented mulberry juice (LBJ), 200 mL of pasteurized mulberry juice was inoculated with L. bulgaricus at 3% (v/v) and fermented at 37 °C. For both fermentation treatments, pH was monitored every 12 h, and fermentation was terminated when the pH reached 3.5–3.8. Pasteurized, unfermented mulberry juice (MJ) was prepared by transferring 200 mL of pasteurized mulberry juice for subsequent analyses without inoculation.

After fermentation termination, samples were aseptically filled into sterile PET bottles and stored at normal temperature (25 °C) and 4 °C for 21 days. Samples were collected at 3-day intervals throughout the storage period.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation was conducted following the method of Zhao et al. (2025) with minor modifications. A trained panel of ten food science students (five males and five females) assessed the sensory attributes of the lactic acid–fermented mulberry juice based on predefined scoring criteria. The evaluation encompassed five core sensory domains: appearance (including homogeneity and sedimentation), color (clarity and gloss), aroma (fruitiness, fermentation notes, and off-odors), flavor (taste balance, complexity, aftertaste, and sweetness–acidity equilibrium), and mouthfeel (astringency and tactile texture). Each attribute was scored on a 10-point scale, where higher scores indicated superior sensory quality. Detailed scoring criteria for all sub-attributes are provided in Supplementary Material (Supplementary Tables 1).

Determination of soluble solids, pH, and protein content

Soluble solids (°Brix) and pH were determined using a handheld digital refractometer (FAL-102, Shenzhen Yuanhengtong Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) and a digital pH meter (PHS-320, Chengdu Shijifangzhou Technology Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China), respectively.

The protein content was determined following the method of Hurtado-Murillo et al. (2025) with slight modifications. The sample was diluted 100-fold, and 1 mL of the diluted sample was transferred into a 10 mL test tube, followed by the addition of 5 mL of G-250 protein dye reagent. After thorough mixing, the reaction mixture was allowed to stand for 10 min and then transferred into a cuvette. The absorbance was measured at 595 nm, and the protein content was calculated by substituting the absorbance value into the standard calibration curve.

Determination of total viable count (TVC)

The total viable count of fermented mulberry juice during storage was determined according to GB 4789.2–2022 National Food Safety Standard – Food Microbiological Examination: Total Viable Count.

Determination of total phenolic content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC), anthocyanin content

The total phenolic content was determined according to the method of Jiang et al. (2014) with minor modifications. Briefly, 0.5 mL of appropriately diluted sample was mixed with 0.5 mL of Folin-Ciocalteau reagent, followed 30 s later by the addition of 3 mL of 7.5% Na₂CO₃ solution. After incubation in the dark at room temperature for 30 min, absorbance was measured at 764 nm. The total flavonoid content was determined following the method of Wang et al. (2021) with slight modifications. A 2 mL sample was mixed with 0.6 mL of 5% NaNO₂ solution and allowed to stand for 6 min. Then, 0.6 mL of 10% Al(NO₃)₃ solution was added and left for another 6 min, followed by the addition of 8 mL of 4% NaOH solution. The mixture was diluted to 15 mL with distilled water and mixed thoroughly. The absorbance was measured at 508 nm using a spectrophotometer. The total flavonoid content was expressed as rutin equivalents (mg/mL).

The anthocyanin content was determined following the method of Anh et al. (2025) with slight modifications. A 1 mL aliquot of sample was mixed separately with 9 mL of KCl buffer (0.025 M, pH 1.0) and 9 mL of CH₃COONa buffer (0.4 M, pH 4.5). Both mixtures were kept in the dark for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at 514 nm and 700 nm using a spectrophotometer, with distilled water serving as the blank. Each sample was measured in triplicate. The anthocyanin content was calculated according to Eq. (2.1).(2.1)Total anthocyanin contentmg/L=A×MW×DF×1000ε×1where A = [(A₅₁₄ − A₇₀₀) at pH 1.0] − [(A₅₁₄ − A₇₀₀) at pH 4.5]; MW (molecular weight of cyanidin-3-glucoside) = 449.2 g/mol; DF = dilution factor; 1 = pathlength (cm); and ε (molar extinction coefficient of cyanidin-3-glucoside) = 26,900 L/(mol·cm).

ABTS+ radical-scavenging activity

The ABTS+ radical-scavenging activity was determined according to the method of Hu et al. (2021) with minor modifications. A 0.1 mL sample was added to 3.9 mL of ABTS+ working solution and left to react in the dark for 6 min. Absorbance was measured at 734 nm. All samples were analyzed in triplicate. Extraction and biofunctional assessment of crude polysaccharides from fermented mulberry juice

Crude polysaccharides were extracted from fermented mulberry juice following a modified protocol of Li et al. (2023). Briefly, juice samples were subjected to hot water extraction (1,15 w/v, 80 °C, 2 h), followed by centrifugation, filtration, and rotary evaporation. Proteins were removed via the Sevag method. Crude polysaccharides were then precipitated overnight at 4 °C with four volumes of anhydrous ethanol, collected by centrifugation, washed with 80% ethanol, and freeze-dried. The resulting crude polysaccharides from L. plantarum– and L. bulgaricus–fermented juices were designated FMP1 and FMP2, respectively.

ADH was determined according to the method of Chen et al. (2025) with slight modifications. Crude polysaccharide solutions with concentrations of 1, 5, and 10 mg/mL were prepared and measured using an ethanol dehydrogenase assay kit.

Hypoglycemic activity was evaluated by measuring the inhibition rates of α-glucosidase and α-amylase. The α-glucosidase inhibition rate was determined following the method of Chen et al. (2016) with slight modifications. Crude polysaccharide solutions (FMP) at concentrations of 1, 5, and 10 mg/mL were prepared. A 40 μL aliquot of the FMP solution was mixed with 20 μL of α-glucosidase solution and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Then, 20 μL of p-nitrophenyl-α-D-glucopyranoside (1.5 mmol/L) was added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After the reaction, 100 μL of 0.2 mol/L sodium carbonate solution was added to terminate the reaction, and the absorbance was measured at 405 nm. Acarbose was used as a positive control. The α-glucosidase inhibition rate was calculated according to Eq. (2.4):(2.4)Inhibition%=1−A1−A2A0×100

Where A₁ = absorbance of the sample solution; A₂ = absorbance of the sample solution without α-glucosidase; A₀ = absorbance of the blank solution.

The α-amylase inhibition rate was determined according to the method of Yang et al. (2023) with slight modifications. Crude polysaccharide solutions at concentrations of 1, 5, and 10 mg/mL were prepared. A 100 μL aliquot of FMP solution was mixed with 100 μL of α-amylase solution and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Then, 200 μL of 1% soluble starch solution was added, and the reaction was continued at 37 °C for 10 min, followed by a 10 min boiling water bath. After the reaction, 200 μL of DNS reagent was added and heated in a boiling water bath for 10 min. Electronic nose (E-nose) analysis

Electronic nose analysis was conducted according to the method of Xu et al. (2025) with slight modifications. A 3 mL sample was placed into a headspace vial and incubated in a 45 °C water bath for 15 min. The sampling needle was then inserted into the vial for measurement. The electronic nose system (PEN3, AIRSENSE Analytics; Germany) was operated under the following conditions: sampling flow rate 300 mL/min, sample preparation time 5 s, sensor cleaning time 120 s, and sample analysis time 120 s. The instrument features a detection unit with 10 metal oxide sensors (W1C, W1S, W1W, W2S, W2W, W3C, W3S, W5S, W5C, and W6S).

Determination of volatile flavor compounds

Volatile compounds were analyzed by headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-SPME) coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS), following the methods of Sui et al. (2024) and Wang et al. (2021) with minor modifications. A 3 mL sample of fermented mulberry juice was placed in a headspace vial, followed by the addition of 1 g NaCl and 2 μL of 2-methyl-3-heptanone solution (0.816 μg/mL). The vial was sealed immediately and incubated at 55 °C for 20 min. After extraction, desorption was performed for 3 min.

GC analysis was performed in splitless mode with an injector temperature of 250 °C and a 1-min solvent delay. The oven program was as follows: 40 °C (held 3.5 min), ramped at 10 °C/min to 100 °C, then at 7 °C/min to 180 °C, and finally at 25 °C/min to 230 °C (held 5 min). MS detection was carried out in electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV, with a transfer line temperature of 240 °C and a mass scan range of m/z 41–330.

Statistical analysis

All treatments and measurements were performed in triplicate, and results were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using Origin 2024. A t-test was used to calculate significant differences at P < 0.05.

Results and analysis

Sensory evaluation results

The sensory evaluation results are shown in Fig. 1 There was no significant difference in color between the two sample groups (P > 0.05). Due to the inherently dark pigmentation of mulberry fruits, no obvious color change was observed after fermentation (Fig. 1). Following fermentation, the fruity aroma weakened, while a lactic acid fermentation odor emerged. The LBJ group exhibited a more pronounced fermented aroma than the LPJ group (P > 0.05), which may be attributed to the stronger lactic acid odor produced by L. bulgaricus.Fig. 1Sensory profile of lactic acid bacteria–fermented mulberry juice.Fig. 1

Sensory profile of lactic acid bacteria–fermented mulberry juice.

The LPJ sample presented a richer mouthfeel, with a stronger overall palatability and moderate acidity (P > 0.05). This may be associated with the ability of L. plantarum to influence malolactic conversion by releasing carboxyl groups from malic acid, thereby producing a greater variety of alcohols. Moreover, under microaerobic conditions, L. plantarum can generate acetic acid or ethyl acetate, contributing to a more complex and layered flavor profile in the juice (Wang et al., 2024). This observation agrees with the results of Chen et al. (2019), who demonstrated that malic acid serves as an effective carbon source for lactic acid production. In addition, commercial strains often exhibit higher metabolic activity than preserved strains, resulting in more pronounced acidity in fermented juice. By contrast, the preserved strain yielded a milder acidity with a more prominent fruity aroma.

Changes in physicochemical properties of fermented mulberry juice during storage

The changes in sugar content and pH reflect the fermentation process. Based on the pH variation during fermentation, the fermentation process was terminated when the pH of the mulberry juice reached 3.5–3.8. Fig. 2 shows the changes in sugar content and pH during the 21-day storage of the samples at 25 °C and 4 °C. After lactic acid bacterial fermentation, the sugar content significantly decreased (P < 0.05), which was attributed to the consumption of most of the sugars by the lactic acid bacteria during their metabolic process. Additionally, the pH significantly decreased after fermentation (P < 0.05), due to the production of acidic compounds by lactic acid bacteria. Moreover, amino acids and reducing sugars generate reducing aldehydes and ketones, which are subsequently oxidized to acidic substances, leading to a decrease in pH (Yang et al., 2025). The pH of the LBJ group was slightly lower than that of the LPJ group (P < 0.05), which might be due to the higher acidity produced by L. bulgaricus fermentation and its stronger adaptation to low pH environments (Khrundin & Nikitina, 2024). Under normal temperature storage, both sugar content and pH gradually decreased after day 12, likely due to temperature fluctuations that caused some microbial regrowth, consuming the nutrients in the mulberry juice. In the 4 °C storage environment, there were no significant changes in sugar content or pH (P > 0.05), which could be attributed to the stability of soluble sugars in the sample, and the low temperature effectively suppressed microbial growth and reproduction, maintaining the stability of the mulberry juice during storage. The data suggest that refrigeration at 4 °C appears to be a critical factor in mitigating post-fermentation biochemical changes, effectively preserving the physicochemical stability of the product.Fig. 2Changes in physicochemical properties of fermented mulberry juice during storage: Brix-25 °C(A), pH -25 °C(B), and protein content-25 °C(C), Brix-4 °C(D), pH -4 °C(E) and protein content- 4 °C(F).Fig. 2

Protein is one of the important nutritional components in mulberry juice, and its content reflects the nutritional value of the product, making it a key indicator of product quality. Lactic acid bacteria utilize proteins in mulberry juice as a nitrogen source for growth and reproduction, and they may secrete enzymes such as pectinase or cellulase during fermentation, which can affect protein content (Wang et al., 2021). As shown in Fig. 2, the protein content in the LPJ group was significantly higher than that of the LBJ group (P < 0.05). This may be attributed to the ability of L. plantarum to secrete more proteases and peptidases during fermentation, which can degrade plant cell walls and release bound proteins. Additionally, its facultative heterofermentative metabolic pathway tends to retain nitrogen sources for microbial protein synthesis (Hu et al., 2024). In contrast, L. bulgaricus exhibits stronger protease activity and can degrade proteins to release aroma compounds (Hou et al., 2015). During normal-temperature storage, the protein content decreased more rapidly, likely because higher temperatures promote microbial activity or enhance the activity of endogenous enzymes in mulberry juice, such as cysteine proteases, resulting in protein degradation. At 4 °C, microbial proliferation and enzymatic activity were markedly inhibited, leading to a slower decline in protein content. These differences may be related to strain-specific metabolic and enzymatic activities during fermentation.

In the 4 °C storage environment, microbial growth was inhibited, and the increase in total viable count was slower. This was likely due to the organic acids such as lactic acid and acetic acid produced by the lactic acid bacteria during fermentation, which suppressed the growth of harmful microorganisms. Alternatively, the rapid proliferation of lactic acid bacteria might have consumed nutrients and occupied living space, thereby limiting the growth of other microorganisms. Therefore, the low-temperature storage environment was more favorable for maintaining the stability of mulberry juice and extending its shelf life.

Changes in TPC, TFC, anthocyanin content during storage of fermented mulberry juice

After lactic acid fermentation, the total phenolic contents of the LPJ and LBJ groups were 596.84 ± 9.68 mg/L and 576.97 ± 9.31 mg/L, respectively. This difference is related to the distinct polyphenol-associated enzymes secreted by different microbial strains, which mediate various biotransformation pathways of phenolic compounds (Jiang et al., 2024). L. plantarum can release β-glucosidase, glycosidases, and other hydrolases that degrade complex bound phenolics into monomeric phenolic compounds, thereby enhancing their bioactivity and bioavailability and ultimately increasing total phenolic content (Wang et al., 2021). Additionally, enzymes such as phenolic acid esterases produced during L. plantarum fermentation can release phenolics bound to proteins or polysaccharides, whereas L. bulgaricus possesses fewer or less active such enzymes (Yang et al., 2024). The stability of phenolic compounds is also influenced by pH. As storage time increased, total phenolic content showed a decreasing trend. The decline was faster at 25 °C and slower at 4 °C, mainly because low-temperature storage effectively inhibits oxidation reactions, slows enzymatic activities, and suppresses microbial metabolism, thereby delaying polyphenol degradation. The elevated phenolic content in LPJ likely stems from superior enzymatic liberation, while refrigeration proves essential for stabilizing these compounds post-fermentation.

Flavonoids are important indicators of mulberry juice quality, and their content is susceptible to oxidation, enzymatic reactions, and microbial metabolism during storage. The changes in flavonoid content can therefore guide the optimization of storage conditions. After fermentation, the total flavonoid content in LPJ and LBJ was 199.03 ± 0.4 mg/L and 176.9 ± 0.19 mg/L, respectively. Lactic acid bacteria utilize flavonoids as carbon sources and catalyze the hydrolysis of glycosidic bonds, resulting in decreased total flavonoid levels (Zhao et al., 2025). For example, L. plantarum can secrete certain extracellular enzymes that promote the hydrolysis of flavonoid glycosides, increasing the content of free flavonoids (Wang et al., 2025). However, L. bulgaricus primarily affects flavonoid stability through acidification and contributes less to flavonoid release (Zhao et al., 2025). Therefore, the LPJ group retained higher total flavonoid content than the LBJ group.

Anthocyanins are key components influencing the color of fermented mulberry juice. The changes in anthocyanin content during storage are shown in Fig. 3 Before fermentation, the anthocyanin content was 87.11 ± 4.89 mg/L. After fermentation, the anthocyanin content increased to 110.68 ± 5.1 mg/L in the LPJ group and 96.65 ± 7.6 mg/L in the LBJ group. During fermentation, lactic acid bacteria facilitate the degradation of plant cell walls, thereby releasing anthocyanins that were previously bound or entrapped within the cellular matrix. Concurrently, the acidic environment generated by bacterial metabolism enhances the stability of these pigments and improves their extractability, resulting in a significant increase in the measured anthocyanin content (P < 0.05). The higher anthocyanin content in the LPJ group (P < 0.05) may result from stronger de-glycosylation capability toward glycosylated phenolics in mulberry juice, enabling the release of conjugated or cell-wall-bound phenolics (Kwaw et al., 2018). Additionally, higher β-glucosidase activity in LAB promotes further anthocyanin release (Lv et al., 2025). On the other hand, L. bulgaricus produces a stronger acidic environment during fermentation, which can accelerate anthocyanin degradation. Changes in antioxidant activity of fermented mulberry juice during storage

The DPPH· radical scavenging activity of mulberry juice before fermentation was 75.8%. After fermentation, the activity increased to 79.8% in the LPJ group and 77.6% in the LBJ group (Fig. 4), indicating that lactic acid fermentation promotes DPPH· radical scavenging capacity. LAB fermentation facilitates the release of total phenolics and flavonoids, and the production of antioxidant metabolites such as γ-aminobutyric acid and ferulic acid during fermentation may further enhance antioxidative potential (Kwaw et al., 2018). However, the relatively high acidity of L. bulgaricus may degrade certain antioxidant compounds, thus lowering its radical scavenging ability.Fig. 4Changes in antioxidant capacity of fermented mulberry juice during storage: DPPH·-25 °C(A), ABTS+-25 °C(B), DPPH·-4 °C(C), ABTS+-4 °C(D).Fig. 4

Similarly, ABTS+ scavenging activity exhibited a trend of initial increase followed by a decline. After fermentation, the ABTS+ clearance rates of the LPJ and LBJ groups were 66.71% and 59.47%, respectively (Fig. 4B and D). The aromatic benzene ring and resonance-stabilized electron pairs on phenolic hydroxyl groups enhance electron delocalization, thereby improving antioxidant activity (Benedek et al., 2020). During storage, antioxidant activity gradually declined, mainly due to the degradation and oxidation of antioxidant components (anthocyanins, flavonoids, total phenolics), intensified oxidation reactions, enzymatic browning, and the effects of light and pH fluctuations. These degradative reactions proceed more rapidly at 25 °C, resulting in a pronounced decrease in antioxidant capacity, whereas storage at 4 °C slows oxidation and degradation processes, helping to maintain the antioxidant activity of mulberry juice. Therefore, low-temperature storage is essential for preserving product quality.

Impact of fermentation on the bioactive potential of mulberry polysaccharides

Given that lactic acid fermentation can modify the yield, molecular weight, and structural features of plant polysaccharides—thereby influencing their bioactivity (Chen et al., 2016). To determine whether strain-specific fermentation confers functional benefits beyond sensory and stability improvements, we extracted crude polysaccharides from samples.

ADH activity was evaluated as an indicator of potential anti-alcoholic liver effects. As shown in Fig. 5A, mulberry crude polysaccharides enhanced ADH activity in a concentration-dependent manner, FMP1 exhibiting significantly higher activation than those from FMP2 at equivalent concentrations. This difference likely results from L. plantarum's heterofermentative metabolism. During fermentation, this strain produces acetic acid, synthesizes exopolysaccharides (EPS), and enzymatically modifies native polysaccharides (Jiang et al., 2025). In contrast, L. bulgaricus is a homofermentative strain with limited EPS production. Similarly, α-glucosidase and α-amylase inhibitory activities were measured to evaluate hypoglycemic potential. Fermented mulberry crude polysaccharides dose-dependently inhibited both enzymes (Fig. 5B-C), again with FMP1 showing stronger α-glucosidase inhibition than FMP-LB. Volatile flavor compounds

Unfermented and fermented mulberry juices were stored at 25 °C and 4 °C for 21 days. Volatile flavor compounds on days 1 and 21 were analyzed using GC × GC–MS. A total of 144 volatile compounds were identified, including 37 alcohols, 21 esters, 10 ketones, 10 aldehydes, and 14 phenolic compounds (Supplementary Tables 2). Compared with unfermented juice, both the number and relative abundance of volatile substances increased after fermentation (Fig. 7).Fig. 7Number (A) and total content (B) of volatile compounds in fermented mulberry juice, with heatmap analysis of major volatile flavor profiles (C).Fig. 7

Number (A) and total content (B) of volatile compounds in fermented mulberry juice, with heatmap analysis of major volatile flavor profiles (C).

Alcohols, which are important contributors to the flavor profile of fermented mulberry juice, typically impart fresh floral and herbal notes and have low sensory thresholds. They originate from carbohydrate, amino acid, and fatty acid metabolism by lactic acid bacteria (Peng et al., 2021). After fermentation, the number of alcohols increased to 21 and 23 species in the LPJ and LBJ groups, respectively. Isoamyl alcohol, benzyl alcohol, and octanol were more abundant in the LPJ group, likely due to the strong glycolytic capacity of L. plantarum, producing ethanol under anaerobic conditions and generating higher alcohols through amino acid degradation (leucine, isoleucine, phenylalanine) (Guan et al., 2024). These compounds collectively enhanced the perceived fruitiness and floral aroma in the LPJ group, as reflected in its higher sensory scores for fruit aroma and lower intensity of fermentation odor (Fig. 1). In contrast, isobutanol and nonanol levels were higher in the LBJ group because L. bulgaricus primarily relies on lactose metabolism to produce lactic acid, resulting in weaker alcohol-producing ability. The dominance of these less fruity alcohols, combined with a simpler volatile profile, contributed to the stronger lactic and fermented note and reduced fruitiness observed in sensory evaluation.

Esters are major contributors to fruity and floral aromas in fermented mulberry juice(Zhu et al., 2018). Ethyl acetate and hexyl acetate were more abundant in the LPJ group due to its higher esterase activity, which facilitates ester formation from alcohols and organic acids (Xie et al., 2025). This richer ester profile directly corresponded to the LPJ's superior sensory rating in fruit aroma. The lower esterase activity of L. bulgaricus results in fewer esters, with ethyl acetate being the predominant one, limiting aromatic complexity and aligning with the LBJ group's more muted fruity perception.

Acids are important flavor-imparting substances produced through lipid breakdown and protein hydrolysis. Acetic acid and butyric acid were present at higher levels in the LPJ group. This is attributed to the heterofermentative metabolism of L. plantarum, which generates various organic acids such as acetic, caprylic, and nonanoic acids, leading to a more complex acidic profile in mulberry juice (Guan et al., 2024). Consequently, the LPJ group exhibited a multi-layered sourness profile in sensory assessment, consistent with its diverse acid composition. In contrast, L. bulgaricus primarily conducts homofermentative lactic acid fermentation, resulting in a simpler acid composition dominated by lactic acid.

Collectively, the metabolic characteristics of L. plantarum result in a more diverse and abundant volatile profile, whereas L. bulgaricus yields a simpler flavor. Among all volatiles, ethyl acetate, isoamyl alcohol, and acetic acid emerged as key drivers of overall flavor due to their high abundance and low odor/taste thresholds; their synergistic presence underpins the characteristic fruity, floral, and sour notes that define the sensory identity of fermented mulberry juice. Although mouthfeel attributes such as homogeneity and astringency are not directly governed by volatiles, the LPJ group showed slightly lower astringency and higher homogeneity. This difference may be attributed to L. plantarum's broader metabolic activity, which could involve modification of polyphenol–protein or polyphenol–polysaccharide interactions, although further validation is needed (Xu et al., 2025).

Conclusion

This study investigated the quality changes of mulberry juice fermented with two lactic acid bacteria (L. plantarum and L. bulgaricus) during storage. After fermentation, the contents of total phenolics, total flavonoids, anthocyanins, and antioxidant activity increased significantly. Among the two strains, L. plantarum was identified as the more suitable starter culture for mulberry juice fermentation, as the LPJ group exhibited higher levels of bioactive substances and antioxidant activity than the LBJ group. However, prolonged storage led to decreases in these bioactive components and antioxidant capacity. Ambient storage at 25 °C was employed as an accelerated degradation model to simulate non-refrigerated (non-cold-chain) conditions, under which the loss of bioactive compounds was rapid. In contrast, low-temperature storage at 4 °C effectively slowed these declines and maintained product stability, representing the optimal and recommended practice for commercial storage. Fermented mulberry juice produced with L. plantarum demonstrated enhanced functional properties, including notable ADH-activation and hypoglycemic activities, supporting its potential application in functional foods and nutraceuticals. For product development, the findings suggest that L. plantarum-fermented mulberry juice, stored at low temperatures, can serve as a promising ingredient for functional beverages targeting blood glucose management and alcohol metabolism support.

Despite these findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. The sensory evaluation involved a relatively small pane, which may limit the generalizability of the sensory results. The polysaccharide-related activities were assessed solely through in vitro assays, and their in vivo efficacy remains to be confirmed. The link between volatile compounds and sensory attributes requires also further validation through correlation or multivariate analysis. Additionally, while pH and anthocyanin content were monitored during storage as proxies for acidity and color stability, direct measurements of total acidity and color difference would have strengthened the quality assessment. Future work may integrate modern omics techniques—such as metabolomics and transcriptomics—to elucidate the metabolic pathways of key bioactive compounds during fermentation. Furthermore, applying lactic acid–fermented mulberry juice in functional foods to explore its potential in modulating gut microbiota, combating exercise fatigue, and alleviating allergies holds promising prospects for sustainable development of the mulberry industry.
