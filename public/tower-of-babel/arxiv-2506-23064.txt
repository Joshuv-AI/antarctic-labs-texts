On sporadic symmetry breaking operators for principal series representations of the de Sitter and Lorentz groups
Víctor PÉREZ-VALDÉS

Abstract
In this paper, we construct and classify all differential symmetry breaking operators 𝔻 λ , ν N , m : C ∞ ​ ( S 3 , 𝒱 λ 2 ​ N + 1 ) → C ∞ ​ ( S 2 , ℒ m , ν ) \mathbb{D}_{\lambda,\nu}^{N,m}:C^{\infty}(S^{3},\mathcal{V}_{\lambda}^{2N+1})\rightarrow C^{\infty}(S^{2},\mathcal{L}_{m,\nu}) between principal series representations with respect to the restriction S ​ O 0 ​ ( 4 , 1 ) ↓ S ​ O 0 ​ ( 3 , 1 ) SO_{0}(4,1)\downarrow SO_{0}(3,1) when the parameters satisfy the condition | m | > N |m|>N . In this case we also prove a localness theorem, namely, all symmetry breaking operators between the principal series representations above are necessarily differential operators. In addition, we show that all these symmetry breaking operators are sporadic in the sense of T. Kobayashi, that is, they cannot be obtained by residue formulas of distributional kernels.



Keywords—
Differential symmetry breaking operator, sporadic symmetry breaking operator, F-method, localness theorem, branching laws, Gegenbauer polynomials, hypergeometric function, conformal geometry.

Introduction

A representation
\Pi
of a group
G
defines naturally a representation of a subgroup
G^{\prime}\subset G
via restriction. In general, irreducibility is not preserved under restriction, and thus, the question of understanding the nature of the resulting representation
\Pi\big\rvert_{G^{\prime}}
becomes a fundamental problem. For instance, if
G
is compact and
\Pi
is finite-dimensional, then
\Pi\big\rvert_{G^{\prime}}
decomposes as a sum of irreducible finite-dimensional representations of
G^{\prime}
, and the structure of this decomposition can be studied by using classical techniques of finite-dimensional representation theory.

In the general case when
G
is non-compact and
\Pi
is infinite-dimensional, the nature of the restriction
\Pi\big\rvert_{G^{\prime}}
is unknown in most cases, and understanding its behaviour—such as its decomposition into irreducible factors—is a notoriously difficult and long-standing problem in representation theory commonly referred to as a
branching problem
.

For example, if
\Pi
is a unitary representation of
G
, we have a notion for the “irreducible decomposition” of the restriction by using the concept of the direct integral (Mautner–Teleman theorem
[
31
,
38
]
). However, in the general case when such a decomposition does not exist, alternative approaches are required to analyze the behaviour of the restriction
\Pi\big\rvert_{G^{\prime}}
.

Approximately ten years ago, T. Kobayashi proposed a program divided in three stages, to address these branching problems for real reductive Lie groups in a general framework (
ABC program

[
18
]
). In this work, he defined the concept of a
symmetry breaking operator
(SBO) as follows: given a representation
\pi
of
G^{\prime}
, a symmetry breaking operator is an element of the space
\text{{Hom}}_{G^{\prime}}(\Pi\big\rvert_{G^{\prime}},\pi)
; in other words, a
G^{\prime}
-equivariant linear map from the restriction
\Pi\big\rvert_{G^{\prime}}
to the representation
\pi
.

Since in a general setting the restriction
\Pi\big\rvert_{G^{\prime}}
does not decompose as a direct sum of irreducible representations of
G^{\prime}
, the study of the space of SBOs
\text{{Hom}}_{G^{\prime}}(\Pi\big\rvert_{G^{\prime}},\pi)
can be thought of as a starting point to understand branching laws in a general situation.

In the present paper, we focus on the problem of constructing concrete SBOs, which corresponds to Stage C in the ABC program of T. Kobayashi. An example of these operators are the classical Rankin–Cohen bidifferential operators, which are symmetry breaking operators for the tensor product of two holomorphic discrete series representations of
SL(2,{\mathbb{R}})
(see
[
5
,
26
,
37
]
).

Another example is given by the conformally covariant differential operators that A. Juhl constructed in the frame of conformal geometry (
[
15
]
), which correspond to symmetry breaking operators for spherical principal series representations of the connected Lorentz group
SO_{0}(1,n+1)
.

The problem of constructing and classifying all SBOs given a concrete pair
(G,G^{\prime})
is a challenging one. It was first fully solved in 2018 for the pair
(G,G^{\prime})=(O(n+1,1),O(n,1))
in the case of differential forms. Concretely, all SBOs that can be written as differential operators—referred to as
differential symmetry breaking operators
(DSBOs)—were constructed and classified in
[
20
]
, while the non-local ones were addressed in
[
27
,
28
]
.

The family of DSBOs constitutes a proper and significant subfamily within the set of all SBOs. In particular, there exists a duality between the space of DSBOs and the space of certain homomorphisms between generalized Verma modules (see
[
25
, Thm. 2.9]
), highlighting the fact that the construction and classification of DSBOs is also algebraically meaningful. Moreover, in some special cases, the sets of SBOs and DSBOs coincide—a phenomenon known as
localness theorem
(cf.
[
25
, Thm. 5.3]
,
[
28
, Thm. 3.6]
)—thus reducing the problem of constructing SBOs to that of constructing only the differential ones. For these reasons, among others, substantial progress has been made in recent years toward the construction and classification of DSBOs.

A major turning point in this line of research came in 2013, when T. Kobayashi introduced a general method—known as the
F-method
—to construct and classify DSBOs for principal series representations (see
[
16
,
17
]
). This approach has proven to be remarkably powerful and has been successfully applied in a variety of settings in recent years (see, for instance
[
20
,
21
,
23
,
25
,
26
,
29
,
30
,
32
,
35
,
36
]
).

Another fruitful technique to construct DSBOs is the
source operator method
, developed by J.-L. Clerc and his collaborators (see
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
). Recently, this method has been applied to the pair
(GL(n+1,{\mathbb{R}}),GL(n,{\mathbb{R}}))
, yielding new examples of DSBOs (see
[
7
]
).

Besides the SBOs that can be written as differential operators, and after the work of T. Kobayashi and B. Speh (
[
27
,
28
]
), a lot of progress has been made in the construction of SBOs for several pairs, such as
(GL(n+1,{\mathbb{R}}),GL(n,{\mathbb{R}}))
,
(O(p+1,q+1),O(p,q+1))
or
(U(1,n+1;\mathbb{F}),U(1,m+1;\mathbb{F})\times U(n-m;\mathbb{F}))
, among others (see, for instance
[
6
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
22
]
).

Following this line of study of symmetry breaking operators, we turn our attention to the connected pair
(G,G^{\prime})=(SO_{0}(n+1,1),SO_{0}(n,1))
and aim to construct and classify all SBOs. For certain principal series representations, this problem is expected to share strong connections with the corresponding problem for the non-connected pair
(O(n+1,1),O(n,1))
, and it is plausible that some of the techniques developed in
[
27
,
28
]
may be adapted to the connected case. However, the problem for general principal series representations remains open, even for
n=3
.

As a first step towards solving this problem for the connected pair, we focus in this paper on the case
n=3
, and aim to construct and classify all SBOs for arbitrary principal series representations. More precisely, we address the following problem:

Problem 1.1
.

For any
\lambda,\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, characterize the space

\text{{Hom}}_{SO_{0}(3,1)}\left(C^{\infty}(S^{3},\mathcal{V}_{\lambda}^{2N+1}),C^{\infty}(S^{2},\mathcal{L}_{m,\nu})\right)

(1.1)

of symmetry breaking operators

In particular, as a subproblem we have:

Problem 1.2
.

For any
\lambda,\nu\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

, characterize the subspace of (
1.1

Problem A

Give necessary and sufficient conditions on the tuple of parameters

such that the space (
1.2
) of differential symmetry breaking operators

Construct explicitly the generators

We give a complete solution to these two problems for

in Theorems

and

Although this paper primarily focuses on the case

. For

4
Proof of Theorem
3.6
: Solving the System
\Xi(\lambda,a,N,m)

In this section we prove Theorem
3.6
, which is the key result to show both Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
. In other words, we solve the system (
3.17
), which is given by the following differential equations:

\begin{array}[]{ll}(A_{j}^{+})&S_{a+m-j}^{\lambda+j-1}f_{j}-2(N-j)\frac{d}{dt}f_{j+1}=0.\\[6.0pt]
(A_{j}^{-})&S_{a-m-j}^{\lambda+j-1}f_{-j}+2(N-j)\frac{d}{dt}f_{-j-1}=0.\\[6.0pt]
(B_{j}^{+})&2(-m(\lambda+a-1)+j(\lambda-1+\vartheta_{t}))f_{j}+(N-j)\frac{d}{dt}f_{j+1}+(N+j)\frac{d}{dt}f_{j-1}=0.\\[6.0pt]
(B_{j}^{-})&2(m(\lambda+a-1)+j(\lambda-1+\vartheta_{t}))f_{-j}-(N+j)\frac{d}{dt}f_{-j+1}-(N-j)\frac{d}{dt}f_{-j-1}=0.\end{array}

(4.1)

Here,
j\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
in
(A_{j}^{\pm})
while
j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
in
(B_{j}^{\pm})
, and the functions
f_{\pm j}
belong to the following space (see (
3.9
)):

f_{\pm j}\in\text{{Pol}}_{a-m\pm j}[t]_{\text{{{even}}}}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}[t]\kern 4.62497pt(j=0,1,\ldots,N).

(4.2)

Even though this system is formed by linear ordinary differential equations of order at most 2 with polynomial solutions, it is an overdetermined system, since there are
2(2N+1)
equations and
2N+1
functions. This makes it difficult to solve, specially since there is not a clear relation between equations of type A and B.

Below we solve the system completely and show that it admits a nontrivial solution (unique up to scalar multiple) only for certain values of
\lambda
. More precisely, we prove that the system
\Xi(\lambda,a,N,m)
admits a non-zero solution
(f_{-N},\ldots,f_{N})
if and only if
\lambda\in\Lambda(N,a)
, and that in this case, the solution is unique up to a constant multiple, and is explicitly given by:

\displaystyle f_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=i^{-j}(-1)^{\nu-1}\sum_{r=0}^{N-j}(-1)^{r}A(N-j,r)\widetilde{C}_{a-m+j+2(1-N-\nu+r)}^{\lambda+N-1-r}(it)

\displaystyle(j=1,\ldots,N),

\displaystyle f_{-j}(t)

\displaystyle=i^{-j}\sum_{r=0}^{N-j}(-1)^{r}B(N+j,r)\widetilde{C}_{a-m+j-2N+2r}^{\lambda+N-1-r}(it)

\displaystyle(j=0,1,\ldots,N).

This is nothing but a restatement of (
3.11
) where
(f_{-N},\ldots,f_{N})=(g_{m+N},\ldots,g_{m-N})
.

We solve the system following a strategy divided in three phases:

Phase 1
:

Solve equations
(A_{N}^{\pm})
and obtain
f_{\pm N}
up to two constants, say
q_{N}^{\pm}\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

Phase 2
:

Inductively, for
j=0,\dots,N-1
, obtain
f_{\pm j}
by solving
(B_{j}^{\pm})
. After that, check that these
f_{\pm j}
satisfy equations
(A_{j}^{\pm})
. For
f_{0}
we obtain two different expressions (one coming from the
+
side and another coming from the
-
side).

Phase 3
:

Check when the two expressions for
f_{0}
obtained in Phase 2 coincide. Here, we obtain a compatibility condition for the constants
q_{N}^{\pm}
and for the parameter
\lambda
.

We carry out this three-phase strategy in the following and give a proof of Theorem
3.6
at the end in Section
4.4
.

After completing Phases 1 and 2, we will have that the space of solutions
\Xi(\lambda,a,N,m)
has dimension at most two, since the polynomials
f_{\pm j}
depend on two constants
q_{N}^{\pm}
that a priori can take any value. In Phase 3 we will deduce that this space has dimension at most one, since we obtain a compatibility condition (i.e., a linear relation) between
q_{N}^{+}
and
q_{N}^{-}
.

The three-phase strategy explained above was also used to solve the system (
4.1
) when
m=N
(see
[
36
]
). However, when
m>N
, the situation changes considerably, and even if the same idea works, the procedure is more complicated; specially Phase 3. One aspect that illustrates the fundamental difference between the cases
m=N
and
m>N
, is that while in the former case the system (
4.1
) has non-zero solution for any
(\lambda,\nu)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
satisfying
\nu-\lambda\in{\mathbb{N}}
, when
m>N
non-zero solutions do not exist if
\lambda\notin{\mathbb{Z}}
(see Proposition
4.1
below).

The proofs in our three-phase approach are not straightforward and use several technical results, including properties of renormalized Gegenbauer polynomials, imaginary Gegenbauer operators and hypergeometric functions, which are listed in the Appendix (Section
8
). Let us start with Phase 1.

4.1
Phase 1: Obtaining
f_{\pm N}
up to constant–Necessary condition on
\lambda

Since the system (
4.1
) does not have non-zero solutions for

(see Proposition
3.3
), from now on we suppose that

.
Recall that we are also under the assumption

, so that

.

The first phase is quite simple and straightforward. By taking a look at (

\displaystyle(A_{N}^{+})\kern 4.62497ptS_{a+m-N}^{\lambda+N-1}f_{N}=0,

\displaystyle(A_{N}^{-})\kern 4.62497ptS_{a-m-N}^{\lambda+N-1}f_{-N}=0.

\displaystyle f_{N}(t)=q_{N}^{+}\widetilde{C}_{a+m-N}^{\lambda+N-1}(it),

\displaystyle f_{-N}(t)=q_{N}^{-}\widetilde{C}_{a-m-N}^{\lambda+N-1}(it),

\displaystyle 0

Remark 4.3

Lemma 4.16
.

Suppose
m-N\leq a<m
. Then, a non-zero solution of the system
(f_{-N},\ldots,f_{N})
must satisfy
\lambda\in\Lambda(N,a)
.

Proof.

First, observe that by (
4.2
) we have

\displaystyle f_{-j}\equiv 0\text{ for all }j=0,\ldots,N,\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994ptf_{j}\equiv 0\text{ for all }j=0,\ldots,m-a-1.

Thus, a solution has the form
(0,\ldots,0,f_{m-a},\dots,f_{N})
. By Proposition
4.11
, this solution must satisfy (
4.15
). Thus, by (
4.2
), the terms of degree greater than
a-m+j
appearing in the expression (
4.15
) of
f_{j}
must vanish. In particular, the term
t^{a-m+j+2}
must be zero. A direct computation by using the definition of the Gegenbauer polynomial shows that this term is equal to

\displaystyle\kern 4.62497pt\frac{\Gamma(N+j+1)}{\Gamma(N+1)}\frac{(-1)^{m-N-1}2^{j}(2i)^{a-m+2}}{(a-m+j+2)!(m-N-1)!}\frac{\Gamma\left(\lambda+a+j\right)}{\Gamma\left(\lambda+\left[\frac{a+m-1}{2}\right]\right)}

\displaystyle\times(N+j+1)_{N-j}\sum_{r=0}^{N-j}\frac{(j-N)_{r}(\lambda+a-N-1)_{r}}{(-2N)_{r}r!}.

If we divide by the non-zero constants, and apply the Chu–Vandermonde identity (
8.3
) in the sum part, this expression amounts to

\frac{\Gamma\left(\lambda+a+j\right)}{\Gamma\left(\lambda+\left[\frac{a+m-1}{2}\right]\right)}\frac{\Gamma\left(\lambda+a+N\right)}{\Gamma\left(\lambda+a+j\right)}=\frac{\Gamma\left(\lambda+a+N\right)}{\Gamma\left(\lambda+\left[\frac{a+m-1}{2}\right]\right)}.

Therefore, the solution
(0,\ldots,0,f_{m-a},\dots,f_{N})
must satisfy

\frac{\Gamma\left(\lambda+a+N\right)}{\Gamma\left(\lambda+\left[\frac{a+m-1}{2}\right]\right)}=0.

(4.40)

On the other hand, we must have

\prod_{s=m-a+1}^{N}\gamma(\lambda,a+m+N-2-s)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\lambda+m-1\right)}{\Gamma\left(\lambda+\left[\frac{a+m-1}{2}\right]\right)}\neq 0,

otherwise we would have a zero solution, by Lemma
4.10
. Hence, by dividing by this gamma factor, (
4.40
) amounts to

\frac{\Gamma\left(\lambda+a+N\right)}{\Gamma\left(\lambda+m-1\right)}=0,

which is clearly equivalent to
\lambda\in\Lambda(N,a)=\{N+1-a-q:q=N-a+m,\ldots,2N\}
.

Appendix: Hypergeometric Series and Gegenbauer Polynomials

In this section we define and give some properties of hypergeometric series
{}_{p}F_{q}
and renormalized Gegenbauer polynomials
\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}
.

8.1
Hypergeometric Series

Here, we recall the definition and some properties of the generalized hypergeometric function
{}_{p}F_{q}
. In particular, we give several useful identities of the functions
{}_{2}F_{1}
and
{}_{3}F_{2}
.

Given
p,q\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{p},b_{1},\ldots,b_{q}\in{\mathbb{C}}
, we define the following series

{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\ldots,a_{p}\\
b_{1},\ldots,b_{q}\end{matrix};z\right):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1})_{n}\cdots(a_{p})_{n}}{(b_{1})_{n}\cdots(b_{q})_{n}}\frac{z^{n}}{n!},

(8.1)

where
(x)_{n}
denotes the Pochhammer rising factorial:

(x)_{n}:=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ if }n=0,\\
x(x+1)\cdots(x+n-1),&\text{ if n }\geq 1.\end{cases}

(8.2)

Here,
b_{j}
are not zero nor negative integers, as that would make the denominator zero. The series above is known as the hypergeometric series, and its radius of convergence is known. For instance, if any
a_{j}
is a non-positive integer, the series has a finite number of terms, and therefore it converges. In the general case when
a_{j}
are not non-positive integers, by using the ratio test one can show that it converges absolutely for any
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
if
p<q+1
, for
|z|<1
if
p=q+1
, and that it diverges if
p>q+1
(cf.
[
1
, Thm. 2.1.1]
). When this series has a non-zero radius of convergence, it defines an analytic function which is often called the
generalized
hypergeometric function. When
p=q+1=2
, this function is known as the
ordinary
(or
Gaussian
) hypergeometric function.

One of the most interesting and studied cases for
p=q+1
is when
|z|=1
, since the ratio test does not give a general criterion of convergence. It can be proved, however, that the hypergeometric series
{}_{q+1}F_{q}
converges absolutely for
|z|=1
if
\text{{Re}}(\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{q+1}a_{j})>0
, conditionally for
z=e^{it}\neq 1
if
-1<\text{{Re}}(\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{q+1}a_{j})\leq 0
and diverges if
\text{{Re}}(\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{q+1}a_{j})\leq-1
. Also, several formulas are known for
z=1
, as is the Gauss formula for
{}_{2}F_{1}
:

Lemma 8.1

(
[
13
, Eq. (48)]
)
.

For
\text{{Re}}(c-b-a)>0
, the following holds.

{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}a,b\\
c\end{matrix}\;;1\right)=\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(c-a-b)}{\Gamma(c-a)\Gamma(c-b)}.

A straightforward corollary is the following one, due to Chu and Vandermonde.

Lemma 8.2

(
[
1
, Cor. 2.2.3]
)
.

For any
a,b\in{\mathbb{C}}
and any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}-n,a\\
c\end{matrix}\;;1\right)=\frac{(c-a)_{n}}{(c)_{n}}.

(8.3)

Similar identities hold for greater
(p,q)
. One example is the Pfaff–Saalschütz identity for the function
{}_{3}F_{2}

Lemma 8.3

(
[

and any

{}_{3}F_{2}\left(\begin{matrix}-n,a,b\\
c,1+a+b-c-n\end{matrix}\;;1\right)=\frac{(c-a)_{n}(c-b)_{n}}{(c)_{n}(c-a-b)_{n}}.

(8.4)

Some other examples are Dixon’s identity (see
[
1
, Eq. (2.2.11)]
), Thomae’s identity (see
[
1
, Cor. 3.3.6]
), or Sheppard’s identity (see
[

, Cor. 3.3.4]
) for
{}_{3}F_{2}
, or Dougall’s identities for

and

(see

We end this subsection by stating two useful transformation identities for

Lemma 8.4

(

d,e\end{matrix}\;;1\right)=\frac{\Gamma(d)\Gamma(d-a-b)}{\Gamma(d-a)\Gamma(d-b)}{}_{3}F_{2}\left(\begin{matrix}a,d-b,1-n\\

(8.5)

8.2
Renormalized Gegenbauer polynomials

For any
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
and any
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the Gegenbauer polynomial (also known as ultraspherical polynomial), named after the Austrian mathematician Leopold H. Gegenbauer (
[
14
]
) is defined as follows (cf.
[
1
, Sec. 6.4]
,
[
8
, Sec. 3.15.1]

\displaystyle C_{\ell}^{\mu}(z)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=0}^{[\frac{\ell}{2}]}(-1)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(\ell+\mu-k)}{\Gamma(\mu)k!(\ell-2k)!}(2z)^{\ell-2k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(\ell+2\mu)}{\Gamma(2\mu)\Gamma(\ell+1)}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}-\ell,\ell+2\mu\\
\mu+\frac{1}{2}\end{matrix}\;;\frac{1-z}{2}\right).

This polynomial satisfies the Gegenbauer differential equation

G_{\ell}^{\mu}f(z)=0,

where
G_{\ell}^{\mu}
is the Gegenbauer differential operator:

G_{\ell}^{\mu}:=(1-z^{2})\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}-(2\mu+1)z\frac{d}{dz}+\ell(\ell+2\mu).

Observe that
C_{\ell}^{\mu}
vanishes when
\mu=0,-1,-2,\cdots,-[\frac{\ell-1}{2}]
. In order to avoid this, we renormalize the Gegenbauer polynomial as follows:

\widetilde{C}^{\mu}_{\ell}(z):=\frac{\Gamma(\mu)}{\Gamma\left(\mu+[\frac{\ell+1}{2}]\right)}C^{\mu}_{\ell}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{[\frac{\ell}{2}]}\frac{\Gamma(\mu+\ell-k)}{\Gamma\left(\mu+[\frac{\ell+1}{2}]\right)}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!(\ell-2k)!}(2z)^{\ell-2k}.

(8.7)

By doing this, we obtain that
\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}(z)
is a non-zero polynomial for any
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
and any
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
. This renormalization of
C_{\ell}^{\mu}
is the same as the one given in
[
20
,
26
,
35
,
36
]
. We write below the first six Gegenbauer polynomials.

•

\widetilde{C}^{\mu}_{0}(z)=1
.

•

\widetilde{C}^{\mu}_{1}(z)=2z
.

•

\widetilde{C}^{\mu}_{2}(z)=2(\mu+1)z^{2}-1
.

•

\widetilde{C}^{\mu}_{3}(z)=\frac{4}{3}(\mu+2)z^{3}-2z
.

•

\widetilde{C}^{\mu}_{4}(z)=\frac{2}{3}(\mu+2)(\mu+3)z^{4}-2(\mu+2)z^{2}+\frac{1}{2}
.

•

\widetilde{C}^{\mu}_{5}(z)=\frac{4}{15}(\mu+3)(\mu+4)z^{5}-\frac{4}{3}(\mu+3)z^{3}+z
.

Observe that from the definition, the degree of each term of
\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}(z)
has the same parity as
\ell
, and the number of terms is at most
\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]+1
. Depending on the value of
\mu
, some terms may vanish, but the term of degree
\ell-2\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]\in\{0,1\}
(i.e., the first term), is non-zero for any
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
By a direct computation one can show that this term is given by:

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]}}{\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]!}(2z)^{\ell-2\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]}.

Note that if
\mu+\left[\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right]=0
, then
\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}(z)
is constant for any
\ell\geq 0
. For
\ell<0
we just define the renormalized Gegenbauer polynomial as
\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}\equiv 0
.

The next result shows that the solutions of the Gegenbauer differential equation on
\text{{Pol}}_{\ell}[z]_{\text{{{even}}}}
are precisely the renormalized Gegenbauer polynomials, where

\text{{Pol}}_{\ell}[z]_{\text{even}}=\text{{span}}_{\mathbb{C}}\{z^{\ell-2b}:b=0,1,\ldots,\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]\}.

Theorem 8.6

(
[
26
, Thm 11.4]
)
.

For any
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
and any
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the following holds.

\{f(z)\in\text{{Pol}}_{\ell}[z]_{\text{{{even}}}}:G_{\ell}^{\mu}f(z)=0\}={\mathbb{C}}\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}(z).

In
{\mathbb{C}}[z]\setminus\text{{Pol}}_{\ell}[z]_{\text{{{even}}}}
, the Gegenbauer differential equation may have other solutions depending on the parameters
\mu,\ell
. For a concrete statement of this fact see
[
26
, Thm. 11.4]
.

For any
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
and any
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the
imaginary
Gegenbauer differential operator
S_{\ell}^{\mu}
is defined as follows (cf.
[
20
, (4.7)]
):

S_{\ell}^{\mu}=-\left((1+t^{2})\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}+(1+2\mu)t\frac{d}{dt}-\ell(\ell+2\mu)\right).

(8.8)

This operator and
G_{\ell}^{\mu}
are in the following natural relation.

Lemma 8.7

(
[
20
, Lem. 14.2]
)
.

Let
f(z)
be a polynomial in the variable
z
and define
g(t)=f(z)
, where
z=it
. Then, the following identity holds:

\left(S_{\ell}^{\mu}g\right)(t)=\left(G_{\ell}^{\mu}f\right)(z).

Next, we give some condition on the parameter
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
for which the “higher terms” of
\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}
vanish. At the end, we recall a “degree decay” property that happens when special parameters are considered.

For any
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
and any
k=0,1,\ldots,\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]
we set

M_{\ell}^{k}:=\left\{-\left[\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right]-d:d=0,1,\ldots,\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]-k-1\right\}.

(8.9)

If
\ell=0,1
or if
k=\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]
we set
M_{\ell}^{k}=\emptyset
. In any other case we have that
M_{\ell}^{k}\neq\emptyset
from the expression above.

Lemma 8.8
.

Let
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then, the term
z^{\ell-2k}\kern 4.62497pt(0\leq k<\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right])
in
\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}(z)
vanishes if and only if
\mu\in M_{\ell}^{k}
. In particular, if
\mu\notin{\mathbb{Z}}
then
\deg(\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}(z))=\ell
.

Proof.

The proof is straightforward from the definition of
\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}
. Note that the term of degree
\ell-2\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]\in\{0,1\}
is always non-zero, so the result is trivially true also for
k=\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]
since we defined
M_{\ell}^{\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]}=\emptyset
.

Suppose
0\leq k<\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]
. From (
8.7
) it is clear that the term
z^{\ell-2k}
vanishes if and only if

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma\left(\mu+\ell-k\right)}{\Gamma\left(\mu+\left[\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right]\right)}

\displaystyle=\left(\mu+\left[\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right]\right)\cdot\left(\mu+\left[\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right]+1\right)\cdots\left(\mu+\left[\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right]+\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]-k-1\right)

\displaystyle=0,

that is, if and only if
\mu\in M_{\ell}^{k}
. The lemma is now proved.
∎

Remark 8.9
.

From the definition of
M_{\ell}^{k}
note that for any
k=0,1,\ldots,\left[\frac{\ell}{2}\right]-1
the following hold:

(1)

M_{\ell}^{k+1}\subsetneq M_{\ell}^{k}
.

(2)

M_{\ell}^{k}\setminus M_{\ell}^{k+1}=\{\ell+k+1\}
.

In particular, by Lemma
8.8
, if
\mu\in M_{\ell}^{k}
, all the terms
z^{\ell-2k},z^{\ell-2(k-1)},\ldots,z^{\ell}
in
\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}(z)
vanish.

Lemma 8.10

(
[
23
, Lem. 4.12]
)
.

For
\ell,b\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
2b\geq\ell
the following holds:

C_{\ell}^{-b}(z)=C_{2b-\ell}^{-b}(z),

or equivalently

\frac{\Gamma\left(-b+\left[\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right]\right)}{\Gamma\left(-b+\left[\frac{2b-\ell+1}{2}\right]\right)}\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{-b}(z)=\widetilde{C}_{2b-\ell}^{-b}(z).

(8.10)

8.3
Algebraic properties of renormalized Gegenbauer polynomials and imaginary Gegenbauer operators

In this section we collect some useful properties of imaginary Gegenbauer operators and renormalized Gegenbauer polynomials. We start by showing some algebraic identities and follow by recalling one recurrence relation among Gegenbauer polynomials.

For
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we define the following gamma factor:

\gamma(\mu,\ell):=\displaystyle{\frac{\Gamma(\mu+\left[\frac{\ell+2}{2}\right])}{\Gamma(\mu+\left[\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right])}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\ell\text{ is odd},\\
\mu+\frac{\ell}{2},&\text{if }\ell\text{ is even}.\end{cases}}

(8.11)

Note that this definition can be extended to
\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. For
\ell<0
it suffices to take
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\ell+2k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and define
\gamma(\mu,\ell):=\gamma(\mu-k,\ell+2k)
.

Another remarkable property of
\gamma(\mu,\ell)
is that it satisfies the following identity, which is straightforward from the definition:

\gamma(\mu,\ell)\gamma(\mu,\ell+1)=\mu+\left[\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right].

(8.12)

During the rest of the section, we denote by
\vartheta_{t}
the one-dimensional Euler operator
t\frac{d}{dt}
.

Lemma 8.11

(
[
20
, Lem. 14.4]
)
.

For any
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
and any
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the following identities hold:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}(it)

\displaystyle=2i\gamma(\mu,\ell)\widetilde{C}_{\ell-1}^{\mu+1}(it),

(8.13)

\displaystyle\left(\vartheta_{t}-\ell\right)\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}(it)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{C}_{\ell-2}^{\mu+1}(it).

(8.14)

Lemma 8.12
.

For any
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
and any
\ell,d\in{\mathbb{N}}
the following recursive relation holds:

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma\left(\mu+\left[\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right]\right)}{\Gamma\left(\mu-d+\left[\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right]\right)}\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}(it)=\sum_{s=0}^{d}\begin{pmatrix}d\\
s\end{pmatrix}\frac{\Gamma\left(\mu+\ell+s-d\right)}{\Gamma\left(\mu+\ell-d\right)}\widetilde{C}_{\ell+2(s-d)}^{\mu-s}(it).

(8.15)

Proof.

The identity above can be proved easily by induction on
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. If
d=0
, the identity is trivial since both sides amount to
\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}(it)
. If
d=1
, then (
8.15
) amounts to

\displaystyle(\mu+\ell-1)\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu-1}(it)+\widetilde{C}_{\ell-2}^{\mu}(it)=\left(\mu+\left[\frac{\ell-1}{2}\right]\right)\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}(it),

(8.16)

which coincides with the three-term relation
[
20
, Eq. (14.15)]
.
Now suppose that (
8.15
) is true for some
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then, for
d+1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma\left(\mu+\left[\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right]\right)}{\Gamma\left(\mu-d-1+\left[\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right]\right)}\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}(it)

\displaystyle=\left(\mu-d-1+\left[\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right]\right)\frac{\Gamma\left(\mu+\left[\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right]\right)}{\Gamma\left(\mu-d+\left[\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right]\right)}\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu}(it)

\displaystyle=\left(\mu-d-1+\left[\frac{\ell+1}{2}\right]\right)\sum_{s=0}^{d}\begin{pmatrix}d\\
s\end{pmatrix}\frac{\Gamma\left(\mu+\ell+s-d\right)}{\Gamma\left(\mu+\ell-d\right)}\widetilde{C}_{\ell+2(s-d)}^{\mu-s}(it)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{d}\begin{pmatrix}d\\
s\end{pmatrix}\frac{\Gamma\left(\mu+\ell+s-d\right)}{\Gamma\left(\mu+\ell-d\right)}(\mu+\ell+s-2d-1)\widetilde{C}_{\ell+2(s-d)}^{\mu-s-1}(it)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\sum_{s=0}^{d}\begin{pmatrix}d\\
s\end{pmatrix}\frac{\Gamma\left(\mu+\ell+s-d\right)}{\Gamma\left(\mu+\ell-d\right)}\widetilde{C}_{\ell+2(s-d-1)}^{\mu-s}(it),

where in the second equality we used the induction hypothesis, and in the third we applied (
8.16
). Then, by straightforward computations, the right-hand side amounts to

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{\ell-2(d+1)}^{\mu}(it)+\sum_{s=1}^{d}\Bigg(\begin{pmatrix}d\\
s-1\end{pmatrix}\frac{\Gamma\left(\mu+\ell+s-1-d\right)}{\Gamma\left(\mu+\ell-d\right)}(\mu+\ell+s-2d-2)+

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}d\\
s\end{pmatrix}\frac{\Gamma\left(\mu+\ell+s-d\right)}{\Gamma\left(\mu+\ell-d\right)}\Bigg)\widetilde{C}_{\ell+2(s-d-1)}^{\mu-s}(it)+\frac{\Gamma\left(\mu+\ell\right)}{\Gamma\left(\mu+\ell-d\right)}(\mu+\ell-d-1)\widetilde{C}_{\ell}^{\mu-d-1}(it)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{d+1}\begin{pmatrix}d+1\\
s\end{pmatrix}\frac{\Gamma\left(\mu+\ell+s-d-1\right)}{\Gamma\left(\mu+\ell-d-1\right)}\widetilde{C}_{\ell+2(s-d-1)}^{\mu-s}(it).

Hence, (
8.15
) holds also for
d+1
.
∎

In (
8.8
) we have defined the imaginary Gegenbauer operator
S_{\ell}^{\mu}
. In the following lemma we give some algebraic properties of this operator.

Lemma 8.13
.

For any
\mu\in{\mathbb{C}}
and any
\ell,d\in{\mathbb{N}}
the following identities hold:

\displaystyle S_{\ell}^{\mu}-S_{\ell}^{\mu+d}

\displaystyle=2d(\vartheta_{t}-\ell),

(8.17)

\displaystyle S_{\ell}^{\mu}-S_{\ell-2d}^{\mu+d}

\displaystyle=2d(\vartheta_{t}+2\mu+\ell).

(8.18)

Proof.

The proof is straightforward from the definition of
S_{\ell}^{\mu}
.
∎

We remark that identities (
8.17
) and (
8.18
) above are a simple generalization of

[
20
, Eq. (14.9)]
and
[
20
, Eq. (14.12)]
respectively.
