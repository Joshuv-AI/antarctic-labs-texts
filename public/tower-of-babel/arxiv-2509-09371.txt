Representation-Aware Distributionally Robust Optimization: A Knowledge Transfer Framework
Zitao Wang, Nian Si, Molei Liu

Abstract
We propose RE presentation- A ware D istributionally Robust Estimation (READ), a novel framework for Wasserstein distributionally robust learning that accounts for predictive representations when guarding against distributional shifts. Unlike classical approaches that treat all feature perturbations equally, READ embeds a multidimensional alignment parameter into the transport cost, allowing the model to differentially discourage perturbations along directions associated with informative representations. This yields robustness to feature variation while preserving invariant structure. Our first contribution is a theoretical foundation: we show that seminorm regularizations for linear regression and binary classification arise as Wasserstein distributionally robust objectives, thereby providing tractable reformulations of READ and unifying a broad class of regularized estimators under the DRO lens. Second, we adopt a principled procedure for selecting the Wasserstein radius using the techniques of robust Wasserstein profile inference. This further enables the construction of valid, representation-aware confidence regions for model parameters with distinct geometric features. Finally, we analyze the geometry of READ estimators as the alignment parameters vary and propose an optimization algorithm to estimate the projection of the global optimum onto this solution surface. This procedure selects among equally robust estimators while optimally constructing a representation structure. We conclude by demonstrating the effectiveness of our framework through extensive simulations and a real-world study, providing a powerful robust estimation grounded in learning representation. Keywords: Wasserstein distributionally robust optimization; Optimal hyperparameter selection; Shrinkage-based transfer learning; Representation learning.



1
Introduction

Driven by increasingly complex experiments and the resulting high-dimensional datasets, traditional
empirical risk minimization
(ERM) methods may suffer from suboptimal estimation. To address the uncertainty inherent in ERM-based stochastic programs, robust optimization techniques have been introduced. Among these,
distributionally robust optimization
(DRO) has emerged as a powerful framework. DRO accounts for uncertainty by acknowledging that the empirical distribution may inadequately represent the true data-generating distribution, or that the underlying distribution may shift (i.e.,
concept drift
) in future applications. A particularly prominent variant is
Wasserstein
-DRO (WDRO), which has gained significant attention due to its strong theoretical properties. In WDRO, uncertainty is modeled via a Wasserstein ball centered at the empirical distribution, and the learner solves for a Nash equilibrium against an adversary that perturbs the data distribution within this ball to maximize the expected loss. The formulation benefits from the linear programming structure of optimal transport problems, which enables tractable dual formulations and efficient computations
(
Blanchet and Murthy, 2019
)
.

While WDRO provides a principled safeguard against distributional uncertainty, its classical formulation treats all perturbations of the covariates equally by adopting a cost function of the form

c(x,u)=\|x-u\|_{q}^{k},

for some
q\geq 1
and
k\geq 1
, which does not adequately capture the geometry of the data. In many applications, however, predictive signals are concentrated in a low-dimensional representation
\Theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times M}
that is more stable across environments than the raw features
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
themselves. When an approximation
\Theta
to such a representation
\Theta^{*}
is available, the extracted features

\Theta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}X\in\mathbb{R}^{M},

typically serve as more robust and reliable predictors of
Y
, and the information they carry should be protected against adversarial perturbations under the Wasserstein framework.

This motivation leads naturally to a modification of the base cost that explicitly encodes the representation structure. We consider

c_{q,\Lambda}(x,u)=\|x-u\|_{q}^{2}+\|\Theta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}(x-u)\|_{\Lambda}^{2},

where
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{M})
denotes a set of alignment hyperparameters and
\|x\|_{\Lambda}^{2}=x^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\Lambda x
. The entries of
\Lambda
can be tuned to reflect the varying predictive strength of the components of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
. This formulation reflects the belief that predictive representations are invariant across environments, whereas spurious components are more likely to drift under distributional shift.

1.1
Related Works

Wasserstein DRO.

Wasserstein DRO has recently garnered significant attention due to its tractability
(
Mohajerin Esfahani and Kuhn, 2018
;
Blanchet and Murthy, 2019
;
Gao and Kleywegt, 2023
)
and generalizability
(
Blanchet et al., 2019
;
Gao et al., 2022
)
. Notably,
(
Blanchet et al., 2019
;
Chu et al., 2022
;
Gao et al., 2022
)
demonstrated that Wasserstein DRO with mean square loss is equivalent to the square root lasso
Belloni et al., 2011
and other norm regularizations. Similarly,
(
Shafieezadeh-Abadeh et al., 2015
;
Shafieezadeh-Abadeh et al., 2019
;
Blanchet et al., 2019
;
Gao et al., 2022
)
established that Wasserstein DRO with logistic loss and hinge loss corresponds to their regularized counterparts. Moreover, the statistical properties of the WDRO estimator have also been investigated in
Blanchet et al., 2021
;
Blanchet et al., 2022
;
Gao, 2023
.

Distributionally Robust Transfer Learning.

The first use of external knowledge in Wasserstein DRO to facilitate transfer learning was introduced by
Wang et al., 2025
. Their
strong-transferring
formulation can be viewed as a special case of our framework, obtained by setting all alignment hyperparameters to be identically infinite, thereby imposing hard constraints in the cost function. And they also propose a
weak-transferring
framework, but it is limited to the 2-norm and single-source representation.
Consequently, their estimators lack adaptivity to the informativeness of prior knowledge, whereas our framework enables adaptive weighting and provides a principled method for selecting the uncertainty set radius and the alignment parameter to flexibly leverage source representations. Related predecessors in the broader DRO literature include
Xiong et al., 2023
and
Zhan et al., 2024
, which construct ambiguity sets over convex mixtures of external distributions under the group DRO framework, as well as
Rychener et al., 2024
, who study Wasserstein DRO with smaller ambiguity sets by intersecting Wasserstein ball around multiple heterogeneous data sources, and
Gui et al., 2024
, who analyze distributionally robust risk evaluation under isotonic constraints. However, existing works in this track do not provide valid inference guarantees for the resulting estimators due to the non-normality caused by model selection or DRO.

Learning Across Tasks and Representations.

A central challenge in modern statistical learning is how to improve prediction accuracy in a target setting by leveraging information from related tasks or learned representations. This general paradigm includes transfer learning, representation learning, and multitask learning, each of which addresses different facets of basically the same problem: how to share statistical strength across multiple heterogeneous knowledge sources while remaining robust to non-informative or misleading ones. In transfer learning, methodological advances focus on integrating diverse source datasets to improve generalization for a target population. Contemporary approaches address challenges such as distributional heterogeneity and limited labeled target data. A common assumption is that the target outcome model partially aligns with source models, thereby enabling knowledge transfer. For example, recent frameworks have employed selective parameter reduction to identify transferable sources and applied sparse or ridge shrinkage to leverage their information
(
Bastani, 2020
;
Li et al., 2021
;
Tian and Feng, 2023
)
. Subsequent works have tackled covariate distribution mismatches and semi-supervised settings, improving robustness when labeled target data is scarce
(
Cai et al., 2024
;
He et al., 2024
;
Zhou et al., 2024
)
. Further innovations include geometric or profile-based adaptations, where the target model is represented as a weighted combination of source coefficients
(
Gu et al., 2024
;
Lin et al., 2024
)
. Model averaging has also been successfully applied in transfer learning, as in
(
Hu and Zhang, 2023
;
Zhang et al., 2025
)
. In multitask and representation learning, the focus shifts to learning low-rank or structured representations that capture commonalities across tasks. Such representations can facilitate generalization and improve estimation in high dimensions. For example,
Tian et al., 2025
investigate a meta-learning framework in which low-rank structures are jointly learned across tasks to enable knowledge transfer.

A key distinction of our framework is its scope of distributional robustness and generalization. Many existing methods are designed to improve performance on a specific target distribution, often under an assumed model of the underlying data-generating process. In contrast, READ provides robust generalization guarantees over an entire class of future distributions that share a common representation
\Theta
, while allowing arbitrary distributional shifts in directions orthogonal to
\Theta
. Since it is common in practice for such representations to remain stable across environments, our framework offers stronger and more realistic guarantees against distributional shifts.

1.2
Our Contributions

In the present work, we introduce the
RE
presentation-
A
ware
D
istributionally Robust Estimation
(READ) framework. READ enables flexible transportation costs across different representation components through a multidimensional alignment parameter
\Lambda
embedded in
c_{q,\Lambda}
. This admits the primal-dual form

\inf_{\beta}\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{N}^{*};c_{q,\Lambda})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}(Y-X^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\beta)^{2}=\inf_{\beta}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\beta\|_{2}+\sqrt{\delta}\,\varphi_{p,\Lambda}(\beta),

where
\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{N}^{*};c_{q,\Lambda})
denotes the ambiguity set and
\delta
is its radius. This formulation induces a novel regularizer
\varphi_{p,\Lambda}(\beta)
on the learning objective, whose squared form is

\varphi_{p,\Lambda}^{2}(\beta)=\inf_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\left\{\|\beta-\Theta\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{p}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|^{2}_{\Lambda^{\shortminus 1}}\right\}.

Within this novel WDRO framework, our contributions are fourfold:

1.

Tractable Convex Duality.

We establish that using an extended-norm cost for a WDRO objective, both in linear regression and in binary classification, is equivalent to introducing a seminorm regularizer on the learning objective. In the READ framework, this regularizer shrinks the estimator onto the column linear span of
\Theta
, with alignment parameters
\Lambda
adaptively controlling the strength of the shrinkage along each component, thereby implicitly characterizes the transferability of each component.

2.

Asymptotic Analysis and Theoretical Guarantees.

We construct confidence regions for the global optimum in the presence of representations and provide an explicit asymptotic characterization of our DRO estimators, including the bias term in the optimal convergence rate. These results hold universally for all possible values of the alignment parameters
\Lambda
, and without requiring any assumptions on the data-generating distribution.

3.

Robust Adaptive Hyperparameter Selection.
Building on our asymptotic analysis, we propose novel selection strategies for both the Wasserstein radius
\delta
(in a
\Lambda
-dependent manner) and the alignment parameters
\Lambda
. The
\delta
-selection ensures, with a given confidence level, that the optimal parameter lies within the confidence region, thereby providing uniform
robustness
across all choices of
\Lambda
. For
\Lambda
, the selection procedure approximates the projection of the global optimum onto a compact set of READ estimators indexed by
\Lambda
. This universality enables statistically valid inference even when
\Lambda
is tuned, while allowing
\Lambda
to
adapt
to the informativeness of the components of
\Theta
. Moreover, the resulting estimator enjoys distributional generalization guarantees over an entire class of future environments that share the representation
\Theta^{*}
while allowing
\Theta^{*}
-invariant shifts in the distribution.

4.

Empirical Validation.

We conduct extensive experiments demonstrating that READ consistently improves learning performance with external knowledge, outperforming transfer learning methods based on distance, angle, or low-rank representations. These gains highlight READ’s robustness and adaptability across a wide range of settings, stemming from its ability to navigate a more informative subspace with data-informed geometry.

2
Seminorm Regularizations as WDRO

In this section, we show that, without requiring any distributional assumptions, wide class of Wasserstein distributionally robust estimation problems can be expressed as
seminorm
-regularized learning problem. These include linear regression with root mean squared error, and binary classification with either the cross-entropy loss or the hinge loss. A seminorm has the appealing property that it vanishes on a linear subspace of the parameter space. We begin with a brief review of the WDRO framework for statistical learning.

2.1
Wasserstein Distributionally Robust Optimization

In statistical learning, the data-generating distribution
\mathbb{P}^{*}
of the target-covariate pair
(X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
is generally unknown, and only samples
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
are observed. Standard
empirical risk minimization
(ERM) solves for

\beta_{\rm ERM}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{N}^{*}}[\ell(X,Y;\beta)]\eqqcolon\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\beta}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell(x_{i},y_{i};\beta),

where
\ell(x,y;\beta)
measures the loss for parameter
\beta
. While ERM fits the observed data well, it may perform poorly if the empirical distribution
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{N}
under-represents
\mathbb{P}^{*}
or if distributional shifts occur.
Distributionally robust optimization
(DRO) addresses this by seeking

\beta_{\rm DRO}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\beta}\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}_{\rm amb}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\ell(X,Y;\beta)],

where
\mathcal{P}_{\rm amb}
is an
ambiguity set
of plausible data-generating distributions. In
Wasserstein
-DRO (WDRO),
\mathcal{P}_{\rm amb}
is taken as a Wasserstein ball of radius
\delta>0
around
\mathbb{P}^{*}_{N}
:

\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}^{*}_{N};c)\coloneqq\left\{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})\,\middle|\,\mathcal{D}_{c}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{P}^{*}_{N})\leq\delta\right\},

where
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
denotes the class of distributions supported on
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, and
\mathcal{D}_{c}
is the optimal transport cost with cost function
c:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Given two distributions
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the transport cost
\mathcal{D}_{c}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})
is defined as

\mathcal{D}_{c}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})\coloneqq\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[c(U,V)],

where
\Pi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})
is the set of distributions on the product space
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
with
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
as the respective marginals. We called
\Pi(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})
the set of
transport plans
. Usually we take
c(u,v)\geq 0
and vanishes only when
u=v
. In WDRO, the resulting solution
\beta_{\rm DRO}
is optimized for the worst-case distributional perturbation within this set, adding a robustness layer beyond ERM.

2.2
Strong Duality of Wasserstein DRO

The Wasserstein DRO problem features an inner maximization over a convex set that typically lies outside any finite-dimensional subspace of the space of signed measures, making it appear computationally intractable. However, by exploiting the linear programming structure of optimal transport, strong duality allows the Wasserstein DRO to be reformulated as a simple univariate optimization problem.

Proposition 1

(Strong Duality,
(
Mohajerin Esfahani and Kuhn, 2018
;
Blanchet and Murthy, 2019
;
Gao and Kleywegt, 2023
)
)
.

Let
c:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to[0,\infty]
be a lower semi-continuous cost function satisfying
c\big((x,y),(u,v)\big)=0
whenever
(x,y)=(u,v)
. Then the distributionally robust estimation problem

\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{\mathbb{P}:\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}^{*}_{N};c)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\ell(X,Y;\beta)\right],

is equivalent to,

\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\inf_{\gamma\geq 0}\left\{\gamma\delta+\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\phi_{\gamma}(x_{i},y_{i};\beta)\right\},

where
\phi_{\gamma}(x_{i},y_{i};\beta)
is given by,

\sup_{(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\big\{\ell(u,v;\beta)-\gamma c\big((u,v),(x_{i},y_{i})\big)\big\}.

Example 1

(The Square Root Lasso)
.

(
Blanchet et al., 2019
;
Gao et al., 2022
)
showed that using the squared
q
-norm on the covariates as the transport cost,

c_{q,0}\big((x,y),(u,v)\big)=\|x-u\|_{q}^{2}+\infty\cdot|y-v|,

(1)

equates Wasserstein distributionally robust linear regression with
p
-norm regularization on the RMSE. Specifically, for any
q\in[1,\infty]
,

\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}^{*}_{N};c_{q,0})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[(Y-\beta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}X)^{2}\right]=\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\sqrt{{\rm MSE}_{N}(\beta)}+\sqrt{\delta}\|\beta\|_{p}\right\}^{2},

where
(p,q)
are Hölder conjugates. The pair
(p,q)=(1,\infty)
recovers the Square Root Lasso
(
Belloni et al., 2011
)
. The cost function
c_{q,0}
perturbs only the observed covariates
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, while keeping the targets
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
fixed. Fixing
Y
often leads to more tractable reformulations and reflects the intuition that, conditional on
X
, we trust the mechanism generating
Y
.

2.3
Tractable Reformulation for Costs with Extended Norms

Our first main result generalizes Example
1
from
q
-norm to arbitrary extended norms. Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be an extended norm. We begin by showing that taking the dual norm establishes a one-to-one correspondence between the collection
\mathcal{G}
of extended norms on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the collection
\mathcal{R}
of seminorms on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
V_{g}\coloneqq\dom{g}
, it is straightforward to verify that
V_{g}
is a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Denote by
V_{g}^{\perp}
its orthogonal complement.

Lemma 1
.

The following statements hold:

1.

The dual norm of
g
, defined by
g_{*}(x)=\sup_{y:g(y)\leq 1}y^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}x
, is a seminorm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with null space exactly
V_{g}^{\perp}
.

2.

The dualization operation is an involution on
\mathcal{G}
; that is,
g_{**}(x)=g(x)
.

3.

There is a one-to-one correspondence between
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{R}
.

4.

The convex conjugate of
g^{2}
satisfies

(g^{2})^{*}(x)=\frac{1}{4}\sup_{g(y)\leq 1}(y^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}x)^{2}=\frac{1}{4}g_{*}^{2}(x),

and therefore
(g^{2})^{*}
is quadratically homogeneous with domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

5.

The convex conjugate of
g
is the convex indicator of the unit ball of the dual seminorm:

g^{*}(x)=I_{\{y:g_{*}(y)\leq 1\}}(x).

Combining Lemma
1
with Toland’s duality
(
Toland, 1978
;
Toland, 1979
)
for difference-of-convex optimization, embedded in the reformulation of Proposition
1
, yields the following tractable reformulation of the worst-case risk under Wasserstein distributional robustness.

Proposition 2

(Linear Regression)
.

Let
\ell(X,Y;\beta)=(Y-\beta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}X)^{2}
be the least-squares loss, and suppose the cost function
c:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is given by

c\big((x,y),(u,v)\big)=g^{2}(x-u)+\infty\cdot|y-v|,

where
g
is an arbitrary extended norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then:

\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}^{*}_{N};c)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[(Y-\beta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}X)^{2}\right]=\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\sqrt{{\rm MSE}_{N}(\beta)}+\sqrt{\delta}\,r(\beta)\right\}^{2},

where
r=g_{*}
is the dual seminorm of
g
.

We observe the continued theme that the regularization parameter is entirely determined by the radius
\delta
of the Wasserstein ambiguity set. Conversely, the correspondence in Lemma
2
implies that any seminorm-regularized linear regression is Wasserstein distributionally robust. We now turn to binary classification tasks, where the response variable
Y\in\{-1,1\}
. Again, without any distributional assumptions, we show that regularized classifiers can also be interpreted through the lens of Wasserstein distributional robustness. In this setting, the ambiguity set is constructed using cost functions given by extended norms on the feature space, in contrast to the squared extended norms used in the previous statement.

Proposition 3

(Binary Classification)
.

Suppose the loss function
\ell(X,Y;\beta)
is either the logistic loss
\log{\left(1+e^{-Y\beta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}X}\right)}
or the hinge loss
(1-Y\beta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}X)^{+}
. Let the cost function
c:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be

c\big((x,y),(u,v)\big)=g(x-u)+\infty\cdot|y-v|,

where
g
is an extended norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then:

\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}^{*}_{N};c)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\ell(X,Y;\beta)\right]=\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell(x_{i},y_{i};\beta)+\delta r(\beta)\right\},

where
r=g_{*}
is the dual seminorm of
g
.

3
Representation-Aware Wasserstein DRO

3.1
The Representation-Aware Cost Function

In the generic WDRO problem, the base cost on the feature space
X
is typically specified as

c(x,u)=g^{k}(x-u)=\|x-u\|_{q}^{k},

for some power
k
of a standard Euclidean
q
-norm, thereby imposing a simple, distribution-agnostic geometry. In the context of representation-aware learning, one can exploit available or data-driven information about the underlying distribution to improve such standard cost functions. In particular, we focus on the case where predictive signals are concentrated in a low-dimensional representation that is more stable across environments than the raw features themselves.

A prototypical example arises when a low-rank structure is given or learned through
Representation Learning
. Suppose the covariates
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are generated from latent factors
U\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
via a loading matrix
\Theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times M}
with
M<d
:

\displaystyle X=\Theta^{*}U+\epsilon_{X},\quad\epsilon_{X}\perp U,

(LowRank)

\displaystyle Y=\alpha^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}U+\epsilon_{Y},\quad\epsilon_{Y}\perp U,

\displaystyle\Theta^{*}{{}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}}\Theta^{*}=\mathbb{I}_{M}.

In this setting, with an approximation
\Theta
of
\Theta^{*}
,
U\approx\Theta{{}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}}X
serves as a compact low-dimensional representation of
X
with higher signal-to-noise ratio for predicting
Y
, and we may equivalently write
Y\sim\alpha^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\Theta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}X
. From a representation learning perspective, new environments may preserve the representation map
\Theta
but alter the distribution of
\epsilon_{X}
. Such perturbations induce shifts in the regression coefficients of models of the form
Y\sim X^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\beta
, thereby degrading generalization.

From a
Causal Invariance Learning
perspective, the advantage of working in the representation space is not only its higher signal-to-noise ratio but also its invariance across environments. Recent work on causal invariance learning has formalized this idea, showing that the conditional relationship between outcomes and representations can remain stable even under heterogeneous interventions on the observed covariates
(
Shen et al., 2025
;
Šola et al., 2025
)
. In this view, distribution shifts often occur in directions orthogonal to the causal representation, while the representation itself captures the stable mechanism. Our READ framework operationalizes this principle by constraining Wasserstein perturbations to respect the invariance of
\Theta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}X
, thereby providing robustness against environment-specific shifts while preserving the causal structure.

Alternatively, the representation matrix
\Theta
can arise in two complementary ways. From a
Transfer Learning
perspective,
\Theta
may be constructed from pre-trained coefficients
\{\theta_{m}\}_{m\in[M]}
obtained in prior experiments, thereby providing a linear projection of a low-rank structure hidden within related external environments
\{(x_{m,i},y_{m,i})\}_{i\in[N_{m}]}
. A naive domain adaptation on a new feature-target pair
(x,y)
with a prior coefficient
\theta_{m}
gives the crude prediction
\widehat{y}_{m}=x^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\theta_{m}
, which, while imperfect, is often informative for the true outcome
y
. By contrast, in
Representational Multi-Task Learning
,
\Theta
is learned jointly across several tasks that share a common representation but differ in their outcome models
(
Tian et al., 2025
)
. At the truth level, this can be modeled by assuming a common loading matrix
\Theta
across
M
tasks
\{(x_{m,i},y_{m,i})\}_{i\in[N_{m}]}
, while allowing for task-specific responses

X_{m}=\Theta^{*}U+\epsilon_{X},\qquad Y_{m}=\alpha_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}U+\epsilon_{Y,m}.

In this case, the task-specific coefficients
\{\Theta\alpha_{1},\ldots,\Theta\alpha_{M}\}
assemble into a low-rank matrix that can be leveraged by READ to improve learning. Thus, whether obtained from external sources or jointly across multiple tasks, the linear projection
\Theta
serves as a stable representation that informs robust generalization.

We now proceed from a transfer learning perspective, and denote the prior coefficients by
\Theta=[\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{M}]
, we arrive at the following representation-aware cost function:

c_{q,\Lambda}(x,u)=\|x-u\|_{q}^{2}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\lambda_{m}\big(\theta_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}(x-u)\big)^{2}=\|x-u\|_{q}^{2}+\|\Theta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}(x-u)\|_{\Lambda}^{2}.

Intuitively, this cost function encourages the ambiguity set to consist primarily of covariate distributions that remain invariant under the feature extraction mapping
X\mapsto\Theta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}X
. Each direction
\theta_{m}
in
\Theta
is associated with its own hyperparameter
\lambda_{m}\in[0,\infty]
, so that
\Lambda\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{M}
. We refer to
\Lambda
as the
alignment hyperparameters
, as they quantify the degree of invariance enforced along each representation direction, thereby controlling how strongly the perturbed distribution must align with the linear mapping induced by
\Theta
. Unlike classical WDRO costs, which treat all feature perturbations uniformly, our newly defined
c_{q,\Lambda}(x,u)
enables direction-specific robustness tailored to the predictive structure of the data. Inspired by Lemma
1
and Theorem
2
&
3
, we compute the convex conjugate of this function for the associated regularization.

3.2
The Representation-Aware Regularizer

Note that
c_{q,\Lambda}(x,u)
only depends on
x-u
,
therefore, by a slight abuse of notation, we let
c_{q,\Lambda}(x,u)=c_{q,\Lambda}(x-u)
, with the definition

c_{q,\Lambda}(x)=\|x\|_{q}^{2}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\lambda_{m}(\theta_{m}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}x)^{2}=\|x\|_{q}^{2}+\|\Theta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}x\|_{\Lambda}^{2}.

Since the square root of a quadratically homogeneous, nonnegative, convex function that vanishes only at the origin defines a norm on its (convex) domain,
c_{q,\Lambda}(x)
is the square of an extended norm. Consequently, in the WDRO estimation, the corresponding regularizer is given by the square root of four times the convex conjugate of
c_{q,\Lambda}
by invoking Lemma
1
. We denote this regularizer by
\varphi_{p,\Lambda}(x)
and proceed to compute it explicitly.

Proposition 4
.

Fix
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times M}
and
\Lambda\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{M}
. Define
c_{q,\Lambda}(x)=\|x\|_{q}^{2}+\|\Theta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}x\|_{\Lambda}^{2}
, whose convex conjugate is denoted by
c^{*}_{q,\Lambda}(x)
, then

c_{q,\Lambda}^{*}(x)=\inf_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\big\{\|x-\Theta\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{p}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{\Lambda^{\shortminus 1}}^{2}\big\},

where
p^{\shortminus 1}+q^{\shortminus 1}=1
, with the conventions
0^{\shortminus 1}=\infty
and
\infty^{\shortminus 1}=0
.

We denote the square of the regularizer by
\varphi_{p,\Lambda}^{2}(x)=4c^{*}_{q,\Lambda}(x)
, which admits an intuitive interpretation:
\varphi_{p,\Lambda}^{2}(\beta)
measures the residual length of a
p
-norm regression that projects
\beta
onto the linear subspace spanned by
\Theta
, penalized by a ridge term induced by
\Lambda^{\shortminus 1}
. A large alignment parameter
\lambda_{m}
corresponds to allowing a greater fit along the
\theta_{m}
direction. We arrive with the following duality results on READ learning, as the corollary of Propositions
2
and
3
.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\ell(x,y;\beta)=(y-x^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\beta)^{2}
denote the squared loss, we have for every
q\geq 1
and
\Lambda\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{M}

\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}^{*}_{N};c_{q,\Lambda})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\ell(X,Y;\beta)\right]=\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\sqrt{{\rm MSE}_{N}(\beta)}+\sqrt{\delta}\varphi_{p,\Lambda}(\beta)\right)^{2},

where

\varphi_{p,\Lambda}(\beta)=\min_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\left\{\sqrt{\|\beta-\mathbf{\Theta}\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{p}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{\Lambda^{\shortminus 1}}^{2}}\right\}.

Similarly, let
\sqrt{c_{q,\Lambda}}
denote the square-root of
c_{q,\Lambda}
whenever
c_{q,\Lambda}<\infty
, suppose the loss function
\ell(X,Y;\beta)
is either the logistic loss
\log{\left(1+e^{-Y\beta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}X}\right)}
or the hinge loss
(1-Y\beta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}X)^{+}
, then for every
q\geq 1
and
\Lambda\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{M}
,

\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}^{*}_{N};\sqrt{c_{q,\Lambda}})}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\ell(X,Y;\beta)\right]=\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell(x_{i},y_{i};\beta)+\delta\varphi_{p,\Lambda}(\beta)\right\}.

We make several remarks. First, compared with the weak-transferring formulation of
Wang et al., 2025
, our result is considerably more general, as it allows
q
and
p
to differ from
2
and accommodates multi-source representations
\Theta
. Secondly, the correspondence between the
q
-norm in
c_{q,\Lambda}
and the
p
-norm in
\varphi_{p,\Lambda}
holds for any pair of dual norms. Second, we allow each entry of
\Lambda
to take values in
\{0,\infty\}
. When
\lambda_{m}=0
, we have
\lambda_{m}^{\shortminus 1}\kappa_{m}^{2}<\infty
if and only if
\kappa_{m}=0
, under the convention that
0\cdot\infty=0
; this agrees with the interpretation that
\lambda_{m}=0
implies no transferring of prior knowledge. Conversely, when
\lambda_{m}=\infty
, we have
\lambda_{m}^{\shortminus 1}\kappa_{m}^{2}=0
for all
\kappa_{m}
, meaning there is no regularization on the projection onto the
\theta_{m}
direction. For the case of
p=q=2
, we have the close form expression

\displaystyle\inf_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\left\{\|x-\Theta\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{\Lambda^{\shortminus 1}}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=x^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\left(\mathbb{I}_{d}-\Theta(\Theta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\Theta+\Lambda^{\shortminus 1})^{\shortminus 1}\Theta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\right)x

\displaystyle=\|x\|^{2}_{\Psi_{\Lambda}},\quad\mbox{where}\quad\Psi_{\Lambda}\coloneqq(\mathbb{I}_{d}+\Theta\Lambda\Theta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T})^{\shortminus 1},

where the second equality follows from the
Woodbury Matrix Identity
. Finally, the convex objective function

\inf_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\sqrt{{\rm MSE}_{N}(\beta)}+\sqrt{\delta}\min_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\left\{\sqrt{\|\beta-\mathbf{\Theta}\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{p}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{\Lambda^{\shortminus 1}}^{2}}\right\}\right\},

can be reformulated into the following
p
-th order cone programming
for efficient optimization,

\displaystyle\inf_{\beta,\boldsymbol{\kappa},t,s_{1},s_{2}}

\displaystyle\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}\beta\|_{2}+\sqrt{\delta}t\right\}

s.t

\displaystyle s_{1}\geq\|\beta-\mathbf{\Theta}\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{p},

\displaystyle s_{2}\geq\|\Lambda^{\shortminus 1/2}\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{2},

\displaystyle t\geq\|[s_{1},s_{2}]^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\|_{2},

\displaystyle s_{1},s_{2},t\geq 0.

4
Asymptotic Analysis of READ

4.1
Overview

In this section, we present the asymptotic analysis of the READ estimator, serving as a technical prelude to the methodologies of the hyperparameter selection in Section
5
. We begin by extending the technique of
Robust Wasserstein Profile Inference
(RWPI), introduced in
(
Blanchet et al., 2019
)
, to the READ framework. This approach enables the construction of confidence regions for the global optimum, whose geometry reflects the influence of both
\Theta
and
\Lambda
. The global optimum
\beta^{*}
is defined as the unique solution to the stochastic program

\beta^{*}\coloneqq\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\left[\ell(X,Y;\beta)\right],

where
\ell(X,Y;\beta)
is a generic convex loss function in
\beta
and
\mathbb{P}^{*}
denotes the true data-generating distribution. In Section
4.2
, Our analysis characterizes the set of parameters
\beta
that are optimal to some distribution in the ambiguity set
\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}^{*}_{N};c_{q,\Lambda})
.

This motivates the
\delta
-radius selection rule such that the probability that
\beta^{*}
is an optimal parameter for some measure in the ambiguity set is at least
(1-\alpha)
.

In Section
4.3
, we derive the limiting distribution of the READ estimator under
\sqrt{N}
-scaling, with an explicit description of the bias term induced, for example, by the
\delta_{\alpha}(\Lambda)
selection algorithm. This characterization allows us to construct estimators indexed by
\Lambda
that share the same robustness guarantee, since their ambiguity sets all yield the same coverage probability for
\beta^{*}
, but may differ in their asymptotic bias.

Therefore, we consider the following selection rule for
\Lambda
: choose the value that minimizes the asymptotic bias of the estimators indexed by
\Lambda
, measured in the Mahalanobis norm induced by the precision matrix of
X
.

To complement the asymptotic convergence results, in Section
4.4
, we study the convergence guarantees for constrained optimization, under the assumption that the prior knowledge eventually contains the true solution, i.e.,
\beta^{*}\in\mathrm{col}(\Theta)
.

For the ease of later development, we assume
\dim(\beta)=\dim(X)=d
. Let the loss function be
\ell(x,y;\beta):(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and denote its
d
-dimensional gradient by
h(x,y;\beta)=\nabla_{\beta}\ell(x,y;\beta)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For instance, if
\ell(x,y;\beta)=(y-x^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\beta)^{2}
is the squared-error loss, then
h(x,y;\beta)=(y-x^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\beta)x
. The function
h
serves as the estimating equation for the objective (
4.1
). By convexity of
\ell
, solving (
4.1
) is equivalent to finding the root
\beta^{*}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[h(X,Y;\beta^{*})]=\mathbf{0}_{d}
.

4.2
RWPI and Confidence Region Constructions

For each distribution
\mathbb{P}^{\#}
in the Wasserstein ambiguity set
\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{N}^{*};c_{q,\Lambda})=\left\{\mathbb{P}:\mathcal{D}_{c_{q,\Lambda}}(\mathbb{P}_{N}^{*},\mathbb{P})\leq\delta\right\}
, we associate the set of minimizers

\mathbb{P}^{\#}\mapsto\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{\#}}\left[\ell(X,Y;\beta)\right].

Since the minimizer need not be unique, this naturally defines a set-valued mapping
\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{N}^{*};c_{q,\Lambda})\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The union of these elements defines the feasible set of candidate solutions for the WDRO problem:

\Omega_{N}(\delta)=\bigcup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{B}_{\delta}(\mathbb{P}_{N}^{*};c_{q,\Lambda})}\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\beta}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\ell(Y,X;\beta)\right].

Given a nominal
(1-\alpha)
-confidence level, we define the optimal radius
\delta_{\alpha,N}(\Lambda)
as the smallest value for which the true optimizer
\beta^{*}
is captured within
\Omega_{N}(\delta)
with probability at least
1-\alpha
:

\delta_{\alpha,N}(\Lambda)\coloneqq\inf\left\{\delta>0:\mathbb{P}^{*}(\beta^{*}\in\Omega_{N}(\delta))\geq 1-\alpha\right\}.

Selecting
\delta
this way provides a probabilistic guarantee that the DRO solution is robust on
\mathbb{P}^{*}
, since the ambiguity set is guaranteed to contain a distribution for which
\beta^{*}
is the optimal solution.

We now consider the
Robust Wasserstein Profile
(RWP) Function

R_{N}(\beta^{*})\coloneqq\begin{cases}\inf_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathcal{D}_{c_{q,\Lambda}}(\mathbb{P}_{N}^{*},\mathbb{Q})\\
\text{s.t. }\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[h(X,Y;\beta^{*})\right]=\mathbf{0}_{d}.\end{cases}

It is intuitive to see that
R_{N}(\beta^{*})
converges to zero as the increase of number of samples, and we observe the set equivalence

\left\{R_{N}(\beta^{*})\leq\delta\right\}=\left\{\beta^{*}\in\Omega_{N}(\delta)\right\},

therefore,
\delta_{\alpha,N}(\Lambda)
is chosen as the upper-
\alpha
quantile of the distribution of
R_{N}(\beta^{*})
. An asymptotic approximation of
\delta_{\alpha,N}(\Lambda)
will serve as our
\delta
-selection rule.

The following remark illustrates that the primal form of RWPI naturally yields adaptivity in
\delta
-selection with respect to the informativeness of
\Theta
to
\beta^{*}
.

Remark 1

(Linear Regression)
.

For simplicity, suppose that we have a single source of prior knowledge in vector
\theta
, and set
\lambda=\infty
.
The tractable reformulation in Proposition
4
suggests that
when the global optimum
\beta^{*}
lies close to
\spn{\theta}
, the optimally selected radius
\delta_{\alpha,N}(\infty)
should be large enough to ensure robustness and generalizability.
A large value of
\delta_{\alpha,N}(\infty)
encourages
\beta_{\rm DRO}
to remain close to
\spn{\theta}
, which is close to
\beta^{*}
due to their collinearity.
In the case where
\beta^{*}\in\spn{\theta}
, the optimal regularization should be
\delta_{\alpha,N}(\infty)=\infty
, yielding the most efficient estimator (the constrained estimator).
This conclusion is consistent with the primal definition of the RWP function
R_{N}(\beta^{*})
, assuming, for instance, a linear regression model with continuously distributed errors as in Example
2
.

Indeed, if
\beta^{*}\in\spn{\theta}
(say
\beta^{*}=\theta
), then a left multiplication of
\theta
on the estimating constraint

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[(Y-X^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\beta^{*})X]=\mathbf{0}_{d}

implies

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[(Y-X^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\beta^{*})\theta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}X]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{N}^{*}}\left[(Y-X^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\theta)\theta^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}X\right]=0.

However, the latter condition is almost surely false, which implies that
R_{N}(\beta^{*})=\infty
almost surely. This reasoning further suggests that as
\lambda
increases, the estimating equation becomes harder to satisfy, leading to larger values of
R_{N}(\beta^{*})
.
In other words, greater confidence in the prior knowledge warrants stronger regularization.

We now derive the limiting distribution of
R_{N}(\beta^{*})
, in order to obtain an asymptotic approximation of
\delta_{\alpha,N}(\Lambda)
and an explicit characterization of
\Omega_{N}(\delta)
. Define that

\Sigma\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\big[\nabla_{\beta}h(X,Y;\beta^{*})\big],\qquad\Xi^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\coloneqq\nabla_{x}h(X,Y;\beta^{*}).

In the case of the squared error loss, we have
\Xi^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}=(Y-X^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\beta^{*})\mathbb{I}_{d}-X{\beta^{*}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}.
We begin by listing the required assumptions.

Assumption 1

(Regularity I)
.

The loss function
\ell(x,y;\beta):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, is a non-negative function that is convex in
\beta
and twice differentiable with respect to
x
and
\beta
.

Assumption 2

(Optimality)
.

There exists
\beta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\left[h(X,Y;\beta^{*})\right]=\mathbf{0}_{d}
. Moreover, assume that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\|h(X,Y;\beta^{*})\|_{2}^{2}<\infty
,
\Sigma\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[\nabla_{\beta}h(X,Y;\beta_{*})]\succ\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}
, and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[\Xi^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\Xi]\succ\mathbf{0}_{d\times d}
.

Assumption 3

(Regularity II)
.

For every
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
p\geq 1
, the function
\|\nabla_{x}^{2}\ell(\,\cdot\,,y;\beta)\|_{p}
in
x
is uniformly continuous and bounded by a continuous function
M(\beta)
.

Assumption 4

(Regularity III)
.

For all
\beta
in a neighbourhood of
\beta^{*}
, there exists a constant
0<M^{\prime}<\infty
such that
\|\nabla_{x}h(x,y;\beta)\|_{q}\leq M^{\prime}(1+\|x\|_{q})
. In addition we have the following locally Lipschitz continuity:

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{x}h(x+\Delta,y;\beta^{*}+u)-\nabla_{x}h(x,y;\beta^{*})\|_{q}

\displaystyle\leq z(x)\left(\|\Delta\|_{q}+\|u\|_{q}\right),

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\beta}h(x+\Delta,y;\beta^{*}+u)-\nabla_{\beta}h(x,y;\beta^{*})\|_{q}

\displaystyle\leq z^{\prime}(x)\left(\|\Delta\|_{q}+\|u\|_{q}\right),

for
\|\Delta\|_{q}+\|u\|_{q}\leq 1
, where
z,z^{\prime}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
are such that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[z(X)^{2}]<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[z^{\prime}(X)^{2}]<\infty
.

Define the following function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, related to
\varphi_{p,\Lambda}^{2}
:

\psi_{p,\Lambda}(x)=\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\big[\varphi_{p,\Lambda}^{2}(\Xi x)\big]=\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\inf_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\left\{\|\Xi x-\Theta\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{p}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}\|_{\Lambda^{\shortminus 1}}^{2}\right\}.

Let
\psi_{p,\Lambda}^{*}(x)
denote its convex conjugate.

Assumption 5

(Positive Definiteness I)
.

For all
\Lambda\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{M}
and all directions
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\mathbb{P}^{*}\left(\varphi_{p,\Lambda}^{2}(\Xi\xi)>0\right)>0.

Assumptions
1
and
2
are standard in the literature on derivation of rates of convergence for
M
-estimators (such as
(
Wellner et al., 2013
, Section 3.2.2)
), while Assumptions
3
–
5
are naturally adapted regularity conditions for the standard WDRO
(
Blanchet et al., 2019
;
Blanchet et al., 2022
)
to our setting. Assumption
2
ensures that the global optimum
\beta^{*}
is unique through the positive definiteness of
\Sigma
, while the invertibility of
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[\Xi^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\Xi]
rules out redundancies such as collinearity in the covariate distribution. Assumption
3
guarantees that the inner maximization in the WDRO objective is finite for all
\Lambda\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{M}
. Finally, Assumption
4
imposes simple growth conditions on the estimating equation, which are useful for establishing the limit theorems.

Assumption
5
serves as a technical condition ensuring that the conjugate function
\psi^{*}_{p,\Lambda}
, which arises in the asymptotic analysis of
R_{N}(\beta^{*})
, is finite almost surely. We will show that this condition holds for the linear regression model (Example
2
) if and only if
\beta^{*}\notin\spn{\Theta_{\infty}}
, where
\Theta_{\infty}
denotes the submatrix of
\Theta
corresponding to entries of
\Lambda
that are equal to
\infty
. If instead
\beta^{*}\in\spn{\Theta_{\infty}}
, then
R_{N}(\beta^{*})\to\infty
, and we recover a constrained optimization problem discussed in more details in in Section
4.4
: the quantiles of
R_{N}(\beta^{*})
are automatically
\infty
, yielding a stronger form of regularization.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
-
5
hold,
then letting
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, we have the joint convergence

1.

N\times R_{N}(\beta^{*}+N^{-1/2}\times\,\cdot\,)\to\psi_{p,\Lambda}^{*}(H-\Sigma\times\,\cdot\,)
uniformly on compact sets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

2.

\sqrt{N}\times\left(\Omega_{N}^{+}(\eta/N)-\beta^{*}\right)\overset{k}{\leadsto}\Sigma^{\shortminus 1}H+\left\{u:\psi^{*}_{p,\Lambda}(\Sigma u)\leq\eta\right\}
,

where
H\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\cov_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}(h(X,Y;\beta^{*}))
and
\Omega_{N}^{+}(\eta/N)=\rm{cl}{\{\cap_{\epsilon>0}\Omega_{N}(\eta/N+\epsilon)\}}
, and
\overset{k}{\leadsto}
denote set convergence in the sense of Kuratowski
(
Rockafellar and Wets, 1998
, Section 4B)
.

The second statement follows directly from the first convergence. Recall that the convex conjugate of a quadratically homogeneous function is itself quadratically homogeneous, so
R_{N}(\beta^{*})
is essentially a quadratic functional of the empirical samples. By the set equivalence (
4.2
), we have the approximation
\delta_{\alpha,N}(\Lambda)\approx\eta_{\alpha}(\Lambda)/N
. By the
order-reversing property of convex conjugation
—that is, if
f\leq g
then
f^{*}\geq g^{*}
—we obtain

\psi_{p,\Lambda_{1}}^{*}(H)\overset{D}{<}\psi_{p,\Lambda_{2}}^{*}(H)\quad\text{whenever}\quad\Lambda_{1}\prec\Lambda_{2},

where
\overset{D}{<}
denotes stochastic dominance. Here
\Lambda_{1}\prec\Lambda_{2}
refers to the
Loewner order
, meaning that
\Lambda_{2}-\Lambda_{1}
is positive semidefinite. Consequently, the
(1-\alpha)
quantile
\eta_{\alpha}(\Lambda)
of
\psi_{p,\Lambda}^{*}(H)
is an increasing function of
\Lambda
in the Loewner order, which is consistent with the intuition of Remark
1
.

We now observe that
\Sigma^{\shortminus 1}H
is the limiting distribution of the empirical risk minimizer (ERM) estimator; that is,
\sqrt{N}(\beta_{\mathrm{ERM}}-\beta^{*})\Rightarrow\Sigma^{\shortminus 1}H
. Since both
\beta_{\mathrm{ERM}}
and
\Omega_{N}(\eta_{\alpha}(\Lambda)/N)
are constructed from the same sample, their joint convergence follows.
This yields the following representation-aware confidence region, centered at the ERM estimator
\beta_{\mathrm{ERM}}
.

Corollary 2
.

Let
\eta_{\alpha}(\Lambda)
denote the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of the distribution of
\psi^{*}_{p,\Lambda}(H)
, then the set

\beta_{\mathrm{ERM}}+\left\{u:\psi^{*}_{p,\Lambda}(\Sigma u)\leq\frac{\eta_{\alpha}(\Lambda)}{N}\right\}

is an asymptotically
(1-\alpha)
-confidence region for
\beta^{*}
. Moreover, if
\psi^{*}_{p,\Lambda}(\Sigma\times\,\cdot\,)
and
\eta_{\alpha}(\Lambda)
are replaced by any consistent estimators, the resulting confidence region remains asymptotically valid at level
(1-\alpha)
.

We now derive the limiting distribution of
\psi^{*}_{p,\Lambda}(H)
in closed form for the linear regression model with
p=q=2
. The resulting expression shows that
\psi^{*}_{2,\Lambda}
varies smoothly with
\Lambda
, and that the corresponding confidence region is an ellipsoid whose boundary deforms continuously as the entries of
\Lambda
change.

Example 2

(Linear Regression Model)
.

Recall that for the case of
p=q=2
, we have the simplified expression

\psi_{2,\Lambda}(x)=4^{\shortminus 1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[\varphi_{2,\Lambda}^{2}(\Xi x)]=4^{\shortminus 1}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\|\Xi x\|_{\Psi_{\Lambda}}^{2}.

Consider the linear regression model
Y=X^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\beta^{*}+e
, where the additive error
e
has zero mean and variance
\sigma^{2}
. The covariance matrix from the estimating equation covariance matrix is
\cov_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}\big(h(X,Y,\beta^{*})\big)=\sigma^{2}\Sigma
, with
\Sigma=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{*}}[X^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}X]
. Now we have,

\displaystyle\psi_{2,\Lambda}(\xi)=4^{\shortminus 1}\xi^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\left(\sigma^{2}\Psi_{\Lambda}+\|\beta^{*}\|_{{\Psi_{\Lambda}}}^{2}\Sigma\right)\xi.

Since
\Sigma
is invertible by assumption, the convex conjugate of
\psi_{2,\Lambda}
in
H
simplifies to

\psi^{*}_{2,\Lambda}(H)=\sigma^{2}Z^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}\Gamma^{\shortminus 1}Z,

with
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{d})
and
\Gamma\coloneqq\|\beta^{*}\|_{\Psi_{\Lambda}}\mathbb{I}_{d}+\sigma^{2}\Sigma^{\shortminus 1/2}\Psi_{\Lambda}\Sigma^{\shortminus 1/2}
, which is invertible as long as
\|\beta^{*}\|_{\Psi_{\Lambda}}>0
. In this case,
\psi_{2,\Lambda}^{*}(H)
follows a generalized chi-square distribution, that is,

\psi_{2,\Lambda}^{*}(H)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\gamma_{i}}W_{i},

where
\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{d}\}=\spec{\Gamma}
is the complete set of eigenvalues of
\Gamma
. Again, we see that we can raise
\psi^{*}_{2,\Lambda}(H)
in the stochastic order by lowering the eigenvalues of
\Gamma
, i.e. raising the entries of
\Lambda
.

As a concrete verification of Assumption
5

4.4

Assumptions
5

6

and

(for each

Assumption 7

This condition requires that the estimation error of the source subspace

Suppose Assumptions

The READ framework delivers robust generalization guarantees against distributional shifts that preserve a common representation
\Theta^{*}
. Suppose the external knowledge
\Theta^{*}
admits the decomposition

\beta^{*}=\Theta^{*}{{}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}}\boldsymbol{\kappa}+\varepsilon,

where, after absorbing the representation-aligned components, the residual
\varepsilon
lies in
(\spn{\Theta^{*}})^{\perp}
. As argued in Section
3
, the representation
\Theta^{*}
is often stable across environments, while distributional perturbations act primarily through the residual. A perturbed future parameter thus takes the form

\beta^{\prime}=\Theta^{*}\boldsymbol{\kappa}+\varepsilon^{\prime},

with
\Theta^{*}{{}^{\scriptscriptstyle\sf T}}\varepsilon^{\prime}\approx 0
, so that
\varepsilon^{\prime}
remains nearly orthogonal to
\Theta^{*}
. Consequently, potential future drifts of
\beta^{\prime}
occur predominantly along
(\spn{\Theta^{*}})^{\perp}
, since

P_{\Theta^{*}}(\beta^{*}-\beta^{\prime})=P_{\Theta^{*}}(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime})=P_{\Theta^{*}}(\varepsilon^{\prime})\approx 0.

Figure
1

\lambda
increases from

to
\infty
, the boundary of the confidence region transitions relative to the standard OLS confidence region (shown in black). Because
\lambda
quantifies confidence in the prior knowledge vector
\theta
, larger values of
\lambda
contract the region along the direction of
\theta
while expanding it in orthogonal directions. In this way, ambiguity is reallocated: it is reduced in directions where structural prior knowledge is trusted, and shifted toward directions where prior information is less reliable.

While the confidence regions have constant volume under the Mahalanobis geometry defined by
\Sigma^{-1}
, their effective geometry changes once supplemented with
\Theta
and the optimal
\Lambda
. In particular, when
\Theta
is consistent and the perturbation
\varepsilon^{\prime}
is nearly orthogonal to
\Theta
. Our READ framework therefore, produces ambiguity sets that are not only valid in the classical sense but also aligned with the invariance of the problem, concentrating on distributions whose optimal solutions are the ones most likely to be realized under future distributional shifts. In doing so, READ provides stronger and more meaningful generalization guarantees than OLS or conventional DRO approaches. Finally, as shown in Figure
1
, the sequence of READ estimators consistently lies closer to the true
\beta^{*}
than the ERM estimator
\beta^{\mathrm{ERM}}
.

\rho\sigma^{2}&\sigma^{2}\end{pmatrix}\right),

where
\rho\in[0,1]
is the correlation between
\beta^{*}
and
\theta
.
Equivalently, given
\beta^{*}\sim N(\mathbf{0}_{d},\sigma^{2}\mathbb{I}_{d})
, we may generate

\theta=\rho\beta^{*}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\,\varepsilon,

with
\varepsilon\sim N(\mathbf{0}_{d},\sigma^{2}\mathbb{I}_{d})
. In this simulation we primarily consider the
\beta^{*}
generating process using a single latent combination on
\Theta
. Generate
\theta_{m}\overset{\rm iid}{\sim}N(\mathbf{0}_{d},\sigma^{2}\mathbb{I}_{d})
, where we typically take
\sigma^{2}=C\cdot d^{\shortminus 1}
to control the size of the vector for some fixed constant
C>0
, and set
\Theta=[\theta_{1};\ldots;\theta_{M}]
. Let
\boldsymbol{\kappa}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
be a unit vector in the Euclidean norm, form
\theta_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=\Theta\boldsymbol{\kappa}
given
\Theta
, then generate

\beta^{*}=\rho\theta_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\varepsilon,\quad\mbox{where}\quad\varepsilon\sim N(\mathbf{0}_{d},\sigma^{2}\mathbb{I}_{d}).

6.2.4
Result IV: Robustness Against Distributional Shift

Figure 5:
Performance comparison in Setting IV across varying
\rho
,
N
, and

.

In this experiment, we evaluate the generalization performance of
READ
and five benchmark models under distributional shifts that are approximately
\Theta
-invariant, as motivated in Section
5.3
. Specifically, we begin with a fixed ground-truth parameter
\beta^{*}
used to generate training data. After fitting each estimator, we assess its out-of-sample predictive performance across 20 test environments. Each test environment is generated by perturbing
\beta^{*}
along directions nearly orthogonal to
\Theta
:

\beta^{\prime}=\beta^{*}+P_{\Theta^{\perp}}(z)+0.2\,P_{\Theta}(z),

where

z\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0},\frac{C}{5d}\mathbb{I}_{d}\right),\quad\text{and}\quad C=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\beta^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\right].

This ensures that
\mathbb{E}\|z\|_{2}^{2}=C/5
, so the perturbation is modest in magnitude. Importantly, the scaling ensures that most of the variation in
\beta^{\prime}
lies in the orthogonal complement of
\Theta
, with only minor leakage into
\spn{\Theta}
. This setup mimics realistic scenarios where the underlying representation remains stable across environments, while the task-specific component varies. The performance metric is computed as the average percentage improvement in mean squared error (MSE) over the baseline ERM estimator across the 20 testing environments. All testing regimes are identical to those described in the settings of Experiment I.

We observe in Figure
5
that
READ
consistently outperforms all benchmark methods across the testing environments. Notably, however, the performance gap between
READ
and
KG-DRO
narrows under this setting. This is expected, as
KG-DRO
corresponds to a limiting case of
READ
where the alignment parameter
\Lambda
is set to infinity. Such a formulation becomes nearly optimal when the distributional shift is entirely
\Theta
-invariant, i.e., when the perturbed parameter takes the form
\beta^{\prime}=\beta^{*}+P_{\Theta^{\perp}}(z)
.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced
READ
, a representation-aware extension of Wasserstein DRO that incorporates alignment hyperparameters into the transport cost, enabling direction-specific robustness. Leveraging convex duality, we derived a tractable formulation and established asymptotic guarantees for both inference and
\sqrt{N}
-scaled limit distributions. These theoretical insights motivated adaptive and statistically valid procedures for selecting both the Wasserstein radius and alignment parameters. The resulting DRO formulation yields radii that are adaptively calibrated to the informativeness of the external knowledge with respect to the target distribution, thereby enabling a data-informed construction of both the transport cost and uncertainty set that has been difficult in distributionally robust estimation. The resulting WDRO framework thus provides generalization guarantees over a class of future distributions that preserve invariance with respect to the representation learned from the current environment. Empirically,
READ
consistently outperforms benchmark methods across diverse regimes, demonstrating robustness even under contaminated or spurious prior knowledge-scenarios where approaches like
KG-DRO
often degrade-as well as under distributional shifts that are invariant to the shared representation. Future research directions include extensions to nonlinear representations, online adaptation to evolving environments, and high-dimensional analysis for applications in domains such as genomics and quantitative finance.
